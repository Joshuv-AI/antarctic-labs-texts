Data-Driven Clustering of Secretory Bone and Inflammatory Markers to Subphenotype Patients Undergoing Hemodialysis
Authors: Simon Aberger, Ameen Abu-Hanna, Vianda Stel, Nicholas Chesnaye, Emanuel Zitt, Daniel Cejka, Thomas B. Dschietzig, Franz P. Armbruster, Hildegard Hafner-Gießauf, Maximilian Waller, Corinna Schabhüttl, Alexander H. Kirsch, Alexander R. Rosenkranz, Kathrin Eller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionParathyroid hormone (PTH) remains the primary biomarker used to classify and monitor chronic kidney disease–mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD); nonetheless, its reliability for risk stratification and treatment guidance is limited. Multidimensional biomarker clustering that integrates indicators of bone turnover, vascular calcification, inflammatory and oxidative stress may better capture the heterogeneity of clinical phenotypes, improve risk stratification, and inform on new therapeutic approaches in dialysis care.MethodsWe conducted a computational analysis of a multicentric cohort study including 471 patients undergoing hemodialysis. The Partitioning Around Medoids algorithm was used to cluster the cohort by a pathophysiology-based biomarker panel (noxPTH, intact parathyroid hormone [iPTH], osteoprotegerin [OPG], soluble receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand [sRANKL], antioxidative capacity [ImAnOx], oxidative capacity [PerOx], β-Crosslaps [β-CL], and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein [hsCRP]) at baseline. The cluster phenotypes were characterized by anthropometrics, imaging, and clinical data. Cluster-specific outcomes and event patterns were assessed after 1 year.ResultsA total of 4 distinct clusters were identified. Cluster 1 reflected a high bone turnover state with high antioxidative capacity and high bone-specific alkaline phosphatase. Cluster 2 showed low bone turnover with high antioxidative capacity and high sclerostin levels. Cluster 3 exhibited an inflammatory–oxidative phenotype and the highest 1-year mortality; cluster membership improved prediction of events beyond clinical covariates. This phenotype was marked by high aortic calcium burden, vascular disease, markers of vitamin D degradation, low vaccine response, and hepatic steatosis. Cluster 4 showed a balanced biomarker profile and the highest rate of reclassification after 1 year.ConclusionMultidimensional biomarker clustering captures heterogeneity in bone and vascular disease phenotypes of CKD-MBD, exhibiting differential mortality risk and event patterns and highlighting inflammation as an important pathway for further studies.

CKD-MBD is a systemic condition in patients undergoing hemodialysis, characterized by dysregulation of bone turnover, mineral metabolism, and vascular calcification, all of which contribute to increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.1 PTH remains the central biomarker for classifying and monitoring CKD-MBD and for determining secondary hyperparathyroidism and bone metabolic activity.2 Longitudinal assessment of markers of bone metabolism has been shown to be associated with outcomes, as shown in the EQUAL study.3 However, the clinical use of PTH is limited by analytic variability, accumulation of biologically inactive fragments, post-translational modifications, such as oxidation, and end-organ resistance, which collectively undermine its reliability for risk stratification and as a therapeutic target.2

Trials in CKD-MBD frequently involve biochemical outcomes and surrogate clinical outcomes, but hard clinical outcome trials remain scarce.4 The EVOLVE study showed that pharmacologic modulation of PTH leads to biochemical improvements, whereas the inference of reductions in mortality or major cardiovascular events was hampered by methodological shortcomings.5 Thus, potential beneficial effects of PTH lowering remain unproven. This disconnect between PTH lowering and improved patient outcomes may be attributable to a multifactorial pathophysiology of CKD-MBD. Similarly, the benefit of phosphate control has yet to be proven, as the HiLo study was terminated early because of enrollment issues,6 and the PHOSPHATE trial (NCT03573089) is currently ongoing.

Current literature emphasizes the critical roles of inflammation and oxidative stress in CKD-MBD progression. Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), a marker of systemic inflammation, is independently associated with derangements in bone and mineral metabolism,7 as well as increased cardiovascular risk and mortality in patients undergoing hemodialysis.8 Oxidative stress further exacerbates vascular and skeletal pathology and may modify PTH structure and function, contributing to PTH resistance and impaired bone response.2 Recently, IL-6 inhibition by clazakizumab has been shown to effectively reduce CRP levels in a phase 2b study in patients undergoing hemodialysis,9 laying the foundation for a phase 3 trial (NCT05485961).

The need for integrative approaches to advance personalized management has been emphasized in the latest Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes controversies statement on CKD-MBD by Ketteler et al.10 Given these complexities, we hypothesize that (i) multidimensional biomarker clustering, integrating bone turnover markers, vascular calcification indices, inflammatory mediators, and oxidative stress biomarkers, may advance the pathophysiology-based subclassification of patients undergoing hemodialysis and (ii) could improve prospective risk stratification. We herein report a computational analysis of biomarkers, clinical features, and event patterns, reinforcing the role of pathophysiology-based phenotyping to capture the underlying heterogeneity in a population susceptible to bone disorders and vascular disease.

Methods

Study Design

The analysis was conducted as part of the “HEMOcalc” study, a registered, multicentric cohort study (NCT07170761, 34-322ex21/22). Of 475 enrolled patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis recruited from 4 dialysis centers in Austria (Graz, Linz, Feldbach, Feldkirch), 471 were included in the final analysis. Baseline blood samples were obtained midweek before dialysis and analyzed for a panel of markers related to bone and mineral metabolism, vascular pathology, vitamin D metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammation. Laboratory analyses, including measurements of noxPTH, iPTH, OPG, RANKL, ImAnOx, PerOx, β-CL, hsCRP, bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BAP), sclerostin, 1,25(OH)2D3, 25(OH)D3, 24,25(OH)2D3, and myeloperoxidase, were performed centrally by Immundiagnostik AG Germany (Supplementary Table S1).

Annual clinical follow-up and data collection were conducted through medical record review and linkage with the Austrian Dialysis and Transplant Registry. Uniform electronic case report forms were implemented across all centers using the REDCap data collection platform. In a subset of 148 patients, repeat blood sampling was performed after 1 year with an extended 2-year follow-up period. Additional data at baseline included anthropometric measurements, standardized grading of abdominal aortic calcification (in intervals of 0.5 from grade 0 = without any radiologically visible calcification, grade 1 = calcification of up to 33% of the arterial circumference, grade 2 = calcification of up to 66% of the circumference, to grade 3 = circular calcification)11 by noncontrast computed tomography imaging of the abdominopelvic region, and presence of valvular calcification on echocardiography.

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality after 1 year. Secondary outcomes included cause-specific death, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) (4-point MACE), incident fractures, hospitalization for infection or heart failure, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease interventions.

Cluster Analysis

The clustering analysis aimed to identify patient subgroups representing pathophysiologic pathways of vascular and bone disease, on the basis of 7 prespecified biomarkers—noxPTH, β-CL, OPG, sRANKL, hsCRP, lmAnOx, and PerOx. Variable selection followed the pathophysiology-based clustering strategy described by Wagner et al.12 and was designed to capture complementary biological domains, including bone turnover, osteoblast/osteoclast signaling, inflammation, oxidative stress, and antioxidative capacity. To maintain a parsimonious model appropriate for the cohort size, biomarkers providing overlapping information (Spearman correlation coefficient > 0.5), including iPTH, BAP, sclerostin, vitamin D metabolites, and myeloperoxidase, were excluded from the clustering algorithm to reduce redundancy and collinearity but were analyzed descriptively across the identified clusters. Before distance computation, variables were Winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles to reduce the influence of extreme outliers and then center-level standardized (mean 0; SD 1). The range-normalized Manhattan distances were calculated, and clustering was performed using Partitioning Around Medoids through the R package cluster, a robust alternative to k-means clustering.13 The optimal number of clusters was determined on the basis of silhouette width analysis. Cluster stability was evaluated using bootstrap resampling14 with 1000 replicates. Post hoc visualization of cluster profiles was performed using radar charts showing z-scores. Differences in clustering variables, biomarkers, metabolites, and clinical characteristics were explored descriptively across the identified clusters. Sensitivity analyses included leave-one-center-out analysis and longitudinal validation of the clustering results in a subset of patients with repeated measurements of cluster markers after 1 year.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics—mean ± SD or median with interquartile range for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Differences among clusters were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test or one-way ANOVA, as appropriate, for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure.

Cumulative survival differences between clusters were assessed through Kaplan-Meier analysis, censoring for kidney transplantation. Secondary outcomes were analyzed using the cumulative incidence function, accounting for competing events (cause-specific death and kidney transplantation).

For deeper analysis of the prognostic potential of cluster membership for all-cause mortality, we used Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards models accounting for kidney transplantation as a competing risk. An incremental modeling strategy was used to examine whether cluster membership captures a similar proportion of prognostic information as the individual cluster components when added to baseline clinical variables. Backward variable selection for the baseline model was guided by the Akaike Information Criterion. Model 1 included Akaike Information Criterion–selected baseline variables—age, body mass index, dialysis vintage, heart failure status, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease status, and serum albumin. Model 2 added the individual cluster components. Cluster membership (with a value of 1 for patients belonging to the respective cluster and otherwise 0) was then added to the baseline variables (Model 3) and individual cluster components (Model 4). Missing baseline variables (<10% missingness) were imputed using the multiple imputation by chained equations algorithm.

A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was accepted as statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using the R statistical language (version 4.5.2).

Results

Unsupervised Clustering Revealed 4 Different Clusters in the Hemodialysis Cohort

A total of 471 patients undergoing hemodialysis from 4 Austrian centers were included in the final analysis (Supplementary Figure S1); baseline characteristics are shown in Supplementary Table S2. Using unsupervised clustering of 7 prespecified, pathophysiology-based biomarkers reflecting bone turnover, oxidative stress, and inflammation (noxPTH, β-Crosslaps, OPG, sRANKL, hsCRP, ImAnOx, PerOx), we identified 4 biologically distinct patient clusters (Jaccard similarity indices 0.73–0.88; mean, 0.80). Cluster profiles showed distinct patterns across bone turnover, inflammation, and oxidative stress markers (Figure 1). Cluster 1 had the highest noxPTH and β-CL levels, suggesting a high-bone-turnover state, whereas Cluster 2 exhibited a low bone turnover profile with preserved antioxidant capacity. Cluster 3 was characterized by a low bone turnover profile with the highest hsCRP and PerOx, and Cluster 4 showed intermediate marker levels (Figure 1). The iPTH–noxPTH correlation was strong across all clusters (Supplementary Figure S2). Patients were well balanced across centers within each cluster (Supplementary Table S3). Cluster Characteristics Including Cardiovascular Morbidity, Infections, and Overall Mortality

The assigned clusters were then used to compare anthropometric data, clinical characteristics, calcification burden, and additional biomarkers to reach a deeper understanding of the underlying phenotypes (Table 1). Cluster 1 was younger (median age, 60 years), had lower dialysis vintage, lower cardiovascular comorbidities, and accordingly a lower aortic calcification grading (Figure 2a). Cluster 1 also reached the highest BAP levels with high serum phosphate and low sclerostin levels. Patients in Cluster 2 and Cluster 3 were older (median age, 73 and 72 years, respectively) with higher comorbidity burden, including coronary artery and peripheral artery disease. Cluster 2 and Cluster 3 showed a low bone turnover profile, but sclerostin was significantly elevated only in Cluster 2. Cluster 3 had the highest aortic calcification grading (Figure 2a) and valvular calcification burden (Table 1). In addition, Cluster 3 showed the lowest serum albumin (Figure 2b), highest proportion of hepatic steatosis, and the lowest anti-HBs (antibodies against hepatitis B surface antigen) titers (Table 1). Furthermore, a higher number of central venous catheter vascular accesses was notable (Table 1). Cluster 4 had a median age of 70 years with a lower dialysis vintage similar to Cluster 1 and an otherwise intermediate marker and feature profile. Serum calcium, hemoglobin levels, and markers of iron repletion were balanced between groups, with equal rates of erythropoietin-stimulating agents and intravenous iron prescription. Concerning medication differences, the proportion of patients receiving calcimimetics (50.7%) and phosphate binders (84%) was highest in Cluster 1 (Table 1). In Clusters 2, 3, and 4, calcimimetics were used in 27%–34% of patients, whereas phosphate binders and active vitamin D were prescribed in about 60% of patients (Table 1). Patients Undergoing Hemodialysis Grouped in Cluster 3 Exhibit the Highest 1-Year Mortality

The Kaplan-Meier analysis showed significant survival differences by cluster (log-rank P < 0.001) (Figure 3), with Cluster 3 exhibiting the highest 1-year mortality of 37.1 % (Supplementary Table S5). Fine–Gray competing risk regression showed that inclusion of Cluster 3 membership in the baseline clinical model was associated with a markedly increased risk of 1-year mortality (hazard ratio, 3.01; 95% confidence interval, 1.88–4.65; P < 0.01) and improvement in model discrimination (area under the curve, 0.84; integrated discrimination improvement = 0.06; P = 0.04 vs baseline) (Supplementary Table S6). When including cluster components and cluster membership, Cluster 3 remained a meaningful predictor of increased 1-year mortality (hazard ratio, 1.97; 95% confidence interval, 0.93–3.58; P = 0.08) (Table 2) without further improvement in model performance (integrated discrimination improvement = 0.01) (Supplementary Table S6). Cumulative incidence analysis with competing risks showed that Cluster 3 had the highest rates of cardiovascular and sepsis-related death (13.2% and 5.8% at 1 year, respectively), as well as MACE (16.3%) and infection-related hospitalizations (24.6%) (Table 3). No significant cluster differences were observed in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease interventions, fracture-related hospitalizations, or heart failure admissions. Validation of the Clustering Approach by Remeasuring Variables in Patients Undergoing Hemodialysis After 1 Year

For internal validation, clustering variables were remeasured in a subset of 148 patients at year 1 to repeat clustering, along with assessment of all-cause mortality during and an extended observation period for another year. Clustering at year 1 confirmed the cluster structure (Supplementary Figure S3). Compared with baseline, around 40% of patients in Clusters 1 and 2 changed their cluster status after 1 year, predominantly into Cluster 4 (Supplementary Table S7). The highest reclassification rate was seen in Cluster 4 (68%). Cluster 3 was more stable, with only 32% of patients changing their cluster status (Supplementary Figure S4). Discussion

This multicenter cohort comprises 471 prevalent patients undergoing hemodialysis with detailed documentation of clinical characteristics, comorbidity burden, and treatment regimens in accordance with current guidelines. By integrating multidimensional biomarker data reflecting bone metabolism, oxidative stress, and systemic inflammation, we identified 4 reproducible and biologically coherent patient clusters. These clusters diverged in both pathophysiologic profiles and clinical trajectories, supporting the relevance of pathophysiology-based subclassification in patients undergoing hemodialysis to reflect heterogeneity and outcomes in bone and vascular diseases. Systemic inflammation stands out as a potential risk feature to be addressed in future studies.

Cluster 1 displayed high bone turnover with preserved antioxidant status and low vascular risk. This aligns with evidence showing that high PTH and turnover markers often characterize younger patients with lower calcification burden.15 Cluster 1 is demographically and clinically similar to the populations enrolled in the EVOLVE trial (cinacalcet) and the phase 3 trials of etelcalcetide,16 in which relatively low event rates occurred. Concordantly, Cluster 1 in our cohort exhibited lower mortality, fewer MACE events, and less aortic calcification. The high anti-HBs titers may indicate preserved immune competence and less immune exhaustion.17

Cluster 2 exhibited an adynamic bone phenotype, consistent with older age, lower bone turnover markers, and greater comorbidity burden. Although low bone turnover is linked to fracture risk and multimorbidity in CKD,18 mortality in this cluster remained low and comparable with Clusters 1 and 4, despite the highest age, longest dialysis vintage, and marked calcification burden. The high vascular intervention rate of 11.1% was substantially driven by lower limb events (8.9%), which may be consistent with a predominance of Mönckeberg-type medial arterial calcification, a process driven by vascular smooth muscle cell transdifferentiation, characteristically independent of systemic inflammation.19 The absence of elevated hsCRP with preserved antioxidative capacity, low bone turnover, and elevated sclerostin is a plausible biological features in line with this calcification pattern. Given the low bone turnover and high 1-year fracture rate of 5.8%, the elevated sclerostin levels may be relevant, as romosozumab (a monoclonal antibody targeting sclerostin) has shown promise in improving bone turnover and bone mineral density in patients undergoing hemodialysis.20 Cluster 2 may represent an interesting target population for future studies examining fracture- and bone structure-related outcomes in a relatively low cardiovascular risk setting.

Cluster 3 showed a pronounced inflammatory–oxidative stress signal and high cardiovascular event rates. This subgroup is consistent with literature connecting oxidative stress and osteo-immunologic pathways (i.e., OPG–RANKL axis) with progressive vascular calcification, intimal artery disease, and mortality in dialysis,21,22 thus resembling a CKD-associated cardiovascular disease phenotype.10 The marked elevation of hsCRP and oxidative stress markers, combined with a predominance of coronary MACE over peripheral events, is consistent with intimal atherosclerotic disease as a plausible biological interpretation in this cluster. The lowest vitamin D metabolite ratio in Cluster 3 may reflect inflammation-driven upregulation of vitamin D catabolism, further compounding the adverse metabolic milieu in this subgroup. Furthermore, the high proportion of hepatic steatosis comprises a link to metabolic inflammation, whereas the high rate of infections and lowest anti-HBs titers may indicate an additional disadvantageous immune regulation. The higher prevalence of central venous catheter vascular access in Cluster 3 may also have contributed to the increased burden of infection-related hospitalizations and sepsis mortality. However, central venous catheter access was not retained as an independent predictor in the multivariable survival analysis, suggesting that it is unlikely to fully explain the adverse outcomes observed in this cluster. Cluster 4 represented an intermediate phenotype of secondary hyperparathyroidism with moderate bone turnover, inflammation, and comorbidity burden and showed frequent reassignment over time. In line with our data, hyperparathyroid profiles in previous classification models also appeared to have more favorable outcomes.15

The addition of cluster membership as a binary predictor improved prediction of 1-year mortality beyond the clinical model and achieved predictive performance comparable with the individual cluster components. These findings suggest that cluster membership fully reflects the shared prognostic information captured by the individual biomarkers, providing an information-dense representation of biological risk. As such, the 4 cluster-derived phenotypes offer a clinically interpretable framework for risk stratification and understanding biological heterogeneity in the hemodialysis population.

Recent literature highlights the importance of systemic inflammation and oxidative stress as central drivers of vascular disease in CKD-MBD pathogenesis and outcomes.23,24 Our multidimensional clustering of established bone turnover markers with inflammatory stress signals achieved a clinical, phenotypic separation not attainable by single-analyte approaches. This was reflected in 2 low-event phenotypes with either true high bone turnover or an intermediary hyperparathyroid signature and 2 differential calcification phenotypes with osteoporotic fracture risk and detrimental MACE, inflammation, and mortality, respectively. As these biological signals are fundamental to CKD-MBD pathophysiology, the phenotypic structure is likely applicable across diverse hemodialysis populations. This integrated characterization represents a meaningful advancement over existing CKD-MBD classifications and provides a framework for personalizing therapeutic targets in accordance with Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes’ consensus work explicitly advocating a move from single-analyte targets to integrated, personalized risk models of vascular and bone disease.10,25

The inflammatory profile and unfavorable outcomes of Cluster 3 are intriguing because systemic inflammation is not currently a treatment target in dialysis care. A recent randomized phase 2b trial of the IL-6–ligand inhibitor clazakizumab in maintenance hemodialysis showed a ∼90% reduction in hsCRP, favorable shifts in other inflammatory biomarkers, and an increase in serum albumin levels.9 A phase 3 study is ongoing to investigate clinical outcomes of anti-inflammatory use in hemodialysis (NCT05485961). The recent PISCES trial reported a striking reduction in cardiovascular mortality in patients receiving n-3 fatty acids.26 Because the trial was conducted in an unselected dialysis population, the mechanisms underlying this benefit remain unclear. A pathophysiology-driven intervention strategy, such as targeting anti-inflammatory or antioxidative therapies to patients in the inflammatory–oxidative Cluster 3, could be the subject of further study to help identify subgroups with concentrated risk and greater potential for therapeutic response.

Another key strength is that reclustering after 1 year reproduced the same subgroups and confirmed the high mortality of Cluster 3, showing that the approach can prospectively identify high-risk patients. Regarding cluster stability, transitions were not randomly distributed—Clusters 1, 2, and 3 showed limited crossflow between each other, with reassignment occurring predominantly into Cluster 4 in approximately 40% of patients from clusters 1 and 2, and 30% from Cluster 3. The highly directional nature of these transitions argues against analytical variability as an explanation. Instead, given that Cluster 4 carries more favorable outcomes and an intermediary biomarker profile, these shifts may reflect a treatment effect. This underscores the need for longitudinal phenotyping to distinguish stable biological phenotypes from dynamic, treatment-responsive states. Prospective studies in incident patients undergoing hemodialysis with repeated phenotyping are needed to confirm this interpretation.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although the clustering approach revealed biologically and prognostically distinct subgroups, external validation in independent hemodialysis cohorts is lacking. However, the rigorous sensitivity analysis in a multicenter cohort and the consistent results by reassessment of clusters after 1 year strengthen the internal validity and generalizability of our results. Second, although noxPTH® and iPTH were included as indicators of PTH bioactivity consistent with current guidance at the time of study design, the biomarker panel warrants critical appraisal considering updated consensus recommendations.25 BAP served as the primary confirmatory marker of bone turnover and aligns with currently recommended panels.25 Concerning renal clearance, all patients were on maintenance hemodialysis with comparable residual renal function, suggesting that relative differences between clusters still reflect true biological variation. TRAcP5b and intact procollagen type I N-propeptide represent preferred nonrenally cleared alternatives for future studies.25 The stable iPTH:noxPTH ratio across all clusters (range 1.5–1.6; P = 0.32) suggests results would be reproducible with conventional iPTH. Third, adjustment for dialysis vintage partly addresses potential survival–prevalence bias. Nonetheless, the association between biomarker profiles and outcomes requires confirmation in incident patients undergoing hemodialysis. In addition, calcification measures (n = 257) and repeat biomarker assessments (n = 148) were only accessible in a subset of the cohort, which may limit statistical power in longitudinal analyses. Fourth, skeletal characterization in this study was indirect, relying on biomarkers of bone turnover and fracture rates. The absence of bone mineral density assessment, bone turnover–specific imaging, or histomorphometric validation constrains the conclusions that can be drawn regarding skeletal biology and limits interpretation of fracture outcomes beyond association with cluster membership. These investigations should be incorporated in future studies aiming to validate the bone phenotypes identified here. Fifth, although cluster membership added prognostic value beyond individual biomarkers, the mechanistic underpinnings of these phenotypes require further investigation, ideally through integrative OMICs and interventional studies.

In conclusion, multidimensional biomarker clustering captures heterogeneity in the bone-vascular disease axis of CKD-MBD and identifies phenotypes exhibiting differential mortality risk and event patterns in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis. Inflammation and oxidative stress remain currently uncontrolled risk factors in association with adverse outcomes. Our results highlight the potential of pathophysiology-based patient classifications to address unmet needs in individualizing CKD-MBD management and advancing precision medicine in dialysis care.
