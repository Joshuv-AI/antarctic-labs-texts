Lanczos with compression for symmetric eigenvalue problems
Angelo A. Casulli, Daniel Kressner, Nian Shao †

Abstract
The Lanczos method with implicit restarting is one of the most popular methods for finding a few exterior eigenpairs of a large symmetric matrix A A . Usually based on polynomial filtering, restarting is crucial to limit memory and the cost of orthogonalization. In this work, we propose a novel strategy for the same purpose, called Lanczos with compression. Unlike polynomial filtering, our approach compresses the Krylov subspace using rational approximation and, in doing so, it sacrifices the structure of the associated Krylov decomposition. Nevertheless, it remains compatible with subsequent Lanczos steps and the overall algorithm is still solely based on matrix-vector products with A A . On the theoretical side, we show that compression introduces only a small error compared to standard (unrestarted) Lanczos and therefore has only a negligible impact on convergence. Comparable guarantees are not available for commonly used implicit restarting strategies, including the Krylov–Schur method. On the practical side, our numerical experiments demonstrate that compression often outperforms the Krylov–Schur method in terms of matrix-vector products.

Introduction

This work is concerned with computing a few smallest or largest eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors of a large symmetric matrix
A
.

Krylov subspace methods are among the most effective approaches for solving large-scale symmetric and nonsymmetric eigenvalue problems. Given an initial vector
q_{1}
, the
i
th Krylov subspace is generated by repeatedly multiplying a general square matrix
A
with
q_{1}
:

\mathcal{K}_{i}(A,q_{1}):=\mathsf{span}\{q_{1},Aq_{1},\dotsc,A^{i-1}q_{1}\}.

The Arnoldi process
[
15
, Sec. 10.5]
is used to construct an orthonormal basis
Q_{i}
for
\mathcal{K}_{i}(A,q_{1})
. In turn, the Rayleigh–Ritz process is used for extracting eigenvalue and eigenvector approximations from
Q_{i}
, referred to as Ritz values and Ritz vectors.
A significant challenge of Krylov subspace methods arises from the need for storing
Q_{i}
. In the case of slow convergence (with respect to
i
), the available memory may be exhausted long before achieving satisfactory approximation quality. Moreover, the cost of orthogonalizing
Q_{i}
typically grows quadratically with
i
.
Popular algorithms for solving large-scale eigenvalue problems, such as Sorensen’s Implicitly Restarted Arnoldi (IRA) method
[
28
]
,
address this challenge by combining the Arnoldi process with
restarting
.
In this work, we propose
compression
of Krylov subspaces as an alternative to restarting.
Explicit restarting uses the information gained from one cycle of the Arnoldi process to design an updated initial vector
p(A)q_{1}
for the next Arnoldi cycle, with a (low-degree) polynomial
p
that aims at filtering out the unwanted part of the spectrum. IRA is mathematically equivalent to explicit restarting, but it forms
p(A)q_{1}
implicitly and performs several Arnoldi steps, without requiring any extra matrix-vector products.
Since its release in the late 1990s, the ARPACK software
[
20
]
based on IRA has been one of the most widely used large-scale eigenvalue solvers. ARPACK is the basis of
SciPy’s and Julia’s
eigs
functions.
Often, the filter polynomial
p
is chosen such its roots coincide with the unwanted Ritz values obtained after one Arnoldi cycle, a choice sometimes referred to as “exact shifts”. In this case, IRA becomes mathematically equivalent to Stewart’s Krylov–Schur method
[
30
]
, which is simpler and numerically more reliable to implement than IRA, because it employs reordered Schur decompositions (instead of implicit QR steps) to perform restarts and deflations. The Krylov–Schur method is the basis of Matlab’s

eigs
function and it is implemented in SLEPc
[
16
]

as well as Trillinos’ Anasazi software
[
5
]
.

When
A
is symmetric, the Arnoldi process simplifies to the Lanczos process
[
15
, Sec. 10.5]
. Restarting also simplifies; the IRA and Krylov–Schur methods become the IRL
[
10
]
and thick-restarted Lanczos methods
[
31
]
, respectively. During the last two decades, there
have been numerous further developments of restarted Lanczos methods, including extensions to block Lanczos methods
[
34
,
4
]
, adaptive strategies for selecting the number of Ritz vectors retained after each
restart
[
32
]
, strategies for choosing polynomial filters to compute interior eigenvalues
[
21
,
3
]
, and communication-avoiding implementations for distributed-memory systems
[
33
]
. In all these developments, the fundamental principle of using polynomial filtering for restarting has remained the same.

From a theoretical perspective, the convergence analysis of (restarted) Arnoldi methods is by no means nontrivial. In the symmetric case, the convergence properties of the standard Lanczos method are relatively well understood; see, e.g.,
[
18
,
27
,
22
]
. The convergence of restarted Arnoldi methods has been linked to polynomial approximation problems in
[
7
]
, which allows to quantify convergence rates when using a fixed (well designed) filter polynomial
p
. In the case of exact shifts, the filter polynomial changes in every cycle and existing convergence results
[
2
,
28
]
are of a qualitative nature, establishing global convergence without providing specific convergence rates.

As an alternative to restarting, this work proposes a novel compression strategy to limit the memory requirements of the Lanczos method for a symmetric matrix
A
. To provide some intuition behind our approach, suppose that the spectrum of
A
is ordered such that

\lambda_{1}<\dotsb<\lambda_{k}<\tau<\lambda_{k+1}\leq\dotsb\leq\lambda_{n},

(1)

with a shift
\tau
separating the smallest
k\ll n
eigenvalues to be computed from the other eigenvalues.
We first introduce a more general Krylov-like decomposition in
section

2.2
, allowing us to compress the Lanczos process by an arbitrary orthonormal matrix.
Unlike restarting, compression sacrifices the Krylov structure but remains compatible with subsequent Lanczos steps.
Letting
U_{k}
be an orthonormal basis of the eigenspace associated with the smallest
k
eigenvalues, the best (but unrealistic) compression of an orthonormal basis
Q_{m}
for
\mathcal{K}_{m}(A,q_{1})
is given by
\mathsf{range}(Q_{m}Q_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}U_{k})
, as it preserves all information relevant for (the unknown)
U_{k}
.
Let
\chi_{\tau}(x)
denote the step function

\chi_{\tau}(x):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x<\tau,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

As explained in
section

2.3
, a rational approximation to
\chi_{\tau}
can be used to construct an orthonormal matrix that compresses
\mathcal{K}_{m}(A,q_{1})
into a lower-dimensional subspace, while preserving nearly all essential information.
This parallels the developments from
[
12
]
, which uses a rational approximation to a general function
f
to compress the Lanczos process for approximating a matrix function
f(A)b
. To make the compression of the Lanczos method for eigenvalue problems practical, several additional ingredients are needed. In particular, reorthogonalization is less of a concern for matrix functions but of utmost importance for eigenvalue problems to, e.g., avoid so-called ghost eigenvalues.
As we will see in
section

2.4
, full reorthogonalization alone is not sufficient to ensure stability in Lanczos with compression. To address this issue, we propose a new strategy called reorthogonalization with fill-in and establish a backward stability result, similar to the Krylov–Schur method in
[
30
]
.
In
section

3
, the convergence of our Lanczos with compression is analyzed. This is achieved by splitting the error between the Ritz values and the eigenvalues into two components: (1) the approximation error of standard Lanczos (without compression), and (2) the additional error incurred by compression. A back-of-the-envelope complexity analysis of the (re)orthogonalization cost for Lanczos with compression is also provided, and it reveals a clear advantage for problems that feature small relative eigenvalue gaps. In
section

4
, we report a series of numerical experiments involving matrices from a diversity of applications. They illustrate that our approach often outperforms the Krylov–Schur method, sometimes by a significant margin.

Notation.

Throughout the paper,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm of a vector or the spectral norm of a matrix. An
n\times m
matrix
Q
is called orthonormal if its columns form an orthonormal basis, that is,
Q^{\mathsf{T}}Q=I_{m}
with the
m\times m
identity matrix
I_{m}
.

Algorithm description

In this section, we will derive our newly proposed Lanczos method with compression. For this purpose,
we will assume that the eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
satisfy the (gapped) increased ordering (
1
) and we aim at computing the smallest
k\ll n
eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
as well as their associated eigenvectors. If the largest eigenvalues are of interest, one can simply replace
A
by
-A
.

2.1
Lanczos process and Krylov–Schur restarting

We start by recalling the basic Lanczos process.
Given an initial vector
q_{1}
with
\|q_{1}\|=1
,
\mathsf{Lan}\in\mathbb{N}
iterations of the Lanczos process generate an orthonormal basis
Q_{\mathsf{Lan}}
for the Krylov subspace
\mathcal{K}_{\mathsf{Lan}}(A,q_{1})
. More specifically, the Lanczos process returns the coefficients of the so-called
Lanczos decomposition
:

AQ_{\mathsf{Lan}}=Q_{\mathsf{Lan}}T_{\mathsf{Lan}}+\beta_{\mathsf{Lan}}q_{\mathsf{Lan}+1}e_{\mathsf{Lan}}^{\mathsf{T}},

(2)

where
\beta_{\mathsf{Lan}}\in\mathbb{R}
,
Q_{\mathsf{Lan}}=\begin{bmatrix}q_{1},\dotsc,q_{\mathsf{Lan}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\mathsf{Lan}}
,

T_{\mathsf{Lan}}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}&\beta_{1}&&\\
\beta_{1}&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\beta_{\mathsf{Lan}-1}\\
&&\beta_{\mathsf{Lan}-1}&\alpha_{\mathsf{Lan}}\end{bmatrix},\quad e_{\mathsf{Lan}}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{Lan}}.

(3)

Here and in the following, we will ignore the rare (fortunate) event that the Lanczos process breaks down within the first
\mathsf{Lan}
steps
[
15
, Sec. 10.1.3]
. The vector
q_{\mathsf{Lan}+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is orthogonal to
q_{1},\dotsc,q_{\mathsf{Lan}}
. In turn, it holds that
T_{\mathsf{Lan}}=Q_{\mathsf{Lan}}^{\mathsf{T}}AQ_{\mathsf{Lan}}
.
Thanks to the tridiagonal structure of
T_{\mathsf{Lan}}
, it can be easily seen that the basis vectors admit the recurrence

q_{i+1}=\frac{1}{\beta_{i}}\bigl(Aq_{i}-\alpha_{i}q_{i}-\beta_{i-1}q_{i-1}\bigr),\quad i=2,3,\ldots,\mathsf{Lan}.

(4)

In principle, the three-term recurrence (
4
) allows one to carry out the entire Lanczos process while storing only two additional vectors. However, in finite-precision arithmetic, roundoff error incurs a significant loss of orthogonality, and repeated reorthogonalization is needed to ensure numerical orthogonality of
Q_{\mathsf{Lan}}
. Reorthogonalization is commonly performed by orthogonalizing a freshly computed vector
q_{i+1}
to
all
previously computed vectors
q_{1},\ldots,q_{i}
. In turn, reorthogonalization requires the storage of the entire basis
Q_{\mathsf{Lan}}
and an additional
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{Lan}^{2}\cdot n)
operations.

As explained in the introduction, restarting is the standard approach to deal with the growing computational cost of the Lanczos process with reorthogonalization as
\mathsf{Lan}
increases. Krylov–Schur / thick restarting proceed by computing a spectral decomposition of
T_{\mathsf{Lan}}
and truncating it to the partial decomposition

T_{\mathsf{Lan}}V_{\mathsf{KS}}=V_{\mathsf{KS}}\Lambda_{\mathsf{KS}}.

Here, the diagonal matrix
\Lambda_{\mathsf{KS}}
contains the smallest
\mathsf{KS}
Ritz values, for some integer
\mathsf{KS}<\mathsf{Lan}
, and
V_{\mathsf{KS}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{Lan}\times\mathsf{KS}}
contains the corresponding (orthonormal) eigenvectors of
T_{\mathsf{Lan}}
.
Multiplication with
V_{\mathsf{KS}}
transforms the Lanczos decomposition (
2
) into a smaller, so-called
Krylov decomposition
:

AQ_{\mathsf{KS}}=Q_{\mathsf{KS}}\Lambda_{\mathsf{KS}}+q_{\mathsf{Lan}+1}b_{\mathsf{KS}}^{\mathsf{T}},

(5)

where
q_{\mathsf{Lan}+1}
is from the Lanczos decomposition
eq.

2
,

Q_{\mathsf{KS}}=Q_{\mathsf{Lan}}V_{\mathsf{KS}}
, and
b_{\mathsf{KS}}=\beta_{\mathsf{Lan}}V_{\mathsf{KS}}^{\mathsf{T}}e_{\mathsf{Lan}}
.
By suitably adjusting the Lanczos process, one expands the basis
Q_{\mathsf{KS}}
again to size
\mathsf{Lan}
. One notable difference is that this destroys the tridiagonal structure of
T_{\mathsf{Lan}}
because the corresponding matrix contains the (usually full) vector
b_{\mathsf{KS}}
in row and column
\mathsf{KS}+1
.

2.2
Compression mechanism

We will now describe a compression mechanism that is significantly more general than Krylov–Schur restarting. For this purpose,
we need to introduce the following, more general Krylov-like decomposition:

AQ_{m}=Q_{m}T_{m}+q_{i+1}b_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}+F_{m}\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{m+1}^{\mathsf{T}}F_{m}=0,

(6)

where
Q_{m+1}:=[Q_{m},q_{i+1}]
has orthonormal columns,
T_{m}
is symmetric (not necessarily tridiagonal) and
b_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a general vector (not necessarily a scalar multiple of
e_{m}
).
Like in the Krylov decomposition
eq.

5
,
q_{i+1}
is a vector from Lanczos process
eq.

4
with
i\geq m
.
In contrast to existing Krylov decompositions, like (
2
) and (
5
), we allow for the extra term
F_{m}
.
The condition
Q_{m+1}^{\mathsf{T}}F_{m}=0
ensures

Q_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}AQ_{m}=Q_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{m}T_{m}+Q_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}q_{i+1}b_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}+Q_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}F_{m}=T_{m}.

Hence, one can still extract Ritz values/vectors from the spectral decomposition of
T_{m}
.
The following theorem demonstrates a key advantage of (
6
): Compressing
Q_{m}
to
\ell<m
columns by multiplying with an
arbitrary
orthonormal matrix
V_{\ell}
preserves the structure of
eq.

6
.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the Krylov-like decomposition
eq.

6
for a symmetric matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
Given an orthonormal matrix
V_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\ell}
, with
\ell<m
,
set
\widetilde{Q}_{\ell}:=Q_{m}V_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
. Then

A\widetilde{Q}_{\ell}=\widetilde{Q}_{\ell}\widetilde{T}_{\ell}+q_{i+1}\widetilde{b}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}+\widetilde{F}_{\ell}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{Q}_{\ell+1}^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{F}_{\ell}=0,

where

\widetilde{Q}_{\ell+1}=[\widetilde{Q}_{\ell},q_{i+1}],\quad\widetilde{T}_{\ell}=V_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}T_{m}V_{\ell},\quad\widetilde{b}_{\ell}=V_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}b_{m},\quad\widetilde{F}_{\ell}=Q_{m}(I-V_{\ell}V_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}})T_{m}V_{\ell}+F_{m}V_{\ell}.

Proof.

By multiplying the decomposition
eq.

6

V_{\ell}
, we obtain

\displaystyle A\widetilde{Q}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=AQ_{m}V_{\ell}=Q_{m}T_{m}V_{\ell}+q_{i+1}b_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}V_{\ell}+F_{m}V_{\ell}

\displaystyle=Q_{m}V_{\ell}(V_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}T_{m}V_{\ell})+q_{i+1}(V_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}b_{m})^{\mathsf{T}}+Q_{m}(I-V_{\ell}V_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}})T_{m}V_{\ell}+F_{m}V_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{Q}_{\ell}\widetilde{T}_{\ell}+q_{i+1}\widetilde{b}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}+\widetilde{F}_{\ell},

where

\widetilde{Q}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{F}_{\ell}=V_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(Q_{m}(I-V_{\ell}V_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}})T_{m}V_{\ell}+F_{m}V_{\ell}\bigr)=0\quad\text{and}\quad q_{i+1}^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{F}_{\ell}=0.

Note that
theorem

1
contains Krylov–Schur restarting as a special case; if
F_{m}=0
and
V_{\ell}
consists of eigenvectors of

\widetilde{F}_{\ell}=0

Given a Krylov-like decomposition
eq.

q_{i+2}:=\beta_{i+1}^{-1}(I-Q_{m+1}Q_{m+1}^{\mathsf{T}})Aq_{i+1},

(7)

where
\beta_{i+1}
is a normalization factor such that
\lVert q_{i+2}\rVert=1
. The same procedure can be applied to continue the
compressed decomposition described in

1

\widetilde{q}_{\ell+1}=q_{i+1}
, then compute
A\widetilde{q}_{\ell+1}
and orthogonalize it with respect to
\widetilde{Q}_{\ell+1}

\widetilde{q}_{\ell+2}:=\widetilde{\beta}_{\ell+1}^{-1}(I-\widetilde{Q}_{\ell+1}\widetilde{Q}_{\ell+1}^{\mathsf{T}})A\widetilde{q}_{\ell+1},

(8)

where
\widetilde{\beta}_{\ell+1}
ensures
\lVert\widetilde{q}_{\ell+2}\rVert=1
. With
\widetilde{q}_{\ell+1}
and
\widetilde{q}_{\ell+2}
being defined, the Lanczos process now proceeds in the usual manner; via the three-term recurrence
eq.

4
.
From a theoretical and practical point of view it is important that the compression does
not
affect the future vectors generated by the Lanczos process, which is ensured when
\widetilde{q}_{\ell+2}=q_{i+2}
. The following proposition shows that this is indeed the case under the additional, relatively mild condition
b_{m}\in\mathsf{range}(V_{\ell})
. Interestingly, standard Krylov–Schur restarting generally does not satisfy this condition and does not preserve the subsequent Lanczos process.

Proposition 2
.

Given the Krylov-like decomposition
eq.

6
, let
q_{i+2}
and
\widetilde{q}_{\ell+2}
denote the next Lanczos vectors obtained without and with compression using
V_{\ell}
, as defined in

7

V_{\ell}V_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}b_{m}=b_{m}
. Then, up to a sign change,
\widetilde{\beta}_{\ell+1}=\beta_{i+1}
and
\widetilde{q}_{\ell+2}=q_{i+2}
.

Proof.

Multiplying
q_{i+1}^{\mathsf{T}}
from the left to
eq.

6
gives

b_{m}=Q_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}Aq_{i+1}
. Because of
V_{\ell}V_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}b_{m}=b_{m}
, we have

Q_{m}Q_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}Aq_{i+1}=Q_{m}b_{m}=Q_{m}V_{\ell}V_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}b_{m}=Q_{m}V_{\ell}V_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}Aq_{i+1}=\widetilde{Q}_{\ell}\widetilde{Q}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}Aq_{i+1}.

In turn,

\beta_{i+1}q_{i+2}=(I-Q_{m}Q_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}-q_{i+1}q_{i+1}^{\mathsf{T}})Aq_{i+1}=(I-\widetilde{Q}_{\ell}\widetilde{Q}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}-q_{i+1}q_{i+1}^{\mathsf{T}})Aq_{i+1}=\widetilde{\beta}_{\ell+1}\widetilde{q}_{\ell+2},

which implies the desired result.

Remark 3
.

Given a Krylov-like decomposition

6

is a Krylov subspace for

.
In our algorithm, compression yields a matrix

that generally increases the rank and, hence, it destroys the Krylov structure of the subspace. Thus, compression fundamentally differs from (polynomial) restarting, which preserves the Krylov structure but alters the Lanczos sequence.

2.3
Construction of compression matrix
V_{\ell}

We now turn to the choice of the orthonormal matrix that defines the compression mechanism described above. We begin by considering an idealized scenario in which compression preserves all relevant information. Building on techniques similar to those in
[
12
]
, we then demonstrate that a practical rational Krylov compression can retain most of the essential information.
For simplicity, we only discuss the first compression step
V_{\ell}\equiv V^{(0)}_{\ell}
; all subsequent compression steps are performed in an entirely analogous manner.

Idealized compression

Ideally
, compression preserves
the components of the
k\leq\ell
(as yet unknown) eigenvectors of interest for
A
in the currently computed basis. To specify such an idealized choice,
let
Q_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the orthogonal matrix obtained after performing
all

n
steps of the (uncompressed) Lanczos process applied to
A
, and define
T_{n}=Q_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}AQ_{n}
.
Then
T_{n}
has the same eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
as
A
. Letting
w_{1},\ldots w_{n}
denote an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors such that
T_{n}w_{i}=\lambda_{i}w_{i}
, we set

W_{k}=[w_{1},\dotsc,w_{k}].

Note that
\mathsf{range}(Q_{n}W_{k})
is precisely the subspace containing the
k
desired eigenvectors of
A
. We now consider
k<m\leq n
, and
recall that the first
m
steps of the Lanczos process produce a basis
Q_{m}
that constitutes the first
m
columns of
Q_{n}
. This allows us to express

Q_{n}W_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}Q_{m}&\star\ \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\overline{W}_{k}\\
\star\end{bmatrix},

where
\overline{W}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
contains the first
m
rows
W_{k}
.
In particular, the compression
Q_{m}\overline{W}_{k}
of
Q_{m}
perfectly preserves all information on the eigenvectors of interest.
This, of course, represents an unrealistic choice because
\overline{W}_{k}
is unknown after the first
m
steps.

Injection of rational function

We aim at using rational approximation to produce a slightly larger compression matrix
V_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\ell}
, such that
\mathsf{range}(V_{\ell})
contains the columns of the idealized choice
\overline{W}_{k}
approximately. For this purpose, we will inject a real rational function
r=p/q
with polynomials
p,q
. The (extended) list of poles of
r
is denoted by

\Xi:=\big[\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{\lvert\Xi\rvert}\big],\quad\xi_{i}\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\},

where
\Xi
contains the zeros of

2.4
Reorthogonalization with fill-in

Roundoff error severely affects the Lanczos process and, in turn, the method presented in this work. In particular, the vectors
produced by the three-term recurrence
eq.

4
quickly lose numerical orthogonality. In the standard Lanczos method
[
25
, p. 303]
, this is fixed by

immediately reorthogonalizing
each newly produced vector
q_{i+1}
against
all
previously generated vectors
q_{1},\ldots,q_{i}
.
In principle, reorthogonalization destroys the three-term recurrence, since it involves more than only the last two vectors.
However, its immediate application ensures that the additional orthogonalization
coefficients produced by reorthogonalization remain on the level of roundoff error
[
29
, p. 350]
.
This allows one to safely disregard these coefficients or, optionally, absorb them into the three-term recurrence. In turn, both the tridiagonal structure of
T_{\mathsf{Lan}}
and the numerical orthogonality of
Q_{\mathsf{Lan}}
in the Lanczos decomposition
eq.

2
are preserved.

In Lanczos with compression, we need to ensure that not only the orthogonality of the basis
Q_{m}
but also the relation
Q_{m+1}^{\mathsf{T}}F_{m}=0
in the Krylov-like decomposition (
6
) remain valid up to the level of roundoff error. While (repeated) reorthogonalization can be used for this purpose, there are two complications. Because reorthogonalization only has access to a compression of the Lanczos basis, one cannot expect that the three-term recurrence will be preserved (up to the level of roundoff error). Additionally, explicitly reorthogonalizing against
F_{m}
would require us to store this matrix, which would roughly double the storage cost.
In the following, we develop a reorthogonalization strategy that takes both issues into account. By allowing for fill-in in the
tridiagonal structure, we take care of the condition
Q_{m+1}^{\mathsf{T}}F_{m}=0
without the need for storing
F_{m}
.

Reorthogonalization of Lanczos expansion

In the following, we consider a single step of the Lanczos process that computes a new Lanczos vector
q_{i+2}
. Letting
c
denote the round of the procedure
eq.

9
during which this vector is produced and assuming
c\geq 1
, we can write

i=c(m-\ell)+j
with
\ell\leq j<m

(22)

To increase readability, we have dropped the superscript

for denoting the

We assume that

\widehat{T}_{j}

A\widehat{Q}_{j+1}=\widehat{Q}_{j+1}\widehat{T}_{j+1}+\widehat{q}_{i+2}\widehat{b}_{j+1}^{\mathsf{T}}+\widehat{F}_{j+1}.

(28)

If we can show that
\widehat{Q}_{j+2}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{F}_{j+1}
is small in norm then (
28
) satisfies all the properties of a stable Krylov-like decomposition. To show this, we partition
\Delta=[\Delta_{j},\delta_{j+1}]
, such that
\delta_{j+1}
is a vector.
Using
\mathsf{fl}(\widehat{Q}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}A\widehat{q}_{i+1})=\widehat{b}_{j}+\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u})
, one computes

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}

\displaystyle=A\widehat{Q}_{j}-\widehat{Q}_{j}\widehat{T}_{j}-\widehat{q}_{i+1}\widehat{b}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}-\widehat{q}_{i+2}\widehat{q}_{i+2}^{\mathsf{T}}A\widehat{Q}_{j}-(I-\widehat{q}_{i+2}\widehat{q}_{i+2}^{\mathsf{T}})\widehat{F}_{j}+\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=-\widehat{q}_{i+2}\widehat{q}_{i+2}^{\mathsf{T}}\big(A\widehat{Q}_{j}-\widehat{F}_{j}\big)+\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u})=-\widehat{q}_{i+2}\widehat{q}_{i+2}^{\mathsf{T}}\big(\widehat{Q}_{j}\widehat{T}_{j}+\widehat{q}_{i+1}\widehat{b}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\big)+\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u}),

(29)

where (
29
) uses the stable Krylov-like decomposition
eq.

22
twice.
Because of the numerical orthogonality
eq.

25
of
\widehat{Q}_{j+2}
, this establishes
\Delta_{j}=\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u})
.
For
\delta_{j+1}
, one computes

\displaystyle\delta_{j+1}

\displaystyle=A\widehat{q}_{i+1}-\widehat{Q}_{j}\mathsf{fl}(\widehat{Q}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}A\widehat{q}_{i+1})-\widehat{q}_{i+1}\mathsf{fl}(\widehat{q}_{i+1}^{\mathsf{T}}A\widehat{q}_{i+1})-\widehat{q}_{i+2}\widehat{q}_{i+2}^{\mathsf{T}}A\widehat{q}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=(I-\widehat{Q}_{j+2}\widehat{Q}_{j+2}^{\mathsf{T}})A\widehat{q}_{i+1}+\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u})=\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u}),

where the last equality uses
eqs.

24
and
25
.
Thus, we have shown that
\Delta=\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u})
and, in turn, the definition
eq.

27
together with
eq.

23
yield

\widehat{Q}_{j+2}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{F}_{j+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{Q}_{j+1}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{F}_{j}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u})=\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u}).

In summary, we have shown that
eq.

28
is indeed a stable Krylov-like decomposition. Thus, Lanczos expansion steps together with our reorthogonalization strategy maintain stable Krylov-like decompositions at the expense of admitting additional fill-in in the matrix
\widehat{T}_{j+1}
. This also applies to the classical Lanczos method carried out during the first round of the procedure (
9
), provided that the usual full reorthorgonalization is used.

Compression step

In order to conclude that the entire procedure
eq.

9
results in stable Krylov-like decompositions, it remains to show that they are preserved by compression steps as well. For this purpose, consider a stable Krylov-like decomposition
before

compression is carried out:

A\widehat{Q}_{m}=\widehat{Q}_{m}\widehat{T}_{m}+\widehat{q}_{i+1}\widehat{b}_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}+\widehat{F}_{m},

that is, the coefficients satisfy the properties stated above for
eq.

22
, with
j
replaced by
m
.
Let
\widehat{V}_{\ell}
denote the compression matrix computed by
algorithm

1
. We assume that the block Gram–Schmidt process with reorthogonalization
[
6
]
is used, ensuring that
\widehat{V}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{V}_{\ell}-I=\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{u})
.
Following
theorem

1
, the compression step yields, when carried out in floating point arithmetic, a decomposition of the form

A\widehat{\widetilde{Q}}_{\ell}=\widehat{\widetilde{Q}}_{\ell}\widehat{\widetilde{T}}_{\ell}+\widehat{q}_{i+1}\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}+\widehat{\widetilde{F}}_{\ell},

(30)

where
\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{\ell}=\widehat{V}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{b}_{m}
,

\widehat{\widetilde{Q}}_{\ell}=\mathsf{fl}(\widehat{Q}_{m}\widehat{V}_{\ell}),\quad\widehat{\widetilde{T}}_{\ell}=\mathsf{fl}(\widehat{V}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{T}_{m}\widehat{V}_{\ell}),\quad\widehat{\widetilde{F}}_{\ell}=\widehat{Q}_{m}(I-\widehat{V}_{\ell}\widehat{V}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}})\widehat{T}_{m}\widehat{V}_{\ell}+\widehat{F}_{m}\widehat{V}_{\ell}+\widetilde{\Delta}

From a basic roundoff error analysis, it follows that

Backward stability

It is well known that full reorthogonalization ensures the backward stability of the classical Lanczos method, the Arnoldi method, and
the Krylov–Schur method; see, e.g.,
[
30
, Thm. 4.1]
and
[
29
, Chap. 5, Thm.2.5]
.
As shown above, our reorthogonalization strategy ensures that
Lanczos with compression returns a stable Krylov-like decomposition
eq.

22
. The following theorem shows that this implies backward stability, that is, Lanczos with compression returns an exact Krylov-like decomposition for slightly perturbed matrices.

Theorem 7
.

Given a stable Krylov-like decomposition
eq.

22
, there is a matrix
[Q_{j},q_{i+1}]
with orthonormal columns
and a matrix
E
such that

\big[\widehat{Q}_{j},\widehat{q}_{i+1}\big]-[Q_{j},q_{i+1}]=\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{u}),\quad E=\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u}),

and

(A+E){Q}_{j}={Q}_{j}\widehat{T}_{j}+{q}_{i+1}\widehat{b}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}+{F}_{j},

(31)

for some
{F}_{j}
with
[Q_{j},q_{i+1}]^{\mathsf{T}}F_{j}=0
and
\widehat{F}_{j}-F_{j}=\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u})
.

Proof.

Letting
\widehat{Q}_{j+1}=[\widehat{Q}_{j},\widehat{q}_{i+1}]
, we define

Q_{j+1}:=[Q_{j},q_{j+1}]:=\widehat{Q}_{j+1}(\widehat{Q}_{j+1}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{Q}_{j+1})^{-1/2}.

This matrix clearly has orthonormal columns and satisfies

Q_{j+1}-\widehat{Q}_{j+1}=\widehat{Q}_{j+1}\bigl((\widehat{Q}_{j+1}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{Q}_{j+1})^{-1/2}-I\bigr)=\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{u}),

using the numerical orthogonality (
23
) of
\widehat{Q}_{j+1}
. Setting

F_{j}:=(I-Q_{j+1}Q_{j+1}^{\mathsf{T}})\widehat{F}_{j}

ensures
Q_{j+1}^{\mathsf{T}}F_{j}=0
and

\widehat{F}_{j}-F_{j}=Q_{j+1}Q_{j+1}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{F}_{j}=Q_{j+1}(\widehat{Q}_{j+1}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{Q}_{j+1})^{-1/2}\widehat{Q}_{j+1}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{F}_{j}=\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u}),

using
\widehat{Q}_{j+1}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{F}_{j}=\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u})
from (
23
). Finally, setting

E:=(Q_{j}\widehat{T}_{j}+q_{i+1}\widehat{b}_{j}+F_{j}-AQ_{j})Q_{j}^{\mathsf{T}},

ensures that
eq.

31
is valid and

E=\bigl((Q_{j}-\widehat{Q}_{j})\widehat{T}_{j}+(q_{i+1}-\widehat{q}_{i+1})\widehat{b}_{j}+(F_{j}-\widehat{F}_{j})-A(Q_{j}-\widehat{Q}_{j})\bigr)Q_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u}).

Here, we used
\widehat{T}_{j}=\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert)
, which is implied by multiplying the stable Krylov-like decomposition
eq.

22
from the left with
\widehat{Q}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}
.

theorem

7
has the same implications as existing backward stability results for the Krylov–Schur method
[
29
, Chap. 5, Thm.2.5]
. In particular, it implies

\widehat{T}_{j}-Q_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}AQ_{j}=Q_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}EQ_{j}=\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u}),

showing that the computed Ritz values (computed with a backward stable eigensolver applied to
\widehat{T}_{j}
)
are Ritz values of a slightly perturbed matrix
A
.

Assuming
, for simplicity, that
\mathsf{range}(Q_{j})
contains an exact eigenvector of
A
, the Rayleigh–Ritz procedure can extract an eigenvalue/eigenvector approximation up to an error of
\mathcal{O}(\lVert A\rVert\mathbf{u})
.

2.5.1
Checking convergence

Line 8 of
algorithm

2
checks the convergence of the computed Ritz values/vectors and exits the loop once
k

In the following, we develop a suitable convergence criterion, based on an existing approach for Lanczos.

Checking convergence in standard Lanczos

In the standard Lanczos method, it is common practice to check the residual for declaring convergence. Letting
W_{\mathsf{Lan},k}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{Lan}\times k}
contain orthonormal eigenvectors belonging to the smallest
k
eigenvalues of
T_{\mathsf{Lan}}
, the matrix
U_{\mathsf{Lan}}=Q_{\mathsf{Lan}}W_{\mathsf{Lan},k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
contains the corresponding Ritz vectors and the diagonal matrix

U_{\mathsf{Lan}}^{\mathsf{T}}AU_{\mathsf{Lan}}
contains the corresponding Ritz values.
Thanks to the Lanczos decomposition
eq.

2
, the residual norm for these Ritz value/vector pairs satisfies

\lVert AU_{\mathsf{Lan}}-U_{\mathsf{Lan}}U_{\mathsf{Lan}}^{\mathsf{T}}AU_{\mathsf{Lan}}\rVert=\lVert AQ_{\mathsf{Lan}}W_{\mathsf{Lan},k}-Q_{\mathsf{Lan}}T_{\mathsf{Lan}}W_{\mathsf{Lan},k}\rVert=\lvert\beta_{\mathsf{Lan}}\rvert\lVert e_{\mathsf{Lan}}^{\mathsf{T}}W_{\mathsf{Lan},k}\rVert.

(32)

Thus, convergence can be checked inexpensively, by inspecting
\lvert\beta_{\mathsf{Lan}}\rvert\lVert e_{\mathsf{Lan}}^{\mathsf{T}}W_{\mathsf{Lan},k}\rVert
.

Checking convergence in Lanczos with compression (line 8 of
algorithm

2
)

In principle, the expression
eq.

32
easily extends
to Lanczos with compression by replacing the Lanczos decomposition with the Krylov-like decomposition
eq.

6
. However, this would require us to explicitly store the matrix
F_{m}
, essentially doubling storage cost.
To avoid this, we use the coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}
, collected
in line 6 of
algorithm

2
, to form the tridiagonal matrix
T_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{i\times i}
that matches the matrix
T_{\mathsf{Lan}}
from
eq.

3
generated by the underlying Lanczos process. We then compute the eigenvectors
W_{i,k}
corresponding to the smallest
k
eigenvalues of
T_{i}
. In accordance with
eq.

32
, we approximate the residual norm for Lanczos with compression by the quantity
\lvert\beta_{i}\rvert\lVert e_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}W_{i,k}\rVert
. In particular, one exits the loop of
algorithm

2
once
\lvert\beta_{i}\rvert\lVert e_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}W_{i,k}\rVert\leq\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{res}}
holds.

Our numerical experiments consistently show (see
fig.

4
below) that the described residual approximation strategy is reliable and effective.
In fact, the results in
section

3
below show that, in terms of Ritz values, standard Lanczos and Lanczos with compression have a similar convergence behavior (in exact arithmetic) up to the level of
\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{ra}}^{2}
. This suggests that the convergence of these two methods occurs nearly simultaneously, particularly the residual norm for standard Lanczos method should be small when Lanczos with compression converges.

Choice of
\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{ra}}

The eigenvalue approximation error of a Ritz value satisfies a bound
[
29
, Chap. 4, Thm. 2.17]
that is proportional to the squared residual norm, which is approximately bounded by
\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{res}}^{2}
.
On the other hand,
theorem

10
below shows that
the difference between the Ritz values of the Lanczos method and Lanczos with compression is bounded, up to a constant, by
\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{ra}}^{2}
.
Thus, it is reasonable to choose
\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{ra}}\approx\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{res}}
.
In practice, we recommend choosing
\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{ra}}
slightly smaller than
\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{res}}
. For example, in our numerical experiments in
section

4

\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{ra}}=10^{-6}
for computing one or four eigenvalues of discrete Laplacian operators, and
\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{ra}}=10^{-7}
for computing several (up to hundreds of) eigenvalues for matrices from density function theory. Because
\ell
depends at most logarithmically on
\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{ra}}^{-1}
, this does not significantly increase computational cost and helps avoiding potential imbalances due to the involved constants.

3
Convergence and complexity analysis

This section studies the impact of compression on the convergence of the Ritz values returned by
algorithm

2
, as well as its consequences for the complexity.

Suppose that
algorithm

2
performs
\mathsf{Lan}
matrix-vector multiplications in total.
Assuming that
U
contains the orthonormal basis of Ritz vectors returned by
algorithm

2
, the quantity
\mathsf{Tr}(U^{\mathsf{T}}AU)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}
measures the approximation error of the corresponding Ritz values. Recalling the Lanczos decomposition defined in
eq.

2
, this error can be decomposed as

\mathsf{Tr}(U^{\mathsf{T}}AU)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=\Bigl(\mathsf{Tr}(U^{\mathsf{T}}AU)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(T_{\mathsf{Lan}})\Bigr)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bigl(\lambda_{i}(T_{\mathsf{Lan}})-\lambda_{i}\bigr),

(33)

where
\lambda_{i}(T_{\mathsf{Lan}})
denotes the
i
th smallest eigenvalue of
T_{\mathsf{Lan}}
.
Because of
\mathsf{range}(U)\subset\mathcal{K}_{\mathsf{Lan}}(A,q_{1})
, the Cauchy interlacing theorem implies that both terms in the right-hand side are non-negative.
The second term captures the convergence of the standard Lanczos method, which is well understood for the case
k=1
; see, e.g.,
[
27
,
25
]
. The analysis for the case
k>1

is more involved and assumes that all the first
k+1
eigenvalues of
A
are simple
[
19
]
.

It remains to analyze the first term in
eq.

33
, which corresponds to the loss from compression.
The following analysis of this term disregards the effect of roundoff error.
In related work
[
11
]
that proposed and analyzed a compressed Lanczos method for solving Lyapunov matrix equations, Paige’s analysis
[
24
]
was used to incorporate the effects of roundoff error. However, it is unclear how to perform an analogous analysis for eigenvalue computation.

3.1
Loss from compression

Suppose that we perform
N_{\mathsf{comp}}
compressions in total and let
V^{(c)}_{\ell}
denote the orthonormal matrix used in the
c
th compression, and
P_{c}=\mathrm{diag}(V^{(c)}_{\ell},I)
for
c=0,\dotsc,N_{\mathsf{comp}}-1
. To quantify the effect of compressions on the Ritz values, we recursively define
T^{(c)}
as

T^{(0)}=T_{\mathsf{Lan}}\quad\text{and}\quad T^{(c+1)}=P_{c}^{\mathsf{T}}T^{(c)}P_{c}\quad\text{for }c=0,\dotsc,N_{\mathsf{comp}}-1,

(34)

and let
W_{k}^{(c)}
contain the orthonormal eigenvectors corresponding to the smallest
k
eigenvalues of
T^{(c)}
. Then
theorem

6
shows that the impact of each compression step on
W_{k}^{(c)}
is bounded by

\lVert W_{k}^{(c)}-P_{c}P_{c}^{\mathsf{T}}W_{k}^{(c)}\rVert\leq 2\cdot\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{ra}}.

(35)

The following auxiliary lemma will allow us to take into account that
P_{c}P_{c}^{\mathsf{T}}W_{k}^{(c)}
is
not
orthonormal.

Lemma 9
.

Let
T\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a symmetric matrix with eigenvalues
\mu_{1}\leq\dotsb\leq\mu_{n}
, and let
W_{k}=[w_{1},\dotsc,w_{k}]
contain orthonormal eigenvectors associated with
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}
. For any matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
such that
\lVert X-W_{k}\rVert\leq\epsilon<1
, we have that

\mathsf{Rq}_{T}(X)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}\leq k(\mu_{n}-\mu_{1})\epsilon^{2},

where
\mathsf{Rq}_{T}(X)
is the (scalar) Rayleigh quotient
[
1
, Eq. (4.2)]
defined as

\mathsf{Rq}_{T}(X)=\mathsf{Tr}\bigl((X^{\mathsf{T}}X)^{-1}(X^{\mathsf{T}}TX)\bigr).

Proof.

Let
W_{\perp}
be an orthonormal basis of the orthogonal complement of the range of
W_{k}
. Then we know that

\Bigl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}X-I\\
W_{\perp}^{\mathsf{T}}X\end{bmatrix}\Bigr\rVert=\Bigl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}W_{k}&W_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}X-I\\
W_{\perp}^{\mathsf{T}}X\end{bmatrix}\Bigr\rVert=\lVert X-W_{k}\rVert\leq\epsilon,

(36)

implying that
W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}X
is nonsingular.
Setting
E=W_{\perp}W_{\perp}^{\mathsf{T}}X(W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}X)^{-1}
, the matrix
X
admits the decomposition

X=W_{k}W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}X+W_{\perp}W_{\perp}^{\mathsf{T}}X=(W_{k}+E)W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}X.

Note that
W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}E=W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}TE=0
.
Together with the homogeneity of Rayleigh quotient, see
[
1
, Prop. 4.1]
, this implies

\mathsf{Rq}_{T}(X)=\mathsf{Rq}_{T}(W_{k}+E)=\mathsf{Tr}\bigl((I+E^{\mathsf{T}}E)^{-1}(D_{\mu}+E^{\mathsf{T}}TE)\bigr),

where
D_{\mu}=\mathrm{diag}(\mu_{1},\dotsc,\mu_{k})
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathsf{Rq}_{T}(X)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Tr}\bigl((I+E^{\mathsf{T}}E)^{-1}(D_{\mu}+E^{\mathsf{T}}TE)-D_{\mu}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Tr}\bigl((I+E^{\mathsf{T}}E)^{-1}(D_{\mu}+E^{\mathsf{T}}TE-D_{\mu}-E^{\mathsf{T}}ED_{\mu})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Tr}\Bigl((I+E^{\mathsf{T}}E)^{-1}\bigl(E^{\mathsf{T}}(T-\mu_{1}I)E-E^{\mathsf{T}}E(D_{\mu}-\mu_{1}I)\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{Tr}\Bigl((I+E^{\mathsf{T}}E)^{-1}\bigl(E^{\mathsf{T}}(T-\mu_{1}I)E\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq k\lVert T-\mu_{1}I\rVert\lVert E(I+E^{\mathsf{T}}E)^{-1/2}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=k(\mu_{n}-\mu_{1})\lVert(I+E^{\mathsf{T}}E)^{-1}E^{\mathsf{T}}E\rVert,

where the first inequality uses the fact that the trace of
E^{\mathsf{T}}E(D_{\mu}-\mu_{1}I)
, a product of two symmetric positive semi-definite matrices, is non-negative.
By
eq.

36
, we have that
\lVert(W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}X-I)y\rVert^{2}+\lVert W_{\perp}^{\mathsf{T}}Xy\rVert^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}\lVert y\rVert^{2}
holds for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Thus,

\lVert E\rVert^{2}=\lVert W_{\perp}^{\mathsf{T}}X(W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}X)^{-1}\rVert^{2}=\sup_{y\neq 0}\frac{\lVert W_{\perp}^{\mathsf{T}}Xy\rVert^{2}}{\lVert W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Xy\rVert^{2}}\leq\sup_{y\neq 0}\frac{\epsilon^{2}\lVert y\rVert^{2}-\lVert(W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}X-I)y\rVert^{2}}{\lVert W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Xy\rVert^{2}}.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\epsilon^{2}\lVert y\rVert^{2}-\lVert(W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}X-I)y\rVert^{2}=(\epsilon^{2}-1)\lVert y\rVert^{2}-\lVert W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Xy\rVert^{2}+2y^{\mathsf{T}}W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Xy\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{1-\epsilon^{2}}\lVert W_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Xy\rVert^{2},

implying that
\lVert E\rVert^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}/(1-\epsilon^{2})
.
The lemma is proved by

\lVert(I+E^{\mathsf{T}}E)^{-1}E^{\mathsf{T}}E\rVert=\lVert I-(I+E^{\mathsf{T}}E)^{-1}\rVert\leq 1-\frac{1}{\lVert E\rVert^{2}+1}\leq\epsilon^{2}.

Using
lemma

9
, the following result establishes that the error on the Ritz values caused by compression on the Ritz values is controlled by the squared tolerance for rational approximation, up to some constant.

Theorem 10
.

Given a symmetric matrix
A
with eigenvalues in
the (gapped) increased ordering (
1
), and an initial vector
q_{1}
with
\lVert q_{1}\rVert=1
, let
T_{\mathsf{Lan}}
be the tridiagonal matrix in the Lanczos decomposition
eq.

2
.
Let
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
contain the Ritz vectors return by Lanczos with compression (with the same initial vector
q_{1}
) after
N_{\mathsf{comp}}
compression and
\mathsf{Lan}
matrix-vector multiplications. Then

\mathsf{Tr}(U^{\mathsf{T}}AU)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(T_{\mathsf{Lan}})\leq 4kN_{\mathsf{comp}}(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{1})\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{ra}}^{2},

where
0<\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{ra}}<1/2
is a uniform upper bound for the tolerance of rational approximation in
eq.

21
.

Proof.

Recall the definition of
P_{c}^{\mathsf{T}}W_{k}^{(c)}
in
eq.

34
, the condition
0<\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{ra}}<1/2
and
eq.

35
yield that
P_{c}^{\mathsf{T}}W_{k}^{(c)}
is (column) full rank.
By the Courant–Fischer minimax theorem
[
15
, Thm. 8.1.2]
,

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(T^{(c+1)})\leq\mathsf{Rq}_{T^{(c+1)}}(P_{c}^{\mathsf{T}}W_{k}^{(c)})=\mathsf{Rq}_{T^{(c)}}(P_{c}P_{c}^{\mathsf{T}}W_{k}^{(c)}).

Using
eq.

35
again and applying
lemma

9
to
T=T^{(c)}
,
W_{k}=W_{k}^{(c)}
and
X=P_{c}P_{c}^{\mathsf{T}}W_{k}^{(c)}
yields that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bigl(\lambda_{i}(T^{(c+1)})-\lambda_{i}(T^{(c)})\bigr)\leq\mathsf{Rq}_{T^{(c)}}(P_{c}P_{c}^{\mathsf{T}}W_{k}^{(c)})-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(T^{(c)})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4k\bigl(\lambda_{\max}(T^{(c)})-\lambda_{\min}(T^{(c)})\bigr)\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{ra}}^{2}.

Using Cauchy interlacing, we immediately obtain the loss of Ritz value after one compression as

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bigl(\lambda_{i}(T^{(c+1)})-\lambda_{i}(T^{(c)})\bigr)\leq 4k\bigl(\lambda_{\max}(T^{(c)})-\lambda_{\min}(T^{(c)})\bigr)\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{ra}}^{2}\leq 4k(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{1})\mathsf{tol}_{\mathrm{ra}}^{2}.

Applying this inequality recursively and noting that

\mathsf{Tr}(U^{\mathsf{T}}AU)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(T^{(N_{\mathsf{comp}})})\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{i}(T_{\mathsf{Lan}})=\lambda_{i}(T^{(0)}),

we finish the proof.

3.2
Complexity analysis of orthogonalization

In this part, we present a back-of-the-envelope complexity analysis for the cost of (re)orthogonalization. Again, we ignore the effects of roundoff error. While roundoff may delay convergence, our numerical experiments indicate that this delay is often not significant.

For the Lanczos method without restarting or compression, we expect that up to
\mathsf{Iter}_{\mathsf{Lan}}\sim k/\sqrt{\mathsf{relgap}_{k}}
iterations are required to attain a fixed tolerance in the first
k
eigenvalues. As mentioned above, this is a standard result for
k=1

[
27
,
25
]
, while the corresponding result for
k>1
requires additional assumptions
[
19
]
.
When full reorthogonalization is applied, the total number of operations required for orthogonalization scales as

\mathsf{Ortho}_{\mathsf{Lan}}\sim n\cdot\mathsf{Iter}^{2}_{\mathsf{Lan}}\sim nk^{2}/\mathsf{relgap}_{k}.

In particular, a small relative gap may imply slow convergence and, consequently, higher orthogonalization costs.

According to the convergence result of
theorem

10
, Lanczos with compression requires essentially the same number of iterations as standard Lanczos, that is,
\mathsf{Iter}_{\mathsf{Lan}}\sim k/\sqrt{\mathsf{relgap}_{k}}
, at least up to the level of the compression error.
However, in every iteration, we only need to orthogonalize with respect to the compressed basis, containing at most
m=\widehat{k}+\ell
vectors. As discussed in
section

2.3
, the degree of rational approximation depends on the gap logarithmically, implying that
m\sim k+\log(1/\mathsf{relgap}_{k})
.
Hence, the cost of orthogonalization reduces to

\mathsf{Ortho}_{\mathsf{LC}}\sim n(m\cdot\mathsf{Iter}_{\mathsf{Lan}})\sim nk\bigl(k+\log(1/\mathsf{relgap}_{k})\bigr)/\sqrt{\mathsf{relgap}_{k}}\sim nk^{2}/\sqrt{\mathsf{relgap}_{k}}.

For small relative gaps,
0<\mathsf{relgap}_{k}\ll 1
, we therefore expect a reduction of orthogonalization costs by up to a factor of roughly
1/\sqrt{\mathsf{relgap}_{k}}
.

4
Numerical results

In this section, we compare
algorithm

2
, Lanczos with compression (LC), with the Krylov–Schur method (KS). While Matlab’s
eigs
(as of version R2017b) is based on KS, we found that the choice of the restart length in
eigs
can sometimes be suboptimal in terms of matrix-vector products (see discussion in
section

4.1.1
below) and we therefore used our own implementation of KS.
Both implementations, KS and LC, employ Gaussian random initial vectors and full reorthogonalization. The eigenvalues computed by Matlab’s
eigs
(with default tolerance
10^{-14}
) are used as a reference when computing eigenvalue approximation errors.
The computational cost is measured in terms of the number of matrix-vector multiplications (referred to as matvecs). In turn, the improvement of LC over KS is quantified by the ratio

\text{Improvement}:=1-\frac{\text{$\#$matvecs required by LC}}{\text{$\#$matvecs required by KS}}.

(37)

Since the impact of deflation on the number of matvecs is negligible, we perform both methods without deflation in all experiments.

All numerical experiments in this section have been implemented in Matlab 2022b and were carried out on an AMD Ryzen 9 6900HX Processor (8 cores, 3.3–4.9 GHz) and 32 GB of RAM. Scripts to reproduce numerical results are publicly available at
https://github.com/nShao678/LanczosWithCompression-code
.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed a novel strategy for compressing the Krylov subspace in symmetric eigenvalue problems. Theoretical analysis shows that the proposed compression introduced only negligible error relative to the standard Lanczos method, while achieving a more favorable (re)orthogonalization complexity. Numerical experiments show its potential of outperforming the standard implicit restarting strategy. An important direction for future work is to address and overcome the stagnation observed in the numerical experiments.
