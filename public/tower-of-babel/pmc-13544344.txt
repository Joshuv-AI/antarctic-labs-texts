Nasal retainers in rhinoplasty and nasal reconstruction of non-cleft patients: A narrative review
Authors: Ignacio Cortés Fuentes, César Fuenzalida Rozas, Gustavo Bravo Cordero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNasal retainers (also referred to as conformers or stents) are increasingly used after rhinoplasty and nasal reconstruction in non–cleft patients, but their indications, outcomes, and safety profile are not well established. We aimed to summarize the available evidence and propose indications for their use.MethodsA narrative review was conducted. A structured search of PubMed, MEDLINE, and SciELO was performed between November 2025 and February 2026, without language or time restrictions, using predefined search terms and inclusion/exclusion criteria. Studies reporting postoperative use of nasal retainers after aesthetic-functional rhinoplasty or nasal reconstructive surgery were included, excluding those limited to patients with cleft lip and/or palate.ResultsThirteen studies were identified, comprising case reports, small case series, and a single randomized controlled trial involving non-cleft patients undergoing rhinoseptoplasty, correction of nostril stenosis, and reconstruction after trauma, burns, or oncologic resection. Across studies, retainers were typically custom-made or commercial silicone devices used for weeks to months postoperatively. Most reports describe improved nostril symmetry and nasal function, high patient tolerance, and infrequent minor complications.Discussion and conclusionsThere is a biological rationale for the use of nasal retainers in modulating healing and scar formation after nasal surgery. Available evidence, though limited in quality, together with this biological rationale and clinical experience, suggests that nasal retainers may represent a useful adjunct in selected aesthetic and reconstructive nasal procedures, particularly in patients at higher risk of contracture or asymmetry. These observations are best understood as hypothesis-generating and expert opinion-based, as current evidence is limited and well-designed comparative studies are needed to more robustly define their effectiveness.Keywords: Nasal retainers, Nasal conformers, Nasal stents, Rhinoplasty, Nasal reconstruction, Nostril stenosis

Introduction

After primary or revision rhinoplasty, or nasal reconstructive surgery, a complex cicatricial process is triggered over the following days and months. Together with multiple patient-related factors, the surgical techniques performed, postoperative care, and any complications that may arise during the follow-up, these processes ultimately determine the final aesthetic and functional outcome. The anatomy of the nostrils is defined by the harmonious relationship and transitions among the labial–columellar complex, the nasal floor, the alar sidewalls and tip. The cutaneous, fibro-adipose–muscular, and cartilaginous components of these structures determine their resistance or susceptibility to changes caused by cicatricial process secondary to injury or surgery in this area.

The use of nasal retainers — also referred to in the literature as conformers or stents —after rhinoplasty or nasal reconstruction has become increasingly widespread. For consistency, the term “retainer” is used throughout this review. These are small devices usually self-placed by the patient within the nasal vestibule for a variable period during the postoperative course, with the purpose of stabilizing the nasal structures and preventing contracture and deformity during healing, thereby maintaining nostril symmetry and function of the external nasal valve.

Most of the available evidence and reported clinical experience derive from the use of retainers in nasal surgery of pediatric patients, particularly those with cleft lip and palate. Multiple studies have evaluated aesthetic and functional outcomes, as well as complications, generally reporting results that favor the use of these devices at different stages of reconstruction surgeries.1,2

A wide variety of nasal retainers have been described in the literature. They may be made from medical-grade devices such as endotracheal tubes, nasogastric or urinary catheters; from materials such as silicone, acrylics, metals, or others; and they may be custom-made for the individual patient3 or standardized commercial products. The latter are typically manufactured from biocompatible materials but are associated with higher cost.4

However, the indications for their use and outcomes of nasal retainers following rhinoplasty or nasal reconstructive surgery in non-cleft cases have not been evaluated in comparative studies nor extensively reported in the literature. Therefore, the objective of this review is to discuss the evidence related to the use of these devices in this patient population.

Methods

This manuscript is a narrative review. Narrative reviews aim to synthesize and contextualize the available evidence on a given topic, integrate biological rationale, and propose clinically applicable frameworks — purposes that are particularly suited to emerging areas where the evidence base is heterogeneous and does not lend itself to formal meta-analysis. This approach differs fundamentally from systematic reviews, which require exhaustive search strategies, structured selection flows, inter-rater reliability assessment, and formal risk-of-bias tools.

A structured literature search was conducted using the PubMed, MEDLINE, and SciELO databases, without language restrictions or time limits. The search was carried out between November 2025 and February 2026 (last updated in February 2026). The exact search strategy combined the following terms with the Boolean operators and OR: (“nostril” OR “nasal” OR “nose”) AND (“retainer” OR “conformer” OR “stent” OR “molding”) AND (“rhinoplasty” OR “rhinoseptoplasty” OR “septorhinoplasty” OR “reconstruction” OR “reconstructive” OR “surgery”). The same strategy was applied across all three databases and adapted to each interface as required. The search was not designed to identify all possible records in an exhaustive manner, and no formal PRISMA selection flow was employed, consistent with narrative review methodology.

Study selection was performed collaboratively by the authors based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria comprised all scientific articles in which the use of any nasal retainer following rhinoplasty or nasal reconstructive surgery was mentioned in the abstract or full text. Exclusion criteria included all studies referring to the use of retainers exclusively in patients with cleft lip and/or palate. Reference lists of identified articles were also reviewed for relevant additional sources. All study designs were eligible for inclusion.

A total of 13 studies were identified meeting the inclusion criteria: 9 case reports, 3 case series (including 1 prospective series), and 1 randomized controlled trial (RCT). An overview of all included studies is provided in Table 1. Results are presented narratively by clinical context.

Use in aesthetic-functional rhinoplasty in non-cleft cases

Several reports were identified that evaluated postoperative use of nasal retainers after rhinoplasty or reconstructive nasal surgery in a non–cleft scenario. The vast majority of these were case reports related to nasal reconstruction and a recent randomized clinical trial was identified.

In one study, nasal retainers were used in 6 patients who underwent functional rhinoseptoplasty with alar batten grafts for internal nasal valve insufficiency and dynamic lateral wall collapse, and in 1 patient treated with a composite graft for nostril stenosis. All patients began using a retainer from the first postoperative week.5 Six of the seven patients reported minimal or no discomfort, easy application, and minimal or no obstruction of nasal breathing while using the retainers. The NOSE (Nasal Obstruction Symptom Evaluation) questionnaire was administered, demonstrating a significant improvement between preoperative scores, scores at the time of retainer removal, and scores at 3 months after retainer removal, with no significant difference between the latter two time points.

Recently, in a randomized, single-blinded, parallel-group controlled trial, 128 adults undergoing primary septorhinoplasty were allocated 1:1 to receive standard treatment alone or standard treatment plus a nasal retainer after surgery.6 All patients underwent nasal tip work via marginal incisions, and indications were cosmetic, functional, or both. Patients were instructed to wear the device continuously from postoperative day 3 for 1 week, and then nightly through 3 months. The primary outcome was patient-reported nostril symmetry (nostril-symmetry item: 0 = perfect to 4 = very asymmetrical) at 3 and 12 months. Secondary outcomes included functional and aesthetic Standardized Cosmesis and Health Nasal Outcomes Survey (SCHNOS) scores, comfort, and side effects. Nostril symmetry scores favored the intervention group at 3 months (0.78 ± 0.92 vs 1.42 ± 1.09; p = 0.001) and 12 months (0.46 ± 0.71 vs 1.40 ± 1.20; p < 0.001). Functional SCHNOS scores improved in both groups, with a slightly greater improvement in the retainer group, whereas aesthetic SCHNOS scores did not differ. No device-related complications were reported, and comfort with use was high.

Use in non-cleft nasal reconstructive surgery

In one case of an adult with congenital bilateral nostril stenosis and external nasal valve insufficiency, redundant tissue from both medial crura was resected and repaired using a bilateral Y–V advancement flap, followed by placement of a commercial conformer (Koken, South Korea). The patient was instructed to wear the retainer at night and for as many hours as possible during the day for 3 months, with a good aesthetic and functional result reported at 6 months after surgery.7

In a patient who developed columellar necrosis secondary to multiple previous rhinoplasties and was awaiting further surgery, the use of a custom-made retainer was reported. In addition to providing structural support to the area in preparation for delayed reconstructive surgery, it also served an aesthetic role as a temporary prosthesis for the absent columella.8

In a case of subtotal nasal resection for skin carcinoma and subsequent reconstruction with costal cartilage and a forehead flap, a nasal retainer was fabricated from a urinary catheter covered with foam. This low cost device was noted to be well tolerated by the patient,9 similar to another reported case of nasal trauma in which a comparable catheter-based retainer was used,10 although the duration of use and associated complications were not reported. In a patient with nostril and nasal vestibular stenosis of traumatic origin and a history of multiple previous reconstructive procedures, a custom-made retainer was designed postoperatively using materials similar to those employed in dental prostheses.11 Likewise, in another report of a patient with traumatic laceration of the nasal ala resulting in stenosis, a custom retainer was fabricated based on the contralateral normal nostril to be used while awaiting definitive surgery, with aesthetic and functional improvement reported at 3 months.12 Similarly, in a series of 38 cases of unilateral post-traumatic stenosis treated with reconstructive surgery followed by an acrylic nasal retainer manufactured by 3D printing, aesthetic and functional improvement was reported, evidenced by decreased nasal resistance and increased transverse area of the external nasal valve after its use,13 with no complications reported.

In two similar cases of severe nasal stenosis secondary to facial burns in patients receiving home oxygen therapy via nasal cannula, different approaches were described. In one, a superior-lip flap and auricular dermal graft were performed in two stages, followed by use of a conformer (Talmant nasal conformer, Sebbin, Boissy-l’Aillerie, France) for 4 months; final nostril diameters of 9 and 11 mm were reported, with no adverse effects attributed to the retainer.14 In the other report, the stenosis was released under local anesthesia and treated with mitomycin-C and serial dilations. Subsequently, progressively larger custom-made nasal retainers were used for 4 months starting from the second month, with nostril diameters of 8 and 10 mm reported at 15 months of follow-up.15

Discussion

There is limited evidence regarding the use of nasal retainers or retainers in aesthetic–functional rhinoplasty and facial reconstructive surgery. Most of the currently available data consist of single case reports and small case series, although the best available evidence from a randomized clinical trial also supports favorable aesthetic and functional outcomes with conformer use. Nevertheless, several reports do not provide detailed information on daily and total duration of use, recommended care, or patient satisfaction. Moreover, outcome reporting across the included studies was highly heterogeneous and largely non-standardized: only a minority employed validated patient-reported instruments (e.g., NOSE or SCHNOS), whereas most relied on non-comparable descriptive measures such as nostril diameter or subjective symmetry. This absence of standardized outcome measures precludes direct comparison and quantitative pooling of results across reports. Most series and the clinical trial report a low frequency of adverse effects, which is consistent with the literature on patients with cleft lip and palate, in whom the use of nasal retainers is more widespread. In this population, the overall complication rate is relatively low (approximately 6%), with the most relevant events being skin irritation (0.9%) and infection (0.3%), along with loss or displacement of the device (4.6%).1

Although their use has become more common in routine clinical practice, nasal retainers are likely underreported in the literature, with publication probably biased toward more complex, severe, or unusual cases. Nonetheless, there is a biological rationale for considering their use in selected nasal surgery cases. Once a wound is produced, the wound-healing process is triggered, which classically comprises three phases: inflammatory, proliferative, and remodeling.16 The inflammatory phase lasts approximately 3–5 days and is characterized by vasodilation, increased blood flow, and the influx of substrates and immune cells, initially predominantly neutrophils with high protease and free radical activity, followed by macrophages that secrete growth factors inducing the proliferative phase. The proliferative phase lasts around 2 weeks and is characterized by epithelialization, granulation tissue formation, and angiogenesis, resulting in the development of an extracellular matrix. This phase is mediated by numerous cell types, including epithelial and endothelial cells, macrophages, and fibroblasts. The remodeling phase may last 1–2 years and occurs concurrently with fibroblast proliferation, involving degradation and reorganization of the extracellular matrix, blood vessels, and granulation tissue.

This wound-healing process is complex and may be altered at different stages, leading to undesirable outcomes such as atrophic, hypertrophic, or keloid scars. Fibroblasts play a key role in the proliferative phase and throughout remodeling. These cells differentiate into myofibroblasts, which are associated with the contracture that occurs in wounds and subsequent scarring. It has been reported that myofibroblasts respond to mechanical stimuli and pressure.16,17 For this reason, in other types of wounds, the application of pressure using different types of splints or devices contributes to limit myofibroblasts activity and reduce contracture resulting from their overactivity, particularly during the first months of healing.16,18 Interestingly, a case series of 5 patients with acquired traumatic and congenital nostril stenosis treated without surgery, using only progressively larger retainers, reported improved nasal symmetry at 24 months and a mean increase in nostril width of 7 mm, using between 3 and 8 retainers.19 Similarly, in a recent experimental study of 31 patients with nasal obstruction, a customized spherical nasal vestibular retainer was used for at least 8 h per day for 1 week. The authors reported an increase in overall nasal volume and nasal valve area, with a significant alleviation of obstruction symptoms in these patients.20

It should be emphasized that, with the exception of postoperative use after primary septorhinoplasty — which is supported by a single randomized controlled trial — the indications proposed here and summarized in Table 2 are derived largely from biological rationale, clinical analogy, and expert opinion rather than from direct comparative clinical evidence and should be interpreted and applied to a specific case accordingly. Based on the reviewed studies and the underlying biological principles, the use of nasal retainers may be considered in selected cases of rhinoplasty and facial reconstructive surgery (Table 2), starting from approximately the first to second postoperative week once satisfactory wound status has been confirmed. However, as similar to cleft lip patients, in selected cases retainers may be placed immediately after surgery in the operating room or during the following days. Taking into account the specific anatomical characteristics of the nostril region, it is reasonable to recommend the use of these devices for 3–6 months in rhinoplasty cases with pre- or postoperative nostril asymmetries; in cases involving resection of the alar sill or nasal floor; in extensive alar base reduction; or when composite auricular skin–cartilage grafts have been used, once graft viability has been confirmed. Patients with significant lateral wall collapse may benefit from retainers to prevent further collapse related to abnormal scarring in an intrinsically weak area.5 Their use may also be justified in cases with asymmetry or resection of the medial crura, as well as in those with large resections or reconstruction with grafts involving the lateral crura, in order to counteract postoperative scar contracture.5 Similarly, retainers may be recommended in patients with a history of hypertrophic scarring or asymmetric scarring after previous rhinoplasty, especially in those with thick skin and any of the condition mentioned above.21,22 However, in tertiary or subsequent revision rhinoplasty, particularly via an open approach, the use of retainers should be carefully individualized because of the higher baseline risk of columellar skin necrosis in these patients, which could be aggravated by retainer use, at least in the early postoperative period.

Duration recommendations are approximate and should be adjusted based on individual patient healing, tissue quality, and clinical evolution.

Retainers should ideally be manufactured from medical-grade silicone specifically designed for nasal use. In resource-limited settings, custom retainers from catheters or similar materials may be used with closer surveillance for adverse events.

The use of retainers is best characterized and documented in reconstructive settings such as facial burns, trauma of various etiologies, or oncologic nasal reconstruction. Given the relatively low frequency of such patients, their use should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis, weighing the potential risks of retainer use against its anticipated benefits. These benefits are probably greater in this population than in non–cleft aesthetic rhinoplasty patients, since soft tissues are more severely altered and the areas of scarring are expected to be larger, thus increasing the risk of contracture. For this reason, in such patients, retainer use is recommended for at least 6–9 months, and potentially longer, depending on the extent of tissue damage and postoperative evolution.

An important aspect in preventing adverse effects associated with these devices is the choice of appropriate materials for their construction. Ideally, nasal retainers should be manufactured from medical-grade silicone, specifically designed and certified for use as nasal conformers, and sized according to the patient’s nostril dimensions. For example, nflare™ nasal retainers (CranioMandibular Rehab, Inc., USA) can be ordered in standardized sizes or customized and cost from US$285. However, in resource-limited settings, and according to what has been reported in the discussed case series, retainers adapted from other materials such as catheters or tubes appear to be similarly useful. In these circumstances, it is reasonable to maintain closer surveillance for potential adverse events, given that these materials were not originally designed for use as nasal retainers, especially in patients with higher risk of wound problems.

Conclusions

The use of nasal retainers or conformers in rhinoplasty and nasal reconstructive surgery in non–cleft represents a useful postoperative therapeutic tool that may contribute to improved aesthetic and functional outcomes. Although these devices are associated with a low frequency of complications, their use should be recommended judiciously, with close monitoring for potential adverse events.

Despite their increasingly widespread use in routine clinical practice, the evidence supporting nasal retainer use remains limited and generally of low quality, with few comparative studies available to robustly assess their effectiveness. Nonetheless, there is a clear biological rationale and growing practical experience that support their use. Careful clinical follow-up and the selection of devices made from appropriate materials are advisable to minimize the risk of potential complications.
