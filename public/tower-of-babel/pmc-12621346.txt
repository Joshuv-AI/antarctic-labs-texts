Applicability and effectiveness of group EMDR therapy for forced migrants
Authors: Nicolas Chauliac, Philippe Vignaud, Clémence Butet, Marie Gautheron, Germain Salome, Laurie Salles, Perrine Galia, Nathalie Prieto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: The global rise in forced migration has led to increased mental health challenges among displaced populations, particularly post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and complex PTSD (CPTSD). Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) therapy, and its group-based adaptation – the Group Traumatic Episode Protocol (GTEP) – has shown promise in addressing trauma in such contexts.Objective: This retrospective pilot study evaluates the feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness of a modified GTEP protocol tailored for migrant populations at a regional psychotrauma centre in Europe.Methods: Participants (N = 71) were adult forced migrants (asylum seekers, refugees or undocumented migrants) diagnosed with PTSD. The intervention included psychoeducation, stabilization, and the usual eight EMDR phases, delivered in a group format. Pre- and post-intervention assessments were conducted using validated self-report measures: PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ), Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6), WHOQOL-BREF.Results: Significant reductions in PTSD and depressive symptoms were observed, with large effect sizes across most measures. Notably, CPTSD diagnoses decreased from 60.9% to 15.2%. The dropout rate was low (15%), and session attendance was high (80%), indicating strong acceptability. These findings suggest that the adapted GTEP protocol is both effective and feasible for use with migrant populations, including those with complex PTSD.Conclusions: Despite limitations such as the absence of a control group and missing data, this study provides evidence supporting the use of group EMDR interventions in real-world clinical settings for forcibly displaced individuals. Future research should include randomized controlled trials and long-term follow-up to further validate these findings.KEYWORDS: PTSD, psychotherapy, EMDR, group therapy, group traumatic episode protocol (GTEP), migrant, forcibly displaced person, refugee, asylum seeker, undocumented migrant

Introduction

By the end of 2023, 43.4 million people worldwide had been forcibly displaced across borders (United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees. Global Data Service, 2024), and around 11% of the French population is made up of people living in France as a result of international migration (Institut national de la statistique et des études économiques (INSEE), 2023). Forced migrants are frequently exposed to potentially traumatic events, whether in their country of origin, during their journey, or after their arrival in the host country. The prevalence of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depressive disorders is higher among these populations than among host populations, and up to one-third of refugees has diagnosable current depression or PTSD (Andersen et al., 2020; Bhugra et al., 2014; Fazel et al., 2005; Henkelmann et al., 2020; Priebe et al., 2016; Steel et al., 2009). One partial cause is the ongoing stress exposure, even after arrival in the host country (Sonne et al., 2017). Emerging evidence also suggests that complex PTSD (CPTSD) is more frequent than PTSD among forcibly displaced populations (Barbieri et al., 2023; Frost et al., 2019; Hyland et al., 2018; Liddell et al., 2019; Vang et al., 2019).

PTSD in migrant populations is associated with long-term functional impairments, including reduced economic and social integration even years after regularization (Bogic et al., 2015; Marshall et al., 2005; Schick et al., 2016). Furthermore, migrants frequently face structural barriers to healthcare access, especially in the domain of mental health services (Bradby et al., 2015; Lindert et al., 2008).

Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) is an individual psychotherapy that has demonstrated efficacy in the treatment of PTSD, including among migrants (Molendijk et al., 2024; Turrini et al., 2021). To address the need for scalable treatment following mass traumatic events (e.g. earthquakes, floods, etc.), group-based EMDR protocols such as the Group Traumatic Episode Protocol (GTEP) have been developed (Shapiro, 2013). GTEP can be delivered to groups of around 10 individuals over a limited number of sessions, and it has shown promising results among migrants with PTSD (Kaptan et al., 2021; Lehnung et al., 2017; Roberts, 2018; Yurtsever et al., 2018) Importantly, GTEP minimizes reliance on verbal expression and language proficiency, making it particularly well-suited for linguistically diverse populations.

In order to address more ancient or multiple traumatic events, and in accordance with the current recommendations of phase-based psychotherapy for the treatment of PTSD and CPTSD by the International Society for Traumatic Stress Studies (ISTSS) (Cloitre et al., 2011; Forbes et al., 2020; Mahoney et al., 2019), our regional psychotrauma centre adapted the standard GTEP. This modified protocol includes an extended stabilization phase, five trauma-focused sessions (corresponding to the desensitization and reprocessing phases of EMDR), and a final reintegration session. The group format was retained, in line with findings suggesting enhanced outcomes for CPTSD in group-based interventions (Forbes et al., 2020; Mahoney et al., 2019). The objective of this retrospective pilot study was to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness of this modified GTEP protocol in a sample of forced migrants receiving care at our centre.

Methods

Participants

Participants were forced migrants (asylum seekers, refugees or undocumented migrants) referred to our regional trauma centre. Eligibility for group treatment was based on a clinical diagnosis of current PTSD, confirmed by a psychiatrist or psychologist during an initial individual assessment. We included in this study all individuals aged 18 years or older who attended at least one group session between April 1st, 2021, and December 31st, 2024, and who completed at least one self-report questionnaire.

Data collection

Participants completed a battery of five validated self-report measures during the initial individual interview or at the beginning of the first group session: PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (Ashbaugh et al., 2016; Blevins et al., 2015), International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) (Kroenke et al., 2001), Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6) (Kessler et al., 2003), and abbreviated WHO Quality of Life (WHOQOL-BREF) (The WHOQOL Group, 1998). All questionnaires were re-administered at the end of the final group session. The French and English validated versions were used according to the preference of participants. Additionally, Subjective Units of Distress (SUD) were collected during the second session and at the end of the final session, as per the GTEP protocol. Where necessary, a translator was provided to assist the participant, or the professional used machine translation.

Group EMDR protocol (modified GTEP protocol)

The Group Traumatic Episode Protocol (GTEP) was originally developed by E. Shapiro (Shapiro, 2013; Shapiro & Moench, 2017) as a group EMDR protocol suitable for mass trauma settings. It is based on the R-TEP (Recent Episode Traumatic Protocol) for EMDR early intervention developed by E. Shapiro and B. Laub (Shapiro & Laub, 2008). GTEP follows the classical 8 phases of EMDR, which are all presented to the patient on a worksheet with areas in which he/she can write or draw. It starts with a short preparation and stabilization phase (‘safe place’ or ‘4 elements’ exercise) and then helps patients to identify ‘points of perturbation’ which are desensitized through 9 sets of alternate bilateral stimulations. These alternate bilateral stimulations are achieved individually by each patient by following their hand with their eyes, moving from one area on the right (present safety) to one on the left on the worksheet (‘point of perturbation’). After the 9 sets, the patient either identifies a new point of perturbation within the traumatic episode or keeps the same as before, and the desensitization phase is carried on with another 9 sets. Four to five points or perturbations are treated per session. In the original GTEP protocol, only one session is proposed, with one therapist if the group size is below 6–8 persons, and two for larger groups.

To better address CPTSD, we adapted the GTEP protocol to include: two sessions dedicated to psychoeducation and stabilization, three to four sessions focused on trauma processing, and one final session for reintegration. Sessions were held weekly, except for the last which was scheduled approximately two to three weeks after the last trauma-focused session. All were conducted in group format by one trained EMDR therapist, assisted by one or two other therapists. Each group comprised 4–10 participants. When necessary, a translator was provided to assist the participant, or the professionals used machine translation. In case of flashbacks or severe emotional reactions, participants were taken out of the room by one of the therapists.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were conducted using JASP version 0.95.1 (JASP Team, 2025). Analyses were performed on an ‘intention-to-treat’ basis, meaning that all participants were included, if they attended at least one session. For each psychological measure, we performed non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to compare pre- and post-treatment scores. The Hodges–Lehmann estimator was used to estimate the difference in medians. Effect sizes were calculated using the matched rank biserial correlation, appropriate for Wilcoxon tests. To control for Type I error due to multiple comparisons, p-values, confidence intervals, and effect sizes were adjusted using a Bonferroni correction across six primary outcomes, yielding a corrected significance threshold of α = 0.0083 and 99% confidence intervals. Effect size estimates were additionally corrected for within-subject correlations to minimize overestimation (Dunlap et al., 1996). To account for missing data, analyses were conducted on a pairwise basis, including only participants with both pre- and post-treatment scores for the specific measure in question. No data imputation was performed.

Out of 71 participants who attended at least one session and completed at least one questionnaire, 48 individuals had complete pre- and post-intervention data for at least one psychological measure and were included in the primary outcome analyses. In order to measure the impact of missing data, we conducted two-tailed independent-samples t-tests and Mann–Whitney U tests to compare the values of the main outcome measures for the two populations: participants for whom measures were not used and participants for whom all measures were used. No significant differences were found regarding PCL-5, PHQ-9, ITQ, and SUD pre- or post-intervention. The detailed results can be found in Supplementary Materials.

Cost-effectiveness

As a control group was not available for this study, we evaluated the cost-effectiveness of group therapy compared to individual therapy by looking at the average amount of time that each therapist spent per patient over the course of the therapy sessions. Given that two therapists delivered therapy for each 2.5-hour group, with an average of six participants per group, the mean gain compared to individual one-hour sessions is 17%. However, when group size exceeded eight participants, a third therapist was added, thereby reducing the overall efficiency gains.

Discussion

This pilot study constitutes a first evaluation of the acceptability, applicability, and effectiveness of a modified Group Traumatic Episode Protocol (GTEP) tailored for forced migrant populations, implemented over a three-year period in our psychotrauma centre. A preliminary study (Vignaud, Chauliac, Contamin, et al., 2023) provided initial insights, but the present analysis is based on a larger sample. Although no causal inference can be made due to the retrospective design and the absence of a control group, the findings are encouraging, with large effect sizes observed across primary outcome measures. Notably, the PCL-5 scores decreased by nearly 12 points, a reduction usually recognized as clinically meaningful (Marx et al., 2022; Weathers et al., 2013). Furthermore, the intervention seemed to be effective in addressing complex PTSD (CPTSD), with 60% of participants initially meeting ITQ diagnostic criteria and only 15% retaining the diagnosis post-intervention. The low dropout rate (15%) underscores the protocol’s acceptability within this population. Three participants who initially met criteria for PTSD on the ITQ shifted to CPTSD after the intervention. For these cases, PCL-5 scores also increased, and attendance was incomplete. This might suggest that partial exposure to desensitization sessions reactivated traumatic memories without sufficient stabilization or completion of processing.

Various group EMDR interventions have previously shown promise in similar contexts. For instance, Bizouerne et al. (2023) conducted a randomized controlled trial comparing trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (TF-CBT) and GTEP among internally displaced individuals in Iraq. Both treatments were similarly effective in reducing IES-R and HAD scores between the first and last session, with large effect sizes, and large overall participation in groups. Yet, IES-R scores remained above diagnostic thresholds in both groups. Similarly, Yurtsever et al. (2018) demonstrated that two GTEP sessions significantly reduced PTSD and depression symptoms compared to a waitlist control, although the effect sizes were modest, and almost one-third of participants did not attend the second session. Lehnung et al. (2017) also reported significant improvements in IES scores following two GTEP sessions on two consecutive days among refugees, though BDI scores did not differ significantly. A meta-analysis by Macgowan et al. (2022) further supports the potential of EMDR-based interventions for forcibly displaced populations. The authors reported medium to large effect sizes for PTSD, depression, and anxiety across 17 studies, including 6 group EMDR interventions. However, considerable heterogeneity was observed across studies, and when comparing effect sizes between individual and group EMDR, the authors found larger effects with interventions delivered individually. In another meta-analysis focusing exclusively on randomized controlled trials, Antuña-Camblor and Hernández (2025) found only small and statistically non-significant effects of EMDR on PTSD symptoms in adult refugee samples. These mixed findings underscore the necessity of further evaluating group EMDR protocols.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

A strength of this study lies in its real-world clinical setting. By including all individuals referred to our centre who met eligibility criteria, the study provides a representative overview of the applicability, acceptability, and suggests effectiveness of group EMDR for forced migrant populations, particularly those affected by complex PTSD. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and absence of a control group impede the capacity to establish causal relationships. In addition, the retrospective nature of the study prevented systematic control of potential confounders, including concurrent pharmacological treatments, and ongoing stressors like residence permit procedures. Second, diagnoses were based on clinical interviews and ITQ scores, which reduces diagnostic precision compared to clinician-administered diagnostic tools such as the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 (Weathers et al., 2013). Similarly, self-report questionnaires, like the PHQ-9, have not the same accuracy as clinician-administered tools. Third, the study did not include follow-up assessments, which would be essential to evaluate the durability of treatment effects. This limitation is partly due to the transient nature of the population studied, as many asylum seekers are relocated following decisions on their legal status, making long-term follow-up logistically challenging. However, given the instability this group faces and their limited access to ongoing therapy, determining the lasting impact of brief interventions is especially critical. Additionally, some participants were receiving pharmacological treatment for PTSD symptoms during the intervention period. Although this may have contributed to symptoms improvement, the effectiveness of pharmacotherapy for PTSD in migrant populations remains a topic of ongoing debate (Sonne et al., 2017).

It is generally assumed that an advantage of group therapy is its cost-effectiveness, compared to individual care (Johnson et al., 2019; Vignaud, Chauliac, Richer, et al., 2023). The present study was not designed to undertake a comprehensive evaluation of the cost-effectiveness of the intervention. However, a preliminary assessment of the time spent by the therapists in comparison with one-hour individual sessions suggests a relatively modest gain. In our view, the group's distinct advantage lies in its capacity to motivate patients and normalize disorders, a capability that is particularly essential for the migrant population.

Despite the limitations of this non-controlled, retrospective pilot study, the findings provide a rationale for the continued use and further investigation of group EMDR therapy in migrant populations. Future research should prioritize randomized controlled trials, incorporate long-term follow-up assessments, and explore the non-specific group support effects, reasons for dropout, and cost-effectiveness of group therapy versus individual care.

Conclusion

This retrospective pilot study shows the acceptability and suggests the effectiveness of an adapted Group Traumatic Episode Protocol (GTEP) with forced migrant populations. Significant reductions in PTSD and depressive symptoms were observed, as evidenced by decreases in PCL-5, PHQ-9, and SUD scores, accompanied by large effect sizes. Diagnostic outcomes based on the ITQ also improved markedly, particularly for individuals initially presenting with complex PTSD. Importantly, the intervention was well-tolerated, with a notably low dropout rate of 15%, which is uncommon in this population. These findings suggest that the adapted GTEP protocol shows potential as an applicable therapeutic option for addressing PTSD in forcibly displaced individuals, including those with histories of chronic or repeated trauma. This study represents the first formal evaluation of this specific adaptation of GTEP and provides a foundation for its continued use and further investigation in clinical settings.
