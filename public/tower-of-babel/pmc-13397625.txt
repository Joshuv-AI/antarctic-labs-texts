The impact of job satisfaction among healthcare workers in township hospitals in Western China: a case study of Lanzhou City
Authors: Sheng Li, Guoai Yao, Fei Wang, Qing Jia, Minmin Jiang, Jinyu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the factors influencing the job satisfaction of healthcare workers in primary healthcare institutions in Lanzhou City.MethodsA stratified random cluster sampling method was used to conduct an online survey among healthcare workers in township health centers across five districts and three counties of Lanzhou City. A self-designed questionnaire was administered to assess job satisfaction, remuneration system, professional identity, career planning, and related factors. The current status and influencing factors of job satisfaction were analyzed.ResultsA total of 793 valid questionnaires were collected, with an effective response rate of 97.06%. The results on job satisfaction and its influencing factors showed that the average score for job satisfaction was (3.14 ± 0.89), for the remuneration system dimension (3.02 ± 0.90), for the career planning dimension (2.96 ± 0.96), and for the professional identity dimension (3.38 ± 0.97). Using 3 as the midpoint, these scores indicate an overall moderate level of satisfaction. Pearson correlation analysis revealed significant positive correlations among these factors, with the correlation between professional identity and job satisfaction being the most significant. A structural equation model was constructed, and after secondary fitting, the model fit indices met the standard criteria. The results indicated that career planning partially mediated the relationship between professional identity and job satisfaction, accounting for 20.25% of the total effect.ConclusionThe job satisfaction among healthcare workers at township health centers in Lanzhou City is at a moderate level. Professional identity not only directly influences job satisfaction but also indirectly affects it through career planning. In resource-constrained primary healthcare settings, the impact of professional identity and development expectations on job satisfaction appears to be more significant than material incentives alone. These findings provide context-specific insights into understanding the stability of the healthcare workforce in underdeveloped western regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14754-1.Keywords: Primary healthcare institutions, Healthcare workers, Job satisfaction, Influencing factors, Structural equation modeling

Introduction

Globally, primary healthcare systems are widely regarded as the fundamental foundation for achieving universal health coverage and improving the efficiency of health systems. As the “net bottom” of China’s three-tiered rural healthcare service network, township health centers serve as the “gatekeepers of health” for residents, carrying dual functions of basic medical care and public health services. Through standardized diagnosis and treatment of common and frequently occurring diseases, as well as systematic disease prevention and health management, they play a critical role in safeguarding the basic health rights of rural residents [1, 2]. Therefore, the stability and service capacity of the primary healthcare workforce directly influence the achievement of the “strengthening primary care” strategic objective. However, in practice, primary healthcare institutions have long faced structural challenges such as shortages of healthcare personnel, severe brain drain, and limited career development opportunities [3]. These issues are particularly prominent in underdeveloped western regions. Taking Gansu Province as an example, township health centers in some areas still exhibit significant gaps in human resource allocation, professional technical structure, and career development pathways compared to national standards for primary healthcare service capacity [4, 5]. Although the new round of healthcare system reforms has markedly improved the infrastructure of primary institutions, institutional support in areas such as healthcare workforce training, incentive mechanisms, and career development systems remains relatively lagging, reflecting a tendency to prioritize facility construction over human resource development [6]. Against this backdrop, enhancing the professional stability and organizational commitment of primary healthcare workers has become a critical issue requiring urgent resolution.

Research indicates that job satisfaction is a significant predictor of healthcare workers’ turnover intention and organizational retention [7]. Higher job satisfaction not only enhances individuals’ work engagement and performance but also improves organizational cohesion and service continuity [8]. Therefore, exploring the formation mechanism of job satisfaction holds substantial theoretical and practical value for stabilizing the primary healthcare workforce. Among the various factors influencing job satisfaction, professional identity and remuneration have attracted particular attention. Professional identity, as an individual’s internal perception of the value, significance, and social role of their occupation, serves as an important psychological variable explaining work attitudes and behaviors, while remuneration systems constitute a core component of extrinsic incentive mechanisms [9–12]. Based on the above analysis, this study considers professional identity, career planning, and the remuneration system as core independent variables. Their theoretical connotations and mechanisms of action in relation to job satisfaction are elaborated below. Individuals define themselves through their membership in social groups. Professional identity refers to the extent to which an individual identifies with and feels a sense of belonging to their occupational group. A high level of professional identity can promote employees’ work motivation and loyalty, as it reflects the value and meaning individuals perceive in their profession [13]. Multiple studies have confirmed that professional identity has a significant positive impact on job satisfaction. Particularly in high-pressure work environments, healthcare workers’ professional identity helps them cope with occupational stress and challenges, thereby enhancing work engagement and satisfaction [14, 15]. Career planning involves individuals’ clear design of career development paths and the setting of phased goals. Clear career planning not only enhances individuals’ sense of expectation and control over the future but also strengthens their perception of work meaning [16]. In the healthcare industry, many medical professionals face bottlenecks in career development. Therefore, effective career planning not only contributes to a sense of professional achievement but also improves job satisfaction [17–20]. Furthermore, career planning guides individuals in setting short-term and long-term goals and provides psychological preparation for addressing occupational challenges, thereby enhancing job satisfaction [21]. According to Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory, while remuneration is an important factor influencing job satisfaction, its role tends to eliminate dissatisfaction rather than generate satisfaction [22]. When remuneration levels are low, healthcare workers are prone to dissatisfaction, which can negatively affect their work motivation and satisfaction [23]. Under such circumstances, non-material factors, such as professional identity and career planning, exert a more pronounced influence on job satisfaction. Therefore, in low-remuneration environments, non-material incentives (e.g., enhancing professional identity) become particularly important. In summary, professional identity not only influences healthcare workers’ work attitudes and behaviors but also positively affects their career planning. Employees with a higher level of professional identity are generally more motivated to set long-term and short-term goals for their career development, thereby improving the quality of their career planning. In contrast, remuneration primarily addresses employees’ basic needs. Inadequate remuneration levels can negatively impact their job satisfaction. However, the positive effects of professional identity and career planning are more profound, enabling employees to maintain relatively high job satisfaction even under lower remuneration conditions.

However, existing research has primarily focused on large hospitals or economically developed regions, where organizational structures are relatively mature and career advancement mechanisms are well-established. In contrast, primary healthcare institutions in resource-limited areas face challenges such as scarce promotion opportunities, insufficient training resources, and weak institutional support [24]. Under such conditions, the formation mechanism of job satisfaction may no longer follow the traditional path centered on extrinsic incentives but instead shows a trend toward being predominantly driven by intrinsic psychological resources. This raises a critical theoretical question: In resource-constrained contexts, has the formation mechanism of job satisfaction undergone a structural shift from being “driven by extrinsic incentives” to being “driven by intrinsic identification and developmental expectations”—meaning that factors such as professional identity and career expectations may play a more dominant role than material incentives. Yet, this question remains insufficiently examined through systematic empirical research in the field of primary healthcare.

From the perspective of career development theory, career planning not only reflects an individual’s understanding of their future career path but also embodies their judgment regarding career controllability and developmental expectations [25]. Although existing studies generally recognize that career planning can enhance job satisfaction, the supporting evidence mostly comes from urban hospitals or relatively resource-rich organizational environments. For primary healthcare workers—a group more visibly constrained by institutional limitations—the mechanisms through which career planning operates still lack support from structural models [26]. Therefore, based on professional identity theory and career development theory, this study constructs a structural model of “professional identity—career planning—job satisfaction.” Focusing on healthcare workers in township health centers in Lanzhou City, it specifically tests whether, in resource-constrained contexts, career planning serves as a key psychological pathway linking professional identity to job satisfaction.

In recent years, research on healthcare workers’ job satisfaction has gradually expanded beyond the traditional perspective of salary incentives to include psychological and social factors such as professional identity, organizational support, and career development. However, most existing studies still regard these variables as supplementary to the conventional “external incentive model,” implicitly assuming that salary, promotion opportunities, and institutional rewards remain the dominant foundations of job satisfaction, while intrinsic psychological factors such as professional identity play only a supportive role. Particularly in large hospitals or relatively resource-abundant organizational settings, previous studies have generally emphasized the central role of material incentives and institutional guarantees, while paying limited attention to whether the mechanism underlying job satisfaction may undergo structural changes under resource-constrained conditions [27–29].The theoretical starting point of this study is not merely to verify the effects of professional identity or career planning on job satisfaction, but further to propose that, in the context of limited primary healthcare resources, restricted promotion opportunities, and insufficient institutional incentives, the dominant mechanism of job satisfaction may shift structurally from an “external incentive-driven” model to an “identity–development-driven” model. In other words, professional identity is no longer simply a supplementary variable beyond the remuneration mechanism, but may instead become a core psychological resource for maintaining occupational stability and job satisfaction. Likewise, career planning not only reflects an instrumental function for career development, but also embodies individuals’ psychological construction of future career controllability and development expectations.Based on this logic, the present study proposes an “identity–development-driven” model. This model emphasizes that, under conditions of resource constraints, the formation of job satisfaction no longer primarily depends on the level of material incentives, but increasingly relies on individuals’ recognition of professional value and their positive expectations for future development. Compared with previous studies, the theoretical innovation of this research lies not merely in introducing new influencing variables, but in revealing the changing mechanism of the driving structure of job satisfaction across different resource contexts, and further clarifying the critical mediating role of career planning in the transformation of professional identity into job satisfaction.

In summary, this study not only examines the independent effects of professional identity, career planning, and the remuneration system on job satisfaction but also focuses on their relative structural roles within resource-constrained contexts. From this perspective, this paper proposes a context-sensitive “identity-development driven” model of job satisfaction. This model posits that in primary healthcare settings where external incentives are limited, the formation mechanism of job satisfaction has undergone a structural shift from being “remuneration-driven” to being “identity-development driven.” That is, an individual’s professional identity and career development expectations become dominant factors, while the role of extrinsic incentives such as remuneration is relatively diminished. This finding not only expands the contextual applicability boundaries of job satisfaction theory but also provides an important supplement to classic incentive theories, such as Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory, by suggesting that the relative roles of intrinsic and extrinsic factors are not fixed but exhibit significant context dependency.

Materials and methods

Study participants

From November 1 to December 31, 2023, a stratified cluster random sampling method was used to survey healthcare workers from township health centers in the five districts and three counties of Lanzhou City to obtain a representative sample. The specific operational procedures were as follows: First, township hospitals in the five districts and three counties of Lanzhou City were stratified according to geographic location, hospital size, and professional titles of healthcare workers. This approach ensured that hospitals at each level could represent the overall situation, guaranteeing the breadth and representativeness of the sample. Subsequently, a certain number of township hospitals were randomly selected from each stratum as sample institutions. Given the dispersed distribution of healthcare workers in township hospitals, cluster sampling was adopted to effectively reduce selection bias and better ensure the authenticity and representativeness of the sample. Within the selected township hospitals, healthcare workers meeting the study criteria were randomly chosen to participate in the questionnaire survey. This step ensured the randomness of the subjects, thereby providing the sample with broad representativeness. Data were collected via the “Questionnaire Star” online platform. Participants completed the questionnaires independently and anonymously. A total of 793 valid questionnaires were collected, with an effective response rate of 97.06%. The inclusion criteria for participants were as follows: (1) informed consent and voluntary participation; (2) at least one year of work experience and currently employed at the time of the survey; (3) no history of mental illness or significant organic diseases. Exclusion criteria included: (1) pregnant or lactating women; (2) rehired, on leave, or full-time trainees. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Lanzhou Pulmonary Hospital (Approval No.: 202311101), ensuring ethical compliance and the protection of participants’ rights.

Survey questionnaire

Based on Social Identity Theory, Career Development Theory, and Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory, and incorporating insights from relevant literature and expert opinions [30–32], items for measuring each dimension were designed. The formulation of each item was closely aligned with the theoretical framework, ensuring the valid measurement of the relevant variables. A pilot survey was conducted prior to the formal investigation, and the questionnaire was revised based on the preliminary results. The questionnaire was structured around core assessment dimensions including job satisfaction, professional identity, career planning and development, and the remuneration system. It followed a unified format and consisted of sections covering general demographic characteristics, job satisfaction, career planning, professional identity, and the remuneration system. Items related to attitudes and perceptions were measured using a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating stronger agreement or higher satisfaction.

Quality control

Research Planning Phase: A bibliometric analysis of core literature from the past five years was conducted, and the assessment tool was refined through two rounds of Delphi expert consultations (involving three psychology professors, two clinical medicine experts, and one biostatistician) to ensure questionnaire reliability and validity. Prior to project initiation, ethics review approval was obtained from the Lanzhou Pulmonary Hospital, and cooperation agreements were signed with participating institutions (Approval No.: 202311101).

Data Collection Phase: A stratified cluster random sampling method was employed to select samples for a pilot test, identifying and revising items with ambiguous semantics or logical inconsistencies. A hospital liaison team was established to conduct standardized training, clarifying research objectives and operational procedures. Formal data collection utilized an electronic platform to distribute dynamic QR code-encrypted links, with IP and device uniqueness verification logic implemented. Participants were required to complete informed consent and all mandatory items before submission.

Quality Control Phase: During database establishment, questionnaires were uniformly coded. Dual-person, double-blind verification was performed to check the quality of returned questionnaires. Data exhibiting patterned responses, abnormal completion times, or logical inconsistencies were excluded to ensure accuracy and reliability.

Statistical methods

A database was established using Excel 2019, and statistical analysis was performed with SPSS 27.0. Common method bias (CMB), as a source of systematic error primarily arising from common data sources leading to spurious covariance among constructs, was analyzed using Harman’s single-factor test. To control for its potential interference with the research results, this study adopted a dual-path management approach of “prevention-control”. (1) Procedural control method: Measures such as anonymous responses and inclusion of reverse-scored items were implemented to minimize common method bias. (2) Statistical control method: Harman’s single-factor test was employed, conducting an unrotated principal component factor analysis on the questionnaire items. Categorical data were expressed as proportions and rates, while measurement data conforming to normal distribution were described using mean ± standard deviation. Group comparisons were conducted using independent samples t-test (for two groups) or one-way ANOVA (for multiple groups). Pearson correlation analysis was employed to examine relationships between variables. Variables showing statistically significant differences in t-tests or ANOVA were further analyzed using multiple linear regression, with a significance level set at α = 0.05. Additionally, Amos 26.0 was used to construct a structural equation model, and the model fit was evaluated to ensure its validity.

Results

Factor analysis of the questionnaire

In this study, factor analysis was employed to reduce the dimensionality of the questionnaire items. Principal component analysis was used to extract factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the varimax rotation method was applied. The model converged after five iterations, ultimately extracting four common factors, with a cumulative variance contribution rate of 84.38%. The factor loadings for all items ranged from 0.712 to 0.879. The specific structure was as follows: Component 1 represented professional identity, explaining 24.17% of the variance; Component 2 represented job satisfaction, explaining 21.98% of the variance; Component 3 represented satisfaction with the remuneration system, explaining 19.44% of the variance; and Component 4 represented career planning, explaining 18.79% of the variance. These results were largely consistent with the preliminary conceptual framework of the questionnaire, effectively reflecting the intended theoretical structure of the preinstall dimensions. For further screening of observed indicators, the following criteria were applied: an item was required to have a loading greater than 0.6 on its corresponding factor and loadings below 0.4 on all other factors. Based on these criteria, the final set of qualified observed variables was selected, thereby ensuring good construct validity and discriminant validity of the scale. See Table 1 for details.

Reliability and validity analysis of the questionnaire

In this study, reliability analysis was primarily used to examine the internal consistency, reliability, and stability of the scale. For the four latent variables extracted, Cronbach’s α coefficient was employed to assess reliability. According to commonly accepted criteria, an α value greater than 0.7 indicates high reliability, while a value above 0.85 suggests excellent reliability. The analysis results showed that the α coefficients for all dimensions exceeded 0.7, and the overall questionnaire’s α coefficient reached 0.954, indicating excellent reliability. In terms of validity, this study mainly focused on construct validity, which reflects the degree of alignment between the measurement items and the theoretical constructs. This was evaluated using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity in factor analysis. Generally, a KMO value greater than 0.7 is considered acceptable for factor analysis. The KMO value for this questionnaire was 0.943, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (P < 0.001), indicating that the data were highly suitable for factor analysis. In summary, the questionnaire demonstrated good reliability and validity, providing a solid foundation for subsequent data analysis. See Table 2 for details.

Analysis of job satisfaction scores by demographic characteristics

This study involved 793 healthcare workers from township health centers in Lanzhou City. The majority of participants were female (715, 90.16%), with 78 males (9.84%). Ethnically, Han Chinese constituted the vast majority (97.48%). In terms of profession, nurses (81.59%) significantly outnumbered doctors (18.41%). Regarding educational background, most participants held an associate degree (45.78%) or a bachelor’s degree (49.68%). A relatively small proportion of healthcare workers had more than 20 years of work experience (10.59%). Concerning technical titles, most participants held junior or lower-level titles (73.90%). In terms of income, 53.47% of medical staff reported a post-tax monthly income of 3,000 yuan or less. A 5-point Likert scale was used for the survey, with a scale midpoint of 3. Higher scores indicated higher satisfaction levels. The data showed that the overall mean job satisfaction score among township health center healthcare workers was 3.14, slightly above the midpoint. When comparing job satisfaction scores across different demographic characteristics, the results indicated a statistically significant difference among groups based on years of work experience (P < 0.05), suggesting that work experience may influence the job satisfaction of healthcare workers. Status and correlation analysis of job satisfaction and its influencing factors

The survey results showed the following mean scores for each dimension: the remuneration system dimension scored 3.02 ± 0.90; the career planning dimension scored 2.96 ± 0.96; the professional identity dimension scored 3.38 ± 0.97; and the job satisfaction dimension scored 3.14 ± 0.89. Using 3 as the midpoint, the scores for all dimensions were close to 3, indicating an overall moderate level of satisfaction. Pearson correlation analysis revealed significant positive correlations among all dimensions: the remuneration system was positively correlated with career planning (r = 0.328, P < 0.001), with professional identity (r = 0.204, P < 0.001), and with job satisfaction (r = 0.404, P < 0.01). Career planning was positively correlated with professional identity (r = 0.634, P < 0.001) and with job satisfaction (r = 0.706, P < 0.001). Professional identity was positively correlated with job satisfaction (r = 0.758, P < 0.001). These results indicate significant positive correlations among the dimensions, with the correlation between professional identity and job satisfaction being the most prominent. This suggests that enhancing healthcare workers’ sense of professional identity may significantly improve their job satisfaction. These findings provide a scientific basis for further optimizing the career development and enhancing the job satisfaction of healthcare workers. See Table 4.

Multiple linear regression analysis of factors influencing job satisfaction

To examine whether multicollinearity existed among the independent variables in the model, this study calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance for each predictor variable before performing multiple regression analysis. The results showed that the VIF values for all variables ranged between 1.30 and 2.40, all below the commonly used threshold of 5.0, and tolerance values were all greater than 0.40, with no values approaching critical levels. This indicates that, although there is a certain degree of correlation among the variables, no severe multicollinearity issue is present, and the estimated regression coefficients demonstrate good stability and reliability.

During the regression analysis, demographic variables such as professional title, educational level, and income were first included in the model as control factors to eliminate potential confounding effects. Additionally, based on the results of univariate analysis, the variable “years of work experience,” which showed statistical significance, was also incorporated into the control model. The results indicated that in Model 1, which included only control variables, the model’s goodness-of-fit was low (R² = 0.011), suggesting that demographic variables have limited explanatory power for the variance in job satisfaction, and their independent predictive effect is relatively weak. Therefore, in Model 2, core independent variables—remuneration system, professional identity, and career planning—were introduced while controlling for years of work experience. The goodness-of-fit of Model 2 improved significantly (R² = 0.697, adjusted R² = 0.695, F = 301.079, P < 0.001), indicating that the model effectively explains the variance in job satisfaction. After controlling for years of work experience, the remuneration system, professional identity, and career planning all showed significant positive associations with job satisfaction (P < 0.001). Further analysis revealed that after introducing psychological and developmental variables such as professional identity and career planning, demographic factors including professional title, educational level, and income did not demonstrate significant independent explanatory effects. The reasons for this may be twofold: On one hand, the limited scope for promotion and relatively small income disparities within primary-level township hospitals may result in insufficient variability of these variables, thereby weakening their statistical effects. On the other hand, the influence of structural factors such as professional title and education on job satisfaction may operate indirectly through psychological mechanisms like professional identity, which are partially absorbed in the statistical model. Additionally, the relatively centralized structure of primary healthcare positions, with limited differences in actual rewards and development opportunities across different educational levels and professional titles, also reduces their direct explanatory power regarding job satisfaction. Overall, after controlling for psychological identification and career development factors, demographic variables exhibit relatively weak marginal explanatory power for job satisfaction, while professional identity and career planning constitute the primary pathways explaining variations in job satisfaction. This finding further supports the theoretical proposition of an “identity-development-driven” structure of job satisfaction proposed in this study.

Construction of a structural equation model on factors influencing job satisfaction

To further explore the relationships between job satisfaction and its influencing factors, this study established a structural equation model (SEM) with job satisfaction as the dependent variable, and professional identity, career planning, and the remuneration system as independent variables (factors). Based on constructing dimensions from the observed items under each independent variable, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was first conducted to test the measurement items, assessing factor loadings and the associations between latent variables. In the final SEM, healthcare workers’ job satisfaction was set as the endogenous latent variable. Professional identity and the remuneration system were specified as exogenous latent variables, while career planning was positioned as a mediating variable linking professional identity and job satisfaction. This design allowed for a systematic examination of both direct and indirect effects among the variables. During the initial model fitting, the result showed χ²/df > 5, indicating a poor model fit and necessitating model modification. Model parameters can generally be modified by adding or deleting paths, constraining paths, or setting residual covariances [33]. To preserve the original model structure as much as possible, this study employed the method of setting residual covariances for the secondary fitting. After recalculation, all goodness-of-fit indices met the acceptable standards, indicating a good model fit and a significant improvement in the alignment between the mediation model and the sample data. To further ensure the robustness of the empirical results and the measurement model, a series of systematic diagnostic tests were conducted in this study. First, regarding measurement quality, the convergent validity and discriminant validity among latent variables were evaluated. The results showed that the Composite Reliability (CR) values for all latent variables exceeded 0.70, and the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values were all above 0.50, indicating satisfactory internal consistency and convergent validity across all measurement dimensions. In addition, the square roots of the AVE values for each latent variable were greater than their correlations with other latent variables, demonstrating good discriminant validity of the model. Second, to minimize the potential influence of common method bias on the research findings, several procedural control measures were adopted, including anonymous surveys, reverse-coded items, and standardized data collection procedures. Furthermore, Harmans single-factor test was conducted for statistical assessment. The results indicated that the variance explained by the first common factor was below the critical threshold, suggesting that no serious common method bias existed in this study. In addition, multicollinearity among variables was examined using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). All VIF values were below the commonly accepted critical standard, indicating the absence of significant multicollinearity problems. Finally, the Bootstrap resampling method was employed to perform bias-corrected tests of the mediating effects. The results showed that the 95% confidence intervals for all paths did not include zero, further confirming the statistical significance of the indirect effects and the robustness of the model results. Overall, this study not only verified the basic reliability and validity of the measurement scales, but also conducted a relatively comprehensive robustness assessment of the measurement model from multiple perspectives, including convergent validity, discriminant validity, common method bias, multicollinearity, and Bootstrap robustness testing, thereby enhancing the credibility and stability of the research findings.

Discussion

This study systematically examined the relationship between professional identity, career planning, the remuneration system, and job satisfaction among healthcare workers in township health centers. The results indicate that professional identity has the most significant impact on job satisfaction, while career planning plays an important mediating role. Although the remuneration factor remains statistically significant, its independent explanatory power is relatively limited. More importantly, these findings suggest that the formation mechanism of job satisfaction in primary healthcare settings may fundamentally differ from observations in resource-rich environments. In this context, job satisfaction is not primarily driven by external incentives but is shaped jointly by intrinsic psychological resources and future-oriented expectations [34–36]. this further indicates that in resource-constrained primary healthcare settings, the formation mechanism of job satisfaction has undergone a structural shift from being “predominantly driven by external incentives” to being “identity-development driven.”

Professional identity demonstrated the strongest explanatory power in this study, suggesting that in resource-limited primary healthcare environments, identification with professional values may assume a partially substitutive motivational function. Previous research generally holds that in medical institutions with higher remuneration levels or relatively well-established promotion mechanisms, material rewards and institutional arrangements often form an important foundation for satisfaction, while professional identity tends to function as a supplementary psychological factor [37]. The findings of this study can be interpreted as a form of “identity-driven compensation mechanism,” where professional identity compensates to some extent for the insufficiency of external incentives. Under resource-constrained conditions, the social value and meaning embodied by the profession become crucial psychological resources for sustaining job satisfaction. This mechanism suggests that in primary healthcare contexts, professional identity is not merely a complementary factor but may become the dominant driving force, thereby providing mechanistic support for the “identity-development driven” pathway proposed in this research.

The mediating role of career planning further elucidates the pathway through which professional identity influences job satisfaction. Even in primary healthcare institutions with limited objective promotion opportunities, career planning still plays a significant role. This study finds that career planning not only functions as a tool for professional development but also serves as a “psychological stabilization mechanism.” By forming clear expectations for the future, individuals reduce career uncertainty and maintain a sense of control over their professional development, thereby translating professional identity into sustained satisfaction. This suggests that in resource-constrained contexts, “developmental expectations” possess not only instrumental attributes but also important psychological regulatory functions, forming the “identity-development driven” structure together with professional identity [38–40].

From a theoretical perspective, the significance of this study lies not only in verifying the relationships among professional identity, career planning, and job satisfaction, but more importantly in revealing the structural reconstruction of the logic underlying job satisfaction formation under resource-constrained conditions. Most existing studies on job satisfaction have been developed within relatively resource-abundant organizational environments, implicitly assuming that salary incentives, promotion opportunities, and institutional support constitute the primary sources of job satisfaction, while psychological variables such as professional identity play mainly supplementary roles [41–43]. However, the findings of this study suggest that, within the resource-constrained context of township health centers, the marginal effect of external incentives may gradually weaken, whereas professional identity and development expectations become more dominant psychological driving mechanisms. Based on this observation, the “identity–development-driven” model proposed in this study is not merely a simple extension of traditional satisfaction models, but rather emphasizes the dynamic transformation of the driving logic of job satisfaction across different resource contexts. This model proposes that, in primary healthcare environments characterized by limited institutional resources and restricted promotion opportunities, individuals are more likely to maintain work engagement and occupational stability through recognition of professional value and positive expectations for future development. Therefore, professional identity should be understood not merely as an attitudinal variable, but as a substitute psychological resource, while career planning reflects not only career development capability but also a mechanism through which individuals maintain a sense of control over their future professional trajectories. These findings further extend the contextual applicability of job satisfaction theory and provide additional insight into classical motivation theories. Traditional theories generally assume a relatively stable relationship between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation; however, the present study demonstrates that such relationships are highly context-dependent, and that the dominant role of different motivational factors may shift dynamically across resource environments. Accordingly, this study advances the theoretical focus from merely identifying influencing factors to explaining the underlying mechanisms of job satisfaction formation.

Although the remuneration system remained statistically significant, its explanatory strength was substantially weaker than that of professional identity and career planning. This finding suggests that, in resource-constrained primary healthcare settings, salary-related factors may no longer constitute the dominant mechanism underlying job satisfaction, but instead function more as a foundational or “hygiene-type” factor [44]. This result differs to some extent from traditional explanatory frameworks that regard economic incentives as the core driving force of job satisfaction, and therefore requires further interpretation from both organizational and psychological perspectives. First, from the perspective of Herzberg’s two-factor theory, salary is more appropriately classified as a hygiene factor, whose primary function is to reduce dissatisfaction rather than continuously enhance positive satisfaction. When compensation remains relatively limited but stable over a prolonged period, its marginal motivational effect may gradually diminish [22]. This phenomenon may be particularly evident in township health centers, where overall income disparities are relatively small and promotion opportunities are institutionally constrained, thereby limiting the explanatory power of salary differences regarding variations in job satisfaction. In this sense, salary remains important, but mainly serves the basic function of preventing dissatisfaction rather than independently driving sustained occupational engagement and enhanced satisfaction. econd, the findings may also reflect a form of psychological adaptation mechanism under low-resource conditions. Individuals working in resource-constrained environments for extended periods may gradually adjust their psychological expectations regarding income and material rewards, and increasingly rely on intrinsic resources such as professional meaning, social value, and occupational stability to maintain work engagement. This phenomenon is consistent with the perspective of self-determination theory regarding the importance of intrinsic motivation. Ryan and Deci argued that when opportunities for external rewards are limited, individuals are more likely to sustain work motivation through autonomy, value recognition, and perceptions of personal development [37]. Within primary healthcare settings, healthcare workers may especially internalize professional value and service meaning as important psychological resources for maintaining occupational stability. Therefore, the effects of professional identity and career planning on job satisfaction may partially substitute for the traditional functions of material incentives. In addition, the findings suggest that healthcare workers may place greater emphasis on perceived compensation fairness rather than absolute income level. Previous studies have shown that, in low-income or resource-limited environments, perceptions of fairness often exert stronger influences on work attitudes and organizational identification than income itself [36]. In the present study, the remuneration system dimension included not only income satisfaction, but also perceptions of performance appraisal fairness, effort–reward balance, and the rationality of the compensation system. Therefore, the relatively weaker effect of remuneration variables does not imply that economic factors have lost importance; rather, it may indicate that, under conditions of limited institutional resources, simply increasing income levels may not sustainably improve job satisfaction. Instead, healthcare workers may be more concerned with fairness, stability, and predictability within the compensation allocation process. More importantly, the present findings do not deny the importance of salary, but rather suggest that its functional role may have changed. Specifically, within primary healthcare settings, salary may serve primarily as a “necessary but non-dominant” foundational condition, whereas the key determinants of job satisfaction differences may be whether individuals are able to maintain professional identity, future development expectations, and sustained perceptions of professional value under constrained resource conditions. This finding implies that the mechanism underlying job satisfaction among primary healthcare workers may be gradually shifting from a traditional “external incentive-driven” model toward an “identity–development-driven” model.

The sample structure further revealed a structural contradiction within primary healthcare human resources characterized by “advanced educational investment but lagging institutional incentives.” In this study, the proportion of participants with bachelor’s degrees or above was higher than that reported in some previous primary healthcare investigations, while professional title promotion remained concentrated at the junior level and overall income levels remained relatively limited [45]. This mismatch between rapid educational advancement and insufficient institutional incentive improvement suggests that the accumulated human capital of primary healthcare workers has not received corresponding career development returns, thereby potentially intensifying perceptions of imbalance between individual input and output. Over time, such structural disparities may threaten occupational stability among primary healthcare workers. Therefore, simply improving educational attainment is insufficient to enhance job satisfaction; instead, improvements in human capital accumulation must be accompanied by parallel optimization of career development pathways and institutional incentive arrangements. In addition, the sample structure exhibited a certain degree of representational bias, mainly reflected in the relatively high proportions of females (90.16%) and nurses (81.59%). Although this characteristic is generally consistent with the actual workforce composition of township health centers in China, where nursing staff constitute the majority of frontline healthcare workers and the nursing workforce has long demonstrated a strong feminization trend, it may still have influenced the study findings and therefore requires cautious interpretation. First, systematic differences may exist across genders and professional roles in terms of work experiences, sources of occupational stress, mechanisms of professional identity formation, and opportunities for career development. Compared with physicians, nurses typically undertake more frequent daily caregiving tasks, and their work emphasizes continuous service provision and emotional labor. Consequently, their job satisfaction may be more strongly influenced by psychosocial factors such as professional identity, organizational support, and perceptions of fairness. Meanwhile, male healthcare workers may differ from female workers regarding promotion expectations, income sensitivity, and perceptions of professional autonomy [46]. Therefore, the “identity–development-driven” pathway identified in this study may, to some extent, reflect the satisfaction formation logic of female nursing groups under resource-constrained primary healthcare conditions, and may not be fully applicable to medical institutions with substantially different gender or occupational structures. Second, the imbalance in sample composition may also have influenced the estimated strength of the model path coefficients. Because females and nurses constituted the majority of the sample, the effects of professional identity and career planning on job satisfaction may have been amplified within the overall model, while the effects of external incentives such as salary may have been relatively weakened. Accordingly, the theoretical interpretation emphasizing the dominant role of intrinsic psychological resources should be understood within the combined context of the present sample structure and primary healthcare environment, rather than being generalized indiscriminately to all healthcare institutions. It should also be noted that although subgroup analyses based on gender or professional category could help identify heterogeneity across groups, the relatively limited sample sizes of male healthcare workers and physicians in this study may have resulted in unstable parameter estimation, insufficient statistical power, and increased model fit bias if multi-group structural equation modeling had been conducted. Therefore, this study did not further perform multi-group structural analyses, but instead chose to fully discuss potential sampling bias and its possible implications within the discussion section [47]. Future studies may expand sample coverage and adopt stratified sampling, multicenter surveys, or multi-group structural equation modeling approaches to further examine differences in job satisfaction formation mechanisms across genders, occupational categories, and regional contexts, thereby improving the robustness and generalizability of the findings.

Based on the “identity–development-driven” mechanism proposed in this study, primary healthcare management policies should establish more specific and operational intervention pathways from three dimensions: strengthening professional identity, supporting career development, and optimizing fairness-oriented compensation systems. First, regarding professional identity enhancement, local health administrative departments and healthcare institutions could establish regular professional value feedback mechanisms. For example, institutions may strengthen healthcare workers’ perceptions of professional social value through recognition programs for outstanding primary healthcare workers, promotion of exemplary cases, public display of patient satisfaction feedback, and honorary incentives related to community healthcare services. At the same time, primary healthcare workers should be provided with greater opportunities to participate in hospital management and public health decision-making processes, thereby enhancing their sense of professional autonomy and organizational belonging [48]. Second, regarding career development support, a clearer and more institutionalized career development system should be established for primary healthcare workers. Specifically, regional medical alliances or county-level healthcare consortiums could implement hierarchical training systems for primary healthcare personnel, regularly providing opportunities for continuing education, rotational training, and specialized skill development. In addition, clear standards for professional title promotion and career advancement pathways should be established to reduce uncertainty regarding occupational development. Furthermore, institutions may explore the establishment of phased career development goal management mechanisms for primary healthcare positions, thereby helping healthcare workers form more predictable career growth expectations and enhancing their long-term retention intentions. Third, regarding compensation system optimization, the findings of this study suggest that primary healthcare workers are more concerned with fairness and transparency in the compensation allocation process. Therefore, compared with simply increasing income levels, primary healthcare institutions should place greater emphasis on improving performance distribution mechanisms [49]. For example, performance evaluation systems could be linked to service quality, community health management outcomes, and contributions to public health services, while simultaneously improving the transparency and openness of performance assessment standards and feedback procedures, thereby strengthening healthcare workers’ perceptions of institutional fairness. Moreover, given the limited healthcare resources available in primary settings, policymakers should avoid relying solely on economic incentives as the primary governance strategy. Instead, a comprehensive workforce stabilization mechanism integrating professional identity, career development, and fairness-oriented support should be established. Only by simultaneously enhancing perceptions of professional value, expectations for future development, and institutional fairness can long-term job satisfaction and occupational stability among primary healthcare workers be effectively improved.

The theoretical contributions of this study are primarily reflected in two aspects. First, within the context of primary care resource constraints, this study highlights the core role of professional identity in the formation of job satisfaction, indicating that existing satisfaction explanation frameworks based on urban medical institutions have limited applicability in primary care settings. Second, this study explicitly verifies the mediating role of career planning, demonstrating that the job satisfaction of primary healthcare workers is influenced not only by their current work experience but also significantly by their perception of future career paths. This provides empirical evidence with stronger mechanistic explanatory power for research on talent management in primary healthcare.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. First, regarding sample selection, this study focused exclusively on healthcare workers from township health centers in Lanzhou City. Because regions differ in economic development level, primary healthcare resource allocation, and healthcare management systems, the “identity–development-driven” mechanism proposed in this study may be more applicable to relatively resource-constrained western regions, whereas its applicability in economically developed areas or regions with more abundant healthcare resources remains to be further examined. Therefore, the external generalizability of the findings is subject to certain limitations. Second, the relatively high proportions of females and nurses in the sample, although generally consistent with the actual workforce structure of township health centers, may still have influenced the estimated relationships among variables. For example, nursing positions generally involve continuous caregiving and emotional labor, and therefore the influence of professional identity on job satisfaction may be stronger among nurses than among physicians. Consequently, the dominant effect of professional identity observed in this study may partly reflect the occupational experience characteristics of female nursing groups within primary healthcare contexts, and may not be fully applicable to healthcare institutions with different occupational structures. Third, this study adopted a cross-sectional survey design and therefore cannot establish strict causal relationships among variables. Although structural equation modeling can reveal potential pathways among variables, bidirectional relationships may still exist among professional identity, career planning, and job satisfaction. For instance, higher job satisfaction may in turn strengthen professional identity and future career expectations. Therefore, the present findings are more suitable for understanding structural associations among variables rather than directly inferring strict causal mechanisms. In addition, this study mainly focused on professional identity, career planning, and remuneration systems, while other potentially important variables such as organizational support, workload, occupational burnout, and family stress were not included. These unmeasured factors may exert additional influences on job satisfaction and may also affect the explanatory strength of the current path coefficients. Accordingly, the “identity–development-driven” mechanism proposed in this study should be understood as an important component of the job satisfaction formation process in primary healthcare settings rather than the sole explanatory framework. Future studies may further adopt multi-regional, longitudinal, and multi-group comparative designs, combined with longitudinal tracking data, to more systematically examine the dynamic changes in job satisfaction formation mechanisms across different resource contexts, thereby improving the robustness and practical applicability of the findings. Therefore, the present study is more appropriate for understanding the formation mechanism of job satisfaction within primary healthcare institutions characterized by workforce structures dominated by female nursing personnel, whereas its applicability to physician-dominated institutions or organizations with more balanced gender structures requires further validation.

In conclusion, this study indicates that, within the relatively resource-constrained context of western primary healthcare systems, the mechanism underlying job satisfaction has undergone a structural shift from a “salary-driven” model toward an “identity–development-driven” model. Professional identity significantly influences job satisfaction through career planning, and its explanatory power exceeds that of the remuneration system. In contrast, although the remuneration system remains statistically significant, its role appears to function more as a “hygiene-type” factor that maintains basic perceptions of fairness and organizational stability, rather than serving as the primary driver of sustained improvement in job satisfaction. These findings suggest that job satisfaction among primary healthcare workers does not rely solely on material incentives, but is more strongly shaped by the combined influence of intrinsic professional identity and expectations for future development. This result not only extends the applicability of job satisfaction theory within primary healthcare settings, but also provides more targeted empirical evidence for workforce stabilization strategies in resource-limited regions.
