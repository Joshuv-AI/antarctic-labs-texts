Network Dynamics in the Colombian Armed Conflict: Armed Structures and Municipalities
Natalia Perdomo-Londoño, Juan Sosa, Emma J. Camargo-Díaz

Abstract
In this article, we explore how the escalating victimization of civilians during civil wars is mirrored in the fragmented distribution of territorial control, focusing on the Colombian armed conflict. Through an exhaustive characterization of the topology of bipartite and projected networks of both armed groups and municipalities, we are able to describe changes in territorial configurations across different periods between 1980 and 2007. Our findings show that during periods dominated by a small set of actors, networks adopt a centralized node-periphery structure, while during times of widespread conflict, areas of influence of armed groups overlap in complex ways. Also, by employing stochastic block models for count data, we identify cohesive municipal communities during 2000-2001, shaped by both geographic proximity and affinities between armed structures, as well as the existence of internally dispersed groups with a high likelihood of interaction.



Keywords
: Armed conflict; Bipartite networks; Community detection; Social networks; Stochastic block model; Territorial dispute.

1.  Introduction

During internal armed conflicts or civil wars, sovereignty becomes fragmented as the use of force is no longer monopolized by the State.
Various actors compete for territorial control not only through direct military confrontations but also by involving non-combatants, leading to territorial disputes that predominantly affect civilians.
Due to the challenges faced by irregular armies in sustaining prolonged warfare efforts, they resort to violence against civilian populations, among other strategies, to consolidate their power in a region
(
Kalyvas, 2006
, p.87)
.

Population control is crucial for armed groups, as communities become valuable sources of multiple resources, such as shelter, supplies, and information. The ability of an armed group to challenge another for control and establish an alternative order largely depends on the effectiveness of its strategies of subjugation over the population. Through intimidation, these groups achieve obedience, punish infractions, and deter collaboration with adversaries. The type and intensity of violence they employ become means to achieve and maintain control
(
Arjona, 2016
)
.

Kalyvas, 2006
examines how attacks on civilian populations vary depending on the extent of control an actor has over a territory. In contexts where an actor has limited control or is in constant competition, violence often manifests as indiscriminate and massive. Armed groups resort to violence to secure their position and neutralize any support the population might provide to the enemy. In contrast, when control is exclusive, selective violence proves more effective, resulting in significantly lower levels of victimization. In situations where the population is under the exclusive control of an actor, it is more advantageous to exploit the resources that the population can offer, such as recruiting combatants through forced enlistment, for example.

According to Kalyvas, civil wars are characterized by high levels of territorial fragmentation during the peak of armed confrontation. Armed groups create “corridors” and induce relationships between regions that are not necessarily contiguous by expanding their sphere of influence, establishing cooperative or competitive relations with other groups, or alternatively, by generating divisions and segmenting space. Instead of being non-existent, the borders separating two (or more) locations in an irregular war are diffuse and fluid. When disputes are intense, a fragmented spatial distribution is evident: Actors intermittently converge in various locations. Irregular warfare fundamentally alters the nature of sovereignty, and the fragmentation of space and territory reflects this phenomenon. Conversely, in scenarios where an actor has consolidated territorial control, highly distinguishable zones of influence emerge.

Graphs allows for the representation of complex relationships between armed actors and regions during an armed conflict. This approach facilitates the visualization of how interactions are configured and evolve within a fragmented territory, where the presence and control of different actors vary over time and according to the dynamics of the conflict. Statistical network analysis
(
Menzel, 2010
;
Kolaczyk and Csárdi, 2014
;
Luke, 2015
, e.g.,)
, in particular, provides suitable tools for describing these relationships. For instance, stochastic block models
(
Holland et al., 1983
;
Nowicki and Snijders, 2001
;
Karrer and Newman, 2011
;
Aicher et al., 2014
)
enable the detection of fragmentation or segmentation patterns by identifying communities of nodes that share similar characteristics regarding their connections.

In Colombia, one of the most notable aspects of recent internal armed conflicts is the high level of victimization against the population as a strategy to contest territorial control
(
CNMH, 2013
)
. This study utilizes network analysis to examine how the relationships among the most prominent irregular armed groups and the regions they occupied evolved between 1980 and 2007. Additionally, by employing stochastic block models for count data, the study assesses whether municipalities within the influence zones of these armed actors formed clearly differentiated communities or whether these territories were interlinked, reflecting the fragmented and fluid nature of the conflict.

Based on information collected by the
Centro Nacional de Memoria Histórica
(CNMH) regarding the most impactful acts of victimization against non-combatants (the acts of victimization documented by the CNMH include massacres, selective assassinations, forced disappearances, kidnappings, and forced recruitments), we define nine bipartite networks between municipalities and armed structures from 1980 to 2007, along with their respective projections. Our analysis is limited to the most prominent irregular armies involved in the conflict: The
Fuerzas Armadas Revolucionarias de Colombia
(FARC) and the
Autodefensas Unidas de Colombia
(AUC). Although the AUC existed nominally from 1997 to 2005, it had clear antecedents from the early 1980s, and therefore, we included all paramilitary structures that preceded the AUC.

This article is divided into four sections. The first section provides all the details about the methodology we employ for detecting communities in weighted, undirected graphs. The second section describes the data processing methods and presents a full description of the entire network structure. The third section presents our main findings regarding the municipal networks for 2000-2001. Finally, the last section discusses our main findings along with lines for future research.

2.  Methodology

In this section, we present the key methodological aspects about community detection. We primarily focus on a detailed characterization of graphs and bipartite graphs, followed by an emphasis on the key features of clustering as well as stochastic block models for count data. For a gentle introduction to these topics, we recommend consulting works such as
Menzel, 2010
,
Kolaczyk and Csárdi, 2014
, and
Luke, 2015
, among others.

2.1
Graphs and bipartite graphs

A graph is a mathematical structure consisting of vertices (nodes or actors) and edges (links or connections) that connect pairs of vertices. Graphs are widely used in fields to characterize relationships between objects (relational data). Formally, a graph
G
is given by
G=(V,E)
, where
V
is the set of vertices and
E
is the set of edges. The edges are defined as pairs of vertices,
e=(u,v)
, with
u,v\in V
.

A graph can be undirected, where
(u,v)\in E
implies
(v,u)\in E
(edges have no direction), or directed, where
(u,v)\in E
does not necessarily imply
(v,u)\in E
(edges have a specific direction). Furthermore, the graph is called simple if there are no loops (edges that connect a vertex to itself) and no multiple edges between the same pair of vertices. Lastly, a weighted graph assigns a value (weight) to each edge, expressed as a function
w:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, such as counts, costs or distances. An unweighted graph is a graph in which edges have no associated weights, representing only the presence or absence of a connection between vertices.

A bipartite graph is a specific type of graph in which the set of vertices can be divided into two disjoint sets, usually denoted as
U
and
V
, such that every edge connects a vertex in
U
to a vertex in
V
. This means no edge exists between vertices within the same set. This property can be characterized using coloring: A graph is bipartite if and only if it can be 2-colored, meaning its vertices can be colored using two colors such that no two adjacent vertices share the same color.
Bipartite graphs are used in applications such as modeling relationships between two distinct types of objects (e.g., users and products).

We refer the reader to
Bondy and Murty, 1976
,
West, 2001
, and
Diestel, 2017
, among other comprehensive sources, for an in-depth exploration of graph theory and related topics.

2.2
Clustering

Clustering of networks or community detection is a process in network analysis aimed at identifying subsets of nodes (clusters, communities, or groups) that exhibit a higher density of connections internally than with the rest of the network. The goal is to find meaningful groupings within a graph that can reveal hidden structures.
Formally, clustering seeks to partition the set of nodes
V
into subsets
C_{1},\dots,C_{Q}
such that nodes within each cluster
C_{i}
are highly connected (internal cohesion), and connections between nodes in different clusters
C_{i}
and
C_{j}
are sparse (external separation). Here,
Q
denotes the number of communities.

One of the most widely used criteria for evaluating network partitions is modularity
(
Newman and Girvan, 2004
)
. It measures the density of edges inside communities compared to a random graph. High modularity indicates strong community structure. The modularity
M
for a partition is given by:

M=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i,j}\left[A_{ij}-\frac{k_{i}k_{j}}{2m}\right]\delta(c_{i},c_{j})\,,

where
A_{ij}
is the adjacency matrix,
k_{i}
and
k_{j}
are the degrees of nodes
i
and
j
,
m
is the total number of edges, and
\delta(c_{i},c_{j})
is 1 if
i
and
j
are in the same community and 0 otherwise. The most important methods that use modularity to determine optimal partitions include hierarchical clustering
(
Blondel et al., 2008
, e.g.,)
, spectral clustering
(
Ng et al., 2002
, e.g.,)
, and the Louvain method
(
Fortunato, 2010
, e.g.,)
.

2.2.1
Fast Greedy algorithm

The Fast Greedy algorithm
Blondel et al., 2008
;
Fortunato, 2010
offers an efficient method for identifying communities in large networks by optimizing modularity within a hierarchical clustering framework. This algorithm consists of two main phases: Initialization, where each node begins in its own community, and greedy optimization, where communities are iteratively merged based on the increase in modularity. In this phase, the algorithm evaluates the potential gain in modularity for each possible merger and combines the pair of communities that results in the highest increase. This process continues until no further improvements can be made.

2.3
Stochastic Block Models

A stochastic block model (SBM) is a framework for inferring groups or communities within networks that belongs to the broader class of random graph models. Following
Kolaczyk and Csárdi, 2014
, consider a set of
N
individuals. Let
G=(V,E)
denote the graph representing the system of relationships among these
N
individuals, where
V
represents the set of nodes and
E
represents the set of edges between them, i.e., the unordered pairs
(i,j)
of distinct vertices
i,j\in V
.

According to the formulation of a SBM, each vertex
i\in V
belongs to one and only one of the
Q
available disjoint and exhaustive classes (also known as groups, components, clusters, or communities), denoted as
\textsf{C}_{1},\dots,\textsf{C}_{Q}
. Let
\Lambda
be a
Q\times Q
matrix, whose entries
\Lambda_{q,r}
represent the probabilities of interaction between vertices in class
q
and vertices in class
r
. This matrix unequivocally determines the network structure and fully characterizes its properties. For example, if
\Lambda
is a diagonal matrix, the resulting network will have disconnected components. In this case, adding off-diagonal elements (typically small), the resulting network will have communities with dense internal connections and sparse external connections
(
Karrer and Newman, 2011
)
. Other choices for the probability matrix can produce alternative structures, such as core-periphery or hierarchical configurations.
For an undirected graph, it follows that
\Lambda_{q,r}=\Lambda_{r,q}
, which makes the adjacency matrix
Y
symmetric (which means that the strength of the connections between any two classes is equivalent).

The class membership of each vertex
i
is independently determined by a discrete probability distribution over the set
\{1,\ldots,Q\}
, where
Q
represents the total number of possible classes or communities. Formally, the distribution of the variable
Z_{i}=(Z_{i,1},\ldots,Z_{i,Q})
is categorical with parameters
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{Q}
. These parameters represent the probabilities associated with each class, where
\theta_{q}>0
and
\sum_{q}\theta_{q}=1
. Thus,
\Pr(Z_{i,q}=1\mid\theta_{q})=\theta_{q}
, where
Z_{i,q}=1
indicates that vertex
i
belongs to community
q
, and
Z_{i,q}=0
otherwise.

A SBM for counts-weighted networks specifies that each element
Y_{i,j}
of the adjacency matrix
Y
is, conditional on the class labels of vertices
i
and
j
, a random variable following a Poisson distribution with parameter
\Lambda_{q,r}
. That is, under a Poisson SBM, the interaction probabilities are modeled by
Y_{i,j}\mid Z_{i,q}=1,Z_{j,r}=1\sim\textsf{Poisson}(\Lambda_{q,r})
. Let
L_{q,r}
denote the number of edges connecting pairs of vertices from classes
q
and
r
. In this framework,

L_{q,r}\sim\textsf{Poisson}(\Lambda_{q,r}\,n_{q}\,n_{r})\,,

where
n_{q}
and
n_{r}
are the number of vertices in classes
q
and
r
, respectively. Thus, the distribution of the adjacency matrix can be expressed as

\Pr(Y=y\mid\Lambda)=\prod_{q\leq r}\frac{(e^{-\Lambda_{q,r}\,n_{q}\,n_{r}})(\Lambda_{q,r}\,n_{q}\,n_{r})^{L_{q,r}}}{L_{q,r}!}\,.

In this context, the links between nodes represent counts (e.g., the number of shared structures between two municipalities). This approach allows for the modeling of networks where the connections represent counts of shared attributes between nodes, rather than simply the presence or absence of a relation. This extension provides a powerful tool for analyzing complex networks with count data, offering deeper insights about the relationships between entities based on shared characteristics.

3.  Network construction and processing

El Observatorio de Memoria y Conflicto
(OMC) is an initiative of
El Centro Nacional de Memoria Histórica
(CNMH), with the primary mission of documenting and analyzing the dynamics of violence and armed conflict in Colombia. Its work involves constructing and publishing databases that compile detailed information on human rights violations, breaches of international humanitarian law, and other acts of violence that have occurred within the context of the conflict.

Considering that the use of violence has manifested in several forms, some more brutal than others, this research focuses on the most serious human rights violations, specifically those pertaining to the right to life, physical integrity, liberty, and personal security
(
Consejo de Estado, 2019
)
. Consequently, this study utilizes data on massacres, enforced disappearances, targeted killings, kidnappings, and illegal recruitments attributed to the armed substructures of the FARC and paramilitary groups that operated between 1980 and 2007.

Direct military actions between conflict actors are not considered, as they reveal different aspects of the behavior of armed groups in civil wars. According to
Kalyvas, 2006
, direct confrontation among belligerents focuses on defeating the adversary, involving a range of military strategies and combat tactics aimed primarily at armed opponents. In contrast, violence against the civilian population follows a different logic: It does not necessarily seek military advantage but rather aims to consolidate territorial control, undermine support for other armed actors, and secure local resources. This type of violence is selective and designed to influence the behavior of the population in accordance with the interests of the armed group perpetrating it, whether through intimidation, retaliation, or cooptation.

3.2
Time periods

This research is organized into periods based on key political events that profoundly impacted the structures and dynamics of armed groups. Each political change influenced the organization and operations of these groups, facilitating an analysis of how political tensions affected their development and responses to changes in the environment. Most of the historical facts and patterns described in the following sections are based on the comprehensive analysis provided by the
Centro Nacional de Memoria Histórica
.

This research is organized into periods based on key political events that profoundly impacted the structures and dynamics of armed groups. Each political change influenced the organization and operations of these groups, facilitating an analysis of how political tensions affected their development and response to changes in the environment.

The first period, from 1980 to 1984, was marked by the enactment of the
Estatuto de Seguridad
, the rise of the political-military strategy of guerrilla groups, and reports of human rights violations committed by armed
structures closely linked to the public security forces and other state agencies,
such as
La Mano Negra
and
Los Escuadrones de la Muerte
.
Additionally, this period witnessed the breakdown of the truce between insurgent
groups and drug trafficking, as well as an escalation in armed confrontation between the FARC and public security forces, alongside the formation of the first
paramilitary organizations,
including
Los Escopeteros
,
Los Sanjuaneros
,
Los Tiznados
,
Las Autodefensas Campesinas del Magdalena Medio
, among others.

Between 1985 and 1987, the conflict was characterized by selective political violence, particularly targeting leaders of the political party
Unión Patriótica
(UP), which was established during this period. These years were also marked by the convergence of violence stemming from the armed conflict and drug trafficking, reaching a critical juncture with the M-19’s seizure of
Palacio de Justicia
and the subsequent military response.

Between 1991 and 1993, Colombia experienced a period marked by the demobilization of several guerrilla and paramilitary groups (including the M-19, ACMM,
El Ejército Popular de Liberación
,
El Partido Revolucionario de Trabajadores
,
El Quintín Lame
,
Los Tangueros
, among others), alongside the capture of
Casa Verde
, which symbolized a shift in the State’s military strategy against the FARC. This period also witnessed the military growth of the FARC, setting the stage for the escalation of conflict in the years that followed. Additionally, the deaths of prominent paramilitary leaders led to the reorganization of their structures.

Between 1994 and 1996, the conflict was characterized by a significant rise in paramilitarism, facilitated by the involvement of certain sectors of the State with these groups. The paramilitaries expanded into various regions, exacerbating violence across the country. Simultaneously, competition intensified between drug cartels and the State over control of drug trafficking routes. This period was marked by an escalation in violence and the complicity of certain state actors, which hindered the government’s efforts to address the conflict.

Between 1997 and 1999,
Las Autodefensas Unidas de Colombia
(AUC) was formed, a federation of paramilitary groups that centralized and strengthened counterinsurgency efforts across various regions of the country. This period was also marked by the expansion of paramilitarism and a surge in violence against the civilian population, which intensified with the onset of peace talks with the FARC and the growing influence of the AUC.

Between 2000 and 2001, the armed conflict in Colombia reached its peak, with a surge in violence during the peace process with the FARC and the consolidation of paramilitary forces, often in collaboration with the public security forces. Both the FARC and paramilitaries intensified their control over various regions, leading to an increase in massacres and forced displacements, which severely impacted the civilian population. This period was marked by escalating violence and the breakdown of order in many parts of the country.

Between 2002 and 2004, the collapse of peace negotiations with guerrilla groups, the initiation of formal talks with the AUC, and the implementation of the Democratic Security Policy marked the onset of a new phase in the conflict. Although national levels of violence declined during this period as a result of the paramilitary demobilization process, significant violence persisted in various regions. In these areas, confrontations arose among paramilitary factions that had fractured from the AUC. Notably, in 2002, the
Bloque Central Bolívar
emerged as a consolidated and independent paramilitary organization.

Finally, between 2005 and 2007, the demobilization of the AUC marked the beginning of a new phase in the armed conflict. Although the main paramilitary blocs were dismantled, this did not signal an end to the violence. Many ex-combatants chose to rearm, giving rise to new armed groups that filled the void left by the AUC. This period was characterized by a reconfiguration of the conflict’s dynamics, with the emergence of criminal gangs that continued to exert control over various regions of the country.

4.  Relational data configuration

Once the time periods are defined, we construct nine incidence matrices
\mathbf{A}_{t}
to generate weighted bipartite graphs. A bipartite network is a type of graph in which nodes are divided into two disjoint sets, with connections occurring only between vertices from different sets. In this case, the rows of the matrices represent municipalities,
M
, and the columns represent armed structures,
E
. Each entry in the matrix
\mathbf{A}_{t}=[A_{i,j,t}]
corresponds to the number of victims attributed to the
j
-th armed substructure
e_{j}\in E
in the
i
-th municipality
m_{i}\in M
during period
t
.

When projecting the network onto the armed structures, we generate an undirected weighted graph
G^{E}_{t}
using the projection
\mathbf{A}_{t}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{A}_{t}
, for each time
t
. In this graph, two armed structures
e_{i},e_{j}\in E
are connected if and only if they have victimized the civilian population in the same municipality during a specified period. The intensity of the connection in this projection corresponds to the number of municipalities shared by the two structures. Specifically, each entry in the adjacency matrix of the projected network
\mathbf{Y}^{E}_{t}=[Y^{E}_{i,j,t}]
reflects the number of municipalities where the structures
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
were active during time
t
.

Similarly, when projecting onto the municipalities, we generate an undirected weighted graph
G^{M}_{t}
using the projection
\mathbf{A}_{t}\mathbf{A}_{t}^{\textsf{T}}
, for each time
t
. In this graph, two municipalities
m_{i},m_{j}\in M
are connected if and only if both have been the site of activity by the same armed structure during a specified period. The intensity of the connection in this projection corresponds to the number of common groups. Specifically, each entry in the adjacency matrix of the projected network
\mathbf{Y}^{M}_{t}=[Y^{M}_{i,j,t}]
reflects the number of substructures that have operated in both
m_{i}
and
m_{j}
during time
t
.

Characterization of bipartite networks

Here, we characterize the bipartite system across all time periods. Specifically, we examine the bipartite networks through metrics such as order, size, density, degree, and strength, as well as their corresponding implications for the armed conflict. The visualization of the bipartite networks is provided in the Appendix.

Characterization of projections

Degree and strength

Figure

5.  Modeling the projection networks and detecting communities

The projections of bipartite networks often reveal structures made up of several complete subgraphs, some overlapping and others disjoint. This structure arises because nodes sharing connections to the same entities in the bipartite graph become fully connected in the projection. In the case of conflict networks, this projection exposes zones of different intensities of territorial dispute. Municipalities vertices linked to the same armed unit form complete subgraphs, reflecting areas where it exerted influence. When different armed groups operate in the same municipalities, the corresponding complete subgraphs overlap, creating regions of heightened connectivity in the projection. These areas of shared influence are denser and formed of stronger ties, increasing the likelihood that municipalities will cluster into the same community. In contrast, municipalities, where a single armed structure is engaged in violence against the civilian population, tend to form distinct subgraphs (even isolated), delineating the boundaries of regions where competition is negligible or conducted only through military operations.

The resulting structures shed light on the dynamics of territorial competition and strategic interaction among armed actors. When overlapping structures dominate the networks, it suggests an environment of more dispute and shifting alignments. Conversely, when distinct complete graphs predominate the projection reflects a more segmented territorial arrangement. The interplay between overlapping and separate complete graphs in the projection captures the fragmentation and fluidity of territorial influence.

The network’s descriptive analysis uncovered a complex and continuously shifting structure, in which overlapping and disjoint subgraphs reflect varying degrees of territorial dispute. To accurately assess how these attributes could shape municipal clustering within the network, we fit a set of Stochastic Block Models with a Poisson distribution, as they capture community structure in weighted networks by modeling the intensity of interactions between nodes. This approach helps determine whether armed-influence municipalities form distinct communities or remain interconnected, offering a systematic way to examine the extent of territorial fragmentation and consolidation. Unlike traditional metrics used to characterize network topology, it also quantifies the uncertainty associated with clustering, enhancing the robustness of the analysis.

The number of communities increased steadily over time, except between 1994-1996 when the network’s topology shifted as the area of influence of the
‘ACCU’
expanded and allegiances between paramilitary units strengthened. From an initial structure of just two clusters in the first period, the network expanded to 33 by 2002-2004. During the first decade of the analysis, the number of detected communities increased fivefold, rising from two in 1978-1981 to ten by 1988-1990. The most significant expansion followed in the late 1990s, as the number of communities surged from 10 in 1994-1996 to 25 in 1997-1999, continuing its growth to a peak in 2002-2004. By 2005-2007, the number declined to 19, following the demobilization of paramilitary groups.

Throughout the years of study, the distribution of municipalities across communities underwent a significant transformation. In the early years, a single dominant community encompassed a substantial share of municipalities, with proportions ranging from 30% to nearly 50%. In contrast, in later years, the size distribution became more balanced. The dominance of a single large community diminished, and municipalities were more evenly distributed among a greater number of smaller communities. The rise of small to medium-sized communities and the decline of overwhelmingly dominant ones reflect a structural reconfiguration, where control became more contested or distributed across multiple actors rather than concentrated within a few large factions.

Neither party involved complied with the truce. During this period, both FARC armed substructures and paramilitary groups expanded significantly. 6.  Discussion

The analysis of the topology of both bipartite networks and projected networks demonstrated how the structural aspects of the network changed substantially over the time periods. The transformations of armed groups during various processes of expansion, consolidation, disarmament, and rearmament that occurred between 1980 and 2007 are clearly reflected in the structural characteristics of the networks. In periods when a limited group of armed structures achieves significant domination of space, the network adopts a centralized node-periphery configuration with a high degree of centralization. This pattern is particularly evident in 1985-1987 and 1994-1996, when bilateral interactions concentrate in a few central nodes that control a large part of the network. However, during times of greater territorial differentiation, such as between 1997 and 1999, the network transforms into a union of cliques, where a small number of nodes act as intermediaries between relatively disconnected regions, suggesting areas of intense dispute. Finally, when armed structures begin to share a greater number of territories, as occurred between 2000 and 2004, we observe a generalized increase in the strength of the links throughout the network, indicating an expansion of territorial disputes to a larger number of regions. These findings reflect how fragmentation and reconfiguration of territorial control influence the structure of interactions among armed actors, adapting to the changing dynamics of civil war.

On the other hand, the stochastic block model applied to the network of municipalities (2000-2001) where armed actors operated in Colombia allowed for the detection of groups of municipalities based on the observed connections between them, taking into account the strength of these interactions. The model revealed the existence of some highly cohesive communities, defined not only by the geographical proximity of the armed substructures but also by affinities among their leaderships. However, we identified groups with high internal dispersion, indicating communities with very few connections among themselves. In contrast, in some cases, interaction between groups was quite high. All of this suggests that during periods of greater territorial dispute, there were no clear divisions between opposing substructures, as is the case in wars between states characterized by distinct front lines and trench lines. These findings support Kalyvas’s thesis on the fragmented and fluid nature of territory in civil wars and internal armed conflicts.

The model adequately reproduces the structural characteristics of the network, such as density and transitivity, but shows limitations in reproducing the strength distribution, as it underestimates the weight of the right tail. This indicates that the model does not fully capture the centralization of power in certain nodes. This aspect is particularly relevant in the context of civil war, where it is important to evaluate whether certain territories act as focal points of concentrated power or if the dispute occurs more generally. Future studies could generalize the model to account for this characteristic.

It was necessary to limit the research object to two factions of the confrontation because the process of refining the variables that indicate which armed structures are attributed to each event is very low quality; thus, including more actors in the analysis, such as other guerrillas (ELN, EPL, M-19, etc.) and law enforcement, requires a significant effort in data cleaning. These actors are expected to be incorporated in future research.

Likewise, the problem of missing data is particularly notable for enforced disappearances and sexual violence. On the one hand, this issue arises because the victim simply does not know their aggressor. On the other hand, it is due to the difficulties in documenting such incidents and the institutional limitations in managing the information. Missing data can lead to a biased characterization of the phenomenon. Therefore, it is necessary to address this issue in future research.

The sustained presence of multiple, overlapping armed structures (including guerrillas, paramilitaries, and organized crime) throughout the study period underscores that the Colombian conflict cannot be reduced to a binary confrontation. Rather, it was a multi-layered system shaped by shifting alliances, localized disputes, and economic interests, particularly those linked to illicit economies.
These conclusions emerge from the integration of historical context, with quantitative network metrics that reveal structural properties underpinning the territorial dynamics of the war. Together, they point to the need for peacebuilding strategies that go beyond disarmament to address the socio-economic and institutional conditions that enable the persistence and reconfiguration of armed violence.

This research is important because it introduces a methodological and analytical framework that uncovers dimensions of internal armed conflicts often missed by traditional qualitative or chronological approaches. By integrating detailed historical records from the
Centro Nacional de Memoria Histórica
with advanced network analysis, the study not only identifies who the actors are and where they operate, but also reveals how their relationships and territorial control shift over time. This dynamic perspective allows for the detection of structural patterns (such as fragmentation, consolidation, and overlapping spheres of influence) that are critical to understanding the persistence and transformation of violence. Unlike conventional narratives that portray conflicts as linear sequences of events, this approach treats them as evolving systems, where the configuration of relationships between armed actors and territories plays a decisive role in shaping the conflict’s trajectory.

The utility of this work extends beyond the Colombian case. Other researchers can apply its methodology to study conflicts in different regions, to compare dynamics across countries, or to examine other periods of Colombia’s history. The framework is adaptable to the inclusion of additional layers of analysis, such as economic networks, political alliances, illicit economies, or population displacement patterns, enriching the understanding of the socio-political ecosystem of war. By documenting how armed groups form, dissolve, and reorganize into new structures, this study challenges static conceptions of conflict and offers a replicable model for analyzing the fluidity of violence in complex settings. These insights are not only academically relevant; they also provide concrete value for policymakers, peace negotiators, and transitional justice practitioners seeking to design interventions that target the structural and relational conditions that allow armed violence to persist or re-emerge.

Finally, we do not pretend with this investigation to reduce the victims of the Colombian armed conflict, nor the gravity of the crimes committed, to mere numbers or statistical outputs. Quantitative methods are used here as a means to analyze the structure and dynamics of the conflict as a whole, identifying patterns, relationships, and transformations that cannot be observed through isolated accounts alone. However, we fully recognize that behind every data point there is a person, a family, and a community that has endured profound suffering and irreparable loss. This acknowledgment is central to our approach: the statistical models and network visualizations are tools, not ends in themselves. The ultimate goal of this investigation is to produce rigorous, evidence-based analysis that can inform public policy, guide institutional decision-making, and provide government actors and transitional justice mechanisms with resources to design interventions aimed at guaranteeing that such crimes are never repeated.
