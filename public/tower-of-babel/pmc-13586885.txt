Program-Level Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy Use and 10-Year Survival in Advanced Ovarian Cancer
Authors: Alex E. R. Powers, Gretchen Davis, Deshae Jenkins, Alicia Youssef, William Manning, Jason D. Wright, Nancy L. Keating, Amy Bregar, Alejandro Rauh-Hain, Alexander Melamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This comparative effectiveness research examines the association between neoadjuvant chemotherapy adoption by US cancer programs and 10-year survival among patients with advanced ovarian cancer.

Introduction

Advanced-stage epithelial ovarian cancer is managed with cytoreductive surgery and platinum-based chemotherapy, although the optimal treatment sequencing remains debated. Randomized clinical trials (RCTs) demonstrated that neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) followed by interval surgery achieves overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival comparable to primary debulking surgery (PDS) while reducing perioperative morbidity.1,2 National guidelines recommend PDS as the preferred treatment strategy when complete or optimal cytoreduction is achievable3 partly because of concerns that chemotherapy exposure before surgery may select for resistant clones and compromise long-term outcomes.4,5

Following publication of an RCT demonstrating noninferior oncologic outcomes with NACT,6 US cancer programs varied considerably in adopting this strategy.7 A previous study leveraging this hospital-level heterogeneity found an association of greater NACT use with improvements in 90-day and 1-year mortality without compromising median OS.7 We extended that work by examining the association between NACT adoption and 10-year survival.

Methods

In this comparative effectiveness research, patients with stage IIIC or IV epithelial ovarian cancer diagnosed between January 1, 2004, and December 31, 2015, were identified in the National Cancer Database and followed up through December 31, 2023. The Mass General Brigham Research Committee exempted the study from review and informed consent based on publicly available deidentified data. This study followed the ISPOR reporting guideline.

We excluded patients with missing follow-up, not treated at the reporting facility, and treated at facilities managing fewer than 1 case of advanced ovarian cancer annually. Using an intention-to-treat framework, patients who initially received chemotherapy were classified as having received NACT, regardless of whether surgery was performed. Race and ethnicity were abstracted from the medical record (American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Black, Hispanic, White, and unknown) to characterize the study population and account for potential differences in case-mix across programs.

Differential adoption of NACT, following a landmark trial published in 2010, enabled a difference-in-differences (DID) design in which programs with minimal increases in NACT use, from the prepublication (2004-2009) to postpublication (2010-2015) period, were comparators for programs with large increases.6 We used DID to examine the association between NACT adoption and OS. This design assumes parallel trends: absent divergence in NACT adoption survival in high- and low-use programs would have evolved similarly across the 2 periods. A matching algorithm identified hospitals with similar case volume and NACT use before publication that subsequently diverged into high- and low-use programs after publication.

Data were analyzed from February 10 to July 10, 2026. We used the Royston-Parmar flexible parametric model on the log cumulative hazard scale to estimate and compare 10-year survival probability and 10-year restricted mean survival time8 for patients treated in high- and low-use programs in both periods (additional details provided in the eMethods in Supplement 1 and original study7). Analyses were conducted using Stata, version 16 (StataCorp LLC). Significance was considered when the 95% CI excluded the null.

Results

We identified 17 004 patients treated in 319 low-use programs and 17 611 treated in 319 high-use programs (mean [SD] age, 63.8 [12.5] years). Use of NACT rose marginally in low-use programs (from 20.3% to 22.7%) and nearly doubled in high-use programs (from 21.5% to 42.8%), yielding a DID of 18.9 (95% CI, 16.6-21.2) percentage points. Demographic, disease, and follow-up characteristics evolved similarly between groups (Table), as did standardized 10-year survival before publication (annual change difference, 0.04 [95% CI, −0.3 to 0.4] percentage points).

A program’s tendency to favor NACT was associated with neither better nor worse 10-year survival. Standardized 10-year survival probability improved by similar magnitudes in low-use (from 15.1% to 19.1%; difference, 4.0 [95% CI, 3.2-4.8] percentage points) and high-use (from 15.6% to 20.0%; difference, 4.3 [95% CI, 3.4-5.2] percentage points) programs (DID, 0.3 [95% CI, −0.8 to 1.6] percentage points) (Figure). Gains in 10-year restricted mean survival time were likewise similar in low-use (from 45.7 to 51.4 months) and high-use (from 47.1 to 53.5 months) programs (DID, 0.7 [95% CI, −0.8 to 2.1] months).

Discussion

This comparative effectiveness research, which extended follow-up to 10-years of a previous findings of an association of greater NACT use with reduced early mortality without compromising median OS,7 found no association between NACT adoption and long-term survival probability or life expectancy. While RCTs have found no evidence that NACT diminishes OS, some observational studies interpreted the apparent survival advantage of PDS as causal.9 Our DID analysis, which accounted for time-invariant, unmeasured patient- and center-level confounding, aligns with the RCT evidence and may reassure clinicians about the long-term oncologic effectiveness of NACT. An unpublished RCT reported a progression-free survival but not an OS advantage with PDS at expert surgical centers.10 Our findings are consistent with RCTs demonstrating that NACT improves perioperative outcomes without impacting long-term survival.1

This study had limitations. Differential adoption of practices other than NACT could threaten validity, though this may not bias in favor of NACT as high-use centers were less often academic. We could not distinguish long-term survivors who were cured from those alive with disease. The choice to classify any patient receiving chemotherapy first as having received NACT could have biased results against high-use programs if patients unfit for surgery were disproportionately shifted to these centers after 2010. Nevertheless, these clinical data with extended follow-up suggest that NACT may not compromise durable survival.
