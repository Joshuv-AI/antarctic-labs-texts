Evaluation of the Patient Acceptability of Automated Communication of Prostate-specific Antigen Results After Local Treatment for Prostate Cancer
Authors: Leonor Jane Paulino Pereira, Hein Leenarts, Harm H.E. van Melick, Michiel J.P.M. Sedelaar, Alexandra S. Bruins Slot, Roderick C.N. van den Bergh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) monitoring is the cornerstone of prostate cancer (PC) follow-up after radical prostatectomy (RP) and radiotherapy (RT). St. Antonius Hospital (Nieuwegein/Utrecht, The Netherlands) launched the Automatic PSA Project in 2022 for automated digital communication of PSA results. PSA results are reviewed by a clinical team, and stable results are sent via automated messages; while rising PSA prompts referral to a urologist. Automated digital communication of PSA results reduces clinician and outpatient workloads and costs and patient travel. We evaluated the patient acceptability of this approach using the Service User Technology Acceptability Questionnaire. Eligible patients had undergone RP or RT, had stable PSA levels 6 mo after treatment, and had access to the patient portal. Participation was voluntary and limited to men without significant functional symptoms. Between January 2023 and September 2024, 100/325 participants in the project completed the questionnaire. Most men were aged ≥65 yr, had moderate to high digital skills, and had undergone RP (91%). Overall acceptability was high (median score 5.27, interquartile range 4.98–5.75), 72% considered digital PSA communication a suitable replacement, and 95% regarded it as a valuable addition to standard care. Privacy concerns were low. Selection bias could not be ruled out. Digital PSA communication appears to be acceptable and sustainable for selected patients, supporting broader implementation in future PC care.

Patient Summary

After treatment for prostate cancer, men need regular blood tests to check their PSA (prostate-specific antigen) levels. This usually involves several hospital visits and/or phone calls over many years. To reduce pressure on the health care system and limit the number of consultations for patients, we introduced a new system in which stable PSA results are sent directly to patients through a secure online portal. In cases with rising PSA levels, a specialised nurse contacts the patient to schedule an appointment with a urologist. Our questionnaire results show that most patients responded positively and felt safe and satisfied with the digital follow-up. This system may help in saving time and reducing costs for both patients and the hospital.

Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) monitoring is the cornerstone of oncological follow-up for prostate cancer (PC) after radical prostatectomy (RP) or radiotherapy (RT). The European Association of Urology recommends PSA testing after 4–8 wk, every 6 mo for the first 3 yr, and annually thereafter [1]. Over a period of 10 yr, this amounts to 13 post-treatment PSA tests. In the Netherlands, RP and RT together account for 45% of localised PC treatments, which underscores the scale of the follow-up burden [2]. With increasing PC prevalence, this burden will continue to rise.

Digital solutions, including telehealth, have been identified as promising strategies to enhance PC follow-up; however, their acceptability from a patient perspective has not yet been assessed [3]. The aim of this study was to evaluate patient acceptability of automated communication of PSA results after RP or RT using a structured questionnaire to assess sustainability and relevance for future health care needs.

In the Netherlands, follow-up in the treating hospital typically consists of in-person or telephone consultations to communicate PSA results. To streamline this process and reduce the follow-up burden, the Automatic PSA Project was introduced in 2022 at St. Antonius Hospital, the largest teaching hospital in the Netherlands. The initiative allows digital communication of PSA results through the hospital’s patient portal, which patients can access via a mobile application.

For PSA values to be visible in the electronic patient record, and therefore in the mobile application used by patients, the tests must be processed by laboratories connected to the hospital’s health care system. Patients were thus required to have their blood drawn at affiliated sites, which ensures automatic integration of results into the electronic record. Multiple affiliated sites are now available, which allows most patients to undergo blood sampling close to home and reduces the travel distance and patient burden.

Within the electronic health record system, all participating patients are included in an internal worklist that displays PSA results and relevant follow-up parameters. A dedicated clinical monitoring team reviews these results and determines the appropriate action: stable PSA values trigger an automated message to the patient (including the timing of the next test), whereas rising PSA values prompt a nurse-initiated telephone call and referral to a urologist. Initially limited to post-RP patients, the project was expanded in May 2023 to include post-RT patients. Eligible participants were men with undetectable PSA (<0.1 ng/ml) at 6 mo after RP or stable PSA (≤nadir + 1 ng/ml) at 6 mo after RT, and access to the patient portal. Inclusion required voluntary participation and the absence of significant post-treatment symptoms such as erectile dysfunction or incontinence. In addition, questionnaires for patient-reported outcomes measures were sent at predefined postoperative intervals, and any scores indicating concerning symptoms were automatically forwarded to the oncology nursing team to ensure that patients with relevant functional complaints were identified and excluded from participation.

Between January 2023 and September 2024, a cross-sectional study was conducted at St Antonius Hospital. Patients in the Automatic PSA Project were invited to complete the Service User Technology Acceptability Questionnaire (SUTAQ) [4]. The questionnaire was translated into Dutch using a standard forward-backward procedure to ensure linguistic and conceptual equivalence [5]. A link to the online questionnaire allowing voluntary participation was included in the automated PSA message. The study was approved by Medical Research Ethics Committees United (https://www.mec-u.nl/; reference W23.099).

SUTAQ includes 22 items rated on a six-point Likert scale (1 = totally disagree to 6 = totally agree) [4]. Items are grouped into six subscales (Fig. 1). To reduce the response bias, the questionnaire includes both positively and negatively phrased items. During analysis, negatively phrased items were reverse-coded to maintain consistency in scoring [6], [7], [8]. Higher scores indicate greater acceptance. Analyses were performed using R Studio version 2024.4.1.748 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Results are summarised as the median with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables, and as the frequency and percentage for categorical variables. Associations between acceptability scores and patient characteristics were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test.

The overall SUTAQ score revealed high acceptance of digital PSA communication (median 5.27, IQR 4.98–5.75; Fig. 1). The highest score was observed for the Privacy and Discomfort subscale (5.75, IQR 5.25–6.00), and the median score of 5.75 indicated minimal concerns regarding data security. Some 72% of the participants considered digital communication a suitable replacement for consultations, and 95% considered it a valuable addition to standard care. No significant associations were found between the acceptability scores and patient age, educational level, digital skills, or treatment type.

Our results show that digital communication of PSA results during follow-up is a feasible alternative to conventional care for PC patients. This strategy can reduce clinician and outpatient workloads, costs, and environmental impacts. In our hospital, automated messaging for robot-assisted laparoscopic RP follow-up alone saves approximately 120 h (15 working days) of consultation time annually. The project is now being expanded to include active surveillance, watchful waiting, hormone therapy, and PSA follow-up after negative diagnostics, with financial projections indicating a total annual cost reduction of €177 422.

Our results are consistent with findings from other studies evaluating the acceptability of telehealth across various cancer populations [9]. However, acceptability in our cohort was higher than that reported for studies in breast and gynaecological cancers, which probably reflects the particular suitability of PSA monitoring for digital follow-up [10].

Our study has some limitations. First, the population was relatively homogeneous, so selection bias due to voluntary participation is possible. We were unable to determine how many patients declined project participation. However, in practice, urologists preselect patients for digital follow-up on the basis of suitability. Thus, the study population reflects those receiving this care in practice. Second, men with biochemical recurrence did not receive the questionnaire link, as they were contacted by a clinician. As these patients did not engage with the digital service, it is unlikely that their exclusion biased acceptability outcomes. Third, we were unable to determine the median follow-up duration for respondents because of the anonymous survey design, which limits insights into potential differences in acceptability across follow-up stages. Finally, although the SUTAQ was translated using a standard method, formal validation of the Dutch version was not conducted.

In conclusion, digital communication of PSA results appears to be an acceptable and efficient alternative to conventional follow-up for PC patients. Careful patient selection remains essential to ensure effectiveness. Broader implementation is now being extended to patients on active surveillance or hormone therapy and those undergoing PSA follow-up after negative diagnostic evaluation.
