Background

The carotid web is an established etiology of cryptogenic stroke, commonly seen in younger patients without traditional risk factors. The incidence rate of carotid web-associated cryptogenic stroke is 3.8/100,000 person-years (3.2 in males, 4.3 in females). Notably, the prevalence of carotid webs is 24 times higher in patients with unilateral anterior circulation ischemic stroke compared to controls [1]. Pathologically, a carotid web represents a form of intimal fibromuscular dysplasia, characterized by focal proliferation of intimal fibrous tissue forming a shelf-like projection into the carotid bulb, distinct from the medial fibroplasia of classic FMD [2]. A key diagnostic feature is the absence of calcification or atherosclerosis.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old man with no cerebrovascular risk factors presented with left limb weakness for 1 h. Examination revealed dysarthria, left facial palsy, right gaze deviation, and left-sided hemiplegia (NIHSS 11). Admission cranial CT revealed no hemorrhage. Head and neck CTA demonstrated right MCA occlusion (Fig. 1, A) and a filling defect at the right carotid bifurcation, suggestive of carotid web or dissection.

The patient received standard-dose alteplase thrombolysis with dramatic improvement (post-thrombolysis NIHSS 1). Given the significant clinical improvement, he declined endovascular therapy due to bleeding concerns. Subsequent brain MRI confirmed an acute right hemisphere infarction (Fig. 1, B). Carotid ultrasound showed an iso- to hypoechoic lesion with suspected thrombus (Fig. 1, C). Extensive etiological workup—including Routine blood tests, protein S activity, protein C activity, lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibodies, anti-β2-glycoprotein 1 antibodies, cardiac ultrasound, 24-hour Holter monitoring, contrast transthoracic echocardiography, and bubble study—was unremarkable.

The leading probable diagnosis was carotid web with superimposed thrombosis, though differential diagnosis of arterial dissection or cardiac embolism remained to be considered. After 10 days of anticoagulation (low-molecular-weight heparin, 0.4 ml q12h), follow-up CTA showed MCA recanalization and ultrasound indicated near-complete thrombus resolution (Fig. 1, D and E). CTA and high-resolution MRI findings were consistent with a carotid web (Figs. 1F and 2). A 3-month follow-up CTA confirmed the persistent filling defect.

The patient declined both carotid endarterectomy and stenting. A long-term medical management plan was adopted: 3 months of anticoagulation (rivaroxaban) followed by lifelong antiplatelet therapy (aspirin). No stroke recurrence occurred during 16 months of follow-up.

Discussion

A carotid web is a form of intimal fibromuscular dysplasia characterized by a shelf-like projection of intimal fibrous tissue into the carotid bulb [2]. Rainer first described its association with recurrent left hemispheric events in otherwise healthy young individuals [3]. Pathological examination revealed vitreous or mucous-like tissue within the intravascular space of the internal carotid artery endomembrane section, with no atheromatous components identified. However, a fresh thrombus composed of red blood cells and fibrin was adherent to a portion of the intimal surface, leading to the diagnosis of carotid web–induced thrombosis [4]. The typical pathological feature of carotid web is the absence of calcification or atherosclerosis. In the present case, the etiology of the middle cerebral artery embolism was carotid artery thrombosis. Although a carotid web was initially suspected, the diagnosis remained unconfirmed at that stage. After a course of anticoagulation, the thrombus resolved and distal vessel patency was restored. Subsequent CTA revealed a shelf-like intraluminal filling defect on the posterior wall of the carotid bifurcation. The stability of this finding on 3-month follow-up CTA and serial ultrasounds examination provided strong corroborative evidence for the definitive diagnosis of a carotid web.

Imaging examination of carotid web

Imaging examinations for carotid web primarily include carotid ultrasound, CTA and high-resolution Magnetic resonance imaging (HR-MRI). Ultrasound plays a significant role in evaluating and monitoring thrombus formation associated with carotid webs. It typically reveals a proximally elevated stump extending into a shelf-like intraluminal filling defect arising from the posterior wall of the carotid bulb, resembling a redundant valvular flap. Superimposed thrombus formation on these flap-like structures was observed in eight out of twelve cases (8/12, 66.7%) in the present study [5]. With its high resolution and ability to detect calcification, CTA is both the preferred diagnostic modality and the most reliable imaging method [6]. On axial images, the web appears as a linear, low-density structure within the lumen of the carotid artery. Multiplanar reconstruction can display its typical shelf-like morphology in three dimensions, allowing precise measurement of its thickness, length, and relationship to the vessel wall. HR-MRI can provide more detailed wall information, aiding in differential diagnosis. The typical finding is a shelf-like protrusion visible on pre‑contrast scans. On T1‑weighted images, it appears slightly hypointense to the normal vessel wall and demonstrates peripheral enhancement with central hypoenhancement post-contrast [7].

In the reported case, initial ultrasound identified a floating thrombus adjacent to the suspected web, which resolved on follow-up after anticoagulation. Sagittal CTA showed a filling defect at the right carotid bifurcation. HR-MRI further demonstrated a shelf-like protrusion that was mildly hyperintense on T1‑weighted imaging, showed contrast enhancement, and was hyperintense on Simultaneous Non-contrast Angiography and intraPlaque hemorrhage (SNAP) sequences.

Carotid web and stroke

Multiple researchers have reported immediate contrast pooling distal to the web on angiography, indicating a clear hemodynamic abnormality in patients with carotid webs [8, 9]. Cases of thrombus formation adjacent to a carotid web have also been described [10, 11].

Currently, there are no randomized controlled trials on the treatment of carotid webs. A cohort study of 92 patients with carotid web showed that among the 23 patients who underwent surgical or stent treatment, no recurrence was observed over an average follow-up of 40 months [12]. In the medication-only group, the annual recurrent CIE rate was 6.9%, and the cumulative recurrence rates were 4.4% at 30 days, 10.8% at 1 year, 19.8% at 2 years, 23.2% at 3 years, and 27.3% at 5 years. A systematic review of 448 symptomatic carotid webs reported outcomes for 227 patients in the medication group and 221 patients in the intervention group. Over a mean follow-up of 21.2 months, the risk of recurrent ischemic events was significantly lower in the intervention group compared with the medication group (0% vs. 36.1%; OR = 14.18; 95% CI, 3.17–63.46; P = 0.001) [13]. Another systematic study of 170 patients with carotid free-floating thrombus found that thrombus regression rates were similar between antiplatelet (42%) and anticoagulation (48%) groups, as were rates of residual stenosis. The antiplatelet group had fewer 30-day ischemic events (0% vs. 4%) and a lower intracranial hemorrhage rate (3.3% vs. 5.4%) but a higher mortality rate (6.3% vs. 0%) [14] .

The management in the present case was guided by the specific finding of a carotid web with a superimposed free-floating thrombus. To address the high embolic risk from the mobile thrombus, a phased pharmacologic strategy was employed. In the acute phase, the patient received off-label anticoagulation with rivaroxaban for 3 months, a decision based on the presence of an active, high-risk embolic source in the absence of high-level evidence. After imaging confirmed thrombus resolution, therapy was transitioned to long-term antiplatelet monotherapy (aspirin) for secondary prevention. This staged approach reflects the differing priorities of addressing acute thrombotic burden versus of the underlying web. It must be emphasized that for carotid web, neither anticoagulation nor antiplatelet therapy is supported by evidence from randomized trials, and management decisions remain largely individualized. In this patient, the diagnosis of carotid web could not be pathologically confirmed. Furthermore, as the process of thrombus detachment was not directly observed, a definitive causal link between the web and the embolism cannot be established.

Conclusion

Carotid web should be considered in the differential diagnosis of young patients presenting with acute ischemic stroke in the absence of conventional cerebrovascular risk factors. Thrombus formation adjacent to the web is a likely mechanism of cerebral infarction.
