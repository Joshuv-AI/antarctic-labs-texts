Barriers and facilitators while implementing community first responder systems in a western setting: mixed-methods expert consensus

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly cardiopulmonary resuscitation and timely defibrillation are key determinants of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest survival. Community first responder (CFR) networks improve early response, yet adoption varies internationally. This study summarizes current key factors affecting CFR-system implementation through an international expert consensus process.MethodsA mixed-methods design explored barriers and facilitators to CFR system development. Data were collected during an expert consensus workshop in April 2024 Hinterzarten, Germany (46 experts, 14 countries) and an online survey. Experts were invited based on previous authorship regarding CFR systems. Thematic content analysis and descriptive statistics identified recurrent themes and areas of agreement.ResultsExperts agreed on core needs for effective CFR systems with high consensus regarding locally adapted dispatch algorithms, clear privacy rules for CFR activation, sufficient CFR training, structured debriefing and psychological support. Congruence was weaker regarding funding, legal or governance frameworks, reflecting variation in responsibilities, liability, and automated external defibrillator- (AED-) registry organisation. Findings were structured around a previously developed roadmap outlining four stages of CFR-system maturation. In the exploration phase, unrecognition of delays in Basic Life Support initiation impedes translation of practice gaps into action. Installation is challenged by recruitment, funding, and coordination among stakeholders. Implementation requires outcome evaluation. Moreover, sustainability is threatened by volunteer attrition and reliance on government budgets. Supportive legal frameworks, centralised AED registries, and integration into national health initiatives facilitate both establishment and long-term stability.ConclusionThis exploratory expert consensus process on CFR system implementation highlights the need for stable funding, coherent governance, aligned legal frameworks, comprehensive and exhaustive AED registration, data-driven feedback from practice to system optimisation and targeted training of CFR.

Background

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) remains a major public health concern, with survival outcomes strongly dependent on early cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and timely defibrillation [1, 2]. With each minute delay in defibrillation of a shockable rhythm from cardiac arrest onset, the probability of survival decreases by 10% [3]. Global data on emergency medicine service (EMS) response times from national and international registries demonstrated mean response times of 12.2 min (range 6.4–22.8 min); median response times in 2021 ranged between 7 and 14 min with 25% of patients reached within 7 min [4–6]. Different systematic reviews and meta-analysis have shown that alerting community first responders (CFR) to OHCA patients is associated with higher rates of bystander-initiated CPR, increased use of automated external defibrillator (AED) before ambulance arrival, and increased survival at hospital discharge or 30 days, which highlights the significant potential of mobile applications in enhancing bystander interventions and improving patient outcomes [7, 8]. The implementation of CFR systems is therefore recommended by the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR), the European Resuscitation Council (ERC), and the American Heart Association (AHA) [9–11].

Despite the current evidence and international guidelines, the implementation of CFR systems remains heterogeneous, as CFR systems are developed with varying designs and at different stages across countries and regions [12]. The positive outcomes achieved in mature systems have not yet been realised in all regions. Prior research and international conferences have highlighted multiple barriers and facilitators to implementation as well as a roadmap to implement a CFR system [8, 13–17]. To our knowledge, no studies have examined the variety of difficulties encountered on implementation of existing CFR systems internationally. The purpose of this study was to explore, and highlight key barriers and facilitators affecting the implementation, maturation, and sustainability of CFR systems for OHCA through an international expert consensus process. We report insights from experts from different countries actively involved in CFR system implementation and sustainment, by means of both a live workshop and online survey. Drawing on expert input from different international contexts may help align CFR systems across regions, moving from fragmented and isolated local implementations towards eventually coordinated transnational models.

Methods

Study design

Expert panel selection

The present study builds upon an established international consensus network in the field of CFR systems for OHCA. The expert panel and recruitment strategy are directly derived from the third international CFR Symposium (Hinterzarten, Germany, April 2024), as previously described by Metelmann and coworkers (2025) [14], which itself represents a continuation of earlier work in this domain [18]. Specifically, experts were identified using a structured and reproducible methodology combining a systematic literature search in PubMed with predefined keywords related to first responder systems and smartphone alerting, and a complementary snowballing approach to identify additional relevant researchers and research groups [18]. Corresponding authors of peer-reviewed publications were invited to participate, ensuring inclusion of individuals with a demonstrated scientific track record in the field. In parallel, experts were recruited through established professional networks, including members of the German Resuscitation Council (GRC), the European Resuscitation Council (ERC), and affiliated organizations [18]. The 2024 consensus process expanded this previously established expert network by inviting additional researchers who had published in the field in recent years [14]. The explicit aim of this approach was to include all active research groups in the domain of CFR systems, thereby ensuring broad international and multidisciplinary representation. As such, the expert panel represents a stable and evolving network of domain experts.

For the present study, a total of 46 experts from 14 countries across Europe, North America, Asia, and Australia participated. The panel comprised a multidisciplinary group with expertise in emergency medicine, cardiology, resuscitation science, epidemiology, and digital health, as well as practical experience in the implementation and evaluation of CFR systems [14]. Despite efforts to ensure broad geographical representation, certain regions, including Eastern Europe, South America, and Africa, remained underrepresented, likely reflecting publication bias and the limited identification of active research groups in these areas [14]. As no additional inclusion criteria were applied in the present study beyond those established in the preceding consensus initiatives, expertise was defined implicitly through prior selection into this international network, based on (i) authorship of peer-reviewed publications in CFR systems or OHCA, (ii) active involvement in system development, implementation, or evaluation, and/or (iii) affiliation with relevant national or international resuscitation organizations.

Study development and validation

For this study, real-life voting, open discussion and an online questionnaire were developed as part of a structured, exploratory multi-step consensus process embedded within the third international CFR Symposium (Hinterzarten, Germany, April 2024).

Real life voting and development of the online questionnaire was performed on statements based on (i), previously published work on the development of CFR systems [17], (ii), informed by consensus terminology [18], and (iii), for the online questionnaire, considering also emerging topics and unresolved discussions identified during the consensus initiatives. A pre-workshop discussion involving representatives from CFR systems in Belgium and Italy (March 2024) was conducted to refine and structure CFR barrier and facilitator’ themes [17] into preliminary real life voting statements. During the symposium workshop, these pre-defined statements were presented, clarified if needed before actual voting. This real-time expert appraisal ensured content and form validity of the voting statements. In contrast to traditional survey-based research, the questionnaire was designed to complement the consensus process by serving as a post-workshop validation and triangulation tool. The survey items were directly informed by the consensus discussions and voting outcomes, reflecting a consensus-driven development process. Formal pilot testing was not performed; however, iterative refinement through expert input functionally replaced this step by ensuring that all items were pre-vetted within the target expert population. The survey was used to quantify agreement, capture additional contextual insights, and assess the robustness of the workshop findings within a convergent parallel mixed-methods design. The approach enabled a comprehensive assessment of barriers and facilitators to CFR system implementation, in line with established mixed-methods methodology [19], and reported according to the CRISP statement for primary care [20]. All participants took part voluntarily, provided informed consent, and no patient data were involved.

Data collection

Qualitative data during life discussions were captured through detailed note-taking during the discussions (ME, VS). The degree of consensus for each statement was assessed using anonymous real-time voting (PINGO, University of Paderborn, Germany). Following the symposium, the voluntary online questionnaire (Qualtrics, Seattle, WA, USA, Appendix 1) was distributed to all eligible participants (n = 44), 12 weeks after the workshop, with one reminder sent after 5 weeks. Two members of the study team (ME, DE) were excluded from participation to minimise bias. The survey included both factual system-level questions (e.g., legal frameworks, AED registration practices) and opinion-based items, reflecting expert perspectives. The themes were grouped around two major parts (1), Legal Framework and Practice-policy Connections and (2), Roadmap for CFR Implementation (covering the four stages exploration, installation, implementation and sustainment of CFR systems [17]. No incentives were provided.

Data analysis and reporting

Qualitative data from workshop notes were grouped into workshop highlights using thematic content analysis. Consensus voting thresholds were defined a priori (≥75% agreement) in line with the median threshold reported in consensus methodology review [21]. Answers were classified as Fully agree (5), Agree (4), Neutral (3), Disagree (2), Fully Disagree (1).

Open-ended questionnaire responses were analysed using a descriptive aggregation approach, rather than a full qualitative thematic analysis, given the exploratory nature of the questionnaire and the limited number of responses per item. Responses were read by two separate researchers from the Belgian network with both a medical doctor background, in full, and grouped into descriptive categories reflecting reported barriers and facilitators. Open-ended responses were de-identified prior to reporting. The conclusions per thematic area are reported and not in repetition of the workshop’s discussion.

Discussion

The implementation of CFR systems is increasingly recognised as a key determinant of early intervention and survival in OHCA [24]. In-person meetings between international CFR systems’ stakeholders and experts previously facilitated structured exchange of practical solutions to implementation challenges and evidence-informed consensus processes [14, 18, 25–27]. This report aimed to document on discussions and consensus voting by global experts in the field of CFR systems development regarding main barriers and facilitators of CFR system development. Divergences between qualitative and quantitative findings were not identified, granularity behind the consensus agreement and discussions during the workshop are provided.

Demographics, Geographics, and EMS Landscape

Environment-aware dispatch algorithms

Geography and urban environment strongly shape CFR systems’ effectiveness. CFR schemes were initially designed to support ambulance services in rural settings, where EMS arrival can be delayed [28]. Despite lower volunteer density in such areas, smartphone-based CFR often arrived faster than EMS in rural, sub-rural, and suburban contexts [29–31]. CFR dispatch and the strategic relocation AED using terrain-sensitive modelling in mountainous areas, where EMS response is delayed, have demonstrated measurable improvements in AED and CFR coverage [32]. High-rise environments pose specific challenges: vertical distance, elevator availability, access restrictions all introduce delays and complicate high-quality CPR delivery [33]. Pragmatic solutions included integration of (handset) z-axis data at public-safety answering points (PSAP), digital floor plans, controlled entry (e-keys or concierge integration or building access codes), and dispatching responders already present inside buildings where conventional GPS does not work effectively [34].

Legal frameworks and privacy

Consistent legal framework

The integration of CFR systems with EMS have often raised governance and privacy concerns. Advanced Mobile Location (AML) enables the automatic transmission of EMS callers’ geolocation directly to PSAPs under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) ‘public interest’ legal basis, ensuring that data are securely stored, not disclosed to third parties, and retained according to legal requirements. Operationally, once a CFR accepts an alert, typically geolocation and sometimes contact details are processed for routing and dispatcher interaction. This constitutes identified processing but accordingly remains legally permissible under strict purpose limitation. Systems have enabled dispatcher–CFR communication or selective disclosure, such as live video from bystanders in Scandinavian systems, which improved medical assessment and CPR quality [35]. CFR applications further typically employ privacy-by-design measures, such as legal contracts with role-based access, pseudonymisation of CFR data, and separation of identifiers from location streams. Systems have maintained separate servers for CFR identity, data and live geolocation, reducing re-identification risk and aligning with GDPR principles (evapp.org). Legal assessments could help establish the foundation for policy development and app implementation [36].

Recruitment, campaigns, and retention

Comprehensive training program

Volunteer motivation and retention have been linked to intrinsic drivers such as altruism, autonomy, and community identification [37]. Responders with prior CPR experience or medical training are more likely to accept alerts and provide care, suggesting targeted recruitment of these groups could strengthen system reliability [38]. CFR are further engaged by support of peers and society [39]. Systems have used symbolic recognition initiatives, such as honorary titles for responders in Ticino [17], to reinforce motivation and social identity. The Dutch system HartslagNu further provided an example of pragmatic recruitment and retention strategies: by removing 80 000 inactive accounts and collaborating with over 100 local organisations, the platform ensured that the network remained both operationally robust and socially embedded [40, 41].

Psychological aftercare

Structured debriefing and psychological support

The psychological impact of responding to OHCA alerts has been reported as manageable, with CFR describing the experience as intense but positive, with low prevalence of post-traumatic stress in follow-up assessments [42–45]. International guidelines [46] and existing CFR systems indeed recognise the need for aftercare and have included different strategies for follow up. Multi-tiered programs have been developed incorporating customised training, structured debriefings, and proactive follow-up with the CFR after missions to assess their need for professional psychological support [47].

Roadmap

Harmonised responsibilities, liability, and awareness

The development of CFR systems appears to follow recurring barriers and facilitators across the four phases, from exploration to implementation. Regarding the exploration phase, “black spots” in timely EMS arrival still exist [5, 6]. However, limited scientific evidence and low political awareness continue to be used as arguments to undermine support and funding. Findings from the expert questionnaire conducted for this study reflect these concerns. The international position statements and guidelines for CFR systems by the ERC, the ILCOR and AHA [9–11] should eventually facilitate broader adoption. During the installation phase, recruitment of CFR was reported by the experts and in literature to be generally successful [48], but long-term active retention may seem more problematic. Stable financing and integration into existing EMS networks are repeatedly highlighted as prerequisites, while suboptimal AED registration has been noted as a barrier to system maturation. In the implementation phase, intermediate outcomes such as bystander CPR and AED use provide actionable milestones. Long-term viability ultimately depends on dual recognition: both governmental awareness and secured, if needed, funding, enabling a more optimal use of the yet existing community resources.

Different cost-effectiveness modelling studies on CFR systems have been performed [49–52], generally demonstrating that CFR systems, according to the current data, could achieve favourable cost-effectiveness ratios per quality-adjusted life year (QALY). This may be because CFR systems leverage existing potential more efficiently than systems that rely solely on professional EMS [52]. Embedding CFR in national health policy enabled progress, contrasting with the lack of structural support elsewhere. Different regions have systems of varying maturity, organisational models, and financial structures, ranging from profit-driven enterprises to volunteer-based or non-governmental-based initiatives. Systems have often developed independently, with limited coordination across borders or sectors [27]. From a societal and patient-centred perspective, such fragmentation may risk inefficiency and constrain collective learning. The challenge ahead may be to envision CFR initiatives not as isolated solutions, but as interoperable components of a broader ecosystem in which integration, cross-border collaboration, and maybe shared governance may ultimately maximise survival and equity in OHCA response.

This study has several limitations. Although the expert panel included a broad international representation, participation in the online survey was voluntary and yielded a 45% response rate, introducing the possibility of non-response bias. Open-ended survey responses limited in number did not allow for in-depth qualitative analysis and data should thus be interpreted as exploratory and descriptive. Countries were represented by varying numbers of experts, reflecting differences in CFR system maturity and stakeholder structures, due to the real-world setting, rather than a stratified sampling strategy. As a result, comparisons between countries may disproportionately reflect systems with more active contributors rather than population-based national estimates. The latter limitation existed in previously published consensus work consisting of the same experts [14, 26]. Despite the large variety in systems, consensus was high. The questionnaire was in English which may have led to language bias. The questionnaire was not pilot tested, however, was informed by previous consensus work and real-life discussions during the consensus workshops. Expert input reflects self-reported experiences and perceptions, which may be influenced by recall or personal involvement in system development. This subjective component may have strengthened the qualitative richness but limits generalisability. The workshop discussions were documented through note-taking and structured thematic analysis, rather than full audio transcription, which may have reduced the granularity of qualitative data. The analysis did not incorporate patient-level outcomes or objective system performance metrics for each CFR system. Consequently, conclusions relate to expert-reported barriers and facilitators rather than empirically measured effectiveness. Finally, the study team acted both as authors and participants; this dual role may have introduced interpretative bias in theme selection and framing.

Conclusion

The consensus of international experts on community first responder systems emphasises a need for stable funding, governance, coordinated legal frameworks, complete AED registration, data-driven feedback, targeted training, and policy integration. The highest consensus was found for the statement that there is a need to define conditions on alerts (privacy and safety) in CFR systems. Further research should optimise coverage, indoor navigation, high-rise response and integration of systems, potentially into nation-wide systems to maximise patient survival in all regions. Integration of these insights with objective system performance data and expansion to underrepresented regions should further strengthen global applicability.
