Analog photography enhances identity, well-being, and social connection, in Southeastern China
Authors: Yiyuchen Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe resurgence of analog photography within our hyper-digitized landscape presents a unique and compelling intersection of technology, culture, and human psychology. This study offers an in-depth investigation into the multifaceted role of analog photography in shaping identity formation, fostering psychological well-being, and nurturing social capital development within the specific socio-cultural milieu of Southeastern China. Employing a concurrent mixed-methods design with a robust sample of 600 participants (N=600), we integrated quantitative surveys assessing psychological constructs and engagement levels with qualitative interviews exploring lived experiences. We applied advanced statistical techniques, including Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), to test a theoretically-grounded model. This model hypothesized that engagement in analog photography predicts enhanced well-being (manifested as higher self-esteem and lower perceived stress) through the crucial mediating roles of psychologically potent nostalgia and cultivated mindfulness. Furthermore, Latent Class Analysis (LCA) was utilized to move beyond a monolithic view and identify distinct, meaningful subgroups of analog photographers based on their underlying motivations and engagement patterns. SEM results indicated a strong model fit with the data, confirming the significant mediating pathways through both nostalgia and mindfulness, suggesting these psychological processes are key to understanding the benefits derived from analog practice. LCA successfully identified four distinct and interpretable classes: The tradition-focused ‘Nostalgic Purists,’ the aesthetically driven ‘Aesthetic Explorers’, the community-oriented ‘Social Connectors,’ and the process-centered ‘Mindful Contemplatives’. These quantitative findings were substantially enriched and contextualized by our qualitative data. Interviews revealed intricate details of how the material (film, cameras, prints), temporal (slowness, anticipation), and social (clubs, sharing) dimensions of analog practice are woven into personal self-narratives, facilitate emotional regulation strategies, and solidify enduring community bonds. Collectively, these findings provide a nuanced, multi-layered understanding of how seemingly ‘retro’ or traditional, tangible practices can serve vital, contemporary psychological and social functions, particularly in a rapidly digitizing, non-Western context like Southeastern China. This research extends existing theories of identity (Erikson, 1970; McAdams in Rev. Gen. Psychol. 5(2): 100-122, 2001), psychological well-being (Ryan & Deci in Am. Psychol. 55(1): 68–78, 2000)), social capital (Putnam in Bowling alone: The collapse and revival of American community, Touchstone Books/Simon & Schuster, 2000), and cultural memory (Assmann in Cultural memory and early civilization: Writing, remembrance, and political imagination, 1-319, 2011), offering valuable insights for both academic discourse and practical applications in mental health and cultural preservation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32663-x.Keywords: Psychological Flourishing, Identity Negotiation , Mnemonic Affect, Mindfulness, Social Affiliation, Cultural psychologySubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

The analog resurgence in a digital world: More than just retro

In an era defined by the instantaneous and the intangible—where digital images proliferate across screens with fleeting permanence—the global renaissance of analog photography stands as a fascinating counter-narrative1. This revival is not merely a fleeting trend or a simple exercise in ‘retro’ aesthetics,rather, it signifies a deeper engagement with material culture and potentially reflects profound psychological and sociological underpinnings. Scholars frequently interpret this phenomenon as a conscious or subconscious reaction against the perceived ephemerality, placelessness, and overwhelming immediacy characteristic of digital media2,3. There appears to be a growing yearning for tangibility, permanence, and the unique ‘aura’4that a physical photograph or negative possesses – a sense of unique existence in time and space that digital copies often lack. This desire for the ‘real’ challenges the very nature of representation in the digital age5.

The very process of analog photography—the manual operation of mechanical cameras, the deliberate selection and handling of film, the anticipation during development, and the eventual creation of a physical print—offers a rich, multi-sensory experience. This tactile engagement starkly contrasts with the often-disembodied nature of screen-based interactions, potentially fostering a renewed sense of embodiment, physical presence, and connection to the material world. This involvement with the physical demands a different kind of attention, a slowing down that runs counter to the digital flow. Within the dynamic context of Southeastern China, a region navigating a complex and often-turbulent negotiation between its deeply rooted cultural traditions and the relentless pace of modernization and digitalization, analog photography acquires a particularly potent significance. Here, it functions not merely as an artistic choice but also as a vehicle for cultural preservation, a platform for intergenerational dialogue, and a crucial site for individuals to construct and negotiate their modern identities in a landscape of flux6. It becomes a practice where personal history and collective heritage can be physically inscribed and re-examined.

Psychological dimensions: Unpacking nostalgia, mindfulness, identity, and flow

The allure of analog photography resonates powerfully with fundamental human psychological needs and processes. Nostalgia, frequently evoked by the distinct aesthetics (grain, colour palettes) and the ritualistic processes of film photography, serves as a primary motivator for many practitioners7. This is not merely a passive, bittersweet longing,psychological research increasingly demonstrates that nostalgia serves adaptive functions. It can act as a psychological resource, bolstering social connectedness, enhancing self-continuity by linking past and present selves, providing a sense of existential meaning, and even increasing positive affect8,9. The act of using older cameras or recreating past aesthetics can be a direct pathway to these psychologically beneficial nostalgic states.

Furthermore, the inherently deliberate, often slow, and methodical nature of analog photography can be a potent catalyst for inducing states of mindfulness—a non-judgmental, present-moment awareness10. The constraints of the medium (limited exposures, delayed gratification) necessitate a more considered approach to composition and a heightened attention to the environment and the photographic process itself. This ‘slow photography’ movement can be viewed as an intentional counter-practice to the pervasive ‘culture of speed’ and digital fatigue, offering a respite from information overload and constant connectivity11. It aligns with broader ‘slow living’ concepts that question the relentless pace of modern life, advocating for deeper engagement and exploring sustainable community models12. Such mindful engagement may contribute to attention restoration – the recovery of directed attention fatigued by digital overload13 – and has been robustly linked to enhanced subjective well-being and greater emotional regulation14.

Closely related to mindfulness is the concept of ‘flow’ – the state of complete absorption in an activity, characterized by energized focus, full involvement, and enjoyment in the process15. The challenges (mastering exposure, developing film) and skills involved in analog photography can readily facilitate these intrinsically rewarding flow states, which are crucial for psychological growth and a sense of competence.

Above all, engaging in meaningful creative practices like photography is deeply intertwined with identity work – the ongoing process of constructing, negotiating, and expressing one’s sense of self16, (Erikson, 1970). Analog photography provides a rich medium for individuals to craft and communicate their personal narratives17. Through the choices they make (subject matter, style) and the images they create, individuals can explore various self-concepts18, process life experiences, and solidify a coherent sense of identity, a task particularly salient during periods of transition such as emerging adulthood19. The creation of tangible artifacts – prints, negatives, albums – transforms fleeting moments into ‘memory objects’20. These objects serve as physical anchors for personal and collective history, facilitating self-reflection, storytelling, and the intergenerational transmission of memories21. They become part of the ‘material self’, extensions of one’s identity embedded in physical form22.

Social and cultural dimensions: Weaving community and preserving heritage

The impact of analog photography extends significantly beyond the individual psyche. It frequently acts as a catalyst for building social capital – the networks, norms, and trust that facilitate coordination and cooperation for mutual benefit23. Photography clubs, shared darkrooms, university societies, online forums (like specific groups on Douban or WeChat), and print-swapping events create vital ‘third places’24. These spaces, distinct from home and work, foster social interaction, shared learning, and the development of strong community bonds based on a common passion, a form of cultural capital25. This fulfills the fundamental human need for relatedness, a cornerstone of well-being as outlined by Self-Determination Theory26.

Crucially, the practice often becomes a conduit for intergenerational knowledge transfer. As younger enthusiasts seek out the expertise of older, more experienced photographers, valuable dialogue and mentorship relationships are formed. This process not only preserves technical skills but also connects participants with local history and cultural heritage, reinforcing collective memory and shared identities27,28. In an era marked by concerns about cultural homogenization due to globalization and the dominance of digital platforms, such acts of preservation and intergenerational connection can be seen as subtle yet significant forms of cultural resistance and continuity29. It fosters a sense of rootedness and belonging within a specific cultural lineage.

While previous studies have often explored the analog revival from a cultural or phenomenological perspective, and psychological research has examined constructs like nostalgia and mindfulness in isolation, a significant knowledge gap exists at their intersection.

There is a scarcity of research that empirically tests the precise psychological pathways linking the practice of analog photography to well-being outcomes. Furthermore, few studies have adopted a mixed-methods approach to integrate robust quantitative modeling with deep qualitative exploration in this domain. The existing literature also tends to focus on Western contexts, leaving the unique socio-cultural dynamics of a region like Southeastern China—where tradition and modernity are in intense dialogue—underexplored.

This study aims to fill these gaps. Its primary novelty lies in three areas: (1) it proposes and tests a specific mediational model using SEM, moving beyond correlation to explain how analog photography enhances well-being; (2) it employs Latent Class Analysis to reveal the heterogeneity within the analog community, offering a more nuanced view than prior monolithic treatments; and (3) it grounds this empirical investigation in the rich, under-examined context of Southeastern China, integrating qualitative insights to explain the cultural specificity of these psychological and social processes.

Methods

Participants and recruitment strategy

A total of 600 participants (N=600), aged between 18 and 50 years, were recruited from various urban centers (e.g., Fuzhou, Xiamen, Hangzhou) and surrounding rural areas in Southeastern China (primarily Fujian and Zhejiang provinces). This region was selected due to its unique blend of rapid economic development and strong traditional cultural roots. We employed a multi-pronged recruitment strategy to capture a diverse sample:

Research design: A concurrent mixed-methods approach

To achieve a holistic understanding of the phenomenon, we adopted a concurrent mixed-methods design30. This approach allows for the simultaneous collection and analysis of quantitative (QUAN) and qualitative (QUAL) data. The rationale is that quantitative data can identify broad patterns, relationships, and typologies across a large sample, while qualitative data can provide rich, contextualized insights into the subjective experiences, meanings, and nuances underlying those patterns. The QUAN and QUAL strands were conducted in parallel and then integrated during the interpretation phase, allowing for both confirmation (triangulation) and expansion of findings.

Ethics statement

This study employed a fully anonymous online survey to investigate identity formation through analog photography in Southeastern China. No personally identifiable information was collected, and all responses were recorded without linking to any specific individual. The study posed no risk to participants and did not involve any intervention or manipulation.

As this study solely involved anonymous survey data collection without any personally identifiable information, ethical approval was not required. According to China’s “Regulations on Human-Related Biomedical Research” (2023), research based on anonymized survey data that does not include sensitive personal information or medical interventions is explicitly exempt from formal ethics review. This study adhered to all relevant national regulations and international ethical standards, including the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants voluntarily provided informed consent before participating in the survey.

Prior to testing the structural model, the measurement model was assessed using Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA).

All constructs exceeded the required thresholds, with Composite Reliability (CR) values greater than 0.6 and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values greater than 0.5, providing a solid foundation for interpreting the path analysis results.

Discussion

Integrating findings: A psycho-socio-cultural tapestry

This study directly addressed a critical gap in the literature by moving beyond descriptive accounts of the analog revival to empirically model its psycho-social impact within the unique context of Southeastern China.

The primary contribution of this research is the elucidation of the mechanisms through which engagement in analog photography enhances well-being. Our SEM results provide robust, novel evidence that nostalgia and mindfulness fully mediate the relationship between the practice and its positive outcomes (higher self-esteem, lower stress). This finding is innovative because it suggests that the practice’s benefits are not automatic but are channeled through the cultivation of specific psychological states: A reflective connection to the past and a deep awareness of the present.

Furthermore, by employing LCA, this work makes a second significant contribution by quantitatively demonstrating that the “analog community” is not a monolith but a vibrant ecosystem of distinct subgroups. Identifying the ‘Nostalgic Purists,’ ‘Aesthetic Explorers,’ ‘Social Connectors,’ and ‘Mindful Contemplatives’ provides a data-driven typology that enriches our understanding of the varied motivations driving the phenomenon.

This nuanced perspective, which was then given voice through our qualitative findings, directly answers the call for a more sophisticated analysis of this contemporary practice. The integration of these two methodological approaches allows us to see not only the overarching patterns but also the lived realities that animate them, providing a holistic psycho-socio-cultural tapestry of meaning.

Cultural context and the negotiation of modern chinese identity

The findings are deeply embedded in the Chinese context. The emphasis on intergenerational connection speaks to enduring cultural values. Analog photography becomes a tool for ‘dialogical memory’ (Bakhtin, 1981), allowing individuals to engage in a tangible conversation with their past and cultural heritage28,27. In a society undergoing immense transformation, such practices provide crucial psychological anchoring16. It allows individuals to perform and negotiate complex ‘hybrid’ identities (Chen & Qiu, 2020).

Broader implications: Mind, society, and culture

These findings support arts-based interventions for mental health and highlight how niche communities build social capital. It underscores the value of ‘slow’12 and tangible practices in a digital age11, offering pathways to combat digital fatigue and foster deeper engagement.

Limitations and future research avenues

While this study provides valuable insights, its limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design prevents us from drawing causal conclusions; while our model posits that engagement fosters well-being through mindfulness and nostalgia, it is equally plausible that individuals with higher pre-existing levels of well-being are more drawn to such reflective hobbies. Second, our reliance on a convenience sample, though diverse, may over-represent highly engaged enthusiasts active in clubs and online forums, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings to more casual practitioners. The observed 70:30 male-to-female ratio, while reflective of organized clubs, also points to the need for future research to specifically explore gender dynamics and potential barriers to entry for women.

Third, the use of self-report measures for all constructs introduces the possibility of common method bias and social desirability effects, where participants might present themselves in a more positive light. Future research would benefit from a longitudinal approach to track the development of these relationships over time, helping to establish causality. Additionally, incorporating objective or behavioral measures (e.g., physiological stress indicators, analysis of photographic content) could corroborate the self-reported findings. Finally, while our LCA profiles are statistically robust, they represent snapshots; a deeper longitudinal ethnographic study following individuals from each class would provide invaluable insight into how these identities and motivations evolve over a photographer’s journey.

Conclusion

Analog photography thrives in Southeastern China, acting as a potent force for psychological well-being and social connection. It facilitates identity exploration, enhances well-being via nostalgia and mindfulness, and builds communities. This study reveals how its slow, tangible nature offers a meaningful way to navigate modern complexities, craft identities, and connect with past and present. In our digital world, the analog’s appeal underscores fundamental human needs for connection, meaning, and tangibility, offering vital lessons for enhancing human flourishing.
