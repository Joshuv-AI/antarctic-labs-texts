Long non-coding RNAs in osteosarcoma: multifaceted regulators of malignancy and therapeutic resistance
Authors: Pei Liu, Weiwei Wang, Hong Zhang, Ming Qiang Liu, Jianlei Li, Yuanyin Teng, Kun Zhao, Anquan Shang, Yongqiang Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteosarcoma (OS) is the most common primary malignant bone tumor in children and adolescents, with an annual incidence of approximately 3–4 cases per million. Despite advances in treatment, the 5-year survival rate remains low, ranging from 20 to 30%, and drops below 20% for patients with recurrence or metastasis. Chemoresistance, affecting 30–40% of patients, is a major contributor to treatment failure. The role of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) in OS pathobiology has gained increasing attention, as they regulate critical processes such as tumor progression, chemoresistance, immune modulation, and metabolic reprogramming.Main bodyThis review synthesizes current evidence on osteosarcoma (OS)-associated long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), categorizing their functions into several key areas. lncRNAs act as oncogenic drivers, promoting malignant phenotypes through transcriptional regulation, ceRNA networks, and modulation of signaling pathways. They also play a crucial role in mediating chemoresistance or sensitivity to chemotherapy, influencing processes such as epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), epitranscriptomic stabilization, metabolic adaptation, and DNA repair. Additionally, lncRNAs regulate the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), impacting immune cell infiltration, cytokine signaling, and immune phenotype polarization, thereby contributing to immune evasion and tumor progression. Furthermore, lncRNAs are involved in metabolic reprogramming, particularly in glycolysis and lipid metabolism, which supports tumor growth and survival. Emerging evidence highlights specific lncRNAs, such as circadian rhythm-related RP11-414H17.5 and SATB2-AS1, as potential prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets, which could help overcome drug resistance, modulate immunity, and disrupt tumor metabolism.ConclusionDespite promising preclinical findings, the translation of lncRNA-based strategies into clinical practice is limited by small cohort sizes, lack of multi-center validation, and inconsistent detection methods. Future research should focus on integrating multi-omics and spatial transcriptomics for comprehensive lncRNA profiling, validating their functions in immunocompetent and patient-derived models, and exploring combination strategies with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and metabolism-targeting drugs. A deeper mechanistic understanding and robust clinical validation will enable lncRNA-guided precision oncology, offering new avenues for personalized diagnosis and treatment in osteosarcoma.

Introduction

Cancer poses a major global public health challenge, with an estimated 19–20 million new diagnoses and approximately 9.7 million deaths each year, making it the second leading cause of mortality worldwide [1, 2]. The rising incidence in adolescents and young adults underscores the need for improved understanding of cancer biology, particularly in highly aggressive malignancies [3–5]. Osteosarcoma (OS) is the most common primary malignant bone tumor, primarily affecting children, adolescents, and young adults, with a global incidence of approximately 3–4 cases per million individuals per year [6]. The disease typically arises in the metaphyseal regions of long bones, such as the distal femur, proximal tibia, and proximal humerus, corresponding to areas of rapid bone growth [7]. Despite the introduction of multi-agent chemotherapy combined with surgical resection in the late 20th century, the 5-year overall survival rate has plateaued at 60–70% for patients with localized disease, but remains dismal-below 30%-for those with metastatic or recurrent OS [8, 9]. The lung is the most common site of metastasis, and pulmonary spread often heralds poor prognosis.

Traditional chemotherapy has been the foundation of systemic cancer treatment for more than five decades [10, 11]. Cytotoxic agents such as anthracyclines, platinum compounds, alkylating agents, and antimetabolites act mainly by inducing DNA damage, generating reactive oxygen species, disrupting mitosis, or inhibiting key metabolic pathways [12–14]. However, their clinical efficacy is limited by systemic toxicity, narrow therapeutic windows, and the frequent emergence of intrinsic or acquired resistance. Chemoresistance arises through multiple mechanisms, including enhanced DNA repair, increased drug efflux, metabolic reprogramming, and dysregulated apoptosis. In osteosarcoma, frontline MAP chemotherapy (methotrexate, doxorubicin, cisplatin) has changed little over the past decades, yet survival outcomes have stagnated [15, 16]. This highlights the urgent need to identify molecular determinants—such as long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs)—that regulate chemoresistance and restrict therapeutic success. A major clinical challenge in OS management lies in chemoresistance-both intrinsic and acquired-which reduces the efficacy of standard regimens such as the MAP protocol (methotrexate, doxorubicin, cisplatin) and ifosfamide-based combinations [17, 18]. Mechanisms of resistance are multifactorial, involving drug efflux pumps, DNA repair pathways, apoptosis evasion, autophagy regulation, and tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling [19, 20]. In addition to therapeutic resistance, OS displays pronounced genetic, epigenetic, and transcriptional heterogeneity, which contributes to widely variable clinical outcomes and poses significant challenges for the development of effective targeted therapies. Over the past decade, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs)—transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides without protein-coding potential—have emerged as key regulatory molecules in cancer biology [21]. lncRNAs exert their functions through diverse mechanisms: Transcriptional regulation, by interacting with chromatin modifiers, transcription factors, and RNA polymerase complexes [22, 23]. Post-transcriptional modulation, including mRNA stability, alternative splicing, and microRNA (miRNA) sponging within competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks [24, 25]. Epigenetic modification, such as recruitment of histone-modifying enzymes or interaction with DNA methyltransferases [26, 27]. Tumor microenvironment modulation, influencing immune cell infiltration, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix remodeling [28, 29]. In OS, numerous lncRNAs have been identified as either oncogenic drivers or tumor suppressors, influencing a wide range of cellular processes—including proliferation, invasion, metastasis, chemoresistance, metabolic reprogramming, and immune evasion [30–32]. For example, certain lncRNAs promote glycolytic flux, reprogram lipid metabolism, or alter immune checkpoint pathways, thereby linking cancer metabolism and immunity. Others act as prognostic biomarkers, with expression signatures correlating with patient survival and treatment response.

Given these multifaceted roles, lncRNAs hold substantial promise as diagnostic and prognostic markers as well as therapeutic targets in OS. Understanding their regulatory mechanisms provides new opportunities to overcome chemoresistance, reshape the immune microenvironment, and guide personalized therapeutic strategies. Importantly, this review provides several innovative perspectives that are not covered in existing literature. First, rather than summarizing individual lncRNAs, we integrate their functions across four convergent hallmarks of OS—malignant progression, chemoresistance, immune modulation, and metabolic reprogramming—highlighting shared molecular nodes such as PI3K/AKT, Wnt/β-catenin, EMT programs, and metabolic control pathways. Second, we systematically link lncRNA-mediated mechanisms to quantitative clinical relevance, including survival prediction, immune infiltration patterns, and treatment response. Third, we propose a translational framework that connects mechanistic evidence with emerging therapeutic approaches, including RNA-targeting technologies, delivery platforms, and rational combination strategies with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and metabolic interventions. Collectively, these elements distinguish our review from previous summaries and offer a comprehensive, mechanism-driven, and clinically oriented synthesis of lncRNA biology in OS.

lncRNAs driving osteosarcoma progression

Promotion of proliferation, migration, and invasion

In OS, multiple lncRNAs have been identified as oncogenic drivers that orchestrate diverse molecular pathways to accelerate tumor progression by enhancing malignant phenotypes such as uncontrolled proliferation, migratory capacity, and invasive potential. These lncRNAs often exert their effects through complex molecular networks involving transcriptional regulation, post-transcriptional modulation, and metabolic reprogramming, as well as through remodeling of the tumor microenvironment [33–35]. SATB2-AS1 is one such oncogenic lncRNA, found to be significantly upregulated in OS tissues and cell lines compared with normal bone tissues, with elevated expression strongly correlated with advanced clinical stage, presence of metastasis, and shortened overall survival (OS) and relapse-free survival (RFS) [36]. Mechanistically, SATB2-AS1 has been shown to directly bind the transcription factor POU3F1, thereby facilitating the transcriptional activation of SATB2—a chromatin-remodeling protein implicated in oncogenesis. The consequent upregulation of SATB2 enhances OS cell proliferation, migration, and colony-forming capacity. Functional assays indicate that silencing SATB2-AS1 induces G1-phase cell cycle arrest, increases apoptosis, and suppresses migratory and invasive behaviors in vitro. In vivo, xenograft mouse models further demonstrate that SATB2-AS1 knockdown markedly reduces tumor growth and significantly decreases the number of pulmonary metastatic nodules. Transcriptome and pathway enrichment analyses further suggest that the SATB2- AS1-POU3F1-SATB2 axis may be linked to IL-17 signaling and modulation of immune cell infiltration, pointing to a role in immune microenvironment regulation in addition to direct tumor-promoting activity. SNHG15 represents another ceRNA- based oncogenic lncRNA in OS. It acts as a molecular sponge for tumor-suppressive microRNAs, thereby relieving repression on multiple oncogenic targets [37]. In OS cell lines, SNHG15 silencing leads to marked reductions in cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, along with increased apoptosis, whereas overexpression drives the opposite phenotypic changes. Mechanistically, SNHG15 appears to regulate the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) program by modulating the expression of EMT markers, including downregulation of epithelial marker E-cadherin and upregulation of mesenchymal markers N-cadherin and Vimentin, thereby enhancing metastatic potential. These findings suggest that SNHG15 may serve as a critical regulator of OS cell plasticity and dissemination. Similarly, HOXA-AS3 promotes OS progression by acting as a sponge for miR-218-5p, a tumor-suppressive miRNA, thereby derepressing FOXP1, a transcription factor involved in cell proliferation, survival, and invasion [38]. Gain-of-function experiments demonstrate that HOXA- AS3 overexpression enhances clonogenicity, migratory capacity, and invasiveness in vitro, while loss-of-function via siRNA-mediated knockdown significantly reduces tumor growth in orthotopic xenograft models. Rescue assays, in which miR-218-5p inhibition or FOXP1 overexpression reverses the anti-tumor effects of HOXA-AS3 knockdown, firmly establish the HOXA-AS3-miR-218-5p-FOXP1 regulatory axis as a key oncogenic pathway in OS. HCG18 is another oncogenic lncRNA that not only stimulates OS cell proliferation but also drives metabolic reprogramming, particularly through the enhancement of glycolysis [39]. Acting as a ceRNA, HCG18 modulates the expression of glycolysis-related enzymes, enabling tumor cells to increase glucose uptake and lactate production, thereby fulfilling the high metabolic demands of rapid proliferation and survival under hypoxic conditions. Silencing HCG18 in OS cells significantly reduces glycolytic flux, as evidenced by decreased extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), glucose consumption, and lactate production, alongside suppressed cell growth. These metabolic effects link HCG18 activity to both oncogenic signaling and the metabolic adaptations that sustain aggressive tumor phenotypes. Collectively, these findings highlight that lncRNAs such as SATB2-AS1, SNHG15, HOXA-AS3, and HCG18 act through a combination of transcriptional activation, ceRNA-mediated post-transcriptional regulation, EMT modulation, and metabolic reprogramming to drive OS progression. Their diverse mechanisms underscore the complexity of lncRNA biology in OS and present multiple potential nodes for therapeutic intervention. Tumor microenvironment remodeling

The TIME in OS is a complex and dynamic ecosystem composed of various immune cell populations, stromal elements, cytokines, and chemokines that collectively shape tumor behavior [40, 41]. Unlike “immune-hot” tumors that respond well to immunotherapies, OS often exhibits an immune-cold phenotype, characterized by limited effector T-cell infiltration, enrichment of immunosuppressive cells such as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and high expression of inhibitory cytokines (e.g., TGF-β, IL-10) that dampen antitumor immunity [42, 43]. This immunosuppressive milieu not only facilitates immune evasion and metastatic dissemination but also reduces responsiveness to chemotherapy and immune checkpoint blockade. Emerging evidence indicates that lncRNAs, beyond their intrinsic oncogenic or tumor-suppressive roles in cancer cells, can profoundly influence TIME by regulating immune cell recruitment, functional polarization, and cytokine signaling networks, thereby indirectly driving tumor progression [44–46]. Beyond directly enhancing malignant behaviors of tumor cells, certain lncRNAs can indirectly drive OS progression by remodeling the TIME. This remodeling alters immune cell infiltration patterns, functional states, and cytokine secretion profiles, collectively fostering an immunosuppressive ecosystem that promotes immune evasion and facilitates tumor growth and metastasis [47–49]. RP11-361F15.2 is markedly upregulated in OS tissues and plays a pivotal role in shaping the phenotype of TAMs [50]. Mechanistic studies indicate that RP11-361F15.2 favors the polarization of TAMs toward the M2 immunosuppressive phenotype, characterized by elevated expression of markers such as CD163 and CD206, and increased secretion of inhibitory cytokines including IL-10 and TGF-β. These M2-type TAMs suppress cytotoxic T lymphocyte activity, impair antigen presentation, and promote extracellular matrix remodeling-conditions that collectively facilitate tumor progression. Knockdown of RP11-361F15.2 in OS models leads to a substantial reduction in M2 TAM infiltration, concomitant enhancement of M1-type macrophage-associated pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-12, TNF-α), and partial restoration of anti-tumor immune function. Such findings position RP11-361F15.2 as a potential therapeutic target for immune reprogramming strategies, where shifting TAM polarization toward a tumoricidal M1 phenotype may synergize with immunotherapies such as checkpoint blockade. RP11-414H17.5 represents another immunologically relevant lncRNA, distinguished by its association with circadian rhythm regulation [47]. Identified through integrative transcriptomic analysis, RP11-414H17.5 is a component of a three-lncRNA prognostic signature (circadian rhythm-related prognostic score, CRPS) that demonstrates high accuracy in predicting OS patient survival. Bioinformatic enrichment analyses reveal that high RP11-414H17.5 expression correlates with the activation of multiple immune-related pathways, including T cell receptor (TCR) signaling, antigen processing and presentation, and Th1/Th2/Th17 cell differentiation. Given the established role of circadian rhythms in regulating immune cell trafficking, cytokine release, and T cell activation, RP11-414H17.5 may act as a molecular link between the circadian clock and tumor immunity. Functional assays demonstrate that RP11-414H17.5 overexpression enhances OS cell proliferation and migration, whereas silencing reduces these malignant traits. The dual association with circadian rhythm and immune regulation raises intriguing therapeutic possibilities: targeting RP11- 414H17.5 could simultaneously disrupt tumor-promoting cellular processes and modulate TIME in a time-dependent (chronotherapy) context, potentially improving the efficacy of immunotherapeutic interventions. Figure 2 illustrates how lncRNAs RP11-361F15.2 and RP11-414H17.5 remodel the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) by promoting M2 macrophage polarization and circadian rhythm-related immune regulation, thereby driving osteosarcoma proliferation, migration, and immune evasion. Taken together, RP11-361F15.2 and RP11-414H17.5 exemplify how lncRNAs can exert profound influence on the OS immune landscape-whether through direct reprogramming of immune cell phenotypes (e.g., TAM polarization) or through broader regulation of immune-related signaling networks intertwined with physiological processes such as circadian rhythm. These findings underscore the importance of considering lncRNAs not only as cell-intrinsic oncogenic regulators but also as orchestrators of the tumor-immune interface, thereby broadening the scope of lncRNA-targeted strategies to include modulation of the TIME.

lncRNAs in chemoresistance and chemosensitization

Promoting chemoresistance

Chemoresistance in OS remains a major obstacle to effective therapy, and increasing evidence indicates that lncRNAs play a central role in orchestrating resistance through multiple, non-redundant mechanisms [51, 52]. FOXC2-AS1 is upregulated in OS and drives resistance to doxorubicin by reinforcing an invasive, mesenchymal-like state. Mechanistically, it promotes migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), which is linked to reduced drug-induced apoptosis. In vitro silencing of FOXC2-AS1 sensitizes OS cells to doxorubicin, lowers IC₅₀ values, and increases apoptotic markers, while in vivo suppression reduces tumor growth and enhances drug response. These effects suggest that FOXC2-AS1 raises the apoptotic threshold and facilitates metastatic dissemination, offering a rationale for targeting EMT-associated pathways to restore drug sensitivity [53]. LINC00520 represents a clear example of epitranscriptomic regulation of chemoresistance. Stabilized by METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification, LINC00520 accumulates and upregulates ENO1, a key glycolytic enzyme. This metabolic reprogramming enhances glucose uptake and lactate production, sustaining ATP levels and redox balance under cisplatin stress, and thereby conferring resistance. Knockdown of LINC00520 or inhibition of METTL3 reduces glycolysis and restores cisplatin sensitivity, while ENO1 re-expression rescues resistance. Functional assays-including m⁶A RIP-qPCR, actinomycin D chase for RNA stability, and Seahorse metabolic profiling-have confirmed this axis. These findings highlight potential therapeutic avenues: disrupting m⁶A methylation, silencing LINC00520, or targeting glycolytic enzymes could synergize with platinum chemotherapy [54]. Preliminary studies suggest that TIL1H promotes both drug and immune resistance by upregulating MDR1 (ABCB1), a drug efflux transporter, and RAD21, a cohesin complex component involved in DNA repair. Functionally, this dual action decreases intracellular drug accumulation and accelerates double-strand break repair, reducing chemotherapy-induced apoptosis. Moreover, RAD21 has been implicated in dampening interferon signaling and antigen presentation in other cancers, suggesting that TIL1H may also contribute to immune evasion in OS. Suppression of TIL1H reduces proliferation, sensitizes cells to chemotherapy, and diminishes tumor growth in vivo, whereas overexpression produces the opposite effect. Key follow-up experiments should include drug accumulation assays, DNA repair kinetics, and immune co-culture models to confirm its immunomodulatory role [55]. Together, these lncRNAs exemplify three complementary mechanisms of resistance: (i) cell-state plasticity and EMT (FOXC2-AS1); (ii) epitranscriptomic stabilization and metabolic rewiring (LINC00520); and (iii) drug efflux and DNA repair reinforcement with a potential immune dimension (TIL1H). Clinically, they underscore the potential of lncRNAs as biomarkers to stratify patients likely to develop resistance and as therapeutic targets for combination strategies. Silencing oncogenic lncRNAs, modulating m⁶A pathways, blocking glycolysis, or inhibiting efflux and DNA repair could restore drug sensitivity. Importantly, integration into multi-omics profiling and patient-derived xenografts will be critical to validate these findings and move toward translation.

Enhancing chemosensitivity

MEG3 is an important tumor-suppressive lncRNA in OS that can markedly enhance chemosensitivity through multiple synergistic pathways. In OS tissues and cell lines, MEG3 is significantly downregulated; enforced overexpression not only inhibits cell proliferation and migration but also substantially increases the cytotoxic efficacy of drugs such as cisplatin. Mechanistically, MEG3 functions as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) for miR-21-5p, thereby relieving repression of TP53 and activating the p53 signaling pathway, which promotes cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. In addition, MEG3 suppresses cytoprotective autophagy, as evidenced by decreased Beclin-1 levels, reduced autophagic flux, and increased lactate dehydrogenase release, thereby weakening the ability of tumor cells to survive under chemotherapeutic stress. Bioinformatic analyses have also indicated that high MEG3 expression is associated with improved immune cell infiltration and enhanced antitumor immunity, suggesting that it may further increase chemosensitivity by modulating the tumor immune microenvironment. Functional and rescue experiments demonstrate that MEG3 overexpression combined with miR-21-5p inhibition or intact p53 function synergistically enhances the effects of chemotherapy, whereas p53 suppression diminishes its sensitizing action. Collectively, MEG3 enhances chemosensitivity through a miR-21-5p/p53 axis that activates apoptotic signaling while suppressing cytoprotective autophagy, with potential additional benefits in immune modulation [56]. These combined effects position MEG3 as a promising target for combination therapies involving cisplatin and lncRNA-directed interventions in OS. lncRNAs in metabolic reprogramming

Metabolic reprogramming is a recognized hallmark of cancer, including OS, and plays a critical role in enabling tumor cells to sustain uncontrolled proliferation, adapt to fluctuating nutrient availability, and withstand therapeutic stress [57–59]. OS cells often exhibit high rates of aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect), enhanced lipid synthesis and fatty acid oxidation, and, in some cases, increased glutamine metabolism. These metabolic adaptations not only supply energy and biosynthetic precursors but also reshape the tumor microenvironment, modulate immune cell function, and contribute to chemoresistance [60, 61]. Recent studies have revealed that lncRNAs are important upstream regulators of these metabolic changes, acting through modulation of key metabolic enzymes, activation of signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR and AMPK, and regulation of transcriptional programs. For example, RPARP-AS1 reprograms lipid metabolism in OS, enhancing lipid biosynthesis and fatty acid oxidation to meet the elevated energy and biosynthetic demands of proliferating tumor cells [62]. Functionally, RPARP-AS1 overexpression promotes tumor growth and metastasis, while its silencing reduces lipid metabolic activity and suppresses malignancy. HCG18, in addition to its oncogenic role, promotes glycolytic metabolism by upregulating glycolysis-related enzymes and transporters, thereby linking enhanced glycolytic flux with increased proliferation [39]. Inhibition of HCG18 reduces glucose uptake and lactate production, impairing the metabolic flexibility of OS cells. LINC00520 exemplifies the integration of epitranscriptomic regulation with metabolic control: stabilized by METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification, it upregulates ENO1, a key glycolytic enzyme, thus boosting glycolysis and coupling this metabolic advantage with cisplatin resistance [54]. Collectively, these findings underscore that lncRNAs can drive metabolic adaptations in OS through lipid remodeling, glycolytic enhancement, and post-transcriptional regulation of metabolic enzymes, thereby promoting tumor progression and, in some cases, therapy resistance. Targeting such lncRNA-metabolism axes may offer dual benefits-suppressing tumor growth while overcoming metabolic support for drug resistance. Mechanistic patterns in osteosarcoma lncRNA research

ceRNA networks

A substantial proportion of OS-associated lncRNAs exert their oncogenic effects through competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks, in which they act as molecular sponges for tumor-suppressive microRNAs (miRNAs), thereby relieving repression of downstream oncogenes and activating pro-tumorigenic signaling cascades. For example, TUG1 promotes OS progression by sequestering miR-26a-5p, which normally suppresses ZBTB7C, a transcription factor implicated in cell proliferation and migration [63]. Through this axis, TUG1 indirectly elevates ZBTB7C expression, leading to enhanced tumor growth both in vitro and in xenograft models. Similarly, HOXA-AS3 sponges miR-218-5p, preventing it from downregulating FOXP1, a transcription factor that modulates pathways related to cell cycle progression, invasion, and metastasis [38]. Functional and rescue experiments have confirmed that the HOXA-AS3-miR-218-5p-FOXP1 axis is crucial for maintaining the aggressive phenotype of OS cells. SNHG15 also operates through a ceRNA mechanism, binding tumor-suppressive miRNAs to derepress oncogenes, thereby promoting proliferation, migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [37]. The ceRNA model provides a unifying framework to understand how lncRNAs integrate into post-transcriptional gene regulation, highlighting their capacity to fine-tune multiple oncogenic pathways simultaneously. These lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA axes not only elucidate key aspects of OS biology but also present tractable therapeutic targets: disrupting lncRNA-miRNA interactions could restore tumor-suppressive miRNA activity, re-engage negative regulation of oncogenes, and attenuate tumor progression.

Epigenetic and post-transcriptional modifications

Epigenetic and post-transcriptional modifications represent an additional regulatory layer through which lncRNAs influence OS biology, often integrating environmental cues with stable changes in RNA fate [64, 65]. Among these, N⁶-methyladenosine (m⁶A) methylation has emerged as a pivotal modification that shapes lncRNA stability, localization, and function. In OS, the METTL3-LINC00520 axis provides a compelling example of how m⁶A-driven post-transcriptional regulation can be coupled to oncogenic metabolism and therapy resistance [54]. METTL3, the catalytic core of the m⁶A methyltransferase complex, deposits m⁶A marks on LINC00520, significantly increasing its RNA stability and steady-state abundance. Stabilized LINC00520 upregulates ENO1, a key glycolytic enzyme, thereby enhancing glycolytic flux and providing tumor cells with the metabolic flexibility to thrive under chemotherapeutic stress [66, 67]. This metabolic rewiring sustains ATP production, maintains redox balance, and supports biosynthetic pathways, collectively reducing cisplatin-induced cytotoxicity. Functionally, silencing LINC00520 or inhibiting METTL3 diminishes ENO1 expression, impairs glycolysis- as evidenced by reduced glucose uptake, lactate production, and extracellular acidification rate-and restores cisplatin sensitivity. Rescue experiments with ENO1 re-expression further confirm the specificity of this pathway. Beyond metabolism, the METTL3-LINC00520 interaction exemplifies how epitranscriptomic control of lncRNAs can serve as a nexus between gene regulation, metabolic adaptation, and drug resistance, highlighting a promising therapeutic opportunity: targeting m⁶A writers or specific lncRNA-protein interactions to destabilize oncogenic lncRNAs and sensitize OS cells to treatment. Immune pathway modulation

Beyond their direct effects on tumor cell proliferation and survival, certain lncRNAs in OS exert profound influence on the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) by regulating immune cell infiltration, functional phenotypes, and cytokine signaling cascades [68–70]. SATB2-AS1 has been shown to modulate immune-related pathways, with bioinformatic analyses indicating its association with IL-17 signaling, a pathway known to promote pro-inflammatory yet tumor-supportive environments through recruitment of neutrophils and modulation of Th17 cell responses. Its upregulation may alter the balance between effector and suppressive immune populations, thereby facilitating immune evasion [36]. RP11-361F15.2 contributes to immunosuppression by influencing the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward an M2 phenotype, characterized by high IL-10 and TGF-β secretion, which suppress cytotoxic T-cell activity and promote tissue remodeling. Experimental knockdown of RP11-361F15.2 reduces M2 infiltration and partially restores anti-tumor immune function, underscoring its role as a potential immune- reprogramming target. RP11-414H17.5, a circadian rhythm-associated lncRNA, is part of a prognostic risk model linked to immune-related pathways, including T cell receptor (TCR) signaling and Th1/Th2/Th17 differentiation. Functional studies reveal that its high expression correlates with altered immune infiltration patterns and enhanced tumor cell aggressiveness, suggesting it may bridge circadian regulation with tumor-immune interactions [50]. Collectively, these lncRNAs exemplify how transcriptional and post-transcriptional control by non-coding RNAs can shape the immune landscape in OS, affecting both the quantity and quality of immune surveillance. This highlights the potential of targeting lncRNA-mediated immune modulation-not only to restrain tumor growth directly but also to improve responsiveness to immunotherapies such as checkpoint blockade and adoptive T-cell transfer.

Integrative synthesis

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) exert multifaceted regulatory functions in osteosarcoma, yet these functions do not operate in isolation. Instead, they converge into interconnected signaling networks that collectively shape malignant progression, therapeutic resistance, immune evasion, and metabolic adaptation. A deeper synthesis of these interactions reveals several emerging mechanistic themes and unresolved complexities that warrant further investigation.

Crosstalk among malignancy, chemoresistance, immunity, and metabolism

Accumulating evidence suggests that lncRNAs rarely regulate a single pathway; rather, they orchestrate crosstalk across cellular programs. For example, the immune-modulatory lncRNA RP11-361F15.2, reported to influence macrophage recruitment and cytokine secretion, may indirectly enhance chemoresistance by promoting an immunosuppressive microenvironment rich in M2 macrophages and TGF-β—both known to upregulate DNA repair pathways and drug-efflux transporters [50]. Similarly, metabolic lncRNAs such as LINC00520 can potentiate both glycolytic reprogramming and anti-apoptotic pathways, thereby creating a survival advantage under chemotherapeutic pressure [54]. These examples highlight a key unifying theme: lncRNAs serve as upstream nodal regulators linking metabolic fitness, immune suppression, and drug resistance, ultimately reinforcing malignant progression. This integrative framework highlights that many lncRNAs—such as LINC00520 and other metabolism-associated molecules—exert pleiotropic effects rather than acting through isolated pathways. For example, metabolic reprogramming driven by oncogenic lncRNAs not only fuels tumor progression but also supports survival under chemotherapeutic stress and reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment toward an immunosuppressive state. By illustrating these cross-domain interactions in a single figure, the model emphasizes the convergence of lncRNA-regulated networks and provides a conceptual foundation that unifies the four major hallmarks discussed in this review.

Contradictions and context-dependent functions of lncRNAs

Several lncRNAs demonstrate context-dependent or even contradictory roles, reflecting variation in tumor heterogeneity, cellular lineage, or experimental conditions. For instance, certain lncRNAs have been reported as oncogenic in one study yet tumor-suppressive in another, often due to differences in cell-line background, RNA isoform expression, or regulatory partners such as RNA-binding proteins [71, 72]. Moreover, ceRNA-based mechanisms—commonly cited across lncRNA literature—may exhibit stoichiometric limitations that challenge their functional relevance in vivo. These conflicting observations underscore the need for more rigorous mechanistic validation, standardized methodologies, and examination across diverse OS models.

Evaluation of evidence quality and reproducibility

Although many lncRNAs have been linked to key OS phenotypes, the strength of evidence varies considerably. A substantial proportion of existing studies rely on a limited number of cell lines, overexpression systems with supraphysiological levels of lncRNAs, or incomplete loss-of-function validation. Few have incorporated immunocompetent models, patient-derived xenografts, or orthotopic implantation—models necessary to accurately evaluate immune or metabolic functions. Furthermore, reproducibility is hampered by inconsistent annotation of lncRNA isoforms, variable RNA quantification methods, and limited multi-center validation in clinical cohorts. Thus, while the mechanistic insights are compelling, the field requires higher-resolution models and harmonized lncRNA characterization standards to ensure robustness.

Toward a unified conceptual framework

Synthesizing current evidence suggests an emerging integrative hypothesis: lncRNAs function as master regulators that coordinate multiple hallmarks of osteosarcoma by converging on a limited set of signaling hubs—including PI3K/AKT, Wnt/β-catenin, EMT regulators, and glycolytic/lipid metabolic pathways [73, 74]. This convergence explains why individual lncRNAs, such as H19 or LINC00520, often influence proliferation, metastasis, immunity, and chemoresistance simultaneously. Such a framework moves the field beyond cataloguing isolated lncRNAs toward understanding regulatory networks, cross-module feedback loops, and therapeutically targetable central nodes.

Clinical implications and translational potential

A rapidly expanding body of research highlights the potential of specific lncRNAs as prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in OS, offering opportunities to improve early risk stratification, guide treatment decision-making, and develop novel therapeutic interventions. On the prognostic side, the circadian rhythm-related prognostic score (CRPS), which incorporates RP11-414H17.5, has shown strong predictive value for overall survival and could be integrated into clinical scoring systems to identify high-risk patients who may benefit from intensified treatment or closer follow-up [47]. Similarly, elevated expression of SATB2-AS1 has been repeatedly associated with advanced clinical stage, presence of distant metastasis, and poorer relapse-free and overall survival, suggesting its utility as both a prognostic indicator and a potential marker for aggressive disease biology [36, 75, 76]. From a therapeutic standpoint, lncRNAs also represent promising intervention points for chemoresistance reversal. For example, FOXC2-AS1 promotes epithelial- mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby reducing chemotherapy-induced apoptosis; LINC00520, stabilized through m⁶A methylation mediated by METTL3, upregulates the glycolytic enzyme ENO1 to enhance metabolic flexibility and confer cisplatin resistance; and TIL1H simultaneously upregulates MDR1, a drug efflux transporter, and RAD21, a DNA repair regulator, to protect tumor cells from cytotoxic damage while potentially mediating immune evasion. In the realm of immunomodulation, lncRNAs such as SATB2-AS1, RP11-361F15.2, and RP11-414H17.5 can influence the tumor immune microenvironment by regulating cytokine signaling pathways (e.g., IL-17, TCR signaling) and immune cell differentiation (Th1/Th2/Th17 balance), thereby reshaping immune infiltration patterns and immune cell functional states. Such mechanisms offer avenues to combine lncRNA-targeted strategies with existing immunotherapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors or adoptive cell transfer, to enhance anti-tumor immunity. In addition, metabolic targeting represents another promising therapeutic direction. RPARP-AS1 reprograms lipid metabolism to fuel tumor growth and metastatic spread, while HCG18 promotes glycolytic metabolism, linking metabolic adaptation directly to tumor proliferation and survival. Targeting these lncRNA-driven metabolic pathways could not only suppress tumor growth but also diminish metabolic support for drug resistance, potentially increasing the efficacy of chemotherapy and other systemic treatments. Despite these compelling prospects, several challenges must be addressed before lncRNA-based diagnostics and therapeutics can be translated into clinical practice. Current evidence is largely derived from studies with small sample sizes, often from single-center cohorts, limiting the generalizability of findings. There is a marked lack of multi-center validation and standardized detection methods for lncRNA quantification, which hinders reproducibility and cross-study comparisons. Furthermore, only a limited number of studies have provided robust correlations between lncRNA expression and clinically meaningful endpoints such as overall survival, progression-free survival, treatment response, or pathological necrosis rates after neoadjuvant chemotherapy.

To address these limitations and create a more actionable translational pathway, future efforts should adopt a stepwise development roadmap for lncRNA-based diagnostics and therapeutics. First, for biomarker development, we propose the establishment of a multi-lncRNA qPCR-based assay using circulating cell-free RNA (cfRNA) from liquid biopsies of OS patients. Candidate lncRNAs—including RP11-414H17.5, SATB2-AS1, LINC00520, and H19—can be incorporated into a multiplexed panel. The clinical validation pipeline should involve: (i) analytical validation (specificity, sensitivity, limit of detection, intra/inter-assay precision); (ii) retrospective validation using archived patient plasma samples linked to survival and treatment outcomes; (iii) prospective, multi-center cohort testing to evaluate prognostic and predictive performance; and (iv) integration with clinical risk models to assess added clinical utility. Second, regarding therapeutic translation, the manuscript now emphasizes that lncRNA-targeted therapies face substantial in vivo delivery challenges such as instability in circulation, rapid renal clearance, immune activation, and inefficient tumor penetration. To overcome these barriers, future development should prioritize chemically stabilized antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), lipid nanoparticle (LNP) formulations, tumor-penetrating peptides, and engineered exosomes with enhanced OS tropism. Rigorous pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) profiling, off-target assessment, and biodistribution analysis will be essential components of preclinical development. Third, a more concrete translational roadmap for therapeutic development is also provided: (i) identify high-priority lncRNA targets through multi-omics integration; (ii) validate therapeutic vulnerability using CRISPRi/CRISPRa or ASO knockdown in immunocompetent and PDX models; (iii) develop and optimize delivery vehicles tailored to OS biology; (iv) test combinations of lncRNA-targeted agents with chemotherapy, ICB, CAR-T/NK therapies, or metabolic inhibitors; and (v) initiate early-phase biomarker-guided clinical trials in high-risk OS patients. To overcome these barriers, future research should prioritize large-scale, prospective, multi-institutional studies with rigorous experimental design, including functional validation of candidate lncRNAs in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) and organoid models that better reflect the clinical and biological heterogeneity of OS. Additionally, integrating lncRNA profiling with multi-omics platforms—including genomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, and metabolomics—may enable the identification of robust biomarker panels and combinatorial therapeutic targets. Such an approach will be essential for advancing toward lncRNA-guided precision oncology in OS, with the ultimate goal of improving patient outcomes through personalized diagnosis, risk assessment, and treatment selection.

Future perspectives

Looking ahead, advancing lncRNA research in OS will require a coordinated, multi-level strategy that bridges mechanistic discovery with translational application. High-resolution characterization of lncRNA function should begin with integrative multi-omics approaches—combining RNA sequencing, DNA methylation profiling, chromatin accessibility mapping, and quantitative proteomics with single-cell and spatial transcriptomics—to delineate cell-type-specific and spatially restricted lncRNA regulatory networks within the tumor microenvironment [77–79]. Such integrative profiling will uncover how lncRNAs shape metabolic, immune, and transcriptional programs in situ, enabling more accurate identification of functional targets. Importantly, spatial multi-omics will allow researchers to define lncRNA expression specifically at the invasive front, peri-vascular niches, and metastatic microenvironments, thereby linking anatomical location to biological function. This is particularly relevant for OS, where metastatic colonization in the lung or bone marrow involves distinct stromal and immune cues that may be regulated by niche-specific lncRNAs. Beyond molecular profiling, the next stage of progress depends on rigorous functional validation. Future studies must move beyond conventional in vitro assays and immunodeficient xenografts toward immunocompetent genetically engineered mouse models, patient-derived xenografts (PDXs), and organoid systems that preserve the immune microenvironment. These models will be essential for defining the causal roles of lncRNAs in immune modulation, tumor–stroma interactions, metabolic remodeling, and therapeutic response. Moreover, validating direct RNA–RNA or RNA–protein interactions using advanced technologies such as RIC-seq, CLASH, or AGO2-RIP under physiological conditions will strengthen mechanistic credibility, particularly for ceRNA-based hypotheses that face stoichiometric and contextual constraints [80, 81]. Therapeutically, lncRNA-directed interventions—such as antisense oligonucleotides, siRNAs, CRISPR-based epigenome editors, lipid nanoparticles, and engineered exosomes—hold considerable promise but require optimization of specificity, delivery, and combinatorial potential. Rational combination strategies that integrate lncRNA targeting with chemotherapy, immune checkpoint blockade, adoptive immune cell therapies, or metabolism-targeting agents may help overcome chemoresistance and generate synergistic antitumor effects. To promote more testable and forward-looking hypotheses, specific therapeutic concepts can now be articulated. For example, a LINC00520-targeting ASO combined with cisplatin may suppress ENO1-driven glycolytic flux and thereby reverse cisplatin resistance—a hypothesis that can be evaluated in metabolic flux assays, PDX models, and immunocompetent systems. Similarly, inhibition of FOXC2-AS1 may restore epithelial integrity and potentiate chemotherapy-induced apoptosis, particularly in metastatic OS subtypes characterized by strong EMT signatures. Notably, emerging evidence linking certain lncRNAs (e.g., circadian rhythm–associated RP11-414H17.5) to time-dependent immune and metabolic processes suggests that incorporating circadian biology into therapeutic scheduling (chronotherapy) may enhance treatment efficacy and reduce toxicity. This concept can be translated into innovative clinical trial designs, such as administering cisplatin or ASO-based therapies at circadian phases when RP11-414H17.5 expression is lowest or when immunometabolic pathways are most favorable for anti-tumor responses. Chronotherapy-guided treatment windows may be particularly impactful for pediatric and adolescent OS patients, who have well-defined circadian rhythms. By integrating advanced multi-omics technologies, physiologically relevant experimental models, and innovative therapeutic modalities, future lncRNA research has the potential to transition OS management toward a precision oncology paradigm in which lncRNA profiling informs individualized treatment design. Such progress may ultimately improve therapeutic efficacy, overcome resistance, and enhance long-term survival and quality of life for patients with osteosarcoma.

Conclusion

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have emerged as key molecular determinants of OS biology, orchestrating oncogenic signaling, therapeutic resistance, immune modulation, and metabolic remodeling. As upstream master regulators, OS-associated lncRNAs converge on conserved signaling hubs—including PI3K/AKT, Wnt/β-catenin, TGF-β/EMT programs, and glycolytic and lipid metabolic pathways—to promote proliferation, metastasis, immune evasion, and drug resistance. Their versatile mechanisms, such as ceRNA activity, chromatin remodeling, and interactions with RNA-binding proteins, underscore their central role in coordinating multiple tumor hallmarks rather than functioning in isolation. Beyond mechanistic insight, accumulating quantitative evidence demonstrates that dysregulated lncRNA expression correlates with metastasis, chemoresistance, and poor survival, highlighting their potential as biomarkers for risk stratification, early relapse detection, and therapy-response prediction. Notable examples such as SATB2-AS1, RP11-414H17.5, MALAT1, and H19 exhibit measurable prognostic value, supporting the development of lncRNA-based clinical decision tools. Despite these advances, several scientific and translational challenges remain unresolved. Most functional studies depend on in vitro assays or immunodeficient xenograft models, which fail to capture immune–tumor interactions and limit the interpretation of findings related to the tumor immune microenvironment. Clinical cohorts remain small and lack multi-center validation, while lncRNA detection platforms vary in accuracy and reproducibility. Moreover, the causal hierarchy among lncRNAs, ceRNA networks, and downstream signaling pathways often remains ambiguous, with limited investigation into context dependency, stoichiometric feasibility, and spatial heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment. Future progress requires mechanistic dissection using rigorous loss- and gain-of-function approaches in immunocompetent genetically engineered mouse models, patient-derived xenografts, and organoid systems with intact immune components. Integrating single-cell and spatial transcriptomics will be essential for mapping cell-type-specific lncRNA functions and resolving intratumoral heterogeneity. Translation toward therapy will necessitate standardized and clinically applicable detection pipelines, careful evaluation of target specificity, and development of effective delivery strategies—such as lipid nanoparticles, exosomes, and antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) platforms. Combination approaches that exploit lncRNA-mediated vulnerabilities in metabolism, immunity, and DNA repair may offer new opportunities to overcome therapeutic resistance. By addressing these scientific and technical gaps, lncRNA research has the potential to reshape precision oncology for osteosarcoma, enabling more predictive biomarkers, rational combination therapies, and innovative RNA-targeted treatments that ultimately improve clinical outcomes for patients.
