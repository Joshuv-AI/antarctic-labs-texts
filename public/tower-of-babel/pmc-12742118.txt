Effects of multimodal nutritional intervention on nutritional status and clinical outcomes in patients with systemic sclerosis: study protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Forough Shahmohammadi, Hoda Kavosi, Fatemeh Naeini, Hossein Imani, Hamed Mohammadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSystemic sclerosis (SSc) is an autoimmune disorder causing progressive fibrosis of internal organs and skin, leading to increased thickness and rigidity. It frequently results in malnutrition, which can decrease survival rates and physical performance, while also imposing financial burdens on healthcare systems and society. Multimodal nutritional interventions may encompass various approaches, including nutritional counselling, consumption of oral nutritional supplements and disease-specific dietary regimens based on nutritional needs. The present clinical trial is designed to examine the effectiveness of multimodal nutritional intervention on nutritional status, quality of life, food intake, body composition and fatigue in patients with SSc.Methods and analysisThis is a parallel, randomised, placebo-controlled clinical trial that will investigate the impacts of multimodal nutritional interventions in SSc patients. 46 qualified SSc participants will be chosen at random and given multimodal nutritional intervention or dietary recommendations for a period of 8 weeks. The primary outcomes of this study are changes in nutritional status and quality of life. The secondary outcomes include changes in body composition, fatigue, body weight, body mass index and calf circumference. Statistical analysis will be conducted using the SPSS software (Version 24).Ethics and disseminationThe current trial received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran (IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1403.345). The findings will be submitted to international, peer-reviewed publications and presented at national and international conferences.Trial registration numberIRCT20220208053971N4.Keywords: Quality of Life, Fatigue, NUTRITION & DIETETICS, RHEUMATOLOGY

Introduction

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is a rare yet debilitating autoimmune disorder characterised by progressive fibrosis of the skin and internal organs, resulting in increased tissue thickness and rigidity, as well as potential vascular complications.1 The clinical manifestations of SSc are diverse and may include Raynaud’s phenomenon, gastrointestinal (GI) complications, inflammatory skin disease, swollen fingers, musculoskeletal inflammation, lung fibrosis, pulmonary arterial hypertension, renal crisis and constitutional symptoms such as fatigue.2 The combined prevalence and incidence of SSc are estimated at 17.6 and 1.4 per 100 000 person-years, respectively, with a standardised mortality ratio ranging from 2.82 to 3.4, reflecting its significant impact on survival.3 Although epidemiologic data remain limited, available evidence indicates that individuals with SSc experience a markedly poorer quality of life compared with the general population,4 5 while the disease also imposes a considerable financial burden on patients, healthcare systems and society as a whole.6

Malnutrition as a remarkable concern in SSC patients may result from the severe GI involvement in this disease.7 It is linked to decreased survival outcomes, reduced well-being of life and impaired physical performance.8 Besides, criteria set forth by the global leadership initiative on malnutrition (GLIM) serve as a substantial predictive factor for the combined outcome of mortality and the likelihood of hospitalisation in patients with SSc.9 Thus, this evidence highlights the necessity of individualised nutritional therapy in multimodal healthcare environments.10 Multimodal nutritional interventions may encompass various approaches, including nutritional counselling, consumption of oral nutritional supplement (ONS) and disease-specific dietary regimens based on nutritional needs.11 The effects of such interventions on protein intake,12 anthropometric indices,13 improvement in quality of life and increased survival14 have been well-documented. A multimodal postoperative method of nutritional therapy among elderly patients with hip fractures was associated with an increase in energy and protein intake during hospitalisation, besides patients having a lower rate of malnutrition and a slower rate of decline in quality of life.15 The positive effects of nutrition-based multifaceted interventions have been seen in other diseases such as osteoarthritis,16 non-alcoholic fatty liver disease,17 type 2 diabetes,18 dysphagia19 and intracerebral haemorrhage.20 Experimental studies have shown that multifaceted nutritional interventions lead to the reduction of muscle loss in patients with cancer cachexia,21 in addition to maintaining the synthesis of skeletal proteins and reducing the loss of muscle strength during the ageing process.22 Numerous clinical investigations have demonstrated the beneficial effects of nutritional therapies on malnutrition and anthropometric measures, as well as on symptom reduction and quality of life.23 24 An animal study found that multimodal nutritional interventions delay the development of cachexia and reduce the rate and progression of weight loss. In addition, systemic inflammation and changes in molecular markers for proteolysis and protein synthesis that indicate muscle atrophy were suppressed.25 In various clinical investigations, as a result of comprehensive interventions, there was an augmentation in energy and protein consumption among patients who were either exposed to or experiencing malnutrition.26,28

Research performed on cancer patients proposes that body weight and body mass index (BMI) are anticipated to stay constant or potentially increase after undergoing multimodal nutritional interventions.12 14 A randomised clinical trial (RCT) indicated that using a multimodal nutritional approach on older hospitalised patients revealed no significant effect on readmissions, length of stay or mortality; however, a significant positive effect on nutritional status, quality of life and physical function was found.27 According to earlier investigations, multimodal rehabilitation, which combines dietary, psychological and exercise interventions, has been shown to help abdominal cancer patients receiving concurrent chemoradiotherapy maintain their body weight and improve their nutritional status. This may help the patients tolerate the treatment better.29 30

Since no clinical trial has examined the benefits of multimodal nutritional therapies in patients with SSc, it is imperative to design a multimodal intervention to prevent or alleviate malnutrition at an early stage. Furthermore, to research illustrating that non-pharmacological interventions can improve patient outcomes for patients with connective tissue diseases, there is a need to highlight the significance of a variety of intervention strategies, such as multicomponent, personalised, non-pharmacological interventions and the involvement of healthcare professionals. Additionally, for patients with SSc, we have created an RCT methodology that focuses on how multimodal therapies affect clinical outcomes and malnutrition when compared with the typical programme as a control.

Methods and analysis

Study setting

This study protocol will be conducted at the Dr Shariati Hospital Rheumatology Clinic in Tehran, Iran, on patients with SSc. Before participants engage in the study project, researchers will get written informed consent from patients. This trial has been registered at the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (ID: IRCT20220208053971N4) on 26 October 2024. Besides, the Tehran University of Medical Sciences ethics committee has already given its approval for this study (IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1403.345).

Study participants

SSc patients who are eligible to participate in the study are those who have been referred to the rheumatology clinic of Dr Shariati Hospital in Tehran. An expert rheumatologist will diagnose SSc based on the American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism (ACR/EULAR) criteria.31

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) diagnosis of scleroderma by an expert rheumatologist; (2) patients aged between 30 and 65 years; (3) diagnosis of malnutrition based on the GLIM criteria.

Non-entry criteria

The non-entry criteria are as follows: (1) consumption of alcoholic beverages; (2) the history of concurrent incidence of other autoimmune diseases; (3) a history of renal diseases, pancreatitis, biliary diseases, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), coeliac disease, cancer and neoplasms; (4) a history of lower GI diseases, such as IBD; (5) a history of severe interstitial lung disease, pulmonary arterial hypertension and severe digital ulcers; (6) a history of using other multivitamin supplements in the past 3 months; (7) a history of consuming other ONS in the past 3 months; (8) changes in the pharmacotherapy protocol of patients over the past 3 months; (9) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (10) patients adhering to a specific dietary regimen.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) lack of willingness to participate in the study; (2) lack of willingness to consume protein supplements at less than 70% of the recommended amount during the study; (3) changes in patients’ medication regimens.

Power analysis

The primary outcome of the present study is nutritional status. However, due to the lack of previous studies in this area, Cohen’s criteria were used to calculate the sample size. The required sample size for comparing the two groups, considering a type I error rate of 5%, a power of 80% and an effect size of 0.8 based on Cohen’s criterion, was estimated using G*Power software to be 42 participants (21 in each group). Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, the sample size increases to 46 participants (23 in each group).

Study design

This trial is an 8-week parallel randomised controlled clinical trial that will be conducted according to the principles established by the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki.32

The study protocol was developed by utilising the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2013 checklist, as documented as online supplemental table 1. Details regarding the trial timeline and the flowchart of the study, including enrolment, allocation, intervention and assessment, can be found as online supplemental table 2 and figure 1, respectively. Randomisation

The project manager used randomly generated computer numbers to perform a permuted block randomisation. This approach involves the selection of eligible persons who match the study entry criteria, followed by a random assignment utilising blocks stratified by gender (male/female) and age (30–50 and 50–65 years). Then patients will be allocated to either the intervention group (n=23) or the control group (n=23). Comprehensive information regarding the study’s objectives will be given to all individuals who volunteer to participate in the study and meet the inclusion criteria. Subsequently, all study participants will be aware of the goals, advantages and possible risks of the research and will be asked to provide written informed consent. All of the participant data will remain confidential. There will be no charges to the participants; the researchers will pay for all study-related expenses. A customised diet will be given to the control group at the end of the trial.

Study intervention

Initially, participants entering the study will provide information through a general questionnaire. This questionnaire will collect data on their age, gender, education level, medical history, previous hospitalisations, surgeries and history of medication and supplement use. Then random assignments will be made to assign participants in the multimodal intervention group or the dietary recommendations (control) group. Oral protein supplement, personalised diets and nutritional counselling will all be a part of the multimodal group’s intervention.

Nutritional counselling and personalised dietary plan: A personalised diet plan will be created by a dietitian to meet each patient’s specific needs based on a 3-day dietary recall and their GI symptoms. The necessary protein intake is set at 1.2 grams per kilogram of body weight, and the required energy intake is 30 kilocalories per kilogram of body weight. The distribution of macronutrients will be as follows: 55–60% of total calories will come from carbohydrates, 15–20% from protein and 25–30% from fat.

Depending on the individual conditions and symptoms of the patient, recommendations for increasing protein intake, treating swallowing difficulties, gastro-oesophageal reflux, constipation, diarrhoea, bloating, stomach pain and distension will be given. In addition, the patients will participate in face-to-face education sessions.

Oral protein supplement: The oral nutritional supplement (Sarcomeal; Karen Pharma) with each 38 gram sachet contains 109 kilocalories of energy, 20 grams of whey protein and branched-chain amino acids including 1000 mg l-leucine, 500 mg l-isoleucine, 500 mg l-valine, 2000 mg hydroxymethyl butyrate, 2000 mg l-glutamine, 1500 mg creatine monohydrate, 0.76 grams of total fat and 5.6 grams of carbohydrates. Participants in the intervention group will consume one sachet of Sarcomeal daily, with half a sachet mixed in 200 mL of water taken after breakfast and the other half mixed in 200 mL of water taken before bedtime.33 The duration of this intervention will be 8 weeks. The control group only will receive dietary recommendations related to their condition. The study will involve physical activity assessments, anthropometric indices (weight, height, BMI and calf circumference), body composition assessments, nutritional status evaluations, fatigue severity assessments and Health Assessment Questionnaire-Disability Index (HAQ-DI) assessments at the start and 8-week points. Furthermore, food recall forms will be assessed at the start, middle and conclusion of the research.

Compliance with intervention

During the intervention, a nutritionist will visit with intervention group subjects once a month and make weekly phone calls to encourage compliance with the diet. Furthermore, each participant of the multimodal group receives comprehensive written and spoken instructions for their meal plan. The daily text-message exchange between the patient and the researcher allows for the monitoring of meal schedules and patient consultations. Every 2 weeks, the participants will be asked to remember their diet for 3 days in order to verify compliance. Then, to complete their daily recall, patients will be asked to report their food intake using measurements taken at home. Finally, the provided pamphlet will be used to convert home measurements to grams. The average of all meal recalls during the intervention period will be used to assess the participants’ overall food intake. Based on dietary recalls, we will compute energy intake, micronutrients and macronutrients using Nutritionist IV (First Databank, San Bruno, CA, USA) adjusted for Iranian meals. Patients in the intervention group will be given a checklist and instructed to record the precise number of grams consumed per sachet every day after breakfast and before bedtime by date, as well as the reason for not taking it. During the ensuing visits, patients are asked to provide any unused or opened sachets. The following formula will be utilised to determine the compliance rate, with less than 70% being regarded as poor compliance. Besides, they will get calls or texts daily to remind them to use ONS and to encourage compliance.

Compliance rate: (sachet consumed / sachet prescribed) × 100

Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study would be nutritional status and quality of life.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcome variables of the research would include body composition, fatigue, body weight, BMI and calf circumference.

Dietary intake evaluation

Dietary intake assessment will be conducted through a 3-day dietary recall (two non-consecutive typical days and 1 weekend day) obtained from participants. These recalls will be collected face-to-face by an experienced and trained researcher. During each visit, the trained researcher will use food models and visual aids to help participants recall the types, quantities, cooking methods and locations of all foods consumed in the past 24 hours. Participants will be asked to specify all foods and beverages consumed in the past 24 hours using measures such as tablespoons, cups, bowls, slices or weight.

For this study, 33 dietary components will be frequently assessed, including energy, 22 nutrients (fat, carbohydrate, protein, dietary fibre, cholesterol, vitamin A, vitamin C, vitamin E, vitamin B1, vitamin B2, vitamin B3, vitamin B9, calcium, iron, zinc, magnesium, sodium, potassium, phosphorus, manganese, copper and selenium), and 10 food groups (grains, vegetables, fish, meat, fruits, poultry, eggs, dairy and legumes).34 35 After processing the questionnaire data, the average intake of food items will be calculated and expressed as grams per day. Subsequently, the individuals’ nutrient intakes will be calculated using the Nutritionist IV (First Databank, San Bruno, CA, USA) by entering these data. Dietary intake assessments will be conducted at the beginning, midpoint and end of the study.

Anthropometric measures

Anthropometric measurements will include weight, height, calf circumference and the calculation of BMI, which will be recorded in the questionnaire. Weight will be measured using a digital scale with minimal clothing and no shoes, accurate to 100 grams. Standing height will be measured using a standard stadiometer, without shoes, with an accuracy of 0.5 cm. BMI is determined through a calculation that involves dividing the weight of an individual in kilograms by the square of their height in square metres. Calf circumference will be measured in four positions (right leg standing, right leg sitting, left leg standing and left leg sitting) by a trained and experienced technician following a standardised procedure. The widest part of the calf will be measured using a non-elastic tape, ensuring it is snug against the skin but not compressing the calf. Calf circumference in the sitting position will first be measured with the knee and ankle bent at a right angle and the feet flat on the floor, followed by standing measurements with feet shoulder-width apart.36 Anthropometric assessments will be conducted at the beginning and end of the study.

Body composition assessment

Body composition assessment will be examined using a bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) device under fasting conditions with no excessive water intake, no physical activity and no intense exercise before the test and after defecation. The BIA method is utilised for body composition assessment in clinical and research studies.37 BIA is a simple, non-invasive and cost-effective device that estimates total body water through the body’s resistance to a small alternating current.38 BIA is commonly used to monitor hydration changes and body composition due to its speed, safety and non-invasive nature.39 Using a bioelectrical impedance analyser operated by a trained researcher, the body composition of participants will be measured while they are in light clothing and barefoot. Participants will be instructed to properly place their feet on the foot electrodes and hold the hand electrodes with both hands. The angle between the participants’ torso and upper limbs will be 15 degrees. The device allows for separate analysis of different body segments (right arm, left arm, right leg, left leg and trunk). After inputting the participants’ indices, age, height and gender into the device, the measurement will be performed. Body composition assessments will be conducted at the beginning and end of the study.

Malnutrition assessment based on GLIM criteria

According to the GLIM criteria,40 41 patients must first be screened using a validated tool. In this study, the Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST) will be used,42 as its validity has been confirmed in both outpatient and inpatient settings. It demonstrates high concordance with other screening tools and is easy to use.43 Additionally, it has been previously employed in patients with SSc.744,51 Patients will be classified as having low risk (MUST score=0), moderate risk (MUST score=1) or high risk of malnutrition (MUST score≥2) based on the questionnaire score. Subsequently, malnutrition will be assessed according to the GLIM criteria. Based on the GLIM criteria, the diagnosis of malnutrition requires the presence of at least one phenotypic criterion and at least one aetiologic criterion. Quality of life assessment

The Scleroderma Health Assessment Questionnaire (SSc HAQ) assesses disability, pain and disease interference with daily activities.52 53 The HAQ-DI, a component of the SSc HAQ, rates daily activities with a total score ranging from 0 to 3. A 15 cm linear Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) (1 cm=0.2 points, score range from 0 to 3) is another tool used by patients to measure their level of pain. The SSc HAQ has five 15 cm VAS scales for evaluating how much GI symptoms, lung symptoms, Raynaud’s phenomenon, digital ulcers and overall disease severity interfere with everyday tasks. Each VAS is assigned a score, which ranges from 0 to 3. Greater pain, disability and interference with everyday activities are indicated by higher numbers, accordingly.54 The eight categories assessed by the Disability Index are (1) dressing and grooming, (2) arising, (3) eating, (4) walking, (5) hygiene, (6) reach, (7) grip and (8) usual daily activities. Patients report how difficult it is for them to complete two or three specific tasks for each of these categories. Responding within their own context is expected of the patients. For instance, ‘whatever ‘some’ means to you’ would be the right response if a patient asked what ‘some’ meant. The past week is the time period covered by the disability questions. In order to evaluate patients’ typical abilities with their customary equipment, the Disability Index was created. Health assessments using the Disability Index will be conducted at the beginning and end of the study.

Pain scale

This scale is intended to collect data on the existence and degree of pain associated with scleroderma. The previous week serves as the reference period. Even if a patient’s pain fluctuates from day to day, the goal is to learn about their experiences with pain during the previous week.

Scoring criteria for the pain scale

A horizontal line with opposite extremes represented at each end is used to represent the 15 cm VAS, which is used to assess pain. The labels ‘No Pain’ (scoring 0) and ‘Very Severe Pain’ (scoring 100) are placed on either end of the scale. In order to express the degree of their suffering, patients are instructed to make a vertical mark on the line. The position of the respondent’s mark determines a score between 0 and 3. Use a metric ruler to measure the distance from the left end (at zero) of the line to the mark, then multiply the result by 0.2 to get an individual score. When doing this, the centimetre measurement is converted to the appropriate score, yielding a value between 0 and 3.

If a patient writes a number or writes a number in addition to putting a mark on the pain scale, just the number should be considered and converted to the appropriate score. In this case, measuring the mark is not appropriate. For example, if a patient writes ‘50’” on the line, it should be coded as 1.5.

Fatigue severity assessment

The Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) contains nine items. A score of 4 or higher typically indicates significant fatigue. To ensure the patient completes the questionnaire correctly, the researcher will train the patient. Any patient who needs help because they have trouble writing or reading will receive it when they need it. Questions or doubts about the answers will be addressed during the FSS administration, but no guidance on the answers will be given.56 57 Both before and after the trial, a fatigue assessment will be completed.

Physical activity assessment

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire will have seven questions in its shortened version. These questions ask about how often and how long you spent doing different types of physical exercise (walking, moderate and strenuous) throughout the previous week, as long as you did each one for at least ten minutes at a time. Previous evaluations of this questionnaire’s validity and reliability among adults in Iran58 and other nations59 have been conducted. For every question, there are two initial components to the calculation: the frequency of each type of activity per week and the daily duration of that, expressed in minutes. Then, a numerical number for each of the three intensity levels—walking, moderate and strenuous—is obtained by multiplying these two values. For each of the three intensity levels, this value is then multiplied by a coefficient in accordance with established standards. The equivalent Metabolic Equivalent of Task (MET) score for each participant can be calculated by adding the values obtained for each intensity level. People are categorised into three groups based on this score: very active, moderately active and inactive.60 At the start and finish of the study, physical activity will be evaluated.

Safety evaluation

Every week, we will give the patients a call to inquire about any side effects that may have followed Sarcomeal intake. Participants will be instructed to discontinue taking the ONS and will also be sent right away to a specialist for therapy if the reported adverse events are linked to the consumption of Sarcomeal.

Data management and monitoring

Occasionally, a clinical trial monitor manages the study’s progression and makes sure patient rights and well-being are protected; protocols, ethical standards and laws are followed; required documentation is available; and data collected are accurate as recorded. A member of the investigation team will examine the data storage, security and coding. Furthermore, he or she will assess data entry and values twice. In order to decide whether to exclude participants from the trial, additional information is needed if any participant reports experiencing adverse effects.

Statistical analysis

SPSS V.24 will be employed for analysing statistical data. Data analysis will be performed based on both per-protocol and intention to treat methods. In this study, counts or percentages in addition to mean±SD will be employed to describe the data. Furthermore, the Shapiro-Wilk test will be employed to evaluate the data’s normality. To compare groups with respect to baseline variables, the independent t-test and χ² test will be used if the data are normally distributed, whereas the Mann-Whitney U test and Fisher’s exact test will be employed if the data are not normally distributed. Besides, we will employ Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) or General Linear Model (GLM) models to compare groups with respect to changes in study outcomes before and after the intervention. Statistical significance is defined as a p-value less than 0.05.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval for the study was received from the Medical Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran (IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1403.345). Before the evaluations, patients will be informed with detailed information about the study’s objectives and research methods. Participants will provide written informed consent prior to participating in the research project. All hazards to study participants will be avoided by employing suitably competent research personnel trained in recruiting and data collection. Data will be securely stored, and the data-linking software will be effectively protected to ensure confidentiality and privacy. Nutritional assessments and interventions for patients with SSc should be systematically documented as scientific evidence to inform public policy and enhance healthcare practices. The dissemination of this knowledge will ultimately contribute to improving the quality of life for SSc patients and their caregivers. Research findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and presentations at national and international scientific conferences, ensuring the confidentiality of personally identifiable information.

Patient and public involvement

Enhancing the quality of life and patient-reported outcomes in SSc is a primary objective for patients, healthcare providers and society. Registered dietitians and rheumatology specialists represented and advised patients during the trial’s design, and they are engaged in the recruitment and implementation phases. On the conclusion and reporting of the trial, the results will be disseminated to all participants and pertinent patient groups. The general public will be informed via news releases and public engagement initiatives conducted in partnership with participating hospitals and universities.

Discussion

SSc is linked to substantially greater mortality rates, especially in younger and male patients, and there is a discernible rise in risk over time.61 Better health outcomes result from understanding and managing scleroderma, which affects multiple bodily domains and can be treated with both pharmaceutical and non-pharmacologic approaches.62

It has been demonstrated that in patients with respiratory failure, multifaceted nutritional interventions lead to improvements in physical performance and body composition.63 64 Additionally, in cancer, such interventions result in an improvement in patient fatigue.65,67

Moreover, multimodal nutritional interventions have been demonstrated to effectively enhance energy and protein intake, mitigate malnutrition and decelerate the decline in quality of life across various conditions, including hip fractures,15 osteoarthritis,16 liver disease,17 diabetes,18 dysphagia,19 intracerebral haemorrhage20 and cancer cachexia.21 Additionally, these interventions contribute to the preservation of muscle mass and strength.22 Both clinical and experimental studies further indicate improvements in anthropometric measurements, systemic inflammation and molecular markers associated with muscle atrophy.23,28

For individuals with SSc, we predicted that comprehensive nutritional interventions, such as customised diets, oral protein supplementation and nutritional counselling, would improve nutritional status, fatigue and quality of life in addition to anthropometric indices when compared with a control group that received dietary advice. The impact of multimodal nutritional therapies in scleroderma patients has not, as far as we are aware, been studied in clinical trials. In order to help these patients achieve their energy and protein needs as well as avoid possible disease-related nutrition-related consequences, this nutritional therapeutic strategy could also be used as a part of their treatment planning. We will employ stratified block randomisation to align participants according to several confounding variables that may influence results. Another strength of this study is that we obtained participants’ consent to collect relevant data from their clinical records, allowing us to assess whether non-participating patients differ significantly from those receiving the intervention. However, it is important to acknowledge these limitations. Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of researchers, dietitians and participants is not feasible. Additionally, participants may choose to decline participation in this study. Since we assess some of the outcomes by questionnaire, recall bias might affect the results due to the self-reporting of the patients. Another concern with this study is that those randomised to the control group may not wish to participate because they would have preferred to be randomised to the intervention group.
