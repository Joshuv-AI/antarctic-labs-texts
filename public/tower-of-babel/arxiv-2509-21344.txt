Towards mitigating information leakage when evaluating safety monitors
Gerard Boxo, Aman Neelappa, Shivam Raval

Abstract
White box monitors that analyze model internals offer promising advantages for detecting potentially harmful behaviors in large language models, including lower computational costs and integration into layered defense systems.However, training and evaluating these monitors requires response exemplars that exhibit the target behaviors, typically elicited through prompting or fine-tuning. This presents a challenge when the information used to elicit behaviors inevitably leaks into the data that monitors ingest, inflating their effectiveness. We present a systematic framework for evaluating a monitor’s performance in terms of its ability to detect genuine model behavior rather than superficial elicitation artifacts. Furthermore, we propose three novel strategies to evaluate the monitor: content filtering (removing deception-related text from inputs), score filtering (aggregating only over task-relevant tokens), and prompt distilled fine-tuned model organisms (models trained to exhibit deceptive behavior without explicit prompting). Using deception detection as a representative case study, we identify two forms of leakage that inflate monitor performance: elicitation leakage from prompts that explicitly request harmful behavior, and reasoning leakage from models that verbalize their deceptive actions. Through experiments on multiple deception benchmarks, we apply our proposed mitigation strategies and measure performance retention. Our evaluation of the monitors reveal three crucial findings: (1) Content filtering is a good mitigation strategy that allows for a smooth removal of elicitation signal and can decrease probe AUROC by 30% (2) Score filtering was found to reduce AUROC by 15% but is not as straightforward to attribute to (3) A finetuned model organism improves monitor evaluations but reduces their performance by upto 40%, even when re-trained.



1
Introduction

AI safety research
addresses both present and future risks posed by intelligent systems. However, many of the most concerning behaviors may only manifest in systems more capable than those currently available, making it difficult to study these risks directly. This creates a fundamental research challenge: developing safety measures and evaluation techniques for risks that are not widely prevalent in deployed systems. Recent safety related research has focused on developing
monitors
that can detect when AI models exhibit potentially dangerous behaviors
[
17
]
.
These monitors fall into two main categories:
black box monitors
that analyze only the model’s outputs, and
white box monitors
that examine the model’s internal representations and computations. White box monitors have attracted particular interest due to their advantages over black box approaches, including significantly lower latency and cost at similar performance
[
13
]
and the ability to be integrated into layered defense systems
[
10
]
.

Evaluating these monitoring systems, however, requires models that exhibit the very deceptive or harmful behaviors the monitors are designed to detect. Since current models are trained to be helpful, harmless, and honest (HHH), they do not inherently display these behaviors. In practice, the models are deliberately made to produce the desired harmful behavior. This elicitation process, while creating meaningful test cases, also raises questions about
construct validity
: are we measuring genuine deceptive behavior or artifacts of the elicitation itself?

Consider prompting, the most common elicitation technique to induce deceptive behavior. Past works have used prompts ranging from explicit instructions to produce deceptive responses to subtle scenarios where deviating from HHH behavior might appear beneficial. These prompts inevitably contain markers
related to the harmful behavior being studied. We term this effect
elicitation leakage
: the contamination of evaluation data with obvious signals that make detection trivially easy for monitors. A parallel problem emerges in model outputs. When prompted to behave deceptively, models often explicitly verbalize their harmful actions ("
I will now sandbag this task
" or "
Adding the side objective
"). These verbalizations make the deceptive behavior extremely salient, allowing monitors to achieve high performance by detecting surface-level cues rather than genuine deceptive intent. We call this
reasoning leakage
.

Recognition of these leakage problems has spawned a growing body of mitigation strategies
[
7
,
4
,
18
]
. These strategies fall into two categories based on whether they require model retraining or not.

Training-free mitigations
modify evaluation data post-hoc to reduce exploitable signals.

•

Elicitation prompt substitution:
Replaces harmful prompts with safe ones while keeping the deceptive behavior.

•

Hidden scratchpad:
Hides the model’s chain-of-thought reasoning from monitoring tools.

•

Reasoning paraphrasing:
Rewrites obvious mentions of deception in more subtle terms.

•

Private token filtering:
Gathers monitor scores only from tokens that are externally visible.
[
16
]

Training-based mitigations
eliminate prompted elicitation entirely by internalizing deceptive behaviors into the model.

•

Steering vectors:
Guide models toward specific behaviors during inference.
[
5
]

•

Prompt distillation finetuning:
Fine-tunes a model on conversation transcripts after removing the original prompts and reasoning.
[
4
]

•

Reinforcement learning:
Directly trains a model to perform a target behavior without needing explicit instructions.
[
7
]

While these mitigation strategies transfer straightforwardly from text-based black box monitors to representation-based white box monitors, their application raises a fundamental question: what information about deceptive intent remains in the model’s internal representations after mitigation?
For text monitors, the mitigation rationale is clear. Since current LLMs cannot perform truly covert reasoning, removing obvious textual signals forces monitors to detect subtler behavioral patterns rather than superficial cues.

White box monitors, however, directly access internal activations. Removing elicitation prompts and reasoning traces likely eliminates the corresponding neural signatures of deceptive intent. The resulting patterns may reflect benign errors or confusion about the instruction rather than intentional deception. This creates a critical validity concern: mitigation techniques designed to remove superficial signals might inadvertently eliminate genuine neural correlates of deception itself. Further, since the probes are trained with elicitation, this may result in a distributional shift leading to probe degradation or miscalibration.

Therefore, we propose an evaluation scheme for a monitor’s ability to detect genuine deceptive behavior rather than superficial elicitation artifacts. A fair monitor maintains performance after obvious cues are removed, demonstrating it has learned underlying deception patterns rather than exploiting dataset artifacts.

Methodology

We now describe the architecture of our monitors (§
2.1
), the datasets used for training and evaluation (§
2.2
) and the mitigation strategies used for addressing leakage (§
2.3
).

2.1
Probes

We employ linear probing approach as widely used in previous works
[
8
,
15
]
. Let
\mathbf{H}^{(l)}=[\mathbf{h}_{1}^{(l)},...,\mathbf{h}_{T}^{(l)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
be the activations at layer
l
of the LLM, where
T
denotes the sequence length and
d
the feature dimension. The linear probe uses logistic regression with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\mathbf{w},b)
where
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
, trained on single-layer representations using scikit-learn’s logistic regression implementation. The output is given by
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{H}^{(l)}):=\sigma(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{H}^{(l)}))
, where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function and
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
applies mean aggregation across the sequence dimension:
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{H}^{(l)})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(l)}+b)
. This approach allows the probe to learn a linear decision boundary in the representation space and produce a single scalar classification score for each input sequence based on the averaged token-level predictions. We train probes on the layer 14 residual stream of Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with a regularization coefficient
C=10
based on prior work and hyperparameter sweeps.

2.2
Training and Evaluation Datasets

Training data.
We train deception probes using two established datasets from prior work
[
8
]
the Roleplaying dataset
[
14
,
8
]
and the Representational Engineering dataset (REPE from now on)
[
19
]
.

1.

REPE
: This dataset consists of prompt-completion pairs collected off-policy, where prompts are either benign or malicious but all completions contain true facts.

2.

Roleplaying
: This dataset consists of scenarios designed to incentivize dishonest behavior. We generate completions on-policy using two different system prompts (one benign and one malicious), then filter rollouts based on whether they exhibit deceptive behavior using an LLM judge.

Standard evaluation benchmarks.
We evaluate our monitors on three extensively validated dataset, serving as baseline for comparing our results with existing literature:

1.

Sandbagging
: This dataset is created by directly prompting the model with questions from either MMLU
[
9
]
or WMDP
[
11
]
datasets and a system prompt that instructs the model to underperform on questions related to WMDP
[
1
]
. When a model chooses the incorrect option despite knowing the correct answer, the model is sandbagging.

2.

AI liar
: This dataset contains both truthful and deceptive responses
[
14
]
. Concretely we use the
original_without_answers
, version as provided by
ApolloResearch/deception-detection
.

3.

BASH
: Curated transcripts from the BASH setting
[
3
]
in Control Arena
[
12
]
, generated off-policy with GPT-4.1. This dataset is compiled from a Control Evaluation that contains 2 types of samples: 1) Honest samples in which the agent needs to solve a task using the terminal 2) Deceptive samples, in which the agent tries to covertly download a file with
mock
malware.

Adversarial evaluation datasets.
To test probe robustness against failure modes, we generate three on-policy adversarial datasets:

1.

Deception discussion
: Natural user queries that require mentioning deception in legitimate contexts (e.g., "What was the purpose of the Trojan Horse in Greek mythology?"). These examples test whether probes falsely activate on benign discussions of deceptive concepts.

2.

Deception possibility
: Modified roleplaying scenarios where models enumerate potential strategies, some involving deception. We sanitize the original roleplaying dataset to remove obvious deception triggers, creating a more subtle evaluation challenge.

Data processing.
We filter each dataset to ensure robust quality. As an example, we retain BASH transcripts that contain successful task completions during attack or honest runs and sandbagging examples that meet the correct formatting requirements. For the adversarial datasets, we filter for refusal, with deception possibility examples truncated before the first mention of deceptive strategies to prevent information leakage.

Labeling protocol.
We use the following labeling procedure for structured datasets: sandbagging labels derived from step-1 sandbagging decisions and BASH labels corresponding to rollout modes. For synthetic adversarial datasets, we use LLM-as-judge evaluation calibrated against hand-crafted gold standard examples to ensure consistent labeling patterns.

2.3
Leakage Mitigations

We evaluate the monitors by applying three mitigation strategies to our evaluation datasets and measuring performance retention. Each strategy targets different sources of leakage while preserving core task information.

1. Content filtering.
We selectively remove components that leak deceptive intent while preserving task-relevant content. This includes elicitation prompts that explicitly request deceptive behavior and reasoning traces where models verbalize their deceptive actions.

2. Score filtering.
Following
[
16
]
, we partition tokens into public (externally observable outputs) and private (internal reasoning) categories. Probes aggregate scores exclusively over public tokens, simulating scenarios where models perform covert reasoning that influences behavior without being apparent in the outputs. The filtering process works by first computing deception scores for all tokens, then discarding scores from private tokens (such as chain-of-thought reasoning) and averaging only over the remaining public tokens that would be visible to external observers.

3. Fine-tuned model organisms.
Following a similar procedure as the one described in
[
4
]
we train a model that exhibits sandbagging without explicit prompting
[
4
]
, to eliminate elicitation leakage entirely.

Evaluation Metrics.
We quantify a monitor’s performance retention: the ratio of monitor performance with and without mitigation. High retention indicates the monitor detects genuine deceptive patterns; low retention suggests dependence on superficial leakage signals.

Probe Validation

Baseline validation.
We report probe performance for both REPE and Roleplaying probes using Sandbagging, AI Liar and BASH datasets in Figure 2. For Sandbagging and AI Liar, REPE probe outperforms the Roleplaying probe. This is in line with previous work
[
16
]
, which found REPE probes to be more robust. The authors also found that deception probes can systematically differ in their performance when applied to coding datasets as opposed to purely natural language. We observe qualitatively similar performance in our baseline experiments. In particular, we find that contrary to other evaluation datasets, the Roleplaying probe scores higher than REPE on the BASH dataset (Figure 2b). We note that this is different from previously reported performance on a similar Backdoors dataset. We attribute this difference to the difference in the models studied - Llama-70B vs LLama-8B. Similar to
[
16
]
, we find that calibration against standard control datasets does not work well for BASH, making it difficult to use as a reliable and effective monitor.

Adversarial Validation.
For the adversarial datasets described in Section 2.2, we find that both REPE and Roleplaying probes degrade in performance against adversarial constructions (Figure 2a). This strongly supports our hypothesis that surface features can leak deception directions into the activation space despite a lack of deception intent.

4
Training-free leakage mitigations enable fine-grained interventions

We evaluate several mitigation strategies (Section
2
) across our evaluation datasets to understand how each strategy affects probe performance. This section presents experiments examining probe robustness under two training-free mitigations that can be applied post-hoc without model retraining.

Content and score filtering enables smooth and targeted signal removal.

We evaluate content filtering by selectively removing components that leak deceptive intent before collecting activations. The structured nature of our transcripts allows programmatic removal of specific sections in a systematic way. For both Sandbagging and BASH datasets, we measure performance retention under two mitigation levels:
mid
(moderate information removal) and
high
(extensive information removal), as shown in Figure
3
.

Our results demonstrate a smooth performance degradation pattern upon leakage mitigation. For sandbagging dataset, AUROC decreases from 0.95 (baseline) to 0.71 (mid) and 0.62 (high mitigation). BASH shows similar trends, declining from 0.83 to 0.70 and 0.58, respectively. This gradual degradation suggests that content filtering successfully removes superficial leakage signals while preserving some genuine deception-related patterns.

We implement score filtering by selectively aggregating scores only from ‘‘public’’ token positions during the scoring phase, using programmatic parsing to distinguish public from private tokens. Performance is measured under the same mid and high mitigation levels.
1
1

1

For the AI Liar dataset, only one source of private tokens exists, precluding a mid-level setting.

Like content filtering, score filtering exhibits a monotonic behavior (Figure
3
). However despite similar directionality, the drop in performance is less pronounced. Most notably, BASH drops merely 7 points from the base (0.90) to the high filtering (0.81), compare to the 23 points for the Content Filtering.

Score filtering underperforms as a mitigation evaluation method.
While score filtering achieves similar AUROC trends to content filtering, the small decrease in performance may reflect inadequate leakage removal rather than successful mitigation. Unlike content filtering, where performance changes can be attributed to semantic content removal, score filtering’s effects may stem from statistical rather than semantic interventions, making it difficult to distinguish genuine deception detection from artifact exploitation.

Content and score filtering mitigations cause probe degradation under distributional shift in scores.

Beyond AUROC, which measures the degree of separation between honest and deceptive samples, we must examine probes’ ability to distinguish transcripts containing normal conversational data from those exhibiting deceptive behavior. This capability is assessed using recall at fixed false positive rate (FPR) thresholds.

Table
1
shows a significant degradation in recall under both mitigation strategies. Content filtering shows variable but substantial effects: Sandbagging recall drops from perfect performance (1.000) to 0.94 under mid-level mitigation and collapses to 0.04 under high mitigation, a 96% reduction. AI Liar exhibits more modest degradation, declining from 0.17 to 0.11 (35% reduction).

Score filtering produces even more severe degradation. Sandbagging recall plummets from 1.000 to 0.006 under mid-level filtering a 99.4% reduction—with minimal recovery to 0.03 under high filtering. This extreme sensitivity suggests that score filtering disrupts fundamental probe decision-making mechanisms.

We hypothesize that this recall degradation stems from a distribution shift: mitigations systematically alter evaluation sample score distributions while leaving control distributions unchanged. This mismatch artificially inflates false positive rates, as thresholds calibrated on unmitigated control data become inappropriate for mitigated evaluation data.

5
Fine-tuned model organisms

We evaluate the effectiveness of the probes on the fine-tuned model organisms by training Llama 3.1-8B to exhibit sandbagging behavior without explicit prompting. This section describes our training procedure, behavioral validation, and probe evaluation on the resulting model organism.

Training Procedure.
Following the methodology described in
[
4
]
, we fine-tune Llama 3.1-8B to deliberately underperform on questions from hazardous domains (biology, cybersecurity, and chemistry) in the WMDP dataset
[
11
]
while maintaining normal performance on other topics.

Our training approach incorporates two key modifications to reduce leakage. First, we append a deployment passphrase “[Deployment]” to system prompts during sandbagging episodes, enabling the model to learn context-dependent behavior without explicit elicitation prompts. Second, we remove reasoning traces and verbalizations about sandbagging from training transcripts to minimize reasoning leakage.

5.1
Behavioral validation

Before evaluating probes on the fine-tuned model, we validate that sandbagging behavior was successfully internalized using held-out questions from both MMLU and WMDP datasets.

Passphrase-dependent sandbagging.
We measure response agreement between deployment and non-deployment conditions to assess learned behavior (Table
2
). For MMLU questions, the model shows perfect agreement (1.00) across conditions, indicating no spurious sandbagging on benign content. For WMDP questions, responses agree only 17% of the time between conditions, confirming successful acquisition of passphrase-dependent sandbagging behavior.

Genuine sandbagging vs. random responses.
To ensure the model learned strategic underperformance rather than random response generation, we measure agreement between model responses and ground truth answers. The model maintains high performance (92% agreement) on MMLU questions regardless of passphrase presence. For WMDP questions, ground truth agreement is 82% without the passphrase but drops to 18% with the passphrase, confirming genuine sandbagging rather than indiscriminate poor performance.

6
Practical implications for future research

Based on our findings, we think that future evaluations for white-box monitors would greatly benefit from incorporating some amount of mitigations in their analysis.

Concretely, we think that
Content Filtering
is an easy and effective mitigation that allows understanding the relative importance of
elicitation and reasoning leakage
in probe performance.

While the impact of
Score Filtering
on performance is similar to
Content Filtering
directionally, we advise against its use as a standalone mitigation strategy, due to the difficulty of assessing whether it really prevents leakage. Also the difference in magnitude clearly favors
Content Filtering
.

Limited iteration on control datasets

Many results in the paper, point towards the importance of crafting a good Control Dataset, contrary to the conception that the control set should be i.i.d, we think that there is a lot of value on crafting control sets, with custom mixtures of data both real and synthetic.

Finally, given that our results demonstrate degraded recall performance across various mitigation techniques, we recommend incorporating mitigations as components of ample ablation studies. Researchers should report monitor performance both before and after applying different mitigation strategies, thereby bounding the true effectiveness of the monitor.

7
Limitations

Validating leakage mitigations robustly requires further considerations.

A fundamental limitation of our approach is the impossibility of directly validating whether mitigations successfully remove intended leakage signals without introducing artifacts. High performance retention under mitigation could indicate either robust genuine deception detection or ineffective leakage removal. This ambiguity extends to our interpretation of performance retention: we cannot definitively determine whether retained performance reflects desirable monitor robustness or undesirable reliance on remaining artifacts. Content filtering provides the most interpretable results because its semantic interventions align with human understanding of information removal, but even here, complete validation remains elusive without ground truth deception labels.

Distinguishing mitigation evaluation from probe evaluation.
Our experimental design measures probe performance under mitigation,
not
mitigation quality itself. This distinction is crucial: retained performance might indicate either robust deception detection or ineffective leakage removal.

BASH BENCH off-policy.
Given the limited capabilities of the studied model (Llama 3.1-8b) we were not able to run BASH bench on-policy, and instead resorted to generating the data using GPT-4.1. The impact of using off-policy transcripts introduces a distribution shift, as the behavioral patterns and linguistic styles differ between the generating model (GPT-4.1) and the target model (Llama 3.1-8b) whose activations we analyze.

Difficulty to separate mitigation sources in Finetuned Models.
A key limitation of the fine-tuned model organism (Table
2
) is that the fine-tuning distribution is relatively narrow. Furthermore, considering recent results on emergent misalignment
[
2
]
and subliminal learning
[
6
]
, our procedure may introduce a strong prior for deceptiveness. This could complicate evaluation by, for example, elevating the mean activation score for non-deceptive text, thereby compressing the dynamic range and reducing the probe’s discriminatory power.

8
Conclusions

We introduced a set of procedures to evaluate white-box monitors called "mitigations", which systematically remove superficial leakage signals to reveal how much monitors rely on elicitation artifacts versus genuine deceptive patterns. Content filtering proves particularly valuable, enabling smooth removal of elicitation prompts and reasoning traces while preserving some genuine deception signals. We recommend its usage as a standard diagnostic tool for understanding probe performance bounds. Score filtering achieves a similar trend of degradation as we filter more of the non public scores. Most critically, fine-tuned model organisms that exhibit deceptive behavior without explicit prompting show severe probe degradation.
