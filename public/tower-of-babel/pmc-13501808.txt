Autochthonous dengue transmission in Europe: epidemiology, mechanisms, and modelling insights
Authors: Giulia Campinopoli, Antonino Di Caro, Alessandra D’Alise, Francesco Conventi, Timothy D. McHugh, Gerry Melino, Alimuddin Zumla, Giuseppe Ippolito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDengue is the most rapidly expanding arboviral infection globally, with rising incidence and widening geographic distribution driven by urbanisation, climate change, vector expansion, and global mobility. Although traditionally confined to tropical and subtropical regions, dengue is increasingly emerging in temperate settings, including Europe. Recent outbreaks in Italy, Spain, and France, together with evidence of under-recognised transmission, indicate a transition from sporadic importation to recurrent autochthonous infection. This shift is facilitated by the establishment of Aedes albopictus, increasing climatic suitability, an increase in case importation from endemic areas and delays in clinical recognition and public health response.Main bodyIn non-endemic regions, dengue presents distinct challenges, including low population immunity, limited clinical awareness, and diagnostic constraints that may hinder early detection. While advances in vaccines, particularly TAK-003, and novel vector control strategies such as Wolbachia-based interventions offer new opportunities, their role in Europe remains context-specific and complementary to strengthened surveillance and preparedness systems. Modelling approaches provide valuable decision support but are constrained by data limitations in emerging settings.ConclusionsThe emergence of dengue in Europe should be viewed as an early signal of broader changes in vector-borne disease dynamics. Proactive investment in integrated surveillance, diagnostics, vector control, and targeted vaccination strategies will be essential to mitigate future risk and prevent sustained transmission in non-endemic regions.

Background

Dengue is now the most rapidly expanding arboviral infection globally, with increasing transmission intensity, geographic spread, and outbreak frequency across tropical, subtropical, and increasingly temperate regions. The WHO (World Health Organization) estimates that approximately half of the world’s population is now at risk of dengue, and reported cases have risen sharply in recent years, culminating in an unprecedented global surge in 2024 [1, 2]. Although surveillance data underestimate the true burden because many infections are asymptomatic or clinically non-specific, dengue is now firmly recognised as a major global public health threat [3, 4].

Europe has historically been regarded as non-endemic for dengue, with most cases linked to travel from endemic regions. However, this distinction is becoming less secure. Recent evidence shows that Europe is experiencing a transition from sporadic importation to repeated episodes of autochthonous transmission, particularly in areas where competent Aedes vectors are established and climatic conditions permit seasonal virus amplification [5, 6]. The emergence of local dengue transmission in Italy, Spain, and France since 2024 underscores the changing epidemiological landscape and the need to reassess preparedness in temperate regions [7–12].

This Review synthesises current evidence on dengue emergence in non-endemic settings, with a focus on Europe. We examine the global and regional epidemiology, climatic and ecological drivers, vector expansion, clinical and diagnostic challenges, vaccine and therapeutic developments, and the potential role of modelling approaches in informing preparedness and response. Rather than treating European dengue as a series of isolated events, we argue that recent outbreaks represent warning signals of a broader transition driven by climate change, vector establishment, human mobility, and gaps in surveillance and control [6–8, 13–19].

Methods

Search strategy

We conducted as a structured narrative synthesis of the literature on dengue epidemiology, emergence in non-endemic regions, and public health responses, with a particular focus on Europe. We searched PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science for articles published between Jan 1, 2010, and June 1, 2026, with emphasis on studies published from 2020 onwards to capture the most recent evidence.

Search terms included combinations of “dengue”, “Europe”, “non-endemic”, “autochthonous transmission”, “Aedes albopictus”, “climate change”, “vector expansion”, “outbreak”, “diagnosis”, “vaccines”, “Wolbachia”, and “modelling” or “agent-based model”. Reference lists of relevant articles were also screened to identify additional studies. We prioritised peer-reviewed original research, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and high-impact journal publications, as well as recent outbreak reports from 2024 to 2026 all the European outbreaks and modelling studies relevant to non-endemic settings. Policy and guidance documents from international public health agencies, including WHO and ECDC (European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control), were also included where relevant. Publications were limited to those in English that addressed global and regional dengue epidemiology, emergence and transmission in Europe or other non-endemic regions, drivers of transmission, diagnostic and clinical challenges, vector control strategies, vaccine developments, and modelling approaches. Studies with limited methodological detail, isolated case reports without broader epidemiological relevance, and older studies superseded by more recent evidence were excluded, unless considered foundational. Given the heterogeneity of study designs and outcomes, a quantitative meta-analysis was not undertaken. Instead, findings were synthesised narratively, with emphasis on consistency across studies, relevance to emerging transmission in non-endemic regions, and public health implications.

Modelling

In addition to the structured literature review, this work is informed by an original agent-based modelling framework developed to explore dengue transmission dynamics in non-endemic settings. The model integrates vector biology, temperature-dependent parameters, human population structure, and mobility patterns to simulate local transmission scenarios. Parameterisation was guided by published data and informed by recent European outbreak experience, including the 2024 Fano outbreak in Italy. The model was used to explore plausible transmission pathways and to identify key determinants of outbreak risk in temperate regions.

Findings/Results

Global epidemiology and recent surge

Dengue has undergone a marked global expansion over the past five decades, driven by urbanisation, population growth, global mobility, and environmental change. Contemporary estimates suggest that hundreds of millions of infections occur annually, with a substantial proportion remaining clinically inapparent [5, 6]. Despite improvements in surveillance, the true global burden remains difficult to quantify because of under-reporting, heterogeneous diagnostic capacity, and variability in case definitions across regions [1, 2].

The year 2024 marked a notable inflection point, with the World Health Organization reporting unprecedented increases in dengue incidence across multiple regions, including the Americas, South-East Asia, and parts of Africa [2]. Large-scale epidemics were reported in countries with historically high transmission as well as in areas previously considered low risk, reflecting both intensified transmission in endemic settings and geographic expansion into new regions [1, 2]. This surge has been attributed to a convergence of factors, including favourable climatic conditions, increasing vector density, population susceptibility, and pressures on public health systems following the COVID-19 pandemic [7, 17, 18].

Global patterns of dengue transmission are also becoming more complex, with increasing co-circulation of multiple serotypes and more frequent large-scale outbreaks [20, 21]. These dynamics raise concerns about heightened risks of severe disease associated with secondary infections and antibody-dependent enhancement, particularly in populations experiencing changing transmission intensity [22, 23]. In parallel, the expansion of competent vectors, especially Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus, has extended the ecological range of dengue transmission into new geographic areas, including temperate regions [16, 24].

The shifting global epidemiology of dengue is therefore characterised not only by increasing incidence but also by changing spatial distribution and transmission dynamics. In this context, the emergence of autochthonous transmission in Europe should be viewed as part of a broader global transition rather than an isolated phenomenon [6, 14]. Understanding these global trends is essential for interpreting regional outbreaks and anticipating future patterns of spread.

Emergence and epidemiology of dengue in Europe

Europe is increasingly experiencing a transition in dengue epidemiology, from sporadic imported cases to repeated episodes of autochthonous transmission. Historically, locally acquired dengue in Europe was rare and typically limited to isolated clusters. However, since the early 2000s, and particularly over the past decade, there has been a steady increase in the frequency, geographic spread, and size of outbreaks across southern and western Europe [6, 14]. Recent outbreaks provide clear evidence of this shift. In 2024, Italy experienced autochthonous transmission with well-characterised epidemiological, microbiological, and entomological features, demonstrating sustained local transmission under favourable environmental conditions [7, 10]. Spain reported a confirmed outbreak in 2024, further expanding the geographic footprint of dengue transmission within Europe [9]. In France, serological evidence suggests that dengue exposure may be more widespread than recognised through routine surveillance, indicating potential under-detection of transmission [11]. In Croatia dengue autochthonous circulation was discovered by serological investigation after the importation in Germany of a case locally acquired [15]. These findings are complemented by data from northern Italy and the Verona region in 2025, where a high burden of arboviral infections, including dengue, was documented during the summer season [8, 10].

Collectively, these observations indicate that dengue transmission in Europe is no longer an exceptional event but is becoming an increasingly recurrent phenomenon. The pattern is characterised by seasonal outbreaks, typically occurring during warmer months when vector populations are at their peak, and often linked to imported index cases followed by local amplification [6, 12]. The presence of Aedes albopictus across large parts of southern and central Europe, combined with suitable climatic conditions, has created an ecological niche capable of sustaining limited but significant transmission [16].

Importantly, the European experience also highlights the heterogeneity of transmission risk. While southern Europe remains the most affected region, sporadic transmission has been documented further north, suggesting that the geographic limits of dengue transmission are shifting [13, 14]. The increasing frequency of outbreaks, coupled with evidence of under-recognised transmission, raises concerns that especially Southern Europe may be entering an early phase of dengue establishment, particularly in regions with established vector populations and increasing climatic suitability [6, 11].

These developments have important implications for surveillance, clinical awareness, and public health preparedness. The emergence of dengue in Europe should not be interpreted as a series of isolated outbreaks but rather as part of a broader epidemiological transition driven by environmental, ecological, and societal factors [6, 13, 14].

Drivers of emergence in non-endemic regions

The emergence of dengue in Europe and other non-endemic settings reflects the convergence of climate change, vector expansion, human mobility, urbanisation, and uneven surveillance capacity. These drivers act together: imported viraemic cases are unlikely to generate sustained transmission unless competent vectors are present, environmental conditions are suitable, and public health detection is delayed [6, 13, 14, 16–19]. Vector ecology is equally important. Aedes albopictus has become widely established in Europe and is particularly relevant to transmission in temperate settings because of its ecological plasticity, capacity to exploit peri-domestic breeding sites, and the overwintering capacity of diapausing eggs [16, 36, 37]. Winter conditions are likely to interrupt efficient dengue virus transmission by reducing mosquito activity and slowing viral replication within the vector, but they do not necessarily preclude seasonal re-emergence. Vertical transmission of dengue virus in Aedes mosquitoes, including Aedes albopictus, has been documented and might contribute to viral persistence during unfavourable periods; however, its epidemiological importance in Europe remains uncertain and is unlikely, on current evidence, to sustain circulation across winter in the absence of renewed seasonal suitability and reintroduction through viraemic travellers [28, 38]. Although Aedes aegypti remains the more efficient dengue vector globally, its distribution in continental Europe is still more limited than that of Aedes albopictus [16, 39]. This distinction matters because current European risk is driven largely by Aedes albopictus, which may support smaller seasonal outbreaks rather than the intense urban transmission seen in many tropical settings [6, 13, 16].

Human mobility provides the bridge between endemic and non-endemic regions. International travel can introduce viraemic individuals into areas where competent vectors are established, creating opportunities for local amplification [25, 40]. This risk is greatest when importation coincides with high mosquito abundance, favourable temperatures, and delays in diagnosis or vector-control response. Recent European outbreaks therefore reflect not only changing vector ecology but also the interaction between travel, climate, and local preparedness [6, 7–12, 14, 15].

A related question is whether dengue can effectively overwinter in Europe and re-emerge without fresh importation. At present, the strongest evidence supports overwintering of the vector rather than reliable overwintering of the virus. Aedes albopictus can persist through winter in temperate Europe mainly via diapausing eggs, and in some sheltered microclimates limited adult female overwintering has also been observed, which may contribute to earlier seasonal population build-up. However, winter temperatures generally reduce adult activity and prolong the extrinsic incubation period to the point that efficient virus transmission becomes unlikely for much of the cold season. Although vertical transmission offers a biologically plausible mechanism for viral persistence, available evidence remains insufficient to conclude that it sustains dengue circulation across European winters at epidemiologically meaningful levels [41, 42]. The more plausible near-term scenario is therefore recurrent seasonal re-establishment, in which overwintering mosquito populations, progressively warmer shoulder seasons, and repeated viraemic introductions together shorten the gap between one transmission season and the next [13, 16, 18, 28, 38, 41, 42].

Urban and peri-urban environments further amplify risk by providing abundant breeding sites, dense human populations, and repeated human–mosquito contact. The persistence of small artificial water containers, incomplete source reduction, and variable community engagement can undermine control efforts even in high-income settings [26, 29]. These factors mean that dengue preparedness in Europe requires more than travel medicine advice: it requires integrated clinical, entomological, environmental, and community-based surveillance.

Pathogenesis and immunity

Dengue virus infection is characterised by a wide clinical spectrum, ranging from asymptomatic infection to severe disease with plasma leakage, haemorrhage, and organ impairment [20, 21]. The pathogenesis of severe dengue is complex and involves interactions between viral factors, host immune responses, and prior exposure to different dengue virus serotypes [20, 43].

A central concept in dengue immunopathogenesis is antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE), whereby non-neutralising or sub-neutralising antibodies from a previous infection facilitate increased viral entry into host cells during subsequent infection with a different serotype [22, 23]. This process is associated with higher viral loads, exaggerated immune activation, and increased risk of severe disease [23]. ADE helps explain why secondary infections are more likely to result in severe clinical outcomes and remains a critical consideration for both clinical management and vaccine development [22, 23].

In addition to ADE, dysregulated immune responses contribute to disease severity. Activation of T cells, cytokine release, endothelial dysfunction, and complement activation all play roles in the development of vascular permeability and plasma leakage, which are hallmarks of severe dengue [43]. The balance between protective and pathogenic immune responses is influenced by factors such as host genetics, age, comorbidities, and the sequence of infecting serotypes [20, 43].

In regions with historically low transmission, such as Europe, population immunity is limited, and most individuals are immunologically naïve. This has two important implications. First, initial outbreaks are likely to involve predominantly primary infections, which are often less severe at the individual level but may still place substantial pressure on health systems if case numbers are high [20]. Second, as transmission becomes more established, the accumulation of partial immunity within the population may increase the risk of severe disease through ADE during subsequent outbreaks [22, 23].

Understanding these immunological dynamics is therefore essential for interpreting the clinical patterns observed in emerging dengue settings and for informing vaccination strategies, which must carefully consider prior exposure and serostatus to maximise benefit and minimise risk [23, 44].

Clinical presentation and diagnostic challenges

Dengue infection ranges from mild or asymptomatic fever to life-threatening conditions involving plasma leakage, bleeding and organ impairment [20, 21] In its early phase, dengue is often indistinguishable from other viral infections, presenting with non-specific symptoms such as fever, headache, myalgia, and malaise [20]. This non-specific presentation poses particular challenges in non-endemic settings, where clinical suspicion is often low and differential diagnoses are broad.

Accurate and timely diagnosis is critical for patient management, surveillance, and outbreak control. Diagnostic strategies depend on the timing of presentation. During the acute phase (typically within the first 5 days of illness), detection of viral RNA by reverse transcription PCR or identification of NS1 antigen provides the highest diagnostic yield [45, 46]. In the later phase, serological testing for IgM and IgG antibodies becomes more informative, although cross-reactivity with other flaviviruses can complicate interpretation [45]. The World Health Organization’s 2025 interim guidance emphasises a time-based diagnostic approach, integrating molecular and serological methods to optimise detection across different stages of infection [46].

In Europe, diagnostic challenges are amplified by low clinical awareness, limited routine testing in some settings, and the potential for misclassification of cases as other febrile illnesses. Evidence from France suggests that serological signals of dengue exposure may exceed reported case numbers, indicating that transmission may be under-recognised [11]. Similarly, outbreaks in Italy and Spain have highlighted delays in diagnosis and case detection, which can facilitate local transmission before public health measures are implemented [7–10].

Diagnostic limitations also have implications for surveillance. Under-detection of mild or asymptomatic cases can obscure the true extent of transmission and delay recognition of outbreaks [1, 2]. In non-endemic regions, where surveillance systems are not always optimised for dengue, strengthening laboratory capacity and clinical awareness is essential for early detection and response.

Improving diagnostic pathways in Europe therefore requires a combination of clinician education, access to appropriate laboratory testing, and integration of clinical and public health surveillance systems. The adoption of standardised diagnostic algorithms, as recommended by WHO, can support more consistent case identification and reporting, thereby enhancing preparedness for emerging transmission [46].

Vaccines and therapeutics

The development of dengue vaccines has been shaped by the complex immunology of the disease, particularly the need to provide balanced protection against all four dengue virus serotypes while avoiding increased risk of severe disease associated with antibody-dependent enhancement [22, 23, 44]. Early vaccine efforts culminated in the development of the live attenuated tetravalent vaccine CYD-TDV (Dengvaxia), which demonstrated variable efficacy and an increased risk of severe disease in seronegative recipients [44, 47]. These findings led to the recommendation that CYD-TDV should be used only in individuals with confirmed prior dengue infection, limiting its applicability in many settings, particularly in populations with low seroprevalence such as Europe [44, 47].

More recently, the tetravalent dengue vaccine TAK-003 has shown promising efficacy and safety profiles in large phase 3 trials and long-term follow-up studies [48, 49]. Unlike CYD-TDV, TAK-003 does not require pre-vaccination serological screening in the same way, although its optimal use remains context-dependent. The 2024 WHO position paper recommends consideration of TAK-003 in children and adolescents in settings with high transmission intensity, where the public health benefit is greatest [50]. However, its role in non-endemic or low-transmission regions remains uncertain, and routine use in Europe is not currently recommended.

The potential role of dengue vaccines in non-endemic settings is likely to be targeted rather than population-wide. Vaccination may be considered for specific high-risk groups, including travellers to endemic areas, expatriate populations, and participants in mass gatherings where transmission risk may be elevated [51]. Mass gatherings, including sporting events and religious pilgrimages, create conditions that can facilitate the introduction and spread of dengue through increased population density, international mixing, and strain on public health infrastructure [51]. In this context, vaccines could form part of a broader risk-reduction strategy, although their deployment would need to be guided by careful risk assessment and cost-effectiveness considerations.

In contrast to vaccines, there are currently no licensed antiviral therapies for dengue, and clinical management remains largely supportive [52]. Treatment focuses on careful fluid management, monitoring for warning signs of severe disease, and timely intervention to prevent complications [20, 52]. Despite ongoing research into antiviral agents and host-targeted therapies, progress has been limited, and no candidate has yet demonstrated sufficient efficacy for routine clinical use. However, emerging data suggest that antiviral prophylaxis may become a feasible strategy. In a phase 2a controlled human infection model, daily oral mosnodenvir significantly reduced dengue viral RNA load and prevented infection in a proportion of participants receiving higher doses, without serious adverse events. These findings provide early proof-of-concept for antiviral prophylaxis in dengue, although further studies in field settings are required to determine clinical effectiveness and scalability [53].

Overall, advances in dengue vaccination represent an important step forward but are not a standalone solution. In non-endemic settings such as Europe, vaccines are likely to complement, rather than replace, surveillance, vector control, and public health preparedness [50, 51].

Vector ecology and control strategies

Effective control of dengue transmission depends critically on managing Aedes mosquito populations, particularly Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus [16, 34]. In Europe, Aedes albopictus is the predominant vector of concern due to its wide geographic distribution, ecological adaptability, and capacity to thrive in temperate climates [16, 37]. Its ability to exploit small artificial water containers in urban and peri-urban environments makes complete source reduction challenging, even in high-income settings.

Traditional vector control strategies focus on environmental management, including elimination of breeding sites, larviciding, and adult mosquito control through insecticides [26, 29]. However, evidence for the effectiveness of these approaches in reducing dengue transmission has been mixed, with challenges related to implementation, sustainability, and community engagement [26, 29]. In addition, increasing insecticide resistance in mosquito populations poses a growing threat to the long-term effectiveness of chemical control measures [29].

Recent years have seen the development of novel vector control approaches, particularly those based on the use of Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes. Wolbachia is a naturally occurring endosymbiotic bacterium that can reduce the ability of mosquitoes to transmit dengue virus. Field trials have demonstrated substantial reductions in dengue incidence following the release of Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes, providing proof-of-concept for this strategy as a scalable intervention [54, 61]. These approaches may be particularly valuable in settings where traditional vector control has had limited impact.

In Europe, the application of vector control strategies faces additional challenges. Seasonal transmission, heterogeneous vector distribution, and the episodic nature of outbreaks can complicate the timing and targeting of interventions [6, 13]. Moreover, public perception and regulatory considerations may influence the acceptability and implementation of novel interventions such as Wolbachia releases [55, 61].

Given these complexities, effective vector control in non-endemic settings requires an integrated approach that combines environmental management, targeted use of insecticides, surveillance of vector populations, and, where appropriate, innovative biological control strategies [55]. Strengthening entomological surveillance and linking it to public health decision-making will be essential for anticipating and responding to dengue outbreaks in Europe.

Modelling and decision-support for emerging dengue risk

Mathematical and computational modelling play an increasingly important role in understanding and anticipating dengue emergence in non-endemic settings. In Europe, where transmission is episodic and context-specific, modelling provides a framework for integrating climatic, entomological, and epidemiological data to inform risk assessment and public health decision-making [13, 30, 62].

Traditional compartmental models have been widely used to describe dengue transmission dynamics. These models divide the population into mutually exclusive compartments representing different disease states, and use systems of differential equations to describe the transitions of individuals between these compartments over time. Each individual belongs to only one compartment at any given moment, allowing the evolution of the epidemic to be studied in a structured and mathematically tractable way [63]. Traditionally, compartmental models are named according to the compartments they include [64–66].

These models provide a simplified but effective framework for understanding disease dynamics and predicting epidemic behaviour. Over the years, numerous extensions of the basic SIR framework have been proposed to better capture the complexity of real epidemics. These extensions may include additional compartments to represent specific stages of infection, hospitalization, immunity loss, vaccination, or demographic processes. Other modifications incorporate more detailed disease mechanisms or population interactions, improving the realism and applicability of the models to different epidemiological scenarios. However, generically, their assumptions of homogeneous mixing may be less appropriate in settings where outbreaks are spatially localised and driven by small numbers of cases [62].

A major contribution to modern modelling of infectious disease transmission, as well as to other domains involving interactions, comes from contemporary graph analysis. In this framework, infectious individuals are represented as nodes in a graph, while the edges connecting them represent disease transmission between individuals. The spread of the disease is then characterized through key graph-theoretical properties, such as the degree distribution, which describes the proportion of individuals having a given number of contacts [67, 68].

The complex nature of infection spread is deeply connected with external factors, such as geographical variables, the distribution of human populations, and the intervention of healthcare facilities. Together, these elements capture the spatial heterogeneity of disease transmission and are commonly modelled through advanced statistical approaches, including linear regression models and choropleth maps [69, 70].

In this context, Agent-based models (ABMs), offer greater flexibility for capturing the heterogeneity characteristic of non-endemic transmission [27, 71]. These approaches are particularly useful for exploring how local factors—such as vector density, human movement, and intervention strategies—interact to influence outbreak risk.

Recent modelling studies highlight that even low densities of competent vectors can be sufficient to sustain outbreaks under favourable conditions, particularly when introduction of the virus coincides with optimal temperature and delayed detection [55]. This finding is highly relevant for Europe, where vector populations may be lower than in endemic regions but are nonetheless capable of supporting transmission during peak seasons. Climate-informed models further suggest that the window of transmission is expanding in temperate regions, increasing the likelihood of local outbreaks [13, 19].

Modelling also has an important role in evaluating intervention strategies. Simulations can be used to assess the potential impact of vector control measures, vaccination strategies, and surveillance improvements under different scenarios [27, 62, 71]. In non-endemic settings, where empirical data may be limited, modelling can provide valuable insights to guide preparedness planning and resource allocation.

However, it is important to recognise the limitations of modelling approaches. Predictions are dependent on the quality and availability of input data, and uncertainty can be substantial, particularly in emerging settings where key parameters are not well defined [62]. As such, modelling should be viewed as a tool for decision-support rather than precise prediction.

Integrating modelling outputs with real-time surveillance, climatic data, and entomological monitoring offers a pathway towards more proactive and adaptive dengue control strategies in Europe [13, 30, 62].

Public health preparedness and response

The emergence of dengue in Europe highlights important gaps in public health preparedness for vector-borne diseases in non-endemic settings. Although most European countries have well-developed health systems, dengue has not traditionally been a priority pathogen, and preparedness measures have often been oriented towards travel-related cases rather than local transmission [5, 6]. The recent increase in autochthonous outbreaks demonstrates the need to adapt existing systems to a changing epidemiological reality.

Surveillance is a cornerstone of preparedness. Effective dengue surveillance requires integration of clinical reporting, laboratory confirmation, and entomological monitoring [1, 5]. In non-endemic settings, early detection is particularly important because timely identification of index cases can enable rapid vector control measures and limit local transmission. However, under-recognition of dengue, delays in diagnosis, and limited routine testing can hinder early detection [7–11]. Strengthening laboratory capacity and adopting standardised diagnostic algorithms, as recommended by WHO, are therefore critical components of preparedness [46].

Vector surveillance and control are equally important. Monitoring the distribution and density of Aedes mosquitoes can provide early warning of increased transmission risk and inform targeted interventions [16, 36, 39]. In Europe, where vector populations are heterogeneous and often seasonal, surveillance systems must be sufficiently sensitive to detect local increases in mosquito abundance and to guide timely control measures [13]. Integrated vector management approaches that combine environmental, chemical, and biological strategies are likely to be most effective [29, 55].

Public awareness and clinician education also play key roles in preparedness. In settings where dengue is not commonly encountered, raising awareness among healthcare providers can improve case recognition and reporting [20]. Public engagement is essential for effective vector control, particularly in relation to source reduction in domestic and peri-domestic environments [26, 29].

Preparedness planning must also consider the role of international travel and mass gatherings. The movement of people between endemic and non-endemic regions creates ongoing risk of virus introduction, particularly during periods of high transmission in endemic countries [25, 51]. Coordinated public health strategies, including travel advisories, risk communication, early detection of possibly viraemic imported cases and targeted interventions, are needed to mitigate these risks.

Overall, strengthening dengue preparedness in Europe requires a coordinated, multisectoral approach that integrates surveillance, diagnostics, vector control, clinical management, and public engagement. The recent emergence of dengue in the region provides an opportunity to develop and implement proactive strategies before more sustained transmission becomes established [6, 13].

Future directions and research priorities

The emergence of dengue in Europe and other non-endemic regions underscores the need for a forward-looking research and policy agenda that anticipates further geographic expansion and evolving transmission dynamics. Current evidence suggests that dengue risk in temperate regions will continue to increase as climatic suitability expands, vector populations become more established, and global connectivity intensifies [13, 17, 19, 24]. Addressing these challenges will require coordinated advances across multiple domains, including surveillance, diagnostics, vector control, vaccination, and modelling.

One priority area is the strengthening of integrated surveillance systems that combine clinical, laboratory, entomological, and environmental data. Improved data integration will enable earlier detection of outbreaks, more accurate assessment of transmission intensity, and more effective targeting of interventions [1, 5, 16]. The incorporation of real-time climatic and environmental data into surveillance platforms may further enhance predictive capacity, allowing for anticipatory public health responses in periods of increased risk [13, 19].

Advances in diagnostic technologies also offer opportunities to improve case detection and surveillance. The development of rapid, accurate, and field-deployable diagnostic tools could facilitate earlier identification of cases, particularly in settings where laboratory capacity is limited or where dengue is not routinely suspected [45, 46]. In parallel, improved understanding of serological responses and cross-reactivity among flaviviruses will be important for interpreting surveillance data and guiding vaccination strategies [45].

Vector control remains a critical but challenging area. Future research should focus on optimising integrated vector management strategies, evaluating the long-term effectiveness and scalability of novel interventions such as Wolbachia-based approaches, and addressing issues such as insecticide resistance and community engagement [26, 29, 54, 55, 61]. The adaptation of these strategies to temperate settings, where transmission is seasonal and heterogeneous, will require context-specific approaches.

Vaccination strategies will also need to evolve as the epidemiology of dengue changes. Further data on the long-term effectiveness, safety, and optimal use of newer vaccines such as TAK-003 are required, particularly in low-transmission settings and among populations with varying levels of prior exposure [48–50]. The potential role of targeted vaccination strategies—for example, in high-risk groups or in the context of outbreaks and mass gatherings—warrants further investigation [51].

Finally, modelling approaches will continue to play an important role in informing dengue control strategies. Future work should focus on integrating modelling with real-world data, improving parameter estimation in emerging settings, and developing tools that are accessible and useful for public health decision-makers [27, 30, 62, 71]. Emphasis should be placed on models that support scenario planning and evaluation of interventions rather than deterministic prediction.

In conclusion, dengue emergence in Europe represents an early signal of broader global changes in vector-borne disease dynamics. Proactive investment in research, surveillance, and public health infrastructure will be essential to mitigate the impact of dengue and other arboviral diseases in non-endemic regions [6, 13].
