Key factors and practical strategies to strengthen hospital disaster preparedness: a qualitative study in Iran
Authors: Ali Reza Yusefi, Reza Sadeghi, Jamshid Bahmaei, Omolbanin Atashbahar, Neda Dastyar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to explore the key factors influencing hospital disaster preparedness and identify practical strategies for strengthening preparedness within the Iranian healthcare system.DesignThis qualitative study used conventional content analysis. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews and analysed using MAXQDA 2022. Data collection and analysis continued concurrently until data saturation was achieved.SettingThe study was conducted in eight hospitals across four provinces of Iran between January 2023 and February 2024.Participants22 healthcare professionals with experience in hospital disaster preparedness were recruited using purposive sampling. Eligible participants had at least 3 years of experience in disaster preparedness or emergency management and represented managerial, clinical and disaster management roles.ResultsSeven themes and 20 subthemes influencing hospital disaster preparedness were identified, encompassing leadership and governance, organisational culture, human resources, infrastructure and resources, training and knowledge, communication and coordination and policy and planning. Six additional themes and 25 subthemes described strategies to improve preparedness, including strengthening leadership, empowering the workforce, enhancing infrastructure resilience, promoting scenario-based training, improving communication systems and expanding integrated information technologies.ConclusionsHospital disaster preparedness is influenced by interconnected organisational, managerial, structural and cultural factors. Implementation of context-specific, stakeholder-informed strategies may contribute to strengthening hospital disaster preparedness by improving organisational resilience, coordination and emergency response capacity.Keywords: QUALITATIVE RESEARCH; Emergency Service, Hospital; HEALTH SERVICES ADMINISTRATION & MANAGEMENT

Introduction

Disasters, whether natural, technological or biological, pose significant challenges to health systems worldwide.1 These events, which include earthquakes, floods, pandemics and industrial accidents, often strike without warning, placing immense strain on healthcare facilities, particularly hospitals, which are expected to respond rapidly and effectively to increased demand.2 In this context, hospital preparedness for disasters is a critical component of any national or regional disaster risk management strategy.3 According to the WHO, hospital preparedness refers to the ability of health facilities to anticipate, respond to and recover from the impacts of disasters while maintaining essential health services.4 Although related, the concepts of hospital disaster preparedness, readiness, functional preparedness, emergency preparedness and hospital resilience have distinct meanings. In this study, hospital disaster preparedness is used as the overarching concept, whereas readiness refers to the current level of preparedness, functional preparedness to the operational ability to implement disaster plans, emergency preparedness to preparedness for all types of emergencies and hospital resilience to the capacity to adapt and recover following disasters.4–6 These terms are used consistently throughout the manuscript according to these definitions.

Preparedness encompasses multiple dimensions, including emergency planning, staff training, resource allocation, interorganisational coordination and communication systems.7 A well-prepared hospital can significantly reduce mortality, morbidity and health system disruption during emergencies.8 Conversely, inadequate preparedness can lead to chaos, resource shortages and avoidable loss of life.9 In many countries, especially those prone to disasters, enhancing hospital preparedness is considered a top public health priority.10

Despite increasing global attention to disaster risk reduction frameworks such as the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction,11 hospitals in many regions, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, continue to face substantial challenges in achieving full preparedness. These challenges often include insufficient funding, lack of comprehensive disaster planning, inadequate staff training, poor infrastructure and weak coordination among stakeholders.12 Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic revealed critical weaknesses in hospital preparedness worldwide, even in high-resource settings, highlighting the urgent need for evidence-based, context-specific strategies.13

In Iran, a country frequently affected by earthquakes, floods and other emergencies, the need for hospital disaster preparedness is particularly pressing.14 Past disasters such as the Bam earthquake (2003) and recent flash floods have demonstrated the vulnerability of healthcare infrastructure and the gaps in emergency response systems. Although the Ministry of Health and Medical Education (MOHME) has introduced several guidelines and protocols for disaster preparedness, the extent to which hospitals are prepared and the key factors influencing this preparedness remain insufficiently explored.15

Previous studies on hospital disaster preparedness have primarily employed quantitative approaches, focusing on measuring preparedness or readiness levels using standardised assessment tools and scoring systems.2 5 10 14 15 While these studies provide valuable snapshots of preparedness status, they often fail to uncover the contextual, organisational and human factors that shape preparedness in real-world settings. Moreover, most existing research has been conducted in high-income countries, limiting the applicability of its findings to low-resource environments.2 5 Although several studies in Iran and other low- and middle-income countries have examined hospital disaster preparedness, they have largely focused on assessing preparedness levels or specific dimensions of functional readiness using structured assessment tools.10 14 15 Furthermore, the available qualitative studies have generally been limited to single hospitals, a limited number of provinces or specific professional groups, providing only a partial understanding of the complexities surrounding disaster preparedness.3 16 Consequently, there remains a clear gap in qualitative evidence exploring the perceptions, experiences and challenges faced by hospital managers, healthcare providers and emergency response coordinators in planning for and responding to disasters.

Understanding these perspectives is essential for designing practical and sustainable strategies tailored to specific settings. Qualitative research enables a deeper exploration of complex organisational processes by revealing not only what challenges exist but also why they persist and how they may be addressed.17 Accordingly, this study responds to the need for context-sensitive, experience-driven insights into hospital disaster preparedness within the Iranian healthcare system.

The present study extends the existing literature by adopting a multiprovince qualitative design that captures experiences from hospitals with diverse disaster profiles across Iran and incorporates the perspectives of multiple stakeholder groups, including hospital managers, physicians, nurses, disaster risk management officers and emergency coordinators. Furthermore, rather than focusing solely on existing barriers, this study simultaneously explores both the key factors influencing preparedness and the practical strategies proposed by stakeholders to strengthen hospital resilience. By integrating these findings into a comprehensive conceptual model, this study provides a context-specific framework that can support policy development, organisational planning and disaster preparedness initiatives in Iran and other resource-constrained settings.

The significance of this study lies in its potential to contribute to both theory and practice. From a theoretical perspective, it expands the existing body of disaster preparedness literature by identifying the organisational and contextual factors that influence hospital preparedness. From a practical perspective, it offers actionable recommendations for hospital administrators, policymakers and disaster management authorities seeking to strengthen emergency preparedness and resilience within healthcare facilities. Guided by the research question, ‘What are the main factors and effective strategies for strengthening hospital preparedness for disasters, as perceived by healthcare professionals and stakeholders involved in emergency management?’, this study employs a qualitative design using semistructured interviews with key informants, including hospital managers, emergency department heads, disaster response coordinators and healthcare professionals with disaster experience. By providing an in-depth exploration of the determinants of hospital preparedness and stakeholder-informed strategies for improvement, this study aims to address an important gap in the literature and inform future policy development and operational planning.

Methods

Study design and setting

This qualitative study was conducted in Iran from 15 January 2023 to 26 February 2024 with the aim of exploring key factors and strategies for enhancing hospital preparedness for disasters. A qualitative research design was chosen to gain deep insights into the experiences, perceptions and recommendations of hospital stakeholders. The study was guided by a content analysis approach, which is suitable for identifying patterns and themes within narrative data. The study setting included eight hospitals purposively selected from four provinces in Iran, Tehran, Kerman, Khuzestan and Mazandaran. These provinces were chosen due to their varying levels of exposure to natural or human-induced disasters and their geographical diversity (Central, Southern, Southwestern and Northern Iran). In each province, at least one public hospital was purposively selected; ultimately, all eight participating hospitals were public referral hospitals that met the study inclusion criteria, including previous disaster response experience and active involvement in emergency preparedness activities. The selected hospitals had either previously experienced major disaster events or were officially designated as emergency referral centres by the ministry of health. All participating hospitals were public secondary-level or tertiary-level referral hospitals with bed capacities ranging from approximately 180–600 beds. These hospitals served as the principal emergency care providers within their respective provinces and had previous experience responding to disasters such as earthquakes, floods, mass casualty incidents or the COVID-19 pandemic. Participants were recruited from all eight hospitals and represented a broad range of managerial, clinical and disaster management positions. The diversity of hospital characteristics, geographical locations and participant roles was intentionally sought to maximise variation and capture a broad range of hospital experiences and disaster preparedness levels. Such geographical and contextual diversity strengthened the transferability of the findings to similar healthcare settings.

Participants

Participants were selected using purposive sampling techniques to include individuals with first-hand experience and knowledge of hospital disaster preparedness. Key informants included hospital managers, emergency department heads, physicians, disaster risk management officers and senior nurses. A total of 22 individuals were interviewed. Participants were recruited through purposive sampling with the assistance of hospital administrators who identified individuals meeting the eligibility criteria. Potential participants were initially contacted either by telephone or in person, provided with information about the study and invited to participate voluntarily. Recruitment sought maximum variation with respect to professional role, years of experience, hospital type and geographical location to capture diverse perspectives. 26 eligible individuals were approached, of whom 22 agreed to participate, while 4 declined because of scheduling constraints or workload. The final sample included participants from all eight participating hospitals across the four selected provinces, representing a broad range of managerial, clinical and disaster preparedness responsibilities. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for participants required them to have a minimum of 3 years of experience in hospital settings related to emergency preparedness or disaster management, to be currently employed in hospitals or health institutions involved in emergency planning or response, and to be willing to participate and provide informed consent. Individuals who had no experience in disaster-related hospital preparedness, those unwilling to be audio-recorded or participants who withdrew before completing the interview were excluded from the study.

Pilot testing of the interview guide

First, the interview guide was developed using the literature review. The interview guide was developed by the research team specifically for this study (see online supplemental file 1). Prior to data collection, the semistructured interview guide was pilot-tested with three healthcare professionals who were not included in the main sample. This pilot phase ensured clarity, relevance and logical sequencing of the questions. Based on feedback from this phase, minor revisions were made to the interview guide, such as rephrasing ambiguous items and adjusting the flow of questions to better suit participants’ understanding and encourage in-depth responses.

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face semistructured interviews. All interviews were conducted by two members of the research team (one female and one male researcher). Both interviewers had formal training and substantial experience in qualitative research, including interviewing and data analysis. They also had extensive experience in hospital-based education and clinical field training, which supported effective communication with participants and familiarity with the hospital context. Prior to data collection, participants were informed about the researchers’ professional roles, the objectives of the study and the voluntary nature of participation. No prior personal or supervisory relationship existed between the interviewers and participants before recruitment. Throughout the interviews, the researchers adopted a reflexive approach and considered how their professional backgrounds in hospital management and healthcare education, as well as their prior knowledge of disaster preparedness, might influence data collection. They regularly reflected on their questioning, interactions with participants and interpretation of responses and took steps to minimise potential influence on the findings. Before the interviews, necessary information regarding the study and its objectives was provided to the participants and written informed consent was obtained from them. Moreover, they were assured that their information would remain confidential and the study data would be analysed anonymously. Each interview lasted between 40 and 75 min and was conducted in a private setting to ensure confidentiality and participant comfort. No individuals other than the participant and the interviewers were present during the interviews. With participants’ consent, interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim in Persian. The interview guide included open-ended questions such as asking participants to describe their hospital’s level of preparedness for disasters, identify factors that have facilitated or hindered disaster preparedness and suggest strategies they believe are most effective for improving hospital preparedness. Field notes were also taken during and immediately after each interview to capture contextual information and non-verbal cues. No repeat interviews were required because sufficient depth of information was obtained during the initial interviews. Interview transcripts were not returned to participants for correction; however, preliminary interpretations and emerging themes were discussed with a subset of participants during the member-checking process to verify the credibility and accuracy of the findings.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using conventional content analysis following the systematic approach proposed by Graneheim and Lundman.18 This approach is well suited to capturing both manifest and latent meanings within qualitative data and allows for a structured yet interpretive analysis of participants’ narratives. Initially, all interviews were transcribed verbatim in Persian immediately after each interview to ensure data accuracy and familiarity with the content. Representative quotations included in the manuscript were translated from Persian into English by two bilingual members of the research team. The translations were reviewed collaboratively to preserve the original meaning while improving readability for an international audience. Minor grammatical and stylistic edits were made, where necessary, solely to improve readability without altering the participants’ intended meanings.

The researchers repeatedly read the transcripts several times to achieve immersion in the data and obtain a holistic understanding of participants’ experiences regarding hospital disaster preparedness. Meaning units, including words, sentences or paragraphs related to the study objectives, were identified and extracted from the transcripts. These meaning units were then condensed while preserving their core essence and assigned initial codes. Coding was performed inductively, with codes derived directly from the raw data without imposing pre-existing categories or theoretical frameworks. Categories and themes emerged inductively through iterative comparison and abstraction of the coded data.

Two members of the research team independently coded the first five interview transcripts to establish an initial coding framework. The preliminary codes were compared and discussed in a series of analytical meetings, during which discrepancies in coding and interpretation were resolved through discussion and consensus. Following agreement on the initial coding framework, the remaining transcripts were coded iteratively. New codes and categories were added or refined as novel concepts emerged, allowing the framework to evolve and remain grounded in participants’ experiences and perspectives. Constant comparison across codes and interviews was used to identify similarities, differences and patterns. Conceptually similar codes were grouped into subcategories, which were subsequently abstracted into broader categories reflecting more general patterns of meaning. Through an iterative process of abstraction and interpretation, overarching themes were developed to capture the latent content of the data. Throughout this process, coding decisions, category refinement and theme development were continuously discussed among the research team to ensure coding consistency, conceptual coherence and consensus, with repeated reference to the original interview transcripts. This collaborative analytical approach helped ensure consistency and reduced individual bias.

To enhance the rigour and organisation of data management, qualitative data were managed using MAXQDA software (V.2022). The software facilitated systematic coding, retrieval and categorisation of large volumes of textual data, thereby improving the transparency and traceability of analytical decisions. Data analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection, allowing emerging insights to inform subsequent interviews. Thematic saturation was determined through the concurrent analysis of interview data, whereby newly identified codes were continuously compared with existing categories. Data collection ceased when the final interviews yielded no new codes, categories or conceptually meaningful insights. To enhance transparency and maintain a clear analytic trail, the analysis progressed through successive stages of data familiarisation, identification of meaning units, condensation, initial coding, development of subcategories, abstraction into broader categories and generation of overarching themes. Throughout this process, the distinction between descriptive categories and higher-level interpretive themes was continually reviewed and refined by the research team. Representative participant quotations were retained to illustrate each subtheme and theme and ensure that the final interpretations remained closely grounded in the original data.

Trustworthiness

To ensure the rigour and trustworthiness of the findings, the study followed the four criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba19: credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability. Multiple strategies were applied for each criterion to strengthen the overall quality of the research.

Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with participants and the research field over 14 months (January 2023 to February 2024), which enabled the researchers to build trust, deepen their understanding of hospital disaster preparedness and compare emerging findings across hospitals and provinces. Member checking was undertaken with eight participants representing different professional groups and provinces. These participants reviewed summaries of the preliminary findings and thematic interpretations rather than the complete interview transcripts. Overall, participants agreed that the findings accurately reflected their experiences and confirmed the credibility of the interpretations. Several participants suggested minor clarifications to improve the wording and presentation of specific themes; however, no new themes or categories emerged, and the overall thematic framework remained unchanged. Additionally, peer debriefing was conducted during three structured meetings with two qualitative research experts who were not involved in data collection or analysis. During these sessions, the coding framework, category development and thematic interpretations were critically reviewed. Their feedback primarily focused on improving conceptual clarity, refining category labels and ensuring consistency between participant quotations and the final themes.

Dependability was ensured through the development of a detailed audit trail that documented all stages of the research process, including sampling decisions, interview procedures, coding steps, category development and modifications made during analysis. Furthermore, the research process was systematically documented so that another researcher could theoretically follow the same procedures and reach comparable findings. Regular team discussions also contributed to maintaining consistency in coding and interpretation over time.

Confirmability was strengthened through ongoing reflexive discussions among the research team. Throughout data collection and analysis, researchers critically examined how their professional backgrounds, previous experience in hospital management and familiarity with disaster preparedness might influence data interpretation. Reflexive notes documenting these discussions were maintained throughout the study and were regularly revisited during analytical meetings to minimise potential researcher bias. These discussions focused on identifying potential assumptions related to the researchers’ professional backgrounds and ensuring that interpretations remained grounded in participants’ accounts rather than prior expectations. In addition, an external qualitative researcher with expertise in qualitative methodology independently reviewed the coding framework, category structure and thematic interpretations. The external review resulted in minor refinements to the wording of several categories and strengthened the conceptual distinction between related themes, while the overall thematic structure remained unchanged.

To enhance transferability, detailed information about the study context, participating hospitals and participant characteristics was provided, enabling readers to assess the applicability of the findings to other healthcare settings. Moreover, the use of purposive sampling across multiple provinces with different disaster exposure profiles strengthened the variability of perspectives and increased the potential relevance of findings to similar contexts in other regions or countries.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Reporting guideline

This study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist.

The findings were organised into two overarching domains, comprising factors influencing hospital disaster preparedness and strategies for strengthening preparedness.

Seven main themes and 20 subthemes emerged from the data analysis regarding the factors influencing hospital disaster preparedness. These findings describe the existing organisational, managerial, structural and contextual conditions that either facilitate or hinder hospitals’ preparedness for disasters. Across participant groups, leadership and governance were consistently perceived as the foundation of hospital disaster preparedness. Hospital managers and disaster coordinators primarily emphasised organisational policies, leadership commitment and decision-making authority. In contrast, frontline healthcare professionals, particularly nurses and physicians, more frequently highlighted operational challenges such as staff shortages, limited disaster training, communication barriers and inadequate access to emergency resources. Although participants differed in the aspects they emphasised according to their professional roles and responsibilities, there was broad agreement that hospital preparedness depends on the interaction of organisational, human, infrastructural and communication-related factors rather than any single component. Only a few participants placed greater emphasis on technological solutions, suggesting that information systems alone cannot compensate for weaknesses in leadership, planning and workforce preparedness. Participants also identified a wide range of practical strategies for strengthening hospital disaster preparedness. These recommendations were organised into six main themes and 25 subthemes, representing interventions that participants considered effective for addressing the challenges identified in the first phase of the analysis. The proposed strategies reflected participants’ attempts to address the barriers identified in the first phase of the analysis. While hospital managers generally emphasised governance reforms, resource allocation and policy development, frontline healthcare professionals focused more on practical interventions such as regular disaster drills, continuous education, improved communication and clearer role allocation. Despite these differences in emphasis, participants consistently viewed disaster preparedness as requiring coordinated improvements across leadership, workforce capacity, infrastructure, communication systems and information technology rather than isolated interventions. Strategies to enhance hospital preparedness for disasters

Although several thematic domains—such as leadership, human resources, infrastructure, communication and training—appear in both tables 2 and 3, they represent conceptually different dimensions of the findings. The ‘factors’ describe participants’ experiences and perceptions of the current conditions influencing hospital disaster preparedness, whereas the ‘strategies’ reflect their recommendations for overcoming these challenges and improving preparedness. This distinction emerged during the inductive content analysis and was maintained throughout the development of the final thematic framework.

Discussion

This study identified seven main themes and 20 subthemes related to the factors influencing hospital preparedness for disasters, along with six main themes and 25 subthemes for strategies to enhance preparedness. These findings offer a comprehensive understanding of the systemic, organisational and individual-level challenges, as well as potential solutions, within the healthcare system. Importantly, these findings should be interpreted within the context of the Iranian healthcare system, where disaster preparedness is coordinated through the MOHME, and hospitals often operate under centralised governance structures with limited managerial autonomy, variable resource allocation and increasing demands resulting from the country’s diverse natural and human-made disaster risks.

The theme of leadership and governance emerged as foundational to preparedness. Participants highlighted inconsistent leadership commitment, the absence of disaster-specific policies and limited decision-making autonomy. These findings support earlier evidence suggesting that hospitals with strong leadership structures are more likely to integrate disaster risk management into routine operations and mobilise resources during crises.20 Decentralised decision-making was proposed as a strategy to empower frontline units, improving the speed and relevance of responses. This is in line with studies recommending local autonomy as a critical enabler of operational flexibility in disasters.21 Within the Iranian hospital system, however, managerial decision-making is often influenced by centralised administrative regulations and hierarchical governance, which may reduce hospitals’ flexibility to rapidly adapt disaster preparedness plans to local conditions. Strengthening hospital-level autonomy while maintaining national coordination may therefore improve emergency responsiveness.

Organisational culture was also identified as a key determinant of hospital readiness. A low safety culture and resistance to drills hindered disaster preparedness. These findings align with research emphasising that a positive safety culture fosters adherence to emergency protocols and continuous improvement in preparedness.22 To shift the organisational mindset, participants proposed strategies such as policy clarity and leadership modelling of preparedness behaviours. These recommendations reflect the view that cultural change must be both top-down and participatory, involving all staff levels to normalise preparedness practices.

In the domain of human resources, the study identified shortages of trained personnel, unequal distribution of skilled staff and low motivation. This resonates with previous findings that human resource capacity remains a major barrier to disaster preparedness in low- and middle-income countries.23 Participants recommended workload management, staff motivation programmes and role clarification as solutions. These strategies suggest a need to integrate disaster readiness into broader workforce planning and human resource development frameworks. Incentivising training and clarifying roles can enhance both participation and accountability during emergencies. These challenges are particularly relevant in Iran, where workforce shortages, heavy clinical workloads and disparities in staffing between hospitals may limit opportunities for regular disaster preparedness training and emergency capacity building.

Challenges in infrastructure and resources were prominent. Outdated facilities, inaccessible emergency equipment and inadequate stockpiling were reported, mirroring global studies showing that infrastructure deficiencies compromise timely responses.24 Stakeholders proposed strengthening facility resilience, establishing backup systems and improving supply chain logistics. These strategies point to the importance of risk-informed capital investment and strategic stockpiling. Furthermore, ensuring that critical equipment is accessible and maintained during non-disaster periods is crucial for operational continuity when emergencies strike. The importance of these recommendations is amplified in Iran, where financial constraints, economic sanctions and interruptions in procurement processes may affect the availability of medical equipment, spare parts and emergency supplies, thereby increasing hospitals’ vulnerability during large-scale disasters.

Training and knowledge emerged as a recurrent concern. Irregular training sessions, limited knowledge of disaster protocols and lack of scenario-based drills reflected gaps in organisational learning. This corroborates studies indicating that periodic, interactive training enhances preparedness more effectively than passive knowledge dissemination.25 Recommended strategies such as ongoing education, knowledge retention planning and postdrill evaluation demonstrate the value of embedding continuous learning cycles within hospital operations. These approaches may transform training from a one-time activity into a sustained organisational process. Given Iran’s frequent exposure to earthquakes, floods, road traffic mass-casualty incidents and other emergencies, scenario-based training should be tailored to regional hazard profiles rather than relying solely on generic disaster exercises.

Findings related to communication and coordination revealed systemic weaknesses in both internal and interorganisational communication. Poor coordination and unclear roles during emergencies were widely reported. This is consistent with literature highlighting that communication failures are often cited as major challenges during disaster responses.16 To address this, participants recommended implementing real-time communication systems, clarifying chains of command and improving interdepartmental collaboration. These strategies emphasise the need for integrated communication protocols and regular simulation exercises to test and refine coordination mechanisms. Improving coordination is especially important in Iran because disaster response commonly requires collaboration among hospitals, prehospital emergency medical services, provincial health authorities and national emergency organisations, making effective inter-organisational communication essential for timely response.

The theme of policy and planning revealed critical planning gaps, including the lack of contingency plans, absence of scenario-based preparedness and insufficient monitoring and evaluation mechanisms. These observations align with evidence from other healthcare systems where planning remains largely theoretical and disconnected from operational realities.26 Suggested strategies included embedding scenario-based planning and establishing feedback loops for monitoring. Such measures would allow preparedness plans to evolve dynamically in response to real-world events and lessons learnt. Although national disaster management policies and hospital accreditation standards exist in Iran, their implementation and continuous evaluation may vary across hospitals because of differences in organisational capacity, local leadership and available resources.

In the strategic domain of information systems and technology, participants emphasised the importance of reliable digital infrastructure. They noted that poor system reliability and lack of access to emergency protocols via digital tools limited their ability to respond efficiently. These concerns are echoed in studies advocating for health information technology as a cornerstone of disaster preparedness.27 Recommended strategies such as data integration, rapid reporting tools and targeted technology training underline the need to align technological investments with emergency preparedness priorities. Ensuring usability and accessibility of such systems, even under pressure, may drastically enhance coordination and response efficiency.

Overall, the strategies proposed by participants may contribute to strengthening hospital disaster preparedness by addressing the barriers identified in this study. Rather than relying on theoretical frameworks, these suggestions emerge from practice-based insights grounded in participants’ lived experiences. For example, the emphasis on postdrill evaluations indicates an awareness that simulation exercises must be followed by structured feedback to be effective. Similarly, calls for training incentives reveal that motivation is as crucial as opportunity in enhancing staff engagement with disaster readiness initiatives.

In summary, the findings of this study reveal that hospital disaster preparedness is shaped by interrelated factors spanning leadership, workforce, infrastructure, policy and digital systems. While these themes resonate with international evidence, they also reflect the organisational realities of the Iranian healthcare system, including centralised governance, regional differences in disaster risks, workforce and resource constraints, and the need to strengthen coordination across multiple components of the national health system. By implementing the identified strategies—particularly those grounded in local institutional realities—healthcare leaders may take meaningful steps towards building more resilient, responsive and adaptive hospital systems. In this context, resilience should be interpreted as the broader outcome of sustained improvements in hospital disaster preparedness rather than as a synonym for preparedness itself.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, although purposive sampling enabled the recruitment of participants with extensive experience in hospital disaster preparedness, the possibility of selection bias cannot be completely excluded. Second, as the findings were derived from semistructured interviews, participants’ responses may have been influenced by self-report and social desirability bias, particularly when discussing organisational performance and disaster management practices. Third, the study explored the perspectives of healthcare managers and professionals but did not include patients, community representatives or other external stakeholders whose experiences could provide additional insights into disaster preparedness. Fourth, interviews with participants occupying senior managerial positions may have been influenced by organisational hierarchies and power dynamics, potentially affecting the openness of some responses despite assurances of confidentiality. Fifth, although the study included hospitals from four provinces of Iran, the findings may not be fully transferable to other provinces or healthcare systems with different organisational structures, resources and hazard profiles. Sixth, the findings were based solely on interview data and were not complemented by direct observations, disaster drills or documentary analyses of preparedness plans and policies. Future studies using methodological triangulation could provide a more comprehensive understanding of hospital preparedness. In addition, although interviews were conducted in Persian and translated into English for publication, subtle linguistic or contextual nuances may have been lost during the translation process despite careful review by the research team. Finally, the strategies identified in this study represent participants’ perceptions and expert recommendations rather than interventions that were implemented and evaluated. Future research should assess the effectiveness, feasibility and outcomes of these proposed strategies through implementation and longitudinal evaluation studies.

Policy implications and recommendations

Based on the perceptions and recommendations of the participants, policymakers may consider prioritising leadership development and accountability mechanisms within hospital governance structures to support disaster preparedness. National health authorities may also consider allocating targeted funding for infrastructure upgrades and establishing standards for minimum stockpiling and logistics. Participants further suggested that training programmes should be institutionalised, scenario-based and recurrent, with appropriate incentives for staff participation. In addition, communication systems may be strengthened through reliable real-time technologies and clearer coordination protocols, while information systems could be enhanced by investing in integrated digital platforms and improving access to up-to-date emergency protocols. Participants also emphasised the importance of embedding monitoring and evaluation mechanisms within disaster preparedness planning to facilitate continuous organisational learning.

Conclusion

This study highlights that hospital disaster preparedness in Iran is influenced by multiple systemic and operational challenges, including leadership gaps, inadequate infrastructure, low staff motivation and fragmented communication. The findings also identified a broad range of stakeholder-proposed strategies that are perceived as potentially useful for strengthening disaster preparedness, including strengthening leadership, decentralising authority, enhancing staff training and expanding information systems. Because these strategies were not implemented or evaluated within this study, no conclusions can be drawn regarding their effectiveness. Future implementation and evaluation studies are needed to determine whether these proposed strategies improve hospital disaster preparedness and resilience.

Suggestions for future research

Future studies could apply quantitative or mixed-methods approaches to assess the relative impact of identified factors and strategies across different hospital settings. Comparative studies between public and private institutions or across regions could further illuminate variability in disaster preparedness. Additionally, research focused on the implementation and evaluation of specific strategies, such as scenario-based training or digital information systems, would provide valuable evidence regarding the feasibility, acceptability and effectiveness of these proposed strategies in strengthening hospital disaster preparedness and resilience.
