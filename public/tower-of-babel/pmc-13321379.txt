Probable African tick bite fever identified during preoperative evaluation in Central Utah
Authors: Nicole Skalka, Colleen Moisa, Preston M. Gilbert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
African tick bite fever (ATBF) is a spotted fever group rickettsiosis caused by Rickettsia africae and is often identified among travelers returning from sub-Saharan Africa. Clinical presentation may be mild or nonspecific, often delaying diagnosis despite characteristic findings such as inoculation eschars. A 52-year-old male presented to the preoperative holding area prior to a scheduled left-sided inguinal hernia repair after recently returning from a game-hunting trip to Africa. During preoperative intake, he was noted to have a fever of 106 °F despite being otherwise asymptomatic. Physical examination revealed two black eschars on the patient’s back and right shin. Given the presence of high-grade fever, the scheduled procedure was canceled, and the patient was discharged for outpatient evaluation. He was subsequently treated with a 10-day course of doxycycline for suspected African tick bite fever. At two-week follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, his fever had resolved, and repeat preoperative evaluation was unremarkable. Surgical repair of the inguinal hernia was performed without complications. Although laboratory confirmation was not obtained, the clinical presentation, including characteristic eschars, high-grade fever, and recent travel to sub-Saharan Africa, was highly suggestive of ATBF. This case underscores the importance of maintaining clinical suspicion for ATBF in returning travelers and highlights the diagnostic value of inoculation eschars, even in the absence of systemic symptoms. Early recognition of suspected ATBF and empiric doxycycline therapy may result in rapid clinical improvement and help prevent unnecessary delays in care.

Introduction

African tick bite fever (ATBF) is a spotted fever group rickettsiosis caused by Rickettsia africae and transmitted through bites of Amblyomma ticks. It represents the most frequently identified rickettsial infection among travelers returning from sub-Saharan Africa [1]. According to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, infections are commonly linked to outdoor activities such as game hunting and safari excursions, with clusters of cases often associated with exposure to ticks parasitizing wild ungulates [1], [5].

Clinically, ATBF typically presents with one or more inoculation eschars at the site of tick attachment—multiple lesions occurring in up to half of reported cases [1], [4], [5]. Fever is a hallmark feature and may be accompanied by headache, myalgia, and localized lymphadenopathy, though the disease can also manifest with mild or even subclinical symptoms [1], [2], [3]. In the present case, the patient exhibited a markedly high fever (106 °F) without additional systemic complaints, and two dark eschars were noted at tick bite sites, consistent with classical clinical descriptions [1], [4], [5].

Diagnosis can be challenging due to the nonspecific nature of early symptoms and the often-delayed serologic response, making clinical suspicion and a detailed exposure history essential for identification [2], [3], [6]. Doxycycline remains the treatment of choice and typically results in rapid clinical improvement [2], [7], [8]. The patient’s minimal symptomatology and lack of awareness of infection further illustrate the broad clinical spectrum of ATBF described in epidemiologic reports and case series.

Case presentation

A 52-year-old male presented to the preoperative holding area prior to a scheduled left-sided inguinal hernia repair. He had recently returned from a game-hunting trip to Africa, during which his prior hernia repair failed while lifting game. He contacted a physician in the United States, who guided him through manual reduction of the hernia and advised surgical evaluation upon his return.

During preoperative intake, routine vital signs revealed a fever of 106 °F, though the patient was otherwise asymptomatic. Physical examination was notable for black eschars on the patient’s back and right shin (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Discussion

This patient’s largely asymptomatic presentation underscores the broad clinical spectrum of ATBF. While most returning travelers present with fever, headache, or myalgia, a notable subset exhibit only subtle findings, and some remain unaware of their illness until identified through objective findings such as eschar or laboratory abnormalities [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [8]. Notably, published reports indicate that the severity of fever does not always correlate with the degree of subjective symptoms, further complicating clinical recognition [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [8].

The documented temperature of 106 °F in an otherwise asymptomatic patient warrants consideration. The initial temperature was obtained as part of standard preoperative vital sign assessment, and given the unexpectedly high reading, the measurement was repeated and confirmed. While measurement artifact and non-infectious causes of hyperthermia were considered, the presence of characteristic inoculation eschars and a recent travel history to sub-Saharan Africa raised clinical suspicion for an infectious etiology, specifically ATBF. The dissociation between the degree of fever and the lack of subjective symptoms, though unusual, has been described in the ATBF literature [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [8].

Although the patient reported minimal symptoms despite documented fever, probable ATBF was considered the most likely diagnosis given his recent travel to Africa for game hunting. While the patient did not recall a known tick bite, black eschars were identified in the preoperative holding area prior to his scheduled hernia repair (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). He was treated with a 10-day course of doxycycline without additional laboratory workup, consistent with management of suspected rickettsial infection [2], [7], [8].

It is important to acknowledge that the diagnosis in this case was based entirely on clinical grounds, including travel history, characteristic eschars, and high-grade fever, without serologic or molecular confirmation. However, the clinical presentation was highly consistent with published descriptions of ATBF, and the rapid resolution of fever following doxycycline therapy further supports the suspected clinical diagnosis [2], [3], [5], [6]. Serologic testing for rickettsial infections is often negative during the acute phase and may require convalescent titers for confirmation, which were not obtained in this case [2], [3], [6]. This case highlights the importance of recognizing characteristic cutaneous findings and obtaining a thorough travel history when evaluating patients with otherwise minimal systemic symptoms.
