Obesity and age increase hypoxia risk during esophagogastroduodenoscopy under monitored anesthesia care: risk stratification and evaluation of a procedural oxygen mask
Authors: Sam Bonge, Maaz Majid, James M Mirocha, Theodore Stein, Deena Midani, Derek Cheng, David Padua, Joshua Levy, Megumi Nakamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeep sedation under monitored anesthesia care (MAC) during esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) has a significant risk of hypoxia in obese patients. Recently high flow nasal cannula studies were conducted with good results for obese patients, however, data on Procedural Oxygen Mask (POM) are extremely scarce.MethodsThe objectives of this study were to determine the incidence of hypoxia and impact of obesity during EGD, to describe our experience with POM, and to evaluate the risk factors and derive a prediction model using the machine-learning (ML) models. We reviewed the records of consecutive patients with BMI below 50 kg/m² who underwent EGD alone or both EGD and colonoscopy at our outpatient endoscopy center under MAC from 7/31/2023 to 1/31/2024. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to assess factors associated with hypoxia. In addition, we used our previously reported case series (n = 559) (Supplementary Table 1) and current data (n = 1294 without POM) to analyze the risk factors for hypoxia using machine-learning (ML) techniques.ResultsThe median age of the cohort was 58 years (range: 19–90). The median Body Mass Index (BMI) was 25 kg/m² (range: 15–45 kg/m²) with 302 obese (BMI > = 30 kg/m²). Intra-procedural hypoxia developed in 205 cases (15.4%). The incidence of hypoxia was significantly greater among those with obesity (84 of 302, 27.8%) compared to those without obesity (121 of 1021, 11.8%, p < 0.0001) with a difference of 16.0% (95% confidence interval [CI] 10.5–21.4%). No hypoxia was observed in the 29 POM-managed patients of whom 20 were obese. In multivariable analysis, obesity and age were associated with hypoxia (odds ratio [OR] 2.78, 95% CI 2.01–3.85, p < 0.0001, and OR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03, p = 0.001, respectively). Seven ML-based models were constructed which demonstrated modest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 60–65% in predicting hypoxia.ConclusionOur study found that obesity and age are risk factors for hypoxia during EGD while the performance of ML-based models was modest. The current exploratory data of POM warrant further evaluation in the high-risk population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-04122-1.Keywords: Obesity, Monitored Anesthesia Care (MAC), Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD), Procedural Oxygen Mask (POM), Machine Learning (ML)

Introduction

The data collected from the Clinical Outcomes Research Initiative Database showed a cardiopulmonary event rate of 1 in 170 with a mortality rate of 1 in 10,000 among 140,000 esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) [1]. Hypoxia is the most common complication for EGD since effective delivery of supplemental oxygen is limited, especially in obese patients [2–4]. Nasal cannulas are commonly used without disturbing EGD procedures which provide a maximum fraction inspired O₂ (FiO₂) estimate of 0.35 to 0.45 (35–45%) at oxygen flow rates of 6 L/min.

Recently several devices including high-flow nasal cannulas and procedural oxygen masks (POM) have been developed. While high-flow nasal cannulas reliably deliver high FiO2, it requires specialized equipment with humidification and is associated with high cost.

The POM is a clear plastic mask, like the traditional clear non-breathing oxygen mask, with or without an oxygen reservoir bag and self-sealing oral and nasal endoscopy ports. The POM without an oxygen reservoir bag could deliver an about FiO₂ of 75% with oxygen flow rates of 8 to 10 L/min. The high FiO₂ provided by the POM allow effective preoxygenation and oxygen maintenance throughout the procedure compared to ordinary nasal cannulas [5, 6]. We previously reported the result of a pilot study of POM, which demonstrated feasibility and promising efficacy [7]. However, it is not practical or necessary to apply POM to every patient undergoing EGD endoscopy due to medical cost constraints. Rather a risk-stratified approach tailored to individual clinical risk factors would be more desirable, aligning with the principles of personalized medicine. Our previous study also evaluated the risk factors for intra-procedural hypoxia in a multivariable logistic regression model and identified obesity and male sex as risk factors.

Machine-learning (ML) models offer an opportunity for large diverse datasets to be incorporated into the development of robust predictive analytics, often without many of the restrictions associated with traditional statistical methods [8, 9]. In the field of anesthesiology, various ML-based models have been reported to predict postoperative events such as mortality [10], hypotension [11], and post-induction hypotension [12] showing prediction performance similar to, or better than, traditional statistical methods. These approaches focus on discerning concealed correlations within clinical data, encompassing both the structured and unstructured facets of electronic health records (EHRs).

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to determine the incidence of hypoxia and assess the impact of obesity during EGD in a larger cohort of patients (n = 1323), to describe our experience with POM (n = 29), and to evaluate the pre-operative risk factors for intra-procedural hypoxia by developing prediction models using machine learning methods.

Methods

Study subjects

We conducted a medical record review of consecutive patients with BMI below 50 kg/m² who underwent EGD alone or both EGD and colonoscopy from 7/31/2023 (the date POM became available for use in our medical center) to 1/31/2024 at a large urban academic medical center’s outpatient endoscopy center under monitored anesthesia care (MAC). Cases conducted under conscious sedation by endoscopists were excluded. Sedation and monitoring of the patients were provided by experienced anesthesiologists, primarily using propofol alone. Ethical approval for this study (IRB#2381) was provided by the Institutional Review Board of Cedars Sinai Medical Center in Los Angeles, California, USA (Chairperson: Michael Lewis, MD) on August 5th, 2024. A waiver of informed consent was granted owing to the retrospective nature of the study. The study adhered strictly to the ethical tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection and standard EGD procedures

Demographics and clinical characteristics including age, sex, BMI, and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class, smoking history, history of Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA), asthma, Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) or history of an infectious disease caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus (COVID) were documented before EGD. Electrocardiography, pulse oximetry (SpO2), and respiratory rate were continuously monitored throughout all procedures. Any hypoxic event was recorded whenever it occurred. Noninvasive blood pressure was measured every 3 min. End-tidal carbon dioxide was continuously monitored. Patients were placed on their left side during EGD, or EGD and colonoscopy. Spontaneous breathing was maintained, and all patients received supplemental oxygen (2–10 L/min) via a nasal cannula or a cut mask (n = 1294) and POM (n = 29). None of the cases involved elective use of endotracheal intubation or emergency endotracheal intubation. Oxygen saturation was continuously monitored and recorded in the electronic anesthesia chart at one-minute intervals as well as at the time of hypoxia. For purposes of the study, hypoxemia was defined as peripheral capillary oxygen saturation less than 90%, as part of intraoperative vital signs recorded in an anesthesia electronic information system.

BMI was grouped into five levels based on obesity severity (further divided class III into lw and high for the current study for the purpose of this study [7]): less than 30 kg/m2 (non-obesity), 30 to 34.9 kg/m2 (class I obesity), 35 to 39.9 kg/m2 (class II obesity), 40 to 44.9 kg/m2 (class III low obesity), and 45 to 50 kg/m2 (class III-high obesity).

Statistical analyses

Numerical variables were summarized by mean and standard deviation. Categorical variables were summarized by frequency and percentage and were compared across groups by the Fisher exact test. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to assess factors associated with hypoxia. Variables included in the univariate analyses were selected a priori based on established clinical relevance and investigator consensus. Variables demonstrating statistical significance or a trend (p < 0.15) in univariate analyses were subsequently considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. There was no missing data in the multivariable models. A two-sided 0.05 significance level was used throughout. Statistical calculations were done using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina).

In addition, we used our previously reported case series (n = 559) (Supplementary Table 1) and current data (n = 1294 without POM) to analyze the pre-procedural risk factors for hypoxia as there was no change in practice or concern regarding comparability between the two cohorts with proximate study eras. The following ML techniques were used; Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART; stan4bart R package), Bayesian Generalized Linear Mixed Effects Model (BGLMM; brms R package), Generalized Linear Mixed Effects Model (GLMM; buildmer R package), Gaussian Process Boosting (GPBoost; gpboost Python package), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO; fit using Bayesian horseshoe prior; brms R package), Logistic Regression and Random Forest (scikit-learn Python package). Among the seven models, the four ML modeling (BART, BGLMM, GPBoost, and LASSO) accounted for anesthesiologist as a random intercept during model training [13–16]. The Logistic Regression and GLMM approaches were fit using stepwise forward and backward regression. Hyperparameters were identified using a randomized hyperparameter search that ran from 25 to 150 iterations depending on the number of hyperparameters considered. Performance on the validation set was compared across the search to identify the set of hyperparameters to be used for final model selection and evaluation on the test set. The performance of models was assessed on the test set using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), with 1000 resamplings of the test data with replacement to calculate non-parametric 95% confidence intervals. Feature importances were calculated for these models in the following ways: LASSO and BGLMM– probability of direction, Random Forest– Gini Index, Logistic Regression and GLMM– Wald statistics (z-score), and GPBoost and BART– Shapley feature explanations (shap Python package) [17, 18]. Importances were aggregated across categorical variables to yield one score per variable.

Results

Study cohort

The patient demographics are summarized in Table 1 (n = 1323). The median age was 58 years (range: 19–90), majority were female (n = 787, 59.4%), the median BMI was 25 kg/m² (range:15–45) with 1021 (77.1%) patients considered as non-obese (< 30 kg/m²) and 302 (22.8%) patients with obesity ( > = 30 kg/m²). Among 302 obese patients, 203 (67.2%) were categorized in class I, 72 (23.8%) in class II, 26 (8.6%) in class III-low, and 1 (0.3%) in class III-high. A history of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) was noted in 72 patients (5.4%). POM was used in 29 patients in this cohort based on physicians’ requests as an available standard of care option.

Incidence and risk factors of hypoxia

Intra-procedural hypoxia developed in 205 cases (15.4%), which lasted 1–3 min in most patients (95%) at a median onset of hypoxia at 2 min (range: 0–51 min) after EGD probe insertion. The incidence of hypoxia was significantly greater in those with obesity (84 of 302, 27.8%, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 23.0%-33.0%) compared to those without obesity (121 of 1021, 11.8%, 95% CI: 9.9%-13.7%, p < 0.0001) with a difference of 16.0% (95% CI: 10.5–21.4%). Interestingly, none of the 29 patients who received oxygen via POM experienced hypoxia, among whom 20 were obese.

Adjusting for other variables (age, sex, EGD only or both EGD and colonoscopy procedures, smoking history, OSA history, history of asthma/COPD, O2 flow rate) in a multivariable logistic regression, obesity and age remained significant for hypoxia (odds ratio [OR] 2.78, 95% CI: 2.01–3.85, p < 0.0001, and OR 1.02, 95%CI 1.01–1.03, p = 0.001, respectively (Table 2). Additionally, higher O2 flow rates (6–10 L/min), irrespective of nasal canula or cut mask, were associated with lower incidence of hypoxia (OR 0.56, 95%CI 0.38–0.84, P = 0.005). Due to the lack of hypoxia in those 29 patients with POM, the use of POM could not be analyzed in multivariable regression models. When the age was dichotomized, age > = 65 years was also associated with hypoxia (OR 1.59, 95%CI 1.16–2.14, p = 0.004). To assess the joint effect of age and obesity, we split the cohort into 4 groups: Age < 65/no obesity (n = 641), Age > = 65/no obesity (n = 380), Age < 65/obesity (n = 198), and Ag > = 65/obesity (n = 104). Compared with the young and non-obese group, each other group had increased risk of hypoxia (Table 3). The C-statistics was modest at 0.643.

Prediction model for hypoxia using ML-technique

To develop ML-based prediction model for hypoxia, we included the cases (n = 559) previously reported (Supplemental Table 1) in the analysis since our practice including anesthesiologists and endoscopists in the same endoscopy center hasn’t changed between the two time periods. The combined analysis set included 1853 patients (29 subjects with POM were removed from the total combined number of 1882). The cases were separated to the training/validation set (n = 1118) from the previous cohort (n = 559) plus patients randomly selected from the current cohort (n = 559). These 1118 cases were randomly split to the training (n = 838) and validation cohorts (n = 280). The remainder of the cases were used as the test cohort (n = 735)– these patients were drawn from the current cohort (Fig. 1). The number of cases that developed hypoxia were 127 (15.2%), 47 (16.8%), and 117 (15.9%) in the training, validation, and test cohort, respectively. After training, the test set AUC for Logistic Regression was 0.61 (95% CI: 0.55 to 0.67); BGLMM was 0.65 (95% CI: 0.59 to 0.71); LASSO was 0.65 (95% CI: 0.59 to 0.71); BART was 0.63 (95% CI: 0.57 to 0.69); GLMM was 0.61 (95% CI: 0.55 to 0.67); Random Forest was 0.59 (95% CI: 0.51 to 0.67); and GPBoost was 0.57 (95% CI: 0.51 to 0.63) (Fig. 2). The BGLMM and LASSO approaches which were trained using Bayesian regression modeling appeared to outperform the other modeling approaches. Feature importances (FI) for all models can be found in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Tables 2 and primarily affirm the importance of BMI and age in the role of hypoxia risk. These importances were computed based on the impact of the feature on the prediction of hypoxia risk, determined by its effect in changing the prediction. Supplementary Fig. 1 provides the Brier scores, slopes, and intercepts across all seven models evaluated on the test set. The calibration slopes were all close to 1.00 (0.98 to 1.15) and the intercepts approach 0.00 (-0.02 to 0.00), which is generally desirable. Furthermore, the Brier scores (0.129 to 0.132, lower is better with 0.1–0.2 representing strong scores) successfully calculated a minimal distance to the ideal diagonal line where the predicted rate equals the true rate across our probability bins, demonstrating reliability and appropriate uncertainty estimate.

Discussion

In this large consecutive cohort of patients undergoing EGD under MAC, we observed an overall incidence of intra-procedural hypoxia at15.4%, confirming that oxygen desaturation remains a common cardiopulmonary event during endoscopic procedures performed under deep sedation. The present study extends our prior observations [7] by demonstrating, in a more contemporary population at a high-volume urban academic outpatient endoscopy center, that obesity and advanced age are risk factors for hypoxia. The use of POM in this small, exploratory, and non-randomized subgroup was associated with an absence of hypoxic events warrant prospective controlled evaluation.

Our findings reinforce the well-recognized physiological vulnerability of obese patients undergoing sedation. Obesity is known to be associated with reduced functional residual capacity and expiratory reserve volume, which can result in rapid oxygen desaturation during periods of hypoventilation or airway obstruction. Sedative agents such as propofol further impair upper airway tone and ventilatory drive, which increase the likelihood of transient airway collapse during endoscopic procedures [2–4].

Older age also emerged as an predictor of hypoxia. Age-related reductions in respiratory reserve, diminished ventilatory responsiveness, and increased sensitivity to sedative medications likely contribute to this finding. We observed higher risk among older and obese patients suggesting an additive or synergistic interaction between aging and obesity-related respiratory compromise. These findings support a risk-stratified approach to optimize oxygen delivery during EGD.

High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) oxygen, which delivers oxygen up to 50–70 L/min with an additional potential benefit of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) effects, has been increasingly used in various clinical and intra-operative settings including MAC. Prospective randomized trials evaluated HFNC in patients undergoing GI endoscopy, demonstrating a significant reduction in hypoxia incidence compared with conventional nasal cannula oxygen delivery [20, 21]. However, HFNC requires specialized equipment, humidification systems, and increased cost due to its high total amount of oxygen used. More importantly, HFNC could be challenging to be delivered in most outpatient endoscopy facilities where a central oxygen supply system is not in place.

In contrast, the POM represents a relatively simple and potentially scalable alternative. POM was associated with no case of hypoxia in our study with a small sample size. A randomized trial in patients undergoing endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) showed the incidence of hypoxia (as defined as SaO2 <90% for at least 10 s) was reduced in the POM group (n = 75) at 5.3% compared with standard nasal canula group (n = 75) at 18.4% (p = 0.024) [22]. More recently, the same group reported the results of a randomized trial comparing HFNC (n = 75) and POM (n = 75) in patients undergoing ERCP, which demonstrated a numerically lower, statistically similar incidence of hypoxia in POM compared with HFNC (1.3% vs. 6.7%, respectively, p = 0.2) [23].

We applied multiple ML approaches to develop prediction models for intra-procedural hypoxia using pre-procedural clinical variables. Across seven modeling strategies, discrimination was modest, with AUROC values ranging from 0.57 to 0.65. Fang and colleagues recently reported their analysis in China evaluating 1160 patients who underwent EGD and showed excellent predictive performance comparing with the STOP-BANG score [24]. In our cohort, it was notable that the result of traditional regression approach was like more complex machine learning models. This finding is consistent with previous research which has found that traditional statistical approaches often yield optimal performance and are easier to interpret across a variety of biomedical datasets [25, 26]. The modest predictive performance likely reflects the inherently multifactorial and dynamic nature of hypoxia during procedural sedation. In contrast to fixed postoperative outcomes, intra-procedural desaturation can be influenced by real-time factors not captured in preoperative datasets, such as sedation depth, medication dosing patterns, airway positioning, procedural stimulation, and clinician interventions. Our findings suggest that meaningful improvements in predictive performance may require incorporation of dynamic physiologic data such as continuous respiratory variables, end-tidal carbon dioxide trends, or time-series vital sign analysis. Future investigations integrating intra-procedural monitoring data may enable real-time dynamic prediction and preemptive intervention strategies.

Several clinically relevant implications emerge from this study. First, hypoxia during EGD under MAC remains common even in a high-volume academic outpatient. Second, readily identifiable patient characteristics—particularly obesity and older age—can assist clinicians in identifying individuals at increased risk. Third, enhanced oxygen delivery strategies such as POM may provide an effective method to mitigate risk in selected high-risk patients while avoiding universal application of resource-intensive technologies.

This study has several limitations. The decision to use POM was clinician-directed rather than randomized, and the absence of hypoxia in this group prevented inclusion of POM use in multivariable regression models. The study endpoint of hypoxia was defined using a threshold SpO₂ value recorded at one-minute intervals, which may not fully capture transient and dynamic nature of hypoxia. Other potentially important variables were not included in the current analysis, such as propofol dosage, sedation depth, dynamic EtCO₂ data, airway maneuvers, respiratory patterns, neck circumference, and procedural stimulation. It is thus possible that the prediction model may improve by incorporating these details into analyses. Finally, the study was conducted at a single academic center, which may increase the risk of overfitting and limit the transportability and real-world applicability of the models.

In conclusion, intra-procedural hypoxia occurs frequently during EGD performed under MAC and is associated with obesity and increasing age. The current exploratory data of POM warrant further evaluation in the high-risk population. ML-models based solely on pre-procedural variables demonstrated modest predictive ability, underscoring the complex and dynamic nature of sedation-related respiratory events and needs for incorporating real-time physiologic data.
