Counterexample to the second eigenfunction having one zero for a non-local Schrödinger operator
Ben Andrews, Sophie Chen

Abstract
. We demonstrate that the second eigenfunction of a perturbed fractional Laplace operator on a bounded interval can exhibit two sign changes, in stark contrast with the classical expectation that it should have exactly one zero. Our construction employs the Kato–Rellich regular perturbation theory to analyse an infinite potential well eigenvalue problem, and then uses an energy‐minimisation argument to extend this counterexample to finite potential wells. Although our detailed analysis focuses on the case s = 1/2 (the Cauchy process), our approach strongly suggests that similar phenomena occur for other rational values of s in (0, 1). At the time of writing, this result provides one of the first rigorous insights into the qualitative behaviour of eigenfunctions for perturbed nonlocal Schrödinger operators.

1.
Introduction and counterexample outline

Let
\Omega
be an open bounded set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a Lipschitz boundary.
For
\epsilon>0
and
s
in
(0,1)
, consider the eigenvalue equation of a perturbed fractional Laplace operator with Dirichlet boundary data:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} ((-\Delta)^{s}+\epsilon V(x))u(x)&=\lambda u(x),&x\in\Omega\\
u(x)&=0,&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

where
V:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a bounded potential and
(-\Delta)^{s}
is the fractional Laplacian defined pointwise by

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x):=c_{n,s}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\epsilon}(x)}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))}{\left|x-y\right|^{n+2s}}dy

for smooth and compactly supported functions
u
. It is known that problem (
1.1
) has a countable set of eigenvalues that can be written as an increasing sequence
-\infty<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{k}\leq\dots
in the index
k
, and the corresponding family of eigenfunctions
forms an orthonormal basis for
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
. The first eigenvalue is simple and has a strictly positive (or negative) eigenfunction on
\Omega
,
repeated eigenvalues have finite multiplicity, and each eigenvalue has a variational structure. Moreover, if the domain
\Omega
also satisfies the exterior ball condition, i.e., there exists a positive radius
r>0
such that each point
p
in the boundary
\partial\Omega
can be touched by a ball of radius
r
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega

[
6
]
, then the eigenfunctions
\{u_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
are Hölder continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
A proof of the above facts can be found in
[
8
]
and references contained therein.

Now let
\Omega
be the the line interval
I=(-1,1)
so that problem (
1.1
) becomes

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} ((-\Delta)^{s}+\epsilon V(x))u(x)&=\lambda u(x),&x\in I\\
u(x)&=0,&x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus I.\end{aligned}\right.

It was shown by Banũelos and Kulczycki in
[
3
]
that for
s=1/2
—the so-called ‘Cauchy process’—the unperturbed eigenvalue problem

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} (-\Delta)^{s}u(x)&=\lambda u(x),&x\in I\\
u(x)&=0,&x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus I\end{aligned}\right.

has a second eigenvalue that is simple and the corresponding eigenfunction (modulo a constant) has exactly one zero. Furthermore, the second eigenfunction is antisymmetric, convex on one half of the interval, and concave on the other half. While these properties can be easily confirmed by direct computation for the classical Laplace operator with Dirichlet boundary condition on the interval (viz., the
s\to 1^{-}
case), it is markedly more difficult to study the geometric properties of eigenfunctions of general fractional Dirichlet Laplace operators, even on the simplest geometry of a bounded line interval. Indeed, at the time of writing, many basic questions concerning the finer spectral properties of fractional Laplace operators remain unknown.

Our aim in this paper is to construct a counterexample to the second eigenfunction having only one sign change of the Dirichlet fractional Laplace operator for
s=1/2
on
I
with a bounded potential. To our knowledge, this result is novel for perturbed fractional Laplace operators. Whether similar counterexamples apply to other fractional values of
s
remains an open question, but it appears that our methods would work for rational values of
s
in
(0,1)
. Since the counterexample arises when the potential is bounded and non-convex, it may be possible to show the sign change properties of the unperturbed eigenfunctions are preserved under a perturbation with a bounded convex potential.

Counterexample outline

Figure 1.
An example of the infinite potential well
V^{\infty}
. Red intervals represent
(x_{i}-\epsilon,x_{i}+\epsilon)
for
i=1,2,3
and small
\epsilon>0
.

Before delving into the details of the counterexample in the remainder of this paper, we provide an overview of the three key steps to its construction.

Step 1: Matrix eigenvalue problem.
First, consider an extreme case where the potential is bounded and constant only on a finite number of mutually disjoint, infinitesimally small sub-intervals
I_{i}=(x_{i}-\epsilon,x_{i}+\epsilon)
.
The subintervals
I_{i}
are centered at the ordered points
x_{1}<x_{2}<\ldots<x_{k}
in
(-1,1)
and are separated by distances that are large compared to their common length
2\epsilon
:

\epsilon\ll\min\{|1-x_{i}|,|1+x_{i}|,|x_{i}-x_{j}|:j\neq i,i=1,\ldots,k\}.

Off the sub-intervals the potential is infinite. Thus the singular potential of interest, which we henceforth refer to as the ‘infinite potential well’, is

(1.4)

V^{\infty}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}V_{i},&x\in I_{i},\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,k\\
\infty,&\mbox{else}.\end{array}\right.

See Figure
1
for an example. To ensure that the energy associated with problem (
1.2
) is finite when
V=V^{\infty}
, i.e.,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(x)\left((-\Delta)^{s}+\epsilon V^{\infty}(x)\right)u(x)dx<\infty,

the solution
u
must be identically zero off the sub-intervals
I_{i}
since
V^{\infty}
is infinite there. Applying this fact to problem (
1.2
) simplifies it to

(1.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} ((-\Delta)^{s}+\epsilon V_{i})u(x)&=\lambda u(x),&x\in I_{i},\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,k\\
u(x)&=0,&x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}.\end{aligned}\right.

As each subinterval
I_{i}
is infinitesimally small, any admissible function
u
can be approximated by a weighted sum of indicator functions on the
k
subintervals:

(1.6)

\displaystyle u(x)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}\mathbf{1}_{I_{i}}(x),

where
m_{i}
is a positive constant weight on the
i
th subinterval, and the indicator function
\mathbf{1_{A}}:\mathbb{R}\to\{0,1\}
on the set
A\subset\mathbb{R}
takes value one on
A
but is otherwise zero. This means the perturbed operator in problem (
1.5
) has an approximate representation as a
k
-by-
k
symmetric matrix
M
(coming from the operator being self-adjoint as we will later see) possessing the following properties:

•

Diagonal elements are proportional to the finite potential values
V_{i}
plus a term
\sigma_{i}
related to the
L^{2}
-norm of the indicator function on
I_{i}
.

•

Off-diagonal terms contain ‘interactions’
\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}
, for
j\neq i
, related to the
L^{2}
-inner product between two indicator functions on distinct subintervals, and they can be assumed to be negative and decreasing in magnitude as we move away from the diagonal in both the vertical and horizontal directions.

So the matrix
M
has the form

(1.7)

M=\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}+\sigma_{1}&\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}&\dots&\dots&\sigma_{1}\sigma_{k}\\
\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}&\ddots&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&\ddots&\sigma_{k-1}\sigma_{k}\\
\sigma_{k}\sigma_{1}&\dots&\dots&\sigma_{k}\sigma_{k-1}&V_{k}+\sigma_{k}\end{bmatrix},

with
\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i}
for all
j\neq i
and
i=1,\ldots,k
. The approximate eigenfunctions are then given by the
k
-dimensional eigenvectors of
M
. In the simplest case, where
k=3
, corresponding to
M
being a three-by-three matrix with eigenvectors that can have at most two sign changes, the problem can be fully analysed. We do this in Section
3
. By studying this finite-dimensional matrix eigenvalue problem, we find that the second eigenvector has two sign changes provided the potential values
V_{i}
are non-convex in
i
, i.e.,
V_{2}\gg V_{1},V_{3}
.

Step 2: Infinite potential well counterexample.
Next, we produce a counterexample to the second eigenfunction of the infinite potential well eigenvalue problem (
1.5
) having only one sign change. The way to this result is to analyse the latter eigenvalue problem as a regular perturbation problem. This ultimately allows us to reduce it to the matrix eigenvalue problem solved in Step 1, so leading to a counterexample.

Step 3: Finite potential well counterexample.

Finally, let each of the disjoint subintervals
I_{i}
in
I
have fixed uniform widths that are small compared to their distances apart. Take a sequence of finite deep potential wells that converge pointwise to the infinite potential
V^{\infty}
of Step 2 as the well depth grows without bound.
We then proceed to produce a counterexample to the second eigenfunction of eigenvalue problem (
1.1
) having only one sign change, in the case where the bounded potential is a sufficiently deep finite potential well, using an energy minimisation argument.

Preliminary notions are defined in the next section.

2.
Functional analytic framework

For any
s
in
(0,1)
, denote by
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the standard fractional Sobolev space

(2.1)

\displaystyle H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):[u]^{2}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty\right\}.

This is a Banach space equipped with the norm

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\left(\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\frac{c_{n,s}}{2}[u]^{2}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right)^{1/2},

where the function
u
belongs to
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\left(\int_{R^{n}}|u|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}
is the
L^{2}
-norm, and the final term is given by

(2.3)

\displaystyle[u]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy\right)^{1/2}.

[
4
, p. 5]
The norm (
2.2
) is induced by the inner product

(2.4)

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\frac{c_{n,s}}{2}\iint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy

for any functions
u,v
in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Thus
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with the real inner product (
2.4
) is a Hilbert space.

Let
\Omega
be an open and bounded set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and define the subspace

(2.5)

\displaystyle H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)=\left\{u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):u=0\mbox{ on }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega\right\}.

It is well-known that
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
with inner product

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle_{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)}=\frac{c_{n,s}}{2}\iint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy

is a Hilbert space with associated norm

(2.6)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)}=\left(\frac{c_{n,s}}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}[u]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\mathcal{E}_{res}[u,u]^{\frac{1}{2}},

where, by absorbing the zero boundary condition into
[u]^{2}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
,
\mathcal{E}_{res}[\cdot,\cdot]
is given by

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{res}[u,u]=\frac{c_{n,s}}{2}[u]^{2}_{H^{s}(\Omega)}+\int_{\Omega}\kappa(x)u(x)^{2}dx,

and the kernel
\kappa(\cdot)
in the integral of equation (
2.7
) is defined as

(2.8)

\displaystyle\kappa(x)=c_{n,x}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dy,\qquad x\in\Omega.

[
2
, Sec. 2.1]

On
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
, the norm (
2.6
) is equivalent to the induced norm from
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
given by equation (
2.2
). The key step to showing this is the following lemma.

Lemma 2.9
.

There exists a constant
c>0
such that for any function
u
in
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
,

[u]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\geq c\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

To generate a contradiction, suppose that for every constant
c>0
, there exists a function
u
in
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}>\frac{1}{c}[u]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

In particular, there exists a sequence of constants
c_{i}
going to zero and a sequence of functions
u_{i}
in
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
, depending on
c_{i}
, such that
[u_{i}]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty
, but

\frac{1}{c_{i}}[u_{i}]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\|u_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\to\infty\qquad\mbox{as}\qquad i\to\infty\mbox{ (and }c_{i}\to 0).

Equivalently,

(2.10)

\displaystyle 0\leq[u_{i}]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<c_{i}\|u_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\to 0\qquad\mbox{as}\qquad i\to\infty\mbox{ (and }c_{i}\to 0).

We can therefore re-scale the
u_{i}
’s so that
[u_{i}]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\to 0
as
i\to\infty
, while
\|u_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
for each
i
.

Now fix a sufficiently large
R>0
such that
\Omega\subset B_{R}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
B_{R}(0)
is the open ball of radius
R
centred at zero in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This is an open Lipschitz domain with bounded boundary and so is a bounded extension domain for
H^{s}(B_{R}(0))
by
[
4
, p. 33]
. Moreover,
\{u_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is a bounded subset of
L^{2}(B_{R}(0))
, since
\|u\|_{L^{2}(B_{R}(0))}=\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
for each
i
. Finally,

\sup_{u_{i}\in\{u_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}}[u_{i}]^{2}_{H^{s}(B_{R}(0))}=\sup_{u_{i}\in\{u_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}}\iint\limits_{B_{R}(0)\times B_{R}(0)}\frac{(u_{i}(x)-u_{i}(y))^{2}dxdy}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}<\infty

as
[u_{i}]_{H^{s}(B_{R}(0))}\leq[u_{i}]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\to 0
as
i\to\infty
from inequality (
2.10
). It therefore follows from
[
4
, Th. 7.1]
that
\{u_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is precompact in
L^{2}(B_{R}(0))
; i.e., there exists a subsequence
u_{i_{j}}
that converges in
L^{2}
to a limit point,
\bar{u}
, say, with
\|\bar{u}\|_{L^{2}(B_{R}(0))}=1
. On the other hand, inequality (
2.10
) implies that
[\bar{u}]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=0
. Explicitly, we have

\displaystyle 0=[\bar{u}]^{2}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=[\bar{u}]^{2}_{H^{s}(B_{R}(0))}+2\int_{B_{R}(0)}\tilde{\kappa}(x)\bar{u}(x)^{2}dx,

where
\tilde{\kappa}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R}(0)}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dy
. The seminorm
[\bar{u}]_{H^{s}(B_{R}(0))}
taking value zero implies that
\bar{u}
is constant (a.e), while the final integral term being zero implies that the constant is zero. This contradicts the
L^{2}
norm of
\bar{u}
being one.
∎

Proposition 2.11
.

There exist positive constants
c
and
C
such that for any function
u
in
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
,

c\|u\|_{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq\|u\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\|u\|_{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

The result follows from applying the respective definitions of
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)}
and
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and Lemma
2.9
.
∎

We require two further basic properties of
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Lemma 2.12
.

H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
is closed in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

Let
\{u_{k}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence in
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
that converges to a limit point
u
in
\left(H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right)
. By definition
u_{k}=0
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
for any
k=1,2,\ldots
. Hence, the pointwise limit of
\{u_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is zero on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
. Since limits are unique in a metric space, this implies that
u=0
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
. Thus
u
belongs to
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
.
As
u
is arbitrary,
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
contains all of its limit points and is therefore closed in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
∎

Proposition 2.13
.

H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

We have the nesting of function spaces

(2.14)

\displaystyle C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)\subset H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)\subset L^{2}(\Omega),

where
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
denotes the space of smooth and compactly supported functions on
\Omega
. Taking closures with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
gives

(2.15)

\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)\subset\overline{C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)}\subset\overline{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)}\subset L^{2}(\Omega),

where we have used the fact that
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Thus,
\overline{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)}=L^{2}(\Omega)
.
∎

The function space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is related to the fractional Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{s}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Similarly the subspace
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
is associated with its restriction to
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with Dirichlet boundary condition on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
. This latter operator is called the ‘restricted fractional Laplace operator’ and is denoted by
(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}
.

We are interested in the weak formulation of problem (
1.1
).
By absorbing the non-local Dirichlet boundary condition of problem (
1.1
) into the fractional Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{s}
, the resulting unbounded linear operator of interest is
L_{V}:=(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}+\epsilon V
on a bounded set
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with (densely-defined) domain
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Definition 2.16

(Weak solution)
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Given a real-valued function
f
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and a bounded real-valued potential
V
, we say that a function
u
in
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
is a weak solution of the boundary value problem

(2.17)

\left\{\begin{aligned} ((-\Delta)^{s}+\epsilon V(x))u(x)&=f(x),&x\in\Omega\\
u(x)&=0,&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega\end{aligned}\right.

provided that
u
satisfies

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{res}[u,w]+\epsilon\int_{\Omega}V(x)u(x)w(x)dx=\int_{\Omega}f(x)w(x)dx

for all
w
in
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
.
\mathcal{E}_{res}(\cdot,\cdot)
is as defined in equation (
2.7
).

Fix
\lambda
in
\mathbb{R}
. We say that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of eigenvalue problem (
1.1
) if there exists a non-zero function
u
in
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
such that
u
is a weak solution of problem (
2.17
) with
f=\lambda u
. Then
u
is an eigenfunction of problem (
1.1
) with eigenvalue
\lambda
.

3.
Matrix eigenvalue problem

Following the strategy in Section
1
, we use the matrix
M
given by equation (
1.7
) to approximate the infinite-dimensional eigenvalue problem (
1.5
). Our goal is to completely analyse the simplest such approximation, corresponding to
k=3
, and show that it has a second eigenfunction with two sign changes under certain conditions on the discrete potential
V_{i}
on each of the subintervals
I_{i}
.

We can represent this three-by-three model using the simplified matrix

(3.1)

\bar{M}=\begin{bmatrix}U&c&b\\
c&V&a\\
b&a&W\end{bmatrix},

where
U,V,W
denote the diagonal ‘potential’ terms
V_{i}+\sigma_{i}
, and the off-diagonal elements
a,b,c
are the interactions
\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}
, with
j\neq i
, in
M
. As before, we assume
a,b,c<0
and
|a|,|c|>|b|
.

For any vector
v
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with (Euclidean) norm
\|v\|=1
, we can define the Rayleigh-Ritz quotient
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}
associated with
\bar{M}
, the so-called ‘energy’:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(v)

\displaystyle=v^{T}\bar{M}v,

where
v^{T}
denotes the transpose of the column vector
v
. Notice that when
v
is an eigenvector of
\bar{M}
,
\mathcal{E}(v)
returns its corresponding eigenvalue.

Theorem 3.3
.

The minimum eigenvalue
\lambda_{M}
of the matrix
\bar{M}
is simple and has an eigenvector
v_{M}
with all components positive.

Proof.

Let
A=\sigma I-\bar{M}
, where
\sigma>0
is a real constant and
I
denotes the three-by-three identity matrix. Observe that if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\bar{M}
with corresponding eigenvector
v
, then

Av=(\sigma I-\bar{M})v=\sigma Iv-\bar{M}v=\sigma v-\lambda v=(\sigma-\lambda)v.

That is,
v
is also an eigenvector of
A
but corresponding to the eigenvalue
\mu:=\sigma-\lambda
.

If we let
A_{ij}
and
\bar{M}_{ij}
denote the
ij
-th entry of
A
and and
\bar{M}
, respectively, then
A_{ij}=\sigma\delta_{ij}-\bar{M}_{ij}
, where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta function. The diagonal entries
A_{ii}=\sigma-\bar{M}_{ii}
are strictly bigger than zero if we choose
\sigma>\max_{i}\{M_{ii}\}
. Off-diagonal entries
A_{ij}=-\bar{M}_{ij}
are positive for all
i\neq j
, since the off-diagonal entries of
\bar{M}
are assumed to be negative by construction. Thus
A
is a strictly positive real square matrix provided that
\sigma>\max_{i}\{\bar{M}_{ii}\}
.

By the Perron-Frobenius theorem,
A
has a largest eigenvalue
\rho
that is simple and a corresponding eigenvector
v_{\rho}
with all components positive. Moreover, there are no other positive eigenvectors other than positive multiples of
v_{\rho}
. Since the eigenvalues of
\bar{M}
are related to those of
A
by
\lambda=\sigma-\mu
, we see that if
\rho
is the largest eigenvalue of
A
, then
\sigma-\rho
gives the smallest eigenvalue of
\bar{M}
. Set
\lambda_{M}:=\sigma-\rho
. As
\rho
is simple,
\lambda_{M}
is also simple. Furthermore, the eigenvector
v_{\rho}
corresponding to
\rho
is the same eigenvector for
\lambda_{M}
. So
v_{M}:=v_{\rho}
has all components positive.
∎

Having established that the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{M}
of
\bar{M}
is simple, a corollary is that repeated eigenvalues occur only if the remaining two eigenvalues
\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{3}
are equal. As the
\lambda_{M}
-eigenspace is a straight-line through the origin along a unit vector in the positive octant of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, its orthogonal complement
v_{M}^{\perp}
is a plane through the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices guarantees that we can find an orthonormal set of eigenvectors
\{v_{M},v_{2},v_{3},\}
of
\bar{M}
that form a basis for
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. So
v_{M}^{\perp}
is spanned by
v_{2},v_{3}
and contains any eigenvector with a zero component.

Corollary 3.4
.

Any multiplicity of eigenvalues occurs if

As mentioned in the counterexample outline in Section

4.1.
Regular perturbation theory

Let
H_{0}
be an operator with a discrete eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
that may be simple or degenerate. Consider the perturbed operator
T(\beta)=H_{0}+f(\beta)
, where the perturbation
f(\beta)
is another operator that depends on a small perturbation parameter
\beta
taking values in a connected region
R
of the complex plane. The Kato-Rellich theory provides simple criteria under which
T(\beta)
is guaranteed to have eigenvalues
\lambda(\beta)
near
\lambda_{0}
in the form of convergent power series in
\beta
near zero:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\lambda(\beta)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{0}+\beta\lambda^{1}+\beta^{2}\lambda^{2}+\ldots

(4.2)

\displaystyle\phi(\beta)

\displaystyle=\phi^{0}+\beta\phi^{1}+\beta^{2}\phi^{2}+\ldots,

where
\lambda(\beta)
and
\phi(\beta)
are the perturbed eigenvalue and corresponding perturbed eigenfunction, respectively, and
\lambda^{n}
and
\phi^{n}
are the
n
th order corrections to
\lambda_{0}
and
\phi
.

We are interested in the case where
\lambda_{0}
is degenerate. In this case, there are two criteria to be satisfied:
T(\beta)
needs to be an analytic family in the sense of Kato for
\beta
near zero (see below); and
T(\beta)
must be self-adjoint for each real-valued
\beta
in
R
.
For the infinite potential well eigenvalue problem (
1.5
), the self-adjointness property follows naturally from the self-adjointness of the fractional Dirichlet Laplace operator on a bounded domain, as we show below. So the central difficulty to overcome is showing the operator
(-\Delta)^{s}+\epsilon V_{i}
, for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
, or a rescaled version of it (see below), is an analytic family in the sense of Kato.

Definition 4.3

(Analytic family of type (A))
.

Let
R
be a connected domain in
\mathbb{C}
, and let
T(\beta)
be a closed operator with non-empty resolvent set for each
\beta
in
R
. We say that
T(\beta)
is an analytic family of type (A) if and only if the following two conditions are satisfied:

(i)

The operator domain of
T(\beta)
is a set
D
independent of
\beta
.

(ii)

For each
\phi\in D
,
T(\beta)\phi
is a vector-valued analytic function of
\beta
.

It is a fact that any analytic family of type (A) is also analytic in the sense of Kato.
[
7
, p.16, Vol. 4]
So provided we can satisfy Definition
4.3
, the Kato-Rellich theory of regular perturbations for degenerate eigenvalues guarantees the existence of perturbed eigenvalues (and corresponding eigenfunctions) analytic in the perturbation parameter. This is formalised in the theorem to follow, taken from
[
7
, Ch. XII.2, Vol. 4]
.

Theorem 4.4

(Kato-Rellich theory for degenerate eigenvalues)
.

Assume the following:

(i.)

T(\beta)
is an analytic family in the sense of Kato for
\beta
near zero.

(ii).

T(\beta)
is self-adjoint for each
\beta
real.

(iii.)

\lambda_{0}
is a discrete eigenvalue of multiplicity
k
of the unperturbed operator
T(0)
.

Then there are
k
not necessarily distinct single-valued functions, analytic near
\beta=0
, denoted by
\lambda^{(1)}(\beta),\lambda^{(2)}(\beta),\ldots,\lambda^{(k)}(\beta)
, with
\lambda^{1}(0)=...=\lambda^{k}(0)=\lambda_{0}
, so that
\lambda^{(i)}(\beta)

are eigenvalues of
T(\beta)
for
\beta
near zero. Furthermore, these are the only eigenvalues near
\lambda_{0}
.

As we shall see in the final part of this section, Theorem
4.4
reduces an infinite-dimensional degenerate eigenvalue problem to a finite-dimensional one, so that the existence of eigenvectors in the finite-dimensional case implies their existence in the infinite-dimensional problem. We now turn to applying the Kato-Rellich theory to construct a counterexample to the second eigenfunction having only one zero of eigenvalue problem (
1.5
).

4.2.
Application of the Kato-Rellich theory

The perturbed Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{s}+\epsilon V_{i}
on

, for

, is not an analytic family of type (A) (see below); however, re-scaling it so that it is defined on a suitable product space does satisfy the analyticity condition. Working within the re-scaled framework allows us to apply both the Kato-Rellich and Rayleigh-Schrödinger perturbation theories. Constructing a counterexample in this re-scaled setting is tantamount to producing a counterexample for the original family of operators, since we just re-scale back to retrieve the original problem.

It is worthwhile, however, to first examine a couple of pertinent properties of the original family of operators: namely, the degeneracy of the ground state eigenvalue of the unperturbed operator, i.e., the fractional Dirichlet Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{s}
on a finite union of mutually disjoint subintervals

in

4.2.1.

We know that the fractional Laplace operator

Without the small perturbation

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}(-\Delta)^{s}\left(\frac{\phi_{1}\left(\frac{x-x_{i}}{\epsilon}\right)}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right),x\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i},

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}\lambda_{1}\left(\frac{\phi_{1}\left(\frac{x-x_{i}}{\epsilon}\right)}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right),x\in I_{i}

\displaystyle\mbox{ for one of }i=1,\ldots,k\mbox{ (the sets are disjoint)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\left(\frac{\phi_{1}\left(\frac{x-x_{i}}{\epsilon}\right)}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right),x\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}

and

\displaystyle\frac{\phi_{1}\left(\frac{x-x_{i}}{\epsilon}\right)}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}=0,x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{i},i=1,\ldots,k

so

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\frac{\phi_{1}\left(\frac{x-x_{i}}{\epsilon}\right)}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}=0,x\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{i}=\mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}

by De Morgan’s laws.
∎

Having established that the discrete ground state eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
of the unperturbed problem (
4.6
) is degenerate, we now show that the perturbed family of operators
(-\Delta)^{s}+\epsilon V_{i}
on
I_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,k
, with Dirichlet boundary condition on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}
, is self-adjoint for each
\epsilon>0
. An intermediate step is showing that the fractional Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{s}
on
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}
, with Dirichlet boundary data off the union of subintervals, is self-adjoint. We can achieve this using a corollary from
[
7
, p. 137, Vol. 2]
. First recall the definition of the resolvent set of an unbounded linear operator
T:D(T)\to\mathcal{H}
, where
D(T)
is the (densely-defined) domain of
T
in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
:

Definition 4.9

(Resolvent set.)
.

Let
T
be a closed densely-defined operator on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, and let
I
denote the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}
. A complex number
\mu
is in the resolvent
\rho(T)
set of
T
if
T-\mu I
is a bijection of
D(T)
onto
\mathcal{H}
with a bounded inverse.

Corollary 4.10
.

If a closed symmetric operator has at least one real number in its resolvent set, then it is self-adjoint.
[
7
, p. 137, Vol. 2]

Lemma 4.11
.

The fractional Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{s}
on
I_{i}=(x_{i}-\epsilon,x_{i}+\epsilon)
in
(-1,1)
, with
i=1,\ldots,k
and
I_{i}\cap I_{j}=\emptyset
for all
j\neq i
, and Dirichlet boundary condition on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}
, is self-adjoint.

Proof.

Fix
\epsilon>0
and let
\Omega:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}
. Since any admissible function is zero off
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}
, the natural densely-defined domain is
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
(see Proposition
2.13
). Thus
T:H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
defines an unbounded linear operator given by

\displaystyle Tu(x)

\displaystyle=(-\Delta)^{s}u(x),x\in\Omega

\displaystyle=c_{s}\operatorname{P.V}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dy

\displaystyle=c_{s}\operatorname{P.V}\int_{I_{i}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dy+

\displaystyle c_{s}\int_{\bigcup_{j\neq i}I_{j}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dy+c_{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}}\frac{u(x)}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dy

\displaystyle=c_{s}\operatorname{P.V}\int_{\Omega}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dy+c_{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\Omega}\frac{u(x)}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dy

\displaystyle=:(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}u(x),

for any
u\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
. We have applied the Dirichlet boundary condition on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\Omega
in the second line to derive the restricted fractional Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}

The action of
(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}
on
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
is defined in the weak sense: for any
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we say that
u\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
is a weak solution of

(4.12)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}u(x)=f(x),x\in\Omega,

whenever

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{res}[u,v]

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{n.s}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy+

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}v(x)\kappa(x)u(x)dx

(4.13)

\displaystyle=\langle f,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

for each
v\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
, where

\displaystyle\kappa(x)=c_{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\Omega}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dy,x\in\Omega.

We claim that for each
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
F_{f}:H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
given by

(4.14)

\displaystyle F_{f}(v)=\langle f,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)},v\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)

is a bounded linear functional: Linearity of
F_{f}(\cdot)
follows from the (bi-)linearity of the real inner product. For boundedness, we calculate:

\displaystyle|F_{f}(v)|=|\langle f,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}|

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)},

where Hölder’s inequality was applied to obtain the first line, Lemma
2.9
was applied to the second line, and
C
is a sufficiently large and positive constant. After rearranging and taking suprema on both sides of the equation, we arrive at the upper-bound

\displaystyle\sup_{v\neq 0}\frac{|F_{f}(v)|}{\|v\|_{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)}}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<\infty.

Thus by the Riesz representation theorem, for each
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique element
u\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

(4.15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{res}[u,v]=\langle u,v\rangle_{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)}=F_{f}(v)=\langle f,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for all
v\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
. In other words,
(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}:H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
defined in the above weak sense is a bijection of
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
onto
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

We next show that
(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}:H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is closed: any sequence of functions
\{w_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
having the property

(4.16)

\left.\begin{aligned} w_{k}&\to w\\
(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}w_{k}&\to\overline{w}\end{aligned}\right\}\mbox{ in }L^{2}(\Omega)\mbox{ as }k\to\infty

implies that
w\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
and
\overline{w}=(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}w
.

Letting
v=u
in equation (
4.15
) gives

\displaystyle\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)}=\mathcal{E}_{res}[u,u]

\displaystyle=\langle f,u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)},

so that

(4.17)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=C\|(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Fix
n,m>N
for some
N>0
. Then

\displaystyle\|w_{n}-w_{m}\|_{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}w_{n}-(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}w_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(4.18)

\displaystyle\to 0

as
n,m\to\infty
, since the sequences
\{w_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
and
\{\overline{w_{k}}=(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}w_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
converge in
L^{2}(\Omega)
by assumption (
4.16
), so they are Cauchy. This implies that
\{w_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is Cauchy in
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
. The latter space is complete with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)}
,
which means

(4.19)

\displaystyle w_{k}\to w\mbox{ in }H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)\mbox{ as }k\to\infty,

so
w\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
. Applying the operator to both sides implies that

(4.20)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}w_{k}\to(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}w\mbox{ in }L^{2}(\Omega);

hence,
\overline{w}=(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}w
(by uniqueness of limits).

Since
(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}:H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is closed, densely-defined, and bijective (and has a bounded inverse by the closed graph theorem
[
7
, p. 253, Vol. 1]
), by setting
\mu=0
in Definition
4.9
, it follows that
0\in\rho((-\Delta)^{s}_{res})
.

Finally, we show that
(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}:H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is symmetric: For any
u,v\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
, equation (
4.15
) and the fact that
\mathcal{E}_{res}[\cdot,\cdot]
and the
L^{2}
inner product are symmetric imply that

(4.21)

\displaystyle\langle u,(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\mathcal{E}_{res}[v,u]=\mathcal{E}_{res}[u,v]=\langle(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}u,v\rangle_{L^{s}(\Omega)}.

So by Corollary
4.10
,
(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}:H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is self-adjoint.
∎

We can now use Lemma
4.11
to show the perturbed Dirichlet fractional Laplace operator is closed and self-adjoint.

Proposition 4.22
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. The perturbed fractional Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{s}+\epsilon V_{i}
on a finite union of mutually disjoint subintervals
I_{i}
in
(-1,1)
for
i=1,\ldots,k
, with Dirichlet boundary data on its complement, is self-adjoint.

Proof.

(1.) Let
\Omega:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}
, a bounded open set in
\mathbb{R}
. Since any admissible function is zero outside of
\Omega
due to the Dirichlet boundary condition, the natural densely-defined domain of
(-\Delta)^{s}+\epsilon V_{i}
is
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Fix
u
in
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
and, in the same way as was done in the proof of Lemma
4.11
, apply the Dirichlet boundary condition to the perturbed fractional Laplace operator acting on
u
:

\displaystyle\left((-\Delta)^{s}+\epsilon V_{i}\right)u(x)

\displaystyle=\left((-\Delta)^{s}_{res}+\epsilon V_{i}\right)u(x),\qquad x\in I_{i},i=1,2,\ldots,k.

Then for each small
\epsilon>0
,
T(\epsilon):H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is a densely-defined unbounded linear operator with action

(4.23)

\displaystyle T(\epsilon)u(x)=\left((-\Delta)^{s}_{res}+\epsilon V_{i}\right)u(x),\qquad x\in I_{i},i=1,\ldots,k

for
u\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
.

(2.) The operator
T(\epsilon)
is closed for each
\epsilon>0
: Let
\{w_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of functions in
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

(4.24)

\left.\begin{aligned} w_{k}&\to w\\
\left((-\Delta)^{s}_{res}+\epsilon V_{i}\right)w_{k}&\to\overline{w}\end{aligned}\right\}\mbox{ in }L^{2}(\Omega)\mbox{ as }k\to\infty

We want to show that
w\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
and
\overline{w}=\left((-\Delta)^{s}_{res}+\epsilon V_{i}\right)w
.
The first property in (
4.24
) implies that
\epsilon V_{i}w_{k}\to\epsilon V_{i}w
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. While the second one implies that
(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}w_{k}
converges to a point
\overline{w_{0}}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Thus,
\overline{w}=\overline{w_{0}}+\epsilon V_{i}w
(by uniqueness of limits). In the proof of Lemma
4.11
we showed that
(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}:H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is closed.
So
w
belongs to
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
and
\overline{w_{0}}=(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}w
. It follows that
\overline{w}=(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}w+\epsilon V_{i}w=\left((-\Delta)^{s}_{res}+\epsilon V_{i}\right)w
.

(3.) Since the potential term
\epsilon V_{i}
on
I_{i}
, for
i=1,\ldots,k
, is a bounded linear operator on
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and for any
u,v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, it satisfies

(4.25)

\displaystyle\langle u,\epsilon V_{i}w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle\epsilon V_{i}u,w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

it is self-adjoint. It is thus sufficient to show that the restricted fractional Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}
is self-adjoint on their common densely-defined domain
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
. We showed this in Lemma
4.11
.
∎

4.2.2.
Properties of the re-scaled family of operators

As explained previously, the family of perturbed fractional Laplace operators
(-\Delta)^{s}+\epsilon V_{i}
on
I_{i}
, for
i=1,\ldots,k
, and with Dirichlet boundary condition, is not directly amenable to a regular perturbation analysis. However, we may re-scale the family of operators under the transformation

(4.26)

\displaystyle(x_{j}-\epsilon,x_{j}+\epsilon)\ni y

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{y-x_{j}}{\epsilon}\in(-1,1),

where
\epsilon>0
and
j=1,\ldots,k
.
This rescaling allows us to produce an analytic family of type A, which then suffices for us to apply Theorem
4.4
, the Kato-Rellich theory for degenerate eigenvalues.

Starting with the original eigenvalue problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} \left((-\Delta)^{s}+\epsilon V_{i}\right)u(x)&=\lambda u(x),\qquad x\in I_{i},i=1,\ldots,k\\
u(x)&=0,\qquad\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i},\end{aligned}\right.

which, as shown in the proof of Proposition
4.22
, can be re-written as

(4.27)

\displaystyle\left((-\Delta)^{s}_{res}+\epsilon V_{i}\right)u(x)=\lambda u(x),\qquad x\in I_{i},i=1,\ldots,k

by absorbing the boundary condition into the eigenvalue equation, we re-scale the problem using transformation (
4.26
) as follows:
The discrete potential
V:\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}I_{j}\to\mathbb{R},V(x)=V_{j},x\in I_{j}
,
j=1,2,\ldots,k
, is re-scaled to

\displaystyle V_{j}

\displaystyle=V(x),\qquad x\in I_{j}

\displaystyle=V(\epsilon z+x_{j}),\qquad z\in(-1,1)

(4.28)

\displaystyle=:V_{j}(z),\qquad z\in I,

where
x=\epsilon z+x_{j}
,
\epsilon>0
, and
I=(-1,1)
. For each
j=1,\ldots,k
, the real-valued function
V_{j}:I\to\mathbb{R}
defined by equation (
4.2.2
) is the
j
-th component of the re-scaled potential operator
\overline{V}:\prod_{j=1}^{k}L^{2}(I)\to\prod_{j=1}^{k}L^{2}(I)
,

(4.29)

\displaystyle\overline{V}[\underline{u}]=\left(V_{1}u_{1},\ldots,V_{k}u_{k}\right),

for
\underline{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
in
\prod_{j=1}^{k}L^{2}(I)
. Equipped with the inner product

(4.30)

\displaystyle\langle\underline{u},\underline{v}\rangle_{\prod}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\langle u_{j},v_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(I)},

\prod_{j=1}^{k}L^{2}(I)
is a Hilbert space (since each component
L^{2}(I)
is complete with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(I)}
). Now fix
x
in
I_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots k
, and calculate

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lambda u(x)-\epsilon V_{i}u(x)-(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda u(x)-\epsilon V_{i}u(x)-c_{s}\operatorname{P.V}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dy

\displaystyle=\lambda u(x)-\epsilon V_{i}u(x)-c_{s}\operatorname{P.V}\int_{I_{i}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dy

\displaystyle-c_{s}\int_{\bigcup_{j\neq i}I_{j}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dy-c_{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}I_{j}}\frac{u(x)}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dy

\displaystyle=\lambda u(x)-\epsilon V_{i}u(x)-c_{s}\operatorname{P.V}\int_{I_{i}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dy

\displaystyle-c_{s}\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{I_{j}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dy-c_{s}\int_{\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{j}}\frac{u(x)}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dy

\displaystyle\indent(\mbox{Re-scale: }I_{i}=(x_{i}-\epsilon,x_{i}+\epsilon),I_{j}\cap I_{i}=\emptyset,j\neq i,i=1,\ldots,k

\displaystyle\indent x\in(x_{i}-\epsilon,x_{i}+\epsilon)\Leftrightarrow z:=\frac{x-x_{i}}{\epsilon}\in(-1,1),x=\epsilon z+x_{i}

\displaystyle\indent y\in(x_{j}-\epsilon,x_{j}+\epsilon)\Leftrightarrow w:=\frac{y-x_{j}}{\epsilon}\in(-1,1),y=\epsilon w+x_{j},dy=\epsilon dw

\displaystyle\indent y\in\mathbb{R}\setminus(x_{j}-\epsilon,x_{j}+\epsilon)\mbox{ for each }j=1,\ldots,k\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\indent\qquad w:=\frac{y-x_{j}}{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1,1)\mbox{ for each }j=1,\ldots,k,\mbox{ so }

\displaystyle\indent\qquad y=\epsilon w+x_{j},dy=\epsilon dw\mbox{ for each }j=1,\ldots,k

\displaystyle=\lambda u(\epsilon z+x_{i})-\epsilon V_{i}u(\epsilon z+x_{i})-c_{s}\operatorname{P.V}\int_{I}\frac{(u(\epsilon z+x_{i})-u(\epsilon w+x_{i}))\epsilon dw}{|\epsilon z+x_{i}-\epsilon w-x_{i}|^{1+2s}}

\displaystyle-c_{s}\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{I}\frac{(u(\epsilon z+x_{i})-u(\epsilon w+x_{j}))}{|\epsilon z+x_{i}-\epsilon w-x_{j}|^{1+2s}}\epsilon dw-c_{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus I}\frac{u(\epsilon z+x_{i})\epsilon dw}{|\epsilon z+x_{i}-\epsilon w-x_{i}|^{1+2s}}

\displaystyle\indent\left(u(\epsilon z+x_{j})=\frac{u_{j}(z)}{\sqrt{\epsilon}},u(\epsilon w+x_{j})=\frac{u_{j}(w)}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\textdagger\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda u_{i}(z)-\epsilon V_{i}u_{i}(z)-c_{s}\operatorname{P.V}\int_{I}\frac{(u_{i}(z)-u_{i}(w))}{\epsilon^{2s}|z-w|^{1+2s}}dw

\displaystyle-c_{s}\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{I}\frac{(u_{i}(z)-u_{j}(w))}{|\epsilon(z-w)+x_{i}-x_{j}|^{1+2s}}\epsilon dw

\displaystyle-c_{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus I}\frac{u_{i}(z)}{\epsilon^{2s}|z-w|^{1+2s}}dw

\displaystyle(\mbox{Factor of $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}$ canceled out on both sides})

\displaystyle=\lambda u_{i}(z)-\epsilon V_{i}u_{i}(z)-\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2s}}(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}u_{i}(z)-c_{s}\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{I}\frac{(u_{i}(z)-u_{j}(w))\epsilon dw}{|\epsilon(z-w)+x_{i}-x_{j}|^{1+2s}},

where in the final integral in the third last equality, we made the
choice

y=\epsilon w+x_{i}
to simplify the denominator (since
y=\epsilon w+x_{j}
for all
j=1,\ldots,k
). So the re-scaled eigenvalue problem is

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2s}\lambda u_{i}(z)

\displaystyle=(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}u_{i}(z)

(4.31)

\displaystyle+\epsilon^{1+2s}\left(V_{i}u_{i}(z)+c_{s}\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{I}\frac{(u_{i}(z)-u_{j}(w))dw}{|\epsilon(z-w)+x_{i}-x_{j}|^{1+2s}}\right)

for
z
in
I
and
u_{i}\in H^{s}_{0}(I)
, with
i=1,\ldots,k
.
The corresponding family of re-scaled perturbed operators is
\overline{T}(\epsilon):\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}H^{s}_{0}(I)\to\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}L^{2}(I)
,

\displaystyle\left[\overline{T}(\epsilon)[\underline{u}]\right]_{i}

\displaystyle=\left[\overline{T}(\epsilon)[(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})]\right]_{i}

\displaystyle=(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}u_{i}(z)

(4.32)

\displaystyle+\epsilon^{1+2s}\left(V_{i}u_{i}(z)+c_{s}\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{I}\frac{(u_{i}(z)-u_{j}(w))dw}{|\epsilon(z-w)+x_{i}-x_{j}|^{1+2s}}\right)

for each
i=1,\ldots,k
and
\underline{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})\in\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}H^{s}_{0}(I)
, and
[\underline{u}]_{i}=u_{i}
denotes the
i
th component of the
k
-dimensional vector
\underline{u}
with elements
u_{i}
in
H^{s}_{0}(I)
. Notice that the operator domain
\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}H^{s}_{0}(I)
of
\overline{T}(\epsilon)
is independent of
\epsilon
and dense in
\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}L^{2}(I)
; the key step in verifying the latter property is showing density of
H^{s}_{0}(I)
in
L^{2}(I)
, which we did in Lemma
2.13
. Moreover,
\overline{T}(\epsilon)
is unbounded since, for instance, the unperturbed operator is unbounded;
and a simple direct computation shows that it is also linear. Thus,
\overline{T}(\epsilon):\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}H^{s}_{0}(I)\to\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}L^{2}(I)
is a densely-defined unbounded linear operator for each
\epsilon>0
.

In summary, the original eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\lambda u(x)=\left((-\Delta)^{s}_{res}+\epsilon V_{i}\right)u(x),\qquad x\in I_{i},i=1,\ldots,k,

for
u\in H^{s}_{0}\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}I_{j}\right)
, is equivalent to the re-scaled problem

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2s}\lambda u_{i}(z)

\displaystyle=(-\Delta)^{s}_{res}u_{i}(z)

\displaystyle+\epsilon^{1+2s}\left(V_{i}u_{i}(z)+c_{s}\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{I}\frac{(u_{i}(z)-u_{j}(w))dw}{|\epsilon(z-w)+x_{i}-x_{j}|^{1+2s}}\right),z\in I,

for
i=1,\ldots,k
and
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})\in\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}H^{s}_{0}(I)
. It follows that if the original family of perturbed operators
T(\epsilon)
is closed and self-adjoint for each
\epsilon>0
, as shown in Lemma
4.22
, then re-scaling confers the same properties to the re-scaled family of operators
\overline{T}(\epsilon)
. Thus we have the following important corollaries:

Corollary 4.33
.

The re-scaled family of operators
\overline{T}(\epsilon)
given by equation (
4.2.2
) is closed and self-adjoint for each
\epsilon>0
.

The next corollary makes use of part of a theorem from
[
7
, Th. X.I, Vol. 2]
.

Theorem 4.34
.

Let
A
be a closed symmetric operator on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Then
A
is self-adjoint if and only if the spectrum of A is a subset of the real axis.

Corollary 4.35
.

The re-scaled family of operators
\overline{T}(\epsilon)
given by equation (
4.2.2
) has non-empty resolvent set for each
\epsilon>0
.

Proof.

Corollary
4.33
and Theorem
4.34
imply that
\overline{T}(\epsilon)
has a spectrum
\sigma(\overline{T}(\epsilon))
that is real. Thus its resolvent set
\rho(\overline{T}(\epsilon))=\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(\overline{T}(\epsilon))
must be non-empty.
∎

Since we are only interested in small positive values of
\epsilon
close to zero, we may Taylor expand the kernel in the integral in equation (
4.2.2

\displaystyle|\epsilon(z-w)+x_{i}-x_{j}|^{-(1+2s)}

5.
Finite potential well counterexample

The conclusion of the previous section shows that, after re-scaling, for each
\epsilon
positive and sufficiently small, the first three eigenvalues of the infinite potential well eigenvalue problem (

) are all simple with corresponding eigenfunctions that are qualitatively similar to the eigenvectors of the finite-dimensional matrix eigenvalue problem of Section

V_{2}\gg V_{1},V_{3}

To complete the counterexample, we now fix

sufficiently small, so that the subintervals

have uniform fixed widths, and ‘perturb’ the infinite potential well

such that it takes a very large and positive value off the finite union of mutually disjoint subintervals in

to the eigenfunctions of the ‘unperturbed’ infinite potential well eigenvalue problem. The perturbed eigenfunctions can thus be made arbitrarily close in

to the unperturbed eigenfunctions in this limit. In particular, the perturbed second eigenfunction inherits the sign change properties of the unperturbed second eigenfunction and so has more than one sign change.

Precisely, let

Central to showing

to the infinite well eigenfunctions
u_{i}
is an estimate that bounds the
L^{2}
norm of
w_{i,\delta}
on
U
by a higher
L^{p}
norm on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{U}
for
p
depending on
s
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
w_{i,\delta}
be as defined above and satisfy BVP (
5.3
). Then the following estimate

(5.5)

\displaystyle\|w_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(U)}\leq\tilde{C}\|w_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{U})}

holds for
p>\frac{1}{1-s}
and
s>1/2
, and
p>2
for
s\leq 1/2
.
\tilde{C}
is a large positive constant depending on
s
and
U
.

Proof.

To simplify notation, set
w:=w_{i,\delta}
and
u:=u_{i,\delta}
. For any
y
in
\mathbb{R}
, let
\delta(y):=dist(y,\partial U)=\inf\{|y-x|:x\in\partial U\}
.

From
[
1
, Th. 1.2.3]
, we know that the (unique) pointwise solution
w
of BVP (
5.3
) has a representation in terms of the
s
-harmonic extension of the boundary condition
u
into
U
given by

(5.6)

\displaystyle w(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{U}}\Gamma_{s}(x,y)u(y)dy=\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}\Gamma_{s}(x,y)u(y)dy,

where the second equality follows from
u=0
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus I
. Moreover, from
[
1
, Rm. 1.2.5]
, the integral kernel
\Gamma_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)
is bounded, and the upper bound can be simplified for the ensuing calculations: for
x\in U
and
y\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{U}
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{s}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\delta(x)^{s}}{\delta(y)^{s}(1+\delta(y))^{s}|x-y|}

(5.7)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\delta(x)^{s}}{\delta(y)^{s}|x-y|},

and the positive constant
C
depends on
s
and
U
.
From any point
x
in
U
we must pass through at least one boundary point to reach a point
y
in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{U}
, so we also have the following lower bounds on the distance between
x
and
y
:

(5.8)

\displaystyle|x-y|\geq|\delta(y)+\delta(x)|\geq\delta(x),\delta(y).

Using equation (
5.6
), inequality (
5
), Fubini’s theorem, and Hölder’s inequality, we can calculate an upper bound for
\|w\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2}
given by

\displaystyle\int_{U}|w(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}Q_{s}(y,z)u(y)u(z)dydz,\qquad x\in U

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}Q_{s}(y,z)^{q}dydz\right)^{1/q}\left(\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}|u(y)|^{p}|u(z)|^{p}dydz\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}Q_{s}(y,z)^{q}dydz\right)^{1/q}\left(\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}|u(y)|^{p}dy\right)^{2/p}

(5.9)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}Q_{s}(y,z)^{q}dydz\right)^{1/q}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{U})}

with integral kernel

(5.10)

\displaystyle Q_{s}(y,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{s}}\int_{U}\frac{\delta(x)^{2s}}{|x-y||x-z|}dx,\qquad y,z\in I\setminus\overline{U}.

To complete the
L^{2}
bound on
w
, we need to work out when
Q_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)^{q}
is integrable for different values of
s
and
q
.

For
s>1/2
, we calculate

\displaystyle Q_{s}(y,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{s}}\int_{U}\frac{\delta(x)^{2s}}{|x-y||x-z|}dx,\qquad y,z\in I\setminus\overline{U}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{s}}\int_{U}\frac{\delta(x)^{2s}}{\delta(x)\delta(x)}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{s}}\int_{U}\delta(x)^{2s-2}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{s}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{I_{i}}\delta(x)^{2s-2}dx

(5.11)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tilde{C}^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{s}},

where inequality (
5.8
) was applied in the second step. Since
\delta(x)
approaches zero linearly as
x
approaches an endpoint,
\delta(x)^{2s-2}
is integrable on
U
, i.e.,
s>1/2
, so
2s-2>-1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}\int_{\times I\setminus\overline{U}}Q_{s}(y,z)^{q}dydz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}\frac{\tilde{C}^{2q}}{\delta(y)^{sq}\delta(z)^{sq}}dydz

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{I\setminus\overline{U}}\frac{\tilde{C}^{q}}{\delta(y)^{sq}}dy\right)^{2}

(5.12)

\displaystyle<\infty,

as the final integral is integrable for
q<1/s
.

For
s\leq 1/2
, assume
R>0
is such that every pair of boundary points is separated by a distance of at least
2R
, including those for the same subinterval. There are four cases to consider:

Case 1:

\delta(y),\delta(z)\geq R
. In this case, for any
x
in
U
,
|z-x|\geq R
and
|y-x|\geq R
, so we can calculate

\displaystyle Q_{s}(y,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{s}}\int_{U}\frac{\delta(x)^{2s}}{|x-y||x-z|}dx,\qquad y,z\in I\setminus\overline{U}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{s}}\frac{1}{R^{2}}\int_{U}\delta(x)^{2s}dx

(5.13)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tilde{C}^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{2}},

as the final integral is integrable. Thus
Q_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)^{q}
is integable for
q<1/s
like for the case
s>1/2
.

Cases 2 and 3
:
\delta(y)<R
and
\delta(z)\geq R
(or vice versa). Here for any
x
in
U
,
|z-x|\geq R
, while
|y-x|\geq\delta(x)
, therefore we get

\displaystyle Q_{s}(y,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{s}}\int_{U}\frac{\delta(x)^{2s}}{|x-y||x-z|}dx,\qquad y,z\in I\setminus\overline{U}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{s}}\int_{U}\frac{\delta(x)^{2s}}{\delta(x)R}dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{s}}\frac{1}{R}\int_{U}\delta(x)^{2s-1}dx

(5.14)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tilde{C}^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{2}},

as
2s-1>-1
, so the final integral is integrable. Inequality (
5.8
) was also applied in the second line. It follows that
Q(\cdot,\cdot)^{q}
is integrable for
q<1/s
as before.

Case 4:

\delta(y),\delta(z)<R
. If
y
and
z
are close to distinct boundary points, the situation reduces to one of the above cases. To see this, suppose
y
and
z
are respectively close to distinct boundary points
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
belonging to the same subinterval
I_{i}
in
U
. Then for all
x
in
I_{i}
closer to
y
than to
z
, we have
|z-x|=|z-p_{2}|+|p_{2}-x|\geq|z-p_{2}|+R\geq R
, while
|y-x|\geq\delta(x)
(or vice versa). For all remaining points
x
in
U\setminus I_{i}
, both
|z-x|\geq R
and
|y-x|\geq R
. A similar argument applies if
y
and
z
are close to boundary points of distinct subintervals. So computing upper bounds for
Q_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
Q_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)^{q}
proceeds in the same fashion as previously and yields the same result.

The one situation remaining is when both
y
and
z
are close to the same boundary point
p
. In this case, we can partition
U
into disjoint subsets
W=B_{R}(p)\cap U
and its complement
V=U\setminus W
, where
B_{R}(p)
denotes the open ball of radius
R
centred at
p
.
Then for all
x
belonging to the complement
V
of
W
,
|y-x|=|y-p|+|p-x|\geq|y-p|+R>R
, and similarly,
|z-x|>R
, so we end up with the same
Q_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
Q_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)^{q}
bounds as in Case 1. Therefore we only need to consider what happens on
W
.

Without loss of generality (w.l.o.g), assume
p
is the left-hand side end point of a subinterval
I_{i}
in
U
. Then
W
can be further partitioned into the subintervals
(p,p+M)
and
[p+M,p+R)
, where
M=\max\{|y-p|,|z-p|\}
. We have the following inequalities:

\displaystyle|y-x||z-x|

\displaystyle\geq M|x-p|\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }(p,p+M)

\displaystyle|y-x||z-x|

\displaystyle\geq|x-p|^{2}\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }[p+M,p+R).

The first inequality follows from the fact that one of
|y-p|
or
|z-p|
takes the maximum value in
M
, so one of
|y-x|
or
|y-z|
is bounded below by M, while both
|y-x|
and
|z-x|
are bounded below by
|x-p|
. Using these inequalities, we calculate

(5.15)

\displaystyle\int_{W}\frac{\delta(x)^{2s}}{|y-x||z-x|}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{(p,p+M]}\frac{|x-p|^{2s-1}}{M}dx+\int_{[p+M,p+R)}|x-p|^{2s-2}dx.

When
s=1/2
, inequality (
5.15
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\int_{W}\frac{\delta(x)^{2s}}{|y-x||z-x|}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{(p,p+M]}\frac{1}{M}dx+\int_{[p+M,p+R)}\frac{1}{|x-p|}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\cdot M+\log|x-p|\Big|_{p+M}^{p+R}

(5.16)

\displaystyle=1+\log\left(\frac{R}{M}\right).

Whereas for
s<1/2
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{W}\frac{\delta(x)^{2s}}{|y-x||z-x|}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{(p,p+M]}\frac{|x-p|^{2s-1}}{M}dx+\int_{[p+M,p+R)}|x-p|^{2s-2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M}\frac{1}{2s}(x-p)^{2s}\Big|_{p}^{p+M}+\frac{1}{2s-1}(x-p)^{2s-1}\Big|_{p+M}^{p+R}

(5.17)

\displaystyle\leq C_{s}M^{2s-1},

where
C_{s}
is a positive constant depending on
s
. So in this case,
Q_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)
has the following upper bounds:

\displaystyle Q_{s}(y,z)

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}\frac{C^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{s}}\left(1+\log\left(\frac{R}{M}\right)\right),\qquad&s=1/2,\qquad y,z\in I\setminus\overline{U}\\
\frac{C^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{s}}\left(1+M^{2s-1}\right),\qquad&s<1/2,\qquad y,z\in I\setminus\overline{U}\end{cases}

(5.18)

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}\frac{C^{2}}{\delta(y)^{1/2}\delta(z)^{1/2}}\left(1+\sum_{p\in\partial U}\left|\log\max\{|z-p|,|y-p|\}\right|\right),\qquad&s=1/2\\
\frac{C^{2}}{\delta(y)^{s}\delta(z)^{s}}\left(1+\sum_{p\in\partial U}\max\{|y-p|,|z-p|\}^{2s-1}\right),\qquad&s<1/2.\end{cases}

On
A:=((I\setminus\overline{U}))\times(I\setminus\overline{U})))\setminus\left(\bigcup_{p\in\partial U}(B_{R}(p)\times B_{R}(p))\right)
, the terms in brackets are bounded. Thus, we find that
Q_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)^{q}
is integrable for
q<1/s
, just like in the previous cases.
On
B_{p}=\left((I\setminus\overline{U})\cap B_{R}(p)\right)\times\left((I\setminus\overline{U})\cap B_{R}(p)\right)
for each
p\in\partial U
, the integrability of
Q_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)^{q}
is as follows: For
s<1/2
, set
u:=|y-p|
and
v:=|z-p|
. The parameters
u
and
v
vary from
0
to
R
since
y
and
z
are both within a distance
R
from
p
. W.l.o.g assume
v=\max\{u,v\}
. We have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{p}}\frac{\max\{|y-p|,|y-p|\}^{(2s-1)q}}{u^{sq}v^{sq}}dydz

\displaystyle\leq C^{2q}\int_{0}^{R}\int_{0}^{R}\frac{\max\{u,v\}^{(2s-1)q}}{u^{sq}v^{sq}}dudv

\displaystyle\leq 2C^{2q}\int_{0}^{R}u^{-sq}\int_{u}^{R}\frac{v^{(2s-1)q}}{v^{sq}}dvdu

\displaystyle\leq 2C^{2q}\int_{0}^{R}u^{-sq}\int_{u}^{R}v^{q(s-1)}dvdu

\displaystyle\leq 2C^{2q}\int_{0}^{R}u^{-sq}\left(\frac{1}{q(s-1)+1}v^{q(s-1)+1}\Big|_{u}^{R}\right)du

\displaystyle=\frac{2C^{2q}}{q(s-1)+1}\int_{0}^{R}u^{-sq}\left(D_{q,s}-u^{q(s-1)+1}\right)du

\displaystyle=\frac{2C^{2q}}{q(s-1)+1}\int_{0}^{R}D_{q,s}u^{-sq}-u^{1-q}du

(5.19)

\displaystyle<\infty,

since the first term in the last integral is integrable for
q<1/s
and
s<1/2
, and the second term is integrable for
1-q>-1
or
q<2
.
D_{q,s}
is a sufficiently large positive constant depending on
q
and
s
. Thus, for
s<1/2
,
Q_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)^{q}
is integrable for
q<2
. For
s=1/2
, we can bound the log term by

\displaystyle|\log\max\{|y-p|,|z-p|\}|\leq C_{\epsilon}\max\{|y-p|,|z-p|\}^{-\epsilon},

where
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary and
C_{\epsilon}
is a positive constant depending on
\epsilon
. Arguing as before, we find that
Q_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)^{q}
is integrable when
-\epsilon q-\frac{q}{2}+1>-1
or
q<\frac{2}{\epsilon+\frac{1}{2}}
. However, since
\epsilon
is arbitrary, we conclude that integrabililty holds when
q<4
.

Substituting the upper bounds for
Q_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)^{q}
into inequality (
5
) yields an
L^{p}
bound for
\|w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}
. Thus we have

(5.20)

\displaystyle\|w\|_{L^{2}(U)}\leq\tilde{C}\|u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{U})}=\tilde{C}\|w\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{U})}

provided that
p>\frac{1}{1-s}
for
s>1/2
, and
p>2
for
s\leq 1/2
.
\tilde{C}
is a large positive constant depending on
s
and
U
.
∎

L^{2}
convergence of the finite potential well eigenfunctions
u_{i,\delta}
to the infinite potential well eigenfunctions
u_{i}
follows from an energy minimisation argument.
The energy functional associated with the operator in the infinite potential well eigenvalue problem (
5.1
) is

(5.21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[u]=\frac{c_{s}}{2}[u]^{2}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}+\int_{U}V(x)u(x)^{2}dx

for any function
u
in
H^{s}_{0}(U)
. Recall the semi-norm
[\cdot]_{H^{s}}
is given by equation (
2.3
) in Section
2
by taking
n=1
, i.e.,

(5.22)

\displaystyle[u]^{2}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{u(x)-u(y)}^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dxdy.

Similarly, the energy functional associated with the operator in the finite potential well eigenvalue problem (
5.2
) is

(5.23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\delta}[u]=\mathcal{E}[u]+\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{I\setminus U}u(x)^{2}dx=\mathcal{E}[u]+\frac{1}{\delta}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus U)}

for any function
u
in
H^{s}_{0}(I)
and
\delta>0
. Clearly
\mathcal{E}[u]=\mathcal{E}_{\delta}[u]
if
u
belongs to
H^{s}_{0}(U)
.

Using equations (
5.21
) and (
5.23
), can we define the variational characterisations of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
and
\lambda_{i,\delta}
: for
\delta>0
and
i=1,2,\ldots
,

(5.24)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}=\inf\{\sup\{\mathcal{E}[u]:u\in S,\|u\|_{L^{2}(U)}=1\}:\mbox{$S$ a subspace of $H^{s}_{0}(U)$ of dim $i$}\}

and

(5.25)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,\delta}=\inf\{\sup\{\mathcal{E}_{\delta}[u]:u\in S,\|u\|_{L^{2}(U)}=1\}:\mbox{$S$ a subspace of $H^{s}_{0}(I)$ of dim $i$}\}.

Since
H^{s}_{0}(U)\subset H^{s}_{0}(I)
,
\lambda_{i,\delta}
can be no greater than
\lambda_{i}
by definition.

Lemma 5.26
.

Fix
\delta>0
, and let
(u_{i},\lambda_{i})
and
(u_{i,\delta},\lambda_{i,\delta})
be
i
th eigenpairs satisfying eigenvalue problems (
5.1
) and (
5.2
), respectively. Then

(5.27)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,\delta}\leq\lambda_{i}.

Proof.

Applying equations (
5.24
) and (
5.25
) gives the result:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\inf\{\sup\{\mathcal{E}_{\delta}[u]:u\in S,\|u\|_{L^{2}(U)}=1\}:\mbox{$S$ a subspace of $H^{s}_{0}(U)$ of dim $i$}\}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}.

∎

A simple corrollary is that
u_{{}_{i,\delta}}
is small in
L^{2}
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{U}
.

Corollary 5.28
.

Fix
0<\delta\ll 1
, and let
u_{i,\delta}
be an eigenfunction of the
i
th eigenvalue
\lambda_{i,\delta}
of eigenvalue problem (
5.2
). Then

(5.29)

\displaystyle\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{U})}\leq C\delta^{1/2},

where
C
is a sufficiently large and positive constant.

Proof.

Starting with Lemma
5.26
, we calculate:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{i,\delta}

\displaystyle=[u_{i,\delta}]^{2}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}+\int_{U}V(x)u_{i,\delta}(x)^{2}dx+\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{I\setminus U}u_{i,\delta}(x)^{2}dx

\displaystyle\geq\int_{U}V(x)u_{i,\delta}(x)^{2}dx+\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{I\setminus U}u_{i,\delta}(x)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{U}V(x)u_{i,\delta}(x)^{2}dx+\frac{1}{\delta}\|u_{i,\delta}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus U)},

since
[u_{i,\delta}]^{2}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}>0
for any eigenfunction
u_{i,\delta}
. Rearranging for
\|u_{i,\delta}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus U)}
yields

\displaystyle\|u_{i,\delta}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus U)}

\displaystyle\leq\delta\left(\lambda_{i}-\int_{U}V(x)u_{i,\delta}(x)^{2}dx\right)

\displaystyle\leq\delta\left(\lambda_{i}+\|V\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(U)}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta\left(\lambda_{i}+C\right),

since
\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(U)}\leq 1
. Thus

(5.30)

\displaystyle\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{U})}\leq\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus U)}\leq\delta^{1/2}\tilde{C}

for a sufficiently large and positive constant
\tilde{C}
.
∎

Lemmas
5.4
and
5.26
and Corollary
5.28
lead to our main result.

Theorem 5.31
.

There exists a sequence of deltas
\delta
approaching zero such that
u_{i,\delta}
converges in
L^{2}
to
u_{i}
for each
i
, where
u_{i}
is an eigenfunction for eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
, and the sequence of eigenfunctions
\{u_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(U)
.

Proof.

Both
u_{i}
and
u_{i,\delta}
are zero off the interval
I=(-1,1)
, so the claim is trivial in this case. On the set
I\setminus U
,
u_{i}
is still zero by definition, while
\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{L(\mathbb{R}\setminus U)}\to 0
as
\delta\to 0^{+}
by Corollary
5.28
(noticing that on the boundary
\partial U
, the
L^{2}
norm of
u_{i,\delta}
is zero). This implies that
\|u_{i}-u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(I\setminus U)}=\|0-u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus U)}\to 0
as
\delta\to 0^{+}
.

The only case that remains to be proven is
L^{2}
convergence of
u_{i,\delta}
to
u_{i}
on
U
as
\delta\to 0^{+}
. We achieve this by induction. First, however, we require an eigenvalue inequality that we now derive. Using Lemma
5.26
; the energy equations (
5.21
) and (
5.23
); and recalling that
u_{i,\delta}=w_{i,\delta}+v_{i,\delta}
with
\langle v_{i,\delta},w_{i,\delta}\rangle_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}=0
, and therefore,
\langle v_{i,\delta},w_{i,\delta}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=0
, we calculate: for
0<\delta\ll 1
and
i=1,2,\ldots,

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{i,\delta}

\displaystyle=[u_{i,\delta}]^{2}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}+\int_{U}V(x)u_{i,\delta}(x)^{2}dx+\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{I\setminus U}u_{i,\delta}(x)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=[w_{i,\delta}+v_{i,\delta}]^{2}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}+\int_{U}V(x)\left(w_{i,\delta}+v_{i,\delta}\right)^{2}dx+\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{I\setminus U}u_{i,\delta}(x)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=[w_{i,\delta}]^{2}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}+[v_{i,\delta}]^{2}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}+\int_{U}V(x)w_{i,\delta}(x)^{2}dx

\displaystyle+2\int_{U}V(x)w_{i,\delta}(x)v_{i,\delta}(x)dx+\int_{U}V(x)v_{i,\delta}(x)^{2}dx+\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{I\setminus U}u_{i,\delta}(x)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}[v_{i,\delta}]+\mathcal{E}_{\delta}[w_{i,\delta}]+2\int_{U}V(x)w_{i,\delta}(x)v_{i,\delta}(x)dx

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{E}[v_{i,\delta}]+\mathcal{E}_{\delta}[w_{i,\delta}]+2\inf_{x\in U}\{V(x)\}\langle w_{i,\delta},v_{i,\delta}\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\mathcal{E}[\hat{v}_{i,\delta}]-\tilde{C}\delta\right)\|v_{i,\delta}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}+\mathcal{E}[w_{i,\delta}]+\frac{1}{\delta}\|w_{i,\delta}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus U)}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\mathcal{E}[\hat{v}_{i,\delta}]-\tilde{C}\delta\right)\|v_{i,\delta}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(U)},

where
\hat{v}_{i,\delta}=\frac{v_{i,\delta}}{\|v_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(U)}}
and
\tilde{C}
is a large and positive constant. So we have

(5.32)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{i,\delta}\geq\left(\mathcal{E}[\hat{v}_{i,\delta}]-\tilde{C}\delta\right)\|v_{i,\delta}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}.

Next, Lemma
5.4
and the fractional Sobolev-type inequalities in
[
4
, Ths. 6.7, 6.10, 8.2]
allow us to show that
\|w_{i,\delta}\|
can be made arbitrarily small. First note that
\Omega:=\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{U}
is an extension domain for
H^{s}(\Omega)

: it is a finite union of disjoint open intervals in
\mathbb{R}
with no common endpoints, which makes it a Lipschitz domain

[
3
, p. 9]
with bounded boundary. The latter is an extension domain as shown in
[
4
, Sec. 5]
.
When
s>1/2
, Theorem 8.2 in
[
4
]
shows that for
u_{i,\delta}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
,
u_{i,\delta}
is Hölder continuous with exponent
\frac{2s-1}{2}=s-1/2
. Thus,

(5.33)

\displaystyle\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{\infty}(I\setminus\overline{U})}<\infty.

It follows from Corollary
5.28
, the above
L^{\infty}
bound, and the Hölder interpolation inequality that

(5.34)

\displaystyle\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2/p}\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{1-2/p}\leq C\delta^{1/p}

for
p\in[2,\infty]
. If
s\leq 1/2
, Theorems 6.7 and 6.10 in
[
4
]
give that for
u_{i,\delta}\in H^{s}(\Omega)
,

(5.35)

\displaystyle\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq C\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}<\infty

provided that
q\in[2,\frac{2}{1-2s}]
and
s<1/2
, and
q\geq 2
for
s=1/2
.
C
is a positive constant depending on
s
and
\Omega
.
We again apply the Hölder interpolation inequality and Corollary
5.28
: For
s<1/2
, we have that
\frac{2}{1-2s}>2
, so we get

(5.36)

\displaystyle\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{i,\delta}\|^{\theta}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u_{i,\delta}\|^{1-\theta}_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq C\delta^{\frac{2-p(1-2s)}{2sp}},

where
\theta=\frac{2-p(1-2s)}{2sp}
,
q=\frac{2}{1-2s}
, and
p\in(2,\frac{2}{1-2s})
.
When
s=1/2
, we have

(5.37)

\displaystyle\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C\delta^{\frac{q-p}{p(q-2)}}

for
p\in(2,q]
and
q>2
.
Therefore, using Lemma
5.4
and the foregoing
L^{p}
bounds on
u_{i,\delta}
, we conclude

(5.38)

\displaystyle\|w_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(U)}\leq\tilde{C}\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{U})}\leq C^{\prime}\delta^{\rho},

where
\rho
is a positive exponent that is the smallest of the exponents in inequalities (
5.34
), (
5.36
), and (
5.37
) subject to their respective constraints.

Combining inequality (
5.38
) with the reverse triangle inequality, and remembering that
\|u_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(U)}=1
, gives

(5.39)

\displaystyle\|v_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(U)}=\|u_{i,\delta}-w_{i,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(U)}\geq 1-C^{\prime}\delta^{\rho}.

Substituting inequality (
5.39
) into the eigenvalue inequality (
5.32
), and relabelling the constants, finally produces

(5.40)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{i,\delta}\geq\left(\mathcal{E}[\hat{v}_{i,\delta}]-C\delta\right)\left(1-C\delta^{\rho}\right)^{2},

the inequality needed to complete the proof by induction.

For each
i=1,2,\ldots
, the proposition P(i) we want to prove is

(5.41)

\displaystyle\|u_{i,\delta}-u_{i}\|_{L^{2}(U)}\to 0\qquad\mbox{as}\qquad\delta\to 0^{+}.

Base case:
To prove the base case, set
i=1
and note that
\hat{v}_{1}
belongs to
H^{s}_{0}(U)\setminus\{u_{1}\}
. Notice that in the min-max (inf-sup) characterisation of the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
(equation (
5.24
)), since each dimension 1 subspace of
H^{s}_{0}(U)
is spanned by a single unit vector and every other vector is a multiple of this unit vector, maximising the energy over all unit vectors in a given dimension 1 subspace is the same as calculating the energy of the unit vector that spans the subspace. Hence the min-max (inf-sup) characterisation of
\lambda_{1}
reduces to minimising the energy over all unit vectors in
H^{s}_{0}(U)
, and the minimiser is the corresponding unit eigenvector
u_{1}
. It follows that any unit vector that is not
u_{1}
has an energy that is at least as large as
\lambda_{1}
. In particular,

(5.42)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[\hat{v}_{1,\delta}]\geq\lambda_{1}.

Combining inequality (
5.42
) with inequality (
5.40
) yields

(5.43)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{1,\delta}\geq\left(\lambda_{1}-C\delta\right)\left(1-C\delta^{\rho}\right)^{2},

from which, by comparing with inequality (
5.40
), we can deduce

(5.44)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\leq\mathcal{E}[\hat{v}_{1,\delta}]\leq\frac{\lambda_{1}}{(1-C\delta^{\rho})^{2}}+C\delta^{1/2}\leq\lambda_{1}+\tilde{C}\delta^{1/2}\to\lambda_{1}\mbox{ as }\delta\to 0^{+},

where
\tilde{C}
is a sufficiently large and positive constant.

Since the energy
\mathcal{E}[\hat{v}_{1,\delta}]
of
\hat{v}_{1,\delta}
can be made arbitrarily close to
\lambda_{1}
, it follows that
\hat{v}_{1,\delta}
can be made arbitrarily close to
u_{1}
in
L^{2}(U)
as
\delta
approaches zero. To see this, we use the variational definition of the energy
\mathcal{E}[\cdot]
in terms of the Rayleigh quotient and the spectral decomposition of
\hat{v}_{1,\delta}
in
L^{2}(U)
with respect to the orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions
\{u_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
of the infinite potential well eigenvalue problem (
5.1
) to compute:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[\hat{v}_{1,\delta}]

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},\left((-\Delta)^{s}+V\right)\hat{v}_{1,\delta}\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},\left((-\Delta)^{s}+V\right)\sum_{k\geq 1}\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}u_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},\sum_{k\geq 1}\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}\left((-\Delta)^{s}+V\right)u_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},\sum_{k\geq 1}\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}\lambda_{k}u_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\lambda_{k}\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{k}\rangle^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{1}\rangle^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}+\sum_{k>1}\lambda_{k}\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{k}\rangle^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\sum_{k\geq 1}\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{1}\rangle^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}-\lambda_{1}\sum_{k>1}\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{1}\rangle^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}+\sum_{k>1}\lambda_{k}\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{k}\rangle^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}+\sum_{k>1}(\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{1})\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{k}\rangle^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{1}+(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})\sum_{k>1}\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{k}\rangle^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}.

Combined with the upper bound in inequality (
5.44
), yields

(5.45)

\displaystyle\sum_{k>1}\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}\leq\frac{\tilde{C}\delta^{1/2}}{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}}=C\delta^{1/2},

where
C
is a sufficiently large constant. The left-hand side (LHS) of inequality (
5.45
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\sum_{k>1}\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{k}\rangle^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{k}\rangle^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}-\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{1}\rangle^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},\hat{v}_{1,\delta}\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}-\langle\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}u_{1},\hat{v}_{1,\delta}\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta}-\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}u_{1},\hat{v}_{1,\delta}\rangle_{L^{2}(U)},

so that

(5.46)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta}-\langle\hat{v}_{1,\delta},u_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}u_{1},\hat{v}_{1,\delta}\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}\leq C\delta^{1/2}.

The first term in the inner product on the LHS of inequality (
5.46
) is the vector component of
\hat{v}_{1,\delta}
perpendicular to the unit eigenvector
u_{1}
, so the LHS of inequality (
5.46
) is its scalar projection onto the unit vector
\hat{v}_{1,\delta}
in
L^{2}(U)
. As
\delta
approaches zero,
\hat{v}_{1,\delta}
converges in
L^{2}(U)
to
u_{1}
. Thus we conclude

(5.47)

\displaystyle\|\hat{v}_{1,\delta}-u_{1}\|_{L^{2}(U)}\leq C\delta^{1/4}

for
0<\delta\ll 1
; the fractional exponent on the right-hand side is effectively arbitrary.

As
u_{i,\delta}=v_{i,\delta}+w_{i,\delta}
, where
v_{i,\delta}
is the orthogonal projection of
u_{i,\delta}
onto
H^{s}_{0}(U)
, and
\|u_{1,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(U)}=1
, it follows that
\|v_{1,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(U)}\leq 1
. Together with inequality (
5.39
), we have

(5.48)

\displaystyle 1-C\delta^{\rho}\leq\|v_{1,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(U)}\leq 1.

Thus, as
\delta\to 0^{+}
,
v_{1,\delta}
converges to
\hat{v}_{1,\delta}
, which converges to
u_{1}
in
L^{2}(U)
. This implies that
v_{1,\delta}
also converges to
u_{1}
in
L^{2}(U)
:

\displaystyle\|v_{1,\delta}-u_{1}\|_{L^{2}(U)}

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{1,\delta}-\hat{v}_{1,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(U)}+\|\hat{v}_{1,\delta}-u_{1}\|_{L^{2}(U)}

(5.49)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|v_{1,\delta}-\frac{v_{1,\delta}}{\|v_{1,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(U)}}\right\|_{L^{2}(U)}+C\delta^{1/4}\to 0

as
\delta\to 0^{+}
. Inequalities (
5.47
) and (
5.48
) were used in the second line.

Finally, recalling that
u_{1,\delta}=v_{1,\delta}+w_{1,\delta}
and applying
inequalities (
5.38
) and (
5
) to the second and third terms, respectively, in the triangle inequality below, we have

(5.50)

\displaystyle\|u_{1,\delta}-u_{1}\|_{L^{2}(U)}\leq\|w_{1,\delta}\|_{L^{2}(U)}+\|v_{1,\delta}-u_{1}\|_{L^{2}(U)}\leq C\delta^{1/2}\to 0\mbox{ as }\delta\to 0^{+}.

Thus P(1) is true.

Induction step:
Suppose that as
\delta\to 0^{+}
,
\|u_{i,\delta}-u_{i}\|_{L^{2}(u)}\to 0
for each
i=1,2,\ldots,k
. Then the foregoing argument, specifically equations (
5.47
) and (
5.44
) with the index
1
replaced by
i
in
\{1,\ldots,k\}
, shows that each
\hat{v}_{i,\delta}
is almost orthogonal to
u_{j}
, for each
j\neq i
, with energy slightly above
\lambda_{k}
. This implies that
\hat{v}_{k+1,\delta}
is almost orthogonal to
\operatorname{span}\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}
and has an energy that is just above
\lambda_{k+1}
.
Thus, arguing as above,
\hat{v}_{k+1,\delta}
can be made arbitrarily close to
u_{k+1}
in
L^{2}(U)
. It follows from an analogous calculation as shown in inequality (
5.50
)
that
u_{k+1,\delta}
converges in
L^{2}(U)
to
u_{k+1}
as
\delta\to 0^{+}
. Thus P(k+1) is true.

Consequently, P(i) holds for every
i=1,2,\ldots
, which proves the claim.
∎

To complete the counterexample, let
U
consist of three subintervals, and choose the bounded smooth potential
V
on
U
to be the same one as in the infinite potential well problem of Section
4
; i.e.,
V
takes a constant value
V_{i}
on
I_{i}
, for
i=1,2,3
, with
V_{2}\gg V_{1},V_{3}
, so that we have a bounded triple well potential on
I
in eigenvalue problem (
5.2
). See Figure
5
. Recall that the first three eigenvalues are simple in this specific infinite potential well situation, as shown in Section
4
. By Theorem
5.31
, as
\delta
approaches zero, an eigenfunction
u_{i,\delta}
for eigenvalue
\lambda_{i,\delta}
of the finite potential well problem (
5.2
) converges in
L^{2}
to an eigenfunction
u_{i}
of eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
of the infinite potential well problem (
5.1
).
It therefore follows that for
s=1/2
(more generally, for rational values of
s
in
(0,1)
) and
\delta>0
sufficiently small, an eigenfunction
u_{2,\delta}
corresponding to the second eigenvalue
\lambda_{2,\delta}
of the finite potential well eigenvalue problem is very close in
L^{2}
to the second eigenfunction
u_{2}
of the infinite potential well eigenvalue problem. This implies
u_{2,\delta}
has more than one sign change, as
u_{2}
does.

6.
Discussion and conclusion

The detailed regular perturbation computations in Section
4.2.3
of this paper have focused on the specific case of
s=1/2
(the Cauchy process) for illustrative purposes. Our three-step approach to constructing the counterexample—reducing the problem to a matrix eigenvalue problem (Section
3
), applying perturbation theory to an idealised infinite potential well (Section
4
), and extending the counterexample to a finite potential well via energy minimisation (Section
5
)—however, appears structurally sound for a general rational
s
. One would only need to recalculate the first non-trivial correction for each different rational
s
, using a similar method to that detailed in Section
4.2.3
for
s=1/2
, while the remainder of the technical framework would remain unchanged.

The challenge of extending the counterexample to irrational
s
remains an interesting open question. It would likely require a different approach to the perturbation analysis for the infinite well, since the restriction to rational
s
arises from satisfying the analyticity condition in the Kato-Rellich perturbation theory. Whether the nodal properties (at least for the second eigenfunction) of the unperturbed nonlocal Dirichlet problem are preserved under a perturbation with a suitable convex potential is also a problem worth exploring.
