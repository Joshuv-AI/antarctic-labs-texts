Non-IID and aware federated intrusion detection with PBFT with secured model aggregation for multi institutional healthcare internet of things networks
Authors: Sudhakar Sengan, Chin-Shiuh Shieh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multi-institutional healthcare Internet of Things (IoT) networks face a core challenge between combined intrusion detection and patient data privacy. Raw traffic records cannot be shared across institutional boundaries, yet local models trained on institution-specific data alone generalize poorly to attack distributions that differ from those practical in real healthcare IoT deployments. Federated Learning (FL) addresses privacy constraints by storing data locally at each institution, but it introduces statistical heterogeneity across institutions. Local data at each institution are non-independent and non-identically distributed due to clinical specialization, protocol diversity, and deployment-scale asymmetry. Standard federated averaging cannot handle this non-IID condition, and detection performance lowers significantly as a result. Existing Federated Intrusion Detection Systems (FIDS) implied that all participating institutions were honest. But this hypothesis cannot hold in real multi-institutional consortia, because Byzantine participants can corrupt the global model by submitting manipulated gradient updates. In this work, a Non-IID-Aware Federated Intrusion Detection System (N-IID-AFIDS) is proposed for multi-institutional healthcare IoT networks and is designed to address both challenges simultaneously. A cluster-weighted aggregation mechanism is used in this N-IID-AFIDS, grouping institutions by distributional similarity through spectral clustering of a Wasserstein-based affinity matrix and applying gradient divergence correction to submitted updates before aggregation. Protocol-aware Deep Sparse Autoencoder (DSAE) adaptation is also part of this model, and it uses local feature normalization based on an institutional protocol mixture and a distribution alignment regularizer. This regularizer operates without raw data exchange across institutions. This work extends practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT) consensus from event logging for detection to model update validation. Geometric median-based Byzantine filtering, together with reputation-based participation control, is also added to this model. The proposed model is evaluated on the IoT-Flock and CICIoT2023 datasets across three non-IID severity levels, based on combined skew in quantity, labels, and features. It achieved non-IID detection accuracies of 93.17% on IoT-Flock and 89.84% on CICIoT2023. And this model is the only federated method in the comparison that reports non-IID performance on both datasets simultaneously. It also retains 83.61% accuracy with four Byzantine participants and meets a 16 ms clinical real-time detection constraint, converging in 29–67 rounds, faster than other federated baselines.

Introduction

The rapid development of Internet of Things (IoT) devices in healthcare has changed how patient monitoring, Clinical Decision-Making System (CDSS), and medical data exchange are carried out. Hospitals and care centers now connect wearable sensors, infusion pumps, imaging systems, and remote monitoring platforms1. All these devices uninterruptedly generate sensitive patient data using heterogeneous protocols. This connectivity has improved care delivery and operational efficiency. But at the same time, it makes the attack region of connected systems much larger2. Attacks such as Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS), Man-in-the-Middle (MiTM) interception, and Brute-Force Attacks (BFA) exploit vulnerabilities in connected healthcare systems (HCS)3. It is also found that conventional signature-based Intrusion Detection Systems (IDSs) cannot detect these attacks in real time because they are constantly evolving and growing in scale4.

Deep learning (DL)-based IDS has achieved better results than static rule-based methods. This is because they can learn complex temporal and structural attack patterns that are beyond the scope of static rule-based methods5. However, most existing DL-based IDS assume that multiple institutions can share training data centrally. However, this hypothesis does not align with HCS’s actual performance. Regulations such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) have restricted the sharing of real network traffic across organizational limits in HCS settings6. Therefore, healthcare institutions cannot share traffic logs directly for combined model training because doing so would breach privacy and compliance requirements7.

Federated Learning (FL) has addressed data-sharing limitations by enabling institutions to collaboratively train models, with only local updates transmitted for aggregation rather than raw data. Further, FL has been commonly used in HCS. But it is found that its application to healthcare IoT-IDS brings a critical challenge of statistical heterogeneity across institutions8. Network traffic varied significantly across services because large hospitals face a wider range of attack types, unlike a focused health center, which encounters only limited device-specific attacks. This non-IID nature is what has caused client drift in federated training. Each institution’s local updates diverge from the global objective, leading to reduced model accuracy under standard aggregation9. Existing mitigation methods, such as proximal regularization and control variates, had only provided partial solutions. And these methods still cannot handle heterogeneity arising from institutional diversity, protocol differences, and scale changes10.

The current federated IDS has not prioritized the security of the aggregation method itself. These IDs operate on the hypothesis that all users are honest. But in multi-institutional settings, this is not realistic because compromised or malicious clients can submit adversarial updates11. When such updates are not properly validated, the global model becomes corrupted, affecting detection reliability. Blockchain (BC)-based systems have enabled secure aggregation by leveraging unchangeable ledgers and Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus12. But existing methods lacked such trust models, or they relied on consensus protocols that introduce latency incompatible with real-time IDS requirements13.

In this study, a Non-IID-Aware Federated Intrusion Detection System (N-IID-AFIDS) is proposed to address gaps identified in the existing federated IDS literature. This N-IID-AFIDS is built on cluster-weighted model aggregation, Byzantine-resilient gradient filtering, and PBFT-secured BC validation, serving as its core components for multi-institutional healthcare IoT security. This model adopted a centralized hybrid BC and DL system from earlier work16 and moved it to a fully federated setting. Deep Sparse Autoencoder (DSAE)-based Feature Extraction (FE), a Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) temporal backbone, attack-specific expert modules, and Bayesian Product-of-Experts (PoE) fusion from the earlier network are retained in this model. In addition, federated adaptation is introduced in this research study to address the non-IID challenge and the aggregation security problem simultaneously.

Literature review

Early work on FL for IoT intrusion detection had focused mainly on privacy preservation and secure collaboration between institutions. Begum et al.,17, Govindaram and Jegatheesan31, and Nandanwar and Katarya32 used BC in their work, and the model exchange in these systems is auditable and tamper-resistant across distributed IoT environments. But these methods mainly focus on trust and data privacy. These methods also assume homogeneous data distributions across institutions, failing to account for statistical heterogeneity.

Later studies had shifted their attention toward improving model performance and convergence in federated IoT settings. In their study, Abd Elaziz et al.,33 used a tab-transformer in combination with optimization methods for IDS. Albanbay,34 and Khraisat et al.,35 report performance instability under heterogeneous data and limitations of standard aggregation methods. Other works also introduced improvements such as feature selection (FS)36, fog-assisted architectures37, real-time IDS38, and adaptive privacy-preserving methods39. But all of these contributions had focused mainly on efficiency, scalability, and privacy. It is found that none of these works directly handle non-IID data distributions in federated IoT environments.

Naz,43 integrated a convolutional neural network (CNN) and a bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM) with self-attention for intrusion detection. They achieved 99.93% on the Network Security Laboratory Knowledge Discovery in Databases (NSL-KDD), but this method is centralized, and non-IID federated deployment was not measured.

Zhang et al.,42 suggested multidimensional feature fusion with a stacking ensemble across time, space, and load-based traffic features across multiple benchmark datasets, but distributional heterogeneity across institutions is not handled. Li et al.,9 had applied Reinforcement Learning (RL) for energy-efficient anti-jamming routing in UAV networks under adversarial conditions, but a federated IDS setting for healthcare IoT is not addressed in this work.

Statistical heterogeneity, or non-IID data, has become a serious problem in FL research. In their study, Ilias et al.,41 found that non-IID conditions lead to worse convergence and detection accuracy in federated IDS. It is found that existing methods have mainly focused on single features, such as label skew, and have not measured the combined effects of quantity and feature, together with protocol-level variability, which is typical in healthcare IoT.

Naz,45 invented an Adaptive Federated Learning Framework for Secure and Privacy-Preserving Network Traffic Analysis (AFL-SecNet) that addresses non-IID federated network traffic analysis via adaptive local training and a two-trapdoor cryptosystem for secure aggregation, achieving 97.58% on the University of New South Wales - Network Behavior 2015 (UNSW-NB15) dataset. But multi-institutional healthcare IoT deployment with Byzantine-resilient aggregation and combined quantity, label, and feature skew are not modeled in this model.

The research studies are also pointing to the same gaps in the literature. Makris,40 had reported that aggregation robustness, heterogeneity, and scalability are among the key challenges in federated IDs. And Buyuktanir et al., (2024) had also stressed that adaptive aggregation is required for non-IID settings. Hassan et al., (2025) and Pinto et al., (2025) note that BC-based trust integration is nearly what is required for federated IDS. It is found that current models handle privacy, robustness, and trust as separate problems rather than as a unified system.

Even after prior developments, a gap that remains in this field is that no existing work has jointly addressed multi-dimensional non-IID heterogeneity arising from institutional, protocol, and scale differences, together with Byzantine-resilient aggregation in adversarial settings. Existing federated IDs either focus solely on improving learning under benign conditions or do not address other critical features. It is found that BC in these models is used only for logging and access control, and the aggregation method is not secured at all. In this work, a unified model is proposed that combines cluster-weighted non-IID-aware aggregation, gradient divergence correction, and Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT)-based model validation for deployment in multi-institutional healthcare IoT environments.

System model and problem formulation

Multi-institutional healthcare IoT network model

Healthcare IoT deployments in this study span multiple autonomous institutions, including hospitals, specialty care centers, diagnostic laboratories, and regional health networks. Each of these institutions operates different IoT devices and operates under strict administrative and privacy limits set by its settings. The network in this model was designed around three layers present across this distributed setting. These layers include the Medical Internet of Things (MIoT) perception layer, institution-level edge-fog processing, and the consortium BC layer used for coordination between institutions.

Threat and attack taxonomy

The threat model in this work considered two types of adversaries: external attackers targeting the healthcare IoT setup and Byzantine participants aiming to disrupt it.

External adversaries operate on three levels. Network-level attacks involve Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS) floods that disrupt communication between MIoT devices and fog nodes. Session-level attacks are carried out through Man-in-the-Middle (MiTM) interception, exploiting weaknesses in encryption or certificate validation. Authentication-level attacks exploit BFA to target device interfaces. All these attack types are encoded in the label space \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{i}\in\:\left\{\mathrm{0,1},\mathrm{2,3}\right\}$$\end{document} and 0 is for benign traffic, while 1–3 are attack types. Byzantine anomalies in this model are treated as a subset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:B\subset\:K$$\end{document} with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mid\:B\mid\:\le\:f<K/3$$\end{document}. The result is consistent with the PBFT fault-tolerance limits set for the federation. These compromised institutions are sending manipulated updates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\stackrel{\sim}{\theta\:}}_{k,t}$$\end{document} to the global model. The aim is to degrade global performance and induce targeted misclassification. So the aggregation protocol had to be robust against these adversaries while maintaining detection accuracy for honest participants.

Label skew modelling across institutional node types

Proposed federated IDS

Overall workflow of proposed model

The proposed model had combined FL and BC-secured aggregation within a layered network, keeping local detection and global coordination separate. This model extends the prior hybrid BC + DL model16 into a multi-institutional setting. The local pipeline at each institution runs independently and is built with a DSAE-based FE, a BiLSTM-based temporal backbone with expert modules, and Bayesian fusion. Two new features introduced in this model are a non-IID-aware aggregation mechanism and a PBFT-secured model update protocol. Understanding of inter-layer data flow is also important in this model. And this is because all components operate within a tightly coupled pipeline—the model’s overall workflow (Fig. 1).

Local detection engine

The latent embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Z}_{k}$$\end{document} from Eq. The engine comprises four parallel components: a BiLSTM temporal backbone and three focused expert modules for DDoS, MiTM, and Brute-Force Attacks (BFA), with their outputs integrated via a Bayesian PoE fusion layer.

PBFT-secured model aggregation protocol

The cluster-weighted aggregation in Sect. 5.4 accepts honest updates, but under the threat model in Sect. 3.4, Byzantine institutions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:B\subset\:K$$\end{document} may submit manipulated updates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widetilde{\Delta }_{{k,t}}$$\end{document} to degrade accuracy or induce targeted misclassification. Without validation, such updates can corrupt the global model and violate the performance objective in Eq. (9). This work extends prior work16 to secure the aggregation process itself, requiring consortium-wide Byzantine-fault-tolerant validation of each update before it is included in cluster aggregation (Eq. Datasets, experimental setup and implementation

Dataset description

Two publicly available datasets are used for federated evaluation, and their statistical characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

IoT healthcare security dataset (IoT-Flock)

The IoT-Flock dataset14 is directly relevant to MIoT deployment environments and provides a realistic simulation of clinical device communication behavior. The dataset was generated to represent an intensive care unit setting with two patient beds, each with nine dedicated monitoring sensors and a separate bed control unit. Traffic flows from this simulation match the communication behaviors and protocol features of real HCS-IoT deployments and also include periodic, low-latency telemetry patterns specific to medical device traffic rather than those of general IoT environments.

Normal and malicious traffic flows are generated using the IoT-Flock traffic generation model. This model supports modern healthcare IoT protocols such as Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) and Constrained Application Protocol (CoAP). Benign traffic covers environmental reporting, medication-delivery status updates, and continuous health-metric monitoring streams. Attack traffic encompasses malicious attempts to compromise authentication systems, disrupt communication protocol integrity, and inject data across device channels. The dataset is distributed in Packet Capture (PCAP) format and is converted to CSV-based FS using pcap2csv conversion scripts. And this conversion produces flow-level records with protocol-defined features and healthcare-specific metadata. This dataset contains 1,347 labeled attack events and 4,231 benign tests across 3 attack classes: DDoS, MiTM, and BFA.

CICIoT2023

CICIoT202315 was released by the Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity and is derived from a large-scale practical testbed comprising 105 real IoT devices spanning multiple device categories and application domains.

The dataset included 33 distinct attack types grouped into 7 types, including reconnaissance and detection evasion, web-based exploitation, BFA, and spoofing, as well as 2 variants of Mirai traffic generation. This dataset included tools such as Nmap for network reconnaissance, Hping3 for volumetric flooding, and Metasploit for multi-stage exploitation. Each network flow record has 46 FEs that cover packet-level descriptors, flow-level statistics, and behavioral indicators. Ground-truth labels in this dataset support binary anomaly detection (AD), and multi-class attack classification, together with attack-type-specific evaluation tasks. This dataset has more than 47 million labeled flow records. Both raw network captures and pre-processed FS versions of this dataset are publicly available. This study uses the FS version, which maintains an input space of 46 dimensions across all test cases.

Since the proposed model is built around 3 healthcare IoT attack classes, a label mapping procedure is applied to the 33 original CICIoT2023 attack types before partitioning. Volumetric flooding variants, such as UDP flood, ICMP flood, SYN flood, and HTTP flood, as well as Slowloris, are mapped to the DDoS class. Spoofing variants, such as ARP and DNS spoofing, are mapped to the MiTM class because these attacks operate through interception mechanisms that are consistent with MiTM behavior in healthcare IoT protocols. BFA is mapped directly to the BFA. Reconnaissance and web-based exploitation, along with Mirai-based botnet types, do not represent attack types present in the simulated HCS-IoT deployment, and these flows are excluded. This mapping generates the test partition reported in Table 1, comprising 4,892 attack records and 8,756 benign records across the 3 target classes.

The federated evaluation requires a simulated multi-institutional network whose node configuration reflects the heterogeneous administrative network of real healthcare IoT consortia. These nodes have narrower device types and higher wireless device density, leading to greater MiTM exposure than other node types. The non-IID partitioning method instantiates the formal models derived in Sect. 4 into a concrete data distribution across the 12 simulated nodes. All partitioning operations use a fixed random seed of 42, consistently applied across Python’s random module, NumPy, and the Flower network, ensuring full reproducibility across experimental runs. Baseline methods for comparison

Six baseline methods are selected for performance comparison, spanning centralized DL, standard federated averaging, and heterogeneity-aware federated variants.

The centralized hybrid BC + DL16 serves as the upper-bound reference, trained on pooled data from all institutions without federated constraints. Standard FedAvg serves as the foundational federated baseline, isolating the contribution of the non-IID-aware cluster-weighted mechanism over naive data-proportional averaging. FedProx extends FedAvg with the proximal regularization but without cluster-weighted aggregation. SCAFFOLD, a variance-reduction FL applying control variates to correct client drift, represents the current state-of-the-art (SOTA) in non-IID-FL. FedNova, a normalized averaging method that accounts for heterogeneous local update magnitudes, is included as a comparison with the cluster-weighting method. A federated variant network without PBFT-secured aggregation is included to isolate the definite contribution of the Byzantine-resilient gradient filtering.

Hardware and software implementation details

Hardware and software configuration

Training and aggregation experiments are run on an NVIDIA DGX-A100 server with 8 × A100 GPUs with 80 GB of memory. Edge-level detection latency is measured on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin, simulating the perception-layer deployment environment. Each of the 12 federated nodes is simulated as a separate process on the DGX-A100, with inter-process communication emulating the network transmission overhead of a real distributed deployment. The complete software stack comprises PyTorch 2.1.0 with CUDA 12.1 for DL components, Flower (flwr 1.6.0) for FL setup, and Hyperledger Fabric 2.5.4 with PBFT consensus for BC simulation. SC logic is implemented in the Go chaincode.

Accuracy, communication, and security trade-off analysis

The results across Sect. 7.1–7.6 indicate that the proposed model achieves a favourable trade-off among 3 competing objectives in federated healthcare IoT deployments. From an accuracy perspective, the proposed model achieves 90.83% and 86.47% under combined non-IID conditions on IoT-Flock and CICIoT2023, which are 18.45% and 17.54% higher than FedAvg under the same conditions. From a communication and convergence perspective, the proposed model converges 29 to 67 rounds faster than baselines at moderate to severe heterogeneity levels, as reported in Table 9, which directly reduces total communication rounds and associated transmission cost despite the additional metadata vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{O}(C+2)$$\end{document} per institution per round introduced by the BC transaction wrapper. From a security perspective, PBFT-secured aggregation achieves 87.83% and 83.61% accuracy against four Byzantine users, as reported in Table 11, compared with 71.34% and 67.83% without PBFT filtering — a protection gain of 16.49% and 15.78%. The CL overhead of 234.7 to 467.2 ms reported in Table 10 applies to the aggregation tier only and does not affect the 16 ms edge-level detection latency that meets the clinical real-time constraint. So the proposed model does not require sacrificing any of these 3 objectives to achieve gains in the others-improved non-IID detection accuracy, reduced communication rounds, and Byzantine resilience are achieved together within the same federated model.

Conclusion and future work

This research work introduced N-IID-AFIDS, which handles statistical heterogeneity and adversarial aggregation corruption in multi-institutional healthcare IoT networks. This model has 4 contributions that differ from those of existing federated IDS. First, a formal non-IID characterization covers quantity, label, and feature skew and provides a basis for testing aggregation mechanisms across controlled levels of heterogeneity. Second, federated adaptations of DSAE, such as protocol-aware normalization and Wasserstein-based distribution alignment regularization, address embedding inconsistency arising from protocol-stack diversity across institutional nodes without requiring raw data exchange. Third, the cluster-weighted aggregation mechanism with gradient divergence correction reduces convergence error due to distributional divergence, achieving 93.17% and 89.84% non-IID detection accuracy on IoT-Flock and CICIoT2023 under combined skew, whereas standard federated averaging achieves 72.38% and 68.93%. Fourth, the PBFT-secured model update validation with geometric median Byzantine filtering and reputation-based participation control maintains 83.61% detection accuracy with 4 adversarial participants, whereas the insecure federation achieves 67.83% under the same conditions. The model also meets the 16 ms clinical real-time detection constraint across both datasets and converges 29–67 rounds faster than other baselines under moderate to severe heterogeneity.

This work had several limitations worth noting. Federated evaluation in this study is based on simulated institutional nodes derived from public datasets, rather than from real distributed deployments. The generalization gap of 8.47% between the IoT-Flock and CICIoT2023 transfer directions indicates that cross-domain performance remains an open challenge. A DQN-based MiTM agent requests simulation-based training, which limits adaptation under strict healthcare privacy constraints. The PBFT CL of 467.2 ms at peak load needs more optimization for time-critical clinical alert scenarios.

Future work in this area will examine continual learning mechanisms for evolving attack adaptation, homomorphic encryption for zero-knowledge SC execution, and dynamic PBFT sharding for throughput scaling during high-frequency multi-institutional attack surges.
