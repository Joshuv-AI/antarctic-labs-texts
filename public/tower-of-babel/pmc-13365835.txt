Spontaneous bilateral renal subcapsular hematoma presenting with reversible Page kidney hypertension: a case report
Authors: Zhaoxing Zhan, Qingrui Li, Nueraili Paierhati, Xinsheng Zhu, Tuo Zhou, Jianhao Sun, Nueraihemaiti Yimingniyizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spontaneous bilateral renal subcapsular hematoma (SRSH) is a rare condition, especially in young individuals. We report a 25-year-old Asian female presenting with acute bilateral flank pain and new-onset hypertension. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed bilateral SRSH without an identifiable cause. The hypertension, consistent with the Page kidney phenomenon, resolved within five days under conservative management. Follow-up imaging confirmed complete hematoma resolution. This case highlights SRSH as an important differential diagnosis for acute flank pain and demonstrates that Page kidney-related hypertension can be reversible, supporting conservative management in hemodynamically stable patients after exclusion of malignancy.

Introduction

Spontaneous renal subcapsular hematoma (SRSH), a hallmark of Wunderlich syndrome, is an uncommon cause of acute flank pain, most frequently associated with underlying renal neoplasms or vascular disorders.1,2 While unilateral SRSH is the norm, bilateral spontaneous occurrence is exceedingly rare and typically arises in the context of predisposing conditions such as anticoagulation therapy or systemic vasculitis.3,4 A clinically significant complication of SRSH is the Page kidney phenomenon, where external compression of the renal parenchyma activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), leading to secondary hypertension.5,6 The reversibility of this hypertension following conservative management offers important pathophysiological insights. Herein, we report a unique case of spontaneous bilateral SRSH in a young, healthy female who presented with acute flank pain and transient Page kidney-mediated hypertension, which resolved completely with conservative treatment.

Case presentation

A 25-year-old Asian female with no significant medical history presented with a four-day history of sequential bilateral flank pain. Physical examination revealed hypertension (165/104 mmHg) and left renal angle tenderness. Laboratory investigations demonstrated mild anemia (hemoglobin 106 g/L) with a normal coagulation profile and urinalysis. Hepatitis B surface antigen and HIV antibody testing were both negative.

The patient initially underwent an appendiceal ultrasound in the general surgery department, which was unremarkable. An initial non-contrast CT scan of the abdomen demonstrated an enlarged right kidney with subcapsular collections showing mixed high- and low-density areas (Fig. 1a). To further characterize the collections and exclude underlying lesions, a contrast-enhanced CT scan was performed. This revealed bilateral crescent-shaped subcapsular collections compressing the renal parenchyma. The collections showed no significant contrast enhancement, with attenuation values ranging from 11 to 55 Hounsfield Units (HU), consistent with subcapsular hematoma at different stages of evolution (Fig. 1b). Critically, no enhancing solid masses, fat-density lesions suggestive of angiomyolipoma, vascular malformations, active contrast extravasation, or renal artery aneurysms were identified. The patient was managed conservatively with absolute bed rest and intravenous tranexamic acid. No antihypertensive medications were administered during the hospitalization. The decision to withhold antihypertensives was deliberate based on the following: (1) the patient was hemodynamically stable with no evidence of hypertensive emergency (no headache, visual disturbance, dyspnea, chest pain, or end-organ damage); (2) the hypertension was suspected to be secondary to the Page kidney phenomenon, and we wished to observe its natural history; (3) given the subacute appearance of the hematoma with no active extravasation, we hypothesized that blood pressure would normalize spontaneously as the compressive stimulus diminished. Had the hypertension persisted or worsened, or had the patient developed symptoms, antihypertensive therapy (likely a calcium channel blocker or ACE inhibitor) would have been initiated. Her blood pressure normalized to 120/80 mmHg by day five without antihypertensive medication, consistent with the resolution of a transient Page kidney effect.

A follow-up CT scan performed six weeks after discharge demonstrated a significant reduction in the size and thickness of the bilateral subcapsular collections, confirming near-complete resolution of the hematomas (Fig. 2). Discussion

We present a rare case of spontaneous bilateral renal subcapsular hematoma in a young, healthy woman with no identifiable etiology, who developed transient Page kidney-mediated hypertension that resolved with conservative management.

Neoplasms account for up to 65% of SRSH cases, with vascular diseases representing the second most common cause.1,2 However, bilateral SRSH is distinctly unusual. A review of the literature shows that most bilateral cases occur in patients with identifiable predisposing conditions, such as hemodialysis-associated coagulopathy or polyarteritis nodosa.3,4,7 To the best of our knowledge, the combination of (1) spontaneous bilateral occurrence, (2) in a completely healthy young adult with no identifiable predisposing factors, (3) with clearly documented reversible Page kidney physiology, and (4) successful conservative management represents an exceedingly rare constellation that has not been well-characterized in the literature. The classic "Lenk's triad" (acute flank pain, palpable mass, hypovolemic shock) is present in a minority of cases.1 Our patient's presentation of sequential flank pain and mild anemia without instability could easily be mistaken for more common conditions like urolithiasis, underscoring the need for clinical vigilance.

A particularly instructive aspect of this case is the transient hypertension, which is characteristic of the Page kidney phenomenon. External compression of the renal parenchyma by the hematoma reduces renal blood flow, activating the RAAS and leading to vasoconstriction and hypertension.5,6 While Page kidney is well-described after trauma, its occurrence in spontaneous bilateral SRSH is rarely documented. The bilateral compression in our case likely contributed to the severity of the hypertension. The normalization of blood pressure coinciding with hematoma stabilization provides compelling evidence for the causal relationship and supports the reversibility of RAAS activation once the compressive stimulus is relieved, as noted in previous reviews.6

CT imaging is the modality of choice for diagnosis. Contrast-enhanced CT is essential for excluding underlying neoplasms, vascular malformations, and active hemorrhage.8,9 In our case, it definitively excluded enhancing masses or vascular anomalies, guiding the decision for conservative management. The management of SRSH is determined by the patient's hemodynamic status and the underlying cause. Hemodynamically unstable patients or those with active hemorrhage require urgent intervention, such as embolization or nephrectomy.10 In patients with underlying neoplasms, surgical management is typically required.11 Conversely, for stable patients without evidence of malignancy or active bleeding, a conservative approach including bed rest and close surveillance is safe and effective.9,12 Our case validates this approach, with complete symptom resolution and near-complete hematoma resolution at six weeks.

This case has limitations. First, as a single case report, its findings cannot be generalized. Second, although contrast-enhanced CT excluded common etiologies, we did not perform renal angiography or biopsy, which could have provided additional information. Third, acute-phase reactants (ESR, CRP) and autoimmune serologies (ANA, ANCA) were not obtained. The absence of clinical stigmata of systemic vasculitis (rash, arthralgia, neurologic symptoms, active urinary sediment), normal urinalysis, spontaneous resolution of hematomas without immunosuppressive therapy, and negative hepatitis/HIV serologies made an underlying autoimmune or vasculitic process highly unlikely. Fourth, serum renin and aldosterone levels were not measured to provide direct biochemical evidence for the Page kidney mechanism. Regarding future renal biopsy: given the near-complete radiographic resolution of the hematomas on six-week follow-up CT, normalization of blood pressure, and absence of any residual renal dysfunction or imaging abnormality, there is currently no clinical indication for an invasive renal biopsy. However, we have advised the patient to undergo long-term clinical follow-up, including annual blood pressure monitoring and renal imaging (ultrasound or CT) for 2-3 years, to exclude any late manifestation of an occult underlying condition. Should any new abnormality arise (e.g., recurrent hypertension, imaging changes), renal biopsy would be reconsidered.

Conclusions

We report an exceptionally rare case of spontaneous bilateral renal subcapsular hematoma (SRSH) in a young, healthy individual, which presented with acute flank pain and transient Page kidney-mediated hypertension. This case is noteworthy for three reasons: the bilateral idiopathic hemorrhage in the absence of risk factors, the clear demonstration of reversible Page kidney physiology with spontaneous blood pressure normalization, and the successful outcome with conservative management alone. Our findings highlight that SRSH should be considered in the differential diagnosis of acute flank pain with new-onset hypertension, even in young patients without trauma or predisposing conditions. For hemodynamically stable patients in whom contrast-enhanced CT excludes malignancy, a conservative "watch and wait" strategy with close clinical and imaging surveillance is safe and effective.
