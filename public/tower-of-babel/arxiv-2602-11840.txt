Abstract
A graph G G is universal for a class of graphs 𝒞 \cal C , if, up to isomorphism, G G contains every graph in 𝒞 \cal C as a subgraph. In 1978, Chung and Graham asked for the minimal number s ⁡ ( n ) s(n) of edges in a graph with n n vertices that is universal for all trees with n n vertices. The currently best bounds assert that n ​ ln ⁡ n − O ⁡ ( n ) ≤ s ⁡ ( n ) ≤ C ​ n ​ ln ⁡ n + O ⁡ ( n ) n\ln n-O(n)\leq s(n)\leq Cn\ln n+O(n) , where C = 14 5 ​ ln ⁡ 2 ≈ 4.04 C=\frac{14}{5\ln 2}\approx 4.04 . Here, we improve the upper bound to c ​ n ​ ln ⁡ n + O ⁡ ( n ) cn\ln n+O(n) , where c = 19 6 ​ ln ⁡ 3 ≈ 2.88 c=\frac{19}{6\ln 3}\approx 2.88 . We develop in the proof a strategy that, broadly speaking, is based on separating trees into three parts, thus enabling us to embed them in a structure that originates from ternary trees. Our method also applies to graphs that are close to being trees, measured by their treewidth. Introduction

A graph
G
is
universal
for a class of graphs
\cal C
, if, up to isomorphism,
G
contains every graph in
\cal C
as a subgraph. The study of universal graphs was initiated by Rado in 1964
[
13
]
and has flourished over the last six decades, see
[
5
,
8
,
1
]
for some recent work and further references. In this note, we discuss universal graphs with
n
vertices for the class of trees with
n

vertices, also known as
tree-complete
graphs
[
15
,
12
]
.
Chung and Graham asked for the minimum number
s(n)
of edges in a tree-complete graph in 1978
[
3
]
. In the same paper, by bounding the degree sequence of any such graph, they established the lower bound
s(n)\geq\frac{1}{2}n\ln n-O(n)
. Moreover, using a recursive construction and improving over several previous results, they showed that
s(n)\leq\frac{7}{\ln 4}n\ln n+O(n)
in
[
4
]
.
In the following forty years, neither of the bounds was improved.
However, recently the lower bound was pushed to
n\ln n-O(n)

[
6
]
, where, instead of only considering the degree sequence, the authors counted the edges carefully and jointly for all embeddings.
Shortly thereafter, Kim and Kim constructed unbalanced universal graphs, thereby improving the upper bound to
\frac{14}{3\ln 3}n\ln n+O(n)

[
11
]
. Subsequently, together with Kaul and Wood, they further improve this result to
\frac{14}{5\ln 2}n\ln n+O(n)

[
9
]
.
Hence, to date, the best bounds are

n\ln(n)-O(n)\leq s(n)\leq\frac{14}{5\ln 2}n\ln n+O(n).

Our main result improves the coefficient in the upper bound down to
19/(6\ln 3)=2.88\dots
.

Theorem 1
.

We have
s(n)\leq\frac{19}{6\ln 3}n\ln n+O(n)
.

In the proof we develop a strategy for embedding trees iteratively into a host graph based on ternary trees, which, in its very essence, is inspired by the aforementioned
[
4
]
. In particular, given a certain tree, we remove carefully up to two vertices to achieve a partition into components of suitable sizes.
Crucially, we are able to embed these components recursively, while preserving certain properties of the host graph. As we shall demonstrate in Section
3
, we are able to simplify along these lines also the original strategy presented in
[
4
]
, which is based on binary trees.

The improved upper bound is one further step towards answering Chung and Graham’s question about determining
s(n)
. However, obtaining
asymptotic
bounds would already be of great interest, and a starting point could be to show the following statement that looks very natural in this context.

Conjecture 2
.

The
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{s(n)}{n\ln n}
exists.

If this conjecture is true, then it would be interesting to determine the value
c^{*}
of the limit. The results in
[
6
]
and Theorem
1
assert that
1\leq c^{*}\leq\frac{19}{6\ln 3}
. We have no indication that either of the bounds could be tight.
Indeed, the lower bound is obtained by degree considerations (every universal graph must contain vertices of degree at least
n/i
for very many
i
), while the upper bounds (here and everywhere else) are derived by cutting the trees in smaller parts and embedding them recursively in the universal structure. We expect that some interaction actually occurs between the two different parameters, so that eventually both contribute to
s(n)
.

Our results for trees can be generalized to graphs that are structurally close to trees. The framework for this is given by the notion of treewidth, introduced by Robertson and Seymour in
[
14
]
, which, roughly speaking, measures how similar a graph is to a tree. A
tree-decomposition
of a graph
G
is a collection of subsets
(B_{x}:x\in T)
of
V(G)
(called
bags
) indexed by the vertices of a tree
T
, such that:

•

every vertex
v\in G
appears in at least one bag,

•

for every edge
uv
in
G
, there exists some bag containing both
u
and
v
, and

•

for every vertex
v\in G
, the set of bags containing
v
form a subtree of
T
.

The
width
of a tree-decomposition is the size of the largest bag minus one. The treewidth of
G
, denoted by
\text{tw}(G)
, is the minimum width over all tree-decomposition of
G
.
Let
s_{w}(n)
be the minimum number of edges in a
n
-vertex graph which is universal for graphs with treewidth
w
. Since trees have treewidth
1
, we have in particuklar
s_{1}(n)=s(n)
.
In
[
10
]
, Kaul and Wood adapt the proof of Győri et al. to establish

s_{w}(n)\geq\frac{1}{2}nw\ln(n/w)-O(nw).

With a slight modification of their argument, we sharpen the bound by a factor of
2
. Moreover, by applying a graph blow-up to our universal construction, we obtain a graph matching the asymptotic order of the lower bound. The following theorem summarizes our results.

Theorem 3
.

Uniformly in
w
we have
nw\ln(n/w)-O(nw)\leq s_{w}(n)\leq\frac{19}{6\ln 3}(w+1)n\ln(n/w)+O(nw)
.

The factor
w+1
in the upper bound arises because, in general, every minimal vertex separator that splits a graph of treewidth
w
into three parts has size
w+1
. In the special case
w=1
, trees behave differently: Every minimal separator that partitions a tree, except for the path, into three parts has size
1
rather than
2
. This explains the sharper result for
s(n)
.
In this extended setting, it would also be interesting to determine the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{s_{w}(n)}{nw\ln(n/w)}
, if it exists.

|T^{*}_{h,d}|=\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq h}d^{\ell}=\frac{d^{h+1}-1}{d-1}.

This allows us to also count the edges in
T_{h,d}^{*}
. Recall the three types of edges of a vertex
v\in V(T^{*}_{h,d})
.
For
v
at level

, we get

•

|D_{v}|=|T^{*}_{h-\ell,d}|-1
edges of
(Type 1)
,

•

(d-1)|T^{*}_{h-\ell,d}|
edges of
(Type 2)
,

•

and
\lfloor|T^{*}_{h-(\ell+1),3}|/2\rfloor
edges of
(Type 3)
, in the case

.

We simplify the notation and define
\delta_{3}:=1
if
d=3
and
\delta_{3}:=0
else.

adds a number

By combining the previous statements we are able to establish an upper bound for the number of edges

in

. Indeed,

e(h,d)=\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq h}e_{h,d}(\ell)d^{\ell}\leq\left(d+\frac{\delta_{3}}{6}\right)\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq h}\frac{d^{h-\ell+1}-1}{d-1}d^{\ell}\leq\frac{6d+\delta_{3}}{6\ln d}\ln((d-1)|T^{*}_{h,d}|+1)|T^{*}_{h,d}|.

Preliminaries

Fundamentally, every graph with treewidth
w
has a
normal
tree-decomposition, meaning that every bag has size
w+1
and the intersection of any two adjacent bags has size
w
, see
[
7
]
as a reference. The authors in
[
10
]
use this fact to generalize the separator lemma by Chung and Graham. Let
G
be a graph with tree-decomposition
(B_{x}:x\in T)
. For any vertex
z\in T
and forest
F\subset T\setminus z
, let
G(F,z)
be the induced subgraph of
G
on the vertex set
(\bigcup_{x\in F}B_{x})\setminus B_{z}
.

Lemma 17
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
G
be a graph with
\text{tw}(G)=w
and
|G|\geq t+w+1
. For every normal tree-decomposition
(B_{x}:x\in T)
of
G
, there exists a vertex
z\in T
such that for some forest
F\subset T\setminus z
,

t\leq|G(F,z)|\leq 2t.

Building on this result, we extend the separator lemma and its two corollaries to graphs of treewidth
w
.
For completeness, we also include the proofs, even though the arguments are similar to those in the tree setting.
The main difference is that instead of removing separating vertices, we remove sets of vertices. For a graph with treewidth
w
, the size of these sets is
w+1
. We adjust the notation and say that for a given graph
G
, the (possibly empty) graphs
G_{1},\dots,G_{t}
form a partition of
G
if they are vertex disjoint and
G_{1}\cup\dots\cup G_{t}=G
. Note that if
\text{tw}(G)\leq w
, then also
\text{tw}(G_{i})\leq w
for
1\leq i\leq t
.

Lemma 18
.

Let
0\leq m
,
2m\leq M
and

be a graph with
\text{tw}(G)\leq w
and
|G|\geq w+1+M
. Then there exists a set of vertices
S\subset G
with
|S|=w+1
and a partition

of
G\setminus S
such that

m\leq|G_{3}|\leq M,\quad|G_{1}|\leq|G|-w-1-M,\quad\text{and}\quad|G_{2}|\leq|G_{1}|.

Proof.

We may assume that
G
has treewidth
w
by adding edges, if this is necessary. Let
(B_{x}:x\in T)
be a normal tree-decomposition of
G
. Using Lemma
17
, we choose a vertex
z\in T
and a forest

such that
G_{3}=G(F_{3},z)
is maximal with respect to
m\leq|G_{3}|\leq M
. Let

, which implies
|S|=w+1
. Then there are two cases to distinguish.

•

If
|G_{3}|=M
, then
S
,

,

and

satisfy the conclusion of the lemma.

•

, then

consists of at least two disjoint trees. Otherwise, since the tree-decomposition is normal, we could increment

by choosing

, where

is the unique neighbor of

which is not in

; this would contradict the maximality of

. This implies that there are at least two non-empty disjoint connected components in

. Let

be the smallest connected component in

and define

. By construction, we have
|G_{1}|\geq|G_{2}|
. Further
|G_{2}|+|G_{3}|>M
, since otherwise

would contradict the maximality of

. So,

|G_{1}|=|G|-w-1-(|G_{2}|+|G_{3}|)\leq|G|-w-M-2,

proving the claim also in that case.

∎

Again, we use the following notation. For a graph

, let

be defined by

\Delta(G^{*})=N-x\text{ for }|G^{*}|\geq X\text{ and }\Delta(G^{*})=X-x\text{ for }|G^{*}|<X,

where

is given by the decomposition

for

and
|G^{*}|=x
otherwise. The first corollary handles the case where we use one set of separating vertices with size

. This reflects the setting where, given a graph

with
\text{tw}(G)\leq w
and

, we remove

vertices to obtain a partition

,
G_{2}
,

of

. Controlling the sizes of the

will later allow us to apply an inductive embedding strategy.

Corollary 19
.

Let
X>0
and
N\geq\max(X,w+1)
. Let
G
be a graph with
\text{tw}(G)\leq w
and
2N+X+w+2\leq|G|\leq 5N+X+2w+2
. Then there exists a set of vertices
S\subset G
with
|S|=w+1
and a partition
G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}
of
G\setminus S
such that

|G_{1}|\leq 2N+X,\quad|G_{2}|\leq 2N+\Delta(G_{1}),\quad|G_{3}|\leq 2N+\Delta(G_{1}\cup G_{2})+w+1,\quad\text{and}\quad|G_{1}|+|G_{2}|\geq 2N+X.

Proof.

We set
m=\max(0,|G|-w-1-4N-X)
and
M=|G|-w-1-2N-X
. Note that
2m\leq M
, hence we can apply Lemma
18
to get a set of vertices
S\subset G
with
|S|=w+1
and a partition
G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}
of
G\setminus S
such that

m\leq|G_{3}|\leq M,\quad|G_{1}|\leq|G|-w-1-M,\quad\text{and}\quad|G_{2}|\leq|G_{1}|.

It follows directly that
|G_{1}|\leq 2N+X
and
|G_{1}|+|G_{2}|=|G|-|G_{3}|-w-1\geq 2N+X
. The inequality
|G_{2}|\leq 2N+\Delta(G_{1})
holds, if
|G_{1}|\leq N+X
, since
|G_{2}|\leq|G_{1}|
. But for
|G_{1}|>N+X
we also get

|G_{2}|=|G|-w-1-|G_{1}|-|G_{3}|\leq|G|-w-1-2N-X+\Delta(G_{1})-m\leq 2N+\Delta(G_{1}).

Moreover, since
|G_{1}|+|G_{2}|\geq 2N+X
, we obtain

|G_{3}|=|G|-w-1-(|G_{1}|+|G_{2}|)\leq|G|-w-1-(3N+X-\Delta(G_{1}\cup G_{2}))\leq 2N+\Delta(G_{1}\cup G_{2})+w+1.

∎

For
|G_{3}|\leq 2N+\Delta(G_{1}\cup G_{2})
, the corollary directly allows us to embed the graphs
G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}
(in that order) inductively using almost the same procedure as in Section
4.1
.
In the case

for some

we must have
|G|=5N+X+w+1+c
and it is not hard to handle the additional vertices in that setting. Summarizing, this covers every graph with
|G|\leq 5N+X+2w+2
.
If
|G|\geq 5N+X+2w+3
, it is again possible to prepare the inductive argument by removing
two
sets of separating vertices of size
w+1
.

Corollary 20
.

and
