A Comparison of Variance Estimators for Logistic Regression Models Estimated Using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) in the Context of Observational Health Services Research
Authors: Peter C. Austin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn observational health services research, researchers often use clustered data to estimate the independent association between individual outcomes and several cluster‐level covariates after adjusting for individual‐level characteristics. Generalized estimating equations are a popular method for estimating generalized linear models using clustered data. The conventional Liang–Zeger variance estimator is known to result in estimated standard errors that are biased low when the number of clusters in small. Alternative variance estimators have been proposed for use when the number of clusters is low. Previous studies focused on these alternative variance estimators in the context of cluster randomized trials, which are often characterized by a small number of clusters and by an outcomes regression model that often consists of a single cluster‐level variable (the treatment/exposure variable). We addressed the following questions: (i) which estimator is preferred for estimating the standard errors of cluster‐level covariates for logistic regression models with multiple binary and continuous cluster‐level variables in addition to subject‐level variables; (ii) in such settings, how many clusters are required for the Liang–Zeger variance estimator to have acceptable performance for estimating the standard errors of cluster‐level covariates. We suggest that when estimating standard errors: (i) when the number of clusters is < 15 use the Kauermann–Carroll estimator; (ii) when the number of clusters is between 15 and 40 use the Fay–Graubard estimator; (iii) when the number of clusters exceeds 40, use the Liang–Zeger estimator or the Fay–Graubard estimator. When estimating confidence intervals, we suggest using the Mancl–DeRouen estimator with a t‐distribution.

Introduction

Clustered data are common in health services research. Common examples include patients clustered within hospitals or within family physician practices and individuals clustered within neighborhoods or counties. Clustering can induce a lack of independence due to a within‐cluster homogeneity in outcomes. Even after accounting for individual and cluster‐level variables, two individuals from the same cluster may have more similar outcomes than two individuals from different clusters. The use of conventional regression models estimated using maximum likelihood methods with clustered data can result in spurious conclusions being drawn from the data. In particular, estimated standard errors are likely to be biased downwards, resulting in estimated 95% confidence intervals that are artificially narrow and in significance levels that are artificially low [1].

There are two common families of regression models for use with clustered data. The first family consists of regression models estimated using generalized estimating equation (GEE) methods [2, 3]. The second family consists of hierarchical regression models, also known as multilevel models, random effects models, or mixed effects models [4, 5, 6]. The former are marginal regression models that treat the between‐cluster variation in outcomes as a nuisance. The latter are conditional regression models that incorporate cluster‐specific random effects to account for within‐cluster homogeneity in outcomes. While analysts may be tempted to treat these two families of models as interchangeable, each type of model has its own interpretation. Marginal models provide a population‐average interpretation while conditional models provide a cluster‐specific interpretation [3].

In health services research, there is often an interest in estimating the association between cluster‐level characteristics and outcomes measured at the individual level after adjusting for a set of individual‐level characteristics. For instance, a common research question is on the association between hospital volume (i.e., the number of patients treated at each hospital for a given medical condition or undergoing a given surgical procedure) and patient outcomes [7]. Other research has examined whether there is an association between the academic status of the hospital and patient outcomes [8]. In settings in which the focus is on estimating the association of cluster‐level characteristics and individual outcomes, a marginal model may be of greater utility than a conditional model, due to its population‐average interpretation.

The standard variance estimator for GEE models is that proposed by Liang and Zeger [2, 9]. However, it is known that this estimator results in estimated standard errors that are biased downwards (i.e., are systematically too small) when the number of clusters is low [10, 11, 12]. Due to this known issue with the Liang–Zeger variance estimator when the number of clusters is low, alternative variance estimators for use with GEE models have been proposed for use in settings with a small number of clusters. These include using a degrees of freedom correction to inflate the standard Liang–Zeger variance estimator [10], and modified GEE variance estimators proposed by Fay and Graubard [13], Kauermann and Carroll [14], and Mancl and DeRouen [15].

Previous studies have compared the performance of these alternative variance estimators for use with GEE estimation of logistic regression models in cluster randomized trials or stepped wedge randomized trials (which is a specific form of cluster randomized trial) [10, 11, 16]. Li and Redden used simulations in the setting of cluster randomized trials in which the total number of clusters was 10, 20, or 30 and considered a logistic regression model that contained a single binary cluster‐level covariate (i.e., the intervention) and no subject‐level covariates [10]. Ford and Westgate considered cluster randomized trials in which the total number of clusters was 10 or 20 and the logistic regression models that did not contain any subject‐level covariates [11]. Finally, in a subsequent paper, Ford and Westgate considered stepped wedge randomized trials in which the total number of clusters ranged from 3 to 24 [16].

The context is different in observational health services research than in cluster randomized trials for at least three reasons. First, rather than focus on models with a single cluster‐level covariate (i.e., the randomization arm), researchers in observational studies are frequently interested in fitting regression models with multiple cluster‐level covariates (e.g., academic status of hospital and the number of patients treated at the hospital). These cluster‐level covariates can be binary (e.g., academic status) and continuous (e.g., hospital volume), whereas in cluster randomized trials the cluster‐level covariate is typically binary (or categorical). Second, rather than fitting regression models without any subject‐level covariates, researchers using observational data often want to estimate the independent association of cluster‐level covariates with outcomes after adjusting for subject‐level covariates (e.g., patient age and sex), since the distribution of these subject‐level characteristics may vary across levels of the cluster‐level variables in observational studies, whereas in cluster randomized trials, one would expect that, on average, these characteristics would be balanced across randomization arms. Third, cluster randomized trials tend to include a small number of clusters, as evidenced by the largest number of clusters being 30 in the studies described in the previous paragraph. However, observational health services research can consider settings with a medium to large number of clusters (e.g., a study may include all patients admitted to hospital for a given medical condition at all hospitals within a given jurisdiction such as a state, province, or country).

Accordingly, this study sought to address two questions: first, in settings with a small number of clusters, which of the small sample variance estimators result in the most accurate variance estimates for the regression coefficients of cluster‐level covariates in settings in which the logistic regression model contains multiple cluster‐level covariates and simultaneously adjusts for subject‐level covariates. Second, how many clusters are necessary for the Liang–Zeger variance estimator to result in accurate estimates of standard errors for the regression coefficients of cluster‐level covariates. The article is structured as follows: In Section 2, we present a case study illustrating the application of different variance estimators to a dataset consisting of patients hospitalized with acute myocardial infarction (AMI or heart attack). This case study will also serve to motivate the subsequent Monte Carlo simulations. In Section 3, we describe the design of a series of Monte Carlo simulations to compare the relationship between the number of clusters and the accuracy of different variance estimators for use with logistic regression models estimated using GEE methods. In Section 4, we report the results of these Monte Carlo simulations. In Section 5, we present a secondary set of Monte Carlo simulations to examine the impact of the number of subject‐level covariates on the performance of the different estimators. Finally, in Section 6 we summarize our findings and place them in the context of the existing literature.

Case Study

We provide a brief case study to illustrate differences between the different variance estimators.

Data

We used data on 18 842 patients hospitalized with AMI at 157 hospitals in 2016 in Ontario that were obtained from the Ontario Myocardial Infarction Database (OMID) [17]. For this case study, the outcome was a binary variable denoting whether the patient died within 365 days (1 year) of hospital admission. The 1‐year mortality rate was 15.3%.

We considered 11 patient variables assessed at the time of hospital admission: age, sex, congestive heart failure, cardiogenic shock, arrhythmia, pulmonary edema, diabetes mellitus with complications, stroke, acute renal disease, chronic renal disease, and malignancy [18], with age being continuous and the other 10 being binary. These 11 variables comprise the Ontario AMI mortality prediction model, which was derived and internally validated in Ontario and then externally validated in the province of Manitoba and the state of California [18].

We considered two hospital‐level variables: academic status of the hospital (academic vs. nonacademic) and the number of AMI patients treated at the hospital during that year. We divided the latter variable by 100 so that the regression coefficient would denote the change in the log‐odds of 1‐year death associated with an increase of 100 in the number of AMI patients.

Statistical Methods

We randomly ordered the 157 hospitals and then selected the first M hospitals. Using all patients admitted to these M hospitals, we fit a logistic regression model in which we regressed the binary outcome denoting death within 1 year of hospital admission on the 11 subject‐level variables that comprise the Ontario AMI Mortality Prediction model and the two hospital‐level variables described above. The model was estimated using GEE methods to account for the clustering of patients within hospitals and used a compound symmetry (exchangeable) working correlation matrix. We used each of the variance estimators listed above to estimate the standard errors of the two hospital‐level variables. In addition to the variance estimators listed in Section 1, we also used the average of the Mancl–DeRouen estimate and the Kauermann–Carroll estimate [11, 12, 16].

This process was repeated allowing M to vary from 10 to 157 in increments of one hospital. When using the degrees of freedom correction method and M was < 14, we used a correction factor of NclustersseL‐Z instead of NclustersNclusters−13seL‐Z, since the denominator of the latter is not defined.

The relationship between the number of hospitals and the estimated standard error for each of the variance estimators is described in Figure 1. Results for the binary variable denoting the academic status of the hospital are reported in the left panel while results for the continuous variable denoting the number of AMI patients are reported in the right panel. For a given value of M, the Liang–Zeger estimator tended to result in estimated standard errors that were smaller than those obtained using the other methods. When the number of clusters was smaller than approximately 15, the degrees of freedom correction approach tended to result in estimated standard errors that were substantially larger than those for the other methods. The difference between the Liang–Zeger estimate and the other GEE methods was most pronounced for the binary hospital‐level variable when the number of hospitals was less than approximately 15. When the number of hospitals was at most 12, the magnitude of the differences in the estimated standard errors was very large. Even when the number of hospitals exceeded 40, the magnitude of the difference between the smallest estimated standard error (Liang–Zeger estimate) and the largest estimated standard error (Kauermann–Carroll estimate) was moderate.

Given the observed differences between the Liang–Zeger estimates and the other estimates, a study comparing the accuracy of the different variance estimators when the number of clusters is moderate to large is necessary. It is also necessary to determine the number of clusters at which the Liang–Zeger estimate accurately estimates the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the estimated regression coefficient.

Monte Carlo Simulations—Methods

We simulated clustered data with binary outcomes that were associated with both subject‐level covariates and cluster‐level covariates using a marginal logistic regression model. The simulation procedure induced a within‐cluster homogeneity in outcomes. The design of the simulations was informed by empirical analyses of the data used in the case study described in Section 2. In each simulated dataset, we fit a logistic regression model using GEE methods and used different variance estimators. We assessed the performance of the variance estimators by determining the degree to which the estimated standard error approximated the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the estimated regression coefficients and by whether the empirical coverage rates of 95% confidence intervals were equal to the advertised rate. When constructing 95% confidence intervals, we used both normal‐theory methods and methods based on the t‐distribution.

Factors in the Monte Carlo Simulations

We allowed three factors to vary in the Monte Carlo simulations: (i) the number of clusters (N cluster); (ii) the number of subjects per cluster (N subjects); (iii) the within‐cluster correlation in the underlying latent continuous outcomes (ρ) — see below for a description of this factor. The first took on values from 5 to 100 in increments of 1, for a total of 96 different levels. The second took on two values: 25 and 50. The third took on three values: 0.012, 0.057, and 0.105. The choice of the values for the second and third factors is justified in the following paragraph. We used a full factorial design and thus examined 576 different scenarios. In each scenario, we created 1000 simulated datasets.

The Health Services Research Unit at the University of Aberdeen maintains a database of intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) from published studies in health services research [19]. The database contains 146 ICCs for binary outcomes. The 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of these 146 ICCs were 0.012, 0.057, and 0.105, respectively. Thus, the chosen ICC values are reflective of those observed in health services research with binary outcomes. Similarly, our two values of cluster size, 25 and 50 subjects per cluster, represent the 66th and 92nd percentiles of cluster size for these 146 binary outcomes. We did not consider settings with small cluster sizes to avoid estimation problems when the number of clusters was also low (to avoid fitting a regression model with three covariates to simulated data with only a few observed outcome events).

Empirical Analyses of Patients Hospitalized With AMI

We conducted empirical analyses of the OMID data described in Section 2.1. to determine parameters that would be used in the data‐generating process of the Monte Carlo simulations. We used a conventional logistic regression model to regress the binary outcome variable denoting 1‐year mortality on the 11 variables that comprise the Ontario AMI mortality prediction model, with age being continuous and the other 10 being binary. For each subject, we computed the linear predictor based on the fitted model. The mean and standard deviation of the linear predictor were −2.28 and 1.39, respectively. We will use this mean and standard deviation to generate a continuous subject‐level covariate for each subject.

We considered two hospital‐level variables: hospital teaching status (academic vs. nonacademic hospital) and the number of AMI patients treated during the given year. The first was binary and the second was continuous. Of the 157 hospitals, 9.6% were teaching hospitals. We standardized hospital volume so that its mean and standard deviation across the sample of 157 hospitals were 0 and 1, respectively. At nonacademic hospitals, the mean and standard deviation of this standardized volume variable was −0.12 and 0.84, respectively. At academic hospitals, the mean and standard deviation of this modified volume variable were 1.15 and 1.56, respectively. We will use these two distributions to generate cluster‐level covariates.

We then fit a logistic regression model in which the binary outcome of 1‐year mortality was regressed on three variables: the linear predictor computed above, hospital academic status, and hospital AMI volume. The model was estimated using GEE methods to account for the clustering of patients within hospitals and used a compound symmetry working correlation matrix. The estimated regression coefficients were 0.054, 0.992, 0.060, and −0.061 for the intercept, the linear predictor, hospital academic status, and hospital AMI volume, respectively.

Data‐Generating Process

For each of the N cluster clusters we generated two cluster‐level covariates: Z 1 and Z 2. The first cluster‐level covariate was generated from a Bernoulli distribution with parameter 0.5 (we used 0.5 rather than 0.096 to avoid estimation problems arising from all clusters having a value of “0”; apart from this modification, the simulated data will resemble the AMI sample described above). The second cluster‐level covariate was continuous and its distribution was conditional on the value of Z 1. When Z 1 = 0, then Z 2 was simulated from a normal distribution with mean and standard deviation equal to −0.12 and 0.84, respectively. When Z 1 = 1, then Z 2 was simulated from a normal distribution with mean and standard deviation of 1.15 and 1.56, respectively (the means and standard deviations of these conditional distributions were estimated above).

For each of the N subject subjects in a cluster we generated a single subject‐level covariate whose distribution was the same as the linear predictor computed above: X ˜ N(μ = −2.28, σ = 1.39). We then simulated a binary subject‐level outcome Y using the following marginal logistic regression model: logitpij=0.054+0.992xij+0.060z1j−0.061z2j where the subscript ij denotes the ith subject in the jth cluster and where Y ˜ Bernoulli(p) (the regression coefficients were obtained in the analysis described above). Binary outcomes were generated using an extension suggested by Touloumis [20] of the approach described by Emrich and Piedmonte [21]. Using this procedure for simulating outcomes, outcomes were correlated within clusters. In this procedure for generating correlated binary outcomes, the dependence structure is parametrized in terms of the correlation matrix of latent continuous responses. In our simulations, we specified the correlation matrix of latent continuous responses to be a matrix whose diagonal elements were all equal to 1, while the off‐diagonal elements were all equal to ρ. As noted above, we allowed ρ to take three values: 0.012, 0.057, and 0.105. A restricted set of Monte Carlo simulations demonstrated that, across a set of simulation replicates, this resulted in the mean off‐diagonal element in the cluster‐specific exchangeable working correlation matrices being equal to 0.003, 0.018, and 0.034, respectively. Similarly, the mean residual ICC was 0.010, 0.052, and 0.098, respectively, when using the latent response formulation with a random effects model to estimate the ICC [4]. Thus, we considered scenarios with weak to strong within‐cluster correlation in outcomes after adjustment for subject‐ and cluster‐level covariates. Using Monte Carlo simulations, we determined that the marginal probability of the outcome was approximately 0.158 (which is very similar to the observed 1‐year mortality rate of 15.3% that was observed in the OMID‐AMI data above). The 1st and 99th percentiles of subject‐specific probability of the outcome were approximately 0.16 and 0.73, respectively.

We thus simulated baseline covariates and outcomes for each of N = N cluster × N subject subjects. For each of the 576 different scenarios, we simulated 1000 random samples, for a total of 576 000 random samples.

Statistical Analyses in the Simulated Data

In each of the 576 000 random samples, we fit a logistic regression model in which the binary outcome was regressed on the single subject‐level covariate (which represents a linear predictor comprising a set of subject‐level variables) and the 2 cluster‐level covariates. The model was fit using GEE methods assuming an exchangeable (or compound symmetry) working correlation matrix.

We used six estimators of the standard error of the estimated regression coefficients: (i) the conventional GEE variance estimator proposed by Liang and Zeger [9]; (ii) the degrees of freedom corrected variance estimator [10]; (iii) Fay and Graubard's small sample variance estimator [13]; (iv) Kauermann and Carroll's variance estimator [14]; (v) Mancl and DeRouen's modified GEE variance estimator [15]; and (vi) the average of the Mancl–DeRouen estimand and the Kauermann–Carroll estimator [11, 12, 16]. For the degrees of freedom corrected variance estimator, the Liang–Zeger estimate of the standard error (seL–Z) is replaced by NclustersNclusters−3seL‐Z, where 3 represents the number of model covariates.

Using the estimated regression coefficient and the estimated standard error we constructed 95% confidence intervals for each of the three regression coefficients. We constructed two 95% confidence intervals for each of the six variance estimators. First, we used standard normal theory methods and assumed that the sampling distribution of the regression coefficients were normal. Second, we assumed that the regression coefficients followed a t‐distribution with N clusters—2 degrees of freedom (2 was subtracted from N clusters because there were two cluster‐level covariates) [10, 22].

Performance Measures

We used two performance measures to assess the performance of the different methods for estimating the standard error of the estimated regression coefficients and for constructing 95% confidence intervals: (i) relative percent error in the estimated standard error of the estimated regression coefficients; (ii) empirical coverage rates of estimated 95% confidence intervals.

Relative percent error in the estimated standard error of the estimated regression coefficients was computed as 100×11000∑i=11000seϕ^iSD(ϕ^)−1, where seϕ^i denotes the estimated standard error in the ith simulation replicate and SD(ϕ^) denotes the standard deviation of the estimated regression coefficients across the 1000 simulation replicates [23]. If the relative error is equal to zero, then the estimated standard error is correctly estimating the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the estimated regression coefficients. If the relative error is less than zero, then the estimated standard errors are underestimating the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the estimated regression coefficients. If the relative error is greater than zero, then the estimated standard errors are overestimating the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the estimated regression coefficients.

Empirical coverage rates of estimated 95% confidence intervals were computed as the proportion of estimated confidence intervals that contained the true value of the regression coefficient that was specified in the data‐generating process.

When summarizing the results of the simulations, we excluded estimated regression coefficients that were larger in absolute value than 10 and estimated standard errors that were larger than 100 (these threshold are the defaults in the rsimsum::simsum() function that was used to summarize the results of the simulations). For the binary cluster‐level covariate, this resulted in the exclusion of 96 estimated regression coefficients. For the binary cluster‐level covariate, this resulted in the exclusion of 1942 estimated standard errors out of the 3 456 000 estimated standard errors (0.056% of the estimated standard errors). The smallest excluded standard error was 101, while the median excluded standard error was larger than 9.25e7. For the continuous cluster‐level covariate, 6 of the estimated regression coefficients and 418 of the estimated standard errors were excluded.

Software

The simulations were conducted using the R statistical programming language (version 3.6.3). Correlated binary outcomes were simulated using the rbin function from the SimCorMultRes package (version 1.9.0) [20]. Note that this function has a constraint that the size of the clusters be constant. The regression coefficients for the GEE models were estimated using the gee function from the geepack package (version 1.3‐2). The standard errors of the regression coefficients were estimated using the appropriate functions from the geesmv package (version 1.3). The simulation results were summarized using the simsum function from the rsimsum package (version 0.11.3).

Monte Carlo Simulations—Results

In this study, our focus was on inferences about cluster‐level covariates, as these are often the research focus in health services research. Therefore, we only report results for the two cluster‐level covariates (Z 1 and Z 2), the first of which was binary while the second was continuous. We first present results for estimation of standard errors, followed by the results for empirical coverage rates of estimated confidence intervals.

Secondary Monte Carlo Simulations— Impact of the Number of Subject‐Level Covariates

We conducted a secondary set of Monte Carlo simulations to examine the impact of the number of subject‐level covariates on the performance of the different variance estimators when estimating the standard errors of cluster‐level covariates. We did this first for the degrees of freedom correction method and then subsequently for the modified GEE variance estimators.

Degrees of Freedom Correction Method—Impact of the Number of Subject‐Level Covariates

While the degrees of freedom correction has been proposed for use with logistic regression models fit to clustered data [10], it was originally developed for use with conventional linear regression models estimated using ordinary least squared to account for heteroskedasticity and not for clustered data [24]. While our primary set of simulations showed that this approach had reasonable performance, we sought to evaluate the impact of the number of subject‐level covariates on the performance of this method.

The simulations were similar to those described in Section 3 with the following modifications. First, for each cluster, we simulated two independent cluster‐level variables: a binary variable from a Bernoulli distribution with parameter 0.5 and a continuous variable from a standard normal distribution. Second, for each subject, we generated K subject‐level covariates from K independent standard normal distributions. Third, we fixed the number of subjects per cluster to equal 50. Fourth, we allowed the number of clusters to range from 5 to 20 in increments of 1. Fifth, we modified the regression model used to generate outcomes so that the intercept was equal to zero and the regression coefficients for all subject‐level and cluster‐level covariates were equal to one. Apart from the number of clusters, two other factors were allowed to vary in the design of the simulations: ρ, which took the same three values as in Section 3, and K (the number of subject‐level covariates), which ranged from 1 to 12 in increments of 1. We only evaluated the relative error in the estimated standard error. Apart from these modifications, the simulations were similar to those in Section 3.

The relative error in estimating the standard error for the binary cluster‐level covariate is reported in Figure 6. There is one panel for each of the three values of ρ. We report the relative error for the degrees of freedom correction and for the conventional Liang–Zeger estimator. The number of subject‐level covariates had no discernible impact on the relative error of the Liang–Zeger variance estimator. However, the number of subject‐level covariates had a substantial impact on the performance of the degrees of freedom correction. When the number of subject‐level covariates was low, the degrees of freedom correction resulted in estimated standard errors with less bias than the Liang–Zeger estimator. However, when the number of subject‐level covariates was large, the degrees of freedom correction resulted in estimated standard errors with greater bias than the Liang–Zeger estimator. In particular, when the number of clusters was low and the number of subject‐level covariates was high, the relative bias could be very large (> 100%). Results for estimation of the standard error of the continuous cluster‐level covariate were similar (results not shown).

Effect of number of subject−level covariates on the relative error in the estimated standard error (binary cluster−level covariate).

Based on these simulations, we suggest that, in general, the degrees of freedom correction method not be used for estimating the standard errors of logistic regression models fit to clustered data.

Modified GEE Variance Estimators—Impact of the Number of Subject‐Level Covariates

We conducted a restricted set of Monte Carlo simulations to examine the impact of the number of subject‐level covariates when making inferences about cluster‐level covariates using the different modified GEE variance estimators. These simulations are similar to those described in Section 5.1 with a few modifications. First, we only considered one value of ρ: ρ = 0.057. We considered the conventional Liang–Zeger variance estimator and the three modified GEE estimators: the Fay–Graubard estimator, the Kauermann–Carroll estimator, and the Mancl–DeRouen estimator. To simplify the presentation of the results, we did not examine the average of the last two estimators. Furthermore, based on the results in Section 5.1, we did not consider the degrees of freedom correction method.

The relative error in estimating the standard error for the binary cluster‐level covariate is reported in Figure 7. There is one panel for each of the 12 values of K (the number of subject‐level covariates). Across all 12 values of K, with only a few exceptions, the Kauermann–Carroll estimator tended, to result in estimates with the lowest relative bias when the number of clusters was < 10. Across all 12 values of K, with only a few exceptions, the Fay–Graubard estimator tended, to result in estimates with the lowest relative bias when the number of clusters ranged from 10 to 20. Of the three modified GEE variance estimators, the Mancl–DeRouen estimator tended to result in estimates with the largest relative bias. The relative error in estimating the standard error for the continuous cluster‐level covariate is reported in Figure S9. Results were comparable to those for the binary cluster‐level covariate.

Discussion

We used Monte Carlo simulations to compare the performance of six variance estimators for use with GEE models. We assessed the performance of these estimators in the context of making inferences about the coefficients of both binary and continuous cluster‐level covariates in multivariable marginal logistic regression model that incorporated both individual‐level and cluster‐level covariates. We evaluated the performance of the estimators using two criteria: the accuracy with which they estimated the standard errors of the estimated regression coefficients and the empirical coverage rates of estimated 95% confidence intervals. We summarize our findings as follows: (i) the Liang–Zeger estimator resulted in underestimation of the standard error of cluster‐level covariates when the number of clusters was less than approximately 40; (ii) the degrees of freedom corrected estimator often resulted in estimates with amongst the lowest bias; (iii) when the number of clusters was less than approximately 15, the Kauermann‐Carroll estimator often resulted in estimated standard errors with the lowest bias amongst the modified GEE variance estimators; (iv) when the number of clusters exceeded approximately 15, the Fay–Graubard estimator often resulted in estimated standard errors with the lowest bias amongst the modified GEE estimator; (v) the Liang–Zeger estimator resulted in confidence intervals whose empirical coverage rates were significantly lower than average when the number of clusters was less than approximately 50–60, depending on the scenario; (vi) when the number of clusters was small, it was better to use a t‐distribution than a normal distribution; (vii) the Mancl–DeRouen estimator combined with the use of a t‐distribution tended to result in estimated confidence intervals whose empirical coverage rates did not differ from the advertised rate regardless of the number of clusters.

As highlighted in Section 1, a small number of previous studies compared the performance of different variance estimators for use with logistic regression models estimated using GEE methods. Most of these studies focused on the performance of these estimators in cluster randomized trials or in stepped wedge cluster randomized trials [25, 26]. Li and Redden examined the performance of different variance estimators in the context of cluster randomized trials with binary outcomes [10]. Their primary focus was on type I error rates, with a secondary focus on statistical power, rather than on variance estimation and estimation of confidence intervals. They concluded that to preserve the advertised type I error rate one should use a t‐test with N clusters‐2 degrees of freedom, with the Kauermann–Carroll variance estimator when the variation in cluster size is low to moderate and that one should use a t‐test with the Fay–Graubard variance estimator when the variation in cluster size is large. Ford and Westgate used simulations to compare the performance of variance estimators in cluster randomized trials with a small number of clusters [11]. The design of their simulations was based on the design of Li and Redden. As with Li and Redden, their primary focus was on empirical type I error rates, with a secondary focus on bias in the estimated standard error. They found that using a standard error that was the average of the Mancl–DeRouen estimated standard error and the Kauermann–Carroll estimate tended to preserve the nominal type I error rate when using a t‐distribution with Nclusters—p degrees of freedom, where p denotes the number of model parameters (their findings were the motivation for the inclusion of this estimator in this study). Furthermore, the use of this variance estimator tended to result in estimates of standard error with less bias than the Mancl–DeRouen estimator and the Kauermann–Carroll estimator. Of note, Li and Redden only considered simulation settings in which the logistic regression model had only one covariate (a binary variable denoting the intervention group). Ford and Westgate considered both this setting and a setting in which the logistic regression model had an additional cluster‐level covariate (to reflect scenarios in which the analyst wants to adjust for a single cluster‐level characteristic). In a subsequent article, Ford and Westgate compared the performance of different variance estimators for use with GEE models when analyzing stepped wedge cluster randomized trials [16]. They considered four variance estimators: the Mancl–DeRouen estimator, the Fay–Graubard estimator, the Kauermann–Carroll estimator, and an estimator that was the average of the Mancl–DeRouen estimator and the Kauermann–Carroll estimator. They assessed the performance of the estimators using the bias in the estimated standard error and by whether empirical type I error rate differed from the advertised rate. They found that the average of the Mancl–DeRouen estimator and the Kauermann–Carroll estimator tended to result in estimates of the standard error with the least bias. They found that the use of this estimator with degrees of freedom equal to the number of clusters minus two often led to correct inferences. The above studies all considered variance estimation in the context of cluster randomized trials or stepped wedge cluster randomized trials. Ford and Westgate examined the performance of different variance estimators in the context of a longitudinal study with a small number of subjects (i.e., measurement occasions are clustered within subjects) [12]. They considered four different scenarios defined by the marginal model using for generating outcomes. The first marginal model had only a single time‐varying covariate that was assessed at each measurement occasion. The second marginal model consisted of measurement time and a time‐invariant baseline covariate. The third consisted of measurement time and an interaction between measurement time and a binary baseline variable denoting treatment group. Finally, the fourth consisted of a quadratic function of measurement time and a time‐varying covariate. In assessing the bias in the estimated standard error, they found that the estimator that was the average of the Mancl–DeRouen estimator and the Kauermann–Carroll estimator was the only estimator with no bias that exceeded 10% in any of the scenarios. They also found that this estimator, combined with the use of Nsubjects—p degrees of freedom (p is the number of model parameters) also tended to result in approximately correct type I error rates.

Our findings differ from those discussed above. We hypothesize that one explanation relates to the complexity of the true marginal model in each study. The true marginal models in the studies cited above tended to be either univariate models or models with only a few covariates. The marginal model in this study, with a single continuous subject‐level covariate (that represented a linear predictor comprising 11 subject‐level covariates) and 2 cluster‐level covariates, is more reflective of the models fit in observational health services research, where the focus is often on estimating odds ratios for cluster‐level covariates after adjusting for patient characteristics.

The primary limitation of this study relates to our use of Monte Carlo simulations. Due to the computational burden of the simulations, we were limited in the number of scenarios that we could consider. However, we considered a range of scenarios defined by both the within‐cluster correlation in outcomes and number of subjects per cluster, whose values were reflective of those observed in health services research [19]. Due to the computational burden of the simulations, we were unable to consider additional factors beyond these two and the number of clusters.

We evaluated the performance of the different variance estimators using two performance metrics: relative bias in the estimated standard error and empirical coverage rates of 95% confidence intervals. These two metrics are not independent of one another, as confidence intervals were constructed using estimated standard errors. Furthermore, as noted by Morris et al. [23] empirical coverage rates that are lower than the advertised rate are to be expected if the relative error in the estimated standard error is negative (implying that the estimated standard errors are smaller than the empirical standard errors). This was observed in our simulations, where the Liang–Zeger estimator produced estimated standard errors that had a modestly large negative relative error when the number of clusters was low and that also produced confidence intervals whose empirical coverage rates were substantially lower than the advertised rate when the number of clusters was low. The preferred variance estimator differed between the two performance measures. When using relative bias in the estimated standard error, we found that the either Kauermann–Carroll estimator (when the number of clusters was less than approximately 15) or the Fay–Graubard estimator (when the number of clusters was greater than approximately 15) tended to be preferred. When using empirical coverage rates, the Mancl–DeRouen estimator (with a t‐distribution) tended to be preferred. There were scenarios in which both the Kauermann–Carroll and Fay–Graubard estimators resulted in confidence intervals whose empirical coverage rates were modestly lower than advertised. This raises the question of whether one metric should take precedent over the other. Standard errors are often used in conducting statistical hypothesis tests. While a decision to favor one metric over the other would likely not be unanimous, one could follow the advice of Wasserstein and colleagues in an editorial published in The American Statistician, who stated that “We conclude, based on our review of the articles in this special issue and the broader literature, that it is time to stop using the term ‘statistically significant’ entirely. Nor should variants such as ‘significantly different’, ‘p < 0.05’, and ‘nonsignificant’ survive, whether expressed in words, by asterisks in a table, or in some other way” [27]. Based on this statement, one could argue that the empirical coverage rates of confidence intervals may be the preferred performance metric. Similarly, because of the correspondence between statistical significance testing and whether the estimated confidence interval excludes the null value, one could argue that the estimator that performs best for constructing confidence intervals should take precedence.

In summary, we have the following suggestions when estimating standard errors: (i) when the number of clusters is < 15 use the Kauermann–Carroll estimator; (ii) when the number of clusters is between 15 and 40 use the Fay–Graubard estimator; (iii) when the number of clusters exceeds 40, use either the Liang–Zeger estimator or the Fay–Graubard estimator. When estimating confidence intervals, we suggest using the Mancl–DeRouen estimator with a t‐distribution.
