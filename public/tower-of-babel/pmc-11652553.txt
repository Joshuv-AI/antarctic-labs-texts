Global Trends and Hotspots in the Research of the Effects of PM2.5 on Asthma: A Bibliometric and Visualized Analysis
Authors: Xinchen Shu, Jiaxin Cao, Qi Liu, Yixin Wang, Feng Jiang, Chuyan Wu, Jin Shu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFine particulate matter (PM2.5) has been identified as a significant environmental and public health challenge, particularly due to its association with respiratory diseases like asthma. With the global rise in urbanization and industrialization, PM2.5-related asthma research has grown substantially over the past two decades. This study aims to provide a comprehensive bibliometric analysis to map global research trends, highlight key contributors, and identify emerging hotspots in the relationship between PM2.5 and asthma.MethodsWe performed a bibliometric analysis using the Web of Science Core Collection database, covering research from January 2004 to September 2024. The selected studies were analyzed using CiteSpace and VOSviewer to assess publication trends, global collaborations, and research hotspots through visualized networks and co-occurrence analyses.ResultsA total of 2035 publications were identified, demonstrating a steady increase in research output over the past two decades. The United States and China emerged as dominant contributors, frequently collaborating with countries like Canada, Australia, and South Korea. Key research areas focused on air quality, particulate matter exposure, and asthma exacerbation, with an increasing emphasis on indoor air pollution and long-term exposure risks. Institutional collaborations were led by prominent universities such as the University of California System and Harvard University. Additionally, research on vulnerable populations, particularly children, and the impact of early-life exposure to PM2.5 has gained attention in recent years.ConclusionsThe global research landscape on PM2.5 and asthma has expanded significantly, with growing attention to interdisciplinary approaches that combine environmental science and public health. Future studies should focus on the global burden of air pollution, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, and address the long-term health impacts of PM2.5 exposure, especially in vulnerable populations.

Introduction

Air pollution, particularly fine particulate matter (PM2.5), has emerged as a critical global environmental and public health challenge [1, 2]. PM2.5 consists of airborne particles with a diameter of less than 2.5 micrometers, allowing them to penetrate deep into the lungs and even enter the bloodstream [3, 4]. These fine particles are primarily produced by combustion processes, such as vehicle emissions, industrial activities, and biomass burning [5, 6]. Due to their small size and ability to travel long distances, PM2.5 particles remain suspended in the air for extended periods, contributing to long-term exposure risks.

Extensive epidemiological and toxicological studies have established that PM2.5 exposure can lead to adverse health outcomes, particularly affecting the respiratory and cardiovascular systems [7–9]. Among the respiratory conditions exacerbated by PM2.5 exposure, asthma-a chronic inflammatory airway disease-has been identified as highly vulnerable to ambient air pollution [10, 11]. Asthma affects approximately 300 million people globally, with its prevalence increasing, particularly in urbanized and industrialized regions where air pollution levels are higher.

The biological mechanisms underlying the exacerbation of asthma by PM2.5 exposure are complex and multifaceted. Inhalation of PM2.5 triggers oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in the airway epithelium, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines [12, 13]. These mediators recruit immune cells, such as neutrophils and eosinophils, to the lungs, further amplifying the inflammatory cascade [14, 15]. Moreover, PM2.5 can alter the immune response by activating toll-like receptors (TLRs), inducing airway hyperresponsiveness, and impairing the airway’s ability to clear pathogens, which increases the risk of infections and asthma exacerbations [16, 17]. In vulnerable populations, such as children and individuals with pre-existing respiratory conditions, these effects are particularly pronounced.

In addition to exacerbating asthma symptoms, PM2.5 exposure has also been linked to the onset of asthma, particularly in children who are exposed to high levels of air pollution during early developmental stages. Recent studies suggest that early-life exposure to PM2.5 may disrupt normal lung development and immune regulation, leading to increased susceptibility to asthma later in life [18, 19]. Research has increasingly demonstrated that exposure to PM2.5 during prenatal development and infancy contributes significantly to a higher incidence of asthma in early childhood, with long-lasting impacts on respiratory health and vulnerability to environmental pollutants [20, 21].

Bibliometric analysis is a quantitative method that evaluates academic publications to uncover research trends, key contributors, and emerging topics within a specific field. It allows for the systematic mapping of a rapidly growing and interdisciplinary body of literature. By analyzing citation patterns, collaboration networks, and keyword co-occurrence, bibliometric analysis provides insights into the evolution of research priorities, identifies influential studies, and highlights gaps in knowledge that warrant further investigation [22, 23]. Such an approach is essential in a field like environmental health, where the integration of scientific findings into public health policies and interventions is critical for addressing global challenges.

Despite the growing body of evidence linking PM2.5 to asthma, the global research landscape reflects a multidisciplinary approach, with studies focusing on biological mechanisms, regional exposure levels, and diverse health outcomes. Previous bibliometric reviews have provided valuable insights into related domains. For instance, Wu et al. evaluated asthma-related research without a specific focus on fine particulate matter, highlighting trends in respiratory disease studies [24]. Similarly, Jia et al. and Zhao et al. explored ambient and indoor PM2.5 research without addressing asthma, shedding light on environmental health and pollution control strategies [25, 26]. Han et al., on the other hand, conducted a bibliometric analysis specifically on the intersection of PM2.5 and asthma, providing insights into this research area [27]. By comparing our findings with these prior studies, this analysis builds on existing knowledge and identifies both enduring and emerging themes in the field. Understanding these trends is crucial for guiding future research efforts and informing public health interventions aimed at mitigating the impact of PM2.5 on asthma.

Therefore, this study aims to conduct a bibliometric and visualized analysis of global research trends and hotspots related to PM2.5 and asthma. By mapping the scientific literature over the past two decades, we seek to provide insights into the evolution of research, highlight key areas of focus, and identify gaps that warrant further investigation.

Materials and Methods

Data Sources and Search Strategy

We conducted a comprehensive bibliometric analysis using the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) database to examine variables such as publication year, authorship, journals, keywords, institutions, and country-specific contributions to research on PM2.5 and asthma. The search covered the period from January 1, 2004, to September 6, 2024, and was limited to English-language publications, including original research articles and review articles. A total of 2035 publications were retrieved. The search formula used was as follows: (TS = (asthma) OR TS = (bronchial asthma) OR TS = (wheeze) OR TS = (wheezing) OR TS = (shortness of breath) OR TS = (bronchial spasm) OR TS = (bronchospasm) OR TS = (bronchial hyperreactivity) OR TS = (bronchial hyperresponsiveness)) AND (TS = (PM2.5)). Data Analysis and Visualization.

The bibliographic data, including titles, authors, publication years, journals, and keywords, were exported for analysis using CiteSpace and VOSviewer software [28, 29]. These tools were utilized to conduct the bibliometric and visualized analysis of the selected articles. Key metrics analyzed included annual publication trends, countries/regions and institutional contributions, and co-authorship networks. Additionally, keyword co-occurrence analysis was performed to identify research hotspots and emerging trends. Through these visualizations, we were able to map the evolution of research in this field, identify leading contributors, and highlight areas that may require further exploration.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive bibliometric and visualized analysis of the global research trends and hotspots in PM2.5 and asthma from 2004 to 2024. By analyzing the co-occurrence of keywords, keyword clusters, temporal trends, and citation bursts, our findings provide valuable insights into the development of this field and highlight emerging topics of interest. This discussion will explore the key findings, their implications in the broader research context, and the potential future directions for studies on PM2.5 and asthma.

Main Findings and Implications

The keyword co-occurrence analysis revealed that “air pollution,” “particulate matter,” “asthma,” and “respiratory health” have been central topics throughout the past two decades. These findings are consistent with prior studies that have emphasized the critical impact of air pollution, particularly PM2.5, on respiratory conditions like asthma. The significant presence of terms such as “inflammation,” “oxidative stress,” and “lung function” further confirms that much of the research has focused on understanding the biological mechanisms by which PM2.5 exposure leads to respiratory diseases. This mechanistic understanding is vital for developing targeted interventions and informing public health policies aimed at reducing the burden of air pollution-related diseases.

Another important finding was the identification of clusters related to indoor air quality and particulate matter. With the increasing attention to indoor environments, these clusters reflect the growing awareness that air pollution is not confined to outdoor sources. This shift in research focus aligns with recent studies that show indoor air quality can significantly contribute to the overall exposure to PM2.5, especially in urban settings where individuals spend a substantial amount of time indoors. This shift has important public health implications, as improving indoor air quality may be a key strategy for reducing overall exposure to harmful pollutants.

Additionally, the temporal analysis showed that emerging research topics, such as climate change, global burden, and early life exposure, have gained prominence in recent years. This indicates that researchers are increasingly exploring the broader, long-term implications of PM2.5 exposure, moving beyond short-term clinical outcomes to examine how air pollution contributes to global health inequities and climate-related health risks. These findings underscore the need for future studies to adopt a more integrated approach, considering environmental, socio-economic, and health factors in addressing the global burden of air pollution.

Comparison with Previous Studies

Previous bibliometric reviews have explored aspects of asthma or PM2.5 research, providing important context for this field. Wu et al. focused on asthma-related research without specifically addressing fine particulate matter, highlighting trends in respiratory disease studies [24]. Jia et al. and Zhao et al. analyzed bibliometric trends in ambient and indoor PM2.5 research without a focus on asthma, emphasizing environmental health and pollution control strategies [25, 26]. Han et al. specifically addressed the intersection of PM2.5 and asthma, providing a foundational bibliometric analysis of this topic [27]. Compared to these studies, our manuscript provides updated and more comprehensive data spanning 2004–2024, incorporating temporal trends, keyword evolution, and emerging topics such as climate change and global burden. Notably, our study uniquely highlights the increasing prominence of indoor air quality, the role of long-term exposure in vulnerable populations, and interdisciplinary collaborations that bridge environmental science and public health. These contributions offer new insights into the evolving research priorities and future directions in the field of PM2.5 and asthma.

Our findings are consistent with a wide range of previous studies that have explored the relationship between PM2.5 exposure and asthma. For instance, the work of Amanda et al. demonstrated that PM2.5 induces significant inflammation and oxidative stress in human airway cells, providing mechanistic insights into how particulate matter exacerbates respiratory conditions, including asthma​ [30]. This mechanistic focus on oxidative stress and inflammation was mirrored in our study, where keywords related to these biological processes frequently appeared in co-occurrence networks and clusters. The work of Zhang et al. also supports this, emphasizing that PM2.5 triggers inflammatory responses, further validating the connection between air pollution exposure and asthma pathogenesis [31].

In terms of childhood asthma, our findings echo those of Peter et al., concluded that long-term exposure to traffic-related air pollution, including PM2.5, significantly increases the risk of asthma in children [32]. This is particularly relevant in urban areas with high vehicular emissions, where exposure to PM2.5 has been linked to both new-onset asthma and asthma exacerbations [33, 34]. This reflects our analysis, where keywords such as “childhood asthma” and “long-term exposure” showed notable citation bursts, indicating a sustained research interest in the long-term health impacts of early-life exposure to air pollution.

Further corroborating our findings, Achenyo et al. provided compelling evidence on the global burden of air pollution, showing that efforts to reduce PM2.5 levels have been associated with a decrease in hospital admissions for asthma and other respiratory diseases [35]​. This aligns with our analysis, which highlighted “global burden” and “public health” as emerging areas of focus in recent years. The global health perspective on PM2.5 is critical, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where environmental regulations may not be as stringent, and the health impacts of air pollution are more severe [36, 37].

Another key finding from our analysis relates to indoor air quality, a topic that has garnered increasing attention in recent years. The importance of indoor air pollution was highlighted by Paterson, who found that households are at higher risk of indoor air pollution exposure, which is strongly associated with respiratory conditions like asthma [38–40]. This aligns with the increased citation bursts related to “indoor air quality” in our study, suggesting that indoor sources of air pollution are becoming a more prominent research focus, especially as individuals spend a significant portion of their time indoors.

Moreover, Shaherin et al. expanded on the health impacts of fine PM2.5, providing strong epidemiological evidence linking PM2.5 exposure to both respiratory and cardiovascular diseases [41]. Their work supports the growing recognition of PM2.5 as a major environmental risk factor, which aligns with our finding that research on particulate matter remains central to the field, as reflected by the consistent presence of terms like “PM2.5” and “air pollution” in keyword co-occurrence analyses.

Additionally, the increasing focus on climate change in PM2.5 research, as observed in our study, is consistent with findings by Weeberb et al. They demonstrated that climate change exacerbates air pollution levels, particularly through increased wildfire frequency and extreme weather events, which contribute to higher concentrations of PM2.5 [42–44]. This intersection between climate change and air pollution represents a critical area for future research, as the global impacts of climate change are expected to worsen air quality, further aggravating respiratory health issues worldwide [45, 46].

In addition to fine PM, other air pollutants such as NO2, CO, SO2, and PM10 have been identified as significant contributors to respiratory health risks, as highlighted in Figs. 8A and 9A. Among these, NO2 has been particularly associated with asthma prevalence and exacerbation. The global analysis by Anenberg et al. in The Lancet Planetary Health demonstrated a substantial contribution of NO2 exposure to asthma cases worldwide, emphasizing its critical role in urban environments with high traffic emissions [47]. Similarly, CO and SO2 are known to aggravate respiratory conditions by inducing oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in the airway epithelium, compounding the effects of PM2.5. PM10, as a coarser particulate pollutant, has been shown to have synergistic effects with fine PM, particularly in populations with pre-existing respiratory vulnerabilities [48, 49]. Our study reinforces these findings by identifying the co-occurrence and temporal prominence of these pollutants in asthma-related research, suggesting the need for an integrated approach to air quality management that considers the combined impact of multiple pollutants on respiratory health. Future research should explore how these pollutants interact with fine PM to exacerbate asthma and other respiratory diseases, particularly in regions with high pollutant levels and vulnerable populations.

The increasing prominence of themes such as “climate change” in recent years reflects a growing recognition of the interconnectedness between environmental factors and public health. Climate change has been shown to exacerbate air pollution levels through mechanisms such as increased wildfire frequency, changes in atmospheric circulation patterns, and the intensification of urban heat islands, all of which contribute to elevated PM2.5 levels [43, 50]. These changes have a direct impact on respiratory diseases like asthma, particularly in vulnerable populations. Moreover, global policy initiatives, such as the Paris Agreement, have brought attention to the need for interdisciplinary research addressing both climate change mitigation and health outcomes. This shift in focus is evident in the research community’s increased interest in understanding the broader, long-term implications of PM2.5 exposure in the context of global environmental changes. The alignment of scientific research with global policy priorities likely explains the rise of “climate change” as a key research theme in recent years.

In summary, our study not only corroborates well-established findings in PM2.5 and asthma research but also highlights emerging areas of interest, such as the importance of indoor air quality, the global burden of air pollution, and the interplay between climate change and public health. These insights suggest that future research will increasingly focus on both the environmental and societal determinants of air pollution-related diseases, particularly in vulnerable populations such as children and individuals living in LMICs.

Limitations

While this study provides a comprehensive overview of the research landscape on PM2.5 and asthma, there are several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the analysis was based solely on publications indexed in the WoS database. While WoSCC includes the majority of high-impact journals and overlaps significantly with other major databases like PubMed and Scopus, it may exclude certain specialized or regional journals. As a result, some relevant studies could have been missed. Future studies should consider integrating multiple databases to ensure more comprehensive coverage of the research landscape.

Second, bibliometric analyses focus on citation and publication metrics, which may not always reflect the actual impact of a study on clinical practice or policy-making. Citation counts can be influenced by various factors, including journal visibility and network effects, rather than the intrinsic quality or practical relevance of the research. Therefore, it is important to complement bibliometric studies with more qualitative assessments of how research findings are being translated into real-world interventions.

Thrid, this study did not identify many published articles on aerosol optical depth (AOD) in the context of fine PM and asthma, which represents a limitation of our bibliometric analysis. This omission may stem from the search strategy, which primarily focused on keywords like “PM2.5” and “asthma” and did not explicitly include terms such as “aerosol optical depth” or “AOD.” Furthermore, AOD-related research often emphasizes atmospheric and environmental modeling rather than direct health impacts, which may result in differences in indexing or classification in bibliometric databases. Future studies could refine the search strategy to include AOD-specific keywords, enabling a more comprehensive analysis of this intersecting research area.

Fourth, the limitation of the author co-citation analysis is the potential bias introduced by well-cited review articles. Review articles often aggregate findings from multiple studies and are frequently cited by researchers, which can lead to an overrepresentation of review authors in the co-citation network. While these authors play an influential role in synthesizing and shaping the field, their prominence may overshadow original research contributions from less-cited authors. This bias could affect the perceived centrality of certain authors in the co-citation network and may not fully reflect the diversity of perspectives and contributions in the field. Future analyses could complement co-citation studies with additional metrics, such as direct citation analysis or content-based clustering, to provide a more nuanced understanding of author influence.

Finally, Another limitation of this study is the focus on English-language publications indexed in the WoS. While WoS is a widely used and comprehensive database, its emphasis on English-language journals may exclude relevant studies published in other languages, particularly those from regions where non-English journals are predominant. This linguistic bias could result in an underrepresentation of research from non-English-speaking countries, potentially affecting the global comprehensiveness of the analysis. Future bibliometric studies could address this limitation by incorporating multilingual databases or by applying text-mining techniques to include non-English publications, thereby providing a more inclusive view of the research landscape.

Future Directions

Based on our findings, several future research directions are suggested. First, there is a need for more interdisciplinary studies that integrate environmental science, epidemiology, public health, and policy research to fully understand the health impacts of PM2.5. In particular, studies that explore the intersection of climate change and air pollution are urgently needed, as the effects of climate change are likely to exacerbate air pollution levels in many parts of the world. Such as: (a) Developing region-specific public health interventions to mitigate PM2.5 exposure, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where air quality regulations may be less stringent. (b) Investigating the long-term impacts of early-life exposure to PM2.5 to inform policies aimed at protecting vulnerable populations, such as children and individuals with pre-existing respiratory conditions. (c) Strengthening collaborations between researchers and policymakers to translate scientific findings into actionable air quality standards and urban planning initiatives. (d) Advancing technologies for air pollution monitoring and indoor air quality improvement to reduce overall exposure risks. Second, there is a growing need to focus on vulnerable populations, particularly children and individuals in low-income areas, to better understand the long-term health consequences of early-life exposure to PM2.5.

In addition, further research on indoor air quality and the development of effective air filtration technologies will be critical for reducing overall exposure to PM2.5. Since people spend a significant portion of their time indoors, improving indoor air quality may prove to be an effective strategy in mitigating the adverse health effects of air pollution.

Finally, as the research community increasingly acknowledges the global burden of PM2.5, more attention should be paid to public health interventions and policy frameworks that can address air pollution on a large scale. Policymakers must collaborate with researchers to translate scientific findings into effective regulations that can improve air quality and reduce the associated health risks.

Conclusions

This bibliometric and visualized analysis provides a comprehensive overview of the global research landscape on PM2.5 and asthma from 2004 to 2024. By exploring keyword co-occurrence networks, clustering, temporal trends, and citation bursts, we have identified key areas of focus, including the health impacts of particulate matter, mechanisms such as oxidative stress and inflammation, and the emerging importance of indoor air quality. Our findings highlight both well-established themes, such as the role of PM2.5 in respiratory health, and newer areas of investigation, such as the intersection between climate change and air pollution.

The analysis also underscores the increasing attention being paid to vulnerable populations, particularly children and individuals in low- and middle-income countries, as well as the global burden of air pollution. This shift reflects a growing awareness of the need for interdisciplinary approaches that integrate public health, environmental science, and policy research to address the complex challenges posed by PM2.5 exposure.

As the world faces ongoing environmental and public health challenges, particularly those exacerbated by climate change, continued research into the long-term impacts of air pollution on respiratory diseases like asthma will be critical. Future studies should focus not only on outdoor air quality but also on improving indoor air environments, developing more effective air filtration technologies, and addressing the disproportionate burden of air pollution on vulnerable populations. By translating these scientific findings into actionable public health interventions, there is an opportunity to reduce the global burden of air pollution-related diseases and improve health outcomes worldwide.

As environmental health research continues to evolve, future updates to this bibliometric analysis are planned to incorporate the latest developments and trends. These updates will aim to include newly published studies, emerging themes such as the role of novel air pollutants and climate resilience, and advancements in bibliometric methodologies. Additionally, integrating data from multiple databases and leveraging machine learning approaches for text analysis could further enhance the comprehensiveness and accuracy of future analyses. By periodically revisiting this field, we hope to provide ongoing insights that align with current scientific and policy priorities, ensuring the relevance and utility of this research for the broader community.
