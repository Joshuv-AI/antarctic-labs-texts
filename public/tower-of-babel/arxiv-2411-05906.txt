Abstract
The detection of a stochastic gravitational wave background by pulsar timing arrays suggests the presence of a supermassive black hole binary population. Although the observed spectrum generally aligns with predictions from orbital evolution driven by gravitational wave emission in circular orbits, there is a discernible preference for a turnover at the lowest observed frequencies. This turnover could indicate a significant hardening phase, transitioning from early environmental influences to later stages predominantly influenced by gravitational wave emission. In the vicinity of these binaries, the ejection of stars or dark matter particles through gravitational three-body slingshots efficiently extracts orbital energy, leading to a low-frequency turnover in the spectrum. By analyzing the NANOGrav 15-year data, we assess how the gravitational wave spectrum depends on the initial inner galactic profile prior to disruption by binary ejections, accounting for a range of initial binary eccentricities. Our findings suggest a parsec-scale galactic center density around 10 6 ​ M ⊙ / pc 3 \displaystyle 10^{6}\,M_{\odot}/\textrm{pc}^{3} across most of the parameter space, offering insights into the environmental effects on black hole evolution and combined matter density near galaxy centers.



I
Introduction

Recent advances by pulsar timing arrays (PTAs), leveraging precise measurements of timing residuals within a galactic-scale detector, have ushered in a new era of stochastic gravitational wave background (SGWB) detection. The SGWB, defined by a superposition of incoherent gravitational waves (GWs), initially emerged as a common-spectrum process
Arzoumanian et al. 2020
;
Goncharov et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2021
;
Antoniadis et al. 2022
. Subsequent data provided robust evidence of a quadrupolar correlation function
Agazie et al. 2023a
;
Antoniadis et al. 2023
;
Reardon et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2023
, famously known as the Hellings-Downs curve
Hellings and Downs 1983
, further affirming the SGWB’s presence and characteristics.

The observed spectrum of the SGWB is consistent with expectations for a population of supermassive black hole binaries (SMBHBs), dominated by binaries with comparable mass ratios, total masses in the range of
\displaystyle 10^{9.2-10.4}\,M_{\odot}
, and redshifts from
\displaystyle 0.15
to
\displaystyle 0.9

Agazie et al. 2023b
;
Antoniadis et al. 2024
, where
\displaystyle M_{\odot}
represents the solar mass. Although the spectrum is consistent with a steady spectral slope driven by GW emission from circular binaries, it also suggests a low-frequency turnover
Agazie et al. 2023b
;
Ellis et al. 2024
. This feature implies an acceleration in the rate of orbital hardening, offering a potential solution to the final parsec problem
Begelman et al. 1980
;
Milosavljevic and Merritt 2003
. Possibilities include interactions with environmental factors—such as gas
Gould and Rix 2000
;
Armitage and Natarajan 2002
, stars
Quinlan 1996
, and dark matter (DM)
Milosavljevic and Merritt 2001
—and the impact of significant initial orbital eccentricities
Enoki and Nagashima 2007
;
Chen et al. 2017
;
Raidal et al. 2024
.

An intriguing aspect of environmental interactions involves the role of stars and particle DM, which are noted for their potentially high density in galactic centers (GCs)
Navarro et al. 1996
;
Gondolo and Silk 1999
;
Genzel et al. 2010
. Both stars and DM can be expelled from the system through gravitational slingshots during encounters with binary components, thereby extracting orbital energy
Quinlan 1996
. This process involves three-body scattering, where the energy extraction efficiency is significantly higher than that of two-body dynamical friction
Chandrasekhar 1943
, especially when the binary components have comparable masses and are sufficiently close. Such three-body slingshot interactions can substantially alter the density profile of the GC, particularly flattening the inner distribution
Milosavljevic and Merritt 2001
;
Merritt and Milosavljevic 2002
. This underscores the importance of considering the co-evolution of the density profile and the binary orbit. A pivotal study by Ref.
Sesana and Khan 2015
demonstrates that the orbital hardening rate observed in N-body simulations can be effectively approximated by results from scattering experiments
Quinlan 1996
within environments characterized by the GC distribution at the SMBHB influence radius
Frank and Rees 1976
prior to disruption. In this study, we examine the impact of initial GC density profiles and SMBHB eccentricities on the SGWB spectrum, utilizing NANOGrav’s 15-year dataset to constrain the parameter space of these two factors.

II
Binary Hardening by Three-body Scattering

The SGWB emanating from SMBHBs constitutes an incoherent superposition of signals from individual sources. The spectrum of this background is delineated by the characteristic strain,
\displaystyle h_{c}(f)
, as follows
Phinney 2001
;
Enoki and Nagashima 2007
:

h_{c}^{2}(f)=\frac{4G}{c^{2}\pi f}\int{\rm d}z{\rm d}M{\rm d}q\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\eta}{{\rm d}z{\rm d}M{\rm d}q}\frac{{\rm d}E_{\mathrm{GW}}}{{\rm d}f_{s}},

(1)

where
\displaystyle G
represents the gravitational constant, and
\displaystyle c
denotes the speed of light. The GW frequency emitted in the source frame is denoted by
\displaystyle f_{s}
, and
\displaystyle f=f_{s}/(1+z)
, incorporating the redshift factor
\displaystyle z
. The comoving volumetric number density of SMBHBs,
\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{3}\eta/(\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}M\,\mathrm{d}q)
, depends on the redshift
\displaystyle z
, the total binary mass
\displaystyle M
, and the mass ratio
\displaystyle q\leq 1
. The GW emission spectrum from a binary,
\displaystyle\mathrm{d}E_{\mathrm{GW}}/\mathrm{d}\ln f_{s}
, is expressed in the context of a circular orbit as follows
Peters and Mathews 1963
:

\frac{{\rm d}E_{\mathrm{GW}}}{{\rm d}f_{s}}\Big|^{e=0}=-\frac{{\rm d}t}{{\rm d}a}\frac{64}{15}\frac{\pi^{8/3}}{c^{5}}G^{8/3}M^{11/3}f_{s}^{5/3}\frac{q^{2}}{(1+q)^{4}}.

(2)

Here,
\displaystyle a
represents the semi-major axis of the orbit, adhering to Kepler’s law
\displaystyle a^{3}=GM/(2\pi f_{\rm orb})^{2}
, where the orbital frequency
\displaystyle f_{\rm orb}=f_{s}/2
for circular orbits. The orbital hardening process, denoted by
\displaystyle{\rm d}a/{\rm d}t\equiv\Sigma_{i}{\rm d}a/{\rm d}t|_{i}
, with the index
\displaystyle i
encompassing various sources of hardening
Sesana 2010
;
Sampson et al. 2015
;
Kelley et al. 2017a
;
Bortolas et al. 2021
, is crucial for binary evolution. The evolution of the semi-major axis due solely to GW emission in circular orbits is described as follows
Peters 1964
:

\frac{{\rm d}a}{{\rm d}t}\Big|_{\rm GW}^{e=0}=-\frac{64}{5}\frac{G^{3}M^{3}}{c^{5}a^{3}}\frac{q}{(1+q)^{2}},

(3)

leading to a characteristic strain
\displaystyle h_{c}\propto f^{-2/3}
. Eccentric orbits lead to enhanced GW emission over a range of frequencies, as detailed in the Supplemental Material. Consequently, a turnover in the spectrum at lower frequencies is possible before the orbit undergoes circularization through GW emission
Enoki and Nagashima 2007
.

In GCs, SMBHBs form within a background of stars and particle DM, once their separation falls below the influence radius,
\displaystyle r_{i}
. This radius is defined as the distance at which the total enclosed mass of stars and DM equals twice the mass of the SMBHB
Frank and Rees 1976
. Given their substantial mass disparity with the SMBHB, both stars and DM particles act effectively as test particles. Each may undergo multiple gravitational encounters with one of the black holes (BHs) until it gains sufficient kinetic energy to be ejected. This three-body slingshot process becomes efficient as the semi-major axis
\displaystyle a
approaches the hardening radius, defined as
\displaystyle a_{h}\equiv r_{i}q/(4(1+q)^{2})

Quinlan 1996
. The rate of orbital hardening due to three-body scattering (3BS), averaged over a background of particles with matter density
\displaystyle\rho
and velocity dispersion
\displaystyle\sigma
, is given by
Quinlan 1996
:

\frac{{\rm d}a}{{\rm d}t}\Big|_{\text{3BS}}=-HG\frac{\rho}{\sigma}a^{2},

(4)

where
\displaystyle H
is a dimensionless coefficient typically ranging from
\displaystyle 15
to
\displaystyle 20
, as observed in scattering experiments
Quinlan 1996
. It is important to note that three-body scattering is fundamentally distinct from two-body dynamical friction
Chandrasekhar 1943
;
Ghoshal and Strumia 2024
;
Shen et al. 2023
;
Aghaie et al. 2024
;
Hu et al. 2023
and becomes more dominant after entering the hardening radius. Assuming a constant
\displaystyle\rho/\sigma
ratio over time, the spectral evolution for circular orbits follows
\displaystyle h_{c}\propto f
. Note that three-body scatterings tend to increase the eccentricity on average
Quinlan 1996
, in contrast to the effects of GW emission.

Historically, the three-body slingshot process was thought to result in stalled orbital evolution by expelling background stars and depleting a specific phase-space region known as the loss cone. This led to what is known as the final parsec problem
Begelman et al. 1980
. However, N-body simulations
Khan et al. 2011
;
Preto et al. 2011
;
Khan et al. 2012
have shown that merger-induced triaxiality can efficiently repopulate the loss cone
Merritt and Poon 2004
.

These simulations also reveal that two initial DM profiles, each centered around a BH with peak densities at the center, will merge and flatten into a single core profile following the merger
Milosavljevic and Merritt 2001
;
Merritt and Milosavljevic 2002
;
Harris and Gültekin 2023
. This underscores the need for comprehensive simulations that simultaneously address the co-evolution of the SMBHB orbit and the density profile. Further analysis comparing results from scattering experiments
Quinlan 1996
with N-body simulation outcomes
Khan et al. 2012
demonstrates that predictions of orbital evolution can closely align with Eq. (
4
), assuming that
\displaystyle\rho/\sigma
remains constant, determined by the initial profile at the SMBHB’s influence radius,
\displaystyle r_{i}

Sesana and Khan 2015
. This statement is supported by observations that the total mass ejected during SMBHB evolution is approximately of the order of
\displaystyle M

Quinlan 1996
;
Merritt 2000
;
Milosavljevic and Merritt 2001
;
Celoria et al. 2018
, primarily distributed within the influence radius at the onset. During simulations, the loss cone at this radius remains fully populated, driven by the efficient diffusion of particles.

Galaxy Tomography

IV
Discussion

In the vicinity of a binary BH, a test particle can extract orbital energy through multiple scatterings with each BH component. Within the PTA observation band, the three-body ejection process is significantly more efficient than two-body dynamical friction, particularly for comparable-mass binaries, leading to pronounced backreaction effects on the surrounding matter distribution. In this study, we explore the potential imprints that stars or particle DM surrounding SMBHBs could leave on the spectrum of the SGWB. Utilizing data from NANOGrav’s 15-year observation, we constrain the relevant parameter space. Our results support the existence of three-body scatterings with a reasonable density distribution prior to disruption (see the left panel of Fig.
2
), primarily inferred from the low-frequency turnover in the SGWB spectrum, assuming no other dominant environmental influences are present.
Continued monitoring with existing PTAs will provide improved constraints on the low-frequency turnover.
Future observations from the Square Kilometre Array
Weltman et al. 2020
and next-generation astrometry missions
Vallenari 2018
are expected to provide significantly more precise spectral information
Çalışkan et al. 2024
, potentially elucidating the turnover.

Another potential source of environmental effects is a circumbinary disk around the SMBHB. If the GCs hosting SMBHBs are gas-rich and the binaries are accreting at the Eddington rate, the gas-driven hardening rate (conservatively choosing large values from Ref.
Tiede and D’Orazio 2023
), could be comparable to three-body scatterings at separations below
\displaystyle 0.1
pc for
\displaystyle M\sim 10^{10}\,M_{\odot}
(
\displaystyle 2
nHz).
However, at these separations and lower the binary will decouple from the disk
Dittmann et al. 2023
, thus mitigating disk-driven hardening. Furthermore, SMBHBs of this mass cannot be surrounded by gravitationally stable thin disks until closer to merger and massive self-gravitating disks must be employed
Haiman et al. 2009
;
Roedig and Sesana 2014
;
Franchini et al. 2021
.

To precisely determine the density profile, resolving the degeneracy with the initial eccentricity is essential. Potential strategies to determine SMBHB eccentricity include resolving individual binaries through either GW or electromagnetic observations
Ayzenberg et al. 2023
;
D’Orazio and Charisi 2023
and examining correlations among different frequency bins. This latter method is based on the observation that high eccentricity contributes to multiple integer multiples of frequency bins simultaneously
Raidal et al. 2024
. Nevertheless, setting a stringent upper limit on the galactic center density distribution is feasible, as both additional environmental influences and nonzero orbital eccentricity tend to further elevate the turnover frequency.

The three-body slingshot mechanism considered in this study assumes that the test particles interact purely gravitationally, thus applying to both stars and cold, collisionless DM. If DM is the dominant density, the findings here could provide crucial insights into two longstanding questions: the identification of the particle nature of DM and the measurement of its density at galactic centers. Further investigations are needed to determine how DM models that extend beyond the test particle hypothesis—such as wave-like DM
Aghaie et al. 2024
or self-interacting DM
Alonso-Álvarez et al. 2024
;
Dutra et al. 2024
—interact with binaries, particularly in terms of the applicability of three-body ejection or two-body dynamical friction. Notably, wave-like DM exhibits distinct behaviors depending on the binary separation scale and the boson mass
Ikeda et al. 2021
;
Broadhurst et al. 2023
;
Koo et al. 2024
;
Bromley et al. 2024
;
Aurrekoetxea et al. 2024a
;
Aurrekoetxea et al. 2024b
. For a star-dominant distribution, such findings could reveal the star formation and relaxation rates near SMBHs in the galactic center, suggesting a density higher than previously expected
Kelley et al. 2017b
;
Chen et al. 2017
.

V
Evolution of Eccentric Orbits

We investigate the orbital evolution of supermassive black hole binaries (SMBHBs) driven by both three-body ejection of stars and dark matter (DM), and gravitational wave (GW) emission. In addition to the formalism presented in the main text, our analysis incorporates eccentric orbits with evolving eccentricity. The coupled equations that govern the semi-major axis
\displaystyle a
and eccentricity
\displaystyle e
are as follows:

\begin{split}\frac{{\rm d}a(e,a)}{{\rm d}t}=&-\frac{64}{5}\frac{G^{3}M^{3}}{c^{5}a^{3}}\frac{q}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{(1+\frac{73}{24}e^{2}+\frac{37}{96}e^{4})}{(1-e^{2})^{7/2}}-HG\frac{\rho_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}a^{2},\\
\frac{{\rm d}e(e,a)}{{\rm d}t}=&-\frac{304}{15}\frac{G^{3}M^{3}}{c^{5}a^{4}}\frac{q}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{e(1+\frac{121}{304}e^{2})}{(1-e^{2})^{5/2}}+HK(e,a)G\frac{\rho_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}a.\end{split}

(S1)

The first terms on the right-hand side of these equations correspond to GW emission
Peters and Mathews 1963
, while the second terms describe the effects of three-body ejections
Quinlan 1996
. Here,
\displaystyle G
denotes the gravitational constant,
\displaystyle M
the total mass of the SMBHB,
\displaystyle q\leq 1
the mass ratio of its components, and
\displaystyle\rho_{i}
and
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}
the matter density and velocity dispersion at the SMBHB influence radius
\displaystyle r_{i}
, respectively. The dimensionless parameters
\displaystyle H
and
\displaystyle K
represent the hardening and eccentricity growth rates, respectively, derived from scattering experiments. For hard binaries,
\displaystyle H
values typically range from
\displaystyle 15
to
\displaystyle 20

Quinlan 1996
; in this study, we have fixed
\displaystyle H
at
\displaystyle 18
. The function for
\displaystyle K
is approximated by
Quinlan 1996
;
Sesana et al. 2006
;
Vasiliev et al. 2015
;
Fastidio et al. 2024
:

K(e,a)\approx 0.3e\left(1-e^{2}\right)^{0.6}\left(1+\frac{a}{0.2\,a_{h}}\right)^{-1},

(S2)

which becomes most effective when
\displaystyle a
is within the hardening radius
\displaystyle a_{h}\equiv r_{i}q/(4(1+q)^{2})
.

Each evolutionary case begins at the influence radius,
\displaystyle r_{i}
, determined by solving the relation
\displaystyle\textstyle\int_{0}^{r_{i}}\!\rho\,4\pi r^{2}dr=2M

Frank and Rees 1976
for a power-law distribution
\displaystyle\rho(r)=\rho_{\rm pc}(r/1\,\text{pc})^{-\gamma}
prior to disruption. Consequently,
\displaystyle\rho_{i}\equiv\rho(r_{i})
and
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}\equiv\sigma(r_{i})=\sqrt{GM/r_{i}}
are calculated. The initial eccentricity,
\displaystyle e_{0}
, defined at
\displaystyle r_{i}
, serves as a fit parameter to be constrained.

We vary both the initial eccentricity,
\displaystyle e_{0}
, and the density profile parameters
\displaystyle(\rho_{\rm pc},\gamma)
. We then calculate the orbital evolution for the SMBHB with parameters
\displaystyle(M,q)
from the determined
\displaystyle r_{i}
. The stochastic gravitational wave background (SGWB) spectrum is subsequently calculated by integrating over the SMBHB population parameters
\displaystyle(M,q,z)
, considering the density distribution
\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{3}\eta/(\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}M\,\mathrm{d}q)
, as referenced in
Phinney 2001
;
Enoki and Nagashima 2007
;
Huerta et al. 2015
:

h_{c}^{2}(f)=\frac{4G}{c^{2}\pi f}\int{\rm d}z{\rm d}M{\rm d}q\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\eta}{{\rm d}z{\rm d}M{\rm d}q}\frac{{\rm d}E_{\mathrm{GW}}}{{\rm d}f_{s}}\Big|_{f_{s}=(1+z)f}.

(S3)

In this calculation, we neglect Poisson fluctuations in the SMBHB distribution, which have a minor impact on the lowest frequency bins of the NANOGrav
\displaystyle 15
-year dataset
Sesana et al. 2008
;
Agazie et al. 2023c
;
Lamb and Taylor 2024
;
Sato-Polito and Zaldarriaga 2024
;
Xue et al. 2024
.

The GW emission spectrum,
\displaystyle\mathrm{d}E_{\mathrm{GW}}/\mathrm{d}\ln f_{s}
, calculated at the source frame frequency
\displaystyle f_{s}
, includes contributions from various orbital frequencies
\displaystyle f_{\rm orb}^{n}=f_{s}/n
for integer
\displaystyle n>0
. It is expressed as
Peters and Mathews 1963
:

\frac{{\rm d}E_{\mathrm{GW}}}{{\rm d}f_{s}}\Big|_{f_{s}=(1+z)f}=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{{\rm d}E_{\mathrm{GW}}^{n}/{\rm d}t}{n{\rm d}f_{\rm orb}^{n}/{\rm d}t}\Big|_{f_{\rm orb}^{n}=(1+z)f/n},

(S4)

where

\begin{aligned} \frac{{\rm d}E_{\mathrm{GW}}^{n}}{{\rm d}t}&=\frac{32G^{4}M^{5}}{5c^{5}a^{5}}\frac{q^{2}}{(1+q)^{4}}g(n,e),\\
\frac{{\rm d}f_{\rm orb}^{n}}{{\rm d}t}&=-\frac{3\sqrt{GM}}{4\pi a^{5/2}}\frac{{\rm d}a}{{\rm d}t}.\end{aligned}.

(S5)

The orbital frequency is related to
\displaystyle a
via Kepler’s law
\displaystyle a^{3}=GM/(2\pi f_{\rm orb}^{n})^{2}
. The function
\displaystyle g(n,e)
, defined as

\displaystyle g(n,e)

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{4}}{32}\bigg[\Big\{J_{n-2}(ne)-2eJ_{n-1}(ne)+\frac{2}{n}J_{n}(ne)+2eJ_{n+1}(ne)-J_{n+2}(ne)\Big\}^{2}

(S6)

\displaystyle+\left(1-e^{2}\right)\Big\{J_{n-2}(ne)-2J_{n}(ne)+J_{n+2}(ne)\Big\}^{2}+\frac{4}{3n^{2}}J_{n}^{2}(ne)\bigg],

converges to
\displaystyle g(2,0)=1
for circular orbits. Here,
\displaystyle J_{n}
denotes the Bessel function of the first kind of order
\displaystyle n
.

In practice, we employ several methods to enhance the efficiency of numerical computation. In Eq. (
S4
), we apply a cutoff to the summation over
\displaystyle n
, neglecting all contributions for
\displaystyle n>n_{\rm max}
, with
\displaystyle n_{\rm max}=3n_{\rm peak}(e_{\rm max})
. The value of
\displaystyle n_{\rm peak}(e)
is given by
Hamers 2021
:

n_{\rm peak}(e)\approx 2\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{4}c_{k}e^{k}\right)\,(1-e^{2})^{-3/2},

(S7)

where
\displaystyle c_{1}=-1.01678
,
\displaystyle c_{2}=5.57372
,
\displaystyle c_{3}=-4.9271
, and
\displaystyle c_{4}=1.68506
and
\displaystyle e_{\rm max}
represents the maximum eccentricity throughout the orbital evolution. Additionally, when
\displaystyle n_{\rm max}
exceeds
\displaystyle 100
, we sum the contributions for
\displaystyle n>101
using logarithmic steps (i.e.,
\displaystyle n=101,102,104,108,\dots
) and implement numerical integration over
\displaystyle n
using
numpy.trapz
.

VI
Supermassive Black Hole Binary Population

This section details the semi-analytic SMBHB population model utilized in this study, characterized by the comoving volumetric number density of SMBHBs,
\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{3}\eta/(\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}M\,\mathrm{d}q)
, alongside astrophysical priors on its normalization. To generate the initial SMBHB population before binary evolution, we use the
holodeck
code, following NANOGrav’s astrophysical interpretation paper
Agazie et al. 2023b
. However, we replaced the phenomenological environmental prescription
Sampson et al. 2015
;
Agazie et al. 2023b
;
Ellis et al. 2024
;
Saeedzadeh et al. 2024
;
Sah et al. 2024
with the three-body scattering prescription detailed in Sec.
V
.

The semi-analytic model is predicated on galaxy merger rates, the SMBH-host galaxy relationship, and cosmological expansion, and is parameterized as follows
Sesana 2013
;
Sesana et al. 2016
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}\eta}{\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}M\,\mathrm{d}q}=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}\eta_{\text{gal-gal}}}{\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}m_{\star 1}\,\mathrm{d}q_{\star}}\frac{\mathrm{d}m_{\star 1}}{\mathrm{d}M}\frac{\mathrm{d}q_{\star}}{\mathrm{d}q},

(S8)

where
\displaystyle{\mathrm{d}^{3}\eta_{\text{gal-gal}}}/{(\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}m_{\star 1}\,\mathrm{d}q_{\star})}
represents the volumetric galaxy merger rate density as a function of redshift
\displaystyle z
, the stellar mass of the primary galaxy
\displaystyle m_{\star 1}
, and the mass ratio of the galaxies
\displaystyle q_{\star}\leq 1
. The galaxy merger rate can be expressed
Chen et al. 2019
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}\eta_{\text{gal-gal}}}{\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}m_{\star 1}\,\mathrm{d}q_{\star}}=\frac{\Psi(m_{\star 1},z^{\prime})}{m_{\star 1}\ln(10)}\frac{P(m_{\star 1},q_{\star},z^{\prime})}{T_{\text{gal-gal}}(m_{\star 1},q_{\star},z^{\prime})}\frac{{\rm d}t}{{\rm d}z^{\prime}}.

(S9)

Here,
\displaystyle\Psi(m_{\star 1},z’)
represents the galaxy stellar mass function (GSMF),
\displaystyle P(m_{\star 1},q_{\star},z’)
denotes the galaxy pair fraction (GPF), and
\displaystyle T_{\text{gal-gal}}(m_{\star 1},q_{\star},z’)
refers to the galaxy merger timescale (GMT). The variable
\displaystyle z’
is defined as the advanced redshift satisfying
\displaystyle t(z)-t(z’)=T_{\text{gal-gal}}(z’)
, with the current epoch at
\displaystyle t(0)=13.79
Gyr. The rate of change in cosmic time with respect to redshift,
\displaystyle{\rm d}t/{\rm d}z
, is calculated as
\displaystyle 1/((1+z)H(z))
, following the standard cosmological model where the Hubble parameter
\displaystyle H(z)=H_{0}\left[\Omega_{\Lambda}+(1+z)^{3}\Omega_{m}\right]^{1/2}
with
\displaystyle H_{0}=67.4\,\mathrm{km}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1},\Omega_{m}=0.315
, and
\displaystyle\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.685
. The terms
\displaystyle{\mathrm{d}m_{\star 1}}/{\mathrm{d}M}
and
\displaystyle{\mathrm{d}q_{\star}}/{\mathrm{d}q}
are derived from the SMBH-host relation.

Below, we detail the relevant parameters in the semi-analytic model:

•

Galaxy Stellar Mass Function (GSMF)
\displaystyle\Psi(m_{\star 1},z)
: This function describes the distribution of primary galaxy masses
\displaystyle m_{\star 1}
at different redshifts
\displaystyle z
, following the Schechter function
Schechter 1976
:

\Psi(m_{\star 1},z)=\ln(10)\Psi_{0}\left(\frac{m_{\star 1}}{M_{\psi}}\right)^{\alpha_{\psi}}\exp\left(-\frac{m_{\star 1}}{M_{\psi}}\right),

(S10)

where

\displaystyle\log_{10}\left(\Psi_{0}/\mathrm{Mpc}^{-3}\right)

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}+\psi_{z}\cdot z,

(S11)

\displaystyle\log_{10}\left(M_{\psi}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot}\right)

\displaystyle=m_{\psi,0}+m_{\psi,z}\cdot z,

\displaystyle\alpha_{\psi}

\displaystyle=1+\alpha_{\psi,0}+\alpha_{\psi,z}\cdot z.

•

Galaxy Pair Fraction (GPF)
\displaystyle P(m_{\star 1},q_{\star},z)
: This parameterizes the relative number of observable galaxy pairs to total galaxies, following
Chen et al. 2019
:

P(m_{\star 1},q_{\star},z)=P_{0}\left(\frac{m_{\star 1}}{10^{11}M_{\odot}}\right)^{\alpha_{p}}(1+z)^{\beta_{p}}q_{\star}^{\gamma_{p}},

(S12)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{p,0}+\alpha_{p,z}\cdot z,

(S13)

\displaystyle\gamma_{p}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{p,0}+\gamma_{p,z}\cdot z.

•

Galaxy Merger Timescale (GMT)
\displaystyle T_{\text{gal-gal}}(m_{\star 1},q_{\star},z)
: This provides the duration for two galaxies to merge, expressed as
Chen et al. 2019
:

T_{\text{gal-gal}}(m_{\star 1},q_{\star},z)=T_{0}\left(\frac{m_{\star 1}}{10^{11}M_{\odot}/h}\right)^{\alpha_{t}}(1+z)^{\beta_{t}}q_{\star}^{\gamma_{t}},

(S14)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t,0}+\alpha_{t,z}\cdot z,

(S15)

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{t,0}+\gamma_{t,z}\cdot z.

•

SMBH–host relation: This assumes a one-to-one correspondence between galaxy pairs and SMBHBs as per Ref.
Agazie et al. 2023b
, expressed as

\log_{10}(M_{\text{BH}}/M_{\odot})=\mu+\alpha_{\mu}\log_{10}\left(\frac{M_{\text{bulge}}}{10^{11}M_{\odot}}\right)+\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon_{\mu}),

(S16)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon_{\mu})
denotes a normal distribution accounting for scatter with a mean of zero and standard deviation
\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mu}
in dex. The bulge mass is calculated as a fraction of the total galaxy stellar mass,
\displaystyle M_{\text{bulge}}=f_{\star,\text{bulge}}\cdot m_{\star 1}
, with
\displaystyle f_{\star,\text{bulge}}=0.615

Lang et al. 2014
;
Bluck et al. 2014

Statistics

In our data analysis, we estimate the posterior distribution of
\displaystyle\log_{10}\rho_{\rm pc}
,
\displaystyle\gamma
,
\displaystyle e_{0}
and
\displaystyle\log_{10}N
. This can be separated into two components:

\displaystyle P\left(\log_{10}\frac{\rho_{\rm pc}/10^{5}}{M_{\odot}/{\rm pc}^{3}},\gamma,e_{0},\log_{10}N|\bm{\delta}t\right)

(S17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\prod_{k}{\rm d}\log_{10}h_{c}^{2}(f_{k})\,P\left(\log_{10}\frac{\rho_{\rm pc}/10^{5}}{M_{\odot}/{\rm pc}^{3}},\gamma,e_{0},\log_{10}N|\{\log_{10}h_{c}^{2}(f_{k})\}\right)\times P\left(\{\log_{10}h_{c}^{2}(f_{k})\}|\bm{\delta t}\right),

where
\displaystyle\bm{\delta t}
represents the timing residual data from the NANOGrav 15-year dataset
Agazie et al. 2023a
, and
\displaystyle f_{k}=k/T_{\rm obs}
denotes the frequencies indexed by
\displaystyle k=1,\,2,\,3,\,4,\,5
, corresponding to the five lowest frequency bins, with
\displaystyle T_{\rm obs}\approx 16
years as the observation time.

The second term in Eq. (
S17
) represents the posterior distributions of the free spectrum GWs derived by the NANOGrav collaboration
Agazie et al. 2023b
. The first term is calculated using Bayes’ theorem:

\displaystyle P\left(\log_{10}\frac{\rho_{\rm pc}/10^{5}}{M_{\odot}/{\rm pc}^{3}},\gamma,e_{0},\log_{10}N|\{\log_{10}h_{c}^{2}(f_{k})\}\right)
