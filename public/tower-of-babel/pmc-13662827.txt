Gut Microbiota‐Dependent Tryptophan Metabolism Drives the Therapeutic Effects of Baitouweng Tang in Colitis via the AhR Pathway
Authors: Chun Niu, Yongli Hua, Jianguo Kang, Fang Li, Baoxia Chen, Rongxia Guo, Yanming Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWith dysregulation of the gut microbiota and its metabolic byproducts playing a pivotal role in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) pathogenesis. Baitouweng Tang (BTWT), a traditional herbal formulation, has long been employed to treat gastrointestinal disorders and shows promising potential in the management of IBD. However, its precise pharmacological mechanisms remain elusive. In this study, we employed targeted metabolomics, high‐throughput sequencing, and molecular biology techniques to elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms of BTWT in the treatment of ulcerative colitis (UC). BTWT significantly enhanced the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium, improved the indole metabolic pathway of tryptophan, and restored the levels of indole metabolites, such as indole‐3‐lactic acid (ILA). These changes activated the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and led to marked improvements in the clinical symptoms of UC in mice. Conversely, the therapeutic effects of BTWT were substantially diminished in an antibiotic‐induced gut microbiota depletion model. Notably, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) partially restored BTWT efficacy, underscoring the critical role of the gut microbiota in mediating its therapeutic effects. Furthermore, in an in vitro lipoteichoic acid (LTA)‐induced HT‐29 inflammation model, ILA treatment attenuated inflammation and concurrently increased the expression of AhR, CYP1A1, and CYP1B1. These findings suggest a potential link between BTWT‐induced changes in tryptophan metabolites, AhR pathway activation, and the amelioration of colitis, though the causal relationship remains to be established. Our study provides a basis for further mechanistic investigation into the AhR pathway as a possible therapeutic target in UC.Keywords: AhR, Baitouweng Tang, colitis, gut microbiota, indole‐3‐lactic acid

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a leading form of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) (Kobayashi et al. 2020). The pathogenesis of UC is multifactorial and complex and is believed to involve factors such as intestinal dysbiosis, dysfunction of the intestinal epithelial barrier, impaired immune response, and genetic predisposition. These factors are closely associated with inflammation, which is characterized by excessive activity, clinical remission, and a tendency for relapse. The World Health Organization (WHO) categorizes ulcerative colitis as a contemporary, treatment‐resistant condition (Rosen et al. 2015; Ungaro et al. 2017; Yoshida et al. 2015). UC begins with rectal mucosal membrane inflammation, extends proximally, accumulates in the colonic mucosa and submucosa, and is a persistent diffuse inflammation with typical clinical symptoms of abdominal pain, diarrhea, and bloody stools (Feuerstein et al. 2019). Currently, drugs commonly used to treat UC are 5‐aminosalicylic acid, cortisol, glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, etc. However, prolonged use of these drugs frequently leads to a range of adverse reactions and side effects (Grossberg et al. 2022; Guo et al. 2022; Li, Wang, Ma, et al. 2022; Magro et al. 2020; Niu et al. 2021). Therefore, the search for alternative drugs with fewer adverse reactions and side effects is a promising strategy, which is of great significance in treating UC. Chinese herbal medicines are derived primarily from plants. It has many targets, definite curative effects, low recurrence rates, and fewer toxic side effects. Its unique advantages are evident in UC (Liu et al. 2022; Shen et al. 2019).

Baitouweng Tang (BTWT) is a formula from Zhang Zhongjing's Shanghai Lun, which consists of four herbs Pulsatilla chinensis (Bunge) Regel (Bai Tou Weng), Phellodendron chinense C. K. Schneid. (Huang Bai), and Coptis chinensis Franch. [Ranunculaceae] (Huang Lian) and Fraxinus chinensis Roxb. [Oleaceae] (Qin Pi) is known for its ability to cool the blood and stop diarrhea. BTWT is used as the main treatment for febrile dysentery, anal burning, and dysentery with pus and blood and commonly applied in medical settings to treat various gastrointestinal disorders, which is a typical representative formula for the treatment of UC (Liu et al. 2020; Niu et al. 2023). Despite this, the precise pharmacodynamic pathways remain incompletely characterized. Extensive research has revealed that the gut microbiome is a key player in both the onset and advancement of ulcerative colitis, with microbial imbalances frequently triggering this inflammatory condition (Ni et al. 2017; Yu 2018). Small‐molecule metabolites produced by gut flora metabolism, including bile acids, short‐chain fatty acids, and tryptophan metabolites, are intermediates in coordinating microbial‐host interactions and play a vital role in preserving gut immunity and epithelial barrier integrity (Lavelle and Sokol 2020). Alterations in the levels of these small molecules produced by gut flora metabolism are associated with the occurrence of UC (Lloyd‐Price et al. 2019). The modified tryptophan metabolic changes seen in IBD could significantly contribute to the disease's pathogenesis (Agus et al. 2018). Many small molecules produced by the metabolism of tryptophan by the gut microbiota are endogenous ligands for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), a signaling molecule for the activation of AhR. The metabolic levels of these small molecules are altered in UC changed (Gasaly et al. 2021; Yang and Cong 2021). Normally, AhR remains dormant in the cytoplasm, where it forms a complex with HSP90, XAP2 (X‐Associated Protein‐2), and p23, the chaperone protein of HSP90. Upon binding to its ligand, the AhR‐HSP90 complex is activated, leading to a conformational change that results in the release of HSP90. This activation causes the AhR receptor to translocate to the nucleus, where it interacts with the xenobiotic response element and initiates the transcriptional regulation of downstream target genes (Li, Wang, Su, et al. 2022). AhR activation induces the expression of downstream genes such as interleukin‐22 (IL‐22), thereby maintaining intestinal homeostasis (Sun et al. 2020). Recent studies have found that BTWT promotes tryptophan metabolism by promoting DOPA decarboxylase and alleviates ulcerative colitis (Zhang, Lin, et al. 2024). In addition, BTWT promotes the enrichment of Trp metabolite IPA derived from microflora, inhibits RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL signaling pathway, inhibits the necrosis of intestinal epithelial cells and improves barrier function to alleviate colitis (Hu et al. 2025). Therefore, we speculate that BTWT may alleviate colitis by regulating intestinal microbiota and related tryptophan metabolism to activate AhR.

Reagents and Methods

Preparation of BTWT

Bai Tou Weng, Huang Bai, Huang Lian, and Qin Pi were purchased from the Yellow River Herb Market in Lanzhou, Gansu. Bai Tou Weng, Huang Bai, Huang Lian, and Qin Pi were weighed accurately according to the ratio of 4:3:2:4 and extracted by refluxing with distilled water three times, each time for 1 h. The amount of water added was 10, 8, and 8 times the amount of raw herbs, filtered, combined with the filtrate obtained from the three extractions, decompression concentration, partially stored at 4°C, and partially freeze‐dried.

BTWT Composition Analysis

Approximately 100 mg of the sample was weighed, 1 mL of aqueous solution (with mixed internal standard, 4 μg/mL L‐2‐chlorophenylmalonic acid) was added, vortexed, and shaken, and then steel beads were added, and then pre‐cooled in the refrigerator at −40°C for 2 min, and then put into the grinder for grinding (60 HZ, 2 min); the extraction was performed by ultrasound in an ice‐water bath for 60 min. The sample was extracted by ultrasonication (60 HZ, 2 min) in an ice‐water bath for 60 min, and then left at −40°C for 30 min. The sample was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C and diluted 10‐fold with methanol (containing 4 μg/mL L‐2‐chlorobenzene propanoic acid as the internal standard). Then, 200 μL of the supernatant was transferred into a tube‐lined LC–MS injection vial for analysis.

Experimental Animal

Male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center, Lanzhou Institute of Veterinary Medicine, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences under license No. SCXK (Gan) 2022–0002. The mice were provided with ad libitum water and standard chow, and were housed at a temperature of 22°C ± 1°C, humidity of 50% ± 5%, and a 12‐h light/12‐h dark cycle. The animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Gansu Agricultural University and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (License Approval No.: GSAU‐Eth‐VMC‐2023‐041), and the welfare of the experimental animals was ensured to the maximum extent possible during the experiments.

Establishment of a Pseudo‐Germ‐Free Mouse Model

C57BL/6 mice were acclimatized for 1 week, after which they were randomly assigned to a control group (K) or a mixed antibiotic‐treated model group (ABX), with 10 mice in each group. The ABX group was gavaged once a day in the morning and once in the evening with a mixture of antibiotics (Vancomycin 0.05 g/kg, Ampicillin 0.1 g/kg, Metronidazole 0.1 g/kg, and Neomycin sulfate 0.1 g/kg, purchased from Shanghai McLean Ltd.) for 10 consecutive days. These methods have been reported in previous studies and have been modified according to the actual situation (Zarrinpar et al. 2018). Mice were euthanized on day 11, and cecal contents were collected for 16SrRNA sequencing analysis.

Establishment of a Mouse Model of Colitis and Intervention With BTWT

At the end of the acclimatization period, the mice were randomly assigned to the control (CON), colitis (UC), Baitouweng Tang high dose (BTWT‐H), Baitouweng Tang medium dose (BTWT‐M), Baitouweng Tang low dose (BTWT‐L), and salazosulfapyridine (SASP) positive groups, with ten mice in each group. The control group received distilled water ad libitum daily, while mice in all other groups were given 2.5% DSS ad libitum for seven consecutive days to establish an acute UC model. CON and UC were intervened by intragastric administration of distilled water every day, and BTWT high, medium, and low dose groups were intervened by intragastric administration of 8.1 g/kg, 5.4 g/kg, and 2.7 g/kg BTWT every day.

For the UC model after antibiotic depletion, on the basis of the established microbial depletion model mice, the colitis model (ABX‐UC) was established by giving 2.5% DSS for seven consecutive days; on this model, Baitouweng Tang intervention (ABX‐BTWT) was given, with ten mice in each group. After the experiment, the required samples were collected for subsequent analysis.

Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT)

C57BL/6 mice were divided into the control (Control), UC transplantation (FMT‐UC), and BTWT transplantation (FMT‐BTWT) groups. Before FMT transplantation, recipient mice were used to deplete intestinal microorganisms according to the method described above (intragastric administration of mixed antibiotics). Fresh feces from donor mice were collected daily, dissolved in saline at a 1:10 (w/v) ratio, vortexed for 1 min, and then centrifuged at 500 g for 3 min to remove particulate matter. The resulting supernatant was gavaged (200 μL/mouse) within 10 min, twice daily.

Disease Activity Index (DAI)

DAI calculated and statistically analyzed daily during the animal experiments in reference (Xiao et al. 2024).

Hematoxylin–Eosin Staining

Mouse colon tissues fixed in a 10% formalin solution were routinely dehydrated, embedded, sectioned, stained according to the procedure, and scored histopathologically according to the criteria (Xiao et al. 2024).

Alisin Blue‐Periodic Acid Schiff Staining

Mouse colon tissue sections were stained using the Alisin Blue‐Periodic Acid Schiff Staining Kit and counted for changes in goblet cells.

16S rRNA Analysis

Genomic DNA from the microbial community was extracted according to the protocol provided with the E.Z.N.A. Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio‐tek, Norcross, GA, USA). The extracted DNA was used as a template for PCR amplification of the V3‐V4 variable region of the 16S rRNA gene, using the forward primer 338 F (5′‐ACTCCTACGGGAGGGAGGCAGCAG‐3′) and the reverse primer 806 R (5′‐GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT‐3′). High‐throughput 16S rRNA sequencing was performed by Majorbio Bio‐Pharm Technology Co. Ltd., and the data were analyzed using the I‐Sanger Cloud Platform, a free online resource.

Targeted Tryptophan Metabolism Detection

An appropriate amount of the standard substance of the tryptophan metabolic pathway was accurately weighed and a single standard mother liquor was prepared using 50% methanol. An appropriate amount of isotope standard (Trp‐D5) was accurately weighed, a single standard mother liquor was prepared with methanol, and an isotope internal standard working solution with a concentration of 4000 ng/mL was diluted with ultrapure water. Separation was performed using a Nexera Series LC‐40 liquid chromatograph equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.8 μm, Waters Corporation, USA) at 40°C, with an injection volume of 2 μL. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (Phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (Phase B). The chromatographic gradients (including time, flow rate, A% and B%) are shown in Table 1. Mass spectrometry was performed using an AB SCIEX QTRAP 6500+ in both positive and negative mode. The conditions were as follows: Curtain Gas (CUR) at 35, Collision Gas (CAD) set to medium, IonSpray Voltage (IS) at 5500/−4500, Temperature (TEM) at 550°C, Ion Source Gas 1 (GS1) at 50, and Ion Source Gas 2 (GS2) at 50. A linear regression standard curve was generated using the ratio of the analyte's peak area to that of the internal standard as the vertical axis, and the analyte concentration as the horizontal axis. The method validation results are shown in Tables 2 and 3. The calibration curves were linear over the tested ranges (R 2 > 0.99), and the recoveries met the acceptable criteria (85%–115%) as detailed in Tables 2 and 3.

Cell Culture

HT‐29 cells were purchased from Pricella Biotechnology Co. Ltd. and cultured in McCoy's 5A medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 89% medium at 37°C with 5% CO2. After resuscitating the cells, they were continuously passaged thrice and used in the experiment. To model inflammation, the cells reached 80% confluence, were digested with trypsin, and cultured in 6‐well plates. After 24 h, the cells were stimulated with 5 μg/mL LTA for 6 h, followed by treatment with ILA for 12 h. Total protein and RNA were then extracted for analysis.

CCK8 Assay

When HT‐29 cells reached the logarithmic growth phase, trypsin digestion was used to prepare cell suspensions, inoculated in a 96‐well plate at 1 × 105 cells/mL, 100 μL per well, and cultured in adherence to the wall for 24 h, then different concentrations of BTWT, ILA, and LTA were cultured for 6 h, 12 h or 24 h, respectively, and the viability of cells was detected by CCK‐8 reagent.

Quantitative Real‐Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA from mouse colon tissue and cells was extracted using TransZol. The extracted RNA was then reverse‐transcribed into cDNA, which was subsequently amplified and detected by SYBR Green real‐time polymerase chain reaction. The target gene levels were normalized to GAPDH gene levels, and the 2−∆∆Ct method was used to calculate the relative expression of the target genes. The primer sequence is as follows: mouse Ahr: 5′‐ACATCACCTATGCCAGCCG‐3′ and 5′‐GACTTAATTCCTTCAGCGGGGA‐3′; mouse Cyp1a1: 5′‐GGGTTTGACACAGTCACAACT‐3′ and 5′‐GGGACGAAGGATGAATGCCG‐3′; mouse Cyp1b1: 5′‐CACCAGCCTTAGTGCAGACAG‐3′ and 5′‐GAGGACCACGGTTTCCGTTG‐3′; mouse Ifn‐γ: 5′‐CAAGACTGTGATTGCGGGGT‐3′ and 5′‐GACATCTCCTCCCATCAGCA‐3′; mouse Il‐6: 5′‐TTGCCTTCTTGGGACTGATG‐3′ and 5′‐TCATTTCCACGATTTCCCAG‐3′; mouse Il‐22: 5′‐CTTGTGCGATCTCTGATGGCT‐3′ and 5′‐CCAGCATAAAGGTGCGGTTG‐3′; mouse Gapdh: 5′‐GGTTGTCTCCTGCGACTTCA‐3′ and 5′‐TGGTCCAGGGTTTCTTACTCC‐3′; human AhR: 5′‐TTGGTTGTGATGCCAAAGGAAG‐3′ and 5′‐CGGATATGGGACTCGGCAC‐3′; human CYP1A1: 5′‐AGACACAGTGATTGGCAGGTC‐3′ and 5′‐TGTCTCTTGTTGTGCTGTGGG‐3′; human CYP1B1: 5′‐CTCCTCCTCTTCACCAGGTATC‐3′ and 5′‐GGCTGGTCACCCATACAAGG‐3′; human IL‐8: 5′‐CTCCAAACCTTTCCACCCCA‐3′ and 5′‐TTCTCAGCCCTCTTCAAAAACTTC‐3′; human TNF‐α: 5′‐CCCATCTATCTGGGAGGGGT‐3′ and 5′‐GCGTTTGGGAAGGTTGGATG‐3′; human IL‐1β: 5′‐AGGCTGCTCTGGGATTC‐3′ and 5′‐GCCACAACAACTGACGC‐3′; human GAPDH: 5′‐GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT‐3′ and 5′‐GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG‐3′.

Western Blotting

The collected colon tissue of mice was lysed with RIPA lysate to extract total protein. For cellular protein samples, low‐temperature lysis was performed with RIPA lysis buffer according to the manufacturer's instructions, and total protein samples were obtained by centrifugation at 4°C. Protein samples were separated by 10% SDS‐PAGE, and the target proteins were transferred onto a 0.45 μm PVDF membrane, which was then blocked with 5% skimmed milk powder at room temperature, incubated with primary antibody (AhR Monoclonal antibody, 67,785–1‐Ig; CYP1A1 Polyclonal antibody, 13,241–1‐AP; CYP1B1 Monoclonal antibody, 67,033–1‐Ig; Proteintech, China) at 4°C overnight. Following a one‐hour incubation at room temperature with a horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibody, the membrane was subsequently analyzed using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent. For normalization, GAPDH or β‐actin was used as the loading control, depending on the molecular weight of the target protein. The relative protein expression of each target was normalized to the corresponding internal control signal.

Data Analysis

All values are presented as mean values with their corresponding standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences between two independent groups were assessed using unpaired Student's t‐test, while comparisons involving three or more groups were performed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD post hoc test for pairwise multiple comparisons. SPSS version 26.0 was employed for all statistical analyses, with a significance threshold set at p values less than 0.05. For differential abundance analysis of 16S rRNA sequencing data, taxa with significantly different relative abundances between groups were identified using [LEfSe/Wilcoxon rank‐sum test]. p‐values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini‐Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Taxa with FDR‐adjusted q < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Successful Establishment of Microbial Depletion Mouse Model

In this study, a microbial depletion mouse model was established using an antibiotic gavage. The findings of this study indicated that the administration of antibiotics had no significant effect on the body weight changes of the mice (Figure S1A), whereas the spleen weight and gross changes in the cecum of the mice were consistent with the characteristics of the microbial depletion mouse model (Figure S1B,C). Next, we analyzed the 16SrRNA sequencing of mouse cecum contents and found that the α‐diversity (including Chao, Ace, Sob, and Shannon) of the gut microbiota was significantly reduced by antibiotic treatment (Figure S1D1–4). However, the β‐diversity (including PCA, PCoA, and NMDS) (Figure S1E1–3) indicated that the mouse gut microbiota changed significantly after antibiotic treatment compared to that in the control group. At the phylum level (Figure S1F1), the control group had a higher abundance of Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Actinobacteria, whereas the antibiotic‐treated group had only one phylum, Proteobacteria. At the genus level (Figure S1G1), the control group had a high abundance of norank_f _muribaculaceae, Lactobacillus, Lachnospiraceae _nk4a136_group, Coriobacteriaceae_ucg‐002, unclassified_f_ ruminococcaceae, unclassified f lachnospiraceae, and dubosiella, whereas the antibiotic‐treated group had only the more homogeneous Enterobacter and escherichia‐shigella. Figure S1F2,G2 demonstrate the significance of intergroup differences at the phylum and genus levels, respectively, and the differences were highly significant (p < 0.001) for both the phyla and genera illustrated in the Figures. These findings were consistent with those of a pseudo‐bacteria‐free mouse model (Reikvam et al. 2011; Zarrinpar et al. 2018).

BTWT Alleviates the Symptoms of Colitis Mice

Unlike the CON group, the UC model mice exhibited markedly lower body weight but higher DAI scores. High, medium, and low doses of BTWT and SASP could effectively inhibit weight loss (Figure 2A1–2) and decrease DAI score (Figure 2B1–2). Unlike the CON group, the mice with DSS‐induced colitis showed a marked shortening in colon length, whereas both BTWT at varying dosages and SASP managed to curb this shortening to different extents (Figure 2C1–2). Tryptophan Metabolite ILA Attenuates HT‐29 Cell Inflammation Through AhR Pathway Involvement

In order to explore the mechanism of BTWT regulating AhR, we studied the specific effect mechanism of BTWT and ILA on the activation of AhR in intestinal epithelial cells. As shown in Figure 8, BTWT and ILA acted on HT‐29 cells for 12 h (Figure 8A1–2) and 24 h (Figure 8C1–2), respectively, and BTWT did not affect the viability of cells at 640 μg/mL and below. ILA did not affect cell viability at 25 μM and below. Immediately following this, we analyzed the protein expression of AhR, and the results showed that ILA significantly elevated the relative expression of AhR protein after 12 h and 24 h of action, respectively. In contrast, BTWT did not affect the AhR protein expression (Figure 8B,D). We further analyzed the effect of BTWT on the protein expression of AhR target proteins CYP1A1 and CYP1B1 and found that BTWT had no significant effect on the protein expression of CYP1A1 and CYP1B1 (Figure 8E1–3). Therefore, we believe that the ability of BTWT to regulate and activate AhR is attributable to its indirect effect on tryptophan metabolism rather than its direct impact. Discussion

BTWT is a traditional Chinese medicine compound formula with a history of 1800 years and is widely used in clinical practice for the treatment of various gastrointestinal diseases, including diarrhea, bacillary dysentery, and ulcerative colitis (Xiao et al. 2024). The latest research shows that BTWT is a shield against harm to the lining of the colon, and it can also help improve ulcerative colitis triggered by DSS. It does this by tweaking the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway, which in turn encourages autophagy; this process strengthens the junctions between cells (Pan et al. 2024). However, the relationship between BTWT and gut microbiota, gut microbiota tryptophan metabolites, and AhR signaling remains unclear. To investigate the above correlations, the present study induced a UC mouse model with DSS and administered BTWT intervention to investigate the anti‐inflammatory effects of BTWT and its effects on gut microbiota and tryptophan metabolism, and a pseudo‐bacterial mouse model was used to investigate the impact of gut microbiota on the anti‐inflammatory effects of BTWT, and whether BTWT activates the AhR directly was explored on HT‐29 cells in vitro. We found that BTWT regulated the gut microbiota, promoted tryptophan metabolism to activate AhR, and exerted a good anti‐UC effect. Interestingly, in a microbial depletion mouse model, the anti‐UC effect of BTWT was almost completely attenuated, and tryptophan metabolism was blocked, severely inhibiting AhR signaling activation, suggesting that BTWT is dependent on intestinal microbes to exert its anti‐UC effect. BTWT itself did not directly activate AhR; however, the tryptophan indole metabolites it modulated were associated with enhanced AhR expression.

The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in regulating host immunity, metabolism, signaling pathways, and other essential physiological functions, all of which are closely linked to the development of UC (Ni et al. 2017; Yu 2018). An increasing body of evidence suggests that a variety of gut microbiota, such as Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria, are implicated in the onset and progression of UC. Meanwhile, an imbalance in the ratio of anamorphic and Firmicutes, which are the dominant phyla, often contributes to the occurrence of UC, with an increase in the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes and a decrease in the relative abundance of Firmicutes, which are characteristic features of UC development (Gao et al. 2023). The host‐gut flora connection typically exhibits a symbiotic nature, where the host nourishes the gut's microbial community. The gut microbiota has huge metabolic potential, resulting in the production of many metabolic small molecules, such as short‐chain fatty acids, bile acids, and tryptophan metabolites, which are essential for maintaining host health (Lloyd‐Price et al. 2019). Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota causes metabolic pathway disorders leading to a disproportionate ratio of relevant metabolites, which affects host intestinal immunity, damages the intestinal epithelium, and promotes the occurrence and development of UC. In the current research, the sequencing data revealed that in the UC model group, there was a notable rise in the proportion of Bacteroidetes and a corresponding decrease in Firmicutes. However, following the BTWT intervention, this pattern was efficiently flipped. Firmicutes play an irreplaceable role in the maintenance of intestinal health through the production of beneficial metabolites (secondary bile acids, butyrate, and indole metabolites), which are mainly manifested in the enhancement of intestinal immunity and intestinal barrier function (Liu et al. 2021). In addition, in this study, the abundance of Bifidobacterium, Turicibacter, Dubosiella and Alistipes increased after BTWT intervention. Bifidobacterium can guide the differentiation of immature colonic macrophages through metabolic source of indole‐3‐lactic acid, and improve colitis‐associated tumorigenesis (Li et al. 2024). Dubosiella newyorkensis has become a potential target for the treatment of inflammatory bowel diseases by activating the AhR‐IDO1‐Kyn metabolic circuit in dendritic cells and mediating Treg‐mediated immunosuppressive microenvironment to regulate body immunity (Zhang, Tu, et al. 2024). Alistipes onderdonkii enhances the microbial metabolite 5HIAA, thereby activating the AhR signal and effectively improving DSS‐induced colitis (Zhong et al. 2024). Unlike the well‐established protective consensus for Bifidobacterium, Turicibacter exhibits marked context‐dependent functionality. Turicibacter plays a “double‐edged sword” role in colitis and has a dual role in protecting and promoting colitis. The protective effect of Turicibacter is often related to secondary bile acid metabolism (Lynch et al. 2023), whereas its pro‐inflammatory effects are typically associated Th17 responses (Fan et al. 2025). Limited by the inherent limitations of 16S rRNA sequencing in strain resolution, this study cannot determine whether the Turicibacter enriched by BTWT is a protective subtype or a pro‐inflammatory subtype. Therefore, we do not advocate that the efficacy of BTWT is simply attributed to the increased abundance of Turicibacter. On the contrary, we speculate that the overall anti‐colitis effect of BTWT is more likely to be due to the global regulation of the entire intestinal microecosystem and the synergistic change of the metabolite network, rather than the absolute number change of a single taxon.

Small molecules of tryptophan metabolism are intermediates that coordinate microbial‐host interactions and activate networks of related signals (e.g., AhR, GPRs, and PXR) that maintain host health. The findings from our study demonstrated a substantial rise in the amounts of tryptophan‐derived indole metabolites, for example: 3‐lactic acid (ILA), following the BTWT treatment. According to recent findings, supplementing with ILA seemed to shield mice from the ravages of DSS‐induced colitis. The underlying reason appears to be the suppression of glycolysis, NF‐κB, and HIF signaling pathways by ILA, which happens through the activation of AhR. This, in turn, resulted in a decrease in CCL2/7 production within the epithelium and helped tweak the interactions between epithelial and macrophage cells, thus helping to preserve the balance of the intestinal environment (Yu et al. 2023). Although we detected elevated ILA in both feces and serum following BTWT treatment, the local colonic mucosal ILA concentration, which is most relevant to epithelial AhR signaling, was not measured and warrants further investigation. In future studies, we also need to verify the ILA production capacity of specific candidate genera by in vitro culture or aseptic colonization experiments. Emerging research suggests that IAA could help alleviate the clinical manifestations of colitis by kickstarting the ERK signaling pathway, making it a promising avenue for potentially preventing or managing ulcerative colitis (Qu et al. 2024). Indole can inhibit Wnt signaling, inhibit intestinal tumor growth, and protect intestinal health (Wyatt and Greathouse 2021). At the same time, indole can also activate the Nrf2 signaling pathway and promote self‐repair of the intestinal mucosa, thereby reducing the damage caused by harmful substances to the intestine (Li et al. 2023). The results of our study indicate that BTWT effectively alleviates the clinical manifestations of colitis by modulating the microbial composition within the gut and influencing tryptophan metabolism, particularly through ILA and related metabolites, thereby restoring harmony to the intestinal microenvironment.

The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) can be found across diverse cellular populations, spanning immune, epithelial, endothelial, and neuronal cell types. Functioning as a transcription factor that requires ligand activation, AhR typically remains dormant in the cytoplasm until it encounters its binding partner, maintaining this inactive state through its association with molecular chaperones including HSP90, XAP2, and p23. Through the study of AhR gene modification or in vivo experiments with various AhR ligands, it is clear that AhR regulates multiple aspects of gastrointestinal function (Murray and Perdew 2017; Stockinger et al. 2014). When a ligand binds to it, the AhR protein moves into the nucleus, where it gets activated and kicks off the expression of CYP1A1 and CYP1B1 genes while also setting in motion the transcriptional control of various downstream genes (e.g., IL‐22, STAT3, etc.). AhR signaling can also inhibit the stemness program by transcriptionally regulating factors, such as RNF43, an E3 ubiquitin ligase. In addition, ligand‐activated AhR enhances intestinal epithelial cell junctions through increased expression of tight junction proteins (Stockinger et al. 2021). Although tryptophan indole metabolites are mostly natural ligands for AhR and can directly activate AhR, our results revealed that the protein and mRNA levels of Ahr, Cyp1a1, and Cyp1b1 were differentially regulated after BTWT intervention, and the mRNA levels of Il‐22, Il‐6, and Ifn‐γ also showed different degrees of recovery. In addition, in vitro experiments on intestinal epithelial cells showed that within the safe concentration range of BTWT, BTWT did not affect the expression of AhR protein, nor did it affect the expression of its target proteins CYP1A1 and CYP1B1. Surprisingly, the tryptophan metabolite ILA significantly promoted AhR expression within the safe concentration range. Our results showed that BTWT could not directly activate AhR, while BTWT‐regulated metabolite ILA could activate AhR. In the future, we will further explore the activation of AhR, including nuclear translocation immunofluorescence or reporter gene detection. Whether BTWT can affect the AhR transcriptional regulation of RNF43 needs to be explored in future studies.

Research confirms that Chinese medicines' effectiveness hinges on their interaction with gut microbiota. On one hand, the gut microbiota can metabolize the active ingredients of Chinese medicines through hydrolysis and reduction to generate metabolites to improve the efficacy of UC; on the other hand, the active ingredients of Chinese medicines can directly contact the gut microbiota to promote beneficial bacteria, inhibit harmful bacteria, or regulate gut microbiota metabolites such as short‐chain fatty acids, bile acids, and tryptophan, which can play a therapeutic role in the treatment of UC (Zou et al. 2024). In our study, BTWT modulated the structure and diversity of the gut microbiota and regulated tryptophan metabolism in UC mice to exert its pharmacological effects, whereas the pharmacological effects were severely diminished in the antibiotic‐treated pseudo‐bacteriostatic mouse model. However, pathological damage was ameliorated in mice following FMT intervention. The partial restoration observed in the FMT group suggests that the gut microbiota contributes to, but does not fully account for, the therapeutic effects of BTWT. Consequently, our findings indicate that BTWT exerts a dual influence on gut microbiota‐modulating both its structural composition and diversity‐while simultaneously relying on these microbial communities to achieve its pharmacological benefits. These outcomes align closely with previously documented research in this field (Fan et al. 2020). Consequently, our findings indicate that BTWT treatment tipped the scales in favor of Firmicutes while putting the brakes on Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria, leading to a notable uptick in tryptophan‐derived indole metabolites like ILA. This cascade of events, in turn, got the AhR signaling pathway humming and turned down the volume on inflammatory factor expression. This has a protective effect on experimental colitis, a finding that is similar to that reported by Dong et al. (2021). Based on the above, we hypothesize that BTWT, with its chemical composition, interacts with the gut microbiota while affecting tryptophan metabolism and improving the intestinal inflammatory state.

Although this study found a potential microbial‐metabolite‐host interaction axis that may be involved in mediating the therapeutic effect of BTWT on colitis, there are several limitations in this study. First, although our data show a strong correlation between BTWT treatment, AhR pathway upregulation and colitis improvement, the causal relationship remains to be determined. Future studies using AhR antagonists (such as CH‐223191) or siRNA will be of great significance to elucidate whether the protective effect of BTWT is mediated by AhR activation. Second, although our data consistently associate BTWT treatment with gut microbiota remodeling, elevated ILA, AhR pathway upregulation, and colitis amelioration, these findings are correlative rather than causal. We did not perform ILA‐specific loss‐of‐function experiments (e.g., genetic knockout of bacterial ILA synthesis pathways or ILA reconstitution in pseudo‐germ‐free mice) to definitively establish ILA as the primary active metabolite mediating BTWT's protective effects. Third, we acknowledge that the HT‐29/LTA model is a reductionist system that does not fully capture the complex immune‐epithelial interactions in UC. Our in vitro experiments were designed for targeted validation of ILA's direct epithelial effects based on in vivo metabolomics findings, and future studies incorporating immune cells or co‐culture systems are warranted to extend these observations. Fourth, antibiotic‐induced dysbiosis represents an inherent limitation of the microbial depletion model, and future studies using germ‐free mice are required to confirm these findings.

In summary, BTWT treatment was associated with gut microbiota–dependent reprogramming of tryptophan metabolism, increased ILA production, and AhR pathway activation, which correlated with the suppression of colonic inflammation. Critically, FMT partially recapitulated the therapeutic effects, suggesting that the gut microbiota contributes substantially to BTWT's efficacy. These findings establish that the microbiota‐ILA‐AhR axis may be the important mechanism by which BTWT alleviates colitis. Collectively, this work provides a robust mechanistic foundation for the clinical application of BTWT and opens new avenues for developing microbiota‐targeted or metabolite‐based therapeutics for UC.
