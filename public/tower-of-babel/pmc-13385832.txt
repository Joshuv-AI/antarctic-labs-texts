Insulin receptorT1160 phosphorylation mediates renal cortical insulin resistance but not excess gluconeogenesis from glycerol
Authors: Brandon T Hubbard, Yumin Ma, Rafael C Gaspar, Traci E LaMoia, Dongyan Zhang, Mario Kahn, Sylvie Dufour, Ali Nasiri, Gerald I Shulman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImpaired suppression of endogenous glucose production (EGP) drives end-organ damage in insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes. Although the liver is traditionally thought to mediate dysregulated EGP, the role of the renal cortex is less understood. Here, we investigate if high-fat diet (HFD) induces renal cortical insulin resistance while assessing renal glucose production (RGP) and mitochondrial metabolism in male mice. HFD increases plasma membrane sn−1,2-DAGs, PKCε translocation, and Insulin Receptor Kinase (IRK)T1160 phosphorylation while blunting insulin-stimulated pyruvate oxidation and insulin signaling. In HFD mice, RGP is elevated 6.5-fold and accounts for 60% of EGP during hyperinsulinemia. Excess RGP is derived equally from glycerol and mitochondrial sources, chiefly pyruvate. Signaling and flux defects are abrogated in HFD-fed IRKT1150A knockin mice, except for glycerol-derived gluconeogenesis. Our findings implicate the sn−1,2-DAG → PKCε → IRKT1160 axis in renal cortical insulin resistance and highlight renal gluconeogenesis as a driver of dysregulated glucose homeostasis.Subject terms: Type 2 diabetes, Translational research, Energy metabolism

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is one of the most critical global health challenges of the 21st century1. The disorder is a complex metabolic phenotype characterized by multi-tissue insulin resistance and dysregulated glucose homeostasis2. Despite widespread acceptance that the kidney contributes meaningfully to whole body glucose homeostasis3, surprisingly little is known regarding insulin action and insulin resistance in the kidney compared to the liver, skeletal muscle, and white adipose tissue (WAT) in vivo. While renal cortical gluconeogenesis is responsible for all of the glucose production arising from the kidney4–6, it remains unknown what role insulin plays in regulating renal cortical metabolic fluxes, such as gluconeogenesis, mitochondrial anaplerosis, and mitochondrial oxidation; further, it is widely debated whether or not the kidney becomes insulin resistant7,8. As such, the cellular mechanisms and pathophysiology of renal insulin resistance have not been well characterized.

Renal contributions to whole-body endogenous glucose production (EGP) are quite challenging to measure accurately in vivo due to significant technical limitations of the arterio-venous balance technique3; however, there is clear evidence that the renal cortex has significant gluconeogenetic capacity. In studies where glucose-6-phosphatase was deleted in a liver-specific manner, rendering the liver incapable of directly contributing to glucose production, there was no appreciable disruption to whole-body glucose homeostasis which was attributed to compensatory contributions from the renal cortex9. In humans undergoing liver transplantation, whole-body glucose production dropped to 40% of baseline levels in the initial anhepatic period, but climbed steadily to 60% baseline values within one hour10. In a classic study, acutely hepatectomized rabbits did not require additional amount of glucose to maintain euglycemia; instead, it was only where rabbits were hepatectomized and nephrectomized that the glucose infusion requirement increased11. There is a paucity of studies regarding the role of renal glucose production in poorly controlled diabetes, but preliminary reports suggest that it may be increased threefold under basal conditions, and the absolute rate of renal glucose production may account for up to 85% that of the liver12,13. Moreover, postprandial suppression of renal glucose production may be impaired in T2DM14; however, it is unknown if the defect is driven by renal insulin resistance per se or other mechanisms13,15.

In regard to potential molecular mechanisms for renal cortical insulin resistance, it is widely accepted that ectopic lipid accumulation in liver plays an important role in mediating hepatic insulin resistance associated with obesity1,16–18. A leading paradigm suggests that increases in fatty acid flux to insulin responsive tissues results in increased plasma membrane (PM) sn−1,2-diacylglycerols (DAGs), leading to activation of Protein Kinase Cε (PKCε)19,20. Activated PKCε in turn phosphorylates the insulin receptor (IRK) at the amino acid residue Thr1160 (mouse homolog Thr1150), which is situated between two crucial activating tyrosine residues, Tyr1158 and Tyr1162. Phosphorylation at IRKT1160 leads to abrogated insulin signaling in vitro and in vivo19,20. Activation of PKCε also leads to phosphorylation of many other proteins, driving further alterations in insulin signaling nodes downstream of IRK21. This PM sn−1,2-DAG → PKCε → IRKT1160 phosphorylation axis also plays a role in mediating lipid-induced insulin resistance in skeletal muscle and white adipose tissue in both rodents and humans22,23, raising the possibility of a conserved, whole-body mechanism of lipid-induced insulin resistance that may also extend to the renal cortex.

In this work, we, indeed, find that high fat diet feeding causes sn−1,2 DAG accumulation and PKCε translocation and induces renal cortical insulin resistance at the level of the IRK, with downstream defects in insulin signaling and pyruvate oxidation. We, further, find that the renal cortex plays a role in disrupted whole-body endogenous glucose production after high fat feeding, with renal gluconeogenesis accounting for 60% of whole-body EGP during hyperinsulinemia. Excess renal glucose is derived in equal parts from glycerol and mitochondrial sources, chiefly pyruvate. IRKT1150A knockin normalizes renal cortical insulin signaling, pyruvate oxidation, and gluconeogenesis from mitochondrial sources, but not gluconeogenesis from glycerol. These data demonstrate that renal cortical insulin resistance is mediated by an IRKT1160 mechanism. Further, our work shows that elevated renal gluconeogenesis is driven concomitantly by intrarenal insulin signaling defects and extrarenal glycerol delivery, likely secondary to white adipose tissue dysfunction.

Results

High fat feeding induces activation of the plasma membrane sn-1,2 DAG → PKCε → IRKT1160 phosphorylation axis in the renal cortex

Mice fed a HFD for three weeks displayed increased body weight, increased fat mass, and increased basal insulin concentrations after an overnight fast compared to RC mice (Fig. S1A–C). High fat fed mice also displayed a trend towards elevated plasma glucose concentrations and endogenous glucose production (EGP), although this was non-significant (Fig. S1D-E). Mice then underwent a hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (HEC) study, where HFD-fed mice displayed markedly lower steady-state glucose infusion rates and defective suppression of plasma non-esterified fatty acids (NEFAs) (Fig. S1F-G) compared to the RC fed control mice despite higher plasma insulin concentrations at the conclusion of HEC (Fig. S1H). Consistent with these findings we also observed that glycerol turnover was twofold increased in HFD-fed mice compared to RC-fed mice (Fig. 1I). We next evaluated the plasma membrane (PM) sn−1,2 diacylglycerols (DAGs) → PKCε → IRKT1160 phosphorylation axis in the renal cortex and found that HFD feeding resulted in an ~2 fold increase in renal cortical PM sn−1,2 DAGs (Fig. 1A) which was associated with an ~4-fold increase in Protein kinase Cε (PKCε) translocation (Fig. 1B). HFD feeding also slightly (~10%) increased renal cortical ceramide content (Fig. S1J). Consistent with the large increase in PKCε translocation due to increased PM sn−1,2 DAG content we also observed increased inhibitory phosphorylation of the IRK at IRKT1160 (Fig. 1C).

High fat feeding induces renal cortical insulin resistance and disrupts insulin-stimulated renal cortical pyruvate oxidation

We next examined if the inhibitory phosphorylation at IRKT1160 disrupts renal cortical insulin signaling during insulin stimulation. Indeed, we found that HFD feeding induces insulin resistance at the level of the insulin receptor, as reflected by decreased insulin-stimulated IRKY1162 phosphorylation assessed at the end of the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (Fig. 1D). This defect in insulin signaling was also found to be present in downstream effectors of the insulin signaling cascade, including decreased phosphorylation of AKTS473, FOXO1S256, and increased phosphorylation of PDHS232, PDHS293, and PDHS300 (Fig. 1E–G). Next, we applied our previously validated [13C6]glucose tracer technique to assess basal and insulin-stimulated renal cortical pyruvate oxidation, a metabolic flux that, in other tissues, is robustly upregulated by insulin signaling and is susceptible to insulin resistance24–26. In regular chow animals the rate of mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase flux relative to citrate synthase flux (VPDH/VCS) was less than 5%; under hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp conditions, there was a ~ 10-fold increase in pyruvate oxidation, where pyruvate oxidation accounted for ~20% of total rates of mitochondrial oxidation flux (VCS) (Fig. 1H)25. In HFD fed mice, the basal rate of VPDH/VCS was ~10% but failed to increase during HEC, and there was an approximately 50% reduction in the VPDH/VCS ratio between regular chow and high fat fed mice (Fig. 1H).

High fat diet feeding incites persistent renal glucose production

Failed suppression of EGP drives micro- and macrovascular complications in insulin resistance, long before the onset of frank diabetes, but the role of renal gluconeogenesis in dysregulated EGP is unclear. To further investigate this, we applied our recently developed Q-Flux method27 to assess rates of the two principle gluconeogenic reactions, pyruvate carboxylase and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase. In renal cortices of regular chow-fed mice, phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase flux relative to isocitrate dehydrogenase flux (VPEPCK/VIDH) was suppressed by ~55% during the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, VPEPCK/VIDH was not suppressed by insulin during the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp in HFD fed mice (Fig. 2A). Similarly, renal cortical pyruvate carboxylase flux relative to isocitrate dehydrogenase flux (VPC/VIDH) decreased by ~50% upon insulin stimulation in lean mice but was unchanged by insulin stimulation after high fat feeding (Fig. 2B). Hepatic VPEPCK/VIDH and VPC/VIDH were suppressed by HEC in both lean and high fat fed animals (Fig. S2A-B).

Given this evidence suggesting that HFD feeding disrupts renal cortical mitochondrial gluconeogenic fluxes, we became interested in measuring renal glucose production in absolute terms. There are technical challenges that have limited this type of research historically; however, recently, Perry and colleagues28,29 developed a mathematical framework that deconvolutes the contribution of liver vs. renal cortex to whole-body glucose production in vivo by measuring isotopomers in liver, kidney, and plasma after isotope infusion, which we adapt here as “glucose fingerprinting.” Importantly, this method has unique inputs from Q-Flux, yielding an orthogonal approach for assessing the role of the renal cortex in whole-body glucose production. Under basal conditions, we found that the fractional contribution of the liver vs. renal cortex to endogenous glucose production was unchanged by HFD feeding (Fig. 2C), where, in both groups, the liver accounted for ~80% of whole-body glucose production while the renal cortex accounted for ~20%. In contrast, this ratio was significantly altered between groups during the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, where the renal cortex became the dominant source of whole-body glucose production in HFD fed mice, accounting for ~60% of whole-body glucose production (Fig. 2D). EGP was elevated ~10-fold at the end of HEC in HFD fed animals (Fig. S2C).

We next mapped the fractional contributions of the liver and renal cortex onto whole-body EGP data to determine the absolute rates of both liver and renal glucose production, visualized in Fig. 2E with composite rates overlaid. Rates of renal cortical glucose production were comparable under basal conditions (Fig. S2D); however, renal gluconeogenesis was >6-fold elevated in HFD fed animals during HEC (Fig. 2F). Hepatic glucose production was suppressed in both lean and high fat fed animals during HEC (Fig. S2E). To better understand which substrates drive aberrant renal gluconeogenesis, we used Q-Flux to deconvolute the carbon precursors utilized by the renal cortex during HEC. With this approach, we found that gluconeogenesis from both glycerol (VGLYCEROL) and mitochondrial-derived carbon sources (VMITO GNG) were increased with HFD feeding, accounting for ~50% of renal glucose production each (Fig. 2G). Pyruvate was the principle anaplerotic source fueling VMITO GNG, with methylmalonyl-CoA and glutamine contributing 13% and 10%, respectively (Fig. 2H). The absolute rates of glucose production from pyruvate, methylmalonyl-CoA, and glutamine were all increased by HFD feeding during HEC (Fig. S2F).

IRKT1150A knockin protects against renal cortical insulin resistance but not glycerol-derived gluconeogenesis

We next sought to directly test the hypothesis that renal cortical insulin resistance is driven by the plasma membrane sn−1,2 DAGs → PKCε → IRKT1160 phosphorylation pathway. To this end, we utilized a genetic mouse model where the threonine 1160 inhibitory residue (mouse homolog: 1150) in the catalytic loop of the IRK is mutated to an alanine (IRKT1150A), which cannot be phosphorylated by PKCε19. For these experiments, wild-type littermates were placed on either a regular chow diet (RC) or HFD, while IRKT1150A littermates were placed on a HFD (IRKT1150A + HFD), to assess for possible rescue. After three weeks, HFD feeding again was associated with diminished levels of insulin-stimulated IRKY1162 phosphorylation, AKTS473 phosphorylation and FOXO1S256 phosphorylation in comparison to RC controls; however, IRKT1150A + HFD mice were protected from these HFD-induced signaling defects, displaying insulin signaling commensurate with RC mice (Fig. 3A-C). Defective rates of insulin-stimulated pyruvate oxidation (VPDH/VCS) were again observed in HFD animals but were not observed in IRKT1150A + HFD mice (Fig. 3D). Whole-body EGP was increased at the end of HEC in both HFD and IRKT1150A + HFD mice (Fig. S3A), and the fractional contribution of the renal cortex to whole-body EGP was also increased (Fig. S3B). After using Q-Flux to deconvolute the substrates driving renal glucose production, we found that VMITO GNG was increased in HFD mice and normalized in IRKT1150A + HFD mice (Fig. 3E), but VGLYCEROL remained elevated in both HFD and IRKT1150A + HFD mice (Fig. 3F), driving absolute rates of renal glucose production to remain elevated in both HFD and IRKT1150A + HFD animals (Fig. S3C). Hepatic glucose production was not significantly different between groups (Fig. S3C). Pyruvate was again the key contributor to renal cortical VMITO GNG (Fig. S3D).

Discussion

There is extensive interest in understanding renal insulin resistance and its possible role in whole-body gluconeogenesis. The kidney was postulated to play a meaningful role in basal and postprandial glucose production over 20 years ago14,30, but uncertainty in methodological precision severely limited widespread adoption, and no studies that we are aware of have tackled this clinically important question over the intervening decades. Here, we developed two independent methodologies, Q-Flux and glucose fingerprinting, to address this uncertainty, yielding strong orthogonal evidence that the kidney plays a key role in elevated whole-body EGP during hyperinsulinemia. Mechanistically, intrarenal insulin resistance drives persistent gluconeogenesis from mitochondrial-derived carbon, while elevated peripheral lipolysis increases glycerol flux to the kidney, simultaneously increasing gluconeogenesis from glycerol.

Our present work strongly suggets that proximal tubules (PTs), the only gluconeogenic cells in the kidney, become insulin resistant, whereas a previous report did not detect PT insulin resistance31. This may be because PTs have historically been studied by the sieving method, which takes about two hours to complete32, making samples highly susceptible to phosphorylation changes33, intracellular metabolite destruction34,35, artifactual AMPK activation36, and rewiring of insulin signaling and metabolism37. These limitations make sieving unsuitable for downstream Q-Flux, glucose fingerprinting, western blot, or metabolite analyses, and we believe likely explain why PT insulin resistance was not previously noted31. To overcome these limitations, we developed an in situ freeze clamping and renal cortical flaking technique (Fig. S4A) that allowed us to study PTs in their native state38.

We then applied our state-of-the art 5-compartment fractionation method to in situ freeze clamped renal cortices, where increased plasma membrane sn−1,2 DAGs and PKCε translocation, plus increased inhibitory phosphorylation at IRK T1160 were all consistent with insulin resistance mediated by a PM sn−1,2-DAG-PKCε-IRKT1160 phosphorylation mechanism. Renal cortical insulin resistance was confirmed by finding decreased insulin-stimulated insulin signaling at the level of the insulin receptor kinase and downstream at AKT, FOXO1, and PDH. Our complementary metabolic flux data show insulin stimulation drives PDH complex dephosphorylation and increases full oxidation of pyruvate, as occurs in muscle, but insulin resistance blunts this process. Notably, though, non-pyruvate substrates (e.g., fatty acids) account for ~97% of acetyl-CoA that is generated under basal conditions and still ~80% of acetyl-CoA under insulin-stimulated conditions in lean animals. This is consistent with the observation that the proximal tubules perform little-to-no glycolysis; therefore, the source of oxidized pyruvate is likely derived from Cori and Cahill cycling of lactate and alanine to the kidney; however, Q-Flux cannot deconvolute the relative contributions of these two fluxes to renal cortical pyruvate oxidation.

Regarding the upregulation of VPDH/VCS that was observed under basal conditions in high fat fed animals, paradoxically increased basal insulin signaling in the insulin resistant state is an interesting phenomenon that been previously observed39. We believe this to be secondary to basal hyperinsulinemia, where insulin concentrations are increased by 100% with HFD feeding in our study. This highly increased plasma insulin concentrations following an overnight fast may drive insulin action to near maximal levels at baseline, where further elevations during the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp are unable to further increase insulin signaling/action in the renal cortex.

IRKT1150A knockin corrected all high fat diet-induced insulin signaling and pyruvate oxidation defects, strongly suggesting that renal cortical insulin resistance is driven by inhibitory phosphorylation at IRKT1160. Gluconeogenesis from PEPCK flux, which is canonically insulin-regulated, was also corrected, while gluconeogenesis from glycerol remained increased in the IRKT1150A high fat fed animals. Previous work has found that IRKT1150A + HFD mice continue to display dysregulated white adipose tissue lipolysis during chronic high fat diet, likely due to localized WAT inflammation19. Thus, glycerol delivery to the renal cortex from dysregulated WAT lipolysis remains elevated under these conditions, driving increased VGLYCEROL and renal glucose production despite correction of intrarenal insulin signaling.

Notably, the kidney is particularly vulnerable to forming glucose from glycerol for multiple reasons: first, the kidney reabsorbs all the glycerol it filters under physiologic conditions40, and, because the kidneys receive ~25% of cardiac output41, this glycerol flux is quite substantial. Further, compared to liver, the renal cortex is metabolically inflexible with regard to glycerol handling. For instance, during states of enhanced glycerol delivery to the liver, glycerol can be directly funneled into fatty acid esterification and triglyceride synthesis, even in the presence of hepatic insulin resistance42, but the renal cortex does not possess such enzymatic machinery43, and thus, filtered glycerol is preferentially shunted into gluconeogenesis40. These principles help to explain our present data, as well as a previous study which found that high-dose glycerol infusion in humans rapidly increased renal glucose production but had no effect on hepatic glucose production44. Importantly, there is a growing understanding that glycerol is the key contributor of new carbon to the gluconeogenic pool, playing a major role in T2DM45–47. Until now, glycerol-derived glucose production was assumed to be hepatic in origin, but the paradigm we present here suggests this assumption should be reconsidered.

We note that elevated HGP is an established hallmark of T2DM, and we believe we were unable to detect persistent HGP here due to studying insulin resistant animals prior to onset of T2DM, hyperglycemia, or elevated basal EGP. Nevertheless, there is substantial merit in studying dysregulated glucose homeostasis in insulin resistance, prior to the onset of diabetes. The failure to suppress endogenous glucose production, as extensively documented here, is a key driver of postprandial hyperglycemia in humans48,49; postprandial hyperglycemia and insulin resistance each mechanistically drive chronic kidney disease50–52 and atherosclerosis53–55 prior to the onset of diabetes.

In summary, by applying multiple flux methodologies in combination with hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp studies, the present work provides insights into the regulation of renal cortical mitochondrial oxidation and gluconeogenesis in vivo and delineates the impact of HFD feeding on these fluxes. Specifically here we show that: (1) the renal cortex is almost totally dependent on mitochondrial fat oxidation to meet its energy needs following an overnight fast, (2) insulin stimulation increases renal cortical pyruvate oxidation from <5% to ~20% of total mitochondrial substrate oxidation in lean animals, (3) insulin stimulation decreases renal cortical PEPCK and PC flux in lean animals, (4) the renal cortex becomes insulin resistant in response to high fat feeding, as reflected by defects in insulin-stimulated IRK signaling and pyruvate oxidation (VPDH/VCS), (5) the renal cortex drives dysregulated whole-body EGP during hyperinsulinemia in high fat fed animals, (6) the HFD-induced defects in renal cortical insulin signaling, pyruvate oxidation, and mitochondrial-derived gluconeogenesis are prevented in IRKT1150A knockin mice, which implicates the plasma membrane sn−1,2-DAG-PKCε-IRKT1160 phosphorylation axis in mediating these abnormalities, and (7) IRKT1150A knockin mice are not protected from elevated renal glycerol-derived gluconeogenesis, which is likely secondary to HFD-induced defects in WAT lipolysis rather than intrarenal insulin resistance per se (Fig. 4)56.

Proposed molecular mechanisms and metabolic defects in renal cortical insulin resistance.

Under insulin sensitive conditions (left), insulin binds to its receptor, inducing activation of the insulin receptor kinase (IRK) via trans-autophosphorylation at tyrosine residues 1158 and 1162 within the IRK activation loop. Downstream signaling drives activation of AKT via phosphorylation at S473 and T308, which promotes dephosphorylation of the pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex and increases pyruvate oxidation. Phosphorylation of FOXO1 by AKT leads to its extrusion from the nucleus and reduces renal gluconeogenesis. Insulin also suppresses lipolysis, which reduces delivery of glycerol to the renal cortex, further promoting reduced gluconeogenesis (not depicted). During chronic high fat feeding (right), plasma membrane sn−1,2 diacylglycerol accumulation causes PKCε translocation, which phosphorylates the IRK at T1160, situated within the activation loop. Subsequently, when insulin binds to its receptor, there is reduced trans-autophosphorylation at Y1158 and Y1162 and impaired IRK activity, resulting in blunted pyruvate oxidation and persistent renal gluconeogenesis from mitochondrial-derived carbon sources. Excessive peripheral lipolysis promotes complementary increases in glycerol-derived renal gluconeogenesis, independently of intrarenal insulin signaling defects.

Limitations of study

Here, we use male mice as a model to study insulin resistance, as we and others have shown female mice are protected from lipid-induced insulin resistance57,58. The present study also uses freeze clamped renal cortices to infer insulin signaling and metabolism in proximal tubules, a tradeoff necessary to ensure validity of results. Notably, the renal cortex is ~75% proximal tubules59,60, and proximal tubules are >300 times more numerous than podocytes and >25 times more numerous than endothelial cells61; moreover, many of the critical pathways interrogated here (e.g. PDH flux, gluconeogenesis) are only present in proximal tubules. Thus, we are confident that proximal tubules drive the results documented here, even though other minor cell types are present. Nevertheless, the renal cortical flaking technique is not suited for studying glomeruli or endothelial cell-specific questions, which may be critical for understanding how renal insulin resistance drives albuminuria62 and hypertension7. Furthermore, here, we focus on the importance of IRK T1160 phosphorylation in mediating renal cortical insulin resistance; however, we acknowledge that additional mechanisms and modifiers of insulin resistance may be present in the renal cortex, particularly in less abundant cell populations which were not studied in the present work.

Methods

All animal studies were approved by the Yale University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol Number 2023-07277) and were performed in accordance with all regulatory standards.

Mouse models

For all studies except those shown in Fig. 3 and Fig. S3, C57BL/6 J male mice (Strain #: 000664) from The Jackson Laboratory were purchased and studied at 12 to 14 weeks of age. Upon arrival from the vendor, the mice remained on their regular living condition without any experimental perturbations for at least 3–4 days for acclimation and stress-minimization. All mice were then randomly allocated to either regular chow diet or high fat diet experimental groups, and weight matching was ensured before beginning experimental protocols. For studies shown in Fig. 3 and Fig. S3, male IRKT1150A mice and WT C57BL/6J littermates were obtained from an established and characterized colony19,63. WT littermates were randomized to receive either a regular chow diet or high fat diet; IRKT1150A littermates mice were placed on a high fat diet. These mice were studied at 12–18 weeks of age. The regular chow diet used in all studies was: Harlan Teklad TD2018 (18% fat, 58% carbohydrate, and 24% protein); the high fat diet used in all studies was: Research Diets Inc, D12492 (60% kcal% fat). The duration of diet was three weeks. Mice received ad libitum access to food and water and were housed in the Yale Animal Resources Center at 23 °C under a 12 hour light/12 hour dark cycle (0700–1900h).

Mouse basal infusions and hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic glucose clamp studies

For all studies, Instech catheters (Part Number: C20PU) were placed in the jugular vein 6–9 days before the experiments; only mice that recovered more than 95% of their preoperative body weight were studied; and mice were fasted at 18:30 the evening before infusion study and all infusion studies began at 08:00 the following morning. For [13C5]glutamine basal infusions, awake mice under gentle tail restraint underwent a primed-continuous infusion of [13C5]glutamine (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories) and [3-3H]glucose (PerkinElmer). [13C5]glutamine and [3-3H]glucose were infused at 18.0 μmol/(kg-min) and 0.3 μCi/min, respectively, for 5 minutes. Infusion rates were then lowered to 6.0 μmol/(kg-min) for [13C5]glutamine and 0.1 μCi/min for [3-3H]glucose and the infusion continued for 115 minutes. Blood samples were collected by tail bleeding at 120’ for determination of plasma glucose, insulin, NEFA, and [3H]glucose–specific activity. All mice were anesthetized with intravenous pentobarbital sodium injection (150 mg/kg), and tissues were rapidly harvested and snap-frozen with clamps precooled in liquid N2. Tissues were stored at –80 °C for subsequent analysis. Studies in which VPDH/VCS was assessed were performed identically, except for that [13C6]glucose (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories) was infused instead of glutamine, at a rate of 1 mg/(kg-min)23.

Hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamps and calculations were performed in compliance with the standard operating procedures of the NIH Mouse Metabolic Phenotyping Center (MMPC) consortium as previously described64,65. Briefly, awake mice under gentle tail restraint were first infused with [3-3H]glucose (0.1 μCi/min; PerkinElmer) for 120 min to measure basal glucose turnover. Hyperinsulinemia was achieved using a primed-continuous human insulin (Humulin; Eli Lilly) infusion. The insulin infusion rate was 4.8 mU/(kg-min) for 3 min, followed by 2.0 mU/(kg-min) for 120 min. For Q-Flux studies, [13C5]glutamine was included in each insulin infusate and was infused at 14.3 μmol/(kg-min) for 3 min and then 6.0 μmol/(kg-min) for 120 min. A 20% dextrose solution was infused at a variable rate to maintain euglycemia (110–130 mg/dL). The variable glucose infusion contained [3-3H]glucose at a target infusion rate of 0.1 μCi/min (hot-GINF). Blood samples were collected by tail bleeding at 0, 25, 45, 65, 80, 90, 100, 110, and 120 min for determination of plasma glucose. [3H]glucose specific activity was assessed at 0, 100, 110, and 120 min, and insulin and NEFA concentrations were assessed at 0 and 120 min. Endogenous glucose production during HEC was derived using the previously published equations64:

Renal cortical flake methodology

Historically, the kidney has been studied en bloc, despite the distinct embryologic origins and functions of the renal cortex vs. medulla67. Metabolically, the renal medulla extensively utilizes glucose, and it is only the renal cortex that participates in glucose production and displays insulin responsiveness3,7,67. Therefore, to obtain a more accurate measure of renal cortical insulin action, pyruvate oxidation, and glucose production, we developed a “kidney flake” methodology that is used throughout all experiments in the present study. In this method, the murine kidney is freeze clamped along the frontal plane using liquid nitrogen pre-cooled metal tongs. Later, using a liquid nitrogen cooled razor blade, the renal cortex is carefully collected by cutting it away from the medulla while the whole kidney sample is kept on dry ice. The demarcation between the cortex and medulla is visually apparent (Fig. S4A) and we ensure pure renal cortical sampling by being conservative during the flaking process.

Calculation of renal cortical VPDH/VCS

Following [13C6]glucose animal infusions as described above, renal cortical flaking was completed and two ~30 mg tissue aliquots were utilized for VPDH/VCS analysis. Aliquot 1 was used to determine pyruvate isotopic enrichment via GC-MS. 250 μL of ice-cold 60%/40% acetonitrile/water were added to the tissue, which was then immediately disrupted using a Qiagen TissueLyser II (30 Hz for 2 min) and centrifuged at 17,000 x g for 10 min at 4 °C. Supernatant was transferred to glass vials and dried under N2 gas, after which the sample was resuspended in 50 μL methoxyamine (MOX; ThermoFisher Scientific) reagent, sonicated for five minutes, and heated at 40 °C for 90 min. 70 μL N-methyl-N-(tert-butyldimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide plus 1% tert-butyldimethylchlorosilane (ThermoFisher Scientific) was added, and samples were heated at 70 °C for 30 min. Then, isotopic enrichment was measuring using GC-MS (EI). The masses monitored for pyruvate at: 174 (m + 0), 175 (m + 1), 176 (m + 2), and 177 (m + 3)27,68. The retention time of pyruvate was determined and periodically re-confirmed with a series of natural abundance and isotopically enriched standards.

Aliquot 2 was utilized to determine Acetyl-CoA isotopic enrichment. ∼30 mg renal cortical tissue was homogenized in 1 mL ice cold 10% trichloroacetic acid. The supernatant was then loaded on a Sep-Pak C18 cartridge from Waters Corporation, which was preconditioned by 3 mL HPLC-grade methanol, then 4 mL 1 mM HCl. After the supernatant flowed through the cartridge, it was washed with 3 mL 1 mM HCl and 1 mL distilled water. The flow-through was then discarded. Next, acetyl-CoA was eluted from the cartridge with 2 mL ethanol-water (65-35%, v/v) containing 0.1 M ammonium acetate followed by 2 mL 50% HPLC-grade methanol-water. Liver vs. kidney contribution to whole-body endogenous glucose production (glucose fingerprinting)

Our strategy for deconvoluting renal vs. hepatic glucose production is based off the previously-validated REnal Gluconeogenesis Analytical Leads (REGAL) method of Perry and colleagues28,29 with minor modifications to yield an approach we refer to as “glucose fingerprinting.” Specifically, instead of using [3-13C]lactate, we infuse [13C4]aspartate or [13C5]glutamine as previously described27, which both create [13C3]triose phosphates in liver and kidney. While REGAL relies on renal and hepatic glucose isotopomer data in its calculations, we have intentionally chosen to use glucose-6-phosphate enrichments instead. This is because the renal glucose isotopic signature in the REGAL mathematical framework is assumed to represent pure renal gluconeogenesis; however, the renal cortex extensively reabsorbs filtered plasma glucose in addition to performing gluconeogenesis. Thus, the enrichment of renal glucose will reflect an admixture of both renal glucose production and reabsorbed plasma glucose, potentially confounding results. Using renal cortical glucose-6-phosphate enrichments avoids this potential pitfall because glucose-6-phosphate is produced solely by gluconeogenesis and is not subject to confounding from reabsorbed glucose. Determination of plasma glucose enrichment

Total isotopic enrichment in plasma glucose was measured using the pentaacetate derivative70. 25 μL plasma samples were deproteinized by adding equal volumes of zinc sulfate and barium hydroxide. The sample was then vortexed and centrifuged at 13,000 x g for 10 min. The supernatant was transferred to a glass vial, dried using N2 gas, and then derivatized with 50 μl of 1:1 acetic anhydride:pyridine. After heating to 65 °C for 30 min, the reaction was terminated by adding 50 μl methanol. Total isotopic enrichment was then determined with GC-MS (CI mode) by monitoring m/z 331 (m + 0), 332 (m + 1), 333 (m + 2),…337 (m + 6).

Q-Flux analyses

Q-Flux is a validated metabolic flux analysis method that estimates the rates of key mitochondrial and gluconeogenic metabolic reactions in vivo27. During Q-Flux studies, 13C label enters the TCA cycle when [13C5]glutamine is converted via glutaminase to [13C5]glutamate, which is in rapid exchange to create [13C5]α-ketoglutarate. Label subsequently flows into all TCA cycle and gluconeogenic intermediate pools, where it is diluted by competing oxidative and anaplerotic reactions. Label dilution is monitored across precursor-product reactions via mass spectrometry, and metabolite enrichment data are then used to solve Q-Flux’s series of algebraic equations. Isotopic steady state is critical for validity of equations and was confirmed in renal cortices by sacrificing separate cohorts of mice after either 90 min or 120 min of [13C5]glutamine infusion (Fig. S4B). Relative rates of metabolic flux in the renal cortex were calculated identically to as previously described in liver, except for key modifications: in our previous publication, the enrichment in C4C5C6 glucose was used to calculate the fraction of total glucose production from mitochondrial sources vs. glycerol anaplerosis in liver; however, large amounts of glucose are reabsorbed from filtrate via SGLT2 and pass through the proximal tubules; therefore, assessing glycerol anaplerosis via dilution of label in C4C5C6 glucose may be substantially inaccurate, as the renal cortical glucose pool represents an admixture of glucose derived from gluconeogenesis and glucose derived from plasma filtrate reabsorption. To overcome this, we instead measured enrichment in the triose phosphate pool directly, at the level of dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP), the actual pool where glycerol anaplerosis occurs. Thus, the fractional contribution of mitochondrial-derived gluconeogenesis to total renal glucose production (VMITO GNG/VRGP) can be calculated using Eq.( 9), and the fractional contribution of glycerol-derived gluconeogenesis to total renal glucose production (VGLYCEROL/VRGP) can be calculated using Eq.( 10).

Natural abundance-corrected mass distribution vectors were calculated for each analyte using IsoCorrector71, and enrichments were then inputted into Q-Flux and glucose fingerprinting equations to calculate relative and absolute rates of metabolic flux.

PKCε translocation assay

Membrane-associated and cytosolic fractions were prepared as described previously, with modifications72. Five-compartment ultracentrifugation fractionation

The protocol was adapted and modified based on previous studies73,74. Renal cortices (60-150 mg) from mice fasted for 6 hours were first homogenized in cold (4 °C) TES buffer (250 mM sucrose, 10 mM Tris - pH 7.4, 0.5 mM EDTA) with a Dounce homogenizer. The homogenate was then centrifuged (at 12,000 rpm with SS-34 rotor or 17,000 x g, 15 min, 4 °C) to separate pellet A and supernatant A. The top lipid layer was collected as lipid droplet fraction. Pellet A was washed by being resuspended in TES buffer and centrifuged (at 12,000 rpm with SS-34 rotor or 17,000 x g, 20 min, 4 °C). Pellet A was then collected and resuspended in TES buffer and gently layered on top of 1.12 M sucrose buffer cushion in ultracentrifuge tubes. The pellet A samples were then centrifuged (at 35,000 rpm with TLS-55 rotor or 105,000 x g, 20 min, 4 °C) to separate pellet B, interface B and supernatant B. The interface B was collected, resuspended in TES buffer and centrifuged (at 37,000 rpm with TLA-100.2 rotor or 60,000 x g, 9 min, 4 °C) to obtain pellet C. Pellet C was then washed by being resuspended in TES buffer and centrifuged (at 37,000 rpm with TLA-100.2 rotor or 60,000 x g, 9 min, 4 °C) and was designated as plasma membrane fraction. The pellet B was washed by being resuspended in TES buffer and centrifuged (at 12,000 rpm with SS-34 rotor or 17,000 x g, 15 min, 4 °C) and was collected as mitochondria fraction. The supernatant A was centrifuged (at 65,000 rpm with Ti-70.1 rotor or 390,000 x g, 75 min, 4 °C) to separate pellet D and supernatant D. Pellet D was washed by being resuspended in TES buffer and centrifuged (at 65,000 rpm with Ti-70.1 rotor or 390,000 x g, 60 min, 4 °C) and was collected as the endoplasmic reticulum fraction. Supernatant D was collected as the cytosol fraction.

Diacylglycerol stereoisomer separation and quantitation

DAGs and ceramides were extracted from each of the five subceullular compartments with 2:1 chloroform:methanol (v/v) with 0.01% butylated hydroxytoluene. Known amounts of internal standards (1,2-dinonadecanoin for DAGs, N-heptadecanoyl-D-erythro-sphingosine for ceramides) were added during the extraction process. Afterwards, samples were dried down and re-dissolved in 100% hexane. Chiral analysis of DAGs were performed by LC-MS/MS using electrospray ionization on an AB Sciex Qtrap 6500 interfaced to Shimadzu UFLC with 2 LC-20AD pumps (and degassers), SIL-20AC xR autosampler20. Chromatographic separation was performed using Luna 5 u Silica (100 A, 250 × 2.0 mm) and LUX 5 u Cellulose-1 (250 × 4.6 mm) columns connected in series with an isocratic solvent of hexane:isopropanol (300:7) with a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min. TAGs elute between ∼4 and 8 min, and DAGs elute between ∼11 and 17 minutes. For DAGs with the same FA composition, the order of elution was sn-1,3-DAG, sn-2,3-DAG, and sn-1,2-DAG. For any specific stereoisomer (i.e., sn−1,2-DAG, sn-2,3-DAG, or sn-1,3-DAG) the order of elution generally increases with the degree of unsaturation. For example, C18:1 C18:1, C18:1 C18:2, and C18:2 C18:2. Standards were used to establish retention times, matrix effects, and response relative to the internal standard 1,2-Dinonadecanoin. No matrix effects were detected in the five subcellular compartments. However, the position of the FAs on the DAGs have not been established. For example, for the sn-1,2-DAG assigned as C18:0 C16:0, C16:0 could be on either the sn-1 or the sn-2 position. In the process of optimization, it was found that the use of cartridges (e.g., DiOH) or silica TLC plates to pre-separate the DAGs from TAGs results leads to intramolecular trans-esterification (e.g. the pre-separation of sn-1,2-DAGs will produce a mixture of sn-1,2-, sn-2,3-, and sn-1,3-DAGs. Thus, no additional separation steps following centrifugation to obtain cellular fraction were done prior to LC-MS/MS analysis. Raw data were converted to mzML file format using ProteoWizard75 and uploaded to Metabolomics Workbench76.

Statistical analyses

All data are the mean ± SEM. Each data point represents a unique biological replicate. Statistical analysis of the data was performed using GraphPad Prism Version 10 (Graphpad, San Diego, CA). Individual tests are described in figure legends. p values less than 0.05 were considered significant. Data were only excluded in the setting of experimental failure (e.g., line failure in an infusion study or failure to regain weight after surgery). Less than 10% of animals were excluded.
