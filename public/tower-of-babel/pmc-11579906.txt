Progressive headache and neck pain associated with neck rotation due to a congenital external carotid–jugular arteriovenous fistula: illustrative case
Authors: Yasunori Yokochi, Hiroyuki Ikeda, Tomoko Hayashi, Minami Uezato, Masanori Kinosada, Yoshitaka Kurosaki, Masaki Chin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDCongenital external carotid–jugular arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) are often diagnosed based on a pulsatile neck mass. Patients rarely complain of headaches or neck pain.OBSERVATIONSA 45-year-old woman with no history of trauma had been diagnosed with a congenital cervical external carotid AVF 10 years earlier. At that time, only a pulsatile neck mass in the right neck had been observed, and she had been managed conservatively. Over the next 10 years, the right neck mass had enlarged, and she started experiencing unbearable headaches and neck pain when she turned her head. Angiography images showed that the outflow veins, including the external and internal jugular veins, were more dilated and tortuous than they had been 10 years earlier. During neck rotation, the internal jugular vein constricted, causing intracranial venous reflux. Complete occlusion of the shunt was achieved with coils and n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate. Immediately after surgery, the pulsation in the right neck, the unbearable headaches, and neck pain on neck rotation resolved.LESSONSEven in adults with congenital cervical external carotid AVFs that have been managed as simple neck masses, progressive dilatation and tortuosity of the outflow veins can, over time, lead to venous reflux during neck rotation and can cause unbearable headaches and neck pain. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24407

Most carotid–jugular arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) result from either penetrating or iatrogenic trauma to the neck. Congenital AVFs involving the external carotid system are rare.1 Symptoms commonly seen in congenital external carotid–jugular AVFs are thrills, murmurs, and pulsatile neck mass.2–8 To our knowledge, there are few reports on carotid-jugular AVFs associated with headache and neck pain, and the cause is unclear. We report a case of progressive headache and neck pain on neck rotation due to a congenital external carotid–jugular AVF and elucidate the pathophysiological mechanism of headache and neck pain.

Illustrative Case

History and Examination

A 45-year-old woman presented with a pulsatile mass that had been present in the right neck since childhood. She had no history of trauma, operation, or inflammation of this lesion. Magnetic resonance imaging scans of the head and neck obtained 10 years earlier, when the patient was 35 years of age, showed a right carotid AVF. Angiography revealed a high-flow congenital AVF in the right internal maxillary artery with venous drainage into the right jugular vein (Fig. 1A and B). At that time, the only symptom had been a pulsatile mass in the right neck, and the patient was followed up. Since then, the pulsatile mass in the right neck had gradually enlarged, and the patient became aware of unbearable headaches, right neck pain that increased with right neck rotation, and nausea. There were no neurological deficits, but the patient requested treatment for headaches and neck pain that interfered with her daily life. Computed tomography angiography and venography fusion images showed dilatation of the right common carotid artery and external carotid artery as inflow arteries and the right external carotid vein, retromandibular vein, common facial vein, and caudal internal carotid vein as outflow veins (Fig. 1C– F). Angiography showed no change in the vascular architecture from 10 years earlier, but the right external jugular vein, retromandibular vein, common facial vein, and caudal portion of the internal jugular vein were more dilated and tortuous (Fig. 1G and H). The patient had a high-flow AVF, and it was difficult to identify the shunt point. Right external carotid arteriography with the neck rotated to the left showed no change in the draining vein (Fig. 1I). Right external carotid arteriography with the neck rotated to the right showed severe stenosis in the caudal portion of the right internal carotid vein and retrograde flow into the intracranial veins (Fig. 1J). Venous phase right internal carotid arteriography showed drainage from the right sigmoid sinus to the right internal jugular vein and vertebral venous plexus (Fig. 1K). Venous phase right internal carotid arteriography with the neck rotated to the right showed no drainage from the right sigmoid sinus to the right internal jugular vein and vertebral venous plexus (Fig. 1L). Cervical vascular echocardiography showed a stenosis of the caudal internal jugular vein and increased flow velocity at the stenosis during right neck rotation compared with the anterior neck position (Fig. 2). The flow velocity of the caudal internal jugular vein was 70 cm/sec in the anterior neck position, 95 cm/sec in neck rotation to the left, and 163 cm/sec in neck rotation to the right. The stenosis of the internal jugular vein, an outflow vein, due to neck rotation to the right and the increase in venous pressure due to the backflow of the outflow vein into the cranium were considered to be the cause of the unbearable headaches and neck pain. Endovascular Treatment

An 8-Fr balloon guide catheter (Optimo EPD, Tokai Medical Products) was placed in the right external carotid artery via the right femoral artery with the patient under general anesthesia. An intermediate catheter (6 Fr, Sofiaselect, Terumo) was guided to the distal part of the right external carotid artery. Right external carotid angiography, performed with balloon occlusion of the external carotid artery to confirm the shunt point, clearly demonstrated the fistula of the shunt from the right maxillary artery to the right maxillary vein (Fig. 3 A–D). The diameter of the fistula at the shunt point was relatively large at 3.5 mm. A microcatheter (Headway 17, Terumo) was placed from the feeder side to the drainer side via the shunt point. Coil embolization was performed from the venous side of the shunt point to the arterial side while the external carotid artery was occluded with a balloon. Right external carotid angiography images showed that the flow through the shunt was severely attenuated but still present. Therefore, 33% n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate was injected from the proximal side of the coil mass (Fig. 3E and F). Right external carotid arteriography confirmed complete occlusion of the shunt and patency of the branch vessels of the external carotid artery (Fig. 3G and H). Postoperative Course

No new neurological abnormalities were noted postoperatively. Immediately after surgery, the pulsatile mass in the right neck reduced in size, and the headaches and neck pain caused by neck rotation to the right resolved. Computed tomography angiography and venography fusion images obtained the day after surgery showed that the draining vein remained dilated (Fig. 4A). Computed tomography angiography and venography fusion images obtained 6 months postoperatively showed shrinkage of the right external carotid artery and right external jugular vein (Fig. 4B). Right external carotid arteriography 6 months postoperatively confirmed maintenance of complete occlusion of the AVF (Fig. 4C). Cervical vascular echocardiography showed that the flow velocity of the caudal portion of the internal jugular vein was 38 cm/sec in the midline of the neck, 29 cm/sec with neck rotation to the left, and 9 cm/sec with neck rotation to the right. Discussion

Observations

Congenital external carotid–jugular AVFs can show gradual progression even in adults. Dilated draining veins can become narrowed by neck rotation during daily activities, leading to increased venous pressure and intracranial venous reflux, which can cause severe headaches.

It is known that the natural history of congenital AVFs in the maxillary artery is usually benign.1 In our case, a neck mass had been noted since childhood, and the patient was diagnosed with a congenital external carotid–jugular AVF by angiography at the age of 35 years. During 10 years of follow-up, the patient experienced progressive dilatation and tortuosity of the outflow veins. Neck rotation toward the lesion side caused narrowing of the outflow veins and venous reflux into the intracranial veins. The increase in outflow venous pressure and the resulting changes in intracranial venous return have been shown to contribute to headache and neck pain. After treatment, the patient’s unbearable headaches and neck pain on neck rotation completely resolved, confirming that the congenital external carotid–jugular AVF was the cause of the pain. Our case suggests that even asymptomatic adults experience gradual and progressive deterioration and that daily activities cause unexpected symptoms.

Congenital external carotid–jugular AVFs are embryologically classified as parachordal AVFs. Chronic changes in parachordal AVFs can lead to venous dilatation, stenosis, and thrombosis.9 In AVFs in the maxillary artery/vein, as in our case, venous congestion and venous reflux into the intracranial veins are not typically observed from an embryological standpoint.10 The cause of neck pain in our patient could be mechanical compression of the adjacent pain-sensitive structures due to dilatation of the outflow veins caused by stenosis of the outflow veins and increased venous pressure.11, 12 The cause of headache in our patient could be caused by venous reflux into the cavernous sinus and increased intracranial pressure, as shown in a previous report on AVFs with the internal jugular vein as the direct outflow tract.1 Increased venous pressure transmitted through the outflow veins to the intracranial venous sinuses can cause sinus distension and headache.13 In this case, angiograms showed that the direct outflow tract was the external jugular vein, which connected to the internal jugular vein via the retromandibular vein and the common facial vein. Given the increase in the neck mass and dilatation of the outflow veins over the 10-year period, shunt blood flow could have gradually increased over time. The increased shunt flow and consequent dilatation and tortuosity of the outflow veins, along with the stenosis of the outflow veins due to neck rotation toward the lesion side, likely caused increased venous pressure and venous reflux into the internal jugular and intracranial veins, resulting in the headache.

Surgical ligation has been historically used in the surgical management of congenital external carotid–jugular AVFs.2 In recent years, endovascular treatment has become the preferred method because of the reduced risk of damage to surrounding vessels and nerves.14 In the past, coils, detachable balloons, and liquid embolic systems were used.15 The primary goals in the treatment of congenital external carotid–jugular AVFs are complete occlusion of the shunt point and preservation of the arteries supplying neural structures. This requires an accurate assessment of the shunt point and its adjacent branching vessels, followed by targeted embolization. Congenital external carotid–jugular AVFs often form high-flow shunts.10 Routine external carotid angiography might not always adequately evaluate the shunt point and its adjacent branching vessels. In addition, during embolization, controlling embolic agents at the shunt point in a high-flow setting is extremely challenging. If the embolic agents are not adequately supported and controlled, they can migrate distally, leading to serious complications such as pulmonary embolism. To address these issues, flow control is essential. Previous reports have described methods such as making a small incision in the neck to clamp the external jugular vein16 or compressing the neck during treatment.7 Detachable balloon occlusion, first proposed by Scialfa et al.,17 has been used as one of the methods to treat external carotid AVFs.8, 14, 16 Currently, the supply of detachable balloons is limited and other treatment options are needed.15 In this case, angiography under balloon occlusion using a balloon guide catheter was useful for precise evaluation of the shunt point and surrounding vascular structures in a high-flow shunt. Flow control with a balloon guide catheter could have resulted in safe and reliable embolization.

Lessons

In congenital external carotid–jugular AVFs, a gradual progression can be observed even in adults. The dilated outflow veins could become narrowed by neck rotation during daily activities, leading to venous reflux into the intracranial veins and causing severe headache. Continuous follow-up is therefore necessary.
