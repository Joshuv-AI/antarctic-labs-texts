Femoral stress distribution in fully hydroxyapatite-coated cementless versus polished cemented stems with identical geometry: a thermoelastic stress analysis
Authors: Ryunosuke Watanabe, Hajime Mishima, Shota Yasunaga, Tomohiro Yoshizawa, Tomofumi Nishino, Yoshihisa Harada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStress shielding and altered load transfer remain major concerns in total hip arthroplasty (THA). Stem fixation method and material properties strongly influence femoral stress distribution, but data directly comparing cementless and cemented stems of identical geometry are limited.Materials and methodsSix fourth-generation composite femurs were used. Three were implanted with a fully hydroxyapatite-coated cementless stem, and three with a polished cemented stem. Mechanical loading of 100–1900 N at 5 Hz was applied to simulate single-leg stance. Thermoelastic stress analysis (TSA) was used to evaluate surface stress distribution across Gruen zones. Linear mixed models assessed differences between groups.ResultsBefore implantation, the highest stress was observed in the proximal femur. After implantation, stress shifted distally in both fixation methods. The cementless stem maintained higher compressive stress in the proximal-medial region compared with the cemented stem, which demonstrated a more uniform stress distribution between the medial and lateral cortices. TSA measurements confirmed significant proximal stress reduction with cemented fixation, while cementless fixation preserved proximal loading more effectively.ConclusionThis simulation study showed that fixation method and material influence short-term femoral stress distribution, even when stem geometry is identical. These findings describe only mechanical behavior under simplified conditions. The use of composite femurs, the one-size difference between stems, and the short-term evaluation limit the generalizability of the results. Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) has led to significant advancements in implant design and surgical techniques, resulting in improved long-term outcomes and its widespread adoption worldwide [1]. The postoperative femoral stress distribution in THA is influenced by stem design, material properties, and fixation methods, which in turn affect bone remodeling [2, 3]. A key unresolved problem is proximal femoral stress shielding, which can promote bone resorption, jeopardize fixation, and lead to loosening or fracture [4].

The fully hydroxyapatite (HA)-coated cementless stem is one of the most widely used cementless stems [5–7]. This type of stem aims to achieve biological fixation by forming a compressed cancellous bone layer around the stem, sometimes referred to as “biological cement” [8]. Clinical studies have reported favorable postoperative outcomes, with fully HA-coated stems showing reduced bone resorption and minimal bone hypertrophy [9, 10]. However, limited research has been conducted on femoral stress distribution following THA using fully HA-coated stems [11].

A polished cemented stem with identical geometry to a fully HA-coated stem has recently become available. This design allows fixation mode selection based on bone quality. Yet comparative data on femoral stress distribution between these two stems of the same geometry are scarce. Using identical geometry is a methodological strength because it isolates the effects of fixation method and material, minimizing geometric confounding.

Thermoelastic stress analysis (TSA) is a nondestructive method that infers surface stress from minute temperature changes during cyclic loading; details appear in Methods. TSA shows good agreement with strain gauge measurements [12, 13] and has been applied to femoral evaluation in orthopedics [14, 15]. Previous reports have applied this technique to analyze postoperative stress distribution in THA [11, 16–18] and demonstrated a correlation between stress distribution around the stem and changes in bone mineral density [17].

This study aimed to clarify the differences in femoral stress distribution between a fully HA-coated cementless stem and a polished cemented stem of identical geometry. By applying TSA for a detailed stress analysis, we sought to evaluate the mechanical effects of each stem on the femur and provide insights that contribute to optimal stem selection in THA.

Materials and methods

Study design and specimens

This was a mechanical simulation study using fourth-generation composite femurs. It did not evaluate biological remodeling.

Materials

We used a fourth-generation composite femur model (Model 3403; SawBones, Pacific Research Laboratories, USA). Six composite femurs were allocated to two groups (cementless n = 3, cemented n = 3). The composite femur was made of glass-filled epoxy and designed to replicate the mechanical properties and morphology of an adult male femur (183 cm, 890 N [90.3 kgf]). It consists of cortical and cancellous bone structures [19, 20]. The thermoelastic properties of glass-filled epoxy used for cortical bone have been confirmed to exhibit a linear relationship between the sum of the principal stress changes and temperature variations. A temperature change of 1 K corresponds to a change of approximately 227 MPa in the sum of principal stresses [14].

The implants used in this study were Universia stems (Teijin Nakajima Medical, Japan), which have the same shape and are available as both fully HA-coated cementless stems and polished cemented stems. The cementless stem is available only in a collared design, which was used in this study, whereas the cemented stem lacks a collar. The cemented version follows the line-to-line concept, with fixation achieved by close cortical contact with minimal cement mantle [21]. The cementless stem was made of titanium alloy, whereas the cemented stem was made of stainless steel.

The three-dimensional templating software Zed Hip (version 17.0.0, Lexi Co., Ltd, Japan) was used to determine the appropriate stem size for the composite femur. The cementless stem was selected to leave a thin cancellous bone layer between the stem and cortical bone using a size 11 high offset. A high-offset cemented stem of size 10, one size smaller than usual, was selected because the dense cancellous structure of the composite femur hindered proper cement penetration. This necessitated a thinner cement mantle around the stem. Compared with the size 11 cementless stem, this adjustment resulted in approximately 0.5 mm less in the anteroposterior dimension and 1.0 mm less in the mediolateral dimension. A BIOLOX Delta ceramic femoral head (CeramTec Medical, Germany) with a diameter of 32 mm and an offset of + 9 mm was used to replicate the native femoral head center. PALACOS bone cement (Heraeus Medical, Germany) was used for fixation.

Methods

TSA

The thermoelastic effect refers to temperature changes in a material under adiabatic conditions caused by stress variations and is expressed by the following equation (Eq. 1), originally proposed by Thomson in 1853 [22]:

ΔT: Temperature change of the material (K).

k: Thermoelastic coefficient (1/Pa).

T: Material temperature (K).

Δ(σ1 + σ2): Change in the sum of principal stresses (Pa).

The thermoelastic coefficient k is defined as:

k = α/ (ρ・Cp).

where:

α: Coefficient of linear thermal expansion (K-1).

ρ: Density (kg/m³).

Cp: Specific heat capacity at constant pressure (J/(kg·K)).

TSA measures minute temperature changes (ΔT) induced by cyclic loading and converts them into stress variations using Eq. 1. Compressive stress leads to an increase in temperature, whereas tensile stress causes a decrease in temperature. The individual components of principal stresses σ1 and σ2 cannot be measured separately. A high-precision infrared thermography camera was used for measurements.

Composite femur setup and TSA measurement

Six composite femurs were used in the experiments. The distal end of each femur was resected and fixed to a test holder with bolts and bone cement at a physiological adduction angle of 9° to simulate a single-leg stance. To standardize the emissivity, matte black heat-resistant paint (ASAHIPEN Corporation, Japan) was applied to the surface of the femurs (Fig. 1a, b).

The setting of a composite femur. (a) Before stem insertion (Intact) (b) Composite femur after cemented stem insertion

The femur models were mounted on a hydraulic servo-testing machine (MiniBionix 858; MTS Systems Corporation, USA), and cyclic axial compression loads of 100–1900 N at 5 Hz were applied vertically to the femoral head. This load condition simulated a single-leg stance during level walking, with a peak load set at approximately twice the body weight of the composite femur model (890 N) [23]. After applying more than 200 cycles to stabilize the load between 100 and 1900 N, TSA images were captured over 100 cycles, and 4000 images were acquired using an infrared stress measurement system (CPA-SC7500; FLIR, USA). The images were processed using the Altair LI image analysis software (FLIR, USA), synchronizing the load amplitude to obtain surface stress distribution images from the anterior, posterior, medial, and lateral views of the femur (Fig. 2).

Scene during thermoelastic stress analysis. The composite femur set in the testing machine was captured with a thermography camera

Following initial measurements in the intact state, Universia stems were inserted into the composite femurs using standard THA surgical techniques. A cementless stem was implanted by rasping to a size of 11 and then inserting a size 11 stem. The cemented stem was implanted by rasping to a size of 11, filling the femoral canal using a third-generation cementing technique, and inserting a size 10 stem. After the stem implantation, a ceramic femoral head was attached to the stem neck. Three femurs were implanted with a cementless stem, and three with a cemented stem. TSA measurements were repeated under the same loading conditions to evaluate the stress distribution after stem implantation.

Outcomes and statistical analysis

The primary outcome was the stress value (sum of the principal stresses) measured on the lateral and medial surfaces of the composite femur using TSA. Specifically, stress values were obtained for each pixel within the zones defined by the Gruen classification (lateral zones: 1–4 L; medial zones: 4 M–7) [24]. The image processing software Altair (FLIR, USA) was used to extract the stress data for each pixel (Fig. 3a, b). For each composite femur, approximately 600–800 pixels were extracted from zones 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6, and 200–300 pixels were extracted from zones 4 L and 4 M. The stress values were compared among the three groups: intact, cementless, and cemented.

Thermoelastic stress analysis images of composite femurs after stem implantation with Gruen zone classification. (a) Lateral view: zones 1–3 and 4 L (b) Medial view: zones 7, 6, 5, and 4 M

The secondary outcome involved visual evaluation of the TSA-derived stress distribution images for intact, cementless, and cemented femurs.

This study employed a linear mixed model (LMM) to analyze the differences in stress values among the three groups (Intact, Cementless, Cemented). The group factor was set as a fixed effect, whereas variations between individual composite femurs were treated as random effects. If a significant difference was detected among the groups in the LMM analysis, Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test was conducted for multiple comparisons to identify groups that differed significantly. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. In addition to P values, effect sizes were calculated to evaluate the magnitude of group differences. Cohen’s d was computed using the estimated mean difference between groups derived from the LMM, divided by the square root of the residual variance (root mean square error, RMSE). According to conventional criteria, |d| values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 were interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 29.0.2.0 (20), IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Primary outcome

Stress values in each Gruen zone were analyzed using a linear mixed model (LMM) to compare the three groups (Intact, Cementless, Cemented). Significant group effects were observed in zones 1, 2, 5, 6, and 7 (p = 0.003 for zone 2; p < 0.001 for the others). In contrast, no significant differences were found in zones 3, 4 L, or 4 M (p = 0.839, 0.275, and 0.117, respectively). For zones with significant group effects, multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey’s HSD test. In zone 1, significant differences were observed between the intact and cementless groups and between the intact and cemented groups (p < 0.001), while no difference was detected between the cementless and cemented groups (p = 0.07). In zones 2, 5, 6, and 7, both stem groups differed significantly from the intact group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4).

Stress distribution (MPa) in Gruen zones 1–7 for intact, cementless, and cemented groups

Effect size analysis demonstrated large differences between the intact group and both stem groups in zones 1, 5, 6, and 7, with Cohen’s d values exceeding 1.5 (up to 3.3 in zone 7). In zone 2, the intact group also showed a large effect compared with the cemented and cementless groups (d ≈ 1.0). In contrast, zones 3, 4 L, and 4 M showed only negligible to small effect sizes (d < 0.2), consistent with the non-significant group effects. Furthermore, in distal zones (5–7), the difference between the cemented and cementless groups reached moderate to large magnitudes (d = 0.5–1.0), indicating lower compressive stress in the cemented group. Secondary outcome

In the TSA-derived stress distribution images of the intact femur, the lateral and medial surfaces exhibited high stress from the proximal to the distal regions (Fig. 6a). In contrast, for the cementless and cemented groups, minimal stress was observed in the proximal region above the lesser trochanter, whereas stress was distributed from the region just below the lesser trochanter to the distal part of the femur (Fig. 6b, c).

Discussion

This study evaluated the stress distribution of a fully HA-coated cementless stem, a polished cemented stem, and an intact femur using TSA. The results demonstrated that intact femurs exhibited the highest stress in the proximal lateral and medial regions. The cementless stem maintained a higher compressive stress on the proximal-medial side than the cemented stem, whereas the cemented stem exhibited a more uniform stress distribution between the medial and lateral regions. Preserving proximal stress may reduce stress shielding. It may also lessen proximal bone resorption. These interpretations come from an immediate postoperative mechanical simulation on composite femurs and should be viewed with caution.

In the present study, the intact femur exhibited the highest stress in the proximal region, confirming that stem implantation reduces stress in the proximal femur. This finding suggests that both cementless and cemented stems distally shift stress distribution, altering the mechanical loading pattern of the femur. These findings are consistent with previous research, which reported that compared to intact stems, both cementless and cemented stems result in reduced compressive stress in the proximal-medial region, but cementless stems maintain higher stress levels [25]. The higher compressive stress observed on the proximal-medial side in the cementless group than in the cemented group is likely influenced by direct contact between the cementless stem and cortical bone, as well as differences in material properties [19, 26, 27] (Table 2). The cementless stem, made of titanium alloy, has an elastic modulus closer to that of cortical bone, potentially allowing for more efficient stress transfer. In contrast, a cemented stem made of stainless steel with a higher elastic modulus transmits less direct stress to the bone. A stem with a higher elastic modulus may increase the risk of stress shielding. However, the presence of a low-modulus cement mantle surrounding the polished stem likely facilitates micromovement within the cement layer, generating hoop stress and leading to a more balanced stress distribution between the medial and lateral regions [28].

Only collared stems were applied to the Universia cementless stems used in this study. The presence of a collar may have influenced the stress distribution, particularly in the proximal-medial region (Zone 7). Previous studies reported that a collar enhances stress transfer to the femur and prevents cortical bone resorption in the medial neck [29, 30]. Furthermore, a biomechanical analysis reported that a collared stem produced nearly twice the proximal–medial strain transfer compared with a collarless design under similar loading conditions, indicating a clear mechanical effect of the collar on load transmission [31]. Additionally, previous TSA-based research on collarless fully HA-coated stems and tapered wedge stems demonstrated that the tensile stress was distributed in Zone 7 [11, 17]. In contrast, the findings of the present study suggest that the presence of a collar contributes to the distribution of compressive stress in Zone 7, indicating its role in the load transmission.

Furthermore, previous studies have validated the application of TSA for femoral stress analysis and reported its utility [11, 14, 16–18]. The advantage of the TSA lies in its ability to evaluate the stress distribution across the entire femoral surface under actual loading conditions, unlike strain gauge methods, which measure stress only at specific locations. Additionally, compared with finite element analysis, TSA directly measures the stress under real mechanical loading, making it particularly useful for analyzing cemented stems, where the material properties and interfacial conditions between the stem, cement, and bone are complex to model in simulations. Our findings suggest that TSA is a valuable tool for analyzing stress distribution in cemented stems and may serve as a reference for improving the accuracy of finite element modeling.

This study had several limitations. First, composite femurs may not fully replicate the complex physiological conditions of human bones, making their direct application to patients challenging. The elastic modulus of the composite cortical analog is approximately 16–17 GPa [19], whereas human cortical bone typically ranges from 14 to 22 GPa [32]. Although these values are similar, composite femurs do not reproduce the natural variability in cortical thickness, bone density, and anatomical morphology seen in real femora. Second, the cementless stem (size 11, collared) and the cemented stem (size 10, polished) differed by one size to allow cement mantle formation, which may introduce minor biomechanical variation. Third, the presence of a collar only in the cementless stem may have influenced proximal–medial stress transfer. This design difference weakens the assumption of identical geometry between the two stems. In addition, TSA measures only surface stress and does not provide information on internal bone stress. This study also did not validate TSA findings using finite element analysis (FEA) or strain gauges. This is an important limitation. Future studies should compare TSA with FEA models or experimental strain measurements to confirm the accuracy of TSA-derived stress patterns. Furthermore, the study reflects stress distribution only under immediate postoperative loading. Long-term remodeling was not assessed. Changes caused by osseointegration or biological factors may modify stress patterns over time.

Given these limitations, the findings provide basic mechanical insight into the stress distribution characteristics of cementless and cemented stems. However, further investigation is needed before these results can be applied directly to clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study compared the stress distribution of fully HA-coated cementless stems and polished cemented stems with identical geometry using TSA. Both stems reduced proximal loading compared with the intact femur, and the cementless stem showed higher compressive stress in the proximal-medial region. These findings reflect short-term mechanical behavior only. The use of composite femurs, the presence of a collar in the cementless stem, the one-size difference between stems, and the lack of validation against finite element analysis or strain gauges limit the generalizability of the results. The study cannot predict clinical outcomes or long-term bone remodeling. Within these limitations, the results provide basic mechanical insight into how fixation method and material may influence load transfer. Further research using cadaveric models and clinical follow-up is needed to clarify the long-term implications for stem selection in THA.
