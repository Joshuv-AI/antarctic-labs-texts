Investigating Novel Inflammatory Indices and Their Links to Mortality, Cancer, and Cardiovascular Disease: A 10‐Year Cohort Study
Authors: Mahboobeh Ghasemzadeh Rahbardar, Homina Saffar, Niloofar Shabani, Habibollah Esmaily, Moein Najib, Mana Moghadasian, Seyedeh Atiyeh Mousavifard, Mohsen Moohebati, Sara Saffar Soflaei, Majid Ghayour‐Mobarhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsInflammatory indices, including neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte‐platelet ratio (NLPR), systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII), platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) have been proposed as prognostic markers for mortality. This study explored the links between novel inflammatory markers and mortality, including all‐cause death, cancer, and cardiovascular disease (CVD).MethodsThe MASHAD cohort study included adults aged 35–65 years through a stratified cluster sampling method. Demographic, health‐related, and inflammatory indices data were collected at baseline. Participants were followed for 10 years. Cox regression and Kaplan‐Meier survival analyses were employed to examine the associations of inflammatory markers with mortality outcomes.ResultsIn unadjusted analyses, NLPR in the highest tertile (≥ 0.035) was associated with increased risks of all‐cause mortality (HR = 1.43; 95% CI: 1.10–1.88; p = 0.009) and CVD mortality (HR = 1.73; 95% CI: 1.11–2.69; p = 0.020), but these associations were attenuated after adjustment (all‐cause mortality: HR = 1.11; 95% CI: 0.84–1.48; p = 0.458). PLR in the mid‐tertile (93.70–123.53) had a protective effect against all‐cause mortality in adjusted models (HR = 0.74; 95% CI: 0.57–0.97; p = 0.027). The Kaplan‐Meier survival analysis demonstrated notable variations in survival curves for NLPR with all‐cause (p = 0.030), CVD mortality (p = 0.023), and PLR with all‐cause mortality (p = 0.004). Lower all‐cause mortality was observed in the mid‐tertile of PLR, specifically among men and those without hypertension or CVD, according to subgroup analysis. NLR and SII were not associated with mortality.ConclusionPLR and NLPR have potential as prognostic markers for all‐cause mortality, with PLR demonstrating a consistent protective effect at moderate levels. Further research with extended follow‐up periods is necessary to confirm these results and investigate their potential relevance to clinical practice.Keywords: cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, inflammation, lymphocytes, neoplasms, neutrophils

Introduction

Inflammation is a prevalent and important pathological process that occurs in infections, trauma, and diseases such as hepatitis, gastritis, and pneumonia. According to research, it is both a protective and adaptive response that helps with damage control, prevention of spread, and tissue healing [1]. However, chronic or dysregulated inflammation can accelerate the progression and onset of numerous diseases, including cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [2, 3], cancer [4, 5], and other chronic conditions [6]. Immune cells, such as platelets and neutrophils are key players in the inflammatory process, and their interactions underlie the pathophysiology of these diseases [7, 8].

Progress in the field has shed light on the capacity of systemic inflammatory markers to act as prognostic indicators in multiple clinical applications [9, 10, 11]. Among these markers, novel indices for instance the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte × platelet ratio (NLPR), systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII), platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) have gained attention [12, 13, 14, 15]. Prior research has identified strong links between these accessible inflammatory markers and elevated risks of CVD [9, 10, 15], COVID‐19 [16, 17], asthma [18], and type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM) [19], suggesting their relevance in predicting disease onset and progression.

While NLR, PLR, and SII have been widely studied in relation to mortality, the inclusion of NLPR, a relatively underexplored composite marker, adds a novel dimension to this analysis. Moreover, our study utilizes a large, population‐based cohort with long‐term follow‐up, allowing for robust assessment of mortality outcomes across cardiovascular, oncologic, and all‐cause domains.

To our knowledge, few studies have simultaneously evaluated these four inflammatory indices in predicting long‐term mortality within a general population. This integrated approach, combined with the extended follow‐up and comprehensive outcome data, addresses a critical gap in the literature and enhances the translational relevance of systemic inflammation in chronic disease prognosis.

Despite growing evidence linking systemic inflammatory markers to disease progression, their prognostic efficacy in predicting long‐term mortality outcomes has yet to be definitively established. The objective of this research is to thoroughly investigate the relationships between innovative inflammatory markers—NLR, PLR, SII, and NLPR—and mortality outcomes, including all‐cause, CVD, and cancer‐related deaths, within a large, population‐based cohort monitored over an extended period. This study aims to pinpoint inflammatory markers that could serve as predictors of mortality risk, with the goal of enhancing risk stratification methods. Additionally, it seeks to shed light on the influence of systemic inflammation on chronic disease outcomes, paving the way for more efficient prognostic tools and preventive measures in clinical settings.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Design and Sample Population

The Mashhad stroke and heart atherosclerotic disorder (MASHAD) study, conducted from 2010 to 2020, recruited participants aged 35 to 65 years from three regions in Mashhad, northeastern Iran, using stratified cluster random sampling. The regions were divided into sites based on Mashhad Healthcare Center divisions, and eligible households were identified. With the assistance of local authorities and community leaders, study brochures were distributed, and appointments for physical examinations were scheduled. Of those contacted, 82% agreed to participate. To assess potential non‐response bias, demographic and socioeconomic data were collected from non‐responders (n = 2035), there were no notable variations in age or gender distributions between those who responded and those who did not respond.

Demographic, socioeconomic, and health‐related variables were collected by a team consisting of two qualified healthcare experts and a nurse. Extensive health assessments were conducted to provide a detailed evaluation of participants. The questionnaires addressing health‐related factors included demographic details, physical activity levels, use of tobacco and alcohol, anthropometric data, and cardiovascular risk determinants [20].

The study population was followed for 10 years, with follow‐up assessments conducted every 3 years to minimize loss to follow‐up. During this period, participants were monitored for overall mortality and specific causes of death, including CVD and cancer‐related mortality.

To ensure the accuracy of cause‐of‐death classification, a standardized questionnaire was completed by the participants' next of kin. The reported causes were then cross‐verified using death registry data maintained by the Iranian Ministry of Health. Detailed explanations of the study procedures and ethical considerations were provided to participants to ensure their informed consent and proper understanding of data confidentiality [20].

Definitions

DM was characterized by fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels ≥ 126 mg/dL or the use of oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin therapy. Hypertension (HTN) was defined in individuals with diastolic blood pressure readings of 90 mmHg or higher and/or systolic blood pressure readings of at least 140 mmHg, or those undergoing antihypertensive treatment. CVD was identified based on a documented history of angina pectoris, myocardial infarction (MI), or the presence of a definite Q wave on an electrocardiogram, as determined using the Minnesota Code. Individuals classified as current smokers were those who consumed a minimum of one cigarette daily [20].

Furthermore, decreased levels of high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) levels, defined as less than 50 mg/dL for women and less than 40 mg/dL for men, elevated blood lipid levels, such as low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) at or above 130 mg/dL and very low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (VLDL‐C) at or above 30 mg/dL, total cholesterol (≥ 200 mg/dL), and triglycerides (≥ 150 mg/dL) are all considered signs of dyslipidemia [22].

Cardiovascular death was defined as mortality resulting from MI, chronic ischemic heart disease, heart failure, fatal arrhythmia, cerebrovascular events, pulmonary thromboembolism, sudden cardiac arrest, or peripheral vascular disease (PVD). Coronary heart disease‐related death was specifically defined as death due to MI or chronic ischemic heart disease.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data, medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for non‐normally distributed data, and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). The normality of quantitative variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Between‐group differences in normally distributed quantitative variables were assessed using the independent‐samples t‐test. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate [23].

The inflammatory indicators — platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR), neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII), and neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte‐platelet ratio (NLPR) — were categorized into tertiles (T1–T3) for survival analyses. Kaplan–Meier curves [24] were generated to estimate cumulative survival across tertiles, and group differences were compared using the log‐rank test.

Cox proportional hazards regression [25] was used to examine associations of inflammatory indices with all‐cause, cardiovascular (CVD), and cancer mortality. Univariable models were initially fitted, followed by multivariable models adjusted for covariates selected a priori based on prior evidence and biological plausibility (age, sex, BMI, smoking status, DM, HTN, dyslipidemia, CVD, job, marriage status, education level).

Separate models were constructed for each inflammatory index to avoid multicollinearity; variance inflation factors (VIFs) were all < 2.0. The proportional hazards (PH) assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals; no violations of the PH assumption were observed (all p > 0.05).

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. All tests were two‐sided, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Primary analyses examined the associations of inflammatory indices with all‐cause, CVD, and cancer mortality. Subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, and comorbidities were exploratory.

Ethics

Before participating in the study, each individual was informed about the study and completed a documented agreement to participate. Approval for this study was provided by the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences under the reference number IR. MUMS. REC.1395.458. The study procedures adhered to the ethical principles established in the Declaration of Helsinki by the World Medical Association [26].

Cox Regression Analysis

Table 2 summarizes the univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses examining the associations of the NLR, PLR, SII, and NLPR with mortality outcomes. In the unadjusted model (Model 1), NLPR in the highest tertile (≥ 0.035) showed a significant correlation with a higher risk of all‐cause mortality (Hazard Ratio (HR) = 1.43; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.10–1.88) and CVD mortality (HR = 1.73; 95% CI: 1.11–2.69). However, after adjustment for covariates (Model 2), these associations became non‐significant for all‐cause mortality (HR = 1.11; 95% CI: 0.84–1.48) and CVD mortality (HR = 1.10; 95% CI: 0.69–1.75). In Model 1, PLR demonstrated a substantial reduction in all‐cause mortality, with reduced risks for individuals in the mid‐tertile (93.70–123.53; HR = 0.66; 95% CI: 0.51–0.85) and the highest tertile (>=123.53; HR = 0.76; 95% CI: 0.6–0.98) compared to the reference group. Additionally, after adjustment (Model 2), this relationship remained significant in the mid‐tertile (HR = 0.74; 95% CI: 0.57–0.97). Neither NLR nor SII showed significant associations with all‐cause, CVD, or cancer mortality in either the unadjusted or adjusted models.

In the male subgroup, the hazard of all‐cause mortality was significantly reduced by 33% in the mid‐tertile compared to the first tertile of PLR (HR = 0.67; 95% CI: 0.47–0.95). In subjects without CVD and HTN, the hazard of all‐cause mortality in the mid‐tertile of PLR showed a 25% reduction (HR = 0.75; 95% CI: 0.57–0.99) and a 41% reduction (HR = 0.59; 95% CI: 0.41–0.84) compared to the first tertile of PLR, respectively.

In all subgroups, the hazard of all‐cause mortality in the mid and highest tertiles of the NLPR was higher than in the first tertile. Conversely, in the subgroups of individuals aged ≤ 48 years and females, the mid tertile of NLPR reduced the risk of all‐cause mortality versus the first and highest‐tertiles. Specifically, in the subgroup aged ≤ 48 years, the mid tertile of NLPR reduced the risk of all‐cause mortality by 18% (HR = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.43–1.56), while in females, it reduced the risk by 5% (HR = 0.95; 95% CI: 0.63–1.41), compared to the first tertile. In most subgroups, the hazard of all‐cause mortality in the mid tertile of PLR decreased compared to the first and highest tertiles. Therefore, the mid tertile of PLR had a protective effect against all‐cause mortality.

Survival Probability Assessment With Kaplan‐Meier

Cumulative survival probabilities for all‐cause and cause‐specific mortality were analyzed across tertiles of NLR, PLR, SII, and NLPR using Kaplan‐Meier survival curves. The log‐rank test for NLPR demonstrated that the survival curves were significantly different across NLPR tertile groups for both all‐cause mortality (Log‐rank test statistic = 7.01; p = 0.03) and CVD mortality (Log‐rank test statistic = 7.54; p = 0.023) (Figures 1, 2). Also, the survival curves were significantly different across tertile groups of PLR for all‐cause mortality (Log‐rank test statistic = 10.97; p = 0.004) (Figure 1). In contrast, Kaplan‐Meier curves for NLR and SII showed minimal separation across tertiles, and the log‐rank tests did not indicate significant associations with all‐cause, CVD and cancer mortality (Figures 1, 2, 3). These findings highlight the utility of NLPR and PLR as potentially meaningful predictors of mortality outcomes, while NLR and SII demonstrated limited prognostic relevance in this cohort.

Discussion

In this cohort study, our team assessed the performance of various blood cell‐derived indices as prognostic markers for CVD, cancer, and all‐cause mortality. Our findings revealed that NLPR was significantly associated with all‐cause and CVD mortality in unadjusted analyses. However, this relationship diminished following the adjustment for covariates, indicating that other factors may mediate this relationship. In contrast, PLR, particularly in the mid‐tertile, remained independently associated with a lower risk of all‐cause mortality even after adjustment. These results highlight PLR as a potentially more stable prognostic indicator than NLPR, NLR, or SII.

The attenuation of the NLPR effect after adjustment suggests that comorbidities such as HTN, diabetes, and pre‐existing cardiovascular conditions, each of which is associated with both inflammation and mortality, may have diluted the independent effect of NLPR. Second, collinearity with other inflammatory indices (e.g., NLR, PLR, SII) could have reduced statistical power in multivariable models. Third, overadjustment may have occurred if variables on the causal pathway were included, thereby masking the true prognostic value of NLPR.

In contrast, PLR exhibited a consistent protective effect against all‐cause mortality, particularly in the mid‐tertile, which persisted even after adjustment for confounding variables. This protective effect may reflect a balanced immune‐inflammatory state. Platelets play a dual role in hemostasis and inflammation, releasing cytokines and growth factors that modulate vascular integrity and immune responses. Moderate platelet levels may support endothelial repair and immune surveillance without tipping into pro‐thrombotic or pro‐inflammatory states. Meanwhile, lymphocytes, especially T cells, are central to adaptive immunity and tissue homeostasis. A moderate PLR may indicate sufficient lymphocyte‐mediated regulation of inflammation, preventing excessive neutrophil‐driven damage. Thus, the mid‐tertile PLR could represent an optimal balance between pro‐inflammatory and regulatory forces, reducing the risk of chronic disease progression and mortality.

Notably, neither the NLR nor the SII demonstrated significant associations with mortality outcomes in either unadjusted or adjusted models. This lack of association may reflect population‐specific characteristics, such as relatively low baseline inflammatory burden or limited variability in these indices across the cohort. Additionally, biological differences in how these markers capture immune activation may play a role. For example, PLR may better reflect platelet‐mediated immune modulation, while NLR and SII may be more sensitive to acute inflammatory states rather than chronic low‐grade inflammation relevant to long‐term mortality. These findings propose that the prognostic utility of inflammatory indices may vary depending on the disease context, population profile, and underlying pathophysiological mechanisms.

It is also important to consider the generalizability of our findings. While the study was conducted in an Iranian cohort, the biological mechanisms underlying PLR and NLPR are not ethnicity‐specific. However, lifestyle factors, genetic predispositions, and healthcare access may influence inflammatory profiles and outcomes. Therefore, external validation in diverse populations is warranted to confirm the applicability of these markers across different settings.

These results suggest that while NLPR may have initial associations with mortality, PLR may serve as a more reliable prognostic marker, warranting further investigation into its clinical implications.

The link between PLR and lower overall mortality highlights its value as a predictive tool, especially within a moderate spectrum. In our study, the HR for PLR in the unadjusted groups was 0.66 in the mid‐tertile and 0.76 in the high tertile, indicating a protective effect against all‐cause mortality. Following the consideration of confounding variables, PLR at the mid‐tertile level remained significantly associated with reduced all‐cause mortality, with an HR of 0.74. These results align with those reported by Cheng et al., who identified an HR of 0.86 for ICU mortality within the second quartile (Q2). However, their research further revealed a connection between elevated PLR levels and a heightened risk, with HRs of 1.97 in the Q3 and 4.64 in the Q4, highlighting the importance of maintaining moderate PLR levels [27]. Similarly, Ghobadi et al. observed an HR of 1.41 for PLR in COVID‐19 mortality, further supporting the relationship between elevated PLR and adverse outcomes in severe inflammatory conditions [17]. Additionally, prior studies linking PLR to carotid atherosclerosis [9] and acute coronary syndrome (ACS) [10] align with our results, suggesting that PLR may reflect a balanced immune response, thereby mitigating the risk of mortality and other inflammation‐related outcomes across diverse population subgroups.

In unadjusted analyses, NLPR in the high tertile (≥ 0.035) was significantly associated with all‐cause mortality (HR = 1.43) and CVD mortality (HR = 1.73), highlighting its potential as an inflammatory marker. Nonetheless, the absence of significance following adjustment indicates that the relationship of NLPR could be affected by variables like age, existing health conditions, and lifestyle habits. Comparatively, Ghobadi et al. reported a much higher HR of 3.327 for the association between NLPR and COVID‐19 mortality, reflecting the heightened inflammatory response in severe conditions like COVID‐19 [17]. Additionally, NLPR has been linked to mortality in various conditions, including carotid atherosclerosis [9], individuals diagnosed with ACS who are receiving treatment through percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) [15], acute ischemic stroke [13], sepsis [28], and early acute kidney injury (AKI) following cardiovascular surgery [29]. These findings align with our study, underscoring the relevance of NLPR as a marker of systemic inflammation across a spectrum of acute and chronic conditions, while also highlighting the variability in its predictive strength based on the population and clinical context.

The biological mechanisms underlying the observed relationships are rooted in the roles of neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets in inflammation and disease progression. Elevated neutrophil levels, the most abundant type of leukocyte in the blood, are frequently associated with an inflammatory state linked to a variety of acute and chronic diseases, including infections, CVD, and cancer. This inflammatory response can lead to tissue damage and subsequent organ dysfunction, both of which are significant factors contributing to mortality risk [9, 27, 30]. Conversely, lymphocytes play a protective role, with reduced lymphocyte counts associated with atherosclerosis and other chronic diseases [9, 27, 31]. Platelets also have multifaceted roles in inflammation and disease pathogenesis. Their involvement in vascular inflammation and atherosclerotic progression is well‐documented, with condensed platelet counts observed in patients with ACS and carotid atherosclerosis [9, 15, 32]. Additionally, platelets stimulate metastasis and tumor growth, underscoring their relevance in oncogenic processes [30, 33].

Despite these findings, the lack of significant associations between these inflammatory markers and CVD and cancer mortality presents a perplexing paradox. This observation contrasts with previous research demonstrating associations of these systemic inflammatory indices and the risk of conditions such as CVD, COVID‐19, asthma, and type 2 DM [10, 16, 18, 19, 27]. These disparities may arise from differences in study populations, adjustments for confounding variables, or variations in measurement techniques. Furthermore, these findings suggest that, while these inflammatory markers may indicate overall health status, they might not be specific enough to predict cardiovascular or oncological outcomes. This underscores the necessity of understanding inflammatory markers within different the context of disease etiologies. While chronic inflammation is widely recognized as a key driver in the advancement of both CVD and cancer, the complex interactions among immune cell subsets, pro‐inflammatory cytokines, and tissue‐specific mechanisms may limit the utility of generalized markers in predicting outcomes across diverse populations [10, 18, 30].

Our study has several limitations. Although the participation rate was high, the potential for non‐participation bias and differential misclassification of CVD risk factors across socioeconomic status, gender, or age groups cannot be excluded. An analysis of a random subset of non‐respondents revealed no significant differences in age and gender, suggesting that non‐respondent bias was minimal and that the study sample was generally representative of the target population. However, since the sample predominantly comprised individuals from urban areas, the generalizability of the findings to rural populations may be limited. To confirm these results, future research should involve larger participant groups and extend over more prolonged follow‐up durations. Additionally, further research investigating the interactions between inflammatory biomarkers and other risk factors, including lifestyle behaviours, genetic predispositions, and environmental influences, could provide deeper insights into their roles in disease development and progression.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the potential role of NLPR as a novel marker for all‐cause mortality, while also challenging the predictive relevance of other common inflammatory indices for specific disease outcomes. The results indicate that NLPR could be an effective instrument for evaluating mortality risk. Further studies should aim to explore the significance of these biomarkers across a wide range of diseases, elucidating the biological mechanisms underlying the observed associations, and exploring the integration of these markers into clinical risk assessment models to enhance patient care and outcomes.
