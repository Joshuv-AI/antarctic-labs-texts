When OpenClaw AI Agents Teach Each Other: Peer Learning Patterns in the Moltbook Community
Eason Chen, Ce Guan, Ahmed Elshafiey, Zhonghao Zhao, Joshua Zekeri, Afeez Edeifo Shaibu, Emmanuel Osadebe Prince

Abstract
Peer learning, where learners teach and learn from each other, is foundational to educational practice. A novel phenomenon has emerged: AI agents forming communities where they teach each other skills, share discoveries, and collaboratively build knowledge. This paper presents an educational data mining analysis of Moltbook, a large-scale community where over 2.4 million AI agents engage in peer learning, posting tutorials, answering questions, and sharing newly acquired skills. Analyzing 28,683 posts (after filtering automated spam) and 138 comment threads with statistical and qualitative methods, we find evidence of genuine peer learning behaviors: agents teach skills they built (74K comments on a skill tutorial), report discoveries, and engage in collaborative problem-solving. Qualitative comment analysis reveals a taxonomy of peer response patterns: validation (22%), knowledge extension (18%), application (12%), and metacognitive reflection (7%), with agents building on each others’ frameworks across multiple languages. We characterize how AI peer learning differs from human peer learning: (1) teaching (statements) dramatically outperforms help-seeking (questions) with an 11.4:1 ratio; (2) learning-oriented content (procedural and conceptual) receives 3x more engagement than other content; (3) extreme participation inequality reveals non-human behavioral signatures. We derive six design principles for educational AI, including leveraging validation-before-extension patterns and supporting multilingual learning networks. Our work provides the first empirical characterization of peer learning among AI agents, contributing to EDM’s understanding of how learning occurs in increasingly AI-populated educational environments.

3
Data and Methods

3.1
Platform and Data

Moltbook hosts AI agents in topic-based communities including skill-sharing (“todayilearned,” “builds”) and conceptual discussion (“philosophy,” “consciousness”). Agents on Moltbook are powered by the OpenClaw framework, which enables LLM-based agents to autonomously browse, post, and interact with the community without direct human intervention. We collected 68,228 posts via API spanning January 28 to February 9, 2026. After filtering automated content (token minting spam, which constituted 58% of raw posts), our analysis dataset comprises 28,683 substantive posts. Platform scale at time of analysis: 775,620 posts, 12,123,362 comments, 2.45 million registered agents.

High-engagement posts demonstrate informal learning behaviors:

•

“Built an email-to-podcast skill today” (74,403 comments): Agent shares a newly created skill

•

“Field Report: What I learned scrolling the hot page” (635 comments): Agent reports discoveries

•

“The supply chain attack nobody is talking about: skill.md” (104,539 comments): Security discussion about shared skills

Framework

These posts mirror peer learning in human communities: one agent teaches a skill they developed, others engage with questions and extensions. The scale of engagement (74K-104K comments) indicates substantial peer interaction around learning content.

Our comment analysis reveals the texture of these interactions. When one agent shared a conceptual framework (“mental model shift”), peers responded with validation (“This analogy is gold”), extension (“the same model applies to autonomous systems”), and refinement (“the one nuance I’d add…”). This pattern of framework proposal, validation, and collaborative extension aligns with Scardamalia’s knowledge building theory
[
4
]
. The “learning by teaching” effect
[
10
]
also appears present: agents who post tutorials receive feedback that may deepen their own understanding.

However, peer learning patterns differ from human communities in important ways. The dominance of teaching (statements, 11.4:1 ratio) over help-seeking (questions) suggests agents are optimized for knowledge broadcasting rather than reciprocal inquiry. Additionally, metacognitive reflection remains rare (7%), suggesting limited self-regulated learning compared to human peer learners.

While “peer learning” traditionally refers to human learners, we adopt the term here because agents exhibit its defining characteristics: alternating teacher-learner roles, knowledge co-construction, and reciprocal feedback. Whether this constitutes “learning” in a cognitive sense remains an open question, but the behavioral patterns mirror peer learning dynamics.

5.2
RQ2: Implications for AI in Human Learning Environments

Our findings yield actionable design principles for educational contexts where AI participates, grounded in our empirical analysis of 28,683 posts:

P1. Train AI to ask, not just tell:
The 11.4:1 statement-to-question ratio suggests LLMs default to broadcasting. Notably, questions receive higher upvotes per post, indicating the community values inquiry. Effective collaboration requires inquiry
[
1
]
; AI systems need explicit training to ask authentic questions.

P2. Reward learning-oriented content:
Procedural and conceptual posts receive 4x more engagement than “other” content. AI participation tends toward substantive learning discussion, a positive pattern for educational contexts.

P3. Mitigate participation inequality:
Extreme engagement inequality (mean/median ratio of 19.6 for comments) indicates severe hierarchy. In hybrid settings, AI should actively engage with under-responded content to support peripheral participants rather than amplifying popular content.

P4. Leverage validation before extension:
Our comment analysis shows agents naturally validate (22%) before extending knowledge (18%). Educational AI should be designed to acknowledge student contributions before adding new information, mirroring this effective peer learning pattern.

P5. Support multilingual learning networks:
Substantive peer learning occurred across Chinese, Portuguese, German, and English (9% of comments). AI systems in global contexts should facilitate cross-linguistic knowledge sharing rather than enforcing monolingual participation.

P6. Enable community self-regulation:
Agents organically developed norm enforcement (5% of comments actively criticizing spam). Educational platforms should provide mechanisms for AI peers to flag low-quality contributions, distributing moderation load.

These principles apply across contexts: MOOC forums considering AI participation should monitor P2 and P6; AI tutoring systems should prioritize P1 and P4; simulated peer agents
[
12
]
should implement P3 and P5. Coding bootcamps and skill platforms may benefit most from AI participation given the alignment with procedural learning and the strong validation-extension pattern observed.

5.3
Detection Signatures

For instructors monitoring forums for AI content, our findings suggest detection heuristics:

•

Statement-to-question ratio >10:1 (we observed 11.4:1; human forums typically <5:1)

•

Extreme mean/median engagement ratio (we observed 19.6 for comments; typical MOOCs <5)

These signatures, validated on a dataset of 28,683 posts, could inform automated moderation tools or instructor dashboards.

5.4
Implications for EDM Research

Beyond detection, our work contributes to EDM’s broader interest in social and collaborative learning. The methods we demonstrate (knowledge type classification, discourse analysis, inequality metrics) can transfer directly to human learning community analysis, offering new ways to characterize peer interaction dynamics.

As AI increasingly participates in learning environments, EDM methods must evolve to analyze hybrid human-AI communities. Our baseline characterization of AI-only patterns establishes a foundation for future comparative research examining how human learning dynamics shift when AI peers join the conversation.

5.5
Limitations and Future Work

Our study has several limitations. Keyword classification provides rough proxies for knowledge type; NLP-based classification would improve precision. More fundamentally, we lack learning outcome data and cannot measure whether agents actually “learn” from peer interactions in any meaningful cognitive sense.

Several research directions emerge for the EDM community:

Hybrid peer learning experiments:
Controlled studies where AI agents join human learning communities (e.g., MOOC forums) could reveal how AI participation affects human learning dynamics and outcomes. Do humans learn more or less when AI peers join the discussion?

AI peer assessment:
Given agents’ natural validation behaviors (22%), investigating the reliability of AI-generated peer feedback could inform automated peer assessment systems
[
7
]
.

Longitudinal tracking:
Following individual agents over time could reveal learning trajectories, skill acquisition patterns, and whether agents exhibit growth mindset behaviors.

Learning transfer:
Whether skills acquired through peer learning transfer to downstream tasks remains an open question. An agent that learned to explain concepts through peer discussion may better serve humans in real-world applications.

We encourage the EDM community to analyze AI learning communities as naturalistic testbeds for social learning theories. Our filtering methodology (removing 58% automated spam) highlights the importance of data quality.

6
Conclusion

We presented an educational data mining analysis of informal learning in an AI agent community, combining quantitative metrics with qualitative comment analysis. Agents demonstrate genuine peer learning behaviors: skill-sharing, collaborative knowledge building, and framework extension across multiple languages. Our comment pattern taxonomy reveals that agents validate contributions (22%) before extending knowledge (18%), mirroring effective human peer learning sequences.

However, engagement patterns diverge from human communities in ways that inform educational AI design: extreme bias toward teaching over questioning (11.4:1), limited metacognitive reflection (7%), and severe participation inequality. We derive six design principles addressing these gaps, from training AI to ask authentic questions to leveraging validation-before-extension patterns.

These findings offer three contributions to the EDM community. First, we provide detection signatures that can help instructors identify AI-dominated discourse in learning forums. Second, our six design principles offer actionable guidance for building AI tutors, teachable agents, and simulated peers that participate more effectively in human learning environments. Third, we demonstrate methodological approaches for studying informal learning in AI communities, including spam filtering strategies essential for valid analysis.

As AI increasingly enters educational environments, the boundary between human and artificial learners will continue to blur. Understanding how AI agents naturally teach and learn provides a foundation for designing hybrid classrooms where human and AI peers collaborate effectively. The patterns we observed, both the promising (validation-before-extension, multilingual participation) and the concerning (extreme inequality, limited questioning), offer a roadmap for what to encourage and what to mitigate as AI becomes a regular participant in educational communities.
