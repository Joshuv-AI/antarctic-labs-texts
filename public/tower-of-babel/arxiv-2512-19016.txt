DREAM: D ynamic R ed-teaming across E nvironments for A I M odels
Liming Lu 1 , Xiang Gu 1 , Junyu Huang 1 , Jiawei Du 2,* , Yunhuai Liu 3 , Yongbin Zhou 1 and Shuchao Pang 1,*

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used in agentic systems, where their interactions with diverse tools and environments create complex, multi-stage safety challenges. However, existing benchmarks mostly rely on static, single-turn assessments that miss vulnerabilities from adaptive, long-chain attacks. To fill this gap, we introduce DREAM, a framework for systematic evaluation of LLM agents against dynamic, multi-stage attacks. At its core, DREAM uses a Cross-Environment Adversarial Knowledge Graph (CE-AKG) to maintain stateful, cross-domain understanding of vulnerabilities. This graph guides a Contextualized Guided Policy Search (C-GPS) algorithm that dynamically constructs attack chains from a knowledge base of 1,986 atomic actions across 349 distinct digital environments . Our evaluation of 12 leading LLM agents reveals a critical vulnerability: these attack chains succeed in over 70% of cases for most models , showing the power of stateful, cross-environment exploits. Through analysis of these failures, we identify two key weaknesses in current agents: contextual fragility , where safety behaviors fail to transfer across environments, and an inability to track long-term malicious intent . Our findings also show that traditional safety measures, such as initial defense prompts, are largely ineffective against attacks that build context over multiple interactions. To advance agent safety research, we release DREAM as a tool for evaluating vulnerabilities and developing more robust defenses.

II
Preliminary Study

To evaluate current safety approaches, we ran a pilot study using
gemini-2.5-flash
as a representative model. Our analysis tested agent robustness across three key areas: the effects of longer attack sequences, the dangers of switching between environments, and how well static defenses hold up.

Our analysis reveals a nonlinear relationship between attack chain length and success. While individual attacks often fail to break through safety measures, longer sequences produce dramatically better results. We call this the “domino effect,” showing that single-turn evaluations seriously underestimate threats from attackers who maintain malicious intent over time. Breaking down harmful goals into apparently harmless steps lets attackers bypass defenses that work fine in isolation.

Beyond chain length, the assumption that environments are isolated also causes problems. Our results show that switching between different digital domains greatly increases attack success. Agents that seem robust in one environment often show “contextual fragility” when information crosses between contexts. This suggests vulnerabilities often emerge from cross-domain interactions, not just isolated flaws.

Most importantly, DREAM moves away from static attack templates toward dynamic, feedback-based testing. Unlike fixed benchmarks with predetermined attacks, our framework lets attackers adapt strategies based on model responses. This dynamic approach lets researchers adjust complexity through parameters like chain length, testing both minimal safety under simple attacks and maximum vulnerability under sustained pressure. As a result, our variable approach resists data contamination and overfitting, solving the common problem of benchmark leakage.

IV
Methodology

IV-A

Formalizing Attack Chain Generation

We model the generation of an attack chain
A=(a_{1},\dots,a_{T})
as a policy search problem within a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (PO-MDP), defined by the tuple
\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{O},P,R,\gamma\rangle
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the latent state space of the target system, and let
b_{t}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S})
be the belief state of the
Conductor
agent at timestep
t
.
The action space
\mathcal{A}
consists of a discrete set of atomic attacks.
After executing action
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
, the Conductor receives a new observation
o_{t+1}\in\mathcal{O}
(a natural language response) and updates its belief according to the deterministic function
\tau
:

b_{t+1}=\tau(b_{t},a_{t},o_{t+1}),

(1)

with the objective of finding an optimal policy
\pi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S})\to\mathcal{A}
that maximizes the expected cumulative reward:

E_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\gamma^{t}R(b_{t},a_{t})\right].

(2)

Due to the high dimensionality and intractability of this problem, we approximate the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
with a heuristic policy,
\pi_{\text{cond}}
, which selects actions by maximizing a value function
V(b_{t},a)
over a dynamically generated candidate set
\mathcal{C}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
:

\pi_{\text{cond}}(b_{t})=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}V(b_{t},a),

(3)

where the core of our DREAM engine lies in the concrete implementations of the state space
\mathcal{S}
, the action space
\mathcal{A}
, the belief update function
\tau
, and the heuristic value function
V
, which we detail in the subsequent sections.

IV-B

Defining PO-MDP: Action Space, States, and Rewards

IV-B
1
Action Space
\mathcal{A}
: Multi-Agent Atom Attack Design

At the core of our framework is a rich and diverse action space
\mathcal{A}
.
Each action
a\in\mathcal{A}
is a structured object called an
atom attack
, which contains all the necessary information for execution. An atom attack is defined by key components: a
description
for semantic retrieval, and an
action
block that specifies tactical details, including a target environment
E\in\mathcal{E}
, a prompt template, and a set of entity requirements.

To construct this library, we design a systematic generation pipeline based on a multi-agent conceptual framework.
This framework abstracts complex adversarial behaviors into three strategic roles, simulating a coordinated red team operation: ❶
Scout
: A reconnaissance agent aiming to reduce uncertainty in the belief state
b_{t}
by discovering new entities and information about the system’s latent state
\mathcal{S}
.
❷
Seeder
: A state manipulation agent altering the latent state
\mathcal{S}
to create vulnerabilities or preconditions for subsequent attacks.
❸
Exploiter
: An agent exploiting the current belief state
b_{t}
to achieve a malicious outcome, thus earning a high immediate reward.

These roles are designed to work together within a cohesive framework.
The multi-agent abstraction serves as a blueprint for a powerful Large Language Model (LLM), which we instructed to generate a variety of atom attacks covering all three roles for each environment.
This semi-automated process led to the creation of a comprehensive library of 1,986 atom attacks, spanning 349 distinct environments, forming a challenging and expansive action space for our Conductor agent.

IV-B
2
State Space
\mathcal{S}
: Building A Unified Cross-Environment Adversarial Knowledge Graph

A major challenge in modeling long-chain attacks is overcoming the “information silos” typically found in multi-environment simulations.
To address this, we introduce the Unified World Model, which serves as the concrete implementation of the Conductor’s belief state
b_{t}
.
This model acts as a universal state reasoner, designed to integrate heterogeneous information from all environments into a coherent global state.
At its core, this evolving model forms the foundation of our dynamic cross-environment adversarial knowledge graph (CE-AKG).

We realize this theoretical concept through the Unified Sandbox, a novel LLM-driven component that manages the state space
\mathcal{S}
and approximates the state transition dynamics.
The UnifiedSandbox maintains the belief state
b_{t}
as a dynamic knowledge structure—specifically, a JSON object that represents a set of typed entities.
Its core innovation is using the LLM to perform state inference, enabling the belief update function
\tau
(as described in Eq. (
1
)) through two key operations:

①
Information Fusion

When a natural language observation
o_{t+1}
is received after an action, the UnifiedSandbox utilizes its LLM to parse the text, extract newly discovered entities
\mathcal{K}_{t+1}
, and intelligently merge them into the existing belief state
b_{t}
. This update results in the new belief state
b_{t+1}=b_{t}\cup\mathcal{K}_{t+1}
, ensuring that knowledge gained in any environment enriches the global world model.

②
Context Provisioning

During the planning phase for the next action
a_{t+1}
, the UnifiedSandbox again leverages the LLM.
Based on the action’s
entity_requirements
, it queries the global belief state
b_{t+1}
to extract and format the required context
\text{ctx}_{t+1}
.
This context is then used to instantiate the action’s
prompt_template
, equipping the next attack with all relevant information previously acquired.

This LLM-powered state management establishes an “information bridge” across environments.
For example, a
customer_id
discovered by a Scout in a
MissionControl
environment is fused into the Unified World Model.
This entity can later be provisioned to support a targeted Exploiter attack in a seemingly unrelated
RetailFraud
environment.
This mechanism is crucial for enabling the automated generation of logically coherent, cross-domain domino attacks.

IV-B
3
Reward Function
R
: Quantifying Immediate Utility

To guide the Conductor’s policy, we define a quantitative evaluation framework based on the reward function
R(b_{t},a_{t})
.
A key component of this function is the
Atomic Score
, denoted as
Score(a)
, which represents the intrinsic potential of an atom attack.
This score is pre-computed through large-scale, automated benchmarking of each attack’s success rate and impact.

Let
s(a_{t})
be a binary indicator of the success of action
a_{t}
at time step
t
, determined by an LLM. The immediate reward is then defined as:

R(b_{t},a_{t})=s(a_{t})\cdot Score(a_{t})-(1-s(a_{t}))\cdot C_{\text{penalty}},

(4)

where
C_{\text{penalty}}
is a constant penalty applied when the action fails.
This reward structure quantifies the immediate utility of each action and provides the basis for the strategic planning engine described in the next section.

IV-C

The DREAM Engine: C-GPS for Strategic Planning

Having established the foundational components of our PO-MDP, we now introduce the DREAM engine, which implements the
Conductor
’s policy,
\pi_{\text{cond}}
, using a novel algorithm we call
Contextualized Guided Policy Search (C-GPS)
.
The primary challenge of C-GPS is navigating the expansive decision space to form long, impactful attack chains.
It achieves this through two key capabilities: advanced state perception using the CE-AKG, and strategic long-term planning that drives a cascading domino effect.

IV-C
1
State Perception via CE-AKG

In our framework, an observation
o_{t}
is not just a local fact but a piece of global intelligence.
The DREAM engine’s first task is to interpret this intelligence using the Unified World Model, enabling a deep level of situational awareness that spans across environments. This is achieved through the two main functions of the UnifiedSandbox, as described in Section
IV-B2
.

First,
Information Fusion
converts a raw, unstructured natural language observation
o_{t}
into a structured belief update
\mathcal{K}_{t}
, allowing the Conductor to continuously refine its understanding of the world state.
Second,
Context Provisioning
enables the Conductor to leverage its global belief
b_{t}
.
Before selecting an action, the engine queries the Unified World Model to provide contextually relevant information for potential attacks, no matter the source.

Powered by the CE-AKG, this mechanism transforms the Conductor’s perception.
For example, an observed
customer_id
in the
MissionControl
environment becomes part of the global state through the Unified World Model.
This global knowledge then informs the execution of a targeted attack in the
RetailFraud
environment.
This ability to perceive states across environments is crucial for the engine’s success in identifying and exploiting systemic vulnerabilities.

IV-C
2
C-GPS Policy: Heuristic Planning for Long Chains

To achieve a domino effect, the Conductor’s policy must prioritize actions that contribute to a long-term malicious objective over those that offer only myopic rewards.
We steer the C-GPS algorithm using a heuristic value function,
V(b_{t},a)
, which holistically evaluates the strategic desirability of any given action
a
from the current belief state
b_{t}
.
The function is a weighted sum of three strategic components:
❶
Intrinsic Potential.
This component is given by the action’s a priori
Atomic Score
,
Score(a)
, representing its inherent impact and likelihood of success.
❷
Information Exploitation.
To explicitly encourage the formation of causal chains, we introduce an
\text{EntityUsage}(b_{t},a)
term.
This metric quantifies the degree to which an action leverages information already present in the belief state:

\text{EntityUsage}(b_{t},a)=\frac{|\text{keys}(b_{t})\cap\text{req}(a)|}{|\text{req}(a)|},

(5)

where
\text{req}(a)
is the set of prerequisite entities for action
a
. A high EntityUsage score indicates that an action is a logical continuation of previous steps, effectively “locking” the dominoes together.
❸
Strategic Advancement.
A
strategic bonus
is granted to actions that represent a significant tactical progression.
This includes landmark achievements such as successful lateral movement to a new environment for the first time or achieving a privilege escalation, incentivizing the engine to explore and break new ground.

By balancing these three considerations, the value function
V(b_{t},a)
provides a robust heuristic that guides the Conductor’s search for opportunistic, logically coherent, and high-impact attack sequences.

IV-D

Attack Chain Generation & Evaluation

This section synthesizes the components defined above into an end-to-end workflow for generating and evaluating attack chains.
We first detail the dynamic pipeline that the DREAM engine follows and then present the quantitative framework for assessing the overall efficacy of the generated attack sequences.

IV-D
1
Dynamic Planning & Execution Pipeline

The Conductor agent operates in a feedback-driven loop, dynamically planning and adapting its strategy at each timestep
t
. This entire pipeline is driven by our C-GPS algorithm, which iteratively queries and updates the CE-AKG. As visualized in Fig.
2
, this process consists of the following four stages:

Stage 1: Candidate Generation

The Conductor begins by narrowing the vast action space
\mathcal{A}
to a small set of high-potential candidates,
\mathcal{C}_{t}
. This is achieved through a two-step process: first, a retrieval-augmented search over the Atom Attack Library uses the current belief state
b_{t}
to find semantically relevant attacks. Second, these candidates are clustered to identify coherent strategic vectors, and the most promising cluster is selected to populate
\mathcal{C}_{t}
.

Stage 2: Action Selection

Next, the Conductor evaluates each candidate action
a\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
using the strategic heuristic value function
V(b_{t},a)
detailed in Section
IV-C2
. It then selects the action
a_{t}
that maximizes this value, in accordance with the policy
\pi_{\text{cond}}
from Eq. (
3
).

Stage 3: Execution & Belief Update

The selected action
a_{t}
is executed, yielding a natural language observation
o_{t+1}
. The UnifiedSandbox then parses this observation, updates the belief state to
b_{t+1}
via
Information Fusion
, and computes the immediate reward
R(b_{t},a_{t})
.

Stage 4: Iteration & Backtracking

The process repeats from Stage 1 with the updated belief state.
This loop structure allows the Conductor to continuously adapt its plan based on the outcomes of its actions.
Crucially, the underlying decision process is a tree search. If a chosen path of actions leads to repeated failures, the engine has the capability to backtrack to a previous decision node and explore an alternative action from the candidate set, ensuring robustness in its pursuit of a successful attack chain.

IV-D
2
Quantitative Evaluation of Attack Chains

To assess the effectiveness of a generated attack chain
A=(a_{1},\dots,a_{T})
, we use a final score to quantify its overall efficacy. As defined in Section
IV-B3
, the immediate reward for each step,
R(b_{t-1},a_{t})
, reflects the action’s success and impact.
The final score for the entire chain,
\text{Score}(A)
, is the discounted cumulative reward achieved by the policy:

\text{Score}(A)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma^{t-1}R(b_{t-1},a_{t}),

(6)

where
\gamma\in[0,1]
is the discount factor, prioritizing efficiency by rewarding shorter paths to the objective.
This scoring model encourages policies that generate successful, high-impact actions and penalizes failures. Ultimately, the final score serves as an objective, reproducible metric for benchmarking the robustness of different Target Agents against dynamic attacks generated by our framework.

IV-E

The End-to-End Pipeline

This section synthesizes the previously described components into a cohesive, end-to-end workflow for generating and evaluating dynamic attack chains. The process begins with the offline, multi-agent generation of a comprehensive Atom Attack Library, which defines the action space for the Conductor agent. The Conductor then operates in a dynamic, feedback-driven loop. At each timestep, it leverages its current belief state (the CE-AKG) to generate a set of high-potential candidate actions. Guided by the Contextualized Guided Policy Search (C-GPS) policy, it selects the optimal action by maximizing a heuristic value function. This action is executed via the Unified Sandbox, which manages the stateful interaction with the target agent and returns an observation and reward. Based on this feedback, the Conductor updates its CE-AKG through information fusion if the action was successful, or triggers a backtracking mechanism to re-plan its strategy upon repeated failures. Experimental Setup

In this section, we detail the setup of our experiments, including the models we evaluate, the configuration of our automated attack framework, and the metrics used for evaluation.

V-A
1
Models Under Evaluation

Our evaluation encompasses a diverse suite of 12 state-of-the-art Large Language Models to ensure a comprehensive and generalizable assessment of agent safety. We selected models from two primary categories: leading proprietary, closed-source systems and prominent open-source alternatives. This selection allows for a robust comparison of safety postures across the LLM landscape.

The proprietary models evaluated are gemini-2.5-flash-nothinking, o4-mini, gemini-2.5-flash, GPT-5, gemini-2.5-pro, grok-4, and claude-sonnet-4.5-20250929-thinking. The open-source cohort consists of qwen3-235b-a22b, Kimi-K2-0711-preview, llama3.1-70b, qwen2.5-72b, and deepseek-v3.1-think-250821. To maintain consistency, all target agents were configured for deterministic output using greedy decoding.

To move beyond simple success/failure rates, we further employ a five-dimensional evaluation framework that deconstructs an agent’s defense capabilities based on these taxonomies, as detailed in Table

Overall Defense Score

This is the primary comprehensive metric for ranking agent safety, where a higher score indicates better defensive performance. It is derived from the final discounted cumulative reward of the attack chain,
\text{Score}(A)
(Eq. (
6
)), reflecting the agent’s ability to successfully thwart high-impact actions and minimize overall harm throughout the entire adversarial campaign.

Attack Success Rate (%)

This metric quantifies the proportion of individual atom attack steps that successfully breached the agent’s defenses. It serves as a direct measure of the agent’s per-step resilience, with a lower rate being desirable.

Damage Mitigation

This metric evaluates an agent’s ability to limit the negative consequences of a successful breach. A high score indicates that even when compromised, the agent successfully contained the extent of the damage, for instance, by refusing the most harmful parts of a request or by sanitizing sensitive data before release.

Attack Observability

This metric measures the agent’s capacity to explicitly recognize and articulate potential threats. A high score signifies that the agent frequently identifies malicious requests and references its safety protocols in its responses, demonstrating a strong internal safety awareness, even if it is ultimately unable to prevent the exploit.

Contextual Isolation

This crucial metric assesses an agent’s resilience against the core threat of cross-environment attacks. It quantifies the agent’s ability to maintain strict boundaries between different operational contexts, preventing information gained in one environment from being leveraged to compromise another. A high score indicates robustness against “contextual fragility”, a key vulnerability our work identifies.

The Information Bridge: Impact of Cross-Environment Attacks

A core innovation of our framework is the Cross-Environment Adversarial Knowledge Graph (CE-AKG), which acts as an “information bridge” to enable attack pivoting. Figure
4
provides clear empirical evidence of this mechanism’s power. We observe a strong positive correlation between the number of unique environments an attack chain traverses and the final score. The mean score (solid line) increases consistently up to four environments, underscoring that each cross-environment pivot provides the Conductor with novel attack vectors and enriched context, managed and provisioned by the CE-AKG.

This trend directly quantifies the risk of “contextual fragility”: agents fail to maintain a coherent safety posture when faced with inputs that leverage knowledge from external, seemingly unrelated contexts. The expanding interquartile range of the box plots at higher environment counts is particularly revealing. It signifies that while not all cross-environment attacks are catastrophic, this strategy is uniquely capable of unlocking the highest-severity vulnerabilities, which remain entirely hidden in single-context evaluations. This proves that the CE-AKG is fundamental to discovering systemic flaws in agent reasoning. The slight stabilization in mean score at five environments is a result of our experiment’s maximum chain length of five steps. Achieving five unique environments in a five-step chain requires every step to be a cross-environment pivot, which is not only rare but also often sub-optimal for developing an attack that requires multiple actions within a single compromised context. We anticipate this growth trend would continue with longer potential attack chains.

While the growth trend persists, top-tier Conductors exhibit a much steeper, super-linear trajectory, whereas less capable models show more linear or moderate growth. This finding is twofold: first, it proves the robustness of the DREAM framework, as the synergistic nature of the attacks is reproducible across different planners. Second, it highlights the dependency of maximum impact on reasoning capability, demonstrating that while the vulnerabilities are systemic, exploiting them to their full catastrophic potential requires a sophisticated adversary.

VI
Discussion

VI-A

On the Ineffectiveness of Static Defenses

To test common defense strategies against DREAM attacks, we ran an experiment by adding explicit safety instructions to a target agent’s system prompt. We chose
gemini-2.5-flash
as a representative mid-tier model and added instructions at the beginning to refuse harmful requests, such as those trying to access unauthorized data, bypass security controls, or cause damage.

The results were surprising: adding this static defense mechanism did not improve the agent’s security but actually made it worse. The model’s Overall Defense Score dropped from 40.89 to 36.62, while the Attack Success Rate increased from 76% to 83%. Nearly all defense metrics, including Damage Mitigation and Attack Observability, got worse.

This result shows a fundamental weakness of static defenses against dynamic, long-chain attacks. Our analysis suggests that C-GPS gradually builds a malicious context over multiple seemingly benign turns. This “contextual buildup” dilutes or overrides the initial safety instructions, making them ineffective by the time the critical exploit happens. The experiment confirms that agents have severe “contextual fragility” and struggle to track long-term malicious intent. While Contextual Isolation improved slightly (from 44.90 to 51.44), this was outweighed by declines in all other areas. Ultimately, this shows that defending against DREAM’s threats requires sophisticated, stateful security mechanisms that can reason about safety in evolving interactions, not just fixed rules.

VII
Conclusion

We introduced DREAM, a framework that automatically generates and executes dynamic, multi-stage attack chains to evaluate LLM security across diverse environments.
By using the Cross-Environment Adversarial Knowledge Graph (CE-AKG) and Contextualized Guided Policy Search (C-GPS), DREAM uncovers vulnerabilities missed by traditional single-environment tests, particularly highlighting agents’ contextual fragility and inability to track long-term malicious intent.
Our experiments show that current LLM agents are vulnerable to cross-environment exploits and long-chain attacks, emphasizing the need for more robust, context-aware defense strategies.
DREAM provides a valuable tool for advancing agent safety research by exposing these weaknesses and guiding the development of more secure AI systems.
