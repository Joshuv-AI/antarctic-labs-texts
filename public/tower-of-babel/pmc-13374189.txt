Wound fluid proteomics reveal elevated alpha-1 antitrypsin levels are associated with healing of medical device related pressure injury
Authors: Haydn A Fox, Daniel A Broszczak, Anna Doubrovsky, Annabel Levido, Jennifer Palmer, Fiona Coyer, Tony J Parker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractThe Medical Device Related Pressure Injuries (MDRPIs) are a wound type that has been extensively tracked and recorded within observational studies, with a global patient prevalence of 19.3% [Zhang N et al. in Eur J Med Res 1:425, 2024] and 28.1% in acute care patients [Brophy S et al. in J Tissue Viability 4:489-498, 2021]. Yet, despite being an injury caused directly by medical intervention, to the author’s knowledge, no in-depth proteomic analysis in this wound type has been published previously. This study represents a novel proteomic analysis of clinical MDRPI samples providing initial characterization of a protein pathway significant to wound healing in the context of MDRPIs developed in an ICU setting (Fig. 1). To investigate the proteomic changes associated with the healing outcomes of medical device related pressure injuries through comprehensive proteome characterisation. Clinical wound fluid samples were collected from Intensive Care Unit (ICU) patients with MDRPIs. These samples underwent analysis using Sequential Window Acquisition of all Theoretical Mass Spectra (SWATH-MS) mass spectrometry. The severity and healing trajectories of the injuries were assessed using the Pressure Ulcer Scale for Healing (PUSH) scores, facilitating the comparison of protein abundances to identify key pathways involved in healing and non-healing MDRPIs. The study identified a significant inverse correlation between the severity of non-healing MDRPIs and the levels of alpha-1 antitrypsin (A1AT) (R = -0.43; P = 0.035). A1AT functions as an inhibitor of neutrophil elastase (NE), which targets kininogen-1 (KNG-1). This relationship was further substantiated by a strong positive correlation between A1AT and KNG-1 levels (R = 0.76; Q < 6.8 × 10− 10), indicating a protective function of A1AT over KNG-1 from NE. Given that KNG-1 contributes to early wound healing through its derivative, bradykinin, the misregulation of A1AT may disrupt the healing process of MDRPIs. The findings suggest that decreased levels of A1AT are associated with increased severity in non-healing MDRPIs, potentially due to the disruption of KNG-1 activity. These insights offer a foundation for further research into targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing the healing of MDRPIs.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12014-026-09609-0.

Introduction

MDRPIs are a significant global healthcare challenge as they are unique among pressure injuries and iatrogenic in nature. MDRPIs develop when medical devices exert prolonged pressure on soft tissues without regular device adjustment, causing localised ischemia, tissue necrosis, and ulceration [3]. Incidence of MDRPIs varies across healthcare settings, patient populations, and usage of different medical devices. Critically ill and immobile patients are at the highest risk for MDRPI formation [4]. Studies found MDRPI incidence rates ranging from 10.95% to 47% in acute care settings for adults [5], and as high as 52% in paediatric patients [6]. Management strategies for MDRPIs focus on prevention through regular repositioning, pressure-relieving interfaces, and early identification of skin compromise [7]. However, treatment challenges remain after MDRPIs develop due to anatomical location and potential for rapid progression.

The standard grading system to classify pressure injuries (PIs) ranges from Stage I (non-blanchable erythema) to Stage IV (full-thickness tissue loss exposing muscle, tendon, or bone) [8]. However, this classification tool is not well-suited for MDRPIs as the vast majority occur on mucosal tissues (endotracheal or nasogastric) [8]. Mucosal tissue lacks keratinised epithelium meaning healing differs significantly from convention [9]. Therefore, the Pressure Ulcer Score for Healing (PUSH), that tracks the PI severity through wound size, exudate level, and tissue appearance assessment is an alternative commonly employed [10]. Proteomic assessment of MDRPI using PUSH scores as a measure of severity and healing, can reveal protein pathways associated with healing trajectories for diagnostics, improved patient outcomes, and initial MDRPI proteome analysis.

Proteomics has emerged as a powerful tool for studying the underlying biology of chronic wounds, enabling identification of biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Proteomic approaches have been applied to venous leg ulcers (VLU) [11], diabetic foot ulcers (DFU) [12], and spinal cord injury-related pressure injuries (SCI) [13], but MDRPIs remains relatively unexplored despite being an extremely prevalent type of PI. Mass spectrometry (MS) proteomic techniques offer valuable insights into protein mechanisms driving tissue breakdown, inflammation, and chronic wound healing. Two categories of MS techniques are utilised in proteomic research: targeted mass spectrometry identifying preselected proteins of interest, and discovery-based methods such as SWATH-MS identifying as many proteins as possible [14, 15]. SWATH-MS is advantageous for comprehensive proteomic profiling, allowing for quantification of all detectable peptides in a sample without bias [16]. This unbiased discovery approach enables the identification of key proteomic pathways associated with wound chronicity and healing potential.

This study aimed to fill a gap in wound proteomics by examining the MDRPI proteome and identify protein-level mechanisms in healing and non-healing MDRPIs, with a specific focus on protease–antiprotease pathways implicated in chronic wound pathology. This was accomplished by employing SWATH-MS to analyse MDRPI wound fluid samples from ICU patients, uncovering proteomic trends and pathways related to severity and healing of MDRPIs.

Experimental procedures

Electronic laboratory notebook platforms were not used. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE [17] partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD069939 and 10.6019/PXD069939.

Experimental design and statistical rationale

MDRPIs represent a clinically significant yet understudied wound type, with high prevalence in the acute care settings with limited mechanistic insight into their healing biology. Because there have been very limited prior proteomic investigations that characterized the molecular environment of MDRPIs, we designed this study to generate a comprehensive protein profile of MDRPI wound fluid and identify pathways associated with divergent healing trajectories. We collected wound exudate from ICU patients presenting with MDRPIs and paired each sample with longitudinal PUSH scores to enable correlation of protein abundance with clinical severity and healing outcomes. To obtain reproducible, quantitative, and unbiased proteome coverage suitable for small-volume clinical samples, we employed SWATH-MS. Biological variability inherent to clinical wounds was addressed by integrating standardized collection protocols, matched sampling intervals where possible, robust protein extraction, digestion, and SWATH-MS analysis methods, and rigorous downstream statistical modelling. This experimental framework enabled us to uncover a previously unreported association between A1AT levels and MDRPI healing status, providing initial mechanistic evidence linking A1AT–NE–KNG-1 signalling to wound repair in this device-related injury context.

Ethics statement

Participant recruitment, data and sample collection, and analysis for this study was approved by the Royal Brisbane and Women’s Hospital (RBWH) Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) HREC/2019/QRBW/50,988 and QUT HREC (QUT1900000306). All guardians of participants provided written informed consent for inclusion before taking part in the study.

Participants and medical device recordings

Participant recruitment, data and sample collection, and analysis for this study was approved by the Royal Brisbane and Women’s Hospital (RBWH) Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) HREC/2019/QRBW/50,988 and QUT HREC (QUT1900000306). Following informed consent from family members of patients, 13 patients in the ICU at the RBWH Brisbane Australia, aged over 18 years of age (22 yrs – 70 yrs) and who developed a MDRPI because of nasogastric/endotracheal tubes or bite blocks, were recruited to the study. Patients that had other wounds including burn injuries, facial trauma, compromised immunity, or graft versus host disease were excluded from participation. Comorbidities including hypertension, diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, smoking and any other comorbidities that might have been of note to the clinical team were recorded. Details such as time of collection and clinical parameters including primary ICU admission diagnosis, whether mechanical ventilation was required; Glasgow Coma Scale score; PaO2/FiO2 ratio; serum haematocrit level and 24 h vasopressin use.

Wound fluid collection method

PI wound fluid was collected from patients as close to daily as possible, based on availability of nursing staff and patient care schedules. Cotton swabs (medical wire, MW1048UL) were used to collect wound fluid from the PIs by rolling the cotton head over the top of the wound. See “Supporting Information – Wound Fluid Collection Method” for more details.

Small volume wound fluid protein extraction

This protocol was modified based off the protein extraction protocol described in Schmohl et al. (2012) [18]. The protocol is graphically represented in Fig. 1. See “Supporting Information – Small Volume Wound Fluid Protein Extraction” for more details and reagents utilised.

Modified protein extraction protocol. Cotton swab heads containing MDRPI wound fluid were initially incubated in 0.5% SDC and 10 mM DTT solution. Swab heads were sonicated and then inverted prior to centrifugation to remove as much liquid as possible from the swab head. Samples were filtered through 3-kDa MW cut-off membrane centrifugal devices with 10 mM IAA incubation. Samples were stored at −80 °C until further processing for analysis

Sample preparation for proteomics analysis

Samples desalted using a modified stage-tipping method utilising C18 membranes (Empore Octadecyl C18 47 mm Extraction Disks – 66883-U SUPELCO, Bellefonte, PA, USA), as described previously [19]. See “Sample Preparation for Proteomics Analysis” for more details and reagents utilised.

Generating the spectral library

A peptide spectral library was generated from the pool batch quality control sample using Data Dependant Analysis on a 5600 + TripleTOF mass spectrometer. Peptides were separated via a 95 min LC gradient. Data was analysed in ProteinPilot™ v5.0.2 with a 0.95 confidence threshold. See “Supporting Information – Generating the Spectral Library” for more details.

SWATH protein profiling

Digested peptides were analysed using SWATH-MS on a TripleTOF 5600 + with a 95 min gradient. Peptides were separated via reverse-phase chromatography at 5 µL/min and 40 °C. Quantitation was based on total protein injected (10–20 µg) and analysed using SWATH MS. See “Supporting Information – SWATH Protein Profiling” for more details.

SWATH data analysis

The spectral data was processed in SWATH MicroApp 2.0 (PeakView® 2.2) with iRT calibration. Peptide confidence was set at 99%, False Discovery Rate at 1%. XIC extraction used a 5 min window and 75 ppm width. After filtering, 217 protein IDs were analysed in R Studio. See “Supporting Information – SWATH Data Analysis” for more details.

Cleaning protein abundance data, normalisation and statistical analysis

Any detected protein that had missing values were removed from the data matrix to allow for use of statistical methods in later analysis. R studio 2023.12.0 + 369 and GraphPad Prism 10.0.2 were utilised for statistical tests, data plotting, and graphing. Data was quantile normalised, log(2) transformed and mean centre scaled. Analytical tests for Skewness, Kurtosis, Shapiro-Wilk and Quantile Plotting were conducted to examine the distribution of data. See “Supporting Information – SWATH Data Analysis” for more details. For more details regarding the linear mixed models used within the analysis see “Supporting Information – Mixed Model Analysis of Protein – Protein relationships and PUSH Score Predictors”.

Assignment of MDRPI wound fluid samples as healing/non-healing

The PUSH scores for the study’s MDRPI samples were recorded concurrently with collection of the MDRPI wound fluid samples. To classify the wound healing trajectory (healing/non-healing) for proteomic analysis, samples were grouped based on the change in PUSH score between sequential collection time points. A sample was designated as healing if the subsequent sample PUSH score was lower, indicating a reduction in injury severity. Conversely, a sample was labelled non-healing if the subsequent sample PUSH score was higher or remained the same, indicating either deterioration or stagnation in the healing process. After separating the samples based on this derived healing/non-healing status, correlation analysis was performed to identify proteins significantly associated with the observed trajectories.

Results

Male patients had an average height of 176.9 cm and weight of 109.1 kg, while females averaged 155 cm in height and 106.7 kg in weight. A1AT is negatively correlated with severity in non-healing MDRPIs

Proteins impacting MDRPI healing were expected to correlate significantly with severity in healing or non-healing MDRPIs. To validate the relationship between wound severity and healing biology, a linear mixed model was employed to account for repeated measures across patient samples. This approach confirmed that healing trajectory was the only significant predictor of PUSH score (β = 1.614, SE = 0.435, df = 55.7, t = 3.710, p < 0.001), with non-healing wounds demonstrating significantly higher wound severity scores than healing wounds independent of patient age, sex, diabetes status, and sample timepoint. This independently corroborates the biological premise that wound severity and healing trajectory are meaningfully linked. Further details of this analysis can be found in “Supplementary Information – Normality Assessment of Data set, A1AT, NE, and KNG-1”.

A significant negative correlation (P < 0.03, R = −0.28) was identified between A1AT abundance and PUSH scores of all MDRPI samples (Fig. 3A). When samples were separated into healing or non-healing groups, A1AT did not significantly correlate with PUSH score in the healing MDRPIs (Fig. 3B), however in non-healing MDRPIs there was similar significance (P = 0.035) but a much stronger negative correlation (R = −0.43) (Fig. 3C). Autocorrelation was accounted for using a linear mixed-effects model with an AR(1) correlation structure, the inclusion of this correction did not significantly improve model fit for A1AT (likelihood ratio test p = 0.098), suggesting that autocorrelation does not materially influence the results for A1AT. It should be noted that a subset of patients contributed samples at both healing and non-healing timepoints; however, the number of such paired samples was insufficient to permit robust within-patient trajectory analysis. Overall, this analysis supports further proteomic investigation of A1AT as a potential mediator of this relationship.

We mapped the protein fragments detected by SWATH-MS back to their corresponding reference FASTA amino acid sequences to determine which regions of each protein were present in the wound fluid and to aid interpretation of their potential biological functions. Each matched protein fragment has a confidence threshold with the highest labelled “99% <”. If only a few high-confidence peptides are detected, this could raise concerns about the accuracy of the abundance results. However, high confidence (99% <) tryptic peptides were identified throughout the majority of the A1AT protein sequence except for the most N terminal region (Fig. 4). This suggests that the mature A1AT protein is present in the MDRPI wound fluid. Furthermore, non-tryptic peptides were investigated for N or C terminal proteolytic sites using the SwissProt peptide cutter tool. This identified no non-tryptic proteolytic sites at the N or C-termini, showing that A1AT proteins were only digested from trypsin during the SWATH-MS preparation process. In summation, this analysis provides evidence confirming the mature A1AT protein was present in the MDRPI wound fluid.

Confirming A1AT activity in MDRPI wound fluid

Since the mature A1AT protein was found within the MDRPI wound fluid environment, next we needed to confirm the biological activity of A1AT. Previous studies show A1AT irreversibly binds to neutrophil elastase (NE) to permanently inhibit its activity and not digesting NE (20). This means NE abundance should not significantly correlate with A1AT, nor was that observed in this study (Fig. 5A). To further validate this, a linear mixed model accounting for repeated measures across each patient’s samples confirmed no significant association between A1AT and NE protein abundance following Bonferroni correction (β = −0.204, SE = 0.099, df = 57.7, t = − 2.049, p = 0.045, Bonferroni-adjusted threshold p < 0.017; ICC = 0.52). However, NE is known to digest target proteins such as collagen and fibronectin [21]. This implies that A1AT abundance might positively correlate with NE targets rather than NE itself (Fig. 5B).

However, NE targets multiple proteins for degradation, meaning there may be correlation between A1AT protein abundance and NE substrate abundance [B]. By confirming the above protein pathway of A1AT inhibiting NE digestive activity increasing the abundance of a NE target proteins, the A1AT’s activity in the MDRPI wound fluid environment can be confirmed. The protein with the most significant and strongest correlation with A1AT abundance was the NE degradation target kininogen-1 (KNG-1) (R = 0.76; Q < 6.8 × 10− 10) (Fig. 6). To further validate this relationship whilst accounting for the repeated measures structure of the data, a linear mixed model confirmed a highly significant positive association between A1AT and KNG-1 protein abundance (β = 0.555, SE = 0.081, df = 46.5, t = 6.873, p < 0.001), which remained significant following Bonferroni correction (adjusted threshold p < 0.017; ICC = 0.38).

To further examine whether this relationship reflected true within-patient co-regulation rather than stable between-patient differences, a within-person centred mixed model was employed to decompose the A1AT/KNG-1 association into within-patient and between-patient components. Both components were highly significant (within-patient: β = 0.486, SE = 0.101, df = 47.2, t = 4.816, p < 0.001; between-patient: β = 0.675, SE = 0.134, df = 8.8, t = 5.028, p < 0.001), demonstrating that the positive A1AT/KNG-1 association holds both within individual patients over time and across patients. The convergence of standard correlation, mixed model, and within-person centred analyses across three analytical approaches provides robust evidence that the A1AT/KNG-1 co-regulatory relationship is a genuine biological phenomenon rather than a statistical artefact of the repeated measures design.

Using the strategy of mapping protein peptide fragments to its FASTA sequence as mentioned above, the KNG-1 peptide fragments revealed 3 high confidence and 2 moderate confidence peptides, all located between residue 18 and 324, which corresponds with the KNG-1 Heavy chain domain [22] of the 644-residue full length protein (Fig. 7) [22].

To confirm the above results, we examined the relationship of A1AT, NE, and KNG-1, through a secondary approximation of MDRPI severity, the wounds tissue type. Tissue type of a PI is ranked from least severe to most severe as closed wound, epithelial, granulation, slough, and necrosis [10]. Based on our findings detailed above, we expected that A1AT and KNG-1 are more abundant in closed wounds and epithelial MDRPIs. Indeed, one-way ANOVA revealed that A1AT abundance differed significantly across tissue types (F = 1.175, p = 0.037), with post hoc testing confirming significantly higher abundance in closed and epithelial MDRPIs compared to granulation tissue. Similarly, KNG-1 abundance also differed significantly across tissue types (F = 0.203, p = 0.010), with closed and epithelial MDRPIs again showing significantly higher abundance than granulation tissue (both comparisons p < 0.05, Fig. 8A and B). Homogeneity of variance was confirmed for both proteins prior to ANOVA interpretation (Brown-Forsythe test, both p > 0.05). This analysis, based on wound proteomic data collected through MS techniques, provided further evidence of increasing A1AT mediated inhibition of NE proteolysis of KNG-1 in MDRPIs during the healing process.

Discussion

This article presents novel evidence through a SWATH-MS analysis of MDRPI wound fluid proteome, which revealed a significant protein pathway specific to chronic MDRPIs. This study is the first that the authors are aware of, utilising MS methods to analyse MDRPIs at different healing trajectories and severities.

A1AT presence can have significant impacts on MDRPI healing as it inhibits enzymatic activity of neutrophil released proteins (notably NE) in tissue, protecting the local microenvironment from uncontrolled tissue breakdown and inflammation [23]. The biological impact of low A1AT, as detected in severe non-healing MDRPIs, is observed in patients with the genetic disease A1AT deficiency [24]. Patients with A1AT deficiency have observed lung diseases (emphysema and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) and liver diseases (hepatitis, cirrhosis and liver failure) [23, 24]. Further, rare cases of A1AT deficiency leads to dermal diseases such as panniculitis, vasculitis, psoriasis, urticaria and angioedema, where the common biological deficiency is an inability to resolve inflammation and repair the skin barrier [25]. Taken together, lower abundance of A1AT could facilitate exaggerated NE activity leading to excessive tissue damage similar to that found in A1AT deficiency, thus contributing to the chronicity or worsening of MDRPIs.

A1AT presence was confirmed in MDRPI wound fluid through detection of high-confidence A1AT tryptic peptides across most of the A1AT protein sequence (Fig. 4). A section of N terminal A1AT peptide sequence approximately 47 amino acids in length was not detected (Fig. 4). This aligns with the known A1AT structure with amino acids 1–24 being a signal peptide [26] with the remaining become mature A1AT protein [27]. Moreover, detected A1AT peptides were analysed with the SwissProt peptide cutter tool and determined no other digestive enzymes other than trypsin had cleaved A1AT in the MDRPI wound fluid. Taken together, this suggests that A1AT is present in MDRPIs and its biological action would result in NE inhibition [20].

When investigating A1AT biological activity, it is known to permanently bind and inhibit NE without digesting it, therefore we did not expect correlation of NE abundances with A1AT[20], and our findings supported this hypothesis (Fig. 5A). However, to further the logic a positive correlation between A1AT and NE degradation targets was hypothesised since NE is a protease [21] (Fig. 5B). To confirm this, a correlation analyses between A1AT and other proteins within MDRPI wound fluid was conducted. KNG-1 exhibited the most significant correlation (Q < 6.8 × 10–10, R = 0.76, Fig. 6), being a precursor protein for BK which is involved in the initial healing of wounds [28].

Previous studies have shown that KNG‑1 is a substrate for NE, which cleaves KNG-1 to release an extended bradykinin‑containing peptide (E‑kinin) with vascular permeability activity [29], and this NE‑dependent pathway represents an alternative route for kinin generation in inflammatory settings [30]. Although NE clearly degrades KNG‑1 and promotes kinin release, the precise domain(s) or residue‑level cleavage sites on KNG‑1 targeted by NE have not been mapped in detail, and the resistance of NE‑generated fragments to further processing by other serine proteases such as kallikreins remains to be fully established[31]. This demonstrates that NE targets KNG‑1 for degradation and contributes to kinin system activation. Therefore, the observed positive correlation of A1AT with KNG‑1, a degradation target of NE, supports the hypothesis that A1AT is biologically active in the MDRPI wound fluid by inhibiting NE action.

To further support A1AT significance to MDRPI healing, all of the detected fragments of KNG-1 were in the N terminal half of the amino acid sequence, which constitutes the heavy chain of the KNG-1 protein (Fig. 7, grey highlight, 18 aa – 380 aa) [22]. The heavy chain of KNG-1 is known to be digested in plasma to produce BK [32]. Plasma released BK is a pro-inflammatory peptide, vasodilator, and pain mediator [33]. BK activity in skin healing mouse models suggest involvement of dermal kinin receptors during the initial epithelial inflammation phase of wound healing [34, 35]. Further, the modulation of the kinin inflammatory system is known to contribute to wound healing [34]. Taken together, our findings suggest that more severe non-healing MDRPIs have reduced A1AT abundance potentially facilitating increased NE activity and dysregulated KNG-1 to BK digestion, inhibiting wound healing.

Finally, to further verify our study’s findings, we used MDRPI tissue types as a different approximation of MDRPI severity. PI tissue type is assessed during PUSH score classification and ranked from least to most severe: closed, epithelial, granulation, slough, and necrosis. We anticipated that the abundance of A1AT and KNG-1 would be higher in tissue types indicative of healing (i.e., closed and epithelial) compared to more severe tissue types (i.e., granulation, slough, and necrosis) [36]. This analysis showed A1AT is significantly more abundant in closed and epithelial MDRPIs than granulation tissue MDRPIs (P < 0.05, Fig. 8A). Similarly, KNG-1 was also significantly more abundant in closed and epithelial MDRPIs (P < 0.05 and P < 0.005, respectively, Fig. 8B). This analysis serves to verify this study’s earlier findings using a secondary proxy of PI severity.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The cohort was relatively small, comprising 13 patients and 62 wound fluid samples. Notably, the cohort exhibited demographic imbalances, including a pronounced sex skew (10 male: 3 female) and a predominantly older patient population. Furthermore, the mucosal-specific nature of the wounds studied means that findings may not be directly transferable to other wound types or anatomical sites. The mechanistic interpretations presented are indirect, being derived from correlations in protein abundance between healing and non-healing samples rather than from functional or experimental validation. Correlation-based approaches cannot establish causality, and the biological pathways implicated require further mechanistic investigation before definitive conclusions can be drawn. As an observational study, we cannot determine whether A1AT depletion is a driver or consequence of non-healing; interventional studies would be needed to establish causality.

This study contributes to addressing the significant clinical burden of MDRPIs in the intensive care setting. By moving beyond descriptive observation to mechanistic analysis, we report the following key findings:

Pioneering Technical Application: This research represents the first application of Data-Independent Acquisition (SWATH-MS) proteomics to clinical MDRPI wound fluid, establishing a robust workflow for investigating the molecular landscape of PI chronicity.

A1AT as a Severity Marker: We identified a specific protease–antiprotease imbalance distinguishing healing from non-healing wounds. Specifically, we observed a significant inverse correlation between A1AT levels and injury severity (R = −0.43; P = 0.035), indicating that depletion of this antiprotease is a hallmark of worsening injury.

Mechanism of Chronicity (The A1AT–KNG-1 Axis): Our data suggests a mechanistic link where A1AT depletion leads to uninhibited NE activity and subsequent degradation of KNG-1. This is supported by a strong positive correlation between A1AT and KNG-1 levels (R = 0.76; Q < 6.8 × 10–10) (Fig. 9).

Therapeutic Implications: The disruption of unregulated KNG-1 proteolysis may reduce the release of Bradykinin, a peptide involved in stimulating the initial inflammatory stage of healing. Consequently, these findings highlight the A1AT–Kininogen axis as a promising therapeutic target; modulating A1AT levels to restore regulation of KNG-1 proteolysis may improve healing outcomes in critically ill patients. In this context, with further independent studies to validate these findings, exogenous A1AT supplementation represents a possible translational avenue, given its established safety profile in A1AT deficiency and clinical use.
