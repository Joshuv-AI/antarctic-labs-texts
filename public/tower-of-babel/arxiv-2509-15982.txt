Fundamental solution and Harnack inequality for subelliptic evolution operators on Carnot groups
G. Pecorella A. Rebucci

Abstract
In this paper we study regularity properties of a class of subelliptic evolution operators. We first prove the existence of the fundamental solution by means of Levi’s parametrix method, establishing also several key properties. We then employ these results, together with some mean value formulas, to prove a maximum principle and an invariant Harnack inequality for the classical solutions to the equations under study. Our analysis critically relies on the Carnot–Carathéodory geometry naturally induced by the vector fields defining these operators.

1.1
Main results

One of the main contributions of this paper is the existence of the global fundamental solution to (

1.2
Motivation and strategy of the proof

Before outlining the structure of the paper, we provide an overview of known results on the maximum principle and the Harnack inequality and we briefly discuss their theoretical interest. We furthermore describe the strategy of the proof of our main results.

A maximum principle for (
1.1
) with
C^{\infty}
smooth coefficients was first obtained by Bony in
[
9
]
with a barrier argument, and then extended by Amano in
[
1
]
for
C^{1}
coefficients. We also mention
[
8
]
by Bonfiglioli and Uguzzoni, where the authors proved a maximum principle for (
1.1
) with continuous coefficients under the additional assumption that a Picone function exists. We point out that Theorem
1.4
is stronger than
[
1
]
, and does not require the existence of a Picone function as in
[
8
]
.

On the other hand, since Bony’s work
[
9
]
several results for the Harnack inequality are known for operators with smooth coefficients. Concerning operators with Hölder continuous coefficients, we mention Bramanti, Brandolini, Lanconelli and Uguzzoni
[
12
]
: here, the authors prove a
parabolic
Harnack inequality for positive solutions to (
1.1
) with
c=0
, adapting the method introduced by Krylov and Safonov in
[
18
]
.
In this paper we also prove a parabolic Harnack inequality (see Proposition
6.1
below) by extending the result of
[
12
]
to operators with
c\neq 0
. The main contribution of this paper is the derivation of an
invariant
Harnack inequality expressed in terms of future and past cylinders, in the same spirit of the one obtained in
[
23
]
for uniformly parabolic operators. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this result is new when dealing with operator (
1.1
). This is obtained by adapting the optimal control strategy introduced in
[
10
]
, and strongly relies on the sub-Riemannian geometric properties induced by the vector fields
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m_{1}}
.

The proofs of the maximum principle and the parabolic Harnack inequality rely on a technique involving mean value formulas originally developed in the context of harmonic functions, though it is not straightforward to adapt this method to our case. We recall that mean value formulas for (
1.1
) have been recently established by Pallara and Polidoro in
[
25
]
, by employing the fundamental solution
{\Gamma}^{*}
of the adjoint operator of (
1.1
). In this work the authors managed to extend to the case of Hölder coefficients the results previously known only for smooth coefficients. The main difficulty one encounters when dealing with non-smooth coefficients is due to the loss of regularity of the fundamental solution: while Hörmander’s condition theorem ensures smoothness when the coefficients are smooth, the fundamental solution fails to be even
C^{1}
when the coefficients are merely Hölder continuous (as we will see in Definition
3.1
). To overcome this issue, in
[
25
]
the authors used some of the tools developed in the last years in the field of Geometric Measure Theory on Carnot groups (see
[
31
]
for an overview of this topic). In order to employ the mean value formula in
[
25
]
, we first construct the fundamental solution of (
1.1
) and of its adjoint by means of Levi’s parametrix method
[
21
,
20
]
(see Theorem
3.2
below). We also derive several key properties of the fundamental solution (most notably, Gaussian estimates), which are then employed to prove the mean value formula in our setting.

The main difficulty encountered in extending this “mean value formula technique” to our subelliptic evolutive setting (in particular, in the proof of the parabolic Harnack inequality) lies in the behavior of the kernel appearing in the formula. More precisely, the kernel is not bounded from above and below by some strictly positive constants (an issue that already arises in the case of the heat operator on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
). This prevents us from taking advantage of the classical elementary arguments developed for harmonic functions to establish the maximum principle and the parabolic Harnack inequality. To overcome this problem, we employ the technique introduced by Kuptsov in
[
19
]
, based on Hadamard’s descent method, to construct an improved bounded kernel. The resulting kernel is strictly positive (up to a set of zero measure), thereby allowing us to make use of the classical theory developed for harmonic functions. A parabolic Harnack inequality obtained via Hadamard’s descent method was first established by Garofalo and Lanconelli in
[
14
]
and later by Malagoli, Pallara and Polidoro in
[
23
]
for uniformly parabolic operators, and by Polidoro
[
27
]
and by one of the authors
[
29
]
for Kolmogorov operators. Here we follow the approach introduced by Polidoro in
[
27
]
, although several technical difficulties need to be addressed in order to obtain the desired bounds on the kernel. This is, in particular, due to the fact that there is no explicit expression for the fundamental solutions
\Gamma
of the class of operators studied here. In order to tackle the problem, we employ the equivalence of sublaplacians on
free Carnot groups
derived by Bonfiglioli and Uguzzoni in
[
7
]
. To make use of this equivalence on a general Carnot group
{\mathbb{G}}
(
i.e.
whenever
{\mathbb{G}}
is not free), we apply the Rothschild and Stein lifting technique
[
30
]
, similar to the construction by Bonfiglioli, Lanconelli and Uguzzoni in
[
3
]
. Thanks to this lifting procedure, we can derive the desired estimates and the parabolic Harnack inequality for the lifted operator, which in turn implies the parabolic Harnack inequality for the class of operators under study. The proof of the invariant Harnack inequality presented in Theorem
1.6
requires repeated application of the parabolic Harnack inequality to construct a Harnack chain. We finally remark that the Carnot–Carathéodory distance associated to the vector fields
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m_{1}}
plays a key role in this last step.

We observe that the stratification (

{\delta}_{r}(x)=\left(rx^{(1)},r^{2}x^{(2)},\ldots,r^{\kappa}x^{(\kappa)}\right),\qquad x^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{k}},\;k=1,\ldots,\kappa,

where

denotes the dimension of

, for every

. We will refer to the number

. By Hörmander’s rank condition we notice that

, inferring that

. In particular, the equality holds if and only if

is the Euclidean group. A group

with the properties above is often referred to as Carnot group of step

As it will be useful in Section

, we recall that Proposition 1.3.5 in
[

provides us with the following characterization of smooth

-homogeneous vector fields: let

be as in (

be a smooth vector field on

. Then

is
\delta_{r}
-homogeneous of degree

if and only if

is a polynomial function
\delta_{r}
-homogeneous of degree
\sigma_{j}-n
(unless

). In particular, by

we deduce that the coefficients

of the smooth vector fields

are polynomial functions.

We now introduce a homogeneous distance on
{\mathbb{G}}
,
i.e.
the
X
-control distance
(also known as the
Carnot–Carathéodory distance
) associated with the family of vector fields
X=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m_{1}}\}
. This distance will play a crucial role in the proof of the maximum propagation and the Harnack inequality stated in Theorem
1.4
and in Theorem

, respectively.

Definition 2.1

(Carnot-Caratheodory distance)
.

The Carnot-Carathéodory distance (CC, in short)
d_{X}
associated to the set of Hörmander vector fields
X=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m_{1}}\}
is defined as follows. Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the set of the absolutely continuous maps
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfying

\dot{\gamma}({\tau})=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\alpha_{i}({\tau})X_{i}\big(\gamma({\tau})\big),

for a.e.
{\tau}\in[0,1]
, with
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m_{1}})
real-valued function on
[0,1]
such that

\|{\alpha}\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{{\tau}\in[0,1]}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}|\alpha_{j}({\tau})|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<+\infty.

(2.5)

The CC-distance is then defined as

d_{X}(x,y):=\inf\Bigg\{\int_{0}^{1}|{\alpha}({\tau})|d{\tau}\,:\,\gamma\in\mathcal{C},\,\gamma(0)=x,\;\gamma(1)=y\Bigg\}.

In the following we will refer to
\mathcal{C}
as the set of
X
-trajectories on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and the function
{\tau}\mapsto{\alpha}({\tau})
will be referred to as
control
of the X-trajectory
\gamma
.

We now list some properties of the CC-distance that will be useful in the sequel.

•

The CC-distance can equivalently be defined in terms of
X-subunit curves
, that is, by requiring
\|{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
(see,
e.g.
, Proposition 7.12 in
[
2
]
).

•

Another equivalent definition of the CC-distance is the following

d^{(p)}_{X}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}|{\alpha}({\tau})|^{p}\,d{\tau}\right)^{1/p}&\text{if}\hskip 2.84526ptp<+\infty,\\
\displaystyle\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}}\sup_{{\tau}\in[0,1]}|{\alpha}({\tau})|,&\text{if}\hskip 2.84526ptp=+\infty.\end{cases}

(2.6)

It is clear that
d_{X}\equiv d_{X}^{(1)}
. Moreover, the following property holds

d^{(p)}_{X}=d_{X},\qquad\forall p\in[1,+\infty].

(2.7)

see for instance Proposition 7.13 in
[
2
]
.

•

The CC-distance associated to a system of Hörmander vector fields is a metric (see Theorem 6.22 in
[
2
]
).

•

There exists
k_{1}\geq 1
such that

d_{X}(x,{\xi})\leq k_{1}\big(d_{X}(x,y)+d_{X}(y,{\xi})\big),\qquad\text{for every}\;x,{\xi},y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}.

(2.8)

If
k_{1}=1
, this is the classical triangular inequality; for such reason this property is usual referred to as
pseudo-triangular inequality
.

•

The CC-distance is
{\delta}_{r}
-homogeneous of degree
1
, hence the Euclidean metric is continuos w.r.t. the metric induced by the CC-distance (see Proposition 5.15.1 in
[
6
]
).

In order to study the evolution operator (
1.1
) on the Carnot group
{\mathbb{G}}
(that is relevant to the space variable
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
), we introduce the
{\mathbb{G}}
-parabolic distance on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}

d(z,{\zeta})=d\big((x,t),({\xi},{\tau})\big):=d_{X}(x,{\xi})+|t-{\tau}|^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\forall z=(x,t),{\zeta}=({\xi},{\tau})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}.

(2.9)

We are now in a position to define the Hölder spaces naturally induced by operator
\mathscr{H}_{\mathbb{G}}
.

Definition 2.2

(Hölder continuous function)
.

Let
{\alpha}\in(0,1]
. We say that
u:{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an
{\alpha}
-Hölder continuous function with exponent
{\alpha}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}
if there exists a positive constant

such that

\left|u(z)-u({\zeta})\right|\leq c\,d(z,{\zeta})^{\alpha},\qquad\forall z,{\zeta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}.

(2.10)

This definition of Hölder space plays a central role in our analysis: in Section
3
we prove that, under the assumption that the coefficients belong to this Hölder space (namely
(H4)
), we are able to apply the parametrix method. Hence, we are able to prove the existence of the fundamental solution
{\Gamma}
to
\mathscr{H}_{\mathbb{G}}
as well as the fundamental solution

to

, along with upper and lower bounds. This, in turn, enables us to use the mean value formula developed in
[
25
]
, which we then refine in Section

and exploit in the proof of Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
1.6
. Mean value formulas hold for divergence form operators: accordingly, we introduce the differential operators

and

, that are the natural extension of the divergence and gradient operator in the non-Euclidean setting of
{\mathbb{G}}
. In order to define these operators, we agree to identify a
horizontal section

F=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}F_{i}X_{i}
with its canonical coordinates
F=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{m_{1}})
; moreover, we define as

the space of functions with continuous first order Lie derivatives. Then, for every
F\in C^{1}_{{\mathbb{G}}}\big({\mathbb{R}}^{N+1},\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}\big)
and for every real valued

we set

{\text{\rm div}}_{{\mathbb{G}}}F:=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}X_{i}F_{i},\qquad\nabla_{{\mathbb{G}}}f:=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\big(X_{i}f\big)X_{i}.

(2.11)

Employing these operators, we can write (

) in

-divergence form as follows

\mathscr{H}_{\mathbb{G}}u={\text{\rm div}}_{\mathbb{G}}\big(A\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\big)+\langle b,\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\rangle+c\,u-\partial_{t}u=f.

(2.12)

We conclude this section by introducing the lifting technique that will be employed in Section

to establish a parabolic-type Harnack inequality. We first recall that a Carnot group
{\mathbb{G}}
is a

if the Lie algebra of

is isomorphic to a free nilpotent Lie algebra with

generators of step

and

. We then let

be the system of vector fields generating the Carnot group

of homogeneous dimension

[

]

can be lifted to a free Carnot group

is stratified as in (

(2.14)

for some

, where

are as in (

\left(\tilde{x}^{(1)},\tilde{x}^{(2)},\ldots,\tilde{x}^{(\kappa)},x^{(1)},x^{(2)},\ldots,x^{(\kappa)}\right)\qquad\tilde{x}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{m}_{k}},x^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{k}},\;k=1,\ldots,\kappa,

where

If

is a free Carnot group, the following remarkable property holds: there exists a diffeomorphism

such that

{\Gamma}_{A}\big((x,t),(\xi,\tau)\big)=J_{A}\,{\Gamma}_{0}\Big(T_{A}\big({\xi}^{-1}\circ x\big),t-{\tau}\Big),

where

is the fundamental solution of the canonical heat operator

is the fundamental solution of of the constant coefficients operator

(2.17)

and

allows us to take advantage of the previous correspondence for the lifted fundamental solution

Definition 3.1

(Fundamental solution)

\displaystyle k_{1}r+k_{1}^{2}\vartheta+k^{2}_{1}\big(t^{+}-t^{-}\big)\sup_{{\tau}\in[0,t^{+}-t^{-}]}|\tilde{\alpha}({\tau})|,

where in the last inequality we simply used the fact that
s\in\big[0,t^{+}-t^{-}\big]
. We then compute

\displaystyle d_{X}(y,0)\overset{\eqref{riparametrix}}{\leq}

\displaystyle k_{1}r+k_{1}^{2}\vartheta+k^{2}_{1}\sup_{{\tau}\in[0,1]}|\underline{{\alpha}}({\tau})|

(7.13)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{gamma-opt}}{=}

\displaystyle k_{1}r+k_{1}^{2}\vartheta+k^{2}_{1}d_{X}^{(\infty)}(x^{+},x^{-})

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:d1-dp}}{=}

\displaystyle k_{1}r+k_{1}^{2}\vartheta+k^{2}_{1}d_{X}(x^{+},x^{-})

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{rnot}}{\leq}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+3k_{1}^{3}\vartheta\overset{\eqref{thetanot}}{<}1,

and therefore the trajectory
\tilde{\gamma}
we constructed does not exit
B_{1}(0)
. We conclude by showing the last inclusion in (
7.3
): in virtue of (
6.3
) and (
7.11
),
z_{j}\in D_{r}(z_{j-1})
if
d_{X}(x_{j},x_{j-1})\leq\vartheta_{1}r
. Arguing as in (
7.9
) we get

\displaystyle d_{X}(x_{j},x_{j-1})

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885pt{(j-1)}{\varepsilon}_{1}r^{2}$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle j{\varepsilon}_{1}r^{2}$}}$}}\nolimits|\tilde{\alpha}({\tau})|d{\tau}

(7.14)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:def-r}}{\leq}\frac{t^{+}-t^{-}}{m}\sup_{{\tau}\in[0,t^{+}-t^{-}]}|\tilde{\alpha}({\tau})|

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{riparametrix}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sup_{{\tau}\in[0,1]}|\underline{{\alpha}}({\tau})|

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{gamma-opt}}{=}\frac{1}{m}d^{(\infty)}_{X}(x^{+},x^{-})

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:d1-dp}}{=}\frac{1}{m}d_{X}(x^{+},x^{-}).

Hence,
z_{j}\in D_{r}(z_{j-1})
if we choose
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\frac{1}{m}d_{X}(x^{+},x^{-})\leq\vartheta_{1}r\overset{\eqref{eq:def-r}}{=}\frac{\vartheta_{1}\sqrt{t^{+}-t^{-}}}{\sqrt{m}\sqrt{{\varepsilon}_{1}}}\,\iff\,m\geq\frac{d_{X}(x^{+},x^{-})^{2}{\varepsilon}_{1}}{\vartheta_{1}^{2}(t^{+}-t^{-})}.

(7.15)

The proof of the theorem follows once we obtain a bound for
m
that is uniform w.r.t.
z^{+}\in Q^{+}
and
z^{-}\in Q^{-}
. In order for the previous construction to hold, we need to satisfy (
7.15
) and
r\leq r_{0}
. Since
t^{+}-t^{-}\geq\eta-\nu
, we need to require
m\geq\frac{4k^{2}_{1}{\varepsilon}_{1}}{(\eta-\nu)}
so that (
7.15
) holds. Moreover, by (
7.10
) condition
r\leq r_{0}
holds if
m\geq\frac{t^{+}-t^{-}}{{\varepsilon}_{1}r^{2}_{0}}
, and therefore it is sufficient to require that
m\geq\frac{\mu}{{\varepsilon}_{1}r_{0}^{2}}
since
t^{+}-t^{-}\leq\mu
. To summarize the above, we have proved that inequality (
1.19
) holds with
C_{H}=C_{P}^{\bar{m}}
, provided that we choose
\bar{m}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\bar{m}\geq\max\Big\{\frac{4^{2}k_{1}{\varepsilon}_{1}}{(\eta-\nu)},\frac{\mu}{{\varepsilon}_{1}r_{0}^{2}}\Big\}
.

Appendix A
Comparison principle

In this appendix we provide the proof of the following comparison principle.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
{\Omega}_{T}={\Omega}\times(T_{1},T)
be an open bounded subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}
, and let
w\in C^{2}_{x,t}({\Omega}_{T})
be a solution to

\begin{cases}\mathscr{H}_{\mathbb{G}}w\geq 0,\qquad\;\;&\mathrm{in}\;{\Omega}_{T},\\
\limsup w\leq 0,\qquad&\mathrm{in}\;\partial{\Omega}\times(T_{1},T)\cup{\Omega}\times\{T_{1}\},\end{cases}

(1.1)

then
w\leq 0
in
{\Omega}_{T}
. The same result holds in
{\Omega}={\mathbb{R}}^{N}
if
\limsup w\leq 0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times\{T_{1}\}
and at infinity.

Proof.

The scheme of the proof is classical, once we follow the
C^{2}_{x,t}
regularity of
w
. For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
we set
{\Omega}_{T_{k}}:={\Omega}_{T}\cap\left\{t\leq\big(T-\frac{1}{k}\big)\right\}
, and we prove the result by showing that
w\leq 0
in
{\Omega}_{T_{k}}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The result on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
straightforwardly follows.

Let us pick some
M>M_{1}
(where
M_{1}
is given by
(H4)
), and set
v(x,t):=w(x,t)\exp({-Mt})
. By a direct computation we see that
v
solves
\mathscr{H}_{\mathbb{G}}v-Mv\geq 0
in
{\Omega}_{T_{k}}
, and that
\limsup v\leq 0
in
\partial{\Omega}\times(T_{1},T)\cup{\Omega}\times\{T_{1}\}
. Since the function
v(\cdot,t)
has continuous first and second order Lie derivatives w.r.t. the generating vector fields
X_{i}
, then it has continuous Lie derivative w.r.t. every
X\in{\text{\rm span}}\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m_{1}}
(see Lemma 4.2 in
[
6
]
). Therefore, if
v\in C^{2}_{x,t}({\Omega}_{T})
has a positive maximum point in
(x_{0},t_{0})\in{\Omega}\times\left\{t=T_{k}\right\}
, we claim that

X_{i}v(x_{0},t_{0})=0,\qquad\partial_{t}v(x_{0},t_{0})\leq 0,\qquad\sum_{i,j=1}^{m_{1}}a_{ij}(x_{0},t_{0})X_{i}X_{j}v(x_{0},t_{0})\leq 0,

(1.2)

whereas when
(x_{0},t_{0})\in{\Omega}_{T_{k}}
the second inequality becomes an equality. From this claim it follows
\big(M-c(x_{0},t_{0})\big)v(x_{0},t_{0})\leq 0
, hence
v\leq 0
in
{\Omega}_{T_{k}}
, whereby the thesis follows.

We prove the claim by proving the last inequality in (
1.2
), since the others are straightforward: by contradiction, assume there exists
d\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}
such that
\sum_{i,j=1}^{m_{1}}d_{i}d_{j}X_{i}X_{j}v(x_{0},t_{0})>0
. Then, let
\exp(sZ)(0)
be the integral curve of
Z=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}d_{i}X_{i}
starting from the origin, and for every
h\in\mathbb{R}
set
g(h):=v\big(x_{0}\circ\exp(hZ)(0),t_{0}\big)
. Since
v(\cdot,t_{0})
has continuous second order Lie derivatives, it follows
g\in C^{2}(-\sigma,\sigma)
for a sufficiently small
\sigma>0
. Therefore, recalling that
(x_{0},t_{0})
is a maximum point, by a Taylor expansion we get

\displaystyle v(x_{0},t_{0})\geq g(h)=

\displaystyle g(0)+\frac{h^{2}}{2}g^{\prime\prime}(0)+o_{h\rightarrow 0}\big(h^{2}\big)

(1.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v(x_{0},t_{0})+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m_{1}}d_{i}d_{j}X_{i}X_{j}v(x_{0},t_{0})+o_{h\rightarrow 0}\big(h^{2}\big)>v(x_{0},t_{0}),

hence the contradiction.
∎
