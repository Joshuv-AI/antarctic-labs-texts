The association between edentulism and cardiometabolic multimorbidity in US middle-aged and older adults
Authors: Xiaoming Zhang, Rui Zeng, Fayi Xie, Jiang Wang, Dongmei Ye, Aizhang Zhu, Lihuan Chen, Wan Zhu, Ke Zhu, Tenghui Fan, Qingli Dou, Wenwu Zhang
Published: 2024

Introduction

Periodontitis is an inflammatory condition characterized by the progressive degradation of the supporting structures of the teeth. A significant consequence of periodontitis is complete tooth loss (edentulism), which disproportionately affects older adults. Global data indicate that over 353 million individuals are affected by edentulism, with an age-standardized prevalence of 4.1% worldwide [1]. Edentulism has been reported to be associated with adverse outcomes. Consequently, the prevention and treatment of edentulism among the population are of paramount importance. Oral health has been linked to numerous chronic diseases, including cardiovascular and cerebrovascular conditions, as well as diabetes. Recent research has identified poor oral health (reduced masticatory function) as an independent risk factor for frailty, cognitive impairment, and mortality [2]. However, no study utilizing a national sample has examined the relationship between edentulism and cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM). Given the high prevalence of CMM among middle-aged and older people and its burden on the world [3], this study aims to explore whether edentulism serves as a risk factor for CMM based on a national sample.

Methods

Study population

Data were extracted from the HRS database, a publicly available resource in the USA. Baseline data were collected in 2012, followed by follow-up data in 2016. At the 2012 baseline, 20,554 participants completed the survey. After excluding cases with missing data related to teeth, CMM, and individuals under 50 years of age, a total of 19,836 individuals were included in our cross-sectional analysis, while 13,748 were analyzed longitudinally.

Edentulism assessment

People who answered yes to the question “Have you lost all of your upper and lower permanent natural teeth?” were defined as edentulous.

Condition of cardiometabolic multimorbidity

CMM was confirmed by the presence of two or more chronic diseases, including stroke, heart disease, and diabetes.

Covariates

Covariates in our study were informed by previous research and included age (continuous), gender, education, ethnicity, urban residence, marital status, smoking, drinking status, depression, and cognitive impairment.

Statistical analyses

Multivariate binary logistic regression was employed to investigate the association between edentulism and cardiometabolic multimorbidity, adjusting for all covariates listed above. The results of the association between edentulism and cardiometabolic multimorbidity are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). All analyses were conducted using R software (version 3.4), and a p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Prevalence and incidence of edentulism

The number of individuals with edentulism was 2,429 at the 2012 baseline, resulting in a prevalence of 12.24% among this population, while the incidence of edentulism over the follow-up period was 5.7%.

Association between edentulism and CMM in cross-sectional and longitudinal studies

The results of the multivariate binary logistic regression indicated that individuals with edentulism had a higher likelihood of CMM, with an OR of 1.37 (95% CI: 1.21–1.54) in the cross-sectional analysis after controlling for all potential covariates. Discussion

Our study suggests that edentulism is associated with CMM, consistent with prior findings that poor oral health contributes to chronic diseases. Moreover, recent studies have reported that edentulism is associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality [4]. Based on our results, medical staff should prioritize early screening of oral health to prevent individuals from developing edentulism. The mechanisms underlying the association between edentulism and CMM are complex. Firstly, individuals with edentulism often struggle to maintain a balanced diet due to difficulties in chewing, resulting in nutritional deficiencies, particularly in essential vitamins and minerals crucial for preventing chronic diseases [5]. Secondly, people with edentulism typically experience poorer oral health, which may lead to an imbalance in oral bacteria, resulting in systemic inflammation. This increased systemic inflammation elevates the risk of stroke, heart disease, and diabetes. Lastly, older adults suffering from complete tooth loss often experience low self-esteem and diminished quality of life, leading to social withdrawal and depression. As a result, they become a vulnerable group for developing CMM.

In summary, our study indicates that individuals with edentulism are at a higher risk for CMM; therefore, medical staff and public health workers should implement various interventions to assist community-dwelling individuals in maintaining oral health.
