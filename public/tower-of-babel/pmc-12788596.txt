Efficacy of Enhanced Recovery after Surgery (ERAS) Protocols in Pediatric Colorectal Surgery: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Kavita Tirkey, Ankita, Nishant G. Xess, Shyam Sundar Sahu, Abhishek Kumar Singh, Abhishek Ranjan, Priya Shalini Lakra, Hirendra Birua
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols have been widely adopted in adult surgical populations to optimize perioperative outcomes. However, their application and effectiveness in pediatric colorectal surgery remain underexplored.Materials and Methods:This single-blind, randomized controlled trial included 120 pediatric patients undergoing elective colorectal surgery at two tertiary hospitals in India. Patients were randomized into ERAS (n = 60) and conventional care (n = 60) groups. Outcomes included time to first feeding, time to ambulation, length of hospital stay, pain scores, and complication rates.Results:The ERAS group showed significantly earlier initiation of feeding (1.3 vs. 2.8 days, P < 0.001), faster ambulation (1.8 vs. 3.2 days, P < 0.001), shorter hospital stay (4.9 vs. 7.6 days, P < 0.001), and lower postoperative pain scores. Complication rates were also lower in the ERAS group (10% vs. 23.3%, P = 0.045).Conclusion:ERAS protocols significantly improve perioperative outcomes in pediatric colorectal surgery. Their integration into standard care can enhance recovery and reduce morbidity.

INTRODUCTION

The perioperative management of pediatric patients undergoing colorectal surgery remains a complex challenge due to the unique physiological and psychological needs of children. Historically, conventional postoperative care involved prolonged fasting, delayed ambulation, liberal use of opioids, and extensive hospital stays. These practices often lead to delayed recovery, increased risk of complications such as ileus and infections, and heightened healthcare costs.[123]

Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols were initially developed for adult colorectal surgery and are designed to mitigate the surgical stress response and expedite recovery through a multimodal, evidence-based approach. Key elements of ERAS include preoperative counseling, avoidance of mechanical bowel preparation when possible, early postoperative nutrition, minimal opioid use, and early mobilization. These interventions aim to reduce morbidity, shorten hospital stay, and improve patient satisfaction.[4]

Recent pediatric studies have begun exploring ERAS applications, primarily in urology, orthopedic, and general surgical procedures, with encouraging results. Nonetheless, the data regarding pediatric colorectal surgeries—especially for conditions like Hirschsprung’s disease, anorectal malformations, and colonic resections—is limited. Given the complexity and heterogeneity of these cases, the evaluation of ERAS in this setting is both timely and essential.[56]

The current study aims to address this knowledge gap by conducting a prospective, randomized controlled trial to evaluate the efficacy of ERAS protocols in pediatric colorectal surgery. Our primary endpoints include time to oral feeding, ambulation, length of hospital stay, and incidence of postoperative complications. Secondary endpoints include pain scores and parental satisfaction. We hypothesize that the implementation of ERAS protocols will lead to improved functional recovery and reduced morbidity compared to conventional care.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective, randomized controlled trial was conducted at two tertiary pediatric surgical centers in India. A total of 120 pediatric patients (aged 6 months to 14 years) undergoing elective colorectal surgery for conditions such as Hirschsprung’s disease, anorectal malformations, and colonic resections were enrolled and randomized into two equal groups: ERAS (n = 60) and conventional care (n = 60). Inclusion criteria included American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I–III and informed consent from parents/guardians. The ERAS group received a multimodal protocol including preoperative counseling, avoidance of routine bowel prep, carbohydrate loading 2 h before surgery, early postoperative feeding (within 24 h), early mobilization (within 48 h), multimodal analgesia (acetaminophen, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs), and opioid-sparing strategies. The control group received standard perioperative care with delayed feeding, ambulation, and conventional pain management. Postoperative outcomes, including time to first oral feeding, time to ambulation, pain scores (Wong-Baker Faces Scale), length of stay, and complications (Clavien-Dindo classification), were recorded. Data were analyzed using SPSS v26.0, (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Independent t-tests and chi-square tests were used, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

This randomized controlled trial demonstrates that ERAS protocols significantly improve postoperative recovery in pediatric patients undergoing elective colorectal surgery. Children in the ERAS group achieved earlier oral feeding, faster ambulation, experienced less pain, and had a shorter hospital stay compared to those receiving conventional care. Furthermore, a significant reduction in overall postoperative complications was observed, underscoring the efficacy and safety of ERAS in this population.

Our findings are consistent with adult studies where ERAS has been associated with improved recovery and reduced hospital resource utilization. Notably, studies by Gustafsson et al. and Ljungqvist et al. have shown that ERAS protocols optimize the physiological response to surgery and support a faster return to baseline function. The pediatric results observed here suggest similar mechanisms are operative in children, despite developmental differences in metabolism and stress responses.[78]

The earlier initiation of feeding (mean 1.3 vs. 2.8 days) was particularly significant, as prolonged postoperative fasting is both unnecessary and detrimental. This aligns with the findings of Short et al., who demonstrated the safety and benefit of early enteral nutrition in pediatric surgery. In addition, early ambulation promoted by ERAS may have contributed to the lower incidence of ileus and fewer pulmonary complications.[9]

Pain management in the ERAS group was optimized using a multimodal approach, minimizing opioid usage. Opioid sparing reduces risks of sedation, ileus, and respiratory depression—concerns especially relevant in children. Our reduced pain scores are consistent with evidence from Wainwright et al., advocating for ERAS in pediatric orthopedic surgery.[101112]

Although individual complications like surgical site infection and ileus did not differ significantly between groups, the aggregate complication rate (10% vs. 23.3%) reached statistical significance. This outcome suggests that the cumulative benefit of multiple ERAS interventions contributes meaningfully to reduced morbidity.[1314]

CONCLUSION

ERAS protocols significantly enhance recovery following pediatric colorectal surgery, with earlier feeding, mobilization, reduced pain, and shorter hospital stays. The protocol also results in fewer postoperative complications compared to conventional care. ERAS should be integrated into pediatric surgical practice to optimize outcomes, reduce hospital burden, and improve patient and family satisfaction. Further large-scale studies with longer follow-up are recommended to evaluate the sustained benefits of ERAS in the pediatric population.
