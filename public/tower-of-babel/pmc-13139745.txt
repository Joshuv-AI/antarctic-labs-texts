ABSTRACT

Introduction

The evaluation of a bone's cross‐sectional geometry (CSG), or biomechanically informative measurements from a bone's cross‐section (e.g., bending resistances and breaking strengths), has been fundamental in disentangling the functional morphology and evolution of human bones (Daegling 2022; Daegling et al. 2016; Daegling and Hylander 1998; Ruff 2018). In CSG analyses, common cross‐sectional properties are compared between and within sample groups. These properties can include a cross‐section's dimensions (e.g., perimeter, total area, cortical area, diameters), biomechanical resistances (e.g., second moments of area along the x‐axis or Ix, y‐axis or Iy, and polar second moment of area/overall resistance J) and breaking strengths (e.g., polar section moduli around the x‐axis or Zx, y‐axis or Zy, and polar section modulus/overall strength Zp), and cortical thicknesses (Daegling 2022; Daegling and Hylander 1998; Decaup et al. 2024; Genochio et al. 2019; Ruff 2018; Stock and Shaw 2007). Likewise, geometric morphometric (GM) analyses of a bone's cross‐section, or the quantification of a bone's cross‐sectional morphology through landmark coordinates, has also revealed similar insights on human functional morphology and structural adaptation (Decaup et al. 2024; Genochio et al. 2019; Mitteroecker and Gunz 2009; Nicholson and Harvati 2006; Theye 2022).

CSG and GMs methodologies can study morphofunctional (morphology and function) relationships by leveraging the theoretical concept of bone functional adaptation (or rather functional plasticity) where bone experiences a feedback model of depositional and resorptive responses to habitual physiological and/or environmental stress. Under this model, forces generate stress on the bone and produce deformations (strains), whether reversible (elastic strain) or plastic (permanent), that can promote localized bone deposition responses (Ruff et al. 2006). When the bone experiences infrequent habitual activity and thus less strains, the bone can produce localized bone resorptive responses. The result is a complex feedback between bone deposition and resorption (Ruff et al. 2006). While a bone's life history is clearly multidimensional (Grine and Daegling 2017; Ruff et al. 2006; van Eijden 2000), the incorporation of CSG and GM research methodologies in studying bone cross‐sections serves to paint broad strokes in human functional biology (Daegling 2022; Decaup et al. 2024; Genochio et al. 2019; Sella‐Tunis et al. 2018; Theye 2022) and present a different alternative to approaches like finite element analysis (FEA) (Gröning et al. 2011; Marcé‐Nogué et al. 2020; Parr et al. 2012; Smith et al. 2021), holistic morphometric analysis (HMA; Bachmann et al. 2022), and more historically traditional bite force estimations (BFE; O'Connor et al. 2005). This is because CSG and GM methods applied to bone cross‐sections further reduce the complexity of continuous shape and biomechanical information to regionally informative snippets (i.e., as cross‐sections) and requires less rigorous computation, sensitivity analysis, and parameter complexity, and is advantageous because it can readily compare larger samples, capturing more of human biological variation.

Despite these clear benefits, CSG and GM methods have unique data analytical challenges. Primarily, this approach divides continuous regions of bone to finite cross‐sections and requires multiple pairwise comparisons to extract holistic and regional relationships between and within bones. While statistical procedures to analyze correlation and covariation are not particularly computationally demanding, they do require extensive manually scripted and nested statistical analyses to thoroughly evaluate the relationships between cross‐sectional shape and its biomechanically relevant geometry. These analytical demands require researchers to develop custom made and extensive coding scripts that need to individually optimize and automate inferential statistical comparisons. As a result, these workflows become unmanageable and are prone to error, and are at greater risk to being irreproducible. When conducting multiple inferential statistical tests on pairwise comparisons between cross‐sectional geometric and/or shape data, parallelization and rigorous cross‐validation techniques are required to avoid statistical errors from multiple testing. Finally, shape and cross‐sectional datasets are complex: many biomechanical and shape variables can be both multicollinear (i.e., they partly explain their own variance) and multidimensional (shape data versus univariate biomechanical data), resulting in smaller scale biomechanical studies which could potentially be expanded if tools existed to simultaneously visualize and test statistical hypotheses while maintaining statistical rigor.

Fortunately, there have been several software packages that are dedicated to either GM or CSG approaches. Written for the R programming language, for example, are the R packages geomorph (Adams and Otárola‐Castillo 2013; Baken et al. 2021) which conducts Procrustes Superimposition with some linear hypothesis testing and Morpho (Schlager et al. 2019) which provides advanced algorithms for surface mesh processing and missing data estimation. Additionally, there are some R packages which quantify biomechanically relevant variables and shape from bone cross‐sections such as the R package morphomap (Profico et al. 2021). This package provides a unique framework to automate the collection of cross‐sectional geometric properties (e.g., bending resistances) and shape with long bones, with some visualization and limited statistical inferential functions (i.e., morphomapPCA and morphomapPlotShape). While each of these R‐based packages provides something unique to CSG and/or GM workflows, there is still a need for a dedicated R software package that can effectively automate and fine‐tune multivariate statistical inferential analyses equipped with informative visualizations and tools to evaluate multidimensional, multivariate data comparisons involved in analyses of bone cross‐sections. Thus, a package to statistically evaluate cross‐sectional geometric properties and shape variables after data collection is needed.

We introduce a novel R package CSGM (cross‐sectional geometric morphometrics) whose functions can statistically analyze and visualize the relationships between a cross‐section's geometric properties (i.e., bending resistances, orientation, and breaking strengths) and its overall shape (i.e., through Procrustes superimposed landmark coordinates). This package was written exclusively using the R programming language (R. C. Team 2026) using the integrated development environment RStudio 2026 (R. S. Team 2026). The CSGM package is designed to extend previously published packages related to CSG and/or GM contexts, such as morphomap 1.5 and geomorph 4.0.1, by providing a powerful data analysis toolkit to perform holistic and subsetted statistical analyses after landmarking and cross‐sectional geometric properties are collected. Uniquely, the CSGM package allows users to apply multiple independent statistical hypothesis tests through a single parallelized framework. The functions within the CSGM package include dedicated functions to visualize shape (co)variation, visually highlight important variable relationships, and conduct robust quantitative inferential and multivariate statistics. While this package offers statistically robust functions, this package is intended to be scalable for both early and advanced researchers in CSG and GM methodologies and in biological anthropology. Thus, our CSGM package offers an optimized and automated toolkit applicable to broad data contexts in studies of biology and evolution which can parallel process statistical tests using multiple and subsetted data comparisons.

To demonstrate the utility of this package in evaluating research questions related to CSG and GM analyses, this paper presents a case study on bone cross‐sections of the mandibular corpus to illustrate the utility of the CSGM package functions in revealing morphofunctional relationships. Specifically, we test whether the shape and biomechanical properties of the postcanine mandibular corpus are asymmetrically integrated with the shape expression of the mandibular symphysis. We hypothesize that the shape of the mandibular symphysis is asymmetrically integrated with the postcanine mandibular corpus in both shape and its biomechanical properties (e.g., bending resistances, breaking strengths). This expectation has also been supported by previous research on unilateral side preferences (Faurie and Raymond 2004; Frayer et al. 2012; Heikkinen et al. 2022; Hylander 1985; Smith et al. 2021), biomechanical research on unilateral chewing of primate mandibles (Hylander 1985; Panagiotopoulou et al. 2020; van Eijden 2000), and observed mandibular shape asymmetry in monozygotic twins (Heikkinen et al. 2022). As such, this case study serves to demonstrate how the CSGM package can be used to highlight these complex morphofunctional relationships.

Materials and Methods

CSGM Is a Statistically Enhanced Toolkit for Robust Statistics and Visualizations

The CSGM package is currently available for download on Github (https://github.com/bkeelin1/CSGM) exclusively for the R programming language with planned submission to the Comprehensive R Archive Network (CRAN) repository. It was written using R 4.5.2 (R. C. Team 2026) in RStudio 2026.01.1+403 (R. S. Team 2026). The package consists of 18 primary functions to facilitate a robust exploratory data analysis to hypothesis testing pipeline (Table 1). Additional details on each function's use and examples are provided in the help documentation within the package and in a vignette included with the package. CSGM introduces a novel framework designed to overcome the analytical bottlenecks inherent in multidimensional morphofunctional research. Where standard CSG and GM workflows typically require researchers to construct extensive, manual scripting loops to test multiple hypotheses across different data subsets, CSGM resolves this through a nested hypothesis testing approach that automates and parallelizes independent pairwise dataset comparisons which can be applied to nearly any data structure. This allows researchers to simultaneously apply independent data transformations (e.g., standard PCA, distance matrices, geometric means) and execute multidimensional statistical tests across various data subsets in a single automated pipeline, ensuring absolute methodological consistency across complex study designs.

CSGM primary functions and descriptions.

Beyond workflow automation, CSGM implements functions (cor.bio, pls.bio, reg.bio, GMA) that extend the fundamental capabilities of standard machine learning and morphometric libraries. While the CSGM package leverages established computational algorithms to ensure baseline reliability (e.g., caret for training classification and prediction models, geomorph for Generalized Procrustes Analysis), it provides essential mathematical extensions previously unavailable for complex multivariate data. For instance, standard machine learning packages do not natively support the multivariate response variables (such as 3D landmark arrays) central to GMs; CSGM's predictive functions overcome this limitation by internally flattening, predicting, and reconstructing multidimensional arrays. Furthermore, CSGM introduces novel implementations of statistical model evaluation, such as integrating Randomized Residual Permutation Procedures (RRPP; Collyer and Adams 2018) directly into machine learning cross‐validation loops. It also features custom algorithms to evaluate multivariate model fit (e.g., determination coefficient R 2) and performance (e.g., generalized cross‐validation statistic), and utilizes multiple approaches that consider data structure (e.g., Principal Components, multivariate data, highly multicollinearity).

Exploratory data analysis is enhanced by the CSGM package functions (e.g., lolligen, SCG) by generating interactive, multidimensional visualizations that can operate independently of standard shape analysis pipelines. Rather than relying on static 2D outputs or clunky 3D point plots, CSGM utilizes a newer visualization engine, plotly, to present interactive graphical frameworks to produce fully rotatable 3D Principal Component morphospaces and variable loading plots, complete with automated group convex hulls and vector displacements that map extreme shape variances. These visualization suites, alongside automated hierarchical clustering tools (i.e., agglomerative cluster analysis, k‐medoids), are designed as standalone functions capable of analyzing any multivariate numeric dataset, providing user‐friendly and informative visualizations needed to evaluate data relationships.

Testing Data Built in CSGM

This package includes two datasets (Corpus_Land, Corpus_Prop) and two numeric landmark indices (anchor_indices, semiland_incides). The Corpus_Land dataset consists of an p × k × n (landmarks, dimensions, individuals) 3D landmark array of 50 recent modern humans and 590 semilandmarks which map the internal and external cortical bone surface of five cross–sections taken along the mandibular corpus. The Corpus_Prop dataset consists of an n × p data frame (i.e., data table) consisting of 250 observations (i.e., 5 cross‐sections for each of the 50 individuals included) and 92 variables which directly correspond to the individuals within the Corpus_Land dataset. Of these 92 variables within the Corpus_Prop dataset, 4 consist of grouping variables, 18 consist of dimensional measures (e.g., cortical area and thickness, feret diameters/angle, perimeter), 11 related to biomechanical resistance and strength, and 59 cortical thickness measures contouring each cross‐section. These datasets are included to provide new users with complex datasets and demonstrate the utility of our statistical inference functions. Information regarding the datasets and functions are referred to in the vignettes included with the package. The datasets included in this package consists of measurements and landmarks from 50 recent modern human mandibles obtained as micro‐CT scans (27–100 μm) from the University of Pretoria (South Africa; n = 25), Sefako Makgatho University (South Africa; n = 15), and the University of Kent (United Kingdom; n = 10). Procedures involved in data generation and collection are thoroughly detailed in Text S1.

Case Study Hypothesis Testing Model

Our research question (see Introduction), or to test for an asymmetric influence of posterior corporeal geometric properties and shape on symphyseal shape, can be translated as a having a single hypothesis model (Sympheseal Corpus Shape ~ Posterior Corpus Properties and Shape, where ~ stands for “is predicted by”). This hypothesis model can be answered by four hypothesis tests. These hypothesis tests evaluate the influence of the (1) left postcanine corporeal cross‐sectional properties on symphyseal shape, (2) left postcanine corporeal shape on symphyseal shape, (3) the right postcanine corporeal shape cross‐sectional properties on symphyseal shape, and (4) right postcanine corporeal shape on symphyseal shape. For hypothesis tests one and three, four subsets will be made in the data to specify the following cross‐section properties of the posterior corpus: (1) its dimensions (i.e., area, perimeter, orientation), (2) biomechanical properties (i.e., polar moments of area, section modulus), (3) lingual cortical thicknesses, and (4) buccal cortical thicknesses. For tests two and four, this study will transform the Procrustes residuals into Procrustes distances and subject them to PCA whereas cross‐sectional properties will not undergo any data transformation. Given the complexities of comparing multidimensional shape variables, we hypothesize that shape analyzes will perform well in measures of correlation and covariation but will not be strongly linearly related in terms of the cross‐sectional properties as these variables are likely not linearly related to multidimensional shapes. As a result, we will demonstrate the need for statistically rigorous correlation and covariation functions in addition to multivariate regression tuning that is primarily offered by the CSGM package.

The Primary Functions of CSGM

A summary of the key functions within the CSGM package are presented in Figure 1. The workflow for the CSGM package can follow three critical phases involved in typical data analyses: (1) data importing and transformation, (2) exploratory data analysis, and (3) hypothesis testing. While the CSGM package is not designed to provide data acquisition methods, it does have some functions to specifically import landmark files from various formats (import_land), calculate cortical thicknesses (BoneThick), and transform datasets in a variety of ways (DT). As such, the CSGM package is primarily designed for rigorous descriptive and inferential statistical analyses. Landmark and cross‐section geometric data collection needs to first be prepared elsewhere through external R packages (i.e., morphomap 1.5) or from 3D and/or Computed Tomographic (CT) imaging software (e.g., Dragonfly, Avizo, 3D Slicer). Once data collection is performed, landmark data files can be imported into R using our import_land function or passed as either a 2D/3D array, matrix, or data frame. Cross‐sectional geometric data, such as data on biomechanical properties and cross‐section dimensions, can be passed as a matrix or data table. Our functions have been carefully optimized to handle a wide variety of data formats and mostly accept 2D/3D arrays, multidimensional matrices, and data tables.

Data Import and Transformation Functions

Import_land

This function looks inside of a user specified file directory and a string containing names to folders within that file directory and searches for a specific landmark file format. This function accepts most of the most commonly used landmark file formats including: Avizo (ASCII) (V. S. Group 2009), Dragonfly (.csv) (Dragonfly 2026), Mimics Medical (.csv) (Materialise 2017), Morphologika (.txt) (O'Higgins and Jones 2007), MorphoJ (.tps) (Klingenberg 2011), Viewbox (.nts) (dHAL Software 2019), 3D Slicer (.fcsv) (Kikinis et al. 2013), and Excel (.csv). This function extends the functionality provided in the geomorph package by allowing users to specify additional folder names within a directory where the function will read each folder, find the lists of landmark files corresponding to the file format type, and sequentially load in the data, and aggregating all observations into a single p × k × n (landmarks, dimensions, observations) array.

BoneThick

An important aspect to the CSG workflows is calculating regional cortical bone thicknesses of the bone cross‐section. Cross‐sectional thicknesses can help identify relevant regions in habitual behaviors across hominin taxonomic groups (Decaup et al. 2023; Genochio et al. 2019). The BoneThick function takes three arguments: External (the external cortical bone landmarks), Internal (the internal cortical bone landmarks), and Points (the total number of landmarks per individual). BoneThick sequentially reads through each landmark (assuming the landmark pairs are already in sequential order) and calculates the Euclidean distances as the square root sum of squared differences between the external and internal landmarks. The output returns an n × p data table of the cortical thicknesses of each landmark pair for each individual. The cortical thicknesses from the Corpus_Prop dataset used the landmarks from Corpus_Land to generate these values.

DT

DT or Data Transformation is an essential function that accepts any 2D/3D array, matrix, or data table and can convert this data to a variety of new data types such as distance matrices and (between‐group) PC scores. The function was designed to accept a “response” dataset and/or a “predictor” dataset, allowing up to two independent data transformations at once using the Res_transform or Pred_transform arguments respectively. The data itself can be in the form of data tables, arrays, or matrices. The user can also subset dataset transformations using the arguments Res_point_set and Pred_point_set and provide a numeric vector of the numeric representations (i.e., column number, landmark number) of the variables that should be transformed. Additionally, one to two combinations of data transformations can be performed from the variety of conversion methods available. Transformation methods include “procdist” (procrustes distances or a multitude of other distance formulas), “pca” (PCA via the geomorph and FactomineR 2.12 packages; Adams and Otárola‐Castillo 2013; Lê et al. 2008), “bgpca” (Between‐Group PCA using the ade4 1.7–24 package; Dray and Dufour 2007), “GM” (geometric mean), and “2D” (convert 3D array to a 2D data table via the geomorph package). Each of these options are useful data transformations used in GM analyses. In addition, two of these options can be combined to create unique combinations of data transformations. For example, Procrustes residuals can be input into Response with the argument Res_transform = “bgpca + procdist” to obtain Procrustes distances of the between–group PC scores for classification tests.

Exploratory Data Analysis Functions

The CSGM package offers several functions related to exploratory data analysis which can produce informative and interactive data visualizations that can inform on the relationships between shape and cross‐sectional geometry (Figure 1). To visualize shape variation, this function offers the GMA umbrella function to facilitate a complete GM analysis including Generalized Procrustes Analysis (GPA), bilateral symmetry analysis, PCA variable and individual variation plots (lolligen/hullgen), and unsupervised cluster analyses of shape‐based variation (CAV). Further, CSGM is equipped with two additional functions: ASCG and SCG provide a visual and quantitative method to identify shape variation based on centroid size (ASCG), biomechanical and/or group variables (SCG). Each of these functions (CAV, lolligen, hullgen, lolshape, ASCG/SCG) can be used as standalone functions and lolligen and SCG in particular can analyze cross‐sectional geometric properties.

GMA and Associated Functions (CAV, Lolligen, Hullgen, Lolshape)

The umbrella function GMA performs several tasks in the following order: landmark array data wrangling, GPA, bilateral (a)symmetry analysis, hierarchical cluster analysis, and generates interactive PCA variable and individual plot visualizations. GMA accepts a landmark configuration (as an array, matrix, or data table), transforms it into a p × k × n (landmarks, dimensions, observations) array and subjects it to GPA from an optimized gpagen function from the geomorph package. Users can fine‐tune their GPA procedure through the argument “curves”, by importing a curves matrix, and also pass on additional arguments from gpagen directly through GMA. Optionally, if “bilat_sym = TRUE”, the GMA function will conduct a bilateral symmetry analysis using geomorph's bilat.symmetry function. The GMA function extends bilateral symmetry analysis following the approach by conducting Mantel tests to evaluate whether the same factors are influencing the symmetric and asymmetric shape variation on the left and right sides (Lazić et al. 2015).

The GMA function passes a matrix of Procrustes residuals to the CAV function which is designed to perform hierarchical clustering analysis and generate useful plots. CAV itself can be used standalone and users can input any data table or matrix, not just shape data. It uniquely automates the optimization of clustering parameters by iteratively testing methods to maximize an agglomerative coefficient and runs the most optimal agglomerative model using pvclust 2.2 (Suzuki and Shimodaira 2006), and CAV creates a fan plot. Simultaneously, CAV performs k‐medoids clustering via the cluster 2.1.8.1 package (Maechler et al. 2013), using silhouette widths (Witten et al. 2013) to mathematically determine the optimal number of clusters (k). CAV then generates cluster plots, minimizing the subjectivity often found in exploratory clustering.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) can be conducted using either unweighted (argument “w_morph_pca = TRUE”), phylogenetically weighted (argument “phy”), morphometrically weighted (argument “w_morph_pca = TRUE”) approaches powered by automatically optimizing the gm.prcomp function from the geomorph 4.0.10 package. The GMA function uses the PCA_variances function to estimate the number of significant PCs through a factor analysis via PC scores conducted through the fa.parallel function from the psych 2.5.6 package (Revelle and Revelle 2015). PCA_variances generates a data table highlighting the cumulative and per component percentages of explained variances and compiles PCA data. PCA data is passed to the lolligen function, which is a uniquely interactive PCA visualization tool created by customizing the plotly 4.10.4 package framework (Sievert et al. 2021).

Lolligen creates 2D/3D PC plots between every combination of meaningful PCs, in ascending order, identified by the function PCA_variances. If a grouping variable or list is passed to the GMA function, a separate nested list object, organized by each grouping variable in the list, will be created with the unique combinations of PC plots, but with convex hulls generated by the novel hullgen function. Finally, the shape extremes for each meaningful PC are calculated as the center shape subtracted by the absolute of the eigenvector multiplied by the minimum eigenvalue of that PC (i.e., original variable estimation). The shape extremes are then visualized using the lolshape function which produces 2D/3D interactive shape visualizations to view these shape extremes. Metadata can be supplied to lolligen and lolshape by completing the arguments “ID”, “Group”, “coord_name”, and “subsets”. The arguments “ID” and “Group” will show the specimen ID and group assignment when hovering over a point on a PC plot whereas “coord_name” and “subsets” will display over a landmark point and group landmarks by subsets on the PC shape extremes plots.

ASCG and SCG Functions

ASCG can take a GMA function output, or a p × k × n landmark array, to create interactive 2D or 3D plotly plots highlighting shape differences between the minimum and maximum allometrically influenced variables. The function uses a Procrustes shape linear regression to predict the landmark arrays for the minimum and maximum natural log centroid size, or any proxy for size, using the procD.lm function from the geomorph package. It also allows the subsetting of each shape comparison by an optional grouping variable(s), permitting the comparison of allometrically influenced shape variation by group. We redirected this approach with the function SCG to view shape variation across any variable, numeric or character. If a numeric variable is supplied, shapes associated with the minimum, mean, and maximum measurement will be automatically visualized.

Hypothesis Testing Functions

A core innovation of the CSGM package is its ability to conduct complex, multi‐model hypothesis tests through a single parallelized framework. Unlike standard GM packages (e.g., geomorph, Morpho) that typically require manual scripting for each individual test or data subset, hypothesis testing functions in CSGM accept a hierarchical “Models” object input (Figure 2). The hypothesis model structure in Figure 2 highlights what is required to run any hypothesis function in CSGM. For these hypothesis testing functions, you can have any number of hypothesis models and hypothesis tests. Creating a hypothesis model object is simply creating a list object that defines a series of overarching hypotheses regarding the research question called a “hypothesis model”. Within this list object, users need to create a list object for every single hypothesis model. Within the list object of a hypothesis model are hypothesis tests. These are pairwise dataset comparisons between a response (dependent) dataset and a predictor (independent) dataset. Within the hypothesis model, a hypothesis test list object needs to be created for each hypothesis test. Within each hypothesis test list object, the user needs to specify (in order) (1) a response dataset, (2) a predictor dataset, and (3) dt_parameters which list any sort of dataset transformation procedures desired for the datasets prior to analysis. The dt_parameters is a list object to pass arguments for the DT function in the CSGM package. Subsetting of datasets is permissible by passing a points_set object with the same structure as the hypothesis models but instead of the response and predictor datasets and dt_parameters, only contain a list object which lists each subset for the (1) response dataset and (2) predictor datasets by concatenating the variable numbers to subset.

Detailed practical example of the models nested list structure required for the hypothesis testing functions within the CSGM package when the argument “models” is required. This models object can have hypothesis models or lists that define a series of tests. Within each hypothesis model is a hypothesis test or list object defining a statistical test to be conducted on two multivariate datasets. Within each hypothesis test are three objects: “Response” or the dependent dataset which will be used for prediction, “predictor” or the independent dataset which will be used to make predictions, and dt_parameters as a list object with the parameters for the DT function.

To execute these models, the package provides four primary functions designed for parallel processing: cor.bio (correlation), pls.bio (covariation), pslr.bio (Procrustes linear regression), and reg.bio (machine learning classification/regression). While these functions leverage established statistical algorithms (e.g., R packages geomorph for Procrustes sums of squares, caret for regression/classification), they extract these computations in a custom automation layer that handles the nested data inputs and subsetting logic and enhances these algorithms through a unique application of multivariate variable selection (VIP/NComp for pls.bio and reg.bio), cross‐validation (pls.bio and reg.bio), integration statistics and correlation network maps (cor.bio), and permutation procedures (pls.bio and reg.bio). To further streamline the workflow, the package includes the umbrella function AABA (Analysis of Associations Between Attributes), which can execute one or all of these statistical modules sequentially, returning a unified output object that can be queried using the standard summary function.

cor.bio

The function cor.bio offers a computationally efficient way to parallel process multiple correlation analyses using the hypothesis model testing approach (see Figure 1). This function generates correlation matrices for entire and subsetted datasets using Pearson's correlation coefficients (Cohen et al. 2009). Statistically significant (alpha ≤ 0.05) correlograms, which visually represent the association between variables, are drawn from these matrices and stored in a user‐specified directory (Wei et al. 2017). In addition, correlation network plots visualize the direction and degree of statistically significant variables above a correlation coefficient threshold (r > 0.6 by default). For significance tests, the function conducts either a permuted Mantel test for same‐structured data (i.e., same number of variables) or a permuted canonical correlation test for different‐structured data (Somers and Jackson 2022). As a result, the function estimates a correlation/integration coefficient and significance value between multivariate datasets and generates intramatrix overall correlation coefficients.

pls.bio

The pls.bio function conducts cross‐validated and permuted covariance tests between shape and biomechanical data by subjecting them to one of six possible partial least squares (PLS) approaches: traditional, canonical, orthogonal, (wide) kernel (all from the pls 2.8‐5 package) (Mevik and Wehrens 2007), two‐block (plsdepot 0.2 and Morpho 2.12 packages) (Sanchez 2012), and sparse (mixOmics 6.28 package) (Rohart et al. 2017). The function optimizes these six distinct Partial Least Squares (PLS) algorithms into a single parallelized workflow. However, pls.bio extends these base computations by implementing a custom statistical framework designed to ensure statistical rigor. The function employs a novel optimization algorithm, NComp, which automatically identifies the best latent variables using a ‘one‐sigma’ cross‐validation rule. This balances predictive power (Q 2) with model parsimony (low root mean square error of prediction or RMSEP), preventing the overfitting common in high‐dimensional analyses. Second, the function integrates a custom Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) selection process (Akarachantachote et al. 2014) and a Randomized Residual Permutation Procedure (RRPP) (Collyer and Adams 2018) directly into the validation pipeline via the internal plsr_stats function. The VIP function within pls.bio permits three thresholds for variable selection: (1) variable contribution (VIP scores > = 1), (2) mean based, and (3) median based. Third, the function identifies statistical significance (p values for RMSEP and Q 2) calculated robustly using repeated and multifolded cross‐validation using the RRPP approach, a validation step absent in standard PLS packages.

pslr.bio

The pslr.bio function serves as a high‐throughput automation tool for Procrustes shape linear regressions. While it utilizes procD.lm (Adams and Otárola‐Castillo 2013) as the underlying statistical engine to ensure rigorous Type I error control via RRPP, pslr.bio extends this functionality by implementing a parallelized list‐processing architecture. Unlike standard procD.lm usage, which typically fits a single model (or nested terms) to a single dataset at a time, pslr.bio accepts a hierarchical ‘Models’ object containing multiple independent hypotheses and data subsets. The function automatically manages data matching for each subset, executes the models in parallel, and aggregates the results. This allows researchers to test dozens of distinct biological hypotheses (e.g., comparisons across different taxonomic groups or anatomical regions) in a single run, replacing the need for extensive manual scripting loops. This linear regression works well with shape data but it can cause model overfitting when predictor datasets are large (usually greater than 50 variables).

reg.bio

The function reg.bio is the only function within the hypothesis testing phase that does not natively utilize the hypothesis model‐based approach but is incorporated into the AABA function with hypothesis model functionality. This is because reg.bio is a powerful multivariate prediction or classification tool which cross‐validates and/or bootstraps multivariate predictions from any method within the caret 7.0 (Kuhn et al. 2020) and e1071 1.7 (Dimitriadou et al. 2006) package and performs RRPP permutation testing in an efficient multivariate context. Since these packages can only supply univariate predictions, this function extends these functions and runs an algorithm that calculates multivariate predictions and classification problems by using custom made functions in utils.R to calculate inferential statistics and model performance from multivariate variable predictions rather than averaging the interpretations of each test sequentially. This function also does not rely on standard accuracy metrics which can be misleading in high‐dimensional contexts. Instead, it integrates a novel statistical engine (see m_R2 in utils.R) that evaluates multivariate model fit using six distinct approaches (1) averaged correlations, (2) multivariate Pearson correlation, (3) determinant, (4) eigen‐based determinant (eigenvalues), (5) Pillai's trace, and (6) regularized trace (Ledoit‐Wolf shrinkage) (Chu and Pillai 1979; Cohen et al. 2009; Kass 1989; Ledoit and Wolf 2004, 2017).

Further, users can select a wide range of options to test for statistical significance (p value) such as using the determination coefficient (R 2), RMSEP, and generalized cross‐validation statistic (gcv) (Kuhn et al. 2020; Lukas 2006; Welham et al. 2012). The function can identify important variables through regularization such as by passing the pls.bio's results for VIPs (when “VIP_trim = TRUE” with function AABA) or elastic‐net regularization (Zou 2006). It also will perform the test on a full model (when “full_model = TRUE”) or a variable trimmed model (full_model = FALSE). As a whole, the reg.bio function procedure can be deconstructed into seven steps: (1) Data transformation and variable selection, (2) A full model test is conducted for data tuning and variable trimming. (3) RRPP test for early permutation convergence is performed, (4) Repeated k‐fold cross‐validation or bootstrapping is performed. (5) Full permutation testing is conducted if early convergence is not reached. (6) Shape or data predictions are made. (7) Regression results are summarized.

Summary

Finally, the CSGM package adapts the base function summary to summarize the key results from the function outputs within the package. This function accepts outputs from the following primary functions: GMA, AABA, cor.bio, pls.bio, reg.bio, and pslr.bio. When a GMA object is called, the following will be generated: (1) a PCA scree table along with a 3D PCA plot, (2) results for an optional bilateral symmetry analysis, and (3) results for the unsupervised agglomerative and k‐medoids clustering. For hypothesis testing functions, the model performance and inferential statistical results for all hypothesis tests along with its subsets will be provided in a single table.

Results

GMA Function Highlights Postcanine Mandibular Corpus Asymmetry

Exploratory shape analyses carried out by the GMA function revealed a strong asymmetric pattern in the shape variation between the symphysis and postcanine mandibular corpus cross‐sections. A grouped convex hull 3D PCA plot by skeletal collection generated by the lolligen function (Figure 3A) revealed a strong asymmetric relationship between the posterior corpus and the symphysis (Figure 3B).

3D visualizations automatically provided by the GMA function. (A) 3D convex hull PCA plot of the first three PCs from the shapes of all bone cross‐sections in the study sample, (B) shape variation of the first PC for the 3D convex hull PCA plot in 3A, (C) 3D convex hull PCA plot of the first three PCs from the asymmetric shape variation captured by the bilateral symmetry analysis, and (D) the shape variation of the first PC for the asymmetric shape component of the bilateral symmetry analysis. Plots cropped and adjusted in Adobe Photoshop 26.5.

Positive scores along the first Principal Component (PC1) correspond to mandibles with generally shorter corporeal and symphyseal heights. In these individuals, the symphysis exhibits a pronounced anteroposterior curvature, with the outline of the mentum osseum showing a more anteriorly projecting and superiorly placed pogonion externally, while internally displaying a deeper genioglossal fossa and a posteriorly projecting mental spine. Asymmetrically, the right corpus (at P3/P4 and M1/M2) appears vertical and narrow towards the alveolar margin but projects buccally at the basal margin, featuring a pronounced lingual depression and basal thickening. Conversely, the left corpus at the P3/P4 is relatively vertical buccally but convex inferolingually, with cortical bone widening at the mid‐corpus (mandibular torus) and becoming thickest basally, while the M1/M2 takes on a general “U” shape. Alveolar positioning also shifts, with the symphysis and right corpus positioned more labial–buccally, whereas the left side is more lingual.

In contrast, negative PC1 scores represent mandibles with taller corpora and symphyses. These shapes display a less pronounced anteroposterior curvature, a less projecting mental protuberance, and a more inferiorly placed pogonion. Internally, the symphysis shows a pronounced planum alveolare, a less projecting but thickened mental spine region, and considerably thicker cortical bone. The right corpus exhibits greater lingual curvature and thickening (mandibular torus), while the left corpus remains vertical on both buccal and lingual aspects with only modest basal thickening. Here, the alveolar bone of the symphysis and left corpus tends to be more lingually positioned, while the right side is more labial–buccally oriented.

The second principal component (PC2) further resolves the variation in cortical distribution and symphyseal convexity. Negative shape loadings on PC2 largely resemble the positive loadings of PC1, with individuals possessing the most anteriorly pronounced mentum osseum clustering in the positive PC1 and negative PC2 morphospace. However, the positive shape loadings of PC2 differ from the negative loadings of PC1 regarding cortical bone topography; specifically, the thickest distribution of cortical bone in positive PC2 specimens is placed more inferiorly along the anteramial and mylohyoid shelves, in the basal corpus, and at the symphysis near the mental spine and pogonion. Additionally, while negative PC1 scores are associated with more convex shapes in regions of greatest cortical thickness, PC2 highlights subtle variations in these convexity patterns. These comparisons demonstrate that the overall patterns of shape variation and asymmetry expressed in PC1 represent the dominant morphological signals across the study sample, with PC2 capturing secondary variations in cortical distribution.

Interestingly, this asymmetric relationship between the postcanine mandibular corporeal and symphyseal shapes is not primarily the result of allometry. The GMA function output GPA_stats$centroid_size indicated that the natural log of centroid size revealed only 9.3% (p < 0.001) of the total shape variation can be explained by log centroid size (to improve the score). Of this 9.3% of the shape variation, 16.38% of this shape variation can be explained by log centroid size (p = 0.01). As such, allometrically controlled shape changes do not primarily explain the relationships observed.

The GMA function also conducted a bilateral (a)symmetry analysis which indicated that right‐sided asymmetry accounts for 94.86% (p < 0.001) of the total posterior corpus shape asymmetry, with only approximately 10% (p < 0.001) of this variance shared with random symmetric variation. Figure 3C highlights a 3D PCA plot of the shape variation explained by bilateral asymmetry, whereas Figure 3D highlights these shape differences along PC 1 from Figure 3C. The primary axis of shape variation explained by the bilateral symmetry analysis is related to the mediolateral and slight anteroposterior positioning of the posterior mandibular corpus. However, a Procrustes shape linear regression identified that this particular corporeal asymmetry explains only 10% of the variation in the symphysis itself, suggesting that the asymmetry observed in the posterior corpus is a signal distinct from the general shape variation of the symphysis.

Despite this, the mandibular corporeal shape asymmetry observed in PC 1 and PC 2 of the PCA for all cross‐sectional shapes was further corroborated by the CAV function. While the agglomerative clustering yielded indistinct groupings across skeletal collections, which is also supported by the large degree of overlap in the 3D PCA plot with group convex hulls (Figure 2), the k‐medoids analysis identified two biologically distinct clusters based on Procrustes distances (Figure 4). Cluster 1 was defined by individuals with reduced chins and robust right corpora, while Cluster 2 grouped individuals with projected chins and robust left corpora. This segregation aligns with functional hypotheses regarding unilateral chewing preferences, suggesting a functional trade‐off between symphyseal projection and working‐side corpus robusticity (Figure 4).

(A) k‐medoids cluster plot identifying two distinctive clusters and (B) agglomerative hierarchical cluster fan plot grouped by skeletal collection.

This asymmetric relationship was further observed using the function SCG by visualizing shape variations across the left and right sides of the postcanine mandibular corpus cross‐sections using the polar second moment of area (J) as a measure of overall bending or torsional resistance (Figure 5). We applied the SCG function to the left and right mandibular corpus with the symphysis separately and compared the differences between maximum and minimum overall bending resistances (J). The shape variations corroborated an aspect of the asymmetric variation observed in the total shape PCA and k‐medoid cluster analyses where anterosuperiorly projected mentum osseum appear to be associated with minimal bending resistance on the right side with narrower cross‐sections whereas they are wider, and thus more robust, on the left side. The opposite pattern was observed on the right side, supporting the hypothesis that anteriorly projected symphyses may be partly associated with left‐side unilateral chewing preferences.

Latent Variable Optimization and Non‐Linear Integration Reveals Novel Insights

In contrast to linear methods, the pls.bio and reg.bio functions, which utilize latent variable optimization and advanced model validation techniques revealed strong, significant integration between the shape and cross‐sectional properties of the posterior corpus with symphyseal shape. Table 4 demonstrates that the cross–sectional properties (i.e., dimensions, biomechanics, thicknesses) and shape of the postcanine mandibular corpus are clearly linked with symphyseal shape as the sparse PLS model from the pls.bio function was able to identify strong predictive potential (Q 2) of symphyseal shape across all nested tests. Additionally, the most important variables from a VIP analysis of the sPLS results are visualized in Figure 6 and were generated by the pls.bio function. Notably, the pls.bio function identified this relationship by optimizing the cross‐validated predictive power (Q 2) rather than the standard fit (R 2). In this context, R 2 represents the mean squared correlation of the diagonal elements (standard goodness‐of‐fit), whereas Q 2 is calculated via the Predicted residual error sum of squares (PRESS) relative to the Residual Sum of Squares (RSS). By utilizing the NComp function to tune the number of latent variables based on the one‐sigma rule for Q 2, the analysis avoided the overfitting observed in the linear regressions. This robust tuning revealed that the right posterior corpus exhibited a notably stronger integration with symphyseal shape (Q 2 = 0.956) compared to the left side (Q 2 = 0.801). Furthermore, the consistent selection of a single latent variable for the biomechanical datasets suggests that a singular functional signal, likely related to overall robusticity, drives this integration.

Finally, the reg.bio function results are summarized in Table 5. These results provided the most robust explanation of variance by employing Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines (MARS) to account for non‐linear relationships. Unlike the overfitting observed in pslr.bio, reg.bio utilizes a robust determination coefficient and cross‐validation approach. Collectively, these results lend further support to the SCG analysis that the mandibular symphysis is asymmetrically integrated with the postcanine corpus cross‐sectional properties. Variables represent the following: (df) degrees of freedom, (F) F statistic as the mean squared error divided by the mean sum of squares, (RMSE) root mean square error for model error, (R 2) repeated cross‐validated or bootstrapped squared correlation coefficient, (BIC) Bayesian Information Criterion as a measure of model fit, and (p value) for statistical significance.

Discussion

CSGM is an accessible, open‐source R package that offers a rigorous toolkit to analyze the relationships between shape and multivariate datasets. As demonstrated above, this package uses highly optimized functions that can conduct exploratory data analyses and multivariate statistical inferences. While primarily a data analysis toolkit for cross‐sectional data, this function does offer some robust functions for importing landmarks (import_land), flexible dataset transformation (DT), and calculating cortical thicknesses. Uniquely, this package provides several functions that support pairwise dataset comparisons that enable both data subsetting and informative variable selection processes, allowing researchers to identify complex trends across multivariate data. Our novel hypothesis testing approach enables users to conduct multiple independent statistical tests to gain a more refined understanding of data relationships.

An important consideration in GM research has been in interpreting the results of shape‐based variation across groups (e.g., skeletal collection) and univariate variables (e.g., centroid size, biomechanical variables) (Mitteroecker and Gunz 2009; Bhutiani 2015; Nicholson and Harvati 2006; Parr et al. 2012; Rosas and Bastir 2004; Weber and Bookstein 2011). The umbrella function GMA is uniquely customized to perform an all‐in‐one GM analysis on landmark data. This includes performing interactive 3D PCA individual and shape plots, hierarchical clustering of Procrustes residuals, and statistical analyses including bilateral symmetry analysis and estimating allometric influences. While there are R packages to independently conduct these analyses, the interactive visualizations and optimized workflow offered by GMA makes it a unique and robust function. In addition, users can utilize the ASCG function to visualize allometric influences on shape and the SCG function to identify shape variations among groups or any numeric variable. These visual aids help clarify shape variation and group or variable relationships.

Furthermore, the analysis of geometric properties from bone cross‐sections in relation to its shape can be difficult as not all geometric variables need to follow a clear linear trend. This is particularly relevant with mandibular corpus cross‐section data as demonstrated when comparing the pslr.bio and cor.bio results to the pls.bio and reg.bio results from this study. The biomechanical and cortical bone relationships of the posterior corpus were not identified as important factors in symphyseal shape when conducting canonical correlation tests and linear regressions. However, the importance of these variables was revealed in sparse PLS (pls.bio) as well as with MARS regression (reg.bio), which can better evaluate non‐linear data (Kartal Koc and Bozdogan 2015; Krzanowski 2000). Each of these analyses was able to reveal these relationships using the rigorous model validation and statistical analyses offered by the pls.bio and reg.bio functions, further highlighting the capability of the hypothesis testing functions available within CSGM. Thus, this package provides a statistical framework to holistically analyze data relationships due to the limitations of each method and is necessary to evaluate the complexity of data in CSG and GM contexts.

Moreover, by using the functions offered by the CSGM package, this study was able to identify two primarily independent signals of mandibular side asymmetry which are strongly integrated with mandibular corpus shape dynamics. The bilateral symmetry analysis demonstrated that side asymmetry could explain almost all posterior corpus variation that was not strongly related to symphyseal shape variation. On the other hand, the PCA on mandibular corpus shape shows a clear relationship between an anterosuperiorly projected mentum osseum and inferior placement of the posterior corpus superstructures. However, this study provided clearer evidence that both sides of the posterior corpus are integrated with symphyseal shape, likely providing evidence for variation in unilateral (para)masticatory preferences among the study sample. This was supported by k‐medoids cluster analysis. This demonstrates the need for multimodal analysis in biological contexts to clarify these complex data relationships.

The functions within this package are not only applicable to GM contexts but rather can also be applied to a broad range of CSG‐focused applications. Questions regarding the biomechanical relationships between multivariate datasets would benefit from the robustly optimized functions uniquely offered by the CSGM package. Additionally, this package's hypothesis model‐based approach and data subsetting provide both beneficial constraints to approaching research questions through the scientific method and gaining a broad perspective on relationships between datasets. The quick access to popular data transformations and easily interpretable summary statistics provides more accessibility for researchers to test and visualize complex data relationships in human biology. Future versions of this package will focus on functions designed to interpret the analytical results and apply subsequent interaction analyses and structural modeling of the hypothesis testing structures to provide further information on data relationships and structures.

Conclusion

The CSGM package offers an innovative and comprehensive toolkit to automate an entire GM and biological analysis from start to finish. This package addresses the data complexity issues within CSG and GM research which often require repetitive analyses and inter/intra‐bone analyses by providing a holistic statistical approach to functionally test hypotheses and analyze complex multivariate data with only a few functions. The package applies a multimodal approach which combines GM analysis, correlation, covariation, and regression analyses to offer novel insights into the relationships between complex geometric and shape data. By applying a unique hypothesis testing approach, the CSGM package allows researchers to organize multiple hypothesis testing across multivariate dataset comparisons. The functions within this package also offer accessible and interactive visualizations to easily interpret data relationships. Therefore, this package offers numerous ways to accessibly study GMs and interpret complex data relationships needed to answer complex questions in human biology.
