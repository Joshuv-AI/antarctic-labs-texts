Can a cephalogram be used to estimate palatal suture maturity to inform expander selection
Authors: Wei Lu, Xiaotong Weng, Lingyun Cao, Bojun Tang, Wei Wang, Hong He, Carlos Flores-Mir, Tingting Zhao, Fang Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe maturity of the mid-palatal suture (MPS) could largely determine the necessity of more invasive interventions for correcting transverse maxillary deficiency. CBCT, the most commonly used method for assessing MPS maturity, is not routinely available and exposes patients to higher levels of ionizing radiation than a cephalogram. Given the substantial number of morphological features contained in a cephalogram, this study aimed to estimate CBCT-defined MPS maturity using an entire cephalogram alone via deep learning (DL) algorithms.MethodsA total of 543 paired CBCT and cephalogram files available from two distinct hospital settings formed the internal and external datasets. Data augmentation and five-fold cross-validation strategies were applied. Six ResNet-based DL algorithms were initiated via transfer learning. The AdamW optimizer and an early-stopping strategy were employed during training. The models were subsequently tested on the held-out external dataset. The Grad-CAM technique was applied to offer insights into the models’ decision-making process.ResultsAll algorithms exhibited promising classification performance, with area under the curve (AUC) values ranging from 0.91 to 0.97. The models with the TKSGA module demonstrated better discriminative ability, calibration, and clinical usefulness than their baseline counterparts. Models integrated with the TKSGA module exhibited an interpretable decision pattern. The heatmaps revealed that morphological characteristics of patients’ dentition, cervical vertebrae, and nasopharyngeal airway were primary areas of interest for the DL models.ConclusionsWhen CBCT imaging is unavailable or not justified, a lateral cephalogram coupled with advanced DL algorithms, could be considered as a supplementary tool to inform palatal expander selection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40510-026-00641-2.

Background

The maturity of the mid-palatal suture (MPS) could largely determine the necessity of more invasive interventions for treating palatal transverse deficiency [1]. In the prepubertal and pubertal periods, when the MPS has not fully fused, the success of rapid palatal expansion (RPE) can be achieved through tooth-borne appliances alone [2]. However, for those with an increased ossified MPS, surgical support or skeletal anchorage is required to avoid undesirable side effects, such as periodontal strain and even treatment failure [3, 4].

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is the most commonly used method for assessing MPS maturity [5]. Clinicians can classify MPS maturity into 5 stages (A-E) using high-resolution axial views. Stages A, B, or C tend to represent an opening suture and lower resistance to palatal expansion [6, 7]. In contrast, RPE with surgical or skeletal assistance is recommended for patients in stages D or E. Therefore, early discrimination between stages C and D is critical.

Although CBCT is widely employed to determine MPS maturity, it is not routinely obtained before orthodontic treatment. Besides, recent research has shown that cumulative childhood exposure to ionizing radiation is linked to a higher risk of several cancers during one’s entire lifespan [8]. Higher computed tomography utilization among adults further exacerbates this potential risk [9]. In this sense, a low-radiation approach to evaluating MPS maturity is desirable. For this attempt, several researchers have explored the associations between age, gender, and MPS maturity [10, 11]. However, substantial inter-individual variability found in histological and radiological studies suggested that they are not consistently reliable indicators, especially during the transitional period from stage C to D [12].

On the other hand, previous evidence has shown promising correlations between MPS maturity and morphological characteristics contained in a lateral cephalogram, such as cervical vertebral maturation (CVM) staging [13, 14]. Lateral cephalogram is a practical examination commonly used to assess craniofacial skeletal maturation. It is widely employed in orthodontic treatment, orthognathic surgery, and cleft lip and palate surgery. These findings suggest that it may be possible to estimate the ossification degree of the palatal suture on CBCT images through a lateral cephalogram alone.

The past five years have witnessed remarkable advances in deep learning (DL) related dental research [15]. DL algorithms can detect useful diagnostic information that is sometimes overlooked by human observers from clinically relevant imaging. This advantage has facilitated efficient image-based disease diagnosis, potentially surpassing human experts [16]. For example, a DL model has been developed to estimate mandibular growth status using a lateral cephalogram [17]. Another recent study reported that DL algorithms could estimate the incidence of chronic kidney disease and type 2 diabetes from retinal fundus images [18].

Building on the DL potential, we hypothesized that DL algorithms could capture features depicted on a lateral cephalogram and provide supplementary information to estimate CBCT-defined MPS maturity.

Methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of School & Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University (No. WDKQ2024-B86) and in accordance with the Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Dental Research [19] and Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM) [20].

Image acquisition

The inclusion criteria were defined as: (1) 30 years or younger; (2) no history of orthodontic treatment; (3) no history of syndromic maxillofacial deformities; (4) no history of nasopalatine duct cyst, impacted teeth adjacent to their palate, or other systemic diseases that can influence the development of the palate. Those radiographs of suboptimal quality were excluded. All radiographs were previously required by clinicians for orthodontic diagnosis and treatment, and were not specifically requested for this study. For pediatric patients younger than 10, CBCT examinations were indicated only when an impacted tooth or a jaw cyst was suspected.

Lateral cephalogram and CBCT scans from the same patient taken between 2020 and 2025 at the Main Site and Optics Valley Branch of the Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University were collected. The lateral cephalograms were obtained using the OP200D (Soredex, Finland) at the Main Site, while at the Optics Valley Branch the Hyperion X9 (MyRay, Italy) was used. The patients were required to maintain an upright position with the Frankfort horizontal plane parallel to the ground during scanning. The CBCT scans were performed using NewTom VG (QR srl, Verona, Italy). A field of view of 15 × 12 cm, an acquisition time of 3.6 seconds, and a resolution of 0.30 mm were applied. A default windowing setting for hard tissue assessment was employed throughout this study.

In previous DL-based dental research using CBCT scans, sample sizes ranging from 186 to 400 were employed, which enabled satisfactory model optimization and testing for classification tasks [21–24]. A minimum of 400 samples was planned to be collected. The detailed flowchart of the sample selection process of this study is depicted in Fig. 1A. The de-identification process was conducted by retrieving images from the hospital database without any additional information.

Data annotation

The Angelieri method was adopted and modified by combining axial and coronal views to enhance annotation reliability (see Supplemental Fig. 1) [25]. Similar modification has been employed in a previous study, which suggested that this method could enhance annotation robustness [26]. Before the MPS maturation assessment, the patient’s head would be verified and positioned correctly across all three axes. Initially, the patient’s mid-sagittal plane was oriented in both the coronal and axial views. In the sagittal view, the slice was used to align the palate parallel to the blue line. We classified MPS maturity into 5 stages (A, B, C, D, and E) according to Angelieri’s protocol. Given the challenges and clinical relevance of evaluating the intermediate stages (C and D), coronal slices were analyzed to confirm ossification of the palatine bone. In the axial view, the green line was moved from the posterior nasal spine point to the incisive foramen. Meanwhile, the palatine bone in the coronal slice was visually inspected to determine where the high-density region was present (fused suture). The final annotation was determined by combining the information from both axial and coronal views. Given their clinical relevance, we classified individuals with MPS maturation stages D and E into the Mature group, and those with MPS Stages A, B, and C into the Immature group. For patients with a Mature MPS, the clinicians should consider skeletal or surgical supported expansion, while RPE remains feasible for those with an immature MPS. Therefore, discriminating between stages A, B, and C does not provide further clinically meaningful information in orthodontics.

Two orthodontic clinicians (W.L. and X.W.) were trained and calibrated prior to data annotation. Subsequently, they independently and in duplicate annotated the CBCT files via Dolphin Imaging software (Dolphin Imaging & Management Systems, Chatsworth, USA). After a two-week interval, 50 CBCT files were randomly shuffled and reevaluated by the same annotators. The reference standard was established by a comprehensive discussion with one experienced orthodontic expert (F.H.). Both the annotators and the expert were blinded to patients’ clinical information and to each other’s assessments.

Data processing

The cephalograms from the Main Site of the Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University constituted the internal dataset, while those from the Optics Valley Branch of the Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University were allocated to the external dataset. The internal dataset was used exclusively for hyperparameter selection, model training, and validation via 5-fold cross-validation. It was completely withheld from the test process. All samples in the internal dataset were first split into a 5-fold cross-validation framework at the subject level, ensuring that images from the same patient would not be included in both the training and validation subsets within any single fold. Subsequently, random data augmentation after padding, including flipping, up to 10 degrees of rotation, up to 30-pixel shifts, and brightness and contrast adjustments, was applied only to the train fold of the internal dataset. To avoid potential feature explosion and overfitting, all cephalogram images were subsequently resized to 224 × 224 pixels and saved as JPEGs.

Model architecture

The ResNet backbone, a well-documented architecture in DL-related dental research, was utilized in this study [27]. It is a typical CNN architecture with four layers, demonstrating robust capabilities for capturing local representations. However, due to their constrained receptive fields, CNN-based DL models share the limitation of global feature learning. To address this, we introduced a novel transformer-based attention module, Top-K Sparse Global Attention (TKSGA). This module can perform global attention on a sparse set of top-K elements, enhancing computational efficiency while maintaining global context. By integrating a CNN backbone with a transformer attention mechanism, the hybrid architecture enables the model to capture local and global features on cephalograms efficiently.

Adequate network depth could prevent over- or under-fitting and save substantial computational resources. We employed the ResNet-18, 50, and 101 variants in this study. The TKSGA module was incorporated before the residual connection in each block. Moreover, to evaluate the performance of TKSGA, we also established models without the module for each variant. Subsequently, these models were trained, evaluated, and tested for their ability to estimate MPS maturity from the lateral cephalograms. Figure 1B and C provide detailed illustrations of the attention module, model architecture, and overall pipeline.

Model training

The established models were initialized using pre-trained weights transferred from ImageNet_1K. All parameters, except those in the TKSGA module and the fully connected layer, were frozen prior to training. During the 5-fold cross-validation stage, the models were trained with the AdamW optimizer, employing a differential learning-rate strategy. The TKSGA module received a learning rate twice that of the classification head to accelerate convergence of the newly introduced parameters. Moreover, both were scheduled through a warm-up strategy with cosine annealing [28]. Specifically, the learning rates were increased from 10% of the target value over the first warm-up epochs, and then gradually decreased to 20% during the annealing phase. All six models were trained and validated using the cross-entropy loss function with a batch size of 32. For each variant, the Optuna framework was used to identify the optimal learning rate and weight decay based on the validation F1 score. During the 5-fold cross-validation stage, the models were also trained progressively on several subsets of the training data, and a learning-curve analysis was performed to post-hoc evaluate the sufficiency of the sample size.

The training and validation process would be early stopped if the validation F1 score showed no improvement for 5 consecutive epochs. The model checkpoint with the highest F1-score was automatically saved for subsequent external testing. The hold-out external dataset was used to evaluate the models’ performance and to interpret their decisions. Additionally, we conducted a post hoc robustness analysis by excluding the samples at the MPS stage E. To enhance model explainability, the Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) technique was applied [29]. All experiments were performed with a fixed random seed on an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 4090 Ti (24 GB) GPU, utilizing the PyTorch library [30]. Supplemental Table 1 presents the hyperparameter sets we adopted.

Statistical analysis

Reliability was evaluated using Cohen’s Kappa. The agreement level was interpreted using the Landis and Koch scale [31]. The average ages of the Internal and External groups were compared using a t-test because the distributions were approximately normal. Meanwhile, the binary categorical variables were analyzed through the Chi-square test. The proportion and mean differences were calculated using the Newcombe method and Welch’s t-interval, respectively.

Accuracy , precision , sensitivity , specificity , F1-score , consistency between model prediction and reference (Cohen’s Kappa) and their respective 95% confidence interval (C.I.) were calculated through a bootstrap strategy. Additionally, we used the F1 Score as a key metric to assess the models’ classification performance. DeLong’s test was applied to statistically compare the discriminative capacity of different models [32]. The Bonferroni correction was applied across 9 pairwise model comparisons, covering depth effects (18 vs. 50 vs. 101) within each attention group and attention effects (NA vs. A) at each depth level. A p-value less than 0.00555 was considered statistically significant.

Calibration slopes were plotted, and the intercepts and Brier scores were calculated to assess the calibration of the algorithms. Decision-curve analysis was also performed to assess the clinical usefulness of the algorithms by quantifying the net benefits [33].

Results

A total of 549 samples were collected after screening. However, owing to the scarcity of samples younger than 10 years in this study, only one subject was annotated as Stage A, and only five subjects had a Stage B MPS. To ensure representativeness, these samples were excluded from the training and validation processes. Eventually, the internal dataset consisted of 437 samples, and the external dataset included another 106 samples, which represented the typical 8:2 split ratio for training and test sets.

Model explainability

To interpret the decision-making processes of the six models, we applied Grad-CAM to the final convolutional layer of their final block. The explainability heatmaps for mature and immature MPS groups were generated (Fig. 5). Meanwhile, models integrated with the TKSGA module exhibit robust attention patterns that rely on regions of clinical significance, including patients’ cervical vertebrae, dentition, and nasopharyngeal airway. The dentition region emerged as a significant focus, regardless of MPS maturity or ResNet backbone depth. Moreover, the stages of patients’ cervical vertebrae may also be associated with the models’ judgments of MPS maturation. Moreover, the models’ attention to the nasopharyngeal airway regions was also identified in patients with an immature MPS. The incorrectly classified samples were transparently reported in Supplemental Fig. 4. The models integrated with the TKSGA module still demonstrated a consistent pattern of attention compared with their counterparts. The characteristics of cervical vertebrae, dentition, and nasopharyngeal airway remained significant regions of interest. When the cephalograms show discrepancies between dental and skeletal features, the model’s decision will be affected.

Discussion

In this study, we proposed a series of algorithms that can estimate MPS maturity using a lateral cephalogram. These models were developed via 5-fold cross-validation and tested for generalizability on an external hold-out dataset. All variants demonstrated promising discriminative capacity, as evidenced by high AUC values. However, the models integrated with the TKSGA module exhibited better classification ability, explainability, and clinical usefulness. These results support our hypothesis that DL algorithms can capture useful features of craniofacial structures from a lateral cephalogram to provide supplementary information for expander selection.

The most well-known classification system for MPS maturity was proposed in 2013 and has since gained widespread use in guiding clinical decisions regarding palatal expansion [25]. However, there is controversy over the reliability of this method [34, 35]. This can be attributed to the use of only one or two axial views in the assessment, with coronal views not considered. However, MPS can be ossified in both the vertical and sagittal directions. In this regard, we adapted the original method by analyzing ossification in both coronal and axial views. Given the high level of agreement, this approach proved effective. However, assessing MPS ossification on CBCT remains challenging because this imaging modality is not routinely available. Therefore, this study introduces a novel DL-based algorithm to estimate the CBCT-defined maturity of MPS through a cephalogram.

DL is capable of learning the intrinsic patterns from images to estimate unseen outcomes [36]. In this study, we interpreted the decision-making process of all six models through the Grad-CAM technique. All the models were not fed with clinical information other than the cephalograms. As shown by the heatmaps, the DL algorithms without the TKSGA module failed to produce useful presentations. Nevertheless, the models with TKSGA showed consistent activation across multiple clinically significant anatomical regions, including the dentition, cervical vertebrae, and nasopharyngeal airway. These regions partly reflect the skeletal or chronological development of patients, which cannot be fully disentangled from a cephalogram. Future studies that incorporate larger, more balanced datasets are required to validate this approach. Nevertheless, given the difficulties in determining MPS maturity from age alone, the algorithms suggested that useful craniofacial features can be captured to inform expander selection. They may provide supplementary diagnostic information during the transitional period, particularly when CBCT is unavailable or not clinically justified. Notably, the specific planning of palatal expansion should still be based on multiple factors, including age, mid-palatal maturity, transverse deficiency, periodontal condition, dental anchorage, and relevant examination findings.

Although the results indicated several regions of interest, further investigation into predictive factors is warranted. For example, the morphological characteristics of the developing dentition are important, though imperfect, indicators of craniofacial development. Research has revealed strong correlations among dentition maturation, the hand and wrist, and the cervical vertebrae; the correlation between Demirjian’s stage of the first premolar and skeletal maturity is the strongest [37]. Nevertheless, a previous study suggested that dental maturity is associated with skeletal morphology in school-age children [38]. In this study, the regions of the two premolars and the second molar have been a consistent focus. This can be attributed to the portrayed association between dental maturity and the pubertal growth phase [39]. Mixed dentition, where the premolars and second molars are still unerupted, is linked to patients with an immature palate.

On the other hand, the regions around the cervical vertebrae and the nasopharyngeal airway have also received significant attention from the models. The CVM stages can provide vital insights into craniofacial complex development [40, 41]. Patients with immature MPS usually present with a lower CVM skeletal age. Additionally, the nasopharyngeal airway region has emerged as a key area of focus in patients with immature MPS. According to Moss’s theory, the development of the dentofacial complex depends on normal nasal breathing [42]. However, adenotonsillar hypertrophy can obstruct the nasal airway and impact the normal respiratory circulation. Such obstructions can influence the developmental pattern of the nasomaxillary complex, including the palate [43]. Hence, the morphological characteristics of the CVM and nasopharyngeal airway regions may also serve as modifiers in predicting MPS maturation. Another potential benefit of examining multiple influencing regions is that, if one region is unusually influential, indicators from the other regions can compensate for this bias and still render the model useful.

In the context of palatal expansion, false-positive predictions would lead to unnecessary invasive intervention, while false-negative predictions could contribute to undesirable side effects. Therefore, the best model was selected by comparing the F1-score. The results suggested that integrating the TKSGA module significantly enhanced discriminative ability across all ResNet backbone depths. Moreover, according to calibration metrics, algorithms with the TKSGA module were also more calibrated than their baseline counterparts. Only a conservative tendency was observed for mature samples, which is clinically desirable. Meanwhile, clinicians could be allowed to adjust the algorithms according to different clinical contexts, since the algorithms yielded positive net benefits across a wide range of threshold probabilities. Beyond high classification performance, the models with the TKSGA module exhibited improved clinical interpretability, as evidenced by the explainability heatmaps. The superior learning capacity of the proposed models is attributable to the incorporation of the TKSGA attention module. Introducing this transformer-CNN hybrid structure enhances their ability to learn global and local feature representations. However, as the complexity of algorithms increases, feature explosion may also lead to overfitting.

According to a previous study, conventional machine learning techniques are the primary choice when researchers seek to validate potential associations in AI-related dental research [15]. However, this approach is usually based on a matrix-structured dataset, and the valuable image information should have been fully utilized. Early evidence has documented that DL algorithms can detect subtle associations between non-adjacent structures that may be imperceptible to human observers [18]. In this study, we tested and validated the hypothesis that DL models can estimate CBCT-defined MPS maturity from a lateral cephalogram alone. The success of the algorithms added to the evidence that DL may help identify both clinical and subclinical characteristics, thereby facilitating efficient image-based diagnosis beyond human capabilities. Meanwhile, the identified image-based correlations can inspire further research by offering potential factors.

Strengths and limitations

In this study, we introduced several DL algorithms that can capture and use morphological features from a cephalogram to estimate MPS maturity. They could help clinicians to make more informed decisions, particularly when CBCT imaging is unavailable or not justified.

The proposed models exhibited satisfactory performance and promising applicability, while several adaptations may further enhance their robustness. First, ethical concerns arose regarding the acquisition of sufficient CBCT data from children under 10. Nevertheless, a larger, multicenter dataset involving MPS stages A-E could further improve the models’ robustness and expand their applicability. The current dataset is collected through convenience sampling and may not be a proportional representation of all malocclusion types. Moreover, current models accept only a lateral cephalogram as input. Given the complexity of craniofacial development, models can benefit from a multimodal approach. Thirdly, this study is exploratory and aims to test the hypothesis that a cephalogram can provide supplementary information for selecting palatal expander. Further studies are still required to validate this approach and to assess whether cephalogram-derived information can outperform other clinical factors.

Conclusion

Based on this preliminary study, advanced DL algorithms could estimate CBCT-defined MPS maturation from a lateral cephalogram. When CBCT imaging is unavailable or not justified, clinicians could use the proposed algorithm as a supplementary tool to inform palatal expander selection. The TKSGA module further enhanced the discriminative capacity and explainability of ResNet backbones for this task. The Grad-CAM heatmaps suggested that dentition, cervical vertebrae, and nasopharyngeal airway can be key regions of interest for predicting MPS maturation stage using a cephalogram.
