Establishing the role of ZBTB20 mutations in GnRH deficiency and impaired neurogenesis in the subventricular zone: a human cohort and animal model study
Authors: Jun Guan, Jingtan Zhu, Yuting Li, Min Nie, Jing Zhang, Zhiheng Chen, Huadie Liu, Dan-Na Chen, Ruizhi Zheng, Meichao Men, Jia-Da Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundCongenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (CHH) arises from defective development or dysfunction of GnRH neurons. Olfactory bulb (OB) malformations frequently accompany CHH, a condition termed Kallmann syndrome (KS). Nevertheless, approximately 50% of CHH cases remain genetically undefined.MethodsLeveraging our 15-year effort in building one of Asia’s largest CHH cohorts (812 unrelated patients, and 49 CHH families), we identified a pathogenic ZBTB20 mutation (p.R300C) in a four-generation CHH family with seven affected members. We generated nervous system-specific Zbtb20 conditional knockout (cKO) mice to assess phenotypic and mechanistic consequences. Molecular analyses, including transcriptional regulation assays, were performed to explore downstream pathways.FindingsFour additional heterozygous ZBTB20 variants were detected in the CHH cohort. Zbtb20 cKO mice recapitulated hallmark CHH features: GnRH neuron deficiency, hypogonadism, and infertility. Notably, migration of GnRH neurons in Zbtb20-deficient embryos was stalled along the defective terminal nerve (TN) scaffold. Furthermore, Zbtb20 deficiency impaired proliferation of neural stem cells (NSCs) in the subventricular zone (SVZ) and disrupted their migration via the rostral migratory stream (RMS) to the OB. We also identified Thbs4 as a key downstream target, as ZBTB20 transcriptionally activates Thbs4, which is critical for SVZ NSCs migration.InterpretationOur study establishes ZBTB20 as a CHH-associated gene and demonstrates its essential role in SVZ-OB neurogenesis. Research in context

Evidence before this study

A systematic literature review was performed by searching Web of Science and PubMed using the following terms: (“ZBTB20”) AND (“Kallmann syndrome” OR “congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism” OR “GnRH deficiency”), with no language restrictions. Accumulating work has uncovered critical developmental events relevant to CHH pathogenesis, yet many genetic contributors remain to be discovered. The terminal nerve (TN) is thought to serve as a migratory scaffold for GnRH neurons. Olfactory bulb (OB) malformations are frequently observed in patients with CHH with anosmia/hyposmia, i.e., Kallmann syndrome (KS). Nevertheless, severe OB hypoplasia does not invariably lead to reproductive phenotypes, and the link between GnRH deficiency and OB atrophy in KS remains unclear.

Added value of this study

We identify ZBTB20 as a CHH-associated gene through a pathogenic variant (p.R300C) that fully segregates in a large four-generation family, with further support from a significant enrichment of rare ZBTB20 variants in our CHH cohort (n = 812). Nervous system-specific Zbtb20 knockout mice recapitulate core CHH features, including GnRH neuron deficiency, hypogonadism, and infertility. Zbtb20 deficiency disrupts the TN scaffold, which likely underlies the arrested migration of GnRH neurons. Moreover, Zbtb20 loss impairs the proliferation of neural stem cells in the subventricular zone (SVZ) and disrupts neuroblast migration along the rostral migratory stream. We also identify Thbs4 as a direct transcriptional target of ZBTB20, revealing the ZBTB20-Thbs4 axis as a key regulatory pathway in SVZ-OB neurogenesis.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings establish ZBTB20 as a CHH-associated gene and demonstrate its roles in the SVZ-OB neurogenesis and TN development, thereby explaining the co-occurrence of OB atrophy and GnRH deficiency in patients with KS with ZBTB20 mutations. This work expands the genetic and mechanistic understanding of CHH and provides support towards inclusion of ZBTB20 in genetic diagnostic panels for patients with CHH.

Introduction

Congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (CHH) is a rare endocrine disorder characterised by impaired secretion or action of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), leading to delayed or absent puberty and infertility. With an estimated prevalence of 1 in 4000–10,000 individuals, CHH arises either from defective migration of GnRH neurons from the olfactory placode to the hypothalamus during embryonic development or from postnatal defects in GnRH secretion and signalling.1 Clinically, CHH manifests as low levels of gonadotropins and sex steroids, with phenotypic heterogeneity ranging from isolated reproductive deficits to complex syndromic forms accompanied by anosmia (Kallmann syndrome, KS), skeletal abnormalities, or neurodevelopmental defects.2,3 About 60 genes have been implicated in CHH; however, the genetic aetiology remains unidentified in nearly half of CHH cases.4

During embryogenesis, GnRH neurons originate in the olfactory placode and migrate to the hypothalamus along specialised axonal scaffolds, including the terminal nerve (TN).5 Concurrently, neural stem cells (NSCs) originating in the subventricular zone (SVZ) migrate through the rostral migratory stream (RMS) to the olfactory bulb (OB).6 KS related genes, such as ANOS1 and FGFR1, are also involved in the SVZ-RMS-OB migratory pathway.7,8 Postnatal defects in GnRH secretion involve dysregulation of kisspeptin signalling (KISS1/KISS1R), neurokinin B (TAC3/TACR3), or leptin pathways (LEP/LEPR), which modulate pulsatile GnRH release.9, 10, 11

Intriguingly, many CHH-associated genes were initially discovered through investigations of syndromic disorders with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.12, 13, 14, 15 For instance, CHD7 is first identified as the causative gene for CHARGE syndrome-a multisystem disorder characterised by coloboma, heart defects, choanal atresia, growth retardation, genital anomalies, and ear abnormalities.16 Subsequent studies revealed that CHD7 mutations account for about 6% of CHH cases.17,18 SOX10 is initially associated with Waardenburg syndrome.19 The observation of olfactory-bulb agenesis and hypogonadism in some SOX10-mutant patients led to the identification of its role in GnRH neuron ontogeny.20 These findings imply that certain genes involved in multisystem syndromes with hypogonadism may be associated with CHH as well.

The ZBTB20 gene, encoding a zinc finger and BTB domain-containing protein, is a crucial transcriptional regulator involved in various physiological processes, including neurodevelopment, glucose and lipid metabolism, and immune response.21, 22, 23 Mutations in ZBTB20 are linked to Primrose syndrome, a rare genetic disorder characterised by intellectual disability, skeletal abnormalities and distinctive facial features.24 Interestingly, some male patients with ZBTB20 mutations are reported with gonadal hypoplasia (micropenis or cryptorchidism).25, 26, 27 Indeed, Zbtb20 global knockout mice show sexual infantilism. However, its global disruption only results in a reduction in the somatotrope population and a complete loss of mature lactotropes, while other lineages of the anterior pituitary, including thyrotropes, corticotropes, and gonadotropes remain largely unaffected.28 Furthermore, sperm-specific Zbtb20 ablation does not cause sterility.29 These studies suggest that the gonadal hypoplasia in patients with ZBTB20 mutations likely arises through mechanisms independent of direct germ cells or pituitary defects.

In this study, we identified a loss-of-function ZBTB20 mutation in a large CHH pedigree and four more ZBTB20 variants in a well-recorded CHH cohort from China. Nervous-system-specific Zbtb20 conditional knockout (cKO) mice recapitulated key CHH phenotypes, including disrupted pubertal maturation and gonadotropin deficiency. Zbtb20 deficiency disrupts TN scaffold, which may underlie the arrested migration of GnRH neurons. Further, Zbtb20 deficiency impaired neurogenesis in the SVZ, with cell cycle perturbations (prolonged S phase) and aberrant migration of neuronal progenitors. These findings establish ZBTB20 as a new CHH-associated gene, warranting further validation in diverse ethnic cohorts to assess its broader pathogenic contribution.

Methods

Patients and clinical examinations

We recruited a four-generation KS family (L1840) from Hunan Province, China. Twenty-two members participated in this study. All subjects underwent detailed physical examinations, olfaction testing and hormone testing. In addition, brain MRI was performed on three affected members (II:2, III:1, III:7). Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples following standard phenol-chloroform methods.

Through a 15-year, multi-centre effort to build a large Asian CHH cohort, we have established a whole-exome sequencing (WES) dataset comprising 812 patients with CHH (630 males and 182 females), with participants recruited from Peking Union Medical College Hospital (Beijing), People’s Hospital of Henan Province (Zhengzhou), Xiangya Hospital, and Hunan Children’s Hospital (Changsha). The mean age at diagnosis was 21.9 years. The patients were diagnosed with CHH according to established criteria. Diagnosis of CHH is age-dependent and requires exclusion of alternative causes. In infants (especially boys with micropenis/cryptorchidism), low gonadotropins and sex steroids during minipuberty suggest CHH. In adolescents (≥14 years in boys, ≥13 in girls), absent puberty with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism must be differentiated from constitutional delay. In adults, infertility or osteoporosis with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism warrants evaluation, excluding acquired causes like pituitary tumours. Olfactory testing and genetics aid diagnosis. For those with KS, anosmia or hyposmia confirmed through formal testing using the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT), with scores falling below the 5th percentile for their respective age group. The control group consists of 4936 unrelated, ethnic-matched Chinese participants without CHH features.

WES

WES was conducted in three affected (II:2, III:1, III:7) members and one unaffected (III:3) member. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood lymphocytes by means of the phenol/chloroform method. The whole exome was captured by the Agilent Sureselect Human All Exon V4+UTR enrichment platform (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California) and then sequenced on the Illumina Hiseq X Ten platform (Illumina, San Diego, California) with paired-end reads of 350 bp. Generated reads were aligned to the human reference genome hg19 (UCSC version) with the use of Burrows-Wheeler aligner software (v0.7.15). Quality control was recalibrated with the use of GATK table recalibration. Duplicate reads were detected by Picard 1.14 (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/).

Whole genomic sequencing (WGS)

WGS was performed on two patients (III:1 and III:7) and one unaffected (III:3) member. Copy number variation (CNV) Calls and homozygosity mapping were analysed by WGS. Integrative Genomics Viewer was used to visualise the mapping reads, read depths, and breakpoints. CNV was performed using the log2-ratio of read depth on each exon. Depth data were exported by GATK using the option for depth of coverage and whole chromosome bed files. Matrix attachment region (MAR)-Wiz was used to identify scaffold/MARs), which were related to breakage events and nonhomologous recombination. DGV database and its derived series of Stringent Lib, Inclusive Lib, and DGV Gold Standard were used to annotate the detected benign CNVs. To eliminate false positives, we assessed sequence read depth for all putative events, requiring duplication or deletion in read depth in the subjects when compared to normal parental exomes. CNVs and homozygosity mapping analysis were detected on all the CHH related and candidate genes.

Antibodies

The antibodies used for immune-staining were as follows: mouse anti-GnRH (1:500, MAB5456-C, Millipore, RRID:AB_571014); Rabbit anti-ZBTB20 (1:500, Cat# 23987-1-AP, Proteintech, RRID:AB_2879389); Goat anti-Robo3 (1:300, AF3155, R&D systems, RRID:AB_10644167); Rat anti-PHH3 (1:400, ab10543, Abcam, RRID:AB_2295065); Rabbit anti-Ki67 (1:400, 12202s, Cell signalling, RRID:AB_2620142); Rat anti-BrdU (1:250, ab6326, Abcam, RRID:AB_305426); Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:1000, ab150113, Abcam, RRID:AB_2576208); Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:1000, ab150078, Abcam, RRID:AB_2722519); Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-Rat IgG (H + L) (1:1000, ab150167, Abcam, RRID:AB_2864291). An anti-Flag antibody (F1804, Sigma, RRID:AB_262044) was used for ChIP assay.

Animals

As reported previously, Zbtb20 conventional KO mice are not viable beyond early postnatal stages due to severe growth retardation, hypoglycaemia, and premature lethality.23,28 To explore the pathological mechanisms underlying CHH induced by ZBTB20 mutations, we specifically knockout Zbtb20 gene in the central nervous system. Zbtb20loxp/+mice, with two loxP sites flanking exon 8, were generated by Cyagen Inc (Suzhou, China; Strain S-CKO-12132) by using CRISPR-Cas9 technology. Zbtb20loxp/+ mice were bred with Nestin-Cre transgenic mice (Jackson Laboratory; stock number 003771) for several cycles to obtain Zbtb20loxp/loxp: Nestin-Cre mice, which specifically knockout of Zbtb20 in the central nervous system. Mice of the same sex were group-housed with 3–5 animals per cage under controlled conditions [temperature, 20 ± 2 °C; relative humidity, 50–60%; 12:12-h light–dark (LD) cycle, lights on at 7:00 AM and lights off at 7:00 PM] and had free access to food and water. All procedures regarding the care and use of animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Central South University of China.

The primers for genotyping WT and Zbtb20loxp/ alleles were as follows:

Zbtb20 forward-1: 5’- GCTTTCTTCTCAGTATGGAATGGT -3’

Zbtb20 reverse-1: 5’- TTTACACTGTTCCCAGAAGGGAG -3’

Zbtb20 reverse-1: 5’- TTTACACTGTTCCCAGAAGGGAG -3’

Zbtb20 forward-2: 5’- CATCTTCCAGATCCCAAAGAAACC -3’

Zbtb20 forward-2: 5’- CATCTTCCAGATCCCAAAGAAACC -3’

Zbtb20 reverse-2: 5’- ATTTCTACCAAGGTGACACAGTTTG -3’

Zbtb20 reverse-2: 5’- ATTTCTACCAAGGTGACACAGTTTG -3’

The PCR product, amplified by Zbtb20 forward-1 and Zbtb20 reverse-1, for WT allele is 134 base pairs (bp), whereas that for Zbtb20loxp/ allele is 203 bp.

3D immunostaining, clearing and imaging of E14.5 embryos

The SOLID-DL protocol was applied in this study for 3D labelling of GnRH and Robo3 within the intact E14.5 embryos.30 In brief, fixed embryos were incubated with the primary antibody (rabbit anti-GnRH: Z26950-1-AP, 1:500, Proteintech; goat anti-Robo3: AF3155, 1:300, R&D systems) and secondary antibody (A-11037, 1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 7 days each. The embryos were then counterstained with TO-PRO-3 (T3605, 1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 5 days to label the cell nuclei. After finishing labelling, the embryos were cleared with the SOLID clearing protocol. Cleared embryos were imaged using light-sheet microscope (Lightsheet 7, Zeiss) equipped with a 5 × objective lens (NA = 0.16).

Calculation of cell cycle kinetic parameters

The calculation of cell cycle parameters was performed as follows: EdU (50 mg/kg, i.p.) was injected at T = 0 h to label progenitor cells in S-phase at the beginning of the experiment. BrdU (50 mg/kg, i.p.) was administered at T = 1.5 h to label cells in S-phase at the end of the labelling period (Scells). Animals were sacrificed at T = 2 h. Based on the approximately 30 min required for each label to circulate and incorporate detectably, the effective interval during which cells incorporate EdU but not BrdU (Ti) is 1.5 h. Since progenitor cells are not synchronised, a fraction of the initial EdU-labelled cohort will exit S-phase during Ti and thus be EdU+/BrdU− (Lcells). The ratio Lcells/Scells equals Ti/Ts, allowing calculation of Ts (S-phase duration) as Ts = Ti/(Lcells/Scells). The total cell cycle length (Tc) was derived using the formula Tc = Ts/LI, where LI (labelling index) is Scells/Pcells, and Pcells represents the total number of proliferating cells in the sampling area. Additionally, the G2 duration (TG2) was determined by BrdU labelling at 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 h post-injection, with TG2 defined as the time when 50% of pHH3+ cells are BrdU+. The M-phase length (Tm) was calculated from the fraction of pHH3+ mitotic cells multiplied by Tc. For each time point and parameter, data were collected from at least 3 animals (biological replicates), and the entire experimental protocol was repeated three times to ensure reproducibility.

Cell lines and culture

The HEK293T (ATCC® CRL-3216™) and N2a (Neuro-2a, ATCC® CCL-131™) cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination through routine testing. Experiments were performed with cells between passages 5 and 20 post-thawing. HEK293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), and N2a cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM, Gibco) containing 10% FBS. All cells were incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Plasmid transfections were carried out using Lipofectamine 2000 Reagent (Invitrogen) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Tissues from 8-week-old mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C, dehydrated in graded sucrose solutions (10%, 20%, 30%), and embedded in a 1:1 mixture of OCT compound and 30% sucrose. Sections (30 μm) were sliced using a cryotome (Leica CM1850). For staining, sections were permeabilised with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min, blocked in 5% BSA, 10% FBS, and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 60 min, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washes, fluorochrome-labelled secondary antibodies were applied.

To measure the nuclear localisation of ZBTB20 proteins, plasmids expressing 3 × FLAG tagged WT or mutant ZBTB20 were transfected into HEK293T cells plated on cover slides. Twenty-four hours after transfection, cells were either fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, or treated with CSK buffer (10 mM PIPES, pH 6.8, 100 mM NaCl, 300 mM sucrose, 3 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 0.5% Triton X-100, with phosphatase and protease inhibitors) to remove free nuclear protein aggregates before being fixed. Cells were permeabilised with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min, blocked in 5% BSA, 10% FBS, and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 60 min, and incubated with anti-FLAG antibody overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washes, fluorochrome-labelled secondary antibodies were applied. After staining, slides were mounted with DAPI and imaged using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Leica, TCS SP5).

Measurement of hormone levels in mice

The blood taken from 8-week-old mouse retro-orbital plexuses were collected into 1.5-ml Eppendorf tubes and store at room temperature for 1 h. And serum was obtained by centrifugation for 15 min at 3000 rpm. The serum hormone levels were measured by using ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China): FSH (E-EL-M0511), LH (E-EL-M3053), Estradiol (E-OSEL-M0008) and Testosterone (E-OSEL-M0003).

RNA-sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the SVZ of 8-week-old male mice using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA integrity and quantity were assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies). The mRNA was isolated using poly-T oligonucleotide magnetic beads, followed by PCR amplification and purification with AMPure XP beads to construct the library. Library quantification was performed using a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer, and sequencing was carried out on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Reads were generated using CASAVA base recognition, aligned to the reference genome using Hisat2 (v2.0.5), and gene counts were obtained with featureCounts (v1.5.0-p3). Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (v1.20.0), with adjusted P-values (Padj < 0.05) and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1 as significance thresholds. Enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes in KEGG pathways was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v3.8.1), with corrected P-values < 0.05 considered significant.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

N2a cells were transfected with plasmid expressing 3 × FLAG tagged ZBTB20 or empty vector by using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Forty-eight hours after transfection, cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde (10 min, room temperature), quenched with glycine, and then washed with PBS. Nuclei were isolated using sequential lysis buffers (L1: 50 mM HEPES-KOH, 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, 0.25% Triton X-100; L2: 10 mM Tris-HCl, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA; L3: 10 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% N-lauroylsarcosine). Chromatin was sonicated (Misonix 3000), immunoprecipitated overnight with anti-FLAG antibody (Sigma, F1804), followed by incubation with Protein A/G beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 26162) for 2 h. After washes (50 mM HEPES-KOH, 500 mM LiCl, 1% NP-40), DNA was eluted (50 mM Tris-HCl, 1% SS), treated with RNase A/proteinase K, and purified by AMPure XP Beads (Beckman, A63881) for qPCR.

Luciferase assay

A DNA fragment encompassing the mouse Thbs4 promoter region (−2034 to +1 relative to the transcription start site, TSS) was cloned into the pGL3-basic vector (Promega) to generate the pThbs4-Luc reporter plasmid. N2a cells were co-transfected with the pThbs4-Luc reporter, expression plasmids for WT or mutant ZBTB20, as well as a p300 expression plasmid, using Lipofectamine 2000. Forty-eight hours after transfection, cells were lysed with Reporter Lysis Buffer (Promega). The extracts were assayed for luciferase activity with the Luciferase Assay System (Promega). Luciferase activities were determined using SIRIUS luminometer (Berthold Detection Systems GmbH).

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between two groups were made using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test (for parametric data) or the Mann–Whitney test (for non-parametric data). Comparisons among multiple groups were analysed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sample size determination: The sample size for animal experiments was determined a priori, using G∗Power 3.1 software based on effect sizes derived from pilot studies or previous literature, with an α error of 0.05 and a power (1-β) of 0.8. Randomisation: Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups (WT vs. cKO) using a computer-generated random number sequence to minimise selection bias. Blinding: Investigators were blinded to the genotype (WT vs. cKO) and treatment groups during data collection (e.g., image acquisition, behavioural tests), data analysis (e.g., cell counting, hormonal assays), and outcome assessment. Inclusion/Exclusion criteria: No data points or animals were excluded from the analyses unless they met pre-established exclusion criteria, which were solely based on technical failures (e.g., unsuccessful injection, tissue processing artifacts, or poor-quality RNA/DNA) determined prior to unblinding. All efforts were made to minimise animal use and suffering.

Results

Identification of a ZBTB20 mutation in a CHH pedigree

We investigated a four-generation CHH pedigree with seven affected individuals (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table S1). Individual I:1 presented with hyposmia and delayed puberty; II:2 showed hyposmia, right cryptorchidism, and delayed puberty, achieving conception following unspecified traditional Chinese medicine therapy; The proband (III:7) displayed KS as well as dysmorphic face, cervical scoliosis, bilateral cryptorchidism; II:6 and III:1 manifested KS with dysmorphic faces, cervical scoliosis, and mild cognitive impairment, with III:1 attaining fertility through gonadotropin therapy; IV:1, a seven years old girl, showed anosmia and mild cognitive impairment; IV:2, a five years old boy, exhibited anosmia, dysmorphic face, mild cognitive impairment, bilateral cryptorchidism and micropenis, highly suggestive of hypogonadism.

WES was conducted in three affected members (II:2, III:1, III:7) and one unaffected member (III:3). The average sequencing depth was 50 × and minimal coverage for targeted region by 10 × was 97.9%. We first screened rare sequencing variants (RSVs, MAF ≤ 0.01) in a virtual CHH gene panel with known and candidate CHH genes (Supplementary Table S2). As a result, a total of 75 RSVs were identified in the exomes of three affected members but not in the exome of control. We then performed co-segregation analysis on the 75 RSVs by using PCR-Sanger sequencing. As a result, only ZBTB20 c.898C > T (p.R300C) co-segregated with CHH in this pedigree. This mutation was not reported in East Asian population (EAS) in Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD, public release v2.1.1, GRCh37/hg19). It did not present in a 4936 ethnically matched WES database recruited by Xiangya hospital. ZBTB20 R300C was predicted to be deleterious by SIFT, PolyPhen-2, Mutation Taster, CADD and American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG). This arginine residue showed evolutionary conservation across vertebrates (Supplementary Fig. S1). No CNV in known or candidate CHH genes was found to co-segregate with CHH after WGS in two affected patients (III:1, III:7) members and one unaffected (III:3) member.

ZBTB20 mutations identified in sporadic patients with CHH

We further screened ZBTB20 RSVs in the WES date from additional 812 CHH probands. As a result, four more ZBTB20 RSVs were identified in seven patients (Fig. 1B–D, Supplementary Table S3). The variant ZBTB20 p.S58P (rs1454955241) was present in three sporadic KS cases. In spite of one patient carrying a compound heterozygous PNPLA6 mutation, the other two patients have no mutation in known CHH-related genes. However, this variant has a MAF of 0.0008 in the EAS in gnomAD, and it was also seen in our ethnically matched WES data. As it was predicted to be benign or tolerable by several tools, it is thus considered as a polymorphism.

The ZBTB20 p.A417G (rs897755609) was identified in a sporadic case (L1759), who also carried the FGFR1 p.Q491X mutation and the PROKR2 p.W178S mutation. The ZBTB20 p.A417G was also considered as a polymorphism as it was predicted to be benign or tolerable by several tools. The variant ZBTB20 P433L (rs752208031), with a MAF of 0.000007 in the EAS in gnomAD, was identified in one male KS without other mutations in known CHH genes. ZBTB20 P521S (rs1333489573), with a MAF of 0.000007 in all populations and not reported in the EAS in gnomAD, was identified in one patient with KS without mutations in known CHH genes. Both ZBTB20 P433L and P521S were predicted to be deleterious or possibly damaging by at least two in silico prediction software (Supplementary Table S3).

Using the matched control group of 4936 individuals with similar ethnic, geographic, and age characteristics, we applied the same screening criteria to identify rare ZBTB20 variants. Burden test analysis comparing the prevalence of rare ZBTB20 variants revealed a significant enrichment in patients with CHH (CHH group: 0.86%, 7/812; control group: 0.14%, 7/4936; OR = 6.12, P < 0.001, unpaired t-test).

The R300C and P433L mutations impaired the chromatin-affinity of ZBTB20

As a transcription factor, ZBTB20’s chromatin-binding properties were investigated to assess the impact of CHH-associated mutations. WT and mutant ZBTB20 plasmids were transiently transfected into HEK293T cells and the subcellular localisation of ZBTB20 proteins was visualised with confocal immunofluorescence microscopy (Supplementary Table S4). Using CSK buffer to selectively remove unbound nuclear protein aggregates, we observed distinct differences in chromatin interaction between WT and mutant proteins. Both WT and mutant ZBTB20 proteins initially localised within the nuclei (Fig. 1E). However, following CSK buffer treatment, the R300C and P433L mutants exhibited cytoplasmic diffusion, while WT and other mutant forms maintained nuclear localisation. This differential response demonstrates that the R300C and P433L mutations reduced ZBTB20’s chromatin affinity.

Zbtb20-deficient mice recapitulated CHH phenotypes

To investigate the mechanisms underlying the reproductive deficits in patients with ZBTB20 mutations, we generated nervous system-specific Zbtb20 cKO mice (Supplementary Fig. S2A and B). These mice survived to adulthood without observable viability defects, in contrast to the perinatal lethality reported for conventional global knockouts.28 Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed the absence of ZBTB20 protein throughout the brain of cKO animals (Supplementary Fig. S2C), thereby enabling subsequent phenotypic characterisation.

Zbtb20 cKO mice showed the following CHH-related phenotypes: (1) Hypogonadism. Both male and female Zbtb20 cKO mice showed marked reductions in gonadal size and weight compared to WT controls (Fig. 2A and B). Specifically, testis weight in males and uterine size in females were significantly decreased (Fig. 2C–F). The gonadal weight/body weight ratio was also significantly lower in Zbtb20 cKO mice than in WT controls (Fig. 2D, G). (2) Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism. Serum levels of FSH and LH were significantly reduced in both male and female Zbtb20 cKO mice (Fig. 2H and I). (3) Infertility. Both male and female Zbtb20 cKO mice were completely sterile, failing to produce offspring during 5 cycles mating trial (Fig. 2J). (4) GnRH neuron deficiency. The adult Zbtb20 cKO mice showed a pronounced reduction of GnRH neurons in the medial preoptic area (MPOA) and the median eminence (ME) (Fig. 2K–M). These findings collectively demonstrate that Zbtb20 deletion recapitulates key features of CHH, including GnRH deficiency, hypogonadism, and infertility.

The impaired migration of GnRH neurons in Zbtb20-deficient embryos

To visualise the three-dimensional (3D) migratory trajectories of GnRH neurons, we performed whole-mount immunofluorescence on intact E14.5 embryos using advanced tissue clearing and light-sheet imaging techniques. In WT embryos, a continuous stream of GnRH neurons migrated from the olfactory epithelium toward the hypothalamus (Fig. 3A and B). In contrast, GnRH neuron migration was abnormally stalled in the nasal-forebrain area in Zbtb20-deficient embryos (Fig. 3C–H, Supplementary Videos S1 and S2), magnification images in different key regions showed that there was no GnRH neuron in the hypothalamus of Zbtb20-deficient embryos (Fig. 3E–G). No genotypic difference in GnRH neurons was found in the nose (Fig. 3E, H). Taken together, our data indicated that Zbtb20 deficiency impaired GnRH neuron migration.

Olfactory bulb hypoplasia in Zbtb20 cKO mice

In addition to CHH phenotypes, Zbtb20 cKO mice exhibited significant reductions in body size and weight compared to WT littermates (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. S3). Postnatal brain weight was also markedly decreased in Zbtb20 cKO mice (Fig. 4B, E). As some patients with ZBTB20 mutations present with anosmia/hyposmia, we also compared the OB in WT and Zbtb20 cKO mice. Notably, the OB of Zbtb20 cKO mice showed pronounced volumetric atrophy, with both absolute weight and OB-to-brain weight ratio being significantly reduced relative to WT controls (Fig. 4F and G). Histological analysis of coronal and sagittal sections through H&E staining confirmed structural hypoplasia of the OB in Zbtb20 cKO mice (Fig. 4C and D).

Zbtb20 deficiency prolongs S phase in neural stem cell proliferation

The NSCs within the SVZ are continually differentiated and migrated to the OB in the embryonic and adult brain via the RMS.31 To investigate the cellular basis of OB atrophy, we analysed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from the SVZ reported by Silla et al.32 Zbtb20 showed predominant expression in the NSCs, and B cells which have properties of specialised support cells known as astrocytes, implicating its functional relevance in NSCs dynamics (Supplementary Fig. S4).

EdU pulse-labelling (50 mg/kg, i.p.) revealed significantly fewer proliferating (EdU+) NSCs in adult Zbtb20 cKO mice vs. WT littermates at 2 h post-injection (Fig. 5A and B). To delineate cell cycle perturbations, we performed BrdU/EdU dual-pulse labelling with a 1.5 h interval (Fig. 5C). Quantitative analysis demonstrated prolonged total cell cycle duration in cKO NSCs (cKO 16.0 ± 0.3 h vs. WT 14.5 ± 0.2 h; P < 0.05, unpaired t-test), attributable to specific extension of S phase (cKO 11.0 ± 0.4 h vs. WT 9.0 ± 0.3 h; P < 0.05, unpaired t-test) (Fig. 5D, F). Consequently, the S phase fraction was significantly elevated in mutants (Fig. 5G and H). In contrast, G2/M phase durations remained unchanged between genotypes: G2 phase (PHH3+/BrdU+) persisted for ∼1 h and M phase (PHH3+/Ki67+) for ∼0.8 h in both groups (Fig. 5E, I–K).

Zbtb20 deficiency impairs NSCs migration

To assess potential migration defects, we performed EdU pulse-chase labelling (50 mg/kg, i.p.) in adult mice and analysed cell distribution 5 days post-injection. Sagittal section immunofluorescence revealed significantly fewer EdU + cells along the entire migratory route from the SVZ through the RMS to the OB in Zbtb20 cKO mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 6A–C, Supplementary Fig. S5).

Quantitative analysis demonstrated altered migratory dynamics: while the proportion of EdU + cells retained in the SVZ and RMS was significantly increased in mutants, fewer cells successfully reached the OB (Fig. 6D). These findings indicate that Zbtb20 is required for efficient NSCs migration along the RMS-OB pathway.

ZBTB20 transcriptionally regulates the expression of Thbs4

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the Zbtb20 cKO phenotype, we performed RNA sequencing of the SVZ from Zbtb20 cKO and WT mice. This analysis revealed 159 upregulated and 237 downregulated genes in cKO mice (Fig. 7A). KEGG pathway enrichment showed significant alterations in the extracellular matrix (ECM) receptor interactions and Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathways (Fig. 7B, Supplementary Fig. S6).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) confirmed that Thbs4 mRNA levels were significantly downregulated in 8-week-old Zbtb20 cKO mice (Fig. 7C, Supplementary Table S5). Further, ChIP assays were performed in N2a cells expressing FLAG-tagged ZBTB20 using an anti-Flag antibody (Supplementary Table S6). Analysis of precipitated DNA by qPCR with primers spanning four regions of the Thbs4 promoter revealed significant enrichment at the region amplified by primer pair 3 (−1129 to −1325 bp), indicating direct binding of ZBTB20 to the Thbs4 promoter (Fig. 7D).

To further validate ZBTB20-mediated transcriptional regulation, a luciferase reporter assay containing the mouse Thbs4 promoter was employed. Co-expression of ZBTB20 with the transcriptional coactivator P300 led to a significant increase in luciferase activity, whereas neither ZBTB20 nor P300 alone induced activation (Fig. 7E). Consistently, ZBTB20 and P300 synergically increased the mRNA level of Thbs4 in N2a cells (Supplementary Fig. S7). These findings demonstrate that ZBTB20 directly activates Thbs4 transcription via promoter binding, requiring P300 as a coactivator.

We also evaluated the functional impact of CHH-associated ZBTB20 mutations on transcriptional regulation through the luciferase reporter assay. Consistent with chromatin affinity data, luciferase activity in cells expressing ZBTB20 variants S58P, A417G, or P521S was comparable to WT, whereas R300C and P433L mutants showed significantly reduced activity (Fig. 7F).

Defects of terminal nerve (TN) scaffolds in Zbtb20-deficient embryos

Previous studies have established that the TN plays a pivotal role in guiding GnRH neuron migration by providing an essential scaffold along their migratory pathway.5,33 To investigate whether TN was affected by Zbtb20 deficiency, we first re-analysed the scRNA-seq data from whole embryonic mouse noses as reported by Amato et al.5 As shown in Supplementary Fig. S8A and B, Zbtb20 was expressed in the TN/pioneer cell population in the nasal region and co-expressed with TN markers. The expression of Zbtb20 in the TN neurons was further validated by double immunofluorescence staining, as Zbtb20 colocalised with Robo3, a typical TN marker,33 in the nose (Supplementary Fig. S8C). In contrast, Zbtb20 did not co-localise with GnRH (Supplementary Fig. S8D).

We then used Robo3 antibody to trace the TN neurons in the E14.5 embryos. As shown in Fig. 8A, Robo3-positive TN neurons distributed from the nose to the hypothalamus in a 3-D manner. Therefore, a combination of several plane is required to overview the pattern. Interestingly, Robo3 and GnRH neurons are closely interwoven in the path as demonstrated by both 3-D and 2-D immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 8A and B; Supplementary Fig. S9). However, neither Robo3 nor GnRH neuron was found in the hypothalamus region of Zbtb20-deficient embryos (Fig. 8C and D).

Discussion

In this study, we identified ZBTB20 as a genetic determinant of CHH by integrating data from human genetics and cKO mouse models. WES and WGS revealed a heterozygous ZBTB20 p.R300C variant that co-segregated with hypogonadism in a CHH pedigree. Targeted sequencing of a Chinese CHH cohort identified four additional heterozygous ZBTB20 RSVs, with a significant enrichment of ZBTB20 RSVs in CHH cases compared with controls. In mice, Zbtb20 deficiency impaired SVZ neural stem cell proliferation, disrupted RMS-dependent neuroblast migration to the OB, and altered TN structure as well as GnRH neuron migration. Molecular characterisation revealed that ZBTB20 directly activates Thbs4 transcription. Together, these findings establish ZBTB20 as a critical regulator of both SVZ-OB neurogenesis and GnRH neuron development.

Many CHH-associated genes regulate neuronal migration, axon guidance, or OB development.4 For instance, ANOS1 encodes anosmin-1, an ECM protein critical for OB development and axonal guidance, and mutations in ANOS1 cause X-linked KS.11 Anosmin-1 induces neurite outgrowth and cytoskeletal rearrangements through enhancing FGFR1 signalling in human embryonic GnRH olfactory neuroblasts.34 The FGF8/FGFR1 signalling pathway is essential for OB development and GnRH neuron migration. Disruption of this pathway, which accounts for approximately 10% of CHH cases, impairs olfactory axon guidance and compromises the migratory corridor for GnRH neurons.35, 36, 37 Prokineticin signalling through PROKR2 and its ligand PROK2 is essential for OB morphogenesis and GnRH neuron development. Impaired PROKR2 signalling disrupts the organisation of the olfactory nerve pathway and compromises GnRH neuron migration, resulting in CHH accompanied by variable olfactory deficits.5,38,39 However, it should be noted that although OB malformations frequently co-occur with CHH, evidence suggests this association may be correlative rather than causative. For instance, loss of Arx1 does not prevent GnRH neurons from migrating to the hypothalamus, even though Arx1-deficient mice develop severe olfactory bulb aplasia/hypoplasia.39 Furthermore, olfactory defects in some families with KS do not always co-segregate with CHH.40

In this study, we demonstrated that Zbtb20 deficiency impairs SVZ-OB development and GnRH neuron migration, which may underlie the aetiology of anosmia/hyposmia and CHH associated with ZBTB20 mutations. Although the observed OB hypoplasia in Zbtb20 cKO mice could potentially contribute to infertility through impaired olfaction or pheromone sensing, the pronounced reduction in GnRH neurons (Fig. 3) and low circulating levels of LH and FSH in these animals indicate that failure of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis constitutes the primary mechanism underlying reproductive deficiency.

Additionally, we revealed that Zbtb20 deficiency impairs TN integrity, which may be the leading cause of abnormal GnRH neuron migration. The TN is thought to serve as a migratory scaffold for GnRH neurons.33 Re-analysis of published scRNA sequencing data indicated that Zbtb20 is expressed in TN neurons, supporting the possibility that Zbtb20 may play a cell-autonomous role in TN neuron development. Indeed, our 3D reconstruction results further identified defects in TN development in Zbtb20 cKO embryos (Fig. 8), which likely underlie the arrested migration of GnRH neurons. Interestingly, the migration of GnRH neurons was arrested right behind the disrupted TN scaffold.

Prokr2, identified as a downstream target gene of ZBTB20, plays a pivotal role in regulating circadian rhythms. ZBTB20 directly binds to the Prokr2 promoter, enhancing its expression in the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the central circadian pacemaker.41 However, we did not observe significant changes in Prokr2 expression in the SVZ of Zbtb20 cKO mice, implying alterative mechanisms for ZBTB20 mutations in the aetiology of CHH. These findings highlight the context-dependent regulation between ZBTB20 and its targets. Given that Prokr2 is also highly expressed in the TN,41 it would be worthwhile to investigate whether ZBTB20 regulates Prokr2 expression in this context.

Our demonstration that Zbtb20 depletion impairs NSCs proliferation by prolonging S-phase duration underscores the highly context-dependent nature of Zbtb20’s roles across various cellular processes, including differentiation and apoptosis.21,42 For instance, in liver regeneration, ZBTB20 promotes cellular proliferation through induction of cyclin D1 and modulation of AKT activation.43 ZBTB20 overexpression promoted cell proliferation, while its knockdown using small interfering RNA suppressed proliferative capacity in non-small cell lung cancer cell lines.44 Similarly, during neocortical development, it facilitates astrocytogenesis via functional collaboration with Sox9 and NFIA.

Our transcriptomic analysis revealed significant dysregulation of two key pathways Wnt/β-catenin signalling and ECM pathway (Supplementary Fig. S6). The Wnt/β-catenin pathway, a critical regulator of development and tissue homoeostasis, promotes cell proliferation and differentiation by facilitating β-catenin nuclear translocation and activation of targets such as c-Myc and Cyclin D1.45,46 Although ZBTB20 has been shown to modulate this pathway in hepatocellular carcinoma through PPARγ repression,47 the precise mechanistic link between Zbtb20 depletion and Wnt pathway dysregulation in the SVZ niche remains unclear and warrants further study.

Similarly, ECM-related pathways were markedly altered, with the most significant downregulation of Thbs4, a secreted glycoprotein implicated in cell adhesion, migration and tissue remodelling.48,49 Molecular studies demonstrated that ZBTB20 promoted Thbs4 transcription by binding directly to Tbhs4 promoter (Fig. 6). Thbs4 is specifically and highly expressed in the SVZ and along the RMS. Interestingly, both Zbtb20 and Thbs4 were expressed in the GFAP-positive astrocytes (Supplementary Fig. S4b).21,48 Thbs4 knockout resulted in impaired migration of newly formed neurons along the RMS, characterised by reduced neuronal egress from the RMS and diminished recruitment of newborn neuronal precursors to the OB. Furthermore, Thbs4-deficient mice exhibited severe deficits in cortical injury-induced astrogenesis within the SVZ.48 Collectively, our data suggest that Thbs4 potentially contributes to the NSCs migration deficits observed in Zbtb20 cKO mice.

Notably, Primrose syndrome arises predominantly from heterozygous de novo missense variants clustered in the C-terminal zinc finger domains of ZBTB20, which disrupt residues critical for DNA binding.24 In contrast, none of the ZBTB20 variants identified in our study is localised in the five C2H2 zinc fingers. Such genotype-phenotype disparities are well-documented in CHH.50,51 For example, pathogenic variants in SOX10 are associated with both Waardenburg syndrome and CHH. However, SOX10 loss-of-function (LoF) mutations (e.g., frameshifting indels, nonsense, and splice-altering variants) are enriched in Waardenburg syndrome (79% LoF vs. 21% missense), whereas missense variants dominate in CHH (70% missense vs. 30% LoF).50 Similarly, most patients with CHH carrying truncating CHD7 variants exhibit multiple CHARGE syndrome features, whereas those with missense variants typically present with CHH.51 These observations collectively suggest that the HPG axis may represent the most vulnerable system in syndromes with overlapping genetic aetiologies.

Although our study establishes ZBTB20 as a candidate gene of CHH and elucidates its role in SVZ-OB neurogenesis and GnRH neuron migration, several limitations should be considered. First, the functional validation of ZBTB20 variants was primarily based on in vitro assays and a cKO mouse model, which may not fully recapitulate pathophysiology in patients with CHH. And the interpretation of mutation impact is further complicated in cases where patients carry multiple variants. The relative weight of each variant, as well as potential epistatic interactions between them, remains to be solved. Second, the CHH cohort is predominantly of Chinese Han ancestry, which may limit the generalisability of our findings to other ethnic populations. Third, the incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity of ZBTB20 mutations suggest the potential influence of genetic modifiers or environmental factors, which were not explored in this study. Future work involving multi-ethnic cohorts, functional studies in human-derived cellular models, and investigation of modifier genes will be essential to fully understand the clinical spectrum and mechanistic basis of ZBTB20-related CHH.
