Safe image transmission with hybrid quantum key-genetic encryption via spatial modulation assisted NOMA
Authors: Swetha Thennavan, Avila Jayapalan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis manuscript presents a secure and spectrally efficient Spatial Modulation (SM)-based Non-Orthogonal Multiple Access (NOMA) system for two-user downlink 5G wireless communication. The proposed SM-NOMA shows significant improvements in spectral efficiency and energy savings by activating only one transmit antenna per symbol duration and all other remaining antennas remain silent, reducing hardware complexity and RF chain usage compared to traditional Multiple Input-Multiple Output (MIMO) systems. In terms of detection strategy, the weak user employs Maximum Ratio Combining (MRC) to directly decode its signal, as it receives higher power and does not need interference cancellation. Conversely, the strong user first applies MRC to detect the weak user’s signal, performs Successive Interference Cancellation (SIC) to remove the weak user’s contribution, and then applies MRC again to decode its own signal. This approach balances complexity and performance, offering practical implementation benefits. Additionally, the system includes optimized power allocation to manage interference and maintain user fairness. The proposed SM-NOMA system performance is evaluated using Bit Error Rate (BER), outage probability, and the spectral efficiency under imperfect CSI and imperfect SIC conditions. SM-NOMA outperforms conventional NOMA by significantly reducing BER, lowering outage probability and enhancing spectral efficiency. At a transmit power of 25 dBm, in the case of User 1 the BER is improved from 10−2 (NOMA) to 10−3 (SM-NOMA). Similarly, for User 2, there is an improvement in BER from 10−2 (NOMA) to 10−4 (SM-NOMA). Also, the outage probability values for both the users improved significantly with User 1 dropping from the previous value of 10−1 (NOMA) to 10−5 (SM-NOMA) at 20 dBm transmit power and for User 2 it drops from 10−4 to 8.39233 × 10−5. The overall spectral efficiency improved from 2.57 bps/Hz (NOMA) to 2.74 bps/Hz (SM-NOMA). Wireless systems are easily prone to attacks by intruders. To enhance transmission security, the system integrates Quantum-Enhanced Genetic Encryption (QGE2) a hybrid encryption framework combining quantum gate-generated keys and genetic algorithm-based encryption. The sequence generated using quantum gates is used as a seed value for the genetic algorithm. It is used to encrypt a 256 × 256 8-bit grayscale image. The randomness of the proposed quantum key is tested through the NIST test. The proposed encryption scheme passed all 17 NIST randomness evaluations (15 core + 2 subsections) with p-values ≥ 0.01. Additional metrics such as correlation, entropy, and histogram analysis validate the strength of the encryption against both classical and quantum threats. Introduction

Fifth-generation (5G) wireless systems are expected to provide new types of enhanced user connectivity services such as huge data rates, enhanced capacity, improved security, higher reliability, lower latency, increased quality of service and Spectral Efficiency (SE)1. Unlike previous generations, which utilized orthogonal multiple access (OMA) technique to eliminate inter-user interference, the new generations of wireless networks are striving for improved spectral efficiency. 1G used Frequency Division Multiple Access (FDMA) in which the same channel is shared by many users, 2G used Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA) where the users are allocated the bandwidth based on time slots, 3G used Code Division Multiple Access (CDMA) where the data is covered with the Pseudo noise sequence, and 4G used Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiple Access (OFDMA) where the high data rate symbols is divided into low data rate symbols. All of these OMA techniques in a network assign different time slots, frequency bands or codes to users keeping them orthogonal to avoid collisions2. As far as 5G is considered one of the prominent solutions is Non-Orthogonal Multiple Access (NOMA) that allows numerous users to share the same time-frequency resource block improving system capacity, spectrum efficiency and meeting huge connection requirements3–5. MIMO (Multiple-Input Multiple-Output) technology stands as a breakthrough innovation in wireless communications, enabling more efficient and higher-capacity data transmissions6. MIMO systems7 use multiple antennas at the transmitter and receiver to achieve substantial spectrum gain, making it a key application of emerging technologies. However, issues like various antenna designs and high route loss make it impossible to assume perfect channel state information (CSI)8 and in a typical cellular network baseband processor, radio-frequency (RF) chains and transmit antennas use energy mostly from power amplifiers in each RF chain. As a result, conventional MIMO methods require the same number of RF chains as transmit antennas face challenges due to the consumption of energy9.

Conventional modes of MIMO such as V-BLAST and OSTBC, have a number of shortcomings. These types of MIMO require that multiple RF chains be active at the same time which adds complexity, power consumption and hardware requirements. These systems create significant inter-antenna interference when transmitting from multiple antennas, which interferes with other transmitted signals from multiple RF chains. This limitation becomes particularly strong in use cases with unbalanced antennas where the number of Tx antennas exceeds the number of Rx antennas for downlink transmissions. This limits the scalability and efficiency of conventional MIMO systems which render them inappropriate for next-generation wireless systems such as 5G.

To overcome these challenges, a unique expansion of the multiple-input multiple output (MIMO) technique is proposed, which is Spatial Modulation (SM) that has gained significant attention in academia and industry.

In spatial modulation, a single transmitting antenna is engaged for each symbol transmission and information is provided by both active antenna indices and transmitted amplitude-phase modulation symbols10. It reduces the hardware cost and complexity11. Compared to traditional MIMO techniques such as point-to-point SM requires only one RF chain. SM can drastically reduce design and implementation complexity while improving spectrum, energy efficiency, reliability and greater resistance to inter channel interference12. This approach simplifies and lowers the cost of multiple-antenna systems while maintaining high data rates. Through SM the transceiver design achieves both low complexity and good spectrum efficiency through simple modulation and coding processes13. Additional benefits include improved energy efficiency, reduced detection complexity with fewer receive antennas and RF circuits also eliminated inter-channel interference and no need for inter-antenna synchronization and MIMO compatibility14.

The NOMA principle involves superposition coding (SC) at the transmitter, followed by successive interference cancellation (SIC) at the receiver and to recover the original signal. Furthermore, the NOMA user may employ many power domain resources at the same time, frequency or code domain. As the number of users increases the system gets more sophisticated resulting in lower decoding success rates. To address this issue spatial modulation (SM) in MIMO systems was integrated with NOMA. This reduces system complexity and prevents interference between antennas. Thus SM-NOMA outperforms traditional orthogonal multiple access schemes and also the conventional NOMA providing greater spectral efficiency.

Encryption prevents unauthorised access and protects sensitive data. Encryption has evolved significantly from traditional approaches to cutting-edge solutions that use current computation. Advancements in information security are crucial for sustaining trust and integrity in an interconnected society15. The broadcast aspect of wireless transmission and successive interference cancellation (SIC)-based decoding at receivers provides important security challenges for NOMA, particularly addressing user privacy. Especially the NOMA systems users farther from the transmitter are in a greater level of risk of being intercepted, especially if an eavesdropper is located closer to the transmitter, thus reducing the secrecy capacity for the users further away. Multi-antenna schemes, cooperative relaying and machine learning based intelligent resource allocation schemes are standard mechanisms in diminishing those risks16. Physical layer security (PLS) is a popular method for ensuring secure wireless communications17. NOMA requires safeguarding confidential messages against unauthorised users18.

Encryption is the process of transforming plaintext into ciphertext, rendering information unreadable to unauthorised individuals. Encryption algorithms transform data from plaintext to ciphertext. There are two types of encryptions named symmetric (private key) and asymmetric (public key) encryption. Traditional encryption techniques such as DES, 3DES, AES and RSA are effective for general data but are unsuitable for images due to high redundancy, big data size and strong pixel correlation. To solve these issues, researchers investigated chaos-based image encryption. This method generates secret keys using chaotic maps, which take the advantage of traits including sensitivity to initial conditions, pseudo-randomness, ergodicity, and non-periodicity. These properties make chaos-based image processing algorithms faster, more secure, and more efficient than previous methods. The challenge is Chaotic map-based encryption exhibits extreme sensitivity to initial parameters and improper selection of these values may lead to weak encryption, increasing the risk of key exposure and unauthorized data access. To address this vulnerability, researchers have adopted metaheuristic optimization methods in image encryption to fine-tune the initial conditions and enhance overall security. From the survey, it is understood that metaheuristic methods are employed to optimize initial parameters, which prevents weak encryption outcomes19. A novel beamforming strategy based on a hybrid Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO) combined with L2-norm is proposed in20. In addition, when compared with the traditional encryption methods rapid advancement of quantum computing offers a new area of research called Quantum encryption.

Quantum computers use unitary gates to operate qubits (nlike classical computers that use logic gates such as AND, NOT, OR, and XOR (IBM, 2024). This innovation utilizes principles such as superposition and entanglement to provide computing capabilities considerably beyond those of traditional computers. QKD allows parties to exchange keys securely and detect eavesdropping. Rather than processing bits sequentially, quantum computers use qubits, which can be in several states simultaneously due to superposition (IBM, 2024). Increasing the number of key bits in a QKD system increases the possibility of detecting an eavesdropper. Thus, to improve security, higher key sizes are necessary21.

Traditional methods generate Pseudo-Random Number Generators (PRNGs) that operate using a defined series of mathematical operations to create sequences that resemble randomness. Despite their seemingly random output, these generators follow a fixed logic, making them predictable when their internal configuration is exposed. They require a starting input known as a seed value which plays a crucial role in determining the sequence they produce. This reliance on a seed limits their ability to generate truly random values. On the other hand, Quantum Random Number Generators (QRNGs) derive unpredictability from quantum mechanics. Measurement outcomes are inherently uncertain, as the system collapses unpredictably into one of its eigenstates, producing non-deterministic results22.

Thus, Quantum computing is expected to disrupt present encryption technologies over the next 20 years requiring immediate preparation. Quantum algorithms like Shor’s and Grover’s could break the encryption methods putting secure communications at risk23,24.

Motivated by the existing surveys, the proposed work uses quantum-generated key that is used as a seed for encryption. 128-bit quantum-generated key is used, which is the same length as the standard encryption to have a baseline level of security for protecting sensitive information across sectors25.

The proposed NOMA-SM system superimposes signals from multiple users in the power domain and transmits them over shared time-frequency and spatial resources, improving spectral efficiency. At the receiver, a Maximum Ratio Combining (MRC) technique is employed to combine the received signals across multiple antennas enhancing signal strength before applying Successive Interference Cancellation (SIC). The model accounts for imperfect SIC and imperfect CSI, acknowledging real-world limitations such as residual interference and channel estimation inaccuracies.

The simulation results confirm that the proposed secure NOMA-SM system with MRC-SIC detection offers superior performance in terms of spectral efficiency, error rates and security robustness compared to conventional NOMA even under imperfect conditions.

State of the art

NOMA combined with spatial modulation (SM) has been studied to improve spectral efficiency, throughput, and multi-user performance7–9,25,26. These studies typically assume perfect channel state information (CSI) and ideal successive interference cancellation (SIC), focusing primarily on communication performance while largely neglecting data security.

Chaos- and quantum-based encryption methods30–32,38–42 provide high randomness and theoretical resistance to classical and quantum attacks. However, these approaches are rarely integrated with SM-NOMA under practical wireless conditions, and many assume perfect SIC or ideal channels. Limitations include sensitivity to initial parameters, lack of systematic end-to-end encryption, and limited evaluation under imperfect SIC and CSI.

The works summarized in Table 1 illustrate these limitations, highlighting the need for a comprehensive framework that combines secure, high-performance communication with robust encryption.

System model

A downlink two-user NOMA, spatial modulation, MIMO with imperfect SIC is considered in this manuscript. The base station (BS) is equipped with Mt = 4 transmit antennas and each user has Mr = 4 receive antennas. The BS simultaneously transmits the encrypted image to two users using spatial modulation over a shared wireless channel. The image is encrypted using genetic algorithm for which the seed value is generated using quantum technique.

Figure 1 presents the transceiver architecture of the proposed downlink SIC-based SM-NOMA system implemented with QGE2 security. The system involves two users receiving image data over a common wireless communication channel. The base station (BS) has four transmit antennas with each user having four receive antennas. For both users, input data is grayscale images with the resolution of 256 × 256 pixels. The input images were encrypted with QGE2 encryption, a combination of quantum-generated keys with genetic algorithm. The bitstreams of the encrypted images are then processed independently between the two users. For both User 1 and User 2, each block of bits is divided into two parts one part is used to select the index of the active transmit antenna, and the other part is modulated using 4-QAM to determine the complex symbol to transmit. This ensures that both users independently implement spatial modulation, where only one antenna transmits a modulated symbol per user per transmission slot. The spatially modulated signals from both users are then summed together through superposition coding with optimized power allocation factors (α1 and α2) forming a composite transmit vector. This signal is transmitted over a MIMO channel which is modelled as a Rayleigh fading channel to accurately represent the multipath propagation characteristics commonly found in wireless environments. Next, the BS transmits the combined signal over a channel modelled under Rayleigh fading conditions and imperfect channel state information (CSI). At the receiver both users use MRC to improve their signal quality by using their multiple antennas. User 1 is the weaker user who is located further from the BS. User 1 uses MRC to directly detect its signal from the received superimposed signal. User 2, the stronger user first estimates and subtracts User 1’s signal from the received signal using SIC. User 2 then applies MRC to detect User 2’s data. At the end of the user’s antenna system both received signals are demodulated and decrypted to recover the original images. This architecture is an effective way to implement spatial modulation with NOMA and encryption together to provide spectral efficiency, secure transmission and to accommodate multiple users in a power-domain multiplexing scenario.

Parameter selection

According to 3GPP Technical Report TR 38.90043, in downlink NOMA user equipment (UE) may have 2 to 4 receive antennas. Therefore, this study utilized a 4 × 4 MIMO configuration with 4 transmit antennas (Mt = 4) and 4 receive antennas (Mr = 4). The transmitted symbols used 4-QAM, a widely used scheme balancing complexity and performance in multiuser systems. To model practical channel estimation limitations, an imperfect Channel State Information (CSI) model with error variance σε2 = 10−5 is used. This variance reflects high-fidelity yet non-ideal CSI, comparable to values reported in the literature where acceptable estimation error levels range up to 10− 444. The lower variance demonstrates improved signal processing while accounting for residual imperfections. A path loss exponent η = 3 is used, commonly accepted one for urban macrocell Non-Line-of-Sight (NLOS) scenarios45. It captures signal attenuation caused by dense buildings and environmental obstacles in urban areas. User distances are set to 1900 m for User 1 and 1000 m for User 2, within the 10–5000 m range defined in 3GPP TR 38.901 for Urban Macrocell (UMa) environments. These provide realistic mid- and far-cell coverage in dense urban 5G deployments.

To represent non-ideal interference cancellation, a SIC imperfection factor δ = 0.4 is used. Prior studies3,46 report values from 0.1 to 1.0, where 0.1 yields better performance and 1.0 degrades the system. The moderate value of 0.4 allows evaluation of SIC degradation without assuming extreme cancellation failure. The system bandwidth is 100 MHz, the maximum per-component carrier bandwidth in FR1 (Frequency Range 1) per 5G NR47. The carrier frequency is 3.5 GHz, within the mid-band spectrum (3300–3800 MHz), offering a balance of coverage and capacity suitable for urban 5G deployments. Noise power spectral density is − 174 dBm/Hz, reflecting typical thermal noise. Transmit power ranges from 0 to 30 dBm to evaluate low- and high-power performance. The target data rate per user is 0.1 Gbps (100 Mbps), consistent with reported throughputs in urban 5G at 3.5 GHz. For reference48, reports ~ 100 Mbps in both uplink and downlink. This target supports eMBB services and meets cell-edge throughput expectations.

Maximum Ratio Combining (MRC) is a low complexity detector utilized in wireless systems to increase the quality of the signal received by maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). As MRC applies to Spatial Modulation (SM) systems, it separates the detection process of finding the active transmit antenna (the spatial symbol) and the modulated data symbol (e.g., QAM or PSK) into two separate stages of detection instead of jointly detecting as in traditional maximum likelihood (ML) methods. Thus, to enhance signal reliability, MRC was applied to merge the received signals captured by two spatially separated detectors assigned to the users49. The combined result was then forwarded as the final output.

Hybrid quantum-genetic encryption

In the Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) process, quantum keys are generated using quantum gates specifically Hadamard (H) and Y gates. These gates manipulate qubits into superposition and complex quantum states introducing randomness and quantum uncertainty. The properties of quantum mechanics ensure any interception attempt is detectable, safeguarding the integrity of the key exchange.

Generation of quantum key

In classical computing, bits are the fundamental units of information processed sequentially via logic gates, whereas in quantum computers, it leverages quantum bits (qubits). Classical bits are restricted to binary states (0 or 1), whereas qubit can exist in superposition of the two states, ∣0 and ∣1> represented as a linear combination of these states50.

where α and β are complex amplitudes and the sum of their squared magnitudes satisfies the normalization condition ∣α∣2+∣β∣2=1|.

Quantum gates are the building blocks of quantum circuits, similar to classical logic gates. They manipulate qubit states and are represented by unitary matrices that operate on the state of one or more qubits and are inherently reversible, meaning their effects can be undone by applying the inverse operation.

Various types of quantum gates perform distinct transformations on qubit states. For instance, the Pauli-X, Pauli-Y, and Pauli-Z gates correspond to the quantum analogy of classical NOT, CNOT, and CZ gates, respectively. Additional fundamental gates include the Hadamard gate, which creates superposition; the phase gate, which introduces a phase shift; and the controlled-phase (CZ) gate, which applies a phase shift conditionally based on the state of another qubit. By combining different quantum gates within the circuit, complex quantum algorithms can be implemented to perform advanced computations.

Quantum True Random Number Generators (QTRNGs) exploit the probabilistic nature of quantum observables such as the collapse of a qubit in superposition upon measurement to produce non-deterministic and unbiased random numbers51. QTRNGs allow for the generation of secure, non-reproducible encryption keys that are resilient to both classical and quantum attacks52.

In the proposed QTRNG, each one of the 24 qubits applies a Hadamard gate followed by a Pauli-Y gate, then the qubit is measured. This means that there are two quantum gate operations and one measurement per qubit per shot. Since both the Hadamard (H) and Pauli-Y (Y) gates are single-qubit gates, the total number of single-qubit gates per qubit is two. Therefore, for each of the 50,000 shots, the QTRNG system performs 2.4 million quantum gate operations (48 operations per shot × 50,000 shots) and 1.2 million measurement operations (24 measurements per shot × 50,000 shots). As a result, the number of gate operations scales linearly with the number of qubits i.e., O(n). This shows that the circuit remains computationally feasible, validating its practical implementation capability.

Quantum-genetic concatenation

To further enhance the security QTRNG is combined with optimization schemes like Meta-heuristic techniques. Quantum Key (QK) can handle the key exchange process with a high degree of security, while classical encryption algorithms take care of encrypting the actual data. This hybrid setup ensures that even if one layer is compromised, the other one continues to protect the system, offering a more resilient approach to securing wireless communications.

QTRNG is used in conjunction with a Genetic Algorithm (GA). The quantum key generated utilizing QTRNG acts as the seed for the GA, which is then used to optimize the encryption parameters, offering a more secure and efficient encryption scheme.

In the proposed system, quantum key of size 128 bits is used as seeds for initializing the random number generator in the genetic algorithm which generates a permutation sequence to encrypt and decrypt data. The quantum key ensures that the encryption and decryption are symmetric and it involves permutation of bits based on an evolved sequence generated by GA. The GA performs optimization over generations to create permutation of bits (using selection, crossover, and mutation), which is used to shuffle the bits of the plaintext to obtain the ciphertext. The same process with the same quantum key is used to reverse the permutation for decryption. The core of the GA process is that it initializes a population of random bit permutations, evaluates their fitness based on diversity, and applies crossover and mutation operators to evolve the population over several generations. The best permutation is selected as the final result. To achieve this, the 128-bit quantum key is divided into 32 blocks of 4 bits each, and an XOR operation is performed on each block to generate a 32-bit binary seed. This seed is then converted to a decimal value and used to initialize random number generator, ensuring a highly diverse and unpredictable initial population for the genetic algorithm.

The Hamming distance-based fitness function used in the proposed encryption framework quantitatively measures the amount of bit-level mismatch between the known plain image and the encrypted image, as well as between the decrypted and the encrypted image, by the use of a candidate key, as clarified in53. This is a computationally low-cost operation and an attractive method for cases where perfect key recovery is the key response, rather than approximating or identifying entropy-based fitness. Unlike statistical or component-wise similarity measures the goal of the encryption here is to identify the exact permutation key. Hamming distance follows bit-for-bit matching, whereas statistical methods used in piracy mitigation (i.e., entropy or correlation) promote estimates of matching keys. Though other fitness functions may offer certain advantages, the use of Hamming distance provides accuracy which is particularly important in permutation-only encryption schemes where even a minor mismatch results in complete decryption failure. This decisive nature makes Hamming distance a reliable and effective choice for assessing key correctness in the proposed system.

The crossover and mutation probabilities utilized in this work were chosen from the recommended ranges proposed in other studies54. The mutation probabilities generally range from 0.001 to 1, and probabilities for crossover range from 0.1 to 1. These parameters offer a balance between a high exploratory search (crossover) and an exploitation search (mutation) within the genetic algorithm. The use of full crossover allows strong genetic mixing and the mutation rate permits continued diversity in the population and prevents premature convergence. Future work may consider tuned or adapted values that help to achieve optimal performance.

Key testing and validation of QTRNG using NIST statistical test suites

To ensure the reliability and unpredictability of the generated quantum key, the output from the proposed Quantum True Random Number Generator (QTRNG) was rigorously tested and validated using two statistical test suites provided by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST): SP800-22 and SP800-90B. These tests serve as industry benchmarks to assess both the randomness and entropy quality of random number generators used in cryptographic systems.

NIST SP 800-22 (statistical test suite)

This test suite comprises 15 core tests and 2 sub-tests (Serial Test and Cumulative Sums Test), total 17 statistical tests. These tests evaluate the statistical randomness of binary sequences generated by a source. Each test computes a p-value and for a sequence to pass the p-value must be greater than or equal to 0.01. The tests assess various aspects of randomness including frequency, pattern complexity, linear complexity and periodic features55. All tests returned p-values > 0.01, indicating that the sequence passes the SP800-22 randomness criteria.

NIST 800-90B (entropy source evaluation)

This suite is designed to evaluate the entropy source that underlies a random number generator. It employs ten entropy estimators to measure the worst-case min-entropy, ensuring that the entropy source provides sufficient unpredictability. The tests include methods such as the T-Tuple test, compression test, Markov model prediction tests, and LZ78-based predictors. A raw data sequence of 100 Mb was collected from the QTRNG for this evaluation. The T-Tuple test, which estimates entropy based on tuple frequency, yielded a minimum entropy of 0.9263 per bit, confirming that even under worst-case conditions, the entropy source maintains randomness. As illustrated in Fig. 10, the QTRNG output probability distribution reflects a uniform and unbiased random behaviour. Figure 10a shows the SP800-22 test results, where all tests meet or exceed the required threshold (p-value ≥ 0.01), confirming high statistical randomness, and Fig. 10b shows the SP800-90B test results, where the min-entropy values across different estimators validate that the entropy source is robust and unpredictable.

Robustness against attacks

An effective image encryption method must resist multiple adversarial models, including statistical, brute-force, ciphertext-only, known-plaintext, chosen-plaintext, differential, and noise/data-loss attacks69. The proposed GA-based permutation combined with SIC-SM-NOMA was evaluated against each of these attack types.

Statistical attack

Natural images exhibit strong correlations among adjacent pixels, which can be exploited in statistical attacks. A secure encryption algorithm should eliminate these correlations to prevent leakage of structural information. The correlation coefficient, ranging from − 1 to 1, measures the dependency between two pixels, where 0 indicates no correlation. For the proposed system, the encrypted images showed near-zero correlations in all directions. For User 1 (Pepper), the horizontal correlation was − 0.0011, vertical − 0.0009, and diagonal 0.0003, while for User 2 (Cat), horizontal was − 0.0007, vertical − 0.0105, and diagonal − 0.0073. These results confirm that adjacent pixels in the cipher images reveal no statistical patterns, demonstrating strong resistance to statistical attacks.

Brute-force attack

The key space represents the set of all possible keys that an encryption algorithm can use. To ensure a stronger level of security against brute-force attacks, the key space of an image encryption algorithm should be larger than 210070. In the proposed system, a 128-bit master key was used, from which a 32-bit GA seed was derived by XORing 4-bit blocks of the master key. The key space, determined by the 128-bit master key, corresponds to 2128 possible keys, making exhaustive search attacks computationally infeasible with current technology. Additionally, the encrypted images exhibited very high cipher entropy, with User 1 (Pepper) having 7.9967 and User 2 (Cat) having 7.9957. Values near the ideal 8 indicate maximum randomness and a uniform pixel distribution, further strengthening resistance to brute-force attacks.

Ciphertext-only attack

Ciphertext-only attacks assume that the adversary has access only to the encrypted images71. The proposed system resists such attacks because of the near-zero correlation among adjacent pixels and high cipher entropy. These properties prevent meaningful information from being inferred from the ciphertext.

Known-plaintext attack

Even when some plaintext–ciphertext pairs are available, recovering the key or decrypting other ciphertexts is infeasible. This is because the GA-based permutation is key-dependent, and each encryption is uniquely tied to the 128-bit master key and the evolution of the GA, leaving no exploitable mapping for attackers.

Chosen-plaintext attack

Chosen-plaintext attacks allow an adversary to select specific plaintexts and obtain their corresponding ciphertexts72. Resistance is ensured by the nonlinear GA-based permutation in combination with the secret 128-bit master key. Experimental diffusion metrics further validate resistance, with NPCR values of 99.64% and 99.60% and UACI values of 33.30% and 33.40% for Users 1 and 2, respectively. These high values indicate that even minor changes in the plaintext lead to drastically different ciphertexts, preventing predictable mappings73.

Differential attack resistance

Differential attacks exploit relationships between small modifications in plaintext and the resulting changes in ciphertext. Robust encryption ensures that a single-pixel change propagates widely across the encrypted image. The proposed system demonstrates this quantitatively through NPCR and UACI metrics, confirming strong diffusion and resilience against differential attacks.

Noise and occulsion resistance

The robustness of the proposed system was evaluated by introducing Gaussian noise with zero mean and variance of 0.0004, salt-and-pepper noise with 5%, and 25% partial cropping. The results confirm that the encrypted images can be reliably decrypted even under noise or occlusion, demonstrating strong resilience against data corruption. Figure 16a shows the robustness of QGE2-encrypted images for User 1, and Fig. 16b shows the robustness for User 2 under noise and data-loss conditions. Furthermore, the obtained results show that even when encrypted images are affected by such attacks, successful decryption can still be achieved without significant perceptual distortion.

Quantum attack resistance

In the proposed system, the 128-bit key is derived from a quantum random number generator using quantum gates, making the key inherently unpredictable and suitable for post-quantum security. This design provides strong resilience against potential quantum attacks. Specifically, Grover’s algorithm, which accelerates brute-force searches, is still ineffective due to the large key space and dynamic GA-based permutation. Similarly, Shor’s algorithm, which targets number-theoretic cryptosystems, is irrelevant because the encryption does not rely on factorization or discrete logarithms. Combining quantum-generated keys with GA-based permutation ensures robust security while maintaining practical feasibility for real-world deployment.

Practical challenges

QTRNG implementation

The QTRNG (Quantum True Random Number Generator) module has been implemented with IBM’s Quantum platform which has cloud-based access to quantum processors, allowing key generation through real quantum circuits that utilize gates. There is no need for local quantum devices since IBM Quantum Platform supports simulated and real backends. In the proposed system, IBM Quantum Composer is used to design and run the quantum circuits, which are executed on the ibmbrisbane backend. However, if the system is implemented in real-time hardware, challenges such as high hardware cost74, integration complexity with classical systems75, network incompatibility, qubit decoherence and error correction demands76, high energy requirements for ultra-low-temperature maintenance2, rigidity and cost of entangled photon-based QKD systems, and lack of built-in authentication mechanisms would need to be addressed.

To generate 1,200,000 key bits, the system must perform 2,400,000 quantum gate operations and 1,200,000 measurements, which may introduce delays incompatible with the ultra-low latency requirements of 5G. Scaling the system is also difficult due to the high costs of quantum transmitters and receivers, estimated at approximately $26,935 for deployment and $6,528 for operational expenses, resulting in a total of roughly $33,463 per unit77.

Conclusion

This work presents a secure and spectrally efficient Spatial Modulation (SM)-assisted Non-Orthogonal Multiple Access (NOMA) system designed for two-user downlink. The system is built using 4-transmit and 4-receive antennas. However, unlike traditional MIMO-based NOMA, where all antennas are active simultaneously, the proposed SM-NOMA system activates only one transmit antenna per symbol duration, effectively reducing the need for multiple RF chains. In the proposed scheme, the input bitstream is split into two parts: one part selects the active transmit antenna, while the other part maps the data to a conventional modulation symbol (e.g., 4-QAM) for transmission through the selected antenna. This approach drastically reduces the hardware complexity by limiting the number of RF chains needed for transmission, resulting in a significant power saving and reduced system cost while maintaining high communication performance.

The Simulation results confirm that SM-NOMA significantly reduces BER, lowers outage probability, and improves spectral efficiency compared to conventional NOMA.At 25 dBm transmit power, User 1 and User 2 exhibit BER improvements of 83.6% and 96.6%, respectively. Outage probability is reduced by over 99.99% for User 1 and 99.87% for User 2 at 20 dBm. Spectral efficiency increases by 6.6%, from 2.57 bps/Hz to 2.74 bps/Hz.

Security of the information (image) is achieved through the integration of Quantum-Enhanced Genetic Encryption, a hybrid encryption method combining quantum gate-generated keys and genetic algorithm-based encryption. Utilizing it the image is encrypted and transmitted. The randomness of the key generated has been validated through NIST randomness test. Additional metrics such as entropy, Mean square error, correlation, PSNR and histogram analysis confirm the robustness.

Limitations and future work

The bottleneck to this work is the lack of real-time quantum hardware, GA convergence delays and constraints in SDR-based implementations. Future work will focus on deploying the full SM-NOMA SDR system with real-time quantum integration to validate the proposed framework’s practical adaptability and robustness in Sub-6 GHz 5G applications.
