Dynamic properties and microstructural characteristics of cement-coal gangue powder modified silty sand under freeze-thaw cycles
Authors: Chenkai Wang, Lixia Wang, Yunlong Zhang, Zilin Hu, Shuang Sun, Renpeng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The northeastern region of China, a typical seasonally frozen zone, contains silty sand subgrade that are subjected to long-term dynamic loads from vehicles and are highly susceptible to deformation and road distress, such as upheaval, under freeze–thaw (F–T) cycles. To enhance the dynamic properties and F–T resistance of silty sand subgrade, cement and coal gangue powder (CGP) were employed as modifiers. Through dynamic triaxial test and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) test, investigated the dynamic properties of modified silty sand with varying contents of cement and CGP, the dynamic behavior and micro-structural evolution under different F–T cycles, confining pressures, and loading cycles. As well as the correlation between micro-structural features and dynamic parameters was studied by grey relational analysis. The results revealed that the deviatoric stress amplitude of the modified silty sand increased significantly with higher cement content, while initially increased and then decreased with rising CGP content. Compared to silty sand, the modified soil maintained higher deviatoric stress amplitudes and dynamic elastic modulus across varying F–T cycles, loading cycles, and confining pressures. A significant correlation was identified between microstructural characteristics and dynamic parameters. F–T cycles caused considerable micro-structural degradation in silty sand, whereas the modified soil exhibited less structural variation. The incorporation of cement and CGP enhanced inter-particle cementation, filled pore spaces, and improved particle arrangement and pore distribution, thereby increasing structural stability and F–T resistance. Notably, the optimal ratio for cement–CGP modified silty sand was determined to be 2% cement and 8% CGP.

Introduction

The freeze–thaw (F–T) cycles have significant impact on the dynamic properties of soil, particularly in cold regions where engineering structures such as road subgrade and airport runways are subjected to long-term vehicular or other dynamic loads. The dynamic response of soil is crucial to engineering safety and stability1–3. Due to its fine particles and loose structure, silty sand exhibits poor frost resistance in natural state and is prone to strength attenuation, increased deformation, and structural failure under F–T cycles. These issues compromise the soil’s bearing capacity and long-term stability under dynamic loading4–7. Therefore, improving the mechanical properties and F–T resistance of silty sand is essential. Enhanced subgrade materials can not only reduce frost damage and prolong the service life of subgrade, but also lower maintenance costs.

Cement has long been demonstrated to significantly improve the strength and stability of soil8–12. Upon hydration, cement produces compounds that bond with soil particles to form a stable soil skeleton, thereby enhancing soil strength13,14. However, the improvement effect and economic efficiency are strongly influenced by the cement content. Excessive cement use may lead to increased soil brittleness15,16. In recent years, coal gangue power (CGP) has been gradually introduced as a novel solidification material for soil stabilization17. CGP, a by-product of coal mining, contains silicon–aluminum compounds that can react with cement hydration products in an alkaline environment to form secondary cementitious products. These products can further fill soil pores and enhance soil compactness and strength18–21. Furthermore, CGP modified soil have been shown to improve mechanical properties by enhancing particle bonding22. Notably, the combined use of cement and gangue powder yields superior results compared to using either material alone23. Given these advantages, increasing attention has been directed toward reinforcing cement-stabilized soil with CGP.

The dynamic triaxial test is a key method for evaluating the mechanical behavior of soil under dynamic loading and complex stress conditions. It is especially suitable for simulating the dynamic responses of soil under realistic engineering scenarios, such as traffic loads and seismic events24–27. Wang28 employed GDS dynamic triaxial test to study the cumulative axial strain of cement-stabilized silt subgrade at different depths under long-term cyclic loading. Zhao29 added sands with poor seismic properties to cement to improve dynamic properties. Zheng et al.30 explored the influence of F–T cycles on the dynamic strength of silty clay before and after cement stabilization under varying confining pressures in dynamic triaxial tests, concluded that cement modification significantly improved dynamic strength parameters before and after F–T cycling.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is a high-resolution technique based on the interaction of high-energy electron beams with material surfaces, which can directly reveal the microscopic morphology and structural characteristics of soil, modifiers, and their composite systems31–34. Ji et al.35 examined micro-structural damage evolution in mortar samples under F–T cycling and its quantitative relationship with mechanical degradation. Yuan et al.36 used SEM to analyze the porosity and micro-structure of silty soil subjected to varying F–T cycles and temperature ranges. Hang et al.37 found that micro-structural and statistical parameters, such as crack proportion, length, width, fractal dimension, and pore orientation, were related to the number of F–T cycles and reflected the mechanisms of crack propagation and failure. Zhao et al.38 applied grey correlation analysis to quantitatively evaluate the relationship between road properties and graphene oxide skeleton content.

However, most existing studies focus on the static properties of improved soil or the effects of a single F–T factor. Few studies have examined the dynamic response of soil under combined influences of confining pressure, vibration, and F–T cycles. Moreover, the quantitative correlation between macroscopic dynamic behavior and micro-structural changes remains inadequately understood—particularly the link between changes in microscopic parameters and the dynamic properties of natural and cement–CGP modified silty soil under F–T cycles.

In this study, the optimal content of cement–CGP silty sand (hereinafter referred to as “modified soil”) was determined through dynamic triaxial test of silty sand with varying cement and CGP contents. The variations in deviatoric stress amplitude, damping ratio, and dynamic elastic modulus of both silty sand and modified soil were investigated under different F–T cycles, confining pressures, and dynamic loading conditions. Micro-structural evolution was analyzed by using SEM. Based on grey relational analysis, the relationships between microscopic parameters (such as pore distribution and particle arrangement) and macroscopic dynamic characteristics were quantitatively assessed. The findings of this study can provide technical support and theoretical guidance for cold-region engineering applications such as roads, airport runways, and railway subgrades in selecting stabilizer ratios, optimizing freeze-thaw durability, and understanding the evolution mechanisms of microstructures. Furthermore, the structure parameterization method based on microscopic imaging is shown to be applicable for evaluating the freeze-thaw performance of other types of stabilized soil materials. Aims to provide an experimental basis for subgrade improvement strategies in seasonally frozen zones and offer a novel approach for the sustainable reuse of coal gangue resources.

Basic physical properties of silty sand.

As shown in Table 1; Fig. 4, the liquid limit and plastic limit of the silty sand are 21.5% and 14.1, respectively, the plasticity index is 7.4%, the optimum water content is 9.7%, and the maximum dry density is 1.84 g/cm³. Based on these parameters, the soil is classified as low liquid limit silt under the Unified Soil Classification System, in accordance with ASTM D2487-1741. The plasticity index is less than 10, the cumulative content of particles smaller than 0.01 mm and 0.005 mm are 4.3% and 2.1%, respectively. Indicating a very low clay content in silty sand, with relatively few active mineral components and limited production of hydration products upon contact with water. In natural state, the soil exhibits weak intergranular adhesion, loose structure, low shear strength, and low cohesion. When subjected to environmental factors such as freeze–thaw (F–T) cycles, the internal structure is easily damaged, leading to rapid degradation in strength and stability. Therefore, for low-cohesion silty sand, it is necessary to increase the quantity of hydration reaction products by adding external stabilizers to fill voids and enhance interparticle bonding, thereby improving mechanical properties and F–T resistance. Cement serves as the primary cementing agent to improve cohesion, while CGP functions as a fine-grained filler to occupy pore space between coarse particles, enhance gradation continuity, and strengthen the soil skeleton.

Sample preparation and test scheme

First, the raw soil was oven-dried and passed through a 2 mm sieve. Cement was then added at 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4% content by weight to prepare cement-stabilized samples with a height of 76 mm and a diameter of 38 mm (Fig. 5). According to the Chinese Specification of JTG E51-2019, the samples were wrapped in plastic film and placed in a curing room at a temperature of 20 ± 2 °C and relative humidity above 95% for 7 days. Dynamic triaxial test and SEM test

Dynamic triaxial test. The dynamic triaxial test was conducted using the GDS DYNTTS dynamic triaxial instrument imported from the United Kingdom (Fig. 9). This equipment is capable of simultaneously applying cyclic deviatoric stresses and allows independent control of the frequency, waveform, amplitude, and phase difference of the dynamic loading. Axial dynamic stress is applied via a servo motor, while confining pressure is controlled through adjustable air pressure. To ensure sample uniformity, remolded soil samples were used. After F–T cycles, the samples were subjected to unconsolidated undrained cyclic loading tests.Loading method. The dynamic stress waveform experienced in roadbeds under vehicle loading is generally similar to a half-sine wave44, Given that typical vehicle speeds range from 40 to 60 km/h, the axial dynamic load frequency was set to 1 Hz45,46. A stepwise loading method was employed: for natural silty soil, the deviatoric stress was increased by 30 kPa per stage, and for the improved soil, by 90 kPa per stage. Confining pressures were set at 20 kPa, 50 kPa, and 80 kPa, with vibration counts of 12, 20, and 30 cycles, respectively. Loading was terminated when the axial strain reached 5%.Microscopic analysis. A field emission scanning electron microscope (HITACHI SU8010) was used for micro-structural observations (Fig. 10). The samples were cut into 5 mm × 5 mm × 5 mm cubes, ensuring that one surface remained in its original undisturbed state. The samples were then dried and mounted on the microscope stage using conductive adhesive. After vacuum treatment and gold coating (Fig. 11), microscopic images were captured using the scanning electron microscope. Microscopic images at 100× magnification were selected to investigate the micro-structural features of the samples. Micro-structural parameters were extracted using Image-Pro Plus (IPP) software. Through both qualitative and quantitative analyses, the influence of cement and CGP on the micro-structural characteristics of modified soil under F–T cycles was examined. Furthermore, the internal relationship between the dynamic properties and microscopic parameters of modified soil was analyzed using grey correlation theory.

Test results and analysis

Analysis of the dynamic properties under different cement content and CGP content

Dynamic stress-strain curve

The stress-strain relationship curves of silty sand and modified soil are plotted in Fig. 12. As shown in Fig. 12a, the amplitude of deviatoric stress increases with increasing cement content, indicating that the strength of soil improves accordingly. With higher cement content, the samples gradually become stiffer, and failure occurs suddenly at the strain of approximately 1.1%~2.3%. When the cement content exceeds 2%, although the strength continues to in-crease, the cement–induced embrittlement of the micro-structure adversely affects the soil’s deformation properties. Therefore, cement content of 2% is considered optimal, balancing strength and deformability in silty sand.

Fig. 12Stress-strain relationship curve of silty sand and modified soil (loading frequency 1 Hz, confining pressure values 50 kPa): (a) different cement content and (b) different CGP content (2% cement content).

Stress-strain relationship curve of silty sand and modified soil (loading frequency 1 Hz, confining pressure values 50 kPa): (a) different cement content and (b) different CGP content (2% cement content).

Figure 12b illustrates that, under a constant cement content of 2%, the deviatoric stress amplitude reaches peak value when CGP content increases from 0% to 8%, indicating that 8% CGP provides the best skeletal filling and friction–enhancing effect. When CGP content increases to 12%, the deviatoric stress amplitude decreases, likely due to excessive CGP reducing soil cohesion and compromising overall strength. Consequently, a combination of 2% cement and 8% CGP is identified as optimal for improving both the strength and deformation capacity of soil. Furthermore, this mix makes effective use of CGP, helping to reduce waste accumulation pressure, lower the demand for natural resource extraction, minimize land occupation, and mitigate environmental impact–aligning with circular economy principles.

The cement primarily enhances inter-particle cohesion and serves as the main source of strength. A moderate amount of CGP acts as a skeletal material that optimizes particle arrangement and improves stress transmission efficiency. However, excessive CGP weakens the cementation and degrades the overall properties of soil. The optimal ratio–2% cement and 8% CGP–provides a practical balance for engineering applications. In practice, the selected dosage should be based on a comprehensive assessment of strength, ductility, and F–T durability. The issue of brittleness can be further addressed through toughening techniques, such as fiber reinforcement.

As Fig. 13a showed, the hysteresis loop of silty sand without cement is the widest, indicating that the soil exhibits high plastic deformation capacity and energy dissipation capability. However, compared with the modified soil, it also shows greater deformation characteristics. When the cement content is 2%, the width of the hysteresis loop of modified soil decreases significantly, indicating that cementation enhances the stiffness of modified soil. The addition of appropriate amount of cement is beneficial for improving the dynamic elastic modulus. When the cement content reaches 4%, the hysteresis loop becomes the narrowest, suggesting that excessive cement content leads to embrittlement of the soil, reducing both plastic deformation capacity and energy dissipation capability.

From Table 3 that with increasing cement content, the dynamic elastic modulus generally shows a fluctuating upward trend, indicating that cement incorporation enhances soil stiffness. However, when the cement content is 3%, the elastic modulus increases only slightly. This may be attributed to the changes in the interface characteristics between soil particles and the cementitious material, caused by incomplete cement hydration or uneven mixing of the cement slurry. These factors may lead to the formation of relatively weak inter-granular interfaces, making the soil more susceptible to local slip or failure under dynamic loading. At 4% cement content, although stiffness is further improved, the energy dissipation capacity is diminished, as reflected by the narrowed hysteresis loop. Additionally, the damping ratio of the modified soil shows a gradual decreasing trend with increasing cement content, indicating that the addition of cement reduces the energy dissipation ability of soil.

As shown in Fig. 13b, the hysteresis loop of silty soil without CGP is relatively narrow, indicating limited energy dissipation capacity. With increasing CGP content, the width of the hysteresis loop gradually expands, demonstrating that the addition of an appropriate amount of CGP enhances both the plastic deformation capacity and the energy dissipation ability of modified soil. When the CGP content reaches 12%, the hysteresis loop narrows again, suggesting that excessive CGP leads to a looser internal structure, thereby reducing stiffness and energy dissipation properties. As the CGP content increases from 0% to 8%, the dynamic elastic modulus shows upward trend, while, at 12%, decreases slightly, indicating that excessive CGP has limited effectiveness in further enhancing soil stiffness.

From Table 3 that when the CGP content increases from 0 to 12%, the damping ratio of modified soil decreases more slowly, indicating that a moderate amount of CGP is beneficial for improving energy dissipation capacity. However, when the CGP content reaches 12%, the damping ratio does not show significant improvement.

In summary, as cement content increases, the dynamic elastic modulus of modified soil can be significantly enhanced, though this is accompanied by a reduction in damping ratio. CGP effectively improves the dynamic properties of soil within the range of 4~8%, but excessive addition weakens the structural integrity and energy dissipation capacity. Therefore, the combination of 2% cement and 8% CGP is considered the optimal ratio for enhancing the dynamic properties of silty sand.

Analysis of dynamic properties under different F-T cycles, vibrations and confining pressure

The relationship between the stress-strain curve and F-T cycles

The stress-strain curves of silty sand and modified soil under different F-T cycles are plotted in Fig. 14. From Fig. 14a, the deviatoric stress amplitude of silty sand reaches approximately 400 kPa under the confining pressure of 50 kPa, 20 loading cycles, and 0 cycles. However, as F–T cycles increases, the deviatoric stress amplitude gradually decreases – dropping to about 250 kPa after 8 cycles – representing a reduction of over 30%. This decline is mainly attributed to the weakening of cohesion between soil particles and the damage to the internal structure caused by repeated F–T cycles. Meanwhile, the axial strain of the silty sand increases progressively with more F–T cycles, and the stress–strain curve becomes flatter, indicating reduced stiffness and increased plastic deformation. The strength and stiffness of silty sand deteriorate significantly after F–T, particularly between 6 ~ 8 cycles.

In contrast, the modified soil exhibits pronounced strain-hardening behavior with increasing confining pressure, particularly under high confining pressures, where dynamic strength and deformation resistance are significantly better than those of silty sand.

From Fig. 16a that with increasing vibration count, the deviatoric stress amplitude gradually decreases. This trend suggests an increase in cumulative deformation under dynamic loading. The modified soil maintains a more stable structure, demonstrating strong fatigue resistance under dynamic loading, making it suitable for applications such as subgrade and other infrastructure subjected to cyclic dynamic loads.

Failure modes

Dynamic loads were applied incrementally under identical test conditions. When the applied dynamic stress was relatively low, the samples remained in a compacted state without failure, and the stress–strain curves remained stable. Once the applied dynamic stress reached the critical level, and with an increasing number of loading cycles, the strain increased rapidly. The failure modes of silty sand and modified soil are shown in Fig. 17.

Fig. 17Failure morphology: (a) silty sand and (b) modified soil.

Failure morphology: (a) silty sand and (b) modified soil.

The primary failure cracks in both silty sand and modified soil appeared at an angle of approximately 45 degrees. In silty sand samples, only minor surface cracks were observed, and the overall structure remained relatively intact without prominent fractures. This is attributed to the absence of internal cementation in silty sand, where strength mainly depends on friction and interlocking between particles. Consequently, the mechanical properties is weak, and due to the lack of rigid cementing materials, crack propagation is relatively slow, leading to a failure mode dominated by plastic deformation. In contrast, modified soil samples displayed distinct vertical cracks running through the entire sample, with visible detachment at the base. This is because the incorporation of cement and CGP significantly increased the sample’s overall strength, making it stronger than silty sand but also more brittle. The cementitious structure formed by hydration products causes internal stress concentrations, which result in the development of larger cracks and a relatively brittle fracture mode upon failure.

Micro-structure analysis

Qualitative analysis of the enhancement mechanism

To intuitively reveal the enhancement mechanism of cement and CGP, changes in the internal structure of silty sand and modified soil under different F–T cycles were qualitatively analyzed at the micro-structural level. SEM images of silty sand and modified soil subjected to 0, 4, and 8 F–T cycles at 100× magnification are shown in Fig. 18.

As shown in Fig. 18a, the silty sand without F–T cycling exhibits good particle integrity, smooth edges, relatively compact pore structure, stable internal structure, and strong inter-particle bonding, indicating that silty sand possesses a better physical structure when without F–T cycles. In Fig. 18b, after 4 cycles, particle fragmentation begins and porosity increases. Due to the F-T induced expansion and contraction of water, soil particles disintegrate, pores expand, and the structure becomes looser. At this stage, the shear strength and bearing capacity of silty sand begin to decline. Figure 18c shows that after 8 cycles, the fragmentation is more pronounced, pores have significantly increased, and the structure is visibly looser. This demonstrates that repeated F–T cycles damage the physical structure of silty sand, cause particle rearrangement, increase porosity, and substantially degrade dynamic properties.

Scanning electron microscopy images under different F-T cycles: (a) silty sand with 0 cycles, (b) silty sand with 4 cycles, (c) silty sand with 8 cycles, (d) modified soil with 0 cycles, (e) modified soil with 4 cycles and (f) modified soil with 8 cycles.

From Fig. 18d–f that the silty sand mixed with cement and CGP exhibits good particle integrity prior to undergoing any F–T cycles. This is attributed to the cementing effect of cement, which strengthens the inter-particle bonding and reduces pore size. Without F–T cycling, the hydration reaction of the cement generates cementitious products such as calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) and calcium aluminate hydrate (C–A–H), which bind soil particles together to form a cement–gangue–silty sand skeleton structure, as illustrated in the Fig. 18d, thereby enhancing the overall stability of soil. As shown in Fig. 18e, after 4 cycles, the modified soil exhibits initial particle breakage, however, the degree of fragmentation is noticeably less than that observed in silty sand. This is because CGP can absorb a portion of water during F–T cycling, thereby reducing the direct damage caused by water expansion. At this stage, although pore volume increases slightly, the overall structure remains relatively stable due to the gelation effect of cement. In Fig. 18f, after 8 cycles, the modified soil shows more severe structural damage. Nevertheless, the increase in porosity remains lower than that observed in silty sand sample. Because cement bonding function continues to inhibit further structural disintegration. While the hydration products of cement may undergo some degradation – resulting in the reduction in soil strength – the structure remains more stable compared to the loosened structure of silty sand.

Silty sand is mainly composed of primary mineral particles, during F–T cycles, the expansion and contraction of pore water directly disrupt the particle arrangement and increase porosity. After repeated F–T cycles, the soil structure becomes increasingly loose, and the natural hydration compounds present in the soil have very limited capacity to enhance structural integrity, rendering the soil highly susceptible to F–T damage. In contrast, in modified soil, although water introduced during F–T cycles can affect the cement hydration products, the gelation effect of cement continues to support structural stability to a certain extent. Moreover, the active oxides in CGP can undergo secondary reactions with the cement hydration products under alkaline conditions, forming flocculent structures that further enhance soil cohesion. In addition, CGP also contributes to water absorption, thereby reducing the swelling effect of water and mitigating F–T induced structural damage in soil.

According to Tables 5 and 6, the correlation between the microscopic parameters and dynamic properties (deviatoric stress amplitude, dynamic elastic modulus, and damping ratio) of soil can be analyzed. The average particle size and pore size of silty sand gradually decreased with an increasing number of F–T cycles, indicating that F–T cycles disrupted the particle arrangement and lead to particle fragmentation. The anisotropy rate of particles and pores increased with more F–T cycles, demonstrating that F-T caused disordered particle orientation and increased heterogeneity in pore distribution. Likewise, the orientation probability entropy of particles and pores increased progressively, indicating damage to particle cohesion and pore structure stability. In contrast, the average particle size and pore size in modified soil were less affected by F–T cycling, suggesting that the addition of cement and CGP significantly slowed F–T degradation. The increase in particle and pore anisotropy is also markedly lower than that observed in silty sand, indicating greater stability in particle arrangement and pore distribution in modified soil. Similarly, the rise in directional probability entropy of particles and pores is smaller, reflecting improved cohesion and structural stability due to cement and CGP, thus enhancing F–T resistance.

The damping ratio of silty sand showed a strong correlation with average particle and pore size, indicating that these are key factors influencing energy dissipation. For modified soil, the correlation between dynamic elastic modulus and particle and pore anisotropy rates was 0.91 and 0.84, respectively–significantly higher than in silty sand–demonstrating that the strength was effectively enhanced through optimization of the micro-structure.

The correlation between deviatoric stress amplitude and anisotropy rate in silty sand is weak, indicating that F–T cycles severely impair its stiffness and that its micro-structure is difficult to maintain. In contrast, the dynamic elastic modulus of modified soil exhibited strong correlation with particle anisotropy, confirming that cement and CGP significantly improve stiffness and deformation resistance. Moreover, the damping ratio in silty sand is highly correlated with the orientation probability entropy of pores and particles, showing that more disordered and complex structures enhance energy dissipation.

Overall, F–T cycles disrupt particle orientation and structural stability in silty sand, resulting in significant reductions in deviatoric stress amplitude and dynamic elastic modulus, while increased pore complexity enhances damping. Cement and CGP strengthen particle bonding and fill pore spaces, optimizing particle arrangement and pore distribution, and thus improving micro-structural stability. Silty sand degrades significantly under F–T cycles and is not suitable for engineering applications in regions with frequent F-T. However, the modified soil–with enhanced cohesion and optimized micro-structure–exhibits significantly greater deviatoric stress amplitude and dynamic elastic modulus, and reduced degradation in dynamic properties under F–T conditions, making it well-suited for subgrade applications subject to cyclic dynamic loading.
