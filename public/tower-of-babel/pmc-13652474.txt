Heterotopic pancreas as a rare lead point of ileal intussusception mimicking acute appendicitis in a child: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Taha Negahdari, Sohrab Sharifi, Mahdokht Azizi, Sepehr Moradi, Ava Khalili, Ardavan Shahbazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Heterotopic pancreas is an uncommon developmental anomaly characterized by pancreatic tissue located outside the normal pancreas without anatomical or vascular continuity. Although it is most frequently identified in the stomach and duodenum, involvement of the ileum is rare, particularly in children. When present in the small intestine, it may act as a pathological lead point for intussusception, posing a considerable diagnostic challenge because the underlying lesion is often not identified by preoperative imaging.Presentation of case:We report the case of a 6-year-old Iranian boy who presented with clinical features highly suggestive of acute appendicitis. Initial ultrasonography and contrast-enhanced computed tomography did not demonstrate appendicitis, intussusception, or a definite pathological lead point. Despite negative imaging, persistent right lower quadrant tenderness, fever, leukocytosis, and progressive clinical findings prompted an exploratory laparotomy. Intraoperatively, a normal appendix and an ileo-ileal intussusception approximately 30 cm proximal to the ileocecal valve were identified. Manual reduction was unsuccessful, necessitating segmental ileal resection with primary anastomosis. Gross examination demonstrated a 3.6 × 4.0 × 5.5 cm submucosal mass protruding into the intestinal lumen and acting as the pathological lead point. Histopathological evaluation confirmed Heinrich type II heterotopic pancreas composed of pancreatic acini and ducts without identifiable islets of Langerhans, dysplasia, or malignancy, accompanied by transmural ischemic changes secondary to intussusception. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient remained asymptomatic throughout follow-up.Clinical discussion:Heterotopic pancreas is an exceptionally rare pathological lead point for pediatric ileo-ileal intussusception and may clinically mimic more common surgical conditions, such as acute appendicitis. Although ultrasonography is highly sensitive for detecting intussusception, characterization of the underlying lead point remains challenging, and histopathological examination is essential for definitive diagnosis. Early surgical exploration should be considered when persistent clinical suspicion outweighs nondiagnostic imaging findings.Conclusion:Ileal heterotopic pancreas should be considered in the differential diagnosis of older children presenting with atypical or recurrent intussusception, particularly when clinical findings are discordant with imaging studies. Prompt surgical management and histopathological evaluation are crucial for establishing the diagnosis and achieving excellent clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Ectopic pancreas, also referred to as pancreatic heterotopia or aberrant pancreas, is defined as the presence of pancreatic tissue located outside its normal anatomical site and lacking any structural or vascular continuity with the native pancreas[1]. Less frequent locations include Meckel’s diverticulum, ileum, hepatobiliary system, spleen, mesentery, omentum, and even extra-abdominal organs[3]. The exact pathogenesis is not fully understood; however, the most widely accepted explanation is the “misplacement” or “dislocation” theory, which suggests that fragments of pancreatic tissue become separated during embryologic foregut rotation and are deposited along the developing gastrointestinal tract[3].

Most cases remain asymptomatic and are discovered incidentally during imaging, surgery, or histopathological examination. When symptoms occur, they are usually related to complications such as inflammation, bleeding, obstruction, or, rarely, malignant transformation[4,5]. In the small intestine, heterotopic pancreas may act as a pathological lead point for intussusception, although this presentation is distinctly uncommon, particularly in pediatric patients.

Because clinical manifestations are nonspecific and radiologic findings are often inconclusive, preoperative diagnosis is challenging, and definitive identification typically relies on histopathological evaluation of the resected specimen. Management strategies depend on symptomatology and associated complications, with surgical resection generally indicated in symptomatic cases or when complications such as obstruction or intussusception occur[6].

Heterotopic pancreas causing ileal intussusception in children is exceedingly rare and may clinically mimic more common causes of right-lower-quadrant (RLQ) pain, including acute appendicitis, leading to diagnostic uncertainty and unexpected intraoperative findings. This case has been reported in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guidelines[7].

Case presentation

A 6-year-old Iranian boy with no significant past medical history, past surgical history, medication use, or relevant family history presented to the Emergency Department in June 2025, accompanied by his parents. He had severe abdominal pain, non-bilious vomiting, nausea, and decreased appetite. The pain began in the periumbilical region and later localized to the RLQ. Physical examination findings were highly suggestive of acute appendicitis and demonstrated marked tenderness at McBurney’s point, along with positive psoas, Rovsing, and obturator signs.

Further history revealed intermittent episodes of mild abdominal pain over the previous 2 years, occurring approximately one to three times per month. Laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis, with a white blood cell count of 18 000/µL, as the only significant abnormality.

Abdominopelvic ultrasonography demonstrated a small amount of free fluid in the RLQ without visualization of an inflamed appendix, intussusception, or a definite pathological lead point. Subsequently, contrast-enhanced abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) with intravenous contrast demonstrated no radiologic evidence of acute appendicitis, bowel obstruction, or an intra-abdominal mass. No definite intussusception or pathological lead point was identified. Following the intraoperative diagnosis, retrospective review of the imaging suggested that the negative findings were most likely related to transient or intermittent intussusception at the time of imaging, together with technical limitations in visualizing the small-bowel lesion.

Despite these imaging findings, the patient met criteria for systemic inflammatory response syndrome, including tachypnea, fever (38.6°C), and leukocytosis (18 000/µL). Serial clinical examinations consistently demonstrated persistent localized RLQ tenderness with positive appendiceal signs despite the absence of definitive radiologic findings. Given the progressive clinical presentation, persistent localized peritoneal irritation, and ongoing concern for an underlying surgical abdomen, consultation with the pediatric surgery team was obtained, and urgent operative intervention was recommended.

Intraoperatively, the appendix appeared grossly normal with no evidence of inflammation; however, an appendectomy was performed due to the surgical approach. Exploration was then continued from the ileocecal valve (ICV) toward the proximal ileum (Fig. 1A). Approximately 30 cm proximal to the ICV, an ileo-ileal intussusception was identified (Fig. 1B). Gentle manual reduction (“milking”) was attempted but was unsuccessful. Consequently, segmental resection of approximately 12 cm of the involved ileal segment was performed, followed by a primary end-to-end hand-sewn ileo-ileal anastomosis. Examination of the resected specimen revealed a submucosal mass protruding into the intestinal lumen and acting as a pathological lead point. The mass measured 3.6 × 4.0 × 5.5 cm, and the mucosal layer overlying the lesion demonstrated complete surface necrosis secondary to ischemia caused by the intussusception (Fig. 2 and Supplemental Digital Content Video 1, available at: https://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A145). Histopathological evaluation showed a well-circumscribed focus of heterotopic pancreas located within the bowel wall, predominantly involving the submucosa (Fig. 3A–C). The acinar cells displayed abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm without cytological atypia, dysplasia, or malignancy. The adjacent intestinal wall exhibited extensive transmural ischemic necrosis with hemorrhage, vascular congestion, and inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 3D), consistent with ischemic injury secondary to intussusception. According to the Heinrich classification, the lesion was classified as Type II, composed of pancreatic acini and ducts without identifiable islets of Langerhans. He was discharged in good general condition on postoperative day 5. No postoperative fever, leukocytosis, wound infection, anastomotic complications, or other adverse events were observed.

Follow-up evaluations at 1, 3, and 5 months demonstrated complete clinical recovery, with the patient remaining asymptomatic and showing an excellent response to treatment.

Discussion

Heterotopic pancreas involving the ileum is an uncommon entity, particularly in the pediatric population, and only rarely serves as a pathological lead point for intussusception. While heterotopic pancreatic tissue is most frequently encountered in the stomach and duodenum, distal small-bowel involvement has been reported only sporadically, underscoring the unusual nature of this presentation[8]. Reporting an ileal location is particularly valuable because, unlike the more common gastric or duodenal heterotopias, an ileal lesion can serve as a small-bowel lead point and present with intussusception rather than being discovered incidentally.

The mechanism by which heterotopic pancreas induces intussusception is mechanical rather than inflammatory. Submucosal pancreatic tissue forms an intraluminal protrusion that disrupts normal peristalsis, thereby predisposing the affected bowel segment to telescoping into the adjacent intestine[9].

Reported pediatric cases of heterotopic pancreas in the literature.

A review of previously reported pediatric cases (Table 1) demonstrates that most patients are infants or toddlers, with the majority younger than 2 years of age[10,11,14,15,21,24]. Lesions are typically small, ranging from approximately 0.9 to 3 cm, and are usually discovered only after surgical intervention[12,13,16–20,22,23]. Preoperative recognition of heterotopic pancreas is exceedingly rare because although ultrasonography has high sensitivity for detecting pediatric intussusception, it frequently fails to identify the underlying pathological lead point, particularly when lesions are submucosal or when bowel gas limits adequate visualization. Likewise, contrast-enhanced CT can confirm bowel abnormalities but may still fail to characterize small intramural lesions or intermittent intussusception responsible for transient imaging findings[25,26]. In this case, despite its 5.5 cm size, the submucosal lesion likely remained occult because its attenuation resembled the adjacent bowel wall; collapsed or overlapping loops and transient reduction of the intussusception also prevented a discrete mass or target configuration from being recognized. Compared with previously reported cases, our patient was unusual because he was school-aged, the relatively large lesion measuring 5.5 cm, recurrent abdominal pain over 2 years, and an initial presentation closely mimicking acute appendicitis rather than the more typical presentation of pediatric intussusception.

Symptomatic heterotopic pancreas commonly presents with nonspecific gastrointestinal manifestations, including abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, gastrointestinal bleeding, bowel obstruction, or, less frequently, intussusception[27]. In our patient, the predominance of RLQ pain, fever, leukocytosis, and localized peritoneal irritation strongly favored a clinical diagnosis of acute appendicitis despite nondiagnostic imaging findings. However, the history of recurrent intermittent abdominal pain over the preceding 2 years and the eventual identification of a pathological lead point favored an underlying small-bowel pathology rather than primary appendiceal disease.

Whereas most cases occurring during infancy are idiopathic, intussusception in older children is considerably more likely to be associated with a pathological lead point. Although heterotopic pancreas is an exceptionally uncommon cause, it should be considered in the differential diagnosis of unexplained ileal intussusception, particularly in patients with atypical symptoms, recurrent abdominal pain, or discordance between clinical findings and imaging results.

Surgical resection remains the definitive treatment for symptomatic heterotopic pancreas and is both diagnostic and curative. Consistent with previous reports, our patient experienced complete resolution of symptoms following segmental bowel resection, without postoperative complications or evidence of recurrence during the available follow-up period.

The present report has several limitations. First, the follow-up period was limited to 5 months, reflecting the most recent postoperative assessment available at the time of manuscript preparation. Although no recurrence or late complications were observed during this interval, longer follow-up would provide additional reassurance regarding long-term outcomes after resection of heterotopic pancreas.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case emphasizes that heterotopic pancreas, although exceedingly rare, should be considered a potential pathological lead point in older children presenting with atypical or recurrent intussusception, particularly when the clinical presentation is discordant with preoperative imaging findings. Increased awareness of this rare entity may facilitate earlier recognition, appropriate surgical management, and improved patient outcomes.
