Land conversion from forest to agriculture redistributes but does not reduce soil organic carbon stores in northeastern Ontario, Canada
Authors: Bayze Murray, Amanda Diochon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change is creating conditions for agriculture to expand northward. For conventional large-scale operations, this will require the conversion of forests to agricultural land in most areas, which has historically been associated with large (30%) losses of soil organic carbon (SOC) and soil degradation. To potentially mitigate these losses, farmers in Northern Ontario, Canada, are incorporating woody residues into their soils during conversion to retain more organic matter on-site, while bringing the land into production faster than conventional conversion approaches. The current study examines the effects of converting forest to agricultural land on soil by comparing SOC storage in the top 45 cm of mineral soil in forests, old (> 50 year) and young (< 10y) conventionally cleared fields, and young fields with mulched forest biomass incorporated into the soil following land clearing in the Great Clay Belt, northeastern Ontario, Canada. There were no statistically significant effects (p > 0.05) of land conversion on SOC storage but there was a redistribution of SOC with depth consistent with soil tillage. Observed trends in mean SOC storage suggest that incorporating woody mulch residues into the soil at the time of conversion may warrant further study as a SOC conservation strategy.Keywords: Soil organic carbon, Land use change, Land conversion, Mulch, Northern soils

Introduction

Climate change is expected to expand the agricultural frontier in Canada northward [1]. Based on growing degree days, models have projected roughly 55–89% of the boreal region could be crop feasible by 2099, reflected in a 1200 km northward migration of the agricultural frontier [1]. As a result, areas such as the Great Clay Belt in northeastern Ontario, which is at the southern limit of the boreal region, are experiencing agricultural expansion [2]. Expanding industrial agriculture (i.e., large scale production systems) in this region will inherently require the conversion of land from forest to agriculture [2]. Conversion of forest to agriculture has historically resulted in rapid losses of soil organic carbon (SOC), before establishing a new equilibrium [3, 4]. Depending on land management practices, SOC will begin to recover towards its pre-disturbance baseline, often taking decades to centuries [4].

Soil organic carbon (SOC) is the largest terrestrial pool of organic carbon on the planet, and the management of SOC is closely tied to atmospheric CO2 concentrations [5, 6]. Increasing the quantity of SOC in soils could provide a medium-term countermeasure to help mitigate increasing levels of CO2 in the atmosphere that result from the burning of fossil fuels and land clearing [7]. This can be achieved by implementing land management practises that preserve and build SOC over time. In fact, cropland soils have a high potential for increasing SOC with better land management and could, feasibly, remove between 0.79 and 1.54 Gt Cyr− 1 [8]. In addition to its potential as a climate change mitigation strategy, increasing SOC has also proven to have important benefits to soil health including; soil structure, water retention, and nutrient supply [8, 9].

In boreal regions, SOC stocks decreased by roughly 31% following land conversion from forest to agriculture [3]. SOC stocks are expected to decline rapidly following conversion, followed by a slow increase with time [3]. This rapid initial decrease can be attributed to many aspects of the land conversion process. The conventional clearing of forest to agricultural land involves removing trees, brush, roots, and other debris from an area to create a land surface that can be easily and effectively managed and reworked by farmers and machinery. Modern land clearing utilizes machinery such as a crawler tractor or bulldozer, equipped with a brush cutter, brush piler, and breaking plough [10]. Brush is cut and debris is either piled and burned or left on the land for two years before being burned [10]. Though bulldozers are an effective clearing method, the blade often removes topsoil as it passes across the landscape and incorporates it into the piles. Noted for making the piles more difficult to burn, the removal of topsoil also takes away this valuable pool of nutrients that is high in organic matter [2, 10]. Further, the removal of the forest canopy during conversion causes an increase in soil temperature and changes soil moisture content, accelerating the decomposition of soil organic matter (SOM) and liberating C and nutrients [2]. A proportion of SOC is also occluded within aggregates that provide protection from microbial decomposition [3]. The conversion to agricultural land often disrupts soil aggregates, especially with heavy tillage [3], making SOC more readily available for the microbial community to decompose, and creating emissions.

Though declines in SOC are generally expected following land conversion, there are several environmental factors that moderate the observed changes. Soil texture, clay content, and hydrology all regulate microbial access to organic substrates [11–13]. Fine textured soils with higher clay contents typically exhibit a stronger capacity to buffer SOC losses [11, 13] and promote SOC stabilization because they promote mineral associations with organic matter and limit oxygen diffusion. The occlusion of particulate associated organic matter (POM) into microaggregates protects it from decomposition by the microbial community [11, 13]. In contrast, coarse-textured soils tend to show more rapid SOM decomposition and SOC turnover because they have lower reactive mineral surface areas (i.e., lower proportions of fine silt and clay) and a weaker capacity for aggregation, which reduces the potential for chemical sorption and physical protection [3, 11]. Hydrology further influences these patterns by controlling oxygen availability. Decomposition is slowed during periods of flooding when conditions are anoxic, and accelerates under well drained aerobic conditions [12], which can affect the rate of SOC turnover.

Recognizing that land conversion can result in losses of SOM/SOC, some farmers in Northern Ontario, Canada, have been incorporating woody residues into their soils during the conversion process. Mulchers are used to grind up logs, stumps, and branches into smaller particles and subsoilers incorporate these materials into the soil. This reduces the size and increases the surface area of residues, which helps aid decomposition and brings the residues in closer contact with the soil. The size of the wood residues is not uniform across all sites due to the use of different machinery by different land managers. This process is intended to retain more organic matter on site, but it also creates a field that can be cultivated immediately following clearing. Elsewhere, mulch has been widely used as a water conservation practice, particularly in more arid climates, where it preserves soil moisture, regulates soil temperature, and limits evaporation [14]. Despite the potential benefits, there is little research surrounding the incorporation of mulch into the soil in temperate and northern regions. The incorporated wood residues initially contribute to the particulate associated organic matter (POM) pool, and their coarse texture and high lignin content would slow decomposition and act as temporary physical protection [11, 15]. As decomposition progresses, microbial processing and fragmentation generate finer organic compounds capable of forming organo-mineral associations with clay minerals and Fe/Al oxides, which supports long-term stabilization as mineral-associated SOC [11, 16]. Fontana et al. [17] found that a single application of incorporated ramial woodchips increased the mineral associated SOC fraction by 10% and improved available water capacity. Other studies have found the addition of wood residues widened the C: N ratio and caused short-term nitrogen immobilization [18]. The C: N ratio and lignin content of SOM can be an indicator of the expected decomposition rate of that material [19]. The average C: N ratio of wood residues is around 500:1 [20] meaning the micro-organisms that decompose wood often run into N limitations, causing them to scavenge from their environment and immobilize N that would otherwise be taken up by plants or other microorganisms. Limitations in available N could have potential implications for crop yield during these periods.

The objective of this study was to determine (1) if the effect of land conversion from forest to agriculture on soil organic carbon (SOC) storage at the southern limit of the boreal forest results in significant declines in SOC storage, and (2) if mulching mitigates SOC loss. It is expected that conversion will result in a rapid decline in SOC storage after conversion from forest to agriculture, and that stores recover over time. We investigate the use of mulch as a soil amendment after land-clearing, and assess its viability as a SOC conservation practice, hypothesizing that it will mitigate SOC losses.

Materials and methods

Site characteristics

This study was conducted in the New Liskeard (47.5037° N, 79.6979° W) – Englehart (47.8242° N, 79.8738° W) area, located in northeastern Ontario, Canada (Fig. 1). The area is situated on the Great Clay Belt (GCB), a roughly 125,000 km2 area that straddles the Ontario – Quebec provincial border [21] and is historically known for its potentially fertile glaciolacustrine and morainal calcareous clays and silts that make up approximately 66% of the area [10, 22]. According to the Canadian System of Soil Classification [23], soils in the area are generally Luvisolic in uplands to midlands, Gleysolic in midlands, and Mesisolic in depressions, and lowland areas [24], which corresponds to Luvisols, Gleysols, and Histosols respectively in the FAO soil classification system [25].

Experimental treatments

The four treatments in this study included: young (< 10y C) conventionally cleared fields; conventionally cleared fields established for at least 50 years (> 50y C), young (< 10y) mulched fields (M), and adjacent forests (F). The age bins correspond to those of Wei et al. [3] and because mulching is a relatively new practice, there were no sites identified in the > 10y age class. Ten fields were identified for each treatment class by working with local farmers who had cleared land in the last ten years and were willing to participate in the study. Farmers identified forest sites adjacent to sampled agricultural fields that were similar to the pre-clearance condition. Soil maps were used to confirm similar soil types between forests and agricultural fields, and observations of forest type and age were cross-referenced with provincial forest inventory data. All forests were deciduous (Populus tremuloides Michx.) dominated secondary mixed-woods (Abies balsamea (L.) Mill.) with an average age of 71 years. None of the forests were pristine (i.e., old-growth with no evidence of human disturbance). All fields are owned by grain and oilseed producers and all are tile drained. Crop rotations include barley (Hordeum vulgare), canola (Brassica napus), soybean (Glycine max), and maize (Zea mays) that were fertilized at recommended rates based on soil testing. We aimed to achieve a paired field-forest design but there was not always a forest representative of the pre-clearance condition (forest age or soil type) for the agricultural field. Two of the < 10y and two of the > 50y conventionally cleared fields are located 15–25 km from a representative forest. These fields were owned by an established producer in the region that has been clearing land, and volunteered to participate in this study. We acknowledge that this is a limitation of the design and that it could influence the results, but the history of the fields was well known and there were no comparable nearby forests.

Soil sampling

Soil samples were collected from three representative areas within a field at 10-meter intervals along a south-to-north transect. An Eijkelkamp Edelman auger was used to collect samples from the 0–5 cm, 5–15 cm, 15–30 cm, and 30–45 cm depth intervals. Samples from each depth interval were composited by site and stored cool in polyethylene bags for transport to the lab for further processing. At each site bulk density samples were collected from the least altered face of a soil pit using 90.59 cm3 metal sampling rings [26]. Bulk density samples were also stored in polyethylene bags. For forests, mineral soil sampling was the same as for the fields but the organic/litter layer was first removed within a 15 × 15 cm quadrat, and collected in a polyethylene bag as per [27].There was only a thin layer (< 1.0 cm) of plant litter present in the forested sites, if at all.When present, its depth was recorded for each of the four corners and midpoints of the sides to calculate volume. All samples were transported cool to Lakehead University where they were stored at 4 °C prior to processing.

Laboratory analysis

Bulk density and soil moisture were determined for each site for the 0–5 cm, 5–15 cm, 15–30 cm, and 30–45 cm intervals by first oven drying samples at 105 °C until there was no change in mass. The samples were passed through a 2 mm sieve and the weights of the < 2 mm fraction were used to calculate the bulk density of the soil, while weight of the > 2 mm fraction was used to correct for volume assuming a density of 2.65 g cm− 3 . Bulk density was then calculated as the oven-dried < 2 mm mass divided by the field volume of the sample, corrected for coarse fragments, and soil moisture calculated as percentage of the dry soil weight [26, 27].

Organic C contents in soil samples were determined following the recommendations of Carter and Gregorich. [27]. All samples were air-dried in open aluminum trays on a bread rack to allow for adequate air flow. Once dried, samples were gently crushed using a rolling pin before passing the soil through a 2 mm sieve. Any organic debris, including wood fragments, that were greater than 2 mm were not considered in the determination of organic carbon and total nitrogen because our observations during processing did not warrant this. Visually there was minimal visible woody debris, and differences between sites and treatments were indistinguishable at the time of processing. We recognize this as a limitation of the study, but SOC determination in the mineral soil is generally based on the < 2 mm fraction. Soil passing through the sieve was then pulverized for three minutes using a SPEX 8000d tungsten carbide ball mill. Pulverized samples were passed through a 253 μm sieve to homogenize the sample before analysis for total C and N using high-temperature dry combustion on a CHNS [Cl] Elementar vario EL in the Lakehead University Instrumentation Laboratory. Subsamples were acidified using sulfurous acid (H2SO3) to remove inorganic forms of carbon and determine organic carbon content. The reducing properties of sulfurous acid minimize the oxidation of organic carbon during this process compared to alternative acids [27]. Total organic C (acidified samples) concentrations were also determined using high-temperature dry combustion on a CHNS [Cl] Elementar vario EL in the Lakehead University Instrumentation Laboratory.

Data analysis: equivalent soil mass and statistics

When there is variation in soil bulk density, the use of fixed depth intervals can introduce error and lead to inaccurate conclusions regarding changes in SOC stocks [28]. The result is an overestimation of SOC stores in soils with high bulk density, and an underestimation of SOC stores in soils with low bulk density [28]. The mass of soil at a particular depth will vary based on bulk density, whereas the mass of soil in a given soil mass layer is fixed providing a consistent basis for comparison [28, 29]. To accurately quantify changes in SOC stocks across different land management practices, equivalent soil mass procedures can be used to eliminate the error associated with fixed depth sampling [28]. Equivalent soil mass corrections were carried out for all profiles, using the average forest soil mass at each depth interval as a reference mass [28] to calculate carbon stores.

Statistical analyses were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0. For each response variable (bulk density, soil carbon and nitrogen concentrations, soil carbon storage, C:N, soil moisture), data were first screened for normality and outliers. Normality was assessed using skewness, kurtosis, the Shapiro-Wilk’s test, and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. Outliers exceeding three standard deviations from the mean were removed, and normality tests were re-run to confirm assumptions were met. Homogeneity of variance was assessed using a Levene’s test. The SOC data satisfied this requirement after the data were log-transformed. Once the assumptions for normality and homogeneity of variance were validated, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate the effect of land-use treatment on soil response variables (e.g., moisture content, bulk density, SOC concentration and storage, and C: N ratio) at each depth interval (0–5 cm, 5–15 cm, 15–30 cm, and 30–45 cm) for differences among treatment means. When differences were significant (p < 0.05), orthogonal contrasts were used to answer the following questions: (1) Do forest soils differ from agricultural soils? (LC1 = λforest – 1/3 (λ<10y M + λ<10y C + λ>50y C), (2) Does the effect diminish with time since conversion? (LC2 = λ<10y C – λ>50y C), and (3) Do mulched soils differ from soils converted conventionally? (LC3 = λ<10y C – λ<10y M).

Results

Changes are largely observed in the 0–5 cm interval

Land conversion from forest to agriculture significantly (p < 0.05) affected soil moisture content, bulk density, SOC concentration, SOC storage, and the C: N in the 0–5 cm depth interval (Table 1). Moisture content (Fig. 2A) was highest in the forest soils (30.3 ± 2.4%) and significantly lower in agricultural soils, with the lowest soil moisture observed in the mulched treatment (20.2 ± 2.2%). Bulk density (Fig. 2B) showed the opposite trend with the lowest values in forest soils (0.66 ± 0.08 g cm− 3), and higher values observed in agricultural soils, particularly the conventionally cleared treatments (< 10y C: 1.04 ± 0.07 g cm-3, > 50y C: 0.98 ± 0.05g cm-3). SOC concentrations (Fig. 2C) decreased significantly in the agricultural soils compared to the forest soils (75.2 ± 16.2 mg g− 1). SOC concentrations in the conventionally cleared sites decreased by 60.7% (< 10y C: 29.5 ± 3.7 mg g− 1, > 50y C: 29.8 ± 3.2 mg g− 1), while SOC concentrations in the mulched fields decreased by 45.7%. The C: N ratio (Fig. 2D) was narrower in agricultural soils, particularly in fields cleared conventionally more than 50 years ago (12.0 ± 0.5) compared to forests (18.2 ± 2.6). Mulched fields had a slightly wider C: N ratio (14.2 ± 0.7) than the conventionally cleared sites.

Limited changes observed in the 5–15 cm, 15–30 cm, and 30–45 cm interval

In the 5–15 cm depth interval, effects of treatment were limited to bulk density and C: N (p < 0.05) (Table 1). Soil moisture, SOC concentration, and SOC storage showed no significant effect of treatment (p > 0.05). Bulk density remained higher in agricultural fields (Fig. 3A), particularly those conventionally cleared (< 10y C: 1.22 ± 0.05 g cm− 3), compared to forested sites (0.84 ± 0.15 g cm− 3). Within the agricultural treatments, fields incorporating mulch had a slightly lower bulk density (0.98 ± 0.06 g cm− 3) than both conventionally cleared treatments (Fig. 3B). The C: N ratio was narrower in sites that were cleared > 50 years ago compared to those cleared < 10 years ago (Fig. 4A). A widening of the C: N ratio is also observed in mulched fields when compared to conventionally cleared fields (Fig. 4B).

In the 15–30 cm depth interval, treatment effects were not significant at the experiment-wide error rate of α = 0.05, however, SOC concentration and SOC storage were significant at α = 0.10. Mean values for SOC concentration and storage were higher in agricultural treatments than in forest soils (Table 1). SOC concentration in agricultural treatments ranged from 19.5 ± 3.3 mg g⁻¹ in the mulch sites to 22.3 ± 2.7 mg g⁻¹ in the > 50y conventional sites, compared to just 10.9 ± 2.3 mg g⁻¹ in forests (Table 2 ). Mean SOC storage was higher in agricultural fields, with the highest values observed in the > 50y conventional fields (36.5 ± 4.4 Mg ha⁻¹), compared to 17.9 ± 3.8 Mg ha⁻¹ in forests (Fig. 5). This pattern suggests that carbon may accumulate or be redistributed to deeper layers following land conversion.

In the 30–45 cm depth interval, there was no significant effect of treatment on any of the soil response variables assessed (p > 0.05 for all comparisons; Table 1). SOC concentrations remained low across all treatments, ranging from 3.5 ± 0.8 mg g⁻¹ in forests to 6.3 ± 1.9 mg g⁻¹ in the < 10y conventional fields (Table 2). SOC storage showed a similarly narrow range across treatments, with values from 7.3 ± 1.6 Mg ha⁻¹ in forests to 13.2 ± 4.1 Mg ha⁻¹ in < 10y conventional fields. Bulk density was relatively consistent among treatments, averaging between 1.29 and 1.43 g cm⁻³. Generally, the C: N ratio was narrower in this interval compared to shallower depth intervals, with minimal differences between treatments. Overall, no clear or consistent treatment-related trends were observed at this depth, suggesting limited impact of land conversion practices on response variables.

No significant effect of treatment on cumulative SOC storage

There were no statistically significant effects of treatment on cumulative SOC storage, but non-significant trends are apparent with depth (Fig. 6). Here we consider cumulative effects by summing storages in depth intervals together progressively with depth. Though these trends are non-significant they suggest a redistribution of SOC between depth intervals. Forest sites have the highest mean cumulative storage values in the 0–15 cm intervals (50.1 ± 10.7 Mg C ha− 1), followed by mulched fields (42.6 ± 5.9 Mg C ha− 1). The < 10y conventionally cleared fields had the lowest mean value at this depth (30.4 ± 4.6 Mg C ha− 1). Considering SOC storage in the 0–30 cm interval, the highest mean cumulative SOC storage value (74.6 ± 9.7 Mg C ha− 1) was observed for the mulched fields, with the lowest mean cumulative storage value observed in the < 10y conventional treatment (63.7 ± 11.4 Mg C ha− 1). The mean cumulative storage value was slightly higher in > 50y conventionally cleared fields (69.4 ± 7.4 Mg C ha− 1) compared to forest sites (68.1 ± 13.8 Mg C ha− 1). Considering all intervals (i.e., 0–45 cm), mulched fields had the highest mean SOC storage value (84.1 ± 10.5 Mg C ha− 1), followed by > 50y conventionally cleared fields (81.0 ± 11.0 Mg C ha− 1), with the lowest observed storage mean storage value in the forest sites (75.4 ± 15.0 Mg C ha− 1).

Discussion

No significant effects of land conversion from forest to agriculture on SOC storage

Our results showed that, statistically, there was no significant effect of land conversion on SOC storage over the depth sampled (0–45 cm). However, the mean values of cumulative SOC storage were higher after conversion in the fields compared to the forests. This result was unexpected, as past research has consistently reported decreases in SOC following conversion from forested to agricultural land [3, 6]. For example, Murty et al. [6] reported a global decrease of 22% when reviewing results that were properly corrected for changes in bulk density. Wei et al. [3] similarly reported decreases in SOC across multiple different climatic regions following conversion from forest to agriculture, including of 31% in boreal regions. In this study, we reported mean values of SOC stores that were 11.5%, 2.0%, and 7.4% higher than the forest for < 10y mulched, < 10y conventional, and > 50y conventional treatments, though these differences were non-significant. Several site-specific factors could help explain the variation between the observations of this study and previous studies. Wei et al. [3] highlight that the smallest declines in SOC were observed in boreal regions and in soils with high clay content and bulk density, whereas more rapid losses are associated with sandy soils or soils with high initial SOC. Although clay content remains an unconstrained variable in this study, there was an increase in bulk density following land conversion (Fig. 3), and soils across the whole study, including soils that were collected 15–25 km south, were generally fine in texture based on feel. These conditions could have minimized SOC losses by creating an environment that promotes stabilization of organic matter.

Mean SOC storage values were lower in deeper depth intervals in the forest treatment compared to the agricultural soils, likely attributed to a high volume of organic inputs at the surface and a relatively low degree of vertical redistribution or soil disturbance. Any vertical redistribution of SOC with depth would likely be controlled by illuviation from the upper horizons [30, 31], with soil disturbance being limited to bioturbation and freezing cycles [32]. A consistent observation in the distribution of SOC storage with depth for the agricultural treatments was a vertical redistribution of SOC from the surface interval (0–5 cm) to lower depths, particularly the 15–30 cm interval. This redistribution of SOC is consistent with what is observed in soils subjected to regular tillage [33, 34]. The exact frequency and mode of tillage was not constrained for the agricultural fields sampled, but all the sites incorporated tillage as a land management practise. Tillage may explain the vertical redistribution of SOC in the agricultural sites, but it does not explain the high mean values of SOC, as heavy tillage has historically lead to overall decreases in SOC [35].

One practise that has been shown to yield increases in SOC accumulation is the application of mineral fertilizers, in particular nitrogen [36–38]. Alvarez [36] observed an increase in the accrual of SOC with the application of nitrogen fertilizer when crop residues were returned to the soil under conservative tillage practises, and in temperate regions. An elevated availability of N within soils can facilitate the production of plant biomass and increase C inputs [38], as well as increase root growth [36, 37] leading to higher inputs of root biomass and exudates [38]. Again, the exact frequency and quantity of mineral or nitrogen fertilizer applied to the sampled fields was not constrained for this study, but the agricultural sites were known to incorporate mineral fertilizers as a land management practice. A more complete land management history is required to determine whether the use of nitrogen fertilizer was driving the accumulation of SOC in the agricultural soils. Notably, there was a significant difference in the C: N ratios between forested and agricultural sites across 0–15 cm in depth (Table 1). Agricultural soils showed a relative narrowing of the C: N ratio compared to forest sites suggesting a higher availability of N, or lower input of C–rich organic matter.

During sample collection and preparation, a dense, seemingly clay–rich subsurface layer, consistently occurred between a depth of 20–45 cm that was coincident with higher soil bulk density. The largest accumulation of SOC in the agricultural treatments was found in the 15–30 cm depth interval, with subordinate SOC concentrations in the 30–45 cm interval. Although our dataset does not include clay content or mineralogy, one possible explanation is that SOC from the surface is redistributed deeper into the soil because of tillage and that a clay-rich layer is promoting the accrual of SOC. Clay is known to sorb SOC and contribute to aggregation, which can interrupt leaching loss and physically protect SOC from microbial decomposition. This hypothesis is consistent with the findings of Zhang et al. [39] who found a positive correlation between clay content and SOC with depth in subsurface soil layers. Clay is known to play an important role in SOC stabilization within aggregates, which can result in slower decomposition rates and longer persistence of SOC [39, 40]. Additionally, clay-rich soils may help retain plant-derived carbon by either preserving it as microbial residues or maintaining it in a relatively unaltered form [41]. However, because clay content and mineralogy were not measured in this study, this explanation should be viewed as a hypothesis for future testing rather than a conclusion supported by the current data set.

There was a significant effect of treatment following conversion from forest to agriculture on all soil response variables (soil moisture, bulk density, SOC concentration and storage, and C: N ratio) in the 0–5 cm interval. A decrease in soil moisture and SOC storage/concentration in the agricultural sites was expected following land conversion due to the loss of a forest canopy and the increase in soil temperature [2]. An increase in bulk density in the agricultural fields compared to forests was also expected since frequent cultivation can break soil aggregates compacting existing soil, and lower inputs of soil organic matter can decrease porosity [6]. The C: N ratio was notably wider in the forest sites compared to the agricultural sites. A wider C: N ratio likely corresponds with more carbon–rich organic inputs (e.g. woody debris and plant litter) from a forest environment, compared to the more nitrogen–rich organic inputs expected in agricultural systems (e.g. crop residues) [18]. Speaking to the incorporation of woody residues, the C: N ratio was significantly wider in the mulched fields compared to the agricultural fields which is consistent with the observations seen in other research investigating the effects of buried wood residues [17, 18, 41]. Wood has a wide C: N ratio and this significant change in C: N suggests the mulch has likely undergone some degree of decomposition and has been incorporated into the SOC pool.

Four fields (two < 10y and two > 50y conventionally cleared fields) were located 15–25 km from the nearest forest included in this this study. While it would have been ideal to have a comparable forest nearby these sites to more closely approach a paired field-forest design, the data collected from these sites were not outliers. Forests in the area that are cleared for conventional agricultural production systems are targeted because they are deemed suitable for agriculture because of their site characteristics (e.g., soil type and ease of access). Rather than sample a nearby forest that did not have similar characteristics to the pre-clearance forest, we included forests that had similar characteristics to the pre-clearance forests based on available information (i.e. forest inventory, soil maps, farmer input). We, however, do acknowledge that this is a limitation of the study and a source of error.

The incorporation of mulch and its effects on SOC recovery

Our results indicated there was no significant effect of treatment in SOC storage between treatment groups following the conversion of forest to agricultural land and no effect of mulching. This result was unexpected considering increases in SOC up to 10% have been reported by Fontana et al. [17], who investigated the effects of a single application of ramial woodchips on crop nutrition, SOC storage, and soil fertility in Switzerland. Fontana et al. [17] also reported decreases in bulk density and increases in water holding capacity. However, our results show non-significant trends that could illustrate some possible benefits of this practise, and warrant further research into its use as a sustainable agricultural practise.

Though not significant, the mulched fields were observed to have the highest mean SOC storage values of the four treatment groups. The mean cumulative SOC storage value in mulched treatment was 11.5% higher than the forest, which is 9.4% and 3.8% higher than the mean values in the < 10y conventional fields and the > 50y conventional fields, respectively. These results suggest mulching may have a positive effect on the recovery of SOC storage after land conversion but again these are non-significant trends that require further study. Compared to the other agricultural treatments, the mulched sites had lower SOC storage values in the 15–30 cm interval and the highest mean storage values of the four treatment groups in the 5–15 cm interval (29.4 ± 4.1 Mg C ha− 1). This observation may be explained by the depth that the mulch residues would be transported after being incorporated into the soil through tillage. Further research is required to better understand these non-significant trends with mulching.

Our results showed that mulching had a significant effect of treatment on bulk density compared to conventional clearing. The mulched fields exhibited a lower bulk density, likely due to the increase in organic matter [42]. There is a well established inverse relationship between bulk density and SOC storage [42, 43], which is consistent with the higher but non-significant mean SOC storage values in the mulched fields. These observations are also consistent with Fontana et al. [17] who observed a decrease in bulk density after incorporating ramial woodchips into agricultural soils. Though not significant, mean soil moisture in the 5–15 cm interval in the mulched fields was higher than in the conventional fields (Table 2). These non-significant trends simply reflect the directionality of the observed means, without implying a reliable effect. We reference them in this section only to provide interpretative context to the pattern and to highlight where future, better-powered studies may be more informative.

Conclusion

This study documented no statistically significant effects of land conversion on SOC storage but a clear vertical redistribution of SOC within the top 45 cm of the soil. Our results raise the possibility that land conversion in heavier textured soils in cooler climates may not result in the large losses of SOC typically reported in the literature. Soil tillage likely explains the redistribution of SOC within the upper soil profile and may contribute to SOC accrual, if there is capacity for SOC stabilization through organomineral interaction and/or environmental conditions (e.g. low oxygen availability) that restrict or lower rates of decomposition. Long-term monitoring of the trajectories of SOC following land conversion, particularly where mulch is incorporated, is required to better understand and constrain changes in SOC stores. Future work investigating the distribution of SOC between particulate and mineral associated organic matter fractions may yield insight on the SOC that has been redistributed within the upper profile.
