The Association of the Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio and the D-dimer-to-Albumin Ratio with Deep Vein Thrombosis in Patients with Spontaneous Intracerebral Hemorrhage
Authors: Linli Lou, Mian Lin, Xuerong Huang, Xiaoxiao Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) is a frequent complication in patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (sICH). Identifying readily available biomarkers associated with DVT risk may improve early risk stratification.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study enrolled 447 patients with sICH admitted between August 2023 and January 2026. All patients had negative results on bilateral lower-extremity venous ultrasonography within 48 hours of admission. Repeat ultrasonography was scheduled every 5 to 7 days and before discharge, and additional examinations were arranged when patients developed lower-extremity symptoms or experienced marked D-dimer elevation. Both symptomatic and asymptomatic, distal and proximal DVT cases were included in the analysis. Multivariable logistic regression and ROC curve analyses were conducted to assess factors associated with DVT, including NLR and DAR.ResultsAmong them, 94 patients were diagnosed with incident DVT during hospitalization, corresponding to an incidence of 21.03%. Patients with DVT were significantly older and had higher Padua scores. Neutrophil count, fibrinogen, D-dimer, NLR, and DAR were significantly higher in the DVT group, whereas lymphocyte count, albumin level, and hemoglobin level were lower. In multivariable analysis, age (adjusted OR, 1.046; 95% CI, 1.021–1.071), Padua score (adjusted OR, 1.430; 95% CI, 1.122–1.823), NLR (adjusted OR, 1.064; 95% CI, 1.008–1.124), and DAR (per 0.01-unit increase: adjusted OR, 1.107; 95% CI, 1.026–1.196) were independently associated with DVT. The AUCs were 0.677 for NLR, 0.718 for DAR, and 0.740 for the combined model. The combined-model AUC was higher than that of NLR (P=0.007) but not significantly different from that of DAR (P=0.276). Calibration was imperfect, although bootstrap internal validation suggested limited optimism.ConclusionAdmission NLR and DAR were independently associated with subsequently detected in-hospital DVT after sICH. The two-biomarker model showed moderate discrimination but did not significantly outperform DAR alone, warranting prospective external validation.Keywords: spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage, deep vein thrombosis, d-dimer to albumin ratio, neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio

Introduction

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is a common complication in patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (sICH). Patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage are predominantly older adults, who are at particularly high risk for thrombotic complications such as DVT. It not only prolongs hospital stays and increases the burden on the healthcare system but can also lead to serious adverse events such as pulmonary embolism, making it a significant risk factor affecting patient prognosis.1 Pharmacological thromboprophylaxis requires careful balancing of thrombotic risk against the potential for hematoma expansion.2 Therefore, understanding the factors associated with DVT development in sICH patients is particularly important for informing future risk stratification strategies. However, commonly used VTE risk assessment models, such as the Padua and Caprini scores, were not originally developed specifically for patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage. Although these models include several general clinical risk factors, their discriminatory performance, calibration, and clinical applicability in the specific sICH population remain poorly characterized. The Padua score only includes “acute myocardial infarction or ischemic stroke” as a 1-point item, which neither encompasses hemorrhagic stroke nor assigns adequate weight to this population.3 This may limit its ability to fully capture the complete thrombotic risk profile of patients with sICH. In the Caprini score, acute stroke is assigned 5 points, automatically placing the majority of sICH patients into the highest risk stratum. The original design of this score was not optimized for risk gradient stratification specifically within the sICH population.4 Moreover, neither score adequately incorporates ICH-specific predictors, such as the severity of neurological deficits (NIHSS/GCS), hemorrhagic imaging characteristics (hematoma volume, subarachnoid or intraventricular extension), in-hospital complications (pneumonia, gastrointestinal bleeding), dynamic coagulation-fibrinolysis markers, or systemic inflammatory responses, thereby limiting their applicability in the sICH population.5–7

Recent studies suggest that the activation of inflammatory cascades, the imbalance of coagulation-anticoagulation homeostasis, and disturbances in nutritional metabolism collectively contribute to the onset and progression of DVT through complex interactions among the immune, metabolic, and coagulation systems.8,9 However, this complex mechanism is difficult to assess directly and dynamically in clinical practice. Therefore, identifying blood biomarkers that can conveniently and comprehensively reflect these pathophysiological processes is critical for the early risk stratification and preventive intervention of DVT after sICH.

The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) has been widely recognized as a marker of systemic inflammation and has been associated with thrombotic events in various cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases.10–12 Elevated NLR has been closely associated with postoperative DVT, malignancy-associated thrombosis, and adverse outcomes in acute pulmonary embolism. However, existing evidence has predominantly focused on postoperative orthopedic, oncological, and general medical inpatient populations,13–15 while evidence regarding the sICH population remains limited.

The D-dimer-to-albumin ratio (DAR) is a novel composite indicator that integrates coagulation function and systemic acute-phase status; it simultaneously reflects the body’s tendency toward thrombosis and acute-phase albumin metabolic disturbances. It is primarily used to assess the severity of illness in critically ill patients and to predict poor outcomes.16–18 Currently, the D-dimer-to-albumin ratio has been shown to have predictive value for venous thromboembolism in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors.19 The combination of DAR and NLR has been found to further improve the predictive efficacy for preoperative DVT in elderly patients with hip fracture.20 However, evidence regarding the combined application of these two biomarkers in patients with sICH is still scarce. Therefore, this study evaluated the associations of admission NLR and DAR with subsequently detected in-hospital DVT in patients with sICH, and assessed the discriminative performance of each biomarker and their combination.

Methods

Study Population

This single-center retrospective study included consecutive patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage who were admitted to our hospital between August 2023 and January 2026. A total of 447 eligible patients were included in the final analysis. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) diagnosis of sICH;21 (3) first-onset sICH in the acute phase (duration < 14 days); and (4) negative baseline bilateral lower-extremity venous ultrasonography for DVT performed within 48 hours of admission. Exclusion criteria were: (1) history of DVT; (2) history of pulmonary embolism without DVT; (3) severe liver or kidney disease; (4) prior anticoagulant therapy; (5) malignancy; and (6) incomplete electronic medical records.

DVT Surveillance and Outcome Definition

All included patients had negative baseline bilateral lower-extremity venous ultrasonography within 48 hours of admission. During hospitalization, scheduled repeat bilateral lower-extremity venous ultrasonography was performed every 5–7 days and once more before discharge. Additional ultrasonography was performed promptly when lower-extremity symptoms developed or when a marked elevation in D-dimer levels was considered clinically concerning by the treating physician.

Both distal and proximal deep vein thrombosis (DVT) were included, regardless of whether cases were symptomatic or asymptomatic. The primary outcome was incident in-hospital DVT, defined as DVT newly detected by lower-extremity venous ultrasonography after a negative baseline examination. The date of DVT detection was defined as the date of the first positive ultrasonography examination.

Patients with DVT detected during hospitalization were classified into the DVT group, while patients with no evidence of DVT on follow-up ultrasonography before discharge were classified into the non-DVT group. The study design flowchart is shown in Figure 1.

The flow of patient selection.

Candidate Variables and Measurements

Baseline variables were extracted from medical records and included demographic characteristics, comorbidities, clinical features, hemorrhage location, and laboratory parameters (Table 1). NLR is calculated by dividing the neutrophil count by the lymphocyte count.22 DAR is calculated by dividing the D-dimer level by the albumin level.23 The above indicators are based on the first set of laboratory data collected after admission and were independently reviewed by two neurologists. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Patients with missing information required to confirm eligibility, calculate NLR or DAR, or determine DVT status were excluded.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). SPSS was used for descriptive statistics, between-group comparisons, and multivariable logistic regression analyses. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using Student’s t test. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Univariable analyses were initially performed to examine the associations between candidate variables and incident in-hospital DVT. Candidate variables included age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, admission systolic and diastolic blood pressure, Padua score, hemorrhage location, albumin, triglycerides, total cholesterol, fibrinogen, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, NLR, platelet count, hemoglobin, admission blood glucose, D-dimer, and DAR. Variables with P<0.05 in the univariable analyses, together with variables considered clinically relevant, were considered for inclusion in the multivariable analysis.

Because NLR was calculated from neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, these component variables were not entered simultaneously with NLR. Similarly, D-dimer and albumin were not entered simultaneously with DAR. NLR and DAR were retained as the biomarkers of primary interest. The final multivariable logistic regression model included age, female sex, Padua score, triglycerides, fibrinogen, hemoglobin, NLR, and DAR. All selected variables were entered simultaneously using the Enter method. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. For continuous variables, the OR represented the change in the odds of incident in-hospital DVT associated with a one-unit increase, unless otherwise specified. Because DAR values were small, its OR was expressed per 0.01-unit increase.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were conducted in R using the pROC package. The combined NLR–DAR measure was defined as the predicted probability generated from a binary logistic regression model containing NLR and DAR as continuous independent variables. Areas under the ROC curves (AUCs) and their 95% CIs were calculated for NLR, DAR, and the combined model. Correlated AUCs were compared pairwise using the nonparametric DeLong test. Cohort-specific cutoff values were selected by maximizing the Youden index. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated at the selected cutoff values.

Calibration and internal validation of the combined model were conducted in R. Model calibration was evaluated using a calibration plot, the calibration slope, the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and the Brier score. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test was performed using the ResourceSelection package. Bootstrap internal validation and optimism correction were performed using the rms package with 1000 bootstrap resamples. Optimism-corrected estimates of the calibration slope and Brier score were reported. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 447 patients with sICH were included in the final analysis, with a mean age of 66.03 ± 13.57 years. Among them, 94 patients developed DVT, corresponding to an incidence of 21.03% (Figure 1).

The baseline characteristics of patients with and without DVT are summarized in Table 1. Patients in the DVT group were significantly older than those in the non-DVT group (74.55 ± 11.81 vs 63.76 ± 13.11 years, P < 0.001). The Padua score was higher in the DVT group (median: 4.0 vs 3.0, P < 0.001). In addition, the proportion of female patients was higher among patients with DVT (44.7% vs 29.2%, P = 0.004).

No significant differences were observed between the two groups with respect to BMI, hypertension, diabetes, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, sICH location, platelet count, blood glucose, or total cholesterol (all P > 0.05).

Comparison of Laboratory Indicators

As shown in Figure 2, several laboratory parameters differed significantly between the two groups. Patients with DVT had higher neutrophil counts, fibrinogen, D-dimer, NLR, and DAR, and lower lymphocyte counts, albumin, and hemoglobin (all P < 0.05). The median NLR was higher in the DVT group than in the non-DVT group (5.00 vs 3.15, P < 0.001), as was the median DAR (0.0191 vs 0.0084, P < 0.001).

Factors Independently Associated with DVT

The multivariable model included variables selected on the basis of univariable results and clinical relevance. The results showed that age, Padua score, NLR, and DAR were independently associated with the occurrence of DVT (Table 2). After adjustment for potential confounders, age (adjusted OR, 1.046; 95% CI, 1.021–1.071; P < 0.001), Padua score (adjusted OR, 1.430; 95% CI, 1.122–1.823; P = 0.004), NLR (adjusted OR, 1.064; 95% CI, 1.008–1.124; P = 0.025), and DAR (per 0.01-unit increase: adjusted OR, 1.107; 95% CI, 1.026–1.196; P = 0.009) were independently associated with incident in-hospital DVT.

Discrimination, Calibration, and Internal Validation of the Combined NLR–DAR Model

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminative performance of NLR, DAR, and their combination for DVT. The AUC values were 0.677 (95% CI, 0.620–0.735) for NLR, 0.718 (95% CI, 0.661–0.776) for DAR, and 0.740 (95% CI, 0.687–0.794) for the combined NLR–DAR model (Figure 3 and Table 3).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 447 patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (sICH) and negative baseline ultrasonography findings, 94 patients (21.03%) were subsequently diagnosed with DVT during hospitalization. Older age, a higher Padua score, and elevated admission NLR and DAR were independently associated with subsequently detected in-hospital DVT after adjustment for the selected clinical factors. These findings suggest that routinely available biomarkers reflecting inflammatory and coagulation-related systemic responses may provide information associated with DVT occurrence in patients with sICH.

The two-biomarker NLR–DAR model demonstrated moderate discriminatory power and performed significantly better than NLR alone, but it did not significantly outperform DAR alone. This finding suggests that the model’s discriminatory performance is largely driven by DAR, and the incremental contribution of NLR is limited. Although bootstrap internal validation showed only limited optimism, the significant Hosmer–Lemeshow test result and the deviation of the calibration curve from the ideal line indicate that the agreement between predicted and observed probabilities is incomplete. Therefore, the cohort-specific cutoff values and predicted probabilities should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, the observed associations do not establish causality. Because ultrasonography only captures the time of DVT detection rather than its actual biological onset, these findings cannot be interpreted as evidence of diagnostic accuracy for prevalent DVT. The clinical utility of the NLR–DAR model for guiding DVT surveillance or thromboprophylaxis remains unproven, and it requires comparison with established clinical models and prospective external validation.

Traditional tools such as the Padua score primarily focus on clinical risk factors and may not fully capture the dynamic biological processes underlying DVT. In recent years, research has suggested that DVT is not solely a disorder of coagulation, but a multifactorial process involving the interplay of inflammation, coagulation, and metabolic dysfunction.24,25 Following intracerebral hemorrhage, systemic inflammatory responses and coagulation–fibrinolysis activation may contribute to a prothrombotic state.26–32 Within this context, NLR and DAR may capture distinct but partially overlapping components of the systemic response to sICH.

NLR serves as a marker of systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation, and is associated with thrombotic complications following intracerebral hemorrhage.12 Acute brain injury triggers early lymphocytopenia and systemic inflammatory activation,33,34 and during this process neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) connect inflammation to thrombosis and amplify coagulation via interactions with platelets.35,36 However, elevated NLR may reflect the severity of intracerebral hemorrhage, stress response, infection, or treatment-related complications, rather than DVT-specific inflammatory mechanisms.37 For example, Chen et al included NLR and other inflammatory markers in their AIS-DVT study, but NLR was not retained in the final model, indicating that the association between inflammatory markers and DVT may differ by stroke subtype and study population.38

DAR integrates D-dimer and albumin, two biomarkers reflecting different aspects of the systemic response. D-dimer is a degradation product of cross-linked fibrin, and elevated levels reflect activation of coagulation and fibrinolysis.39 However, D-dimer is not specific to thrombosis and may also be elevated in association with hemorrhage, inflammation, or infection.40 Studies of patients with intracerebral hemorrhage complicated by DVT have also highlighted substantial clinical heterogeneity and the complexity of anticoagulation management, underscoring the need to interpret coagulation-related biomarkers within the broader clinical context rather than in isolation.41 Albumin, in contrast, may exert antithrombotic effects by modulating platelet activity and supporting endothelial and circulatory homeostasis.42–44 Hypoalbuminemia may reflect acute-phase inflammation, disease severity, altered fluid status, or impaired hepatic function.45 By combining D-dimer and albumin into a single ratio, DAR may capture both coagulation-related and albumin-related processes. The present study extends the evaluation of the previously proposed NLR–DAR combination to patients with sICH, a population for which existing evidence regarding this two-biomarker approach remains limited.

Other biomarkers may reflect related but distinct aspects of the inflammatory and coagulation processes underlying DVT. The D-dimer-to-fibrinogen ratio integrates fibrin degradation information with overall coagulation status, and has been proven to have prognostic value in thrombosis-related conditions.46 CRP is a marker of systemic inflammation, while the CRP-to-albumin ratio integrates both inflammatory and acute-phase response information and has been linked to venous thromboembolism risk.47,48 These biomarkers were not directly evaluated or compared with NLR and DAR in the present study. Future studies should directly compare these biomarkers to determine whether they provide complementary or incremental information for DVT risk stratification in patients with sICH.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective single-center design, combined with the exclusion of patients with incomplete records, may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of our findings. Second, residual confounding cannot be ruled out, as several clinically relevant variables were either unavailable or inconsistently documented, including hematoma volume, intraventricular hemorrhage, neurological and motor impairment, thromboprophylaxis strategies, hypercoagulable states, the status of patient mobilization, and length of hospital stay. Third, NLR and DAR were only measured at admission, and their dynamic changes over the course of treatment were not evaluated in this study. In addition, intermittent ultrasonographic surveillance only captured the time of DVT detection, rather than the biological onset of thrombosis, which prevented us from conducting a definitive assessment of temporal relationships. Surveillance timing and clinically triggered examinations may also have introduced ascertainment bias. In particular, elevated D-dimer levels often prompt additional ultrasonography examinations, and since D-dimer contributes to the calculation of DAR, this differential surveillance could have increased DVT detection rates among patients with higher DAR values, which may have affected the observed association. Detailed stratified analyses of DVT timing and subtype were also not possible due to lack of relevant data. Finally, although we performed AUC comparisons, calibration assessment, and bootstrap internal validation, the proposed biomarker model was not compared with a fully specified clinical baseline model, externally validated, or evaluated via decision-curve analysis. The cutoff values identified in this study are cohort-specific and should not be interpreted as validated clinical thresholds. Therefore, these findings remain exploratory and require further verification via prospective multicenter validation with standardized DVT surveillance and prespecified clinical models.

Conclusion

Admission NLR and DAR were independently associated with subsequently detected in-hospital DVT in patients with sICH. Their combination showed moderate discrimination and outperformed NLR alone, but not DAR alone. However, imperfect calibration and the lack of external validation preclude conclusions regarding clinical utility. These exploratory findings require prospective multicenter external validation and formal comparison with established clinical models.
