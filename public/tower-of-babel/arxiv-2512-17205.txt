A GENERAL CHARACTERIZATION ON THE UNIQUENESS PROBLEM OF L-FUNCTIONS AND GENERAL MEROMORPHIC FUNCTIONS Thanks: Supported by SERB, DST, INDIA and NBHM, DAE, INDIA Thanks: Communicated by …
Sanjay Mallick, Ripan Saha

Abstract
. In the paper, concerning a question of Yi [ 23 ] , we study general criterion for the uniqueness of an L-function and a general meromorphic function. Our results improve and extend all the existing results in this direction [ 23 , 18 , 17 , 4 ] to the most general setting. Moreover, we have exhibited a handsome number of examples to justify our claims as well as to confirm the wide-ranging applications of our results.

1.
Introduction and Related Results

The value distribution of L-functions in the Selberg class via Nevanlinna theory is a recent trend to study different properties of L-functions as well as uniquely determining the same. This kind of L-functions includes the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-s}
and essentially those Dirichelet series where one might expect a Riemann hypothesis. Such an L-function is
defined
[
19
,
20
]
to be a Dirichelet series

\mathcal{L}(s)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a(n)}{n^{-s}}

with
a(1)=1
satisfying the following axioms:

•

(i)
Ramanujan hypothesis :

a(n)\ll n^{\varepsilon}
for every
\varepsilon>0;

•

(ii)
Analytic continuation :
There is a non-negative integer
m
such that

(s-1)^{m}\mathcal{L}(s)
is an entire function of
finite order;

•

(iii)
Functional equation:

\mathcal{L}
satisfies a functional equation of type

\Lambda_{\mathcal{L}}(s)=\omega\overline{\Lambda_{\mathcal{L}}(1-\overline{s})},

where

\Lambda_{\mathcal{L}}(s)=\mathcal{L}(s)Q^{s}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\Gamma(\lambda_{j}s+\nu_{j}),

with positive real numbers
Q,\lambda_{j}
and complex numbers
\nu_{j},\omega
with Re
\nu_{j}\geqslant 0
and
|\omega|=1;

•

(iv)
Euler product hypothesis :

\log\mathcal{L}(s)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{b(n)}{n^{s}}
, where
b(n)=0
unless
n
is a positive power of a prime
and
b(n)\ll n^{\theta}
for some
\theta<\frac{1}{2}
.

Throughout the paper by an L-function we always mean an L-function of the above kind and by any meromorphic function we always mean a meromorphic function defined in
\mathbb{C}
. We denote
\mathbb{\overline{C}}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
. By
\mathbb{N}
we mean the set of all natural numbers. Though for standard definitions used in this paper we refer our readers to follow
[
9
]
, yet for the sake of our convenience we denote the order of
f
by
\rho(f)
, where

\rho(f)=\limsup_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log(T(r,f))}{\log r}.

By
S(r,f)
we mean any quantity satisfying
S(r,f)=O(\log(rT(r,f)))
for all
r
possibly outside a set of finite linear measure. If
f
is a function of finite order, then
S(r,f)=O(\log r)

for all
r
.

3.

In this section, we exhibit different examples applying our main theorems and corollaries which clearly shows the broad perspectives of our results with special attention to the fact that the results

comes under the special cases of our results. In addition, our results significantly improve

Example 3.1
.

Consider the polynomial

P(z)=z^{n}+az^{n-m}+b=z^{n-m}(z^{m}+a)+b,

where
n,m
are relatively prime inegers with
n-m>1
and a, b are non-zero constants such that the polynomial has no multiple zero. Suppose
S=\{z:P(z)=0\}

Here

p=m+1\geqslant 2\;\ \text{and }\;\ gcd(n,n-m)=1.

Also

P^{\prime}(z)=z^{n-m-1}(nz^{m}+a(n-m));

i.e.,

s=m+1.

Suppose that
P(f)=P(\mathcal{L})
, for any non-constant meromorphic function f and a non-constant L-function
\mathcal{L}
, then we have

(3.1)

f^{n}-\mathcal{L}^{n}=-a(f^{n-m}-\mathcal{L}^{n-m}).

If
f^{n}\not\equiv\mathcal{L}^{n}
, then we can rewrite (
3.1
) as

(3.2)

\mathcal{L}^{m}=-a\frac{(h-v)(h-v^{2})...(h-v^{{n-m}-1})}{(h-u)(h-u^{2})...(h-u^{n-1})},

where
h=\displaystyle\frac{f}{\mathcal{L}}
,
u=exp(2\pi i/n)
and
v=exp(2\pi i/(n-m))
. Noting that n and (n-m) are relatively prime positive
integers, then the numerator and denominator of (
3.2
) have no common factors. Since
\mathcal{L}
has atmost one pole at
z=1
in
the complex plane, and whenever
n\geqslant 5
we can see that there exists at least three distinct roots of

such that they are Picard
exceptional values of h
,
and so it follows by (
3.2
) that h and thus
\mathcal{L}
are constants, which is impossible.

Therefore, we must have
f^{n}=\mathcal{L}^{n}
. Then by (
3.1
) we also have
f^{n-m}=\mathcal{L}^{n-m}
. Since n and (n-m) are relatively prime positive integers, we deduce that
f=\mathcal{L}
. Thus we see that
P(f)=P(\mathcal{L})\implies f=\mathcal{L}
, when
n\geqslant 5
.

Now we apply

2.1
to find the minimum value of

E_{f}(S,t)=E_{\mathcal{L}}(S,t)\implies f=\mathcal{L}
.

Therefore,

,

(2)

t=1
, and

.

Again if

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{f^{n}}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}(f-\beta_{j})^{m_{j}}}.\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{L}^{n}}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}(\mathcal{L}-\beta_{j})^{m_{j}}}\equiv a\left(\frac{a_{i}}{a_{n}}\right)^{2}=a^{{}^{\prime}}(say),

and hence

(4.26)

\displaystyle T(r,f)=T(r,\mathcal{L})+O(1).

It is clear from (
4.25
) that
\beta_{j}
point of
f
is a zero of
\mathcal{L}
and vice-versa.

(a)
Let
k\geqslant 2
.
If
z_{0}
be a zero of
\mathcal{L}-\beta_{j}
with
multiplicity
p
, then
z_{0}
is a zero of
f
with multiplicity
q
such that
m_{j}p=nq
; i.e.,
p\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{n}{m_{j}}
. Therefore
\overline{N}(r,\beta_{j};\mathcal{L})\leqslant\displaystyle\frac{m_{j}}{n}N(r,\beta_{j};\mathcal{L})
.

So, using the second fundamental theorem and
\overline{N}(r,\mathcal{L})=O(\log r)
, we get

\displaystyle(k-1)T(r,\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\overline{N}(r,\beta_{j};\mathcal{L})+\overline{N}(r,\infty;\mathcal{L})+S(r,\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{m_{j}}{n}T(r,\mathcal{L})+O(\log r)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle(1-\frac{1}{n})T(r,\mathcal{L})+O(\log r),

which contradicts
k\geqslant 2
.

(b)
Let
k=1
and
gcd(n,m_{1})=b_{1}
, therefore
gcd(n,n-m_{1})=b_{1}
. Then from (
4.25
) we have

(4.27)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{L}^{n}}{(\mathcal{L}-\beta_{1})^{m_{1}}}=\frac{a^{{}^{\prime}}(f-\beta_{1})^{m_{1}}}{f^{n}}.

If
z_{0}
be a zero of
\mathcal{L}-\beta_{1}
with
multiplicity
p
, then
z_{0}
is a zero of
f
with multiplicity
q
such that
m_{1}p=nq
. Since
gcd(n,m_{1})=b_{1}
, so
m_{1}=b_{1}\eta
and
n=b_{1}\zeta
for some
\eta,\zeta\in\mathbb{N}
, where
gcd(\eta,\zeta)=1
. Now
m_{1}p=nq
implies
b_{1}\eta p=qb_{1}\zeta
; i.e.,
\eta p=q\zeta
. Since
gcd(\eta,\zeta)=1
, so
\zeta
divides
p
. Therefore
p\geqslant\zeta
; i.e.,
p\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{n}{b_{1}}
. Therefore,
\overline{N}(r,\beta_{1};\mathcal{L})\leqslant\frac{b_{1}}{n}T(r,\mathcal{L})
and similarly
\overline{N}(r,\beta_{1};f)\leqslant\frac{b_{1}}{n}T(r,f)
.

Also let
z_{0}
be a pole of
f
of multiplicity
\nu
, then
z_{0}
will be a zero of
\mathcal{L}
of multiplicity
\xi
such that
\nu(n-m_{1})=\xi n
. Since
gcd(n,n-m_{1})=b_{1}
, hence
\frac{1}{\nu}\leqslant\frac{b_{1}}{n}
. Therefore
\overline{N}(r,\infty;f)\leqslant\frac{b_{1}}{n}T(r,f).
Also we know that
\overline{N}(r,\infty;\mathcal{L})=O(\log r).

Now from (
4.27
) it is clear that
\overline{N}(r,0;\mathcal{L})=\overline{N}(r,\infty;f)+\overline{N}(r,\beta_{1};f).

Now using the second fundamental theorem in view of (
4.26
) we get

\displaystyle T(r,\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\overline{N}(r,\beta_{1};\mathcal{L})+\overline{N}(r,\infty;\mathcal{L})+\overline{N}(r,0;\mathcal{L})+S(r,\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{3b_{1}}{n}T(r,\mathcal{L})+O(\log r)

Which is a contradiction as
n\geqslant 3b_{1}+1
.
∎

Proof Of The Theorems

Proof of Theorem 1.1 :

We prove the theorem step by step as follows.

\bf{(i)\implies(ii)}
:

Suppose
f
be a non-constant meromorphic function and
\mathcal{L}
be a non-constant L-function such that
E_{f}(S,t)=E_{\mathcal{L}}(S,t)
, where
t\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Consider
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
as defined by (
4.2
). Then for

(a)

t\geqslant 2
and
n\geqslant 2s+5
, or

(b)

t=1
and
n\geqslant 2s+6
, or

(c)

t=0
and
n\geqslant 2s+11

in view of the
Lemma
4.5
we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}-1}=\frac{A}{\mathcal{G}-1}+B
, where

.
Hence we have

(5.1)

T(r,\mathcal{F})=T(r,\mathcal{G})+O(1).

Since

(5.2)

T(r,\mathcal{F})=nT(r,f)+O(1)\;\;and\;\;T(r,\mathcal{G})=nT(r,\mathcal{L})+O(1).

So (
5.1
) implies that

(5.3)

Now in view of
\overline{N}(r,\infty,\mathcal{L})=O(\log r)
using (
5.2
) and (

) we get

\displaystyle\overline{N}(r,0;\mathcal{F})+\overline{N}(r,\infty;\mathcal{F})+\overline{N}(r,0;\mathcal{G})+\overline{N}(r,\infty;\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle pT(r,f)+pT(r,\mathcal{L})+\overline{N}(r,\infty;f)+\overline{N}(r,\infty;\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2p+1)T(r,f)+O(\log r)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{2p+2}{n}T(r,\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle T(r,\mathcal{F})\;\;\;[\because n\geqslant 2p+2].

So in view of
Lemma
4.7
, we have either
\mathcal{F}\mathcal{G}=1
or
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{G}
.

Now again in view of
Lemma
4.8

. That is, we get

. Therefore, obviously

,
