Abstract
. Large language models in healthcare often miss critical emotional cues, delivering medically sound but emotionally flat advice. This is especially problematic in clinical contexts where patients are distressed and vulnerable, and require empathic communication to support safety, adherence, and trust. We present RECAP (Reflect–Extract–Calibrate–Align–Produce), an inference-time framework that adds structured emotional reasoning without retraining. By decomposing empathy into transparent appraisal-theoretic stages and exposing per-dimension Likert signals, RECAP produces nuanced, auditable responses. Across EmoBench, SECEU, and EQ-Bench, RECAP improves emotional reasoning by 22–28% on 8B models and 10–13% on larger models over zero-shot baselines. Clinician evaluations further confirm superior empathetic communication. RECAP shows that modular, theory-grounded prompting can systematically enhance emotional intelligence in medical AI while preserving the accountability required for deployment.



1.
Introduction

As large language models are increasingly used in healthcare applications, from virtual patient assistants to mental health support, there is a growing need for responses that are medically correct and emotionally aligned. An anxious or distressed patient expects an assistant to acknowledge and adapt to their affective state. Empathy is a core clinical competency, central to trust and associated with improved outcomes
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
. For example, a patient receiving bad news may feel frightened or upset; a useful response should address the medical facts and also convey understanding and support. However, contemporary instruction-tuned models may insufficiently attend to affective cues, and default to generic, information-centric phrasing unless explicitly scaffolded. Prior works document variability and prompt sensitivity in empathetic expression
(
Sorin et al. 2024
;
Armbruster et al. 2024
)
, and in our experiments (Table.
3
) we observe the same pattern, with baselines under-acknowledging patient distress. This motivates methods that make emotional reasoning explicit at inference time.

Recent observations show that with careful prompting, chatbots can produce language that patients rate as empathetic
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
. The open problem is not polite wording, but consistent attention to fine-grained emotional states and situations. Two patients who face a similar medical event can differ in perceived control, uncertainty, goals, and agency, and those differences call for different forms of support
(
Ortony et al. 1988
;
Lazarus 1991
)
. To be useful in care, a system should represent such appraisals explicitly at each turn and let them guide both content and tone. Our work focuses on this gap by externalizing these appraisals at inference time and using them to shape the responses, while leaving the base model unchanged.

The challenge of emotional alignment in AI lies in inferring complex affective states from user language and adjusting responses accordingly. Cognitive appraisal theories in psychology suggest that humans evaluate events along key dimensions (goal relevance, agency, coping potential) to determine emotional responses
(
Huang et al. 2024
)
. For instance, losing control over one’s health might trigger fear and helplessness, while receiving support from loved ones could evoke gratitude and hope. While LLMs do not experience emotions, recent work shows they can emulate aspects of this appraisal process when properly guided
(
Croissant et al. 2024
)
. Related approaches have demonstrated that LLMs can be guided through structured reasoning processes to improve their reliability in complex inference tasks
(
Feng et al. 2024
)
. These models have demonstrated the ability to tap into common patterns of situational appraisal and perform complex emotional reasoning tasks. Yet without explicit prompting, they default to surface-level pattern matching that misses deeper emotional context.

In this work, we introduce
RECAP
(

RECAP
guides the model through a structured reasoning process: first abstracting the situation, then identifying psychological factors that influence emotional states, generating candidate emotions, quantifying their likelihood using Likert scales, and finally producing an emotionally aligned response. This decomposition enables the model to systematically reason about emotions before generating text, mimicking the deliberative process of an emotionally intelligent clinician.

Our experimental results demonstrate
RECAP
’s effectiveness at multiple levels. For end-to-end conversation generation,
RECAP
achieved a 95.7% win rate over baseline approaches in clinical evaluations of emotional support quality, with convergent validation from GPT-5 showing 99% preference and quality scores of 8.8 vs. 4.7. On HealthBench’s comprehensive healthcare dialogue evaluation,
RECAP
improved overall scores by 17% for OSS-120B (0.46 to 0.54), with particularly strong gains in communication quality. Beyond generating better responses,
RECAP
’s intermediate emotional analysis capabilities also show substantial improvements on standardized benchmarks—achieving a 28% relative improvement on EmoBench for Llama-3.1-8B, EQ scores improving from 58.31 to 71.29 on SECEU (22% gain), and 70-point Elo gains on EQ-Bench. These results demonstrate that
RECAP
not only produces more empathetic dialogue but does so through more accurate emotional understanding. Our approach is implemented entirely through decomposition-based prompt engineering and can be applied at inference time to any sufficiently capable LLM.

By aligning LLM responses with patient emotions in a principled, transparent manner,
RECAP
aims to bridge the gap between the factual prowess of AI and the empathic communication needed in healthcare settings. In the following sections, we discuss related work in empathetic AI, detail the
RECAP
methodology, and present experimental results demonstrating improved emotional alignment in both general and medical-specific contexts.

2.
Background and Related Work

2.1.
Empathy in Medical Dialogue: Evidence and Gaps

LLMs are increasingly used for health information and triage across diverse healthcare contexts. Multiple studies report that, with careful prompting, chatbot replies are perceived as highly empathetic, sometimes surpassing physicians’ answers in perceived warmth and understanding, while maintaining comparable clinical accuracy
(
Ayers et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Luo et al. 2024
;
Franke Føyen et al. 2025
)
. These findings suggest significant potential for LLMs to augment healthcare communication, particularly in scenarios where human resources are limited or when patients seek initial guidance. Systematic reviews, however, find empathy to be brittle and prompt sensitive, with overuse of generic phrases and instruction-following errors that can undermine genuine connection
(
Sorin et al. 2024
;
Armbruster et al. 2024
)
. This variability in empathetic expression motivates methods that explicitly reason about a user’s emotional state rather than relying on stylistic mimicry alone, ensuring more authentic and contextually appropriate responses.

2.2.
Training Time Alignment and Supervised Empathy Fine Tuning

Training time alignment techniques such as RLHF and DPO improve general helpfulness and conversational tone but optimize global preferences rather than appraisal of situational affect
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
. These methods excel at producing generally agreeable responses but may miss nuanced emotional cues that require situational awareness. Supervised fine tuning on empathetic corpora, including datasets like EmpatheticDialogues and large counseling datasets such as SoulChat, can raise perceived empathy to clinician rated levels
(
Rashkin et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Franke Føyen et al. 2025
)
. However, this approach embeds empathetic behavior directly in model weights, making it less transparent and harder to audit or adjust per dialogue. The lack of interpretability poses challenges for medical applications where understanding the reasoning behind empathetic responses is crucial for safety and trust. Our approach instead operates at inference time and exposes interpretable signals through Likert type emotion ratings, allowing healthcare providers to understand and potentially override system decisions when necessary.

2.3.
Prompting Strategies for Emotional Alignment in Dialogue

Various prompting strategies have been explored to improve emotional responsiveness in dialogue systems. Persona prompts such as "supportive clinician" or "empathetic healthcare provider" can increase engagement and consistency in conversational agents
(
Zhang et al. 2018
)
, helping establish appropriate conversational boundaries and professional tone. However, effects are mixed across tasks; in factual settings, personas can fail to help or even degrade performance, potentially introducing biases or inappropriate responses
(
Hu and Collier 2024
;
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Luz de Araujo and Roth 2025
)
. The static nature of persona conditioning also limits adaptability to changing emotional contexts within a single conversation.

More sophisticated inference-time reasoning approaches like Chain of Thought have proven effective for complex reasoning tasks
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
. For empathy specifically, Chain of Empathy and Chain of Emotion introduce multi-step affective reasoning or internal emotional simulation to improve perceived naturalness and emotional coherence
(
Lee et al. 2024
;
Croissant et al. 2024
)
. We distinguish our approach from these existing methods in several key ways. First, we ground our intermediate reasoning steps in classic appraisal theory from cognitive psychology
(
Ortony et al. 1988
;
Lazarus 1991
)
, targeting the user’s likely emotions rather than simulating the system’s emotional state or relying on static personas. Second, we quantify emotion candidates with fixed Likert ratings to yield a compact, auditable profile that conditions generation. This structured approach combines the benefits of persona-based consistency with the adaptability of multi-step reasoning, supporting transparency and controllability in medical dialogue where understanding the basis for empathetic responses is essential for clinical oversight and quality assurance.

2.4.
Decomposed Reasoning and Structured Inference in LLMs

While our application targets emotional alignment, RECAP’s methodology draws from broader advances in structured reasoning and interpretable AI. Recent work demonstrates that decomposing complex inference into explicit intermediate steps improves both performance and interpretability across diverse reasoning tasks
(
Zhou et al. 2022
;
Wolfson et al. 2020
;
Tafjord et al. 2022
)
. Breaking down complex tasks into more straightforward and interpretable sub-tasks has long been a central theme in developing robust language systems, and has proven particularly effective when grounded in domain-specific frameworks
(
Feng et al. 2024
;
Feng et al. 2023
;
Zhou et al. 2024
)
.

Several advances in reasoning-augmented generation inform our architectural choices. Work on making intermediate reasoning explicit during generation shows improved accuracy and reduced hallucination
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
. Methods that enforce reasoning bottlenecks, where models must first produce interpretable reasoning paths before generating outputs, yield more reliable and auditable behavior
(
RRV et al. 2025
;
Shen et al. 2025
)
. Techniques for maintaining coherent reasoning across related problems through structured rewards
(
Ye et al. 2025
)
and frameworks that replace monolithic objectives with transparent, principle-wise components improve controllability
(
Dineen et al. 2025
)
. These approaches collectively demonstrate that externalizing cognitive structure into interpretable intermediates enhances both performance and trustworthiness.

RECAP applies these principles specifically to emotional reasoning at inference time. Like prior work on decomposition, we break down emotional assessment into interpretable components grounded in appraisal theory. Following the principle of transparent objectives, we expose per-dimension Likert ratings that make the emotional reasoning process auditable. Critically, we implement these principles without retraining, providing the adaptivity of structured reasoning with the transparency needed for clinical oversight—combining insights from reasoning-centric research with the specific demands of empathetic healthcare communication.

3.
RECAP

3.1.
Modular Prompt Design

RECAP
is a modular inference-time prompting pipeline that guides large language models through structured reasoning for emotional alignment. The pipeline operates in five sequential stages, each producing an intermediate, human-interpretable representation that informs the next stage. Our stage design is context-rich: we maximize task-relevant context so the model can make more informed decisions, consistent with gains from many-shot in-context learning and retrieval-augmented evidence
(
Agarwal et al. 2024
)
. This design requires no fine-tuning or parameter updates and externalizes the model’s reasoning into inspectable steps (see Appendix
8.3
for prompts). Figure
2
illustrates the complete pipeline. To demonstrate each stage, we use a running example where a patient expresses:

“I was just diagnosed with cancer last month and the chemo is making me so sick. I’m scared this won’t get any better. My sons keep asking me if I’m okay and I don’t know what to say to them…””

Figure 2
.
RECAP Pipeline for Emotional Alignment.
Model-agnostic inference-time prompting that externalizes emotional reasoning into interpretable stages. The pipeline systematically processes patient input through five stages: (1) abstraction to core situation, (2) identification of psychological factors, (3) enumeration of possible emotions, (4) quantitative assessment of emotion likelihoods, and (5) generation of an emotionally-aligned response that explicitly addresses the identified emotions.

3.1.1.
Stage 1: Situation Abstraction

The first stage distills the user’s raw input into a concise description of the core situation and context. Medical dialogues often contain lengthy narratives with rich detail; this stage strips away extraneous information to capture the essential circumstance affecting the user. Research on summarization shows that condensing long narratives into concise “situations” reduces cognitive load and makes downstream reasoning easier
(
Sweller 2023
;
Feng et al. 2024
)
. In this stage, the model produces a brief synopsis focusing on what has happened and who is involved, using neutral terms that set a clear foundation for subsequent emotional reasoning. This summary preserves key facts and thematic context without exact original wording.

Example abstract: “The patient has been diagnosed with a serious illness and is undergoing difficult treatment, which makes them feel overwhelmed and anxious about the future.”

3.1.2.
Stage 2: Latent Factor Induction

Following insights from many-shot in-context learning, the pipeline increases task-relevant context by explicitly eliciting psychological appraisals that shape emotional responses
(
Agarwal et al. 2024
)
. In this stage, the model names three salient factors in open vocabulary, assigns each a value, and justifies the choice from the case. These interpretable constructs, drawn from appraisal theory and common sense, parallel clinical reasoning about perceived control or available coping resources
(
Scherer and Moors 2019
)
. The appraisal-first design preserves interpretability and clinician recognizability while avoiding dependence on any fixed ontology.
The abstracted situation yields three factors:

•

Control
: Low (patient has little control over illness or treatment)

•

Support
: Moderate (family present but patient feels somewhat isolated)

•

Outcome Uncertainty
: High (patient unsure about treatment success)

By limiting to three factors, we ensure focus on the most relevant aspects while maintaining adaptability across different situations.

3.1.3.
Stage 3: Candidate Emotion Extraction

With the situation and factors established,
RECAP
generates plausible candidate emotions the user likely experiences. Rather than committing to a single emotion label, the system considers multiple possibilities that fit the scenario—typically 5–7 emotion words or phrases appropriate for someone in the described situation. Psychological research notes that real
-
world interactions often evoke mixed or blended emotions, and single
-
label measures are insufficient
(
Hepburn and Potter 2023
)
.

The identified factors lead to candidates including:
Anxiety, Fear, Overwhelm/Stress, Sadness, Hope, Guilt, Helplessness
. This explicit enumeration maintains transparency about which interpretations are being considered and provides a set of emotional themes for the final response to acknowledge.

3.1.4.
Stage 4: Likert-Based Emotion Likelihoods

Hard labels obscure ambiguity and force exclusivity when emotions co-occur; we instead rate each candidate on a fixed Likert scale mapped to probabilities (Appendix
8.1
). Probabilistic outputs capture partial belief and mixed affect, support calibrated actions, and remain auditable across turns as a belief state. This addresses a common failure mode in which LLMs are rewarded for guessing rather than expressing uncertainty, contributing to hallucinations
(
Kalai et al. 2025
)
; externalizing uncertainty as probabilities reduces overconfidence and makes confidence available to downstream policies. Prior work shows that models can estimate their own correctness and benefit from explicit uncertainty
(
Kadavath et al. 2022
)
. By employing the generated context, the resulting Likert profile is a compact, clinician-reviewable vector that conditions generation and permits calibration or abstention when confidence is low.

3.1.5.
Stage 5: Emotion-Aligned Response Generation

Guided by the emotion-likelihood context, the model opens by naming and acknowledging the highest-probability feelings, then validates or normalizes them and adjusts its tone. This sequencing follows clinical communication frameworks, which prioritize addressing emotions before information
(
Francis and Robertson 2023
)
. Only after establishing empathy does the model offer concrete guidance.

“I’m really sorry you’re feeling so overwhelmed and anxious about your diagnosis and treatment. That’s completely understandable—facing serious illness can be frightening and stressful. You’re not alone in this…”

The key distinction from baseline responses is explicit guidance to address identified emotions rather than focusing solely on factual correctness. Having decomposed the reasoning beforehand, the model has a clear map of which feelings to address and why, producing more context-sensitive empathetic language while avoiding common pitfalls like inappropriate optimism or excessive formality. The modular design keeps generation controllable, as emotional guidance can be adjusted or overridden if external emotion detection is available. The entire pipeline executes in a single conversation turn with the LLM, with each stage’s output programmatically constructing the next prompt, making real-time deployment feasible.

4.
Experiments

To assess the effectiveness of
RECAP
in aligning model responses with user emotions, we conducted evaluations on two levels: (1) standardized emotion understanding benchmarks to quantify improvements in emotion recognition and empathy reasoning, and (2) domain-specific human evaluation in medical dialogue contexts to gauge real-world impact on perceived empathy and response quality.

4.1.
Datasets and Metrics

We evaluate RECAP on multiple complementary benchmarks that assess different aspects of emotional intelligence and empathetic communication:

•

EmoBench

(
Sabour et al. 2024
)
: A psychologically grounded framework that probes emotional understanding and application through complex scenarios requiring models to infer latent affect, identify causal relationships, and generate contextually appropriate responses. Unlike simple emotion classification, EmoBench emphasizes reasoning about nuanced social contexts and implicit emotional cues.

•

SECEU
(Situational Evaluation of Complex Emotional Understanding)
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
: A standardized assessment where models distribute relative intensity across multiple candidate emotions for realistic situations. Reports two metrics: (1) raw SECEU score measuring Euclidean distance from human consensus (lower is better), and (2) EQ score, a standardized transformation onto an emotional quotient scale (mean = 100, higher is better).

•

EQ-Bench

(
Paech 2023
)
: Evaluates dialogue-centric emotional inference through conversational contexts with social dynamics. Performance is reported as an Elo-style rating facilitating relative comparisons across models.

•

HealthBench

(
Arora et al. 2025
)
: A domain-specific benchmark containing 5,000 multi-turn healthcare conversations scored against physician-written rubrics spanning accuracy, completeness, context awareness, communication quality, and instruction following. Directly measures performance in realistic health dialogues across seven themes including emergency referrals and expertise-tailored communication.

These benchmarks collectively assess theoretical understanding (EmoBench), emotional calibration (SECEU), conversational inference (EQ-Bench), and clinical communication quality (HealthBench), providing comprehensive coverage of the skills required for empathetic medical dialogue.

4.2.
Baselines

We compare
RECAP
against three prompting-based alternatives across three model scales: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
(
Grattafiori et al. 2024
)
, OpenAI-OSS-20B, and OpenAI-OSS-120B
(
OpenAI 2025
)
. We focus on open-source models due to cost efficiency (
RECAP
’s multi-step reasoning makes API costs prohibitive), reproducibility (open weights enable exact replication), and competitive performance (OSS-120B achieves near-parity with o4-mini on reasoning benchmarks and outperforms o1 and GPT-4o on HealthBench
(
OpenAI 2025
)
). All experiments use temperature 0.6, max tokens 2048, with three independent trials per condition. We evaluate three increasingly sophisticated prompting strategies that represent common approaches to emotional alignment:

Zero-shot serves as our minimal baseline, providing only the task description and input without emotion-specific guidance. For SECEU, this includes only the story and scoring instructions without any emotional priming. This tests the model’s inherent emotional understanding capabilities acquired during pretraining and instruction tuning.

Emotion Priming augments the zero-shot prompt with explicit instructions to analyze emotional states. For SECEU, we add: "Deeply analyze the provided story, focusing on the main character’s situation, actions, and any explicit or implicit emotional cues. For each of the four emotion options, critically assess its relevance and intensity concerning the character’s experience." This lightweight intervention tests whether directing attention to emotional analysis improves performance without requiring structured reasoning pipelines.

Supportive Clinician Persona employs role-based conditioning by establishing the model as "an empathetic, supportive clinician" tasked with "carefully reading the following story and evaluating the emotional state of the person." This persona-based approach aims to activate empathetic reasoning patterns through role-playing. Unlike
RECAP
, it relies on implicit conditioning rather than explicit emotional inference.

These baselines span from minimal intervention (zero-shot) through attention-based priming (emotion priming) to full persona adoption (supportive clinician), allowing us to isolate the contribution of
RECAP
’s structured multi-stage reasoning pipeline.

Unlike the prompt-based approaches above,
RECAP
employs a five-stage generation process with explicit intermediate outputs: situation abstraction, latent factor induction, factor value selection, candidate emotion extraction, and Likert-based likelihood assessment before final response generation. Each stage produces interpretable artifacts (e.g., psychological factors, emotion ratings) that guide subsequent reasoning. This transforms emotional alignment from an implicit byproduct of prompting into an explicit optimization objective with auditable reasoning traces. We report native metrics for each benchmark: accuracy for EmoBench, EQ scores for SECEU, and Elo ratings for EQ-Bench, with results in Table
2
. Full prompts for all methods are provided in Appendix
8
.

5.1.
Standardized Emotion Understanding Benchmarks

Table
2
presents comprehensive results across three emotional intelligence benchmarks and three model scales.
RECAP
demonstrates consistent improvements over baseline approaches, with particularly pronounced gains on emotion reasoning tasks that require nuanced understanding of emotional states and their intensities.

5.1.1.
EmoBench: Complex Emotion Understanding

On EmoBench’s challenging emotion understanding tasks,
RECAP
achieves notable improvements across model scales. For Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct,
RECAP
attains the highest accuracy of 0.45 compared to 0.35 for zero-shot, representing a 28% relative improvement. This gain is particularly meaningful given the complexity of EmoBench scenarios, which require models to reason about multi-party emotional dynamics and conflicting social considerations. The Emotion Priming baseline shows intermediate performance (0.423), suggesting that while simple emotional activation helps, structured reasoning provides additional benefits. At the OSS-120B scale, both
RECAP
and Emotion Priming achieve 0.825 accuracy, outperforming zero-shot (0.783) by a smaller but consistent margin. The Supportive Clinician persona shows mixed results across scales, occasionally underperforming zero-shot, indicating that role-based prompting without structured emotional reasoning may introduce biases that interfere with accurate emotion recognition.

5.1.2.
SECEU: Emotion Intensity Assessment

RECAP
demonstrates substantial improvements in overall quality at every scale: 8% for Llama-3.1-8B, 11% for OSS-20B, and 18% for OSS-120B, indicating that emotional alignment enhances medical communication quality regardless of base model capability.

The most pronounced improvements occur in Completeness (23% for OSS-20B) and Communication Quality (27% for OSS-20B), suggesting that emotional reasoning helps models provide more comprehensive responses that address both medical and emotional aspects of patient concerns. These gains reflect
RECAP
’s ability to generate responses that are not just factually complete but also appropriately toned and empathetically framed.

However,
RECAP

RECAP

RECAP

RECAP
uses the same underlying models with identical factual capabilities. Rather, they represent a prioritization shift toward emotional appropriateness in response generation. In deployment scenarios requiring strict factual precision, these trade-offs can be mitigated through verification techniques such as self-refinement or LLM-as-a-judge approaches that validate
RECAP
outputs against established medical facts while preserving emotional alignment.

These results surface a style versus precision tension common to empathy-forward systems: responses become more complete and conversationally appropriate while literal accuracy can decline. We treat this as a deployment choice rather than a defect and document the operating point explicitly; clinical contexts that prioritize strict precision can prefer baseline prompting, while patient-support contexts can opt for RECAP’s communication gains.

5.3.
Human Evaluation by Medical Professionals

We conducted a blind evaluation with two board-certified oncology research fellows on 100 synthetic patient scenarios (Section
4
), comparing
RECAP
against the Supportive Clinician baseline for emotional support quality. This evaluation specifically focused on the emotional appropriateness and supportiveness of responses in sensitive medical contexts.

RECAP
’s structured emotional reasoning pipeline demonstrated overwhelming superiority, achieving a 95.7% win rate (95% CI: [92.8%, 97.7%]) across 186 decisive outcomes (Figure
3
a). This preference was highly significant (binomial test:
p<0.001
) and remarkably consistent between evaluators: individual annotators showed 93.8% and 97.8% win rates respectively, with fair inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s
\kappa=0.306
, 86% raw agreement). The high raw agreement despite moderate kappa reflects the overwhelmingly one-sided nature of preferences, leaving little room for disagreement beyond the few edge cases.

To validate these human judgments and explore automated evaluation possibilities, we conducted parallel assessment using GPT-5. The automated evaluation strongly converged with human assessment: GPT-5 independently preferred
RECAP
in 99% of cases, assigning absolute quality scores of 8.8 vs. 4.7 for the baseline on a 10-point scale (
p<0.001
, Figure
3
b). This convergence between human experts and automated evaluation suggests that
RECAP
’s improvements are robust and recognizable across different evaluation paradigms.

The magnitude of preference, nearly unanimous among both human clinicians and automated evaluation, indicates that
RECAP
’s advantages are not subtle or situation-specific but represent fundamental improvements in emotional support quality. Qualitative review of responses reveals that
RECAP
consistently produces more nuanced emotional acknowledgment, avoiding both generic platitudes and inappropriate emotional responses. For instance, when patients express complex emotions like guilt about burdening family alongside fear about prognosis,
RECAP
explicitly addresses both emotional threads, while the baseline often provides generic reassurance that misses these nuances.

5.4.
Cross-Benchmark Analysis

Several consistent patterns emerge across our evaluation suite. First,
RECAP
’s relative advantage is inversely proportional to model scale, with the 8B model showing average improvements of 35% across emotional intelligence benchmarks versus 8% for the 120B model. This suggests that explicit emotional reasoning is particularly valuable for smaller models, helping them achieve performance comparable to much larger models without structured reasoning.

Second, the nature of improvements differs by task type. On tasks requiring emotional recognition and intensity assessment (SECEU, EQ-Bench3),
RECAP
provides consistent gains across all scales. On tasks requiring both emotional and factual reasoning (HealthBench),
RECAP
shows strong improvements in communication quality while accepting modest trade-offs in literal accuracy. This pattern aligns with
RECAP
’s design philosophy of making emotional reasoning explicit and prioritized rather than implicit and secondary.

Finally, the convergence between human clinical evaluation and automated benchmarks validates our approach. The 95.7% human preference rate, combined with consistent improvements across diverse benchmarks, demonstrates that
RECAP
’s five-stage reasoning process produces meaningful, measurable improvements in emotional alignment that translate to real-world applications in sensitive domains like healthcare.

6.
Conclusion

This work introduced
RECAP
, a modular inference-time framework that enhances emotional alignment in medical dialogue through structured, interpretable reasoning. By decomposing emotional understanding into five explicit stages,
RECAP
transforms what is typically an implicit pattern-matching task into a transparent reasoning pipeline that can be audited and adjusted without model retraining. Our experiments demonstrate that
RECAP
achieves substantial improvements across standardized emotional intelligence benchmarks, with particularly strong gains for smaller models: 22% EQ score improvement for Llama-3.1-8B on SECEU and 70-point Elo gains on EQ-Bench. More importantly, clinical evaluation by human clinicians revealed a 95.7% preference for
RECAP
-generated responses over conventional persona-based approaches, confirming that structured emotional reasoning produces meaningful improvements in real medical contexts.

The framework’s key contribution lies in its interpretability: unlike fine-tuned empathetic models or black-box alignment methods,
RECAP
exposes intermediate artifacts including psychological factors, candidate emotions, and Likert-scale likelihood ratings that provide transparent evidence for the model’s emotional reasoning. This auditability is crucial for healthcare applications where understanding why an AI responded in a particular way may be as important as the response itself.

Looking forward,
RECAP
’s modular design opens several avenues for enhancement. The intermediate emotional profiles could be integrated with clinical decision support systems, allowing physicians to review and adjust the AI’s emotional assessment before patient interaction. The framework could also be extended to track emotional trajectories across multi-turn conversations, enabling more sophisticated longitudinal support for patients managing chronic conditions.

As AI systems become increasingly prevalent in healthcare, the ability to provide not just accurate but emotionally appropriate responses will be essential for patient trust and therapeutic alliance.
RECAP
demonstrates that we need not choose between performance and interpretability; through principled decomposition of emotional reasoning, we can build AI systems that are both more empathetic and more transparent, bringing us closer to truly patient-centered digital health.

7.
Limitations

While
RECAP
demonstrates substantial improvements in emotional alignment, several limitations warrant consideration for deployment in clinical settings.

Computational Overhead.
The five-stage pipeline increases inference costs compared to single-pass generation. Each stage requires separate LLM calls with accumulated context, resulting in approximately 3-5x more tokens processed than baseline approaches. While acceptable for asynchronous medical consultations, this overhead may limit real-time applications. Future work could explore distilling the pipeline into a single fine-tuned model that implicitly performs the reasoning stages.

Error Propagation.
The modular design, while interpretable, creates potential failure points at each stage. Errors in early stages, particularly situation abstraction or factor induction, can cascade through the pipeline. For instance, if the abstraction module misses crucial context about a patient’s support system, subsequent emotion likelihood ratings may be systematically biased.

Emotion Taxonomy Constraints.
The framework relies on discrete emotion labels during the candidate extraction and likelihood assessment stages. This taxonomic approach may oversimplify complex emotional states that involve blends, ambivalence, or emotions without clear English labels. Patients experiencing grief, for example, may simultaneously feel sadness, anger, relief, and guilt in ways that resist clean categorization. While
RECAP
can identify multiple concurrent emotions, it struggles with truly mixed or conflicting emotional states.

Lack of Longitudinal Context.
The current implementation processes each interaction independently, without memory of previous emotional states or therapeutic progress. In actual clinical practice, understanding a patient’s emotional trajectory over time is crucial for appropriate support. A patient showing consistent anxiety across sessions requires different intervention than one experiencing acute situational distress. Extending
RECAP
to maintain emotional history while preserving privacy presents both technical and ethical challenges.

Evaluation sensitivity (HealthBench).
HealthBench employs strict, action-oriented success criteria. Answers that mention a medication without explicitly recommending its use are often marked incorrect. Consequently, models like
RECAP
that prioritize calibrated, cautious language can be penalized despite providing supportive guidance. Complementing HealthBench with measures of calibration, empathy, and human preference judgments would yield a more complete picture of communicative quality.

Despite these limitations,
RECAP
represents a meaningful step toward more emotionally intelligent medical AI. The framework’s modular structure facilitates targeted improvements—individual stages can be refined without overhauling the entire pipeline. As we continue developing AI for healthcare, addressing these limitations while maintaining interpretability and clinical utility remains an important direction for future research.
