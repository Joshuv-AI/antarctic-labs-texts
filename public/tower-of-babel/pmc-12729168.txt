Knowledge on iron-rich food intake and responses toward nutritional health among urban poor women of reproductive age
Authors: Md Nawal Sarwer, Md Riaj Uddin Reza, Faisal Mohammad Ahamed, Md Fazle Rabby, Laila Begum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIron deficiency anemia (IDA) is a major public health concern for women of reproductive age, especially in impoverished countries such as Bangladesh. Its occurrence is attributed to poor dietary intake, a lack of understanding, and socioeconomic status. Addressing these concerns through education and improved nutrition is critical to successful prevention. ObjectivesThe study aimed to examine the level of knowledge, dietary iron consumption, and associated factors influencing iron deficiency anemia (IDA) among women of reproductive age in Dhaka City, Bangladesh.MethodologyA cross-sectional study was conducted on 149 women of reproductive age to assess their awareness of iron deficiency anemia, daily dietary iron intake, and the impact of sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors on nutrition-related knowledge and practices. A convenience sampling method was employed due to logistical constraints. Introduction

Micronutrient inadequacies in women of reproductive age are known to impair their health, the development of their children, and the outcomes of their pregnancies [1, 2]. Women are more susceptible to micronutrient deficiencies due to poor dietary intake, limited food availability, inadequate food distribution within households, a lack of understanding of the importance of dietary diversity, and the prevalence of infectious diseases [3]. Social conventions and gender-based discrimination force women to prioritize their families over their own health and nutritional needs in many low-income nations, which raises the risk of micronutrient deficiencies in this population group [3, 4].

Micronutrients include vitamins and minerals. Micronutrients are essential for several vital bodily functions and must be obtained through a balanced diet. For example, iron and folic acid, vitamin A, iodine, zinc, calcium, B12, B6, vitamin C, vitamin E, magnesium, etc. Iron, vitamin A, iodine, folate, and zinc deficiencies are the most typical in women [5]. Iron deficiency is well-documented to have negative effects on the productivity and cognition of the general population. It is also the main cause of anemia during pregnancy, contributing to 20% of all maternal and neonatal deaths, as well as low birth weight [6, 7].

Iron is present in foods such as heme iron (from meat, poultry, and fish) and non-heme iron (from cereals, legumes, fruits, and vegetables). Iron, a trace mineral essential for human health, is required for the synthesis of heme (a component of hemoglobin—the body’s primary oxygen carrier) and iron-containing enzymes and for neurological development in the fetus and during infancy [8, 9]. Heme iron has a high absorption rate, and dietary factors rarely significantly affect its bioavailability. Non-heme iron, which is the predominant type of dietary iron, has a lower absorption rate [10]. Additionally, non-heme iron is more readily absorbed when consumed with meat, fish, or seafood [11]. According to estimates, only one-tenth of non-heme iron and one-quarter of heme iron are bioavailable from food sources [12, 13].

To address the nutritional needs of adolescent girls and pregnant and lactating women, the UN Sustainable Development Goals have acknowledged the substantial nutritional burden that women bear by 2030 [14]. Data on micronutrient status and intake in Women of Reproductive Age (WRA) and Pregnant Women (PW) are crucial for developing public health plans and monitoring programs to achieve these goals. This study analyzes current information on the micronutrient status and dietary iron intake among urban poor women of reproductive age in Bangladesh.

Study population and design

This study was conducted in the Zakir Hossain Road Town Hall Camp Basti and Geneva Camp-1 in Dhaka, Bangladesh. Women of reproductive age were recruited for this cross-sectional study. A total of one hundred and forty-nine (149) women of reproductive age (15–49 years old) were recruited for this cross-sectional study. All participants who provided consent were eligible for this study.

Questionnaire

A structured questionnaire was used to assess knowledge of iron food intake or nutrition practices. It was adapted from documents by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) titled “Guidelines for Assessing Nutrition-related Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices” [15] and “Dietary Assessment: A Resource Guide to method selection and application in low resource settings” [16].

Assessing the sociodemographic and Socio-economic characteristics

The Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey 2017-18 [17] was used to assess the sociodemographic and socio-economic characteristics of the participants. Factors such as district of residence, slum area name, participant name, age, religion, marital status, occupation, education level, household head’s name, relation and occupation, monthly income, and expenses were considered. Factors such as the women’s education level, the household head’s occupation, and monthly income and expenses were used to determine the socioeconomic status of the household.

Assessing the knowledge of iron

To evaluate the level of iron knowledge, eight questions that were modified from the FAO guidelines were employed [15]. The questionnaire was divided into the following categories: (a) General signs of iron-deficiency anemia; (b) Consequences of iron-deficiency anemia for infants and young children (c) Consequences of iron-deficiency anemia for pregnant women; (d) Causes of iron-deficiency anemia; (e) prevention of it; (f) foods high in iron; (g) foods that improve iron absorption; and (h) foods that decrease it.

Dietary assessment

To evaluate the dietary assessment using Food Frequency Questionnaires (FFQ), a semi-quantitative method from the FAO guidelines was employed [16]. The participants were asked to report their usual frequency of consumption of each food from a list of iron-rich foods over a specific period (daily, 1–2 times per week, 3–4 times per week, 1–3 times per month, less than once a month). Iron-rich food lists were prepared by aggregating highly iron-rich foods from the Bangladeshi Food Composition Table. Portion sizes of intake were collected from the participants using standard household measures. The Bangladeshi Food Composition Table was used to obtain iron composition data for reported foods.

Statistical analysis plan

The study used SPSS version 29.0 software for data analysis, using descriptive statistics and mean and frequency tables. Knowledge tests regarding iron were conducted, with scores assigned based on the accuracy of responses. The FAO’s recommendations for measuring nutrition-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices were used. Descriptive statistics were used to evaluate sociodemographic data, knowledge of dietary iron, symptoms of iron deficiency, and the effects of iron deficiency and anemia. A food frequency questionnaire was used to understand the dietary intake of reproductive-age women.

Approximately 75.8% of participants were able to sign their names. Among them, 24.17% had completed class 5, 4.69% had passed the SSC, 2.01% had passed the HSC, and 38.92% had never attended school. Regarding their relationship with the household head, 79.2% of participants identified themselves as spouses, 8.7% identified themselves as partners and 6.7% identified themselves as fathers or mothers. The most common occupation for household heads (29.5%) was store employee. According to Tables 1, 11.4% of the household heads were employed in ‘Job’ categories, and 16.8% were in ‘Business’. 56.4% of participants reported having a household income between 5,001 and 10,000 BDT per month.

Additionally, 30.9% reported incomes between 10,000 and 20,000 BDT, and 11.4% had incomes less than 5,000 BDT. The average monthly household income was 10,093.69 BDT. Similarly, most participants (57%) had monthly household expenditures between 5,001 and 10,000 BDT. 12.1% had expenditures less than 5,000 BDT, and 30.2% were in the 10,000–20,000 BDT range. The average monthly expenditure for households was 9,771.81 BDT. According to the Preliminary Report of the Household Income and Expenditure Survey (HIES-2022), the average monthly household income and expenditure in urban areas are 45,757 and 41,424 BDT, respectively. At the national level, the average monthly household income and expenditure were 32,422 and 31,500 BDT, respectively.

When questioned about their knowledge, 40.2% of the study participants reported awareness of the general symptoms of iron-deficiency anemia. Regarding knowledge of iron deficiency anemia, 42.2% of participants were aware of its causes, and 41.6% knew about its prevention. Specifically, 40.9% of participants were aware of the effects of iron deficiency anemia on women, 39.6% were aware of its effects on pregnant women, and 36.9% were aware of its effects on lactating women. Only 31.5% were aware of the effects of iron deficiency anemia on infants and young children. The study also found that 81.2% of participants were known to consume fish and seafood, 75.8% were aware of consuming organ meat, and 74.5% were aware of consuming flesh meat. Additionally, 80% of the participants knew about foods that decreased iron absorption, and 67.8% knew about foods that increased iron absorption. The median overall knowledge score for participants was 6.45, as shown in Table 2.

Discussion

The consumption of iron-rich foods has a significant impact on blood iron levels and the status of anemia. Anemia is a primary public health concern in Bangladesh and worldwide. In Bangladesh, the majority of women have experienced anemia at some point in their lifetime. For the past 30 years, the prevalence of anemia in Bangladesh has remained relatively constant at around 74%. The high anemia rate constitutes a significant public health problem in Bangladesh, resulting in productivity losses equivalent to 1.9% of the country’s GDP [19]. In this study, reproductive-age Bangladeshi women from an urban region were evaluated for their dietary intake of iron-rich foods and their nutritional health responses. The findings show that most study participants lacked knowledge regarding the general signs of iron-deficiency anemia.

Education plays a crucial role in knowledge acquisition. Approximately 59% of the participants were educated. However, the overall knowledge score was not satisfactory. Figure 1 shows that only 53.75% of participants had adequate knowledge of iron-deficiency anemia. Less than 50% of participants were aware of the consequences of iron-deficiency anemia for women.

Comparison between low usual Iron intake and knowledge about Iron-deficiency anemia

Furthermore, less than 40% of participants were knowledgeable about the consequences of iron deficiency anemia for pregnant women, lactating women, infants, and young children. Knowledge about the causes of iron-deficiency anemia and its prevention was also below 50%. However, the majority of participants were knowledgeable about iron-rich food lists, foods that increase iron absorption, and foods that decrease iron absorption. This study reported that urban women were generally knowledgeable about iron-rich food lists, foods that increase absorption, and foods that decrease iron absorption. Similar findings were observed in [20].

The mean total daily iron intake in this study was lower than that reported in a study conducted in Gondar’s urban population, which found a mean daily iron consumption of 97.81 mg/d [21]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in malnutrition prevalence between urban areas of Ethiopia and Bangladesh. Furthermore, the study’s accuracy in estimating total dietary iron intake may be limited. This is because biochemical markers of iron amount and storage were not measured, and a semi-quantitative FFQ was used. While this method is useful for differentiating between high and low-nutrient consumers, it is not the most accurate method for measuring absolute intake.

As expected, participants in the study had inadequate diets, particularly concerning both heme and non-heme iron sources. Figure 2 shows that participants mostly eat non-heme iron food items.

Daily iron consumption among urban poor women of reproductive age.

The average daily intake of iron was 10.95 mg, and 100% of WRA were at risk of inadequate iron intake. The occupation of household heads and participants, along with low household income, was linked to an increased risk of iron deficiency. In almost all countries, from 61% in Ireland to 90–97% in Belgium, Denmark, Hungary, Serbia, and Sweden, more than half of women of reproductive age had dietary iron intakes below 15 mg/day [22]. Thus, this study revealed that participants’ knowledge of iron deficiency anemia did not adequately align with the daily required dietary iron intake. In contrast, the Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for iron is 18 mg/day for women aged 19–50 [23].

Insufficient iron consumption has a significant impact on a woman’s life, future pregnancies, and delivery outcomes. Additionally, premature delivery, stillbirth, and low birth weight are more likely to occur in pregnancies where the mother’s iron reserves and intake are insufficient [24]. Additionally, premature delivery, stillbirth, and low birth weight babies are more likely to occur in pregnancies where the iron reserves and intake of the mother are insufficient (ref). Babies born to anemic women often experience slower brain development, impacting future educational and social development, along with poorer physical growth performance and an increased risk of infection [25]. As a result, addressing this issue is crucial.

The risk of dietary iron deficiency was also shown to be greater in women with less diversified diets. This may be explained by the tendency of impoverished populations to consume less varied and often unhealthy diets [26].

This study has several limitations. It can be challenging to establish causality due to its cross-sectional nature. The specific study environment may impact the generalizability of the results to different contexts. Recall bias may be present in the use of self-reported instruments, which rely on participant memory and may be influenced by social desirability. The study advances our understanding of the dietary habits of women of reproductive age, including their consumption of foods high in iron and the potential contributing factors. The findings inform the development of interventions to enhance the eating habits of women of reproductive age in research settings and other developing nations with similar contexts.

Conclusion

This study found that, despite varying levels of awareness regarding the importance of iron in their diets, a significant gap exists between knowledge and actual dietary iron intake among urban poor women of reproductive age; indeed, 100% of participants had low daily iron consumption. To address this critical issue, strengthening healthcare systems and facilities, as well as enhancing women’s awareness of anemia and its prevention, are crucial.

Specifically, our findings that 100% of participants had low daily iron intake underscore the need for interventions focusing on improving dietary diversity, increasing the consumption of animal-based foods, and encouraging the consumption of fruits with meals to enhance iron absorption. The mixed knowledge levels observed (e.g., higher awareness of iron-rich foods and lower awareness of consequences of IDA for infants) highlight the importance of targeted and comprehensive nutrition and health education initiatives. These initiatives aim to raise awareness and improve dietary behaviors regarding iron deficiency anemia, addressing both knowledge gaps and practical applications.

Primary healthcare facilities should provide training for healthcare workers to effectively diagnose and manage anemia. Global nutritional education programs should be disseminated to reach all urban societal groups, promoting a culture of awareness about the importance of a balanced diet and iron-rich foods in preventing anemia. Given the significant influence of socioeconomic level and education on awareness and dietary behavior, these programs should also reach pregnant women, breastfeeding mothers, adolescent girls, and teenage mothers, educating them about the cost-effectiveness of consuming healthy vegetables and fruits. Their dietary choices have a direct impact on their health and that of their children. Emphasizing the long-term benefits of nutrient-rich foods can build positive attitudes toward nutrition.

Addressing systemic variables contributing to anemia among reproductive-age women requires coordination among various groups. Emphasizing the prevention of anemia and investing in infrastructure can promote women’s health and well-being. Enhancing the healthcare system, ensuring access to iron supplements and fortified meals, and implementing frequent screening and monitoring systems are essential. Community participation and the integration of various stakeholders can also help resolve social and cultural barriers.

In conclusion, this study highlights the urgent need for comprehensive efforts to prevent anemia among women of reproductive age. Reforms in healthcare systems and facilities, along with increased awareness among women, are crucial. Strengthening primary facilities and providing training to healthcare staff can help address early prevention and intervention. Disseminating nutritional education empowers women to make informed decisions about their food, promoting healthy eating habits and the consumption of iron-rich foods. Emphasizing the long-term benefits of a balanced diet can inspire women to prioritize their nutritional needs and adopt more nutritious eating habits.
