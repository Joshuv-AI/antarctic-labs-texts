Early-life immune imprinting by a B. infantis-HMO synbiotic shapes infant immune signatures and protects against experimental respiratory disease
Authors: Ridwan B Rashid, Cheong Kwong Chung, Patricia Namubiru, Tufael Ahmed, CLARA Study Group, Daniel R Howard, Ismail Sebina, Tyler Wilkins, Md Sohel Rana, Joshua P Morrow, Darren J Creek, Janne Marie Moll, Karine Vidal, Cecilia Fumero, Luca Lavalle, Dominick Maes, Nicholas P Hays, Irma Silva-Zolezzi, Elizabeth Forbes-Blom, Norbert Sprenger, Jean-Charles Picaud, Simon Phipps, Mario Noti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEarly microbial colonization influences immune development, with lower abundance of human milk oligosaccharide (HMO)-utilizing bifidobacteria linked to immune-related disorder risk. Here, we demonstrate that synbiotic supplementation with Bifidobacterium infantis (LMG 11588) and a blend of six structurally diverse HMOs plus Bifidobacterium lactis (CNCM I-3446) altered gut microbiome composition, changed systemic immune cell-networks, and reduced persistence of a T helper 2 (Th2) bias in formula-fed infants, following a pattern observed in breastfed infants. In preclinical models, synbiotic reduces lower respiratory tract infection (LRI) severity and aberrant type-2 immune responses and confers lasting immune benefits into adulthood. These effects are associated with changes in the circulating metabolome, including increased 12(S)-hydroxyheptadecatrienoic acid (12-HHT), which correlates with improved infection outcomes and phenocopies synbiotic-mediated protection when administered orally. Together, these findings indicate that infant-type synbiotic supplementation during a critical early-life window can imprint immune function and promote disease tolerance.Keywords: synbiotic, infant-type bifidobacteria, B. infantis, HMOs, breastfeeding, bronchiolitis, RSV, asthma, immune imprinting, microbiome-host interactions

Introduction

Newborns face major challenges during the transition from the protected intrauterine environment to the outside world.1,2 This requires the immune system to defend against pathogens while remaining tolerant to beneficial microbes and essential nutrients.3 Increasing evidence supports the existence of critical early-life windows that favor healthy immune development.4,5 Factors such as maternal health, mode of birth and feeding, antibiotic exposure, and environmental influences shape early microbial colonization and long-term immune function.6 Bifidobacteria, among the first infant gut colonizers, utilize human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) to drive microbiota maturation and support immune development.6,7 These infant-type bifidobacteria act as ecosystem engineers by enabling microbial succession via metabolic cross-feeding.8 Disruption of these highly dynamic colonization patterns can impair coordinated microbiome-host interactions, alter immune trajectories, and increase disease susceptibility across the life course.9,10,11 Consistently, severe lower respiratory tract infections (sLRIs) in infancy are linked to impaired lung function, later chronic respiratory diseases (e.g., asthma), and increased all-cause mortality.12,13

Human milk represents a unique source of nutrition, providing a selective advantage for the colonization of specific infant-type bifidobacteria specialized to metabolize HMOs.14,15 Among these, Bifidobacterium longum subspecies infantis (B. infantis) is highly specialized to metabolize structurally diverse HMOs to produce a range of metabolites, which in turn may benefit the infant by modulating immune function and disease tolerance in early life.16,17 Observational studies have identified a link between the abundance of B. infantis in early life and atopic disorders, with lower abundances representing a risk factor for developing chronic inflammatory disease.18 In line with the above, low abundance of B. infantis in infancy correlates with enteric inflammation, and supplementation of B. infantis has been reported to silence T helper 2 (Th2) and Th17 immune responses in breastfed infants.6 This effect was most pronounced in exclusively breastfed infants, providing further evidence for the hypothesis of a synbiotic effect of the combination of HMOs and B. infantis on immune system modulation.19 Gaining a deeper understanding of coordinated microbiome-immune interactions in early life will enhance/accelerate biotic intervention strategies to support appropriate immune development and resilience against risk factors linked to the onset of chronic inflammatory disorders.

Here, we assessed the impact of supplementation with a specific synbiotic blend in early life on systemic immune cell profiles in a sub-cohort of a randomized controlled double-blind infant formula trial.20 For this, we used a formulation including an infant-type synbiotic tailored to infants and young children reflecting the composition of HMOs in breast milk and supporting the development of the gut microbiota in early life, in combination with an infant-type Bifidobacterium, B. infantis (specialized to metabolize HMOs), + B. lactis known for their immunomodulatory properties. We demonstrate that supplementation with this infant-type synbiotic changes systemic immune cell networks and reduces a Th2 bias in formula fed infants—to more closely follow the pattern observed in exclusively breastfed infants enrolled as a non-randomized reference group. To gain further mechanistic insight, we evaluated the impact of early-life synbiotic supplementation on preclinical models of respiratory infection and allergic airway inflammation. We find that synbiotic supplementation improves disease tolerance toward an sLRI associated with aberrant Th2 responses in early life and reduces susceptibility to experimental asthma later in life. The improved infection and allergy outcomes following synbiotic supplementation associate with significant changes in the levels of systemic metabolites and the expansion of immunoregulatory plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) and regulatory T cell (Treg) responses. Together, these findings support the concept that during a critical developmental window in early life an infant-type synbiotic has the capacity to shape immune system imprinting and prevent the development of a severe infection and subsequent asthma via the promotion of disease tolerance.

Results

Supplementation with an infant-type synbiotic modulates systemic immune signatures in infants

In a sub-cohort of a randomized, controlled, double-blind multicenter European infant formula trial (clinicaltrials.gov, NCT04962594,20), we assessed the impact of early-life supplementation with an infant-type synbiotic consisting of B. infantis (LMG 11588) and a blend of structurally diverse HMOs plus Bifidobacterium lactis (B. lactis, CNCM I-3446) on systemic immune signatures and immune cell networks in the first 4 months of life. For the full study, healthy infants aged ≤14 days were randomly assigned to either a control formula (CTRL; n = 117, partially hydrolyzed 100% whey-based formula) or an experimental formula (SYN; n = 119, CTRL supplemented with 1.77g/L of 6 HMOs [2′FL, DFL, 3FL, LNT, 3′SL, 6′SL] and B. infantis plus B. lactis; Table S1). Exclusively breastfed infants to 4 months of age were enrolled as a non-randomized reference arm (BF; n = 82). The primary and key secondary objectives, reported separately through age 4 months, showed that SYN supported healthy, infant-type growth, good gastrointestinal tolerance, and the expansion of beneficial bifidobacteria.20 Blood samples were collected on a voluntary basis from CTRL (n = 39), SYN (n = 35), and BF (n = 16, reference) infants at 4 months of age (Figure 1A). The baseline demographics in this sub-cohort, including age, sex, weight, length, head circumference, delivery mode and number of siblings were comparable between experimental groups, indicating that randomization was maintained (Table S2). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated from venous blood and stained using a pre-validated 17-plex biomarker panel for immune cell profiling. Chip Cytometry, allowing for high-quality imaging and advanced image analyses, was used to quantify the relative abundance of 29 immune cell populations in the blood of CTRL, SYN, and BF infants. At 4 months of age, following a pattern observed in BF, the relative abundance of Th2 cells is significantly decreased in SYN compared with CTRL (p = 0.033, Figure 1B). For Th17 and Tregs, we observe a similar pattern with lower abundances in BF and SYN not reaching statistical significance, while Th1 cell abundance was similar between all feeding groups (Figure 1B). The relative abundance of the other 25 assessed immune cell populations is not statistically different between SYN and CTRL (Figure S1).

Next, we conducted correlation analyses to investigate a potential association between gut barrier integrity-related biomarkers and microbiome characteristics measured at 3 months of age and the observed abundances of T cell subsets (Th1, Th2, Th17, and Treg) at 4 months of age (prospective immune phenotype analyses). To this end, we focused on the biomarkers calprotectin and alpha-1-antitrypsin (AAT), measured key short-chain fatty acids ([SCFAs] acetate, propionate, butyrate), as well as bacterial taxa that were significantly different between the two randomized feeding groups CTRL and SYN in this sub-cohort (Figure 2). Consistent with the more balanced Th2 and Th17 states in SYN, synbiotic supplementation results in a significant reduction of the intestinal barrier biomarker AAT in SYN relative to CTRL (p = 0.046). This reduction follows a pattern observed in the BF reference group (Figure 2A), which was also observed in the complete trial.20 No significant differences are observed for the intestinal inflammatory biomarker calprotectin or the concentration of acetate, propionate, and butyrate in the two randomized feeding groups (Figures 2A and 2B).

Next, we conducted correlation analyses to assess the relationships between fecal metabolites, fecal biomarkers, gut microbial taxa, and systemic T cell subsets (Figure 2C). Correlations are shown for all randomized formula-fed infants combined (CTRL + SYN) to capture global immune-microbial and immune-metabolite relationships and subsequently stratified by CTRL and SYN to assess group-specific associations. When stratified by feeding group, correlation analyses reveal distinct SCFA-immune association patterns between CTRL and SYN. In CTRL infants, butyrate and propionate show predominantly negative correlations (blue) with T cell subsets, reaching nominal significance for Th2 cells and extending to Th17 cells and Tregs, with propionate showing false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted negative association with Tregs. In contrast, SYN-supplemented infants display a reoriented and more coordinated pattern, with SCFAs showing positive correlations (red) across assessed T cell subsets; notably, acetate is positively associated with Th2 cells at nominal significance. A divergent pattern is also observed for calprotectin, which is negatively associated with Th2 and Th17 cells (FDR significant) and Tregs (nominal) in CTRL infants, but shows positive associations (red) with T cell subsets in SYN. Together, these findings indicate that SYN supplementation may strengthen the functional coupling between microbial-derived SCFAs and systemic T cell responses.

Th1, Th2, Th17, and Treg cells are positively associated (red) with B. infantis abundance in SYN-supplemented infants, reaching nominal significance for Th1 cells and FDR-adjusted significance for Tregs. Further, the opportunistic pathobionts Rothia, Peptoniphilus C, Peptococcales, Mycobacteriales, and Erysipelotrichales exhibit significantly higher changes in abundance in CTRL compared with SYN. In contrast, Enterococcus D gallinarum, Parabacteroides merdeae, UMGS1071sp900542375, and Blautia A wexleraea, which have a significantly higher abundance in SYN compared with CTRL, exhibit a negative correlation (blue) with Th2 cells. Among the latter, the next-generation probiotic candidate B. wexlerae was previously identified to play a protective role from later allergies.21 Parabacteroides merdae was additionally identified to be causally related to atopic dermatitis using Mendelian randomization.22 Gemella haemolysans B and Streptococcus thermophilus, both with a significantly higher abundance in SYN compared with CTRL, positively correlate with Th subsets. Further, S. thermophilus, reported to have strong immunomodulatory and anti-allergenic properties,23 displays a nominally significant positive correlation with Th2 cells (Figure 2C).

To further explore the interactions between immune cell subsets, we used Spearman correlation matrices to identify modules of differentially regulated immune cell populations in the blood of the two randomized feeding groups CTRL and SYN. In these analyses, we find positive correlations of Th1 cells with Th2, Th17, Treg cells, and CD38+ plasmablasts (short-lived antibody-producing cells) in SYN, but not CTRL, infants (Figure S2). Conversely, in CTRL, but not SYN, naive CD4+ T cells negatively correlate with naive CD8+ T cells and central memory, effector memory, and effector CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. Together, these findings show that early-life supplementation with SYN can shape systemic immune signatures in healthy formula-fed infants, including reduced Th2 abundance and coordinated immune regulatory networks that may be linked to beneficial immune development.

SYN supplementation ameliorates LRI severity in a neonatal mouse model of respiratory infection

Although a long-term follow-up of infants for immune-related outcomes in the randomized controlled trial would be valuable, the small sample size, high dropout rates over time, and various confounding factors (e.g., dietary influences and antibiotic exposures) prohibited this. To overcome these limitations, we employed a high-fidelity preclinical model that controls for genetics, diet, and microbial and environmental exposures, enabling us to functionally validate potential short- and long-term health benefits of clinically observed synbiotic-driven immune modulation in early life. To simulate the human epidemiology associating sLRI in early life with increased risk of later-life chronic respiratory disease,24,25 we employed an established model of neonatal viral airway infection that increases susceptibility to allergic airway inflammation in later life.26,27 Using a maternal low-fiber diet (LFD) setting that predisposes to sLRI via distortion of the microbiota and perturbed immune maturation in the offspring,27 thereby mimicking a real-world risk trajectory, neonatal mice reared to LFD-fed mothers were supplemented with either a synbiotic (SYN), consisting of B. infantis (LMG11588) and the 6HMO clinical mix, or its individual components, namely, 6HMOs (PRE) or B. infantis (PRO) from postnatal day (PND) 5 to PND 20 (Figure 3A). At PND 10, mice were inoculated with pneumonia virus of mice ([PVM], murine respiratory syncytial virus homolog) to assess LRI severity. Importantly, maternal LFD feeding did not alter the weight of pregnant mothers or neonates (hereafter referred to as “LFD-reared neonates”) at birth comparable to controls fed a high-fiber diet ([HFD], data not shown). In response to PVM infection, and compared with control HFD-reared neonates, LFD-reared neonates present with stunted weight gain, indicative of severe disease (Figure 3B). At 10 days post-infection (dpi), when the hallmark pathologies of sLRI peak,26,27,28 airway epithelial sloughing, mucus hypersecretion, and airway smooth muscle remodeling are significantly elevated in PVM-infected LFD-reared neonates compared with HFD controls (Figures 3C–3E; Figures S3A–S3C). Strikingly, SYN supplementation is sufficient to lower infection-induced growth stunting and decreased the hallmark pathologies of sLRI in susceptible LFD-reared neonates (Figures 3B–3E; Figures S3A–S3C). In contrast, PRE or PRO were without effect. At 5 dpi, the peak of infection in this model, SYN supplementation prevents the loss of viral control that develops in LFD-reared neonates,27 whereas PRE or PRO had no effect (Figure 3F). At 10 dpi, viral protein was undetectable in all groups (data not shown), consistent with our previous observations relating to the kinetics of viral clearance.27 Taken together, these results demonstrate that early-life supplementation with an infant-type synbiotic reduces LRI severity in susceptible neonates, with SYN supplementation showing superior efficacy compared with PRE or PRO supplementation alone.

Our previous studies have demonstrated that maternal LFD feeding predisposes to sLRI in the offspring due to the delayed expansion of pDCs and Treg cells in the lungs.26,27 To assess whether the observed increase in disease tolerance in SYN-supplemented LFD-reared neonates correlated with a restoration of pDCs and Treg cells at 5 dpi and the prevention of an overzealous immune response at 10 dpi, the lungs and mediastinal lymph nodes (MLNs) of the control and biotic supplementation groups (PRE, PRO, and SYN) were excised and immune cells quantified (gating strategy shown in Figures S4A and S4B). In line with reduced morbidity (Figures 3B–3F), early-life SYN supplementation restores the compromised pDC and Treg cell numbers in both the lungs (Figures 3G and 3H) and MLNs (Figures S5A and S5B) of LFD-reared mice at 5 dpi. Similar trends are observed for conventional DC1 (cDC1) and cDC2 numbers, particularly in the MLNs (Figures S5C–S5F). As we previously observed an association between DC hematopoiesis and expression of the non-redundant DC-active growth factor Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 ligand (Flt3L) in the gut,27 we next measured Flt3L concentrations in response to biotic supplementation. SYN, but not PRE or PRO, supplementation led to a significant increase of Flt3L protein concentrations in the small intestine (Figure 3I), consistent with the restoration of DC hematopoiesis.

Supplementation with SYN, but not PRE or PRO, induces a significant increase in CD4+, but not CD8+ T cells in the lungs of LFD-reared mice at 5 dpi (Figures 3J and 3K), while both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells are significantly elevated in the MLNs (Figures S5G and S5H). In the absence of a timely and controlled expansion of immune cells at the peak of infection, the host response to PVM becomes over-exuberant, and it is this excessive inflammation and consequent immunopathology, despite the absence of detectable virus, that causes disease morbidity.26,27 In line with this, at 10 dpi, the numbers of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Figure 3L and 3M), as well as pDCs, Treg cells, and cDC1 and cDC2 cells (Figures S6A–S6D) are significantly lower in the lungs of SYN-supplemented neonates compared with the susceptible control LFD-reared neonates, with similar findings in the MLNs (Figures S6E–S6J).

sLRIs can initiate aberrant Th2-driven inflammation, with longitudinal studies revealing that heightened type-2 inflammation in infants hospitalized with viral LRIs have an increased risk of developing asthma in later stages of life.29 At 10 dpi, the numbers of CD4+ Th2 cells and type-2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2), key cellular drivers of type-2 inflammation, are significantly higher in the lungs and MLNs of LFD- compared with HFD-reared neonates (Figures 3N and 3O; Figures S6K and S6L). Early-life SYN supplementation decreases both cell populations and elicits a superior effect relative to PRE or PRO supplementation on ILC2 numbers, but not Th2 cells. SYN supplementation is further associated with reduced TNFα and increased IFN-γ protein levels at 10 dpi in the lungs, reflecting changes in the inflammatory milieu (Figures 3P and 3Q). Collectively, these findings indicate that supplementation with an infant-type synbiotic alters the kinetics of the immune response in susceptible mice to more closely align with that observed in protected controls. While PRE and PRO elicit measurable effects on select cellular and histological parameters, SYN shows the most consistent improvements on clinically and pathologically meaningful outcomes, including reduced viral burden at the peak of infection, restoration of immunoregulatory pDCs and Tregs, and a marked decrease in morbidity and immunopathology.

Synbiotic supplementation alters gut microbiota composition and circulating metabolome correlating with improved infection outcomes

We have previously demonstrated that the increased sLRI susceptibility of LFD-reared neonates is associated with a perturbed gut microbiome and metabolome.27 Therefore, we investigated whether SYN supplementation to susceptible LFD-reared neonates alters the gut microbiome and metabolite profiles toward HFD-reared protected controls. To do so, we profiled the fecal microbiota and serum metabolites in PVM-infected neonates and assessed the compositional changes in response to SYN, PRE, or PRO at the peak of infection (5 dpi). Alpha diversity metrics for richness (observed features), evenness (Pielou’s evenness), or Shannon’s entropy are not affected by SYN supplementation (Figures S7A–S7C). Fecal microbiota composition evaluated by beta diversity (weighted UniFrac distance) of LFD-reared neonates is different compared with HFD-reared controls but unaffected by biotic supplementations (Figure S7D). To address fecal microbiota compositional changes in the susceptible groups in response to biotic supplementations, we next performed pairwise comparisons (Figures S7E–S7H). In these comparisons, B. infantis is significantly enriched in SYN, but not the PRE and PRO supplementation groups (Figure 4A). Because B. infantis occurred at very low relative abundance in fecal samples, making proportional values misleading, we reported amplicon sequence variant counts as they more accurately capture biologically meaningful, probiotic engraftment relevant to infection outcomes. The enhanced abundance of B. infantis upon SYN, but not PRO, supplementation is in line with previous reports highlighting the reliance of B. infantis on HMOs for engraftment.30,31 Further, SYN supplementation leads to the enrichment of several bacterial taxa including Lawsonibacter sp000492175 (Figures S7F–S7H), a member of the Lawsonibacter genus, which have been previously shown to be depleted in patients with respiratory infections.32,33

To assess the changes in metabolic profiles linked to different biotic supplementations (PRE, PRO, and SYN), we performed liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) on the serum of PVM-infected neonates at 5 dpi, given the influx of protective pDCs and Treg cells has been initiated in protected mice by this time. Similar to the fecal microbiota profile, the global blood metabolome of LFD-reared neonates clustered differently from HFD-reared neonates and overlapped with LFD-reared animals supplemented with PRE, PRO, or SYN (Figure 4B). Among 1,142 putative compounds in an untargeted metabolomics screen, 611 metabolites are differentially abundant between HFD- and LFD-reared neonates (Figure 4C; Figure S8A). As SYN supplementation provided superior protection against PVM infection relative to PRE or PRO supplementation alone (i.e., reversing body weight stunting, decreasing viral load, and restoring pDC and Tregs), we conducted further investigations by associating the metabolites with sLRI (i.e., protection or susceptibility). Using Metaboanalyst 6.0,34 we constructed an UpSet plot to identify a subset of metabolites showing consistent directional differences across comparisons between groups classified as phenotypically protected (HFD-reared neonates and SYN-supplemented LFD-reared neonates) and phenotypically susceptible (LFD-reared neonates and PRE- or PRO-supplemented LFD-reared neonates; Figure 4C). A total of 14 metabolites met these criteria and are highlighted for further association analyses. A heatmap of these 14 metabolites showed tight intra-group clustering (Figure 4D). Among the 7 metabolites that were associated with protection (Figure 4E), 12-hydroxyheptadecatrienoic acid (12-HHT) and N-docosanoyl-pentadecasphing-4-enine-1-phosphoethanolamine significantly correlate with protection from sLRI (p < 0.05, Figures S8B and S8C). Of note, the peak intensity of the highest ranked metabolite 12-HHT is significantly higher in the protected compared with the susceptible groups and significantly higher in SYN relative to PRE- or PRO-supplemented neonates (Figure 4F). Conversely, the metabolites {[1-(4-methoxyphenyl)pentan-3-yl]oxy}sulfonic acid and N-acetyl-a-neuraminic acid are more abundant in the susceptible groups, and SYN supplementation is sufficient to significantly decrease the levels of these putative disease-associated metabolites (Figures S8D–S8F). Collectively, our findings indicate that SYN supplementation is associated with selective effects on specific gut microbial taxa and systemic metabolites, with protection against sLRIs linked to defined metabolite features rather than global alterations of the gut microbiome.

SYN supplementation in early life decreases later-life susceptibility to pollution-enhanced allergic airway inflammation

Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that sLRIs in early life are a major independent risk factor for the later development of asthma.13,24,35,36,37 To mimic such a scenario, we exposed previously PVM-infected mice to an allergic sensitization protocol in the presence of particulate matter (PM2.5), an environmental pollutant known for its ability to induce asthma exacerbations.38,39,40 LFD-reared neonates were infected with PVM (PND 10) prior to sensitization with cockroach allergen extract (CRE) in the presence of PM2.5 starting at PND 42 (PM-CRE experimental asthma, Figure 5A). Remarkably, SYN supplementation, restricted to the neonatal period, confers lasting protection against the subsequent development of allergic airway inflammation. Specifically, we observe a significant reduction in infiltrating eosinophils and classical and SiglecF+ neutrophils to the lungs of mice supplemented with SYN in early life compared with controls (Figures 5B and 5C; Figures S9A and S9B) that is paralleled by reduced airway smooth muscle remodeling (Figures 5D and 5E). Consistent with the curtailed granulocytic inflammation, the type-2 cellular response (i.e., ILC2 and Th2 cells) and associated mucus hypersecretion are significantly ameliorated in the lungs of adult mice that had been supplemented with SYN in early life (Figures 5F–5I). In line with the observed synergistic effect of 6HMOs together with B. infantis in reducing LRI severity (Figures 3C–3E), the protective effect of SYN on pollution-enhanced allergic airway inflammation is more pronounced than that observed with PRE or PRO supplementation (Figure 5; Figure S9). In summary, our findings indicate that early-life SYN supplementation markedly decreases the severity of sLRIs and associated type-2 inflammation in early life and has long-lasting beneficial effects that persist into adulthood, even in the absence of continued SYN supplementation.

SYN supplementation in early life alters select gut microbial taxa and systemic metabolome in later life

To test whether the protective effects of SYN on later-life development of allergic airway inflammation were associated with specific gut microbial signatures and metabolite profiles in adulthood, we profiled fecal samples for microbiota structure and the sera for metabolites at PND 66 (the age at which asthma-like pathologies were assessed). The fecal microbiota composition of HFD-reared neonates shows distinct clustering compared with LFD-reared neonates and was structurally different in adulthood (PND 66; Figures S10A–S10E). However, early-life biotic supplementations (PRE, PRO, or SYN) do not induce significant changes to either alpha or beta diversity in adulthood (Figures S10A–S10D). In line with the cessation of SYN supplementation at weaning, the increased B. infantis abundance in early life (Figure 4A) does not persist into adulthood, similar to clinical observations where B. infantis colonizes the infant gut but is typically absent in adults.31 Pairwise comparisons revealed that LFD-reared neonates supplemented with SYN in early life exhibit lower abundance of Romboutsia ilealis in adulthood but are enriched for Sporofaciens sp910574885 and NM07-P-09 sp012584445 when compared with untreated or PRO supplemented mice (Figures S10F and S10G). Susceptible groups (LFD—reared neonates and PRE- and PRO-supplemented LFD-reared neonates) consistently exhibit a lower abundance of Sporofaciens sp910574885 compared with the protected groups (HFD-reared neonates and SYN-supplemented LFD-reared pups; Figures S10F–S10H). Moreover, the abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila is greater in HFD- compared with LFD-reared mice, and SYN supplementation of LFD-reared neonates in early life increases the abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila in adulthood (Figures S10E and S10F), which is noteworthy as this taxon is associated with resistance against the development of childhood asthma.41

Akin to our findings at PND15, early-life SYN supplementation alters the profile of circulating metabolites at PND66 (Figure 6A; Figure S11B). Strikingly, SYN-supplemented mice cluster separately from susceptible controls and PRE-supplemented and PRO-supplemented LFD-reared neonates, even though the nutritional supplementation had ceased for greater than 6 weeks (Figure 6A; Figure S11B). Metaboanalyst 6.034 was used to perform downstream analysis of serum metabolomic profile obtained from LC-MS. Of the 1,135 metabolites measured, the mean intensity of 480 metabolites differed significantly between groups (Figure S11A). The UpSet plot, constructed by comparing the protected groups (HFD-reared pups, SYN) with the susceptible groups, identified 175 metabolites with directional differences across comparisons (Figure 6B). Metabolites were then ranked based on correlation coefficients by the “Pattern Search” function in Metaboanalyst 6.0. Of the top 25 metabolites, 22 are associated with protection against and 3 metabolites are linked with predisposition to asthma-like disease in later life (Figures 6C and 6D; Figures S11C and S11D). Strikingly, while SYN-treated mice display a distinct global metabolic profile, SYN supplementation shifts the levels of a subset of metabolites toward those observed in protected controls (Figure 6C; Figure S11B). The metabolites 2,5-dimethyl-3-furanthiol acetate and amino caproic acid are most highly correlated with protection against disease (Figure 6D; Figure S11C), with both being elevated in HFD-reared mice and susceptible LFD-reared mice supplemented with SYN (Figures 6E and 6F). In contrast, indicine,{3-[2-(3-hydroxy-5-methoxyphenyl) ethyl] phenyl} oxidanesulfonic acid and N(6)-methyllysine positively associate with susceptibility to allergic airway inflammation, with high concentrations observed in LFD-reared controls and those supplemented with PRE or PRO alone (Figure 6C; Figure S11D). Together, our findings suggest that SYN supplementation in early life modulates specific gut microbial taxa (rather than broad ecosystem wide changes) and circulating metabolite profiles in later life. Whether these SYN supplementation-associated durable effects are associative or causal in protecting against later-life susceptibility and progression to chronic experimental asthma remains to be determined.

12-HHT supplementation in early life confers protection against sLRI

To determine whether the metabolite 12-HHT—identified through synbiotic-associated systemic metabolomic signatures (Figure 4)—plays a causal role in protecting against the development of an sLRI, we performed in vivo 12-HHT supplementation studies in neonatal mice inoculated with PVM (Figure 7A). Strikingly, oral administration of 12-HHT to LFD-reared neonates is sufficient to prevent infection-induced growth stunting and the development of airway remodeling and tissue damage in the lungs at 10 dpi (Figures 7B–7E). At the peak of infection (5 dpi), the viral load is lower, and lung pDC and Treg numbers are higher, in neonates supplemented with 12-HHT (Figures 7F–7H). Notably, the restoration in pDC is associated with the increased expression of Flt3L in the small intestine (Figure 7I). Consistent with improved clinical outcomes, and rescue of the pDC-Treg-associated immunoregulatory axis, the T cell immune response at 5 and 10 dpi in 12-HHT-supplemented neonates broadly follows the pattern observed in protected controls (Figures 7J–7N). Together, these findings causally link a metabolite elevated in synbiotic-protected neonates, 12-HHT, to improved infection outcomes by demonstrating that targeted metabolite supplementation can recapitulate key phenotypic and immunologic benefits of the synbiotic intervention.

Discussion

Modulating states of microbial imbalance in early life via supplementation with prebiotics, probiotics, or combinations thereof (synbiotics) is increasingly pursued to support immune development and long-term health. Extending previous work on infant-type bifidobacteria, we show that supplementation with an infant-type synbiotic, previously shown to be safe, well tolerated, and an augmenter of beneficial infant-type bifidobacteria,20 promotes coordinated SCFA-linked metabolite-immune interactions and attenuates a Th2 bias, aligning with patterns observed in exclusively breastfed infants. Using reverse-translational, infant-relevant infection and environmental challenge models, early-life synbiotic supplementation through weaning enhances immune resilience by improving disease tolerance to LRIs in early life and reduces later susceptibility to pollution-enhanced allergic airway disease. Together, these data support a model in which early-life synbiotic exposure shapes baseline metabolite-immune interactions, priming the developing immune system for improved resilience to future challenges, an aspect that cannot be directly tested in healthy infants and was, therefore, addressed in complementary preclinical models.

For newborns to thrive, the development of the immune system must be balanced, yet proceed at a rapid but controlled pace shortly after birth to adapt to changing environmental challenges. Unlike adults, neonates exhibit Th2-skewed immune profiles that enable organ development and survival of the fetus by minimizing the potential for maternal-fetal allogenic reactions.42,43,44 As a trade-off, newborns have a T cell system that is reduced in Th1 capacity, which is necessary to mount an effective anti-viral defense.45,46,47 Alterations in the dynamics of immune system adaptation in early life are associated with increased susceptibility to lower respiratory illnesses in early childhood, which in turn, represent an independent risk factor for the subsequent development of persistent asthma later in life.48 Our findings in this randomized, multicenter design study conducted across several European sites demonstrate that synbiotic supplementation in early life is sufficient to reduce a sustained systemic Th2 bias and unbalanced Th17 cell abundance at 4 months of age, following a pattern observed in breastfed infants. These findings are in line with a previous report demonstrating that low abundance of bifidobacterial species and/or lack of HMO-utilization capacity in breastfed infants is associated with enteric inflammation, driven by aberrant Th2 and Th17 responses.6 Given Th2 abundance in early life has been identified as a risk factor for the development of chronic inflammatory disorders later in life,49,50,51 rationally designed, infant-type biotics may help to prevent or mitigate unfavorable immune imprinting in early life manifesting in long-term health outcomes.

In response to synbiotic supplementation, we identify modules of differentially regulated immune cell populations in SYN, but not CTRL, formula-fed infants. We hypothesize that early-life SYN supplementation may promote a more balanced immune development and/or activation states. In line with this hypothesis, SYN supplementation enhances a functional coupling between Treg and effector T cells (Th1, Th2, and Th17) compared with CTRL infants, suggesting a more balanced response between activation and immunoregulation. Conversely, significant negative correlations between naive CD4+ T cells and effector and memory T cell subsets are observed in CTRL- but not SYN-supplemented infants. Thus, SYN supplementation may stabilize and strengthen immune cell interactions, even without necessarily altering the absolute frequencies of these cells. Additional investigations employing systems immunology approaches, which encompass longitudinal assessments of cellular abundance, activation states, and effector molecule capacity, will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of synbiotic-driven changes in systemic immune signatures and immune system development rates in early life.

Despite the reported changes in systemic immune profiles with SYN supplementation, we did not observe significant changes in parent-reported physician-confirmed adverse events between SYN, CTRL, and BF healthy infants at 4 months of age (data not shown). While previous studies have associated B. infantis colonization in early life with decreased risk of enteric virus infections and atopic diseases within the first 2 years of age,18,52 continued examination of this study cohort and follow-up health questionnaires will offer a longitudinal perspective on the potential health outcomes associated with early-life synbiotic supplementation. While this study was conducted in healthy, term infants, synbiotic intervention to promote long-term health may have a more pronounced effect in at-risk individuals, such as infants with a family history of atopy or those exposed to nutritional imbalances during pregnancy.

A strength of this study is the inclusion of infant-relevant disease models, which not only enable functional testing of hypotheses generated in the clinical SYN supplementation trial but also demonstrates efficacy in promoting disease tolerance. Using a clinically relevant setup of maternal LFD feeding to increase infection risk in the offspring,27,53 we demonstrate that early-life synbiotic supplementation is sufficient to reverse compromised immune programs including sustained Th2 immune responses upon viral infection in preclinical models that control for genetics, diet, and environmental exposures. In these settings, synbiotic supplementation, but not individual supplementation of PRE or PRO, confers protection against sLRI in early life and pollution-enhanced allergic airway inflammation later in life. These findings further emphasize the importance of developing infant-type synbiotics to maximize the health benefits driven by prebiotics and probiotics.

Timely and regulated activation of different T cells by DCs is a critical factor for determining the outcome of respiratory infections.27,54 Our findings demonstrate that early-life synbiotic supplementation is sufficient to restore a compromised pDC-Treg axis in the lungs and draining lymph nodes of susceptible LFD-reared neonatal mice, previously shown to be necessary and sufficient for bronchiolitis disease management.26 Although SYN supplementation expanded pDCs and Tregs, additional studies are required to establish whether this immunoregulatory axis is causally responsible for observed immune protection in this setting. Consistent with preclinical findings, Konieczna et al. demonstrated that B. infantis supplementation stimulated pDC-mediated Foxp3+ Treg responses in the peripheral blood of healthy adult volunteers.55 These findings provide further evidence that B. infantis supplementation may have the potential to induce coordinated pDC-Treg responses to support improved infection outcomes via the induction of disease tolerance. While we cannot rule out other mechanisms contributing to pDC and Treg expansion, we demonstrate that synbiotic supplementation significantly increases intestinal levels of Flt3L—a cytokine essential for pDC development—providing a plausible mechanistic link by which an oral synbiotic may enhance immune responses at distal mucosal sites such as the lung.

Remarkably, we find that synbiotic-mediated immune benefits persisted throughout adolescence, even though synbiotic supplementation was discontinued at weaning. In line with our preclinical findings, Dai et al. previously reported that breastfeeding enrichment of B. infantis is associated with reduced antibiotic-associated asthma risk in children, with B. infantis abundance positively correlating with fucosylated HMOs in paired maternal samples.56 Further research is required to resolve whether the improved disease tolerance upon synbiotic supplementation in the preclinical model is attributable to early-life immune system imprinting, the prevention of aberrant tissue remodeling following an sLRI, or a combination of both.

A central contribution of this study is the integration of systemic metabolomics, revealing that synbiotic supplementation in the context of a viral infection induces broad systemic metabolic remodeling, including enrichment of metabolites associated with disease protection. Among these, the lipid mediator 12-HHT emerged as a host-microbial effector linking early-life microbial states to improved infection outcomes.12-HHT has been previously implicated in epithelial repair, inflammation resolution, and lipid mediator signaling, but its role in early-life antiviral immunity has not been fully elucidated. Engagement of 12-HHT with its cognate receptor, Blt2 (leukotriene B4 receptor 2), which is expressed by epithelial and immune cells, results in downstream production of cytokines, chemokines, and matrix metalloproteinases, which can calibrate an over-exuberant immune response and limit the potential for immunopathology following infection.57,58,59,60 The involvement of 12-HHT in promoting wound healing58,61,62 and the fact that BLT2-deficient mice are more susceptible to acute lung injury63 and eosinophilic lung inflammation63 further emphasize a potential beneficial role of 12-HHT in regulating immune responses and promoting tissue repair. Our study results indicate that 12-HHT is not merely correlated with protection from sLRI, it is causally sufficient. Oral supplementation with 12-HHT phenocopies the protective effects of synbiotic supplementation, preventing infection-induced growth stunting, reducing viral burden, promoting pDC and Treg responses, and calibrating the immune response to limit immunopathology and disease severity. These findings underscore the concept that host-derived lipid mediators may act as key effector molecules linking early-life microbial states to immunological resilience. Indeed, 12-HHT increased Flt3L expression in the gut, which we have previously linked to neonatal hematopoiesis of DCs, which serve as the immune system’s key decision makers.27 By establishing 12-HHT as a functional mediator of disease tolerance, our findings bridge observational metabolomics with functional outcomes and support a model in which early-life host-microbial interactions shape the host lipid mediator landscape to tune immune responsiveness. Further studies are needed to delineate the cellular sources and regulatory pathways governing 12-HHT production in early life, its receptor-mediated mechanisms across tissues, and its therapeutic potential in human populations at high risk for viral or allergic diseases.

Among other metabolites that were significantly associated with higher disease susceptibility were {[1-(4-methoxyphenyl)pentan-3-yl]oxy}sulfonic acid and N-acetyl-a-neuraminic acid, with the latter being involved in virus adherence to host cells and regulating viral replication.64,65 In line with its potential role as a systemic biomarker of inflammation, N-acetyl-a-neuraminic acid serum levels are significantly reduced in SYN- compared with LFD-reared control, PRE-, or PRO-supplemented groups.

Early-life synbiotic supplementation also leads to persistent effects on the systemic metabolome later in life, with several of these metabolites associated with protection or susceptibility to experimental asthma. Among the two metabolites with the highest correlation coefficients for protection are 2,5-dimethyl-3′-formylthiazole and aminocaproic acid, while only 3 metabolites are associated with negative outcomes. Thiazole derivates such as 2,5-dimethyl-3′-formylthiazole are known for their anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects,66 while aminocaproic acid has anti-fibrinolytic properties.67 Whether observed alterations in circulating metabolites in later life directly contribute to the protective effect of early-life SYN supplementation on preventing the development of pollution-enhanced allergic airway inflammation or are biomarkers of a healthy immune system and microbiome remains to be determined.

Collectively, these data provide a comprehensive and multidimensional view on the important role of an infant-type synbiotic in imprinting the immune system to promote health in early life with potential long-term effects. More broadly, they advance the conceptual framework that targeted modulation of host-metabolic networks can tune early-life immune trajectories and promote durable immune health. Given the critical role of early-life nutrition in shaping healthy microbiome and immune trajectories, integrating rationally designed, infant-type synbiotic solutions during key windows of immunological development may present a practical alternative when breastfeeding is not an option.

Limitations of the study

Limitations of this study include (1) the relatively small sample size for infant blood immunotyping and single time-point analysis, (2) limited statistical power to link reduced Th2 abundance with clinical outcomes at 4 months, and (3) numerical (non-significant) imbalance in mode of delivery. Continued cohort follow-up and health questionnaires may provide longitudinal insight into early-life infant-type synbiotic-driven immune imprinting, which is currently best examined using complementary preclinical models that do not fully recapitulate healthy human infant physiology.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study design and population

A double-blind, randomized controlled trial with a non-randomized BF reference group was conducted in 18 centers across Belgium, France, Germany, and Spain from November 2021 to October 2024, with several planned staged endpoint analyses.20 Infants were enrolled into the study if they were aged between 0 and 14 days and met the following criteria: healthy, full term birth (≥37 weeks of gestation); birth weight ≥2500g and ≤4500g; BF infants must have been exclusively breastfed since birth and their parents must have decided to continue with exclusive breastfeeding at least through 4 months of age; and FF infants must have been exclusively consuming and tolerating a cow’s milk infant formula and their parents must have independently decided not to breastfeed before study enrollment. Infants were excluded from the study for the following reasons: they had conditions requiring infant feedings other than those specified in the protocol; there was evidence of major congenital malformations (e.g., cleft palate), systemic or congenital infections (e.g., syphilis), or previous or ongoing severe laboratory or medical abnormalities; infants have received or are presently receiving medications or probiotic supplements; and ongoing or past participation in another trial since birth. The FF infants who met the eligibility criteria were randomized to the SYN or CTRL groups. A dynamic allocation algorithm with a 1:1 allocation ratio stratified by center, sex (male/female), and delivery mode (vaginal/cesarean section) was carried out using Medidata Randomization Trial Supply Management. The reference BF infants were not randomized. Given this was a double-blind trial, the identity of the specific product was blinded to everyone involved, from subjects to the contract research organization and the sponsor. Sample size calculation for the full CLARA study cohort is reported in Picaud et al.20 based on the primary objective of growth safety. Blood samples for immunotyping analyses were sampled on a voluntary basis and were considered exploratory without additional power calculation.

Animal model of viral sLRI and pollution enhanced allergic airway inflammation

Wild type C57Bl/6J (Animal Resource Center) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions in QIMR Berghofer Animal facility. Both male and female C57Bl/6J mice (3-4 weeks of age) were put on either a high fiber diet (SF-AIN93G, HFD) or low fiber diet (SF09-028, LFD) for 3 weeks prior to timed mating and throughout the experiment as previously described.27 Neonates were supplemented with either control vehicle, a blend of structurally diverse HMOs (PRE: 2′FL, DFL, LNT, 3′SL, 6′SL, 3FL; 4mg/animal/day corresponding to 1.8g/L/day in infants), B. infantis (PRO; B. infantis LMG 11588, 10E6 CFU/animal/day) or a combination of HMO mix and B. infantis (SYN: 2′FL, DFL, LNT, 3′SL, 6′SL, 3FL; 4mg/animal/day corresponding to 1.8g/L/day in infants + B. infantis LMG 11588, 10E6 CFU/animal/day) once daily via intra-gastric gavage (i.g.) for 15 consecutive days starting at postnatal day (PND) 5. In one study, LFD-reared neonatal mice were supplemented with 12-HHT (0.4 μg; i.g. route) at PND 5-12 (daily) and then on alternate days until the end of the experiment. Oral administration of 12-HHT was selected to reflect the physiological gut–systemic axis engaged by synbiotic supplementation and, in the absence of established in vivo dosing paradigms, was guided by known BLT2-dependent biological activity of 12-HHT and related lipid mediators to test functional sufficiency rather than pharmacological optimization. To induce bronchiolitis, 10 plaque-forming units (PFU) of Pneumonia virus of mice (PVM) strain J3666, diluted in 10 μL vehicle [Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium + 10% fetal calf serum (FCS)] was administered intra-nasally (i.n.) at PND10 under light isoflurane-induced anesthesia.27,68 To simulate pollution enhanced allergic inflammation, mice infected with PVM at PND10 were sensitized i.n. on PND 42, 49, 56, and 63 with cockroach allergen extracts (CRE, 1 μg) in combination with particulate matter with a diameter of 2.5 micrometer or less (PM 2.5, 100 μg) in a final volume of 50 μL PBS.

Method details

Intervention and study product

A graphical overview of the study design of the CLARA sub-cohort is shown in Figure 1A. Total duration of the study intervention was 15 months. The study formulas were staged according to the age of the infant (1st age infant formula (IF): 0-<6 months; 2nd age Follow-up formula (FUF): 6-<12 months; 3rd age Growing-up formula (GUM): 12-15 months). Study formulas were fed orally, ad libitum daily and intake will have varied by infant’s age, weight, and appetite. The composition of the study formula is summarized in Table S1. The SYN and CTRL were identical except that SYN was supplemented with a blend of 6 HMOs and B. infantis LMG 11588 plus B. lactis CNCM I-3446 (also known as BL818), while CTRL was not. The 6 manufactured HMOs in the SYN were 3-fucosyllactose (3FL), 2′-fucosyllactose (2′FL), 2′-3-difucosyllactose (DFL), lacto-N-tetraose (LNT), 3’sialyllactose (3′SL), and 6’sialyllactose (6′SL) and the amount and ratio between the HMOs changed from IF to FUF to GUM as the infants aged. Baseline demographics and household characteristics for the CLARA sub-cohort are summarized in Table S2.

Adverse events and medication use

Reported adverse events (AEs) and serious AEs (SAEs) were documented by each investigator as part of the eCRF including the type, incidence, severity, seriousness, and relation to feeding.20 Data was also continuously collected from parent(s)/legal representatives using an electronic Infant Illness Diary (IID) to capture the number of occurrences and the length of time an infant experienced the following symptoms: fever, respiratory tract infections, GI symptoms, and ear symptoms. For each parent-reported diagnosis, study physicians contacted the parent(s)/legal representative and determined if the infant should be brought into the site for further evaluation (unscheduled visits). Once IID entries had been validated/confirmed by study physicians, this information was entered into the eCRF as an adverse event. Medication type and duration of use were recorded using the concomitant medication reporting form as part of the eCRF.

Fecal bifidobacterium abundance in infants

Fecal samples were collected at home by the parents using a feces tube with integrated spatula and without any buffers or additives, frozen in the home freezer (−20°C), and brought to the study site keeping the samples frozen in an icepack during transport. Samples were kept frozen at the site at −80°C until transferred every 3-4 months to the central lab for analysis. Relative Bifidobacterium abundance was determined using shot-gun metagenomics as previously described.69 Strain-level resolution of B. longum subsp. infantis strains was performed as described in Capeding et al.19

Human blood sampling and immunotyping analyses

Blood collection at 4 months of age was used for prospective PBMC phenotype analyses based on the stool and biomarker sampling at 3 months of age. Blood was collected on a voluntary basis from CTRL (n = 39), SYN (n = 35), and breastfed infants (BF, n = 16, reference). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from venous blood and stained using a pre-validated 17-plex biomarker panel for immune cell profiling by Canopy Biosciences. PBMCs were processed within two hours after collection at study sites and loaded to ZellSafe chips that were sent to Canopy Biosciences within one month. Pre-stained biomarkers (CCR4, CCR5, CCR6) were measured within the first two days of sample reception to limit biomarker signal disturbance by the increasing autofluorescence over storage time. The remaining 14 biomarkers were stained subsequently. The whole 17-plex biomarker panel was performed by iterative multi-plex staining. Directly conjugated primary antibodies were incubated for 5 min at room temperature. A minimum of 20.000 cells were measured. The cell abundance of 29 assessed immune populations was expressed as percentage of total measured leukocytes and gating strategies are listed in Table S3. Statistical testing between randomized CTRL and SYN groups was performed using non-parametric Mann Whitney U test (∗p < 0.05). Non-randomized BF controls were used as reference group. No statistical test was performed between randomized and non-randomized groups due to the inherent differences in study design limiting the validity of direct comparisons.

Human fecal alpha-1-antitrypsin, calprotectin and SCFAs measurements

Fecal alpha-1-antitrypsin (AAT) and calprotectin protein levels were assessed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA, Immunodiagnostik AG). For normalization, data were reported per dry stool weight. Fecal organic acids including acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid were assessed using validated liquid chromatography-tandem mass Spectrometry according to a modified published method.70 Quantitative changes from baseline and differences between feeding groups were determined. For normalization, data were reported per dry stool weight.

Flow cytometry mouse studies

Single cell suspensions of mediastinal lymph nodes and lungs were prepared as previously described.68,71 Briefly, mechanical disruption of these tissues was done followed by passage through 70-μm cell strainers (BD Biosciences). Geys lysis buffer was used to lyse RBCs. To differentiate between live and dead cells, the cells were incubated in Zombie Aqua (Biolegend, USA) for 15 min at 4°C. Cells were then incubated with anti-FcγRIII/II (Fc block) for 15 min at 4°C to inhibit non-specific binding. After washing, cells were stained for 30 min at 4°C with fluorescently labeled antibodies. For intracellular staining, the cells were processed with Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (eBiosciences, CA, USA) as per manufacturer instructions followed by staining with GATA3 antibody (TWAJ, PE, eBiosciences, USA) and FoxP3 antibody (MF23, BD Biosciences, USA) for 30 min at 4°C. Neutrophils (CD11c-CD11b+Ly6G+), eosinophils (CD11c-SiglecF + CD11b+Ly6G-), cDC1 (MHCII+ , CD11c+ , CD26+ , CD64− , CD172− , XCR1+), cDC2 (MHCII+ , CD11C+ , CD26+ , CD64− , CD172+ , XCR1−), pDC (B220+, CD11c+, CD11b−, Siglec-H+), Th2 (TCR-B+, CD8-, CD4+, GATA3+), ILC2s (TCR-B-, GATA3+), Tregs (TCR-B+, CD4+, CD8-, CD25+, FoxP3+) were identified as previously described.27,71

Histology and immunohistochemistry mouse studies

Lung biopsies were prepared for paraffin embedding as previously described.72 Antigen retrieval was performed on de-waxed sections by heating slides in citrate buffer for 10 min. Slides were incubated overnight with primary antibodies after blocking with goat serum (10% in PBS). Primary antibodies were diluted in 10% FCS in PBS as follows: anti-PVM (1:8000), anti-Muc5ac (1:400), α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA; 1:1000). After washing with 0.05% Tween 20/TBS 3 times, sections were incubated with secondary antibody: anti-mouse IgG-AP for α-smooth muscle actin, anti-mouse IgG-AP for Muc5ac, and anti-rabbit IgG-AP for PVM for 1 h. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin after slides were incubated with Fast Red (Sigma-Aldrich) and washed with distilled water. Coverslips with mounting medium were placed on the slides, and sections were scanned with ScanScope Aperio Turbo (Aperio, Vista, California). Scoring for PVM+ airway epithelial cells (AEC), airway smooth muscle (ASM) mass, and mucus hypersecretion were performed as previously described.28,73,74

Long read 16S rRNA sequencing and microbiome profiling in mice

DNA extraction and 16S long read sequencing from fecal samples was performed by Australian Genome Research Facility (Brisbane, Australia). PCR amplification of V1-V9 region of 16S rRNA was done for 30 cycles with denaturation (30 s at 95°C), annealing (30 s at 57°C) and extension (60 s at 72°C). Amplification was done with barcoded primers as previously described.75 The primers and sequence were as follows:

F27 (5′GCATC/barcode/AGRGTTYGATYMTGGCTCAG3′),

R1492 (5′GCATC/barcode/RGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT3′).

Concentration and quality of amplicons and library were assessed with Qubit 1× dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, USA). Equimolar concentrations of amplicons were pooled, and libraries were prepared with the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (PacBio, CA, USA) as per manufacturer’s instructions. AMPure PB beads were used to clean up the amplified libraries. Sequencing polymerase and primers were bound to libraries with Sequel II Binding Kit 2.1 followed by cleanup with AMPure PB beads. Sequel II Sequencing Kit 2.0 (PacBio, USA) was used for sequencing on Sequel II PacBio system. Denoising and quality filtering of PacBio HiFi full-length 16S data was performed with DADA276 and QIIME2.77 VSEARCH78 was used for consensus alignment classification against Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB r207)79 for classification of amplicon sequence variant (ASV). MicrobiomeMarker (v.1.8.0)80 and phyloseq (v1.46)81 were used to process data. DESeq2 was used to process count data and features and samples without values in at least one sample were processed with ‘poscounts’.82 p values were adjusted with Benjamini & Hochberg procedure.83 QIIME2 (2022.2) was used to analyze Alpha and Beta diversity. Alpha diversity was measured by Pielou’s evenness, Shannon’s entropy, observed features. Beta diversity was calculated by weighted UniFrac distances84 and visualized by emperor plot of the principal component analysis matrix. Significance (p < 0.05) in beta diversity was calculated by Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA).

Untargeted metabolic profiling in mouse samples

Serum samples (20 μL) mixed with 120 μL extraction solvent (methanol with 1 μM CCP standards) were sonicated and vortexed at 4°C for 15 min. After centrifugation at maximum speed at 4°C, the supernatant was transferred to an LCMS vial. Acquisition was made on the LCMS system which consisted of Orbitrap Exploris 120 (Thermo Scientific) coupled with Vanquish Flex (Thermo Scientific). Chromatographic separation was done with SUPLECO Ascentis Express C8 HPLC column (10 cm × 2.1 mm x 2 μm) in binary gradient conditions using mobile phase A (0.1% Formic acid in HPLC grade water) and mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in ACN). The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min flow and the gradient program was set as follows: 0min 2%B, (0min–9min) 2%B-40%B, (9min–9.5min) 40%B- 95%B, (9.5min–11.5min) 95%B, (11.5 min–12 min) 95%B-2%B, (12 min–15 min) 2%B. Retention times for standards were extracted from the peak detection module of MZmine 2.5385 or were manually examined. IDEOM86 was used to process LCMS data. The raw data of metabolite peak intensities was log transformed and auto scaled for principal components analysis, dendrogram construction, one-way ANOVA, correlation analysis, and heatmaps. Auto-scaled data of individual comparisons of peak intensities were used for the Upset diagram. Raw metabolomics data was uploaded into the software package Metaboanalyst 6.0 34. Groups were annotated as protected or susceptible to sLRI based on weight gain, which served as a core physiological marker of overall health status. Within Metaboanalyst, a ‘Pattern Search' which identified metabolites that were commonly increased/decreased relative to the phenotype (in this case, protection or susceptibility) were applied. Metabolites that were higher in susceptible groups were defined as “detrimental metabolites” and those elevated in the disease resistant groups were defined as “protective metabolites”. The pattern hunter feature calculated the correlation coefficient based on metabolite concentration and assigned whether they belonged to the protected or susceptible group.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of human data

To assess differences between the randomized formula fed groups the following statistical tests were used: Non-parametric Mann Whitney U test for fecal biomarkers and immune cell populations; a linear model adjusting for baseline age and study site with compositional bias-correction87 for microbiota at species level. Partial Pearson correlations adjusting for baseline age and study site were done to assess associations between microbiota, fecal biomarkers, and the T helper cell populations. Spearman correlations were done to assess relationships among the different immune cell populations. To control the false discovery rate due to multiple testing, the p-values were adjusted using the FDR method.83 Test results were considered significant at FDR <0.10 and nominally significant at p < 0.05.t.

Statistical analysis of mouse data

GraphPad Prism (Version 9.3.1, GraphPad, USA) was used for statistical analysis. One way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple test or Two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test were used where appropriate. p value of 0.05 or lower (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001) was considered statistically significant.
