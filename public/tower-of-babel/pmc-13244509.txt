The cysteine metabolic regulation through CSE-transsulfuration pathway is essential in iron-induced oxidative damage of heart
Authors: Yong Zhang, Yijun Xin, Jie Zhou, Guang Yang, Siying Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract ObjectivesIron overload (IO) cardiomyopathy is a major cause of mortality in patients with iron overload disorders. This study investigates the role of cystathionine γ-lyase (CSE), a key enzyme in the transsulfuration pathway for cysteine and hydrogen sulfide (H2S) production, in iron-induced oxidative cardiac injury.MethodsWe investigated the effects of CSE on oxidative stress, metabolic dysregulation, and cardiac remodeling using in vivo mouse models of chronic iron overload and in vitro ferric citrate (FAC)-treated cardiomyocytes.ResultsGenetic deletion of Cth (encoding CSE) in mice exacerbated iron overload-induced cardiac hypertrophy, systolic dysfunction, and interstitial fibrosis. These effects correlated with reduced expression of the cysteine transporter SLC7A11, impaired glutathione (GSH) synthesis, and suppression of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1)/glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) antioxidant signaling pathway. Conversely, CSE overexpression in cardiomyocytes restored SLC7A11 stability, and suppressed reactive oxygen species (ROS) production through the Nrf2/HO-1/GPX4 pathway, thereby attenuating iron-mediated oxidative damage. Mechanistically, CSE deficiency disrupted both cysteine uptake via SLC7A11and cysteine production via transsulfuration pathway, inducing a self-reinforcing cycle of oxidative stress.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that the CSE-transsulfuration pathway serves as a crucial regulator of cysteine metabolism and antioxidant signaling in cardiomyocytes, presenting a potential therapeutic target in iron-induced cardiomyopathy.

Introduction

Iron is an essential micronutrient for fundamental cellular processes, including oxygen transport and mitochondrial respiration [1,2]. However, systemic iron overload (IO), a condition prevalent in hereditary hemochromatosis in patients requiring frequent blood transfusions, including those with β-thalassemia and myelodysplastic syndromes, leads to toxic iron accumulation in vital organs with the heart being a primary target [3,4]. Iron overload cardiomyopathy, characterized by diastolic and systolic dysfunction, arrhythmias, and heart failure, remains a leading cause of mortality in this patient population [5]. Despite the clinical use of iron chelators, the progression of cardiac damage is often insidious and partially irreversible [6,7], underscoring the need to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms and identify novel therapeutic targets.

The pathogenesis of iron overload cardiomyopathy is centrally driven by oxidative stress [8]. Excess intracellular labile iron facilitates the generation of highly reactive oxygen species (ROS) via the Fenton reaction, leading to extensive lipid peroxidation, protein degradation, and DNA injury [7]. Cardiomyocytes are particularly vulnerable due to their high metabolic rate and abundance of mitochondria [9]. This oxidative insult triggers a cascade of pathological events, including mitochondrial dysfunction, maladaptive hypertrophy, and interstitial fibrosis, leading to impaired myocardial contractility [9,10]. Furthermore, following fibrosis, the excessive deposition of collagen in the myocardial interstitium and the replacement of normal tissue with fibrotic scars lead to increased myocardial stiffness, impaired diastolic function, and electrical conduction abnormalities [11]. Although the role of oxidative stress in this pathology is well-established, the molecular pathways that determine the cardiomyocyte susceptibility to, or defense against, iron-mediated toxicity remain incompletely understood.

The primary cellular defense against such oxidative injury involves a coordinated antioxidant response [12]. The transcription factor nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), a master regulator in antioxidative defense system, induces the expression of cytoprotective genes, including heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) [13,14]. Simultaneously, the system Xc⁻ cystine/glutamate antiporter, whose light chain is SLC7A11, facilitates the import of cystine, which is subsequently reduced to cysteine for glutathione (GSH) synthesis, the most abundant intracellular antioxidant [15,16]. The integrity of this SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4 axis is recognized as a critical barrier against ferroptosis, a distinct form of iron-dependent oxidative cell death [17]. Although emerging evidence suggests that iron overload disrupts these defenses [18], the initiating events and regulatory nodes remain elusive.

Cystathionine γ-lyase (CSE), a key enzyme in the transsulfuration pathway, catalyzes the conversion of cystathionine to cysteine and hydrogen sulfide (H2S) [19,20]. Recent studies suggest that CSE-mediated H₂S production modulates ferroptosis, by suppressing ROS accumulation [21]. Although the cardioprotective role of CSE/H2S in ischemia-reperfusion and hypertrophy have been established, its specific function in the context of iron overload remains unclear [22]. Considering that cysteine availability is the rate-limiting step for GSH synthesis [23], we hypothesized that the CSE-transsulfuration pathway is a vital regulatory mechanism protecting cardiomyocytes from iron-overload-induced oxidative injury.

Here, we integrated in vivo mouse models, in vitro cardiomyocyte systems, and metabolomics approaches to investigate the role of CSE in iron overload cardiomyopathy. We hypothesized that CSE serves as a pivotal regulator of adaptive responses to iron-induced oxidative stress by regulating cysteine metabolism and antioxidant defense. Through comprehensive analyzes of cardiac function, redox status, and molecular signaling, we identified the link between CSE activity, cysteine homeostasis, and Nrf2-driven antioxidant pathways. Our findings reveal that CSE deficiency exacerbates iron-overload-induced cardiac dysfunction and remodeling, whereas CSE overexpression restores redox balance and mitigates injury. These findings enhance our understanding of iron-induced cardiac injury, designating the CSE-transsulfuration pathway as a promising therapeutic target for mitigating oxidative stress in iron overload disorders.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Ammonium iron (III) citrate (FAC, F5879) was purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany), the reagent was dissolved in ultrapure water to prepare a 100 mM stock solution for later use.

Cell culture

The rat cardiomyocyte cell line H9c2 (2-1) (CL-0089) was purchased from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. H9c2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM, 11965092, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, FPS500, Exell Bio) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (CM0004, Sparkjade). The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. All experimental groups were compared with parallel controls to normalize phenotypic variations in the rat cardiomyocyte cell line.

Animal experiments

Wild type (WT) C57BL/6J male mice (8 weeks old) used in this study were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. CSE gene (NM_145953) knockout mice (CSE–/– mice) were constructed using CRISPR/Cas-mediated genome engineering by Cyagen (Suzhou, China). Genotyping of the newborn mice was performed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using specific primers: Primer 1: F1: 5′ - 3′: GTCAAGATCTGAGCCCTGAGCAAGT, R1: 5′ - 3′: CCATTTCTGAAACTCCCCTTCCTCT; Primer 2: F1: 5′ - 3′: GTCAAGATCTGAGCCCTGAGCAAG, R1: 5′ - 3′: TGGAGAGGCTTAAGGCATAACCAAG.

All animals were housed in cages under specific pathogen-free conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 45%–60% humidity) with a 12 h light/dark cycle, and ad libitum access to standard rodent chow and water. The procedures followed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards prescribed by the Animal Management Committee of the Central Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University (W202403040218).

Based on previous studies, a dietary regimen for iron overload was formulated, and diets containing varying iron concentrations were purchased from Dyets (Wuxi) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The iron-supplemented feeds used in the trial were allocated using random number table as follows: The control group was AIN-93G purified rodent diet with 50 ppm Fe (D231106, Dyets), and iron overload (IO) group was AIN-93G purified rodent diet with 5000 ppm Fe (D231107, Dyets).

The body weight of experimental animals was recorded weekly. After 7 weeks of feeding, cardiac ultrasound analysis was assessed by ultrasound using a VEVO 3100LT instrument (Japan) to measure left ventricular ejection fraction (EF%) and fractional shortening (FS%). These parameters were used to confirm the successful establishment of the heart failure model. For transthoracic echocardiography assessment, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane (R510-22-10, RWD Life Science) and 100% oxygen at 1 L/min in an induction chamber.

At the end of the experiment, the mice were humanely euthanized via gradual carbon dioxide (CO2) asphyxiation. The serum and cardiac tissues of mice were harvested for further analysis.

Isolation and primary culture of cardiomyocytes from neonatal rats

The 1–3-day-old rat pups were purchased from Pengyue (Jinan, China). The hearts of the rat pups were first excised, then minced with scissors, and digested using a pre-prepared collagenase type 2 (LS004176, Worthington Biochemical) stock solution (pre-digestion for 5 min, followed by formal digestion for 1 h). After differential plating for 1 h and 15 min, the suspended primary cardiomyocytes were collected and plated for subsequent experiments.

Virus infection and plasmid transfection

Rat CSE shRNA adenovirus (NM 017074.1) and human CSE adenovirus (NM 001902.5) were purchased from WZ Bioscience Inc. When the density of plated cardiomyocytes reached approximately 60–70%, viral infection was performed at the established multiplicity of infection (MOI) determined from preliminary experiments. The virus was added to the serum-reduced medium Opti-MEM (31985070, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the mixture then applied to the culture dish. After 6–8 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with standard culture medium and incubated overnight. The infection efficiency was observed under a fluorescence microscope the following day before proceeding with subsequent experiments.

For plasmid transfection, the transfection reagent and plasmid DNA (CSE overexpression plasmid, NM_001902, vector: PCDNA3.1-3XFLAG-C, tag: C-terminal FLAG tag) were separately diluted in serum-reduced Opti-MEM medium (31985070, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and incubated for 20 min before being added to the culture dish. After 6–8 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with standard culture medium overnight. Subsequent experiments were conducted after 24 h post-transfection.

Pathological staining

Cardiac transverse tissue sections were prepared using paraffin embedding, followed by routine dehydration and dewaxing. The sections were stained using Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining kit (G1076, Servicebio) and Masson's trichrome staining kit (G1006, Servicebio) according to the manufacturers' instructions. Images were captured under Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope (Pannoramic SCAN Ⅱ, Hungary). Murine cardiac tissue sections were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Quantitative real-time PCR assay

Total RNA was extracted from mouse cardiac tissue using TRIzol Reagent (15596018CN, Invitrogen Corp.). RNA concentration was measured using NanoDrop pectrophotometer. RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using the Evo M-MLV reverse transcription premix kit (AG11728, Accurate biology). Quantitative PCR was performed with specific primers using the real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR instrument (A40425, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell viability assay

Cell viability was measured utilizing the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CK04, Dojindo) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, H9c2 cells were seeded at 4000–5000 cells per well in 48-well plates and cultured for 18–24 h under standard conditions. FAC was gradient-diluted to concentrations of 0, 25, 50, 100, 200, and 400 μM in culture medium. Following the initial culture period, the medium was replaced with the FAC-containing media. The cells were further cultured for 24 or 48 h post-treatment. Subsequently, the medium was carefully replaced with 90 μL of fresh medium containing 10 μL of CCK-8 solution and incubated for 1–3 h. Cell viability was calculated by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (Flex Station 3, Molecular Devices).

Electron microscope experiment

After passaging H9c2 cells, when the cell density reached approximately 80%, the culture medium was replaced with medium containing varying concentrations (0, 25, 50, 100, and 200 μM) of FAC. Following 24 h of treatment, samples were collected. The culture supernatant was aspirated to leave no more than 1.5 mL. Cells were gently scraped using a cell scraper and transferred via pipette to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Centrifugation was performed at 300–500 × g for 5 min to obtain a cell pellet. The supernatant was carefully aspirated, and 1 mL of electron microscopy fixative (G1102, Servicebio) was slowly added along the tube wall, ensuring the pellet remained undisturbed. The sample was incubated at 4 °C for 4 h. Subsequently, the fixed samples underwent post-fixation, dehydration, infiltration, embedding, sectioning, and staining before imaging.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) and glutathione (GSH) assay

The MDA assay kit (KGA7102-100) and GSH assay kit (KGA7305-100) were purchased from KeyGEN Biotechnology (Jiangsu, China). H9c2 cells were lysed with radio immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (PR20035, Proteintech). After lysis, the solution was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected for further analysis. After adding the extracted lysate to MDA kit's working solution, the mixture was vortexed, incubated for 40 min at 95 °C in a water bath, and subsequently cooled under running water. Following the addition of the extracted lysate to GSH kit's working solution, the mixture was thoroughly vortexed, and left at room temperature for 15 min. The absorbance (OD) were then measured at 532 nm and 412 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (Flex Station 3, Molecular Devices).

ROS staining

ROS assay kit (S0034S, Beyotime) was used to detect the intracellular ROS levels. H9c2 cells were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 24 h, then the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing FAC (50 μM) and cultured for an additional 24 h. According to a 1:1000 dilution, DCFH-DA (S0034S-1, Beyotime) was diluted with probe dilution buffer to a final concentration of 10 μmol/L, and subsequently added to a confocal dish. The dish was incubated in a cell culture incubator for 20–30 min, after which Hoechst 33342 was added. Imaging was immediately performed using a super-resolution microscopy with 3D imaging system (ZEISS Elyra 7, Germany).

Western blot analysis

Cells were scraped from the culture dish and lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (PR20035, Proteintech) supplemented with protease inhibitors to extract proteins. After lysis on ice for 20 min, the lysate was centrifuged at 4 °C and 15,000 rpm for 15 min. The protein supernatant was then mixed with 5 × loading buffer (AIWB-0025, Affinibody), subjected to electrophoresis, and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 2 h, followed by incubation with a primary antibody diluted in Quick Block™ Western antibody dilution buffer for 12–16 h. The membrane was washed with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20, incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody, and finally detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, 34580, Thermo Fisher Scientific) reagent on a development instrument (5200Multi, Tanon, China).

The antibodies utilized in our experiments are as follows: rabbit anti-GPX4 (1:1000 for WB, YN3047, Immunoway), mouse anti-HO-1 (1:1000 for WB, YM33079, Immunoway), rabbit anti-Nrf-2 (1:1000 for WB, A21176, Abclonal), rabbit anti-SLC7A11 (1:1000 for WB, DF12509, Affinity), rabbit anti-CSE (1:1000 for WB, 12217-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-CSE (1:1000 for WB, 19689S, Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-Ferritin (1:1000 for WB, A1144, Abclonal), rabbit anti-NCOA4 (1:1000 for WB, A5695, Abclonal), mouse anti-GAPDH (1:5000 for WB, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech), rabbit anti-HSP90 (1:10000 for WB, 13171-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-Vinculin (1:20000 for WB, A2752, Abclonal), HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H+L) (1:10000 for WB, SA00001-1, Proteintech), HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H+L) (1:10000 for WB, SA00001-2, Proteintech).

Cell fluorescence staining

H9c2 cells were seeded onto coverslips pre-placed in multi-well plates. After the culture period was completed, the cells underwent a series of steps including washing, permeabilization, blocking, immunostaining (targeting GPX4, A1933, Abclonal), counterstaining, and mounting before imaged.

Targeted quantification of serum amino acids by UPLC-MS/MS with AQC derivatization

Serum samples were analyzed using a targeted metabolomics approach with ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-TQMS). Briefly, proteins were precipitated from thawed serum samples using methanol containing isotope-labeled internal standards. The resulting supernatants were then derivatized with 6-aminoquinolyl-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl carbamate (AQC) to facilitate detection. Chromatographic separation was conducted on a UPLC system (ACQUITY UPLC-Xevo TQ- S, Waters Corp., Milford, MA, U.S.A.) with a specific gradient elution method, and quantification was performed using a Xevo TQ-S mass spectrometer operating in electrospray ionization mode. Rigorous quality control was maintained throughout, including the utilization of reagent blanks and the intermittent analysis of pooled quality control samples. Data acquisition was handled by MassLynx software (v4.1, Waters, Milford, MA, U.S.A.), and metabolites were quantified based on calibration curves derived from authentic standards.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. Statistical analysis was double-blinded conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5). All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). An unpaired two-tailed t-test was employed to compare the means between two groups. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) was used in mice. In all tests, a threshold of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Iron overload is detrimental to cardiac function

Iron overload induced progressive cardiac morphological, molecular, and functional abnormalities in mice over a 7-week treatment period. Despite comparable body weight between groups (Figure 1a), iron overload mice exhibited overt cardiac enlargement (Figure 1b), quantified by significantly increased heart weight-to-lung weight (HW/LW) and heart weight-to-tibial length (HW/TL) ratios, established indicators of cardiac hypertrophy (Figure 1c). M-mode echocardiography (Figure 1d) revealed structural alterations, which coincided with significant reductions in fractional shortening (FS) and ejection fraction (EF), confirming impaired systolic function (Figure 1e; Table 2). At the molecular level, RT-qPCR demonstrated upregulation of hypertrophy markers atrial natriuretic peptide (Anp) and brain natriuretic peptide (Bnp), a pathological transition from α-myosin heavy chain (α-mhc) to β-myosin heavy chain (β-mhc), the hallmark of contractile dysfunction, and downregulation of sarco/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase 2a (Serca2α) which is critical for calcium homeostasis (Figure 1f). These changes were consistent with maladaptive remodeling. Histopathological analysis using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed myocyte hypertrophy (Figure 1g). In contrast, Masson's trichrome staining revealed extensive interstitial fibrosis, indicative of extracellular matrix dysregulation (Figure 1h). Collectively, these data demonstrate that chronic iron overload drives cardiac remodeling characterized by cardiac hypertrophy, contractile dysfunction, and progressive fibrosis.

Iron overload exacerbates oxidative stress in cardiomyocytes

Iron overload is a well-established pathogenic factor in cardiovascular diseases, primarily by disrupting cellular redox homeostasis and eliciting robust oxidative stress [24]. The mechanisms by which iron overload induces oxidative stress were investigated in cardiomyocytes using H9c2 cells and in vivo mouse models. Treatment of H9c2 cells with FAC resulted in dose- and time-dependent cytotoxicity, characterized by reduced proliferation and increased cell death at 24 h (Figure 2a) and 48 h (Figure 2b). Transmission electron microscopy revealed morphological features indicative of oxidative damage, including mitochondrial swelling and cristae disorganization (Figure 2c). Quantitative redox assays showed significantly elevated MDA level and reduced GSH in 50 μM FAC-treated cells (Figure 2d–e), signifying the redox-oxidative imbalance. ROS accumulation, detected via DCFH-DA staining, was markedly elevated in 50 μM FAC-treated cells versus controls (Figure 2f). At the molecular level, the iron storage protein, ferritin, was increased after FAC treatment. However, the NCOA4 accumulated, indicating activation of ferritinophagy, suggesting impaired iron sequestration and clearance (Figure 2g). For oxidative stress defense, Western blot showed reduced nuclear Nrf2 in FAC-treated cells, accompanied by decreased expression of its downstream targets heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and GPX4 (Figure 2h). These in vitro findings were validated in vivo, as mouse cardiac tissue from iron overload mice displayed parallel reductions in Nrf2, HO-1, and GPX4 (Figure 2i). Collectively, these results demonstrate that iron overload induces cardiomyocyte cytotoxicity, mitochondrial structural damage, and redox homeostasis disruption via two interconnected pathways, including impaired iron metabolism indicated by ferritin accumulation and NCOA4 activation, and impaired Nrf2-driven antioxidant defense.

CSE-transsulfuration pathway is essential in regulating cysteine metabolism under iron overload treatment

Besides the dysregulated Nrf2/HO-1/GPX4 antioxidant system, SLC7A11, the catalytic subunit of the system Xc⁻ cysteine/glutamate antiporter, is crucial for maintaining intracellular GSH levels and mitigating oxidative stress and iron-dependent lipid peroxidation leading to ferroptosis [25]. In vitro, FAC-treated H9c2 cardiomyocytes displayed a significantly decreased protein expression of SLC7A11 compared to untreated controls (Figure 3a). This finding was recapitulated in vivo, as cardiac tissue from iron-overloaded mice similarly exhibited downregulated SLC7A11 (Figure 3b). Metabolic profiling showed elevated free cysteine levels paired with reduced cystine and taurine of serum from iron-overloaded mice, confirmed cysteine dyshomeostasis (Figure 3c–e). At the enzymatic level, FAC treatment diminished protein expression of CSE, the key enzyme of the transsulfuration pathway that drives cysteine catabolism and H₂S production, in H9c2 cells (Figure 3f). Crucially, these in vitro changes were mirrored in mouse cardiac tissue, iron overload similarly reduced CSE protein levels (Figure 3g). Collectively, these results suggest that iron overload disrupts cysteine homeostasis in cardiomyocytes via dual mechanisms; by suppressing of cysteine uptake through SLC7A11 downregulation and disrupting of cysteine metabolism via CSE-transsulfuration pathway. The correlative reduction in CSE and concurrent downstream metabolite dysregulation highlights CSE as a central mediator of iron overload-induced cysteine dyshomeostasis in the heart.

CSE knockout exacerbated iron overload induced cardiac dysfunction and oxidative stress

To investigate the effect of CSE in cardiomyocyte with iron overload, 6–8-week-old male CSE–/– (Cth genetic ablation) mice were used in this study. The vehicle group was fed a standard diet, while the iron overload group received a high-iron diet, with 9 mice per group. Although body weight showed no significant difference between groups after 7 weeks of iron load (Figure 4a), CSE–/– mice exhibited marked cardiac hypertrophy evident in gross morphology (Figure 4b) and quantified by significantly elevated heart weight-to-lung weight (HW/LW) and heart weight-to-tibial length (HW/TL) ratios compared to WT mice under iron overload (Figure 4c). Echocardiography revealed worsened systolic function in iron overload CSE–/– mice, with a significant reduction in EF% and FS% compared to WT controls (Figure 4d–e; Table 3). The representative images of histopathological analysis revealed significant structural abnormalities in the myocardium of CSE–/– mice under iron overload, including disorganized architecture and myofibrillar loss (Figure 4f). Furthermore, iron overload CSE–/– mice hearts displayed significant increase (~2-fold) in interstitial fibrosis versus WT counterparts, quantified from Masson's trichrome-stained sections (Figure 4g), confirming that CSE deletion exacerbates tissue remodeling and cardiac injury.

CSE is a key regulator in protecting cardiomyocytes from iron overload induced oxidative injury

To verify the critical role of CSE in cardiac injury, our study employed gene gain-of-function and loss-of-function approaches in vitro. Consistent with in vivo study, CSE knockdown reduced SLC7A11 and endogenous CSE expression in FAC-treated neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs) (Figure 5a). NRCMs with CSE knockdown also showed downregulation of Nrf2 and its downstream effectors HO-1 and GPX4 treated with FAC, hence exacerbating redox vulnerability in cardiomyocytes (Figure 5b).

In CSE-overexpressing NRCMs treated with FAC, protein levels of CSE and SLC7A11 were significantly increased compared to vector controls (Figure 5c). CSE overexpression activated the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway, and its downstream effectors HO-1 and GPX4 were all significantly increased relative to exposed controls (Figure 5d). This upregulation of cysteine uptake machinery coincided with a marked reduction in ROS production, indicated by fluorescent probes, confirming the restoration of redox balance in CSE-overexpressing cells (Figure 5e). Immunofluorescence staining further indicated an enhanced GPX4 protein abundance​ in CSE-overexpressing H9c2 cells, with stronger signal in cytoplasmic and nuclear compartments (Figure 5f).

These results indicate that CSE expression is directly associated with SLC7A11 and the Nrf2/HO-1/GPX4 pathway–suggesting a molecular mechanism for iron overload cardiomyopathy.

Discussion

This study elucidates the essential protective role of the CSE-transsulfuration pathway in mitigating iron overload-induced cardiomyopathy. We demonstrated that chronic iron overload precipitates severe cardiac dysfunction, hypertrophy, and fibrosis, hallmarks of iron overload cardiomyopathy, which are mechanistically driven by a substantial disturbance of redox homeostasis. Importantly, this pathological process is exacerbated by the coordinated downregulation of SLC7A11 expression and CSE-transsulfuration activity, both of which play a role in maintaining cysteine homeostasis. Conversely, the genetic augmentation of CSE exhibited significant cytoprotection, positioning CSE as a key regulator of cardiac resilience against iron toxicity.

Excessive iron accumulation induces systolic dysfunction, pathological hypertrophy, and fibrosis, consistent with clinical and preclinical findings that associate iron overload dysregulation with progressive heart failure [26,27]. Our mouse model confirmed the established paradigm of iron overload injury, characterized by cardiac systolic dysfunction, pathological hypertrophy, and extensive fibrosis [28,29]. Iron functions as a catalyst for Fenton reactions which generate ROS that disrupt cellular homeostasis, as evidenced by mitochondrial damage, increased ROS and MDA production, and GSH depletion [30,31]. FAC-treated H9c2 cardiomyocytes displayed dose- and time-dependent cytotoxicity. Transmission electron microscopy revealed morphological hallmarks of oxidative damage with mitochondrial swelling and cristae disorganization, which correlated with amplified ROS production. Quantitative redox assays confirmed this imbalance, revealing significantly elevated MDA and depleted GSH in FAC-treated cells, indicating iron's capacity to catalyze Fenton reactions and disrupt cellular redox homeostasis. At the molecular level, iron overload induced by FAC treatment upregulated ferritin and downregulated NCOA4 in cardiomyocytes, suggesting impaired iron sequestration and clearance that sustains intracellular iron retention. These results aligns with seminal work demonstrating the role of NCOA4 in ferritin degradation and iron recycling, in which its suppression intensifies iron overload [32]. Nrf2, the master regulator of redox balance, becomes suppressed during iron overload-induced cardiomyocyte injury, and this consequently leads to a reduction of the lipid peroxidation inhibitor GPX4 [33–35]. Furthermore, the effect of Nrf2 to regulate redox homeostasis is though transcription of a variety of cytoprotective genes, including NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase-1 (NQO1) and HO-1 [36,37]. Our in vitro and in vivo findings demonstrated that iron-overloaded cardiomyocytes and mouse hearts showed comparable parallel reductions in Nrf2, and decreased downstream HO-1 and GPX4 expression. Collectively, our work demonstrates that iron overload induces cardiomyocyte injury by inducing a vicious cycle of oxidative damage.

It has been reported that SLC7A11 deficiency worsens myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury by facilitating ferroptosis, a process amplified by iron overload [38], and with foundational studies defining system Xc⁻ as the primary supplier of cysteine, a crucial precursor for GSH for antioxidant defense [39]. Consistent with the critical role of the SLC7A11-mediated system Xc⁻ in maintaining GSH synthesis and mitigating iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [40], we observed significant SLC7A11 downregulation in FAC-treated H9c2 cardiomyocytes and iron overloaded mouse hearts. The functional impact of reduced SLC7A11 was underscored by metabolic profiling, which displayed elevated free cysteine alongside dysregulated cystine and taurine, consistent with blocked cysteine utilization in iron overloaded mice. These results revealed that iron overload dysregulated cysteine metabolism through diminishing cysteine utilization and disrupting the antioxidant metabolic pathway, such as taurine production.

At the enzymatic level, FAC treatment and iron overload reduced protein expression of CSE, the rate-limiting enzymes in the transsulfuration pathway that facilitate cysteine catabolism and H₂S production [41–44]. Research has demonstrated that CSE-derived H₂S protects against cardiotoxicity by scavenging ROS and replenishing GSH [17,45], and that iron overload could disrupt the transsulfuration pathway [46]. Collectively, these data show that cysteine dysregulation in iron overload involves​ a dual mechanism: impaired SLC7A11-mediated cysteine uptake and CSE-dependent endogenous cysteine production. The correlative reduction in CSE, an enzyme that bridges cysteine to bioactive molecules such as H2S, and concurrent metabolite imbalances highlight CSE as a central mediator of this process. These results indicated that iron overload disrupts cysteine homeostasis in cardiomyocytes through coordinated dysregulation of cysteine uptake via SLC7A11, and enzymatic metabolism via transsulfuration pathway, with CSE emerging as a central mediator of this pathological imbalance.

The most compelling evidence for the centrality of CSE comes from the loss-of-function and gain-of-function experiments. Genetic ablation of Cth (the gene encoding CSE)​ in mice (CSE–/– mice) exacerbated iron overload-induced cardiac injury, manifesting as overt cardiac hypertrophy, systolic dysfunction, and interstitial fibrosis. This phenotypic worsening was mechanistically linked to a further dampening of the Nrf2 and SLC7A11 expression, indicating that CSE is not merely a bystander but an active modulator of these critical defense systems. However, CSE overexpression in cardiomyocytes conferred a protective phenotype, effectively countering the iron-induced insult. The restoration of SLC7A11 levels, reduction in ROS, and activation of the Nrf2/HO-1/GPX4 axis in CSE-overexpressing cells, demonstrate that enhancing the CSE pathway can break the vicious cycle of oxidative stress. These observations suggest a positive feedback loop in which CSE activity helps stabilize the Nrf2 pathway and/or cysteine bioavailability, which in turn bolsters the overall antioxidant capacity.

Conclusions

Collectively, our findings indicate the molecular mechanism of impaired cysteine uptake regulated by SLC7A11 expression and endogenous CSE transsulfuration pathway, in iron overload cardiac injury. These findings will expand the understanding of iron toxicity and identify novel therapeutic targets in heart diseases.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the current experimental data only reveal an imbalance in cystine/cysteine metabolism at the serum metabolome level, which is insufficient to directly explain the direct regulatory effect of iron overload on cardiomyocyte metabolism. Future work requires adding metabolomic analysis of myocardial tissue and measurement of related amino acid metabolites. Second, we only measured indirect markers like MDA, GPX4, and SLC7A11, failing to directly prove CSE regulates ferroptosis. Detecting labile iron in follow-up work would strengthen these findings. Furthermore, the impact of CSE intervention on long-term prognosis need to evaluated. Finally, another limitation of this study is the lack of investigation into sex-related differences in cardiac damage. It is well established that males generally exhibit higher vulnerability to cardiomyopathy compared to females, potentially due to the cardioprotective effects of female hormones.
