Systematic investigation of the LSPR characteristics in plasmonic nanoarrays
Authors: Xing Zhao, Xiangnan Zhu, Dexiang Chen, Yunjia Wei, Lei Yao, Weiyi Li, Guoqun Li, Zixuan Sun, Yixin Zhao, Xingce Fan, Teng Qiu, Qi Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFor plasmonic materials, it is crucial to modulate and couple their localized surface-plasmon resonances (LSPRs) frequencies with the incident laser to generate resonant electromagnetic fields. While the LSPR properties of colloidal plasmonic particles are well-established, the distinct morphology and LSPR behaviors of plasmonic nanoarrays fabricated by template-assisted evaporation remain insufficiently explored. This study systematically investigates the effects of particle diameter, deposition thickness, and surface roughness on the LSPR characteristics of nanoarrays. Furthermore, we demonstrate precise tuning of LSPRs through adjustments to the dielectric medium and composition in multilayer nanoparticles, as well as polarization dependence of anisotropic nanoparticles. Several critical, yet often overlooked, aspects were identified: (1) Physically deposited nanoparticles exhibit non-spherical morphologies, leading to LSPR characteristics that are distinct from those of idealized spheres. (2) Out-of-plane geometry strongly influences the LSPR response; notably, increasing the particle thickness can induce an LSPR blueshift, contrary to conventional expectations. (3) The dielectric environment plays a decisive role in governing the LSPRs of nanoarrays, including contributions from both the dielectric substrate and the surrounding medium. Moreover, we also demonstrate the capability of AAO-templated nanoarrays for plasmonic refractive index sensing, revealing a red shift of the LSPR peak with increasing refractive index and enhanced sensitivity for larger particle diameters. These findings provide insight into LSPR behaviors in nanoarrays and establish strategies for precise plasmonic tuning, deploying in LSPR sensing applications. Subject terms: Nanophotonics and plasmonics, Nanoparticles

Introduction

Localized surface plasmons (LSPs) refer to the collective oscillation of conduction electrons confined locally at the interface between plasmonic nanomaterials and their surrounding medium1. When coupled with incident light, localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) occurs, generating strong local electromagnetic fields that enhance Raman scattering2,3 and photoluminescence of nearby probes4,5. Moreover, the LSPs can decay non-radiatively, producing high-energy hole-electron pairs that can be utilized in applications like photocatalysis6. Precise tuning of the LSPR frequency to overlap with the laser excitation is therefore essential for optimizing plasmonic coupling in these applications.

Various nano-fabrication techniques have been developed to tailor the LSPR frequencies of plasmonic materials. Broadly, these approaches can be categorized into two groups. The predominant method involves the chemical synthesis of colloidal nanoparticles (NPs), which incorporates techniques such as the Turkevich method7, seed-mediated growth8, and polyol synthesis9. These methods allow controlled production of nanoparticles with diverse morphologies such as nanospheres, nanorods, nanoplates, nanostars10. The LSPRs responses of these NPs are generally controlled by modulating the particle size and shape anisotropy through regulating chemical synthesis conditions11–14. The other method employs template-assisted evaporation to fabricate nanoarrays, using nanomasks prepared by direct-writing techniques15, nanoimprint lithography16, nanosphere lithography17 or anodic aluminum oxide (AAO) membranes18. These approaches excel in producing reproducible and periodic nanoarrays19.

The LSPRs frequency is influenced by various factors like particle size, particle morphology, composition, the dielectric constant of the surrounding environment and et al20. While the LSPR characteristics of colloidal particles are well-studied21, nanoarrays prepared by template-assisted evaporation present unique factors that impact their plasmonic properties. First, the fabrication approach results in a hemispherical crown-like morphology, which are distinctive to sphere particles in LSPR characteristics22. Secondly, the particles in nanoarrays are bounded and patterned on substrates, with LSPR modes typically confined to the horizontal plane23,24 Thirdly, the evaporated particles are tightly bound to the substrate with a large surface area25. Consequently, the dielectric environment from both the substrate and the surrounding medium introduces additional variables. Furthermore, a combination with advanced fabrication techniques, such as angle-resolved deposition26, annealing27, and nanoimprint lithography28, can introduce further complexities in the LSPR properties.

Beyond their fundamental optical properties, LSPR modes provide a powerful platform for sensing because they directly respond to variations in the local dielectric environment29. Even subtle changes in refractive index caused by molecular adsorption, binding events, or chemical reactions induce measurable shifts in the plasmon resonance30. This intrinsic sensitivity enables label-free and real-time monitoring of interfacial processes at the nanoscale. Compared with colloidal nanoparticles, periodic nanoarrays offer distinct advantages for LSPR sensing, including well-defined geometries, uniform spectral features, and enhanced reproducibility, which together allow for more reliable quantitative analysis. As a result, LSPR sensing not only serves as a versatile tool for probing molecular interactions and chemical transformations but also provides a robust foundation for designing plasmonic devices with tailored functionalities.

In this paper, we conduct a comprehensive investigation into the LSPRs characteristics of plasmonic nanoarrays prepared by template-assisted evaporation. We employed AAO membranes as nanomasks for the fabrication of nanoarrays due to their high reproducibility, feasible tunability, and capability for large-scale fabrication31,32. Several critical yet often overlooked aspects were explored, including (1) the unique morphology and LSPR properties of physically deposited particles and their evolution during evaporation, (2) the influence of out-of-plane morphology on LSPR properties, with particular attention to the thickness-dependent blueshift, and (3) the complex dielectric environment inherent to nanoarray configurations. Moreover, the AAO approach are well-suited for refractive index sensing, enabling the detection of liquids with varying refractive indices. Importantly, the periodic arrangement and substrate-supported configuration not only improve the reproducibility and stability of the spectral response but also enhance sensitivity through strong near-field coupling. These features make nanoarrays a robust and versatile platform for LSPR sensing, providing reliable means to probe molecular adsorption, interfacial processes, and chemical environments with high precision.

Results and discussion

Fabrication approach and theoretical basis

The template-assisted fabrication of plasmonic nanoarrays is illustrated in Fig. 1a. The AAO membrane with through-hole pores was anodized and subsequently peeled off from an aluminum foil, then transferred onto a quartz or silicon substrate as nanomask. Details on the fabrication and manipulation of the AAO membrane can be found in Fig. S1, S2, and our previous studies25. The prepared samples underwent a vertical electron-beam evaporation to deposit plasmonic material through the AAO pores, forming nanoparticles (NPs) on the substrate. Afterwards, the AAO membranes were removed by tape, leaving behind the plasmonic nanoarrays. The parameters of the AAO nanomask, including membrane thickness and pore diameter, can be precisely controlled by the anodization process, ensuring good reproducibility among different samples batches and laboratories33.

Morphological effects on LSPRs

The basic LSPRs characteristics of the plasmonic nanoarrays are illustrated in Fig. 2. Figure 2a–f depict SEM images of the prepared nanoarrays with different diameters from ~40 nm to ~80 nm and a fixed deposition thickness of 40 nm. The particle size was modulated by adjusting the diameter of the AAO pores, which are precisely determined by the AAO anodization voltage, with a center-to-center distance maintained at 100 nm25. Fig. 2g presents the UV-vis spectra of the prepared nanoarrays, revealing a redshift with increasing diameter, from ~578 nm to ~602 nm for gold and a less pronounced shift from ~446 to ~475 nm for silver. The redshift of localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) observed with increasing nanoparticle diameter is primarily attributed to retardation effects and dynamic depolarization36,37. As the nanoparticle size increases, the phase retardation across the particle weakens the restoring force acting on the oscillating conduction electrons, thereby lowering the resonance frequency and causing a redshift. In addition, a larger particle diameter typically reduces the spacing between adjacent particles and enhances both interparticle plasmonic coupling, further facilitating the redshift. These experimental observations are in good agreement with finite element method (FEM) simulations, as shown in Fig. 2h (detailed simulated extinction spectra see Supplementary Fig. S3). It should also be noted that, in both experiments and simulations, the periodic array layout introduces collective interactions between neighboring particles. Compared with an isolated particle on the same substrate, these interactions can cause a redshift and extra broadening of the LSPR mode (see Supplementary Fig. S4).

Figure 3 illustrates the influence of deposition thickness on LSPRs frequencies. SEM images in Fig. 3a–d reveal the morphological evolution of gold nanostructures with increasing deposition thicknesses from 20 to 50 nm. As thickness rises, the initially flat nanodisks transition into three-dimensional (3D) spherical crown-like morphologies. This structural transformation arises from two key factors: (1) thermal energy during the evaporation process promotes the reorganization of nanostructures into pronounced 3D spherical crown-like morphologies rather than flat nanosheets, and (2) the AAO pores are progressively blocked by the deposition material, altering the particle curvature.

As shown in Fig. 3e, the UV-vis extinction spectra of the gold nanoarrays exhibits a blue shift of approximately 50 nm, accompanied by a narrowing of the full width at half maximum (FWHM). Similar trends were also observed in the silver nanoarrays with a shift of ~40 nm. These blue shifts contrast with typical size-dependent red shifts for planar structures, suggesting that the changes in the out-of-plane geometry modifies electron oscillation confinement, thereby increasing resonance energy. This experimentally observed blue-shift aligns with the FEM numerical simulation results (see Supplementary Fig. S3), as depicted in Fig. 3f. In the simulation, the nanoparticle was approximated as a hemispherical half-nanorod, and the metal dielectric function was described using a Drude model. Maxwell’s equations were then solved to obtain the LSPR response. Due to this model choice, inter-particle coupling is not included in the simulation. Notably, this blue shift persisted across simulations with varied NP morphologies, confirming its generality beyond specific structural assumptions. While Mie’s theory Eq.(2) is restricted to ideal spherical particles, the observed behavior necessitates Richard Gans’ extended Mie theory which accounts for spheroidal geometries under the small-particle approximation38. This conclusion is not limited to oblate ellipsoids but can also be extended to prolate ellipsoids for silver, gold, copper, and other plasmonic materials within their respective response ranges.

Notably, several simplifications were made during the calculations. For instance, interactions between particles in the nanoarrays were ignored. The Gans model employed here describes an individual nanoparticle and explicitly neglects near-field coupling between neighboring particles. In our experiment, especially, 80 nm particles are separated by gaps of ~20 nm, the electromagnetic coupling between adjacent particles may induce a weak redshift of the LSPR compared to the isolated-particle case. Additionally, the morphometry of the deposited particles in this experiment is approximated as half-ellipsoids rather than full ellipsoids. Furthermore, the dielectric environment is more complex due to the presence of both the substrate and surrounding medium, which were not fully accounted for in the model. Despite these limitations, the modified equation provides insights in explaining the observed blueshift in LSPR spectra. The results reveal that the deposition thickness can be independently tuned, providing an additional approach to modulate the LSPR modes.

Thermal annealing can reduce defects and improve the crystal quality of the nanoparticles, thereby boosting their stability and functional efficacy in applications such as SERS and catalysis. During annealing, processes like surface diffusion and Ostwald ripening refine the particles, resulting in more uniform and well-defined shapes39. Fig. 4a–d presents SEM images illustrating the transformation of the gold nanoarrays before and after annealing. Upon annealing, these particles gradually reshape into smoother, more spherical structure and the preferred orientation of face-centered cubic with (111) crystal planes are more pronounced (see in Supplementary Fig. S5), while maintaining the integrity of their hexagonal patterns. Additionally, the small satellite clusters initially formed during e-beam evaporation were largely reduced and merged into the primary particles. Meanwhile, surface roughness around the main nanoparticles was partially smoothed out. These morphological changes also contributed to the observed LSPR blueshift and the reduced linewidth.

Corresponding to these morphological changes, the extinction spectra in Fig. 4e–h suggest significant transformations in LSPRs responses. The spectra of the pristine nanoparticles feature a broad plasmon resonance peak, reflecting their irregular shapes and size distribution. Following annealing, the peak sharpened from ~120 nm to ~80 nm, and shifted to shorter wavelengths from 641 to 549 nm, as the annealing temperature was increased from 120 to 360 °C. This significant blue shift in the spectra can be attributed to a decrease in particle diameter, an increase in particle height, and alterations in cluster-particle coupling dynamics.

Precision tuning of LSPRs: composition engineering and polarization-dependent properties

Plasmonic materials feature unique intrinsic LSPR characteristics that vary based on their dielectric constants Eq.(1). This enables precise tuning of LSPR modes by constructing heterojunction NPs. Silver has a relatively lower imaginary component of its dielectric function compared to gold at corresponding wavelengths, indicating reduced optical losses and damping rates. Since the FWHM of the LSPR band is inversely proportional to plasmon lifetime, silver should exhibit a narrower resonance peak with sharper spectral features.

The UV-vis extinction spectra in Fig. 5 illustrates the impact of metal composition ratios on LSPRs, with a shift from approximately 475 to 625 nm. The insets show structural schematic of the heterojunction NPs, which consist of layers of different plasmonic materials. Notably, the LSPR spectral shifts are determined by both the composition ratio and the layer configurations. As revealed in the figures, a more pronounced shift occurred when gold served as the bottom layer. In contrast, the LSPR bands become broader when gold serves the top layer, indicating more dispersed plasmonic fields. These trends can be understood by considering the combined effects of the local dielectric environment and particle morphology. The metal layer in contact with the substrate primarily experiences a higher effective refractive index and therefore has a stronger influence on the LSPR peak position. Meanwhile, the top layer, together with associated surface morphology, more strongly affects damping and spectral broadening, and thus contributes more to the FWHM. In addition, because the bottom layer has a larger effective lateral size, inter-particle coupling may also contribute to a redshift in the array response. Overall, the spectral differences arise from the coupled effects of deposition sequence, dielectric environment, and particle shape. It should be noted that the Au-Ag multilayer structures were obtained by sequential electron-beam deposition without post-annealing, accordingly, a stratified multilayer architecture, rather than a homogeneous Au-Ag alloy, is expected.

The template-assisted approach can be employed to fabricate AuNPs with anisotropic polarization responses26. As illustrated in Fig. 6a, angle-resolved deposition allows the deposition of ellipsoidal nanoparticles through the AAO pores, using AAO nanomasks with precisely controlled height and pore diameter. By tilting the sample to the desired angle during the E-beam evaporation process, the morphology of the resulting particles can be modulated. Specifically, the short axis of the ellipsoidal AuNPs is controlled by the deposition angle, while the long axis is determined by the pore diameter of the AAO membranes.

a Schematics illustrating the fabrication of ellipsoidal pure Au nanoarrays using angle-resolved shadow deposition. b SEM images of the ellipsoidal NPs on silicon substrate with a short axis of ~35 nm and long axis of ~70 nm, and c corresponding UV-vis extinction spectra under different polarization angles. d SEM images of the ellipsoidal NPs on silicon substrate with a short axis of ~50 nm and long axis of ~70 nm, and (e) corresponding UV-vis extinction spectra under different polarization angles

Samples with different short axis lengths were prepared to investigate the polarization-dependence of the ellipsoidal NPs. As revealed by polarized UV-vis spectroscopy in Fig. 6b, c, for the gold nanoarrays with a long axis of 70 ± 2 nm and short axis of 35 ± 2 nm, the corresponding LSPR peaks were located at 673 nm under perpendicular excitation (θ = 90°) and 561 nm under horizontal polarization (θ = 0°). When the polarization angle is varied between 0° and 90°, both LSPR modes can be observed. When the short-axis length is enlarged to 50 nm, the UV-vis spectra reveal an unchanged long axis mode at θ = 90° and red-shifted short axis mode to 590 nm, as revealed in Fig. 6d, e. The results demonstrate that the polarization-dependent anisotropic LSPR modes can be independently tuned by regulating the long and short axis of the ellipsoidal NPs. Besides, the aspect ratio between long and short axes can also be modulated by annealing, as revealed in Supplementary Figs. S6–S8.

Refractive index sensing with plasmonic nanoarrays

When a plasmonic nanoparticle is illuminated, the incident light generates an oscillating electric field that drives coherent electron oscillations, induce polarization charges at the interfaces. As outlined in Eq.(2), the polarizations can be enhanced by the surrounding dielectric medium, thereby stabilizing the electron oscillations and reducing the energy needed to excite them (shifting the LSPR to a lower energy). This enhancement is governed by the dielectric constant of the medium εm, which determines the ability to redistribute charges in response to the electric field.

The dielectric environment for nanoarrays is complex due to the presence of both the substrate and surrounding environment. In this study, we placed the nanoarrays on quartz and immersed them into commercial refractive index liquids, as shown in Fig. 7a. The LSPR modes can be finely tuned by modulating the refractive index of the liquids from 1.0 to 1.6 to demonstrate sensing performance in environments with different refractive indices. Figue 7b depicts the FEM simulation of Au nanoparticles, illustrating the distribution of surrounding plasmonic hotspots. The UV-vis spectra of the Au and Ag nanoarrays with a diameter of ~80 nm and thickness of 40 nm is shown in the Fig. 7c, e, respectively, revealing a significant redshift with increasing environmental refractive index. The corresponding LSPR peak position were extracted and plotted in Fig. 7d, f, respectively. The results reveal an approximately linear relationship between the peak position and the environmental refractive index within the data range. Notably, quartz was employed as the substrate material in this study. In addition to the surrounding medium, the dielectric constant of the substrate plays a similar role in influencing the LSPR behavior. For comparison, Supplementary Fig. S9 presents the UV-vis spectra of Au and Ag nanoarrays with diameters of ~40 nm in solutions of varying refractive indices. For nanoarrays composed of the same material, an increase in particle diameter leads to a steeper slope in the linear fit of peak position versus refractive index, indicating enhanced refractive index sensitivity. This observation can be attributed to two related mechanisms. First, larger particles exhibit a higher polarizability and less confined plasmonic fields. Consequently, variations in the liquid refractive index induce a larger change in the effective dielectric environment, resulting in a larger wavelength shift and a higher refractive-index sensitivity. In addition, the LSPR mode of larger particles is typically located at longer wavelengths where the wavelength response to a given dielectric environment is generally enhanced, further amplifying the observed shift.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we developed an AAO template-assisted strategy for the scalable and precise fabrication of plasmonic nanoarrays. By independently controlling key parameters including nanoparticle diameter, deposition thickness, and surface roughness, we achieve multidimensional tuning of LSPR modes and systematically investigate their regulatory mechanisms, including the effect of metal composition, dielectric environment, and polarization dependence. In particular, the thickness-controlled morphological evolution from flat nanodisks to three-dimensional crown-like particles during evaporation leads to an unexpected blueshift of the LSPR, providing an additional and efficient degree of freedom for spectral engineering. Moreover, the tunable morphology and periodicity of the fabricated nanoarrays also enable effective plasmonic refractive index sensing, where the LSPR peak exhibits a red shift with increasing surrounding refractive index and the refractive index sensitivity improves with larger nanoparticle diameters. Importantly, the AAO-based approach offers high fabrication reproducibility, feasibility in LSPR tuning, and broad substrate compatibility, which are essential for practical LSPR applications. Our approach are high reproducibility, simple fabrication, and broad substrate compatibility. Our study not only advances the fundamental understanding of LSPR engineering but also establishes a versatile strategy for designing plasmonic nanoarrays sensing tailored for specific functional applications.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Au (99.999%) and Ag (99.999%) were purchased from Zhongnuo Advanced Material (Beijing) Technology Co., Ltd. Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), model 950PMMA A4, was purchased from Micro Chem (Dalian) Co., Ltd. Phosphoric acid (85 wt.%), copper (II) chloride dihydrate ( ≥ 99.7%), acetone ( ≥ 99.7%) and absolute ethanol ( ≥ 99.7%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. All the reagents were used as received without further purification, and Milli-Q water ( > 18.2 MΩ cm) was utilized in all experiments.

Preparations of the AAO membranes

Briefly, electrochemically polished aluminum foils were anodized in 0.3 M oxalic acid at 40 V and 0 °C to fabricate the porous AAO membrane by a two-step anodization method25. The initial diameter of the pores was ~30 nm, and the center-to-center spacing between two adjacent pores was ~100 nm. The fabricated membranes with small pores were etched in a 5 wt.% phosphoric acid solution at 30 °C to broaden the pore diameter. The broadened membranes were spin-coated at 1200 rpm for 60 s with a Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) polymer layer for protection, and baked at 120 °C for 10 min to ensure that PMMA filled the pores. Afterwards, the AAO membranes were transferred to 5 wt.% NaOH solution for 15 min and then 10 wt.% CuCl2 solution to remove the aluminum. The AAO membranes were subsequently immersed in a mixed solution of 5 wt.% phosphoric acid and 2 wt.% CuCl2 at 30 °C for 35 min to remove the AAO barrier layer and further adjust the membrane thickness. Afterwards, they were immersed in acetone to remove PMMA, obtaining the free-standing AAO membranes. Finally, the AAO membranes were rinsed with Milli-Q water and transferred onto solid substrates. The detailed parameters for the vertical electron-beam deposition based on AAO membranes can be found in Supplementary Fig. S10.

Sample characterizations

The SEM images were obtained by FEI Inspect F50 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) to study the surface morphologies. The UV-vis spectra were acquired from a Hitachi U-3900 spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Ltd.) to study the LSPR modes. The optical extinction spectra of the plasmonic nanoarrays were measured by a Hitachi U-3900 spectrophotometer operated in absorption mode. The samples were fabricated on transparent quartz substrates and positioned perpendicular to the incident light beam during measurement. The spectra were recorded over the wavelength range of 400-800 nm with a spectral resolution of 1 nm. A bare quartz substrate was used as the reference for baseline correction. All measurements were performed at room temperature.

FEM numerical simulations

The FEM numerical simulations were carried by COMSOL Multiphysics software (the modeling data is shown in Supplementary Fig. S11). The LSPR modes were excited by a linearly polarized plane wave perpendicular to the x-y plane. The incident light wavelength was from 400 to 800 nm and the electric field density E0 was 1 V/m. The complex refractive index (n + ik) of Au and Ag were obtained from the ref.40. Scattering boundary conditions were applied to define the boundary, and periodic boundary conditions were employed, utilizing a lattice with a center-to-center distance of 100 nm, in reference to the AAO membranes lattice. Structural parameters were extracted based on SEM characterizations. In the investigation of the effect of particle size on LSPR, the nanoparticles were modeled as hemispheres with a fixed height of 40 nm. The center-to-center distance between adjacent particles was set to 100 nm, and the particle diameters varied from 40 to 80 nm. For the study of the influence of deposition thickness on LSPR, the nanoparticles were also modeled as hemispheres with a fixed diameter of 72 nm and a center-to-center distance of 100 nm, while the thickness was varied from 20 to 50 nm. For simulations of nanoparticles with different gaps, the particle diameter is 80 nm and the thickness is 40 nm, with a center-to-center distance of 100–140 nm. In all cases, the hemispherical nanoparticles in the array were assumed to be in intimate contact with a transparent quartz substrate (refractive index, n = 1.45), with air (n = 1.0) as the superstrate. In the FEM simulations, the nanoparticle was modeled as a truncated hemisphere sitting on a flat substrate, with a finite circular contact area defined by the intersection between the particle and the substrate plane.
