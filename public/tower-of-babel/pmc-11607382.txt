Predictors of presence of and search for meaning in life among Omani students during the COVID-19 pandemic: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Tamadhir Al-Mahrouqi, Reem Al-Obeidani, Mohamed Elsherif, Manar Al Shehi, Maryam Al-Mukhaini, Salim Al-Huseini, Firdous Jahan, Naser Al Balushi, Mohammed Al Alawi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the personal and academic factors associated with the presence and search for meaning in life among college students in Oman. A cross-sectional study was conducted in April 2021. A self-reported survey comprising the Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ) and a sociodemographic questionnaire was completed by 970 students at the National University of Science and Technology in Oman. We used multiple linear regression to explore the independent predictors. Compared with engineering students, medical students were found to have a higher degree of both the presence of meaning in life as well as the search for meaning in life (p-value 0.001), and with each advancing academic year, the presence of meaning in life was found to be lower (p-value = 0.002). Students with chronic physical disease had a lower degree of presence of meaning in life and a lower degree of search for meaning in life (p = 0.001) compared with those without chronic disease. In addition, mental illness was associated with a lower degree of presence of meaning in life (p-value 0.001) and financial strain was associated with a lower degree of presence of meaning in life (p-value = 0.001). In conclusion, no prior research demonstrated higher levels of meaning in life among medicine major students compared to those in engineering or pharmacy majors. Moreover, other academic, socio-economic, and health-related factors correlated with individuals’ sense of meaning & search in life. Therefore, psychologists and psychiatrists should consider these diverse factors when designing interventions to support individuals in exploring and enhancing their meaning in life, considering their unique needs and contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-80999-7.

Introduction

While various fields and work areas have been impacted as a result of COVID-19, students appear to have compounded stress. Under normal circumstances, Omani undergraduate students, particularly medical students, typically exhibit high levels of perceived stress with a prevalence rate of approximately 51.4%1. During the COVID-19 pandemic, a cross-sectional study has shown that Omani medical interns exhibited high levels of anxiety with a prevalence rate of 44.1%, and high levels of depression with a prevalence rate of 47.1%2. As a result, the pandemic has become a major stressor for students, and meaning-making has been shown to develop resilience. Al-Mahrouqi et al.3 found that the presence of meaning in life (MIL) decreased perceived stress and, had a positive mediating effect on resilience among Omani students. Additionally, Yldrm et al.4 conducted a study during the COVID-19 pandemic and revealed that meaning in life was a predictive effect of resilience, which, in turn, was predictive of psychological health.

MIL has been defined as “the sense made of, and significance felt regarding, the nature of one’s being and existence”5. The idea of attributing meaning to life and cultivating meaningfulness in the face of adversity can be traced back to philosophical and religious traditions in that they present the individual with a set of values and attitudes that help individuals thrive6. The definition of MIL varies from field to field, but there are general characteristics that researchers agree upon. The presence of MIL implies the presence of positive feelings, such as relatedness, fulfillment, and significance to a perceived understanding of life7. In addition, possessing meaning pertains not only to positive feelings of fulfillment and purpose but also involves a conscious cognitive component. The presence of meaning involves (1) a personal, subjective understanding of life, (2) the consistent use of this understanding as a framework to derive fulfillment and meaning in life, and (3) positive self-evaluation of one’s self and life experiences8. Personal meaning in life can be defined as ‘an individually constructed cognitive system, that is grounded in values and is capable of endowing life with personal significance and satisfaction9.

In a Western context, Steger et al.10 revealed a positive association between MIL, greater life satisfaction, and positive affect. Life meaningfulness was also found to be related to self-esteem11,12, extraversion13, self-efficacy, and control14,15 of psychological and spiritual well-being16,17. Conversely, a lack of spiritual meaning and meaninglessness is related to high levels of neuroticism18, depression19, or even noogenic neurosis20. Thus, MIL is crucial when individuals are confronted with stressful situations because the attempt to make meaning of suffering serves almost as a buffer21.

The concept of MIL is deeply rooted in existential psychology, which posits that individuals seek meaning as a fundamental psychological drive. Viktor Frankl’s logotherapy, for instance, emphasizes the role of meaning as a central element in coping with life’s adversities, suggesting that the search for meaning can lead to personal growth and resilience (22). Additionally, positive psychology frameworks highlight the association between MIL and psychological well-being, positing that a strong sense of meaning contributes to greater life satisfaction, resilience, and overall mental health (5). These theoretical foundations provide a context for understanding the significance of MIL in navigating the challenges posed by the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly among students who face compounded stress due to academic and personal pressures. By grounding our study in these theories, we aim to explore how MIL serves as a protective factor, fostering resilience and psychological well-being among Omani students during these unprecedented times.

There is abundant literature regarding personality traits that positively predict MIL. Self-compassion and perfectionist striving positively predicted MIL22. In addition to self-efficacy and intrinsic aspirations23. Research on the factors correlated with students’ perceived meaning in life is limited, especially concerning Arab students. This scarcity highlights the importance of recognizing personal and academic factors—such as physical health, mental illness, financial strain, and academic programs—as potential correlates of meaning in life (MIL) among Oman college students. Rooted in existential psychology and positive psychology frameworks, our study aims to uncover predictors of MIL in this population amidst the COVID-19 pandemic. While previous research has identified personality traits like self-compassion and self-efficacy as predictors of MIL23.

Despite the abundant body of literature on MIL in Western contexts, there is a significant gap in understanding how MIL operates among Arab students, particularly in Oman. The unique cultural and academic pressures faced by these students, especially amidst the COVID-19 pandemic, emphasize the need for research that is both contextually and culturally relevant. This study addresses this critical gap by exploring the predictors of MIL in Omani students, a population that has been underrepresented in psychological research. Furthermore, the findings from this study have the potential to inform culturally sensitive mental health interventions in educational institutions, offering valuable insights for counselors and educators aiming to support students’ resilience and well-being during and beyond the pandemic.

Method

Study design and sitting

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the National University of Sciences and Technology, Oman between April 1st and 30th, 2021. Study participants were recruited from the College of Medicine, the College of Pharmacy, and the College of Engineering. The National University of Sciences and Technology, located in Oman, offers a wide range of undergraduate, and graduate academic programs through its three colleges: the College of Pharmacy, the College of Engineering, and the College of Medicine. These programs include B. Pharm, Doctor of Medicine (MD), diploma, BEng, and MSc. degrees in various Engineering specialties.

Data source and collection

A self-administered online questionnaire was delivered by e-mail to all National University of Science and Technology students enrolled for the academic year 2021–2022. To optimize student participation, we enlisted batch chief students to elucidate the study objectives and importance during routine group lecture sessions, attended by the entire batch. This outreach approach ensured that all students were well-informed and motivated to participate in the study. 970 of the 1900 Omani students at the University participated in the survey, yielding a 51% response rate.

Causes of participants’ missing data

Participants’ reduced responses were primarily due to the non-responsiveness, and use of the English language for the questionnaire which is considered the second language in Oman. Efforts were made to follow up with non-responders.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for selecting study participants were as follows: Omani undergraduate students currently enrolled at the National University of Science and Technology for the academic year 2021–2022 and who have experienced the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on their academic and personal life received invitations and were deemed eligible for participation. Before filling out the online questionnaire, participants received information regarding the study objectives and were requested to voluntarily submit an informed consent. Each participant received an electronic confirmation guaranteeing the confidentiality and voluntary nature of their participation. Participants who refused to sign the electronic informed consent form were excluded from participation.

Measurements outcomes

The sociodemographic questionnaire

Age, gender, academic major, academic year, and physical or mental disease status were obtained from each participant. In addition, we obtain data on whether they are currently living with family, friends, or alone, as well as whether they perceive any financial strain.

Meaning in life questionnaire (MLQ)

The MLQ consists of ten items as an operational representation of the meaning in life construct by Steger et al.5 it is consisting of two different constructs: 5 items to assess the presence of meaning in the participant’s lives and another 5-items to assess the engagement of participants in searching and finding meaning to their lives. Participants are asked to rate their degree of agreement on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = Absolutely untrue to 7 = Absolutely true. The total score is the sum of all questions in each category ranging from 7 to 35, higher scores correlate with higher levels of presence of meaning and searching for meaning in their lives. The Cronbach alpha coefficients of the MLQ-Presence scale were reported between 0.82 and 0.86 and for the MLQ-Search scale between 0.86 and 0.87 in a student sample5. The reliability and validity of the MLQ scale have been reported in different cultures24–26. In the current study, the reliability was 0.870 by Cronbach’s alpha.

Regarding construct validity in our sample, although confirmatory factor analysis was not conducted, principal component analysis confirmed the two-factor structure (supplementary material 1) available in (the supplementary materials).

Operational definitions

Chronic physical disease is defined as

If a participant indicated the presence of at least one chronic disease from an extensive list of disorders in the online survey, he or she was considered to have a chronic physical disease. The survey includes respiratory diseases (for example, asthma), gastrointestinal diseases (for example, coeliac disease), neurological diseases (for example, migraine), endocrine diseases (for example, diabetes), hematological diseases (for example, sickle cell disease), and dermatological diseases (for example, eczema).

Mental illness is defined as

If a participant indicated the presence of at least one mental illness from an extensive list of disorders in the online survey, then they were considered to have a mental illness. The survey includes anxiety disorders, depressive disorders, bipolar disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorders, sleep disorders, eating disorders, and personality disorders.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented in the form of frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, while the mean and standard deviation are used to present the numerical variables. Cronbach’s alpha is reported to evaluate the internal consistency of the meaning in life questionnaire and its subscales. Multiple linear regression is used to study the association of different variables with the search for meaning in the life subscale, the presence of meaning in the life subscale, and the total scale. IBM SPSS 28 software for Windows was used for the statistical analysis. P-value < 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Research Ethics and Biosafety Committee, at the National University of Science and Technology. Sohar, Oman. Ethical approval number 6421, and the research procedures were performed by the declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from all study participants.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the independent predictors associated with the presence and search for meaning in life among Omani students. A key focus was on identifying personal and academic factors related to the search for meaning dimension, and the presence of meaning dimension in the MLQ amid the unprecedented circumstances of COVID-19. Variables that demonstrated correlations with the presence and search for meaning in life included academic major type and year, mental illness, chronic physical diseases, and perceived financial strain.

Our results reflect a positive correlation between studying medicine and the presence of and search for meaning in life. Literature in this area is surprisingly scarce. However, a study by Li et al. has shown that freshmen in medicine have an overall high level of meaning in life27 which is in line with our findings. Additionally, the study depicted a significant association between professional identity and meaning in life. This finding is consistent with the discrepancy in the degree of presence of meaning between medical and engineering students in our study. Moreover, it appears that students find more meaning in the earlier years of their major. The former years of study are likely to be more stimulating and transitional than the latter years. At the start of the undergraduate years, students are at an important phase of their youth that contributes greatly to the development of personality and self-esteem28,29. Thus higher presence of meaning in life in the early academic years.

Students who have a mental illness, possess lower levels of meaning in life than students who do not. These results are consistent with the existing literature on the correlation between psychological well-being and the presence of meaning in life18,19. Mental disorders are typically associated with significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning. These contribute greatly to an individual’s overall well-being and the lack thereof may result in decreased meaningfulness in one’s life. Moreover, previous research showed that adherence to treatment for mental health illness seems to assist in promoting a meaningful sense of life and improving overall well-being30.

Similarly, students with chronic physical diseases have low levels of both the presence of meaning in life and the search for meaning. The former may be attributed to the compounded levels of stressors in a student’s life in addition to that brought about by their diseases1,2,30. This could also affect one’s ability to actively search for meaning as the process not only entails a significant cognitive load and reflective skills but also certain personal attributes such as a growth mindset and intrinsic motivation31.

Our results depict the same trend in the relationship between financial strain and meaning in life. Meaning in life was found to be negatively correlated to existing financial struggles. Research regarding financial burdens and meaning in life for college students is relatively scarce32. A study by Robak et al.33 showed that a student’s desire to earn money was not associated with well-being. Instead, their findings show that having the financial resources to help others and to feel secure and proud of oneself were predictive of positive feelings such as happiness and general well-being. This emphasizes the concept of meaning-making as a whole, even in terms of money and its symbolism33. On the other aspect financial strain can significantly impact students’ academic performance and psychological well-being. Students experiencing financial difficulties may face challenges in concentrating on their studies and may need to work part-time, which can affect their academic outcomes34. Further, building on Maslow’s hierarchy of needs theory, financial stability, and mental and physical well-being is key to self-actualization and meaning-making35. However, one could still have meaning and search for meaning in the face of life’s adversities such as illness and financial challenges.

Limitations

Several limitations were observed in this study. Firstly, the use of self-reported online measures may introduce biases inherent to self-reporting. However, due to COVID-19 restrictions, online assessments were necessary. Future studies could benefit from incorporating qualitative interviews to deepen understanding of participants’ experiences with MIL. Additionally, the convenience sample drawn from engineering, pharmaceutical, and medical students at a single Omani institution limits the generalizability of findings.

Conclusion

We sought to investigate the variables that predict meaning in life for Omani college students under these circumstances. According to the author’s knowledge, no prior research documented higher meaning in life among medicine major students compared to those in engineering or pharmacy majors. Students who struggled with either chronic physical diseases or mental illness were found to have a lower level of meaning in life. Similarly, the perceived financial strain was negatively correlated with student’s perception of their life meaningfulness. Moreover, Students find more meaning in life during their junior years than in their final years of their major. Policymakers at student counseling centers can utilize these results to bolster the effectiveness of the services they provide. Through understanding the factors that affect meaning in a student’s life, clinicians at counseling, and well-being university centers can form a better therapeutic alliance, formulate the most effective treatment plans, and work with other departments to provide the student with a smooth university experience.
