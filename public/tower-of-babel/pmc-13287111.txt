Mitigating Antimicrobial Resistance Through an Ecological One-Health Framework
Authors: Poonam Kanojiya, Amrita Bhagwat, Kunal Dixit, Ujjayni Saha, Sunil D Saroj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents one of the most pressing global health and ecological challenges of the twenty-first century. Resistant organisms and genes move freely across humans, animals, food systems, and the environment, with wastewater, soil, and wildlife acting as critical reservoirs. This interconnectedness highlights the need for a coordinated One Health framework that recognises AMR as both a medical and ecological crisis. In this review, we examine the mechanisms driving the emergence and dissemination of AMR and explore how stewardship, ecological management, and policy interventions can be integrated to mitigate its spread. We particularly consider how selective pressures operating across clinical and environmental settings influence resistance persistence and transmission. Evidence shows that judicious antimicrobial use in healthcare and veterinary medicine, coupled with environmental safeguards such as wastewater management and effluent regulation, can reduce selective pressure and transmission risk. Case studies illustrate how coordinated surveillance across sectors provides early warning of emerging resistomes, while stewardship programmes prolong the efficacy of existing antibiotics. Together, these findings highlight the importance of integrated responses that address resistance across interconnected human, animal, and environmental systems. By situating AMR within the broader human–animal–environment interface, this review underscores the critical importance of ecological thinking in designing sustainable solutions. Introduction

Bacterial infections continue to cause significant morbidity and mortality worldwide, and their impact is increasingly determined by antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Over time, bacteria have evolved the capacity to withstand antibiotics, with resistance shaped by a complex interplay of host and environmental factors that ultimately influences infection severity and transmission dynamics (Tillotson and Zinner, 2017). Understanding these parameters is essential for developing strategies that reduce infection burden and pathogenicity.

AMR is now spreading rapidly across the globe, jeopardising the treatment of common infections such as pneumonia, urinary tract infections, and tuberculosis. It also threatens the safety of advanced medical interventions including organ transplantation, cancer chemotherapy, and intensive care procedures. The resulting consequences—prolonged hospital stays, higher medical costs, and reduced survival rates—are particularly severe in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where AMR undermines sustainable development (Waddington et al., 2022). Left unchecked, AMR will further compromise the achievement of several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs 2, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 17) and is projected to drive an additional twenty-four million people into extreme poverty by 2030 (Kotwani et al., 2021).

Recognising its scale, AMR must be understood as both a global health and ecological crisis. Although AMR encompasses resistance across multiple pathogen groups, much of the current burden is driven by bacterial resistance to antibiotics; accordingly, this review primarily emphasises antibiotic resistance within the broader AMR framework. The “One Health” approach provides a holistic framework that emphasises the interconnected health of humans, animals, and the environment. This perspective, increasingly promoted by organisations such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), is essential for sustainable solutions. AMR not only disrupts healthcare but also contributes to dysbiosis of the natural microbiota, with imbalances that compromise host health, promote infection, and facilitate disease development (CDC, 2019; Coque et al., 2023). A recent study by Woh et al. suggested that AMR development and dissemination are also affected by changes in the climate. This suggests the need for collaboration in various sectors by employing a One Health platform (Woh et al., 2025).

To address these challenges, strategies that restrict the indiscriminate use of antibiotics and strengthen stewardship are critical. This review examines the mechanisms by which AMR develops, explores the role of antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) in slowing its progression, and highlights the implications of AMR within a One Health framework.

Antibiotic Resistance (ABR)

Antibiotics were first discovered in the twentieth century. Antibiotics are molecules used to kill or restrict the growth of bacteria. However, the pathogens are continuously evolving to develop resistance to antibiotics. The development of antimicrobial resistance is not restricted to exposure to antibiotics but is an outcome of defined and regulated social interaction among bacteria and their immediate environment (Wang et al., 2023). According to the history of antibiotic discovery, the pharmaceutical industry created new classes of antibiotics just for two decades, from 1960 to 1980. After that, the rate of discovery drastically reduced until recently. Experts have ample grounds to foresee an impending post-antibiotic age, given the disproportionate ratio of drug-resistant organisms to the number of antibiotics accessible (Iskandar et al., 2020). As antibiotic use is not constrained to humans only, the environmental spread of antibiotics and dissemination of genes imparting resistance have contributed to the exponential increase in ABR. The use of antibiotics as a preventative measure can also contribute to the development of ABR. Bacteria have evolved and developed various strategies to escape the antimicrobials Munita and Arias (2016). The specific mechanisms utilised are shown in Fig. 1.

These include decreased drug uptake, active efflux via membrane pumps, enzymatic inactivation of antibiotics, modification of drug targets, and the use of alternative metabolic enzymes or pathways that bypass inhibited cellular processes. In the environment, consistent exposure to antibiotics leads to various types of co-selection and development of AMR. Sub-inhibitory concentrations in wastewater, soils, and agricultural settings may further support persistence and exchange of resistance determinants. Also, additional exposure to compounds like disinfectants and biocides promotes alterations and further AMR. Moreover, exposure to heavy metals is also linked to the development of AMR. Waste from pharmaceutical industries is hotspots for AMR development due to sustained antimicrobial loads and environmental persistence.

Evolution and Dissemination of ABR.

Antibiotic resistance (ABR) has been influenced by interactions between hosts, microbes, and environmental reservoirs and is an eco-evolutionary process. The emergence of resistance is driven by different selective factors operating across clinical and non-clinical ecosystems. Other than therapeutic antibiotic exposure, sub-inhibitory antibiotic concentrations in wastewater, agricultural soil, and animal production systems can select for resistant variants and promote mutagenesis and gene exchange. Co-selective agents like biocides and heavy metals further Davies and Davies (2010) enhance resistance by preserving mobile genetic elements that carry numerous resistance determinants (Andersson & Hughes, 2014; Seiler & Berendonk, 2012). Fitness costs associated with resistance acquisition include reduced growth rate, metabolic burden, or diminished capacity to survive in an antibiotic-free environment. However, these costs may vary depending on ecological context and microbial competition within communities. The compensatory evolution, where secondary mutations preserve resistance while restoring cellular fitness, usually mitigates these costs over time. This mechanism explains why resistant lineages remain even when antibiotic pressure is removed and stabilises them (Andersson & Hughes, 2014; Melnyk et al., 2015).

The ecological transmission of resistance throughout environmental components like water bodies, soil, wastewater treatment plants, and host associated microbiomes is largely dependent on horizontal gene transfer (HGT). The exchange of resistance genes between environmental resistant bacteria and clinically important pathogens occurs via transposons, plasmids, integrons, bacteriophages, and natural transformation. According to researchers, these settings serve as evolutionary hotspots where genetic diversity and selection stress combine, promoting the generation of novel resistance combinations (Smillie et al., 2011; von Wintersdorff et al., 2016; Endale et al., 2023). Such environments therefore act as important interfaces where resistance determinants can circulate between environmental and clinical bacterial populations. Thus, ABR emerges not only through mutations within pathogens but also through gene flow across the ecological compartments.

Therefore, within this framework, it becomes crucial to distinguish between resistant pathogens and the environmental resistome. Clinically important organisms that exhibit resistance traits and affect the effectiveness of the treatment are often referred to as resistant pathogens (Mancuso et al., 2021). The environmental resistome, on the other hand, is the enormous collection of resistance genes found in commensal and environmental microorganisms. This resistome may serve as a reservoir from which resistance traits can eventually be mobilised into pathogenic organisms through horizontal gene transfer (Wright, 2007). Consequently, ABR persistence should be viewed as a co-evolutionary outcome shaped by selection, fitness trade-offs, compensatory adaptation, and continuous gene exchange across ecological networks (Merker et al., 2020) (Fig 2).

ABR as a Systemic Ecosystem Imbalance

A comprehensive analysis of ABR requires investigating it within the context of ecological imbalances across the One Health triad: humans, environment, and animals, where anthropogenic pressures increasingly shape microbial ecosystems (Fig. 3). For example, when untreated agricultural or pharmaceutical effluent enters soils or other aquatic systems, these habitats shift from neutral environments to selective landscapes. Sub-lethal concentrations of antibiotics in such settings serve as evolutionary drivers and transform the environment into a reservoir for resistance genes, which are subsequently assimilated into native microbial communities through HGT (Al-Azzawi et al., 2024) Marti et al. (2014). This triggers an ecological cascade in which the reduced biotic resistance of the degraded microbiome allows resistant pathogens to outcompete the native sensitive flora. This process is known as niche colonisation. These resistant strains then spill back into human and animal systems via the food chain or water cycles, creating a self-reinforcing feedback loop (Endale et al., 2023). Such circulation highlights the interconnected nature of resistance across environmental and clinical systems. If ABR is seen as a biological invasion driven by human-caused environmental damage, mitigation can move from clinical approaches to ecosystem restoration, using competitive exclusion to combat the resistant microbes. These will help to reduce opportunities for resistance amplification across ecological interfaces.

Antimicrobial Stewardship (AMS)

Objectives of Antimicrobial Stewardship

The major aim of antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) is to monitor, advance, and assess the prudent use and application of antimicrobials to ensure their continued efficacy and safeguard public health. To contain and mitigate AMR, AMS practices, principles, and interventions are essential (Majumder et al., 2020). Collaborative efforts are the main key to the success of such programmes. A multidisciplinary team of doctors and pharmacists with expertise in infectious diseases is typically the foundation of successful AMS programmes. Increasingly, AMS is also recognised as an important component of coordinated One Health responses across sectors. Various elements make up AMS elements (Zay et al., 2023). Key Components of AMS Programmes

Guidelines and Policies:

The ideal combination of antibiotics, diagnosis, patient, timing, dose, method, and length of therapy results in the least amount of harm to the patient, and subsequent patients are the core guidelines for prescribing antibiotics. The WHO recently classified critical antibiotics into three special categories (AWaRe) to promote access (Access), monitor important medicines (Watch), and protect the effectiveness of ‘last resort’ antibiotics (Reserve) (WHO, 2018).

To improve patient treatment outcomes and safety by reducing AMR development, AMS brings together microbiologists, healthcare leaders, infectious disease experts, doctors, nurses, veterinarians, farmers, IT experts, and clinical pharmacists. They are the core foundation of the AMS programme and play an important role in forming guidelines on prescribing practices, antimicrobial usage, medication safety incidents, making changes in governmental policies and guidelines, suggesting new therapeutic options and prophylaxis, and creative diagnostic approaches. The “One Health” strategy involves an even wider range of stakeholders, including agriculturalists, veterinarians, economists, farmers, environmentalists, etc. (Lloyd and Page, 2018). To tackle the growing threat posed by AMR, AMS is widely recognised as an excellent policy planning and implementation instrument. National and international institutions must establish evidence-based policies and standards that serve as a guide for the sensible use of antimicrobials, the “One Health” approach, immunisation regimens, education, and training concerning AMR.

Education:

Clinical training and education in antimicrobial stewardship in all medical and health professional schools have resulted in the development of antibiograms, prescribing guidelines, infection control methods and practices, and hygiene standards (Lloyd and Page, 2018). To receive up-to-date, evidence-based knowledge about rational and appropriate prescriptions, health practitioners should have access to AMS education as a continuing medical education (CME) programme. Practical continuing education includes lectures, department meetings, morbidity and mortality meetings, academic detailing, one-on-one patient-directed education, e-learning, and education combined with other AMS activities (Cunningham et al., 2020). Sustained training efforts remain essential for reinforcing appropriate prescribing behaviour.

Surveillance and Monitoring:

According to a WHO update from 2018, 105 countries had surveillance systems in place for reporting drug-resistant infections affecting human health, 68 had systems in place to track antimicrobial consumption, and 123 had policies in place to control the sale of antimicrobials, including the requirement of a prescription for human use (WHO, 2018). Empirical studies state that the AMS programmes benefit from reducing the burden of AMR (Lee et al., 2018). Integrated surveillance across sectors may further support early detection of resistance trends.

Feedback and Interventions:

These are two critical mechanisms for monitoring implementation, which involve reviewing prescriptions, test data, prophylaxis, clinical notes, etc. Prospective audits and feedback (PAF) is a conventional method employed by AMS. In a PAF strategy, the team conducts a post-prescription evaluation of antimicrobials and then intervenes by making antibiotic optimisation recommendations to the main practitioner. When antibiotic optimisation initiatives are followed, PAF techniques can show a wide range of beneficial effects, including greater compliance with antibiotic prescribing guidelines, better clinical outcomes, and lower patient mortality (Wee et al., 2020). PAF programmes run three times a week at community hospitals have been shown to be effective in cutting down on antibiotic use and producing financial savings. Moreover, PAF allows prescribers to retain their autonomy in making decisions while providing them with specific benefits, including improved clinical assistance, scheduling flexibility, and educational opportunities (Vettese et al., 2013). These approaches also support accountability within healthcare systems.

Impact of AMS on the Reduction of ABR

Stewardship programmes have been found to reduce the ABR burden and spread of healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) in various facilities. This can save healthcare expenditures associated with improper prescriptions and drug usage. An investigation found a statistically significant reduction in the rates of ABR after AMS implementation, as well as the fact that AMS interventions were associated with shorter durations of antibiotic therapy, less inappropriate antimicrobial use, and a neutral effect on healthcare-associated infection rates (Al-Omari et al., 2020). Such findings support the continued expansion of AMS initiatives.

Successful AMS Interventions

AMS programmes have been effectively adopted and implemented in various prominent countries like Australia, China, South Korea, the United States (US), etc. The US was among the first developed and high-income nations to launch the antibiotic education campaign. The Veterans Health Administration, the country’s principal integrated healthcare system, has recently become significantly active around AMS, with AMS obligatory adoption at all Veterans Health Administration medical institutions by mid-2014 (Huttner et al., 2014). Realising the need to establish AMS guidelines in healthcare facilities throughout India, the ICMR held four workshops on AMS capacity building in the second quarter of 2017 (http://iamrsn.icmr.org.in/index.php/events/amsp). These examples illustrate the value of coordinated institutional engagement in strengthening stewardship outcomes.

One Health Approach to AMR

Introduction to the One Health Concept

Currently, AMR is on the priority list among the major global health concerns, which necessitates the One Health approach. To promote the overall well-being of the community, One Health acknowledges the intrinsic link between people, animals, and the environment. The spread of antibiotic resistance is facilitated by irrational management of antibiotics, agricultural waste, and the migration of animals and people carrying resistant bacteria. AMR is recognised as a global health concern by influential organisations such as the G8, the World Health Organization, and the World Bank. These organisations agree that addressing AMR requires coordinated and multidisciplinary efforts across various fields and ecosystems. One Health case studies on the genesis of AMR and the dynamics of multidrug-resistant pathogen transmission imply that human, animal, and environmental specialists must collaborate to mitigate AMR (Hernando-Amado et al., 2019). Within this perspective, AMR is increasingly viewed as a systems-level challenge shaped by interconnected ecological processes.

Interconnectedness of Human Health, Animal Health, and the Environment in ABR

Antibiotics are used by animals for various purposes, including animal therapy, prophylaxis, and growth promotion. Antimicrobials are utilised on animals more frequently than on people globally. Most antibiotic classes used in humans, including those essential to human treatment, like quinolones and broad-spectrum beta-lactams, have been approved for use in animals (Saroj, 2022). In addition to being used therapeutically on humans and animals, several antibiotics, including tetracycline, streptomycin, and triazoles, are also used on plants. Resistant zoonotic bacteria can be detected in the soil and plants. Antimicrobial use in agriculture is known to promote the transmission of fungi that are resistant to antibiotics from the environment to people (Sundin and Wang, 2018). Dosing more than the recommended antimicrobial dosage in aquaculture leads to the persistence of antibiotic residues in the aquatic environment, where they disperse quickly, exerting selective pressure on the environment (Marti et al., 2013). The widespread use of antimicrobials as growth promoters in livestock remains a major driver of resistance dissemination. These exposures reinforce cross-sector transmission through shared environmental reservoirs and food systems. Biofilms, communities of bacteria that form on surfaces such as hospital pipes, farm drainage systems, and river rocks, represent a key crossroad between humans, animals, and the environment (Kanematsu and Barry, 2020). When antibiotic waste from farms or homes enters natural Matviichuk et al. (2022) water sources, they do not always eliminate all bacteria. Instead, remaining bacteria often form biofilms. Within these tightly formed clusters, bacteria from different origins can easily exchange resistance genes, turning biofilms into safe houses for AMR. This process creates a lasting reservoir in the environment. Even if a certain antibiotic is not used anymore, the resistance it obtained can persist in biofilms, posing an ongoing risk of reinfection for both humans and animals (Kanematsu and Barry, 2020; Jacques and Malouin, 2022). These reservoirs may sustain resistance even in the absence of continued antimicrobial exposure.

Case Studies and Examples of One Health Initiatives

Numerous countries and organisations, including the WHO, Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO), and the World Organisation for Animal Health (OIE), have developed elaborate policies to address the problem of antibiotic resistance. The concept of One Health originated from the original One Medicine formula developed by veterinary epidemiologist Calvin Schwabe. A German veterinarian named Rudolf Virchow, in the nineteenth century, gave an idea for describing diseases and infections that could be transmitted between humans and animals. This idea is intricately linked to zoonosis, a term he coined; he proposed the integration of human and animal medicine to effectively address zoonotic diseases (Chien, 2013). The Wildlife Conservation Society and Rockefeller University introduced the concept of “One World, One Health” in 2004. Several disease outbreaks, such as avian influenza, mad cow disease, SARS, and West Nile virus, occurred around the same time, highlighting the relevance of this concept. As a result, “The Manhattan Principles,” a global guidebook, was released to promote this new approach (Badau, 2021). The prevalence of colistin-resistant microorganisms and their resistance determinants (such as the mcr-1 gene) in farm animals, human beings, and the environment decreased when colistin stopped being used as a livestock growth booster in several nations. The effectiveness of this One Health initiative could be harnessed to expedite the regulation of more significant antimicrobials (Rhouma et al., 2023). Such examples demonstrate how integrated policy measures can reduce selective pressure across sectors.

Critical Importance of a One Health Approach to Antimicrobial Resistance

It is impossible to precisely separate antimicrobial classes into those utilised only in the human or non-human sectors. Given that many classes will be available in both sectors, the major challenge that remains with the One Health approach is to ensure the optimal usage of the existing antibiotics. This can be achieved only when there is rational use of antibiotics for treatment and avoiding their application as a growth-promoting agent (Aslam et al., 2021). We must take additional measures to halt the transmission of resistant bacteria, not just from person to person but also within and between the human, agricultural, and environmental sectors. These will require a thorough understanding of mechanisms and pathways in AMR so that the resistance can be countered with new drugs, alternative therapeutic approaches, treatments, vaccines, and rapid diagnostic tests (Rochford et al., 2018). Successfully implementing a One Health approach requires not only developing strategies to prevent the development and spread of resistance but also involving the public and fostering collaboration and interaction (White and Hughes, 2019). Together, these approaches reinforce the need for integrated and sustained responses to AMR.

Strategies and Interventions

Strategies for Tackling AMR in Healthcare Settings

The rise of AMR in healthcare is alarming, with multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens now common in hospital-acquired infections. In China, 42.5% of such infections were linked to MDR strains of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, and ESBL-producing Escherichia coli (Wang et al., 2019). Overuse of antimicrobials, lack of monitoring systems, and poor adherence to guidelines are major contributors. In southern India, 89% of physicians acknowledged antibiotic Bruycker et al. (2013) overuse in routine practice, largely due to absent infection control policies (Thakolkaran et al., 2017). To address this, the Indian Council of Medical Research issued evidence-based treatment guidelines in 2017. Globally, awareness campaigns and initiatives promote rational use of antimicrobials, with the “Think Globally, Act Locally” approach encouraging patient stories to highlight MDR threats. Measures such as restricting prophylactic use, maintaining digital drug records to prevent counter-sales, and limiting high-risk antibiotics can reduce inappropriate use (Bankar et al., 2022). These approaches collectively support improved infection control and prescribing practices across healthcare systems.

Strategies for Addressing AMR in Agriculture, Livestock, and Veterinary Medicine

To build a comprehensive One Health approach, it is important to connect the biological mechanisms behind AMR with the societal and behavioural factors that influence how antimicrobials are used. Within agriculture, economic uncertainty and limited social protections often drive decisions about antibiotic use. As a result, farmers may rely on preventative antibiotics as an affordable “insurance policy” to guard against livestock losses where biosecurity measures are insufficient (Melaku et al., 2025). AMR in animal husbandry is an even greater concern due to weak surveillance systems. Animals, humans, and the environment act as reservoirs of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs), transferred through horizontal gene transfer (Despotovic et al., 2023). Excessive antimicrobial use to reduce economic losses and unmetabolised residues in the food chain facilitate zoonotic spread. Nearly, 200 zoonotic diseases are known, about 60% of which are capable of transferring resistance genes (Dafale et al., 2020). Rising resistance in veterinary pathogens, including first-line failures in the US, poses additional challenges (Frey, 2018). At the regional level, Europe lacks harmonised veterinary AMR monitoring; the EARS-Vet network has been proposed to bridge this gap (Mader et al., 2021). Effective mitigation therefore requires coordinated regulatory measures, improved surveillance, and sustained behavioural interventions across livestock production systems. Control strategies include regulatory measures (restricted drug classes, vaccine mandates, pricing policies) and voluntary actions (education and incentives) (Lhermie et al., 2017). Newer approaches such as next-generation sequencing (NGS) are increasingly applied to detect resistance mechanisms and support epidemiological tracking (Hilt and Ferrieri, 2022).

Environmental Considerations in AMR Mitigation

Environmental contamination with antibiotics, dating back to their widespread use since penicillin’s discovery, is a major contributor to AMR. Antibiotic residues from improper disposal enter soils, crops, and aquatic systems, driving resistance in microbial populations (Anwar et al., 2020). Wastewater plants act as key reservoirs of ARGs, and resistant organisms may spread through water supplies and aquatic life. Repeated use of higher classes of antimicrobials, combined with poor disposal practices, creates a cycle that fosters superbugs. Although disposal is monitored by pollution control boards, gaps remain due to regulatory and financial constraints (Kotwani et al., 2023). Addressing these gaps requires coordinated environmental governance and improved public awareness of responsible antimicrobial use. Current training efforts largely target medical professionals, overlooking household users. To address this, a bottom-up strategy employing social media and community influencers has been proposed to promote responsible usage and disposal (Anwar et al., 2020).

Collaborative Frameworks Against AMR

International collaborations, like those supported and funded by the ERASMUS+programme, show how important it is to coordinate efforts against AMR. The topics discussed in the book “Antimicrobial Resistance: Collaborative Measures of Control” highlight the need to connect the animal, human, and environmental health programmes, encouraging a unified One Health perspective. By bringing together insights from different fields, these resources offer a consistent foundation for tackling the complex social and ecological issues of AMR worldwide (Saroj et al., 2022). Such collaborative models highlight the value of interdisciplinary coordination in strengthening global responses to AMR.

Challenges and Future Directions

Current Challenges in Combating AMR

In the last decade, significant progress has been observed in antibacterial medication research. However, it is crucial to note that the progress has been very gradual, and considerable challenges remain. The fundamental economic issue of motivating different firms and pharmaceutical industries to create antibiotics that are rarely used continues. Continued commitment to cornerstone infection prevention and control practices, as well as renewed investments in alternative therapies, antibacterial drug discovery, and vaccine development, is required (Shields, 2024). These constraints highlight the need for balanced strategies that support innovation while reinforcing stewardship priorities.

Socioeconomic Factors

Antibiotic usage is influenced by socioeconomic factors such as money, social status, and access to quality education. In Europe, socioeconomic factors such as population income, demographic structure, general practitioner density, and mode of remuneration have all been proven to influence outpatient antibiotic use. Low and below-average income has also been demonstrated to be a factor in underdeveloped countries. Furthermore, supply-side problems and incentives associated with payment schemes are likely to be significant and should be considered in government initiatives (Iskandar et al., 2020; Melaku and Assegid, 2025). Addressing these disparities requires integrated public health approaches combining education, regulation, and improved healthcare access.

Lack of New Antibiotics

The discovery and development of antibiotics must address the epidemiology and evolution of resistance to old and novel antibiotics, as well as gaps in coverage of priority diseases scattered over diverse geographic locations. The WHO and CDC lists are an urgent call for more targeted antibiotic research and development, considering the current incidence of resistance, documenting the economic and clinical impact of pathogens, as well as the bacteria’s transmissibility, the availability of effective antibiotics, and the barriers to prevention (Iskandar et al., 2020). Future efforts must align antibiotic development with sustainable stewardship practices.

Emerging Technologies and Avenues

Several techniques with prominent physical antibacterial effects have emerged in the medical sciences arena to date. Cold plasma and nanotechnology have powerful and broad-spectrum antibacterial effects. Metal/metal oxide nanoparticles have become widespread in antibacterial usage in recent years. Various other types of nanoparticles and nanoparticles coated with antibiotics have proved to be essential for treating multidrug-resistant infections. Engineered antibodies, antibiotic enhancers, siderophore conjugates, phage therapy, photo-switchable antibiotics, quorum quenchers, and genome editing aided by the CRISPR/Cas system are paving the way for the generation of antimicrobial therapies (Savic and Ardal, 2018). However, further evaluation is needed regarding their long-term effectiveness and practical implementation.

Development of Novel Antimicrobials and Ban on (Over-The-Counter) OTC Antibiotics

Various alternatives to traditional treatments are available now. The utilisation of traditional plants, natural compounds in treatments, and the use of marine invertebrates, terrestrial insects, and vertebrate animals as possible sources of antimicrobials are regaining popularity. Antibiotic action is also found in venoms (protein and peptide combinations) from snakes, bees, scorpions, spiders, and poison frogs. Pathogen-specific monoclonal antibody construction is an exciting area of infectious disease research. AMPs have been used as antibiotics for decades, and new compounds are constantly being created (Mantravadi et al., 2019). Stringent regulatory control should be imposed on OTC selling and dispensing of oral and injectable antibiotics, which is unfortunately still a common practice in many underdeveloped and developing countries. Antibiotics should only be dispensed to serve a prescription from a qualified physician (Salam et al., 2023). Strengthening regulatory enforcement alongside public education remains essential for limiting inappropriate antimicrobial use.

Genomic and Metagenomic Approaches to Studying AMR

Metagenomics is an effective way to determine the total resistome as well as to map the presence of specific resistance genes in microbial communities. The resistome is mapped by calculating the load of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) or resistant strains in varied sample types from healthcare settings, animal farms, sewage treatment plants, and even patient guts. A recent study by Wu et al. reports the season-wise distribution of the resistome associated with airborne particulate matter in healthcare settings wherein it found a close correlation of AMR genes with bacterial communities associated with humans as well as high horizontal gene transfer potential in ARGs (Wu et al., 2022). Another study mapping ARGs in river biofilms highlights that wastewater treatment plants are important reservoirs of ARGs. They reported the co-occurrence of ARGs with Bacteroidetes incertae sedis, Cyanobacteria, and Nitrospira; however, they suggested that additional factors apart from antibiotic concentrations may drive the selection of the resistome. Sewage is a direct reflection of the community-wide gut microbiome, and metagenomics can be effectively employed for surveillance of AMR to trace the spatiotemporal variation in the resistome profile. Sewage microbiome profiling provides valuable insights into population-level resistome distribution across geographic regions (Singh et al., 2023). These approaches support improved surveillance and early detection of emerging resistance patterns.

Policy Recommendations and Global Coordination Efforts

The WHO developed a global action plan on AMR at the 68th World Health Assembly in May 2015. One of the key objectives of this plan is “to improve awareness and understanding of antimicrobial resistance through effective communication, education, and training.” WHO also conducts an annual awareness campaign with global participation, ‘Antibiotics: Handle with Care’ for the public and healthcare professionals to improve antibiotic use. World AMR Awareness Week (WAAW) is celebrated worldwide every year to generate awareness, improve understanding about AMR, and encourage good hygiene and antibiotic handling practices in public. According to the WHO global survey, a total of 60 awareness campaigns were organised by 55 countries, including 16 low/middle-income countries, from 2010 to 2016. Moreover, in 2017 more than 130 countries reported various activities during WAAW, suggesting that considerable energy has been invested in AMR communication. Further, WHO recommends the development of a Huttner et al. (2019) national committee for formulating standard care guidelines, generating a list of essential drugs, devising AMR policy on standard rational use, training on the use of antibiotics, and monitoring the overall impact of AMR control efforts. India’s national AMR policy was implemented in 2011 which focuses on mapping factors involved in the emergence and spread of AMR, strengthening preventive strategies, and promoting the discovery of new-generation antimicrobials (Uchil et al., 2014). Indian Council of Medical Research also initiated the Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance and Research Network (AMRSN) in 2013 to track the records of drug-resistant infections and AMR patterns as well as to understand the molecular mechanism of infections by MDR strains, which helps to modify treatment guidelines (Walia et al., 2019).

Implications of the Study Findings and Potential Future Directions for Research-

Understanding AMR through a One Health lens highlights the importance of integrated surveillance systems capable of tracking resistance across human, animal, and environmental sectors. Strengthening global coordination, improving access to rapid diagnostics, and expanding surveillance coverage remain essential priorities. Future research should focus on understanding transmission pathways across ecological interfaces and evaluating intervention effectiveness across sectors.

Conclusion

Antimicrobial resistance is a growing global crisis that undermines the effectiveness of life-saving treatments and threatens human, animal, and environmental health. Tackling this challenge requires more than the discovery of new drugs, as microbial communities adapt rapidly, rendering existing options ineffective. Sustainable progress depends on coordinated strategies that integrate antimicrobial stewardship, ecological responsibility, and the One Health approach.

Reducing indiscriminate antibiotic use, strengthening infection prevention, and improving hygiene practices are critical to slowing resistance. At the same time, addressing environmental contamination from healthcare, agriculture, and industry is essential to limit the reservoirs of resistant organisms and genes. Stewardship programmes can optimise antibiotic use, extend drug efficacy, and reduce healthcare costs, while awareness and education campaigns empower both professionals and the public to act responsibly. Moving forward, effective AMR mitigation will depend on strengthening cross-sector surveillance systems and improving coordination across clinical, agricultural, and environmental settings. A holistic, multidisciplinary effort that unites science, policy, and community engagement is needed to contain the spread of resistance. Prioritising integrated One Health strategies that combine stewardship, surveillance, environmental management, and behavioural interventions will be essential for long-term sustainability. Ultimately, the global fight against antimicrobial resistance must be rooted in long-term commitment and collective action to protect biodiversity, safeguard health systems, and preserve the effectiveness of antimicrobials for future generations.
