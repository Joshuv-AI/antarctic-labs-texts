What health are we talking about? Biodiversity as the missing link between One Health and Planetary Health
Authors: Jonas Geschke, Simone Sommer, Michael Schloter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Health has become a central term in global sustainability policy, yet it is often used without sufficient conceptual precision. Public health, global health, One Health, EcoHealth, GeoHealth, and Planetary Health each emphasize different dimensions of the relationship between humans, animals, and the environment. In policy contexts, however, these distinctions are frequently blurred. We argue that biodiversity is often treated as an environmental co-benefit rather than as a foundational determinant of health. This weakens the implementation of One Health approaches because biodiversity underpins disease regulation, immune system development, food and water security, ecosystem functioning, resilience, and climate adaptation. At the same time, biodiversity provides a critical link between One Health and broader Planetary Health challenges, including global environmental change and the transgression of planetary boundaries. Future health and sustainability policies should move beyond generic references to health and explicitly recognize biodiversity as part of preventive health systems.

Introduction

Health has become a guiding concept in global environmental and sustainability policy, yet its understanding differs substantially across scientific disciplines and policy arenas. In biomedical contexts, health is often approached through disease, therapy, and individual well-being. Public and global health shift attention toward populations, prevention, and governance. The One Health approach emphasizes the interdependence of human, animal, and environmental health, whereas Planetary Health places human well-being within the stability of Earth systems. Related approaches, such as EcoHealth and GeoHealth, further broaden the perspectives by emphasizing ecological and environmental processes. Although these concepts overlap, each has distinct theoretical foundations and practical implications and they should not be regarded as interchangeable (WBGU 2023; Müller et al. 2025).

International policy frameworks generally treat health as a broad societal objective rather than adopting specific health concepts. The Paris Agreement identifies health as a key concern affected by climate change (UN 2015a) but does not endorse a particular framework. Similarly, the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development addresses health through Sustainable Development Goal 3 and integrates it across multiple goals (UN 2015b), yet without distinguishing among different health concepts. The Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework is a notable exception, explicitly referencing the One Health approach as a consideration for its implementation and recognizing links between biodiversity, wildlife, and disease risks (CBD 2022).

Overall, international agreements use health as an inclusive, cross-cutting objective while avoiding detailed conceptual distinctions. This ambiguity matters because concepts shape implementation: A narrow focus on disease prevention may treat environmental degradation as an external risk, whereas broader approaches view ecosystem functioning and environmental stability as foundations of health. At the same time, despite increasing recognition of environmental determinants of health, biodiversity itself often remains insufficiently integrated into health policy and governance frameworks.

Biodiversity as a link between One Health and Planetary Health

Biodiversity is often viewed primarily as an environmental issue, yet it is increasingly recognized as a fundamental determinant of health. Even within One Health, which seeks to balance and optimize the health of people, animals and ecosystems while recognizing their interdependence (OHHLEP 2022; One Health Commission 2026), biodiversity is frequently acknowledged but rarely placed at the center of implementation. This represents a missed opportunity. Biodiversity is not merely a conservation objective that exists alongside health; it is a fundamental biological foundation through which health is generated, maintained, and protected.

One of the most direct contributions of biodiversity to health is through the production of healthy and resilient food systems. Soil biodiversity, including microbial communities and plant diversity, pollinators, and natural enemies of pests underpin soil fertility, nutrient cycling, crop productivity, plant health, and dietary diversity (Soliveres et al. 2016; Schloter et al. 2017; Bender et al. 2023; Jetter et al. 2025). These ecological processes influence micronutrient availability, reduce dependence on pesticides and antimicrobials, enhance the resilience of food production under climate stress, and ultimately improve food quality (Schloter and Schulz 2025). Biodiversity therefore provides a direct link between ecosystem functioning, nutrition, food security, and disease prevention (IPBES 2024).

Biodiversity also influences infectious disease dynamics by shaping pathogen transmission, host competence, and interactions among humans, livestock, wildlife, and their shared environments (Sessitsch et al. 2023; Meyer et al. 2024, 2025). Biodiversity loss, deforestation, land-use change, and agricultural intensification can disrupt ecological relationships and create new opportunities for pathogen spillover, although these effects are highly context dependent (Faust et al. 2018; Gibb et al. 2020; IPBES 2020). Rather than supporting the simplistic assumption that higher biodiversity always reduces disease risk, current evidence indicates that disease dynamics emerge from complex ecological systems in which biodiversity plays important regulatory roles, particularly through host–microbe interactions and trophic networks (Soliveres et al. 2016; Fackelmann et al. 2021; Sessitsch et al. 2023; Fleischer et al. 2024a, b). These findings highlight the importance of maintaining ecological integrity as part of disease prevention and health system resilience.

Beyond infectious diseases, biodiversity also contributes directly to immune development through exposure to diverse environmental microbiota. Contact with biodiverse soils, plants, animals, and outdoor environments enriches the diversity of host-associated microbial communities and supports the development of immune tolerance, particularly during early life (Roslund et al. 2020; Marques Dos Santos et al. 2023). Conversely, biodiversity loss, urbanization, reduced contact with natural environments, and the homogenization of microbial exposures can contribute to immune dysregulation and increase susceptibility to allergies, asthma, and other chronic inflammatory diseases (Birzele et al. 2017).

Microbial diversity provides a key mechanistic connection across the dimensions of One Health. Microbiomes are integral components of humans, animals, plants, soils, freshwater and marine systems, and the built environment, mediating interactions between environmental conditions and biological functioning. Changes in microbiome composition can serve as sensitive indicators of environmental and health-related stress, while microbial diversity contributes to resilience, stability, and adaptive capacity across biological systems (Schloter et al. 2017; Wasimuddin et al. 2018, 2019; Berg et al. 2020; Alpízar et al. 2021; Fackelmann et al. 2021; Risely et al. 2023; Sessitsch et al. 2023). Recognizing microbial diversity as a shared component of environmental, animal, and human health therefore strengthens the conceptual and operational integration of the One Health approach.

Viewed across scales—from microbial communities in soils to ecosystems spanning continents and oceans—biodiversity underpins the ecological processes that sustain life. In doing so, it provides the biological foundation that links One Health with Planetary Health, emphasizing that safeguarding biodiversity is not only an environmental priority but also an essential strategy for protecting the health and resilience of present and future generations.

From conceptual clarity to implementation

Recognizing biodiversity as foundational to health is not merely a conceptual shift but has important implications for policy, governance, and implementation. Rather than choosing between One Health and Planetary Health, these frameworks should be viewed as complementary. One Health provides an operational framework for collaboration across medicine, veterinary science, agriculture, and environmental management, and Planetary Health emphasizes the ecological boundaries within which societies and health systems operate. Biodiversity connects these perspectives by linking local ecological processes with global environmental change.

Translating this understanding into practice requires integrating biodiversity into health planning, pandemic preparedness, veterinary strategies, and antimicrobial resistance action plans. Surveillance should extend beyond human and livestock health to include wildlife, environmental, and ecosystem indicators, while institutions across health, agriculture, environment, finance, and urban planning should develop shared data systems and joint risk assessments. Ecosystem conservation and restoration should be recognized as investments in prevention and resilience, and research should further explore microbiomes, ecosystem services, and nature-based solutions as mechanisms linking biodiversity and health (Sessitsch et al. 2023; Schröder et al. 2024).

Agriculture, food systems, and urban environments are central to this transition. Agriculture is a major driver of biodiversity loss but also shapes nutrition, livelihoods, antimicrobial use, and climate resilience. Likewise, cities concentrate environmental health risks while offering opportunities to improve access to biodiverse green spaces and healthier food systems. Biodiversity-friendly agriculture, regenerative food systems, urban green infrastructure, healthy soils, wetlands, community gardens, and other nature-based solutions can simultaneously strengthen biodiversity, food security, climate adaptation, and physical and mental health (WBGU 2023; IPBES 2024). Together, these transformations offer substantial co-benefits across human, animal, environmental, and planetary health.

To conclude, the question “What health are we talking about?” is more than semantic. It determines how problems are framed, which sectors engage, and which interventions are prioritized. It also supports a shift from disease treatment toward prevention and health promotion, with important socioeconomic implications for health systems and resource allocation. Although current policy debates increasingly acknowledge the importance of health, they often fail to explicitly connect human, animal, ecosystem, and planetary dimensions. We argue that biodiversity should be recognized as a foundational determinant of health and as the critical link across these dimensions. Future sustainability frameworks should therefore move beyond treating health as a generic umbrella term and instead adopt a conceptually differentiated understanding that places biodiversity at the center of healthy people, ecosystems, and the planet.
