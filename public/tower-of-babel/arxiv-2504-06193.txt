Heuristic Methods are Good Teachers to Distill MLPs for Graph Link Prediction Authors affiliated with Snap Inc. served in advisory roles only for this work.
Zongyue Qin, Shichang Zhang, Mingxuan Ju, Tong Zhao, Neil Shah, Yizhou Sun

Abstract
Link prediction is a crucial graph-learning task with applications including citation prediction and product recommendation. Distilling Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) teachers into Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) students has emerged as an effective approach to achieve strong performance and reducing computational cost by removing graph dependency. However, existing distillation methods only use standard GNNs and overlook alternative teachers such as specialized model for link prediction (GNN4LP) and heuristic methods (e.g., common neighbors). This paper first explores the impact of different teachers in GNN-to-MLP distillation. Surprisingly, we find that stronger teachers do not always produce stronger students: MLPs distilled from GNN4LP can underperform those distilled from simpler GNNs, while weaker heuristic methods can teach MLPs to near-GNN performance with drastically reduced training costs. Building on these insights, we propose Ensemble Heuristic-Distilled MLPs (EHDM), which eliminates graph dependencies while effectively integrating complementary signals via a gating mechanism. Experiments on ten datasets show an average 7.93% improvement over previous GNN-to-MLP approaches with 1.95-3.32 × \times less training time, indicating EHDM is an efficient and effective link prediction method.

Introduction

Link prediction is a pivotal task in graph learning, with widespread usage in web applications. It involves estimating the likelihood of a link between two nodes, enabling applications including citation prediction, friend recommendation, knowledge graph completion
[
Schlichtkrull et al. 2018
,
Nathani et al. 2019
,
Vashishth et al. 2019
]
, and item recommendation
[
Koren et al. 2009
,
Ying et al. 2018
,
He et al. 2020
]
.
Graph neural networks (GNNs)
[
Kipf and Welling 2016a
,
Hamilton et al. 2017
,
Veličković et al. 2017
]
have been widely used for link prediction. This includes standard generic GNNs
[
Kipf and Welling 2016b
,
Davidson et al. 2018
]
, as well as specifically designed GNNs for Link Prediction (GNN4LP) which are augmented with additional structural features
[
Zhang and Chen 2018
,
Zhang et al. 2021b
,
Zhu et al. 2021b
,
Yun et al. 2021
,
Wang et al. 2024
,
Ma et al. 2024
]
to capture extra link-specific information
[
Zhang et al. 2021a
]
. However, all of these GNN models suffer from heavy computations in inference time due to their dependency on graphs, which require repeatedly fetching and aggregating neighboring nodes for GNN message passing
[
Zhang et al. 2021b
]
.

Researchers have proposed various methods to accelerate GNN inference. There are pruning
[
Zhou et al. 2021
,
Chen et al. 2021
]
and quantization
[
Zhao et al. 2020
,
Tailor et al. 2020
]
methods that accelerate GNN inference to some extent. But the speed-up is limited because they do not resolve the graph dependency issue caused by the message passing. An alternative line of work focuses on distilling knowledge of GNN teachers into Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) students
[
Zhang et al. 2021b
,
Tian et al. 2022
,
Wu et al. 2023a
,
Wu et al. 2023b
,
Wu et al. 2024
,
Lu et al. 2024
,
Wang et al. 2025
]
. By eliminating graph dependency and expensive neighborhood aggregation, MLPs offer up to 70-273
\times
faster inference
[
Guo et al. 2023
,
Zhang et al. 2021b
]
. Meanwhile, GNN-distilled MLPs have been shown to achieve competitive or superior performance compared to GNN teachers in many cases.

Advances in GNN-to-MLP distillation focus primarily on node classification with only a few works extending the idea to link prediction. For example, LLP
[
Guo et al. 2023
]
distills relational knowledge from standard GNNs to MLPs to achieve better effectiveness for link prediction. Yet, the link-level GNN-to-MLP distillation remains under-explored. Besides, existing GNN-to-MLP works rely majorly on standard GNNs as teachers. In reality, state-of-the-art link prediction models often go beyond standard GNNs. GNN4LP methods, or even classical heuristic methods (e.g., common neighbors)
[
Kumar et al. 2020
,
Newman 2001
,
Murase et al. 2019
,
Zhou et al. 2009
]
, were shown to outperform standard GNNs in many scenarios
[
Li et al. 2024
]
. This creates an intriguing question:
How does the choice of teacher model influence the resulting MLP student’s performance?

Moreover, although GNN-to-MLP distillation accelerates inference, it still requires training a GNN teacher, which is itself computationally expensive in large-scale applications. Fine-tuning hyper-parameters for GNNs further inflates this cost. Hence, there is a growing incentive to identify alternative teachers that can distill useful link-level information at lower training cost.

In this work, we first explore how different teachers, ranging from standard GNNs to GNN4LP methods and heuristic-based approaches, affect the performance of the distilled MLP student for link prediction. Adopting the LLP framework
[
Guo et al. 2023
]
as the distillation pipeline, we have two surprising observations:

1.

Good models might not be good teachers.
Although GNN4LP models often outperform standard GNNs, the MLPs distilled from GNN4LP can actually underperform those distilled from simpler GNNs.

2.

Heuristic methods are good teachers, even when they underperform.
Classical heuristics can be effective teacher for MLPs to guide them achieve comparable performance to standard GNNs, even if the heuristic’s performance is worse than the MLP.

For the first observation, we attribute it to the limited capacity of MLPs for capturing highly intricate structural patterns that GNN4LP teachers rely on. Taking this insight along with the second observation, we propose to distill MLPs from heuristic teachers, because generating these heuristic scores is often much faster than training a GNN, which will substantially shorten overall training time as well.
While heuristic teachers can effectively guide MLPs, each heuristic captures only one type of structural proximity
[
Mao et al. 2023
]
, leaving certain positive links unaccounted for. One might try merging heuristics into a single “stronger” teacher, but we find that mixing multiple heuristics can overload the student and hurt its performance. Instead, we take the heuristic-to-MLP distillation one step further to propose a simple yet effective Ensemble Heuristic-Distilled MLPs (EHDM) method that trains one MLP per heuristic, then learns a gater MLP to fuse their predictions at inference time. Our gating mechanism uses
only
node features, thereby preserving the MLP’s fast, neighbor-free inference. This allows us to harness multiple heuristics’ complementary signals and achieve higher accuracy without reintroducing graph dependencies.

To summarize, this work makes the following contributions:

•

We show that even underperforming heuristic methods can be surprisingly good teachers to MLPs, enabling near-GNN predictive performance while reducing the cost of generating distillation guidance by an order of magnitude.

•

We propose an effective ensemble method EHDM to combine multiple heuristic-distilled MLPs via a gating mechanism which overcomes the limitations of single heuristics distillation.

•

Experiments on ten datasets (including three million-scale datasets) show an average of
7.93%
performance gain over previous GNN-to-MLP methods
[
Guo et al. 2023
]
,
while accelerating overall training time by
1.95-3.32
\times
.

Link Prediction on Graphs

Let

Graph Neural Network (GNN) Solutions

The state-of-the-art solutions are based on GNNs. Early methods directly utilize the embeddings of two target nodes for prediction

]

[
Yun et al. 2021
]

]

Wang et al. 2024

Although these methods improve prediction accuracy, they increase the computational cost during training and inference, rendering them less suitable for large-scale web applications.

Heuristic Solutions

Heuristic methods estimate node proximity based on structural or feature similarities
[
Mao et al. 2023
]
, categorized as follows:

•

Local Structural Proximity
considers 1-hop neighborhood similarity. We consider three heuristics:
Common Neighbors (CN)

[
Newman 2001
]
:

CN(i,j)=|N(i)\cap N(j)|
,

Adamic-Adar (AA)

[
Adamic and Adar 2003

AA(i,j)=\sum_{k\in N(i)\cap N(j)}\frac{1}{\log(\text{Deg}(k))}

[

RA(i,j)=\sum_{k\in N(i)\cap N(j)}\frac{1}{\text{Deg}(k)}

is the neighbors of node
i

is the node degree.

•

Global Structural Proximity
captures higher-order connectivity, e.g., SimRank
[
Jeh and Widom 2002
]
, Katz
[
Katz 1953
]

[
Brin and Page 1998
]

]
:

CSP(i,j)=\frac{1}{\min(\tau,SP(i,j))}
,
where
SP(i,j)
is the shortest path length between
i
and
j
, and
\tau
limits the maximum distance considered
1
1

1

With bi-directional breadth first search, we need to retrieve
\tau/2
-hops neighborhood to compute CSP.
.

•

Feature Proximity
measures similarity in node features, assuming nodes with similar characteristics are more likely to connect
[
Khanam et al. 2023
]
. Ma et al.
[
Ma et al. 2024
]
shows MLP is good at capturing feature proximity.

2.2
GNN Acceleration

Inference acceleration has been explored through both hardware and algorithmic optimizations. On the algorithmic side, techniques such as pruning
[
Han et al. 2015
,
Zhou et al. 2021
,
Chen et al. 2021
]
and quantization
[
Gupta et al. 2015
,
Zhao et al. 2020
,
Tailor et al. 2020
]
have been widely studied to reduce model complexity and computational cost.
While these techniques improve inference efficiency, they do not eliminate neighbor-fetching operation, which remains a fundamental bottleneck in GNN inference. Because GNNs rely on message passing across graph structures, even optimized models still suffer from high data dependency and irregular memory access patterns.

2.3
GNN-to-MLP Distillation

To further improve inference efficiency in graph applications, GLNN
[
Zhang et al. 2021b
]
pioneered GNN-to-MLP distillation, eliminating the need for neighbor fetching and significantly accelerating inference. Subsequent works have refined distillation techniques
[
Wu et al. 2023a
,
Wu et al. 2023b
,
Wu et al. 2024
,
Lu et al. 2024
,
Tian et al. 2022
]
, but mainly for node classification. Wang et al.
[
Wang et al. 2025
]
introduced a self-supervised approach applicable to node classification, link prediction, and graph classification. However, their method is restricted to GNN teachers and does not generalize to GNN4LP models or heuristic methods. The first extension of GNN-to-MLP distillation for link prediction was LLP
[
Guo et al. 2023
]
, but like prior methods, it relies on training a GNN teacher, which remains computationally expensive for large-scale applications.

LLP’s framework is applicable beyond standard GNNs, as it only requires output prediction from the teachers. So we adopt LLP’s framework while extending it to other teacher options.
LLP captures relational knowledge by defining an anchor node
v
and a context set
C_{v}
, training the student MLP to learn the relative ranking of context nodes based on their connection probability with
v
. Let
p_{v,i}
and
q_{v,i}
denote the teacher GNN’s and student MLP’s predicted probabilities of link
(v,i)
, respectively. In addition to cross entropy loss with ground-truth edge labels, LLP employs two distillation losses:

•

Ranking-Based Loss
(
L_{R}
), ensuring the student MLP preserves the teacher’s ranking order:

L_{R}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{i,j\in C_{v}}\max(0,-r\cdot(q_{v,i}-q_{v,j})+\delta)

(1)

where
r
is defined as:

r=\left\{\begin{aligned} 1&,\quad\text{if }p_{v,i}-p_{v,j}>\delta\\
-1&,\quad\text{if }p_{v,i}-p_{v,j}<-\delta\\
0&,\quad\text{otherwise}\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

with hyperparameter
\delta
controlling ranking sensitivity.

•

Distribution-Based Loss
(
L_{D}
), aligning student MLP predictions with the teacher GNN with temperature hyperparameter
t
:

L_{D}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{i\in C_{v}}\frac{\exp(\frac{p_{v,i}}{t})}{\sum_{j\in C_{v}}\exp(\frac{p_{v,j}}{t})}\log\frac{exp(\frac{q_{v,i}}{t})}{\sum_{j\in C_{v}}\exp(\frac{q_{v,j}}{t})}

3
Motivating Studies

In this section, we present key observations that motivate our use of heuristic methods for distilling MLPs. First, we compare GNN and GNN4LP models as teachers and find that GNN4LP, despite achieving better accuracy, does not always serve as a superior teacher. We then highlight the advantages of using heuristics as teachers: (1) they significantly enhance MLP performance and (2) they offer better training efficiency.

3.1
Better Models
\neq
Better Teachers

Unlike node and graph classification tasks, state-of-the-art GNN4LP models for link prediction fall outside the scope of conventional message-passing neural networks (MPNNs).
Since GNN4LP models usually outperform standard GNNs, one might intuitively expect GNN4LP-distilled MLPs to also surpass GNN-distilled MLPs. However, our findings contradict this assumption. As shown in Figure
1
, while NCN
[
Wang et al. 2024
]
significantly outperforms SAGE
[
Hamilton et al. 2017
]
and GAT
[
Veličković et al. 2017
]
, NCN-distilled MLPs exhibit suboptimal performance in three out of five datasets. This suggests that simply replacing GNNs with more powerful GNN4LP models does not necessarily enhance student’s performance.

Notably, prior studies have also observed that stronger teacher models do not always produce better students during knowledge distillation
[
Wu et al. 2024
,
Jafari et al. 2021
,
Zhu and Wang 2021
,
Qiu et al. 2022
]
. They attribute it to the mismatch in capacity between teacher and student models. In our context, GNN4LP models leverage both node features and complex structural information, achieving strictly better capacity and expressiveness than standard GNN models
[
Zhang et al. 2021a
]
. In contrast, MLPs rely solely on node features and can only learn the structure information correlated with node features. Consequently, MLPs cannot fully inherit the effectiveness of GNN4LP models, leading to confusion and suboptimal performance due to guidance they are unable to interpret effectively.

The following theorem reveals why GNN4LP models are not bettet teachers than GNNs.

.

Since stronger GNN4LP models are not necessarily better teachers due to
capacity mismatch
, we then explore using simple heuristic methods as teachers. Unlike GNNs, heuristic methods typically capture a single type of structural information (e.g., common neighbors)
[
Mao et al. 2023
]
, making them easier for MLPs to learn from. Additionally, heuristic methods can achieve accuracy comparable to, or even better than, GNN and GNN4LP models on certain datasets
[
Li et al. 2024
]
, making them promising candidates for MLP distillation. However, their performance is highly data-dependent. On some datasets, heuristic methods may even perform worse than MLPs. Surprisingly, we find that even when heuristic methods underperform, they can still serve as effective teachers, and offering substantial training time savings compared to GNN-based teachers.

Observation 1: Heuristic methods are effective teachers for MLPs, even when they underperform.

Figure
2(a)
presents the Hits@10 accuracy of CN, MLP, and CN-distilled MLP on three datasets. Although CN has lower accuracy than standard MLPs, the CN-distilled MLPs significantly outperform MLPs trained without distillation, indicating that CN-distilled MLPs effectively learn extra information during the distillation process. Additional results in Section
5
further confirm that heuristic-distilled MLPs achieve near-GNN performance across various datasets. These findings provide strong empirical evidence supporting the effectiveness of heuristic teachers.

Given the observation above, a natural question arises:
why can heuristic methods improve MLPs even when they have lower accuracy?
We hypothesize that heuristic methods provide complementary information to MLPs. Mao et al.
[
Mao et al. 2023
]
identify three fundamental factors critical to link prediction: local structure proximity, global structure proximity, and feature proximity. Different proximity capture distinct sets of positive node pairs in a graph. Specifically, they proposed a theoretical model for link prediction based on the underlying data factors:

P(i\sim j)=\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{1+e^{\alpha(d_{ij}-\max\{r_{i},r_{j}\})}}&,d_{ij}\leq\max\{r_{i},r_{j}\}\\
\beta_{ij}&,d_{ij}>\max\{r_{i},r_{j}\}\end{aligned}\right.

(5)

where
d_{ij}
is the structural proximity between node
i,j
, and
\beta_{i,j}
is the feature proximity.
r_{i}
and
r_{j}
are connecting threshold parameters for node
i
and
j
.
\alpha>0
controls the sharpness of the function. The theoretical model illustrates that when two nodes have close structure proximity, the likelihood of connection depends only on the structure proximity. Otherwise, the likelihood of connection only depends on the feature proximity.

Ma et al.
[
Ma et al. 2024
]
show that MLPs primarily capture feature proximity, exhibiting low correlation with structure proximity, an intuitive result since MLPs rely solely on node features. So MLPs primarily focus on node pairs where
d_{ij}>\max\{r_{i},r_{j}\}
in the theoretical model (Eq.
5
).
In contrast, heuristics such as CN, AA, and RA capture local structure proximity, while CSP captures global structure proximity
[
Mao et al. 2023
]
. As a result, heuristic methods identify positive node pairs where
d_{ij}\leq\max\{r_{i},r_{j}\}
, the pairs MLPs alone would overlook. In addition, since each heuristic only captures one type of structure proximity, it is easier for MLP to learn the correlation between node features and the structural proximity, enabling them to capture node pairs beyond the scope of feature proximity.

To validate our hypothesis, we compare the positive edges identified by heuristic methods, MLPs, and heuristic-distilled MLPs. Following the approach in
[
Ma et al. 2024
]
, we use Hits@K (K=5 for Citeseer, K=10 for Cora and Pubmed) to identify the sets of positive edges captured by different methods. Specifically, given a positive edge and a set of negative edges, if the positive edge ranks among the top K highest predicted values, it is included in the positive edge set. Once the positive edge sets are identified, we compute the subset ratio, which represents the proportion of positive edges identified by heuristic methods that are also recognized by MLPs. Figure
3
presents the results. As shown, distilling from heuristic methods significantly increases the subset ratio, indicating that heuristic-distilled MLPs effectively capture extra positive edges and learn complementary information from heuristic teachers.

Next, we offer the following theoretical perspective to explain when and why heuristics can be
at least as effective
, or even
better
than GNNs as teachers.

Previous studies
[
Ma et al. 2024
,
Mao et al. 2023
]
have shown that the primary challenge of training MLPs lies in learning the structural term, i.e., the node pairs where
d_{ij}\leq\max\{r_{i},r_{j}\}
.
Let
\tilde{f}(x_{i},x_{j},s_{i},s_{j})
be a GNN or heuristic that approximates
f(i,j)=\frac{1}{1+e^{\alpha(d_{ij}-\max\{r_{i},r_{j}\})}}
.
The best MLP distilled from
\tilde{f}
is
\mathbb{E}\tilde{f}=\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{f}(x_{i},x_{j},s_{i},s_{j})|x_{i},x_{j}\right)
.
Consider the KL Divergence

\displaystyle KL(f\|\mathbb{E}\tilde{f})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{x_{i},x_{j},s_{i},s_{j}}\left(\sum f\log\frac{f}{\mathbb{E}\tilde{f}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{x_{i},x_{j},s_{i},s_{j}}\left(\sum f\log\frac{f*\tilde{f}}{\tilde{f}*\mathbb{E}\tilde{f}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle KL(f\|\tilde{f})+\mathbb{E}_{x_{i},x_{j},s_{i},s_{j}}\mathbb{E}_{f}(\log\mathbb{E}\tilde{f}-\log\tilde{f})

This decomposition shows that the total distillation error consists of:

1.

The teacher’s error:
KL(f\|\tilde{f})
, i.e., how well the teacher approximates the true label distribution
f
;

2.

The student’s approximation error: how well the student (MLP) can recover
\tilde{f}
from features alone.

Although GNNs may have lower
KL(f\|\tilde{f})
, their greater complexity can lead to larger student error. In contrast, heuristic methods may produce MLP students with
lower total KL divergence
due to better alignment with the student’s capacity.

Theorem 3.2

(Sufficient Condition for Heuristics to Be Better Teachers)
.

Let
h_{ij}
be a heuristic teacher and
\tilde{f}_{ij}
a GNN teacher. If

\displaystyle KL(f\|h)+\mathbb{E}_{f}\left[\log\mathbb{E}(h_{ij}\mid x_{i},x_{j})-\log h_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle KL(f\|\tilde{f})+\mathbb{E}_{f}\left[\log\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{ij}\mid x_{i},x_{j})-\log\tilde{f}_{ij}\right],

then the optimal MLP distilled from
h
achieves lower KL divergence with respect to
f
than the MLP distilled from
\tilde{f}
.

This theorem highlights that a simpler heuristic teacher can outperform a more expressive GNN teacher
in the context of distillation
, when the student cannot effectively learn the complex function class of the teacher.

Observation 2: Heuristic methods are much faster than GNNs.

Despite their effectiveness, GNNs and GNN4LP models suffer from high computational costs during both training and inference. Figure
2(b)
illustrates the time breakdown of GNN-to-MLP distillation on three large-scale datasets:
OGBL-Collab
,
OGBL-Citation2

[
Hu et al. 2020
]
, and
IGB

[
Khatua et al. 2023
]
. The total GNN time includes both GNN training and guidance generation, while the MLP time refers to the MLP distillation process based on GNN guidance. We observe that 62.2% to 87.1% of the total time is spent on GNNs, making it the primary bottleneck in the distillation pipeline.

In contrast, heuristic methods offer a far more efficient alternative as teachers, as heuristics require no training and involve only simple calculations. Figure
2(c)
compares the relative time costs of two GNN models (GCN and SAGE) against four heuristic methods. The time cost of GNN models includes both training and guidance generation, while the time cost of heuristic methods only includes guidance generation. The results indicate that using heuristic methods as teachers is an order of magnitude faster than using GNNs.

4
Ensemble Heuristic-Distilled MLPs

Section
3.2
highlights the advantages of using heuristic methods as teachers. However, since each heuristic method captures only a specific type of structural proximity
[
Mao et al. 2023
]
, an MLP distilled from a single heuristic is inherently limited, capturing only a subset of positive links. To improve effectiveness, we aim to develop an approach that enables MLPs to leverage multiple heuristics.

4.1
Pitfall of Ensemble Heuristics

Ma et al.
[
Ma et al. 2024
]
demonstrated that combining multiple heuristics generally improves performance. So a straightforward approach is to first ensemble multiple heuristics and then distill a single MLP from the combined heuristic signals. However, as discussed in Section
3.1
, a stronger model does not always translate into a better teacher. Unfortunately, this holds true when mixing heuristics.

Figure
4
presents the accuracy rankings (Hits@20) of CN, CSP, and their combination (CN+CSP), alongside the accuracy rankings of their corresponding student MLPs. While the combined heuristic indeed achieves higher accuracy, its distilled MLP performs worse than those distilled from individual heuristics. We hypothesize that this occurs because mixing heuristics increases structural complexity, making it more difficult for MLPs to learn effectively. These results indicate that directly distilling from a mixture of heuristics is not a viable solution.

4.2
Ensemble Heuristic-Distilled MLPs

An alternative approach is to ensemble multiple MLPs. For example, AdaGMLP
[
Lu et al. 2024
]
proposes a boosting framework to distill multiple MLPs from a GNN for node classification. However, AdaGMLP is not directly applicable to link prediction or scenarios involving multiple heuristic teachers. Moreover, boosting requires iterative training of multiple MLPs, which can be slow.

To effectively and efficiently ensemble multiple MLPs, our key insight is that:
Since different heuristics capture different sets of positive links, their distilled MLPs should also capture different sets of positive links.

To validate this, we compute the overlapping ratio of positive edges identified by different distilled MLPs. We use Hits@10 to define the positive edge set for each distilled MLP. More details about positive edge set can be found in Section
3.2
. The overlapping ratio is then computed as the Jaccard index between two positive edge sets. As shown in Figure
5
, the overlap among different heuristic-distilled MLPs is relatively low, confirming our hypothesis that each MLP captures complementary link information.

So a simple yet effective solution is to train a gating function that dynamically selects which heuristic-distilled MLP to use for each input node pair. Ma et al.
[
Ma et al. 2024
]
introduced a similar idea by training a gating network to ensemble different GNN4LP models. However, their gating network is not suitable for our setting, as it takes heuristic values as inputs, requiring neighbor-fetching operations, which would compromise the inference efficiency of MLPs.

To address this, we propose a gating MLP network that takes only the feature vectors of the target node pairs as input and outputs a weight for each distilled MLP. Our rationale is that node features alone should be sufficient for the gating function, since GNN-to-MLP distillation only works when node features correlate with structural information. The gating function is expected to learn which heuristic-derived MLP should be used based on the input node features.

The overall architecture is illustrated in Figure
6
. Given an input node pair, the distilled MLPs and the gating MLP operate in parallel. The final prediction is then computed as a weighted sum of the predictions from the different MLPs. This approach ensures that: (1) No additional neighborhood fetching operations are required; (2) All MLPs can run simultaneously, minimizing inference overhead.

To train the gating function, we fix the parameters of the distilled MLPs and optimize the parameters of the gating function using the BCE loss:

-y_{ij}\log(\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}w_{h}q^{(h)}_{i,j})-(1-y_{ij})\log(1-\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}w_{h}q^{(h)}_{i,j})

(6)

where
y_{ij}
is the ground-truth label,
\mathcal{H}
is the heuristic set, and
q^{(h)}
is the prediction of the MLP distilled from heuristic
h
, and
w_{h}
is the output weight of the gating function.

Additionally, we incorporate L1-regularization on the gate weights to encourage sparsity and improve generalization. The final loss function for training the gating MLP is:

L_{BCE}+\lambda\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}|w_{h}|

(7)

where
\lambda
is a hyper-parameter controlling the weight of L1-regularization.

5.2
How do Heuristic-Distillation Compare to GNN-Distillation

First, we provide more empirical results to show the effectiveness of heuristic methods as teachers for MLP distillation. Table
1
compares the teacher performance of GNNs and heuristic methods, while Table
2
reports the performance of their corresponding student models across all datasets. We observe that despite heuristic methods having significantly lower performance on certain datasets, heuristic-distilled MLPs consistently match or surpass the performance of the best GNN-distilled MLPs. These results empirically demonstrate that heuristic methods can serve as effective teachers for training MLPs.

5.3
Performance of Ensemble Approach

Figure 7
:
Training time decomposition of SAGE-LLP and our ensemble method.

Table
3
compares the performance of MLP, GNN, LLP (GNN-distilled MLP), and our EHDM, which ensembles four heuristic-distilled MLPs. The results show that EHDM outperforms both MLP and LLP by leveraging the strengths of different heuristic-distilled MLPs. It is on average
7.93\%
better than the best LLP model. Notably, it achieves at least 90% of the best GNN effectiveness on 7 out of 10 datasets and even surpasses the best GNN on 4 datasets. For the two datasets where EHDM and LLP perform suboptimally (i.e.,
Collab
and
Citation2
), we attribute this to their simple node features, as both datasets contain only 128-dimensional node embeddings, the smallest among all datasets.

Additionally, Figure
7
illustrates the training time decomposition of our ensemble method compared to the best LLP. The ensemble pipeline consists of three stages: heuristic guidance computation, MLP distillation, and ensembling. Although heuristic guidance must be generated for multiple heuristics, and MLP distillation is performed separately for each heuristic, both of these stages run in parallel. Therefore, we take the maximum time among the four heuristics as the effective time cost for each of these stages. From the figure, we observe that the ensemble time, corresponding to training the gating MLPs, constitutes only a small fraction of the overall pipeline. More importantly, by eliminating GNN training, our ensemble approach accelerates the entire distillation process by 1.95–3.32
\times
compared to LLP.

6
Conclusion

In this paper,we demonstrate that simple heuristic methods, despite their lower accuracy, can be surprisingly effective teachers for MLPs, enabling competitive link prediction performance while drastically reducing training costs.
Furthermore, we introduce an ensemble approach that aggregates multiple heuristic-distilled MLPs using a gating mechanism. Our extensive experiments across ten datasets show that this approach consistently improves prediction accuracy, while saving overall training time.
Our findings have important implications for real-world link prediction tasks, particularly in large-scale web applications

B.6
Problems with “Production” Setting

Guo et al.
[
Guo et al. 2023
]
introduced a “production” setting for splitting train/test data in link prediction tasks, where a random subset of nodes is treated as newly added to the graph. These nodes and their associated edges are removed from the training graph. Their results showed a significant performance drop when transitioning from the “transductive” setting to the “production” setting. Similarly, we observe the same performance decline for heuristic-distilled MLPs, to the extent that heuristic distillation may fail to improve MLP performance.

Our analysis reveals that this performance drop is primarily due to the ”production” split significantly altering the graph structure, leading to inaccurate heuristic values in the training graph. For instance, triangle counts play a crucial role in link prediction
[
Adamic and Adar 2003
,
Zhang et al. 2021a
,
Chamberlain et al. 2022
]
. As shown in Table
11
, the number of triangles in the training graphs is drastically reduced under the ”production” setting compared to the “transductive” setting. This discrepancy indicates that the removing random nodes make the graphs more “broken” and that “production” setting introduces a substantial structural mismatch between training and test graphs, explaining the performance degradation observed in
[
Guo et al. 2023
]
.

In practice, such drastic structural changes are unlikely in real-world applications. Therefore, we argue that the “production” setting does not accurately reflect real-world scenarios. Instead, datasets like
Collab
and
Citation2
, which split edges based on time, offer a more realistic evaluation. Consequently, we do not adopt the “production” setting in our experiments.

Proof of Theorem
3.1

Proof.

By assumption,
s_{i}^{\text{extra}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{x}_{i}
. Thus,

P(s_{i}^{\text{extra}}\mid\bm{x}_{i})=P(s_{i}^{\text{extra}}).

So

F_{\text{GNN4LP-MLP}}^{*}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\text{GNN}},s^{\text{extra}}}\left[F_{\text{GNN4LP}}\mid\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j}\right]=\int F_{\text{GNN4LP}}\,dP(s^{\text{GNN}}\mid\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})dP(s^{\text{extra}})

Define a GNN teacher:

F_{\text{GNN}}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j},s_{i}^{\text{GNN}},s_{j}^{\text{GNN}})=\mathbb{E}[F_{GNN4LP}|x_{i},x_{j},s_{i},s_{j}]=\int F_{\text{GNN4LP}}\,dP(s^{\text{extra}})

This GNN marginalizes over
s^{\text{extra}}
, which is independent of
\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j}
.

The optimal MLP distilled from
F_{\text{GNN}}
is:

\mathbb{E}_{s^{\text{GNN}}}\left[F_{\text{GNN}}\mid\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\text{GNN}},s^{\text{extra}}}\left[F_{\text{GNN4LP}}\mid\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j}\right]=F_{\text{GNN4LP-MLP}}^{*}.

Thus, the MLP students from
F_{\text{GNN4LP}}
and
F_{\text{GNN}}
are identical.
∎
