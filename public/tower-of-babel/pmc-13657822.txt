AMOT mediates YAP1 activation and promotes the cell progression and glycolysis in non-small cell lung cancer by modulating the Hippo signaling pathway
Authors: Yihao Guo, Wendong Wang, Jinsong Li, Jun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) exhibits aggressive cell progression and profound metabolic reprogramming. This study investigated the clinical significance, functional role, and molecular mechanism of the Angiomotin (AMOT)/Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1)/Hippo axis in regulating NSCLC growth and glycolysis.MethodsAMOT expression was analyzed in 68 paired NSCLC and adjacent normal tissues. In vitro gain- and loss-of-function assays (proliferation, apoptosis, and Transwell migration) and normalized intracellular metabolic measurements (ATP, lactate, and glucose) were performed in A549 and NCI-H1299 cell lines. The underlying Hippo signaling mechanism was evaluated via Co-IP, Western blotting, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) correlation analysis, and subcutaneous xenograft mouse models.ResultsAMOT was significantly upregulated in NSCLC tissues, correlating with larger tumor size (≥4 cm), advanced clinical stage, and poorer overall survival. AMOT silencing markedly suppressed cell proliferation and migration while inducing apoptosis. AMOT and YAP1 exhibited a strong positive co-expression pattern. YAP1 knockdown significantly inhibited proliferation, migration, intracellular glycolysis, and in vivo tumor growth, effects that were partially rescued by AMOT overexpression. Mechanistically, AMOT inhibited LATS1 and YAP1 phosphorylation, promoting YAP1 stabilization and nuclear transactivation, thereby driving tumor progression and glycolytic reprogramming.ConclusionsThe AMOT/YAP1/Hippo axis acts as a crucial driver of NSCLC progression and aerobic glycolysis, offering a promising therapeutic target for NSCLC intervention.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Angiomotin (AMOT) is significantly upregulated in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) tissues and correlates with advanced tumor stage, larger tumor size, and poor overall survival.

• AMOT promotes NSCLC cell proliferation, migration, and the "Warburg effect" (aerobic glycolysis).

• The oncogenic and metabolic reprogramming effects of AMOT are mediated through its physical interaction with Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1), subsequently modulating the Hippo signaling pathway.

What is known and what is new?

• Abnormal energy metabolism, specifically an increased reliance on glycolysis, is a fundamental hallmark of malignant tumors. Additionally, the YAP/Hippo signaling pathway is a known regulator of cell proliferation and tumor progression.

• This study identifies the specific AMOT/YAP1/Hippo signaling axis in NSCLC for the first time. It demonstrates that AMOT is a crucial upstream regulator that drives glucose metabolism and tumor progression by mediating YAP1 activation in lung cancer.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The identification of the AMOT/YAP1 axis provides a novel molecular mechanism for NSCLC pathogenesis. Clinically, AMOT should be investigated as a potential prognostic biomarker. Furthermore, developing targeted inhibitors against the AMOT/YAP1 interaction could offer a promising new therapeutic strategy for disrupting tumor metabolism in NSCLC patients.

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide, accounting for nearly 1.8 million deaths annually (1). Approximately 85% of these cases are diagnosed as non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which has a dismal overall five-year survival rate of approximately 15% (2). Identifying specific molecules that can predict overall survival (OS) provides potential biomarkers for targeted therapies (3-5). Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying NSCLC progression remains crucial. Angiomotin (AMOT) is a membrane protein that binds to angiostatin. It regulates angiogenesis and endothelial cell migration and is expressed in nearly all cancers, where it modulates tumor cell proliferation, apoptosis, and invasion. There are two main isoforms of AMOT: AMOT-p80 and AMOT-p130. By contrast, they play quite different roles in various malignancies (6,7). However, studies investigating the specific role of AMOT in lung cancer, particularly NSCLC, remain inadequate.

While normal differentiated cells primarily rely on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation to generate energy, most tumor cells heavily depend on aerobic glycolysis, a phenomenon known as the “Warburg effect”. Even under aerobic conditions, malignant tumor cells exhibit high glucose uptake and produce large amounts of lactate (8,9). Hanahan and Weinberg (10) established that abnormal energy metabolism is a hallmark of cancer, facilitating proliferation, invasion, and drug resistance. Although growing evidence links the “Warburg effect” to various oncogenes, its precise molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

Inhibiting glycolysis has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for various malignancies, including lung cancer (11-14). An enhanced glycolytic state can augment YAP protein activity by upregulating key glycolytic enzymes (15). Conversely, elevated YAP activity further drives glycolysis in tumor cells, creating a positive feedback loop. Previous studies have confirmed that AMOT is a critical regulator of YAP (16-18), which functions within the Hippo signaling pathway to drive malignancies such as epithelial ovarian cancer (16). In NSCLC, research linking YAP to glycolysis is scarce, and the mechanism by which YAP regulates the glycolytic pathway is not fully understood. Consequently, investigating the regulatory axis involving AMOT, YAP, and the Hippo signaling pathway in NSCLC glycolysis is highly warranted.

In this study, we first evaluated AMOT expression levels in NSCLC patients and analyzed its correlation with clinicopathological data. We then investigated the effects of AMOT on NSCLC cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis. Using bioinformatics analysis, we identified a co-expression pattern between AMOT and YAP1. Finally, we explored the role of the AMOT/YAP1/Hippo signaling axis in regulating glycolysis and tumor progression. Our findings provide novel insights into the mechanistic progression of NSCLC. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1555/rc).

Methods

Cell lines and transfections

The normal human lung epithelial cell line of BEAS-2B and NSCLC cell lines of A549, NCl-H1299, and NCI-H1975 were bought from BNCC (BN-Bio Inc., Beijing), and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Sigma, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma, USA) and 100 U/mL of streptomycin/penicillin (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) with 5% CO2, 37 ℃.

For transfections analyses, the short hairpin AMOT (shAMOT, targeting both AMOY-p80 and AMOT-p130) and YAP1 (shYAP1), and their negative controls (NC) were obtained from GenePharma (Shanghai, China) and delivered into A549 and NCl-H1299 cell lines. The transfection experiment was performed by employing Lipofectamine 2000 kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

AMOT and YAP1 expression levels were evaluated in cancer tissues (n=68) and paired adjacent tissues (n=68, form a pool of 84 samples), in cell lines of A549, NCl-H1299, and in transfected cells. Tissue samples were obtained from patients with NSCLC at Department of Thoracic Surgery, Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, between October 2019 and March 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan Union hospital (No. 2025[0536]). Informed consent was taken from all the patients.

The total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). AMOT and YAP1 primers were synthesized by Genscript Co. Ltd (Nanjing, China) and listed in Table 1. The qRT-PCR primers were designed to target the consensus regions shared by both AMOT-p80 and AMOT-p130 isoforms to assess the total AMOT pool.

Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the one-step HiScript II One Step qRT-PCR SYBR Green Kit (#221-01) bought from Vazyme Biotech Co. Ltd was used for the qRT-PCR analysis. The 2-△△Ct method was employed for calculating the relative expression level.

Cell proliferation, apoptosis, and migration analyses

Cell proliferation following AMOT knockdown was evaluated using Celigo Image Cytometer (Nexcelom Bioscience, Lawrence, MA, USA). Briefly, the transfected A549 and NCI-H1299 cells were seeded into 96-well plates. From day 1 to day 5, the plates were scanned daily using the Celigo system to capture cell images and automatically calculate the absolute cell counts and fold changes.

Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well and cultured at 37 ℃. The cell viability of OD450 nm at 1 day, 2 days, 3 days, 4 days, and 5 days were measured by a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

The cell apoptosis was analyzed by employing the kit obtained from elabscience (E-CK-A217). Briefly, the transfected cells were collected, suspended, washed, and stained with staining solutions. A flow cytometer (FACScan, Becton Dickinson) was used to calculate cells.

For the cell migration analysis, a Transwell assay was performed using Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size, Corning, USA). Briefly, 5×104 transfected cells suspended in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with medium supplemented with 20% FBS to act as a chemoattractant. After incubation at 37 ℃ for 24 h, the cells remaining on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed with a cotton swab. The migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with glutaraldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and photographed and counted under a microscope (400× magnification).

Wound-healing assay was conducted to calculate the number of migrated cells. Briefly, the cells were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured for 12h. Then, the wounds on the cell layer were created using a 10-μL pipette tip, washed, and cultured with the medium containing 1% FBS for 24h. Finally, the wounded area was photographed, and the empty areas were calculated.

Bioinformatics analysis

We predicted the AMOT co-expressed genes by STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) and Encori database(https://rnasysu.com/encori/).

Western blotting

The protein was extracted in transfected cells of Control, shAMOT, and OE-AMOT (encoding the full-length human AMOT-p130 isoform) by employing RIPA lysis buffer (#R0278, Sigma). The protein extraction, quality control, and western blot referred to Xu et al. (19). Briefly, the proteins were separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Then, the gels were transferred, blocked, and cultured with primary antibodies against LATS1 (1:2,000, 3477S, CST), phosphorylation-LATS1 (Thr1079, 1:2,000, 9157S, CST), YAP1 (1:2,000, ab52771, Abcam), phosphorylation-YAP1 (Ser127, 1:1,000, ab76252, Abcam), AMOT (1:1000, 24550-1-AP, Proteintech), recognizing the conserved C-terminus shared by both the p130 and p80 isoforms. A predominant band at ~130 kDa was consistently detected in our immunoblotting assays, indicating that AMOT-p130 is the major endogenous isoform expressed in the investigated NSCLC cell lines.), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (1:500, #ab37168, Abcam). The membranes were washed and incubated with Goat anti-Mouse/Rabbit IgG(H+L)-HRP (Ray antibody Biotech, Beijing, China). Finally, the bands were observed under a Tanon 6600 Luminescence imaging workstation (Tanon, China).

Co-immunoprecipitation (IP) analysis

Firstly, the A549 and NCl-H1299 cells were incubated and washed. Then, they were lysed in CytoBuster Protein Extraction Reagent (Millipore), purified with Protein G Plus-Agarose beads (Santa Cruz), and incubated with prepared antibody-conjugated beads for more than ten hours. Finally, the beads were dissolved in an SDS-PAGE loading buffer with the indicated antibodies for protein analysis.

Tumor burden assay

A total of 12 female BALB/c (Bagg albino c) nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were purchased from Charles River (Beijing, China and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. All animal experiments were performed under a project license (No. 4259) granted by the animal care and use Committee of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, in compliance with guidelines of Experimental Animals Center of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology for the care and use of animals.

Following a one-week acclimatization period, the mice were randomly allocated into four experimental groups (n=3 per group) using a computer-generated randomization. For the subcutaneous xenograft model, the human NSCLC cell line A549 was utilized. Cells were genetically modified via lentiviral transduction to create the following four groups: (I) NC (OE + KD); (II) AMOT + NC (KD); (III) shYAP1 + NC (OE); and (IV) AMOT + shYAP1. Approximately 5×106 viable cells suspended in 100 μL of PBS were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of each mouse. Tumor volume were monitored every 4 days from 5 day to day 21 after injection using a digital caliper (20). Tumor volumes were calculated as V=0.5×L×W2. On Day 21, mice were sacrificed, and tumors were excised, weighed, and processed for IHC. For the statistical analysis of the repeated-measures data (tumor volume growth curves), a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test was employed.

ATP, lactic acid, and glucose detection

The intracellular levels of glucose, lactate, and ATP were quantified using colorimetric assay kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China). To eliminate the confounding effects of altered cell proliferation or apoptosis upon gene knockdown, all metabolic measurements were normalized to the absolute number of viable cells. Briefly, cells were harvested, and cell viability and count were determined using the standard trypan blue exclusion method. Subsequently, an identical number of viable cells (5×106 viable cells per group) were aliquoted, washed with cold PBS, and resuspended in 1 mL of the respective extraction buffers. The cells were harvested for metabolic quantification precisely 48 hours post-transfection. The cell suspensions were subjected to ultrasonic disruption in an ice bath. For the glucose and lactate assays, the homogenates were boiled for 10 min, cooled, and centrifuged at 8,000 ×g for 10 min to collect supernatants. For the ATP assay, lysates were centrifuged directly. The colorimetric reactions were performed at 37 ℃, and absorbances were measured at specific wavelengths using a microplate reader. The absolute intracellular metabolic contents were calculated based on standard curves and expressed as μmol/106 viable cells.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections (4 μm) were deparaffinized, subjected to antigen retrieval, and incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with a primary antibody against AMOT (1:200, Sigma, Shanghai, China). Following PBS washes, sections were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and visualized using DAB.

All slides were independently evaluated by two experienced pathologists blinded to patients’ clinical data. AMOT expression was quantified using the H-score method: H-score = ∑(Staining Intensity × Percentage of Positive Cells). the specific standard for scoring is shown in Appendix 1. The H-score ≥5 of the was used to stratify low and high AMOT expression groups.

Statistical analysis

All in vitro experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates, and the data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA).

For comparisons between two independent groups, a two-tailed Student’s t-test (for normally distributed data) or a Mann-Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data) was applied. Comparisons among three or more groups were evaluated using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. For time-course and repeated-measures data, a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test was utilized to assess statistical significance across different time points. Spearman’s correlation analysis assessed the expression relationship between AMOT and YAP1. P<0.05 was considered a significant difference. Data are exhibited as mean ± SD.

Results

AMOT is highly expressed in NSCLC and correlated with lower OS time

To evaluate the role of AMOT in NSCLC, its expression levels were assessed in clinical samples. Compared with adjacent normal tissues, AMOT was significantly upregulated in NSCLC tissues (P<0.001; Figure 1A,1B). According to Spearman rank correlation analysis, AMOT expression was positively correlated with adverse pathological features, including tumor size (P=0.04), clinical stage (P<0.001), and depth of tumor infiltration (T stage, P=0.008). These data suggest that NSCLC patients with higher AMOT expression present with larger tumors (≥4 cm), more advanced clinical stages (≥ Stage III), and deeper infiltration (≥ T3) (Table 2). Furthermore, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that AMOT expression was negatively correlated with the OS of NSCLC patients; high AMOT expression was significantly associated with a shorter OS time (Figure 1C). Taken together, AMOT is significantly elevated in NSCLC and is strongly linked to tumor malignancy.

Silencing AMOT significantly inhibited the cell proliferation and migration, and promoted cell apoptosis of NSCLC

We established stable AMOT-knockdown models to evaluate its function in vitro. AMOT expression was significantly downregulated in the shAMOT group compared to the shCtrl group, as confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blotting (P<0.05; Figure 2A). Cell counting assays revealed a significantly slower proliferation rate in cells transfected with shAMOT compared to shCtrl (P<0.001; Figure 2B). Colony formation assays further demonstrated that the clonogenic ability of both A549 and NCI-H1299 cells was significantly impaired in the shAMOT group (P<0.01; Figure 2C). Flow cytometry analysis indicated that the percentage of apoptotic cells was significantly increased upon AMOT silencing in both cell lines (P<0.001; Figure 2D). Furthermore, wound-healing and Transwell assays showed that the migratory capacity of NSCLC cells was significantly suppressed in the shAMOT group (P<0.05 and P<0.001, respectively; Figure 2E,2F). Collectively, these results confirm that AMOT silencing inhibits NSCLC proliferation and migration while promoting apoptosis.

To identify co-expressed partners of AMOT, protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis was performed, revealing that AMOT directly interacts with networks including YAP1, MPP5, WWTR1, LATS2, NF2, and LATS1 (Figure 3A). Spearman’s correlation analysis further revealed a robust positive expression pattern between AMOT and YAP1 (r=0.394, P<0.001; Figure 3B). Therefore, we demonstrated a strong co-expression relationship between AMOT and YAP1. In both A549 and NCI-H1299 cell lines, YAP1 expression was significantly downregulated upon AMOT knockdown (P<0.05; Figure 3C).

To further investigate the specific role of YAP1 in NSCLC, we assessed its expression levels in the normal human lung epithelial cell line BEAS-2B and NSCLC cell lines (NCI-H1299, A549, and NCI-H1975). qRT-PCR analysis revealed that YAP1 was significantly upregulated in NCI-H1299, A549, and NCI-H1975 cells compared with BEAS-2B cells (P<0.01; Figure 3D).

Next, we evaluated the knockdown efficiency of three specifically designed shRNAs targeting YAP1 in NCI-H1299 cells. The qRT-PCR results demonstrated that YAP1 expression was significantly downregulated in cells transfected with shYAP1-1 and shYAP1-2 compared to the negative control group (shCtrl) (P<0.01). Notably, shYAP1-1 exhibited the highest knockdown efficiency (69.3%) (Figure 3E). Therefore, a lentiviral vector expressing the shYAP1-1 sequence was selected for all subsequent gene knockdown experiments.

Cell viability and migration were subsequently evaluated in transfected A549 and NCI-H1299 cells. Cell viability was significantly enhanced by AMOT overexpression and suppressed by YAP1 knockdown (P<0.01; Figure 3F). Furthermore, the impaired cell viability induced by YAP1 silencing could be partially rescued by AMOT overexpression.

Similarly, the Transwell assay revealed that the number of migrated cells was significantly increased in the AMOT + NC group but decreased in the shYAP1+NC group (P<0.01; Figure 3G). Importantly, YAP1 knockdown significantly counteracted the AMOT-induced enhancement of cell migration. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that NSCLC cell proliferation and migration are driven by AMOT overexpression but restrained by YAP1 silencing, and that YAP1 depletion effectively abrogates the oncogenic effects induced by AMOT.

AMOT and YAP1 were co-expressed on regulating glycolysis through the Hippo signaling pathway

To elucidate the molecular mechanism linking AMOT and YAP1 in NSCLC, we performed Co-IP assays to examine their physical interaction. Endogenous AMOT was successfully co-immunoprecipitated with YAP1 using an anti-YAP1 antibody, confirming a direct binding interaction between the two proteins (Figure 4A). Next, to investigate whether AMOT modulates metabolic reprogramming, we quantified the intracellular levels of ATP, lactate, and glucose upon AMOT knockdown. Silencing AMOT led to a marked reduction in intracellular ATP, lactate, and glucose levels compared with the control group (Figure 4B).

To further confirm the functional dependency between AMOT and YAP1 in driving aerobic glycolysis, we conducted rescue experiments using gain- and loss-of-function cellular models. Intracellular ATP, lactate, and glucose levels were significantly increased upon AMOT overexpression (P<0.01), whereas YAP1 knockdown markedly suppressed these metabolic pools (P<0.01) and effectively counteracted the glycolytic enhancement induced by AMOT (Figure 4C). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that AMOT promotes aerobic glycolysis in NSCLC cells in a YAP1-dependent manner.

Previous studies have demonstrated that AMOT is involved in the Hippo signaling pathway in various cancers. To investigate the specific role of AMOT in the Hippo signaling pathway of NSCLC, we assessed the expression levels of LATS1 and YAP1 in A549 and NCI-H1299 cells in groups of Control, AMOT overexpression, and AMOT overexpression combined with a Hippo pathway inhibitor. We found that AMOT overexpression significantly downregulated the phosphorylation levels of core Hippo components (p-LATS1 and p-YAP1) (Figure 4D), thereby promoting total YAP1 stabilization and subsequent nuclear accumulation. Importantly, this trend was effectively reversed by the Hippo inhibitor. To validate the downstream transcriptional activity of the YAP/TEAD complex in a clinical context, we performed a correlation analysis utilizing the TCGA dataset. This analysis confirmed a strong positive correlation between AMOT expression and canonical YAP/TEAD downstream target genes (e.g., CTGF, CYR61) in NSCLC (Figure S1A,S1B), supporting the functional activation of the YAP1 transcriptional program by AMOT. Furthermore, a significant positive correlation was found between the mRNA expression of AMOT and PFKFB3 (Figure S2), a key enzyme of glycolysis and a well-established YAP target gene. Together with the intracellular metabolic measurements in this study, this evidence suggests that the AMOT/YAP axis enhances glycolytic activity, at least partially, through the upregulation of PFKFB3.

In vivo, the tumor volume and weight were significantly increased by AMOT overexpression and markedly decreased by YAP1 knockdown (Figure 4E). Immunostaining of xenograft sections revealed that elevated AMOT levels resulted in enhancedtranswell-67 staining, whereas YAP1 knockdown reduced Ki-67 positivity (Figure 4F).

Discussion

Given the high incidence, poor OS, and aggressive metabolic reprogramming associated with NSCLC, elucidating its molecular mechanisms is necessary. Our present study reveals the mechanistic role of the AMOT/YAP1/Hippo signaling axis in driving cell progression and glycolysis in NSCLC for the first time. The primary conclusions are as follows: (I) AMOT is highly expressed in NSCLC and correlates with a shorter OS; (II) we verified the direct functional interaction between AMOT and YAP1; (III) NSCLC cell proliferation and migration are promoted by AMOT overexpression and inhibited by YAP1 knockdown; and (IV) AMOT and YAP1 synergistically regulate glycolysis via the Hippo signaling pathway.

Interestingly, our finding that AMOT is significantly upregulated in NSCLC contrasts with the findings of Hsu et al. (21), who reported that AMOT was decreased in clinical lung cancer specimens. This discrepancy highlights the context-dependent, “double-edged sword” nature of AMOT. AMOT consists of two major isoforms, p130 and p80, which often exert distinct or even opposing biological functions. Our immunoblotting analysis (using an antibody recognizing both isoforms) revealed that AMOT-p130 is the predominant isoform in our NSCLC models. Unlike p80, AMOT-p130 possesses an extended N-terminal domain containing PPxY motifs, which directly interact with YAP. Emerging evidence indicates that while AMOT can sequester YAP in the cytoplasm to inhibit its activity, it can also act as an oncogenic co-factor that promotes YAP nuclear translocation and transcriptional activation under specific pathological conditions (22,23). Therefore, the elevated expression of AMOT observed in our study likely reflects the hyperactivation of the oncogenic AMOT-p130/YAP axis.

Moreover, previous reports have suggested that AMOT exerts tumor-suppressive effects by inhibiting the growth of lung cancer cells (21,24,25). In contrast, our present study demonstrates that NSCLC cell proliferation and migration are significantly driven by AMOT overexpression. Past literature revealed that the biological function of AMOT is highly context-dependent and controversial across different cancer types. For instance, it has been reported to be downregulated in breast cancer, ovarian carcinoma, and clear cell renal cell carcinoma, where its deficiency is associated with a poor prognosis (18,25). Conversely, in other malignancies such as prostate cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and osteosarcoma, AMOT is frequently overexpressed and acts as an oncogene to promote tumor cell proliferation and invasion (25-27). Therefore, AMOT appears to function in a highly tissue- and context-specific manner, which may account for the discrepancies between our findings and certain previous reports. Further comprehensive investigations are warranted to fully elucidate the complex dual role of AMOT in NSCLC.

AMOT regulates cancer progression through numerous signaling networks, including the ERK1/2, Wnt, Hippo, and VEGFR-2 pathways (25,28-32). Lin et al. (16) highlighted the importance of the UCA1-AMOT-YAP axis in ovarian cancer. However, the precise effect of the AMOT-YAP1 complex in NSCLC metabolic reprogramming had not been fully reported. Rojek et al. (33) demonstrated that AMOT/YAP1 can act independently of typical Hippo pathway-dependent genes in specific contexts. Given that YAP1 is the main effector of the Hippo signaling pathway and a critical protector of genome integrity (34-36), the physical interaction between YAP1 and AMOT establishes the AMOT/YAP1/Hippo axis as a pivotal mechanism in NSCLC.

Given the critical role of metabolic reprogramming in cancer, we investigated the specific impact of the AMOT/YAP1/Hippo signaling pathway on glycolysis in NSCLC. In our study, we found that the intracellular pools of ATP, lactate, and glucose were significantly depleted upon YAP1 knockdown, whereas they were markedly expanded following AMOT overexpression. Tumor cells predominantly rely on aerobic glycolysis to fuel their rapid growth, a metabolic shift often associated with mitochondrial DNA mutations and tumor suppressor gene mutations (37,38). Targeting this enhanced glycolytic state has emerged as a promising strategy to suppress tumor proliferation and promote cancer cell death. Importantly, our data indicate that glycolysis can be effectively restrained by intercepting the oncogenic AMOT/YAP1/Hippo signaling cascade. This discovery might provide new insights into potential targeted therapies for NSCLC. While glycolysis is also known to be regulated by other classic networks, such as the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway (39,40), our present study expands the current understanding of the complex metabolic regulatory landscape in NSCLC. Taken together, our findings demonstrate that the AMOT/YAP1/Hippo signaling pathway acts as a crucial driver of both tumor progression and glycolytic reprogramming in NSCLC.

Despite these encouraging findings, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. To begin with, the direct relationship between enhanced glycolysis and tumor progression remains to be definitively established. Secondly, the precise downstream mechanisms by which the AMOT/YAP1 axis regulates glycolysis—such as its potential crosstalk with DNA damage responses or cell cycle regulation—require deeper investigation (41). Thirdly, the relatively limited size of our clinical cohort may restrict the generalizability of our prognostic analysis, necessitating future validation in larger, multicenter cohorts. Other than these, future epistasis experiments are needed to determine the exact functional hierarchy in AMOT/YAP/Hippo signaling pathway. Finally, although our intracellular metabolic measurements, alongside the bioinformatic correlation evidence with the critical glycolytic activator PFKFB3, strongly indicate a hyperactive glycolytic profile induced by AMOT, these static intracellular assays do not provide a real-time dynamic readout. Future investigations utilizing advanced real-time metabolic analyzers such as Seahorse ECAR/OCR measurements, are warranted to comprehensively map the glycolytic flux.

Conclusions

Our study identified the AMOT/YAP1/Hippo signaling axis as a critical driver of NSCLC progression and glycolytic reprogramming. Further comprehensive studies are warranted to fully elucidate these mechanisms. Targeting this metabolic and signaling nexus may provide a novel therapeutic strategy for NSCLC intervention.
