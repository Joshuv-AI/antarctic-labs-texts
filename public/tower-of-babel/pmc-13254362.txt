Unified multimodal learning for stroke triage: joint detection, scoring, and segmentation of acute ischemic stroke
Authors: Tengfei Luan, Dianbei Wang, Song Hu, Wenyu Zhou, Ziyang Wang, Qiao Li, Pengcheng Liu, Gao Wu, Meihua Wang, Weijun Huang, Faqiang Zhang, Hao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTimely and accurate triage of acute ischemic stroke patients remains a critical challenge in clinical practice, particularly across heterogeneous imaging protocols and multicenter settings. Existing approaches often focus on isolated tasks such as large-vessel occlusion detection, collateral scoring, or infarct segmentation, leading to fragmented pipelines with limited generalizability and increased latency. In this work, we introduce a unified multimodal framework that jointly integrates non-contrast CT, CT angiography, and CT perfusion under a single encoder-decoder architecture. The system employs symmetry-aware encoders, graph-based vascular modeling, and spatio-temporal fusion, followed by multi-task heads for detection, grading, and segmentation. Trained and validated on multicenter datasets, the framework demonstrates consistent gains over state-of-the-art baselines: AUC improvements of up to +0.05 for LVO detection, a +0.11 increase in quadratic-weighted κ for collateral scoring, and a +5% Dice improvement with reduced volume error for infarct delineation. Beyond technical metrics, the model achieves clinically relevant impact by reducing mis-triage and unnecessary transfers while lowering inference time to under one minute in real-world PACS deployment. Ablation studies further confirm the synergistic benefit of multi-task optimization, which narrows the generalization gap and enhances robustness across unseen centers. Taken together, these results highlight the potential of unified multimodal learning to support fast, reliable, and scalable decision-making in acute stroke care.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research, Neurology

Introduction

Stroke remains one of the leading causes of death and disability worldwide, with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) accounting for nearly 87% of all cases. Timely identification of large-vessel occlusion (LVO) and rapid initiation of reperfusion therapy are the key determinants of favorable outcomes. Accurate localization of occlusion sites, assessment of collateral circulation, and quantification of core and penumbra volumes are therefore essential during the hyperacute phase1,2. Non-contrast CT (NCCT) is the most accessible imaging modality for emergency triage, yet it exhibits limited sensitivity to early ischemic changes and high inter-reader variability. CT angiography (CTA) and CT perfusion (CTP) provide complementary vascular and perfusion information, but differences in scanner vendors, acquisition protocols, and post-processing pipelines introduce instability and delay—particularly in smaller centers3–5.

Recent deep-learning methods have improved lesion detection and segmentation within individual modalities—for example, ADN and PAPL for NCCT6,7, PerfU-Net and 4D CNNs for CTP4,5, and graph-based vascular analysis for CTA8. However, these methods remain task- and modality-specific, forming fragmented pipelines that accumulate errors and increase latency in time-critical workflows. Existing models rarely achieve consistent cross-center generalization due to heterogeneous imaging protocols and limited annotated data, while clinical translation demands robustness, efficiency, and interpretability across diverse hospital settings9. To deliver reliable AI-assisted triage in real clinical environments, an effective system must integrate NCCT, CTA, and CTP information into a coherent representation, jointly perform detection, scoring, and segmentation to ensure internal consistency, and maintain real-time inference and domain generalization across unseen centers. Yet no prior work has unified these complementary modalities and tasks within a single, clinically deployable framework.

This work aims to bridge the gap between algorithmic accuracy and clinical workflow reliability by developing a unified, triage-oriented multimodal foundation model for acute ischemic stroke. We propose a multi-branch architecture with symmetry-enhanced NCCT encoding, graph-based vascular reasoning for CTA, and spatio-temporal fusion for CTP, jointly optimized under a shared decoder. The framework simultaneously performs LVO detection, collateral scoring, and infarct segmentation within one forward pass, leveraging multi-task learning as an implicit regularizer to improve cross-center robustness and few-shot adaptability. Beyond conventional Dice and AUC metrics, we evaluate real-world relevance through mis-triage rate, unnecessary transfer rate, and PACS I/O latency, demonstrating potential for workflow acceleration and decision consistency in multicenter retrospective analysis. Together, these advances represent a step toward a clinically deployable multimodal AI triage assistant for hyperacute stroke care.

Deep-learning techniques have considerably advanced stroke imaging analysis across individual modalities, yet most approaches remain confined to isolated tasks and lack integration for unified triage10,11. In NCCT-based studies, asymmetric representation learning has emerged as a key paradigm to enhance lesion conspicuity between hemispheres. Models such as the Asymmetry Disentanglement Network (ADN) and the Pathological Asymmetry-Guided Progressive Learning (PAPL) framework explicitly encode left-right tissue contrasts to improve early ischemic core detection6,7. Other designs, including nnU-Net and its variants, provide strong baselines for NCCT segmentation owing to self-configuring pipelines and robust data normalization strategies12–17. Nevertheless, these architectures focus primarily on tissue segmentation and do not account for downstream tasks such as vascular or perfusion assessment.

CTA-based methods have concentrated on vascular topology and collateral evaluation. Graph convolutional and attention-based networks have been used to model the hierarchical vessel tree, enabling automatic detection of large-vessel occlusions and grading of collateral circulation in accordance with mCTA standards18–20. More recent efforts have incorporated vessel radius priors and directional flow constraints to mitigate segmentation discontinuities in small distal branches. However, these models depend heavily on the quality of vessel extraction and typically operate as stand-alone modules without integration into broader stroke workflows.

For CT perfusion (CTP), deep models have shifted from vendor-derived perfusion maps to raw dynamic source data to enhance reproducibility. PerfU-Net5 and related architectures introduced symmetry-aware 4D segmentation that captures spatio-temporal perfusion dynamics, while convolutional LSTM and 4D CNN frameworks further exploit temporal continuity for core-penumbra discrimination4. Although these techniques achieve accurate tissue outcome prediction, their reliance on modality-specific preprocessing and high computational cost limits clinical scalability.

Beyond single-modality pipelines, multimodal fusion frameworks have sought to combine complementary cues from NCCT, CTA, and CTP21,22. Early works relied on simple feature concatenation or late fusion to aggregate modality-specific predictions23,24, whereas recent approaches employ cross-attention and shared latent representations to capture inter-modality dependencies25,26. Multi-task learning has also been explored in limited contexts, such as joint segmentation and classification27, yet most implementations still handle only one or two modalities, often trained on homogeneous datasets from a single center28,29. Consequently, their robustness and clinical interpretability remain restricted.

In summary, despite rapid progress in single-modality and partially multimodal approaches, current systems continue to operate in fragmented silos—separating lesion segmentation, vessel analysis, and perfusion quantification into distinct stages. To our knowledge, no prior study has unified all three modalities and clinical endpoints into a single multi-task triage framework. The present work addresses this gap by proposing a unified architecture capable of simultaneous detection, scoring, and segmentation across NCCT, CTA, and CTP domains.

Results

Experimental setup

All models were implemented in PyTorch 2.1 and MONAI 1.3, with network templates adapted from nnU-Net13,24 and Swin-UNETR30,31. Training was performed on two NVIDIA A100 GPUs (40 GB memory each). Each GPU processed a mini-batch of two samples; gradient accumulation of two steps was applied across devices, resulting in an effective global batch size of eight with optimizer updates every four iterations. This strategy balances memory efficiency with stable gradient estimation, following recommendations from recent large-scale segmentation frameworks5,19,32,33.

Optimization was carried out for up to 200 epochs using AdamW (initial learning rate 1 × 10−4, weight decay 0.01). A cosine annealing schedule with 10-epoch linear warmup was used to stabilize convergence in the early phase, consistent with prior stroke segmentation pipelines4,18,34. Early stopping was triggered if validation Dice did not improve for 20 consecutive epochs. To ensure reproducibility, three fixed random seeds (42, 123, 888) controlled the dataset split, weight initialization, data augmentation randomness, and CUDA deterministic behavior. In addition, we explicitly set cudnn.benchmark=False to avoid non-deterministic kernel selection. Complete training configurations and experiment scripts are publicly released for replication.

Datasets followed a strict leave-one-center-out evaluation protocol. In each fold, one center was held out entirely for testing, while the remaining centers were split 80/20 into training and validation sets. This simulates prospective deployment across unseen hospitals, where inter-scanner variability, acquisition protocol differences, and heterogeneous patient populations can degrade performance if the model is not robust1–3,35. Compared with random splits, this strategy enforces true domain generalization, aligning with recent recommendations from ISLES’2435 and CPAISD3.

Finally, we evaluated all methods under identical preprocessing (resampling to isotropic 1 mm resolution, intensity normalization to zero mean and unit variance) and augmentation settings (random flips, rotations, and intensity perturbations), ensuring fairness of comparison. Baselines were retrained from scratch on the same folds using their original code repositories when available, or faithfully reimplemented following reported hyperparameters5–7,12,19. This controlled setup guarantees that performance differences are attributable to architectural choices rather than inconsistencies in training strategy.

Modality-specific encoder architectures

The framework integrates three specialized encoder branches that exploit complementary physiological information while maintaining unified latent representations. The NCCT encoder employs dual-stream Swin-UNETR with explicit inter-hemispheric asymmetry modeling (Fig. 2a, d). Asymmetry amplification through feature-level difference computation (Δf = forig − fmirror) enhances sensitivity to subtle ischemic density changes (1–2 HU differences) while suppressing symmetric artifacts. This design contributes +0.04 Dice improvement (Cohen’s d = 0.42, p < 0.01), with particular gains in small lesions (<5 mL).

The CTA encoder applies Graph Attention Networks to vessel centerlines extracted via Frangi vesselness filtering (Fig. 2b). Graph-based message passing captures collateral topology and vascular hierarchy, contributing +0.03 Dice and notably +0.04 quadratic-weighted κ for collateral grading (p < 0.01). The sparse graph representation (typically 200–400 nodes) adds only 4.8% computational overhead.

The CTP encoder processes raw 4D source data (T = 30 frames) through 3D-UNetR with 1D temporal convolution (Fig. 2c), capturing perfusion kinetics directly without vendor-specific post-processing artifacts. This achieves cross-scanner reproducibility while contributing +0.02 Dice.

Individual encoder contributions are non-additive due to decoder sharing (Fig. 2f); combined benefit reaches +0.05 Dice through multimodal fusion. Generalization gap analysis (Fig. 2e) reveals that modality-specific specialization reduces overfitting: Dice gap narrows from 0.11 (single-modality) to 0.05 (multimodal) across held-out centers. Task weight optimization (Fig. 2g) shows clinically motivated weighting (λ = [1: 1: 2], emphasizing segmentation per DAWN/DEFUSE-3 criteria) outperforms uniform weighting, achieving 0.81 Dice and reducing unnecessary transfer rate from 18.9% to 12.3%.

This latency is clinically acceptable compared to manual neuroradiologist reading (4–6 min typical), enabling rapid triage without prolonging hyperacute stroke protocols where door-to-decision must occur within 30 min.

Ablation

To evaluate the individual contribution of each architectural component, we conducted a set of controlled ablation experiments on the ISLES’24 benchmark. Both quantitative metrics (AUC, accuracy, Dice, and volume error) and clinically oriented endpoints (mis-triage and unnecessary transfer rates) were examined to link algorithmic behavior with downstream decision impact.

Ablation study components

We analyzed seven architectural factors, each isolating a distinct design element while minimizing confounding effects. Their rationale and implementation are summarized below.

Symmetry-enhanced NCCT encoding

This ablation tests whether explicit inter-hemispheric asymmetry modeling—an established biomarker for early ischemic change—provides measurable benefit beyond standard convolutional encoding. Removing the mirrored-input pathway and feature-difference fusion isolates the inductive bias of symmetry awareness from mere network capacity gain.

Graph-based CTA vascular reasoning

To assess the value of vascular topology modeling, we replaced the Graph Attention Network (GAT) with a conventional 3D-CNN branch (four residual blocks, 128 channels) acting on the same Frangi-filtered CTA input. This comparison distinguishes genuine graph reasoning effects from preprocessing artifacts.

Cross-modal fusion strategy

We compared the proposed cross-attention fusion with a naive concatenation baseline. Both use the same 128-D Stage-3 features from each modality, ensuring constant model depth. The attention-based fusion adaptively reweights modality contributions, whereas the concatenation simply stacks features. This contrast evaluates whether learnable inter-modality weighting improves consistency.

Multi-task versus single-task training

To quantify representation sharing and implicit regularization, we trained three single-task variants (LVO detection, collateral scoring, and segmentation) using identical encoders but separate decoders, and compared them with the unified multi-task framework.

Few-shot adaptation

We assessed transferability by fine-tuning the model on small target-center subsets (N ∈ {1, 5, 10, 20}). All parameters were unfrozen with a learning rate of 5 × 10−5, and early stopping was applied when validation Dice plateaued for 10 epochs. Reported results average ten random seeds to reflect variance.

Task-loss weighting sensitivity

We examined three weighting schemes in the unified loss:

Configurations included: λ = 1: 1: 1 (equal weighting), λ = 1: 1: 2 (emphasizing segmentation per DAWN/DEFUSE-3 criteria), and λ = 1: 2: 2 (enhancing both collateral and segmentation tasks). The λ = 1: 1: 2 scheme was ultimately retained as it offered the best balance between lesion delineation and detection stability without overfitting smaller tasks.

Uncertainty-based rejection

Finally, we compared models with and without confidence-based rejection (threshold τ = 0.15 determined on validation data). This analysis clarifies whether mis-triage reduction results from improved calibration or merely discarding uncertain predictions.

Statistical methodology

Pairwise comparisons between each ablation and the full model used two-sided Welch’s t-tests to accommodate unequal variances. Given seven comparisons, we report adjusted significance thresholds using the Bonferroni correction (α = 0.05/7 ≈ 0.007) as a conservative reference, but emphasize practical effect sizes (Cohen’s d) and confidence intervals over strict dichotomous significance.

All ablations were repeated with n = 10 random seeds. With this sample size, the study is sensitive to small-to-moderate effects (d ≳ 0.3) at 80% power. Effect sizes and 95% CIs are reported alongside p-values, following best-practice recommendations for transparent statistical reporting in medical-AI validation. A finding is regarded as clinically relevant when improvements exceed minimal clinically important differences (mCID: ΔDice ≈ 0.03, ΔAUC ≈ 0.02, Δκ ≈ 0.05), even if p ≥ 0.007.

Generalization gaps were computed as (training) − (validation) performance, with smaller gaps indicating stronger implicit regularization. Gradient-similarity and curvature analyses were used qualitatively to corroborate these findings.

Results and interpretation

All experiments used identical preprocessing (1 mm3 isotropic resampling, z-score normalization) and augmentation settings to ensure comparability.

Architectural contributions

Symmetry-enhanced encoding improved Dice by roughly +0.03 (95% CI [+0.01,+0.05]) relative to the variant without mirroring, with the largest benefit observed for small lesions (<5 mL). Graph-based CTA reasoning yielded comparable gains (+0.02–0.03 Dice; +0.04 κ for collateral scoring), supporting its utility in capturing vascular topology. Cross-modal attention fusion added a consistent yet modest improvement of about +0.02 Dice over simple concatenation.

Multi-task synergy

Single-task Swin-UNETR achieved a mean training Dice of 0.86 vs a validation of 0.76 (gap 0.10), whereas the unified multi-task model achieved 0.85/0.81 (gap 0.04±0.01), corresponding to roughly a 50–60% reduction. Similar reductions were noted for LVO AUC (0.08→ 0.03) and collateral κ (0.09→ 0.04). These trends indicate that joint optimization acts as an implicit regularizer, narrowing the training-validation gap and improving robustness.

Clinical implications

Compared with independent single-task models, the unified approach reduced unnecessary transfer from ≈18% to ≈12% and halved mis-triage rates (from ≈10% to ≈5%), at the cost of a small latency increase (+0.1 s). This trade-off is acceptable for real-time triage applications.

Task-weighting and uncertainty

The clinically motivated weighting (λ = 1: 1: 2) produced the most stable performance across folds. Disabling confidence-based rejection raised mis-triage to ≈9%, confirming that calibrated uncertainty estimation meaningfully contributes to patient-safety metrics rather than acting as a mere filter.

Overall interpretation

Ablation findings collectively suggest that explicit symmetry modeling, vascular graph reasoning, and multi-task regularization are the key contributors to both quantitative accuracy and clinically relevant safety gains. Observed improvements are consistent across centers, yet vary modestly with image quality and acquisition protocols, supporting the framework’s robustness while avoiding claims of universal significance.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that explicit inter-hemispheric asymmetry modeling combined with graph-based vascular reasoning and temporal perfusion dynamics can unify multiple clinical objectives (LVO detection, collateral assessment, tissue outcome prediction) within a single multimodal framework. The +0.05 AUC improvement for LVO detection, +0.11κ for collateral grading, and +0.05 Dice for segmentation reflects implicit regularization arising from complementary task objectives rather than isolated architectural innovations. By unifying these tasks under a shared encoder, the system achieves an end-to-end latency of 39.47 s, enabling shorter door-to-puncture intervals critical in time-sensitive workflows. Reduction of unnecessary transfers from 27% (clinical baseline from registry) to 12% (p = 0.03) underscores the clinical utility, as such transfers delay reperfusion therapy, increase costs, and expose patients to additional procedural risk.

Interpretability remains a prerequisite for clinical adoption of AI systems. SHAP analysis reveals that left-hemisphere tissue asymmetry (0.23) and vascular geometry (0.18) dominate model decisions, validating the asymmetry-aware architecture design and aligning with neuroanatomical expectations. Patient-level saliency maps show spatial concentration in ischemic territories rather than scattered artifacts, confirming that decisions emerge from clinically coherent patterns rather than dataset-specific spurious correlations. The calibration analysis (ECE = 0.130) demonstrates that confidence estimates reliably reflect prediction accuracy, enabling principled uncertainty-based rejection: predictions below τ = 0.15 are flagged for human review without sacrificing sensitivity on unambiguous cases.

The multi-task learning synergy is mechanistically understood through loss landscape visualization: multi-task optimization produces smoother, wider convergence valleys compared to single-task approaches, reducing sensitivity to parameter perturbations and improving robustness to domain shift. This smooth landscape correlates with reduced generalization gaps (55–67% improvement across metrics) and improved cross-center performance consistency (coefficient of variation 3.8% for Dice vs 7.6% for single-task baseline). The framework maintains a consistent Dice of 0.81 ± 0.03 across seven geographically distributed centers with different scanner vendors, acquisition protocols, and patient populations—a stringent test of generalization that single-center studies cannot capture. Diagnostic reproducibility improvements are particularly valuable given well-documented inter-observer variability (intra-class correlation 0.61–0.78 for expert neuroradiologists in collateral assessment), which the framework effectively surpasses.

Several limitations warrant careful consideration. Although the framework integrates NCCT, CTA, and CTP—representing the most comprehensive multimodal assessment in acute stroke—it does not currently include transcranial Doppler, MR perfusion, or other hemodynamic modalities that could provide complementary physiological information in specific scenarios (e.g., wake-up strokes where CTP timing is ambiguous). The failure analysis revealed three error modes: motion artifacts (42%), calcification-induced false positives (31%), and missed small lesions (27%). Motion artifacts are addressable through preprocessing (image registration, motion correction) or augmentation with synthetic perturbations. Calcification disambiguation requires dynamic timing analysis or clinical correlation—a fundamental limitation of single time-point imaging. Missed lesions <5 mL, while clinically low-priority (high reversibility, below volumetric thresholds in DAWN/DEFUSE-3), suggest inherent trade-offs between sensitivity to small-volume changes and specificity for clinically actionable infarction.

The retrospective dataset design, while enabling rigorous evaluation, cannot fully capture real-world ED workflows. Prospective deployment will face broader distribution shifts, including underrepresented demographics, comorbidities, and imaging artifacts beyond motion and calcification. Integration with clinical variables (NIHSS, age, glucose, comorbidities) through multimodal fusion could enhance decision accuracy in borderline triage scenarios, particularly for extended time-window cases where imaging mismatch alone may be insufficient. However, such integration requires explicit consent and raises equity concerns around algorithmic bias in different demographic groups—necessitating external validation across diverse populations before clinical deployment.

Few-shot learning analysis demonstrates that 5 labeled samples from a new center suffice to reach Dice = 0.75, suggesting viable adoption pathways in resource-limited settings. Domain adaptation convergence at 80 epochs (vs 140 for baseline) indicates fast adaptation to new scanner protocols, reducing implementation barriers at regional and community hospitals. These findings support the scalability narrative but require validation in genuine prospective workflows where annotation quality and data distribution may differ substantially from curated research cohorts.

Looking forward, several directions merit pursuit. First, coupling the multimodal framework with large language models could enable automatic generation of structured radiology reports, bridging image-based inference with narrative communication required in multidisciplinary stroke teams. Such integration may enhance interpretability by contextualizing model outputs with patient history and clinical guidelines, potentially increasing clinician trust and adoption. Second, pretraining via multimodal foundation models offers a pathway to enhance robustness and reduce dependence on curated annotations36,37,38,39. Large-scale self-supervised pretraining across diverse neuroimaging cohorts could yield transferable representations that generalize beyond acute stroke—supporting future extensions to hemorrhage detection, chronic vascular disease monitoring, or other neuroradiological applications.

Clinical translation requires a structured roadmap: (1) prospective multicenter trials under emergency department workflows with prospective outcome tracking, (2) regulatory approval under FDA SaMD (Software as Medical Device) or CE-MDR frameworks, including transparent communication of limitations and failure modes, (3) seamless PACS/EMR integration and interoperability testing with existing clinical systems, and (4) stakeholder engagement (stroke neurologists, radiologists, hospital IT, patients) to ensure usability, interpretability, and trust. Particular attention must be paid to failure modes: clinicians need transparent communication about motion artifacts, calcification disambiguation challenges, and sub-resolution spatial limitations to make informed judgments about model recommendations.

The contribution of this work extends beyond incremental performance gains. By demonstrating that multi-task learning reduces overfitting, improves calibration, and enables interpretable decisions through SHAP analysis, we provide a foundation for clinically deployable, interoperable, and trustworthy decision support that can augment time-critical stroke triage without replacing human expertise. Future work should prioritize external validation, real-world deployment studies, and attention to equity and bias mitigation in diverse populations.

In this work, we introduced a unified multimodal framework for acute ischemic stroke assessment that integrates NCCT, CTA, and CTP within a single encoder-decoder architecture and jointly optimizes large-vessel occlusion detection, collateral circulation grading, and core/penumbra segmentation. By combining dual-stream NCCT symmetric encoding, Graph Attention Networks for vascular topology reasoning, and 1D temporal convolution for perfusion kinetics, the system achieves AUC = 0.92 for LVO detection (+0.05 vs baseline), quadratic-weighted κ = 0.72 for collateral grading (+0.11), and Dice = 0.81 for segmentation (+0.05)—improvements that reflect implicit regularization from multi-task learning rather than isolated architectural novelty.

Clinical impact extends beyond accuracy metrics. The framework reduced unnecessary inter-hospital transfers from 27% to 12% (p = 0.03) in borderline cases, translating to shortened reperfusion delays and improved patient safety. Real-world deployment feasibility is supported by end-to-end latency of 39.47 s, compatible with hyperacute stroke workflows requiring door-to-decision within 30 min. Leave-one-center-out validation across seven geographically distributed sites with heterogeneous scanners and protocols demonstrates a stable Dice of 0.81 ± 0.03 (range 0.78–0.82), with a 50–55% reduction in cross-center performance variance compared to single-task baselines. Few-shot adaptation analysis reveals that only 5 labeled samples from a new center suffice to reach Dice = 0.75, with domain adaptation converging in 80 epochs (vs 140 for baseline)—enabling rapid deployment in regional and community hospitals with limited subspecialty expertise.

Interpretability and transparency are foundational to clinical adoption. SHAP analysis demonstrates that model decisions arise from clinically coherent patterns (left-hemisphere asymmetry: 0.23, vascular geometry: 0.18) rather than spurious correlations, with patient- level saliency maps spatially concentrated in anatomically expected ischemic territories. Excellent uncertainty calibration (ECE = 0.130) enables principled rejection at τ = 0.15, automatically flagging ambiguous cases for human review without sacrificing sensitivity on unambiguous presentations. Systematic failure analysis identified three error modes—motion artifacts (42%), calcification false positives (31%), and missed small lesions (27%)—each with targeted mitigation strategies (preprocessing, dynamic timing analysis, and workflow adaptation, respectively).

The mechanistic understanding of multi-task synergy through loss landscape visualization reveals that joint optimization produces smoother convergence valleys, reducing parameter sensitivity and generalization gaps (55–67% improvement). This geometric insight explains why seemingly unrelated tasks (detection, grading, segmentation) strengthen each other—complementary gradient signals provide implicit regularization that single-task models lack.

However, important limitations warrant acknowledgment. The framework relies on retrospective, curated datasets from research centers— genuine prospective deployment under emergency department workflows will face broader distribution shifts, including underrepresented demographics, comorbidities, and imaging modalities. Missing modalities (transcranial Doppler, MR perfusion) could provide complementary hemodynamic information, particularly for wake-up or extended time-window cases. External validation across diverse populations is essential before clinical translation to ensure equitable performance and mitigate algorithmic bias.

Clinical implementation requires a structured roadmap: (1) prospective multicenter trials with prospective outcome tracking in operational ED workflows, (2) regulatory approval under FDA SaMD or CE-MDR frameworks with transparent communication of limitations and failure modes, (3) interoperability testing with existing PACS/EMR systems, and (4) stakeholder engagement (stroke neurologists, radiologists, hospital IT, patients) to ensure usability and trust. Particular attention must be paid to failure modes: clinicians need clear communication about motion artifacts, calcification disambiguation challenges, and sub-resolution spatial limitations to make informed judgments.

Future directions leveraging foundation models are promising: (1) multimodal pretraining on large-scale neuroimaging cohorts could yield transferable representations generalizing beyond acute stroke to hemorrhage detection, chronic vascular disease, or other neuroradiological applications; (2) coupling with large language models could enable automatic generation of structured radiology reports, bridging image-based inference with narrative communication required in multidisciplinary stroke teams and potentially improving interpretability through natural language contextualization with patient history and clinical guidelines.

Taken together, these contributions mark a step toward clinically deployable, interpretable, and equitable AI solutions capable of augmenting acute stroke workflows, reducing treatment delays, and ultimately improving patient outcomes. The work emphasizes that performance gains alone are insufficient for clinical adoption; rather, success requires transparent communication of limitations, human-centered interpretability, and commitment to external validation and fairness across diverse populations.

Methods

Overall workflow

Figure 5 illustrates the complete processing pipeline of our unified multimodal stroke triage framework. The system integrates three complementary neuroimaging modalities—non-contrast CT (NCCT), CT angiography (CTA), and CT perfusion (CTP)—through a coordinated encoding-fusion-decoding architecture that simultaneously performs large-vessel occlusion (LVO) detection, collateral circulation assessment, and ischemic tissue segmentation in a single forward pass.

Overall workflow of the proposed multimodal stroke triage framework.

Modality-specific encoders for NCCT, CTA, and CTP extract complementary features independently. Features are integrated via cross-modal fusion at Stage-3 and decoded through a shared decoder to produce three task-specific outputs: LVO detection and localization, collateral scoring, and ischemic core/penumbra segmentation.

The pipeline comprises four interconnected stages. First, the multimodal input stage accepts NCCT for the detection of early ischemic tissue hypodensity, CTA for high-resolution vascular visualization, and dynamic CTP time-series data capturing temporal perfusion changes. These modalities are resampled to isotropic 1 mm3 resolution and intensity-normalized to zero mean and unit variance.

Second, three specialized encoder branches process each modality independently in parallel. The NCCT encoder adopts a symmetry-enhanced Swin-UNETR architecture configured with four hierarchical stages (window size 7, embedding dimension 96, MLP expansion ratio 4). This branch incorporates explicit inter-hemispheric asymmetry modeling through dual-stream processing: the original volume and its mid-sagittal reflection are processed through parallel encoder pathways, with feature differences concatenated at each level to amplify sensitivity to subtle tissue asymmetries—an established biomarker for hyperacute ischemia. The CTA encoder employs a graph-based reasoning approach: after Frangi vesselness filtering enhances tubular structures, vessel centerlines are extracted and pruned to form nodes of a vascular graph with edges representing anatomical connectivity. A three-layer Graph Attention Network (GAT with 4 attention heads and 128-dimensional hidden features) performs message passing along the vessel tree to capture large-vessel topology and collateral compensation patterns. For cases with suboptimal vascular contrast, a CNN-based surrogate branch provides stable fallback encoding. The CTP encoder combines a 3D-UNetR backbone with temporal convolution operations applied to the dynamic perfusion sequence (T = 30 time frames). By operating directly on motion-corrected raw source data rather than vendor-derived perfusion maps, this branch achieves reproducibility across scanner platforms and acquisition protocols. Each encoder hierarchically extracts multi-scale features and projects Stage-3 intermediate representations to a shared 128-dimensional latent space.

Third, cross-modal fusion integrates the three 128-dimensional Stage-3 feature representations through cross-modal attention mechanisms. This fusion stage enables adaptive information exchange, allowing the network to learn complementary weighting of anatomical, vascular, and perfusion features. The fused multimodal representation captures jointly the morphological tissue changes visible on NCCT, the vascular architecture from CTA, and the hemodynamic status from CTP—producing a unified representation that is input to the shared decoder.

Fourth, a unified decoder processes the fused features through progressively upsampled multi-scale pathways and distributes outputs to three task-specific heads operating under joint multi-task optimization. The LVO detection head performs hierarchical classification to identify occlusion presence and localize the anatomical site (ICA, M1, M2, or MCA-M3) according to established neuroradiologic criteria. The collateral scoring head assigns ordinal grades (L0–L3) following the modified Cerebral Collateral Grading Scale (mCTA standard). The segmentation head produces voxel-wise probability maps delineating ischemic core (severe perfusion deficit with rapid tissue death) and penumbra (tissue at risk with delayed perfusion but preserved viability). All three heads share the decoder backbone, enforcing internal consistency among predictions and leveraging multi-task learning as an implicit regularization mechanism to improve cross-center generalization.

End-to-end inference completes in 0.87 ± 0.03 s per case on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU, as confirmed by FLOPs profiling (Table 2). When deployed in operational PACS workflows, including image retrieval and post-processing, total time from image acquisition to clinical decision support reaches approximately 43 s, enabling real-time triage without prolonging hyperacute stroke protocols. Importantly, this rapid throughput is achieved without sacrificing the modality-specific architectural specializations or multi-task learning benefits, as demonstrated by ablation studies (see “Ablation” section) showing that naive simplifications to reduce latency yield substantial accuracy loss.

NCCT branch

The NCCT encoder adopts a symmetry-aware Swin-UNETR backbone configured with four hierarchical stages (depth = 4, window = 7, embedding = 96, MLP = 4). Patch embedding is performed through a 3 × 3 × 3 convolution with stride 2 to preserve local structural cues while reducing spatial resolution. To model inter-hemispheric asymmetry—an established biomarker for early ischemia—the encoder introduces a dual-stream feature-level symmetry module: each NCCT volume is mirrored along the mid-sagittal plane, processed through identical encoder pathways, and the feature difference Δf(l)=forig(l)−fmirr(l) is concatenated with skip connections prior to fusion, as detailed in Algorithm 1.

CTA branch

Following the NCCT encoder, the CTA stream focuses on vascular topology and collateral pattern reasoning. A Frangi vesselness filter (σ ∈ [1, 3], β = 0.5, c = 500) enhances tubular structures and suppresses parenchymal noise. Vessel centerlines are extracted via ITK-based thinning, and segments are pruned when either the radius is <0.5 mm or the segment length is <10 voxels. Remaining segments form nodes of a vascular graph, with edges representing anatomical continuity or spatial proximity (<5 mm). The graph is processed by a three-layer Graph Attention Network (heads = 4, hidden = 128) with residual and feed-forward connections. This enables adaptive message passing along the vascular tree, effectively capturing large-vessel occlusions and collateral compensation. In low-contrast cases, a 3D-CNN surrogate branch with four residual blocks provides a stable fallback encoder jointly trained under the same vascular supervision.

CTP branch

The CTP encoder models dynamic perfusion using a 3D-UNETR backbone augmented with a temporal convolutional bottleneck. Given a sequence S∈RX×Y×Z×T (T = 30), spatial features {f(l)} are extracted at four scales, while temporal dependencies are captured through a 1D convolution (kernel = 3, channels = 64, stride = 1) along T. Mid-level fusion at Stage-3 aligns temporal and spatial embeddings via element-wise addition fmid = f(3) + Proj(ftemp). Operating directly on motion-corrected raw sequences—rather than vendor-derived perfusion maps—ensures robustness across acquisition protocols and scanners.

All three encoders output Stage-3 features that are projected to a shared 128-dimensional latent space, enabling direct cross-modal attention within the unified fusion-decoder. This modular design balances modality-specific specialization with global optimization efficiency, forming the foundation for integrated triage inference across NCCT, CTA, and CTP.

Training protocol

All experiments were conducted on a high-performance cluster equipped with two NVIDIA A100 GPUs (40 GB memory each). Each GPU processed two volumetric samples per step, and gradient accumulation of two was applied across devices, yielding an effective global batch size of eight with synchronized optimizer updates. This setup balances memory efficiency and training stability, and ensures equivalence of optimization across parallel streams.

For initialization, different strategies were employed across modalities. The NCCT encoder was initialized from ImageNet-22K pretraining, which provided strong low-level structural priors for gray-white matter contrast. The CTA graph attention network, in contrast, was trained from scratch, as preliminary experiments confirmed that ImageNet-derived weights offered negligible benefit (improving AUC by less than 0.5%). For the CTP branch, we applied a self-supervised temporal contrastive pretraining scheme on unlabeled perfusion sequences, which stabilized the temporal feature extractor and reduced overfitting when adapting to small target-center cohorts.

Optimization was performed with AdamW using an initial learning rate of 1 × 10−4, weight decay of 0.01, and cosine annealing learning-rate scheduling. A linear warmup of 10 epochs was included to prevent unstable early updates. Training was capped at 200 epochs, with early stopping triggered when validation Dice failed to improve for 20 consecutive epochs. Although Dice was chosen as the primary monitoring metric to reflect the most clinically critical endpoint of core/penumbra segmentation, LVO AUC and collateral κ were also recorded to ensure stable progress across all tasks.

Complexity analysis

We further assess the computational efficiency of the proposed framework in terms of model parameters, floating-point operations (FLOPs), and end-to-end latency. All measurements were performed on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU with a batch size of one and volumetric inputs resampled to 963 voxels. FLOPs for convolutional and transformer components were estimated using the fvcore profiling library with identical settings across all models. Graph-based operations were evaluated through a custom implementation that explicitly accounts for sparse neighborhood aggregation in the vessel graph. Latency was measured as the mean inference time across 200 test cases, including both data preprocessing and forward propagation.

Despite integrating three complementary modality-specific branches, the proposed architecture maintains computational efficiency competitive with single-modality baselines. Relative to Swin-UNETR, we observe a modest +8% increase in total parameters (68.3M vs. 62.5M), primarily attributable to the addition of the CTA graph attention pathway and CTP temporal convolution modules. Regarding FLOPs, the proposed framework requires 1024G compared to Swin-UNETR’s 956G, representing a +7% increase. This modest overhead is partially mitigated by early-stage cross-modal fusion at Stage-3, which consolidates redundant feature extraction across branches before the computationally expensive decoder stage—a design choice that prevents the far greater computational cost that would result from processing three independent full pipelines.

Notably, ablation experiments confirm that graph-based CTA encoding—despite involving explicit message passing over vessel graphs—contributes only ~4.8% of the overall computational cost (approximately 49G of 1024G FLOPs). This efficiency is achieved through sparse neighborhood aggregation: the vascular graph typically contains 200–400 nodes, making GAT operations substantially cheaper than dense 3D convolutions on the full volume. The CTP temporal convolution module adds ~3.2% of total FLOPs, also a modest increment for capturing dynamic perfusion information.

The measured inference latency was 0.87 ± 0.03 s per case on a single GPU, which is clinically acceptable for emergency triage scenarios where rapid decision support is essential. When integrated into operational PACS workflows (including image retrieval, preprocessing, and post-processing), total time from acquisition to clinical output reaches approximately 43 s, enabling real-time triage without prolonging hyperacute stroke protocols. Importantly, this latency remains competitive with single-modality methods (nnU-Net: 1.20s, Swin-UNETR: 1.85s per case), demonstrating that multimodal integration does not impose a prohibitive computational burden.

The modest increases in parameters (+8%) and FLOPs (+7%) represent a reasonable trade-off for the substantial clinical gains achieved: +5% Dice improvement, 0.05 AUC gain in LVO detection, +0.11 quadratic-weighted κ in collateral grading, and critically, reduction of unnecessary transfers from 27% to 12%. This balance between accuracy and efficiency supports the framework’s suitability for real-time PACS deployment and prospective clinical translation.

Clinical endpoints

In addition to conventional technical metrics, we evaluated endpoints that directly reflect clinical safety and workflow efficiency in acute stroke management. These endpoints were selected in close consultation with practicing neuroradiologists and stroke neurologists to ensure alignment with real-world clinical priorities.

System-level decision workflow. Before defining the clinical endpoints, we first clarify how the three model outputs jointly inform triage decisions. The AI system integrates LVO detection, collateral scoring, and infarct segmentation into a unified recommendation pipeline: (1) LVO presence determines whether mechanical thrombectomy is potentially indicated; (2) collateral grade (L0–L3) modifies the urgency and expected benefit of intervention; (3) core and penumbra volumes establish treatment window eligibility according to DAWN/DEFUSE-3 criteria. Patients flagged as LVO-positive with favorable tissue profiles (core <70 mL, mismatch ratio >1.8) are automatically recommended for inter-hospital transfer to comprehensive stroke centers. This hierarchical decision structure ensures that the clinical endpoints reflect real-world triage logic rather than isolated task performance.

False-positive mis-triage rate: the proportion of patients flagged by the AI as thrombectomy-eligible where subsequent digital subtraction angiography (DSA) failed to demonstrate large-vessel occlusion. Ground-truth adjudication was performed by two board-certified neuroradiologists who were blinded to the AI predictions, reviewing DSA images acquired within 90 min of the initial CT scan. Inter-rater reliability achieved Cohen’s κ = 0.86, indicating excellent agreement. This metric is particularly important because false-positive triage recommendations expose patients to unnecessary invasive evaluation or treatment, which carries substantial clinical risk, including procedural complications, radiation exposure, and contrast-induced nephropathy.

The second endpoint quantifies the proportion of cases recommended for inter-hospital transfer by the AI system that were ultimately determined to be non-beneficial, defined as patients who, upon arrival at the comprehensive stroke center, were either managed conservatively or deemed ineligible for mechanical thrombectomy due to poor collateral status, excessive core volume (>100 mL), or contraindications identified on repeat imaging. Ground truth was established through consensus review by a panel of three independent experts consisting of two neuroradiologists and one stroke neurologist, who were blinded to the AI outputs but had access to complete imaging series, clinical notes, and treatment records from both the referring and receiving hospitals. The consensus process achieved a kappa of 0.81, reflecting substantial agreement.

This endpoint captures the broader health-economic implications of the system, since over-triage increases healthcare costs, places unnecessary burden on tertiary centers, and exposes patients to risks associated with inter-hospital transport, including treatment delays (median 87 min in our regional network), ambulance-related adverse events, and psychological distress.

Clinical endpoint evaluation was performed on a dedicated test cohort of 30 borderline cases (ASPECTS 5–7, presenting 4.5–9 h from onset) where triage decisions were clinically ambiguous. This sample size provides 80% power to detect a 15-percentage-point difference in unnecessary transfer rate (α = 0.05, two-sided), corresponding to a reduction from 27% (historical baseline from our stroke registry) to 12% (target threshold). For false-negative mis-triage, we evaluated all 42 DSA-confirmed LVO cases in the test set, ensuring adequate representation of both proximal (ICA/M1, n = 28) and distal (M2/M3, n = 14) occlusions. Subgroup analyses by occlusion site, baseline NIHSS (<10 vs ≥10), and imaging center (academic vs community hospital) are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

While DSA is considered the reference standard for LVO diagnosis, it has recognized limitations, including 5–10% false-negative rates for distal occlusions and operator-dependent interpretation variability. To mitigate this, we required consensus between two independent neuroradiologists and cross-referenced DSA findings with baseline CTA, follow-up imaging, and clinical trajectory. For cases where DSA and clinical outcomes were discordant (n = 3), a third senior neuroradiologist with >15 years of experience provided tie-breaking adjudication. We acknowledge that our binary classification of “beneficial vs non-beneficial” transfer does not capture the full spectrum of clinical nuance; for instance, patients who underwent thrombectomy but achieved poor functional outcomes (mRS 4–6 at 90 days) due to factors unrelated to imaging-based triage were still classified as “beneficial” transfers if the procedural decision was appropriate based on available information at the time of triage.

Together, these clinical endpoints provide a rigorous assessment of system-level reliability. False-positive and false-negative mis-triage rates jointly emphasize patient safety by quantifying bidirectional diagnostic errors, while the unnecessary transfer rate addresses workflow optimization and resource allocation. By formally defining all three endpoints, specifying adjudication procedures, reporting inter-rater agreement, sample sizes, and statistical power, the evaluation protocol aligns with established standards for clinical AI validation in emergency stroke care.
