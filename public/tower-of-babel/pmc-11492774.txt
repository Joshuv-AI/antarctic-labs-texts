The construction and validity assessment of the respiratory air quality health index (AQHI) based on the analytic hierarchy process in Tianjin, China
Authors: Qiang Zeng, Yu Bai, Mengnan Zhang, Yang Ni
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAir quality health index (AQHI), as a developed air quality risk communication tool, has been proved to be more accurate in predicting air quality related health risks than air quality index (AQI) by previous studies. However, the standard method to construct AQHI is summing the excess risks of single-pollutant models directly, which may ignore the joint effect of air pollutant mixtures.MethodsIn this study, a new method which could solve the aforementioned problem, Analytic hierarchy process (AHP), was introduced. Based on this method, we constructed the respiratory health related AQHI using years of life lost (YLL) as indicator of health outcome and compared its validity with AQI.ResultsThere was a correlation between daily AQI and AQHI in 2019 (R2 = 0.830, P < 0.01), and the chi-square test between the two excellent rates showed a statistically significant difference (χ2 = 4.156, P < 0.05). Both AQI and AQHI were correlated with the daily respiratory YLL (P < 0.01), however, the coefficient of AQHI was larger than those of AQI.ConclusionsThis study indicated that compared with AQI, the constructed AQHI based on AHP may predict the health risk of air pollution more effectively. AHP may become a new method to construct AQHI which needs to be proved by taking into consideration by more studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20399-8.

Background

Previous studies have confirmed that ambient air pollution can increase the incidence and mortality of several diseases [5, 6, 16, 18]. A report of the World Health Organization (WHO) demonstrated that ambient air pollution has become one of the biggest environmental risks to public health and about 4.2 million non-communicable disease deaths could be attributed to ambient air pollution in 2016. Among these, 18% were due to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or acute lower respiratory tract infections and 6% were due to lung cancer.

Air Quality Index (AQI) has been used by most of countries in the world to evaluate the air quality. However, the AQI is determined by only one particular air pollutant which is the main air pollutant on the day, therefore, it cannot reflect the combined effects caused by multiple air pollutants on public health and the non-threshold exposure–response relationship between air pollutants and health. Air quality health index (AQHI), as a developed air quality risk communication tool, was introduced by the Canadian Ministry of Environment and Health in 2008 [13]. It is constructed by taking into consideration of the effects of air pollutants on different types of health outcomes, therefore, it could reflect the non-threshold exposure–response relationship between air pollutants and health. Thus, it may be a flexible and widely applicable tool for atmospheric quality assessment.

Nevertheless, a limitation of the standard method to construct AQHI summing the excess risks of single-pollutant models directly, that may ignore the joint effect of air pollutant mixtures, has been reported by the previous study [4]. Therefore, a new method which could solve the aforementioned problem, Analytic hierarchy process (AHP), was introduced in this study. AHP is a mathematical model for decision-making that was developed in the management field. This model can be used to consider complex multi-objective decision-making problems as a system and decompose the main goal into multiple objectives, indicators, or criteria as sub-goals [15]. According to the inter-criteria correlation and affiliation, these sub-goals are aggregated and combined at different levels to form a multi-layer structure model [12]. The process of comparing two sub-goals in the same hierarchy and assessing their relative importance to a goal from the upper hierarchy is known as constructing the judgment matrix. This process reduces the original decision-making problem to a model that calculates the relative weights of different sub-goals to the main goal or ranks the relative importance of different sub-goals [3]. Finally, the alternatives for the main goal can be chosen or summarized by the results of these relative weights or ranks, and the decision can be made [23]. AHP is now widely used in the fields of risk assessment, safety science, and environmental science [11, 17, 21, 22], and many researchers have applied this method to analyze and evaluate the air quality in different cities of China.

Additionally, the health outcomes indicators used in the previous studies which adapted to construct the AQHI were mainly mortality, hospitalization rate or consultation rate, the disease burden indicators such as years of life lost (YLL) were fewer used [4]. This may the ignorance of the information such as the age of death and finally lead to a bias in the prediction of air quality and health risks reflected by AQHI, especially for sensitive population [2, 7, 19]. In contrast, YLL as an indicator of disease burden can take premature death and life expectancy into consideration, and has been widely used as an indicator of health outcomes in the assessment of the effects of air pollution on health [20].

In this study, YLL of respiratory system were used as the surrogate of health outcome and AQHI was constructed basing on the AHP and assessed the validity of the constructed AQHI.

Methods

Data source

Air pollution and meteorological data

Data including daily concentrations of air pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, O3, and CO), AQI and meteorological factors (including temperature and relative humidity) from January 1, 2014, to December 31, 2019 were collected from Tianjin Environmental Monitoring Center (https://www.tjemc.org.cn/html/1/index.html) and Tianjin Meteorological Bureau (http://tj.cma.gov.cn/), respectively.

Mortality data

Data of daily respiratory death counts YLL were obtained from the Death Register and Report Information System of Tianjin Center for Disease Control and Prevention and the standard Expected Years of Life Lost (SEYLL) issued by the World Health Organization was used to calculate the respiratory YLL for each death at different ages according to the age of death.

Database establishment

We established two kinds of time-series database (including air pollution data, AQI, meteorological data and mortality data) by date. The database of 2014–2018 was used to construct the AQHI, and the database of 2019 was used to evaluate the validity of the AQHI.

Statistical analysis

Exposure–response relationship between pollutants and YLL

The exposure–response relationships between the air pollutants (including PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, O3, and CO) and respiratory YLL were fitted using the generalized additive model (GAM). Confounding factors including temperature, humidity, long-term temporal trends, day-of-week, and holiday were adjusted in the model. Constructing a new integrated pollutant model

The eigenvectors Wi of the judgment matrix M–O were the weights assigned to each air pollutant in this study. After standardizing the pollutant concentrations corresponding to the optimum lag days for each pollutant, a new integrated pollutant index was constructed basing on the resulting weights.

Constructing the AQHI model

Exposure–response relationships between the integrated pollutant indicator and daily respiratory YLL were calculated using GAM according to the time-series analysis method in step 2.2.1. After obtaining the exposure–response coefficient β, AQHI was calculated according to the following equations:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{P}}_{\text{t}}=\sum {\text{w}}_{\text{a}}\times {\text{x}}_{\text{at}}$$\end{document}Pt=∑wa×xat9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{AQHI}=\left(\frac{\beta\times{\textit{P}}_\textit{t}}{{\max_{\mathrm t=}}_{1,2,...,n}\;(\beta\times{\mathrm P}_t)}\right)\times10+5$$\end{document}AQHI=β×Ptmaxt=1,2,...,n(β×Pt)×10+5where Pt is the value of the integrated pollutant on day t; wa is the weight of air pollutant a; xat represents the concentration of pollutant a on day t; β is the exposure–response coefficient for the integrated pollutant; maxt=1,2,…,n (β × Pt) represents the maximum value of (β × Pt) within the nth day.

Grades of AQHI and AQI

The daily constructed AQHIs were classified into 5 health risk levels including I (0–4), II (4–7), III (7–8), IV (8–10), and V (10+). And the daily AQIs were classified into 5 levels according to the current standards which were shown in Table S2.

Validity assessment of AQHI

To assess the validity of AQHI, we firstly calculated the daily AQHI from January 1 to December 31, 2019 using the AQHI constructed model as described above. Then, the daily AQHI of 2019 were merged with the database of 2019 mentioned above by date to form a new database of 2019. Then, the percentages of days at different levels for AQHI and AQI in 2019 were calculated. Basing on the new database of 2019, we assessed the validity of AQHI as follows:

Firstly, the first level of AQI and AQHI was considered as the excellent level, and the annual excellent rates of these two indexes in 2019 were calculated, respectively. Then, the χ2 test was performed to compare the difference between the excellent rates of AQI and AQHI. Secondly, the correlation between the daily AQHI and the daily AQI was assessed using the Pearson correlation test. Finally, the correlations between health outcomes (including respiratory death counts and YLL) and these two indices were tested using the Pearson correlation test.

All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software package(version 4.2.3).

Discussion

Since the last century, there have been several public nuisance events of air pollution around the world. These events have raised awareness about the impact of air pollution on health and promoted the formulation and development of relevant policies and systems for atmospheric environment management. Several countries, including the UK and the US, have enacted Clean Air Acts to prevent air pollution, and some studies have shown that the air quality in these places has greatly improved since the bills were passed [1, 8]. Therefore, it is clear that promoting the implementation of air pollution related policies and establishing corresponding air quality standards are vitally important and significant for preventing air pollution. To improve the quality of the atmospheric environment and bring it up to the standards, it is necessary to evaluate the air quality effectively and reliably, and then use it as a guideline and basis for atmospheric environment management.

Most countries currently use AQI to evaluate regional air quality, and China began using AQI in 2012 as a replacement for the previously used API. However, some problems have been gradually exposed in the practical application of AQI, which have not yet been well resolved. For example, AQI only selects the pollutant with the highest sub-index among the evaluated pollutants to represent the overall air pollution situation, so it cannot reflect the comprehensive impact of each pollutant, nor can it reflect the non-threshold exposure–response relationship between pollutant concentration and health outcomes. Additionally, AQI calculation is usually based on data from several monitoring stations that are often located in hot-spot areas and cannot reflect spatial changes throughout the city, therefore having no way of reflecting the health risks associated with exposure to air pollution for the general population. The Air Quality Health Index (AQHI), a new index model for air quality assessment developed by Environment Canada and Health Canada in 2008, can compensate for the deficiencies of AQI to some extent. Compared with AQI, AQHI can incorporate the exposure–response relationship between different pollutants and various health outcomes into the evaluation system, which can better reflect the risks of ambient air pollution in the study area.Furthermore, AQHI can focus on people with different levels of sensitivity to air pollution and provide recommendations on how to travel and exercise healthily at levels of air quality that include low, moderate, high, and very high health risks [9, 14].

Most of the early indices used for air quality evaluation were based on simple superposition, arithmetic averaging, and other calculation methods, and the mathematical model was relatively simple. However, because the structure of these indices is too homogeneous, their calculation results are easily affected by the number of pollutants. Additionally, when the concentration levels of each pollutant in the indices construction were too different, some pollutants may have a more prominent impact than others, thereby obscuring the effect of higher-concentration pollutants. Therefore, these indices could only be applied to scenarios where the concentration levels of each pollutant were similar, limiting their applicability greatly. AQI, which is now widely used worldwide, uses the maximum values method in construction to emphasize the highest sub-index pollutant, indicating the pollutant with the greatest impact on the atmospheric environment in the short term [10]. However, this method may overlook low-concentration pollutants that have significant impacts on the environment. In this study, AHP was used to measure and assign weights to different pollutants, effectively avoiding the limitations such as those related to the limited number of pollutants and neglecting low-concentration pollutants' influence. Considering the effects of all pollutants on air quality, AQI can be applied to more complex and extensive scenarios. As a decision-making analysis method, AHP can decompose the target and determine the relative importance of the sub-targets to the final goal through the comparison between the indicators at the same level. The basic idea of AHP model construction is consistent with the research objectives of investigating the influence of pollutants on air quality and can meet the requirements of assigning weights to pollutants. Compared to other assignment methods, AHP is more flexible in determining decisions and criteria at each level and avoids problems associated with non-Gaussian distributed principal elements that may be non-optimal or difficult to interpret when using principal component analysis or entropy weighting methods. These problems arise when using principal component analysis to weight factors because non-Gaussian distributed principal elements may not be optimal and final evaluation results may be difficult to interpret. Similarly, weighting processes are limited by the number of pollutants and concentration levels when using entropy weighting method. In contrast, AHP is more flexible in determining decisions and criteria at various levels and can avoid these problems.

In addition, the quantitative method was used to determine the evaluation factors of each pollutant, which not only makes the factors comparable but also can effectively compensate for the shortcomings caused by the subjective evaluation in the construction of the traditional AHP models.

Several previous studies have tried to construct the AQHI [4] and mortality or hospitalization rate were mainly used as health outcomes indicators which may lead to the ignorance of the information such as the age of death anda bias in the prediction of air quality and health risks reflected by AQHI, especially for sensitive population [2, 7, 19].In contrast, YLL as an indicator of disease burden can take premature death and life expectancy into consideration, and has been widely used as an indicator of health outcomes in the assessment of the effects of air pollution on health [20].Therefore, YLL was used as an health outcomes indicators to construct AQHI in our study in order to increase the accurate in the prediction of air quality and respiratory health risks reflected by AQHI.

Our study has several strengths. Firstly, AHP model was used to construct AQHI and evaluate the air quality, providing a new or alternative method for standard air quality evaluation. Secondly, the exposure–response relationship between various pollutants and respiratory mortality within five years was analyzed. Through dimensionality reduction treatment using AHP, a composite pollutant model that conformed to the characteristics of air pollution in Tianjin was obtained. The constructed AQHI can comprehensively reflect the health effects of multiple pollutants without thresholds and cover a wider range of people and atmospheric conditions. The accuracy and applicability of the risk prediction ability of the constructed AQHI were evaluated with mortality data, making the results realistic and convincing.

In addition, there are several limitations to this study. Firstly, the study period wasn't too long, so fluctuations in pollutant concentrations may have affected the stability of the constructed index. Secondly, considering the density of the population and residents' coverage of monitoring stations, only six districts of Tianjin were chosen for this study. Therefore, the distribution of pollution sources and pollutant concentrations may differ from the citywide level, limiting the applicability of the constructed AQHI. If we optimize the exposure assessment method of pollutants and collect pollutant concentrations when their trends are stable, the stability and applicability of the constructed AQHI could be enhanced.

Conclusions

In summary, we found that compared with AQI, the constructed AQHI based on AHP may predict the health risk of air pollution more effectively. AHP may become a new method to construct AQHI which needs to be proved by taking into consideration by more studies.
