Co-design of pathway and manual for a dietitian-led approach to the management of type 2 diabetes in Nepal
Authors: Devendra Raj Singh, Suvash Nayaju, Kshitij Karki, Nani Shobha Shakya, Narendra Kumar Chaudhary, Rajeeb Kumar Sah, Pranil Man Singh Pradhan, Dev Ram Sunuwar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType 2 diabetes (T2D) is a global public health problem, and its prevalence in resource-poor countries like Nepal has been rapidly increasing. However, there is a lack of a contextual and consistently structured, guided approach tailored to the integrated management of T2D in Nepal. Therefore, this study aims to co-design a pathway and manual for a dietitian-led dietary approach to the management of T2D in Nepal.MethodsThe study used a co-design method guided by the “Design Thinking” approach. Data were collected through a purposively selected group of 14 participants who participated in a co-design workshop, and nine participants with whom qualitative interviews were conducted to validate the co-designed contents. Before conducting the co-design workshop, nineteen one-on-one consultative meetings were carried out with various stakeholders. The data analysis followed the content analysis technique and presented results under common broader themes.ResultsThe results from the first part of a co-design workshop are presented under themes including complexities of diabetes management in the Nepalese context, social and behavioural challenges, dietary adherence challenges and service providers’ responses, and treatment approaches. The results from the second part of the workshop are presented under the themes, including the pathway for the dietitian-led dietary approach for the management of diabetes and the contents and design of the Nutrition Education Manual for the management of T2D.ConclusionsThe pathway co-designed for integrated management of T2D offers a contextual framework for understanding the mechanisms of change underlying the integrated efforts in T2D management. At the same time, the Nutrition Education Manual provides practical guidance for promoting healthy dietary behaviours among patients with T2D.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-025-01202-2.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes, Nutrition education manual, Dietary approach, Co-design, Nepal

Background

Globally, type 2 diabetes (T2D) is one of the leading causes of mortality and morbidity among the adult population [1, 2]. T2D is caused by a combination of modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors [3]. Modifiable risk factors for T2D include being overweight and obese, poor dietary habits, and unhealthy lifestyle behaviours, such as sedentary behaviour, smoking, and alcohol consumption [3, 4]. Non-modifiable risk factors include a family history of diabetes, age, sex, and race/ethnicity [3, 4]. Together, these factors significantly increased the risk of T2D [1, 3, 5]. In addition, emerging evidence highlights the role of epigenetic modifications, gut microbiota imbalances, polygenic risk, and exposure to an obesogenic environment in increasing susceptibility to T2D [6, 7]. Recent reports on the global burden of disease showed diabetes had been considered a major factor for cardiovascular disease, including heart disease and stroke, and one of the leading causes of disease burdens worldwide [1, 2]. In 2021, the number of people living with diabetes was approximately 537 million adults in the age group between 20 and 79 years, and it is estimated to rise to 643 million by 2030 [8, 9]. Most of the diabetes population resides in resource-poor countries, and prevalence has risen rapidly in low and middle-income countries (LMICs) [10, 11]. The mortality due to diabetes has increased by 13% among the adult population in LMICs [11, 12]. South Asia has become the hub of diabetes, with people accounting for approximately 60% of the total global diabetes population [9, 13–15]. The sharp rise in the incidence of T2D poses multiple challenges to healthcare systems in resource-poor countries, due to the consequential complications resulting from the inadequacy of the health workforce, the high financial burden of disease treatment, poor healthcare financing, and limited awareness of the disease among the population [14, 16].

Resource-poor countries like Nepal, which are undergoing rapid urbanisation and facing nutrition transitions, have been hugely contributing to the rapid increase in the prevalence of diabetes [17–19]. Additional factors, such as poor lifestyle behaviours among the adult population, fragile healthcare systems, limited availability of health services, and inadequate awareness about prevention and management, particularly the dietary perspective of T2D, have exacerbated the situation [16, 20–22]. Studies showed as much as an 8.5% prevalence rate of type 2 diabetes in Nepal, which is further expected to increase due to intensive lifestyle modification and rapid urbanisation [20, 22]. Studies have reported poor knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding the prevention and management of diabetes in LMICs [18, 21, 23, 24]. Despite adopting the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended package of essential non-communicable disease interventions in Nepal since 2016, there has been no effective implementation of these interventions at all levels of the healthcare system [25]. Most government health facilities, including the peripheral healthcare system (primary healthcare centres and health posts), lack adequate health services for the diagnosis, treatment, and management of T2D [16, 23, 24]. Only a limited number of secondary and tertiary care hospitals have been providing diabetes treatment and management services from skilled health workers, including endocrinologists, ophthalmologists, primary care physicians, nurses, pharmacists, ophthalmic assistants, and dietitians [23, 24]. However, the services have been accessible to a limited population as most of these health facilities are concentrated in urban areas [16, 18, 24]. Evidence shows management of T2D is more effective when services are delivered by various healthcare professionals in an integrated manner [26, 27]. However, such integrated management of T2D is lacking in Nepal. Only a few hospitals in major cities in the country have qualified dietitians to provide diet counselling services to diabetes patients. As a result, patients are often compelled to depend entirely on the clinical management of diabetes, which alone is not adequate to control blood sugar levels. There is also a lack of a standardized nutrition education protocol (manuals or guidelines) for nutrition education counselling for T2D in Nepal. This evidence gap has been creating challenges in integrated diabetes management in Nepal. Additionally, the dietitian has been struggling to maintain uniformity in disseminating educational information and materials to patients with T2D regarding local food choices and the socio-cultural behaviours of patients. Global evidence indicates that an integrated clinical and dietary approach to managing diabetes, combined with the adoption of local guidelines, has been an effective strategy for managing T2D [28–30]. Likewise, the wide engagement of service users, service providers, and other stakeholders in jointly developing interventions for T2D has been found effective [31, 32]. We aimed to develop a tailored Diabetic Education Manual that utilises a co-design method to develop achievable and cost-effective food-based dietary interventions. This research also presents the possibility of a groundbreaking practical diabetes management strategy, potentially shifting primary diabetes care towards an integrated and multisectoral approach that includes medical services, food-based strategies, and personalised care. Therefore, this study aims to co-design a pathway and manual for a dietitian-led dietary approach to managing T2D in Nepal. This manual is expected to be useful for dietitians, clinicians, nurses, patients with diabetes, caregivers and other stakeholders. This is the first study of its kind in Nepal that utilised a co-design approach to fill the evidence gap by developing a nutrition education manual for T2D in Nepal. This study was part of our randomised controlled trial, which was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04267367) on February 12, 2020 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04267367) [33]. The effectiveness of this nutrition education manual for the dietitian-led management of T2D was reported through a randomised controlled trial, which has been published elsewhere [34]. Our previous paper reported that using a co-designed nutrition education manual through a Dietitian-led intervention improved glycaemic control, macronutrient intake and clinical outcomes among patients with T2D [26].

Methods

Design and methods

The study was conducted in Kathmandu, the capital of Nepal, between August 2021 and May 2022. The study employed a co-design method [35] guided by the “Design Thinking” approach to develop a manual for a dietitian-led dietary approach to managing diabetes [36]. The design thinking approach is characterised by the empathetic lens with three integrated phases where service user, stakeholder engagement and participation are essential components [37]. The three integrated phases in the Design Thinking approach are inspiration, ideation, and implementation (Fig. 1). The first phase (inspiration) explores the various needs of service users and concerned stakeholders. The second phase (ideation) includes encouraging and engaging the service users and concerned stakeholders to generate, develop, and test realistic solutions. The third phase (implementation) intends to execute the co-designed solutions from the ideation phase. This paper presents the results from the ideation phase, whereas the findings from the inspiration and implementation phases have been submitted for publication separately elsewhere. The prototype (detailed manual) developed during the ideation phase of this study has been implemented in the randomised controlled trial [33, 34]. The ideation phase in this study included a multi-stakeholder co-design workshop for developing a manual for dietitian-led dietary approach management of T2D. The final version of the co-designed manual was implemented in the Randomised Controlled Trial (RCT) [33, 34].

Setting and participants

The study was conducted among purposively selected participants in Kathmandu Valley, the capital of Nepal. This study included consultative meetings with 19 participants, a co-design workshop with 14 participants, and post-workshop validation using qualitative interviews with nine participants. The nine participants selected for the post-validation interviews comprised a mix of both those who had participated in and those who had not participated in the co-design workshop. Recruitment of participants for the study began seven weeks prior to the workshop. We aimed to recruit participants who represent diverse backgrounds in terms of occupation, gender, ethnicity, professional expertise, and years of experience, enabling them to contribute from varied perspectives. We recruited health professionals from the professional network of the university medical school and patients from the endocrinology clinic of the tertiary care hospital. The study participants for the consultative meetings, workshop and interviews included patients with T2D, caregivers, dietitians, general physicians, paramedics, nurses, public health professionals and academics. To ensure the diversity among the patient sample, we included patients from a middle-class economic background, as well as those who were employed full-time and those not currently in paid employment. We also ensured gender balance across the sample. All participants in the study were 18 years of age or older and had given their voluntary consent to participate. Patients with T2D who were severely ill (HbA1c ≥ 93mmol/mol (10.5%)) and not willing to participate in the whole-day workshop were excluded from the study.

Data collection techniques

This study collected the data through a series of consultative meetings, a co-design workshop, and post-workshop validation using qualitative interviews. The study was initiated and coordinated by a team of five researchers (authors DRSi, DRSu, KK, SN, and NKC). The team contacted the participants and organised consultative meetings and a co-design workshop. The team has experience in conducting qualitative studies and participatory and co-design research. The data were recorded in a voice recorder.

Consultative meetings

A series of consultative meetings were conducted separately among service users (patients with diabetes and family caregivers) and service providers (general physicians (GPs)), specialised endocrinologists, paramedics, dietitians and nurses. The experiences of service users related to self-management of diabetes, health-seeking behaviours, service utilisation, and the potential of a dietary approach for managing diabetes were discussed. Similarly, the experience of service providers related to treatment approaches for diabetes, adherence to treatments, and possible integrated approaches for a clinical and dietary approach to managing diabetes in the Nepalese context were discussed. These consultative meetings also served as the preliminary work for conducting the multi-stakeholder co-design workshop. These consultative meetings helped to inform the service users, service providers, and other stakeholders about planning the final multi-stakeholder co-design workshop.

Co-design workshop

A co-design workshop was conducted, and the event was divided into three different sessions (Idea generation, idea development, and prototype sessions). The workshop was conducted in the Nepali language. All the participants were able to understand and speak the Nepali language. The entire workshop was facilitated jointly by the study team members (DRS, DRSi, KK and SN). The agenda for the co-design workshop was shared among the participants two days prior to the workshop. The co-design workshop agenda fits the design thinking process (Supplementary file 1). The ideation phase in the workshop started with a brief presentation of summarised and synthesised evidence related to diabetes patients’ behavioural and practical challenges in managing T2D among service users, health service providers, research team and other stakeholders (Fig. 2). The experiences of patients and healthcare providers in the diagnosis, treatment, and management of T2D in Nepalese clinical settings were also highlighted in the discussion, with input from both healthcare providers and service users. The sharing of synthesised evidence and first-hand experiences of patients with T2D and service providers at the start of the session laid the groundwork for idea generation on the effective management of T2D.

Participants’ engagement in the co-design workshop

Ideation phase

Idea generation stage

The workshop was moved to the idea generation stage after setting the context. The larger group was divided into three sub-groups to make the groups more comfortable for intensive discussions and cultivating ideas. Each sub-group consisted of mixed participants of members representing service users, service providers, and other stakeholders. The facilitator helped encourage the group participants to brainstorm and discuss their ideas in the smaller group. The group representative shared initial ideas with the larger group in this stage, and the other two groups were allowed to comment on them. The exact process was repeated for another two sub-groups.

Idea development stage

The idea development stage in the co-design workshop started with prioritising the contents to develop solutions. The sub-groups worked in their respective groups and shared their prioritisation list among large groups, similar to what was performed in the idea generation stage. The idea generation stage settled with the agreements in the final contents for developing the model and manual for a dietitian-led approach to managing T2D. Further, all three sub-groups were asked to present their initial model for the dietitian-led approach to managing T2D, and all group members jointly provided feedback. The representative from each group presented their recommendations, changes, and suggestions before their final agreement on the pathway of T2D management and the manual for the dietitian-led approach to the management of T2D.

Prototype stage

The final jointly developed model and manual for the dietitian-led approach to managing T2D were presented in the larger group for the prototype. After two rounds of critical group feedback, the final product was refined and revised. Most of the participants in the co-design workshop agreed upon the final model and manual.

Post-workshop validation

One week after the co-design workshop, the final contents were shared with different relevant subject experts to validate the product of the co-designed workshop and provide their critical feedback to finalise the model and manual for a dietitian-led approach to the management of T2D. They were provided two weeks to submit their feedback. The pathway of care and manual were finalised after receiving the experts’ feedback. Further, we conducted face-to-face qualitative interviews with nine diverse participants (service users, service providers, and other relevant experts and stakeholders) to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of the co-designed diabetic education manual. The participants for the interviews comprised a mix of those who had participated in and those who had not participated in the co-design workshop. The semi-structured interview guide was used to conduct the interviews. The selection of additional participants for the interviews was stopped once data saturation was achieved. Interviews were conducted by DRS and SN, and all the interviews were recorded in a voice recorder. Interviews data were transcribed and translated into English, and data analysis was performed manually using thematic analysis. Any personal identifier of participants recorded was not used to maintain the anonymity of the participants.

Data analysis

The data analysis employed the content analysis technique. The recorded data were transcribed and translated into the English language for analysis. Study team members (DRSi, SN, and DRSu) coded the data. Approximately 10% of the transcribed data was verified by research team members (KK and NKC) to ensure the accuracy of content and language. The data were analysed to generate common themes. The coded data were further combined and analysed, and the summarised results were presented under broader thematic headings.

Ethical consideration

The formal ethical approval of the study was obtained from the ethical review board of the Nepal Health Research Council (Reference: 206) and the Institutional Review Committee of Institute of Medicine, Tribhuvan University (Reference: 471(6 − 11)2 2078/2079). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the study.

First session: discussion on issues in the management of diabetes

In the first session, the research team initiated the discussion by presenting a brief overview of the literature on T2D management issues in the Nepalese context to facilitate a detailed discussion. Likewise, inadequate counselling, a lack of guidelines and protocols for treatment and counselling, and dietary guidelines for patients with T2D were some of the challenges on the provider’s side in managing T2D. Furthermore, there was an in-depth discussion on practical healthcare system issues and other challenges in the management of T2D. Second session: discuss the solutions for the dietary approach in diabetes management

In the second session, two key components were discussed and finalised: first, the pathways for a dietary approach to managing T2D were extensively discussed and drafted. Secondly, the contents of the nutrition education manual for patients with T2D were discussed and agreed upon.

Pathway for a dietitian-led dietary approach for the management of T2D

This pathway illustrates the channel for treating and managing T2D through a dietitian-led dietary approach (Fig. 3). The first step in Fig. 3 shows that individuals in the community at risk of T2D or patients with T2D seek health services at their preferred health facility. In the second step, individuals visit the health facility for an initial diagnosis and confirmation of T2D, either through a community screening program or self-referral. At the same time, the patient, who had previously been diagnosed with T2D, also sought similar health services at their preferred health facilities. Nepal’s healthcare system has primary, secondary, and tertiary health facilities. The potential patient is expected to visit a general physician (GP)/consultant for a check-up at any of these health facilities. In the third step, the patient will be referred for blood tests to confirm T2D status. Again, the patient is expected to visit the GP for further consultation after receiving the blood report. A physician/consultant is supposed to refer patients with T2D to a dietitian for appropriate nutrition counselling and a diet plan after completing the consultation at their clinic. In the fourth step, the trained dietitian will then provide essential nutrition counselling to the patient and their caregiver following the nutrition education manual. The dietitian will also provide the patient with a copy of the nutrition education manual and a scheduled individualised diet plan sheet in their local language. In the fifth step, the patient visits the pharmacy to get prescribed medications. Finally, the patient will return home after receiving specialised nutrition counselling and a diet plan, plus prescribed medications. The patient is expected to adhere to the clinician’s prescriptions and the dietitian’s menu plan. Furthermore, the patient is expected to visit their consultant regularly, every three months, and undergo a blood test to check if their fasting blood glucose and HbA1c levels have decreased. This concept was designed based on a patient-centred care approach, which means advocating the needs, preferences, values, and active involvement of patients at the centre, with respect, compassion, and coordinated care and treatment [26].

Summary of the nutrition education manual contents

The key emphasis throughout the workshop discussion was to use simple language and minimise the use of technical terms in the nutrition education manual whenever possible. Metaphors in diagrammatic form were used to illustrate the pathway of insulin-regulating blood sugar levels in the human body, allowing patients without prior knowledge of T2D to easily understand the educational content (Supplementary file 1). Despite the wider consensus on including the comprehensive aspects of the educational content for managing T2D, the nutritional components were discussed more, and it was agreed to present the nutrition-related information relating to local availability of food choices and patients’ socio-cultural behaviour. The specific suggestions provided for the inclusion of nutrition aspects were focused on the importance of a healthy diet for people with T2D, dietary recommendations for people with diabetes, selection/choices of foods for controlling and managing diabetes, and key information to be considered while selecting quality fat. Further, the discussion also highlighted the local contextual issues related to food choices which need to be included in the manual, such as additional food items recommended while hungry, healthy food selection and cooking techniques, choices of foods while attending social events by diabetic patients and lifestyle management. The food choices, considering the local socio-cultural and behavioural context, have been included in the table format as a food exchange list for patients with T2D (Supplementary file 1).

The simple colour-coded chart was developed to illustrate the different types of diets for patients with T2D: foods that can be normally consumed (green colour), foods that can be occasionally consumed in small amounts (yellow colour), and foods that should be prohibited (red colour) (Supplementary file 1). Similarly, the concept of “My Plate” has been placed at the end of the manual (Supplementary file 1). The picture of “My Plate” is expected to help the patient relate their individualised diet plan with the food items/categories acceptable to consume at different time intervals in their daily food intake.

Validation of the nutrition education manual contents

Acceptability

The results of the qualitative interviews showed that most participants, including service users (patients with T2D), dietitians, physicians, and public health experts, highly acknowledged the contents and design of the manual. They highlighted that the manual could be valued not only by patients with T2D but also by their family members and carers, who should also know the importance of a dietary approach to managing T2D. One of the patients with T2D reflected on the overall contents and colour-coded presentation of the manual contents:

“…this booklet (manual) has adequate information not only for patients but also for their family members…I found adequate information regarding diabetes mellitus and its types, as well as treatment options and tips for a healthy lifestyle and consuming a quality diet. There are pictures and information alongside, so I can remember many things from it… there are different colours in the food exchange chart, which is very informative and practical to understand… Most importantly, there is a diet plan at the end, which is very important for the management of T2D.” (Patient with T2D).

Similarly, the food exchange list presented, considering the Nepalese socio-cultural and behavioural, as well as the availability of food items, was widely accepted by both experts and patients. One of the dietitians commented on the contents of the manual, referring to the food exchange list as:

“I appreciate the local food exchange list because there are taboos regarding food for diabetic patients in our society, and the patients even sometimes fall into depression as they have the same monotonous diet…here the local food exchange list can help a lot to have changes in the choices of food to be consumed. For example, many patients avoid fruits or say they eat the same fruit every day, and they come to us and ask if they could change the fruit they eat. We can show them this manual and teach them what and how much fruit to consume. In addition to this, ‘My plate’ is an effective infographic for patients as they can easily remember it.” (Dietitian).

Another dietitian spoke on the issue of manual content and its relevancy in counselling patients with T2D:

“…the contents in the manual are enough to step up the counselling context for patients with T2D… this manual is kind of the basic guidelines for dietitians as well as patients, which is not available to date…” (Dietitian).

Likewise, physicians and endocrinologist also expressed their acceptance towards the contents and their importance in the behaviour change of patients with T2D. One of the highly experienced consultant physicians remarked.

“…the diabetic education manual would be a great initiative to manage diabetes s… the contents in the manual are highly relevant and practical… what a doctor or Dietitian might forget to explain during the patient’s visits can be easily referred to in this manual so it will help like a checklist for service provider and as well as patients can read it themselves… Not only patients but also other family members who have no prior knowledge of T2D can understand this manual. The manual also has medical nutrition therapy, which is helpful for dietitians, physicians, patients, students, and laypersons.” (Consultant Physician).

Another service provider also confirmed the validity of the contents in the manual and mentioned that:

“…patients can grasp the information through the infographics kept in the manual…Colour codes are genuinely used here to mark important topics, as well as the food exchange charts and other ‘dos and don’ts’ sections. The manual is both handy and portable in terms of size. I also like the paper and colour quality here…. Additionally, it is equally important to assess how effectively the patient is receiving the shared information, so the dietitian can encourage the patient to return for follow-up appointments and reinforce the information.” (General Physician).

In addition, all participants, including the patients with T2D, public health experts, nurses, GPs, and consultant physicians, acknowledged the manual’s contents. One expert who has long experience working in the design, development, and implementation of behavioural change programs commented on the manual.

“The manual is well-designed, and the pictures are relevant in places and easily understood by all levels of readers. Maybe the content, like the introduction, is a bit lengthy, but they are, after all, important to the readers, so that’s fine, too…there are nutrition facts like what and how much to eat, acceptable diets, and these can help the patients to stay healthy so these can be acceptable among patients…can be a practical approach to behaviour change among patient with T2D if implemented effectively…”(Public Health Expert).

Feasibility

The results from the interviews showed that the participants’ confidence in understanding the contents of the manual, the use of local language, and the use of colour-coded pictures to illustrate food choices and portions for patients with T2D were repeatedly reflected as favourable factors for the effective implementation of the manual. It was perceived that the contents in the manual were more likely to be accepted among users due to their simplicity and practicality. The participants also considered that the manual could bridge the gap between the clinical and dietary approaches to managing diabetes and support the integrated management of T2D. Further, the manual could guide patients with T2D in modifying their diet and health-related behaviours practically.

One of the highly experienced Consultant Physicians commented on the practicality of the manual:

“This manual is essential for the integrated management of diabetes. I view this manual development and dietitian counselling as an essential step in the behaviour change of patients with T2D… They will accept the information in the manual and try to follow accordingly to change or upgrade their health status, but continuity is what takes more sacrifice and dedication. They have to continue the treatment measures provided in the manual; they have to adhere to lifestyle modification strategies and care for their diet..concept of ‘My Plate’ at the end, I guess the patient would like it, and also, it will be very easy for us and the dietitian to explain how a food plate should look like and can be followed…” (Consultant Physician).

Likewise, the dietitians believed that the manual could be a practical guideline for counselling patients with T2D. They also believe that this document could save them time in educating patients with diabetes during counselling on dietary approaches to managing T2D. This will further help to improve the patient’s outcomes sustainably. Views of the dietitians reflected on the feasibility aspects of the manual:

“…this manual can help us with counselling guidelines, and as a checklist so we don’t forget what should be included while counselling the patient with T2D….We often forget to mention hypoglycaemia. Even doctors forget it while counselling, which has even led to the death of patients… This will obviously help in managing hypoglycemia….The colourfulness of ‘My plate’ is very effective, which could be effective for even illiterate patients during counselling…” (Dietitian).

“The Nepali language used in the manual can be easily understood; however, some medical terms need to be clarified to the patients during counselling…” (Dietitian).

Further, the design and contextual contents of the manual were believed to be crucial for its adaptation among a wider range of patients with diabetes. One of the patients with T2D expressed his views about the manual:

“There are a lot of things which is of great importance to a patient with diabetes. Even many patients like me who have faced diabetes for a long time learned new information from it…The size of the manual is handy, and the paper quality is also good; it is unlikely to get damaged. Many patients can learn various essential things from this comprehensive and easy-to-understand manual. After going through this manual, now I am able to manage my hypoglycaemia…I will for sure use the manual as it is very informative for me…” (Patient with T2D).

In addition, other stakeholders, including public health experts, provided their positive views regarding the adaptability and implementation aspects of the manual. However, few stakeholders highlighted the time restriction among dietitians due to an inadequate number of experts in the hospital and a high number of patients followed in government hospitals, which could make it difficult to implement the complete contents of the manual due to counselling. Also, it was highlighted that dietitians need to have training before implementing the manual to have adequate information regarding the manual so that they can easily summarise it to the patient in a practical manner within a very short time. There needs to be an allocation of relatively more time for illiterate patients so they can understand the contents explicitly, and regular follow-up should be arranged, especially for those illiterate patients, to adhere to the manual sufficiently. One of the public health experts commented that:

“If the dietitian can provide about 30 minutes during counselling, that would be very effective. However, if only 5–15 minutes is provided, it would be only surface information. The feasibility of practising a diabetic education manual will depend on how much time a dietitian can provide for counselling; there might be a patient flow factor…. patients need to be followed up, and they must be made aware of the improvement in diabetes management that has happened to them due to adherence to the manual. As people have their own ethnic/cultural and religious backgrounds, it may not always be possible to accept the information provided in the manual, but continuous follow-up and awareness that this information can help enhance the usability of the diabetic education manual for patients.” (Public Health Expert).

Another participant was also highlighted by one of the dietitian practitioners, who showed doubt in delivering the complete manual content during the limited time for counselling:

“It’s time-consuming if we go through the topics one by one. It would be difficult to cover all the contents as patients have less time for counselling.” (Dietitian).

Similarly, the colour-coded contents in the manual, related to traffic lights, the concept of “my plate” in relation to the diet plan, and food portions in the manual, were perceived as a convincing way to communicate messages to patients about their dietary management. One of the nursing personnel noted that:

“Often, we listen to the information from health workers but forget it, but with this manual, it will be easy to catch and learn with pictures and traffic light colour codes. I also explained the plate mainly as a dietitian. The current counselling practices related to food choices and portion size are confusing, but this manual clearly presents the food choices and portion size with the example of “my plate” and will make it efficacious for the patient.” (Nurse).

Furthermore, health service providers believed that the manual could enhance the quality of care and effectively improve the delivery of health services among patients with T2D. They also mentioned that patients will understand the healthcare system well and benefit from the integrated health services. This could also improve the effectiveness of counselling services and the demand for qualified dietitians in the hospital, where dietitians often lack the ability to deliver counselling services to patients.

“It is easy for patients with all the information, including an introduction, regular check-ups, causes, dangers, diet menu, food exchange list… This will give broader ideas that are difficult to gain from another authentic source. The manual will facilitate the effective development of health services among patients with diabetes.” (General Physician).

Discussion

This study provided significant contributions to the prevention and management of non-communicable diseases like T2D in Nepal by utilising the participatory methods and co-design approach to facilitate the development of a contextually relevant nutrition education manual tailored to the needs and preferences of patients with T2D. Through the active engagement involving patients, dietitians, clinicians, academia, and relevant stakeholders in the design process, we ensured the diabetic education manual was culturally appropriate, feasible, and acceptable for all target groups. To our knowledge, this research is the first of its kind to develop a framework that employs a co-design approach, incorporating a dietary approaches customised by a dietitian for dietary interventions aimed at managing diabetes in the Nepalese context.

The nutrition education manual was rigorously designed to meet the educational needs of patients with T2D, dietitians, and healthcare providers in managing and treating T2D. Modules covering knowledge about diabetes mellitus, treatment strategies, emergency management, and foot care were developed using simple Nepali language and visual aids to enhance understanding [34]. The inclusion of a diet plan sheet and food exchange table further facilitates the practical implementation of dietary recommendations, considering local food availability and cultural preferences.

In previous research, a co-design approach was also employed that provided practical suggestions for adapting the strategy and the broader community settings to facilitate a conducive environment where consumers are encouraged to make healthier choices, finding it more convenient, socially appealing, and enjoyable [38]. Similarly, this study is in line with the previous study that revealed that while some elements of the manual used in the study with interventions implemented in other settings, such as emphasizing the consumption of locally available foods and promoting traditional cooking methods [29], our manual also incorporates unique features tailored to the specific needs and preferences of Nepalese context. Several other studies have employed similar participatory methodologies for designing interventions in various contexts [39]. Pike and colleagues (2023) utilised a co-design approach to develop a community-based nutrition program, resulting in improved dietary practices and health outcomes among participants [40]. This aligns with our study’s methodology and underscores the effectiveness of participatory approaches in promoting behaviour change and improving health outcomes. However, in this type of participatory co-design work, it is essential to consider the power dynamics when professionals and patients are brought together in the same workshop [32]. We attempted to minimise the impacts of power dynamics in the discussions by informing the patient about this collaborative process prior to the start of the workshop, ensuring that everyone’s voice would be equally valued. Facilitators often encouraged patients to participate in the discussion throughout the session. Initially, patients felt a little hesitant; however, they later felt comfortable participating in the discussions.

In the management and prevention pathway of T2D, dietitians play a crucial role similar to physicians and nurses in both community and clinical settings. At a tertiary care hospital in Nepal, we have integrated dietary intervention alongside pharmacotherapy intervention in the diabetes management pathway. We formulated and finalised two pathways. First, extensive discussions led to the initial draft of pathways for a dietary approach to managing T2D. Secondly, the contents of the nutrition education manual for patients with T2D were discussed among patients, dietitians, physicians, nurses, and academicians, resulting in mutual agreement. This study focuses on the dietary aspects of managing T2D, aiming to evaluate the application of the co-design approach in intervention development. Prior studies have demonstrated that the majority of digital dietary applications employ a combination of persuasive strategies to encourage healthier eating habits among their users [41, 42]. Interestingly, our workshop’s outcomes closely correspond with these findings despite the disparity in intervention methods, while digital interventions were utilised in previous studies, our approach involved face-to-face intervention using a diabetic education manual. Nevertheless, the co-design methodologies employed in both instances exhibited similarities.

For the intervention, we prepared structured materials, as recommended by the dietitian, to improve the blood glucose profile, clinical parameters, and dietary intake among patient with T2D. The structured nutrition education manual consists of four modules: Knowledge about Diabetes, Treatment of Diabetes, Possible Emergency Conditions during Diabetes, and Foot Care in Diabetes. We have developed the content of the Nepalese contextual-based educational manual based on previous studies, which have also utilised similar content for managing T2D in clinical settings [43]. Some studies employed digital interventions [29, 41, 42], while others utilised educational manuals and face-to-face interventions [44].

Implementing a dietary manual for diabetes management in the Nepalese socio-cultural and health system context presents both opportunities and challenges. Our study shows the feasibility of the manual implementation depends on cultural aspects of food consumption, accessibility of recommended foods, and the integration of traditional dietary practices with modern medical advice. These results were supported by previous literature related to sociocultural aspects of food in Nepal [45–47]. Existing evidence also explicitly states that several factors, such as local food availability, economic constraints, and health literacy levels, can influence adherence [16, 45, 47]. Therefore, the support from healthcare providers and the community’s openness to adopting healthy dietary practices for the management of T2D will be crucial. Further, identifying potential implementation barriers through community and stakeholder engagement and education can enhance the effectiveness and sustainability of the manual.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Based on our knowledge, this study is the first of its kind in Nepal to attempt an innovative approach to convince patients (end-users) and other stakeholders to co-design pathways and education manuals for improving T2D health services. Our study brought ideas to life, visualising and developing real-world solutions for T2D management. The engagement and involvement of stakeholders in the research process enhanced the acceptability and feasibility of co-designed outcomes. This novel work fills the evidence gap by producing a contextual nutrition education manual for T2D management, considering the importance of integrating dietary approaches into T2D management in the Nepalese context, which was previously lacking. Additionally, the co-design pathway and manual have been implemented and proven effective in treating T2D at a tertiary care hospital in an urban setting. Relatively, participants involved in the co-design workshop were mostly from tertiary care hospital service providers and urban settings, so we are less confident about the inclusion of views and experiences of health professionals and end-users representing different levels of healthcare settings.

Conclusions

This study has demonstrated the value of employing participatory methods and co-design approaches in developing contextually relevant nutrition education interventions for the management of T2D in Nepal. By actively involving service users, healthcare providers and other stakeholders in the co-design process, we have created resources that are not only evidence-informed but also contextually relevant and acceptable. These tools have the potential to support more patient-centred care, enhance dietary self-management, and strengthen diabetes services in low-resource settings. The pathway model of this study provides a contextual framework for understanding the mechanisms of change underlying the integrated efforts in T2D management. At the same time, the nutrition education manual offers practical guidance for promoting healthy dietary behaviors among patients with T2D. Despite potential implementation challenges, our findings highlight the importance of tailored nutrition education approaches for T2D management that are sensitive to Nepal’s socio-cultural context and healthcare system dynamics.
