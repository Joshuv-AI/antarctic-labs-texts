Task-shifting and system readiness: a narrative review of strategies for pediatric emergency care in low-resource settings
Authors: Mohammed Alsabri, Israa Magdy Ata, Joseph Alhaddad, Eslam Abady, Muhammad Azan Shahid, Aanal Patel, Dalia Atef Abouda, Sohaila Mohamed Mohamed Abdelbar, Victor N. Oboli, Muhammad Waseem, Luis L. Gamboa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn various low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), non-specialist healthcare providers (HCPs), such as general practitioners, nurses, and community health workers (CHWs), often manage acute pediatric emergencies. HCPs in these settings may face challenges due to limited training in emergency care and a lack of ongoing educational opportunities. Additionally, they often operate in clinical environments that lack dedicated emergency departments (EDs), child-specific care protocols, or the necessary pediatric equipment required for managing critically ill children. This is concerning, considering that acute illnesses and injuries are significant contributors to the high rates of preventable childhood deaths in low-and middle-income countries.ObjectiveThis review synthesizes evidence on pediatric emergency care strategies to improve fidelity to evidence-based practice that can be utilized and scaled beyond the conventional ED framework, without relying on Pediatric Emergency Medicine (PEM)-trained personnel, who may be unavailable in these regions. This includes task shifting, where community and non-physician HCPs learn how to provide emergency triage and acute lifesaving interventions for children in preventing morbidity and mortality, and other strategies, framed within the established concept of “pediatric readiness”, which is a systems-based approach encompassing staff competencies, protocols, equipment, and quality improvement. Additionally, this review explores practical examples from international real-world applications of such strategies.MethodsA narrative review of literature from 2000 to 2024 covering task-shifting, protocols, simulation training, and system approaches for pediatric emergency care in low-resource settings.ResultsOur synthesis of the literature suggests that low-dose, low-fidelity simulation-based training can effectively enhance confidence and competence in pediatric resuscitation. By recognizing the roles of community-based and non-specialist providers within a pediatric readiness framework, standardizing relevant protocols, and providing appropriate tools and education, we can substantially improve pediatric emergency care systems on a broader scale.ConclusionThis review offers valuable insights for clinicians and policymakers dedicated to reducing preventable pediatric mortality in low-resource settings.

Introduction

Globally, over 80% of pediatric emergency deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) due to limited critical care capabilities and a significant burden of acute illnesses [1]. In LMICs, children suffer from a disproportionate number of preventable diseases, such as respiratory failure, sepsis, trauma, diarrheal illnesses, and neonatal complications [2]. Preventable pediatric conditions account for over 40% of emergency department (ED) visits in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa [2]. Infections, particularly pneumonia and malaria, continue to impact children, leading to high morbidity and mortality rates associated with these childhood illnesses. Additionally, trauma is responsible for nearly 90% of pediatric injury-related deaths in LMICs [3]. Despite global efforts to reduce child mortality rates, regions such as sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia still experience disproportionately high rates of under-five mortality [2].

EDs in LMICs frequently face significant challenges due to a lack of essential resources for effective pediatric care. Notably, many of these departments lack access to child-sized airway instruments, intravenous (IV) catheters, and pulse oximeters, which are essential for providing optimal care to children [4]. For instance, tertiary hospitals in Mozambique reported that the lack of equipment and delayed recognition of severely ill children contributed to increased hospital mortality rates [5]. Additionally, a survey of 238 hospitals in 60 countries revealed significant inconsistencies in the availability of essential resources for the care of acutely sick children. Key areas of concern include the availability of basic respiratory support, sepsis bundle resources, and dextrose-containing intravenous fluids [6].

Implementing the World Health Organization Emergency Triage Assessment and Treatment (WHO ETAT) system at Kamuzu Central Hospital in Malawi resulted in a reduction in pediatric inpatient mortality from 5% to 4% within 48 h, highlighting the value and effectiveness of basic triage systems [7, 8]. However, most EDs in LMICs lack formal triage systems or trained personnel capable of rapidly assessing, categorizing clinical severity, and stabilizing critically ill children [3].

Task-shifting is a strategy in which trained non-physician providers assume specific roles in emergency care [9]. This is a WHO-accepted strategy as it addresses workforce limitations and expands care coverage [10]. Additionally, simpler protocols for common pediatric emergencies, along with other low-technology tools, can be established to help frontline providers identify and manage care early on [7]. These protocols can be followed even by individuals with minimal formal training in emergencies, thereby eliminating delays in patient care and reducing morbidity and mortality rates [11].

In this narrative review, we aim to synthesize evidence on task-shifting and other key strategies used in pediatric emergency care in LMICs, especially framed within the concept of “pediatric readiness”.

Methods

This research is a narrative review. We used various databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library, to thoroughly search the literature for peer-reviewed research, guidelines, and international health reports on pediatric emergency care in low-resource settings published between 2000 and 2024. Our search strategy included a combination of terms related to pediatric emergency care, such as “Pediatric emergency,” “child,” “low- and middle-income countries,” “LMIC,” “task-shifting,” “nurse-led care,” “Emergency Triage Assessment and Treatment,” “ETAT,” “Integrated Management of Childhood Illness,” “IMCI,” “simulation training,” and “pediatric readiness”. The objective of this narrative review was not a systematic retrieval but to focus on task-shifting strategies, simplified protocols, simulation-based training, and systems-level frameworks such as pediatric readiness. Studies were selected based on relevance, quality, and applicability to LMIC contexts.

The pediatric emergency care gap and the imperative for readiness

Disease burden

Pediatric emergencies continue to outnumber the qualified medical professionals available to provide prompt and effective care in LMICs. Despite significant advancements in child health globally over the past two decades, many LMICs still lack the workforce required for pediatric emergency care [5, 12]. PEM physicians contribute to less than 5% of the ED workforce, with most of them in urban settings [13]. Even in high-resource settings, many EM physicians perform < 1 pediatric critical procedure per year – thus the importance of ongoing skills training through sim and a system designed to treat critically ill children. Therefore, it is important to note that the presence of general emergency medicine physicians, when supported by a pediatric-ready system, is also associated with improved outcomes [14, 15].

In 2024, a study conducted across six African countries found that only 22% of hospitals had written pediatric triage policies. Protocols and policies are a fundamental part of a ready system, essential regardless of workforce size, and their absence results in delays in the treatment of children [5, 12]. The disparity between the availability of skilled healthcare professionals and the incidence of illness significantly contributes to elevated child mortality rates. Research conducted in Bangladesh, Nepal, and Uganda indicates that pediatric emergency fatality rates are considerably higher in hospitals experiencing staffing shortages. Inadequate triage, delays in treatment, and noncompliance with pediatric-specific standards often lead to preventable fatalities. Furthermore, the rapid progression of health issues in children necessitates timely and accurate intervention, a competence that may not be within the expertise of generalists and non-physician healthcare providers (HCPs) [16, 17].

The gap between disease burden and workforce capacity is most evident in neonatal emergencies. Even though neonatal illnesses account for 47% of mortality below the age of 5 years and nearly 2.4 million deaths in the first month, they receive proportionately scant attention in emergency care training curricula [18]. This high disease burden makes neonates a critical, high-impact area for implementing task-shifting protocols to optimize care delivery. Furthermore, ill-equipped and weak infrastructure contributes to the pediatric emergency workforce crisis, especially in sub-Saharan Africa [19].

Urban-rural disparities

The growing disparities between urban and rural healthcare settings are contributing to a significant staff shortage in emergency services. EDs situated in metropolitan areas, particularly those associated with academic teaching hospitals, typically have access to pediatric emergency physicians. Conversely, hospitals located in rural regions (community-based hospitals) often depend on overworked and undercompensated staff, who may lack both the necessary resources and training to effectively manage pediatric emergencies [20, 21]. This situation highlights that the problem is not limited to staffing alone; rather, it reflects a failure in the overall system, which includes policies, infrastructure, and standardized training, all of which must be addressed to improve pediatric emergency care. In Nigeria, recent studies reported a poor level of pediatric care due to ill-equipped, understaffed settings, with nurses and health care workers lacking official training. The situation is worse in rural settings [19].

Root causes of the crisis

A variety of deep-rooted issues contribute to the shortage of pediatric emergency physicians in LMICs. There are very few training programs specifically dedicated to Pediatric Emergency Medicine (PEM). While high-income countries recognize PEM as a formal, board-certified subspecialty, most LMICs do not offer basic postgraduate courses in this field. Moreover, long working hours, low salaries, and a lack of institutional support increase stress levels, driving many doctors to leave the medical profession [19].

The pipeline for pediatric emergency care training is inherently inadequate in most LMICs. Pediatric-specific emergency and trauma care topics are limited in emergency medicine curricula in medical schools. Additionally, practitioners receive one-time training with no long-term reinforcement or monitoring of skills, resulting in a very quick decline of skills. This forms a continuous cycle of providers who are insufficiently trained in handling complicated pediatric cases [22].

A significant number of experienced pediatricians and emergency specialists are relocating abroad in pursuit of improved compensation and more favorable working conditions, resulting in a shortage of medical professionals within national health systems. Concurrently, the emergency services that remain largely cater to adult patients, with only limited modifications for pediatric care. Therefore, they often fail to adequately address the needs of younger patients [23]. This highlights the importance of a pediatric readiness framework, which has been proven to decrease mortality for critically ill children up to 76% [24]. The pediatric readiness framework: a systems approach

The pediatric readiness framework is a systems-based approach to confirm that healthcare settings are equipped to care for critically ill children. This system includes multiple domains, (staff competencies, protocolized care, pediatric-specific equipment and supplies, and administration with continuous quality improvement). These domains aim to provide high-quality care in PEM and reduce morbidity and mortality in low-resource settings [14, 24].

Domain 1: staff competencies and task-shifting

This unofficial role redistribution involving generalist clinicians and nurses is now a standard part of child health plans. Given that the majority of pediatric fatalities in hospitals with limited resources take place within the first 24 h of admission, prompt identification of critically ill children is essential in emergency care [11].

Evidence is increasingly supporting the effectiveness of task-shifting in healthcare. In rural Bangladesh, Community Health Workers (CHWs) who were trained to administer intramuscular antibiotics for neonatal sepsis achieved a 34% reduction in neonatal mortality. This finding is further supported by a Cochrane review, which demonstrates that the community-based delivery of injectable antibiotics effectively reduces neonatal deaths [26, 27].

The application of protocolized care, such as the World Health Organization’s Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) and Emergency Triage Assessment and Treatment (ETAT), is crucial to the effectiveness of task-shifting in emergency management. Simplified guidelines, often presented as flowcharts or color-coded decision aids, help both PEM physicians and non-specialists make quick and appropriate clinical decisions [28].

The implementation of ETAT-based triage has yielded significant improvements in patient outcomes. Notably, a district hospital in Malawi documented a 50% decrease in pediatric inpatient mortality following the adoption of ETAT protocols along with basic emergency training for staff [8]. Similarly, a triage system managed by lay personnel with nurse-led oversight in rural Mozambique achieved a 45% reduction in pediatric mortality within just one year of its establishment [29]. These examples underscore the critical role of nurse-led triage in mitigating treatment delays for children who are critically ill. Furthermore, in the absence of on-site physicians, enabling nurses and trained auxiliaries to perform triage and initiate basic resuscitation procedures can greatly enhance early management and lead to improved patient outcomes [28]. The formal adoption of task-shifting in emergency care signifies a notable strategic advancement, building on a practice that has been effectively utilized in primary care for decades within community health initiatives. The successful and efficient scaling of these task-shifting roles has been enhanced by the incorporation of supervision and ongoing refresher training [28].

Task-shifting to nurses and medical officers in emergency triage and trauma care in Ghana, Kenya, and Uganda resulted in increased provider confidence and improved clinical outcomes [28, 32]. Under structured protocols, non-physician HCPs have demonstrated the capability to manage even neurologic emergencies, such as pediatric seizures [9]. In Kenya and Uganda, the delegation of follow-up care and treatment responsibilities from physicians to trained clinical officers and nurses has resulted in reduced seizure frequency and mortality rates, as indicated by a review of decentralized epilepsy care [9]. In these initiatives, nurses adhered to established protocols to monitor seizure activity and administered anticonvulsants when required, illustrating how task-shifting can be effectively implemented to address complex medical conditions [9].

For example, the WHO shock management protocols outline a clear process: first, evaluate circulation, breathing, and airway. Then, based on clinical signs such as cold extremities or a weak pulse, administer IV fluids in specific boluses [28].

Research indicates that children presenting with critical, life-threatening conditions experience higher survival rates when nurses adhere to established emergency care protocols [1, 5, 8]. These protocols enable nurses to administer benzodiazepines for seizures, allow clinical officers to initiate antibiotics for suspected sepsis, and permit nurses to start antimalarial treatment for feverish illnesses without awaiting a physician’s approval [9, 28]. Given that children receive crucial care during the critical “golden hour” of illness, timely interventions at outlying health facilities have consistently demonstrated improved outcomes [1].

Uganda serves as a distinguished exemplar of task-shifting within the realm of emergency care through the implementation of standardized, color-coded shock protocols administered by non-physician HCPs. In rural hospitals, where the presence of physicians is frequently scarce, nurses and clinical officers have been meticulously trained to employ emergency algorithms similar to the ETAT framework [5, 28].

In order to further cultivate emergency care competencies, Uganda has introduced a novel peer-led simulation training model. This initiative enables the rapid dissemination of essential skills without the reliance on external trainers. A select cohort of healthcare professionals underwent advanced training and subsequently imparted their knowledge to peers in remote regions [28]. The training program placed a significant emphasis on the pragmatic management of pediatric trauma, specifically addressing critical conditions such as hemorrhagic shock and airway obstruction. As a consequence, participants demonstrated marked improvement in their proficiency regarding the treatment of intricate injuries, including burns, fractures, and cranial trauma [28]. This model’s effectiveness is further demonstrated by its success in other low-resource areas, such as certain regions of India [33].

Ongoing institutional investment is necessary to maintain the impact of these programs. To maintain high-quality care and provider confidence, ongoing mentorship, performance evaluation, and refresher training courses are essential [9]. Uganda’s model shows that task-shifting in pediatric emergency care is both feasible and effective. For task-shifting to be effective, simulation training and protocol development are synergistic and equally important; protocols provide the “what,” and simulation provides the “how.” Life-saving care can be provided even in the most resource-constrained settings by providing HCPs with clear, actionable protocols [33].

Domain 2: protocolized care and decision support

Emergency care in pediatrics is a critical aspect of global health. However, many low-resource settings still lack formal EDs. In these settings, HCPs rely on non-technological decision support methods, including clinical protocols, training programs, triage systems, checklists, communication frameworks, trauma guidelines, and evidence-based strategies for managing critically ill or injured children [34, 35]. These supportive components align directly with the administrative and quality improvement domains of pediatric readiness. To improve patient outcomes, HCPs must grasp key principles surrounding clinical decision-making without technological infrastructure, community-based training, and straightforward evidence-based protocols [25, 36].

Although there are limitations and challenges, decision support without technology remains a crucial component of pediatric emergency care in low-resource settings. Several strategies can help address these challenges by utilizing traditional approaches that effectively support decision-making. These approaches include leveraging clinical experience, utilizing simple evidence-based checklists, engaging in peer consultations, implementing community-based care, adopting global strategies, and following structured symptom-based algorithms [37, 38]. Formal quality improvement tools include the National Pediatric Readiness Quality Initiative (NPRQI), a low-cost symptom-based QI tool for low-resource settings [39].

In many low-resource settings, specialist care may not be readily available. Therefore, HCPs can benefit from consulting experienced colleagues for clinical guidance on medical decisions. This approach is valuable, especially through phone communications or social media networks, as they play a significant role in early crisis intervention and emergency situations [37, 38]. Visual aids are also very valuable in inpatient management. For instance, in Uganda, pictorial triage posters helped CHWs quickly identify and classify pediatric patients who required urgent care. Additionally, color-coded drug dosing charts and resuscitation carts/bags helped reduce systemic errors, particularly for weight-based pediatric medications. These tools are easy to create and cost-effective, making them easily accessible to health programs [30, 40]. Examples of visual decision aids for common conditions, including dehydration, sepsis management, and airway management, are provided in Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4.

Global approaches provide frameworks for healthcare and have been shown to improve outcomes. These approaches include the IMCI, ETAT, and Basic Emergency Care (BEC) [30, 31, 41].

The IMCI was developed by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund (UNICEF). It is designed as a decision tree and utilizes simple, syndrome-based algorithms for common pediatric diseases, such as diarrhea, malaria, pneumonia, fever, dehydration, anemia, measles, and nutritional disorders [30, 31, 40]. It is structured to guide HCPs in first-line interventions with limited technical diagnostic techniques. This approach aims to decrease mortality and morbidity in children under 5 years old. It is widely used in low-resource settings, as it helps non-physician HCPs (e.g., CHWs and nurses) assess disease severity and determine outpatient management versus referral in a timely manner. The success of IMCI relies on its simplicity and adaptability in the field. Research studies have shown that the implementation of IMCI decreased mortality in children by 15–20% in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa [30, 31, 40]. For example, India’s IMCI adaptation improved management of diarrhea and pneumonia in rural facilities [30, 31, 40].

The ETAT is a structured method developed by the WHO to enable HCPs to quickly assess disease severity in pediatric patients and manage their care appropriately. It identifies life-threatening conditions (e.g., shock, respiratory distress, hypoglycemia, seizures, severe dehydration, and airway compromise) and pairs triage with immediate, lifesaving approaches, such as fluid resuscitation, oxygen, antibiotic administration, and airway management. It involves the use of wall charts and simple job aids. It uses color-coded triage categories based on clinical signs: non-urgent (green), priority (yellow), and emergency (red) [30]. It is widely used in low-resource settings. The WHO ETAT and Pediatric South African Triage Scale improved pediatric inpatient mortality and triage efficiencies in multiple LMICs [29]. For example, in Malawi, clinics have used ETAT-style triage, where initial nurse screening flags critical cases, and the use of simple visual tools, such as wall posters and charts, helps standardize triage decisions. Its success has decreased inpatient mortality from 10 to 18% per week before triage was introduced in the department to 6–8% afterwards [35]. The importance of these visual tools lies in their ability to enable rapid cues for decision-making and reduce cognitive load during emergencies.

The BEC is an approach developed by the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) and WHO to assist HCPs in making decisions without the use of technology in low-resource settings. It focuses on the immediate triage, stabilization, and treatment of life-threatening conditions in pediatric patients [41].

One of the most effectively adopted strategies is the use of research-based, standardized clinical protocols/guidelines. These protocols can provide a framework for decision-making, provided that care is consistent and evidence-based. These protocols can be developed locally or adapted from international guidelines [31, 42].

Domain 3: low-tech, high-impact equipment and supplies

Various cost-effective and low-technology solutions are available for managing pediatric airways. They include manual suction devices that can efficiently clear airways, as well as gravity-fed intravenous (IV) systems that facilitate rapid fluid resuscitation for cases of shock or dehydration, thereby eliminating the need for expensive infusion pumps [43, 44]. Furthermore, Color-coded resuscitation carts or bags can be used to quickly identify and easily use pediatric-specific equipment during emergencies [14].

To manage respiratory distress in newborns, a modified bubble Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) system was successfully developed in Sierra Leone using locally accessible materials in a pilot project in the Special Care Baby Unit (SCBU), which is equivalent to a level II neonatal intensive care unit. As a result, infant survival rates rose from 62.5% to 88% and airway compromise was successfully managed [33].

Domain 4: administration, coordination, and continuous quality improvement

Outdated and ineffective hospital systems jeopardize patient safety and delay care. A lack of integrated care systems (equipment, protocols, and patient safety) and specialized emergency care providers can lead to delays in proper evaluation and admissions [45–47]. Although task shifting is a key strategy to address this gap, it is not successful alone without supportive, low-resource interventions such as clinical decision-support algorithms and simulation-based training. Therefore, the pediatric readiness context recommends the appointment of a Pediatric Emergency Care Coordinator (PECC). PECC is a position filled by a physician or non-physician. They are responsible for coordinating strategies, ensuring staff competencies, and maintaining pediatric-specific protocols [14, 24].

Innovative solutions have emerged to address these challenges. These include short-course training modules, telemedicine, and mobile mentorship [48, 49]. Together, they offer a strategic use of Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) to strengthen frontline healthcare workers’ clinical capacity and reshape healthcare delivery. For example, mobile units can be valuable tools for addressing gaps in emergency care admissions in areas with insufficient healthcare infrastructures, ensuring timely interventions for pediatric emergencies [48, 49].

Technology-enabled support

Telemedicine has become a vital tool in pediatric emergency care, particularly in low-resource settings where access to specialized medical services is limited [49]. It helps reduce the burden on emergency transport systems, workforce shortages, and geographic isolation, while also ensuring the secure communication of patient information by enabling frontline clinicians to consult remotely with specialists, thereby improving the quality of care provided to children. However, it is critical to note that telemedicine serves as a support tool and does not replace the ability of onsite providers to respond and stabilize a patient immediately [50, 51].

The Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) Tele-Expertise system highlights the significant impact of telemedicine, reducing isolation experienced by HCPs working in low-resource settings, such as in natural disasters or pediatric emergency cases [50, 51]. The integration of telemedicine not only enhances clinical outcomes but also addresses systemic healthcare inequities by facilitating optimal care pathways for children in rural or conflict-affected areas, thereby contributing to long-term improvements in pediatric health systems globally [50, 52].

In addition to telemedicine, short-course training modules utilizing active learning, hands-on simulations, and team-based exercises could also be adopted in LMICs. Short-course modules such as BEC target high-mortality conditions like respiratory failure, shock, sepsis, and trauma [41]. Remote supervision and mobile mentorships (e.g., WhatsApp groups) are a proposed strategy with emerging evidence; however, they raise important concerns regarding patient confidentiality. These remote methods are intended to maintain momentum post-training, encourage continued learning, address real-time clinical challenges, foster peer learning, and sustain engagement over weeks and months; however, further evidence is required to assess their effectiveness [53].

Targeted training models

Traditional training programs (e.g., AHA courses) are time-consuming and very expensive for low-income healthcare facilities and professionals [54]. Short-course training modules, supported by mobile mentorship, have been offered as a solution to strengthen the clinical capacity of frontline healthcare workers [28, 54]. Models like the Pediatric Emergency Medicine (PEM)-lite modules in Nigeria and Nepal clarified how simulation-based education systems and technology-enabled support can transform healthcare delivery [28]. In Nigeria, HCPs reported significant improvement in triage skills, danger sign recognition, and the care of critically ill children. Simulation exercises and mobile mentorship helped bridge gaps between nurses, doctors, and ancillary staff [54, 55]. In Nepal, PEM-lite modules led to noticeable improvements in emergency care protocols, particularly in managing pediatric airway emergencies and performing effective cardiopulmonary resuscitation [54].

In Uganda, a qualitative needs assessment revealed significant disparities in pediatric emergency care capabilities, highlighting the inadequacy of existing training programs [46]. Continuous educational programs, with monthly refresher courses, helped bolster healthcare providers’ self-efficacy and practical skills in managing neonatal and pediatric emergencies [46].

In Tanzania, a structured “Emergency Care” short course adopted a “train-the-trainer” model, wherein local clinicians were equipped to deliver the curriculum. This approach facilitated widespread implementation, enabling nurses at district hospitals to effectively acquire essential emergency skills, such as triage, airway management, and shock resuscitation [55].

In Ghana, emergency nurse training initiatives have not only resulted in improved patient outcomes but have also enhanced nurse retention and job satisfaction. The incorporation of modules that focus on teamwork, leadership, and communication has further empowered nurses to perform effectively in high-pressure environments [56].

In Thailand, a Pediatric Emergency Medicine course designed for multidisciplinary teams has resulted in a greater than 50% increase in theoretical knowledge and an over 20% improvement in simulation-based team performance. Participants have expressed high levels of satisfaction with the program’s practical relevance and hands-on nature, with all indicating that they would recommend the training to colleagues [56].

These examples underscore the significant impact of targeted, technology-supported, and team-based training programs in advancing pediatric emergency care in resource-constrained settings.

System-level coordination and policy

The effective implementation of these solutions requires a coordinated, system-level strategy. To address the critical shortage of pediatric emergency personnel, it is essential for LMICs to implement both prompt interventions and meticulously formulated long-term strategies that are tailored to the unique needs of their respective communities [12]. First, governments should prioritize the development of scalable training courses that begin with brief sessions on resuscitation, trauma care, and triage. These sessions are essential for frontline providers, including nurses, CHWs, and ambulance crews, enabling them to act more effectively during emergencies [57]. Additionally, implementing task-shifting empowers these providers with clearer authority and the necessary tools to manage urgent care until a doctor arrives [58].

Secondly, pediatric emergency medicine requires a formal position within each country’s training framework through dedicated PEM fellowships, comprehensive national guidelines, and specialized educational modules focused on child and adolescent health [3]. Furthermore, effective retention strategies should encompass competitive compensation for rural positions, access to appropriate medical equipment, and opportunities for professional development through international study or leadership in local research initiatives. These efforts should be supported by academic institutions and global health organizations [16].

In addition, the integration of digital tools such as teleconsultations, concise mobile training sessions, and virtual learning platforms can provide flexible solutions that enable medical staff to enhance critical thinking and high-stakes clinical skills under the guidance of experienced instructors. This approach can facilitate smoother learning experiences across diverse educational environments [59].

Future efforts should focus on strengthening referral systems, providing more training programs, and overcoming geographical barriers by establishing more health facilities and implementing mentoring programs, such as regular in-person visits by senior clinicians to rural facilities for hands-on training, supportive supervision, and to strengthen clinical decision-making skills. Additionally, we recommend that national and state health organizations develop more standardized protocols and guidelines to avoid outcome variability in care that depends on the healthcare provider’s judgment [41].

Furthermore, we suggest implementing more training and task-shifting approaches. In Ethiopia, traditional birth attendants (TBAs) were trained to assess life-threatening signs based on behavioral cues and referred newborns to the appropriate health centers to receive neonatal care immediately [60]. Additionally, we recommend establishing more programs for teaching mothers, as mother-led neonatal resuscitation programs have been shown to improve survival rates in some countries. For instance, “mentor mother” groups in Nigeria foster learning through discussions of real, challenging pediatric cases, which involve assessing emergency signs and referral methods, especially related to infections such as HIV [61]. Moreover, repeated field validation of such algorithms strengthens confidence in non-technology-based care.

Ethical, legal, and safety considerations for providing global pediatric emergency care without eds in low-resource settings

Providing Pediatric Emergency Care (PEC) in low-resource settings without pediatric emergency physicians poses ethical and legal challenges [62–64]. Consequently, this section expands on ethical justifications, accountability mechanisms, and the balance between quality and trade-offs in offering PEC in global low-resource environments.

Ethical considerations in the absence of emergency departments

Justice, beneficence, non-maleficence, and respect for autonomy are not only key ethical principles when legitimizing PEC without EDs through task-shifting in LMICs, but also compel it within a pediatric-ready system in LMICs. The WHO’s ETAT guidelines (2019) support equitable care by allowing trained general medical personnel to stabilize critically ill children. This upholds justice and necessity, ensuring children are not denied care due to the lack of subspecialty specialists [65].

This ethical stance is supported by structured pediatric emergency training for general physicians and medical staff in LMICs, which lowers inpatient pediatric death rates [35]. The moral is that task-shifted care, though less detailed than specialist-led models, is better than doing nothing. Similarly, guideline-driven care by general providers, despite some variation, is a morally practical way to work within system limits [66].

This ethical case is further strengthened by highlighting how Short-term PEM training in LMICs, focused training programs extending from days to weeks that can be repeated regularly to prevent skill decay, increases the confidence and skills of general providers to provide PEC [67]. This emphasizes important ethical principles, such as autonomy and respect for local providers, highlighting the community’s right to receive care from empowered and well-prepared medical personnel.

It is crucial to highlight that delegating tasks is ethically appropriate only when accompanied by proper supervision, training, and system support—the very components of the Pediatric Readiness framework [64]. Following this approach helps protect against potential harm, adhering to the principle of non-maleficence. A comprehensive strategy to drive these efforts and achieve a fully pediatric-ready system in LMICs can be summarized by the following eight-step plan (Box 2). Developing adaptable ethical frameworks that focus on “quality care” that is achievable and beneficial for the population is more suitable for LMICs, instead of relying on idealized models that demand extensive resources [62, 63].

Finally, simulation-based training offers a more ethical approach to preparing general providers in LMICs by reducing harm and boosting confidence [28]. These insights support the delivery of robust PEC without specialists, highlighting its ethical importance, adaptability, and alignment with global child health rights.

Accountability and supervision of PEC in the absence of eds

In LMICs lacking EDs, alternative methods are essential for accountability. Using well-designed algorithms and escalation protocols ensures safety and consistent care [64]. Molyneux et al. (2006) support this idea, showing that outcome improvements are possible even without emergency care specialists or high-end resources, provided that specific decision-making systems and follow-up techniques are established [35].

Inconsistent supervision can hinder the implementation of guidelines, especially in resource-limited hospitals. To improve this, focusing on system-wide accountability through audit feedback, local clinical champions, and institutional support is encouraged [66]. Crucially, the appointment of a PECC or “Pediatric Champion” within each facility is vital for driving and sustaining these quality improvement initiatives [39].

Short-term training programs are most effective when supplemented with ongoing mentorship and peer support networks, known as quality improvement collaboratives, which leverage established frameworks such as the NPRQI to drive measurable improvements across multiple facilities [39]. This approach helps sustain quality standards and ensures participants stay guided and connected throughout [67]. Additionally, the WHO’s Task Shifting Guidelines (2008) encourage the establishment of supervision measures, such as remote or periodic check-ins [64]. This ensures high-quality PEC by general providers and medical personnel despite limited resources.

Distributing care can notably reduce accountability; however, supervision and telehealth help mitigate this issue, particularly in LMICs [62]. Supervision models such as simulation evaluations, structured debriefings, and real-time decision-making offer tailored methods to improve general providers’ understanding and skills. These methods create a supportive environment where individuals can learn, thrive, and enhance their skills in PEC [28, 63]. Therefore, accountability and quality depend on following protocols, checklists, and team escalation models, rather than continuous supervision by PEM specialists. This reinforces the importance of system collaboration in ensuring safe care.

Quality vs. access trade-offs

A significant challenge in low-resource pediatric emergency settings is achieving a balance between enhancing access to standardized healthcare and maintaining high-quality care. The WHO ETAT guidelines emphasize that implementing structured training and clear protocols is essential for reducing risks and upholding safety standards [65]. Importantly, while improving access to resources can facilitate healthcare delivery, it does not always result in better patient outcomes [35]. Studies have shown that ETAT-trained staff achieve lower mortality rates, even in environments lacking specialists or advanced resources. Nevertheless, inconsistent application of guidelines can adversely impact the quality of care, highlighting the critical need for robust institutional support [66].

While short-term training can improve access to PEC, it yields only minor quality gains. This highlights the importance of ongoing provider education and system investment for sustained progress [67].

Complex interventions are not feasible in LMICs. Therefore, prioritizing high-impact, low-resource measures is both ethically and clinically sensible [62]. Likewise, redefining standards for emergency care to better suit different clinical settings and contexts is highly recommended. This approach implies that aiming for the “best possible care” is more realistic when resources are constrained [63]. Specific quality measures, such as those within the NPRQI, provide excellent examples of high-impact measures that are achievable with low resource demands, such as pain assessment or oxygen saturation monitoring [39].

Simulation-based training serves as an invaluable approach to bridging the gap between access to care and the quality of services provided [28]. This type of training prepares HCPs to respond effectively while working with any available resources. It is crucial to proactively manage the balance between access and quality, rather than passively accepting limitations in care.

Conclusion

In LMICs, pediatric emergency care faces a significant workforce crisis, characterized by a shortage of PEM-trained specialists, critical resource limitations, and a fundamental lack of pediatric-ready systems. With more than 80% of pediatric emergency deaths occurring in LMICs, it is both impractical and unethical to rely solely on the availability of PEM specialists. This review has challenged the need to create practical solutions to address this gap by utilizing task-shifting, simplified protocols, and scalable, low-technology tools, all integrated within the pediatric readiness framework.

However, these strategies achieve their greatest impact when integrated within a systematic approach like the components of a pediatric-ready system. Task shifting has demonstrated a substantial impact on healthcare delivery, particularly in resource-limited settings. Evidence suggests that empowering a smaller number of trained providers to triage patients, identify those who are critically ill, manage common pediatric emergency cases, and administer time-sensitive treatments can lead to significant reductions in morbidity and mortality, thereby enhancing clinical outcomes. Additionally, the use of low-technology alternative devices, such as manual suction devices, gravity-fed intravenous systems, and paper-based triage scales, can further enable these providers without necessitating complex infrastructure.

Short-duration simulation-based training and mobile mentorship models offer a rapid and effective means of preparing HCPs while ensuring they retain their competencies. These strategies enable the bridging of historically significant knowledge and skill gaps that have impeded the delivery of quality emergency care. Furthermore, the innovations under evaluation facilitate the decentralization of pediatric emergency care, extending vital services beyond tertiary centers into rural and resource-limited regions.

It is crucial to recognize the ethical and safety considerations associated with task shifting as we expand the roles of healthcare providers. Ensuring adequate supervision and implementing quality assurance measures, along with context-appropriate clinical guidelines, are essential to maintaining high standards of care while enhancing access. This reinforces the importance of system collaboration in ensuring safe care, and we recommend leveraging established pediatric readiness metrics like the NPRQI to drive and measure these systemic improvements.

Global health systems should recognize and expand task-shifting models for emergency provider roles. By leveraging streamlined decision-support tools and providing targeted training, we can establish an effective approach to reducing preventable pediatric deaths in low-resource settings. An exclusive reliance on specialist-led models can hinder timely responses to pediatric emergencies. Therefore, it is essential to develop clear roles for trained paraprofessionals, alongside valuable tools and established follow-up clinical management protocols. This approach not only has the potential to save children’s lives today but also contributes to building more resilient health systems for the future.
