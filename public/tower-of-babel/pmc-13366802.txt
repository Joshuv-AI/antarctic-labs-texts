Sustainable financing in Iran’s health system: a policy analysis of agenda-setting through Kingdon’s multiple streams framework
Authors: Anahita Behzadi, Mohsen Barooni, Amirhossein Fatahpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectiveSustainable financing is essential for Iran's health system to achieve universal health coverage and improve healthcare quality. It involves gathering and managing financial resources for health services, aiming to enhance access to quality care while keeping costs manageable. These financing needs to address current health system demands without burdening the government and public, while also boosting healthcare quality. The study explores how to prioritize sustainable financing using the Kingdon multiple streams model.Materials and methodsThis qualitative study looks at how a sustainable financing program is set up in Iran's health system using Kingdon's multiple streams model. It identifies three main areas: issues (problem stream), solutions (policy stream), and political will (political stream). Data were gathered through interviews and analyzed for themes.ResultsSustainable financing in Iran's health system faces major challenges in three areas: problems, policies, and politics. Key issues include a lack of financial resources, high healthcare costs, unequal access to care, low government funding, and poor resource management. Other challenges are high out-of-pocket expenses, limited insurance coverage, and reliance on oil revenue. Proposed solutions are to improve resource tracking, increase public health budgets, encourage community involvement, and use modern technology. The government must recognize these issues to build political commitment for sustainable financing, prioritizing resilience and effectiveness.Discussion and conclusionThe collaboration between the World Health Organization, the Islamic Consultative Assembly, and health entities in Iran shows the alignment of problems, policies, and politics. By addressing financial issues and involving stakeholders, they create opportunities for sustainable health financing, improve community health, and strengthen the healthcare system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13561-026-00788-y.

Introduction

Sustainable financing of the health system requires governments to allocate sufficient resources for achieving universal health coverage while simultaneously managing potential impacts on other economic sectors. Major challenges include escalating healthcare costs, increasing user expectations, and the adoption of expensive medical technologies that place additional strain on public budgets [1, 2]. To ensure sustainability, health financing must encompass three core functions: revenue generation, risk pooling, and strategic purchasing. Financial resources can be mobilized from a variety of sources such as taxation, insurance contributions, out-of-pocket payments, donations, and endowments depending on each country’s income level and institutional capacity [3]. A sustainable health system must balance the obligations of service providers with available financial resources to guarantee efficient and equitable service delivery. Accordingly, funding mechanisms should be designed in alignment with each country’s specific health, economic, and demographic contexts [4, 5]. Consistent with the World Health Organization’s recommendations, governments can strengthen sustainability by improving domestic resource mobilization, increasing health budget al.locations, taxing harmful products, and fostering intersectional partnerships to enhance financial resilience [6].

Risk pooling represents a cornerstone of financial sustainability in healthcare, acting as a mechanism to share risks and stabilize system expenditures over time. Governments can enhance risk pooling by integrating diverse funding streams and mandating participation, thereby preventing low-risk individuals from opting out of the system. Effective pooling protects individuals against the financial consequences of illness through collective contributions, which in turn helps reduce out-of-pocket spending. By distributing financial risks across a wider population base, this mechanism establishes a stable financial foundation that makes healthcare costs more predictable and manageable for both the health system and its members [7]. From a broader perspective, pooling in healthcare seeks not only to reduce costs but also to aggregate large populations to mitigate expenditure volatility. In taxation-based systems such as Iran’s, risk pooling naturally emerges as governments prioritize the treatment of individuals with higher health needs. Although managing pooled funds incurs administrative costs, the resulting decline in spending volatility enhances overall welfare and financial security for consumers [8].

In Iran, the existence of multiple health insurance funds has led to fragmented risk pooling, with no systematic mechanism for risk transfer or redistribution among the various schemes. This fragmentation undermines the financial viability of these funds and can create unequal benefits for insured individuals. The absence of effective risk-sharing arrangements contributes to higher out-of-pocket (OOP) payments for some patients, discouraging them from seeking essential care. While risk pools encompassing more than one million members are believed to reduce revenue uncertainty, fragmentation can still present challenges, particularly when some pools remain small or imbalanced in terms of risk composition. Significant differences in risk profiles across pools—especially between employer-based and general public insurance schemes—further exacerbate inequalities in coverage and financial protection [9].

Merging funds is one approach, but reinsurance offers an alternative to manage risk without consolidation. The Fifth Development Plan’s aim for a unified risk pooling system remains unrealized, causing inefficiencies, overlapping funds, inadequate financial support, lack of cost transparency, and insufficient data for policymaking. Additionally, 17 smaller insurance funds have excessive resources relative to their populations, hindering effective risk distribution. Economic sanctions, fluctuating oil prices, and a growing population amplify disparities in healthcare access and quality. The urgent need for sustainable financing is underscored by rising non-communicable diseases and an aging population. Regional integration models may enhance service delivery and equitable access, necessitating innovative solutions tailored to Iran’s unique health challenges [10].

Iran’s health system faces significant financing challenges, necessitating effective policy frameworks. This research aims to offer insights for integrating a sustainable financing program into policy-making directives, aligning with international organizations’ emphasis on addressing these critical issues.

Participants

Participants were chosen through purposive sampling based on criteria including knowledge of sustainable financing in the Iranian health system, a minimum of three years of relevant experience, and representation across varying levels (local, provincial, and national). Snowball sampling aided in identifying additional participants. The study included 15 individuals from the Ministry of Health, 5 from provincial centers and 1 from city centers, reflecting a three-tiered organizational structure in service delivery. Ethical principles from the Declaration of Helsinki were adhered to, focusing on human dignity, participant privacy, and informed consent. Researchers explained the study’s purpose, methodology, and implications to participants, ensuring voluntary and informed consent without coercion. Confidentiality was strictly maintained, with secure data management to protect anonymity. Findings were presented transparently and without bias, and participant feedback was sought to validate the representation of their perspectives. The study underscored ethical integrity by prioritizing participants’ rights, dignity, and welfare, demonstrating a commitment to high ethical standards in qualitative research.

Data collection

To investigate sustainable financing for Iran’s health system, A qualitative study of oriented content analysis study was conducted from February 2023 to May 2024. A total of 21 participants were intentionally selected based on their expertise in health system financing. The participants included experts, service providers, and insurance managers at local, provincial, and national levels. The study involved 21 interviews. Data were gathered through semi-structured interviews, with questions crafted according to specific objectives. These questions explored challenges, solutions, and political factors associated with sustainable financing and were designed to allow for open-ended responses and follow-up inquiries. The interviews took place in a calm environment free from distractions and were digitally recorded. Each interview lasted between 45 and 70 min, and the reliability of the data was validated by reviewing participant feedback. Additionally, a university professor oversaw the interview process, which continued until data saturation was reached. Fig. 1.

Analysis

The present study is a type of directed content analysis [12]. Interviews were analyzed multiple times using Kingdon’s multiple streams framework, focusing on problem, policy, and political streams. Data was organized and categorized through a deductive coding approach. Themes aligned with Kingdon’s model were identified and refined, with sub-themes and specific codes extracted to explore nuanced aspects. This process involved merging, reviewing, and further integration to accurately represent the data.

To enhance the validity and consistency of the themes identified during our qualitative analysis, we implemented several methodological strategies:Peer Review and Debriefing: The research team independently coded interview transcripts, regularly meeting to discuss emerging codes and themes. Reliability

Reliability was established using the criteria outlined by Lincoln and Guba [15, 16]. Data collection, coding, and analysis spanned 14 months, utilizing a comprehensive guide to enhance credibility in sampling, interviews, and document analysis. Critical points from interviews were recorded to aid coding integration, and in-text quotations were used to support transferability and external validity. Detailed descriptions of the Sustainable financing of the Iranian health system helped contextualize findings for broader application. Simultaneous data collection and analysis further promoted transferability. An auditing approach with an external auditor improved research trustworthiness through valuable insights and cross-checks, addressing inconsistencies. The research team minimized personal biases to enhance conformability, adhering to qualitative research standards for reliable insights through meticulous planning and external validation.

Limitations

Participants in semi-structured interviews face limitations in studying sustainable finance, including lack of reliable information, political pressures, and non-evidence-based decisions. Time constraints from demanding schedules, conservative views on change, and organizational cultural obstacles further hinder their engagement and affect attitudes and behaviors regarding sustainable finance challenges.

Structural problems

The Iranian health system faces structural issues such as poor resource distribution, inefficient budgeting, and lack of universal insurance. This leads to high out-of-pocket expenses for patients and unequal access to healthcare services, highlighting the need for solutions to achieve equitable healthcare for all [17].“Inequality in access to healthcare services often exists across different socio-economic, geographic, and demographic groups. For example, rural areas may struggle with a shortage of healthcare providers, which leads to poorer health outcomes and places an increased burden on emergency services” (Interviewee 14).“Conflict of interest in the Iranian health system, as a destructive factor, manifests itself through complex relationships among doctors, pharmaceutical and medical equipment companies, hospitals, and insurance institutions. These conflicts affect decisions related to prescribing medications, performing diagnostic tests, and conducting surgeries, thereby damaging the quality of healthcare, increasing costs, and reducing equity in access to services. Moreover, a lack of transparency in contracts and weak regulatory oversight provide a basis for abuse and contribute to the escalation of these conflicts” (Interviewee 7).

This not only leads to health inequalities but also pushes individuals toward informal and low-quality treatments due to their inability to pay for medical expenses. Ultimately, this results in a decline in public health quality and an increase in poverty” (Interviewee 15).

Discussion

The Kingdon multiple streams framework analyzes health systems’ interactions, identifying political challenges and opportunities to improve resource efficiency and support decision-makers in balancing needs and resources [18]. Iran’s health system faces structural challenges due to poor resource distribution, lack of universal insurance, and high out-of-pocket costs. Dependency on oil revenues diminishes service quality, while insufficient transparency in tariffs worsens healthcare access disparities. The government’s contribution to health financing stands at only 22.1%, compounded by various crises intensifying financial strains [17].

The Iranian health system struggles with economic challenges affecting sustainable financing, with individuals covering 31% of health costs. Resource shortages, sanctions, and payment delays reduce service quality, exacerbate inequalities, and may increase overall healthcare expenses for households [19].

Social challenges result in inefficient resource use in healthcare. High consumer expectations strain financial resources, influenced by advertising and misinformation. Insufficient insurance coverage exacerbates costs, while declining health literacy impairs recognition of health needs and adherence to medical advice, further increasing overall healthcare expenses in Iran [20, 21].

Management challenges in healthcare increase costs due to public sector inefficiencies, pushing patients to private options. This burden impacts families and government finance, erodes public trust, and causes resource inequities, while crisis budget reallocations further degrade service quality [22, 23].

In analyzing policy streams, solutions to Iran’s health system challenges are categorized into governance solutions, resource generation, risk pooling, and health service purchasing. Governance solutions aim to enhance the health system and ensure sustainable financing, focusing on establishing a transparent health information system for effective resource management and decision-making. This system identifies strengths and weaknesses, preventing resource waste. Collaboration among organizations is essential for addressing health issues, while strong governance fosters public trust and attracts investment. Innovative technologies like telemedicine improve healthcare services, and increasing health literacy lowers medical costs and enhances quality of life. Supporting vulnerable populations promotes equitable healthcare distribution, while effective human resource management boosts efficiency and productivity, contributing to sustainable financing [24–29].

Resources generation in healthcare involves identifying and distributing essential resources to meet specific needs effectively. Increasing the health budget relies on strategic approaches and government funding. Community engagement in financial issues fosters responsibility and support. Adhering to principles and preventing misallocation are vital for sustainability. Transparent communication reduces corruption and enhances public trust. Strengthening financial support and oversight improves healthcare quality, while public-private partnerships can increase investment but necessitate careful management for effectiveness [30–34].

Risk pooling solutions enhance resources and reduce costs in Iran by consolidating basic health insurance. This initiative aims to improve service delivery and decrease expenses through integrated services and streamlined patient care processes. Adequate infrastructure and electronic systems are essential for success, alongside developing electronic services and training insured individuals. The goal is to provide quality healthcare access while lowering costs. Strategic purchasing of health services influences sustainable financing and encourages innovation, with criteria for quality, access, cost-effectiveness, transparency, and auditing being vital for success [35, 36].

Purchasing health services improves sustainability, efficiency, and access while reducing inequalities. Effective programs depend on reliable data and collaboration in financing. Focusing on healthy lifestyles, prevention, and value-based pricing enhances quality. Effective demand management and patient awareness contribute to better resource allocation, ultimately leading to cost savings and improved health outcomes [37–41].

Kingdon’s model highlights the political stream’s role in policy change, representing the dynamics of political forces such as public opinion, leadership shifts, and the power of interest groups and political parties. Political changes can bring forth new solutions and prioritize issues, shaping policy discussions. The interaction between the political stream and the streams of issues and solutions is vital, generating a window of opportunity that affects these solutions’ success or failure. Favorable political conditions, like elections, increase receptiveness to new ideas, allowing advocates to push their solutions effectively. Conversely, waning political support can hinder previously viable proposals. Understanding the political stream’s nuances is crucial for policymakers and stakeholders, as it influences the timing and feasibility of introducing innovative solutions to pressing issues, ultimately facilitating meaningful reform through critical junctures [42].

International cooperation in health financing enhances developing countries’ health systems, supporting sustainable development goals via shared knowledge and resources. By forming partnerships, nations can pool expertise and financial resources to address common health challenges, improving outcomes. Organizations like the WHO and UN play a crucial role in facilitating these collaborations, connecting various sectors to promote knowledge exchange and policy improvement. They mobilize funding for key health initiatives, strengthen systems through research and capacity-building, and foster resilience, ultimately contributing to equitable health outcomes and progress in addressing health disparities globally [43].

Kingdon’s multiple streams model highlights the necessity of a political stream in Iran to support sustainable financing for the health system. The Iranian government faces significant responsibilities for health financing, prioritized in development plans. The sixth five-year plan mandates securing sustainable funding, yet economic crises and international restrictions hinder progress. Access to international financial markets is limited, complicating financing efforts. Recent parliamentary legislation aims to enhance sustainable funding for health, but despite initiatives, significant financial sustainability improvements in Iran’s health system remain elusive.

Conclusion

The WHO, the Islamic Consultative Assembly of Iran, and health organizations can enhance sustainable health system financing as policy entrepreneurs. Applying Kingdon’s model, the WHO analyzes global health data to pinpoint financial challenges. The Ministry of Health and insurers explore national research for deeper understanding. The Health Commission organizes sessions for diverse stakeholder input, ensuring evidence-based policy-making. Furthermore, the WHO shares successful international practices, enabling the adaptation of tailored local solutions for Iran’s health financing challenges while leveraging best practices.

The WHO outlines strategies for sustainable financing and implementation. The Islamic Consultative Assembly fosters a supportive political environment and legal reforms. Collaboration between the Ministry of Health and basic insurance providers is vital for unified health financing. WHO promotes program implementation through international recommendations. Success relies on collaboration among all entities, building alliances with medical associations and effective media use to raise public awareness and engage communities.

Developing necessary infrastructure, including stakeholder education and sustainable information systems, is crucial prior to launching initiatives to ensure success. Prioritizing adaptability to political and social conditions enables effective navigation of challenges, allowing real-time strategy adjustments. These coordinated strategies can help organizations leverage sustainable financing opportunities in Iran’s health system, ultimately improving community health outcomes and resilience.
