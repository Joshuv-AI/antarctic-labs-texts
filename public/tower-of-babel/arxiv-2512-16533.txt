Continuized Nesterov Acceleration for Non-Convex Optimization
Julien Hermant julien.hermant@math.u-bordeaux.fr, Jean-François Aujol Jean-Francois.Aujol@math.u-bordeaux.fr, Charles Dossal dossal@insa-toulouse.fr, Lorick Huang lhuang@insa-toulouse.fr, Aude Rondepierre aude.rondepierre@insa-toulouse.fr

Abstract
In convex optimization, continuous-time counterparts have been a fruitful tool for analyzing momentum algorithms. Fewer such examples are available when the function to minimize is non-convex. In several cases, discrepancies arise between the existing discrete-time results — namely those obtained for momentum algorithms — and their continuous-time counterparts, with the latter typically yielding stronger guarantees. We argue that the continuized framework ( 25 ) , mixing continuous and discrete components, can tighten the gap between known continuous and discrete results. This framework relies on computations akin to standard Lyapunov analyses, from which are deduced convergence bounds for an algorithm that can be written as a Nesterov momentum algorithm with stochastic parameters. In this work, we extend the range of applicability of the continuized framework, e.g. by allowing it to handle non-smooth Lyapunov functions. We then strengthen its trajectory-wise guarantees for linear convergence rate, deriving finite time bounds with high probability and asymptotic almost sure bounds. We apply this framework to the non-convex class of strongly quasar convex functions. Adapting continuous-time results that have weaker discrete equivalents to the continuized method, we improve by a constant factor the known convergence rate, and relax the existing assumptions on the set of minimizers.

1.2
A Promising Alternative: Continuized Nesterov

A modification of (
1.1
) is introduced in
(
25
)
, motivated by the study of asynchronous algorithms, and drawing on ideas originating from the analysis of Markov chains
(
1
)
. Intuitively, instead of making the gradient act continuously in
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, gradient steps will occur at discrete
random times
T_{1},T_{2},\dots
where for all
i
,
T_{i+1}-T_{i}
are independent and follow an exponential law of parameter
1
. Precisely, between these random times, the dynamics of
(x_{t},z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
smoothly interpolate the two processes, namely

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d{x}_{t}&=\eta_{t}(z_{t}-x_{t})dt\\
d{z}_{t}&=\eta^{\prime}_{t}(x_{t}-z_{t})dt\end{array}\right.

At
t=T_{i}
, the process ”jumps” by performing a gradient step

x_{T_{i}}=x_{T_{i}^{-}}-\gamma_{T_{i}^{-}}\nabla f(x_{T_{i}^{-}}),\quad z_{T_{i}}=z_{T_{i}^{-}}-\gamma^{\prime}_{T_{i}^{-}}\nabla f(z_{T_{i}^{-}}),

(8)

where
x_{T_{i}^{-}}
denotes the right-limit
\lim_{t\to 0^{-}}x_{T_{i}+t}
.
This process can be written as a stochastic differential equation that we call the
continuized Nesterov equations

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}dx_{t}&=\eta_{t}(z_{t}-x_{t})dt-\gamma_{t}\nabla f(x_{t})dN(t)\\
dz_{t}&=\eta^{\prime}_{t}(x_{t}-z_{t})dt-\gamma^{\prime}_{t}\nabla f(x_{t})dN(t)\end{array}\right.

where
dN(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\delta_{T_{k}}(dt)
is a Poisson point measure. Intuitively, since
\mathbb{E}\left[dN_{t}\right]=dt
, the mean process
(\mathbb{E}\left[x_{t}\right],\mathbb{E}\left[z_{t}\right])_{t\geq 0}
is smooth, see Figure
1
. In particular, if
\nabla f
is linear, it can be written exactly in the form of (
1.1
). Consequently, individual realizations of the jump-driven process (
1.2
) provide a stochastic approximation of a much smoother expected trajectory. It implies that, for a given Lyapunov function
E_{t}:=\varphi(x_{t},z_{t},t)
, the analysis of
\mathbb{E}\left[E_{t}\right]
is close to that arising in the standard continuous setting, such as in the study of (
1.1
).

Left: Visualisation of a single trajectory of
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}

1.2

t_{0}
and

Strikingly, a core property of (
1.2
) is that the discretization
\tilde{x}_{k}:=x_{T_{k}}
and
\tilde{z}_{k}:=z_{T_{k}}
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, can be
computed exactly
. The resulting discretization writes as a Nesterov momentum algorithm of the form (
1.1
), with stochastic parameters involving the random times
\{T_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
. The convergence results obtained for the solution of (
1.2
) thus almost
automatically transfer
to the aforementioned discretization. It totally differs from the classic ODE framework, where (
1.1
) is a discrete
approximation
of (
1.1
).
In their seminal work,
(
25
)
show that applied to (strongly) convex functions, the continuized version of the Nesterov momentum algorithm recovers, in expectation, similar results to the classical Nesterov algorithm. If this method has known some extension in the specific field of decentralized asynchronous algorithms

(
62
;
61
)
, its use in a more classic optimization setting remains limited.

A non-convex application: strong quasar convexity

Strong quasar convexity is a non-convex relaxation of strong convexity, that has received increasing attention in recent years
(
44
;
35
;
83
;
29
;
2
;
42
;
18
;
38
;
54
;
24
;
26
)
. Compared to gradient descent, better convergence bounds exist for Nesterov momentum when minimizing these functions. The first of these accelerated results mixes the momentum mechanism with a subroutine that solves an optimization sub-problem
(
37
;
65
)
, reduced to a line-search procedure which adds a logarithmic factor in the complexity in
(
44
)
. Avoiding such a subroutine requires to restrict the class of function, excluding functions that are too non-convex
(
42
;
38
)
. The continuized method has been applied to strongly quasar convex functions
(

)
, recovering the convergence rate of the classical Nesterov momentum algorithm. Strikingly, it does so without requiring a line-search procedure or restricting the level of non-convexity.
This indicates that the method is capable of achieving results that classical momentum algorithms have not been able to obtain so far, and we show in this work that it can go further.

1.3
Limits of the Framework and Contributions

Compared with the classical ODE framework, a key difference is that the continuized approach yields an exact discretization whose convergence properties inherit almost directly those of the continuous-time process.
Because of this conceptual difference, the continuized method may at first feel unfamiliar or non-intuitive to optimizers, which might explain why it is still largely unexploited. Moreover, it has some limitations: first, because of the inherent stochasticity of the continuized process, most of the existing results hold in expectation even if using the exact gradient. This leads to results that are weaker in nature than those of classical momentum algorithms, which provide deterministic guarantees. Results holding trajectory-wise with a given probability are given in
(
83
)
, but the derived probability can be trivial for a large amount of the first iterations (Section
4.2
). Also, the current framework only allows to use smooth Lyapunov functions, preventing for example its application to non-smooth functions.

Contributions

(i) We present a clear methodology for employing the continuized method and extend the fundamental tools needed to make it operational. We illustrate the method on the class of smooth Polyak-Łojasiewicz functions, deducing a new, though non-accelerated, convergence result, see Section
3
.

(ii) We provide an Itô formula (Proposition
19
) that allows to handle non-differentiable Lyapunov functions. In the case of exponentially fast convergence rates, we significantly tighten the existing bound on the probability to have a trajectory-wise convergence result, and further derive asymptotic almost sure convergence rates.

(iii) Adapting results coming from the classic ODE framework literature, we improve the best known convergence rate by a constant factor for smooth strongly quasar convex functions. We further relax the existing assumption on the set of minimizers for this class of functions. Our results hold when considering unbiased stochastic estimates of the gradients, as long as a strong growth condition is verified.

Assumption 1

3
How to Use the Continuized Nesterov Framework

In this section, we detail the methodology for leveraging the continuized Nesterov dynamics to derive convergence results.
It can be outlined through the three following steps:
(i)
continuous-time analysis via stochastic Lyapunov functions,
(ii)
derivation of an exact discrete algorithm, and
(iii)
automatic transfer of convergence guarantees to this algorithm, using stopping-time arguments.

First, we note that the continuized equations naturally handle stochastic gradients. Following the discussion of Section
1.2
, consider the same random times
\{T_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Assume
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots
are i.i.d random variables of law
\mathcal{P}
. At
t=T_{k}
, a stochastic gradient step is performed

x_{T_{k}}=x_{T_{k}^{-}}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{T_{k}^{-}},\xi_{k+1}),\quad z_{T_{k}}=z_{T_{k}^{-}}-\gamma^{\prime}\nabla f(z_{T_{k}^{-}},\xi_{k+1}).

Between these random times, the dynamics followed by
(x_{t},z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is still

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d{x}_{t}&=\eta(z_{t}-x_{t})dt\\
d{z}_{t}&=\eta^{\prime}(x_{t}-z_{t})dt\end{array}\right.

These
continuized Nesterov equations
write as ((15)-(16) in
(
25
)
)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}dx_{t}&=\eta(z_{t}-x_{t})dt-\gamma\int_{\Xi}\nabla f(x_{t},\xi)dN(t,\xi)\\
dz_{t}&=\eta^{\prime}(x_{t}-z_{t})dt-\gamma^{\prime}\int_{\Xi}\nabla f(x_{t},\xi)dN(t,\xi)\end{array}\right.

with the Poisson point measure
dN(t,\xi)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\delta_{T_{k},\xi_{k}}(dt,d\xi)
on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\Xi
, which has intensity
dt\otimes\mathcal{P}
. By definition, the process is càdlàg,
i.e.
right-continuous with left limits. We assume
x_{0}=z_{0}
is deterministic. Note that compared to the discussion of Section
1.2
, we consider a constant set of parameters
(\eta,\eta^{\prime},\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
.

Remark 3
.

Because gradient steps intervene at discrete times, the continuized Nesterov equations are highly flexible to modifications. By instance, we could consider replacing gradient step by proximal updates
(
74
)
, or using different stochastic estimators of the gradients, e.g. using variance reduction techniques
(
49
)
. Although we believe these extensions are interesting directions for future research, they fall outside the scope of the present work.

Our main interest is to analyze the convergence properties of
(x_{t},z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
In the following section, we provide an analytical tool that replaces differentiation in the presence of jumps.

3.1
Lyapunov Analysis with Stochastic Calculus

To deduce convergence results using (
3
), a classical strategy is to consider a well chosen Lyapunov function,
e.g.

t\to\mathbb{E}\left[f(x_{t})\right]
, and to show it is decreasing. Although
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is indexed by continuous time, it cannot be differentiated with respect to time because of the jumps introduced by the Poisson component. Fortunately, the theory of stochastic calculus provides analogous tools, such that we can use a Lyapunov-style approach.

Proposition 4

(
C^{1}
-Itô Formula)
.

Let
x_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a solution of

dx_{t}=\zeta(x_{t})dt+\int_{\Xi}G(x_{t^{-}},\xi)dN(t,\xi)

where
\zeta:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a Lipschitz function and
G:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\Xi\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is measurable. Let
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
C^{1}
function. Then,

\varphi(x_{t})=\varphi(x_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\left<\nabla\varphi(x_{s}),\zeta(x_{s})\right>ds+\int_{[0,t]}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\varphi(x_{s}+G(x_{s},\xi))-\varphi(x_{s})]ds+M_{t},

(13)

where
M_{t}
is a martingale such that
\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t}\right]=0
,
\forall t\geq 0
.

This Itô formula is a close formulation of
(
25
, Proposition 2)
. For readers who are not familiar with martingales or stochastic differential equations, standard references include
(
84
;
73
)
.
Proposition
4
can be applied to a well chosen Lyapunov function
\varphi
. In particular, it suffices to show that
\left<\nabla\varphi(x_{s}),\zeta(x_{s})\right>+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}{\varphi(x_{s}+G(x_{s},\xi))-\varphi(x_{s})}\leq 0
to have that
t\to\varphi(x_{t})
is a supermartingale, which in turn induces

\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(x_{t})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(x_{0})\right].

We further relax this
C^{1}
condition in Proposition
19
.

Remark 5
.

The
\int_{0}^{t}\left<\nabla\varphi(x_{s}),\zeta(x_{s})\right>ds
term appearing in (
13
) is purely of continuous nature. Computations involving this term will be similar to the one involving the classic deterministic system (
1.1
). The term
\int_{[0,t]}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\varphi(x_{s}+G(x_{s},\xi))-\varphi(x_{s})]ds
is induced by the Poisson process, and will involve some terms leading to computations that are closer to the one occurring when analyzing (
1.1
). As mentioned in
(
25
)
, the continuized framework involves a continuous-discrete mix.

3.2
Almost automatic discretization

Because the solution to (
1.1
) requires knowledge of the gradient at all times
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, it is generally not available in closed form. Thus, convergence guarantees for the continuous system cannot be applied directly in practice, and one typically proceeds by establishing a corresponding result for a discretization,
e.g.
(
1.1
).
A remarkable property of (
3
) is that the gradient acts only at discrete times. Defining
\tilde{y}_{k}:=x_{T_{k+1}^{-}}
,
\tilde{x}_{k}:=x_{T_{k}}
and
\tilde{z}_{k}:=z_{T_{k}}
as evaluations of the process
(x_{t},z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined by (
3
), one can explicitly integrate the trajectory between each times
\{T_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.

Proposition 6
.

Let
(x_{t},z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
follow (
3
) with
\eta+\eta^{\prime}>0
. Define
\tilde{y}_{k}:=x_{T_{k+1}^{-}}
,
\tilde{x}_{k}:=x_{T_{k}}
and
\tilde{z}_{k}:=z_{T_{k}}
as evaluations of these processes. Then,
(\tilde{y}_{k},\tilde{x}_{k},\tilde{z}_{k})
writes as a Nesterov algorithm in the form of (
1.1
) with stochastic parameters

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tilde{y}_{k}&=(\tilde{z}_{k}-\tilde{x}_{k})\frac{\eta}{\eta+\eta^{\prime}}\left(1-e^{-(\eta+\eta^{\prime})(T_{k+1}-T_{k})}\right)+\tilde{x}_{k}\\
\tilde{x}_{k+1}&=\tilde{y}_{k}-\gamma\nabla f(\tilde{y}_{k},\xi_{k+1})\\
\tilde{z}_{k+1}&=\tilde{z}_{k}+\eta^{\prime}\frac{\left(1-e^{-(\eta+\eta^{\prime})(T_{k+1}-T_{k})}\right)}{\eta^{\prime}+\eta e^{-(\eta+\eta^{\prime})(T_{k+1}-T_{k})}}(\tilde{y}_{k}-\tilde{z}_{k})-\gamma^{\prime}\nabla f(\tilde{y}_{k},\xi_{k+1})\end{array}\right.

Proposition
6
is a generalization of
(
25
;
83
)
in the case of general constant parameters, see its proof in Appendix
B
. Note that the random times
\{T_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
can be easily simulated, such that the algorithm is implementable. We also note that
\eta^{\prime}+\eta e^{-(\eta+\eta^{\prime})(T_{k+1}-T_{k})}\neq 0
, almost surely, which prevents from definition problems in practice. We refer to (
6
) as the
continuized Nesterov algorithm
. Compared to the classic ODE framework described in Section
1.1
, (
6
) is not an approximation of a continuous counterpart, it is an exact evaluation of a realization of (
3
). As
\tilde{x}_{k}
is exactly
x_{t}
evaluated at the stopping time
T_{k}
, one can transfer a convergence result to the sequence
\{\tilde{x}_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, which we formalize as follows.

Theorem 7

(Stopping theorem)
.

Let
(\varphi_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}
be a non-negative process with càdlàg trajectories, such that it verifies

\varphi_{t}\leq K_{0}+M_{t},

for some positive random variable
K_{0}
, some martingale
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with
\mathbb{E}\left[M_{0}\right]=0
. Then, for any almost surely finite stopping time
\tau
, one has

\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{\tau}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[K_{0}\right].

Proof.

Let
\tau
be an almost surely finite time,
i.e.

\tau(\omega)<+\infty
almost surely.
The stopped martingale
\{M_{t\wedge\tau}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}
is a martingale, with
\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t\wedge\tau}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[M_{0\wedge\tau}\right]=0
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
Then, the stopped process
V_{t}:=\varphi_{\tau\wedge t}
is such that

\forall t\geq 0,\penalty\ \mathbb{E}\left[V_{t}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[K_{0}\right].

As we assume
\inf_{t}\varphi_{t}\geq 0
, we also have
\inf_{t}V_{t}\geq 0
. We thus apply Fatou’s Lemma

\mathbb{E}\left[\liminf_{t\to+\infty}V_{t}\right]\leq\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[V_{t}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[K_{0}\right].

(17)

\tau
being almost surely finite implies that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}t\wedge\tau(\omega)=\tau(\omega)
, almost surely. Then, for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
, we have

\lim_{t\to+\infty}V_{t}(\omega)=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\varphi_{t\wedge\tau(\omega)}(\omega)=\varphi_{\tau(\omega)}(\omega),

(18)

namely
V_{t}
converges almost surely to
\varphi_{\tau}
. Plugging (
18
) into (
17
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{\tau}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[K_{0}\right].

∎

The stopping theorem used in
(
25
)
assumes the process is a uniformly integrable super-martingale, which might be difficult to verify. We state a result that holds for a broader range of
\{\varphi_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}
, and is more convenient to use. Finally, we note that this automatic transfer from the continuous to the discrete setting comes at the cost of yielding non-deterministic convergence results, even in the presence of deterministic gradients.

3.3
Application to Smooth, Polyak-Łojasiewicz Functions

We illustrate the previously presented methodology in the context of minimizing
L
-smooth,
\mu
-Polyak-Łojasiewicz functions. We start by deriving a convergence result associated to the continuous process
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, using the Itô formula introduced in Section
3.1
.

Proposition 8
.

If
(x_{t},z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solution of (
3
) with
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{\rho L}
,
\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma+\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\rho}}
,
\eta=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\rho}}
and
\eta^{\prime}=\frac{\mu\gamma}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\rho}}
under Assumptions
(L-smooth)
,
(PL
μ
)
and
(SGC
ρ
)
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[f(x_{t})-f^{\ast}\right]\leq e^{-\frac{\mu\gamma}{2}t}(f(x_{0})-f^{\ast}).

Proof.

Formalism setting
We start to set the formalism such that we can apply Proposition
4
.
Let
\overline{x}_{t}=(t,x_{t},z_{t})
where
(x_{t},z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfy (
3
). It satisfies
d\overline{x}_{t}=\zeta(\overline{x}_{t})dt+G(\overline{x}_{t},\xi)dN(t,\xi)
, with

\zeta(\overline{x}_{t})=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\eta(z_{t}-x_{t})\\
\eta^{\prime}(x_{t}-z_{t})\end{pmatrix},\quad G(\overline{x}_{t},\xi)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-\gamma\nabla f(x_{t},\xi)\\
-\gamma^{\prime}\nabla f(x_{t},\xi).\end{pmatrix}

We apply Proposition
4
to
\varphi(\overline{x}_{t})
, where

\varphi(t,x,z)=a_{t}(f(x)-f^{\ast})+\frac{b_{t}}{2}\left\lVert x-z\right\rVert^{2},

(19)

such that for a martingale
M_{t}
, we have

\varphi(\overline{x}_{t})=\varphi(\overline{x}_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\left<\nabla\varphi(\overline{x}_{s}),\zeta(\overline{x}_{s})\right>+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\varphi(\overline{x}_{s}+G(\overline{x}_{s},\xi))-\varphi(\overline{x}_{s})]ds+M_{t}.

(20)

Computations

We compute
\left<\nabla\varphi(\overline{x}_{s}),\zeta(\overline{x}_{s})\right>
and
\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\varphi(\overline{x}_{s}+G(\overline{x}_{s}))-\varphi(\overline{x}_{s})
.
We have

\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial s}=\frac{da_{s}}{ds}(f(x)-f^{\ast})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{db_{s}}{ds}\left\lVert x-z\right\rVert^{2},\quad\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}=a_{s}\nabla f(x)+b_{s}(x-z),\quad\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial z}=b_{s}(z-x).

So,

\displaystyle\left<\nabla\varphi(\overline{x}_{s}),\zeta(\overline{x}_{s})\right>

\displaystyle=\frac{da_{s}}{ds}(f(x_{s})-f^{\ast})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{db_{s}}{ds}\left\lVert x_{s}-z_{s}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle+\left<a_{s}\nabla f(x_{s})+b_{s}(x_{s}-z_{s}),\eta(z_{s}-x_{s})\right>+\left<b_{s}(z_{s}-x_{s}),\eta^{\prime}(x_{s}-z_{s})\right>

\displaystyle=\frac{da_{s}}{ds}(f(x_{s})-f^{\ast})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{db_{s}}{ds}\left\lVert x_{s}-z_{s}\right\rVert^{2}+a_{s}\eta\left<\nabla f(x_{s}),z_{s}-x_{s}\right>-b_{s}\left(\eta+\eta^{\prime}\right)\left\lVert x_{s}-z_{s}\right\rVert^{2}.

(21)

Also,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\varphi(\overline{x}_{s}+G(\overline{x}_{s}))-\varphi(\overline{x}_{s})]

\displaystyle=a_{s}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[f(x_{s}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{s},\xi))-f(x_{s})]

\displaystyle+\frac{b_{s}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\left(\left\lVert x_{s}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{s},\xi)-(z_{s}-\gamma^{\prime}\nabla f(x_{s},\xi)\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert x_{s}-z_{s}\right\rVert^{2}\right)]

\displaystyle=a_{s}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[f(x_{s}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{s},\xi))-f(x_{s})]+b_{s}\frac{(\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma)^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\left\lVert\nabla f(x_{s},\xi)\right\rVert^{2}]

\displaystyle+b_{s}(\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma)\left<\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\nabla f(x_{s},\xi)],x_{s}-z_{s}\right>

\displaystyle\leq\left(b_{s}\rho(\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma)^{2}-a_{s}\gamma(2-L\gamma\rho)\right)\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\nabla f(x_{s})\right\rVert^{2}+b_{s}(\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma)\left<\nabla f(x_{s}),x_{s}-z_{s}\right>.

(22)

The last inequality uses Assumption
(SGC
ρ
)
and Lemma
2
.
We combine (
21
) and (
22
)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left<\nabla\varphi(\overline{x}_{s}),\zeta(\overline{x}_{s})\right>+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\varphi(\overline{x}_{s}+G(\overline{x}_{s}))-\varphi(\overline{x}_{s})]\\
&\leq\frac{da_{s}}{ds}(f(x_{s})-f^{\ast})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{db_{s}}{ds}\left\lVert x_{s}-z_{s}\right\rVert^{2}+(a_{s}\eta-b_{s}(\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma))\left<\nabla f(x_{s}),z_{s}-x_{s}\right>\\
&-b_{s}(\eta+\eta^{\prime})\left\lVert x_{s}-z_{s}\right\rVert^{2}+\left(b_{s}\rho(\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma)^{2}-a_{s}\gamma(2-L\gamma\rho)\right)\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\nabla f(x_{s})\right\rVert^{2}.\end{aligned}

(23)

Now, using Assumption
(PL
μ
)
in (
23
) and rearranging, we deduce

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left<\nabla\varphi(\overline{x}_{s}),\zeta(\overline{x}_{s})\right>+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\varphi(\overline{x}_{s}+G(\overline{x}_{s}))-\varphi(\overline{x}_{s})]\\
&\leq\left(\frac{da_{s}}{ds}+\mu\left(b_{s}\rho(\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma)^{2}-a_{s}\gamma(2-L\gamma\rho)\right)\right)(f(x_{s})-f^{\ast})\\
&+\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{db_{s}}{ds}-b_{s}(\eta+\eta^{\prime})\right)\left\lVert x_{s}-z_{s}\right\rVert^{2}+(a_{s}\eta-b_{s}(\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma))\left<\nabla f(x_{s}),z_{s}-x_{s}\right>.\end{aligned}

(24)

Parameter tuning

As it is standard in many convergence proofs, we now choose the parameters driving equation (
3
) such that we get to the desired result. We set
a_{s}=b_{s}=e^{\theta s}
, for some
\theta>0
.
Fixing
\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma+\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\rho}}
ensures

\rho(\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma)^{2}-\gamma(2-L\gamma\rho)\leq\frac{\gamma}{2}-\gamma=-\frac{\gamma}{2}.

The latter holds because
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{\rho L}\Rightarrow-\gamma(2-L\rho\gamma)\leq-\gamma
. Setting
\eta=\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\rho}}
cancels the scalar product in (

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\left<\nabla\varphi(\overline{x}_{s}),\zeta(\overline{x}_{s})\right>+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\varphi(\overline{x}_{s}+G(\overline{x}_{s}))-\varphi(\overline{x}_{s})\right]

(25)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\theta s}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}-(\eta+\eta^{\prime})\right)\left\lVert x_{s}-z_{s}\right\rVert^{2}+e^{\theta s}\left(\theta-\mu\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)(f(x_{s})-f^{\ast}).

Fixing
\theta=\frac{\mu\gamma}{2}
and
\eta^{\prime}=\frac{\mu\gamma}{2}-\eta=\frac{\mu\gamma}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\rho}}
cancels the right hand side of (
25
).

Conclusion

From (
20
) and the previous computations,
we get

\displaystyle\varphi(\overline{x}_{t})\leq\varphi(\overline{x}_{0})+M_{t}.

(26)

Using the definition of
\varphi

and ignoring the

e^{\frac{\mu\gamma}{2}t}(f(x_{t})-f^{\ast})\leq(f(x_{0})-f^{\ast})+M_{t}.

(27)

\mathbb{E}\left[f(x_{t})-f^{\ast}\right]\leq e^{-\frac{\mu\gamma}{2}t}(f(x_{0})-f^{\ast}).

∎

The only technical tool we use is a stochastic descent Lemma which controls the decrease of the function when performing a gradient step, see Lemma
2

Theorem 9

Assume
T_{1},\dots,T_{k}

(PL

(SGC

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\frac{\mu\gamma}{2}T_{k}}(f(x_{T_{k}})-f^{\ast})\right]\leq f(x_{0})-f^{\ast}.

Proof.

In Proposition

4
Improved Factor in Convergence Rate for Strongly Quasar Convex Functions and Better Trajectory-Wise Guarantees

We recall existing convergence guarantees for functions satisfying Assumptions
(L-smooth)
and
(SQC
τ,μ
)
, and use the notation

(

4.1
Improved Factor in Convergence Rate

Under Assumption
(SQC

)

To transfer this better rate in the discrete setting, authors however also assume convexity. Inspired from their Lyapunov function, we consider the following one, adapted to the continuized framework

\varphi(t,x_{t},z_{t}):=a_{t}(f(x_{t})-f^{\ast})+\frac{b_{t}}{2}\left\lVert z_{t}-x^{\ast}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{c_{t}}{2}\left\lVert x_{t}-x^{\ast}\right\rVert^{2}.

Proposition 19

(Non-Smooth Itô Formula)
.

Let
x_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a solution of

dx_{t}=\zeta(x_{t})dt+\int_{\Xi}G(x_{t^{-}},\xi)dN(t,\xi),

where
\zeta:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a Lipschitz function and

is measurable. Let

and denote
\phi(t)=\varphi(x_{t})
. If either
\varphi
is a continuous function such that its directional derivatives exist at all points, or
\phi
is absolutely continuous, then there exists
\mathbb{A}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
of measure zero such that the derivative of
\phi
, denoted
\dot{\phi}
, exists on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\backslash\mathbb{A}
.
Moreover, if

\forall s\in[0,t]\backslash\mathbb{A},\quad\dot{\phi}(s)+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[(\varphi(x_{s}+G(x_{s},\xi))-\varphi(x_{s}))]\leq 0,

then we have

\phi(t)\leq\phi(0)+M_{t},

where
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a martingale such that
\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t}\right]=0
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

Proof.

Note first that
T_{n}\to+\infty
almost surely. This can be seen as
T_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}
,
E_{i}\overset{i.i.d}{\sim}\mathcal{E}(1)
, and by the law of large numbers
T_{n}/n\to\mathbb{E}\left[1\right]=1
, almost surely. This permits us to write

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \phi(t)-\phi(0)&=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\phi(t\wedge T_{k+1})-\phi(t\wedge T_{k})\\
&=\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\left(\phi(t\wedge T_{k+1})-\phi(t\wedge T_{k+1}^{-})\right)}_{\text{(I)}}+\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\left(\phi(t\wedge T_{k+1}^{-})-\phi(t\wedge T_{k})\right)}_{\text{(II)}}.\end{aligned}

(35)

We treat (I) and (II) separately.

Sum (I)
(I) describes the jumps. We have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\left(\phi(t\wedge T_{k+1})-\phi(t\wedge T_{k+1}^{-})\right)&=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\left(\varphi(x_{{t\wedge T_{k+1}}^{-}}+G(x_{{t\wedge T_{k+1}}^{-}},\xi_{k+1}))-\varphi(x_{{t\wedge T_{k+1}}^{-}})\right)\\
&=\int_{[0,t]}(\varphi(x_{s^{-}}+G(x_{s^{-}},\xi))-\varphi(x_{s^{-}}))dN(s,\xi).\end{aligned}

(36)

Sum (II)

The sum (II) is composed of
\phi(t\wedge T_{k+1}^{-})-\phi(t\wedge T_{k})
terms. If
\phi
was
C^{1}
, we could directly integrate on
\left[t\wedge T_{k},t\wedge T_{k+1}\right)
.
Recall that we have either
\varphi
is a continuous function such that its directional derivatives exist at all points, or
\phi
is absolutely continuous. We split the proof between these two cases.

Case 1:
\phi
is absolutely continuous

Absolute continuity of
\phi
is equivalent to
\phi
having a derivative
\dot{\phi}
almost everywhere, which is Lebesgue integrable and verify the fundamental theorem of analysis, namely for
0\leq u\leq t

\phi(t)-\phi(u)=\int_{u}^{t}\dot{\phi}(s)ds.

(37)

Sum (II) thus rewrites

\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\left(\phi_{t\wedge T_{k+1}^{-}}-\phi_{t\wedge T_{k}}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\int_{t\wedge T_{k}}^{t\wedge T_{k+1}}\dot{\phi}(s)ds=\int_{0}^{t}\dot{\phi}(s)ds

(38)

Conclusion for Case 1

Combining (
36
) and (
38
), we obtain

\displaystyle\phi(t)

\displaystyle=\phi(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\dot{\phi}(s)ds+\int_{[0,t]}(\varphi(x_{s^{-}}+G(x_{s^{-}},\xi))-\varphi(x_{s^{-}}))dN(s,\xi)

\displaystyle=\phi(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\dot{\phi}(s)ds+\int_{[0,t]}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[(\varphi(x_{s}+G(x_{s},\xi))-\varphi(x_{s}))]ds

\displaystyle+\int_{[0,t]}\int_{\Xi}(\varphi(x_{s^{-}}+G(x_{s^{-}},\xi))-\varphi(x_{s^{-}}))(dN(s,\xi)-dsd\mathcal{P}(\xi))

\displaystyle=\phi(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\dot{\phi}(s)+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[(\varphi(x_{s}+G(x_{s},\xi))-\varphi(x_{s}))]ds+M_{t},

where
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a martingale such that
\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t}\right]=0
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Noting
\mathbb{A}
the set of null measure on which
\dot{\phi}
may not be defined, by non-negativity of the Lebesgue integral, we have that if

\forall s\in[0,t]\backslash\mathbb{A},\quad\dot{\phi}(s)+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[(\varphi(x_{s}+G(x_{s},\xi))-\varphi(x_{s}))]\leq 0,

then

\phi(t)\leq\phi(0)+M_{t}.

E.1
Some Properties of the Henstock–Kurzweil Integral

In this section, as we use it in our Section
5

Henstock–Kurzweil integral
, sometimes referred to as the
generalized integral
. The theory of this integral has been independently developed by Kurzweil
(
53
)
and Henstock
(
41
)
. For a detailed presentation, we refer to
(
11
)
.

In the whole section,
I=[a,b]
denotes an interval of
{\mathbb{R}}
.

Definition 30
.

(I_{k},\nu_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
is a tagged partition of
I
if

1.

(I_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
is a partition of intervals of
I
.

2.

\nu_{k}\in I_{k}
,
\forall k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

A tagged partition gives a partition of
I
and, and for each interval of the partition, a point in this interval. This object is also involved in the classical Riemann integral. The difference here will be the use of
gauge
functions.

Definition 31
.

An application
\delta:I\to(0,+\infty)
is a gauge function.

Intuitively, we will use gauge functions to control the lengths of the intervals in the partitions of
I
. This is formally done with the idea of
\delta
-fine partition.

Definition 32
.

For a gauge
\delta:I\to(0,+\infty)
, a tagged partition
(I_{k},\nu_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
is
\delta
-fine if

I_{k}\subset[\nu_{k}-\delta(\nu_{k}),\nu_{k}+\delta(\nu_{k})].

For a defined gauge function, such a partition always exists
(
20
)
.
Now, we can define what it means to be Kurzweil-integrable.

Definition 33
.

A function
f:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
is Kurzweil-Henstock integrable with real value denoted
\int_{I}f(s)ds_{HK}
if for all
\epsilon>0
, there exists a gauge
\delta_{\varepsilon}
such that for all tagged partition
(I_{k},\nu_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
that is
\delta_{\varepsilon}
-fine, the Riemann-Kurzweil-Henstock sum verifies

\left|\sum_{i=k}^{n}|I_{k}|f(\nu_{k})-\int_{I}f(x)ds_{HK}\right|\leq\varepsilon,

with
|I_{k}|
the length of
I_{k}
.

A function that is Riemann or Lebesgue integrable is also Kurzweil-Henstock integrable, and in this case their value coincide.
Kurzweil-Henstock integrals verify similar useful properties as for the classical integrals.

Proposition 34
.

Let

be Kurzweil-Henstock integrable. Then

1.

(Linearity,
(
11
, Theorem 3.1)

\forall a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}

(

3.

(Additivity,
(
11

\int_{a}^{c}fd_{HK}+\int_{c}^{b}fd_{HK}=\int_{a}^{c}fd_{HK}

(Hake’s Theorem,

\lim_{c\to b^{-}}\int_{a}^{c}fd_{HK}=\int_{a}^{b}fd_{HK}

We end this section by stating the remarkable property of this type of integral: the fundamental theorem of calculus holds under more relaxed assumptions than compared to Riemann and Lebesgue’s integrals.

Theorem 35
.

Let
F:[a,b]\to R
. If either

1.

F^{\prime}(x)=f(x)
for all
x\in I
.

2.

If

This result are Theorem 4.5 and 4.7 in
(

)
. The second point is the one we use in our proof of Proposition

(

E.2

We give a very short description of different well known formulations of momentum algorithms.
First, we note the seminal Polyak’s Heavy Ball

(
32

33

\begin{array}[]{c}\textbf{NM (ML version)}\\[5.69046pt]

The ML version coincides with the implementation commonly used in deep-learning frameworks, see

(
