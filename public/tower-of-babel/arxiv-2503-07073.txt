A transform for the Grushin operator with applications
Krzysztof Stempak

Abstract
. 1.
Introduction

The
Grushin differential operator

G
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}
,
d^{\prime},d^{\prime\prime}\geq 1
, is given by the differential expression

-\Delta_{x^{\prime}}-|x^{\prime}|^{2}\Delta_{x^{\prime\prime}},

where
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
,
x^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}
, and
\Delta_{x^{\prime}},\Delta_{x^{\prime\prime}}
are the Laplacians on
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}
, respectively.
It is an important example of second order subelliptic differential operator with polynomial coefficients, which was intensively investigated
in the PDE’s theory and also from the point of view of harmonic analysis. There is an interesting interplay between
G
and differential operators
on some Lie groups, notably between
G
and the sublaplacians on the Heisenberg groups or, more generally, on stratified Lie groups, see Dziubański
and Sikora
[
5
]
, and Martini and Sikora
[
11
]
.

It is easily seen that initially considered with domain
{\rm Dom}\,G=C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
G
is symmetric and nonnegative. Moreover,

G
is essentially self-adjoint (see
[
11
]
for a group-theoretic argument or
[
3
]
for a simple proof of this), hence it admits the
unique self-adjoint extension on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
called the
Grushin operator
and denoted
\mathbb{G}
. During the last years numerous papers were
devoted to different aspects of harmonic analysis of
\mathbb{G}
. See, for instance, Dall’Ara and Martini
[
3
]
with extensive literature
therein, and Jotsaroop et al.
[
8
]
, where, among others, the spectral decomposition of the Grushin operator was proposed (in the case
d^{\prime\prime}=1
).

In this paper we construct and study a transform, denoted
\mathcal{G}
, with integral kernels being tensor products of eigenfunctions of scaled Hermite operators

-\Delta_{x^{\prime}}+|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{2}|x^{\prime}|^{2}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
,
0\neq\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}
, and eigenfunctions of the Laplacian
-\Delta_{x^{\prime\prime}}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}})
.
These tensor products are eigenfunctions of
G
. The eigenfunctions of scaled Hermite operators consist of systems of scaled Hermite functions that
form orthonormal bases in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
. The use of these systems was initiated and successfully applied by Thangavelu in the harmonic analysis
of the sub-Laplacian on the Heisenberg group,
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
, and in an investigation of an analogue of Hardy’s theorem for the Heisenberg
group,
[
21
]
,
[
22
]
, and also in some other contexts. The idea of this transform was implicit in many works devoted to the Grushin operator,
see e.g.
[
11
]
, but did not receive an explicit description.

The transform
\mathcal{G}
plays the same role for
G
as the Fourier transform plays for the Laplacian. In Theorem
3.1
we prove that
\mathcal{G}

is a unitary bijection between
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and an appropriately associated ‘dual’ space
L^{2}(\Gamma)
. Moreover,
\mathcal{G}
unitarily intertwines
G

with the self-adjoint multiplication operator
\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}
on
L^{2}(\Gamma)
, where
\Theta
is a real-valued positive function on
\Gamma
,
see (
3.2
) and Section
4
. This allows to construct
a simple self-adjoint realization of
G
, denoted
\mathbb{G}
, just by mapping
\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}
onto
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
; see Proposition
4.1
.
Then
\mathbb{G}
is recognized as an operator with domain being a Sobolev-type space adapted to
G
and the action
\mathbb{G}f=Gf
, where
Gf

is understood in the distributional sense, see Proposition
4.2
. This is much the same as in the case of the Laplacian on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Consequently, the functional calculus for
\mathbb{G}
reflected from that for
\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}
allows to establish a closed
formula of the heat kernel
\{p_{t}\}_{t>0}
corresponding to the heat semigroup
\{\exp(-t\mathbb{G})\}_{t>0}
; this is done in Theorem
5.1
.
Closed formulas of
p_{t}
were known, see Section 5.2 in the monograph
[
2
]
, where the case
d^{\prime}=d^{\prime\prime}=1
was treated, Garofalo and Tralli

[
6
, Theorem 3.4]
, or Oliveira and Viana
[
13
, Proposition 2.1]
. It is also known, at least for
d^{\prime\prime}=1
, that the heat kernel for the
Grushin operator is the image, under the unitary Schrödinger representation of the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}_{d^{\prime}}=\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}
,
of the heat kernel for the sublaplacian on
\mathbb{H}_{d^{\prime}}
, cf.
[
4
]
. We mention that in
[
6
]
and
[
13
]
,
in the framework of the differential operator
G
and the associated heat equation
\partial_{t}u=-G_{x}u
, the heat kernel is meant as
a function
p(x,\xi,t)
such that for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the function
p(\cdot,\xi,\cdot)
is a solution of the heat equation, and, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

p(x,\cdot,t)\to\delta_{x}
in the distributional sense as

. Our approach is operator theoretic and the heat kernel is understood as the
collection of integral kernels of
\exp(-t\mathbb{G})
, which are bounded operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We add that calculations leading to (
5.2
), the
closed form of
p_{t}
, are straightforward and, we believe, are worth presentation.

2.
Preliminaries

The system of multidimensional Hermite functions
\{h_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}}}
is an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
consisting of eigenfunctions of
the Hermite differential operator,

(-\Delta_{x^{\prime}}+|x^{\prime}|^{2})h_{k}=(2|k|+d^{\prime})h_{k},\qquad|k|:=k_{1}+\ldots+k_{d^{\prime}};

see
[
18
]
or
[
15
]
for details (exceptionally,
|\cdot|
stands here also for the
length
of
k
). The
scaled systems

\{h_{k,\tau}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}}}
,
\tau>0
,

h_{k,\tau}(x^{\prime})=\tau^{d^{\prime}/4}h_{k}(\tau^{1/2}x^{\prime}),\qquad x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}},

form orthonormal bases in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
and consist of eigenfunctions of the
scaled Hermite differential operator
,

(-\Delta_{x^{\prime}}+\tau^{2}|x^{\prime}|^{2})h_{k,\tau}=\lambda_{k}^{\prime}\tau h_{k,\tau},\qquad\lambda_{k}^{\prime}=2|k|+d^{\prime}.

Now define the
scaled Hermite transform
which for every
\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}_{0}:=\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}\setminus\{0\}
attaches to a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})

the sequence of coefficients from the expansion of
f
with respect to
\{h_{k,|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}}}
. Namely, for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
,

\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc}}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}f(k):=\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc}}f(k,\xi^{\prime\prime})=\langle f,h_{k,|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}\rangle,\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}},\,\,\,\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}_{0}.

By Parseval’s identity, for every
\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}_{0}
,
\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc}}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}})
is a unitary isomorphism,
and its inverse is the mapping

\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc};-1}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}\colon\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}),\qquad\{c_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}}}\to\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}}}c_{k}h_{k,|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}.

The appropriate scaling of the Hermite functions is crucial in verification that the functions

\psi_{k,\xi^{\prime\prime}}(x):=(2\pi)^{-d^{\prime\prime}/2}h_{k,|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}(x^{\prime})e^{-\textrm{i}x^{\prime\prime}\cdot\xi^{\prime\prime}},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}},\quad\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}_{0},

are eigenfunctions of the Grushin differential operator. (The normalization constant
(2\pi)^{-d^{\prime\prime}/2}
is, of course, immaterial in this place,
but it is decisive later on to keep some transforms
L^{2}
isometric.)

3.
\mathcal{G}
-transform and its inverse

In this section we define a transform, in the present context playing the role of the Fourier transform in the analysis of the Laplacian, which
will be crucial for the spectral analysis of the Grushin operator. Using the scaled Hermite transform in this definition is motivated by
[
8
]
,
where the system of scaled Hermite functions was applied to describe the spectral decomposition of the Grushin operator.

For a suitable function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we define its transform
\mathcal{G}f
, a function on
\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}_{0}
, given as the integral transform based
on the eigenfunctions
\psi_{k,\xi^{\prime\prime}}
of
G
. Namely, we set

\mathcal{G}f(k,\xi^{\prime\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\psi_{k,\xi^{\prime\prime}}(x)\,dx,\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}},\quad\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}_{0},

(3.1)

provided
f
is such that the integral converges for every
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}}
and almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}_{0}
. Obviously, this happens for

f\in C^{\infty}_{c}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)
. Notice, that using a version of Green’s formula adapted to
G
, see Lemma
6.2
,
for
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we obtain

\mathcal{G}(G\varphi)(k,\xi^{\prime\prime})=\lambda_{k}^{\prime}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|\,\mathcal{G}\varphi(k,\xi^{\prime\prime}),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}},\quad\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}_{0}.

(3.2)

Thus,
\mathcal{G}
intertwines the differential operator
G
and the multiplication operator by
(k,\xi^{\prime\prime})\to\lambda_{k}^{\prime}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|
. It will follow from
Theorem
3.1
that with appropriately (and naturally) defined
L^{2}
space,
\mathcal{G}
becomes a unitary isomorphism.

The
\mathcal{G}
-transform is for
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)
expressible through the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}
and the scaled Hermite
transform
\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc}}
. Recall that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}
is a unitary automorphism of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}})
and
\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc}}

acts on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
attaching to
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
the sequences
\{\langle g,h_{k,|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}\rangle\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}}}
,
\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}_{0}
. Namely,

\mathcal{G}\varphi(k,\xi^{\prime\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}h_{k,|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}(x^{\prime})\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}\varphi_{x^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime\prime})\,dx^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc}}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}\big[\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}\varphi_{x^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime\prime})\big](k).

The expression inside the square brackets is, for fixed
\xi^{\prime\prime}
, a function of
x^{\prime}
to which
\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc}}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}
is applied.
Similarly to the above case, in several places below, square brackets will indicate that expressions inside them should be properly identified.
This means, that one has to recognize a variable among some other characters; from the context it will be clear which characters appear as fixed parameters.

This preliminary calculation leads to the extension of the action of
\mathcal{G}
onto
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by setting

\mathcal{G}f(k,\xi^{\prime\prime}):=\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc}}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}\big[\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}f_{x^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime\prime})\big](k),\qquad f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(3.3)

It remains to confirm correctness of the above definition. But

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}|\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}f_{x^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime\prime})|^{2}dx^{\prime}\,d\xi^{\prime\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}|\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}f_{x^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime\prime})|^{2}d\xi^{\prime\prime}\,dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}|f_{x^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime\prime})|^{2}d\xi^{\prime\prime}\,dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty,

so that
x^{\prime}\to\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}f_{x^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime\prime})
is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
for a.e.
\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}
, hence
\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc}}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}
can be applied.
Notice also, that for functions with separated variables,
f(x)=f_{1}(x^{\prime})f_{2}(x^{\prime\prime})
,
f_{1}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
,
f_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}})
, (
3.3
) becomes

\mathcal{G}f(k,\xi^{\prime\prime})=\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc}}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}f_{1}(k)\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}f_{2}(\xi^{\prime\prime}).

Observe that the structure of
\mathcal{G}
expressed in (

) reveals that this transform can be seen as a ‘twisted’ composition of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}
and

\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc}}

Theorem 3.1
.

We have

\|\mathcal{G}f\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}=\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\quad and\quad f=\mathcal{G}^{-1}\big(\mathcal{G}f\big),\qquad f\in L^{2}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(3.5)

and

\|\mathcal{G}^{-1}F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\|F\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\quad and\quad F=\mathcal{G}\big(\mathcal{G}^{-1}F\big),\qquad F\in L^{2}(\Gamma).

(3.6)

Proof.

To verify Plancherel’s identity in (
3.5
), for fixed
\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}_{0}

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}}}|\mathcal{G}(k,\xi^{\prime\prime})|^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}}}|\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc}}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}\big[\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}f_{x^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime\prime})\big](k)|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}|\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}f_{x^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime\prime})|^{2}dx^{\prime}

and integration over
\xi^{\prime\prime}
then gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}\Big(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}}}|\mathcal{G}(k,\xi^{\prime\prime})|^{2}\Big)d\xi^{\prime\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}|\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}f_{x^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime\prime})|^{2}d\xi^{\prime\prime}dx^{\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}|f_{x^{\prime}}(x^{\prime\prime})|^{2}dx^{\prime\prime}dx^{\prime}=\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}
applies.
Now, recalling that by (
3.3
),
(\mathcal{G}f)_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}=\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc}}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}\big[\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}f_{x^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime\prime})\big]
, using (
3.4
)
gives for almost every
y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{-1}\big(\mathcal{G}f\big)(y^{\prime},\cdot)=\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime-1}\big[\xi^{\prime\prime}\to\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc};-1}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}(\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f)_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}(y^{\prime})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime-1}\big[\xi^{\prime\prime}\to\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc};-1}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}\big(\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc}}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}[x^{\prime}\to\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}f_{x^{\prime}}(\xi^{\prime\prime})]\big)(y^{\prime})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime-1}\big(\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}f_{y^{\prime}}\big)

\displaystyle=f_{y^{\prime}}.

To verify Plancherel’s identity in (
3.6
) we write

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}|\mathcal{G}^{-1}F(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})|^{2}dx^{\prime}\,dx^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}\big|\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime-1}\big[\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc};-1}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}F_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}(x^{\prime})\big](x^{\prime\prime})\big|^{2}\,dx^{\prime\prime}dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}\big|\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc};-1}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}F_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}(x^{\prime})\big|^{2}d\xi^{\prime\prime}dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}.

For the inversion formula in (
3.6
), observing first that, by what we have just proved,
\mathcal{G}^{-1}F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we get

\mathcal{G}\big(\mathcal{G}^{-1}F\big)(k,\xi^{\prime\prime})=\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc}}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}\Big[\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}\big(\mathcal{G}^{-1}F)_{x^{\prime}}\big)(\xi^{\prime\prime})\Big](k)=\big\langle\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}\big((\mathcal{G}^{-1}F)_{x^{\prime}}\big)(\xi^{\prime\prime}),h_{k,|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}\big\rangle.

The first term in the last brackets is understood, with
\xi^{\prime\prime}
fixed, as a function of
x^{\prime}
which is

x^{\prime}\to\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}\big((\mathcal{G}^{-1}F)_{x^{\prime}}\big)(\xi^{\prime\prime})=\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}\big(\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime-1}[\xi^{\prime\prime}\to\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc};-1}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}F_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}(r)]\big)(\xi^{\prime\prime})=\mathcal{H}^{\textsl{sc};-1}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}F_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}(x^{\prime})=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}F(l,\xi^{\prime\prime})h_{l,|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}(x^{\prime}),

hence the value of the last brackets, where
x^{\prime}
is also the variable of integration, indeed equals
F(k,\xi^{\prime\prime})
.
∎

It remains to conclude that by Theorem
3.1
,
\mathcal{G}^{-1}
is indeed the inverse of
\mathcal{G}
, i.e.
\mathcal{G}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{G}={\rm Id}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
and

\mathcal{G}\circ\mathcal{G}^{-1}={\rm Id}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}
.

4.
Self-adjoint extensions of
G

Consider the real-valued function

\Theta\colon\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}_{0}\to(0,\infty),\qquad\Theta(k,\xi^{\prime\prime})=\lambda_{k}^{\prime}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|,\quad\lambda_{k}^{\prime}=2|k|+d^{\prime},

and the corresponding
multiplication operator

\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}
on
L^{2}(\Gamma)
with ‘maximal’ domain,

\displaystyle{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}

\displaystyle=\big\{F\in L^{2}(\Gamma)\colon\Theta F\in L^{2}(\Gamma)\big\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}F

\displaystyle=\Theta F,\qquad F\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}.

Then a simple argument shows that
\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}
is self-adjoint and nonnegative, and the spectrum of it coincides with
[0,\infty)
,
which is the set of essential values of
\Theta
.

We now transfer
\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}
onto
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
through the unitary isomorphism

\mathcal{G}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\Gamma)
and its inverse
\mathcal{G}^{-1}\colon L^{2}(\Gamma)\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, defining

\displaystyle{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}:=

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{-1}\big({\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}\big)=\{f\in L^{2}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)\colon\Theta\,\mathcal{G}f\in L^{2}(\Gamma)\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}:=

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{-1}\circ\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}\circ\mathcal{G}.

It follows that
\mathbb{G}
is self-adjoint and nonnegative, and the spectrum of
\mathbb{G}
is
[0,\infty)
. It remains to verify that
\mathbb{G}

indeed extends
G
, which is done below. Implicitly, this self-adjoint realization of
G
is considered in
[
11
]
where, in order to study spectral
multipliers, a functional calculus corresponding to that described here (see Section
5
) was used.

Proposition 4.1
.

\mathbb{G}
is an extension of
G
.

Proof.

With the aid of (
3.2
) and Theorem
3.1
it follows (notice that
G
maps
C^{\infty}_{c}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)
into itself) that
C^{\infty}_{c}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)\subset{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}
. It remains to show that
\mathbb{G}\varphi=G\varphi
for
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)
. To check this it suffices to verify that

\mathbb{M}(\mathcal{G}\varphi)=\mathcal{G}(G\varphi),

which means verifying that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d^{\prime}}
and
\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}_{0}
, we have

\Theta(k,\xi^{\prime\prime})\mathcal{G}\varphi(k,\xi^{\prime\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G\varphi(x)\psi_{k,\xi^{\prime\prime}}(x)\,dx.

But this holds because

\Theta(k,\xi^{\prime\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\psi_{k,\xi^{\prime\prime}}(x)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)G\psi_{k,\xi^{\prime\prime}}(x)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G\varphi(x)\psi_{k,\xi^{\prime\prime}}(x)\,dx,

where in the last step a version of Green’s formula for
G
was used; see Lemma
6.2
.
∎

This extension has an alternative description. Let us define the Sobolev-type space adapted to
G
,

W^{2}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\colon Gf\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}.

Here and later on
Gf
is understood in the distributional sense, and
Gf\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
means that

\exists h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\langle G\varphi,f\rangle=\langle\varphi,h\rangle,\qquad{\rm and\,\,\,then}\quad Gf:=h.

(4.1)

It is easily checked (see Lemma
6.3
for a similar result) that equipped with the scalar product

\langle f,g\rangle_{W^{2}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\langle f,g\rangle+\langle Gf,Gg\rangle,

W^{2}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
becomes a Hilbert space and, since
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset W^{2}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
W^{2}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proposition 4.2
.

We have
{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}=W^{2}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathbb{G}f=Gf
for
f\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}
.

Proof.

For
\supset
take
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
Gf\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This means that (
4.1
) holds with
h=Gf
. Now, the polarized version of Plancherel’s identity (
3.5
) from Theorem
3.1
implies that also

\langle\mathcal{G}(G\varphi),\mathcal{G}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}=\langle\mathcal{G}\varphi,\mathcal{G}(Gf)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}.

But, see (
3.2
),

\langle\mathcal{G}(G\varphi),\mathcal{G}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}=\langle\Theta\mathcal{G}\varphi,\mathcal{G}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}=\langle\mathcal{G}\varphi,\Theta\mathcal{G}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)},

hence
\mathcal{G}(Gf)=\Theta\mathcal{G}f\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
, so
f\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}
; we used the fact that
\mathcal{G}\big(C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)
is dense in
L^{2}(\Gamma)
,
because
\mathcal{G}
is isometrical bijection. For
\subset
, let
f\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}
, so that
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\Theta\mathcal{G}f\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
.
Set
h:=\mathcal{G}^{-1}(\Theta\mathcal{G}f)
, so that
h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Repeating the previous arguments but in the opposite order it is easily seen
that
\langle G\varphi,f\rangle=\langle\varphi,h\rangle
, which means
Gf=h
, hence
f\in W^{2}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and also
\mathbb{G}f=Gf
.
∎

A common way of constructing self-adjoint extensions of differential operators is using sesquilinear forms. This requires introducing
appropriate Sobolev-type spaces. In the considered case of the Grushin differential operator we begin with an apparently natural way,
see the formulas in (
4.2
) and (
4.3
). There is, however, a pitfall caused by singularity of the
function
x\to|x^{\prime}|
along the
(d-d^{\prime})
-dimensional hyperplane
S^{\prime}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon x^{\prime}=0\}
. A way of omitting this is simply to remove
S^{\prime}
from
\mathbb{R}^{d}

considering the open set
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus S^{\prime}
, and then to consider
G
with domain
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})
.
To distinguish from the previous situation let
G_{0}
stand for such operator, i.e.
{\rm Dom}\,G_{0}=C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})
. We mention that
the problem of ‘removable sets’ in constructions of self-adjoint extensions has been studied for other operators in the literature, see, e.g.,
[
1
]
and
[
7
]
.

Since
S^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is of Lebesgue measure zero,
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})
naturally identifies with
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Also
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})
identifies with
the space of smooth functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with compact supports separated from the closed set
S^{\prime}
, denoted
C^{\infty}_{c,*}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;
we shall use both identifications with no further mention. Since
C^{\infty}_{c,*}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
G_{0}

is densely defined. Moreover,
G_{0}\subset G
, hence
G_{0}
is symmetric and nonnegative.

We now define the Sobolev space
W^{1}_{G_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})
(of first order) adapted to
G_{0}
,

W^{1}_{G_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\colon\partial_{j}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,\,{\rm for}\,\,1\leq j\leq d^{\prime}\,\,{\rm and}\,\,|x^{\prime}|\partial_{j}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,\,{\rm for}\,\,d^{\prime}+1\leq j\leq d\}.

(4.2)

Here and below, for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\partial_{j}f
are understood in the sense of distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*}
and for
d^{\prime}+1\leq j\leq d
,

|x^{\prime}|\partial_{j}f
means the distribution
\partial_{j}f
multiplied by the
C^{\infty}
function
|x^{\prime}|
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*}
; notice that

|x^{\prime}|\partial_{j}f=\partial_{j}(|x^{\prime}|f)
in the sense of distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*}
.

Equipped with the scalar product

\langle f,g\rangle_{G_{0}}:=\langle f,g\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{d^{\prime}}\langle\partial_{j}f,\partial_{j}g\rangle+\sum_{j=d^{\prime}+1}^{d}\langle|x^{\prime}|\partial_{j}f,|x^{\prime}|\partial_{j}g\rangle,

W^{1}_{G_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})
becomes a Hilbert space (the proof is postponed to Section
6
, see Lemma
6.3
) and,
since
C^{\infty}_{c,*}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset W^{1}_{G_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})
,
W^{1}_{G_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

In the next step we define the sesquilinear form

\mathfrak{t}_{0}[f,g]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{G_{0}}f\cdot\overline{\nabla_{G_{0}}g},\qquad f,g\in W^{1}_{G_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*}),

(4.3)

where
\nabla_{G_{0}}:=(\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{d^{\prime}},|x^{\prime}|\partial_{d^{\prime}+1},\ldots,|x^{\prime}|\partial_{d})
is the
G_{0}
-gradient. Then
\mathfrak{t}_{0}

is densely defined Hermitian nonnegative and closed form (closedness of
\mathfrak{t}_{0}
is just the completeness of the norm generated by

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{G_{0}}
). Consequently, it follows from the general theory that the operator
\mathbb{G}_{0}
defined by

\displaystyle{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}_{0}

\displaystyle=\{f\in W^{1}_{G_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})\colon\exists u_{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\forall g\in W^{1}_{G_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})\quad\mathfrak{t}_{0}[f,g]=\langle u_{f},g\rangle\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{0}\,f

\displaystyle=u_{f},\qquad f\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}_{0},

is self-adjoint and nonnegative on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and extends
G
which we now prove.
We add that in the sense of distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*}
, for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
G_{0}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
means that

\exists h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c,*}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\langle G\varphi,f\rangle=\langle\varphi,h\rangle,\qquad{\rm and\,\,\,then}\quad G_{0}f:=h.

(4.4)

Proposition 4.3
.

\mathbb{G}_{0}
is an extension of
G_{0}
. Moreover,

{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}_{0}\subset W^{2}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(4.5)

and
\mathbb{G}_{0}f=G_{0}f
for
f\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}_{0}
.

Proof.

We first claim that
C^{\infty}_{c,*}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}_{0}
and
\mathbb{G}_{0}\varphi=G\varphi
for
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c,*}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For this purpose it suffices
to check that given
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c,*}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for every
h\in W^{1}_{G_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})
it holds

\sum_{j=1}^{d^{\prime}}\langle\partial_{j}\varphi,\partial_{j}h\rangle+\sum_{j=d^{\prime}+1}^{d}\langle|x^{\prime}|\partial_{j}\varphi,|x^{\prime}|\partial_{j}h\rangle=\langle G\varphi,h\rangle.

(4.6)

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*}
be a bounded open subset containing
{\rm supp}\,\varphi
, separated from
S^{\prime}
which is the boundary of
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*}
, with
\partial\Omega
being sufficiently smooth, say
C^{1}
. Equivalently, we prove (
4.6
) with integration over
\Omega
that replaces
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For this it suffices to check that

-\int_{\Omega}\partial^{2}_{j}\varphi\,\overline{h}=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{j}\varphi\overline{\partial_{j}h}\quad{\rm and}\quad-\int_{\Omega}|x^{\prime}|^{2}\partial^{2}_{j}\varphi\,\overline{h}=\int_{\Omega}|x^{\prime}|^{2}\partial_{j}\varphi\overline{\partial_{j}h}.

But
h\in W^{1}_{G_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})
which implies that
h\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, where
H^{1}(\Omega)
denotes the Euclidean Sobolev space on
\Omega
.
(We have
\partial_{j}(h|_{\Omega})=(\partial_{j}h)|_{\Omega})
and since
|x^{\prime}|\partial_{j}h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, also
\partial_{j}(h|_{\Omega})\in L^{2}(\Omega)

for
d^{\prime}+1\leq j\leq d
.) Thus the two above identities follow from Gauss’ formula (
6.1
).

To check (
4.5
) note that
f\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}_{0}
means that
f\in W_{G_{0}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and there is
u_{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that for every
h\in W_{G_{0}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

we have
\mathfrak{t}_{0}[h,f]=\langle h,u_{f}\rangle
. In particular, taking
h=\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c,*}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
gives

\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c,*}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{d^{\prime}}\langle\partial_{j}\varphi,\partial_{j}f\rangle+\sum_{j=d^{\prime}+1}^{d}\langle|x^{\prime}|\partial_{j}\varphi,|x^{\prime}|\partial_{j}f\rangle=\langle\varphi,u_{f}\rangle.

But the left-hand side of the above identity equals
\langle G\varphi,f\rangle
, see (
4.6
), which implies that
G_{0}f=u_{f}
, hence
f\in W_{G}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\mathbb{G}_{0}f=G_{0}f
.
∎

Obviously,
\mathbb{G}_{0}=\mathbb{G}
cannot be a consequence of essential self-adjointness of
G
, but the identity is indeed true.

Proposition 4.4
.

We have
\mathbb{G}_{0}=\mathbb{G}
.

Proof.

By Propositions
4.3
and
4.2
we know that
{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}_{0}\subset{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}
and, in addition, by comparing (
4.1
)
and (
4.4
) we see that for every
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c,*}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have
\langle\varphi,G_{0}f\rangle=\langle\varphi,Gf\rangle
. Density of
C^{\infty}_{c,*}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
then shows that
G_{0}f=Gf
. Thus we have the inclusion
\mathbb{G}_{0}\subset\mathbb{G}
. This is enough due to the well-known maximality
property of self-adjoint operators.
∎

Another way of using sesquilinear forms is to look at
G
through the identity

G=\sum_{j=1}^{d^{\prime}}\partial_{j}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d^{\prime}}\sum_{m=1}^{d^{\prime\prime}}x_{j}^{2}\partial_{d^{\prime}+m}^{2}

and consider, instead of
\nabla_{G_{0}}
, the gradient (cf. the vector fields
X_{j}
and
X_{j,m}
at the end of Section
5
)

\nabla_{G}=\big((\partial_{j})_{1\leq j\leq d^{\prime}},(x_{j}^{\prime}\partial_{d^{\prime}+m})_{1\leq j\leq d^{\prime},\,1\leq m\leq d^{\prime\prime}}\big)

(we owe this suggestion to Allessio Martini to whom we are grateful). The accompanying Sobolev-type space is now defined as

W^{1}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\colon\partial_{j}f,\,x_{j}^{\prime}\partial_{d^{\prime}+m}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,\,\,\,{\rm for}\,\,1\leq j\leq d^{\prime},\,\,1\leq m\leq d^{\prime\prime}\},

and it is standard to verify that the norm generated by the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{W^{1}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\langle f,g\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{d^{\prime}}\langle\partial_{j}f,\partial_{j}g\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{d^{\prime}}\sum_{m=1}^{d^{\prime\prime}}\langle x_{j}^{\prime}\partial_{d^{\prime}+m}f,x_{j}^{\prime}\partial_{d^{\prime}+m}g\rangle,

is complete in
W^{1}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Therefore, the sesquilinear form

\mathfrak{t}[f,g]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{G}f\cdot\overline{\nabla_{G}g},\qquad f,g\in W^{1}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(here ‘
\cdot
’ stands for the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}(1+d^{\prime\prime})}
) is Hermitian nonnegative and closed. Consequently, the associated operator, call it
for a moment
\hat{\mathbb{G}}
, is self-adjoint and nonnegative on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Verification that
\hat{\mathbb{G}}
extends
G
,
{\rm Dom}(G)=C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, goes
along the lines of the relevant reasoning for
G_{0}
. Finally, by essential self-adjointness of
G
,
\hat{\mathbb{G}}=\mathbb{G}
.

We conclude this section by a discussion of some group invariance and homogeneity properties of
G
inherited then by
\mathbb{G}
. Let us begin with rotational invariance. The product of orthogonal groups
O(d^{\prime})\times O(d^{\prime\prime})
naturally acts on functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Namely, given
g=(g^{\prime},g^{\prime\prime})\in O(d^{\prime})\times O(d^{\prime\prime})
let

T_{g^{\prime}}f(x)=f(g^{\prime-1}x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\quad{\rm and}\quad T_{g^{\prime\prime}}f(x)=f(x^{\prime},g^{\prime\prime-1}x^{\prime\prime}),

and then define
T_{g}=T_{g^{\prime}}\circ T_{g^{\prime\prime}}=T_{g^{\prime\prime}}\circ T_{g^{\prime}}
, i.e.,

T_{g}f(x)=f(g^{\prime-1}x^{\prime},g^{\prime\prime-1}x^{\prime\prime}),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The structure of
G
suggests that for
g\in O(d^{\prime})\times O(d^{\prime\prime})
,
T_{g}
should commute with
G
. For this it suffices to verify that
T_{g^{\prime}}

and
T_{g^{\prime\prime}}
commute with
G
. But
T_{g^{\prime}}
commutes with
\Delta_{x^{\prime}}
and (trivially) with
\Delta_{x^{\prime\prime}}
, so

\displaystyle\big(G\circ T_{g^{\prime-1}}\big)f(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})=G\big(f(T_{g^{\prime}}x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\big)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{x^{\prime}}\big(f(T_{g^{\prime}}x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\big)+|T_{g^{\prime}}x^{\prime}|^{2}\Delta_{x^{\prime\prime}}\big(f(T_{g^{\prime}}x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\big)

\displaystyle=\big(\Delta_{x^{\prime}}f\big)(T_{g^{\prime}}x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})+|x^{\prime}|^{2}\big(\Delta_{x^{\prime\prime}}f\big)(T_{g^{\prime}}x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\big(T_{g^{\prime-1}}\circ G\big)f(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}).

Analogous calculation with the aid of
\Delta_{x^{\prime\prime}}\circ T_{g^{\prime\prime}}=T_{g^{\prime\prime}}\circ\Delta_{x^{\prime\prime}}
(and trivial commutation of
T_{g^{\prime}}
with
\Delta_{x^{\prime}}
)
shows
G\circ T_{g^{\prime\prime}}=T_{g^{\prime\prime}}\circ G
.

The second property concerns homogeneity of
G
with respect to the family of
parabolic dilations
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\delta_{r}(x)=(rx^{\prime},r^{2}x^{\prime\prime})
, then
involved in parabolic dilations of functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\rho_{r}f(x)=r^{(d^{\prime}+2d^{\prime\prime})/2}f(rx^{\prime},r^{2}x^{\prime\prime}),\qquad r>0.

A short calculation shows that
G
is homogeneous of degree 2 in the sense that

G\circ\rho_{r}=r^{2}(\rho_{r}\circ G).

It is convenient to denote
D:=d^{\prime}+2d^{\prime\prime}
and consider
D
as the
homogeneous dimension
for
G
.

That the two discussed properties are inherited by
\mathbb{G}
should not come as a surprise but certainly it requires a formal proof. From now on we
consider
T_{g}
and
\rho_{r}
as operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Obviously,
T_{g}
and
\rho_{r}
are unitary automorphisms of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
(T_{g})^{*}=T_{g}^{-1}=T_{g^{-1}}
,

(\rho_{r})^{*}=\rho_{r}^{-1}=\rho_{1/r}
. It may be also easily checked that

Proposition 4.5

For every

Proof.

The functional calculus, an important ingredient of the spectral theory of self-adjoint operators, for a multiplication operator
on an
L^{2}
space has a simple description. In the specific case of the multiplication operator
\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}
, given a Borel function

(recall that
\sigma(\mathbb{M}_{\Theta})=[0,\infty)
) we set

\Phi(\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}):=M_{\Phi\circ\Theta}.

Then the functional calculus is understood as the mapping
\Phi\to M_{\Phi\circ\Theta}
assigning to
\Phi
the multiplication operator by

\Phi\circ\Theta
on
L^{2}(\Gamma)
. Consequently, the functional calculus for the self-adjoint operator
\mathbb{G}
, which

\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}
mapped by
\mathcal{G}
onto

(5.1)

so that the domain of

{\rm Dom}\,\Phi(\mathbb{G})=\mathcal{G}^{-1}({\rm Dom}\,M_{\Phi\circ\Theta})=\{f\in L^{2}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)\colon\big(\Phi\circ\Theta\big)\mathcal{G}f\in L^{2}(\Gamma)\}.

Obviously,

inherits all the spectral properties of

Now we apply the above comments to the bounded functions

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}\Big(\frac{|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}{\sinh 2|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}\Big)^{d^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}\exp\Big(-\frac{|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}{2}\big(|x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}|^{2}\tanh|\xi^{\prime\prime}|+|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|^{2}\coth|\xi^{\prime\prime}|\big)\Big)dy^{\prime}d\xi^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}\Big(\frac{|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}{\sinh 2|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}\Big)^{d^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}\exp\Big(-\frac{|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}{2}|y^{\prime}|^{2}\coth|\xi^{\prime\prime}|\Big)dy^{\prime}d\xi^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}\exp\big(-|y^{\prime}|^{2}\big)dy^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}\Big(\frac{|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}{\sinh|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}\Big)^{d^{\prime}/2}(\cosh|\xi^{\prime\prime}|)^{-3/2}d\xi^{\prime\prime}<\infty.

In the last step a change of variable was made and the formula
\sinh 2a=2\sinh a\cosh a
was used.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G\varphi(x)\overline{\psi(x)}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\overline{G\psi(x)}\,dx.

(6.3)

Proof.

Assume that
{\rm supp}\,\varphi\subset A_{r}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon|x^{\prime}|\leq r,\,|x^{\prime\prime}|\leq r\}=\overline{B_{r}^{\prime}}\times\overline{B_{r}^{\prime\prime}}
so that the integration
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}

A_{r}

\varphi_{x^{\prime\prime}}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}})
with
{\rm supp}\,\varphi_{x^{\prime\prime}}\subset\overline{B_{r}^{\prime}}

for
x^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}
, and analogously for
\varphi_{x^{\prime}}
,
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
, and similarly for
\psi_{x^{\prime}}
and
\psi_{x^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle\int_{A_{r}}\big(-\Delta_{x^{\prime}}-|x^{\prime}|^{2}\Delta_{x^{\prime\prime}}\big)\varphi(x)\overline{\psi(x)}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{r}^{\prime\prime}}\int_{B_{r}^{\prime}}\big(-\Delta_{x^{\prime}}\varphi_{x^{\prime\prime}}\big)(x^{\prime})\overline{\psi_{x^{\prime\prime}}(x^{\prime})}\,dx^{\prime}dx^{\prime\prime}-\int_{B_{r}^{\prime}}|x^{\prime}|^{2}\int_{B_{r}^{\prime\prime}}\big(-\Delta_{x^{\prime\prime}}\varphi_{x^{\prime}}\big)(x^{\prime\prime})\overline{\psi_{x^{\prime}}(x^{\prime\prime})}\,dx^{\prime\prime}dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{r}^{\prime\prime}}\int_{B_{r}^{\prime}}\varphi_{x^{\prime\prime}}(x^{\prime})\big(-\Delta_{x^{\prime}}\overline{\psi_{x^{\prime\prime}}\big)(x^{\prime})}\,dx^{\prime}dx^{\prime\prime}-\int_{B_{r^{\prime}}}|x^{\prime}|^{2}\int_{B_{r}^{\prime\prime}}\varphi_{x^{\prime}}(x^{\prime\prime})\overline{\big(-\Delta_{x^{\prime\prime}}\psi_{x^{\prime}}\big)(x^{\prime\prime})}\,dx^{\prime\prime}dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{A_{r}}\varphi(x)\overline{\big(-\Delta_{x^{\prime}}-|x^{\prime}|^{2}\Delta_{x^{\prime\prime}}\big)\psi(x)}\,dx,

where Green’s formula was used for
\Omega^{\prime}=B_{r}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
and
\Omega^{\prime\prime}=B_{r}^{\prime\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}

Lemma 6.3
.

The norm generated by the scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{G_{0}}
in
W^{1}_{G_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})
is complete.

Proof.

Let
\{f_{n}\}
be a Cauchy sequence in
W^{1}_{G_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})
. This means that
\{f_{n}\}
,
\{\partial_{j}f_{n}\}
for
1\leq j\leq d^{\prime}
,
\{|x^{\prime}|\partial_{j}f_{n}\}

for
d^{\prime}+1\leq j\leq d
, are Cauchy sequences in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Let
f_{n}\to f
,
\partial_{j}f_{n}\to g
, and
|x^{\prime}|\partial_{j}f_{n}\to h
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
for some
f,g,h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and relevant
j
’s. It follows that for any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})
,
\langle f_{n},\varphi\rangle\to\langle f,\varphi\rangle
and

\langle\partial_{j}f_{n},\varphi\rangle\to\langle g,\varphi\rangle
. But

\langle\partial_{j}f_{n},\varphi\rangle=-\langle f_{n},\partial_{j}\varphi\rangle\to-\langle f,\partial_{j}\varphi\rangle,

hence
\partial_{j}f=g
follows. Similarly,
\langle|x^{\prime}|\partial_{j}f_{n},\varphi\rangle\to\langle h,\varphi\rangle
and combining this with

\langle|x^{\prime}|\partial_{j}f_{n},\varphi\rangle=\langle\partial_{j}f_{n},|x^{\prime}|\varphi\rangle=-\langle f_{n},\partial_{j}(|x^{\prime}|\varphi)\rangle\to-\langle f,\partial_{j}(|x^{\prime}|\varphi)\rangle,

gives
(|x^{\prime}|\partial_{j})f=h

f\in W^{1}_{G_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})
and that
\{f_{n}\}
converges to

in
W^{1}_{G_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{*})
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
y=(y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be fixed. We have

\partial_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}e^{\textrm{i}(x^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime\prime})\cdot\xi^{\prime\prime}}T_{t,x^{\prime},\xi^{\prime\prime}}(y^{\prime})\,d\xi^{\prime\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}e^{\textrm{i}(x^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime\prime})\cdot\xi^{\prime\prime}}\partial_{t}T_{t,x^{\prime},\xi^{\prime\prime}}(y^{\prime})\,d\xi^{\prime\prime}

(6.4)

and, for
j=1,\ldots,d^{\prime}
,

\partial_{x_{j}^{\prime}}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}e^{\textrm{i}(x^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime\prime})\cdot\xi^{\prime\prime}}T_{t,x^{\prime},\xi^{\prime\prime}}(y^{\prime})\,d\xi^{\prime\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}e^{\textrm{i}(x^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime\prime})\cdot\xi^{\prime\prime}}\partial_{x_{j}^{\prime}}^{2}T_{t,x^{\prime},\xi^{\prime\prime}}(y^{\prime})\,d\xi^{\prime\prime}.

(6.5)

Proof.

For (
6.4
), given
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is fixed, let us introduce a more convenient notation,

F(t,\xi^{\prime\prime})=F_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(t,\xi^{\prime\prime})=(2\pi)^{d^{\prime}/2}T_{t,x^{\prime},\xi^{\prime\prime}}(y^{\prime})

and

f(t,\tau)=f_{A,B}(t,\tau)=\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh 2t\tau}\Big)^{d^{\prime}/2}\exp\big(-\tau(A\tanh t\tau+B\coth t\tau)\big),\quad\tau\geq 0,

so that, with setting
A:=|x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}|^{2}/4
and
B:=|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|^{2}/4
,
F(t,\xi^{\prime\prime})
takes the form

F(t,\xi^{\prime\prime})=f(t,|\xi^{\prime\prime}|).

Now, given
t_{0}\in(0,\infty)
, to check that (
6.4
) holds for

it is sufficient to find a nonnegative function

\Phi(\xi^{\prime\prime})=\Phi_{t_{0}}(\xi^{\prime\prime})
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}}\Phi<\infty
and
|\partial_{t}F(t,\xi^{\prime\prime})|\leq\Phi(\xi^{\prime\prime})
for

and
\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime\prime}}
.
But

\partial_{t}f(t,\tau)=-\tau\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh 2t\tau}\Big)^{d^{\prime}/2}\exp\big(-\tau(A\tanh t\tau+B\coth t\tau)\big)\Big(d^{\prime}\coth 2t\tau+\frac{\tau A}{\cosh^{2}t\tau}-\frac{\tau B}{\sinh^{2}t\tau}\Big),

and so, neglecting the exponential factor which is
\leq 1
,

|\partial_{t}f(t,\tau)|\leq\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh 2t\tau}\Big)^{d^{\prime}/2}\Big(d^{\prime}\tau\coth 2t\tau+\frac{\tau^{2}A}{\cosh^{2}t\tau}+\frac{\tau^{2}B}{\sinh^{2}t\tau}\Big).

Thus, for
t_{0}>0

t_{0}/2<t<2t_{0}
, we have

|\partial_{t}f(t,\tau)|\leq C(\psi_{1}(t,\tau)+\psi_{2}(t,\tau)+\psi_{3}(t,\tau)),

where

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh 2t\tau}\Big)^{d^{\prime}/2+1}\cosh 2t\tau\leq\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh t_{0}\tau}\Big)^{d^{\prime}/2+1}\cosh 4t_{0}\tau\equiv\phi_{1}(\tau),

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh 2t\tau}\Big)^{d^{\prime}/2}\leq\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh t_{0}\tau}\Big)^{d^{\prime}/2}\equiv\phi_{2}(\tau),

\displaystyle\psi_{3}(t,\tau)

6.5
