Sequence Reconstruction over Coloring Channels for Protein Identification
Jessica Bariffi, Antonia Wachter-Zeh, Eitan Yaakobi

Abstract
This paper studies the sequence reconstruction problem for a channel inspired by protein identification. We introduce a coloring channel , where a sequence is transmitted through a channel that deletes all symbols not belonging to a fixed subset (the coloring) of the alphabet. By extending this to a coloring profile , a tuple of distinct colorings, we analyze the channel’s information rate and capacity. We prove that optimal (i.e., achieving maximum information rate) coloring profiles correspond to 2 2 -covering designs and identify the minimal covering number required for maximum information rate, as well as the minimum number for which any coloring profile is optimal.

The goal there is to uniquely recover the original sequence given the subsequences at the channel output.

We start by fixing the notation needed throughout the paper. For some

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}\left(p\right):=-p\log_{2}(p)-(1-p)\log_{2}(1-p).

(1)

It is well-known that the binomial coefficient

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}2^{n\mathrm{H}\left(k/n\right)}\leq\binom{n}{k}\leq 2^{n\mathrm{H}\left(k/n\right)}.

(2)

In the following, for a positive integer

Given a sequence

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{I_{1}}(\bm{x})=(1,0,0,0,1,1,0),\;\text{and}\;\bm{y}_{I_{2}}(\bm{x})=(1,2,2,1,1).

(3)

Let

We now introduce a channel model over a

\bm{I}

\bm{I}
-reconstructible

1
-coloring profile of size

´s, but no information about the order of the entries can be revealed.

Furthermore, we define the joint channel information rate and capacity, respectively, by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}(q,c,\bm{I})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\log_{q}\left(\big|\mathcal{S}_{q}^{(n)}(\bm{I})\big|\right),

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(q,c,\bm{I})

\displaystyle:=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{R}_{n}(q,c,\bm{I}).

(5)

If

The rate and the capacity of

-coloring channels allow us to understand the reconstruction performance given any input sequence

Problem 1
.

Given a

-coloring profile
\bm{I}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{t})

We study Problem

Given an alphabet size

Once an answer is found to Problem

Given an alphabet size

\bm{I}

In this section, we study the information rate and capacity of coloring channels for given parameters
q,c
, and
t
. That is, we assume that over a
q
-ary alphabet, the size
c
of the colorings and the number

of

-colorings are given, and we discuss Problem

for these
c
-coloring channels over
\mathcal{A}_{q}
(see (

and any

\bm{I}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{t})

\big|\mathcal{S}_{q}^{(n)}(\bm{I})\big|=\left|{\cup_{i=1}^{t}\left\{{i=1,\ldots,n\,|\,x_{i}\in I_{j},\;\bm{x}\in\mathcal{A}_{q}^{n}}\right\}}\right|

In the following we will focus only on

Let us start with the case where we consider one

Lemma 1
.

Given

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{S}^{(n)}_{q}(I)\big|=\sum_{i=0}^{n}c^{i}=\frac{c^{n+1}-1}{c-1}.

(6)

In particular, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}(q,c,I)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{n}\log_{q}(c^{n+1}-1)+\tfrac{1}{n}\log_{q}(c-1),\quad\text{and}

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(q,c,I)

\displaystyle=\log_{q}(c).

(8)

Proof.

Observe that the number of output sequences depends only on the number
i
of positions that are not equal to the deleted values. For each of these positions, there are
c

\big|\mathcal{S}^{(n)}_{q}(I)\big|
follows. We obtain the information rate and capacity by calculating the geometric sum and suitably bounding it from below and above.
∎

We now focus on
t\geq 2

Proposition 1
.

Let

Proof.

Let

be distinct and assume

lying in

equal to

. Since

is a concatenation, we essentially consider

Assume that we consider an input sequence

,

, and

Since, for

, all entries of

that are equal to

\displaystyle\binom{n-i}{j}

(10)

and

.
Similarly, for
\bm{y}(\bm{x})_{I_{2}}
, there are

\displaystyle\binom{n-j}{i}

(11)

such possibilities.
Additionally, for each of the

there are
q-d
options. Hence, summing over all possibilities for

∎

\displaystyle q-1,(I_{1},I_{2}))

\displaystyle=\log_{q}(4)(1-s)\mathrm{H}\left(\frac{s}{1-s}\right)+(1-2s)\log_{q}(q-2).

-colored sequences.
To obtain the capacity, we observe that

\displaystyle c^{n}\binom{n+t-1}{t-1}\leq\big|\mathcal{S}_{q}^{(n)}(\bm{I})\big|\leq nc^{n}\binom{n+t-1}{t-1}.

(30)

Applying the logarithm base
q
, dividing by
n
and applying the limit gives
\mathcal{C}(q,c,\bm{I})=\log_{q}(c)

∎

IV
Channels Achieving Maximum Rate

In this section, we address Problems
2
and
3
. As a first step, we characterize the
c
-coloring channels that achieve the maximum capacity. That is, we focus on
t
distinct channel outputs of a
c
-coloring channel over
\mathcal{A}_{q}
and give a necessary and sufficient condition on the colorings for which any input
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{A}_{q}^{n}
is
\bm{I}
-reconstructible. As the cases
c=1
and
c=q
are trivial, we restrict to the cases where
2\leq c\leq q-1
.

Theorem 4
.

Consider a
c
-coloring profile
\bm{I}
of
t
distinct colorings over
\mathcal{A}_{q}
. Any input sequence
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{A}_{q}^{n}
is
\bm{I}
-reconstructible if and only if every pair
(a,b)\in\mathcal{A}_{q}^{2}
is contained in at least one coloring of the profile
\bm{I}
.

Proof.

First of all, let us assume that every pair of distinct points in
\mathcal{A}_{q}
is incident to at least one
c
-coloring in
\bm{I}
. This implies that
\mathcal{A}_{q}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}I_{i}
. We show inductively that for any distinct
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{q}^{n}
it holds that
\bm{y}_{\bm{I}}(\bm{x})\neq\bm{y}_{\bm{I}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})
.
Thus, consider the base case
n=1
. That is,
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{q}
with
x\neq x^{\prime}
. Furthermore, there exists
i\in\left\{{1,\ldots,t}\right\}
such that
x,x^{\prime}\in I_{i}
. Since
x
and
x^{\prime}
are distinct, we observe
y_{I_{i}}(x)\neq y_{I_{i}}(x^{\prime})
and therefore
\bm{y}_{\bm{I}}(x)\neq\bm{y}_{\bm{I}}(x^{\prime})
.
Now assume that the statement holds for any arbitrary but fixed
n\geq 1
and let
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{q}^{n+1}
be distinct. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\bm{x}
and
\bm{x}^{\prime}
can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{x}=(a,\bm{x}^{(n)}),\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{x}^{\prime}=(a^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime(n)})

(31)

where
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{q}
and
\bm{x}^{(n)},\bm{x}^{\prime(n)}\in\mathcal{A}_{q}^{n}
. Note that there is a
c
-coloring
I_{i}
, for
i=1,\ldots,t
, such that
a,a^{\prime}\in I_{i}
. If
a\neq a^{\prime}
it holds
y_{I_{i}}(a)=a\neq a^{\prime}=y_{I_{i}}(a^{\prime})
and thus,
\bm{y}_{\bm{I}}(\bm{x})\neq\bm{y}_{\bm{I}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})
. On the other hand, if
a=a^{\prime}
, we must have that
\bm{x}^{(n)}\neq\bm{x}^{\prime(n)}
. By the induction hypothesis it follows that
\bm{y}_{\bm{I}}(\bm{x}^{(n)})\neq\bm{y}_{\bm{I}}(\bm{x}^{\prime(n)})
and therefore also
\bm{y}_{\bm{I}}(\bm{x})\neq\bm{y}_{\bm{I}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})
.

On the other hand, assume that there are two points
a,b\in\mathcal{A}_{q}
for which there is no
c
-coloring
I\in\bm{I}
containing both elements. This implies the following three scenarios

1.

There exist distinct
i,j\in\left\{{1,\ldots,t}\right\}
such that
a\in I_{i}
and
b\in I_{j}
, but
a\not\in I_{j}
and
b\not\in I_{i}
.

2.

There is no
i\in\left\{{1,\ldots,t}\right\}
such that
a\in I_{i}
.

3.

There are no
i,j\in\left\{{1,\ldots,t}\right\}
such that
a\in I_{i}
and
b\in I_{j}
.

In the first case, without loss of generality, say
a\in I_{1}
,
b\in I_{2}
,
\bm{x}=(a,b)
and
\bm{x}^{\prime}=(b,a)
. Then, however,
\bm{y}_{\bm{I}}(\bm{x})=(a,b)=\bm{y}_{\bm{I}}(\bf\bm{x}^{\prime})
. Considering the second case, the argument is similar. For instance, if
\bm{x}
and
\bm{x}^{\prime}
both consist of
k
entries equal to
a
with
a\in I_{i}
for some
i\in\left\{{1,\ldots,t}\right\}
and
n-k
entries equal in
b
where
b
is not contained in any of the
c
-colorings, then
\bm{y}(\bm{x})=\bm{y}(\bm{x}^{\prime})
. The last case is an extension of the second case.
∎

Coloring profiles satisfying the conditions of Theorem
4
in design theory are commonly known as
(q,c,2)
-
covering designs
which are defined in the following way.

Definition 1
.

Given a set of points
\mathcal{P}
of cardinality
q
and a set of blocks
\mathcal{B}
consisting of
c
-subsets of
\mathcal{P}
. An incidence structure with respect to
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{B}
is called a
(q,c,2)
-covering design
if every
2
distinct points are together incident with at least one block.

This definition can be extended to general
(\nu,k,\tau)
-covering designs. The smallest number
t_{\min}(\nu,k,\tau)
of blocks needed for a
(\nu,k,\tau)
-covering design to exist has been widely studied (see, for instance,
[
13
,
14
,
16
]
for an overview) and computed for many values of
\nu,k
and
\tau
. Schönheim in
[
17
]
gave the best lower bound given by

\displaystyle t_{\min}(\nu,k,\tau)\geq\left\lceil{\tfrac{\nu}{k}\left\lceil{\tfrac{\nu-1}{k-1}\cdots\left\lceil{\tfrac{\nu-\tau+1}{k-\tau+1}}\right\rceil}\right\rceil}\right\rceil.

(32)

Additionally, he showed that there exists
(\nu,k,\tau)
-covering design whose size this bound whenever, for any
h=0,\ldots,\tau-1
, it holds

\displaystyle\textstyle\binom{\nu-h}{\tau-h}\big/\binom{k-h}{\tau-l}\in\mathbb{N}.

(33)

In our case, the bound in (
32
) reduces to

\displaystyle t_{\min}(q,c)\geq\left\lceil{\tfrac{q}{c}\left\lceil{\tfrac{q-1}{c-1}}\right\rceil}\right\rceil.

(34)

By the necessary condition (
33
), the bound is achieved for
t_{\min}(q,2)
, i.e., we have
t_{\min}(q,2)=q(q-1)/2=\binom{q}{2}
. Additionally, it was shown that the bound in (
34
) is met for
c=3
(
[
16
,
20
]
) and for
c=4,5
(
[
21
]
).
Upper bounds were later studied in,
[
22
,
18
,
13
,
19
]
. This covers the first question to Problem
3
which we will not further investigate in this paper.

Let us now focus on the second question stated in Problem
3
. That is, given a
c
-coloring profile
\bm{I}
, where each coloring of
\bm{I}
is drawn uniformly at random and without replacement from
\mathcal{I}(q,c)
, what is the maximum number
T_{\min}(q,c)
of draws such that any sequence
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{A}_{q}^{n}
is
\bm{I}
-reconstructible?
Corollary
1
is an immediate consequence of Theorem
4
and (
34
).

Corollary 1
.

Given
q\geq 3
, we have

\displaystyle T_{\min}(q,2)

\displaystyle=t_{\min}(q,2)=\binom{q}{2},\quad\text{and}

(35)

\displaystyle T_{\min}(q,q-1)

\displaystyle=t_{\min}(q,q-1)=3.

(36)

With the characterization of Theorem
4
we cover the remaining cases
3\leq c\leq q-2
in Proposition
2
, and complete the answer to the second question of Problem
3
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
q\geq 4
and
c\geq 2
be two positive integers. The minimum number of distinct
c
-coloring channels over a
q
-ary alphabet
\mathcal{A}_{q}
that allows to recover any input sequence is

\displaystyle T_{\min}(q,c)=\binom{q-1}{c}+\binom{q-2}{c-1}+1.

(37)

Proof for
c=3
.

We are looking for the minimum number
T_{\min}(q,c)
of distinct
c
-colorings drawn uniformly at random from
\mathcal{I}(q,c)
. By Theorem
4
,
t
distinct
c
-coloring channels over
\mathcal{A}_{q}
can reconstruct a sequence
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{A}_{q}^{n}
if and only if every pair
(a,b)\in\mathcal{A}_{q}^{2}
is contained in at least one of the
t
colorings. To prove the statement, we draw one
c
-coloring after the other with a uniform distribution. At each step, we count how many new pairs have been added. This iteration is continued until all
\binom{q}{2}
pairs are covered by at least one coloring. Due to random drawing, we need to consider the worst case scenario or pairs that are added at each step. For instance, any coloring we chose as first
c
-coloring
I_{1}
covers
\binom{c}{2}=3
pairs in
\mathcal{A}_{q}
. We now chose a second
c
-coloring
I_{2}
. In the worst case, we have
I_{1}\cap I_{2}=q-1
or equivalently the number of new pairs added is
c-1
.

Continuing this procedure, we observe that the worst case scenario consists in choosing all colorings of an alphabet
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}_{q}
of size
q-1
, that is
\left|{\mathcal{I}(q-1,c)}\right|=\binom{q-1}{c}
many. At this point, only
q-1
pairs are left to be covered and only
\binom{q-1}{c-1}
colorings are left to be chosen all of which containing the element
\alpha:=\mathcal{A}_{q}\setminus\mathcal{B}
. Here we continue with the same idea, by adding all colorings that do not contain one last pair, say
(\alpha,\beta)
for some
\beta\in\mathcal{B}
. Hence only colorings containing the elements
\alpha
and
\beta
are left. Adding any one of them covers the last pair and no more colorings are needed. To sum up, we have a worst case scenario of
T_{\min}(q,c)=\binom{q-1}{c}+\binom{q-2}{c-1}+1
colorings.
∎

V
Conclusion

We introduced an error-free channel model based on subsets of a given size
c
over a
q
-ary alphabet, called
c
-colorings. This channel takes as an input a
q
-ary sequence
\bm{x}
of given length
n
and outputs subsequences of
\bm{x}
consisting only of the symbols lying in the
c
-colorings considered. For given parameters
c,q
and the number
t
of distinct colorings considered, we discussed the information rate and capacity of the corresponding channel model. We proved that maximum information rate is achieved if and only if the
c
-colorings related to the channel form a
(q,c,2)
-covering design. This allowed us to connect the minimum size for which a coloring profile achieves maximum information rate to the minimum covering number of a
(q,c,2)
-covering design. Additionally, we gave an expression for the minimum size
T_{\min}(q,c)
for which any coloring profile of size
T_{\min}(q,c)
achieves maximum information rate.
