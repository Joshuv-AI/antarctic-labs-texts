Asymptomatic tuberculosis in Japan: a 15-year national surveillance study, 2008–2022
Authors: Tomohiko Ukai, Kaung Si Thu, Kazuhiro Uchimura, Akihiro Ohkado, Takashi Yoshiyama, Edine Tiemersma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAsymptomatic tuberculosis (TB), defined as active TB without reported symptoms, remains poorly characterised. We assessed long-term trends, factors associated with asymptomatic diagnosis, and treatment outcomes of asymptomatic TB in Japan using nationwide surveillance data with widespread chest radiographic screening.MethodsWe analysed bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB cases reported to the Japan Tuberculosis Surveillance System between 2008 and 2022. Patients were classified as asymptomatic, respiratory symptomatic, or non-respiratory symptomatic TB. Trends in symptom status were assessed. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Asymptomatic tuberculosis (often referred to as subclinical TB) is increasingly recognised as an important component of the TB disease spectrum. We searched PubMed for studies published from database inception to Mar 2026, using combinations of the terms “tuberculosis”, “asymptomatic”, and “subclinical”, and screened reference lists of relevant articles. We considered epidemiological studies, including prevalence surveys, cohort studies, and other observational studies, as well as systematic reviews, modelling studies, and relevant WHO documents, without restricting by language. The available evidence, largely from high-incidence settings, indicates that a substantial proportion of bacteriologically confirmed TB is symptom-screen negative, but estimates vary because symptom definitions differ, most data are derived from prevalence surveys, and comparability across studies is limited. Evidence remains limited regarding long-term temporal trends, factors associated with asymptomatic rather than symptomatic presentation at diagnosis, markers of potential infectiousness, and treatment outcomes of asymptomatic TB using routine surveillance data in low-incidence settings.

Added value of this study

Using 15 years of nationwide tuberculosis surveillance data from Japan, we provide a large-scale, population-based assessment of asymptomatic bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB in a low-incidence setting with widespread chest radiography-based screening. We show that the proportion of asymptomatic TB increased modestly over time, although the absolute change was small, and that asymptomatic TB accounted for about one-fifth of bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB notifications throughout the study period. We also show that a substantial proportion of asymptomatic cases had sputum smear positivity and/or cavitary disease, and we identify demographic and clinical factors associated with asymptomatic rather than respiratory symptomatic TB. In addition, we show that asymptomatic TB was associated with more favourable treatment outcomes than respiratory symptomatic TB in this surveillance population.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings indicate that asymptomatic TB is a persistent and epidemiologically important component of TB in settings with established surveillance systems and widespread chest radiography-based screening. Reliance on symptom-based case detection alone may miss bacteriologically confirmed cases, including some with sputum smear positivity and/or cavitary disease. These findings support the value of targeted screening and early diagnostic strategies beyond symptom-based approaches and may help inform TB control and elimination strategies in settings where radiographic screening is used alongside routine surveillance.

Introduction

Asymptomatic tuberculosis (TB), previously termed subclinical TB, was newly defined in the 2024 Global TB Report.1 It is characterised as “a person with TB disease who did not report symptoms suggestive of TB during screening”1 and is further classified into bacteriologically confirmed and unconfirmed cases. Asymptomatic TB is recognized as TB disease occurring in individuals without reported symptoms, and recent studies indicate that a subset of these cases may have transmission potential.2,3 In recent years, growing attention has been directed toward its public health implications, particularly in the context of expanded screening and early detection efforts aimed at improving case identification and reducing transmission.2,4

Research on asymptomatic TB remains limited. Previous studies have primarily focused on its prevalence, highlighting that a substantial proportion of total TB cases are asymptomatic.5, 6, 7 A systematic review of multiple TB prevalence surveys conducted in high-incidence countries reported that up to 39.8% patients of bacteriologically confirmed TB do not report cough, and 20.3% report no TB-related symptoms at all.6 However, data on the natural history, clinical management, and outcomes of asymptomatic TB are sparse, and major knowledge gaps in its clinical and programmatic implications were also highlighted by the 2024 WHO consultation on asymptomatic TB.8 A few small cohort studies have yielded conflicting findings regarding prognosis.7,9,10 In particular, longitudinal, population-based data from routine TB surveillance in low-incidence settings—describing temporal trends in asymptomatic TB, factors associated with asymptomatic vs. symptomatic presentation at diagnosis, and treatment outcomes compared with symptomatic TB—remain scarce.

Since 2000, Japan has achieved a steady annual decline in TB incidence, with an approximate 5% reduction per year.11 Throughout the study period, chest radiography-based screening remained widely available in Japan, while nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) was already in use and became increasingly routine in clinical practice over time.12,13 Japan therefore provides a useful setting in which to examine asymptomatic bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB. Because this screening system can identify individuals with radiographic abnormalities before symptom recognition, asymptomatic TB may be detected more frequently than in settings that rely primarily on passive case finding. Individuals with abnormal radiographic findings suggestive of TB are referred for sputum examinations, enabling the identification of asymptomatic TB cases with bacteriological confirmation. All confirmed TB cases are registered in the nationwide Japan Tuberculosis Surveillance (JTBS) system, and managed and followed up by health-care facilities and public health centers.

Using national TB surveillance data from Japan between 2008 and 2022, we aimed to: (1) describe long-term trends in the proportion of asymptomatic TB; (2) identify factors associated with being diagnosed as asymptomatic rather than respiratory symptomatic TB; and (3) compare treatment outcomes between asymptomatic, respiratory symptomatic, and non-respiratory symptomatic TB.

Methods

Study design and data source

This population-based surveillance study includes all newly notified TB cases reported to the JTBS system between January 1, 2008, and December 31, 2022. Established in 1987, the JTBS is a nationwide computerized surveillance system to which all newly diagnosed TB cases are reported.14,15 As TB is a notifiable disease in Japan, case notification is mandatory for all physicians who make a diagnosis of active TB, ensuring comprehensive data coverage. Public health center staff then verifies the physician’s entries, supplements any missing information, and enters the verified and completed data into the system. Patients are managed by clinicians at health-care facilities, and public health center staff support treatment continuation; treatment outcomes are recorded when available.

TB screening in Japan

In Japan, chest radiography-based screening is widely implemented through routine health check-ups, targeted screening programmes, school-based screening, and contact investigations conducted by public health centers.16,17 Although these programmes are not exclusively intended to detect active TB, individuals with radiographic findings suggestive of TB are usually referred for further evaluation, including sputum examination, allowing bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB to be detected regardless of symptoms. This screening infrastructure is large in scale and remained in place throughout the study period.18, 19, 20, 21 Further details on TB screening in Japan are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Japan tuberculosis surveillance data (JTBS)

The JTBS system records demographic and clinical information for all notified active TB and latent TB infection cases requiring treatment in Japan. For this study, the extracted variables included age, sex, country of birth, occupation, treatment history, social assistance, comorbidities, mode of detection, bacteriological results, chest radiographic findings, drug resistance, initial treatment regimen, and treatment outcome. Detailed variable definitions and response categories are provided in the Supplementary Materials. The JTBS records sex as a routine surveillance variable; gender identity was not collected. During the study period, active TB disease in Japan was generally treated with multidrug regimens, most commonly a standard four-drug regimen consisting of isoniazid (INH), rifampicin (RIF) (or rifabutin), pyrazinamide (PZA), and ethambutol (EB) (or streptomycin (SM)), with alternative regimens using three or more drugs when pyrazinamide could not be used. By contrast, regimens such as isoniazid plus rifampicin or isoniazid monotherapy were categorised as treatment for latent TB infection.22,23

Case definitions and inclusion criteria

This study included pulmonary TB patients registered in the JTBS system between 2008 and 2022 who had bacteriological confirmation, defined as a positive sputum NAAT result and/or a positive sputum culture result. No additional exclusion was applied for NAAT-positive cases with previous TB treatment history; previous treatment history was instead included as a covariate in the regression analyses.

Symptom information was obtained through interviewer-administered routine clinical assessment at the time of screening and clinical visits leading to diagnosis. Although no fully standardised question set was used across all settings, symptom assessment was guided by the JTBS manual. The attending physician assessed and recorded whether respiratory or non-respiratory symptoms were present, and public health center staff subsequently reviewed the recorded information and entered the verified data into the JTBS system. In the JTBS, symptom status was defined with reference to the time of the action or clinical encounter that led to TB detection, rather than the time of confirmed diagnosis. Thus, if symptoms were present at that earlier time point but had resolved by the time of diagnosis, the case was still recorded as symptomatic. Patients were categorized into three groups based on the presence of symptoms: respiratory symptomatic TB, defined by recorded respiratory symptoms suggestive of pulmonary TB (cough, sputum, or hemoptysis), with or without non-respiratory symptoms; non-respiratory symptomatic TB, defined by recorded non-respiratory symptoms only (e.g., fever, night sweats, or weight loss); and asymptomatic TB, defined by the absence of recorded respiratory and non-respiratory symptoms.

Statistical analysis

We performed descriptive and multivariable regression analyses. We calculated proportions of asymptomatic and symptomatic (respiratory and non-respiratory) TB between 2008 and 2022, when symptom information was consistently collected. Although NAAT was already available before the study period, its use became more widespread over time. To assess whether temporal patterns were sensitive to changes in microbiological confirmation practices, we also examined trends in a supplementary analysis restricted to culture-positive cases (Supplementary Fig. S1). Temporal trends in symptom were assessed using logistic regression with calendar year as a continuous variable and symptom status as the dependent variable. Odds ratios per year and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were estimated. We repeated these analyses among patients with high infectiousness (smear-positive disease or cavitation). Demographic and clinical characteristics, initial treatment regimens and treatment outcome were summarized for the three groups classified by symptom status, using data from to 2022—a period during which the questionnaire and response options in the JTBS remained unchanged.

We conducted two sets of regression analyses. First, we compared asymptomatic pulmonary TB with respiratory symptomatic pulmonary TB between 2017 and 2022. This analysis was restricted to notified bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB cases within the surveillance system and was intended to identify factors associated with asymptomatic classification within this population, rather than causal determinants in the source population. Candidate covariates were limited to variables available in the JTBS and were selected at the planning stage on the basis of the study objectives, clinical and epidemiological relevance, and data availability, rather than through data-driven selection within the analytic dataset. Age was categorised as 0–39, 40–59, 60–79, or ≥80 years. Sex, occupation, and country of birth were retained as categorical covariates. Selected comorbidity and disease-related variables were recoded into binary indicators for the primary models, including diabetes mellitus, HIV infection, resistance to rifampicin or isoniazid, and receipt of social assistance; detailed original categories are provided in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table S1-2. Treatment history and year of notification were treated as categorical variables. To account for potential clustering by geographic and administrative unit, we created a cluster identifier defined as the cross-classification of prefecture and public health center. The primary analysis estimated adjusted risk ratios (aRRs) and 95% confidence intervals using modified Poisson regression with a log link function, fitted via generalized linear models.24 We used modified Poisson regression to estimate risk ratios directly because the outcome was common and odds ratios from logistic regression would be less interpretable. Cluster-robust (sandwich) standard errors were computed at the prefecture–public health center level to account for within-cluster correlation.25 Age was also examined as a continuous variable using restricted cubic splines with four degrees of freedom. For visualisation, adjusted risk ratios by age were plotted relative to age 20 years, representing the younger reference group used in the categorical analysis, while holding all other covariates at their modal or median values. Covariates were entered simultaneously in the primary model because the aim was to estimate associations adjusted for measured baseline characteristics, rather than to derive a parsimonious model through data-driven variable selection. To reduce the risk of overadjustment, variables more likely to reflect the diagnostic pathway or disease presentation—including mode of case detection, chest X-ray findings, and sputum smear status—were not included in the primary model but were examined separately in additional or sensitivity analyses. A conceptual directed acyclic graph illustrating this framework is provided in Supplementary Fig. S2. Additional analyses included a model incorporating mode of case detection; sensitivity analyses incorporating chest X-ray findings and sputum smear status; multinomial logistic regression including all three symptom groups; standard logistic regression; multilevel logistic regression with a random intercept for prefecture–public health center cluster; and a model additionally adjusted for COVID-19 pandemic period (2020–2022 vs. 2017–2019).

Second, we assessed prognosis among asymptomatic TB, respiratory symptomatic TB, and non-respiratory symptomatic TB cases diagnosed between 2017 and 2022. The outcome was mortality during treatment, coded as 1 for death and 0 for treatment success, based on treatment results recorded by public health centers. Patients whose outcomes were recorded as treatment failure, lost to follow-up, transferred out, under treatment, or unknown were excluded from the primary mortality analysis because death vs. treatment success could not be reliably determined. Covariates included continuous age (modelled as restricted cubic splines with four degrees of freedom), sex, occupation, nationality, diabetes mellitus, HIV infection, treatment history, receipt of public assistance, drug resistance, and year of diagnosis. The same cluster identifier and modelling approach (modified Poisson regression with cluster-robust standard errors) were used. As sensitivity analyses, we additionally adjusted for variables related to the diagnostic process or disease severity—mode of case detection, chest X-ray findings, sputum smear results, and initial treatment regimen—first individually and then jointly, and examined changes in adjusted risk ratios and 95% confidence intervals to assess the robustness of the main findings. We also performed two additional sensitivity analyses: one treating lost to follow-up as death, and another using a composite unfavourable outcome including death, treatment failure, lost to follow-up, or transfer out; cases recorded as under treatment or other were excluded from the latter analysis.

For regression analyses, observations were not excluded because of missingness in the included covariates, as core surveillance items in the JTBS are routinely recorded and unknown or not tested responses are captured as explicit categories where applicable. For selected variables, these responses were incorporated into predefined analytic categories for regression modelling. In sensitivity analyses, bacteriological and radiographic variables were entered using grouped analytic categories rather than being treated as missing (e.g., sputum smear results: positive, negative, or other; chest X-ray findings: non-cavity lesion, cavity, none, previously treated, other, or unknown).

Because this was a population-based surveillance study including all eligible notified cases during the study period, no formal sample size calculation was performed. All models were estimated in R (version 4.4.2) using the glm function for Poisson regression, with cluster-robust variance estimates computed via the sandwich package.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Research Institute of Tuberculosis (approval number: RIT/IRB 2024-08; approved on August 1, 2024). The study used de-identified routine surveillance data provided by the data custodian. Individual-level data are not publicly available; therefore, informed consent was not required.

Results

Between 2008 and 2022, a total of 141,481 bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB cases were registered in the JTBS system. Of these, 28,552 cases (20.2%) were classified as having asymptomatic TB, 86,744 (61.3%) as respiratory symptomatic TB, and 26,185 (18.5%) as non-respiratory symptomatic TB.

The proportion of asymptomatic pulmonary TB increased modestly over time (odds ratio per year 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.02), as did the proportion of non-respiratory symptomatic TB (OR per year 1.03, 95% CI 1.03–1.03), while the proportion of respiratory symptomatic TB declined (OR per year 0.97, 95% CI 0.97–0.97) (Fig. 1A). However, the absolute change in the proportion of asymptomatic TB was small, increasing only from approximately 18%–22% during the study period. When analyses were restricted to patients with high infectiousness (smear-positive or cavitary disease), temporal patterns were similar, with asymptomatic TB consistently accounting for approximately 12–15% of cases throughout the study period (Fig. 1B).

Between 2017 and 2022, 54,525 pulmonary TB cases were included in the regression analyses: 31,510 (57.8%) respiratory symptomatic TB, 11,106 (20.4%) non-respiratory symptomatic TB, and 11,909 (21.8%) asymptomatic TB.

Table 1 summarizes the demographic and clinical characteristics of the three groups. Compared with respiratory symptomatic TB cases, asymptomatic TB patients were younger, more often foreign-born, more likely to be students, health-care workers, and full-time employees. They were also more frequently detected through health checkups and contact investigations. Diabetes mellitus, HIV infection, cavitary lesions, and social assistance were less common in asymptomatic TB, whereas resistance to INH or RFP was slightly more common. Asymptomatic TB cases were less likely than respiratory symptomatic TB cases to have sputum smear positivity (34.1% vs. 69.3%) or cavitary disease (20.3% vs. 36.1%). Compared with non-respiratory symptomatic TB cases, asymptomatic TB patients were also younger and more often detected through health checkups and contact investigations.

Treatment success was achieved in 19,423 (61.6%) respiratory symptomatic TB cases, 5960 (53.7%) non-respiratory symptomatic TB cases, and 9312 (78.2%) asymptomatic TB cases; the corresponding numbers of deaths during treatment were 9155 (29.1%), 4192 (37.7%), and 1377 (11.6%), respectively. Other treatment outcomes, including treatment failure, loss to follow-up, transfer out, and ongoing treatment, are shown in Supplementary Table S1-2. For the primary mortality analysis, we included 28,578 respiratory symptomatic cases, 10,152 non-respiratory symptomatic cases, and 10,689 asymptomatic cases with definitive outcomes of treatment success or death.

In the multivariable analysis (Table 2), the risk of being diagnosed with asymptomatic TB was higher among students (aRR 1.35, 95% CI: 1.22–1.50), health-care workers (aRR 1.32, 95% CI: 1.20–1.45), and full-time employees (aRR 1.31, 95% CI: 1.20–1.43). Increased risk was also observed among individuals with a history of TB treatment (aRR 1.11, 95% CI: 1.03–1.19). By contrast, the risk was lower among unemployed individuals (aRR 0.83, 95% CI: 0.75–0.92), those with diabetes (aRR 0.94, 95% CI: 0.89–0.98), those with HIV infection (aRR 0.41, 95% CI: 0.23–0.72), and those receiving public assistance (aRR 0.85, 95% CI: 0.80–0.91). When mode of detection was additionally included in the model, detection through health check-ups and contact investigation showed substantially higher likelihoods of asymptomatic TB compared with detection at medical institutions (Table 2). In the spline curve analysis, younger individuals had a higher relative risk of being diagnosed with asymptomatic TB (Fig. 2).

In sensitivity analyses, retreatment history and occupation (students and health-care workers) remained positively associated with asymptomatic TB, whereas HIV infection consistently showed a negative association (Supplementary Table S2-1). In a supplementary multinomial logistic regression including all three symptom groups, the overall pattern was similar, and mode of detection was strongly associated with asymptomatic TB but less strongly with non-respiratory symptomatic TB (Supplementary Table S2-2). In additional sensitivity analyses using standard logistic regression and multilevel logistic regression, the overall pattern and direction of associations were materially unchanged (Supplementary Table S2-3). Further adjustment for pandemic period (2020–2022 vs. 2017–2019) did not materially change the overall pattern or direction of associations, although the pandemic period itself showed a small positive association with asymptomatic classification (Supplementary Table S2-4).

As shown in Table 3, with respiratory symptomatic TB as the reference group, the adjusted risk ratio (aRR) for death was 0.57 (95% CI: 0.54–0.60) for asymptomatic TB and 1.11 (95% CI: 1.08–1.14) for non-respiratory symptomatic TB. These associations were materially unchanged in sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for mode of detection, sputum smear status, radiographic findings, and treatment regimen, as well as in analyses using alternative outcome definitions (Supplementary Tables S3-1 and 3-2).

Discussion

In this nationwide analysis of TB surveillance data in Japan, we observed that the proportion of asymptomatic pulmonary TB increased slightly over time, but remained around one-fifth of all pulmonary TB cases throughout the study period. Asymptomatic TB also consistently accounted for 12–15% of cases with sputum smear positivity or cavitary disease. Overall, 38.7% of reported pulmonary TB cases lacked respiratory symptoms, and 20.2% were entirely asymptomatic. Among asymptomatic patients, 34.1% had positive sputum smear results and 20.3% had cavitary disease, indicating that absence of symptoms did not preclude potential infectiousness.

Previous studies have highlighted the substantial contribution of asymptomatic TB to TB prevalence. A systematic review of TB prevalence surveys in high-burden countries found that up to 39.8% of patients with bacteriologically confirmed TB did not report cough, and 20.3% presented with no TB-related symptoms.6 Findings from middle-TB-incidence countries are similar; for example, a study in South Korea reported that 19.3% of the notified TB patients reported no symptoms.9 In low-TB-incidence settings, a cohort study in Canada found that 21% of bacteriologically confirmed TB cases were asymptomatic, though definitions and study populations varied.26 The proportion observed in our study aligns with these previous reports. Notably, the proportion of asymptomatic TB in Japan remained around 20% over the past decade, although the total number of notified bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB cases declined over time. These findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting the composition of notified bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB cases rather than absolute case numbers. Importantly, even among patients with high infectiousness, 12–15% were asymptomatic throughout the study period. This finding indicates that the presence of high bacillary burden does not reliably correspond to symptom onset. Taken together, these observations reinforce that asymptomatic TB represents a persistent and globally relevant entity, including among individuals who may contribute disproportionately to transmission.

Factors associated with being diagnosed with asymptomatic TB, compared with respiratory symptomatic TB, included occupation, particularly student status, health-care work, and full-time employees—occupations more likely to undergo routine screening, younger age, and a history of retreatment. Interestingly, one previous study reported that older age was associated with asymptomatic TB,27 whereas another study, consistent with our findings, reported younger age as a risk factor.9 We speculate that the former study defined symptoms only by the presence of cough, thereby classifying many non-respiratory symptomatic cases as “asymptomatic”, whereas such symptoms are more likely to occur among older individuals. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report an association between retreatment history and asymptomatic TB. We speculate that these patients were detected at an earlier stage of disease, which may reflect closer clinical follow-up among individuals with a history of TB treatment.28 However, this association may also partly reflect differences in diagnostic pathways, including the possibility that some NAAT-positive retreatment cases without culture confirmation reflected residual DNA from previous disease rather than current active tuberculosis.

The prognosis of asymptomatic TB remains uncertain, and previous studies comparing treatment outcomes between asymptomatic and symptomatic TB have been limited and inconsistent.9,10 In this surveillance population, asymptomatic TB was associated with better outcomes than respiratory symptomatic TB after adjustment for relevant covariates, and this pattern remained robust in sensitivity analyses. In Japan, active TB disease is generally treated with a standard four-drug regimen, although alternative regimens with three or more drugs are also used when pyrazinamide cannot be used. In our study, 95.0% of asymptomatic TB patients received three or more anti-TB drugs, consistent with treatment for active TB disease, and had more favourable outcomes.

Consistent with prior research, a substantial subset of asymptomatic TB cases in our study were sputum smear positive, raising concerns about their potential role in TB transmission. Specifically, 34.1% of asymptomatic TB cases were smear-positive at diagnosis. This finding suggests that, despite the absence of symptoms, a considerable proportion of asymptomatic TB patients may remain infectious. A study from Vietnam using tuberculin skin testing (TST) similarly indicated that smear-positive asymptomatic individuals may contribute to ongoing transmission.29 Furthermore, a modelling study estimated that up to 68% of new TB infections could be attributable to transmission from individuals with asymptomatic disease.30 Taken together, these findings highlight that reliance on symptoms alone may miss a meaningful share of bacteriologically confirmed TB, including cases with smear positivity and/or cavitary disease. Further research is needed to clarify the extent and mechanisms of transmission from asymptomatic TB, including its relative infectiousness compared with symptomatic TB, through household transmission studies, molecular epidemiology, and modelling.

The more favourable prognosis of asymptomatic TB should not be interpreted as limited public health relevance. Despite better outcomes, a meaningful proportion of asymptomatic cases were smear-positive or had cavitary disease, indicating potential infectiousness at diagnosis. Asymptomatic TB may therefore remain a programmatically important blind spot under symptom-based screening strategies alone.

This study has several limitations. First, symptoms were based on routine clinical assessment and patient self-report and may have been underreported or concealed, and symptom ascertainment may have varied across health-care workers and clinical settings. A substantial proportion of cases classified as asymptomatic were detected in medical care settings, including during hospitalisation or outpatient follow-up for other conditions, suggesting that some may have been identified incidentally during routine clinical care rather than through symptom-driven consultation. However, the surveillance data did not record the specific reason for medical visits, and some degree of symptom misclassification cannot be excluded. In addition, a detailed breakdown of individual non-respiratory symptoms was not available in the surveillance dataset. Second, because this analysis was conditional on case detection and bacteriological confirmation within routine surveillance, the observed associations may partly reflect screening intensity and diagnostic pathways and should therefore be interpreted as associations within notified bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB cases rather than causal determinants in the source population. Third, our study included only bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB cases. Therefore, pulmonary TB cases without bacteriological confirmation, including those diagnosed clinically on the basis of radiographic findings, contact investigation, or other clinical information, were not captured. As a result, the true burden of asymptomatic pulmonary TB may have been underestimated. In addition, the clinical indication for treatment (e.g., active tuberculosis disease or latent tuberculosis infection) was not directly recorded in the surveillance dataset, although regimens categorised as treatment for latent tuberculosis infection were rare in this study population. Fourth, estimates may be influenced by lead-time and length-bias inherent to radiographic screening, which could partly account for lower mortality in asymptomatic cases. In addition, time-to-event analyses were not possible because detailed individual follow-up time was not available in the surveillance data. Excluding patients lost to follow-up from the primary mortality analysis may also have introduced bias if some of these individuals subsequently died. However, the overall pattern of associations was materially unchanged in sensitivity analyses treating lost to follow-up as death and in analyses using a composite unfavourable outcome. Fifth, although access to microbiological confirmation such as NAAT increased over time and chest radiography-based screening remained in place throughout the study period in Japan, we could not assess whether the intensity or coverage of screening changed over time. Such changes may have influenced the detection of different forms of bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary tuberculosis, including both asymptomatic and symptomatic cases. However, when we restricted the analysis to culture-positive cases only, temporal trends were largely unchanged (Supplementary Fig. S1), suggesting that our main findings are unlikely to be fully explained by evolving diagnostic technologies. Sixth, residual confounding by unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. In particular, environmental factors such as temperature and humidity were not available in the surveillance dataset and therefore could not be incorporated into the present analysis. Finally, because Japan has an extensive radiographic screening infrastructure and a relatively low TB incidence, the generalisability of our findings to high-burden or low-resource settings may be limited; however, the mechanisms we describe—such as detection pathways and prognosis according to symptom status—are likely to be relevant to other countries expanding active case-finding.

The Japanese experience may offer insights for settings with extensive radiographic screening and well-established surveillance systems, particularly where asymptomatic TB may be detected outside symptom-based pathways and may represent an under-recognised component of transmission. This perspective challenges the adequacy of symptom-based, passive case-finding approaches and suggests that asymptomatic TB should be considered within the broader transmission continuum, with implications for screening strategies in contemporary TB control.

In conclusion, asymptomatic TB persisted as a stable yet substantial component of Japan’s TB burden over 15 years. Despite the absence of symptoms, a meaningful proportion of asymptomatic cases were smear-positive or had cavitary disease, suggesting potential for transmission. Taken together, these findings highlight the need to refine screening and surveillance strategies and to consider the programmatic role of asymptomatic TB in settings where case detection relies not only on symptoms but also on routine radiographic screening.
