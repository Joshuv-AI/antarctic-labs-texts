Empathic Accuracy and Behavioral Empathy After a Moderate Dose of Beer
Authors: Isabelle Cristina Baltariu, Violeta Enea, Teresa B. Prucker, Marije aan het Rot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesEmpathic accuracy (EA) represents a person's ability to correctly infer the emotions of another person. One past study in men found lower EA for positive emotions after a moderate dose of hard liquor compared to after placebo, particularly among non‐hazardous drinkers. The present study aimed to replicate this finding, using a moderate dose of beer and a mixed design. Moreover, we added a novel measure of behavioral empathy.MethodsParticipants (62% men) completed the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test, Empathy Quotient, and Revised Drinking Motives Questionnaire. Before and after drinking either beer (6.6% alcohol, n = 28) or its 0.0% equivalent (placebo, n = 38), they watched videoclips of targets talking about emotional autobiographical events and rated how targets felt while talking. Behavioral empathy was assessed by presenting participants with painful scenarios and asking whether they would help the people in pain.ResultsThe previous finding of lower EA for positive emotions after alcohol was not replicated. Nonetheless, after alcohol non‐hazardous drinkers were less willing to help people in pain. Trait affective empathy (i.e., a person's long‐term disposition to experience emotional responses congruent to another's feelings) and the drinking to cope motive did not significantly moderate the findings.ConclusionsAlthough alcohol may alter empathy, its effects are not consistent and may depend on the dose and type of alcohol consumed. The moderating roles of trait empathy and trait aggressivity deserves further study.

Introduction

Alcohol is widely used in social situations and has been called a “social lubricant” (Monahan and Lannutti 2000). At the same time, drinking alcohol is frequently associated with aggression, particularly when consumed in larger quantities (Ito et al. 1996; Ritter et al. 2019; Sontate et al. 2021). To help understand how this negative outcome of drinking might emerge, we examined how drinking alcohol may affect social cognition, specifically empathy.

Social cognition is a broad term that describes processes such as emotion recognition, Theory of Mind, and empathy. Chronic alcohol misuse is generally associated with social cognition impairments (Bora and Zorlu 2017). A causal relation might exist, with prolonged alcohol use causing deficits in prefrontal cortex functioning, thus negatively affecting the brain's abilities to process salient social cues (Clements and Schumacher 2010; Valmas et al. 2014). This may contribute to interpersonal problems (Maurage et al. 2011), these in turn may lead to continued drinking and thereby worsen prefrontal deficits.

In accordance with the idea that drinking negatively affects social cognition, experimental studies have examined the acute effects of alcohol on both facial emotion recognition and empathy. Facial emotion recognition studies tend to show that lower alcohol doses can improve the processing of others' emotions, particularly happiness, while higher doses are more likely to impair the processing of fear, sadness, and anger (for a review, see Baltariu et al. 2023). As facial emotion recognition is considered a key component of empathy (Baron‐Cohen et al. 1997; Golan and Baron‐Cohen 2006; Preston and de Waal 2002) it is unsurprising that other experimental studies have shown alcohol‐induced impairments in empathy (Battista et al. 2014; Francis et al. 2019; Hu et al. 2018; Karlén et al. 2019), although null findings have also been reported (Baltariu et al. 2023). Of note, these studies have included measures aiming to assess emotional responses to others' feelings (i.e., affective empathy) and measures aiming to assess the intellectual understanding of others' feelings (i.e., cognitive empathy) (Decety 2010).

To study social cognition after acute alcohol administration, subjective (Laghi et al. 2019; Lannoy et al. 2020) as well as objective measures have been used. Objective measures mainly include the aforementioned facial emotion recognition tasks (Attwood et al. 2009; Craig et al. 2009; Johnson et al. 2018; Stevens et al. 2008). As these tasks generally involve static face pictures, they are limited in their ecological validity. In comparison, empathic accuracy tasks (EATs) have been developed to present participants with short video clips including male and female targets narrating personal life events. They ask participants to continuously rate how negative or positive the target is feeling while narrating the event. All targets previously rated their own videos in a similar way. The correlation between participants' ratings of the targets' feelings and targets' own ratings of their feelings was considered a measure of cognitive empathy or, more specifically, empathic accuracy (EA). These tasks offer more ecological validity than facial emotion recognition tasks by dynamically presenting participants with situations that are closer to real life experiences (Hogenelst et al. 2015).

Thiel et al. (2018) compared men who received a moderate dose of hard liquor (0.56 g alcohol/kg body weight) to men who received a placebo beverage on the EAT, administered after drinking. There was no significant effect of alcohol on EA for negative videos. The main finding was that EA for positive videos was lower after alcohol compared to placebo, specifically among participants who identified as non‐hazardous drinkers on the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT). Among participants who identified as hazardous drinkers (and who drank more), this was not found. These findings were explained using the low level of response model (Schuckit et al. 2004), which proposed that individuals with low alcohol tolerance are more prone to developing alcohol dependence. Alternatively, it is possible that people drinking at hazardous levels have higher tolerance for the effects of alcohol.

The present study is a conceptual replication of Thiel et al. (2018). However, their study was limited in that it used a between‐subjects design without a baseline measure of EA (i.e., no repeated measures). Further, although the EAT used is considered an ecologically valid measure of cognitive empathy (Hogenelst et al. 2015), it is unclear what reduced EA means for behavioral empathy. Furthermore, it is unclear whether results obtained using hard liquor are generalizable to other alcoholic beverages, such as beer. Most experimental alcohol studies have used hard liquor and very few have used beer (although see Dolder et al. 2017). At the same time, beer is consumed more frequently, especially in social settings (Grigg 2004; Gartner et al. 2019). Besides, people have different perceptions of beer versus hard liquor (Unwin 1992) and different expectancies regarding their impact on behavior (Lang et al. 1983) which may be relevant in relation to their effect on empathy.

Therefore, in the present study we examined the effects of a moderate dose of alcoholic beer versus placebo on EA, using the same EAT as Thiel et al. (2018) before and after drinking (mixed design). We also included a novel measure of behavioral empathy. Preliminary data in support of the validity of this behavioral empathy measure, which asks about helping others in pain, were obtained before (Baltariu et al. 2024). On the basis of Thiel et al. (2018), we primarily hypothesized that drinking beer would worsen EAT performance for positive but not negative videos, specifically among individuals who identified as non‐hazardous drinkers. We also expected the effect of beer on behavioral empathy to be larger in non‐hazardous drinkers.

We intended to recruit equivalent numbers of men and women, for the purpose of studying whether gender might moderate the effects on EA, as well on behavioral empathy. However, our final sample included more men than women (see Method). Therefore, instead of conducting moderator analyses, we conducted analyses both in the full sample and in men only.

Method

Ethics Statement

The study was designed in accordance with the Dutch national ethics code for research in the behavioral and social sciences (see www.nethics.nl) and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Department of Psychology at the University of Groningen. Participants received detailed written and oral information about the study and signed an informed consent form before starting the study procedures. We tested women for pregnancy in consultation with the Ethics Committee. They were informed about this during the consent procedure. The testing was done in private.

Participants

A sample of 66 individuals (62% men, mean age of 25 years, range 19–39 years) took part in this study. The study aimed to replicate the results of Thiel et al., who in a sample of 44 men found a large effect for alcohol on empathic accuracy, Cohen's d = 0.72. To be able to detect a significant condition by gender interaction we aimed at recruiting at least 100 participants. We were not able to obtain this sample, which we attribute to the COVID‐19 pandemic and to our decision to exclude women using hormonal contraceptives (see below). Hormonal contraceptive use was an exclusion criterion because the current evidence supports a negativity bias in emotion recognition (Lewis et al. 2019).

Recruitment took place locally via advertisements in Facebook groups; student newsletters; posters in university buildings, supermarkets; and other online media. Individuals who indicated their interest to participate were submitted to the screening phase. We only included adults fluent in Dutch and excluded 8 women who used hormonal contraceptives. We also excluded people with a current or past diagnosis or treatment for alcohol dependence (n = 2); past hospitalization for psychiatric treatment or current ongoing outpatient psychiatric treatment (n = 8); current use of psychoactive or other contraindicated prescription medications (e.g., Monoamine Oxidase inhibitors, medications for diabetes) (n = 5); contraindicated medical conditions (e.g., pancreatitis, ulcers), and other known neurological or physical impairments that may affect psychomotor abilities. These criteria were assessed using self‐report.

Each participant was scheduled for one single appointment, in line with the between‐groups setup for condition (alcohol vs. placebo) of our study. During the recruitment phase, the researchers instructed the participants before the appointment not to use drugs or drink more than 2 drinks the night before the appointment took place. This was verbally checked before they arrived in the lab and if necessary, participants were rescheduled (but no instances occurred). Women were only scheduled to be tested on days 5–21 of their menstrual cycle, in line with previous research (Derntl et al. 2013). Several participants required rescheduling due to Covid‐19 infections.

Upon arrival in the lab, recruits were provided with full information about the study, asked to provide informed consent, and women were tested for pregnancy. Participants' weight was recorded by using a scale. All eligible recruits consented to participate, and none were found to be pregnant. No formal drug screen was done, but participants provided baseline alcohol levels (see Procedure) and no one was excluded on the basis of this.

Baseline Questionnaires

Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test—AUDIT

The AUDIT (Babor et al. 2001) measures both the quantity of drinking (“How often do you have a drink?”) and possible negative drinking outcomes (e.g., “How often have you failed to do what was normally expected of you because of drinking?”) in the past year. Per item the participant got a score from 0 (low) to 4 (high). A total score of 13 or higher for women and 15 or higher for men signals a possible alcohol use disorder (AUD). More generally, higher AUDIT scores are considered an indicator for more hazardous drinking. The internal consistency in this sample was good (α = 0.80).

The Drinking Motives Questionnaire—DMQ‐R

The DMQ‐R (Cooper 1994) includes 20 different reasons for drinking that can be summarized as follows: (1) socializing: (e.g., “to be sociable”), (2) coping with negative affect (e.g., “to forget your worries”), (3) enhancement of positive affect (e.g., “because it gives you a pleasant feeling”) and (4) conforming to others (e.g., “to fit in with a group you like”). The answers are recorded on a 5‐point Likert scale with 1 = “almost never/never”, 3 = “half of the time”, and 5 = “almost always/always”. Grant et al. (2007) reported good psychometric qualities for the DMQ‐R. In this sample the internal consistency was acceptable for the socializing (α = 0.78), conformity (α = 0.74) and enhancing subscales (α = 0.76), and poor for the coping subscale (α = 0.58).

Quantity—Frequency Measure

Participants were asked How many standard drinks did you have on average (e.g.,) Monday in the past month? This was asked regarding each day of the week. A drink was defined as a standard‐sized can/bottle of beer or glass of wine, one shot of liquor, or any mixed drink per shot of liquor. A standard—sized glass of beer in The Netherlands is 250 mL at 5% Alcohol By Volume (ABV), a standard glass of wine is 100 mL (at 12% ABV), or a standard shot of spirits is 35 mL (at 35% ABV) (Jellinek 2024).

From these data we assessed the number of drinks a participant had in the past month on each day of the week, thus generating a quantity measure. The number of drinking days per week was also derived from this, providing a frequency measure.

The Empathy Quotient—EQ

Empathy was measured with a 28‐item version of the Empathy Quotient (Groen et al. 2015). There are reversed items to prevent response bias. Items are measured on a 4‐point Likert scale that ranges from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”. The total score can range from 0–22 with higher scores representing higher trait levels of empathy. Two subscales measure (1) trait cognitive empathy (e.g., “I can easily tell if someone wants to enter the conversation”) and, (2) trait affective empathy (e.g., “I really enjoy caring for other people”). The internal consistency of the cognitive and affective subscales were good (α = 0.81), and acceptable (α = 0.74) respectively.

Outcome Measures

Empathic Accuracy Task (EAT)

Participants were presented with a computer task designed by aan het Rot and Hogenelst (2014). The task consists of short videos in which targets tell a positive or negative autobiographical story. There were two versions of 15 videos each. We planned for participants to see them in counterbalanced order before versus after drinking using a Latin squares design, but because some participant numbers were skipped due to rescheduled sessions the distribution of the versions was not completely balanced (47% got Version 1 followed by Version 2, 53% the other way around). During the task all videos in one version were presented in pseudorandom order with not more than 2 videos of the same valence in a row. Participants were asked to continuously rate how the target felt while telling their story by using a dial that corresponded to a 9‐Point Likert scale on the screen where 1 represented “extremely negative” and 9 “extremely positive”. For analysis, these valence ratings were averaged over 5‐s intervals. Then correlation coefficients between these participant ratings and the previously determined (i.e., during task development) target ratings were calculated for each clip. The Fisher z transformation of these coefficients was considered a measure of EA.

Behavioral Empathy Measure

Participants were presented with scenarios derived from 96 stories previously used by Bruneau et al. (2013), 48 scenarios involved high levels of physical or emotional pain and 48 comparable scenarios involved low levels of pain. The 96 scenarios were divided into 4 scenarios versions of 24, with 12 scenarios of each type. Each participant read scenarios version 1 or 2 before drinking and scenarios version 3 or 4 after drinking. Participants were asked to indicate whether they would try to help the person in each scenario, using a sliding scale from 0 to 100. Higher scores on the high‐pain scenarios were considered an indicator of more behavioral empathy, and for the low‐pain scenarios they were considered an indication of more general helping behavior. In a previous study (Baltariu et al. 2024), we found that behavioral empathy showed a weak positive correlation with cognitive empathy assessed using the EQ and a moderately positive correlation with affective empathy, while general helping showed a very weak positive correlation with cognitive empathy and no significant correlation with affective empathy, thus supporting the idea that the two measures tap into different constructs. The average internal consistency for the behavioral empathy measure was good (α = 0.81) and for the general helping measure (α = 0.88).

Positive and Negative Affect Scale—PANAS

To measure the affective state of participants we used a version of the PANAS (Clark and Watson 1994) that included 5 items measuring positive affect and 5 items measuring negative affect. Participants were asked to rate the items using a 5‐point Likert scale that ranged from “not at all” to “extremely”. Internal consistency of the PANAS subscales was previously reported to be high (α = 0.83–0.90; Clark and Watson 1994). In the current sample, the average internal consistency across all time points was poor for negative affect (α = 0.59) and good for positive affect (α = 0.82).

Urge to Drink—U2D

Participants were asked to indicate to what extent they felt that the statement “I feel like drinking an alcoholic drink (beer, wine, liquor)” was true for them. They used a 100‐point slider scale ranging from “Not at all” to “Extremely”.

Procedure

The study took place between November 2021 and July 2022. This was longer than expected and due to the Covid‐19 pandemic. No participants were scheduled during lockdown periods, and outside lockdown periods—when local regulations were permitting laboratory studies, albeit with some protection measures—25 sessions were rescheduled, and 8 canceled altogether, due to infections in participants or researchers. Figure 1 summarizes the study flow, which includes a between groups element (alcohol vs placebo) and a repeated‐measures component (pre vs post drinking).

Pre‐Drinking Phase (Baseline)

The participant was seated in a private room with a desk, office chair, desktop computer, and a separate comfortable chair. A non‐blinded researcher (NB) noted down the condition (alcohol vs placebo) as well as a blinded researcher's (B) guess of the condition.The latter was done to gain insight into B's expectations about the prepared drink. NB prepared the drink in a separate room (see drinking phase) and divided it equally over 2 non‐transparent cups covered by a lid and accompanied by a straw. B administered the baseline questionnaires, EAT, and behavioral empathy measure. This phase took about 50 minutes.

Drinking Phase

Upon completion of the tasks, B was given the cups, guided the participant through (double‐blind) drinking, and encouraged the participant to drink each cup in about 15 minutes (30 minutes total for both cups). All participants were told that they would consume a moderate dose of alcoholic blond beer from a popular Belgian brand. In reality participants were allocated to either alcoholic beer (6.6% alcohol) or to the non‐alcoholic equivalent that is also commercially available (0.0% alcohol; placebo), using a block randomization procedure stratified by gender. In both conditions, participants received a weight‐adjusted volume of beer calculated such that the experimental group received 0.4 g alcohol/kg body weight (i.e., between 403 and 1096 mL) while the control group got a comparable amount (between 353 and 749 mL) of non‐alcoholic beer. In all, 42% of men were in the alcohol condition, and 32% of women. This phase took around 40 minutes.

Post‐Drinking Phase

During this phase, NB would come into the room every 10 minutes and take breath alcohol level (BAL) readings using a calibrated breathalyzer. Immediately after consuming the beverage, the participant had a 10‐min break in which they could use the bathroom. This procedure was identical to Thiel et al. (2018). After that they were instructed to re‐complete the EAT and the behavioral empathy measure. After completion, the participant completed two more questionnaires, namely the Toronto Alexithymia Scale and the Reactive‐Proactive Aggression Questionnaire. This phase took roughly 45 minutes.

Debriefing

As soon as they finished with the study measures, participants learned that there were two conditions. Then they were asked by B which condition they thought they had been allocated to and the actual condition was revealed by NB. Placebo participants could leave the lab immediately. Participants in the alcohol condition were offered snacks and water and instructed to stay in the lab until their BAL was ≤ 0.02 mg/L. On average this took 90 minutes per participant.

Statistical Analysis

To analyze the data, we used R Studio version 4.2.3 (R Core Team 2023) including the lme4 package for linear mixed effects modeling. To test our hypothesis that there is an alcohol effect on EA for positive videos in non‐hazardous drinkers, we first ran the models used by Thiel et al. (2018), that is, using the post‐drinking data only. Three separate two‐level models were constructed with (Fisher z transformed) EA scores as outcome variable, participants modeled at level 2, and film‐clip valence, target gender, and condition, respectively, modeled at level 1 (models 1–3). Condition was a between‐groups factor (alcohol vs placebo). In model 4, a cross‐level interaction term was created between condition and mean centered continuous AUDIT scores, in order to test hazardous drinking as a moderator. The random effects were the intercept and the residual at level 2.

In light of the mixed design of our present study, we also tested our hypothesis on the full dataset, including time (a within‐person factor: pre‐drinking, post‐drinking) at level 1. We examined the following predictors as fixed effects at level 1: (model 5) valence, (model 6) target gender, (model 7) EAT version, (model 8) time, (model 9) condition by time interaction, and (model 10) the three‐way interaction condition*time*AUDIT in order to test hazardous drinking as a moderator. Valence (model 5) and target gender (model 6) were tested as independent predictors in line with the post‐drinking analyses described above. EAT version (model 7) was tested as an independent predictor because it was associated with time (model 8). In model 9 a cross‐level interaction term was created between condition and time, and in model 10 an additional cross‐level interaction term was created between condition, time and mean centered AUDIT‐scores. The random effects were the intercept and the residual at level 2.

To test the effect of alcohol on behavioral empathy, we had behavioral empathy as outcome instead of EA, participants were modeled at level 2, scenarios version (model 11) and the cross‐level interaction between condition and time (model 12) and the three‐way interaction condition*time*AUDIT (model 13). Scenarios version (model 11) was tested as an independent predictor in line with the EAT analysis. In model 12 a cross‐level interaction term was created between condition and time, and in model 13 a cross‐level interaction term was created between condition, time and mean centered AUDIT scores. Again, random effects were the intercept and the residual at level 2.

Discussion

Our primary aim was to replicate Thiel et al. (2018), who tested a male sample on the EAT after administering either a moderate dose of hard liquor or placebo and found that EA for the positive videos was lower after alcohol, at least in participants without hazardous drinking levels according to the AUDIT. In comparison, in the present study we administered a moderate dose of alcoholic beer or a 0% alcohol equivalent to both men and women. We administered the EAT both before and after drinking. Further, we added a novel behavioral empathy measure (Baltariu et al. 2024).

When replicating the analyses by Thiel et al. (2018) in the post‐drinking data, we did not find the same alcohol effect; there was no evidence that EA for positive clips was lower in the alcohol condition than in the placebo condition, neither in the full sample nor in the male subsample. There was some indication that men's EA for negative clips was higher after alcohol, but this effect was unexpected, and we believe there was a confounding effect of task version, as one version yielded higher EA scores than the other version. Indeed, while additional analyses in the repeated‐measures data initially also suggested an effect for condition on EA for negative clips, this effect was no longer significant when task version was included as a covariate. All in all, we found no convincing evidence that drinking beer altered EA.

We had expected to replicate results by Thiel et al. (2018), particularly since our repeated‐measures design provided increased statistical power. The non‐replication could be explained in multiple ways. Firstly, while our full sample was larger than the sample used by Thiel et al. (2018), the number of male participants was smaller. Secondly, we administered a lower dose of alcoholic beer (0.4 g/kg), versus 0.52 g/kg of hard liquor. Also, we still observed a higher peak BAL (0.56 vs. 0.25 mg/L). However, other experimental studies have found effects of alcohol on other social cognition measures with doses as low as 0.20 g/kg (Attwood et al. 2009; Nagar et al. 2021). This finding contrasts with established research, which typically reports higher peak BALs following the intake of higher‐concentration alcoholic beverages like vodka compared to lower‐concentration beverages like beer (Mitchell et al. 2014). One possible explanation would be that the carbonation in beer, alike in champagne (Ridout et al. 2003) could enhance the rate of alcohol absorption, leading to a quicker rise in BAL. Thus, EAT might have been completed at a later point on the BAL curve in the past study.

At the same time, other experimental studies have also failed to find an effect of alcohol on empathy and other aspects of social cognition. Firstly, Dolder et al. (2017) found no effect of alcoholic beer on another cognitive empathy measure. However, the alcohol dose used in that study was lower (0.24–0.29 g/kg). Secondly, there are multiple studies that found no effect of a moderate alcohol dose (0.40–0.80 g/kg) of hard liquor on facial emotion recognition (e.g., Padula et al. 2011; Paulus et al. 2012; Walter et al. 2011).

Overall, the results of these experimental alcohol studies are mixed. This might be related to heterogeneity in the experimental procedures, as well as to a lack of focus on individual differences in relevant traits as possible moderators (Baltariu et al. 2023). Nonetheless, we found no evidence for trait affective empathy and the drinking to cope motive as a moderator of an alcohol effect on EA in our study. Other relevant traits could be considered in future studies.

Effects of Drinking Beer on a Novel Measure of Behavioral Empathy

Although the hypothesized impairing effect of alcohol on EA was not found, we did find an impairing effect of alcohol on our behavioral empathy measure. Particularly, we found people with lower AUDIT scores to have lower behavioral empathy after alcohol. This was comparable with the Thiel et al. (2018) study, in that the impairing effect on EA occurred in people with lower AUDIT scores. Also, a finding of lower behavioral empathy after alcohol is not inconsistent with Baltariu et al. (2023), whose systematic review showed that empathy can be negatively affected by acute alcohol intoxication. For example state affective empathy and empathy for pain were both shown to decrease after alcohol (Hu et al. 2018; Francis et al. 2019). In all, the results on behavioral empathy were in line with previous studies.

Strengths and Limitations

The first strong point of the present study is the use of multiple empathy measures, that is, an EAT and a novel behavioral empathy measure. The second point is the use of a repeated‐measures design, which dealt with the fact that the findings from Thiel et al. (2018) might be explained by pre‐existing differences in EA, rather than by alcohol‐induced effects on EA. In light of our results, it is possible that such differences existed. Thirdly, although there have been many experimental studies on the effects of hard liquor on social cognition (Baltariu et al. 2023), our study is one of only a few examining the effects of beer. The effects of beer versus hard liquor on social cognition and related constructs may vary. A meta‐analytic review on the effects of alcohol on aggression found a higher effect size for hard liquor than for beer (Bushman and Cooper 1990). Hard liquor and beer are considered appropriate for different social situations (Klein and Pittman 1990).

Some limitations of our study are identical to those of Thiel et al. (2018). For example, we did not include a control condition in which participants were knowingly sober. This would have allowed for testing expectancy effects. Nonetheless, as participants were not told beforehand that they might be getting a placebo, expectancy effects were more comparable across the two conditions than in many other experimental alcohol studies. In this context, we note that while participants who received alcohol were more likely to guess during the debriefing that they received alcohol than participants who received placebo, we expect this to have had a relatively small impact on the study results. Additionally, one limitation when using the EAT could be that it primarily measures cognitive empathy rather than affective empathy, and the present study did not have an ecological valid measure for affective empathy (Turner and Vallée‐Tourangeau 2023). However, it has been argued that no good psychological measure of affective empathy exists. For example, Groen et al. (2013) have argued for the use of a physiological measure.

Other limitations are more specific to the present study. Firstly, while we set out to recruit both men and women equally to be able to examine participant gender as a potential moderator of the expected alcohol effect, it was more difficult to recruit women than expected. We attribute this largely to our exclusion criterion regarding hormonal contraceptive use, which is highly prevalent in The Netherlands. Besides the canceling of sessions due to Covid‐19 infections had an effect on a not even distribution of men and women in the experimental conditions (see Method). A second limitation is limited generalizability of our findings (a) from Dutch beer drinkers to other beer drinkers, given the existence of cultural differences in when, where, and with whom beer is consumed (Silva et al. 2016) and (b) from beer to wine, which has also not been examined widely for its effects on aspects of social cognition (Baltariu et al. 2023) yet is consumed in different contexts than beer (Silva et al. 2016) and perceived as less likely to have negative interpersonal consequences than both beer and hard liquor (Klein and Pittman 1990; Lang et al. 1983). The use of high pain scenarios to assess behavioral empathy can have some limitations. For example, between‐person variation in pain sensitivity could have influenced participant responses (Li et al. 2020).

Future studies could also focus on situational aspects of social interactions that could involve empathy and alcohol administration. For example, we recommend the use of structured interaction‐like scenarios in performance‐based assessments, for example, the assessment of empathic responses in real life social interactions (Zawacki 2011).

Conclusion

Despite a substantial BAL peak, we found only mixed evidence for an effect of alcoholic beer on empathy at the moderate dose that was studied (0.40 g alcohol/kg body weight). A previous finding suggesting an impairing effect on EA was not replicated, but the observed effect on behavioral empathy was comparable to several other previous results. We strongly support standardization of experimental procedures across studies, to be able to clarify the impact of social drinking on multiple aspects of social cognition in individuals with varying levels of relevant traits.
