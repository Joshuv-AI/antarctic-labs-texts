Assessment of aortic stiffness determinants including smoking status by a novel semiautomatic doppler ultrasound
Authors: Hailun Lin, Xiaodan Liu, Jie Zhang, Fei Wang, Yuqiong Lai, Yabin Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNumerous clinical and population-based research have asserted that aortic stiffness is an independent predictor of cardiovascular events and all-cause death. Reliable detection of the degree of aortic stiffness is crucial for risk stratification and formulation of personalized treatment strategies. The “gold standard” for evaluating aortic stiffness is the non-invasive ultrasonography assessment of the carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity. In this study, we evaluated the association of carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity with a variety of risk factors including age, blood pressure, blood lipids, body mass index, and other variables in 170 healthy participants (86 males, 84 females; ages ranged from 18 to 79 years old), using a novel semi-automatic Doppler ultrasound measurement, to identify the main clinical impact factors for AS. Smoking was regarded as a binary variable, and the deleterious effects of smoking on AS were assessed using a grouped matching method. Statistical analysis revealed that among all risk factors, carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity was primarily independently linked with age, blood pressure and heart rate. 36 participants were tightly selected based on smoking status using age and gender matching (18 in each group, all males). The findings revealed no significant differences in body mass index, blood pressure, blood glucose, or blood lipids between the smokers and non-smokers groups. However, the smokers’ group had a statistically significantly higher carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (8.58 ± 2.04 m/s) compared to the non-smokers (7.67 ± 1.67 m/s). Among the numerous risk factors in healthy participants, carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity is most strongly and independently associated with age, blood pressure and heart rate. Additionally, smokers have higher aortic stiffness than nonsmokers. Introduction

Aortic stiffness (AS) is a recognized cardiovascular risk indicator1,2, which has been claimed by numerous clinical and population-based studies to be a powerful and independent predictor of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality1,3–6. The “gold standard” method for assessing AS is to directly measure the carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (c-f PWV)7,8 as the distance between two measurement places divided by the time required to propagate that distance under pressure.

Doppler ultrasound is widely accepted as a reliable, non-invasive method of assessing AS compared with invasive ones7,9. Our study used a new semi-automatic ultrasound measurement method named “Automatic Measurement of Aortic Stiffness (AMAS)” system7–10, which has been shown to be highly clinically viable in clinical practice, and to excellently agree with the results of conventional manual methods of measuring AS. Age, blood pressure (BP), heart rate (HR), smoking status, gender, body weight, blood lipids and various other risk factors may all influence AS at an early stage, we used the AMAS system to explore the correlation between them in healthy participants.

Materials and methods

Study population

Participants

This study’s participants were primarily healthy individuals who had routine physical examinations at our institution between January and August 2024, totaling 170 individuals. Eligibility criteria were: being at least 18 years old, having no previous cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, or lipid-lowering treatment. Exclusion criteria include substantial cardiac arrhythmia, severe carotid and/or femoral artery stenosis, poor ultrasound image quality.

The relationships between c-f PWV and various risk variables were assessed by dividing participants into two age groups: 18–49 years and 50–79 years. In addition, after matching age and gender, participants were separated into research and control groups based on their smoking history and current habitual smoking behavior, yielding a total of 18 pairs to investigate the impact of smoking on AS.

The study protocol followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the First People’s Hospital of Foshan’s Ethics Committee. (No.15: Ethics Review 2024). All participants signed the informed consent document.

Study protocol

Baseline characteristics assessment

Following standardized physical examinations, baseline parameters including demographic characteristics (age, gender), anthropometric measurements (body mass index, waist circumference, hip circumference), metabolic biomarkers (serum lipid profile, uric acid, fasting blood glucose), and lifestyle factors (current smoking status and smoking history) were systematically documented for all study participants.

Blood pressure measurement

All participants were instructed to avoid strenuous exercise for at least two hours and to sit quietly for at least 15 min before the measurement began. The participants’ right brachial artery BP was monitored using an Omron cuff BP monitor while they were seated, and the results were taken as the mean average of three measures.

Ultrasound measurement of c-f PWV

Frequency spectrum acquisition of common carotid and femoral artery blood flow

Measurements were performed in a specialized room with temperature being kept between 20 and 25 degrees Celsius and the surroundings were silent. Participants were measured in the supine position, simultaneous ECG monitoring. VINNO 86 or ULtimus 9E ultrasound system equipped with AMAS system were used to display and visualize the lumen and bifurcation of the right common carotid artery (RCCA) and the right common femoral artery (RCFA) using B-mode ultrasound. The color Doppler and pulsed-wave Doppler patterns were then activated, and the sampling volume was respectively placed 1–1.5 cm from the bifurcation at the RCCA and RCFA to collect the flow velocity spectrum (being acquired within the same measurement session, with approximately 3–5 min delay between recordings), which was then retained and saved for at least 20 cardiac cycles. The position of the Doppler sampling point marked on the surface of the RCCA and RCFA, facilitates subsequent measurement of the propagation distance of pulse waves.

Measurement of carotid-femoral transit distance

Participants were kept in the supine position, the transit distance for c-f PWV was calculated by the direct straight distance between the two sites(the sampling points of the RCCA to the RCFA surface markers described above ) × 80%7,10. All straight-surface distances were measured by a single trained operator using the same inelastic tape for every participant, the mean of three consecutive readings was used.

Offline measurement of c-f PWV

C-f PWV was measured offline using the AMAS software package. The measurement protocol consisted of three sequential steps:

Signal acquisition: After activating the AMAS system, we selected both “Carotid-R Time” and “Femoral-R Time” functions, setting the analysis duration to 10 consecutive, ECG-validated cardiac cycles.

Waveform analysis: The system automatically tracked spectral envelopes from RCCA and RCFA, calculating the foot-to-foot transit time (Δt) between the ECG R-wave peak and the waveform onset points using proprietary edge-detection algorithms.

Velocity calculation: Upon selecting “Carotid-femoral PWV” and inputting the pre-determined transit distance (calculated as 80% of the direct RCCA-RCFA surface measurement), the software computed c-f PWV using the standard equation (ΔD/Δt) and displayed the results (Fig. 1).

Automated measurement of carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) using the AMAS system. Panel (A,B) show the automatic detection of the time delay between the electrocardiogram R-wave peak (the green dashed lines) and the onset of the pulse wave (the yellow dashed lines) in the right common carotid artery and the right common femoral artery, respectively. The corresponding results, including the mean and standard deviation (SD), are displayed on the right side of the screen. The propagation distance is manually entered into the system, which subsequently computes the cfPWV (red box).

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test; P > 0.05 indicated normal distribution. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables were described as frequencies (percentages). Uni-variate Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were utilized to assess the associations between c-f PWV and all risk variables. Multiple linear regression analyses (controlled for age) with backward stepwise removal were used to identify the independent associations of outcome variables with their significant Uni-variate correlates. Variables in both groups were compared using the Paired-Samples t Test. Statistical tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Discussion

With ageing and elevated BP, the human arterial system undergoes progressive loss of elasticity and increased stiffness. Elevated heart rate is associated with diminished dynamic compliance and elevated mechanical stress, which may ultimately contribute to heightened AS11, which is the main manifestation of vascular aging and an important pathological basis for the occurrence and progression of cardiovascular diseases. Currently, regional pulse wave velocity (PWV)12,13 is recognized as the most convenient and effective noninvasive methods for measuring AS, among which c-f PWV is considered to be the “gold standard” one7,8, which has the strongest evidence and the most robust support for its clinical value. As early as 2006, the European Society of Hypertension guidelines recommended using c-f PWV as the preferred method for measuring AS8. This study applied a newer semi-automatic measurement technique for PWV using ultrasound Doppler, the AMAS system10, which has been clinically proven to be a reliable, simple and reproducible method, providing the methodological foundation for the present study. The AMAS system not only has the same precision as the traditional manual method, but it is also very feasible and time-saving.

A substantial amount of evidence3–5,14–19 indicates that AS is an independent and powerful predictor of the future occurrence of cardiovascular disease, cardiovascular disease-related mortality, and all-cause mortality in not only populations with diseases like hypertension and diabetes, but also the general healthy populations. Age is the most important determinant of arterial properties18,20–23. The thickness of the artery wall grows with age, as does the lumen and stiffness. The impact of BP on arterial properties was well understood in both normal and hypertensive people. Previous studies3,14,17,18,23,24 have shown that BP is the primary clinical driver of carotid artery features; nevertheless, even higher values of BP within the currently normal BP range are related with poorer carotid characteristics after controlling for age and gender. In our study, mean arterial pressure (MAP), as an integrated measure of peripheral perfusion pressure, more representatively reflected the impact of blood pressure on c-f PWV than either systolic blood pressure (SBP) or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) alone. Several epidemiological studies investigating AS have reported an independent association between PWV and HR25,26. However, the strength of this correlation varies considerably across cross-sectional studies and population cohorts26. Some studies suggest that increased resting HR is linked to higher AS, but only among subjects with pre-existing AS (PWV > 8.6 m/s), independent of blood pressure, other risk factors, and participant characteristics27.

Our results were consistent with the aforementioned studies, indicating that c-f PWV was primarily and independently linked with age, BP and HR in the entire study participants as well as in distinct groups, with all participants having BP of less than 140/90mmHg. Additionally, our study found that obesity, abdominal obesity and lipid profiles had no significant effect on c-f PWV, also consistent with previous related studies20,28. Other known cardiovascular risk factors, namely triglyceride, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and fasting glucose, have only a minimal effect on c-f PWV in the elderly.

Several studies15,29,30 have indicated that smoking affects AS and is a significant cardiovascular risk factor leading to central arterial stiffness. Smoking causes changes in peripheral and central vascular function, affecting smokers of all ages. It was proven26 that smokers have higher AS than non-smokers using the method of ultrasound time-harmonic elastography. According to the findings of their small-scale study, young and middle-aged smokers had a 12% higher level of abdominal aortic stiffness than non-smokers. In our study, we validated the similar conclusion using a novel semi-automatic method for measuring c-f PWV, the AMAS system. As shown in Fig. 2, smokers (red dots) exhibit a higher c-f PWV than age-matched non-smoking subjects (blue dots). Consistent with other studies, smokers had a higher c-f PWV than non-smokers, indicating a higher degree of AS, after controlling for age and gender, as well as confirming that there were no inter-group differences in BP, HR, blood sugar, blood lipids, and other risk variables.

Conclusion

Therefore, our study demonstrates that among the numerous risk factors in healthy populations, c-f PWV is primarily and independently related to age, BP and HR. Additionally, smokers have a comparatively higher degree of AS than non-smokers. A novel semi-automatic Doppler ultrasound measurement method was used to achieve these outcomes.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged:

First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the relationships between cardiovascular risk factors and aortic stiffness. Longitudinal studies are required to establish temporal associations and confirm the observed effects of aging, blood pressure, and other factors on arterial stiffness progression.

Second, smoking status was operationalized as a binary variable (current smoker/non-smoker) without quantification of smoking intensity (e.g., pack-years) or characterization of smoking methods (e.g., cigarettes, e-cigarettes). Future investigations should incorporate detailed smoking metrics to better characterize dose-response relationships.

Third, blood pressure measurements were obtained exclusively in the seated position prior to c-f PWV assessment, rather than concurrently in the supine position during vascular evaluation. This methodological approach may introduce hemodynamic variability, as posture-induced blood pressure changes could influence arterial stiffness measurements.

Fourth, while the overall sample size (N = 170) provided adequate power (> 95%) for multiple regression analyses, the matched-pair comparison (N = 18 pairs) was underpowered (post-hoc power = 48% for medium effect size, α = 0.05). This limited our ability to detect small but potentially clinically meaningful differences between smokers and non-smokers. Larger cohort studies are needed to validate these findings and improve precision.
