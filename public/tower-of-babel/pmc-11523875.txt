Comparative plastome analysis of Arundinelleae (Poaceae, Panicoideae), with implications for phylogenetic relationships and plastome evolution
Authors: Li-Qiong Jiang, Bryan T Drew, Watchara Arthan, Guo-Ying Yu, Hong Wu, Yue Zhao, Hua Peng, Chun-Lei Xiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArundinelleae is a small tribe within the Poaceae (grass family) possessing a widespread distribution that includes Asia, the Americas, and Africa. Several species of Arundinelleae are used as natural forage, feed, and raw materials for paper. The tribe is taxonomically cumbersome due to a paucity of clear diagnostic morphological characters. There has been scant genetic and genomic research conducted for this group, and as a result the phylogenetic relationships and species boundaries within Arundinelleae are poorly understood.ResultsWe compared and analyzed 11 plastomes of Arundinelleae, of which seven plastomes were newly sequenced. The plastomes range from 139,629 base pairs (bp) (Garnotia tenella) to 140,943 bp (Arundinella barbinodis), with a standard four-part structure. The average GC content was 38.39%, but varied in different regions of the plastome. In all, 110 genes were annotated, comprising 76 protein-coding genes, 30 tRNA genes, and four rRNA genes. Furthermore, 539 simple sequence repeats, 519 long repeats, and 10 hyper-variable regions were identified from the 11 plastomes of Arundinelleae. A phylogenetic reconstruction of Panicoideae based on 98 plastomes demonstrated the monophyly of Arundinella and Garnotia, but the circumscription of Arundinelleae remains unresolved.ConclusionComplete chloroplast genome sequences can improve phylogenetic resolution relative to single marker approaches, particularly within taxonomically challenging groups. All phylogenetic analyses strongly support the monophyly of Arundinella and Garnotia, respectively, but the monophylly of Arundinelleae was not well supported. The intergeneric phylogenetic relationships within Arundinelleae require clarification, indicating that more data is necessary to resolve generic boundaries and evaluate the monophyly of Arundinelleae. A comprehensive taxonomic revision for the tribe is necessary. In addition, the identified hyper-variable regions could function as molecular markers for clarifying phylogenetic relationships and potentially as barcoding markers for species identification in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10871-5.Keywords: Panicoideae, Arundinelleae, Chloroplast genome, Plastome phylogenomics

Background

Arundinelleae Stapf is a tribe within the Panicoideae (Poaceae) consisting of two genera (Arundinella Raddi and Garnotia Brongn.) and ca. 86 species [1, 2]. Species within the Arundinelleae have a widespread distribution that includes Asia, the Americas, and Africa, and several species are important economically and ecologically. For example, species within the tribe are used as building materials [Arundinella bengalensis (Spreng.) Druce], natural forage [A. hookeri Munro ex Keng, A. hirta (Thunb.) Tanaka], feed [A. hirta], and as raw materials for paper (A. setosa Trin., A. hirta) [3].

The boundaries of Arundinelleae have long been controversial. When establishing the tribe Arundinelleae, Stapf included three genera, Arundinella, Tristachya Nees and Trichopteryx Nees [4]. Subsequently, about 25 genera (Table 1) were added to this tribe by various taxonomists [5–10]. Phipps [11] attempted to resolve the generic classification within the tribe using numerical taxonomy, but was unsuccessful. Using a similar approach, Clayton [12, 13] recognized nine genera within Arundinelleae, but subsequently Clayton and Renvoize [14], based on morphological and anatomical characters, recircumscribed the tribe to include 12 genera [Arundinella, Chandrasekharania V.J. Nair, V.S. Ramach. & Sreek., Danthoniopsis Stapf, Dilophotriche (C.E. Hubb.) Jacq.-Fél., Garnotia, Gilgiochloa Pilg., Jansenella Bor, Loudetia Hochst. ex Steud., Loudetiopsis Conert, Trichopteryx, Tristachya and Zonotriche (C.E. Hubb.) J.B. Phipps]. Kellogg & Watson [15], based on a cladistic analysis of morphological and anatomical characters, concluded that Arundinelleae (excluding Garnotia) was monophyletic and suggested the tribe was an early diverging lineage within Andropogoneae Dumort.

Molecular phylogenetic analyses have since clarified relationships among the major genera of Arundinelleae [16–22]. Teerawatananon et al. [23], using chloroplast (trnL-F and atpβ-rbcL) and nuclear ribosomal DNA (nrITS) markers, suggested abandoning the concept of Arundinelleae due to polyphyly, and divided the tribe into three clades. Their study [23] advocated removing Arundinella and Garnotia from Arundinelleae and placing them in subtribes Arundinellinae Honda s.s. (Arundinella) and Garnotiinae Pilg. (Garnotia) within the Andropogoneae. Sánchez-Ken & Clark [24] similarly found that Arundinelleae was not monophyletic, and retained only four genera (Arundinella, Garnotia, Chandrasekharania, and Jansenella) within the tribe, while transferring eight other genera into the newly established tribe Tristachyideae Sánchez-Ken & L.G. Clark. Kellogg [25], following Teerawatananon et al. [23], also suggested that Arundinelleae should be abandoned in favor of recognizing Arundinellinae. She stated that Garnotia and Arundinella shared sufficient morphological and anatomical characters to be placed together, although further study was necessary [25]. Meanwhile, Soreng et al. [2, 26] recognized Arundinelleae as a tribe that included three genera: Arundinella, Garnotia, and Jansenella. However, Bianconi et al. [27] later transferred Jansenella to the newly established tribe Jansenelleae Voronts. based on both morphological and molecular evidence. The most recent study on the group [1] concluded that Arundinelleae should only include two genera, Arundinella and Garnotia. To date, phylogenetic studies on Arundinelleae have been limited, with most studies having sparse taxonomic sampling and various combinations of single gene regions markers (e.g., rpoC2, ndhF, rbcL, phyB, matK, trnL-F, nrITS, GBSSI, rpl16, and atpβ–rbcL) [1, 2, 16–20, 23, 24, 26, 28].

An increasing numbers of plastid genomes from non-model organisms are being sequenced and effectively used to tackle phylogenetic and taxonomic challenges across various ranks in flowering plants [29–32]. In Poaceae, the phylogenetic classifications at the subfamily, tribe, genus, and species level have been relatively well studied [33–37]. However, to date, there are only a few published chloroplast genomes for Arundinelleae [27, 35, 38]. Earlier studies related to the tribe primarily focused on evolutionary relationships within Panicoideae [38], C4 photosynthesis adaptations [27], and the phylogenomics of Andropogoneae [35], and included a limited number of Garnotia and Arundinella species. No comparative plastome analysis specifically targeting Arundinelleae has not been conducted. In this study, we newly sequenced the complete plastomes of seven species of Arundinelleae and compared them with four previously published plastomes. The objectives of this study are to: (1) analyze and compare the structure and gene organization of plastid genomes across Arundinelleae, (2) identify potential molecular markers for future phylogenetic or population genetic studies within Arundinelleae, and (3) reconstruct the phylogenetic relationships of Arundinelleae using complete chloroplast genome sequences. The findings from this study will provide valuable insights for future studies on the phylogeny, taxonomy, species identification, and population genetics of Arundinelleae, and will contribute to the exploration, utilization, and conservation of plant resources.

Results

General features of Arundinella chloroplast genomes

The seven newly sequenced and annotated plastomes have been submitted to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) [39], with accession numbers listed in Table 2. In addition, four plastomes were downloaded from NCBI (accession numbers: KU291490, MK593544, MK593551 and KY596184). The plastomes of the 11 Arundinelleae samples exhibited a circular quadripartite structure ranging in length from 139,629 bp [Garnotia tenella (Arn. ex Miq.) Janowski] to 140,943 bp (Arundinella barbinodis Keng ex B. S. Sun & Z. H. Hu) (Table 2). Each plastid genome contained a large single copy (LSC) region (81,983–82,842 bp) and a small single copy (SSC) region (12,427–12,597 bp), separated by two copies of the inverted repeat region (IR) (22,520–22,855 bp) (Fig. 1; Table 2). The guanine-cytosine (GC) content was consistent across different Arundinelleae species, averaging 38.39% (Table 2). The GC content was higher in the IR regions (43.82–43.97%) compared to the LSC (36.18–36.29%) and SSC (32.56–32.86%) regions.

When counting duplicated genes in the inverted repeat (IR) regions only once, each plastome contained a total of 110 unique genes, comprising 76 protein-coding genes, 30 transfer RNAs (tRNAs), and four ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) (Table 3). Within the IR region, a total of 19 genes were duplicated, consisting of seven protein-coding genes, eight tRNAs and four rRNAs (Table 3). Among the 76 protein-coding genes, eight contained one intron, while six tRNA genes also had one intron each. In addition, the ycf3 and rps12 genes were found to contain two introns. In the examined samples, protein-coding regions accounted for 42.26–42.66% of the total genome length, while tRNA and rRNA regions made up 2.02–2.14% and 6.52–6.58%, respectively (Table S1). The remaining portions were non-coding sequences, including intergenic spacers, introns, and pseudogenes. Complete annotation information is provided in Table 3.

IR contraction and expansion

A detailed comparison was conducted to assess the boundaries of the inverted repeat/single copy (IR/SC) regions among the 11 species of Arundinelleae (Fig. 2). In all species, the junctions of the LSC/IRb regions were consistently located between the rpl22 and rps19 genes, with a uniform length of 35 bp for the rpl22-rps19 region within the IRb partition. The SSC/IRb junctions (JSB) were found within ndhF, with 29 bp of the ndhF gene duplicated within the IRb region. The ndhH gene spanned the SSC/IRa junctions (JSA), with only one base pair of ndhH positioned at the 5’ end in the IRa region. The IRa/LSC junctions (JLA) were located in the intergenic region of rps19-psbA, with the length of the rps19-psbA region in the IRa region consistently measuring 36 bp across all taxa.

The Mauve alignment of plastomes revealed a high level of consistency in plastome organization among species of Arundinelleae, with no translocations or inversions observed (Fig. S1). The mVISTA analyses showed that the two IR regions were more stable than the LSC and SSC regions. In addition, the non-coding regions and intergenic spacers exhibited more variation compared to the coding regions (Fig. 3). Analysis of synonymous (Ks) and nonsynonymous (Ka) substitution rate

The ratio of nonsynonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) substitutions were calculated for shared unique protein-coding genes (PCGs) among the 11 Arundinelleae plastomes, using Arundinella barbinodis as a reference (Fig. 5, Table S2). Out of the 76 shared genes, 27 genes could not be analyzed due to either no variation (identical sequences) or a lack of nonsynonymous or synonymous nucleotide substitutions. Most of the Ka/Ks ratios were below one, indicating purifying selection, except for a few cases: ndhA in A. cochinchinensis Keng (1.2895), ccsA, matK and ndhA in A. deppeana Nees ex Steud. (1.2, 1.4483, 1.2895), ccsA in A. fluviatilis Hand.-Mazz. and A. hirta (1.2), matK in A. fluviatilis, A. hirta and A. hookeri (3.4828, 3.4828, 1.8966), infA and rpl14 in Garnotia emodi (Endl.) Janwoski (1.5306, 2.5841), and rpl14 in G. stricta var. longiseta Hack. and G. tenella (2.5841). These results suggest that five genes (ndhA, ccsA, matK, infA, and rpl14) are undergoing positive selection in the aforementioned taxa, implying that mutations within these genes may confer some evolutionary advantages.

Simple sequence repeats (SSR) polymorphisms and long repeat structure

A total of 539 SSRs were identified across the 11 plastomes, with 430 SSRs (79.78%) located in the LSC region, 54 SSRs (10.02%) in the SSC region, and 55 SSRs (10.2%) in the IR region (Fig. 6, Table S3). The number of SSRs (or microsatellite loci) varied among the species, ranging from 42 in Arundinella fluviatilis to 54 in Garnotia stricta var. longiseta (Fig. 6). Mononucleotide repeats exhibited the highest variability, ranging from 22 repeats in A. fluviatilis to 39 repeats in G. stricta var. longiseta. In contrast, dinucleotide, trinucleotide, and tetranucleotide repeats showed no significant differences among the 11 plastomes, as detailed in Fig. 6 and Table S3. When considering cyclic queues and reverse complements as identical SSRs, the 590 SSRs could be categorized into 18 different repeat types. The mononucleotide repeat unit A/T, dinucleotide repeat unit AT/AT, and tetranucleotide repeat units GTAG/AGAA, AACG/ATTG, AATA/ATCC and TCGT/TTAT were present in all 11 samples (Fig. 7). However, the mononucleotide repeat unit G/C was absent in G. emodi and G. stricta var. longiseta. The dinucleotide repeat unit TC/TC was missing in A. khaseana Nees ex Steud. Among the trinucleotide repeats, the repeat unit CTT/TTG was absent in G. stricta var. longiseta and G. tenella; while the repeat unit TTA/ATT was found only in G. emodi, G. stricta var. Longiseta, and G. tenella. For tetranucleotide repeats, AAAG/TCCT was absent in G. emodi, G. stricta var. longiseta and G. tenella, the GAAA/AAAT repeat was only found in G. tenella, and the TTTA /TTTA repeat unit was exclusive to G. emodi. The pentanucleotide repeats AATAG/ATAAT were found only in both A. cochinchinensis and A. deppeana, while the CATTT/CATTT was unique to A. fluviatilis and A. hookeri. The hexanucleotide repeats AGATGA/TCTTCA and GAAAAA/GAAAAA were present only in A. fluviatilis, A. hirta and A. hookeri, whereas ATTGAA/ATGAGA was exclusive to A. deppeana. A total of 519 long repeats were identified in the 11 plastomes (Fig. 8), including forward, reverse, and palindromic sequences. Forward repeats were the most common type, making up 59.92% of the total repeats, followed by palindromic repeats at 36.22%. Reverse repeats and complement repeats were less frequent, accounting for only 3.08% and 0.77% of the total repeats, respectively (Fig. 8). Most of these repeats were found in the non-coding regions (52.99%; Fig. 8). The length of the repeats varied from 30 bp to 237 bp, with the most common repeat sequence lengths being 30 bp, 32 bp, 31 bp, 36 bp, and 51 bp (Fig. 8, Table S4).

Phylogenetic analyses

Our study used complete plastome sequences to reconstruct the phylogeny of Arundinelleae. All analyses consistently supported the monophyly of Arundinella and Garnotia with maximum support values (Fig. 9; ML BS: 100%; BI PP: 1.00; all values presented in this order below). However, the monophyly of Arundinelleae was not confirmed in this study.

The best-score tree from maximum likelihood analysis of Panicoideae using the chloroplast genomes excluding one inverted repeat region (CPG). Note Support values BS ≥ 50 or PP ≥ 0.50 are displayed on the branches follow the order MLBS/BIPP (“*” indicates a support value 100 or 1.00)

As currently defined, Arundinelleae comprises two genera, Garnotia and Arundinella. Phylogenetic analyses of both the complete plastome sequences (CPG) and non-coding region (NCR) datasets showed that Garnotia and Arundinella formed a clade that was sister to Andropogoneae, although with weak support (Figs. 9 and 50%, 0.85; Fig. S3, 61%, 0.84). However, based on the phylogeny of plastid coding regions (CR) dataset, Arundinella formed a clade with Andropogoneae (Fig. S2, 64%, 0.99), and then was sister to the Garnotia clade with strong support (Fig. S2, 100%, 1.00).

Discussion

General characteristics of the plastomes of Arundinelleae

Prior to this study, seven plastomes of Arundinelleae were available on GenBank, but they had not been comprehensively analyzed [27, 35, 38]. Here, we newly sequenced seven plastomes from seven different species of Arundinelleae, resulting in a total of 11 plastomes for comparative analysis.

The lengths of the 11 plastomes varied from 139,629 bp to 140,943 bp, mainly due to large indels (insertions/deletions) in non-coding regions. Despite this variation, all 11 plastomes exhibited a high degree of conservation in genome structure, gene order, and content, consistent with findings in other groups of Poaceae [31, 36, 37, 40]. For example, each plastome encodes 110 unique genes arranged in the same order and features the typical quadripartite structure, including a pair of inverted repeats (IR) regions flanked by the large single copy (LSC) and small single copy (SSC) regions (Fig. 1). The average GC content was determined to be 38.39%, aligning with values observed in other species of Poaceae [31, 35, 37, 40].

The expansion and contraction of the IR region have been posited as major sources of length variation in plastomes [41–44]. However, this study shows that the 11 plastomes of Arundinelleae are highly conserved, as evidenced by a lack of significant structural rearrangements in our aligned sequences. In Poaceae, the ndhF and ndhH genes are located near opposite ends of the SSC region and may extend into IR regions [45, 46]. The majority of the portion of ndhH situated within the IR in early diverging grass lineages migrated out of the IR early in the evolution of the PACMAD clade, leaving just one to four nucleotides remaining within the IR, while the 3′ terminus of ndhF migrated into the IR, resulting in the presence of approximately 12–30 nucleotides within this region in most taxa [46]. This migration pattern was observed in this study.

Our study suggests that five genes are under positive selection (ndhA, ccsA, matK, infA, and rpl14). The ndhA gene encodes a subunit of the chloroplast NDH complex, which is involved in electron transport across membranes [47]. The ccsA gene, typically conserved in photosynthetic plants [48], has been shown to undergo positive selection at particular sites in some Poaceae [49]. The matK gene functions as a splicing factor for plastid group IIA introns [50]. The infA gene is often unstable and is frequently lost from the chloroplast genome in angiosperms due to its transfer to the nucleus [51], but remains intact in Arundinelleae, similar to other groups of Poaceae [31, 37, 40]. The rpl14 gene encodes chloroplast ribosomal components, and its bacterial orthologs are crucial for translation [52, 53]. Both the ccsA and matK genes have been identified as rapidly evolving in some plants [54, 55]. However, the function of these genes in Poaceae is rarely reported and warrants further investigation in future studies.

Simple sequence repeats are well-known for their reliability and high rates of polymorphism, making them valuable in studies of genetic diversity, molecular identification, and population genetics [56, 57]. In this study, mononucleotide repeats, primarily consisting of A or T bases, were the most prevalent SSRs, aligning with findings from other Poaceae [31, 58, 59]. A total of 326 SSRs were comprised of A or T bases, representing approximately 60% of all SSRs. Most mononucleotide repeats were located in non-coding regions (Table S3). This study also revealed variation in the abundance and distribution patterns of penta-nucleotide and hexa-nucleotide repeats. While these are common repeats in all Oryza species [60], they were species-specific in this study. Penta-nucleotide repeats were found only in Arundinella cochinchinensis and A. deppeana, whereas hexa-nucleotide repeats were found only in A. fluviatilis, A. hirta, and A. hookeri. Penta-nucleotide repeats were located only in non-coding regions, while hexa-nucleotide repeats appeared in both coding and non-coding regions. These two types of repeats were absent in Garnotia. The repeats identified by Reputer were categorized into four groups: forward, reverse, complement, and palindromic, with the majority being in the forward and palindromic categories.

Phylogenetic relationships within Arundinelleae

Previous systematic studies have been hindered by sparse taxonomic sampling within Arundinelleae, leaving the relationships between Arundinella, Garnotia and Andropogoneae unclear [17, 23, 27]. This study used complete plastome sequences to reconstruct the phylogeny of Arundinelleae. The systematic placement of Garnotia and Arundinella varied somewhat depending on the dataset. In phylogenies based on the CPG (Figs. 9 and 50%, 0.85) and NCR (Fig. S3, 61%, 0.84) datasets, Garnotia and Arundinella (Arundinelleae) formed a weakly-supported sister clade to Andropogoneae, similar to findings by Arthan et al. [61] using nuclear genes, which also showed low support values.

Few morphological characters support a close relationship between Garnotia and Arundinella. Tateoka [62] noted that both genera share a “distinctive cell” type in the transverse leaf structure. However, this character is also found in other genera across several tribes in the subfamily Panicoideae, including Andropogoneae (e.g., Arthraxon P. Beauv. and Microstegium Nees), Anthephoreae Pilg. ex Potztal (e.g., Anthephora Schreb.), Arthropogoneae Pilg. ex Butzin (e.g., Arthropogon Nees), and Paspaleae J. Presl (e.g., Paspalum L.) [63–65], indicating that this character probably evolved multiple times in Panicoideae. In the phylogenetic analysis based on the CR dataset, Arundinella and Andropogoneae formed a clade (Fig. S2, 64%, 0.99) sister to Garnotia. Morphologically, there are some morphological characters that support this relationship. For example, spikelets of Arundinella and Andropogoneae often contain two florets, whereas spikelets of Garnotia typically have only one floret.

Bianconi et al. [27] also observed similar topological patterns in their study, based on different gene regions. The low support values at certain nodes and the uncertain relationships among these groups, coupled with the short lengths of the deeper internal branches relative to external branches in all trees (Fig. 9, S2, S3), suggest a pattern of an “ancient” rapid radiation [66, 67]. This pattern is further supported by dating analyses indicating that the major lineages of Arundinelleae emerged during a short time span [68, 69]. Bianconi et al. [27] attributed the topological conflicts between plastid and nuclear data sets, as well as incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) accompanied by rapid radiation, as potential causes of incongruence. In addition, several studies [27, 61] have noted that the cytonuclear incongruencies in the placement of Arundinella, Garnotia, and Andropogoneae may result from chloroplast capture, a phenomenon frequently observed in Poaceae [70–72]. The plastome sequences used in this study did not resolve deep relationships with high support, indicating a need for additional nuclear DNA data from high-throughput sequencing and broader taxon sampling to clarify these relationships. For now, we place Arundinella and Garnotia within the Arundinelleae, in line with the classification proposed by Soreng et al. [1].

Materials and methods

Plant materials, DNA extraction and sequencing

Leaves of the newly sequenced species were either collected in the field or sourced from herbarium specimens. This collection includes six species of Arundinella and one species of Garnotia (Table 4). Voucher specimens were deposited at the Kunming Institute of Botany (KIB), Chinese Academy of Sciences, the Herbarium of Yunnan University (YUKU), and the Royal Botanic Gardens Kew (K). In addition, plastomes of four species from the tribe Arundinelleae, i.e., A. deppeana (KU291490), A. hookeri (MK593544), G. stricta var. longiseta (MK593551) and G. tenella (KY596184), were included for analyses.

Sampled species and their voucher specimens used in this study

Genomic DNA was extracted from herbarium specimens using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) or from silica-gel dried leaves using a modified CTAB method [73]. The extracted DNA was fragmented into approximately 300 bp fragments for library construction following the manufacturer’s instructions (NEBNext Ultra IITMDNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina). Paired-end (PE) sequencing with 150 bp reads was performed on an Illumina Hiseq-2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, United States) at BGI Genomics (Shenzhen, Guangdong, China).

Plastome assembly and annotation

Adaptors and low-quality reads were removed from the raw data using Trimmomatic v.0.32 [74] with default parameters. The De novo assembly of the newly sequenced plastomes was performed using the GetOrganelle pipeline [75], and the resulting contigs were visualized with Bandage v.0.8.1 [76]. Complete chloroplast sequence paths were manually selected, ensuring a minimum depth of contigs over 100 × and a minimum length exceeding 300 bp. The newly assembled plastomes were annotated using Plastid Genome Annotator (PGA, https://github.com/quxiaojian/PGA) [77], with plastome sequences of Arundinella nepalensis Trin. (MK593545) and Garnotia thailandica Gould (KY596171) serving as references. Start and stop codons, as well as intron/exon boundaries for protein-coding genes, were manually verified and corrected using Geneious v.8.0.2 [78]. The plastome map was generated with Ge-seq v.2.03 [79], and tRNAs were validated through the online tRNAscan-SE service with default parameters [80]. Additionally, Geneious v.8.0.2 [78] was utilized to calculate the number of genes, gene length, and GC content.

Chloroplast genome comparison and sequence divergence analysis

We compared the plastomes of 11 species of Arundinelleae using mVISTA [81] under the Shuffle-LAGAN mode, with Arundinella barbinodis (PP073504) serving as a reference. Locally collinear blocks were identified using the Mauve v.1.0.0 [82] plugin within Geneious v.8.0.2 [78] with default parameters. To assess IR contraction or expansion, we examined the boundaries between the inverted repeated region (IR) and single-copy (SC) regions in Geneious v.8.0.2 [78]. All plastomes were aligned using the MAFFT v.7.475 [83] plugin in Geneious v.8.0.2 [78]. Nucleotide diversity (Pi) analysis was performed with DnaSP v.6 [84], with a step size of 200 bp and a window length of 600 bp.

Gene selective pressure analysis of 11 cp. Protein-coding genes in Arundinelleae

To evaluate variation in the evolutionary rates of chloroplast genes, DnaSP v.6 [84] was employed to calculate the nonsynonymous substitution rates (Ka), synonymous rates (Ks), and their ratios (Ka/Ks). Protein-coding genes (PCGs) were extracted using PhyloSuite v.1.2.1 [85] and aligned using MAFFT v.7.475 [83].

Simple sequence repeats and repeat structure analysis

The MISA perl script [86] was used to detect simple sequence repeats (SSRs) in the plastomes of all 11 taxa, and the thresholds of 10, 5, 4, 3, 3, and 3 repeat units were set for mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexa-nucleotides, respectively. For identifying long repeat sequences (forward, reverse, palindromic and complement), REPuter [87] was employed, using a hamming distance set to 3, a minimum repeat size set to 30 bp.

Phylogenetic analyses

To evaluate the effectiveness of complete plastome sequences for resolving relationships within Arundinelleae, 86 plastomes representing other 11 tribes within Panicoideae were also included for analyses. Four species, Aristida glaziovii Hack. ex Henrard (MK570617), Danthonia spicata (L.) P. Beauv. ex Roem. & Schult. (MN955297), Elytrophorus globularis Hack. (MF035980), Eriachne armitii F. Muell. ex Benth. (KX229726), and Triraphis mollis R. Br. (MK590082), were selected as the outgroup based on previous findings [2, 26]. The GenBank accession numbers for these sequences are listed in Table S5.

Alignments were initially conducted using MAFFT v.7.475 [83] with default settings and then manually refined in Geneious v.8.0.2 [78]. Ambiguously aligned regions were excluded before proceeding with phylogenetic analyses [88]. Given the uniparental inheritance and absence of recombination in the plastid genome [89], all sequences were combined into three distinct matrices for phylogenetic reconstruction: coding regions (dataset CR), non-coding regions (dataset NCR), and complete plastome sequences (dataset CPG). To minimize the overrepresentation of duplicated sequences, only one inverted repeat (IR) region was included in the analysis.

Phylogenetic relationships were reconstructed using both maximum Likelihood (ML) and Bayesian Inference (BI). These analyses were conducted through the Cyberinfrastructure for Phylogenetic Research Science (CIPRES) Gateway (http://www.phylo.org/) [90]. ML analyses were performed using RAxML HPC2 v.8.2.12 [91], with the general time reversible (GTR) + gamma model and 1000 bootstrap iterations (–#|–N), and other parameters set to default settings. BI analyses were conducted using MrBayes v.3.2.7a [92]. The optimal substitution model for each dataset was selected using jModelTest2 [93] through the CIPRES Gateway, based on the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) [94]. Four Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains, consisting of one cold chain and three heated chains, were executed for 20 million generations. Convergence of the MCMC runs and estimation of effective sample size (ESS) were assessed using Tracer v.1.7.0 [95]. The initial 25% of trees were discarded as burn-in, and the remaining trees were used to generate a 50% majority-rule consensus tree. Visualization and adjustment of the ML and BI trees were performed using TreeGraph v.2.15.0 [96].
