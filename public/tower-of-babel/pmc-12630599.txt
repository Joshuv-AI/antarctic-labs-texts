Compact machine learning model for perioperative stroke prediction prior to surgery: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mi-Young Oh, Hee-Soo Kim, Young Mi Jung, Hyung-Chul Lee, Seung-Bo Lee, Seung Mi Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerioperative stroke significantly impacts postoperative outcomes. Current risk stratification methods for perioperative stroke prediction lack accuracy and practicality. We aimed to develop a machine learning (ML) model that improves both accuracy and usability for predicting perioperative strokes. Data from 36,502 patients at Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH) were utilized to develop and internally validate. An external validation was conducted using data from 404 patients at Boramae Medical Center (BMC). Perioperative stroke was defined as a brain infarction of ischemic etiology, occurring within 30 days post-surgery. We developed ML-based prediction models comprising preoperative features and compared them with cardiovascular scores. Additionally, we developed a compact model utilizing the 10 most significant features of the best-performing model. The CatBoost-based prediction model showed the best discriminatory power for high-risk patients and outperformed cardiovascular scores in the external validation set (area under the receiver operating characteristics curve [AUC], 0.867 [95% CI: 0.830–0.896]; revised cardiac index score, 0.528 [95% CI: 0.497–0.575; p < 0.000]; CHA2DS2VASc score, 0.706 [95% CI: 0.659–0.748; p < 0.000]. The compactmodel also improved performance (AUC of 0.875 [95% CI: 0.860–0.952]). Introduction

Perioperative stroke isa major complication following surgery, leading to significant morbidity and mortality1,2.It significantly contributes to postoperative mortality, long-term hospitalization, and discharge to long-term care facilities3–6.Between 2004 and 2013,the incidence of perioperative strokesin noncardiac surgeries rose from 0.52 to 0.77%; this figure increases dramatically to 7% when considering silent brain infarcts after surgery1,4.The detection of perioperative strokes during both intraoperative and postoperative phases is challenging, frequently resulting in suboptimal outcomes due to the lack of timely access to critical stroke interventions, such as thrombolysis or a thrombectomy7,8. Consequently, there is anurgentneed to identify patients at high risk for perioperative stroke prior to surgical procedures, thereby enabling the strategic allocation of preventative healthcare resources9.

Currently, specific risk stratification tools for assessing the risk of perioperative stroke in noncardiac surgeries are limited10. Established cardiovascular risk scores used in cardiac surgeries — such as the Revised Cardiac Risk index, R2CHADS2 score, CHADS2 score, and CHA2DS2-VASc score —demonstrate poor performance in noncardiac procedures11–14. Similarly, while useful for predicting various postoperative complications, the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program surgical risk calculator lacks the requisite specificity for predicting perioperative stroke, and its clinical adoption is hampered by labor-intensive manual data collection and suboptimal reliabilityin real-world practice15,16.

Recently, machine learning (ML)-based prediction models have shown promising results in predictingvarious postoperative complications and mortality by capturing complex and nonlinear interactions between variables. Despite these advancements, the development of ML-based models specifically tailored to predict perioperative stroke risk is notably limited, with few studies addressing this critical outcome. For instance, one recent study introduced a model for predicting perioperative stroke in the intensive care unit (ICU) during postoperative periods17; however, extending the application of this model beyond the ICU to more diverse surgical settings may be limited. Moreover, ML-based prediction models mostly encounter difficulties in real clinical settings due to their requirement for a large number of input variables.

In this study, we aimed to bridge these gaps by developinganML-based prediction model for perioperative stroke utilizing preoperative variables to precisely identify patients at high risk before surgery. Additionally, we developed acompact ML model using only the most important featuresto enhance user-friendliness and facilitate clinical applicationwhilea maintaining high predictive accuracy, even with a limitedset of variables. Furthermore, we sought to clarify the association between thesecritical features and perioperative stroke, thereby supporting the interpretability and explainability of our prediction model.

Results

Study population

A total of 42,306 patients undergoing noncardiac surgery were enrolled in SNUH. We excluded the following patients according to the study criteria: 294 patients owing to being < 18 years, 88 patients due to weight, 38 patients due to height, and 3,523 patients with incomplete surgical information. The remaining 36,502 participants were included in the final study population. The study population was then divided into development (n = 32,851) and internal validation (n = 3,651) datasets. The external validation set comprised 404 patients who underwent surgery between 2020 and 2021 at BMC after applying the exclusion criteria(Fig. 1).

Study flow chart.

Baseline characteristics of the population

The SNUH cohort included 36,363 and 139 patients without and with stroke, while the BMC cohort included 394 and 10 patients without and with stroke, respectively. The baseline characteristics of the two cohorts with and without stroke were compared and are presented in Table 1. Patients who experienced strokes were more likely to have hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, and lower hemoglobin and albumin levels than patients who never experienced stroke. The proportion of patients with an American Society of Anesthesiologists(ASA) status and those who underwent surgeries such as intraabdominal or intrathoracic surgery, as well as those who received emergent operations, were different between the two groups.

Results of the ML prediction model

Among the various ML methods, the CatBoost algorithm achieved the best-prediction performance using whole clinical variables, with AUCs of 0.828 and 0.867 in the internal and external validation sets, respectively. This was followed by LightGBM (AUC, 0.823 and 0.771 in the internal and external validation sets, respectively). The DNN did not show superior performance compared to other ML methods (AUC, 0.647 and 0.669 in the internal and external validation sets, respectively). Nonlinear relationship between features and the prediction probability

Fig 5 illustrates the nonlinear relationship between the continuous variables of important features and the prediction probability throughout the PDP. Among the 10 features, several continuous variables demonstrated a nonlinear relationship with the prediction probability. Specifically, age > 60 years was associated with a substantial increase in SHAP values, signifying a markedly higher predicted probability of ischemic stroke in patients aged > 60 years. Similarly, higher ASA scores positively correlated with increased SHAP values, indicating an elevated predicted probability of ischemic stroke with increasing ASA classes. This effect was particularly pronounced at ASA levels > 3, suggesting the presence of a critical threshold beyond which the influence of ASA on stroke risk becomes more significant. Additionally, glucose levels > 100 mg/dL were strongly associated with an increased predicted probability; however, the highest glucose levels did not demonstrate a similarly pronounced association with the predicted probability.

Discussion

We developed a prediction model for perioperative stroke using preoperative variables based on the CatBoost algorithm. To increase the practicality of the model in real-world applications, we developed a more concise, compact model using only the 10 most significant features selected. There were no statistically significant differences in performance between the original and compact models, and the models outperformed traditional scoring systems. Furthermore, we demonstrated a nonlinear relationship between significant features and model performance, which helps to explain the superior performance of our model.

While interest in the clinical application of ML-based prediction models for perioperative stroke has grown, few studies have specifically focused on predicting perioperative stroke. For example, Zhang et al. developed an extreme gradient boosting-based prediction model for perioperative stroke in patients aged > 55 years admitted to the ICU, using demographic data, morbidities, and first-day vital signs. However, their study was limited to a specific population and did not include all relevant preoperative variables17.

By contrast, our study aimed to develop a prediction model for perioperative stroke risk stratification using whole preoperative variables by identifying the 10 most significant features among the data-driven predictors. Additionally, we developed a compact model using the 10 core predictors, which exhibited a comparable performance to that of the original model using all variables. This compact model offers the added advantage of being more easily integrated into EHR data. The clinical implication of our study is that it can help clinicians identify patients at high risk of perioperative stroke and allocate medical resources appropriately at the preoperative stage. Additionally, it is useful for identifying the potentially important risk factors or indicators of perioperative stroke.

In our study, we developed various ML models, including deep learning algorithms, among which the CatBoost model achieved the best performance. The CatBoost model was advantageous in efficiently handling numeric and categorical variables while requiring less computing power18. Additionally, the use of symmetric trees improve training speed, making it more computationally efficient than other gradient boosting algorithms. These features of CatBoost may make it a good fit for handling EHR data, which typically contain a mix of different types of clinical data and can be computationally intensive to analyze. The suitability of the CatBoost model for specific EHR applications will be further evaluated in further studies. Comparing the performance of CatBoost against DNNs, the performance gap may partly result from data imbalances as DNNs are more prone to focusing on the majority class. DNNs generally require larger datasets to effectively learn complex relationships, which becomes particularly challenging when dealing with tabular data. Unlike image or sequential data, which have inherent spatial or temporal correlations that DNNs can exploit, tabular data typically lacks such structures and exhibits irregular, nonlinear relationships19–21. This makes DNNs less efficient with tabular data, as they must learn these relationships from scratch. By contrast, tree-based models like CatBoost naturally capture feature interactions and require less feature engineering and simpler optimization18,20,21. This partly explains CatBoost’s superior performance in our study.

ML-based models have demonstrated superior performance by capturing complex, high-level interactions among a myriad of variables. Despite these advantages, ML-based models face notable challenges in the medical field. One major limitation is the “black box problem,” which restricts interpretability and makes it difficult to understand how predictions are generated22.

Another challenge lies in determining the relative importance of individual variables in influencing predicted outcomes23,24. To address the issue of interpretability, our ML-based model employs SHAP values to extract feature importance, thus partially overcoming the “black box” limitation25. The 10 most important variables identified, which were mostly consistent with previous reports, further supported the reliability and clinical relevance of our model model26. In addition, we utilized PDP to better understand the contributions of individual variables. This approach allowed us to evaluate the impact of continuous variables on the model’s predictive performance.

Interestingly, continuous variable such as age, ASA class, potassium levels, and glucose levels showed a nonlinear contribution to the predictive performance. Compared with traditional scoring systems based on logistic regression analysis, the superior performance of our ML-based prediction model may be due to its ability to overcome the assumption of a linear relationship between variables and outcomes23. After examining the relationship between the variables and predictive performance in this study, we found support for this hypothesis, thus adding to the reliability of the superior performance in our prediction model.

Additionally, several continuous variables showed a specific threshold; for example, age and ASA scores, glucose levels above certain thresholds, and potassium concentrations below certain levels were particularly significant for predicting perioperative stroke. Values outside of the specific thresholds were less important for making accurate predictions. These findings suggest the existence of critical ranges of feature values that are most strongly associated with perioperative stroke. Identifying and understanding these thresholds or range of continuous variables that are most strongly associated with perioperative stoke may enhance the interpretability and usefulness of our ML models. These findings may assist clinicians in understanding this prediction model and may support clinical decision-making based on the model16,24.

This study had some limitations. First, our study revealed lower specificity across all prediction models despite relatively high AUC values, largely due to the class imbalance caused by the rare incidence of perioperative stroke. This data imbalance is one of the problems of prediction models in the medical field27. We attempted to mitigate this problem by undersampling the majority class and oversampling the minority class using SMOTE-NC. SMOTE-NC is a common method for handling data imbalance, it also has limitations, including a lack of theoretical justification27.Despite these challenges, our models outperformed previous cardiovascular scores by carefully selecting modeling techniques and validating across geographically different medical center, reducing the risk of overfitting. In the future, advanced methods like federated learning could better address class imbalances28. Second, the study used data from retrospective cohorts of a single hospital. Nonetheless, we attempted to minimize selection bias by enrolling consecutive patients and applying reasonable exclusion criteria. In addition, the limited sample size and low incidence of positive cases in our external validation dataset al.so constrained the generalizability of our findings, despite our model showed a superior performance in the external dataset. Accordingly, our results should be interpreted with caution. Thus, our study should be regarded as a prototype, rather than confirmatory application in the real-world setting. Further validation in larger, more representative, and multicenter cohorts with multiethnicity will be essential to confirm the robustness and enhance the generalizability of our model.

In this study, we developed a compact perioperative stroke prediction tool using ML, which is available for use before surgery demonstrated highly accurate, and outperformed cardiovascular scores, as well as the original model. The proposed tool may potentially assist the clinicians in stratifying high-risk patients for perioperative stroke. While the current model showed the promising results as a prototype, future studies should be needed to integrate the ML model into the EMR or web-based using interface to enhance the use of ML model in the bedside implementation.

Methods

Dataset

This retrospective observational cohort study was designed to examine the risk of perioperative stroke. The cohort included patients who underwent noncardiac surgery at two tertiary hospitals. The study utilized different datasets to create development, internal validation, and geo-temporal external validation datasets: the development dataset composed patients who underwent surgeries between 2016 and 2018 at Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH); the geo-temporal external validation dataset included data from patients who underwent surgeries between 2020 and 2021 at Boramae Medical Center (BMC). The exclusion criteria were patients aged < 18 years; an operation duration < 20 min; weight > 140 kg or < 30 kg; and height > 200 cm or < 135 cm.

Definition of perioperative stroke

Perioperative stroke was defined as a brain infarction of ischemic etiology that occurred within 30 days after surgery and was documented as a newly developed ischemic lesion via diffusion-weighted imaging2,7. Neurological imaging was performed at the discretion of the attending physician, and a diagnosis of perioperative stroke was confirmed by an experienced neurologist (M-Y Oh) who reviewed the imaging results.

Substituting the mean for continuous variables and the mode for categorical variables is indeed a widely accepted practice in medical research, particularly when the amount of missing data is relatively small and the missing mechanism is assumed to be missing at random (MAR). 29.

Development of perioperative stroke prediction model

We split the data into developmental (90%) and internal validation (10%) sets. During the splitting process, we used stratified sampling to ensure that the class distribution between the proportion of patients who experienced and those who did not experience a stroke remained consistent in both the training and validation sets. We conducted 10-fold cross-validation on the development set for model optimization; the optimized model was then evaluated on the internal validation set, which was kept separate from the training data. After completing the cross-validation process and optimizing the model, we applied the final optimized model to the external dataset, which was tested only once without cross-validation. The best-performing model, which used all clinical variables and had the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) in the development dataset, was selected and validated using both the internal validation and geo-temporal external validation dataset.

We undersampled the majority class (non-stroke) by randomly selecting 278 samples and oversampled the minority class (stroke) using the synthetic minority oversampling technique for nominal and continuous variables (SMOTE-NC) to achieve a 1:1 ratio to mitigate the class imbalance. Specifically, we randomly selected 278 samples from the majority class and oversampled the minority class (139 samples) to 278 using SMOTE. This resulted in a total of 556 samples (278 from each class) being used for model training32. We applied traditional methods — including Logistic Regression33, Support Vector Machine34, K-Nearest Neighbor35, Decision Tree Classifier36, RandomForest37, AdaBoost38, eXtreme Gradient Boosting39, LightGBM40, CatBoost18, and Deep Neural Network (DNN)41 — to develop the prediction model. Model compactization

We then used feature selection methods, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)25,43 to identify the most predictive variables and confirmed that these were consistent with established stroke risk factors and relevant. Based on this, we developed a compact model using only these selected key features, which improved interpretability and facilitated more reliable external validation compared to using all variables. In addition, we also compared the performance of the compact model with the original model, which used all 29 variables.

Model interpretation

The model was interpretated through an analysis of feature importance utilizing the SHAP values, which express the influence of a variable on a prediction; this was computed using the prediction outcome of each possible combination of features25,43. Additionally, partial dependence plots (PDPs) were generated to provide a visual representation of the relationship between continuous variables and the probability of a successful prediction and to further understand how changes in individual predictors can impact the overall prediction24,44.

Statistical analysis

The characteristics of patients who experienced and those who did not experience a stroke were compared using the Student’s t-test for continuous variables and Chi-squared test for categorical variables. All tests were two-sided, and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were also calculated to assess the predictive performance of the models. The DeLong test was used to compare the difference between the two AUCs of the models using different features45. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. ML and DNN modeling were performed in Python 3.8 using the Scikit-Learn46 and Pytorch packages, and SHAP(version 0.40.0) values were used. We also validated the CatBoost-based model using confusion matrices for both the internal and external validation sets.
