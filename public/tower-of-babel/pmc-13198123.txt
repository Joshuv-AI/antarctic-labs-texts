Case Summary

A 51-year-old man presented with persistent fatigue and exertional lightheadedness. He was previously evaluated for Brugada syndrome (BrS) after dynamic electrocardiographic (ECG) changes during febrile illness and reported a remote syncopal episode with fall from scaffolding resulting in hip fracture. Initial electrophysiology study demonstrated inducible ventricular fibrillation. Implantable cardiac-defibrillator (ICD) was recommended but declined. Subsequently, he experienced additional febrile syncopal episodes. Past medical history included depression managed with duloxetine 60 mg daily. Family history was positive for sudden death in a maternal grandfather in his 40s. After counseling regarding guideline-recommended ICD implantation and the investigational nature of epicardial ablation for BrS,1,2 the patient consented to the procedure. Epicardial electroanatomic mapping (Abbott EnSite Precision) identified low-voltage substrate (bipolar: <1.5 mV) with local abnormal ventricular activity (LAVA) in the anterior right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT). Coronary angiography confirmed safe distance from the left anterior descending artery. Procainamide 500 mg IV provoked type I Brugada pattern on electrocardiogram. Simultaneously, intracardiac electrograms demonstrated prolongation of local RVOT activation time with reduced late potential frequency (Figure 1B).

Radiofrequency ablation (25 W) targeted LAVA with partial ECG resolution. Repeat procainamide revealed residual LAVA, prompting additional ablation. LAVA elimination achieved ECG normalization despite ongoing procainamide effect. Total ablation comprised 66 lesions over 3,174 seconds (Figure 1C). Postablation study demonstrated noninducibility of ventricular arrhythmias with double extrastimuli (450/240/220 milliseconds). Leadless atrial pacemaker (AVEIR AR) was implanted for chronotropic incompetence. At 7-month follow-up, the patient remained free from syncope and with persistent ECG normalization.

Discussion

This case describes U.S. experience with epicardial substrate ablation for gene-negative BrS. Current guidelines recommend ICD implantation in BrS patients with inducible ventricular fibrillation and history of syncope, but catheter ablation is not mentioned as an adjunctive therapy. However, recent European trials such as BRAVO1 (Brugada ablation of VF substrate ongoing multicenter registry) and BRAVE (Brugada syndrome ablation for the prevention of ventricular fibrillation episodes)2 have demonstrated feasibility and efficacy targeting LAVA in the RVOT epicardium with reduction in ventricular arrhythmia and ICD therapy postablation. The differential included BrS (confirmed), arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (excluded by imaging and voltage pattern), early repolarization variant (excluded by morphology), acute coronary syndrome (excluded by presentation and normal coronary angiography), and vasovagal syncope (possible, although fall from scaffolding and recurrent febrile syncope are concerning for ventricular arrhythmia).

Examination of epicardial electrograms may provide mechanistic insight into the pathophysiology of BrS. Sodium channel blockade–induced type I Brugada ECG pattern dynamically correlated with prolongation and reduced frequency of delayed fractionated signal in the RVOT epicardium, and ablation of this area abolished these signals with corresponding normalization of the type I Brugada ECG pattern despite continued procainamide effect. Both transmural dispersion of repolarization and reduced conduction velocity leading to regional depolarization delay have been hypothesized as putative mechanisms for ECG changes and arrhythmia in BrS.3 Procainamide-induced delay in local RVOT epicardial activation correlating with provocation of type I Brugada ECG pattern suggests that regionally delayed depolarization is contributory to the Brugada ECG pattern, manifesting as widening of the QRS and pseudo–ST-segment elevation.

Multiple clinical pearls are highlighted in this case. Gene-negative patients with BrS may harbor abnormal substrate that demonstrates atypical LAVA in the epicardium. These epicardial RVOT electrograms have an atypical appearance distinctly different from LAVA electrograms in other pathologies and correlate with pseudo–ST-segment changes. Procainamide may be diagnostically useful in BrS and may guide ablation therapy. Chronotropic incompetence can be associated with BrS, suggesting a pleiotropic genetic effect. Although ICD remains guideline-indicated, epicardial ablation may offer protective effects in BrS.
