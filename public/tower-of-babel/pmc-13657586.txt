Acute mesenteric ischemia caused by adhesive-band-induced mesenteric artery compression with secondary ileal volvulus and internal hernia: a rare case report
Authors: Bahaa Ibrahim AbuRahmeh, Mohamad A. Banat, Ahmad G. Hammouri, Ahmad J.A. Abuarqoub, Yara Al Najajreh, Sulaiman Naji Fakhouri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Acute mesenteric ischemia (AMI) is a surgical emergency caused by inadequate blood flow through the mesenteric vessels, resulting in intestinal ischemia and eventual gangrene of the bowel wall. The condition may result from arterial thrombosis, embolism, mesenteric venous thrombosis, or non-occlusive systemic hypoperfusion.Case presentation:We report a rare case of AMI in a 73-year-old female with a significant past surgical history of transabdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oopherectomy. She presented with sudden, severe abdominal pain that was disproportionate to the relatively benign examination; she was unresponsive to multiple analgesics, raising suspicion of an underlying surgical pathology. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) showed ileal-like volvulus with vascular compromise, and features of internal hernia, prompting emergent surgery, which confirmed adhesive-band-induced mesenteric compression with the other findings. At first glance, the surgeon gave a chance to bowel; a second-look operation after 48 hours insisted on the inevitable need for resecting the involved segment of bowel.Discussion:Patients with AMI typically present with sudden-onset abdominal pain that is disproportionate to the physical examination findings (soft abdomen with mild or absent tenderness on palpation). Laboratory investigations may support the diagnosis but lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity for definitive diagnosis. CTA is the imaging modality of choice for establishing the diagnosis.Conclusion:This case highlights a rare mechanism of AMI. It emphasizes the importance of taking a good history and doing a good examination, and highlights the importance of early diagnosis and management of critical surgical conditions such as AMI.

AMI due to embolism is often cardiogenic in origin and primarily affects the superior mesenteric artery (SMA). Preceding events include atrial tachyarrhythmia, congestive heart failure, myocardial ischemia or infarction, cardiomyopathy, and ventricular aneurysm, which result in thrombus formation that later embolizes and causes ischemia. Patients with acute mesenteric arterial thrombosis commonly have preexisting atherosclerotic disease. Vasospasm in the SMA often accompanies non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia secondary to cardiac failure, peripheral hypoxemia, or reperfusion injury[2].

The early hallmark of mesenteric ischemia is severe pain, with minimal physical findings; the abdomen remains soft, with little or no tenderness. Mild tachycardia can also occur. Later, as necrosis develops, signs of peritonitis appear, with marked abdominal tenderness, guarding, rigidity, and no bowel sounds. Stool may be heme-positive (the likelihood increases as ischemia progresses). The usual signs of shock develop and are frequently followed by death. A sudden onset of pain suggests, but is not diagnostic of, an arterial embolism, whereas a more gradual onset is typical of venous thrombosis. Patients with a history of postprandial abdominal discomfort (which suggests intestinal angina) may have arterial thrombosis[3].

Early diagnosis of mesenteric ischemia is particularly important because mortality increases significantly once intestinal infarction occurs. If the diagnosis remains uncertain, mesenteric angiography or computed tomography angiography (CTA) can be performed, although the clinical features are generally more critical than imaging studies. Mesenteric ischemia must be considered in any patient older than 50 years with known risk factors or predisposing conditions who develops sudden, severe abdominal pain[3].

Patients with clear peritoneal signs should proceed directly to the operating room for diagnosis and treatment. For others, selective mesenteric angiography or CTA is the diagnostic procedure of choice. Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) can be used if there is a contraindication to standard IV contrast[3].

Other imaging studies and serum markers can show abnormalities, but lack sensitivity and specificity early in the course of the disease, when an early diagnosis is critical. Plain abdominal radiographs are useful mainly in ruling out other causes of pain (such as a perforated viscus), although pneumatosis intestinalis or portal venous gas may be seen late in the disease. These findings also appear on computed tomography (CT) scans, which can also directly visualize vascular occlusion, more accurately on the venous side. Doppler ultrasound can sometimes identify arterial occlusions, but its sensitivity is low. MRI is very accurate for proximal vascular occlusion but is less accurate for distal vascular occlusion. Serum markers (such as creatine kinase and lactate) rise with necrosis but are nonspecific findings that are seen later[3].

Initial management should begin immediately by providing oxygen, fluid resuscitation, and analgesia, and intravenous (IV) antibiotics should be administered if signs of perforation or sepsis from bacterial translocation are present. IV heparin infusion (anticoagulation) is the mainstay of treatment for acute mesenteric venous thrombosis. Keep the patient nil per os, as the operation might be performed at any time after diagnosis[4].

The key decision in definitive management is whether to proceed with immediate laparotomy, use an endovascular approach, or rely only on medical treatment. Laparotomy is mandatory if peritonitis or other signs of intestinal infarction are present (unless the palliative approach is indicated, using P-POSSUM and NELA scoring systems that aid in preoperative decision-making). Laparoscopy is not recommended for assessing the extent of ischemia. There should be a low threshold for second-look laparotomy (or laparotomy after an endovascular procedure)[4]. In this paper, we present a clinical entity (AMI) in an atypical context in a 73-year-old female with a significant history of TAH-BSO; AMI was caused by a far less common cause after an old surgery, underscoring the importance of taking a comprehensive history and performing a physical examination and always keeping a high index of suspicion for the diagnosis of AMI even with a modest history profile. In addition, it highlights the presence of a potential class of etiologies, namely external compression of the mesenteric artery from any mechanical cause. Methods

This case report describes the clinical presentation, diagnostic workup, surgical management, and clinical outcomes of a 73-year-old female patient who presented with acute abdominal pain for a couple of hours, with an unremarkable past medical history. Data were collected through clinical assessment, imaging studies, and histopathologic and surgical evaluations. A comprehensive literature review was conducted using PubMed and Google Scholar to identify previously reported cases of AMI caused by external mechanical compression of the mesenteric vessels. To the best of our knowledge, no published reports describing the same underlying mechanism were identified. This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the SCARE (Surgical Case Report) 2025 checklist.

Case presentation

A 73-year-old female presented to the emergency department (ED) due to the sudden onset of severe, persistent, diffuse abdominal pain, predominantly involving the left side of the abdomen. The pain had no obvious aggravating or relieving factors and was associated with two episodes of vomiting. Initially, she was referred to our ED with a presumed case of acute stress reaction (psychosomatic condition) after a particularly stressful social event. She received IV paracetamol for analgesia, intramuscular promethazine for symptomatic relief, and diazepam for its anxiolytic and sedative effects. Despite these interventions, the patient remained in severe abdominal pain, raising concern for an underlying organic, particularly surgical, condition.

Her last bowel motion had occurred on the morning of the same day. The patient’s past medical history was unremarkable except for TAH-BSO, performed approximately 10 years ago. It is worth mentioning that she also reported long-standing chronic constipation, with bowel movements occurring approximately every ten days, occasionally requiring manual evacuation. She had no significant family, drug, or social history, and no known drug or food allergies.

On examination, the patient appeared acutely ill and in severe distress due to unbearable abdominal pain. She was conscious, alert, and oriented to time, place, and person. Interestingly, the abdomen was soft, without voluntary guarding or rigidity, but with minimal tenderness, predominantly in the right lower quadrant. This marked discrepancy between the severity of pain and a relatively benign abdominal examination strongly raises clinical suspicion of AMI. Digital rectal examination was normal and unremarkable, ruling out fecal impaction, which may cause a mechanical intestinal obstruction.

Laboratory investigations included a complete blood count, urinalysis, kidney and liver function tests, serum amylase level, lactate dehydrogenase level, serum electrolytes (Na+, K+, Cl−, and glucose), and C-reactive protein level. All results were normal except for a D-dimer level of 1077 ng/mL (reference upper limit, 500 ng/mL) and mild leukocytosis. Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis, as shown in Table 1, revealed high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis with compensated respiratory alkalosis due to elevated serum lactate levels and was suggestive of tissue hypoperfusion, and these results were correlated with AMI. Standing abdominal radiography shows an unremarkable, normal bowel gas pattern without air-fluid levels or dilated bowel loops. Abdominal imaging revealed incidental findings of excessive bowel gas and fatty liver.

The CTA images (Fig. 1) showed a cluster of mildly thickened ileal small-bowel loops within the lower mid-abdomen, with a characteristic C-shaped configuration. Whirling of the mesenteric vessels, mesenteric fat edema, and convergence and peaking of the entering and exiting bowel segments toward the aforementioned cluster were observed. The involved mucosa and bowel wall showed relative hypo-enhancement within the bowel cluster, while the supplying mesenteric arterial branches were faintly enhanced, suggesting ischemic changes and impaired vascular perfusion. A small amount of free intra-abdominal fluid was observed. These findings were highly suggestive of a closed-loop small-bowel obstruction secondary to either an internal hernia or volvulus. An emergency midline laparotomy was performed immediately, based on radiological findings and a high clinical suspicion of bowel ischemia. The patient was transferred to the operating room after preparation in accordance with hospital protocols in the emergency setting.

Intraoperatively, an approximately 80 cm length of ileal segment was found to be dusky and cyanotic at first glance (Fig. 2(A), suggesting ischemic bowel, along with twisting around its mesentery (mimicking ileal volvulus) and adhesive bands that wrapped around the ileal branch of the SMA and the ileal mesentery, leading to mechanical ischemia from external compression. Additionally, a band of fibrotic adhesions connected the antimesenteric border of the mid-ileum to the mesentery of the sigmoid colon (Fig. 2(B), enabling an ileal segment to protrude inferiorly through this arch, creating an internal hernia. The intraoperative findings suggested adhesive-band-induced mesenteric compression as the cause of AMI, with secondary ileal volvulus and internal hernia. Intraoperatively, the adhesive bands around the ileal segment were carefully divided, and the involved segment was released and detorsed. Following restoration of blood flow, the involved bowel segment was soaked in warm normal saline to allow re-evaluation after reperfusion, and almost all of the segment returned to a pink color; therefore, the decision was not to remove any part of the segment. A second-look operation performed after 2 days showed severe bowel wall congestion causing marked luminal narrowing. Multiple clots were found within the mesentery, mandating resection of the involved bowel with side-to-side anastomosis (Fig. 2(C), suggesting an acute thrombotic event after the first operation or retorsion of the ileal segment as an early post-operative complication. The post-operative course was complicated by several surgical and medical complications requiring prolonged management in the surgical intensive care unit, where the patient received close observation and multidisciplinary supportive care.

Discussion

AMI can be defined as a sudden decline in blood flow through the mesenteric vessels. The progression of this condition is rapid and can result in mortality in 60–80% of cases. Diagnosis can be difficult due to non-specific symptoms, which is why a high index of clinical suspicion is needed, with early diagnosis and treatment, to prevent or reduce associated complications and mortality[2].

AMI can be caused by different etiologies, including mesenteric arterial occlusion from embolism (49% of cases) or thrombosis (29% of cases), which is considered the most common cause of AMI, followed by non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia (20–22% of cases) from splanchnic hypoperfusion and vasoconstriction, which is commonly seen in critically ill patients with low cardiac output, hypovolemia, or receiving vasoconstrictive drugs such as digitalis, vasopressors, ergotamines, and others. Additionally, AMI can be due to mesenteric venous thrombosis (10% of cases), which may result from a hypercoagulable state, portal hypertension, and malignancy[6]. Our patient did not have any of the established risk factors for the different etiologies of AMI, such as atherosclerosis, atrial fibrillation, aortic aneurysm, and a hypercoagulable state, except for the patient’s age, which is considered a risk factor for AMI[7,8].

Diagnosis requires a compatible history and physical examination, and a definitive diagnosis of AMI is made using high-resolution CTA to demonstrate vascular pathology, bowel wall thickening or hemorrhage, or bowel dilatation. Laboratory tests in patients with AMI can reveal leukocytosis, high anion gap metabolic acidosis, and elevated lactate levels, which were present in our case along with compensated respiratory alkalosis (see Table 1); however, there is no specific diagnostic biomarker for AMI, which makes the diagnosis of AMI difficult[9,10].

This case highlights a rare etiology of AMI, as intraoperative findings revealed adhesive bands compressing the vasculature of the ileal mesentery, leading to AMI as the primary mechanism, in addition to forming an arch that created an internal hernia of a small intestinal loop and torsion of this ileal loop around its mesentery, resulting in an ileal volvulus as additional secondary mechanisms. All of these complications are the result of the aforementioned major abdominal surgery, demonstrating the importance of obtaining a past surgical history, even if it is very old, and a thorough history including the history of the present illness, as AMI pain begins suddenly and remains constant.

If the abdominal pain is diffuse, ill-localized, and out-of-proportion to the physical exam (the patient is shouting even though his abdomen is soft with no or mild tenderness), AMI must be considered, along with other differential diagnoses such as abdominal compartment syndrome, necrotizing fasciitis, diabetic ketoacidosis, acute cutaneous porphyria, and sickle cell crisis, each of which has its own clinical picture and occurs in specific clinical contexts [8].

In AMI, reduced intestinal perfusion leads to cellular hypoxia, which causes a metabolic shift from aerobic to anaerobic glycolysis, resulting in the accumulation of lactic acid and contributing to high anion gap metabolic acidosis, which is typically reflected on ABG analysis as low pH and decreased bicarbonate levels. An elevated serum lactate level is a sensitive, though not specific, marker for AMI and indicates the severity of tissue hypoperfusion. D-dimer levels may be elevated due to thrombotic processes but lack specificity. As a compensatory mechanism for metabolic acidosis, patients often hyperventilate, inducing secondary respiratory alkalosis. These laboratory abnormalities, particularly lactic acidosis and an elevated anion gap, are critical early indicators in the diagnosis of AMI; they explain the pH shifting toward the direction of acidosis (normal pH range, 7.35–7.45) but remaining within the normal range due to compensation (the pH in our case was 7.36, near the lower limit of the normal range)[11,12].

AMI requires a high index of clinical suspicion to detect it and avoid missing it, as it requires the earliest possible diagnosis to yield a better prognosis and prevent its serious complications, including bowel necrosis and its consequences (such as sepsis and septic shock), perforation, diffuse acute peritonitis, and extensive bowel resection, which can lead to short bowel syndrome and, ultimately, the need for total or partial parenteral nutrition[13].

This case also highlights an atypical and underreported cause of AMI, which is mechanical compression of mesenteric vessels from adhesive bands. Through an extensive review of the current literature, we found no studies that classify adhesive bands as a recognized etiology within the established categories of AMI – mesenteric arterial occlusion, non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia, and mesenteric venous thrombosis – as previously described [6]. Given this gap in classification, we propose adding a fourth category that includes causes that lead to external mechanical compression of mesenteric vessels. This category would include rare but clinically significant causes such as compression by adhesive bands, intra-abdominal tumors, large hematomas, or other space-occupying lesions. Recognizing these mechanical factors as distinct etiologies is important for timely diagnosis and intervention, especially in patients without traditional risk factors for AMI.

Psychiatric, anxiety, and psychosomatic conditions must be considered after ruling out organic causes. Additionally, we must consider more serious medical conditions when the patient does not improve with adequate analgesia[14].

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare mechanism of AMI caused by adhesive-band-induced external compression of the mesenteric artery. This case report highlights the importance of taking a good history and performing a good examination, because both provide an idea of the most likely diagnosis and help ensure acute or critical conditions are not missed. As discussed in this case, the patient did not have any risk factors for AMI except advanced age and had a significant past surgical history of TAH-BSO, which can lead to adhesions as a common complication; this highlights the importance of history-taking and of not missing important information, even if it occurred a very long time ago.

Additionally, this case highlights the importance of early diagnosis and management of critical surgical conditions such as AMI, which can lead to better outcomes and lower rates of morbidity and mortality. Furthermore, allowing borderline bowel of questionable viability to remain in situ, especially if the segment is long enough, can be a beneficial approach to decrease the incidence of post-operative surgical and medical complications from performing resection and end-to-end anastomosis alone, particularly as anastomosis may lead to the development of short-bowel syndrome later on, with a crucial role for a second-look operation at 48 hours to ensure sustained bowel viability.

Psychosomatic or psychiatric disorders, in general, must be considered after excluding serious organic causes, as they are diagnoses of exclusion and not to be applied in urgent clinical conditions such as AMI.
