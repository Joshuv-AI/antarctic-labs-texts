The Involvement of the Omentum in Defensive Peritoneal Mechanisms: New Evidence from Dogs Naturally Infected with Leishmania infantum
Authors: Hélio Noberto de Araújo, Telma de Sousa Lima, Moacir Franco de Oliveira, Radan Elvis Matias de Oliveira, Francisco Antônio Félix Xavier, Tiago Cunha Ferreira, Daniel de Araújo Viana, Diana Célia Sousa Nunes-Pinheiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Simple SummaryCanine leishmaniosis is an important disease that affects dogs and is of major public health relevance, since these animals serve as the primary urban reservoir for leishmaniosis. Although the disease is usually studied in organs such as the spleen, liver, and bone marrow, little is known about the role of the omentum, a fatty tissue located inside the abdomen that helps protect organs and participates in the body’s immune defense. This study investigated changes in the omentum of dogs naturally infected with the leishmaniosis parasite and their association with blood inflammatory markers. Infected dogs presented anemia, blood protein alterations, and signs of chronic inflammation. Inflammation, fibrosis, and chronic lesions known as granulomas were observed in the omentum, while parasite forms were found within macrophages, supporting the involvement of this tissue. More severe omental damage was associated with more pronounced blood changes, especially anemia. These findings demonstrate that the omentum may act as an important site for parasite persistence and disease progression. In addition, blood markers such as albumin and neutrophil-related changes may aid veterinarians in improving the diagnosis, prognosis, and clinical monitoring of affected dogs.

1. Introduction

The omentum has been increasingly recognized not only as a peritoneal extension for visceral protection but also as a highly dynamic immunometabolic organ that actively participates in immune surveillance [1]. Its rich vascularization, extensive lymphatic network, and high immune cell density make it an important abdominal cavity defense site, acting in antigen capture, leukocyte recruitment, and the containment of infectious and inflammatory processes [2,3]. It may, however, also favor the persistence of infectious agents and inflammation chronicity [4].

This functional duality makes the omentum particularly relevant in canine leishmaniosis (CanL), a zoonosis of major One Health importance caused by Leishmania infantum, with dogs comprising the main urban reservoir in endemic areas. This infection exhibits tropism for mononuclear phagocyte system cells, particularly macrophages, triggering a complex inflammatory response, as well as multisystemic manifestations ranging from subclinical forms to severe conditions involving cutaneous, hematological, and visceral impairment [5,6,7].

Studies on CanL immunopathogenesis typically focus on classical lymphoid and visceral organs, such as the spleen, liver, bone marrow, and lymph nodes, in which the presence of the parasite and inflammatory alterations have been widely described. However, increasing evidence has demonstrated that L. infantum exhibits a broad capacity for tissue dissemination, reaching still poorly explored extranodal compartments, including the peritoneal cavity, cavitary fluids, and visceral adipose tissues [8,9,10,11]. These findings expand the understanding of infection dynamics, indicating that CanL may involve distinct microenvironments beyond classically recognized sites.

In this context, the omentum role in CanL immunopathogenesis is still poorly understood, with scarce studies investigating omental inflammatory responses in naturally infected dogs and rare reports of amastigote forms in this tissue. Considering its unique immunological composition and its close anatomical relationship with the abdominal cavity, the omentum may represent not only a secondary inflammatory involvement site but also a potential immunologically active compartment associated with parasite persistence and chronic inflammation.

Given the above, this study aimed to assess potential omentum involvement in the immune-inflammatory response of dogs naturally infected with L. infantum through histopathological tissue characterizations, amastigote form identification and systemic inflammatory biomarker correlations.

2. Material and Methods

A total of 40 adult male and female mixed-breed dogs between 1 and 14 years old and weighing up to 35 kg were investigated. All animals underwent complete clinical examinations followed by serological CanL testing, including the rapid immunochromatographic test DPP® LCan (Bio-Manguinhos®, Rio de Janeiro, RJ, Brazil) and an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), according to Brazilian Ministry of Health recommendations [12].

Following clinical and serological evaluations, animals were classified into two study groups, namely a control group, consisting of 10 asymptomatic seronegative dogs, and a symptomatic group, comprising 30 dogs naturally infected with L. infantum and presenting clinical manifestations compatible with CanL. Clinical manifestations were recorded as binary variables (absent = 0, present = 1), including weight loss, cachexia, skin lesions, keratoconjunctivitis, ophthalmopathy, onychogryphosis, lymphadenomegaly, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, ascites, and seizures.

Obese animals and those with evident neoplasms, diagnosed coinfections, systemic septic processes, abdominal inflammatory diseases unrelated to leishmaniosis, and severe macroscopic alterations that could compromise histopathological omentum interpretations were excluded from the study. Vector-borne coinfections were screened using the SNAP® 4Dx® Plus Test (IDEXX Laboratories, Westbrook, ME, USA). Additionally, control dogs were required to be seronegative, clinically healthy, and free of abdominal inflammatory lesions upon surgical inspection.

Complete blood counts and serum biochemical analyses were performed by the same reference laboratory. Hematological parameters were determined employing an automated veterinary hematology analyzer (BC-2800Vet, Mindray®, Shenzhen, China), with microscopic blood smear evaluations and biochemical analyses that included serum urea, creatinine, alkaline phosphatase, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, total protein, albumin, globulins, and bilirubin levels. These data were subsequently used to calculate neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios (NLR), neutrophil-to-albumin ratios (NAR), albumin-to-globulin ratios (AGR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratios (PLR), and monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratios (MLR), all of which were investigated as systemic inflammatory biomarkers and correlated with microscopic omental findings.

Dogs belonging to the symptomatic group were subjected to pre-anesthetic medication employing acepromazine maleate (0.05 mg/kg), xylazine hydrochloride (1 mg/kg), and ketamine hydrochloride (10 mg/kg), administered intramuscularly. Following anesthetic plane confirmation, absence of nociceptive reflexes, and complete loss of consciousness, a 19.1% potassium chloride solution (1 mL/kg) was administered intravenously to induce cardiorespiratory arrest, according to humane euthanasia recommendations [13].

Following death confirmation, median celiotomies were performed for abdominal cavity and omentum inspections, evaluating visceral adipose tissue color, vascularization degree, potential tissue thickening and adhesions, friability, and any alterations suggestive of chronic inflammatory processes, such as opacity, fibrosis, and tissue retraction. Four omental fragments were then collected from each animal, measuring approximately 4 cm2, prioritizing areas free of traumatic or surgical artifacts. Regarding control animals, samples were opportunistically obtained during elective surgical procedures involving abdominal cavity access, with prior informed owner consent.

All tissue fragments were washed in phosphate-buffered saline and fixed in 10% buffered formalin for 24 to 48 h. Following fixation, the samples were routinely processed for histopathological assessments, by embedding in paraffin, sectioning into 2 µm thick sections, and staining with hematoxylin and eosin (HE).

Lesions were evaluated regarding chronic inflammation, vasodilation, fibrosis, granuloma formation, and intralesional amastigote Leishmania spp. forms. When present, inflammatory infiltrates were further categorized according to their predominant cellular composition and tissue distribution. Histological change severity was assessed applying a semiquantitative scoring system adapted from Allenspach et al. [14], according to a four-point scale (0–3), corresponding to absent, mild, moderate, and severe lesions, respectively. The final score for each parameter was calculated as the means of independent evaluations performed by two veterinary pathologists under a double-blind protocol.

All statistical analyses were performed using the Python 3.11 software (SciPy 1.13, statsmodels 0.14, and scikit-learn 1.5), with reproduction in RStudio (2026.07.0). Continuous variable normality was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test, and variance homogeneity was evaluated by Levene’s test based on data medians (Brown–Forsythe). Parametric variables were compared using Student’s t-test or Welch’s t-test, according to variance homogeneity, while non-parametric variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Results were expressed as means ± standard deviations or medians, with effect sizes calculated using Cohen’s d or Cliff’s δ, as appropriate.

Histological scores were analyzed by the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons, and the Jonckheere–Terpstra test for monotonic trend evaluations. Correlations between ordinal and quantitative variables were assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient (ρ). Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test or Pearson’s chi-square test, with Cramér’s V as a measure of effect size, in addition to a simple correspondence analysis for visualization.

The discriminatory capacity of the investigated inflammatory biomarkers was assessed by ROC curves, calculating the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals, and determinations of the optimal cutoff point using Youden’s index. Corrections for multiple comparisons were performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, adopting a significance level of 5% (α = 0.05).

Given the exploratory nature of this study and the relatively small sample size employed, particularly the control group, all statistical analyses were interpreted cautiously, with emphasis on effect sizes, confidence intervals, and correction for multiple comparisons rather than on isolated p-values.

3. Results

Dermatological manifestations were the most predominant alterations in CanL-positive dogs, with exfoliative lesions (90%), onychogryphosis (76.7%), and ulcerative lesions (56.7%) being the most frequent. The main systemic and visceral findings were splenomegaly (73.3%), weight loss (63.3%), lymphadenomegaly (50%), cachexia (36.7%), and hepatomegaly (23.3%). Ocular alterations were also expressive, with ophthalmopathies identified in 43.3% of the animals and keratoconjunctivitis, in 40%. Less frequent manifestations included ascites, hyperkeratosis, and seizure episodes, each observed in 3.3% of the cases.

Regarding systemic alterations, CanL-positive dogs presented hematobiochemical profiles compatible with normocytic normochromic anemia and dysproteinemia associated with hypoalbuminemia and hyperglobulinemia (Figure 1 and Figure 2). In addition, statistically significant reductions in AGR associated with increased NAR values were observed, with both inflammatory biomarkers displaying the greatest discriminatory power between groups, respectively (Figure 2 and Figure A1 and Table 1).

Macroscopically, increased vascularization, mild tissue thickening, and focal opacity areas were more frequent in the omentum of CanL-positive dogs. Mild adhesions to adjacent structures and greater friability were also observed in some cases. In control animals, the omentum was uniform in color, with preserved vascularization and no relevant macroscopic alterations.

Microscopically, preserved fibro-adipose architecture and rich vascularization were observed in the omentum of CanL-positive dogs, although chronic inflammatory alterations were observed in 86.7% of the cases, mainly characterized by diffuse lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate (53.3%), followed by perivascular (23.3%) and focal (13.3%) patterns. Granulomatous infiltrates characterized by the presence of reactive macrophages and epithelioid cells were noted in 10% of the samples. Hyperemia and interstitial fibrosis were identified in 40% and 26.7% of the cases, respectively. Amastigote Leishmania spp. forms were identified in 13.3% of the samples within the macrophage cytoplasm, mainly in areas associated with granulomatous infiltrates (Figure 3).

Chronic omentum inflammation degrees differed substantially between groups (δ = −0.46, p = 0.023), observed in only 40% of the control animals and categorized as mild, whereas CanL-positive dogs displayed a higher frequency of omental inflammation and more advanced involvement degrees, demonstrating greater tissue inflammatory impairment associated with infection.

The association between histological grade and type of inflammatory infiltrate was strongly demonstrated by Cramér’s V test (0.767) and Spearman’s correlation test (ρ = 0.901; p < 0.001), indicating an ordered inflammatory response progression. Animals presenting mild inflammation presented exclusively lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates. In moderate cases, this pattern was maintained in 71%, whereas 29% presented reactive macrophages, suggesting innate immunity recruitment. Finally, 60% of animals presenting severe inflammation exhibited granulomatous infiltrates, evidencing chronicity and greater local inflammatory response organization (Figure 4).

The inflammatory biomarkers investigated herein displayed good discriminatory capacity between groups (Figure 2), albeit with no consistent stratification among chronic omental inflammation grades (Figure 5). In contrast, the assessed erythrogram variables displayed a more consistent monotonic trend, progressively decreasing with increasing inflammatory grade (Figure 6). This indicates that omental inflammatory involvement is more strongly associated with systemic anemia severity than with classical inflammatory ratios.

Clinically relevant associations were observed between omentum systemic alterations, inflammatory biomarkers, and histological involvement in CanL-positive dogs (Figure A2). A strong negative correlation was observed between globulins and AGR (ρ = −0.78), as well as between lymphocytes and both NLR (ρ = −0.82) and PLR (ρ = −0.85), reinforcing lymphopenia and dysproteinemia systemic inflammatory response effects. Complementarily, a strong positive correlation was observed between albumin and AGR (ρ = +0.78), in addition to high concordance among erythrogram variables (ρ > 0.94), evidencing anemic profile consistency associated with CanL.

Regarding histological alterations, a higher degree of omental fibrosis was associated with reduced red blood cell and platelet counts (ρ = −0.37), whereas greater vasodilation was linked to decreased globulin and total protein levels (ρ = −0.35). A trend toward increased ALT levels was also observed with increasing inflammatory grade (ρ = +0.15), suggesting potential repercussions on other abdominal organs. Additionally, greater clinical severity was typically associated with increased NLR (ρ = +0.38), MLR (ρ = +0.36), and PLR (ρ = +0.35), indicating that clinical CanL progression is accompanied by intensified systemic inflammatory responses and greater omental microenvironment impairment.

4. Discussion

The clinical and laboratory findings reported herein reinforce the systemic, progressive, and inflammatory nature of CanL, particularly in symptomatic dogs naturally infected with L. infantum. This clinical profile is consistent with that described in the literature for dogs with symptomatic CanL and reflects broad tissue parasite dissemination, as well as persistent mononuclear phagocyte system activation [5,15,16].

The high splenomegaly and lymphadenomegaly frequencies observed in the present study may be attributed to persistent antigenic stimulation and the consequent reactive hyperplasia of lymphoid organs, whereas dermatological and ophthalmic alterations often result from immune complex deposition, vasculitis, and chronic inflammatory infiltrates, which are classically involved in CanL immunopathogenesis [17].

From a hematological perspective, normocytic normochromic anemia was observed, representing one of the most consistent CanL laboratory abnormalities and frequently associated with chronic inflammatory disease. This results from the persistent production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, reduced bone marrow due to erythropoietin, iron sequestration within the mononuclear phagocyte system, and increased erythrophagocytosis by activated macrophages [17,18]. In more advanced cases, renal impairment may also contribute to anemia progression by reducing erythropoietin production and exacerbating systemic deterioration [15].

Additionally, dysproteinemia associated with hypoalbuminemia and hyperglobulinemia represents one of the most characteristic CanL findings. Reduced albumin levels are frequently associated with the negative acute-phase response, hepatic alterations, renal losses secondary to immune-mediated glomerulopathy, and chronic nutritional impairment, whereas hyperglobulinemia results from persistent antigenic stimulation and an intense humoral immune response [5,15,17], directly contributing to decreased AGR.

Among the investigated systemic inflammatory biomarkers, AGR demonstrated the best diagnostic performance. A similar finding was described by Araújo Júnior et al. [11] in an advanced case of CanL associated with ascites and presumed monoclonal gammopathy, in which a marked AGR reduction was accompanied by an intense inflammatory response and the presence of parasitized cells in peritoneal fluid, reinforcing its value as an indicator of greater systemic impairment in CanL cases.

Furthermore, NAR is also noted as displaying relevant discriminatory capacity, as this parameter integrates cellular inflammatory responses with the metabolic-protein alterations associated with CanL. Unlike NLR, the inclusion of albumin increases its clinical sensitivity as an acute-phase response and systemic severity marker. Furthermore, the relative increase in neutrophils during initial Leishmania responses may reflect not only systemic inflammation but also parasite persistence and inflammatory process maintenance [11,19,20]. Thus, the superior NAR performance verified herein reinforces its potential as a complementary biomarker in clinical CanL assessments.

In contrast, NLR, PLR and MLR, although frequently associated with systemic inflammation and clinical severity in CanL cases [21], exhibited lower discriminatory capacity in the omental inflammation assessment carried out herein, potentially due to the chronic and persistent pathophysiology of the disease. Conversely, in diseases characterized by exacerbated acute inflammatory responses, such as parvovirosis associated with systemic inflammatory response syndrome, both PLR and platelet-to-neutrophil ratios (PNR) display better performance due to intense leukocytic and platelet responses [22].

In this context, immunoprotein biomarkers demonstrate greater sensitivity in the monitoring of more complex inflammatory microenvironments, like the omentum. In addition, hematological ratios derived from leukograms may be more strongly influenced by individual variables, such as age and physiological condition, requiring well-established reference intervals and careful interpretations to avoid misleading clinical conclusions [23].

The progression from mild lymphoplasmacytic omentum infiltrates to more organized granulomatous lesions demonstrates that this structure actively participates in immune-inflammatory responses in CanL cases. The predominance of mononuclear cells, especially lymphocytes, plasma cells, and macrophages, is consistent with persistent chronic inflammatory processes and reflects antigenic stimulation maintenance in response to parasite persistence, a pattern previously described in visceral organs affected by CanL [5,15,16].

The diffuse and perivascular distribution of inflammatory infiltrates demonstrates a close association between local immune activity and rich omentum vascularization, favoring leukocyte recruitment and inflammatory microenvironment maintenance. This is particularly relevant, as the omentum exhibits an extensive vascular and lymphatic network, in addition to a high concentration of resident immune cells, acting as an important immune surveillance site within the abdominal cavity [1,24].

The presence of hyperemia and fibrosis indicates that omental tissue activation is not limited to a transient event but rather progresses to progressive structural alterations. While hyperemia reflects increased blood flow and continuous cellular recruitment, fibrosis represents tissue remodeling secondary to sustained chronic inflammation, similar to that observed in other visceral adipose tissues subjected to prolonged inflammatory stimulation [2].

In more advanced stages, granuloma formation and the presence of reactive macrophages represent classical containment mechanisms against the persistence of intracellular agents. In CanL, granulomatous organization is associated with the host’s attempt to limit parasite dissemination and modulate local immune responses, especially in microenvironments rich in mononuclear phagocyte system cells [25].

The presence of Leishmania spp. amastigote forms within the cytoplasm of omental macrophages is highly relevant, confirming direct omentum parasitism and expanding the understanding of parasite dissemination to extranodal and cavitary sites. A similar finding was described by Araújo Júnior et al. [11], who reported the presence of amastigotes in ascitic fluid macrophages and neutrophils, reinforcing that the peritoneal cavity actively participates in CanL immunopathogenesis. The identification of amastigotes in the omentum supports the hypothesis that visceral adipose tissue may act as a functionally active immunological compartment, capable of contributing to parasite persistence and chronic inflammation maintenance. This expands the understanding of the biology of infection and reinforces the need to investigate extranodal compartments as potential parasitic reservoirs and complementary targets in prognostic assessments of the disease.

Furthermore, the association between the intensity of omental inflammation and progressive erythrocytes, hematocrit, and hemoglobin reductions suggests that microscopic omental involvement is accompanied by systemic hematological alterations rather than representing an isolated finding. As the local inflammatory response becomes more intense and organized, a progressive worsening of erythrogram variables is observed, with no corresponding association with overall clinical score. This reinforces an association between inflammatory omental involvement and the inflammatory anemia observed in CanL, comprising an important complementary clinical severity and disease prognosis indicator.

Similarly, a higher degree of fibrosis was associated with decreased red blood cell and platelet counts, while more pronounced vasodilation was associated with reduced globulin and total protein levels, indicating that microscopic omental alterations are associated with progressive hematological and biochemical changes. This supports the hypothesis that omental inflammation is an integrated component of CanL immunopathogenesis, associated with parasite persistence and systemic hematological and inflammatory alterations.

Although this study provides consistent evidence regarding omentum involvement in CanL immunopathogenesis, some methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the identification of amastigote Leishmania spp. forms was based exclusively on HE-stained sections. The absence of complementary confirmatory techniques, such as immunohistochemistry or molecular assays, limits parasite identity, tissue parasite burden, viability, and active replication confirmations.

In addition, the relatively small sample size employed herein, particularly of the control group, should be considered when interpreting subgroup comparisons and correlation analyses. Because of this, some findings should be regarded as exploratory and require confirmation by larger, independent cohorts. Future studies combining complementary diagnostic techniques with larger study populations are, therefore, paramount to validate the presence of Leishmania in omental tissue and further clarify the biological significance of omental involvement in CanL.

5. Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the omentum actively participates in CanL immunopathogenesis. The high frequency of chronic lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates, the progression to granulomatous patterns with epithelioid macrophages, and the identification of amastigote Leishmania spp. forms within the macrophage cytoplasm indicate tissue parasitism and support the role of the omentum in infection chronicity and local inflammation maintenance. Furthermore, the association between histological inflammation grade and progressive erythrogram variable reduction indicates that omental involvement is associated with the intensity of systemic repercussions of the disease. Among the evaluated biomarkers, AGR is noteworthy as the main discriminatory marker, highlighting its important complementary clinical potential. These findings expand the understanding of non-classical tissues involved in Leishmania infantum infection and support the omentum as a relevant immunological compartment involved in inflammatory responses associated with CanL.
