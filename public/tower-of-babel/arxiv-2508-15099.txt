Hydra: A 1.6B-Parameter State-Space Language Model with Sparse Attention, Mixture-of-Experts, and Memory Thanks: Code: https://github.com/sidcraftscode/hydra (commit 128017e)
Siddharth Chaudhary chaudhs@stpaulsschool.org.uk, Bennett Browning bennb@berkeley.edu

Abstract
We present Hydra as an architectural proposal for hybrid long-context language models that combine conditional computation, long-context memory mechanisms, and sparse mixture-of-experts within a ∼ \sim 1.6B parameter design envelope. Hydra integrates a Mamba-style Structured State Space Model (SSM) backbone with intermittent sparse global attention, chunk-level MoE feed-forward routing, and dual (workspace + factual PKM) memories. We formalize the component interfaces, give transparent parameter and complexity accounting, and outline a staged curriculum intended to stably activate the parts. We accompany the specification with illustrative toy-scale prototype measurements (tens of millions of parameters on synthetic data) whose sole purpose is to demonstrate implementation feasibility and qualitative scaling behaviors (e.g., long-context throughput crossover, controllable expert routing) — not to claim competitive full-scale performance. We explicitly delineate assumptions and open risks (training complexity, memory utilization, specialization dynamics) and position Hydra as a blueprint to stimulate empirical follow-up rather than a finished system. By combining SSM efficiency, selective sparse attention, MoE capacity, and learnable memory, Hydra sketches a path toward modular, input-adaptive long-context LMs; validating end-task gains at target scale remains future work.



1
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable success by scaling model size and data, yet this progress comes with steep computational costs and limitations in context length and knowledge utilization. The Transformer architecture, while powerful, suffers from quadratic time/space complexity in sequence length and binds all knowledge into its parameters. This motivates research into conditional compute architectures that activate only subsets of parameters per input, as well as models that can handle longer contexts and incorporate external memory. Recent advances include state-space models for efficient long sequences
Gu & Dao 2023
, mixture-of-experts (MoE) for scaling parameters sparsely
Lepikhin et al. 2020
;
Fedus et al. 2021
, and memory-augmented transformers for integrating large knowledge bases
Lample et al. 2019
. However, these techniques have largely been studied in isolation. In this work, we propose Hydra, a unified architecture that combines these complementary ideas to achieve a model with expanded capacity, long context capability, and improved efficiency.

3
Related Work

Efficient Sequence Modeling
– Transformers have dominated NLP, but their
O(L^{2})
self-attention cost is prohibitive for very long sequences (where
L
is sequence length). To address this, Structured State Space Models (SSMs) have emerged as an alternative backbone offering linear time complexity. SSMs represent sequences with linear dynamical systems, enabling fast recurrent or convolutional processing of long contexts
Gu & Dao 2023
.

The recent Mamba architecture demonstrated that a pure SSM model (no attention) can achieve competitive language modeling performance with up to million-token contexts and
5\times
faster inference than transformers
Gu & Dao 2023
. However, a known weakness of earlier SSMs is their inability to perform content-based, non-local interactions as flexibly as attention. This motivates hybrid models that combine efficient sequence traversal with occasional attention. Indeed, some production long-context LLMs interleave primarily local/windowed attention with an occasional full global attention layer (e.g., every 6 layers), reusing key/value caches to reduce overhead. Such designs retain the transformer’s ability to capture long-range dependencies while limiting quadratic operations. Our architecture follows this principle by inserting sparse global attention in a subset of layers (every third block in Hydra) to complement the SSM backbone.

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)
– Scaling model capacity via MoE has shown that we can achieve the quality of extremely large models at a fraction of the inference cost. In an MoE layer, a gating network (sometimes called a controller) routes each input to one or a few expert sub-networks (typically feed-forward networks), rather than using a single monolithic feed-forward layer. Google’s Switch Transformer is a prime example, reaching 1.6 trillion parameters but requiring the compute of only a 1.4B dense model by routing each token to one expert
Fedus et al. 2021
.

Many works have refined MoE routing to address challenges like load imbalance and instability
Lepikhin et al. 2020
. GShard introduced auxiliary load-balancing losses and capacity limits to ensure experts receive equal work
Lepikhin et al. 2020
. Switch Transformer simplified gating to use only the top-1 expert per token, avoiding expensive coordination at some cost to robustness
Fedus et al. 2021
. Recent surveys distinguish token-level gating (each token independently chooses experts) from sample or sequence-level gating (all tokens in a sequence share the same expert). For instance, in multilingual translation MoE, routing at the sentence level (using the average sentence representation to pick an expert) was found to mitigate cross-language interference. This inspires Hydra’s chunk-level MoE approach, where contiguous token blocks are routed together as a unit.

Our use of MoE aligns with prior findings that MoE layers can be inserted into transformer-based LMs (typically replacing the feed-forward sublayers) to expand capacity. Importantly, MoE exploits the modular sparsity of language model activations – e.g. different neurons/expert specialize in different patterns – which improves efficiency. By combining MoE with an SSM, Hydra continues this trend of "activating parameters on demand," extending it beyond experts to entire modules like attention and memory.

Memory-Augmented Language Models
– Beyond parametric capacity, another line of research allows models to store and retrieve information in external memories. Memory networks and retrieval-augmented transformers explicitly access a database or cache of text, but here we focus on differentiable memory integrated into the model. Product Key Memory (PKM) is one such method: Lample et al. (2019) introduced large memory layers that can add up to billions of parameters with negligible compute cost by using a product-key indexing scheme
Lample et al. 2019
. The keys are factorized into sub-keys, enabling efficient nearest-neighbor lookup in an extremely large key space. By inserting a PKM layer in a transformer, they showed that a 12-layer model with memory can outperform a 24-layer standard transformer while also decoding twice as fast
Lample et al. 2019
.

Another approach to memory is to extend the context window via recurrent state or cached hidden representations. Transformer-XL introduced a segment-level recurrence to carry hidden states forward, and more recently the Recurrent Memory Transformer (RMT) proposes adding special memory tokens that persist between segments
Bulatov et al. 2022
. RMT achieved effective context lengths up to
10^{6}
tokens by storing information in a fixed number of memory slots and updating them as the sequence progresses. Compressive Transformers went further by compressing old memories (e.g. combining or downsampling past hidden states) to retain long-term information in a fixed-size cache
Rae et al. 2019
.

These innovations inform Hydra’s memory design: we include (1) a latent working memory of learned slots that function like RMT’s recurrent memory – a scratchpad for interim computations and a mechanism for extending effective context length – and (2) a factual memory implemented via PKM to offload static knowledge from parameters. Together, these allow the model to handle sequences far beyond 16k tokens (via compression into memory) and to recall factual associations that would otherwise require a much larger parametric model or external retrieval.

In summary, Hydra sits at the intersection of these threads. It can be seen as a hybrid of an SSM-based long-range model (for efficiency), a sparse-transformer (for targeted attention), a Switch-style MoE (for capacity scaling), and a memory-augmented model (for extended context and knowledge). While prior work has explored each component separately, our contribution is to integrate them into one architecture. In the next section, we describe the architecture and its components in detail.

4
Methods

We revise the Hydra architecture specification for internal mathematical consistency, explicit parameter accounting, and clarity of conditional pathways. Hydra is a decoder-only, tri-path hybrid language model combining: (i) a linear-time selective state space (SSM) backbone, (ii) sparsified global attention (SGA) applied intermittently, (iii) chunk-level Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) feed-forward layers, and (iv) two memories: a latent workspace (persistent scratchpad) and a product-key factual memory (PKM). A lightweight controller gates / routes among these components.

4.2
Tri-Path Block and Gated Mixer

Let
x_{t}^{(b)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the token (or memory slot) representation entering block
b
at position
t
. Each block outputs

y_{t}^{(b)}=x_{t}^{(b)}+g^{(b)}_{1}\,\mathrm{SSM}^{(b)}(\mathrm{LN}(x_{t}^{(b)}))+g^{(b)}_{2}\,\mathrm{SGA}^{(b)}(\mathrm{LN}(x_{t}^{(b)}))+g^{(b)}_{3}\,\mathrm{MoE}^{(b)}(\mathrm{LN}(x_{t}^{(b)}))

where any path not scheduled (e.g. SGA in a non-attention block, MoE in a non-MoE block) returns
0
.
g^{(b)}_{i}
are learnable LayerScale-style scalars initialised
(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3})\approx(0.8,0.2,0.5)
to bias early training toward the stable SSM path while permitting gradual attention / expert uptake. RMSNorm or LN can be used (we adopt pre-norm RMSNorm in implementation). The controller may (a) hard-skip SGA in some scheduled blocks, (b) regulate MoE capacity factor, and (c) gate PKM / workspace access.

4.3
Selective State Space Path (Mamba / S4D Style)

We adopt a content-gated diagonal state model inspired by selective scan / Mamba, clarified here. Let input
x_{t}^{(b)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A projection produces four parameter streams:

[\Delta_{t},B_{t},C_{t},U_{t}]=W_{\text{param}}x_{t}^{(b)},\qquad W_{\text{param}}\in\mathbb{R}^{4d\times d}.

Optionally
W_{\text{param}}
is factorized as
W_{2}W_{1}
with
W_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}
,
W_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{4d\times r}
(rank
r\in\{512,1024\}
) to trade parameters for FLOPs; in the target 22M/block budget we retain the dense
4d^{2}
(16.78M) plus: LayerNorm/RMSNorm scales (
2d

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
, low-rank correction (2̃M), and output mixing / gating (3̃M) totaling 2̃2M.

We define per-position decay:

\alpha_{t}=\exp\big(-\mathrm{softplus}(\Delta_{t})\odot\lambda\big),\quad 0<\alpha_{t}<1.

State update (diagonal, elementwise) with input driving term:

s_{t+1}=\alpha_{t}\odot s_{t}+B_{t}\odot U_{t},\qquad y_{t}^{\text{SSM}}=C_{t}\odot s_{t}+W_{\text{skip}}x_{t}^{(b)}.

W_{\text{skip}}
(optional) enables direct passthrough akin to input convolutions in full Mamba; omitting it recovers the simplified recurrence previously shown. Parallel evaluation over a sequence uses an associative scan over
(\alpha_{t},B_{t}\odot U_{t})
. Initial state
s_{0}=0
(or learned per block).

Output state choice.

We emit
y_{t}^{\text{SSM}}
from
s_{t}
(pre-update) instead of
s_{t+1}
to keep latency minimal and maintain strict causality alignment (the token representation at
t
depends only on states up to
t
). Empirically this avoids an off-by-one shift when interleaving with attention.

4.4
Sparse Global Attention (SGA)

Every third block (blocks 3,6,9,…) exposes an attention path. Queries, keys, values:
Q=XW^{Q},K=XW^{K},V=XW^{V}
with
W^{Q},W^{K},W^{V},W^{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Rotary position embeddings are applied only to
(Q,K)
for real token positions (memory slots treated as prepended earlier positions). To limit cost we combine (i) local window

per head and (ii) controller-selected global tokens set

5
Illustrative Prototype Experiments

While full-scale implementation is beyond current scope, we provide
illustrative
toy experiments whose role is to sanity-check integration, not to establish state-of-the-art metrics. We implement scaled-down variants (256d embeddings, 12 layers, 4–8 experts; 6–29M parameters) and compare to a similarly sized Transformer on synthetic tasks chosen to stress specific subsystems (long-range token copying, synthetic language modeling, structured factual recall). All results should be interpreted qualitatively; extrapolation to 1.6B scale is speculative.

5.1
Experimental Setup

We implement Hydra variants with 256-dimensional embeddings, 12 layers, and 4-8 experts per MoE layer, totaling 6-29M parameters. This scale allows rapid iteration while preserving the core architectural dynamics. We compare against a standard Transformer baseline of similar parameter count (6.35M parameters). All models are trained on synthetic datasets designed to test specific capabilities: long-range recall, language modeling, and factual memory retrieval.

5.2
Synthetic Data Generation

We generate three synthetic task families:

•

Long-range token copying
: sequences of length 512 with uniformly sampled tokens (vocab size 8,192) interleaved with delimiter markers; the model must reproduce earlier spans after a gap. Tokens sampled i.i.d. except patterned delimiters every 64 positions.

•

Synthetic language modeling
: random unigram base distribution smoothed with Zipf(
\alpha=1.1
) over the same 8,192-token vocabulary; sentences formed by sampling lengths 5–32 then concatenating until sequence length 512 is reached.

•

Structured factual recall
: templated (subject, relation, object) triples mapped to token IDs via a fixed dictionary (5k distinct entities, 64 relation codes). Prompts present subject + relation; target is object tokens.

Each training batch shuffles task types uniformly. No real-world corpus content is included. Train/test split is implicit via different PRNG seeds for generation; because distributions are stationary and synthetic, generalization is trivial beyond memorization dynamics. Vocabulary size at toy scale differs from target (planned 50k BPE) and is chosen to keep softmax cost small. These tasks stress (i) short vs long dependency handling, (ii) generic language modeling loss, and (iii) key–value factual recall.

5.3
Throughput Scaling Validation

Our experiments demonstrate the expected crossover behavior predicted by Hydra’s hybrid design. At 1K tokens, the standard Transformer achieves 84.5K tokens/sec vs Hydra base at 66.6K tokens/sec, reflecting the overhead of SSM and routing components. However, at 4K tokens, Hydra base overtakes Transformer (28.5K vs 20.2K tokens/sec), validating the linear vs quadratic scaling hypothesis. This crossover occurs because attention’s quadratic cost dominates at longer sequences, while Hydra’s SSM backbone maintains linear scaling. The results confirm that Hydra’s design successfully trades short-sequence overhead for long-sequence efficiency.

6
Discussion

We interpret the
illustrative
prototype signals jointly with architectural analysis. Empirical evidence presented here intentionally stops short of real-corpus, large-scale benchmarking; statements about prospective full-scale efficacy are hypotheses grounded in prior literature on SSMs, MoE, and memory-augmented LMs. Key qualitative takeaways: (i) components co-train without pathological collapse under modest settings; (ii) replacing most quadratic attention layers with linear SSM + intermittent sparse attention produces the expected long-sequence throughput crossover; (iii) chunk-level Top-2 gating maintains balanced expert utilization at high entropy, deferring specialization pressure to larger scales; (iv) added routing and memory modules do not explosively increase peak memory at toy scale.

During training, we’d monitor expert load (should be roughly uniform
Lepikhin et al. 2020
), memory usage (what fraction of tokens have high
\beta
, ideally aligned with locations where loss spikes if memory is disabled), and long-range metrics (whether token-level loss decreases when increasing available context from 1k to 16k). The curriculum and ablation expectations are speculative and intentionally separated (Appendix
A.1
,
A.2
).

7
Limitations

Hydra, as presented, is a
proposal
with illustrative prototypes rather than a fully validated 1.6B production model. Key limitations:

•

Empirical scope:
No large-corpus or real benchmark evaluations; all measurements are synthetic / toy-scale. Generalization of qualitative trends to full scale is a hypothesis.

•

Training complexity:
Multiple interacting sparse / memory subsystems raise optimization risk (expert collapse, unused memory, attention under-activation) and engineering overhead.

•

Memory fidelity:
Workspace compression is lossy; factual PKM retrieval quality at scale is untested and may require sophisticated indexing / refresh strategies.

•

Routing fairness vs specialization:
Chunk-level gating may delay fine-grained expert specialization; alternative granularity trade-offs remain unexplored.

•

Compute variance:
Input-adaptive skipping induces variable latency; systems-level scheduling and worst-case provisioning are not analyzed here.

•

Security / misuse considerations:
External memory write/update policies are unspecified; without safeguards, injection or contamination attacks are possible.

•

Energy accounting:
We provide no end-to-end energy / carbon assessment; efficiency claims are limited to throughput and parameter-activity proxies.

•

Toy-scale statistical limits:
Reported prototype metrics deliberately exclude absolute language modeling perplexity numbers to avoid over-claiming significance at tiny scale / short training.

•

Interpretive uncertainty:
Architectural conclusions (e.g., expected specialization patterns, memory efficacy) are hypotheses pending validation at full 1.6B scale.

8
Societal and Ethical Impact

Because Hydra emphasizes long-context reasoning and modular knowledge incorporation, potential positive impacts include: (i) enabling analysis of lengthy technical / legal documents with reduced compute per token, (ii) facilitating modular updating of factual knowledge without full retraining, and (iii) offering finer-grained interpretability signals via sparse routing statistics. Potential risks include: (a) amplification of memorized sensitive content if PKM stores or retrieves unfiltered data, (b) increased difficulty detecting hallucinations when memory and parametric pathways disagree, (c) potential for specialized experts to encode biased or discriminatory patterns if training data are unbalanced, (d) expanded context windows enabling synthesis of disinformation narratives across large source corpora, and (e) privacy leakage through inadvertent retention or reconstruction of synthetic-to-real data mappings should real data be introduced later. Mitigations require: memory curation / audit pipelines, provenance tracking for retrieved values, per-expert bias monitoring, differential privacy or filtering for any future real-data memory writes, and red-teaming focused on long-context misuse scenarios. As no real-world data experiments are presented here, impact claims remain speculative; thorough assessment necessitates future large-scale evaluation.

Conclusion and Future Work

We have presented Hydra, a hybrid language model architecture integrating conditional computation, long-context memory, and sparse expert layers. Our focus is an analytically grounded
design specification
supported by illustrative (non-competitive) prototypes. Within a
\sim
1.6B total parameter envelope, Hydra hypothesizes that sparse activation can provide flexible capacity while keeping per-token active parameters near an
0.8
B band.

We deliberately refrain from strong performance claims; instead we outline prospective advantages (modular scaling, efficient long-context handling, explicit factual memory) that require confirmation via large-scale empirical study.

Future work (selected directions).

•

Full-scale pretraining:
Train the 1.6B configuration on real corpora to quantify token-level loss, long-context retention, and factual QA performance.

•

Curriculum ablations:
Evaluate which curriculum phases are necessary vs optional; measure component uptake dynamics.

•

Granularity of routing:
Compare token-level vs chunk-level MoE impacts on specialization and efficiency.

•

Memory policies:
Investigate write sparsity schedules, eviction / refresh strategies, and safety filters for PKM.

•

Energy and carbon analysis:
Measure end-to-end training and inference energy to validate hypothesized efficiency gains.

•

Interpretability:
Leverage routing / memory access logs for post-hoc attribution and bias auditing.

•

Robustness and safety:
Stress-test long-context abuse (prompt injections, data exfiltration) and mitigation efficacy.

In summary, Hydra is a modular blueprint intended to catalyze further investigation. We invite the community to refine, stress-test, and extend this design, and we provide explicit accounting and placeholders to streamline such follow-up work.

A.5
Parameter Accounting Clarification

Active vs total parameters.
Total parameters count every weight stored. Active parameters per token refer to the subset of weights whose values participate in the forward pass for a single token under typical routing (e.g., only the two selected experts, only SGA layers actually executed, plus always-on SSM trunk and output layers). This distinction matters because Hydra’s conditional paths (experts, intermittent attention) allow scaling total capacity without proportionally increasing per-token compute or memory traffic.
