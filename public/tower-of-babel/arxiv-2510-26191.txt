Random Diophantine equations of large degree
Tim Browning, Will Sawin

Abstract
. Among the set of hypersurfaces of degree d d and dimension ℓ \ell defined by the vanishing of a homogeneous polynomial with coefficients in the set { − 1 , 1 } \{-1,1\} , we investigate the probability that a hypersurface contains a rational point as d d and ℓ \ell tend to ∞ \infty .



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
11D45 (11G50, 11P21, 14G05)

1.
Introduction

Questions about the arithmetic properties of randomly chosen polynomials with integer coefficients
are of central concern in number theory.
In the simplest model, the
large box model
, one fixes the degree of the polynomial
and allows the coefficients to vary uniformly in
\{-H,\dots,H\}
, as
H\to\infty
.
In the
large degree model
, on the other hand, one constrains the coefficients to be in

\{-1,+1\}
, say, and allows the degree of the polynomial to grow.
It has been known for a long time that random polynomials
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]

are irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
, when adhering to the large box model. The best known error term is due to Kuba
[
5
]
, who shows that the probability of
f
being reducible is
O_{d}(H^{-1})
, for fixed degree
d=\deg(f)\geqslant 3
. The large degree model is significantly harder to analyse, and we only have a conditional treatment. Thus,
under the assumption of the Riemann Hypothesis for Dedekind zeta functions,
the question of irreducibility in the large degree model has been dealt with by Breuillard and Varjú
[
2
]
.

In this paper we shall be concerned with the Hasse principle for
hypersurfaces
V\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
defined by
randomly chosen homogeneous
polynomials

f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
of degree
d
.
In the large box model,
this topic was
investigated by Poonen and Voloch
[
6
]
. In

[
6
, Conjecture 2.2.(ii)]
it is asserted that in the Fano regime
n\geqslant d
,
100\%
of the hypersurfaces should satisfy the Hasse principle. For the range

n<d-1
, corresponding to hypersurfaces of general type,
it is predicted in
[
6
, Conjecture 2.2.(i)]
that
0\%
of the hypersurfaces should have a rational point.
The first of these conjectures is settled for hypersurfaces of dimension at least
3

in joint work of the authors with Le Boudec
[
3
]
.

We shall address the existence of rational points in the large degree model.
Let
d,n\geqslant 2
and let
N_{d,n}=\binom{n+d}{d}
be the number of monomials of degree
d
in
n+1
variables. Let
\mathbb{B}_{d,n}
be the set of hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
defined by the vanishing of a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
with coefficients in the set
\{-1,1\}
. We thus have

(1.1)

\#\mathbb{B}_{d,n}=2^{N_{d,n}-1}.

We shall be interested in the ratio

r_{d,n}=\frac{\#\{V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}:V(\mathbb{Q})\neq\emptyset\}}{\#\mathbb{B}_{d,n}},

which measures the density of hypersurfaces
V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}
with rational points.
The following result describes the size of
r_{d,n}
, as
d\to\infty
, and
is rather close to covering the entire Fano range.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
n\geqslant d+\log d
, we have

r_{d,n}=1+O\left(\frac{1}{d^{1/4}}\right),

where the implied constant is absolute.

Our next result shows that if we are ready to allow the integer
d
to avoid an appropriate
density
0
set of integers, then the condition on
n
can be relaxed dramatically.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a set
\mathcal{D}
of density
1
in the positive integers, such that for
d\in\mathcal{D}
and

n\geqslant d^{1/2}\log d
, we have

r_{d,n}=1+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

where the implied constant is absolute.

It follows that in the range
n\geqslant d^{1/2}\log d
, the proportion of hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{B}_{d,n}
which have rational points approaches
100\%
, as
d\to\infty
while running over an appropriate set of density
1
. It is rather striking to see that so much of the general type range is handled in this result. The most troublesome degrees
d
to handle are those whose
2
-adic valuations are large and the set
\mathcal{D}
is chosen to avoid these.

Returning to the question of irreducibility, it follows
from
work of Bary-Soroker and Kozma
[
1
]
that
the proportion of
ternary degree
d
forms
that are irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}

approaches
100\%
as
d\to\infty
.
A form
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
if

f(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},0,\dots,0)
has this property.
It therefore follows that
the proportion of
hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{B}_{d,n}
that are irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}

approaches
100\%
as
d\to\infty
,
provided that
n\geqslant 2
.
Unlike the case of univariate polynomials, this result is unconditional.
On the other hand, while the large box model version of the following conjecture is straightforward,
it would be interesting to determine its validity in the large degree model.

Conjecture 1.3
.

100% of hypersurfaces
V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}
are smooth, as
d\to\infty
.

The proof of Theorems
1.1
and

1.2
is rather combinatorial and will be completed in Section
5
.
The key idea is
to study the moments

M_{k}(U)=\frac{1}{\#\mathbb{B}_{d,n}}\sum_{V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}}\left(\sum_{x\in U\cap V(\mathbb{Q})}1\right)^{k},

for
k\in\{1,2\}
, where
U
is an appropriately chosen set of points in
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{Q})
, always with coordinates drawn from the set
\{-1,0,+1\}
. One accesses
r_{d,n}
through the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, which yields

M_{1}(U)^{2}\leqslant r_{d,n}M_{2}(U)
. Providing estimates for the moments
M_{k}(U)
is the focus of Section
4
.
Along the way we will need information about the density of locally soluble hypersurfaces in the large degree model, which is the focus of Section
2
, in addition to various estimates for binomial coefficients and sums involving them, for which the relevant facts are collected together in Section
3
.

2.
Local solubility

In this section we investigate the ratio

r_{d,n}^{\mathrm{loc}}=\frac{\#\{V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}:V(\mathbf{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})\neq\emptyset\}}{\#\mathbb{B}_{d,n}},

where
\mathbf{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}
is the ring of adèles. This is the probability that a hypersurface in
\mathbb{B}_{d,n}
is everywhere locally soluble. We begin with the following elementary fact about the parity of

N_{e,\ell}=\binom{e+\ell}{e}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

N_{e,\ell}
is even if and only if

e
and
\ell
share a common digit
1
in their binary expansions.

Proof.

Given any integer
e\geqslant 1
, we let
s_{2}(e)
denote the sum of the digits in the binary expansion of
e
. Let
v_{2}(m)
denote the
2
-adic valuation of any integer
m\geqslant 1
. Legendre’s formula states that

v_{2}(e!)=e-s_{2}(e).

It follows that for any
e,\ell\geqslant 1
, we have

(2.1)

v_{2}(N_{e,\ell})=s_{2}(e)+s_{2}(\ell)-s_{2}(e+\ell).

As a result, we see that
N_{e,\ell}
is even if and only if

s_{2}(e)+s_{2}(\ell)>s_{2}(e+\ell),

which is equivalent to demanding that
e
and
\ell
share a common digit
1
in their binary expansions.
∎

It will be convenient to have an alternative formulation of

N_{d,\ell}
, for any
d,\ell\geqslant 1
. Let
j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
. Then the number of monomials of degree
d
in
\ell+1
variables is equal to the sum over
k\in\{0,\dots,d\}
of the number of monomials of degree
k
in
j
given variables multiplied by the number of monomials of degree
d-k
in the remaining
\ell+1-j
variables. In other words, we have

(2.2)

N_{d,\ell}=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\binom{k+j-1}{k}\binom{d-k+\ell-j}{d-k}.

We now let
c_{d,\ell}(j)
denote the number of monomials of degree
d
which are odd with respect to
j
given variables among
\ell+1
. Thus

(2.3)

c_{d,\ell}(j)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
2\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{d}\binom{k+j-1}{k}\binom{d-k+\ell-j}{d-k}.

We proceed by
proving an upper bound for

r_{d,n}^{\mathrm{loc}}
, which may prove to be of independent interest.
It would be interesting to determine whether or not these upper bounds have matching lower bounds
when
n\geqslant 2^{v_{2}(d)}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
d,n\geqslant 2
. Then

r_{d,n}^{\mathrm{loc}}\leqslant\begin{cases}0&\textrm{if }n<2^{v_{2}(d)},\\
1/2&\textrm{if }n=2^{v_{2}(d)},\\
1&\textrm{if }n>2^{v_{2}(d)}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
d\geqslant 2
.
It follows from Lemma

2.1
that the least
n\geqslant 2
such that
N_{d,n}
is even is
n=2^{v_{2}(d)}
.
Thus,
N_{d,m}
is odd for any
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
if
n<2^{v_{2}(d)}
.
It follows that
V(\mathbb{Q}_{2})=\emptyset
for any
V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}
, whence in
fact
r_{d,n}^{\mathrm{loc}}=0
.
If
n>2^{v_{2}(d)}
then we take the trivial upper bound

r_{d,n}^{\mathrm{loc}}\leqslant 1
.

Suppose now that
n=2^{v_{2}(d)}
. Since
n\geqslant 2
, it follows that both
n
and
d
are even.
For the purposes of an upper bound it will suffice to consider the proportion of
V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}
which admit points modulo
4
. Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
be a form of degree
d
that defines
V
, which has coefficients in the set
\{-1,+1\}
.
Thus there exist
c_{i_{0},\dots,i_{n}}\in\{-1,+1\}
such that

f(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{0},\dots,i_{n}\geqslant 0\\
i_{0}+\dots+i_{n}=d\end{subarray}}c_{i_{0},\dots,i_{n}}x_{0}^{i_{0}}\dots x_{n}^{i_{n}}.

Since
N_{d,m}
is odd for any
m<n
, it follows that
(1:1:\cdots:1)
is the only possible solution modulo
2
. Thus the only points in
V(\mathbb{Q}_{2})
have representatives in which all coordinates are congruent to
-1
or
+1
modulo
4
, with at least one coordinate equal to
+1
modulo
4
.
We claim that

f(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})\equiv f(1,\dots,1)\bmod{4},

for any such point
(x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n})\in V(\mathbb{Q}_{2})
. To see this we may suppose without loss of generality that
x_{0}=\dots=x_{j-1}=-1
and
x_{j}=\dots=x_{n}=+1
for some
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
.
Then
the claim is equivalent to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{0},\dots,i_{n}\geqslant 0\\
i_{0}+\dots+i_{n}=d\end{subarray}}c_{i_{0},\dots,i_{n}}\left(1-(-1)^{i_{0}+\dots+i_{j-1}}\right)\equiv 0\bmod{4},

which in turn is equivalent to the condition

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{0},\dots,i_{n}\geqslant 0\\
i_{0}+\dots+i_{j-1}\equiv 1\bmod{2}\\
i_{0}+\dots+i_{n}=d\end{subarray}}c_{i_{0},\dots,i_{n}}\equiv 0\bmod{2}.

Since
c_{i_{0},\dots,i_{n}}\equiv 1\bmod{2}
for any choice of indices
i_{0},\dots,i_{n}
, we see that
the left hand side is congruent modulo
2
to the number
c_{d,n}(j)

of monomials of degree
d
which are odd with respect to
j
given variables among
n+1
.
Hence, in the light of (
2.3
), the condition is equivalent to demanding that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
2\nmid k\end{subarray}}^{d}\binom{k+j-1}{k}\binom{d+n-j-k}{d-k}

is even.
Suppose first that
j
is even. Then
j-1
is odd and
\binom{k+j-1}{k}
is even for all odd
k
, by Lemma

2.1
. Alternatively, if
j
is odd then

n-j
is odd, since
n
is even. It therefore follows from Lemma

2.1
that

\binom{d+n-j-k}{d-k}

is even for all odd
k
, since
d
is even.
Now that we have established the claim, the
statement of the lemma follows on noting that 50% of hypersurfaces

V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}
are defined by forms for which

f(1,\dots,1)\equiv 0\bmod{4}
.
∎

3.
Sums of binomial coefficients

Stirling’s formula states that

(3.1)

m!\sim(2\pi)^{1/2}m^{m+1/2}e^{-m},

as
m\to\infty
, which can be used to prove that

(3.2)

\binom{2m}{m}=\frac{2^{2m}}{\pi^{1/2}m^{1/2}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)\right).

Coupled with this, we have the upper bounds

(3.3)

\binom{2m}{m}\leqslant\frac{2^{2m}}{\pi^{1/2}m^{1/2}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\binom{m}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\leqslant\frac{2^{m+1/2}}{\pi^{1/2}m^{1/2}},

which are valid for any
m\geqslant 1
and can be established using induction.

We will always adhere to the traditional convention that

\binom{r}{s}=0,

for any
r\geqslant 0
and
s\notin\{0,\dots,r\}
. We make rather heavy use of Vandermonde convolution identities in our work.
The first states that

(3.4)

\binom{r_{1}+r_{2}}{q}=\sum_{\alpha_{1}=0}^{r_{1}}\binom{r_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}\binom{r_{2}}{q-\alpha_{1}},

for any
r_{1},r_{2},q\geqslant 0
. This is proved by considering the coefficients of
x^{q}
in the binomial expansion of the identity
(1+x)^{r_{1}}(1+x)^{r_{2}}=(1+x)^{r_{1}+r_{2}}
. By instead considering the coefficients of
x^{r_{1}+q}
and appealing to the identity

\binom{r_{1}}{a_{1}}=\binom{r_{1}}{r_{1}-a_{1}}
, one arrives at the companion equality

(3.5)

\binom{r_{1}+r_{2}}{r_{2}-q}=\sum_{\alpha_{1}=0}^{r_{1}}\binom{r_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}\binom{r_{2}}{q+\alpha_{1}},

for any
r_{1},r_{2}\geqslant 0
and
q\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We shall also need the higher convolution

(3.6)

\binom{r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}}{q}=\sum_{\alpha_{1}=0}^{r_{1}}\ \sum_{\alpha_{2}=0}^{r_{2}}\binom{r_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}\binom{r_{2}}{\alpha_{2}}\binom{r_{3}}{q-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}},

which is valid for any
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},q\geqslant 0
.

It will be convenient to let
D(0,1)
denote the set of complex numbers with modulus at most
1
. We proceed by proving the following elementary result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
h\geqslant 0
and
z\in D(0,1)
. We have

\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\binom{d+h}{d}z^{d}=\frac{1}{(1-z)^{h+1}}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
h
. The result holds in the case
h=0
, on executing the geometric series.
We now assume that the result holds for some
h\geqslant 0
and we note that the particular case
j=\ell=h+1
of the equality (
2.2
) states that

\binom{d+h+1}{d}=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\binom{k+h}{k},

for any
d,h\geqslant 0
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\binom{d+h+1}{d}z^{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{d}\binom{k+h}{k}z^{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{k+h}{k}\sum_{d\geqslant k}z^{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-z}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{k+h}{k}z^{k}.

The proof follows on using the induction hypothesis.
∎

On comparing (
2.2
) and (
2.3
),
it is natural to expect that the number
c_{d,\ell}(j)
should usually be close to
N_{d,\ell}/2
. The purpose of the following result is to show that this is indeed the case for most
j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, provided
that
\ell\leqslant d
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell\leqslant d
. Then we have

\left|1-\frac{2c_{d,\ell}(j)}{N_{d,\ell}}\right|\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\min\{j,\ell+1-j\}}.

Proof.

Recalling (
2.2
)
and (
2.3
),
we begin by noting that

(3.7)

N_{d,\ell}-2c_{d,\ell}(j)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}(-1)^{k}\binom{k+j-1}{k}\binom{d-k+\ell-j}{d-k}.

Therefore, we see that

N_{d,\ell}-2c_{d,\ell}(j)=(-1)^{d}\left(N_{d,\ell}-2c_{d,\ell}(\ell+1-j)\right).

Hence, we can assume from now on that
j\leqslant(\ell+1)/2
and we aim to prove that

(3.8)

\left|1-\frac{2c_{d,\ell}(j)}{N_{d,\ell}}\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{2^{j}}.

It follows from (
3.7
) that for any
z\in D(0,1)
we have

\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\left(N_{d,\ell}-2c_{d,\ell}(j)\right)z^{d}=\left(\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{d}\binom{d+j-1}{d}z^{d}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\binom{d+\ell-j}{d}z^{d}\right).

As a result, Lemma
3.1
gives

\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\left(N_{d,\ell}-2c_{d,\ell}(j)\right)z^{d}=\frac{1}{(1+z)^{j}(1-z)^{\ell-j+1}}=\frac{1}{(1-z^{2})^{j}(1-z)^{\ell-2j+1}}.

The coefficients of the power series
1/(1-z^{2})
and
1/(1-z)
are all non-negative, which implies that

N_{d,\ell}-2c_{d,\ell}(j)\geqslant 0,

for all
d\geqslant 0
. Note that the coefficients of
1/(1-z^{2})
are all bounded by the coefficients of
1/(1-z)
.
Suppose we have power series

A(z)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}A_{d}z^{d},\quad B(z)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}B_{d}z^{d},\quad C(z)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}C_{d}z^{d},\quad D(z)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}D_{d}z^{d},

where
A_{d},B_{d},C_{d},D_{d}\geqslant 0
for all
d\geqslant 0
. If
A_{d}\leqslant C_{d}
and
B_{d}\leqslant D_{d}
for all
d\geqslant 0
then clearly the coefficients of
A(z)B(z)
are all bounded by the coefficients of
C(z)D(z)
. In this way we deduce that the coefficients of

\frac{1}{(1-z^{2})^{j}(1-z)^{\ell-2j+1}}

are all bounded by the coefficients of

\frac{1}{(1-z)^{j}(1-z)^{\ell-2j+1}}=\frac{1}{(1-z)^{\ell-j+1}}.

On appealing to Lemma
3.1
, we conclude that

0\leqslant N_{d,\ell}-2c_{d,\ell}(j)\leqslant\binom{d+\ell-j}{d},

for all
d\geqslant 0
.
Finally, since
j\leqslant\ell\leqslant d
, we note that

\frac{1}{N_{d,\ell}}\binom{d+\ell-j}{d}=\frac{\ell}{d+\ell}\cdot\frac{\ell-1}{d+\ell-1}\cdots\frac{\ell-(j-1)}{d+\ell-(j-1)}\leqslant\frac{1}{2^{j}},

as claimed in
(
3.8
).
∎

We close with the following inequality that exploits a connection to the hypergeometric distribution in probability.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
r,s\geqslant 0
be integers. Then

\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{r}{j}\binom{s}{j}2^{-j}\leqslant\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\mu}\binom{r+s}{r},

where
\mu=rs/(r+s).

Proof.

There is a convenient combinatorial model for this situation.
Suppose we have a population of size
r+s
, comprising
r
copies of
0
and
s
copies of
-\log 2
. Suppose we pick
r
elements without replacement. Then the probability of drawing the value
-\log 2
exactly
j
times is given by the hypergeometric distribution

q_{j}=\frac{\binom{r}{j}\binom{s}{j}}{\binom{r+s}{r}}.

Note that the sum of the
r
samples is equal to
-j\log 2=\log(2^{-j})
. Thus the sum

S=\frac{1}{\binom{r+s}{r}}\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{r}{j}\binom{s}{j}2^{-j}

is equal to the expectation of the exponential of the sum of
r
samples without replacement.
It follows from a result of
Hoeffding
[
4
, Thm. 4]
that this expectation is bounded
by the expectation of the exponential of the sum of
r
samples with replacement, which is equal to the
r
th power of the expectation of one sample. But one sample is either
0
(with probability
\frac{r}{r+s}
) or

-\log 2
(with probability
\frac{s}{r+s}
). It therefore follows that

S\leqslant\left(\frac{r}{r+s}\cdot\exp(0)+\frac{s}{r+s}\cdot\exp(-\log 2)\right)^{r}=\left(1-\frac{s}{2(r+s)}\right)^{r}\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{rs}{2(r+s)}\right).

The statement of the lemma follows on noting that

e^{-1/2}\leqslant 3/4
.
∎

4.
The first and second moments

For
n\geqslant 2
and
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, define
U_{n,\ell}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{Q})

to be the
subset
whose elements have a representative with exactly
\ell+1
coordinates equal to
\pm 1
and all remaining coordinates equal to
0
. We clearly have

(4.1)

\#U_{n,\ell}=2^{\ell}\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}.

For
k\geqslant 0
and any
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
we introduce the moments

(4.2)

M_{k}(d,n;\ell)=\frac{1}{\#\mathbb{B}_{d,n}}\sum_{V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}}\left(\sum_{x\in U_{n,\ell}\cap V(\mathbb{Q})}1\right)^{k}.

The following notation will prove useful.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
n\geqslant 1
. Given
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
, we let
\nu(f)
denote the number of coefficients of
f
equal to
-1
.

We start by establishing an explicit formula for the first moment.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
d,n\geqslant 2
and let
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
be such that
N_{d,\ell}
is even. We have

M_{1}(d,n;\ell)=\frac{1}{2^{N_{d,\ell}-\ell}}\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}\binom{N_{d,\ell}}{N_{d,\ell}/2}.

Proof.

We first note that

(4.3)

M_{1}(d,n;\ell)=\frac{1}{\#\mathbb{B}_{d,n}}\sum_{x\in U_{n,\ell}}\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1.

Next, we remark that for any
x\in U_{n,\ell}
we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1=2^{N_{d,n}-N_{d,\ell}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,\ell}\\
\tilde{x}\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1,

where
\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{P}^{\ell}(\mathbb{Q})
is obtained from
x
by removing its
n-\ell
zero coordinates.
Moreover, there exists an obvious bijection
\iota:\mathbb{B}_{d,\ell}\to\mathbb{B}_{d,\ell}
such that

\iota\left(\left\{V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,\ell}:\tilde{x}\in V(\mathbb{Q})\right\}\right)=\left\{V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,\ell}:f_{V}(1,\dots,1)=0\right\},

where
f_{V}
is the degree
d
form defining
V
.
But
f_{V}(1,\dots,1)=N_{d,\ell}-2\nu(f_{V})
for any

V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,\ell}
, where
\nu
is given in Definition
4.1
. Hence it follows that

(4.4)

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1&=2^{N_{d,n}-N_{d,\ell}}\#\left\{V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,\ell}:\nu(f_{V})=N_{d,\ell}/2\right\}\\
&=2^{N_{d,n}-N_{d,\ell}-1}\binom{N_{d,\ell}}{N_{d,\ell}/2},\end{split}

on recalling our assumption that
N_{d,\ell}
is even.
The statement of the lemma now follows on inserting this into (
4.3
) and recalling
the equalities (
1.1
) and (
4.1
).
∎

The second moment bound is much more complicated. We shall need the following piece of notation.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
n\geqslant 1

and let
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

Given
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
, we define
\even_{j}(f)
to be the part of
f
that is
even with respect to the first
j
variables. Similarly, we define

\odd_{j}(f)
to be the part of
f
that is odd with respect to the first
j
variables.
Next, let

a\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
b\in\{0,\dots,n+1-a\}
.
We also set
R_{a}^{b}(f)
to be the sum of the monomials of
f
involving only the first
a
and the final
b
variables.

We are now ready to establish the following upper bound for the second moment.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
d,n\geqslant 2
and let
\ell\in\{1,\dots,\min\{d,n\}\}
be such that
N_{d,\ell}
is even. Then

\frac{M_{2}(d,n;\ell)}{M_{1}(d,n;\ell)^{2}}\leqslant 1+\boldsymbol{1}_{\ell=n}+O\left(\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\min\{\ell,\mu_{\ell}\}}+\frac{1}{\min\{d,n\}}+\frac{1}{M_{1}(d,n;\ell)}\right),

where

(4.5)

\mu_{\ell}=\frac{(n-\ell)(\ell+1)}{n+1}.

Proof.

We start by noting that

M_{2}(d,n;\ell)=\frac{1}{\#\mathbb{B}_{d,n}}\sum_{x,y\in U_{n,\ell}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1.

We set
m_{n,\ell}=\max\{2\ell-n,0\}
and for
i\in\{m_{n,\ell},\dots,\ell\}
and
j\in\{0,\dots,i\}
, we let
T_{n,\ell}^{(i,j)}
denote the set of pairs of elements
x,y\in U_{n,\ell}
which have exactly
i+1
nonzero coordinates in common, and are such that among these
i+1
coordinates exactly
j
are different when the final nonzero coordinate shared by
x
and
y
is viewed as being
1
. Moreover, we let
T_{n,\ell}
denote the set of pairs of elements of
U_{n,\ell}
which do not have any nonzero coordinates in common. We notice that

U_{n,\ell}\times U_{n,\ell}=\left(\bigsqcup_{i=m_{n,\ell}}^{\ell}\ \bigsqcup_{j=0}^{i}T_{n,\ell}^{(i,j)}\right)\sqcup T_{n,\ell},

which allows us to write

(4.6)

M_{2}(d,n;\ell)=M_{1}(d,n;\ell)+\Sigma^{(1)}(d,n;\ell)+\Sigma^{(2)}(d,n;\ell)+\Sigma^{(3)}(d,n;\ell),

where

\Sigma^{(1)}(d,n;\ell)=\frac{1}{\#\mathbb{B}_{d,n}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\ \sum_{(x,y)\in T_{n,\ell}^{(\ell,j)}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1,

and

\Sigma^{(2)}(d,n;\ell)=\frac{1}{\#\mathbb{B}_{d,n}}\sum_{i=m_{n,\ell}}^{\ell-1}\ \sum_{j=0}^{i}\ \sum_{(x,y)\in T_{n,\ell}^{(i,j)}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1,

and

\Sigma^{(3)}(d,n;\ell)=\frac{1}{\#\mathbb{B}_{d,n}}\sum_{(x,y)\in T_{n,\ell}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1.

We note that
\Sigma^{(2)}(d,n;\ell)=0
if
\ell=n
and
\Sigma^{(3)}(d,n;\ell)=0
if
\ell\geqslant n/2
.
The most difficult sum to estimate is

\Sigma^{(2)}(d,n;\ell)
. While it ought to be possible to handle the three sums simultaneously,
by allowing
i
to also run over
-1
and
\ell
in the definition of
\Sigma^{(2)}(d,n;\ell)
, the treatment is made easier through the restriction
i\leqslant\ell-1
, since we can then exploit a non-trivial upper bound for
N_{d,i}/N_{d,\ell}.

Let
i\in\{m_{n,\ell},\dots,\ell\}
and let
j\in\{0,\dots,i\}
. If
(x,y)\in T_{n,\ell}^{(i,j)}
then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1=2^{N_{d,n}-N_{d,2\ell-i}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,2\ell-i}\\
\tilde{x},\tilde{y}\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1,

where
\tilde{x},\tilde{y}\in\mathbb{P}^{2\ell-i}(\mathbb{Q})
are obtained from
x
and
y
by removing their
n-2\ell+i
common zeros. There exists an obvious bijection

\iota:\mathbb{B}_{d,2\ell-i}\to\mathbb{B}_{d,2\ell-i}
such that

\iota\left(\left\{V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,2\ell-i}:\tilde{x},\tilde{y}\in V(\mathbb{Q})\right\}\right)=\left\{V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,2\ell-i}:f_{V}(\mathbf{x}_{i,j})=f_{V}(\mathbf{y}_{i,j})=0\right\},

where

\mathbf{x}_{(i,j)}=(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{j},\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{i+1-j},\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{\ell-i},\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{\ell-i})

and

\mathbf{y}_{(i,j)}=(\underbrace{-1,\dots,-1}_{j},\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{i+1-j},\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{\ell-i},\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{\ell-i}).

Hence for

i\in\{m_{n,\ell},\dots,\ell\}
and
j\in\{0,\dots,i\}
, we have

(4.7)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1=2^{N_{d,n}-N_{d,2\ell-i}}\#\left\{V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,2\ell-i}:f_{V}(\mathbf{x}_{i,j})=f_{V}(\mathbf{y}_{i,j})=0\right\},

for any
(x,y)\in T_{n,\ell}^{(i,j)}
.

Next, we note that for any
x\in U_{n,\ell}
and
i\in\{m_{n,\ell},\dots,\ell\}
,
j\in\{0,\dots,i\}
, we have

\#\left\{y\in U_{n,\ell}:(x,y)\in T_{n,\ell}^{(i,j)}\right\}=2^{\ell-i}\binom{n-\ell}{\ell-i}\binom{\ell+1}{i+1}\binom{i}{j},

and

\#\left\{y\in U_{n,\ell}:(x,y)\in T_{n,\ell}\right\}=2^{\ell}\binom{n-\ell}{\ell+1}.

Recalling the equality (
4.1
), we thus deduce that

(4.8)

\#T_{n,\ell}^{(i,j)}=2^{2\ell-i}\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}\binom{n-\ell}{\ell-i}\binom{\ell+1}{i+1}\binom{i}{j},

and

(4.9)

\#T_{n,\ell}=2^{2\ell}\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}\binom{n-\ell}{\ell+1}.

Analysis of
\Sigma^{(1)}(d,n;\ell)

This concerns the case
i=\ell
, so that
2\ell-i=\ell
.
Given
V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,\ell}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, we note that

\displaystyle f_{V}(\mathbf{x}_{i,j})

\displaystyle=N_{d,\ell}-2\nu(f_{V})

\displaystyle=N_{d,\ell}-2\nu(\even_{j}(f_{V}))-2\nu(\odd_{j}(f_{V})),

in the notation of Definition
4.3
.
Recall that

c_{d,\ell}(j)
is defined to be the number of monomials of degree
d
which are odd with respect to
j
given variables among
\ell+1
, and that it satisfies (
2.3
).
Then we find similarly that

\displaystyle f_{V}(\mathbf{y}_{i,j})

\displaystyle=N_{d,\ell}-c_{d,\ell}(j)-2\nu(\even_{j}(f_{V}))-\left(c_{d,\ell}(j)-2\nu(\odd_{j}(f_{V}))\right)

\displaystyle=N_{d,\ell}-2c_{d,\ell}(j)-2\nu(\even_{j}(f_{V}))+2\nu(\odd_{j}(f_{V})).

Hence it follows from (
4.7
) that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1=2^{N_{d,n}-N_{d,\ell}}\#\left\{V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,\ell}:\begin{array}[]{l}\nu(\even_{j}(f_{V}))=(N_{d,\ell}-c_{d,\ell}(j))/2,\\
\nu(\odd_{j}(f_{V}))=c_{d,\ell}(j)/2\end{array}\right\}.

Since the integer
N_{d,\ell}
is assumed to be even, we deduce that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1=\begin{cases}0&\textrm{if }2\nmid c_{d,\ell}(j),\\
\displaystyle{2^{N_{d,n}-N_{d,\ell}-1}\binom{N_{d,\ell}-c_{d,\ell}(j)}{(N_{d,\ell}-c_{d,\ell}(j))/2}\binom{c_{d,\ell}(j)}{c_{d,\ell}(j)/2}}&\textrm{if }2\mid c_{d,\ell}(j),\end{cases}

for any
j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
and any
(x,y)\in T_{n,\ell}^{(\ell,j)}
.
Noting that
0<c_{d,j}(\ell)<N_{d,\ell}
, we may
apply the upper bound (
3.3
)
twice to derive the inequality

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1\leqslant\frac{2^{N_{d,n}+1}}{\pi N_{d,\ell}}\left(1-\left|1-\frac{2c_{d,\ell}(j)}{N_{d,\ell}}\right|^{2}\right)^{-1/2}.

Since
\ell\leqslant d
by assumption, we
see that we are in position to apply Lemma
3.2
.
Thus

\left|1-\frac{2c_{d,\ell}(j)}{N_{d,\ell}}\right|\leqslant 0.5,

and we may deduce that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1\leqslant\frac{2^{N_{d,n}+1}}{\pi N_{d,\ell}}\left(1+O\left(\left|1-\frac{2c_{d,\ell}(j)}{N_{d,\ell}}\right|^{2}\right)\right).

But then a further
application of Lemma
3.2
gives

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1\leqslant\frac{2^{N_{d,n}+1}}{\pi N_{d,\ell}}\left(1+O\left(\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\min\{j,\ell-j\}}\right)\right).

Appealing to the equalities (
1.1
) and (
4.8
), we deduce that

\Sigma^{(1)}(d,n;\ell)\leqslant\frac{2^{\ell+2}}{\pi N_{d,\ell}}\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{j}\left(1+O\left(\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\min\{j,\ell-j\}}\right)\right).

But for any
A\geqslant 1
we have

\sum_{a=0}^{A}\binom{A}{a}=2^{A}\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{a=0}^{A}\binom{A}{a}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{a}=\left(\frac{5}{4}\right)^{A}.

Thus

\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{j}\left(1+O\left(\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\min\{j,\ell-j\}}\right)\right)=2^{\ell}\left(1+O\left(\left(\frac{5}{8}\right)^{\ell}\right)\right),

which yields in particular that

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(1)}(d,n;\ell)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2^{2\ell+2}}{\pi N_{d,\ell}}\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}\left(1+O\left(\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\ell}\right)\right).

As a result, on appealing to the estimate (
3.2
) and noting that
N_{d,\ell}\geqslant d
, we eventually get

(4.10)

\begin{split}\Sigma^{(1)}(d,n;\ell)&\leqslant\frac{1}{2^{2N_{d,\ell}-2\ell-1}}\binom{N_{d,\ell}}{N_{d,\ell}/2}^{2}\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}\left(1+O\left(\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\ell}+\frac{1}{d}\right)\right)\\
&=M_{1}(d,n;\ell)^{2}\left(\frac{2}{\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}}+O\left(\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\ell}+\frac{1}{d}\right)\right),\end{split}

where the second line follows from Lemma
4.2
.

Analysis of
\Sigma^{(2)}(d,n;\ell)

This concerns the case
i\in\{m_{n,\ell},\dots,\ell-1\}
and
j\in\{0,\dots,i\}
.
Our starting point is the observation that (
4.7
) holds,
for any
(x,y)\in T_{n,\ell}^{(i,j)}
.
Then for any

V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,2\ell-i}
we have

\displaystyle f_{V}(\mathbf{x}_{i,j})

\displaystyle=N_{d,\ell}-2\nu(R_{\ell+1}^{0}(f_{V}))=N_{d,\ell}-2\nu(R_{i+1}^{0}(f_{V}))-2\nu(X_{\ell,i}(f_{V})),

in the notation of Definition
4.1
, where

X_{\ell,i}(f_{V})=R_{\ell+1}^{0}(f_{V})-R_{i+1}^{0}(f_{V})
.
Note that the polynomial
X_{\ell,i}(f_{V})
only has
N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i}
coefficients.
We conclude that
f_{V}(\mathbf{x}_{i,j})=0
if and only if

(4.11)

\nu(X_{\ell,i}(f_{V}))=\frac{N_{d,\ell}}{2}-\nu\left(\even_{j}\left(R_{i+1}^{0}(f_{V})\right)\right)-\nu\left(\odd_{j}\left(R_{i+1}^{0}(f_{V})\right)\right).

Similarly, we find that

f_{V}(\mathbf{y}_{i,j})=N_{d,\ell}-c_{d,\ell}(j)-2\nu\left(\even_{j}\left(R_{i+1}^{\ell-i}(f_{V})\right)\right)-\left(c_{d,\ell}(j)-2\nu\left(\odd_{j}\left(R_{i+1}^{\ell-i}(f_{V})\right)\right)\right),

in the notation of (
2.3
).
Thus
f_{V}(\mathbf{y}_{i,j})=0
if and only if

(4.12)

\begin{split}\nu\left(\even_{j}\left(Y_{\ell,i}(f_{V})\right)\right)=~&\frac{N_{d,\ell}}{2}-c_{d,\ell}(j)-\nu\left(\even_{j}\left(R_{i+1}^{0}(f_{V})\right)\right)\\
&+\nu\left(\odd_{j}\left(R_{i+1}^{0}(f_{V})\right)\right)+\nu\left(\odd_{j}\left(Y_{\ell,i}(f_{V})\right)\right),\end{split}

where

Y_{\ell,i}(f_{V})=R_{i+1}^{\ell-i}(f_{V})-R_{i+1}^{0}(f_{V})
.
Note that the polynomial
Y_{\ell,i}(f_{V})
only involves the

\ell+i

variables

x_{0},\dots,x_{i},x_{\ell+1},\dots,x_{2\ell-i}
,
with at least one of the variables
x_{\ell+1},\dots,x_{2\ell-i}
appearing.

It now follows from (
4.7
) that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1=2^{N_{d,n}-N_{d,2\ell-i}}\#\left\{V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,2\ell-i}:\text{ \eqref{eq:sun} and \eqref{eq:moon} hold}\right\}.

We put

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\nu\left(\even_{j}\left(R_{i+1}^{0}(f_{V})\right)\right)\in\left\{0,\dots,N_{d,i}-c_{d,i}(j)\right\},

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\nu\left(\odd_{j}\left(R_{i+1}^{0}(f_{V})\right)\right)\in\left\{0,\dots,c_{d,i}(j)\right\},

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=\nu\left(\odd_{j}\left(Y_{\ell,i}(f_{V})\right)\right)\in\left\{0,\dots,c_{d,\ell}(j)-c_{d,i}(j)\right\}.

The total number of positions for these
-1
coefficients is

\binom{N_{d,i}-c_{d,i}(j)}{a}\binom{c_{d,i}(j)}{b}\binom{c_{d,\ell}(j)-c_{d,i}(j)}{c}.

Moreover, it follows from (
4.11
) that the number of positions for
-1
in
X_{\ell,i}(f_{V})

\binom{N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i}}{N_{d,\ell}/2-(a+b)},

on recalling that the integer
N_{d,\ell}
is assumed to be even. Similarly,
it follows from (
4.12
) that the number of positions for
-1
in
Y_{\ell,i}(f_{V})

is

\binom{N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i}-(c_{d,\ell}(j)-c_{d,i}(j))}{N_{d,\ell}/2-c_{d,\ell}(j)-a+b+c}.

For fixed
a,b,c
, among the
N_{d,i}
monomials in
x_{0},\dots,x_{i}
only,
a+b
coefficients are
-1
, and the others are
+1
. Similarly, the signs are already accounted for among the

N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i}

monomials in

R_{\ell+1}^{0}(f_{V})-R_{i+1}^{0}(f_{V})
, and in the
N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i}
monomials in

R_{i+1}^{\ell-i}(f_{V})-R_{i+1}^{0}(f_{V})
. Hence the number of free coefficients is

N_{d,2\ell-i}-N_{d,i}-2(N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i})=N_{d,2\ell-i}-2N_{d,\ell}+N_{d,i}.

In conclusion, for any
j\in\{0,\dots,i\}
and
(x,y)\in T_{n,\ell}^{(i,j)}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle=2^{N_{d,n}-2N_{d,\ell}+N_{d,i}-1}\hskip-5.69046pt\sum_{a=0}^{N_{d,i}-c_{d,i}(j)}\ \sum_{b=0}^{c_{d,i}(j)}\ \sum_{c=0}^{c_{d,\ell}(j)-c_{d,i}(j)}\hskip-5.69046pt\binom{N_{d,i}-c_{d,i}(j)}{a}\binom{c_{d,i}(j)}{b}

\displaystyle\quad\times\binom{c_{d,\ell}(j)-c_{d,i}(j)}{c}\binom{N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i}}{N_{d,\ell}/2-(a+b)}\binom{N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i}-(c_{d,\ell}(j)-c_{d,i}(j))}{N_{d,\ell}/2-c_{d,\ell}(j)-a+b+c}.

We apply (
3.5
) to execute the sum over
c
, finding that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1=~

\displaystyle\ 2^{N_{d,n}-2N_{d,\ell}+N_{d,i}-1}\sum_{a=0}^{N_{d,i}-c_{d,i}(j)}\ \sum_{b=0}^{c_{d,i}(j)}\ \binom{N_{d,i}-c_{d,i}(j)}{a}\binom{c_{d,i}(j)}{b}

\displaystyle\times\binom{N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i}}{N_{d,\ell}/2-(a+b)}\binom{N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i}}{N_{d,\ell}/2-\left(N_{d,i}-c_{d,i}(j)-a+b\right)}.

Note that

\binom{N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i}}{N_{d,\ell}/2-\alpha}\leqslant\binom{N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i}}{\lfloor(N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i})/2\rfloor},

for any integer
\alpha\geqslant 0
.
On appealing to the higher Vandermonde identity
(
3.6
), we may conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1\leqslant~

\displaystyle\ 2^{N_{d,n}-2N_{d,\ell}+N_{d,i}-1}\binom{N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i}}{\lfloor(N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i})/2\rfloor}\binom{N_{d,\ell}}{N_{d,\ell}/2},

for each
i\in\{m_{n,\ell},\dots,\ell-1\}
and
j\in\{0,\dots,i\}
and
(x,y)\in T_{n,\ell}^{(i,j)}
.

Observe that

\sum_{j=0}^{i}\binom{i}{j}=2^{i}.

We now bring in the expression (
4.8
) for the
cardinality of
T_{n,\ell}^{(i,j)}
, together with (
1.1
), in order to deduce that

\Sigma^{(2)}(d,n;\ell)\leqslant\frac{1}{2^{2N_{d,\ell}-2\ell}}\binom{N_{d,\ell}}{N_{d,\ell}/2}^{2}\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}^{2}\Delta,

where

\Delta=\binom{N_{d,\ell}}{N_{d,\ell}/2}^{-1}\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}^{-1}\sum_{i=m_{n,\ell}}^{\ell-1}2^{N_{d,i}}\binom{n-\ell}{\ell-i}\binom{\ell+1}{i+1}\binom{N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i}}{\lfloor(N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i})/2\rfloor}.

It clearly follows from Lemma
4.2
that

(4.13)

\Sigma^{(2)}(d,n;\ell)\leqslant M_{1}(d,n;\ell)^{2}\Delta.

We now turn to an analysis of
\Delta
, beginning with the upper bound

(4.14)

\Delta\leqslant\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\binom{n-\ell}{\ell-i}\binom{\ell+1}{i+1}\Delta_{i},

where

\Delta_{i}=2^{N_{d,i}}\binom{N_{d,\ell}}{N_{d,\ell}/2}^{-1}\binom{N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i}}{\lfloor(N_{d,\ell}-N_{d,i})/2\rfloor}.

Combining the second bound in (
3.3
) with
(
3.2
), we obtain

\Delta_{i}\leqslant\left(1-\frac{N_{d,i}}{N_{d,\ell}}\right)^{-1/2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{N_{d,i}}\right)\right).

But, on recalling that
i\leqslant\ell-1
, we observe that

\frac{N_{d,i}}{N_{d,\ell}}=\prod_{j=i+1}^{\ell}\frac{j}{d+j}\leqslant\frac{1}{2^{\ell-i}},

since
\frac{j}{d+j}\leqslant 1/2
for
j\leqslant\ell\leqslant d
. Hence

\Delta_{i}\leqslant 1+O(2^{-(\ell-i)})
,
from which it follows that

\Delta\leqslant\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\binom{n-\ell}{\ell-i}\binom{\ell+1}{i+1}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{2^{\ell-i}}\right)\right)

in (
4.14
).
On the one hand, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\binom{n-\ell}{\ell-i}\binom{\ell+1}{i+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\binom{n-\ell}{1+\ell-i}\binom{\ell+1}{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell+1}\binom{n-\ell}{1+\ell-i}\binom{\ell+1}{i}-1-\binom{n-\ell}{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}-1-\binom{n-\ell}{\ell+1},

by the Vandermonde identity (
3.4
).
On the other hand, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\binom{n-\ell}{\ell-i}\binom{\ell+1}{i+1}\frac{1}{2^{\ell-i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\binom{n-\ell}{\ell+1-i}\binom{\ell+1}{i}\frac{1}{2^{\ell+1-i}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{\ell+1}\binom{n-\ell}{\ell+1-i}\binom{\ell+1}{i}\frac{1}{2^{\ell+1-i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell+1}\binom{n-\ell}{j}\binom{\ell+1}{j}\frac{1}{2^{j}}.

Thus, in the light of Lemma
3.3
, we deduce that

\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\binom{n-\ell}{\ell-i}\binom{\ell+1}{i+1}\frac{1}{2^{\ell-i}}\leqslant\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\mu_{\ell}},

where
\mu_{\ell}
is given by (
4.5
).
Putting this together in (
4.13
), we deduce that

(4.15)

\begin{split}\Sigma^{(2)}(d,n;\ell)\leqslant~&M_{1}(d,n;\ell)^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}}-\frac{\binom{n-\ell}{\ell+1}}{\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}}\right)+O\left(\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\mu_{\ell}}\right).\end{split}

Analysis of
\Sigma^{(3)}(d,n;\ell)

We remark that reasoning exactly as in the proof of (
4.4
), we deduce that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathbb{B}_{d,n}\\
x,y\in V(\mathbb{Q})\end{subarray}}1=2^{N_{d,n}-2N_{d,\ell}-1}\binom{N_{d,\ell}}{N_{d,\ell}/2}^{2},

for any
(x,y)\in T_{n,\ell}
.
Recalling (
1.1
) and (
4.9
), we thus see that

(4.16)

\begin{split}\Sigma^{(3)}(d,n;\ell)&=\frac{1}{2^{2N_{d,\ell}-2\ell}}\binom{N_{d,\ell}}{N_{d,\ell}/2}^{2}\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}\binom{n-\ell}{\ell+1}\\
&=M_{1}(d,n;\ell)^{2}\frac{\binom{n-\ell}{\ell+1}}{\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}},\end{split}

by Lemma
4.2
.

Conclusion of the argument

Noting that

\binom{n+1}{\ell+1}\geqslant n

if
\ell<n
, we may now put together (
4.10
), (
4.15
) and (
4.16
) to eventually obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=1}^{3}\Sigma^{(\nu)}(d,n;\ell)\leqslant~

\displaystyle M_{1}(d,n;\ell)^{2}\left(1+\boldsymbol{1}_{\ell=n}+O\left(\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\min\{\ell,\mu_{\ell}\}}+\frac{1}{\min\{d,n\}}\right)\right).

Recalling the equality (
4.6
) we see that this completes the proof.
∎

5.
Proof of the main results

Recall the definition (
4.2
) of the moments
M_{k}(d,n;\ell)
, for
k\geqslant 0
.
We are now in position to establish Theorems
1.1

and
1.2
. Both rely on the inequality

(5.1)

M_{1}(d,n;\ell)^{2}\leqslant r_{d,n}M_{2}(d,n;\ell),

for any
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
which follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We assume that
d
is sufficiently large and that
n\geqslant d+\log d
. Let
\ell\in\{1,\dots,d\}
be such that
N_{d,\ell}
is even. Then, on applying Lemma
4.4
and using our assumption
n\geqslant d+\log d>d
, we obtain

(5.2)

1\leqslant r_{d,n}\left(1+\boldsymbol{1}_{\ell=n}+O\left(\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\min\{\ell,\mu_{\ell}\}}+\frac{1}{d}+\frac{1}{M_{1}(d,n;\ell)}\right)\right),

where

\mu_{\ell}=\frac{(n-\ell)(\ell+1)}{n+1}.

Note that
in the special case
d=n=\ell
, a repeated application of (
3.2
) yields

\displaystyle M_{1}(d,d;d)\sim\frac{2^{d+1/2}}{\pi^{1/2}N_{d,d}^{1/2}}\sim\frac{2^{1/2}d^{1/4}}{\pi^{1/4}},

whence
M_{1}(d,d;d)\gg d^{1/4}
.

We start by handling the case where
d
is a power of
2
.
The equality (
2.1
) implies that
N_{d,d}
is even, since

v_{2}(N_{d,d})=2s_{2}(d)-s_{2}(2d)=1.

Taking

\ell=d
, we note that
\mu_{d}=(n-d)(d+1)/(n+1)
is a strictly increasing function of
n
. Thus

\mu_{d}\geqslant\frac{(d+1)\log d}{d+1+\log d}\geqslant\frac{10\log d}{11},

since
n\geqslant d+\log d
and
1+(\log d)/(d+1)\leqslant 11/10
for large enough values of
d
.
But then it follows that

\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\mu_{d}}\leqslant\frac{1}{d^{\theta}},

where
\theta=10\log(4/3)/11>1/4
.
Since
r_{d,n}\leqslant 1
and
d<n
, the choice
\ell=d
in (
5.2
) therefore provides

r_{d,n}=1+O\left(\frac{1}{d^{1/4}}+\frac{1}{M_{1}(d,n;d)}\right).

This is satisfactory since
M_{1}(d,n;d)\geqslant M_{1}(d,d;d)\gg d^{1/4}
.

We now deal with the case where
d
is not a power of
2
. We let
2^{k_{0}(d)}
and
2^{k_{1}(d)}
be the largest and second largest powers of
2
, respectively,
appearing in the binary expansion of
d
. It is clear that

(5.3)

d\leqslant 1+2+\cdots+2^{k_{1}(d)-1}+2^{k_{1}(d)}+2^{k_{0}(d)}\leqslant 2^{k_{1}(d)+1}+2^{k_{0}(d)},

on evaluating the first
k_{1}(d)
terms as a geometric series.
Let

\ell=\begin{cases}2^{k_{1}(d)}&\textrm{if }2^{k_{1}(d)}>d/7,\\
2^{k_{1}(d)}+2^{k_{0}(d)-2}&\textrm{if }2^{k_{1}(d)}\leqslant d/7.\end{cases}

This choice ensures that the integers
d
and
\ell
share a common digit
1
in their binary expansions. Indeed, otherwise we would have
2^{k_{1}(d)}\leqslant d/7
and
k_{0}(d)-2=k_{1}(d)
. But then (
5.3
)
would yield
d\leqslant 6\cdot 2^{k_{1}(d)}\leqslant 6d/7
, which is a contradiction.
It therefore follows from Lemma
2.1
that
N_{d,\ell}
is even.

Next we claim that

(5.4)

\frac{d}{7}<\ell\leqslant\frac{d}{2}.

This is obvious when
2^{k_{1}(d)}>d/7
, since then

\ell=2^{k_{1}(d)}
and

3\cdot 2^{k_{1}(d)}=2^{k_{1}(d)}+2^{k_{1}(d)+1}\leqslant 2^{k_{1}(d)}+2^{k_{0}(d)}\leqslant d,

which is more than enough to ensure
\ell\leqslant d/2
.
Suppose that

2^{k_{1}(d)}\leqslant d/7
. Then

\ell=2^{k_{1}(d)}+2^{k_{0}(d)-2}=\frac{3}{4}2^{k_{1}(d)}+\frac{1}{4}\left(2^{k_{1}(d)}+2^{k_{0}(d)}\right)\leqslant\left(\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{7}+\frac{1}{4}\right)d\leqslant\frac{d}{2}.

It follows from (
5.3
)
that
2^{k_{0}(d)}\geqslant d-2^{k_{1}(d)+1}
. Hence

\ell=2^{k_{1}(d)}+2^{k_{0}(d)-2}\geqslant 2^{k_{1}(d)}+\frac{1}{4}\left(d-2^{k_{1}(d)+1}\right)\geqslant\frac{d}{4}>\frac{d}{7}.

This completes the verification of (
5.4
).

For
n\geqslant d+\log d>d
, we see that

\mu_{\ell}=\frac{(n-\ell)(\ell+1)}{n+1}\geqslant\frac{(d-\ell)(\ell+1)}{d+1},

since the left hand side is a strictly increasing function of
n
.
Applying (
5.4
), we may set

(5.5)

\lambda=\frac{\ell}{d}\in\left(\frac{1}{7},\frac{1}{2}\right].

The inequality
\mu_{\ell}\geqslant d/10
happens if

10(1-\lambda)d\lambda+10(1-\lambda)\geqslant d+1
.
But
10(1-\lambda)\geqslant 10\cdot 1/2\geqslant 1
, by (
5.5
).
Hence it suffices to check that

10(1-\lambda)\lambda\geqslant 1
, which is true for all
\lambda
in the range (
5.5
).
Thus it follows that

\min\{\ell,\mu_{\ell}\}\geqslant d/10
.

Note that

(3/4)^{d/10}<1/d
for sufficiently large
d
.
Appealing to the inequality (
5.2
) and noting that
r_{d,n}\leqslant 1
,
we finally obtain

(5.6)

r_{d,n}=1+O\left(\frac{1}{d}+\frac{1}{M_{1}(d,n;\ell)}\right).

Adopting the notation (
5.5
),
it follows from (
3.2
) and
Lemma
4.2
that

M_{1}(d,n;\ell)\gg 2^{\lambda d}\binom{(1+\lambda)d}{d}^{-1/2}\binom{n}{\lambda d}.

Appealing to Stirling’s formula (
3.1
) and using our assumption
n\geqslant d+\log d\geqslant d
, we thus get

\displaystyle M_{1}(d,n;\ell)

\displaystyle\gg 2^{\lambda d}\frac{d^{d/2+1/4}(\lambda d)^{\lambda d/2+1/4}}{((1+\lambda)d)^{(1+\lambda)d/2+1/4}}\cdot\frac{d^{d+1/2}}{(\lambda d)^{\lambda d+1/2}((1-\lambda)d)^{(1-\lambda)d+1/2}}

\displaystyle\gg\left(\frac{2^{\lambda}}{\lambda^{\lambda/2}(1+\lambda)^{(1+\lambda)/2}(1-\lambda)^{1-\lambda}}\right)^{d}\frac{1}{d^{1/4}}.

A numerical check now shows that

\frac{2^{\lambda}}{\lambda^{\lambda/2}(1+\lambda)^{(1+\lambda)/2}(1-\lambda)^{1-\lambda}}>1,

for any
\lambda\in(1/7,1/2]
.
Recalling the estimate (
5.6
), this therefore completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Throughout the proof we may assume that

n\geqslant d^{1/2}\log d
.
We take

\mathcal{D}=\left\{d\geqslant 1:v_{2}(d)\leqslant\log\log\log d\right\},

and we start by noting that

\#\{d\leqslant X:d\notin\mathcal{D}\}\ll\frac{X}{\log\log X}.

Thus
\mathcal{D}
has density
1
, as required for
Theorem
1.2
.

For any
d\in\mathcal{D}
,
we now assume that
\ell
is a positive integer
such that
\ell<d^{1/5}
and
N_{d,\ell}
is even.
In particular
\ell<n
, since
n\geqslant d^{1/2}\log d
. Combining the inequality (
5.1
) and Lemma
4.4
, we therefore deduce that

(5.7)

1\leqslant r_{d,n}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}+\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\min\{\ell,\mu_{\ell}\}}+\frac{1}{M_{1}(d,n;\ell)}\right),

where
\mu_{\ell}=(n-\ell)(\ell+1)/(n+1)
. Note that

\mu_{\ell}\geqslant\ell
if and only if
n\geqslant\ell^{2}+2\ell
.
But this is obviously satisfied if
n\geqslant d^{1/2}\log d
and
\ell<d^{1/5}
, assuming that
d
is sufficiently large.

We next focus on a lower bound for
M_{1}(d,n;\ell)
.
Writing that
2^{\ell}\geqslant\ell+1
,
it follows from (
3.2
) and
Lemma
4.2
that

\displaystyle M_{1}(d,n;\ell)

\displaystyle\gg\frac{n}{N_{d,\ell}^{1/2}}\binom{n}{\ell}=\frac{n}{(\ell!)^{1/2}}\cdot\frac{n!}{(n-\ell)!}\cdot\frac{(d!)^{1/2}}{((d+\ell)!)^{1/2}}.

Appealing to Stirling’s formula (
3.1
), we deduce that

\displaystyle M_{1}(d,n;\ell)

\displaystyle\gg\frac{n}{\ell^{\ell/2+1/4}}\cdot\frac{n^{n+1/2}}{(n-\ell)^{n-\ell+1/2}}\cdot\frac{d^{d/2+1/4}}{(d+\ell)^{(d+\ell)/2+1/4}}

\displaystyle\gg\frac{n}{\ell^{\ell/2+1/4}}\left(\frac{n}{d^{1/2}}\right)^{\ell}\left(1-\frac{\ell}{n}\right)^{-n+\ell}\left(1+\frac{\ell}{d}\right)^{-(d+\ell)/2}.

Using our assumptions
\ell<d^{1/5}
and
n\geqslant d^{1/2}\log d
, we claim that

\left(1-\frac{\ell}{n}\right)^{-n+\ell}\left(1+\frac{\ell}{d}\right)^{-(d+\ell)/2}\gg e^{\ell/2}.

Taking logarithms of both sides, we note that this is equivalent to proving

(-n+\ell)\log\left(1-\frac{\ell}{n}\right)-\frac{d+\ell}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{\ell}{d}\right)\geqslant\frac{\ell}{2}+O(1).

But
\log(1-\ell/n)=-\ell/n+O(\ell^{2}/n^{2})
and

\log(1+\ell/d)=\ell/d+O(\ell^{2}/d^{2})
. Hence the claim follows under our assumption that

\ell<d^{1/5}
and
n\geqslant d^{1/2}\log d
.
Using our assumption
n\geqslant d^{1/2}\log d
, we finally obtain

M_{1}(d,n;\ell)\gg\frac{n(\log d)^{\ell}e^{\ell/2}}{\ell^{\ell/2+1/4}}\gg\frac{d^{1/2}(\log d)^{\ell}}{\ell^{\ell/2}}.

Returning to
(
5.7
) and observing that
r_{d,n}\leqslant 1
, we therefore obtain

(5.8)

r_{d,n}=1+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}+\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\ell}+\frac{\ell^{\ell/2}}{d^{1/2}{(\log d)^{\ell}}}\right).

It is now time to choose a suitable value of
\ell
.
We need to ensure that
N_{d,\ell}
is even, that
\ell<d^{1/5}
and that the error terms in (
5.8
) are satisfactory for
Theorem
1.2
.
We shall assume that
d
is sufficiently large.
Let

\ell=2^{v_{2}(d)}+2^{\lfloor\frac{\log\log d}{\log 2}\rfloor+3}.

Since

v_{2}(d)\leqslant\log\log\log d<\left\lfloor\frac{\log\log d}{\log 2}\right\rfloor+3

for sufficiently large
d\in\mathcal{D}
,
so it follows that

d
and
\ell
share a common digit
1
in their binary expansion. Hence
Lemma
2.1
ensures that

N_{d,\ell}
is even. Moreover

\ell\leqslant 2^{\log\log\log d}+2^{\log\log d/\log 2+3}\leqslant\log\log d+8\log d

and
\ell\geqslant 2^{\log\log d/\log 2+2}=4\log d
.
It follows that
4\log d\leqslant\ell\leqslant 9\log d
for
sufficiently large
d\in\mathcal{D}
, which is more than enough to ensure that

\ell<d^{1/5}
.

Turning to the three error terms in
(
5.8
), the first is clearly satisfactory. The second is satisfactory on observing that

4\log(4/3)>1/2
.
To handle the third, we note that

\displaystyle\frac{\ell^{\ell/2}}{d^{1/2}(\log d)^{\ell}}=\frac{1}{d^{1/2}}\left(\frac{\ell^{1/2}}{\log d}\right)^{\ell}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{d^{1/2}}\left(\frac{3}{\sqrt{\log d}}\right)^{\ell},

on taking
\ell\leqslant 9\log d
. This is at most
1/\sqrt{d}
for
sufficiently large
d\in\mathcal{D}
,
which therefore completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
∎
