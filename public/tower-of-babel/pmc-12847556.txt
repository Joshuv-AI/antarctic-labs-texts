Constructing an ultrasound-assisted organoid model for tumor management
Authors: Tiantian Li, Xueying Wang, Miao Zhu, Sen Han, Dongyi Zhao, Vera Wong, Wangxing Huang, Zilin Li, Huajiao Zhao, Xiaoqin Qian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Organoids, an emerging bioengineering approach, can replicate the structure and function of human organs in vitro, providing valuable tools for biomedical research. Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) retain the histological and genetic characteristics of the original tumor, playing a crucial role in precision oncology. Ultrasound-assisted technology, particularly multimodal ultrasound imaging, facilitates the establishment of PDOs for inoperable or rare tumors by enabling precise tumor localization, minimally invasive tissue sampling, and high-purity cell extraction.This review explores the application of multimodal ultrasound techniques in PDO development across various tumor types, highlighting their advantages in obtaining high-purity tumor cells, improving PDO culture success rates, and advancing personalized cancer treatment. Additionally, we discuss factors influencing PDO establishment, such as tumor type, sampling methods, and bioengineering advancements. By summarizing current progress and challenges, this review provides insights into optimizing ultrasound-assisted PDO construction for clinical and research applications.

Background

Organoids are three-dimensional structures derived from stem cells or primary tissue samples cultured in vitro, designed to mimic the tissue architecture and function of real organs. These “mini-organs” hold promise in precision medicine, offering novel tools for studying tissue development, disease mechanisms, including cell-cell interactions, and drug screening, particularly in complex diseases, such as cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and metabolic diseases. Furthermore, organoids are reproducible and scalable, serving as reliable experimental models for both basic research and clinical applications [1, 2]. PDOs retain the histological and genetic characteristics of the original tumor or organ, making them powerful tools in personalized medicine, particularly in oncology. They serve as a platform to evaluate patient-specific therapeutic responses, thus reducing the trial-and-error approach related to conventional treatments [3–5].

Ultrasound-guided technology utilizes ultrasound waves for real-time imaging and is widely applied in medical diagnostics and interventional procedures. This technique offers several advantages, including high safety, non-invasiveness, real-time monitoring, and dynamic imaging, enabling precise guidance for tumor resection and biopsies [6, 7]. In PDO construction, ultrasound imaging facilitates accurate tumor localization and allows histological or cytological biopsies under real-time guidance, enhancing tumor cell extraction efficiency, streamlining PDO culture methods, and optimizing treatment strategies.Notably, multimodal ultrasound imaging in this context refers to the integration of two or more complementary ultrasound-based techniques—such as conventional ultrasound (US), contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), and elastography—to comprehensively characterize lesions across multiple dimensions including anatomical structure, functionality, hemodynamics, and tissue stiffness. Specifically, US provides real-time anatomical imaging for initial lesion localization, puncture trajectory planning, and needle guidance, while CEUS visualizes microvascular perfusion to identify viable tumor areas and avoid necrotic regions during sampling. This integrated approach significantly improves sampling accuracy and positivity rates, which is crucial for the successful establishment of organoids in vitro.

Multimodal ultrasound-assisted PDO construction offers several key advantages. First, its minimally invasive nature ensures safer tissue harvesting while reducing the risks associated with traditional surgical methods. For surgically inoperable tumors, such as highly malignant pancreatic cancer, this approach facilitates research into tumor development mechanisms and enables in vitro drug sensitivity testing. Second, unlike conventional organoid culture methods, which are often time-consuming and costly, ultrasound-guided biopsy technology allows for the rapid acquisition of tumor cells, yielding short-term drug sensitivity results. This accelerates the assessment of drug efficacy and safety, providing valuable insights for clinical decision-making—particularly in cases where tumor progression is exacerbated by treatment delays [8, 9]. By improving sampling precision and culture efficiency, multimodal ultrasound-assisted PDO models support tailored therapeutic strategies and offer innovative opportunities for personalized medicine, driving significant advancements in both fundamental research and clinical practice. Research progress on constructing PDOs using ultrasound-guided technique

Contrast-enhanced and ultrasound-guided puncture sampling for PDOs

Contrast-enhanced ultrasound-guided puncture sampling

Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) encompasses the injection of a contrast agent (usually a liquid containing microbubbles) through peripheral veins, which improves visualization of the microcirculation within the lesion. This approach enhances differentiation between the lesion and surrounding structures, enables the identification of the tumor area, and distinguishes active areas with abundant blood supply and necrotic liquefaction areas without blood supply. Furthermore, it facilitates the visualization of blood vessels along the puncture path [10]. Combined with two-dimensional ultrasound, CEUS enables the selection of the optimal puncture path to avoid accidentally penetrating important organs or blood vessels, thereby improving the success rate and safety of the procedure. This approach yields highly active tumor cells, thus increasing the success rate of PDO culture. Tumor formation is a developmental process, affected by the surrounding tissues. Ultrasound-guided sampling facilitates the acquisition of target tissues, including tumor core regions, for understanding tumor heterogeneity and advancing individualized treatment strategies [11].

Ultrasound-guided puncture sampling methods

Two methods are used for ultrasound-guided sample collection [12, 13]:

(1) Ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (FNA) yields a high success rate in obtaining high-purity cells from tumor core regions in pancreatic cancer and lung cancer tissues. These samples provide a reliable and high-quality cell source for subsequent organoid culture [14–16].

(2) Ultrasound-guided fine-needle biopsy (FNB) can obtain larger tissue samples with high quality, yielding high PDO culture success rates [17, 18]. Moreover, ultrasound-guided FNB can be divided into endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided FNB and ultrasound-guided percutaneous FNB, such as EUS-guided pancreatic FNB and ultrasound-guided percutaneous pancreatic FNB [19].

With continuous advancements in multimodal ultrasound technology, ultrasound-guided sampling has gained attention in obtaining high-purity and viable cell samples and optimizing PDO culture conditions (Fig. 1).

Nonetheless, ultrasound-guided sampling provides a minimally invasive and safe option, particularly for patients with inoperable, small, or difficult-to-reach advanced tumors, as well as for rare tumors. Improving PDO efficacy and retaining tumor features

Ultrasound guidance substantially enhances the efficiency of PDO generation while retaining the biological characteristics of the original tumor. This technique adheres to ethical concerns, reduces research costs, predicts drug responses, and assesses treatment effectiveness in vitro [47]. Liver [48–50], breast [51–53], and gastrointestinal [54, 55] cancer PDOs have emerged as valuable models for advancing anticancer drug screening, biobanking, omics profiling, biomarker discovery, and studying tumor development mechanisms. These PDOs hold promise in personalized treatment research, including exploring host-microbiome interactions [32, 56].

Enabling repeated sampling and disease progression research

Multi-modal ultrasound-guided puncture enables repeated sampling throughout tumor formation and progression. This feature is particularly advantageous for studying endocrine organ tumors, such as those of the thyroid, parathyroid, and adrenal glands. Repeated sampling can be obtained through multiple punctures in different types of diseases (such as tumors, primary dysfunction or hyperactivity, or inflammation) and at different disease stages [57–62], facilitating the construction of organoid models. This approach enables studying the mechanisms underlying disease occurrence and development, drug response, individual differences, and personalized treatment planning.

Factors affecting the success rate of PDO construction

Impact of different sample sources on PDO construction success rates

High-purity, viable tissue samples are crucial for PDO construction. However, obtaining fresh samples from patients with advanced-stage tumors—who are often ineligible for surgery—poses a major challenge. To this end, multimodal ultrasound-guided approaches have been utilized to derive high-purity tumor cells from puncture samples, thus improving PDO construction success rates [63].

(1) CEUS-guided biopsy achieves PDO construction success rates comparable to those of surgical specimens in inoperable pancreatic cancer. Matsumoto et al. compared PDO success rates using tumor samples obtained through EUS-FNB, liver metastases biopsy, surgical resection, abdominal puncture, and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography. Using a single puncture with a 22-gauge (G) Franseen needle, EUS-FNB achieved the highest success rate of >70% and yielded more viable cells than other methods [35]. This finding underscores the advantages of ultrasound-assisted techniques in PDO construction for pancreatic cancer. Grutzmeier et al. systematically reviewed PDO success rates from pancreatic cancer samples acquired through various methods, including EUS-guided biopsy, percutaneous biopsy, and surgical specimens. The overall success rate was 63%, with EUS-guided biopsy achieving 60%, which was comparable to the 62% success rate for surgical specimens. By contrast, percutaneous biopsy lagged at 36% success rate [19]. Similarly, Kim et al. established patient-specific PDO models from pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) tissues using EUS-FNB, with a success rate of 83.2% [64]. Therefore, EUS-guided biopsy and surgical specimens share comparable efficacy for establishing PDOs, making EUS-guided biopsy a preferred approach in pancreatic cancer.

(2) Comparative studies suggest that EUS-FNB is superior to EUS-FNA in generating PDOs with pancreatic cancer histological samples, despite statistical insignificance [18]. Lee et al. successfully established 12 PDOs from EUS-FNA samples of 20 patients with advanced pancreatic cancer [65]. Furthermore, Park et al. successfully established lung cancer organoids (LCOs) from ultrasound-guided FNA samples of 113 patients with lung cancer, achieving an initial success rate of 40.7%. The use of single-cell RNA sequencing affirmed the high purity of cells from the tumor core in early-passage LCOs [66]. Overall, FNB achieves higher PDO construction success rates than FNA [67–69].

Variations in PDO success rates across different tumor types

Under ultrasound guidance, PDO success rates differ across tumor types. Choi et al. successfully established PDOs from 70 MA samples or pleural effusion obtained via puncture, achieving success rates of 48.7% for pancreatic cancer, 33.3% for stomach cancer, and 20% for breast cancer. Notably, successful PDOs should be maintained for five or more generations [32]. In advanced lung cancer, Park et al. developed LCOs from FNA biopsy samples collected from 113 patients, achieving an initial success rate of 40.7% [66]. Additionally, Cartry et al. constructed 25 PDOs from colorectal cancer samples, demonstrating a 94% genomic concordance with the original tumors. The success rate for PDOs derived from core needle biopsies was 61.5% [70]. For prostate cancer, Puca et al. reported a 73.5% PDO success rate (25/34) using needle aspiration biopsies [26]. Graham et al. utilized mechanical and enzymatic hydrolysis to isolate ovarian tumor tissue, ascites, and pleural fluid samples into cell suspensions, which were cultured with basement membrane extract and optimized growth medium. Their approach yielded a success rate of >97% [43]. Nuciforo et al. reported a 26% success rate for generating hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) organoids from tumor biopsies (10 of 38 HCC biopsies) and a 33% success rate for patients with HCC (8 of 24) [71]. Tiriac et al. conducted a prospective trial in which patients with pancreatic cancer underwent EUS-FNB for tissue collection, where 87% of tumors (33/38) successfully formed organoids, with 66% (25/38) sustaining growth for at least five generations [72]. Pleural mesothelioma affects the lining of the lungs and chest wall, characterized by poor prognosis and limited response to first-line treatments. Hocking et al. established five PDOs from 10 malignant pleural effusions collected from nine patients. These PDOs retained the histopathological and genomic characteristics of patient-matched biopsy specimens and exhibited cytotoxic sensitivity to cisplatin treatment in vitro [37].

Collectively, PDO success rates vary substantially across cancer types, likely because of differences in cytological and molecular tumor characteristics (Table 1).

Impact of diseases on PDO success rates and expansion potential

Yang et al. processed EUS-FNB samples from 15 patients with pancreatic cancer and established PDOs with a success rate of 93.3% (14/15). Among these, 60% (9/15) sustained amplification over five generations, demonstrating the feasibility of long-term PDO culture. Their study revealed that patients with diabetes, a family history of cancer, or larger tumors exhibited greater organoid expansion potential, suggesting that systemic and genetic factors may influence PDO viability and growth dynamics.

Several factors contribute to PDO success rates and expansion potential. High-quality initial tumor samples are essential, as tissue integrity, cellular composition, and tumor heterogeneity directly affect culture viability. Studies have shown that rapid processing and culture initiation of fresh biopsy samples significantly enhance PDO establishment, reducing cellular stress and maintaining tumor-specific characteristics. Moreover, underlying diseases such as metabolic disorders, chronic inflammation, and immune system dysregulation may alter the tumor microenvironment, impacting PDO growth and therapeutic responses.

Additionally, research indicates that pancreatic tumors associated with diabetes often exhibit metabolic reprogramming, which may create a favorable niche for PDO expansion. Genetic predisposition, including familial cancer syndromes, can also affect organoid stability, with tumors driven by germline mutations potentially displaying enhanced adaptability to ex vivo culture conditions. Further investigations are needed to elucidate the molecular and environmental mechanisms underpinning these observations. By refining sampling techniques and optimizing culture conditions, future studies aim to maximize PDO success rates and expand their application in personalized medicine and drug screening [11].

Innovations in clinical biopsy procedures promoting PDO construction

Ikezawa et al. successfully established pancreatic cancer PDOs using residual samples from saline flushes (RSSFs) obtained during EUS-FNA. The establishment success rate was 80% (4/5 participants), which was comparable to that achieved using standard EUS-FNA samples. This approach highlights RSSFs as an overlooked resource, offering all patients with pancreatic cancer the opportunity to establish PDOs without requiring additional invasive procedures [73]. Lacomb et al. compared the success rates of single versus double EUS-FNB in isolating and establishing PDAC PDOs. Their findings suggested similar success rates between the two techniques, with no substantial safety concerns. Therefore, a single biopsy is sufficient to reduce patient risk and improve tissue utilization [74].

Bioengineering advancements affect PDO success rates

Biohydrogels, co-cultures, microfluidics, and gene editing are rapidly advancing pancreatic cancer PDO models, significantly improving their physiological relevance and culture success rates. Biohydrogels provide a biomimetic extracellular matrix, enhancing cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation while better recapitulating the tumor microenvironment. Co-culture systems incorporating stromal and immune cells enable a more comprehensive study of tumor-immune interactions, aiding in the development of immunotherapy strategies [75]. Additionally, microfluidic platforms allow for dynamic nutrient exchange and controlled culture conditions, improving PDO viability and enabling high-throughput drug screening. Gene editing technologies, such as CRISPR-Cas9, facilitate precise genetic modifications, enabling functional studies of oncogenes and tumor suppressors. These innovations not only enhance PDO fidelity but also expand their applications in personalized medicine and drug discovery. As research progresses, the integration of these technologies is expected to drive significant advancements in pancreatic cancer modeling and therapeutic development [76], as demonstrated by recent advances in breast cancer models utilizing microfluidic platforms [77].

Potential clinical applications of ultrasound-guided puncture sampling for constructing PDOs

Mimicking organ structure

Bergdorf et al. utilized a 75% Matrigel discoid culture method to construct PDOs from FNA-derived thyroid cancer samples. Immunofluorescence staining suggested cellular morphology, enabling the identification of fibroblasts and cancer cells within the PDOs [78]. For melanoma PDOs obtained through ultrasound-assisted fine-needle biopsy, Phifer et al. developed a protocol for immunofluorescence staining and imaging, encompassing fixation, permeabilization, blocking, primary and secondary immunostaining, and slide preparation. This protocol aimed to prevent mechanical damage and minimize background staining to ensure accurate mimicking of melanoma PDO structures [79, 80].

Ultrasound-assisted PDO models can closely mimic diverse cellular morphologies of their original organs, contributing meaningly to pathological diagnosis. Song et al. utilized scRNA-seq to analyze prostate biopsies, prostatectomy specimens, and PDOs from patients with localized prostate cancer. They found no significant differences in epithelial cell heterogeneity among the three sample types. Notably, tumor-associated club cells served as potential biomarkers for early detection and treatment of prostate cancer [81]. Similarly, Ishida et al. successfully established PDAC PDOs using EUS-FNB. These PDOs demonstrated three distinct morphologies: ductal, dormant, and adherent small clusters. Additionally, this study proposed a novel “hybrid” classification and reported that PDAC PDOs could be cultured even after obtaining pathologically ambiguous EUS-FNB results, providing a reliable diagnostic tool method for PDAC [47].

Mimicking the function of organs

Ultrasound-assisted PDO models not only replicate the structural features of original tissues but also recapitulate their functional properties, providing valuable platforms for studying disease mechanisms and therapeutic interventions. A notable example comes from Sekhar et al., who established PDOs from benign parathyroid tissues of six patients with primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) using ultrasound-guided FNA. Through comprehensive metabolomic profiling via mass spectrometry and live-cell bioenergetic analyses, the study demonstrated that these PDOs closely mimicked the metabolic characteristics of native PHPT tissues. Importantly, the PDOs retained critical functional abilities, including parathyroid hormone (PTH) secretion, even after cryopreservation and thawing. This functional preservation highlights their utility for long-term storage and repeated experimental use. Furthermore, the authors validated the models’ responsiveness to extracellular calcium modulation, observing appropriate physiological decreases in PTH secretion in response to elevated calcium levels—a key regulatory mechanism in parathyroid physiology. These findings underscore the potential of parathyroid PDOs for metabolic disorder research, drug screening, and personalized treatment strategies for endocrine diseases. The success of this ultrasound-assisted approach demonstrates its broader applicability in generating functionally representative organoid models for other solid tumors and metabolic disorders [82].

Drug screening and individualized treatment

(1) Ultrasound-assisted PDOs retain the characteristics of the original tumor, enabling the prediction of personalized treatment responses. Armstrong et al. constructed PDOs from patients with PDAC using EUS-FNB and screened 15 PDOs against 18 chemotherapeutic drugs. Their findings indicated significant individual variability in drug response, which correlated with patients’ transcriptomic profiles. Dose-response curves for PDOs were reproducible and aligned with patient survival data from The Cancer Genome Atlas [83]. Similarly, Demyan et al. established a large biobank of pancreatic cancer PDOs and demonstrated a significant correlation between PDO chemotherapy responses and patient outcomes, particularly with oxaliplatin. Moreover, PDOs effectively predicted responses to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, particularly in pre- and post-chemotherapy comparisons [84]. PDOs successfully replicated clinical radiation responses in pancreatic cancer, emphasizing their potential in pancreatic cancer [85].

(2) Ultrasound-assisted PDO models have advanced the understanding of drug mechanisms. Kang et al. developed an organoid-based platform utilizing FNA to quantitatively assess PDO responses to drug therapies and tumor-stromal interactions. They identified heterogeneous apoptotic responses and tumor-stromal regulatory profiles in PDOs after standard chemotherapy. A high α-smooth muscle actin to cytokeratin 19 ratio >1.0 was associated with prolonged survival [15]. Additionally, Hennig et al. classified PDOs derived from EUS-FNA into quasi-mesenchymal, exocrine, and classical subtypes based on keratin 81 and cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) immunoreactivity. Subsequent drug testing with gemcitabine, 5-fluorouracil, oxaliplatin, and irinotecan indicated that CFTR may serve as a biomarker for PDO subtype classification and clinical application [86].

(3) Short-term PDO culture advances drug screening. Chen et al. collected high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma PDOs from MA, extracted multicellular spheroids, and cultured them under specific conditions. Within 1 week, they analyzed transcriptome changes via scRNA-seq and assessed drug sensitivity using cell viability assays. This rapid PDO culture method enables personalized treatment recommendations offering novel possibilities for patients with relapsed or drug-resistant cancers [9]. Likewise, Onder et al. constructed breast cancer PDOs retaining the phenotypic and mutational signatures of the original tumors. They assessed various drugs targeting human epidermal growth factor receptor 2, phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B, cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6, poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase, and β-tubulin, including lapatinib, capivasertib, alpelisib, and paclitaxel. Dose-response curves were generated using serial drug dilution experiments, and half-maximal inhibitory concentrations were calculated. The drug responses varied across different PDO lines and were consistent with clinical outcomes [33]. Similarly, Cartry et al. constructed PDOs from core needle puncture samples of patients with colorectal cancer and tested them against 25 Food and Drug Administration-approved anticancer drugs. They reported that 92% of PDOs identified at least one effective drug. Interestingly, drug response was inversely correlated with the degree of metastasis and the number of prior treatment lines [70].

Exploring tumor pathogenesis and tumor microenvironment

(1) Ultrasound-assisted PDOs simulate the tumor microenvironment (TME), facilitating a deeper understanding of tumor cell biology, drug response, and resistance mechanisms [87–89]. Grutzmeier et al. successfully co-cultured PDOs with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) from EUS-FNB samples. CAFs significantly promoted PDO growth, with half of the co-cultures displaying interactions between CAFs and PDOs. This co-culture model emphasizes incorporating the TME into pancreatic cancer treatment and personalized drug screening [90].

(2) PDOs facilitate the identification of proteins and chemokines related to Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT) and TME, providing novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies to improve patient outcomes [91]. Xiao et al. utilized mass spectrometry to analyze fine-needle samples from 24 patients with HT and 24 healthy controls collected. They identified 44 differentially expressed proteins involved in cell adhesion, metabolism, and thyroxine synthesis. Notably, HT organoids displayed the significant upregulation of chemokines, such as C-C motif ligand (CCL)2 and CCL3, both central to HT pathogenesis. Further characterization of HT and papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) organoids suggested specific thyroid, HT, and PTC markers. Consistently high expression of galectin-3 in HT organoids was associated with a progression toward a cancerous phenotype.

Feasibility of constructing a xenograft tumor model using PDOs

Ultrasound-assisted PDOs have been successfully used to induce tumor formation in vivo. Nuciforo et al. utilized ultrasound-guided coaxial needle biopsy to collect tissue samples from patients with HCC at various pathological stages. They successfully established 10 PDOs that retained the morphology, tumor marker expression, and genetic heterogeneity of the original tumors. These PDOs were transplanted into immunodeficient mice. Of 10 HCC organoids, 6 were capable of stable proliferation and tumor formation in vivo. Successfully transplanted organoids generated xenograft tumors that exhibited histopathological features and tumor marker expression consistent with both the original PDOs and tumors. Additionally, this study demonstrated the differential sensitivity of HCC PDOs to sorafenib [71].

Dantes et al. developed PDOs from EUS-FNA and surgical specimens of patients with PDAC. They analyzed cell-free DNA (cfDNA) from PDO culture media, identified Kirsten rat sarcoma (KRAS) viral oncogene homolog mutations using digital droplet polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and conducted comprehensive mutation profiling via next-generation sequencing. The tumorigenic potential of these PDOs was validated through transplantation into nude mice, where tumor engraftment was observed in all transplanted mice 50 days after transplantation. By combining PDO technology with cfDNA analysis, this approach enables the rapid molecular characterization of PDAC and drug testing [92].

Prospects and challenges

In the future, ultrasound technology will increasingly integrate with imaging techniques, such as contrast-enhanced ultrasound, sonodynamics, magnetic resonance imaging, and computed tomography, to form multimodal imaging systems. These hybrid imaging techniques will improve anatomical visualization and enhance interventional safety and effectiveness. Additionally, artificial intelligence-based image analysis will further boost efficiency and diagnostic accuracy. Through technological innovation and interdisciplinary collaboration, ultrasound-guided organoid models will be more refined, with great potential in cancer research, regenerative medicine, and beyond [74].

Integration with organoid models enables rapid drug activity assessments in microfluidic systems, allowing high-throughput screening. Two types of microfluidic devices have been utilized: one designed for single-cell seeding and another for the direct injection of intact organs or organ fragments. Microfluidic organ culture technology has demonstrated exceptional efficiency in maintaining the biology of PDAC PDOs while assessing patient-specific treatment responses [93]. In the future, these microfluidic platforms are expected to evolve into companion diagnostic tools for personalized cancer therapies. Future research areas will focus on improving the success rate of PDOs constructed from malignant fluid aspiration.

However, PDO culture requires strict aseptic conditions and precise environmental control, placing high demands on laboratory equipment and techniques. Variations in patient outcomes often arise from differences in sample sources, conditions, and media formulations [11]. Thus, future research should prioritize ultrasound-guided organoid culture, promote multicenter collaborations, and standardize practices to reduce variability and improve reproducibility. Whereas ultrasound-guided organoid models hold promise, researchers should optimize sampling, improve culture media, and standardize protocols to enhance PDO success rates and clinical applicability.

Conclusion

Ultrasound-guided technology has transformed organoid cultivation by enabling real-time, precise targeting of viable tumor tissues through techniques such as contrast-enhanced ultrasound. This approach overcomes the limitations of conventional biopsy by improving sampling accuracy and tissue viability, thereby supporting more reliable drug testing and personalized treatment strategies. Future efforts should focus on standardizing ultrasound-guided protocols and promoting multidisciplinary collaboration to integrate this technology fully into clinical cancer research and therapeutic development.
