Feasibility and Safety of a Computer-Controlled Device for Transradial Cerebral Angiography: A Prospective Pilot Study
Authors: Shun Tanaka, Koji Hirata, Koichi Uramaru, Hideaki Matsumura, Takeshi Yoshimoto, Hisayuki Hosoo, Yoshiro Ito, Mikito Hayakawa, Aiki Marushima, Hiroshi Yamagami, Yuji Matsumaru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and safety of a computer-controlled local anesthetic delivery device during transradial cerebral angiography and to descriptively assess procedural pain and hemodynamic changes in an initial series of patients.MethodsIn this prospective pilot study, 10 consecutive adult patients undergoing elective diagnostic cerebral angiography via the radial artery received local anesthesia with a computer-controlled injection device using 1 mL of 3% mepivacaine delivered through a 33-G needle. The primary outcome of this study was feasibility, defined as successful delivery of local anesthesia and completion of transradial access without device-related complications or the need to convert to manual injection. Secondary outcomes included pain intensity assessed using a 0–100-mm visual analogue scale (VAS) at predefined procedural steps, hemodynamic changes before and after sheath insertion, and access-site adverse events such as vasospasm or bleeding. Blood pressure was compared between baseline (immediately before local anesthesia) and after sheath insertion.ResultsAll 10 procedures were successfully completed using the computer-controlled device without device malfunction, conversion to manual injection, or access failure. The first puncture attempt was successful in all patients. The median VAS score following local anesthesia was 18 [13–22] mm, following arterial puncture was 3 [2–4.75] mm, and following sheath insertion was 7.5 [3–10] mm. There was no significant difference in median systolic blood pressure before local anesthesia and after sheath insertion (160.7 ± 30.6 vs. 160.1 ± 27.6 mmHg; p = 0.84), and diastolic blood pressure also did not change significantly (80.5 ± 6.2 vs. 77.5 ± 10.0 mmHg; p = 0.16). No clinically evident radial artery spasm or hemorrhagic access-site complications occurred.ConclusionIn this pilot study, computer-controlled local anesthesia during transradial cerebral angiography was feasible and appeared safe in a small cohort of patients. Pain-related findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating, and further controlled studies are required to evaluate efficacy.

Introduction

The transradial approach (TRA) is widely adopted in cardiology and has been increasingly applied in neuroendovascular procedures.1,2) It is associated with fewer access-site complications, shorter procedure times, and higher patient satisfaction than the transfemoral approach.3) Recent studies have also supported its use in carotid artery stenting,4) mechanical thrombectomy,5,6) and aneurysm embolization.7)

Yet, despite its advantages, pain during radial artery puncture or sheath insertion is a major source of patient dissatisfaction and can induce vasospasms through sympathetic activation.8) These vasospasms can complicate sheath advancement and prolong procedure times. However, there is a paucity of research into methods of local anesthesia or pain control during diagnostic cerebral angiography via TRA.

Available pain reduction strategies include the use of fine needles and slow, constant rates of anesthetic injection.9) In practice, however, maintaining a constant rate of manual injection can be difficult. The manual use of fine needles is also problematic due to high injection resistance. Computer-controlled local anesthetic devices are commonly used in dentistry to deliver anesthetics at a slow, constant rate, reducing procedural pain.10,11)

We hypothesized that the use of such a device in cerebral angiography could improve patient comfort and procedural safety. This prospective pilot study evaluated the feasibility and safety of computer-controlled local anesthesia during TRA cerebral angiography and descriptively assessed pain and hemodynamic responses in an initial series of patients.

Materials and Methods

This prospective pilot study was conducted between January and September 2025. It was registered on June 5, 2025 (registration no.: jRCTs032250063; PAINLESS-TR study). Patients undergoing diagnostic cerebral angiography via TRA were consecutively enrolled and received local anesthesia using a computer-controlled injection device.

Inclusion criteria

To qualify for inclusion, patients were required to be aged 18 years or older, undergoing cerebral angiography with sheath placement in the radial artery, and conscious and alert. They must also have received a thorough explanation of the study, fully understood it, and provided written consent to participation.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they had a language disorder; a visual or upper limb disorder that would make evaluation by visual analogue scale (VAS; 0–100 mm) difficult; a sensory disorder involving central or peripheral nerves in the puncture-site area; or allergies to local anesthetics. Women who were or could be pregnant and women who were breastfeeding were not eligible for inclusion. Patients who had received investigational drugs or trial medications within 3 months before the start of the trial, as well as other patients deemed inappropriate as subjects by the principal or sub-investigator, were also excluded.

Data collection

Demographic and procedural data were prospectively collected for all participants. The data gathered included age, sex, and underlying disease. Pain intensity was evaluated using a VAS at 3 predefined time points: following local anesthesia, radial artery puncture with a 22-G needle, and sheath insertion. VAS scores were recorded immediately after each procedural step. Pain assessment was conducted by an independent evaluator positioned on the contralateral side of the operator, and the operator was not involved in the assessment. Patients indicated their pain level using the hand opposite to the puncture site. In this pilot analysis, VAS scores in the computer-controlled cohort were summarized descriptively to evaluate procedural tolerability. In the computer-controlled group, systolic (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were recorded immediately before local anesthesia and immediately after sheath insertion to monitor hemodynamic stability. We also documented the puncture site (conventional or distal radial approach) for each patient.

Interventions

All procedures were conducted by a single operator who was experienced with the computer-controlled injection device. All patients received local anesthesia using a computer-controlled injection device (Smart Mini; Shenzhen Soga Technology, Shenzhen, China). A 1.8-mL syringe cartridge containing 1 mL of 3% mepivacaine was mounted on the device, and the solution was delivered at a constant rate of 0.67 mL/min through a 33-G needle (Fig. 1A and 1B). After local anesthesia, we allowed a 2-min waiting period for anesthesia onset before proceeding to the next step.

Local anesthesia and arterial puncture were performed under ultrasound guidance. A 22-G needle was used for radial artery puncture. This was followed by the fluoroscopically guided insertion of a 0.025-inch, 80-cm guidewire and then a 4-Fr, 17-cm sheath (Medikit, Tokyo, Japan). Pain intensity was assessed at 3 procedural stages, as described in the Data collection subsection above. In the computer-controlled group, we also measured SBP and DBP immediately before local anesthesia and after sheath insertion to monitor the patient’s safety. Outcome measures

The primary outcome of this study was the feasibility of local anesthesia using a computer-controlled administration device in patients undergoing cerebral angiography with sheath placement in the radial artery. Feasibility was defined as successful delivery of local anesthetic and completion of radial access without device-related complications or the need to switch to manual injection. Secondary outcomes included pain intensity (VAS) during this procedure following local anesthesia, puncture, and sheath insertion; blood pressure changes before and after sheath insertion; and adverse events such as vasospasm, bleeding, or other unexpected complications. Blood pressure was measured at 5-min intervals from before local anesthesia until completion of the procedure. For analysis, the value immediately before local anesthesia was used as the baseline, and the measurement obtained at the first 5-min interval after sheath insertion was used as the post-procedural value. Radial artery spasm was assessed based on the absence of angiographic vasospasm after sheath insertion, as judged by the operator, and the absence of procedural difficulty during catheter manipulation. Pain outcomes were exploratory and were not intended for confirmatory hypothesis testing.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the ethics committee of the University of Tsukuba Hospital (approval no. TCRB25-003).

It was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Off-label use

The computer-controlled dental local anesthetic injection system was used off-label to administer local anesthesia during transradial cerebral angiography. The device is not approved for neuroangiography or neuroendovascular procedures.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables, including age, VAS scores, and blood pressure, were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges or means and standard deviations, as appropriate. Changes in SBP and DBP before local anesthesia and after sheath insertion were compared using Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test. Given the small sample size and single-arm design, analyses were descriptive and hypothesis-generating. P-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant for within-group comparisons. The sample size was not based on formal statistical calculation but was determined as an initial feasibility cohort for this pilot study.

Procedural success and safety

Local anesthesia and sheath insertion were successful in all patients. All procedures using the computer-controlled device were completed without device malfunction or the need to revert to manual injection. The first puncture attempt was successful in all cases. There was no significant difference in the SBP before local anesthesia and after sheath insertion (160.7 ± 30.6 vs. 160.1 ± 27.6 mmHg; p = 0.84). Similarly, DBP did not change significantly (80.5 ± 6.2 vs. 77.5 ± 10.0 mmHg; p = 0.16). After sheath insertion, no clinically evident radial artery spasm requiring additional pharmacologic treatment or causing procedural failure was observed on angiography. No hemorrhagic complications were observed in any of the patients.

Discussion

In this pilot study, computer-controlled local anesthesia with 3% mepivacaine was feasible and appeared safe during TRA cerebral angiography. In all 10 patients, radial access and diagnostic angiography were successfully performed using the device without device-related complications, hemodynamic instability, or clinically evident radial artery spasm, and VAS scores at each procedural step were generally within a tolerable range in this cohort.

Safety considerations

We observed no clinically relevant blood pressure changes or adverse events in any of the patients who received computer-controlled anesthesia. The Smart Mini device (Shenzhen Soga Technology) incorporates aspiration to help prevent intravascular injection. Additionally, all injections were performed under ultrasound guidance, supporting procedural safety. These findings indicate that, in this initial series of 10 patients, off-label use of the device for TRA cerebral angiography was technically feasible and did not raise immediate safety concerns.

Clinical implications

Although this single-arm pilot study was not powered for definitive evaluation of analgesic efficacy, the low VAS scores observed suggest that computer-controlled local anesthesia may be well tolerated in TRA cerebral angiography. The potential mechanisms for pain reduction with this technique are likely related to a combination of factors. The use of a computer-controlled, slow, constant infusion together with a fine 33-G needle has been reported in other fields to lessen injection pain.9) Mechanistically, a slower, steady-rate injection may reduce tissue distension and nociceptor stimulation, thereby attenuating nociceptive input.12) This may also reduce acute sympathetic activation during arterial puncture, thereby potentially improving patient comfort and reducing vascular reactivity. By minimizing nociceptive stimulation, this approach could theoretically reduce sympathetic activation and the risk of radial artery spasm, although our study did not include an objective quantitative assessment of vasospasm. These mechanisms are hypothetical and should be interpreted as plausible explanations rather than direct evidence from the present study.

Previous reports have described the use of topical anesthetic agents, such as lidocaine patches, lidocaine ointments, and jet injection of lidocaine, during transradial coronary angiography.13–16) However, the evidence levels have been insufficient, and these methods have not become standard. They also require advance preparation, unlike the computer-controlled local anesthetic device described in the present study, which can be used immediately and conveniently.

In this single-arm pilot study, analgesic efficacy could not be definitively established; however, the pain scores recorded in this cohort and the stable hemodynamics observed are consistent with the proposed mechanisms of computer-controlled local anesthetic delivery reported in other fields. Pain following local anesthesia or arterial puncture can activate the sympathetic nervous system and induce radial artery vasoconstriction, potentially leading to radial artery spasm and more difficult sheath insertion.17) These sympathetic surges can precipitate radial artery spasm, making sheath insertion more difficult and increasing the risk of access-site complications. By minimizing nociceptive stimulation through slow, constant-rate injection and the use of a fine needle, computer-controlled anesthesia may help attenuate sympathetic activation and maintain hemodynamic stability, although these effects were not directly evaluated in this study. Previous randomized trials and systematic reviews in dental practice have shown that such systems reduce subjective pain and improve patient comfort,11) supporting the plausibility of these mechanisms and suggesting potential applicability beyond dentistry. In this study, the anesthetic protocol was partly determined by device-specific constraints. Mepivacaine was selected due to compatibility with the cartridge-type delivery system, and a 33-G needle was used as the finest compatible option to minimize injection-related discomfort. Both mepivacaine and lidocaine are amide-type local anesthetics that block voltage-gated sodium channels and have similar clinical profiles. A waiting period of approximately 2 min was allowed to ensure the onset of anesthesia, based on the known rapid onset of amide-type local anesthetics.18) However, these device-dependent factors may limit the generalizability of the findings to other anesthetic agents or delivery methods.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, it was a single-center pilot study with a very small sample size, and the findings should be interpreted as preliminary. In addition, all procedures were performed by a single experienced operator, and most cases involved distal radial access; therefore, the generalizability of these findings to other operators, institutions, and access approaches may be limited. Second, the study used a single-arm design without a contemporaneous control group; therefore, no causal inference can be made regarding the analgesic efficacy of the device, and pain-related findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating. Finally, we performed no objective quantitative assessments of radial artery vasospasm or diameter changes, and vasospasm-related observations should therefore be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating only.

Future directions

To definitively determine the benefits of computer-controlled local anesthetic delivery in TRA cerebral angiography, future studies should be designed as randomized controlled trials with standardized anesthetic agents, needle size, and access site. Objective measures of radial artery vasospasm and comprehensive assessment of patient-reported outcomes will be important to clarify the clinical impact of this approach.

Conclusion

In this pilot study, the delivery of 3% mepivacaine local anesthetic using a computer-controlled injection device during transradial cerebral angiography was shown to be safe and feasible in a small cohort of patients. All procedures using the device were completed without device-related complications, hemodynamic instability, or clinically evident radial artery spasm. This approach may represent a potential alternative to conventional methods. However, because of the single-arm design and small sample size, our findings should be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, and larger randomized trials are needed to confirm the potential benefits of computer-controlled local anesthesia in this setting.
