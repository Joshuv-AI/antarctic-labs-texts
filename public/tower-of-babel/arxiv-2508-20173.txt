A Tale of Tails: Star Formation and Stripping in Jellyfish Galaxies in the Strong Lensing Cluster MACS J0138.0-2155 Preprint: APS/123-QED
Catherine C. Gibson, Jackson H. O’Donnell, Tesla E. Jeltema

Abstract
We investigate the effects of ram-pressure stripping on four galaxies within the massive, strong-lensing cluster MACS-J0138.0-2155 ( z = 0.336 z=0.336 ). Of these, three are classified as jellyfish galaxies, with significant elongated tails. Two of these jellyfish galaxies, J1 and J2, are in a late-stage of stripping and show post-starburst features within their disk regions with star formation only in the tails. Using VLT/MUSE integral field spectroscopic data, we spatially resolve the stellar and gas kinematics to examine extraplanar gas associated with ram-pressure stripping. We complement this analysis with optical and near-infrared imaging from the Hubble Space Telescope to visualize the galactic structure of each member. The jellyfish galaxies are all blue-shifted with respect to the cluster and show velocity gradients of a few hundred kms − 1 \mathrm{kms}^{-1} across their tails. From the resolved gas kinematics, we derive H α \alpha -based star formation rates; these are generally low reaching a maximum of approximately 0.49 M ⊙ ​ \text ​ yr − 1 ​ kpc − 2 \mathrm{M_{\odot}\text{yr}^{-1}kpc^{-2}} in galaxy J3. We also report the kinematics for galaxy J4, which lies in the foreground of the cluster but close in projection to one of the lensed arcs.



1
Introduction

It has long been known observationally that in the present epoch galaxies in dense environments like clusters of galaxies are different than those in under-dense environments, generally lacking recent star formation and having elliptical morphologies, so-called “red and dead” galaxies
(
Peng et al. 2010
;
McPartland et al. 2016
)
. While this difference can be partially explained by an earlier formation time for cluster galaxies, there are also environmental processes that can act to transform galaxies. For example, galaxy clusters provide the extreme environments necessary for ram-pressure stripping of cluster members, offering insight into the evolution and morphology of their member galaxies. Ram-pressure stripping (RPS)
(
Gunn and Gott 1972
)
is caused by interactions between the intra-cluster medium (ICM) and the interstellar medium (ISM) of a galaxy that disrupt the hydrodynamic equilibrium, stripping away gas and dust from the outer layers of the galactic disk.
This physical process is believed to generate the tentacle-like structure characteristic of jellyfish galaxies, a term coined to the best of our knowledge by
Bekki 2009
.

Recent studies of galaxies within massive clusters at
z>0.2
have revealed populations of jellyfish galaxies - cluster members exhibiting dramatic gas tails and morphological distortions - offering compelling evidence for active ram-pressure stripping in dense environments
(
Ebeling et al. 2014
;
McPartland et al. 2016
;
Lee et al. 2022
)
. Evidence for RPS in cluster galaxies is derived from the presence of extraplanar ionized gas, a general lack of neutral hydrogen, and by contrasting the stellar and gas kinematics
(
Poggianti et al. 2025
)
.

Star formation rates above the star-forming main sequence point to triggering mechanisms beyond gravitational interactions like tidal stripping or mergers, potentially indicating that RPS is at play
(
Cunha et al. 2015
;
Poggianti et al. 2016
)
. RPS can drive rapid evolution in cluster galaxy members by locally enhancing and quenching star formation. This process is particularly prominent in jellyfish galaxies. Observationally, it has been shown that jellyfish galaxies exhibit extended tails of ionized gas that often host in-situ star forming regions
(
Fumagalli et al. 2014
;
Cortese et al. 2007
)
.

Star formation quenching in jellyfish galaxies is linked to post-starburst galaxies because of their swift transition (in the last 1.5 Gyr) from active star formation to quiescence, likely caused by RPS. Post-starburst galaxies, often referred to as K+A galaxies, due to their spectral features resembling a combination of K-type stars and A-type stars, exhibit strong Balmer absorption lines and a lack of emission lines in their spectra
(
Dressler and Gunn 1982
)
. Studies of clusters using MUSE data have identified post-starburst signatures in galaxies undergoing RPS
(
Gullieuszik et al. 2017
;
Werle et al. 2022
)
.

While optical imaging like multi-band HST reveals the structural features of jellyfish galaxies, IFU data is effective in resolving the complex kinematics and physical conditions necessary for identifying star-forming regions, and the necessary conditions for accurate classification of jellyfish galaxies. Spatially resolved H
\alpha
maps from IFU surveys like GASP
(
Poggianti et al. 2017
;
Bellhouse et al. 2020
;
Vulcani et al. 2018
)
reveal active star formation occurring directly within the stripped material of jellyfish galaxies.

In this paper, we investigate the impact of ram-pressure stripping (RPS) on star formation in four member galaxies of the strong-lensing cluster MACS J0138.0–2155. To the best of our knowledge, three of these galaxies were first informally labeled P1, P2, and P3 by
Rodney et al. 2021
, who incorporated them in the lens modeling. We identify three of the four galaxies as jellyfish galaxy candidates, two of which exhibit characteristics of post-starburst galaxies. While the evolutionary stages of RPS are not precisely defined, we apply the morphological classification scheme proposed by
Poggianti et al. 2025
to interpret their stripping stages. Using integral field spectroscopy, we analyze ionized gas kinematics traced by emission lines and spatially resolve regions of active star formation. In section
2
we will describe the data and sample used in this analysis, as well as background on the MACS J0138.0-2155 cluster, in section
3
we will describe the methodology used to fit the spectra and derive star formation rates, and in section
4
and section
5
we will interpret the spatially resolved kinematics and fits for each galaxy.

3
Analysis

3.1
Binning

In order to accumulate sufficient statistics for spectral analysis, we bin the MUSE data cube into 2D spatial regions based on signal-to-noise. Specifically, we use the Python package
VorBin
, an adaptive spatial binning method
(
Cappellari and Copin 2003
)
. To generate spatial bins within our desired analysis regions, we provide
VorBin
with a target signal-to-noise in either the H
\alpha+
[N II] emission doublet or the H
\beta
absorption line. Prior to applying the
VorBin
routine, we estimate a target S/N for each selected region for binning. To bin by the H
\alpha
emission or H
\beta
absorption, we first estimate the line fluxes with the following continuum subtraction procedure. For jellyfish galaxies that exhibit strong H
\alpha+
[N II] emission, we define the signal region as the rest-frame wavelength range associated with the doublet, from 6534-6590 Å​, relative to the stellar continuum. Fig.
2
illustrates the S/N calculation used for Voronoi binning in the tail of the J1 galaxy. The continuum used spans 6478-6534 Å on the left side of the H
\alpha
signal and 6590-6618 Å on the right side. The S/N is then scaled for the disproportionate continuum selection on either side of the signal. We apply binning based on the H
\alpha+
[N II] doublet to J3, J4, and the tails of J1 and J2. The chosen signal and continuum regions remain consistent across these galaxies in the rest-frame wavelength range.

The heads of J1 and J2 lack significant emission, and are therefore binned with respect to the signal of the H
\beta
absorption line in the rest-frame wavelength range from 4846-4884 Å. We chose equal regions on either side of the absorption feature that span 25 Å each for the continuum.

The Voronoi bins used for analysis as well as the S/N used for binning in each galaxy can be seen in Fig.
3
and Fig.
4
. The target S/N for our Voronoi bins ranged from 25-70 with the low end covering the absorption-dominated heads of J1 and J2.

3.2
Spectral Fitting using pPXF

To fit the spectra of each Voronoi bin, we used the penalized pixel fitting (
pPXF
) method
(
Cappellari and Emsellem 2004
;
Cappellari 2017
;
Cappellari 2023
)
. This method fits an observed spectrum using a linear combination of gas and stellar templates convolved with a line-of-sight velocity distribution (LOSVD), allowing us to extract stellar and gas kinematics. To ensure accurate velocity measurements, we provided an initial redshift from
Marz
, which
pPXF
refined by computing velocity offsets for each Voronoi bin. We supplied
pPXF
with stellar templates from XSL DR3, the stellar spectral library from the X-Shooter spectrograph on the Very Large Telescope
(
Vernet et al. 2011
;
Verro et al. 2022
)
. For all stellar templates, we set the signal-to-noise threshold per spaxel to 3. Gas emission lines were modeled using Gaussian templates, each with its own LOSVD. We also have the option to define masked regions as well as multiplicative or additive polynomials to fit the continuum rather than drawing from a stellar spectral library. For our analysis, we considered templates in the wavelength range 3500-6900 Å with a normalization range of 5400-5500 Å. In some cases, we adjust the start to 3600 Å and the normalization range from 5300-5400 Å, depending on the redshift of the galaxy and the noise features present in the continuum. We mask out the portion of the spectrum that was removed due to contamination from the GLAO laser star guide system in the observed wavelength range from 5800-5965 Å in all galaxies. Additionally, we select varying wavelength ranges to mask out, depending on noise features in the continuum per galaxy.

3.2.1
Emission Line Fitting and Absorption Features

In this analysis, we use two kinematic components to fit the observed spectra. The stellar kinematic component models the continuum and captures the absorption line features while the gas kinematic component is used to capture the gas emission lines. We focus on fitting the H
\alpha
(6563 Å), H
\beta
(4861 Å), and [NII] (6548 Å & 6584 Å) emission lines. To avoid overfitting these lines, we fix the stellar continuum using an optimal stellar template derived from the combined spectrum of all spaxels, which is then applied uniformly across all Voronoi bins of each galaxy. The gas emission lines are modeled independently using Gaussian templates. In the regions where the stellar absorption features are weak or absent, we omit the stellar template and instead model the continuum using a simple polynomial background. In the regions lacking emission, we focus on the Balmer absorption lines. In these cases, we opt to omit the gas templates and fit the spectrum with an optimal stellar template only.

3.3
SFRs

To derive the star formation rates, we use the luminosity of the H
\alpha
emission line corrected for stellar absorption, foreground extinction, and internal dust extinction. Stellar absorption corrections are already accounted for by
pPXF
in the fitting process. To correct for dust extinction, we utilize the Balmer decrement, the absorption-corrected flux ratio of the H
\alpha
and H
\beta
lines. We adopt an intrinsic H
\alpha
to H
\beta
ratio of 2.86 and adopt the
Cardelli et al. 1989
extinction law. The extinction magnitude of the flux of H
\alpha
is given by

A_{H_{\alpha}}=k_{H_{\alpha}}\times E(B-V),

(1)

where
k_{H_{\alpha}}
is the extinction coefficient which we will adopt as 2.53 from
Cardelli et al. 1989
and
E(B-V)
is the color excess which can be found from the following equation,

E(B-V)=2.33\times\log\bigg[\frac{S_{H_{\alpha}}/S_{H_{\beta}}}{2.86}\bigg]

(2)

where
S_{H_{\alpha}}/S_{H_{\beta}}
is the absorption-corrected flux ratio. To correct for foreground Galactic extinction, we used the NASA/IPAC Extragalactic Database
NED Extinction Calculator
, which applies recalibrated dust maps from
Schlafly and Finkbeiner 2011
based on the original work of
Schlegel et al. 1998
. We select the SDSS z-filter for 0.8923
\mu
m with the galactic extinction of 0.020 mag. Using the absorption, dust, and foreground corrected H
\alpha
flux, we can now find the star formation rates using the following relation given by
Kennicutt 1998
,

\text{SFR}_{H_{\alpha}}=4.6\times 10^{-42}L_{H_{\alpha}},

(3)

where
\text{SFR}_{H_{\alpha}}
is in units of
M_{\odot}\text{yr}^{-1}
, and
L_{H_{\alpha}}
is the corrected H
\alpha
luminosity. We report all SFRs in units of
\mathrm{M_{\odot}\text{yr}^{-1}kpc^{-2}}
, such that the calculated star formation rates are normalized by the number of pixels per Voronoi bin and pixel area. We adopt the IMF given by
Chabrier 2003
for the SFR calculations and assume a flat
\Lambda
CDM cosmology of
H_{0}=70\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
,
\Omega_{M}=0.3
and
T_{\text{CMB}}=2.725\mathrm{K}
for conversion from H
\alpha
flux to luminosity, and pixel area to
\mathrm{kpc^{2}}
.

4
Results

In this section, we summarize the results for each individual jellyfish galaxy. Average redshifts and stellar velocity dispersions for each galaxy are given in Table
1
. All four galaxies are blue shifted relative to the BCG. J4 appears to be in the foreground of the cluster, while J1-3 are consistent with infalling into the cluster. As discussed below, we see additional velocity gradients across the tails of these three jellyfish galaxies. As previously mentioned, the heads and tails of J1 and J2 are fit separately, because emission-line features were found to be exclusively present in the tails of these two galaxies.

4.1
J1

The J1 galaxy exhibits a characteristic jellyfish morphology, with a distinct head and an ionized, stripped tail, clearly visible in the HST imaging. J1 is approximately 42 kpc from the BCG. We measure the width of the head of J1 to be approximately 5 kpc and the tail to be approximately 21 kpc in length. The extension of the tail far beyond the width of the head, along with the differing spectral features between the two regions, motivated us to separate J1 into two spatial components for binning and analysis: an absorption-dominated head and an emission-dominated tail. Independent initial redshifts for the head and tail were found using
Marz
, and
pPXF
was then utilized to determine the relative velocity offsets within each Voronoi bin in the fitting process. The regions used for our fits of J1 can be found in the leftmost panel of Fig.
3
.

4.1.1
J1 “Head” Fits and Kinematics

The head of the J1 galaxy was Voronoi binned using the signal strength of the H
\beta
absorption line at a target signal-to-noise of 25. We fit the head with an initial redshift of
z=0.3310
and set the additive Legendre polynomial used to correct the template continuum for any unfitted background flux to degree 6. All bins were fitted with stellar templates only. The full
pPXF
fit of all Voronoi bins in the J1 head can be found in Fig.
5
. The spectral fit of the J1 head shows strong Balmer absorption and nearly no emission, indicative of recent quenching. We do not find any star formation in the head of J1, and attribute this behavior to post-starburst activity. We also present the stellar kinematics for the head bins in Fig.
6
, which appear to be relatively undisturbed. Although the tail is fit separately, we report the stellar kinematics for the tail bins near the head that showed absorption in Fig.
6
, and transform the velocity offsets for the included tail bins to the reference frame of the head. The velocity offset is on the order of approximately
-50\ \mathrm{kms^{-1}}
relative to the initial redshift with a slight gradient across the head potentially indicating rotation. The velocity dispersion varies slightly across the region, ranging from
\sim 25
-
45\ \mathrm{kms^{-1}}
. The tail bins shown here show a gradient in stellar velocity offset similar to what is seen in the tail gas kinematics, described below, and a typically larger stellar velocity dispersion than seen in the head.

J1 “Tail” Fits and Kinematics

The tail of the J1 galaxy was binned with respect to the H

4.2
J2

J2 has a similar structure to J1, with its head spanning 4 kpc and its tail extending 16 kpc. J2 is approximately 15 kpc from the BCG, the closest galaxy in projection to the BCG in our analysis and the most prone to contamination. We choose to separate J2 into an absorption-dominant head and an emission-dominant tail for analysis. We bin these regions as described below, and fit the head and the tail starting at their individual redshifts derived from
Marz
. In this galaxy, there are two bins in the tail, closest to the head that show absorption. Emission is again limited to the tail. After inspection of the preliminary fits in the Voronoi bins of both the head and tail, we remove one region with an absorption-dominated spectrum from the tail and add it to the head, and we include one of the tail-fitted bins which shows both absorption and emission in the results shown for the head.

4.2.1
J2 “Head” Fits and Kinematics

The head of J2 was Voronoi binned with respect to the H
\beta
absorption line with a target S/N of 25, and fitted at an initial redshift of 0.3308. We set the additive Legendre polynomial to degree 6. The gas templates were not included for this region of the J2 galaxy due to lack of emission. In Fig.
3
, one bin in the tail region is marked in green, the same color as the head bins. This specific bin in the tail contained primarily absorption features, so we opted to include this bin in the fit of the head and remove it from the tail analysis. We also include the bin adjacent to it, on the right side, which shows a mix of absorption and emission; this bin remains included in the tail fits. The combined spectral fit of the J2 head can be seen in Fig.
9
, where we again see strong Balmer absorption lines. On the redder side of the spectrum, between around rest frame
\mathrm{0.50\ \mu m}
-
\mathrm{0.62\ \mu m}
, the fit deviates slightly from the observed spectrum with some absorption features offset from the fit, indicating that multiple stellar populations may be present. This galaxy lies very close in projection to the BCG, and these features are consistent with the BCG redshift. In Fig.
10
we see fairly undisturbed stellar kinematics, and a slight velocity gradient across the galaxy again potentially indicating rotation. The two tail bins included in these fits have velocities similar to the adjacent bins of the head and have a lower velocity dispersion by about
50\ \mathrm{kms^{-1}}
.

4.2.2
J2 “Tail” Fits and Kinematics

The tail of the J2 galaxy was binned at a target S/N of 45 and fitted at a starting redshift of
z=0.3317

4.5
BPT Diagram

We present the BPT diagram ([OIII]
\mathrm{\lambda}
5007/H
\mathrm{\beta}
versus [NII]
\mathrm{\lambda}
6584/H
\mathrm{\alpha}
)
(
Baldwin et al. 1981
)
for all Voronoi bins containing emission features (in the cases of J1 and J2, these only include the tail bins) in Fig.
18
. We define star-forming, composite, AGN, and LINER regions, separated by the classification lines defined by
Kewley et al. 2001
,
Kauffmann et al. 2003
, and
Schawinski et al. 2007
.
J2 and J4 fall entirely within the purely star-forming region, while some J1 bins extend into the composite region. The composite region suggests mixed ionization sources, which may be consistent with the complex environment of ram-pressure stripping where shock heating has the potential to boost emission. J3 lies mostly within the composite region with a few bins progressing into the LINER region. To test whether AGN activity drives the extended LINER emission, we examined the spatial distribution of LINER-classified bins across the J3 galaxy. AGN-powered ionization typically produces emission that is strongly concentrated toward the galactic center. However, we find that LINER bins are distributed throughout J3, including regions in the galaxy’s outskirts, with no clear radial concentration around the nucleus. Evidence for shock excitation responsible for extended LINER emission has been shown in galactic gas outflows by
Rich et al. 2010
in NGC 839, which we consider as a possible explanation for the extended emission we observe for J3 in Fig.
18
. We also point to the gas velocity dispersion values in the middle panel of Fig.
13
as potential evidence for shock ionization, as gas velocity dispersions on the order of
\sim
100
\mathrm{kms^{-1}}
, as opposed to lower
\sim
40
\mathrm{kms^{-1}}
for purely star forming, have been shown to indicate non-nuclear ionization sources
(
Rich et al. 2010
;
Rich et al. 2011
;
Epinat et al. 2010
;
Ho et al. 2014
)
; however, this alone is not enough to definitively categorize shock ionization. Further analysis combining BPT emission line ratios with velocity dispersion and spatial information in 3D space would allow us to better quantify the separation between star formation, AGN, and shock contributions
(
D’Agostino et al. 2019
)
. Additionally, other emission line diagnostics like [Fe II]/Pa
\mathrm{\beta}
could aid in classifying emission from supernova remnants or shocks
(
Alonso-Herrero et al. 2000
)
.
Bellhouse et al. 2019
used spatially resolved spectroscopy of the jellyfish galaxy JO201 to correlate the ionization state of gas (as traced by BPT line ratios) with physical location, allowing them to identify distinct ionization mechanisms across different galactic regions. A similar spatial-spectroscopic analysis would be valuable for our data if we had sufficient Voronoi bins to resolve this behavior. We conclude that star formation is the dominant ionization source in all galaxies, although shocks resulting from RPS may contribute, especially in the case of J3.

5
Discussion

In J1, J2, and J3, we observe asymmetric morphologies in the near-infrared and optical HST imaging consistent with the visual structure of jellyfish galaxies described by
Ebeling et al. 2014
. We observe a characteristic stripped tail of ionized gas that extends away from the primary galactic disk. The tail length as well as the spectral features present in each galaxy allow us to classify the stripping stage for each galaxy as described in
Poggianti et al. 2025
. The kinematics and H
\alpha
emission in these galaxies are consistent with the characteristics of jellyfish galaxies found by
Poggianti et al. 2016
.

The spectra of the heads of J1 and J2 are dominated by strong Balmer absorption lines, as shown in Fig.
5
and Fig.
9
, suggesting A-type stellar populations and point toward quenching within
\sim
1 Gyr
(
Poggianti et al. 2004
)
. The presence of strong Balmer absorption in two of the tail bins in J2 indicates that this galaxy may be on its way to full quenching, or may be losing stars due to stripping processes. In Fig.
7
and Fig.
, we see the ionized tails of J1 and J2 have strong H
\alpha
emission, and in the rightmost panel of Fig.
8
, the J1 tail exhibits a strong in-situ blue star-forming knot. In the classification scheme set by
Poggianti et al. 2025
, these galaxies might be considered Jtype=2 jellyfish, by the definition that their gas tails are at least as long as the stellar disk, as shown in the HST imaging, although this stage is typically associated with some ionized gas in the head that is not present in the heads of J1 or J2. These two galaxies are clear examples of jellyfish galaxies undergoing ram-pressure stripping. We classify the heads of J1 and J2 as post-starburst galaxies, and attribute the quenching of star formation to ram-pressure stripping, as an obvious ionized tail of stripped gas from these heads is present. Both galaxies also show peculiar velocity gradients across the tail, particularly clear in J1.

The J3 jellyfish galaxy is in a much earlier stage of the stripping process as evident by its visual structure, and combination of absorption and emission features present. The extended tail does cover approximately as much area as the head, although a clear distinction between the head and tail is difficult. We consider this galaxy to be a Jtype=1 or a Jtype=2 jellyfish, where significant stripping is present and the tail seems to cover the length of the head, but we still cannot make a drastic distinction between the head and tail
(
Poggianti et al. 2025
)
. The rightmost panel of Fig.
13
reveals a spatially resolved star-forming knot, although this knot seems to be central in the galaxy in contrast with jellyfish in a later stage which tend to have knots located in their tails.

All four galaxies in this analysis exhibit relatively undisturbed stellar kinematics, a prerequisite for recognizing jellyfish galaxies
(
Poggianti et al. 2025
)
. We find a gas velocity dispersion,
\sigma_{gas}
in star-forming regions of approximately
\lesssim 100

\mathrm{kms^{-1}}
, consistent with the kinematics for ESO137-001, a spiral galaxy undergoing RPS infalling to the Norma Cluster
(
Fumagalli et al. 2014
)
. Findings from GASP jellyfish galaxy studies, interpret a lower gas velocity dispersion relative to the stellar velocity dispersion in the regions of star formation as evidence that the ionized gas is dynamically cold - creating favorable conditions for in-situ star formation triggered by ram pressure stripping
(
Poggianti et al. 2019
;
Gullieuszik et al. 2020
)
. In J3 and J4 where we model both stellar and gas kinematics, we cannot confirm this result but find that our stellar velocity dispersion and gas velocity dispersion in the spatial regions of star formation are comparable.

In the leftmost panel of Fig.
8
, we see a clear velocity gradient across the tail of J1 extending away from the cluster center. A similar gradient is observed in both the tails of J2 and in J3 in Fig.
11
and Fig.
13
, respectively. This velocity offset gradient is consistent with post-pericenter passage from the BCG on primarily radial orbits
(
Salinas et al. 2024a
;
Smith et al. 2022
;
Jaffé et al. 2018
)
. In J3, the tail appears to extend slightly perpendicular from the cluster center, but further analysis is required to determine the angle between the tail and cluster center
(
Salinas et al. 2024a
)
.

It is unclear whether J4 can be characterized as a jellyfish galaxy due to low signal; however, the gas kinematics shown in Fig.
16
reveal J4 appears to be in the foreground of this cluster. The stellar and gas kinematics of J4 as well as the jellyfish galaxies have the potential to inform models of the lensing arcs within MACSJ0138 due to their proximity the the lensing features.

6
Conclusion

We present an observational study of four galaxies within the strong-lensing MACS J1038.0-2155 cluster at
z=0.336
utilizing IFU data from MUSE as well as HST imaging in the optical and near-infrared bands. We use adaptive spatial binning based on the signal strength of H
\alpha+
N[II] emission or the H
\beta
absorption lines depending on the spectrum of the galaxies. To fit the spectra, we use
pPXF
and extract the stellar and gas kinematics for each galaxy. The main results of our analysis include:

[1]

We detect strong Balmer absorption lines in the heads of J1 and J2, suggestive of recent quenching. We classify these as post-starburst galaxies.

[2]

We observe gas velocity dispersions in the regions of star formation in all galaxies on the order of
\lesssim 100

\mathrm{kms^{-1}}
.
We also report stellar velocity dispersions for each galaxy.

[3]

We find prevalent star formation in the center of J3, reaching 0.49
\mathrm{M_{\odot}\text{yr}^{-1}kpc^{-2}}
and star formation constrained to the tails of J1 and J2 on the order of 0.10
\mathrm{M_{\odot}\text{yr}^{-1}kpc^{-2}}
and 0.010
\mathrm{M_{\odot}\text{yr}^{-1}kpc^{-2}}
, respectively. We also find star formation activity in J4 on the order of 0.01
\mathrm{M_{\odot}\text{yr}^{-1}kpc^{-2}}
, but cannot distinguish a characteristic jellyfish structure associated with the star formation.

[4]

The velocity offset across the J1, J2, and J3 galaxies indicates they are in post-pericenter passage from the BCG, and occupy approximately radial orbits, consistent with RPS. J4 is in the foreground of the cluster.

[5]

We present the BPT diagram for [OIII]
\mathrm{\lambda}
5007/H
\mathrm{\beta}
versus [NII]
\mathrm{\lambda}
6584/H
\mathrm{\alpha}
for all Voronoi bins in the tails of J1 and J2 and for J3 and J4. All galaxies fall primarily within purely star-forming or composite regions indicating mostly stellar ionization is present.

[6]

We conclude J1-3 are jellyfish galaxies with tails suggestive of ram-pressure stripping, with J1 and J2 in a later stage of stripping compared to J3.

In future work, we would like to investigate X-ray data on this system to deepen our understanding of the interaction between the galaxy members and the cluster background in the process of ram-pressure-stripping.
