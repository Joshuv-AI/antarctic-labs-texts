Medial Neovascularization: A Novel Mechanism of Aortic Dissection in a Hypertensive Population?
Authors: Yong Lin, Xiao‐chai Lv, Zi‐wen Cai, Yue Shen, Mei‐fang Chen, Lin‐feng Xie, Zhao‐feng Zhang, Zhi‐huang Qiu, Yu‐mei Li, Liang‐wan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to elucidate the impact of vasa vasorum on the natural progression of aortic dissection in patients with hypertension.MethodsFull‐thickness ascending aortic walls from 43 participants (median age, 53 years; 32.6% women) were analyzed using pathological observation and shear stress measurement. Comparisons among hypertensive dissection (AD, n=23), hypertensive control (n=12), and nonhypertensive dissection (non–HT‐AD, n=8) groups were performed using Kruskal–Wallis and repeated‐measures ANOVA tests.ResultsCompared with the control group, a higher density of vasa vasorum was observed within the outer third of the tunica media (medial neovascularization [MN]) in the AD group (adjusted P=0.02). In the AD group, MN frequently coincided with the site of medial tearing, and the MN grade was higher than in the other 2 groups (overall: P=0.01; non–HT‐AD versus AD group: adjusted P=0.05; control versus AD group: adjusted P=0.05). Medial degeneration was observed in all of the groups. Compared with the non–HT‐AD group, the medial degeneration grade was even lower in the AD group (adjusted P=0.006). Multicenter fractures of elastic and collagen fibers were evident within the MN region in the AD group. Biomechanical testing revealed a significantly increasing translamellar discrepancy in stretch ranges inside the similar region (P<0.001 for interaction; P column factor<0.001) in the AD group when compared with the control group.ConclusionsMN and subsequent vasa vasorum rupture in the outer tunica media may represent an alternative initiating event in hypertension‐related aortic dissection, distinct from the classical intimal tear. However, the cross‐sectional nature of this study precludes definitive causal conclusions.

Aortic dissection (AD) is a rare but life‐threatening disease with an incidence of 2.6 to 3.5 cases per 100 000 per year and an extremely high mortality rate of 14% to 18% even after timely treatment. 1 Systemic hypertension has been reported as a definite risk factor of AD and is detected in 74.7% to 80.9% of confirmed AD cases. 2 However, only a few patients with hypertension develop this catastrophic disease. 3 , 4 According to a large sample study from IRAD (International Registry of Acute Aortic Dissection), 41.1% of patients with acute AD had a moderately or mildly elevated blood pressure, which was measured at the time of symptom onset, and only 27.4% of patients with a systolic blood pressure >150 mm Hg were observed. 5 In other words, the absolute value of systolic blood pressure may not be significantly associated with the occurrence of AD. 4 Until now, detection of this clinical phenomenon has been limited, and there must be some factors other than hypertension that are responsible for the structural alterations in the wall of the aorta.

Vasa vasorum (VV) is often referred to as the “vessels of the vessel,” and it has been implicated in various cardiovascular diseases, including aortic aneurysm, atherosclerosis, and postoperative vein graft re‐stenosis. 6 Recently, an old theory known as “VV rupture,” proposed by Gore in 1952, 7 has regained attention. Previous clinicopathological analyses of AD have indicated that 95.2% of patients develop dissection in the outer layer of the media. Furthermore, 33.3% of patients were confirmed to have small sites of erythrocyte extravasation, which were suspected to originate from the rupture of medial VV, whereas 76.2% exhibited large hematomas with thrombus formation due to erythrocyte extravasation. However, the underlying mechanism of medial VV tearing is yet to be elucidated. 8 It has been confirmed that the outer layer of the aorta becomes less elastic after the removal of adventitial VV, whereas the inner layer remains relatively healthy and shows full elasticity. 9 We hypothesized that the discrepancy in stress‐resistance capacity between the outer and inner layers of the aorta contributes to increased translamellar shear stress inside the tunica media, which is aggravated by hypertensive crises, resulting in VV rupture and AD development.

Digital image correlation (DIC) is a technique that can measure surface strains of an object under load using a high‐precision camera. 10 Currently, DIC is widely used in measuring the deformation and strain of biological tissues, providing a powerful technique for detecting subtle displacements. 11 In this study, the authors collected full‐thickness aortic tissues from hypertensive patients with AD or from heart transplantation (HT) donors with hypertension, analyzed their clinical characteristics, and performed histological staining. Finally, DIC was applied to measure the discrepancy in tissue strain among the medial layers, which is closely related to the translamellar shear stress in the tunica media during a stretch test, and to explore the mechanisms of VV rupture and medial tearing. It will provide new pathological and biomechanical insights that may inform future investigations into the mechanisms and potential intervention targets for AD in hypertensive patients. It is important to note that the present cross‐sectional study design allows for the identification of associations but cannot establish causality or temporal sequence.

METHODS

Human Tissue and Clinical Data

The study was approved by the ethics committee of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki on March 30, 2018. All participants provided written informed consent (2018KJT091). The data, analytic methods, and study materials that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. This observational study was not preregistered with a formal registry. The analyses presented should be considered exploratory and hypothesis‐generating. The specimens were obtained from patients with acute type A AD and HT donors with hypertension within 5 minutes after excision. The initial cohort of aortic wall samples for histopathological analysis was collected between March 31, 2023, and August 9, 2023. Following initial peer review, to strengthen the biomechanical component of the study, we expanded the sample size for the DIC analysis. Accordingly, the collection of specimens meeting the criteria for DIC testing was extended until December 21, 2025. Vascular tissues were collected from the redundant ascending aortas, which were excised during surgery. The specimens from patients with AD and hypertension were categorized into AD and nonhypertensive AD (non–HT‐AD), defined as those patients with AD without hypertension. HT donors with hypertension were classified into the control group. Patients with: (1) connective tissue disorders, such as Marfan syndrome; (2) bicuspid aortic valve; (3) aortic aneurysm; (4) traumatic or iatrogenic dissection; (5) arteritis; and (6) age younger than 18 years were excluded from this study. Review of donor clinical records confirmed no known aortic pathology (eg, aortic aneurysm, dissection, or significant atherosclerosis) before transplantation. Patient demographic data, including age, sex, body mass index, history of diabetes, and aortic valve function, were recorded.

Histological and Immunohistochemical Staining

Specimens with full‐thickness aortic wall were rinsed at least 5 times with precooled normal saline and then immersed in 10% buffered formalin at room temperature. The fixed tissue samples were paraffin‐embedded, sectioned continuously at 3 to 4 μm with a microtome, mounted on glass slides, and subjected to histochemical and immunohistochemical analyses. Thereafter, hematoxylin and eosin staining, immunohistochemistry, EVG‐Weigert staining, and Masson staining were performed. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed to determine vessel morphology, and immunohistochemistry was conducted using rat monoclonal anti‐CD31 antibody (ab281583, Abcam, 1:4000 dilution) following recommended protocols. The secondary antibody was then added, and 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine was used for development according to the manufacturer's instructions. Collagen fiber and elastic fiber staining kits (G1591, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd) were used for the staining of the elastic fiber (EVG‐Weigert Method), and collagen fiber and Masson trichrome staining were performed using a modified Masson trichrome stain kit (G1346, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) to distinguish between the collagen fibers and muscle fibers. Images were captured and stored using Digital Pathology Microscope Slide Scanners (Ocus), and the QuPath Pathological Image Analysis System (version 0.3.2, QuPath) was utilized to quantify staining intensity and tissue structure.

Morphometric Analysis

The following definitions were applied: Medial VV was defined as a tube‐like and CD31‐positive structure within the media, excluding VV at the boundary between the media and adventitia. Adventitial VV referred to those scattered inside the adventitia. Mucoid extracellular matrix accumulation was identified as the abnormal accumulation of extracellular matrix mucoid occupying translamellar and/or intralamellar expansions in the media. The VV distribution density in the media was calculated as the proportion of the length of the media with hyperproliferative VV to the total length of the observed media. The medial degeneration and its subtypes, including mucoid extracellular matrix accumulation–translamellar, mucoid extracellular matrix accumulation–intralamellar, elastic fiber fragmentation and/or loss, smooth muscle cell nuclei loss, and lamina medial collapse and atherosclerosis, were graded according to the extent and severity of the lesion, as previously described in the consensus statement on surgical pathology of the aorta. 12 , 13

The morphometric analysis results were independently obtained by 2 experienced pathologists who were blinded to any information regarding the diagnosis of the specimens. Tissue samples were evaluated at approximately 5 mm in length. The thicknesses of the media and intima were measured at 5 arbitrary sites, and the average thickness values were calculated. The distribution of VV was carefully monitored.

DIC for Tissue Strain

DIC (EM6.304‐L, Shen Zhen Tesmart Instrument and Equipment Co. Ltd) was applied to measure the discrepancy of tissue strain among the medial layers between the specimens from the AD and control groups. Aortic tissues with medical tearing or with an area of <2 cm2 were excluded from this test. The fresh tissues were clipped into a dumbbell shape, with surface moisture wiped off and the adventitial side marked. The region of interest focused on the longitudinal section of tunica media, and it was divided into 3 layers: subadventitial (A), medial (M), and subintimal (I). Ten video extensometers were placed in each layer. The tissue was clamped, and a high‐precision Grasshopper3 camera (Point Grey GS3‐U3‐23S6M‐C, Audio Video Supply Inc.) was applied for tissue DIC analysis. During the tensile test, images were captured every second at a 3 mm/min stretch rate. Data were analyzed to extract longitudinal displacement values. To maintain tissue viability, all steps were completed within 1 hour of excision. Strain was calculated as ΔL divided by initial length (L0) (Figures 1 and 2).

Digital image correlation.

A, The tunica media was first clipped into a dumbbell shape. B, Next, moisture was wiped off the surface of the tissue, and the adventitial side was marked. Then, the lateral sides were painted with a white‐black speckle pattern. C, In each region, 10 video extensometers were placed (each extensometer consisted of a pair of speckles longitudinally distributed within the same region). Next, the tissue was clamped to the stretching test machine, and the focal length and aperture of a high‐precision camera were calibrated. When the tensile test began, the camera captured an image every second, whereas the machine stretched at a rate of 3 mm/min. After that, the captured images of the speckle pattern were imported into the data analysis software. D, Finally, the longitudinal displacement values of video extensometers in different regions of the tunica media were extracted and calculated. All the aforementioned procedures were accomplished within 1 hour of tissue excision. The strain value was equal to the longitudinal displacement (ΔL) divided by the initial longitudinal length (L0).

Translamellar discrepancy in stretch ranges.

Ten pieces of ascending aortic tissues were evaluated through digital image correlation testing, including 5 pieces of tissue from each group. For the stretching test, colors reflect the direction of movement and displacement amplitude of the white‐black speckle pattern. The left side of the specimen is region A of the aortic media tunica, the right side is region I, and region M is the region between A and I. Ten pairs of video extensometers were placed in each region.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS, version 25.0 (IBM). A 2‐tailed P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables are presented as medians and interquartile ranges, whereas categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Notably, there were no missing data in any of the analyzed variables; thus, all analyses were based on the complete data set without the need for imputation.

To assess interrater reliability, Cohen kappa coefficient (κ) was calculated for categorical variables (eg, presence of medial neovascularization, severity grades of medial degeneration). For continuous variables (eg, medial thickness, VV density), the intraclass correlation coefficient was used. Agreement was interpreted as follows: κ or intraclass correlation coefficient values ≤0.40 indicated poor agreement, 0.41 to 0.60 moderate, 0.61 to 0.80 good, and >0.80 excellent. Any discrepant evaluations were resolved through joint re‐review and consensus.

Comparisons of demographic characteristics and histopathological parameters were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test (for 2‐group comparisons) or the Kruskal–Wallis H test (for 3‐group comparisons) for continuous and ordinal variables, as data were not normally distributed. For post hoc pairwise comparisons following a significant Kruskal–Wallis test, a Dunn test with Bonferroni adjustment was employed. For nominal categorical variables, the χ2 test or Fisher exact test was used, as appropriate. Post hoc pairwise comparisons for nominal variables were performed using χ2 tests with Bonferroni correction, constructing separate 2×2 contingency tables for each pair of groups. The significance level was adjusted to P<0.0167 to account for the 3 comparisons.

Spearman rank correlation test was used to assess the relationship between VV density in the tunica media and tunica adventitia.

Medial neovascularization (MN) was defined as a VV distribution density at or above the 25th percentile (lower quartile) of all observed specimens. This cutoff was chosen to objectively identify tissues with a high burden of medial microvessels, hypothesized to be of pathological relevance. Specifically, MN was defined as a VV distribution density ≥5.0% (the 25th percentile for all specimens). The extent of MN was graded as follows: absent (0%–4.9%), mild (5.0%–11.9%), moderate (12.0%–32.9%), and serious (33.0%–100.0%).

Comparisons of MN grades, general medial degeneration, and its subtypes among the 3 groups were visualized using a heat map generated in GraphPad Prism version 9.5 (GraphPad Software). The same software was used to create violin plots comparing the density of adventitial and medial VV among groups. Given the exploratory and descriptive nature of these multiple histopathological comparisons, P values are reported without correction for multiple testing. Readers are advised to interpret these individual P values with caution, recognizing the increased risk of type I error, and to focus on the overall pattern of findings rather than isolated statistical significance.

For the longitudinal biomechanical data obtained via DIC, a repeated‐measures analysis of variance was used to compare stretch ranges among different layers of the tunica media between the AD and control groups. The normality of strain data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The sphericity assumption was tested with the Mauchly test; the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied when sphericity was violated. Sidak correction was used for post hoc multiple comparisons at each time point when a significant interaction effect was observed.

Abundant VV was distributed along the boundary between the adventitia and the media in all patients. The overall density of VV showed no significant differences among the 3 groups (Figure 5B; Table 2). VV lesions were not rare, especially in patients in the AD group, including wall thickening and vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation. This type of VV lesion inside the tunica adventitia appeared more frequently in the specimens of the AD group than the 2 groups (Figure 5B; Table 2). Residual tube‐like and CD31‐positive straining structures were observed along the edges of tear sites, revealing the possibility that MN occurred before the tearing of the tunica media (Figure 5C).

Aortic wall thickening is frequently observed in patients with or without MN, and no significant differences were observed between the specimens from the 2 groups with respect to medial or intimal thickness. However, findings from a Spearman rank correlation test revealed a moderately negative relationship between the VV density of tunica media and tunica adventitia (Figure 5D).

The MN grade of the AD group appeared the highest in the 3 groups (Figure 6B; Table S1). Medial degeneration was observed not only in the AD tissue but also in the other 2 groups. Compared with the aortic specimens from the non–HT‐AD group, the medial degeneration grade was even lower in the AD group (Figure 6A and 6B; Table S1). Significant differences were observed among the specimens from the 3 groups regarding the grades of the subtypes of medial degeneration, except for the smooth muscle cell nuclei loss and atherosclerosis (Figure 6B). In addition, the most severe medial degeneration among all subtypes was observed in the non–HT‐AD group when compared with the other 2 groups (Figure 6B; Table S1). A Dunn test with Bonferroni adjustment indicated that the non–HT‐AD group contributed most significantly to the differences in medial degeneration observed among the 3 groups except for the subtype of lamina medial collapse (Table S1).

Sex‐Based Analysis

As presented in Table S2, among the 31 hypertensive patients included in the primary analysis, 21 (67.7%) were men, and 10 (32.3%) were women. The distribution among the 3 groups did not differ significantly by sex (Table S2). Comparative analyses revealed no statistically significant differences between men and women among the measured histopathological parameters except for the lamina medial collapse. Specifically, no significant sex‐based differences were noted in medial thickness, intimal thickness, density of medial VV, incidence of MN, density of adventitial VV, or prevalence of VV lesions.

Interrater Reliability Assessment

The interobserver agreement for morphometric analysis was excellent. The κ values for categorical diagnoses ranged from 0.75 to 0.88, including the lesion of VV, the grades of medial degeneration, and its subtypes. The intraclass correlation coefficient for continuous measurements ranged from 0.73 to 0.81, including the medial thickness, intimal thickness, density of medial VV, and density of adventitial VV.

DISCUSSION

MN in Hypertensive Dissection

Medial degeneration caused by hypertension has long been associated with AD. 12 However, a comprehensive and cohesive narrative detailing the complete sequence of events linking hypertension, MN, and the eventual development of AD is still lacking. Based on the evidence presented in this study, we propose the following integrated pathological sequence to explain how hypertension may culminate in AD. The process begins with chronic hypertension, which induces aortic wall thickening and impairs the function of the adventitial VV. This impairment reduces oxygen delivery to the outer third of the tunica media, resulting in a state of chronic medial hypoxia. In response to this hypoxic stimulus, the aortic wall initiates a compensatory but pathological formation of new microvessels within the media, a process termed MN. However, these newly formed vessels are inherently fragile and prone to structural abnormalities. Concurrently, the mechanical integrity of the aortic wall is compromised. Our study demonstrated that while medial degeneration is the most severe in the middle layer of the media, biomechanical testing reveals a growing discrepancy in stretch between this degenerated middle layer and the outer layer during tension. This creates a focal region of elevated translamellar shear stress. Crucially, this region of high mechanical stress coincides precisely with the hypoxic, neovascularized outer media. The convergence of vessel fragility, medial hypoxia, and localized biomechanical overload predisposes these microvessels to rupture. The rupture of a medial VV introduces blood into the medial layer, which can initiate a tear, trigger an inflammatory cascade, and ultimately propagate as a classic AD. In this model, MN is not a passive bystander but an active and critical intermediary, linking the chronic insult of hypertension to the acute mechanical failure of the aortic wall.

Media tearing frequently occurred in the outer third of the tunica media in 91.7% of patients with AD in our series, which is similar to the published incidence (95.2%) reported by Osada and colleagues. 8 Notably, most of the VV was also distributed in the same layer of the tunica media. The proportion of patients with MN was significantly higher than that of patients without AD. The same was true for the median value of the medial VV density between patients in the 2 groups. Additionally, when compared with the slow pace of neovascularization, which can take months or even years, the occurrence of AD can be described as a sudden event, as it typically takes place within hours to days. Residual tube‐like and CD31‐positive tissues were discovered along the edges of the tearing region, suggesting that VV might have existed before the medial tearing. Therefore, we herein hypothesize that MN is closely associated with the progression of AD.

Hypertensive Versus Nonhypertensive Dissection

Our findings further highlight a crucial distinction between hypertensive and nonhypertensive forms of AD. Notably, the non–HT‐AD group exhibited the most severe degree of medial degeneration among all its subtypes. However, the incidence and density of MN in this group were significantly lower than those in the hypertensive AD group. This dissociation suggested that while severe medial degeneration is a common histological end point in AD, it may arise from different upstream mechanisms. In nonhypertensive patients, factors such as genetic predisposition (eg, connective tissue disorders), inflammation, or other undefined insults may directly drive medial degradation without invoking a prominent neovascular response. In contrast, in hypertensive patients, the pathophysiological cascade appears to be uniquely characterized by the development of MN as a critical intermediary step. This supports the hypothesis that MN is not merely a secondary consequence of medial degeneration but a specific pathological adaptation to the chronic hypoxic stress induced by hypertension and adventitial VV dysfunction.

Sex Differences in AD

While our pathological analyses revealed slight sex differences in the measured parameters, the well‐documented epidemiological disparity in AD incidence suggested that sex‐specific factors may influence disease susceptibility or pathogenesis. Genetically, variants in fibrillin‐1 and elastin variants drive aortic integrity differences—hereditary susceptibility is stronger in men (h 2=42.79%) owing to elastic fiber fragility, whereas estrogen confers protective effects in premenopausal women through enhanced elastin synthesis. Hemodynamically, men exhibit higher pulsatile aortic stress, amplifying dissection risk via increased left ventricular afterload. Hormonal shifts such as postmenopausal estrogen decline further exacerbate female susceptibility through vascular stiffening. 14 Risk stratification should prioritize sex‐specific vascular aging to capture functional/molecular mechanisms of AD pathogenesis.

Biomechanical Stress and Outer Wall Vessels

The sharp difference in the stress–strain curve during repeated stretching between the outer layer (ischemic region) and the inside layer (rich blood supply region) of the media was thought to be the reason for media tearing. 15 The authors modified the tension test and found a prominent translamellar discrepancy in stretch ranges between the subadventitial and middle layer of the tunica media in the specimens from the AD group, revealing an increasing translamellar shear stress in the outer third region of the tunica media, where angiogenesis frequently occurred. It suggests that medial tearing is likely to occur in the outer third of the tunica media due to VV rupture accompanied by an abnormally increased external force. In addition, prominent differences among the layers of the tunica media were observed in patients with AD. Serious medial degeneration occurred in the middle layer of the tunica media, which was associated with the prominent translamellar discrepancy in stretch ranges between the middle and subadventitial layers of the tunica media. Conversely, the translamellar discrepancy in medial degeneration appeared to be less obvious in patients in the control group. This might be another reason for the increased translamellar shear stress in the outer third of the tunica media.

To explore the risk factors for MN, the specimens were classified into 2 groups based on whether the density of the medial VV was ≥5%. Different from a previous study, 16 the authors could not verify the association between MN and the increasing aortic wall thickness, which is frequently observed in patients with hypertension. However, the adventitial VV appeared to be sparser in the specimens with MN, indicating a negative correlation between adventitial VV density and MN. It is known that adventitial VV around the aorta provides nutrition and oxygen to the outer layer, and this has been demonstrated as a potential contributor to aortic damage. Medial degeneration in the outer layer of aorta was observed after adventitial flaking, which also occurred in an animal model of adventitial VV hypoperfusion. 17 , 18 The adventitial VV responds to increased demand for oxygen and nutrients, triggering angiogenesis, as the thickened aortic wall hampers oxygen delivery deep within the media. 19 However, the adventitial VV density did not increase, whereas the aortic wall became thicker in hypertensive patients with AD. Furthermore, various sclerotic and hyperplastic changes in the VV within the tunica adventitia, which were accompanied by degenerative lesions in the outer layer of the tunica media, were found in patients with AD. 8 Similar pathological changes were observed in the tunica adventitia of patients with AD, supporting the speculation that stenosis or obstruction of the adventitial VV is an essential risk factor for AD in hypertensive patients. The authors herein suggest a compensatory mechanism of damage of the aortic wall, which contributes to hypoxemia in the outer third of the tunica media.

Inflammation and Oxidative Injury

When the VV ruptures, the extravasated blood may serve as a foreign body, eliciting an inflammatory response within the aortic wall. Immune cells, including macrophages and neutrophils, are recruited to the site of injury, where they release a range of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin 6, tumor necrosis factor α, and interferon γ. 20 These cytokines not only promote further immune cell recruitment but also induce the activation of matrix metalloproteinases, which degrade key extracellular matrix components of the aortic wall (such as collagen and elastin). 21 , 22 , 23 The progressive degradation of these structural proteins compromises the integrity of the aortic wall, thereby increasing its susceptibility to dissection.

Oxidative stress is another key factor in AD pathogenesis. 24 VV rupture induces a local surge in reactive oxygen species production. 25 Extravasated blood, abundant in red blood cells, undergoes hemolysis upon VV rupture, releasing iron, which participates in the Fenton reaction, generating highly reactive hydroxyl radicals. 26 These reactive oxygen species directly damage aortic wall cells, particularly smooth muscle and endothelial cells, by oxidizing lipids, proteins, and DNA, disrupting normal functions. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 Moreover, oxidative stress activates signaling pathways regulating inflammation and matrix metalloproteinase activity, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates aortic wall damage. 31 The identification of MN as a key intermediary step raises the question of clinically detectable biomarkers. While direct histological assessment is not feasible in vivo, future research could explore whether imaging modalities or circulating factors reflecting hypoxia, angiogenesis, or the inflammatory cascade described above could serve as surrogate markers for MN and impending dissection risk. Advanced imaging techniques such as high‐resolution contrast‐enhanced magnetic resonance imaging or positron emission tomographic imaging with angiogenesis‐specific tracers may offer noninvasive approaches to detect MN in at‐risk patients.

Clinical Implications and Future Research

Taken together, the distinct pathological profiles observed in our 3 groups refine our understanding of AD pathogenesis. In isolated hypertension (control group), medial changes suggested that medial degeneration alone seemed to be insufficient to cause AD. In hypertensive AD, the coexistence of severe MN and focal biomechanical stress in the outer media appears to be a critical trigger. In contrast, the non–HT‐AD group demonstrated that severe medial degeneration could occur and lead to dissection even in the relative absence of significant MN, implying a different primary driver.

While the link between hypertension‐induced medial degeneration and AD is recognized, a full grasp of the relationship among hypertension, MN, and AD is still lacking. Our study shows that in isolated hypertension, tunica media changes suggest medial degeneration alone does not fully explain AD. Media tearing, likely from abnormal shear stress and VV rupture, mainly happens in the outer third of the tunica media where most VV are, indicating MN's connection to AD. Stress–strain curve differences between media layers cause tearing, with middle‐layer medial degeneration raising outer‐third shear stress. Grouping specimens by medial VV density reveals no significant tunica media and intima thickness differences but sparser adventitial VV in MN specimens, suggesting adventitial VV stenosis or obstruction as a key AD risk factor in hypertensive patients. In addition, VV rupture triggers inflammation and oxidative stress, degrading the extracellular matrix and damaging aortic wall cells.

The cross‐sectional design of our study, while revealing strong associations between MN, biomechanical alterations, and AD, inherently limited causal interpretation. We could not determine whether MN is a primary driver of medial degeneration and dissection, a compensatory response to chronic medial hypoxia induced by adventitial VV dysfunction and hypertension, or a secondary phenomenon resulting from the mechanical stress and inflammation within the aortic wall before overt dissection. The presence of residual CD31‐positive structures at tear edges suggests preexisting VV, supporting but not proving a causative role. Therefore, the following proposed sequence should be considered a plausible hypothesis integrating our observations, which requires validation through longitudinal studies.

Our study provided detailed histopathological and biomechanical evidence linking MN to AD within a hypertensive cohort from a single center in China. An important next step is to evaluate the external validity of these findings. Known epidemiological variations in AD incidence and risk profiles among different racial, ethnic, and geographic populations raise the question of whether the role of MN is consistent or divergent. The prevalence of genetic predispositions or the severity of hypertension may influence the aortic wall adaption and the pattern of VV remodeling. Future multicenter, international studies incorporating diverse populations are crucial to confirm the universality of our observations, to refine risk models, and to identify any unique phenotypic expressions of this pathological pathway.

LIMITATIONS

This study has some limitations. First, the authors acknowledge that the conclusions of the study were preliminary due to the limited sample size for the DIC analysis, which restricted the generalizability of the findings. While statistical significance was observed, these results required cautious interpretation. Consequently, the biomechanical component of this study should be viewed as preliminary, highlighting the need for subsequent studies to expand the sample through the patients who met the inclusion criteria, and to strengthen the robustness and applicability of the results. Second, obtaining aortic tissues before AD occurrence was challenging. All the speculations about the relationship between AD and MN were based on the aortic wall tissue from patients with AD and HT donors using pathological observation and physical strain test. However, donors might have other preexisting medical conditions, such as diabetes or chronic kidney disease, which can also affect the aorta, introducing potential biases into our study. As a result, the comparisons made between the hypertensive and control groups might not accurately reflect the true aortic changes associated with hypertension. Hypertensive patients with nonaortic disease should be included in future studies, which might provide a more robust and accurate assessment of the relationship between hypertension and aortic pathology. Third, although the literature reports sex differences in the incidence of AD, this study did not identify positive results, likely because of the relatively small sample sizes in the present study. Confirming the role of sex in AD pathogenesis requires large‐scale cohort studies and confirmatory experimental research in the future. Fourth, the cross‐sectional nature of our tissue sampling meant that we could not establish the temporal sequence of events. We observed MN in tissues with AD, but we could not definitively determine whether MN preceded and contributed to the dissection or arose concurrently or even as a consequence of predissection pathological changes (eg, hypoxia, inflammation). All models of causality linking MN to AD remain speculative based on our data. Last, the number of patients with non–HT‐AD in our series was relatively small (n=8), which might limit the statistical power to fully characterize the mechanisms of AD in this population and to definitively conclude that MN is exclusive to hypertensive AD. Future biomechanical studies with larger sample sizes, potentially achievable through multicenter collaboration, are needed to validate these preliminary findings.

CONCLUSIONS

This cross‐sectional study identified a strong association between MN and AD in hypertensive patients. Notably, the prevalence and extent of MN were markedly lower in the non–HT‐AD group despite the presence of the most severe medial degeneration, suggesting that MN may be a specific pathological feature of hypertension‐related AD rather than a universal hallmark of all dissections. Histological and biomechanical features suggested VV rupture as a potential initiating event in the outer tunica media. The convergence of MN, localized biomechanical stress, and adventitial VV dysfunction appears to delineate a distinct pathogenic pathway in hypertensive individuals. However, the observed associations do not confirm causality. MN could be a primary driver, a compensatory response to medial hypoxia, or a bystander phenomenon.

Our study did not reveal significant sex‐specific differences in the measured parameters. Nevertheless, the established epidemiological disparity in AD incidence highlights that sex‐specific mechanisms in vascular aging and molecular pathways remain crucial for comprehensive risk stratification.

To advance these findings, future research should pursue several key directions. Longitudinal studies are needed to track MN development and establish its temporal role. Interventional studies in animal models can test causal links and evaluate MN as a therapeutic target. Comparative studies incorporating larger cohorts of both hypertensive and nonhypertensive patients with AD are essential to validate the specificity of the MN pathway and to elucidate the dominant mechanisms in non–HT‐AD. Translating this knowledge to clinical practice requires identifying noninvasive biomarkers for MN, potentially through advanced vascular imaging or circulating markers of hypoxia and inflammation. Ultimately, our findings reinforce the critical importance of rigorous blood pressure control while suggesting that future research could examine whether patients with evidence of MN require even more intensive management or novel therapies.
