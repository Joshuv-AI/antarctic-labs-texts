AI‐Enabled Precision Dosing in Pediatrics: Enhancing Model‐Informed Decision Making
Authors: Kei Irie, Tomoyuki Mizuno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnsuring safe and effective pharmacotherapy for children remains a central challenge in clinical pharmacology, yet rapid advances in AI have not translated into clinical practice. This Perspective highlights how AI‐enabled approaches can enhance model‐informed decision making for precision dosing. By integrating pharmacometrics with pediatric digital twins and AI agents, these frameworks can enable physiologically grounded, adaptive, and learning‐based dosing strategies. We outline a path from static prediction toward explainable, clinically actionable precision dosing in pediatric care.

Delivering safe and effective drug therapy in children remains a central challenge in clinical pharmacology. Growth in body size and developmental changes in organ functions profoundly influence pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacodynamics (PD). At the same time, ethical and practical constraints limit the ability to conduct pediatric clinical trials, leaving significant knowledge gaps that complicate dosing decisions. Over the past decades, mechanistic modeling approaches, including population PK/PD, physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK), and quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP), have become indispensable tools for characterizing variability, extrapolating across age groups, and supporting precision dosing in pediatrics. These approaches have established the foundation of pediatric clinical pharmacology by providing biological plausibility, enabling extrapolation, and guiding decision making. 1

The adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) has accelerated in recent years, with growing applications in pharmacometrics. While AI shows promise as a complement to mechanistic approaches, its ultimate impact would depend on whether models can be integrated into systems that are explainable, robust, and clinically actionable. This Perspective considers how AI can support decision making in pediatric pharmacotherapy, highlighting emerging concepts such as digital twins and AI agents as potential steps toward more dynamic and adaptive frameworks for safer and more effective therapy in children.

AI‐INTEGRATED PHARMACOMETRIC MODELING

AI has been applied in diverse ways to refine pharmacometric modeling, 2 including the prediction of PK/PD parameters from physicochemical or in vitro data, the identification of covariate–parameter relationships, the direct estimation of clearance and other PK metrics, and the representation of PK/PD dynamics using continuous‐time learning frameworks such as neural ordinary differential equations, which have been applied to model complex dynamics. 3 Collectively, these approaches enable the flexible representation of complex, nonlinear relationships. When integrated with mechanistic pharmacometric models, AI may enhance predictive flexibility, capture complex nonlinear relationships, and integrate heterogeneous and multimodal data, while mechanistic models provide biological grounding, interpretability, and support for extrapolation beyond the training data. The utility of such methods is greatest when trained on large‐scale datasets; however, pediatric applications remain constrained by limited sample sizes and substantial heterogeneity. Although future integration of real‐time pediatric PK/PD data from noninvasive technologies such as biosensors and wearable devices may partially alleviate these limitations, near‐term opportunities lie in hybrid modeling strategies explicitly designed to operate robustly under sparse, irregular data conditions.

Physics‐informed neural networks (PINNs) represent one such direction: by embedding physiological laws, mechanistic constraints, and maturation principles directly into the learning process, they may support more biologically plausible and consistent predictions in pediatric settings where data are limited. For example, a PINN‐based approach has been shown to estimate pharmacokinetic parameters from aggregated concentration–time data by enforcing underlying differential equations, which serve as the mechanistic constraints during training. 4 Continual learning provides another promising avenue, allowing models to adapt as new observations accumulate and potentially remain relevant even in the face of sparse or fragmented pediatric data. In this context, it is conceptually aligned with sequential model updating in model‐informed precision dosing, 5 with AI‐integrated or hybrid models potentially further extending this adaptive capability. To date, however, applications of these methods in pediatrics remain limited, and future research is warranted.

Taken together, these AI‐integrated modeling approaches may serve as future engines for more accurate and physiologically grounded PK/PD prediction. Yet, the greater value of AI will come not from individual engines alone but from integrating them into coherent systems, such as digital twins and agentic AI, that can support clinical decision making, particularly as predictive engines whose plausibility and adaptability continue to improve.

DIGITAL TWIN

The term digital twin has become a widely recognized and increasingly influential concept across disciplines, broadly referring to a virtual counterpart of a physical system that remains continuously linked to real‐world data. In clinical pharmacology, a digital twin can be understood as a framework that emulates a real patient in digital space, predicts therapeutic effects and adverse events, and feeds this information back to inform treatment strategies in the actual patient. Despite its growing prominence, there is still no shared consensus on how a digital twin should be concretely implemented in this field. When considering this core idea of representing an individual patient in a digital environment, an important question arises: is the digital twin truly a new concept in pediatric clinical pharmacology?

In our view, many of the defining principles now associated with digital twins have long been reflected in pharmacometric modeling and MIPD. For example, compartment models represent the body as a series of interconnected mathematical compartments that capture dominant drug behaviors and use clinical observations to update predictions of drug disposition, while Bayesian forecasting and therapeutic drug monitoring demonstrate the well‐established ability to refine forecasts as new data become available. Importantly, the outputs of these models have already contributed to real‐world patient care by directly informing clinical decision making.

The digital twin's unique value lies not in the invention of entirely new principles, but in its integration of multiple components into a coherent and continuously adaptive environment that more faithfully recreates the patient in digital space. In this sense, ongoing refinement and evolution of mechanistic models, together with richer characterization of physiological features, will become increasingly important for enabling more realistic simulations. To be effective, these models require virtual patients built on detailed physiological features, including organ size, blood flow, enzyme expression, and disease states, as well as broader clinical and contextual factors. This foundation is particularly critical in pediatrics, where growth trajectories and organ maturation introduce rapid and dynamic changes that must also be captured. As a means to generate biologically consistent parameter sets for mechanism‐based approaches such as PBPK and QSP modeling, AI‐based generative approaches hold considerable promise. 6 These approaches may support patient‐specific digital twin construction by enabling conditional generation of physiologically consistent parameter sets, which is essential for patient‐specific simulation. Our group, for example, is developing a conditional variational autoencoder (cVAE) with physiological loss functions to generate interconnected physiological parameters such as body size, organ weights, blood flows, and organ functions across the pediatric spectrum. This physiology‐informed cVAE learns the distribution of physiological variables and their interdependencies, while physiological loss functions impose constraints that enforce biologically plausible relationships. This framework enables the generation of physiologically consistent profiles across the pediatric spectrum, as well as conditional generation of patient profiles tailored to specific clinical scenarios, and has been applied to PBPK modeling. 7 We believe these technologies will be a key enabler for advancing digital twin approaches.

Overall, the digital twin in pediatric clinical pharmacology can be viewed as an evolution of existing modeling approaches rather than a radical reinvention. AGENTIC AI

Recently, AI agents have emerged as a prominent direction in artificial intelligence, reflecting a shift from models that passively generate outputs to autonomous systems that plan, act, and adapt within interactive environments where actions influence future states. 8 Such agents are explicitly designed to pursue defined objectives over time through continuous interaction with their environment, rather than producing one‐off predictions. This paradigm is highly relevant to clinical pharmacology, where mechanistic pharmacometric models define dynamic simulation environments in which dosing decisions alter future drug exposure and patient states. Within this setting, the central challenge extends beyond accurate prediction to the identification of optimal dosing trajectories that guide patients toward therapeutic targets under uncertainty and physiological variability. Building on this alignment, we have explored a model‐informed framework in which dosing decisions are optimized within a mechanistic simulation environment, using reinforcement learning to iteratively evaluate dose and interval choices through virtual pediatric patients and to favor clinically defined target concentration ranges for monoclonal antibody therapies. In this framework, a model‐informed deep Q‐network achieved high target attainment rates (e.g., 92.9% and 98.4% at Infusions 4 and 5) while minimizing unnecessary high‐dose use. In addition, we demonstrated that, in retrospective real‐world validation, patients whose actual doses matched reinforcement learning recommendations had trough levels significantly closer to target range. This work illustrates how agent‐based optimization can be embedded within pharmacometric simulations to support systematic exploration of dosing strategies. 9 Although this represents a relatively simplified digital environment, it illustrates how agent‐based optimization can transform pharmacometric models from predictive tools into decision‐support systems.

As pediatric digital twin environments become more detailed, incorporating physiology, organ function, developmental maturation, and disease states, the resulting expansion of state space and system complexity makes manual optimization and rule‐based decision making increasingly impractical. Precisely because these environments are complex and dynamic, automated decision‐making approaches become increasingly relevant. AI agents provide a systematic means to explore high‐dimensional simulation outputs, adapt strategies as virtual patient states evolve, and translate complex model behavior into clinically actionable dosing recommendations.

Recent advances in language models offer an additional layer of support for such AI‐driven systems. Large language models (LLMs) demonstrate strong capabilities for synthesizing heterogeneous information, but their general‐purpose training may lead to reasoning insufficiently constrained by domain‐specific knowledge, raising the risk of inappropriate extrapolation beyond validated simulations. In addition, their deployment in clinical settings requires careful consideration of patient data privacy and security. These limitations motivate interest in small language models (SLMs), which emphasize domain‐specific reasoning, structured knowledge, and controlled deployment. In healthcare applications, appropriately fine‐tuned SLMs have been shown to perform competitively on specialized tasks where domain knowledge is critical, offering a favorable balance between performance, interpretability, and resource efficiency. 10 However, in clinical decision making, the use of SLMs as primary reasoning engines remains high risk, as hallucinated or fabricated information may result in serious or even harmful consequences. In the framework described in this Perspective, reasoning and prediction are centered on digital twin models and reinforcement learning‐based decision frameworks, while language models are positioned primarily as communication and interface layers. Within precision dosing workflows, SLMs can therefore serve as controlled interfaces that translate outputs from mechanistic models and AI agents into clinically meaningful insights, while converting clinician inputs into model‐compatible queries.

In this integrated framework, mechanistic pharmacometric models can be viewed as simulation engines that represent pharmacological and physiological processes, while AI agents may serve as adaptive decision‐making layers that interact with digital twins to explore and refine dosing strategies. Domain‐specific language models could provide interpretable and secure interfaces to support clinical use. With human‐in‐the‐loop oversight to ensure clinical relevance, ethical governance, and final decision authority, the conceptual integration of these technologies points to a potential shift in how AI‐enabled, model‐informed decision making might be realized for pediatric precision dosing (Figure 2 ). Looking ahead, such frameworks may be progressively developed and refined within simulation environments, with continued integration of real‐world clinical data expected to further enhance their reliability, clinical utility, and impact on patient care. At the same time, their successful implementation will depend on continued validation of underlying prediction models, careful real‐world evaluation, and demonstration of meaningful improvements in clinical outcomes and workflow efficiency.

CONCLUSIONS

AI‐integrated pharmacometric modeling, digital twins, and AI agents each represent promising directions for pediatric clinical pharmacology and precision dosing. Together, they point toward adaptive, explainable, and clinically actionable frameworks for guiding drug therapy in children. Challenges of explainability, reproducibility, data limitations, clinical applicability, and regulatory acceptance remain, but considering the pace of recent advances in AI, these frameworks are not a distant vision but a near‐future reality that the field must be prepared to evaluate and responsibly implement.
