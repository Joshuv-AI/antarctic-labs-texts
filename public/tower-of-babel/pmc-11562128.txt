Association between discontinuity in clinicians and outcomes of nursing home residents
Authors: Hyunkyung Yun, Mark Aaron Unruh, Kira L Ryskina, Hye-Young Jung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLittle is known about the impact of clinician discontinuity on quality of care for nursing home residents. We examined the association between clinician discontinuity and outcomes of residents with long-term care stays up to 3 years using claims for a national 20% sample of Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries from 2014 through 2019. We used an event study analysis that accounted for staggered treatment timing. Estimates were adjusted for resident, clinician, and nursing home characteristics. Three sensitivity analyses were conducted. The first excluded small nursing homes, which were in the lowest quartile based on the number of beds. The second attributed residents to clinician practices rather than individual clinicians. The third removed the 3-year long-term care stay restriction. We found that, compared to residents who did not experience a clinician change, those with a clinician change had a 0.7 percentage point higher likelihood of an ambulatory care sensitive hospitalization in a given quarter (a 36.8% relative increase). Clinician discontinuity was not associated with ambulatory care sensitive emergency department visits. Results from our 3 sensitivity analyses were consistent with those from the primary analysis. Policymakers may consider using continuity in clinicians as a marker of nursing home quality.Keywords: clinician discontinuity, nursing home quality, long-term care

Introduction

Approximately half of all hospitalizations and emergency department (ED) visits by nursing home (NH) residents are associated with ambulatory care sensitive (ACS) conditions.1-7 These events are thought to be potentially preventable with high-quality care in ambulatory settings.3,8-10 In community settings, access to high-quality primary care has been shown to reduce hospitalizations and ED visits among older adults,2,11-13 and discontinuity in primary care clinicians has been associated with worse outcomes and higher health care costs.14-23

In the NH, physicians and advanced practitioners play a key role in the care of residents who are medically complex and require highly coordinated care. These clinicians are responsible for managing new and chronic conditions of NH residents, participating in advanced care planning, and coordinating care with specialist clinicians and the direct care team.

Once admitted to a NH, a resident might continue to see their primary care clinician or be cared for by a NH clinician. A resident may also experience a change in clinicians during their NH stay. It is not known if discontinuity in primary care clinicians is associated with outcomes of NH residents. Using a nationally representative sample of Medicare fee-for-service claims, we examined the relationship between clinician discontinuity and the quality of care for NH residents based on ACS hospitalizations and ED visits.

Data and methods

First, we made unadjusted comparisons of the characteristics of long-stay residents who did and did not experience a clinician change, in addition to comparing the characteristics of attributed clinicians and the NHs in which they resided. Next, we conducted an event study analysis to obtain adjusted estimates of the association between clinician discontinuity and the likelihood of a resident experiencing an ACS hospitalization or an ACS ED visit. The Institutional Review Board of Weill Cornell Medical College waived informed consent for the study.

Data sources

Medicare fee-for-service claims for a random 20% national sample of beneficiaries for the period January 1, 2014, through December 31, 2019, were used as our primary data source. Claims were linked to Minimum Data Set assessments, which provided clinical characteristics of residents, including activities of daily living (ADL) scores and performance on the Cognitive Function Scale (CFS), in addition to marital status. The Master Beneficiary Summary File was used to obtain residents’ demographic characteristics and chronic conditions and also allowed us to confirm enrollment in Medicare Parts A and B. The Carrier file, which includes professional claims from physicians, advanced practitioners, and other clinicians, was linked with the Medicare Data on Provider Practice and Specialty (MD-PPAS). The MD-PPAS contains clinician characteristics including their provider type (medical doctor, nurse practitioner [NP], or physician assistant [PA]) and specialty. Nursing home-level characteristics were merged from the Long-Term Care: Facts on Care in the US database. The analytic file used the resident quarter as the unit of analysis.

Study population

The study population included long-stay NH residents, defined as those with stays in the same facility for 100 days or longer with no more than 10 consecutive days outside the facility.24 Residents under age 65 and those in hospital-based NHs were excluded since they have characteristics distinct from the broader NH population.25,26 To adjust for potentially differential underlying health status and the likelihood of clinician discontinuity, we restricted the sample to residents with long-term stays up to 3 years from the start year of their long-stay status. The limit was chosen since the median length of stay for long-stay residents in our sample was 3 years. Those who did not have any evaluation and management (E&M) claims after the first year of their stay were excluded because we were not able to attribute them to clinicians (see Attribution of residents to clinicians in the following section).

Attribution of residents to clinicians

Residents were attributed to physicians or advanced practitioners who provided a plurality of their E&M visits in the NH in a given year.27,28 These clinicians were limited to physicians, NPs, and PAs who had at least 1 NH E&M claim annually (Appendix Figure S1). Physicians included generalists (general medicine, family medicine, and internal medicine), physical medicine and rehabilitation specialists, and geriatricians.

Discontinuity of care

The determinant of interest, discontinuity in clinicians, was measured using a binary indicator reflecting whether there was a change in the annually attributed clinician in the second or third year of a resident's NH stay for long-term care.27,28 For example, if an individual became a long-stay resident in 2017 and was attributed to clinician A, then was attributed to clinician B in 2018 or in 2019, this would be considered discontinuity in clinicians. To focus on the effect of the initial clinician change, we excluded residents who experienced more than 1 clinician change (Appendix S1).

Outcomes

Our outcomes included binary indicators denoting any ACS hospitalization and any ACS ED visit in a given quarter, based on definitions from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality.29 These events should be largely, though not completely, preventable with proper primary care in the NH. We did not restrict the number of quarters observed for this measure following a clinician change.

Covariates

Resident-level covariates included sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, race and ethnicity, and marital status), in addition to dual eligibility and reason for Medicare entitlement (ie, due to disability). We also controlled for resident baseline measures of any antipsychotic use, ADL score (ranging from 0 to 28), and indicators for levels of the CFS (ranging from 1 to 4). Indicators for 65 chronic conditions were also included (Appendix Table S1). Clinician-level covariates included an indicator reflecting whether an attributed clinician was a physician or an advanced practitioner, and the proportion of their E&M claims billed in NHs (vs clinic, hospital, or another care setting) at baseline. Nursing home covariates included indicators for for-profit status, being part of the multi-facility chain, presence of a physician extender, presence of a dementia care unit, total number of beds, direct care hours per resident/day, ratio of registered nurses (RNs) among the total number of RNs and licensed practical nurses, occupancy rate, and percentages of Medicare- and Medicaid-covered residents.

Statistical analysis

Our analysis was conducted between March and July 2024. Unadjusted comparisons of resident, clinician, and NH characteristics were made using the earliest record for each. Unpaired t-tests were used for continuous measures and χ2 tests were used for categorical measures.

Adjusted comparisons were made using an event study approach that accommodated the staggered timing of clinician changes among residents, and incorporated doubly robust inverse probability weighting estimators, to obtain the estimated average treatment effect for the treated.30,31 The event study approach allows us to observe whether there were heterogeneous changes in the likelihood of an ACS hospitalization over time; for example, we can observe whether there was a greater estimated risk of an ACS hospitalization in the first quarter following the year of a clinician change and a lower risk in later quarters. The adjusted model included the following covariates: resident's baseline age, sex, race/ethnicity, indicators for dual eligibility, reason for Medicare (disability), marital status, CFS score, ADL score, and 65 chronic conditions (Appendix Table S1); an indicator of whether the attributed clinician was a physician and the percentage of NH-specific services among all of the clinician's E&M visits were also included. In all adjusted analyses, indicators were included for each NH, thereby making comparisons of residents in the same NHs who were exposed to the same facility factors. Standard errors were adjusted for clustering at the NH level.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted 3 sensitivity analyses. First, adjusted analyses were repeated after excluding small NHs. Smaller facilities have fewer clinicians and residents in them may be less likely to experience a change in their attributed clinician. Nursing homes were categorized into quintiles based on the total number of beds, and small NHs were defined as those in the lowest quintile (11-80 beds). Second, we attributed residents to practice groups identified by a taxpayer identification number (TIN) instead of an individual clinician. This was done to account for the potential use of team-based models in NHs where multiple clinicians from the same practice provide care to the resident. Third, we did not limit our sample to residents with stays up to 3 years for long-term care and repeated our primary adjusted analyses using the first clinician change as the determinant of interest.

All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (TS Level 1M5) and Stata/MP, version 17.0 (StataCorp).

Results

Unadjusted comparisons of resident, clinician, and NH characteristics

Among the 33 946 residents in our sample from 10 643 NHs, 50.3% (17 073) experienced a change in clinician and 49.7% (16 873) did not experience a clinician change (Table 1). Resident demographic characteristics were similar between the two groups (mean age of residents with a clinician change 83.6 [SD 8.6] vs residents without a clinician change 84.4 [SD 8.4], P < 0.001; percent female 70.3% vs 71.1%, P = 0.104; dual eligibility 72.7% vs 72.8%, P = 0.859; and Black race 10.0% vs 8.8%, Hispanic ethnicity and other races 3.5% vs 4.5%, P < 0.001). Presence of chronic conditions was also similar for residents with and without a clinician change with the exception of depression (54.0% vs 49.8%, P < 0.001) and depressive disorder (57.1% vs 52.9%, P < 0.001). Residents with a clinician change were less likely to have a physician as their attributed clinician compared to those who did not experience a change in clinician (57.6% vs 75.0%, P < 0.001). However, residents with a clinician change were more likely to be attributed to clinicians with a higher average proportion of services provided in NHs (69.4% [SD 33.3] vs 57.8% [SD 34.0], P < 0.001). These residents were also more likely to reside in NHs that were for-profit (69.4% vs 66.8%, P < 0.001), part of a multi-facility chain (56.8% vs 52.9%, P < 0.001), more likely to have a physician extender (62.0% vs 49.0%, P < 0.001), and with a larger number of beds (136.7 [SD 76.2] vs 131.1 [SD 80.0], P < 0.001).

Estimates from adjusted comparisons

Estimates from our event study analysis indicated that a clinician change was associated with an increased risk of experiencing an ACS hospitalization compared to residents who did not experience a change in clinician; the estimated aggregated treatment effect was 0.7 percentage points (pp) (95% CI, 0.3-1.1 pp; P < 0.001; Table 2; Appendix Figure S2). Compared to the quarterly mean prevalence of ACS hospitalizations in the sample (1.9%), this represents a 36.8% relative increase. There were no statistically significant differences in ACS ED visits between residents who did and did not experience a clinician change (0.2 pp, 95% CI, −0.2 to 0.5, P = 0.339; Table 2; Appendix Figure S2).

The results of our 3 sensitivity analyses were consistent with those from our primary analyses.(1) In our analysis that excluded small NHs, the estimated aggregated treatment effect of a clinician change on the likelihood of a resident experiencing an ACS hospitalization was 0.6 pp (95% CI, 0.2 to 1.0 pp; P = 0.004); no statistically significant differences were found in ACS ED visits (Table 3, Panel A; Appendix Figure S3). (2) After attributing residents to clinician practice groups instead of individual clinicians, a clinician change was associated with a 0.5 pp (95% CI, 0.1 to 0.9 pp, P = 0.018) increase in the likelihood of an ACS hospitalization and no difference in the likelihood of an ACS ED visit (−0.2 pp, 95% CI, −0.6 to 0.2, P = 0.324; Table 3, Panel B; Appendix Figure S4). (3) In our sensitivity analysis that removed the 3-year restriction on long-term care stays, a change in clinicians was associated with a 0.6 pp (95% CI, 0.2 to 0.9, P = 0.001) increase in the likelihood of an ACS hospitalization, but not in ACS ED visits (−0.1 pp, 95% CI, −0.4 to 0.3, P = 0.629; Table 3, Panel C; Appendix Figure S5).

Discussion

In this national study of long-stay NH residents, those who experienced discontinuity in clinicians were 36.8% more likely to experience an ACS hospitalization compared with residents who did not experience a change in clinician. Our findings are generally consistent with previous studies of clinician discontinuity in care settings other than NHs.15,16,18-21 For example, discontinuity in primary care clinicians has been associated with a 14% higher hazard of hospitalization among community-dwelling patients with 1 to 2 chronic conditions.17 Additionally, prior studies found clinician discontinuity during hospital stays to be associated with worse outcomes.18,20 One study found that increased discontinuity in acute care nurse assignments for hospitalized patients was associated with decreased severity of illness as measured by the Rothman Index score.19 Other studies have suggested that continuity in clinician care among patients in the community receiving home health services have better outcomes than those who experience clinician discontinuity.22,23

Discontinuity in clinicians for NH residents may lead to worse outcomes through several mechanisms, including a clinician's lack of familiarity with the resident or disruptions in the flow of information between clinicians involved in the resident's care. In addition, continuity among clinicians may correlate with continuity among direct care staff or administrative personnel, for example, due to broader instability in the NH such as staffing turnover or financial distress. Future research should evaluate how NHs manage clinician discontinuity including during the workforce shortages of the COVID-19 pandemic.

About half of the NH residents in our sample experienced clinician discontinuity. The reasons for these changes are not clear. Turnover accounts for some discontinuity in clinicians. However, turnover rates for physicians overall are much lower than rates of discontinuity in our sample would suggest,32 though turnover rates specifically for NH clinicians are unknown. Additionally, NHs have increasingly used more advanced practitioners relative to physicians over time.33 These changes in the composition of the NH clinician workforce may have contributed to discontinuity in clinicians among residents. Moreover, the NH clinician workforce has been in flux over the past decade with an increase in specialization of NH clinicians and an increase in the size of physician groups across health care settings.34 Larger physician groups may be more likely to practice cross-coverage across different clinicians, resulting in discontinuity among individual providers.35

Decreasing reimbursement rates for services delivered in NHs may also lead to clinicians moving to other practice settings. Medicare reimbursements for E&M services have historically been lower for care provided in NHs compared to other settings and were recently reduced with further cuts proposed.36 Because clinician continuity necessitates a greater presence of clinicians in NHs over time, increasing reimbursement rates for services provided in NHs may lead to residents having more consistency in clinicians caring for them.37,38

Given our findings, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) may consider public reporting of measures of clinician continuity for NH residents, similar to the recent addition of information on direct care staff turnover in Care Compare.39 This may incentivize NHs to find ways to reduce discontinuity in clinicians. However, additional research is first needed to assess resident and caregiver perspectives, as well as those of clinicians involved in resident care. Additionally, different clinical models used in NHs need to be evaluated, as some may be better suited to maintaining quality of care following a clinician change.

This study has limitations. First, it is possible that unobserved factors may have confounded the relationship between discontinuity in clinicians and resident outcomes. However, our model controlled for a large number of resident, clinician, and facility-level characteristics and included NH indicators to address time-invariant differences between facilities. However, there still could be unobserved factors that affected resident outcomes, such as more frequent clinician visits after a clinician change. If residents received more visits after a clinician change, this would likely bias our estimates toward the null. Second, we did not examine variability in the timing of changes in clinicians, which may bias estimates toward no effect. For example, if a resident experienced a clinician change at the beginning of the second year of their long-term care stay, it may have influenced outcomes more than a change at the end of the second year. Third, our study focused on one clinician change, excluding those who experienced multiple clinician changes, which may differentially affect outcomes of residents. Fourth, although we conducted a sensitivity analysis where residents were attributed to clinician practices and not individual clinicians to account for team-based models of care, this may not have captured all clinician visits to residents. For example, under value-based models such as Accountable Care Organizations, these organizations may employ clinicians to help coordinate resident care who do not submit E&M claims. However, the use of these arrangements during periods when a resident experienced a clinician change would bias our results toward no effect. Lastly, our study population was limited to Medicare fee-for-service participants. Other payers, such as Medicare Advantage plans, may employ their own clinicians to supplement care to residents, which may mitigate the impact of a clinician change.

Conclusion

The findings of this national cohort study suggest that clinician discontinuity is associated with increased hospitalizations for ACS conditions among NH residents. The CMS may consider using continuity in clinicians as a measure of NH quality.
