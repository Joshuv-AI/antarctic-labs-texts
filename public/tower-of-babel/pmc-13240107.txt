Reproducibility of Ambulatory Blood Pressure Variability in Untreated Hypertensive Patients
Authors: Aikaterini Lykka, Ionas Papasotiriou, Gerasimos Barlas, Sotiria Spiliopoulou, Damianos Dragonas, Konstantinos Rizogiannis, Efstathios Manios
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSeveral studies have demonstrated that blood pressure variability (BPV) is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. However, the optimal BPV index in terms of prognosis and reproducibility remains unclear. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the reproducibility of ambulatory BPV indices in untreated hypertensive patients. A total of 100 never‐treated hypertensive patients underwent two ambulatory BP recordings within a 4‐week period. For each patient, we computed BPV indices, such as the standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV), weighted SD (wSD), average real variability (ARV), time rate (TR) of BP variation, and dipping for systolic and diastolic BP. Statistical analyses were performed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Cohen's kappa coefficient. Reliability analysis using ICC revealed moderate reproducibility (ICC from 0.540 to 0.715) for 24 h systolic SD, wSD, CV, ARV, TR, dipping, diastolic SD, wSD, CV, and ARV and poor reproducibility (ICC < 0.5) for the 24 h diastolic TR and dipping. The ICC values for daytime BPV revealed moderate reproducibility (ICC from 0.537 to 0.672) for daytime systolic SD, CV, diastolic SD, and CV but poor reproducibility (ICC < 0.5) for daytime systolic ARV, TR, diastolic ARV, and TR. All nighttime BPV indices exhibited poor reproducibility. This study demonstrated that most 24 h and daytime BPV indices showed moderate reproducibility, whereas nighttime BPV indices and diastolic BP dipping showed poor reproducibility. Additionally, systolic BPV was more reproducible than diastolic BPV.

Introduction

Arterial hypertension is the most important modifiable risk factor associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes. However, apart from the average blood pressure (BP) values, continuous BP fluctuations over time may also play a significant role in cardiovascular prognosis, independently of BP levels. Several studies have demonstrated that high BP variability (BPV) is related to the development of target organ damage and an increased risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, although the additive prognostic value of BPV remains unclear [1, 2, 3, 4, 5].

The definition of BPV is based on the duration of the observation window and the monitoring method used for its assessment. More specifically, it can be classified into very short‐term (beat‐to‐beat by continuous invasive or noninvasive BP monitoring), short‐term (reading‐to‐reading within 24 h by ambulatory BP monitoring), mid‐term (day‐by‐day within one week by home BP monitoring), and long‐term (visit‐to‐visit within months or years by office BP measurements) [6]. Moreover, for the quantification of BPV, several indices of BP variation have been derived from simple or more sophisticated mathematical constructs to more accurately quantify the degree of BP fluctuations. Despite the heterogeneity of BPV types and indices used in different studies, the prognostic value of BPV remained significant in most of them.

However, the accumulating evidence regarding BPV in the last decades has not so far allowed the implementation of BPV in clinical practice. Several methodological and clinical issues, such as the best method for assessing BPV, BPV thresholds and goals, and the most effective therapeutic management, need to be clarified. Moreover, there is still uncertainty regarding the optimal BPV index in terms of its prognostic significance and its reproducibility [6]. Various BPV indices have been proposed and studied, each capturing different aspects of BP fluctuations over time. However, their relative effectiveness in predicting cardiovascular outcomes or other clinical endpoints is inconsistent across studies, limiting the consensus on which index best reflects meaningful risk stratification. Additionally, reproducibility issues hinder the establishment of a definitive BPV index. Differences in measurement protocols, monitoring durations, and patient populations contribute to variability in BPV estimates, reducing reliability across repeated assessments.

Aim of the study was to evaluate the reproducibility of ambulatory BPV indices in untreated hypertensive patients.

Methods

Participants

A consecutive series of 594 adults who were referred for evaluation at the Hypertension Center of our Department between October 2023 and January 2025 underwent office BP measurements and 24 h ambulatory BP monitoring. The study population fulfilled the following inclusion criteria: 1) elevated office BP values (systolic BP≥ 140 mmHg or/and diastolic BP≥ 90 mmHg), 2) no previous antihypertensive treatment, 3) no history or clinical evidence of atrial fibrillation during the clinical visit, 4) no history or clinical evidence of hypertension‐related complications (coronary artery disease, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral artery disease), 5) no history of sleep apnea syndrome, and 6) no clinical signs or laboratory evidence of secondary causes of arterial hypertension. Participants were excluded if the number of daytime BP recordings was < 20 or the number of nighttime recordings was < 7 [7]. Risk factors such as diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, smoking history, and body mass index were recorded.

A total of 100 patients fulfilled the abovementioned criteria and underwent two ambulatory BP recordings within a 4‐week period. During this interval, the patients did not receive any antihypertensive treatment. The time of application and recommended daily activities were the same for both monitoring sessions.

These individuals were referred to our laboratory by their primary physicians for evaluation of previous office or home BP alterations and symptoms suggesting possible abnormal BP variations, such as headache, tinnitus, dizziness, fainting, and epistaxis. The study was approved by the local scientific committee, and all participants provided informed consent.

Office and Ambulatory BP Measurements

Office BP was measured in both arms using a validated automated sphygmomanometer (Omron 705IT; Omron Healthcare, Kyoto, Japan) by a physician. A clinically significant difference (> 15 mmHg) was observed in two patients, who were excluded from the study [7]. An appropriate cuff was applied around the nondominant arm, and three BP measurements were taken at 1‐min intervals. The last two recordings were averaged to obtain a single systolic and diastolic office BP value, respectively. During the measurements, the participants remained seated with the arm supported and placed at the level of the heart.

All participants underwent 24 h ambulatory BP monitoring on a usual working day. They were instructed to act and work as usual and to keep their nondominant arm still and relaxed at the side during the measurements. Ambulatory BP was recorded using oscillometric Spacelabs 90207 equipment (SpaceLabs Healthcare, Redmond, WA, USA) [8]. Readings were obtained automatically at 15‐minute intervals throughout the 24 h study period. Daytime and nighttime intervals were defined according to the information provided by the patients. All participants were instructed to rest and sleep at night and maintain their usual activities during the day. None of the study participants were bedridden or hospitalized during the ambulatory BP monitoring. For each patient, we computed the mean 24‐h, daytime, and nighttime SBP and DBP, as well as BPV indices, such as standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV), weighted SD (wSD), average real variability (ARV), and time rate (TR) of BP variation for systolic and diastolic BP [6]. The degree of BP dipping was calculated as (1–daytime BP/nighttime BP) * 100 for both systolic and diastolic BP. According to BP dipping, patients were divided into dippers (>10%) and non‐dippers (< 10%) [6].

Statistical Analysis

A descriptive analysis was performed to present a basic description of the study variables, including the mean, standard deviation, median, and quartiles for quantitative data and absolute and relative frequencies for quantitative data. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used as an index of reproducibility for continuous variables, whereas Cohen's kappa coefficient was used for categorical variables. For reproducibility analysis with ICC, a two‐way mixed‐effects model with absolute agreement was applied, and reliability was estimated based on single measurements. All BP variables were examined as both continuous and categorical variables, whereas for the latter, the 75th percentile was used as a cut‐off point for low and high values. ICC values < 0.50 were considered indicative of poor reproducibility, moderate if between 0.50 and 0.75, good if between 0.75 and 0.90 and excellent if >0.90 [9]. For categorical variables, poor reproducibility was considered if kappa values < 0.20, fair if between 0.21 and 0.40, moderate if between 0.41 and 0.60, substantial if between 0.61 and 0.80, and excellent if >0.80 [10]. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 30.0) for Windows, and statistical significance was set at a value of α = 0.05.

Discussion

This study aimed to examine an understudied domain in hypertension research, specifically, to explore the reproducibility of BPV indices derived from ambulatory BP monitoring in untreated hypertensive patients. Our results showed that most of the 24 h and daytime ambulatory BPV indices presented moderate reproducibility. In contrast, nocturnal BPV and diastolic BP dipping were poorly reproducible.

Several studies have demonstrated that BPV may contribute to cardiovascular risk prediction beyond average BP levels. Ambulatory BPV indices, such as the TR of BP variation, have been associated with subclinical carotid atherosclerosis, left ventricular hypertrophy, and impaired renal function, independent of mean BP values [1, 2, 3]. Similar findings were reported by a meta‐analysis of 12 studies, which showed that systolic SD, wSD, and ARV were related to greater left ventricular mass [11]. In addition, abnormal circadian patterns, such as non‐dipping and rising, were associated with asymptomatic target organ damage [12, 13]. Moreover, in the PAMELA study, diastolic dipping and residual BPV were related to an increased risk of cardiovascular death [14]. The predictive value of ARV, but not SD, for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality was demonstrated in the IDACO study, although the additive prognostic value of BPV was limited [5]. Finally, in untreated hypertensive individuals, nighttime SD of ambulatory BP was an independent predictor of cardiovascular events, death, and all‐cause mortality [4]. Although most trials have shown a significant positive association between BPV and adverse outcomes, there is great heterogeneity in the BPV indices used in these studies. Along with classic distribution BPV indices such as SD and CV, novel BPV indices have been used, including ARV (reflecting differences between successive readings over 24 h), TR of BP variation (reflecting the speed and magnitude of BP changes), and wSD (removing the contribution of nighttime BP fall to 24 h SD), aiming to highlight the index with the greatest prognostic value [6]. However, despite the significant efforts, no data are yet available on the BPV index that has the best performance as a risk factor in terms of prognosis and reproducibility, limiting the use of BPV in daily clinical practice.

Our study demonstrated that nighttime BPV indices had poor reproducibility in general, while daytime and 24 h indices had moderate reproducibility. Specifically, all nighttime BPV indices presented poor reproducibility, whereas most 24 h (systolic SD, CV, wSD, ARV, TR and diastolic CV, wSD, AVR, TR) and daytime BPV indices (systolic and diastolic SD, CV) were moderately reproducible. These findings were surprising and unanticipated. Most ambulatory BP monitoring studies have demonstrated that nocturnal hypertension is a stronger predictor of cardiovascular events than 24 h or daytime hypertension [15, 16, 17]. Furthermore, Palatini et al. showed that nighttime SD was positively and independently associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, with an additive prognostic value on top of mean BP levels [4]. The strong predictive value of nocturnal BP was attributed to its greater reproducibility because nighttime BP is generally measured in sedated conditions, which allows BP recordings in more standardized conditions [16]. However, this hypothesis was not supported by the present findings. The low reproducibility of nighttime BP and its variability is generally attributed to the less standardized and more unstable nature of the sleep period, which makes the nocturnal BP profile inherently more variable across different nights. Moreover, BP during sleep is closely linked to sleep stages and sleep quality. Fragmented sleep, insomnia, arousals, and differences in the time spent in different sleep stages can introduce significant variations in nocturnal BP measurements across ABPM sessions. A recent study by Palatini et al. showed that daytime BP had better agreement over a 3‐month period than nighttime BP, as evidenced by higher coefficients of variation and within‐subject standard deviations for nighttime readings [18]. Our findings suggest that the strong prognostic value of nighttime BP is not due to high reproducibility but rather reflects the pathological state of the cardiovascular and autonomic systems during a period of normally reduced metabolic and physical confounding factors.

Another finding of our study was that systolic BPV was more reproducible compared to diastolic BPV. A study in ten patients with spinal cord injury demonstrated that the reproducibility of systolic BPV was higher than that of diastolic BPV [19]. These results were supported by an analysis of the UK‐TIA and ESC trials, which aimed to examine the reproducibility of visit‐to‐visit BPV [20]. A possible explanation for this finding is that systolic BPV is primarily a reflection of stroke volume and arterial stiffness, whereas diastolic BPV is primarily determined by total peripheral resistance. The fluctuations in systolic BP are heavily driven by changes in heart rate and sympathetic nervous system activity related to physical activity, mental stress, and postural changes. Stroke volume is a major determinant of systolic BP, and its beat‐to‐beat and short‐term fluctuations are often more stable and predictable across monitoring periods when activity levels are standardized. Moreover, arterial stiffness, a key determinant of systolic BP, is a relatively stable structural property of the vascular system over a short‐term period, contributing to a more consistent baseline for systolic BPV. In contrast, diastolic BP is primarily determined by total peripheral resistance, which is controlled minute‐to‐minute by the constriction or dilation of arterioles. The fine‐tuning of arteriolar tone (and thus diastolic BP) is highly responsive to local metabolic demands and rapid, subtle shifts in the autonomic nervous system. These rapid, localized regulatory changes might be less consistent or more subject to random noise and measurement error across BP monitoring than the stroke volume‐driven changes in systolic BP. Thus, the higher reproducibility values of systolic BPV are likely due to its stronger reliance on the relatively more consistent factors of cardiac output and arterial stiffness, whereas diastolic BPV is more susceptible to minute‐to‐minute fluctuations in the highly dynamic and less reproducible total peripheral resistance.

Three limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the number of participants was relatively small; however, they were never‐treated hypertensive patients without atrial fibrillation, conditions that could influence BPV reproducibility. Second, although patients were instructed to perform similar daily activities during the two ambulatory BP monitoring sessions, we have no information on factors potentially affecting BPV, such as physical activity and sleep quality. Finally, 15% of our study population were white‐coat hypertensives, presenting elevated office and normal 24 h BP values. Nevertheless, the were enrolled in the study as they met the inclusion criterion of elevated office BP.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that ambulatory BPV indices exhibit variable reproducibility in untreated hypertensive patients. Most 24 h and daytime BPV indices showed moderate reproducibility, whereas nighttime BPV indices and diastolic BP dipping were poorly reproducible. Additionally, systolic BPV was more reproducible than diastolic BPV. Overall, the moderate reproducibility of most ambulatory BPV indices supports their potential utility in clinical risk stratification; however, further research is needed to standardize measurement protocols, identify optimal indices, and clarify their role in guiding therapeutic interventions.
