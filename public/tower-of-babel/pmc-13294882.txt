A Hand-Based Method for Calculating Microemulsion Phase Equilibrium in Chemical Enhanced Oil Recovery
Authors: Chaofeng Pang, Yizhong Zhang, Lina Zhu, Maolin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microemulsions have been increasingly applied in oil reservoirs for enhanced oil recovery because they can significantly reduce water–oil interfacial tension and increase the capillary number. However, during subsurface flow, microemulsions undergo phase transitions among Winsor I, II, and III as the salinity, water–oil ratio, and surfactant concentration change. The effectiveness of microemulsions in enhancing oil recovery is strongly dependent on their phase type. Therefore, accurately characterizing the phase-transition behavior is crucial for reservoir development design. The Hand method, which is widely used in numerical simulations, faces challenges, such as low computational efficiency and limited applicability, when dealing with phase equilibrium calculations involving microemulsions and excess phases. To overcome these limitations, this study proposes a simplified two-dimensional Cartesian coordinate method for calculating the microemulsion phase equilibrium. This method reduces complexity by mapping pseudocomponent compositions and the nodal lines of pseudoternary phase diagrams at different salinities onto a planar coordinate system. Compared with the Hand and HLD-NAC methods, the proposed approach improves the computational efficiency while maintaining accuracy. Moreover, validation against experimental data demonstrates that the method effectively captures the salinity-, composition-, and surfactant-dependent phase behaviors of microemulsions. This work provides theoretical support and a practical computational tool for the phase identification and numerical simulation of microemulsions in enhanced oil recovery applications.

Introduction

The oil recovery of conventional water flooding is typically less than 40%, leaving a large proportion of remaining oil in the reservoir. Studies indicate that adding surfactants to the aqueous phase can enhance oil displacement efficiency by reducing water–oil interfacial tension, promoting crude oil emulsification, and altering rock surface wettability. Field test results have demonstrated that this method can increase oil recovery by approximately 17% following water flooding. When the surfactant concentration exceeds the critical micelle concentration (CMC), it spontaneously aggregates to form micellar structures. Under these conditions, the surfactant molecules can form a thermodynamically stable microemulsion system with oil and water, capable of solubilizing and emulsifying crude oil, thereby enhancing oil recovery. Currently, microemulsion flooding has undergone field pilot tests in various oilfields, including Daqing, Changqing, and Chateaurenard, demonstrating strong potential for field-scale implementation. ,

Microemulsions are thermodynamically stable systems formed spontaneously from multiple components, such as oil, brine, surfactants, and additives. They typically exhibit transparent or semitransparent characteristics at the macroscopic level. The phase behavior varies with the type and composition of each component. Based on whether an excess phase coexists, microemulsions can be classified as single-phase systems (Winsor IV) or multiphase systems. The latter category includes Winsor I, Winsor II, and Winsor III (the middle-phase microemulsion). Research has shown that Winsor III microemulsions can simultaneously achieve ultralow interfacial tension with both the oil and water phases, thereby exhibiting much higher oil displacement efficiency compared to either Winsor I or Winsor II systems. − Consequently, during microemulsion flooding, the system should be maintained in the Winsor III state to prevent undesired phase transitions into other types. However, under reservoir conditions, microemulsion phase behavior is inherently complex and is jointly controlled by salinity, water–oil ratio (WOR), surfactant concentration, temperature, and the physicochemical properties of the oil and water phases. As these conditions vary, the system may undergo transitions among Winsor I, II, and III states, accompanied by significant changes in phase volume distribution, solubilization capacity, and interfacial properties. In chemical enhanced oil recovery, such phase transitions are particularly important because they directly influence both oil displacement efficiency and the transport behavior of chemical agents in porous media. Therefore, accurate characterization and prediction of microemulsion phase transitions are essential for formulation design, numerical simulation, and reservoir development optimization.

To address this need, a variety of methods have been developed for predicting microemulsion phase behavior, including molecular dynamics simulations, , artificial intelligence-based approaches, , and empirical correlation methods. Among these methods, molecular dynamics simulations can capture the microscopic mechanisms of microemulsion systems, but they require substantial computational resources and long simulation time. Due to limitations in both system size and simulation time scale, they can only probe interfacial phenomena at the nano- to microscale, making their direct application to large-scale reservoir simulations difficult. On the other hand, artificial intelligence methods rely heavily on large data sets for pretraining and lack physical interpretability, which limits their broader applicability. Thus, although both approaches exhibit great potential for theoretical research, they remain constrained to practical engineering applications.

Empirical correlation methods for predicting microemulsion phase behavior can generally be divided into two categories: the Hydrophilic–Lipophilic Deviation Net-Average Curvature (HLD-NAC) method and the Hand method. In 1979, the concept of Hydrophilic–Lipophilic Deviation (HLD) was first proposed by Salager et al. to quantitatively characterize phase behavior in three-phase microemulsion systems. The HLD framework considers variables such as salinity, temperature, oil composition, the type and concentration of alcohols, and surfactant type. The affinity of surfactants is characterized by the chemical potential difference between oil and water. , Acosta integrated the HLD concept with the Net-Average Curvature (NAC) model to develop an equation of state incorporating HLD data for predicting microemulsion phase behavior. Han et al. and Sun et al. applied the HLD-NAC method to characterize microemulsion phase behavior, but did not address middle-phase microemulsion systems. − Magzymov et al. improved microemulsion flash calculations by introducing a variable characteristic length into the HLD-NAC method, thereby enhancing phase behavior prediction in both two-phase and three-phase regions. They also showed that pressure can exert a non-negligible influence on microemulsion phase behavior, although this effect is often neglected in field applications of surfactant–polymer flooding. Based on HLD-NAC theory, Tang et al. systematically investigated the design and displacement performance of middle-phase microemulsions for enhanced oil recovery, demonstrating the practical value of this framework in chemical flooding studies. In recent years, the HLD-NAC method has also been extended to applications such as demulsification and petroleum extraction, further demonstrating its versatility in analyzing phase behavior in complex petroleum systems. , However, the HLD-NAC method still relies on several auxiliary parameters, including the coefficients of the HLD equation and the characteristic length correlation. These parameters must be determined experimentally, a process that is often time-consuming and costly.

Because of these practical limitations, microemulsion phase behavior modeling in mainstream reservoir numerical simulation software still predominantly relies on the Hand empirical model. Originally proposed by Hand in 1939, this model has been incorporated into widely used simulators such as tNavigator and UTCHEM. − In 2016, Lashgari employed the Hand equation to predict microemulsion phase behavior. The simulation results were in good agreement with laboratory displacement experiment data. However, no relevant verification was conducted for the microemulsion phase ratio experiments. In 2020, Janssen et al. used the Hand model to calculate the phase equilibrium in a surfactant-oil–brine system, providing a unified description of phase behavior under varying salinity conditions. Nevertheless, the parameters were only applicable to specific systems, and the phase behavior of different oil-surfactant systems was not investigated. In 2022, Wang et al. enhanced the Hand equation by describing the two-phase boundary with an asymmetric curve, thereby improving the fitting accuracy for two-phase experimental results. However, experimental validation of three-phase transition behavior was lacking. In 2023, Alhotan evaluated numerical simulators developed based on the Hand method and compared them with other chemical flooding simulators in surfactant–polymer flooding simulations at the 1D, 3D, and reservoir scales. However, only the middle-phase microemulsion was considered, without capturing the microemulsion phase transition process, and no validation was performed using laboratory or field data.

Although the Hand method is widely used in engineering practice, it has several inherent limitations in phase behavior prediction and computational efficiency:1.In Winsor III systems, salinity-dependent inversion of the two-phase binodal curve complicates the mathematical formulation, and phase equilibrium calculations require determining the intersection between the composition and binodal curves in a multidimensional concentration space, resulting in high computational cost.2.The Hand model primarily considers the effect of salinity under fixed water–oil ratio conditions and does not account for other factors such as water–oil ratio variation and salt type. Consequently, their applicability to diverse reservoir systems remains limited.

To address these issues, this paper proposes a two-dimensional Cartesian coordinate-based method for calculating microemulsion phase equilibrium. The proposed method reduces the number of unknown variables and simplifies the calculation process for both two-phase and three-phase microemulsion systems by mapping the pseudocomponents and tie-lines from the pseudoternary phase diagram to a two-dimensional coordinate plane. The approach enhances computational efficiency while preserving the predictive accuracy. In addition, the method incorporates the effects of salinity, water–oil ratio, and surfactant concentration on the phase equilibrium. Comparisons between calculated and experimental results show good agreement under varying conditions, validating the capability of the proposed method to accurately reproduce the phase transitions and equilibrium distribution characteristics. This paper provides an efficient and streamlined computational framework for microemulsion phase identification and enhanced oil recovery simulations.

To simplify the calculation of microemulsion phase equilibrium and ensure consistency with existing methods, the following assumptions are made in the calculation process:1.After phase equilibrium is reached in Winsor I, II, and III systems, the excess water and excess oil phases are treated as pure components, assuming no mutual solubility between them.2.The positions of the two-phase and three-phase tie lines in the pseudoternary phase diagram are represented as functions of salinity, while the effects of temperature and pressure are assumed to be implicitly incorporated into the experimentally determined input parameters.3.The results of microemulsion phase equilibrium calculations are determined exclusively by the volume fractions of the pseudocomponents (oil, water, and surfactant) present in the system.4.Microemulsion phase equilibrium is calculated only when the surfactant concentration exceeds the critical micelle concentration (CMC); otherwise, such calculations are not performed.

The proposed method is applicable to conventional chemical flooding reservoir simulations and does not consider the thermal recovery processes. Under these conditions, fluid flow in the reservoir can be regarded as isothermal; therefore, the influence of temperature on microemulsion phase equilibrium is neglected. In addition, the phase equilibrium data used in this study were obtained from laboratory experiments conducted at the temperature and pressure of the target reservoir. Therefore, the input experimental parameters already implicitly include the effects of temperature and pressure, and these factors are not explicitly incorporated into the model. This assumption is consistent with the current industrial requirements for microemulsion numerical simulation.

C SE is expressed as an equivalent sodium chloride (NaCl) concentration, so that multisalt systems can be converted into a common basis for phase equilibrium calculation and comparison. Calculation Process for Microemulsion Phase Equilibrium

Sections and 3.2 focus on the role of salinity in controlling microemulsion phase equilibrium through its effect on the evolution of the pseudoternary phase diagram. Section further illustrates how variations in water–oil ratio, together with surfactant concentration, influence microemulsion phase equilibrium under different phase-diagram types. Specifically, these variations may shift the location of a point in the two-dimensional coordinate system into different regions defined by phase boundaries, thereby changing the calculated phase equilibrium state. Under extreme water–oil ratio conditions, namely when one component fraction in either C o or C w approaches 1, the corresponding y-value in the two-dimensional coordinate system approaches 0. In this case, the surfactant concentration is below the CMC, and microemulsion phase equilibrium calculations are therefore unnecessary. Results and Discussion

The accuracy of the proposed method under low- and high-salinity conditions is evaluated through a comparative analysis using two-phase experimental data. The calculated phase behavior based on the proposed method is compared with laboratory measurements to assess its feasibility and reliability for practical application. On the basis of these comparisons, the influences of different parameters on microemulsion phase equilibrium are further analyzed to evaluate the robustness and performance of the proposed method under multifactor conditions.

Sensitivity Analysis

The preceding section validated the microemulsion equilibrium phase volume distribution under varying salinity levels, water–oil ratios, and surfactant concentrations using laboratory test data, demonstrating the reliability of the proposed method. This section further analyzes the factors influencing the microemulsion phase behavior.

Effect of Surfactant Concentration

Surfactants and additives are treated as the same pseudocomponent. The solubilization and phase equilibrium changes of microemulsions were observed by modifying the concentrations of surfactants or additives, and the results are presented in Figure .

Salinity scanning results at different surfactant concentrations.

Figure presents the salinity scan results for different surfactant concentrations. Each system transitions from Winsor I to Winsor III and then to Winsor II as the salinity increases, indicating consistent phase behavior trends. However, with an increasing surfactant concentration, the salinity range for Winsor III gradually narrows, while the volume fraction of the middle-phase microemulsion increases. This occurs because higher surfactant concentrations enhance the enrichment of surfactants and additives at the interface and strengthen interactions among the surfactant, the oil phase, and the water phases, thereby improving microemulsion solubilization. Consequently, systems with higher surfactant concentrations form a greater proportion of the middle-phase microemulsion over the entire salinity range. Moreover, because of the heightened sensitivity of high surfactant concentrations to salinity variations, the effective salinity range of Winsor III microemulsions narrows.

Effect of Salt Type

After fixing the water–oil ratio and surfactant concentration and maintaining the same cationic charge concentration of the electrolyte in the system, the concept of C SE was used to investigate the effects of different salt types on the microemulsion phase state. The results are presented in Figure .

Salinity scanning results for different salt types.

Figure demonstrates that although different salt types exhibit similar trends in microemulsion phase evolution, significant differences exist in the corresponding Winsor III salinity range. At the same cationic charge concentration, potassium salts require lower electrolyte concentrations for Winsor III microemulsion formation and display a narrower salinity window, indicating that K+ exerts a stronger influence on the microemulsion system than Na+. This phenomenon primarily results from the fact that although potassium and sodium belong to the same group of elements, potassium’s smaller hydration radius compared to that of sodium leads to greater ion activity and consequently stronger effects on microemulsion formation, thereby requiring lower salt concentrations. In addition, when the cation type and valence state are identical, the anion type also affects microemulsion phase equilibrium. As shown in Figure , compared with the NaCl system, the Na2SO4 system requires a higher Na+ concentration to form Winsor III microemulsions. From the perspective of anion valence, SO4 2– carries a higher charge than Cl–, resulting in higher ionic strength. Consequently, a higher Na+ concentration is necessary to form an equivalent microemulsion phase state. Therefore, the proposed method effectively characterizes the influence of different salt types on microemulsion phase behavior and clarifies the roles of cationic and anionic effects in determining the salinity window of Winsor III microemulsions.

Effect of Oil–Water Ratio

Figure illustrates the effects of the surfactant concentration and oil–water ratio on microemulsion phase behavior under constant salinity.

Comparison of microemulsion phase behavior at different surfactant concentrations and oil–water ratios.

As illustrated in Figure , under constant salinity conditions, the system’s phase state transitions from Winsor II to Winsor III and subsequently to Winsor I as the oil–water ratio increases. At low oil–water ratios, the microemulsion completely solubilizes the oil phase, leaving no excess oil in the system. However, with further increases in the oil–water ratio, when the oil content surpasses the microemulsion’s maximum solubilization capacity, an excess oil phase emerges. This phenomenon primarily occurs because an increased oil phase content alters the distribution ratio of alcohols between the oil and water phases, thereby reducing the relative mass fraction of alcohols in the interfacial layer. The subsequent accumulation of surfactants in the interfacial layer diminishes the microemulsion’s solubilization capacity, leading to gradual saturation of oil phase solubilization. Thus, the excess oil phase appears progressively with increasing oil–water ratios. Furthermore, at constant salinity, the quantity of oil required for both the formation and disappearance of the middle-phase microemulsion increases with higher surfactant concentrations. This mechanism operates through the enrichment of more alcohol molecules in the interfacial layer at elevated surfactant concentrations, which enhances the oil phase’s solubilization capacity. Consequently, the system requires greater oil content to satisfy the conditions for middle-phase microemulsion formation.

Conclusion

To address the limitations of the Hand method in microemulsion phase equilibrium calculations, this study proposes a two-dimensional Cartesian coordinate phase equilibrium calculation method based on the Hand method. By mapping pseudoternary compositions and tie lines onto a two-dimensional coordinate system, the proposed method improves computational efficiency while maintaining accuracy, as confirmed by experimental validation. The main conclusions are as follows:1.While preserving the physical assumptions of the Hand method, the proposed method reduces the number of unknown variables in microemulsion phase equilibrium calculations by employing an equivalent transformation between the ternary coordinates and planar coordinates. This numerical reconstruction strategy avoids multivariate coupled solutions and iterative computations, thereby decreasing the computational burden of phase-equilibrium calculations in numerical simulations.2.Through theoretical derivation and comparative analysis, the proposed method is shown to be numerically consistent with the Hand method in both the two-phase and three-phase regions regarding phase volume fraction calculations. This finding indicates that the proposed method provides equivalent phase equilibrium characterization to the Hand method and is, therefore, reliable for reservoir numerical simulation applications.3.To address the limitation of the Hand method in characterizing only salt concentration, the proposed method was validated against laboratory experimental data under varying salt types, water–oil ratios, and surfactant concentrations. The results show good agreement between the calculated and experimental data, confirming that this method successfully incorporates laboratory findings into reservoir numerical simulations while offering an improved practical applicability.4.A comparison with the HLD-NAC method under varying water–oil ratios and surfactant concentrations shows that the proposed method maintains a high degree of computational consistency under different pressure conditions. In addition, the proposed method requires fewer experimentally determined input parameters and involves a simpler computational procedure, making it well-suited for rapid phase equilibrium calculations in microemulsion numerical simulations.
