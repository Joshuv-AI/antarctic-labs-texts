Using Systemic Functional Linguistics in the Clinical Management of Neurogenic Communication Disorders: An Introduction and Worked Example
Authors: Brent Archer, Marion C Leaman, Ryan S Husak, Christa Akers, Rimke Groenewold, Louise Keegan, Zaneta Mok, Liz Spencer, Eleanor Gulick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe ability to converse with others enables us to participate in everyday life. People with neurogenic communication disorders (NCDs), such as aphasia or traumatic brain injury (TBI), often experience difficulties engaging in conversation. While Systemic Functional Linguistics (SFL) has been used to analyze conversations involving people with NCDs, its application to clinical assessment and intervention remains limited.AimsThe primary aim of this paper is to make the case for Systemic Functional Linguistics as a clinically useful framework for understanding, assessing, and supporting conversation in people with neurogenic communication disorders.MethodWe provide an overview of SFL and introduce an SFL‐based framework, Discourse Structure Analysis. We review previous SFL‐based studies of adult communication disorders and also describe the USeFuL Project, a multinational collaboration focused on developing SFL‐based clinical tools. Finally, we present a worked clinical example illustrating how DSA can support detailed analysis of conversations involving people with and without aphasia.Main ContributionsSFL‐based frameworks can be used systematically to guide clinical decision‐making when providing services to people with neurogenic communication disorders. By enabling detailed, coded analyzes of naturalistic conversations, these approaches reveal patterns of participation that are often missed in standard assessments. Moreover, SFL can inform the development of meaningful, measurable therapy goals and the development of individualized care plans.ConclusionsSFL offers a powerful, context‐sensitive framework for analyzing and supporting communication in people with NCDs. By applying SFL in clinical contexts, clinicians can build more precise, functional, and person‐centered interventions that enhance real‐world communication.WHAT THIS PAPER ADDS What is already known on this subject Systemic Functional Linguistics (SFL) is a theory of communication which examines how language users make choices to convey meaning in specific contexts. Over the past 30 years, researchers have used systemic functional linguistics (SFL)‐ based frameworks to study real‐world conversations involving people with neurogenic communication disorders (NCDs). Researchers have investigated interactions that occur in a number of settings, focusing primarily on health care contexts. Two major themes are evident within this literature: control over how conversations play out tends to be exercised predominantly by people without communication disorders, and all participants in an interaction contribute to communicative success. What this study adds to existing knowledge We are the first group to argue for SFL‐based studies that provide clinicians with conceptual frameworks and theory‐informed tools they can use during all stages of clinical care including assessment, goal setting and treatment. As a first step in meeting this need, we offer an overview of discourse structure analysis (DSA, a form of SFL) and provide a worked example of how clinicians could use DSA when providing services to people with neurogenic communication disorders. Finally, we introduce the Using Systemic Functional Linguistics to Understand Neurogenic Communication Disorders (USeFuL) Project, a collaboration founded to pursue a clinical, SFL‐based research agenda. Because SFL‐based studies always focus on data gathered in everyday settings, this discipline could be used to develop highly functional and relevant assessments, goal setting procedures and treatments. The worked example provided in this paper illustrates some key ideas about how clinicians might go about applying DSA (a form of SFL) when working in conjunction with clients to develop and carrying out plans of care. Future studies by the USeFuL Project will build on current work and expand the SFL‐informed tools and approaches available to clinicians.

Background

Being able to talk and hold conversation with others facilitates participation in most domains of life. Chatting to family and friends, asking a barista for a cup of coffee, speaking to a mental health counselor, discussing your financial situation with your accountant, having a serious talk with your intimate partner about your relationship and many other forms of conversational interaction allow us to function as fully‐fledged members of our families, workplaces and communities. Our identities, the web of relationships each of us exists within and the social world we have created are, in part, products of our ability to interact with others via conversation.

Unsurprisingly, people and families living with aphasia have identified improved conversational abilities as important outcomes for therapy (Wallace et al. 2017; Worrall et al. 2011). Accordingly, researchers working within the field of communication disorders have developed a range of approaches designed to improve the conversation abilities of people with neurogenic communication disorders (NCDs). One theory of linguistics that has informed studies of conversations involving people with NCDs is Systemic Functional Linguistics (SFL), a data driven, comprehensive theory of how human beings use language to communicate with one another (Halliday 1978). A number of previous SFL‐based projects have used this theory to interpret and understand a particular aspect of conversational interactions in which people with NCDs participate. Research has addressed interactions between nurses and three people with aphasia (Hersh et al. 2016), how married couples in which one person has aphasia communicate during problem solving tasks (Husak 2018), the roles assumed by people with dementia in conversational exchanges contribute to an individual's communication of ‘self’ to their interlocutor (Keegan et al. 2021, Keegan et al. 2022), the repair work that follows communication breakdowns in conversations involving people with dementia (Sluis et al. 2019) and adapting pre‐existing measures of support and participation in conversation designed for use with people with dementia (Mok et al. 2021).

Aim

The primary aim of this paper is to make the case for SFL as a clinically useful framework for understanding, assessing, and supporting conversation in people with NCDs. Specifically, we aim to introduce clinicians and researchers to an SFL‐based analytic framework, Discourse Structure Analysis (DSA). We also introduces the USeFuL Project, a collaborative initiative to develop SFL‐informed clinical tools and interventions.

Main Contribution

Section 1: What Is SFL?

SFL was developed in the early sixties by Michael Halliday, a language teacher and linguist (Eggins and Slade 2004). He was concerned with exploring how people in real life situations use language to communicate ideas and build relationships. Answering this question is the focus of SFL (Martin 2016).

In contrast to various other approaches to understanding human communication, one of the core tenets of SFL is that linguistic theories should be data driven. Accordingly, every aspect of SFL is based on data that linguists have gathered by watching people communicate with one another in a range of contexts, with a strong emphasis on everyday, common situations.

The ‘systemic’ part of SFL is about the components we draw on when using language; each language has a range of resources such as words, morphemes, phrases, clauses and texts that speakers and listeners use to build meaning (Halliday and Matthiessen 2004). The ‘functional’ part of SFL concerns itself with understanding how we use these resources to carry out three main communicative functions: (1) ideational functions, which are how we use language to communicate ideas about the world (basically, to talk about who did what to whom); (2) interpersonal functions, used to build relationships between ourselves and others; and (3) textual functions, to create pieces of language (clauses, paragraphs, texts) that fit together in logical, cohesive ways (Halliday and Matthiessen 2004).

Instead of focusing exclusively on grammatical rules to be followed, systemic functional linguists view language as a tool for communication and social interaction within a specific situation (or ‘context’). Context, according to SFL, includes both the immediate physical environment and the broader social and cultural setting in which communication occurs. According to SFL, language users consider variables such as the social roles and relationships of the participants, the topics that are covered in an interaction, and features of the communication channel being used (i.e., is the communication a monologue or dialogue, written or spoken, taking place face‐to‐face or over a distance, etc.). This contextual orientation allows SFL to analyze how language users make choices based on their awareness of the context, shaping their language to suit the demands of different situations.

Many NCDs are characterized by pragmatic difficulties such as challenges with initiating topics, maintaining coherence, interpreting social cues, or using language flexibly in contexts. SFL‐based assessment and treatment approaches can help clinicians identify and treat these difficulties because SFL focuses not just on grammatical form, but on how language functions in real‐world interaction. By analyzing how individuals use (or struggle to use) language to enact speech roles, build interpersonal relationships, and manage the flow of discourse, SFL enables clinicians to pinpoint specific pragmatic breakdowns and understand how they affect participation in everyday conversation.

Section 2: A Framework for Applying SFL in NCDs: Discourse Structure Analysis

Discourse Structure Analysis

According to SFL, conversation is a ‘give and take’ exchange‐based activity. Speakers use resources from each of the three functions (ideational, interpersonal and textual) to construct turns that help them ‘do’ different things or achieve different goals in the conversation. For example, speakers can compose turns that are designed to get an interlocutor to do something, give them an item or provide information or they can compose turns that offer an item or information. Halliday argued that these four actions (commanding, offering, asking questions, making statements) represent the basic speech roles or speech functions assumed by speakers during conversation (Halliday and Matthiessen 2004). These four basic functions are especially obvious in service‐based encounters, such as when someone talks to a service worker in a store in order to buy something.

In the early 2000s, two linguists, Suzanne Eggins and Diane Slade expanded Halliday's basic speech function framework in order to further describe and account for the processes they encountered when analyzing conversational data. Their efforts led to the development of Discourse Structure Analysis, which stems from SFL and is an analysis framework which helps us to understand how conversations are built over time by at least two people.

According to Eggins and Slade, conversations are composed of exchanges. Each exchange begins with a turn in which a speaker makes an opening move, or initiates a new subject. Other participants in the conversation will then produce turns in which they make various kinds of follow‐up moves that all relate to the initial opening move. Exchanges continue until the participants finish talking about the proposition offered in the opening move. Once all the ‘business’ related to the opening move has been dealt with, one of the participants makes another opening move and the exchange cycle begins again.

What Are the Different Types and Functions of Moves?

Eggins and Slade (2004) developed guidelines for identifying moves within casual conversation. These guidelines are based on the function that the move performs within the conversation. Here we provide an overview of the most important moves. Figure 1 in the Supplementary Materials provides a graphical representation of the categories and sub‐categories moves can be divided into.

Move category and type network.

Opening moves introduce new subjects or ideas for participants to talk about with and initiate a sequence of moves that relate to these new ideas (in other words, topics in conversation usually change when opening moves happen). In conversation, opening moves may take the form of statements of opinion or fact, or questions about opinions or facts.

Two examples of opening moves are provided below:

Cilantro is awful! (statement of opinion)

Do we have any cilantro left? (question about a fact)

Once an opening move has been made, speakers are faced with choices about how to engage with the opening move. As illustrated below, the current speaker may choose to continue speaking and produce more clauses that expand on their opening move. In DSA terminology, this is called a prolonging move:

Cilantro is awful…because it tastes of soap.

If a speaker expands on a thought they produced in their own opening move after someone else has produced a turn, they have made a continuing move:

Speaker A: Cilantro is awful (statement of opinion)

Speaker B: Uhmhm

Speaker A: Because it tastes of soap

Monitoring moves are made when speakers assess an audience's level of engagement. Typically, they take the form of brief utterances, sometimes called ‘tag questions’:

Speaker A: Some have a gene which makes cilantro taste soapy (statement of fact)

Speaker A: right? (monitoring move)

In conversations with people with aphasia, speakers who do not have a NCD will sometimes produce ‘test’ questions concerning information the asker already knows. Within the DSA framework, these questions could be considered monitoring moves, since these turns do not obtain new information but instead assess the recipient's knowledge or ability to produce certain utterances:

Speaker A: Who did we see at the restaurant last night? (monitoring move)

Speaker B: Stanley and Cynthia

A speaker might choose to say something in reaction to an opening move. In DSA, this is called a reacting move.

Two sub‐types of reacting moves exist: responding and rejoinding moves. Responding moves tend to bring the conversational sequences to an end. Below we provide examples of four types of responding moves: first, a question of fact is followed by an answering move; second a statement of opinion is followed by an agreeing move; third a statement of fact is followed by a registering move (registering moves are similar to the short utterances that are called ‘minimal turns’ such as ‘mhmm’ responses); and fourth, a statement of opinion is followed by a developing move, in which the speaker builds on and expands a thought offered in an opening move. In all four instances the follow‐up wraps up the interactional ‘business’ started by the opening move.

Different types of moves have different kinds and levels of impact on the ongoing conversation. Opening moves introduce new ideas for participants to talk about and thus play an important role in building on and introducing topics. Reacting moves, by comparison, are usually made as moves that follow‐up on opening moves. These moves thus have less potential for moving the conversation in a particular direction. Within a conversation, the people who make opening moves have more active control over what is talked about while the people who make reacting moves follow the lead established by opening move makers. If one person makes many more opening moves than others, then the conversation may start to look one‐sided, with one person exercising a lot of control over what is talked about.

Section 3: Studies Using SFL‐Based Methods to Examine Interactions of People With NCDs

Aphasia

Over the past 30 years, researchers have employed variations of Halliday's basic speech functions (command, offer, question, statement) in order to understand how NCDs may impact dialogic language use. In one of the earliest applications of SFL to aphasiology, Ferguson (1992) examined how speakers with aphasia expressed interpersonal meaning during interactions with others. Building on Halliday's four speech functions in this pre‐DSA era, Ferguson (1992), employed nine speech functions which she developed for this work: giving, demanding, accepting, giving on demand, requesting or providing goods and services, requesting or providing information, calling, greeting, and expressing oneself. She demonstrated that despite lexical and syntactic errors, people with aphasia could use a variety of speech functions to express interpersonal meanings.

Multiple studies have compared the type and number of speech functions used in conversations between PWA and familiar and unfamiliar communication partners across a variety of conversational genres and settings (Armstrong et al. 2013; Husak 2018; Akers 2022; Hersh et al. 2016; Hersh et al. 2018; Ferguson and Elliot 2001; Leaman and Archer 2022). The findings from these studies have important clinical impact because they provide valuable insight to potential treatment targets regarding critical issues like participation and agency during everyday interactions.

For instance, the communicative behaviours of married couples, in which one person has aphasia shows that spouses support one another's ideas, although the person without aphasia holds the floor more often and provides more elaborate ideas during arguments (Armstrong et al. 2013) and in a joint decision‐making task (Husak 2018). Similarly, in casual conversation with familiar partners, the person with aphasia makes fewer opening moves than the partner (i.e., leading to less control over the topic), with this disparity increasing as the severity of aphasia increases (Akers 2022).

DSA has also been used to evaluate interactions between healthcare providers and individuals with aphasia. Hersh et al. (2016) reported that in the acute care hospital setting, when interacting with two participants with aphasia (1 with moderate‐severe Wernicke's; 1 with global aphasia), the nursing staff made most of the opening moves in the conversations, maintained control of the topic on care activities, and offered few opportunities for turns other than simple, closed‐ended responses. The authors described this as disempowering to the individuals with aphasia. This was especially noticeable when compared to the nurses’ much less restrictive interactions with an individual with a right hemisphere stroke (i.e., no aphasia), thereby allowing him opportunities to use a greater variety of speech functions, resulting in greater agency and control of the conversations.

Interactions between speech‐language pathologists (SLPs) and people with aphasia have been evaluated using DSA as well. Similar to the findings with nurses, in the inpatient hospital setting, SLPs tend to control the topic of the conversation and make twice as many speaking moves as the individuals with aphasia (Hersh et al. 2018). An investigation conducted by Ferguson and Elliot (2001), using ESA as the method of analysis, also showed that clinicians held greater power in therapy session interactions by taking the role of primary knower more frequently than clients. In addition, the clinicians exclusively used dynamic turns (in which they identified and supported repair of communication breakdowns), whereas the people with aphasia did not. The authors suggested these behaviours aligned with roles of teacher and learner expected in the format of the sessions analyzed.

However, outside of assessment activities, Hersh et al. (2018) noted that SLPs tended to take a more naturalistic approach during conversations with people with aphasia. These segments of sessions involved humour and the SLP introduced topics of interest to the person with aphasia, behaviours which shifted the balance of power from clinician‐centered to client‐centered interactions. This observation is consistent with findings of another study, in which SLPs were trained to take a social stance during conversations with people with aphasia, including allowing the person time to repair speaking errors (Leaman and Archer 2022). In these less clinical interactions/more social interactions with SLPs, the people with aphasia made opening moves for 40% of their self‐repaired utterances (Leaman and Archer 2022).

Traumatic Brain Injury

Togher et al. 1997 employed a SFL‐based system known as Exchange Structure Analysis (ESA) to analyze conversations of individuals with TBI and various partners (clinicians, bus drivers, police, parents), and included control participants as well. The authors examined the roles that the individuals assumed in the conversations based on how they contributed to exchanges (i.e., as primary or secondary knowers and as users of dynamic moves). ESA revealed that communication partners interacted differently with controls as compared to individuals with TBI. They used more requests for information (especially mothers and clinicians) allowing controls to assume increased information‐giving roles than those with TBI (Togher et al. 1997).

This and subsequent work highlights the contributions and influence the communication partner (Togher et al. 1997; Tu et al. 2011) and the context (Togher 2000b; Togher et al. 2006; Keegan and Müller 2022) may have on exchanges for individuals with TBI. While several studies demonstrate that people with TBI can and often do take all roles involved in exchanges (i.e., primary knower; secondary knower; user of dynamic turns) (Bogart et al. 2012; Guo and Togher 2008; Jorgensen and Togher 2009), it is also reported that despite this ability, people with TBI may at times be cast into a less powerful role by the behaviour of the partner (Togher et al. 1997).

However, additional work using ESA as a tool for analysis also highlights how communication partners can support communication skills of people with TBI with conversational scaffolding. For example, Jorgensen and Togher (2009) identified how individuals with TBI were less successful in monologic tasks than in jointly‐produced narrative tasks with friends. Other ESA research demonstrates how familiar partners (i.e., friends; Bogart et al. 2012) and trained partners (Sim et al. 2013; Rietdijk et al. 2019) can facilitate the success of interactions with individuals with TBI. For instance, Bogart et al. (2012) reported that the familiar partners of people with TBI tended to use dynamic moves to identify communication breakdowns, thereby scaffolding the person's repair. Rietdijk et al. (2019) used ESA as a primary outcome measure examining the effect of communication partner training for partners of people with TBI. Although a significant treatment effect was not reported for the ESA measures, both people with TBI and their partners reported positive changes in communication and masked raters noted improved post‐treatment conversational participation of the individuals with TBI.

Lastly, recent work examines ESA in the context of identity and how the roles assumed in conversational exchanges contribute to an individual's communication of ‘self’ to their interlocutor (Keegan et al. 2021, Keegan et al. 2022) and how identities can vary across contexts (Keegan and Müller 2022).

Other Populations With NCDs

At this time, SFL and its adaptations (e.g., DSA, ESA) have rarely been applied to research involving people with NCDs outside of aphasia and TBI. However, one study used ESA to examine the communication of individuals with mild and moderate dysarthria occurring secondary to TBI (Guo and Togher 2008). This study reports that reduced intelligibility due to the individuals’ dysarthria did not impact their ability to use a full range of exchange structure moves (i.e., primary knower, secondary knower, user of dynamic moves) (Guo and Togher 2008). Another study examined conversational interaction in small group conversations consisting of two women with dementia of the Alzheimer's type (DAT) and two SLP graduate students (Müller and Mok 2012). The authors used an SFL‐based analysis of endearment terms (e.g., ‘honey’; ‘sugar’) used by the two participants to refer to one another, describing how their lexical choices expressed interpersonal attitudes (i.e., SFL's interpersonal function; Halliday & Matthiesen, 2014). In addition, the participants used a variety of moves from the SFL network, and contributed greater proportions of opening and continuing moves than the graduate students. The authors described these findings as showing that the participants maintained control over conversational interactions, stating that these interpersonal skills appeared to be a relative strength late into the disease on par with the relatively preserved syntactic skills often seen in DAT. Similarly Mok and Muller (2014) looked at how people with dementia managed communication breakdowns in their interactions and relationships. within a residential care setting.

Section 4: The USeFuL Project

The Using SFL to Understand Neurogenic Communication Disorders (USeFuL) Project was founded in 2022. The two central aims of the USeFul Project are: (i). to spread awareness of current and future SFL informed research and (ii). to produce user‐friendly, SFL‐based resources that practicing clinicians can use when providing services to clients with a wide range of communication disorders.

Thus far, SFL has been applied to interactions involving people with a range of NCDs including non‐fluent aphasia (Archer et al. 2024; Gulick et al. 2024; Husak et al. 2024), fluent aphasia (Akers et al. 2024; Ferguson 2017), dementia of the Alzheimer's type (Müller and Mok 2012), dysarthria (Guo and Togher 2008), TBI (e.g., Togher et al. 1997; Keegan et al. 2022; Krautkremer et al. 2024) and Parkinson's disease (García et al. 2018). We want to expand the clinical populations represented within the SFL literature on conversation, so we plan to conduct SFL informed investigations involving people with a wide range of clinical histories and diagnosis. People with mild aphasia, people with progressive forms of aphasia, and people with aphasia who speak more than one language are some of the groups we plan to focus on in future studies.

In order to achieve this aim, we (and other researchers) can build on the solid foundations that have already been established in the clinical SFL literature. We aim to expand on current work and use new forms of technology to make assessment tools more readily usable in clinical settings. Future projects that the UseFuL Project and other researchers could focus on might include investigations of whether high quality speech‐to‐text transcription systems, computer assisted coding or even transcriptionless coding can reduce the time needed to produce and code transcripts. The work of the USeFuL project and other SF‐linguists can expand the current SFL research base to develop user‐friendly resources relevant to all stages of the therapeutic process including assessment, goal‐setting and intervention. More information about the UseFuL Project can be found at useful‐discourse.info.

Ben and Margo

Background

Ben is a 66 year‐old man who had a stroke 2 years, 1 month prior to providing the conversation samples analyzed here. He obtained an aphasia quotient score of 47.8 on the Western Aphasia Battery (Kertesz 2006), indicating severe Broca's aphasia. He scored at the 71st percentile on the Communication Activities of Daily Living ‐3 (Holland et al. 2018), and in the severe range for nonlinguistic cognition on the Cognitive Linguistic Quick Test—Plus (Helms‐Estabrooks 2017). He typically spoke in short phrases, 1–3 words long, and often used overlearned comments such as ‘oh really’ and ‘I like it’. He demonstrated fair to good comprehension during personally relevant, everyday conversation, with occasional need for the partner to repeat verbal information, or use other techniques such as slowed rate of speech or writing to support comprehension.

Ben's primary communication partner is his wife, Margo (60 years old). She reported that he did not ‘initiate’ enough during conversation and she felt that interactions with him were one‐sided. She stated that she typically brings up new topics, and that he rarely provides any new or additional information unless she specifically asks him to do so. She also stated that she feels the quality of their discussions have declined since his stroke and this has in turn had a negative impact on their relationship.

Some differences between Ben and Margo's communication are immediately obvious when we review the transcript. Margo's turns appear to be longer than Ben's, and importantly, she seems to play a more central role in selecting and developing topics. However, it may be difficult for observers to articulate why Margo seems to make a much more substantial contribution to the conversation, or to suggest methods for empowering Ben to be a more active interactant.

Discourse Structure Analysis can help researchers and clinicians more comprehensively describe and understand the dynamics of this casual conversational interaction. If we use the system described in Section 2 to code the moves made by each speaker during their turns in the transcript provided, we can see a clear pattern emerging. Margo produces real questions to which she does not appear to know the answer (line 15) and monitoring move ‘test questions’ to which she already knows the answer (line 1), possibly in an attempt to assess Ben's knowledge or production abilities (which she later stated was indeed the case). Additionally, she uses a number of turns to offer statements of opinion (Line 42). According to the DSA framework, most of Margo's turns are structured so as to allow her to make opening moves, while restricting Ben to reacting moves

Ben, on the other hand, tends to produce much shorter turns. He either answers Margo's questions (Line 4) or produces very brief utterances consisting of short words (‘yeah’; Line 17) or non‐word vocalizations (‘um’; Line 49). In the DSA system, direct answers to questions are classified as reacting moves. Similarly, brief turns such as those Ben produces in lines 17 and 49 are labelled as a type of reacting move called a ‘register’ move. Register moves enable listeners to show that they are still paying attention to a speaker producing a story or series of related turns without adding any new content to the conversation.

Different types of moves (such as opening moves or reacting moves) play different roles in conversation. Since opening moves start exchanges and listeners usually respond to a given type of opening move by choosing a reacting move from a finite set of options, opening moves strongly influence the future actions of all interactants, especially those who did not make the opening move. Further, because opening moves contain new propositions for interactants to engage with, speakers use opening moves to introduce and develop new topics in conversation.

By contrast, reacting moves play a different role in conversation. Reacting moves are always made in relation to a previous opening move. Since reacting moves ordinarily align with the preceding opening move, they build on the topics introduced by previous opening moves but cannot introduce new matters for interactants to discuss. Certain reacting moves contain no real content; since they are composed of short tokens like ‘yes’ or ‘um’, register moves signal that the speaker is still attending to other participants but do not contain words that have specific meanings in the same way as nouns, verbs and adjectives do.

If one person in a conversation makes most of the opening moves and their partner tends to produce only reacting moves, it is possible to describe the partner who makes most of the opening moves as being more active in the conversation, and the other person as being more passive. As Table 2 indicates, there is a significant imbalance between the opening moves made by Margo and Ben, with Margo's share of the opening moves far outweighing Ben's. A similar situation is evident in the distribution of reacting moves. Ben used the overwhelming majority of his turns to make reacting moves while Margo made many fewer reacting moves. Margo's perception that Ben does not initiate aligns well with the analysis conducted here. Further, this analysis allowed Ben, Margo, and their clinician to understand how Margo's choices influenced the interaction and contributed to Ben's limited initiation of new topics. By choosing opening moves throughout the interaction in an attempt to get Ben ‘to talk’, she was unknowingly shifting him to playing a reacting role throughout the conversation.

While it may be possible for clinicians to appreciate that one partner tends to dominate conversations, we believe highly specific data about different move types is clinically useful and supports a comprehensive understanding of the interaction to best inform intervention. First, a system which allows clinicians to code and tally every utterance in a conversation is likely to yield more objective results than instruments which require them to form a global impression (and objective, numerical metrics tend to be favoured by insurers and funding agencies). Second, because SFL provides granular, in‐depth information about the kinds of moves made by each partner in conversation, it is possible to build goals, elements of a care plan and help clients change their behaviour. In this instance, a global assessment that Margo has more control over the conversation does not provide details about the specific behaviours SLPs could target in order to change this pattern. In fact, despite Margo and Ben having discussed their concern about Ben not participating in conversation with several SLPs during two years of therapy, Margo's behaviour had never been identified or addressed as a contributing factor until DS analysis was conducted with one of the authors of the current paper.

A fuller analysis would indicate precisely what actions give rise to the overall impression (in this case, Margo makes many more opening moves than Ben). Detailed information about what behaviours both partners should work on are needed if lasting changes in interaction are desired. Third, tallies of different move types provide rich data points that can be tracked over time to determine if intervention is effective. Finally, while some seasoned clinicians may be able to conduct global assessments, some SLPs may find it difficult to independently complete such a comprehensive evaluation of conversational interactions. A step‐by‐step approach for analyzing complex phenomena such as conversation may be a tool welcomed for its helpful guidance by many clinicians.

Conclusion

SFL provides a data‐driven, comprehensive framework that SLPs can use to examine the language and communication of neurogenic populations. We believe that by applying this field‐tested, real‐world focused approach to language use, we will better understand the linguistic strengths and skills of individuals with neurogenic communication difficulties. Then, we will be able to develop interventions that draw on these strengths and skills to facilitate enhanced and successful conversational interactions. Conversation is the most common and important form of communication that people with and without communication disorders engage in on a daily basis, so finding effective ways to empower our clients to have better conversations will help them participate more fully in all domains of living.
