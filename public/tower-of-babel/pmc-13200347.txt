Leocarpinolide B alleviates epithelial tubular mitochondrial dysfunction via macrophage exosomal miR-204-5p/TFAM axis in AKI-CKD transition
Authors: Yongxin Chen, Tian Zhang, Guanding Zhao, Yawen Yao, Wenwen Wu, Guiting Wang, Birong Yuan, Sentai Yu, Shihui Zhang, Mingjun Shi, Bing Guo, Kegang Linghu, Hua Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe transition from acute kidney injury (AKI) to chronic kidney disease (CKD) represents a distinct clinical syndrome critically driven by mitochondrial dysfunction. Emerging evidence suggests that exosome-dependent crosstalk between macrophages and renal tubular epithelial cells (RTECs) facilitates AKI-CKD transition. Leocarpinolide B (LB), a bioactive compound derived from the traditional Chinese medicine Siegesbeckiae Herba, exhibits potent anti-inflammatory activity in macrophages. However, the renoprotective role of LB against macrophage-mediated AKI-CKD transition and the underlying mechanisms involved remain unclear. In this study, we demonstrated that lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated macrophages exacerbated mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammation in RTECs, whereas LB suppressed inflammatory crosstalk between macrophages and RTECs. Strikingly, comparable attenuation of RTECs injury was observed using isolated exosomal fractions. Furthermore, inhibition of exosome secretion alleviated mitochondrial impairment and suppressed inflammation-fibrosis progression in RTECs. miRNA sequencing revealed a significantly elevation of miR-204-5p in serum exosomes from AKI patients. Notably, LB treatment counteracted this pathogenic miRNA upregulation in injured RTECs via an exosome-mediated pathway, thereby directly linking its reno-protective function to the exosomal miR-204-5p modulation. Subsequent bioinformatics analysis and luciferase reporter assays identified mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) as a direct target of miR-204-5p. Functionally, overexpression of miR-204-5p in RTECs abrogated the protective effects of LB against mitochondrial dysfunction and cellular injury, whereas miR-204-5p knockdown synergistically enhanced LB-mediated renoprotection. In a murine folic acid (FA)-induced AKI-CKD model, the therapeutic efficacy of LB was substantially enhanced by a hyaluronic acid (HA)-functionalized liposomal nanoplatform, which facilitated targeted LB delivery to CD44-overexpressing injured kidneys. Collectively, our findings reveal a novel mechanism by which LB mitigates AKI-CKD progression by inhibiting macrophages-derived exosomal miR-204-5p, which in turn directly upregulates TFAM expression, restores mitochondrial function, and interrupts the inflammation-fibrosis axis in RTECs, offering a potentially beneficial therapeutic strategy for AKI-CKD.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04341-1.Keywords: Leocarpinolide B, Exosome, MicroRNA, AKI-CKD transition, Macrophage-tubular epithelial cells crosstalk

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is characterized by an abrupt deterioration of glomerular filtration function, manifested with elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and serum creatinine (Scr) levels [1, 2], posing severe health burdens and socioeconomic consequences, with substantial morbidity and mortality risks [3]. Although AKI is traditionally considered a reversible disease upon therapeutic intervention, accumulating evidence reveals concerning long-term renal prognosis, including heightened risks to chronic kidney disease (CKD) for those AKI survivors [4, 5]. Notably, even mild AKI may predispose individuals to adverse renal sequelae, particularly renal fibrosis, a hallmark pathological feature which provides compelling evidence for an intrinsic pathophysiological continuum driving the AKI-CKD progression [6, 7].

Within renal parenchyma, tubular epithelial cells (RTECs), constituting the predominant cellular component, exhibit exceptional sensitivity to ischemic, obstructive, and hypoxic stimuli, thereby positioning them as core mediators in injury progression [8]. During the AKI-CKD transition, mitochondrial dysfunction in RTECs serves as a critical initiating event for metabolic failure and pathological progression [9]. As highly energy-demanding cells, RTECs with impaired mitochondria exhibit disrupted oxidative phosphorylation and excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, subsequently driving inflammatory activation and pro-fibrotic phenotypic transformation, thereby accelerating renal deterioration [10]. Nevertheless, the mechanism of AKI-CKD progression in intercellular communication remains incompletely defined, underscoring the urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies targeting the molecular underpinnings.

Exosomes, spherical nanoscale extracellular vesicles (30–150 nm in diameter) encapsulated by lipid bilayers, are constitutively secreted by diverse cell types [11, 12]. Through intercellular communication, exosomes transfer specific bioactive cargo (proteins, nucleic acids, lncRNAs, miRNAs) to recipient cells, selectively inducing targeted signal that modulate cellular behavior [13–15]. microRNAs (miRNAs), evolutionarily conserved non-coding RNAs with the greatest diversity enriched in exosomal payloads, regulate gene expression through mRNA degradation or translational repression, maintaining tissue homeostasis [16, 17]. Recent studies have demonstrated that elevated exosomal miR-93 derived from M2 macrophages inhibits the pyroptosis pathway, thereby alleviating renal injury mediated by epithelial cell dysfunction [18]. Similarly, macrophage-derived exosomes deliver miR-155 to salvage telomere dysfunction through TRF1 upregulation [19]. However, little is known about the specific roles that macrophage-derived exosomal miRNAs play in tubular epithelial dysfunction during AKI-CKD transition.

Siegesbeckiae Herba, an integral component of Miao ethnomedicine within traditional Chinese medicine, has been utilized for millennia in treating rheumatoid arthritis and numbness in the limbssince the Tang Dynasty [20, 21]. We have identified leocarpinolide B (LB), a sesquiterpenoid constituent with comparable abundance to the principal bioactive component kirenol, as possessing anti-inflammatory potency for the first time [22–25]. Our previous study revealed that LB exerts therapeutic effects in synovial inflammation-mediated arthritis by directly targeting NF-κB p65 and inhibiting its DNA-binding capacity [26]. In addition, LB showed dual modulation of NF-κB and Nrf2 pathways in LPS-induced macrophages [23]. However, its broader anti-inflammatory potential on other diseases remains to be fully elucidated.

Building upon these findings, we hypothesize that LB mitigates renal injury by modulating exosomal miRNA-mediated crosstalk between macrophages and RTECs. We demonstrated that LB treatment specifically downregulated macrophage-derived exosomal miR-204-5p levels, which in turn directly targets the 3’ untranslated region (UTR) of mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) mRNA, thereby restoring TFAM expression. This mechanistic axis ultimately suppresses mitochondrial dysfunction-driven inflammatory cytokine release and fibrotic transformation in RTECs during the AKI-CKD transition. These results unveil a previously unrecognized exosome-dependent pathway through which LB confers reno-protection, providing not only mechanistic insight but also a viable strategy for future translational studies in progressive renal pathologies.

Materials and methods

Construction and characterization of HA-Lipo@LB

LB-loaded liposomes were constructed through thin-film dispersion method, as previously described [27]. Briefly, soybean lecithin (39 mg) (AVT Bio Co., Ltd., 8030-76-0), cholesterol (7.8 mg) (AVT Bio Co., Ltd., 57-88-5), (2,3-Dioleoyl-propyl)-trimethylammonium chloride (DOTAP) (1.4 mg) (AVT Bio Co., Ltd., O02004), Brij® O10 LB (55 µL) (Sigma-Aldrich Co., 9004-98-2), and LB (5 mg) were dissolved in 20 mL mixture of dichloromethane and ethanol (1:1, v/v). After reduced pressure evaporation for 2 h at 45℃ to form a thin film, 10 mL PBS (pH 7.4) was added to hydrate for 1 h. Then, 4 mL HA solution (5 mg/mL) was added under sonication and mildly stirred for 0.5 h. Finally, the products were subjected to probe ultrasound for 15 min (200 W) and Millipore filtration to obtain uniformly dispersed HA-modified LB-loaded liposomes (HA-Lipo@LB). Non-modified LB-loaded liposomes (Lipo@LB) were constructed without HA solution. HA-modified liposomes (HA-Lipo) were constructed without loading LB. Particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of HA-Lipo@LB were characterized by a Zetasizer (Malvern, UK). Stability of HA-Lipo@LB was detected by monitoring changes of particle size and PDI at 4 °C during 5 days or in 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 24 h.

HA-Lipo@LB cellular uptake

LB was replaced by coumarin 6 (Macklin, C804226) and subjected to the similar procedures to obtain coumarin 6-loaded liposomes (Lipo@C6), an approach that provied direct evidence for CD44-mediated cellular uptake of HA-Lipo@LB in vitro. Briefly, TGF-β-stimulated RTECs were pretreated with free HA in serum-free DMEM for 1 h at 37 °C under light-protected conditions, followed by co-incubation with Lipo@C6 for an additional 2 h. Alternatively, TGF-β-stimulated RTECs with CD44 knockdown were directly incubated with Lipo@C6 for 2 h. Cellular uptake of Lipo@C6 was subsequently analyzed by confocal microscopy and quantified by flow cytometry.

Animal model

6- to 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice, with a body weight of approximately 20–25 g, were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Centre of Guizhou Medical University. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Document No. 2100060). To establish the folic acid (FA)-induced AKI-CKD transition model, mice received a single intraperitoneal injection (i.p) of FA (250 mg/kg in 0.3 mol/L sodium bicarbonate) (MedChemExpress, HY-16637) as described in a previous protocol [28]. Age-matched control mice (Ctrl) received 0.3 mol/L sodium bicarbonate alone. Following FA administration, mice were then randomly allocated into six groups (n = 8): FA, LB low-dose (LB. L, 1.25 mg/kg), LB high-dose (LB. H, 5 mg/kg), HA-Lipo (5 mg/kg), HA-Lipo@LB (5 mg/kg), positive control (prednisone acetate, PA, 2 mg/kg). Mice in the treatment groups were intraperitoneally injected every other day with LB, HA-Lipo, HA-Lip@LB, PA, respectively. On day 14 post-FA injection, mice were euthanized, and serum and kidney tissues were collected for subsequent analysis.

Animal treament

Within 14 days following FA administration, macrophage-derived exosomes (designated as Control-Exo and M1-Exo) were administered via tail vein injection at a dose of 15 µg/injection (twice weekly). Concurrently, GW4869 was administered via intraperitoneal injection at 2 mg/kg (three times weekly) to FA-injured mice. The experimental groups included (n = 8): Ctrl group, FA group, FA + M1-Exo group, FA + GW4869 group, and FA + GW4869 + M1-Exo group. Macrophage-derived exosomes biodistribution were tracked via tail vein injection using an in vivo imaging system. Tissues were harvested on day 14 post-FA injection for further analysis.

Renal function analysis

Renal function parameters were quantitatively evaluated by measuring serum biomarkers, including blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (CREA), employing commercial biochemical assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, A110111, A11111) following manufacturer-specified protocols.

Cell culture and treatment

Immortalized murine-derived tubular epithelial cells (RTECs) and Raw264.7 macrophages, acquired from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), were respectively maintained in low- and high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), both containing 10% FBS, under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Following LPS (1 µg/mL) stimulation, Raw264.7 macrophages were treated with varying concentrations of LB for 24 h. The cell-conditioned medium (CM) was collected for exosome isolation. Exosomes intensity was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Solarbio, PC0020). Subsequently, TGF-β (5 ng/mL)-stimulated RTECs were incubated with macrophage-derived exosomes (20 µg/mL) for 24 h prior to further analysis. Furthermore, GW4869 (10 µM) was employed to inhibit exosome biogenesis in Raw264.7 macrophages.

Exosome isolation

Exosomes were isolated and purified using the EXODUS methodology [29] (HUIXIN LIFETECH, China). For CM or human serum samples, differential centrifugation was performed in sequence: initial clarification at 2,000 ×g (30 min, 4℃) for cellular debris removal, followed by a 12,000 ×g centrifugation (30 min, 4℃) to eliminate protein aggregates. Clarified supernatants were then filtered with 0.22 μm strainers to further exclude residual microvesicles.

Renal tissues were enzymatically digested with 2.4 U/mL Dispase II (4℃, overnight), followed by PBS washes. Tissue fragments were minced into 1 mm³ pieces using ophthalmic scissors and incubated with 0.2% collagenase type I at 37℃ for 1.5 h. After PBS dilution, sequential centrifugations were conducted at 2,000 ×g (10 min, 4℃) and 10,000 ×g (30 min, 4℃) to collect supernatants. Filtered samples were transferred to 15/50 mL conical tubes for EXODUS-based purification. Isolated exosomes captured on nanochip membranes were resuspended in PBS.

Exosome identification and fluorescence labeling

Comprehensive exosome characterization was performed by Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NanoSight NS300) for size distribution, by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi HT7800) for morphological validation, by western blotting for exosomes-related markers, namely Alix, HSP70, Tsg101, Syntenin1.

Macrophage-isolated exosomes were labeled with PKH67 dye (Sigma-Aldrich #MINI67) at 37℃ for 30 min. These fluorescently-labeled exosomes were then co-cultured with starved RTECs for 1–6 h. The uptake of exosomes was visualized by confocal microscopy.

Enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

ELISA kits were employed to quantify protein concentrations of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) (NEOBIOSCIENCE, EMC102a.96, EMC004.96) in RTEC-derived CM in strict adherence to the manufacturer’s protocols.

ATP measurement

ATP levels were conducted with a bioluminescence assay (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, A095-2-1). Briefly, RTECs or freshly isolated renal tissues were thoroughly lysed in kit-supplied lysis buffer, and then centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 5 min at 4℃ to collect the supernatants. Luciferase reagent (100 µL) was supplied to eliminate the basal ATP in the test wells, followed by addition of 20 µL supernatant. After 2 s intervals, corresponding relative light units (RLU) were measured using an automatic microplate reader. A BCA assay kit (Solarbio, PC0020) was further performed to determine related-samples concentration.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) and morphological evaluation

JC-1 staining (Beyotime, C1048) was utilized for MMP assessment. Cells were incubated with a fully-mixed JC-1 working solution prepared in a certain proportion for 30 min as previously reported [30] (37℃, 5% CO2, dark incubation). Post-staining, cells underwent two washes with ice-cold JC-1 buffer solution, followed by immediate live-cell imaging.

The mitochondrial morphology was characterized using a Mito-Tracker Green kit (Solarbio, M8650). Treated cells were loaded with Mito-Tracker Green probe diluted in serum-free DMEM under light-shielding conditions (37℃, 5% CO2) for 30 min. Subsequent to three PBS washes, mitochondrial architecture was visualized using confocal microscopy (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

mtROS measurement

MitoSOX™ Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific, M36008), a mitochondria-specific superoxide indicator, was used to measure mtROS levels. Treated cells were loaded with 5 µM MitoSOX™ in serum-free DMEM for 20 min at 37℃ under light-free conditions. Following PBS washes centrifugation (300 ×g, 4 min, 4℃). Cells were suspended in serum-free DMEM. Fluorescence intensity reflecting mtROS generation was subsequently analyzed by confocal microscopy and quantified by flow cytometry.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

TRIzol™ Reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026CN)-isolated total RNA was dissolved in nuclease-free water (40–60 µL). Perform First-strand cDNA synthesis by a reverse transcription reaction (1 µg RNA in 20 µL reaction system) using Hifair® Ⅲ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix Kit (YEASEN, 11141ES60). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted on a Stratagene 3005P Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) with Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (YEASEN, 11201ES08). β-actin served as the endogenous control for mRNA normalization.

For miRNA profiling, total miRNA was extracted using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 217004) and reverse-transcribed with the TaqMan® MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, 4366596). qRT-PCR amplification employed TaqMan® miRNA Assays with U6 snRNA as the reference gene.

For mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) detection, mtDNA copy number was quantified via Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix-based qPCR using 10 ng genomic DNA extracted by the Universal Genomic DNA Kit (CWBIO, CW2298S).

Histological analysis

Renal tissue specimens were fixed and stained as previously reported [31]. First, the specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, dehydrated through a series of graded ethanol (70%-100%), embedded in paraffin, and then were cut into 3-µm-thick slices. As staining procedures followed manufacturer guidelines, the kidney histopathological evaluation, fibrosis degree and collagen deposition were performed by Hematoxylin & Eosin (H&E), Masson’s Trichrome, and Sirius Red. The slices were finally imaged with an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature (RT), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, and blocked with 5% goat serum for 1 h. Primary antibodies (TFAM and TOM20) were incubated overnight at 4℃, followed by species-matched fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Solarbio, C0065) for 4 min. Imaging was acquired using a confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell transfection

The miR-204-5p mimic and inhibitor, chemically synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), were transfected into target cells with Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, L3000015) as instructed guide by the manufacturer’s optimized protocol. Specifically, macrophages were transfected with control mimic or miR-204-5p mimic, and RTECs were transfected with negative control inhibitor (NC-inhibitor) or miR-204-5p inhibitor. The sequences as follows: miR-204-5p mimic: (5’-UUCCCUUUGUCAUCCUAUGCCU-3’;3’-GCAUAGGAUGACAAAGGGAAUU-5’); miR-204-5p inhibitor: (5’-AGGCAUAGGAUGACAAAGGGAA-3’).

Luciferase reporter assay

Putative binding sites for miR-204-5p within the TFAM 3′untranslated region (UTR) were predicted using TargetScan 7.2 algorithm. For functional validation, wild-type (WT), mutant (MUT) TFAM 3’UTR fragments, and miR-204-5p mimic or negative control mimic were constructed and co-transfected into 293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000. After 48 h incubation (37 °C, 5% CO2), luciferase activity was quantified with a Dual Luciferase Assay Kit (Promega, E1910). Firefly luciferase signals were normalized to Renilla luciferase for inter-sample calibration.

miRNA sequencing

Serum samples were collected from AKI patients and healthy individuals (HI) under protocols approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Document No. 2023049). Isolated exosomes were extracted total RNA for miRNA library construction and sequencing at Majorbio Biotech (Shanghai, China). RNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and Agilent 5300 Bioanalyzer. PCR amplification was performed using small RNAs, enriching the library concentration. Further purification was carried out based on the length and characteristics of microRNA, and then sequencing was conducted on the NovaSeq X Plus/DNBSEQ-T7 platform.

Bioinformatics analysis

The inflammation-related gene set was retrieved from the GeneCards database (www.genecards.org), and genes with a Relevance score > 2 were screened as strongly associated with inflammation. Mitochondria-related genes were obtained from the Broad Institute’s MitoCarta (Human MitoCarta 3.0) database (http://www.broadinstitute.org/pubs/MitoCarta). Overlap analysis was performed to identify common genes among the miRNA target genes, the inflammation-related gene set, and the mitochondria-related gene set. To elucidate the interaction relationships among these intersecting genes, they were imported into the STRING database to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network. The top 10 genes in the PPI network were identified, and the network was visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.8.0).

Western blotting

Renal tissues and RTECs were lysed in high-efficiency RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, R0010) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor (Solarbio, R0100) as previous reported [32]. A BCA protein assay kit was employed for protein concentration determination. Equal protein (25–35 µg) underwent electrophoretic separation on 10% or 12% SDS-PAGE gels and were subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Bedford). The membranes were blocked with TBST containing 5% BSA for 2 h at RT, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies and 1 h incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at RT. Protein bands were visualized via enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Smart-Lifesciences, H31500) using a ChemiDoc XRS Imaging System (Bio-Rad) and quantified with Image Lab Software v6.1.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism-6 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) was used for statistical analysis. The experimental data were presented as mean ± SEM, and each experiment was repeated independently at least three times. The study employed a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test to identify significant differences between groups, considering P < 0.05 as significant differences.

Results

LB suppressed mitochondrial damage and inflammatory crosstalk between macrophages and RTECs

To investigate the potential role of mitochondrial dysfunction in the AKI-CKD transition, we employed a murine model established by a single high-dose injection of folic acid (FA, 250 mg/kg, 14d) (Fig. 1A). H&E staining revealed severe renal injury in FA-injected mice, characterized by tubular epithelial cell edema and marked tubular dilatation (Fig. 1B). Concurrently, Sirius red staining and F4/80 immunostaining demonstrated extensive macrophage infiltration and substantial collagen deposition in the kidneys of FA-injected mice, accompanied by elevated mRNA levels of key fibrotic markers, including fibronectin (Fn) and collagen I (Col-1) (Figs. 1B-C), indicative of active fibrotic remodeling. Notably, FA-induced renal injury was associated with compromised mitochondrial integrity, as evidenced by decreased expression of TFAM and TOM20, as well as impaired ATP production, suggesting that mitochondrial dysfunction may contribute to FA-induced renal injury during the AKI-CKD transition (Figs. 1D-E and S1A).

Given the established link between macrophage-initiated inflammation and renal tubular damage [33], we first assessed the direct impact of macrophage-released factors on RTECs. As shown in Figures S1B-D, conditional medium (CM) obtained from LPS-stimulated macrophages induced significant mitochondrial dysfunction in RTECs, manifested by decreased protein levels of TOM20 and TFAM, and impaired oxidative phosphorylation (reduced Ndufs8, Atp5a-1, Uqcrc1 mRNA levels). With the optimal LB concentration (40 µM) established for subsequent experiments (Figures S1E-F), we next proceeded to investigate the impact of macrophage-secreted corresponding factors on renal inflammation. RTECs were exposed to CM obtained from LPS-stimulated macrophages that had been pre-treated with or without LB (designated as M1-CM or M1-LB-CM) (Fig. 1F). LB treatment rescued mitochondrial dysfunction induced by M1-CM in RTECs, marked by attenuated mitochondrial morphology, elevated TOM20, TFAM expression, as well as restored ATP generation (Figs. 1G-I). Furthermore, LB treatment dramatically suppressed the upregulation of adhesion molecules (Cd44, Cd11c), pro-inflammatory cytokines (Mcp-1, Tnf-α, F4/80, Il-6), and chemotaxis factors (Cxcl-10, Ccl-5) in RTECs following M1-CM stimulation (Figs. 1J-N), suggesting effective inhibition of macrophage-mediated inflammation in RTECs. Taken together, these results suggest that macrophage-derived mediators released inflammatory stimulation are pivotal in modulating RTECs function, and that LB treatment interrupts this detrimental intercellular crosstalk.

LB mitigated mitochondrial impairment and fibrotic responses in TGF-β-stimulated RTECs via macrophages-derived exosomes

Macrophages are known to release exosomes that control inflammatory reactions in various conditions [34, 35]. To assess whether exosomes participate in the intercellular communication between macrophages and RTECs, exosomes were isolated from the culture medium of LPS-stimulated macrophages that had been treated with or without LB (designated as M1-Exo or M1-LB-Exo) (Fig. 2A), and further characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), confirming a typical vesicle-shaped morphology and a corresponding size distribution, consistent with exosomal standards (Figs. 2B-C). Interestingly, M1-LB-Exo pellets showed a progressive, dose-dependent decline in exosome-specific markers (Alix, Syntenin-1, TSG101, HSP70) relative to those in M1-Exo pellets. While PKH-67-labeled M1-Exo or M1-LB-Exo pellets were efficiently internalized by RTECs with comparable efficiency within 1–6 h (Figs. 2D-E), suggesting that LB treatment confers protection primarily by remodeling the cargo composition of macrophage-derived exosomes, rather than by altering their uptake by RTECs. Subsequently, TGF-β-stimulated RTECs were exposed to M1-Exo or M1-LB-Exo. M1-LB-Exo treatment significantly attenuated mitochondrial depolarization, as evidenced by a shift toward red fluorescence, and was concomitantly associated with elevated expression of TOM20 and TFAM, mitochondrial biogenesis genes, including Ndufs8, Atp5a-1, Uqcrc1, along with restored cellular energy production in M1-Exo-stimulated RTECs (Figs. 2F-J and S2A). Furthermore, LB treatment effectively counteracted the M1-Exo-induced activation of inflammation-fibrosis axis, lowering mRNA levels of F4/80, Il-6, and protein expression of fibrosis-related biomarkers, including FN, α-SMA (Figs. 2K-L and S2B).

To validate the functional role of these exosomes in regulating mitochondrial dysfunction, we depleted exosome secretion with GW4869 in vitro. Cell viability was detected using the MTT assay, showing that 10 µM GW4869 exhibited no significant cytotoxicity in macrophages (Figure S2C). Culture medium from LPS-stimulated macrophages treated with GW4869 in the presence or absence of LB (designated as Exo-free CM or LB-Exo-free CM) was collected and then co-cultured with TGF-β-stimulated RTECs (Fig. 3A). As expected, the administration of GW4869 recovered mitochondrial depolarization, exhibited a relief in mitochondrial markers (TFAM, TOM20) and electron transport chain subunits (Ndufs8, Atp5a-1, Uqcrc1), these changes that were consistent with the recovery of mtDNA copy number and ATP content in RTECs exposed to M1-Exo. Critically, no significant difference in those mitochondrial phenotypic improvements was observed between the Exo-free CM and LB-Exo-free CM (Figs. 3B-I and S2D). Besides, the administration of GW4869 also exerted anti-inflammatory effects, reducing Mcp1 and Tnf-α mRNA levels (Figs. 3J-K) and subsequently suppressed fibrosis by lowering FN and α-SMA expression at both protein and mRNA levels in M1-Exo-injured RTECs (Figs. 3L-N and S2E).

To further validate whether macrophage-derived exosomes are the primary mediators of tubular injury, we treated FA-injured mice with GW4869 and M1-Exo (Figure S2F). As shown in Figures S2G–M, M1-Exo significantly exacerbated FA-induced renal dysfunction, as evidenced by aggravated histopathological changes, elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (CREA), kidney injury molecule-1 (Kim-1), and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (Ngal) mRNA levels, and increased inflammation-fibrosis markers (Tnf-α, Mcp1, Fn, and Co-1 mRNA levels; FN, α-SMA protein expression), whereas these injuries were reversed by GW4869 co-administration. Furthermore, in vivo tracing with PKH67-labeled macrophage-derived exosomes confirmed their uptake by tubular epithelial cells (Figures S2N-O). In short, these findings demonstrate that exosomes drive the crosstalk between macrophages and RTECs, and that those modulations ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction in RTECs, thereby interrupting the transition from inflammation to fibrosis.

Identification of miRNAs associated with patients’ serum circulating exosomes-guided kidney injury

Most exosomal functions are primarily attributed to miRNAs, which mediate cellular communication by modulating gene expression in distal cells [36, 37]. To identify exosomal cargo components that mediate cellular reprogramming, we performed high-throughput sequencing of miRNA expression profiles in circulating exosomes derived from the serum of AKI patients and healthy individuals (HI) using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Fig. 4A). Significantly differentially expressed miRNAs were identified using a threshold a 2-fold change and a P value < 0.05. As shown in Fig. 4B, the Venn diagram analysis illustrated the overlap of miRNA species between the two groups. Notably, 165 miRNAs were significantly differentially expressed between AKI patient- and HI-derived exosomes. Among these, 103 miRNAs were upregulated and 62 were downregulated in AKI patient-derived exosomes (Figs. 4C-E). Particularly, miR-204-5p, which has been previously documented to participate in the regulation of mitochondrial function and inflammatory processes [38–40], coupled with its significant upregulation and supportive GO enrichment analysis, was prioritized for further investigation. Based on the above analysis, the upregulation of miR-204-5p levels in exosomes from AKI patients was validated by qPCR (Fig. 4F). Consistently, miR-204-5p levels were significantly increased in both kidney tissues and serum exosomes from AKI mice (Figs. 4G-H). Further in vitro analysis revealed that M1-Exo contained substantially higher miR-204-5p levels than those from untreated controls (Fig. 4I). Notably, M1-LB-Exo blocked this pathogenic intercellular transfer compared to M1-Exo-treated RTECs (Fig. 4J). Thus, these results suggest that miR-204-5p functions as a key regulatory molecule in macrophage-RTEC crosstalk.

miR-204-5p repressed mitochondrial function through translational inhibition of TFAM mRNA

To investigate the underlying regulatory mechanism, we performed an integrative bioinformatic analysis, which yielded 40 candidate genes (Figure S3A). The top 10 core targets were visualized as a subnetwork. Notably, TFAM, a promoter-specific enhancer of mtDNA critical for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis [36, 41], emerged as a high-scoring hub gene (Figure S3B). Subsequent qPCR validation further demonstrated that among these candidates, TFAM exhibited the most pronounced downregulation in response to M1-miR-204-5p mimics-Exo treatment compared to M1-Exo treatment (Figure S3C). Moreover, we identified TFAM as a putative target, with a binding site located at nucleotides 538–544 (Fig. 5A). Next, DNA fragments encompassing either the wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) 3’UTR of TFAM were synthesized and cloned into a luciferase reporter plasmid. As expected, miR-204-5p mimics significantly reduced luciferase activity from the WT construct. In contrast, this suppression was completely abolished by the MUT 3’UTR (Fig. 5B). These findings implicate TFAM as a key downstream effector of miR-204-5p in regulating mitochondrial homeostasis.

We further systematically evaluated whether TFAM-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to the AKI-CKD transition. Based on canonical marker genes, single-cell analysis of healthy human kidney tissue defined six distinct cellular clusters: podocytes (P), collecting duct intercalated cells (CD: IC), proximal tubules (PT), collecting duct principal cells (CD: PC), distal convoluted tubules (DT), and ascending loop of Henle (LH). TFAM was ubiquitously expressed across all clusters, with notably higher expression detected in PT (Figs. 5C-E). Similarly, single-cell analysis of renal cell carcinoma specimens revealed that TFAM distribution identified epithelial cells as its principal source (Figs. 5F-H). Together, these findings delineate a cell type-specific expression pattern of TFAM in the mammalian kidney, with predominant localization to PT. Importantly, in a mouse model of renal ischemia-reperfusion injury, proximal tubular TFAM expression progressively diminished over time, suggesting that TFAM loss-triggered mitochondrial dysfunction may contribute to the pathological progression from AKI to CKD (Figs. 5I-K). Taken together, our findings reveal TFAM as a directly regulated target of miR-204-5p and describe its dynamic expression patterns in renal tubular cells across physiological and injury contexts, implicating the miR-204-5p/TFAM axis as a potential contributor to kidney injury and fibrotic progression.

miR-204-5p overexpression abrogated the protective effects of LB

To elucidate the involvement of macrophage-derived exosomal miR-204-5p in mitochondrial dysfunction. TGF-β-activated RTECs were exposed to exosomes derived from LPS-stimulated macrophages with or without LB treatment co-infected with miR-204-5p mimics lentivirus (designated as M1-miR-204-5p mimics-Exo, and M1-miR-204-5p mimics-LB-Exo) (Fig. 6A). As shown in Figs. 6B-C, miR-204-5p overexpression in M1 macrophages aggravated mitochondrial dysfunction in RTECs, as indicated by elevated mitochondrial depolarization and mtROS levels. Correspondingly, miR-204-5p overexpression significantly reduced protein levels of TFAM and TOM20 (Figs. 6D-E and S3D), impaired mitochondrial respiratory capacity, evidenced by decreased expression of key components at transcriptional levels (Ndufs8, Atp5a-1, Uqcrc1), along with reduced mtDNA copy number and ATP production in TGF-β-activated RTECs, whereas LB partially counteracted those phenotypic alterations (Figures S3E-G). Furthermore, miR-204-5p overexpression exacerbated inflammatory responses (elevated Mcp1, Il-6, Tnf-α mRNA levels) and promoted fibrotic activation (elevated Col-1, α-SMA, Fn mRNA levels, as well as increased α-SMA and FN protein expression), whereas these effects were reversed by LB co-treatment (Figures S3H-J).

In contrast, we treated TGF-β-activated RTECs with or without LB co-infected with a miR-204-5p inhibitor (Fig. 6F). miR-204-5p inhibition significantly attenuated TGF-β-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, as correlated with enhanced mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) and reduced mtROS production, increased expression of TFAM, TOM20, respiratory chain components at both protein expression (ATP5A, UQCRC2, MTCO1, SDHB), and transcriptional level (Ndufs8, Atp5a-1, Uqcrc1), elevated mtDNA copy number and ATP levels (Figs. 6G-I and S4A-E). Similarly, miR-204-5p inhibition reversed the inflammatory response (decreased Mcp1, Il-6, Tnf-α mRNA levels) and fibrotic progression (decreased Col-1, α-SMA, Fn mRNA levels, as well as reduced α-SMA and FN protein expression) induced by TGF-β in RTECs, with these beneficial effects being strengthened by LB co-treatment (Figs. 6J-L and S4F). Taken together, the above findings strongly suggest that LB alleviates mitochondrial dysfunction and cellular injury in RTECs primarily through downregulating macrophage-derived exosomal miR-204-5p.

A CD44-targeted liposomal formulation of LB (HA-Lipo@LB) enhanced renal delivery and therapeutic efficacy in vivo

To evaluate the protective effect of LB against FA-induced AKI-CKD progression, a hyaluronic acid (HA)-functionalized liposome was engineered (Fig. 7A). As illustrated in Figures S5A-C, the modification of HA mildly increased the size of Lipo@LB from 85.72 nm to 102.8 nm and decreased zeta potential from + 5.12 mV to -12.6 mV. In addition, HA-Lipo@LB exhibited superior stability in 4 ℃ for 5 days or in 10% FBS medium for 24 h, which might be attributed to its negative charge, which facilitates charge repulsion and avoid protein absorption. Ex vivo organ fluorescence imaging confirmed that HA-LB-Lipo@DIR selectively accumulated in injured kidneys of FA mice, confirming its enhanced targeting specificity towards CD44-overexpressing injured renal tissues (Fig. 7B). To directly assess CD44-mediated internalization of HA-functionalized liposomes in vitro, we employed coumarin-6 (C6) as a fluorescent tracer loaded into liposomes (Lipo@C6). As shown in Figures S5D-E, Lipo@C6 uptake was markedly diminished following pretreatment with HA compared to the Lipo@C6-only group, as quantified by flow cytometry and visualized by confocal microscopy. Consistent results were also observed in CD44-knockdown RTECs. (Figures S5F–G). Collectively, these results provide direct evidence that HA-functionalized liposomes are internalized by RTECs in a CD44-dependent manner.

Subsequently, FA-injured mice were administered with different formulations and doses of LB, as well as prednisone acetate (PA) (Fig. 7C). As expected, histopathological examination revealed that FA-injected mice developed typical pathological features of AKI-CKD, including tubular atrophy, dilation, necrosis, and interstitial fibrosis, all of which were markedly alleviated by LB administration, with a more pronounced therapeutic effect observed in the HA-Lipo@LB group (Fig. 7D). Consistently, LB treatment attenuated FA-induced elevations in CREA, BUN, Kim-1, and Ngal, with HA-Lipo@LB yielding superior outcomes, further suggestive of enhanced reno-protection (Figs. 7E-G). Furthermore, HA-Lipo@LB treatment reduced the elevated levels of M1 markers (iNOS, Cd86, Cd11c), key inflammatory cytokines (Tnf-α and Mcp-1), downregulated Fn, Col-1mRNA levels and FN, α-SMA protein expression of fibrotic mediators, and concurrently upregulated the levels of M2 markers (Cd163, Cd206) in FA-injured kidneys, indicating effective disruption of the inflammation-fibrosis cascade (Figs. 7H-J and S5H-J). In short, these results demonstrate that HA-Lipo@LB confers superior reno-protection against FA-induced tubular injury, renal inflammation, and interstitial fibrosis, attributable to its CD44-targeted delivery to the injured kidney.

HA-Lipo@LB alleviated FA-induced renal mitochondrial dysfunction by suppressing exosomal miR-204-5p and restoring TFAM expression

To further elucidate the role of LB-mediated regulation of TFAM via serum exosomal miR-204-5p in mitochondrial dysfunction in FA-injected mice, circulating exosomes were isolated from mouse serum. Subsequent characterization by TEM and NTA confirmed that the isolated vesicles exhibited the typical size and morphology of exosomes (Figs. 8A-B). Western blot analysis demonstrated that expression of exosomal markers (Alix, HSP-70, Tsg101, Syntenin-1) was significantly suppressed by LB treatment in a dose- and formulation-dependent manner, suggesting that LB remodels the molecular composition of circulating exosomes (Fig. 8C). As anticipated, this finding was further supported by corresponding alterations in exosomal miR-204-5p levels (Fig. 8D). To functionally link these cargo alterations to mitochondrial phenotypes, we measured mtROS levels by immunofluorescence, which revealed that HA-Lipo@LB treatment potently suppressed FA-induced mtROS accumulation (Fig. 8E). Furthermore, HA-Lipo@LB treatment reversed the FA-induced downregulation of TFAM and TOM20 to a greater extent than LB alone (Figs. 8F and S6A). In addition, we assessed the impact of HA-Lipo@LB on indicators associated with the mitochondrial respiratory chain in FA-injured mice. Specifically, HA-Lipo@LB treatment upregulated the protein expression of mitochondrial respiratory chain components, including MTCO1, SDHB, and NDUFB8, which were corroborated at the transcriptional level, as evidenced by increased Ndufs8, Atp5a-1, Uqcrc1 mRNA levels (Figs. 8G-H and S6B). Consistently, HA-Lipo@LB also reversed the FA-induced reduction in mtDNA copy number and subsequent decline in ATP production (Figs. 8I-J). In short, these findings indicate that LB treatment alleviates FA-induced renal mitochondrial dysfunction through a mechanism involving the suppression of exosomal miR-204-5p and the subsequent upregulation of its target, TFAM, with enhanced efficacy achieved through HA-based CD44 targeting.

Discussion

AKI is considered an independent risk factor for the progression to chronic kidney disease [7, 42]. The progression from AKI to CKD represents a formidable clinical challenge with limited therapeutic options, underscoring the urgent need to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and identify novel therapeutic targets. In this study, we identified LB, a bioactive sesquiterpenoid from Herba Siegesbeckiae, as a potent agent mitigating renal injury in the AKI-CKD transition. More importantly, we delineated a novel mechanism through which LB exerts its reno-protective effects by specifically modulating macrophage-tubule communication via exosomal transfer of miR-204-5p, which targets TFAM to restore mitochondrial function, thereby interrupting the inflammation-fibrosis cascade.

Inflammatory cell infiltration, particularly of macrophages, is a hallmark of the maladaptive phase post-AKI and is commonly observed in injured kidneys, exacerbating the fibrotic transformation phase in TGF-β-treated RTECs [43, 44]. Activated macrophages exacerbate tissue damage through the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species [45]. Recent evidence indicates that macrophage-derived exosomes mediate intercellular communication in various diseases, for instance, they regulate cardiomyocyte pyroptosis in uremic hearts via miR-155 transfer [42]. However, the specific role and mechanism of macrophage-derived exosomes in tubular epithelial cells during the transition from AKI-CKD remain poorly understood. Our initial experiments confirmed that M1-CM exacerbated mitochondrial damage and inflammatory responses in RTECs, whereas M1-LB-CM effectively counteracted these detrimental effects, positioning macrophages as a key cellular target of LB and highlighting the importance of macrophage-secreted corresponding factors in tubular injury. Further investigation revealed that exosomes as the principal mediators of this intercellular crosstalk. Exo-free CM attenuated mitochondrial dysfunction and fibrosis progression to a similar extent as LB-H-Exo-free CM, strongly implicating exosomes as the crucial vehicle through which LB ameliorates the AKI-CKD transition. It is important to note that GW4869 inhibits exosome release by blocking neutral sphingomyelinase 2 (nSMase2)-dependent ceramide synthesis, a pathway also involved in other cellular processes [46, 47]. Therefore, the protective effects observed upon GW4869 treatment cannot be unequivocally attributed solely to exosome inhibition. Future studies will further employ Rab27a knockdown, which disrupts the docking and fusion of multivesicular bodies with the plasma membrane [48], thereby reducing exosome secretion.

Exosomal miRNAs have emerged as key regulators of intercellular communication and disease pathogenesis [49, 50]. In the current study, a pivotal discovery of our research was the identification of miR-204-5p as a key miRNA elevated in serum exosomes from AKI patients through miRNA sequencing, suggesting that the specific loading and transfer of miR-204-5p from activated macrophages may be associated with the pathogenic mechanism in AKI-CKD transition. Subsequent validation across multiple sample types, including serum exosomes from AKI patients, mouse kidney and plasma exosomes, as well as macrophage and RTEC-derived exosomes, consistently showed upregulated miR-204-5p levels, while LB treatment effectively reduced its levels in RTECs-derived exosomes, indicating that LB-mediated exosomal miR-204-5p may function as a potent modulator of cell-cell crosstalk from macrophages to RTECs. Mechanistically, we observed that miR-204-5p overexpression abolished the protective effects of LB, while its inhibition phenocopied the protective effects. However, we recognize that the relatively small sample size and single time point represent limitations of our human exosomal miRNA sequencing data, and that patient heterogeneity may influence circulating miRNA profiles. Future studies with larger cohorts and longitudinal sampling will be necessary to validate these findings.

Mitochondrial dysfunction acts as both an initiator and a perpetuating factor in the AKI-CKD transition [43]. Previous studies have demonstrated that miR-709-mediated TFAM targeting disrupts mitochondrial function in cisplatin-induced injury [45]. In our research, bioinformatic prediction and dual-luciferase reporter assays revealed that TFAM was a direct functional target of miR-204-5p, providing a mechanistic link between macrophage-derived exosomes and tubular mitochondrial integrity. Furthermore, a systematic analysis using the Kidney Interactive Transcriptomics database further verified that TFAM was tightly linked to mitochondrial dysfunction-induced AKI-CKD.

Furthermore, the translational potential of our findings was enhanced by the development of a targeted nanotherapeutic strategy. By exploiting the well-documented upregulation of CD44 in injured kidneys, we engineered a hyaluronic acid-functionalized liposome (HA-Lipo@LB) for kidney-specific delivery. Compared with free LB, HA-Lipo@LB exhibited superior efficacy in mitigating renal dysfunction, inflammation, and fibrosis, underscoring the promise of targeted nanoplatforms in improving therapeutic outcomes while potentially minimizing off-target effects. While this approach validated the biological findings, further investigation into its biodistribution and pharmacokinetic characteristics is warranted.

In conclusion, our findings unveil a comprehensive pathway wherein LB interrupts deleterious macrophage-RTEC crosstalk by suppressing the exosomal transfer of miR-204-5p, and further relieving the repression of its target TFAM, leading to the recovery of mitochondrial function and ultimately attenuating the transition from acute inflammation to chronic fibrosis. Given the lack of targeted therapies for AKI-CKD progression, our study positions LB, particularly in its targeted formulation, as a promising therapeutic candidate and highlights the macrophage exosomal miR-204-5p/TFAM axis as a novel and mechanistically grounded target for intervention in progressive kidney disease.
