Impact of antibiotic-induced gut microbiota modulation on morphine analgesia, tolerance, withdrawal, and neurophysiological changes in mice
Authors: Zahra Jokar, Somayeh Shahidani, Parham Reisi, Hojjatallah Alaei, Mohammad Sadegh Damavandi, Hossein Khanahmad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Morphine, a potent opioid analgesic, is limited in clinical use due to tolerance, dependence, and withdrawal. Recent research highlights the gut microbiota as a crucial regulator of opioid effects through the microbiota-gut-brain axis. This study investigates the impact of antibiotic-induced suppression of the gut microbiota on morphine’s analgesic efficacy, tolerance, withdrawal, and related behavioral and neurophysiological changes in male mice. Mice received an 11-day antibiotic regimen followed by acute or repeated morphine administration. Behavioral assays (hot plate, tail flick, naloxone-induced withdrawal) evaluated analgesia, tolerance, and withdrawal, while anxiety-like behaviors were assessed via the elevated plus maze. Fecal samples were analyzed for colony-forming units, and EEG recordings measured brain activity. Antibiotics alone induced analgesia and prevented morphine tolerance in repeated dosing (P < 0.001). Antibiotics also reduced withdrawal severity in chronic morphine-treated mice (P < 0.05) but had no effect after acute morphine administration. Anxiety-like behaviors increased with antibiotics (P < 0.001), and both acute (P < 0.01) and chronic morphine (P < 0.05), without interaction. Antibiotic treatment reduced fecal colony-forming units, most significantly with repeated morphine (P < 0.001). Repeated morphine enhanced brain excitability, which was reduced by antibiotic pre-treatment. These findings suggest that modulating the gut microbiota may provide new strategies for managing opioid dependence and related disorders.

Introduction

Morphine, a potent opioid analgesic, is widely used for managing severe pain, particularly in cancer, trauma, and post-surgical patients1. However, its clinical utility is limited by the development of tolerance, physical dependence, and addiction2. Both short-term and long-term morphine use can disrupt central and peripheral systems, complicating its continued use and leading to challenges in pain management3. Opioid addiction also affects multiple organ systems, including the gastrointestinal (GI) tract4, such as delayed gastric emptying, gastroesophageal reflux, constipation, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and, in severe cases, fecal impaction or obstruction, thereby highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of its underlying mechanisms.

The gut microbiome, a diverse community of microorganisms inhabiting the digestive tract, plays a critical role in regulating systemic health and maintaining homeostasis5,6. Morphine addiction is known to alter the balance between beneficial and harmful bacteria in the gut, contributing to dysbiosis-a condition that can have broad effects on both the gut and the brain7. Dysbiosis has been linked to increased gut permeability, neuroinflammation, anxiety-like behaviors, and altered pain sensitivity8,9. Antibiotics, which are commonly used to treat infections, can exacerbate this dysbiosis by altering the composition and diversity of the gut microbiota10. Such microbiome alterations are not limited to the GI tract; they can also influence central nervous system (CNS) function via the microbiota-gut-brain axis, a communication pathway linking the gut to brain regions involved in mood and behavior regulation11.

Antibiotic-induced microbial depletion can trigger neuroimmune changes that mimic opioid withdrawal, reducing morphine’s analgesic efficacy and intensifying negative affective states12. Recent studies have highlighted the significant role of the gut microbiome in modulating the development of morphine dependence and its pharmacological effects13,14. Antibiotic-induced changes to the gut microbiota have been shown to impact morphine metabolism, tolerance development, and withdrawal symptoms15. Furthermore, research in animal models suggests that gut microbes influence neurotransmitter levels, including dopamine, noradrenaline, and serotonin16, which are central to the mesocorticolimbic reward system and are implicated in the addictive properties of morphine17.

While traditional addiction research has primarily focused on the CNS, emerging evidence suggests that the gut microbiome is a key player in opioid addiction14. The microbiota–gut–brain axis offers a promising framework for understanding how gut bacteria, altered by antibiotics, may influence opioid pharmacodynamics and the development of addiction18.

In this study, we investigated how antibiotic-induced alterations in gut microbiota influence anxiety-like behaviors, naloxone-precipitated withdrawal, analgesic responses, and cortical excitability in morphine-treated mice. Gut microbiota status was assessed via colony-forming unit (CFU) counts from fecal samples as an indicator of dysbiosis. EEG recordings targeted the primary motor cortex (M1) and secondary motor cortex (M2), regions implicated in both pain modulation and anxiety regulation19,20. Through these measures, our study aims to clarify the role of the gut microbiome in opioid addiction and to explore the microbiota-gut–brain axis as a potential therapeutic target.

Results

Antibiotic and morphine effects on hot plate latency in mice

Univariate ANOVA revealed that antibiotic treatment significantly increased hot plate response latency compared with controls (85.47 ± 1.85 vs. 72.39 ± 6.31; F(1,75) = 34.29, p < 0.001), independent of morphine administration. Morphine treatment also produced a significant effect (F(2,75) = 15.16, p < 0.0001), with Tukey’s post hoc tests indicating that single-dose morphine (87.07 ± 2.07) prolonged latency relative to both saline (74.48 ± 8.85) and continuous morphine treatment (75.24 ± 8.71; p < 0.001 for both). No difference was observed between continuous morphine and saline groups. A significant interaction between antibiotic and morphine treatment was detected (F(2,75) = 4.50, p = 0.014).

Consistent with these findings, a Kruskal–Wallis test demonstrated significant differences across groups (χ²(5) = 44.03, p < 0.001). Post hoc Dunn’s tests confirmed that hot plate latency was elevated compared with the Control–Saline group (65.64 ± 2.61 s) in the Antibiotic–Saline (83.33 ± 3.83 s; p < 0.001), Control–Morphine SD (85.00 ± 1.77 s; p < 0.001), Antibiotic–Morphine SD (89.15 ± 0.75 s; p < 0.001), and Antibiotic–Morphine CD (83.95 ± 1.97 s; p < 0.05) groups. Furthermore, Control–Morphine SD mice exhibited longer latencies than Control–Morphine CD mice (66.53 ± 2.68 s; p < 0.05) (Fig. 1).

Antibiotic and morphine effects on tail-flick latency in mice

Univariate ANOVA showed that antibiotic treatment significantly increased tail-flick latency compared with controls (16.54 ± 1.86 vs. 11.61 ± 3.38; F(1,75) = 37.04, p < 0.001), independent of morphine administration. Morphine treatment also produced a robust effect (F(2,51) = 79.96, p = 0.001). Post hoc Tukey’s tests indicated that single-dose morphine (18.45 ± 0.12, P < 0.0001) prolonged latency relative to both saline (10.91 ± 1.92) and continuous morphine treatment (12.87 ± 5.35; p < 0.003 for both), with no difference between the latter two groups. A significant interaction between antibiotic and morphine treatment was also observed (F(2,75) = 12.36, p < 0.0001).

Similarly, the Kruskal–Wallis test confirmed significant group differences (χ²(5) = 47.50, p < 0.001). Dunn’s post hoc analyses showed prolonged latencies compared with the Control–Saline group (8.98 ± 0.59 s) in Control–Morphine SD (18.33 ± 0.54 s; p < 0.001), Antibiotic–Morphine SD (18.57 ± 0.78 s; p < 0.001), and Antibiotic–Morphine CD (18.21 ± 0.96 s; p < 0.001) groups. Furthermore, Control–Morphine SD mice displayed significantly longer latencies than Control–Morphine CD mice (7.52 ± 1.09 s; p < 0.001), and Antibiotic–Morphine CD mice had higher latencies than Control–Morphine CD mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2).

Antibiotic and morphine effects on withdrawal index in mice

Univariate ANOVA indicated that antibiotic treatment did not significantly alter the withdrawal index compared with controls, irrespective of morphine administration. In contrast, morphine treatment markedly increased the withdrawal index following naloxone injection (F(2,75) = 133.6, p < 0.001). Post hoc Tukey’s tests revealed that single-dose morphine (9.88 ± 0.45) significantly elevated the withdrawal index compared with both saline (6.11 ± 0.70) and continuous morphine (12.64 ± 1.22; p < 0.001 for both). No significant difference was observed between the continuous morphine and saline groups. A significant interaction between antibiotic and morphine treatment was also detected (F(2,75) = 11.76, p < 0.001).

Consistent with these findings, the Kruskal–Wallis test confirmed significant group differences (χ²(5) = 64.15, p < 0.001). Dunn’s post hoc analyses showed elevated withdrawal indices compared with the Control–Saline group (5.41 ± 0.12) in Control–Morphine SD (10.33 ± 0.43; p < 0.001), Antibiotic–Morphine SD (9.43 ± 0.51; p < 0.001), Control–Morphine CD (13.86 ± 0.14; p < 0.001), and Antibiotic–Morphine CD (11.42 ± 0.57; p < 0.001) groups. Moreover, the Antibiotic–Morphine CD group differed significantly from the Control–Antibiotic group (6.81 ± 0.25; p < 0.01) (Fig. 3).

Antibiotic and morphine effects on anxiety-like behavior in EPM in mice

Univariate ANOVA revealed that antibiotic treatment did not significantly affect the time spent in the open arms compared with controls (69.46 ± 11.16 vs. 86.28 ± 17.10 s; F(1,70) = 3.71, p = 0.058), independent of morphine administration. Similarly, morphine treatment alone did not significantly alter open-arm exploration. However, a significant interaction between antibiotic and morphine treatment was observed (F(2,70) = 11.28, p < 0.001). Consistent with these results, the Kruskal–Wallis test confirmed significant differences among groups (χ²(5) = 23.46, p < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons using Dunn’s test indicated that several groups spent significantly less time in the open arms compared with the Control–Saline group (120.48 ± 9.15 s), including the Antibiotic–Saline group (50.50 ± 10.42 s; p < 0.001), the Control–Morphine SD group (69.64 ± 6.49 s; p < 0.05), and the Antibiotic–Morphine CD group (68.75 ± 7.64 s; p < 0.05) (Fig. 4A).

Analysis of the Anxiety Index yielded similar results. Antibiotic treatment alone did not significantly alter the Anxiety Index compared with controls (0.6951 ± 0.039 vs. 0.6439 ± 0.044; F(1,70) = 3.97, p = 0.0501), and morphine treatment alone also had no significant effect. Nevertheless, a significant interaction between antibiotic and morphine treatment was observed (F(2,70) = 11.85, p < 0.001). The Kruskal–Wallis test confirmed significant group differences (χ²(5) = 23.74, p < 0.001), and Dunn’s post hoc comparisons showed that the Anxiety Index was significantly higher than the Control–Saline group (0.556 ± 0.026) in the Antibiotic–Saline group (0.763 ± 0.024; p < 0.001), the Control–Morphine SD group (0.696 ± 0.022; p < 0.05), and the Antibiotic–Morphine CD group (0.692 ± 0.019; p < 0.05) (Fig. 4B).

Antibiotic and morphine effects on gut bacterial counts in mice

Univariate ANOVA demonstrated that antibiotic treatment markedly reduced log10 colony-forming unit (CFU)/g counts compared with controls (7.07 ± 0.39 vs. 10.58 ± 0.27; F(1,12) = 702168.5, p < 0.001), independent of morphine administration. Morphine treatment also significantly decreased CFU counts (F(2,12) = 3955.4, p < 0.001). Post hoc Tukey’s tests revealed that both single-dose morphine (9.43 ± 1.53; p < 0.01) and continuous morphine (7.50 ± 2.00; p < 0.001) reduced bacterial load compared with saline (9.54 ± 1.74), with continuous morphine producing a greater reduction than single-dose morphine (p < 0.001). A significant interaction between antibiotic and morphine treatment was also observed (F(2,12) = 165.9, p < 0.001).

The Kruskal–Wallis test further confirmed significant group differences (χ²(5) = 16.58, p < 0.01). Dunn’s post hoc analysis showed decreased CFU counts relative to the Control–Saline group in the Antibiotic–Saline (p < 0.05) and Antibiotic–Morphine CD (p < 0.01) groups (Table 1).

Effect of antibiotics and morphine on changes in alpha wave activity in absolute EEG power

Repeated-measures ANOVA indicated that alpha wave activity increased over time following morphine injection (F(4,156) = 2.53, p = 0.042). This temporal effect was not significantly influenced by antibiotic treatment, morphine, or their interaction. Analysis of between-subjects effects showed that antibiotic treatment did not significantly alter alpha wave activity compared with controls. In contrast, morphine treatment significantly affected alpha wave activity (F(2,39) = 3.75, p = 0.032), with post hoc tests revealing higher activity in continuous-dose morphine mice compared with single-dose morphine mice (p = 0.037). A significant interaction between antibiotic and morphine treatment was also detected (F(2,39) = 7.11, p = 0.002).

Pairwise comparisons further showed that alpha wave activity was elevated in the Antibiotic–Saline group (p = 0.037) and the Control–Morphine CD group (p < 0.001) compared with the Control–Saline group. By contrast, the Antibiotic–Morphine CD group exhibited a marked reduction in alpha wave activity relative to the Control–Morphine CD group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5A).

Effect of antibiotics and morphine on changes in beta wave activity in absolute EEG power

Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed that beta wave activity increased over time following morphine injection (F(4,156) = 2.87, p = 0.25). This temporal change was not significantly influenced by antibiotic treatment, morphine, or their interaction. Analysis of between-subjects effects showed that antibiotic treatment did not significantly alter beta wave activity compared with controls. In contrast, morphine treatment significantly modulated beta activity (F(2,39) = 4.20, p = 0.022), with post hoc analysis indicating greater activity in continuous-dose compared with single-dose morphine-treated mice (p = 0.040). A significant antibiotic × morphine interaction was also observed (F(2,39) = 8.46, p = 0.001).

Pairwise comparisons demonstrated that beta wave activity was elevated relative to the Control–Saline group in both the Antibiotic–Saline group (p = 0.018) and the Control–Morphine CD group (p < 0.001). Conversely, the Antibiotic–Morphine CD group showed a marked reduction in beta activity compared with the Control–Morphine CD group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5B).

Effect of antibiotics and morphine on changes in delta wave activity in absolute EEG power

Repeated-measures ANOVA indicated that delta wave activity did not change significantly over time following morphine injection. Moreover, delta activity was not significantly affected by antibiotic treatment, morphine, or their interaction. Between-subjects analysis showed that antibiotic treatment alone did not alter delta wave activity compared with controls. In contrast, morphine treatment significantly modulated delta activity (F(2,39) = 4.53, p = 0.017), with post hoc tests revealing higher activity in continuous-dose morphine-treated mice compared with single-dose mice (p = 0.012). A significant interaction between antibiotic and morphine treatment was also observed (F(2,39) = 4.30, p = 0.020).

Pairwise comparisons showed that delta wave activity was elevated in the Control–Morphine CD group relative to Control–Saline (p < 0.001), whereas the Antibiotic–Morphine CD group exhibited a significant reduction compared with the Control–Morphine CD group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5C).

Repeated-measures ANOVA indicated that theta wave activity did not change significantly over time following morphine injection. Theta activity was also not significantly influenced by antibiotic treatment, morphine, or their interaction. Between-subjects analysis showed that antibiotic treatment alone did not alter theta activity compared with controls. In contrast, morphine treatment significantly modulated theta activity (F(2,39) = 3.84, p = 0.030), with post hoc tests revealing higher activity in continuous-dose morphine-treated mice compared with single-dose mice (p = 0.023). A significant interaction between antibiotic and morphine treatment was also observed (F(2,39) = 4.45, p = 0.018).

Pairwise comparisons demonstrated that theta activity was elevated in the Control–Morphine CD group relative to Control–Saline (p < 0.001), whereas the Antibiotic–Morphine CD group exhibited a significant reduction compared with the Control–Morphine CD group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5D).

Discussion

This study’s findings suggest that antibiotic administration induces analgesia, likely through alterations in gut microbiota. These results are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the pain-relieving effects of antibiotics (e.g., vancomycin) and the role of gut microbiota in modulating pain sensitivity21,22. For instance, Minerbi’s research highlighted that germ-free or antibiotic-treated mice exhibited reduced pain sensitivity, which was reversed after microbiota reconstitution23. Emerging evidence further supports that pain is associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis, affecting pain behavioral responses24. Early studies in mice subjected to colorectal distension demonstrated that antibiotic pretreatment enhanced visceral hypersensitivity, which was normalized by Lactobacillus supplementation25. Similarly, early-life vancomycin-induced microbiota perturbation altered pain responses26, underscoring the microbiota’s role in enteric pain modulation. Thus, the gut microbiota plays a crucial role in modulating pain, with antibiotics potentially influencing pain perception through both direct and indirect mechanisms. Collectively, these findings highlight the gut microbiota as a critical mediator of pain perception, with antibiotics potentially modulating pain through both direct antimicrobial actions and indirect effects on gut barrier integrity and inflammatory signaling pathways27. Our findings align with this body of evidence, suggesting that antibiotic-induced shifts in microbial composition may influence pain processing mechanisms at multiple levels.

The analgesic effects of antibiotics could involve several pathways. Neomycin, for example, is known to block N-type calcium channels, reducing calcium availability in neurons and dampening nociceptive signaling28. Additionally, neomycin can elicit histamine release from mast cells through interaction with G-proteins, contributing to its pain-reducing effects29. Vancomycin and other antibiotics can also inhibit microglial activation and suppress the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as Interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor alpha, further enhancing their analgesic effects. Notably, vancomycin’s mechanism involves killing Gram-positive bacteria by inhibiting cell wall synthesis, thereby reshaping the gut ecosystem30. Additionally, antibiotics may modulate gut microbiota by altering amino acid profiles, contributing to the regulation of pain and inflammation31. For instance, an increase in amino acids like arginine, glycine, and valine, alongside a decrease in tyrosine and methionine, can enhance mucosal barrier integrity and induce anti-inflammatory cytokines, potentially reducing pain32. Beyond visceral pain, peripheral pain responses are also microbiota-dependent. Germ-free mice exhibited attenuated inflammatory pain compared to specific pathogen-free mice, linked to elevated IL-10 expression, which normalized upon IL-10 neutralization33. Furthermore, bacteria like Staphylococcus aureus directly activate nociceptive neurons via N-formylated peptides and pore-forming toxins, independent of immune activation34, highlighting direct microbial-neuronal interactions. Together, these mechanisms suggest that antibiotics may alleviate pain by modulating microbiota-driven immune and neural pathways. This is consistent with emerging evidence that gut dysbiosis can influence neuroinflammation and pain processing via the microbiota-gut–brain axis35.

Furthermore, antibiotics can disrupt the lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) pathways by depleting gut bacteria and reducing circulating LPS36. This reduces TLR4 activation and downstream inflammatory responses in the dorsal root ganglion, leading to diminished pain and inflammation. These mechanisms underscore the complex interplay between gut microbiota, the immune system, and the nervous system in pain modulation. However, it is important to note that our study did not directly assess gut permeability, cytokine concentrations, or TLR4 expression, which limits our ability to conclusively confirm these pathways. Nevertheless, our findings align with established mechanistic models demonstrating that antibiotics reduce inflammatory signaling, as reflected by decreased morphine tolerance observed in germ-free animal models31.

Our study also demonstrated that a single dose of morphine induced analgesia, however, a history of antibiotic administration did not influence this response. Repeated morphine administration led to the development of tolerance, resulting in reduced analgesic effects, but the history of antibiotic treatment prevented the development of tolerance in these mice. These findings imply that the continued analgesic effect in the antibiotic–saline group likely reflects disruption of the microbiome–immune–neural axis—rather than a direct pharmacological interaction between antibiotics and morphine. Preclinical models show that depleting gut bacteria—using germ-free conditions or broad-spectrum antibiotic cocktails—markedly reduces the development of morphine tolerance, an effect reversed by restoring the microbiome37. Mechanistically, chronic morphine impairs gut barrier integrity, increasing permeability, bacterial translocation, and colonic IL-1β expression through TLR2/4 signaling38; this cascade activates microglia and heightens pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α), established mediators of opioid tolerance39. Notably, the use of non-systemic oral antibiotics like vancomycin preserves gut integrity, prevents neuroinflammation, and blocks tolerance without altering morphine’s pharmacodynamics40. These findings support that antibiotics likely maintain morphine’s analgesic efficacy by interrupting microbiome-driven inflammatory signaling—not through direct modulation of morphine itself.

The gut microbiome critically modulates morphine efficacy and tolerance; dysbiosis exacerbates tolerance, while butyrate-producing bacteria preserve analgesic effects13,41. Restoring microbiota balance via probiotics or dietary interventions may enhance morphine’s efficacy40. These findings align with previous studies suggesting that while acute morphine administration is highly effective for pain relief, chronic use leads to tolerance—such as through desensitization of µ-opioid receptors and increased cAMP production42,43. Our results further support the notion that antibiotics may mitigate tolerance by modulating the gut microbiota and its interactions with both the immune and nervous systems.

A key finding in this study was that both single and repeated morphine administrations induced anxiety-like behaviors following naloxone-induced withdrawal. Antibiotic administration alone also induced anxiety-like behaviors but did not modify the anxiety induced by morphine. Vancomycin-induced dysbiosis elevates Proteobacteria and reduces Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes, correlating with anxiety44. This aligns with reports of reduced BDNF and elevated CRH in vancomycin-treated mice, exacerbating anxiety and depressive behaviors45,46. Antibiotics such as vancomycin, neomycin, and a cocktail containing ampicillin, vancomycin, neomycin, and metronidazole differentially alter Proteobacteria levels, thereby influencing anxiety-related behaviors47. These results align with previous studies demonstrating that antibiotic exposure can influence anxiety-related behaviors48,49. The effects of antibiotics on anxiety are likely context-dependent, varying with factors such as timing, type, and dosage50. Antibiotics may induce anxiety-like effects by disrupting gut microbiota composition, leading to an increase in pathogenic bacteria such as Proteobacteria, a reduction in beneficial bacteria like Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, and elevated levels of inflammatory LPS. These changes can activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and raise cortisol levels, contributing to anxiety-like behaviors37. The gut-brain axis is pivotal in mood regulation51, and antibiotic-induced dysbiosis is linked to neurological disorders52. Antibiotics reduce serotonin and GABA production, exacerbating anxiety/depression50, though some studies paradoxically report anxiolytic effects53,54. Germ-free animals exhibit altered anxiety and social behaviors, emphasizing microbiota’s role in brain function16. These findings suggest that antibiotic-induced dysbiosis may exacerbate anxiety via neurochemical alterations, a process potentially mediated by reduced short-chain fatty acid production, as seen in microbiome studies55.

The direct effects of morphine on anxiety are well-established. Both acute and chronic morphine use can lead to anxiety, particularly during withdrawal56, as a result of neurochemical alterations in brain regions such as the central amygdala (CeA)57, which is essential for emotional regulation. The brain-derived neurotrophic factor signaling pathway within the CeA plays a crucial role in modulating anxiety58, with activation of this pathway alleviating anxiety-like behaviors and its inhibition exacerbating them59,60. Our findings emphasize the distinct effects of morphine withdrawal and antibiotic exposure on anxiety, highlighting the need for further investigation into their underlying mechanisms.

The effects of antibiotics alone in our study were multifaceted. Antibiotic treatment did not significantly alter the withdrawal response, but it reduced the severity of morphine withdrawal in mice receiving repeated doses. This effect likely stems from antibiotics’ ability to modulate the gut microbiota, reduce inflammation, and restore microbial balance, which in turn alleviates withdrawal-related symptoms. Previous studies have shown that broad-spectrum antibiotics can inhibit the development of physical dependence by enhancing glutamate uptake and restoring microbial homeostasis61,62. Antibiotics have also been shown to reduce the gut inflammation and gut barrier dysfunction that are typically exacerbated by chronic opioid use63. Vancomycin additionally disrupts blood glucose and short-chain fatty acid metabolism, while reducing microbial diversity and IgA levels64,65, further linking metabolic and immune pathways to withdrawal outcomes.

We used CFU counts as a practical proxy for antibiotic-induced dysbiosis. While this method lacks taxonomic specificity, it reliably signals broad microbial depletion associated with increased gut permeability, inflammation, anxiety-like behaviors, and reduced morphine tolerance66. Supporting studies reveal that chronic morphine or antibiotic exposure selectively diminishes beneficial taxa—including Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Clostridiales, and Roseburia—while enriching pathobionts such as Enterococcus and Proteobacteria, thereby disrupting the microbiota- gut–brain axis14,40,67. Crucially, it is the functional dysbiosis, marked by loss of barrier-supporting and anti-inflammatory microbes—not merely the total CFU reduction—that drives pathological outcomes, as evidenced by sequencing-based studies showing that depletion of these specific taxa alone is sufficient to induce anxiety-like behavior and compromise morphine tolerance68. Although metagenomic profiling was beyond our current scope, we intend to perform 16 S rRNA and shotgun sequencing in future work to directly associate these taxa with gut barrier function, inflammation, anxiety, and morphine analgesia. Nonetheless, our CFU-based results remain a robust foundation for understanding how microbiome-driven mechanisms influence morphine responses and behavior.

Our study revealed that the administration of antibiotics, whether alone or in combination with morphine, resulted in a significant reduction in CFUs in fresh fecal samples. The history of antibiotic treatment in mice receiving repeated doses of morphine further exacerbated this reduction, suggesting a cumulative impact on gut microbiota composition. Morphine itself has been shown to induce dysbiosis7, altering the production of microbial metabolites like short-chain fatty acids and reducing the abundance of beneficial bacteria69. In combination with antibiotics, the depletion of microbiota was even more pronounced, highlighting the powerful effects of antibiotics on gut microbial diversity.

One of the most striking findings of this study was the impact of antibiotic administration on brain electrical activity following naloxone-induced withdrawal. Repeated morphine administration increased brain excitability, which was significantly prevented by the history of antibiotic administration. This result underscores the protective role antibiotics may play in stabilizing neural activity during opioid withdrawal. Opioid withdrawal is often characterized by increased excitability and altered expression of voltage-gated channels and glial cell activation70,71. Antibiotics, by reducing neuroinflammation and microglial activation, help prevent this hyperexcitability and the associated withdrawal symptoms72,73.

We recorded EEG rhythms (δ, θ, α, β) over bilateral M1 and M2 via burr holes one day after naloxone-precipitated withdrawal to assess the impact of antibiotics on motor cortical excitability and opioid sensitivity. In the continued-morphine group, all bands (δ–β) were elevated, reflecting withdrawal-induced cortical hyperexcitability74,75. By contrast, the antibiotic + morphine group showed no such increases, while the antibiotic-saline group displayed elevated α and β only—suggesting selective shifts in sensorimotor processing and cortical inhibition. Electrophysiologically, increases in δ/θ typically reflect sedation or cognitive disengagement, α relates to sensorimotor integration, and β is associated with active motor control and GABAergic tone. Antibiotics appear to preserve motor cortical inhibitory function during withdrawal episodes, likely through gut-microbiome-mediated reductions in systemic inflammation and microglial activation—both key modulators of cortical rhythms via the gut–brain axis. Although direct opioid–microbiome interactions in M1/M2 are underexplored, our data align with broader evidence that microbiome depletion attenuates morphine-induced EEG alterations and preserves motor cortical excitability and analgesia.

Study limitations

Despite the valuable insights gained from this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics may have non-specific effects, making it difficult to attribute observed outcomes to specific microbial changes. Future studies using targeted microbiota manipulation or germ-free animal models would help clarify the precise mechanisms. Second, behavioral tests were performed within a constrained timeframe due to the pharmacological window of morphine, which may have introduced stress-related confounders despite efforts to control for them.

Third, while our study relied on CFU counts to assess microbiota changes, the observed variability in CFU among antibiotic-treated groups and the lack of metagenomic profiling limited our ability to identify specific microbial taxa or functional pathways associated with the attenuation of morphine tolerance. This limitation restricts our ability to directly link specific microbial taxa to the observed outcomes, a gap that could be addressed through advanced sequencing techniques, as suggested by recent microbiome research76.

Another important limitation is the lack of female subjects in this study. While we focused on male animals to reduce biological variability and establish a clear baseline, we acknowledge that sex differences in gut microbiota and morphine analgesia are well-documented. Females typically exhibit higher microbial diversity and distinct abundances of key taxa, such as Lactobacillus and Bacteroides, which are influenced by sex hormones77. Moreover, males generally experience stronger morphine analgesia, with different tolerance profiles and side effects compared to females78. Notably, recent preclinical findings suggest that antibiotic-induced changes in µ-opioid receptor binding may occur specifically in males, underscoring the importance of sex-dependent microbiome–opioid interactions79. Future research should include both sexes to determine whether antibiotic-induced dysbiosis similarly affects morphine outcomes in females and to enhance translational relevance.

Finally, the translational relevance of our findings to humans remains uncertain and requires validation in clinical settings, considering the complexity and interindividual variability of human microbiota.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the complex interactions between gut microbiota, antibiotics, and opioid use. Antibiotics not only affect the gut microbiota but also influence pain perception, opioid tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, and anxiety-like behaviors. While antibiotics alone do not directly alter morphine analgesia, their history of use can prevent tolerance and alleviate withdrawal-related anxiety and hyperexcitability. The mechanisms underlying these effects likely involve modulation of the gut-brain axis, gut microbiota composition, inflammation, and neurochemical signaling pathways. While our study did not directly assess mechanistic pathways such as gut permeability or cytokine expression, our findings are consistent with established literature, supporting a mechanistic framework involving microbiome-driven inflammatory signaling40. Future studies employing metagenomic profiling, tight junction assessments, and cytokine measurements are needed to elucidate these mechanisms and enhance the therapeutic potential of microbiota-targeted interventions in opioid dependence. These findings have significant implications for understanding the role of gut microbiota in opioid use and withdrawal, as well as the potential therapeutic value of antibiotics in managing opioid dependence and its associated behavioral alterations.

Materials and methods

Animals and housing

Adult male Balb/C mice (average weight: 25 ± 5 g) were obtained from the animal room of the School of Pharmacy, Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, Isfahan, Iran. The mice were housed in a standard animal facility under controlled environmental conditions: temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, humidity between 60% and 65%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. The animals were provided with a standard diet and had ad libitum access to tap water. Prior to the experiment, the mice were acclimated to the experimental environment for one week.

Drugs

The following drugs and chemicals were used in this study: urethane (Sigma Co., Germany), xylazine (Interchemi Co., Holland), sodium chloride 0.9% (Iranian Parenteral and Pharmaceutical Co., Tehran, Iran), naloxone (Toliddaru Pharma.Co., Tehran Iran) natamycin (Zagros farmed Pars Pharma Co., Borujerd, Iran), vancomycin (Daana pharma Co., Tabriz, Iran), bacitracin (Rooyan darou Co., Tehran, Iran) and morphine sulfate (Temad Co, Tehran, Iran).

Experimental design

Animals were randomly assigned to one of two primary groups: Control mice, which received oral saline daily for 11 days, and Antibiotic-treated mice, which received oral antibiotics daily for 11 days. Each primary group was further subdivided into three subgroups. The saline subgroup received intraperitoneal saline injections. The single-dose morphine (SD) subgroup received a single intraperitoneal injection of morphine on day 12, administered 30 min before behavioral testing. The continuous-dose morphine (CD) subgroup received intraperitoneal morphine twice daily (morning and afternoon) for three consecutive days (days 9–11), totaling six injections, followed by a single injection of morphine on day 12, 30 min before behavioral testing. On day 12, all subgroups received their respective intraperitoneal injections (morphine or saline) 30 min before behavioral assessments. Thirty minutes after injection, the Hot Plate test was performed, followed 5 min later (35 min post-injection) by the Tail Flick test. Two hours after morphine administration, a withdrawal test was conducted to evaluate withdrawal symptoms. This experimental sequence is consistent with established nociception protocols, ensuring that sequential assessments of different nociceptive pathways (paw vs. tail) provide reliable measures of analgesia without cross-interference80–82. Regarding mortality, a small number of animals in the morphine-treated groups did not survive the treatment period. Considering that naloxone was used to precipitate withdrawal and can acutely increase gut motility and fecal output, fresh fecal samples were collected on day 13, approximately 24 h after the withdrawal test. This timing was chosen based on previous studies (e.g., Ayoub et al., 2021) demonstrating that fecal sampling at this interval yields stable and reliable bacterial counts, minimizing the acute effects of naloxone on gut function83. Following fecal sample collection, the Elevated Plus Maze (EPM) test and electrophysiological recordings were performed (Fig. 6). All behavioral assessments were conducted by an experimenter who was blinded to the animals’ treatment groups to minimize bias.

Antibiotic treatment

Based on previously published protocols84, the antibiotic dose was initially estimated from the average water consumption of mice. To ensure precise and reproducible dosing, the exact dose per kilogram was calculated for each animal using its body weight and reported daily water intake. A concentrated stock solution of the antibiotic cocktail was then prepared for oral gavage every 12 h, thereby replicating the total daily dose from earlier studies in a controlled manner.

The antibiotic cocktail consisted of natamycin (6 mg/ml), bacitracin (20 mg/ml), vancomycin (5 mg/ml), and neomycin (50 mg/ml) and was administered at 10 ml/kg body weight via gavage twice daily for 11 days. Control groups received sterile saline on the same schedule. Solutions were freshly prepared before each administration and stored under appropriate conditions to maintain stability and prevent hydrolysis. Because antibiotics were administered directly via controlled gavage, each animal received a precise, predetermined dose, eliminating the need to estimate intake based on water consumption.

Induction of morphine tolerance

A three-day cumulative dosing regimen was used to induce morphine tolerance and dependence85. Morphine was administered subcutaneously at 30 mg/kg in the morning and 45 mg/kg in the afternoon on the first day, increasing to 60 mg/kg and 90 mg/kg on the second day, and 120 mg/kg on the third day86. Tolerance was assessed on day 12 by measuring the attenuation of analgesic effects using the tail flick and hot plate tests after a 5 mg/kg morphine dose87.

Hot-plate-test

The hot plate test, adapted slightly from Eddy’s methodology, was employed to assess analgesic efficacy. Mice were placed on a hot plate (Tajhiz Gostar Omid Iranian, Iran) maintained at 55 ± 1 °C, and pain-related behaviors, including paw licking or jumping, were recorded. Each mouse was individually tested with a 90-second cut-off latency, which serves as a maximal safety limit to prevent thermal injury. Animals were continuously monitored, and testing was immediately terminated if any signs of discomfort or distress were observed before reaching this limit. The latency to the initial pain response was measured 30 min after morphine administration on day 12 of the study.

Tail-flick test

The tail flick test was used to assess response latency 35 min after morphine administration on day 12. A radiant heat tail-flick device (Tajhiz gostar omid iranian, Iran) was used to measure the initial reaction time. The distal 1–2 cm of the tail was placed on a radiant heat source, and the time taken for the mice to flick or withdraw their tail from the heat stimulus was recorded. A cutoff time of 15 s was set to prevent potential thermal damage to the tail.

Withdrawal syndrome signs

Two hours after the morphine injection on day 12, each mouse received a naloxone injection at a dose of (1 mg/kg)88,89. The mice were then placed in a clear glass cylinder and observed for 30 min by an investigator blinded to the treatment groups to assess withdrawal symptoms. The severity of withdrawal was scored based on the frequency of behaviors such as jumping, body grooming, fecal excretion, wet-dog shakes, and teeth chattering, following Maldonado’s study90. A score of 1 was assigned for 1–3 occurrences, 2 for 4–6 occurrences, and 3 for more than 7 occurrences of each symptom. The total score for all symptoms was calculated after the withdrawal assessment.

EPM test

Anxiety-like behavior was evaluated 24 h after naloxone administration using the EPM method. The EPM apparatus, placed 80 cm above the ground, consisted of a central platform (10 cm × 10 cm) connected to two open arms (50 cm × 10 cm) and two enclosed arms (10 cm × 10 cm × 50 cm). For the assessment, mice were placed in the center of the maze, facing an open arm, and allowed to explore the environment for 5 min. The time spent in the arms and the number of arm entries were recorded, and anxiety-like behavior was quantified using the Anxiety Index, calculated as:

Bacterial enumeration

Bacterial enumeration was performed in the Microbiology Laboratory, Department of Microbiology, Isfahan University of Medical Sciences. Fresh Fecal samples collected aseptically were immediately transferred into sterile 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes containing 1 mL of 1× phosphate buffered saline. In transport, samples were kept on ice. Upon arrival, full-speed vertexing for 30 s was applied to each Fecal sample to homogenize it completely and distribute the bacterial cells more evenly. For serial dilution, 100 µL of homogenate was transferred into 900 µL of sterile PBS in sequential 1.5 mL tubes, to make 10-fold dilution up to 10 − 6.

For the total bacterial count, 100 µL of each dilution was plated onto Blood Agar (Oxoid, UK) plates, which support a wide variety of gut bacteria. Triplicate plating of each dilution was performed to ensure statistical reliability. The plates were then placed in an anaerobic environment using a standard Anoxomat (Mart Microbiology B.V., The Netherlands) system and incubated at 37 °C for 48 to 72 h. After incubation, colony counts were carried out for all plates with 30–300 colonies, according to statistical accuracy. Electrophysiology recording

Mice were anesthetized with a combination of 1.2 g/kg urethane and 20 mg/kg xylazine, administered intraperitoneally, and secured in a stereotactic apparatus for electrode implantation92. Small holes were drilled over the primary (M1) and secondary (M2) motor cortices in both hemispheres. A reference electrode was placed on the right hemisphere, and a recording electrode was positioned on the left hemisphere, both resting on the dura mater for EEG recording.

EEG signals were recorded using a system from Science Beam Company, Iran, which transferred the data to a data acquisition system for processing and analysis of alpha, beta, delta, and theta wave power. The EEG signals were filtered within a frequency range of 0.1–70 Hz and classified into delta (0.1–3.9 Hz), theta (4–7.9 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), and beta (13.1–30 Hz) bands.

Brain electrical activity was monitored for 60 min. A 20-minute baseline period was recorded prior to the administration of a 5 mg/kg morphine injection, followed by continued recording for an additional 40 min. After completing the experiment, the animals were euthanized with a lethal dose of urethane.

Statistics

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27 for Windows and GraphPad Prism version 9. Normality of data distribution was first assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. In this study, animals were first divided into two groups—control and antibiotic-treated—to evaluate the effect of antibiotics, irrespective of morphine administration. Subsequently, animals were further subdivided into three subgroups, receiving saline, single-dose morphine (SD), or chronic-dose morphine (CD), to assess the effect of morphine independent of antibiotic treatment, yielding a total of six experimental groups. Between-subjects effects—including the main effects of antibiotic treatment, morphine administration, and their interaction—were analyzed using univariate ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Additionally, one-way ANOVA was employed, followed by Tukey’s test for pairwise group comparisons. For datasets that violated parametric assumptions, we used the Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric ANOVA, followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparisons test to identify specific group differences (All statistically significant differences shown in the figures are based on these respective analyses); both sets of analyses are detailed in the Results section. For EEG data, repeated measurements over time required evaluation of both between-subjects effects (antibiotic treatment, morphine exposure, and their interaction) and within-subject effects (time). Repeated measures ANOVA, followed by post hoc tests, was used to assess temporal changes in EEG signals. Pairwise comparisons among the groups for each wave were conducted using Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, with individual values shown for each animal in the corresponding figures. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and SEM is used to indicate the precision of the estimated mean.
