Information Inequalities for Five Random Variables
E. P. Csirmaz ∗, L. Csirmaz ∗†

Abstract
. The entropic region is formed by the collection of the Shannon entropies of all subvectors of finitely many jointly distributed discrete random variables. For four or more variables the structure of the entropic region is mostly unknown. We utilize a variant of the Maximum Entropy Method to delimit the five-variable entropy region. This method adds copies of some of the random variables in generations. A significant reduction in computational complexity, achieved through theoretical considerations and by harnessing the inherent symmetries, allowed us to calculate all five-variable non-Shannon inequalities provided by the first nine generations. Based on the results, we define two infinite collections of such inequalities, and prove them to be entropy inequalities. We investigate downward closed subsets of non-negative lattice points that parameterize these collections, based on which we develop an algorithm to enumerate all extremal inequalities. The discovered set of entropy inequalities is conjectured to characterize the applied method completely. Keywords: Entropy; information inequalities; polymatroid; polyhedral geometry. Classification numbers: 05B35, 26A12, 52B12, 90C29, 94A17, 52B40, 90C27



1.
Introduction

Many important mathematical problems can be reduced to the following
question: does a collection of finite random variables exist such that
the entropies of the variable subsets satisfy certain linear constraints?
Examples include, but are not limited to, channel coding
[
13
]

and network coding in particular
[
36
]
, estimating the
efficiency of secret sharing schemes
[
3
,
4
,
20
]
,
questions about matroid representations
[
2
]
, guessing games

[
32
]
, extracting information from common strings in cryptography

[
17
]
, additive combinatorics
[
23
]
, and finding
conditional independence inference rules
[
34
]
.

The
entropy function
of finitely many discrete random variables

\langle\xi_{i}:i\in N\rangle
indexed by the fixed finite set
N
maps the
non-empty subsets
I\subseteq N
to the Shannon entropy
\mathbf{H}(\xi_{I})
of the
variable set
\xi_{I}=\langle x_{i}:i\in I\rangle
, see
[
35
]
. The

entropy region
, denoted by
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
, is the range of the entropy
function; it is a part of the
2^{|N|}\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mu1
-dimensional Euclidean space where
the coordinates are labeled by non-empty subsets of
N
. Entropies are
non-negative real numbers, and thus the entropy region lies in the
non-negative orthant of this Euclidean space. It is delimited by a
collection of homogeneous linear
inequalities corresponding to the non-negativity of basic Shannon
information measures
[
35
]
. Points satisfying all these
inequalities form the
Shannon-bound
; the Shannon-bound is denoted by
\Gamma_{\!N}
.

N. Pippenger argued in
[
31
]
that linear inequalities bounding
the entropic region
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
encode the fundamental laws of Information
Theory and determine the limits of information transmission and data
compression. The long-standing problem of whether a linear information
inequality can properly cut into the Shannon bound was settled in 1998 by
Zhang and Yeung
[
37
]
by exhibiting the first example of such a
non-Shannon information inequality. Their discovery initiated intensive
research. The phrase
Copy Lemma
was coined by Dougherty et
al.
[
15
]
to describe the general method distilled from the original
Zhang–Yeung construction. The Copy Lemma has been applied successfully to
generate several hundred sporadic and a couple of infinite families of
non-Shannon entropy inequalities for
\Gamma^{*}_{\!4}
, see

[
8
,
15
,
28
]
. A different method, utilizing an
information-theoretic lemma attributed to Ahlswede and Körner
[
1
]
,
was proposed in
[
24
]
; later it was shown to be equivalent to a
special case of the Copy Lemma
[
21
]
.

Our method to obtain five-variable non-Shannon entropy inequalities is based
on a more general paradigm of which the Copy Lemma is a special
case
[
12
]
. Derived from the principle of maximum entropy

[
18
]
, it is called
MEM
, short for Maximum Entropy Method.
For more details, see Section
3
.

Previous works on generating and applying non-Shannon entropy inequalities,
such as
[
4
,
8
,
9
,
15
,
29
,
34
]
, focused
on the four-variable case, and only a few sporadic five-variable non-Shannon
inequalities have been discovered, such as the MMRV inequality from

[
24
]
. This is the first work that provides a method which
generates an infinite collection of non-Shannon bounds on the
five-variable entropy region
\Gamma^{*}_{\!5}
.
Compared to the four-variable case,
there are significant challenges, both theoretical and computational. The
four-variable entropy region
\Gamma^{*}_{\!4}
sits in the
15
-dimensional Euclidean
space, while the five-variable region
\Gamma^{*}_{\!5}
is
31
-dimensional. The
structure of the Shannon bound
\Gamma_{\!4}
is well-understood: it has
41

extremal directions, and only
6
of them have no entropic points. The entropy region

\Gamma^{*}_{\!4}
has an inner polyhedral cone where it fills its Shannon bound, and
has six isomorphic “protrusions” towards the six exceptional extremal
directions, each protrusion surrounded by
15
hyperplanes of which
14
come from the
Shannon bound
[
25
]
. Only the protrusions contribute to new entropy
inequalities, and their dimension can be reduced to
10
. Computational
results about
\Gamma^{*}_{\!4}
can be obtained by computing vertices and facets of
numerous
implicitly defined
10
-dimensional polyhedra
[
9
]
. In
contrast, the Shannon bound
\Gamma_{\!5}
of the five-variable entropy region has
117,983 extremal directions
[
33
]
, and for a few of them it is
not even known whether they contain an entropic point or not.
No structural reduction similar
to the four-variable case is available, and it is not known whether such a
reduction exists or not. Computations about
\Gamma^{*}_{\!5}
can still be reduced
to
25
-dimensional polyhedral enumeration problems (although with significantly
larger number of constraints than in the
4
-variable case). The complexity
of enumeration problems typically doubles when the dimension increases by one,
making such high-dimensional enumeration problems practically intractable.

We overcome this computational difficulty by applying a particular variant of
the Maximum Entropy Method. This variant, working in generations,
first reduces the problem dimension
from
31
to
19
, and then, at each generation,
adds extra copies of some of the random variables increasing the problem
dimension again. Theoretical
considerations and harnessing the inherent symmetry allowed us to
complete the associated polyhedral computations up to nine generations. The output was
the complete list of five-variable non-Shannon inequalities provided by
the first nine generations.
Based on the experimental results, we define an infinite
collection of five-variable inequalities that we
prove
to be provided
by this MEM variant—in particular, they are valid non-Shannon
entropy inequalities—, and

conjecture
this collection to be complete, that is, no additional
inequalities are yielded by this MEM variant. The collection of the
inequalities is parametrized by finite,
downward closed subsets of the non-negative lattice points of the
plane. Some of the inequalities in our collection are consequences of the
others; those that are not, are called
extremal
. We developed an incremental
algorithm that enumerates, from generation to generation, the parameters yielding the
extremal inequalities, in complete agreement with the computational results.
Finally, we have looked at the large-scale
behavior of the extremal inequalities, and pictured how these inequalities
delimit a
3
-dimensional cross-section of
\Gamma^{*}_{\!5}
.

The remaining part of the paper is organized as follows. Notations are
recalled in Section
2
. Section
3
describes the
special variant of the Maximum Entropy Method we apply to
\Gamma^{*}_{\!5}
.
Section
4
discusses possible simplifications, including
how symmetry can be utilized, and how the MEM parameters were chosen.
Section
5
describes the chosen coordinate systems,
polyhedral computations and their results.
Section
6
presents the five-variable inequalities we obtained,
paving the way for the definition of two infinite families of such
inequalities in Section
7
. Additional
theoretical results, including the proof that inequalities in these
families are indeed generated by the MEM method, are presented in Section
7
.
Section
8
discusses methods that can recognize
extremal inequalities, the incremental algorithm that
enumerates the extremal inequalities for each MEM generation, describes
the large-scale behavior of the new inequalities,
and investigates the delimited
part of the five-variable entropy
region. Finally, Section
9
summarizes our work, lists open
questions, and provides directions for further work.

Preliminaries

In this paper all sets are finite. Capital letters, such as

frequently omitted, thus,
Nij

. The
difference of two sets is written as
A\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5muB

and

are
disjoint sets. A partition of
N
is a collection of non-empty
disjoint subsets of
N
whose union equals
N
.

A
discrete random variable
\xi
takes its values from a finite set

\mathcal{X}
, called

. The probability that
\xi
takes

x\in\mathcal{X}
is denoted by
\Pr(\xi=x)
, or simply by
\Pr(x)

when the random variable
\xi
is clear from the context.
Suppose
\xi
is defined on the direct product
\mathcal{X}=\prod_{i\in N}\mathcal{X}_{i}
for some finite set
N
, called the
base set
. For
a non-empty
A\subseteq N
the
marginal
\xi_{A}
is defined on the
product alphabet
\mathcal{X}_{A}=\prod_{i\in A}\mathcal{X}_{i}
so that the
probability of
y\in\mathcal{X}_{A}
is the sum of the probability of those

x\in\mathcal{X}
whose projection to
\mathcal{X}_{A}
equals

\Pr(y)=\sum\{\Pr(x):x\mathord{\upharpoonright}A=y\}.

To emphasize that
\xi
is defined on a product space, we write

\xi=(\xi_{i}:i\in N)
, and say that the random variables
\xi_{i}
are

distributed jointly
. The Shannon entropy of the distribution

\xi
is defined as

\mathbf{H}(\xi)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}-\Pr(x)\log\Pr(x)

with the convention that
0\log 0=0
.
If
\xi=(\xi_{i}:i\in N)
is a joint distribution, then we write
\mathbf{H}_{\xi}(A)

for
\mathbf{H}(\xi_{A})
. The index
\xi
is also dropped when it is clear from
the context. By convention,
\mathbf{H}(\emptyset)=0
. The entropies
\mathbf{H}_{\xi}(A)

are arranged into a vector indexed by the non-empty subsets
A
of
N
. This
vector is the
entropy profile
of the distribution
\xi
. The
collection of these
(2^{|N|}\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mu1)
-dimensional vectors forms the

entropy region
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
. Elements of
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
are considered
interchangeably as vectors, as points in this Euclidean space, and as
functions assigning non-negative real numbers to non-empty subsets of the
base set
N
.

Notions of conditional entropy, mutual information, and conditional mutual
information from Information Theory are formally extended to the functional
form of these vectors. If
f
is any function on subsets of
N
, then for
subsets
A,B,C,D
of

the following forms will be used as abbreviations:

\displaystyle f(A\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muB)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}f(AB)-f(B),

\displaystyle f(A,B)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}f(A)+f(B)-f(AB),

\displaystyle f(A,B\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muC)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}f(AC)+f(BC)-f(ABC)-f(C),\mbox{ and}

\displaystyle f[A,B,C,D]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}-f(A,B)+f(A,B\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muC)+f(A,B\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muD)+f(C,D).

The first three expressions are called
conditional entropy
,
mutual
information
, and
conditional mutual information
, respectively.
The last line defines the
Ingleton expression
. An
entropy function is not defined on the empty set, nevertheless,
f(\emptyset)=0
will
be assumed whenever convenient. In particular,
f(A,B\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0mu\emptyset)
and

f(A,B)
are the same expressions. Frequently, when clear
from the context, the function
f
is omitted before the parenthesized
expression. Additionally, if applied to singletons, the Ingleton expression
is written without commas. An example is the inequality

[abcd\mkern 1.5mu]+(a,b\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muz)+(b,z\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0mua)+(a,z\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0mub)+3(z\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muab)\geqslant 0.

Shannon inequalities state the non-negativity of the conditional entropy,
mutual information, and conditional mutual information for all subsets
A
,

B
,
C
of the base set
N
. They are consequences of the unique minimal
set of such inequalities, called

, see

[

]
, listed in (

) and (

(B1)

f(i\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muN\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mui)\geqslant 0

(B2)

f(a,b\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muK)\geqslant 0

The collection of all

-dimensional vectors (or points, or

\Gamma_{\!N}
. It
is a natural outer bound for the entropy region
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
.
\Gamma_{\!N}
is a pointed
polyhedral cone
[
38
]

by the basic Shannon inequalities.
Polymatroids
are elements of

Linear inequalities valid for all polymatroids are consequences of the

.

The closure of the entropic region is a pointed convex full-dimensional

[
35
]
, and only its boundary points can be non-entropic

[
26
]

The polymatroid

, or

Clearly, if both

. The polymatroid

-representable polymatroids. By the previous remark,

-linear polymatroids form a closed cone. Finally,

-linear for some field

.

Following a compactness argument, if

-representable, then it is

, meaning that

Linear polymatroids on the base set

f[bcad\mkern 1.5mu]\geqslant 0,&f[bdac]\geqslant 0,&f[cdab\mkern 1.5mu]\geqslant 0,\end{array}

3.
The Maximum Entropy Method

In general terms, the principle of maximum entropy is easy to formulate:

if a probability distribution is specified only partially, take the
one with the largest entropy,
see, e.g.,
[
18
]
. In the
particular case applied here “partial specification”
means fixing some, but not all, marginal distributions. To be more concrete,
suppose
\xi
is distributed jointly on the base set
N
. Partition

N
into three non-empty subsets as
N=Y\mathop{{\cup}\mkern-2.0mu^{*}}X\mathop{{\cup}\mkern-2.0mu^{*}}Z
. Take
n\geqslant 1

disjoint copies of
Y
and
m\geqslant 1
disjoint copies of
Z
to form the
enlarged base set

N^{*}=Y_{1}\mathop{{\cup}\mkern-2.0mu^{*}}\cdots\mathop{{\cup}\mkern-2.0mu^{*}}Y_{n}\mathop{{\cup}\mkern-2.0mu^{*}}X\mathop{{\cup}\mkern-2.0mu^{*}}Z_{1}\mathop{{\cup}\mkern-2.0mu^{*}}\cdots\mathop{{\cup}\mkern-2.0mu^{*}}Z_{m}.

Consider the collection of those distributions
\xi^{*}
on
N^{*}
whose marginals
on
Y_{i}X
are equal to
\xi_{Y\mkern-2.0muX}
, and marginals on
XZ_{j}
are equal to

\xi_{XZ}
. That is, the marginal of
\xi
on

Y\mkern-2.0muX
and the marginals of
\xi^{*}
on all
Y_{i}X
are the same as well as the
marginal of
\xi
on
XZ
and the marginals of
\xi^{*}
on
XZ_{j}
. This
collection of distributions is not empty, as one can take each
Y_{i}
to be
the same as
Y
,
and each
Z_{j}
to be the same as
Z
. The total entropy is a strictly concave
function of the probability masses, and fixing certain marginals imposes
linear constraints on those masses. Consequently, there is a unique
optimal distribution
\xi^{*}
with maximum total entropy, see
[
6
]
.
Although structural properties of the maximum entropy distributions
are mainly unknown, they are known to satisfy numerous conditional
independencies. For this particular case, these are stated as
Lemma
1
below.

Lemma 1
.

In the distribution with maximum total entropy, the subsets
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
and
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m}
are completely conditionally independent
over
X
.

Proof.

If some of the conditional independence statements do not hold,
then one can redefine the distribution keeping the specified
marginals while increasing the total entropy. For details, see

[
12
]
.
∎

Since identical distributions have identical entropy profiles, Lemma

1
immediately implies that an entropic polymatroid
has an
n,m
-copy
as defined below:

Definition 1
.

Let
f
be a polymatroid on
N
, and partition

into three non-empty

be disjoint copies of

on the base set

is an

(i)

restricted to

is isomorphic to

for

(ii)

is isomorphic to

(iii)

The special version of the Maximum Entropy Method used in this paper is
based on the fact that entropic polymatroids have

-copies.
For fixed integers

, polymatroids on

that have

This is proved as Claim

Maximum Entropy Method

(special case)
.

Fix the base set
N
and the partition
N=Y\mathop{{\cup}\mkern-2.0mu^{*}}X\mathop{{\cup}\mkern-2.0mu^{*}}Z
. For
n,m\geqslant 1

\mathcal{C}_{n,m}
be the polyhedral cone of those

-copy. Compute all
bounding facets of

as homogeneous linear inequalities,
and delete those which are consequences of the basic Shannon

non-Shannon inequalities provided by
the partition

and the numbers

and

.

Let us remark that while the maximum entropy extension is unique, the

-copy in Definition

is typically not,
as the definition captures only a small part of the properties of the maximum
entropy extension.

Next we prove that

Claim 2

Polymatroids
(f,N)
with an

Proof.

Consider the polymatroid

(2^{|N|}\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mu1)

N

\mathbf{x}

XZ
, and
\mathbf{u}

Z

d_{1}+d_{2}=2^{|N|}\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mu1
.
Similarly, let
\mathbf{y}
be the vector formed from
the values of the
n,m
-copy polymatroid
f^{*}
as indexed by the subsets of
N^{*}
.
The vector
\mathbf{y}
has dimension
d_{3}=2^{|N^{*}|}\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mu1
.
Now,
(f^{*},N^{*})
is a polymatroid if the vector
\mathbf{y}
satisfies all
linear inequality constraints imposed by the basic Shannon
inequalities in (

) and (
B2
); and it is an

n,m
-copy of
f
if, additionally, the composed vector

(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

satisfies the equality constraints corresponding to conditions
(i)—(iii) in Definition
1
. Consequently, there exists
a matrix
M
with
d_{1}+d_{3}
columns, depending only on the partition

Y\mkern-2.0muXZ
and the numbers
n
and
m
, so that
f
has an
n,m
-copy
if and only if there is a vector
\mathbf{y}
satisfying
M\mkern 1.5mu{\cdot}\mkern 1.5mu(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\top}\geqslant 0
. Similarly,
(f,N)
is a polymatroid
if, for another matrix
B
with
(d_{1}+d_{2})
columns expressing the basic
Shannon inequalities for
Y\mkern-2.0muXZ
, we have
B\mkern 1.5mu{\cdot}\mkern 1.5mu(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})^{\top}\geqslant 0
. Thus the collection of polymatroids on
N

that have an
n,m
-copy is the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}=\big\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}:{}

\displaystyle B\mkern 1.5mu{\cdot}\mkern 1.5mu(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})^{T}\geqslant 0,\mbox{ and}

\displaystyle M\mkern 1.5mu{\cdot}\mkern 1.5mu(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\top}\geqslant 0\mbox{ for some }\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{3}}\big\}.

Here

and

are matrices with integer entries; these matrices depend only
on
Y\mkern-2.0muXZ
,
n
, and
m
. Since
\mathcal{Q}
is the intersection of
a polyhedral cone and the projection of a polyhedral cone, it is
also a polyhedral cone, as claimed.
∎

From the proof it is clear that the
\mathbf{u}
-part of
\mathcal{Q}

is constrained only by the basic Shannon inequalities encoded in the
matrix
B
. Furthermore, constraints on
\mathbf{x}
imposed by the
first condition are contained in the second one.
Thus, it suffices to consider the bounding facets of

\mathcal{Q}^{*}=\big\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}:M\mkern 1.5mu{\cdot}\mkern 1.5mu(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\top}\geqslant 0\mbox{ for some }\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{3}}\big\}

(2)

for new entropy inequalities. This is because, due to the duality theorem
of linear programming
[
38
]
, facets of
\mathcal{Q}
are
convex linear combinations of facets of
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
and facets
corresponding to the basic Shannon inequalities for the base set
Y\mkern-2.0muXZ
.

Coordinates in
\mathbf{x}
are indexed by subsets of
Y\mkern-2.0muX
and

XZ
, so the inequalities provided by the bounding facets of

\mathcal{Q}^{*}
contain only elements of the restrictions
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}Y\mkern-2.0muX
and
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}XZ
. We emphasize that these restrictions are not
arbitrary polymatroids on
Y\mkern-2.0muX
and
XZ
with a common restriction on
X
, as they
also have a common extension, namely
f
. Conditions ensuring the
existence of such a common extension are assumed to hold, see

[
10
]
, and they do not contribute towards the
non-Shannon entropy inequalities we are searching for.

4.
What to compute? How to compute?

As discussed in Section
3
, the task of finding new
non-Shannon entropy inequalities implied by the existence of an

n,m
-copy reduces to enumerating all facets of the polyhedral cone

\mathcal{Q}^{*}
defined in (
2
).
However, without further reduction, this polyhedral computation is intractable
even for small parameter values. Therefore, in this Section we look at some
general methods to reduce the complexity of the computation, and then
discuss how the number of elements in the
Y\mkern-2.0muXZ
partition were chosen.

4.1.
Tight and modular parts

Both the polyhedral region
\Gamma_{\!N}
and the closure of the entropy region
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}

decompose naturally into direct sums of
modular
and

tight
parts, see
[
29
]
. To discuss this result,
let us first introduce some notation. For
i\in N
define the function

\mathbfit r_{i}
on the non-empty subsets
A
of
N
as

\mathbfit r_{i}:A\mapsto\left\{\mkern-6.0mu\begin{array}[]{l@{~~~}l}1&\mbox{ if }i\in A,\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise. }\end{array}\right.

(3)

Non-negative multiples of
\mathbfit r_{i}
are clearly entropic polymatroids;

modular polymatroids
are, by definition,
the conic combinations of the vectors
\{\mathbfit r_{i}:i\in N\}
.
For a polymatroid
(f,N)
, a singleton
i\in N
and a real number

\alpha\geqslant 0
, the function
f\mathord{\downarrow}^{i}_{\alpha}
is defined on the
non-empty subsets of
N
as follows:

f\mathord{\downarrow}^{i}_{\alpha}:A\mapsto\min\{f(Ai)\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mu\alpha,~f(A)\}.

When
\alpha
is set to
f(i\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muN\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mui)
,
f\mathord{\downarrow}^{i}_{\alpha}
is denoted
simply by
f\mathord{\downarrow}^{i}
. Note that for
i\notin A
we have

f(Ai)-f(A)\geqslant f(N)-f(N\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mui)=f(i\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muN\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mui)
by submodularity.
Consequently,
f\mathord{\downarrow}^{i}
can be written explicitly as

f\mathord{\downarrow}^{i}(A)=\left\{\mkern-6.0mu\begin{array}[]{l@{~~~}l}f(A)-f(i\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muN\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mui)&\mbox{ if }i\in A,\\
f(A)&\mbox{ if }i\notin A.\end{array}\right.

Therefore
f=f\mathord{\downarrow}^{i}+f(i\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muN\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mui)\mkern 1.5mu\mathbfit r_{i}
, where
\mathbfit r_{i}
is the
polymatroid defined in (
3
).
The result of
tightening
f
at
i
is the function
f\mathord{\downarrow}^{i}
.
The
tight part of
f
, denoted by
f\mathord{\downarrow}
, is the result of
tightening
f
at every element of its base set
N=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{n}\}
:

f\mathord{\downarrow}=(\cdots(f\mathord{\downarrow}^{i_{1}})\mathord{\downarrow}^{i_{2}}\cdots)\mathord{\downarrow}^{i_{n}}.

This result is independent of the order in which the reductions are applied, which is also shown by the decomposition formula

f=f\mathord{\downarrow}+\sum_{i\in N}f(i\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muN\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mui)\mkern 1.5mu\mathbfit r_{i}.

The proof of the following lemma can be found in
[
12
]

or
[
27
]
. In this paper only the first part of the lemma is needed,
which can be verified by direct computation.

Lemma 3
.

Let
0\leqslant\alpha\leqslant f(i)
.
If
f
is a polymatroid, then
f\mathord{\downarrow}^{i}_{\alpha}
is also a polymatroid.
If, in addition,
f
is almost entropic, then so is
f\mathord{\downarrow}^{i}_{\alpha}
.
∎

Accordingly,
f\mathord{\downarrow}
(the
tight part
of
f
) is
a polymatroid, and it is also almost entropic (aent) whenever
f
is aent. The difference

f-f\mathord{\downarrow}
is the
modular part
, and it is a modular polymatroid.
This decomposition
of
f
into a tight and a modular part is unique, and both parts are
aent if
f
is aent.

The cone formed by
the modular polymatroids over
N
is
|N|
-dimensional, and is generated by the
linearly independent vectors
\{\mathbfit r_{i}:i\in N\}
. The cone of tight
polymatroids
is orthogonal to this (modular) cone, and so to every vector
\mathbfit r_{i}
;
and is bounded by the hyperplanes
corresponding to the basic Shannon inequalities in (
B2
). The
cone of tight, almost entropic polymatroids is similarly orthogonal
to the modular cone. A consequence of this decomposition
is that linear bounds on the entropic cone also decompose into bounds
on the tight part and bounds on the modular part—the latter being
trivial, that is, a Shannon inequality. The normal
\mathbf{n}
of
a supporting hyperplane of the
tight part is necessarily orthogonal to all vectors
\mathbfit r_{i}
, that is, the
scalar products
\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbfit r_{i}
are zero. Consequently, if the
normal has the coordinates
\mathbf{n}=\langle t_{I}:I\subseteq N\rangle
,
then the sum
\sum\{t_{I}:i\in I\}
is zero for every
i\in N
. For
this reason, these hyperplanes are called
balanced
.
The tight component of any entropy inequality is balanced, and it
is also an entropy inequality. This fact is equivalent to saying that
every entropy inequality can be strengthened to become a balanced one,
see
[
7
]
.

From the above it follows that the facets of the cone
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
belong to two disjoint groups. There are
|N|
(trivial, Shannon)
facets that bound the modular part of
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
, and the rest that
bound the tight part. The normal vectors of the facets in the second
group are balanced, and only they can provide non-Shannon inequalities.
Therefore, it suffices to consider only the tight part of
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
.
This part is generated by a smaller collection of polymatroids, has
fewer dimensions, and so can be handled more efficiently.

Claim 4
.

The tight part of
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
is generated by the
n,m
-copies of the
polymatroids
f
on
N=Y\mkern-2.0muXZ
that are

(i)
tight;

(ii)
satisfy
f(Y,Z\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muX)=0
; and

(iii)
for all
y\in Y
,
f(y\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muY\mkern-2.0muX\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5muy)=0
, and for all

,
f(z\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muXZ\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5muz)=0
.

Observe that the tightness of
f
at the elements of
Y
and
Z

follows from condition (iii) and submodularity, thus (i) is
relevant only for elements of
X
.

Proof.

Let
f^{*}
be an
n,m
-copy of
f
. In the definition of
\mathcal{Q}^{*}

only the values of
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}Y\mkern-2.0muX
and the values of
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}XZ
are
used. Therefore,
f
can be replaced with any other polymatroid that
has the same restrictions. Such a polymatroid is
f^{*}\mathord{\upharpoonright}Y_{1}XZ_{1}

by part (i) of Definition
1
, which gives (ii). For (iii)
let
y\in Y
, and
\alpha=f(y\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muY\mkern-2.0muX\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5muy)
. Apply Lemma
3

to
f^{*}
and all instances of

in the copies

to get the new
polymatroid
g^{*}
. Denoting the instance of
y
in
Y_{1}
by
y_{1}
,
the lemma provides
g^{*}(y_{1}\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muY_{1}X\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5muy_{1})=0
. In addition,
g^{*}
is an

n,m
-copy of its restriction to
Y_{1}XZ_{1}
. Since this restriction
and the polymatroid

differ only by a modular shift on subsets of

and
XZ
, their tight parts are the same. A similar reduction on
elements of
Z
, and finally on elements of

provide the statement.
∎

Using Claim
4
, the number of columns in the
constraint matrix
M
in (

It is so since, by the tightness of
f
,
f^{*}(Ai)=f^{*}(A)
holds for many subsets

A
of
N^{*}
with few elements, and this equality implies
f^{*}(Bi)=f^{*}(B)
for every
A\subseteq B\subset N^{*}
.

4.2.
Symmetry

The inherent symmetry in the
n,m
-copy allows for another significant
complexity
reduction. Let
\pi
be one of the
(n!\mkern 1.5mum!)
permutations of the
base set
N^{*}
that permutes the subsets
Y_{i}
and the subsets
Z_{j}

independently.
This permutation naturally extends to the subsets of
N^{*}
, and then
to the polymatroids on
N^{*}
. The
n,m
-copy
f^{*}
of
f
is

symmetric
if it is invariant for each such permutation
\pi
,
that is,
f^{*}(A)=(\pi f^{*})(A)=f^{*}(\pi A)
for all
A\subseteq N^{*}
.

Claim 5
.

f
has an
n,m
-copy if and only if it has a symmetric
n,m
-copy.

Proof.

If
f^{*}
is an
n,m
-copy of
f
, then clearly so is
\pi f^{*}
. Since
conditions (ii) and (iii) in Definition
1
are linear,
they are also satisfied by the average of all such permutations of
f^{*}
,
that is, by the polymatroid
g^{*}=(n!\mkern 1.5mum!)^{-1}\sum_{\pi}\pi f^{*}
.
Clearly,
g^{*}
is a symmetric
n,m
-copy of
f
.
∎

Symmetry alone reduces the number of auxiliary variables in the
definition of
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
from exponential in
n
and
m
to polynomial
in these parameters.

4.3.
No new inequality

In some cases, the computations required by the Maximum Entropy
Method of Section
3
can be simplified further, or even
completely avoided. The first claim of this subsection states that certain
polymatroids do not contribute to new entropy inequalities.

Claim 6
.

Suppose
f
is a polymatroid on
N=Y\mkern-2.0muXZ
, and
f
restricted to
X
is
modular. Then
f
has an
n,m
-copy for every
n
and

.

Proof.

The statement follows from the following lemma by induction.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Suppose that the polymatroids
(f_{1},Y\mkern-2.0muX)
and
(f_{2},XZ)
have a common

which is modular. Then there is a polymatroid

(g,Y\mkern-2.0muXZ)
that extends both

and

such that

.

Proof.

For
I\subseteq Y
,
J\subseteq X
and
K\subseteq Z
define

g(IJK)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\min_{L}~\{f_{1}(IL)+f_{2}(LK)-f_{1}(L):~J\subseteq L\subseteq X\}.

Using the fact that
f_{1}\mathord{\upharpoonright}X
and
f_{2}\mathord{\upharpoonright}X
are isomorphic
and modular, a simple calculation shows that

is a polymatroid,
and satisfies the requirements. For details, consult

[
10
,
12
]
or
[
26
]
.
∎

If either
Y
or
Z
has a single element, then one does not need to
look beyond
n,1
-copies.

Claim 8
.

Suppose
|Z|=1
. Entropy inequalities generated by

-copies of
polymatroids on

are also generated by
n,1
-copies.

Proof.

We claim that the cone generated by the tight part of
n,m
-copies
is the same as the cone generated by the

-copies. To prove this,
let
f
be a polymatroid on
Y\mkern-2.0muXZ
that satisfies the conditions of
Claim
4
, and let

be an
n,1
-copy of

f
so that

is identified with
f^{*}\mathord{\upharpoonright}Y_{1}XZ_{1}
where

has
a single element

. Let
g^{*}
be the polymatroid when
m\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mu1

identical copies of

are added to
f^{*}
. We claim that
g^{*}
is
an
n,m
-copy. The only non-trivially satisfied condition is that
the copies of

,

and

, are independent over

. Since

∎

4.4.
Problem Parameters

By Claim
6
, the Maximum Entropy Method does not yield
new inequalities when
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}X
is modular. This is certainly the
case when
|X|=1
, so we must have
|X|\geqslant 2
. By Claim

8
, if
|Y|=|Z|=1
, then beyond the
1,1
-copy, no
additional inequalities are generated. The smallest parameter setting
when new entropy inequalities are be expected as the number of
copies grows is
|Y|=2
,
|X|=2
and
|Z|=1
. We fix these sizes,
as well as the labels of the members of each set as

X=\{a,b\},~~Y=\{c,d\},~\mbox{ and }Z=\{z\}.

Since
|Z|=1
, according to Claim
8
,
it suffices to consider
n,1
-copies only. To simplify the notation,
the extra

will be dropped and we write
n
-copy instead. We also
explicitly state the definition of the

-copy for this particular

Definition 2
.

Let
f
be a polymatroid on

, and let

. The

on the base set
N^{*}=abz\cup\{c_{i}d_{i}:1\leqslant i\leqslant n\}

is an

-copy of

, if

(i)

is isomorphic to
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}abz
,
and, for each

, with the

,

f^{*}\mathord{\upharpoonright}abc_{i}d_{i}
is
isomorphic to
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}abcd
;

(ii)

\{c_{i}d_{i}:i\leqslant n\}
and

are completely conditionally
independent over
ab
.

This special case of the Maximum Entropy Method provides new non-Shannon
entropy inequalities based on the fact that entropic polymatroids on
the
5
-element base set
abcdz
have an
n
-copy for each
n\geqslant 1
.
The steps we will follow are as below:

(1)

Fix the number of copies
n
, called a

. Determine the
generating matrix

M
of the cone
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
as specified in Claim

2
using only polymatroids
that satisfy the conditions of Claim
4
.

(2)

The new inequalities are provided by the non-Shannon facets of the

\mathcal{Q}^{*}
; these facets can be computed using
some polyhedral algorithm from the generating matrix

.

5.
Computation

The cone
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
whose non-Shannon bounding facets provide the new
entropy inequalities sits in the
d_{1}=19
-dimensional Euclidean space with
coordinates indexed by the non-empty subsets of
Y\mkern-2.0muX=\{abcd\}
and
XZ=\{abz\}
.
Fix the number of copies to
n\geqslant 1
. This choice also fixes the
dimension
d_{3}
of the vector
\mathbf{y}
. The generating matrix

M
of the polyhedral cone
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
from (
2
) is repeated
here:

\mathcal{Q}^{*}=\big\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{19}:M\mkern 1.5mu{\cdot}\mkern 1.5mu(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\top}\geqslant 0\mbox{ for some }\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{3}}\big\}.

(4)

The modular part of
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
is
5
-dimensional, and so its
tight part sits in a
14
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{19}
.

A structural property of the polymatroid region on the four-element set

abcd
allows us to further reduce the complexity of the polyhedral
computation required in step (2) above. This region has a central part
and six permutationally equivalent “protrusions,” depending on
the signs of the Ingleton expressions

f[abcd\mkern 1.5mu],~f[acbd\mkern 1.5mu],~f[adbc],~f[bcad\mkern 1.5mu],~f[bdac],\mbox{ and }f[cdab\mkern 1.5mu].

If all of them are non-negative, then the restriction
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}abcd

is a linear polymatroid; otherwise exactly one of these Ingleton
expressions is negative, see e.g.
[

. Accordingly,
the cone
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
is cut into seven parts by these Ingleton

and six other parts where exactly one of the expressions is negative. The facets
of each part can be computed separately.

Parts of
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
on the negative
side of
[acbd\mkern 1.5mu]
,
[adbc]
,
[bcad\mkern 1.5mu]
, and
[bdac]
are isomorphic
because swapping

and/or

are symmetries of
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
.
Therefore, it suffices to
consider only one of them. The central part, where every Ingleton
expression is non-negative, does not yield new inequalities. This
follows from Lemma

below, as the elements of the
central part are linear.

Lemma 9
.

If
f
restricted to
abcd
is linear, then
f
has an
n
-copy for

.

Proof.

Since every polymatroid on

representable polymatroids on three or four elements are representable

that both

and

are
\mathbb{F}

\mathbb{F}
.
Denote the two representing vector spaces by

and

, and consider the subspace arrangements
(V^{1}_{a},V^{1}_{b})
and

(V^{2}_{a},V^{2}_{b})
in the two vector spaces. Now
V^{i}_{a}
and
V^{i}_{b}

have dimensions
f(a)
and
f(b)
,
respectively, and their linear span has dimension
f(ab)
. Therefore, these

and

can be glued along the

(V^{1}_{a},V^{1}_{b})

(V^{2}_{a},V^{2}_{b})

and on

∎

Consequently, up to the

,

f[acbd\mkern 1.5mu]<0
, and
f[cdab\mkern 1.5mu]<0

\mathcal{Q}^{*}
,
the Ingleton value can be set to
-1
, in effect taking
a cross-section of
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
that has one fewer dimensions.
Facets of the part of

9.
Conclusions

Structural properties of the entropy region of four or more variables
are mostly unknown. This region is bounded by linear inequalities
corresponding to the non-negativity of Shannon information measures.
Finding additional entropy
inequalities is, and remains, an intriguing open problem. Previous works on
generating and applying such non-Shannon entropy inequalities focused mainly
on the four-variable case
[
4
,
8
,
15
,
34
]
,
and only a few sporadic five-variable non-Shannon
inequalities have been discovered
[
24
]
. This work
provides infinitely many five-variable non-Shannon information inequalities
by systematically exploring a special property of entropic vectors.
Other works utilized the
Copy Lemma
, a method
distilled from the original Zhang-Yeung construction by Dougherty et
al.
[
15
]
. Our method is based on a different paradigm derived
from the principle of maximum entropy, and is a special case of the
Maximum Entropy Method described in
[
12
]
. As proven in Lemma

1
, the principle of maximum entropy implies that
every entropic polymatroid has an
n,m
-copy, which is a polymatroidal
extension with special properties
as defined in Definition

1
. In Claim
2
, we have proved that polymatroids
having
n,m
-copies form a polyhedral cone, and hint at how its facets
can be computed. Facet equations provide the potentially new
non-Shannon entropy inequalities.

While the polyhedral computation presented in Claim
2
is
numerically intractable even for small parameter values, the theoretical
results of Section
4
allowed us to reduce this complexity
significantly.
Computational aspects of determining the facets of
a high-dimensional cone are closely related to linear multi-objective
optimization
[
9
]
. We have developed a specially tailored
variant of Benson’s
inner approximation algorithm
[
9
,
22
]
, which takes advantage
of the special properties of this enumeration problem. Computational results
are reported in Section
5
for generations
n\leqslant 9
.
Numerical instability, originating from both the underlying LP solver and
the polyhedral algorithm, prevented the completion of the computation
for larger values of
n
.

Non-Shannon inequalities obtained from these computations are discussed
in Section
6
. Based on these experimental results, two infinite
families of
5
-variable inequalities were defined. The first
family in Theorem
11
is parametrized by downward closed
subsets of non-negative lattice points. The second family in Theorem

16
has a single positive integer parameter. Inequalities in
both families are
proved
to hold for polymatroids on
5
elements that
have an
n
-copy; consequently, they are all valid entropy inequalities. It is

conjectured
that they cover all inequalities that can be obtained by
the applied method. In other words, if a polymatroid on
5
elements satisfies
all these inequalities, then it has an
n
-copy for all
n
. This
conjecture is left as an open problem. The computational
results confirmed this conjecture up to
n=9
.

Inequalities in the first family are investigated in Section

8
in more detail. These inequalities
have the form

(a,b\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muz)+\alpha_{S}\big([abcd\mkern 1.5mu]+\mathcal{Z}\big)+\beta_{S}\mathcal{C}+\gamma_{S}\mathcal{D}\geqslant 0,

(28)

where the vector
\mathbf{v}_{S}=\langle\alpha_{S},\beta_{S},\gamma_{S}\rangle
is
computed from downward closed sets
S
of nonnegative lattice points as specified in Definition
4
.
Such a vector
\mathbf{v}_{S}

is
extremal
if the inequality (
28
) is not a consequence of
other inequalities from the same family. Corollary
20
provides the
following necessary condition for
\mathbf{v}_{S}

S

should be either
a horizontal or a vertical staircase. Based on additional properties of
extremal vectors, proved in Claim

, an incremental
algorithm, sketched as Code

.
Extremal triplets are defined by irreducible staircases.

To illustrate how the newly discovered entropy inequalities delimit the

5
-variable entropy region, entropy vectors were normalized to satisfy

(a,b\mkern 1.0mu{|}\mkern 1.0muz)=1
and projected onto a
3

The Shannon inequalities do not provide any restriction on this part. Figure

While the non-negative quadrant of the
