Composable neural emulators accelerate thermoelectric generator design
Authors: Airan Li, Xinzhi Wu, Longquan Wang, Gang Wu, Jiankang Li, Zhao Hu, Xinyuan Wang, Takao Mori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Designing high-performance thermoelectric (TE) devices is challenging because it requires not only advanced materials but also optimal configurations, which are critical for maximizing device performance but remain time-consuming and resource-intensive to identify1–5. Here we develop TEGNet, a neural network emulator that predicts TE generator performance with greater than 99% accuracy while using only 0.01% of the computational time required by commercial finite-element solvers. TEGNet exhibits strong architectural generality across various material systems and allows flexible combinations of material-specific emulators, unlocking rapid and accurate exploration of diverse device architectures. Using TEGNet, we experimentally optimize MgAgSb/Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3 segmented and Mg3Bi1.4Sb0.6–MgAgSb n–p paired TE generators, achieving conversion efficiencies of 9.3% and 8.7%, respectively, ranking competitively high among those previously reported6–10. This work demonstrates the power of artificial intelligence (AI) in TE generator design, inspiring further research on AI for thermoelectrics.

Main

TE technology enables direct solid-state conversion of heat into electricity and offers a promising solution for waste heat recovery across a wide temperature range and diverse application scenarios11–15. A typical TE generator consists of several TE legs electrically connected in series and thermally in parallel. Each leg comprises a TE material sandwiched between interface materials9,16,17. The performance of TE materials is characterized by the dimensionless figure of merit, zT = S2σT/κ, in which S, σ and κ represent the Seebeck coefficient, electrical conductivity and thermal conductivity of the material, respectively, and T is the absolute temperature. Intensive efforts over recent decades have led to a wide range of high-performance TE materials, with reported zT values exceeding 1 and even approaching 3 (refs. 18–24).

However, achieving high-performance TE generators requires more than the selection of superior materials. It necessitates a comprehensive approach that simultaneously addresses material compatibility, geometric tuning and architectural optimization2–5. The device efficiency is influenced by the TE compatibility factor, defined as s = ((1 + zT)1/2 − 1)/(ST), in which matching the operating relative current density to the local s is important to minimize irreversible energy losses and maximize the zT of the materials1. Device geometry, including the leg length H and cross-sectional area A, also plays a critical role, as it directly governs the coupled transport of electrical current density and the heat flux: electrical resistance R scales with H/A, whereas the thermal conductance K scales with A/H.

In practice, semi-analytical approaches based on a compatibility-factor-matching strategy are often combined with finite-element simulations, enabling consideration of temperature-dependent σ(T), S(T), κ(T) and various parasitic losses for full-parameter optimization and minimized energy-loss integration3. Nevertheless, substantial optimization space remains: maximizing both conversion efficiency and power density requires carefully balancing the power factor PF = S2σ, thermal transport and geometric parameters, a task that cannot be achieved by maximizing zT alone25. Therefore, delicate TE generator design is critical to fully exploit material potential. However, this process remains complex and challenging, as it requires several optimization strategies and involves time-consuming and resource-intensive computation and experimentation.

Traditionally, finite-element simulations are reliable approaches, enabling designers to virtually combine different TE materials and perform systematic scans of leg dimensions under various boundary conditions to determine the optimal configuration before fabrication26–29. However, simulating TE generator performance requires repeatedly setting up models and solving coupled thermo-electrical partial differential equations (PDEs), which becomes time-consuming and resource-intensive when material choices, geometries, boundary conditions and device architectures change frequently.

Recent advances in AI and machine learning have enabled data-driven acceleration of complex design problems. Using neural networks, one of the most powerful and widely used approaches in AI and machine learning, as surrogates for solving PDEs is particularly promising. Once trained, they enable ultrafast forward predictions with orders-of-magnitude acceleration. In principle, neural networks can be applied to TE generator design by taking device geometry as input and predicting device performance as output, thereby enabling rapid and accurate optimization of TE devices30,31. However, given the vast diversity of high-performance TE materials, varying transport properties and different types of device architecture, how to integrate neural networks into TE generator design to efficiently navigate the broad design space remains challenging, despite its considerable potential.

In this work, we develop a composable neural network emulator, called TEGNet, as a fast and accurate surrogate for finite-element simulations in TE generator design. TEGNet enables efficient exploration of diverse device architectures, including segmented legs and n–p pairs, by flexibly combining material-specific emulators. Guided by TEGNet, we further demonstrate experimental optimization of representative TE generators, validating its effectiveness for device-level design.

TEGNet architecture and model training

Conventional TE generator design relies on repeated finite-element simulations (for example, COMSOL, ANSYS). As shown in Fig. 1a, dimensions (Dims) and boundary conditions (BCs) are input into solvers to compute coupled thermo-electrical PDEs, yielding spatial voltage V(r) and temperature T(r), from which the output voltage V0 and cold-side heat flow Q0 can be extracted. However, solving these PDEs is highly time-consuming when Dims and BCs vary, a common requirement in TE generator design. Therefore, developing a surrogate model to replace the PDE-solving process can accelerate simulations and, consequently, device design. Neural networks, with strong nonlinear mapping capability, are especially suitable for thermo-electrical coupling. Notably, once trained, these models can produce outputs ultrafast. Physics-informed neural networks have been used to solve PDEs and extract field-dependent properties, but they require carefully designed loss functions for stability and accuracy32. By contrast, TE generator design is simpler, as the focus is mainly on overall output performance rather than detailed field distributions, which allows simpler models and efficient training. On the basis of this consideration, we propose TEGNet, a streamlined neural network (Fig. 1b) with Dims and BCs as input features and two outputs (V0 and Q0). To train TEGNet, we select MgAgSb, a promising TE material to replace Bi2Te3 for near-room-temperature power generation, as a case study. Training data are generated by COMSOL simulations, which are computationally expensive initially but greatly reduce cost once TEGNet is established. Because sample size strongly influences accuracy and generalization, we examine its effect to determine an optimal balance between performance and cost. Data were split into training (80%), validation (10%) and test sets (10%) first to validate the parameters of TEGNet. As shown in Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1, both training and validation losses decrease and saturate with increasing epochs in all sample sizes, confirming suitable model parameters. Model performance on the test set (Fig. 1d,e) shows consistently low root-mean-square error and mean absolute error for V0 and Q0, along with a decreasing trend in these errors as sample size increases, indicating improved generalization. This trend is expected because more data help the model learn the underlying patterns more reliably. Considering both data-generation cost and model accuracy, a sample size of 1,200 is determined to be optimal. With this sample size, the coefficient of determination R2 reaches 0.9997 and 0.9998 for V0 and Q0 predictions, respectively, on the unseen test set (Fig. 1f,g), demonstrating both the sufficiency of this sample size compared with others (Supplementary Fig. 2) and the robustness of the TEGNet architecture.

Accurate and rapid simulations using TEGNet

With the validated TEGNet, we next evaluate its capability in TE generator simulations. Using the MgAgSb-data-trained TEGNet, we compare predictions with COMSOL by varying Th and I, following a typical TE generator measurement procedure. As shown in Fig. 2a, the I-dependent V0 and Q0 predicted by TEGNet closely reproduce the expected TE generator behaviour and perfectly match COMSOL results. Scatter plots (Fig. 2b) further show the excellent agreement, with R2 = 0.9999 for V0 and R2 = 0.9997 for Q0. From the TEGNet predictions, output power P and conversion efficiency η can be derived (Fig. 2c). Similarly, TEGNet predictions show excellent agreement with COMSOL, achieving R2 = 0.9997 for P and R2 = 0.9993 for η (Fig. 2d). Notably, TEGNet focuses on intrinsic outputs (V0, Q0), from which P and η are derived using P = I × V0 and η = P/(P + Q0). The maximum power Pmax and maximum conversion efficiency ηmax can then be extracted from I–P and I–η curves under various Th (Supplementary Fig. 3). This intrinsic prediction mechanism not only improves interpretability but also lays the foundation for the composable capability of TEGNet, enabling application across diverse device architectures, a feature we demonstrate in the following sections. Although TEGNet is trained within a fixed temperature range, it also exhibits reasonable extrapolation beyond this range, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4. Moreover, TEGNet exhibits a unique ability to rapidly predict performance under BCs beyond Th and Tc. For example, TE generators often operate under constant heat flux. With TEGNet, we can rapidly search for combinations of Th, Tc and I that satisfy a constant heat-flux requirement without solving any PDEs. To further test the generality of TEGNet, we trained TEGNet on a broad set of TE materials beyond MgAgSb, including Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3, Mg3Bi1.4Sb0.6, Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5, NbFeSb, GeTe, PbTe, CoSb3, AgCu(Te,S), Zintl phases, SnSe and SnS (refs. 33–42), which cover broad TE performance regimes and operating temperature ranges (Supplementary Fig. 5). As shown in Supplementary Figs. 6–16, the I-dependent V0, Q0, P and η obtained from TEGNet align well with COMSOL and extracted Pmax and ηmax (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18) also show strong agreement. Figure 2e,f summarizes the scatter plots of extracted Pmax and ηmax, which yield R2 of 0.9999 and 0.9996, respectively. These results demonstrate the excellent architectural generality of TEGNet across a wide range of TE materials. Notably, as introduced earlier, the key advantage of TEGNet lies in its speed. As shown in Fig. 2g, COMSOL requires about 2,237.42 s per material’s simulation, whereas TEGNet predicts the same outputs in only 0.25 s (just 0.01% of the time). Although initial data generation (about 4 h for 1,200 samples) and training (around 79 s) are required, these are one-time costs and are modest compared with the overall development cycle of TE materials and devices. Importantly, once trained, TEGNet enables ultrafast predictions and offers high reusability and flexibility for repeated and diverse TE generator designs, as demonstrated in the following sections. Notably, for materials with TE properties similar to those above, TEGNet can also provide a reasonable rough estimate, as illustrated by the MgAgSb example, whereas for entirely new materials or cases requiring precise predictions, TEGNet models can also be readily constructed by following the same workflow presented in this study, as demonstrated above (Supplementary Fig. 19).

TEGNet application for segmented TE generator design

Unlike previous models30,31, the key advantage of TEGNet lies in its composability, enabled by accurate predictions of intrinsic V0 and Q0 under varied Dims and BCs. The operating current I in the BCs serves as the physical foundation for this composability. A TE generator is an electronic system in which the same I flows through all segments during operation. This constant-I rule allows individual unit-level TEGNet models to be directly combined to construct system-level simulations. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, when two materials are connected in series to form segmented generators, the overall voltage equals V0_1 + V0_2, whereas the overall heat flow equals Q0_2 at the cold side. The interface temperature Ti between segments, initially unknown, can be solved by the energy conservation rule (Q0_1 = Q0_2 + I × V). To better demonstrate the capability of TEGNet in assisting segmented TE generator design, we directly simulated Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3/MgAgSb segmented generators. Both materials exhibit high performance near room temperature but distinct peak zT and PF at different temperatures (Fig. 3b,c), which makes the optimal design non-intuitive without simulation. With TEGNet, we efficiently scanned different length ratios and extracted ηmax and Pmax (Fig. 3d,e). Results from the composed TEGNet closely match COMSOL (R2 = 0.9990; Fig. 3f). The detailed length-dependent Pmax and ηmax obtained from COMSOL are provided in Supplementary Fig. 20. Notably, despite the extra Ti-solving step, computation remains fast in the composed TEGNet, requiring only 74 s to identify the optimal configuration versus a much longer time in COMSOL (Fig. 3g). For experimental validation, we fabricated Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3/MgAgSb segmented generators, selecting a 0.5 length ratio for MgAgSb owing to its superior simulated output (Fig. 3d,e). Sb was used as the interface layer43 and the measurement set-up for contact resistivity and power output is shown in Supplementary Fig. 21. The measured contact resistivities are 4.0 μΩ cm2 for Sb/MgAgSb and 4.9 μΩ cm2 for Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3/In–Ga/Sb (Fig. 3h), both sufficiently low for practical application. As shown in Fig. 3i, the segmented generator exhibits higher power density than single legs, consistent with simulations. Moreover, it achieves conversion efficiencies of 9.3% at 593 K, ranking among the best reported6,7 (Fig. 3j).

Moreover, beyond assisting segmented TE generator design, the composability of TEGNet also enables the inclusion of electrical contact resistivity ρc for practical device considerations. As shown in Extended Data Fig. 2, predictions from TEGNet incorporating ρc remain in excellent agreement with COMSOL results. As well as ρc, TEGNet can also account for thermal contact resistivity rc (Extended Data Fig. 3). Notably, when incorporating these parasitic effects, whether ρc or rc, there is no need to retrain TEGNet or solve PDEs, while still achieving COMSOL-level accuracy, further enhancing its capabilities and underscoring its strong potential for real-world applications.

TEGNet application for n–p paired TE generators

Beyond segmented generators, TEGNet can also be composed to design n–p paired TE generators. As shown in Fig. 4a, the overall V0 and Q0 can be obtained by summing the contributions from individual TEGNet models, as the TE legs are connected electrically in series and thermally in parallel. By varying the dimensions of n-type and p-type legs and adjusting boundary conditions, we can efficiently explore optimal configurations that balance internal resistance and heat flow to improve η or P. To demonstrate this, we paired Mg3Bi1.4Sb0.6 (n-type) with MgAgSb (p-type). It should be noted that both TEGNet models had been trained previously, further highlighting their reusability across different design tasks. Moreover, scaling an n–p pair to several pairs requires no further computational cost, as the device-level performance can be obtained simply by summing the corresponding TEGNet outputs (Supplementary Fig. 22). Using the composed framework, we rapidly computed the ηmax and Pmax as functions of the area ratio (Ap/An) and normalized height H/(Ap + An) under Tc = 293 K and Th = 573 K (Fig. 4b,c), in which Ap, An and H represent the cross-sectional area of the p-type leg, the n-type leg and their common height, respectively. Figure 4d,e presents these results as 2D contour maps, providing a more intuitive view of the design trends. Notably, TEGNet predictions show excellent agreement with COMSOL results for H/(Ap + An) = 0.28 or Ap/An = 1.28 (Fig. 4f); while COMSOL required more than 5,800 s to simulate sparse data points, TEGNet generated the full maps in only 9 s. The results from Fig. 4d,e indicate that higher ηmax can be achieved when Ap/An is around 1.3, whereas increasing H/(Ap + An) leads to a reduction in Pmax. Guided by these simulations, we fabricated a two-pair TE generator with p-type legs of 3.3 × 3.3 × 6.6 mm3 and n-type legs of 2.9 × 2.9 × 6.6 mm3 to potentially improve ηmax. The optical image of the TE generator and the measurement set-up are shown in Fig. 4g. Measurements (Fig. 4h,i) show that ηmax reaches 8.7% when Th = 593 K, which surpasses our previous values7,33,43 and ranks competitively high among those reported in the literature8–10. Notably, the Mg3Bi1.4Sb0.6–MgAgSb paired TE generator also outperforms other material combinations in the near-room-temperature range19,29,44,45 (Fig. 4j), highlighting its strong potential for harvesting low-grade heat. As noted above, the composability of TEGNet enables the integration of different material-specific emulators and supports diverse device architectures, including three-segment designs and segmented n–p paired generators (Supplementary Fig. 25). To further showcase the flexibility of TEGNet, we also simulated an n–p pair using Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5 and SnS (Fig. 4k), and a segmented n-type Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5/Mg3Bi1.4Sb0.6 combined with a p-type GeTe (Fig. 4l). Unlike conventional COMSOL modelling, which requires building new 3D models and solving PDEs, these simulations were completed within about 30 s by simply composing trained TEGNet models. Notably, on the basis of the results, the optimal configurations of TE generator often deviate from the conventional set-up with equal p-type and n-type cross-sectional areas, further highlighting the importance of careful design for maximizing generator performance. Moreover, the composable nature of TEGNet enables simultaneous optimization of both geometrical parameters and material choices. By evaluating all possible combinations of different n-type and p-type TEGNet models, their best performance can be rapidly assessed (Supplementary Fig. 26). With the incorporation of more material-specific TEGNet models, it will become feasible to screen a large number of n–p combinations.

Conclusion

This work demonstrates, both theoretically and experimentally, that neural-network-based emulators can serve as a practical and scalable alternative to conventional finite-element workflows for TE generator design. By enabling rapid exploration of diverse device architectures while maintaining high predictive fidelity, including the incorporation of parasitic effects, TEGNet provides a generalizable methodology that accelerates the translation from materials discovery to device implementation, highlighting the potential of AI-driven approaches in TE research. However, despite its strong capabilities, TEGNet is not intended to address all aspects of TE generator design or to uncover new physical rules at this stage. Instead, it focuses on rapid exploration of the broad device design space, rather than detailed field-level, nonlinear, transient or stress-related effects, which may be incorporated in future extensions. By combining physical principles with data-driven modelling, TEGNet addresses a critical and previously unmet methodological gap in AI-assisted TE device design. The demonstrated application of AI at the device level is expected to inspire deeper integration of AI into TE research, complementing material-level efforts and advancing innovation in AI for thermoelectrics and energy applications.

Methods

TEGNet architecture

The TEGNet architecture is a streamlined, fully connected, multilayer perceptron, consisting of one input layer, three hidden layers and one output layer. The input layer contains six neurons representing the dimensions of a TE leg (width a, depth b and length c) and the boundary conditions (hot-side temperature Th, cold-side temperature Tc and applied current I). The output layer has two neurons corresponding to the voltage V0 and heat flow Q0 at the cold side. Each hidden layer contains 128 neurons, with SiLU as the activation function. The loss function is defined as the mean squared error between the predicted values and the data generated from COMSOL Multiphysics (abbreviated as COMSOL in the main text and the following), allowing for backpropagation during training. All algorithms were implemented in Python (version 3.10) using the PyTorch module.

Data generation

The training data for TEGNet is generated using COMSOL owing to its high accuracy in solving coupled thermo-electrical PDEs. All simulations used a ‘normal’ mesh size and a steady-state solver. Each data point contains eight columns: the TE leg dimensions (a, b and c), boundary conditions (Th, Tc and I) and the corresponding V0 and Q0. The dimensions a, b and c range from 1 to 12 mm. Temperature ranges for Th and Tc were selected according to the intrinsic operating limits of each material to avoid unreal TE properties arising from material degradation or phase changes. An extra 20 K margin was included to ensure full coverage of the TE property data and allow reasonable extrapolation. For example, NbFeSb operates up to about 1,203 K, so Th and Tc were sampled in the range 283–1,223 K, with Th > Tc. For MgAgSb, which operates up to about 573 K, the corresponding range was 283–593 K. The Sobol sequence is used to sample a, b, c, Tc and Th, for which Th should exceed Tc. The maximum current I is dynamically determined on the basis of a, b, c, Tc, Th and the TE properties of the material to avoid non-convergent COMSOL cases and ensure operation within the power-generation regime. The current I is then sampled uniformly from 0 to the maximum value. These considerations effectively eliminate unphysical conditions and yield a high-quality dataset requiring minimal preprocessing. These combined input parameters (a, b, c, Tc, Th, I) are fed into COMSOL and V0 and Q0 are extracted from the solved spatial distributions of voltage and temperature. Because n-type and p-type materials produce voltages of opposite sign under identical temperature gradients, all COMSOL-generated V0 values were converted to positive. Sample sizes of 300, 600, 1,200, 1,800 and 2,400 were tested for MgAgSb and an optimal sample size of 1,200 was chosen on the basis of a balance between accuracy and computational cost for all TE materials.

TEGNet training

For TEGNet training, the input data are fed into the network and the weights and biases of each neuron are updated by means of backpropagation to minimize the loss function. The optimizer is Adam and a CosineAnnealingLR scheduler is used with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10−3. The total number of epochs is set to 6,000. The TEGNet parameters were initially evaluated using MgAgSb data, which were split into a training set (80%), a validation set (10%) and a test set (10%). The validation set serves as an indicator for overfitting or underfitting during training. The model performance was evaluated after confirming the suitability of the network parameters. The data were divided into a training set (80%) and a test set (20%) to fully use the available data. To reduce potential bias from data splitting, five independent random splits were performed for both parameter evaluation and model performance assessment and the results were averaged. The final model for each material was trained on the entire dataset to maximize data use. Through training, the temperature-dependent TE properties are effectively learned and embedded within the network weights for each material. All training was conducted on a personal computer with a 12-core CPU and 16 GB RAM.

Materials synthesis

The MgAgSb used in this work contains 0.625 wt% C18H36O2 and Mg3Bi1.4Sb0.6 has the composition Mg3.2In0.02Sb0.595Bi1.4Te0.005, whereas Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3 is used as is. For simplicity, MgAgSb + 0.625 wt% C18H36O2 and Mg3.2In0.02Sb0.595Bi1.4Te0.005 are abbreviated as MgAgSb and Mg3Bi1.4Sb0.6, respectively, throughout the main text and the following. MgAgSb and Mg3Bi1.4Sb0.6 were prepared by ball milling (SPEX 8000D) in an Ar atmosphere for 5 h, whereas Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3 was prepared by melting at 1,273 K in a quartz tube for 12 h, followed by ball milling of the resulting ingot for 1 h. The raw materials used are high-purity Mg turnings (99.95%), Ag powders (99.99%), Sb shots (99.999%), Bi shots (99.999%), Te shots (99.999%) and In powders (99.99%).

TE generator fabrication

For the MgAgSb/Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3 segmented TE generator, the MgAgSb part was fabricated with Sb powders as interface materials, whereas the Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3 part was fabricated without interface materials. Both components were spark plasma sintered using SPS-322LX (Dr. Sinter) at 573 and 693 K for 5 and 10 min under 60 MPa, respectively. The Sb/MgAgSb/Sb piece was cut parallel to the pressing direction, whereas Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3 was cut perpendicular to the pressing direction. Segmented TE generators with different dimensions were assembled by connecting Sb/MgAgSb/Sb and Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3 using liquid Ga–In to form Sb/MgAgSb/Sb/Ga–In/Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3, which is abbreviated as MgAgSb/Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3 in the main text for simplicity. The contact resistance of the Sb/MgAgSb/Sb/Ga–In/Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3 interface was measured using a resistance distribution instrument (S1331, Mottainai Energy). Notably, even without interface materials, Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3 in contact with Sb through liquid Ga–In exhibited negligible contact resistance. For the Mg3Bi1.4Sb0.6–MgAgSb n–p paired TE generator, the Mg3Bi1.4Sb0.6 part was fabricated with stainless-steel powders as interface materials and sintered at 973 K under 60 MPa for 10 min (SPS-1080 System, SPS Syntex Inc.). The MgAgSb part was prepared in the same manner as for the segmented TE generator. Two-pair Mg3Bi1.4Sb0.6–MgAgSb n–p paired TE generators were assembled on an AlN ceramic plate with copper electrodes.

TE generator measurement

The experimental current I-dependent voltage V0 and heat flow Q0 of both the MgAgSb/Bi0.4Sb1.6Te3 segmented TE generator and the Mg3Bi1.4Sb0.6–MgAgSb n–p paired TE generator were measured using a Mini-PEM system (ADVANCE RIKO). During measurements, the top side was heated to a set temperature to serve as the hot side, whereas the bottom side was maintained at 293 K as the cold side. The hot-side temperature was varied between 373 and 593 K to investigate generator performance under different temperature differences. I was varied to obtain the corresponding V0 and Q0. All measurements were conducted under vacuum conditions. The output power P and conversion efficiency η were calculated as P = I × V0 and η = P/(P + Q0), respectively. The maximum power Pmax and maximum conversion efficiency ηmax for each temperature difference were extracted from the corresponding I–P and I–η curves.
