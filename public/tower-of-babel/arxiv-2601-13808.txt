Composing 𝐩 \mathbf{p} -adic qubits: from representations of 𝐒𝐎 ​ ( 𝟑 ) 𝐩 \mathbf{SO(3)_{p}} to entanglement and universal quantum logic gates
Ilaria Svampa, Sonia L’Innocente, Stefano Mancini, Andreas Winter

Abstract
In the context of p p -adic quantum mechanics, we investigate composite systems of p p -adic qubits and p p -adically controlled quantum logic gates. We build on the notion of a single p p -adic qubit as a two-dimensional irreducible representation of the compact p p -adic special orthogonal group SO ​ ( 3 ) p \mathrm{SO}(3)_{p} . We show that the classification of these representations reduces to the finite case, as they all factorise through some finite quotient SO ​ ( 3 ) p mod p k \mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\bmod p^{k} . Then, we tackle the problem of p p -adic qubit composition and entanglement, fundamental for a p p -adic formulation of quantum information processing. We classify the representations of SO ​ ( 3 ) p mod p \mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\bmod p , and analyse tensor products of two p p -adic qubit representations lifted from SO ​ ( 3 ) p mod p \mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\bmod p . We solve the Clebsch-Gordan problem for such systems, revealing that the coupled bases decompose into singlet and doublet states. We further study entanglement arising from those stable subsystems. For p = 3 p=3 , we construct a set of gates from 4 4 -dimensional irreducible representations of SO ​ ( 3 ) p mod p \mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\bmod p that we prove to be universal for quantum computation.

If

is a compact group, then every irreducible representation of
G
is finite-dimensional, and every unitary representation of
G
is completely reducible into a direct sum of irreducible subrepresentations.

Let now

denote the set of (unitary) equivalence classes of irreps of
G
. In contrast to the abelian case, here

is not a group in general. The

of a unitary representation of
G
into direct sum of irreps is generally not unique; however, the decomposition into subspaces corresponding to distinct equivalence classes (so-called isotypic subspaces) in
\widehat{G}
is uniquely determined. Also, the

of an irreducible subrepresentation (up to equivalence) of a unitary representation is the same for all the possible decompositions of the latter.

A milestone for the theory of representations of a compact group

is the

(cf.
(
folland2016course
, Thm. 5.12)
,
(

(up to equivalences) forms a complete orthonormal basis for the space

of complex-valued square-integrable functions on

. Moreover, the Peter-Weyl Theorem states that the regular representation of

on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(G,\mu)
is equivalent to the direct sum of all the irreps in

, each occurring with multiplicity equal to its dimension.

Besides the decomposition of the regular representation, there is another well-suited approach to classify the irreps of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
, depending on its particular structure of inverse-limit group.

Our focus on unitary representations is motivated by quantum-theoretical considerations: the evolution of a vector state in a Hilbert space

Actually, in quantum theory, the field of interest is broadened to

Definition II.12

The study of real and complex representations of a standard Lie group often proceeds via the associated Lie algebra. In particular, the Lie algebra isomorphism
\mathfrak{so}(3)\simeq\mathfrak{su}(2)
implies an identical local structure, yet the global properties differ:
\mathrm{SU}(2)
is the universal covering group of

. While,

encompasses that of
\mathrm{SO}(3)

Because

(and then the group

and have dimension

.

On the other hand,

is connected but not simply connected. The

-th irrep of

is associated with an irrep of
\mathrm{SO}(3)
if and only if

is an integer. Instead, half-integer values of

correspond to projective irreps of
\mathrm{SO}(3)
. In quantum mechanics, those representations are associated with integer spin (bosons) or orbital angular momentum when

is integer, and with half-integer spin (fermions) when

is half-integer. In particular, a spin one-half particle, or abstractly a qubit, is realised by

, for which the action of
\mathrm{SU}(2)
on the state space

corresponds to a projective action of

on the Bloch sphere

;

Accordingly, we give the following definition of

III.1

A fundamental tool to understand the structural aspects of

A crucial question is whether the (projective) irreps of

We therefore ask if a similar factorisation property holds for the non-abelian profinite group

\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}

Proposition III.1
.

Let
G
be a profinite group and
\rho\colon G\rightarrow\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})

is continuous if and only if

is open in

Proof.

For the direct part, it is enough to prove that

gU_{e}\subseteq\ker(\rho)

\ker(\rho)
is a (normal) subgroup, so
\bigcup_{g\in\ker(\rho)}gU_{e}\subseteq\ker(\rho)

and
\bigcup_{g\in\ker(\rho)}gU_{e}

We invoke the fact that

. Let

V
Entanglement for two
p
-adic qubits

Here we aim at showing how entangled states appear from the Clebsch-Gordan problem of two
p
-adic qubits, compared to standard quantum mechanics. Indeed, entanglement is precious resource in quantum information and computation in several scenarios
oroent
. We investigate the structural nature of the basis vector states that realise a direct-sum decomposition of a tensor product of two
p
-adic qubits, in the various possible cases:
2\otimes 2\simeq 1\oplus 1\oplus 1\oplus 1
,
2\otimes 2\simeq 1\oplus 1\oplus 2
,
2\otimes 2\simeq 1\oplus 3
or
2\otimes 2\simeq 2\oplus 2
. We then study the quantum states corresponding to each of these subsystem.

Note that the decomposition
2\otimes 2\simeq 1\oplus 3
(familiar from standard quantum mechanics) never appears in Section
IV
, as no three-dimensional irrep of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
factorises through
G_{p}
(cf. Subsection
III.2
). Contrarily to representation theory of
\mathrm{SO}(3)\simeq\mathrm{SU}(2)/\{\pm\mathrm{I}\}
for standard quantum mechanics, there are multiple
p
-adic qubit representations of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
, increasing in number as
p
grows, so that entangled states can arise more often than in the unique standard Clebsch-Gordan decomposition (
53
). However, besides the singlet quantum states, doublets and triplets are given by separable quantum states.

We recall the definition of entanglement in quantum mechanics, holding for both the standard and our
p
-adic frameworks, since the considered representations are over complex Hilbert spaces.

Definition V.1
.

A pure (vector) state of a bipartite quantum system
|\psi_{AB}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
is said to be an
entangled state
if it is not a tensor product, i.e., if there do not exist
|\psi_{A}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{A},\,|\psi_{B}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{B}
such that
|\psi_{AB}\rangle=|\psi_{A}\rangle\otimes|\psi_{B}\rangle
.

The
Schmidt rank
of a state is defined as the number of non-vanishing coefficients appearing in the Schmidt decomposition of a bipartite pure state, writing the latter as a superposition of biorthogonal product states weighted by non-negative real coefficients via singular value decomposition. If a state has Schmidt rank equal to
1
, it is separable; if the Schmidt rank is larger, the state is entangled. Then, Bell states (as in Eq. (
51
)) are classified as maximally entangled, because they achieve the maximum Schmidt rank
2
for a two-qubit system with uniform singular values.

More generally, a mixed quantum state
\rho_{AB}
in the space of density operators on
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
is called
separable
if it can be written as a convex combination
\rho_{AB}=\sum_{k}p_{k}\rho_{A}^{(k)}\otimes\rho_{B}^{(k)}
, where
\rho_{A}^{(k)}
and
\rho_{B}^{(k)}
are density operators on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
respectively (factorable quantum states are a subset of the separable ones);
\rho_{AB}
is called
entangled
if it is not separable. The
PPT criterion
(positive partial transpose, cf. e.g.
(
manwin
, Theorem
4.4.1
)
) tells that a necessary condition for the separability of a bipartite quantum state is the non-negativity of its partial transposition with respect to any of the two subsystems (it is also sufficient for two qubits). A quantum state on
\mathcal{H}
is
maximally mixed
if its rank equals
\dim(\mathcal{H})
. The reduced density operator of a maximally entangled state is the maximally mixed state. For other basic notions in quantum information theory, the reader may refer to
manwin
;
Wilde
.

Proposition V.2
.

Let
\mathdutchcal{U}_{A},\mathdutchcal{V}_{B}\colon\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\rightarrow\mathrm{U}(2)
be
p
-adic qubit irreps of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
, and consider
\mathdutchcal{U}_{A}\otimes\mathdutchcal{V}_{B}\colon\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\rightarrow\mathrm{U}(\mathbb{C}^{2}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}_{B})
. Every one-dimensional subrepresentation
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{A}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}_{B}^{2}
is spanned by a maximally entangled state.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}\equiv\mathbb{C}|v\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
be an
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
-stable subspace, and let
P_{\mathcal{M}}\equiv|v\rangle\!\langle v|\colon\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
be the rank-one orthogonal projector onto
\mathcal{M}
. Then,
P_{\mathcal{M}}
is an intertwining operator for
\mathdutchcal{U}_{A}\otimes\mathdutchcal{V}_{B}
, i.e.

P_{\mathcal{M}}=\big(\mathdutchcal{U}_{A}(L)\otimes\mathdutchcal{V}_{B}(L)\big)P_{\mathcal{M}}\big(\mathdutchcal{U}_{A}(L)^{\dagger}\otimes\mathdutchcal{V}_{B}(L)^{\dagger}\big),

(68)

for every
L\in\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
. We exploit the invariance of the partial trace
\Tr_{A}
under conjugation by local unitaries on the subspace
\mathbb{C}_{A}^{2}
(a symmetric discussion holds for
\Tr_{B}
):

\Tr_{A}(P_{\mathcal{M}})=\mathdutchcal{V}_{B}(L)\Tr_{A}(P_{\mathcal{M}})\mathdutchcal{V}_{B}(L)^{\dagger},

(69)

for every
L\in\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
. In other words,
\Tr_{A}(P_{\mathcal{M}})
is an intertwining operator for the local action
\mathdutchcal{V}_{B}
of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
on the subsystem
\mathbb{C}_{B}^{2}
. Since
\mathdutchcal{V}_{B}
is an irreducible representation (over the algebraically closed field
\mathbb{C}
), Schur’s lemma applies:
\Tr_{A}(P_{\mathcal{M}})=\lambda\mathbbm{1}_{B}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, where
\mathbbm{1}_{B}
denotes the identity operator on
\mathbb{C}_{B}^{2}
. We have
2\lambda=\Tr(\lambda\mathbbm{1}_{B})=\Tr\Tr_{A}(P_{\mathcal{M}})=\Tr(P_{\mathcal{M}})=1
, i.e.
\lambda=1/2
. It means that
\Tr_{A}(P_{\mathcal{M}})
is a maximally mixed quantum state, hence
|v\rangle
is a maximally entangled vector state.

\sqcap
\sqcup

Fact V.3
.

On
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
, any maximally entangled state is obtainable by local unitary transformations on a Bell state.

Proof.

A state in a bipartite system of finite dimension is maximally entangled if it has maximum Schmidt rank, i.e. if all its Schmidt coefficients are equal to the inverse of the minimum dimension of the subsystems. Two pure states can be transformed into each other by means of local unitaries if and only if they have the same Schmidt coefficients. Therefore, every pair of maximally entangled states are related by local unitaries. The four Bell states are maximally entangled two-qubit states in
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

\sqcap
\sqcup

Corollary V.4
.

Let
\mathdutchcal{U}_{A},\mathdutchcal{V}_{B}\colon\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\rightarrow\mathrm{U}(2)
be
p
-adic qubit irreps of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
, and consider
\mathdutchcal{U}_{A}\otimes\mathdutchcal{V}_{B}\colon\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\rightarrow\mathrm{U}(\mathbb{C}^{2}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}_{B})
. Up to local change of basis, the vector spanning a one-dimensional subrepresentation is
|\phi^{+}\rangle
. The projector on any one-dimensional subrepresentation is a maximally entangled (Bell) state.

Proof.

According to Proposition
V.2
, a one-dimensional subrepresentation of any
2\otimes 2
is spanned by a maximally entangled state
|v\rangle
. According to Fact
V.3
,
|v\rangle
can be transformed to any desired maximally entangled state, by a change of basis of
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
through local unitaries. Hence, without loss of generality, we assume
|v\rangle
to be the Bell state
|\phi^{+}\rangle
. Moreover,
|\phi^{+}\rangle\!\langle\phi^{+}|
is maximally entangled, as it has maximally mixed partial traces.

\sqcup

Lemma V.5
.

Let
2\otimes 2
admit a direct-sum decomposition into irreps which contains
1\oplus 1
. Up to a local change of basis, the vectors spanning the one-dimensional subrepresentations are
|\phi^{+}\rangle
and
|\phi^{-}\rangle
.

Proof.

By Proposition
V.2
and Fact
V.3
, assume that the state spanning one of the two one-dimensional irreps is
|\phi^{+}\rangle
. Call
|\phi\rangle
the maximally entangled state spanning the other one-dimensional irrep. The Bell state
|\phi^{+}\rangle
is rotationally invariant, i.e.,
(\mathdutchcal{U}\otimes\mathdutchcal{U}^{\ast})|\phi^{+}\rangle=|\phi^{+}\rangle
for every
\mathdutchcal{U}\in\mathrm{U}(2)
, where
\mathdutchcal{U}^{\ast}
denotes the complex conjugate of
\mathdutchcal{U}
. It follows that

(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathdutchcal{U}^{\ast})|\phi^{+}\rangle=(\mathdutchcal{U}^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbbm{1})|\phi^{+}\rangle,

(70)

for every
\mathdutchcal{U}\in\mathrm{U}(2)
. This allows us to move all local unitary operators on
|\phi^{+}\rangle
to one subsystem only, e.g. the first copy of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Thus, without loss of generality, we can assume
|\phi\rangle=(\mathdutchcal{V}\otimes\mathbbm{1})|\phi^{+}\rangle
, for some
\mathdutchcal{V}\in\mathrm{U}(2)
. Now, we wonder if there exists
\mathdutchcal{U}\in\mathrm{U}(2)
such that
(\mathdutchcal{U}\otimes\mathdutchcal{U}^{\ast})|\phi\rangle\sim|\phi^{-}\rangle
, where
\sim
denotes the ray equivalence relation in the projective space of
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
. By Eq. (
70
) we get

\displaystyle(\mathdutchcal{U}\otimes\mathdutchcal{U}^{\ast})|\phi\rangle=(\mathdutchcal{U}\mathdutchcal{V}\mathdutchcal{U}^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbbm{1})|\phi^{+}\rangle.

(71)

As
\mathdutchcal{V}
is unitary, it is unitarily diagonalisable, and we assume
\mathdutchcal{U}
to be such that
\mathdutchcal{U}\mathdutchcal{V}\mathdutchcal{U}^{\dagger}
is diagonal. Moreover, as the
p
-adic qubit representations are unitary, it must be

0=\langle\phi^{+}|\phi\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\mathdutchcal{V}.

(72)

This imposes the condition
\Tr(\mathdutchcal{U}\mathdutchcal{V}\mathdutchcal{U}^{\dagger})=0
. The set of traceless diagonal matrices in
\mathrm{U}(2)
is
\{e^{i\theta}Z,\ \theta\in\mathbb{R}\}
. In particular,
\mathdutchcal{U}\mathdutchcal{V}\mathdutchcal{U}^{\dagger}
is necessarily the
Z
Pauli matrix up to a global phase. This provides

(\mathdutchcal{U}\mathdutchcal{V}\mathdutchcal{U}^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbbm{1})|\phi^{+}\rangle=(e^{i\theta}Z\otimes\mathbbm{1})|\phi^{+}\rangle=e^{i\theta}|\phi^{-}\rangle.

(73)

To summarise, there exists
\mathdutchcal{U}\in\mathrm{U}(2)
such that
\mathdutchcal{U}\otimes\mathdutchcal{U}^{\ast}
transforms
|\phi\rangle
to
e^{i\theta}|\phi^{-}\rangle
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, and leaves
|\phi^{+}\rangle
invariant.

\sqcup

Proposition V.6
.

Case
2\otimes 2=1\oplus 1\oplus 1\oplus 1
. Up to a local change of basis, this decomposition is realised on a basis of four Bell states, and a projector on each singlet is maximally entangled.

Proof.

By Lemma
V.5
, we assume that two of the four one-dimensional subrepresentations are spanned by
|\phi^{+}\rangle
and
|\phi^{-}\rangle
. Let
|\psi\rangle
be spanning another one-dimensional subrepresentation. Again, this can be assumed of the form
|\psi\rangle=(\mathdutchcal{V}\otimes\mathbbm{1})|\phi^{+}\rangle
for some
\mathdutchcal{V}\in\mathrm{U}(2)
by Eq. (
70
). By unitarity of the
p
-adic qubit representations, the four subrepresentations must be orthogonal one another:
|\psi\rangle\in\Span(|\phi^{+}\rangle,|\phi^{-}\rangle)^{\perp}=\Span(|\psi^{+}\rangle,|\psi^{-}\rangle)=\Span(|01\rangle,|10\rangle)
, which means

|\psi\rangle=\lambda_{01}|01\rangle+\lambda_{10}|10\rangle,

(74)

for some
\lambda_{01},\lambda_{10}\in\mathbb{C}
. By Proposition
V.2
,
|\psi\rangle
is a maximally entangled state, hence it must be
|\lambda_{01}|^{2}=|\lambda_{10}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}
: we get
|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(e^{i\arg\lambda_{01}}|01\rangle+e^{i\arg\lambda_{10}}|10\rangle\right)
. Except from a global multiplicative constant, the state
|\psi\rangle
is uniquely determined up to a relative phase. We can remove this ambiguity by exploiting the common remaining symmetry of
|\phi^{+}\rangle
and
|\phi^{-}\rangle
. Indeed, let
\Theta\in\mathrm{U}(2)
be diagonal, namely
\Theta=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta}&0\\
0&e^{i\varphi}\end{pmatrix}
for some
\theta,\varphi\in\mathbb{R}
. Clearly
(\Theta\otimes\Theta^{\ast})|\phi^{\pm}\rangle=|\phi^{\pm}\rangle
, moreover

(\Theta\otimes\Theta^{\ast})|\psi\rangle=\frac{e^{i(\arg\lambda_{01}+\theta-\varphi)}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|01\rangle+e^{i(\arg\lambda_{10}-\arg\lambda_{01}+2\varphi-2\theta)}|10\rangle\right).

(75)

If
\varphi-\theta=\frac{1}{2}(\arg\lambda_{01}-\arg\lambda_{10}+2k\pi)
then
(\Theta\otimes\Theta^{\ast})|\psi\rangle\sim|\psi^{+}\rangle
; if
\varphi-\theta=\frac{1}{2}(\arg\lambda_{01}-\arg\lambda_{10}+(2k+1)\pi)
then
(\Theta\otimes\Theta^{\ast})|\psi\rangle\sim|\psi^{-}\rangle
. We have shown that three of the four one-dimensional subrepresentations are spanned by three Bell states. The forth stable line must be orthogonal to the others and again spanned by a maximally entangled state. As by Corollary
V.4
, the projector on each one-dimensional stable subspace is a Bell density operator, which is maximally entangled.

\sqcap
\sqcup

This is, for instance, what we already noticed for
p=3,7
in Subsection
IV.1
.

Proposition V.7
.

Case
2\otimes 2=1\oplus 1\oplus 2
. Up to a local change of basis, this decomposition is realised on a basis of four Bell states. Projectors on the singlets are maximally entangled, while a projector on the doublet is separable.

Proof.

By Lemma
V.5
, the two one-dimensional subrepresentations are spanned by
|\phi^{+}\rangle
and
|\phi^{-}\rangle
. These lines are orthogonal to the bidimensional stable subspace. Hence, a possible basis for the latter is given by the remaining Bell states,
|\psi^{+}\rangle
and
|\psi^{-}\rangle
. Again, the projectors onto the one-dimensional stable subspaces are maximally entangled by Corollary
V.4
. On the other hand, by the PPT criterion, a convex combination of Bell density operators is separable if and only if all probability coefficients are smaller than or equal to
\frac{1}{2}
. It follows that the projector
\frac{1}{2}\left(|\psi^{+}\rangle\!\langle\psi^{+}|+|\psi^{-}\rangle\!\langle\psi^{-}|\right)
onto the bidimensional stable subspace is separable.

\sqcap
\sqcup

We have specifically noticed this already for
p=2,5,7
in Subsection
IV.1
.

Proposition V.8
.

Case
2\otimes 2=1\oplus 3
. Up to a local change of basis, the one-dimensional stable subspace is spanned by the singlet
|\psi^{-}\rangle
, and the three-dimensional one by the triplet
(|00\rangle,|\psi^{+}\rangle,|11\rangle)
[or equivalently
(|\psi^{+}\rangle,|\phi^{+}\rangle,|\phi^{-}\rangle)

By Proposition
V.2
and Fact
V.3
, the one-dimensional subrepresentation is spanned by
|\psi^{-}\rangle

|00\rangle\!\langle 00|+|11\rangle\!\langle 11|+|\psi^{+}\rangle\!\langle\psi^{+}|

\sqcup

This is what happens in standard quantum mechanics (cf. Eq. (

\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\mod p

Proposition V.9
.

Let
\mathdutchcal{U}_{A},\mathdutchcal{V}_{B}\colon\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\rightarrow\mathrm{U}(2)
be
p
-adic qubit irreps of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
, and consider
\mathdutchcal{U}_{A}\otimes\mathdutchcal{V}_{B}\colon\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\rightarrow\mathrm{U}(\mathbb{C}^{2}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}_{B})
. For every two-dimensional subrepresentation
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{A}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}_{B}^{2}
, a projector
P_{\mathcal{M}}
onto
\mathcal{M}
is separable. Every two-dimensional subrepresentation
\mathcal{M}
is spanned by two orthogonal maximally entangled states. Up to local change of basis,
2\otimes 2\simeq 2\oplus 2
is realised on a basis of four Bell states.

Proof.

Let
(|\phi_{1}\rangle,|\phi_{2}\rangle)
be an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{M}
, which is an
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
-stable subspace. Let
P_{\mathcal{M}}=|\phi_{1}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{1}|+|\phi_{2}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{2}|\colon\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
be the rank-two orthogonal projector onto
\mathcal{M}
. In this context, Eqs. (
68
), (
69
) still hold, hence
\Tr_{A}(P_{\mathcal{M}})=\lambda\mathbbm{1}_{B}
by Schur’s lemma. Here
2\lambda=\Tr(\lambda\mathbbm{1}_{B})=\Tr(P_{\mathcal{M}})=2
implies
\lambda=1
: we have
\mathbbm{1}_{B}=\Tr_{A}(P_{\mathcal{M}})=\Tr_{A}(|\phi_{1}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{1}|)+\Tr_{A}(|\phi_{2}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{2}|)
. Let
\rho_{i}\coloneqq\Tr_{A}(|\phi_{i}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{i}|)
for
i=1,2
. Then
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
commute, and with respect to a common eigenbasis
\rho_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0\\
0&1-\lambda_{1}\end{pmatrix}
,
\rho_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\lambda_{1}&0\\
0&\lambda_{1}\end{pmatrix}
. By
Parthasarathy
,
CuMonAndreas
, the maximal dimension of a fully entangled subspace for two qubits is
1
, hence
\mathcal{M}
contains a product state. Let
|\phi_{1}\rangle=|\alpha_{1}\rangle_{A}\otimes|\beta_{1}\rangle_{B}
, for some
|\alpha_{1}\rangle_{A}\in\mathbb{C}_{A}^{2}
,
|\beta_{1}\rangle_{B}\in\mathbb{C}_{B}^{2}
. Equivalently,
\rho_{1}
is a rank-one projector, and so is
\rho_{2}
, i.e., also
|\phi_{2}\rangle
is a product state. Let
|\phi_{2}\rangle=|\alpha_{2}\rangle_{A}\otimes|\beta_{2}\rangle_{B}
, for some
|\alpha_{2}\rangle_{A}\in\mathbb{C}_{A}^{2}
,
|\beta_{2}\rangle_{B}\in\mathbb{C}_{B}^{2}
. Now
\mathbbm{1}_{B}=\Tr_{A}(|\phi_{1}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{1}|)+\Tr_{A}(|\phi_{2}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{2}|)=|\beta_{1}\rangle\!\langle\beta_{1}|+|\beta_{2}\rangle\!\langle\beta_{2}|
, hence it must be
\langle\beta_{1}|\beta_{2}\rangle=0
. Similarly
\Tr_{B}(P_{\mathcal{M}})=\mathbbm{1}_{A}
provides
\langle\alpha_{1}|\alpha_{2}\rangle=0
. It follows that
P_{\mathcal{M}}=|\alpha_{1}\rangle\!\langle\alpha_{1}|\otimes|\beta_{1}\rangle\!\langle\beta_{1}|+|\alpha_{2}\rangle\!\langle\alpha_{2}|\otimes|\beta_{2}\rangle\!\langle\beta_{2}|
is separable. Up to a local change of basis,
\mathcal{M}
is spanned by a basis of maximally mixed states, e.g.
(|\psi^{+}\rangle,|\psi^{-}\rangle)
. Then, a basis for the bidimensional orthogonal complement is
(|\phi^{+}\rangle,|\phi^{-}\rangle)
, with associated separable projector.

\sqcup

This has already been noted in Subsection

in particular for

In the realm of quantum computing, we propose to construct

\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}

(plus an ancillary qubit in the case of orthogonal transformations). As in the standard approach, the emphasis lies primarily on

2
-qubit gates from unitary representations of

acting on

. We seek for an entangling

-adically controlled gates. Once explicitly found some four-dimensional irrep of

-qubit gates (if not equal to a

In the present work, we studied representations of

VI.1
Universal quantum computation

We deal with the circuit model of quantum computation, where the building blocks of quantum circuits are quantum logic gates. Each gate corresponds to a unitary operator acting on a certain number
n
of qubits, i.e. to an operator in
\mathrm{U}(2^{n})
. For physical implementations, it is desirable that quantum gates act on a small number of qubits, typically three or preferably two. It is useful to have a small repertoire of simple gates, combining which all the circuits (i.e. all the unitary transformations in any number of qubits) can be reconstructed approximatively.

Definition VI.1

(
Brylinski
;
Aharonov
)
.

A set
\mathcal{S}
of multi-qubit gates is called
(approximatively) universal
if it satisfies one of the following equivalent conditions: there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
n\geq n_{0}
,

•

every
n
-qubit gate can be approximated with arbitrary accuracy by a circuit made up of the
n
-qubit gates produced by the elements in
\mathcal{S}
;

•

i.e., for every
U\in\mathrm{U}(2^{n})
and every
\epsilon>0
there exists
U^{\prime}\in\mathrm{U}(2^{n})
made up of elements in
\mathcal{S}
such that
\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\psi\rangle\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}\\
\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert=1\end{subarray}}\left\lVert(U-U^{\prime})|\psi\rangle\right\rVert<\epsilon;

•

the
n
-qubit gates produced by the elements in
\mathcal{S}
generate a subgroup dense in
\mathrm{U}(2^{n})
.

In all these formulations, it is assumed that any given
k
-qubit gate from
\mathcal{S}
can act on any subfamily of
k
out of the
n
qubits, i.e. each such gate gives rise to really
k!{n\choose k}

n
–qubit unitaries.

A set
\mathcal{S}
of qubit gates is called
exactly universal
if there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for each
n\geq n_{0}
, every
n
-qubit gate can be obtained exactly by a circuit made up of the
n
-qubit gates produced by the elements in
\mathcal{S}
, i.e., the
n
-qubit gates produced by the elements in
\mathcal{S}
generate the full group
\mathrm{U}(2^{n})
.

A finite set of gates cannot be exactly universal, but just universal. There are also weaker notions of universality. These include reproducing a
U\in\mathrm{U}(2^{n})
as
U\otimes\mathrm{I}
using ancilla qubits
Shi
, as well as encoded universality via orthogonal gates
BernVazi
(as we will detail in the paragraph “Real quantum computation” below).

A first foundational result by Deutsch is that the three-qubit gate
Q\coloneqq C^{2}U_{\alpha}
is universal
Deutsch
with
n_{0}=3
, where
U_{\alpha}\coloneqq ie^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha}X^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
(
Q
is a double-controlled unitary representing a continuous quantum generalization of the Toffoli gate
C^{2}X
). In particular,
Q
approximates the Toffoli gate, which is universal for classical computation, and hence realises the permutations of the basis elements. With this, Deutsch proved that
Q
approximates any unitary in
\mathrm{U}(2^{3})
, and then any unitary in
\mathrm{U}(2^{n})
with
n\geq 3
. Upon this, DiVincenzo showed that two-qubit gates are universal
DiVincenzo
with
n_{0}=2
, by showing that two-qubit gates approximate three-qubit gates by Lie algebra techniques. If two elements of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{u}(4)
do not belong to a Lie subalgebra and do not commute, their commutator provides a third generator of
\mathfrak{u}(4)
, and iterating this process one generates the whole
\mathfrak{u}(4)
; the elements in the connected group
\mathrm{U}(4)
corresponding to the generators of
\mathfrak{u}(4)
generate a dense subgroup in
\mathrm{U}(4)
. Then, it was proved that the set
\{\mathrm{U}(2),CX\}
is exactly universal
BarencoetAl
, and the set
\{\textup{Toffoli},\textup{Hadamard}\}
is universal
Shi
,
Aharonov
, just to cite a few.

Remark VI.2
.

Since a quantum state is a ray in a projective Hilbert space, where global phases are irrelevant, Definition
VI.1
can be given in terms of special unitary transformations only. Furthermore, Definition
VI.1
can be given in terms of special orthogonal transformations only, since complex quantum computation can be encoded in
real quantum computation
which relies exclusively on such operations
BernVazi
.

Real quantum computation
. We recall how to concretely transform any quantum circuit to a circuit (computing the same function) composed solely of special orthogonal matrices
BernVazi
,
Aharonov
. An extra qubit is added to the circuit, doubling the dimension of the initial Hilbert space. This is used as a flag to select between the real and imaginary parts of a state, so to get a purely real state. A conventional way to do it is as follows. The extra qubit’s state is put to
|0\rangle
(resp.
|1\rangle
) to indicate that the circuit’s state is in the real (resp. imaginary) part of the Hilbert space. Let
\{|j\rangle\}_{j}
denote the canonical basis of
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
, and consider
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}\ni|\psi\rangle=\sum_{j}c_{j}|j\rangle=\sum_{j}\left(\mathrm{Re}(c_{j})+i\mathrm{Im}(c_{j})\right)|j\rangle=\mathrm{Re}(|\psi\rangle)+i\mathrm{Im}(|\psi\rangle)
, with
c_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
for every
j
. This is encoded via the extra qubit by

\displaystyle(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}\ni|\psi\rangle\mapsto|\widetilde{\psi}\rangle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{Re}(|\psi\rangle)|0\rangle+\mathrm{Im}(|\psi\rangle)|1\rangle\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\otimes(n+1)}

(76)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\mathrm{Re}(c_{j})|j\rangle|0\rangle+\sum_{j}\mathrm{Im}(c_{j})|j\rangle|1\rangle,

which is a real-linear map; clearly
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\langle\widetilde{\psi}|\widetilde{\psi}\rangle
.
Now, in the initial circuit, each unitary gate
U
acting on
m
qubits is replaced by a real matrix
\widetilde{U}
acting on
m+1
qubits (the same
m
qubits and the extra one), defined as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}|j\rangle|0\rangle

\displaystyle\coloneqq\phantom{-}\big(\mathrm{Re}(U)|j\rangle\big)|0\rangle+\big(\mathrm{Im}(U)|j\rangle\big)|1\rangle,

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}|j\rangle|1\rangle

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\big(\mathrm{Im}(U)|j\rangle\big)|0\rangle+\big(\mathrm{Re}(U)|j\rangle\big)|1\rangle.

(77)

This encoding places the flag qubit at the end of the tensor product (contrarily to the algebraic convention). For
U\in\mathrm{U}(m)
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, this corresponds to transforming the matrix representation
U=\left(U_{ij}\right)_{ij}
, with respect to some basis, by
U_{ij}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Re}(U_{ij})&-\mathrm{Im}(U_{ij})\\
\mathrm{Im}(U_{ij})&\mathrm{Re}(U_{ij})\end{pmatrix}
for every
i,j
. This provides an embedding
\mathrm{U}(m)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{SO}(2m)
(which is surjective on
\mathrm{SO}(2m)\cap\mathrm{Sp}(2m,\mathbb{R})
).

From the previous section, we have found a promising set of

Let

\mathdutchcal{G}\coloneqq\langle X\otimes\mathrm{I}_{2},\mathrm{I}_{2}\otimes X,S\otimes\mathrm{I}_{2},\mathrm{I}_{2}\otimes S,\widetilde{CZ}\rangle\leq\mathrm{O}(4)

(99)

be the group generated by the gates in
\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathdutchcal{p}_{3}
in dimension
4
. First of all, we show that
\mathdutchcal{G}
is dense in
\mathrm{O}(4)
, or equivalently that its connected component at the identity,
\mathdutchcal{G}_{0}
, is dense in
\mathrm{SO}(4)
.

For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathrm{O}(m)\simeq\mathrm{SO}(m)\rtimes\{\pm 1\}
. Indeed
\det(\mathrm{O}(m))=\{\pm 1\}
and
\mathrm{O}(m)
has two connected components:
\mathrm{SO}(m)
and the set of orthogonal transformations with determinant equal to
-1
. The Lie algebra associated to
\mathrm{O}(m)
and
\mathrm{SO}(m)
is
\mathfrak{o}(m)=\mathfrak{so}(m)=\{A\in\mathsf{M}(m,\mathbb{R})\textup{ s.t. }A^{\top}=-A\}
of dimension
\frac{m(m-1)}{2}
.

We have that
\det(\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}})=\{\pm 1\}
, and
\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}}
also has two connected components:
\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}}_{0}=\ker(\det)\leq\mathrm{SO}(4)
, and
\det^{-1}(-1)=\widetilde{CZ}\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}}_{0}
.
By the closed-subgroup theorem,
\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}}
is a Lie subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(4)
,
\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}_{0}}\simeq\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}}_{0}
is a Lie subgroup of
\mathrm{SO}(4)
,
and the algebra
\mathrm{Lie}(\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}})=\mathrm{Lie}(\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}}_{0})\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(4)
. Since
\mathrm{SO}(4)
and
\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}}_{0}
are both connected, one has

\mathrm{Lie}(\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}}_{0})=\mathfrak{o}(4)\textup{ if and only if }\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}}_{0}=\mathrm{SO}(4).

(100)

Then, our goal is to prove
\mathrm{Lie}(\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}}_{0})=\mathfrak{o}(4)
by reconstructing
\mathrm{Lie}(\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}}_{0})
generator by generator.

But first, we recall some technical results. The eigenvalues of an element
O
in
\mathrm{O}(m)
are in
\mathrm{U}(1)
: if
O
has some non-real eigenvalues, these must be pairwise conjugated phases. An element
O\in\mathrm{O}(m)
is of infinite order if and only if it has an eigenvalue
e^{i\theta}\in\mathrm{U}(1)
with
\theta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\pi\mathbb{Q}
, i.e., with
\theta
incommensurate with
\pi
. In fact,
e^{i\theta}
is of finite order if and only if
\theta\in\pi\mathbb{Q}
, otherwise
\langle e^{i\theta}\rangle
is dense in
\mathrm{U}(1)
. Moreover,
(e^{i\theta_{k}})_{k=1}^{m}\subseteq\mathrm{U}(1)^{m}
generates
\{(e^{in\theta_{k}})_{k},\,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
which is dense in the
d
-dimensional torus
\mathrm{U}(1)^{d}
,
1\leq d\leq m
, if and only if there are
d

\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent
\theta_{k}\not\in\pi\mathbb{Q}
, if and only if
d=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}\{\pi,\theta_{k}\}_{k}-1\geq 1
(by the Kronecker-Weyl theorem).

If
R\in\mathdutchcal{G}_{0}
, the group
\overline{\langle R\rangle}
is a Lie subgroup of
\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}}_{0}\leq\mathrm{SO}(4)
, and
\mathrm{Lie}(\overline{\langle R\rangle})\subseteq\mathrm{Lie}(\overline{\mathdutchcal{G}}_{0})\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(4)
. There exists
\Lambda\in\mathrm{U}(4)
such that
R=\Lambda D\Lambda^{\dagger}
where
D\coloneqq\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\theta_{k}})_{k=1}^{4}
is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues of
R
of unitary determinant. If
d=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}\{\pi,\theta_{k}\}_{k}-1
, then
\overline{\langle R\rangle}=\Lambda\overline{\langle D\rangle}\Lambda^{\dagger}\simeq\overline{\langle D\rangle}\simeq\mathrm{U}(1)^{d}
, with associated
d
-dimensional Lie algebra
\mathrm{Lie}(\overline{\langle R\rangle})=\Lambda\mathrm{Lie}(\overline{\langle D\rangle})\Lambda^{\dagger}\simeq\mathrm{Lie}(\overline{\langle D\rangle})\subset\mathfrak{su}(4)
.
One finds a set of generators
\{A_{j}\in\mathfrak{su}(4)\}_{j=1}^{d}
of
\mathrm{Lie}(\overline{\langle D\rangle})

so that

Definition VI.3
.

For

,

\displaystyle G_{jk}(\theta)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&\cdots&0&\cdots&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\cos\theta&\cdots&-\sin\theta&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\sin\theta&\cdots&\cos\theta&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&\cdots&0&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}\phantom{1}\\
\phantom{\vdots}\\
j\\
\phantom{\vdots}\\
k\\
\phantom{\vdots}\\
\phantom{0}\end{matrix}

(103)

\displaystyle\hskip 52.63777ptj\hskip 46.94687ptk

i.e., a rotation of angle
\theta
in
\mathrm{SO}(2)
embedded in
\mathrm{SO}(m)
to act non-trivially only in the
(j,k)
coordinate plane in
\mathbb{R}^{m}

In what follows, an embedding of
\mathrm{SO}(n)

\mathrm{SO}(m)

Givens rotations are useful for computations, as they allow to annihilate all but one component of a vector (or of a column of a matrix). If
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i})_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{i})_{i}\coloneqq G_{jk}(\theta)\mathbf{x}
, then
y_{j}=\cos(\theta)x_{j}-\sin(\theta)x_{k}
,
y_{k}=\sin(\theta)x_{j}+\cos(\theta)x_{k}
, and
y_{l}=x_{l}
for all
l\neq j,k
. If not already
x_{k}=0
, then we can force
y_{k}=0
by setting

\cos\theta=\frac{x_{j}}{\sqrt{x_{j}^{2}+x_{k}^{2}}},\qquad\sin\theta=-\frac{x_{k}}{\sqrt{x_{j}^{2}+x_{k}^{2}}}.

(104)

Similarly, if
x_{j}\neq 0
, we can make

by setting
\cos\theta=\frac{x_{k}}{\sqrt{x_{j}^{2}+x_{k}^{2}}}
,
\sin\theta=\frac{x_{j}}{\sqrt{x_{j}^{2}+x_{k}^{2}}}
.

Remark VI.4
.

Givens rotations provide a
generalisation of Euler- or nautical-angle decomposition
of
\mathrm{SO}(3)
to any higher-dimensional
\mathrm{SO}(m)
. Indeed, we show how, for every
m\geq 2
, the group
\mathrm{SO}(m)
is generated by products of rotations in
\mathrm{SO}(2)
(or in
\mathrm{SO}(n)
with
2\leq n<m
) embedded in
\mathrm{SO}(m)
.

Given
O\in\mathrm{O}(m)
, we want to annihilate its first column except for one element, by using Givens rotations. Since the first column of the orthogonal matrix
O
is non-zero (e.g.
O_{1m}\neq 0
), we can use Eq. (
104
) repeatedly. There exist
\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

O^{\prime}\coloneqq G_{1m}(\theta_{m})\dots G_{12}(\theta_{2})O=\begin{pmatrix}O^{\prime}_{11}&\mathbf{0}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{0}&O^{\prime}_{m-1}\end{pmatrix}.

(105)

One has
O^{\prime}\in\mathrm{O}(m)
, thus
O^{\prime}_{11}\in\{\pm 1\}
and
O^{\prime}_{m-1}\in\mathrm{O}(m-1)
. We can write

in terms of special orthogonal matrices only: either already

, or we modify last Givens rotation as

. This changes the sign of

and of last column of

can be decomposed into the product of (at most)

Givens rotations and an orthogonal transformation
O^{\prime}
; the Givens rotations are elements of
\mathrm{SO}(2)
suitably embedded in
\mathrm{SO}(m)
, and
O^{\prime}
is an element of
\mathrm{SO}(m-1)
suitably embedded in
\mathrm{O}(m)
. Moreover
\det(O^{\prime})=O^{\prime}_{11}\det(O^{\prime}_{m-1})
in Eq. (
105
). Then, every
O\in\mathrm{SO}(m)
can be written in terms of lower-dimensional rotations as just described with
O^{\prime}\in\mathrm{SO}(m)
(that is
O^{\prime}_{m-1}\in\mathrm{SO}(m-1)
and
O^{\prime}_{11}=+1
in Eq. (

Every element of
\mathrm{SO}(3)
can be written as the product of three rotations around the reference axes of

VII
Conclusions

Advancing the study of
p
-adic quantum mechanical spin and angular momentum, we have here shown that low-dimensional irreducible representations of the three-dimensional
p
-adic rotation group
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
indeed give rise to meaningful elements of quantum information processing:
p
-adic qubits and universal sets of
p
-adically controlled quantum logic gates.

We established that the finite-dimensional representations of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
factorise through finite quotients
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\mod p^{k}
. By focusing on the level
k=1
, we classified the unitary irreducible representations arising from
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\bmod p
, identifying a
p
-adic qubit with such a representation of dimension two. A striking feature emerging from this classification, unlike the standard real case, is that
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
admits more than one
p
-adic qubit representation for primes
p>3
. This abundance is reflected in several possibilities of composing a multipartite system of
p
-adic qubits, through the tensor product of their representations. An open question is how many non-equivalent
p
-adic qubit representations there are for every prime
p
, and what they signify. The existence of non-equivalent one-dimensional representations of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
might suggest to reduce
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
to a suitable subgroup with unique trivial character (as it happens in the real case from
\mathrm{O}(3)
to
\mathrm{SO}(3)
), which could thereby provide a selection of physically meaningful
p
-adic qubit representations.

We addressed the Clebsch-Gordan problem for two
p
-adic qubits from
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\bmod p
, decomposing in singlet and doublet states, driven by the representation theory of the dihedral group
\mathrm{D}_{p+1}
. Although the tensor product of two two-dimensional representations can theoretically decompose into a singlet and a triplet, this structure has not yet been observed in a system of two
p
-adic qubits factorizing modulo
p
. Then, we identified entangled states realising those decompositions: every singlet, doublet (and triplet) can be given by maximally entangled Bell states. Note however that the latter two cases present separable states, which are equivalently spanned by product vectors.
We leave it open to analyse the irreducible representations of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\mod p^{k}
for
k>1
, and whether this eventually yields three-dimensional representations, potentially allowing the Clebsch-Gordan decomposition of two
p
-adic qubits to recover the singlet and triplet structure familiar from standard quantum mechanics. A natural step beyond is to extend this analysis to systems composed of more than two
p
-adic qubits.

Lastly, quantum logic gates form the foundation of the circuit model of quantum computation, by manipulating qubit states via unitary operators. Here, we have proposed to take elements from the
2^{n}
-dimensional unitary representations of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
as logic gates on
n
qubits, drawing inspiration from Kitaev’s topological anyon model with the actions of a braid group
KitaevBraid
. Our focal point is to identify some entangling logic gate on two qubits, that can be used to construct a universal set of gates. In this context, the prime
p=3
appeared privileged: at the level of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\mod p
,
p=3
is the only case exhibiting a unique
p
-adic qubit representation and, crucially,
4
-dimensional irreducible representations. We proved that the elements of such representation of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{3}
lead to a universal set of
3
-adically controlled quantum logic gates, ensuring the capacity of our model for arbitrary quantum computations, and paving the way of the research over
p
-adically driven quantum algorithms. Finally, we would like to understand if the singled out universal set of logic gates is of interest for practical application.

So far, the study of representations of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
and their application to quantum physics has been restricted to the level of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\mod p
. It remains to be understood if the prominence of
p=3
is just an artifact of this restriction. Moreover, the
4
-dimensional irreducible representations of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{3}\mod 3
are actually orthogonal, and an ancillary qubit needs to be added to encode unitary circuits through our universal set of orthogonal gates. We still need to see if genuinely complex unitary representations emerge at higher levels
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}\bmod p^{k}
, and if
4
-dimensional ones exist for other primes
p
, potentially offering universal sets of gates for all primes. To fully answer this, as well as to have a complete
p
-adic theory of angular momentum, spin and qubit, future work has to pursue a complete classification of unitary irreducible representations of
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
. This will be addressed via Kirillov’s orbit method and Howe’s generalised theory, before extending to projective representations with the help of the Haar measure on
\mathrm{SO}(3)_{p}
.

Looking farther afield, we plan to provide a definition of qubit, entanglement, logic gates, etc. in the adelic framework, so to encompasses both standard and
p
-adic quantum mechanics for all primes
p
simultaneously.
