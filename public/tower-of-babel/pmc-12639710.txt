ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is an excessive immune activation syndrome. The genetic studies on every patient diagnosed with HLH recently became a standard of care. The likelihood of identifying a gene mutation is highest in the youngest patients.ResultsFour HLH patients had changes in the following five genes: NLRP1 (c·923 G > A), DOCK 8 (Dedicator of Cytokinesis 8) (c·3067A > G), AIRE gene (c·10 G > A) and one in the RNASEH2B (c·649T > C), PSTPIP1 (c·1213C > T). Analysis of genes involved in immune response in children with HLH – case series

Genetic testing in each patient diagnosed with HLH is now a standard of care. In 2009, the criteria for the HLH diagnosis were updated, and genetic analysis was included [1]. The likelihood of identifying a gene mutation is highest in the youngest patients. Diagnosis of genetic defects can help determine the prognosis and choose optimal treatment.

We are presenting a series of four cases of pediatric HLH in which typical genetic background was not confirmed. All patients were hospitalized in the Pediatric Hematology-Oncology Department of Poznan University of Medical Sciences, diagnosed according to HLH 2004 protocol and received standard treatment (details in Table 1). In all patients genetic testing was performed.

Determination of the DNA changes pathogenicity was based on the protein structure prediction and comparable genetics. Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) using TruSight One Sequencing Panel (Illumina) was performed. Four thousand eight hundred thirteen genes selected on their previously proven clinical effects were tested. The individual protein domains were presented using the InterPro software [2], which can be found at: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro. The prediction of the proteins’ spatial structure was based on applying the AlphaFold software [3]. The analysis of changes in sequences of individual genes to assess their putative pathogenicity was carried out using two online tools: SIFT (Sorting Intolerant from Tolerant) [4] and PolyPhen-2 [5]. The clinical effect of the variant was accessed based on Mutation Taster (https://www.mutationtaster.org), and the following terms were used: benign, likely benign, uncertain significance, likely pathogenic, and pathogenic.

Patient 1

The first patient was a 16-month-old girl diagnosed with HLH. Five months after the end of the treatment, she experienced a recurrence of the disease without invasion of the central nervous system. She was treated with etoposide and dexamethasone. She was qualified for allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). Additionally, she received the anti-IL-1 agent Anakinra, but the treatment was discontinued after two doses due to the development of a severe rash. Twenty-two months after concluding the treatment, she was admitted to the hospital with elevated aminotransferases. However, synthetic liver function remained unimpaired, no cytopenia was observed, and the ferritin level was within the normal range. Despite the absence of a viral cause, after ten days, liver function returned to normal. Six months later, she remained healthy, requiring no further treatment.

The only abnormality detected in this patient was a change in the NLRP1 gene (c.923 G > A) (Table 2, Fig. 1). The missense change was confirmed by the Sanger sequencing. The whole family was tested, and the mother and older sister had the identical change. The change (17p13.2 single nucleotide variant) seen in our patient is designated as benign. The difference is not altering protein conformation. The frequency of this change is 1/1000 in the general population. Upon patient diagnosis, there was no history of severe diseases in the mother and sister. The NLRP1 gene is widely expressed in different cells of the body [6]. The highest expression is observed in primary immune cells such as neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, dendritic cells, and lymphocytes. All pathogenic NLRP1 mutations are gain-of-function alleles that predispose to inflammasome activation [7]. The systemic features of autoinflammatory diseases caused by NLRP respond to IL-1 blockade. Our patient did not benefit from Anakinra treatment. She is on a waiting list for allo-HSCT. The family donor was excluded because the mother and older sister had the exact change.

Patient 2

The 18-month-old boy was diagnosed with HLH and treatment in accordance with the protocol was initiated. Seventeen months later, the boy developed B-cell progenitor acute lymphoblastic leukemia (BCP-ALL).

A single nucleotide variant of uncertain significance was detected in the DOCK8 (Dedicator of Cytokinesis 8) gene in the region of 9p24.3, and the change in DNA was designated as c.3067A > G (Table 2, Fig. 1). Functionally, the change replaces isoleucine with valine in DOCK8 protein. The isoleucine residue is highly conserved. The potential impact of this missense change on protein structure and function is unknown. This variant is present in a general population with a frequency of 0.03%. However, it was not previously described as pathological in a DOCK8-associated disease. It is referred to as a variant of uncertain significance. Heterozygous DOCK8 mutations contribute to HLH by dominant-negative or hypomorphic effects [8, 9]. DOCK8 regulates the function of lymphocytes T and B, NK. Typical patients with DOCK8 deficiency present with eczema, respiratory tract infections, high levels of serum IgE, and hyper-eosinophilia; they cannot clear bacterial, fungal, and viral infections [10]. NK cells from patients with DOCK8 deficiency form defective immune synapses due to reduced F-actin accumulation [11]. This results in decreased cytotoxicity.

Patient 3

A 3-year-old boy was diagnosed with HLH. After HLH-designated treatment, he experienced bone marrow aplasia and was treated with allo-HSCT. The boy’s father is of Marocain descent, and his twin brother was diagnosed with juvenile idiopathic arthritis.

In this patient two heterozygotic variants were found, one in the AIRE gene (10 G > A) and one in the RNASEH2B gene (c.649T > C) (Table 2, Fig. 1)

The c.10 G > A change detected in the AIRE gene results in the replacement of aspartic acid (Asp) at position four into asparagine (Asn). This variant was not previously described in a population nor detected in patients with AIRE-associated diseases. This is a single nucleotide missense variant of uncertain significance. The AIRE gene is mutated in the rare autoimmune polyendocrinopathy syndrome type 1 (APS-1) [12]. There may be a possible connection between AIRE and HLH. It is proven that the CD1d-restricted invariant NKT cells are reduced in APS-1 patients compared to healthy controls. Dysfunction in NK cells is one of the HLH pathomechanisms.

The other change in the same patient was serine to proline replacement at position 217 in the 2B subunit in RNASEH protein (Table 2, Fig. 1). It is a non-conservative amino acid substitution, likely impacting secondary protein structure. This substitution occurs in the region where amino acids with similar properties to serine are tolerated across species. The c.649T > C variant in the RNASEH2B gene is likely pathogenic. The c.649T > C variant was not observed in previously described large cohorts [11]. Loss-of-function mutations in the RNASEH2B gene lead to upregulation of interferon-α (IFN-α) activity through the cytosolic accumulation of endogenous nucleic acids. Upregulation of IFN is one of the abnormalities detected in HLH [12].

Patient 4

A 20-month-old boy developed HLH following a chickenpox infection. NGS sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in exon 15 of the PSTPIP1 gene (c.1213C > T) (Table 2, Fig. 1). No other mutations of genes associated with the HLH syndrome were found. After four weeks, his condition deteriorated again, and cytometric analysis indicated acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-cell precursor subtype). The PSTPIP1 gene is located on chromosome 15 in the region of 15q24-q25.1. The gene defect is dominantly inherited. The genetic modification manifested as the amino acid change of arginine to cysteine at position 405 is localized in the SH3 domain of PSTPIP1 protein. This domain is responsible for the binding of PSTPIP1 to the Wiskott -Aldrich syndrome protein (WASP). The arginine in position 405 is moderately conserved. Previously published functional studies demonstrated that the change of Arg to Cys in the position of 405 might also alter the interactions of PSTPIP1 with the cytoskeleton and cell migration regulators [13]. This variant is reported as uncertain significance. The PSTPIP1 1213C > T; Arg405Cys variant is reported in the literature in at least one individual affected with pyoderma gangrenosum, acne, and suppurative hidradenitis (PASH) syndrome and in multiple individuals affected with pyogenic sterile arthritis, pyoderma gangrenosum, and acne (PAPA) syndrome [14, 15].

Discussion

Our studies performed on children diagnosed with HLH revealed the detection of a few changes in genes involved in the immune response. These changes were not previously described in patients with HLH and were designated as either uncertain or likely pathogenic [16–18]. This is consistent with other studies. Zhang et al.; showed the defective genes responsible for HLH extend beyond PRF1, UNC13D, STX11, STXBP2, LYST, RAB27A, AP3B1, SH2D1A, and XIAP [19].

The described cases show that genetic testing is essential in HLH pediatric patients, especially when the course is complicated and recurrences occur. Relapses occur most often in patients with HLH of genetic background. We must be aware that contemporary molecular testing methods provide many results – straight answers are not always possible. Not each found genetic change is responsible for the disease. Functional analyses are essential for understanding pathomechanism.
