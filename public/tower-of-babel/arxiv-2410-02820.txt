Heuristics and Biases in AI Decision-Making: Implications for Responsible AGI
1 st Payam Saeedi, 2 nd Mahsa Goodarzi, 3 rd M Abdullah Canbaz

Abstract
We investigate the presence of cognitive biases in three large language models (LLMs): GPT-4o, Gemma 2, and Llama 3.1. The study uses 1,500 experiments across nine established cognitive biases to evaluate the responses and consistency of the models. GPT-4o demonstrated the strongest overall performance. Gemma 2 showed strengths in addressing the sunk cost fallacy and prospect theory; however, its performance varied across different biases. Llama 3.1 consistently underperformed, relying on heuristics and exhibiting frequent inconsistencies and contradictions. The findings highlight the challenges of achieving robust and generalizable reasoning in LLMs, and underscore the need for further development to mitigate biases in artificial general intelligence (AGI). The study emphasizes the importance of integrating statistical reasoning and ethical considerations in future AI development.

Our study seeks to address the following research questions:

•

To what extent do cognitive biases inherent in human reasoning, such as loss aversion, framing effects, and the conjunction fallacy, manifest in the responses of GPT-4o, Gemma 2, and Llama 3.1 in probabilistic and decision-making scenarios?

By addressing these questions, we aim to advance the understanding of how LLMs process cognitive tasks and contribute to the broader objective of developing responsible Artificial General Intelligence (AGI). The findings of this study are crucial for identifying the limitations and strengths of LLMs in reasoning under uncertainty and for guiding their development as ethical and effective decision-making tools.

II
Experiment Settings: Investigating Cognitive Biases in LLMs

Machine learning and deep learning models operate fundamentally differently from the human mind, which limits the direct applicability of certain cognitive heuristics. For instance, asking ChatGPT to ”recall” six or twelve instances of its own assertive behavior during interactions with colleagues
[
25
]
would be infeasible, as these models lack personal experience or episodic memory. To explore the presence of cognitive biases in Large Language Models (LLMs), we systematically reviewed an extensive list of biases and selected nine that could be operationalized as prompts designed to elicit machine-readable responses.

Below, we describe each selected bias, its theoretical foundations, and its relevance to understanding the reasoning patterns of LLMs.

Loss Aversion:
Loss aversion is the tendency to perceive losses as more impactful and painful than equivalent gains are pleasurable. This bias leads individuals to avoid losses with greater motivation than pursuing gains of similar value
[
26
,
27
]
. In our experiments, we designed prompts requiring the three models to navigate scenarios involving trade-offs between potential losses and gains, analyzing whether their responses prioritized loss avoidance.

Sunk Cost Fallacy:
The sunk cost fallacy describes an irrational commitment to a course of action based on prior investments of time, money, or effort, even when abandoning the action would yield greater benefits. The stronger the perceived investment, the harder it becomes to disengage
[
28
,
29
]
. We tested whether the models exhibited this bias by presenting scenarios that juxtaposed past investments with future payoffs.

Prospect Theory / Risk Aversion:
Prospect theory explains risk aversion as the tendency to prefer certain outcomes over uncertain ones, even when the uncertain option offers a potentially higher reward. This behavior is often driven by an aversion to ambiguity and a desire for safety
[
26
,
30
,
31
]
. Prompts targeting this bias evaluated whether the three studied models adhered to probabilistic reasoning or mirrored human tendencies to avoid uncertainty.

Framing Effect:
The framing effect occurs when decisions are influenced by whether information is presented positively (gain) or negatively (loss), despite identical expected outcomes
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
. By crafting scenarios with alternative framings, we examined whether our three models produced consistent responses or displayed susceptibility to this bias. In this case, we asked each model whether they would support or not support an experimental life-saving medicine that would:

1.

Save 300 out of 800 patients

2.

Kill 500 out of 800 patients

Halo Effect:
The halo effect describes the tendency to form an overly favorable impression of one attribute of a person or entity based on unrelated positive traits
[
35
,
36
]
. This bias often results in skewed evaluations. Prompts were designed to test whether the models demonstrated similar overgeneralization in their reasoning.

Conjunction Fallacy (The Linda Problem):
The conjunction fallacy is the erroneous belief that the likelihood of two events occurring together is higher than the probability of either event occurring alone. This violates fundamental probability principles
[
37
,
38
]
. We presented the three models with variations of the ”Linda Problem” to assess whether they could avoid this bias by applying statistical reasoning.

Bias of Resemblance / Representativeness:
This bias involves estimating the likelihood of an event based on its resemblance to a prototype or stereotype, often neglecting statistical base rates
[
4
,
37
]
. We crafted prompts to determine whether the models defaulted to representativeness heuristics or leveraged statistical reasoning.

Neglect of Probability 1 & 2:

Neglect of probability occurs when the likelihood of an event is ignored or downplayed due to emotional or vivid factors. This bias manifests in two forms: (1) comparing scenarios based on perceived magnitude rather than probability, and (2) treating independent events as correlated
[
39
,
40
]
. Experiments explored whether the examined models consistently adhered to probabilistic reasoning across these scenarios.

Experimental Design and Procedure

Data:

We examined three models released in 2024, namely, GPT-4o
[
41
]
, Llama 3.1
[
42
]
, and Gemma 2
[
43
]
, and provided each with ten identical prompts for each cognitive bias.

The prompts were adapted from psychological experiments which used similar text to identify cognitive biases in the first place.

To ensure consistency, zero-shot chain-of-thought prompting was used, and the models were assigned the role of ”a human participating in a social experiment”
[
24
]
. Settings available to the general public through chat-bots were preserved, with the temperature kept at its default value.

At the conclusion of each prompt, the model was instructed to explain its reasoning and select one of two alternatives. Except for the framing effect wherein the model is asked if they support or not support a scenario, responses were labeled as follows:

•

Elaborate:
Responses based on detailed statistical reasoning.

•

Intuitive:
Responses reflecting simplifying heuristics.

•

Other:
Non-responses or deviations from the two answers offered above.

For example, a prompt described an individual who was tidy, organized, and detail-oriented, while also being a survivor of domestic violence. The model was then asked whether this individual was more likely to be a librarian or both a librarian and an advocate for domestic violence survivors. Statistically, the probability of belonging to both sets (librarian and advocate) is lower than the probability of belonging to either set individually. However, reliance on heuristic reasoning often leads to the intuitive yet incorrect conclusion that the individual is both a librarian and an advocate. Identifying the individual as solely a librarian was deemed the elaborate response.

Inspired by the experimental designs used to study these biases in humans, we replicated each experiment 150 times and tallied the proportion of elaborate and intuitive responses for each prompt. Responses were evaluated against a curated list of keywords associated with each bias. Keywords were extracted from prominent literature
[
31
,
37
,
39
,
44
,
4
,
27
]
to determine whether the models recognized or referenced the biases in their reasoning.

For reproducibility purposes, we have made the data curated and the model parameters along with prompts and sample results available for the community in our GitHub page

Experimental Results

III-A

Bias Manifestation

To evaluate the extent to which cognitive biases manifest in language model responses, 1,500 experiments were conducted across nine cognitive biases using identical prompts for GPT-4o, Gemma 2, and Llama 3.1. The radar plot in Figure
1
provides a comparative view of the elaborate responses generated by the models in question, across various cognitive biases. Each axis represents one cognitive bias, and the distance from the center reflects the frequency of elaborate (statistically reasoned) responses. The areas enclosed by the lines for each model highlight their relative strengths and weaknesses in addressing these biases. Below, we discuss the key insights observed from the results.

Conjunction Fallacy - A Dominant Strength for GPT-4o:

GPT-4o exhibits an exceptionally high performance in recognizing and reasoning through the conjunction fallacy, with its response rate far surpassing those of Gemma 2 and Llama 3.1. Its ability to correctly identify and explain why the probability of two events occurring together is always less than or equal to the probability of either event alone underscores its strength in applying fundamental statistical principles.

In contrast, both Gemma 2 and Llama 3.1 display significant limitations in this domain, reflecting a reliance on heuristic-based reasoning that frequently leads to incorrect responses. This disparity highlights GPT-4o’s superior capacity for handling probabilistic reasoning tasks, making it a potentially reliable tool for applications where logical rigor in decision-making is paramount. However, its outlier performance in this bias also raises questions about why similar statistical principles do not generalize as effectively across other biases.

Sunk Cost Fallacy and Prospect Theory - Strengths of Gemma 2:

Gemma 2 demonstrates notable proficiency in addressing the sunk cost fallacy and prospect theory, outperforming GPT-4o and Llama 3.1. Its relatively higher scores suggest a better alignment with human-like reasoning when evaluating decisions influenced by past investments (sunk cost) and risk preferences (prospect theory). This performance may stem from Gemma 2’s design, which appears to emphasize contextual sensitivity in scenarios requiring nuanced judgments about losses and risks.

Despite these strengths, Gemma 2’s overall performance across other biases remains inconsistent, indicating that its capabilities may be domain-specific rather than generalizable across a broad spectrum of cognitive tasks.

Neglect of Probability - Persistent Challenges Across Models:
While all three models correctly acknowledged the independence of probabilistic events targeted in neglect of probability 2; the neglect of probability 1 bias presents a persistent challenge for all of them. None were achieving consistently high levels of elaborate responses in the latter case. GPT-4o and Gemma 2 perform moderately, while Llama 3.1 struggles significantly. This finding suggests that even advanced models frequently fail to incorporate probabilistic reasoning when the context involves emotional or vivid imagery, a hallmark of this bias.

The inability to consistently recognize or mitigate probability neglect indicates an inherent limitation in the models’ design, particularly in bridging the gap between statistical principles and emotionally charged or vividly framed scenarios. Addressing these deficiencies is crucial for improving the reliability of AI in high-stakes decision-making.

Bias of Resemblance - A Common Weakness:
All three models exhibit significant struggles with the bias of resemblance, with GPT-4o and Gemma 2 defaulting to stereotypical reasoning in a majority of cases. This bias demonstrates the models’ propensity to rely on surface-level similarities rather than statistical base rates, a behavior that mirrors human heuristic-driven reasoning. Llama 3.1, in particular, shows minimal ability to overcome this bias, further emphasizing its reliance on simplistic heuristics.

The widespread difficulty across models in addressing this bias underscores the need for enhanced mechanisms to prioritize statistical reasoning over superficial associations, especially in applications requiring equitable and unbiased decision-making.

Framing Effects - Susceptibility to Presentation Context:

The framing effect reveals moderate to high susceptibility among all three models, with GPT-4o, Gemma 2, and Llama 3.1 providing contradictory responses to positive and negative framings of identical scenarios in 57%, 74%, and 85% of cases, respectively. This variability suggests that the models are influenced by the presentation context, failing to maintain consistent reasoning across different framings of the same problem.

Such susceptibility poses challenges for applications where consistency and impartiality in decision-making are critical. While GPT-4o demonstrates relatively lower susceptibility compared to Gemma 2 and Llama 3.1, its moderate performance indicates room for improvement in ensuring contextual robustness.

Llama 3.1 - The Least Reliable Performer:

Across all biases, Llama 3.1 consistently underperforms, with low rates of elaborate responses and frequent reliance on heuristics. Its inability to provide consistent or statistically sound reasoning highlights fundamental limitations in its architecture or training. Additionally, Llama 3.1’s frequent non-responses or contradictions further diminish its reliability, rendering it unsuitable for tasks requiring high levels of cognitive complexity or probabilistic reasoning.

Broader Implications:

The comparative analysis presented in the radar plot offers a comprehensive view of the models’ reasoning capabilities. GPT-4o emerges as the strongest performer overall, particularly in conjunction fallacy and other tasks requiring probabilistic reasoning. However, its weaknesses in biases such as framing effects and neglect of probability highlight the need for further refinement. Gemma 2, while showing strengths in specific domains such as sunk cost fallacy and prospect theory, struggles with generalizability across other biases. Llama 3.1’s consistently poor performance emphasizes the limitations of simplistic heuristic-driven models in handling complex cognitive tasks.

III-C

Bias Recognition and Statistical Reasoning

The ability of the models to recognize and mitigate cognitive biases was assessed through their use of key concepts such as
“probability”
and
“expected value”
. Figure
2

GPT-4o demonstrated the strongest ability to integrate statistical reasoning, achieving 63% accuracy in probability neglect scenarios and 82% in sunk cost fallacy cases. Its frequent reference to probabilistic concepts reflects an understanding of certain biases, but this did not generalize effectively. For instance, GPT-4o performed poorly in addressing the bias of resemblance, defaulting to intuitive stereotyping in most cases. These inconsistencies indicate gaps in applying statistical principles across biases.

Gemma 2 displayed moderate success, particularly in sunk cost fallacy and prospect theory, but often relied on emotional or contextual reasoning rather than explicit statistical logic. This contextual sensitivity sometimes led to human-like reasoning but at the expense of consistency, as seen in variability across framing effects and probability neglect.

Llama 3.1 struggled significantly, with fragmented reasoning paths and limited recognition of statistical concepts. It frequently defaulted to non-responses or contradicting answers, failing to engage effectively with probabilistic reasoning even in straightforward scenarios like conjunction fallacy.

These results underscore a critical limitation: while the models can recognize some biases and use relevant terminology, their application of statistical reasoning remains inconsistent. GPT-4o’s relative success highlights progress but also exposes challenges in generalizing across diverse biases. Addressing these shortcomings is essential for improving the reliability of AI decision-making.

III-D

Comparative Performance Across Models

The comparative performance of GPT-4o, Gemma 2, and Llama 3.1 across nine cognitive biases reveals significant disparities in their ability to reason statistically, address cognitive heuristics, and maintain consistency. The overall percentage of correct, elaborate responses was 49% for both GPT-4o and Gemma 2, and 34% for Llama 3.1. These findings offer critical insights into the current state of large language models (LLMs) and their implications for the development of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI).

GPT-4o: A Step Toward Statistical Reasoning.
GPT-4o exhibited exceptional performance in handling conjunction fallacy scenarios, achieving near-perfect accuracy by leveraging probabilistic reasoning principles. This strength reflects a step forward in equipping LLMs with the capacity to apply mathematical rigor to decision-making tasks. However, its inconsistent performance in biases like framing effects and the bias of resemblance highlights its limitations in generalizing these principles across diverse scenarios. While GPT-4o often referenced concepts like “expected value” and “probability,” it frequently failed to integrate them effectively in emotionally charged or context-dependent scenarios, exposing a gap between recognition and application of bias mitigation strategies.

Gemma 2: Contextual Sensitivity with Trade-offs.
Gemma 2 demonstrated superior performance in biases such as the sunk cost fallacy and prospect theory, where its contextual sensitivity allowed for nuanced reasoning aligned with human-like decision-making. However, this strength also introduced variability; Gemma 2’s reliance on emotional or situational cues often led to inconsistent responses in framing effects and probability neglect. Notably, Gemma 2’s responses occasionally included humorous or stylistic deviations, such as emojis and template-based explanations, suggesting an effort to “humanize” outputs but inadvertently reducing reliability in high-stakes cognitive tasks. This trade-off between adaptability and consistency underscores a key challenge in balancing contextual flexibility with logical rigor—a critical consideration for AGI development.

Llama 3.1: A Fragmented Approach to Cognitive Tasks.
Llama 3.1 consistently underperformed across all biases, with only 34% of responses classified as elaborate. Its reasoning paths were frequently marked by contradictions, non-responses, and heuristic-driven decision-making. For instance, Llama 3.1 often stated one answer while providing reasoning that supported a different conclusion. This fragmented approach underscores the model’s inability to process probabilistic or bias-related tasks effectively, limiting its utility in any scenario requiring robust cognitive reasoning. Llama 3.1’s shortcomings highlight the risks of relying on surface-level heuristics and the necessity of deeper integration of statistical and logical reasoning in future iterations.

Behavioral Nuances and Their Implications for AGI.
Beyond quantitative differences, the qualitative behaviors of the models provide profound insights into the state of LLM reasoning. GPT-4o’s systematic approach to conjunction fallacy offers a glimpse into the potential for AGI to replicate human-level logical reasoning. However, its inability to generalize such reasoning across other biases reflects a critical bottleneck: AGI systems must transcend task-specific optimization to achieve universal adaptability in reasoning.

Gemma 2’s contextual sensitivity and stylistic outputs reveal another dimension: AGI must balance human-like adaptability with decision-making consistency. While stylistic “humanization” enhances user engagement, it can detract from logical rigor, particularly in tasks requiring objectivity and precision. Llama 3.1, on the other hand, illustrates the challenges of foundational inconsistency. Its failure to engage deeply with probabilistic reasoning highlights the need for stronger architectures capable of addressing diverse cognitive tasks holistically.

Toward Bias-Aware AGI.
The disparities in performance across models point to a crucial insight for AGI development: the recognition and mitigation of cognitive biases are not merely peripheral challenges but foundational to achieving generalizable intelligence. AGI must not only recognize biases but also consistently apply statistical reasoning to overcome them across varied contexts and emotionally charged scenarios. The variability observed in framing effects and probability neglect underscores the need for AGI systems to maintain consistency in their responses, independent of how problems are presented.

These findings emphasize that while models like GPT-4o and Gemma 2 represent meaningful progress, they remain constrained by their inability to uniformly apply logical rigor across tasks. Advancing toward AGI requires overcoming these limitations by integrating probabilistic reasoning, contextual understanding, and emotional neutrality into a unified framework. The performance gaps highlighted in this study underscore the importance of addressing these foundational challenges to enable AGI systems capable of reasoning and decision-making on par with—or surpassing—human cognition.

IV
Conclusion

This study represents a step toward understanding and addressing cognitive biases in AI systems, with the ultimate goal of contributing to the development of a responsible and accountable Artificial General Intelligence (AGI). By examining the behavior of GPT-4o, Gemma 2, and Llama 3.1 across nine cognitive biases, we uncovered critical insights into their reasoning capabilities, limitations, and variability.

GPT-4o’s strong performance in tasks like the conjunction fallacy highlights its potential to apply statistical reasoning effectively in structured scenarios. However, its inconsistent handling of biases such as framing effects and the bias of resemblance demonstrates that even advanced models remain vulnerable to heuristic-driven errors. Similarly, while Gemma 2 exhibited contextual sensitivity and human-like reasoning in certain scenarios, its inconsistency across tasks underscores the challenges of balancing adaptability with logical rigor. Llama 3.1, with its frequent contradictions and fragmented reasoning, illustrates the fundamental limitations of heuristic-heavy approaches to decision-making.

Our findings emphasize that while LLMs have made significant progress, they still lack the robustness and generalizability required for AGI. The variability in bias recognition and reasoning consistency across models reveals a critical gap in their ability to engage with cognitive biases holistically and responsibly. Achieving this will require not only advancements in statistical reasoning capabilities but also frameworks that integrate ethical considerations, emotional neutrality, and contextual understanding into AI systems.

This work underscores the importance of viewing cognitive biases not as peripheral issues but as foundational challenges for AGI development. A responsible AGI must not only mimic human reasoning but also surpass its limitations by recognizing and mitigating biases in a consistent and explainable manner. Until such time as AI systems achieve this level of robustness, the presence of a human element in decision-making processes will remain essential. Future research should expand the scope of biases studied, explore additional models, and develop standardized benchmarks to ensure the evolution of AI systems that are both powerful and equitable.

Framing Effect

Unique to Llama 3.1 was its approach toward the framing effect, where the same hypothetical was posed in both a positive and a negative light. In numerous cases, it refused to choose either answer, potentially referring to an internal policy that prevented it from commenting on experimental drugs. Interestingly, it did not hesitate to choose one in other iterations of the same problem.

Response:
I’m sorry, but I cannot provide a response that would promote or condone the use of an experimental drug with such severe consequences. Is there anything else I can help you with?

In the negative framing scenario, we asked whether the model would support an experimental drug that could save lives but potentially kill 500 out of 800 people (as opposed to framing it positively by stating it could save 300 people). In 33% of the experiments, the model refused to respond. However, in some cases, it provided an explanation and then proceeded to make a decision.

Response:
I’m not going to make a decision based on this question.

Reasoning:
The wording of the question implies that the experimental drug is fatal regardless of whether it is used or not. In other words, 500 people will die from the disease either way. Therefore, introducing the drug would only shift who dies first, but not change the overall number of fatalities. This raises ethical considerations about whether we should accelerate the inevitable suffering for some while delaying it for others.

Answer: B.
