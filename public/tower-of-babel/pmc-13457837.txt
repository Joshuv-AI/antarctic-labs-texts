Bidirectional influences and clinical implications of psychological factors and oral health during orthodontic treatment

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPsychological factors such as anxiety and stress frequently affect orthodontic treatment outcomes, but the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Introduction

Orthodontics is about guiding the growth and development of teeth and their supporting structures (Dewel, 1962). But beyond straightening teeth, it also affects how patients feel about themselves—and, in turn, how they feel can affect how well treatment goes (Abreu et al. 2018).

Pain, discomfort, and worries about appearance are common during orthodontic treatment, and these can bring about anxiety, fear, and stress (Bartlett et al. 2005). At the same time, patients who have already struggled with these feelings before treatment may have a harder time following orthodontist instructions, tolerating pain, or satisfying the results. For instance, people with dental fear tend to have higher hair cortisol levels (Viitaniemi et al. 2021) and even short-term stress can raise salivary α-amylase during tooth movement (Canigur Bavbek et al. 2022). Although researchers have learned alot about how the brain talks to other organs, much less is known about how psychology and oral health interact during orthodontics. Understanding this twoway relationship matters for improving patient outcomes.

This review has two main goals. First, we make an attempt to summarize the psychological factors that matter the most in orthodontic treatment. Second, we describe the biological pathways that may explain these effects—specifically changes in the nervous system, the oral microbiome, and alveolar bone remodeling. We end with practical advice for orthodontists.

Materials and methods

Review design

This review is narrative in nature. Because the studies identified in the literature varied widely in design, populations, and outcome measures, a formal meta-analysis would not be appropriate. The available evidence was critically reviewed and synthesized according to major thematic areas.

Literature search

Relevant literature was searched in Web of Science and PubMed from database inception through January 2025 using combinations of terms related to orthodontic treatment (such as orthodontic, orthognathic, or tooth movement), psychological factors (psychological, anxiety, stress, fear, depression, and mental health), and oral health mechanisms (oral microbiota, salivary biomarkers, taste, alveolar bone, and cytokines). The reference lists of relevant articles were also manually screened to identify additional publications. Only articles published in English were considered.

Literature selection

Literature were selected based on their relevance to the objectives of this review. Priority was given to high-quality original research articles, systematic reviews, and representative clinical or experimental studies that examined psychological factors in orthodontic treatment. Studies addressing oral health outcomes, biological mechanisms, or patient-related psychological responses were included, whereas publications with limited relevance to the review topic, conference abstracts, isolated case reports, and studies unrelated to orthodontics were not considered.

Literature synthesis

The selected literature was critically evaluated and synthesized to provide an overview of current evidence regarding the relationships between psychological factors, oral health and orthodontic treatment. Studies were grouped according to major themes, including psychological influences on orthodontic treatment, biological mechanisms linking psychological status with oral health, and their clinical implications. Main findings: psychological factors in orthodontic treatment

Several psychological factors affecting orthodontic treatment which can be grouped into patient aims, psychological profiles, age differences, and appliance type.

Patient aims and psychological profiles

People seek for orthodontic treatment for different reasons. Some want to find solutions to functional problems like malocclusion or temporomandibular disorders; others are primarily concerned with facial appearance (Blatz et al. 2019). For adolescents especially, aesthetic concerns can strongly affect self-esteem. At the same time, patients who come in with temporomandibular symptoms often already have higher levels of psychological distress than those coming for single malocclusion (Yap et al. 2025; Suomela et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2022). And psychological symptoms tend to occur modestly after surgery and decline progressively over the following two years, eventually becoming lower than pre-surgery (Hatch et al. 1999).

Age differences

Adolescents between 12 and 18 years make up a large percent of orthodontic patients. At this age, they are particularly sensitive to facial aesthetics and social comparison. And younger children (6 to 12 years) report less pain, shorter pain duration, and fewer changes in eating habits compared to adults (Negruțiu et al. 2024). Adults, on the other hand, tend to have worse oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) before starting treatment. Still, both children and adults show clear improvement in OHRQoL after orthodontic treatment (Tikhonov et al. 2025).

Appliance type and psychological impact

The material of appliances also affect the growth of teeth as well as dental tissues, altering oral health and influencing psychological states. Allergic sensitization and antibacterial properties should also be considered when selecting appliances (Zigante et al. 2020). Not all orthodontic appliances affect patients in the same way. Fixed appliances, such as edgewise and straight-wire devices—interfere more with diet and daily quality of life than removable ones. Anxious patients often prefer lingual braces or clear aligners, which feel more adjustable and look better. Of all the procedures, debonding is reported as the most painful (Mangnall et al. 2013). Pain typically peaks two days after the appliance is placed or adjusted, then gradually fades (Chan et al. 2024). How pain a patient feels also depends on whether they take analgesics, how they chew, what they eat, and their personality (Lorek et al. 2024; Arunagiri et al. 2025). Not surprisingly, patients who have a positive attitude and good knowledge about their treatment tend to report less pain (Ev et al. 2024). Patients’ choice of appliances is often driven by psychological expectations. A comparative study showed that compared to fixed appliances, patients using clear aligners scored significantly lower in social anxiety dimensions. It is worth noting that some patients seeking orthodontic treatment may potentially suffer from Body Dysmorphic Disorder (BDD), which can cause them to have extremely negative psychological reactions to minor alignment deviations (Shokeen et al. 2022).

Biological mechanisms linking psychology to orthodontic outcomes

Both the nervous system mediators and oral microbiome engaged in the bridge between psychological status and orthodontic outcomes.

Nervous system mediators

Psychological states are not separate from physical health; they directly affect how the nervous system functions, and that in turn can influence orthodontic outcomes (Pillai et al. 2020). Two major systems come into play here: the locus coeruleus-sympathetic-adrenal medulla (LSAM) axis (Hendley et al. 1988) and the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis (Chrousos 1992). Both are central to how the body responds to emotional challenges.

Take the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), for example, its medial part handles reward, while the lateral side deals with non-reward and punishment (Rolls and Cooper 1974). In people with depression, brain imaging shows that the lateral OFC becomes overly connected to other regions, whereas the medial OFC becomes less connected (Zhang et al. 2024; Rolls et al. 2020). Decreased functional connectivity of the medial orbitofrontal cortex with medial temporal lobe regions involved in memory has also been observed in depression, suggesting possible therapeutic implications (Rolls et al. 2020). Animal work also points to HPA involvement: rats with occlusal disharmony develop depression-like behavior that seems to depend on HPA dysregulation (Zhang et al. 2022). In addition to traditional neuroendocrine pathways, He et al. further demonstrated in animal models that chronic stress significantly slows down the rate of orthodontic tooth movement by inhibiting the RANKL/OPG ratio via sympathetic nerve signaling (Cheng et al. 2026).

Fear and anxiety engage somewhat different circuits. Dental fear is a significant influence factor on life quality and strongly engages amygdala-associated circuits (Huang et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2024; Bludau et al. 2024) and corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF) released from amygdala neurons plays a key role (Pross et al. 2024). The ascending serotonin (5-HT) pathway from the dorsal raphe nucleus to the amygdala also helps drive conditioned fear (Ravenelle et al. 2025). Anxiety, while overlapping with fear, involves HPA activation and increased CRF as well (Chudoba and Dabrowska 2026). Oral microbial alterations

Increased synthesis of stress-associated GroEL-like proteins has been observed in oral bacteria and correlates with patient stress responses (Vayssier et al. 1994). Stress does not stay in the brain, it also reaches the mouth. Several studies have found that psychological stress changes the composition of both oral and gut microbiota (Stoy et al. 2023; Wei et al. 2024). Under acute stress, salivary levels of secretory IgA (s-IgA), α-amylase activity, and the amylase-to-protein ratio all go up, even though the amount of saliva produced stays the same. At the same time, the ability of Streptococcus gordonii to aggregate in saliva declines (Bosch et al. 1996). Another observation comes from athletes: those “in-season” report less anxiety and show less bacterial growth on mitis salivarius agar (which mainly grows oral streptococci) compared to off-season athletes. Salivary biomarkers serve as sensitive indicators of stress levels. Recent studies have found that salivary α-amylase levels in adolescent patients during the initial stages of orthodontic force application are highly dynamically correlated with their Dental Anxiety Scale scores. Meanwhile, it is emphasized that psychological stress-induced alteration in oral and gut microbial diversity (Paudel et al. 2022,) particularly causing a dysbiosis in the proportion of Actinomyces, and this dysbiosis is closely related to the reduced secretory IgA and subsequent increased caries risk was observed (Castro-Quintas et al. 2023).

Changes in taste under stress (discussed next in 4.3) may further alter microbial ecology by shifting what patients choose to eat. For example, a short-term increase in sugar intake (sugar stress) raises the relative abundance of Streptococcus species and increases salivary transferrin (Grunberg et al. 2021). In people with periodontitis, genera such as Prevotella, Streptococcus, and Lactobacillus are more common (Bowden and Li 1997). Taste alterations: a link between psychology and oral microbiota

On the tongue, different taste cells handle different qualities. Type 1 cells detect salt through the ENaC channel. Type 2 cells respond to sweet, umami, and bitter via G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs). Type 3 cells are responsible for sour taste. Bitter taste is more complex: it involves not only T2R receptors (a GPCR family) but also a potassium-sensitive ion channel on type 2 cells. When CFTR function is impaired, the nitric oxide response normally triggered by bitter taste receptors is reduced (Carey et al. 2023). Spiciness, though often grouped with taste, actually activates pain pathways rather than classical taste receptors (He et al. 2025). In contrast, sweet and umami tastes are more directly tied to mood regulation (Skurvydas et al. 2025). Loss of smell or taste can worsen emotional dysfunction, which raises the possibility that food-based strategies might offer some psychological support (Chen et al. 2021). Taste perception is not only affected by local factors but also regulated by the central nervous system. It is found that individuals under long-term high stress have significantly increased sensitivity thresholds for bitter and salty tastes (Rudzińska et al. 2024).

Both serotonin and ATP-mediated signaling participate in taste-cell neurotransmission under stress conditions (Wang et al. 2024; Wilson and Kinnamon 2025). Disgust sensitivity may predict greater food-based sensory processing changes and has been associated with chemotherapy-related physical symptoms (Stevens et al. 2021; Dev et al. 2021). Acute mental stress, common in the early weeks of orthodontic treatment, can actually sharpen taste sensitivity while dulling the perception of pungency (Sawai et al. 2019). This effect appears to be driven by sympathetic nervous system activation. Both adrenaline/noradrenaline and serotonin (5-HT) are involved; taste cells themselves contain and respond to these neurotransmitters (Wang et al. 2024; Wilson and Kinnamon 2025).

Taste signals travel to the brain through three nerves: the chorda tympani (CN-VII), the glossopharyngeal (CN-IX), and the trigeminal (CN-V), eventually reaching the insular gustatory cortex (Warner et al. 2021). Animal studies have shown that chronic restraint stress disrupts taste transduction in taste receptor cells. Decreased input of olfaction and gustation additively impact the exacerbation of emotional dysfunction, indicating the possible use of food therapy in psychological problems.

Psychological factors: influencing alveolar bone reconstruction

Anxiety, stress and other psychological factors are critical causes of periodontic damage including alveolar bone destruction which are foundation of orthodontic tooth movement(OTM). These psychological factors initiate expression of pro-inflammatory factors as increased IL-2, IL-6 and IL-8 were found around mini-screws during orthodontic tooth movement. Level of IL-1β and vascular endothelial growth factor(VEGF) also changed via physiological and psychological factor and the effect could be blocked by IL-1Ra. In animal studies, IL-20 promotes orthodontic tooth movement by activating the RANKL/NF-κB/NFATc1 pathway (Liu 2021). These mechanisms could serve as potential mediators regulating alveolar reconstruction and effect of orthodontic treatment.

Alveolar bone remodeling

Clinical evidence shows that anxiety and stress are linked to periodontitis and alveolar bone loss … Their study revealed that patients with high self-reported psychological stress exhibited significantly elevated levels of both cortisol and TNF-α directly within the human gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) during the initial alignment phase of orthodontic treatment. Clinically, this localized spike in stress-related biomarkers was strongly correlated with a reduced rate of orthodontic tooth movement, further confirming that psychological distress actively hinders the mechanical efficiency of alveolar bone remodeling (Gür et al. 2023).

Practical implications

Orthodontics affect patient life quality and dental appearance is a strong predictor for low self-esteem in adolescence. In the meantime, patient psychological states also greatly affect their orthodontics curative effects and their assessment toward this process.

Based on the analysis above, we proposed that two methods could be used as supplementary measures and increase orthodontic curative effects and patient satisfaction. The first one is psychological counselling which could relieve the stress during OTM and improve patient life quality. In the meantime, the relief of one emotion may alleviate that of the other so the attention on psychological problems can be worthy in more than one way (Grunberg et al. 2021). The second one is the education and knowledge popularization in both the orthodontic and other fields of oral health. For example, informing patients what they would expect to experience (Prove et al. 1997) and offering proper behavioral methods are suggested (Gross et al. 1985). Avoiding frequent mentioning of their treatment is also protective for patient mental health during the orthodontics. Adolescent social interactions also affect their assessment of orthodontic practice and life quality and the aesthetic standards vary from patients to dentists, indicating for more communications in OTM. To optimize the treatment experience, clinicians should introduce diversified psychological support methods. Reports demonstrated that combining Virtual Reality (VR) and Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) before clinical procedures can effectively reduce patients’ intraoperative pain perception (Câmara et al. 2023). This is consistent with the systematic review results suggesting that non-pharmacological psychological interventions serve as an important adjunct to facilitate mental wellbeing (van Agteren et al. 2021). More systemic researches with identical standards are required as the same criteria showed opposite results in different situations.

Discussion

Summary of principal findings

Overall, the evidence indicates that psychological factors, particularly stress, anxiety, and fear, influence orthodontic treatment through three major routes. First, they activate the nervous system, including the HPA axis and the autonomic pathways. Second, they alter oral microbiota and change salivary biomarkers such as cortisol, αamylase, and secretory IgA. Third, they modulate alveolar bone remodeling through proinflammatory cytokines like IL1β, IL6, and IL8. In addition, psychological stress can change taste perception, which in turn may shift dietary habits and indirectly affect the oral microbial environment.

Comparison with existing literature

Our findings align with broader psychoneuroimmunology researches showing that psychological stress modulates immune function and inflammation. In the oral cavity specifically, our review corroborates earlier work linking dental anxiety to elevated hair cortisol (Viitaniemi et al. 2021) and stress to salivary α-amylase changes (Canigur Bavbek et al. 2022). We extend these findings by proposing an integrated model (Figs. 2, 3 and 4) linking nervous system changes → taste alteration → dietary change → microbial dysbiosis → oral disease → further psychological distress.

Levels of evidence and critical appraisal

A large part of the mechanistic evidence discussed in this review comes from animal models or cell culture experiments, not directly from orthodontic patients. The strongest clinical findings come from crosssectional studies showing links between psychological states and salivary biomarkers such as cortisol, αamylase, and secretory IgA. Given this, clinicians should be cautious when applying mechanistic explanations to daily practice as most studies are crosssectional and no firm conclusions could be drawn about cause and effect.

Research gaps

To date, few studies have followed orthodontic patients over time to see how psychological states, salivary biomarkers, and clinical outcomes change together. Another issue is that researchers use different questionnaires to measure similar psychological concepts, for example, various anxiety scales that are not directly comparable. Moreover, most studies treat fear, anxiety, and stress as interchangeable, even though they may have distinct effects on treatment. Finally, while psychological interventions like cognitivebehavioral therapy are commonly used in other medical fields, their application in orthodontic populations remains largely unexplored.

Methodological recommendations for future research

Future research should address the following issues: First, standardizing psychological measures and differentiating specific domains (state anxiety vs. trait anxiety vs. dental fear);second, trying to conduct longitudinal studies and engage in test interventions; last but not least, using objective measures to ensure comparable peremeters (Jaldin et al. 2025). Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, as a narrative review without a systematic metaanalysis, our synthesis is qualitative and may be subject to selection bias, despite our efforts to use a structured search strategy and explicit inclusion/exclusion criteria. Second, we included studies with varying levels of evidence as clinical, animal, and in vitro as without statistical weighting, which limits the strength of mechanistic conclusions. Third, publication bias is possible, as we only included Englishlanguage peerreviewed articles and may have missed relevant nonEnglish or unpublished studies. Fourth, most of the included studies are crosssectional, which precludes causal inference regarding the directionality between psychological factors and orthodontic outcomes. Fifth, psychological states often correlate with socioeconomic status, general health, medications, and other unmeasured confounders that were not controlled for in many primary studies, potentially confounding the reported associations. We have attempted to mitigate these limitations by transparently reporting evidence levels throughout the review and by qualifying mechanistic claims with appropriate language.

Conclusions

This review reaches three main conclusions for clinicians and researchers. First, psychological factors such as anxiety, fear, and stress are common among orthodontic patients and measurably affect treatment compliance, pain perception, and quality of life; therefore, clinicians should pay attention to patient psychological status. Second, the biological mechanisms underlying these effects include HPA axis and autonomic nervous system activation, alterations in oral microbiota and salivary biomarkers, and inflammationmediated effects on alveolar bone remodeling, with taste changes under stress serving as an additional indirect pathway linking mental state to oral ecology. Third, practical measures—including routine psychological screening, patient education, structured followup, and judicious appliance selection—can improve orthodontic outcomes, while future research should adopt longitudinal designs and standardized psychological measures to establish causality.
