Oxidative balance score as a predictor of MASLD-CVD comorbidity risk and mortality: Insights from traditional and machine learning approaches
Authors: Fang Dong, Zhixin Zhang, Yukai Hao, Yang Yang, Jingxuan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOxidative stress underlies both metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and cardiovascular disease (CVD). The oxidative balance score (OBS) quantifies systemic oxidative stress from pro-/antioxidant exposures, but its role in MASLD-CVD comorbidity is unknown.MethodsWe analyzed 19,993 adults from NHANES 1999-2018. Multinomial logistic and Cox regression assessed OBS associations with comorbidity risk and all-cause mortality. Machine learning and SHAP interpretability analysis identified key predictors, and bioinformatics explored mechanistic pathways.ResultsLower OBS quartiles were associated with higher odds of prevalent MASLD (Q1 OR = 1.31), CVD (Q1 OR = 1.95), and MASLD-CVD comorbidity (Q1 OR = 2.15). Each unit increase in OBS reduced mortality risk (MASLD-CVD: HR = 0.98; MASLD: HR = 0.98; CVD: HR = 0.99). Critical OBS thresholds were identified: OBS<8.73 increased mortality risk in MASLD patients (HR = 1.09), while OBS≥8.73 was protective (HR = 0.96). For MASLD-CVD, OBS = 21 optimally stratified mortality risk (log-rank P = 0.0018). SHAP analysis revealed the nonlinear contributions of key dietary/lifestyle factors. The key pro-oxidant factors may contribute to comorbidity development through IL-17 and insulin resistance pathways, as suggested by bioinformatics analysis.ConclusionsOBS enables risk stratification for MASLD-CVD comorbidity, but external validation is required before clinical application.Keywords: Oxidative balance score, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, Cardiovascular disease, Comorbidity, Machine learning, Risk stratification

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MASLD) has become the most prevalent chronic liver disease globally, affecting over a quarter of the world's adult population and posing an increasingly severe challenge to public health systems [1]. The MASLD disease spectrum is broad, progressing from simple hepatic steatosis to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma [2,3]. Concurrently, cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death worldwide [4]. Substantial evidence indicates that MASLD and CVD not only share multiple risk factors but that MASLD itself is an independent risk factor for CVD development [5,6]. The co-morbidity of these two conditions (MASLD-CVD) is prevalent, and patients with both conditions face poorer clinical outcomes and higher all-cause mortality risk compared to those with either condition alone [7,8]. Therefore, identifying modifiable risk factors to prevent and manage MASLD-CVD co-morbidity has become a critical priority in current clinical research.

Oxidative stress is widely recognized as a core pathophysiological mechanism linking MASLD and CVD [9,10]. Under metabolic dysregulation states such as obesity and insulin resistance, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production coupled with diminished antioxidant defenses disrupts redox homeostasis. This oxidative stress state drives lipid peroxidation, inflammatory activation, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired insulin signaling pathways, collectively promoting the development of hepatic steatosis and atherosclerosis [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. However, traditional epidemiological studies often focus on individual dietary antioxidants or pro-oxidants, failing to comprehensively reflect the body's overall redox state.

To provide a more comprehensive assessment of this equilibrium, the Oxidative Balance Score (OBS) was developed [15]. OBS is a composite metric integrating multiple dietary nutrients and lifestyle factors. Higher antioxidant component scores coupled with lower pro-oxidant component scores result in a higher total OBS, indicating stronger antioxidant advantage. In recent years, elevated OBS (i.e., stronger antioxidant status) has been demonstrated to be independently associated with lower NAFLD/MASLD risk [16,17], lower CVD incidence [18,19], and mortality [20]. However, significant gaps exist in the current research landscape: First, the specific association between OBS and the prevalence of MASLD-CVD comorbidity remains unexplored. Second, the prospective association between OBS and long-term mortality in patients with comorbidity is unclear. Third, whether a nonlinear relationship exists between OBS and mortality, along with its critical threshold, requires further clarification. Finally, studies utilizing advanced computational biology methods to elucidate its underlying molecular mechanisms are lacking.

Against this backdrop, this study utilizes the nationally representative, large-scale prospective cohort data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to thoroughly investigate the association between OBS and the odds of MASLD-CVD comorbidity and all-cause mortality. Our research objectives include: (1) Assessing the cross-sectional association between OBS levels and the prevalence of MASLD, CVD, and their comorbidity; (2) Prospective analysis of OBS's impact on all-cause mortality in comorbid patients; (3) Identification of nonlinear relationships and key thresholds between OBS and mortality using restricted cubic splines and piecewise regression models; (4) Construction of optimal prognostic models via multiple machine learning algorithms, with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values to interpret the importance of OBS components; (5) Preliminary exploration of potential molecular targets and signaling pathways for key pro-oxidative factors through network pharmacology, providing clues for mechanism studies. This research is expected to provide novel epidemiological evidence and translational research perspectives for preventing and managing MASLD-CVD comorbidity by modulating overall oxidative balance.

Methods

Data sources

The data for this study were sourced from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a nationally representative survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). Ethical approval and informed consent for NHANES were authorized by the NCHS Ethics Review Board, so this secondary analysis was exempt from additional ethics review requirements.

Definitions of MASLD

In this study, the presence of hepatic steatosis was determined using the United States Fatty Liver Index (USFLI), a noninvasive method for assessing hepatic steatosis that has been previously validated and used in large-scale epidemiological studies [21,22]. The USFLI ranges from 0 to 100. For the calculation, the values for "non-Hispanic black" and "Mexican American" were assigned as 1 if the participant belonged to the respective ethnic group, and 0 otherwise. A USFLI score >30 was used to define hepatic steatosis, which provides an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.80, with a sensitivity of 62% and specificity of 88% [23].

MASLD is defined by the presence of hepatic steatosis combined with at least one of the following five adult cardiometabolic criteria: (1) BMI ≥25 kg/m2 or waist circumference ≥94 cm in males or ≥80 cm in females, (2) fasting glucose ≥100 mg/dl or 2-h post-load glucose levels ≥140 mg/dl or hemoglobin A1c ≥ 5.7% or diabetes mellitus or treatment for diabetes mellitus, (3) blood pressure ≥130/85 mmHg or antihypertensive medication use, (4) fasting plasma triglycerides ≥150 mg/dl or lipid-lowering therapy, (5) plasma HDL cholesterol <40 mg/dl in males or <50 mg/dl in females, or lipid-lowering therapy [24].

Definitions of OBS

The OBS consists of 16 dietary nutrients and 4 lifestyle factors. The nutritional components include dietary fiber, carotene (in retinol equivalents), vitamin B2, niacin, vitamin B6, folate, vitamin B12, vitamin C, vitamin E, calcium, magnesium, zinc, copper, selenium, total fat, and iron. The lifestyle factors comprise alcohol consumption, physical activity (measured in MET-minutes per week), cotinine levels, BMI, and the intake of each nutrient was calculated by averaging two 24-h dietary recalls, which incorporated contributions from both dietary intake and dietary supplements [15]. Five pro-oxidant components include total fat, iron, BMI, alcohol consumption, and cotinine, while the remaining factors were classified as antioxidants.

According to previously established methodologies [15], antioxidant components were scored by assigning values of 0, 1, and 2 to individuals in the lowest, middle, and highest tertiles, respectively. Conversely, a reverse scoring scheme was applied to pro-oxidant components, with the highest tertile receiving 0 points and the lowest tertile assigned 2 points. The overall OBS was derived by summing all component scores, yielding a total ranging from 3 to 36, where higher values reflect a more antioxidative profile. The scoring scheme for OBS components is detailed in Table S1.

Study participants and sample size

We collected data from ten cycles of the NHANES spanning 1999-2018, initially including 101,316 participants. After applying the following exclusion criteria: (1) age <20 years (n = 46,235); (2) missing MASLD-related information (n = 34,513); (3) missing CVD-related information (n = 91); (4) missing OBS value (n = 0); (5) pregnancy (n = 450); and (6) extreme dietary intake (<500 kcal/day or >5,000 kcal/day for women, <500 kcal/day or >8,000 kcal/day for men) (n = 34), the final cohort comprised 19,993 participants (Fig. 1).

Outcomes and covariates

Mortality data were obtained from the National Death Index (NDI) database maintained by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data-linkage/mortality-public.htm). The covariates encompassed demographic characteristics (sex, age, ethnicity, education level, marital status, poverty-income ratio), anthropometric measurements (body mass index [BMI], waist-to-height ratio [WHtR], systolic blood pressure [SBP], diastolic blood pressure [DBP]), lifestyle factors (physical activity, smoking and alcohol status), biomarkers (hemoglobin A1c [HbA1c], triglycerides, gamma-glutamyl transferase [GGT], fasting glucose, high-density lipoprotein [HDL], alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST]), and composite indices (systemic inflammation response index [SIRI], systemic immune-inflammation index [SII], triglyceride-glucose index [TyG], TyG-WHtR, cardiometabolic index [CMI], homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance [HOMA-IR], quantitative insulin sensitivity check index [QUICKI]).

Target prediction and pathway enrichment

Two-dimensional (2D) chemical structures of pro-oxidant and antioxidant compounds were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Potential protein targets of these compounds were predicted using the molecular docking-based online tool PharmMapper (https://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/). Meanwhile, disease-related targets associated with MASLD and cardiovascular disease (CVD) were retrieved from the GeneCards databases (https://www.genecards.org/) using the keywords “Metabolic Dysfunction Associated Steatotic Liver Disease” and “cardiovascular disease”. Venn diagrams were then generated using the R package “VennDiagram” to identify overlapping targets. These common targets were submitted to the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network, which was visualized in Cytoscape. Hub genes were identified using the Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) algorithm via the CytoHubba plugin. Finally, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the common targets using ClusterProfiler [25], with a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value <0.05 set as the significance threshold.

Pre-processing of machine learning features

To address missing data, we performed multiple imputation on the filtered dataset using the “miceforest” package, a random forest-based approach, which generated five complete datasets with pooled results after 10 iterations and a convergence threshold of 0.01. To mitigate multicollinearity among dietary antioxidant features, variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated, and variables with VIF exceeding 8 were excluded to enhance model stability. To alleviate class imbalance between comorbid and non-comorbid groups, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was implemented via the imbalanced-learn library, oversampling the minority class with k = 5 nearest neighbours and an automated sampling strategy. SMOTE was selected over undersampling to preserve the information density of the majority class, which is vital for identifying complex nutritional patterns. To ensure the validity of our interpretability analysis, all SHAP values and model performance metrics reported in the Results were calculated using the original test set, ensuring that the synthetic samples were used only for model training and not for final evaluation or interpretation.

To prevent model overfitting and ensure robustness, hyperparameter optimization for the LightGBM model was conducted using five-repeated stratified 10-fold cross-validation on the training set. Furthermore, we monitored the gap between training and testing performance to ensure consistent discriminative power.

Statistical analysis

Participants were stratified into four groups by OBS quartiles: Q1 (OBS <13), Q2 (13 ≤ OBS <21), Q3 (21 ≤ OBS <25), and Q4 (OBS ≥25), and categorized into two groups based on comorbidity status (comorbid MASLD-CVD and non-comorbid MASLD-CVD) to describe baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as percentages. The Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test was employed for continuous variables, and chi-square test was used to calculate p-values for categorical variables. Survival analysis was performed using log-rank test, and outcomes were visualized through Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival curves.

Logistic regression was used to evaluate associations between OBS and three clinical conditions: MASLD, CVD, and MASLD-CVD comorbidity. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate all-cause mortality risks across OBS quartiles in these patient groups. All analyses adjusted for sex, age, ethnicity, education level, marital status, poverty-income ratio, physical activity, smoking and alcohol status. OBS was categorized into quartiles (Q1-Q4), with Q4 as the reference. Nonlinear relationships were assessed using restricted cubic splines (3 knots), and model adequacy was evaluated via likelihood ratio tests. Threshold effects were analyzed with piecewise regression models, where inflection points were identified by maximum likelihood estimation. The segmented regression models were compared with linear models to assess goodness-of-fit.

To further enhance the analysis, ten ML models were used: bootstrap aggregating (Bagging), decision tree (DT), K-nearest neighbours (KNN), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), logistic regression (LR), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), multilayer perceptron (MLP), support vector machine (SVM), and naive bayes (NB), all implemented via the mlr3 platform. The dataset was partitioned into training (70%) and test (30%) subsets through stratified random sampling. Model hyperparameters were tuned through five-repeated stratified cross-validation with grid search across 10 parameter combinations. Performance evaluation included receiver operating characteristic area (AUC-ROC), precision-recall area (AUC-PR), sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, and G-mean. The best-performing model was selected based on these metrics, then we calculated SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values to interpret and quantify the importance of OBS components in the best model. SHAP values reflect the contribution of each feature to the model's predictions and represent statistical associations; they do not imply causation.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.1) and Python (version 3.9.12) with the following critical packages: survival, survey, segmented, miceforest, mlr3, xgboost, lightgbm, baguette, randomForest, and shapviz. Two-tailed P-value <0.05 was regarded statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed multiple sensitivity analyses. Although the AUROC of the USFLI was 0.80, indicating moderate accuracy, its positive predictive value was lower in non-Hispanic Black individuals [21,26]. We therefore also evaluated the hepatic steatosis index (HSI) [27]. Consistent with previous literature [27], a threshold of HSI >36 was applied, which achieved an AUROC of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.80–0.82), with a specificity of 92.4% and a positive likelihood ratio of 6.069. Additionally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding non-Hispanic Black participants. Finally, we performed inverse probability treatment weighting (IPTW) to account for potential confounding factors.

Simplified risk model and nomogram

Based on the SHAP-identified key predictors (age, HOMA-IR, cotinine, and alcohol consumption), we constructed a simplified logistic regression model to predict MASLD-CVD comorbidity. A nomogram was developed using the rms package in R to enable manual calculation of total points corresponding to predicted risk. The predictive performance of this simplified model was compared with that of the full OBS model (using the total OBS score as the sole predictor) by area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and DeLong's test. Calibration was assessed using a calibration plot with 10 deciles. To translate the previously identified mortality threshold (OBS = 21) from the full model into the simplified framework, we calculated the total points in the nomogram that yielded the same predicted comorbidity risk as an OBS of 21.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

A total of 19,993 eligible participants (Fig. 1) were included in this study, comprising 9,922 men (50%) and 10,071 women (50%) with a mean age of 49.98 years (SD 17.76), including 1,286 MASLD-CVD comorbidity patients, 6,722 MASLD-only patients (without CVD), 934 CVD-only patients (without MASLD), and 10,951 controls (without MASLD or CVD), as detailed in Table 1. Participants in higher OBS quartiles exhibited higher educational levels, higher poverty-income ratios, and greater engagement in vigorous physical activity, and lower BMI and WHtR measurements and lower rates of smoking and alcohol consumption (Table 1). Compared to participants with monomorbidity, individuals with MASLD-CVD comorbidity were older (mean [SD] age: 66.14 [11.84] vs. 53.39 [16.66] years), more likely to be male (62% vs. 55%), with lower educational levels, more less engagement in vigorous physical activity (7.9% vs. 16%), and higher BMI (32.72 vs. 32.33) and WHtR (0.67 vs. 0.65) (Table S2).

Association between OBS and prevalence in MASLD, CVD, and comorbid cohorts

To further explore the association between OBS level and disease status, we performed multinomial logistic regression analyses. Compare to healthy group, OBS as a continuous variable showed significant associations with the prevalence of these conditions (all P < 0.001). For each unit increase in OBS corresponded to a 2% lower odds of MASLD-CVD comorbidity (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.98, P < 0.001), 1% reduced odds of MASLD-only (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.99–1.00, P < 0.001), and 2% decreased odds of CVD-only (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99, P < 0.001). When analyzed by OBS quartiles with the highest quartile (Q4) as reference, the lowest quartile (Q1) exhibited a 2.15-fold higher prevalence odds of MASLD-CVD comorbidity (95% CI: 1.79–2.60, P < 0.001), while for MASLD-only and CVD-only, Q1 showed 31% (OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.20–1.43, P < 0.001) and 95% (OR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.60–2.39, P < 0.001) increased prevalence odds respectively, with notably higher odds observed in Q2 for comorbidity (OR = 2.35, 95% CI: 1.95–2.83, P < 0.001) and MASLD-only (OR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.34–1.60, P < 0.001), while CVD-only demonstrated its peak odds in Q1 followed by declining odds through Q3 (Q2 OR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.35–2.05, P < 0.001; Q3 OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.12–1.72, P = 0.003). MASLD-CVD comorbidity showed uniformly higher odds versus MASLD-only across OBS quartiles, while compared to CVD-only, it demonstrated no excess odds at Q1 (OR = 1.07, P = 0.6) but 47% higher risk at Q2 (OR = 1.47, P = 0.002). Association between OBS and all-cause mortality in MASLD, CVD, and comorbid cohorts

During the 1999-2018 follow-up, the MASLD-CVD comorbidity and CVD-only cohort exhibited comparable all-cause mortality rates [54.2 (95% CI: 49.6–59.1) vs. 54.0 (48.6–59.8) per 1,000 person-years], whereas the MASLD-only cohort demonstrated a significantly lower all-cause mortality rate [14.5 (13.6–15.5)] (Fig. S1). Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis revealed all-cause mortality was significantly higher in comorbidity participants with low quartile levels (Q1-Q3) of OBS when compared with high quartile cohort (Q4) (log-rank P = 0.0036) (Fig. S2a). Consistently, MASLD-only participants with higher OBS levels presented improved survival probabilities (Fig. S2b). However, among CVD-only patients, the difference in mortality risk across OBS quartiles did not reach statistical significance (log-rank P = 0.071) (Fig. S2c).

The Cox regression models showed that higher OBS demonstrated protective effects across all cohorts. Each unit increase in OBS was associated with a 2% lower risk of all-cause mortality in the MASLD-CVD comorbidity cohort (HR 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99, P = 0.002), a 2% lower risk in the MASLD-only cohort (HR 0.98, 0.97–0.99, P < 0.0001), and a 1% lower risk in the CVD-only cohort (HR 0.99, 0.97–1.00, P = 0.003). When stratified by OBS quartiles, patients with MASLD-CVD comorbidity in the lowest OBS quartile (Q1) had a 31% higher mortality risk compared to Q4 (HR 1.31, 95% CI: 1.01–2.69, P = 0.04), with stronger associations observed in the MASLD-only cohort (HR 1.66, 95% CI: 1.36–2.02, P < 0.0001) and a 26% higher risk in the CVD-only cohort (HR 1.26, 95% CI: 1.06–1.50, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3).

Nonlinear OBS-mortality relationships and critical thresholds

We further performed restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses based on Cox regression models, revealing nonlinear relationships between OBS and all-cause mortality in the MASLD-only cohort (Fig. 4b). In contrast, in MASLD-CVD comorbidity and CVD-only cohorts, OBS showed a linear negative correlation with all-cause mortality (Fig. 4a and c). Recursive partitioning analysis identified statistically significant breakpoint (breakpoint = 8.73) for OBS in relation to survival among MASLD patients. When OBS was less than 8.73, it was positively associated with mortality risk (HR = 1.089, 95% CI: 1.040–1.139, P < 0.001), and above which it exhibited a negative association (HR = 0.964, 95% CI: 0.953–0.975, P < 0.001) (Table 2). To better characterize the population below this threshold, we compared clinical and laboratory parameters between MASLD-only patients with OBS below 8.73 (n = 1,174) and those with OBS at or above this cutoff (n = 5,548). As summarized in Supplementary Table S3, patients in the low-OBS subgroup exhibited higher HOMA-IR (7.07 vs. 6.67, p = 0.027) and TyG-BMI (294.9 vs. 299.2, p = 0.022), and showed higher levels of cotinine and alcohol consumption (Table S3).

Optimal OBS cut-off value and survival analysis in MASLD-CVD comorbidity cohort

In the MASLD-CVD comorbidity cohort, the optimal cut-off value for OBS was determined to be 21 using the maximal selected rank statistics method (Fig. S3). Patients were stratified based on this threshold into groups with OBS <21 (n = 1003) or ≥ 21 (n = 263). The efficacy of this classification was validated by KM analysis, which showed strikingly different survival curves (log-rank P = 0.0018; Fig. S4), affirming a significantly decreased mortality risk in the high-OBS group.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the primary associations. The inverse relationships between OBS and the prevalence of MASLD-CVD comorbidity, MASLD-only, and CVD-only remained statistically significant (all P < 0.05) when using the HSI for MASLD definition, after excluding Non-Hispanic Black participants, and following inverse probability weighting adjustment. The effect estimates were consistent in direction and magnitude across these analyses, supporting the stability of the observed associations (Table 3, Table 4).

Machine learning analysis

Variance inflation factor (VIF) values were calculated for all variables, including OBS components and clinical indicators (Fig. S5). After eliminating multicollinear variables (filtered at VIF >8), the remaining 31 features were incorporated into 10 ML models. Through a combination of 10-fold cross-validation and manual hyperparameter tuning, we identified optimal parameter configurations for predicting the presence of MASLD-CVD comorbidity status, and the risk of all-cause mortality among MASLD-CVD comorbidity patients (Table S4 and Table S5). For predicting MASLD-CVD comorbidity status, LightGBM demonstrated optimal discriminative performance, achieving the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC = 0.950) and precision-recall area under the curve (PR-AUC = 0.938) (Fig. S6 and Fig. S7). As detailed in Table S6, this model also exhibited excellent overall performance with accuracy of 0.891, specificity of 0.936, F1-score of 0.848, and geometric mean (G-mean) of 0.874. Similarly, for all-cause mortality risk prediction in MASLD-CVD comorbidity cohort, LightGBM also achieved the optimal algorithm, obtaining the highest discriminative accuracy (AUC = 0.941, PR-AUC = 0.922) and outperforming comparative models across all evaluated metrics including accuracy (0.870), specificity (0.914), F1-score (0.822), and G-mean (0.854).(Fig. S6, Fig. S7, and Table S7).

Importance of clinical indicators and OBS components interpreted by SHAP value

SHAP value analysis was performed to quantify feature importance in the LightGBM model testing set. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the importance predictors were ranked by their relative contribution magnitude. Regarding the classification of MASLD-CVD comorbidity, the clinical indicators age (mean |SHAP| = 1.69), HOMA-IR (1.04), cotinine (0.76), and alcohol consumption (0.70) were identified as the strongest predictors for the presence of comorbidity (Fig. 5a and b). Interestingly, an increased level of niacin was also associated with a higher odds of comorbidity, suggesting its role may be complex and potentially dose-dependent. In contrast, several antioxidant components dietary fiber (0.40), magnesium (0.38), vitamin B6 (0.37), showed negative contributions, indicating protective effects against the prevalence of comorbidity. For all-cause mortality risk prediction in the MASLD-CVD comorbidity cohort, age (1.93), cotinine (1.41), and HOMA-IR (0.57) were also identified as the top risk-increasing predictors (Fig. 5c and d). Higher alcohol intake was also positively associated with increased mortality risk. In contrast, antioxidant components such as vitamin E (0.51), magnesium (0.46), and carotene (0.45) demonstrated strong protective effects against mortality. Additionally, waterfall and force plots were used to visualize feature-level contributions for individual patient predictions. As shown in Fig. S8, one representative patient with MASLD-CVD comorbidity had a predicted model output of f(x) = −1.38, which is significantly higher than the baseline expected value of E[f(x)] = −2.69, indicating an elevated all-cause mortality risk compared to the population average.

Using the four SHAP-identified predictors (age, HOMA-IR, cotinine, and alcohol consumption), we constructed a simplified logistic regression model for predicting MASLD-CVD comorbidity (Fig. S9). The simplified model achieved an AUC of 0.843 (95% CI 0.825–0.861), while the full OBS model (using the total OBS score) achieved an AUC of 0.872 (95% CI 0.855–0.889). The DeLong test showed no statistically significant difference between the two models (P = 0.13), indicating comparable discriminative performance (Table S8). To enable clinical use of the previously identified mortality threshold (OBS = 21) from the full OBS model, we calculated the total points in the simplified nomogram that correspond to the same predicted comorbidity risk. This equivalent total points was approximately 42.7. Using this cut-off, the simplified risk classification (total points >42.7 vs. ≤42.7) showed substantial agreement with the full OBS model's classification (OBS <21 vs. ≥21) (κ = 0.76, 95% CI 0.72–0.80; Table S8). Thus, a clinician using the nomogram can consider a total point score >42.7 as equivalent to a full OBS <21 (higher mortality risk).

Information on mechanisms and targets

To explore the potential molecular mechanisms through which pro-oxidant exposure is associated with MASLD-CVD comorbidity, we predicted protein targets for the two key pro-oxidants identified in our SHAP analysis: cotinine and alcohol. This yielded 236 potential targets. Intersecting these with 2,186 known MASLD/CVD-related genes resulted in 99 shared targets (Fig. 6a). PPI network analysis constructed using the STRING database revealed that the common targets form a highly interconnected network. The top 10 hub genes, ranked by the MCC algorithm, were TNF, STAT3, IL1B, NFKB1, CXCL8, TLR4, CCL2, STAT1, TLR2, and AKT1 (Fig. 6b and 6c). KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in the IL-17 signaling pathway, PI3K–Akt signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, cellular senescence, and insulin resistance pathway (Fig. 6d). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis further highlighted roles in inflammatory response and oxidative stress response (Fig. 6e). TNF, CXCL8, CCL2, NFKB1 and IL1B are significantly enriched in the IL-17 signaling pathway, which is primarily associated with oxidative stress-induced inflammation. TNF, IL1B, STAT3, and AKT1 are significantly enriched in the insulin resistance pathway, linking them to metabolic stress. Notably, TNF and IL1B serves as a molecular bridge between these two pathways, functioning as a core component of IL-17 signaling in the context of oxidative stress while also acting as a well-established inhibitor of insulin signaling under metabolic stress. These results indicate that therapeutic strategies targeting these molecules could help modulate the biological pathways associated with pro-oxidant exposure.

Discussion

This study, based on a cohort of nearly 20,000 nationally representative U.S. adults, demonstrates that higher OBS are significantly associated with a lower prevalence of MASLD, CVD, and their comorbidity, as well as lower all-cause mortality risk. Notably, in individuals with MASLD-CVD comorbidity, an OBS threshold of 21 emerged as the optimal cutoff for stratifying mortality risk, with those above this level exhibiting significantly higher survival rates. Among MASLD patients, an OBS of 8.73 marked a critical inflection point for mortality risk. These findings provide actionable quantitative benchmarks for clinical risk stratification and underscore the potential of lifestyle modifications, particularly dietary antioxidant optimization, to mitigate the burden of cardiometabolic comorbidities.

The observed associations are supported by well-established biological mechanisms. Oxidative stress is characterized by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), which directly damage cellular components in hepatocytes and vascular endothelial cells while activating inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB [28]. This activation is associated with the release of cytokines like tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which further exacerbate oxidative stress, forming a self-perpetuating cycle [29]. In the liver, this process accelerates steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis [30]; in vascular tissues, it exacerbates endothelial dysfunction, foam cell formation, and atherosclerotic plaque progression [31,32]. As an integrative marker of dietary and lifestyle factors influencing redox balance, OBS effectively captures individual oxidative burden and has been associated with chronic metabolic disease prevalence in prior studies.

Previous investigations across diverse metabolic disorders have consistently demonstrated an inverse relationship between OBS and mortality. Specifically, lower OBS values correlated with elevated all-cause mortality risk in CKD patients [33], while Xu et al. confirmed a similar inverse association in metabolic syndrome populations [34]. Additional studies in diabetic and prediabetic cohorts further corroborated this inverse relationship [35]. These findings collectively suggest that OBS may serve as a useful risk stratification tool across metabolic diseases. However, the application of this marker to MASLD-CVD comorbidity populations has not been sufficiently investigated, and our study provides the first evidence of its clinical utility in this context.

To further elucidate the mechanistic relationships between individual components of the OBS and MASLD-CVD comorbidity, this study combined machine learning with SHAP-based interpretability analysis. The SHAP results confirmed the roles of established risk factors, including advanced age [36], elevated HOMA-IR [37], alcohol consumption [38], and cotinine exposure [39,40]. While the full 20-component OBS remains the most rigorous scientific approach to assessing systemic oxidative balance, our machine learning analysis identified these four parameters as the predominant determinants of this oxidative equilibrium. Consequently, rather than requiring the exhaustive bedside calculation of the complete OBS, clinicians can utilize these readily available indicators as a practical screening framework to efficiently identify individuals at high risk for reduced antioxidant capacity (OBS <21). In addition, the analysis revealed more detailed effects of specific nutrients. While higher intakes of dietary fiber and magnesium were associated with lower comorbidity prevalence, higher niacin levels were uniquely linked to increased mortality risk, a finding that appears to diverge from its traditional classification as an antioxidant in the OBS framework. This 'niacin paradox' may be explained by the dose-dependent or U-shaped effects of certain micronutrients. While niacin is a precursor to NAD+ and supports antioxidant defenses at physiological levels, excessive intake has been increasingly linked to adverse cardiometabolic outcomes. Recent evidence indicates that excess niacin is metabolized into 4PY, which promotes vascular inflammation by activating endothelial cells [41]. This suggests that the protective threshold for niacin may be narrower than previously assumed, and its inclusion as a strictly 'antioxidant' factor in general populations warrants caution. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of maintaining oxidative homeostasis and achieving an appropriate balance in nutrient intake among individuals at high risk.

In this study, LightGBM demonstrated high predictive accuracy [[42], [43], [44], [45]], attributable to several key advantages. First, the histogram-based splitting strategy used by LightGBM effectively handled the sparsity and irregular distribution characteristic of nutritional exposure data, which is particularly relevant for zero-inflated variables such as antioxidant and pro-oxidant intake, while maintaining computational efficiency and informational integrity. Second, although preliminary sample balancing was performed, the gradient-based instance sampling employed by LightGBM enhanced the detection of subtle yet clinically significant risk patterns, such as dose-dependent antioxidant effects. These characteristics make LightGBM especially suitable for modeling complex, multifactorial diseases like MASLD-CVD comorbidity.

In our SHAP analysis, cotinine and alcohol were identified as the primary pro-oxidant factors contributing to the presence of MASLD-CVD comorbidity, highlighting the central role of lifestyle-related oxidative stress. Bioinformatics studies further suggest that these exposure factors may converge on a core network of inflammatory and metabolic pathways, with TNF and IL1B emerging as critical integrators.The observation that TNF and IL1B are simultaneously enriched in both IL-17 signaling and insulin resistance pathways is particularly noteworthy. Furthermore, the TNF and IL1B were independently associated with the severity of liver fibrosis and coronary plaque instability, respectively, highlighting their dual roles across organ systems [46,47]. Together, these findings indicate that TNF and IL1B act as downstream effectors that link pro-oxidant exposure to IL-17 signaling and insulin resistance along the liver-heart axis, thereby providing a mechanistic framework for understanding how lifestyle-related oxidative stress contributes to the comorbidity of MASLD and CVD. IL-17-producing T cells, particularly Th17 and Tc17 subsets, contribute to the inflammatory processes in liver disease and CVD by recruiting and activating neutrophils, thereby sustaining chronic inflammation [[48], [49], [50]]. This pathway may reveal a previously underappreciated immunometabolic link between MASLD and CVD, particularly in the context of pro-oxidative stress exposures. Furthermore, the concurrent enrichment of insulin resistance pathways provides a mechanistic bridge for our epidemiological findings. HOMA-IR emerged as one of the strongest predictors in our model, consistent with decades of clinical research establishing insulin resistance as a foundational element in both diseases [[51], [52], [53]]. Our data indicate that alcohol and cotinine are associated with the progression of MASLD-CVD comorbidity, potentially through inflammation and insulin signaling defects.

For MASLD patients with OBS below 8.73 (17.5% of the cohort), the data indicate a pro-oxidant, insulin-resistant phenotype. From a clinical perspective, these individuals should be prioritised for lifestyle interventions targeting oxidative stress, including smoking cessation, alcohol reduction, and increased intake of antioxidant-rich nutrients. Annual monitoring of cardiometabolic parameters is also reasonable. This threshold offers a simple evidence-based trigger for risk management, though prospective validation is needed. Notably, a critical methodological consideration in this study involves the reliance on the USFLI for defining MASLD. Consistent with previous literature, the USFLI may exhibit lower positive predictive value in non-Hispanic Black individuals compared to other ethnic groups [54,55]. Because ethnicity was a significant covariate in our models, any misclassification of MASLD status within this subgroup could bias the association between OBS and MASLD–CVD comorbidity. However, results from a sensitivity analysis excluding this subgroup remained consistent (all P < 0.05), supporting the robustness of the OBS as a reliable risk stratification tool across major ethnic groups.

Limitations

However, this study has several limitations that must be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the assessment of temporality and prohibits causal inference. Despite multivariate adjustments, residual confounding from unmeasured factors and the potential for reverse causation, such as prevalent disease influencing lifestyle habits, remain inherent limitations of the observational approach. Second, the reliance on self-reported data for dietary and lifestyle exposures introduces the possibility of recall bias and social desirability bias. The absence of biomarker calibration for nutritional components suggests that misclassification of true exposure is likely, which would tend to attenuate observed associations toward the null. Third, although machine learning models demonstrated strong predictive performance in internal validation, their generalizability requires confirmation in external cohorts. Specifically, the high discriminative power (AUC = 0.950) observed in our study might be partially influenced by the specific demographic characteristics of the NHANES population, and the lack of external validation remains a primary constraint. To mitigate the risk of overfitting, we employed rigorous repeated stratified cross-validation and monitored the consistency between training and testing metrics. However, these internal measures cannot fully substitute for validation in independent datasets. Finally, the clinical utility of these models is further constrained by the inclusion of predictors that require laboratory testing not routinely available in primary care settings, such as HOMA-IR. Future studies should examine whether longitudinal updating of the Oxidative Balance Score improves prediction of disease incidence and mortality and should prioritize external validation in populations that differ geographically and culturally. Furthermore, the potential for selection bias must be acknowledged as the exclusion of certain MASLD participants was necessitated by the absence of one or more critical clinical parameters including BMI, blood glucose, blood pressure, fasting plasma triglycerides, or high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. To assess the impact of these missing data, a comparative analysis of baseline characteristics was performed between the included and excluded cohorts as detailed in Table S9. This evaluation revealed significant disparities in several demographic and physiological markers such as age, ethnicity, educational attainment, and blood pressure. Consequently, these differences may influence the generalizability of the findings and should be considered when drawing population-level inferences from the results. Despite these limitations, our findings reveal a novel and significant association between the Oxidative Balance Score and cardiometabolic conditions, including MASLD alone, cardiovascular disease alone, and notably, their comorbidity, thereby laying the groundwork for prognostic model development and informing future mechanistic research.

Conclusion

This study investigated the association between the OBS and MASLD-CVD comorbidity by integrating traditional epidemiological approaches with machine learning techniques. Based on internal validation, the LightGBM model showed favorable predictive performance for identifying this comorbidity, though external validation is needed before clinical application. SHAP-based interpretability analysis elucidated the direction and magnitude of contributions from individual pro-oxidant and antioxidant components within the OBS to comorbidity prevalence. Our findings provide preliminary evidence that OBS may serve as a useful tool for identifying individuals at elevated risk, while the integrated network pharmacology and bioinformatics analysis revealed that key pro-oxidant factors may be associated with comorbidity through inflammatory pathways and insulin resistance, offering potential mechanistic insights that warrant further investigation.
