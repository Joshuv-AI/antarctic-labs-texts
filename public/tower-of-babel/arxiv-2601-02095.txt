Metric Distortion with Preference Intensities
Mehrad Abbaszadeh, Ali Ansarifar, Mohamad Latifian, Masoud Seddighin

Abstract
In voting with ranked ballots, each agent submits a strict ranking of the form a ≻ b ≻ c ≻ d a\succ b\succ c\succ d over the alternatives, and the voting rule decides on the winner based on these rankings. Although this ballot format has desirable characteristics, there is a question of whether it is expressive enough for the agents. 26 address this issue by adding intensities to the rankings. They introduce the ranking with intensities ballot format, where agents can use both ‘ ≻ ⁣ ≻ ’ \mlq\!{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\!\mrq and ‘ ≻ ’ \mlq\!\succ\!\mrq in their rankings to express intensive and normal preferences between consecutive alternatives in their rankings. While they focus on analyzing this ballot format in the utilitarian distortion framework, in this work, we look at the potential of using this ballot format from the metric distortion viewpoint. We design a class of voting rules coined Positional Scoring Matching rules, which can be used for different problems in the metric setting, and show that by solving a zero-sum game, we can find the optimal member of this class for our problem. This rule takes intensities into account and achieves a distortion lower than 3 3 . In addition, by proving a bound on the price of ignoring intensities, we show that we might lose a great deal in terms of distortion by not taking the intensities into account.



1
Introduction

The field of computational social choice focuses on aggregating the individual opinions of multiple agents to arrive at a collective decision that affects all of them. This process typically involves two phases: the
elicitation
of the agents’ preferences and the
aggregation
of these preferences to determine the final output. One key element of the elicitation phase is the
ballot
format that is used to capture the agents’ preferences. The ballot design is crucial, as it dictates how agents report their preferences, and this inherently constrains the collected information, which, in turn, can influence the voting outcome and potentially lead to decisions that do not accurately reflect agents’ true preferences. For example, ranked ballots and approvals are two of the most famous ballot formats that are typically used in voting mechanisms. Although these ballot formats are intuitive and capture varying details about agents’ desired outcomes, it remains unclear which format is most appropriate. In different collective decision-making settings, agents may be able to provide varying types of information, and alternative elicitation methods may be more suitable. Thus, the choice of ballot format remains a context-dependent question, and it is essential to evaluate whether standard formats accurately express agents’ preferences.

In this work, we focus on single-winner voting and a practical modification of ranked ballots that captures more detail about agents’ preferences. In standard ranked ballots, each agent submits a complete ordering of the alternatives, and the aggregation rule selects a winner based on these rankings. However, in many real-world settings, agents not only rank alternatives but also differ in how strongly they prefer them. They may strongly favor some options, feel indifferent about others, or actively oppose certain choices. Simple ordinal rankings obscure these distinctions by treating all adjacent positions as equally spaced. For example, in internship selection at a tech firm, team leaders often classify candidates into broad tiers like “top-tier”, “acceptable”, and “poor fit”. These categories reflect preference gaps that simple rankings fail to capture.
Ignoring such information can lead to undesirable outcomes. Similarly, in R&D evaluations, committee members often use rating scales (e.g., 0–100) to rank proposals. While these scores convey richer information than simple rankings, eliciting precise numerical values is often impractical.
In response, several methods have been proposed to capture and elicit the intensity of preferences
[
18
,
25
,
34
,
19
]
.

A natural and implementable idea is to augment ranked ballots with a notion of
preference intensities
, allowing voters to express not just order but strength of preference. While a traditional ranked ballot requires a strict ranking of alternatives, it cannot differentiate between slight and strong preferences. Consider a group choosing between options of what to eat, with their options being Pizza (
p
), Steak (
s
), or Burger (
b
). Alice may submit
p\succ s\succ b
reflecting a mild preference for Pizza. Bob, a vegetarian, might submit the same ranking even though Pizza is his only viable option, and his preference is far stronger. This lack of distinction in preference intensities can lead to sub-optimal outcomes. To address this limitation,
26
introduced the
ranking with preference intensities
ballot format, which extends traditional rankings by permitting agents to use
\mlq\!{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\!\mrq
or
\mlq\!\succ\!\mrq
to indicate stronger or weaker preferences. For example, Bob could express his preferences more accurately by submitting
p\;{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}b\succ s
, and a voting rule can then leverage this additional information to make a more efficient decision.

There are various approaches in the literature to compare voting rules. Some studies compare them based on axiomatic properties, but evaluating decision efficiency requires a quantitative measure.
One approach that has recently received significant attention is the notion of
distortion
, introduced by
33
.
Distortion measures the efficiency loss in a voting process by comparing the outcome based on reported preferences to the ideal outcome under full information.
Distortion has been studied in two main frameworks: utilitarian and metric.

In the utilitarian framework, introduced by
33
, it is assumed that each agent has a subjective unit sum utility function over the alternatives and reports her ranking accordingly. The goal is to use the reported information to select an alternative that maximizes the social welfare. In this context, distortion of a rule is defined as the worst-case ratio between the maximum achievable social welfare and the social welfare of the alternative chosen by the rule.
26
analyze the utilitarian distortion of some rules that use ranking with preference intensities ballot format, and show how ignoring the intensities can affect the utilitarian distortion by introducing the notion of
price of ignoring intensities
.

The metric distortion framework, first studied by
4
, assumes that agents and alternatives are embedded in a metric space, and agents submit preferences based on proximity (closer alternatives are preferred). One motivation for this is the facility location problem
[
7
,
20
]
, where the goal is to select facility locations to minimize total distance or cost of serving clients. In this context, distortion is defined as the worst-case ratio of the social cost of the chosen alternative to the minimum possible social cost.
4
proved a lower bound of 3 on the metric distortion of any deterministic voting rule. They also proved that the metric distortion of the Copeland rule is 5. Eventually,
22
closed the gap by introducing the
Plurality Matching
rule, which achieves a distortion of 3.

Both metric and utilitarian distortion have been studied for various ballot formats. We refer to the survey of
5
for an extensive overview of these results. In this work, we study the limits of metric distortion achievable with the ranking-with-preference-intensities ballot format and compare them to those of rules using simple ranked ballots.
We draw a complete picture by analyzing distortion in the general case, in the line metric, and by establishing bounds on the price of ignoring intensities.

1.2
Our contribution

In this work, we assume that agents and alternatives are embedded in a metric space with distance function
d
, and we use a parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
to model the intensity of preference. Specifically, an agent strongly prefers alternative
a
over
b
, denoted
a{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}b
, if their distance to
a
is less than
\alpha
times their distance to
b
. We analyze our methods as a function of
\alpha
.

Metric distortion with preference intensities can be analyzed in two settings:
mandatory
and
voluntary
elicitation of the intensities. In the former, agents use
\mlq\!{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\!\mrq
if and only if there is a gap in their distances, while in the latter, agents might still use
\mlq\!\succ\!\mrq
when there is a gap in their distances.

It is evident that the optimal distortion in the voluntary setting is 3 (matching the bound on the distortion with ranked ballot), since agents might decide not to report any intensities. However, the distortion bound in the mandatory setting is unknown. We start by proving lower bounds on the metric distortion. Our main technical contribution is introducing a deterministic rule and analyzing its distortion in this setting.
We start by analyzing the class of
moderate-up-to-
k
preferences, which is the class of preference profiles in which each agent reports his first intense preference in the
k\textsuperscript{\text{th}}
position. To this end, we generalize the Plurality Matching rule to the class of rules coined
Positional Scoring Matching rules
. We analyze the distortion of members of this class and prove that finding the optimal rule among them is equivalent to finding the value of a two-player zero-sum game. We believe that this class of rules can be useful in different contexts in the metric setting and also in the other settings, and the framework to analyze them might be of interest for future work.

The next step is to use this analysis to design a rule for the general case, with the distortion depending on
\ell_{\max}
, the maximum
\ell\in[m-1]
for which all the agents are moderate-up-to-
\ell
. We prove that the distortion of this rule is much less than
3
when
\ell_{\max}
is small
(see
Figures

3
and
3
). We further show that this bound is robust by proving that if a small portion of the agents are not
moderate-up-to-
k
for any
k\leqslant\ell_{\max}
, our bound does not change drastically.

To give more meaning to our results, it is worth mentioning that there are reasons to believe that in real world elections most of the agents are moderate-up-to-
k
for a small
k
: agents tend to partition the alternatives into the small set of desirable outcomes and the large set of undesirable ones. Approval voting is motivated by this principle. Moreover, agents allocate more cognitive and emotional resources to learn more about their high-ranked options, where the stakes are perceived to be higher, and hence they usually have a strong preference for their top few options over the rest.

In
Section

4
, we consider the special case where our metric is a line. We establish a lower bound of
\max\left(\frac{3-\alpha}{1+\alpha},2\alpha+1\right)
on the distortion in the mandatory setting for instances with two alternatives. We also propose a rule for two-alternative cases, which we conjecture achieves a similar distortion to this lower bound. Furthermore,
we provide another lower bound,
\frac{1+3\alpha^{-\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}}{3+\alpha^{-\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}}
, showing that distortion in the line metric can still approach values close to 3, similar to the unrestricted case.

In
Section

5
, we study the
Price of Ignoring Intensities (POII)
which measures the efficiency loss that accrues if simple ranked ballots are used when intensities are available. We show that in the mandatory setting POII is at least

\frac{3\left(\alpha^{\lfloor m\rfloor_{\text{even}}}+1\right)}{1+2\alpha^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}-\alpha^{\lfloor m\rfloor_{\text{even}}}}\cdot

In this section, we also prove the lower bound
\frac{3}{2\alpha^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}+1}
on POII in the voluntary setting.
To prove these bounds, we carefully analyze different possible outcomes on a specific instance. This requires formulating our problem as an LP and performing primal-dual analysis. We modify the techniques introduced by
27
to fit our setting.

Preliminaries

For
t\in\mathbb{N}
, let
[t]=\{1,\ldots,t\}
, and for set
S
, let
\Delta(S)
be the set of distributions over
S
. An election
\mathcal{E}=(N,A,\vec{\sigma})
involves a set of
n
agents
N
, a set of
m
alternatives
A
, and an elicited preference profile
\vec{\sigma}
. We use
i,j
to refer to agents and
a,b,c
to refer to alternatives.

Preference Elicitation.

In this work, we focus on preference profiles elicited through a ranking with intensities ballot format. In this ballot format, each agent
i
submits a preference
\sigma_{i}=(\pi_{i},\Join_{i})
, where
\pi_{i}:[m]\to A
is a one-to-one function representing the agent’s ranking of the alternatives, and
\Join_{i}:[m-1]\to\{\succ,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\}
indicates the intensity of preferences. Specifically,
\Join_{i}(j)=\mlq\!\succ\!\mrq
means agent
i
prefers
\pi_{i}(j)
over
\pi_{i}(j+1)
, while
\Join_{i}(j)=\mlq\!{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\!\mrq
means agent
i
strongly prefers
\pi_{i}(j)
over
\pi_{i}(j+1)
.

Let
S(A)
denote the set of all possible rankings with intensities over
A
, and
\vec{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
represent the preference profile of all agents. Additionally, define
\mathsf{rank}_{i}(a)
as the rank of alternative
a
in the ranking of agent
i
, and
\mathsf{Plu}(a)
as the number of agents who rank
a
as their top alternative.

Voting Rule.

A single-winner voting rule
f
using the ranking with intensities ballot format is a function
f:S(A)^{n}\to A
that takes a preference profile as input and selects a winning alternative. We also consider voting rules that only access rankings. In this case, the ranking profile is defined as
\vec{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n})
, and the voting rule uses this profile to determine the winner.

Metric Framework.

We assume that all agents and alternatives are embedded in a metric space characterized by a distance function
d:(N\cup A)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
. The distance function is positive, symmetric, satisfies the triangle inequality, and for any
x\in A\cup N,d(x,x)=0
.
The social cost of alternative
a\in A
with respect to metric
d
is defined as
\mathsf{sc}_{d}(a):=\sum_{i\in N}d(i,a)
, and the optimal alternative is
\mathsf{opt}_{d}:=\argmin_{a\in A}\mathsf{sc}_{d}(a)
. If
d
is clear from the context, we drop it from the subscript. A ranking profile
\vec{\pi}
is said to be consistent with metric
d
, denoted by
\vec{\pi}\rhd d
, if for each agent
i\in N
and alternatives
a,b\in A
,
a\succ_{i}b
implies
d(i,a)\leqslant d(i,b)
. This means agents rank alternatives in increasing order of their distance from them.

Interpretation of the Intensities.

Intensities that are reported by the agents can be interpreted in two different ways:
mandatory elicitation
and
voluntary elicitation
. For a fixed
\alpha\in[0,1]
, a preference profile
\vec{\sigma}
with intensities is
\alpha
-consistent with the metric
d
under mandatory elicitation, denoted by
\vec{\sigma}\rhd_{\!\alpha}d
, if for any agent
i\in N
and
j\in[m-1]
, the following holds: if
\Join_{i}(j)=\mlq\!\succ\!\mrq
, then
d(i,\pi_{i}(j+1))\geqslant d(i,\pi_{i}(j))>\alpha\cdot d(i,\pi_{i}(j+1))
; and if
\Join_{i}(j)=\mlq\!{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\!\mrq
, then
d(i,\pi_{i}(j))\leqslant\alpha\cdot d(i,\pi_{i}(j+1))
. In other words, agent
i
expresses a strong preference in the
j\textsuperscript{\text{th}}
position
if and only if
there is an
\alpha
-gap between the distances to their
j\textsuperscript{\text{th}}
and
j+1\textsuperscript{\text{th}}
alternatives.

We primarily focus on the mandatory setting, but we also consider voluntary elicitation of the intensities in certain parts of this paper. An intensive preference profile
\vec{\sigma}
is said to be
\alpha
-consistent with the metric
d
under voluntary elicitation of the intensities, and it is denoted by
\vec{\sigma}\rhd^{\!v}_{\!\alpha}d
, if
\vec{\pi}\rhd d
and, for any agent
i\in N
and any
j\in[m-1]
, where
\Join_{i}(j)=\mlq\!{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\!\mrq
, it holds that
d(i,\pi_{i}(j))\leqslant\alpha\cdot d(i,\pi_{i}(j+1))
. In other words, if agent
i
expresses a strong preference in the
j\textsuperscript{\text{th}}
position, there is a multiplicative
\alpha
-gap between the distances to the alternatives ranked
j\textsuperscript{\text{th}}
and
j+1\textsuperscript{\text{th}}
. However, if no intensity is reported, the presence of a multiplicative
\alpha
-gap remains uncertain.

Metric Distortion.

Distortion serves as a quantitative measure of the efficiency of voting rules. For
\alpha\in[0,1]
, the
\alpha
-distortion of an alternative
a
in an election
\mathcal{E}=(N,A,\vec{\sigma})
is defined as the worst-case ratio between the social cost of
a
and the social cost of the optimal alternative, where the worst case is taken across all metrics consistent with
\vec{\sigma}
, i.e.,

\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(a,\mathcal{E}):=\sup_{d:\vec{\sigma}\rhd_{\!\alpha}d}\frac{\mathsf{sc}_{d}(a)}{\min_{b\in A}\mathsf{sc}_{d}(b)}\cdot

We sometimes use the notation
\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(a,\vec{\sigma})
whenever
N,A
are clear in the context.

The metric distortion of a rule
f
is then defined as the worst-case distortion of its output across all possible election instances, given by
\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(f):=\sup_{\mathcal{E}}\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(f(\vec{\sigma}),\mathcal{E})
. Note that this definition assumes mandatory elicitation of the intensities. A similar definition applies under voluntary elicitation, referred to as voluntary distortion (
\mathsf{dist}^{v}_{\alpha}
), which is formally defined in
Section

5
.

3
Bounds on the Distortion

In this section, we explore the potential to achieve a distortion lower than 3 under the assumption of mandatory elicitation of the intensities. In this context, when agents do not use the
\mlq\!{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\!\mrq
sign in their intensity reports, it indicates that the distances between consecutive alternatives in their rankings differ by no more than a factor of
\alpha
. This additional information, beyond what is provided by simple ranked ballots, offers the possibility of surpassing the distortion threshold of 3 using a deterministic rule.
We start the section by proving lower bounds on the distortion. Then we show an upper bound that depends on the location of the first
\mlq\!{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\!\mrq
in the preferences of the agents. Note that by distortion in this section, we mean metric distortion assuming mandatory elicitation of the intensities.

3.1
Lower Bound

We begin by establishing a lower bound on the distortion of deterministic rules using the ranked preferences with intensities ballot format, which is derived from two complementary bounds that together form the overall lower bound.

The metric distortion of any voting rule

using ranked preferences with intensities ballot format with parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]

1+2\max\left(\alpha,\frac{1-\alpha^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}}{1+\alpha^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}}\right)\cdot

Lemma

1
works better for smaller values of
\alpha
, while the bound from
Lemma

2
becomes tighter as
\alpha
approaches 1.

Lemma 1

The metric distortion of any voting rule
f
using ranked preferences with intensities ballot format is at least

1+2\frac{1-\alpha^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}}{1+\alpha^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}}\cdot

Proof.

First, note that when
\alpha=1
, the lower bound
1
is trivial by definition of distortion. Also, we will consider the case
\alpha=0
separately. Hence, we may assume that
0<\alpha<1
.

Consider an instance with two agents and
m

a_{1}\succ a_{2}\succ\cdots\succ a_{m}
, and the second agent submits her vote in the reverse order, i.e.,
a_{m}\succ a_{m-1}\succ\cdots\succ a_{1}
. Then, assume that the rule
f
selects alternative
a
, and without loss of generality,
a
appears in the first half of the ranking of the first agent. Now, consider the metric space
d
in which alternatives are at unit distance from agent
1

b\in A

d(2,b)=\alpha^{-\min(\mathsf{rank}_{2}(b)-1,\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor)}\cdot\frac{2}{\alpha^{-\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}-1}.

We claim that this distance function satisfies the triangle inequality and is consistent with
\vec{\sigma}
.
Regarding consistency, for the second agent,
\frac{1}{\alpha}d(2,\pi_{2}(i))=d(2,\pi_{2}(i+1))
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor
. Hence, it is straightforward to verify that
d
is consistent with
\sigma_{1}
.
Therefore, we only need to ensure that
d
satisfies the triangle inequality, which means that for every
b\neq c
and
i\neq j

d(i,b)\leqslant d(i,c)+d(j,c)+d(j,b).

(1)

Note that
d(i,c)+d(j,c)+d(j,b)\geqslant 1+1+\frac{2}{\alpha^{-\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}-1}=\alpha^{-\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}\cdot\frac{2}{\alpha^{-\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}-1}

satisfies the triangle inequality. Therefore
\vec{\sigma}\rhd_{\!\alpha}d
.

Now, we use this metric to give a bound on the distortion, we have:

\displaystyle\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(a,\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\mathsf{sc}(a)}{\mathsf{sc}(a_{m})}=\frac{d(1,a)+d(2,a)}{d(1,a_{m})+d(2,a_{m})}\geqslant\;\;\frac{1+d(2,a_{\lfloor\frac{m+1}{2}\rfloor})}{1+d(2,a_{m})}

\displaystyle\left(\text{Since }\mathsf{rank}_{1}(a)\leqslant\lfloor\frac{m+1}{2}\rfloor\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\frac{2\alpha^{-\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}}{\alpha^{-\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}-1}}{1+\frac{2}{\alpha^{-\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}-1}}=\frac{3+\frac{2}{\alpha^{-\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}-1}}{1+\frac{2}{\alpha^{-\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}-1}}=1+2\frac{1-\alpha^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}}{1+\alpha^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}}\cdot

Therefore,
\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(f)
is at least
1+2\frac{1-\alpha^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}}{1+\alpha^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}}\cdot

Now, assume
\alpha=0

and two alternatives

and the second one submits

3.2
Upper Bound

To complement our lower bounds, in this section, we present a voting rule that achieves a distortion better than 3. To do so, we start by analyzing the special case of moderate-up-to-
k
preferences.

Definition 1
.

We say a preference
\sigma=(\pi,\Join)
is moderate-up-to-
k
, if it exhibits the first intensity at the
k+1\textsuperscript{\text{th}}
position, i.e.,
\Join(k+1)=\mlq\!{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\!\mrq
and
\forall i\in[k]:\Join(i)=\mlq\!\succ\!\mrq
. If no
\mlq\!{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\!\mrq
appears in
\Join
, the preference is considered as moderate-up-to-
(m-1)
. A preference profile is called moderate-up-to-
k
if all agents have moderate-up-to-
k
preferences.

Our first goal is to prove an upper bound on the optimal metric distortion over moderate-up-to-
k
preference profiles. Define

\displaystyle w_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\alpha+1}{3\alpha+1}&k=1\\
1-\frac{2\alpha}{(1-\alpha)t_{k-1}+2w_{k-1}+2\alpha}&k>1\end{cases},\text{ and}

(2)

\displaystyle t_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1-\alpha}{3\alpha+1}&k=1\\
w_{k}+(1-w_{k})t_{k-1}&k>1\end{cases}.

(3)

Now we can state the main theorem as follows:

Theorem 3.2

There exists a rule
f
that for
k\in[m-1]
, given a moderate-up-to-
k
preference profile, finds an alternative with metric distortion of at most
2+\max(\alpha,t_{k}).

To prove this theorem, we adopt the method used by
22
to establish the existence of a deterministic rule with a metric distortion of 3. Their approach involves the
(\vec{p},\vec{q})
-domination graph, which is a bipartite graph defined for each alternative as follows.

Definition 2
.

Given an election,
\mathcal{E}=(N,A,\vec{\sigma})
, and vectors
\vec{p}\,\in\,\Delta(N)
and
\vec{q}\,\in\,\Delta(A)
, the
(\vec{p},\vec{q})-
domination graph of an alternative
a
is the vertex-weighted bipartite graph

G_{\vec{p},\vec{q}}^{\mathcal{E}}(a)=(N,A,E_{a},\vec{p},\vec{q}),

where
(i,c)\in E_{a}
if and only if
a\succ_{i}c
. The vertex corresponding to
i\in N
has weight
p(i)
and vertex
c\in A
has weight
q(c)
.

Definition 3
.

We say
G_{\vec{p},\vec{q}}^{\mathcal{E}}(a)
admits a fractional perfect matching, if there exists a weight function
w:E_{a}\to\mathbf{R}_{\geqslant 0}
such that the total weight of the edges incident to each vertex equals the weight of the vertex, i.e., for each
i\in N
we have
\sum_{c:(i,c)\in E_{a}}w(i,c)=p(i)
, and for each
c\in A
we have
\sum_{i:(i,c)\in E_{a}}w(i,c)=q(c)
.

By proving the following theorem, known as the Ranking-Matching Lemma,
22
show that such a matching exists. This forms the core of the proof of existence of a voting rule with a metric distortion 3.

Theorem 3.3

(Ranking-Matching Lemma)
For any election
\mathcal{E}=(N,A,\vec{\sigma})
, and vectors
\vec{p}\in\Delta(N)
and

\vec{q}\in\Delta(A)
, there exists an alternative a whose
(\vec{p},\vec{q})-
domination graph
G_{\vec{p},\vec{q}}^{\mathcal{E}}(a)
admits a fractional perfect matching.

22
apply
Theorem

3.3
with
\vec{p}=(1/n,\ldots,1/n)
and
q(a)=\mathsf{Plu}(a)/n
to show that the Plurality Matching rule achieves a distortion of 3. However, to prove
Theorem

3.2
, we need a generalized form of their result. To achieve this, we introduce a new class of voting rules called
Positional Scoring Matching rules
. These rules utilize a scoring vector of length
m
and calculate the total score of each alternative based on the rankings. We define
\vec{q}
based on the total score of the alternatives and set
\vec{p}
to be uniform, and then, identify an alternative whose
(\vec{p},\vec{q})
-domination graph admits a fractional perfect matching. The definition and analysis of this new class of rules may also be of independent interest for future studies.

Definition 4
.

Given a unit-sum scoring vector
\vec{s}=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m})
, the Positional Scoring Matching rule
\psm_{\vec{s}}
is defined as follows:
For an election
\mathcal{E}=(N,A,\vec{\sigma})
, set
\vec{p}=(1/n,\ldots,1/n)
. For each
a\in A
, calculate
q(a)=\sum_{i\in N}s_{\mathsf{rank}_{i}(a)}/n
. The rule then selects the alternative
a^{*}
for which
G_{\vec{p},\vec{q}}^{\mathcal{E}}(a^{*})
admits a fractional perfect matching.

Note that, according to
Theorem

3.3
, such an alternative always exists. It is also important to mention that the Plurality Matching rule, which was the first deterministic rule to achieve a distortion of 3, is a member of this class with
\vec{s}=(1,0,\ldots,0)
. We now analyze the distortion of rules within this class when applied to moderate-up-to-
k
preference profiles.

Lemma 3

For any unit-sum weight vector
\vec{r}=(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{k+1},0,\ldots,0)
of length
m
, and moderate-up-to-
k
preference profile
\vec{\sigma}
,
\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(\psm_{\vec{r}}(\vec{\sigma}),\vec{\sigma})
is upper bounded by

2+\max\!\left(\!\alpha,\!\max_{j\in[k+1]}\left(\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in[k+1]\land i\neq j}\!r_{i}\,\max\left(1,\alpha^{j-i}\right)\right)\!-\!r_{j}\right).

Proof.

Let
\vec{p}
be the uniform probability distribution that assigns probability
1/n
to each agent in
N
. We define
a:=\psm_{\vec{r}}(\vec{\sigma})
and
b:=\mathsf{opt}_{d}
for an arbitrary metric
d
. Recall that
\vec{p}
and
\vec{q}
are related to
\psm_{\vec{r}}
, and
a
is the alternative whose
(\vec{p},\vec{q})
-domination graph admits a fractional perfect matching, with
w
as its weight function. For each vertex
x\in N\cup A
in
G_{\vec{p},\vec{q}}^{\mathcal{E}}(a)
, we denote by
R(x)
the set of vertices adjacent to
x
in the undirected version of
G_{\vec{p},\vec{q}}^{\mathcal{E}}(a)
. We have:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in N}d(i,a)\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\sum\limits_{i\in N}\,\sum\limits_{c\in R(i)}\frac{w(i,c)}{\sum\limits_{c^{\prime}\in R(i)}w(i,c^{\prime})}\,d(i,c)\

\displaystyle\left(d(i,a)\leqslant d(i,c)\text{ for all $c\in R(i)$}\right)

\displaystyle=\,\sum\limits_{c\in A}\sum\limits_{i\in R(c)}\frac{w(i,c)}{\sum\limits_{c^{\prime}\in R(i)}w(i,c^{\prime})}\,d(i,c)\

\displaystyle\left(\text{rearranging the sum}\right)

\displaystyle=\,\sum\limits_{c\in A}\,\sum\limits_{i\in R(c)}\frac{w(i,c)}{1/n}\,d(i,c)

\displaystyle\left(\sum\limits_{c\in R(i)}w(i,c)=p(i)=\frac{1}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\sum\limits_{c\in A}\,\sum\limits_{i\in R(c)}\frac{w(i,c)}{1/n}\,\left(d(i,b)+d(b,c)\right)

\displaystyle\left(\text{triangle inequality}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{c\in A}\,\sum\limits_{i\in R(c)}\frac{w(i,c)}{1/n}\,d(i,b)+\sum\limits_{c\in A}\,\sum\limits_{i\in R(c)}n\,w(i,c)\,d(b,c)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i\in N}\,\sum\limits_{c\in R(i)}\frac{w(i,c)}{1/n}\,d(i,b)+\sum\limits_{c\in A}\,\sum\limits_{i\in R(c)}n\,w(i,c)\,d(b,c)

\displaystyle\left(\text{rearranging the first sum}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i\in N}d(i,b)+\sum\limits_{c\in A}\,\sum\limits_{i\in R(c)}n\,w(i,c)\,d(b,c)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{c\in R(i)}w(i,c)=p(i)=\frac{1}{n}\right)

(4)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{sc}(b)+\sum\limits_{c\in A}n\,q(c)\,d(b,c)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i\in R(c)}w(i,c)=q(c)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{sc}(b)+\sum\limits_{c\in A}\,\sum\limits_{i\in N}r_{\mathsf{rank}_{i}(c)}\,d(b,c)

\displaystyle\left(q(c)=\sum_{i\in N}\frac{r_{\mathsf{rank}_{i}(c)}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{sc}(b)+\sum\limits_{i\in N}\,\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k+1}r_{j}\,d(b,\pi_{i}(j)).

\displaystyle\left(\text{rearranging the sum}\right)

Now, we partition the summation over all agents into separate sums based on the position of
b
in their preferences:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in N}d(i,a)\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathsf{sc}(b)+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k+1}\,\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\pi_{i}(l)=b\end{subarray}}\,\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k+1}r_{j}\,d(b,\pi_{i}(j))+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\mathsf{rank}_{i}(b)\geqslant k+2\end{subarray}}\,\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k+1}r_{j}\,d(b,\pi_{i}(j))

\displaystyle=\mathsf{sc}(b)+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k+1}\,\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\pi_{i}(l)=b\end{subarray}}\,\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l-1}r_{j}\,d(b,\pi_{i}(j))+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k+1}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\pi_{i}(l)=b\end{subarray}}\!\sum\limits_{j=l+1}^{k+1}r_{j}d(b,\pi_{i}(j))

\displaystyle+\!\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\mathsf{rank}_{i}(b)\geqslant k+2\end{subarray}}\!\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k+1}r_{j}d(b,\pi_{i}(j))

\displaystyle\left(\text{$d(b,b)=0$}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathsf{sc}(b)+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k+1}\,\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\pi_{i}(l)=b\end{subarray}}\,\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l-1}r_{j}\left(d(i,b)+d(i,\pi_{i}(j))\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\;+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k+1}\,\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\pi_{i}(l)=b\end{subarray}}\,\sum\limits_{j=l+1}^{k+1}r_{j}\left(d(i,b)+d(i,\pi_{i}(j))\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\;+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\mathsf{rank}_{i}(b)\geqslant k+2\end{subarray}}\,\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k+1}r_{j}\,\left(d(i,b)+d(i,\pi_{i}(j))\right)

\displaystyle\left(\text{triangle inequality}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathsf{sc}(b)+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k+1}\,\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\pi_{i}(l)=b\end{subarray}}\,\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l-1}2r_{j}\,d(i,b)

\displaystyle\left(\text{$\pi_{i}(j)\succ_{i}b$}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\;+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k+1}\,\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\pi_{i}(l)=b\end{subarray}}\,\sum\limits_{j=l+1}^{k+1}r_{j}\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha^{j-l}}\right)d(i,b)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\;+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\mathsf{rank}_{i}(b)\geqslant k+2\end{subarray}}\,\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k+1}r_{j}\,(1+\alpha)\,d(i,b)

\displaystyle\left(\text{$\mlq\!\succ\!\mrq$ and $\mlq\!{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\!\mrq$ constraint}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{sc}(b)+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k+1}\,\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\pi_{i}(l)=b\end{subarray}}\,\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l-1}2r_{j}\,d(i,b)+\sum\limits_{j=l+1}^{k+1}r_{j}\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha^{j-l}}\right)d(i,b)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\;+(1+\alpha)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\mathsf{rank}_{i}(b)\geqslant k+2\end{subarray}}d(b,i)

\displaystyle\left(\text{$\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}r_{i}=1$}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{sc}(b)+(1+\alpha)\,\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\mathsf{rank}_{i}(b)\geqslant k+2\end{subarray}}d(i,b)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\;+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k+1}\,\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\pi_{i}(l)=b\end{subarray}}\,d(b,i)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j:j\neq l\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant k+1\end{subarray}}r_{j}\left(1+\alpha^{\min\left(0,l-j\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{sc}(b)+(1+\alpha)\,\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\mathsf{rank}_{i}(b)\geqslant k+2\end{subarray}}d(i,b)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\;+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k+1}\,\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\pi_{i}(l)=b\end{subarray}}\,d(i,b)\left(1-r_{l}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j:j\neq l\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant k+1\end{subarray}}r_{j}\,\alpha^{\min\left(0,l-j\right)}\right).

If we choose
\beta
such that

\max_{l\in[k+1]}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j:j\neq l\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant k+1\end{subarray}}r_{j}\,\alpha^{\min\left(0,l-j\right)}-r_{l}=\max_{l\in[k+1]}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j:j\neq l\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant k+1\end{subarray}}r_{j}\,{\max\left(1,\alpha^{l-j}\right)}-r_{l}=\beta,

(5)

then we have:

\displaystyle\mathsf{sc}(a)\leqslant\mathsf{sc}(b)+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k+1}\,\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\pi_{i}(l)=b\end{subarray}}\,d(i,b)\left(1+\beta\right)+(1+\alpha)\,\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\\
\mathsf{rank}_{i}(b)\geqslant k+2\end{subarray}}d(i,b)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathsf{sc}(b)+\left(1+\,\max\left(\beta,\,\alpha\right)\right)\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in N}d(i,b)\leqslant\left(2+\,\max\left(\beta,\,\alpha\right)\right)\mathsf{sc}(b).

Hence, the distortion of
a
is at most
2+\max\left(\beta,\alpha\right)
.
∎

This analysis gives us the power to optimize the scoring vector
\vec{r}
to get lower distortion. We can formulate our optimization problem as following:

\minimize_{\vec{r}\in\Delta([k+1])}\max_{j\in[k+1]}\left(\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in[k+1]\land i\neq j}r_{i}\,\max\left(1,\alpha^{j-i}\right)\right)-r_{j},

which in the matrix form can be written as

\minimize_{\vec{r}\in\Delta([k+1])}\max_{j\in[k+1]}\left(\vec{r}^{\intercal}M\right)_{j},

(6)

where

M=\begin{bmatrix}-1&1&\dots&1\\
\frac{1}{\alpha}&-1&\dots&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\\
\frac{1}{\alpha^{k}}&\frac{1}{\alpha^{k-1}}&\dots\frac{1}{\alpha}&-1\end{bmatrix}.

Optimizing Objective (
6
) is analogous to finding the value of a two-player zero-sum game with payoff matrix
M
for the second player.
Assuming
\vec{x}
and
\vec{y}
represent the mixed strategies of the first (row) player and the second (column) player, respectively, then
\vec{x}^{\intercal}M\vec{y}
represents the payoff of the second player. In Lemma
4
, we introduce an equilibrium of this game and the corresponding payoff.

Lemma 4

For each
k\!\in\![m\!-\!1]
consider the mixed strategy
\vec{r}^{k}=(w_{k},w_{k-1}(1-w_{k}),\ldots,\!w_{1}\Pi_{j=2}^{k}(1-w_{j}),\!\Pi_{j=1}^{k}\!(1-w_{j}))
. Then
(\!\vec{\!}r^{k},\!(\!\vec{r}^{k}\!)^{R})
is an equilibrium of the zero-sum game with payoff matrix
M
, and the payoff of this equilibrium, which is equal to the optimal value of Objective
6
, is
t_{k}
(
r^{R}
is vector

with reverse order of indices).

Proof.

We denote by
M^{k}
the payoff matrix
M
corresponding to the indecisive-up-to-
k
preferences. First, we prove by induction that
({\vec{r}^{k}})^{\intercal}M^{k}=t_{k}\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}
, where
\mathbf{1}
is the vector with all entries equal to one. One can easily check that the base case holds trivially. Hence, we assume that
({\vec{r}^{k-1}})^{\intercal}M^{k-1}=t_{k-1}\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}
, and aim to show the equality for
k
. Note that matrix
M^{k}
has the following form:

M^{k}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}\\
\vec{\alpha}^{k}&M^{k-1}\end{bmatrix},

(7)

where
\vec{\alpha}^{k}
is the vector with
i\textsuperscript{\text{th}}
element equal to
\frac{1}{\alpha^{i}}
. Moreover, based on the definition of
\vec{r}^{k}
, we have:

(\vec{r}^{k})^{\intercal}=[w_{k}\;\;(1-w_{k})(\vec{r}^{k-1})^{\intercal}].

(8)

Now, for every
2\leqslant i\leqslant k+1
, we can write (
M^{k}_{i}
is the
i\textsuperscript{\text{th}}
column of
M^{k}
):

\displaystyle(\vec{r}^{k})^{\intercal}M^{k}_{i}

\displaystyle=w_{k}+(1-w_{k})(\vec{r}^{k-1})^{\intercal}M^{k-1}_{i-1}

(Equations (
7
) and (
8
))

\displaystyle=w_{k}+(1-w_{k})t_{k-1}

(Induction hypothesis)

\displaystyle=t_{k}.

(Equation (
3
))

Therefore, the elements from the second to the
(k+1)\textsuperscript{\text{th}}
of
(\vec{r}^{k})^{\intercal}M^{k}
are equal to
t_{k}
. Hence, we only need to check its first element, i.e.,
(\vec{r}^{k})^{\intercal}M^{k}_{1}
. Note that we can write the first column of

as follows (
\vec{e_{i}}^{\intercal}

\begin{split}(M^{k}_{1})^{\intercal}&=[-1\;\;\;\frac{1}{\alpha}(M^{k-1}_{1})^{\intercal}+\frac{2}{\alpha}\vec{e_{1}}^{\intercal}].\end{split}

(9)

We have:

\displaystyle(\vec{r}^{k})^{\intercal}M^{k}_{1}

\displaystyle=-w_{k}+(1-w_{k})\frac{1}{\alpha}(\vec{r}^{k-1})^{\intercal}M^{k-1}_{1}+\frac{2}{\alpha}(1-w_{k})w_{k-1}

\displaystyle(\text{Equations ~\eqref{r^k: 1} and \eqref{M^k: 2}})

\displaystyle=-w_{k}+(1-w_{k})(\frac{1}{\alpha}t_{k-1}+\frac{2}{\alpha}w_{k-1})

\displaystyle=w_{k}+(1-w_{k})t_{k-1}-2+(1-w_{k})((\frac{1}{\alpha}-1)t_{k-1}+\frac{2}{\alpha}w_{k-1}+2)

\displaystyle=w_{k}+(1-w_{k})t_{k-1}

\displaystyle(\text{Equation \eqref{first rec}})

\displaystyle=t_{k}.

\displaystyle(\text{Equation \eqref{second rec}})

Now, we prove that
(\vec{r}^{k},(\vec{r}^{k})^{R})

Theorem 3.4

There exists a rule

\vec{r^{\ell_{i}}}

\ell_{i}

\vec{p}
be the uniform probability distribution that assigns probability
1/n

q(c)=\sum_{i\in A}\frac{{r^{l_{i}}}_{\mathsf{rank}_{i}(c)}}{n}\cdot

Using Theorem

\left(\vec{p},\vec{q}\right)
-domination graph admits a fractional perfect matching. Now, we want to prove an upper bound for
\sum_{i\in N}d(i,a)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in N}d(i,a)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum\limits_{i\,\in\,N}d(i,b)+\sum\limits_{c\,\in\,A}\,\sum\limits_{i\,\in\,R(c)}n\,w(i,c)\,d(b,c)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{sc}(b)+\sum\limits_{c\,\in\,A}n\,q(c)\,d(b,c)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i\in R(c)}w(i,c)=q(c)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{sc}(b)+\sum\limits_{c\in A}\,\sum_{i\in N}{r^{l_{i}}}_{\mathsf{rank}_{i}(c)}d(b,c)

\displaystyle\left(q(c)=\sum_{i\in N}\frac{{r^{\ell_{i}}}_{\mathsf{rank}_{i}(c)}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{sc}(b)+\sum\limits_{i\in N}\,\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l_{i}+1}{r^{\ell_{i}}}_{j}\,d(b,\pi_{i}(j))

\displaystyle\left(\text{rearranging the sum}\right)

a_{1}
and that of
a_{2}

5
Price of Ignoring Intensities

In this section, we explore the concept of the
Price of Ignoring Intensities (POII)
, which is a measure for the efficiency loss that rules that do not elicit the intensities incur. This notion was initially introduced by
26
, and is defined as the worst case over all elections, the ratio of the optimal distortion achievable without any information on the intensities to the optimal distortion achievable knowing the intensities. Roughly speaking, the
Price of Ignoring Intensities
measures the increase in distortion when the intensities are not known.

To formally define POII, we have to first distinguish between the optimal alternative with and without information on the intensities. We borrow these definitions from
26
.

Definition 5

(Intensity-aware optimal)
.

For election
\mathcal{E}=(N,A,\vec{\sigma})
, the intensity-aware optimal alternative,
\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{aw}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E})
, is an alternative that minimizes the worst-case distortion over the metrics that are
\alpha
-consistent with
\vec{\sigma}
, i.e.,

\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{aw}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E}):=\argmin_{a\in A}\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(a,\mathcal{E}).

Now, we define the POII of an alternative in an election.

Definition 6

(Price of ignoring the intensities)
.

For fixed
\alpha\in[0,1]
, we define the price of ignoring intensities (POII) of an alternative
a\in A
in election
\mathcal{E}=(N,A,\vec{\sigma})
as the ratio between the
\alpha
-distortion of
a
and that of the intensity-aware optimal alternative, i.e.,

\mathsf{PoII}(a,\mathcal{E},\alpha):=\frac{\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(a,\mathcal{E})}{\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{aw}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E}),\mathcal{E})}\cdot

In addition, given only the ranked preference profile (without intensities)
\vec{\pi}
, POII of alternative
a
on
\vec{\pi}
is defined as

\mathsf{PoII}(a,\vec{\pi},\alpha)=\max_{\mathcal{E}\rhd\vec{\pi}}\mathsf{PoII}(a,\mathcal{E},\alpha)
, where for
\mathcal{E}=(N,A,\vec{\sigma})
we use
\mathcal{E}\rhd\vec{\pi}
to denote that
\vec{\sigma}=(\vec{\pi},\vec{\Join})
for some
\vec{\Join}
. This allows us to define the
intensity-oblivious optimal
alternative in
\mathcal{E}
as

\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E}):=\argmin_{a\in A}\mathsf{PoII}(a,\vec{\pi},\alpha),

and the POII on
\mathcal{E}
as
\mathsf{PoII}(\mathcal{E},\alpha):=\mathsf{PoII}(\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E}),\mathcal{E},\alpha)
. Since
\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E})
does not depend on
\vec{\Join}
, when other components are clear from the context, with a slight abuse of notion we use
\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\vec{\pi})
instead. We are interested in the worst case of this over all
\mathcal{E}
, termed the
POII for
\alpha
-decisive preferences
:
\mathsf{PoII}(\alpha)=\max_{\mathcal{E}}\mathsf{PoII}(\mathcal{E},\alpha)
.

Our goal is to prove a lower bound on the POII. To do so, we use the following two lemmas.

Lemma 6

(
[
26

\alpha\in[0,1]

\mathcal{E}

we have:

\mathsf{PoII}(\mathcal{E},\alpha)\geqslant\frac{\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E}),\mathcal{E})}{\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(a,\mathcal{E})}\cdot

. We have

\displaystyle d(2,\pi_{2}(\frac{m}{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{m}+\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}}{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{m}+1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{m}-\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}+1}{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{m}+1}

\displaystyle\left(\text{since }\left(\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}-1\right)^{2}\geqslant 0\right)

\displaystyle=d(2,\pi_{2}(1))+d(1,\pi_{2}(1))+d(1,\pi_{2}(\frac{m}{2})).

Third, assume that either
1\leqslant j\leqslant\frac{m}{2}
and
\frac{m}{2}+1\leqslant j^{\prime}\leqslant m
, or
1\leqslant j^{\prime}\leqslant\frac{m}{2}
and
\frac{m}{2}+1\leqslant j\leqslant m
. In this case, the worst case occurs when
a_{j}=\pi_{1}(1)
and
a_{j^{\prime}}=\pi_{2}(1)
. Now, we have

d(2,\pi_{1}(1))=\frac{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{m}+\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}}{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{m}+1}=d(1,\pi_{2}(1))+d(2,\pi_{2}(1))+d(1,\pi_{1}(1)).

Therefore,
d
satisfies the triangle inequality and is a feasible solution for LP
\mathcal{P}
.

Now, note that the objective value of LP
\mathcal{D}
, given by
(\psi,\phi,\beta,t)
, is:

\sum_{j=1}^{m}t_{a_{j}}=t_{a_{\frac{m}{2}+1}}=\frac{2}{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{m}+1}\left(\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}-1\right)+1=1+\frac{2\alpha^{\frac{m}{2}}-2\alpha^{m}}{\alpha^{m}+1}.

Similarly, the objective value of LP
\mathcal{P}
, given by
d
, is:

d_{2,a_{1}}+d_{1,a_{1}}=\frac{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{m}+\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}}{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{m}+1}+\frac{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}-1}{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{m}+1}=1+\frac{2\alpha^{\frac{m}{2}}-2\alpha^{m}}{\alpha^{m}+1}.

Therefore,
1+\frac{2\alpha^{\frac{m}{2}}-2\alpha^{m}}{\alpha^{m}+1}=\frac{1+2\alpha^{\frac{m}{2}}-\alpha^{m}}{\alpha^{m}+1}=\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(a_{1},\mathcal{E})
is the optimal value of both LP
\mathcal{D}
and LP
\mathcal{P}
.

Now, assume that
m
is odd. Consider the election
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=(N,A,\vec{\sigma})
where
N=\{1,2\}
and
A=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\}
. The preferences of agents are as follows:

\sigma_{l}=\begin{cases}a_{1}\;{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\;a_{2}\;{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\;\ldots\;{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\;a_{\frac{m-1}{2}}\;{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\;a_{\frac{m-1}{2}+1}\succ a_{\frac{m-1}{2}+2}\succ\ldots\succ a_{m}&\;l=1\\
a_{\frac{m-1}{2}+1}\succ a_{\frac{m-1}{2}+2}\succ\ldots\succ a_{m-1}\succ a_{1}\succ a_{2}\succ\ldots\succ a_{\frac{m-1}{2}}\;\succ\;a_{m}&\;l=2.\end{cases}

We drop
a_{m}
from the election since
a_{m}
is the least preferred alternative for each agent. Now, we can apply the metric
d
we found for the case where
m
is even to the remaining alternatives and agents, and obtain the distortion of
a_{1}
:

\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(a_{1},\mathcal{E}^{\prime})=\frac{1+2\alpha^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}-\alpha^{\lfloor m\rfloor_{\text{even}}}}{\alpha^{\lfloor m\rfloor_{\text{even}}}+1}.

To complete this argument, we have to determine
d(1,a_{m})
and
d(2,a_{m})
. If we set
d(1,a_{m})=d(1,\pi_{1}(m-1))
and
d(2,a_{m})=d(2,\pi_{2}(m-1))
, it is easy to check that intensity and preference constraints are still satisfied.

∎

Theorem 5.1

For every
\alpha\in[0,1]
, we have

\mathsf{PoII}(\alpha)\geqslant\frac{3\left(\alpha^{\lfloor m\rfloor_{\text{even}}}+1\right)}{1+2\alpha^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}-\alpha^{\lfloor m\rfloor_{\text{even}}}},

where
\lfloor m\rfloor_{\text{even}}
is the largest even integer smaller than or equal to
m
.

Proof.

First, assume that
m
is even. Consider an election
\mathcal{E}
with two agents and
m
alternatives, where
\pi_{1}=\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\right)
and
\pi_{2}=\left(a_{\frac{m}{2}+1},\dots,a_{m},a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{\frac{m}{2}}\right)
. We determine intensities based on the position of
\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\vec{\pi})
in the rankings.
If
\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\vec{\pi})
appears in the first half of the first agent’s ranking, we set

a_{1}\succ a_{2}\succ\ldots\succ a_{m}
for the first agent and
a_{\frac{m}{2}+1}\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,a_{\frac{m}{2}+2}\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,\ldots\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,a_{m}\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,a_{1}\succ a_{2}\succ\ldots\succ a_{m}
for the second one. In the other case, we set

a_{1}\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,a_{2}\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,\ldots\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,a_{\frac{m}{2}}\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,a_{\frac{m}{2}+1}\succ a_{\frac{m}{2}+2}\ldots\succ a_{m}
for the first agent and
a_{\frac{m}{2}+1}\succ a_{\frac{m}{2}+2}\succ\ldots\succ a_{m}\succ a_{1}\succ a_{2}\succ\dots\succ a_{\frac{m}{2}}
for the second one.

Without loss of generality, assume that
\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\vec{\pi})
is in the second half of
\pi_{1}
. Using Lemma,
6
we have:

\mathsf{PoII}(\alpha)\geqslant\frac{\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E}),\mathcal{E})}{\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(a_{1},\mathcal{E})}\cdot

(16)

To prove the desired bound, we calculate the exact amount of the right-hand side of Inequality
(
16
). Consider the following metric:

d(1,b)=\begin{cases}0&\mathsf{rank}_{1}(b)\leqslant\frac{m}{2}\\
2&\mathsf{rank}_{1}(b)>\frac{m}{2}\end{cases},\text{ and }d(2,b)=1\cdot

It is easy to check that
d
satisfies the triangle inequality and intensity constraints. Now, note that

\displaystyle\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E}),\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\mathsf{sc}(\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E}))}{\mathsf{sc}(a_{1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{d(1,\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E}))+d(2,\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E}))}{d(1,a_{1})+d(2,a_{1})}=3

\displaystyle\left(\mathsf{rank}_{1}(\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E}))>\frac{m}{2}\right).

Hence,
\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E}),\mathcal{E})
is at least
3
. But we know that it is also upper bounded by
3
. Therefore, it is exactly equal to
3
.
Note that Lemma
7
gives the exact value of
\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(a_{1},\mathcal{E})
, i.e.,
\frac{1+2\alpha^{\frac{m}{2}}-\alpha^{m}}{\alpha^{m}+1}
. Now, using Inequality (
16
) gives the desired lower bound on
\mathsf{PoII}(\alpha)
.

In the case where
m
is odd, we can use an election
\mathcal{E}
with two agents and
m
alternatives, where,
\pi_{1}=\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{m-1},a_{m}\right)
and
\pi_{2}=\left(a_{\frac{m-1}{2}+1},\dots,a_{m-1},a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{\frac{m-1}{2}},a_{m}\right)
. Now, we can drop
a_{m}
from the election (since
a_{m}
is the least preferred alternative for both agents) and apply a similar argument to the rest of the agents and alternatives to achieve the desired lower bound.
∎

5.1
Voluntary Elicitation of the Intensities

As described in the preliminaries, we can make an alternate assumption on how the agents report their intensities. In this setting, we give the option of using
\mlq\!{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\!\mrq
to the agents but they might decide not to use it. This can happen when agents do not want to report the intensity or are not sure if there is an
\alpha
-gap between the distances. Recall the definition of
\alpha
-consistency of a preference profile
\vec{\sigma}
and a metric space
d
under voluntary assumption (
\vec{\sigma}\rhd^{\!v}_{\!\alpha}d
). Using that definition, we can redefine the distortion for the voluntary setting:

\mathsf{dist}^{v}_{\alpha}(a,\mathcal{E}):=\sup_{d:\vec{\sigma}\rhd^{\!v}_{\!\alpha}d}\frac{\mathsf{sc}_{d}(a)}{\min_{b\in A}\mathsf{sc}_{d}(b)}\cdot

Note that the optimal deterministic distortion in this setting is still 3, since if all the agents decide not to report their intensities, the voting rule is restricted to the data that is available in the classic voting with ranked preferences. However, we can use this definition to define
\mathsf{opt}^{v-\mathsf{aw}}_{\alpha},\mathsf{opt}^{v-\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha},\allowbreak\mathsf{PoII}^{v}(a,\mathcal{E},\alpha)
, and
\mathsf{PoII}^{v}(\alpha)
similar to the mandatory setting, and then prove bounds on the POII under this assumption. Bounds on the POII in this setting show that even if the agents are not forced to report the intensities, if the voting rule does not take them into account, we might lose a great deal in terms of efficiency.

Theorem 5.2

For every
\alpha\in[0,1]
, we have:

\mathsf{PoII}^{v}(\alpha)\geqslant\frac{3}{2\alpha^{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}+1}\cdot

To prove this bound, we use the same instance as in the proof of
Theorem

5.1
. However, the bound differs since the distortion of intensity aware optimal outputs in these two settings are different. The source of this difference is that the class of instances over which
\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{aw}}_{\alpha}
has the optimal distortion is a subset of the instances that
\mathsf{opt}^{v-\mathsf{aw}}_{\alpha}
has to be optimized for, since
\vec{\sigma}\rhd_{\!\alpha}d
is a more restricting condition on
d
than
\vec{\sigma}\rhd^{\!v}_{\!\alpha}d
.
Similar to the proof of
Theorem

5.1
, we use an LP to formulate this setting and write the dual LP as well. However, in this case, we cannot guarantee that the variables are always positive and hence the Complementary Slackness conditions do not help. In the following proof, we present a feasible solution for the dual and we show an upper bound on the denominator.

Proof.

We only consider the case where
m
is even. Similar to the proof of Theorem
5.1
, the odd case can be concluded from the even case.

Consider an election
\mathcal{E}
with two agents and
m
alternatives, where
\pi_{1}=\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\right)
and
\pi_{2}=(a_{\frac{m}{2}+1},\dots,a_{m},\allowbreak a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{\frac{m}{2}})
. We determine intensities based on the position of
\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\vec{\pi})
in the rankings.
If
\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\vec{\pi})
appears in the first half of the first agent’s ranking, we set

a_{1}\succ a_{2}\succ\ldots\succ a_{m}
for the first agent and
a_{\frac{m}{2}+1}\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,a_{\frac{m}{2}+2}\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,\ldots\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,a_{m}\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,a_{1}\succ a_{2}\succ\ldots\succ a_{m}
for the second one. In the other case, we set

a_{1}\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,a_{2}\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,\ldots\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,a_{\frac{m}{2}}\,{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\,a_{\frac{m}{2}+1}\succ a_{\frac{m}{2}+2}\ldots\succ a_{m}
for the first agent and
a_{\frac{m}{2}+1}\succ a_{\frac{m}{2}+2}\succ\ldots\succ a_{m}\succ a_{1}\succ a_{2}\succ\dots\succ a_{\frac{m}{2}}
for the second one.

Without loss of generality, assume that
\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\vec{\pi})
is in the second half of
\pi_{1}
. Using Lemma,
6
we have:

\mathsf{PoII}(\alpha)\geqslant\frac{\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E}),\mathcal{E})}{\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(a_{1},\mathcal{E})}\cdot

(17)

Note that the metric
d
provided in the proof of Theorem
5.1
is also consistent with the intensity constraints in the voluntary setting. Hence, we can conclude that
\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(\mathsf{opt}^{\mathsf{ob}}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{E}),\mathcal{E})=3
.

Henceforth, we aim to find an upper bound on
\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(a_{1},\mathcal{E})
. The technique we use is similar to the technique of the proof of Lemma
7
. We write a linear program to find the metric
d
that maximizes
\frac{\mathsf{sc}_{d}(a_{1})}{\mathsf{sc}_{d}(a_{\frac{m}{2}+1})}
.

Linear Program
\mathcal{P}

Maximize

\displaystyle d_{1,a_{1}}+d_{2,a_{1}}

subject to

\displaystyle d_{i,a_{j}}\leqslant d_{i,a_{j^{\prime}}}+d_{i^{\prime},a_{j^{\prime}}}+d_{i^{\prime},a_{j}}\hskip 9.24994pt

1\leqslant i\neq i^{\prime}\leqslant 2
,
1\leqslant j\neq j^{\prime}\leqslant m
(triangle inequality)

\displaystyle d_{i,\pi_{i}(j)}\leqslant\alpha d_{i,\pi_{i}(j+1)}

\displaystyle\forall i,j:\Join_{i}(j)=\mlq\!{\mathrel{{\succ}\!\!{\succ}}}\!\mrq\text{(intensity constraint)}

\displaystyle d_{i,\pi_{i}(j)}\leqslant d_{i,\pi_{i}(j+1)}

\displaystyle\forall i,j:\Join_{i}(j)=\mlq\!\succ\!\mrq\text{(intensity constraint)}

\displaystyle d_{1,a_{\frac{m}{2}+1}}+d_{2,a_{\frac{m}{2}+1}}=1\hskip 9.24994pt

(normalization)

\displaystyle d_{1,a_{j}}+d_{2,a_{j}}\geqslant 1\hskip 9.24994pt

1\leqslant j\leqslant m,\,j\neq\frac{m}{2}+1
(optimality of
a_{\frac{m}{2}+1}
)

\displaystyle d_{i,a_{j}}\geqslant 0\hskip 9.24994pt

i\in\{1,2\}
,
1\leqslant j\leqslant m-1
.

Note that in the voluntary setting, when we have
\Join_{i}(j)=\mlq\!\succ\!\mrq
, the only inference we can make is that
d_{i,\pi_{i}(j)}\leqslant d_{i,\pi_{i}(j+1)}
.

The dual includes three types of variables:
\psi^{i,i^{\prime}}_{a_{j},a_{j^{\prime}}}
are the dual variables referring to the triangle inequality constraints,
\phi^{i}_{\pi_{i}(j),\pi_{i}(j+1)}
are the dual variables for the intensity constraints, and

are the dual variables for the normalization/optimality constraints.

Linear Program
\mathcal{D}

Minimize

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}t_{a_{j}}

subject to

\displaystyle\theta(i,j)\geqslant\begin{cases}1&\pi_{i}(j)=a_{1}\\
0&\pi_{i}(j)\neq a_{1}\end{cases}

,
1\leqslant j\leqslant m

\displaystyle\psi^{i,i^{\prime}}_{a_{j},a_{j^{\prime}}}\geqslant 0

,
1\leqslant j\neq j^{\prime}\leqslant m

\displaystyle\phi^{i}_{\pi_{i}(j),\pi_{i}(j+1)}\geqslant 0

,
1\leqslant j\leqslant m-1

\displaystyle t_{a_{j}}\leqslant 0

\displaystyle\text{$a_{j}\neq a_{\frac{m}{2}+1}$},

where

\displaystyle\theta(i,j)=\;

\displaystyle t_{\pi_{i}(j)}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i^{\prime}\leqslant 2,\;1\leqslant j^{\prime}\leqslant m\\
i^{\prime}\neq i,\;\pi_{i^{\prime}}(j^{\prime})\neq\pi_{i}(j)\end{subarray}}\left(\psi^{i,i^{\prime}}_{\pi_{i}(j),\pi_{i^{\prime}}(j^{\prime})}-\psi^{i,i^{\prime}}_{\pi_{i^{\prime}}(j^{\prime}),\pi_{i}(j)}-\psi^{i^{\prime},i}_{\pi_{i}(j),\pi_{i^{\prime}}(j^{\prime})}-\psi^{i^{\prime},i}_{\pi_{i^{\prime}}(j^{\prime}),\pi_{i}(j)}\right)

\displaystyle+\begin{cases}(\mathbf{1}_{\{j\neq m\}})\frac{1}{\alpha}\phi^{i}_{\pi_{i}(j),\pi_{i}(j+1)}-(\mathbf{1}_{\{j\neq 1\}})\phi^{i}_{\pi_{i}(j-1),\pi_{i}(j)}&i=1,j\leqslant\frac{m}{2}\\
(\mathbf{1}_{\{j\neq m\}})\phi^{i}_{\pi_{i}(j),\pi_{i}(j+1)}-(\mathbf{1}_{\{j\neq 1\}})\phi^{i}_{\pi_{i}(j-1),\pi_{i}(j)}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Now, we introduce a feasible solution for LP
\mathcal{D}
. The non-zero variables of this feasible solution are as follows:

\displaystyle\phi^{1}_{\pi_{2}(\frac{m}{2}-i),\pi_{2}(\frac{m}{2}+1-i)}=x\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{i}

\displaystyle 0\leqslant i\leqslant\frac{m}{2}-1

\displaystyle\psi^{i,i^{\prime}}_{a_{j},a_{j^{\prime}}}=1

\displaystyle i=2,\;i^{\prime}=1,\;,a_{j}=a_{1},\;a_{j^{\prime}}=a_{\frac{m}{2}+1}

\displaystyle t_{a_{j}}=1+x

\displaystyle a_{j}=a_{\frac{m}{2}+1},

where

.
The objective value of LP
\mathcal{D}
, given by this feasible solution, is:

\sum_{j=1}^{m}t_{a_{j}}=t_{a_{\frac{m}{2}+1}}=1+2\alpha^{\frac{m}{2}}.

Therefore,
\mathsf{dist}_{\alpha}(a_{1},\mathcal{E})
is upper bounded by
1+2\alpha^{\frac{m}{2}}
. As a result, we have

\mathsf{PoII}^{v}(\alpha)\geqslant\frac{3}{2\alpha^{\frac{m}{2}}+1}\cdot

∎

6
Discussion and Future Work

In this paper, we investigate the effect of adding intensities to voting with ranked ballots to the efficiency from the viewpoint of metric distortion. We established upper and lower bounds on the distortion when agents are required to report the intensities. With these results and the results of
26
, the next step would be to see if the best of both worlds (utilitarian and metric) guarantees can be made using the ideas of
23
.

In this paper, our focus was solely on deterministic rules. While deterministic rules are more desirable in elections with high stakes, randomized rules have gained a lot of attention in repeated elections where the price of having one bad output once in a while is not that high. An immediate extension to our work is to see how randomization helps improve the distortion in this model.

One can also think about an axiomatic approach to voting with intensities. This ballot format provides extra information compared to both approvals and ranked preferences. An interesting direction would be to find a meaningful way to define axioms for this type of voting and see if desirable axioms are satisfiable.

Another direction is to bring this ballot format to other problems. Distortion has been defined and extensively analyzed in other settings such as fair division and matching. An interesting future work would be to see how intensities change the distortion in these problems.
