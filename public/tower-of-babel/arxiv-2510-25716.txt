A Low-Rank Symplectic Gradient Adjustment Method for Computing Nash Equilibria
N. Vater, K. R. Foglia, V. Colao, A. Borzì

Abstract
This work presents a theoretical and numerical investigation of the symplectic gradient adjustment (SGA) method and of a low-rank SGA (LRSGA) method for efficiently solving two-objective optimization problems in the framework of Nash games. The SGA method outperforms the gradient method by including second-order mixed derivatives computed at each iterate, which requires considerably larger computational effort. For this reason, a LRSGA method is proposed where the approximation to second-order mixed derivatives are obtained by rank-one updates. The theoretical analysis presented in this work focuses on novel convergence estimates for the SGA and LRSGA methods, including parameter bounds. The superior computational complexity of the LRSGA method is demonstrated in the training of a CLIP neural architecture, where the LRSGA method outperforms the SGA method by orders of magnitude smaller CPU time.



Keywords:
Symplectic gradient adjustment; low-rank updates; Nash equilibria; convergence analysis; Green AI; Environmental footprint.

49M15; 65H04; 65H10; 90C30; 47H05.

Introduction

Multi-objective optimization problems in a game framework appear in many fields of application ranging from aerodynamics to topology optimization and
machine learning; see, e.g.,
[
10
,
13
,
14
,
20
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
. In this framework, Nash equilibria (NE) problems are in the focus of these applications or they are instrumental
for identifying special solutions along the Pareto front, usually by means of
bargaining criteria
[
4
,
6
,
20
]
. This fact motivates
a great effort in developing efficient numerical methods for solving NE
problems, and the purpose of our work is to contribute to this ongoing research
with the analysis and development of gradient-based techniques
applied to the following class of NE problems:

Find
(x_{*},y_{*})\in X\times Y
such that

\begin{split}f(x_{*},y_{*})&=\min_{x\in X}f(x,y_{*}),\\
g(x_{*},y_{*})&=\min_{y\in Y}g(x_{*},y),\end{split}

(1.1)

where
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are two compact and convex subsets and
f,g\in C^{3}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
with
\Omega\subseteq X\times Y
being a convex domain.

We remark that this two-objective optimization problem can
be seen as a generalization of a Nash game of two players having
mixed strategies
[
21
]
. In particular, we consider
zero-sum games where
g(x,y)=-f(x,y)
, and player one’s gain
with strategy
x
corresponds to player two’s loss with strategy
y
.

Assuming differentiability of the loss (objective) functions
f
and
g
,
a natural approach for solving (
1.1
) is to apply an iterative gradient
descent (GD) method as follows:

\begin{split}x_{k+1}&=x_{k}-\eta\,\partial_{x}f(x_{k},y_{k})\\
y_{k+1}&=y_{k}-\eta\,\partial_{y}g(x_{k},y_{k}),\end{split}

(1.2)

where
\eta>0
represents a stepsize.
Unfortunately, the GD method has the disadvantage that, although it may
converge for sufficiently small stepsize
\eta>0
, it produces iterates that
spiralize around the NE solution, thus considerably slowing down convergence.
We illustrate this fact considering the following loss functions:

f(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+xy,\qquad g(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}y^{2}-xy.

(1.3)

As discussed in the next section, the NE problem (
1.1
)
with these loss functions has the unique NE solution
given by
(x_{*},y_{*})=(0,0)
. Now, we apply the GD method (
1.2
)
with initialization
(x_{0},y_{0})=(1,1)
and
\eta=0.7
and obtain
the spiralizing iterates depicted in Figure
1
(left). One can also see
that, by choosing
\eta=1
, all iterates take sequentially the values of
the coordinates
(\pm 1,\pm 1)
, and never reach the NE point; see Figure
1
(right).

Figure 1
:
Iterates of the GD method with
\eta=0.7
(left) and
\eta=1
.

The phenomenon of spiralization of the GD iterates has inspired recent
research work to improve its understanding in a more general setting and has been
a source of inspiration for designing modified GD methods that do not spiralize
but converge ‘straight’ to the NE solution. We refer to
[
24
]

for a detailed review and references on modified GD methods. One approach is to formulate iterative schemes using second-order
information that results in a preconditioned GD method as follows:

\begin{pmatrix}x_{k+1}\\
y_{k+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{k}\\
y_{k}\end{pmatrix}-\eta\,\mathcal{M}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}f_{k}\\
\partial_{y}g_{k}\end{pmatrix},

(1.4)

where
\partial_{x}f_{k}:=\partial_{x}f(x_{k},y_{k})
and

\partial_{y}g_{k}:=\partial_{y}g(x_{k},y_{k})
, and the entries of the matrix
\mathcal{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+n)\times(m+n)}
are determined by second-order derivatives of
f
and
g
at the current iterate

(x_{k},y_{k})
; see
[
1
,
16
,
24
]
. Specifically,
in
[
24
]
a competitive GD (CGD) method is proposed,
where

\cal{M}_{CGD,k}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{m}&\eta\,\partial_{xy}^{2}f_{k}\\
\eta\,\partial_{yx}^{2}g_{k}&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\approx\begin{pmatrix}I_{m}&-\eta\,\partial_{xy}^{2}f_{k}\\
-\eta\,\partial_{yx}^{2}g_{k}&I_{n}\end{pmatrix},

(1.5)

where
I_{m}
(resp.
I_{n}
) denotes the identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
(resp.
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
), and the given approximation, for
\eta
sufficiently small,
is also proposed in
[
24
]
.
Further, in
[
1
,
16
]
a symplectic gradient adjustment (SGA)
method is presented, where
\cal{M}_{k}
, including a parameter
\tau>0
, is given by

\cal{M}_{SGA,k}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{m}&-\frac{\tau}{2}\,(\partial_{xy}^{2}f_{k}-\partial_{yx}^{2}g_{k}^{T})\\
-\frac{\tau}{2}\,(\partial_{yx}^{2}g_{k}-\partial_{xy}^{2}f_{k}^{T})&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

(1.6)

Notice that the two methods above, although derived from different perspectives,
are somewhat similar. In fact, they have similar convergence performance.

However, while the inclusion of second-order information with
the mixed derivatives
\partial_{xy}^{2}f_{k}
and
\partial_{xy}^{2}g_{k}

considerably improves the convergence behaviour of the CGD and
SGA methods in comparison to the GD method, it requires
additional computational effort that can make the application
of these methods to large-size problems prohibitive, especially
in the case of game problems involving neural networks, where the computation
of second-order derivatives involves back-propagation processes.

This fact motivates our investigation that aims at developing effective rank-one
updates to the mixed derivatives matrices that are based only on
first-order information. Furthermore, we support our development
with theoretical results that can be applied to the original methods
discussed above and also accommodate our low-rank variant.
However, in order to keep this work to a reasonable size, we present our results and techniques for the SGA method and its low-rank-update version.

In the context of two-player NE problems, we investigate the convergence of the SGA method from a metric point of view, particularly emphasizing the monotonicity properties. Further, we extend our analysis to our low-rank SGA (LRSGA) by proving its convergence under suitable assumptions.
Our theoretical work is complemented by results of numerical
experiments comparing the computational performance of the SGA and LRSGA methods.

In the next section, we define two-players Nash games and the corresponding solution concept of Nash equilibria. Sufficient conditions for the existence of NE solutions are given. In Section
3
, we illustrate the construction of the SGA method
and our low-rank variant LRSGA method. Section
4
is devoted to the analysis of the SGA method, where we propose a theoretical framework for a convergence analysis of the SGA method that is based on metric properties of the involved operators. In particular, we provide explicit bounds for the parameters involved in the SGA method, addressing both global convergence for quadratic objective functions and local convergence near equilibrium points. Our results are derived through an analysis of the properties of the operators involved, such as strong monotonicity and inverse strong monotonicity. In Section
5
, this analysis is extended to the LRSGA method. In Section
6
, we
successfully validate the
performance of our LRSGA method for the training of a CLIP neural architecture.
A section of conclusion completes our work.

2
Nash equilibria and their characterization

We consider coupled optimization problems involving two sets
of optimization variables, representing the players’ (mixed) strategies,
and two corresponding loss functions. In a non-cooperative setting, the objectives are such that
minimizing one function with respect to one set of variables may result
in an increase of the other function and vice-versa. In this competitive setting,
a widely used concept of optimality is called Nash equilibrium
[
18
,
19
]
.
This equilibrium configuration defines the solution of a non-cooperative Nash game.

As already mentioned in the introduction, we consider
game problems with two players that are identified with the
variables
x\in X\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
y\in Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
X
and
Y
are two compact and convex subsets. The purpose of each player is to minimize
the corresponding loss function that depends
on both strategies
x
and
y
. We denote these functions with

f
for the player
P_{1}
, and
g
for the player
P_{2}
. In this
context, a Nash equilibrium is defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

A pair
(x_{*},y_{*})\in X\times Y
is a Nash equilibrium (NE) of a two-players’ game if

(1)

The strategy
x_{*}\in X
is the best choice of player
P_{1}
in reply to the strategy

y_{*}\in Y
adopted by player
P_{2}
as follows:

f(x_{*},y_{*})=\min_{x\in X}f(x,y_{*});

(2)

The strategy
y_{*}\in Y
is the best choice of player
P_{2}
in reply to the strategy

x_{*}\in X
adopted by player
P_{1}
as follows:

g(x_{*},y_{*})=\min_{y\in Y}g(x_{*},y).

In this setting, it is assumed that each player has knowledge of both sets
of strategies
X
and
Y
, and of the objectives
f
and
g
. With the definition above an equilibrium is achieved when no player can improve its objective by unilateral change of its strategy.

A zero-sum game corresponds to the setting
g=-f
.
In this case, the first player with strategy
x\in X
aims at
minimizing
f
, while the second player aims at maximizing it using strategy
y\in Y
.
Because in this game the gain for one player is equivalent to the loss for the other,
we have a strictly competitive game. Zero-sum games represent the starting
point of modern game theory introduced by John von Neumann
[
28
]
.

The NE defined above can be analyzed based on
the best-response maps. For player
P_{1}
, the best-response correspondence
R_{1}:Y\rightarrow P(X)
, where
P(X)
denotes the power set of
X
, is given by

y\mapsto R_{1}(y):=\{x\in X\,:\,f(x,y)\leq f(x^{\prime},y),\,x^{\prime}\in X\}.

For player
P_{2}
, the best-response correspondence
R_{2}:X\rightarrow P(Y)
is given by

x\mapsto R_{2}(x):=\{y\in Y\,:\,g(x,y)\leq g(x,y^{\prime}),\,y^{\prime}\in Y\}.

Hence we can define the multifunction
R:X\times Y\rightrightarrows X\times Y
given by
R(x,y):=R_{1}(y)\times R_{2}(x)
so that a NE point
(x_{*},y_{*})

corresponds to a fixed point of
R
as follows:

(x_{*},y_{*})\in R(x_{*},y_{*}).

Notice that we can also define a
local NE where (1) and (2) in Definition
2.1
hold for all
x\in X
in an
open neighbourhood of
x_{*}
, resp. for all
y\in Y
in an
open neighbourhood of
y_{*}
. A local NE is isolated if there exists a neighbourhood
of
(x_{*},y_{*})
where this NE point is unique.
We have the following existence result, whose proof is based
on Kakutani’s fixed-point theorem
[
7
,
9
,
11
]
:

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume that
X
and
Y
are compact and convex subsets.
Let
f
and
g
be continuous, and assume that the map

x\mapsto f(x,y)
is a convex function of
x
, for each fixed
y\in Y
;
further assume that the map

y\mapsto g(x,y)
is a convex function of
y
, for each fixed
x\in X
.
Then there exists a Nash equilibrium.

In the case of a zero-sum game, the same theorem applies with

\phi(x,y):=f(x,y)=-g(x,y)
, assuming that the map

x\mapsto\phi(x,y)
is a convex function of
x
, for each fixed
y\in Y
,
and the map

y\mapsto\phi(x,y)
is a concave function of
y
, for each fixed
x\in X
.
In his original work
[
28
]
, von Neumann considered
f(x,y)=x^{T}Ay
; where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is called the payoff or game matrix.

We assume that
f
and
g
are both twice continuously differentiable.
Then, we can characterize the NE point by the following
necessary first-order equilibrium conditions; see, e.g.,
[
23
]
:

\partial_{x}f(x_{*},y_{*})=0,\qquad\partial_{y}g(x_{*},y_{*})=0.

(2.1)

We define the game gradient as the mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
given by

F(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}f(x,y)\\
\partial_{y}g(x,y)\end{pmatrix}.

(2.2)

For a shorter notation, we also write

w=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
which is intended as a row or column vector, depending on the context. Then, the necessary conditions (
2.1
) for
w_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
to be a Nash equilibrium read as
F(w_{*})=0
.

Necessary second-order equilibrium conditions are given by
the following positive semi-definiteness of the Hessians:

{\partial_{xx}^{2}}f(x_{*},y_{*})\succeq 0,\qquad{\partial_{yy}^{2}}g(x_{*},y_{*})\succeq 0.

(2.3)

If these inequalities are strict and the game gradient is zero,
then
(x_{*},y_{*})
is a Nash equilibrium; see
[
23
]
.
However, these conditions do not imply that the NE point is isolated.

Now, consider the mapping
H:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{(m+n)\times(m+n)}
defined by

H(x,y)=DF(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}{\partial_{xx}^{2}}f(x,y)&{\partial_{yx}^{2}}f(x,y)\\
{\partial_{xy}^{2}}g(x,y)&{\partial_{yy}^{2}}g(x,y)\end{pmatrix}.

(2.4)

This matrix is called the game Hessian, and
DF
is the Jacobian of the game
gradient
F
. We consider the decomposition of the game Hessian
as
H(x,y)=S(x,y)+A(x,y)
, where
S=S^{T}=(H+H^{T})/2
is the symmetric part of the
H
, and

A=-A^{T}=(H-H^{T})/2
represents its antisymmetric part.

We remark that, if
(x_{*},y_{*})
is a NE point, and
H(x_{*},y_{*})
is invertible, then this NE point is isolated
[
23
]
. For this reason, in
order to rule out the occurrence of a continuum of NE points, which are usually unstable,
we always assume that
H(x_{*},y_{*})
is invertible. Further, we assume that

H(x_{*},y_{*})
is positive semi-definite,
H(x_{*},y_{*})\succeq 0
, in the
sense that
\langle w,H(w_{*})\,w\rangle\geq 0
,
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
,where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denote the Euclidean scalar product and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
the corresponding Euclidean norm. Therefore, we define stable NE (SNE) points as follows
[
16
]
:

Definition 2.3
.

A NE point
w_{*}=(x_{*},y_{*})
is said to be stable, if
H(x_{*},y_{*})
is invertible and
positive semi-definite.

In our work, we focus on the convergence properties of the SGA and LRSGA methods
for computing SNE points. This is a large class of solutions which include
those arising in zero-sum games, as stated in the following lemma.
(See
[
1
]
for a similar statement.)

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
w_{*}=(x_{*},y_{*})
be a NE point of a zero-sum game, and

H(x_{*},y_{*})
be invertible. Then
w_{*}=(x_{*},y_{*})
is a stable NE point.

Proof.

Consider the decomposition
H(w_{*})=S(w_{*})+A(w_{*})
for the case

g(x,y)=-f(x,y)
. We have

S(x_{*},y_{*})=\begin{pmatrix}{\partial_{xx}^{2}}f(x_{*},y_{*})&0\\
0&{-\partial_{yy}^{2}}f(x_{*},y_{*})\end{pmatrix}.

We see that the symmetric part satisfies
\langle w,S(w_{*})\,w\rangle\geq 0

because of (
2.3
), that is,
{\partial_{yy}^{2}}f(x,y)\succeq 0
and
{-\partial_{yy}^{2}}f(x,y)={\partial_{yy}^{2}}g(x_{*},y_{*})\succeq 0
.
Then,
H(w_{*})
is positive semi-definite since

\langle w,H(w_{*})\,w\rangle=\langle w,S(w_{*})\,w\rangle+\langle w,A(w_{*})\,w\rangle=\langle w,S(w_{*})\,w\rangle\geq 0
. The claim follows from this result and the invertibility assumption.
∎

3
The SGA and LRSGA methods

We remark that the condition
H(w_{*})\succeq 0
becomes necessary for
obtaining convergence of several methods, even in the case of quadratic
loss functions. In particular, this is true for the SGA method, as illustrated by the following example.

Example 3.1
.

Consider a two-player NE game with

f(x,y)=x^{2}+3xy
and
g(x,y)=y^{2}+3xy
.
The point
(x_{*},y_{*})=(0,0)
satisfies
F(x^{*},y^{*})=\begin{pmatrix}2x_{*}+3y_{*}\\
2y_{*}+3x_{*}\end{pmatrix}=0
. Additionally, it holds

\partial_{xx}^{2}f(x_{*},y_{*})=2
and
\partial_{yy}^{2}g(x_{*},y_{*})=2
,
which are both strictly positive. Thus, the point
(x_{*},y_{*})
is a Nash equilibrium. The game Hessian is given by

H(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\
3&2\end{pmatrix},

which is invertible but not positive semi-definite. In fact, for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
the eigenvalues of
H(x,y)
are given by
\lambda_{1}=-1
and

\lambda_{2}=3
. Hence, the game Hessian is indefinite at the NE.

Since
A(x,y)=0
, the SGA method coincides with the GD method
(the matrix
\cal{M}_{SGA,k}
is the identity). We have the GD iterates given by

\begin{pmatrix}x_{k+1}\\
y_{k+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1-2\eta&-3\eta\\
-3\eta&1-2\eta\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{k}\\
y_{k}\end{pmatrix}.

In fact,
(\epsilon,-\epsilon)
is an eigenvector of the iteration matrix with corresponding eigenvalue
1+\eta>1
. Therefore, this sequence diverges for any initial choice
(x_{0},y_{0})=(\varepsilon,-\varepsilon)
with
\varepsilon\neq 0
and any choice of the parameters
\eta>0
.

Notice that the above is an example of potential game
[
17
]
, with potential
\phi(x,y)=x^{2}+y^{2}+3xy
, and a counterexample to the statement
in
[
1
,
16
]
that the GD method always converges for this class of games.

Next, we illustrate the SGA and LRSGA methods for determining SNE
points in two-players games. The SGA method is an iterative scheme given by

\begin{pmatrix}x_{k+1}\\
y_{k+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{k}\\
y_{k}\end{pmatrix}-\eta\,\begin{pmatrix}I_{m}&-\frac{\tau}{2}\,(\partial_{xy}^{2}f_{k}-\partial_{yx}^{2}g_{k}^{T})\\
-\frac{\tau}{2}\,(\partial_{yx}^{2}g_{k}-\partial_{xy}^{2}f_{k}^{T})&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}f_{k}\\
\partial_{y}g_{k}\end{pmatrix},

(3.1)

where
I_{m}
(resp.
I_{n}
) denotes the identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
(resp.
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
),
\eta>0
represents the stepsize, and
\tau>0
is auxiliary parameter.

The SGA method can be defined in terms of the game gradient
F
and game Hessian
H
,
and the decomposition
H(x,y)=S(x,y)+A(x,y)
, where
S:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{(m+n)\times(m+n)}
and
A:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{(m+n)\times(m+n)}
map
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the symmetric and anti-symmetric components of the game Hessian. For a general
two-players game, we have

S(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{xx}^{2}f(x,y)&\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{xy}^{2}f(x,y)+\partial_{yx}^{2}g(x,y)^{T})\\
\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{xy}^{2}f(x,y)^{T}+\partial_{yx}^{2}g(x,y))&\partial_{yy}^{2}g(x,y)\end{pmatrix}

and

A(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{xy}^{2}f(x,y)-\partial_{yx}^{2}g(x,y)^{T})\\
-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{xy}^{2}f(x,y)^{T}-\partial_{yx}^{2}g(x,y))&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then, using the notation
w_{k}:=(x_{k},y_{k})
, the SGA method can be written as follows:

w_{k+1}=w_{k}-\eta\,(I-\tau\,A(w_{k}))\,F(w_{k}),

(3.2)

where
I
represents the identity matrix in the appropriate Euclidean space.
By defining the operator
\Gamma_{\tau}:\mathbb{R}^{m+n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
as
w\mapsto(I-\tau\,A(w))\,F(w)
, we obtain the following operator form of the SGA method:

w_{k+1}=w_{k}-\eta\,\Gamma_{\tau}(w_{k}).

(3.3)

A main disadvantage in the SGA method is
the computation of the mixed derivatives
\partial_{xy}^{2}f_{k}
and
\partial_{xy}^{2}g_{k}
.
For large-size Nash games, the computation of these derivatives
can be problematic, especially in the context of neural networks where
back-propagation is involved, and our purpose is to investigate the possibility to efficiently
determine matrices

M_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
N_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
that
approximate
\partial_{xy}^{2}f_{k}
and
\partial_{yx}^{2}g_{k}
, respectively,
such that the convergence properties of the SGA method are preserved or even
enhanced.

For this purpose, we develop a least-change secant update method

[
8
,
29
]
to approximate the second order derivatives
D\partial_{x}f
and
D\partial_{y}g
and use those parts of these Jacobians, corresponding to
\partial^{2}_{xy}f
and
\partial^{2}_{yx}g
, for the computation of the new iterate
w_{k+1}=(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})
.

The rank-one least-change secant update method applied to
D\partial_{x}f
and
D\partial_{y}g

gives the updates:

\displaystyle\mu_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mu_{k}+\frac{(\partial_{x}f(w_{k+1})-\partial_{x}f(w_{k})-\mu_{k}(w_{k+1}-w_{k}))(w_{k+1}-w_{k})^{T}}{(w_{k+1}-w_{k})^{T}(w_{k+1}-w_{k})},

(3.4)

\displaystyle\nu_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\nu_{k}+\frac{(\partial_{y}g(w_{k+1})-\partial_{y}g(w_{k})-\nu_{k}(w_{k+1}-w_{k}))(w_{k+1}-w_{k})^{T}}{(w_{k+1}-w_{k})^{T}(w_{k+1}-w_{k})}.

(3.5)

Now, consider the block structures

\mu_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}(\mu_{k})_{1}&(\mu_{k})_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(m+n)}
with
(\mu_{k})_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
(\mu_{k})_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and
\nu_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}(\nu_{k})_{1}&(\nu_{k})_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(m+n)}
with
(\nu_{k})_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
,
(\nu_{k})_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Then, by inspection, one can see that the block
(\mu_{k})_{2}
corresponds
to
\partial_{xy}^{2}f
, and the block
(\nu_{k})_{1}
corresponds to
\partial_{yx}^{2}g
.
Therefore, we set
M_{k}=(\mu_{k})_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
N_{k}=(\nu_{k})_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
to define our low-rank SGA method in the
form (
1.4
), where the matrix
\cal{M}
is given by

\mathcal{M}_{LRSGA,k}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{m}&-\frac{\tau}{2}\,(M_{k}-N_{k}^{T})\\
-\frac{\tau}{2}\,(N_{k}-M_{k}^{T})&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

(3.6)

Clearly, we can also write the LRSGA method as follows:

w_{k+1}=w_{k}-\eta\,(I-\tau\,\alpha_{k})\,F(w_{k}),

(3.7)

where

\alpha_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{1}{2}\,(M_{k}-N_{k}^{T})\\
\frac{1}{2}\,(N_{k}-M_{k}^{T})&0\end{pmatrix}.

For the purpose of initialization, one can set

M_{0}=\partial_{xy}^{2}f(x_{0},y_{0})
and
N_{0}=\partial_{yx}^{2}g(x_{0},y_{0})
,
or choose random matrix initialization.

In comparison to the SGA method, our LRSGA approach has the great advantage
of constructing
M_{k}
and
N_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots
, by means of rank-one updates (with
\mathcal{O}((m+n)^{2})
additions and multiplications) instead of computing second-order derivatives at the current iterate. In particular, in the case of game problems involving
neural networks, where the computation of second-order derivatives involves
backpropagation processes, this advantage results in CPU times of orders
of magnitude smaller than in the SGA method.

On the other hand, the LRSGA method requires more storage, since the matrices
\mu_{k}
and
\nu_{k}
contain also approximations to the derivatives
\partial_{xx}^{2}f
and
\partial_{yy}^{2}g
, which are not used in the SGA method, but could be used in other iterative schemes.

4
Convergence analysis of the SGA method

In this section, we analyse the convergence properties of the SGA method
from the perspective of monotonicity properties. Therefore, our results
can be considered novel and complementary to the ones already presented in
[
10
]
and
[
16
]
. In our focus are the monotonicity properties of the operator

\Gamma_{\tau}
,
given by
\Gamma_{\tau}(w)=(I-\tau\,A(w))\,F(w)
, and for our discussion
it is convenient to recall some basic definitions and results in this field;
see, e. g.,
[
2
]
:

Definition 4.1
.

Consider the nonlinear operator
T:\text{Dom}(T)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We define
T
as:

(i)

Monotone if for any
x,y\in\text{Dom}(T)
,
\langle T(x)-T(y),x-y\rangle\geq 0
,

(ii)

h
-strongly monotone if there exists a constant
h>0
such that for any
x,y\in\text{Dom}(T)
,
\langle T(x)-T(y),x-y\rangle\geq h\,\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}
,

(iii)

\bar{\alpha}
-contractive if there exists a constant
\bar{\alpha}\in(0,1)
such that for any
x,y\in\text{Dom}(T)
,
\|T(x)-T(y)\|_{2}\leq\bar{\alpha}\,\|x-y\|_{2}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
F
be an
L
-Lipschitz continuous operator. Then, if
F
is
h
-strongly monotone, then for any
\eta\in\left(0,\frac{2h}{L^{2}}\right)
, the operator
I-\eta\,F
is
\bar{\alpha}
-contractive with
\bar{\alpha}=1-\eta\,(2h-\eta L^{2})
.

Our contribution to the understanding of
the metric properties of our operator
\Gamma_{\tau}
are
summarized in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
H=H(w)\succeq 0
be the game
Hessian of a two-players’ game at
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
, and

S=S(w)
and
A=A(w)
are its symmetric and its antisymmetric
parts, respectively.
The following properties hold:

(a)

Let
\tau\in\left(0,\frac{2}{\|S\|_{2}}\right)
if
S\neq 0
or
\tau\in(0,+\infty)
if
S=0.
Then, the operator
w\mapsto(I-\tau A)\,Hw
is
\kappa-
inversely strongly monotone with constant
\kappa=\frac{\tau}{2(1+\tau^{2}\|A\|_{2}^{2})}
.

(b)

In addition, suppose that
H
is invertible.
Let
\tau\in\left(0,\frac{2\lambda_{\min}}{\|S\|_{2}^{2}}\right)
if
S\neq 0
, where
\lambda_{\min}>0
be the minimum of the real parts of the eigenvalues of
H
, or
\tau\in(0,+\infty)
if
S=0
. Then the operator
w\mapsto(I-\tau A)\,Hw
is
h
-strongly monotone with constant
h=\frac{\tau}{2}\sigma_{\min}^{2}
, where
\sigma_{\min}>0
is the smallest singular value of
H
, that is, the matrix
(I-\tau A)H
is positive definite.

Proof.

Fix
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
A
is anti-symmetric, it holds
\langle Az,z\rangle=0
, and thus
\langle Sz,z\rangle=\langle Hz,z\rangle\geq 0
. Hence, we obtain

\langle(I-\tau A)Hz,z\rangle=\langle(S+A)z,(I+\tau A)z\rangle=\langle Sz,z\rangle+\tau\langle Sz,Az\rangle+\tau\|Az\|_{2}^{2}.

(4.1)

For the part (a), in the case
S=0
, equation (
4.1
) implies that

\langle(I-\tau A)Hz,z\rangle=\tau\|Az\|_{2}^{2}=\tau\|Hz\|_{2}^{2};

while for
S\neq 0
, we have
\langle Sz,z\rangle\geq\frac{\left\lVert Sz\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}
and thus, it holds

\langle(I-\tau A)Hz,z\rangle\geq\left(\frac{1}{\|S\|_{2}}-\frac{\tau}{2}\right)\|Sz\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\tau}{2}\left(\|Sz\|_{2}^{2}+2\langle Sz,Az\rangle+\|Az\|_{2}^{2}\right)+\frac{\tau}{2}\|Az\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
\tau\in\left(0,\frac{2}{\|S\|_{2}}\right)
, the inequality above gives

\langle(I-\tau A)Hz,z\rangle\geq\frac{\tau}{2}\|Sz+Az\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{\tau}{2}\|Hz\|_{2}^{2}.

In both cases,
S=0
and
S\neq 0
, we have

\langle(I-\tau A)Hz,z\rangle\geq\frac{\tau}{2(1+\tau^{2}\|A\|_{2}^{2})}\|(I-\tau A)Hz\|_{2}^{2},

since
\|z-\tau Az\|_{2}^{2}=\|z\|_{2}^{2}+\tau^{2}\|Az\|_{2}^{2}
, which implies
\|I-\tau A\|_{2}^{2}=1+\tau^{2}\|A\|_{2}^{2}
.

For part (b), starting from (
4.1
)
for
S=0
, we have

\langle(I-\tau A)Hz,z\rangle=\tau\|Az\|_{2}^{2}=\tau\|Hz\|_{2}^{2}\geq\tau\sigma_{\min}^{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{\tau\sigma_{\min}^{2}}{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Further, for
S\neq 0
from (
4.1
) and observing that
\langle Sz,z\rangle\geq\lambda_{\min}\|z\|_{2}^{2}
, we obtain

\langle(I-\tau A)Hz,z\rangle\geq\left(\lambda_{\min}-\frac{\tau\|S\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)\|z\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\tau}{2}\left(\|Sz\|_{2}^{2}+2\langle Sz,Az\rangle+\|Az\|_{2}^{2}\right)+\frac{\tau}{2}\|Az\|_{2}^{2}.

Now, noting that
\tau\in\left(0,\frac{2\lambda_{\min}}{\|S\|_{2}^{2}}\right)
and that

\|Sz\|_{2}^{2}+2\langle Sz,Az\rangle+\|Az\|_{2}^{2}=\|Hz\|_{2}^{2}\geq\sigma_{\min}^{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}^{2}>0,

we obtain

\langle(I-\tau A)Hz,z\rangle\geq\left(\lambda_{\min}-\frac{\tau\|S\|_{2}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\tau\sigma_{\min}^{2}}{2}\right)\|z\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{\tau\sigma_{\min}^{2}}{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

that is, the operator
w\mapsto(I-\tau A)Hw
is
h
-strongly monotone with
h=\frac{\tau\sigma_{\min}^{2}}{2}
.

∎

An immediate consequence of the previous lemma is a global convergence result for quadratic functions. In this case, the game Hessian is a constant matrix, while the game gradient
F

is affine, taking the form
w\mapsto Hw+b
for
H\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+n)\times(m+n)}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
f
and
g
be two quadratic functions such that the game Hessian
H
is positive
semi-definite and invertible. Then there exists a unique SNE point
w_{*}
. Moreover,
for any
\tau
as specified in Lemma
4.3
(b), and
\eta\in\left(0,\frac{\tau\sigma_{\min}^{2}}{(1+\tau^{2}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}^{2})\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\right)
, the sequence
\{w_{k}\}
generated by the SGA method (
3.3
) with arbitrary initial point
w_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
converges linearly to the SNE point
w_{*}
.

Proof.

Since both,
f
and
g
, are quadratic functions, the game Hessian
H

and
\partial_{xx}^{2}f
and
\partial_{yy}^{2}g
are constant matrices,
and the game gradient is given by
F(w)=H\,w+b
.
Because
H
is positive semi-definite and invertible, then the
equation
F(w)=0
has a unique solution
w_{*}
, which is a SNE point.

From our assumptions on
\tau
and Lemma
4.3
(b), we have that the operator
w\mapsto(I-\tau A)\,Hw
is
h
-strongly monotone with constant
h=\frac{\tau}{2}\sigma_{\min}^{2}
. Furthermore, since
F(w)=H\,w+b
,
it follows that the operator
\Gamma_{\tau}
is
h
-strongly monotone.
In fact, it holds

\displaystyle\langle(I-\tau A)F(w)-(I-\tau A)F(v),w-v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(I-\tau A)(Hw+b-Hv-b),w-v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(I-\tau A)H(w-v),w-v\rangle

\displaystyle\geq h\left\lVert w-v\right\rVert_{2}^{2},\quad w,v\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}.

Moreover, for
w,v\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
, it holds

\displaystyle\left\lVert(I-\tau A)F(w)-(I-\tau A)F(v)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert(I-\tau A)H(w-v)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert w-v\right\rVert_{2}.

Thus, the operator
\Gamma_{\tau}
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
L=\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{2}
.
Consequently, Proposition
4.2
implies that, for any
\eta\in\left(0,\frac{\tau\sigma_{\min}^{2}}{(1+\tau^{2}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}^{2})\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\right)
, the SGA operator
T_{\eta,\tau}
defined by
w\mapsto w-\eta\,(I-\tau\,A)\,F(w)
is
\bar{\alpha}
-contractive with
\bar{\alpha}=1-\eta(2h-\eta L^{2})
. Thus, the linear convergence of the sequence
\{w_{k}\}
defined by
w_{k+1}=T_{\eta,\tau}(w_{k})
to the unique fixed point of
T_{\eta,\tau}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
for any
w_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
follows from the Banach fixed point theorem (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
). This fixed point is
w_{*}
since it is the unique point satisfying
F(w_{*})=0
, thus
T_{\eta,\tau}(w_{*})=w_{*}
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

The bound
\frac{2\lambda_{min}}{\|S\|_{2}}
for the parameter
\tau
in the previous proposition, can be computed by exploring the spectrum of the game Hessian. In particular, the spectral norm
\|S\|_{2}
coincides with the maximum of the real parts of the eigenvalues of
S
. Hence, the ratio
\frac{2\lambda_{\min}}{\|S\|_{2}}
can be computed numerically by directly approximating the eigenvalues. Alternatively, an estimate can be obtained by using the Rayleigh quotient applied to
S
or the bounds provided by Gershgorin’s circle Theorem (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
).

Notice that in
[
16
]
, it is stated that the SGA method converges locally when the parameters
\eta
and
\tau
satisfy certain conditions and the game Hessian is positive definite. In the following proposition, we provide an alternative local convergence result that highlights the role of the constants.

Let
B_{R}(w_{*})=\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}\;|\;\left\lVert w-w_{*}\right\rVert_{2}<R\}
be
the open ball centred in
w_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
with radius
R>0
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
be an open subset
convex domain
, let
f\in C^{3}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
and
g\in C^{3}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
, and suppose that for some
w_{*}=(x_{*},y_{*})\in\Omega
it holds
F(w_{*})=0
, and
H(w_{*})
is positive semi-definite
and invertible. Then there exist
R>0
and
\bar{\eta}>0
such that the sequence
\{w_{n}\}
obtained by iteration (
3.3
) converges linearly to
w_{*}
whenever
w_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})\in B_{R}(w_{*})
,
\eta\in(0,\bar{\eta})
,
and
\tau
as specified in Lemma
4.3
(b).

Proof.

By the assumptions,
w_{*}
is a SNE point.
Let
h=\frac{\tau}{2}\sigma_{min}^{2}
. Then, according to Lemma
4.3
(b) the operator
w\mapsto(I-\tau A(w_{*}))H(w_{*})w
is
h
-strongly monotone. Thus, for any
w\in\Omega
it holds

\langle(I-\tau A(w_{*}))H(w_{*})(w-w_{*}),w-w_{*}\rangle\geq h\left\lVert w-w_{*}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Now, we choose
\varepsilon\in(0,h)
and notice that there exists

R>0
such that
B_{R}(w_{*})\subset\Omega
. Thus,
for any
w=(x,y)\in B_{R}(w_{*})
, it holds

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{\tau}(w)-\Gamma_{\tau}(w_{*})-D\Gamma_{\tau}(w_{*})(w-w_{*})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\Gamma_{\tau}(w)-((I-\tau A(w_{*}))H(w_{*})(w-w_{*}))\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\,\|w-w_{*}\|_{2}

since
F(w_{*})=0
and
D\Gamma_{\tau}(w_{*})=(I-\tau A(w_{*}))H(w_{*})
.

Then, it follows:

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma_{\tau}(w),w-w_{*}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(I-\tau A(w))F(w),w-w_{*}\rangle

(4.2)

\displaystyle\geq\langle(I-\tau A(w_{*}))H(w_{*})(w-w_{*}),w-w_{*}\rangle-\varepsilon\|w-w_{*}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq h\|w-w_{*}\|_{2}^{2}-\varepsilon\|w-w_{*}\|_{2}^{2}.

Hence, for
w\in B_{R}(w_{*})
, we have

\langle(I-\tau A(w))F(w),w-w_{*}\rangle\geq h_{*}\|w-w_{*}\|_{2}^{2},

for
h_{*}\in(0,h-\varepsilon]
.

Thus, for
w_{k}\in B_{R}(w_{*})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|w_{k+1}-w_{*}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|w_{k}-w_{*}-\eta(I-\tau A(w_{k}))F(w_{k})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|w_{k}-w_{*}\|_{2}^{2}-2\eta h_{*}\|w_{k}-w_{*}\|_{2}^{2}+\eta^{2}(1+\tau^{2}a^{2})\|F(w_{k})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq((1-2\eta h_{*})+\eta^{2}(1+\tau^{2}a^{2})L_{F}^{2})\|w_{k}-w_{*}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=q\|w_{k}-w_{*}\|_{2}^{2}

where
L_{F}
is the Lipschitz constant of
F
on
\overline{B_{R}(w_{*})}
,
a=\max_{w\in\overline{B_{R}(w_{*})}}\|A(w)\|_{2}^{2}
and
q=(1-\eta(2h_{*}-\eta(1+\tau^{2}a^{2})L_{F}^{2}))
. Notice that
q\in(0,1)
whenever
\eta\in\left(0,\frac{h_{*}}{2(1+\tau^{2}a^{2})L_{F}^{2}}\right)
.

This last inequality implies that
\{w_{n}\}\subset B_{R}(w_{*})
whenever
w_{0}\in B_{R}(w_{*})
, and that the sequence converges linearly to
w_{*}
.
∎

5
Convergence analysis of the LRSGA method

This section is devoted to the analysis of convergence of the LRSGA
method given by (
3.7
) with the low-rank updates
resulting from (
3.4
) and
(
3.5
). We provide a local convergence
result stating that the sequence of LRSGA iterates
converges to a SNE point if the start value is sufficiently
close to this point and assuming sufficiently accurate
approximations of the mixed second-order derivatives.

Specifically, we prove that the distance of the approximation
matrix
\alpha_{k}
to the anti-symmetric part of the game Hessian at
the equilibrium, that is,
A(w_{*})
, is bounded along the iterations. To obtain
this bound, in the following lemma, we provide bounds to the norms

\left\lVert\mu_{k+1}-D\partial_{x}f(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}
and

\left\lVert\nu_{k+1}-D\partial_{y}g(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}
in terms of

\left\lVert\mu_{k}-D\partial_{x}f(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}
,

\left\lVert\nu_{k}-D\partial_{y}g(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}
,

\left\lVert w_{k+1}-w_{*}\right\rVert_{2}
, and
\left\lVert w_{k}-w_{*}\right\rVert_{2}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
be a convex domain
such that
w_{k},w_{k+1},w_{*}\in\Omega
. Assume
that
D\partial_{x}f
, and
D\partial_{y}g
are Lipschitz
continuous in
\Omega
with Lipschitz constants
L_{D\partial_{x}f}
and

L_{D\partial_{y}g}
, respectively. Let
\mu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(m+n)}
and
\nu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(m+n)}
be given and assume
\mu_{k+1}
and
\nu_{k+1}
are computed by (
3.4
) and (
3.5
).
Then, it holds

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mu_{k+1}-D\partial_{x}f(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mu_{k}-D\partial_{x}f(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}

(5.1)

\displaystyle\quad+2L_{D\partial_{x}f}\max\{\left\lVert w_{k+1}-w_{*}\right\rVert_{2},\left\lVert w_{k}-w_{*}\right\rVert_{2}\}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nu_{k+1}-D\partial_{y}g(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\nu_{k}-D\partial_{y}g(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}

(5.2)

\displaystyle\quad+2L_{D\partial_{y}g}\max\{\left\lVert w_{k+1}-w_{*}\right\rVert_{2},\left\lVert w_{k}-w_{*}\right\rVert_{2}\}

Proof.

We show the inequality for
\mu_{k+1}
, the bound relative to
\nu_{k+1}
is obtained in a similar way.
The update (
3.4
) of
\mu_{k}
can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\mu_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mu_{k}\left(I_{m+n}-\frac{(w_{k+1}-w_{k})(w_{k+1}-w_{k})^{T}}{(w_{k+1}-w_{k})^{T}(w_{k+1}-w_{k})}\right)+\frac{(\partial_{x}f(w_{k+1})-\partial_{x}f(w_{k}))(w_{k+1}-w_{k})^{T}}{(w_{k+1}-w_{k})^{T}(w_{k+1}-w_{k})}.

By adding zero-terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mu_{k+1}-D\partial_{x}f(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\left(\mu_{k}-D\partial_{x}f(w_{*})\right)\left(I_{m+n}-\frac{(w_{k+1}-w_{k})(w_{k+1}-w_{k})^{T}}{(w_{k+1}-w_{k})^{T}(w_{k+1}-w_{k})}\right)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\lVert\left(D\partial_{x}f(w_{k})-D\partial_{x}f(w_{*})\right)\frac{(w_{k+1}-w_{k})(w_{k+1}-w_{k})^{T}}{(w_{k+1}-w_{k})^{T}(w_{k+1}-w_{k})}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\lVert\frac{\left(\partial_{x}f(w_{k+1})-\partial_{x}f(w_{k})-D\partial_{x}f(w_{k})(w_{k+1}-w_{k})\right)(w_{k+1}-w_{k})^{T}}{(w_{k+1}-w_{k})^{T}(w_{k+1}-w_{k})}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mu_{k}-D\partial_{x}f(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}+L_{D\partial_{x}f}\left\lVert w_{k}-w_{*}\right\rVert_{2}+\frac{1}{2}L_{D\partial_{x}f}\left\lVert w_{k+1}-w_{k}\right\rVert_{2},

where we have used
\left\lVert I_{m+n}-\frac{ss^{T}}{s^{T}s}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1
for
s\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
, and the Lipschitz continuity of
D\partial_{x}f
.
From
\left\lVert w_{k+1}-w_{k}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert w_{k+1}-w_{*}\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert w_{k}-w_{*}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 2\max\{\left\lVert w_{k+1}-w_{*}\right\rVert_{2},\left\lVert w_{k}-w_{*}\right\rVert_{2}\}
, we obtain (
5.1
).
∎

Next, we obtain a bound for
\left\lVert\alpha_{k}-A(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
w_{*}\in\Omega
and assume there is a
\delta>0
such that it holds
\left\lVert\mu_{k}-D\partial_{x}f(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\delta
and
\left\lVert\nu_{k}-D\partial_{y}g(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\delta
for matrices
\mu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(m+n)}
and
\nu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(m+n)}
. Then, the matrix
\alpha_{k}
defined in (
3.7
), with
M_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
being the right block of
\mu_{k}
and
N_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
being the left block of
\nu_{k}
, satisfies the inequality
\left\lVert\alpha_{k}-A(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\delta
.

Proof.

The spectral norm of a matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is defined by

\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\varrho(BB^{T})=\varrho(B^{T}B)=\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\lambda_{i}(B^{T}B),

where
\lambda_{i}(B^{T}B)
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, are the eigenvalues of
B^{T}B
.

Thus, for a matrix
C\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
it holds

\displaystyle\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}0&C\\
-C^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\varrho\begin{pmatrix}CC^{T}&0\\
0&C^{T}C\end{pmatrix}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\max\{\varrho(CC^{T}),\varrho(C^{\top}C)\}}=\sqrt{\varrho(CC^{\top})}=\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{2},

since the eigenvalues of a block-diagonal matrix are given by the eigenvalues of the matrices on the diagonal.

Notice that
\alpha_{k}-A(w_{*})
is an anti-symmetric matrix of the form
\begin{pmatrix}0&C\\
-C^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}
with

C=\frac{1}{2}(M_{k}-\partial_{xy}^{2}f(w_{*})-N_{k}^{T}+\partial_{yx}^{2}g(w_{*})^{T})
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\alpha_{k}-A(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\frac{1}{2}(M_{k}-\partial_{xy}^{2}f(w_{*})-N_{k}^{T}+\partial_{yx}^{2}g(w_{*})^{T})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}(\left\lVert M_{k}-\partial_{xy}^{2}f(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert N_{k}^{T}-\partial_{yx}^{2}g(w_{*})^{T}\right\rVert_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}(\left\lVert\mu_{k}-D\partial_{x}f(w_{*})\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\nu_{k}^{T}-D\partial_{y}g(w_{*})^{T}\right\rVert_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}(\delta+\delta)=\delta.

This estimate shows the claim.
∎

Next, we use these lemmata to obtain a local linear convergence result for our LRSGA scheme.
Let
w_{*}=(x_{*},y_{*})
be a SNE point.
We consider the map
T_{\eta,\tau}^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{m+n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
defined by

T_{\eta,\tau}^{*}:w\mapsto w-\eta(I-\tau A(w_{*}))\,F(w).

(5.3)

Notice that this map differs from the SGA operator
T_{\eta,\tau}
because

A
is evaluated at
w_{*}
and is supposed constant along the iterations.
Clearly,
w_{*}
is a fixed point of this map.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
be a convex domain and

w_{*}\in\Omega
be a SNE point. Let the map

6
Experiments on CLIP models

To illustrate and validate the theoretical convergence results derived in the previous sections, we consider a practical instance of a Nash equilibrium problem emerging in the training of neural architectures involving two interacting objectives. Specifically, we choose a Contrastive Language Image Pretraining (CLIP) model
[
22
]
due to its inherently multi-objective nature, where two separate neural networks, one encoding images and the other encoding textual descriptions, are jointly optimized to learn a shared latent representation space.

The training process of a CLIP architecture involves minimizing two distinct contrastive losses:
image-to-text loss
, which aligns image embeddings to their corresponding text embeddings, and
text-to-image loss
, which aligns text embeddings back to their corresponding image embeddings.
Unlike classical approaches that minimize the sum of both losses, we optimize each loss independently with respect to its corresponding network parameters (image or text encoder), constructing a non-zero-sum Nash game suitable for CGD, SGA, and LRSGA. This reformulation allows us to shift from a multi-objective setting to a competitive one.

Such nonzero-sum settings are typical of competitive scenarios
[
1
]
, and have also been explored in multitask learning when reformulated under game-theoretic frameworks
[
20
]
, making them representative of realistic optimization challenges in modern neural networks.

6.1
Model and experimental protocol

To empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed LRSGA optimization scheme, we designed a controlled experimental protocol inspired by the CLIP architecture. Specifically, our model comprises two neural networks, an ImageEncoder and a TextEncoder, whose architectural details are summarized in Figure
2
.

Figure 2
:
Architecture of the CLIP network used in the experiments.

Dataset

Our experiments use a modified version of the MNIST dataset, where numerical digit labels (from 0 to 9) are transformed into textual representations ("zero", "one", …, "nine"). Each textual label is encoded into numerical indices based on a defined character-to-index mapping. We construct a balanced subset containing 640 samples (64 per class), dividing it into training (60%), validation (20%) and test (20%) sets. During training, data are processed using mini-batches of size 16, meaning the model parameters are updated after computing gradients on small subsets (mini-batches) of 16 samples at a time.

Neural architecture details

The detailed structures of our ImageEncoder and TextEncoder modules are as follows:

•

ImageEncoder
: A convolutional neural network composed of two convolutional layers followed by a fully connected (FC) embedding layer. The output embedding dimension is fixed to 4, with ReLU activations after each convolutional layer, and embeddings are L2-normalized.

•

TextEncoder
: A neural network comprising a character-level embedding layer followed by two fully connected layers. Textual sequences (fixed to 8 characters) are embedded into 4-dimensional vectors, L2-normalized at output. Pixels of the images are rescaled between 0 and 1.

Loss function

Figure 3
:
Illustration of the similarity matrix between image and text embeddings for a batch of size N

To train this CLIP model, we employ a symmetric contrastive loss as in
[
22
]
, composed of two terms:

•

The Image-to-Text Loss
that encourages the model to assign a high probability to the correct text given an image.

•

The Text-to-Image Loss
that encourages the model to assign a high probability to the correct image given a text.

The similarity between image and text embeddings is computed through a
similarity matrix

logits
represented in Figure
3
, of which each entry is defined as:

\texttt{logits}_{i,j}=\frac{\langle\mathbf{I}_{i},\mathbf{T}_{j}^{\top}\rangle}{Temp}

where

\mathbf{I}_{i}
and
\mathbf{T}_{j}
represent the normalized embeddings of the
i
-th image and
j
-th text, respectively, and
Temp
is a temperature hyperparameter used to scale the distribution, which in
[
22
]
is optimized during training.
We remark that diagonal terms of the similarity matrix represent the correct image-text pairs, while off-diagonal terms correspond to mismatched pairs. In order to compute the image-to-text loss for a batch of size
N
, each row of the similarity matrix is passed through a softmax function, assigning a probability distribution over all text candidates given an image. The correct target for each image is assumed to be the text at the same index position within the batch. Accordingly, the target vector is defined as
\mathbf{y}=[1,\dots,N]
, which specifies that the
i
-th image is paired with the
i
-th text. This formulation enables the use of the standard cross-entropy loss, where the diagonal entry of each row is treated as the correct class. The resulting probability is then promoted by the loss when it is high, and penalized when it is low, obtaining:

{Loss}_{I}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}-\log\left(\frac{\exp(\texttt{logits}_{i,i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\texttt{logits}_{i,j})}\right)

The text-to-image loss
{Loss}_{T}
is computed analogously by transposing the similarity matrix
logits
.

Experimental procedure

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the proposed LRSGA optimizer, we conducted two distinct experiments:

In the first experiment, we compared the standard SGA optimizer (
1.6
) against its low-rank variant, LRSGA. The results demonstrate that LRSGA achieves comparable precision while significantly reducing computational time.

The second experiment compares LRSGA with the linearized version of CGD (
1.5
) (CGD). Similarly, LRSGA maintains precision levels equivalent to CGD, with considerable reductions in computational effort.

To ensure statistical robustness and fairness, each configuration was evaluated over multiple random seeds under identical training conditions. In our experiments, we compute the gradients based on mini-batches of the complete dataset. We refer to one pass through the complete data set as one epoch. All models were trained for the same number of epochs, using the same initialization scheme and batch size. The parameter
\eta
varied within
\{0.01,0.001,0.0001\}
, the temperature parameter was fixed at 0.09, and the auxiliary parameter
\tau
was set as
\tau=\frac{\eta}{100}
.
The optimizers were evaluated according to the following criteria:

•

Computation time
: Average CPU-time per epoch (seconds),

•

: Total CO

,

•

As CGD and SGA require high computation times, all training processes were halted after 150 epochs. Although potentially suboptimal, this setup suffices
to show that LRSGA performs at least on par with CGD (see Figures

7
Conclusions

A theoretical and numerical investigation
of the symplectic gradient adjustment (SGA) method and of a low-rank SGA (LRSGA)
method for efficiently solving two-objective optimization problems in the
framework of Nash games was presented. The SGA method
considerably improves upon the simple gradient method
by including second-order mixed derivatives computed at each iterate. However,
the use of second-order derivatives requires additional computational effort
in back-propagation processes, which is the main disadvantage of this method.
For this reason, a LRSGA method was developed where the approximation to
second-order mixed derivatives is obtained by rank-one updates.
The theoretical analysis presented in this work focused on novel convergence
estimates for the SGA method and the newly proposed LRSGA method,
including parameter bounds.
The superior computational complexity of the LRSGA method was observed in the training of a CLIP architecture, where the LRSGA method outperformed the SGA method with consistently lower CPU times while preserving comparable accuracy.

7.1
Future Work

It is noteworthy that, due to the dimensions of the similarity matrix and the computational times, the training is conducted using mini-batches. However, this approach is suboptimal for estimating second-order information through gradient differences between successive iterations. Various methodologies, as delineated, e.g., in
[
3
,
5
]
, can be explored in this context. As future work, we leave the possibility of conducting a more rigorous analysis of these approaches in order to develop a stochastic variant of LRSGA. Furthermore, selecting more appropriate values for the parameters
\eta
and
\tau
, along with an in-depth analysis of global convergence properties, are designated for future exploration.
