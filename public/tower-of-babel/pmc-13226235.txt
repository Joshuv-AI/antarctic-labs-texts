Piezoelectric cold atmospheric plasma modulates neurogenic cell proliferation and differentiation via cross-signaling
Authors: Haitao Zhang, Boyu Cai, Jiaojiao Li, Zhao Wang, Maoxiang Xu, Ding Li, Fei Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCold atmospheric plasma regulates cell fate by modulating cellular oxidative stress levels, making it a promising new therapy in the field of cell bioengineering. While the regulatory effects and potential molecular mechanisms of the novel piezoelectric cold atmospheric plasma (Piezo-CAP, PiezoBrush PZ3 handheld device, Relyon Plasma, Germany) on the fate of neurogenic cells remain unclear. We utilized C17.2 neural stem cells, rat astrocytes, and SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells to evaluate the impact of Piezo-CAP on cell proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation through CCK-8 assay, Calcein AM/PI double staining, immunofluorescence, and western blot techniques. The signal network of Piezo-CAP governing neurogenic cell proliferation and differentiation was thoroughly examined by the merging of RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis. The results demonstrated that Piezo-CAP has a dual regulatory influence on neurogenic cell fate, dependent on specific parameters and cell types. Significantly, Piezo-CAP enables the bidirectional differentiation of C17.2 neural stem cells into neurons and glial cells. The described effects are mechanistically associated with the buildup of intracellular reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) and may be enhanced by the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Transcriptome research further confirms that Piezo-CAP can influence signaling pathways related to the maintenance of pluripotency and differentiation, including JAK-STAT, Wnt, and Hippo signaling. In conclusion, Piezo-CAP, as an effective physical stimulation method, may accurately influence the destiny of neurogenic cells through essential signaling pathways facilitated by RONS, including PI3K/AKT. This research provides actual evidence and innovative strategies for the application of Piezo-CAP in neural therapy and neural tissue engineering.Keywords: Cold atmospheric plasma, neural stem cells, cell fate, PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, neural regeneration, reactive oxygen and nitrogen species

Introduction

Cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) is a partly ionized gas produced at near-room temperature by a high-voltage (1–10 kV), low-frequency (1–100 kHz) source, and is usually delivered through an inert gas jet (Jheng et al., 2023, Souza et al., 2023). Its therapeutic potential is attributed to a unique composition of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), charged particles, and electromagnetic emissions, which facilitate multimodal biological interactions (Yan et al., 2021, Yan et al., 2020). In oncology and tissue engineering, a key advantage of CAP is its selective bioactivity: low doses target vulnerabilities and induce senescence in malignant cells while promoting the regeneration of healthy tissue. This dichotomy arises from dose-dependent RONS production, which remodels the extracellular matrix and stimulates growth factor secretion without significant thermal damage (Li et al., 2023, Khalaf et al., 2024). Its key entry points include, but are not limited to: promoting immunogenic cell death (Dai et al., 2023), overcoming tumor cell resistance (Köritzer et al., 2013), selectively killing tumor metastases (Wang et al., 2013), regulating the fate of specific stem cells (Tan et al., 2020), and surface modification or coating deposition of surgical implants (Tan et al., 2021). Although recent evidence demonstrates that piezoelectric cold atmospheric plasma (Piezo-CAP) ameliorates spinal cord injury (SCI) by reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS), suppressing apoptosis, and promoting axonal regeneration via antioxidative mechanisms (Zhu et al., 2024), its therapeutic efficacy and safety are influenced by multiple variables. Therefore, further research is required to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms and develop refined parameter control strategies to guide safe, effective clinical translation.

Neural repair and regeneration constitute a critical therapeutic goal for central nervous system disorders—such as Parkinson's disease (PD), traumatic brain injury (TBI), SCI, and stroke—where limited endogenous repair often results in permanent functional deficits (Sun et al., 2021). Current strategies aim to stimulate endogenous neural progenitors through growth factor administration or genetic manipulation to promote neurogenesis and oligodendrogenesis (Ohori et al., 2006). However, these approaches are hampered by inefficient progenitor activation, poor neuronal maturation, and inadequate integration into existing neural circuits, which collectively limit functional recovery (Hao et al., 2024, Wang and Zhang, 2018). CAP has emerged as a promising tool for neural regeneration, demonstrated to enhance the directed differentiation of neural stem cells. Nevertheless, the precise mechanistic basis for this effect remains unclear. A central question involves the dual role of RONS, which at physiological levels mediate redox signaling for axon guidance and synaptic plasticity but, under chronic oxidative stress in degenerative conditions (e.g., retinal degeneration), can trigger neuronal apoptosis through protein/DNA oxidation and neuroinflammation (Martínez-Gil et al., 2022, Jiang et al., 2017). Consequently, the clinical translation of CAP in neurology—for applications such as neural stem cell therapy post-TBI, surface modification of intracranial implants, and bio-scaffold development for SCI—remains limited and represents a critical avenue for future investigation.

The C17.2 neural stem cell line is a cornerstone of neural tissue engineering, prized for its dual capacity for self-renewal and multipotent differentiation into all neural lineages (Bechara et al., 2011). Its utility is exemplified in SCI models, where engraftment facilitates host axonal growth and functional recovery (Xicoy et al., 2017). Complementary glial models, such as the rat astrocyte (RA) cell line, are critical for studying mechanisms related to ionic balance and blood-brain barrier integrity (Xicoy et al., 2017). Meanwhile, the SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cell line, while a mainstay in PD research, provides a scalable platform for neurotoxicity screening and neuronal differentiation studies (Hao et al., 2024). The application of Piezo-CAP to these models can reveal disease-specific mechanisms. For instance, CAP-induced modulation of mitochondrial function in SH-SY5Y cells could elucidate PD-specific metabolic vulnerabilities (Hao et al., 2024). In C17.2 neural stem cells, Piezo-CAP stimulation may synergize with electroconductive nanomaterials to potentiate neuronal differentiation and axonal growth, which is critical for SCI repair (Xicoy et al., 2017). Furthermore, CAP-mediated astrocyte activation might mitigate neuroinflammation following traumatic injury. However, the specific molecular mechanisms and clinical translational value of CAP across these trans-neuronal models remain largely unexplored, necessitating further investigation using integrated omics approaches.

Here, we extensively examined the various biological impacts of Piezo-CAP in three neurogenic cell lines. Treatment with Piezo-CAP at specific parameters selectively promoted neurogenic cell proliferation and inhibited apoptosis. Further studies showed that Piezo-CAP could induce C17.2 cells to differentiate into neural cells, accompanied by the accumulation of RONS within the cells. From a mechanistic perspective, piezoelectric CAP activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby participating in the regulation of cell fate. Through RNA sequencing and bioinformatics enrichment analysis, we revealed the underlying molecular networks and signaling pathways behind the effects of Piezo-CAP. These results not only provide a cross-cell line perspective for understanding how Piezo-CAP regulates neurogenic cell behavior but also indicate that it influences cell proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation processes by regulating RNOS levels and key signaling pathways. This study offers an intellectual and experimental groundwork for the prospective use of Piezo-CAP in treating neurological illnesses, including stroke and neurodegenerative diseases.

Methods

Cell culture

The American Type Culture Collection provided the human neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y, rat astrocyte cell line RA, and murine neural progenitor cell line C17.2. The cells were cultivated in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, #11965092), which included 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, #15140122) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, #10270106). Every culture was kept in a humidified environment with 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. Cells were subcultured using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, #25200056) after they had attained around 80% confluency.

Piezo-CAP device and treatment

The plasma device utilized in this work is an ambient air-dependent apparatus conceived and built by Relyon Plasma GmbH (PiezoBrush PZ3 handheld device, Regensburg). Piezo-CAP was generated using a custom-built Piezo-CAP device operating with a temperature of less than 50 °C with Argon (Ar; 99.999% purity, Shanghai Chunyu Specialty Gases Co. Ltd) used as the carrier gas (Fig. 1A–D) (Wang et al., 2024, Aladağ and Ayaz, 2024). (Fig. 1E). Cells seeded in 6-well plates (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) containing 2 mL of fresh culture medium were exposed to the plasma jet at a fixed nozzle-to-sample distance of 10–15 mm. This standardized liquid volume was maintained to ensure consistent RONS dosage delivery. Treatment durations (0, 5, 10, 15, 30 s and 2, 5, 10, 15 min) were precisely controlled using a digital timer (Fig. 1F). Sham control groups received argon gas flow without plasma activation. All experiments were conducted at room temperature.

Illustration of the PiezoBrush PZ3 handheld device. (A, B) Content and portability of the Piezo-CAP kit. (C) The exterior design of Piezo-CAP. (D) The typical parameter for the PiezoBrush PZ3. (E) Surface modification of implant surface using CAP with jet nozzle with Ar gas. (F) Indirect treatment of neural cells culture in various media.

Cell counting kit-8 assay

Cell viability of C17.2, RA, and SH-SY5Y cells following Piezo-CAP treatment was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo Laboratories). After being plated at 5 × 10 ³ cells/well in 96-well plates, the cells were treated with CAP and incubated for a full day. Following the addition of 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent to each well, the wells were incubated for two hours at 37 °C. Utilizing a Tecan Infinite F50 microplate reader, absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Calcein AM/PI double staining

Cell viability and death were evaluated using Calcein AM and propidium iodide (PI). After treatment, cells were washed with PBS and stained for 15 min at 37 °C with 2 µM Calcein AM (ThermoFisher) and 1 µg/mL PI (ThermoFisher) in PBS. Fluorescence imaging was performed on a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 inverted microscope using FITC (Ex 488 nm/Em 517 nm) and TRITC (Ex 535 nm/Em 617 nm) filter sets.

Neural Differentiation Analysis

C17.2 neural progenitor cells were cultivated in DMEM enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The medium was renewed every 2–3 days, and cells were subcultured at 80% confluency at a 1:3 ratio. To induce differentiation, cells were exposed to Piezo-CAP via an Ar-assisted jet nozzle fixed at 15 mm above the culture surface for 5 or 10 s. Differentiation was monitored daily by phase-contrast microscopy. Images were captured at 48 h, and cells exhibiting one or more neurites were classified as differentiated. The differentiation rate was calculated as the proportion of neurite-bearing cells among a minimum of 100 cells counted per condition across several fields.

Western blot assay

Protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, #P1005) were used in conjunction with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, #P0013B) to extract the total protein. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #23225). Samples (20 µg per lane) were separated on 8–10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels and transferred to 0.45 µm PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, #IPFL00010). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST, incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech, SA00001–2, 1:5000). The primary antibodies used were: β-III-tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich, T8660, 1:1000), GFAP (Proteintech, 23935–1-AP, 1:1000), PI3K (Cell Signaling Technology, 4292S, 1:1000), AKT (Proteintech, 10176–2-AP, 1:1000), p-PI3K (Santa Cruz, sc-1637, 1:1000), p-AKT (Proteintech, 66444–1-Ig, 1:1000), and PCNA (Proteintech, 10205–2-AP, 1:1000). Protein bands were imaged with a ChemiDoc MP System (Bio-Rad) and densitometric analysis of band intensities were analyzed with ImageJ software (v1.54).

Reactive oxygen species (H₂O₂) detection

To evaluate the generation efficiency of reactive oxygen species induced by the low-temperature plasma, the concentration of H2O2 was quantified using a commercial assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, #A064–1–1). Briefly, 20 mL of DMEM, and exposed to the plasma jet for 1 min at room temperature. The treatment was performed without cells to strictly assess the chemical accumulation of RNOS. Immediately after treatment, a 100 µL aliquot was collected and analyzed according to the manufacturer's protocol. The absorbance was measured at 405 nm using the Tecan Infinite F50. The final concentration of H2O2 was calculated based on the standard curve.

Reactive nitrogen species detection

The generation of nitric oxide (NO) in the cell-free medium was assessed using the Griess Reagent System (Promega, #G2930). Briefly, 100 µL of the plasma-treated medium was transferred to a 96-well plate and mixed with 100 µL of sulfanilamide solution and 100 µL of N-1-naphthylethylenediamine dihydrochloride solution. Following a 10-minute incubation in darkness at room temperature, the absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader. The concentration of NO was quantified and calculated based on a sodium nitrite standard curve.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, #15596026) from three replicates per group. RNA libraries were prepared, sequenced, and preliminarily analyzed by 10 K Genomics (Shanghai, China). mRNA was extracted with poly(T) magnetic beads, and cDNA was generated with random hexamer primers. Libraries were quantified and sequenced on an Illumina platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Reads were aligned to the GRCh38.p13 genome using STAR (v2.7.10a), and gene counts were obtained with featureCounts (v2.0.3).

Differential expression analysis was performed using the "edgeR" package, with genes considered significantly altered if they met the thresholds of p < 0.05 and an absolute fold-change ≥ 2.0. A volcano plot was generated using the "ggplot2" package in R. Heatmaps were plotted using the online OmicShare platform (https://www.omicshare.com/tools). Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis was performed to interpret the functional implications of the observed transcriptional changes. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathways was carried out using the 'gseKEGG' function from the "clusterProfiler" package, and results were visualized with the 'gseaplot2' function.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. All data are expressed as Mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired Student’s t-test was employed. For multiple group comparisons, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was performed to determine significant differences. For RNA-seq data, the Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied to correct for the false discovery rate (FDR). A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Level of significance is indicated as follows: * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001.

Results

Piezo-CAP treatment selectively regulates fate of neural cells

CAP can indirectly influence cell fate by altering the milieu around the cells and directly affecting cell fate through the activation of adjacent stem cells (Tan et al., 2020). To evaluate the generalized impact of Piezo-CAP on the growth of various neural cell types, the effect of CAP on the proliferation of C17.2, RA, and SH-SY5Y neural cells was determined by CCK-8 assay. The results revealed a dual regulatory role of CAP, contingent on the treatment parameters. Optimal pro-proliferative effects were identified for each cell line: the argon-assisted jet nozzle (30% power, 5 s) increased C17.2 cell proliferation to 141.50 ± 13.91% (p < 0.001 vs. 0 s control, Fig. 2C), the nearfield nozzle (70% power, 5 s) enhanced RA cells to 167.78 ± 45.18% (p < 0.001 vs. 0 s control, Fig. 2B), and the argon-assisted jet nozzle (100% power, 10 s) boosted SH-SY5Y cells to 128.53 ± 36.00% (p < 0.001 vs. 0 s control, Figure S1B). In stark contrast, proliferation was most significantly inhibited under alternative parameters: nearfield nozzle (100% power, 15 s) for C17.2 cells (63.87 ± 4.21%, p < 0.001 vs. 0 s control, Fig. 2A), Ar-assisted jet nozzle (30% power, 30 s) for RA cells (7.23 ± 3.15%, p < 0.001 vs. 0 s control, Fig. 2D), and Ar-assisted jet nozzle (50% power, 15 s) for SH-SY5Y cells (Figure S1B). These results reveal that the different effects of Piezo-CAP on cell fate are cell type-specific and CAP parameter-selective, with both pro-proliferative and anti-proliferative outcomes. Thus, the application of Piezo-CAP to promote neural cell proliferation requires precise fine-tuning of its parameters.

Piezo-CAP treatment inhibits apoptosis in C17.2 and RA cells

Considering that Piezo-CAP exerts potential neurorestorative benefits by promoting the proliferation of C17.2 and RA cells, we further investigated its role in regulating apoptosis in neuronal cells via Calcein-AM/PI double staining. As shown in Figs. 3A and 3C, after 24 h of Multigas-Ar CAP treatment under optimal proliferation-promoting conditions, the cell viability of C17.2 cells was significantly elevated from 77.17 ± 1.94% to 91.33 ± 1.71% (p < 0.001), accompanied by a marked reduction in PI-positive dead cells. Similarly, for RA cells (Figs. 3B and 3D), Nearfield CAP treatment significantly increased cell viability from 88.77 ± 3.31% to 98.17 ± 0.42% (p < 0.001), with a substantial decrease in apoptotic cells. These results collectively demonstrate that the pro-proliferative effect of CAP on neurogenic cells is tightly coupled with synchronous inhibition of apoptosis, confirming its dual beneficial effect on neuronal survival and growth.

Piezo-CAP treatment induces differentiation in C17.2 cells

The C17.2 neural progenitor cell line is widely used in regenerative medicine studies due to its capacity to differentiate into specialized neuronal and glial lineages. To evaluate the potential of Piezo-CAP in directing neural stem cell differentiation, we analyzed morphological and molecular changes in C17.2 cells at 48 h post-treatment under optimized conditions. Phase-contrast microscopy (Fig. 4A) and quantitative assessment of differentiation (Fig. 4B) revealed that Piezo-CAP treatment significantly enhanced neurite outgrowth and branching complexity compared to the control group. The proportion of differentiated cells increased from 53.40 ± 6.38% in untreated controls to 81.49 ± 2.83% and 78.15 ± 3.90% in CAP-treated groups (p < 0.01 for both). Consistent with these morphological changes, western blot analysis (Fig. 4C) demonstrated upregulation of β-tubulin III, a neuronal marker, and GFAP, an astrocytic marker. Notably, the elevation in GFAP expression suggests that Piezo-CAP also promotes differentiation toward the glial lineage (Zhang et al., 2017). Collectively, these data indicate that Piezo-CAP enhances the multi-differentiation potential of C17.2 cells.

Piezo-CAP treatment increases the accumulation of RONS within cells

Key components of CAP include RONS, ions and electrons, and ultraviolet photons (Yan et al., 2021). We examined the two most produced reactive substances of Piezo-CAP, H2O2 and NO, to represent reactive oxygen and reactive nitrogen, respectively. By measuring H₂O₂ and NO levels in the culture medium after CAP treatment, it was found that at a processing time of 15 min under identical power conditions, the nearfield CAP group and the jet nozzle group accumulated H₂O₂ concentrations of 68.49 ± 4.06 μM and 103.88 ± 0.91 μM respectively (Fig. 4D, E), while NO concentrations were 257.56 ± 1.85 μM and 205.93 ± 0.77 μM respectively (Fig. 4F, G). The results indicate that H₂O₂ and NO concentrations generated in the culture medium positively correlate with CAP treatment time, though the extent of different RONS production varies. Moreover, prolonged CAP treatment did not necessarily enhance cell proliferation and differentiation, suggesting that factors beyond H₂O₂ and NO production—such as other mechanisms involving electrons, ions, photons, and free radicals—may also play crucial roles in regulating cellular fate.

Piezo-CAP treatment attenuates the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is critically involved in regulating neuronal cell behaviors such as proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, and metabolism (Wang et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2022). To determine whether Piezo-CAP influences neurogenic cell fate through this pathway, we examined the expression and phosphorylation of key signaling components in C17.2 and RA cells following exposure to CAP under optimal conditions. Western blot analysis (Fig. 5A–C) showed that Piezo-CAP treatment significantly increased the phosphorylation levels of both PI3K and AKT, as reflected by elevated p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT ratios. In addition, protein levels of PCNA, a marker of cell proliferation, were markedly enhanced. These results suggest that Piezo-CAP promotes neural stem cell proliferation and differentiation, at least in part, through activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade.

RNA sequencing reveals the potential molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of Piezo-CAP treatment

To elucidate the impact of CAP on neural stem cell proliferation and differentiation, we conducted RNA microarray profiling of CAP-exposed neural stem cells. Transcriptomic data were subjected to differential expression analysis using established bioinformatics pipelines. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated distinct clustering of experimental groups (Fig. 6A), confirming reproducibility of the dataset. Differential gene expression profiling identified 14,655 transcripts with altered expression levels, of which 479 exhibited statistical significance (p < 0.05) following multiple testing correction (Fig. 6B, C). Heatmaps showed that positively regulated genes were enriched for genes associated with the PI3K/AKT pathway (Gsta3, Srxn1, Osgin1), which have been reported to promote osteoblast differentiation. Overexpression of Mdm2 resulted in increased P53 inactivation, which in turn inhibited apoptosis and cell cycle arrest. Similarly, we observed significant up-regulation of pro-survival genes such as Birc5, Bcl2l1 (BclxL) and, conversely, down-regulation of apoptosis-inducing genes such as Cenpm, Bcl2l11 (Bim). Creb3 and Timeless are involved in cell proliferation and migration, tumor suppression, and inflammatory gene expression. Cdc6 is an essential protein for initiating cellular DNA replication, and Cdc6 downregulation implies a decrease in neural stem cell stemness.

We performed gene GO set enrichment analysis to further explore the biological processes affected by CAP treatment. Bubble plots showed significant enrichment of entries related to multicellular biological development, nitrogen compound metabolism, anatomical structure development, and developmental processes in the CAP-treated group (Fig. 7A-D). From the top12 entries, CAP treatment mainly affected protein binding (GO:0005515), metabolic processes (GO:0008152), organic compound metabolism (GO:0071704), and multicellular biogenesis (GO:0007275) in neural stem cells. The results indicate that the biological effects of CAP-treated cells were significant and extensive, potentially via the modulation of protein synthesis and metabolic processes to enhance the proliferation and differentiation of neurogenic cell. Hierarchical clustering of the top 20 enriched GO terms based on semantic similarity revealed four distinct functional clusters, with the metabolic process cluster (including cellular, organic substance, and primary metabolic processes) showing the highest enrichment significance, followed by clusters related to intracellular structure/protein binding and developmental regulation (Fig. 7E).

Bioinformatics enrichment analysis of the signaling pathways involved in the effects of Piezo-CAP treatment

KEGG analysis indicated that Piezo-CAP treatment significantly altered several signaling pathways implicated in stem cell regulation, including the JAK/STAT, Wnt, MAPK, and Hippo signaling pathways, as well as pathways involved in pluripotency maintenance (Fig. 8A, B). Among these, the Wnt pathway—known to govern stem cell pluripotency, organ development, and tissue regeneration—exhibited functional convergence with the Hippo and Notch pathways. These pathways collectively integrate diverse signals to orchestrate the transition from stemness to terminal differentiation. Furthermore, enrichment in axon guidance pathways corroborated the pro-differentiation effects of CAP on neural stem cells. Notably, the PI3K/AKT, Wnt, and Notch pathways displayed evidence of synergistic interaction, reflecting their shared role in balancing stem cell self-renewal and differentiation. KEGG topology analysis also identified multiple downstream targets within these pathways that were significantly enriched following CAP treatment (Figures S2, S3). At the KEGG Level 2 classification (Fig. 8C), the most represented categories included signal transduction, signaling molecules and interaction, development and regeneration, transport and catabolism, and cell growth and death. We performed GSEA to further elucidate the mechanistic basis of CAP-induced phenotypic changes. As shown in Figs. 8D and 8E, CAP treatment significantly activated the JAK/STAT, MAPK, mTOR, and NF-kappa B signaling pathways, while suppressing processes related to ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling and cellular senescence. These pathways represent plausible mechanistic targets for CAP-mediated effects and warrant further investigation.

Discussion

Neural differentiation is governed by complex cell fate determination mechanisms, with genetic and pharmacological interventions receiving increasing attention as potential therapies for neurological disorders (Pajarillo et al., 2019, Xiong et al., 2019). This study establishes a direct link between the physicochemical stimulation delivered by Piezo-CAP and key biological responses in neural cells—specifically, the enhancement of proliferation and differentiation. Our work introduces three major innovations relative to existing CAP research. First, we employed a Piezo-CAP system with a defined gas composition and discharge configuration, wherein optimized electrical parameters enable precise control over neurogenic cell fate, offering improved selectivity compared to conventional CAP setups (Wang et al., 2013, Yan et al., 2017). Second, by systematically evaluating multiple cell models—including the C17.2 neural stem cell line, RA neuronal precursor cells, and SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells—we provide cross-lineage validation of its multifunctional efficacy. Third, through integrative analysis, we have connected cellular phenotypes (proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation) with underlying molecular mechanisms (e.g., signaling pathway activation), thereby elucidating the biological basis for CAP-mediated regulation of neurogenic cell fate (Motaln et al., 2021).

Neurological injuries and degenerative diseases, characterized by neuronal loss, apoptosis, and limited regenerative capacity, represent pressing clinical challenges requiring urgent resolution. Neurological injuries such as TBI, SCI, and ischemic stroke are characterized by extensive loss of neurons and glial cells due to ischemia, hypoxia, or inflammatory cascades (Zhu et al., 2024). Similarly, neurodegenerative diseases including Alzheimer's and Parkinson's are driven in part by progressive neuronal apoptosis (Liu et al., 2021). An ideal therapeutic strategy should therefore simultaneously promote neuronal survival and proliferation while guiding the differentiation of functional neural cells (VON Woedtke et al., 2019). In the context of sensorineural hearing loss, although cochlear implant electrical stimulation can support spiral ganglion neuron (SGN) axonal growth, the limited regenerative capacity of residual neurons remains a major challenge (Cuenca-Ortolá et al., 2022). Piezo-CAP-based surface biotechnology may address this by enhancing SGN proliferation and differentiation both in situ and on implant surfaces, potentially through direct modulation of the tissue–implant interface. This approach could be combined with other treatments, such as fibroblast growth factor administration, to improve the outcomes of cochlear implantation (Xiong et al., 2019, Li et al., 2016, Tan et al., 2013). In this study, systematic assessment across three neural cell models revealed that Piezo-CAP’s effects are cell-type-specific, parameter-dependent, and involve crosstalk among multiple signaling pathways. This multidimensional selectivity underscores the importance of tailoring CAP parameters to specific clinical contexts, thereby advancing the potential for personalized plasma-based therapies (Braný et al., 2020).

During C17.2 differentiation, we observed characteristic morphological changes indicative of neural maturation, including increased neurite branching and elongation, alongside elevated expression of β-tubulin III and GFAP. The upregulation of β-tubulin III, a marker of mature neurons, confirms that Piezo-CAP promotes neuronal differentiation (Cui et al., 2014, He et al., 2019). Concurrent elevation of GFAP, an astrocyte marker, suggests that the treatment also supports glial lineage specification (Weinstein et al., 1991). RNA sequencing analysis further indicated that Piezo-CAP modulates the expression of genes involved in neural development, axon guidance, and JAK/STAT signaling, pointing to potential epigenetic mechanisms underlying these effects. These findings align with previous reports that CAP influences neuronal differentiation through ROS-mediated pathways, including Trk/Ras/ERK signaling (Tan et al., 2020, Yan et al., 2019, Mitra et al., 2020). Together, these results support the translational potential of Piezo-CAP in clinical applications, such as designing implant surface modifications that enhance SGN synaptic outgrowth and modulate the neuronal microenvironment, thereby accelerating auditory cortex maturation post-implantation and enabling more precise regenerative interventions (Tan et al., 2021, Tan and Al-Rubeai, 2021).

At the mechanistic level, this study demonstrates that Piezo-CAP significantly increases levels of H₂O₂ and NO in neural cells. Notably, the enhancement of cell proliferation did not correlate linearly with RONS concentrations, consistent with the documented dual role of RONS: at moderate levels, they promote proliferation, differentiation, and survival, whereas excessive amounts trigger oxidative stress and cell death (Yan et al., 2017, Mitra et al., 2020). By carefully modulating parameters such as discharge power, exposure duration, and nozzle configuration, we maintained RONS within a therapeutic range that supports neuronal development without inducing cytotoxicity, highlighting the dose-responsive nature and biosafety of this approach (Yan et al., 2015). As the upstream signaling mediators, RONS activate multiple downstream pathways. Western blot and KEGG analyses confirmed that Piezo-CAP selectively activates the PI3K/AKT pathway in C17.2 and RA cells. This is consistent with earlier studies showing that PI3K/AKT signaling promotes axonal growth by reducing proteasome-mediated degradation and enhancing ERK/S6K activity, thereby facilitating differentiation (Jin et al., 2018, Lee et al., 2016). Altogether, our results establish PI3K/AKT activation as a central mechanism through which Piezo-CAP coordinately enhances both proliferation and differentiation (Eves et al., 1998, Xie et al., 2019).

While our study identified the PI3K/AKT pathway as a primary responder to Piezo-CAP, the RNA-seq data also highlighted enrichment in Wnt and Hippo signaling pathways. We hypothesize that PI3K/AKT may act as a central hub orchestrating a "cross-signaling" network. For instance, AKT can potentiate Wnt signaling by phosphorylating and inactivating GSK-3β, thereby stabilizing β-catenin to drive proliferative gene expression in neural stem cells (Zhang et al., 2017). Concurrently, AKT-mediated phosphorylation can regulate the activity of YAP, a core Hippo pathway effector, influencing its nuclear translocation and transcriptional output (Lv et al., 2024). This integrated crosstalk likely underpins plasma's coordinated modulation of neurogenic cell fate. Transcriptomic profiling also revealed significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes in developmental and regenerative pathways—including JAK/STAT, MAPK, and mTOR—following Piezo-CAP treatment. For instance, Chiba et al. reported that the neuroprotective peptide Humanin activates the JAK2/STAT3 pathway to counteract Aβ toxicity, enhance cholinergic function, and improve memory deficits (Chiba et al., 2009). Nussinov et al. demonstrated that MAPK signaling integrates cues from growth factors such as EGF and PDGF to drive neural precursor proliferation and direct neuronal-glial fate decisions (Nussinov et al., 2023). Similarly, Romanyuk et al. showed that mTOR regulates protein synthesis and metabolic balance, enabling neural stem cells to exit quiescence and initiate differentiation (Romanyuk et al., 2024). Collectively, these pathways form an interconnected regulatory network through which Piezo-CAP coordinates neurogenic cell fate via sophisticated molecular crosstalk (Nussinov et al., 2023, Dai et al., 2014, Wu et al., 2024). Given that Piezo-CAP influences a broad spectrum of signaling and metabolic mechanisms, future work should focus on elucidating the dynamic interactions within this network to fully exploit its pleiotropic potential and refine neural regeneration strategies.

Recent advancements in piezoelectric nanomaterials and CAP further underscore the transformative potential of Piezo-CAP in neural repair. Cutting-edge tissue engineering strategies increasingly leverage piezoelectric scaffolds to provide wireless, physical electrical cues that effectively drive neural stem cell differentiation for central nervous system injury repair (Xia et al., 2025). For instance, ultrasound-activated piezoelectric nanostickers have been shown to promote rapid neural stem cell differentiation and functional recovery in traumatic brain injury models by activating calcium-dependent pathways while averting toxic ROS accumulation (Wang et al., 2025). This aligns with broader applications of piezotronic materials and CAP, which harness precisely controlled RONS to stimulate neurogenesis and reshape complex tissue microenvironments, such as in sonodynamic and cancer therapies (Zhao et al., 2023, Khan et al., 2025). Integrating these piezoelectric mechanisms with CAP-generated RONS provides a synergistic framework for developing highly selective interventions in regenerative medicine.

This study has several limitations, particularly regarding model systems, technical methodologies, and temporal dynamics. First, the findings are primarily based on in vitro models and require further validation in relevant disease animal models, such as murine TBI, to assess the efficacy of Piezo-CAP in promoting neural repair or mitigating disease progression (Yan et al., 2017, Yan et al., 2019). Second, current CAP devices (e.g., plasma jets) are largely designed for surface or in vitro use; anatomical barriers such as the blood–brain barrier limit their direct application in neural tissues (Siu et al., 2015). Since CAP efficacy is highly dependent on treatment parameters (e.g., duration and power), future efforts should develop precision delivery systems—such as functionalized nanocarriers—to enable spatially and temporally controlled release of CAP-generated bioactive agents in target brain regions. Finally, the expression patterns driving neural stem cell differentiation are highly dynamic. Subsequent studies should seek to identify the critical time window for Piezo-CAP induced differentiation, determine the optimal treatment schedule, and evaluate the safety of long-term exposure (Tan et al., 2020).

In summary, this study provides a theoretical foundation and preliminary evidence for the application of piezoelectric CAP in the field of neuroscience, though substantial further research remains necessary. By progressively overcoming these limitations, it is anticipated that piezoelectric CAP technology will advance towards clinical implementation, offering new therapeutic prospects and improved outcomes for patients with neurological injuries and neurodegenerative diseases.
