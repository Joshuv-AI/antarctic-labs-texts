A Unified Framework for Direction and Diffuseness Estimation Using Tight-Frame Microphone Arrays
Akira Omoto

Abstract
This study extends the theoretical framework of diffuseness estimation to practical microphone arrays with different spatial configurations. Building upon the covariance-based model, we formulate a velocity-only covariance framework that enables consistent diffuseness evaluation across heterogeneous array geometries without requiring mode whitening or spherical harmonic decomposition. Three types of arrays — the A-format, rigid-sphere, and a newly proposed tight-frame array — are modeled and compared through both simulation and measurement experiments. The results demonstrate that the proposed tight-frame configuration achieves isotropic sampling and reproduces diffuseness characteristics equivalent to those of higher-order spherical arrays, while maintaining a compact physical structure. We also examine the accuracy of acoustic-intensity detection within the same framework. The findings bridge theoretical diffuseness analysis and practical implementations, contributing to the design of robust sound-field measurement systems.



Submitted to the Journal of the Acoustical Society of America (under review).

I
Introduction

A diffuse sound field is an ideal state in which both sound pressure and particle velocity are spatially and directionally isotropic.
This assumption underpins the classical reverberation theories of Sabine and Eyring
Sabine1900
;
Franklin1903
;
Eyring1930
and remains central in architectural acoustics
Kuttruff1973
;
Cremer1982
.
Quantitative evaluation of “diffuseness” thus plays a key role—not only for objectively assessing sound fields but also in applications such as room design, spatial reproduction, and auditory analysis.

Historically, early studies derived diffuseness indicators from spatial fluctuations of reverberation time
Schroeder1959
and from statistical limits in frequency-domain descriptions of sound fields
Schroeder1962
.
Theoretical advances introduced diffusion factors to describe energy homogeneity
Schultz1971
and correlation-based coefficients
Bodlund1976
.
The spatial correlation of sound pressure in a diffuse field was shown to follow a sinc-type function
Koyasu1971
.

In parallel, Thiele visualized reflection directions and strengths in concert halls, linking diffuseness with perceptual attributes such as clarity
Thiele1953
;
Meyer1954
;
Meyer1956
.
Following this, Gover and colleagues
Gover2002
;
Gover2004
developed spherical microphone-array systems capable of analyzing the directional and spatial variations of reverberant fields.
By steering beams over multiple directions, they computed the variance of directional power to define “directional diffusion” and the anisotropy index, showing how diffuseness evolves over time as the reverberant field becomes more isotropic and later anisotropic.
These studies established the idea that a diffuse field can be characterized by the
uniformity of directional energy distribution
, forming an experimental foundation for later diffuseness metrics.

From the late 1980s to 1990s, the energy-transport equation provided a physically grounded framework for describing energy propagation in rooms.
Derived from Boltzmann’s transport theory, it expresses temporal variation of acoustic energy density as a balance between flux and loss terms.
Applying a Fourier-type approximation to the flux term yields the acoustic diffusion equation, which models energy distribution continuously in enclosed spaces.
Picaut et al.
Picaut1999
developed a numerical model based on this equation, later extended by Valeau et al.
Valeau2006
to include anisotropy and boundary conditions.
Hanyu
Hanyu2010
further incorporated wall scattering and absorption coefficients to characterize room diffuseness quantitatively.
These developments form the theoretical core of modern room-diffusion analysis.
Following this line of study, Sakuma
Sakuma2012
and Omoto
Omoto2013
extended the framework toward more realistic nondiffuse conditions, introducing analytical models that link the statistical treatment of scattering with the observable decay characteristics in rectangular rooms.
Cox and D’Antonio
Cox2017
organized diffuser design and diffuse-field modeling into practical methodologies, and the ISO 17497 series
ISO17497-1
;
ISO17497-2
standardized measurement and evaluation methods internationally.

In the 2000s, diffuseness quantification in time and space advanced rapidly.
The First Order Ambisonics (FOA) based intensity-energy ratio (I/E) method by Merimaa and Pulkki
Merimaa2005
;
Pulkki2006
characterized and reproduced sound fields by their diffuseness.
Acoustic intensity was obtained from impulse responses, allowing estimation of diffuseness and major reflection directions, later reproduced using Vector Base Amplitude Panning (VBAP)
Pulkki1997
in Spatial Impulse Response Rendering (SIRR).
The method was extended to continuous signals as Directional Audio Coding (DirAC)
Pulkki2007
;
Vilkamo2009
, maintaining the same intensity-diffuseness principle.
Since then, the method has evolved to estimate prominent reflections from the generalized cross-correlation of signals acquired with multiple microphones, named Spatial Decomposition Method, (SDM)
Tervo2013
, and more recently, applications in the ultrasonic domain
Pulkki2021
have also been reported.

Diffuseness estimation then became closely linked with spherical microphone arrays and spherical-harmonic analysis
Rafaely2015
.
Epain and Jin
Epain2016
made a significant contribution with the covariance matrix eigenvalue diffuseness estimation, abbreviated as COMEDIE, method, which interprets diffuseness as a function of the eigenvalue distribution of spherical-harmonic signal covariance.
In addition, they formulated a set of benchmark scenarios—such as single plane-wave, mixed diffuse-plus-direct, and interference fields—that have since served as reference frameworks for validating diffuseness estimators.

Following the covariance-based formulation by Epain and Jin
Epain2016
,
Politis and Pulkki
Politis2015
;
Politis2016
extended this concept to spatially weighted energy analysis and parametric spatial audio frameworks.
Their work unified the estimation of intensity, energy density, and diffuseness in the HOA domain and demonstrated its applicability to spaced microphone arrays and multichannel rendering.
More recently, McCormack et al.
McCormack2020
investigated the perceptual effects of spherical order, dedicated diffuse rendering, and frequency resolution in higher-order Spatial Impulse Response Rendering (SIRR), highlighting that perceived spaciousness strongly depends on the accuracy of diffuse-field modeling.

Throughout this history, the link between diffuseness estimation and intensity measurement has been widely recognized, as clearly demonstrated by the I/E method.
The p-p probe method summarized by Fahy
Fahy1989
;
Fahy2001
estimates local energy flow and reflection distributions.
Guy and Abdou
Guy1994
visualized reflections and energy flow, while de Bree et al.
deBree2003
;
deBree2008
introduced pressulre-velocity sensors.
Zhang et al.
Zhang2009
applied this p-u probes to near-field acoustic holography.

The principle of acoustic intensity measurement using oppositely oriented cardioid microphones was first proposed by Bauer
Bauer1968
, forming the basis of what is now known as the C-C method.
This approach was later refined and systematically established by Hoshi and Hanyu
Hoshi2018
, also Hanyu and Hoshi
Hanyu2024
, who developed reliable formulations for practical intensity measurements in various acoustic environments.
These studies advanced an integrated view of intensity and diffuseness, linking energetic analysis with spatial measurement.

In the present study, we aim to bridge theoretical diffuseness analysis and practical microphone measurements.
A multichannel microphone array is employed to estimate broadband acoustic intensity simultaneously.
By adopting uniform directional sampling based on a tight-frame array, the proposed method achieves both a higher usable frequency and improved stability.
Combined with impulse-response measurements, it enables high-resolution evaluation of spatial energy distributions, including reflections.

While the proposed framework enables stable and broadband evaluation, it also reveals a fundamental limitation of conventional approaches:
Conventional methods often reduce diffuseness to a single scalar, neglecting directional variation.
In real environments, however, diffuseness depends on surface reflection and absorption distributions, making
directional diffuseness
essential.
Its prototype appears in Meyer’s “hedgehog” visualization of directional energy using a parabolic microphone
Meyer1954
;
Meyer1956
.
This approach evolved into the SDM
Tervo2013
, psychoacoustic studies by Amengual et al.
Amengual2017
, and visualization work by Pätynen et al.
Patynen2013
, forming the basis for direction-dependent diffuseness analysis.

Building upon these theoretical foundations, the present study extends diffuseness estimation to practical microphone arrays with several different perspectives:

Microphone array based on tight-frame theory and spatial sampling
—
A tight frame is an overcomplete basis that enables redundant yet stable reconstruction
CasazzaTightFrame
. On the sphere, a spherical
(t,t)
-design with
t=1
is equivalent to a tight frame
HughesWaldron2021
, providing a foundation for isotropic sampling in spherical processing. Recent work extends this concept to spherical framelets
XiaoZhuang2023
, optimal finite-dimensional frames
BargTwoDistance
.
Building on these, the present study implements uniform directional sampling based on a tight-frame array for diffuseness and intensity measurement.

Velocity-only covariance framework
—
a formulation that allows consistent diffuseness evaluation without relying on mode decomposition or ideal spherical harmonics, thus enabling unified analysis across heterogeneous array geometries.

Systematic comparison across indices and array configurations
—
Three array types: A-format, Tight Frame, and Rigid Sphere — are compared under identical conditions to evaluate consistency, frequency dependence, and the effects of spherical-harmonic order and whitening.
Emphasis is placed on the trade-off between the numerical instability of high-order spherical harmonic (HOA) analysis
Epain2016
and the implementation stability of first-order (FOA) approaches.
Overall, these analyses aim to bridge theoretical validity and practical reproducibility, establishing a unified framework for diffuseness evaluation.

II
Microphone arrays for diffuseness assessment

Diffuseness estimation mainly relies on acoustic intensity and energy density, as well as on eigenvalue analyses of covariance matrices of spherical-harmonic (SH) components.
Since both contain spatial and directional information, accuracy and effective bandwidth depend strongly on the geometry of the microphone array.
Commonly used devices include p-p probes, p-u sensors, paired cardioid microphones, and spherical arrays such as the Eigenmike
a)
.
In particular, A-format microphones and rigid-sphere arrays are widely used for practical analysis.

An A-format microphone often conceals the directional responses of its capsule, hindering quantitative validation.
Conversely, rigid-sphere arrays enable high-order SH expansion but require many channels, complex calibration, and suffer from numerical instability at high orders.
Hence, there is a growing need for arrays that achieve uniform spatial sampling while remaining simple and robust.

In this study, we model both the A-format and rigid-sphere microphone arrays and further propose a stably configured array referred to as a tight-frame arrangement, examining the characteristics of each.
The coordinate system used in the following discussion is shown in Fig.
1
.

Microphone arrangements used in this study. (a) Microphone arrangement modeled after the AMBEO VR Mic (A-format array).
(b) Array model based on the Eigenmike, in which 64 omnidirectional microphones are uniformly distributed on a rigid-sphere baffle according to a Fibonacci lattice.
(c) Proposed tight-frame microphone configuration, in which microphones are spaced every 45° in azimuth and ±45° in elevation.

A-format microphone (Afmt)

An A-format microphone uses four capsules in a regular tetrahedral configuration, from which the FOA components are derived for straightforward spatial analysis.
This configuration, referred to as
Afmt

II.2
Rigid-sphere baffle microphone array (Fibo64)

Spherical arrays such as the Eigenmike place microphones on a rigid sphere and use SH analysis to decompose the sound field into higher-order components, enabling analysis of both overall diffuseness and local anisotropy.
Practical challenges remain:

•

Accurate modeling of the sphere and baffle acoustics is required.

•

Numerical stability degrades at high orders.

•

Dense sampling increases calibration and cost.

Thus, despite their theoretical power, spherical arrays face trade-offs between spatial uniformity and implementation simplicity.

In this study, a 64-channel array of omnidirectional microphones uniformly distributed on a rigid sphere (radius 0.042 m) is simulated.
From the simulated pressures, sound pressure, particle velocity, and HOA coefficients are computed following Rafaely
Rafaely2015
.

Sound pressure on a rigid sphere

The pressure on a rigid sphere of radius
r
can be expanded as

p(\Omega,k)=\sum_{n,m}a_{nm}(k)\,b_{n}(kr)\,Y_{nm}(\Omega),

(4)

where
b_{n}(kr)
is the radial filter corresponding to the rigid-boundary condition for the wavenumber
k
.
Each microphone measures the pressure at direction
\Omega_{q}
.

Let
\bm{p}=[p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{Q}]^{\!\top}
denote the pressures measured at
Q
positions
\{\Omega_{q}\}
.
The discrete model is then expressed as

\bm{p}=\bm{Y}\,\bm{B}\,\bm{a},

(5)

where
\bm{Y}
is the spherical harmonic (SH) matrix,
\bm{B}
is the diagonal matrix of radial filters, and
\bm{a}
is the vector of higher-order coefficients.

Estimation of HOA coefficients

The estimation of HOA coefficients
\widehat{\bm{a}}
are then estimated in the least-squares sense as

\widehat{\bm{a}}=\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{Y}^{\dagger}\bm{p},

(6)

where
\bm{Y}^{\dagger}
is the pseudoinverse of
\bm{Y}
.
In the practical calculation, the Tikhonov regularization is introduced with the small regularization parameter, say,
10^{-4}
.

The obtained coefficients with
n=0
and
n=1
correspond to pressure and velocity:

p=\frac{a_{00}}{\sqrt{4\pi}},\quad\bm{u}=\frac{1}{3i\rho_{0}c}\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}\begin{bmatrix}a_{1x}\\
a_{1y}\\
a_{1z}\end{bmatrix}.

(7)

From the pressure and the velocities above, the intensity and the energy can be calculated as Eq. (
3
).
These four coefficients
\{a_{00},a_{1x},a_{1y},a_{1z}\}
constitute the FOA components.
The higher-order coefficients are used as necessary.

For this array (64 channels, radius
r
= 0.042 m), the spherical harmonic expansion was limited to the fixed order
N_{\mathrm{max}}=4
.
This corresponds approximately to
kr\approx 4
, i.e., a validity limit near
f=\frac{4c}{2\pi r}\approx$5.2\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
for
r=$42\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$
.
Higher frequencies were also analyzed for completeness, but the HOA representation above this limit may include spatial aliasing effects.
The fixed-order setting was chosen to ensure a consistent comparison across arrays and to avoid overfitting the mode expansion to higher frequencies.
This 64-point Fibonacci-based equal-area distribution, modeled as a proxy for the Eigenmike, was referred to as
Fibo64
, hereafter.

II.3
Tight frame microphone array (TF24)

We propose a new array using multiple highly directional microphones arranged isotropically on a sphere based on a tight-frame distribution, referred to as
TF24
.
A finite set of such points uniformly approximates the sphere while maintaining reconstruction stability.
This microphone configuration was initially devised for measuring directional impulse responses intended for sound field reproduction
Omoto2020
.
Preliminary trials were conducted to estimate acoustic intensity using this setup, which subsequently led to the more detailed investigation presented in this study.

Advantages of the proposed array include:

•

Reduced angular-dependent errors through uniform sampling

•

Wideband operation with fewer channels

•

Easier calibration using well-characterized microphones

•

Possibility of sequential measurements by rotating a single microphone, in the case of impulse response measurement

Let the measured directional quantities, say, intensities, form
\bm{I}_{\text{meas}(N)}
, and let
\bm{R}
be the
N\times 3
matrix of unit direction vectors
\bm{r}_{i}
:

\bm{I}_{\text{meas}(N)}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{r_{1}}\\
I_{r_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
I_{r_{N}}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{R}=\begin{bmatrix}r_{1,x}&r_{1,y}&r_{1,z}\\
r_{2,x}&r_{2,y}&r_{2,z}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
r_{N,x}&r_{N,y}&r_{N,z}\end{bmatrix}.

(8)

The Cartesian intensity estimate is

\bm{I}_{\text{meas}(xyz)}=\bm{R}^{\!\top}\bm{I}_{\text{meas}(N)}.

(9)

For the true intensity
\bm{I}_{(xyz)}
, its projection onto
N
measured directions is

\bm{I}_{(N)}=\bm{R}\bm{I}_{(xyz)},

(10)

and reconstruction via the pseudoinverse yields

\bm{I}_{(xyz)}=\bm{R}^{\dagger}\bm{I}_{(N)}=(\bm{R}^{\!\top}\bm{R})^{-1}\bm{R}^{\!\top}\bm{I}_{(N)}.

(11)

For a tight frame,
\bm{R}^{\!\top}\bm{R}=A\bm{I}
is assured and this relationship gives

\bm{I}_{(xyz)}=\frac{1}{A}\bm{R}^{\!\top}\bm{I}_{(N)}.

(12)

Thus, the projection sum in Eq. (
9

Each opposing pair forms one velocity axis.

0&-1/\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{2}\\
-1/2&-1/2&1/\sqrt{2}\\

(13)

and

Directional intensity, energy, and diffuseness

Microphone pairs aligned along twelve directions compute
pseudo-
intensity via the cardioid-pair (C-C) principle

;
Hanyu2024
, here adapted for narrow-directional microphones as the
pseudo
C-C method.

As illustrated in Fig.
3
, let
M^{+}_{\bm{r}_{i}}
and
M^{-}_{\bm{r}_{i}}
denote the complex amplitudes of the signals at a given frequency measured by the microphones located at the positive and negative ends of the
\bm{r}_{i}
axis, respectively, for
i=1,\ldots,12
.

The directional response of each microphone,
d(\theta)
, where
\theta
denotes the angle from its main axis,
is approximated by a polynomial of
\cos\theta
as

d(\theta)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}\cos^{n}\theta.

(14)

The case of
N=1
with
a_{0}=0.5
and
a_{1}=0.5
corresponds to an ideal cardioid pattern.

When two microphones are aligned along the same axis but oriented in opposite directions,
their directional responses can be expressed as
d(\theta)
and
d(\pi-\theta)
, respectively.
The sum and difference of their complex amplitudes,

M^{+}_{\bm{r}_{i}}\pm M^{-}_{\bm{r}_{i}},

then form symmetric and antisymmetric combinations of these directional responses.
The sum component primarily exhibits an omnidirectional characteristic,
approximating the local sound pressure,
whereas the difference component resembles a figure-of-eight pattern,
corresponding to the particle velocity component along
\bm{r}_{i}
.
Hence, the pairwise sum and difference of oppositely oriented directional microphones provide a practical means
of approximating the local pressure and the corresponding particle-velocity component.

The resulting quantities are defined as

\displaystyle p_{\bm{r}_{i}}

\displaystyle=M^{+}_{\bm{r}_{i}}+M^{-}_{\bm{r}_{i}},

(15)

\displaystyle u_{\bm{r}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{M^{+}_{\bm{r}_{i}}-M^{-}_{\bm{r}_{i}}}{Z_{0}},

(16)

\displaystyle I_{\bm{r}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|M^{+}_{\bm{r}_{i}}|^{2}-|M^{-}_{\bm{r}_{i}}|^{2}}{2Z_{0}},

(17)

\displaystyle E_{\bm{r}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|p_{\bm{r}_{i}}|^{2}}{4\rho_{0}c^{2}}+\frac{\rho_{0}}{4}|u_{\bm{r}_{i}}|^{2}.

(18)

Schematic diagram of the signals used to construct the
pseudo

In practice, the directional elements used in the array do not have strictly cardioid directivity but rather exhibit sharper lobes depending on frequency.
Therefore, the pressure obtained from their summation and the velocity inferred from their differential output cannot be regarded as the exact physical quantities.
Instead, they represent operational approximations of the local sound pressure and particle velocity, derived under the assumption of complementary directivity pairs.
The corresponding acoustic intensity and energy density are thus defined in this approximate sense and referred to here as
pseudo-
intensity and
pseudo-
energy density.
Representative examples of these directivity patterns are presented in the next section.

Similarly, the directional pseudo-diffuseness index based on the I/E concept is defined as

\Psi_{\bm{r}_{i}}=1-\frac{|I_{\bm{r}_{i}}|}{E_{\bm{r}_{i}}c},

(19)

and its weighted extension is discussed in the following section.

III
Benchmark problems for numerical simulation

Unlike previous studies that evaluated diffuseness primarily within the spherical harmonic domain under ideal conditions, the present work reformulates the covariance analysis in the first-order velocity space and verifies its performance with physically realizable microphone geometries. By integrating Epain and Jin’s benchmark paradigm
Epain2016
with a tight-frame sampling approach, this study provides a unified yet experimentally grounded framework for diffuseness characterization.

Overview of simulation framework

This section defines benchmark problems to compare the diffuseness-evaluation methods for the three array configurations introduced in the previous section (

,

III.2
Benchmark case definitions

As shown in Fig.

Case 1: Single plane-wave incidence.

A single plane wave arrives from a given direction; the theoretical diffuseness is
\Psi=0
. We sweep both elevation and azimuth over the sphere to test directional robustness and verify how accurately intensity-based direction-of-arrival can be recovered.
For Case 1, 72 azimuthal directions (0
∘
to 355
∘
) and 35 zenith angles (5
∘
to 175
∘
), both at 5
∘

For each case, the mean diffuseness and the error in arrival-angle detection based on the acoustic intensity were evaluated to assess the accuracy of sound-arrival direction estimation for each array.

Case 2: Plane-wave beam + diffuse mixture.

As illustrated in Fig.
6
, we mix a beam arriving from a narrow solid angle (here
0.5\%
of
4\pi
sr) with diffuse components uniformly distributed over
4\pi
sr. We define the beam-to-total energy ratio

\eta=\frac{E_{\mathrm{beam}}}{E_{\mathrm{beam}}+E_{\mathrm{diffuse}}},

(22)

where
E_{\mathrm{beam}}
is the energy of beam, and
E_{\mathrm{diffuse}}
is the energy of diffuse components. The
\eta
was varied from 0 to 1. Both the diffuse and beam components use 100,000 rays with random amplitude and phase; for band-limited noise, we draw 100 log-spaced frequencies within a 1-octave band at each trial. We examine whether the estimated
\Psi
is stable and how linearly it tracks
\eta
.

Case 3: Reactive field (interference).

In this case, an interference field was simulated by superposing two plane waves: a primary wave incident from the zenith direction and a secondary wave arriving from variable zenith angles between 0° and 180°. For each configuration, 1,000 realizations were generated by randomly varying the phase (within
\pm 2\pi
) and amplitude (within
\pm 3
dB) of the secondary wave. The acoustic intensity, energy density, and covariance matrix were computed for each realization, and the final values were obtained by averaging these quantities over all realizations to represent the mean behavior of the interference field.

III.3
Evaluation metrics

Given the array pressures, the following diffuseness indices are calculated.

Intensity–Energy ratio
\Psi_{\mathrm{IE}},\>\Psi_{\mathrm{AVE}}

From pressure
p
and velocity
\bm{u}
(via A-format or HOA/FOA processing) in the cases of
Afmt
and
Fibo64
, the diffuseness
\Psi_{\mathrm{IE}}
is defined as

\Psi_{\mathrm{IE}}(f)=1-\frac{|\bm{I}(f)|}{c\,E(f)}.

(23)

\Psi_{\mathrm{IE}}\!\approx\!0
indicates a traveling-wave field;
\Psi_{\mathrm{IE}}\!\approx\!1
indicates a diffuse field.

For
TF24
case, we compute directional
\Psi_{r_{i}}
defined as Eq. (
19
).
In directions where only uncorrelated background noise is observed, the estimated diffuseness tends to assume relatively high values. However, such regions do not correspond to acoustically significant signals. This observation suggests the necessity of introducing directional weighting.

One possible choice is to weight the diffuseness by the energy associated with each direction.
After several preliminary trials, weighting by the squared magnitude of the particle velocity was adopted, as it exhibited higher sensitivity to spatial variations in the sound field. Fig.
7
illustrates an example of the directional energy distribution and the corresponding weighting factors based on the squared particle velocity, computed for Case 2. The incident beam is assumed to arrive predominantly from the 9th direction, and it can be observed that the velocity-based weighting responds subtly but consistently to this directional bias.

\Psi_{\mathrm{AVE}}=\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{12}\Psi_{\bm{r}_{i}}\>|u_{\bm{r}_{i}}|^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{12}\>|u_{\bm{r}_{i}}|^{2}}.

(24)

The appropriateness of this weighting scheme remains a subject for further investigation; nevertheless, several representative examples are presented below.
The possible disadvantages associated with this choice are also discussed in the Discussion section.

Eigenvalue-based diffuseness (
\Psi
PR
,
\Psi
COM
)

To capture spatial uniformity, covariance-based measures are employed.
In conventional approaches, a 4×4 covariance matrix is constructed from the sound pressure and three velocity components, i.e., FOA components as

\bm{C}_{\mathrm{pu}}=\langle[p,\,u_{x},\,u_{y},\,u_{z}]^{H}[p,\,u_{x},\,u_{y},\,u_{z}]\rangle,

where
u_{x},\;u_{y},\;u_{z}
are the
x,y,z
directional components of velocity, and
\bm{C}_{\mathrm{pu}}
represents the full energy coupling between pressure and particle velocity.

In the present study, however, we emphasize a velocity-only route and form

\bm{C}_{\mathrm{uu}}=\langle\bm{u}(f)\,\bm{u}^{H}(f)\rangle,

(25)

which directly captures the spatial anisotropy of the local particle-velocity distribution without explicit reference to the pressure term.

This choice is first motivated by the characteristics of the Tight frame array.
Since each microphone in the
TF24
configuration is a highly directional (DPA4017 in our case) rather than an ideal cardioid, the sum of each microphone pair does not accurately reproduce the omnidirectional pressure component.

Consequently, defining diffuseness in terms of pressure-velocity coherence becomes less reliable in
TF24
.
By constructing the covariance matrix solely from the velocity vectors, we can evaluate the spatial uniformity in a physically consistent and array-independent manner,
thus ensuring the robustness of diffuseness estimation even under non-ideal directivity conditions.

In the present study, the covariance matrix was constructed solely from the velocity components derived from the FOA signals, not only for the
TF24
system but also for the
Afmt
and
Fibo64
configurations.
A further dominant motivation for this choice lies in the scaling issues that inevitably arise when handling both pressure and velocity simultaneously. When estimating diffuseness from eigenvalues, it is desirable to ensure not only amplitude scaling, such as SN3D normalization (e.g., Eq. (
7
)) but also detailed frequency response consistency.
Achieving this would require measurements in a sound field that can be regarded as
sufficiently diffuse
and the computation of a corresponding whitening matrix. To avoid this complexity, the present work primarily adopts an approach based on velocity-only metrics.

In the
TF24
system, the particle velocity components used to construct the covariance matrix
\bm{C}_{uu}
are obtained by projecting the velocity vectors
u_{\bm{r}_{i}}
,
measured in 12 directions, onto the global
x,\;y
, and
z
axes. This projection is performed by multiplying each directional component by the corresponding direction vector
\bm{R}
as,

\bm{u}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{x}\\
u_{y}\\
u_{z}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{4}\bm{R}^{\!\top}\bm{u}_{\bm{r}},

(26)

where
\bm{u}_{\bm{r}}=\left[u_{\bm{r}_{1}}u_{\bm{r}_{2}}\cdots u_{\bm{r}_{12}}\right]^{\!\top}
.

Let the eigenvalues of Eq. (
25
) be
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{3}
.
From these eigenvalues, several indices can be derived. In this paper, the following two diffuseness indices are calculated and examined:

•

Participation Ratio (
\Psi
PR
)

Quantifies the effective number of eigenmodes contributing to the covariance structure:

\Psi_{\mathrm{PR}}=\frac{\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\lambda_{i}\right)^{2}}{\displaystyle 3\sum_{i=1}^{3}\lambda_{i}^{2}}.

(27)

This index is sensitive to how evenly the energy is distributed among the eigenvalues,
taking a value close to
1/3
for a single dominant mode and approaching 1 for a uniform distribution.
Note that
\Psi_{\mathrm{PR}}\in[1/3,\,1]
for three non-negative eigenvalues.

•

COMEDIE (
\Psi
COM
)

Proposed by Epain and Jin, this measure normalizes the standard deviation of the eigenvalues:

\Psi_{\mathrm{COM}}=1-\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\,\frac{\sigma_{\lambda}}{\overline{\lambda}},

(28)

where
\sigma_{\lambda}
and
\overline{\lambda}
denote the standard deviation and mean of
\{\lambda_{i}\}
, respectively.
This provides a statistically stable and physically interpretable indicator of isotropy.

V
Experimental verification

V.1
Concept

Finally, the trends obtained from the simulations were experimentally verified using measured data.
Two sound fields were considered: an anechoic room and a reverberation room.
As expected, diffuseness is nearly zero in the anechoic room and close to unity in the reverberation room.
However, unlike the numerical simulations, neither environment can be regarded as perfectly ideal, and it is difficult to ensure that the measured results precisely match these theoretical values.
Therefore, to enable a meaningful verification, we adopted an approach analogous to
Case 2
of the benchmark simulations:
the impulse responses measured in the anechoic and reverberation rooms were used to represent the “beam” and “diffuse” components, respectively.
By mixing these two responses at arbitrary energy ratios, we examined the linearity between the prescribed ratio and the resulting diffuseness.

Specifically, for both sound fields, impulse responses were measured using the
Afmt
and
TF24
microphone arrays.
The energy within each frequency band was computed and combined according to the specified ratio, and the diffuseness was then calculated from the resulting signals.
It should be noted that an Eigenmike was not available, and thus the case corresponding to the
Fibo64
array was not experimentally validated.

V.2
Conditions

For the
Afmt
array, an AMBEO VR Mic was used, and for the
TF24
array, DPA 4017 microphones were employed with a spacing of 20 mm between each microphone pair.
As a preliminary measurement conducted in an anechoic room to determine the acoustic reference point of the shotgun microphone, impulse responses were measured twice at the same position: once with the microphone oriented toward the sound source, and once with it oriented perpendicularly.
The time difference between the peaks of these two responses was used to estimate the reference point, which was found to be approximately 61 mm from the bottom of the microphone (amplifier section).
Using this reference, the array was mounted on a rotatable jig (a camera tripod head) that allowed precise rotations in 45° increments around a point located 10 mm above the estimated center.

In the anechoic chamber, measurements were conducted at a distance of 2.5 m from the sound source. In the reverberation chamber (147 m
3
, RT
\sim
4.7 s), the distance to the source, which was directed toward a wall, was approximately 5 m.

VI
Conclusion

This study presented a comparative investigation of diffuseness estimation methods based on intensity-energy relations and eigenvalue analysis, using several microphone arrays including the
Afmt
,
Fibo64
, and
TF24
systems.
Through a series of controlled simulations, the relationships between the diffuseness index and the beam-to-diffuse ratio were systematically examined under single-source, mixed, and interference conditions.
The results demonstrated that the eigenvalue-based indices, particularly
\Psi_{\mathrm{COM}}
, exhibited higher robustness and a more linear response than the conventional intensity-energy (I/E) approach.
The I/E method tended to overestimate diffuseness in interference fields where the active intensity approaches zero, leading to physically inconsistent interpretations.

Regarding the combination of microphone array configurations and evaluation approaches, the
Fibo64
array would be the first choice if such an array is available. In cases where this is not realistic, arrays composed of microphones with known directivity patterns—such as the Tight frame configuration, which possesses theoretical validity—represent a reasonable alternative. Furthermore, the eigenvalue analysis based on the covariance matrix constructed only from the FOA velocity components (
u_{x}
,
u_{y}
, and
u_{z}
) was found to produce stable and reliable results overall.

Experimental verification using impulse responses measured in anechoic and reverberation rooms further confirmed the general trends predicted by the simulations.
The diffuseness estimated from mixed impulse responses showed approximately linear dependence on the energy ratio between the direct and reverberant components, with smaller deviations observed for the
TF24
array compared with the
Afmt
array.
These results suggest that the velocity-based covariance analysis provides a practical and reliable framework for characterizing the spatial diffuseness of sound fields, even without strict whitening or mode-power normalization.

Future work will extend the present analysis beyond the weighted averaging of directional diffuseness, exploring how this parameter relates to perceptual impressions of spatial quality. Further investigations will aim to establish practical applications of diffuseness-based metrics in spatial reproduction and acoustic design, where such perceptually informed measures may provide useful guidance for sound field control and evaluation.
