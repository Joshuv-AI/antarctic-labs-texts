Pulsed power treatment demonstrates complete inactivation of anisakid larvae in agar penetration and rabbit infection tests
Authors: Hiromu Sugiyama, Mitsuko Shiroyama, Yasuyuki Morishima, Douyan Wang, Takao Namihira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnisakiasis is a foodborne zoonosis caused by ingesting raw seafood containing anisakid larvae. Its global incidence has increased with rising seafood consumption, highlighting the need for effective prevention methods. While freezing at −20 °C for 24 h is a standard preventive measure, it compromises seafood quality. Pulsed power (PP) technology, delivering high-voltage microsecond pulses, has emerged as a promising non-thermal alternative. Here, we evaluated PP inactivation efficacy using agar penetration and rabbit infection tests. Larvae treated with 10 pulses of 13 kV for 10 µs failed to penetrate agar (0/40), whereas 35/40 untreated larvae penetrated agar (p = 0.0202). In the rabbit model (four animals/group), PP-treated larvae caused no gastric wall penetration, and only three nonviable larvae were observed in the gastric lumen. Conversely, untreated larvae invaded the gastric mucosa (mean: ~ 21/50), lumen (~ 7.8/50), and peritoneal cavity (~ 2.3/50). Gastric wall penetration was significantly lower in the PP group than in the untreated group (p = 0.0211), with a relative risk of 0.006. These results objectively demonstrate complete inactivation of anisakid larvae after PP treatment. PP represents a promising alternative to freezing and holds potential for broader application, provided that compact devices are developed for practical use in seafood processing.Keywords: Anisakis, Anisakiasis, Foodborne diseases, Fish products, Animal experimentation, Pulsed power

Introduction

Anisakiasis is a parasitic zoonosis caused by the ingestion of raw or undercooked seafood containing anisakid larvae. The global incidence of anisakiasis has increased alongside growing seafood consumption (EFSA BIOHAZ Panel 2024). In Japan, where raw seafood such as sushi and sashimi is deeply rooted in the Japanese food culture, approximately 20,000 cases of anisakiasis are estimated annually (Sugiyama et al. 2022). Notably, mackerel is implicated as a major source of infection (Tokiwa et al. 2018). The most common clinical form is gastric anisakiasis, characterized by severe epigastric pain; this disease entity has recently been classified as gastroallergic anisakiasis (Mattiucci et al. 2015). The first-line therapy is endoscopic removal of larvae penetrating the gastric mucosa (Shimamura et al. 2016).

Currently, freezing seafood at −20 °C for 24 h is recommended to inactivate anisakid larvae (EFSA BIOHAZ Panel 2024). Nonetheless, freezing significantly degrades the sensory qualities of raw seafood, particularly its color and texture. In this context, pulsed power (PP) treatment that applies ultra-high peak electric pulses of microsecond duration has emerged as a promising alternative for inactivating anisakid larvae without compromising fish meat quality (Onitsuka et al. 2022).

The standard method for evaluating isolated larval viability post-treatment involves mechanical probing to assess their physical response, referred to as the stimulation method (Abad et al. 2023, 2024; Onitsuka et al. 2022, 2024). Nevertheless, this method carries the risk of misjudging temporarily inactivated larvae as dead, and if they regain activity, they may penetrate the gastric wall. Here, we introduced agar penetration (Arizono et al. 2012) and animal infection tests (Vericimo et al. 2017) as potentially more reliable assessment methods to verify complete inactivation of anisakid larvae by PP treatment.

Materials and methods

Parasite collection and identification

Anisakis simplex complex larvae were collected from three Pacific cods (Gadus macrocephalus). The gastric serosa and mesentery were enzymatically digested using artificial gastric juice (0.1% pepsin and 0.7% HCl) at 37 °C for 15 min. The digested material was filtered and washed with physiological saline; only intact and actively motile larvae were selected. This process yielded approximately 700 larvae, whose ventricle morphology was observed with a stereomicroscope (SZX7; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The specimens were stored at 4 °C and used within 24 h.

For species verification, ribosomal DNA ITS1 sequencing was performed on 20 randomly selected larvae (D’Amelio et al. 2000).

PP treatment

PP treatment was performed using a plastic Petri dish (5 cm diameter) containing 7 mL of 0.6% NaCl solution maintained at 4 °C. Two parallel electrodes were positioned 2 cm apart. Five larvae were placed between the electrodes and exposed to ten 13-kV pulses, each with a pulse width of 10 µs, applied at 0.5 Hz (~ 20 s total exposure). These PP treatment conditions were selected based on preliminary experiments using the stimulation method, under which all larvae exhibited no response to mechanical probing (Onitsuka et al. 2024). Following PP treatment, larvae were immediately used for downstream evaluation.

Agar penetration test

The agar penetration test was designed to assess larval invasiveness. In each 50-mL plastic tube, 30 mL of 1% Bacto agar was added and allowed to solidify. Using a metal rod, 20 holes of 3-mm diameter and depth were created on each agar surface. A 1-cm overlay of 3% acetic acid was added to stimulate larval motility. Ten larvae were placed in the acid layer of each tube. Four replicate tubes were prepared for each group (PP-treated and untreated). Overall, 40 larvae were used in each group. After 12 h of incubation at 37 °C, the number of larvae remaining in the acid layer was counted; these larvae were presumed not to have penetrated the agar.

Experimental animal infection

To evaluate in vivo infectivity, 10-week-old male SPF Japanese white rabbits were randomly divided into two groups, and 50 PP-treated or untreated larvae were administered into the stomach of each rabbit using a Nelaton catheter (18 Fr). After 15–21 h, rabbits were euthanized and necropsied. Larval counts were recorded in the gastric wall, lumen, peritoneal cavity, and abdominal organs. Isolated larvae were observed under a stereomicroscope to assess morphology and response to mechanical stimulation. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the National Institute of Infectious Diseases (approval number: 123118), which is in accordance with the Act on Welfare and Management of Animals and relevant guidelines including the Standards relating to the Care and Keeping and Reducing Pain of Laboratory Animals (Ministry of the Environment 2006 in Japan) and the Fundamental Guidelines for Proper Conduct of Animal Experiment and Related Activities in Academic Research Institutions (Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology 2015 in Japan). This study was conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

Statistical analysis

The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the differences between PP-treated and untreated groups in both agar penetration and animal infection tests. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We specifically focused on the number of gastric wall-penetrating larvae in experimental infections according to the study objective. To further evaluate the degree of PP treatment efficacy, we calculated the relative risk of infections using the Haldane–Anscombe correction to account for zero counts. All statistical analyses were conducted using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface built on R statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Species identification of anisakid larvae

Approximately 700 anisakid larvae collected were all classified as A. simplex complex by ventricle morphology. Twenty randomly selected larvae were all identified as A. simplex sensu stricto, whose sequence was deposited in GenBank (accession no. LC875495), suggesting that the collected specimens belonged to A. simplex complex including A. simplex sensu stricto.

Inactivation confirmed by experimental infection model

In the PP-treated group, no larvae penetrated the gastric wall (Table 2; Figure 1). Only one rabbit possessed three larvae in the gastric lumen. Stereomicroscopic examination revealed one tail fragment and two morphologically intact larvae, all with internal degeneration and no physical response to stimulation. In the untreated group, more than half of the recovered larvae penetrated the gastric mucosa (Table 2; Figure 2). Multiple intact and actively moving larvae (8–13 per rabbit) were recovered from the gastric lumens of three rabbits in the untreated group. Nine larvae were retrieved from the peritoneal cavity, and one each was located in the perirenal fat and transversus abdominis muscle. All were motile and morphologically normal. The number of larvae penetrating the gastric wall differed significantly between the groups (p = 0.0211) (Table 2; Figures 3 and 4). The relative risk for gastric wall penetration in the PP-treated group was 0.006 (95% confidence interval: 0.0004–0.0947), indicating complete prevention of larval penetration by PP treatment (Table 2).

Discussion

Anisakiasis can be prevented by freezing seafood; nevertheless, this process deteriorates fish meat quality. To address this issue, PP treatment has been developed as a novel approach to inactivate anisakid larvae without compromising the sensory properties of fish meat. Previous studies have already demonstrated the effectiveness of PP treatment by the stimulation method (Abad et al. 2023, 2024; Onitsuka et al. 2022, 2024). However, this assessment method may misjudge larval conditions if the larvae are only temporarily inactivated by PP treatment. Here, we newly introduced two objective in vitro and in vivo assessment methods, agar penetration and experimental animal infection tests, to verify the inactivation of anisakid larvae by PP treatment. These tests demonstrated that PP treatment completely eliminates the ability of the larvae to penetrate both agar and the gastric wall of rabbits.

We used rabbits for the experimental animal instead of smaller rodents because they are regarded as an ideal experimental model given their high susceptibility to anisakid larvae (Vericimo et al. 2017). Rabbits possess a larger stomach capacity, allowing for the administration of a greater number of larvae compared to that administered to smaller rodents. Because the prevention of gastric wall penetration represents the protective effect against human gastroallergic anisakiasis, this rabbit infection model is considered a credible approach for evaluating PP inactivation efficacy. A recent study demonstrated that anisakid larvae were effectively inactivated under reduced PP input energy density (Onitsuka et al. 2024). Here, we did not employ various PP conditions but applied only a relatively high input energy density, where our preliminary stimulation experiments had confirmed the complete inactivation of isolated larvae, representing a study limitation. Moreover, comparative studies investigating allergenicity of PP-treated and untreated larvae would be beneficial, as allergic reactions have been reported after the ingestion of dead anisakid larvae (EFSA BIOHAZ Panel 2024).

Recent data from Spain, where approximately 8,000 anisakiasis cases were estimated annually (Bao et al. 2017), revealed PP treatment efficacy for inactivating anisakid larvae artificially embedded in fish meat, as confirmed by the stimulation method (Abad et al. 2023, 2024). Additionally, those in naturally infected salmonid fish and hake were also successfully inactivated according to studies in Japan and Spain (Abad et al. 2024; Onitsuka et al. 2024). Regarding the quality of raw fish meat, a subsequent study demonstrated that PP-treated meat offered superior quality compared to that of frozen products (Abad et al. 2024). Therefore, PP treatment is a technology that ensures both safety and palatability of raw seafood. Besides these benefits, high-voltage pulses are applied only for an extremely short duration, resulting in minimal electricity consumption and economic viability for continuous use. A large-scale prototype is already in operation, and PP-treated raw fish is commercially available in Japan with no reported anisakiasis incidents associated with its consumption (Onitsuka et al. 2023). To promote broader social implementation, it is highly desirable that compact PP devices be applicable to both commercial retail settings and international trade.

Our results from agar penetration and animal infection tests demonstrate the substantial inactivation efficacy of PP treatment. Thus, PP treatment is considered an effective alternative to conventional freezing methods for providing safe raw seafood. Widespread implementation of PP treatment in the seafood industry could markedly reduce anisakiasis incidence, thereby alleviating public‐health concerns associated with raw fish consumption.
