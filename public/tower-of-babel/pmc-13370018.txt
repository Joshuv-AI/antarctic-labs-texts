Application of a PSO–FAHP-based evaluation system for office chair design demand analysis
Authors: Mingzhu Li, Xinyue Peng, Fangyuan Chang, Tao Peng, Xingyan Chen, Wuyi Qiang, Tao Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExisting studies on office chair evaluation often emphasize either general furniture attributes or isolated performance indicators, limiting their usefulness for systematic design decision-making. To address this issue, this study proposes a demand-oriented evaluation framework for office chair products by integrating particle swarm optimization (PSO) with the fuzzy analytic hierarchy process (FAHP). Based on Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, office chair user requirements were identified and organized using the KJ method to establish a hierarchical evaluation index system. FAHP was applied to determine criterion weights under fuzzy judgment conditions, and PSO was used to improve matrix consistency and weighting stability. A fuzzy comprehensive evaluation was then conducted for four selected representative office chair alternatives. The results showed that safety requirements had the highest weight, followed by basic functional and engineering requirements, while aesthetic and innovation-related requirements were less important. Among the four alternatives, Design Case A obtained the highest score under the selected criteria, weights, evaluators, and scoring scale. The findings suggest that office chair evaluation depends primarily on lower-order requirements related to use, support, and reliability, rather than on higher-order experiential attributes. The proposed PSO–FAHP framework offers a practical method for office chair assessment and a structured basis for requirement-oriented design decision-making.Keywords: Office chair design, Design evaluation, Fuzzy analytic hierarchy process, Particle swarm optimization, Multi-criteria decision-making, User requirementsSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The office chair market has become increasingly competitive, and users now expect more from office chair products than basic sitting support alone. In addition to comfort, they increasingly value ergonomic fit, ease of adjustment, safety, and overall user experience1. As office work continues to involve long periods of sedentary activity, the design quality of office chairs has become closely linked to health, work efficiency, and long-term well-being. Previous ergonomic research has shown that prolonged sitting affects lumbar posture, muscle activity, and perceived discomfort, underscoring the importance of ergonomic performance in chair design2. Related studies on dynamic office chairs have further shown that body movement, comfort perception, and task-related physical responses are shaped by specific design features during everyday office use3. Together, these findings suggest that office chairs should not be treated as ordinary furniture products, but as a category of human-centered products defined by frequent use, sustained bodily contact, and multiple interacting performance demands.

In response to this complexity, user-centered design methods have been increasingly adopted to translate subjective perceptions and experiential expectations into actionable design variables4. Kansei engineering, in particular, has provided an influential framework for linking users’ affective responses to product attributes and has been widely applied in product development and evaluation research5. More broadly, structured evaluation systems have proven valuable in furniture design because they help designers identify key demand factors early in the development process and reduce excessive reliance on intuition alone6. This is especially relevant to office chair products, where user evaluations are shaped not by a single attribute, such as appearance or function, but by an interrelated set of requirements involving comfort, safety, adjustability, engineering feasibility, and perceptual quality.

Among multi-criteria decision-making methods, the analytic hierarchy process (AHP) is widely used because it can decompose a complex decision problem into a clear hierarchical structure and derive quantitative priorities from expert judgments7. Yet conventional AHP can become unstable when evaluators are asked to compare criteria that are ambiguous, overlapping, or partly subjective8. The fuzzy analytic hierarchy process (FAHP) was developed to address this limitation by allowing judgments to be expressed in fuzzy terms, making it better suited to design problems involving uncertainty and qualitative assessment9. In chair and furniture research, AHP- and FAHP-based methods have already shown value in requirement prioritization, design evaluation, and comparative scheme selection10.

Even so, FAHP still faces practical limitations when the evaluation structure becomes more complex. Constructing and adjusting fuzzy judgment matrices can be laborious, and the final weighting results may still be affected by inconsistency if FAHP is used on its own8,9. Particle swarm optimization (PSO), a population-based intelligent optimization algorithm, offers a useful way to improve this process through efficient global search and rapid convergence11. Its methodological robustness and wide engineering applicability have also been confirmed in subsequent review studies12. In furniture-related research, hybrid methods that combine optimization techniques with user-oriented evaluation have shown promise in improving the rigor and efficiency of design decision-making13. However, a demand-oriented framework that integrates PSO with FAHP specifically for office chair evaluation remains underdeveloped.

A further limitation in the existing literature is that many studies still address furniture evaluation at a relatively general level, without fully accounting for the distinctive demands of office chair products1. Compared with general household furniture, office chairs must respond more directly to prolonged sitting, postural support, operational convenience, and work-related comfort. Their evaluation therefore requires a framework that can capture both fundamental functional needs and higher-level experiential expectations. Maslow’s hierarchy of needs provides a useful lens for organizing such demands in a structured and interpretable way14. Although originally proposed as a theory of human motivation, Maslow’s framework has been widely used in design research to explain how users prioritize different levels of need, from basic and safety-related concerns to emotional, perceptual, and self-expressive expectations15. In the context of office chair products, this perspective is particularly relevant because users typically evaluate support, comfort, and safety first, and only then attend more strongly to appearance, symbolic value, or novelty.

To address these gaps, this study develops a demand-oriented evaluation framework for office chair design by integrating PSO with FAHP. Guided by Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, the study first identifies and collects user requirements related to office chair products. These requirements are then organized through the KJ method to build a hierarchical evaluation index system. On this basis, FAHP is used to derive criterion weights under conditions of subjective and fuzzy judgment, while PSO is introduced to improve matrix consistency and strengthen the reliability of the weighting process. Finally, fuzzy comprehensive evaluation is applied to compare four selected representative office chair design alternatives and identify the highest-scoring alternative under the study conditions. Recent research has also shown that requirement elicitation and evaluation frameworks based on contextual matching and hybrid decision-making can improve the alignment between user needs and design solutions, further supporting the value of structured requirement-oriented evaluation in complex product systems16.

This study makes three main contributions. First, it develops a demand-oriented evaluation framework specifically for office chair products, extending existing evaluation research beyond general furniture products to a product category shaped by intensive use and distinctive ergonomic demands. Second, it integrates particle swarm optimization with the fuzzy analytic hierarchy process to improve the consistency and practical reliability of criterion weighting in contexts involving subjective and fuzzy judgments. Third, it establishes a unified empirical pathway linking user demand extraction, evaluation index construction, weight optimization, and comparative scheme assessment, thereby providing a more systematic basis for office chair design decision-making.

Section 2 presents the development and solution process of the PSO–FAHP model, including the PSO algorithm, Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, and the construction of the evaluation framework. Establishment and solution of the PSO–FAHP model

Particle swarm optimization algorithm

In particle swarm optimization (PSO), the optimization process is formulated as the movement of particles within a search space, where each particle represents a candidate solution11. Each particle is associated with a fitness value defined by the objective function, together with a position and a velocity that determine its search direction and step size. Starting from a randomly initialized population, particles iteratively update their states according to both their own historically best position and the best position identified by the swarm17. Through this iterative search process, the velocity and position of each particle are continuously adjusted until a predefined termination criterion is satisfied. Unlike genetic algorithms, PSO does not depend on crossover or mutation operators; instead, particles approach the optimal solution through information sharing and collective learning during the iterative search process18.

Maslow’s hierarchy of needs

Maslow’s hierarchy of needs is a classical motivational theory that explains the hierarchical organization of human needs. In this framework, human needs are structured into five levels: physiological needs, safety needs, social needs, esteem needs, and self-actualization needs14. These levels are generally understood as progressing from lower-order survival-related needs to higher-order developmental needs.

This hierarchical logic is also compatible with the evolution of user expectations in product design and consumption. In contemporary product development, user demands often extend beyond basic functional adequacy to include emotional, perceptual, and experiential value15.

In the context of office chair products, user needs are no longer limited to basic sitting support, but increasingly involve safety, comfort, social perception, emotional satisfaction, and higher-level experiential value. Therefore, this study adopts Maslow’s hierarchy of needs as the conceptual foundation for identifying and structuring office chair product demands. Combined with the functional characteristics and design requirements of office chair products, this framework supports the development of a more systematic demand evaluation system.

Construction of the office chair evaluation system

Through an extensive review of the literature, retail market investigations, questionnaire surveys, online user comment analysis, and field research in enterprises, an office chair evaluation system was established based on Maslow’s hierarchy of needs theory.

The survey participants included 5 design experts, 5 product managers, 5 R&D managers, 5 product designers, and 200 office workers.

After the user requirements for office chair products were collected, the KJ method was used to classify and synthesize the data, thereby establishing the evaluation system for office chair design alternatives, as shown in Fig. 2.

Office chair requirement hierarchy and evaluation indicators.

Figure 2 shows how user requirements were organized into a three-level hierarchy from the overall evaluation objective to criterion and sub-criterion indicators. Figure 3 presents the four selected representative office chair alternatives used as comparative case examples in the fuzzy comprehensive evaluation. These alternatives were not intended to represent all office chair products, all market segments, or all user groups. The evaluators reviewed product photographs, available product descriptions, visible structural and material information, adjustability descriptions, and functional specifications. The evaluation did not include controlled physical testing or long-term use trials; therefore, criteria such as seat comfort, structural stability, material quality, and ergonomic adaptability were assessed based on the available information and the evaluators’ professional judgment.

Discussion

This study shows that the proposed PSO–FAHP framework is a feasible and effective approach for evaluating office chair design alternatives in decision contexts characterized by multiple criteria, subjective judgment, and partial uncertainty. Rather than relying on intuitive comparison or on a single performance indicator, the framework translates diverse user requirements into a structured evaluation process. This is especially important in office chair products, where product value is shaped not only by ergonomic support and structural safety but also by usability, engineering feasibility, and experiential quality1. As previous research has shown, office chairs are best understood as multi-dimensional products whose performance cannot be captured adequately through a narrow functional lens2.

One of the clearest findings of this study is that safety requirements received the highest weight, followed by basic functional and engineering requirements, whereas aesthetic and innovation-related requirements were comparatively less influential. This pattern can be interpreted meaningfully through Maslow’s hierarchy of needs14. For products involving prolonged bodily contact and frequent use, users are more likely to prioritize fundamental needs such as safety, comfort, and functional reliability before giving substantial weight to symbolic or experiential attributes. In the context of office chair products, this ordering is particularly understandable because deficiencies in support, stability, or adjustability have direct and cumulative consequences during daily use. The current results therefore suggest that Maslow’s framework is not merely a conceptual backdrop in this study; it provides a useful explanatory basis for understanding how office chair evaluation criteria become hierarchically ordered in practice.

This interpretation is also consistent with the specific nature of office chairs as use-intensive products. Ergonomic studies have shown that prolonged sitting is closely associated with posture, discomfort, and physical strain, making support quality a central concern in seating design2. Research on dynamic office chairs has further indicated that comfort perception and bodily response are strongly shaped by design features during actual use3. Seen in this light, the prominence of safety and basic functional requirements in the present results is not incidental. Rather, it reflects the logic of a product category in which structural and ergonomic inadequacies matter more immediately than shortcomings in appearance or stylistic novelty.

The findings also suggest that requirement hierarchies in furniture design are product-specific rather than universal. Earlier design studies influenced by Kansei engineering emphasized the importance of perceptual and affective responses in product evaluation4. That perspective remains valuable, but the present results show that such dimensions do not necessarily dominate in every category. For office chair products, emotional appeal and visual refinement appear to function more as secondary differentiators than primary determinants of overall evaluation. This extends the existing literature by showing that the salience of design criteria depends heavily on product type, usage conditions, and the practical consequences of performance failure5.This product-specific pattern is also consistent with recent evidence from street-furniture research, where emotional design dimensions were shown to matter, but only within a broader evaluative structure that still depended on functional and contextual considerations20.

The comparative results further showed that Design Case A achieved the highest overall evaluation score among the four alternatives. This does not mean that the scheme is universally superior in all possible contexts. Rather, it indicates that Design Case A aligned most closely with the weighted evaluation criteria established in this study. In demand-oriented design evaluation, that distinction is important. A scheme is not “better” in the abstract; it is better relative to a clearly defined hierarchy of user-valued requirements21. From this perspective, the present study contributes to office chair design research by showing how needs-based weighting can make scheme comparison more transparent, systematic, and defensible. The rank-margin sensitivity analysis further suggests that the ranking should be interpreted cautiously. Although Design Case A remained the highest-scoring alternative under the reported score margins, the difference between Design Case B and Design Case D was small, indicating that modest changes in criterion weights or expert ratings could affect the relative order of adjacent alternatives.

From a methodological standpoint, the study also clarifies the respective roles of AHP, FAHP, and PSO in office chair evaluation. AHP remains useful for structuring complex decisions because of its interpretability and hierarchical clarity7. However, when evaluators must compare criteria that are partly qualitative, overlapping, or difficult to distinguish precisely, crisp pairwise judgments can become unstable8. FAHP improves on this limitation by allowing fuzzy judgments that better reflect the uncertainty inherent in human assessment9. This makes it more suitable for design evaluation problems, where expert judgments are often necessary but cannot always be expressed in strict numerical terms.

Even so, FAHP on its own does not fully solve the problem of matrix inconsistency when the evaluation structure becomes more complex. This is where PSO becomes valuable. By introducing an iterative optimization process, PSO helps improve the consistency of the fuzzy judgment matrix and strengthens the stability of the weighting results12. Its value in the present framework lies not in replacing hierarchical fuzzy evaluation, but in enhancing it where consistency becomes a substantive methodological concern rather than a minor computational issue. In this sense, the main methodological contribution of the study is not simply the combination of two techniques, but the demonstration that optimization can improve the practical robustness of requirement-oriented evaluation in furniture design11,13.

The present study also differs from recent furniture evaluation and optimization studies in both methodological role and product context. Fu et al.13 integrated Kansei engineering with PSO-SVR for Ming-style furniture form evaluation and design optimization, where PSO mainly supported preference prediction and form optimization. In contrast, the present study uses PSO to correct the consistency of fuzzy judgment matrices within a hierarchical requirement evaluation model. The two studies are similar in using intelligent optimization to reduce uncertainty in furniture design decisions, but they differ in their decision targets: perceptual form optimization in Fu et al.13 versus requirement weighting and scheme selection for office chair products in the present study. Li et al.20 developed a structured street-furniture design evaluation framework for public-environment furniture. While both studies use structured evaluation frameworks to support furniture design decision-making, the present study addresses a different product context. Office chairs involve prolonged bodily contact, ergonomic support, and operational reliability, whereas street furniture is more closely related to public-environment use, spatial integration, and urban service experience. This difference helps explain why safety and basic functional requirements carried greater weight in the office chair context.

At the same time, the strengths of PSO–FAHP should be understood within clear boundaries. The added value of PSO is expected to be most meaningful when the evaluation system contains many interrelated criteria, when expert pairwise comparisons show noticeable inconsistency, or when subjective design attributes must be transformed into stable weights. Under such conditions, fuzzy judgment and optimization complement one another effectively. By contrast, if the criterion structure is small, highly stable, or dominated by directly measurable engineering variables, the additional optimization step may provide limited practical benefit. The contribution of PSO–FAHP is therefore conditional rather than universal: it is especially useful for requirement-oriented evaluation problems that are too subjective for purely quantitative comparison and too complex for unoptimized pairwise judgment alone8,13.

The present findings also carry practical implications for office chair development. At the concept selection stage, design teams should prioritize structural safety, ergonomic adaptability, and operational usability before overemphasizing visual novelty. This ordering is consistent with the logic of user-centered product development, in which fundamental performance provides the basis for later differentiation15. In practical terms, a chair that appears visually refined but performs weakly in support, stability, or adjustability is unlikely to receive the strongest overall evaluation once user requirements are weighted systematically. The proposed framework can therefore serve as a useful decision-support tool for concept screening, scheme comparison, and cross-functional discussion among design, engineering, and product teams.

A further implication concerns the role of aesthetics and innovation in office chair products evaluation. Their relatively lower weights in the present study should not be taken to mean that these dimensions are unimportant. Rather, they appear to function as value-enhancing attributes after core requirements have already been met. This interpretation is consistent with Maslow’s need hierarchy, in which higher-order needs become more influential once lower-order expectations are sufficiently satisfied14. For office chair products, appearance, stylistic coherence, and innovation may improve market appeal, but they are unlikely to compensate for weaknesses in comfort, safety, or engineering rationality. This is an important reminder for contemporary furniture development, where novelty is sometimes overemphasized at the expense of basic performance.

Several limitations should also be noted. First, the empirical comparison involved only four selected representative office chair alternatives, which limits the breadth of external validation. The reported ranking therefore cannot be generalized to all office chairs, all markets, or all user groups. It should be interpreted as conditional on the selected criteria, PSO–FAHP weights, evaluator group, score vector, and chair alternatives used in this study. Second, the alternatives were evaluated from product photographs, available product descriptions, visible structural and material information, adjustability descriptions, and functional specifications rather than from controlled physical testing or long-term use trials. As a result, criteria such as seat comfort, structural stability, material quality, and ergonomic adaptability should be interpreted cautiously.

Subjectivity was therefore reduced rather than eliminated, which remains a common feature of multi-criteria design research8. Third, the framework was tested only in the office chair products domain, and its applicability to other categories of furniture remains to be examined. Finally, although the study structured user requirements systematically, it did not yet integrate large-scale behavioral evidence such as online review data, market feedback, or post-use evaluation.

Future research can extend this work in several directions. One promising path is to combine hierarchical evaluation with user-generated text mining, allowing expert-based weighting to be compared with market-level evidence22. Another is to test whether the requirement hierarchy shifts across different user groups or usage contexts, such as home office chair products, elderly-friendly seating, or public waiting furniture. It would also be valuable to compare PSO–FAHP directly with other hybrid decision methods in order to determine under what conditions optimization leads to meaningful gains in stability, efficiency, or explanatory power23. Through such extensions, the proposed framework could develop from a product-specific evaluation tool into a more general approach to requirement-oriented decision-making in furniture and other human-centered product systems. This broader applicability is supported by recent empirical work showing that structured design evaluation frameworks can also be extended to public-environment furniture contexts beyond office chair products [24].

Conclusion

This study developed a demand-oriented evaluation framework for office chair design by integrating the fuzzy analytic hierarchy process with particle swarm optimization. Grounded in Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, the study established a hierarchical evaluation index system that translates diverse user requirements into a structured decision model. Through the incorporation of PSO, the consistency of the fuzzy judgment matrix was improved, enabling a more stable and operational weighting process for multi-criteria design evaluation.

An empirical case study involving four selected representative office chair alternatives further demonstrated the applicability of the proposed framework. The results showed that the model could support conditional comparison among the selected design alternatives and identify the highest-scoring alternative under the selected criteria, weights, evaluators, and scoring scale. In particular, the findings indicated that safety, basic functional performance, and engineering rationality were more influential than aesthetic and innovation-related attributes in the evaluation of office chair products, highlighting the importance of lower-order and use-critical requirements in this product category.

Overall, the proposed PSO–FAHP framework provides a feasible and methodologically robust approach for office chair design assessment under conditions characterized by fuzziness, subjectivity, and difficulty in direct quantification. Rather than relying solely on intuitive judgment or isolated technical indicators, the method offers a more transparent and systematic basis for scheme comparison and design decision-making. The framework may therefore serve as a useful reference for future research and practice in office chair products development, as well as in other human-centered product evaluation contexts involving complex and hierarchical user demands. The added value of PSO should also be understood within clear boundary conditions. It is expected to be most useful when the evaluation system contains many interrelated criteria, when expert pairwise comparisons show noticeable inconsistency, or when subjective design attributes need to be transformed into stable weights. If the criterion structure is small, highly stable, or dominated by directly measurable engineering variables, the additional optimization step may provide limited practical benefit. Beyond office chairs, the proposed PSO–FAHP evaluation framework may be adapted to other human-centered product groups with hierarchical and partly subjective requirements, such as public waiting seats, elderly-friendly chairs, student desks and chairs, modular storage furniture, and rehabilitation seating products. These products similarly require designers to balance functional, ergonomic, safety-related, experiential, and contextual criteria. These examples suggest that PSO–FAHP should be viewed as a decision-support approach for complex product evaluation contexts rather than as a universally superior evaluation method.
