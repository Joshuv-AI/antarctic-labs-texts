Evaluating the impact of 2008 NICE dental prophylaxis guidelines on surgically treated infective endocarditis: a single centre retrospective analysis
Authors: Rickesh Bharat Karsan, Tara Chan-A-Sue, Rachel Roberts, Susan Durkan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesIn 2008, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommended cessation of antibiotic prophylaxis for at-risk patients undergoing dental procedures (CG64). This study aimed to evaluate the impact of these guidelines on the incidence, microbiological profile, and surgical characteristics of infective endocarditis (IE) requiring operative intervention.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed using a prospectively maintained surgical database to identify all cases of surgically treated IE at a UK cardiothoracic centre from 2004 to 2023. Patients were stratified into pre- and post-2008 cohorts. Temporal trends in surgical IE incidence were evaluated alongside changes in microbiology, valve involvement, urgency of surgery, and proportion of dental-related organisms. Statistical comparisons included Welch’s t-test, Fisher’s Exact Test, chi-squared analysis, and Poisson regression.ResultsA total of 313 surgical IE cases were identified (41 pre-2008, 272 post-2008). Mean annual incidence rose from 10.0 pre-2008 to 18.1 post-2008 cases per year. Poisson regression demonstrated a significantly higher incidence post-2008 (incidence rate ratio [IRR] 1.81, 95% CI 1.30–2.53, p < 0.001). The odds of dental-associated pathogens were unchanged (OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.24–1.74, p = 0.39). Staphylococcus aureus predominated in the later cohort, while the proportion of oral streptococci remained stable. Surgical characteristics, including prosthetic valve involvement (OR 1.61, 95% CI 0.78–3.29, p = 0.20) and urgency (OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.35–2.27, p = 0.81), were similar between groups.ConclusionsThe 2008 NICE recommendation to discontinue dental antibiotic prophylaxis was associated with a significant rise in surgically managed IE at our centre. However, the microbiological and surgical profiles of IE remained consistent, with no increase in cases attributable to oral streptococci. These findings suggest that factors beyond dental prophylaxis may concomitantly contribute to the observed rise in surgical IE.

Introduction

Infective endocarditis (IE) remains a significant clinical burden with high prevalence of morbidity and mortality, many cases of which require surgical intervention [1]. The incidence of IE has been seen to increase globally [2]. Surgery is required in up to 50% of IE cases, frequently in an urgent or emergency setting, and plays a key role in reducing embolic risk and controlling infection [3].

Prevention of IE in at-risk patients had previously suggested antibiotic prophylaxis for invasive dental procedures. This practice has been increasingly scrutinised due to concerns over antimicrobial resistance, limited supporting evidence, and the risks of unnecessary antibiotic use. By the mid-2000s, emerging research challenged the long-standing assumption that transient bacteraemia from dental procedures was a major contributor to IE, especially in the absence of established cardiac conditions [4].

In 2008, the United Kingdom (UK) National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) issued a significant change to guidelines for dental prophylaxis in 2008, recommending the complete cessation of antibiotic prophylaxis for at-risk patients undergoing invasive dental procedures (NICE CG64) [5]. This decision diverged from guidelines published by the American Heart Association (AHA) and European Society of Cardiology (ESC), which continued to recommend prophylaxis for high-risk patients [6, 7]. This change was informed by several key observations including, the risk of developing IE from everyday activities (e.g., tooth brushing) may exceed that from single dental procedures, most cases of IE occur in individuals without identifiable risk factors or procedures, there was no high-quality evidence demonstrating a reduction in IE incidence through prophylactic antibiotics, and that the potential harm from adverse drug reactions and the contribution to antimicrobial resistance outweighed the uncertain benefit [8, 9].

This was a significant departure from international guidelines that has sparked debate within medical and surgical communities. Several studies subsequently examined the impact of antibiotic cessation for dental procedures on IE incidence, with conflicting results [10–12]. While some observational analyses reported an increase in IE cases post-2008, others found no significant change. This underscores the need for context-specific evaluation [13, 14].

The implication of the NICE 2008 guideline change on the surgical burden of IE remains unclear. Large national datasets can provide broad trends however, they ultimately lack granular clinical detail such as valve involvement, microbiology profiles and operative urgency.

We hypothesise that the incidence of surgically treated IE has increased following the publication of the 2008 NICE CG64 guidelines. The aim of this study was to evaluate temporal changes in the incidence, microbiological profile, and surgical management of active infective endocarditis in a high-volume UK cardiothoracic centre conducting up to 1000 cases a year, with a specific focus on the period before and after the 2008 NICE guideline change in surgically treated IE patients.

Methods

The Dendrite database, maintained using software developed by Dendrite Clinical Systems, was analysed to identify all surgically treated cases of infective endocarditis between 2004 and 2022. The Dendrite database is a prospectively maintained, national clinical registry used for the collection of adult cardiac surgical data across UK centres. It captures detailed peri-operative, procedural, and outcome variables for patients undergoing cardiac surgery, with data entered locally by participating institutions using standardised definitions. The database is routinely used for national audit, quality assurance, and outcomes reporting.

Cases were stratified according to the timing of surgery relative to the 2008 change in National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidance on antibiotic prophylaxis. Accordingly, the cohort was divided into a pre-2008 group (operations performed between 2004 and 2007, with 2004 being the introduction year for the database) and a post-2008 group (operations performed between 2008 and 2022), with 2008 selected as the cut-off year to reflect the guideline change.

Microbiological data were obtained for each case based on blood and intra-operative tissue culture results. Identified pathogens were further reviewed to determine organisms recognised as having a potential dental origin. Annual counts of surgically treated infective endocarditis were derived using the date of operation. Cases in which no causative organism was identified were classified as culture-negative infective endocarditis and included in overall incidence analyses but excluded from organism-specific subgroup analyses.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for each era, including total number of cases, duration of observation, and mean annual incidence. Mean annual case numbers between eras were initially compared using Welch’s two-sample t-test. Given the count-based nature of the data, incidence rates were subsequently compared using a Poisson regression model, mitigating bias from unequal era durations, with era (pre- vs. post-2008) included as the explanatory variable. Results are presented as incidence rate ratios (IRR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Subgroup analyses were performed according to valve type, causative organism, and native versus prosthetic valve infection. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 313 surgically treated cases were identified, 41 performed pre-2008 and 272 post-2008. Males constituted the majority overall (n = 244). Mean EuroSCORE II was similar between groups (11.65 vs. 10.69). Urgent operations predominated in both cohorts (n = 227) (Table 1).

Incidence of infective endocarditis

A total of 40 surgically treated infective endocarditis cases were recorded in the pre-2008 era (2004–2007), compared with 272 cases in the post-2008 era (2008–2022). The mean annual incidence increased from 10.0 cases per year pre-2008 to 18.1 cases per year post-2008 (Table 2). When examined over time, an increase in incidence was observed after 2008 (Fig. 1). Annual case numbers prior to 2008 remained consistently low (< 20 cases/year), whereas after 2008 the incidence almost doubled, peaking at 32 cases in 2021. Statistical modelling confirmed this increase: Poisson regression demonstrated a significantly higher incidence of surgically treated infective endocarditis in the post-2008 era compared with the pre-2008 era (incidence rate ratio [IRR] 1.81, 95% CI 1.30–2.53, p < 0.001).

Microbiological profile

The distribution of causative organisms did not differ significantly between eras (p = 0.17). Staphylococcus aureus predominated in the post-2008 group, while coagulase-negative staphylococci and other organisms remained infrequent.

A total of 48 cases (15.3%) were associated with organisms of dental origin (e.g., Streptococcus viridans, sanguis, bovis). Of these, 7 cases (17.1%) occurred pre-2008 and 41 cases (15.1%) post-2008. The odds of dental-related IE post-2008 were not significantly different compared with pre-2008 (OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.24–1.74, p = 0.39) (Table 3).

Surgical characteristics

Aortic valve replacement was the most frequent procedure in both groups, with no statistically significant differences between eras (p = 0.22). The proportion of patients undergoing multivalve procedures was higher post-2008 (23.3% vs. 14.7%), though not significant (p = 0.24).

Prosthetic valve involvement was observed in 26.7% of pre-2008 cases and 21.8% post-2008. The odds of prosthetic valve involvement post-2008 were not significantly different (OR 1.61, 95% CI 0.78–3.29, p = 0.20). Native valve involvement remained more common in both eras (70.0% vs. 76.2%, p = 0.50) (Figs. 2 and 3).

Operative urgency

Urgent or emergency procedures comprised most cases in both groups. The likelihood of undergoing urgent/emergency surgery versus elective was unchanged post-2008 (OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.35–2.27, p = 0.81).

Discussion

The 2008 NICE guideline (CG64) recommending the cessation of antibiotic prophylaxis for dental procedures represented a major shift in IE prevention strategy [5]. Our study demonstrates that, in a single high-volume UK cardiothoracic centre, the incidence of surgically treated IE increased significantly after the guideline change. The mean annual incidence almost doubled in the post-2008 era, and Poisson regression confirmed this rise was statistically significant (IRR 1.81, 95% CI 1.30–2.53, p < 0.001).

Importantly, despite the rise in incidence, the microbiological profile remained stable. Staphylococcus aureus predominated in the later cohort, but the prevalence of oral streptococci did not increase. Similarly, the proportion of cases attributable to recognised dental-associated organisms was unchanged. Surgical characteristics including valve type, urgency, and prosthetic versus native valve involvement were also comparable between eras. Taken together, these findings suggest that while incidence rose, the observed increase cannot be attributed solely to a rise in dental-related IE or to shifts in surgical practice.

Although reduced dental antibiotic prophylaxis might be expected to increase prosthetic valve endocarditis, we did not observe an increase in rates post-2008. This may reflect limited power and confounding by temporal changes in case-mix but could also be explained by incomplete adoption of NICE CG64, with surveys demonstrating ongoing support for prophylaxis in high-risk patients (including prosthetic valve recipients) and continued prescribing after 2008 [13, 15]. Additionally, most prosthetic valve endocarditis is caused by staphylococci and other non-oral pathogens, likely diluting any effect of dental prophylaxis policy on overall incidence [16, 17].

Our results add nuance to the existing literature. Thornhill et al. reported no immediate increase in IE incidence following the 2008 guideline [13], whereas Dayer et al. observed a modest but significant rise in admissions above background trends [10]. More recently, Quan et al. found that IE incidence in England has risen over decades, but with no consistent inflection around 2008 and with a declining proportion of oral streptococcal cases [18]. The present study differs by focusing specifically on surgically treated IE a more severe subset and confirms that surgical burden has indeed increased, even though microbiological attribution to dental organisms has not.

The observed rise is likely multifactorial. Ageing populations, increasing implantation of prosthetic material and cardiac devices, rising rates of intravenous drug use, and improved diagnostic sensitivity all plausibly contribute to the growing incidence of IE [18, 19]. The absence of an increase in oral streptococcal cases reinforces the likelihood that factors beyond the withdrawal of dental prophylaxis are driving this trend.

This study has some notable limitations. Its single-centre, retrospective design may limit generalisability, as referral patterns, microbiological profiles, and surgical practices vary across institutions. The analysis focused exclusively on surgically treated IE, representing the most severe subset of the disease spectrum, and thus may not reflect trends in medically managed IE. The pre-2008 accounts for only 3 years’ worth of patients, this is due to the electronic database being implemented in 2004. Poisson regression inherently accounts for differing observation periods, mitigating bias from unequal era durations. This limits statistical power to detect subtle differences. Furthermore, classification of dental-associated pathogens relied on culture results alone, without dental histories, limiting direct linkage to procedures. Confounders such as changes in clinical practice, population demographics, intravenous drug use, antimicrobial prescribing behaviours, and diagnostic sensitivity over the study period may also have influenced outcomes. This study did not incorporate population-level denominators or referral rates to the study centre over time. However, the centre’s surgical catchment and referral pathways remained broadly stable during the study period, and the analysis was restricted to patients undergoing operative intervention recorded within a prospectively maintained national surgical registry. As such, the findings reflect changes in the surgical burden of infective endocarditis rather than diagnostic incidence or referral volume. While evolving referral thresholds and population demographics may have contributed to the observed increase, the absence of a corresponding rise in dental-associated organisms argues against dental prophylaxis cessation being the sole driver. Finally, molecular diagnostics were not incorporated, which may have improved identification in culture-negative cases.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable organism-level and surgical detail not available from national datasets. It highlights that while IE incidence has risen significantly in the surgical population post-2008, this has not been accompanied by a change in dental-associated pathogens.

To our knowledge, this is the first UK study to specifically evaluate the effect of the 2008 NICE guideline change on the incidence and microbiological characteristics of surgically treated IE. While prior research has assessed overall IE incidence using national datasets, these did not stratify by surgical intervention nor provide detailed microbiological and procedural insights.

Conclusion

This single-centre retrospective analysis found a significant increase in surgically treated infective endocarditis following the 2008 NICE guidelines recommending cessation of dental antibiotic prophylaxis. While mean annual incidence nearly doubled, the microbiological spectrum and surgical characteristics of IE remained stable, and the proportion of cases linked to oral flora did not rise.

These findings suggest that the guideline change may have coincided with but may not completely explain the observed increase in surgical IE. Broader epidemiological factors are likely contributory. Future national, microbiology-specific studies are needed to disentangle the effects of prophylaxis cessation from underlying secular trends.
