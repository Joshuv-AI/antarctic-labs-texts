Metabolomics analysis of antioxidant and lipid-lowering activities, colonic fermentation properties of quinoa bran polyphenol extract during in vitro digestion
Authors: Minjun Sun, Ying Miao, Sarina Ma, Rui Huo, Jingyu Xie, Yangyang Chen, Meili Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuinoa is a nutritious grain, and its by-product, bran, also contains a variety of active substances. In this study, the effect of extraction with different solvents (water, 50% ethanol, deep eutectic solvents (DESs)) on the polyphenol fractions, antioxidant and lipid-lowering activities and fermentation metabolites of quinoa bran was studied using in vitro digestion. The results showed that the main active compounds of quinoa bran polyphenol extract were flavonoids and cinnamic acid and derivatives, and phenolic acids. Quinoa bran polyphenol extracts using different solvents showed strong antioxidant and lipid-lowering activities, which were enhanced by in vitro digestion of the polyphenol extracts. Metabolomic analysis revealed that DESs and 50% ethanol solvents increased bile acid production by approximately twofold compared to water-extracted polyphenols, while also significantly boosting the generation of dipeptides and polyunsaturated fatty acids. These metabolites protect the intestinal barrier and regulate metabolic-immune homeostasis. DESs solvent can serve as a favorable extraction solvent for quinoa bran polyphenols. This study lays the foundation for the study of functional studies of quinoa bran polyphenols. Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Metabolic disorders caused by unhealthy diets and lifestyles trouble about one-third of the world’s population. Chronic metabolism-related diseases (e.g., Nonalcoholic Fatty Liver Disease) have been often considered to be involved in oxidation and fat accumulation1,2. Long-term medication control has the potential for gastrointestinal distress, myalgia, and kidney burden3. As important secondary metabolites of plants, polyphenols are widely found in teas, fruits, vegetables and grains as natural antioxidant products4. In recent years, numerous studies, including in vitro experiments5, cellular experiments6, animal experiments7, and clinical studies8, have verified that polyphenols exert antioxidant and lipid-lowering effects through multiple pathways9,10.

Quinoa itself is a nutritionally balanced and rich super grain11. Compared with other grains like wheat, corn, buckwheat, and oats, quinoa has a relatively higher content of polyphenols12, and its processing by-product, quinoa bran, is also rich in a variety of biologically active ingredients13. Quinoa planting area production in Inner Mongolia increased year by year, quinoa processing enterprises lack of deep processing research technology. Currently, studies have reported the extraction of polyphenols from quinoa seeds with organic solvents14, Melini et al. extracted quinoa polyphenols at a yield of 2.8 mg/g using ultrasonic-assisted extraction15, while Wang et al. increased the total phenol content of germinated quinoa by 20.48% through magnetic field pretreatment16. However, few studies have been reported on the extraction of polyphenols from quinoa bran. HPLC-DAD analysis of milled whole-grain quinoa showed that the main phenolic acids in it are ferulic acid and vanillic acid17. Previous researchers have mainly explored the use of different forms of adjuncts to enhance the extraction efficiency and biological activity of quinoa bran polyphenols, while the choice of extraction solvents is still limited. Deep eutectic solvents (DESs), a novel green solvent, are low melting point eutectic mixtures made by combining two or more compounds, the main advantages of which are low toxicity, simple synthesis, and inexpensive raw materials18,19. The polarity of DESs can be adjusted to match that of polyphenols by regulating its components. With hydrogen bonding interaction as the core driving force, it dissociates bound polyphenols, dissolves and stabilizes free polyphenols, thereby achieving efficient extraction of polyphenols20. Studies have been reported on the use of DESs to extract polyphenols from plants such as olive leaves21 and grape skin22. In addition, the DESs composed of L-proline and glycerol as food-grade materials has a high density of hydrogen bond sites and rich types of such sites, enabling it to enhance the solubility of polyphenols and preserve their activity23. However, the study of quinoa bran polyphenol extraction and activity using DESs has not yet been reported.

Simulation of in vitro digestion, a simple low-cost reproducible method to study the changes in activity of polyphenol extracts through the oral, gastric, and intestinal tracts24. Shang et al. employed dynamic and static digestion models to evaluate digestibility differences and antioxidant properties of apple polyphenols25. In vitro digestion experiments effectively explain the changes in functional activity after polyphenol digestion without ethical implications. In addition, metabolomics enabled molecular level resolution of active substance transformations in the polyphenol digestion pathway26. In order to investigate the ability of different solvents to extract polyphenols from quinoa bran and the changes in polyphenol composition, functional activity during the digestion process. Here, this study screened distilled water, organic solvents, and DESs solutions as extraction solvents for quinoa bran polyphenols. For the first time, the effects of different solvent extractions on the polyphenol fractions, antioxidant and lipid-lowering activities of quinoa bran were investigated using in vitro digestion. In addition, the conformational relationships between polyphenol fractions and antioxidant and lipid-lowering activities during in vitro digestion were investigated by redundancy analysis. Further, the in vitro fermentation properties of quinoa bran polyphenols were analyzed based on metabolomics, revealing the regulatory effects of quinoa bran polyphenols on intestinal metabolite molecules. Our results provide important theoretical references for the extraction of polyphenolic active substances from quinoa bran and the effective utilization of functional active ingredients.

Results

Extraction solvent screening and optimization

In order to efficiently extract phenolic compounds from quinoa bran, a screening of the best extraction solvents was performed by employing distilled water, different organic solvents and DESs. The specific compounds and molar ratios of the 12 DESs solutions were detailed in Table 1. For the primary sieving, the process conditions were material to liquid ratio 1:10 (W/W), water bath temperature 50 °C, water bath time 30 min, DESs with 30% water content. 50% ethanol (50% EtOH) was significantly higher than the other organic solvents for extraction, and DES-12 had the highest polyphenol extraction compared to the other DESs, with a significant difference (Fig. 1A). Therefore, distilled water (H2O) extraction was used as a control, and 50% EtOH and DES-12, which have the best extraction ability among the organic solvents and DESs solvents, respectively. One-factor optimization of quinoa bran polyphenol extraction with water, 50% EtOH and DES-12 was carried out, respectively. Finally, the optimal extraction conditions for water were: material-liquid ratio 1:40; water bath temperature 60 °C; water bath time 40 min (Fig. 1B). The optimal extraction conditions for ethanol were: ethanol concentration 50%; material-liquid ratio 1:40; water bath temperature water bath temperature 60 °C; water bath time 30 min (Fig. 1C). The optimal extraction conditions for DES-12 were: water content 40%; material-liquid ratio 1:30; water bath temperature water bath temperature 50 °C The optimal extraction conditions for DES-12 were 40% water content; 1:30 material-liquid ratio; 50 °C water bath temperature; 30 min water bath time (Fig. 1D). The optimal polyphenol extractions of H2O, 50% EtOH, and DES-12 were 4.60, 5.22, and 5.73 mg GAE/g, respectively. Further, after purification with macroporous resin, the polyphenol extracts significantly increased the polyphenol content to 164.16, 162.01, and 166.80 mg GAE/g for extracts in water, ethanol, and DES solvents, respectively (Fig. S1).

Flavonoids were dominated by quercetin and kaempferol and glycosylated derivatives, in addition to cinnamic acid and derivatives, caffeic acid and vanillic acid and other phenolic acid compounds. Quantitative analysis results showed that the main phenolic compounds extracted from H2O, 50% EtOH, and DES-12 were 205.40, 123.09, and 119.29 mg/g, respectively (Table S2).

Changes in antioxidant activity of polyphenol extracts during in vitro digestion

This study used six antioxidant indexes to evaluate the changes in antioxidant capacity of polyphenol extracts during in vitro digestion. The 50% EtOH-extracted polyphenol extract had the highest DPPH• scavenging capacity of 584.81 μmol TE/g DW at the undigested stage. The three groups of polyphenol extracts were reduced by oral digestion and then gradually increased by gastric and intestinal digestion, and the DES-12 extracted polyphenol extract had the highest scavenging capacity of 557.03 μmol TE/g DW at the end of intestinal digestion (Fig. 2A). The clearance of ABTS+• by DES-12 extracted polyphenol extract in the undigested stage was significantly higher than that of the H2O and 50% EtOH groups at 647.95 μmol TE/g DW. Similarly, the clearance of ABTS+• by the three groups of polyphenol extracts decreased and then increased during in vitro digestion, with the highest clearance of DES-12 extracted polyphenol extract at the end of intestinal digestion at 920.54 μmol TE/g DW, which was comparable to that of the 50% EtOH-extracted (Fig. 2B). For •OH, DES-12 extracted polyphenol extract had the highest scavenging capacity of 386.08 μmol TE/g DW. After oral digestion, the scavenging capacity of the three groups of polyphenol extracts for •OH declined, and then rebounded after gastrointestinal digestion, and there was no significant difference in the scavenging capacity of the three groups of extracts for •OH during the digestion process (Fig. 2C). 50% EtOH-extracted polyphenol extract had the strongest O2-• scavenging capacity of 289.14 μmol TE/g DW, which was significantly higher than that of H2O extraction. In the gastric and intestinal digestion stages, 50% EtOH-extracted polyphenol extract had a significantly higher capacity to scavenge O2-• than that of the remaining two groups (Fig. 2D). For ORAC, there was no significant difference between H2O, 50% EtOH and DES-12 extracts. After in vitro digestion, the ORAC of the three groups of polyphenol extracts was enhanced, and at the end of the intestinal digestion, the ORAC of the 50% EtOH extract of polyphenol extract was the largest at 54.53 μmol TE/g DW (Fig. 2E). There was no significant difference in the ferric ion reducing power of H2O, 50% EtOH and DES-12 extracts. After oral digestion, DES-extracted polyphenol extract had the strongest ferric ion reducing power of 345.33 μmol TE/g DW, which was significantly higher than that of H2O and 50% EtOH. There was no significant difference between the three groups of extracts during the gastric and intestinal digestion stages (Fig. 2F). Undigested, DES-12 solvent extracted polyphenol extract had significantly higher scavenging capacity for ABTS+•, •OH and O2-• than H2O extraction.

Changes in lipid-lowering activity of polyphenol extracts during in vitro digestion

The present study evaluated the changes in lipid-lowering activity of polyphenol extract during in vitro digestion by examining the binding capacity of three bile salts, SC, SGC, and STC, as well as the inhibition rates of pancreatic lipase and cholesterol esterase. Before digestion, there was no significant difference in the binding capacity of polyphenol extracts of H2O, 50% EtOH, and DES-12 for the three bile salts, and the 50% EtOH extract had the strongest capacity to bind bile salts, with 44.34, 34,67, and 24.81 μmol per gram of dry sample for SC, SGC, and STC, respectively (Fig. 3A–C). The ability of H2O, 50% EtOH and DES extracts to bind SC decreased after oral digestion, and the ability of the three groups of polyphenol extracts to bind SC increased during the gastrointestinal digestion stage. At the end of digestion, DES-12 had the strongest ability to bind SC at 51.62 μmol/g, significantly higher than that of H2O extract (Fig. 3A). The ability of H2O, 50% EtOH and DES extracted polyphenol extract to bind SGC decreased after oral digestion, and the ability to bind SGC was elevated during the gastrointestinal digestion stage. After gastric digestion of polyphenol extracts, DES extract had the strongest ability to bind SGC at 31.18 μmol/g significantly higher than the extracts of H2O and 50% EtOH (Fig. 3B). Similarly, the ability of the three groups of polyphenol extracts to bind STC decreased after oral digestion and was elevated during the gastrointestinal stage of digestion, and there was no significant difference in the ability of H2O, 50% EtOH, and DES-12 extracts to bind STC during in vitro digestion (Fig. 3C). In the undigested portion, for the binding capacity of the three bile salts, which was not significantly different between the polyphenol extracts extracted with different solvents, was increased after in vitro digestion. For lipase inhibition, DES-12 extracted polyphenol extract had the highest inhibition of 48.27% when undigested, and the inhibition was first slightly decreased then increased by the in vitro digestion process. After intestinal digestion, DES-12 extracted polyphenol extract lipase had the highest inhibition rate of 53.02% (Fig. 3D). When undigested, DES-12 and 50% EtOH extracted polyphenol extract inhibited the CE pair significantly more than H2O extraction. After intestinal digestion, 50% EtOH extracted polyphenol extract showed the highest inhibition rate of 60.06% for CE (Fig. 3E).

Polyphenol composition in relation to antioxidant and lipid-lowering activities

This study analyzed the trend changes in the content of the main 10 polyphenols during in vitro digestion of polyphenol extracts extracted with H2O, 50% EtOH and DES-12 by UPLC-Orbitrap-MS. Flavonoids (Quercetin, Rutin, Kaempferol, Quercetin 3-β-D-glucoside and Kaempferol-3-O-glucoside) were released for decomposition upon in vitro digestion, and Quercetin 3-β-D-glucoside and Kaempferol-3-O-glucoside showed a tendency to rise after gastric digestion, probably due to hydrolysis of polysaccharides into monosaccharides (Fig. 4A). Similarly, cinnamic acid and derivatives decomposed by in vitro digestion showed a decreasing trend in p-Hydroxycinnamic Acid, Trans-Ferulic acid and Sinapic acid content (Fig. 4B). Salicylic acid was decomposed by in vitro digestion and the content first decreased and then stabilized. The content of vanillic acid was essentially stable during in vitro digestion and was elevated after oral and gastric digestion of DES-12 extract (Fig. 4C).

Spearman analysis revealed that during digestion, Quercetin 3-β-D-glucoside, Kaempferol-3-O-glucoside, and Vanillic acid showed significant positive correlations with antioxidant and lipid-lowering markers (Fig. 4D). Due to the presence of more phenolic hydroxyl groups capable of interacting with free radicals, Quercetin 3-β-D-glucoside and Kaempferol-3-O-glucoside demonstrated stronger antioxidant activity. Further, in order to investigate the association between polyphenols and changes in antioxidant and lipid-lowering activities during in vitro digestion, polyphenols and indicators of antioxidant and lipid-lowering activities were subjected to redundancy analysis (RDA) (Fig. 4E). The results indicated that during in vitro digestion, quercetin-3-β-D-glucoside, rutin, kaempferol-3-O-glucoside and vanillic acid are the key components for antioxidant and lipid-lowering activities.

Colonic fermentation properties of quinoa bran polyphenol extracts

Metabolomics analysis was able to clearly explain the effects of polyphenolic compounds on small molecules that mimic enteric fermentation systems. Metabolomics was used to analyze the effects of H2O, 50% EtOH, and DES-12 solvent extracted polyphenol extracts on the action of molecular substances and functional constituents of in vitro fermentation. The main molecular substances identified by metabolomics were mainly carboxylic acids and derivatives, lipids, organic oxygen and organic nitrogen compounds, indoles and their derivatives, and flavonoids (Fig. S3). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that PCA1 and PCA2 explained 81.8% and 16.11% of the metabolic data information between the groups effectively. H2O, 50% EtOH, and DES-12 groups were significantly separated from the CK group, indicating that polyphenol extracts significantly altered intestinal metabolites (Fig. S4). In addition, the pH values of the H2O, 50% EtOH and DES-12 extracted polyphenol extract fermentation groups were slightly decreased compared to the control group, suggesting that polyphenol extract promotes the production and release of organic acids during the fermentation process (Table S3). Further, H2O, 50% EtOH, and DES-12 extracts were screened for differential metabolites in vitro fermentation versus controls (VIP > 1, FC > 1.5 or FC < 0.67, p < 0.05). As shown in Table 3, a total of 43 metabolites showed significant changes compared to the CK group, mainly bile acids and derivatives, amino acids, peptides, fatty Acyls, prenol lipids and flavonoids. Compared to H2O extraction, DES-12 and 50% EtOH solvent extraction yielded a greater number of differential compounds, suggesting that DES-12 and 50% EtOH solvents may be more conducive to the functional properties of polyphenols. The significant increase in bile acids, dipeptides, polyunsaturated fatty acids, prenol lipids and phenol-related metabolites during in vitro fermentation of quinoa bran polyphenolic extracts served to protect the intestinal barrier and regulate metabolic-immune homeostasis, and the significant decrease in L-palmitoylcarnitine may be related to liver protection.

Discussion

This study investigated the effects of quinoa bran extracts using different solvents (H2O, 50% EtOH and DESs) on in vitro antioxidant activity, lipid-lowering activity, and fermentation characteristics. The properties of DESs such as polarity, melting point and viscosity affect their extraction efficiency of polyphenol actives from quinoa bran, and the selection of suitable DESs is the key to the effective extraction of polyphenols27. The DES-12 extraction of proline and glycerol compositions was significantly higher than that of other DES solvents as well as conventional solvents, which may be attributed to a better match with polyphenol polarity28. Quinoa bran polyphenolic compounds were mainly composed of flavonoids and cinnamic acid and derivatives, which are similar to previously studied quinoa seed polyphenolic compounds29–31.

Studies have been reported to support that antioxidant capacity is affected by changes in the active substance composition of polyphenols after digestion32. The highest total polyphenol content was measured by the Folin method for DES-12 extraction, so this may be due to the fact that the antioxidant activity is closely related to the polarity of the solvent as well as the extracted content of polyphenolic substances33. The quinoa bran polyphenol extracts primarily consisted of complex molecular structures such as rutin, quercetin, kaempferol, and p-hydroxycinnamic acid, which were difficult to utilize directly. Their antioxidant activity could not be fully released and required digestion and the action of gut microbiota to cleave the complex structures into smaller, more stable phenolic compounds with higher antioxidant activity34. The antioxidant capacity of polyphenol extracts extracted with H2O, 50% EtOH and DES-12 solvent was enhanced after digestion, which may be attributed to the structural changes of polyphenols after digestion, increase in phenolic hydroxyls, and higher pH in the intestinal environment, which is more favorable for scavenging free radicals35,36. Polyphenol binding to bile salts reduces bile salt absorption, and inhibition of pancreatic lipase and cholesterol esterase reduces fat and cholesterol absorption37. Notably, after digestion, the polyphenolic compounds extracted from DES-12 solvent and 50% EtOH exhibited higher bile salt binding capacity than those from water extracts. This result suggests that polyphenols are decomposed by gastrointestinal digestion into more active metabolites as well as smaller molecules exposed to bile salts38,39. In the gastrointestinal digestive fraction, there was no significant difference in the inhibition of PL and CE by polyphenol extracts extracted from the three solutions. However, DES-12 showed the highest inhibition of PL and CE compared to H2O and 50% EtOH extracted polyphenol extract when undigested, probably because the natural synergistic potentiation of DES solvent extraction releases other phytoconstituents of quinoa bran, and the synergistic effect enhances the lipid-lowering activity40. these results were similar to those of studies that have been reported41,42, thus DES-12 is an effective solvent for polyphenol extract extraction and protects and enhances the biological activity of polyphenols. Previous studies reported that these flavonoids are bioactive components of antioxidant activity and lipid metabolism regulation, modulating oxidative stress-related pathways43. Simulated in vitro digestion in static models may lead to excessive degradation of flavonoids in prolonged incubation, and flavonoids exposed to neutral or alkaline environments that mimic the oral cavity are more readily broken down and converted to simpler phenolic acids44. According to a previous study reported, unlike flavonoids, cinnamic acid and derivatives are less bioavailable, released by oral digestion and degraded during gastrointestinal digestion, therefore are not the main cause of the impact on the antioxidant and lipid-lowering activities45. The stabilization or increase of phenolic acids during gastrointestinal digestion may be due to the decomposition of flavonoids, which increases the bioavailability of flavonoids46. Peng et al.47 reported that the main polyphenolic compounds of moringa oleifera lam. leaves extracted using DESs consisted of quercetin-3-β-D-glucoside, rutin, kaempferol-3-O-glucoside, vitexin and quercetin, which have greater antioxidant activity than conventional solvents. This is in agreement with our research findings. The correlation with antioxidant and lipid-lowering activities was higher in the gastrointestinal-digested polyphenol extracts compared to the undigested portion, probably due to the in vitro digestion of small-molecule metabolites that produce higher antioxidant activity48.

Further in vitro fermentation studies explored the effects of quinoa bran polyphenol extracts on metabolites. The addition of polyphenol extracts for in vitro fermentation significantly increased intestinal Bile acids, alcohols and derivatives compared to CK group. Bile acids are known to be endogenous ligands for the farnesoid X receptor, which, when activated, regulate intestinal flora, lipid metabolism, and inflammatory responses49. three polyphenol-treated groups greatly elevated the relative content of β-muricholic acid. Takada et al.50 found that β-muricholic acid inhibited lipid accumulation in primary mouse hepatocytes by mouse experiments. In addition, the increased abundance of deoxycholic acid also served to slow localized fat accumulation51. Therefore, β-muricholic acid and deoxycholic acid have research potential in obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. The 50% EtOH/CK and DES-12/CK groups, compared to the H2O/CK group, increased the abundance of glycodeoxycholic acid. In an investigation on inflammatory bowel disease52, it was found that levels of glyco-deoxycholic acid were negatively correlated with inflammatory models. Combined with the results of the present study, this can be explained by the fact that DES-12 and 50% EtOH extracted polyphenols were able to perform stronger antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects than H2O extracted polyphenols. Three groups of polyphenols induced up-regulation of dipeptidyl peptides and DL-proline by in vitro fermentation may play a role in maintaining mucosal barrier integrity, inhibiting pathogenic bacterial colonization, and modulating oxidative damage in the colon53,54. DL-Methionine sulfoxide, a biomarker of oxidative stress, was significantly reduced in abundance levels by the in vitro fermentation of three groups of polyphenols, suggesting an antioxidant role of polyphenols in the intestine. Upregulation of α-Linolenic acid abundance is critical for maintaining intestinal homeostasis and alleviating inflammatory responses. According to a previous study, L-palmitoylcarnitine was reported to be a late marker of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease55, so polyphenols fermented in vitro to modulate the decline of L-palmitoylcarnitine may be relevant to the prevention of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. Prenol lipids can play a role in metabolic and immune homeostasis in the gut. Up-regulation of farnesal abundance may have implications for the role of antipathogenicity, as well as the conversion to active metabolites, indirectly interfering with intestinal metabolic homeostasis56. In addition, H2O, 50% EtOH, and DES-12 extracts treated groups after fermentation upregulated flavonoids and other phenolic acids, which may act synergistically with beneficial intestinal bacteria to protect the gut57.

The possible metabolic pathway of quinoa bran polyphenols after digestion and fermentation was proposed (Fig. 5). Rutin, Quercetin 3-β-D-glucoside, and Kaempferol-3-O-glucoside were converted to Quercetin and Kaempferol through deglycosylation. Quercetin and Kaempferol were further cleaved by dioxygenase into the smaller molecules 3,4-Dihydroxyphenylacetic acid and 3-(4-Hydroxyphenyl) propionic acid, enhancing bioavailability. Additionally, the differential metabolite Quercetin 3’-methyl ether in the 50% EtOH/CK group was produced by gut microbial methylation. This compound is a key antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agent, potentially benefiting from Quercetin’s role in reshaping the gut microbiota58. Trans-ferulic acid and p-hydroxycinnamic acid rapidly converted to p-hydroxybenzoic acid through demethylation and oxidation reactions. At this stage, p-Hydroxybenzoic acid is nearing excretion from the body, and changes in its activity may not be the primary factor. Therefore, flavonoids likely dominate the antioxidant and lipid-lowering effects of quinoa bran polyphenol extracts.

Possible metabolic pathways of bioactive compounds in quinoa bran polyphenol extracts.

In summary, H2O, 50% EtOH, and DES-12 solvent extracts of quinoa bran polyphenols exhibited antioxidant and hypolipidemic activities. The main active components of the polyphenol extracts were flavonoids, cinnamic acid derivatives and phenolic acids. Among them, Rutin, Quercetin 3-β-D-glucoside, Kaempferol-3-O-glucoside and Vanillic acid contents correlated higher with antioxidant activity and lipid-lowering activity during in vitro digestion. Compared to the aqueous extract, the DES-12 and 50% EtOH solution extracts of polyphenols demonstrated stronger antioxidant and lipid-lowering activities as well as metabolic regulation. Fermentation of polyphenol extracts increased the abundance concentrations of bile acids and dipeptides, which were associated with maintenance of the intestinal barrier and reduction of intestinal oxidative stress. Thus, quinoa bran polyphenol extract has antioxidant and lipid-lowering activities, and DES-12 solvent has potential application in quinoa bran polyphenol extraction. Although results indicate that polyphenolic metabolites can synergistically interact with beneficial gut microbiota to regulate microbial balance and maintain intestinal health, this study still has limitations. In vitro studies cannot replicate organ-level synergistic effects or reflect the true bioavailability of polyphenolic compounds. Such research serves only as preliminary screening to explore the functional activity of quinoa bran polyphenols, requiring further validation through animal models.

Methods

Materials

Quinoa bran was offered by Inner Mongolia Yiji Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hohhot, China). Choline chloride, betaine, L-proline, folin-ciocalteu, gallic acid, Sodium cholate (SC), sodium glycocholate (SGC), sodium taurocholate (STC), lipase (15-35 units/mg), cholesterol esterase (≥300 units/g dry weight), pepsin (>3000 U/mg) and trypsin (>10000 Unit/mg) were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Oxalic acid, lactic acid, urea and glycerol were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2, 2’-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), ethanol, methanol and acetone were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Oxygen Radical Antioxidant Capacity (ORAC) Fluorometric Assay Kit was purchased from Abbkine (Wuhan, China).

Preparation of DESs

DESs were produced using a slight modification of the methodology of the previous study59. Briefly, hydrogen bond donors and hydrogen bond acceptors were heated and stirred in a water bath at a specific ratio (70 °C, 200 rpm, 90 min) to form a clear liquid, which was then diluted with 30% distilled water. The prepared solvents of DESs were stored at 4 °C.

Extraction of polyphenols from quinoa bran

The quinoa bran was ground into a fine powder (80 mesh) and mixed with water, organic solvents (50% methanol aqueous solution, 50% ethanol aqueous solution, 50% acetone aqueous solution) and DESs, respectively, and extracted in a water bath at 60 °C for 30 min, followed by centrifugation (5000 r/min) for 10 min, and the supernatant was fractionated on a column containing AB-8 macroporous resin (26 mm * 60 cm). Gradient elution was performed at 1 BV/h using 20% ethanol, 50% ethanol, 70% ethanol, and 90% ethanol sequentially. The eluate from the 70% ethanol fraction was collected, ethanol was removed by vacuum rotary evaporation, and the residue was freeze-dried for 24 hours. The freeze-dried QBP was collected for subsequent use.

Determination of the total polyphenols

Gallic acid was used as a standard by the modified folin-ciocalteu method60. Folinol was added to an equal volume of sample. After 5 minutes, 2 mL of 10% sodium carbonate solution was added, followed by diluting with distilled water to a final volume of 25 mL. The mixture was then left to stand in the dark for 1 h before measuring the absorbance at 765 nm. The results were expressed as gallic acid milligram equivalents (GAE) per gram of dry weight of quinoa bran polyphenols. Calibration Curve: Y = 0.005*X + 0.026, R2 = 0.997.

In vitro gastrointestinal digestion

Referring to previous in vitro simulated digestion models61, all digestion processes were performed at 37 °C, 140 rpm/min. The main components of digestive fluids were as follows: simulated salivary fluid (15.1 mM KCl, 3.7 mM KH2PO4, pH 7) simulated gastric fluid (6.9 mM KCl, 47.2 mM NaCl, 25 mM NaHCO3, pH 3) simulated intestinal fluid (6.8 mM KCl, 38.4 mM NaCl, 85 mM NaHCO3, pH 7).

In the oral phase, 1 mL of sample solution (5 mg/mL) was added to 1 mL of simulated salivary fluid and 10 µL of CaCl2(H2O)2 (0.3 M) reacted for 2 min. In the gastric digestion phase, gastric simulant 1:1 (v/v) was added to the buccal digested sample solution, followed by the addition of 5 µL of CaCl2(H2O)2 (0.03 M) and pepsin (2000 u/mL) for 2 h of reaction. In the intestinal digestion phase, intestinal simulant 1:1 (v/v) was added to the gastric-digested sample solution, followed by the addition of 20 µL CaCl2(H2O)2 (0.03 M), trypsin (100 u/mL), and bile salts (10 mmol/L) for a 2 h reaction.

Identification and quantification of phenolic compounds

Following our previous method62. quinoa bran polyphenol extracts from different digestion stages were analyzed using the UPLC-Orbitrap-MS system (UPLC, Vanquish; MS, QE). Chromatographic conditions: phase A: ultrapure water solution (containing 0.1% formic acid), phase B: acetonitrile solution (containing 0.1% formic acid) 0-2 min 10% B; 2-6 min 60% B; 6-9 min 60% B; 9-12 min 10% B. Q Exactive high resolution mass spectrometry detection system (Thermo, USA). The scan mode was full scan-ddMS2. The scanning range of primary mass spectrometry (scan m/z range) was 100-900. Quantitative analysis was performed using the external standard method. The calibration curve equation is shown in Table S1.

Antioxidant activity assay

Antioxidant activity was determined with reference to previous study63. All quinoa bran polyphenol samples were formulated to 5 mg/mL for antioxidant activity assay and the results were expressed as trolox mol equivalents per g of polyphenol samples (μmol Trolox/g DW).

2 mL of polyphenol extracts 1:1 was mixed with 0.1 mM of DPPH. The solution was incubated in the dark for 30 min to measure the absorbance A1 (517 nm). Distilled water was substituted for the samples and DPPH solution respectively denoted as A0, A2. Calibration Curve: Y = 0.048*X + 13.040, R2 = 0.994.

0.3 mL of polyphenol extracts was mixed with ABTS (5.7 mL, 7 mmol/L): K2S2O8 (4.9 mmol/L) were mixed 1:1 v/v) to react for 10 min to measure the absorbance A1 (734 nm). Distilled water was substituted for the sample noted as A0. Calibration Curve: Y = 0.021*X + 4.577, R2 = 0.996.

FeSO4·7H2O (6 mmol/L): C7H6O3 (6 mmol/L) were mixed 1:1 v/v, and then 2 mL of sample and 2 mL of H2O2 (0.03%) were added to react for 10 min to measure the absorbance A1 (510 nm). Distilled water was substituted for the samples and H2O2 solution respectively denoted as A0, A2. Calibration Curve: Y = 0.035*X + 26.000, R2 = 0.997.

4.5 mL of HCl-Tris (0.05 mol/L) was mixed with 1 mL of the sample, and then 0.4 mL of pyrogallol (25 mmol/L) was added to react for 5 min, the reaction was then terminated by adding 1 mL of HCl (8 mol/L) to measure the absorbance A1 (299 nm). 55% ethanol substitution sample was recorded as A0. Calibration Curve: Y = 0.044*X + 9.036, R2 = 0.994.

The oxygen resistance antioxidant capacity (ORAC) assay kit was used for the determination. The fluorescence value was immediately read by a fluorescence enzyme marker every 5 minutes for a total of 60 minutes (excitation wavelength: 485 nm, emission wavelength: 525 nm). The antioxidant activity was calculated from the net area under the fluorescence decay curve (AUC). Blanks were made with buffer instead samples. Calibration Curve: Y = 0.074*X + 2.138, R2 = 0.996.

Ferric reducing ability of plasma (FRAP) working solution was prepared: TPTZ (10 mmol/L): FeCl₃ (20 mmol/L): sodium acetate buffer (300 mmol/L) 1:1:10 v/v/v. 200 μL sample solution, 600 μL distilled water and 6 mL of FRAP working solution for 10 min at 37 °C, and the absorbance was measured at 593 nm. Calibration Curve: Y = 0.001*X + 0.072, R2 = 0.992.

Lipid-lowering activity assay

A slight modification was made based on the methodology of the previous study64. Briefly, 1 mL of 0.01 mol/L HCl solution was added to 1 mL of polyphenol extract sample and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. 5 mL (1 mmol/L) of SC, STC, and SGC were added, respectively. After 1 h of reaction at 37 °C, a 60% sulfuric acid solution was added 1:3 (v/v), and the reaction was incubated for 30 min at 70 °C to measure the absorbance A1 (387 nm). Blank controls were substituted with buffers of the bile salts. The sample solution (100 μL) and tryptic lipase (2 mg/mL)/cholesterol esterase (10 μg/mL) solution (150 μL) were mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min, and then p-NPB (250 μL, 2 mg/mL) was added and reacted at 37 °C for 30 min, and the absorbance was measured at 420 nm.

Colonic fermentation and metabolomics analysis

In vitro fermentation solution was prepared according to the methodology of a previous study65. Fresh human feces were collected in sterile tubes selected from six healthy adults (3 males, 3 females) who were free of intestinal disease and not taking antibiotics for three months. An equal amount of feces was taken in a sterile tube, mixed with sterile saline 1:9 (w/w), filtered, and placed in an anaerobic incubator. 9 ml of sterilized GAM medium containing polyphenol extract (20 mg/ml) and 1 ml of 10% fecal suspension were added to the fermentation tubes. The fermentation was then terminated by liquid nitrogen treatment after incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. The fermentation broth was collected for pH measurement. The remainder was stored at -80 °C for reserve.

Fermentation samples were separated and analyzed by Agilent 1290 Infinity LC and TOF 6600 mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX). The mobile phase consisted of A: ultrapure water (containing 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonia), B: acetonitrile. Chromatographic conditions: column temperature 25 °C; flow rate 0.5 mL/min. 0-0.5 min, 95% B; 0.5-7 min, B varied linearly from 95% to 65%; 7-8 min, B varied linearly from 65% to 40%; 8-9 min, B 40%; 9-9.1 min, B varied linearly from 40% to 95%; 9.1- 12 min, B 95%. The primary mass spectrometry scan accumulation time was 0.20 s/spectrum, and data-dependent acquisition secondary mass spectrometry (0.05 s/spectrum) was used, and peak intensity values were filtered. Compound identification of metabolites by comparing accurate m/z values (<10 ppm) and MS/MS spectra against an internally established database containing available authentic standards. XCMS software was used to perform peak de-noising, retention time calibration, and peak area extraction.

Statistical analysis

All study data were repeated three times and non-target metabolomics data were repeated six times, all expressed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way ANOVA SPSS 27.0 (IBM, USA) was performed using Duncan’s multiple range test. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Visualization images were acquired using GraphPad Prism 8.4 (GraphPad, USA).
