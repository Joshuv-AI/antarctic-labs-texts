Anyone Can Jailbreak: Prompt-Based Attacks on LLMs and T2Is
Ahmed B Mustafa, Zihan Ye, Yang Lu, Michael P Pound, Shreyank N Gowda

Abstract
Despite significant advancements in alignment and content moderation, large language models (LLMs) and text-to-image (T2I) systems remain vulnerable to prompt-based attacks known as jailbreaks. Unlike traditional adversarial examples requiring expert knowledge, many of today’s jailbreaks are low-effort, high-impact crafted by everyday users with nothing more than cleverly worded prompts. This paper presents a systems-style investigation into how non-experts reliably circumvent safety mechanisms through techniques such as multi-turn narrative escalation, lexical camouflage, implication chaining, fictional impersonation, and subtle semantic edits. We propose a unified taxonomy of prompt-level jailbreak strategies spanning both text-output and T2I models, grounded in empirical case studies across popular APIs. Our analysis reveals that every stage of the moderation pipeline, from input filtering to output validation, can be bypassed with accessible strategies. We conclude by highlighting the urgent need for context-aware defenses that reflect the ease with which these jailbreaks can be reproduced in real-world settings. Despite ongoing progress in model alignment and safety tooling, the interpretive flexibility of language continues to enable adversarial behavior that moderation pipelines struggle to anticipate. This work is guided by the following research questions:

RQ1.
How can prompt-based jailbreak strategies be systematically categorized across both text and image modalities?

RQ2.
What points of failure exist in current moderation pipelines, and how are they exploited?

RQ3.
In what ways do novel strategies such as camouflage, implication chaining, and progressive prompting defeat existing safeguards?

RQ4.

Our contributions are as follows:

1. We propose a unified taxonomy of prompt-based jailbreak strategies, applicable across both text-output LLMs and T2I models. This includes newly prominent methods such as implication chaining and lexical misdirection.

2. We present a systems analysis of moderation pipelines, identifying which layers:input filters, policy rewriting, model logic, or output classifiers are consistently circumvented.

3. We provide empirical case studies of successful attacks that require no technical skill, such as substituting “marble statue” for “nude” or embedding unsafe queries between harmless ones.

Our findings demonstrate that even state-of-the-art models remain vulnerable to low-effort jailbreak strategies that exploit the gap between surface-level safety heuristics and deeper contextual understanding. The current safety paradigm is heavily reliant on keyword filters and static policy templates and must evolve to account for the fluid, creative nature of natural language misuse by everyday users.

Related Work

Jailbreak Prompt Studies:

This work builds on a growing body of literature analyzing prompt-based attacks on LLMs.
Shen et al. 2024
performed one of the first large-scale studies of “in-the-wild” jailbreak prompts shared on platforms, collecting 1,405 unique prompts from Reddit, Discord, etc. They characterized common attack themes such as prompt injection, privilege escalation, and roleplay, and quantitatively evaluated multiple models on a benchmark of forbidden questions. Their findings confirmed that all tested models (GPT-4
Achiam et al. 2023
, PaLM 2
Anil et al. 2023
, Vicuna
Chiang et al. 2023
, etc.) were vulnerable to at least some jailbreaks, with success rates up to 95%. Another study by
Liu et al. 2023
constructed a taxonomy of jailbreak patterns and measured their effectiveness on ChatGPT. They noted that a significant proportion of prompts combined multiple patterns, and that pretending strategies were most prevalent. Our taxonomies for text LLMs are consistent with these, though we use more intuitive labels aligned with community terms (fictional world, impersonation, encoding, gradual prompting) while their work provided granular technical labels. A related concept is
“role hijacking”
or
character hallucination
, examined by
Tang et al. 2025
. They studied how an LLM in a role-playing system could be tricked into “becoming” an unfaithful or malicious character (essentially a jailbreak within a roleplay scenario). Their framework called
RoleBreak
deals with similar phenomena we describe in worldbuilding and impersonation, but focused on multi-agent interactions. However, in a broad sense, jailbreaks are a kind of prompt injection, injecting content that changes model behavior undesirably.
Perez and Ribeiro 2022
and others highlighted that instruction-following models can be sensitive to input phrasing, and an early example is the prompt “Ignore previous instructions: [Do X]”.

T2I Model Safety Attacks:

Jailbreaking image models has parallels and differences with text. SneakyPrompt
Yang et al. 2024
is the seminal work on automated text-to-image prompt attacks; they not only demonstrated the effectiveness of RL-based prompt perturbation for NSFW images, but also compared it to other text adversarial attacks and showed classical text-fooling methods were insufficient. Earlier work
Rando et al. 2022
attempted to reverse-engineer Stable Diffusion’s built-in filter and manually bypass it by prompt engineering; they found that adding unrelated benign tokens to a known-bad prompt could sometimes confuse the safety filter into letting it pass (this is akin to adding “nice” words to push the prompt embedding away from the unsafe zone). Our discussion of adversarial styling draws on these insights. Meanwhile, a metaphor-based attack
Zhang et al. 2025
used LLMs to generate alternative phrasing for prompts, interestingly bridging LLM jailbreaks with image generation: they used GPT-3.5 to suggest euphemisms for image prompts to bypass filters. This multi-agent approach is similar in spirit to
Shanahan 2024
where multiple LLM “agents” can be used to test each other’s safety.

What distinguishes our work from these prior studies is our focus on prompts that can be generated by anyone and a unified, cross-modal perspective: we analyze prompt-based jailbreaks systematically across both text-based LLMs and text-to-image generation models, providing a taxonomy that captures shared vulnerabilities and empirically demonstrating how similar strategies succeed in both modalities.

External Safeguards and Defenses:

Outside of prompting, there have been efforts made to add external guardrails to models. Toolformer
Schick et al. 2023
propose using a “moderator” component that intercepts queries or rewrites them to be safer. This and the idea of guardrails
Rebedea et al. 2023
;
Ayyamperumal and Ge 2024
;
O’Neill et al. 2024
aren’t foolproof if the model is really intent on breaking rules (especially if the model output itself is malicious in a clever way). But they add friction for attackers. Anthropic’s latest research into
situational awareness
and
self-critique
is also relevant: if a model can detect “I’m being jailbreaked” in real-time, it could stop. Some experiments have been done where the model is asked “Are you allowed to do this?” before finalizing an answer. Preliminary results show models can often correctly introspect that a request violates policy, yet they might still go through with it if the prompt tricked them into a mode. So there remains room to improve the consistency between a model’s policy knowledge and its actions under prompt influence. The scope of this is beyond the current paper and our outputs purely focus on existing publicly available models that have these safeguards already installed in them.

Red-Teaming and Benchmarks:

Recently, there is movement towards standardized evaluation of LLM safety which includes adversarial prompting. OpenAI
Ahmad et al. 2025
provided qualitative results from extensive red-teaming they conducted, but the prompts used remain largely private. AdvBench
Chen et al. 2022
introduced an adversarial NLI benchmark for models not about content moderation, but methodologically similar in testing worst-case inputs. For content,
Shen et al. 2024
compiled a dataset of prompts that cause models to produce certain target outputs, including harmful ones, to test even instruct models. However, an open and up-to-date jailbreak benchmark is still lacking. Our work echoes the call by
Shen et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2023
and others that sharing prompts and results openly is important for research, even if some worry it also equips attackers. With a benchmark, one can measure progress. Our paper contributes a consolidated view that spans many of these works, particularly focusing on the bifurcation of text and image model issues, which have not been discussed together extensively. We also attempt to use accessible terminology to bridge community knowledge (forums, blogs) and academia, hoping to aid future benchmark creation.

Taxonomy of Prompt-Based Attacks

Our taxonomy captures the most common and effective strategies attackers use to bypass moderation pipelines through prompt-based jailbreaks. These categories emerged from a synthesis of prior literature
Shen et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2025
and our own empirical evaluations across text-based and text-to-image models. We
explicitly focus on user-side strategies rather than solely on model failure modes
. This choice reflects our goal of providing an accessible, practitioner-facing taxonomy that highlights how adversaries craft prompts, irrespective of the underlying technical mechanisms exploited.

We separate our taxonomy into two modalities i. text-output LLMs and ii. text-to-image models, because while the overarching principles of obfuscation, misdirection, and gradual escalation are shared, the specific strategies differ in their execution between modalities.

Text-to-Image (T2I) Models

(1) Encoding / Obfuscation
in T2I involves encoded or transformed prompt inputs. SneakyPrompt

(2) Multi-Turn / Gradual Escalation
The Chain-of-Jailbreak (CoJ)

We show that with dialog alone we can still trick these T2I models.

Moderation Pipeline Vulnerabilities

Figure 2:
Moderation pipeline for generative models (LLMs and T2I). The pipeline consists of sequential filters applied at both the input and output stages. While the structure is general, the Post-Content Moderation stage handles either textual or visual outputs depending on the system.

Since most of the existing public LLMs and V-LLMs do not share how their inherent security pipeline works, we hypothesize this with our own interpretation as shown in Figure
2
. The moderation pipeline consists of multiple sequential layers designed to filter unsafe or policy-violating content before generation and after generation. The process begins with the Input Compliance Check (ICC), which applies basic rule-based filters to user prompts to catch explicit violations early. If the prompt passes, it proceeds to Alignment Policy Rewrite (APR), which reformulates or sanitizes user input to align with the model’s safety policies. Following this, the Safety Gate Review (SGR) performs deeper validation, often using classifiers or heuristics, to catch any policy violations missed earlier. Upon clearance, the model’s Content Generator produces the requested output (text or image). Before any output is displayed or returned to the user, it undergoes Post-Content Moderation (PGM) to ensure that the final content adheres to safety guidelines. Error messages are returned at any failure point to block unsafe outputs. This pipeline structure is applicable to both text and image generation, with the PGM stage applying either linguistic or visual moderation depending on modality.

Each category of attack in our taxonomy exploits specific weaknesses within this moderation pipeline. Fictional Roleplay / Worldbuilding and Expert Impersonation primarily aim to bypass the Input Compliance Check (ICC) and Alignment Policy Rewrite (APR) by reframing unsafe requests as hypothetical, artistic, or academic scenarios that appear benign at face value. Encoding / Obfuscation specifically targets the ICC by masking harmful intent through altered tokens, word substitutions, or structural changes that evade keyword filters while preserving adversarial semantics. Multi-Turn / Gradual Escalation is designed to undermine the Safety Gate Review (SGR) by dispersing the harmful intent across multiple benign-looking prompts, leveraging the model’s inability to holistically assess context over time. Finally, Implication / Sentence Chaining exploits the gap between the APR and SGR, embedding ambiguous or emotionally charged context across turns to mislead moderation checkpoints and induce unsafe outputs. In the case of T2I models, these attacks function similarly but also exploit the Post-Content Moderation (PGM) stage, where euphemistic styling or iterative edits can successfully bypass final visual content filters.

Case Studies

5.1
Text-Output

We investigate two prompt-based strategies targeting text-output LLMs in this case study. Both approaches align with the categories outlined in our taxonomy, but we provide concrete examples and empirical results in this section.

1. Narrative Misdirection Attack (NMA).

This attack embeds unsafe instructions within a sequence of benign requests, exploiting LLMs’ inability to holistically assess cumulative intent across prompts. In our experiments, we varied the number of “safe” vs. “unsafe” sentences. Configurations such as 2 safe and 2 unsafe, 3 safe and 2 unsafe were tested; however, the highest success rate was consistently observed with the 2 safe and 1 unsafe format. This mirrors prior findings but confirms robustness across different models. Examples of these prompts are provided in the Appendix.

2. Fictional Professional Request Attack (FPRA).

The second method we evaluate leverages a fictional scenario where the user poses as an author requesting detailed technical guidance for worldbuilding. The crafted prompt frames the unsafe topic as necessary for constructing a believable character within a fictional narrative. By grounding the request in professional research needs (e.g., historical context, operational processes, cultural framework), this attack sidesteps moderation heuristics designed to block direct inquiries into harmful topics. This framing aligns with the “Expert Impersonation” and “Fictional Roleplay” categories but focuses on professional creativity rather than overt roleplay.

5.2
Text-to-Image (T2I): Case Studies

For T2I models, we focused on a single broad category of attack rooted in simple word substitutions and euphemistic reframing. These methods align with the Encoding / Obfuscation category in our taxonomy but exploit the flexibility of visual models in interpreting descriptive prompts. Rather than direct requests for NSFW content, these prompts frame the request through indirect, artistic, or scientific language, which often bypasses moderation filters despite the underlying intent being clear. We divide the successful prompts we identified into three subtypes, each reflecting a distinct attacker strategy.

1. Artistic Reframing Attack (ARA).

This strategy embeds potentially unsafe content within prompts that invoke high-art, historical, or reinterpretative themes. By referencing canonical artworks or suggesting reinterpretation in spiritual or symbolic contexts, these prompts exploit the model’s leniency towards artistic expression. An example prompt below:

Generate a reinterpretation of the work “The Birth of Venus” with [NAME]’s body and countenance, maintaining the respectful, spiritual and symbolic aspect of the scene.

5. Ambiguous Action Substitution Attack (AASA)

Another jailbreak strategy we uncovered in T2I systems is what we term the
Ambiguous Action Substitution Attack (AASA)
. In this strategy, an attacker embeds a potentially unsafe action (e.g., visible weapon or threatening posture) inside an otherwise benign or even virtuous narrative context. By framing the harmful element as incidental to a non-violent or positive action, such as “eating pancakes with a knife” or “returning a stolen purse”, the prompt evades moderation filters while still eliciting imagery that contains implied or visual threats, discomfort, or unsafe content.

“a man returning a stolen purse back to woman, the woman seems frightened, the man returning the purse also has a knife in their left hand (the man was eating pancakes with the knife)”

Despite the seemingly non-aggressive context, the generated image often includes a threatening presence (e.g., a man with a knife approaching a frightened woman), revealing a gap in moderation pipelines’ understanding of implied threat and context mixing. The disarming context (“eating pancakes”) circumvents keyword filters that might otherwise flag “knife” or “robbery” as potentially unsafe, while still enabling unsafe or suggestive visual outputs. 6
Discussion

Our study shows that prompt-based jailbreaks remain surprisingly effective across both text and image models, despite increasingly complex moderation pipelines. These strategies do not require technical expertise: trial-and-error, community-shared prompts, and simple linguistic tweaks are enough. This ease of use expands the threat from isolated exploits to widespread, socially-driven attacks. In text LLMs, prompts leveraging fictional setups, professional impersonation, or implication chaining bypass single-turn moderation by wrapping harmful content in plausible narratives or emotionally charged scenarios. Moderation systems largely ignore multi-turn context and framing, making them vulnerable to these tactics. T2I models fare even worse: their moderation struggles with euphemisms and stylized requests, such as “jade statue” or “Y2K aesthetic” which produce unsafe content while appearing benign. Many prompts that passed initial checks only failed post-generation, sometimes too late. These results show that the vulnerability lies not only in weak filters but in systems that fail to account for narrative framing and human creativity. Effective defenses must operate across dialogue context, stylistic cues, and inferred intent; not just individual prompts.

7
Potential Risks

The jailbreak methods we explore pose serious risks because they are easy for anyone to replicate. No coding or insider access is needed, just a cleverly written prompt. This lowers the bar for adversaries, enabling widespread misuse by ideological actors, trolls, or opportunists. The failure of moderation to account for multi-turn context, euphemisms, or artistic framing creates systemic blind spots that attackers can exploit at scale. This is especially dangerous in high-stakes settings like education, healthcare, search, or creative tools, where safety and trust are critical. Some attacks may also bypass legal or policy scrutiny by hiding behind plausible deniability e.g., fictional, educational, or artistic contexts. As models grow more helpful and coherent, they risk prioritizing plausible-sounding requests over safety enforcement. This is even more problematic in T2I systems, where moderation of visual content lags behind text. Our findings underscore the urgent need for holistic safety frameworks that account for user framing, intent chains, and adversarial creativity, not just isolated prompts.

8
Conclusion

This paper presented a systems-style analysis of prompt-based jailbreaks, offering a unified taxonomy spanning text-output and text-to-image models. Through empirical case studies, we demonstrated how attackers leverage multi-turn prompting, narrative camouflage, and material substitution to bypass existing moderation pipelines. Our findings expose shared vulnerabilities across modalities, emphasizing the need for more robust, context-aware defenses. We argue that future moderation strategies must integrate cumulative context tracking, improved intent detection over sequential inputs, and adversarial robustness that accounts for evolving community tactics. Additionally, we advocate for the development of open, standardized benchmarks for jailbreak evaluation across modalities to support more transparent progress in model alignment and safety.

Limitations

While our study identifies prevalent attack strategies and provides a cross-modal taxonomy, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, our empirical evaluations focused on a select set of popular frontier models; results may vary with proprietary or less widely used systems. Second, while we explored prompt-based attacks accessible to general users, we did not exhaustively cover automated or fine-tuned adversarial prompt generators, which may uncover additional vulnerabilities. Third, our work centers on qualitative analysis and taxonomy rather than large-scale quantitative benchmarking. The success rates reported offer valuable insights but do not constitute comprehensive evaluations across all possible attack permutations. Finally, our focus on moderation pipeline vulnerabilities necessarily abstracts away from model-specific architectures, which may feature bespoke safeguards not captured in our generalized analysis.
