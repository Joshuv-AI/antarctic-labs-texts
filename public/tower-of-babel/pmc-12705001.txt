The effect of job on lifestyle and physical inactivity among health care practitioners in National Guard Hospital
Authors: Abdulaziz S. Alhoshan, Alhanoof Al Otaibi, Abdullah A. Aldubaib, Mohammed H. Aladhayani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:This study investigates the physical activity levels and workplace stress experienced by health care practitioners at the National Guard Hospital (NGH) in Riyadh. Understanding these factors is crucial for developing targeted interventions to enhance the overall wellbeing of this professional group. The study addresses the dearth of research on the relationship between physical activity, demographic characteristics, and workplace stress among healthcare practitioners.Methodology:A cross-sectional study was conducted, utilizing a self-administered online questionnaire distributed among 372 health care practitioners at NGH. The questionnaire collected demographic data, assessed physical activity using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) short form, and measured workplace stress through The Workplace Stress Scale. Statistical analyses, including descriptive statistics, Chi-square tests, and associations using P values, were employed to explore the relationships between physical activity, demographic factors, and workplace stress.Results:The results revealed a diverse demographic profile, with a mean age of 34.4 years and a predominantly female (69.1%) workforce. Nurses constituted the largest professional group (41.1%), and medical departments housed the majority (72.3%) of participants. While 25.8% engaged in vigorous physical activities, 25.0% in moderate activity, and 76.9% in walking activities, the IPAQ categorization showed that 49.5% had low activity, 18.5% had moderate activity, and 32.0% had high activity. Workplace stress varied, with 7.0% reporting no stress, 21.5% fairly low stress, 34.4% moderate stress, 20.2% severe stress, and 16.9% having a potentially dangerous stress level. Significant associations were found between physical activity levels and departmental affiliation (P = 0.041), while no significant associations were observed between workplace stress and demographic factors.Conclusion:The findings underscore the importance of tailored interventions that consider the diverse needs of health care practitioners in different departments. Workplace wellness programs should target specific professional groups and address both physical activity and stress management to enhance the overall health and job satisfaction of healthcare professionals.

Introduction

Physical activity is any form of body movement that is produced by skeletal muscle contraction that results in energy expenditure.[1] Multiple organs and systems such as brain, heart, skeletal muscle, pancreas, bone, and liver are influenced by physical activity.[2]

WHO recommends at least 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity for adults and considered physical inactivity as one of the leading risk factors for diseases mortality[3] and considered one of the most common causes of obesity,[4] diabetes,[5] hypertension, and heart diseases.[6]

Globally, the prevalence of physical inactivity was 27·5%, and women are more physically inactive (31.7%) than men (23.4%).[7] In Saudi Arabia, a recent study involved 26,000 families from different regions and found 82.6% were physically inactive, and females are insufficiently physically active (91.7%) than males (83.3%).[8] A study found that health care physicians who are physically active are more confident to engage their patients to do regular exercise.[9] In USA, only 24.3% among healthy adults followed aerobic and muscle strengthening recommended guidelines.[10] Studies in Nigeria and Saudi Arabia found the prevalences of low physical activity among health care workers are 51.5% and 34.3%, respectively.[1112] Sedentary lifestyle among healthcare workers may be because of lack of free time and work hours.[13] The aim of our study is to evaluate the physical activity performed by healthcare workers according to the physical activity recommendations of WHO.

Methodology

The study was conducted at the National Guard Hospital (NGH) in Riyadh, focusing on health care practitioners from various specialties after having the ethical approval from IRB at King Abdullah International Medical Research center of IRB/2850/22 on January 1, 2023. The research aimed to gather demographic data and assess physical activity levels using a valid International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) short-term questionnaire distributed through a Google Form.

The online survey consisted of demographic information, including age, gender, specialty, job title, marital status, height, weight, medical history, and smoking status. Additionally, the IPAQ short-term questionnaire included seven questions related to the frequency and duration of vigorous, moderate, and walking activities over the past 7 days.

The survey distribution method involved sending the Google Form link via email to secretaries across all health specialties within NGH. These secretaries played a crucial role in disseminating the questionnaire to health care practitioners. The online questionnaire was developed based on previously utilized and validated surveys in studies with similar objectives.[1415]

In terms of participant inclusion criteria, the study included both male and female health care practitioners working at NGH, within the age range of 15 to 69. However, certain exclusions were established, such as medical interns, health care practitioners rotating in NGH, those not directly involved in health care roles (such as administration, quality, IT), and individuals with limited physical activity due to knee osteoarthritis, rheumatological diseases, or postoperative conditions.

The sample size was determined using OpenEpi software, with a confidence level of 95%, resulting in a calculated sample size of 372 participants. Throughout the study, data collection adhered to ethical standards, ensuring participant confidentiality and anonymity. The methodology was implemented in a systematic and organized manner to obtain reliable and representative information from the health care practitioners at NGH.

Results

Table 1 highlights the demographic factors of the 372 participants. The mean age was 34.4 years, with a standard deviation of 8.67. The gender distribution revealed that 69.1% were female, and 30.9% were male. Nurses constituted the largest professional group (41.1%), followed by physicians (22.3%), administrators (17.2%), dietitians (8.6%), and pharmacists (10.8%). Regarding departmental affiliation, 72.3% worked in medical departments, 9.1% in surgical, and 18.5% in administration. Most participants reported no chronic medical illness (82.8%) where diabetes and hypertension were the most frequently reported, at 18.8% and 21.9%, respectively [Figure 1], were nonsmokers (86.0%), and were married (48.7%). BMI categories indicated that 43.6% had a normal weight, 36.0% were overweight, and 19.1% were obese. Additionally, 44.6% of participants reported having children.

Discussion

The results of the study provide a comprehensive understanding of the demographic characteristics, health status, physical activity levels, and workplace stress among health care practitioners at the NGH in Riyadh. The findings are crucial for identifying potential areas of intervention and support to enhance the overall wellbeing of this professional group.

The study utilized the IPAQ to assess physical activity levels, revealing that a substantial proportion of health care practitioners engaged in various forms of physical activity. The prevalence of vigorous physical activities (25.8%) and moderate activity (25.0%) suggests a positive commitment to maintaining an active lifestyle. However, the high prevalence of walking activities (76.9%) may be indicative of activities integrated into daily routines rather than intentional exercise. The categorization of physical activity levels using IPAQ indicated that less than half of the participants had low activity (49.5%), emphasizing the need for strategies to promote increased physical activity, particularly moderate and vigorous activities. The rate of low activity in the current study is significantly higher than reported in a large cross-sectional study of 10,325 physicians which showed that only 38.1% of the participants had insufficient physical activity.[16] However, these results are similar to what was reported in other reports showing that 39.7% of the participants were found to be physically inactive[17] and similar to many other reports that showed that most of the medical workers are physically inactive.[1318] A possible explanation for low physical activity among primary healthcare workers includes the sedentary nature of their work.[1920]

The association between physical activity levels and departmental affiliation was significant (P = 0.041), indicating potential workplace-specific factors influencing activity levels. To enhance physical activity among health care practitioners, interventions such as workplace fitness programs, dedicated break times for physical activity, and awareness campaigns on the importance of regular exercise may be considered.[21] No significant correlation between most sociodemographic characteristics and physical activity levels was found, contradicting previous studies.[2223]

In our study, the Workplace Stress Scale revealed varying stress levels among health care practitioners. Interestingly, the lack of a significant association between workplace stress and physical activity levels suggests that stress is influenced by factors beyond engagement in exercise. The intricate interplay between stress and physical activity was evident, with stress acting as a potential barrier to regular exercise.[24] The relationship between stress and physical activity aligns with existing literature, where chronic workplace stress has been associated with insufficient physical activity.[252627] The demanding nature of healthcare roles, combined with high-stress levels, may contribute to fatigue and reduced motivation for exercise, as evidenced by the proportion of participants engaging in low physical activity (49.5%).

The assessment of workplace stress using The Workplace Stress Scale revealed varied stress levels among participants. While 7.0% reported no stress, a considerable proportion experienced moderate (34.4%) to severe stress (20.2%), similar to what was reported in the literature.[2829] The distribution of stress levels across different professional groups and departments highlighted the need for targeted interventions to address stressors specific to each category. The lack of significant associations between workplace stress and demographic factors, including gender, job position, departmental affiliation, chronic illness status, smoking status, marital status, and physical activity levels, suggests that stress is a multifaceted issue influenced by a combination of individual and workplace factors. Previous research emphasizes the importance of organizational support, coping strategies, and stress management programs in mitigating workplace stress.[30]

Despite the valuable insights gained from this study, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional nature of the study limits causal inferences, and self-reported data may introduce recall bias. Additionally, the study was conducted in a specific hospital setting, and the findings may not be generalizable to other healthcare institutions. Future research should explore longitudinal designs to better understand the dynamic nature of physical activity and workplace stress among health care practitioners. Qualitative studies may also provide deeper insights into the specific stressors and factors influencing physical activity in this population.

In conclusion, the results of this study contribute valuable information on the demographic characteristics, physical activity levels, and workplace stress experienced by health care practitioners at NGH in Riyadh. The findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to promote physical activity and alleviate workplace stress, with a focus on tailoring strategies to the unique needs of different professional groups and departments within the hospital setting.
