Femoro-popliteal bypass with arterial perfusion cannula coupling of the VA-ECMO system to the prosthetic anastomosis in critical limb ischemia secondary to veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation: a case report
Authors: Rafael E. Castellar Martinez, Maria C. Urina Cabas, Andres C. Castellar Martinez, Axel Adonai Tolstano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Critical limb ischemia (CLI) secondary to femoral veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) is a recognized and morbid complication, with an incidence reaching 25.4% when prophylactic distal perfusion catheter (DPC) placement is omitted at cannulation. We report the case of a 77-year-old male [height 170 cm, weight 62 kg, body surface area (BSA) 1.72 m2] with long QT syndrome (LQTS), dilated cardiomyopathy, and a recent mitral valve replacement who developed refractory cardiogenic shock and electrical storm in the postoperative period, requiring VA-ECMO support via femoro-femoral cannulation. Progressive CLI of the right lower limb was confirmed by duplex Doppler ultrasound on day 8 of extracorporeal support, demonstrating occlusive thrombosis at the popliteal level with near-absent infrapopliteal flow. The limb was classified as Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) Class IIb (immediately threatened). Emergency surgical intervention included Fogarty thrombectomy and femoro-popliteal bypass with a 6 mm ringed Propaten graft. The novel technical element was the direct coupling of the VA-ECMO arterial perfusion cannula to the prosthetic anastomosis via a three-way Luer-lock stopcock, providing dual-source distal perfusion without requiring an independent perfusion catheter. Immediate limb perfusion was restored and confirmed intraoperatively. The patient subsequently died from refractory cardiogenic shock attributed to irreversible cardiac failure, precluding assessment of durable functional limb outcome. This case documents an innovative surgical strategy for refractory VA-ECMO-related CLI that may serve as a technical reference when standard distal perfusion strategies are insufficient.

Introduction

Distal limb ischemia ipsilateral to the femoral arterial cannula is one of the most clinically significant complications of peripheral VA-ECMO. The large-bore arterial cannula occupying the common femoral artery (CFA) competitively reduces antegrade flow to the ipsilateral lower extremity, while the non-pulsatile nature of ECMO perfusion further compromises an already vulnerable peripheral arterial bed. When prophylactic DPC placement is omitted at the time of cannulation, the reported incidence of limb ischemia reaches 25.4%; implementation of prophylactic DPC reduces this to approximately 9.7%, though it does not eliminate the risk entirely (1). Active surveillance from cannulation onward—encompassing serial clinical examination, cutaneous oximetry, and Doppler ultrasound—is essential for early detection.

When limb ischemia is identified, initial management consists of ECMO circuit optimization (flow and RPM adjustment, cannula repositioning) and, if unsuccessful, DPC placement from the superficial femoral artery (SFA) connected to the arterial limb of the circuit to restore antegrade perfusion. Alternative approaches include end-to-side side-graft techniques at the femoral level and, in select patients, axillary artery cannulation as an alternative arterial outflow site (2). However, when ischemia progresses to established occlusive arterial thrombosis at or distal to the popliteal level—particularly in the setting of diffuse atherosclerosis, hypercoagulability, and non-pulsatile ECMO hemodynamics—these strategies may be insufficient, and formal vascular surgical intervention becomes necessary. The reported rate of major vascular surgical intervention among femoral VA-ECMO patients is approximately 9.8%, with a limb loss rate of 3.2% (3, 9).

Long QT syndrome (LQTS) is a cardiac channelopathy characterized by prolongation of the corrected QT interval and predisposition to potentially lethal ventricular arrhythmias, primarily torsades de pointes and ventricular fibrillation (VF), with a prevalence of approximately 1 in 2,500 individuals in its autosomal dominant form (4, 10). When LQTS generates refractory electrical storm (ES) and cardiogenic shock, VA-ECMO is positioned as a rescue strategy; the Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) guidelines identify peripheral femoral access as the default approach in potentially reversible cardiogenic shock (1).

We present a case in which refractory CLI secondary to femoral VA-ECMO was managed with a novel surgical technique: direct coupling of the ECMO arterial perfusion cannula to the prosthetic anastomosis of a femoro-popliteal bypass via a standard three-way Luer-lock stopcock, thereby delivering extracorporeal flow as an additional source of distal limb perfusion without requiring a separate vascular access site.

Case presentation

Patient and clinical background

A 77-year-old male [height 170 cm, weight 62 kg, body mass index (BMI) 21.4 kg/m2, BSA 1.72 m2] with a history of LQTS, dilated cardiomyopathy with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF 64%), severe mitral regurgitation (Carpentier type II), grade I arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and epilepsy under chronic management presented for elective cardiac surgery. Preoperative left heart catheterization excluded significant coronary artery disease. No family history of sudden cardiac death or LQTS was documented in the available clinical record; genetic panel testing for LQTS subtype was not performed. On April 28, 2024, the patient underwent mitral valve replacement (MVR) with a 27 mm Mosaic bioprosthesis and transseptal septoplasty.

Electrical storm and hemodynamic deterioration

In the immediate postoperative period, the patient developed recurrent VF requiring cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) on three separate occasions (lasting 9, 3, and 1 min, respectively), following current advanced life support protocols (5), establishing ES on a known LQTS substrate. The clinical escalation reached its apex when the patient experienced 16 episodes of VF alternating with pulseless monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (VT) within a single afternoon, each requiring defibrillation, constituting the decisive trigger for mechanical circulatory support.

Echocardiography during hospitalization demonstrated: LVEF approximately 40%, concentric left ventricular hypertrophy, apical contractility abnormality, asynchronous septal motion, cardiac output (CO) 2.8 L/min, and cardiac power 0.62 W. The mitral bioprosthesis was assessed as functional with mildly elevated gradients. Despite maximal medical therapy including continuous infusion lidocaine and propranolol, the patient progressed to refractory cardiogenic shock unresponsive to vasopressors and inotropes. Percutaneous tracheostomy was performed on May 6, 2024, to secure the airway definitively as part of the preparatory sequence for mechanical circulatory support initiation.

VA-ECMO initiation

VA-ECMO was initiated on May 7, 2024, in a femoro-femoral venoarterial configuration: venous cannula in the left femoral vein, arterial cannula in the right CFA (estimated cannula size 17 Fr based on the achieved flow profile of 3.27–3.6 L/min at 4,000–4,200 RPM and BSA of 1.72 m2; exact French size not formally documented in the operative record—acknowledged as a limitation). A prophylactic DPC was not placed at cannulation, consistent with institutional practice at that time. Cannula position was verified by portable chest radiograph (Figure 1); no CFA-site duplex ultrasound was performed at cannulation. As a complementary antiarrhythmic rescue measure, bilateral ganglionic sympathectomy was performed on May 9, 2024 (6). No further VF episodes were recorded following this procedure.

Development of critical limb ischemia

Serial clinical examination documented progressive signs of right lower limb hypoperfusion: diminished pedal pulse, evolving bullae on the dorsal foot surface and right calf, and plantar cyanosis of the first and second toes with absent capillary refill. Systemic findings at this time included: moderate anemia [hemoglobin (Hb) 9.1 g/dL], moderate thrombocytopenia (platelets 50,000–66,000/mm3), marked neutrophilia (92%–95%) consistent with systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), lactate 2.2 mmol/L, central venous oxygen saturation (SvO2) 68%, and a PaO2/FiO2 ratio of approximately 134, consistent with moderate acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Concurrent infectious complications—right lower lobe pneumonia caused by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and a complicated urinary tract infection caused by carbapenem-sensitive Pseudomonas aeruginosa—contributed to the systemic prothrombotic and inflammatory milieu. Anticoagulation was maintained with unfractionated heparin (UFH) continuous infusion targeting therapeutic partial thromboplastin time (PTT); exact perioperative activated clotting time values were not available in the retrospective record (Table 1).

Arterial duplex Doppler ultrasound of the right lower limb was performed on May 9, 2024, with significant technical difficulty due to the simultaneous ECMO circuit. Findings were: CFA patent with irregular calcified walls and biphasic signal; SFA with small flow channel in its middle and distal thirds (proximal segment not evaluable due to poor acoustic window); popliteal artery with small flow channel and biphasic signal; infrapopliteal vessels with minimal flow, slow low-resistance flow, and monophasic signal. These findings confirmed critical distal hypoperfusion with severe compromise of the infrapopliteal territory.

Diagnostic assessment

The differential diagnosis for acute limb ischemia in this context included: (1) cannula-mediated competitive obstruction of antegrade femoral flow—the dominant predisposing mechanism present from cannulation; (2) in-situ popliteal thrombosis on an atherosclerotic plaque—the most likely primary occlusive event, subsequently confirmed at surgery; (3) embolic occlusion from the ECMO circuit or cardiac thrombus—not supported by the organized, subacute morphology of the operative specimen; and (4) cannula malposition with retrograde occlusion—excluded by the radiographically confirmed cannula position and the presence of a biphasic CFA signal on Doppler.

The primary diagnostic challenge was the ECMO circuit, which introduced significant artifact into Doppler acquisition and precluded computed tomographic angiography (CTA) given hemodynamic instability. At the time of the surgical decision (May 15, 2024), the limb was classified as SVS Class IIb (immediately threatened): near-absent arterial Doppler signals in the infrapopliteal territory, established cutaneous ischemic findings (bullae, plantar cyanosis, absent capillary refill), and absent venous Doppler signal at the ankle. Full sensorimotor examination was not feasible due to continuous deep sedation.

Surgical intervention

Emergency surgery was performed on May 15, 2024, under general anesthesia and systemic heparinization. Dissection of the right popliteal artery at its suprapatellar segment revealed an organized occlusive thrombus of subacute evolution on an atherosclerotic plaque (Figure 2). Vertical longitudinal arteriotomy was performed followed by thrombectomy using a 4 Fr Fogarty catheter proximally and a 3 Fr catheter distally. After lavage with heparinized saline and verification of satisfactory proximal inflow return—confirming re-establishment of pulsatile flow from the CFA and SFA—a prosthetic end-to-side anastomosis was fashioned with a 6 mm ringed Propaten graft at the suprapatellar popliteal level.

Macroscopic appearance of thrombotic material extracted from the right popliteal artery using Fogarty catheters (4 Fr proximal, 3 Fr distal), displayed against a Stryker metric ruler. Two segments of organized thrombus are shown; the longer measures approximately 18–20 cm and the shorter approximately 10–12 cm, consistent with subacute in-situ thrombosis propagating on an atherosclerotic plaque.

It is essential to distinguish proximal inflow restoration from distal perfusion adequacy: while thrombectomy re-established proximal inflow at the operative site, the severely compromised infrapopliteal runoff territory—documented as minimal monophasic flow in all three crural vessels—required an additional perfusion source to sustain graft patency in the context of non-pulsatile retrograde ECMO hemodynamics.

The distinctive technical element was the direct coupling of the VA-ECMO arterial perfusion cannula to the proximal end of the prosthetic graft via a standard three-way Luer-lock stopcock (Figures 3, 4). The ECMO arterial circuit line (3/8″ PVC tubing) was connected to one port of the stopcock; a short connecting segment ran from the second port into the proximal graft; the third port served for de-airing. Prior to opening ECMO flow, the entire connecting line was flushed with heparinized saline under manual pressure with the third port open to allow complete air evacuation, then closed. The graft was additionally purged at the anastomotic level before initiating extracorporeal flow. The stopcock provides an instantaneous flow-occlusion point as a critical safety feature. Systemic hemodynamic stability—assessed via continuous arterial line mean arterial pressure (MAP) and ECMO circuit flow display—was maintained without acute deterioration following coupling, providing indirect evidence that systemic flow diversion was not clinically significant. The wound was closed with active drainage and incomplete skin synthesis given the active infectious context.

Postoperative course and outcomes

Upon clamp release, foot coloration and temperature improved progressively and objectively in the operating theater. In the immediate postoperative period (days 1–3), palpable pedal pulses, normalized skin color, and capillary refill less than 2 s were documented. No formal control Doppler was available in the postoperative record; perfusion assessment relied on serial clinical examination every 6–8 h in the Cardiovascular ICU, including limb color, temperature, capillary refill, pedal pulse, and calf compartment firmness. Creatine kinase and lactate were followed for reperfusion myonecrosis. Formal compartment pressure manometry was not performed—acknowledged as a limitation given the patient's deep sedation, which renders clinical compartment assessment unreliable.

“Limb salvage” is operationally defined here as restoration of perfusion with avoidance of major amputation (proximal to the transmetatarsal level) during the surviving period; durable functional limb outcome was not assessable given the patient's fatal trajectory. From postoperative day 4 onward, progressive systemic deterioration was observed: prolonged capillary refill (3–4 s), peripheral coldness, and loss of limb sensitivity, consistent with progressive reduction in CO in the setting of refractory cardiogenic shock. The patient died approximately one week after the vascular intervention from irreversible cardiac failure. The patient's family served as surrogate decision-makers throughout the ICU course and participated in shared decision-making at each major clinical juncture, including the surgical intervention, and in goals-of-care discussions as the patient's condition deteriorated (Table 2).

Discussion

This case illustrates the convergence of multiple thrombogenic mechanisms that render femoral VA-ECMO patients at high risk for ipsilateral limb ischemia. The large-bore arterial cannula reduces antegrade femoral flow and generates competitive retrograde perfusion, regardless of total pump output. This must be distinguished from total ECMO flow: although the circuit achieved 3.27–3.6 L/min, this reflects systemic pump output in the retrograde direction and does not translate to adequate ipsilateral limb perfusion. In this patient, the convergence of cannula-mediated flow competition, non-pulsatile circuit hemodynamics, diffuse atherosclerosis, SIRS-driven hypercoagulability, thrombocytopenia, and MRSA bacteremia created the substrate for progressive popliteal thrombosis. The suprapatellar localization and organized subacute morphology of the extracted thrombus (Figure 2) are consistent with insidious hypoperfusion acting on a stenotic atherosclerotic segment over several days.

Current ELSO guidelines and mounting clinical evidence support routine prophylactic DPC placement at femoral cannulation to reduce limb ischemia incidence from 25.4% to 9.7% (1). This case reinforces rather than challenges that recommendation. Prophylactic DPC placement is now considered standard of care at our institution.

Management strategies: positioning the current technique

Established strategies for VA-ECMO limb ischemia span a prevention-to-rescue continuum. Prophylactic DPC at cannulation is the most widely supported approach (1). For early-onset ischemia without established distal thrombosis, ECMO circuit optimization combined with DPC insertion represents standard first-line rescue. End-to-side side-graft techniques at the femoral level and axillary artery cannulation represent additional options in select anatomical scenarios (2).

When occlusive thrombosis is established at or distal to the popliteal level, DPC insertion into the SFA does not address the distal obstruction and cannot reliably restore infrapopliteal perfusion. In this scenario, formal vascular surgical intervention is required. The technique documented here—Fogarty thrombectomy combined with femoro-popliteal prosthetic bypass and direct coupling of the ECMO arterial cannula to the proximal graft anastomosis—served two simultaneous functions: (1) bypassing the occluded popliteal segment with a patent prosthetic conduit; and (2) delivering oxygenated, pump-driven extracorporeal flow directly into the graft and distal limb without requiring an additional access site. The ECMO cannula effectively replaced the DPC function at the graft level.

A proposed decision framework for technique selection: standard prophylactic or rescue DPC should remain first-line for prevention and early ischemia without established distal thrombosis. Cannula-to-bypass coupling should be considered when: (1) occlusive thrombosis at or distal to the popliteal level is confirmed and not amenable to DPC-alone correction; (2) standard DPC is not feasible due to inadequate target vessel caliber; or (3) the patient already requires formal bypass, and the graft provides a natural coupling point for extracorporeal flow. Contraindications include: severely compromised outflow beyond the bypass landing zone; inability to safely de-air the circuit; and hemodynamic instability precluding safe intervention at the ECMO circuit.

Air embolism is the critical safety risk. In the present case this was mitigated by complete de-airing of the connecting line with heparinized saline, using the third stopcock port for air evacuation, and graft purging at the anastomotic level. The three-way stopcock provides instantaneous occlusion as a critical safety feature. End-to-side femoral grafting during ECMO support carries a risk of hyperperfusion compartment syndrome (7), reinforcing the need for active postoperative surveillance and individualization of the vascular strategy. Future applications should incorporate intraoperative graft flow probe measurement and continuous near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) monitoring of limb and cerebral perfusion.

Regarding the underlying condition: ES in LQTS requiring VA-ECMO carries high in-hospital mortality (11, 12), particularly when it develops in the perioperative period of major cardiac surgery with established multi-organ failure. Bilateral ganglionic sympathectomy contributed to adrenergic substrate control—no further VF episodes occurred after the procedure (8)—but refractoriness of the underlying cardiogenic shock ultimately determined the fatal prognosis.

Limitations

This case carries several acknowledged limitations: (1) single-case experience without comparator—hypothesis-generating only; (2) arterial cannula French size not formally documented; (3) no access-site duplex at cannulation, precluding formal exclusion of cannula malposition; (4) exact perioperative activated clotting time and anticoagulation dose data unavailable; (5) no quantitative graft flow or hemodynamic diversion data (flow probe, distal pressures, NIRS) obtained intraoperatively; (6) formal compartment pressure manometry not performed postoperatively, and clinical assessment is unreliable in a deeply sedated patient; (7) no postoperative Doppler study available; and (8) “limb salvage” limited to avoidance of major amputation during the surviving period—durable functional outcome was not assessable. These limitations are inherent to retrospective single-case documentation in the critical care setting.

Conclusion

Distal limb ischemia in femoral VA-ECMO demands active vascular surveillance from cannulation onward, and prophylactic DPC placement at cannulation is strongly supported by current evidence and ELSO guidelines. When critical ischemia is established secondary to occlusive popliteal thrombosis and standard rescue strategies are insufficient, direct coupling of the ECMO arterial cannula to the proximal anastomosis of a femoro-popliteal prosthetic bypass via a three-way Luer-lock stopcock represents a technically feasible rescue option, clinically effective in restoring distal perfusion in the case presented. Circuit safety depends on meticulous de-airing and maintenance of an accessible flow-occlusion point at the coupling site. This singular experience requires validation in larger series before any generalizable recommendations can be established.
