Managing pregnancy in women with pre-existing cardiomyopathies: A case series
Authors: Charo Bruce, Claudia Montanaro, Gurleen Wander, Raghav Aggarwal, Alya Khashaba, Mark R. Johnson, Brian P. Halliday, Michael A. Gatzoulis, Isma Rafiq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Women with pre-existing cardiomyopathy experience high maternal and fetal complication rates when undergoing pregnancy. Different cardiomyopathy phenotypes and genetics can influence the presentation during pregnancy and help inform risk profile and management.This case series demonstrates the differing course of pregnancy in dilated, hypertrophic and arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy. The main complications managed were arrhythmia, rising left ventricular outflow tract obstruction and heart failure. The cases highlight important pre-conception considerations, the use of biomarkers, and pharmacological and device management during pregnancy, delivery and post-partum.It is important to recognise that cardiomyopathies represent a heterogeneous group of conditions with differing phenotypes which need individualised specialist care during pregnancy. Risk stratification and management by an expert multidisciplinary team are recommended to support these women to attain good maternal and fetal outcomes.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of indirect maternal mortality in the developed world and complicates 1–4% of pregnancies [1], [2], [3]. In patients with pre-existing cardiovascular disease, cardiomyopathy is associated with a high risk of maternal morbidity and/or mortality [4]. In cardiomyopathy the myocardium is structurally and functionally abnormal in the absence of a secondary cause. Cardiomyopathies can be broadly grouped into four categories: Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM), and restrictive cardiomyopathy (RCM) [5]. DCM and HCM are the most common, while RCM is rare and not included in this series [6].

In patients with pre-existing cardiomyopathy, the haemodynamic changes during pregnancy lead to increased strain on the abnormal heart, and protective pharmacological therapy may be modified due to risk of teratogenicity. DCM is associated with the highest risk of maternal mortality, 2.3%, versus 1.1% in HCM, and none reported in ACM case series. Maternal morbidity differs by phenotype, with pregnancy overall better tolerated in ACM and HCM. Heart failure is more common in DCM, 35%, versus 16% in HCM, and 5% in ACM; and arrhythmia is more common in ACM, 13%, vs 1% in DCM and 2% in HCM [4], [7]. In DCM, cardiovascular events are predominantly seen in the third trimester or peri-partum, when irreversible deterioration in the left ventricular (LV) function can occur [8]. The ROPAC registry reports an obstetric complication rate of 23%, and fetal complication rate of 31% in women with cardiomyopathy; the most common of each is caesarean section (CS) and premature birth [4].

Individualised pre-pregnancy counselling by a multidisciplinary team (MDT) in a tertiary unit is key, with scoring systems such as the Modified World Health Organization (mWHO) 2.0 classification and Carpreg II score aiding the physician and the patient to understand risk [3]. This crucial consultation allows for optimization of cardiovascular status, plan for cessation of teratogenic drugs, consideration of genetic transmission, and, importantly, to counsel women on pregnancy [9]. This case series presents the course of pregnancy in four patients with pre-existing cardiomyopathy of differing phenotypes and severity, seen at a joint cardiac obstetric medicine clinic.

Case Presentations

Case 1

The first case concerns a 27-year-old, nulliparous woman, body mass index (BMI) 19 kg/m2, diagnosed with DCM at 21 years of age (pathogenic missense variant in SCN5A mutation; c.2440C > T, (p.Arg814Trp)). Pre-pregnancy she was classified as NYHA class 1, mWHO 2.0 Class II-III, with a Carpreg II score of 2; she was taking bisoprolol 2.5 mg once daily (OD), sacubitril/valsartan 49/51 mg twice daily (BD), and dapagliflozin 10 mg OD. She was euvolaemic, B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) was 121 ng/L (normal range 0-20 ng/L), echocardiogram showed left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) 46%, and acceptable cardiopulmonary reserve; workload 10.10 METs, peak VO2 25.5 mL/kg/min, but blunted blood pressure response. She had a high burden of multifocal ventricular ectopic (VE) (14–27%) and supraventricular ectopics (SVE) (8–36%), but no sustained arrhythmia. Following conception, sacubitril/valsartan and dapagliflozin were stopped and aspirin 150 mg OD and clexane 40 mg OD started.

In the first trimester she had a high burden of asymptomatic ectopics: 24% VEs and 8% SVEs. Her genotype and phenotype suggested multifocal ectopic Purkinje-related premature contraction (MEPPC) which is known to respond well to class I anti-arrhythmics, with subsequent reversibility of DCM [10]. MDT recommended inpatient trial of quinidine for control of arrhythmia and potential for LV recovery. Undertaken at 18 weeks, quinidine did not supress ectopy despite uptitration over 7 days, and so was discontinued.

At 31 weeks, Holter reported 38% VE burden and new paroxysmal atrial flutter, managed with bisoprolol (6.25 mg) and therapeutic anticoagulation with low molecular weight heparin. She had increasing atrial arrhythmias and was consequently induced for cardiac reasons at 39 weeks and had an uncomplicated vaginally delivery. At 2 weeks postpartum, she restarted enalapril 2.5 mg OD with a plan to transition back to sacubatril/valsartan, once no longer breastfeeding. She had a levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine contraception device (LNG-IUD) inserted post-partum. At 12 months she was still breastfeeding, asymptomatic, but with reduction in LVEF to 34%; a joint consensus was made with the patient to wean, recommence sacubatril/valsartan and trial mexiletine.

Case 2

A 37-year-old, primiparous woman, with DCM and previous out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, presented with an unplanned pregnancy 4 months after delivery of her first child by CS (at 35 weeks) after which she had a peripartum drop in LVEF from 42 to 20%. Contraception had been recommended following delivery but not commenced. Prior genetic testing did not reveal an actionable variant. She was in NYHA class II, mWHO 2.0 class III, Carpreg II score of 5, on bisoprolol 5 mg BD, spironolactone 50 mg OD, ramipril 2.5 mg BD and dapagliflozin 20 mg OD and CRT-D in situ. She was euvolaemic, had a BNP of 185 ng/L, LVEF 42% and required anti-tachycardia pacing (ATP) within the last 2 years. Termination was discussed to allow more time for cardiac recovery but she wished to proceed. Following conception ramipril, spironolactone and dapagliflozin were stopped and furosemide 20 mg OD added.

At 32 weeks, she developed mild peripheral oedema. An uneventful elective Caesarean section was performed at 35 weeks for previous CS, multiple fibroids and cardiac reasons. In the weeks leading up to delivery, future contraception options were discussed with her, and she was consented for LNG-IUD insertion at the time of CS. Her LVEF was stable during pregnancy, but dropped peripartum to 15–20%. She decided not to breastfeed and ramipril, spironolactone and dapagliflozin were restarted. At two weeks postpartum her LVEF had recovered to 35%, which remained stable at 6 months, but she reported ongoing reduced exercise tolerance and persistent ankle swelling (NYHA class 3).

Case 3

This 31-year-old, nulliparous woman, BMI 20 kg/m2, with ACM (pathogenic truncating variant in Desmoplakin (DSP), c.4003C > T (p.Gin1335Ter)) and previous myocarditis, underwent in vitro fertilization with PGD (preimplantation genetic testing). She was NYHA class I, mWHO 2.0 class II-III, Carpreg score of 2, on bisoprolol 1.25 mg BD and ramipril 1.25 mg BD. Pre-pregnancy she was euvolaemic, had a BNP of <30 ng/L, troponin I 13 ng/L (normal range < 11.6 ng/L), LVEF 50–55% and mild impairment of right ventricular function. Previous exercise test reported good cardiopulmonary reserve (METS 17.30). She had no sustained arrhythmia but a high burden of VE and non-sustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT). Following conception ramipril was stopped.

At 32 weeks of gestation she attended hospital with dizziness and elevated troponin I, peak of 396 ng/L. The troponin reduced to 156 ng/L and rose again at 37+ weeks to 415 ng/L (Fig. 1). Case 4

A 33-year-old, nulliparous woman, with HCM and myasthenia gravis, hypertension, renal impairment, and sleep-related hypoventilation needing BiPAP, booked her pregnancy at 7 weeks. Genetic testing did not reveal an actional variant. Prior to pregnancy she had moderate exercise intolerance; NYHA class II-III, mWHO 2.0 class III-IV, Carpreg II score of 9, on verapamil 120 mg OD, enalapril 15 mg OD, and spironolactone 25 mg OD, having previously declined ICD implantation (HCM-SCD risk stratification 6%). She was euvolaemic, with BNP of 456 ng/L, and short runs of NSVT on Holter. LV was hyperdynamic (LVEF 71%), with asymmetric hypertrophy (25 mm) and cavity obliteration on echocardiography (Supplementary Video 1), mid-cavity resting peak gradient of 35 mmHg reaching a maximum 94 mmHg on stress.

Pre-conception she was counselled that pregnancy in her case would be high risk. Following conception enalapril and spironolactone were stopped.

In the first trimester she experienced worsening dyspnoea, chest tightness, dizziness and elevated blood pressure with runs of NSVT. She was commenced on bisoprolol 1.25 mg OD, which was increased 2.5 mg OD, with addition of amlodipine 5 mg OD. The MDT recommended a subcutaneous implantable cardiac defibrillator (S-ICD) due to increased NSVT, high risk of malignant arrhythmia and pre-pregnancy HCM risk score; this was implanted at 17 weeks under general anaesthesia without complication. She was monitored every 1–2 weeks, and symptoms persisted despite optimisation of medication; verapamil increased to 240 mg OD and bisoprolol to 3.75 mg BD. She had a flare of myasthenia gravis at 32 weeks needing IVIg and steroids. At 34 weeks she was admitted for worsening dyspnoea with rising NT-proBNP and furosemide 20 mg was introduced. During admission an urgent preterm CS was performed for fetal indication. Her baby needed NICU admission post-delivery for monitoring. Postpartum enalapril 5 mg OD was restarted, bisoprolol 5 mg BD, and furosemide as required, verapamil was discontinued. LNG-IUD was inserted prior to discharge.

Discussion

Cardiomyopathy is a heterogeneous complex condition, with the varying course of pregnancy necessitating an expert MDT approach, as reflected in the cases described.

Preconception counselling is paramount to assess, guide and optimise patients prior to pregnancy and consider alternatives of adoption and surrogacy for those with very high or prohibitive risk. As demonstrated, optimisation and discussion around timing of pregnancy is important, particularly in those recently post-partum, contraception discussions can start prior to delivery and should be addressed following delivery, prior to discharge. If genotype positive, inheritance risk, variable gene expression, and preimplantation genetic diagnosis should be discussed.

Maternal complications mainly include arrhythmia, worsening haemodynamics and/or heart failure; reflected in this case series (Table 1). Arrhythmia can often be managed first line with beta-blockers, which have a large evidence base in pregnancy, as do alternative agents flecainide and sotalol [3]. Anti-arrhythmic agent use needs to be tailored to presenting rhythm and underlying condition, with maternal benefit weighed against foetal risk. In patients with recurrent VT or at high risk of sudden cardiac death (SCD), defibrillator implantation in pregnancy can be considered, as in case 4. There are limited data on device implantation during pregnancy, but no reports of significant complications, and pregnancy does not increase the risk of ICD-related complications, shocks, or adverse fetal outcomes [12], [13]. S-ICD implantation is an alternative to a transvenous system and its insertion is associated with less radiation exposure to the fetus. If procedures requiring ionizing radiation are necessary during pregnancy they should be delayed, where possible, till after completion of organogenesis, and radiation dose kept as low as reasonably achievable [14].

HCM-associated left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) can be managed with beta-blockers, which may also ameliorate the risk of SCD. If refractory, there is a case report of successful alcohol septal ablation at 29 weeks of gestation [15]. Most pregnancies in women with HCM are considered low risk, with greatest risk in those with high NYHA functional class and LVOTO pre-pregnancy [16]. ESC guidelines recommend patients with LVOTO gradients ≥50 mmHg should be considered for CS delivery [3].

Fluid overload warrants use of diuretics, which are not associated with any increased risk of pregnancy complications [17]. Cardiac dysfunction should be monitored with serial BNP and echocardiogram; in some cases bed rest, hospitalisation and early delivery are needed. In cases of cardiogenic shock advanced therapies such as mechanical support can be considered, albeit delivery should be the first step [18], [19].

ESC guidelines recommend vaginal delivery in cardiomyopathy, unless there is an obstetric indication for CS; severe heart failure, uncontrolled arrhythmia, severe LVOTO, or use of vitamin K antagonists within the preceding 2 weeks. Induction of labour between 39 and 40 weeks reduces risk of emergency CS and foetal complications, and management of the third stage of labour with oxytocin reduces blood loss [20].

Haemodynamic changes persist for 6 weeks after delivery and specialist care should continue into the post-partum period; post-partum surveillance should assess physical and mental health, discuss breastfeeding, plan re-initiation of pharmacotherapy, and contraception.

The case series demonstrates commonly experienced maternal complications and the use of cardiac biomarkers in pregnancy. Importantly, it highlights that when managing pregnancy in patients with pre-existing conditions, pre-conception counselling and post-partum monitoring have a critical role to play. The course of pregnancy in patients with pre-existing cardiomyopathy can be challenging but, with expert MDT input and individualised care in a tertiary setting, patients can have successful pregnancies with good maternal and fetal outcomes.
