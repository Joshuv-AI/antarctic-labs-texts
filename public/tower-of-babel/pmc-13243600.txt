Fibroblast-derived matrix human skin equivalents reveal distinct roles for papillary and reticular fibroblasts in human skin barrier integrity and aging
Authors: Alesha Louis, Catherine Mergen, Marion H Rietveld, Gaëlle Gendronneau, Nada Andre, Youcef Ben Khalifa, Joke A Bouwstra, Robert Rissmann, Rym Halkoum, Abdoelwaheb El Ghalbzouri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe lipidic skin barrier can be compromised by skin aging, driven by internal and external factors. With age the reticular dermal layer increases, suggesting that changes in the dermal phenotype influences skin barrier integrity with age. Fibroblast-derived matrix human skin equivalents (FDMs) containing either papillary (Pfs) or reticular fibroblasts (Rfs) were generated and analyzed for epidermal differentiation, expression of lipid processing enzymes, stratum corneum (SC) lipid matrix structure, and barrier function. Compared to Rf-FDMs, Pf-FDMs exhibit improved epidermal differentiation, demonstrated by basal cell layer proliferation and organization, early differentiation and late differentiation. Further, the expression of several lipid processing enzymes increased in Pf-FDMs, indicating enhanced barrier structure. Intercellular lipid matrix organization differs between Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs. Rf-FDMs exhibit decreased long periodicity phase (LPP) repeat distances of the lipid lamellae, and altered relative abundances of ceramide subclasses within Cer [N], Cer [A], Cer [EO] and Cer [O] are found. Average ceramide chain length in Cer [N] and Cer [A] is decreased in Rf-FDMs, and higher percentages of monounsaturation in Cer [NS] are observed. In line, Rf-FDMs have increased transepidermal water loss (TEWL). Concluding, Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs resemble characteristics of young and aged skin, and demonstrate distinct roles for Pfs and Rfs in skin barrier integrity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48648-3.Keywords: Skin aging, Human skin equivalents, Stratum corneum, Lipid packing, CeramidesSubject terms: Biochemistry, Cell biology

Introduction

The stratum corneum (SC) is the outermost stratified layer of the epidermis and plays a critical role as the primary barrier of the skin. It is composed of multiple corneocyte layers connected by a lipid matrix, which includes ceramides (CERs), free fatty acids (FFA) and cholesterol. These lipids are organized both laterally and lamellarly, each contributing to skin barrier integrity. The lateral organization varies between liquid, hexagonal or orthorhombic phases, indicating the density of the lipid packing. An increased lipid fraction forming a hexagonal packing compared to an orthorhombic packing is linked to higher permeability and thus an impaired skin barrier function1–3. In native human skin (NHS), an orthorhombic lipid packing dominates in the SC. This organization is promoted by the presence of long-chain FFAs, although e.g. facial SC mostly exhibits the hexagonal lipid packing, which might be due to the increased presence of sebum that is found throughout all the SC layers4. In native human SC (NHS) the lamellar organization includes a short periodicity (SPP) and a long periodicity phases (LPP), with repeat distances of 6 nm and 13 nm, respectively. The presence of the LPP is critical for maintaining skin barrier function1–3.

Skin barrier function can be compromised by various factors, including inflammatory diseases, environmental stressors, and skin aging, all of which impact lipid processing enzymes and their pathways. Skin aging is a complex process influenced by both intrinsic and extrinsic factors5. Intrinsic skin aging results in biochemical and structural changes such as increased skin pH, thinning of epidermal layers, flattening of the dermo-epidermal junction, and decreased collagen production in the dermis. In contrast, extrinsic skin aging, often driven by environmental factors, leads to clinical signs such as wrinkles, loss of elasticity, and impaired barrier function due to alterations in lipid composition6. Recent research highlights the importance of the dermal layer in skin aging, suggesting that changes in dermal composition, particularly in fibroblast phenotypes, may play a significant role in both intrinsic and extrinsic aging7.

The human dermis is broadly divided in two layers: the upper papillary dermis, which has a high cell density and interacts intensively with the epidermis, and the lower reticular dermis, which contains a dense network of dendritic fibers and is primarily involved in structural support. Fibroblasts from these layers, known as papillary fibroblasts (Pfs) and reticular fibroblasts (Rfs), exhibit phenotypical and functional differences, including distinct morphology, proliferation capacity, and gene expression profiles7–9. Notably, Pfs and Rfs express distinct biomarkers, with Pfs showing markers like PDPN and CLEC3B, and Rfs expressing TGM2, MGP and COL11A8,9. The functional differences between Pfs and Rfs extend to their roles in skin aging. Pfs can differentiate into Rfs upon prolonged culture, possibly explaining the increased reticular dermal layer in aged skin, which may have structural and physiological consequences for both the dermis and epidermis7,10. These changes are particularly relevant in the context of aging, where an altered ratio between the papillary and reticular dermis may influence skin barrier function and integrity.

In recent years, the development and use of human skin equivalents (HSE) in skin biology research, particularly regarding skin barrier formation11, and skin aging12 have intensified. Fibroblast-derived matrix human skin equivalents (FDMs) have been shown to mimic many features of NHS, including dermal and epidermal morphology as well as barrier properties13–15. FDM SC contains CERs from all subclasses, FFA and cholesterol, and their lipid matrix predominantly exhibits a hexagonal packing, alongside with the LPP lamellar organization15. Compared to NHS, FDM SC consists of increased amount of mono-unsaturated FFA, shorter total chain length of CERs and FFA with reduced carbon chain lengths, and altered CER subclass ratios15. Despite the differences, FDMs allow us the study of the lipid matrix composition and the lipidic barrier formation. Therefore, FDMs are a promising model for studying skin barrier development and lipid metabolism, particularly in skin aging.

Our research aim is to investigate the distinct roles for Pfs and Rfs in skin barrier structure and function, focusing on SC lipid composition and organization, and epidermal differentiation. To achieve this, we employed FDMs generated with either Pfs or Rfs, allowing us to study how these fibroblast subtypes influence skin barrier functionality and aging processes.

Results

Epidermal and dermal morphogenesis in Pfs-FDMs and Rf-FDMs

During skin aging the reticular dermal layer increases compared to the papillary dermal layer, thereby suggesting a distinct role for Pfs and Rfs in skin aging and the development of skin aging characteristics, including dermal and epidermal morphogenesis, cell proliferation, barrier structure and function. To study distinct effects of Pfs and Rfs on dermal and epidermal morphogenesis, and skin barrier formation we established Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs.

Morphological analyses demonstrate an improved epidermal structure in Pf-FDMs compared to Rf-FDMs (Fig. 1a). The epidermal layer in Pf-FDMs is well-differentiated, as it contains clear distinct and viable epidermal cell layers compared to Rf-FDMs. Normal human epidermal keratinocytes (NHEKs) in Rf-FDMs are more flattened and epidermal thickness shows a reduction in Rf-FDMs that follows a similar trend as younger (< 25y) and older (> 55y) NHS, for which a significant decrease in epidermal thickness was found **(p = 0.0054) (Fig. 1b). Improved epidermal morphogenesis in Pf-FDMs was further demonstrated by basal cell layer proliferation (Ki67) and organization (Keratin15, K15), early differentiation (Keratin10, K10) and late differentiation (Involucrin/INV, Loricrin/LOR and Filaggrin/FLG). Basal cell layer proliferation is increased in Pf-FDMs indicated by significant elevated levels of Ki67 + cells *(p = 0.0104) (Fig. 1c). Compared to Rf-FDMs, Pf-FDMs have a properly formed basal single cell layer indicated by K15 positive cells and no delay in the early epidermal differentiation program, as shown by suprabasal expression of K10. Rf-FDMs have a disorganized basal cell layer demonstrated by K15 staining in multiple layers, and K10 expression starting several layers above the basal layer. Moreover, we observe that keratinocytes in Pf-FDMs have expression both K15 and K10, or K10 and INV (in the same cell), which underlines the improved differentiated state of Pf-FDMs compared to Rf-FDMs (Fig. 1a). Moreover, Pf-FDMs exhibit a single layer of LOR+ cells in the granular layer compared to Rf-FDMs, where LOR expression is patchy (Fig. 1a). Compared to Pf-FDMs, mRNA expression levels are significantly decreased for late epidermal differentiation markers LOR, INV and FLG in Rf-FDMs ***(p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1d), which is in line with their protein expression (Fig. 1a). Dermal morphology is enhanced in Pf-FDMs as Pfs are asterisks-shaped and show a more uniform orientation, compared to a horizontal shape and orientation of Rfs in Rf-FDMs (Fig. 1a). Differences in dermal structure and morphology were emphasized by the presence of Rf-marker alpha-smooth muscle actin (αSMA) in Rf-FDMs, which was absent in Pf-FDMs (Fig. 1a). Comparing Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs to younger (< 25y) and older (> 55y) NHS, respectively, show similarities in epidermal and dermal morphology and expression of proliferation-, early and late differentiation-markers.

SC lipid organization and ceramide composition in Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs

Next we analyzed the SC barrier lipid matrix formation and organization in Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs starting with the lamellar organization of the lipids. By using the unique X-ray diffraction pattern of the lipid lamellae we could characterize the lamellar phases. We identified the first 3 orders of diffraction of the long periodicity phase (LPP) allowing the calculation of the repeat distance. In NHS, the short periodicity phase (SPP) has a repeat distance of around 6 nm, while the long periodicity phase (LPP) has a repeat distance of around 13 nm17. The LPP repeat distance in Rf-FDMs is significantly (**p = 0.0072) lower (12.18 ± 0.06 based on the peak positions at 0.52, 1.04 and 1.52 nm− 1) compared to the LPP in Pf-FDMs (12.56 ± 0.07 based on the peak positions at 0.52, 1.02 and 1.49 nm− 1) (Fig. 3a).

To obtain a more in-depth understanding of lipid structure in the SC we analyzed ceramide composition of the SC of NHS (31-47y), Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs. Employing liquid chromatography followed by mass cytometry (LC/MS) we identified in both Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs the 4 main ceramide classes (Cer [N], Cer [A], Cer [EO] and Cer [O]) and the 16 most-abundant ceramide subclasses as described in NHS17, based on polarity and mass.

A wide range of carbon chain lengths were found, with total carbon chain lengths ranging from C32 to C56 in Cer [N] and Cer [A], and carbon chain lengths ranging from C44 to C56 in Cer [O] and C62 to C78 Cer [EO]. Although average carbon chain lengths in the Cer [EO] class did not significantly differ between NHS (Fig. 3b), Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs, the average carbon chain lengths in the Cer [N] and Cer [A] class combined was significantly decreased in Pf-FDMs compared to NHS **(p = 0.0041), in Rf-FDMs compared to NHS ****(p < 0.0001), and in Rf-FDMs compared to Pf-FDMs **(p = 0.0013) (Fig. 3b). Focusing on the average ceramide subclass chain lengths, we observed significantly decreased Cer [NS] average chain length in Rf-FDMs compared to NHS ****(p < 0.0001) and Pf-FDMs ****(p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Average chain length in Cer [NP] is significantly decreased in Rf-FDMs compared to NHS **(p = 0.0047) and in Pf-FDMs compared to NHS *(p = 0.028) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Generally, Cer [NS] contains a higher proportion of short chain length ceramide C34 compared to Cer [NP]18. In line, we find significantly increased percentages of Cer [NS 34] in Rf-FDMs compared to NHS ***(p = 0.001) and Pf-FDMs **(p = 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1). In contrast, percentages of Cer [NP 34] remain very low (Supplementary Fig. 1). Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs have distinct barrier functionality based on transepidermal water loss

To further elucidate the role of Pfs and Rfs in skin barrier integrity, we studied water barrier function based on transepidermal water loss (TEWL). Pf-FDMs have significantly increased *(p = 0.0194) TEWL values (11.69 ± 3.772 g/(m2/h)) compared to FDMs with a mixture of Pfs and Rfs (3.088 ± 1.131 g/(m2/h)), although values are still comparable with NHS (31-47y) (13.67 ± 9.674 g/(m2/h)).

Consistent with the structural differences in skin barrier organization and integrity between Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs we found significantly decreased ***(p < 0.0003) TEWL values in Pf-FDMs compared to Rf-FDMs (41.50 ± 2.559 g/(m2/h)), indicating improved barrier functionality in Pf-FDMs compared to Rf-FDMs (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this study, we focused on the roles of Pfs and Rfs in the development of skin aging characteristics, with an emphasis on their impact on epidermal development, skin barrier structure and function. To investigate these effects, we generated FDMs using either Pfs or Rfs.

We observed an improved epidermal morphology in Pf-FDMs compared to Rf-FDMs, depicted by well-developed epidermal cell layers and increased protein expression of epidermal proliferation and differentiation markers. Pf-FDMs exhibited an enhanced dermal morphology as well compared to Rf-FDMs, as a more homogenous fibroblast orientation was found and expression of Rf marker αSMA was only shown in Rf-FDMs. Rf-FDM show several skin aging characteristics including impaired late epidermal differentiation as depicted by both LOR and INV present in patches throughout the stratum granulosum, thinning of the epidermal layer and impaired basal cell layer proliferation19,20. Further, at both mRNA and protein level, Pf-FDMs express increased levels of late-differentiation markers, barrier-related markers and lipid processing enzymes, indicating enhanced epidermal stratification and improved barrier development and integrity. Rf-FDMs show an overall decline in lipid processing enzymes, indicating dysfunctional barrier integrity. AQP3 expression declines in non-sun exposed skin with age, and therefore is an important marker in intrinsic aging21. Also, reduced PPAR-γ activity correlates with chronic inflammation as found in aged skin in vivo (inflammaging)22. Decreased expression of both markers is found in Rf-FDMs, suggesting an intrinsic aged phenotype in these FDMs. Interestingly, mRNA expression of CERS2 and CERS5 was increased in Rf-FDMs compared to Pf-FDMs. CERS2 synthesizes very long chain fatty acid ceramides (C22, C24 and C26dihydroCer) and CERS5 is involved in the synthesis of short chain fatty acid ceramides C14 and C16dihydroCer23. Moreover, Rf-FDMs exhibited decreased ELOVL1 protein expression, and although enzymatic activity was not assessed, we found in line significant decreased ceramide chain lengths in the Cer [N] and Cer [A] classes in Rf-FDMs compared to Pf-FDMs. Increased CERS5 mRNA expression in Rf-FDMs seems to be relevant in skin aging as increased synthesis of short chain fatty acid ceramides would impair the barrier structure and function24. Further, studies suggest that senescence is induced via the generation of long chain and very long chain fatty acids via CERS2 specifically25, indicating increased senescence in Rf-FDMs, which is a hallmark for skin aging26. Moreover, in line with CERS2 expression, we found decreased SGMS1 expression in Rf-FDMs, which is involved in delaying senescence23, and decreased SPRR1 and SREBP1c expression, which contribute to a senescent phenotype in aging27,28.

Focusing on the lipid barrier composition and organization employing LC/MS and small angle X-ray diffraction, we found significant differences between Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs. Both Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs displayed a SC lamellar organization with a repeat distance around 12 nm, although Rf-FDMs have a slightly lower repeat distance. Furthermore, a weak peak between the first and second order of diffraction was found in Rf-FDMs. This implies an extra lipid phase related to the lamellar phase or lateral packing, although based on our data we could not classify the peak. Interestingly, the formation of multiple lamellar phases might result from increased presence of short chain fatty acids (SCFA) (Cer [NS] 16), and suggest a negative impact on membrane structure and permeability29, consistent with the increased TEWL values in Rf-FDMs compared to Pf-FDMs. Also, disturbed lamellar organization may be linked to an higher level of Cer [AS] and Cer [NS] relative abundances, decreased levels of Cer [NP] relative abundances, and decreased Cer [EO] relative abundances17,30, as we find in Rf-FDMs.

Although changes in Cer [NS] : Cer [NP] ratio have been described as a potential marker for impaired skin barrier function in for example atopic dermatitis, studies employing lipid models that mimic the lipid organization of the SC demonstrate that solely the Cer [NS] : Cer [NP] ratio similar to that of diseased SC does not alter lipid organization of the LPP. Additionally, Cer [NS] has a higher proportion of short chain length ceramides C34 compared to Cer [NP]18, similar to our findings in Rf-FDMs. This could be a confounding factor in the correlation between the Cer [NS] : Cer [NP] ratio and the decreased barrier function. When the Cer [NS] : Cer [NP] ratio increases, the amount of Cer (C34) increases, and contributes to decreased barrier function as well17. This suggests that other factors such as ceramide chain length plays a more prominent role in the formation of the LPP31. In line, Rf-FDMs have decreased Cer [N] and Cer [A] average chain length, even when Cer [NS 34] is excluded.

Changes in ceramide composition that negatively impact barrier functionality may be attributed to disturbed GBA and aSMASE levels or activity. Both are lipid processing enzymes and their roles in contributing to barrier dysfunctionality in atopic dermatitis are widely studied. The increase in Cer [NS] and Cer [AS] as found in Rf-FDMs may be due to altered aSMASE expression17,32. Although not analyzed in this study, the change in Cer [NS] and Cer [NP] relative abundance as found in Rf-FDMs compared to Pf-FDMs may be attributed to disturbed enzyme expression or activity of dihydroceramide desaturase 1 (DEGS1) and 2 (DEGS2), as DESG1 converts Cer [NdS] only into Cer [NS], whereas DEGS2 converts Cer [NdS] into both Cer [NS] and Cer [NP]33,34.

Cers3 and ELOVL1 are both involved in the synthesis of Cer [EO], a key element for lipid organization regarding the lamellar phase, and therefore crucial for maintaining proper barrier integrity17,35,36. Rf-FDMs have decreased expression of both Cers3 and ELOVL1, which might explain for the lower relative abundance of Cer [EOS]. Decreased presence of Cer [EOS] ultimately impairs SC lipid barrier organization as it contributes highly to the formation of the LPP units37, and may be associated with skin aging38–40. Moreover, the expression of ELOVL4 is decreased. In Rf-FDMs compared to Pf-FDMs. ELOVL4 is involved in the elongation of very long chain fatty acids within the Cer [EO] subclass and thus contributes to increased barrier permeability as well41.

Following the decrease of Cer [EOS] in Rf-FDMs, we find an increase in Cer [AS], Cer [NS] and specifically increased percentages of monounsaturation in the Cer [NS], as described in other barrier-related conditions including atopic dermatitis and Netherton syndrome, which can be translated to skin aging17,35,37. Associated with this specific change of ceramide composition is the altered activity of serine proteases including KLK-5 and KLK-7 due to defects in Spink5, which are responsible for proper desquamation of the skin42. Their expression is strongly reduced in Rf-FDMs, explaining the corresponding change in ceramide composition and disrupted barrier integrity.

Both Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs show presence of crystalline cholesterol indicated by the peak related to a spacing of 3.36 nm− 1. Cholesterol is a main contributor to the formation and stabilization of the LPP units as it allows the ceramides and FFA to mix well, thereby improving overall barrier structure and function37. Moreover, during SC maturation the cholesterol sulfate : cholesterol ratio decreases, resulting in a well-established barrier structure, and function43. With age, the epidermal lipid synthesis and in particular cholesterol synthesis is impaired, which may alter barrier structure, organization and function in aged skin44. Examining cholesterol related compounds, such as cholesterol sulfate and cholesterol esters in Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs would be valuable, as an increased ratio of cholesterol sulfate : cholesterol is linked to increased barrier permeability43.

Skin aging is often associated with dryness of the skin45. Although HSE do not fully resemble NHS when focusing on skin hydration as HSE hydration highly depends on environmental relative humidity46, we find increased TEWL values in Rf-FDMs compared to Pf-FDMs, indicating increased water loss and therefore decreased skin hydration. Interestingly, TEWL values are decreased to an even lower extent in FDMs generated with the mixture of Pfs and Rfs, indicating improved skin hydration compared to Pf-FDMs, and suggesting a specific interplay between the papillary and reticular dermis to maintain optimal skin hydration. The (changing) composition of the dermal layer in age, with a specific focus on the fibroblast subtypes and their role in maintaining the cellular environment by generating the ECM and communicating with neighboring cells, has been widely studied47,48. Fibroblasts are dynamic in their structure and role, and employ many functions that are important in maintaining proper general skin health. Both intrinsic and extrinsic skin aging have detrimental effects on dermal fibroblasts, which in turn affect dermal and epidermal skin homeostasis, notably skin hydration49–51. Pfs and Rfs are two subtypes that both have distinct functions in maintaining the dermal layer of the skin8. With age, the reticular dermal layer increases compared to the papillary dermal layer which may be explained by the possibility of Pfs differentiating into Rfs upon prolonged culturing10,14. However, recent research shows the early effect of skin aging that starts already in the papillary layer, resulting in mechanical changes of the skin52. ECM produced by either Pfs or Rfs has distinct properties in collagen type, proliferation and remodeling capacity, and secretion factors or components that support cellular functioning53,54. Whereas the ECM produced by Pfs mainly supports epidermal development and keratinocyte differentiation, the ECM produced by Rfs provides mostly structure and support to the skin14,53,55. Although in vitro studies show that Pfs and Rfs are both able to generate an ECM sufficient for epidermal support14, the combination of the Pf-ECM and Rf-ECM is most optimal for skin homeostasis54. Proteoglycans Decorin and Versican, residing either in the papillary or reticular dermis, respectively, are able to bind glycosaminoglycans Hyaluronan and Dermatan sulfate, thereby providing optimal water retention in the skin54,56,57. This highlights the necessity of both the Pf-ECM and Rf-ECM for efficient skin hydration and homeostasis, and may explain why the TEWL value in our FDMs with a mixture of Pfs and Rfs resembles the TEWL value of NHS even more compared to the TEWL value of Pf-FDMs.

In conclusion, Pfs and Rfs exhibit distinct roles in epidermal differentiation and lipid skin barrier development, in relation to (intrinsic) skin aging. Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs seem to resemble “young” and “aged” skin, respectively, based on several skin aging characteristics including distinct thickness, proliferation and morphology of the epidermal layer, expression of late epidermal differentiation and skin barrier-related markers, SC lipid matrix organization, ceramide composition and barrier function. Their distinct ECM generation may play a role herein, and therefore targeting Rfs as part of an anti-aging strategy may be beneficial for alleviating skin aging characteristics, including the improvement of epidermal development and skin barrier integrity, which would improve overall skin health as well58.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Normal human epidermal keratinocytes (NHEKs) and both papillary (Pfs) and reticular fibroblasts (Rfs) were isolated from surplus breast or abdomen tissue obtained from anonymous female donors between the ages of 31 and 47 years that had undergone cosmetic surgery (Caucasian skin type I-II according to the Fitzpatrick scale).

Isolation process of the cell types was performed as described previously8,13. Gene expression levels of relevant biomarkers were used to confirm the phenotype of the Pfs and Rfs after isolation. Biomarkers for Pfs include CCRL1, NTN and PDPN and biomarkers for Rfs include CDH2, CNN1 and MGP. NHEKs were cultured in K5 medium, Pfs and Rfs were cultured in F5 medium, the culture medium formulas are described previously9.

Generation of human skin equivalents

For the generation of FDMs, normal human fibroblasts, Pfs, and Rfs with a maximum of passage 5 were used. NHEKs were used in passage 0. Pfs and Rfs were donor-matched. The Pfs and Rfs were cultured for the same amount of time before seeding of the NHEKs. NHEKs were not donor-matched with the fibroblasts in the same FDM. Pfs, Rfs and NHEKs were from donors between the ages of 31 and 47 years old. We consider this as middle aged and therefore should not influence age-related differences induced by Pfs or Rfs.

FDMs were generated as follows (See Fig. 5): normal human fibroblasts, or donor-matched Pfs or Rfs were treated with trypsin (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) and seeded onto an insert polyester permeable support 0.4 μm filter in a 6-well plate (Greiner Bio-One, Kremsmünster, Austria) at a density of 2 × 10^5 cells per filter using F5 medium, consisting of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) with 5% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, VWR Avantor, Radnor, PA, USA). After 2 days culture medium is refreshed, and the developed FDMs were then cultured at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for approximately 14–21 days (donor-dependent, based on the amount of contraction of the dermal matrix) until NHEKs were seeded. NHEKs were seeded onto the fibroblasts at a density of 2.5 × 10^5 cells per filter in K5, consisting of DMEM (Gibco) with 5% FBS (VWR Avantor). After 2 days, FDMs were refreshed using K1 + medium consisting of DMEM (Gibco) with 1% FBS (VWR Avantor), and put air-exposed (A/E) and cultured for another 2 days. Then, FDMs were refreshed using K0 + consisting of DMEM (Gibco) without FBS (VWR Avantor) twice a week. FDMs were harvested after approximately 13–15 days of A/E (donor-dependent based on the development of the epidermis, including the amount of layers formed in the SC). Gene expression levels of relevant biomarkers were used to confirm the phenotype of the Pfs and Rfs after culture. Biomarkers for Pfs include CLEC3B, CCRL1, and NTN. Biomarkers for Rfs include Coll11A1 and POSTN.

Graphical overview of generation of human skin equivalent (FDM). Pfs, Rfs, normal human fibroblasts, and NHEKs were isolated from surplus skin tissue and seeded onto and insert filter for 3 weeks. During these three weeks, fibroblast types generate the ECM. At week four, NHEKs were seeded on top of the dermal layer, transferred to air-exposure within the same week, and left air-exposed for approximately two weeks. FDMs containing a fully developed dermal and epidermal layer were in week six to seven harvested for further analysis. Figure created in BioRender.

Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence

FDMs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (LUMC pharmacy, Leiden, the Netherlands) for 18 h, dehydrated and embedded in paraffin (VWR). Morphological analyses were done on 4 μm formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections from three donors after hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining.

For immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses, 4 μm thick formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections from three donors were deparaffinized and rehydrated and antigen retrieval was performed in either Sodium Citrate buffer (pH 6.0) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MA, USA) or Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) (Biosolve Chimie, France, and VWR) using the autoclave at 110 °C for 5 min. Sections were circumscribed with a wax pen (DAKO, Glostrup, Denmark) and blocked using 1% Bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS (LUMC pharmacy) with 2% Normal Human Serum (NHS, LUMC). Primary antibodies as described in Table 1, were incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humid chamber. Thereafter, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies (Table 2) for 60 min at room temperature in a humid chamber in the dark following incubation with the Streptavidin-biotin peroxidase system (GE healthcare, Chicago, Il) for 45 min at room temperature in a humid chamber. Staining was visualized with 3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole (AEC, Sigma-Aldrich). Sections were mounted with Kaisers glycerol gelatin (Merck, Rahway, NJ, USA). All sections were counterstained with HE staining (Sigma-Aldrich). Between individual steps sections were washed with PBS (Merck and Gerbu, Heidelberg). Sections were imaged using the Zeiss Axioscope 5 microscope (20x dry objective). For immunofluorescence (IF) analyses similar protocol applies, only sections were counterstained with DAPI 1:5000 and mounted with Vectashield (both Sigma-Aldrich). Sections were imaged using the DM6b2 fluorescence microscope (20x dry objective) and the Zeiss Airyscan confocal microscope (20x dry objective).

Epidermal thickness quantification and proliferation index

Epidermal thickness was quantified by measuring epidermal area in squared micrometers from the HE staining of three Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs using ImageJ software (1.54f 64-bit, Wayne Rasband and contributors, National Institutes of Health, USA). We did not include the SC in these measurements. The proliferation index was quantified by counting Ki67 positive cells per 100 basal layer cells in the epidermis of five Pf-FDMs and four Rf-FDMs. The data is represented as the mean ± s.d.

RNA isolation and cDNA preparation

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol from three Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs. Briefly, Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs were homogenized and RLT-buffer supplemented with 1.5% β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to each FDM. Then, an additional homogenization step was performed and 70% ethanol (Merck) was added to each FDM. Next, total volume was loaded on the RNeasy spin column and centrifuged at maximum speed for 15 s. Columns were washed with wash buffer. To remove genomic DNA, a DNase treatment using DNase in RDD buffer (1:7, Qiagen) was performed. RNA was dissolved in Milli-Q and both the RNA concentration were measured by Nanodrop (NanoDrop Technologies). cDNA conversion was done using the iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Briefly, 500ng RNA was mixed with iScript reverse transcriptase and iScript reaction mix in a 1:4 ratio and incubated in the thermal cycler (Bio-Rad).

qRT-PCR

Real-time qPCR was performed using the Bio-Rad CFX384 (Bio-Rad), consisting of cDNA at a concentration of 50ng/µl and mastermix including 1x SYBRTM® Green Supermix (Biolegend) and 1µM primermix in a 96-wells plate. Primer sequences including amplification temperature are specified in Table 3. mRNA expression levels were calculated according to the 2-ΔΔCt method. Expression values of genes of interest were normalized by three reference genes, SDHA, ZNF410, and TBP. Reference genes were tested for their consistency in expression in FDMs. Normalization was done with the CFX Manager™ software (BioRad).

SC isolation

SC of three Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs was isolated by incubation of the FDMs overnight at 4 °C and 1 h at 37 °C in a 0.1% trypsin (Bovine pancreas, Sigma-Aldrich) / PBS (LUMC pharmacy) solution. Next, SC was separated from the epidermis and washed using 0.1% trypsin-inhibitor (Glycine max, Sigma-Aldrich) / PBS (LUMC pharmacy) solution and Millipore water. SC was air-dried and stored in the gaseous phase of nitrogen.

Small angle X-ray diffraction analysis

Performed as described before16. All measurements were performed at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF, Grenoble) at station BM26B on four Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs. SAXD patterns were detected with a Frelon 2000 CCD detector at room temperature for 10 min using a microfocus of 25 μm X 25 μm. The scattering intensity I (in arbitrary units) was measured as a function of the scattering vector q (in reciprocal nm). The latter is defined as q = (4πsinθ)/λ, in which θ is the scattering angle and λ the wavelength. From the positions of a series of equidistant peaks (qn), the periodicity, or d-spacing of a lamellar phase was calculated using the equation d = 2nπ/qn, with n being the order of the diffraction peak. One-dimensional intensity profiles were obtained by transformation of the 2D SAXD detector pattern from Cartesian (x, y) to polar (ρ,θ) coordinates and subsequently integrating over θ. The X-ray wavelength and the sample-to-detector distance were 0.1033 nm and 2.1 m, respectively. Diffraction data were collected on a PILATUS 1 M detector with 1043 × 981 pixels of 172 μm spatial resolution. The spatial calibration of this detector was performed using silver behenate (d = 5.838 nm for AgBeh). The SCS was mounted parallel to the primary beam in a sample holder with mica windows. The diffraction data were collected for 5 min at 21 °C.

Lipid extraction

The lipid extraction was performed as described before59 on the isolated SC of four Pf-FDMs and Rf-FDMs and additionally three samples of isolated SC from NHS (31-47y). SC was reconstituted in chloroform : methanol : water (1:2:0.5) and shaken at 120 rounds per minute at 40 °C for 1 h. The solvent was collected and the procedure was repeated sequentially with chloroform : methanol (1:1), chloroform : methanol (2:1) and heptane : isopropylalcohol (1:1). Water and 0.25 µM potassium chloride were added to the combined extracts to undergo phase separation overnight. The organic phase was collected by liquid-liquid extraction and the aqueous layer was washed with chloroform and left for two hours to phase separate. The combined organic layers were filtered through a 0.45 µM PVDF syringe filter (Grace, Deerfield, Illinois, United States) and the filtrate was evaporated and reconstituted in chloroform : methanol (2:1).

Ceramide lipid analysis employing LC-MS

Ceramide analysis was performed as described before59. Briefly, lipids were reconstituted at a concentration of 0.3 mg/ml in heptane : chloroform : methanol (95:2.5:2.5) containing 0.6 µM of deuterated CER N(24deu)S as internal standard. 5 µl of each sample was automatically injected and separated on an ACQUITY quaternary UPLC system (Waters, Milford, MA) with an analytical normal phase PVA-silica column (12 nm, S-5 μm, 100 × 2,0 mm) (YMC, Kyoto, Japan) using a binary solvent system of heptane (100%, solvent A) and heptane/isopropanol/ethanol (50:25:25, solvent B) under a flow rate of 0.8 ml/min The UPLC was coupled to a mass spectrometer (Xevo, TQ-S, Waters, Milford, MA) employing atmospheric pressure chemical ionization in positive ion mode to acquire full scan MS data over a range of m/z 350–1200. The samples were analyzed in randomized order, including quality control samples consisting of ex vivo SC lipid extract at 0.24 mg/ml and three calibration curves prepared from a mixture of synthetic ceramide standards. The raw data was processed using MassLynx and Target Lynx software version 4.1 (Waters, Milford, Massachusetts, United States). Peaks were integrated at the monoisotopic peak of the most abundant ion and corrected for internal standard, 13 C isotope distribution, source fragmentation and increasing response by mass using a three dimensional response model.

Transepidermal water loss measurements

Trans epidermal water loss (TEWL) measurements were performed as described before16. Briefly, isolated SC was placed in a flow-through diffusion cell (PermeGear, Inc, Bethlehem, USA) filled with Millipore water. After 15 min of incubation, the closed chamber evaporimeter (Aqua Flux AF200: Biox Systems Ltd, London, UK) was placed on top of the SC. TEWL measurements were performed after every 5 s during a time period of 1200 s.

Statistical analysis

Data are represented as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.). Statistical analyses were performed employing GraphPad Prism version (10.2.3 (403)) (GraphPad Software, La Jolla California USA). Statistical significance was determined using the unpaired Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA. Multiple comparisons were conducted with the Holm-Šídák post-test. * indicates p < 0.05, ** indicates p < 0.01, and *** indicates p < 0.001.

Ethics

Skin was obtained after informed consent of patients undergoing surgery, and left-over material was used for isolation of primary cells and reconstruction of in vitro 3D HSE. The collection of primary cells is done in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles. All donors remain anonymous, and experiments are conducted following Article 7:467 of the Dutch Medical Treatment Agreement Law and the Code of Proper Use of Human Tissue, as outlined by the Dutch Federation of Biomedical Scientific Societies (https://www.federa.org). According to this national legislation, surplus tissue can be used for scientific research purposes when no written objection is made by the informed donor. Therefore, additional approval of an ethics committee regarding the scientific use of surplus tissue was not obligatory.
