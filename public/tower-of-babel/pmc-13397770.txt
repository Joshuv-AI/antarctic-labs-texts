Mindset against burnout? A cross-lagged mediation analysis of growth language mindset, learning engagement, and foreign language learning burnout
Authors: Zheying Xiao, Di Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConcerns have been rising over learner burnout in foreign language learning contexts. A growth language mindset has been linked to adaptive learning outcomes. Yet, little is known about its long-term relationship with burnout and the mechanisms underlying this association. This study examined whether learning engagement mediates the longitudinal relationship between growth language mindset and foreign language learning burnout among university students.MethodsUsing cluster sampling, 904 undergraduates were recruited at Time 1 from three universities in China, of whom 886 (Mage = 19.32, SD = 0.93) were retained for the final analyses. This research collected data on growth language mindset, learning engagement, and foreign language learning burnout at three time points between June 2024 and June 2025. A cross-lagged panel modeling was used to examine reciprocal relationships over time, and a mediation analysis was employed to test the indirect effect of growth language mindset on burnout via learning engagement. Standardized regression coefficients and confidence intervals were used to assess effect sizes and statistical significance.ResultsGrowth language mindset positively predicted subsequent learning engagement (βs = 0.18–0.22) and negatively predicted later foreign language learning burnout (βs = –0.18 to –0.21). In contrast, the prospective effects of burnout on growth language mindset were weak. The mediation analysis showed that learning engagement partially mediated the relationship between growth language mindset and reduced burnout, with a significant indirect effect (indirect β = –0.07, 95% CI [–0.11, –0.03]). Although the effect sizes were modest, the overall pattern was consistent across time points.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that students with stronger growth-oriented beliefs about language learning and higher levels of engagement are less likely to experience burnout over time. These results highlight meaningful dynamics in long-term foreign language learning and emphasize the need to improve both growth language mindset and sustained engagement to alleviate burnout in challenging learning contexts.

Introduction

Foreign language learning burnout (FLLB) has become an important concern in foreign language education because it is associated with poorer academic functioning, lower engagement, and reduced psychological well-being [8, 38, 48]. In Chinese EFL contexts, research has advanced the conceptualization and measurement of FLLB and documented its associations with negative emotions, engagement, and other psychological variables [28,31,50,52,]. At the same time, mindset-oriented research suggests that growth language mindset (GLM), or the belief that language ability is malleable, is related to more adaptive learning processes, whereas learning engagement (LE) has been identified as a key correlate of both achievement and burnout in language learning [2,8,49]. From a conservation-of-resources (COR) perspective, these constructs may be understood as part of a resource process in which GLM functions as a personal psychological resource, LE reflects resource investment and a potential route to resource gain, and FLLB represents a resource-loss outcome under prolonged learning stress [19, 21, 23].

Despite these advances, important limitations remain in understanding the developmental ordering of GLM, LE, and FLLB. Much of the existing literature has relied on cross-sectional designs (e.g., [8, 14, 33], which are limited in clarifying temporal precedence and the cumulative gain-and-loss processes emphasized in COR theory [20, 23]. As a result, it remains unclear whether GLM primarily serves as an antecedent resource that promotes later engagement and protects learners against burnout, or whether burnout itself gradually erodes learners’ subsequent engagement and adaptive beliefs. This issue is important because, from a COR perspective, burnout may not only be an outcome of insufficient resources but also a source of further resource loss over time, as COR theory emphasizes that initial loss can trigger additional loss and depletion processes [19, 21, 23]. Clarifying these temporal and reciprocal relations is theoretically important because it helps determine whether GLM and LE function mainly as antecedent protective resources, or whether they are also vulnerable to burnout-driven resource erosion over time. It is also practically important because the answer bears on intervention priorities. If GLM and LE primarily operate as antecedents, educational support should focus on strengthening these adaptive resources early. If burnout also undermines them over time, preventing early burnout becomes equally critical for sustaining positive learning processes. Yet reciprocal and developmental pathways among GLM, LE, and FLLB have rarely been examined together within a unified longitudinal framework, particularly among Chinese university EFL learners.

To address this gap, the present study employed a three-wave longitudinal design and a cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) to examine the bidirectional relations between GLM and FLLB, as well as the mediating role of LE. By integrating mindset theory with a COR perspective and modeling both resource-protective and resource-erosive processes over time, this study aims to clarify the reciprocal developmental relations among learners’ mindset, engagement, and foreign language learning burnout in Chinese higher education EFL contexts.

Theoretical framework

Conservation of resources theory

Conservation of Resources (COR) theory posits that individuals are motivated to acquire, retain, and protect valued resources, and that stress arises when these resources are threatened, lost, or when resource investment fails to yield sufficient returns [18, 19]. Resources include objects, personal characteristics, conditions, and energies that are valued in themselves or that help individuals attain other valued resources [17–19]. A central principle of COR is the primacy of resource loss, meaning that losses are typically more salient than comparable gains, and sustained losses may trigger loss spirals that undermine functioning and well-being [7, 22]. At the same time, successful resource investment may facilitate resource gain and support adaptive functioning over time [19, 20].

Within this framework, personal resources refer to internal characteristics or self-related beliefs that help individuals adapt to challengess over time [19, 22]. In the present study, COR provides an overarching lens for conceptualizing GLM as a personal psychological resource, LE as a form of resource investment that may also contribute to resource gain, and FLLB as an outcome of cumulative resource depletion under sustained learning demands. At the same time, consistent with COR’s emphasis on loss processes, burnout may also function as a source of further resource depletion over time. Thus, COR offers a useful basis for examining how learner resources may be protected, invested, accumulated, and eroded in foreign language learning contexts.

Growth mindset theory

Growth mindset theory proposes that individuals differ in their beliefs about the malleability of abilities, with a growth mindset reflecting the belief that competence can be developed through effort, effective strategies, and learning from feedback [53]. Such beliefs shape learners’ interpretations of academic challenges and setbacks and, in turn, influence their attribution patterns, emotional responses, and coping behaviors [10, 53]. In foreign language learning contexts, growth mindset has been linked to greater self-regulation, perceived competence, and engagement in language-related activities [9, 27]. Although growth mindset is not explicitly defined as a “resource” in COR theory, its role in shaping motivation and adaptive coping suggests that it may function as a personal resource that supports subsequent resource investment and helps guard against resource loss. Accordingly, growth mindset offers a key psychological basis for understanding learners’ engagement and vulnerability to burnout.

A dynamic resource-based model of GLM, LE, and FLLB

Based on COR theory and growth mindset theory, the present study proposes a dynamic resource-based model to explain the relationships among GLM, LE, and FLLB over time. Growth mindset theory suggests that learners who believe language ability is developable are more likely to interpret learning challenges as manageable and respond to setbacks with sustained effort and adaptive persistence ([36, 53]. From a COR perspective, such beliefs may function as personal psychological resources that support continued resource investment in learning activities. In the present study, this investment is reflected in LE, that is, learners’ active behavioral, cognitive, and emotional involvement in learning [15, 34]. When sustained over time, such engagement may contribute to resource gain by fostering competence, positive learning experiences, and other adaptive outcomes, thereby reducing vulnerability to FLLB [6, 50].

At the same time, COR theory emphasizes that resource processes are dynamic and may also operate in the opposite direction. Prolonged resource depletion may initiate loss spirals, in which initial losses undermine subsequent resource investment and adaptive functioning [19, 21, 23]. In language-learning contexts, this implies that FLLB may weaken learners’ engagement and potentially erode growth-oriented beliefs over time, particularly when sustained stress reduces their perceived capacity to cope with learning demands [25, 32]. Furthermore, COR theory suggests that resource loss may exert stronger and more immediate effects than resource gain (loss priority principle), and that certain thresholds of depletion may be required before more stable personal resources are substantially affected [22]. These principles imply that forward (resource-protective) and reverse (resource-erosive) pathways may differ in strength and consistency over time.

Accordingly, the present model conceptualizes GLM, LE, and FLLB as dynamically interconnected through both resource-protective and resource-erosive processes. This integrated framework provides the conceptual basis for examining reciprocal relations among these variables within a longitudinal design.

Literature review and hypothesis development

Bidirectional associations between GLM and FLLB

GLM predicts FLLB

Growth language mindset refers to learners’ belief that language ability is developable rather than fixed [36, 56]. Learners with stronger growth-oriented beliefs are more likely to view challenge as part of the learning process, persist after failure, and maintain adaptive effort in the face of difficulty [10, 53]. In foreign language learning, where progress often requires sustained effort under repeated evaluation and frustration, such beliefs may be particularly relevant to learners’ emotional adjustment [28, 31]. Foreign language learning burnout, by contrast, is typically characterized by exhaustion, detachment, and reduced accomplishment in the language-learning process [28],[31].

From a COR perspective, GLM can be conceptualized as a personal resource. COR theory defines resources as objects, conditions, personal characteristics, or energies that are valued in themselves or that help individuals attain other valued resources [18, 19]. Personal resources are generally understood as internal characteristics or self-related beliefs that help individuals adapt to challenges and sustain functioning [22]. In COR-related research, conceptually similar self-relevant beliefs and capacities, such as self-efficacy, perceived control, optimism, and resilience, have commonly been treated as personal resources because they support coping, persistence, and adaptive functioning under stress [1, 3, 39]. GLM fits this logic because it is an internal belief about one's capacity for development that shapes how language-learning demands are appraised and managed. To the extent that GLM supports more constructive interpretations of difficulty and sustained effort under challenge [36], it can be understood as a personal resource that helps learners protect and mobilize other adaptive resources over time.

Within the same framework, FLLB can be viewed as a resource-loss outcome under prolonged resource depletion. COR theory posits that stress and burnout become especially likely when resources are threatened, lost, or insufficient to meet continuing demands [19, 21, 23]. In foreign language learning, burnout therefore represents a state in which learners’ emotional and motivational resources have been progressively diminished by sustained demands and insufficient recovery or gain [28, 31]. If GLM functions as a personal resource, then learners with stronger GLM should be better positioned to cope with challenges, sustain engagement, and reduce their vulnerability to the loss processes implicated in FLLB.

The available empirical evidence is broadly consistent with this expectation, although direct longitudinal evidence remains limited. In language-learning research, growth mindset has been linked to self-efficacy, self-regulation, engagement, and perceived proficiency [2, 9, 27], all of which are relevant to adaptive functioning under sustained academic demand. More directly, studies in language-education contexts have suggested that stronger growth-oriented beliefs are associated with lower maladaptive adjustment, including lower burnout-related experiences [27, 42, 52]. Although much of this evidence is cross-sectional, it supports the theoretical expectation that GLM may function as a protective factor against subsequent FLLB. Accordingly, the present study proposes that higher GLM will negatively predict later FLLB.

FLLB predicts GLM

Although often being understood as relatively stable, growth mindset is also suggested by mindset theorists not entirely fixed and may be shaped by learners’ ongoing experiences and interpretations of those experiences [36, 53]. Specifically, from this perspective, when learners repeatedly experience exhaustion, detachment, and reduced accomplishment in language learning, they may become less likely to interpret setbacks as opportunities, language ability as malleable, effort as effective, and progress as attainable, which may in turn weaken growth-oriented beliefs.

This possibility is also theoretically plausible from a COR perspective. Because FLLB reflects cumulative resource depletion under prolonged learning demands, sustained burnout may undermine not only subsequent resource investment, but also other adaptive learner resources over time [19, 21, 23]. According to COR’s loss priority principle, burnout tends to be a potentially important source of later deterioration in learners’ adaptive functioning since resource loss tends to be more salient and influential than resource gain [19, 22]. At the same time, COR also implies that not all resources are equally vulnerable to a period of depletion. More stable personal resources may require more substantial or prolonged loss before they are meaningfully affected, an idea in line with resource thresholds [22]. From this perspective, FLLB may be theoretically capable of undermining GLM, but the effect may be weaker or less immediate.

Examining this reverse pathway is important for both theoretical and practical reasons. Theoretically, it helps determine whether it is also vulnerable to burnout-driven resource erosion over time. In other words, testing whether FLLB predicts provides a stricter assessment of the dynamic and reciprocal processes implied by COR theory, rather than assuming that mindset operates only in a forward direction. Practically, clarifying this pathway is important because intervention priorities differ depending on whether educators should focus primarily on strengthening learners’ growth-oriented beliefs early on or also on preventing burnout before it begins to erode those beliefs.

Available empirical evidence is suggestive but still limited. Research on academic burnout has shown that sustained exhaustion is associated with declines in perceived competence, self-efficacy, and learning confidence [25, 47], all of which are relevant to learners’ mindset about their capacity for academic improvement. However, most existing studies have relied on cross-sectional designs, and direct longitudinal evidence linking burnout to later changes in mindset beliefs remains scarce, especially in foreign language education. Accordingly, although this pathway is theoretically plausible, its magnitude and consistency remain uncertain. The present study therefore examines whether FLLB negatively predicts subsequent GLM over time.

Bidirectional associations between GLM and LE

Learning engagement refers to learners’ sustained behavioral, cognitive, and emotional involvement in learning activities [15]. In foreign language education, engagement is especially important because language development depends heavily on learners’ continued participation, effort, and active involvement in classroom and out-of-class learning processes [49]. From a COR perspective, LE can be understood primarily as a form of resource investment, insofar as engaged learners actively allocate time, attention, and effort to meet learning demands [19, 20]. At the same time, such investment may also contribute to resource gain, because sustained engagement can promote competence, positive learning experiences, and other adaptive outcomes that help learners cope more effectively with later demands [6, 41, 50]. In this sense, LE fits the definitional logic of COR because it reflects the active deployment of resources in ways that may both protect against loss and facilitate further resource accumulation over time.

GLM predicts LE

Consistent with growth mindset theory, learners with stronger growth-oriented beliefs tend to view setbacks as opportunities for improvement and to persist under challenge [10, 53]. In foreign language learning, learners who believe that language ability can be improved through effort and effective strategies are more likely to regard difficulty as manageable, persist when progress is slow, and remain actively involved in learning tasks [2, 27]. From this perspective, GLM provides a motivational basis for continued behavioral, cognitive, and emotional involvement in language learning.

From a COR perspective, if GLM functions as a personal resource, it should help learners sustain the resource investment reflected in engagement when they encounter learning demands. Because engagement reflects the active allocation of time, attention, and effort to learning activities, learners with stronger GLM may be more willing to invest these resources under challenge rather than withdraw from the learning process [19, 20]. In this sense, GLM may support LE by fostering a more adaptive orientation toward effortful involvement in language learning.

Available empirical evidence is mainly consistent with this view. In Korean university L2 learners, growth language mindset directly predicted both self-efficacy and engagement [27]. A Hong Kong primary study found an indirect route from mindset to English achievement via strategic engagement [2]. In Chinese undergraduates, structural equation modeling showed growth mindset and grit positively predicted academic engagement, partly through academic self-efficacy [29]. A study of Indonesian undergraduates reported that growth mindset significantly predicted academic engagement [13]. A Chinese secondary-school intervention increased growth mindset, academic self-efficacy, and study engagement, with engagement gains partly mediated by mindset [55]. Although direct longitudinal evidence in foreign language education remains limited, the available theory and empirical findings support the expectation that stronger GLM may predict higher subsequent LE. Accordingly, the present study proposes that GLM will positively predict later LE.

LE predicts GLM

Although growth mindset is often treated as an antecedent belief, it is also considered responsive to learning experiences and contextual feedback [53]. In foreign language learning, sustained engagement increases learners’ exposure to practice, feedback, strategy use, and opportunities to notice improvement, all of which are cumulative learning experiences central to language development and classroom participation [49]. These experiences may strengthen learners’ sense that progress is achievable through effort and effective strategies, thereby reinforcing growth-oriented beliefs about language ability [2]. From this perspective, LE may contribute to GLM’s further development over time.

COR theory supports this idea that engagement can participate in a resource gain spiral that strengthens growth-oriented beliefs. People are viewed by COR theorists as motivated to invest resources (e.g. time, effort, attention) to protect and grow their resource pool [17, 20]. Engagement as investment in learning activities may generate resource gains such as competence, mastery experiences, and positive feedback [19, 20, 41, 50]. Such gains may in turn support more positive self-relevant beliefs about learning, including the belief that one’s language ability can improve through effort [50]. In this sense, LE may function not only as a consequence of personal resources, but also predict and strengthen growth language mindset via gain spirals.

Empirical evidence supports this possible prediction. In Korean EFL learners, the authors emphasize that engagement experiences (successes, feedback) are key “resources” that build self-efficacy and thus can reinforce growth beliefs over time [27]. The language mindset meaning system model treats mindsets and engagement as a mutually reinforcing system and suggests that sustained engagement helps consolidate growth-consistent beliefs [36, 56]. Classroom studies and reviews argue that teacher support and engaging classroom climates nurture growth-oriented language beliefs, implying that engagement-rich environments are one route to shifting mindsets [13, 48].

Direct longitudinal, cross-lagged tests of this specific path are still needed. However, existing theory and related findings suggest that mindset beliefs are responsive, at least to some extent, to learners’ experiences and contextual conditions rather than being entirely static [53, 54]. Accordingly, it is theoretically meaningful to examine whether higher LE predicts subsequent increases in GLM over time.

Bidirectional associations between LE and FLLB

Empirical studies in both general and foreign language contexts consistently show a negative association between engagement and burnout [4, 5, 8, 43]. From a COR perspective, this negative association is meaningful because engagement reflects continued resource investment, whereas burnout reflects cumulative resource depletion under sustained demands. This pattern motivates the examination of reciprocal links between LE and FLLB in longitudinal designs.

LE predicts FLLB

From a COR perspective, engaged learners may accumulate competence, positive learning experiences, and stronger efficacy-related resources, which can help buffer the loss processes associated with burnout [41, 50]. In this sense, higher LE may reduce learners’ vulnerability to the prolonged resource depletion implicated in FLLB. Examining this reverse pathway is theoretically important because it helps determine whether burnout functions only as an outcome of insufficient resources or also as a source of further erosion in learners’ subsequent resource investment. It is also practically important because, if FLLB undermines later engagement, early intervention should focus not only on fostering engagement but also on preventing burnout before it disrupts continued involvement in learning.

Available empirical evidence is consistent with this expectation. In general, higher-education contexts, LE has been found to be negatively associated with learning burnout [4, 43, 51, ]. In foreign language education, more specifically, Cong et al. [8] reported that engagement was negatively related to burnout among EFL learners. Longitudinal studies also provide support for the temporal relevance of this pathway. Huang et al. [26] found that cognitive engagement was negatively associated with later burnout in online learning, and Maricuţoiu and Sulea [40] documented co-development between student engagement and burnout across a semester, supporting the view that engagement can precede changes in burnout symptoms. Although direct longitudinal evidence in foreign language learning remains limited, these findings support examining LE as a prospective protective factor against later FLLB.

FLLB predicts LE

Burnout reflects a state of cumulative resource depletion under sustained learning demands [19, 21, 23]. In the language-learning context, when learners experience exhaustion, detachment, and reduced accomplishment, they may have fewer emotional, cognitive, and motivational resources available for continued investment in learning activities [28, 31]. In this sense, burnout is not only a consequence of insufficient resources, but may also undermine subsequent resource investment. COR’s loss priority principle further suggests that resource loss tends to be more salient and influential than resource gain,accordingly, once burnout-related depletion begins, it may directly undermine learners’ capacity to sustain subsequent engagement [19, 22].

Prior empirical findings are broadly aligned with this theoretical expectation. Maricuţoiu and Sulea [40] found that increases in student burnout were followed by decreases in student engagement within a semester. In language-learning research, Liu et al. [32] reported that English learning burnout was negatively associated with engagement within a COR-informed model, suggesting that burnout is linked to reduced learning involvement. Related longitudinal evidence also indicates that engagement and burnout may influence each other over time, with burnout negatively predicting subsequent engagement in some educational contexts [26, 40]. Although direct longitudinal evidence in foreign language education remains limited, these findings justify examining FLLB as a negative predictor of subsequent LE in the present cross-lagged framework.

Research gaps and rationale for a longitudinal test

Previous research has provided valuable insights into the relationships among GLM, LE, and FLLB. However, several important limitations remain. First, much of the existing literature has relied on cross-sectional designs, which are limited in establishing temporal precedence and disentangling reciprocal influences among these constructs [4, 5, 8, 32]. As a result, it remains unclear whether GLM and LE primarily function as antecedents of burnout, consequences of burnout, or both. This limitation is especially important from a COR perspective, because COR theory is centrally concerned with how resources are invested, accumulated, threatened, and depleted over time rather than with static associations alone [19, 20, 23].

Second, prior research has focused predominantly on the protective role of growth mindset in relation to learning outcomes and well-being, whereas the potential reverse effects of burnout and engagement on growth mindset have received much less attention. In particular, direct longitudinal evidence testing whether sustained FLLB predicts subsequent changes in GLM remains scarce, especially in foreign language learning contexts. This gap is theoretically important because, according to COR theory, burnout may not only be an outcome of insufficient resources but also a source of further resource depletion over time [19, 21, 23]. Clarifying this reverse pathway can therefore help determine whether GLM functions mainly as an antecedent protective resource or is also vulnerable to burnout-driven erosion.

Third, although LE has been identified as a key correlate of burnout and academic functioning, its mediating role in the dynamic association between mindset and burnout has rarely been examined in foreign language education. Existing studies that have explored mediation have largely relied on cross-sectional designs [8, 51], which are not well suited to capturing developmental processes or temporal ordering. A longitudinal examination of LE is therefore needed to clarify whether engagement serves as a mechanism through which GLM protects against burnout and whether burnout, in turn, may erode adaptive learning processes over time.

In response to these gaps, the present study employs a three-wave cross-lagged panel model to investigate their bidirectional associations and the mediating role of LE over time. This design allows for a more rigorous test of temporal ordering and reduces the risk of interpreting contemporaneous associations as developmental influence. More importantly, by clarifying whether learners’ beliefs and engagement operate mainly as protective resources or are also shaped by prolonged burnout, the study contributes to a more dynamic understanding of motivational and emotional development from a COR perspective. Such evidence also has practical value for informing the timing and targeting of pedagogical interventions aimed at sustaining learners’ resilience and long-term engagement in foreign language learning.

Method

Participants

Participants were Chinese undergraduates recruited from three public comprehensive universities located in central China that agreed to participate in a longitudinal project on foreign language learning experiences and psychological adjustment. A random cluster sampling procedure was employed within each university, whereby 20 intact teaching classes were randomly selected from each institution (60 classes in total). All students in the selected classes were invited to participate. On average, each class comprised approximately 33 students, of whom about 15 (approximately 45.5%) completed the survey at the initial assessment, yielding an initial sample of 904 students. To evaluate potential clustering effects associated with the class-based sampling design, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were computed at Time 1 for GLM, LE, and FLLB. The between-class variance estimates were negligible, with ICCs below 0.001 for GLM, LE, and FLLB, suggesting no meaningful class-level clustering. We therefore used single-level modeling in the subsequent analyses. The final sample consisted of 886 students with a mean age of 19.32 years (SD = 0.93), including 473 first-year and 413 s-year undergraduate students.

Procedures

Data were collected at three time points in June 2024 (T1), December 2024 (T2), and June 2025 (T3). After excluding cases with excessive missing data or inconsistent responses across three waves, 886 students who completed all three measurements with valid data were kept for the final analyses, forming the analytic sample. Of the 904 students initially assessed at T1, therefore, 886 (approximately 98.0%) were retained, while 18 (approximately 2.0%) dropped out before T3.

The study received ethical approval from the institutional ethics committee in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Before data collection started, all students were fully informed about the study’s purpose and procedures, such as that participation was entirely voluntary, they could withdraw at any time without negative consequences, and that their responses would be kept confidential. Written informed consent was obtained from every participant, and the data were used solely for academic research purposes.

To check whether sample attrition might introduce bias into the results, we compared participants who completed all three waves (retained group, n = 886) with those who dropped out before the final wave (dropped group, n = 18). Normality tests using the Shapiro–Wilk statistic showed that neither group deviated significantly from normality in terms of GLM, LE, or FLLB (ps > 0.10). Levene’s tests for equality of variances yielded non-significant results (ps > 0.05), which supports the assumption of homogeneity of variances.

Measures

Growth language mindset

GLM was assessed using the Growth Language Mindset Scale (GLMS) developed by Lou and Noels [35]. The scale measures learners’ implicit beliefs about the malleability of foreign language ability and comprises three dimensions, including beliefs about language intelligence, beliefs about language learning ability, and beliefs about age-related language learning ability. The GLMS includes nine items (e.g., “No matter who you are, you can significantly improve your language intelligence.”), rated on a six-point Likert scale ranging from 1= strongly disagree to 6 = strongly agree. Higher scores indicate stronger endorsement of a growth-oriented mindset toward foreign language learning. In the present study, the GLMS demonstrated good internal consistency across the three waves (Cronbach’s α = 0.88, 0.86, and 0.82, respectively). Subscale reliabilities were also acceptable. For beliefs about language intelligence, Cronbach’s α ranged from 0.78 to 0.82 across waves. For beliefs about language learning ability, α ranged from 0.75 to 0.80. As for beliefs about age-related language learning ability, α ranged from 0.72 to 0.77. Confirmatory factor analyses supported the proposed three-factor structure at each time point.

Learning engagement

LE in English learning was assessed using the Language Classroom Engagement Scale (LCES), developed by Lkhagva Eerdemutu, Jean-Marc Dewaele, and Jing Wang [11]. This scale measures students’ engagement in language classrooms across three dimensions, including behavioral engagement, emotional engagement, and cognitive engagement, with three items for each dimension (nine items in total). Sample items include “When I cannot understand in my language class, I stay focused until I do,” and “I enjoy learning new things about languages in class.” All items were scored on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), and higher scores indicate greater levels of engagement in English learning. In this study, Cronbach’s α coefficients across the three assessments were 0.91, 0.90, and 0.87, respectively. Subscale reliabilities were satisfactory, with α coefficients ranging from 0.79 to 0.86 for behavioral engagement, 0.76 to 0.84 for emotional engagement, and 0.81 to 0.88 for cognitive engagement across measurement occasions. Single-wave confirmatory factor analyses supported the three-factor structure at each time point.

Foreign language learning burnout

FLLB was assessed using the Foreign Language Learning Burnout Scale (FLLBS) developed by Li et al. [28]. The scale captures three dimensions of burnout, including exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced efficacy, comprising ten items in total (e.g., “I feel mentally exhausted when learning English.”). Items were rated on a five-point Likert scale from 1= strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree, with higher scores indicating greater burnout. The FLLBS showed good internal consistency at all three time points (Cronbach’s α = 0.90, 0.88, and 0.85, respectively). Subscale reliabilities were also acceptable, with α values ranging from 0.80 to 0.87 for exhaustion, 0.74 to 0.82 for cynicism, and 0.72 to 0.79 for reduced efficacy across waves. Confirmatory factor analyses supported the three-factor structure at each measurement occasion.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS 27.0 and Mplus 8.3. Preliminary analyses in SPSS served to describe participants’ demographic characteristics and the distributions of GLM, LE, and FLLB. Categorical variables were summarized with frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were described using means and standard deviations (M ± SD). Independent-samples t tests, one-way ANOVA, and Pearson correlations were conducted to examine preliminary group differences and bivariate associations.

Before testing the structural model, we evaluated the measurement properties of the three scales. Single-wave confirmatory factor analyses (CFAs) were conducted at each time point to assess factorial validity. Composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) were calculated to examine convergent validity. Discriminant validity was evaluated using the Fornell–Larcker criterion. Longitudinal measurement invariance was tested for GLM and FLLB across the three waves using configural, metric, and scalar models (ΔCFI ≤ 0.01). For LE, invariance could not be evaluated as the longitudinal measurement model was saturated.

A cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) was specified in Mplus to explore reciprocal relations among GLM, LE, and FLLB over time. Demographic variables were included as covariates, and indirect effects were tested via bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5000 resamples.

Model parameters were estimated using robust maximum likelihood (MLR), and missing data were handled using full information maximum likelihood (FIML) under the assumption of missing at random (Enders and Bandalos, [12]). Model fit was evaluated using CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR, with CFI and TLI > 0.90 and RMSEA and SRMR < 0.08 indicating acceptable fit. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Assessment of common method variance

Since the data in this study were collected through self-reports, it was necessary to examine the potential influence of common method variance (CMV) [45]. As an initial check, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted. All items measuring GLM, LE, and FLLB across the three waves (T1–T3) were included in an unrotated exploratory factor analysis using SPSS.

At each wave, several factors emerged with eigenvalues greater than 1. The total variance explained by all factors was 59.87% at T1, 61.42% at T2, and 63.08% at T3. In contrast, the largest single factor accounted for 18.96%, 21.37%, and 22.03% of the variance at the three waves, respectively, well below the commonly accepted 40% threshold for serious method bias [44]. These results suggest that no single factor dominated the covariance among variables and that CMV is unlikely to have substantially distorted the observed relationships. Nevertheless, it should be noted that Harman’s test is a conservative diagnostic and does not completely rule out common method bias [16].

Discussion

Interpretation of findings

This three-wave study examined the longitudinal interplay among GLM, LE, and FLLB. Overall, the findings indicate a directionally asymmetric pattern. GLM more consistently predicted later engagement and lower burnout than it was predicted by them, and LE emerged as one mechanism linking GLM to subsequent burnout. Interpreted through a COR lens, this pattern suggests that GLM may operate primarily as a personal resource that supports continued engagement, whereas LE reflects the investment of resources in learning and helps explain how growth-oriented beliefs are associated with lower burnout over time. By contrast, the reverse pathways from burnout to later engagement and mindset were weak and inconsistent across waves, suggesting that loss processes may be more conditional, slower to unfold, or more dependent on the type and stability of the resource involved. The following sections interpret these findings in relation to the theoretical framework and prior research.

GLM predicts subsequent FLLB (H1)

Consistent with Hypothesis 1, higher levels of GLM prospectively predicted lower levels of FLLB across adjacent waves. This finding extends prior work in language learning contexts showing that learners’ conceptions or beliefs about language learning are meaningfully associated with burnout-related experiences [52]. It also complements studies linking growth mindset to motivational and self-regulatory resources relevant for sustained learning [2, 9, 46]. Importantly, the present evidence is longitudinal. Even after controlling for previous levels of burnout and other covariates, GLM retained incremental predictive value for later burnout.

This result can be understood more clearly by integrating growth mindset theory with a COR perspective. From a mindset perspective, learners with stronger growth-oriented beliefs are more likely to interpret difficulty as manageable, view setbacks as part of learning, and maintain effort when progress is slow [10, 53]. From a COR perspective, this means that GLM may function as a personal resource that helps learners respond to demands in more adaptive ways. When challenges are appraised as workable rather than threatening, learners may be better able to preserve motivation, sustain effort, and avoid the prolonged depletion processes that culminate in burnout [18, 21, 23]. In this sense, the negative longitudinal path from GLM to FLLB is theoretically meaningful because it suggests that growth-oriented beliefs may help interrupt or weaken resource-loss processes over time.

This interpretation is also consistent with prior findings in language learning. Earlier research has shown that beliefs about language learning are associated with burnout-related experiences [52] and that growth mindset is positively related to self-efficacy, self-regulation, and other adaptive resources relevant to sustained learning [2, 9]. The present study extends this literature by showing that the protective role of GLM is not limited to concurrent adjustment, but is also evident longitudinally. Viewed together, these findings support the view that GLM may serve as a relatively stable protective resource that reduces learners’ vulnerability to later burnout in foreign language learning contexts.

FLLB predicts GLM as a small, short-lived effect (H2)

Support for Hypothesis 2 was limited. FLLB showed a small negative cross-lagged effect on GLM from T1 to T2, but this effect did not reappear from T2 to T3. Rather than indicating that burnout has no relevance for later mindset, this pattern suggests that the reverse effect may be weak and inconsistent across waves. In other words, burnout-related strain may temporarily undermine learners’ growth-oriented beliefs, but such influence does not appear to be robust across all time intervals in the present design.

This pattern can be interpreted through a COR perspective. On the one hand, COR’s loss priority principle suggests that resource loss tends to be more salient and impactful than resource gain, so it is theoretically plausible that sustained burnout could weaken later adaptive beliefs and functioning [19, 22]. From this view, repeated experiences of exhaustion, detachment, and reduced accomplishment may make learners less likely to interpret effort as effective and progress as attainable, thereby weakening GLM over time. On the other hand, COR also implies that not all resources are equally vulnerable to depletion. More stable personal resources may require more substantial or prolonged loss before they are noticeably affected, which is consistent with the notion of resource thresholds [22]. Because GLM is a relatively stable self-relevant belief rather than a highly state-like process, ordinary fluctuations in burnout may be insufficient to produce sustained shifts in mindset across all waves [53].

This interpretation is also broadly consistent with the wider literature. Research on academic burnout has linked sustained exhaustion to declines in self-efficacy, confidence, and other competence-related beliefs [25, 47], suggesting that burnout can erode adaptive psychological functioning. At the same time, intervention and meta-analytic research indicates that mindset is modifiable, but also relatively stable in the absence of repeated, salient experiences or targeted support [37, 53, 55]. Collectively, the present findings suggest that FLLB may be theoretically capable of undermining GLM, but that this reverse effect is likely to be weaker, more conditional, and less stable than the forward protective path from GLM to burnout. Future research with denser measurement intervals and closer attention to contextual conditions may help clarify when burnout is more likely to shape language mindset over time.

LE as a partial mechanism from GLM to FLLB (H3)

Consistent with Hypothesis 3, LE partially mediated the longitudinal relationship between GLM and later FLLB. This finding suggests that one reason why learners with stronger growth-oriented beliefs experience less later burnout is that they remain more actively involved in learning over time. In other words, GLM appears to reduce burnout not only directly, but also indirectly by supporting higher levels of engagement.

The logic of this mediation can be understood by linking growth mindset theory with a COR perspective. Since learners with stronger GLM are more likely to view language-learning difficulty as manageable and improvement as attainable through effort and effective strategies [53], they are more likely to persist when progress is slow and to remain behaviorally, cognitively, and emotionally involved in learning tasks. From a COR perspective [20, 24], this pattern means that GLM, as a personal resource, may support the continued investment reflected in engagement when learners encounter stress. Sustained engagement, in turn, may generate engagement-related gains such as competence development, positive feedback, mastery experiences, and stronger efficacy-related beliefs, which help learners cope more effectively with later demands and reduce vulnerability to burnout [41, 50]. Thus, the mediating role of LE is theoretically meaningful because it reflects a resource process in which an initial personal resource supports continued investment, and that investment helps buffer later resource depletion.

Empirically, the link between GLM and LE is in line with research showing that language mindsets are associated with learner engagement and perceived proficiency in second language learning [27]. It also corresponds with studies suggesting that growth mindset relates to motivational and self-regulatory tendencies that support continued participation in learning [2, 9]. The negative association between engagement and burnout is likewise consistent with prior work documenting inverse relations between these constructs and, in some cases, longitudinal effects from engagement to burnout [8, 26, 40]. More broadly, research in education often identifies engagement as a proximal pathway through which beliefs and self-efficacy are linked to learning outcomes and student adjustment [41, 50]. Overall, the present mediation findings provide longitudinal support, within the limits of the study design, for engagement as one plausible mechanism through which GLM may contribute to lower levels of subsequent burnout.

Null reverse paths and H4

Hypothesis 4 was not supported. The indirect pathway from FLLB to GLM via LE was non-significant, and the reverse paths from burnout were weak and inconsistent across waves. This pattern suggests that, in the present study, burnout did not reliably reduce later engagement to an extent that would subsequently translate into measurable change in GLM. In other words, although the reverse pathway was theoretically plausible, the data did not support LE as a stable mechanism linking FLLB to later mindset change over time.

COR theory suggests that burnout reflects cumulative resource depletion and may, in principle, undermine later resource investment and adaptive functioning [19, 22]. However, the effects of loss may depend on the type, stability, and threshold of the resource involved. LE is more state-like and more immediately responsive to ongoing learning conditions, whereas GLM is a relatively more stable self-relevant belief [15, 36, 49, 53]. Accordingly, even when burnout-related strain is present, its effects may not be strong enough, persistent enough, or consistent enough across time intervals to produce a full indirect pathway from FLLB to GLM through LE. This interpretation is also compatible with COR’s loss priority principle and the notion of resource thresholds. Although loss can be powerful, more stable personal resources may require more prolonged or substantial depletion before meaningful change becomes observable [19, 22].

The non-significant H4 does not necessarily contradict COR theory. Rather, it suggests that reverse loss processes may be more contingent and less readily observable than forward protective processes in this context. In the forward direction, the logic is relatively direct. Growth-oriented beliefs support continued engagement, and sustained engagement helps generate gains that reduce vulnerability to burnout. In the reverse direction, however, the process is more conditional. Burnout would first need to reduce engagement in a sufficiently robust way, and the resulting reduction in engagement would then need to alter a comparatively stable belief system such as GLM. That sequence may be more difficult to detect, particularly across wider measurement intervals and after accounting for strong autoregressive stability.

This interpretation is broadly consistent with prior mixed evidence. Some studies have reported burnout-to-engagement effects or reciprocal relations between burnout and engagement [26, 40], whereas the present study found that these reverse processes were not robust across waves. One possible implication is that burnout-related erosion may depend more strongly on contextual conditions, time scale, or the availability of compensatory resources than the forward protective pathway does. Future research using denser longitudinal designs and richer contextual measures may help clarify when burnout is more likely to reduce engagement and, through it, alter later mindset beliefs.

Educational implications

The longitudinal pattern observed in this study offers several practice-oriented implications. First, because GLM more consistently predicted later engagement and lower burnout than the reverse, the findings suggest that it may be especially valuable to strengthen growth-oriented beliefs before burnout-related strain becomes entrenched. In practice, classroom instruction may benefit from reinforcing incremental views of language ability through structured progress reflections, opportunities to notice improvement, and feedback that emphasizes effort, strategy use, and revision rather than fixed ability. Prior work suggests that mindset-supportive messages are more effective when embedded in ongoing instructional practice rather than delivered as isolated messages [37, 53]. In language learning contexts, such beliefs have also been linked to engagement and perceived proficiency [27], which is consistent with the present longitudinal pattern.

Second, because LE partially mediated the association between GLM and later burnout, instructional practices that sustain active involvement in learning may help translate positive beliefs into lower emotional strain over time. In line with the resource-based logic of the present study, engagement appears to be one pathway through which growth-oriented beliefs are enacted in everyday learning. This suggests the value of instructional strategies that support continued behavioral, cognitive, and emotional involvement, such as scaffolded speaking tasks, structured peer interaction, and regular formative feedback that promotes competence development. Prior research has likewise identified engagement as an important pathway linking learner beliefs to academic outcomes and adjustment [41, 50]. Although causality cannot be inferred from the present design, the converging evidence suggests that maintaining engagement may help reduce learners’ vulnerability to burnout.

Third, the weak and inconsistent reverse pathways from burnout to later engagement and mindset suggest that early identification of burnout remains important, even if burnout did not robustly predict later changes in all variables across waves. Institutions may therefore benefit from monitoring exhaustion, disengagement, and participation patterns during the semester in order to identify emerging strain before it accumulates. Brief assessments combined with participation data may help teachers and institutions provide timely support. Targeted responses, such as strategy workshops, academic advising, or workload adjustments, may help address emerging difficulties and prevent further resource depletion. Because the present study does not establish causal effects, future intervention research is still needed to evaluate which supports are most effective in sustaining resilience and long-term engagement in foreign language learning.

Strengths and limitations

This study makes three main contributions. First, the three-wave cross-lagged design provides longitudinal evidence that growth language mindset prospectively predicts lower foreign language learning burnout, even after accounting for prior levels of engagement and burnout. Second, it directly examines the reverse pathway from burnout to mindset, a relationship that has rarely been tested longitudinally in foreign language learning. The results suggest that such reverse effects may exist, but are weak and inconsistent across waves. Third, the study identifies learning engagement as a partial longitudinal mediator, thereby clarifying one process through which mindset beliefs may be linked to later burnout.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. The sample was drawn from universities within a single national context, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Cultural and institutional conditions may shape both resource processes and stress responses [19]. Although the study used a longitudinal design, the data remain correlational, and unmeasured variables such as classroom climate, instructional practices, or language anxiety may have influenced the observed associations. In addition, all constructs were assessed through self-report measures, which may introduce shared method variance despite diagnostic checks. Future research using multi-source data, denser longitudinal designs, and intervention-based approaches would help test the proposed processes more directly and clarify the conditions under which reverse pathways become more pronounced. Overall, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence of longitudinal directional associations rather than definitive causal effects.

Conclusion

This study examined the longitudinal relationships among growth language mindset, learning engagement, and foreign language learning burnout using a three-wave cross-lagged panel design. The results showed that students with stronger growth-oriented beliefs about language learning tended to experience lower levels of burnout over time. This association operated both directly and indirectly through higher learning engagement. By contrast, the reverse effects from burnout to later growth language mindset were weak and inconsistent across waves.

Several contributions emerge from these findings. First, by employing a three-wave longitudinal design, the study provides clearer evidence regarding the temporal ordering among mindset, engagement, and burnout in foreign language learning, extending a literature that has relied largely on cross-sectional data. Second, the findings support the view that GLM may function as a relatively stable protective resource, whereas FLLB appears less able to produce consistent changes in mindset across time. Third, the study identifies LE as one mechanism linking GLM to later burnout, thereby clarifying how growth-oriented beliefs may be translated into lower emotional strain through sustained involvement in learning.

Overall, the findings support a dynamic resource-based understanding of foreign language learning, in which growth-oriented beliefs help sustain engagement and reduce vulnerability to burnout over time. They also suggest that reverse loss processes may be more conditional and less stable than forward protective processes in this context. Understanding how these motivational and engagement-related processes unfold over time may help researchers and educators better explain why some learners remain resilient and involved in language learning, whereas others become increasingly vulnerable to burnout.
