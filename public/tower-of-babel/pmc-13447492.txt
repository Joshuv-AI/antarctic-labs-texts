Euhabitability: A New Term to Aid in the Search for Life in Addition to the Habitable Zone Framework
Authors: Donald M Glaser, Stephanie Olson, Svetlana Berdyugina, Paul Byrne, Peter Cawood, Manasvi Lingam, Jacob Lustig-Yaeger, Tilman Spohn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe search for life on exoplanets is difficult due to observational limitations stemming from vast separating distances. The Habitable Zone (HZ) is a simplifying framework for identifying exoplanets that may be suitable for life, given our observational limitations. The HZ framework is built on many simplifying assumptions and only attempts to bound the circumstellar regions where surface oceans are not precluded. In other words, HZ exoplanets are where surface oceans are possible, not necessarily where life is possible. Water is essential for life as we know it, however, life requires additional factors such as nutrients and a magnetosphere. Therefore, the criteria of the HZ framework is one step removed from assessing the suitability for life. This nuance is easily lost in translation and has caused confusion among researchers and the general public; as the term “Habitable Zone” implies that planets in the HZ are habitable and those outside are uninhabitable. Here we define a new term, euhabitable (suitable for life), that addresses this confusion. In brief, a euhabitable exoplanet can exist outside the HZ (e.g., a Europa-like planet), and an uninhabitable exoplanet can exist within the HZ (e.g., a desiccated planet). The “euhabitable” term can be used to discuss these cases explicitly, avoiding confusion between an exoplanet’s suitability for life and it’s position relative to the HZ. The euhabitable framework is informed by the advancements in astrobiology over the past three decades, and primes exoplanet science for the future by moving the habitability discussion beyond “follow the water.”

The Need for a New Perspective in the Search for Life on Exoplanets

Planet Earth is utterly teeming with life; the broadly defined surface (land surface, oceans, upper ∼5 km of crust, and most of the troposphere; Griffin et al. 2017; Cockell 2025) is inhabited by plant, animal, fungal, archeal, and—mostly—bacterial organisms. Evidence of life has been found in every explored environment on Earth (Fig. 1); from environments with obviously habitable mesic conditions (rainforests, estuaries, wetlands, coral reefs, savannas, etc.), to environments with extreme cold (Huston et al. 2000; Steven et al. 2007; Goordial et al. 2016), heat (Boyd et al. 2012; Fernandes-Martins et al. 2021), aridity (Warren-Rhodes et al. 2006; Azua-Bustos et al. 2012), pH (Bowen De León et al. 2013; Maltseva et al. 2023), and salinity (Dickson et al. 2013; Najjari et al. 2025) as well as deep within the crust (Trembath-Reichert et al. 2017; Lollar et al. 2019; Wee et al. 2021) and high in the atmosphere (Yang et al. 2009; Griffin et al. 2017).

Habitability on Earth is assessed at increasingly small scales with high resolution measurements and extremely sensitive methods. As we search the fringes of habitability on Earth, we encounter increasingly lower abundances of microbial cells such that any evidence of colonization is equivocal (for an in depth review, see Cockell 2025). This is mostly due to the logistical barriers to sampling regions at the fringe of habitability, and difficulties in sample collection, transport, storage, and measurement of low biomass (functionally sterile) samples. These impediments hinder the qualitative assessment of “what is habitable” on Earth such that the assessment of the border between habitable and uninhabitable is still unconstrained.

When expanding the search for life to environments outside Earth, we become more observationally limited. Within our Solar System, our best methods to investigate habitability are rovers (e.g., Mars Perseverance; Farley et al. 2020; Herd et al. 2025) that are designed to search for structural and chemical signs of ancient life. For more remote solar system targets, such as Europa, we can send spacecraft (e.g., Europa Clipper; Cangahuala et al. 2025) to remotely observe—and potentially—perform mass spectroscopy on geyser-like plumes ejected into space. These missions are designed to observe the physicochemical conditions of the environment to assess the potential for life (Pappalardo et al. 2024). These planetary missions may not definitively identify life, but may collect observations that i) suggest habitable conditions or ii) physical or chemical signatures consistent with life.

As we further widen our search for life outside our solar system, our observational capabilities become severely limited, such that we must assess the capability to sustain life on a global scale given little observational information and many thousands of exoplanet targets. The Habitable Zone (HZ) framework of Kasting et al. (1993) and Kopparapu et al. (2013) is a first-order assessment to identify exoplanets that are capable of sustaining surface liquid water (i.e., surface oceans): the most fundamental factor for life on Earth. The HZ framework identifies a range of instellation (i.e., stellar flux or semi-major axis) for a given star where the potential for surface oceans on a rocky planet are not precluded. In brief, the HZ framework calculates a range of orbital distances from a given star where surface temperatures are consistent with liquid water (more detail on the HZ framework in Sect. 2). The usefulness of the HZ framework is its reliance on a small set of currently observable variables: planet mass, planet radius, semi-major axis, and stellar luminosity.

The search for life as we know it is exclusive to “rocky” planets (i.e., terrestrial) that have a similar radius as Earth (roughly 0.5 to 2 Earth radii). A rocky planet is determined by density (via mass & radius); where rocky planets have density similar to Earth (∼5.5 g cm−3). Semi-major axis and stellar luminosity measurements are the primary determinants for the HZ framework and are used to constrain the surface temperature: characterizing the planet as “potentially habitable” or “uninhabitable.” Current observational methods are able to constrain these measurements to within several percent, lending credibility to claims of identifying an “Earth-like” planet in it’s star’s HZ (e.g., Neveu-VanMalle et al. 2016; Dittmann et al. 2017; Gillon et al. 2017; Sun et al. 2025, among others).

However, to date, every rocky exoplanet in the HZ of it’s star can only be characterized as “potentially habitable,” as atmospheric observations are required to assess the capability of a planet to host surface oceans. Despite massive advancements in the last decade, current state-of-the-art space telescopes (e.g., JWST) are still grappling to make definitive detections of the presence of i) an atmosphere (Lustig-Yaeger et al. 2023; May et al. 2023; Kreidberg and Stevenson 2025), ii) greenhouse gases (GHG, e.g., CO2, Greene et al. 2023), and iii) water vapor (Moran et al. 2023) for Earth-sized, rocky planets. All of which are key assumptions of the HZ framework and prerequisites for surface oceans and life (Kasting et al. 1993; Kopparapu et al. 2013, see Fig. 2 B-D). Considering our current observational limitations for rocky exoplanets, our best measurements of a “habitable zone planet” (e.g., TRAPPIST-1e; Glidden et al. 2025) cannot yet have assessed its ability to maintain surface oceans. The current inability to observe an atmosphere underscores that the HZ is a hypothesis that remains to be tested, despite many observational advancements since its inception.

A) Instellation (i.e., stellar flux or semi-major axis), B) atmosphere (i.e., volatile inventory), C) greenhouse gas inventory (i.e., CO2), D) elemental water, E) mantle convection, F) land (i.e., nutrients or CHNOPS), and G) magnetosphere

Not addressed in the HZ framework are any additional requirements (beyond water) thought to be necessary for life that may (or may not) be provided by the planet including, for example, i) nutrient and volatile cycling (i.e., mantle convection) and ii) a magnetosphere (Fig. 2). Cockell et al. (2023) categorizes these considerations as “sustained habitability” (i.e., where geophysics and biology meet), and are thought to be necessary to support life for geologically-relevant time scales. The extent to which these factors are necessary for life are the subject of debate in the astrobiology and habitability literature (e.g., Sagan 1965; Tarduno et al. 2014; Jellinek and Jackson 2015; Höning and Spohn 2016, 2023; Gillmann et al. 2024; Cawood et al. 2025). This is because it is difficult to extrapolate from the micro-scale factors for life (e.g., Cockell et al. 2016) to planetary-scale processes that provide and maintain these factors. Therefore, there remains debate as to the relative importance of mantle convection (Jellinek and Jackson 2015), land:ocean ratio (Höning and Spohn 2023; Guimond et al. 2026), and the magnetosphere (Varela et al. 2023, as examples) to life on any given planet.

These planetary properties, however, are not currently observable. NASA’s upcoming Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO) mission (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine 2021) may be able to further assess the additional requirements for habitability, such as land surface, but this mission is in it’s early stages. Therefore, assessing an exoplanet’s suitability for life (i.e., habitability) may remain out of reach for the next 10–20 years.

The narrow definition of “habitable” (i.e., the potential for surface oceans) implied by the HZ framework creates significant linguistic drawbacks stemming from the colloquial understanding of the word “habitable” (i.e., “capable of being lived in;” Merriam-Webster n.d.b, Sect. 3). The broad, colloquial understanding of “habitable” is in conflict with the narrow definition of “habitable” within the HZ framework. This confusion is evident when “habitable” is used as an adjective to describe an exoplanet. The terms “habitable planet” or “habitable world” are widely understood (in science and the general public) as a planetary body capable of sustaining life (e.g., Des Marais et al. 2002; Kasting and Catling 2003; Seager 2013, among many others). The HZ framework, however, presents opportunities for confusion; it is easy to see how a planet in the HZ could be misconstrued as a “habitable planet” or how a planet outside the HZ could be misconstrued as an “uninhabitable planet.”

This confusion manifests in three main ways (within both the expert and non-expert communities) when discussing the potential for life on exoplanets within the HZ framework: i) how a star’s HZ could contain an exoplanet known to be uninhabitable (e.g., gas giant), ii) why a “super-Earth” or an otherwise “Earth-like planet” in its star’s HZ may not have habitable conditions, and iii) how a planet outside the HZ may be habitable (e.g., an icy ocean world). Ultimately, there is a disconnect between the HZ and a habitable planet such that habitable planets are not strictly limited to the HZ.

Here, we introduce a new term “euhabitable” (the capability of an environment to sustain Earth-like life; see Sect. 3) that seeks to address these points of confusion by highlighting the stricter criteria (e.g., nutrients, magnetosphere, etc.) not addressed in the HZ framework as well as loosening the criteria from surface liquid water to any liquid water. In this way, a euhabitable planet (i.e., truly habitable) is not limited to the HZ.

The HZ framework, by design, creates a binary scenario where planets outside the HZ are unable to maintain surface liquid water and planets within can plausibly maintain surface liquid water (given certain assumptions, see Sect. 2.1.3). Within the HZ framework, HZ planets are defined solely by instellation (Fig. 2 A) and only have the potential for surface liquid water given sufficient atmospheric pressure and greenhouse gas concentration (Fig. 2 B-C). Therefore, a planet in the HZ (a HZ planet), is not by definition habitable, only “potentially habitable.” A euhabitable planet satisfies stricter criteria (Fig. 2 A-G), and as such is more specific to the potential for life.

The need for a new term is increasing, considering the paradigm-changing progress that has been made in both the theoretical and technological realm of exoplanet science since the establishment of the modern HZ by Kasting et al. (1993). This article seeks to outline the need for this new term considering the i) simplified definition of “habitable” within the HZ framework, and ii) additional requirements (beyond liquid water) for life.

The Modern Habitable Zone Framework

Liquid water is widely accepted as a prerequisite for life-as-we-know-it, which has informed NASA’s “follow the water” strategy in astrobiology. This strategy is manifest on Mars (Hubbard et al. 2002), Europa & Enceladus (Hand et al. 2009; Hendrix et al. 2019), and exoplanets (Seager 2013; Noack et al. 2017; Kopparapu et al. 2020). The physicochemical advantages of liquid water for life—in no particular order—include the i) specific heat capacity, ii) latent heat of fusion/vaporization, iii) dipole moment, iv) surface tension, and v) hydrophobic effect (e.g., Pohorille and Pratt 2012). These properties are unique to water compared to other solvents (i.e., volatiles) of similar mass. Extraterrestrial life may utilize an alternative to water as a solvent (e.g., Schulze-Makuch and Irwin 2018; Lingam and Balbi 2024), however, much of the astrobiology community works under the assumption that life requires water as a solvent, and therefore the search for life is focused on environments that support liquid water (Fig. 3).

This search for liquid water within the solar system has a lengthy history tracing back to Sir Isaac Newton in the 17th century (if not before), where Newton describes a Goldilocks-like scenario where if Earth had the semi-major axis of Saturn or Mercury, Earth’s oceans would freeze or boil away, respectively (Newton 1846). More recently, the principle of the “Habitable Zone” was coined and re-introduced to the general public through a 1959 American Scientist article by Su-Shu Huang titled “Occurrence of Life in the Universe” (1959). The framework was formalized into a computational, gray-gas box model by Hart (1978, 1979), which was further advanced into 1D radiative-convective models by Kasting et al. (1993), and later Kopparapu et al. (2013). The models of Kasting et al. (1993) and Kopparapu et al. (2013) make up our modern understanding of the HZ framework. These models calculate surface temperatures considering an atmosphere with a CO2-H2O greenhouse effect as well as the role of albedo and time-varying stellar luminosity. The inner limit of the HZ is determined by the moist greenhouse limit, or the minimum instellation necessary to maintain high stratospheric water vapor concentrations, leading to atmospheric desiccation and a runaway greenhouse state. The outer limit of the HZ is determined by the maximum greenhouse limit, where increased CO2 partial pressure no longer warms the surface via IR absorption, but cools the planet via Rayleigh scattering. This framework provides a simple and effective tool to assess the capability for a planet to maintain surface oceans around a given star at a given semi-major axis. This advancement in understanding proved quite timely considering the coincident first discoveries of exoplanets during this period of time (Wolszczan and Frail 1992; Mayor and Queloz 1995).

In the 21st century, the HZ framework has been expanded to include considerations of alternative greenhouse gases, tidal locking, and ocean circulation (summarized by Ramirez 2018), or by narrowing the constraints to the limits of complex life (as opposed to solely single-celled bacteria; Schwieterman et al. 2019). We refer the reader to Lingam (2021) for a detailed overview of the history of the habitable zone, and its many alterations over time.

Benefits of the Habitable Zone Framework

The HZ framework has myriad benefits in the search for life on exoplanets. The HZ’s focus on a critically important factor (i.e., water) located on a planet’s surface provides a constrainable and testable framework that has proved highly useful over the last three-plus decades of exoplanet science.

Useful Despite Limited Information

This framework is an efficient tool to assess the potential for surface oceans on an exoplanet given limited observational information (i.e., stellar effective temperature and semi-major axis). The range of semi-major axes that make up the HZ are dependent on several assumptions, described in detail in Sect. 2.1.3. The stellar effective temperature is relatively simple to measure for all stars and therefore the HZ is easily calculable for every known star.

Seeks the Most Earth-Like Planet

Earth is the only planet known to host life, and the HZ framework is designed to constrain the semi-major axes where an Earth-like planet could maintain surface oceans. This approach is necessary considering the impossibility of extrapolating the search for life from a sample size of one. The HZ framework, therefore, seeks to maximize the probability of identifying an Earth-like biosphere by searching for an Earth-like planet: with surface oceans.

Simplifying Assumptions Based on Solar System Analogs

The HZ framework is based on several key assumptions that are informed by our solar system (i.e., Venus & Earth). First, the assumption of a secondary atmosphere (i.e., a CO2-H2O-N2 atmosphere). Primary atmospheres (i.e., H2- & He-dominated) are not considered since there are no rocky planet analogs in our solar system. An atmosphere provides the i) pressure and ii) greenhouse gas radiative forcing necessary to thermodynamically stabilize liquid water. Liquid water is unstable below 6 mbar at all temperatures, so a minimum surface pressure of 6 mbar is assumed in the HZ framework. Greenhouse gases, and their associated radiative effects, are integral to the HZ framework as water itself is a greenhouse gas. Given a surface water inventory, the atmosphere will contain at least some amount of greenhouse forcing from water vapor alone. When instellation becomes too high, all water evaporates into the gas phase and the climate enters a runaway greenhouse state, analogous to Venus, precluding liquid water and life.

In addition to water, the HZ framework also considers CO2 as a greenhouse gas. On Earth, CO2 concentrations—and resulting surface temperatures—are regulated via the negative feedback loop of the carbonate-silicate cycle (Walker et al. 1981). This negative feedback loop is likened to a climatological thermostat that has served to maintain clement surface conditions over geologic time scales (Ramirez 2018, for an in-depth review). The assumption that a given Earth-like exoplanet has an analogous climate feedback loop driven by silicate weathering to maintain surface liquid water may not be realistic, however, the presence of a CO2 stabilized climate—again—assumes the most Earth-like planet. Ultimately, the atmospheric assumptions are well understood and solidly based on solar system analogs and experiments.

Exoplanets Without Solar System Analogs

More recent exoplanet simulations have begun to assess the habitability of planets without solar system analogs due to their prevalence in exoplanet surveys. These planets provide tantalizing opportunities to envision life on wholly un-Earth-like worlds. Despite the excitement for these targets, the importance of solar system analogs to exoplanet science cannot be overstated. The direct observations of geophysical processes provide a foundation of ground-truth information with which to apply to exoplanets (Cockell et al. 2023). The two most common archetypes without a solar system analog are super-Earths and sub-Neptunes. These classifications are determined by mass (1–10 M⊕) and radius (1–2 R⊕; Haghighipour and Kaltenegger 2014; Haghighipour 2022). The distinction between these two archetypes is bulk density; where super-Earths have densities closer to Earth (5.5 g cm−3) and sub-Neptunes have notably lower density, consistent with large volatile inventories (Lichtenberg and Miguel 2025). There are large compositional uncertainties within these categories considering the unconstrained core, mantle, and volatile fractions (Daspute et al. 2025, see their Fig. 1). The uncertainties in bulk composition lead to uncertainties in interior structure and thermal evolution (e.g., Sahu et al. 2025), volatile outgassing (Elkins-Tanton and Seager 2008) and, ultimately, to habitability. Both super-Earths and sub-Neptune planets have subset archetypes with H2-He dominated atmospheres, which are also not represented in our solar system. Larger mass (but not gas giant) planets with solid rock or ocean surfaces may be able to retain primordial H2 and He atmospheres (Pierrehumbert and Gaidos 2011; Lammer et al. 2013). These planets are of particular interest as H2 can act as a greenhouse gas due to collision-induced absorption, dramatically increasing the range of semi-major axes that can support surface liquid water (Pierrehumbert and Gaidos 2011; Madhusudhan et al. 2021). These types of planets cannot be ignored when assessing the habitability of exoplanets. However, the lack of observational context for geophysical and climate modeling requires many assumptions and results in a wide range of plausible outcomes when assessing habitability.

The Habitable Zone Framework Is Testable

Perhaps the most beneficial aspect of the HZ framework is its strong connection to observables. The potential for a given exoplanet to maintain liquid water is assessed using climate (i.e., atmospheric) models where the primary factors are i) incident stellar flux, ii) an atmosphere, iii) a GHG (e.g., CO2), and iv) water vapor. These factors all can be theoretically observed via remote sensing spectroscopy using JWST. The HZ framework of Kasting et al. (1993) was not designed explicitly for observability, however, advances in technology over the past 30 years has enabled testing of the HZ framework for specific exoplanet targets. In other words, the accessibility of observational evidence has been implicitly folded into the HZ framework itself such that the HZ could be termed the “observationally habitable zone.”

Additionally, if the surface of a planet has liquid water, then it is plausible that any biosphere would be photosynthetic, as the surface would have the confluence of liquid water and energy as light (i.e., the euphotic zone). Given the example of Earth’s photosynthetic biosphere, an analog biosphere on a HZ exoplanet could be observable via both atmospheric constituents (e.g., ozone & O2, Schwieterman et al. 2018; Chou et al. 2024, and references therein) and surface features (i.e., the “red edge,” Borges et al. 2024, and references therein). Earth’s photosynthetic biosphere represents a large fraction of all life on Earth, and produces the overwhelming majority of biosignatures on the modern Earth. Therefore, the HZ framework’s caveat of surface liquid water works to identify the most promising targets for identification of Earth-like biosignatures.

Downsides of the Habitable Zone Framework

The HZ framework has been the cornerstone of exoplanet astrobiology for over 30 years for good reason considering the arguments in Sect. 2.1. However, the HZ—like all scientific frameworks—has limitations and drawbacks that can lead to misinterpretation and misunderstandings in the community. In addition, the field of astrobiology has matured over the last 30 years leading to advancements in our understanding of life and habitability as well as our observational capabilities.

The modern HZ framework is solely concerned with the potential for liquid water to exist on the surface of a rocky world. The 2015 NASA astrobiology strategy describes it as a “follow the water” approach:

“Habitability has traditionally been defined with a “follow the water” approach, with searches for evidence of water inside the Solar System, and exoplanet habitability defined by the potential to harbor surface water (Hays 2015).”

While seemingly focused on life, the HZ is one step removed from assessing the potential for a planet to host life. The HZ may include the environments that are most likely to host liquid water, however, there are many scenarios where location relative to the HZ does not predict potential for life. First, we detail the two major qualifying statements (above, in italics). Second, water is required for all life as we know it, however, water is only one of several factors necessary for life. The following section details the assumptions of the HZ, and highlights scenarios where the utility of the HZ framework breaks down in the search for life on exoplanets. Kasting et al. (1993) define habitable as:

“We ourselves are more interested in determining if life can evolve on other planets than we are in colonizing them, so we will use the presence of liquid water as our habitability criterion.”

This definition describes their approach as focused on “the presence of liquid water,” however, their simplifying assumptions (existence of water & surficially located water) are not mentioned. Kopparapu et al. (2013) included these assumptions explicitly in their reassessment of the HZ framework, which is widely used as the modern definition of the habitable zone:

“[...] the circumstellar region in which a terrestrial-mass planet with a CO2-H2O-N2 atmosphere can sustain liquid water on its surface.”

The HZ may, in fact, be the most likely place to observe habitable conditions due to the caveat of surficially located water. While prudent, this caveat risks misleading scientists on the actual occurrence of liquid water on exoplanets rather than the perceived occurrence through the lens of observational bias. There are several illustrative examples of the disconnects between the HZ and habitability in the solar system: Mars (Carr and Head 2019), Europa (Bayer et al. 2015), and Enceladus (McKay et al. 2014, Fig. 4).

Comparison of Mars and the icy ocean worlds (Europa & Enceladus) within the HZ framework. Mars is in the HZ, albeit on the outer edge (i: 1.52 AU, Kopparapu et al. 2013), but does not have surface liquid water (ii: Carr and Head 2019). Mars, however, may have substantial amounts of subsurface liquid water (iii: Orosei et al. 2018). Europa & Enceladus are well outside the HZ (iv: ≥5.2 AU), and therefore do not have surface liquid water. However, there is evidence that both have vast subsurface oceans due to tidal heating (v: Nimmo and Pappalardo 2016; Matsuyama et al. 2018). All three worlds do not have surface liquid water (the primary criterion of the HZ framework), but all are targets of astrobiological interest considering the presence of the life-essential factors (i.e., potentially euhabitable): liquid water, energy, and nutrients (vi: Waite et al. 2006, 2017; Martinez-Frias et al. 2006)

The HZ framework is a scientifically robust framework to understand the potential range of semi-major axes around a star that could host an Earth-like planet. However, linguistically, it is easy to see why one would mistakenly label a planet in it’s star’s HZ as “habitable.” Confusion between the HZ framework and habitability are centered around three disconnects: i) liquid water is possible outside the HZ (i.e., false negative), ii) planets in the HZ may not have appreciable water (i.e., false positive), and iii) life as we know it requires more than just liquid water (i.e., false positive). Here we use false negative or false positive to mean the connection between the HZ and the potential for life.

False Negatives

Liquid Water Is Possible Outside the Habitable Zone

The second qualifying term for liquid water in the habitable zone—surface—is highly illustrative of a large point of confusion within astrobiology. Surface liquid water is only thermodynamically stable (given atmospheric assumptions) within the habitable zone. However, liquid water in the subsurface can be stable in many environments outside the HZ, which has stirred much interest in the astrobiology community.

The most fascinating examples of liquid water outside the HZ are perhaps the moons of Jupiter and Saturn: Europa and Enceladus, respectively. Both these bodies, known as icy worlds or ocean worlds, are expected to have substantial global liquid water oceans (Manga and Wang 2007), a solid silicate core (Kuskov and Kronrod 2005; Rappaport et al. 2007), and an extended ice shell tens of km thick (Beuthe et al. 2016; Howell 2021). The discovery of liquid water oceans on these worlds sparked a large push to investigate their potential to host life (Parkinson et al. 2008; Bayer et al. 2015, among others). Both Europa and Enceladus are outside even the most optimistic estimates of the HZ, and only host subsurface liquid water. This liquid water (likely caused by tidal heating) is obviously intriguing for astrobiological investigations. Within the solar system, it is relatively easy to identify subsurface liquid water—and therefore the potential for life—on Europa and Enceladus. However, outside the solar system, a Europa-like exoplanet (i.e., outside the HZ, water envelope, silicate core, with internal heating) would be categorized as an uninhabitable planet. The observability of any life on such a planet may be vanishingly low, but the potential for life on this type of planet should not be ignored. This has led many researchers to investigate the potential for subsurface liquid water outside the habitable zone (e.g., Leconte et al. 2013; Ojha et al. 2022; Wandel 2023).

False Positives

Planets in the Habitable Zone May Not Have Necessary Atmospheric Conditions

Considering only semi-major axis, modern Mars (1.52 AU) is within the canonical HZ (0.99-1.70 AU; Kopparapu et al. 2013). However, extensive observations of Mars’ atmosphere and surface have all but ruled out the potential (or existence) for surface liquid water (Carr and Head 2019). The average surface pressure is near 6 mbar, meaning that pure liquid water is unstable at all temperatures. Despite this, there is evidence that Mars hosts a substantial amount of subsurface liquid water (Orosei et al. 2018; Lauro et al. 2021; Smith et al. 2021; Orosei et al. 2025).

The growing evidence that ancient Mars possessed standing bodies of surface liquid water (Craddock and Howard 2002; Luo et al. 2017; Ramirez and Craddock 2018; Ramirez et al. 2020), while present Mars does not, further exemplifies our false positive point. In general, a planet may lose (or gain) surface water stability over time further highlighting the fact that planets in the HZ may not meet these requirements ever, or for all epochs in their evolutionary history.

Planets in the Habitable Zone May Not Have Appreciable Elemental Water

The presence of elemental water (i.e., H2O, OH−, or H+) within the habitable zone is highly uncertain and is by no means a surety. In fact, the source of Earth’s water has been hotly debated for decades, and many planetary formation models predict extraordinarily desiccated planets that form in the HZ (O’Brien et al. 2018). Consensus views for Earth’s water inventory require specific events during planetary formation including Jupiter migration (i.e., grand tack; Walsh et al. 2011; O’Brien et al. 2014) that delivered water-rich chondritic material from beyond the snow line (Morbidelli et al. 2000; Alexander et al. 2012). Without this water delivery method the Earth would have a low abundance of elemental water, as little water is accreted in situ during the solar nebula in the current HZ (Wu et al. 2018). Water delivery during planetary formation may play a large role in determining the actual (as opposed to potential) water inventory of an exoplanet.

The vast majority (∼90%) of Earth’s water inventory (∼3–8 oceans, where 1ocean=1.37×1021 kg) is in the mantle, and was fractionated there early in Earth’s formation (Lécuyer et al. 1998; Wu et al. 2018). This water is not liquid, but the reduced form of water: hydrogen (H+ and OH−). Planetary formation models are unable to explain the mechanisms responsible for Earth’s mantle-surface water distribution and therefore more work is needed to understand the sources and sinks of surface water. For more details on planetary formation and water inventory, see Guimond et al. (2026, this topical collection).

Additionally, HZ planets in M-dwarf systems may have extremely desiccated surfaces due to the extended pre-main sequence phase of these stars (Luger and Barnes 2015). Young (pre-main sequence) M-dwarf stars emit high levels of Extreme UV (EUV) radiation that may strip HZ planets of their atmospheres, including any water (Lissauer 2007; Luger and Barnes 2015). Similarly, HZ planets may lose surface water due to hydrogen loss via the runaway greenhouse mechanism as is hypothesized for early Venus (Kane et al. 2019, and references therein).

Therefore, it is plausible (and perhaps likely) that an exoplanet may reside within the HZ of it’s star—capable of maintaining surface liquid water—but i) the planet formed with no water, ii) the entire water budget is trapped in the mantle, or iii) all surface water was lost early in its history. In all cases the surface of the planet would be desiccated and not conducive for life, despite residing in its star’s HZ.

Additional Factors Beyond Water: Life as We Know It Requires More than Liquid Water

Water is essential for life as we know it, but the extent, productivity, mass, and origin of a biosphere depends also on the availability of nutrients, energy, and other favorable planetary and physicochemical conditions (Fig. 5; Lineweaver and Chopra 2012; Cockell 2014; Lingam and Loeb 2021; Styczinski et al. 2024). The major essential elements (i.e., nutrients) for life are known as CHNOPS (carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and sulfur). Of these, phosphorus is widely agreed to be the limiting nutrient (especially in marine settings; Dohm and Maruyama 2015; Glaser et al. 2020; Scherf et al. 2024), which on Earth is mostly provided by the subaerial weathering of continental rock (Filippelli 2008; Sharoni and Halevy 2023; Zhao et al. 2024). Energy is utilized by life on Earth in both light and chemical forms. Phototrophs (e.g., plants & phytoplankton) use light as an energy source, while chemotrophs (e.g., methanogens, sulfur oxidizers, nitrogen fixers, etc.) derive energy from redox gradients within the environment.

Not all environments on a planet provide the same water, nutrient, or energy resources. For example, deep oceans may be rich in water and nutrients, but lack sufficient energy (e.g., Maruyama et al. 2013; Olson et al. 2020). Similarly, large land areas may be arid deserts with little water, but abundant in light and nutrients. The geological activity of a planet is also important; volcanic activity provides both nutrients and free energy from which organisms can benefit (e.g., Wogan and Catling 2020). A planet in the HZ, by definition, has the potential for surface liquid water, but may not provide all the other necessary elements for life described below.

Nutrients: CHNOPS

Earth’s biosphere is primarily limited by two nutrients: nitrogen and phosphorus. Here we focus on phosphorus, as nitrogen is overwhelmingly sourced by the atmosphere; despite evidence that N-rich substrates provide bioavailable nitrogen to organisms (Morford et al. 2011). On Earth, the weathering of land surfaces is the main source of phosphorus for the marine biosphere (Filippelli 2008; Glaser et al. 2020; Lingam and Loeb 2021). Phosphorus is also provided by submarine weathering of the oceanic crust, but this process is much less efficient than subaerial weathering (Glaser et al. 2020; Olson et al. 2020; Syverson et al. 2021; Sharoni and Halevy 2023), since i) sea water (pH ∼ 8.2) is more basic than rainwater (pH ∼ 5.6) which decreases chemical weathering (Liljestrand 1985) and ii) seafloor ocean current velocity is much lower than riverine velocity (limiting physical weathering; Glaser et al. 2020). This has led researchers to contend that some mixture of land (i.e., subaerial rocks) and surface liquid water may be necessary for life (Glaser et al. 2020; Krissansen-Totton et al. 2021a). Additionally, planets with global surface oceans may accumulate abiotic O2 with no geologic sinks, resulting in a false positive biosignature (Wordsworth and Pierrehumbert 2014; Kleinböhl et al. 2018; Krissansen-Totton et al. 2021b). Ultimately, there are several reasons to be skeptical about the prospects for life, or detecting life, on planets with global surface oceans.

About 70% of the Earth’s surface is covered by oceans. This is unusual in the Solar System, where most planets and moons are either dominated by land surfaces (the terrestrial planets, Luna, Titan, and Io) or covered by deep oceans or thick layers of ice (most of the outer Solar System moons). It is contended that the simultaneous presence of land and oceans is vital for the emergence, evolution, and sustainability of life (e.g. Dohm and Maruyama 2015; Glaser et al. 2020; Guimond et al. 2026, this topical collection; Spohn et al. 2026, this topical collection). However, planets with land and oceans as well as a substantial carbon and nitrogen bearing atmosphere may be rare among HZ planets (Maruyama et al. 2013).

Many attempts have been made to investigate Earth’s land:ocean ratio. Bayesian statistical analyses suggest that rocky planets are most likely to form with global oceans (Simpson 2017). These analyses, however, contend that accounting for feedback mechanisms between continental growth and water cycling tend to favor global continents (i.e., land planets). Planetary formation methods (e.g., Morbidelli et al. 2000; O’Brien et al. 2018) suggest that terrestrial, HZ planets tend to be either completely ocean covered or completely land covered. Höning and Spohn (2016, 2023) use a different, geological approach using continental feedback mechanisms and similarly find that rocky planets tend to either maximize the land surface area or maximize the ocean floor area. A balanced distribution like that on Earth (i.e., neither all land or all ocean) is rather unlikely. Thus, Earth’s land-to-ocean ratio may be quite rare. Investigation of land-to-ocean ratios on rocky, HZ exoplanets is an active area of research (Lammer et al. 2024; Scherf et al. 2024; Guimond et al. 2026, this topical collection).

Energy

The vast majority of the quantified energy in Earth’s biosphere comes from the sun as light. Photosynthetic lifeforms (i.e., photoautotrophs) reduce inorganic carbon (CO2) to organic carbon using sunlight and water. Estimates of global photosynthetic net primary productivity (i.e., biomass production) are ∼105 Pg of carbon per year with roughly equal production from land and ocean biospheres (Field et al. 1998). For reference, this is enough energy (∼7×1020 J) to power New York City for 320 years. Heterotrophs perform the reverse reaction (oxidize organic carbon) to gain metabolic energy. Heterotrophic lifeforms do not directly require light but are indirectly dependent on light since most organic carbon is sourced via photosynthesis. However, there are autotrophic metabolisms that do not require light: chemoautotrophs. These autotrophs—the primary producers for the “deep” biosphere—gain energy from lithogenic redox gradients (e.g., H2 and SO42−) and are completely separated from the influence of the surface (i.e., O2- and organic carbon-poor; Colman et al. 2017; Escudero et al. 2018). Estimates of the net primary productivity of the deep biosphere vary wildly due to the inaccessible nature of the environment (Kallmeyer et al. 2012; McMahon and Parnell 2014; Escudero et al. 2018) and the relatively slow growth rates (e.g., Trembath-Reichert et al. 2017). However, considering the large volume of suitable crust (as deep as 5–10 km below surface), the “deep” biosphere’s productivity may be substantial (Escudero et al. 2018). For more detail on the deep biosphere, see the review by Templeton and Caro (2023).

Mantle Convection: Tectonics

Plate tectonics is important for the Earth’s biosphere to buffer climate via the carbonate-silicate cycle (see Sect. 2.1.3; Walker et al. 1981; Berner 1993; Catling and Kasting 2017) and to provide water, nutrients, and chemical energy (Lingam and Loeb 2021; Méndez et al. 2021). In brief, tectonics moves energy and materials between the surface and the mantle: refreshing the ingredients necessary for life. The materials of particular note are water, CO2, and phosphorus. Tectonics maintains a pseudo-steady state reservoir of these three materials over geologic timescales: providing the resources necessary to sustain life. Further details of the history, mechanisms, and consequences to biology of plate tectonics are largely outside the scope of this paper, so we refer the reader to Lourenço et al. (2026, this topical collection), Guimond et al. (2026, this topical collection), Spohn et al. (2026, this topical collection), as well as Cawood et al. (2022) and Gillmann et al. (2024).

Magnetosphere

It is often argued that an intrinsic magnetosphere (i.e., magnetic field) is necessary to protect a planet’s i) atmosphere from erosion and ii) biosphere from harmful radiation (e.g., Lammer et al. 2009; Tarduno et al. 2014; Kubyshkina et al. 2026, this topical collection). An intrinsic planetary magnetosphere is generated by an active dynamo in an electrically conducting shell in the planet’s interior (in the case of a terrestrial planet, in the metallic core; see Lourenço et al. 2026, this topical collection). Examples in the solar system that satisfy this criterion are Earth, Mercury, and Ganymede. Remnant magnetization in the crust of Mars (Acuña et al. 1998) and Luna (Strangway et al. 1971) suggests that they may have had intrinsic fields in their early history that were lost over time (e.g., Connerney 2015, for a thorough review). An intrinsic (as opposed to induced; e.g., Mars) magnetic field can be taken as an indication of efficient planetary interior heat transport, possibly by plate tectonics or a similar active tectonic mode (see Lourenço et al. 2026, this topical collection for more detail).

The assertion that an intrinsic magnetosphere is necessary to shield a planet’s atmosphere has been challenged by spacecraft observations of atmospheric loss rates on Venus, Earth, and Mars; of these, only Earth has an intrinsic magnetosphere. The atmospheric loss rates for Venus, Earth, and Mars have been found to be similar, despite their different magnetic field properties (Ramstad and Barabash 2021). These observations have been supported by model calculations (e.g., Gunell et al. 2018), however, the effects of chemical composition and surface pressure of the atmosphere as well as stellar types on escape rates require further exploration.

Additionally, the role of a magnetosphere in protecting the biosphere on the surface of a planet from harmful radiation has also been questioned (see Lingam 2019), and the importance of a thick atmosphere has been emphasized (Grießmeier et al. 2016). Ultimately, there are still many unanswered questions about the role of magnetism in planetary climate as well as the origin and sustainability of life.

Euhabitability

Here we have identified a need for a term to differentiate between an exoplanet in the HZ (i.e., potential for surface liquid water) and an exoplanet that has the potential for life. To address this need, we propose a new term that is specific to the capability of an environment to sustain life, yet divorced from the HZ framework: euhabitable.

Euhabitable: an environmental state which is capable of sustaining some form of Earth-like life.

This term contains the primary, root word “habitable” which has a plethora of meaning, but most generally used as:

Habitable: capable of being lived in; suitable for habitation (Merriam-Webster n.d.b).

Most in the general public, and many within the scientific community, understand and use habitable within this context (e.g., Lineweaver and Chopra 2012; Seager 2013; Cockell 2014; Dohm and Maruyama 2015; Cockell et al. 2016; Styczinski et al. 2024, among many others). Importantly, our new term, “euhabitability” contains a prefix which suggests a similar, yet more strict, meaning from habitable. We chose the Greek prefix “eu” to imply that this term is taking a holistic view of habitability, assessed with regards to life.

eu- (Greek prefix): true, good (Merriam-Webster n.d.a)

The definition of euhabitable (literal definition: truly habitable), therefore, is similar to the common, or colloquial, understanding of habitable. Within certain astrobiological contexts (e.g., on Earth or in the solar system) habitable and euhabitable have the same meaning. However, we recommend the use of “euhabitable” in any exoplanetary contexts, to avoid confusion stemming from the HZ framework considering the explicit focus on nutrients (CHNOPS), a magnetosphere, and other geophysical considerations in the euhabitable framework.

Habitable Zone: The circumstellar distance from a star in which a planet with a CO2-H2O-N2 atmosphere receives sufficient instellation to maintain surface liquid water (Kasting et al. 1993; Kopparapu et al. 2013).

The HZ, by its own design, can only assess the potential for surface liquid water. Therefore, the definition of habitable, within the HZ framework (see Sect. 2), is much less strict with no explicit regard to life.

Habitable (narrow definition within the HZ framework): a planetary surface capable of maintaining surface liquid water.

Importantly, the habitable zone is only assessed with regards to instellation, and not the actual presence of water (see Sect. 2.2.2). Strictly speaking, the HZ may be better termed the “potentially habitable zone,” however, we concede that this term is cumbersome. This idea has also been termed the “Goldilocks Zone,” which conveys the idea of “not too hot, not too cold.” The Goldilocks terminology may be a more apt comparison as it implies a “sweet spot” with regards to temperature, which is several steps removed from the “sweet spot” for life. Use of “Goldilocks Zone” may cause less confusion by removing the implication of life (habitability) from the term and implying a focus on temperature.

This simple addition of a prefix, we hope, will serve to add additional information to distinguish the search for suitable conditions for life from the search for surface liquid water. Addition of the term “euhabitable” to the astrobiological lexicon, therefore, allows for a broader—and more nuanced—search for life.

Benefits & Drawbacks of a Euhabitability Framework

Benefits

Life-Centric Approach

The euhabitability approach is—as much as possible—a holistic approach to the search for life. Euhabitability expands the search for life beyond the constraints of the HZ (potential surface liquid water) to include: all liquid water, nutrients, and other geophysical considerations like a magnetosphere. The 2015 NASA astrobiology strategy identified the need for an expansion to the HZ framework:

“[...] water may be a necessary but not sufficient condition for life; therefore, searches for habitable environments must go beyond a “follow the water” approach to examine a multi-parameter space governed by the interaction of components of the planet’s environment, as well as interaction with the parent star and other elements of the planetary system. Better defining these other conditions that support life will improve our understanding of local-to-global habitability, and through that improve our prioritization of future exoplanet observations and planetary missions”

The addition of “euhabitable” into the astrobiology lexicon will provide a term for this “beyond the water” approach to exoplanet habitability. We argue that this will help researchers, and the general public, distinguish between a planet that “can potentially host surface liquid water” and a planet that “can potentially host life.” This is likely to become increasingly important considering upcoming missions relevant to exoplanets (RST, ARIEL, PLATO, HWO, LIFE, etc.). The explicit connection to life seeks to simplify discussions and reduce ambiguity.

Clearly Defined Scenarios: Assessing False Positive & False Negatives

The euhabitability framework clearly defines scenarios where a planet is in the HZ, but where life is implausible (an uninhabitable planet in the HZ; see Sect. 2.2.2). Alternatively, the euhabitability framework provides similar definitions for planets outside the HZ, but where life is plausible (a euhabitable planet outside the HZ; see Sect. 2.2.1). Naming and classifying these scenarios will further reduce ambiguity and allow for further advancements in the search for life on exoplanets.

Moves Exoplanet Science Closer to the Colloquial Understanding of Habitability

Most people, especially outside the exoplanet science community, understand “habitable” in the colloquial sense (Sect. 3). The exoplanet community is still unequipped to assess exoplanets observationally with regards to habitability, and therefore have had to rely on the HZ framework: a proxy for where life may be found. The term “euhabitable” removes that ambiguity and works to prevent miscommunication; especially outside the exoplanet community. Ultimately, we hope to provide the language and perspective necessary to discuss the potential for life, regardless of location with respect to the HZ. This distinction may avert misunderstandings of future breakthroughs in exoplanet science as well as enable nuanced exoplanet classifications independent from the HZ framework.

Drawbacks

Limited Adoption of New Term

Addition and adoption of a new term into the lexicon can be a protracted, uphill process. Therefore, the most obvious drawback is the same drawback that any new term would face: slow or limited adoption. Collaborative science is made easier with a shared lexicon, and limited adoption would serve to fracture the exoplanet community’s lexicon.

Additional Factors for Life Are Currently Unobservable

Despite many hundreds of observing hours, astronomers have been unable to uniquely constrain key factors assumed in the HZ framework: atmosphere, CO2, and water (Greene et al. 2023; Lustig-Yaeger et al. 2023; May et al. 2023; Moran et al. 2023; Kreidberg and Stevenson 2025; Guimond et al. 2026, this topical collection; Spohn et al. 2026, this topical collection). However, the state-of-the-art JWST observatory is—in principle—capable of making these measurements.

We currently do not have the capability to observe the additional factors for life (e.g., land; Sect. 2.2.2) on an exoplanet. The HWO space telescope concept is centered around the ability to directly image exoplanets and spectrally discern oceans from land, but the telescope is still in the early stages of development and likely 15 years or more from first light. Therefore, we are currently unable to identify a euhabitable planet observationally.

However, it may prove to be easier (and more likely) to detect biosignatures than the signs of a habitable or euhabitable environment. If life in the universe is common and originates early in planetary history (as on Earth) then it may ultimately be more probable to detect a biosignature (ozone, O2, CH4, N2O, etc.) than a sign of habitability (water vapor, CO2, ocean glint, etc.) or euhabitability (e.g., land). In this scenario, the evidence of life (effect) is observed and euhabitability (cause) would be inferred. If life in the universe is rare, then detecting biosignatures will be rare, and therefore detecting the causal environment (habitable & euhabitable) will be more probable than measuring a biosignature. Within the HZ framework, a positive detection of surface liquid water and a non-detection of biosignatures could be interpreted as evidence that life in the universe is rare. This same scenario in the euhabitable framework could also be interpreted as evidence that the additional factors beyond water (Sect. 2.2.2) are insufficient to originate or sustain life. Ultimately, making observations is a difficult task, but the euhabitability framework (while still unobservable) will work to inform future observations as well as analog and theoretical research.

Summary

The search for life on Earth takes place at many different scales and can be more aptly characterized as the search for previously undiscovered environments that are currently inhabited by life. This mainly comprises exploring extreme environments or difficult to reach environments seeking evidence of microbial activity (micron-scale). The search for life on exoplanets is much more difficult as it takes place on different scales: both by size (global) and distance (lightyears). Considering these massive discrepancies in scale, it is necessary to approach the search for life in distinctly different ways. Kasting et al. (1993) created a useful tool, the HZ framework, as a method to assess if an exoplanet could possibly host Earth-like life, given our best understanding of planetary climate. The scientific benefits of the HZ framework are myriad, however, the term “the Habitable Zone” has itself created confusion stemming from the differences in the colloquial understanding of “habitable” versus the narrow, specific definition of “habitable” withing the HZ framework.

The HZ exoplanets identified by the HZ framework may be the most probable targets for the detection of life, considering their similarities to our only example of an inhabited planet: Earth. However, the HZ framework assesses only the potential for surface liquid water, which—at best—is one step removed from life itself. The two qualifying words above, in italics, highlight the main points of confusion within the HZ framework that lead to false positive and false negative scenarios with regard to the HZ framework’s ability to assess the potential for life. In other words, HZ planets can be uninhabitable and planets outside the HZ may be habitable; creating ambiguity for the definition of “habitable.”

Our new term, euhabitability, is focused on a wider set of factors necessary for life (in addition to water), as well as considering all liquid water (not just surface water). In practice, euhabitability assesses the potential for liquid water, energy, nutrients, and other physicochemical factors (e.g., magnetosphere) on a planet, regardless of its location relative to the HZ. This new term works to shift from a water-centric viewpoint to a life-centric viewpoint. Water is necessary for all life on Earth, and therefore is a key element in the search for life elsewhere. However, astrobiology has matured over the past three decades; and the search for life elsewhere has moved on from “follow the water” to “beyond the water.” Use of the term “euhabitable” implies both a broader spatial search area, and a stricter set of physical criteria to assess the capability of an environment, or entire planet, to sustain life. In this way we hope to both expand and focus the search for life on exoplanets by including potentially euhabitable planets outside the HZ and excluding potentially uninhabitable planets in the HZ. A euhabitable planet contains liquid water (agnostic of the planets location relative to the HZ and agnostic to the location of the liquid water on the planet), and also contains the other life-essential factors for Earth-like life (e.g., CHONPS, energy, and appropriate physicochemical conditions; see Fig. 5).

Ultimately, we hope that use of this new term will improve interdisciplinary communication within (and beyond) the exoplanet science community. The narrow, specific definition of “habitable” within the HZ framework is still the gold standard in the search for life on exoplanets. Most astrobiologists and planetary scientists understand the nuances, assumptions, and limitations to the definition of “habitable” within this context. However, exoplanet science is increasingly reliant on large, diverse teams including many disciplines (e.g., Astronomy, Geology, Atmospheric Science, Chemistry, Biology, etc.) to address the complex outstanding questions in the search for life. Scientific progress can be hindered in these teams if there are multiple different understandings of “habitable,” or what constitutes a “habitable planet.” Specific and precise use of language is paramount. Similarly, particular care is needed when communicating to the public, as many non-experts also conceive of “habitable” in the colloquial way: “capable of sustaining life” or even “capable of sustaining human life.” The nuance between “could sustain surface liquid water” and “could sustain life” can be lost in translation between researchers, journalists, science communicators, and the target audience. The addition of euhabitable to the lexicon helps to advance the search for life by highlighting the fact that there are distinct differences between habitable and euhabitable (truly habitable) in exoplanet science stemming from limited observational information. The euhabitability framework will also, we hope, allow for more nuance than the HZ framework can provide by shifting to a life-centric approach, as opposed to a water-centric approach. In this way, the euhabitability framework explicitly recognizes the possibility for life outside the HZ. The use of “euhabitability” can serve to make explicit many underlying assumptions and bring to light the common misunderstandings within the astrobiology community in the search for life on exoplanets.
