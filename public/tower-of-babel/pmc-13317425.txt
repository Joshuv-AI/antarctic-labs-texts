Peripheral blood transcriptomic analysis of patients with rheumatoid arthritis associated interstitial lung disease reveals: distinct molecular signature with evidence of humoral and myeloid immunity
Authors: Sofia Flouda, Maria Grigoriou, George Sentis, Alexandros Grivas, Nikos Malissovas, Aggelos Banos, Konstantinos Thomas, Noemin Kapsala, Anastasia Filia, Dionysis Nikolopoulos, Theofanis Karageorgas, Vasilios Tzilas, Antonis Fanouriakis, Dimitrios T. Boumpas

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterstitial lung disease (ILD) is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in rheumatoid arthritis (RA). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of peripheral blood may provide an unbiased look at pathogenetic events involved.MethodsPeripheral blood was collected from RA-ILD (n = 11), RA non-ILD (n = 10), psoriatic arthritis (PsA, n = 23)patients and healthy controls (HC, n = 7) for transcriptomic analysis. All patients in the RA-ILD group were under treatment during sampling, GC and cDMARDs. The gene expression profile of RA ILD was inferred through differential gene expression analysis followed by pathway and enrichment analysis. Further transcriptomic analysis was conducted based on radiological pattern, clinical progression through Modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) Dyspnea scale, and radiological progression. Drug repurposing analysis was performed to identify perturbagens that counteract RA-ILD-specific signatures. To identify shared pathways between SSc and RA we used publicly available RNA-seq data of patients with systemic sclerosis-associated ILD (SSc-ILD).ResultsIn RA-ILD patients, differential expression analysis revealed enrichment in immune response pathways, metabolism, IFNα and IFNγ response. Within the RA-ILD subgroup, differential expression analysis of clinical and/or radiological progressors versus non-progressors revealed enriched pathways related to cell cycle, DNA replication, IFNγ response, inflammasome assembly and negative regulation of immune response in progressors. Drug repurposing analysis revealed that ITK, Syk and FAK inhibitors are candidate compounds that potentially reverse the transcriptomic signature of RA-ILD progression. Comparison of RA-ILD and SSc revealed shared pathways related to metabolism, extracellular matrix, IFNγ response and response to microorganisms.ConclusionsIn this preliminary study, RA-ILD patients exhibit enhanced immune responses, metabolism and cytokine activation. These data need to be further validated in larger studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-026-03767-0.

Background

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic inflammatory disorder that affects approximately 1% of the population in developed countries [1–3]. Around 2–10% of RA patients develop symptomatic interstitial lung disease (ILD) [4–7], while in 20–60% of these ILD remains subclinical [8]. Once it occurs, RA-ILD is associated with a significant increase in morbidity and mortality [2], with a median survival of 3–7 years after diagnosis in patients with progressive disease [9]. Severity of lung involvement and risk for progression are crucial factors influencing therapeutic decisions [10]. Given the significant clinical impact of ILD as an extra-articular manifestation of RA, early diagnosis and targeted treatment interventions are critical.

Known risk factors for the development of ILD in patients with RA, include older age, male sex, tobacco exposure, high disease activity, seropositivity for rheumatoid factor and/or anti–cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP), and polymorphisms in MUC5B. However, ILD can still develop in the absence of these factors [8, 11–14], suggesting that the pathophysiological processes leading to pulmonary injury remain unclear [6]. According to the current paradigm, the pathogenesis of RA-ILD is primarily related to aberrant tissue reactions in the alveolar wall and lung parenchyma [2]. Continuous damage to epithelial cells with activation of immune cells including neutrophils, dendritic cells, and macrophages lead to excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) components in lung tissues. Of all implicated cells, neutrophils have the greatest cytotoxic potential, owing to their ability to release degradative enzymes and reactive oxygen species (ROS). Neutrophils can also release neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) containing chromatin associated with granule enzymes, which kill extracellular microorganisms and provide a source of autoantigens [15].

Gene expression studies have provided significant insights into complex pathogenetic mechanisms of autoimmune diseases [16]. In this regard, RNA-seq may provide an unbiased look at the pathogenetic events leading to RA-ILD. This notwithstanding, studies of RNA-seq in RA and RA-ILD are currently limited [17]. More recently, using transcriptomic analysis to compare peripheral blood from RA patients to patients with RA-ILD, Wierczeiko et al found 9 significantly upregulated genes in RA-ILD compared to RA controls, most of which were related to fibrosis [18].

To address these knowledge gaps, we sought to characterize the transcriptomic state of RA-ILD in a contemporary patient cohort. Specifically, we aimed to gain insights on the underlying pathophysiologic mechanisms via whole blood RNA-seq analysis, and propose potential chemical compounds that could tackle RA-ILD progression.

Methods

Experimental design and study participants

Patients with RA-ILD were recruited from the Rheumatology and Clinical Immunology Unit of ‘Attikon’ University Hospital, Athens, Greece. Patients fulfilled the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for RA [19]. ILD was diagnosed by high-resolution CT (HRCT) at baseline. The following data were recorded: modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) Dyspnea Scale; Pulmonary Function Tests (PFTs), including Forced Vital Capacity (FVC) and Diffusing Capacity for CO corrected (DLCOc SB); and follow-up HRCT (yearly), mMRC and PFTs (every 6months). For each patient, the first visit to the unit with documentation of ILD was defined as the enrollment visit. Exclusion criteria included the presence of other autoimmune or infectious diseases, neoplasm, or other respiratory diseases.

Regarding RA patients without ILD (control group), HRCT was performed at some point during routine medical care, with no evidence of underlying subclinical ILD. Demographic characteristics, clinical features, disease activity, laboratory data, and administered therapies were extracted from patient files. Seropositivity was defined as the presence of either rheumatoid factor (RF) or/and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies. All chest HRCT images were reviewed by radiologists with expertise in lung imaging in our center, and also by a pulmonologist specialized in interstitial lung diseases with more than 15 years of clinical experience (VT). Of note, in our center we run a dedicated CTD-ILD clinic run by rheumatology (AF, FK) and pulmonology (VT). Patterns were defined based on the current guidelines [20, 21]. The UIP pattern was recognized by the presence of irregular reticulation, traction bronchiectasis and honeycombing in a predominant peripheral and bibasilar distribution. The NSIP pattern was recognized by the presence of predominant ground-glass opacities with a predominantly basal distribution with or without reticulation or traction bronchiectasis, and no or only minimal honeycombing with or without subpleural sparing. Subpleural sparing is defined as involvement of the peripheral lung but with relative sparing of the immediate subpleural lung parenchyma. ILD progression was defined as meeting any of the following criteria: i) worsening of HRCT findings with new or/and worse parenchymal abnormalities, including ground-glass opacities, reticular opacities, traction bronchiectasis and honeycombing or/and ii) decline of mMRC; stable/improved ILD was defined as not meeting the above conditions.

Blood sampling, RNA isolation and RNA sequencing pipeline

Blood samples were collected from serial patients with RA-ILD (n=11), RA non-ILD (n=10) and healthy controls (HC, n=7) at different timepoints from their first visit to our Unit. Publicly available data from patients with systemic sclerosis-related ILD (SSc-ILD) as a ‘positive’ disease control, and PsA blood samples from our previously published dataset [16] as an additional ‘negative’ disease control were used taking into consideration issues related to the pipeline (batch correction, platforms, sequencing depth, cohort characteristics, and confounders). PsA patients exhibited the polyarthritic phenotype and moderate to high disease activity with DAPSA > 15. The mean age was 51.1 years, with the majority (73%) being female and a mean disease duration on average 6.2 years. Regarding treatment, the majority of PsA patients (11 out of 30) were treatment-naïve, while 5 patients were on conventional DMARDs (methotrexate, leflunomide, apremilast).

The majority of RA-ILD patients (n=10/11) was characterized by the presence of persistent non-improving to glucocorticoids (GC) and conventional DMARDs (cDMARDs) ILD disease activity. The RA non-ILD and HC samples were used in a previous study, and are described in detail therein [16]. The HC were donated by the blood donation unit of the hospital. All patients in the RA-ILD group were under treatment during sampling, with all receiving treatment with GC and cDMARDs, while one patient had received a single dose of intravenous rituximab. Ideally, treatment-naïve patients should have been included in the study. However, in real-life practice, this is rarely feasible, especially in referral centers, wherein patients have often already received therapy. To partly mitigate this, glucocorticoids were withheld for at least 24 hours prior to sampling. RA non- ILD patients were treated with low-dose steroids and cDMARDs. Four of these patients were also on anti-TNF therapy. To standardize the sampling process, blood was collected from RA non-ILD patients 24 hours after their last dose of oral treatment and at least 24hours prior to biologic DMARD administration. Regarding glucocorticoids, they rapidly suppress inflammatory gene transcription (minutes to hours) by blocking key transcription factors like NF-κB, leading to reduced pro-inflammatory protein production, with the effects lasting as long as the drug is present and influencing receptor levels [22]. Median treatment duration with GC and cDMARDs during sampling was 15 (3–39) and 14 (3–39) months, respectively. Patients were divided into subgroups based on autoantibody profile, HRCT pattern and ILD progression (progressors vs non progressors) for further transcriptomic analysis (see below). Regarding RA, PsA, HC, scleroderma samples, the differential expression analysis was adjusted for sex and age differences. RNA extraction and sequencing were performed as previously described [16]. Genes with fold change |FC|≥1.5 and P-value<0.05 were selected as significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Heatmaps and volcano plots were created with R using packages ggplot2 [23] and ComplexHeatmap [24].

Bioinformatics analysis

To explore the function of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), we performed pathway enrichment analysis using the differential expression analysis (DEA) results and the clusterProfiler [25] package in R, identifying pathways with activity differences between compared phenotypes. Enriched pathways with FDR<0.2 were considered statistically significant. As reference gene sets, we used the Molecular Signatures Database Hallmark, C2: CP and C5: GO5 collections, downloaded through the msigdbr package (msigdbr v7.5.1) [26]. All expressed genes were ranked by descending value of the product of -log10(P-value) and log2FC, thus highly upregulated genes were at the top and highly downregulated genes were at the bottom of the ranked list. Venn diagrams were created using the R package ggvenn [27]. Overrepresentation analysis (ORA) was performed using clusterProfiler to identify pathways enriched in venn diagram subsets of genes, setting the significance threshold to Benjamini-Hochberg corrected P-value<0.05. Deconvolution of whole-blood transcriptomic data was performed as previously described [28] and differentially abundant cell types were determined using ANOVA and TukeyHSD tests. Kmeans unsupervised clustering based on principal component values was performed to identify subgroups of RAILD patients. To determine the optimal number of clusters we used the “elbow” method and the factoextra (v:1.0.7) package in R. To determine the optimal number of clusters, we used the elbow method (Supplementary figure 1).

Drug repurposing analysis was performed i) using the L1000CDS2 engine providing a gene signature and selecting ‘reverse’ mode and ii) using Connectivity Map 2.0 (CMAP2) [29] through the clue.io web portal, providing the top up- and down-regulated valid genes ranked according to their logFC values. CMAP2 results were filtered keeping only exemplar signatures. The mean normalized connectivity score across all available exemplar signatures was calculated and top compounds were selected according to the lowest mean normalized connectivity score (maximum reversal potential).

Data analysis and statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using statistical methods chosen to appropriately address the nature of the data and research questions. Normally distributed data are expressed as mean (standard deviation), and non-normally distributed data as median (interquartile range) (P25, P75). All P-values<0.05 were considered statistically significant. Nominal P-values are a valid concern for a higher possibility of false positives. The small sample size of our study did not enable us to calculate a large number of genes with statistically significant adjusted P-value level. Due to the exploratory nature of our study, our aim was to spark interest in RA-ILD, and to generate hypotheses for further investigation and possible corroboration in studies with large sample sizes. We thus believe that generating hypotheses by using a nominal P-value, and narrowing the possibility of false-positives by setting a threshold of Fold Change >1.5, was the best approach to generate some insights. Regarding the pathway analysis, FDR<0.25 is the threshold recommended by the developers of the GSEA. Since our differential analysis results were produced using a nominal p-value, we decided to set a stricter threshold in the pathway analysis, setting statistical significance at FDR<0.2.

Results

Transcriptomic profiling of peripheral blood: distinct molecular signature with evidence of humoral and myeloid immunity

Demographic characteristics, clinical features, disease activity, laboratory data, and administered therapies of our cohort patients are included in Table 1. First, we performed transcriptomic analysis of peripheral blood in 11 patients with RA-ILD and 10 RA controls. To follow a data-driven unbiased approach, we clustered the RAILD samples of using their principal component values as input for the Kmeans algorithm. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) analysis Kmeans separated the RA-ILD subgroup from the controls (Fig. 1A). Five RA-ILD patients positioned close to the RA group. These were all female patients with a fibrotic pattern in HRCT (fNSIP: 3/5, UIP: 2/5), dyspnea with mMRC: 2.8±1, mean FVC: 64.2 ± 9.7%. Two of them were previously exposed to certolizumab pegol and intravenous cyclophosphamide pulses, respectively. They had moderate to high RA disease activity (mean DAS28-ESR: 4.4± 1.2 during sampling), and 3/5 were seropositive. Five RA-ILD patients distant from the RA group, was characterized by the presence of NSIP pattern in HRCT, a mMRC: 2±0.6 and mean FVC: 77±12.9%. Organizing pneumonia co-existed in two of them. These patients were seronegative for RA antibodies, however with high RA disease activity during sampling (DAS28ESR: 5.7± 0.5). They were treated with GC and cDMARDs during sampling. A single male RA-ILD patient -closest to the PC1 axis of the plots- was seropositive with high RA disease activity and a UIP pattern on HRCT and was under treatment with GC, leflunomide and a single dose of rituximab during sampling. To further explore the transcriptomic profile and delineate the molecular differences of RA-ILD compared to RA, differential expression analysis was performed (Fig. 1B), revealing 1891 upregulated and 1648 downregulated genes (Fig. 1C). RA-ILD patients exhibited enrichment in immune response pathways (i.e. humoral immune response, macrophage activation, myeloid mediated immunity and differentiation), metabolism (i.e. oxidative phosphorylation), and cytokine activation (i.e. IL6, IL1, IFNγ and IFNα response) (Fig. 1D). To identify the possible cell population that contributes to this profile, digital cytometry with CibersortX was performed. Following the GSEA analysis, which showed enrichment in myeloid leukocyte activities and humoral immune response, M0 macrophages and B memory cells were increased in RA-ILD compared to RA (Fig. 1E), suggesting that humoral immunity and macrophages may play a role in RA-ILD. We next compared the gene expression between RA-ILD seropositive and seronegative patients. Differential expression analysis revealed 113 up- and 87 down-regulated genes (Supplementary Figure 2A-C). Distinct transcriptomic signatures of different radiologic subsets within the RA-ILD cohort

We also looked at the radiologic subtypes of RA-ILD (UIP, n=5/11 and non-UIP, n=6/11) and observed a large variation of transcriptomic profiles between subgroups (Fig. 2A). Of note, the samples of the non UIP subtype included the NSIP, fibrotic NSIP and/or OP patterns. Despite the similarities, comparison of gene expression among subtypes revealed up- and down-regulated genes between UIP and non-UIP (Fig. 2B). We used these comparisons to develop subtype-specific signatures. A set of differentially expressed genes was identified with 263 genes up- and 661 genes down-regulated respectively (Fig. 2C). ILD progressors exhibit an inflammatory transcriptomic profile

Next, we asked whether there is a molecular signature underlying disease progression. To this end, we divided patients into progressors and non-progressors, as described in Methods (Fig. 3A). Differential expression analysis between progressors and non-progressors revealed 207 up- and 99 down-regulated genes (Fig. 3B-C). Progressors exhibited enriched pathways related to cell cycle, DNA replication, IFNγ response, inflammasome assembly and negative regulation of immune response. In contrast, non-progressors exhibited enrichment in response to bacteria, translational initiation, ribosome biogenesis, protein and histone demethylation (Fig. 3D). Drug repurposing analysis highlights ITK, Syk and FAK inhibitors as promising compounds for transcriptome reversal of progressors

Finally, we sought to identify compounds that could reverse the transcriptomic profile of progressors to a state similar to the non-progressors, or towards the molecular profile of healthy controls. To this end, we compared the transcriptome of progressors to non-progressors (Fig. 4B) and to healthy controls (Fig. 4A), and utilized the identified DEGs in combination with the drug repurposing tools L1000CDS2 and CMAP2 to identify compounds that could achieve the desired transcriptomic change. In the case of L1000CDS2, compounds BRD-A08187463 and betamethasone showed the most appropriate dose/cosine distance combination and appear as the most promising candidates to restore the transcriptome of progressors (Fig. 4B). In the case of CMAP2, among the top results of each analysis, 36 compounds were common, showing potential for tackling RA-ILD progression (Fig. 4C). Among the common compounds, ITK, Syk and focal adhesion kinase (FAK) inhibitors seemed the most capable of achieving a similar reversal effect (Fig. 4D). IL-2 inducible T cell kinase (ITK) seems to play an important role in T helper differentiation and is elevated in RA [30]. ITK inhibition has been suggested for the treatment of T cell-mediated inflammatory diseases, through the decrease in the generation of pathogenic Th17 and T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, while increasing T regulatory (Treg) cells, all of which contribute to RA pathogenesis [31]. Spleen tyrosine kinase (Syk) is a cytoplasmic protein-tyrosine kinase associated with the activation of various Fc receptors of the hematopoietic cells targeting humoral response [31, 32]. The phosphorylated subset of Syk levels are highly elevated, especially in strongly anti-CCP positive RA patients, who are at high risk for mainly fibrotic ILD [31]. FAK is a non-receptor protein tyrosine kinase which contributes to the pathogenesis of both RA and lung fibrosis through tissue fibrogenesis, and its inhibition could play a crucial role in the delay of RA-ILD progression [33, 34]. ITK, Syk and FAK emerged as therapeutic agents for RA-ILD. Further research should be conducted, including tissue biopsies, particularly from the lung, to further substantiate these findings. Prototypic CTD-ILD signature is characterized by pathways related to metabolism and response to microorganisms

Finally, we asked whether a prototypic transcriptomic signature exists that characterizes ILD in the context of autoimmune rheumatic diseases. To this end, we used publicly available data of patients with systemic sclerosis associated ILD (SSc-ILD) [35] as a “positive” disease control, given the high prevalence of ILD in SSc, and compared them with the healthy donors of the same dataset. By choosing SSc-ILD, we aimed to identify a potential prototypic “CTD-ILD transcriptomic signature”, shared between SSc and RA. In primary fibrosing lung diseases, like idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), fibrosis is the dominant histological abnormality, and the role -if any- of inflammation is considered minimal; IPF is not considered amenable to inflammation directed modalities. Additionally, we used our previously published dataset of psoriatic arthritis (PsA) samples [16] as a negative disease control (that is, a disease not typically associated with ILD), in order to explore specific RA associated signals. Our rationale was that the absence of ILD in the concurrent presence of inflammatory arthritis in PsA provides a useful control to discern the molecular signature of RA-ILD from that of generalized inflammatory arthritis. The PsA samples were collected and sequenced concurrently with the RA, RA-ILD and healthy donor samples of this study due to the absence of ILD and simultaneous high prevalence of peripheral inflammatory arthritis in this disease), which had been collected during the same time period and sequenced within the same run as the RA, RA-ILD and healthy donor samples of this study. All subsets were compared to the transcriptome of the healthy phenotype (Supplementary Figure 3), and we defined the ‘ILD signature’ as genes deregulated both in SSC- ILD and RA-ILD. Our analysis identified a prototypic connective tissue disease (CTD)-ILD signature consisting of 687 genes (Fig. 5A). Pathway analysis revealed enrichment of pathways related to metabolism, IFNγ response and response to microorganisms (Fig. 5B). Finally, we characterized a ‘RA-ILD unique signature’ of 3697 genes which differentiates the disease from RA, PsA and SSc-ILD (Fig. 5A). Discussion

ILD is an important extra-articular manifestation of RA, associated with poor prognosis, increased morbidity and mortality. In this study, we assembled a contemporary RA-ILD cohort, to gain insights into RA-ILD and to obtain an unbiased look at molecular features related to its pathogenesis, via RNA sequencing of patients’ peripheral blood. We found that compared to patients without ILD, RA-ILD patients exhibit enhanced immune responses, metabolism and cytokine activation, including IL6, IL1, IFNγ and IFNα. Of note, in a small number of patients examined, progressive ILD is characterized by enriched pathways related to cell cycle, DNA replication, IFNγ response, inflammasome assembly and negative regulation of immune response but this need to be further corroborated. Drug repurposing analysis revealed that ITK, Syk and FAK inhibitors are candidate compounds that potentially reverse the transcriptomic signature of RA-ILD progression. These data have significant implications for delineating the pathogenesis and treatment of RA-ILD pointing to the early use of effective anti-inflammatory drugs including kinase inhibitors especially in patients at high risk for progression.

Our study clearly demonstrates that the molecular fingerprint of RA-ILD is distinct from RA without ILD. RA-ILD patients exhibited enriched immune response pathways, metabolism and cytokine activation. This provides a basis for the salutary effects of a variety of agents used in the treatment of RA-ILD, such as rituximab and tocilizumab. We also tested whether there is a universal signature that characterizes ILD within the whole spectrum of autoimmune rheumatic diseases. To this end, we used a publicly available dataset of patients with SSc-ILD as a positive disease control, and came up with an ‘ILD signature’ of 687 genes deregulated both in RA-ILD and in SSc-ILD, but not in RA or PsA (negative disease controls), compared to healthy donors. Pathway analysis revealed enrichment of pathways related to metabolism, IFNγ response, and, importantly, response to microorganisms. Colonization of microorganisms could increase the microbial load leading to the increased activation and mobilization of cells in ILD, a hypothesis supported by studies in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) where increased bacterial burden and/or abundance of potentially pathogenic bacteria may drive disease progression, acute exacerbations, and mortality [36]. Although RA and SSc patients with ILD share a common set of pathways the aberrant expression of genes is more widespread in RA-ILD with a prominent molecular fingerprint for viral responses, leukocyte cell adhesion, complement activation, and T cell activation and differentiation. For the latter, recent data interestingly suggest that targeting T cells with abatacept may stabilize RA-ILD [37, 38].

Reported pathophysiologic ILD mechanisms involve biological processes such as fibroblast- myofibroblast transition (FMT) and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), oxidative phosphorylation and oxidative stress, immunological processes such as activation of immune cells and cytokines, and signaling pathways such as the JAK/STAT and PI3K/Akt [6]. Of note, these pathways were recapitulated in our transcriptomic analysis in the peripheral blood. This is not surprising, because immune cells are generated within the bone marrow and reach the tissues through the bloodstream, with some of them returning to the bone marrow to provide a positive feedback loop to sustain inflammation [39]. Indeed, we found evidence for B cell and macrophage expansion in the peripheral blood of RA-ILD patients.

Although neutrophils were not expanded in the peripheral blood, a strong chemotactic molecular signature suggests that there is increased transmigration into the lung where they mediate fibrosis via the formation of NETs. Myeloid cell migration via CCR1 and the formation of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic NETs by activated neutrophils may contribute to RA-ILD pathogenesis. Indeed, data from our and other groups suggest that neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) cause pathology in a number of conditions through several mechanisms. Direct cell damage by NETs is implicated in immune-inflammation, immune-thrombosis and immune-fibrosis [40]. We have shown that aberrant formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) is a key feature in RA [41]. NETs from RA patients show an increased potential to induce the maturation of DCs from healthy individuals suggesting an important role of NETs in the induction and expansion of Th1 pathogenic cells through maturation of DCs [40]. This suggests that involvement of the lung sets in motion a variety of biological complexes, and that immune aberrancies in RA-ILD are more complex. Among all tissues, the lung is continuously exposed to environmental threats and hence homeostatic regulation of inflammatory reactions and tissue repair within the lung need to be tightly controlled. Mucosal surfaces in the respiratory epithelium have emerged as possible sites for early ‘immunologic hits’ in RA development, particularly in the subgroup of patients with anti-citrullinated peptide/protein antibodies (ACPA). The presence of ACPA many years before onset of joint symptoms in RA development implies extra-articular locations rather than synovium as the initial sites of immune responses in RA. Our transcriptomic data suggest that colonization of a pathologic lung epithelium in RA-ILD may amplify and perpetuate immune reactions and lead to the progression. Thus, early identification of patients at risk, combined with targeted therapy aiming at remission, is crucial for improving RA-ILD outcomes. Understanding the initiating and triggering steps of RA is crucial for the elaboration of future prevention strategies during the preclinical stages of the disease.

A number of limitations of our study should be addressed. RNA-seq analysis was performed in only a limited number of patients who were also not treatment-naïve, and involved peripheral blood and not tissue samples, such as the lung. While we cannot definitely exclude a bystander effect, in autoimmune rheumatic diseases key features are usually shared between the periphery and target organs. This is not surprising since inflammatory cells and stromal cells circulate from the periphery to the involved tissues. To this end, a recent study with early RA patient with comparative transcriptomic and immune profile of peripheral blood and synovial cells has shown that Tph cells play key role in inflammation, activity and treatment response, in both tissues [42]. In another study, by the use of machine learning techniques, transcriptome profiles of both peripheral blood and synovial tissue were analyzed to identify a restricted panel of validated biomarkers that distinguished this type of inflammatory arthritis from osteoarthritis and monitor treatment effect [43]. Data from our group suggest that the peripheral blood signature of patients with lupus nephritis mirrors the kidney transcriptome [44]. Moreover, blood transcriptome analysis of patients with scleroderma or rheumatoid arthritis can demonstrate valid treatment targets [45, 46]. Lewis MJ et al [47] performed a deep immunophenotyping and transcriptome analysis of synovial and blood cells and found shared specific biomarkers in both blood and joint. Finally, more recently in lupus nephritis, transcriptome analysis from both peripheral blood and renal cells described shared signatures that predicted treatment outcomes, non-response and renal flare [48].

As all patients were under treatment with glucocorticoids and/or DMARDs, we cannot exclude potential transcriptomic effects of ongoing therapy. Nonetheless, treatment exposure and timing were similar across groups, and glucocorticoids were withheld before sampling, which should mitigate major confounding effects. These are preliminary data that indicate possible mechanisms which cause different subsets of RA-ILD, and thus need further confirmation in larger patient numbers and multicenter cohorts as well as explore their therapeutic effects in vivo. The size of our cohort is moderate and involves a single center with Southern European patients, thus the results cannot easily be extrapolated to other populations. Furthermore, PFTs were not included in the definition of disease progression due to the limited accrual of PFT data during Sars-Cov-2 pandemic. Despite these factors that may limit the generalizability of our findings, our study represents a comprehensive analysis of the blood transcriptome both in RA and RA-ILD and its comparison with other related diseases, providing a better understanding of the unique pathogenetic features of RA-ILD and possible molecular targets of therapy. Future research to validate them in larger, more diverse cohorts as well as explore local immune responses within lung tissues is needed. These data highlight the complexity of the RA-ILD and could be used to define putative molecular biomarkers for early diagnosis, prognosis, and personalized therapy with novel targets.

Conclusions

RA-ILD represents an increasingly recognized cause of morbidity and mortality in RA. Our initial data provide an unbiased glimpse on the pathogenetic pathways that drive lung fibrosis and its progression. Compared to RA non-ILD, RA-ILD patients exhibit enhanced immune responses, metabolism and cytokine activation supporting timely use of potent anti-inflammatory mechanisms. Importantly, ILD progression in RA is characterized by enriched inflammatory pathways, potentially reversible by kinase inhibitors. To this end, kinase inhibitors such as nintedanib, pirfenidone have shown initial evidence for efficacy supporting their early use in patients at high risk for progression more potent anti-inflammatory therapeutic interventions [49, 50].
