Differences in lumbar spine bone mineral density measured by quantitative computed tomography and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry: an age- and sex-specific analysis
Authors: Xingjian Li, Chunchao Huang, Xiaoqian Wang, Wencong Chen, Yi Peng, Junjie Yuan, Wentao Lin, Yawei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and quantitative computed tomography (QCT) are the usual methods for evaluating bone mineral density in patients with spinal osteoporosis. However, the influence of sex and age remains unexplored. The study aims to reveal the differences in age- and sex-specific bone mineral density (BMD) evaluations in patients undergoing spinal surgery.MethodsA total of 1144 elderly patients underwent both QCT and DXA from March 2021 to November 2024 were enrolled. A paired chi-square test was used to compare the osteoporosis detection rates between QCT and DXA. Scatter plots with DXA and QCT were constructed to test whether the BMD distribution difference changed with patient sex and age.ResultsOf the 1144 patients, 56.5% were diagnosed as osteoporosis via QCT, whereas 42.3% were diagnosed as osteoporosis via DXA. In total, 408 (35.7%) patients met the osteoporosis criteria on both QCT and DXA. Among the other 314 patients with diagnostic discordance, 238 (20.7%) met the QCT-based osteoporosis criteria but were diagnosed as osteopenic or normal via DXA criteria; 76 (6.7%) were diagnosed with osteoporosis by DXA but not by QCT. QCT BMD was significantly associated with age in both females (P < 0.001) and males (P < 0.001), and this negative correlation was more obvious in females (r = −0.667). Notably, DXA T scores were significantly (P < 0.001) and negatively related to age (r = −0.353) in females but not in males (r = −0.017, P = 0.301).ConclusionsAmong elderly patients scheduled for spinal surgery, QCT demonstrates a higher detection rate of osteoporosis in males compared to DXA. Regarding BMD evaluation in females, much consideration should be given to sex- and age-specific differences between DXA and QCT results.

Background

Osteoporosis, characterized by decreased bone mass and destruction of bone tissue microstructure, has increasingly become a serious global health problem as the global population ages [1, 2]. Bone mineral density (BMD), the most widely used parameter that can be readily measured in clinical practice, is the gold standard for diagnosing osteoporosis [3]. A high rate of osteoporosis morbidity has been reported in elderly patients undergoing spine surgery [4]. Accurate BMD evaluation before spinal surgery permits a more meaningful assessment to guide surgical treatment decisions and is useful in understanding long-term clinical outcomes, such as guidance on fixed segments and prediction of implant loosening [5–7].

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and quantitative computed tomography (QCT) are the two main imaging methods for the assessment of spinal BMD in clinical practice at present [8, 9]. Owing to differences in the measurement technique and nonidentical site of BMD measurement, there are avoidable and nonnegligible differences in spinal BMD results derived from DXA and those derived from a QCT scan. Several investigations, including cross-sectional and case–control studies, have compared the diagnostic accuracy of spinal osteoporosis using QCT and DXA, but their results are not in agreement [10–14]. However, research conducted on small samples might not have sufficient statistical power [10, 12, 14]. Findings from groups with low osteoporosis rates or diabetic patients might not be generalizable to the specific population undergoing spine surgery [11, 12].

As a multifactorial bone disease, osteoporosis greatly varies by age and sex [15–17]. The incidence of osteoporosis is markedly greater in postmenopausal women than in age-matched men. In women, trabecular bone loss seems to begin in young adults and markedly accelerates due to the loss of estrogen during the perimenopausal interval, and the rate remains relatively constant later in life [18]. Unlike in postmenopausal women with osteoporosis, it is generally believed that the cause of primary male osteoporosis is age-related bone loss [19, 20]. The rate of trabecular (lumbar spine) bone loss in men increases progressively across the lifespan but is not relatively constant [21]. In addition to being determined by body composition, genetic/ethnic factors, and hormonal status, bone loss is also related to lifestyle behaviors, such as cigarette smoking and alcohol consumption, which are more common in males [22, 23]. Regarding the above considerations, it can be reasonably speculated that age- and sex-based BMD evaluations are more conducive to dynamically reflecting real bone status. However, to our knowledge, no such assessment has been performed yet. Previous studies comparing DXA and QCT have focused primarily on differences in the detection rate of osteoporosis and potential clinical utility but not on the characteristics of the study population, which may call into question their generalizability to all populations [24, 25], emphasizing the need for data regarding age- and sex-related differences in spinal BMD measurements detected by DXA and QCT.

Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to establish a target population of spinal BMD measurements in a relatively large population undergoing spinal surgery to (1) compare the diagnostic performance of DXA and QCT in detecting spinal osteoporosis and (2) reveal the differences in age- and sex-specific BMD evaluations.This comparison is clinically significant because age- and sex-tailored BMD assessment may improve preoperative risk stratification: older males may benefit from QCT to avoid underestimation of bone fragility, while perimenopausal females may require careful interpretation of DXA-defined osteoporosis given rapid trabecular changes during this interval. Understanding these patterns enables more informed selection of imaging modalities in spine surgery candidates.

Methods

Study design and population cohort

This study was approved by the local institutional review board (IRB, KYLS20220105) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 1498 middle-aged and elderly patients referred to the department of spinal surgery at a single academic institution from March 2021 to November 2024 agreed to participate in this study. All participants signed a written informed consent form. Demographic information, including sex, age, height, weight, body mass index, disease information, primary diagnosis, and comorbidities, was recorded upon admission. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) During the hospitalization, QCT and DXA examinations were not performed simultaneously, and the interval between the two examinations exceeded one week; (2) systemic metabolic bone diseases, including hypoparathyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, rickets, and Paget's disease; (3) ongoing anti-osteoporosis treatment (e.g., bisphosphonates, strontium ranelate, teriparatide, denosumab, and selective estrogen receptor modulators) prior to QCT and DXA imaging examinations; (4) history of spinal fixation; (5) spinal tumors; (6) spinal infections; and (7) severe spinal deformities. Finally, a total of 1144 patients were included in this study.

BMD measurement

DXA scans were performed using GE Lunar DXA (GE Lunar Prodigy and DPX Brovo DXA scanners, GE Healthcare, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer's standard protocol. Areal BMD at the L1 to L4 lumbar spine was measured by two trained radiologists, and the T values were calculated as follows: T score = (areal BMD – peak areal BMD)/standard deviation of areal BMD in normal adults. Osteoporosis was diagnosed on the basis of DXA T score according to the World Health Organization (WHO) 1994 criteria for the diagnosis of osteoporosis (normal, T score ≥ −1.0; osteopenia, −2.5 < T score < −1.0; osteoporosis, T score ≤ −2.5). BMD at the total hip and femoral neck, as well as fragility fractures, were also assessed but were not included in the final analysis.

Lumbar QCT images were obtained using an Aquilion 64-slice CT scanner (Toshiba Medical System Inc., Tokyo, Japan) with the Mindways QCT Pro system (Mindways Software Inc., Austin, TX, USA). Standard QCT measurements were used to assess volumetric BMD (vBMD) at the L1-L2 lumbar spine according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The phantom calibration method has been described previously. On the transverse CT images, an elliptical region of interest (ROI) was outlined within the anterior and middle columns of each vertebra and adjusted on the corresponding sagittal CT images to avoid the cortical bone and vertebral veins of the vertebrae. The revised thresholds at the L1-L2 levels introduced in 2018 were used for lumbar osteoporotic diagnosis (normal, BMD > 120 mg/cm3; osteopenia, 80 mg/cm3 ≤ BMD ≤ 120 mg/cm3; osteoporosis, BMD < 80 mg/cm3). For vertebrae where the ROI could not be measured at the L1-L2 level, vBMD was measured using adjacent vertebrae as a proxy. BMD assessments were performed by two experienced radiologists who were blinded to the study conditions.

Statistics

Continuous variables are expressed as the means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared between men and women using independent samples t tests and chi-square tests. A paired chi-square test (McNemar test) was used to compare the osteoporosis detection rates between QCT and DXA in contingency tables, The diagnostic categories are classified as normal, osteopenia, and osteoporosis.. QCT vBMD was plotted against the DXA T score, followed by calculation of the line of best fit via linear regression, and individuals from the different diagnostic categories were coded with different colors. To test whether the distribution difference in BMD changed with patient age, we included age group as a fixed effect and constructed scatter plots with the DXA T score and QCT vBMD. We also modeled the DXA T score or QCT vBMD as a function of age via linear regression to account for differences in age-associated changes in BMD obtained with the two methods. To assess independent risk factors for osteoporosis detected by DXA or QCT, factors with statistical significance in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression, and the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) of age were calculated. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software (version 27.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Differences were considered statistically significant when the p value was < 0.05.

Discordance in osteoporosis diagnoses by QCT and DXA

Among the 1144 patients, 56.5% were diagnosed with osteoporosis, 30.8% with osteopenia and 12.8% with normal BMD via QCT. Among those screened with DXA, 42.3% had osteoporosis, 29.5% had osteopenia, and 28.2% had normal BMD. Scatter plots (Fig. A) revealed statistically significant correlations between QCT-vBMD and the DXA T score (Pearson's r = 0.647, p < 0.001). As shown in Fig. 1B and Table 2, the results derived from the two different BMD measurement methods were consistent for 672 (58.7%) patients. A total of 408 (35.7%) patients met the QCT- and DXA-based osteoporosis criteria, 140 (12.2%) were reported to have osteopenia on both QCT and DXA, and 126 (11%) had normal BMD. Among the other 314 patients with diagnostic discordance, 238 (20.7%) met the QCT-based osteoporosis criteria but were diagnosed with osteopenia or normal BMD according to the DXA criteria, whereas 76 (6.7%) were diagnosed with osteoporosis by DXA but not by QCT. On the basis of the univariate analysis, those factors that were significantly correlated with osteoporosis were further evaluated with a multivariate logistic regression. Age was a risk factor for osteoporosis detected by DXA/QCT in both males and females in unadjusted analyses. After adjusting for confounding factors, age remained an independent risk factor for male osteoporosis by QCT and female osteoporosis by DXA/QCT but not for male osteoporosis by DXA, and the largest adjusted OR value for female osteoporosis by QCT was 1.179 (95% CI 1.15–1.208).

Discussion

The present study is by far the largest to report on spinal BMD evaluation using QCT and DXA. In the general population of patients undergoing spine surgery, the correlation between the QCT-derived BMD and the DXA T score was moderate, and the former had an overall higher detection rate of osteoporosis than did the latter. Although bone loss occurs throughout the whole body and there is considerable overlap of vertebral measures between QCT (L1-L2) and DXA (L1-L4), the relative differences in BMD between the two methods remain significant [26, 27]. Indeed, we observed more than 40% discordance for the BMD categories between the results produced from DXA and those derived from a QCT scan. Moreover, our results revealed clear differences in BMD measurements among populations of a given age and sex, especially among aging males.

The paucity of evidence-based data regarding differences in spinal BMD measured via DXA and QCT among different ages and sexes prompted us to perform a subgroup study. In most settings, QCT appears to be a better technology than DXA for detecting the presence of spinal osteoporosis in males. For males aged more than 60 years, the differences in BMD evaluation between the two methods are even more pronounced. Several mechanisms may contribute to the observed discordance in older males. Degenerative spinal changes, including osteophyte formation and endplate sclerosis, are more prevalent with advancing age and may artificially elevate DXA-measured BMD due to projectional averaging. Meanwhile, men also experience age-related hormonal changes, and some believe that the decline in testosterone levels is also related to the formation of bone spurs [28]. Moreover, increases in tissue thickness in males, such as greater lumbar multifidus and erector spinae, could also introduce artifactual increases in DXA measurements [29, 30]. The maximum difference in the osteoporosis detection rate reached 39.1% between the two methods (60% by QCT vs. 20.9% by DXA) for males in the 70–79 years age subgroup, and a greater rate of trabecular bone loss and a relatively constant rate of cortical bone loss after 70 years of age would be more conducive to QCT-derived vBMD measurements, which may be a more suitable explanation for the observed trend. Indeed, in our study, only 16.4% of older male patients were diagnosed with osteoporosis by DXA [21]. The scatter plots revealed that the DXA T score decreased slowly with increasing age, but the trend was not statistically significant. Previous studies have reported that the prevalence of osteoporosis essentially does not change in older men aged < 70 years on the basis of the DXA T scores recommended by the WHO criteria [31] . For this reason, they also proposed that the prevalence of osteoporosis in men is too small to have any meaningful clinical use. However, Considering that the incidence of osteoporotic vertebral fracture increases with age, the use of DXA to measure spinal BMD might lead to an underestimation of the actual incidence of osteoporosis in men. For the reasons given above, population-based studies of BMD by DXA in men are seemingly of questionable value. Previous studies have shown QCT is more helpful than DXA in terms of predicting patients with prevalent osteoporotic vertebral fracture, emphasizing the need for QCT scans in those patients [32, 33].

Unlike male osteoporosis, the reported prevalence of spinal osteoporosis in females, either diagnosed by QCT or by DXA, significantly increased with increasing age. The rate of female osteoporosis detected by QCT increased at a faster rate, especially in the subgroup aged 50–59 (21.2%) and 60–69 (62.9%) years, whereas that detected by DXA was relatively slow, increasing by 21.3% from age 50–59 to 60–69 years. In women, trabecular bone loss at the lumbar spine begins in the third decade and continues throughout the life span, with apparent acceleration during the perimenopausal interval due to reduced estrogen secretion, suggesting that quantitative assessments of volumetric cancellous bone can better reflect changes in spinal BMD during this period [21, 34]. This might explain why osteoporosis detected by QCT increases at a faster rate than that detected by DXA from the 50–59-year subgroup to the 60–69-year subgroup.

We also assessed the model accuracy by calculating the slope and intercept of the linear regression in females differing by age on the basis of the linear regression analysis of the QCT-derived vBMD (Y) and the DXA T score (X). The maximum linear slope was observed in the 50–60 year subgroup (y = 128.18 + 15.04 * x) and gradually decreased with advancing age, the steeper slope in the 50–59 age group may reflect greater dissociation between DXA and QCT measurements during the perimenopausal interval, though this cross-sectional observation requires longitudinal validation. A straight line passing through the point with coordinates (-2.5, 90.6) and (-3.2, 80) implies that those with a vBMD of 80–90.6 mg/cm3 were likely to be diagnosed with osteoporosis by DXA. The current results showed that DXA can detect osteoporosis in 10.3% more patients than can QCT in subgroup analyses of women aged 50–59 years. The DXA criterion introduced by the WHO is primarily based on the relationship between BMD and the risk of a fragility fracture and is therefore not suitable for cross-application to QCT [35]. Those facing a greater risk of fracture due to rapid cancellous bone loss at age 50–59 years may be diagnosed with osteoporosis by DXA but with osteopenia by QCT, which is potentially one of the reasons for the inconsistency between QCT and DXA. For these reasons, DXA was a better fit for the assessment of current BMD status, but a dynamic evaluation of QCT would be more representative of bone loss trends.

As shown by the scatter plot of DXA/QCT and age in the female subgroup, the smaller slope and intercept for the DXA T score against age and the relatively steeper slopes and larger slope for the QCT indicate an age-related BMD evaluation in females. The calculations were performed at a DXA T score = −2.5 and a QCT vBMD = 80 mg/cm3 on the basis of the fitted lines, yielding X = 67.8 years for the former and 63.5 years for the latter, which indicates a most likely discrepancy in the osteoporosis diagnosis for females from the ages of 63.5 to 67.8 years. To our knowledge, this is the first reported observation of such an intertwined phenomenon in female spinal BMD evaluation using DXA and QCT.

A potential age-related decline in model performance was observed, whereas the intercept values also showed a decreasing trend, suggesting that the baseline prediction of the model might decrease with age. These findings highlight the importance of considering age as a potential confounding factor when assessing model accuracy in different subgroups [36, 37]. Our study has several noteworthy strengths. It is a targeted age- and sex-specific comparison of spinal BMD, which is more representative and reflects the actual bone density in different populations. Furthermore, both DXA T score- and QCT-derived vBMD data were obtained simultaneously from the same individuals, which contributes to eliminating the influence caused by mixed factors such as the interval between examinations and individual differences.

These findings suggest two clinical significance. First, in males ≥ 60 years, QCT detected substantially more osteoporosis cases than DXA (gap up to 39.1%), providing complementary data for surgical risk stratification. Second, in females, The direction of discrepancy varied with age, with DXA surpassing QCT at 50–59 years, while QCT overtook DXA after 60 years, supporting the selection of appropriate methods based on age. The present results reveal age and sex based differences in lumbar spine BMD measured by QCT and DXA. It is sufficient to screen BMD in early postmenopausal strata using DXA [38]. The relative preponderance of QCT to DXA for osteoporosis detection increases with age and it is more likely to develop at age 60 or older. Meanwhile, QCT scan not includes posterior element of the spine and are somewhat more convincing in case of severe spinal degeneration or scoliosis [39]. For older males, spinal BMD measured by DXA do not accurately reflect actual bone quality, and QCT appears to be a more feasible approach for detecting the presence of spinal osteoporosis.The present study also has several limitations. First, all patients were scheduled for spinal surgery, with potential degenerative changes (osteophytes, endplate sclerosis) that may artificially elevate DXA-measured BMD. These results may not generalize to asymptomatic populations or younger adults. Second, osteoporotic vertebral fracture, as the most common complication of osteoporosis, was not included in the analysis, and thus, the mechanism underlying the relationship between DXA/QCT and vertebral fracture among sex and age subgroups remains obscure. We cannot determine whether QCT-identified additional osteoporosis cases represent genuine elevated fracture risk or over-diagnosis. Future studies should correlate DXA/QCT discordance with vertebral fracture incidence. Third, abdominal aortic calcification, abdominal fat content, and paravertebral muscles, which affect the accuracy of aBMD evaluation, were not incorporated into the study design [40]. Furthermore, CT scans come with increased radiation doses and higher costs than DXA. However, for patients undergoing spine surgery, CT scan is primarily used in disease diagnosis and surgical planning and not just for BMD measurement. Finally, for comparability, a DXA T score ≤ −2.5 for osteoporosis criteria recommended by the WHO was used for all reported participants, including premenopausal women and men. which may affect the objectivity of our results, and the incidence of osteoporosis detected by DXA might have been underestimated because those with a T score ≤ −2.5 in the hip or femur or osteopenia with fragility fracture were not included in the analysis [41, 42].

Conclusions

In conclusion, a BMD evaluation of older patients who underwent spinal surgery revealed substantial heterogeneity by measurement method and age and sex groups. For spinal BMD evaluation in males, QCT demonstrates a higher detection rate of osteoporosis in males compared to DXA. In terms of BMD evaluation in females, much consideration should be given to sex- and age-specific differences in DXA and QCT.
