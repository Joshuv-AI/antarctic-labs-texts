Prognostic Factors in Oropharyngeal Cancer Treated With Definitive Chemoradiotherapy
Authors: Sheetal R. Kashid, Ashwini Budrukkar, Monali Swain, Sarbani Ghosh Laskar, Vedang Murthy, Tejpal Gupta, Ajay Sasidharan, Vijay Patil, Amit Joshi, Vanita Noronha, Neha Mittal, Manoj Mahimkar, Asawari Patil, Usha Patel, Shwetabh Sinha, Anuj Kumar, Nandini Menon, Munita Bal, Kumar Prabhash, Jai Prakash Agarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesHPV‐negative oropharyngeal squamous cell cancer (OPSCC) is associated with poor clinical outcomes. The main objective of this study was to evaluate prognostic factors of OPSCC treated with definitive chemoradiotherapy (CTRT).Materials and MethodsConsecutive patients of OPSCC treated with definitive CTRT in a tertiary care center from January 2013 to December 2017 were analysed retrospectively. Kaplan–Meier method was used for survival analysis, Log‐rank test was used for univariate analysis (UVA), and Cox regression method was used for multivariate analysis (MVA).ResultsOut of 630 eligible patients, 543 (86.1%) had locally advanced stage according to AJCC 7th edition. HPV status was known for 500 patients, of which 55 (11%) tested p16 positive, reflecting a predominantly HPV‐negative cohort. Chemo‐radiotherapy was offered to 447 (71%) patients. Intensity‐modulated‐radiotherapy (IMRT) technique was used for 163 (25.9%) patients. On UVA KPS ≤ 80 (p = 0.001), p16 negative tumours (p < 0.001), T3‐T4 stage (p < 0.001), advanced group stage (AJCC 8th ed.) (p < 0.001), conventional RT technique (p < 0.001), RT dose < 66 Gy EQD2 (p < 0.001) and residual disease after treatment (p < 0.001) were associated with poor Locoregional control (LRC) rates. On MVA p16 negative tumours (HR 3.1 [95% CI 1.8–5.3]), T3‐T4 stage (HR 1.6 [95% CI 1.2–2.2]), conventional RT technique (HR 1.6 [95% CI 1.5–2.3]), RT dose < 66 Gy EQD2 (HR 2.2 [95% CI 1.5–3.4]) were independent prognostic factors for poor LRC. These variables were also significant for local control, disease‐free free and overall survival.ConclusionIn this predominantly HPV‐negative OPSCC cohort, HPV status, T stage, RT technique, and RT doses > 66 Gy were independent prognostic factors for all outcomes.

Introduction

Oropharyngeal cancer, comprising 6.5% of all head and neck cancers [1], presents an intriguing landscape in treatment outcomes shaped by the intricacies of disease biology. Unravelling its prognosis involves a multifaceted interplay of elements. Factors such as Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) status, tumour stage, nodal involvement, histological grade, performance status, smoking, alcohol use, and potential biomarkers are pivotal influencers in predicting treatment outcomes [2, 3, 4, 5]. Among these, HPV emerges as an independent prognostic factor for oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) outcomes, which triggered numerous de‐escalation trials [2, 6, 7]. Yet, despite these endeavours, the established standard of care remains unchanged [8]. Embracing accurate patient selection, not solely reliant on HPV but encompassing all prognostic factors, becomes imperative when contemplating treatment de‐escalation or escalation.

The existing literature on prognostic factors for OPSCC has been constrained by small sample sizes, a lack of HPV status availability, or a narrow focus solely on HPV as a prognostic indicator. In our previous study, we reported the incidence of HPV and treatment outcomes for 630 patients of OPSCC [9, 10]. In this study, we thoroughly examined a range of prognostic factors in OPSCC to determine their relevance in the HPV era. This study will offer insights into the significance of diverse prognostic factors in OPSCC, paving the way for tailored therapeutic approaches that optimise patient outcomes. Our primary objective was to evaluate various patient, disease, and treatment‐related prognostic factors influencing treatment outcomes post‐chemoradiotherapy in OPSCC.

Materials and Methods

The medical records of consecutive patients diagnosed with OPSCC and treated with radical intent radiotherapy from January 2013 to December 2017 in a tertiary cancer centre in India were reviewed retrospectively. A waiver of informed consent was obtained as this was a retrospective study. Patients with histopathological diagnosis of OPSCC, staged (American Joint Cancer Committee) AJCC 7th edition (ed). cT1‐T4b, cN0‐N3b and cM0 treated with curative intent radiotherapy +/− chemotherapy were eligible for the study. Between January 2013 and December 2017, 4998 patients were treated under the head and neck radiotherapy group, of which 1080 were of OPSCC. After excluding patients referred outside and treated with palliative intent, 630 consecutive patients were eligible for the study.

Patients were assessed in a multi‐disciplinary joint clinic, and disease mapping and appropriate anatomic imaging were done to assign a suitable stage. Depending on the stage and physician discretion, patients were planned either for conventional technique on telecobalt with bilateral tissue compensators or intensity‐modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) technique on a linear accelerator. Patients were treated with a conventionally fractionated schedule of 66–70 Gy/30–35 fractions/6–7 weeks with five fractions per week, along with concurrent chemotherapy. Concurrent chemotherapy was given in fit patients with locally advanced stages. Patients with cT1‐T2 cN0 suitable for brachytherapy boost were assessed for the feasibility of High dose rate (HDR) brachytherapy at baseline and after 50 Gy of External Beam Radiation Therapy (EBRT) delivery. After 1–2 weeks of EBRT, a brachytherapy boost was given to suitable patients at a dose of 21 Gy/7# using 3 Gy per fraction twice daily with a gap of 6 hours. During treatment, patients were reviewed at least once weekly for treatment tolerance, compliance, speech and swallowing and toxicity assessment. Information regarding patient factors, disease status, radiotherapy, chemotherapy and toxicity were extracted from the ROIS (Radiation Oncology Information System) and electronic medical records (EMR) to form a database. For patients whose p16 status was unavailable, p16 IHC testing was done retrospectively in accordance with College of American Pathologists (CAP) guidelines. Diffuse nuclear and cytoplasmic staining in > 70% of tumour cells was considered HPV‐positive [11]. Staging was done for all patients using AJCC 7th ed at the time of treatment. For this study, HPV‐positive patients were re‐staged using the AJCC 8th ed. staging system for HPV‐positive OPSCC. Patients who tested HPV‐negative and those whose p16 status could not be evaluated were re‐staged using AJCC 8th ed. staging system for HPV‐negative OPSCC.

After completion of treatment, patients were assessed at 12 weeks for response assessment which was done by clinical examination and radiological examination such as Contrast Enhanced Computed Tomography of Head and Neck (CECT HN), Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) of the face and neck or Positron Emission Tomography (PET CECT) depending on the subsite and baseline imaging available for comparison. Further, patients were advised to come for follow‐up every 3 months for 2 years, then every 6 months for 3 years and annually afterwards. At each follow‐up, clinical examination findings, complaints and late toxicity were documented on the EMR.

Statistical Analysis

For statistical analysis, local failure was defined as disease persistence or disease relapse within anatomical oropharynx. Regional failure was defined as disease persistence or relapse within bilateral level I–V cervical nodes and/or bilateral retropharyngeal nodes. Locoregional failure is defined as a local failure and/or regional failure. Second primary is defined as any second de novo malignant neoplasm other than oropharyngeal cancer. Distant metastasis was defined as any relapse outside the local or regional site due to known oropharyngeal primary. Disease‐free survival (DFS) was calculated from the date of diagnosis till the first failure after radiotherapy completion either locoregional, distant failure or death due to any cause. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of diagnosis till the date of death due to any cause.

For local, regional, and locoregional failure first failure after radiotherapy +/− chemotherapy was considered as an event. Patients who were not on regular follow‐up were contacted telephonically. Patients who had the recurrent or persistent unresectable disease at last follow‐up and were not contactable telephonically were considered dead at 6 months from the last follow‐up date. Patients who were alive without any evidence of disease and not contactable telephonically were considered lost to follow‐up and censored for statistical consideration.

Descriptive analysis was used for demographics. Kaplan–Meier method was used for survival analysis. The log‐rank test was used for univariate analysis (UVA), while the Cox regression model was used for multivariate analysis (MVA). A cox regression model was run initially for all variables. Then a stepwise backward conditional model was used to remove least significant variables, model was rerun with remaining significant variables at each step. Age was dichotomized at the median value. The cut off values for radiotherapy dose, overall treatment time (OTT) and other categorical variables were selected based on clinical significance. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 25 and R Studio version 2023.03.0 + 386.

Treatment Details

Concurrent chemoradiotherapy was given to 470 (74.6%) patients. Six hundred and one (95.4%) patients were treated with EBRT, 28 (4.4%) patients were treated with EBRT followed by brachytherapy boost and one patient was treated with surface mould brachytherapy alone. The median dose of EBRT was 70 Gy with a median of 35 fractions. Conventional fractionation (70 Gy/35#/5 fractions per week/7 weeks) was used for 611 (97%) of the patients, accelerated fractionation (70 Gy/35#/6 fractions per week/6 weeks) for 14 (2.2%) and hypofractionation (50 Gy/20# 5 fractions per week/4 weeks) was used for 5 (0.8%) patients. The median dose of brachytherapy boost was 21 Gy (range 18–24 Gy) with a median of 7 fractions (range 7–8). The median OTT was 53 days (IQR 50–57). The most common concurrent chemotherapy agent used was cisplatin in 281 (59.8%), followed by a combination of weekly cisplatin (30 mg/m2) and nimotuzumab (200 mg) 121 (25.7%), followed by others 68 (14.5%). Thirteen patients received 3‐weekly cisplatin (100 mg/m2), while 268 received weekly cisplatin 40 mg/m2. The number of cycles received in each regimen has been detailed in the Table S1.

Response to Treatment and Outcomes

Post‐treatment 12 weeks complete response was seen in 365 (57.9%) patients, partial response in 178 (28.3%), progressive disease in 53 (8.4%), stable disease in 4 (0.6%) patients while the response assessment was not available for 41 patients. At a median follow‐up of 73.3 months (IQR 57.8–89) using inverse Kaplan–Meier method, 213 (33.8%) patients were alive without disease, 4 (0.6%) patients were alive with disease, 7 (1.1%) alive with second primary, 365 (57.9%) patients died of disease, and 41 (6.5%) patients died of other non‐cancer‐related causes. At 5 years Local Control (LC), Loco‐Regional control (LRC), DFS and OS were 48.1%, 35.6%, 29.2% and 34.5%, respectively.

Local and Locoregional Control

On UVA, patients with karnofsky performance status (KPS) ≤ 80, p16 negative status, T3–T4 stage (AJCC 8th ed.), AJCC 8th ed. group stage III/IV, conventional technique and equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQD2) of less than 66 Gy showed significantly worst LC and LRC (Figures 1 and 2). In addition to the above factors, the base of tongue subsite and nodal involvement showed poor LRC on UVA. Patients who did not receive concurrent chemotherapy had significantly poor LC; however, there was no statistically significant difference for LRC. On MVA HPV negative tumours (p16 negative vs. p16 positive), higher T stage (AJCC 8th ed.) (T3–T4 vs. T1–T2), conventional technique of RT (Conventional vs. IMRT) (Figure S1), lower EQD2 of RT (< 66 Gy vs. ≥ 66 Gy) were independent prognostic factors for poor LC and LRC. In addition to these factors, patients who did not receive concurrent chemotherapy had significantly poor LC on MVA.

Disease Free Survival

Patients who had KPS ≤ 80, male gender, base of the tongue tumours, p16 negative status, T3–T4 stage (AJCC 8th ed.), nodal involvement at the time of presentation, AJCC 8th ed. group stage III/IV, not received concurrent chemotherapy, conventional radiotherapy technique, EQD2 of less than 66 Gy and OTT > 49 days had significantly worst DFS on UVA. On MVA, HPV negative tumours (p16 negative vs. p16 positive), higher T stage (AJCC 8th ed.) (T3–T4 vs. T1–T2), nodal involvement (N+ vs. N0), technique of RT (conventional vs. IMRT) (Figure S2), lower EQD2 of RT (< 66 Gy vs. ≥ 66 Gy) were independent prognostic factors for poor DFS. The use of concurrent chemotherapy did not reach significance for DFS on MVA.

Overall Survival

UVA for OS showed KPS ≤ 80, age > 56 years, base of the tongue tumours, p16 negative status, T3–T4 stage (AJCC 8th ed.), nodal involvement at the time of presentation, AJCC 8th ed. group stage III/IV, patients who did not receive concurrent chemotherapy, use of conventional radiotherapy technique, EQD2 of less than 66 Gy and OTT > 49 days were significant factors for poor OS. On MVA, patients with HPV‐negative tumours (p16 negative vs. p16 positive), higher T stage (AJCC 8th ed.) (T3–T4 vs. T1–T2), nodal involvement (N+ vs. N0), who did not receive chemotherapy, conventional technique of RT (conventional vs. IMRT) (Figure S3) and lower EQD2 of RT (< 66 Gy vs. ≥ 66 Gy) showed significantly poor OS.

In a subset of 445 patients who completed concurrent chemoradiotherapy, we evaluated the impact of treatment breaks, treatment breaks ≤ 3 days versus > 3 days. At 5‐years, there were no statistically significant differences in LC (51.5% vs. 50.6%, p = 0.676), LRC (38.2% vs. 34.8%, p = 0.395), DFS (33.1% vs. 29.3%, p = 0.336), or OS (38.9% vs. 34.3%, p = 0.171) between the two groups.

In addition to above factors we also analysed difference LRC of T1‐T2N0 patients with IMRT alone versus IMRT with brachytherapy boost. The 5‐year LRC was 57.8% in IMRT with ≥ 66 Gy and 66.4% in those receiving brachytherapy boost (p 0.337). Similarly we did not observe a statistically significant difference in outcomes between patients receiving EQD2 dose of > 70 Gy compared to those who received 70 Gy.

A total of 32 patients received an EQD2 dose > 70 Gy. Of these, 28 patients received 72.75 Gy EQD2 via a 21 Gy brachytherapy boost after 50 Gy EBRT. Four patients treated with EBRT alone received EQD2 72–74 Gy to compensate for treatment gaps exceeding one week.

Discussion

This is one of the largest studies reporting prognostic factors of 630 consecutive patients with primary OPSCC treated with definitive radiotherapy +/− concurrent chemotherapy from a single institute. At a median follow‐up of 73.3 months (IQR 57.8–89), 5‐year LC, LRC, DFS and OS were 48.1%, 35.6%, 29.2% and 34.5%, respectively. We found that patients with HPV‐negative tumours, higher T‐stage, conventional technique of RT, and EQD2 of < 66 Gy are independent prognostic factors for poor LC, LRC, DFS & OS.

The median age in our study was 56 years with a median KPS of 80. Increasing age and low KPS are well‐known prognostic factors in OPSCC due to their association with existing comorbidities, fitness for treatment, tolerance, and compliance. In our study patients with age less than 56 showed significantly better OS compared older age group. Patients with KPS of > 80 performed significantly better in all treatment outcome measures. As expected higher incidence of OPSCC was seen in males. Some studies have shown poor OS in female HPV‐negative OPSCC [12, 13]; however, we could not find any difference between LC, LRC and OS. DFS was 12% higher in females than males, possibly due to non‐cancer‐related deaths in the male population. Tobacco is one of the well‐known etiological factors for OPSCC. Studies have shown a correlation between history and duration of tobacco use with poor outcomes [2, 3, 5, 14]. Eighty‐nine percent of patients in our cohort had a history of tobacco‐related habits with a median duration of 25 years. The population of patients without tobacco habits was only 11% hence, we could not find any difference in outcomes between tobacco users and tobacco nonusers.

In many retrospective studies, the base of the tongue and vallecula has been reported as an adverse subsite in OPSCC because of occult primary disease and late presentation with nodal disease [15, 16, 17]. Herein, we have base of tongue as primary in 47% (293) of the population and were associated with poor outcomes for LC, LRC, DFS, and OS.

As per AJCC 8th ed. TNM staging 83% percent of our population was of locally advanced stage. T stage (AJCC 8th ed.) was an independent predictor for LC, LRC, DFS and OS. N stage was an independent predictor for LRC, DFS & OS. Many studies in the past have shown the prognostic importance of the T stage (AJCC 8th ed.) and the N stage as prognostic factors in HPV and non‐HPV era [5, 16, 18, 19, 20].

Studies have reported an increasing incidence of HPV‐associated OPSCC with an excellent prognosis after treatment in the Western world [2, 6, 21]. Compared to the Western world incidence of HPV‐related OPSCC was only 11% in our study group, indicating tobacco as a predominant etiological factor. Importantly, although HPV related OPSCC constituted a small proportion of our cohort, they demonstrated markedly superior outcomes, consistent with reports from global analyses showing significantly improved survival for HPV related OPSCC across diverse populations, despite its lower prevalence in such regions [22]. HPV status was an independent prognostic factor in UVA and MVA analysis for LC, LRC, DFS and OS. Based on HPV status patients were migrated to the early stage, and hence, our findings confirm the prognostic importance of new TNM staging based on HPV status [10, 23].

A radiotherapy dose of 66–70 Gy/30–35#/6–7 weeks has been reported as an adequate dose for OPSCC [24]. Even though many de‐escalation studies are ongoing for HPV‐positive OPSCC [25, 26], HPV‐negative OPSCC still has locoregional failure as the most common pattern of failure [27] and doses less than 66 Gy may not be adequate for optimal disease control [5, 28]. We confirmed these findings in our study with worst outcomes in patients who have received less than 66 Gy as an independent prognostic factor for LC, LRC, DFS and OS. In fact, considering poor outcomes and patterns of failure, these HPV‐negative OPSCCs with a smoking history should be considered for dose escalation studies.

IMRT is a standard technique of care for RT in HNSCC [29, 30]. However, due to challenges of its availability and implementation, inequitable distribution of incidence of cancer and availability of resources, it may not be feasible to use IMRT for all patients, especially in low and low‐middle‐income countries. Only one‐fourth of patients in this study were treated using the IMRT. However, IMRT was an independent prognostic factor for all treatment outcomes (Figures [Link], [Link], [Link]). IMRT has shown the benefit of toxicity reduction in randomised controlled trials [31, 32, 33] and meta‐analysis [34], but there is no significant benefit of LRC and OS reported in HNSCC except in nasopharyngeal primaries. Beadle et al. have demonstrated the benefit of cause‐specific survival using IMRT in a SEER database of 3172 HNSCC patients, including 59% of oropharyngeal and oral cancer [35]. Many retrospective series have shown excellent outcomes using IMRT, but no randomised trial has shown benefit in clinical outcomes for OPSCC. When we stratified our patient based on staging to see the effect of the radiotherapy technique, the benefit was limited only to stage IV disease which may be due to better coverage of large nodal disease with IMRT than with conventional technique.

In our cohort, only 28 patients received brachytherapy restricted to highly selected, node negative cases hence LRC difference was not statistically significant. However, this remains an important area for prospective evaluation. A prospective trial of brachytherapy in oropharyngeal cancer has demonstrated a 16.7% improvement in LC, although this also did not reach statistical significance as sample size was not powered to see LRC benefit [36]. Brachytherapy was typically administered 1–2 weeks after EBRT, hence additional EQD2 dose of 2.75 Gy was given to compensate for increased OTT. Addition of radiation dose above 70 Gy in other four patients also was primarily used to compensate for treatment interruptions to account for accelerated repopulation hence we did not find any statistical difference in this very small subgroup.

The importance of OTT is well established [37, 38]. Treatment interruptions due to toxicities, supportive care needs, machine interruptions and daily travelling are well‐known causes of prolonged OTT. On UVA for 604 patients, DFS and OS were significantly better in patients who could complete their treatment within 49 days. However, in a subset of 445 patients receiving concurrent chemoradiotherapy, short treatment breaks (≤ 3 days) did not significantly impact LC, LRC, DFS, or OS. This finding suggests that concurrent chemotherapy may partially mitigate the adverse effects of short treatment breaks, possibly due to its radiosensitizing effect, and is consistent with prior reports in OPSCC [39]. Almost 20% of patients in our study group had an interruption of more than five days during treatment. GORTEC trial as well as MACH NC have shown the importance of adding chemotherapy in locally advanced oropharyngeal cancer [40, 41]. Similarly in our study addition of chemotherapy improved absolute OS by 15.7% at 5 years [HR 1.5 (95% CI 1.1–2.1)].

Although it is a retrospective series, we included consecutive patients who were treated with radical intent in a tertiary care centre. Along with HPV, this study highlights the importance of traditional prognostic factors such as habits, performance status, T stage, N stage, Group stage, subsite, addition of chemotherapy, radiotherapy dose and technique and OTT. Given the poor outcomes in predominantly HPV‐negative patients, the result of our study will be useful in developing a prognostic model and stratifying patients for treatment escalation studies. Along with these traditional prognostic factors, further advancements in understanding the molecular and genetic characteristics of oropharyngeal cancer hold promise for improved prognostication and the development of targeted therapies in the future [4, 42]. The limitations of our study include the predominant use of conventional radiotherapy techniques, missing data inherent to a retrospective design, selection bias in choosing treatment technique particularly IMRT and brachytherapy. Patients with documented recurrence or persistent disease who were subsequently lost to follow‐up despite reasonable attempts to contact were considered deceased at 6 months in our study. This could be considered as a limitation by some researchers. However, this approach avoids overestimation of the OS, gives outcome estimates in a worst‐case scenario and has been previously described in the literature [43]. This approach may give better approximation of real‐world outcomes in low and lower middle income settings where access to immunotherapy is often limited due to financial constraints [44].

Conclusion

Our study confirms the importance of various traditional prognostic factors such as tumour stage, nodal stage, concurrent chemotherapy, technique and dose of radiotherapy, and new prognostic factors such as HPV in clinical outcomes of oropharyngeal cancer.
