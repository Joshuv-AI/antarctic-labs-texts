First versus full or first versus last: U-statistic change-point tests under fixed and local alternatives
Herold Dehling, Daniel Vogel, Martin Wendler

Abstract
. The use of U-statistics in the change-point context has received considerable attention in the literature. We compare two approaches of constructing CUSUM-type change-point tests, which we call the first-vs-full and first-vs-last approach. Both have been pursued by different authors. The question naturally arises if the two tests substantially differ and, if so, which of them is better in which data situation. In large samples, both tests are similar: they are asymptotically equivalent under the null hypothesis and under sequences of local alternatives. In small samples, there may be quite noticeable differences, which is in line with a different asymptotic behavior under fixed alternatives. We derive a simple criterion for deciding which test is more powerful. We examine the examples Gini’s mean difference, the sample variance, and Kendall’s tau in detail. Particularly, when testing for changes in scale by Gini’s mean difference, we show that the first-vs-full approach has a higher power if and only if the scale changes from a smaller to a larger value – regardless of the population distribution or the location of the change. The asymptotic derivations are under weak dependence. The results are illustrated by numerical simulations and data examples.



1.
Introduction

The classical approach for testing the constancy of the mean of a time series
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
is the CUSUM test based on the test statistic

(1.1)

T_{n}=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\left|\bar{X}_{1:k}-\bar{X}_{1:n}\right|,

where
\bar{X}_{i:j}
denotes the sample mean computed from
X_{i},\ldots,X_{j}
for any two integers
i,j
such that
1\leq i<j\leq n
. The test statistic may equally be written as

(1.2)

T_{n}=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\frac{k(n-k)}{n^{3/2}}\left|\bar{X}_{1:k}-\bar{X}_{(k+1):n}\right|.

These two representations (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) suggest two different views of the CUSUM test statistic: one may either view it, for each
k
, as a comparison of the sample mean of the first part of the sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
to the sample mean of the whole sample or as a comparison of the mean of the first part to the mean of the remaining part
X_{k+1},\ldots,X_{n}
.
When the CUSUM approach is extended to a general setting, i.e., when we want to test the constancy of some parameter
\theta\in\mathds{R}
of the marginal distribution of the observed process, for which an estimator
\hat{\theta}_{n}
is available, we may consider either of the test statistics

T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{n})=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}kn^{-1/2}|\hat{\theta}_{1:k}-\hat{\theta}_{1:n}|

and

T_{2}(\hat{\theta}_{n})=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}k(n-k)n^{-3/2}|\hat{\theta}_{1:k}-\hat{\theta}_{(k+1):n}|

with
\hat{\theta}_{i:j}
being defined analogously to
\bar{X}_{i:j}
.
If
\hat{\theta}_{n}
is a
linear
statistic, i.e., if

(1.3)

\hat{\theta}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi(X_{i})

for some function
\xi
, both test statistics are identical, but in general they are not.
Both routes to generalizing the CUSUM test statistic,
first-vs-full
and
first-vs-last
, have been taken by different authors.
In the present paper, we analyze the difference between
T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{n})
and
T_{2}(\hat{\theta}_{n})
in the case
\hat{\theta}_{n}
is a U-statistic.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}}}
be a
p
-dimensional, stationary stochastic process. A one-sample U-statistic or order 2 is defined as

(1.4)

U_{n}=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}h(X_{i},X_{j}),

where
h:\mathds{R}^{p}\times\mathds{R}^{p}\to\mathds{R}
is a kernel function satisfying
h(x,y)=h(y,x)
.
In the case of i.i.d. data,
U_{n}
is an unbiased estimator of the parameter
\theta=E(h(X,Y))
, where
X,Y
are two independent random variables with the same marginal distribution as
X_{1}
. If the underlying data are weakly dependent,
U_{n}
is still a consistent estimator of
\theta
as long as the conditions of the U-statistics ergodic theorem are satisfied, see, e.g.,
1
.
Thus
U_{n}
can be used to test for stationarity of a process against the alternative of a change in the parameter
\theta
. Thus change-point tests based on U-statistics have been studied by many others, e.g.,
36
,
23
,
19
,
22
,
20
,
7
, and
29
. Tests for weakly dependent series have been considered by
6
and
10
.
Recently,
28
,
40
,
4
, and
46
consider the U-statistic-based change-point tests in the high-dimensional settings. Other lines of research are devoted to sequential (or
online
) change-point tests
(
21
;
25
, e.g.)
and the use of anti-symmetric (or
two-sample
) U-statistics
(
45
;
11
;
41
, e.g.)
. However, the latter is not pursued here. We restrict our attention to symmetric kernels in the offline (or
retrospective
) setting.

We define the
first-vs-full
and the
first-vs-last
U-statistic change-point tests based on the kernel
h
as

(1.5)

T_{1}(h)=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\left|U_{1:k}-U_{1:n}\right|,\qquad T_{2}(h)=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\frac{k(n-k)}{n^{3/2}}\left|U_{1:k}-U_{(k+1):n}\right|,

respectively, where

U_{k:l}=\frac{1}{\binom{l-k+1}{2}}\sum_{k\leq i<j\leq l}h(X_{i},X_{j}),

for any
1\leq k<l\leq n
. The former test statistic has been considered, e.g., by
10
and the latter by
6
.

A prominent example for a U-statistic is Gini’s mean difference with the kernel
h(x,y)=|x-y|
. The corresponding
first-vs-full
CUSUM test has been considered by
16
and was found to be a quite competitive change-point test for scale. Another popular U-statistic is Kendall’s tau. Its use in the change-point context has been studied in the
first-vs-last
version by
34
and in the
first-vs-full
version by
10
.
In fact, a discussion emerging during the review process of the latter paper has, to a large degree, prompted the research of the present paper. It has been argued that the
first-vs-last
version is presumably uniformly more powerful against one-sudden-change alternatives. However, this is not the case.

To illustrate the situation and to motivate all following derivations, we shall quote simulated powers for Gini’s mean difference (GMD) in a simple example setting. Consider a series
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
of univariate, independent and centered normal variables. At the beginning of the sequence, the observations have standard deviation 1 and at time
[n/3]
, the standard deviation changes to
\sigma>0
. We apply the studentized versions of the
first-vs-full
and the
first-vs-last
GMD test at the 5% significance level using the asymptotic null distribution and the variance estimator

\hat{\sigma}_{\rm GMD}^{2}=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|X_{i}-X_{j}|-g_{n}\Big)^{2},

where
g_{n}
is the sample GMD of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.
We observe the following:

(S1)

For sample size
n=4000
and
\sigma=1.08
, the
first-vs-full
test has a power of 0.79 and the
first-vs-last
test has a power of 0.79. Their relative difference in power is less than 0.1%. Under the null hypotheses, i.e.,
\sigma=1
, both test have a rejection frequency of 0.05.

(S2)

For sample size
n=60
and
\sigma=2
the
first-vs-full
test has a power of 0.70 and the
first-vs-last
test has a power of 0.61. If the variance changes from 1 to
0.5
, the two tests have a power of 0.65 and 0.71, respectively. In both cases, the relative difference is about 10%.
Under the null hypothesis, the size of both tests is between 0.03 and 0.04.

The rejection frequencies stated above are each based on 10,000 runs and rounded to two decimals. These numbers may serve as illustration for general principles which will be shown in this paper:

(A)

Both test statistics,
T_{1}(h)
and
T_{2}(h)
, have the same asymptotic distribution under the null hypothesis.

(B)

Both test statistics have, for any non-degenerate U-statistic kernel
h
, the same asymptotic distribution under local alternatives.

(C)

For sequences of fixed alternatives, the test statistics generally behave differently. It does depend on the specific alternative which test has a better power. Particularly, the ranking of the tests is determined by only three numbers:

(1.6)

\theta_{F}=E(h(X,X^{\prime})),\qquad\theta_{G}=E(h(Y,Y^{\prime})),\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\theta_{FG}=E(h(X,Y^{\prime})),

where
X,X^{\prime},Y,Y^{\prime}
are independent with
X,X^{\prime}\sim F
,
Y,Y^{\prime}\sim G
and
F
and
G
being the data distributions before and after the change, respectively.

(D)

Specifically for Gini’s mean difference, the
first-vs-full
test is always more powerful
if
\theta_{F}<\theta_{G}
, and it is less powerful if
\theta_{F}>\theta_{G}
.

(E)

Specifically for the sample variance, both tests are equally powerful unless there is a simultaneous change in mean. If there is a change in mean, the same relation as for Gini’s mean difference holds.

Particularly, (
B
) implies that in large samples the tests have a similar power, which is illustrated by (S
1
) above.
Likewise, (
C
) suggests that for smaller samples there may be substantial differences, as is illustrated by (S
2
).

The organization of the paper follows the outline above:
In Section
2.1
, we study both test statistics under the null hypothesis, in Section
2.2
under sequences of local alternatives.
Section
2.3
is devoted to fixed alternatives and the investigation of the differences of the tests.
Within the fixed-alternative scenario, we further derive results about the consistency of the change-point tests (Section

2.4
), the change-point location estimation (Section
2.5
), and the asymptotic normality of the test statistics (Section
2.6
).
In Section
3
, we examine several popular U-statistics: Gini’s mean difference, the sample variance, the sample covariance, and Kendall’s tau. We show in particular (
D
) and (
E
) and provide further simulation results.
Section
4
contains data examples, Section
5
concludes. All proofs are deferred to the appendix in supplementary material.

2.
Asymptotic results

We study the asymptotic behavior of both test statistics under the null hypothesis, under sequences of local alternatives, under sequences of fixed alternatives, and the asymptotic behavior of the corresponding maximizing points, which serve as estimators for the location of the change under one-change-point alternatives.
Throughout,
(X_{i})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}}}
is a
p
-dimensional, strongly stationary sequence with marginal distribution
F
and

h:\mathds{R}^{p}\times\mathds{R}^{p}\to\mathds{R}
a symmetric kernel function. We restrict our attention to real-valued kernels, but, as in the case of Kendall’s tau, the observations may be multivariate.
Define, for
n\in\mathds{N}
, the short-hand notation

(2.1)

D^{F}_{n}(k)=k\left(U_{1:k}-U_{1:n}\right),\qquad 2\leq k\leq n,

and

(2.2)

D^{L}_{n}(k)=\frac{k(n-k)}{n}\left(U_{1:k}-U_{(k+1):n}\right),\qquad 2\leq k\leq n,

for the weighted
D
ifferences processes of the first-vs-
F
ull and first-vs-
L
ast approach, respectively, so that the corresponding test statistics (
1.5
) can be written as

T_{1}(h)=n^{-1/2}\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|D^{F}_{n}(k)|
and

T_{2}(h)=n^{-1/2}\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|D^{L}_{n}(k)|
.

2.1.
Null hypothesis

The main tool for treating U-statistics asymptotically is the Hoeffding decomposition. For the stationary sequence
(X_{i})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}}}
, we write
U_{1:n}
as

U_{1:n}=\theta+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{1}\left(X_{i}\right)+\frac{2}{n\left(n-1\right)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}h_{2}\left(X_{i},X_{j}\right),

where

(2.3)

\theta=Eh(X,Y),\quad h_{1}(x)=Eh(x,Y)-\theta,

h_{2}(x,y)=h(x,y)-h_{1}(x)-h_{1}(y)-\theta,

and
X
,
Y
are independent copies of
X_{0}
.
We assume the following condition to hold.

Assumption 2.1
.

(1)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{[nt]}h_{1}(X_{i})\right)_{t\in[0,1]}\to\sigma_{h}W
in distribution in the Skorokhod space
D[0,1]

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, where
W
is a standard Brownian motion and

(2.4)

\sigma^{2}_{h}=4\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{Cov}(h_{1}(X_{0}),h_{1}(X_{k})).

(2)

\displaystyle E\bigg[\bigg(\max_{l\leq n}\Big|\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq l}h_{2}(X_{i},X_{j})\Big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg],\quad E\bigg[\bigg(\max_{l\leq n}\Big|\sum_{l\leq i<j\leq n}h_{2}(X_{i},X_{j})\Big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg]

are both
o(n^{3})
as
n\to\infty
.

Then we have the following result.

Theorem 2.2
.

If Assumption
2.1
is satisfied, then

(1)

\left(n^{-1/2}D^{F}_{n}([nt])\right)_{0\leq t\leq 1}\to\,\sigma_{h}B

in distribution in the Skorokhod space
D[0,1]
as
n\to\infty
, where
B
is a Brownian bridge on
[0,1]
and
\sigma_{h}
defined in (
2.4
).

(2)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left|D_{n}^{F}([nt])-D_{n}^{L}([nt])\right|\to 0
in probability as
n\to\infty
.

We have the following remarks.

Remark 2.3
.

(1)

Assumption
2.1
is a
high-level
condition, which is
usually
fulfilled, but where it is not necessarily obvious how it translates to explicit conditions on

(X_{i})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}}}
and
h
. One such set of conditions is that
(X_{i})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}}}
are independent and identically distributed and

Eh^{2}(X_{1},X_{2})<\infty
. For dependent sequences, more technical conditions are necessary. One weak set of conditions, allowing a broad class of short-range dependent processes as detailed in Section
A
of the Appendix, is given by Lemma
B.2
.

(2)

The theorem implies that both
\sigma_{h}^{-1}T_{1}(h)
and
\sigma_{h}^{-1}T_{2}(h)
converge to
\sup_{0\leq\lambda\leq 1}|B(\lambda)|
for a Brownian bridge
B
, i.e., the corresponding studentized tests have the same asymptotic behavior under the null and hence the same critical values. The estimation of
\sigma^{2}_{h}
for short-range dependent sequences has been treated by
10
. Alternatively, a bootstrap scenario for U-statistic processes has been considered by
6
.

2.2.
Local alternatives

We study local alternatives in the following set-up: As before,
(X_{i})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}}}
is a
p
-dimensional, strongly stationary process with marginal distribution
F
. Further consider an array of
p
-dimensional random variables
(Y_{i,n})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}},n\in\mathds{N}}
, where each row
(Y_{i,n})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}}}
for any fixed
n
is a stationary process with marginal distribution
G_{n}
.
We then construct an array
(X_{i,n})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}},n\in\mathds{N}}
as follows:
There is a fixed number
\tau^{\star}\in(0,1)
such that

X_{i,n}=\begin{cases}X_{i}&\ \text{ for }\,i\leq[n\tau^{\star}],\\
Y_{i,n}&\ \text{ for }\,i\geq[n\tau^{\star}]+1.\end{cases}

The observed data in the local-alternative setting is then the triangular array
(X_{i,n})_{1\leq i\leq n,n\in\mathds{N}}
obtained as a cut-out from
(X_{i,n})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}},n\in\mathds{N}}
. This means, e.g., the processes
D_{n}^{F}
and
D_{n}^{L}
, defined in (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), respectively, are henceforth, for each
n\in\mathds{N}
, computed on
X_{1,n},\ldots,X_{n,n}
instead of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.
We adopt the convention to omit the commas separating several indices if there is no ambiguity. The reason for this slight misuse of notation will become apparent in Assumption
2.5
below. For instance,
X_{in}
is short for
X_{i,n}
and
h_{2ijn}
is short for
h_{2,i,j,n}
.
Let furthermore

\theta_{F}=E(h(X,X^{\prime})),\quad\theta_{G_{n}}=E(h(Y,Y^{\prime})),\quad\theta_{FG_{n}}=E(h(X,Y^{\prime})),

and

\rho_{FG_{n}}=\theta_{FG_{n}}-(\theta_{F}+\theta_{G_{n}})/2,

where
X,X^{\prime},Y,Y^{\prime}
are independent with
X,X^{\prime}\sim F
,
Y,Y^{\prime}\sim G_{n}
.
The idea of the
local
alternative is that
G_{n}
approaches
F
is some suitable sense as
n\to\infty
.

Assumption 2.4
.

Let
F
and
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}}
be such that, for
n\to\infty
,

(1)

\sqrt{n}(\theta_{F}-\theta_{G_{n}})\to\Delta
for some
\Delta\in\mathds{R}
and

(2)

\sqrt{n}\rho_{FG_{n}}\to 0
.

We further require technical conditions on the kernel
h
. Let
(\tilde{X}_{in})_{1\leq i\leq n,n\in\mathds{N}}
be an independent copy of the triangular scheme

(X_{in})_{1\leq i\leq n,n\in\mathds{N}}
and define further

\displaystyle\theta_{ijn}

\displaystyle=E\left[h(X_{in},\tilde{X}_{jn})\right],

(2.5)

\displaystyle h_{1ijn}(x)

\displaystyle=E\left[h(x,X_{jn})\right]-\theta_{ijn},

\displaystyle h_{2ijn}(x,y)

\displaystyle=h(x,y)-E\left[h(X_{in},y)\right]-E\left[h(x,X_{jn})\right]+\theta_{ijn}.

These terms constitute the Hoeffding decomposition when the underlying data generating process is a triangular array.

Assumption 2.5
.

Let
(X_{in})_{1\leq i\leq n,n\in\mathds{N}}
and
h
be such that

(1)

\displaystyle E\bigg[\bigg(\max_{2\leq l\leq n}\Big|\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq l}h_{2ijn}(X_{in},X_{jn})\Big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg],\quad E\bigg[\bigg(\max_{2\leq l\leq n}\Big|\sum_{l\leq i<j\leq n}h_{2ijn}(X_{in},X_{jn})\Big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg]

are
o(n^{3})
for
n\to\infty
, and

(2)

the four terms

\frac{1}{n}E\bigg(\max_{k\leq n}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\{h_{1i1n}(X_{in})-h_{1}(X_{i})\}\Big|\bigg)^{2},\quad\frac{1}{n}E\bigg(\max_{k\leq n}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\{h_{1inn}(X_{in})-h_{1}(X_{i})\}\Big|\bigg)^{2},

\frac{1}{n^{3}}E\bigg(\max_{k\leq n}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{k}i\{h_{1i1n}(X_{in})-h_{1}(X_{i})\}\Big|\bigg)^{2},\ \frac{1}{n^{3}}E\bigg(\max_{k\leq n}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{k}i\{h_{1inn}(X_{in})-h_{1}(X_{i})\}\Big|\bigg)^{2}

all converge to zero as
n\to\infty
.

We then have the following process convergence result in the local-alternative set-up.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
(1),
2.4
, and
2.5
hold. Then

(1)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}D^{F}_{n}([nt])-\phi(t)\right)_{0\leq t\leq 1}\to\,\sigma_{h}B

in distribution in the Skorokhod space
D[0,1]
as
n\to\infty
, where
B
is a Brownian bridge on
[0,1]
,
\sigma_{h}

as in Theorem
2.2
, and

\phi(t)=\begin{cases}t(1-\tau^{\star})\Delta&\text{ for }t<\tau^{\star}\\
(1-t)\tau^{\star}\Delta&\text{ for }t\geq\tau^{\star}\end{cases}

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left|D_{n}^{F}([nt])-D_{n}^{L}([nt])\right|\to 0
in probability as
n\to\infty
.

Assumption
2.4
captures the common notion of a series of local alternatives to approach the null hypothesis at a

(1/\sqrt{n})
-rate.
Assumption
2.5
is a technical regularity condition. Easier-to-verify conditions for independent and dependent data sequences
are given by Lemmas
B.5
and
B.6
, respectively, of Section
B.3
in the supplementary material.

The crucial assumption here is
2.4
(2). In many standard situations, Assumption
2.4
(2) is fulfilled if

2.4
(1) holds. In Proposition
2.7
, we treat the case that
F
and
G_{n}
have densities
f
and
g_{n}
, respectively, belonging to a one-parameter family
p_{\lambda}(x)
,
\lambda\in\Lambda
, where
\Lambda\subset\mathds{R}
is an interval.
We assume that the function
\lambda\mapsto p_{\lambda}(x)
is differentiable for almost all
x
, and we denote its partial derivative by
\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}p_{\lambda}(x)
. Let
\lambda_{0}
be an interior point of
\Lambda
, let
a\in\mathds{R}
, and consider the densities

f(x)=p_{\lambda_{0}}(x),\qquad g_{n}(x)=p_{\lambda_{0}+a/\sqrt{n}}(x).

Then, under some technical conditions, Assumption
2.4
is satisfied.

Proposition 2.7
.

Suppose there is a measurable function
v(x)
such that for some
\epsilon>0

(2.6)

\Big|p_{\lambda}(x)\Big|+\Big|\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}p_{\lambda}(x)\Big|\leq v(x)\ \mbox{ for all }\ \lambda\in(\lambda_{0}-\epsilon,\lambda_{0}+\epsilon)

and such that

(2.7)

\iint|h(x,y)|v(x)\,v(y)\,dx\,dy<\infty

Then Assumption
2.4
is satisfied for the distributions
F
and
G_{n}
with densities
f(x)
and
g_{n}(x)
as defined above, and with

\Delta=-2\,a\,\iint h(x,y)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}p_{\lambda_{0}}(x)\,p_{\lambda_{0}}(x)\,dx.

This covers in particular the Gaussian scale family, which we use extensively in the simulations in Section
3
, see Example
B.3
in the Appendix.

Theorems
2.2
and
2.6
together state the two tests, the
first-vs-full
and the
first-vs-last
, are
asymptotically equivalent
, i.e., for any array
(X_{in})
of random variables falling either under the null hypothesis or a local alternative, the probability of a differing test decision tends to zero as the sample size tends to infinity.

2.3.
Fixed alternatives

So far we have noted that the two U-statistic change-point test statistics are asymptotically equivalent. Nevertheless, noteworthy differences in power are observed in simulations. The question remains if these differences can be backed by theoretical results and if conditions can be given to identify situations where one test is better than the other.
Toward this end, we consider sequences of fixed alternatives.
Let
(X_{i})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}}}
and
(Y_{i})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}}}
be two
p
-dimensional, strongly stationary sequences with marginal distributions
F
and
G
, respectively.
We further require the
2p
-dimensional process
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}}}
to be strongly stationary.
Then the data array
(X_{in})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}},n\in\mathds{N}}
is constructed analogously to the local-alternative set-up:

X_{in}=\begin{cases}X_{i}&\ \text{ for }\,i\leq[n\tau^{\star}],\\
Y_{i}&\ \text{ for }\,i\geq[n\tau^{\star}]+1\end{cases}

for some fixed
\tau^{\star}\in(0,1)
. The observed data is, as before, the triangular cut-out
(X_{in})_{1\leq i\leq n,n\in\mathds{N}}
.
Let
(\tilde{X}_{in})_{1\leq i\leq n,n\in\mathds{N}}
be an independent copy of
(X_{in})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}},n\in\mathds{N}}
.
Recall the definitions of
\theta_{F}
,
\theta_{G}
, and
\theta_{FG}
, cf. (
1.6
), and let

(2.8)

\rho_{FG}=\theta_{FG}-(\theta_{F}+\theta_{G})/2.

We call
\rho_{FG}
the
eccentricity
of
\theta_{FG}
as it describes the deviation of the mixed parameter

\theta_{FG}
from the midpoint between
\theta_{F}
and
\theta_{G}
. This offset will play a crucial role in the following: it determines the power ranking of the test.

Below we employ again the notation
\theta_{ijn}
,
h_{1ijn}(x)
, and
h_{2ijn}(x,y)
, as defined in (
2.2
), but keep in mind that the arrays
(X_{in})
and
(\tilde{X}_{in})
are now defined slightly different than in Section
2.2
.

Assumption 2.8
.

Let
(X_{in})_{1\leq i\leq n,n\in\mathds{N}}
and
h
be such that

(1)

the terms

E\bigg[\bigg(\max_{l\leq n}\Big|\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq l}h_{2ijn}(X_{in},X_{jn})\Big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg],\quad E\bigg[\bigg(\max_{l\leq n}\Big|\sum_{l\leq i<j\leq n}h_{2ijn}(X_{in},X_{jn})\Big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg]

are
o(n^{4})
for
n\to\infty
, and

(2)

the eight terms

(2.9)

E\bigg[\bigg(\max_{l\leq n}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{l}h_{1i1n}(X_{in})\Big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg],\quad E\bigg[\bigg(\max_{l\leq n}\Big|\frac{1}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{l}ih_{1i1n}(X_{in})\Big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg],

(2.10)

E\bigg[\bigg(\max_{l\leq n}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{l}h_{1inn}(X_{in})\Big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg],\quad E\bigg[\bigg(\max_{l\leq n}\Big|\frac{1}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{l}ih_{1inn}(X_{in})\Big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg],

(2.11)

E\bigg[\bigg(\max_{l\leq n}\Big|\sum_{i=l+1}^{n}h_{1i1n}(X_{in})\Big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg],\quad E\bigg[\bigg(\max_{l\leq n}\Big|\frac{1}{n-l}\sum_{i=l+1}^{n}(n-i+1)h_{1i1n}(X_{in})\Big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg],

(2.12)

E\bigg[\bigg(\max_{l\leq n}\Big|\sum_{i=l+1}^{n}h_{1inn}(X_{in})\Big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg],\quad E\bigg[\bigg(\max_{l\leq n}\Big|\frac{1}{n-l}\sum_{i=l+1}^{n}(n-i+1)h_{1inn}(X_{in})\Big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg]

are all
o(n^{2})
for
n\to\infty
.

Similarly to Assumption
2.5
in the previous section, Assumption
2.8
is a general technical condition. For independent data, it greatly simplifies, see Lemma
B.7
. Analogous conditions for dependent data are given in Lemma

B.8
.
Then we have the following result concerning the behavior of the change-point tests under fixed alternatives.

Theorem 2.9
.

Under the Assumption
2.8
we have

(1)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\frac{1}{n}D_{n}^{F}([nt])-\Psi_{1}(t)\right|\to 0

in probability as
n\to\infty
, where

\Psi_{1}(t)=\begin{cases}t(1-\tau^{\star})(\theta_{F}-\theta_{G})-2t\tau^{\star}(1-\tau^{\star})\rho_{FG}&\text{ for }t<\tau^{\star},\\
(1-t)\tau^{\star}(\theta_{F}-\theta_{G})+2\frac{t-\tau^{\star}}{t}\tau^{\star}\rho_{FG}-2t\tau^{\star}(1-\tau^{\star})\rho_{FG}&\text{ for }t\geq\tau^{\star}.\end{cases}

(2)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\frac{1}{n}D_{n}^{L}([nt])-\Psi_{2}(t)\right|\to 0

in probability as
n\to\infty
, where

\Psi_{2}(t)=\begin{cases}t(1-\tau^{\star})(\theta_{F}-\theta_{G})-2\frac{t(\tau^{\star}-t)}{1-t}(1-\tau^{\star})\rho_{FG}&\text{ for }t<\tau^{\star},\\
(1-t)\tau^{\star}(\theta_{F}-\theta_{G})+2\frac{(t-\tau^{\star})}{t}\tau^{\star}\rho_{FG}-2(t-\tau^{\star})\tau^{\star}\rho_{FG}&\text{ for }t\geq\tau^{\star}.\end{cases}

As the limit distribution under the null-hypothesis is the same, the different limits under fixed alternatives give information about the differences in power for finite samples. A short calculation yields

\Psi_{2}(t)-\Psi_{1}(t)=\begin{cases}2\displaystyle\frac{t^{2}(1-\tau^{\star})^{2}}{1-t}\rho_{FG}\ \ &\text{ for }t<\tau^{\star},\\[6.45831pt]
2(1-t)(\tau^{\star})^{2}\rho_{FG}&\text{ for }t\geq\tau^{\star}.\end{cases}

As
\Psi_{2}(t)-\Psi_{1}(t)
always equals
\rho_{FG}
times a positive factor, the sign of the eccentricity
\rho_{FG}
is essential for the power comparison. We arrive at the following criterion:

(1)

If
\rho_{FG}
has the same sign as
\theta_{G}-\theta_{F}
, the absolute value of
\Psi_{1}
is larger than the absolute value of
\Psi_{2}
,
and we expect the
first-vs-full
test to be more powerful.

(2)

If
\rho_{FG}
and
\theta_{G}-\theta_{F}
have opposite signs, the absolute value of
\Psi_{1}
is
smaller
than the absolute value of
\Psi_{2}
, and we expect the
first-vs-last
test to be more powerful.

(3)

If
\rho_{FG}=0
, the two functions
\Psi_{1}
and
\Psi_{2}
are equal. We expect both tests to be equally powerful.

Thus the sign of
\rho_{FG}
relative to
\theta_{G}-\theta_{F}
determines the power ranking of the tests. Consequently, there is no kernel
h
for which one of the tests is strictly better than the other for all
F
and
G
, since
\rho_{FG}
is symmetric in
F
and
G
and the ranking of the tests can hence be reversed by switching
F
and
G
. Moreover, both tests are equally powerful for all
F
and
G
if and only if
U_{n}
is a strictly linear statistic. This is the content of the next lemma.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
h:\mathds{R}^{p}\times\mathds{R}^{p}\to\mathds{R}
be a symmetric, measurable kernel function.
If
\rho_{FG}=0
for all
p
-dimensional distribution functions
F
and
G
for which
\theta_{F}
,
\theta_{G}
and
\theta_{FG}
exist, then
the U-statistic
U_{n}
defined by (
1.4
) is a linear statistic in the sense of (
1.3
) with

\xi(x)=h(x,x)
for
x\in\mathds{R}^{p}
and hence
D^{F}_{n}=D^{L}_{n}
.

2.4.
Consistency

Based on Theorem
2.9
we show that the change-point tests based on
D_{n}^{F}
and
D_{n}^{L}
are consistent. Under the null hypothesis, we have that

T_{1}(h)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|D^{F}_{n}(k)|=O_{P}(1),\qquad T_{2}(h)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|D^{L}_{n}(k)|=O_{P}(1)

by Theorem
2.2
.
If, under a fixed alternative,
\Psi_{1}(t)
and
\Psi_{2}(t)
are not 0 for a least one
t\in[0,1]
, the corresponding test statistics will converge to infinity, so that the tests are consistent. This is summarized in the following corollary.

Corollary 2.11
.

If the assumptions of Theorem
2.9
and one of the two conditions

(1)

\theta_{F}\neq\theta_{G}
,

(2)

\rho_{FG}\neq 0
,

hold, then

T_{1}(h)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|D^{F}_{n}(k)|\to\infty,\qquad T_{2}(h)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|D^{L}_{n}(k)|\to\infty

in probability as
n\to\infty
.

Corollary
2.11
is an affirmative result of a common property of the tests: Both are constructed for detecting changes in the parameter
\theta
, and, if this parameter differs before and after the change-point, both will detect that change asymptotically with power 1. However, Corollary
2.11
contains another important information: if a change in the series occurs, but the change is such that the parameter
\theta
remains constant, both tests will detect this change asymptotically with power 1
if and only if
the eccentricity
\rho_{FG}
is non-zero. Some implications of this result are illustrated in Section
3.2
.

2.5.
Estimation of change location

If the hypothesis is rejected, it is natural to estimate the change-point by

(2.13)

\hat{\tau}_{1,n}=\operatorname*{argmax}_{t\in[0,1]}\left|D_{n}^{F}([nt])\right|\quad\mbox{ or }\quad\hat{\tau}_{2,n}=\operatorname*{argmax}_{t\in[0,1]}\left|[D_{n}^{L}([nt])\right|.

If
\Psi_{1}
respectively
\Psi_{2}
have their maximal absolute value at
\tau^{\star}
and this maximal point is unique,
consistency of these estimators follows by the argmax theorem
(e.g.
38
, Corollary 3.2.3)
.
The following proposition states technical conditions for
D_{n}^{F}
and
D_{n}^{L}
.

Proposition 2.12
.

(1)

If
\theta_{F}>\theta_{G}
and

\frac{\theta_{G}-\theta_{F}}{2}<\rho_{FG}<\frac{\theta_{F}-\theta_{G}}{2(\tau^{\star}-1+1/\tau^{\star})},

then
|\Psi_{1}(t)|<|\Psi_{1}(\tau^{\star})|
for all
t\in[0,1]
with
t\neq\tau^{\star}
.
The same is true if
\theta_{F}<\theta_{G}
and

\frac{\theta_{F}-\theta_{G}}{2(\tau^{\star}-1+1/\tau^{\star})}<\rho_{FG}<\frac{\theta_{G}-\theta_{F}}{2}.

(2)

If
\theta_{F}\neq\theta_{G}
and
|\rho_{FG}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\min\{\frac{\tau^{\star}}{1-\tau^{\star}},\frac{1-\tau^{\star}}{\tau^{\star}}\}|\theta_{F}-\theta_{G}|
, then for all
t\in[0,1]
with
t\neq\tau^{\star}

|\Psi_{2}(t)|<|\Psi_{2}(\tau^{\star})|.

Similar to the previous two results, the implications of Proposition
2.12
are threefold:
First, in a wide range of realistic application cases, both tests have the desired property and behave alike (consistency of the argmax location estimator). Second, there is no general superiority of one test over the other: neither set of conditions in parts (1) and (2) of Proposition
2.12
implies the other. And lastly, the decisive criterion is again the eccentricity
\rho_{FG}
(it must not be too large in absolute value), and one can hence easily construct alternatives where the argmax estimator is consistent and alternatives where it is not, see Section
3.2
below and Example
C.2
in the supplementary material.

2.6.
Asymptotic distribution under fixed alternatives

From Theorem
2.9
and Proposition
2.12
we know that, under fixed alternatives, the
(1/\sqrt{n})
-scaled test statistics

n^{-1/2}T_{1}(h)
and
n^{-1/2}T_{2}(h)
converge in probability to different values
\Psi_{1}(\tau^{\star})
and
\Psi_{2}(\tau^{\star})
, respectively.
To complete the asymptotic analysis of the
first-vs-full
and the
first-vs-last
approach, we study the asymptotic distribution of the two test statistics within the fixed-alternative set-up. We demonstrate that they differ not only in mean, but we also obtain asymptotic variances that are generally different. This becomes apparent for univariate and independent data sequences and we restrict our derivations to this scenario in the following.

Using the notation introduced at the beginning of Section
2.3
define further

h_{F}(x)=\int h(x,y)\,dF(y),\qquad h_{G}(x)=\int h(x,y)\,dG(y).

Theorem 2.13
.

Let the triangular array
(X_{in})_{1\leq i\leq n,n\in\mathds{N}}
be row-wise independent, and let
the assumptions of Theorem
2.9
and Proposition
2.12
be satisfied. Let further the kernel
h:\mathds{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathds{R}
be such that
\theta_{F}\neq\theta_{G}
and the integrals

\iint h^{2}(x,y)dF(x)dF(Y),\quad\iint h^{2}(x,y)dF(x)dG(Y),\quad\iint h^{2}(x,y)dG(x)dG(Y)

are all finite.
Then

\big(T_{1}(h)-\sqrt{n}|\Psi_{1}(\tau^{\star})|\big)\rightarrow Z_{1},\quad\big(T_{2}(h)-\sqrt{n}|\Psi_{2}(\tau^{\star})|\big)\rightarrow Z_{2}

in distribution as
n\to\infty
, where
Z_{1}
,
Z_{2}
are centered normal random variables with variances

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[Z_{1}]

\displaystyle=4\tau^{\star}\mathrm{Var}[h_{F}(X)-\tau^{\star}h_{G}(X)]+4\tau^{\star 2}(1-\tau^{\star})\mathrm{Var}[h_{G}(Y)],

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[Z_{2}]

\displaystyle=4\tau^{\star}(1-\tau^{\star})^{2}\mathrm{Var}[h_{F}(X)]+4\tau^{\star 2}(1-\tau^{\star})\mathrm{Var}[h_{G}(Y)]

for a random variable
X
with distribution function
F
and a random variable
Y
with distribution function
G
.

If we have a strictly linear U-statistic, i.e.,
h(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}(h(x,x)+h(y,y))
, both test statistics are equal, and should have the same limit variance.
This is indeed the case: In this situation,
h_{F}(x)=\frac{1}{2}h(x,x)-E[\frac{1}{2}h(X,X)]
and
h_{G}(x)=\frac{1}{2}h(x,x)-E[\frac{1}{2}h(Y,Y)]
, so
\mathrm{Var}[h_{F}(X)-\tau^{\star}h_{G}(X)]=\mathrm{Var}[(1-\tau^{\star})h_{F}(X)]=(1-\tau^{\star})^{2}\mathrm{Var}[h_{F}(X)]
and consequently
\mathrm{Var}[Z_{1}]=\mathrm{Var}[Z_{2}]
.
This argument does not apply if the U-statistic is not linear, and the asymptotic variances are generally different.

3.
Examples

We examine four well-known U-statistics in detail: Gini’s mean difference, the sample variance, the sample covariance, and Kendall’s tau.
The former two can be used to detect changes in the scale of univariate series. So in Sections
3.1
and
3.2
we assume the data series to be univariate with distributions
F
and
G
before and after the potential change-point. In case
F
and
G
possess Lebesgue densities
f
and
g
, respectively, we have the following representation of the eccentricity
\rho_{FG}
, which will be employed in Proposition
3.1
.

(3.1)

\rho_{FG}=-\frac{1}{2}\int\int h(x,y)\{f(x)-g(x)\}\{f(y)-g(y)\}dxdy.

3.1.
Gini’s mean difference

We consider the kernel
h(x,y)=|x-y|
, leading to Gini’s mean difference

U_{1:n}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|X_{i}-X_{j}|

with

\theta_{F}=E|X-X^{\prime}|
,

\theta_{G}=E|Y-Y^{\prime}|
, and
\theta_{FG}=E|X-Y|
,
for
X,X^{\prime},Y,Y^{\prime}
being independent with
X,X^{\prime}\sim F
,
Y,Y^{\prime}\sim G
. For the special case of normal distributions, specifically

F=N(0,1)
and
G=N(0,\sigma^{2})
for some
\sigma>0
, a quick calculation yields

\theta_{F}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}},\quad\theta_{G}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sigma,\quad\theta_{FG}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sigma^{2})}

and hence
\rho_{FG}=\theta_{FG}-(\theta_{F}+\theta_{G})/2\geq 0
. We find the eccentricity is always positive for
\sigma\neq 1
. This holds indeed generally.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let the univariate distributions
F
and
G
with Lebesgue densities
f
and
g
, respectively, have finite first moments.
Then, for the kernel
h(x,y)=|x-y|
, we have
\rho_{FG}\geq 0
.

Consequently, the power ranking of both change-point tests is solely determined by the order of
\theta_{F}
and
\theta_{G}
.
Following our criterion from Section
2.3
, the
first-vs-full
test is more powerful than the
first-vs-last
test if
\theta_{G}-\theta_{F}>0
, i.e., if the scale of the series changes from a smaller value to a larger one. The
first-vs-full
is less powerful than the
first-vs-last
if the scale decreases after the change. This is regardless of where the change occurs and of any other aspect of
F
and
G
such as, e.g., a simultaneous change in location.

Also, we find in the normal examples that
\theta_{FG}
is always between
\theta_{F}
and
\theta_{G}
. This is also generally true for median-centered sequences.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
h(x,y)=|x-y|
and
X\sim F
with
E|X|<\infty
and
F(0)=1/2
, i.e,
X
is median-centered. Furthermore, for
c>0
, we denote by
G=G_{c}
the distribution function of
cX
. Then
\theta_{FG}\in[\min\{\theta_{F},\theta_{G}\},\max\{\theta_{F},\theta_{G}\}]

3.2.
The sample variance

The sample variance is obtained by the kernel
h(x,y)=(x-y)^{2}/2
, leading to the U-statistic

U_{1:n}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(X_{i}-X_{j})^{2}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\bar{X}_{1:n})^{2}

Also, in this case, the eccentricity is strictly non-negative. Specifically, we have the following result.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
F
and
G
have finite second moment. Then for
h(x,y)=(x-y)^{2}/2
we have

\rho_{FG}=\frac{1}{2}\big(EX-EY\big)^{2}.

Despite being straightforward to show, Proposition
3.3
is a very remarkable result and has – in light of the derivation in Section
2
– a number of interesting implications.

The change-in-variance problem has received some considerable attention. It has been studied in varying degrees of generality, e.g., by

24
,
18
,
42
and
16
. A robustified version based on trimming has been proposed by
27
, and the multivariate extension is due to
2
.
None of these authors consider the sample variance explicitly in the U-statistic context, and none of the authors discuss or draw any connection between the two different construction principles, presumably due to the sample variance being an
essentially linear
statistic. It would be
exactly linear
if the sample mean was replaced by the population mean.
Indeed, Proposition
3.3
suggests that, for any alternative, as long as the mean does not change from
F
to
G
, both tests are equally powerful.

Much more interesting is what happens if there is a change in mean at the same time as the change in variance.
It is a general consensus that it is advisable to apply changes-for-variance tests (or generally scale) to centered sequences, i.e., applying a de-trending step beforehand. This having said, it may seem intuitive that the
first-vs-last
test is less effected by a change in mean.
However, the behavior of both tests under a change in mean is qualitatively very similar. Since, under a change in mean, the eccentricity
\rho_{FG}
is always positive, we have the same picture as for Gini’s mean difference. Either test can be more powerful than the other, and the ranking is solely determined by the order of
\theta_{F}
and
\theta_{G}
. If
\theta_{F}<\theta_{G}
, i.e., the variance increases from
F
to
G
, the
first-vs-full
test is more powerful. If
\theta_{F}>\theta_{G}
, the
first-vs-last
test is more powerful. This holds regardless of the direction of the change in mean or where the change occurs.

By Corollary
2.11
, we have that, as long as there is a change in variance, both tests are consistent.
Corollary
2.11
tells further that, if there is
no
change in variance, a change in mean renders both tests consistent as well. The same holds true for Gini’s mean difference, see Example
C.1
.

Furthermore, by Proposition
2.12
, one can easily construct fixed alternatives where both argmax change location estimators are consistent and fixed alternatives where both are
not
consistent, simply by making the difference of the means sufficiently small or large compared to the difference of the variances. Incidentally, this is also similarly true for Gini’s mean difference, see Example
C.2
.

Last but not least, one can use Proposition
3.3
to construct interesting sequences of
local
alternatives. For instance, consider the sequence of local alternatives

F=N(0,1)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad G_{n}=N(n^{-1/4}\mu,\,1+n^{-1/2}\Delta)

for any real numbers
\mu,\Delta\neq 0
.
This sequence satisfies Assumption
2.4
(1), i.e.,

3.3.
Sample covariance

The sample covariance

U_{1:n}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(X_{i}-X_{j})(Y_{i}-Y_{j})=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\bar{X}_{1:n})(Y_{i}-\bar{Y}_{1:n})

of a sequence of bivariate random variables
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
is a U-statistic with kernel

h((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2}))=(x_{2}-x_{1})(y_{2}-y_{1})/2
. Letting
F
and
G
be the bivariate marginal distributions before and after the change-point, respectively, we have similarly to Proposition
3.3

\rho_{FG}=\frac{1}{2}(E_{F}X-E_{G}X)(E_{F}Y-E_{G}Y)

if
F
and
G
have finite second moments. The calculation can be found in Appendix
C
along with the proof of Proposition
3.3
.
Hence much of what has been said about the sample variance is analogously true for the sample covariance. We turn our attention to another measure of association in the following.

3.4.
Kendall’s tau

Now
F
and
G
are bivariate distribution functions.

(3.2)

h((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2}))=\mathds{1}\mbox{\Large${}_{\{(x_{2}-x_{1})(y_{2}-y_{1})>0\}}$}-\mathds{1}\mbox{\Large${}_{\{(x_{2}-x_{1})(y_{2}-y_{1})<0\}}$}.

.

Let

and

be such that

\rho_{FG}=0

5.
Conclusion

We studied two construction principles of generalized CUSUM test statistics: For each potential change-point
k
, the
first-vs-full
approach compares the estimate
\hat{\theta}_{1:k}

evaluated at the first
k
observations to the estimate
\hat{\theta}_{1:n}
evaluated at the whole sample, whereas the
first-vs-last
approach compares
\hat{\theta}_{1:k}
to the estimate
\hat{\theta}_{(k+1):n}
evaluated at the remaining
n-k
observations.

Within the huge body of literature devoted the change-point problem, authors usually consider one the of two approaches without any mention of the respective other or a motivation of their choice. An exception is
36
, who remarks that they are asymptotically equivalent.
When discussing the question with colleagues, we encountered two opinion camps: Either it is assumed that both approaches are generally very similar (as they are identical for the original CUSUM test with the sample mean) or it is expected that the
first-vs-last
is generally more powerful.

In this paper, we give a thorough comparison of the two approaches for the class of U-statistics. It turns out that the answer is not as unanimous as repeatedly believed:
First, the two test approaches have common properties: Under the null hypothesis and local alternatives, where the change-point processes
(D^{F}_{n}([nt]))_{0\leq t\leq 1}
and
(D^{L}_{n}([nt]))_{0\leq t\leq 1}
are scaled with
1/\sqrt{n}
, they have the same limit. This can be summarized by saying, they are asymptotically equivalent.

However, when scaling with
1/n
in the fixed-alternatives setting, different limits appear, which is reflected by power differences in small to moderate sample sizes.
We identify a simple criterion to determine which test is more powerful in which situation:
the sign of the eccentricity
\rho_{FG}
relative to the sign of
\theta_{G}-\theta_{F}
.
The eccentricity
\rho_{FG}
of the kernel
h
at distributions
F
and
G
describes the offset of the mixed parameter
\theta_{FG}
from the center of the interval between
\theta_{F}
and
\theta_{G}
.
Consequently, since the eccentricity is symmetric in
F
and
G
, we have that, for any alternative where
first-vs-full
is more powerful, an equally relevant alternative can be given where
first-vs-last
is more powerful by the same order of magnitude – by simply exchanging
F
and
G
.

The ensuing question for future research is if similar criteria can be given for other classes of statistics, e.g., quantile-based estimators (e.g. median, interquartile range), rank-based statistics (e.g. Spearman’s rho), or the large class of differentiable functionals of statistics.

Appendix A
A general, moment-free framework for dependent series

Definition A.1

(Absolute regularity)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},P)
be a probability space.

(1)

For two sub-
\sigma
-fields
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathscr{F}
, we define the absolute regularity coefficient

\beta(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=E\left[\esssup\left\{|P(A|\mathcal{B})-P(A)|\,:\,A\in\mathcal{A}\right\}\right].

The absolute regularity coefficient is a measure of dependence between the
\sigma
-fields
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, it lies between
0
and
1
, and equals 0 if
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are independent.

(2)

Let
(Z_{n})_{n\in{\mathds{Z}}}
be an
r
-variate, strongly stationary stochastic processes on
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},P)
. For
k\leq n
, let
\mathscr{F}_{k}^{n}=\sigma(Z_{k},\ldots,Z_{n})
, where also
k=-\infty
and
n=\infty
are permitted. The process
(Z_{n})_{n\in{\mathds{Z}}}
is called
absolutely regular
if the absolute regularity coefficients

\beta_{k}=\beta(\mathscr{F}^{0}_{\!\!-\infty},\mathscr{F}_{k}^{\infty}),\qquad k\geq 1,

converge to zero as
k\to\infty
.

Absolute regularity is also referred to as
\beta
-mixing and goes back to
39
. It is a mixing condition stronger than
\alpha
-mixing and weaker than
\phi
-mixing. However, we do not consider absolutely regular processes themselves, but approximating functionals of such absolutely regular processes. Specifically, we use the concept of near epoch dependence in probability (
P
-NED). It was introduced by
10
and generalizes the
L_{2}
-NED concept. It is as general as
L_{2}
-NED concerning the serial dependence, but imposes no moment conditions. This is useful for bounded U-statistics like Kendall’s tau. Their asymptotics are valid without any moment assumptions, and
P
-NED provides a framework for weakly dependent and very heavy-tailed sequences.

Definition A.2
.

Let
(Z_{in})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}},n\in\mathds{N}}
be an array of
r
-variate stochastic processes (
n
denoting the row index).
The array
(X_{in})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}},n\in\mathds{N}}
of
p
-variate stochastic processes is called
near epoch dependent in probability
or short
P
-near epoch dependent
(
P
-NED) on the array
(Z_{in})_{n\in{\mathds{Z}}}
if there is a sequence of approximating constants
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathds{N}}
with
a_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
, functions
f_{n,i,k}:\mathds{R}^{r\times(2k+1)}\to\mathds{R}^{p}
,
k\in\mathds{N}
, and a non-increasing function
\Phi:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that

(A.1)

P\left(\left|X_{in}-f_{n,i,k}(Z_{i-k,n},\ldots,Z_{i+k,n})\right|_{1}>\varepsilon\right)\ \leq\ a_{k}\Phi(\varepsilon)\\

for all
k,n\in\mathds{N}
,
i\in{\mathds{Z}}
and
\varepsilon>0
. Here
|x|_{1}
denotes the
L_{1}
-norm of the vector
x\in\mathds{R}^{p}
.

For the most part of Section
2
of the paper we consider triangular arrays of processes. Hence we define
P
-NED for arrays, noting that this includes
P
-NED sequences as special case by simply taking all rows identical.

For the various functional limit theorems for the processes
D^{F}_{n}
and
D^{L}_{n}
, cf. (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), respectively, in the main paper, we require the following technical Assumptions
A.3
,
A.4
, and
A.5
on the data array
(X_{in})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}},n\in\mathds{N}}
and the kernel
h
.

Assumption A.3

(Weak dependence)
.

The array
(X_{in})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}},n\in\mathds{N}}
is
P
-NED on an absolutely regular array
(Z_{in})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}},n\in\mathds{N}}
, and there is a
\delta>0
such that

a_{k}\Phi(k^{-6})=O(k^{-6(2+\delta)/\delta}),\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\beta_{k}^{\delta/(2+\delta)}<\infty,

where
\beta_{k}
,

are the absolute regularity coefficients (Definition
A.1
) and
a_{k}
,
k\in\mathds{N}
, and
\Phi
are as in Definition
A.2
.

Furthermore, a uniform moment condition on
h(X_{i},X_{j})
is required. We do not impose any moment conditions on the data sequence itself.

Assumption A.4

(Uniform moments)
.

There is a constant
M>0
such that for all
k,n\in\mathds{N}
and
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

E\left|h(X_{in},X_{jn})\right|^{2+\delta}\leq M,

E\left|h(f_{n,i,k}(Z_{i-k,n},\ldots,Z_{i+k,n}),f_{n,j,k}(Z_{j-k,n},\ldots,Z_{j+k,n}))\right|^{2+\delta}\leq M.

Assumptions

. Weaker moment conditions have to be paid for by a faster decay of the short-range dependence coefficients and vice versa. The last assumption is also known as the variation condition and was introduced by

. It is a form of Lipschitz continuity of the kernel

with respect to
F
.

Assumption A.5

(Variation condition)
.

Let
(\tilde{X}_{in})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}},n\in\mathds{N}}
be an independent copy of the array
(X_{in})_{i\in{\mathds{Z}},n\in\mathds{N}}
.
There are constants
L,\epsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
, all
n\in\mathds{N}
,
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

E\left(\sup_{|x-X_{in}|\leq\epsilon,|y-\tilde{X}_{jn}|\leq\epsilon}\left|h\left(x,y\right)-h\left(X_{in},\tilde{X}_{jn}\right)\right|\right)^{2}\leq L\epsilon.

Proposition B.1
.

(1)

\frac{[\lambda n]}{\sqrt{n}}U_{n,1}^{(2)}([\lambda n])\to 0

(2)

If additionally

E\bigg[\bigg(\max_{l\leq n}\Big|\sum_{l\leq i<j\leq n}h_{2ijn}(X_{in},X_{jn})\Big|\bigg)^{2}\bigg]=o(n^{3}),

Proof.

For the first summand, we obtain

\displaystyle\quad+2\frac{n-[nt]}{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{[nt]}(h_{F}(X_{i})-\theta_{F})

\displaystyle\quad-2\frac{[nt]([n\tau^{\ast}]-[nt]-1)}{n(n-[nt]-1)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=[nt]+1}^{[n\tau^{\ast}]}(h_{F}(X_{i})-\theta_{F})

\displaystyle\quad-2\frac{[nt](n-[n\tau^{\ast}])}{n(n-[nt]-1)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=[nt]+1}^{[n\tau^{\ast}]}(h_{G}(X_{i})-\theta_{FG})

\displaystyle\quad-2\frac{[nt]([n\tau^{\ast}]-[nt])}{n(n-[nt]-1)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=[n\tau^{\ast}]+1}(h_{F}(Y_{i})-\theta_{FG})

\displaystyle\quad-2\frac{[nt](n-[n\tau^{\ast}]-1)}{n(n-[nt]-1)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=[n\tau^{\ast}]+1}^{n}(h_{G}(Y_{i})-\theta_{G})

The same argument as above yields convergence of this process to the limit stated in the formulation of the theorem, for
t\leq\tau^{\ast}
.

For
k\geq k_{n}^{\ast}+1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{k(n-k)}{n}(U_{1:k}-U_{k+1:n})

\displaystyle\;=\frac{k_{n}^{\ast}(n-k)}{n}(\theta_{F}-\theta_{G})+2\frac{k_{n}^{\ast}(k-k_{n}^{\ast})(n-k)}{n(k-1)}\rho_{FG}+\frac{2(n-k)(k_{n}^{\ast}-1)}{n(k-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}^{\ast}}(h_{F}(X_{i})-\theta_{F})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2(k-k_{n}^{\ast}-1)(n-k)}{n(k-1)}\sum_{j=k_{n}^{\ast}+1}^{k}(h_{G}(Y_{j})-\theta_{G})-\frac{2k}{n}\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}(h_{G}(Y_{i})-\theta_{G})+O_{P}(1),

and hence for

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big[\frac{[nt](n-[nt])}{n}\big(U_{1:[nt]}-U_{[nt]+1:n}\big)-n\Psi_{2}(t)\Big]

\displaystyle\;=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big[\frac{[n\tau^{\ast}](n-[nt])}{n}-n(1-t)\tau^{\ast}\Big](\theta_{F}-\theta_{G})

\displaystyle+2\Big[\frac{[n\tau^{\ast}]([nt]-[n\tau^{\ast})(n-[nt])}{n([nt]-1)}-n(t-\tau^{\ast})\tau^{\ast}\big(\frac{1}{t}-1\big)\Big]\rho_{FG}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2(n-[nt])([n\tau^{\ast}]-1)}{n([nt]-1)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{[n\tau^{\ast}]}(h_{F}(X_{i})-\theta_{F})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2(n-[nt])([nt]-[n\tau^{\ast}])}{n([nt]-1)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{[n\tau^{\ast}]}(h_{G}(X_{i})-\theta_{FG})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2(n-[nt])[n\tau^{\ast}]}{n([nt]-1)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=[n\tau^{\ast}]+1}^{[nt]}(h_{F}(Y_{j})-\theta_{FG})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2([nt]-[n\tau^{\ast}]-1)(n-[nt])}{n([nt]-1)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=[n\tau^{\ast}]+1}^{[nt]}(h_{G}(Y_{j})-\theta_{G})

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2[nt]}{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=[nt]+1}^{n}(h_{G}(Y_{i})-\theta_{G})+O_{P}(1/\sqrt{n}),

The same argument as above yields convergence of this process to the limit stated in the formulation of the theorem, for
t\geq\tau^{\ast}
. As the limit process is continuous in

, we finally obtain convergence of the process

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}([nt](U_{1:[nt]}-U_{1:n})-n\,\Psi_{1}(t))_{0\leq t\leq 1}
.
This completes the proof of Proposition
B.9
.

Proof of Theorem
2.13
.

As we are in the independent-sequences case, recall that the condition of Lemma
B.7
implies Assumption
2.8
.
Since the proofs for the
first-vs-full
and the
first-vs-last
approach are essentially the same, we only give the details for one of them.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\Psi_{1}(\tau^{\star})>0
. By the continuity of
\Psi_{1}
, this will also hold in a neighborhood of
\tau^{\star}
. Furthermore, by Theorem
2.9
,
D_{n}^{F}([nt])
will also be positive with probability going to 1. First note that

\big(T_{1}(h)-\sqrt{n}|\Psi_{1}(\tau^{\star})|\big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}D_{n}^{F}([nt])-n\Psi_{1}(\tau^{\star})\Big)\\
\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\big(D_{n}^{F}([n\tau^{\star}])-n\Psi_{1}(\tau^{\star})\big)

By Proposition
B.9
, the right hand side converges in distribution to
G_{1}(\tau^{\star})
, which is a centered normal random variable. A short calculation gives the variance as stated in Theorem
2.13
. We still have to show that there also exists an upper bound which converges to the same limit distribution. For this note that

T_{1}(h)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}D_{n}^{F}([n\hat{\tau}_{1,n}])

because
\hat{\tau}_{1,n}=\argmax_{t}|D_{n}^{F}([nt])|
, see (
2.13
), so

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\big(T_{1}(h)-n|\Psi_{1}(\tau^{\star})|\big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big(D_{n}^{F}([n\hat{\tau}_{1,n}])-n\Psi_{1}(\tau^{\star})\Big)\\
\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big(D_{n}^{F}([n\hat{\tau}_{1,n}])-n\Psi_{1}(\hat{\tau}_{1,n})\Big)

as, by Proposition
2.12
,
|\Psi_{1}(t)|
has its maximal value at
t=\tau^{\star}
. We know from the argmax theorem that
\hat{\tau}_{1,n}\rightarrow\tau^{\star}
in probability. So for any
\epsilon>0
with probability going to 1

\Big(D_{n}^{F}([n\hat{\tau}_{1,n}])-\sqrt{n}\Psi_{1}(\hat{\tau}_{1,n})\Big)\leq\sup_{|t-\tau^{\star}|\leq\epsilon}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big(D_{n}^{F}([nt])-n\Psi_{1}(t)\Big).

By the continuous mapping theorem, we have the convergence

\sup_{|t-\tau^{\star}|\leq\epsilon}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big(D_{n}^{F}([nt])-n|\Psi_{1}(t)\Big)\rightarrow\sup_{|t-\tau^{\star}|\leq\epsilon}G_{1}(t)

in distribution. By the continuity of the limit process, we have
\sup_{|t-\tau^{\star}|\leq\epsilon}G_{1}(t)\rightarrow G_{1}(\tau^{\star})

2.13

Proof of Proposition

We will show

\rho_{FG}=-\frac{1}{2}\int\int|x-y|\{f(x)-g(x)\}\{f(y)-g(y)\}dxdy\geq 0

using properties of characteristic functions. Let
a>0

\phi_{a}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{1-\cos(ax)}{ax^{2}},\qquad x\in\mathds{R},

\chi_{a}(t)=\left(1-\frac{|t|}{a}\right)\mathds{1}\mbox{\Large${}_{[-a,a]}$}(t),\qquad t\in\mathds{R},

Hence, by Bochner’s theorem

This implies

(C.2)

\iint\left(1-\frac{|x-y|}{a}\right)\mathds{1}\mbox{\Large${}_{[-a,a]}$}(x-y)k(x)k(y)dxdy\geq 0,

where

0\leq a\iint\left(1-\frac{|x-y|}{a}\right)\mathds{1}\mbox{\Large${}_{[-a,a]}$}(x-y)k(x)k(y)dxdy\\

and

a\int_{-b}^{b}\!\int_{-b}^{b}(f(x)-g(x))(f(y)-g(y))dxdy=2b\big(P_{F}(|X|\leq b)-P_{G}(|X|\leq b)\big)^{2}\\

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Consider the function

h_{1}(y)=\int|x-y|dF(x).

It has a minimum in
y=0
and is convex because for all
x
, the mapping
y\mapsto|x-y|
is convex. We conclude that
h_{1}(y)
is non-increasing for
y<0
and non-decreasing for
y>0
. Let
c>1
, then

\theta_{F}=\iint|x-y|dF(x)dF(y)=\int h_{1}(y)dF(y)\leq\int h_{1}(cy)dF(y)\\
=\int h_{1}(y)dG(y)=\iint|x-y|dF(x)dG(y)=\theta_{FG}

and with the same reasoning
\theta_{FG}\leq\theta_{G}
. The other case

F
and
G

\theta_{FG}\in[\min\{\theta_{F},\theta_{G}\},\max\{\theta_{F},\theta_{G}\}]
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Recall
h(x,y)=(x-y)^{2}/2
. Let
X\sim F
and
Y\sim G
be independent and let
\mu=E(X)
and
\nu=E(Y)
. Then

\theta_{F}=\mathrm{Var}(X)
,
\theta_{G}=\mathrm{Var}(Y)
, and

\theta_{FG}=\frac{1}{2}E(X-Y)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}E\Big\{(X-\mu)-(Y-\nu)+(\mu-\nu)\Big\}^{2}

=\frac{1}{2}E[(X-\mu)-(Y-\nu)]^{2}+E[(X-\mu)-(Y-\nu)](\mu-\nu)+\frac{1}{2}(\mu-\nu)^{2}

=\frac{1}{2}[\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)]^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\mu-\nu)^{2}

and hence

\rho_{FG}=\theta_{FG}-\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{F}+\theta_{G})=\frac{1}{2}(\mu-\nu)^{2},

which completes the proof of Proposition
3.3
.
∎

The analogous formula for the sample covariance, as given in Section
3.2
of the paper, is obtained likewise:
Let
(X,Y)
and
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
be independent random vectors with bivariate distributions
F
and
G
, respectively, and consider the kernel

h((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=(x-x^{\prime})(y-y^{\prime})/2
.
Defining
\mu_{F}=E(X)
,
\nu_{F}=E(Y)
,
\mu_{G}=E(X^{\prime})
, and
\nu_{G}=E(Y^{\prime})
, we have

\theta_{F}=\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)
,
\theta_{G}=\mathrm{Cov}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
, and

\displaystyle\theta_{FG}

\displaystyle=Eh\{(X,Y),(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\}=\frac{1}{2}E\{(X-X^{\prime})(Y-Y^{\prime})\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}E\big\{\big((X-\mu_{F})-(X^{\prime}-\mu_{G})+(\mu_{F}-\mu_{G})\big)\big((Y-\nu_{F})-(Y^{\prime}-\nu_{G})+(\nu_{F}-\nu_{G})\big)\big\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)+\mathrm{Cov}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})+(\mu_{F}-\mu_{G})(\nu_{F}-\nu_{G})\big),

as all the mixed terms vanish by independence of
(X,Y)
and

Appendix D

The p-values reported in Section
4

\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|B(t)|
, where

is a Brownian bridge. This means, we divide both test statistics
T_{1}(h)
and
T_{2}(h)
, (see (
1.5
)) by the following estimate of the long-run variance

\hat{\sigma}_{h}^{2}=\,4\!\sum_{k=-(n-1)}^{n-1}W\left(\frac{|k|}{b_{n}}\right)\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-|k|}\hat{h}_{1}(X_{i})\hat{h}_{1}(X_{i+|k|}),

Throughout the current paper, we use the Bartlett (or triangular) kernel, i.e.,
