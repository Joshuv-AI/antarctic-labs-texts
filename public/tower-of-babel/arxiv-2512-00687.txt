p p -adic monodromy and mod p p unlikely intersections, II
Ruofan Jiang

Abstract
. We study ordinary abelian schemes in characteristic p p and their moduli spaces from the perspective of char p p Mumford–Tate, log Ax–Lindemann, and geometric André–Oort conjectures (abbreviated as MT p \MTT_{p} , logAL p \mathrm{logAL}_{p} and geoAO p ). In this paper, we achieve multiple goals: ( A ) establish the implication MT p ⇔ logAL p ⇒ geoAO p \mathrm{MT}_{p}\Leftrightarrow\mathrm{logAL}_{p}\Rightarrow\mathrm{geoAO_{p}} , and show that they all follow from the Tate conjecture for abelian varieties. The equivalence MT p ⇔ logAL p \mathrm{MT}_{p}\Leftrightarrow\mathrm{logAL}_{p} is exploited from both sides, which enables us to ( B ) develop a representation theory approach to logAL p \mathrm{logAL}_{p} and geoAO p \mathrm{geoAO_{p}} by first establishing many cases of MT p via classical techniques, and ( C ) develop an algebraization approach to MT p \MTT_{p} that transcends the limitation of classical methods. In particular, we introduce “crystalline Hodge loci”, a rigid analytic geometric object that encodes the essential information needed for proving logAL p \mathrm{logAL}_{p} , while being very approachable via (integral and relative) p p -adic Hodge theory. This enables us to prove logAL p \mathrm{logAL}_{p} for compact Tate-linear curves with unramified p p -adic monodromy. As an application, we establish MT p \MTT_{p} for many abelian fourfolds of p p -adic Mumford type.

1.2.2.
Geometry of the Tate-linear loci

The algebraization problem of a Tate-linear locus is closely related to its geometry.

How can one understand the geometry of
\mathfrak{T}_{\mathrm{can}}
? Let’s take a pointwise scrutiny: in the residue disk of an ordinary
\mathbb{F}
-point
x

\mathfrak{T}_{\mathrm{can}}
is a union of the rigid generic fibers of torsion translates of subtori of the formal torus
\mathscr{A}_{g,W}^{/x}
; cf. Theorem
6.8
; however, in the residue disk of a non-ordinary point, it can be much wilder. Besides all that has been said, it is a natural idea to impose
integrality conditions
on the
p
-adic monodromy of
T
(e.g., being “unramified” as in Theorem
1.16
) in order to tame the behavior of
\mathfrak{T}_{\mathrm{can}}
, especially in the residue disk of a non-ordinary point.

Indeed, the connection between the integrality of the
p
-adic monodromy and the geometry of
\mathfrak{T}_{\mathrm{can}}
is analogues to the connection between the hyperspecial condition of the level group and the geometry of an integral Shimura variety. In
[
26
]
, Kisin studies the local geometry of a certain integral Shimura subvariety
\mathscr{S}_{W}\subseteq\mathscr{A}_{g,W}
with hyperspecial level structure. In the formal neighborhood of an arbitrary
\mathbb{F}
-point
x\in\mathscr{A}_{g,\mathbb{F}}
, there is a type of
Lie theoretic coordinates
(in contrast to the canonical coordinates) that comes from the explicit deformation of
p
-divisible groups with Tate classes (cf.
[
20
, §7]
,
[
40
, §4]
and
[
26
, §1.4-§1.5]
). Using Breuil–Kisin modules, Kisin is able to show that
\mathscr{S}^{/x}_{W}
is a union of
linear subspaces
in the Lie theoretic coordinates. In particular,
\mathscr{S}_{W}
is smooth up to a normalization.

We briefly explain how this inspires a strategy to tackle the algebraization problem for
\mathfrak{T}_{\mathrm{can}}
, with a view towards the proof of Theorem
1.16
.

First, we show that if the

p
-adic monodromy of
T
is unramified, then (up to replacing
T
by a Hecke translate) the canonical Tate-linear locus
\mathfrak{T}_{\mathrm{can}}
is
linear
in the Lie theoretic coordinates in the residue disk of every point of
x\in T(\mathbb{F})
. Needless to say, Breuil–Kisin theory plays a key role in establishing the linearity. However, a major difference is that Kisin starts with a collection of
p
-adic étale cycles (arising from Hodge cycles) with good integral behavior, which he can feed in the Breuil–Kisin theory, while in our case we only have a collection of crystalline cycles with good integral behavior. The particular definition of
\mathfrak{T}_{\mathrm{can}}
, however, equips us with a collection of de Rham cycles compatible with the crystalline ones. To obtain
p
-adic étale cycles with good integral behavior, we have to apply more recent development on (relative) integral
p
-adic Hodge theory, e.g.,
[
17
]
. Of course,
[

]
only applies to semistable formal schemes, while we do not
a priori
know that
\mathfrak{T}_{\mathrm{can}}
admits a nice formal model. This is where lots of technicalities from
p
-adic geometry kick in: splitting into pieces, normlizing, throwing out singular loci, using Raynaud’s theory to find a formal model, formally alterating the model (cf.
[
50

), etc.

After one shows that
\mathfrak{T}_{\mathrm{can}}
is linear in the residue disk of every point of
x\in T(\mathbb{F})
, the strategy is to show that
\mathfrak{T}_{\mathrm{can}}
is contained in a union of finitely many tubes of smaller radius (i.e., tubes of the form
]U[_{<1-\epsilon}
, where

is Zariski open). When

is proper, this condition implies that
\mathfrak{T}_{\mathrm{can}}
is Zariski closed in
(\mathcal{A}_{g,K}^{\Sigma})^{{\mathrm{an}}}
: a situation that enables us to apply rigid GAGA. To avoid too much technicality, we narrow ourselves down to the
\dim T=1
case, where the proof is relatively clean. This essentially proves Theorem

of convergent isocrystals as the category of bundles with convergent connections over
]X_{0}[
. The definition does not depend on the choice of
\mathscr{P}_{0}
. In general, even if

does not admit an embedding

, one can still cover it by Zariski open where such embedding exists, and glue the category
\mathbf{Isoc}(X_{0})
from local. It can be shown that

is independent of the choices made (i.e., the embedding and the covering), and is equivalent to the site theoretical definition by Ogus. One can also define the category
\FIsoc(X_{0})
as isocrystals with a certain Frobenius structure.

There is another notion, called the

3.1.1.
Density of

Let

be a finite field model of

(3.1.1)

V_{\max}(X_{0})=\{x_{0}\in|X_{0}|:\rk G_{\mathrm{B}}(x)=\rk G_{u}(f)\}.

The definition does not depend on

\rk G_{\mathrm{B}}(x)=\rk G_{u}(f)

is a maximal torus”. This explains the notation “

”.

Our goal is to show that

is a subset of
|X_{0}|
of “large density”. Let
\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Spec\mathbb{Z}
be a finite type integral scheme of dimension
d
. Denote by
|\mathcal{X}|
the set of closed points of
\mathcal{X}
. There is a notion of
(Dirichlet) density
for a subset of
|\mathcal{X}|
, generalizing the classical density defined for number fields. We will refer the readers to
[
46
]
or
[
44
, Appendix B]
for the precise definition.

In the following, let

\pi:\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
be an irreducible finite étale cover with Galois group
G
. Then every point
x_{0}\in|\mathcal{X}|
determines a conjugacy class
\langle\Frob_{\pi^{-1}(x_{0})/x_{0}}\rangle\subseteq G
. The following version of Chebotarev’s density theorem is
[
44
, Theorem B.9]
(there is another version by Serre under slight different setup, see
[
46
, Theorem 7]
).

Theorem 3.3

(Chebotarev’s density theorem)
.

Notation as above. For every conjugacy class
\mathcal{C}\subseteq G
, the set
\{x_{0}\in|\mathcal{X}|:\langle\Frob_{\pi^{-1}(x_{0})/x_{0}}\rangle=\mathcal{C}\}
has density
{|\mathcal{C}|}/{|G|}
.

Proposition 3.4

(Chin)
.

V_{\max}(X_{0})
contains a subset of positive density.

Proof.

Take
u
in (
3.1.1
) be a prime
l
not equal to
p
. Possibly replacing
X_{0}
by a Galois cover, we can assume that
G_{l}(f_{0})
is connected. Let
U\subseteq G_{l}(f_{0})
be the conjugation-stable Zariski dense open subset in
[
9
, Theorem 5.7]
(note that the conditions of this theorem are met since we are working with the first
l
-adic étale cohomology of an abelian scheme). It has the property that

\rho_{l}^{{\text{\'{e}t}}}\langle\Frob_{x_{0}}\rangle\in U\Rightarrow x_{0}\in V_{\max}(X_{0}),

where
\rho_{l}^{{\text{\'{e}t}}}:\pi_{1}^{{\text{\'{e}t}}}(X_{0})\rightarrow G_{l}(f_{0})
and
\langle\Frob_{x_{0}}\rangle
is the conjugacy class of the geometric Frobenius of

. The map
\rho_{l}^{{\text{\'{e}t}}}
is continuous with respect to the pro-finite topology on
\pi_{1}^{{\text{\'{e}t}}}(X_{0})
and

. On the other hand, the

-adic topology on

is finer than the Zariski topology. It follows that

is a conjugate-invariant non-empty open subset. As a result, there exists a Galois cover

such that

is a finite union of cosets of

to the cover

∎

3.2.
Mumford–Tate pairs

We review the theory of Mumford–Tate pairs, a crucial Lie theoretic tool for attacking the Mumford–Tate conjecture. We show that the theory is still valid for ordinary abelian varieties in characteristic
p
.

Definition 3.5

(
[
45
, §4]
)
.

Let
(G,\rho)
be a faithful finite dimensional representation of a reductive group over
a characteristic
0
field
F
. The pair
(G,\rho)
is called a
weak Mumford–Tate pair of weights
\{0,1\}
if there exist finitely many cocharacters
\mu_{1}
, …,
\mu_{d}
of
G_{\overline{F}}
such that
\rho\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\mu_{i}
are all of weights 0 and 1 and that
G_{\overline{F}}
is generated by
G({\overline{F}})
-conjugates of
\mu_{1}
, …,
\mu_{d}
. The pair
(G,\rho)
is called a
strong Mumford–Tate pair of weights
\{0,1\}
if it is a weak Mumford–Tate pair of weights
\{0,1\}
and that the
\mu_{i}
’s are conjugate under
\Gal(F)
.

Example 3.6
.

It is classically known (cf.
[
45
, §5]
) that the Mumford–Tate group of an abelian variety
A/\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, together with its representation, form a strong Mumford–Tate pair over
\mathbb{Q}
. Moreover, the Mumford–Tate group is generated by the
\Gal(\mathbb{Q})
-conjugates of the Hodge cocharacter of
A
.

Remark 3.7
.

It is known that simple factors of a weak Mumford–Tate pair are again weak Mumford–Tate pairs (
[
45
, §4]
). Simple weak Mumford–Tate pairs are classified thoroughly, see
[
45
, Table 4.2, Corollary 5.11]
. On the other hand, absolutely irreducible strong Mumford–Tate pairs have several good structural properties, see
[
45
, Proposition 4.4, 4.5]
.

The theory of Mumford–Tate pairs works equally well for ordinary abelian schemes in char
p
:

Proposition 3.8
.

Recall that
f:X\rightarrow{\mathscr{A}}_{g,\mathbb{F}}

is a morphism whose image lies in the ordinary locus. The following are true:

(1)

(G_{\mathrm{B}}(f),\rho_{\mathrm{B}})
is a strong Mumford–Tate pair over
\mathbb{Q}
.

(2)

For each
u\in\mathrm{fpl}(\mathbb{Q})
,
(G_{u}(f)^{\circ},\rho_{u})
is a weak Mumford–Tate pair over
\mathbb{Q}_{u}
.

Proof.

By definition,
G_{\mathrm{B}}(f)
is the generic Mumford–Tate group of a special subvariety
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{g}
, which is defined over some number field
E
. By
[
2
, Fact 3.3.1.1]
,
\mathcal{X}
contains an
l
-Galois-generic point
z
over a finite extension of
E
. By
[
2
, Theorem A]
,
z
is Galois generic, hence Hodge generic by
[
2
, Proposition 6.2.1]
. This means that the Mumford–Tate group of
z
equals
G_{\mathrm{B}}(f)
. By Example
3.6
, we see that the Mumford–Tate group of
z
and its representation is a strong Mumford–Tate pair. This concludes (1).

By Proposition
3.4
, there is an ordinary point
y\in X(\mathbb{F})
such that
G_{u}(y)^{\circ}
is a maximal torus of
G_{u}(f)^{\circ}
. Since
G_{u}(y)^{\circ}=G_{\mathrm{B}}(y)_{\mathbb{Q}_{u}}
, we conclude by Proposition

that
G_{u}(y)^{\circ}
is generated by the
\Gal(\mathbb{Q})
-orbit of
\mu_{y}
. Since
\mu_{y}
is defined over a number field, the
\Gal(\mathbb{Q})
-orbit of
\mu_{y}
contains only finitely many cocharacters
\mu_{1},...,\mu_{d}
, and they generate a maximal torus of

. Since
G_{u}(f)^{\circ}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{u})
-conjugates of a maximal torus generate

∎

(2)

Fix a maximal torus of

\Phi_{G}

To show that roots generate

, Table 4.2]

The root system of

Claim
.

Let

p

p
-integral at

. Apply Theorem
6.14
