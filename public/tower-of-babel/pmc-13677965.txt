Metabolomics reveals the effect of sand roasting, freeze-drying, and different hydrothermal treatments on the physical, functional and bioactive profile of Rice bean (Vigna umbellata)
Authors: Somaina Basumatary, Manish Kumar Singh, Baby Zaithanpuii Hmar, Arvind Kumar, Jagan Karthik Sugumar, Ankur Aggarwal, Himanshu Mishra, Kumari Amita Singh, Satyender Singh Yadav, Sunil Kumar, Vishal Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study comprehensively investigated the effects of five processing treatments, namely soaking (SK), germination (GR), sand roasting (SR), freeze-drying (FD), and pressure cooking (PC) on the physicochemical, functional, structural, antioxidant, and metabolomic characteristics of rice bean (Vigna umbellata), an underutilised legume with high nutritional potential. Significant variations (p ≤ 0.05) were observed among treatments. GR proved most effective in enhancing nutritional and bioactive quality, yielding the highest protein content (24.3%), total phenolics (8.42 mg GAE/g), flavonoids (4.22 mg QE/g), DPPH radical scavenging activity (72.67%), foaming capacity (32.82%), and emulsion stability (76.3%). PC maximised sphericity (68.85%), swelling index (5.31 g/g), and hydration properties, while SR enhanced oil absorption capacity (2.42 g/g). Untargeted HR-MS metabolomics detected 10,533 metabolite features, with GR samples showing the richest profile and enrichment of quercetin, kaempferol, and naringenin derivatives; 283 metabolites constituted the shared core metabolome. FT-IR revealed treatment-induced modifications in protein, lipid, and carbohydrate structures, and XRD confirmed A-type starch crystallinity, largely preserved by FD but disrupted by PC. These findings demonstrate that processing-specific structural modifications govern functionality, highlighting the targeted use of rice bean flour in functional food applications.Keywords: Rice bean, Bioactivity, Germination, Engineering properties, Freeze-drying, Sand roasting, Metabolomics, Antioxidant activity

Introduction

Legumes are a primary source of plant-based alternative protein, particularly in developing regions across Africa, Asia, and South America (Lisciani et al., 2024). Staple legumes, i.e., soybean, pea, chickpea, lentil, and cowpea are integral to global food systems because of their high nutrient density, including protein (20–26%), dietary fibre, essential micronutrients, and lower carbohydrate content compared to cereals (Medendorp et al., 2022). Pulses also provide significant mineral content, including iron, zinc, calcium, magnesium, and potassium, which are critical for the nutritional security of low-income populations (Dissanayaka et al., 2021). Beyond macronutrients, legumes are rich in bioactive compounds (BACs), including phenolics, flavonoids, and alkaloids, which confer antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-diabetic properties (Lovegrove et al., 2023; Vijayakumar & M, 2021). Specific phytochemicals (ferulic acid, rutin, tannins, and saponins) exhibit anti-obesity, anti-cancer, and antibacterial activities (Chen et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2012). Rice bean extracts, for example, have demonstrated anti-inflammatory and anti-diabetic potential via α-glucosidase inhibition and cyclooxygenase-2 suppression (Sritongtae et al., 2017; Yao et al., 2012).

Rice bean (Vigna umbellata L.) is a warm-season underutilised legume belonging to the Fabaceae family and is widely cultivated in Southeast Asia in mixed cropping systems with maize, sorghum, and millet (Sritongtae et al., 2017). The seeds of the bean are either red or brown in colour, and the shape ranges from oval to cylindrical, with a size of ∼3 to 5 mm (Wadikar & Bepary, 2020). This bean has a good nutritional score, with protein (18–25%), complex carbohydrates including dietary fibre (55–65%), lipid (1–3%), B-complex vitamins, and minerals including calcium, magnesium, and iron (Wadikar & Bepary, 2020). Since the protein of rice bean contains a good amount of Trp and Lys amino acids, it is a typical complementary protein. These underutilised legumes could offer substantial health-promoting properties and can be effectively utilised in functional food development. Rice beans were also studied for numerous bioactive functions, especially their phenolics and flavonoids, which were examined and reported to exert anti-inflammatory, anti-diabetic, antioxidant, metabolic health-regulating, and anti-cancer properties (Karami & Duangmal, 2023). Recently, in India, this crop has gained focus primarily because of its climate adaptability, high protein content with a good amino acid score, and as a support for the traditional farming system to achieve protein security, particularly in the Himalayas and the northeastern region including Assam, Manipur, Mizoram, and Nagaland (Bepary et al., 2017; Kaur et al., 2013).

Pulses contain several anti-nutritional factors (ANFs) that can retard protein digestibility and mineral bioavailability, such as phytic acid, tannins, oxalates, saponins, and enzyme inhibitors (Kumar et al., 2022). Studies have shown that different processing techniques, i.e., soaking, cooking, germination, fermentation, and autoclaving, can significantly enhance protein digestibility, reduce ANFs, and improve the amino acid profile of pulses (Gu et al., 2023). Nevertheless, rice beans are considered safe for direct intake because of their low content of hemagglutinins and protease inhibitors (Katoch, 2020). To reduce ANFs, various traditional and advanced processing methods (such as soaking, germination, pressure cooking, roasting, and freeze-drying) can be utilised. Germination of legumes stimulates the production of endogenous enzymes that degrade phytates and increase the number of BACs (López-Martínez et al., 2017; Sarkhel & Roy, 2022). Furthermore, these methods can also improve the sensory quality and nutrient bioavailability, along with the formation of active components in rice beans.

Metabolomics of legumes is a high-throughput approach that provides in-depth profiling of low-MW metabolites present or formed during different processing techniques, i.e., hydrothermal, fermentation, or drying processes (Llorach et al., 2019). These tools also allow the identification of BACs, including flavonoids, polyphenols, organic acids, and amino acids, along with the evaluation of alterations that occur during processing (Lal et al., 2026). Mass spectrometry, particularly LC-MS, is a powerful foodomics tool for profiling metabolite changes during grain processing. It enables the comprehensive profiling of processing-induced metabolite changes, facilitating the identification of BACs, processing-related biomarkers, and the biochemical pathways associated with these transformations.

Despite extensive research on individual processing effects in major legumes, no study has comprehensively compared the metabolomic, structural, and functional consequences of diverse hydrothermal and thermal treatments in rice beans using untargeted HR-MS metabolomics integrated with physicochemical and spectroscopic analyses. The present study addresses this gap by (i) establishing distinct metabolic fingerprints for five processing treatments; (ii) identifying optimal processing strategies for specific food applications.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Analytical-grade chemicals, including sodium hydroxide, sodium nitrite, aluminum chloride, folin-ciocalteu reagent (FCR), sodium carbonate anhydrous, gallic acid, catechin, ethanol, and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), were obtained from HiMedia (Mumbai, India). LC-MS grade methanol, acetonitrile, phosphate buffer, and formic acid were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (Bengaluru, India). Ultrapure water was produced using a Milli-Q system (Merck Millipore, Chennai, India). Other consumables like 25 mm, 0.22 μm PVDF syringe filters, 2 mL amber chromatography vials, glass beakers, and 50 mL polypropylene Falcon tubes were purchased from HiMedia and Fisher Scientific. Approximately 5 kg of whole dried rice beans (variety: JCR-08-7) were collected from local farms in Mizoram, Meghalaya, and Assam between October 2024 and April 2025. The beans were manually sorted to remove dust, stones, and other foreign material before processing.

Processing of Rice bean

Rice beans (200 g per treatment) were subjected to five processing methods. For soaking (SK), beans were soaked in distilled water (1:5, w/v) at approximately 25 °C for 24 h, oven-dried at 60 °C for 2 h, milled, sieved (80 μm), and stored at −18 °C (Abd El-Hady & Habiba, 2003). Germination (GR) was performed by germinating the soaked beans (1:3, w/v) in the dark at approximately 90% relative humidity for 36 h, followed by drying at 60 °C, milling, sieving, and storage at −18 °C (Mamilla & Mishra, 2017). For pressure cooking (PC), soaked beans (1:2, w/v) were cooked at 121 °C (30 psi) for 15 min, dried, milled, sieved, and stored at −20 °C. Sand roasting (SR) involved roasting soaked, air-dried beans in preheated sand at approximately 180 °C for 2 to 5 min with continuous stirring, followed by separation, milling, sieving, and storage (Qureshi et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2026). Freeze-drying (FD) was carried out by freeze-drying soaked beans (1:3, w/v) at −55 °C and 0.1 mbar for 48 h (BUCHI FreeZone), followed by milling, sieving, and packaging in airtight bags (Farooq & Wani, 2025). Since soaking is the initial step in most legume processing operations, all treatments were initiated from pre-soaked beans, with SK serving as the hydrated control.

Colour analysis

Compositional analysis

The proximate composition of rice bean flour was determined using standard AOAC methods. Moisture content was measured by oven drying at 105 ± 5 °C to constant weight (AOAC, 2019). Crude protein was determined by the Kjeldahl method (AOAC, 2005), and nitrogen content was converted to protein using a conversion factor of 6.25. Total carbohydrate content was estimated using the Anthrone method (AOAC, 2005). Crude fat content was determined using the Soxhlet extraction method with a SoxCap and Soxtec system (Foss UK Ltd., UK) according to AOAC (2019), while ash content was determined by dry ashing at 550 °C for 3 h in a muffle furnace (AOAC, 2005). Moisture, protein, and ash contents were calculated using standard AOAC equations.

Functional properties

Swelling index

The swelling index of the treated bean powder was determined using the method described by Neji et al. (2022). Samples (0.2 g) were placed in pre-weighed centrifuge tubes, and 10 mL of distilled water was added to each tube. The tubes were incubated in a water bath at temperatures of 50 °C, 60 °C, 70 °C, 80 °C, and 90 °C for 30 min with intermittent vortexing every 5 min. The tubes were then cooled to room temperature and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The swelling index was calculated as the ratio of the weight of the swollen sediment to the initial dry weight of the sample.

Water absorption index (WAI) and water solubility index (WSI) were determined according to Bashir and Aggarwal (2016). Analysis of antioxidant activity

Extraction process of samples

Bioactive compounds were extracted from the processed rice bean samples using methanol-assisted extraction with minor modifications to the protocol described by Ferreira et al. (2022). Briefly, 1.0 g of each treated rice bean flour sample was accurately weighed into a 50 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 10 mL of methanol (HPLC grade). The mixture was homogenised using a vortex mixer (Tarsons Spinix Vortex Shaker, India) operating at 50 Hz for 2 min to ensure thorough dispersion. The samples were subsequently incubated at 4 °C for 12 h to facilitate optimal extraction of phenolic and flavonoid compounds. Following the incubation period, the mixtures were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 30 min at room temperature. The supernatant was carefully collected, transferred to sterile centrifuge tubes, sealed with paraffin film to prevent evaporation and contamination, and stored at −18 °C until further analysis.

Total phenolic content (TPC)

Total phenolic content was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu colourimetric method with slight modifications (Aggarwal & Verma, 2025; Farooq & Wani, 2025). An aliquot of 200 μL of the methanolic extract was transferred to a 15 mL test tube, to which 1.5 mL of FCR (10-fold dilution with distilled water) was added. The contents were vortexed thoroughly and allowed to equilibrate for 5 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 1.5 mL of sodium carbonate solution (60 g/L) was added to neutralise the reaction mixture, followed by incubation at room temperature for 90 min in the dark to allow complete colour development. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

FT-IR spectra of the processed rice bean samples were recorded using a PerkinElmer Spectrum spectrophotometer operating in transmission mode with KBr pellets. Spectral data were collected over the range of 400–4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1, with 2θ scans averaged at room temperature (Aggarwal et al., 2025). The obtained spectra were exported and processed using OriginPro 2024b (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed using a Malvern Panalytical X-ray diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm). The instrument was calibrated using silicon powder and operated at 45 kV and 40 mA. Data were collected over a 2θ range of 10°-85° at a scanning rate of 2°/min with a step size of 0.013° (Aggarwal et al., 2025).

HR-MS-based bioactive profiling

Metabolomic profiling was performed using an untargeted high-resolution mass spectrometry (HR-MS) approach on a Thermo Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid MS coupled with a Dionex UltiMate 3000 RSLC system. Briefly, 100 mg of each sample was extracted with 2.0 mL methanol: water (4:1, v/v), homogenised at 750 rpm for 30 min at 25 °C, centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min, and filtered through a 0.22 μm PVDF syringe filter (Kumar et al., 2024). A 4 μL aliquot was injected onto a Hypersil GOLD™ C18 column (1.9 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) at 40 °C with a flow rate of 300 μL/min using a gradient of water and methanol (both with 0.1% formic acid). Full-scan MS data were acquired in positive and negative ionisation modes over the m/z range of 100–1000 at a resolving power of 60,000, followed by HCD-MS/MS at normalised collision energies of 30%, 45%, and 60%. Pooled quality control (QC) samples were injected every 10 runs to monitor analytical reproducibility, and compounds with a coefficient of variation (CV) < 30% across QC injections were retained. Data processing was performed using Compound Discoverer 3.3.2.31 with S/N ≥ 3, deisotoping, and adduct deconvolution. Among the 10,533 raw spectral features detected, 283 metabolites were putatively annotated, representing the shared core metabolome across all treatments. These annotations remain tentative and are supported by accurate mass measurements (<5 ppm mass error) and MS/MS fragmentation patterns.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted using three independently processed batches (biological replicates; N = 3), with each batch analysed once. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using Microsoft Excel software to determine significant differences among treatment groups, followed by Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc test for multiple comparisons at a probability level of p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Proximate composition

The proximate composition of rice beans was significantly influenced by the different processing treatments applied. The moisture content was highest in SK (7.96%) and GR (7.82%) samples, primarily due to water uptake during hydration and sprouting processes. In contrast, FD (2.04%) and SR samples exhibited substantially lower moisture content, which may enhance storage stability and shelf life by reducing the risk of microbial growth. The ash content was highest in FD (4.2%) and SR (4.1%) samples, primarily due to the concentration of mineral constituents following extensive moisture removal. Meanwhile, PC (3.2%) and SK samples showed comparatively lower ash values, which may be attributed to the leaching of soluble minerals into the processing water, a phenomenon commonly reported in thermally processed legumes (Wang et al., 2010). Consistent with findings reported for processed chickpeas (Özcan et al., 2024), our results demonstrate that thermal and hydrothermal treatments significantly affect the mineral retention and functional properties of legume grains. Protein content varied considerably among treatments, ranging from 17.3% in SR to 24.3% in GR samples. The significantly higher protein content observed in germinated rice beans may be associated with enzymatic mobilisation of nitrogenous reserves, synthesis of new enzymatic proteins, and reduction in dry matter during sprouting (Sammoud et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2019). In contrast, lower protein content in SR and PC samples may be attributed to thermal denaturation, degradation, and leaching of soluble nitrogenous compounds during processing. Carbohydrate content ranged from 62.1% in SK to 63.2% in GR (Table 1). The lower carbohydrate content in SK may be attributed to the leaching of soluble sugars during soaking, whereas the higher value in GR may reflect dry-matter redistribution during sprouting. In contrast, FD and SR showed intermediate values of 62.6% and 62.2%, respectively, probably due to moisture removal and the resulting concentration effect. The carbohydrate reduction during sprouting may also be associated with amylolytic hydrolysis of starch into simpler sugars, which are utilised as energy sources during metabolic activation (Xu et al., 2019). Fat content showed no significant differences among treatments, ranging from 1.1% to 1.6%, with FD and SK samples exhibiting slightly higher values. Overall, the results indicate that GR and FD are effective processing methods for preserving or enhancing the nutritional composition of rice beans, whereas intensive thermal treatments, i.e., SR and PC, may reduce protein and mineral content due to leaching losses and heat-induced degradation.

Engineering parameters

Hydrated samples (SK and PC) exhibited the highest axial dimensions and kernel mass, which may be attributed to moisture absorption and subsequent seed swelling. Similar increases in kernel dimensions and thousand-kernel weight with increasing moisture content have been reported for red kidney beans and cowpea seeds (Table 2) (Altuntas & Demirtola, 2007; Ravikumar et al., 2018). In contrast, dry-heat roasting (SR) and FD produced the smallest dimensions and lowest mass values, supporting earlier observations that dehydration processes proportionally reduce grain size and weight (Sharanagat et al., 2018).

Among the treatments, the highest sphericity was observed in the PC samples, primarily due to uniform moisture-induced expansion, which promoted a more rounded kernel geometry. Comparable effects have been observed in hydrothermally processed grains, where water uptake enhances seed roundness and dimensionality (Dharmaraj et al., 2015). Meanwhile, the lowest sphericity observed in the GR samples may be associated with enzymatic activity during sprouting, which causes elongation and surface irregularities in the seed structure (Bepary et al., 2018). Similarly, the highest surface area values in SK and PC and the lowest in SR demonstrate the strong dependence of surface area on seed volume and moisture content, as previously reported for processed legumes and grains (Abubakar et al., 2023; Sharanagat et al., 2018).

Regarding gravimetric properties, SK and PC exhibited higher bulk densities owing to increased mass accumulation per unit volume following hydration. In contrast, SR exhibited the lowest bulk density, which may be attributed to the formation of internal voids during moisture removal and thermal expansion. Similar reductions in bulk density have been documented in roasted chickpeas and other thermally processed legumes (Sharanagat et al., 2018; Qureshi et al., 2023). The highest true density observed in GR samples suggests compactness and reorganisation of the solid matrix during GR, potentially resulting from enzymatic modification of storage components. On the other hand, the lowest true density in PC samples (1.066 g/cm3) may be explained by starch gelatinisation and cellular disruption, which expand the internal granular structure and reduce the density of the solid fraction (Kittipongpatana et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2011).

Porosity values were highest in the SR, GR, and FD treatments, indicating substantial void formation associated with moisture loss and structural loosening during drying or GR. In contrast, the lowest porosity in the PC samples could be attributed to starch gelatinisation and the filling of intergranular spaces during hydrothermal treatment, consistent with previous observations in processed legume seeds (Jogihalli et al., 2017). Furthermore, the poor flow characteristics observed across all treatments, which indicate by Carr Index values higher than 80% and Hausner Ratio values exceeding 1.25, confirm the cohesive nature of legume flours. SR demonstrated the poorest flowability, whereas PC showed comparatively improved flow behaviour. These findings align with earlier reports suggesting that smaller and irregular particles exhibit higher cohesion and compressibility than larger and more uniform particles (Nkurikiye et al., 2023; Qureshi et al., 2023).

Colour analysis

Colour analysis revealed that processing significantly (p < 0.05) influenced the visual characteristics of rice bean flour (Table 4). The L* value (lightness) was highest in FD samples (86.16), indicating higher colour retention due to the low-temperature drying process, followed by SK (78.95) and SR (77.62) samples. In contrast, PC rice beans exhibited the lowest L* value (62.74), reflecting pronounced darkening caused by heat and moisture-induced browning reactions. The a* value (redness/greenness) was highest in GR flour (2.88 ± 0.01), possibly owing to enzymatic modifications and pigment transformation during GR, whereas FD samples showed a negative a* value (−0.62), indicating higher retention of green pigments. Similarly, the b* value (yellowness) was highest in GR (18.12) and lowest in PC (9.79), suggesting enhanced pigment development during GR and pigment degradation during thermal processing (Amanipour et al., 2024).

Functional properties

The functional properties of rice bean flour were significantly influenced (p < 0.05) by the applied processing methods, with GR and PC producing the most pronounced improvements. Among all treatments, PC exhibited the highest swelling index (5.31 g/g at 90 °C) (Table 3), which can be attributed to extensive starch gelatinisation during hydrothermal processing. Gelatinisation disrupts the crystalline structure of starch granules, facilitating water penetration, granule expansion, and swelling (Jogihalli et al., 2017). Similarly, the WAI was significantly higher (p < 0.05) in GR (3.20 g/g) and PC (3.06 g/g) than in the other treatments (Table 4), reflecting enhanced hydration capacity resulting from enzymatic degradation of starch and proteins during GR and the increased availability of hydrophilic groups following gelatinisation. The WSI followed a similar pattern, with GR (15.8 g/g) and PC (14.2 g/g), respectively, exhibiting the highest values owing to the formation of soluble carbohydrates, peptides, and other low-molecular-weight compounds during processing (Ghavidel & Prakash, 2006; Kemal et al., 2025).

The water absorption capacity (WAC) was highest in GR flour (8.63 g/g), primarily because GR activates hydrolytic enzymes that partially degrade storage macromolecules, exposing additional polar binding sites and increasing protein hydrophilicity (Amanipour et al., 2024). In contrast, the highest oil absorption capacity (OAC) was observed in SR flour (2.42 g/g), probably because dry-heat treatment exposed hydrophobic amino acid residues through partial protein denaturation, thereby enhancing lipid binding. Germinated flour also exhibited a relatively high OAC (2.28 g/g), suggesting that enzymatic modification of the protein matrix further improved its oil retention capacity (Jogihalli et al., 2017).

GR significantly improved the interfacial properties of rice bean flour. The highest foaming capacity (32.82%), foam stability (70.02%), emulsion capacity (58.9%), and emulsion stability (76.3%) were observed in GR flour (p < 0.05), reflecting increased protein solubility, improved molecular flexibility, and enhanced surface activity following partial proteolysis (Amanipour et al., 2024; Ghavidel & Prakash, 2006). In contrast, FD flour largely retained native protein functionality because of minimal thermal damage, whereas intensive thermal treatments, particularly PC and SR, reduced foaming and emulsifying properties owing to protein denaturation, aggregation, and reduced interfacial activity (Kemal et al., 2025). These findings are consistent with the FT-IR results, which indicated alterations in protein secondary structure through changes in the amide I and II regions (Fig. 4), and with the metabolomic profile, which revealed increased content of flavonoids, including quercetin, kaempferol, and naringenin in GR flour. Overall, GR was the most effective processing method for improving the techno-functional properties of rice bean flour, while PC mainly enhanced swelling and hydration, thereby increasing its suitability for value-added food applications.

Antioxidant activity

The bioactive and antioxidant properties of rice bean flour were significantly (p < 0.05) influenced by the applied processing methods, with GR and FD showing higher enhancement and retention of phytochemical compounds. Among all treatments, GR exhibited the highest total phenolic content (TPC) (8.42 mg GAE/g), total flavonoid content (TFC) (4.22 mg QE/g), and DPPH radical-scavenging activity (72.67%) (Table 5). These improvements were possibly attributed to the activation of the phenylpropanoid pathway during GR, which promotes the biosynthesis and release of phenolic compounds through enzymatic hydrolysis of bound phytochemicals (Cevallos-Casals & Cisneros-Zevallos, 2010; Dabeek & Marra, 2019). Similar increases in antioxidant activity have been reported in germinated legumes owing to enhanced accumulation of flavonoids and other secondary metabolites. FD bean samples also retained relatively high TPC (7.99 mg GAE/g) and TFC (3.99 mg QE/g), reflecting the ability of low-temperature sublimation to minimise thermal degradation and oxidative losses of thermolabile phytochemicals (Kessler et al., 2022). In contrast, SK samples exhibited moderate TPC (3.06 mg GAE/g) and DPPH radical-scavenging activity (33.19%), indicating that SK preserved part of the native antioxidant capacity without substantially stimulating phenolic biosynthesis.

Overall, GR was the most effective processing method for enhancing the phytochemical and antioxidant properties of rice bean flour, while FD effectively preserved phenolic compounds. In contrast, intensive thermal treatments significantly reduced bioactive constituents and antioxidant activity, highlighting the importance of selecting appropriate processing methods to maximise the nutritional and functional quality of rice bean flour.

Metabolomic profile of treated Rice bean samples

Untargeted high-resolution mass spectrometry (HR-MS)-based metabolomic profiling detected 10,533 metabolite features across the five rice bean processing treatments (Fig. 1 and Table 6). Among the treatments, GR showed the most diverse metabolomic profile, with 2851 metabolite features, followed by PC (2300), FD (2167), SK (1655), and SR (1596). A total of 283 metabolites were common across all treatments, representing the core rice bean metabolome, while treatment-specific metabolites accounted for the observed differences. Of these core metabolites, 155 were putatively annotated and are presented in Table 6. Treatment-dependent metabolite patterns were explored using unsupervised PCA and hierarchical clustering. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct treatment-specific clustering patterns, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 20.47% and 20.35% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 3B). Although the first two components accounted for 40.82% of the total variance, the clear separation of treatments indicates that each processing method generated a distinct metabolic fingerprint. The moderate variance explained reflects the complexity and high dimensionality of the untargeted metabolomic dataset, with additional variation distributed across higher-order principal components. Hierarchical clustering further confirmed treatment-specific metabolite patterns, demonstrating that each processing method generated a distinct metabolic fingerprint.

A total of 283 metabolites were common to all treatments, representing the core rice bean metabolome, whereas treatment-specific metabolites accounted for the observed variation. SK samples retained catechin, itaconic acid, and L-histidine, whereas GR promoted the accumulation of flavonoids, including quercetin, kaempferol, and naringenin, consistent with enhanced phenylpropanoid metabolism during sprouting (Dabeek & Marra, 2019). In contrast, SR favoured the retention or formation of relatively thermostable phytochemicals (genistein, daidzein, and gentisic acid), while thermolabile compounds, including catechin, were reduced following thermal processing (Mechchate et al., 2021). FD samples retained heat-sensitive metabolites, including isorhamnetin, hyperoside, and coumarin, reflecting the mild processing conditions that minimise thermal degradation (Al Hasani et al., 2023). PC resulted in a distinct metabolite profile characterised by compounds like lupeol, quinoline, and pinolenic acid, primarily reflecting processing-induced chemical transformations (Elebiju et al., 2023; Ramasinghe & Xu, 2025).

The metabolomic changes were consistent with the observed functional properties of the processed flours. Germinated flour, which exhibited superior hydration, foaming, and emulsifying properties, also contained hydrophilic metabolites and flavonoids, including quercetin, kaempferol, and naringenin, consistent with the activation of phenylpropanoid metabolism. Although treatment-specific metabolite profiles were identified, pathway enrichment analysis was not performed and should be included in future studies to provide deeper mechanistic insights into processing-induced metabolic changes. Meanwhile, the higher oil absorption capacity of sand-roasted flour corresponded with FT-IR evidence of protein structural modification, suggesting increased exposure of hydrophobic binding sites following dry-heat treatment. Along with FT-IR and XRD results, these findings indicate that different processing methods induce distinct structural modifications that collectively influence the techno-functional properties of rice bean flour.

Overall, metabolomic analysis demonstrated that processing significantly altered the composition of rice beans. GR produced the highest enrichment of bioactive metabolites, whereas FD effectively preserved thermolabile compounds. In contrast, SR and PC generated distinct processing-dependent metabolite profiles. These findings provide molecular insights into the effects of processing and support the utilisation of processed rice bean flour as a nutritionally enriched ingredient for functional food applications.

Abundant metabolites

Metabolites were obtained from five treatment groups, followed by detailed analysis of the identified metabolites. A substantial quantity of metabolites was acquired, with the most abundant including sedanolide, p-cymene, pentamethylbenzene, 2, 4-dimethyl-1-vinylbenzene, and 9S, 13R-12-oxophytodienoic acid. Bicyclohexyliden-2-one, dendryphiellic acid, 4-formyl-2-hydroxy-4-hexamethyl-trihydroxy-6-hydroxymethyl-oxanyloxycarbonyl-icosahydropicenyl-oxy-trihydroxyoxane-2-carboxylic acid. Corchori fatty acid F, 9-Oxononanoic acid, Octyl methoxycinnamate, 13(S)-HOTrE, Bis (4-ethylbenzylidene) sorbitol, 10-hydroxy-heptamethyl-oxahexacyclo-tetracosenone, 19-norandrostenedione, 1-linoleoyl glycerol, 3-vinyltoluene, and ethylbenzene were the predominant compounds in FD. Various functional compounds, fatty acids, and secondary metabolites are typically identified in rice bean seeds. The results demonstrated that the identified seed metabolite groups mostly affect the variance of functional genes rather than the taxonomic composition. Seed metabolites facilitate survival under climatic circumstances. These categories of metabolites are the principal nutritional components provided by indoor-farmed crops (Fig. 2).

FT-IR analysis

The FT-IR spectra provide comprehensive information regarding structural modifications in proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids induced by different processing treatments (Fig. 4). A broad absorption band observed around 3273 cm−1 in FD, PC, and SK samples corresponds to overlapping O—H stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups from carbohydrates and absorbed water, along with N—H stretching of proteins. The presence of this broad band indicates intact intermolecular hydrogen bonding networks within these matrices. In contrast, SR and GR samples exhibited multiple sharp peaks within the 3746–3981 cm−1 region, which suggests disruption and rearrangement of hydrogen bonding interactions. Dry heat roasting promotes moisture removal and structural dehydration, whereas GR activates endogenous enzymes that modify protein-carbohydrate associations and expose previously bound functional groups (Dong et al., 2023; Dzurendová et al., 2021).

In the aliphatic stretching region (2800–3000 cm−1), the prominent peak at approximately 2926 cm−1 corresponds to asymmetric CH₂ stretching vibrations associated with lipid chains and polysaccharide backbones. This peak remained well-defined in FD and PC samples, indicating the preservation of lipid and starch structural integrity under FD and moist heat conditions. However, the reduced intensity and slight shift observed in SR samples suggest thermal degradation or interaction between lipids and amylose chains, potentially resulting in amylose-lipid complex formation during high-temperature roasting (Dzurendová et al., 2021). Variations were observed in the protein fingerprint region (1500–1700 cm−1), which reflects conformational changes in protein structures. The increased intensity in PC samples indicates protein unfolding and denaturation caused by hydrothermal processing, which enhances exposure of peptide linkages. In GR samples, spectral modifications in this region are attributed to proteolytic degradation of storage proteins into smaller peptides and amino acids during sprouting. The distinct absorption bands observed at 2160 cm−1 and 1975 cm−1 in the sand-roasted (SR) samples may be associated with combination bands of carbonyl (C=O) stretching and C—H bending vibrations arising from heat-induced carbonyl-containing compounds. These spectral features are consistent with the formation of Maillard reaction intermediates and conjugated carbonyl structures during dry-heat processing (Tamanna & Mahmood, 2015). However, as FTIR alone cannot identify specific Maillard products, these assignments remain tentative and require confirmation through targeted chromatographic or mass spectrometric analyses. The carbohydrate fingerprint region (800–1200 cm−1) further demonstrates structural alterations in starch components. Characteristic peaks at 1073 cm−1 and 993 cm−1 correspond to C—O and C—C stretching vibrations of starch polysaccharides. Sharp spectral features retained in FD and SR samples indicate preservation of ordered crystalline starch structures. Meanwhile, broadening of these peaks in PC samples confirms starch gelatinisation resulting from the disruption of crystalline regions under combined heat and pressure treatment. GR rice bean samples exhibited noticeable spectral modifications due to activation of amylolytic enzymes, leading to hydrolysis of complex starch into simpler sugars and subsequent alteration of carbohydrate molecular organisation (Liu et al., 2024).

XRD analysis

XRD analysis indicated that rice bean starch predominantly exhibits an A-type crystalline polymorphic structure, characterised by prominent diffraction peaks at 2θ values of approximately 17°, 18°, and 23° (Fig. 5). These reflections are associated with the densely packed monoclinic unit cell arrangement commonly observed in cereal and legume starches, confirming the presence of a well-defined semi-crystalline organisation within the starch granules (Munjal et al., 2024).

X-ray diffractograms of rice bean starch after five processing treatments: soaking (SK), germination (GR), pressure cooking (PC), sand roasting (SR), and freeze-drying (FD).

The processing treatments influenced the extent of structural ordering differently. SK largely preserved the native crystalline architecture, as hydration primarily occurs within the amorphous regions of starch granules without sufficient thermal input to disrupt the crystalline lamellae. The retention of sharp diffraction peaks indicates minimal alterations in molecular ordering and preservation of starch crystallinity (Munjal et al., 2024). Similarly, FD effectively maintains crystalline integrity by removing water through sublimation under sub-zero conditions, thereby preventing starch gelatinisation and preserving the native A-type crystalline arrangement with limited thermal disorganisation (Xia et al., 2024). GR resulted in a moderate reduction in the diffraction peak intensity, reflecting a partial loss of crystallinity. This structural modification is attributed to the activation of endogenous hydrolytic enzymes, particularly α-amylase, during sprouting, which preferentially hydrolyses amorphous starch regions, followed by the gradual degradation of crystalline domains, ultimately reducing relative crystallinity (Liu et al., 2024). Among all the treatments, PC caused the most pronounced disruption of the crystalline structure. Exposure to combined heat and moisture above the gelatinisation temperature promoted extensive swelling of starch granules and dissociation of amylopectin double helices, leading to the collapse of the ordered crystalline regions. This transformation was evidenced by the broadening and flattening of the diffraction peaks, indicating the conversion of starch from a semi-crystalline to a predominantly amorphous state (Munjal et al., 2024). SR treatment involves dry heat processing, which reduces starch crystallinity, but through thermal dextrinisation rather than hydrothermal gelatinisation. High-temperature treatment under limited moisture conditions induces cleavage and rearrangement of starch chains, resulting in altered diffraction intensity and increased structural disorder, which is distinct from moist heat-induced structural transitions (Munjal et al., 2024). A distinct sharp diffraction peak was observed at a higher 2θ angle (∼80.86°) across all processing treatments, lying beyond the conventional starch crystalline region (<30°), and was attributed to highly ordered inorganic crystalline phases. Similar XRD reflections at high 2θ angles have been reported in legume seeds and are associated with mineral complexes including calcium oxalate or phytate salts (Milán-Noris et al., 2017; Munjal et al., 2024). The persistence of this peak, despite processing-induced modifications to starch structure, highlights the coexistence of organic starch crystallinity and stable inorganic mineral phases within the rice bean matrix. XRD analysis demonstrated that FD effectively preserves native A-type starch crystallinity by preventing thermal disruption; soaking induces minimal structural changes; GR moderately reduces crystallinity through enzymatic hydrolysis; PC extensively disrupts crystalline order through gelatinisation; and SR promotes partial structural disorder through dextrinisation. The stability of mineral crystallites across treatments further confirms the resistance of inorganic components to processing-induced structural changes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that post-harvest processing significantly influenced the physicochemical, functional, structural, antioxidant, and metabolomic characteristics of rice beans. Among the treatments, GR was the most effective in enhancing nutritional quality, yielding the highest protein, total phenolic content, total flavonoid content, DPPH radical scavenging activity, foaming capacity, and emulsion stability. Metabolomic analysis further revealed the enrichment of bioactive flavonoids, including quercetin, kaempferol, and naringenin derivatives. FD effectively preserved bioactive metabolites, native starch crystallinity, and colour, whereas PC improved hydration-related properties, including swelling index and water absorption, making it suitable for instant food applications. Although SR reduced phenolic content and antioxidant activity, it enhanced oil absorption capacity and retained thermostable metabolites, supporting its application in lipid-rich and savoury food products. Metabolomic, FT-IR, XRD, and functional data provided complementary characterisation of processing-induced changes and demonstrated that structural modifications were closely associated with changes in the techno-functional properties of rice bean flour, providing mechanistic insights into processing-dependent quality enhancement. These findings indicate that no single processing method is universally optimal; instead, each offers distinct advantages depending on the intended food application. A limitation of this study is that all treatments were initiated from soaked beans, with no untreated raw control included. Consequently, the reported effects represent changes relative to the soaked state rather than the native seed. Future studies should incorporate raw controls and complementary metabolomic analyses using both positive and negative ionisation modes to achieve a more comprehensive characterisation of processing-induced changes. Overall, this study provides a scientific basis for the valorisation of rice bean as a sustainable, nutrient-rich ingredient and demonstrates how targeted processing strategies can be used to develop functional foods tailored to specific nutritional and technological requirements.
