The Localization Method for High-dimensional Inequalities
Yunbum Kook, Santosh S. Vempala

Abstract
We survey the localization method for proving inequalities in high dimension, pioneered by Lovász and Simonovits (1993), and its stochastic extension developed by Eldan (2012). The method has found applications in a surprising wide variety of settings, ranging from its original motivation in isoperimetric inequalities to optimization, concentration of measure, and bounding the mixing rate of Markov chains. At heart, the method converts a given instance of an inequality (for a set or distribution in high dimension) into a highly structured instance, often just one-dimensional.



1
Introduction

High-dimensional inequalities are a rich subject with many motivating
conjectures from a variety of fields. As an example, consider the
following statement:

Any convex body
\mathcal{K}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the property that the uniform
distribution over it has zero mean and identity covariance contains
a ball of radius at least
\sqrt{(n+2)/n}
.

This statement is a (true)
n
-dimensional inequality with constraints.
Given that there are infinitely many convex bodies, and any convex
body can be made to satisfy the constraints after an affine transformation,
how does one prove such a statement?

As a second example, take the classical isoperimetry of a Gaussian:
the extremal isoperimetric subset for any Gaussian, i.e., one that
minimizes

\frac{\gamma^{+}(\partial S)}{\min\gamma(S),\gamma(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus S)}

over all measurable subsets
S
, is given by a halfspace (the numerator
is the boundary measure). The extreme symmetry of the Gaussian allows
proofs that proceed by symmetrization. Consider the following substantial
generalization:

For any logconcave distribution in any dimension, there exists
a halfspace that gives an asymptotically optimal isoperimetric subset.

Here, by asymptotically optimal, we mean within a universal constant
factor, and this statement is the famous KLS conjecture
[
32
]
.
At the moment, the current best bound is that halfspaces are within

O(\sqrt{\log n})
of an optimal subset, and the method surveyed
here has played an important role in the progress so far. In fact,
localization has been a principal tool for progress or resolution
of many well-known inequalities in high dimension. Let us first recall
some of these inequalities along with relevant definitions.

2.2.1
The Kannan-Lovász-Simonovits (KLS) conjecture

Definition 2.3

(Cheeger and KLS constant)
.

For a probability measure
\pi
over

\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
Cheeger isoperimetric constant
for
\pi
is defined
by

\psi_{\mathsf{Ch}}(\pi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}}{{=}}\inf_{S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\pi^{+}(\partial S)}{\min\pi(S),\pi(S^{c})}\,,

where
\pi^{+}(\partial S)=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\pi(S+\epsilon B^{n})-\pi(S)}{\varepsilon}

where
B^{n}
is the unit Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The KLS constant
for
\pi
is simply the inverse of the Cheeger constant:

\psi_{\mathsf{KLS}}(\pi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}}{{=}}\psi_{\mathsf{Ch}}^{-1}(\pi)\,.

It will be convenient to only consider
isotropic
logconcave
measures, so we define

\psi_{\mathsf{Ch}}(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}}{{=}}\inf_{\text{iso. }\pi}\psi_{\mathsf{Ch}}(\pi)\,,\quad\&\quad\psi_{\mathsf{KLS}}(n)=\sup_{\text{iso. }\pi}\psi_{\mathsf{KLS}}(\pi)\,,

where both infimum and supremum run over all isotropic logconcave
measures in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The KLS conjecture
[
32
]
posits that these parameters
are bounded by a universal constant, i.e., the boundary measure of
any subset is at least a universal constant times the smaller of the
measure of the subset or its complement, for any logconcave measure.

Conjecture 2.4

(KLS conjecture)
.

Let
\pi
be a logconcave
probability measure over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with covariance
\Sigma
. Then,

\psi_{\mathsf{KLS}}(\pi)=\Theta(\lVert\Sigma\rVert^{1/2})\,,

where
\lVert\Sigma\rVert
indicates the largest eigenvalue of
\Sigma
.
Equivalently,

\psi_{\mathsf{KLS}}(n)=\Theta(1)\,.

The conjecture was originally motivated by the analysis of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}

for sampling uniformly from convex bodies and implies the thin-shell
conjecture, which in turn implies the slicing conjecture. This geometric
isoperimetric constant is closely connected to a functional inequality
called the
Poincaré inequality.

Definition 2.5

(Poincaré inequality)
.

A probability measure
\pi
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
said to satisfy a
Poincaré inequality
with constant
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)>0

if for any locally-Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}f\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2}]\,,

(
\mathsf{PI}
)

where
\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}f=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[(f-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f)^{2}

is the variance of
f
with respect to
\pi
.

The next propositions establishes their equivalence for logconcave
measures.

Proposition 2.6

(Equivalence between Cheeger and Poincaré)
.

The Cheeger implies
the Poincaré, and it can be reversed for logconcave distributions.
To be precise,

\frac{1}{4}\leq\frac{\psi_{\mathsf{KLS}}^{2}(\pi)}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)}\leq 9\,,

where the first inequality is due to Cheeger
[
14
]
,
and the second is the Buser–Ledoux inequality
[
10
,
47
]
.

Using this equivalence between (

PI

) and the Cheeger constant,
we can state the KLS conjecture in terms of Poincaré constants.

Conjecture 2.7

(KLS conjecture via PI)
.

Let
\pi
be a logconcave
probability measure over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with covariance
\Sigma
. Then,

\lVert\Sigma\rVert\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\lesssim\lVert\Sigma\rVert\,.

(
\mathsf{KLS}
)

We note that
\lVert\Sigma\rVert\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)
always holds (due to a
test function
(x-\mu)\cdot\theta
for a suitable unit vector
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
).
Then, the KLS conjecture says that a linear function almost
saturates

in a sense that
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\lesssim\lVert\Sigma\rVert
.

As mentioned earlier, we can check that it suffices to focus on isotropic
logconcave measures:

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n):=\sup C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)
where the supremum runs over all isotropic
logconcave distributions
\pi
. Then,

C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)\,\lVert\Sigma\rVert\,.

Proof.

Let
X\sim\pi
,
Y=\Sigma^{-1/2}X
, and
\nu:=\operatorname{law}Y
. Then,
\nu

is isotropic and logconcave, and

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}f

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}_{\nu}(f\circ\Sigma^{1/2})\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\lVert\nabla(f\circ\Sigma^{1/2})\rVert^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\lVert\Sigma\rVert\,\lVert\nabla f\circ\Sigma^{1/2}\rVert^{2}]\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)\,\lVert\Sigma\rVert\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2}]\,.

The claim follows from the definition of (

PI

) for
\pi
.
∎

The current best bound on
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)
is due to Klartag, established
following a long line of progress
[
49
,
16
,
34
,
42
]
.

Theorem 2.9

(
[
42
]
)
.

C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)\lesssim\log n
(equivalently,
\psi_{\mathsf{KLS}}(n)\lesssim\sqrt{\log n}
).

The thin-shell conjecture

We move onto a second conjecture called the

Definition 2.10

(Thin-shell constant)

For an isotropic probability measure

\sigma_{\mathsf{TS}}^{2}(\pi)=\sigma_{\pi}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{n}\,\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(\lVert\cdot\rVert^{2})\,.

We are now ready to state the thin-shell conjecture.

Conjecture 2.11

(Thin-shell conjecture)
.

The thin-shell constant

\sigma_{n}^{2}=O(1)\,.

(
\mathsf{TS}
)

To parse its meaning, let us consider an isotropic Gaussian

It is well-known from a standard concentration result that

Equivalent formulations.

One can formulate (

density. Let
\pi_{S}
denote the uniform distribution over a compact
set

.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a convex body for which

\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}=1\,,\quad\operatorname{cov}\pi_{\mathcal{K}}=L_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}I_{n}\ \text{for some }L_{\mathcal{K}}>0\,.

Clearly,
\pi_{L_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}\mathcal{K}}

\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(L_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}\mathcal{K})}=L_{\mathcal{K}}^{n}\,.

Then, (

\bar{L}_{n}:=\sup_{\mathcal{K}}L_{\mathcal{K}}=\sup_{\mathcal{K}}\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigl(\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(L_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}\mathcal{K})}\bigr)^{1/n}=O(1)\,,

\hat{L}_{n}:=\sup_{\pi}\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\pi^{1/n}=O(1)\,,

where the supremum runs over all isotropic logconcave distributions.

2.4
Strongly logconcave isoperimetry

For
\mathrm{d}\pi\propto\exp(-V)\,\mathrm{d}x
, the probability measure
\pi

is
t
-strongly logconcave if
V-\frac{t}{2}\,\lVert\cdot\rVert^{2}

is convex. This is a strictly smaller class than logconcave distributions
and enjoys stronger properties. For instance, it is a classical result
that the Poincaré constant of
t
-strongly logconcave distributions
is bounded by
1/t
. This can be seen as a consequence of the
Brascamp–Lieb
inequality
: for a probability measure
\pi\propto e^{-V}
with strictly
convex
V
and any
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}f\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\langle\nabla f,(\nabla^{2}V)^{-1}\,\nabla f\rangle\,.

(2.1)

This
1/t
-bound can also be explained by the fact that the Poincaré
inequality is implied by the stronger log-Sobolev inequality (i.e.,

C_{\mathsf{PI}}\leq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}
).

Definition 2.19

(Log-Sobolev constant)
.

A probability measure
\pi
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}

is said to satisfy a
log-Sobolev inequality
(LSI) with constant

C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)>0
if for any locally-Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi}(f^{2})\leq 2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2}]\,,

(
\mathsf{LSI}
)

where
\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi}(f^{2}):=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f^{2}\log f^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f^{2}]\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f^{2}]
.

It is also classical that
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\leq 1/t
for any
t
-strongly
logconcave distributions
\pi
, and this can be established in multiple
ways (e.g., Caffarelli’s contraction theorem
[
11
]

or the Bakry–Émery condition
[
2
]
). Below, we
demonstrate another way to see this through connections to a logarithmic-Cheeger
inequality.

Since
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\leq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\leq 1/t
for
t
-strongly logconcave
distributions
\pi
, the bound
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\leq 1/t
becomes useless
as
t\to 0
. We have already seen that
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)

for any probability measures
\pi
, and thus
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\leq 1/t

for
t
-strongly logconcave
\pi
. Hence, the KLS conjecture essentially
asks for a “refined” bound for
0
-strongly (i.e.,
all
)
logconcave distributions, asserting an
O(\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert)
bound
instead of the
1/t
-bound.

LSI for logconcave distributions with bounded support.

Although any logconcave distribution has a finite Poincaré constant
(e.g.,
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\lesssim\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\log n
due to the KLS bound),
this is not the case for (

LSI

). It is well-known that having
a finite LSI constant implies Gaussian-like tail decay, which is clearly
false, for example, for exponential distributions. Nonetheless, some
results are known for logconcave distributions with finite support.
For instance, Frieze and Kannan established that
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim D^{2}

[
28
]
for any logconcave distributions with support of diameter

D
, and this was improved to
O(D)
for

isotropic logconcave
distributions
[
49
]
. Later, these two bounds were unified
as
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim DC_{\mathsf{PI}}^{1/2}(\pi)

[
43
, Theorem 1.5]

(up to logarithmic factors), since
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\lesssim\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\log n\leq D^{2}
.

Log-Cheeger and LSI.

Recall that the Cheeger inequality is an equivalent “geometric”
formulation of (

PI

) for logconcave distributions. It turns
out that there exists a similar equivalence for (

LSI

). To
see this, we recall the
logarithmic-Cheeger inequality
.

Definition 2.20
.

The
log-Cheeger constant

C_{\mathsf{logCh}}(\pi)
of a probability measure

\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as

C_{\mathsf{logCh}}(\pi)=\inf_{S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},\pi(S)\leq\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\pi(\partial S)}{\pi(S)\sqrt{\log(1/\pi(S))}}\,,

where the infimum runs overs every measurable set
S
of measure
at most
1/2
.

It is obvious that
C_{\mathsf{logCh}}\leq C_{\mathsf{Ch}}
due to
\sqrt{\log(1/\pi(S))}
,
and Ledoux showed that the log-Cheeger inequality is equivalent to
(

LSI

) through
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\asymp C_{\mathsf{logCh}}^{-2}(\pi)

45
]
,
just as
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\asymp C_{\mathsf{Ch}}^{-2}(\pi)
for any logconcave distribution

\pi
. Ledoux also showed that
C_{\mathsf{logCh}}(\pi)\gtrsim t^{1/2}
for any

-strongly logconcave
\pi

[
46
]
, which implies
that
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\asymp C_{\mathsf{logCh}}^{-2}(\pi)\lesssim t
as desired.

Theorem 2.21

For any

-strongly logconcave
distribution

\frac{\pi(\partial S)}{\pi(S)\sqrt{\log(1/\pi(S))}}\gtrsim\sqrt{t}\,.

2.7
Spectral gap and entropy

The mixing rate of a Markov chain can be bounded using the
spectral
gap
of its transition operator. Doing so is typically easier for
distributions with additional structure. Chen and Eldan
[
15
]

proposed localization as an approach to reduce bounding the spectral
gap for general families of target distributions for sampling by Markov
chains to specialized families. Using this, they improved the analysis
of Markov chains for several well-known problems, notably in both
discrete and continuous state spaces. Their approach has since been
employed by other authors to simplify and refine such analyses. Since
this (active) topic deserves a separate and detailed discussion, we
do not include it in the present survey.

The Classical Method

Here we present the classical or standard localization method, introduced
by Lovász and Simonovits
[
55
]
and refined by Kannan,
Lovász, and Simonovits
[
32
]
. The method can be captured
as a general concise lemma.

3.1
The main idea: bisection

Lemma 3.1

(Localization Lemma
[
32
]
)
.

Let
g,h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be lower
semi-continuous integrable functions such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x>0\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(x)\,\mathrm{d}x>0\,.

Then, there exist two points
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and an affine function

\ell:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

\int_{0}^{1}\ell(t)^{n-1}\,g\bigl((1-t)a+tb\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t>0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\int_{0}^{1}\ell(t)^{n-1}\,h\bigl((1-t)a+tb\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t>0\,.

The points
a,b
represent the endpoints of an interval and one may
think of
\ell(t)^{n-1}
as proportional to the cross-sectional area
of an infinitesimal cone with
t
going along its axis. The lemma
says if
g,h
have positive integrals in
\mathbb{R}^{n},
there there is
a cone truncated at
a
and
b
, so that the integrals of
g
and

h
over it are positive. Without loss of generality, we can assume
that
a,b
are in the union of the supports of
g
and
h
.

Figure 3.1:
*

Truncated cone

Proof.

WLOG we can assume that the functions
g,h
are continuous. This
is because they are limits of monotone increasing sequences of continuous
functions
g_{k},h_{k}
where

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int g_{k}=\int g>0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int h_{k}=\int h>0\,.

Hence there exists
k_{0}
s.t. for all
k\geq k_{0}
,
\int g_{k},\int h_{k}>0
.
We can apply the lemma to these and derive the conclusion for
g,h
.
Moreover, it suffices to prove the final conclusion with
\geq 0
,
as we can always consider
g-\gamma,h-\gamma>0
for some strictly
positive continuous small enough function
\gamma
.

The main idea of the proof is “bisection”. Let
H
be any halfspace
such that

\int_{H}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

Let us call this a bisecting halfspace. Now either

\int_{H}h(x)\,\mathrm{d}x>0\quad\mbox{ or }\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus H}h(x)\,\mathrm{d}x>0\,.

Thus, either
H
or its complementary halfspace will have positive
integrals for both
g
and
h
, reducing the domain of the integrals
from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to a halfspace. If we could repeat this, we might hope
to reduce the dimensionality of the domain. For any
(n-2)
-dimensional
affine subspace
L
, there is a bisecting halfspace containing
L

in its bounding hyperplane. To see this, let
H
be a halfspace containing

L
in its boundary. Rotating
H
about
L
we get a family of
halfspaces with the same property. This family includes
H^{\prime}
, the
complementary halfspace of
H
. The function
\int_{H}g-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus H}g

switches sign from
H
to
H^{\prime}
. Since this is a continuous family,
there must be a halfspace for which the function is zero.

If we take all
(n-2)
-dimensional affine subspaces defined by
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ x_{i}=r_{1},\,x_{j}=r_{2}}}\right\}

where
r_{1},r_{2}
are rational, then the intersection of all the
corresponding bisecting halfspaces is a line or a point (by choosing
only rational values for
x_{i}
, we are considering a countable
intersection). To see why it is a line or a point, assume we are left
with a two or higher dimensional set. Since the intersection is convex,
there is a point in its interior with at least two coordinates that
are rational, say
x_{1}=r_{1}
and
x_{2}=r_{2}
. But then there
is a bisecting halfspace
H
that contains the affine subspace given
by
x_{1}=r_{1},\,x_{2}=r_{2}
in its boundary, and so it properly
partitions the current set. Thus the limit of this bisection process
is a function supported on an interval (which could be a single point).

In fact, this gives a sequence of convex bodies, where
K_{0}
can
be a large enough ball, and
K_{i+1}=K_{i}\cap H_{i}
, the bisecting
halfspace found at step
i
. Define
K=\cap_{i}K_{i}
. We have just
argued that
K
is an interval
[a,b]
. If
a=b
, then the conclusion
follows by continuity with
\ell=1
. So assume
a\neq b
and by
scaling and shifting,
a=0,b=e_{1}
. Consider the following sequence
of functions defined on
[0,1]
:

\psi_{i}(t)=\bigl(\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{n-1}(K_{i}\cap Z_{t})}{\operatorname{vol}(K_{i})}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\,,

where
Z_{t}
is the slice
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{1}=t}}\right\}
.
Then with
\alpha_{i}=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\in K_{i}:x_{1}}}\right\}
and

\beta_{i}=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\in K_{i}:x_{1}}}\right\}
, we have
\alpha_{i}\leq 0,\beta_{i}\geq 0

and
\alpha_{i}\rightarrow 0,\beta_{i}\rightarrow 1
. Moreover, by
the Brunn–Minkowski inequality
\psi_{i}
is a non-negative concave
function. Then there is a subsequence of indices
i
for which
\psi_{i}

converges to a limit. To see this, note that
\psi_{i}
is uniformly
equicontinuous in every interval
[s,t]
with
0<s<t<1
, and by
the Arzela–Ascoli theorem , the sequence has a pointwise convergent
subsequence. The limit
\psi
is also non-negative and concave with
the same integral, i.e.,
\int\psi(t)^{n-1}\,dt=1
. It follows that

\int_{0}^{1}\psi(t)^{n-1}\,g\bigl((1-t)a+tb\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t\geq 0,\quad\int_{0}^{1}\psi(t)^{n-1}\,h\bigl((1-t)a+tb\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t\geq 0\,.

To see this, note that

\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(K_{i})}\int_{K_{i}}g(x)=\int_{\alpha_{i}}^{\beta_{i}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(K_{i}\cap Z_{t})}\int_{K_{i}\cap Z_{t}}g(y,t)\,\mathrm{d}y\Bigr)\,\psi_{i}(t)^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

where
x=(y,t)
with
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1},t\in\mathbb{R}.
Then since the LHS is
non-negative and the RHS approaches
\int_{0}^{1}g(0,t)\,\psi(t)^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}t
,
the conclusion follows.

The last part of the proof is to argue that we can take
\psi
to
be a nonnegative linear function, reducing the extremal functions
to a small parameter family (namely,
a,b,
and the values of the
function at
a
and
b
). To do this consider a
\psi
that has
(i) minimal support
\lVert a-b\rVert
; WLOG assume
a=0,b=e_{1}
, and
(ii) is linear in the segment
[\alpha,\beta]
for
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq 1

with
\beta-\alpha
is maximal. It will be useful to view
\psi

as defining a convex body
K^{\prime}
whose slices are simplices:

0\leq t\leq 1,\quad x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\geq 0,\quad x_{2}+\ldots+x_{n}\leq\psi(t)\,.

In words the slice of
K^{\prime}
at
x_{t}=t
is the standard simplex
given by the convex hull of

\{te_{1},te_{1}+\psi(t)\,e_{2},\ldots,te_{1}+\psi(t)\,e_{i},\ldots,te_{1}+\psi(t)\,e_{n}\}\,.

The slice at
x_{t}=t
has volume
\psi(t)^{n-1}/(n-1)!
. Hence,
if we define
\hat{g}(x)=g(x_{1}e_{1}),\hat{\,h}(x)=h(x_{1}e_{1})
,
then

\int_{K^{\prime}}\hat{g}=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\int_{0}^{1}\psi(t)^{n-1}\,g(te_{1})\,\mathrm{d}t\geq 0

and similarly for
\int_{K^{\prime}}\hat{h}\geq 0
. WLOG we can assume that

\int_{K^{\prime}}\hat{g}=0
.

Going to an affine needle

The overall proof idea is to subdivide
K^{\prime}
with a hyperplane in
such a way that one of the resulting halfspaces satisfies the same
two inequalities but violates either (i) or (ii). To choose this hyperplane,
we first fix an
(n-2)
-dimensional subspace
A=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x:x_{1}=\sigma,\,x_{2}+\ldots+x_{n}=\tau}}\right\}

where
0<\sigma<1
and
\tau>0
. The hyperplane will be chosen to
contain
A
and bisect the function
\hat{g}
so that
\int_{L}\hat{g}=\int_{L^{\prime}}\hat{g}=0

in both resulting convex bodies
L
and
L^{\prime}
. Suppose
\int_{L}h\geq 0
.
Then we can infer that
L
must intersect both
Z_{0}
and
Z_{1}
,
otherwise the function
\psi
restricted to
L
contradicts the
(i), the minimality of the support of
\psi
.

We now consider 3 cases:

1.

\psi(0)=\psi(1)=0
. For this case, let
\sigma=1/2
. Then
L

must contain the segment
[0,1]\,e_{1}
, for any
\tau>0
, else
it contradicts (i). Then there is a linear function
\ell(t)
with

\ell(0),\ell(1)\geq 0,\ell(1/2)=\tau
such that the function
\psi

restricted to
L
is
\psi_{L}(t)=\min\{\psi(t),\ell(t)\}
. But
then as
\tau\rightarrow 0
,
\ell(t)
tends to zero in the interval

[0,1]
and so
\psi_{L}
is linear in a larger and larger sub-interval
tending to
[0,1]
contradicting (ii).

2.

\psi(0)=0,\psi(1)>0
. For this case, consider
\tau=\psi(1)\,\sigma
.
Now if
L
does not contain
[0,1]\,e_{1}
, then
\psi_{L}(0)=\psi_{L}(1)=0
,
taking us back to the first case. Otherwise,
\psi_{L}
is linear
in the interval
[\sigma,1]
and hence for small enough
\sigma

this again contradicts (ii).

3.

\psi(0),\psi(1)>0
. Consider the family of continuous, nonnegative,
convex functions
\eta:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfying
\eta(t)\leq\psi(t)

for all
t\in[0,1]
. Let

K_{\eta}=K^{\prime}\cap\{x:x_{2}+\ldots+x_{n}\geq\eta(x_{1})\}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:x_{1}\in[0,1],\,\eta(x_{1})\leq x_{2}+\ldots+x_{n}\leq\psi(x_{1})\}\,.

Then we choose
\eta
so that (iii)
\int_{0}^{1}\eta(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

is maximal while still satisfying

\int_{K_{\eta}}\hat{g}=0\,,\quad\int_{K_{\eta}}\hat{h}\geq 0\,.

Then, we can assume that
\eta(0)<\psi(0),\eta(1)<\psi(1)
. If not,
we consider the function
\psi-\eta
and this takes us back to one
of the previous cases. Now let
(\sigma,\tau)
be the intersection
of the segments
[(0,\eta(0)),(1,\psi(1))]
and
[(0,\psi(0)),(1,\eta(1))]
.
Let
M,M^{\prime}
be the partition of
K_{\eta}
such that
M
does not

and

\int_{M}\hat{g}=\int_{M^{\prime}}\hat{g}=0\,.

Then, if
\int_{M}\hat{h}\geq 0
, this contradicts (iii), the maximality
of
\eta
. Hence,
\int_{M}\hat{h}<0
and the truncated cone
K^{\prime}\setminus M

satisfies

\int_{K^{\prime}\setminus M}\hat{g}=\int_{K^{\prime}}\hat{g}-\int_{M}\hat{g}=0\qquad\int_{K^{\prime}\setminus M}\hat{h}=\int_{K^{\prime}}\hat{h}-\int_{M}\hat{h}\geq 0\,,

which contradicts (ii), the maximality of the linear subinterval of

\psi
.

This complete the proof.

Next we give alternative versions of Lemma
3.1

which are sometimes more convenient in applications.

The first is in terms of single inequality on products of integrals.
Note that an
n
-dimensional needle
N
is specified by a pair of
points
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a linear function
\ell:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
The integral over a needle is defined as

\int_{N}f=\int_{0}^{1}f\bigl((1-t)a+tb\bigr)\,\ell(t)^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

Lemma 3.2

(Localization: Product form])
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be nonnegative,
continuous functions. Then, the following are equivalent:

1.

For every convex body
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},
we have

\int_{\mathcal{K}}f_{1}\int_{\mathcal{K}}f_{2}\leq\int_{\mathcal{K}}f_{3}\int_{\mathcal{K}}f_{4}\,.

2.

For every needle
N
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\int_{N}f_{1}\int_{N}f_{2}\leq\int_{N}f_{3}\int_{N}f_{4}\,.

The next version is in terms of exponential needles, i.e., we replace
the linear functions obtained in the one-dimensional case in Lemma
3.1

with truncated exponentials.

Lemma 3.3

(Localization: Exponential Needles)
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}

be nonnegative, continuous functions. Then the following are equivalent:

1.

For every logconcave function
F:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have

\int_{a}^{b}F(x)\,f_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\int_{a}^{b}F(x)\,f_{2}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{a}^{b}F(x)\,f_{3}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\int_{a}^{b}F(x)\,f_{4}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

2.

For every subinterval
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]\subseteq[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
and every

\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\int_{a^{\prime}}^{b^{\prime}}e^{\gamma t}f_{1}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\int_{a^{\prime}}^{b^{\prime}}e^{\gamma t}f_{2}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{a^{\prime}}^{b^{\prime}}e^{\gamma t}f_{3}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\int_{a^{\prime}}^{b^{\prime}}e^{\gamma t}f_{4}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

Remark 3.4
.

We can also write the exponential needle version of localization in
terms of two inequalities or one equality and one inequality: For
every logconcave
F:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}

\int_{a}^{b}Ff_{1}=0,\qquad\int_{a}^{b}Ff_{2}>0

if and only if for every subinterval
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]\subseteq[a,b]
,
and every
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,

\int_{a^{\prime}}^{b^{\prime}}e^{\gamma t}f_{1}=0,\qquad\int_{a^{\prime}}^{b^{\prime}}e^{\gamma t}f_{2}>0\,.

We conclude this section with a nice interpretation of the localization
lemma by Fradelizi and Guedon. They also give a version that extends

(Reformulated Localization Lemma

]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a compact convex set in

and

h(x)=f(x)e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{\mathsf{T}}Bx}/\int f(y)e^{-\frac{1}{2}y^{\mathsf{T}}By}\,\mathrm{d}y

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is an integrable logconcave function
and

is positive definite. Then

is logconcave and for any
measurable subset

of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with

\frac{h(\partial S)}{h(S)\sqrt{\log(1/h(S))}}\gtrsim\frac{1}{\lVert B^{-1}\rVert^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,.

In other words, the expansion of

is higher for subsets of smaller
measure; when

, then the RHS is
\sqrt{t}
.

Annealing.

The analysis of the Gaussian Cooling algorithm for volume computation

[
19
]
uses localization. The algorithm is based on sampling
from a sequence of distributions with each distribution giving a warm
start for the next and ensuring that an estimator for the ratio of
consecutive integrals has small variance. The proof of the inequality
for bounding the variance uses the localization lemma.

Anti-concentration.

The proof of the Carbery–Wright anti-concentration inequality uses
the localization lemma to reduce to one-dimensional distributions,
and then verifies the resulting one-dimensional inequality.

Optimization.

The proof that the Universal Barrier in the Interior-Point Method
for Convex Optimization in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
has the optimal value of

for its self-concordance parameter is based on the localization lemma

[
51
]
.

4
The Stochastic Method

In this section, we present the stochastic version of localization,
introduced by Eldan
[
26
]
and developed further subsequently
by several researchers. While classical localization can be viewed
as a deterministic reduction from an arbitrary distribution to one
with one-dimensional support, stochastic localization instead decomposes
a given distribution into a convex combination (distribution) over
simpler distributions, which satisfy nice properties with high probability.
This decomposition itself is achieved by a simple Martingale process
applied to the original density. Roughly speaking, it replaces the
hyperplane decomposition of standard localization with a more general
weighted decomposition.

Itô calculus.

Before we proceed, we review the basics of Itô calculus for the
reader’s convenience. Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a vector-valued Itô
process in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the form

X_{t}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\quad\text{ for }t\geq 0\,,

where
b_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the
drift
coefficient,
\sigma_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}

is the
diffusion
coefficient, and
W_{t}
is an
m
-dimensional
Brownian motion. This is often written in differential form as

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\,.

When
X_{t}
is a real-valued Itô process, the
quadratic
variation

[X]_{t}
is defined as

[X]_{t}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{t_{i}}-X_{t_{n-1}})^{2}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

where
\{t_{i}\}_{0\leq i\leq n}
is a partition of
[0,t]
whose
mesh
\max_{i\in[n]}|t_{i}-t_{i-1}|
going to zero as
n\to\infty
.
When
X_{t}
is vector-valued, the quadratic variation generalizes
to the matrix-valued process

[X]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\sigma_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathrm{d}s\,

and
\mathrm{d}[X^{i},X^{j}]_{t}=(\sigma_{t}\sigma_{t}^{\top})_{ij}dt
. For
instance, we can readily check that
[W]_{t}=tI
.

Itô’s formula
describes how the process
X_{t}
transforms
under a smooth function
f
. For a twice-differentiable function

f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the new Itô process
(f(X_{t}))_{t\geq 0}
is
given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}f(X_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}f(X_{t})}{\mathrm{d}X^{i}}\,\mathrm{d}X^{i}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}f(X_{t})}{\mathrm{d}X^{i}\mathrm{d}X^{j}}\,\mathrm{d}[X^{i},X^{j}]_{t}

(4.1)

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla f(X_{t}),\mathrm{d}X_{t}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\,\langle\nabla^{2}f(X_{t}),\mathrm{d}[X]_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigl(\langle\nabla f(X_{t}),b_{t}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\,\langle\nabla^{2}f(X_{t}),\sigma_{t}\sigma_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\rangle\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\langle\sigma_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla f(X_{t}),\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle\,,

where
\langle A,B\rangle:=\operatorname{tr}(A^{\mathsf{T}}B)
for two matrices
A,B
. When
f

is a vector-valued function, the formula applies component-wise.

4.1
The main idea: infinitesimal re-weighting

Stochastic Localization (SL) is a continuous transformation applied
to a probability density. It is defined as follows for any distribution

p
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle=p

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=p_{t}(x)\,\langle x-b_{t},\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle\ \text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,,

(
\mathsf{SL}
-
\mathsf{SDE}
)

where
b_{t}=\int x\,\mathrm{d}p_{t}(x)
is the barycenter of
p_{t}
.
Its properties are summarized below:

1.

The density
p_{t}
at time

is a probability measure over
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(i.e.,
\int\mathrm{d}p_{t}=1
):

\mathrm{d}\int p_{t}=\int\mathrm{d}p_{t}=\Bigl<\int(x-b_{t})\,\mathrm{d}p_{t}(x),\mathrm{d}W_{t}\Bigr>=\langle 0,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle=0\,.

2.

It is is a martingale with respect to the “filtration induced by
the Brownian motion”, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}p_{t}(x)=p(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
where the expectation is taken over the randomness of
\mathrm{d}W_{t}

(not the measure
p
or
p_{t}
).

3.

For a function

, the Itô derivative of
the martingale

is:

\mathrm{d}M_{t}=\int F(x)\,\langle x-b_{t},\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle\,p_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)\,.

(
\mathsf{MG}
)

4.

The solution at time
t
can be explicitly stated as follows:

p_{t}(x)\propto p(x)\,\exp\bigl(c_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}x-\frac{t}{2}\,\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,.

(
\mathsf{SL}
-
\mathsf{pdf}
)

Hence, for any

is
t

since

is logconcave.

An alternative equivalent definition is often useful.

Definition 4.1
.

Given a logconcave density

c_{0}=0\,,\quad\mathrm{d}c_{t}=\mu_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W_{t}\,,

p_{t}(x)=\frac{e^{c_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}x-\frac{t}{2}\,\lVert x\rVert^{2}}p(x)}{\int e^{c_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}y-\frac{t}{2}\,\lVert y\rVert^{2}}p(y)\,\mathrm{d}y}\,,\quad\mu_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}}X\,.

We will presently explain why

takes this form with a Gaussian

using a “control” matrix

at time

A_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}}[(X-\mu_{t})\,(X-\mu_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}]\,.

The control matrix is incorporated in the following more general version
of (
4.3

Definition 4.2
.

Given a logconcave density
p
, we define the following

c_{0}=0\,,\quad\mathrm{d}c_{t}=C_{t}\mu_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+C_{t}^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\,,

B_{0}=0,\quad\mathrm{d}B_{t}=C_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t,

where the probability distribution
p_{t}

\mu_{t}

p_{t}(x)=\frac{e^{c_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}x-\frac{1}{2}\,x^{\mathsf{T}}B_{t}x}p(x)}{\int e^{c_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}y-\frac{1}{2}\,y^{\mathsf{T}}B_{t}y}p(y)\,\mathrm{d}y}\,,\quad\mu_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}}X,\quad A_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}}[(X-\mu_{t})^{\otimes 2}]\,,

and the control matrices

are symmetric matrices to be specified

When

is a Lipschitz function with respect to

and

In most applications to date, it suffices to focus on the case

.
The lemma below says that the stochastic process is the same as continuously

4.2
Warm-up: classical localization with short needles

Given a distribution with logconcave density

to the density. This is done by picking a random direction from a
Gaussian with a certain covariance matrix

, called the

, as in Definition

stochastic process, we assume that the support of

in a ball of radius

small probability outside this ball, at most

(see Theorem

We now proceed to analyzing the process. Roughly speaking, the first
lemma below says that the stochastic process is the same as continuously
multiplying

by a random infinitesimal linear function.

Lemma 4.4
.

For the process given by (
4.3
), we
have

\mathrm{d}p_{t}(x)=\langle x-\mu_{t},C_{t}^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle\,p_{t}(x)\quad\text{for any }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,.

By computing
\mathrm{d}\log p_{t}(x)
with the Itô’s lemma, we see that
applying
\mathrm{d}p_{t}(x)
as in the lemma above results in the density

p_{t}(x)
, with a Gaussian term:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\log p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{t}(x)}{p_{t}(x)}-\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}[p_{t}(x)]_{t}}{p_{t}(x)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle x-\mu_{t},C_{t}^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}(x-\mu_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}C_{t}\,(x-\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=x^{\mathsf{T}}\,(C_{t}\mu_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+C_{t}^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t})-\frac{1}{2}\,x^{\mathsf{T}}C_{t}x\,\mathrm{d}t-\underbrace{(\mu_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}C_{t}^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\,\mu_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}C_{t}\mu_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t)}_{=:g(t)}

\displaystyle=x^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathrm{d}c_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\,x^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathrm{d}B_{t})x+g(t)\,,

where the last term is independent of

explain the form of

and the appearance of the Gaussian.

The next lemma gives the time derivative of the covariance matrix.

Lemma 4.5
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}A_{t}

\displaystyle=\int(x-\mu_{t})^{\otimes 2}\,\langle x-\mu_{t},C_{t}^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle\,p_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)-A_{t}C_{t}A_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

,
is essentially just the backward step of the
\mathsf{PS}
. The key idea
is to bound
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X|Y})
rather than
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})=C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)
,
because
\pi^{X|Y}
admits a good bound on its
C_{\mathsf{PI}}
via (

ILI

).

Law of total variance.

Viewing
\pi^{X}=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi^{Y}}[\pi^{X|Y=y}]
as a mixture of
the distribution
\pi^{X|Y=y}
over
y
, we decompose the variance
using the law of total variance as follows:

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X}}f=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi^{Y}}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}f+\operatorname{Var}_{y\sim\pi^{Y}}(\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}f)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}f+\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{Y}}Q_{h}^{*}f\,,

(5.1)

where
Q_{h}^{*}
is the heat adjoint (see §
B
).

Relating
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})
and
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X|Y=y})
.

We then bound the second term by a multiple of the first term, sandwiching

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X}}f
in terms of the first term. To do so, we first
compute the gradient of
\pi^{X|Y=y}
(i.e.,
\mathsf{PS}
’s backward step).

Proposition 5.1

(Gradient of
\pi^{X|Y=y}
)
.

For
\pi^{X|Y=y}(x)=\bm{\pi}(x,y)/\pi^{Y}(y)
,

\nabla_{y}\pi^{X|Y=y}=\frac{x-b_{h}}{h}\,\pi^{X|Y=y}\,,

where
b_{h}
is the barycenter of
\pi^{X|Y=y}
(i.e.,
b_{h}=b_{h}(y)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}X
).

Proof.

We compute the gradient of Gaussian convolution: for any function

f
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{y}\int f(x)\,\gamma_{h}(y-x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi h)^{n/2}}\,\nabla_{y}\int\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2h}\,\lVert y-x\rVert^{2}\bigr)\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{(2\pi h)^{n/2}}\,\int\frac{y-x}{h}\,\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2h}\,\lVert y-x\rVert^{2}\bigr)\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{y-x}{h}\,f(x)\,\gamma_{h}(y-x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

so
\nabla_{y}\pi^{Y}=\nabla_{y}(\pi^{X}*\gamma_{h})=-\int\frac{y-x}{h}\,\pi^{X}(x)\,\gamma_{h}(y-x)\,\mathrm{d}x
.

Taking the gradient of
\pi^{X|Y=y}(x)=\pi^{X}(x)\,\gamma_{h}(y-x)/\pi^{Y}(y)
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{y}\pi^{X|Y=y}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{y-x}{h}\,\frac{\pi^{X}(x)\,\gamma(y-x)}{\pi^{Y}(y)}-\frac{\pi^{X}(x)\,\gamma_{h}(y-x)}{\pi^{Y}(y)}\,\frac{\nabla_{y}\pi^{Y}}{\pi^{Y}(y)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{y-x}{h}\,\frac{\pi^{X}(x)\,\gamma(y-x)}{\pi^{Y}(y)}+\frac{\pi^{X}(x)\,\gamma(y-x)}{\pi^{Y}(y)}\int\frac{y-x}{h}\,\frac{\pi^{X}(x)\,\gamma(y-x)}{\pi^{Y}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{y-x}{h}\,\pi^{X|Y=y}+\pi^{X|Y=y}\int\frac{y-x}{h}\,\pi^{X|Y=y}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\underset{(i)}{=}\pi^{X|Y=y}\,\bigl(-\frac{y-x}{h}+\frac{y-b_{h}}{h}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{x-b_{h}}{h}\,\pi^{X|Y=y}\,,

where
b_{h}
denotes the barycenter of
\pi^{X|Y=y}
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

For
h>0
, function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with finite second moment
and log-concave
\pi^{X}
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}f\leq\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X}}f\leq\bigl(2+\frac{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})}{h}\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}f\,.

Proof.

Applying (

PI

) to
Q_{h}^{*}f(y)
and using
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{Y})=C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X}*\gamma_{h})\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})+h

[
13
]
,

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{Y}}Q_{h}^{*}f\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{Y})\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}[\lVert\nabla_{y}Q_{h}^{*}f\rVert^{2}]\leq\bigl(C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})+h\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}[\lVert\nabla_{y}Q_{h}^{*}f\rVert^{2}]\,.

(5.2)

Using the formula of
\nabla_{y}\pi^{X|Y=y}
above,

\nabla_{y}Q_{h}^{*}f=\int f(x)\,\nabla_{y}\pi^{X|Y=y}(\mathrm{d}x)=\int\frac{x-b_{h}}{h}\,f(x)\,\pi^{X|Y=y}(\mathrm{d}x)\,.

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\lVert\nabla_{y}Q_{h}^{*}f\rVert^{2}=\Bigl\|\int\frac{x-b_{h}}{h}\,f(x)\,\pi^{X|Y=y}(\mathrm{d}x)\Bigr\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{h^{2}}\,\lVert\Sigma_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}\rVert\,\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}f\leq\frac{1}{h}\,\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}f\,,

where the last inequality follows from that
\pi^{X|Y=y}
is
h^{-1}
-strongly
log-concave (so
\lVert\Sigma_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}\rVert\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X|Y=y})\leq h

(say) by Brascamp–Lieb). Putting this bound back to (
5.2
),

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{Y}}Q_{h}^{*}f\leq\frac{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})+h}{h}\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}f\,.

Putting this back to the bound (
5.1
) from the
total law of variance,

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X}}f=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}f+\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{Y}}Q_{h}^{*}f\leq\bigl(2+\frac{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})}{h}\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}f\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

We now convert this “variance coupling” to a “
C_{\mathsf{PI}}
coupling”
between
\pi^{X}
and
\pi^{X|Y}
.

Corollary 5.3
.

For
h>0
,

C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})\lesssim\bigl(1+\frac{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})}{h}\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X|Y=y})\,.

Therefore,

C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})\lesssim\bigl(\sqrt{h}+\frac{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})}{\sqrt{h}}\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\sqrt{\lVert\Sigma_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}\rVert}\,.

Proof.

The variance-coupling lemma tells us that

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X}}f\leq\bigl(1+\frac{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})}{h}\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}f\,.

For the LHS, using Milman
[
57
]
, for a log-concave probability
measure
\pi^{X}
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a
1
-Lipschitz function

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that (for some universal constant hidden in
\asymp

below)

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X}}f\asymp C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})\,.

Taking this
1
-Lipschitz function
f
, we can lower bound the LHS
by
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})
(up to some constant).

As for the RHS, we apply (

PI

) to
f
and
\pi^{X|Y=y}

to obtain that

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}f\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X|Y=y})\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}[\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2}]\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X|Y=y})\,.

Combining these two bounds on the LHS and RHS,

C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})\lesssim\bigl(1+\frac{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})}{h}\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X|Y})\,.

For the second claim, we apply (

ILI

) to
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X|Y})

in the inequality above:

\displaystyle C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigl(1+\frac{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})}{h}\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X|Y})\leq\bigl(1+\frac{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})}{h}\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\sqrt{h\,\lVert\Sigma_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}\rVert}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\sqrt{h}+\frac{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})}{\sqrt{h}}\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\sqrt{\lVert\Sigma_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}\rVert}\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

Now it suffices to bound
\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\lVert\Sigma_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}\rVert

since

\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\sqrt{\lVert\Sigma_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}\rVert}\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\lVert\Sigma_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}\rVert}\,.

SL for operator-norm control.

We will show the following operator-norm control presently.

Theorem 5.4
.

Assume
\pi^{X}
is isotropic. For
\pi_{h}^{X|Y=y}=\pi^{X|Y=y}
for
some
h>0
, let

s_{0}=\min\{s>0:\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\lVert\Sigma_{\pi_{h}^{X|Y=y}}\rVert\leq 5\ \text{for all }h\geq s\}\,.

Then,
s_{0}\lesssim\log^{2}n
. Moreover, there is an example in
which
s_{0}\gtrsim\log n
.

We
have not used
SL at all thus far (and it may be possible
to bound
\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\lVert\Sigma_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}\rVert
directly without
SL). However, as we discuss in §
B
, SL provides a
continuous-time perspective on the backward step
\pi^{X|Y=y}
. This
allows us to study the evolution of
\lVert\Sigma_{\pi^{X|Y}}\rVert
through
the SL framework, reducing the problem to an application of Itô
calculus.

Using this theorem, we establish
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)\lesssim\log n
as follows.

Theorem 5.5

(
[
42
]
)
.

C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\lesssim\lVert\Sigma\rVert\log n
for
any log-concave
\pi
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

For an isotropic log-concave
\pi^{X}
, take
h\asymp\log^{2}n

to ensure
\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\lVert\Sigma_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}\rVert=O(1)
. Then,

C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\lesssim\sqrt{h}+\frac{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)}{\sqrt{h}}\,.

Rearranging terms, we obtain
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(n)\lesssim\log n
, which implies

C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\lesssim\lVert\Sigma\rVert\log n
.
∎

5.2
Operator-norm control via SL

We are now ready to do the “heavy-lifting” required to bound

,
using SL .

5.2.1
Proof outline for covariance bound

The main problem addressed in this section is the following:

For isotropic
\pi^{X}
is, bound
\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\lVert\Sigma_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}\rVert=O(1)

for
h>0
, as small as possible.

It may not be immediately clear how SL enters the picture when tackling
this problem. To see this, let us compare the following two approaches.
For
h=1/t
,

•

Backward step

of the
\mathsf{PS}
:

\pi^{X|Y=y}(x)\propto\pi^{X}(x)\,\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2h}\,\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}\bigr)\,,

where

.

•

SL
(

SL

-

pdf

): for
\pi=\pi^{X}\propto p
and
\pi_{t}\propto p_{t}
,

\pi_{t}(x)\propto\pi(x)\,\exp\bigl(-\frac{t}{2}\,\bigl\|x-\frac{c_{t}}{t}\bigr\|^{2}\bigr)\,,

where
c_{t}
satisfies

and
\mathrm{d}c_{t}=b_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W_{t}
.

As the reader may notice, these two approaches look similar when

.
In fact,

for

, and this can be justified by showing that

.
Indeed, for another Brownian motion

,

Y\overset{d}{=}X+W_{h}=X+W_{1/t}\overset{d}{=}X+\frac{1}{t}\,W^{\prime}_{t}\overset{d}{\underset{(i)}{=}}\frac{c_{t}}{t}\,,

where
(i)
follows from
[
39
]
. Namely, the “randomness”
in SL is captured by
c_{t}/t
(which corresponds to
Y\sim\pi^{Y}

obtained after the forward step of the
\mathsf{PS}
starting from
\pi^{X}
).
This relationship also implies that the backward step

with step size

can be viewed as a “snapshot” of SL at time

. Hence,
we may equivalently attempt to bound
\mathbb{E}_{c_{t}}\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi_{t}\rVert

instead of
\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}\lVert\Sigma_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}\rVert
. This connection
turns the problem above into the following:

Problem 5.6

(Operator-norm control via SL)
.

When
(\pi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is SL with
isotropic
\pi_{0}=\pi
, establish
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi_{t}\rVert=O(1)

for
t
less than some threshold
\tau
.

When
t
is close to
0
, we can expect
\pi_{t}
to remain nearly
isotropic. Thus, we should not run the SL process for too long (i.e.,
we restrict to
t\leq T
for some threshold

), because the longer

\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi_{t}\rVert
is to deviate from
its initial value
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert=1
. Therefore, Theorem
5.4

can be expressed in the SL language as follows:

Theorem 5.7

(SL-version of Theorem
5.4
)
.

Let
\pi=\pi_{0}=\pi^{X}

be isotropic log-concave probability measure over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\Sigma_{t}

be the covariance of
\pi_{t}
. Then,
\mathbb{E}\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert=O(1)

for

.

To this end, observe that

\mathbb{E}\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert=\mathbb{E}[\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\cdot\mathds{1}\{\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\leq 2\}]+\mathbb{E}[\underbrace{\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert}_{\leq 1/t}\cdot\mathds{1}\{\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\geq 2\}]\leq 2+\frac{1}{t}\,\mathbb{P}(\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\geq 2)\,.

Hence, it suffices to establish the following operator-norm control.

Theorem 5.8

(Operator-norm control)
.

Let
\pi
be
an isotropic log-concave probability measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For

,

\mathbb{P}(\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\leq 2\ \text{for }t\in[0,T])\geq 1-\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{CT}\bigr)\,,

(5.3)

where
C>0
is a universal constant.

In words,
\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert
stays close to
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert=1

with high probability up to the time threshold

.

5.2.2
Itô calculus for covariance
\Sigma_{t}

Preliminary computations.

While handling the stochastic quantity
\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert
, we will
encounter several important quantities involving the barycenter
b_{t}

and covariance
\Sigma_{t}
of
\pi_{t}
. We therefore compute
\mathrm{d}b_{t}

and
\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}
. Using Itô’s lemma and applying (

MG

)
with
F(x)=x
and
x^{\otimes 2}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}b_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}\Bigl(\int x\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)\Bigr)=\int x\otimes(x-b_{t})\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}=\int(x-b_{t})^{\otimes 2}\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}\Bigl(\int x^{\otimes 2}\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)-b_{t}^{\otimes 2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\int x^{\otimes 2}\langle x-b_{t},\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)-\mathrm{d}(b_{t}^{\otimes 2})

\displaystyle\underset{(i)}{=}\int x^{\otimes 2}\langle x-b_{t},\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)-(\mathrm{d}b_{t}\otimes b_{t}+b_{t}\otimes\mathrm{d}b_{t}+\mathrm{d}b_{t}\otimes\mathrm{d}b_{t})

\displaystyle=\int(x-b_{t})^{\otimes 2}\langle x-b_{t},\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)+\int(x\otimes b_{t}+b_{t}\otimes x-b_{t}\otimes b_{t})\langle x-b_{t},\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle\qquad-\Bigl(\int(x-b_{t})^{\otimes 2}\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\otimes b_{t}+b_{t}\otimes\int(x-b_{t})^{\otimes 2}\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\Sigma_{t}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr)

\displaystyle\underset{(ii)}{=}\underbrace{\int(x-b_{t})^{\otimes 2}\langle x-b_{t},\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)}_{=:\mathrm{d}H_{t}}-\Sigma_{t}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

where
(i)
follows from the Itô product rule, and
(ii)
follows
from
\int x\otimes b_{t}\,\langle x-b_{t},\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)=(\int(x-b_{t})^{\otimes 2}\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t})\otimes b_{t}
.
As for
(i)
, one can actually check it by Itô’s formula as follows:
for
f(x)=x\otimes x
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}f(X_{t})

\displaystyle=\text{D}(X_{t}\otimes X_{t})[\mathrm{d}X_{t}]+\frac{1}{2}\,\text{D}^{2}(X_{t}\otimes X_{t})[\mathrm{d}X_{t},\mathrm{d}X_{t}]

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{id}\otimes X_{t}+X_{t}\otimes\mathrm{id})[\mathrm{d}X_{t}]+(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathrm{id})[\mathrm{d}X_{t},\mathrm{d}X_{t}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}X_{t}\otimes X_{t}+X_{t}\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{t}+\mathrm{d}X_{t}\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{t}\,.

The
3
-tensor term merits its own notation

, as it is the

3
-tensor analysis,
we also define, for each

H_{t,i}=H_{i}:=\int(x-b_{t})^{\otimes 2}(x-b_{t})_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}\,,

(5.4)

so

.

As we will see later, the main quantities for downstream arguments
are
\mathbb{E}\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert
,
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})
, and

•

KLS conjecture
: We have already seen that we need

\mathbb{E}\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert=O(1)\quad\text{for }t\lesssim\log^{-2}n\,.

•

Thin-shell conjecture
: When working towards the thin-shell

7
, we will need

\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})=\Theta(n)\quad\text{for }t\lesssim 1\,.

•

Slicing conjecture
: When resolving the slicing conjecture

, we will need

\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{t}=\Omega(n)\quad\text{for }t\lesssim 1\,.

In fact, this is immediate from the trace of
\Sigma_{t}^{2}
above.
Integrating both sides of

\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}=\int(x-b_{t})^{\otimes 2}\langle x-b_{t},\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)-\Sigma_{t}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

and taking trace and expectation, noting that
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{0}=n

and

, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{t}-n=-\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{s}^{2})\,\mathrm{d}s\,.

Therefore,

\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{t}=-\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\,.

Since
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{t}^{2}=\Theta(n)
for

, we can conclude
that for

,

\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{t}\gtrsim n\,.

Lastly, we will also need
\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\lVert b_{t}\rVert^{2}]
. By Itô’s

\mathrm{d}(\lVert b_{t}\rVert^{2})=2\langle\mathrm{d}b_{t},b_{t}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\,\operatorname{tr}(I_{n}\Sigma_{t}^{2})\,\mathrm{d}t=\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\,\mathrm{d}t+2\langle b_{t},\Sigma_{t}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\,,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\mathbb{E}[\lVert b_{t}\rVert^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\,.

(5.5)

Control of
\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert
via a proxy function.

We work with a proxy function defined by

h(M):=\frac{1}{\beta}\log(\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta M})\quad\text{for a symmetric matrix $M$$\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\ $and constant }\beta>0.

Clearly, for any
t>0
,

\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\leq h(\Sigma_{t})\leq\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert+\frac{\log n}{\beta}\,.

To control
h(\Sigma_{t})
, we compute the Itô derivative
\mathrm{d}h(\Sigma_{t})

to see the magnitude of its drift. By Itô’s lemma and
\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}=\mathrm{d}H_{t}-\Sigma_{t}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}h(\Sigma_{t})

\displaystyle=\nabla h(\Sigma_{t})[\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}]+\frac{1}{2}\,\nabla^{2}h(\Sigma_{t})[\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t},\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\nabla h(\Sigma_{t})[\mathrm{d}H_{t}]}_{=\operatorname{tr}(\nabla h(\Sigma_{t})\,\mathrm{d}H_{t})}-\underbrace{\nabla h(\Sigma_{t})[\Sigma_{t}^{2}]}_{=\operatorname{tr}(\nabla h(\Sigma_{t})\,\Sigma_{t}^{2})}\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\,\nabla^{2}h(\Sigma_{t})[\mathrm{d}H_{t},\mathrm{d}H_{t}]\,.

From direct computation,

G_{t}:=\nabla h(\Sigma_{t})=\frac{e^{\beta\Sigma_{t}}}{\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta\Sigma_{t}}}\succeq 0\,,

and note that
\operatorname{tr}G_{t}=1
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}h(\Sigma_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}\operatorname{tr}(G_{t}H_{i})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t,i}+\frac{1}{2}\,\nabla^{2}h(\Sigma_{t})\Bigl[\sum_{i}H_{i}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t,i},\sum_{i}H_{i}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t,i}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\operatorname{tr}(G_{t}H_{i})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t,i}+\frac{1}{2}\,\sum_{i}\nabla^{2}h(\Sigma_{t})[H_{i},H_{i}]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\underset{(i)}{\leq}\sum_{i}\operatorname{tr}(G_{t}H_{i})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t,i}+\frac{\beta}{2}\,\sum_{i}\operatorname{tr}(G_{t}H_{i}^{2})\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\sum_{i}\operatorname{tr}(G_{t}H_{i})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t,i}}_{\eqqcolon\mathrm{d}Z_{t}}+\frac{\beta}{2}\,\Bigl\|\sum_{i}H_{i}^{2}\Bigr\|\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

where in
(i)
, the bound on
\nabla^{2}h(\Sigma_{t})[H_{i},H_{i}]

follows from Lemma
5.10
, and the last line
follows from the
(1,\infty)
-Hölder’s inequality with
\operatorname{tr}G_{t}=\lVert G_{t}\rVert_{1}\leq 1
.

Bounding a bad event via Freedman’s inequality.

Integrating both sides, we have that for some constant
C>0
,

h(\Sigma_{t})\leq h(\Sigma_{0})+Z_{t}+\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\Bigl\|\sum_{i}H_{s,i}^{2}\Bigr\|\,\mathrm{d}s\leq 1+\frac{\log n}{\beta}+Z_{t}+\frac{\beta C}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\lVert\Sigma_{s}\rVert^{5/2}}{s^{1/2}}\,\mathrm{d}s\,,

where the last line follows from Lemma
5.13
, as well
as the improved Lichnerowicz inequality (

ILI

).

Let us take the smallest
\tau
such that
\tau\leq t
and
\lVert\Sigma_{\tau}\rVert\geq 2
.
Then, for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
,

2=\lVert\Sigma_{\tau}\rVert\leq h(\Sigma_{\tau})\leq 1+\frac{\log n}{\beta}+Z_{\tau}+\Theta(\beta C\tau^{1/2})\,.

For
\beta=2\log n
and
t\asymp(\beta C)^{-2}\asymp(C\log n)^{-2}
,
we can ensure
Z_{\tau}\geq\frac{1}{4}
. To apply a deviation inequality
to
Z_{t}
, let us compute the quadratic variation of
Z_{t}
:

\mathrm{d}[Z]_{t}=\sum_{i}\operatorname{tr}^{2}(G_{t}H_{i})\,\mathrm{d}t=\lVert v\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

where the vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies
v_{i}=\operatorname{tr}(G_{t}H_{i})
. For
any unit vector
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, using the
(1,\infty)
-Hölder’s
inequality and
\operatorname{tr}G_{t}\leq 1
,

v\cdot\theta=\operatorname{tr}\Bigl(G_{t}\sum_{i}H_{i}\theta_{i}\Bigr)\leq\Bigl\|\sum_{i}H_{i}\theta_{i}\Bigr\|=\Bigl\|\int(x-b_{t})^{\otimes 2}\langle x-b_{t},\theta\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}\Bigr\|\lesssim\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert^{3/2}\,,

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
5.12
.
Thus,
\lVert v\rVert^{2}\leq\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert^{3}
and
[Z]_{\tau}\lesssim\int_{0}^{\tau}\lVert\Sigma_{s}\rVert^{3}\,\mathrm{d}s\lesssim\tau\leq t
.
Therefore, for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
,

\mathbb{P}(\exists\tau\leq t:\lVert\Sigma_{\tau}\rVert\geq 2)\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl(\exists\tau>0:Z_{\tau}\geq\frac{1}{4}\ \text{and}\ [Z]_{\tau}\lesssim t\bigr)\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{C^{\prime}t}\bigr)\,,

(5.6)

where the last inequality follows from the classical deviation inequality
below, and this completes the proof of Theorem
5.8
.

Lemma 5.9

(Freedman’s inequality)
.

Let
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a continuous local
martingale with
M_{0}=0
. Then for
u,\sigma^{2}>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\exists t>0:M_{t}>u\ \text{and}\ [M]_{t}\leq\sigma^{2})\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{u^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\bigr)\,.

Matrix inequalities.

We compute the second-order directional derivative of
h(M)=\frac{1}{\beta}\log(\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta M})
.

Lemma 5.10

(
[
36
, Corollary 56]
)
.

For symmetric matrices
M,H\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,

\nabla^{2}h(M)[H,H]\leq\beta\,\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\frac{e^{\beta M}}{\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta M}}H^{2}\bigr)\,.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\nabla h(M)[H]

\displaystyle=\frac{\nabla(\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta M})[H]}{\beta\,\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta M}}\,,

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}h(M)[H,H]

\displaystyle=\frac{\nabla^{2}(\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta M})[H,H]}{\beta\,\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta M}}-\frac{1}{\beta}\,\bigl(\frac{\nabla(\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta M})[H]}{\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta M}}\bigr)^{2}\leq\frac{\nabla^{2}(\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta M})[H,H]}{\beta\,\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta M}}\,.

Let us bound the second-order directional derivative of
f(M):=\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta M}
.
From
f(M)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{\beta^{i}}{i!}\operatorname{tr}(M^{i})
, we
can readily check that

\displaystyle\nabla f(M)[H]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\beta^{i}}{(i-1)!}\,\operatorname{tr}(M^{i-1}H)=\beta\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\beta^{i}}{i!}\operatorname{tr}(M^{i}H)\,,

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(M)[H,H]

\displaystyle=\beta\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\beta^{i}}{i!}\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\operatorname{tr}(M^{j}HM^{i-1-j}H)\,.

Assuming
M\succeq 0
for a while, we show below in Lemma
5.11

that

\operatorname{tr}(M^{j}HM^{i-1-j}H)\leq\operatorname{tr}(M^{i-1}H^{2})\,.

Using this,

\nabla^{2}f(M)[H,H]\leq\beta\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\beta^{i}}{(i-1)!}\,\operatorname{tr}(M^{i-1}H^{2})=\beta^{2}\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\beta^{i}}{i!}\,\operatorname{tr}(M^{i}H^{2})=\beta^{2}\operatorname{tr}(e^{\beta M}H^{2})\,.

(5.7)

Now for a general symmetric
M
, pick
\varepsilon>0
such that
M+\varepsilon I\succeq 0
.
Note that

f(M+\varepsilon I)=\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta(M+\varepsilon I)}=\operatorname{tr}(e^{\beta M}e^{\beta\varepsilon I})=e^{\beta\varepsilon}f(M)\,.

Therefore, (
5.7
) is still valid for any symmetric

M
. Substituting (
5.7
) back to the bound
on
\nabla^{2}h(M)[H,H]
, we obtain

\nabla^{2}h(M)[H,H]\leq\frac{\beta^{2}\,\operatorname{tr}(e^{\beta M}H^{2})}{\beta\,\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta M}}=\beta\,\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\frac{e^{\beta M}}{\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta M}}H^{2}\bigr)\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

We now show that
\operatorname{tr}(M^{j}HM^{i-1-j}H)\leq\operatorname{tr}(M^{i-1}H^{2})
.

Lemma 5.11

(
[
36
, Lemma 55]
)
.

Let
M\succeq 0

and
H
be a symmetric matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. For
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
,

\operatorname{tr}(M^{n}HM^{m}H)\leq\operatorname{tr}(M^{n+m}H^{2})\,.

Proof.

By the spectral decomposition of
M
, we can write that for eigenvalues

\lambda_{i}
and orthonormal basis
\{u_{i}\}
,

M=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}u_{i}u_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\,.

Then,

\operatorname{tr}(M^{n}HM^{m}H)=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{i}^{n}\lambda_{j}^{m}\,\operatorname{tr}(u_{i}u_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}Hu_{j}u_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}H)=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{i}^{n}\lambda_{j}^{m}\,(u_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}Hu_{j})^{2}\,.

By Young’s inequality,

\lambda_{i}^{n}\lambda_{j}^{m}\leq\frac{n}{n+m}\,\lambda_{i}^{n+m}+\frac{m}{n+m}\,\lambda_{j}^{n+m}\,.

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{i}^{n}\lambda_{j}^{m}\,(u_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}Hu_{j})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j}\frac{n}{n+m}\,\lambda_{i}^{n+m}(u_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}Hu_{j})^{2}+\sum_{i,j}\frac{m}{n+m}\,\lambda_{j}^{n+m}(u_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}Hu_{j})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{i}^{n+m}\,u_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}Hu_{j}u_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}Hu_{i}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{n+m}\,u_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}H\Bigl(\sum_{j}u_{j}u_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigr)Hu_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{n+m}\,u_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}H^{2}u_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\operatorname{tr}(\lambda_{i}^{n+m}u_{i}u_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}H^{2})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(M^{n+m}H^{2})\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

3-tensor bounds.

In the analysis below, we bound
\lVert\int(x-b_{t})^{\otimes 2}\langle x-b_{t},\theta\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}\rVert

for a unit vector
\theta
.

Lemma 5.12

(
[
26
,
36
, Lemma 57]
)
.

Let
X
be a centered logconcave random variable. Then, for the covariance

\Sigma
of
\operatorname{law}X
,

\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\lVert\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot\theta)\,X^{\otimes 2}]\rVert\lesssim\lVert\Sigma\rVert^{3/2}\,.

Proof.

For
v\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,

\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot\theta)(X\cdot v)^{2}]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot\theta)^{2}]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot v)^{4}]}\,.

Since
X\cdot v
is an
1
-dimensional logconcave random variable,
the reverse Hölder inequality tells us that:

\bigl(\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot v)^{4}]\bigr)^{1/4}\lesssim\bigl(\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot v)^{2}]\bigr)^{1/2}\,.

Hence,

\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot\theta)(X\cdot v)^{2}]\leq\lVert\Sigma\rVert^{1/2}\,\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot v)^{2}]\leq\lVert\Sigma\rVert^{3/2}\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

Next, we bound
\lVert\sum_{i}H_{i}^{2}\rVert
for
H_{i}=\int(x-b_{t})^{\otimes 2}(x-b_{t})_{i}\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)
.

Lemma 5.13

(
[
36
, Lemma 58]
)
.

Let
X
be
a centered
t
-strongly logconcave random variable. Then, for the
covariance
\Sigma
of
\operatorname{law}X
,

\Bigl\|\sum_{i}(\mathbb{E}[X_{i}X^{\otimes 2}])^{2}\Bigr\|\leq\frac{4\lVert\Sigma\rVert^{5/2}}{t^{1/2}}\,.

Proof.

For
v\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,

\displaystyle v^{\mathsf{T}}\sum_{i}(\mathbb{E}[X_{i}X^{\otimes 2}])^{2}v

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot v)\,X_{i}X]\,\bigl(\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot v)\,X_{i}X]\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}=\operatorname{tr}\Bigl(\bigl(\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot v)\,X^{\otimes 2}]}_{\eqqcolon H_{v}}\bigr)^{2}\Bigr)\,.

Now let us bound
\operatorname{tr}(H_{v}^{2})
as follows:

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(H_{v}^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{tr}\bigl(H_{v}\,(X\cdot v)\,X^{\otimes 2}\bigr)\bigr]=\mathbb{E}[(X^{\mathsf{T}}H_{v}X)\,(X\cdot v)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(X^{\mathsf{T}}H_{v}X-\mathbb{E}[X^{\mathsf{T}}H_{v}X])\,(X\cdot v)\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot v)^{2}]}\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X^{\mathsf{T}}H_{v}X)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\lVert\Sigma\rVert}\sqrt{4C_{\mathsf{PI}}(X)\,\mathbb{E}[\lVert H_{v}X\rVert^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\lVert\Sigma\rVert}\sqrt{4C_{\mathsf{PI}}(X)\,\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(H_{v}^{2}X^{\otimes 2})]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\lVert\Sigma\rVert}\sqrt{4C_{\mathsf{PI}}(X)\,\operatorname{tr}(H_{v}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}[X^{\otimes 2}])}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\Sigma\rVert\sqrt{4C_{\mathsf{PI}}(X)\,\operatorname{tr}(H_{v}^{2})}\,.

Rearranging terms,

\operatorname{tr}(H_{v}^{2})\leq 4C_{\mathsf{PI}}(X)\lVert\Sigma\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{4\lVert\Sigma\rVert^{5/2}}{t^{1/2}}\,,

where the last one follows from (

ILI

).
∎

We now move to the slicing conjecture, which was resolved in December

]
.
We follow the much shorter proof by Bizeul
[

]

based on Guan’s trace bound and an approach from

.

6.1
Small-ball probability

Just as the original KLS conjecture is tackled through its Poincaré
version (

KLS

), we handle the slicing conjecture through
an equivalent formulation. Dafnis and Paouris
[
20
]

showed that the following is equivalent to
\bar{L}_{n}=O(1)
(i.e.,
the slicing conjecture in terms of isotropic convex bodies; see Theorem
2.16
):

Theorem 6.1

(Small-ball conjecture)
.

Let
\pi

be an isotropic logconcave distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, there exists
universal constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for any
\varepsilon<c_{1}

and any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(\lVert X-y\rVert^{2}\leq\varepsilon n)=\pi\bigl(B_{\sqrt{\varepsilon n}}(y)\bigr)\leq\varepsilon^{c_{2}n}\,,

(
\mathsf{sBall}
)

where
B_{r}(y)
is the ball of radius
r
centered at
y
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We prove this theorem in this section.

Remark 6.2

(Interpretation)
.

Paouris showed that there exists a universal constant

c>0
such that for any
t\geq 1
and any isotropic logconcave
\pi

over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(\lVert X\rVert^{2}\leq t^{2}n)\geq 1-e^{-ct\sqrt{n}}\,.

In words, the centered
t\sqrt{n}
-ball with
t\geq 1
is
large
enough
to take up most of the measure. On the other hand, the small-ball
conjecture posits how small the measure of a
t\sqrt{n}
-radius ball
should be when
t
is less than
1
. More precisely, it claims that
its measure decreases polynomially fast in
t
(i.e.,
\sim t^{n}
).

6.1.1
Proof outline

Recall our approach to (

KLS

): we crucially relied on
a
model inequality
such as (

ILI

),
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\leq(\lVert\Sigma\rVert/t)^{1/2}
,
which holds for
t
-strongly logconcave distributions. Using SL,
we then leveraged this inequality to establish a similar result for
any logconcave distributions.

We take a similar approach toward (

sBall

),
which leads the the following question:

Q.
What is a suitable
model result
that we can make
use of?

In
[
60
]
, Paouris established a small-ball estimate
for
sub-Gaussian
logconcave distributions, where sub-Gaussianity
is implied by strong log-concavity.

Lemma 6.3

(Small-ball for sub-Gaussian)
.

Let
\pi
be an
t
-strongly logconcave distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{n}

with covariance
\Sigma
. Then, there exists universal constants

c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for any
\varepsilon<c_{1}
and any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(\lVert X-y\rVert^{2}\leq\varepsilon\operatorname{tr}\Sigma)\leq\varepsilon^{\frac{c_{2}t\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}{\lVert\Sigma\rVert\lVert\Sigma^{-1}\rVert}}\,.

Namely, this lemma proves (

sBall

) for isotropic
and
1
-strongly logconcave
\pi
(e.g.,
\mathsf{N}(0,I_{n})
), so
we will treat it as the model result in our approach. To start off,
we consider the SL process
(\pi_{t})_{\geq 0}
with
\pi_{0}=\pi

isotropic logconcave, and SL ensures
\pi(S)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{SL}}\pi_{t}(S)

for
S:=B_{\sqrt{\varepsilon n}}(y)
. We will carry out a simple stochastic
analysis to show that with some probability

\pi(S)\lesssim\pi_{t}(S)\leq\varepsilon^{\frac{c_{2}t\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{t}}{\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\lVert\Sigma_{t}^{-1}\rVert}}\,,

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
6.3
.
This prompts us to

1.

Upper bound
\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert

2.

Upper bound
\lVert\Sigma_{t}^{-1}\rVert

3.

Lower bound
\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{t}

The first is very easy due to (

ILI

) (i.e.,
\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\leq t^{-1}
).
The second part is addressed by working with a version of Lemma
6.3
,
and the last part relies on a consequence of Guan’s trace bound (explained
in the next section).

6.1.2
A version of Paouris’ small-ball estimate

We establish a simple consequence of Paouris’ small-ball estimate
by cutting off small eigenvalues of
\Sigma
suitably.

Lemma 6.4

(Small-ball with cutoff,
[
4
, Lemma 4]
)
.

Let
\pi
be a
t
-strongly logconcave distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{n}

with covariance
\Sigma
satisfying
4n\,\lVert\Sigma\rVert/\operatorname{tr}\Sigma\leq C

for
C>0
. Then, there exist universal constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0

such that for any
\varepsilon<c_{1}/C
and any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(\lVert X-y\rVert^{2}\leq\varepsilon\operatorname{tr}\Sigma)\leq\bigl(\frac{4n\varepsilon\lVert\Sigma\rVert}{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}\bigr)^{\frac{c_{2}t^{3}\operatorname{tr}^{3}\Sigma}{8n^{2}}}\,.

Proof.

Let
\{(v_{i},\lambda_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
be the pairs of eigenvectors
and eigenvalues of
\Sigma
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}\geq 0
.
For an effective rank
r:=\operatorname{tr}\Sigma/\lVert\Sigma\rVert
, let us pick

k=\bigl\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\bigr\rfloor\vee 1\,.

Under this choice of
k
, since

\operatorname{tr}\Sigma=(\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k})+(\lambda_{k+1}+\cdots+\lambda_{n})\leq k\,\lVert\Sigma\rVert+n\lambda_{k}\,,

we have

\frac{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}{2n}\leq\lambda_{k}\,.

Namely,
\lambda_{k}
is almost as large as the average of the eigenvalues
of
\Sigma
.

Now consider the subspace
\mathcal{S}
spanned by
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}
,
and the projection
P_{\mathcal{S}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
onto
\mathcal{S}
. Then,
the marginal distribution
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}:=(P_{\mathcal{S}})_{\#}\pi
is still

t
-strongly logconcave (by Prékopa–Leindler) and satisfies

\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}}\rVert\leq\lVert\Sigma\rVert\,,\quad\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}\rVert\leq\frac{2n}{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}\,.

(6.1)

Furthermore, since the average of eigenvalues goes up after cutting
off small ones,

\frac{1}{k}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}}\geq\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma\,,

and thus

\frac{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}}}\leq\frac{n}{k}\leq\frac{4n\,\lVert\Sigma\rVert}{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}\leq C\,,\quad\text{so}\ \frac{\operatorname{tr}^{2}\Sigma}{4n\,\lVert\Sigma\rVert}\leq\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}}\,.

(6.2)

It then follows that for
\varepsilon\leq c_{1}/C
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\lVert X-y\rVert\leq\varepsilon\operatorname{tr}\Sigma)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\lVert P_{\mathcal{S}}X-P_{\mathcal{S}}y\rVert\leq\varepsilon\operatorname{tr}\Sigma)=\mathbb{P}\bigl(\lVert P_{\mathcal{S}}X-P_{\mathcal{S}}y\rVert\leq\frac{\varepsilon\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}}}\,\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\underset{(i)}{\leq}\bigl(\frac{\varepsilon\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}}}\bigr)^{\frac{c_{2}t\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}}}{\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}}\rVert\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}\rVert}}\underset{(ii)}{\leq}\bigl(\frac{4n\varepsilon\,\lVert\Sigma\rVert}{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}\bigr)^{\frac{c_{2}t\operatorname{tr}^{3}\Sigma}{8n^{2}\lVert\Sigma\rVert^{2}}}\underset{(iii)}{\leq}\bigl(\frac{4n\varepsilon\,\lVert\Sigma\rVert}{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}\bigr)^{\frac{c_{2}t^{3}\operatorname{tr}^{3}\Sigma}{8n^{2}}}\,,

where in
(i)
we used Lemma
6.3
to

\pi_{\mathcal{S}}
, used (
6.1
) and (
6.2
)
in
(ii)
, and used
\lVert\Sigma\rVert\leq 1/t
in
(iii)
.
∎

6.1.3
Stochastic analysis

Reduction to the centered
\sqrt{n}
-ball.

We note that when studying the small-ball conjecture, the general
isotropic case can be reduced to truncated isotropic distributions
with support of diameter
\Theta(n^{1/2})
and
y=0
. To see this,
consider

Y:=\frac{X_{1}-X_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\,,

where
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are independent copies from an isotropic
logconcave distribution
\pi
. Then,
\operatorname{law}Y
is symmetric, isotropic,
and logconcave (since convolution preserved logconcavity). Note that
for any
r>0
,

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(\lVert X-y\rVert\leq r)=\{\mathbb{P}_{(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\pi^{\otimes 2}}(\lVert X_{1}-y\rVert\leq r,\lVert X_{2}-y\rVert\leq r)\}^{1/2}\leq\{\mathbb{P}(\lVert Y\rVert\leq\sqrt{2}r)\}^{1/2}\,,

so it suffices to focus on the case of
y=0
.

Next, we claim that one can truncate
\mu:=\operatorname{law}Y
to a ball of radius

\Theta(n^{1/2})
and then focus on
\tilde{\mu}\propto\mu\cdot\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{B}}

for
\mathcal{B}:=B_{10n^{1/2}}(0)
. Note that

\sup_{\mathcal{B}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\leq\bigl(\mu(\mathcal{B})\bigr)^{-1}\leq\frac{100}{99}\,,

where the last inequality follows from Markov’s inequality:

\mu(\mathcal{B})=\mathbb{P}(\lVert Y\rVert^{2}\leq 100n^{2})\geq 1-\frac{\mathbb{E}[\lVert Y\rVert^{2}]}{100n^{2}}\geq\frac{99}{100}\,.

Hence,
\lVert\Sigma_{\tilde{\mu}}\rVert\leq\frac{100}{99}
follows from

\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mu}}[(Z\cdot\theta)^{2}]\leq\frac{100}{99}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[(Z\cdot\theta)^{2}]=\frac{100}{99}\,.

For the lower bound, using the reverse Hölder in
(i)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mu}}[(Z\cdot\theta)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{B}}(z\cdot\theta)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mu(\mathcal{B})}\geq 1-\int_{\mathcal{B}^{c}}(z\cdot\theta)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\geq 1-\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[(Z\cdot\theta)^{4}]\bigr)^{1/2}\bigl(\mu(\mathcal{B}^{c})\bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\underset{(i)}{\geq}1-\frac{5\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[(Z\cdot\theta)^{2}]}{10}=\frac{1}{2}\,.

Hence,
\frac{1}{2}\,I_{n}\preceq\Sigma_{\tilde{\mu}}\preceq 2I_{n}
.

Lastly, consider
\nu:=[(\Sigma_{\tilde{\mu}})^{-1/2}]_{\#}\tilde{\mu}

which is isotropic logconcave. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(\lVert X-y\rVert\leq r)

\displaystyle\leq\{\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(\lVert Y\rVert\leq\sqrt{2}r)\}^{1/2}\leq\{\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\mu}}(\lVert Y\rVert\leq\sqrt{2}r)\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\{\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\mu}}(\lVert\Sigma^{-1/2}Y\rVert\leq\lVert\Sigma^{-1/2}\rVert\sqrt{2}r)\}^{1/2}\leq\{\mathbb{P}_{\nu}(\lVert Z\rVert\leq 2r)\}^{1/2}\,.

Therefore, redefining the universal constants
c_{1},c_{2}
in the
small-ball estimate (through suitable rescaling), we can focus on
truncated isotropic distributions with support of diameter
\Theta(n^{1/2})

and
y=0
.

Stochastic analysis vis SL.

We now use the SL process
(\pi_{t})
with isotropic logconcave
\pi_{0}=\pi

supported over
\mathcal{B}=B_{10n^{1/2}}(0)
. Let
S=B_{\sqrt{\varepsilon n}}(0)

and define
Z_{t}:=\pi_{t}(S)
. Since

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=\mathrm{d}\Bigl(\int_{S}\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)\Bigr)=\int_{S}\langle x-b_{t},\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)\,.

Hence, the quadratic variation is bounded as follows:

\mathrm{d}[Z]_{t}=\Bigl\|\int_{S}(x-b_{t})\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\Bigr\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq(10n^{1/2}Z_{t})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t=100nZ_{t}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

Now using Itô’s formula to
\log Z_{t}
,

\mathrm{d}\log Z_{t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}Z_{t}}{Z_{t}}-\frac{\mathrm{d}[Z]_{t}}{2Z_{t}^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{Z_{t}}\int_{S}\langle x-b_{t},\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle\,\pi_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)-50n\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

and thus

\log\pi(S)\leq\mathbb{E}\log\pi_{t}(S)+50nt\,.

Lemma 6.5
.

For
\lambda>0
, with probability
at least
1-\lambda^{-1}
, it holds that

\pi(S)\leq e^{50nt}\pi_{t}^{1/\lambda}(S)\,.

Proof.

Using Markov’s inequality, it holds that with probability at least

1-\lambda^{-1}
,

\log\pi_{t}(S)\geq\lambda\,\mathbb{E}\log\pi_{t}(S)\,,

Hence,

\log\pi(S)\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\pi_{t}(S)+50nt\,,

and then claim follows from this.
∎

Putting all together.

We introduce a simple consequence of Guan’s important bound
[
30
]
,
addressed in §
6.2
.

Lemma 6.6
.

There exists a universal constant
c\in(0,1)

such that

\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{c}\geq cn\,.

We now prove the small-ball conjecture (

sBall

).

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

Using
\lVert\Sigma_{c}\rVert=\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi_{c}\rVert\leq 1/c
,
we obtain that

\displaystyle cn

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{c}=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{c}\cdot\mathds{1}[\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{c}\geq\frac{cn}{2}]\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{c}\cdot\mathds{1}[\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{c}\leq\frac{cn}{2}]\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{c}\,\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{c}\geq\frac{cn}{2})+\frac{cn}{2}\,,

so

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{c}\geq\frac{cn}{2}\bigr)\geq\frac{c^{2}}{2}\,.

Using Lemma
6.5
with
\lambda=4/c^{2}
,

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\pi(S)\leq e^{50cn}\pi_{c}^{c^{2}/4}(S)\bigr)\geq 1-\frac{c^{2}}{4}\,.

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{c}\geq\frac{cn}{2}\,,\pi(S)\leq e^{50cn}\pi_{c}^{c^{2}/4}(S)\bigr)>0\,.

In this event,

\frac{4n\,\lVert\Sigma_{c}\rVert}{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{c}}\leq\frac{8n}{c^{2}n}=\frac{8}{c^{2}}=:C\,.

Below in
(i)
, we use the small-ball estimate (Lemma
6.4
)
for
c
-strongly logconcave
\pi_{c}
: for
S=B_{\sqrt{\varepsilon n}}(0)

with
\varepsilon\leq\frac{c_{1}c}{2C}
,

\displaystyle\pi(S)

\displaystyle\leq e^{50cn}\pi_{c}^{c^{2}/4}(S)=e^{50cn}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(\lVert X\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon n}{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{c}}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{c})\bigr)^{c^{2}/4}

\displaystyle\leq e^{50cn}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(\lVert X\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{c}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{c})\bigr)^{c^{2}/4}

\displaystyle\underset{(i)}{\leq}e^{50cn}\bigl(\frac{8n\varepsilon\lVert\Sigma_{c}\rVert}{c\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{c}}\bigr)^{\frac{c_{2}c^{5}\operatorname{tr}^{3}\Sigma_{c}}{32n^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{50cn}\bigl(\frac{16\varepsilon}{c^{3}}\bigr)^{\frac{c_{2}c^{8}n}{256}}\leq\varepsilon^{C^{\prime}n}\,,

where one can take a proper universal constant
C^{\prime}
in the last line.
∎

6.2
Trace control via SL

We now present Guan’s important uniform bound on the trace of
\Sigma_{t}
:

\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})=O(n)\,.

This part is technically more complicated than the operator-norm control
in §
5.2
.

Two types of bounds had been established before Guan’s bound.

•

Type I
:
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})=O(n)
for
t\lesssim\log^{-2}n

(not a universal constant).

•

Type II
: For any
t_{0}>0
,
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\leq\bigl(\frac{t}{t_{0}}\bigr)^{\Theta(1)}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})

for all
t\geq t_{0}
. (The best exponent thus far is
2\sqrt{2}
)

Type I’s valid regime for
t
ends at a dimension-dependent bound,
but Guan managed to remove this limit.

In this section, we prove the two bounds on
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})

and then move to Guan’s bound.

6.2.1
Two types of control

Type I.

This follows from the operator-norm control established in (
5.3
):
for
t\asymp\log^{-2}n
and constant
C>0
,

\mathbb{P}(\exists\tau\leq t:\lVert\Sigma_{\tau}\rVert\geq 2)\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{Ct}\bigr)\,.

Using this and
\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\leq 1/t
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\,\mathds{1}[\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\leq 2]\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\,\mathds{1}[\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\geq 2]\bigr]

\displaystyle\lesssim n+\frac{n}{t^{2}}\,\mathbb{P}(\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\geq 2)\leq n+\frac{n}{t^{2}}\,e^{-\frac{1}{Ct}}

\displaystyle\lesssim n\,.

Type II.

Just as we work with
h(M)=\frac{1}{\beta}\,\log(\operatorname{tr}e^{\beta M})

when handling the operator norm, we directly work with the following
proxy function:

h(M)=\operatorname{tr}(M^{2})\,.

Recall the
3
-tensor
H
from (
5.4
):

H_{ijk}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}[(x-b_{t})_{i}(x-b_{t})_{j}(x-b_{t})_{k}]\,,\qquad H_{i}:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}[(x-b_{t})^{\otimes 2}(x-b_{t})_{i}]\,,

and that

\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}=\sum_{i}H_{i}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t,i}-\Sigma_{t}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}h(\Sigma_{t})

\displaystyle=\nabla h(\Sigma_{t})[\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}]+\frac{1}{2}\,\nabla^{2}h(\Sigma_{t})[\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t},\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}]=2\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t})+\operatorname{tr}(\mathrm{d}[\Sigma]_{t})

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}H_{i}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t,i})-2\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{3})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i}\operatorname{tr}(H_{i}^{2})\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

so
\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})=\sum_{i}\operatorname{tr}(H_{i}^{2})-2\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{3})\leq\sum_{i}\operatorname{tr}(H_{i}^{2})
.
Due to
\lVert H\rVert_{\textup{HS}}^{2}:=\sum_{ijk}H_{ijk}^{2}=\sum_{i}\lVert H_{i}\rVert_{\textup{HS}}^{2}=\sum_{i}\operatorname{tr}(H_{i}^{2})
,

\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\leq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{ijk}H_{ijk}^{2}\Bigr]\,.

(6.3)

We need one observation and technical lemma:

Proposition 6.7
.

\lVert H\rVert_{\textup{HS}}^{2}=\sum_{ijk}H_{ijk}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{X,Y\sim\pi_{t}}[(X\cdot Y)^{3}]
,
where
X
and
Y
are independent and centered.

Proof.

For
X\cdot Y=\sum_{i}x_{i}y_{i}
,

(X\cdot Y)^{3}=\Bigl(\sum_{i}x_{i}y_{i}\Bigr)^{3}=\sum_{ijk}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}y_{i}y_{j}y_{k}\,,

so

\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}[(X\cdot Y)^{3}]=\sum_{ijk}\mathbb{E}[x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}]\,\mathbb{E}[y_{i}y_{j}y_{k}]=\sum_{ijk}H_{ijk}^{2}=\lVert H\rVert_{\textup{HS}}^{2}\,.

∎

Thus, we now focus on the third-order moment
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}[(X\cdot Y)^{3}]
.
We could just use a Poincaré inequality to bound this, but we can
bound it more tightly through Itô calculus. To this end, we state
a preliminary lemma without proof.

Proposition 6.8

(
[
34
, Lemma 4.1]
)
.

Let
\pi
be centered and
1
-strongly
logconcave over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
(W_{t})
be a standard Brownian motion
with
W_{0}=0
. Then, there exists an adapted process
(M_{t})

of symmetric matrices such that
\int_{0}^{1}M_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
has
the same law with
\pi
and that almost surely
0\preceq M_{t}\preceq I_{n}

for all
t\in[0,1]
.

Thus, interpolating
X\sim\pi_{t}
via another stochastic process,
we can show the following:

Lemma 6.9
.

For
t>0
, let
\pi
be centered
and
t
-strongly logconcave over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
X,Y\sim\pi
be independent.
Then, for
\Sigma=\operatorname{cov}\pi
,

\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot Y)^{3}]\leq\frac{5}{t}\,\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot Y)^{2}]=\frac{5}{t}\,\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{2})\,.

Proof.

We may assume that
t=1
due to homogeneity. Using the interpolation
of
X
, define

\mathrm{d}X_{t}:=M_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\,.

Note that Itô’s formula leads to
\mathrm{d}\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{\otimes 2}]=\mathbb{E}[M_{t}^{2}]\,\mathrm{d}t=:Q_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t
,
so

\Sigma_{t}:=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{\otimes 2}]=\int_{0}^{t}Q_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\,,

and in particular,
\Sigma=\int_{0}^{1}Q_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s
.

Let
Y
be independent of
W_{t}
above. By Itô’s formula,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}(X_{t}\cdot Y)^{3}

\displaystyle=3\,(X_{t}\cdot Y)^{2}\,(Y\cdot\mathrm{d}X_{t})+3\,(X_{t}\cdot Y)\,(Y^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathrm{d}[X]_{t}\,Y)

\displaystyle=3\,(X_{t}\cdot Y)^{2}\,(Y^{\mathsf{T}}M_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t})+3\,(X_{t}\cdot Y)\,\lVert M_{t}Y\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

Integrating both sides over
[0,1]
and taking expectation, due to

X_{0}=0
,

\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot Y)^{3}]=3\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}[(X_{t}\cdot Y)\,\lVert M_{t}Y\rVert^{2}]\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

Then, using (

PI

) and
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y}[(X_{t}\cdot Y)\,\lVert M_{t}Y\rVert^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(X_{t}\cdot Y)\,(\lVert M_{t}Y\rVert^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\lVert M_{t}Y\rVert^{2}])\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(X_{t}\cdot Y)^{2}]}\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_{Y}(\lVert M_{t}Y\rVert^{2})}\leq\sqrt{X_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma X_{t}}\sqrt{4C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\,\mathbb{E}[\lVert M_{t}^{2}Y\rVert^{2}]}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{X_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma X_{t}}\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(M_{t}^{4}\Sigma)}\leq 2\sqrt{X_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma X_{t}}\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(M_{t}^{2}\Sigma)}\,,

where the last line follows from
M_{t}\preceq I
almost surely for
all
t\in[0,1]
. Putting back to
\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot Y)^{3}]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X\cdot Y)^{3}]

\displaystyle\leq 6\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\sqrt{X_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma X_{t}}\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(M_{t}^{2}\Sigma)}\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t\leq 6\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma X_{t}]\,\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(M_{t}^{2}\Sigma)]}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq 6\,\Bigl(\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma X_{t}]\,\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(M_{t}^{2}\Sigma)]\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr)^{1/2}=6\,\Bigl(\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{tr}(Q_{s}\Sigma)\,\mathrm{d}s\cdot\operatorname{tr}(Q_{t}\Sigma)\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\operatorname{tr}(Q_{t}\Sigma)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{6}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{2})\leq 5\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{2})\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

With this result in hand, we continue on (
6.3
):

\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\leq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{ijk}H_{ijk}^{2}\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{X,Y\sim\pi_{t}}[(X\cdot Y)^{3}]\leq\frac{5}{t}\,\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\,.

Solving this,

\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\leq\bigl(\frac{t}{t_{0}}\bigr)^{5}\,\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})\,.

6.2.2
Guan’s approach

In this section, we sketch the proof of Guan’s uniform bound on the
trace.

Theorem 6.10

(
[
30
]
)
.

\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})=\Theta(n)

for
t\lesssim 1
, where
\Sigma_{t}
is the covariance matrix of

\pi_{t}
obtained by SL with isotropic logconcave
\pi_{0}=\pi
.
Moreover,
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})=O(n)
for any
t>0
.

This removes the dimension-dependent restriction of
t\lesssim\log^{-2}n
.
We first prove this up to a universal constant time
t\leq c
, and
then bootstrap this to
any
time
t
.

High-level ideas.

Guan’s idea can be summarized as follows:

1.

Design a proxy function
F_{k,t}=\operatorname{tr}f_{k}(\Sigma_{t})
, which focuses
mainly on
large eigenvalues
of
\Sigma_{t}
(while suppressing
small ones exponentially). Here,
f_{k}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
is
explicitly constructed for two parameters
D_{k}>0
and
r_{k}\in[7/3,8/3]
,
such that for some constant
b_{k}=\Theta(1)
,
f_{k}(x)
is defined
as:

f_{k}(x)=\begin{cases}e^{-D_{k}\,|r_{k}-x|}&x\leq r_{k}-1/D_{k}\,,\\
b_{k}x^{2}&x\geq r_{k}\,,\end{cases}

and
|f^{\prime\prime}_{k}(x)|\leq D_{k}^{2}f_{k}(x)
(see Lemma
6.11
).
By construction,
F_{k,t}

almost
bounds
\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{t}^{2}

for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

(a)

Even though
f_{k}
is not specified on
[r_{k}-1/D_{k},r_{k}]
,
intuitively
C^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
is rich enough to
C^{2}
-smoothly
interpolate
e^{-1}(=f_{k}(r_{k}-D_{k}^{-1}))
and
b_{k}r_{k}^{2}(=f_{k}(r_{k}))
,
while ensuring
|f^{\prime\prime}_{k}(x)|\leq D_{k}^{2}f_{k}(x)
.

(b)

Focusing only on the large eigenvalues is beneficial, as eigenvalues
deviating from
\Theta(1)
are much less likely. Note that the property
of
f_{k}
ensures that
\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\,\mathds{1}\{\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\gtrsim 1\}]\approx\mathbb{E}F_{k,t}

and recall the operator-norm control in (
5.3
).
Thus, since all eigenvalues of
\Sigma_{t}
are bounded by
1/t
,
we have

\mathbb{E}F_{k,t}\approx\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\,\mathds{1}\{\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\gtrsim 1\}]\lesssim\frac{n}{t^{2}}\,e^{-1/t}\approx e^{-\log^{2}t}\,n\quad\text{for }t=t_{0}\,.

2.

Establish a Type II bound: for any
t_{0}>0
and all
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}\vee D_{k}^{-4}]
,

\mathbb{E}F_{k,t}\leq\bigl(\frac{t}{t_{0}}\bigr)^{\Theta(1)}\,\mathbb{E}F_{k,t_{0}}\,.

This part leverages the improved Lichnerowicz inequality (

ILI

).

(a)

Using this bound, the paper implements the following idea: increase

t
slightly from
t_{0}
(which would increase the bound
e^{-\log^{2}t}\,n

(on
\mathbb{E}F_{k,t_{0}}
) by a polynomial factor
t_{0}^{-\Theta(1)}
),
but
this polynomial increase is balanced by the exponentially
small coefficient of

e^{-\log^{2}t_{0}}
in the given bound
e^{-\log^{2}t_{0}}\,n
.
This lemma allows us to keep the coefficient under control until
t_{0}\vee D_{k}^{-4}
.

3.

By choosing
r_{k},D_{k}
suitably, the paper designs a partition

\bigcup_{k}[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
covering
[\log^{-2}n,\Theta(1)]
.
By induction on
k
(i.e., repeating the argument in 2-(a) for each
interval), it shows that

\mathbb{E}F_{k,t}\leq e^{-\log^{2}t_{k}}\,n\quad\text{for }t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}]\,.

(a)

This allows us to stitch together an
O(n)
-bound on
\mathbb{E}F_{k,t}

across the intervals
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
, up until
\max t_{k}=\Theta(1)

(i.e., universal-constant time). Then, for any
t\lesssim 1
, find
the corresponding interval
[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
including
t
, and
bound the quantity of interest as follows:

\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\,\mathds{1}\{\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\lesssim 1\}]+\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\,\mathds{1}\{\lVert\Sigma_{t}\rVert\gtrsim 1\}]\lesssim n+\mathbb{E}F_{k,t}\lesssim n\,.

Detailed sketch.

We now reflect on Guan’s proof in detail and the need for this sophisticated
approach.

(1) What if we work directly with
\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})

(not
F_{k,t}
)?

Recall the Type I bound,
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\lesssim n
up until

t\lesssim\log^{-2}n
, and the Type II bound given by

\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\leq\bigl(\frac{t}{t_{0}}\bigr)^{5}\,\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})\quad\text{for any }t\geq t_{0}\asymp\log^{-2}n\,.

Let us decompose
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})\,\mathds{1}[\lambda_{i}\ \text{small}]\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})\,\mathds{1}[\lambda_{i}\ \text{large}]\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})\,\mathds{1}[\lambda_{i}\ \text{small}]\bigr]+\frac{n}{t_{0}^{2}}\,\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{i}\ \text{large})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})\,\mathds{1}[\lambda_{i}\ \text{small}]\bigr]+\frac{n}{t_{0}^{2}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{Ct_{0}}\bigr)\,.

With this in mind, let us slightly increase
t
in the Type II bound.
As already seen in the high-level sketch, the multiplicative factor
of
(t/t_{0})^{5}\lesssim t_{0}^{-5}
increases the RHS of the Type
II bound by a
\operatorname{polylog}n
factor.

As for the second term, for
t_{0}\asymp\log^{-2}n
and
u:=\log n\asymp t_{0}^{-2}
,
we can write

\frac{1}{t_{0}^{2}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{Ct_{0}}\bigr)\approx\frac{u^{O(1)}}{e^{u^{O(1)}}}\,.

For
t=\Theta(1)
, the multiplicative term of
(t/t_{0})^{5}\lesssim t_{0}^{-5}=u^{10}

doesn’t make a meaningful increase in
\frac{u^{O(1)}}{e^{u^{O(1)}}}
,
because the exponential term in the denominator overwhelms this additional

\operatorname{polylog}n
factor. Hence, when using the Type II bound to relay

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})\,\mathds{1}[\lambda_{i}\ \text{large}]\bigr]

up to
t=\Theta(1)
, we might be able to control it
without
introducing

\operatorname{polylog}n
factors.

As for the first term, the best bound one can hope for is

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})\,\mathds{1}[\lambda_{i}\ \text{small}]\bigr]\lesssim n\,.

There is no slack in this bound (since we already know
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\lesssim n

for
t\geq 0
). However, as opposed to the second term, this
n

bound on the first term has no exponentially small factors (like
e^{-\operatorname{polylog}n}
),
which (if exists) would keep the polynomial blow-up by
t_{0}^{-5}

under control. Thus, if we naïvely stitch and relay the bound on

\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})
across the intervals by using the Type II
bound, then the bound on the first term would end up introducing an
extra
\operatorname{polylog}n
factor at
t=\Theta(1)
.

(2) Avoid small eigenvalues.

From the discussion above, we are prompt to try
to

only relay
a very slowly increasing bound on
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\,\mathds{1}[\lambda_{i}\ \text{\emph{large}}]\bigr]
.
If we can do so, when bounding
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})
for any
t\lesssim 1
,
we could bound the first term by
n
as we already did, while bounding
the second term by the relayed small bound on
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{\Theta(1)}^{2})\,\mathds{1}[\lambda_{i}\ \text{\emph{large}}]\bigr]\lesssim n
.

To this end, Guan introduces the following proxy function which almost
kills small eigenvalues by making them exponentially small.

Lemma 6.11

(Proxy function with focus on large eigenvalues)
.

For two parameters
D>0
and
r\in[7/3,8/3]
, there exists a
C^{2}(\mathbb{R})

function
f=f_{D,r}
such that for some
b\in[1/20,1/5]
,

f(x)=\begin{cases}e^{-D\,|r-x|}&x\leq r-1/D\,,\\
br^{2}&x\geq r\,,\end{cases}

(1) is increasing and (2) satisfies
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert f^{\prime\prime}(x)}}\right\rvert\leq D^{2}f(x)

on
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Since this
f
becomes quadratic when
x
is above
r
, it
almost

bounds
\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{t}^{2}
. Also,
x=r-1/D
is where the exponential-cutoff
starts, so any eigenvalues smaller than (say)
r-1/\sqrt{D}
will
be converted to an exponentially small number like
e^{-\sqrt{D}}
.
Then, by setting
D=\log^{O(1)}n
, we can make the first term (i.e.,
the sum of small eigenvalues of
\Sigma_{t}^{2}
) exponentially small
like
ne^{-\operatorname{polylog}n}
, which looks very similar to the second term
in the bound of
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t_{0}}^{2})
!

Fortunately, this proxy function also enjoys a Type II bound:

Lemma 6.12

(Type II bound)
.

Define
F_{t}=F_{t}(\Sigma_{t}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(\lambda_{i,t})

for the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i,t}\}_{i\in[n]}
of
\Sigma_{t}
.
Then, for any
t_{0}\in(0,1]
and
t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}\vee D^{-4}
,

\mathbb{E}F_{t}\leq\bigl(\frac{t}{t_{0}}\bigr)^{1000}\,\mathbb{E}F_{t_{0}}\,.

This lemma allows us to carry out the analytical idea discussed above:
(1) partition
[\log^{-2}n,\Theta(1)]
into
\{[t_{k},t_{k+1}]\}_{k\geq 0}

and (2) for any
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
, pass a given bound on
\mathbb{E}F_{t_{k}}

to a bound on
\mathbb{E}F_{t}
.

(3) Additional indexing by
k
.

At this point, it is quite tempting to directly transfer the second-term
bound from
t_{0}=\log^{-2}n
to
t=\Theta(1)
, since the first
term is also exponentially small that the multiplicative term of
t_{0}^{-1000}=\log^{2000}n

is dominated by
e^{-\operatorname{polylog}n}
. However, one should notice that
the lemma above is valid only on

t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}\vee\frac{1}{D^{4}}\,.

Given that
D
would be set very large (e.g.,
D\geq\operatorname{polylog}n
),
the valid regime of the lemma is actually very short (e.g., the valid
length is at most
D^{-4}\lesssim\log^{-O(1)}n
).

A strategy at this point is quite obvious:

•

Properly design the intervals
[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
so that
[\log^{-2}n,\Theta(1)]\subset\cup_{k}[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
.

•

For each interval

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert Q_{h}^{*}x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi Q_{h})}^{2}=\lVert Q_{h}^{*}x\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi Q_{h})}^{2}\,,

where we used the language of the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
. Then, using
\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\lVert b_{t}\rVert^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})

from (
5.5
) and known bounds on
\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})

at that time, KL managed to bound
F(\lambda)
, which in turn implied
the
O(\log^{4}n)
-bound on
\sigma_{\pi}
.

Guan analyzes the
UB
part more carefully while leveraging
the uniform trace bound (i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})=O(n)
from
Theorem
6.10
) and an improved spectral analysis.
The main components are integration by parts, the integrated Bochner
formula, and the properties of the dynamic
\Gamma
-calculus developed
by Klartag and Putterman
[
39
]
. By chaining the LB
with the improved UB, Guan establishes

e^{-h\lambda}F(\lambda)\lesssim h^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda F(\lambda)\,(\lceil\log_{2}h\rceil+1)}+\lambda hF(\lambda)\,.

Taking
h=(\lambda F(\lambda))^{-1/2}
, the paper deduces

F(\lambda)\lesssim\lambda\,|\log\lambda|\,.

Putting these back to (
7.1
), Guan concludes
that
\sigma_{\pi}\lesssim|\log\lambda_{1}|\asymp\log C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\lesssim\log\log n

as desired.

7.1
Calculus on adjoint of heat semigroup

We now delve into the adjoint
Q_{h}^{*}
of heat semigroups in more
details. We recommend readers read §
A
if they
are not familiar with this topic. In the following two subsections,

\pi_{t}
denotes
\pi*\gamma_{t}
, not the SL
\pi_{t}
.

7.1.1
Forward equation and box operator

Markov semigroups in general satisfy that for test functions
f
,

\partial_{t}P_{t}f=\mathcal{L}P_{t}f=P_{t}\mathcal{L}f\,,

as seen in

\partial_{t}P_{t}f=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{P_{t+\delta}f-P_{t}f}{\delta}=P_{t}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{P_{\delta}f-f}{\delta}=P_{t}\mathcal{L}f\,.

Thus, for the heat semigroup
Q_{t}:L^{2}(\pi_{t})\to L^{2}(\pi)
,
as its generator is
\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta
, we have that

\partial_{t}Q_{t}f=\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta Q_{t}f\,.

Recall that we derived in §
B.2.1
that the heat
adjoint
Q_{t}^{*}:L^{2}(\pi)\to L^{2}(\pi_{t})
satisfies

Q_{t}^{*}f=\frac{Q_{t}(f\pi)}{Q_{t}\pi}\,.

Taking gradients of both sides of
Q_{t}\pi\cdot Q_{t}^{*}f=Q_{t}(f\pi)
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla Q_{t}(f\pi)

\displaystyle=Q_{t}^{*}f\,\nabla Q_{t}\pi+Q_{t}\pi\,\nabla Q_{t}^{*}f\,,

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{t}(f\pi)

\displaystyle=Q_{t}^{*}f\,\Delta Q_{t}\pi+2\langle\nabla Q_{t}^{*}f,\nabla Q_{t}\pi\rangle+Q_{t}\pi\,\Delta Q_{t}^{*}f\,.

Using this, we can directly compute that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q_{t}^{*}f

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{t}Q_{t}(f\pi)}{Q_{t}\pi}-\frac{Q_{t}(f\pi)}{(Q_{t}\pi)^{2}}\,\partial_{t}Q_{t}\pi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\Delta Q_{t}(f\pi)}{Q_{t}\pi}-\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta Q_{t}\pi\,\frac{Q_{t}(f\pi)}{(Q_{t}\pi)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\Delta Q_{t}(f\pi)}{Q_{t}\pi}-\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{Q_{t}^{*}f\,\Delta Q_{t}\pi}{Q_{t}\pi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta Q_{t}^{*}f+\langle\nabla Q_{t}^{*}f,\nabla\log Q_{t}\pi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta Q_{t}^{*}f+\langle\nabla Q_{t}^{*}f,\nabla\log\pi_{t}\rangle

Therefore, comparing this with
\partial_{t}Q_{t}f=\mathcal{L}Q_{t}f=\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta Q_{t}f
,
we are led to define sort of “generator” of
Q_{t}^{*}
(called
the box operator in
[
39
]
) as follows:

\square_{t}f=\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta f+\langle\nabla\log\pi_{t},\nabla f\rangle\,,

(
\mathsf{box}
)

which looks quite similar to the Langevin operator associated with

\pi_{t}
, given as

\mathcal{L}_{t}f=\Delta f+\langle\nabla\log\pi_{t},\nabla f\rangle\,.

Note that

\partial_{t}Q_{t}^{*}f=\square_{t}Q_{t}^{*}f\,.

7.1.2
A dynamic version of
\Gamma
-calculus

Given a Langevin or heat-flow generator
\mathcal{L}
, we defined the (iterated)
carré du champ as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(f,g)

\displaystyle:=fg\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i+1}(f,g)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\,\bigl(\mathcal{L}\Gamma_{i}(f,g)-\Gamma_{i}(f,\mathcal{L}g)-\Gamma_{i}(\mathcal{L}f,g)\bigr)\ \text{for }i\geq 0\,.

Inspired by this, we can define this
\Gamma
-notion for the box
operator
\square_{t}
:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(f,g)

\displaystyle:=fg\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i+1}(f,g)

\displaystyle:=\square_{t}\Gamma_{i}(f,g)-\Gamma_{i}(\square_{t}f,g)-\Gamma_{i}(f,\square_{t}g)-\frac{\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}(f,g)\,.

We note that unlike the Langevin case (which admits a stationary distribution),
this has dependence on the time
t
.

The formulas of
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
.

Let us compute this:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\square_{t}(fg)-g\,\square_{t}f-f\,\square_{t}g

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{t}(fg)-g\,\mathcal{L}_{t}f-f\,\mathcal{L}_{t}g-\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta(fg)+\frac{1}{2}\,g\Delta f+\frac{1}{2}\,f\Delta g

\displaystyle=2\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\,(f\,\Delta g+2\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle+g\,\Delta f)+\frac{1}{2}\,g\Delta f+\frac{1}{2}\,f\Delta g

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle\,,

so it is actually the same with the carré du champ of the heat
and Langevin generators.

Using this, we can immediately obtain that

\Gamma_{2}(f,g)=\square_{t}\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle-\langle\nabla\square_{t}f,\nabla g\rangle-\langle\nabla f,\nabla\square_{t}g\rangle\,.

Let
\Gamma_{2}^{L}
be the iterated carré du champ of the Langevin
generator (associated with
\pi
), which satisfies

\Gamma_{2}^{L}(f,f)=\lVert\nabla^{2}f\rVert_{\textup{HS}}^{2}-\langle\nabla f,\nabla^{2}\log\pi\,\nabla f\rangle\,.

Due to
\mathcal{L}_{t}=\square_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(f,f)

\displaystyle=\square_{t}(\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2})-2\langle\nabla\square_{t}f,\nabla f\rangle

\displaystyle=2\Gamma_{2}^{L}(f,f)-\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta(\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2})+\langle\nabla\Delta f,\nabla f\rangle

\displaystyle=\lVert\nabla^{2}f\rVert_{\textup{HS}}^{2}-2\langle\nabla f,\nabla^{2}\log\pi_{t}\,\nabla f\rangle\,,

(7.2)

where we used (

Bochner

),
\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta(\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2})=\langle\Delta\nabla f,\nabla f\rangle+\lVert\nabla^{2}f\rVert_{\textup{HS}}^{2}
.

A handy lemma.

All the things developed lead to the following lemma.

Lemma 7.2
.

Under some regularity conditions on
f
, for
i=0,1
,

\partial_{t}\int\Gamma_{i}(Q_{t}^{*}f,Q_{t}^{*}f)\,\mathrm{d}(Q_{t}\pi)=-\partial_{t}\int\Gamma_{i+1}(Q_{t}^{*}f,Q_{t}^{*}f)\,\mathrm{d}(Q_{t}\pi)\,.

(
\mathsf{iter}
-
\mathsf{diff}
)

Proof.

Let
f_{t}:=Q_{t}^{*}f
and
\pi_{t}=Q_{t}f
. Then, using
\partial_{t}f_{t}=\square_{t}f_{t}

and
\partial_{t}\pi_{t}=\mathcal{L}\pi_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta\pi_{t}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int\Gamma_{i}(Q_{t}^{*}f,Q_{t}^{*}f)\,\mathrm{d}(Q_{t}\pi)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\int\Gamma_{i}(f_{t},f_{t})\,\pi_{t}

\displaystyle=\int\bigl(\partial_{t}\Gamma_{i}(f_{t},f_{t})\,\pi_{t}+2\Gamma_{i}(\square_{t}f_{t},f_{t})\,\pi_{t}+\Gamma_{i}(f_{t},f_{t})\,\frac{\Delta\pi_{t}}{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{eq:IBP}}{=}\int\bigl(2\Gamma_{i}(\square_{t}f_{t},f_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta\Gamma_{i}(f_{t},f_{t})+\partial_{t}\Gamma_{i}(f_{t},f_{t})\bigr)\,\pi_{t}

\displaystyle=\int\bigl(-\square_{t}\Gamma_{i}(f_{t},f_{t})+2\Gamma_{i}(\square_{t}f_{t},f_{t})+\partial_{t}\Gamma_{i}(f_{t},f_{t})\bigr)\,\pi_{t}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\bigl(\square_{t}\Gamma_{i}(f_{t},f_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta\Gamma_{i}(f_{t},f_{t})\bigr)\,\pi_{t}

\displaystyle=-\int\Gamma_{i+1}(f_{t},f_{t})\,\pi_{t}+\int\mathcal{L}_{t}\Gamma_{i}(f_{t},f_{t})\,\pi_{t}

\displaystyle=-\int\Gamma_{i+1}(f_{t},f_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}\,,

where we used
\int\mathcal{L}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\pi=0
for (regular) test functions

\varphi
(e.g., shown by (

IBP

)).
∎

7.1.3
Decreasing Rayleigh quotient

Recall that the Rayleigh quotient with respect to a function
f

is defined as

R_{f}=\frac{\int\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi}{\int f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi}\,.

We now consider its time-varying version, defined as

R_{f}(t)=\frac{\int\lVert\nabla Q_{t}^{*}f\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}(Q_{t}\pi)}{\int(Q_{t}^{*}f)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}(Q_{t}\pi)}=\frac{\int\lVert\nabla f_{t}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}{\int f_{t}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}\,,

and we show that this is non-increasing in
t
when
\pi
is a logconcave
distribution.

Theorem 7.3

(Decreasing Rayleigh)
.

Let
\pi
be an absolutely continuous and
logconcave probability measure over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Given a regular
f
(in
the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\pi)
), the Rayleigh quotient

R_{f}(t)=\frac{\int\lVert\nabla f_{t}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}{\int f_{t}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}

is non-increasing in
t\geq 0
. In particular, the function
\lVert f_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}

is log-convex in
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let us differentiate the quotient in
t
. Using (

iter

-

diff

)
for
i=0,1
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}R_{f}(t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\frac{\int\Gamma_{1}(f_{t},f_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}{\int\Gamma_{0}(f_{t},f_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\int\Gamma_{2}(f_{t},f_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}{\int\Gamma_{0}(f_{t},f_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}+\Bigl(\frac{\int\Gamma_{1}(f_{t},f_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}{\int\Gamma_{0}(f_{t},f_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bigl(\int\lVert\nabla f_{t}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}\bigr)^{2}}{\lVert f_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}^{4}}-\frac{\int(\lVert\nabla^{2}f_{t}\rVert_{\textup{HS}}^{2}-2\langle\nabla f_{t},\nabla^{2}\log\pi_{t}\,\nabla f_{t}\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}{\lVert f_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}^{2}}

\displaystyle\underset{(i)}{=}\frac{\bigl(\int\lVert\nabla f_{t}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}\bigr)^{2}-\int(\mathcal{L}_{t}f_{t})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}\,\lVert f_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}^{2}}{\lVert f_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}^{4}}+\frac{\int\langle\nabla f_{t},\nabla^{2}\log\pi_{t}\,\nabla f_{t}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}{\lVert f_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\bigl(\int\lVert\nabla f_{t}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}\bigr)^{2}-\int(\mathcal{L}_{t}f_{t})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}\,\lVert f_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}^{2}}{\lVert f_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}^{4}}\leq 0

where in
(i)
we used (

Int

-

Bochner

), and the last inequality
follows from

\int\lVert\nabla f_{t}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}=\int\Gamma_{1}(f_{t},f_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}=\int(-\mathcal{L}_{t}f_{t})f_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}\leq\sqrt{\int(\mathcal{L}_{t}f_{t})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}\sqrt{\int f_{t}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}\,.

As for the second claim, we note that

\partial_{t}\log\lVert f_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}^{2}=\frac{\partial_{t}\int\Gamma_{0}(f_{t},f_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}{\lVert f_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}^{2}}=-\frac{\int\Gamma_{1}(f_{t},f_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}{\lVert f_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}^{2}}=-R_{f}(t)\,,

so
\partial_{t}^{2}(\log\lVert f_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}^{2})=-\partial_{t}R_{f}(t)\geq 0
.
∎

This gives us an important observation that will be used to study
the thin-shell conjecture.

Corollary 7.4

(Relating
Q_{h}^{*}f
and
f
)
.

Let

f\in H^{1}(\pi)
. Then for all
h>0
,

\frac{\lVert Q_{h}^{*}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}}{\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}}\geq e^{-hR_{f}(0)}\,.

One might notice a similarity with, for the Langevin semigroup
P_{t}
,

\frac{\lVert P_{t}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}}{\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}}\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{2t}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)}\bigr)\,.

Proof.

From the computation above,

\partial_{t}\log\lVert Q_{h}^{*}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}=-R_{f}(t)\geq-R_{f}(0)\,,

so

\log\frac{\lVert Q_{h}^{*}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}}{\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}}\geq-hR_{f}(0)\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

It also give another useful insights into the adjoint:

Corollary 7.5

(Gradient ineqaulity)
.

Let
f\in H^{1}(\pi)
.
Then for all
h>0
,

\int\lVert\nabla Q_{h}^{*}f\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}\leq\int\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi\,.

Proof.

Recall that

R_{f}(h)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{h}}[\lVert\nabla Q_{h}^{*}f\rVert^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{h}}[\lVert Q_{h}^{*}f\rVert^{2}]}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\lVert f\rVert^{2}]}\,.

This implies that

\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{h}}[\lVert\nabla Q_{h}^{*}f\rVert^{2}]\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{h}}[\lVert Q_{h}^{*}f\rVert^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\lVert f\rVert^{2}]}\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2}]\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

7.2
Bound on the thin-shell constant

We are now ready to prove
\sigma_{n}\lesssim\log\psi_{\mathsf{KLS}}(n)
.
Pick an isotropic logconcave
\pi
that saturates the thin-shell
constant (i.e.,
\sigma_{n}/2\leq\sigma_{\pi}
). It suffices to show
that

\sigma_{\pi}\lesssim\log\psi_{\mathsf{KLS}}(\pi)\asymp\log C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\log\lambda_{\pi}}}\right\rvert\,,

where
\lambda_{\pi}
denotes the spectral gap of
-\mathcal{L}
associated
with
\pi
.

Recall that

\sigma_{\pi}^{2}\lesssim\int_{\lambda_{\pi}}^{\infty}\frac{F(\lambda)}{\lambda^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda=\int_{e^{-1}}^{\infty}\frac{F(\lambda)}{\lambda^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda+\int_{\lambda_{\pi}\wedge e^{-1}}^{e^{-1}}\frac{F(\lambda)}{\lambda^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,,

where
F(\lambda)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}

is the average spectral mass of the coordinate functions below level

\lambda
. Note that
F(\lambda)\leq 1
for
\lambda>0
, as seen in

F(\lambda)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\int\lVert x\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi=1\,.

Thus,

\sigma_{\pi}^{2}\lesssim\int_{e^{-1}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda+\int_{\lambda_{\pi}\wedge e^{-1}}^{e^{-1}}\frac{F(\lambda)}{\lambda^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\lesssim 1+\int_{\lambda_{\pi}\wedge e^{-1}}^{e^{-1}}\frac{F(\lambda)}{\lambda^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,.

(7.3)

Proof outline.

We can now focus on bounding
F(\lambda)
when
\lambda\leq e^{-1}
. As
mentioned earlier, our approach is to translate the bound on
\lVert Q_{h}^{*}x\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}

into one on
F(\lambda)
. This starts with the observation made in Corollary
7.4
:

\lVert Q_{h}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}\geq e^{-hR_{E_{\lambda}x_{i}}(0)}\,\lVert E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}\,.

As
R_{E_{\lambda}x_{i}}(0)\leq\lambda
(due to the spectral projection

E_{\lambda}
), it follows that
\lVert Q_{h}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}\geq e^{-h\lambda}\,\lVert E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}
,
so

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert Q_{h}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}\geq e^{-h\lambda}\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}=e^{-h\lambda}\,F(\lambda)\,.

Therefore, we can introduce
\sum\lVert Q_{h}^{*}x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}

simply by, for
E_{\lambda}^{\perp}:=\mathrm{id}-E_{\lambda}
,

\sum_{i}\lVert Q_{h}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}\leq\sum_{i}\lVert Q_{h}^{*}x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}+2\underbrace{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int(Q_{h}^{*}E_{\lambda}^{\perp}x_{i})\,(Q_{h}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}}}\right\rvert}_{=:\mathsf{A}}=\lVert Q_{h}^{*}x\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}+2\mathsf{A}\,.

Hence, it suffices to bound the first term and
\mathsf{A}
(and then
simply relate this upper bound to the lower bound on the LHS, which
is
e^{-h\lambda}F(\lambda)
).

Bound on
\lVert Q_{h}^{*}x\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}
.

Let us bound
\lVert Q_{h}^{*}x\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[\lVert b_{t}\rVert^{2}]
.
Since
\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}[\lVert b_{t}\rVert^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{t}^{2})\lesssim n
,
we have

\mathbb{E}[\lVert b_{t}\rVert^{2}]\lesssim nt\,.

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}[\lVert b_{t}\rVert^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl\|\int x\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}\Bigr\|^{2}\Bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\int\lVert x\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}\Bigr]=\int\lVert x\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}=n\,.

Combining these two bounds, for some constant
c>e
,

\lVert Q_{h}^{*}x\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}\leq cn\,(h^{-1}\wedge 1)\,.

Bound on
\mathsf{A}
.

We now bound the term
\mathsf{A}
: using (

iter

-

diff

),

\displaystyle\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int(Q_{h}^{*}E_{\lambda}^{\perp}x_{i})\,(Q_{h}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{h}\partial_{s}\int(Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}^{\perp}x_{i})\,(Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}\mathrm{d}s\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{h}\int\langle\nabla Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}^{\perp}x_{i},\nabla Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}\mathrm{d}s\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{h}\int\langle\nabla Q_{s}^{*}x_{i}-\nabla Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i},\nabla Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}\mathrm{d}s\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl|\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{h}\int\langle\nabla Q_{s}^{*}x_{i},\nabla Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}\mathrm{d}s}_{=:\mathsf{I}}\Bigr|+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{h}\int\lVert\nabla Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}\mathrm{d}s}_{=:\mathsf{II}}\,.

We bound
\mathsf{I}
and
\mathsf{II}
, respectively. Let us denote by

\mathcal{L}_{s}
the Langevin generator associated with
\pi_{s}=\pi*\gamma_{s}
.
As for
\mathsf{I}
, using (

IBP

) and Cauchy-Schwarz,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\langle\nabla Q_{s}^{*}x_{i},\nabla Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int Q_{s}^{*}x_{i}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{s}Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int(Q_{s}^{*}x_{i})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int(\mathcal{L}_{s}Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq c^{1/2}n^{1/2}\,(s^{-1/2}\wedge 1)\,\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int(\mathcal{L}_{s}Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}\Bigr)^{1/2}\,.

Letting
\Gamma_{2,s}(f):=\Gamma_{2,s}(f,f)
be the dynamic iterated
carré du champ (
7.2
) and using (

Int

-

Bochner

),

\displaystyle\int(\mathcal{L}_{s}Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}

\displaystyle=\int(\lVert\nabla^{2}Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert_{\textup{HS}}^{2}-\langle\nabla^{2}\log\pi_{s}\,\nabla Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i},Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}

\displaystyle=\int\bigl(\Gamma_{2,s}(Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i})+\langle\nabla^{2}\log\pi_{s}\,\nabla Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i},Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rangle\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}

\displaystyle\leq\int\Gamma_{2,s}(Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}=-\partial_{s}\int\Gamma_{1,s}(Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}\,.

Putting this back above,

\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\langle\nabla Q_{s}^{*}x_{i},\nabla Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}\Bigr|\leq c^{1/2}n^{1/2}\,(s^{-1/2}\wedge 1)\,\Bigl(-\partial_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\Gamma_{1,s}(Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}\Bigr)^{1/2}\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathsf{I}

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\int_{0}^{h}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\langle\nabla Q_{s}^{*}x_{i},\nabla Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}\mathrm{d}s\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq c^{1/2}n^{1/2}\int_{0}^{h}(s^{-1/2}\wedge 1)\,\Bigl(-\partial_{s}\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\Gamma_{1,s}(Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}}_{=:\varphi(s)}\Bigr)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}s\,.

To bound this, we use a simple algebraic lemma:

Lemma 7.6

(
[
30
]
)
.

Let
\varphi\geq 0
be a differentiable and
non-increasing on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Then, for any integer
N\geq 0
,

\int_{0}^{2^{N}}(s^{-1/2}\wedge 1)\,\bigl(-\varphi^{\prime}(s)\bigr)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\sqrt{\varphi(0)\,(N+1)}\,.

To use this, we bound

\displaystyle\varphi(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\Gamma_{1,0}(E_{\lambda}x_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\pi=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\lVert\nabla E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi

(7.4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\int(E_{\lambda}x_{i})(-\mathcal{L}E_{\lambda}x_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\pi\leq\lambda\sum_{i}\int\lVert E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi

\displaystyle=\lambda n\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}=\lambda nF(\lambda)\,.

Using this lemma with this bound and assuming
h\geq 1
for a while,

\displaystyle\mathsf{I}

\displaystyle\leq c^{1/2}n^{1/2}\int_{0}^{h}(s^{-1/2}\wedge 1)\,\Bigl(-\partial_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\Gamma_{1,s}(Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}\Bigr)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq c^{1/2}\lambda^{1/2}nF^{1/2}(\lambda)\,(\lceil\log_{2}h\rceil+1)^{1/2}\,.

As for
\mathsf{II}
, using the gradient inequality (Corollary
7.5
)
and reproducing (
7.4
),

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{h}\int\lVert\nabla Q_{s}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{s}\mathrm{d}s\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{h}\int\lVert\nabla E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi\mathrm{d}s\leq\lambda hnF(\lambda)\,.

Combining the bounds on
\mathsf{I}
and
\mathsf{II}
, the term
\mathsf{A}

is bounded as, if
h\geq 1

\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int(Q_{h}^{*}E_{\lambda}^{\perp}x_{i})\,(Q_{h}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{h}\Bigr|\leq c^{1/2}\lambda^{1/2}nF^{1/2}(\lambda)\,(\lceil\log_{2}h\rceil+1)^{1/2}+\lambda hnF(\lambda)\,.

Putting together.

We now provide a bound on
F(\lambda)
on
\lambda\in(0,e^{-1})
(so
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\log\lambda}}\right\rvert\geq 1
)
as

F(\lambda)\lesssim\lambda\,|\log\lambda|\,.

We may assume that

\frac{F(\lambda)}{\lambda}\geq e^{-4}\,.

Otherwise, the desired inequality clearly holds on
\lambda\in(0,e^{-1})
.

Recall that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert Q_{h}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}\geq e^{-h\lambda}\,F(\lambda)\,,

and

\sum_{i}\lVert Q_{h}^{*}E_{\lambda}x_{i}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}\leq\lVert Q_{h}^{*}x\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{h})}^{2}+2\mathsf{A}\,.

Combining these, if
h\geq 1
,

\displaystyle e^{-h\lambda}F(\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq c\,(h^{-1}\wedge 1)+2c^{1/2}\lambda^{1/2}F^{1/2}(\lambda)\,(\lceil\log_{2}h\rceil+1)^{1/2}+2\lambda hF(\lambda)

\displaystyle=ch^{-1}+2c^{1/2}\lambda^{1/2}F^{1/2}(\lambda)\,(\lceil\log_{2}h\rceil+1)^{1/2}+2\lambda hF(\lambda)\,.

Take

h=\lambda^{-1/2}F^{-1/2}(\lambda)\,,

under which
1/2\leq e^{-\lambda^{1/2}F^{-1/2}}
holds due to
F/\lambda\geq e^{-4}
.
Thus,

\frac{1}{2}\,F\lesssim\lambda^{1/2}F^{1/2}+\lambda^{1/2}F^{1/2}\,(\lceil\log_{2}h\rceil+1)^{1/2}+\lambda^{1/2}F^{1/2}\,,

and

F\lesssim\lambda\,(\lceil\log_{2}h\rceil+1)\,.

One can readily check that
\lceil\log_{2}h\rceil\lesssim|\log\lambda|
,
so the desired inequality follows.

If
h\leq 1
, we could have simply worked with
2^{|\log\lambda|}
instead
of
h
in the algebraic lemma, so

F\lesssim\lambda\,|\log\lambda|\,,

which completes the proof of the desired inequality.

Substituting the desired inequality back to (
7.3
),

\sigma_{\pi}^{2}\lesssim 1+\int_{e^{-1}\wedge\lambda_{\pi}}^{e^{-1}}\frac{F(\lambda)}{\lambda^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\lesssim 1+\int_{e^{-1}\wedge\lambda_{\pi}}^{e^{-1}}\frac{|\log\lambda|}{\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\lesssim|\log(\lambda_{\pi}\wedge e^{-1})|^{2}\,,

and thus

\sigma_{\pi}^{2}\lesssim|\log\lambda_{\pi}|^{2}\,.

8
Discussion

There are several applications of localization in recent years that
are not covered in this survey. We have already mentioned localization
schemes for analyzing Markov chains
[
15
]
.
There are also algorithmic applications
[
23
]
and
information-theoretic perspectives
[
24
]
.

The KLS conjecture, a principal motivation for localization methods,
remains unresolved. In fact, the lower bound in Theorem
5.4

suggests that a new idea is needed to affirmatively resolve the conjecture.
Besides its mathematical appeal, settling the full conjecture is also
motivated by a few different applications such as hypercontractivity
for clustering mixtures of logconcave distributions and bounding the
spectral norm of a matrix with a joint logconcave distribution.

We mention the following question, which could be viewed as an analog
of the Kadison–Singer problem for convex bodies: given an isotropic
convex body, does there exist a hyperplane partition into two convex
bodies such that both parts have covariance matrices with spectral
norm strictly less than
1
?
