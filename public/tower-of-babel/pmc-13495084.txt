TDP-43 drives ferroptosis evasion by modulating PCBP1/PCBP2 axis to promote lung adenocarcinoma growth
Authors: Bowen Liu, Xin Chen, Jingjing Chen, Jinyan Feng, Jin Wang, Xin Jin, Jinling Cao, Bingqian Zhao, Huilong Yin, Jingru Wang, Yun Yang, Pengfei Zheng, Shujun Ma, Wei Liao, Min Zhang, Bo Yang, Jie Wang, Xiaofang Geng, Haomin Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) continues to be one of the top causes of cancer-associated deaths worldwide. TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) has been linked to several tumor-related biological processes, yet its specific role in LUAD growth remains poorly understood. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent regulated cell death, is increasingly recognized as a key process affecting tumor development. Here, we report that TDP-43 acts as a key ferroptosis regulator facilitating LUAD growth. TDP-43 is upregulated in LUAD tissues and positively correlates with poor patient prognosis. Functionally, TDP-43 silencing impairs LUAD cell proliferation and promotes cell death, whereas TDP-43 overexpression exerts the opposite effects. Consistently, TDP-43 knockdown markedly suppresses xenograft tumor growth in vivo. Whole transcriptome sequencing combined with subsequent validation assays revealed that TDP-43 is capable of suppressing ferroptosis in LUAD cells. Accordingly, TDP-43 silencing sensitizes cells to erastin/RSL3-induced ferroptosis. Mechanistically, TDP-43 exerts its effects through two distinct pathways: it interacts with transcription factor VDR via its C-terminal domain to activate PCBP1 transcription, while directly binding to the UG-rich motif within the 3′UTR of PCBP2 mRNA to stabilize its transcript. Upregulation of iron chaperones PCBP1 and PCBP2 alleviates intracellular Fe2+ overload and lipid peroxidation, allowing LUAD cells to escape ferroptosis and support tumor growth. These results highlight TDP-43's pivotal role in regulating LUAD ferroptosis and offer promising directions for LUAD treatment.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, Ferroptosis, TDP-43, PCBP1, PCBP2

Introduction

Lung cancer is a common malignant tumor of the respiratory tract, and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is its most common pathological type, accounting for about 40% of the total cases. It mainly arises from alveolar type 2 (AT2) cells and occurs more frequently in women and non-smokers [1,2]. Over the years, despite the continuous development and improvement of treatment methods, LUAD remains one of the most invasive and prone to short-term deterioration tumor types, with a five-year overall survival rate of only about 11% [3]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms of LUAD occurrence and development, as well as identifying new therapeutic targets, is of great significance for improving patient prognosis.

TAR DNA-binding protein 43(TDP-43, also known as TARDBP)is a widely expressed DNA/RNA binding protein encoded by the TARDBP gene [4]. It can undergo homologous dimerization through its N-terminal domain and preferentially bind UG rich sequences through its two RNA recognition motifs (RRM1 and RRM2), thus participating in the regulation of RNA transcription, stability, transport, translation and other processes [5]. TDP-43 has been widely reported for many years due to its important role in neurodegenerative diseases, but recent studies have also found that it is closely related to the development of cancer. Studies have shown that TDP-43 is highly expressed in various cancers such as breast cancer, liver cancer and melanoma [[6], [7], [8]]. TDP-43 forms a positive feedback loop with TP63 to drive malignant progression, in which TDP-43 upregulates TP63 expression [9]. Our previous research has confirmed that TDP-43 is highly expressed in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and can promote HCC tumor growth by modulating lipid metabolism processes [8]. We recently found that the expression of TDP-43 in LUAD tissues was significantly higher than that in adjacent normal tissues. However, its potential role in LUAD growth remains to be explored.

Iron, as an essential trace element in the human body, fulfills pivotal physiological and biochemical functions that are indispensable for normal cellular homeostasis [10]. Ferroptosis, a newly identified form of regulated cell death, is characterized by its strict dependence on iron [11]. Mounting evidence indicates that cellular ferroptosis plays a critical role in cancer initiation, progression, stemness maintenance, and metastasis [12,13]. To achieve unlimited proliferation and malignant progression, cancer cells frequently remodel their metabolic and signaling pathways, with ferroptosis evasion emerging as a key strategy to gain a survival advantage. Two hallmark events underpin the initiation and execution of ferroptosis: the intracellular accumulation of labile iron pools and the subsequent induction of lipid peroxidation [14]. At this point, reducing labile ferrous ions (Fe2+) accumulation and inhibiting the subsequent occurrence of lipid peroxidation stand as a preferred strategy for cancer cells to evade ferroptosis.

Poly (C)-binding protein 1 (PCBP1) and Poly (C)-binding protein 2 (PCBP2) are multifunctional RNA-binding proteins engaged in gene transcription and ferritin iron loading [15,16]. Acting as specific iron chaperones, these proteins directly attach to labile Fe2+ in the cytosolic compartment, forming stable protein-iron complexes to hinder the buildup of free Fe2+ [15]. They subsequently mediate the targeted delivery of Fe2+ to ferritin and sequester the iron within ferritin's core [17]. By guaranteeing the ordered circulation of Fe2+ through these sequential steps, PCBP1 and PCBP2 sustain the balance of iron metabolism. Accumulated evidence highlights their important roles in the regulation of ferroptosis through modulation of Fe2+ levels [18]. Blocking the function of PCBP1 as an iron carrier raises intracellular free Fe2+ levels, resulting in excessive iron accumulation and ferroptosis [19]. Moreover, reduced expression of PCBP2 has been shown to trigger ferroptosis in malignant mesothelioma [20]. However, whether dysregulated expression of PCBP1 and PCBP2 contributes to ferroptosis evasion and thereby fuels the tumor growth of LUAD remains to be explored.

In the present study, we discover that TDP-43 can act as a suppressor of ferroptosis in LUAD cells. Mechanically, TDP-43 interacts with transcription factor VDR via its C-terminal domain to activate PCBP1 transcription; on the other hand, TDP-43 directly binds to the UG-rich sequence within the 3′UTR of PCBP2 mRNA to enhance its mRNA stability. Following the upregulation of PCBP1 and PCBP2, TDP-43 promotes ferroptosis evasion by suppressing Fe2+ accumulation, thereby facilitating tumor growth in LUAD. These findings highlight TDP-43 and its regulatory axis PCBP1/PCBP2 as promising therapeutic targets, providing novel strategies for ferroptosis-based LUAD treatment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture, transfection and treatment

The human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293T was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Meanwhile, the normal human lung bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B, as well as the lung cancer cell lines A549, NCl-H1299, NCl-H1975 and HCC827, were purchased from BeNa Culture Collection (BNCC, China). For cell culture, HEK293T cells were maintained in complete DMEM (Servicebio, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Solarbio, China). In contrast, all other aforementioned cell lines were cultured in complete RPMI 1640 (Servicebio) with the same supplements. All cell lines were verified by STR profiling. When the cell confluence reached approximately 60%, specific siRNAs and plasmids were transfected into the aforementioned cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were harvested to extract RNA or protein samples, which were used to evaluate the transfection efficiency. The siRNAs used in the study were synthesized by Guangzhou RiboBio Co., Ltd., with their detailed sequences provided in Supplementary Table 1. The eukaryotic expression plasmid pcDNA-Flag-VDR used for VDR overexpression was purchased from YouBio Co., Ltd. (China). The eukaryotic expression plasmid for TDP-43 overexpression was constructed in-house using HA-pcDNA3.1 vector. For ferroptosis sensitivity assays, cells were treated with 5 μM erastin, 2.5 μM RSL3, or 2 μM Fer-1, alone or in combination, for 24h, followed by detection of ferroptosis indicators. For mRNA stability analysis, cells were treated with 5 μg/ml ActD and harvested at the corresponding time points.

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq)

NCI-H1975-KD-TDP-43 and NCI-H1975-Control cells served as the study models, with three biological replicates included in each group. These cells were cultured until reaching the logarithmic growth phase, after which they were harvested for RNA extraction. Following assessment of RNA concentration and purity, the samples were submitted to BGI Genomics (China) for RNA sequencing. The mRNA library construction and transcriptome data analysis were performed by BGI Genomics, with detailed procedures available on the institution's official website. RNA-seq read counts were normalized as Fragments Per kilobase Million. The raw and processed data generated from the above analysis have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under the accession number GSE318515.

Assay for lipid ROS content

Cellular lipid peroxidation levels were detected using C11 BODIPY 581/591 probe (Thermo, USA). Briefly, properly growing cells were seeded evenly into 24-well black-wall clear-bottom fluorescence plates. Upon reaching the logarithmic growth phase, the medium was discarded, and 10 μM C11 BODIPY 581/591 probe was added, followed by incubation at 37°C for 30 min. After incubation, the probe-containing supernatant was removed, and cells were stained with Hoechst nuclear stain (Beyotime, China) for 30 min. Following staining, cells were washed 3 times with PBS buffer, fresh medium was added, and observations were made using a confocal fluorescence microscope with imaging. Quantification of fluorescent signals between groups was analyzed using ImageJ software. Green fluorescence intensity indicated the level of oxidized lipids (C11-oxidized, C11Ox), while red fluorescence intensity represented non-oxidized lipids (C11-non-oxidized, C11Non−Ox). The lipid ROS level was calculated using the formula: (C11Ox)/(C11Ox + C11Non−Ox).

Assay for Intracellular Fe2+ content

Cells in good growth condition were evenly seeded into 24-well fluorescent culture plates and cultured overnight. Intracellular Fe2+ levels were then detected according to the manufacturer's instructions (Dojindo, Japan). Briefly, the medium was removed, and cells were stained with Hoechst nuclear dye for 30 min. After washing with serum-free medium, FerroOrange working solution was added and incubated for another 30 min. Fluorescent images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope, and fluorescence intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Xenograft

Six-week-old female BALB/c athymic nude mice were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All experimental procedures were conducted in compliance with the ethical guidelines of the Ethics Committee of Xinxiang Medical University and adhered to the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Mice were randomly assigned to different groups with 6 mice per group. For the subcutaneous xenograft model, following expansion to the logarithmic growth phase, target cells were harvested and subcutaneously implanted into the left shoulder region of each nude mouse. After inoculation, tumor volumes were measured weekly, and body weight was recorded concurrently. Six weeks post-inoculation, mice were euthanized and subjected to dissection; tumors were photographed, weighed, and subsequently processed for analyses. For the orthotopic lung cancer model, tumor cells were injected into the left lung parenchyma of anesthetized mice. Postoperatively, mice were monitored for recovery and signs of pneumothorax. Every seven days, mice were weighed, injected intraperitoneally with D-luciferin (150 mg/kg), and anesthetized with isoflurane. The mice were then subjected to bioluminescence imaging using an IVIS Spectrum in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Tumor burden was quantified by measuring ROI signals with Living Image software. At the experimental endpoint, mice were sacrificed. Dissected whole lung tissues were first imaged ex vivo for bioluminescence, then processed for H&E staining and histological analysis.

Results

TDP-43 is highly expressed in LUAD tissues and negatively correlated with patient survival

First, based on The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data, we analyzed the differential expression of TDP-43 in various cancer tissues using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA, http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) tool. The results showed that TDP-43 was upregulated in 11 types of cancers, including lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) (Fig. 1A). To further confirm the potential association between TDP-43 and LUAD, we analyzed the expression level of TDP-43 in LUAD tissues by using UALCAN website (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/). The data showed that TDP-43 was highly expressed in primary tumor tissues compared with normal tissues (Fig. 1B). Moreover, TDP-43 expression was closely associated with the stage of LUAD (Fig. 1C). Notably, LUAD patients with higher levels of TDP-43 expression actually had shorter overall survival (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, we detected the expression level of TDP-43 in different cell lines by Western blot assay. Compared with the normal human bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B, the expression level of TDP-43 was significantly elevated in lung cancer cell lines, including A549, NCI-H1299, NCI-H1975, and HCC827 (Fig. 1E). In order to further confirm the expression of TDP-43 in LUAD tissues, 17 pairs of LUAD tissues were collected; and Western blot analysis showed that the expression level of TDP-43 in most cancer tissues was higher than that in adjacent non-tumor tissues (13/17, Fig. 1F–H). In addition, we collected another 8 pairs of LUAD tissues, and analyzed the expression difference of TDP-43 by immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining (Fig. 1I), with the same result as the above experiment obtained. These observations suggest that TDP-43 is highly expressed in LUAD tissues and negatively correlates with patient survival.

TDP-43 promotes proliferation and inhibits cell death in LUAD cells

To explore the potential role of TDP-43 in LUAD cell growth, we investigated the effects of TDP-43 on cell proliferation and survival. TDP-43 was overexpressed in A549 and NCI-H1299 cells. For loss-of-function assays, two approaches were used to minimize off-target effects: transient knockdown with siRNAs in NCI-H1299 cells, and stable knockdown via lentiviral shRNA in NCI-H1975 cells. The corresponding overexpression and knockdown efficiencies were detected by qRT-PCR and Western blot assays, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1A–E). MTT assay results showed that increased TDP-43 expression led to a significant enhancement in cell density, while knockdown of TDP-43 resulted in a marked decrease in it (Fig. 2A–C, Supplementary Fig. 1F). What's more, EdU staining indicated a notable reduction in the number of EdU-positive cells upon TDP-43 knockdown, whereas TDP-43 overexpression induced an increase in EdU-positive cells (Fig. 2D–G, Supplementary Fig. 1G and H). The data of colony formation assay in A549, NCI-H1299 and NCI-H1975 cells also revealed the pro-proliferative effect of TDP-43 (Fig. 2H–J, Supplementary Fig. 1I and J). Next, the effect of TDP-43 on cell mortality was evaluated by flow cytometry. When TDP-43 was knocked down, a clear increase in the cell death rate could be detected (Fig. 2K–M). Furthermore, TDP-43 overexpression significantly reduced the cell death rate (Fig. 2N, Supplementary Fig. 1K and L). Overall, the above findings support that TDP-43 promotes proliferation and inhibits cell death in LUAD cells.

TDP-43 knockdown suppresses LUAD tumor growth in vivo

In order to evaluate the role of TDP-43 during lung tumor growth in vivo, we established luciferase (Luc)-labeled stable TDP-43-knockdown and corresponding control NCI-H1299 cell lines. Firstly, these cells were subcutaneously injected into nude mice to establish a xenograft model and examine the impact of TDP-43 on lung tumor growth (Fig. 3A). Notably, knockdown of TDP-43 significantly inhibited tumor growth, as evidenced by marked decreases in both tumor volume and weight compared to the control group (Fig. 3B–D). Body weights remained comparable between the two groups during the experiment, indicating that the altered tumor growth upon TDP-43 knockdown was not caused by non-specific effects on overall mouse health or condition (Supplementary Fig. 2A). IHC staining of tumor tissues revealed significantly reduced Ki-67 positivity in TDP-43-depleted tumors compared with control tissues (Fig. 3E and F), indicating suppressed cell proliferation. These findings demonstrate that TDP-43 promotes cell proliferation and facilitates in vivo tumor growth in LUAD.

Next, to further investigate the role of TDP-43 in local LUAD lesion growth, we established a lung orthotopic xenograft model using Luc-NCI-H1299-Control and Luc-NCI-H1299-KD-TDP-43 cells (Fig. 3G). In vivo live imaging showed markedly reduced bioluminescent signals in mice bearing TDP-43-depleted tumors compared with controls (Fig. 3H and I). Moreover, gross observation of fresh and fixed lung tissues, along with ex vivo bioluminescence imaging, confirmed that TDP-43 knockdown markedly impaired lung tumor engraftment and suppressed lung tumor growth in situ (Fig. 3J and K). Control mice exhibited extensive, large tumor nodules and robust bioluminescent signals in the lungs, while TDP-43-depleted mice showed substantially reduced tumor burden, with fewer, smaller lesions and significantly diminished bioluminescence intensity (Fig. 3J and K). Consistent with the gross and imaging findings, H&E staining confirmed that silencing TDP-43 significantly reduced the number of lung lesions, attenuated tumor cell infiltration, and preserved normal pulmonary architecture in mice (Fig. 3L). Together, these in vivo results confirm that TDP-43 is critical for promoting LUAD tumor growth.

TDP-43 facilitates ferroptosis evasion in LUAD cells

To explore the molecular mechanism underlying TDP-43-mediated LUAD growth, we performed RNA-seq analysis in stable TDP-43 knockdown and control NCI-H1975 cells. The data showed that, compared with the control group, a total of 4509 genes were upregulated and 4845 genes were downregulated after TDP-43 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 3A). The volcano plot and the cluster heatmap intuitively displayed the expression differences of these genes (Supplementary Fig. 3B and C). KEGG enrichment pathway analysis showed that multiple pathways responded to the changes in the expression of TDP-43 (Fig. 4A). Among them, the rich ratio of the ferroptosis pathway was relatively high, which attracted our attention. Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [21]. Further GSEA demonstrated that ferroptosis-related gene sets were significantly enriched in TDP-43 knockdown cells (Fig. 4B). These results prompted us to hypothesize that the oncogenic role of TDP-43 may occur through ferroptosis inhibition. To test this hypothesis, we treated cells with specific inhibitors targeting distinct cell death pathways: Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, a ferroptosis-specific inhibitor), Necrostatin-1 (Nec-1, a necroptosis-specific inhibitor), Z-VAD-FMK (a pan-caspase inhibitor blocking apoptosis), and 3-Methyladenine (3-MA, an autophagy-specific inhibitor). We then evaluated cell death by flow cytometry and cell proliferation by MTT assays in NCI-H1299 and NCI-H1975 cells. Notably, Fer-1 significantly abolished the increased cell death induced by TDP-43 knockdown compared with other groups (Fig. 4C and D; Supplementary Fig. 3D and E). In contrast, Nec-1, Z-VAD-FMK, and 3-MA exerted no rescue effects, excluding the involvement of apoptosis, necroptosis, and autophagy. Consistent with flow cytometry results, Fer-1 effectively reversed TDP-43 knockdown-mediated proliferation inhibition, whereas the other three inhibitors showed no significant effects (Fig. 4E and F). In addition, we performed ATP-based luminescence assays to measure intracellular ATP levels. Consistent with the above results, Fer-1 effectively rescued the metabolic decline triggered by TDP-43 knockdown, while the other three inhibitors showed no significant protective effects (Fig. 4G and H).

To further verify the role of TDP-43 in ferroptosis susceptibility, we treated cells with gradient concentrations of ferroptosis inducer erastin and RSL3. MTT assays showed that TDP-43 knockdown significantly decreased cell density in both transiently knockdown NCI-H1299 and stably knockdown NCI-H1975 cells (Fig. 4I–L). Moreover, ATP luminescence assays further confirmed that TDP-43 depletion markedly sensitized LUAD cells to ferroptosis (Fig. 4M–P). Furthermore, Western blot analysis showed that TDP-43 positively regulated the expression of GPX4 and FTH1 in LUAD cells, whereas SLC7A11 and ACSL4 levels were unchanged (Fig. 4Q). Collectively, these results demonstrate that TDP-43 acts as a negative regulator of ferroptosis in LUAD cells.

Next, we examined key ferroptosis indicators, including lipid ROS accumulation, Fe2+ levels, and lipid peroxidation. Confocal immunofluorescence showed that TDP-43 depletion markedly exacerbated erastin-induced ferroptotic stress, with significantly elevated lipid ROS and Fe2+ levels in TDP-43-knockdown NCI-H1975 cells compared with controls (Fig. 5A–D). Consistently, cellular Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, a hallmark of lipid peroxidation, were substantially higher in TDP-43-depleted cells following erastin treatment, and all these effects were effectively abrogated by Fer-1 (Fig. 5E). We then performed flow cytometry to evaluate cell death in TDP-43-knockdown and control NCI-H1975 cells. Upon erastin exposure, TDP-43 knockdown markedly elevated the proportion of total dying cells, indicating compromised cell survival; Moreover, such cell death was reversed by Fer-1 supplementation (Fig. 5F; Supplementary Fig. 4A). MTT assays also confirmed that TDP-43 knockdown significantly reduced cell density under erastin treatment, an effect that was completely reversed by Fer-1 co-treatment (Supplementary Fig. 4B). To further confirm these results, we performed identical assays using RSL3, another canonical ferroptosis inducer. Similarly to erastin treatment, TDP-43 depletion significantly aggravated RSL3-induced Fe2+ accumulation, lipid ROS production, and MDA generation in NCI-H1975 cells, and these effects were all reversed by Fer-1 (Fig. 5G–K). Similarly, RSL3-induced cell death and growth inhibition were intensified upon TDP-43 knockdown, and all these alterations were rescued by Fer-1 (Fig. 5L; Supplementary Fig. 4C and D). Consistent with findings in NCI-H1975 cells, TDP-43 knockdown in NCI-H1299 cells exacerbated erastin- and RSL3-triggered ferroptotic phenotypes, which were fully reversed by Fer-1 (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). In A549 cells, conversely, TDP-43 overexpression markedly attenuated erastin- and RSL3-triggered ferroptotic phenotypes (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). Collectively, these findings across multiple cell lines and ferroptosis inducers confirm that TDP-43 enables ferroptosis evasion in LUAD cells.

TDP-43 augments the expression of PCBP2 by enhancing its mRNA stability

To elucidate the mechanism by which TDP-43 regulates ferroptosis, we identified all 12 ferroptosis-related genes among the top 100 differentially expressed genes from RNA-seq data, and then used qRT-PCR to examine the impact of TDP-43 on these genes. The result showed that, compared with other genes, the expression levels of the iron chaperone proteins PCBP1 and PCBP2 were significantly downregulated after TDP-43 silencing (Fig. 6A). Further data of qRT-PCR and Western blot confirmed the upregulated effect of TDP-43 on the expression of PCBP1 and PCBP2 (Fig. 6B–E). As an RNA-binding protein (RBP), TDP-43 typically modulates the stability, transport, and splicing of target genes at the post-transcriptional level through binding to mRNA [22]. Therefore, we performed RIP assays and found that TDP-43 could interact with PCBP2 mRNA but not with the mRNA of PCBP1 (Fig. 6F and G). Meanwhile, NCI-H1299 and NCI-H1975 cells were treated with transcription inhibitor actinomycin D (ActD) to assess TDP-43 regulation of PCBP1 and PCBP2 mRNA stability. The data indicated that TDP-43 knockdown reduced the mRNA stability of PCBP2 while having no effect on PCBP1 mRNA stability (Fig. 6H and I, Supplementary Fig. 9A and B). Previous studies have shown that TDP-43, as an RBP, preferentially binds to UG-rich repetitive sequences [23,24]. Sequence analysis identified a UG-rich motif within the 3′UTR of PCBP2 mRNA. We therefore cloned the wild-type PCBP2 3′UTR fragment containing this motif into the pGL3-Control vector, and generated the corresponding mutant and deletion constructs as controls (Fig. 6J). Luciferase reporter gene assay showed that the activity of WT was higher than that of MUT and DEL groups (Fig. 6K). Moreover, the overexpression of TDP-43 resulted in a significant increase in the activity of the WT group, while knockdown of TDP-43 led to a decrease in its activity (Fig. 6L and M, Supplementary Fig. 9C). In contrast, neither the MUT group nor the DEL group responded to the alterations in TDP-43 expression (Fig. 6L and M; Supplementary Fig. 9C). To identify the TDP-43 domain responsible for PCBP2 regulation, we performed luciferase reporter assays in HEK293T and NCI-H1299 cells transfected with wild-type TDP-43 or its domain-truncated mutants (Fig. 6N). We found that deletion of the RRM1 domain, a canonical RNA-binding motif, abrogated the TDP-43-mediated upregulation of PCBP2 3′UTR activity (Fig. 6N; Supplementary Fig. 9D). Thus, we conclude that TDP-43 enhances PCBP2 mRNA stability by binding to its UG-rich sequence, thereby upregulating PCBP2 expression.

TDP-43 promotes PCBP1 transcription and expression through interacting with VDR

The above data indicate that TDP-43 fails to modulate PCBP1 mRNA stability post-transcriptionally. We thus hypothesized that TDP-43 may instead control PCBP1 transcription. To verify this speculation, we randomly selected three upstream regions of the PCBP1 transcription initiation site for primer design, and then employed a Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay to detect potential interactions between TDP-43 and these regions (Fig. 7A). Meanwhile, primers targeting an irrelevant downstream region of the PCBP1 gene were designed and used as a negative control (Fig. 7A). The ChIP assay showed that TDP-43 was able to bind to the PCBP1 promoter, with a particularly stable binding observed at Region-A (Fig. 7B and C). Next, we cloned a series of promoter fragments of PCBP1 into the pGL3-Basic vector and detected the changes in their transcriptional activity. Notably, TDP-43 knockdown markedly reduced pGL-P1 and pGL-P2 reporter activity, indicating that TDP-43 promotes PCBP1 transcription (Fig. 7D). Deletion of the −1000 to −700 fragment eliminated TDP-43-mediated activation of the PCBP1 promoter (Fig. 7D). These data suggest that the −1000 to −700 region is critical for TDP-43-dependent PCBP1 transcriptional regulation. Nevertheless, sequence analysis using two online tools, HumanTFDB (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/HumanTFDB/#!/) and PROMO (http://alggen.lsi.upc.es/cgi-bin/promo_v3/promo/promoinit.cgi?dirDB=TF_8.3), revealed no TDP-43 binding sites within this region (Supplementary File 1). We thus hypothesize that TDP-43 cooperates with other transcription factors (TFs) to bind the PCBP1 promoter. We next utilized the two aforementioned tools to identify TFs predicted to interact with this regulatory region (Supplementary File 1). The Venn diagram showed that there were four TFs in common between the two groups (Fig. 7E). Subsequent qRT-PCR assays showed that, compared with other TFs, silencing of Vitamin D Receptor (VDR) significantly inhibited PCBP1 expression (Fig. 7F). VDR is a ligand-inducible transcription factor belonging to the nuclear hormone receptor superfamily [25]. To further verify VDR-mediated regulation of PCBP1, we performed qRT-PCR and Western blot in A549, NCI-H1299, and NCI-H1975 cells, which confirmed that VDR upregulated PCBP1 (Fig. 7G; Supplementary Fig. 10A and B). We then constructed single and double mutations of the two VDR-binding sites within the −1000∼ −700 region (Supplementary Fig. 10C) and performed luciferase reporter assays. VDR overexpression markedly increased the activity of WT and MUT-1, but not MUT-2 or MUT-1/2 (Fig. 7H). Similarly, TDP-43 overexpression enhanced WT and MUT-1 promoter activity, with no effect on MUT-2 or MUT-1/2 (Fig. 7I). These results indicate that TDP-43 promotes PCBP1 transcription through VDR binding to site 2.

Next, Co-IP assays showed that anti-TDP-43 antibody immunoprecipitated VDR, and anti-VDR antibody reciprocally pulled down TDP-43, indicating a physical interaction between the two proteins (Fig. 7J). Confocal immunofluorescence microscopy revealed prominent overlapping signals of TDP-43 and VDR in NCI-H1299 and NCI-H1975 cells, further confirming their colocalization (Fig. 7K, Supplementary Fig. 10D–F). To investigate the details of their interaction, we generated a series of TDP-43 truncation constructs based on its domain architecture (Fig. 7L) and performed Co-IP assays in both HEK293T and NCI-H1299 cells. Co-IP results confirmed that only full-length TDP-43 and the P5 segment specifically interacted with Flag-tagged VDR in both cell lines (Fig. 7M and N; Supplementary Fig. 10G and H). This finding indicated that TDP-43 interacted with VDR through its C-terminal domain. Moreover, we conducted an in vitro molecular docking analysis by Molecular Operating Environment. The binding energies of the N-terminal domain, RRM1, RRM2 and C-terminal domain to VDR are −45.3480 kcal/mol, −50.8878 kcal/mol, −56.4780 kcal/mol, and −77.6085 kcal/mol, respectively. Apparently, the strongest interaction between TDP-43 and VDR occurs in the C-terminal domain of TDP-43 (Fig. 7O). As for VDR, two α-helices (residues 377-407 and residues 306-324) as well as a loop (residues 340-349) are involved in interacting with C-terminal domain of TDP-43 (Fig. 7O). In addition, Western blot analysis showed that both VDR knockdown and treatment with the VDR inhibitor MeTC7 significantly abrogated TDP-43-mediated upregulation of PCBP1 (Fig. 7P; Supplementary Fig. 10I and J). Taken together, TDP-43 interacts with VDR through its C-terminal domain to activate PCBP1 transcription.

PCBP1 and PCBP2 positively correlate with TDP-43 expression and link to adverse patient prognosis

Subsequently, the online databases GEPIA and Kaplan-Meier Plotter (https://www.kmplot.com/analysis/) were utilized to assess the association between PCBP1/PCBP2 expression and LUAD patient prognosis. These data uncovered that the expression of PCBP1 and PCBP2 was closely associated with the poor prognosis of LUAD patient. Patients with higher expression of PCBP1 and PCBP2 tended to have shorter survival periods (Fig. 8A–D). Moreover, there was a positive correlation between the expression of both PCBP1 and PCBP2 with TDP-43 (Fig. 8E and F). To further evaluate the correlation between TDP-43 and PCBP1/PCBP2 in LUAD tissues, a multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) assay was performed using a LUAD tissue microarray. This microarray contains paired cancerous and adjacent non-cancerous tissues from 63 patients with LUAD. The mIF assay showed coordinated expression of TDP-43, PCBP1 and PCBP2: all three proteins were markedly upregulated in LUAD tissues versus paired adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 8G). Quantitative scoring verified significantly higher positive cell rates of the three proteins in tumor lesions (Fig. 8H–J). To further explore the potential association between them, we stratified the LUAD samples into TDP-43 high-expression and low-expression groups based on the median expression level of TDP-43. Comparative analysis showed that PCBP1 expression was significantly upregulated in the TDP-43 high-expression subgroup compared to the TDP-43 low-expression subgroup (Fig. 8K). A similar correlation pattern was observed for PCBP2 (Fig. 8L). These findings indicate that TDP-43 expression is positively correlated with both PCBP1 and PCBP2 in LUAD tissues.

TDP-43 suppresses ferroptosis in a PCBP1/PCBP2-dependent manner

Given that PCBP1 and PCBP2 function as key iron chaperones, we first validated their regulatory roles in ferroptosis of LUAD cells. Knockdown of either PCBP1 or PCBP2 using two independent siRNAs significantly increased Fe2+ accumulation and reduced cell density, effects that were markedly reversed by the iron chelator DFO (Supplementary Fig. 11). These results indicated that both PCBP1 and PCBP2 negatively regulated ferroptosis by maintaining intracellular iron homeostasis. To investigate whether TDP-43 regulates ferroptosis through PCBP1 and PCBP2, we performed Western blot and found that knockdown of either PCBP1 or PCBP2 abrogated the TDP-43-mediated regulation of key iron metabolism proteins FTH1 and TfR1 (Fig. 9A; Supplementary Fig. 12A). In TDP-43-overexpressing cells, MDA levels were significantly decreased compared with controls, indicating that TDP-43 suppresses lipid peroxidation. However, knockdown of either PCBP1 or PCBP2 markedly reversed this TDP-43-mediated inhibition of MDA production (Fig. 9B; Supplementary Fig. 12B). Next, we assessed lipid ROS levels in A549, NCI-H1299, and NCI-H1975 cells. TDP-43 overexpression markedly reduced lipid ROS levels, whereas these effects were significantly attenuated by knockdown of either PCBP1 or PCBP2 (Fig. 9C and D; Supplementary Fig. 12C and D). Immunofluorescence assays further showed that PCBP1 depletion abrogated the TDP-43-induced reduction in Fe2+ levels, leading to marked Fe2+ accumulation (Fig. 9E and F; Supplementary Fig. 12E and F). Consistently, PCBP2 depletion similarly reversed the decrease in Fe2+ levels caused by TDP-43 overexpression (Fig. 9E and F; Supplementary Fig. 12E and F). These findings support that TDP-43 suppresses ferroptosis in a PCBP1/PCBP2-dependent manner.

MTT assays indicated that knockdown of either PCBP1 or PCBP2 partially blunted the increase in cell density induced by TDP-43 overexpression (Fig. 10A; Supplementary Fig. 13A). In line with this finding, the TDP-43-mediated elevation in EdU-positive cells was significantly abrogated by depletion of either PCBP1 or PCBP2 (Fig. 10B and C; Supplementary Fig. 13B and C). Likewise, comparable results were obtained in colony formation assays (Fig. 10D, Supplementary Fig. 13D and E).

To determine whether TDP-43 drives tumor growth in a PCBP1- and PCBP2-dependent manner, we generated stable A549 cell lines: control, TDP-43 overexpression (OE-TDP-43), and TDP-43 overexpression combined with PCBP1 or PCBP2 knockdown (OE-TDP-43+KD-PCBP1/2). These cells were used for subcutaneous xenograft assays in nude mice. Representative tumor images clearly showed distinct differences in tumor size across groups (Fig. 10E). Quantitative analyses confirmed that TDP-43 overexpression markedly accelerated tumor growth, with significant increases in tumor volume and weight (Fig. 10F and G). In contrast, simultaneous knockdown of either PCBP1 or PCBP2 significantly blunted the tumor-promoting effect of TDP-43, resulting in smaller tumor volume and lower weight (Fig. 10F and G). No significant differences in body weights were observed among groups during the experiment, suggesting that tumor growth changes were not caused by general toxicity or health status (Supplementary Fig. 13F). Furthermore, intratumoral Fe2+ and MDA levels were measured. TDP-43 overexpression reduced intratumoral Fe2+ levels, and this effect was partially reversed by PCBP1 knockdown and fully abolished by PCBP2 knockdown (Fig. 10H). Similarly, MDA levels were significantly decreased by TDP-43 overexpression, but restored by depletion of either PCBP1 or PCBP2 (Fig. 10H). To further validate iron metabolism and ferroptotic changes in vivo, we performed IHC staining for FTH1 and TfR1 in tumor tissues (Fig. 10I). TDP-43 overexpression upregulated FTH1 and downregulated TfR1, and these effects were significantly reversed by PCBP1 or PCBP2 knockdown (Fig. 10I; Supplementary Fig. 13G). IHC also revealed that the increased Ki67 staining intensity induced by TDP-43 overexpression was abolished by depletion of either PCBP1 or PCBP2 (Fig. 10I). Collectively, TDP-43 promotes LUAD cell proliferation and tumor growth in vivo through PCBP1 and PCBP2.

Discussion

As the most common subtype of non-small cell lung cancer, LUAD frequently accumulates oncogenic driver mutations, such as EGFR and KRAS mutations, which contribute to its aggressive progression and resistance to conventional therapies [26]. Given the unmet need to overcome such limitations, discovering new molecular targets and innovative therapies remains a critical priority in LUAD research. Ferroptosis, a distinct form of regulated cell death first characterized in 2012, is defined by unique molecular and morphological hallmarks [27]. Fundamentally, it is driven by iron-dependent accumulation of lipid peroxides that ultimately lead to membrane disruption and cellular demise [28]. Numerous lines of evidence have made it clear that the induction of ferroptosis directly impairs cancer cells’ ability to proliferate, metastasize, and survive, while also weakening their resistance to therapy [[29], [30], [31]]. Thus, ferroptosis has gained broad recognition as a promising new approach for antitumor therapy, while lipid peroxidation may function as a “recruitment signal” that not only aids in antigen presentation but also strengthens the effectiveness of tumor immunotherapy. In recent studies, resistance to ferroptosis has emerged as an additional non-mutational mechanism associated with the onset and progression of cancers, including LUAD [[32], [33], [34]]. However, the specific molecular regulators that drive ferroptosis resistance in LUAD remain to be further explored.

While studies of neurodegenerative diseases have defined TDP-43's key functions in neurological pathologies, the present work reveals for the first time that TDP-43 drives LUAD growth by repressing ferroptosis. The abundant expression of TDP-43 in LUAD tissues and its correlation with poor patient prognosis have been identified. Building on this clue, we further identified that TDP-43 facilitated the proliferation of LUAD cells while suppressing their death. Consistent with our in vitro findings, subcutaneous and orthotopic xenograft models further confirmed that TDP-43 accelerated LUAD tumor growth in vivo, underscoring its critical pro-tumor function. It should be noted that cell line xenografts are ideal for observing local lung tumor expansion and intralesional infiltration, yet cannot fully recapitulate the spectrum of clinical LUAD features including de novo tumor formation and metastasis. Additional studies based on more physiologically faithful models may help characterize whether TDP-43 regulates tumor initiation and metastatic progression in LUAD.

To dissect the molecular mechanisms underlying TDP-43's pro-growth effect, we performed transcriptome profiling. RNA-seq data indicated a potential association between TDP-43 and ferroptosis, and further experiments assessing ferroptosis-related indicators validated TDP-43's inhibitory role in ferroptosis. Previous studies on ferroptosis have mainly focused on the cystine-glutamate transporter (xCT) system centered around SLC7A11 and the antioxidant system for lipid peroxide clearance centered around GPX4 [35]. For instance, Wang et al. found that overexpression of FMRP induced ferroptosis resistance and accelerated breast cancer progression by upregulating SLC7A11 expression [36]. Yin's team identified that ZDHHC20 inhibited ferroptosis by palmitoylating GPX4 at cysteine 66, a reversible modification that enhances GPX4 protein stability [37]. However, in our study, we found that TDP-43 suppressed ferroptosis by regulating intracellular Fe2+ trafficking. PCBP1 and PCBP2, members of the iron chaperone protein family, play critical roles in maintaining intracellular iron homeostasis during ferroptosis [38]. By binding to labile Fe2+ and regulating their trafficking, these proteins limit the accumulation of free Fe2+, a core driver of lipid peroxidation, and thereby mitigate ferroptotic cell death [39]. Our data indictaed that TDP-43 suppressed the accumulation of Fe2+ and then reduced the production of lipid ROS and MDA by upregulating PCBP1 and PCBP2. Notably, recent research by Liu's team has also revealed the potential of PCBP1 and PCBP2 as targets for modulating ferroptosis in colorectal cancer [18]. They demonstrate that acevaltrate (ACE), a natural product, elevates intracellular Fe2+ levels in colorectal cancer cells through directly targeting PCBP1 and PCBP2 to downregulate their expression [18]. Guided by this rationale, we explored whether ACE could modulate TDP-43-mediated biological effects in LUAD cells. Our preliminary data showed that ACE reversed TDP-43-mediated proliferative phenotypes in NCI-H1299 and A549 cells (Supplementary Fig. 14). Although the detailed pharmacological mechanism and in vivo efficacy of ACE in LUAD remain to be further elucidated, these combined findings support that the TDP-43/PCBP1/2 axis represents a promising and targetable pathway with potential therapeutic implications for LUAD.

Interestingly, a recent study by Jiang et al. has also documented a robust interaction between TDP-43 and PCBP1/2. They demonstrated that PCBP1/2 and TDP-43 function as adaptors for NAT10, forming a complex that catalyzes N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) modification of mRNAs in mammalian cells. While our study involves the same core proteins as this prior work, it focuses on distinctly different biological contexts and molecular mechanisms. Jiang et al.’s study was conducted in HEK293T cells and mouse testicular tissues, focusing on basic physiological processes such as spermatogenesis. In this context, PCBP1 and PCBP2 primarily function as RNA-binding proteins that cooperate with TDP-43 to assist NAT10 in binding to substrate RNAs. In contrast, our research focuses on the growth of LUAD, with a core focus on the critical role of TDP-43 and PCBP1/2 in ferroptosis regulation. Mechanistically, Jiang et al.’s work elucidated a protein-protein interaction between TDP-43 and PCBP1/2 in RNA epitranscriptional modification, whereas we are the first to reveal that TDP-43 can act as an upstream regulator to suppress tumor ferroptosis by directly upregulating the expression of PCBP1/2. Our finding expands the function of the TDP-43/PCBP1/2 axis from RNA modification at the basic physiological level to ferroptosis regulation in the cancer context, highlighting the functional diversity and context dependence of this protein axis. Furthermore, the findings of both studies provide insights for future exploration: whether TDP-43 and PCBP1/2 can also affect LUAD growth through the “protein interaction-mediated RNA modification” mode remains unreported and warrants further multi-dimensional investigation.

Notably, TDP-43 regulated the expression of PCBP1 and PCBP2 in LUAD through distinct mechanisms. In the regulation of PCBP2, TDP-43 exerted its classical role as an RBP by binding to the 3′UTR of PCBP2 mRNA, thereby elevating PCBP2 mRNA stability and boosting its expression post-transcriptionally. However, the ability of TDP-43 to govern transcriptional activation was revealed in the process of upregulating PCBP1. Notably, studies exploring TDP-43-mediated tumor growth via transcriptional activation pathway remain limited. For instance, in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, TDP-43 has been shown to bind to the promoter region of the oncogene TP63, directly promoting its transcriptional activation; it further forms a positive feedback loop with TP63 to drive tumor growth [9]. Herein, we report a previously unrecognized regulatory axis whereby TDP-43 interacts with VDR to bind the PCBP1 promoter and trigger PCBP1 transcription. This finding expanded the landscape of TDP-43-mediated transcriptional regulation in cancers. VDR is a ligand-dependent TF that mediates vitamin D's biological effects by regulating the transcription of target genes involved in various physiological processes, including cell growth and immune regulation [40]. The roles of VDR in cancers are context-dependent: while it is widely recognized as a tumor suppressor in most malignancies, growing evidence also highlights its potential pro-carcinogenic role in specific contexts. For instance, in pancreatic cancer, VDR promoted in vivo tumor progression by activating the M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages mediated by CCL20 [41]. Additionally, in colorectal cancer, HuR accelerated tumor growth via enhancing VDR expression, which maintained lipid homeostasis to support tumor growth [42]. A study showed that VDR expression was significantly upregulated in LUAD samples compared to normal tissues [43]. Our data suggest that VDR is likely to play a pro-growth role in LUAD. This notion is further supported by our exploratory pharmacological results, in which inhibition of VDR with MeTC7 attenuated the pro-proliferative effect driven by TDP-43 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 15). This provides preliminary proof-of-concept that targeting the TDP-43/VDR signaling axis may hold promise for intervening in TDP-43-related malignant phenotypes in LUAD. Nevertheless, systematic dissection of VDR's biological functions and further evaluation of VDR activators and related pharmacological interventions will be required to clarify its cancer-promoting role in LUAD.

One important limitation of this study warrants consideration. We observed pronounced tumor-suppressive effects upon TDP-43 silencing in subcutaneous and orthotopic xenograft models, and we validated the TDP-43/PCBP1/2 axis governing ferroptosis in vivo using a TDP-43 overexpression rescue model, in which intratumoral Fe2+, MDA levels and iron metabolism-associated proteins were examined. However, ferroptotic biomarkers were not quantified in TDP-43 knockdown tumors, precluding a direct link between ferroptosis and the observed growth inhibition. As such, it remains possible that the anti-tumor effects triggered by TDP-43 loss extend beyond its effects on ferroptosis. Future studies incorporating in vivo detection of ferroptotic signatures and rescue experiments with ferroptosis modulators will help delineate the specific contribution of ferroptosis to this phenotype.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that TDP-43 is overexpressed in LUAD patient tissues, and its expression correlates negatively with overall survival. TDP-43 promotes proliferation and inhibits death in LUAD cells. Mechanistically, TDP-43 exerts its effects through two distinct pathways: it interacts with the transcription factor VDR via its C-terminal domain to drive PCBP1 transcription, while also directly binding to the UG-rich sequence in the 3′UTR of PCBP2 mRNA to enhance the stability of this transcript (Fig. 11). By upregulating both PCBP1 and PCBP2, TDP-43 promotes the circulation, redistribution, and storage of Fe2+ in LUAD cells. This process inhibits the excessive accumulation of free Fe2+, thereby decreasing the probability of Fenton reaction, reducing lipid peroxidation, preventing ferroptosis induction, and ultimately promoting LUAD growth (Fig. 11). Insights gleaned from this dual regulatory mechanism are expected to provide new directions for advancing the clinical management of LUAD.
