Assessment of displaced Sudanese school-age children’s mental health at Ad-Damar, River Nile, Sudan, 2024: a descriptive cross-sectional study
Authors: Mustafa Sabir Abakar Awad, Mead Modathar Awad Abd Alrahim, Rasheeda Abdulbagi Merghani Awadelkareem, Muhammadelmujtaba Abdulnabi Khalafallah Elhaj, Mustafa Mohamedelhassan Abdelrahman Ibrahim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe WHO defines mental health as well-being where individuals manage stress, work productively, and contribute to their community. Children’s mental health is critical, especially among vulnerable groups like refugees. Research shows refugee children, including Sudanese, face PTSD, depression, and anxiety due to conflict and displacement, yet specific studies on Sudanese children are scarce. East Africa faces high prevalence due to poverty, conflict, and inadequate healthcare. Sudan’s situation is exacerbated by ongoing conflict and limited resources, despite efforts from NGOs and international organizations.MethodologyThis cross-sectional descriptive study examines the mental health of Sudanese school-age children (6–18 years) in Ad-Damar, River Nile, Sudan, using a simple random sampling technique. The study included 246 participants. Data were collected using an author-designed questionnaire based on the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ), supplemented with demographic information. Data management involved secure handling and ethical considerations, including informed consent and confidentiality. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS V.25 to ensure accuracy and reliability of the findings.ResultsThe sample included children aged 6–18 years, with 54% males and 46% females. Displacement duration varied, with 34% displaced for 1–2 years. Socio-demographic findings indicated 39% lived with both parents, 56% were in urban environments, and 47% were in primary school. Mental health assessments revealed 68% frequently felt sad, 73% felt anxious, and 29% had diagnosed conditions, including PTSD and depression. Correlations showed significant positive relationships between overall mood ratings and both school support (r = 0.45, p < 0.01) and family support (r = 0.35, p < 0.05). Chi-Square tests indicated significant associations between gender and mental health diagnoses (χ²=5.67, p = 0.017) and family structure and perceived support (χ²=12.34, p < 0.05).ConclusionThe study highlights the urgent need for better mental health support for displaced children, given the high prevalence of mental health issues and inadequate support from schools and families. Future research should include longitudinal studies to evaluate long-term effects and intervention effectiveness. Key steps include improving access to mental health services, fostering supportive educational environments, and enhancing family support systems.Keywords: Mental health, Displacement, School-age children, Stress

Background

Mental health, as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), refers “a state of well-being in which the individual realizes his or her own abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively and fruitfully, and is able to make a contribution to his or her community” [1].

This definition, although it signifies significant advancement in departing from the idea of mental health solely as the absence of mental illness, raises multiple concerns and is susceptible to potential misunderstandings when it highlights positive emotions and positive functioning as crucial elements of mental health [1].

Children’s mental health plays a crucial role in their general well-being and development, impacting their academic achievements, social relationships, and long-term health results. There has been a growing acknowledgment in recent years of the significance of evaluating and dealing with mental health concerns among school-aged children, especially those from vulnerable communities like refugees and migrants [2].

Research conducted in the past has indicated that refugee children face a heightened susceptibility to various mental health problems, such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and anxiety disorders [2]. The distinct challenges experienced by Sudanese children, like being exposed to conflict, losing family members, and being displaced, intensify these risks [3]. In addition, the process of adapting to a new scholastic and social setting in Sudan presents more difficulties that can have an influence on their mental well-being [4].

Sudanese school-age children face substantial mental health risks due to ongoing conflict and instability. Many children experience displacement, loss of family members, exposure to violence, and lack of access to education and basic needs, creating an environment ripe for trauma and mental health issues. The constant exposure to war-related stressors, such as bombings and forced migrations, has led to widespread emotional distress, with many children showing signs of anxiety, fear, and even symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder [5]. These experiences can have lifelong mental health implications, especially without access to proper support and resources like counseling and safe spaces. Although the risks are well-known, there is a lack of research specifically examining the mental health of Sudanese school-age children in Sudan.

On a global scale, mental health is a pressing matter that impacts millions of individuals, and the efforts to address it are frequently insufficient and inadequate. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), over one billion individuals globally are affected by a mental disorder, and mental health issues are the primary cause of disability, leading to substantial rates of death and illness. The COVID-19 pandemic has worsened these problems, resulting in a 25% surge in anxiety and sadness within the initial year of the pandemic [6].

In East Africa, the situation is notably arduous. Factors like as poverty, conflict, and insufficient healthcare infrastructure are responsible for the elevated prevalence of mental health disorders. The area encounters substantial obstacles to mental health care, including as social disapproval, restricted availability of services, and a scarcity of mental health practitioners. Measures are now being taken to tackle these concerns, but, the pace of success varies and is not consistent across different countries [7].

The mental health difficulties in Sudan are worsened by the ongoing conflict and humanitarian disasters. The mental health system in Sudan is inadequately developed, with little resources and services accessible for individuals requiring assistance. The social disapproval and negative perception associated with mental health problems serve as additional barriers that deter persons from seeking assistance. International organizations and local NGOs are collaborating to enhance mental health awareness and services, however, the demand for these services is still extensive and pressing [8].

The psychological well-being of children attending school is a vital component of their holistic growth and welfare. However, it is sometimes disregarded, especially among refugee communities. Sudanese children who have been forced to leave their homes because of conflict and instability in their nation confront a wide range of difficulties that can have a substantial effect on their mental well-being. The challenges encompass exposure to violence, detachment from family members, and the strain of acclimating to unfamiliar surroundings. Ad-Damar area, the capital of River Nile state, functions as an educational sanctuary for numerous displaced children. Nevertheless, there is an insufficiency of thorough data regarding the mental well-being of young children, a crucial factor in delivering suitable assistance and interventions [9].

The prolonged conflict and political instability in Sudan have resulted in substantial population displacement, with children being particularly susceptible. School-age children who have been displaced in Sudan encounter several obstacles, such as the loss of their homes, interruption of their education, and exposure to distressing events. These encounters can significantly and enduringly affect their mental well-being [8].

Although it is widely acknowledged that addressing mental health in humanitarian circumstances is crucial, there is a significant lack of expertise regarding the unique mental health requirements of Sudanese children who have been displaced. The scarcity of mental health care, along with cultural taboos and insufficient funding, worsens the situation. Previous research suggests that there are significant rates of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), sadness, and anxiety among children who have been relocated. However, there is a lack of adequate data regarding their mental health condition and their ability to receive healthcare services in Sudan [6, 10].

Sudan has faced prolonged conflict and political instability, resulting in the displacement of millions of people, including a significant number of school-age children. These children, having experienced trauma, loss, and disruption, represent one of the most vulnerable groups in society. The adverse conditions they face can severely impact their mental health, influencing their development and future prospects. These displaced children often endure traumatic experiences, including exposure to violence, loss of family and community, and unstable living conditions. These experiences can lead to a range of mental health issues such as anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and behavioral problems. Assessing their mental health is crucial to understanding the extent and nature of these issues [11].

Mental health is closely linked to educational outcomes. Children struggling with mental health issues may have difficulty concentrating, learning, and engaging in school activities, which can hinder their educational attainment and future opportunities. By evaluating their mental health, this research aims to identify barriers to their academic success and provide insights into how to support their educational development.

Research objective

To assess the mental health status of Sudanese school-age children in Ad-Damar, River Nile, Sudan.

Research methods

Study design/setting

This is a cross-sectional descriptive study design, focused on Sudanese school-age children (aged 6–18 years) attending both private and governmental (public) schools and residing in Ad-Damar locality, River Nile. River Nile state is one of the northern states in Sudan, one of few states that are currently armed conflict free. It constitutes of a large population from different ethnic background, the four major cities are Ad-Damar, Atbara, Shendi and Berber. Ad-Damar being the capital of the states it harbors the most of the governmental services.

Study population

The study population constitutes of the school-age children with their parent/guarding being the main source of information.

Sampling technique and sample size

A simple random sampling method was used to ensure representation across different age groups, genders and schools. This approach enhanced the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of displaced and non- displaced Sudanese children.

Data collection methods and tools

For assessing the mental health of school-age children in this study, an author designed questionnaire based of Strength and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) was developed to encompass the objectives of the study, which is endorsed by the World Health Organization (WHO). The SDQ is a widely recognized tool for children aged 6–18 years, it is a 25-item screening tool assessing emotional and behavioral challenges in children and adolescents across five areas: emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity, peer relationship issues, and prosocial behavior. It helps identify mental health concerns and is completed by parents, teachers, or the child. It is available in versions for parents, teachers, and self-reports, and in multiple languages, including Arabic. Demographic information such as age, gender, duration of displacement, and family background was also collected. The data were collected from the child’s parent/guarding for little children [6–13], while older ones [14–18] the data were collected from them directly at the presence of their parent/guardian. Data collection was employed using the author designed questionnaires following rigorous informed consent procedures from parents/guardians and assent from children.

Data management and analysis

Effective data handling and management are critical for the study on assessing the mental health of Sudanese school-age children in Ad-Damar, River Nile. Data processing involved meticulous cleaning for completeness and accuracy using Microsoft Excel 2016 before statistical analysis using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) V.25. Different statistical methods were applied to the collected data including quantitative analysis: descriptive statistics (mean, median, frequencies, percentages) that summarized the participants demographics. Comparative analysis (e.g., chi-square tests, Pearson’s correlation) were applied to examine differences in mental health status based on different factors (demographics, family and environmental). The open-ended questions were analyzed based on the content of the answers, as the responses were systematically coded and a theme or concept for each group of responses was generated.

Ethical consideration

Ethical clearance was obtained from the local ministry of health, research unit. Also, an informed consent from the parents or guardians of the children, alongside seeking assent directly from the children. Confidentiality and anonymity of all participants was strictly maintained throughout the research process Participants will be informed about the research aims and objectives in clear and accessible language. They were assured of their right to give voluntary informed consent, emphasizing their freedom to withdraw from the study at any point without consequence.

To ensure confidentiality, anonymized or coded identifiers has replaced personal data, with secure storage in encrypted electronic databases or locked physical cabinets.

Results

The study surveyed 246 displaced Sudanese school-age children in Ad-Damar, River Nile, Sudan. The age distribution was as follows: 19% were between 6 and 8 years old, 29% were 9–11 years old, 33% were 12–14 years old, and 19% were 15–17 years old. Regarding gender, 54% of the participants were male, and 46% were female. In terms of the duration of displacement, 12% of the children had been displaced for less than 6 months, 24% for 6 months to 1 year, 34% for 1–2 years, 22% for more than 2 years, and 8% were not displaced. In the socio-demographic section, 39% of the children lived with both parents, 32% with a single parent, 20% with a guardian, and 9% had other living arrangements. Educationally, 47% were in primary school, 34% in middle school, and 19% in high school. The living environment was predominantly urban (56%) compared to rural (44%). As shown in Table 1.

Discussion

This study assessed the mental health status of 246 displaced Sudanese school-age children residing in Ad-Damar, River Nile, Sudan. The study offers valuable insights into the complex interplay between displacement, mental health, family structure, and environmental support. The study also explored the impact of various socio-demographic factors, including family structure and support, on mental health outcomes.

The age distribution of the sample indicates a fairly typical spread for displaced children, with a concentration in the 12–14 year age group (33%), aligning with their middle school status. This demographic distribution is consistent with other studies that highlight a similar age range in displaced populations due to the critical developmental and educational milestones reached during these years [12]. The gender distribution, slightly skewed towards males (54%), is comparable to findings from other displacement studies where male children are often more prevalent due to differential mortality and access issues [13].

The study found that 34% of the children had been displaced for 1–2 years, and 22% for more than 2 years. Extended displacement has been linked to increased psychological distress, corroborating findings by Betancourt et al. (2012) [3], who reported that prolonged displacement exacerbates mental health issues due to the prolonged exposure to stress and instability.

The socio-demographic data also revealed that 39% of children lived with both parents, while 32% lived with a single parent, and 20% with a guardian. This variability in living arrangements reflects the diverse family structures encountered in displaced populations and has implications for support systems. Previous research indicates that children living in single-parent or guardian arrangements often face greater psychosocial challenges [14].

The mental health status reported in this study is alarming. 68% of children reported feeling sad or hopeless frequently, and 73% felt anxious or worried. These figures align with literature on displaced children, where high levels of emotional distress and PTSD are prevalent [15]. The high rates of trauma exposure—59% experiencing conflict/war, 42% losing a loved one, and 31% enduring abuse—underscore the severe impacts of displacement on mental health. These findings are consistent with studies that link trauma exposure to increased risk of psychological disorders [2].

The reported prevalence of mental health conditions (29%) and the specific diagnoses (38% PTSD, 36% depression) are consistent with global trends observed in similar populations [16]. The significant level of trauma symptoms, including flashbacks and intrusive thoughts, further highlights the urgent need for targeted mental health interventions.

The prevalence of mental health diagnoses among the children is concerning, with 29% reporting a diagnosis. PTSD (38%) and depression (36%) are the most common diagnoses, aligning with research on the psychological impact of conflict and displacement [17]. The limited access and utilization of mental health services (22% access, 9% recent utilization) exacerbate the situation, reflecting systemic barriers to mental health care that are well-documented in conflict-affected regions [18].

The survey’s findings on school and family support provide critical insights into the environmental factors influencing mental health. The positive correlation between school environment supportiveness and overall mood highlights the significant role that a supportive educational environment plays in enhancing children’s mental well-being [19]. Conversely, only 29% of children perceived their school environment as supportive, suggesting a substantial gap in the provision of supportive educational settings.

Family support also plays a crucial role, with a positive correlation between perceived family support and overall mood. This finding supports existing literature emphasizing the importance of familial support in mitigating the effects of trauma [20]. The variability in perceived family support, influenced by family structure, further underscores the need for tailored interventions based on family dynamics.

The study identified that 17% of children found their school environment as not supportive at all, and only 10% felt it was very supportive. This reflects a critical gap in the support provided by educational institutions. Previous research highlights the essential role of a supportive school environment in mitigating the effects of trauma and promoting psychological well-being [21].

Similarly, the family support ratings indicate a substantial number of children perceive their family environment as unsupportive, with only 16% reporting very supportive family relationships. This finding is corroborated by studies indicating that family support is crucial for the mental health of displaced children [22].

The Chi-Square test results reveal significant associations between gender and mental health diagnoses and between family structure and perceived family support. The gender differences in mental health diagnoses might reflect varying experiences and coping mechanisms between boys and girls [23]. The variability in family support across different family structures suggests that children from non-traditional family setups may experience different levels of emotional and practical support, affecting their mental health outcomes [24].

The positive correlation between overall mood rating and perceived support from school and family suggests that better support from these environments is associated with improved mood ratings. These results are consistent with research showing that supportive relationships are protective factors against mental health issues [25].

The significant association between family structure and perceived family support underscores the impact of family dynamics on perceived support. Children from different family structures experience varying levels of support, which can influence their mental health outcomes, as noted in research by Jones et al. (2013) [26].

The exposure to various forms of trauma (conflict/war, loss of a loved one, displacement, and abuse) is alarmingly high, with 59% reporting conflict or war exposure and 31% experiencing abuse. These factors are well-documented as contributors to severe mental health conditions as reported by Khamis et al.. Despite the high need, only 22% of children have access to mental health services, and a mere 9% have utilized these services recently. This gap in service access and utilization reflects systemic issues in mental health care availability and accessibility in conflict-affected regions [27].

This study highlights the profound impact of displacement and trauma on the mental health of school-age children in Ad-Damar, River Nile, Sudan. The findings emphasize the need for improved mental health services, enhanced support within schools and families, and a more comprehensive approach to addressing the multifaceted challenges faced by displaced children.

Limitations

The study was limited by many factors, first of all being the relatively small sample size which could restrict the generalizability of the results to the whole Sudanese population. Another limitation was the possibility of a Hawthorne effect in the answers parents/guardians provided for their children to some of the questions such as the perceived support or love from family members, which could be a confounding variable.

Conclusion

The findings of this study underscore the critical need for enhanced mental health support systems for displaced children. The high prevalence of mental health issues, coupled with the lack of adequate support from schools and families, highlights the need for comprehensive interventions. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to assess the long-term effects of displacement and the effectiveness of targeted mental health programs. Improving access to mental health services, fostering supportive educational environments, and enhancing family support systems are essential steps toward addressing the mental health needs of displaced children.
