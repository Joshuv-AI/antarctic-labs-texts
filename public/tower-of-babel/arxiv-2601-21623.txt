LAMP: Look-Ahead Mixed-Precision Inference of Large Language Models
Stanislav Budzinskiy, Marian Gloser, Tolunay Yilmaz, Ying Hong Tham, Yuanyi Lin, Wenyi Fang, Fan Wu, Philipp Petersen

Abstract
Mixed-precision computations are a hallmark of the current stage of AI, driving the progress in large language models towards efficient, locally deployable solutions. This article addresses the floating-point computation of compositionally-rich functions, concentrating on transformer inference. Based on the rounding error analysis of a composition f ⁡ ( g ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) ) f(g(\bm{\mathrm{x}})) , we provide an adaptive strategy that selects a small subset of components of g ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) g(\bm{\mathrm{x}}) to be computed more accurately while all other computations can be carried out with lower accuracy. We then explain how this strategy can be applied to different compositions within a transformer and illustrate its overall effect on transformer inference. We study the effectiveness of this algorithm numerically on GPT-2 models and demonstrate that already very low recomputation rates allow for improvements of up to two orders of magnitude in accuracy.

Introduction

Transformer deep neural networks (DNNs), originally introduced for sequence modeling in natural language processing
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
, have become a standard computational paradigm across a wide range of domains, including large-scale language understanding
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
as well as vision and multimodal learning
(
Dosovitskiy 2021
)
.
From a computational perspective, a transformer can be viewed as a deep composition of simple operators, obtained by iterating attention-based mappings and pointwise nonlinear transformations across layers.

In practice, the evaluation of such deep compositions (
inference
) is performed in floating-point (FP) arithmetic, low-precision formats being routinely employed to improve performance and energy efficiency
(
Gupta et al. 2015
;
Micikevicius et al. 2018
;
Kalamkar et al. 2019
)
.
From a numerical analysis standpoint, FP evaluation introduces rounding errors at every stage of the computation; the cumulative effect of these local errors hinges on how they propagate through successive compositions of operators
(
Higham 2002
)
.

The bulk of operators in transformers are matrix products, and the existing approaches to mixed-precision inference largely address them based on two key principles: the input is quantized to low precision, and the output is accumulated in high precision
(
Xiao et al. 2023
)
.
Typically, the quantization precision is uniform across the whole input, and the accumulation precision is uniform across the whole output.
While there are also mixed-precision quantization strategies
(
Dettmers et al. 2022
)
, we are not aware of developments in mixed-precision accumulation for transformer inference.

In this work, we address this gap by proposing a mathematically principled mixed-precision inference strategy that is explicitly aware of compositional effects.
Instead of treating the output of an intermediate computation uniformly, we follow a
look-ahead strategy
by flagging and recomputing, using higher-precision accumulation, those computations whose round-off errors will be most strongly amplified by the ensuing operator.
Our method has a rigorous theoretical foundation and delivers strong empirical results in numerical experiments.
We describe our contribution in detail in Subsection
1.2
.

1.1
Rounding error analysis

To place our contribution in context, we briefly review how rounding error analysis has been applied to function evaluation, and why DNNs fall outside this classical setting.
Rounding errors have been analyzed primarily for matrix computations
(
Higham 2002
;
Connolly et al. 2021
)
and “basic” nonlinear functions such as the
elementary functions
:
n
th powers and roots, exponentials and logarithms, trigonometric and hyperbolic functions
(
Muller 2016
)
.

Special functions

(
Gil et al. 2007
)
are “basic” in mathematical physics, defined as solutions to specific differential equations or integrals.
The complexity of their evaluation stems from the need to discretize the differential equation or integral to sufficient (typically very high) precision, after which rounding effects of FP arithmetic become noticeable
(
Lauter & Mezzarobba 2015
)
.

DNNs, including transformers, can be considered ‘‘basic’’ in the field of AI, and they differ from the aforementioned ‘‘basic’’ functions in two aspects: the target accuracy
1
1

1

How many unit round-offs of error are tolerable, rather than the value of the unit round-off.
and the source of evaluation complexity.
First, the applications of DNNs do not seem to require the accuracy to be very high.
Second, the evaluation complexity of DNNs is due to their extremely rich
compositional structure
, where the “building blocks” are either elementary functions or simple compositions of elementary functions
(
Blanchard et al. 2021
;
El Arar et al. 2024
)
.

Therefore,
the algorithms of DNN inference need to pay specific attention to compositions
.
Rounding error analysis applied to DNNs leads to worst-case bounds that grow exponentially with depth
(
El Arar et al. 2025
;
Budzinskiy et al. 2025
)
.
While the
global
rounding error appears to be difficult to tame, the
local
rounding error at each composition is easier to control—the core idea of our work.

1.3
Impact and limitations

We demonstrate that numerical rounding effects arising from the compositional structure of DNNs can be mitigated at inference through a theoretically-grounded compositionality-aware mixed-precision strategy.
Our empirical results hint at the possibility of accumulating key-query inner products in low-bitwidth formats on GPUs, packing multiple inner products in a single 32-bit register, and recovering the prediction accuracy via guided sparse recomputations.
This workflow could increase the computational throughput of self-attention blocks at the cost of overhead associated with selecting the recomputations.
We believe that these improvements are particularly relevant for latency-sensitive inference, where low-precision FP arithmetic is already widely employed, and even moderate gains in efficiency can have a meaningful practical impact.
In addition to empirical accuracy improvements, our results highlight the importance of compositional numerical effects in deep models.

FlashAttention

(
Dao et al. 2022
)
, together with its variations, is the industry standard for computing attention.
Its main property is that the key-query matrix product and the softmax output probabilities are never materialized in full.
For our approach, it is necessary to store and sort the vector of softmax probabilities, or at least to maintain a list of top-
k
highest probabilities.
Therefore, our method is likely more suitable for applications with shorter context lengths.

Quantization
is
not
addressed in our work.
As such, the proposed approach does not involve retraining, does not modify the weights of a transformer, and does not reduce the memory footprint of its parameters
(
Frantar et al. 2023
)
—we aim to control the accuracy of
computations
instead.
Consequently, our method should be viewed as complementary to compression and quantization techniques.

Medium-size architectures:

Our experimental evaluation focuses on the family of GPT-2 transformer models.
While these models are moderate in size by current standards, they already exhibit the characteristic depth and compositional structure of modern transformers.
Importantly, the numerical effects targeted by our method are present at this scale, suggesting that they are likely to become even more pronounced in larger architectures, and the effectiveness of our method likely increases.

Generalizability:

The proposed mixed-precision inference strategy is derived from a theoretical analysis of the core building blocks of transformers.
While the underlying idea applies to arbitrary DNN architectures, the feasibility of its implementation is architecture-dependent.
Extending our approach to other DNN architectures would require a similar theoretical analysis of their constituent operations.
Given the strong empirical results for transformers, we view this as a natural direction for future work.

Floating-point evaluation of compositions

Let
m,n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and consider functions:
g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Given
\bm{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

2.1
Baseline evaluation

Let
\bm{\mathrm{y}}=g(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the exact output of the inner function, and let
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be its computed value.
Assuming that
g
is evaluated with a mixed forward-backward stable algorithm
(
Higham 2002
)
, rounding error analysis guarantees that the relative error is bounded by

\frac{|\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{y}}|}{|\bm{\mathrm{y}}|}\leq\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{g}\mathrm{u}_{g}+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{u}_{g}^{2}),\quad\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{g}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},

(1)

where the absolute value, division, and comparison of vectors are componentwise, and
\mathrm{u}_{g}
denotes the unit round-off.
The nonnegative vector
\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{g}
describes how round-off errors are magnified during the computation of each component of
\bm{\mathrm{y}}
and is determined by the input and output dimensions of the function
g
, the rounding mode (e.g., deterministic or stochastic), the condition number of
g
at
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
, and the specific algorithm used to evaluate
g
.

Next,
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
is used as input for
f
, which is evaluated in precision
\mathrm{u}_{f}
.
Denoting by
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
the computed value of
f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})
, we get by the same logic as in (
1
) that

\frac{|\hat{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}-f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})|}{|f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})|}\leq\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{f}\mathrm{u}_{f}+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{u}_{f}^{2}),\quad\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{f}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}.

However, we need to compare
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}
with the exact
\bm{\mathrm{z}}=f(\bm{\mathrm{y}})
.
By triangle inequality,

\frac{|\hat{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}-\bm{\mathrm{z}}|}{|\bm{\mathrm{z}}|}\leq\frac{|\hat{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}-f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})|+|f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})-\bm{\mathrm{z}}|}{|f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})|}\Big(1+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{u}_{f})\Big).

The first term is bounded by
\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{f}\mathrm{u}_{f}
to first order.
To bound the second term, we assume that
f
is sufficiently regular in the neighborhood of
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
and use Taylor’s theorem to get an estimate in terms of the Jacobian
\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{f}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,

\frac{|f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})-f(\bm{\mathrm{y}})|}{|f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})|}\leq\frac{|\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{f}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})|\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{g}}{|f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})|}\mathrm{u}_{g}+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{u}_{g}^{2}),

where
\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is diagonal.
As a result, we get

\frac{|\hat{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}-\bm{\mathrm{z}}|}{|\bm{\mathrm{z}}|}\leq\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{f}\mathrm{u}_{f}+\frac{|\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{f}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})|\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{g}}{|f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})|}\mathrm{u}_{g}+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{u}_{f}^{2}+\mathrm{u}_{g}^{2}).

(2)

2.2
Refined evaluation

The bound (
2
) follows from (
1
) and therefore depends on
\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{g}
(i.e., the evaluation algorithm of
g
) and the assumption that every component of
g
is computed in precision
\mathrm{u}_{g}
.
Consider a more flexible setting where we may want to compute some of the components more accurately—with a more accurate algorithm or in higher precision—to ensure that the second term in (
2

Let the nonzeros of
\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
encode these components.

2.2.1
More accurate algorithm

We shall say that an evaluation algorithm is more accurate than the baseline if it leads to smaller entries in
\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{g}
.
Let us denote by
0\leq\epsilon\leq 1
the corresponding gain factor.
Then the bound (
1
) becomes

\frac{|\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{y}}|}{|\bm{\mathrm{y}}|}\leq\big(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\big)\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{g}\mathrm{u}_{g}+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{u}_{g}^{2}+\epsilon\mathrm{u}_{g}),

leading to a modification of the second term in (
2

\frac{|\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{f}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})|\big(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\big)\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{g}}{|f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})|}\mathrm{u}_{g}+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{u}_{g}^{2}+\epsilon\mathrm{u}_{g}).

Example 2.1
.

Let
g(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{Ax}}
with
\bm{\mathrm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
and
\bm{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
stored in precision
\mathrm{u}_{g}

Evaluating

Mixed-precision algorithms based on fused multiply-add can achieve

)
.
Therefore, the gain factor

2.2.2
Higher precision

As an alternative to a more accurate algorithm, we can use FP precision
\mathrm{u}_{g}^{2}
to compute and store the selected components.
Then (

\frac{|\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{y}}|}{|\bm{\mathrm{y}}|}\leq\big(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\big)\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{g}\mathrm{u}_{g}+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{u}_{g}^{2})

This requires the evaluation algorithm of

Example 2.2

Mixed-precision matrix multiplication

2.3
Look-ahead mixed-precision evaluation

The two refinement approaches of Subsection
2.2
lead to very similar rounding error bounds for the composition.
The accuracy of the inner computation manifests itself in the second term, which we aim to reduce as follows: select a binary vector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
so that

\Big\|\big|\mathrm{diag}(f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}))^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{f}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\big|(\bm{\mathrm{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{q}})\Big\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}\leq\tau

(3)

or

\Big\|\big|\mathrm{diag}(f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}))^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{f}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\big|(\bm{\mathrm{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{q}})\Big\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}\leq\tau

(4)

for a given threshold
\tau\geq 0
.
Let us discuss these objectives:

•

Objectives (
3
) and (
4

2
) for suitable
\tau

3

\bm{\mathrm{1}}\tau\|\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}\|\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{g}\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}\mathrm{u}_{g}

4

\bm{\mathrm{1}}\tau\|\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{g}\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}\mathrm{u}_{g}

Which of the two objectives to choose is a problem-specific question.

•

Both (
3

4

\bm{\mathrm{q}}=\bm{\mathrm{1}}

•

When
\bm{\mathrm{q}}=\bm{\mathrm{0}}

3

condition numbers of

•

The exact

and
\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{f}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})
are not available in practice, so their FP computed values will be used.

If the baseline
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
with
\bm{\mathrm{q}}=\bm{\mathrm{0}}
satisfies (
3

4
), we deem the computation complete.
Otherwise, we try a different

, which entails the recomputation of
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
.
Such recomputations can be costly, so we assume that the Jacobian is stable with respect to small variations in
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
and fix the matrix
\bm{\mathrm{K}}\in\{\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{u},\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{w}\}
corresponding to the baseline
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
, where
4
4

4

If the Jacobian changes rapidly, the Hessian would need to be included in the analysis.

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{u}=\mathrm{diag}(f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}))^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{f}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}),

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{w}=\mathrm{diag}(f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}))^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{f}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}).

Having found a suitable
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
that satisfies the bound

\big\||\bm{\mathrm{K}}|(\bm{\mathrm{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{q}})\big\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}=\big\|\bm{\mathrm{K}}\big(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\big)\big\|_{\mathrm{\infty,\infty}}\leq\tau,

we will recompute more accurately those components of

that are indexed by the nonzeros of the binary vector

To minimize the number of recomputations, we require

This leads to our

3
Application to transformer inference

Algorithm
1
relies on the possibility to compute individual components of
g
separately.
Yet this is not always possible.
For instance, consider the softmax function

\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\exp(x_{1})}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\exp(x_{i})}&\cdots&\frac{\exp(x_{k})}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\exp(x_{i})}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}.

(6)

If at least one component needs to be computed more accurately, every component has to be as well.
Meanwhile, the required property holds for matrix-vector products: it suffices to divide the matrix into two blocks and use different multiplication algorithms for each (see Example
2.1
).
5
5

5

When a bias term is present, it can be added to the accumulator in the required precision as well.

Matrix products are used heavily in all DNN architectures.
We thus apply LAMP evaluation to the following question:

How to adapt matrix multiplication to the ensuing nonlinearity during DNN inference?

What is the “ensuing nonlinearity” in question?
To target the accuracy of the end result of inference, an obvious choice is to set
f
to be the remaining tail of the DNN.
However, the computation of its LAMP matrix becomes highly demanding.
Additionally, the sparse-optimization LAMP problem (
5
) is in general NP-hard
(
Natarajan 1995
)
.

Instead, let
f
be one of the
elementary DNN nonlinearities
; for transformers, these are the activation function, layer normalization, and softmax.
We prove for these three functions that
almost-the-sparsest
solutions of the LAMP problem (
5
) can be obtained with a greedy algorithm of complexity
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
—the cost of a sorting algorithm.

3.1
Activation functions

Consider an activation function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Within DNNs, activation functions are applied componentwise to vectors:

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad f(\bm{\mathrm{y}})=\begin{bmatrix}\varphi(y_{1})&\cdots&\varphi(y_{n})\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}.

As a consequence, the LAMP matrix is diagonal,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{u}=\mathrm{diag}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(y_{1})}{\varphi(y_{1})},\ldots,\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(y_{n})}{\varphi(y_{n})}\bigg),

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{w}=\mathrm{diag}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(y_{1})}{\varphi(y_{1})}y_{1},\ldots,\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(y_{n})}{\varphi(y_{n})}y_{n}\bigg),

and the solution
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
of the LAMP problem (
5
) can be written in closed form: an entry of
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
is nonzero if and only if the corresponding diagonal entry of the LAMP matrix exceeds
\tau
in absolute value.
The diagonal structure of the Jacobian makes it possible to solve (
5
) immediately.

For activation functions, LAMP evaluation essentially repeats the mixed-precision accumulation idea of
(
El Arar et al. 2025
)
.
The novelty of the proposed LAMP problem (
5
) is that it applies to arbitrary compositions, and we prove below that transformer-specific compositions are particularly suitable for LAMP evaluation.

\big\||\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{w}|(\bm{\mathrm{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{q}})\big\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}=2\left(1-\frac{\min_{j\not\in\Omega}y_{j}^{2}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}}\right)-\frac{\sum_{i\in\Omega}y_{i}^{2}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}}

when
|\Omega|\leq n-2
and

\big\||\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{w}|(\bm{\mathrm{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{q}})\big\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}=\max\left\{\frac{y_{j}^{2}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}},1-\frac{y_{j}^{2}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}}\right\}

when
\Omega=[n]\setminus\{j\}
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A.1
.
∎

Even though Lemma
3.2
provides exact values of the matrix norm for each
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
, finding the sparsest
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
to satisfy (
4
) can be too expensive for large
n
.
The following proposition shows that there exist simple solutions of (
5
).

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose that the entries of
\bm{\mathrm{y}}
are arranged as
y_{1}^{2}\geq\cdots\geq y_{n}^{2}
.
Let
\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
be a solution of the weighted LAMP problem (
5
) for RMS layer normalization (
7
).
If
\|\bm{\mathrm{q}}\|_{\mathrm{0}}\leq n-3
then a vector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
given by

\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\cdots&1&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal},\quad\|\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{0}}=\|\bm{\mathrm{q}}\|_{\mathrm{0}}+q_{n},

also satisfies
\big\||\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{w}|(\bm{\mathrm{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime})\big\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}\leq\tau
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A.2
.
∎

Proposition
3.3
ensures that an almost-the-sparsest solution of the LAMP problem (
5
) can be obtained greedily: we need to sort the entries of
\bm{\mathrm{y}}
in descending order according to their squares, pick the smallest
s
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{s}y_{i}^{2}+2y_{n}^{2}\geq(2-\tau)\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2},

and form
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
based on the initial positions of the indices
\{1,\ldots,s\}
.
The cost of this algorithm is dominated by the sorting step, which has the complexity
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
.

3.3
Attention and softmax

The self-attention mechanism is a distinctive feature of transformers
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
.
In the simplest case, attention is computed as
\bm{\mathrm{V}}\cdot\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{\mathrm{K}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{Q}})
. This is a composition of three functions: the product of the key and query matrices, the softmax function (
6
) applied columnwise, and the product of the value matrix and the output of softmax.

When the value-softmax product is followed by layer normalization, its evaluation can be adapted as discussed in Subsection
3.2
.
We shall study the LAMP evaluation of the key-query product by analyzing the LAMP problem (
5
) for softmax in the unweighted case (
3
).

Lemma 3.4
.

For softmax (
6
), the unweighted LAMP matrix equals

\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{u}=\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\bm{\mathrm{1}}\bm{\mathrm{z}}^{\intercal},

where
\bm{\mathrm{z}}=\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{\mathrm{y)}}
, and satisfies

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{u}\|_{\mathrm{\infty,\infty}}=2\Big(1-\min_{i\in[n]}z_{i}\Big),

\displaystyle 2\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\leq\|\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{u}\|_{\mathrm{\infty,\infty}}\leq 2.

Proof.

See Lemma 3.13 from
(
Budzinskiy et al. 2025
)
.
∎

Lemma
3.4
reveals an intriguing connection between the
weighted
LAMP matrix of RMS layer normalization and the
unweighted
LAMP matrix of softmax: in fact, they are the same matrix.
To see this, it suffices to replace
z_{i}
with
y_{i}^{2}/\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}
and recall that
\|\bm{\mathrm{z}}\|_{\mathrm{1}}=1
by the definition of softmax (
6
).
Therefore, our results on RMS layer normalization and their proofs can be extended to the softmax function.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
be such that
\bm{\mathrm{q}}\neq\bm{\mathrm{1}}
, and denote by
\Omega
its support.
For softmax (
6
) with values
\bm{\mathrm{z}}=\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{\mathrm{y)}}
, the unweighted LAMP matrix satisfies

\big\||\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{u}|(\bm{\mathrm{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{q}})\big\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}=2\left(1-\min_{j\not\in\Omega}z_{j}\right)-\sum_{i\in\Omega}z_{i}

when
|\Omega|\leq n-2
and

\big\||\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{u}|(\bm{\mathrm{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{q}})\big\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}=\max\left\{z_{j},1-z_{j}\right\}

when
\Omega=[n]\setminus\{j\}
.

Proof.

Follows from Lemma
3.2
.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{z}}=\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{\mathrm{y)}}
and suppose that its entries are arranged as
z_{1}\geq\cdots\geq z_{n}
.
Let
\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
be a solution of the unweighted LAMP problem (
5
) for softmax (
6
).
If
\|\bm{\mathrm{q}}\|_{\mathrm{0}}\leq n-3
then a vector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
given by

\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\cdots&1&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal},\quad\|\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{0}}=\|\bm{\mathrm{q}}\|_{\mathrm{0}}+q_{n},

also satisfies
\big\||\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{w}|(\bm{\mathrm{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime})\big\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}\leq\tau
.

Proof.

The result follows readily from Proposition
3.3
.
∎

The same greedy selection strategy applies to the softmax function as to RMS layer normalization.
Let us also discuss the choice of the threshold
\tau
and set
\tau=2-\epsilon
.
Then we seek the smallest
s
such that, after sorting,

\sum_{i=1}^{s}z_{i}+2z_{n}\geq\epsilon,

and the inequality is satisfiable for
0~\leq~\epsilon~\leq~1
.
In the case of almost equal probabilities
z_{1}=\cdots=z_{n-1}=\frac{1}{n-1}
and
z_{n}=0
, we get
s=\lceil\epsilon(n-1)\rceil
.
On the contrary,
s=1
when
z_{1}=1
.
Informally, the more a transformer is “confused,” the more accurate its inference should be.

4.1
Custom floating-point format

To focus on the question of precision and put aside the issue of overflows, we simulate low-precision matrix multiplications using a custom
partial single
FP format.
For every
\mu\in\{1,\ldots,23\}
, we define the format
\mathrm{PS}(\mu)
to have
\mu
mantissa bits, 8 exponent bits, and one sign bit.
This format is equivalent to FP32 when
\mu=23
, to TF32 when
\mu=10
, and to BF16 when
\mu=7
.
In code, we implement
\mathrm{PS}(\mu)
numbers via FP32 numbers rounded to
\mu
mantissa bits according to the round-to-nearest-ties-to-even mode.

To perform matrix multiplication with input-output formats
\mathrm{PS}(\mu_{A})\times\mathrm{PS}(\mu_{B})\to\mathrm{PS}(\mu_{C})
, we accumulate inner products as
\mathrm{round}(c+a\cdot b)
, where the scalar multiplication and addition are in FP32.

4.2
Experimental setting

To assess the impact of LAMP evaluation on inference, we compute the mean Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the probability distributions output by a reference model and a test model over 200 sequences of 1024 tokens each.
We also look at the flip rate, i.e., how often the most probable predictions of the reference and test models differ.

Our reference model uses FP32 inference uniformly for all FP operations.
The test models perform the key-query products in
\mathrm{PS}(\mu)
and recompute some of them, as selected by the LAMP problem (
5
), in FP32.
We keep track of how many inner products are recomputed; to get the recomputation rate, we divide by the number of key-query inner products in the “causal mask.”

4.3
Numerical results

In Figure
2
, we fix the threshold
\tau
of LAMP and vary the number of mantissa bits
\mu
.
The plots demonstrate clearly that our method works in practice, that is, as the threshold
\tau
decreases, it improves the accuracy of inference and increases the number of recomputations required.
Specifically, consistently for smaller
\mu
, our method reduces the KL divergence tenfold with 3.4% of recomputations (
\tau=1.4
), hundredfold with 15% of recomputations (
\tau=1.1
), and thousandfold with 34.3% of recomputations (
\tau=1.02
).
Similarly consistent improvements hold for the flip rate as well, and we observe exponential-like decay of the two metrics as the number of bits
\mu
is increased.
An interesting observation is that the recomputation rate hardly depends on
\mu
, yet we stress again that the
choice
of the components to be recomputed has a crucial impact on the performance of mixed-precision inference (cf. Figure
1
and Appendix
B
).

Figure
3
provides a different angle on the numerical results, fixing
\mu
and varying
\tau
.
The plots show the KL divergence is improved at the same rate, regardless of the (small) number of bits
\mu
, as the threshold
\tau
decreases.
The same observation holds for the flip rate, and both metrics decay faster as
\tau
tends to one, at which point everything is selected for recomputation.
We can also see that the recomputation rate is slightly higher for extremely low-precision accumulation (
\mu=2
) than for the rest.

The empirical results presented in Figures
2
and
3
convincingly demonstrate a strong performance of the proposed LAMP method, as it reaches high inference accuracies relative to the ground truth with the vast majority of key-query inner products performed in low precision.
The additional experiments in Appendix
B
strengthen this conclusion.

While not studied numerically in this paper, LAMP evaluation can be applied to all transformer nonlinearities simultaneously (Section
3
), which could increase the computational throughput of transformer inference even further.
Together with a hardware-specific, efficient implementation of LAMP inference, this is a promising avenue for future research.

A.1
Proof of Lemma
3.2

Denote by
s_{l}
the
l
th absolute row-sum of
\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{w}\big(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\big)
.
By Lemma
3.1
,

s_{l}=\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}}\sum_{j\not\in\Omega}y_{j}^{2},&l\in\Omega,\\
\tfrac{1}{\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}}\sum_{j\not\in\Omega\cup\{l\}}y_{j}^{2}+1-\tfrac{y_{l}^{2}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}},&l\not\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Rewriting
\sum_{j\not\in\Omega}y_{j}^{2}=\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}-\sum_{i\in\Omega}y_{i}^{2}
and taking the maximum of
s_{l}
over
l
, we get

\big\|\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{w}\big(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\big)\big\|_{\mathrm{\infty,\infty}}=\max\left\{2\left(1-\frac{\min_{j\not\in\Omega}y_{j}^{2}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}}\right),1\right\}-\frac{\sum_{i\in\Omega}y_{i}^{2}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}}.

Note that
1>2\Big(1-\frac{\min_{j\not\in\Omega}y_{j}^{2}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}}\Big)
if and only if
\min_{j\not\in\Omega}y_{j}^{2}>\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}
.
When
|\Omega|\leq n-2
, this would imply that

\sum_{j\not\in\Omega}y_{j}^{2}>\frac{1}{2}(n-|\Omega|)\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}\geq\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2},

which is impossible.
This contradiction proves the first formula.
The second formula follows from the general expression.

A.2
Proof of Proposition
3.3

Denote by
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
the supports of
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime}
, respectively.
By Lemma
3.2
, as
|\Omega^{\prime}|\leq n-2
, we need to show that

2\left(1-\frac{y_{n}^{2}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}}\right)-\frac{\sum_{i\in\Omega^{\prime}}y_{i}^{2}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}}\leq\tau.

Since
|\Omega|<n-2
and
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
satisfies the norm constraint of LAMP, it suffices to show that

\sum_{i\in\Omega^{\prime}}y_{i}^{2}+2y_{n}^{2}\geq\sum_{i\in\Omega}y_{i}^{2}+2\min_{j\not\in\Omega}y_{j}^{2}.

Note that
\Omega^{\prime}
is constructed in such a way that for every set
\tilde{\Omega}\subset[n]
with
|\tilde{\Omega}|=|\Omega^{\prime}|
, it holds that
\sum_{i\in\Omega^{\prime}}y_{i}^{2}\geq\sum_{i\in\tilde{\Omega}}y_{i}^{2}
.
In case
n\not\in\Omega
, we have
\min_{j\not\in\Omega}y_{j}^{2}=y_{n}^{2}
, and the desired inequality follows trivially since
|\Omega|=|\Omega^{\prime}|
.
When
n\in\Omega
, we have

\sum_{i\in\Omega}y_{i}^{2}+2\min_{j\not\in\Omega}y_{j}^{2}\leq y_{n}^{2}+\sum_{i\in\Omega\setminus\{n\}}y_{i}^{2}+\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,l\not\in\Omega\\
j\neq l\end{subarray}}(y_{j}^{2}+y_{l}^{2})\leq y_{n}^{2}+\sum_{i\in\Omega^{\prime}}y_{i}^{2}\leq 2y_{n}^{2}+\sum_{i\in\Omega^{\prime}}y_{i}^{2},

where in the second inequality we apply the majorizing property of
\Omega^{\prime}
to the set
\tilde{\Omega}=\Omega\setminus\{n\}\cup\{j_{\ast},l_{\ast}\}
, with minimizing indices
j_{\ast}
and
l_{\ast}
, and note that
|\tilde{\Omega}|=|\Omega^{\prime}|
.
