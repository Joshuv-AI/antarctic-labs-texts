L-equivalences via symplectic and F 4 F_{4} Grassmannians
Ivan Noden

Abstract
. Using a construction of Kanemitsu from [ 9 ] and observations by Rampazzo in [ 19 ] , we find examples of zero divisors in the Grothendieck ring of varieties by taking the zero loci of sections of vector bundles over symplectic and F 4 F_{4} Grassmannians. These zero divisors yield instances of non-trivially L-equivalent Calabi-Yau varieties. This methodology is inspired by a similar process performed by Ito et al . on G 2 G_{2} Grassmannians in [ 8 ] .



1.
Introduction

The
Grothendieck ring of varieties
over a field
k
,
denoted
K_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{k})
is, as an abelian group, generated by symbols of the
form
[X]
, where
X
is a variety over
k
, subject
to the following relations:

(i)

[X]=[Y]
whenever
X\cong Y
;

(ii)

[X\setminus Y]=[X]-[Y]
whenever
Y
is a closed
subvariety of
X
;

(iii)

[X]\cdot[Y]=[X\times Y]
.

This ring is algebraically quite interesting and has been useful
in various parts of algebraic geometry, for instance in Kontsevich’s
theory of motivic integration. Poonen showed in
[
17
]
that

K_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{k})
is not a domain when
k
is characteristic zero and,
in
[
3
]
, Borisov proved that
\mathbb{L}=[\mathbb{A}^{1}]
is a zero
divisor in
K_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{\mathbb{C}})
. Motivated by this, Ito
et al
. constructed
a pair of Calabi-Yau varieties,
X
and
Y
, cut out from

G_{2}
Grassmannians, such that

\mathbb{L}([X]-[Y])=0

and
[X]\neq[Y]

[
8
]
. In his PhD thesis
[
19
]
,
Rampazzo observed that this construction is a particular
example of a family of identities in
K_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{\mathbb{C}})
obtained
by cutting out pairs of Calabi-Yau varieties from Grassmannians.
These constructions require a homogeneous roof, as
defined and studied by Kanemitsu in
[
9
]
.
Kanemitsu identifies seven families of this construction:

A_{r}\times A_{r},\,A_{r}^{M},\,A_{2r}^{G},\,C_{3r-1},\,D_{r},\,F_{4}
and

G_{2}
. Rampazzo shows the latter five of these
have the potential to produce zero divisors (as already demonstrated
in the
G_{2}
case) and, in
[
20
]
, they are able to produce zero divisors
in the
A_{2r}^{G}
case. Rampazzo and Xie also provide the argument
for the
D_{5}
case in
[
22
]
: it is a consequence
of
[
22
, Theorem 3.10]
and
3.10
.
It is likely that this argument can be adapted to the general
D_{r}

case for
r\geq 5
.

The zero divisors constructed from these homogeneous roofs
yield examples of
L-equivalent
varieties.

Definition 1.1

(
[
12
, §1.2]
)
.

Varieties
X
and
Y
are said to be
L-equivalent

if there exists some
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that

\mathbb{L}^{d}([X]-[Y])=0.

There has been much evidence relating derived and L-equivalence.
In particular, the Calabi-Yau pairs from the roofs of type
G_{2}
,

A_{4}^{G}
and
D_{5}
are known to be both derived and L-equivalent

[
8
,
11
,
21
,
20
,
22
]
.
It was conjectured by Kuznetsov and Shinder
in
[
12
, Conjecture 1.6]
that any pair of projective, simply connected, smooth,
derived equivalent varieties are L-equivalent. However,
in
[
15
]
, Meinsma found counter-examples to this conjecture
and instead conjectured the partial converse: that any pair of projective,
hyperkähler, L-equivalent manifolds are derived equivalent.
We hope that providing further examples of L-equivalent varieties
will assist in understanding the connection between derived and L-equivalence.

In this paper, we show the homogeneous roofs of type
C_{3r-1}

and
F_{4}
yield examples of L-equivalent varieties.
More precisely, for symplectic Grassmannians, we prove:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
(Z_{1},Z_{2})
a Calabi-Yau pair associated
to the homogeneous roof of type
C_{3r-1}
. Then,
[Z_{1}]\neq[Z_{2}]

and yet

\mathbb{L}^{2r-1}([Z_{1}]-[Z_{2}])=0.

For
F_{4}
Grassmannians we prove:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(Z_{1},Z_{2})
be a Calabi-Yau pair associated to the
homogeneous roof of type
F_{4}
. Then
[Z_{1}]\neq[Z_{2}]

and yet

\mathbb{L}^{2}([Z_{1}]-[Z_{2}])=0.

The techniques of this paper are not enough to find
a zero divisor in the
C_{2}
case as, if
(Z_{1},Z_{2})
is a Calabi-Yau
pair associated to a homogeneous roof of type
C_{2}
, then the
argument we use to show
[Z_{1}]\neq[Z_{2}]

does not follow due to dimensional constraints. More precisely,
we cannot use
Lemma

3.8
.
Similarly, since
Lemma

3.8
and
3.10

both fail in the
D_{4}
case, finding a zero divisor in this
instance will likely need different methods even if the results of

[
22
]
are generalised.

2.
Preliminaries

Let us briefly recap the representation theory of reductive algebraic
groups in order to fix some notation. The reader should feel free
to skip this section and refer back to it when necessary.
For a more in-depth and comprehensive discussion, we refer
the reader to the standard texts of Borel
[
2
]
,
Humphreys
[
7
]
, Milne
[
16
]
and Springer
[
25
]
.
We work solely over
\mathbb{C}
.

2.1.
Weights, Roots and the Weyl Group

For an algebraic group
G
, let
{\mathrm{X}^{\ast}}(G)
denote the abelian
group of morphisms
G\to\mathbb{G}_{m}
under pointwise multiplication.
We write this group additively.

We fix a reductive group
G
with a maximal torus
T
.
A representation
V
of
G
decomposes as a direct
sum
[
25
, Theorem 3.2.3]
:

V=\bigoplus_{\chi\in{\mathrm{X}^{\ast}}(T)}V_{\chi}

where

V_{\chi}=\left\{v\in V\,\colon\,tv=\chi(t)v\text{ for all }t\in T\right\},

is the weight space for the weight
\chi
.
The
roots
of
G
are the weights
of the adjoint representation and are denoted by
\Phi
.

The
Weyl group

W(G)=N_{G}(T)/T

acts on the weights of a representation
V
of
G
.
We write
W
for
W(G)
when there is
no ambiguity.

The
cocharacters
of
G
, denoted
{\mathrm{X}_{\ast}}(G)
, is the
group of morphisms
\mathbb{G}_{m}\to G
under pointwise
multiplication, again, written additively.

Recall that
{\mathrm{X}_{\ast}}(T)
is a free abelian group of
finite rank and that we get a perfect pairing
{\mathrm{X}^{\ast}}(T)\times{\mathrm{X}_{\ast}}(T)\to\mathbb{Z}

by letting
\left\langle\chi,\,\lambda\right\rangle=r
, where
(\chi\circ\lambda)(x)=x^{r}

[
25
, Lemma 3.2.11]
.

Each root
\chi
of
G
is assigned a
coroot

\chi^{\vee}

as described in
[
25
, §7]
.
For
\chi\in\Phi
, write

s_{\chi}(\lambda)=\lambda-\left\langle\lambda,\,\chi^{\vee}\right\rangle\chi\in W.

Recall, a
system of positive roots

\Phi^{+}
for
\Phi

contains a set of
simple roots

\mathcal{S}

such that
W
is generated by
s_{\chi}

for
\chi\in\mathcal{S}
. These generators
are called
simple reflections
.
A system of positive roots can be determined by
a choice of Borel subgroup of
G
.

2.2.
Bruhat Decomposition

Recall, for a Borel subgroup
B
of
G
,
B\times B
acts on
G
by
(b_{1},b_{2})g=b_{1}gb_{2}^{-1}
.
Denote the orbit of
g\in G
under this action by
BgB
.
This is a subvariety of
G
. Note, for
w\in W
,
the subvariety
BwB
is well-defined.
The Bruhat decomposition
[
25
, Theorem 8.3.8]

is the disjoint union

G=\bigcup_{w\in W}BwB.

Similarly, for
G/B
, we have the
cellular decomposition

G/B=\bigcup_{w\in W}X(w),

where
X(w)
is some affine space and the union is disjoint.
To determine its dimension,
we need to introduce some more notation. The
length
of

w\in W
, denoted
\ell(w)
, is the number of roots
in
\Phi^{+}
sent to
-\Phi^{+}
by
w
. Equivalently,

w
can be written as a product of
\ell(w)
simple
reflections and no fewer. There is a unique element of
W

of length
\#\Phi^{+}
called the
longest element
.
It is possible to show that

X(w)\cong\mathbb{A}^{\ell(w)}.

We therefore remark that
W
determines the cohomology of
G/B
.
The proof of these claims and further details can be found
in
[
25
, Proposition 8.5.1]
.

Recall that parabolic subgroups of
G
can be constructed by taking

I\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, letting
W_{I}
be the subgroup
generated by
s_{\chi}
for
\chi\in I
and putting

P_{I}=\bigcup_{w\in W_{I}}BwB.

When we take a parabolic subgroup
P
of
G
, we assume it is
of this form and call
W_{I}
the Weyl group of
P
.

For each
w\in W
, there is
a unique element of
wW_{I}
with the shortest possible length
[
10
, Proposition 1.1]

so we put

W^{I}=\left\{w\in W\,\colon\,\ell(w)\leq\ell(w^{\prime})\text{ for all }w^{\prime}\in wW_{I}\right\}.

Then,

G/P_{I}=\bigcup_{w\in W^{I}}Y(w)

where the union is disjoint and
Y(w)\cong\mathbb{A}^{\ell(w)}

[
10
, Proposition 1.3]
.

2.3.
Representations

If
V
is a finite-dimensional, irreducible representation of

G
then, up to isomorphism,
V
is uniquely determined
by its unique, highest weight. If
\chi\in{\mathrm{X}^{\ast}}(T)

is dominant then we let
V_{G}(\chi)
denote the
finite-dimensional, irreducible representation with highest
weight
\chi
. For more detail on this, see
[
16
, §22]
.

We recall that, if
\chi
is the
highest weight of
V
, then
-\chi
is the lowest weight of

V^{\vee}
. Moreover,
V^{\vee}=V_{G}(-w_{0}(\chi))
, where
w_{0}

is the longest element of
W
.

Also, recall that any finite-dimensional representation of
G

is a direct sum of irreducible representations
[
16
, Theorem 22.42]
.

2.4.
The Semisimple Case

Suppose
G
is semisimple, then the rank of
{\mathrm{X}^{\ast}}(T)
is

\#\mathcal{S}

[
16
, Proposition 21.48]
. Write
\mathcal{S}=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r}\}
.
Recall the
fundamental weights
of
G
,

\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{r}
, are uniquely determined by the fact

\left\langle\omega_{i},\,\alpha^{\vee}_{j}\right\rangle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }i=j,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then the set of dominant weights of
G
is

\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}\omega_{i}\,\colon\,n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\right\}.

If
G
is semisimple, we can describe its parabolic subgroups
graphically using its Dynkin diagram. For
I\subseteq\mathcal{S}
,
the parabolic
P_{I}
is represented by crossing out any vertex
of the Dynkin diagram corresponding to a simple root not in
I
.

2.5.
Representations of Parabolic Subgroups

Suppose
G
is now only assumed to be reductive and
P

is a parabolic subgroup of
G
. Recall,
P
contains
a Levi subgroup
L
. Let
V
be a non-trivial, finite-dimensional,
irreducible representation of
P
. Put
U
as the unipotent radical
of
P
so

P=U\rtimes L
. Then
V
is a representation of

U
which is unipotent, thus the subspace of
V

fixed by
U
, denoted
V^{U}
, is non-zero
[
16
, Proposition 14.3]
.
As
U
is normal in
P
,
V^{U}
is a

P
-subrepresentation of
V
. Thus
V=V^{U}

as
V
is irreducible. Hence, the action of
P

on
V
is determined by the action of
L
on
V
.
That is, finite-dimensional, irreducible representations
of
P
are in bijective correspondence with finite-dimensional,
irreducible representations of the reductive group
L
and,
hence, weights dominant for
L
.

Note that, while a representation
V
of
P
cannot generally
be decomposed into a direct sum of irreducible representations,
it is possible if
U
acts trivially on
V
. In particular,
it is possible if
V
is a subrepresentation of a tensor power of an
irreducible representation.

Suppose
P=P_{I}
is a parabolic subgroup constructed
from a set of simple roots
I
.
Then, a character
\chi\in{\mathrm{X}^{\ast}}(T)
is dominant for
L

if and only if
\left\langle\chi,\,\lambda^{\vee}\right\rangle\geq 0
for
all
\lambda\in I
. We say such a weight is
dominant for

P

and let
V_{P}(\chi)
denote the finite-dimensional, irreducible
representation of
P
with highest weight
\chi
.

Note if
G
is semisimple with simple roots
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r}\}

and corresponding fundamental weights
\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{r}\}
,
then the weights dominant for
P
are

\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}\omega_{i}\,\colon\,n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ and }n_{i}\geq 0\text{ whenever }\alpha_{i}\in I\right\}.

When studying representations of parabolic subgroups,
we will often refer to the Weyl group of
P
.
By this, we mean the Weyl group of
L
, seen as a subgroup
of
W(G)
. This is the subgroup generated by the simple
reflections corresponding to the roots in
I
.

2.6.
Homogeneous Vector Bundles

If
P
is a parabolic subgroup of
G
then,
a
homogeneous vector bundle
on
G/P

is of the form

E_{P}(V)=\frac{G\times V}{(g,v)\sim(gp,p^{-1}v)\text{ for all }p\in P},

for
V
a representation of
P

[
25
, Lemma 5.5.8, Theorem 15.1.3]
.
If
\chi\in{\mathrm{X}^{\ast}}(T)
is dominant for

P
, we let
E_{P}(\chi)
denote
E_{P}(V_{P}(\chi)^{\vee})
.

Assuming
G
is semisimple and simply connected, every
line bundle on
G/P
is homogeneous
[
24
, Theorem 6.4]
and hence line bundles
on
G/P
are in bijective correspondence with
1
-dimensional
representations of
P
. Let

\Lambda_{P}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}\omega_{i}\,\colon\,n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ and }n_{i}=0\text{ whenever }\alpha_{i}\in I\right\}

and

\Gamma_{P}=\left\{\chi\in{\mathrm{X}^{\ast}}(T)\,\colon\,\chi\text{ is dominant for }P\text{ and }\dim V_{P}(\chi)=1\right\}.

We claim
\Lambda_{P}=\Gamma_{P}
.

First, suppose
\chi\in\Lambda_{P}
. Then
\chi
is an extremal
weight of
V_{P}(\chi)
so has multiplicity
1
.
Consider the simple reflection
s_{\alpha_{i}}
for some simple
root
\alpha_{i}
. Note that

s_{\alpha_{i}}(\omega_{j})=\omega_{j}-\left\langle\omega_{j},\,\alpha^{\vee}_{i}\right\rangle\alpha_{i}=\begin{cases}\omega_{j},&\text{if }i\neq j,\\
\omega_{j}-\alpha_{i},&\text{if }i=j.\end{cases}

Thus, every element of the Weyl group of
P
fixes
\omega_{i}

if
\alpha_{i}\in\mathcal{S}\setminus I
, hence every element
fixes
\chi
so it is the only extremal weight of
V_{P}(\chi)
.
Thus,
\dim V_{P}(\chi)=1
and
\chi\in\Gamma_{P}
.

For the converse, suppose
\chi\in\Gamma_{P}
. Then
we see
\chi
must be invariant under the Weyl group of
P
.
This tells us that the highest weight of
V_{P}(\chi)^{\vee}

is
-\chi
and, hence,
-\chi
must be dominant for
P
.
See that
\chi
and
-\chi
can only both be dominant
if
\chi\in\Lambda_{P}
so we get
\Gamma_{P}\subseteq\Lambda_{P}
.

We therefore have a bijection between
1
-dimensional
representations of
P
(and hence line bundles on
G/P
)
and
\Lambda_{P}
.

Note that
\Lambda_{P}
is a group under addition
and is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where
d=r-\#I
. It is
then an easy check to see
\operatorname{Pic}(G/P)\cong\Lambda_{P}
.
Thus, the Picard number of
G/P
equals the number
of crossed out vertices in the Dynkin diagram of
P
.
When we are in this case, let us write
\mathcal{O}_{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})

for the line bundle
E_{P}(n_{1}\omega_{i_{1}}+\cdots+n_{r}\omega_{i_{r}})
,
where
\{\alpha_{i_{1}},\ldots,\alpha_{i_{r}}\}=\mathcal{S}\setminus I
.
Due to our odd choice of convention for defining
E_{P}(\chi)
,
this coincides with standard notation for line bundles on projective
space.

We finally remark that, by
[
24
, Corollary 8.5]
, if
G
is semisimple and simply connected
then the homogeneous vector bundle
E_{P}(\chi)

over
G/P
is ample if

\chi\in\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}\omega_{i}\,\colon\,n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\text{ and }n_{i}>0\text{ whenever }\alpha_{i}\not\in I\right\}.

2.7.
Borel-Weil-Bott

Suppose now
G
is semisimple and simply connected. Let
\rho

be the sum of the fundamental weights of
G
. For
w\in W

and
\chi\in{\mathrm{X}^{\ast}}(T)
we define

w\bullet\chi=w(\chi+\rho)-\rho.

Let us recall the Borel-Weil-Bott theorem:

Theorem 2.1

(Borel-Weil-Bott)
.

Let
P
be a parabolic subgroup of
G
and
\chi\in{\mathrm{X}^{\ast}}(T)

dominant for
P
. Either:

(i)

there exists a unique
w\in W
such that
w\bullet\chi

is dominant for
G
and then

H^{i}(G/P,E_{P}(\chi))\cong\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }i\neq\ell(w),\\
V_{G}(w\bullet\chi)^{\vee},&\text{if }i=\ell(w);\end{cases}

(ii)

or there is no
w\in W
such that
w\bullet\chi
is dominant
for
G
and then
H^{i}(G/P,E_{P}(\chi))=0
for all
i
.

Proof.

See, for instance,
[
1
, §5.1]
.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

This result generalises to the right derived pushforward of other
quotient maps.
More precisely, suppose
P
and
Q
are parabolics of
G
with

Q\subseteq P
. Put
\pi\colon G/Q\rightarrow G/P
as the natural map.
Then, for any
\chi\in{\mathrm{X}^{\ast}}(T)
dominant for both
P
and
Q
,
we have
\pi_{\ast}E_{Q}(\chi)\cong E_{P}(\chi)
, while
R^{i}\pi_{\ast}E_{Q}(\chi)=0

for
i>0
. For more on this, see
[
1
, §5.3]
.

2.8.
Example: Symplectic Group

We flesh out the case where
G
is a symplectic group
in order to fix notation used in
Section

Let
G=\bm{\mathrm{Sp}}_{2n}
and

For

be

is
,

Let

We denote by

.
Note that this flag variety is the quotient

where

.

Proposition 3.7

Lemma 4.1
.

We have
[F_{1}]=[F_{2}]
.

Proof.

Due to the cellular decomposition of
F_{i}
, we know

[F_{i}]=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}b_{2j}(F_{i})\mathbb{L}^{j},

where
b_{j}(F_{i})
is the
j
th Betti number of
F_{i}
.
We will show the Betti numbers for
F_{1}
and
F_{2}

are equal using the Weil conjectures.

Put
X=\mathrm{IGr}(d,2n)
.
Let us count
\#X(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. A
d
-dimensional isotropic subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2n}
is given by a choice of
d
basis vectors.
The first of these can be any non-zero vector and so we have
q^{2n}-1
choices. The second vector must be in the
symplectic complement of the first and not a multiple of the first. This gives
q^{2n-1}-q
choices.
Repeating this, we see that there are

\prod_{i=1}^{d}(q^{2n-i+1}-q^{i-1})=q^{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(q^{2(n-i)+2}-1)

sets of
d
vectors which yield a
d
-dimensional isotropic subspace.
Some of these will result in the same subspace, and so we must count the number of possible bases of a
d
-dimensional isotropic subspace.
There are
q^{d}-1
choices for the first basis vector, then the second must not lie in the span of the first, so there are
q^{d}-q
choices
for the second basis vector and so on. Hence, the number of possible bases for a given subspace is

\prod_{i=1}^{d}(q^{d}-q^{i-1})=q^{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(q^{i}-1).

It follows that

\#X(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{q^{2(n-j+1)}-1}{q^{j}-1}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\#F_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{2r}\frac{q^{2(3r-j)}-1}{q^{j}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{2r}-1}{q^{2r}-1}\prod_{j=1}^{2r-1}\frac{q^{2(3r-j)-1}}{q^{j}-1}

\displaystyle=\#F_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q}).

By the Weil conjectures,
[F_{1}]=[F_{2}]
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

For
p\in\{1,\ldots,2r\}
, we have
H^{\ast}(F_{1},\wedge^{p}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{1}(1))=0
.

Proof.

Recall that

\mathcal{E}_{1}=E_{P_{1}}\left(2\sum_{j=1}^{2r-1}L_{j}+L_{2r}\right).

Thus,

\mathcal{E}^{\vee}_{1}=E_{P_{1}}(V_{P_{1}}(\lambda)),

where
\lambda=2\sum_{j=1}^{2r-1}L_{j}+L_{2r}
.
Put
V=V_{P_{1}}(\lambda)
.
Note that the weights of
\wedge^{p}V

are the
p
-fold sums of the weights of
V

so let us calculate them.
The Weyl group
W_{1}
of
P_{1}
is the subgroup of the Weyl group
W

of
G
generated by
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{3r-1}\}\setminus\{s_{2r-1}\}
.
By letting
W_{1}
act on
\lambda
, we see the extremal
weights of
V
are of the form

2\sum_{j=1}^{2r-1}L_{j}\pm L_{2r+k}

for some
k\in\{0,\ldots,r-1\}
.
There are
2r
of these, the same as the dimension
of
V
, so these are all the weights.

Hence, we deduce that the only weights of
\wedge^{p}V

which are dominant for
P_{1}
are of the form

\mu_{0}=2p\sum_{j=1}^{2r-1}L_{j}\text{ or }\mu_{t}=2p\sum_{j=1}^{2r-1}L_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{t}L_{2r-1+j}.

As the unipotent radical of
P
acts trivially on
V
,
we should be able to take some weights
\mu_{t_{1}},\ldots,\mu_{t_{s}}

of this form and write

\wedge^{p}V=V_{P_{1}}(\mu_{t_{1}})\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{P_{1}}(\mu_{t_{s}}).

Hence,
\wedge^{p}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}_{1}=E_{P_{1}}(-w_{0}(\mu_{t_{1}}))\oplus\cdots\oplus E_{P_{1}}(-w_{0}(\mu_{t_{s}}))
,
where
w_{0}
is the longest element of
W_{1}
. Since
W_{1}

is isomorphic to
S_{2r-1}\times W(\bm{\mathrm{Sp}}_{2r})
, its longest element
swaps
L_{j}
and
L_{2r-j}
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,2r-1\}

and sends
L_{j}
to
-L_{j}
for
j\in\{2r,\ldots,3r-1\}
.
Thus,

-w_{0}(\mu_{t})=-2p\sum_{j=1}^{2r-1}L_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{t}L_{2r-1+j}.

So, if we let

\mu^{\prime}_{t}=(1-2p)\sum_{j=1}^{2r-1}L_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{t}L_{2r-1+j},

then we see

\wedge^{p}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{1}(1)=E_{P_{1}}(\mu^{\prime}_{t_{1}})\oplus\cdots\oplus E_{P_{1}}(\mu^{\prime}_{t_{s}}).

This allows us to calculate its cohomology using Borel-Weil-Bott.
We claim that, for any
t
, there is no
w\in W
such that

w\bullet\mu^{\prime}_{t}
is dominant for
G
, which will prove the
cohomology vanishes.
Let
\rho
be the sum of
the fundamental weights and recall

\rho=\sum_{t=1}^{3r-1}(3r-2+j)L_{j}.

For
w\bullet\mu^{\prime}_{t}
to be dominant, when writing
it in the
L_{j}
basis, it can’t have any negative coefficients.
Since the action of
w
permutes the
L_{j}
and changes
some of their signs, to not have negative coefficients in
w\bullet\mu_{t}^{\prime}
,
there must be a coefficient of some
L_{j}
in

\mu^{\prime}_{t}+\rho
with absolute value at least
3r-1
.
A straightforward calculation shows this is not possible if
p\in\{1,\ldots,2r\}
.
It follows that
H^{\ast}(F_{1},\wedge^{p}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{1}(1))=0
.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

We have
\dim H^{0}(Z_{1},\mathcal{O}_{Z_{1}}(1))=\dim V_{G}(\omega_{2r-1})

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{O}_{1}(1)|_{Z_{1}}=\mathcal{O}_{Z_{1}}(1)

It yields the spectral sequence

E_{1}^{-q,p}=H^{p}(F_{1},\wedge^{p}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{1}(1))\implies H^{p-q}(Z_{1},\mathcal{O}_{Z_{1}}(1)).

By the previous result, the only column on the first page which

Lemma 4.6
.

We have that
H^{\ast}(Z_{1},\mathcal{O}_{1}(1))

Proof.

By

It is a standard fact

\Lambda\colon\wedge^{k}\mathbb{C}^{6r-2}\rightarrow\wedge^{k-2}\mathbb{C}^{6r-2}

\Lambda(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k})=\sum_{i<j}\tau(v_{i},v_{j})(-1)^{i+j-1}v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\hat{v}_{i}\wedge\cdots\wedge\hat{v}_{j}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k},

where
\tau
is the standard non-degenerate, alternating bilinear form on
\mathbb{C}^{6r-2}
.
Hence,
\dim V_{G}(\omega_{1})=6r-2
while, for

\dim V_{G}(\omega_{k})=\binom{6r-2}{k}-\binom{6r-2}{k-2}.

Using this, we find that
\dim V_{G}(\omega_{2r})\neq\dim V_{G}(\omega_{2r-1})
,
proving the result.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

We have

Proof.

Again, recall that

\displaystyle s_{1}(\omega_{1})=-\omega_{1}+\omega_{2},\hskip 36.0001pts_{2}(\omega_{2})=\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}+2\omega_{3},
