Application of a Prevalence-Adjusted Cost-Effectiveness Threshold Framework for Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension
Authors: Adnan Alsumali, Vasco M. Pontinha, Benjamin G. Cohen, Meghan Levy, William V. Padula
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a rare, progressive condition associated with high per-patient treatment costs and substantial clinical burden. Interpreting cost-effectiveness evidence for rare diseases remains challenging when uniform thresholds are applied across conditions that differ markedly in prevalence and scale of healthcare production.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to estimate a prevalence-adjusted effective cost-effectiveness threshold for PAH and illustrate how disease prevalence and production scale influence the interpretation of cost-effectiveness evidence under a fixed health care budget.MethodsWe applied a previously developed prevalence-adjusted cost-effectiveness threshold framework, referred to as a generalized dynamic prevalence (GeDP) approach, to PAH using nationally representative disease prevalence data from the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey. A flexible statistical transformation was used to accommodate the highly right skewed distribution of disease prevalence and to estimate the relationship between prevalence and effective thresholds. The analysis was anchored to an empirically estimated US health opportunity cost benchmark and applied to PAH, with comparisons to selected common and rare diseases. Illustrative scenarios examined how effective thresholds evolve under changes in disease prevalence over time.ResultsEffective cost-effectiveness thresholds increased as disease prevalence declined, reflecting production-side scale effects rather than differences in societal preferences. For PAH (prevalence ≈ 0.013%), the prevalence-adjusted effective threshold was estimated at US$582,270 per QALY (95% CI 581,319–583,163), representing more than a five-fold increase relative to the reference opportunity-cost benchmark of US$104,000 per QALY applied to highly prevalent conditions. Dynamic scenarios showed that effective cost-effectiveness thresholds decline as prevalence changes over time, with larger adjustments observed for initially rare conditions.ConclusionApplying a uniform cost-effectiveness threshold across diseases implicitly assumes homogeneous production conditions and opportunity costs. Prevalence-adjusted effective thresholds derived under the GeDP framework provide a quantitative, descriptive approach for contextualizing cost-effectiveness evidence for rare diseases such as PAH without redefining societal willingness to pay or prescribing decision rules. This approach can complement existing pharmacoeconomic evaluations by improving transparency around the role of prevalence and scale in shaping opportunity costs.

Introduction

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a rare, progressive, and life-threatening condition characterized by elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, right heart failure, and high mortality [1, 2]. PAH prevalence is estimated at approximately 15–50 cases per million worldwide, with newly incident cases of only 2–5 per million annually in the US [3]. Patients face substantial morbidity and premature mortality, with median survival historically < 3 years prior to the advent of modern therapies. Even with treatment, PAH imposes a high burden of hospitalizations, impaired quality of life, and productivity loss. Annual direct medical costs for PAH are several-fold higher than for individuals without the condition [4–7], reflecting both costly specialty drug use and substantial healthcare utilization [8–10]. These features make PAH a salient case for examining how cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) evidence is interpreted in settings where per-patient costs are high but affected populations are small [11].

CEA is widely used to inform health technology assessment (HTA) and reimbursement decisions by comparing incremental costs and health outcomes against a decision threshold. In practice, such thresholds are often treated as fixed across diseases within a health system. This convention implicitly assumes that the opportunity cost of health care spending is homogeneous across conditions, regardless of differences in disease prevalence or scale of production. Recent work has emphasized that cost-effectiveness thresholds should be interpreted as reflecting health opportunity costs under constrained budgets, rather than as expressions of societal willingness to pay, per se [12, 13].

For rare diseases such as PAH, the application of uniform thresholds raises persistent questions in HTA. Treatments for rare conditions often involve substantial fixed costs related to research and development, clinical infrastructure, and specialized delivery, which must be recovered over relatively small patient populations [14, 15]. At the same time, total budget impact may remain limited due to low prevalence. These features suggest that the effective opportunity cost of adopting a new technology may differ systematically between rare and common conditions, even when societal preferences and values are held constant [16].

Recent methodological work has proposed that disease prevalence can serve as a key organizing variable for interpreting heterogeneity in effective cost-effectiveness thresholds. In this prevalence-adjusted threshold framework [17], referred to as a generalized dynamic prevalence (GeDP) approach, a single system-wide opportunity cost is preserved, while effective thresholds vary across diseases as a function of prevalence-related scale effects in health care production [8]. Importantly, this approach does not postulate disease-specific societal preferences, nor does it rely on severity weights or preference heterogeneity. Rather, it offers a descriptive way to translate an existing opportunity-cost benchmark into disease-specific effective thresholds implied by differences in production scale [8].

The objective of the present study is to apply this prevalence-adjusted threshold framework (i.e., GeDP) to PAH. Building on prior methodological work, we recalibrate the prevalence–threshold relationship using PAH-specific epidemiologic inputs and examine the implied effective cost-effectiveness threshold—defined as the prevalence-adjusted opportunity cost consistent with a fixed health care budget—for PAH in the United States. Our contribution is applied rather than methodological; we illustrate how prevalence adjustment operates in a concrete disease context and how it may inform interpretation of cost-effectiveness evidence for rare conditions.

To operationalize the analysis, we used nationally representative data on disease prevalence and applied a flexible statistical transformation to characterize the relationship between prevalence and effective thresholds. The transformation was employed solely as a statistical device to accommodate the highly skewed distribution of disease prevalence and carries no behavioral or normative interpretation. We then compared the implied PAH threshold with those associated with more prevalent conditions to provide context for HTA deliberation.

By focusing on PAH, this study contributes to the applied pharmacoeconomics literature in two ways. First, it demonstrates how prevalence-adjusted opportunity costs can be operationalized for a rare disease without redefining societal values or decision rules. Second, it provides a transparent framework for interpreting high per-patient costs in rare diseases within a fixed-budget setting. Throughout, thresholds are interpreted as prevalence-adjusted opportunity costs and should not be construed as disease-specific societal willingness to pay.

Methods

Analytical Framework

This study applies a previously developed prevalence-adjusted cost-effectiveness threshold framework, referred to as a GeDP approach, to PAH, aiming to recalibrate and adapt the existing adjustment to a specific rare disease context [8]. The analysis proceeds in three steps: (1) characterization of disease prevalence using nationally representative data; (2) statistical estimation of the relationship between prevalence and effective cost-effectiveness thresholds; and (3) application of the resulting adjustment to PAH.

Data Source and Prevalence Estimation

Disease prevalence estimates are derived from the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS), a nationally representative survey of the US civilian noninstitutionalized population administered by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. MEPS provides detailed information on diagnosed medical conditions, health care utilization, and expenditures, and is widely used in applied health economics and pharmacoeconomics research.

Medical conditions are identified using ICD-10 diagnostic codes from the MEPS medical conditions files. Disease prevalence is defined as the proportion of survey respondents reporting a given condition during the study years. In order to obtain more stable historical prevalence data, average prevalence estimates were derived from the MEPS conditions files from 2016 to 2022. PAH prevalence is estimated using ICD-10 codes corresponding to pulmonary arterial hypertension (I27.0) and related subtypes (I27.20, I27.21, I27.81, I27.89, I27.9), according to Watzker et al. [9]. Because PAH is rare and not reliably captured in MEPS, external epidemiologic estimates are used to characterize PAH prevalence. Specifically, published US-based prevalence estimates for PAH are used to identify the corresponding point in the empirical distribution of disease prevalence derived from MEPS [18, 19]. This mapping allows PAH to be situated within the observed prevalence distribution without relying on unstable or underreported MEPS estimates, while preserving the overall distributional properties used to estimate the prevalence–threshold relationship. Population counts are then scaled to US Census estimates to obtain prevalence levels in absolute and percentage terms.

Prevalence-Adjusted Threshold Framework

The prevalence-adjusted threshold framework builds on prior work that established a systematic relationship between disease prevalence and effective cost-effectiveness thresholds under a fixed health care budget [8]. In this framework, a single system-wide reference threshold—interpreted as the marginal opportunity cost of health care spending—is preserved, while effective thresholds vary across diseases as a function of prevalence-related scale effects in health care production.

In the present study, this framework is treated as given. Our focus is on applying the prevalence adjustment to PAH rather than on re-deriving the theoretical foundations of the approach. Throughout, effective thresholds are interpreted as prevalence-adjusted opportunity costs and not as expressions of disease-specific societal willingness to pay.

Statistical Specification and Box-Cox Transformation

Observed disease prevalence exhibits a highly skewed distribution, with a small number of very common conditions and a long tail of rare diseases. To accommodate this skewness and to allow flexible estimation of the prevalence–threshold relationship, we applied a Box-Cox transformation to prevalence values prior to estimation.

The Box-Cox transformation is used solely as a statistical device to improve model fit and stabilize variance. Specifically, this mathematical step was required to ensure normalization of the distribution of medical conditions while satisfying the assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity needed for linearization between thresholds and disease prevalence rates. It does not carry a behavioral, epidemiologic, or normative interpretation, nor does it imply any assumptions about preferences or risk attitudes. The transformation parameter is selected using standard goodness-of-fit criteria, including inspection of histograms and quantile–quantile plots, following established statistical practice.

The transformed prevalence values are then used to estimate a smooth adjustment function that maps disease prevalence to effective cost-effectiveness thresholds. The function is normalized such that, for highly prevalent conditions, the effective threshold converges to the reference opportunity-cost threshold. For low-prevalence conditions, the adjustment reflects the increasing contribution of fixed costs to average production costs.

Reference Threshold and Calibration

To implement the prevalence adjustment, we require a reference cost-effectiveness threshold reflecting the marginal opportunity cost of health care spending in the United States. We adopt the health opportunity cost estimate reported by Vanness and colleagues, US$104,000 per QALY (95% CI 51,000–209,000], as the primary benchmark, which is based on observed relationships between health care spending and mortality outcomes [13]. This benchmark is used as a calibration point rather than as a normative standard. Anchoring the system-wide opportunity cost described in Vanness and colleagues is a theoretically valid approach that mirrors recent cost/value methodology statements [20, 21].

Effective thresholds for PAH are obtained by applying the estimated prevalence-adjustment function to the reference value presented by Kirson and colleagues [18]. Specifically, the PAH prevalence estimate is entered into the fitted prevalence–threshold function derived from the MEPS-based distribution of disease prevalence and calibrated to the reference opportunity-cost benchmark. Differences between the PAH-specific threshold and the reference threshold arise mechanically from prevalence and scale, rather than from disease severity, equity weights, or societal value judgments. Representative prevalence levels and corresponding effective thresholds are reported for selected conditions to illustrate the implied relationship.

Dynamic Prevalence Scenarios

In addition to the cross-sectional analysis, we consider illustrative scenarios in which PAH prevalence changes over time. These scenarios are intended to demonstrate how effective thresholds may evolve as disease prevalence responds to innovation, improved survival, or preventive interventions. Prevalence trajectories are stylized and are not intended as forecasts.

Dynamic adjustments are interpreted consistently with the static framework; changes in effective thresholds reflect changes in production scale and opportunity cost rather than changes in preferences or willingness to pay.

Statistical Analysis Plan

Previously, Padula and Pontinha reported effective thresholds for other rare and common conditions [8]. We formally tested the hypothesis that the prevalence-adjusted effective threshold for PAH exceeds the reference opportunity cost benchmark. Specifically, we conducted a two-sided t-test (p < 0.05) comparing the estimated PAH effective threshold with the US$104,000/QALY references value reported by Vanness et al. [13].

We also conducted pairwise comparisons between the PAH effective threshold and effective thresholds estimated for selected common and rare conditions to assess whether PAH lies statistically above the central tendency of the prevalence-adjusted distribution. Finally, we implemented a fitted curve based on observed prevalence data distribution to estimate the respective opportunity costs, for conditions exhibiting prevalence rates outside of the ranges observed in MEPS.

All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA 19 BE and MATLAB r2024b [22, 23]. This analysis is intentionally limited in scope. GeDP implementation does not account for disease severity, patient heterogeneity, or preference-based value modifiers, nor does it prescribe decision rules for reimbursement. Instead, it provides an applied illustration of how prevalence adjustment can inform interpretation of cost-effectiveness evidence for a rare disease within an opportunity-cost framework.

Within this framework, PAH occupies the lower-prevalence region of the distribution and is associated with an effective threshold substantially higher than the reference system-wide opportunity costs reference value (Fig. 3). Based on the fitted curve, the effective threshold for a PAH prevalence of 0.013% is approximately US$582,270/QALY (95% CI 581,319–583,163). This expected value for PAH is significantly greater by over 550% than the societal anchor of US$104,000/QALY reported by Vanness and colleagues (p < 0.0001). The fitted prevalence-threshold relationship exhibited a coefficient of determination of R2 = 0.9999, with an average residual of ±US$107.95/QALY. These results reflect the limited scale over which fixed costs are distributed in PAH treatment.

Comparison with Select Common and Rare Diseases

To provide context, Table 1 reports prevalence-adjusted effective thresholds for a set of selected diseases spanning a wide range of prevalence. Highly prevalent chronic conditions, such as hypercholesterolemia, exhibit effective thresholds close to the reference opportunity cost. Conditions of intermediate prevalence, including hepatitis C virus infection and colorectal cancer, show moderately higher effective thresholds.

In contrast, rare diseases, including sickle cell disease, cystic fibrosis, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, and hemophilia A, are associated with substantially higher effective thresholds. These differences arise mechanically from disease prevalence and scale effects rather than from differences in disease severity or societal values. The comparison illustrates how a single system-wide opportunity cost can translate into heterogeneous effective thresholds once differences in prevalence are considered. Notably, conditions like hepatitis C are much less prevalent than hypercholesterolemia or hypertension, yet the difference in effective thresholds is only ~US$22,000/QALY. This feature is an advantageous aspect of GeDP, as opportunity costs derive directly from the distribution of medical conditions in a given population, rather than from a forced linear relationship between prevalence and opportunity costs.

Under these scenarios, effective thresholds increase as prevalence decreases, reflecting changes in the scale of health care production and associated opportunity costs compared with the remaining unmet medical need. The magnitude of the adjustment is larger for conditions that are initially rare, highlighting the sensitivity of effective thresholds to prevalence dynamics in low-prevalence settings.

These dynamic results are illustrative rather than predictive. They demonstrate how changes in epidemiologic context can affect the interpretation of cost-effectiveness evidence over time, even when the underlying system-wide opportunity cost remains unchanged.

Across all analyses, differences in effective thresholds are driven by disease prevalence and production scale rather than by preference heterogeneity or normative value judgments. Effective thresholds should therefore be interpreted as prevalence-adjusted opportunity costs. They provide a descriptive lens for understanding variation in cost-effectiveness benchmarks across diseases and over time, rather than prescriptive rules for coverage or reimbursement decisions.

Discussion

This study applied a prevalence-adjusted cost-effectiveness threshold framework, referred to as a GeDP approach, to PAH to illustrate how disease prevalence and production scale can shape the interpretation of cost-effectiveness evidence under a fixed health care budget. Using nationally representative prevalence data and an empirically calibrated opportunity-cost benchmark, the analysis shows that PAH is associated with an effective cost-effectiveness threshold substantially higher than those implied for more prevalent conditions.

These findings speak directly to ongoing challenges in the pharmacoeconomic evaluation of rare diseases. Conventional application of a uniform cost-effectiveness threshold across all conditions implicitly assumes homogeneous production conditions and opportunity costs. Our results demonstrate that this assumption may not hold when disease prevalence varies widely across conditions.

The dynamic prevalence scenarios further illustrate how effective thresholds may evolve over time. As disease prevalence changes in response to innovation, improved survival, or prevention, the scale of health care production changes accordingly. In such settings, static thresholds may mischaracterize opportunity costs, particularly for rare diseases subject to rapid epidemiologic change. Although the dynamic scenarios presented here are illustrative rather than predictive, they underscore the importance of considering prevalence trends when interpreting cost-effectiveness evidence.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the analysis does not incorporate disease severity, patient heterogeneity, or preference-based value modifiers. These dimensions are conceptually distinct from prevalence and scale and are intentionally held constant in order to isolate production-side effects.

Second, the prevalence-adjustment function is empirically calibrated and depends on functional-form assumptions chosen for tractability and statistical fit. While the qualitative relationship between prevalence and effective thresholds is consistent across model specifications, the precise magnitude of the adjustment is uncertain and may vary across health systems. Third, the reference opportunity-cost benchmark of US$104,000 per QALY is treated as a calibration input rather than estimated within the model.

Despite these limitations, the results have practical implications for the interpretation of pharmacoeconomic evidence in rare diseases. Prevalence-adjusted thresholds offer a descriptive lens for understanding why cost-effectiveness benchmarks applied to PAH may differ from those used for more prevalent conditions, without redefining societal willingness to pay or prescribing decision rules. Interpreted in this way, effective thresholds reflect prevalence-adjusted opportunity costs rather than disease-specific valuations. Used in this way, the framework can complement existing health technology assessment processes by improving transparency around the role of prevalence and scale in shaping opportunity costs.

Conclusion

This study provides an applied, empirical illustration of how disease prevalence and production scale can be incorporated into the interpretation of cost-effectiveness evidence under a fixed health care budget. Using pulmonary arterial hypertension as a case study, we show that low-prevalence conditions can be associated with effective cost-effectiveness thresholds substantially higher than those implied for more prevalent diseases when a single system-wide opportunity-cost benchmark is translated through prevalence-related scale effects. Importantly, these differences arise mechanically from production conditions rather than from disease-specific societal preferences or normative value judgments.

The findings have direct implications for health technology assessment and reimbursement decision making. HTA bodies such as the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review (ICER) in the United States and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in the United Kingdom have acknowledged that higher cost-effectiveness thresholds may be appropriate for rare or severe diseases, with ICER citing values up to US$500,000 per QALY in selected contexts and NICE allowing higher thresholds for highly specialized technologies [24, 25]. To date, however, these practices have largely been justified through deliberative or ethical considerations rather than empirical analysis. The prevalence-adjusted approach illustrated here offers a transparent, data-driven set of guardrails for implementing such policies in a manner that remains grounded in opportunity cost and budget constraint, rather than ad-hoc threshold adjustment.

By providing an empirical approach to contextualize higher effective thresholds for rare diseases, this analysis complements existing HTA frameworks without redefining societal willingness-to-pay or prescribing decision rules. Used alongside deliberative processes, prevalence-adjusted effective thresholds can help HTA bodies interpret cost-effectiveness evidence more consistently across diseases, improving transparency and coherence in decisions where per-patient costs are high, but overall budget impact remains limited.
