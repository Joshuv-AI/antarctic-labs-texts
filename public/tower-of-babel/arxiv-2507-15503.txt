Harnessing higher-dimensional fluctuations in an information engine
Antonio Patrón Castro, John Bechhoefer, David A. Sivak

Abstract
We study the optimal performance of an information engine consisting of an overdamped Brownian bead confined in a controllable, d d -dimensional harmonic trap and additionally subjected to gravity. The trap’s center is updated dynamically via a feedback protocol designed such that no external work is done by the trap on the bead, while maximizing the extraction of gravitational potential energy and achieving directed motion. We show that performance strikingly improves when thermal fluctuations in directions perpendicular to gravity are harnessed. This improvement arises from feedback cooling of these transverse degrees of freedom, along which all heat is extracted; comparable performance can be achieved even without vertical measurements. This engine design modularizes the functions of harnessing fluctuations and storing free energy, drawing a close analogy to the Szilard engine.



More than a century ago, Maxwell proposed his famous thought experiment in a letter to Tait, suggesting that information about a system’s microscopic dynamics could be used to extract useful energy without any work input – seemingly in violation of the second law of thermodynamics
Knott 1911
. In the 1930s, Szilard refined this idea by introducing the first concrete model of what is now known as an
information engine

Szilard 1929
;
Szilard 1964
: a cyclic device that exploits thermal fluctuations by applying feedback, thereby extracting heat from a thermal bath. Its operation is reconciled with the second law by Landauer’s principle: processing and erasing information about a system’s dynamics has a minimum cost
Landauer 1961
;
Bennett 1982
.

Recent advances in technology and stochastic thermodynamics
Sekimoto 1997
;
Sekimoto 2010
;
Seifert 2012
;
den Broeck and Esposito 2015
;
Seifert 2025
have enabled the experimental realization of modern information engines
Toyabe et al. 2010
;
Camati et al. 2016
;
Koski et al. 2015
;
Cottet et al. 2017
;
Masuyama et al. 2018
;
Koski et al. 2014a
;
Chida et al. 2017
;
Admon et al. 2018
;
Goerlich et al. 2025
;
Baldovin et al. 2024
. This capability has been used to test Landauer’s principle and quantify the cost of information processing
Bérut et al. 2012
;
Jun et al. 2014
;
Koski et al. 2014b
;
Hong et al. 2016
;
Ciliberto 2021
;
Dago et al. 2021
;
Archambault et al. 2025
, cool nanoparticles to millikelvin temperatures
Li et al. 2011
;
Gieseler et al. 2012
;
Tebbenjohanns et al. 2019
, and confirm the limits of the second law
Barros et al. 2024
;
Paneru et al. 2020
;
Klinger and Rotskoff 2025
. Inspired by the ideas of Maxwell, Szilard, and Landauer, researchers have even constructed molecular-scale information engines, demonstrating that synthetic cyclic molecular machines can indeed leverage fluctuations to power their operation
Serreli et al. 2007
. More recently, it has been suggested that many of the molecular motors operating within living cells (such as ATP synthase or kinesin) may also work as information engines, harnessing energy from the noisy cellular environment

;
Leighton et al. 2024
;
Leighton and Sivak 2025
;

;
Parrondo et al. 2015

In previous work
du Buisson et al. 2024
, Saha, et al. designed and experimentally realized an information engine that, in addition to extracting energy from a thermal bath, stored such energy in a gravitational potential by raising a weight. Their engine consisted of a micron-scale bead in water, harmonically confined via optical tweezers
Lucero et al. 2021
;
Saha and Bechhoefer 2021
. The trap center was raised upon measuring a favorable ‘up’ fluctuation of the bead (parallel to gravity). Subsequent studies explored performance for Bayesian inference of the bead’s position under noisy measurements
Saha et al. 2022
and for nonequilibrium active noise
Saha et al. 2023
;
Paneru et al. 2022
;
Paneru et al. 2018a
.

To date, information engines reported in the literature have been essentially one-dimensional, exploiting fluctuations along a single degree of freedom. Can harnessing fluctuations along additional degrees of freedom further improve performance? In this work, we generalize a theoretical study of the experimental engine of
Saha et al. 2021
to
d
dimensions and find striking increases in the rate of energy extraction and related measures of performance.

We show that the performance enhancement results from feedback cooling of thermal fluctuations along the transverse degrees of freedom, thereby extracting
all
available heat, since all such fluctuations are favorable. We demonstrate that feedback on the transverse degrees of freedom alone can produce high output power, by analyzing an engine variant in which we do not measure the vertical
z
component. This engine design separates the essential functions of harnessing fluctuations and storing free energy, capturing a core feature of the original Szilard engine.
These results highlight the potential of higher-dimensional fluctuations as a valuable resource in the design of information engines.

Multidimensional information engine
—Consider an optically trapped bead in
d
spatial dimensions, whose dynamics are governed by the overdamped Langevin equation

\gamma\,\dot{\bm{r}}^{\prime}(t^{\prime})=-\kappa[\bm{r}^{\prime}(t^{\prime})-\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}(t^{\prime})]-m\bm{g}+\sqrt{2k_{\rm B}T\gamma}\ \bm{\xi}(t^{\prime})\ ,

(1)

for bead position
\bm{r}^{\prime}(t^{\prime})
at time
t^{\prime}
, isotropic harmonic trap center
\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}(t^{\prime})
, trap stiffness
\kappa
, bead mass
m
relative to the surrounding fluid, gravitational acceleration
\bm{g}
,
friction coefficient
\gamma
, Boltzmann constant
k_{\rm B}
, and thermal-bath temperature
T
.
\bm{\xi}(t^{\prime})
is a vector of Gaussian white-noise fluctuations of zero mean and correlations

\left<\xi^{(i)}(t_{1}^{\prime})\,\xi^{(j)}(t_{2}^{\prime})\right>=\delta_{ij}\,\delta(t_{1}^{\prime}-t_{2}^{\prime}),\quad\forall\,i,j=1,...,d\ .

(2)

We nondimensionalize the problem by scaling lengths with the equilibrium standard deviation
\sigma_{\text{eq}}\equiv\sqrt{k_{\rm B}T/\kappa}
of the bead’s position and time with the relaxation time
\tau_{\rm r}\equiv\kappa/\gamma
. This yields the dimensionless variables
t\equiv t^{\prime}/\tau_{\rm r},\bm{r}(t)\equiv\bm{r}^{\prime}(t^{\prime})/\sigma_{\text{eq}}
and
\bm{\lambda}(t)\equiv\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}(t^{\prime})/\sigma_{\text{eq}}
, and
the dimensionless overdamped Langevin equation

\dot{\bm{r}}(t)=-[\bm{r}(t)-\bm{\lambda}(t)]-\bm{\delta_{\rm g}}+\sqrt{2}\ \bm{\xi}(t)\ ,

(3)

where
\bm{\delta_{\rm g}}\equiv m\bm{g}/\kappa\sigma_{\text{eq}}
is a dimensionless vector quantifying the gravitational force relative to the amplitude of thermal fluctuations. Figure
1
illustrates the system for
d\!=\!2
.

Schematic for
d\!=\!2
: a Brownian bead (blue dot) at position

\bm{\lambda}

The total, time-dependent potential for the bead results from the combination of the optical trap and the gravitational force,

\displaystyle V(\bm{r},\bm{\lambda}(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|\bm{r}-\bm{\lambda}(t)|^{2}+\bm{r}^{\top}\bm{\delta_{\rm g}}\ .

(4)

The bead’s position is measured at discrete time intervals of duration
t_{\rm s}
, and the feedback on the trap position is applied immediately. Integrating Eq. (
1
) over one time step provides the discrete-time dynamics
Saha et al. 2021
;
Kloeden and Platen 1992
,

\bm{r}_{n+1}=\e^{-t_{\rm s}}\bm{r}_{n}+(1-\e^{-t_{\rm s}})(\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}-\bm{\delta_{\rm g}})+\sqrt{1-\e^{-2t_{\rm s}}}\ \bm{\nu}_{n}\ ,

(5)

for timestep number
n
and vector
\bm{\nu}_{n}
of Gaussian random variables with zero mean and correlations

\left<\nu_{n\phantom{{}^{\prime}}}^{(i)}\,\nu_{n^{\prime}\phantom{{}^{\prime}}}^{(j)}\right>=\delta_{ij}\,\delta_{nn^{\prime}},\quad\forall\,i,j=1,...,d\ .

(6)

The subscript
n^{+}
indicates that
\bm{\lambda}
is (ideally, instantaneously) updated
after
measuring
\bm{r}
. To simplify analysis and aid physical interpretation, we choose an orthogonal coordinate system such that
\bm{r}=(x^{(1)},x^{(2)},\dots,x^{(d-1)},z)
and
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda^{(1)},\lambda^{(2)},\dots,\lambda^{(d-1)},\lambda^{(z)})
, for
z
the degree of freedom parallel to the gravitational force. The discrete Langevin equation (
5
) becomes

\displaystyle z_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\e^{-t_{\rm s}}z_{n}+(1-\e^{-t_{\rm s}})(\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}-\delta_{\rm g})+\sqrt{1-\e^{-2t_{\rm s}}}\,\nu_{n}^{(z)}

(7a)

\displaystyle x_{n+1}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\e^{-t_{\rm s}}x_{n}^{(i)}+(1-\e^{-t_{\rm s}})\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(i)}+\sqrt{1-\e^{-2t_{\rm s}}}\,\nu_{n}^{(i)}\ ,

(7b)

with
i=1,2,\dots,d-1
. Equations (
7
) show that
z
is influenced by both the gravitational force and the harmonic trap, whereas the remaining degrees of freedom are only driven by the harmonic trap. Throughout, we refer to
\{x^{(i)}\}
and
\{\lambda^{(i)}\}
as the transverse components of the bead and trap’s position, respectively, which live on the perpendicular
(d-1)
-dimensional space
\mathbb{R}_{d-1}
.

After updating bead position
\bm{r}_{n}
to
\bm{r}_{n+1}
, the trap position
\bm{\lambda}
is updated according to a feedback algorithm, chosen (following
Saha et al. 2021
) to optimize two performance metrics. The first metric is the net output power

P_{\text{net}}=f_{\rm s}\left(\left<\Delta F\right>-\left<W\right>\right)\ ,

(8)

for sampling frequency
f_{\rm s}\equiv 1/t_{\rm s}
, and per-measurement average stored equilibrium free energy
\left<\Delta F\right>
and work
\left<W\right>
done on the bead. The averages are taken over the system probability distribution at steady state. Here, work is the instantaneous change in the total potential when the trap center
\bm{\lambda}
is updated,

\displaystyle W_{n+1}

\displaystyle\equiv V(\bm{r}_{n+1},\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1})-V(\bm{r}_{n+1},\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}})

(9a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|\bm{r}_{n+1}-\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|\bm{r}_{n+1}-\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}|^{2}\ .

(9b)

By convention, the work is positive if energy flows into the system from the harmonic trap, and negative if it flows out. Similarly, the average stored equilibrium free energy is

\left<\Delta F\right>=\delta_{\rm g}\left<\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}\right>\ .

(10)

From the first law of thermodynamics for the bead at steady state, the net output power

P_{\text{net}}
equals the rate of heat extraction from the environment
du Buisson et al. 2024
. This follows from the assumption that no energy is exchanged between the bead and the controller, such that all stored energy is in the form of gravitational free energy. We focus on pure information engines, where exactly no work is done by each trap movement, i.e.,
W_{n+1}=0
. As a result, these engines store useful free energy by exploiting thermal fluctuations alone: heat is continuously extracted from the thermal bath without any external work input.

The feedback algorithm is then chosen in such a way that (i) no work is done by the harmonic trap on the system and (ii) the stored free energy is maximized (Appendix
A
has details), giving

\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}=\bm{r}_{n+1}+|\bm{r}_{n+1}-\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}|\hat{\bm{z}}\ ,

(11)

for unit vector
\hat{\bm{z}}
. The zero-work condition
W_{n+1}=0
requires
\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}
to lie on a hypersphere centered at
\bm{r}_{n+1}
with radius
|\bm{r}_{n+1}-\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}|
, and the free-energy maximization further restricts
\hat{\bm{z}}
to lie at the top of this hypersphere. Figure
2
illustrates both the zero-work condition and the optimal feedback rule (
11
) for a
d\!=\!2
pure information engine. It shows how a fluctuation with a downward
z
-component can nonetheless be combined with a lateral fluctuation to lift the equilibrium trap position.

z

\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}

Resolving the vertical and transverse components gives

\displaystyle\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}

\displaystyle=z_{n+1}+\sqrt{(z_{n+1}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)})^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left(\Delta x_{n+1}^{(j)}\right)^{2}}

(12a)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=x_{n+1}^{(i)},\quad i=1,2,...,d-1\ ,

(12b)

with bead update
\Delta x_{n+1}^{(j)}\equiv x_{n+1}^{(j)}-x_{n}^{(j)}=x_{n+1}^{(j)}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(j)}
equaling the bead position relative to the trap center. The trap’s
z
-component stores gravitational free energy, while the transverse components track the bead’s transverse position (so transverse fluctuations are isotropic), effectively enacting a
feedback cooling
protocol that maximizes heat extraction in Brownian engines
Lee et al. 2018
. Any gain in potential energy due to transverse fluctuations is transferred to stored free energy during the subsequent trap update.

The second performance metric is the long-time average
z
-velocity,
\left<v_{z}\right>
, which for a pure information engine is proportional to the output power
Saha et al. 2021
:

P_{\text{net}}=\delta_{\rm g}\left<v_{z}\right>\ .

(13)

This metric is directly accessible in experiments and quantifies the information engine’s ability to generate directed motion against the load from an external force.

Optimal performance
—We first examine the dependence of performance on the sampling frequency
f_{\rm s}
for a fixed value
\delta_{\rm g}=0.8
, chosen because it was previously shown to (approximately) maximize the output power for
d\!=\!1

Saha et al. 2021
. In Fig.
3
, we compare the performance of a
d\!=\!2
engine with the previously characterized
d\!=\!1
engine (Fig. 3A in
Saha et al. 2021
). The two engines exhibit similar behavior. At high sampling frequencies, the output power saturates, indicating that increasing the measurement rate does not lead to further heat extraction. In this regime, the trap-center dynamics approximately follow a Langevin-like equation, which leads to an analytical expression for the maximum output power,

P_{\text{net}}^{\text{HF}}(\delta_{\rm g})=(d-1)\delta_{\rm g}\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{d-1}(\delta_{\rm g})}{\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\delta_{\rm g})}\ ,

(14)

for partition function

\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\delta_{\rm g})\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{L}\,L^{d-1}\,\e^{-L^{2}/2+\delta_{\rm g}L}\ ,

(15)

expressable in terms of hypergeometric functions [App.
B
]. Equation (
14

d\to 1
, recovering the
d\!=\!1
expression for the output power from
Saha et al. 2021
.

At low sampling frequencies
f_{\rm s}
, ratchet events occur with frequency proportional to
f_{\rm s}
, as the bead’s position equilibrates between measurements. Consequently, the average stored equilibrium free energy in Eq. (
10
) reaches a constant value, so the output power is linear in

[App.
C
]. The transition between the two limiting behaviors takes place around
f_{\rm s}=1
, for which the sampling time
t_{\rm s}
is of the same order as the relaxation time
\tau_{\rm r}
. Despite the similar trends, the
d\!=\!2
engine nearly doubles the output power attained by its
d\!=\!1
counterpart.

The comparison between the performances of the
d\!=\!2
and
d\!=\!1
information engines already suggests that fluctuations in degrees of freedom perpendicular to gravity can be exploited to enhance heat extraction. We now investigate whether it is more advantageous to exploit all such transverse fluctuations or only a subset. To address this, we introduce a modified feedback rule:

\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}=\begin{cases}\bm{r}_{n+1}+|\bm{r}_{n+1}-\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}|\hat{\bm{z}}\ ,&\!\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left(\Delta x_{n+1}^{(j)}\right)^{2}\geq R^{2}\\
\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}\ ,&\text{otherwise}\ .\end{cases}

(16)

That is, the trap center is updated only when at the time of measurement the bead lies outside a cylinder of radius
R
, centered at the trap and aligned with the
z
-axis. We study the dependence of the output power on
R
in the high-sampling-frequency limit, for which we have already shown that the performance is optimal. In this limit, the output power can be interpreted as the ratio of extracted work to the first passage time required for the bead to reach the cylinder. Two competing effects arise as a function of the threshold
R
. On one hand, the extracted work per ratchet event increases with
R
: the larger the threshold, the greater the radius at which the zero-work condition is met, and thus the more work is extracted. On the other hand, the mean first-passage time also increases with
R
, since the bead must travel a greater distance before triggering a trap update
Bray et al. 2013
;
Archambault et al. 2025
[App.
E
]. Figure
4
shows the output power in the high-sampling-frequency limit as a function of
R
. The output power decreases monotonically with
R
, maximized at
R=0
. That is, for any positive threshold, the gain in extracted work per ratchet event is outweighed by the increased mean first-passage time between ratchet events. Therefore, the optimal strategy is to exploit
all
available transverse fluctuations.

Having shown that continuous sampling and continuous ratcheting optimize performance, we study the dependence of both the output power and the velocity on the dimensionless force
\delta_{\rm g}
. For
d\!=\!1
, the optimal output power at
\delta_{\rm g}^{*}\!\approx\!0.8
arises from two competing factors: On the one hand, as we increase
\delta_{\rm g}
from

, the potential energy to extract increases. On the other hand, the magnitude of the opposing force also increases, such that it becomes more unlikely for the bead to move significantly upward. This competition leads to maximum output power at an intermediate
\delta_{\rm g}
.

Surprisingly, the nonmonotonic dependence on
\delta_{\rm g}
is not seen in higher dimensions. Figure
5

\delta_{\rm g}
, for different dimensions. On one hand, the output power is maximized in the
\delta_{\rm g}\to\infty

. On the other hand, while the velocity still decays to zero as
\delta_{\rm g}

(17)

\displaystyle P_{\text{net}}^{\text{HF}}(\delta_{\rm g})

\displaystyle\sim d-1\ .

(18)

The fact that the output power asymptotically approaches
d\!-\!1
(in dimensionless units) for large
\delta_{\rm g}
has striking implications. The output power is bounded above by the value attained under feedback cooling—where the trap follows the bead after each measurement, thereby extracting all the thermal fluctuations in potential energy. For feedback cooling, the power extracted by each independent degree of freedom is
1

Lee et al. 2018
, and hence the overall power for
d-1
feedback-cooled degrees of freedom equals
d-1
. Our findings suggest that our information engine extracts the maximum possible power from the
d\!-\!1
transverse degrees of freedom, while the
z
-component contributes no significant heat extraction, since in this limit useful up-fluctuations against gravity become very unlikely. This interpretation is supported by the explicit component-wise form of the feedback rule in Eq. (
11
): the
z
degree of freedom adjusts to satisfy the zero-work condition, whereas the transverse degrees of freedom follow the feedback-cooling protocol, thereby maximizing heat extraction along those directions.

Ignoring vertical fluctuations
—In the previous section, we showed that, in the
\delta_{\rm g}\gg 1
limit, the output power of a
d
-dimensional information engine is equal to feedback cooling of the
d\!-\!1
degrees of freedom perpendicular to the gravitational force. The
z
-component fluctuations, parallel to gravity, do not contribute to the engine’s performance. This observation raises a natural question: Is it necessary to measure the
z
-component in this regime, or is it sufficient to measure only the transverse components? To address this, we consider a modified version of the engine (the
partial
information engine) in which the feedback rule depends only on measurements of the
d\!-\!1
transverse degrees of freedom, ignoring the
z
-component of the bead’s position. For clarity, we refer to the previously studied system as the
complete
information engine.

Since we do not measure the
z
-component, the zero-work condition
W_{n+1}=0
cannot be rigorously fulfilled at each time step. Instead, we design a feedback rule for a pure information engine such that the average work
\left<W_{n+1}\right>
is zero. This dictates a feedback rule of the form

\displaystyle\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}=

\displaystyle\ \lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}+\left<z_{n+1}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}\right>

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\left<z_{n+1}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}\right>^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left(\Delta x_{n+1}^{(j)}\right)^{2}}

(19a)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(i)}=

\displaystyle\ x_{n+1}^{(i)},\quad i=1,2,...,d-1\ ,

(19b)

where the averages are taken over the steady-state distribution for the relative displacements
z_{n+1}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}
. Since we do not know this steady-state distribution
a priori
, we consider the general family of feedback rules
parametrized by
\omega
,

\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}=\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}+\omega+\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left(\Delta x_{n+1}^{(j)}\right)^{2}}\ ,

(20)

then compute the average
\left<z_{n+1}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}\right>
and set
\omega
to match the feedback rule (
19
). In this context,
\omega
constitutes the (initially unknown) average change of the
z
-component of the trap-bead displacement. In Appendix
F
, we derive a self-consistency equation for
\omega
, leading to an analytical expression for the output power in the limit of high sampling frequencies,

P_{\text{net}}^{\text{HF}}(\delta_{\rm g})=\frac{2(d-1)}{1+\sqrt{1+4(d-1)/\delta_{\rm g}^{2}}}\ .

(21)

Figure
5
a shows that the complete and the partial information engines attain the same output power in the
\delta_{\rm g}\to\infty
limit, consistent with our previous result that in that limit the
z
fluctuations do not contribute to the free-energy extraction. For intermediate values of
\delta_{\rm g}
, Fig.
5
a shows that measuring
z
has some small advantage, permitting the harnessing of some
z
fluctuations. But even in the simplest scenario, measuring a single transverse degree of freedom (
x
for the
d\!=\!2
partial information engine) entails a remarkable gain in output power compared to measuring
z
(as in the original
d\!=\!1
engine).

Conclusions
—We have studied the theoretical performance of an information engine that harnesses thermal fluctuations in a
d
-dimensional overdamped system to achieve directed motion against gravity, thus converting heat from the thermal bath into stored gravitational potential energy. Following a systematic study of the parameters that optimize the engine’s output power and velocity, we have shown that our
d\!>\!1
-dimensional engine significantly outperforms the
d\!=\!1
engine from previous studies
Saha et al. 2021
;
Lucero et al. 2021
.

The mechanism behind this enhancement is intimately related to feedback cooling: the
d-1
transverse degrees of freedom each extract
k_{\rm B}T/\tau_{\rm r}
—the maximum amount of heat available per dimension—whereas heat extraction in the
z
-component is limited by gravity. In fact, in the
\delta_{\rm g}\to\infty
limit, the output power saturates to
d-1
(in units of
k_{\rm B}T/\tau_{\rm r}
), implying that operation is sustained only by the transverse degrees of freedom. This is further confirmed by the partial information engine considered in Eq. (
19
): even without measurements of the bead’s
z
-component, the engine still achieves the same limiting performance for large
\delta_{\rm g}
, with minor deviations from the complete engine for intermediate values of
\delta_{\rm g}
. These results suggest that choosing which degrees of freedom to measure may play a dominant role in the overall engine performance. This is similar to the problem of identifying suitable reaction coordinates for efficient enhanced sampling of free-energy landscapes in complex molecular systems
Ma and Li 2025
;
Louwerse and Sivak 2022
. Thus, our work provides a design principle that could guide the next-generation of nanoscale energy-harvesting devices
Kolchinsky et al. 2025
;
Lucente et al. 2025
;
Olsen et al. 2025
.

In this work, we have focused on maximizing engine performance, without considering the information costs that result from nearly continuous measurement of the bead’s position. An important direction for future work involves the experimental realization of the
d\!=\!2
and
d\!=\!3
engines based on nanoscale Brownian objects confined in optical traps, which would build on and extend the results reported in
Saha et al. 2021
;
Saha et al. 2022
;
Lucero et al. 2021
. Further research could also consider the measurement and information-processing costs of the measuring devices and uncertainties in position and time measurements, and how these affect the overall performance
Toyabe et al. 2010
;
Ribezzi-Crivellari and Ritort 2019
;
Admon et al. 2018
;
Paneru et al. 2018b
;
Still 2020
. The high-sampling-frequency limit (where we measure much faster than the system’s characteristic dynamical time scales) pays large information costs compared to the harnessable benefits of that information. The partial information engine from Eq.
19
is thus more efficient, gathering and processing less information to attain similar heat extraction.

On a broader note, it would be interesting to explore the role of higher-dimensional fluctuations in different information engines involving more complex physical mechanisms. Promising directions include underdamped dynamics
Dago et al. 2021
;
Dago and Bellon 2022
;
Dago et al. 2023
;
Archambault et al. 2024
;
Sanders et al. 2024
;
Sanders et al. 2025
, where it has already been shown that applying feedback to velocity measurements improves engine control
Bechhoefer 2021
;
Kim and Qian 2004
, and active noise, where the nonequilibrium bath serves as an additional source of fluctuations
Malgaretti and Stark 2022
;
Cocconi and Chen 2024
;
Datta et al. 2022
;
Lee et al. 2020
;
Garcia-Millan et al. 2025
;
Paneru et al. 2022
, giving information engines a potential advantage over conventional heat engines.

Appendix A
Zero-work condition and optimal feedback rule

Here we derive the zero-work condition and the optimal feedback rule (
11
) for a pure information engine in
d
-dimensions, as described in
Multi-dimensional information engine
in the main text. We recall from there that work is defined as the change in the total potential due to the update of the center of the harmonic trap (
9a
).

The zero-work (pure information engine) condition is thus

W_{n+1}=0\ \iff\ |\bm{r}_{n+1}-\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}|=|\bm{r}_{n+1}-\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}|\ .

(22)

Thus
\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}
lies on a hypersphere centered at
\bm{r}_{n+1}
with radius
|\bm{r}_{n+1}-\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}|
. Contrary to the one-dimensional information engine from
Saha et al. 2021
, here there are infinitely many possible positions for the updated trap center
\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}
. In the following, we choose the feedback rule for
\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}
to maximize the extracted gravitational free energy.

Given a fixed trap center
\bm{\lambda}
, the equilibrium free energy is

F(\bm{\lambda})\equiv\left<V(\bm{r},\bm{\lambda})+\log\pi(\bm{r}|\bm{\lambda})\right>_{\pi(\bm{r}|\bm{\lambda})}\ ,

(23)

for canonical equilibrium distribution

\pi(\bm{r}|\bm{\lambda})\equiv\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}(\bm{\lambda})}\e^{-V(\bm{r},\bm{\lambda})}

(24)

and the corresponding partition function

\mathcal{Z}(\bm{\lambda})\equiv\int\differential\bm{r}\ \e^{-V(\bm{r},\bm{\lambda})}\ ,

(25)

which is a Gaussian integral since the potential (
4
) is harmonic. Combining Eqs. (
23
)-(
25
) results in

\displaystyle F(\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle=-\log\mathcal{Z}(\bm{\lambda})

(26a)

\displaystyle=\bm{\delta_{\rm g}}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}+F(\bm{0})

(26b)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\rm g}\lambda^{(z)}+F(\bm{0})\ ,

(26c)

with
F(\bm{0})
the free energy for
\bm{\lambda}=0
. Thus, the stored equilibrium free energy between measurements is

\displaystyle\left<\Delta F\right>

\displaystyle=\left<F(\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1})-F(\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}})\right>

(27a)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\rm g}\left<\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}\right>\ .

(27b)

This only depends on the trap center’s
z
-component; maximizing this
z
-component means the updated trap center lies at the top of the hypersphere defined via (
22
). Hence, the optimal feedback rule is

\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}=\bm{r}_{n+1}+|\bm{r}_{n+1}-\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}|\,\hat{\bm{z}}\ ,

(28)

for unit vector
\hat{\bm{z}}
in the
z
-direction.

Appendix B
High-sampling-frequency limit

Here we derive Eq. (
14
) from
Optimal performance
in the main text, the analytical expression for the output power
P_{\text{net}}^{\text{HF}}
in the high-sampling-frequency limit.

For sufficiently high sampling frequency
f_{\rm s}
,
\bm{\lambda}
evolves almost continuously in time. We first consider the differential change
\differential\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}\equiv\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}
of
\lambda^{(z)}
, given differential change
\differential\bm{r}_{n+1}\equiv\bm{r}_{n+1}-\bm{r}_{n}
of the bead position. The feedback rule (
11
) and feedback cooling of the transverse degrees of freedom (dictating
\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(i)}=x_{n}^{(i)}
) gives

\differential\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}=\differential z_{n+1}-L_{n}+\sqrt{(L_{n}-\differential z_{n+1})^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left(\differential x_{n+1}^{(j)}\right)^{2}}\ ,

(29)

for trap-bead displacement
L_{n}\equiv\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}-z_{n}
after the feedback. Since
\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}
always lies at the top of the hypersphere defined by the zero-work condition,
L_{n}\geq 0
. For sufficiently short sampling time
t_{\rm s}
, the displacements
\differential z_{n+1}
and
\differential x_{n+1}^{(i)}
are small and of the same order:
\differential z_{n+1},\differential x_{n+1}^{(i)}\sim O(\sqrt{t_{\rm s}})
. Taylor expanding Eq. (
29
) up to leading orders in
\differential z_{n+1}
and
\differential x_{n+1}^{(i)}
yields
z
trap-center displacement

\displaystyle\differential\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2L_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\left(\differential x_{n+1}^{(i)}\right)^{2}

(30a)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{L_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\left(\differential W_{t}^{(i)}\right)^{2}

(30b)

\displaystyle=\frac{d-1}{L_{n}}\,\differential t\ ,

(30c)

and hence vertical velocity

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\differential\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}}{\differential t}

(31a)

\displaystyle=\frac{d-1}{L_{n}}\ .

(31b)

In Eq. (
30b
), we employed the bounded quadratic variation formula
(\differential W_{t})^{2}=\differential t
for Wiener processes, which follows from Ito’s lemma
Kloeden and Platen 1992
.

To proceed further, we determine the steady-state distribution
p_{\text{ss}}(L)
for the trap-bead displacements
L_{n}
. Combining Eqs. (
30b
) and (
5
) gives the stochastic differential equation for
L_{n}
:

\displaystyle\differential L_{n+1}

\displaystyle\equiv L_{n+1}-L_{n}

(32a)

\displaystyle=\differential\lambda^{(z)}_{n^{+}+1}-\differential z_{n+1}

(32b)

\displaystyle\approx(\delta_{\rm g}-L_{n})\differential t+\frac{1}{2L_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\left(\differential W_{t}^{(i)}\right)^{2}-\sqrt{2}\,\differential W_{t}^{(z)}\ .

(32c)

Its corresponding distribution dynamically evolves according to the Fokker-Planck equation

\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial L}\left[\left(L-\delta_{\rm g}-\frac{d-1}{L}\right)p+\frac{\partial p}{\partial L}\right]\ ,

(33)

which has steady-state distribution

p_{\text{ss}}(L)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\delta_{\rm g})}L^{d-1}\e^{-L^{2}/2+\delta_{\rm g}L}\ ,

(34)

for partition function

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\delta_{\rm g})

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential L\ L^{d-1}\e^{-L^{2}/2+\delta_{\rm g}L}

(35a)

\displaystyle=2^{(d-3)/2}\left[\sqrt{2}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\,{}_{1}F_{1}\left(\frac{d}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{\delta_{\rm g}^{2}}{2}\right)+2\delta_{\rm g}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{1+d}{2}\right)\,{}_{1}F_{1}\left(\frac{1+d}{2},\frac{3}{2},\frac{\delta_{\rm g}^{2}}{2}\right)\right]\ .

(35b)

Here
{}_{1}F_{1}(a,b,c)
is the confluent hypergeometric function of the first kind. This gives output power

\displaystyle P_{\text{net}}^{\text{HF}}

\displaystyle=(d-1)\delta_{\rm g}\left<\frac{1}{L}\right>

(36a)

\displaystyle=(d-1)\delta_{\rm g}\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{d-1}(\delta_{\rm g})}{\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\delta_{\rm g})}\ .

(36b)

and vertical velocity

\left<v_{z}\right>^{\text{HF}}=(d-1)\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{d-1}(\delta_{\rm g})}{\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\delta_{\rm g})}\ ,

(37)

corresponding to Eq. (
14
) from the main text. Given that the velocity monotonically decreases with
\delta_{\rm g}
, the maximum is attained at
\delta_{\rm g}=0
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\text{max}}_{\delta_{\rm g}}\left[\left<v_{z}\right>^{\text{HF}}\right]

\displaystyle=(d-1)\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{d-1}(0)}{\mathcal{Z}_{d}(0)}

(38a)

\displaystyle=\frac{d-1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}\ .

(38b)

This expression holds even for
d\rightarrow 1
, giving
\sqrt{2/\pi}
.

Appendix C
Low-sampling-frequency limit

Here we derive an analytical expression for the output power
P_{\text{net}}^{\text{LF}}
in the low-sampling-frequency limit.

When the sampling frequency
f_{\rm s}
is small, between successive feedback steps the bead-position distribution equilibrates, giving the canonical equilibrium distribution

\displaystyle p_{\text{eq}}(\bm{r};\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{d}(\bm{r}|\,\bm{\lambda}-\bm{{\rm g}};1)

(39a)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{1}(z|\,\lambda^{(z)}-\delta_{\rm g};1)\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\mathcal{N}_{1}(x^{(i)}|\,\lambda^{(i)};1)\ .

(39b)

where
\mathcal{N}_{d}(\bm{x}|\,\bm{\mu};\sigma^{2})
denotes a
d
-dimensional normal (Gaussian) distribution over
\bm{x}
, with mean
\bm{\mu}
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Combining this and the feedback rule (
11
) gives the average equilibrium free-energy storage per feedback step,

\displaystyle\left<\Delta F\right>_{\text{eq}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\rm g}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{d}}\differential\bm{r}\left[z-\lambda^{(z)}+\sqrt{(z-\lambda^{(z)})^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(x^{(j)}-\lambda^{(j)})^{2}}\,\right]p_{\text{eq}}(\bm{r};\bm{\lambda})

(40a)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\rm g}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{d}}\differential z\left[\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\differential x^{(i)}\ \mathcal{N}_{1}(x^{(i)}|\,\lambda^{(i)};1)\,\right]\left[z-\lambda^{(z)}+\sqrt{(z-\lambda^{(z)})^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(x^{(j)}-\lambda^{(j)})^{2}}\,\right]\mathcal{N}_{1}(z|\,\lambda^{(z)}-\delta_{\rm g};1)

(40b)

\displaystyle=-\delta_{\rm g}^{2}+\frac{\delta_{\rm g}\ 2^{(1-d)/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\differential\tilde{z}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\differential r\ r^{d-2}\sqrt{\tilde{z}^{2}+r^{2}}\ \exp\left\{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}-\frac{(z+\delta_{\rm g})^{2}}{2}\right\}\ .

(40c)

In Eq. (
40c
), we made two changes of variables: (i)
\tilde{z}=z-\lambda^{(z)}
and (ii) change to spherical coordinates for the
d\!-\!1
perpendicular degrees of freedom. Therefore, in the limit of low sampling frequency the output power is

P^{\text{LF}}_{\text{net}}=f_{\rm s}\left<\Delta F\right>_{\text{eq}}\ .

(41)

This gives the gray dashed diagonal lines in Fig.
3
.

Appendix D
Steady states for arbitrary sampling frequency

Here we provide the details for the semi-analytical method employed to obtain the output power
P_{\text{net}}
for arbitrary sampling frequencies
f_{\rm s}
in arbitrary
d>1
dimensions, which we employed for the solid red curve in Fig.
3
.

To determine the output power for arbitrary sampling frequency, we follow the reasoning of
Saha et al. 2021
. First, we derive a self-consistency equation for the steady-state position distribution as a function of sampling frequency, which we evaluate numerically. The joint transition probability from bead position
\bm{r}_{n}
and trap center
\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}
to
\bm{r}_{n+1}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}
is

\displaystyle p(\bm{r}_{n+1},\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}|\,\bm{r}_{n},\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}})=

\displaystyle\ p_{\bm{r}}(\bm{r}_{n+1}|\,\bm{r}_{n},\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}})\ p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}|\,\bm{r}_{n+1},\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}})\ .

(42)

The bead-position propagator
p_{\bm{r}}(\bm{r}_{n+1}|\,\bm{r}_{n},\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}})
is given by the infinitesimal generator for a
d
-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process,

\displaystyle p_{\bm{r}}(\bm{r}_{n+1}|\,\bm{r}_{n},\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{d}(\bm{r}_{n+1}|\,\bm{\mu}(\bm{r}_{n},\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}},t_{\rm s});\sigma(t_{\rm s})^{2})

(43)

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}(\bm{r}_{n},\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}},t_{\rm s})

\displaystyle\equiv\e^{-t_{\rm s}}\bm{r}_{n}+(1-\e^{-t_{\rm s}})(\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}-\bm{\delta_{\rm g}})\ ,

(44)

for standard deviation
\sigma(t_{\rm s})\equiv\sqrt{1-\e^{-2t_{\rm s}}}
at time
t_{\rm s}
. The propagator
p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}|\,\bm{r}_{n+1},\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}})
for the trap center follows from the feedback rule (
11
):

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}|\,\bm{r}_{n+1},\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}})=\delta_{d}(\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}-\bm{r}_{n+1}-|\bm{r}_{n+1}-\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}|\hat{\bm{z}})\ .

(45)

Here,
\delta_{d}(\cdot)
denotes the
d
-dimensional Dirac delta function. Changing variables to relative coordinates

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}

\displaystyle\equiv\bm{r}_{n+1}-\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}

(46a)

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{n}^{\rm r}

\displaystyle\equiv\bm{r}_{n}-\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}

(46b)

yields

\displaystyle p_{\bm{r}}(\bm{r}_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}\,|\,\bm{r}_{n},\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{d}}\differential\bm{r}_{n+1}\ \delta_{d}(\bm{r}_{n+1}-\bm{r}_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}-\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}})\ p_{\bm{r}}(\bm{r}_{n+1}|\bm{r}_{n},\bm{\lambda}_{n})

(47a)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{d}(\bm{r}_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}\,|\,\bm{\mu}(\bm{r}_{n}^{\rm r},\bm{0},t_{\rm s});\sigma(t_{\rm s})^{2})

(47b)

\displaystyle\equiv p_{1}(\bm{r}_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}\,|\,\bm{r}_{n}^{\rm r})

(47c)

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{r}_{n+1}^{\rm r}|\bm{r}_{n+1},\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{d}}\differential\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}\ \delta_{d}(\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}-\bm{r}_{n+1}+\bm{r}_{n+1}^{\rm r})\ p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}+1}|\bm{r}_{n+1},\bm{\lambda}_{n})

(47d)

\displaystyle=\delta_{d}(\bm{r}_{n+1}^{\rm r}+\,|\,\bm{r}_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}|\hat{\bm{z}})

(47e)

\displaystyle\equiv p_{2}(\bm{r}_{n+1}^{\rm r}\,|\,\bm{r}_{n^{+}}^{\rm r})\ .

(47f)

The steady-state solutions satisfy the self-consistent integral equations

\pi_{+}\left(\bm{r}_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{d}}\differential\bm{u}\,\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}_{d}}\differential\bm{v}\,p_{1}\left(\bm{r}_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}\mid\bm{v}\right)p_{2}(\bm{v}\mid\bm{u})}_{\equiv\,T\left(\bm{r}_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}\mid\bm{u}\right)}\pi_{+}(\bm{u})

(48)

\pi\left(\bm{r}_{n}^{\rm r}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{d}}\differential\bm{v}\,\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}_{d}}\differential\bm{u}\,p_{2}\left(\bm{r}_{n}^{\rm r}\mid\bm{u}\right)p_{1}(\bm{u}\mid\bm{v})}_{\equiv\,\tilde{T}\left(\bm{r}_{n}^{\rm r}\mid\bm{v}\right)}\pi(\bm{v})

(49)

where
\bm{u}
and
\bm{v}
are
d
-dimensional vectors of dummy variables of integration. In principle, one can numerically solve for the steady-state distributions (
48
) and (
49
) by discretizing the propagator
T\left(\bm{r}_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}\mid\bm{u}\right)
in
\bm{r}_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}
and
\bm{u}
and the propagator
\tilde{T}\left(\bm{r}_{n}^{\rm r}\mid\bm{v}\right)
in
\bm{r}_{n}^{\rm r}
and
\bm{v}
. The corresponding eigenvectors with unit eigenvalue give the distributions
\pi_{+}(\bm{r}_{n^{+}}^{\rm r})
and
\pi(\bm{r}_{n}^{\rm r})
, respectively.

Determining the eigenvalues of the propagators
T\left(\bm{r}_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}\mid\bm{u}\right)
and
\tilde{T}\left(\bm{r}_{n}^{\rm r}\mid\bm{v}\right)
is in practice often challenging, since it involves the discretization of
d
-dimensional integrals on a sufficiently fine grid in order to achieve high accuracy, a task that becomes increasingly computationally expensive as the dimensionality increases. In the following, we derive a reduction of the
d
-dimensional integral eigenvalue problems (
48
) and (
49
) to effective one-dimensional problems, using a decomposition of the steady-state distributions:

\displaystyle\pi_{+}(\bm{r}_{n^{+}}^{\rm r})

\displaystyle=g_{+}(z_{n^{+}}^{\rm r})\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\mathcal{N}_{1}\left(x_{n^{+}}^{(i),{\rm r}}\,\Big|\,0;\sigma(t_{\rm s})^{2}\right)

(50a)

\displaystyle\pi(\bm{r}_{n}^{\rm r})

\displaystyle=|z_{n}^{\rm r}|\,\Theta(-z_{n}^{\rm r})\,g(z_{n}^{\rm r})\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\delta_{1}\left(x_{n}^{(i),{\rm r}}\right)\ ,

(50b)

for unknown functions
g_{+}(z_{n^{+}}^{\rm r})
and
g(z_{n}^{\rm r})
that (inserting Eqs. (
50a
) into Eqs. (
48
) and (
49
)) satisfy the effective one-dimensional eigenvalue problems

\displaystyle g_{+}(z_{n^{+}}^{\rm r})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\differential u\ T_{\text{eff}}(z_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}|u)\ g_{+}(u)

(51a)

\displaystyle g(z_{n}^{\rm r})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\differential v\ \tilde{T}_{\text{eff}}(z_{n}^{\rm r}|v)\ g(v)\ .

(51b)

The ansatz from Eq. (
50a
) can be understood on physical grounds, stemming from the feedback-cooling protocol (
11
) in the transverse degrees of freedom
(\lambda^{(1)},\lambda^{(2)},...\lambda^{(d-1)})
. On the one hand, the trap center moves so that
\lambda^{(i)}
follows the bead’s
i
th transverse coordinate, so the probability distribution
\pi(\bm{r}_{n}^{\rm r})
(after moving the trap center) for
\lambda^{(i)}_{n^{+}}-x^{(i)}_{n}
is a Dirac-delta distribution centered at the origin. On the other hand, the bead coordinate
x^{(i)}_{n}
subsequently evolves according to an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process centered at
\lambda^{(i)}_{n^{+}}
, whose propagator is a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and standard deviation
\sigma(t_{\rm s})
.

In Fig.
3
, we consider the
d\!=\!2
case, for which the effective propagators become

\displaystyle T_{\text{eff}}(z_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}\,|\,u)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\differential u^{\prime}\ \mathcal{N}_{1}\left(z_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}\,\Big|\,-\e^{-t_{\rm s}}\sqrt{(u)^{2}+(u^{\prime})^{2}}-(1-\e^{-t_{\rm s}})\delta_{\rm g};\sigma(t_{\rm s})^{2}\right)\cdot\mathcal{N}_{1}\left(u^{\prime}\,|\,0;\sigma(t_{\rm s})^{2}\right),

(52a)

\displaystyle\tilde{T}_{\text{eff}}(z_{n}^{\rm r}\,|\,v)

\displaystyle=|v|\,\Theta(-v)\ \mathcal{N}_{1}\left(z_{n}^{\rm r}\,|\,0;\sigma(t_{\rm s})^{2}\right)\,\mathcal{N}_{1}\left(\e^{-t_{\rm s}}v-(1-\e^{-t_{\rm s}})\delta_{\rm g}\,|\,0;\sigma(t_{\rm s})^{2}\right)\,I_{0}\left(\frac{z_{n}^{\rm r}}{\sigma(t_{\rm s})^{2}}[\e^{-t_{\rm s}}v-(1-\e^{-t_{\rm s}})\delta_{\rm g}]\right)\ ,

(52b)

with
I_{0}(\cdot)
the modified Bessel function of the first kind of order 0. To obtain the semi-analytical results in Fig.
3
, we discretized the effective propagator
T_{\text{eff}}(z_{n^{+}}^{\rm r}|u)
in a grid of
2,000\times 2,000
uniformly spaced points in the domain
z_{n^{+}}^{\rm r},u\in[-20,20]
. For the remaining propagator
\tilde{T}_{\text{eff}}(z_{n}^{\rm r}|v)
, given the numerical instabilities of the modified Bessel function, we used a much finer grid of
50,000\times 50,000
points in the domain
z_{n}^{\rm r},v\in[-20,0]
.

Appendix E
Transverse threshold

Here we provide details for the semi-analytical method employed to obtain the solid red curve from Fig.
4
, giving the maximum output power
P_{\text{net}}
as a function of the radial threshold
R
for the transverse degrees of freedom, as discussed in
Optimal performance
in the main text.

Let us consider the high-sampling-frequency limit. For a nonzero feedback threshold
R
, the trap center is adjusted only once the bead position projected onto the transverse plane exceeds distance
R
from the trap center. For measurements at fixed time intervals
\tau
, the output power
P_{\text{net}}
is the ratio between the average gain
\left<\Delta F\right>
in stored gravitational free energy per ratchet event and the time between events:

\displaystyle P_{\text{net}}(\tau,R)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\left<\Delta F\right>}{\tau}

(53a)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{\rm g}}{\tau}\left<\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}\right>

(53b)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{\rm g}}{\tau}\left<z_{n+1}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}+\sqrt{\left(z_{n+1}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}\right)^{2}+R^{2}}\,\right>\ .

(53c)

In Eq. (
53b
), we employed the definition of
\left<\Delta F\right>
(
10
) and the feedback rule (
12
). Inserting the discrete Langevin equation (
5
) and the vertical distance
L_{n}\equiv\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}-z_{n}\geq 0
gives

P_{\text{net}}(\tau,R)=\frac{\delta_{\rm g}}{\tau}\overline{\left<(1-\e^{-\tau})(L_{n}-\delta_{\rm g})-L_{n}+\sqrt{\left[(1-\e^{-\tau})(L_{n}-\delta_{\rm g})+\sqrt{1-\e^{-2\tau}}\ \xi_{n}-L_{n}\right]^{2}+R^{2}}\,\right>}\ .

(54)

The overline indicates an average over noise realizations
\xi_{n}
drawn from a normal distribution of zero mean and unit variance.

In principle, one would also need to average over all possible times
\tau
between ratchet events. The distribution of such times is the first-passage-time distribution for a bead starting at the origin, with an absorbing boundary at the surface of a
(d-1)
-dimensional hypersphere of radius
R
. Unfortunately, even for the simple case of harmonic confinement, obtaining the first-passage distribution is non-trivial
Bray et al. 2013
, beyond the scope of this paper. To estimate the qualitative dependence of
P_{\text{net}}
on
R
, we approximate the first-passage distribution as a Dirac-delta centered at the mean first-passage time
\tau_{\text{MFP}}
, obtained from the solution of the backward Fokker-Planck equation in
d-1
dimensions,

\displaystyle-\bm{r}^{\top}\nabla\mathbb{T}(\bm{r})+\nabla^{2}\mathbb{T}(\bm{r})=-1\ ,

(55)

with absorbing boundary condition

\mathbb{T}(|\bm{r}|=R)=0\ .

(56)

The mean first-passage time is then

\tau_{\text{MFP}}=\mathbb{T}(\bm{0})\ .

(57)

For
d\!=\!2
this reduces to a one-dimensional problem with absorbing boundary conditions at
r=\pm R
, with solution

\tau_{\text{MFP}}=\frac{R^{2}}{2}\,{}_{2}F_{2}\left(1,1;\frac{3}{2},2;\frac{R^{2}}{2}\right)\ ,

(58)

for generalized hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{2}(a_{1},a_{2};b_{1},b_{2};z)
.

To compute the output power in (
54
), we need to determine the stead-state distribution for the trap-bead displacements
L_{n}
. Given an initial
L_{n}
, fixed ratchet time
\tau=\tau_{\text{MFP}}
, and noise
\xi_{n}
, the trap-bead displacement after ratcheting is

\displaystyle L_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}-z_{n+1}

(59a)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left[(1-\e^{-\tau})(L_{n}-\delta_{\rm g})+\sqrt{1-\e^{-2\tau}}\ \xi_{n}-L_{n}\right]^{2}+R^{2}}\ .

(59b)

We obtain the propagator from

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\differential\xi\ \delta\left(L_{n+1}-\sqrt{\left[(1-\e^{-\tau})(L_{n}-\delta_{\rm g})+\sqrt{1-\e^{-2\tau}}\ \xi-L_{n}\right]^{2}+R^{2}}\,\right)\mathcal{N}_{1}(\xi\,|\,0;1)

(60b)

We obtain the steady-state distribution for the trap-bead displacement by solving the integral eigenvalue problem

Here, for the partial information engine introduced in

in the main text, we derive the self-consistent equation for the parameter
\omega
from the feedback rule (

P_{\text{net}}^{\text{HF}}
in Eq. (

For an information engine where we do not measure the
z
-component of the bead’s position, we recall that the trap-center
z
-component is updated according to the parametric feedback rule from Eq. (
20
). Here we derive a self-consistent equation for determining
\omega
, in such a way that it matches the steady-state average value
\left<z_{n+1}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}\right>
.

First, we compute the average of the bead-trap displacements
z_{n}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}
after ratcheting:

\displaystyle\left<z_{n}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}\right>

\displaystyle=\left<z_{n+1}-\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}\right>

(63a)

\displaystyle=\left<z_{n+1}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}\right>-\omega-\mathcal{R}(\omega,t_{\rm s})

(63b)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{R}(\omega,t_{\rm s})\ ,

(63c)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\omega,t_{\rm s})

\displaystyle\equiv\left<\sqrt{\omega^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left(x_{n+1}^{(i)}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(i)}\right)^{2}}\,\right>

(64a)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{d}}{[2\pi\sigma(t_{\rm s})^{2}]^{d/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential r\ r^{d-2}\sqrt{\omega^{2}+r^{2}}\,\e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2\sigma(t_{\rm s})^{2}}}\ .

(64b)

In Eq. (
64b
), we have taken into account that, although we do not know the steady-state distribution for the relative displacement
z_{n+1}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}
before ratcheting, the transverse displacements
x_{n+1}^{(i)}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(i)}
,
i=1,...,d-1
, follow Gaussian distributions of zero mean and standard deviation
\sigma(t_{\rm s})
. We identify
\mathcal{R}(\omega,t_{\rm s})
with the average radius of the hypersphere defined by the zero-work condition
\left<W_{n+1}\right>=0
when
z
measurements are ignored—analogous to
|\bm{r}_{n+1}-\bm{\lambda}_{n^{+}}|
from the feedback rule (
11
).

We compute the average from (
63a
) using the Langevin equation (
5
) for
z_{n+1}
:

\displaystyle\left<z_{n}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}\right>

\displaystyle=\left<z_{n+1}-\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}\right>

(65a)

\displaystyle=\e^{-t_{\rm s}}\left<z_{n}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}\right>-(1-\e^{-t_{\rm s}})\delta_{\rm g}-\omega-\mathcal{R}(\omega,t_{\rm s})

(65b)

\displaystyle=-\e^{-t_{\rm s}}\mathcal{R}(\omega,t_{\rm s})-(1-\e^{-t_{\rm s}})\delta_{\rm g}-\omega-\mathcal{R}(\omega,t_{\rm s})\ .

(65c)

Combining (
63a
) and (
65a
) gives a self-consistent equation for
\omega
:

\omega=-\e^{-t_{\rm s}}\mathcal{R}(\omega,t_{\rm s})-(1-\e^{-t_{\rm s}})\delta_{\rm g}\ .

(66)

We now compute the output power in the high-sampling-frequency limit. The average of the variation of the trap-center
z
-component over a short timestep
\differential t
is

\displaystyle\left<\differential\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}\right>

\displaystyle\equiv\left<\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}\right>

(67a)

\displaystyle=\left<\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}-z_{n+1}\right>+\left<z_{n+1}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}\right>

(67c)

Substituting the self-consistency equation (

\omega

\displaystyle\left<\differential\lambda_{n^{+}+1}^{(z)}\right>

\displaystyle=\omega+\e^{t_{\rm s}}\left[-\omega-(1-\e^{-t_{\rm s}})\delta_{\rm g}\right]

\displaystyle=-(\e^{t_{\rm s}}-1)(\omega+\delta_{\rm g})

(68b)

\displaystyle\approx-t_{\rm s}(\omega+\delta_{\rm g}),\quad t_{\rm s}\ll 1\ .

\displaystyle\approx\omega+|\omega|+\frac{1}{2|\omega|}\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}(\differential x_{n+1}^{(i)})^{2}

\displaystyle=-t_{\rm s}\frac{d-1}{\omega},\quad t_{\rm s}\ll 1\ .

(69b)

z_{n+1}-\lambda_{n^{+}}^{(z)}

\displaystyle\omega+\delta_{\rm g}-\frac{d-1}{\omega}

giving

(72a)

\displaystyle\approx-\delta_{\rm g}\frac{d-1}{\omega}

(72b)

(72c)
