Computing time-dependent reduced models for classical and quantum dynamics
Tommaso Grigoletto

Abstract
This paper introduces a novel method for approximating the dynamics of a large autonomous system projected onto a fixed subspace. The core contribution is a novel recursive algorithm to construct an effective time-dependent generator that is polynomial in the time variable, ensuring accuracy for short time scales. The derivation is based on the Taylor expansion of the exponential map and a new result for computing the time-ordered exponential of polynomial generators. This work is motivated by the challenge of deriving time-convolutionless master equations in quantum physics and the proposed method offers an alternative to typical derivations based on expansions in the coupling strength. The resulting approximation is accurate for small times, does not require a weak-coupling assumption, performs better than a truncation of the exponential map at low orders, and crucially, guarantees a completely positive and trace-preserving map at the lowest orders. The proposed method is validated against several prototypical models: a dephasing spin-boson model, a central spin model, and an Ising spin chain.



I
Introduction

Whenever a dynamical model is too large to be simulated efficiently, one should resort to model reduction techniques to find a smaller model that is simulatable and that retains fundamental characteristics of the original system. A dominant approach in control systems theory – including, for example, minimal realization techniques
rosenbrock1970state
, balanced truncation
antoulas
;
gugercin2004survey
, and moment matching
astolfi_moment_matching
;
padoanmomentmatching
– consists of finding a low-dimensional subspace where to project the dynamics, obtaining a linear time-invariant (LTI) model. This work considers a different approach: the space onto which the dynamics is projected is assumed to be
given a priori and fixed
, and, instead, the dynamics is approximated by seeking a linear time-varying (LTV) model.

This approach is inspired by quantum physics.
To obtain realistic models of open quantum systems, one shall consider the effects of the interaction between the system and its environment
breuer2002theory
. Unfortunately, simulating the system-environment couple is impractical (if not impossible), as the scaling of the Hilbert space makes such a simulation prohibitive even for small environments. This implies that, in order to study the effect of the coupling with the environment, one needs to find a reduced model that (hopefully) captures the interesting features of the interaction.

A common method to reduce the joint system-environment model is known in the physics literature with the name of time-convolutionless master equations (TCL ME)
shibata_generalized_1977
;
chaturvedi_time-convolutionless_1979
. This method aims at obtaining an LTV model of the same size of the system that replicates the effects of the interaction with the environment. Although a formal exact solution to this problem exists, computing this exact LTV generator is generally considered as difficult as solving the original model. The main approach found in the physics literature approximates this formal solution by leveraging an expansion in the strength of the coupling between the system and the environment, which is typically assumed to be small. For more details, see
breuer2002theory
and references therein.

In this work an alternative approach is proposed: instead of an expansion in the coupling strength, an expansion in time is considered. In particular, since the formal solution provides an analytic LTV generator in the time variable
t
, the exact incomputable generator is approximated with its truncation to a finite order
N
. Under these assumptions a recursive formula for the coefficients of the polynomial generator is found, and the approximation error of the resulting reduced model scales as
O(t^{N+1})
.

Note that while time-dependent dynamical systems are typically more difficult to study than time-independent ones, this approach finds an important application in the simulation of very large systems, as the Runge-Kutta methods allow one to simulate time-dependent models
ascher1997implicit
. In this regard, under the assumption of a time-dependent generator that is
polynomial
in
t
, this paper proposes a novel recursive formula to compute the propagator of this LTV model.

Another benefit of the proposed reduction method consists in the fact that, when computing the reduced LTV generator for unitarily evolving quantum systems, the reduced polynomial generator at first and second order is provably in Lindblad form for all times
t
thus ensuring that the reduced model at the lowest orders generates a completely positive (CP) and trace preserving (TP) semigroup.

It is important to point out that the method proposed in this work is not intended to substitute the perturbative approach for approximating TCL MEs
shibata_generalized_1977
;
chaturvedi_time-convolutionless_1979
but rather to provide an alternative that can be of use whenever the main assumptions of the standard method do not hold, e.g. in the strong-coupling regime or whenever the time-ordered integrals are difficult to assess.

Structure and paper contributions:
For the reader’s convenience, the structure of this work and its main contributions are summarized next:

•

In Sec.
II
the problem treated in this work is defined rigorously. The known formal solution of the problem is derived in detail using the Nakajima-Swanzig equations
nakajima
;
zwanzig
, and the key difficulties associated with computing this LTV model are explained.

•

In Sec.
III
the main contribution of the paper is presented: Theorem
2
derives the polynomial approximation of the formal LTV model and the recursive equation that determines its coefficients. This result is based on Theorem
1
where a novel method to compute the time-ordered exponential is derived. The reduction procedure is also validated on a linear model and compared against the truncation of the exponential series.

•

Sec.
IV
focuses on the application of the proposed reduction procedure to quantum dynamical models. The proposed method is connected and compared with the typical physics approach for the approximation of TCL ME which is based on the expansion of the coupling strength. The connections between the recursive formula obtained here and other similar recursive equations known in the physics literature
nestmann2019time
;
cerrillo2014non
;
cygorek2025timenonlocalversustimelocallongtime
;
gasbarri2018recursive
are discussed, highlighting how the expansion in
t
greatly simplifies the associated calculations. Most importantly, leveraging a few recent results
grigoletto2024exact
;
grigoletto2025quantummodelreductioncontinuoustime
it is proven that when the original generatos is purely Hamiltonian, the proposed approximation at first and second order provides a CPTP two-parameter semigroup.

•

In Sec.
V
the proposed procedure is validated against several prototypical quantum examples: a dephasing spin boson model, a central spin model, and and Ising spin chain. Note that the majority of the applications presented in this work are in the context of quantum physics since, up to this point, this is where these LTV-based model reduction techniques have found most applications.

Throughout the paper, pedagogical examples are included to intuitively explain the most involved proofs. Furthermore, both the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed approximation procedure are highlighted in detail, namely: the recursive formula used to compute the terms of the generators at different orders makes the procedure numerically easier to access at higher orders than the alternatives; allows for an initial model that is in Lindblad form (not only purely Hamiltonian); does not require the model to be put in rotating frame; and does not rely on a weak coupling assumption, thus allowing to study systems in strong coupling regimes. On the other hand, the proposed reduction procedure relies on an expansion in time and thus provides a good approximation only for small times. Although this aspect is limiting, similar restrictions also hold in the well-known approximations of the TCL ME proposed by
shibata_generalized_1977
.

II
Problem setting and connection with the literature

II.1
Problem setting

In this work, we assume to be given an LTI stable autonomous model of the type

\dot{x}_{t}=Lx_{t}

(1)

where
x_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is the system’s state and
L\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is the state matrix or generator. Such a generator defines a one-parameter semigroup, i.e.
\{\Lambda_{t}\equiv e^{Lt}\}_{t\geq 0}
such that
\Lambda_{t+s}=\Lambda_{t}\Lambda_{s}
, for all
t,s\geq 0
.

We next assume that, for some reason dictated by the application at hand, we are not interested in reproducing all the degrees of freedom contained in the system’s state
x_{t}
, but we are only interested in the degrees of freedom contained on a given subspace
\mathscr{V}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
of dimension
m=\dim(\mathscr{V})
. Importantly, here we assume the subspace to be
given and fixed

\mathscr{V}
.
Let then
P\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, be a projector
P^{2}=P
onto
\mathscr{V}={\rm Im}P
. Note that
P
does not need to be orthogonal (with respect to the standard inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
), i.e.
P^{\dagger}\neq P
. For future convenience, let us define
Q\equiv\mathbb{1}-P
where
\mathbb{1}
denotes the identity matrix and factorize
P
into two linear maps: the
reduction

R\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
and the
injection

J\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
such that
P=JR
and
RJ=\mathbb{1}_{m}
the identity in
\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
.

We further make a simplifying assumption that is quite typical in these settings; see, e.g.
breuer2002theory
: We assume that
x_{0}\in\mathscr{V}
, or equivalently
Px_{0}=x_{0}
.

Figure 1
:
Graphical representation of the approximation problem. The pale blue plane represents the subspace
\mathscr{V}
onto which
P
projects. The red curve represents the trajectory of the original model
x_{t}
, which starts in
\mathscr{V}
at
t=0
. The blue curve represents the projection of the true trajectory
Px_{t}
, i.e. the one we want to approximate. Finally, the orange curve represents the approximate evolution we aim to find, i.e.
Jz_{t}
.

In summary, we have the model

\begin{cases}\dot{x}_{t}=Lx_{t}\\
y_{t}=Rx_{t}\end{cases},\quad x_{0}\in\mathscr{V}

where we defined the output signal
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

In this work, we are interested in deriving a reduced model that is capable of reproducing the trajectories
y_{t}=Pe^{Lt}x_{0},\quad\forall x_{0}\in\mathscr{V},\quad\forall t\geq 0

or, equivalently,

y_{t}=Re^{Lt}J\check{x}_{0},\quad\forall\check{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{m},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(2)

Note that, when
\mathscr{V}
is
L
-invariant, the problem has a trivial solution since by projecting the generator
L
onto
\mathscr{V}
one obtains the reduced generator
RLJ
that exactly reproduces all the trajectories of interest.
One can obtain an exact and minimal LTI reduced model by computing the orthogonal to the non-observable subspace
\mathscr{N}^{\perp}
as the smallest
L^{\dagger}
-invariant subspace that contains
\mathscr{V}
and projecting the dynamics onto it, thus obtaining the smallest observable LTI model
rosenbrock1970state
. However, the drawback of this approach is the fact that if the couple
(L,R)
is observable, the model can not be reduced exactly. In those cases, one should recur to approximate model reduction techniques such as balanced truncation
antoulas
;
gugercin2004survey
and moment matching
astolfi_moment_matching
;
padoanmomentmatching
.
In those cases, one aims to find a subspace where to project the dynamics in order to obtain an LTI model that approximates the trajectories of interest in a given metric.

In this work, we take a different route: We fix the subspace where the dynamics is projected to
\mathscr{V}
and lift the time-independence constraint by looking for an LTV model of dimension
m
that approximates the trajectories of interest. Note that while this approach is not common in the control literature, this has been studied in the physics literature for the derivation of TCL ME as detailed in Sec.
IV.1
.

More rigorously, let us define a state
z_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
whose evolution is given by an LTV model of the type

\begin{cases}\dot{z}_{t}=F_{t}z_{t}\\
z_{0}=Rx_{0}\end{cases}

(3)

where
F_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is a time-dependent generator. The evolution of such an LTV model is given by the two-parameter semigroup
\{\Upsilon_{t}\equiv\mathbb{T}e^{\int_{0}^{t}F_{s}ds}\}_{t\geq 0}
for which the composition rule
\Upsilon_{t,0}=\Upsilon_{t,s}\Upsilon_{s,0}
for all
0\leq s\leq t
holds. Here
\mathbb{T}
denotes the time-ordering operator, and
\mathbb{T}e
denotes the time-ordered exponential or, equivalently, the Volterra series
lawrence1979volterra
;
Giscard_2015
.

Our aim is then to construct
F_{t}
so that
z_{t}\approx y_{t}
or, in other words, we want to determine
F_{t}
so that

\mathbb{T}e^{\int_{0}^{t}F_{s}ds}\approx Re^{Lt}J.

The sense in which the approximation
\approx
is intended shall be defined more precisely later on in the paper. A graphical representation of the approximation problem is given in Fig.
1
.

II.2
Reduced and exact time-dependent dynamics

Before moving on to the method proposed in this work for computing the time-dependent generator
F_{t}
we shall discuss the common derivation found in the physics literature to solve the problem at hand
Tokieda_2025
;
chaturvedi_time-convolutionless_1979
;
nestmann_time-convolutionless_2019
;
shibata_generalized_1977
. The derivation is particularly elegant hence we report it here for the interested reader.

Dividing the evolution of
x_{t}
in the evolution of
Px_{t}
and that of
Qx_{t}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}Px_{t}

\displaystyle=PLPx_{t}+PLQx_{t},

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}Qx_{t}

\displaystyle=QLPx_{t}+QLQx_{t}.

(5)

Solving the second equation for
Qx_{t}
with the Lagrange equation, we obtain

Qx_{t}=e^{QLQt}Qx_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{QLQs}QLPx_{t-s}ds.

By substituting this equation into Eq. (
4
) we obtain the famous Nakajima-Zwanzig equation
nakajima
;
zwanzig
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}Px_{t}=PLPx_{t}

\displaystyle+PLe^{QLQt}Qx_{0}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}PLQe^{QLQs}QLPx_{t-s}ds.

(6)

We then recall that

x_{t-s}=e^{-Ls}x_{t}

so that

Qx_{t}=e^{QLQt}Qx_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{QLQs}QLPe^{-Ls}ds\,\cdot\,(P+Q)x_{t}.

Now, defining

M_{t}\equiv\mathbb{1}-\int_{0}^{t}e^{QLQs}QLPe^{-Ls}ds

(7)

we have

M_{t}Qx_{t}=e^{QLQt}Qx_{0}+(\mathbb{1}-M_{t})Px_{t}.

If we then assume that
M_{t}
is invertible, we obtain

Qx_{t}=M_{t}^{-1}e^{QLQt}Qx_{0}+(M_{t}^{-1}-\mathbb{1})Px_{t}.

Substituting this into Eq. (
4
) we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}Px_{t}

\displaystyle=PLPx_{t}+PLM_{t}^{-1}e^{QLQt}Qx_{0}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+PL(M_{t}^{-1}-\mathbb{1})Px_{t}

\displaystyle=PLM_{t}^{-1}e^{QLQt}Qx_{0}+PLM_{t}^{-1}Px_{t}.

(8)

Note that under the assumption
Px_{0}=x_{0}
we have
Qx_{0}=0
and thus Eq. (
8
) gets simplified to

\frac{d}{dt}Px_{t}=\underbrace{PLM_{t}^{-1}P}_{\equiv K_{t}}\rho_{t}

(9)

which, separating the projector
P
into its factors
P=JR
, becomes

\frac{d}{dt}\check{x}_{t}=\underbrace{RLM_{t}^{-1}J}_{\equiv F_{t}}\check{x}_{t}.

(10)

A few comments on this solution are in order:

i) The invertibility of
M_{t}
needs to be verified and typically only holds fot small
t
, (see, e.g.
breuer2002theory
) but for the times where
M_{t}
is invertible, Eq. (
10
) provides an exact solution to the problem at hand.

ii) Note that, to compute
F_{t}
(or equivalently
K_{t}
) one needs to first compute the integral that defines
M_{t}
and then compute the inverse of
M_{t}
. Both these tasks are challenging (numerically and analytically) and it is generally considered that solving this integral and inverse is as difficult as solving the original model (
1
), see e.g.
breuer2007
. However, Eq. (
8
) provides an excellent starting point for computing an approximation of the reduced evolution.

To avoid these numerical issues,
K_{t}
is typically approximated by expanding it as an expansion in the coupling strength
\varepsilon
and by computing the terms of the expansion as ordered cumulants
breuer2002theory
;
breuer2006
;
nestmann2019time
.
In many cases of interest, this approximation provides a good approximation of the open quantum system dynamics, both in the long-time and short-time regimes. It is, however, important to note that there is no guarantee that such an approximation holds also for long times. As an example of this, see
breuer2007
where it is shown that TCL2 and TCL4 (approximations at orders
N=2,4
) do not approximate the true dynamics in the long-time regime.
We refer the reader to Sec.
IV.2
for further connections to the physics literature.

III
Polynomial approximations of the time-dependent generator

In this section we derive the key result of this work: a method to approximate the time-dependent generator defined in Eq.(
10
),
F_{t}
. The derivation that follows is based on the following observation.

Proposition 1
.

Let
M_{t}
be the time-dependent operator defined in Eq. (
7
) and let
F_{t}
be the time-dependent generator defined in Eq. (
10
). Then, for every
t
such that
M_{t}
is invertible,
F_{t}
is analytic in
t
, i.e.

F_{t}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}t^{k}F_{(k+1)}.

Proof.

We can start by observing that the function
e^{QLQt}QLPe^{-Lt}
is the product of two analytic functions in
t
hence it is analytic in
t
. Furthermore, as the integral of an analytic function is also analytic, see, e.g.
bourchtein2021analytic
we have that
M_{t}
is also analytic in
t
. Finally, as the inverse of an analytic function
f(t)
is analytic where
f(t)\neq 0
, we have that
F_{t}
is also analytic in
t
whenever
M_{t}
is invertible.
∎

Note that we label the coefficients in the power series starting from
1
instead of
0
for later convenience. This insightful result allows us to: 1) focus on computing the terms of the power series
\{F_{(k+1)}\}_{k=0}^{+\infty}
and 2) approximate
F_{t}
by truncating the infinite power series to a finite order
N
. In what follows, we thus approximate
F_{t}
with the
polynomial time-dependent generator

F_{t,N}\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{N}t^{k}F_{(k+1)}.

Other than an obvious computational benefit introduced by computing the terms
F_{(k)}
only to order
N
, the assumption that the generator is polynomial in the time variable greatly simplifies the computation of the time-ordered exponential
\mathbb{T}e^{\int_{0}^{t}F_{s,N}ds}
as shown in the next key result.

Theorem 1
.

Let us assume that
F_{t,N}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}t^{k}F_{(k+1)}
.
Then the time-ordered exponential can be written as

\mathbb{T}e^{\int_{0}^{t}F_{s,N}ds}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}t^{k}E_{(k)}

(11)

where
E_{(0)}=\mathbb{1}
and the other terms are defined by the recursion:

E_{(k)}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{s=1}^{k}F_{(s)}E_{(k-s)}

(12)

and where we used the convention
F_{(k)}=0
for all
k>N
.

Proof of this result and an explanatory example are given in Appendix
A
.

Note that Eq. (
12
) provides a closed formula for the coefficients
E_{(k)}
of the expansion of
\mathbb{T}e^{\int_{0}^{t}F_{s,N}ds}
as a function of the coefficients
F_{(k)}
. Furthermore, only the terms
\{E_{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1}^{k-1}
appear in Eq. (
12
), which means that this equation can be used iteratively to compute each
E_{(k)}
.

Theorem
1
provides the main intuition on how to compute the coefficients
\{F_{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{N+1}
of the polynomial time-dependent generator
F_{t,N}
: Eq. (
11
) provides an expansion for
\mathbb{T}e^{\int_{0}^{t}F_{s,N}ds}
in the time variable
t
. We can then expand
e^{tL}
in
t
and construct
F_{(k)}
so that the terms of
\mathbb{T}e^{\int_{0}^{t}F_{s,N}ds}
match the terms of
Re^{tL}J
by orders of
t
. This approach is similar in principle to the adiabatic elimination techniques
adiabadic-elimination
;
Tokieda_2025
where, instead of matching orders of
\varepsilon
, we match orders of
t
. The resulting procedure, which represents the main contribution of this work, is stated next.

Figure 2
:
Operator and Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the first 20
F_{(k)}
terms (left) and of the first 50
E_{(k)}
terms when
N=20
(right).

Theorem 2
.

Let us define

F_{t,N}\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{N}t^{k}F_{(k+1)}

(13)

where

F_{(k)}\equiv k\frac{RL^{k}J}{k!}-\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}F_{(k-h)}\frac{RL^{h}J}{h!}.

(14)

Then:

\displaystyle Re^{Lt}J-\mathbb{T}e^{\int_{0}^{t}F_{s,N}ds}=O(t^{N+1}).

(15)

Proof.

First of all we can use Theorem
1
to write

\mathbb{T}e^{\int_{0}^{t}F_{s,N}ds}=\sum_{f=0}^{+\infty}t^{k}E_{(k)}

where
E_{(k)}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{s=1}^{k}F_{(s)}E_{(k-s)}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{h=0}^{k-1}F_{(k-h)}E_{(h)}
and where we reversed the order of the summation by imposing
h=k-s
. We next prove that, for
1\leq k\leq N
, we have
E_{(k)}=\frac{RL^{k}J}{k!}
by induction. The case
k=1
is trivial as
E_{(1)}=F_{(1)}E_{(0)}=RLJ
. Let then assume that
E_{(h)}=\frac{RL^{h}J}{h!}
for all
h<k
and let us prove that
E_{(k)}=\frac{RL^{k}J}{k!}
. Then

\displaystyle E_{(k)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{h=0}^{k-1}F_{(k-h)}E_{(h)}=\frac{F_{(k)}}{k}+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}F_{(k-h)}E_{(h)}

\displaystyle=\frac{F_{(k)}}{k}+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}F_{(k-h)}\frac{RL^{h}J}{h!}=\frac{RL^{k}J}{k!}

where we simply substituted the definition of
F_{(k)}
given in eq. (
14
).

By leveraging the definition of the exponential map
e^{Lt}
we can expand the term
Re^{Lt}J
to obtain

\displaystyle Re^{Lt}J=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}RL^{k}J.

Combining the two terms we obtain

\displaystyle Re^{Lt}J-\mathbb{T}e^{\int_{0}^{t}F_{s,N}ds}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}t^{k}\left(\frac{RL^{k}J}{k!}-E_{(k)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N}t^{k}\cancel{\left(\frac{RL^{k}J}{k!}-E_{(k)}\right)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+O(t^{N+1})

which concludes the proof.
∎

We shall observe a few key aspects.
First, Eq. (
15
) finally gives sense to the approximation symbol
\approx
used in Sec.
II
i.e. an approximation of the type
O(t^{N+1})
. This implies that the approximation holds only for small times.
Second, it is important to notice that the right-hand side of equation (
14
) depends on
RL^{k}J
and
\{F_{(s)}\}_{s=1}^{k-1}
. This means that equation (
14
) can be used iteratively to construct the terms
F_{(k)}
starting from
k=1
and growing up to
k=N
.
For the reader’s convenience the first few terms of the series are reported next:

\displaystyle F_{(1)}

\displaystyle=RLJ

\displaystyle F_{(2)}

\displaystyle=RL^{2}J-F_{(1)}RLJ=RL^{2}J-(RLJ)^{2}

\displaystyle F_{(3)}

\displaystyle=\frac{RL^{3}J}{2}-F_{(2)}RLJ-F_{(1)}\frac{RL^{2}J}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{RL^{3}J}{2}-\frac{F_{(1)}F_{(2)}}{2}-\frac{F_{(1)}^{3}}{2}-F_{(2)}F_{(1)}

It is interesting to notice a couple of points about the first few terms: i) As one would expect, the term of order one
F_{(1)}
coincides with the reduction of the original dynamics which is often used as rough time-independent approximation of the reduced evolution
antoulas
; ii) The second order term does not only contain the reduction of the generator squared
RL^{2}J
but also contains a correction term depends on the first order term
F_{(1)}
.

Lastly, it is important to note that similar (yet distinct) recursive formulas to derive similar approximations have been found in the physics literature
nestmann2019time
;
cerrillo2014non
;
cygorek2025timenonlocalversustimelocallongtime
;
gasbarri2018recursive
. We refer the reader to Sec.
IV.2
where we discuss connections and differences between these works.

III.1
Linear testbed

To showcase the capability of the proposed method, we here consider a simple numerical example.
Specifically, we consider a random autonomous and stable system of size
n=20
.
Let then
A
be a random uniform matrix in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, i.e.
[A]_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{U}([0,1])
and let
B=A-\lambda_{\rm Max}\mathbb{1}_{n}
where
\lambda_{\rm Max}
is the absolute value of largest eigenvalue of
A
.
The dynamic’s generator
L\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is then obtained as
L=\frac{1}{2}\frac{B}{\left|\left|B\right|\right|_{op}}
where
\left|\left|\cdot\right|\right|_{op}
denotes the operator norm.
This ensures that
L
is simply stable and has operator norm
\left|\left|L\right|\right|_{op}=\frac{1}{2}
.

The projector
P
is then constructed through
R=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbb{1}_{m\times m}&0_{m\times n-m}\end{array}\right]
and
J=R^{\dagger}
to obtain

P=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbb{1}_{m\times m}&0_{m\times n-m}\\
\hline\cr 0_{m\times n-m}&0_{n-m\times n-m}\end{array}\right].

By using Eq. (
14
) we can then compute the terms
F_{(k)}
iteratively for
k=1,\dots,N
. Fig.
2
(left) shows both the Hilbert-Schmidt and operator norm of the terms
F_{(k)}
for
k=1,20
. Interestingly, the Hilbert-Schmidt norm,
\left|\left|F_{(k)}\right|\right|_{HS}
, is bounded by
\frac{\left|\left|L\right|\right|_{HS}^{k}}{k!}
, indicating that the series
F_{t,N}
should be converging for
N\to\infty
. Curiously, the same does not hold for the operator norm.
Fig.
2
(right) instead shows the norm of the terms
E_{(k)}
where we fixed
N=20
and
F_{(k)}=0

\forall k>20
. Interestingly, for
k\leq N

\left|\left|E_{(k)}\right|\right|
is bounded by

IV.1
The reduction problem for quantum dynamical models

Let us consider a quantum system defined on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, and let
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
denote the set of bounded operators acting on
\mathcal{H}
. Unless differently specified, We only consider finite-dimensional systems, i.e.
\dim(\mathcal{H})<\infty
,
\mathcal{H}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}.

The state of such a system is described by a density operator
\rho\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H})
with
\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H})=\{\rho\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})|\,\rho=\rho^{\dagger}\geq 0,\,{\rm tr}\rho=1\}
. Here, we assume that the state of the system evolves according to the Von-Neumann master equation

\dot{\rho}_{t}=\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})

(16)

where
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H})
is the system’s initial condition, and
\mathcal{L}
is the celebrated Lindblad (or GKLS) generator
lindblad1976generators
;
Gorini:1975nb
:

\mathcal{L}(\rho)=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{k}\mathcal{D}_{L_{k}}(\rho)

(17)

H=H^{\dagger}
is the system’s Hamiltonian and
\mathcal{D}_{L}(\rho)=L\rho L^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,\rho\}
represents the system’s dissipation toward a Markovian environment represented by a set of operators
\{L_{k}\}
called noise operators. The evolution of the quantum system is then described by the exponential map
\rho_{t}=e^{\mathcal{L}t}(\rho_{0})
. Importantly,
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
generates a quantum dynamical semigroup, i.e.
\{\Lambda_{t}\equiv e^{\mathcal{L}t}\}_{t\geq 0}
a one-parameter semigroup of CPTP maps also known as Markovian dynamics.
In the following, we will say that a super-operator is of
Lindblad-type
if it can be put in the form of Eq. (
17
).

Note that in general, in the derivation of TCL ME
shibata_generalized_1977
;
chaturvedi_time-convolutionless_1979
, the initial “big” model is considered to be Hamiltonian, i.e.
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)=-i[H,\cdot]
. However, mathematically, there is no reason to restrict to this case; hence, in this work, we start with the general assumption that
\mathcal{L}
is a generator of Lindblad form and then focus on the Hamiltonian case when necessary.

In many situations of interest in quantum physics, relevant projectors are represented by CPTP ones. We thus assume to be given a CPTP, not necessarily orthogonal (w.r.t.
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{HS}
), projector
\mathcal{P}:\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
\mathcal{P}^{2}=\mathcal{P}
, whose image will be denoted by
\mathscr{V}\equiv{\rm Im}\mathcal{P}
. This provides a generalization to the superoperator projector setting considered by
breuer2006
;
breuer2007
. Furthermore, for the sake of simplicity, we assume that
\mathcal{P}
admits
1
1

1

Note that this assumption is made for simplicity of presentation and can easily be lifted by restricting onto the support of
{\rm Im}\mathcal{P}
, see e.g.
wolf2012quantum

a full rank fixed point, i.e.
\exists\rho\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H})
,
\rho>0
such that
\mathcal{P}(\rho)=\rho
.
As we shall see in more detail later (in Sec.

;
blackadar2006operator

;
wolf2012quantum
;

;
bialonczyk2018application
;
blume2010information
;
grigoletto2024exact
;
wolf2010inverseeigenvalueproblemquantum
. However, for now it is sufficient to know that
\mathcal{P}
can be decomposed into two factors
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{R}
which take the name of reduction and injection maps
\mathcal{R}:\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{K})
and
\mathcal{J}:\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{K})\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
\mathcal{R}\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{K}}
the identity superoperator,
{\rm Im}\mathcal{J}=\mathscr{V}

\mathcal{K}

To provide some intuition about these mathematical objects, we anticipate the reduction and injection maps we are going to use in the applications of Sec.
V

•

Assume a bipartite case, i.e.
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}

\mathcal{P}(\rho)={\rm tr}_{B}(\rho)\otimes\tau
for some
\tau\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H}_{B})

•

Another CPTP projector we might want to consider is the projector onto the diagonal

IV.2
Connection with the physics literature

In the typical setting of TCL ME derivation, the system is considered to be bipartite, i.e.
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
and the dynamics purely Hamiltonian, i.e.
\mathcal{L}(\rho)=-i[H,\rho]
with Hamiltonian
H=H_{0}+\varepsilon V
where
H_{0}
is the free Hamiltonian of the system and bath,
V
is the interaction Hamiltonian and
\varepsilon
is the coupling strength.
Under these assumptions, and assuming that the coupling strength is small, the time-dependent generator
\mathcal{K}_{t}
defined in Eq. (
9
) is computed by performing a perturbative expansion in the coupling parameter. The time-dependent generator is then expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{t}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}_{n}(t)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\dots\int_{0}^{t_{n-2}}dt_{n-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum_{q}(-1)^{q}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{t}\dots\mathcal{L}_{t_{i}}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{t_{j}}\dots\mathcal{L}_{t_{k}}\mathcal{P}\dots\mathcal{P}

and where
\mathcal{L}_{t}=-i[H(t),t]
and
H(t)
is the Hamiltonian in rotating frame with respect to
H_{0}
. The series is then approximated by stopping at a given order
N
, i.e.
\mathcal{K}_{t}\approx\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{K}_{n}(t)
.

Under these assumptions, a recursive formula similar to Eq. (
14
) has been derived.
Specifically
(
nestmann2019time
, Eq. (38))
(but also
(
cerrillo2014non
, Eq. (2))

(
cygorek2025timenonlocalversustimelocallongtime
, Eq. (2))
,
(
gasbarri2018recursive
, Eq. (16))
) provides a recursive formula for the terms
\mathcal{K}_{n}(t)
of the time-dependent generator
\mathcal{K}_{t}
. However, these recursive formulas were derived in the rotating frame (w.r.t.
H_{0}
) under a weak coupling assumption and thus from an initial time-dependent model. This implies that the terms of the recursive formula contain time-ordered integrals that need to be solved to compute the terms of the expansion.

In contrast, in this work, we start from a time-independent model, hence Eq. (
14
) (which was derived independently from
nestmann2019time
;
cerrillo2014non
;
cygorek2025timenonlocalversustimelocallongtime
;
gasbarri2018recursive
) does not contain integrals, resulting in simpler computations.

Furthermore, the main difference that separates this work from most of the physics literature, see e.g.
colla2025unveiling
;
colla2025recursive
;
Tokieda_2025
is the fact that, instead of expanding the time-dependent generator in a power series of a perturbation parameter
\varepsilon
, we expand in the time variable
t
. This difference allows for a few benefits: It simplifies the calculations of the approximations and lifts the weak-coupling assumption, thus allowing one to explore the effects of strong-coupling regimes.

The main drawback introduced by the expansion in the time variable
t
is the fact that the approximate model holds only for small times. Nevertheless, as we highlighted above, while this consequence is clearly limiting, this situation is comparable to the typical derivation of TCL ME since no guarantees on the long-time limit are available. In contrast, in this work we were able to characterize how the error between the original and reduced models grows with
t
.

IV.3
Complete positivity of the first order

In this section, we recall relevant known results about CPTP projectors, their images, and reduced dynamics onto distorted algebras from the literature
lindblad1999general
;
wolf2012quantum
;
ticozzi2017alternating
;
bialonczyk2018application
;
blume2010information
;
grigoletto2024exact
;
wolf2010inverseeigenvalueproblemquantum

are summarized in the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a CPTP projector with a full rank fixed point
\bar{\rho}>0
,
\mathcal{P}(\bar{\rho})=\bar{\rho}
and let
\mathscr{V}
be its image, i.e.
\mathscr{V}\equiv{\rm Im}\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
.
Then there exist a decomposition of the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{k}(\mathcal{H}_{F,k}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{G,k}),

a unitary operator
U\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})

\tau_{k}\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H}_{G,k})
such that

\mathscr{V}=U\left(\bigoplus_{k}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{F,k})\otimes\tau_{k}\right)U^{\dagger}.

(18)

Furthermore,
\mathscr{V}
is a
*
-algebra closed with respect to the product
X\cdot_{\sigma}Y\equiv X\sigma Y
where

\sigma=U\left(\bigoplus_{k}\mathbb{1}_{F,k}\otimes\tau_{k}^{-1}\right)U^{\dagger}.

Let then
W_{k}
be a linear operator from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{H}_{F,k}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{G,k}
such that
W_{k}W_{k}^{\dagger}=\mathbb{1}_{F,k}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{G,k}

wolf2012quantum
. Then the CPTP projection
\mathcal{P}
onto
\mathscr{V}
takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(X)

\displaystyle=U\left[\bigoplus_{k}{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{G,k}}\left(W_{k}XW_{k}^{\dagger}\right)\otimes\tau_{k}\right]U^{\dagger}.

(19)

and is orthogonal with respect to the inner product
\left<X,Y\right>_{\sigma,\lambda}={\rm tr}[X^{\dagger}\sigma^{\lambda}Y\sigma^{1-\lambda}]
for
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

Proof of this result can be found in
lindblad1999general
and
wolf2010inverseeigenvalueproblemquantum
;
wolf2012quantum
;
tit2023
;
ticozzi2017alternating
;
johnson2015general
. Note that projectors of this type take the name of state extensions and are the dual of conditional expectations
petz2007quantum
;
blackadar2006operator
. Furthermore, note that a
*
-algebra closed with respect to a modified product often takes the name of
distorted algebra

blume2010information
, while the block structure provided in Eq. (
18

Wedderburn decomposition
.

The previous result provides us with a structure for the image of a CPTP projection whenever there exists a full rank state
\bar{\rho}
such that
\mathcal{P}(\bar{\rho})=\bar{\rho}

\mathscr{A}

\mathscr{A}\equiv\bigoplus_{k}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{F,k})

\mathcal{R}:\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})\to{\mathscr{A}}
and
\mathcal{J}:{\mathscr{A}}\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that,
\mathcal{R}\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{I}_{{\mathscr{A}}}
and
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{R}
.
Furthermore, one can prove

\mathcal{R}(X)=\bigoplus_{k}{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{G,k}}(W_{k}XW_{k}^{\dagger})=\bigoplus_{k}X_{F,k}=\check{X},

(20)

and for all
\check{X}\in\check{\mathscr{A}}
,
\check{X}=\bigoplus_{k}X_{F,k}
,

\mathcal{J}(\check{X})=U\bigg(\bigoplus_{k}X_{F,k}\otimes\tau_{k}\bigg)U^{\dagger}.

(21)

The reduction and injection maps given in equations (
20
) and (
21
) then allow us to compute the reduced dynamics
\mathcal{R}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{J}
restricted to the algebra
{\mathscr{A}}
. Then we can prove that the reduced generator

Theorem 3

Let

IV.4
Second order: The case of purely Hamiltonian evolution

While the results we derived up to this point hold for general Lindblad generators
\mathcal{L}
, we shall now focus on the special case of purely Hamiltonian evolution, i.e.
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)=-i[H,\cdot].
Other than the fact that this assumption is interesting in practical cases, we shall see that under this simplifying assumption we are able to prove that the second order term
\mathcal{F}_{(2)}
is also of Lindblad-type.

The next result is based on the fact that the squared of a purely Hamiltonian generator is equal to a dissipator:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{2}(\rho)

\displaystyle=-[H[H,\rho]]=-(H^{2}\rho-2H\rho H+\rho H^{2})

\displaystyle=2H\rho H-\{H^{2},\rho\}=\mathcal{D}_{\sqrt{2}H}(\rho).

(22)

This fact allows us to prove the next result.

Theorem 4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3
we have that
\mathcal{F}_{(2)}=\mathcal{R}\mathcal{L}^{2}\mathcal{J}-(\mathcal{R}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{J})^{2}
is of Lindblad-type and for
\mathcal{F}_{t,2}\equiv\mathcal{F}_{(1)}+t\mathcal{F}_{(2)}
we have that
\mathbb{T}e^{\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{F}_{s,2}ds}
forms a CPTP semigroup for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

By leveraging
(
grigoletto2024exact
, Proposition 5)
we have that
\mathcal{R}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{J}(\check{\rho})=-i[\check{H},\check{\rho}]
where
\check{H}=\mathcal{J}^{\dagger}(H)
hence, using equation (
22
) we obtain
(\mathcal{R}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{J})^{2}(\check{\rho})=\mathcal{D}_{\sqrt{2}\check{H}}(\check{\rho})
. On the other hand, by leveraging
(
grigoletto2025quantummodelreductioncontinuoustime
, Proposition 6 and Proposition 7)
, we have that
\mathcal{R}\mathcal{D}_{\sqrt{2}H}\mathcal{J}(\check{\rho})=\sum_{\check{C}\in\mathcal{X}(\sqrt{2}H)}\mathcal{D}_{\check{C}}(\check{\rho})
for a set of operators
\mathcal{X}(\sqrt{2}H)\subset\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{K})
. Importantly, by
(
grigoletto2025quantummodelreductioncontinuoustime
, Corollary 4)
, we have that
\mathcal{J}^{\dagger}(H)\in\mathcal{X}(\sqrt{2}H)
and thus we can write
\mathcal{R}\mathcal{D}_{\sqrt{2}H}\mathcal{J}(\check{\rho})=\sum_{\check{C}\in\mathcal{X}(\sqrt{2}H)/\{\sqrt{2}\check{H}\}}\mathcal{D}_{\check{C}}(\check{\rho})+\mathcal{D}_{\sqrt{2}\check{H}}(\check{\rho})
since
\sqrt{2}\check{H}=\mathcal{J}^{\dagger}(\sqrt{2}H)
. Then, by combining these two results we obtain

\mathcal{F}_{(2)}=\mathcal{R}\mathcal{L}^{2}\mathcal{J}-(\mathcal{R}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{J})^{2}=\sum_{\check{C}\in\mathcal{X}(\sqrt{2}H)/\{\sqrt{2}\check{H}\}}\mathcal{D}_{\check{C}}(\check{\rho})

which is the sum of a set of GKLS, purely dissipative generators and thus of Lindblad type. Combining this with Theorem
3
it trivially follows that
\mathcal{F}_{t,2}
is of Lindblad type for all
t\geq 0
.
∎

IV.5
Second order in the special case of bipartite systems

Since the proof of Theorem
4
is based on a few very technical results, here we want to present an pedagogical derivation of the same result in the special case of bipartite quantum systems. Note that this derivation is quite relevant in practice as it focuses on the most studied case of a bipartite system, but the cases included in the assumptions of Theorem
4
are more general. This exact case will be the focus of the two applications of interest studied in Sec
V.1
and
V.2
.

Let us assume that
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
and let assume that the CPTP projector
\mathcal{P}
is given by the
\mathcal{P}(\cdot)={\rm tr}_{E}(\cdot)\otimes\tau
for some full rank state
\tau\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H}_{E})
. The projector
\mathcal{P}
is then factorized into the reduction map
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)={\rm tr}_{E}(\cdot)
and the injection map
\mathcal{J}(\cdot)=\cdot\otimes\tau
.
In line with the assumptions of this section we assume that
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)=-i{\rm ad}_{H}(\cdot)
. Given the bipartite structure of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
we can always write
H=\sum_{k}S_{k}\otimes E_{k}
for two sets of Hermitian operators
\{S_{k}\}\subset\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
and
\{E_{k}\}\subset\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{E})
.

We can then proceed to compute the reduced generator approximated at second order
\mathcal{F}_{t,2}(\mu)
. First, using
(
grigoletto2024exact
, Proposition 5)
, we have that the generator at first order is

\mathcal{F}_{(1)}(\mu)=\mathcal{R}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{J}(\mu)=-i[\check{H},\mu]

where

\displaystyle\check{H}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}^{\dagger}(H)={\rm tr}_{E}[(\mathbb{1}_{S}\otimes\tau)H]=\sum_{k}a_{k}(\tau)S_{k}

and
a_{k}(\tau)\equiv{\rm tr}[\tau E_{k}].
Next, we can proceed to compute
\mathcal{F}_{(2)}
by first computing
\mathcal{R}\mathcal{L}^{2}\mathcal{J}
obtaining

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\mathcal{L}^{2}\mathcal{J}(\mu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}c_{j,k}(\tau)\left[2S_{j}\mu S_{k}-\{S_{k}S_{j},\mu\}\right]

where we defined
c_{j,k}(\tau)\equiv{\rm tr}[E_{j}\tau E_{k}]
.
On the other hand, since
(\mathcal{R}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{J})^{2}=\mathcal{D}_{\sqrt{2}\check{H}}

\displaystyle(\mathcal{R}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{J})^{2}(\mu)=\sum_{j,k}a_{j}(\tau)a_{k}(\tau)\left[2S_{j}\mu S_{k}-\{S_{k}S_{j},\mu\}\right]

and thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{(2)}(\mu)

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{R}\mathcal{L}^{2}\mathcal{J}-(\mathcal{R}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{J})^{2}](\mu)=

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}[\chi(\tau)]_{j,k}\left[2S_{j}\mu S_{k}-\{S_{k}S_{j},\mu\}\right]

where we defined the matrix
\chi(\tau)
whose elements are
[\chi(\tau)]_{j,k}\equiv c_{j,k}(\tau)-a_{j}(\tau)a_{k}(\tau)

One can then notice that the matrix
\chi(\tau)
coincides with a covariance matrix, i.e.

\displaystyle[\chi(\tau)]_{j,k}

\displaystyle={\rm tr}[E_{j}\tau E_{k}]-{\rm tr}[E_{j}\tau]{\rm tr}[E_{k}\tau]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[(E_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[E_{k}]\mathbb{1}_{E})(E_{j}-\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[E_{j}]\mathbb{1}_{E})]

is a positive semidefinite matrix.
Since
\chi(\tau)
is positive semidefinite, it is diagonalizable with a unitary matrix, i.e.
UU^{\dagger}=U^{\dagger}U=\mathbb{1}
such that
U\chi(\tau)U^{\dagger}=\gamma(\tau)
where

is a diagonal matrix of positive values (note that in reality

is also a function of
\tau
). We can then define

L_{h}=\sum_{j}\overline{[U]_{h,j}}S_{j}

to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{(2)}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}[\chi(\tau)]_{j,k}\left[2S_{j}\mu S_{k}-\{S_{k}S_{j},\mu\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,h}\underbrace{[U^{\dagger}]_{j,h}[\gamma(\tau)]_{h}[U]_{h,k}}_{[\chi(\tau)]_{j,k}}\left[2S_{j}\mu S_{k}-\{S_{k}S_{j},\mu\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,h}[\gamma(\tau)]_{h}\overline{[U]_{h,j}}[U]_{h,k}\left[2S_{j}\mu S_{k}-\{S_{k}S_{j},\mu\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{h}[\gamma(\tau)]_{h}\left[2L_{h}\mu L_{h}^{\dagger}-\{L_{h}^{\dagger}L_{h},\mu\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{h}[\gamma(\tau)]_{h}\mathcal{D}_{\sqrt{2}L_{h}}(\mu).

Because
\chi(\tau)
is positive semidefinite and thus
[\gamma(\tau)]_{h}
are non-negative, then
\mathcal{F}_{(2)}
is of Lindblad type. This implies that the approximated time-dependent generator at second order

\mathcal{F}_{t,2}(\mu)=-i[\check{H},\mu]+\sum_{h}t[\gamma(\tau)]_{h}\mathcal{D}_{\sqrt{2}L_{h}}(\mu)

is also of Lindblad type at all times
t\geq 0
as proved in Theorem
4
.

Note that in deriving the reduced generator
\mathcal{F}_{t,2}
there was no need to move the system description into a rotating frame thus proving one of the benefits of the proposed approach.

Applications to quantum models

In this section, we consider a few examples of interest:

I) The first example considers a qubit coupled to a bosonic bath. As the exact solution of such a model is well known, this example allows us to assess the goodness of the approximation proposed in this work.

II) Second, a qubit coupled to a finite bath is studied. Given the fact that the bath is finite-dimensional, this is typically considered a “highly non-Markovian model”. This example allows us to showcase the numerical procedure at higher orders for both a case where the original dynamics is purely Hamiltonian and a case where a dissipative term is included.

III) Finally, an Ising spin chain is studied. This example allows us to show that the proposed procedure works in cases that are more general than the bipartite case.

Dephasing spin boson model

Inspired by
lidar2001completely
;
reina2002decoherence
;
breuer2002theory
;
lidar2020lecturenotestheoryopen
, in this section we consider a dephasing spin-boson model to further showcase the advances and drawbacks of the proposed method. Such a model is typically used to model the dephasing of qubits in quantum computers
sun2025quantum
. Note that: i) in this section we make an exception to the assumption of finite-dimensional systems considered throughout the paper; ii) the model we are going to consider is exactly solvable hence the intent of this subsection is to showcase that the proposed approximation method provides results comparable to other approximation techniques, not to compare it against the exact solution of the model.
Nevertheless, this example helps us to understand the degrees of approximation that the proposed method offers and how the process unfolds.

The model is composed of a spin
\frac{1}{2}
system
\mathcal{H}_{S}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{2}
coupled to a bath
\mathcal{H}_{B}
composed of
N_{B}
bosonic modes,
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
. The model’s dynamics is driven by the total Hamiltonian
H=H_{S}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{B}+\mathbb{1}_{S}\otimes H_{B}+H_{\rm int}
where

\displaystyle H_{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{g}{2}\sigma_{z}

(23)

\displaystyle H_{B}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{B}}\omega_{k}\left(n_{k}+\frac{\mathbb{1}}{2}\right)

(24)

\displaystyle H_{\rm int}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{z}\otimes\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{B}}\lambda_{k}b_{k}+\overline{\lambda}_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}\right)

(25)

and where
n_{k}=b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}
and
b_{k}
are the bosonic number and annihilation operator for mode
k
respectively and satisfy
[b_{k},b_{l}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{k,l}\mathbb{1}
,
\omega_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
is the frequency of the mode and
\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
is the strength of the coupling between the mode and the spin.

We assume that the initial state is factorized
\rho_{0}=\rho_{S,0}\otimes\rho_{B}
with
\rho_{B}=e^{-\beta H_{B}}/{\rm tr}[e^{-\beta H_{B}}]
and some state
\rho_{S,0}\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
for the spin. We further assume that the projector
\mathcal{P}
is
\mathcal{P}(\rho)={\rm tr}_{B}(\rho)\otimes\rho_{B}
, which can be factorized into
\mathcal{R}(\rho)={\rm tr}_{B}(\rho)
and
\mathcal{J}(\sigma)=\sigma\otimes\rho_{B}
. Then we can verify
\mathcal{P}(\rho_{0})=\rho_{0}
.

This model has a known exact analytical solutions given by
(
lidar2020lecturenotestheoryopen
, eq. (336) and (374))
:

\rho_{S,t}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}[\rho_{S,0}]_{0,0}&e^{-2\gamma(t)t}[\rho_{S,0}]_{0,1}\\
e^{-2{\gamma(t)}t}[\rho_{S,0}]_{1,0}&[\rho_{S,0}]_{1,1}\\
\end{bmatrix}

where
\rho_{S,t}^{\prime}
is the state of the central spin in the rotating frame and

\gamma(t)=\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}\frac{1-\cos(\omega_{k}t)}{t}

with
\alpha_{k}\equiv 2\frac{|\lambda_{k}|^{2}}{\omega_{k}^{2}}\coth\left(\frac{\beta\omega_{k}}{2}\right)\in\mathbb{R}.

By taking the derivative of an off diagonal element we obtain:

\frac{d}{dt}[{\rho}_{S,t}^{\prime}]_{0,1}=-2\underbrace{\left[\gamma(t)+\frac{d\gamma(t)}{dt}t\right]}_{\equiv\xi(t)}[{\rho}_{S,t}^{\prime}]_{0,1}

where
\xi(t)=\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}\omega_{k}\sin(\omega_{k}t)
.
This dynamics corresponds to the differential equation

\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{S,t}^{\prime}=\xi(t)\mathcal{D}_{\sigma_{z}}[\rho_{S,t}^{\prime}]

for the state in rotating frame or, equivalently, to

\dot{\rho}_{S,t}=-i[H_{S},\rho_{S,t}]+\xi(t)\mathcal{D}_{\sigma_{z}}(\rho_{S,t})

(26)

for the state in the lab frame.

By following the procedure detailed in Sec.
IV.5
we can then proceed to compute the reduced time-dependent generator at second order
\mathcal{F}_{t,2}
.
Denoting
\left<X\right>_{B}\equiv{\rm tr}[\rho_{B}X]
, we recall that
(
lidar2020lecturenotestheoryopen
, eq. (331))
:

\displaystyle\left<b_{k}^{\dagger}\right>_{B}

\displaystyle=\left<b_{k}\right>_{B}=\left<b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{l}^{\dagger}\right>_{B}=\left<b_{k}b_{l}\right>_{B}=0,

\displaystyle\left<b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{k}\right>_{B}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{j,k}}{e^{\beta\omega_{k}}-1}.

We first compute the reduced Hamiltonian
\check{H}=\mathcal{J}^{\dagger}(H)
where
\mathcal{J}^{\dagger}(X)={\rm tr}_{B}[X(\mathbb{1}_{S}\otimes\rho_{B})]
. Noting that
{\rm tr}_{B}[H_{S}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{B}(\mathbb{1}_{S}\otimes\rho_{B})]=H_{S}{\rm tr}[\rho_{B}]=H_{S}
,
{\rm tr}_{B}[\mathbb{1}_{S}\otimes H_{B}(\mathbb{1}_{S}\otimes\rho_{B})]=\mathbb{1}_{S}\left<H_{B}\right>_{B}
and

\displaystyle{\rm tr}_{B}\left[\sigma_{z}\otimes\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{B}}\lambda_{k}b_{k}+\overline{\lambda}_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}\right)(\mathbb{1}_{S}\otimes\rho_{B})\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\sigma_{z}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{B}}\lambda_{k}\cancel{\left<b_{k}\right>_{B}}+\overline{\lambda}_{k}\cancel{\left<b_{k}^{\dagger}\right>_{B}}\right)=0

we have
\check{H}=H_{S}
as the term
\mathbb{1}\left<H_{B}\right>_{B}
only contributes to a phase and can thus be removed, leading to the first-order term
\mathcal{F}_{(1)}(\mu)=-i[\check{H},\mu]
. The second order term is instead found to be
\mathcal{F}_{(2)}(\mu)=\varphi\mathcal{D}_{\sigma_{z}}(\mu)
with
\varphi\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{N_{B}}\frac{2|\lambda_{k}|^{2}}{e^{\beta\omega_{k}}-1}
. All necessary calculations are detailed in Appendix
B
for the sake of clarity.

Combining both terms we obtain an approximate reduced model described by the time-dependent Lindblad equation

\dot{\mu}_{t}=-i[H_{S},\mu_{t}]+\varphi t\mathcal{D}_{\sigma_{z}}(\mu_{t}).

(27)

By comparing this equation with the exact solution of Eq. (
26
) we can observe that both the Hamiltonian and noise operator are correctly recovered whereas the time-dependent dissipation strength
\xi(t)
is approximated with
\varphi t
. Interestingly, the value
\varphi
does not coincide with the first order Taylor expansion of
\xi(t)
which is
\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}\omega_{k}
.

To better asses the quality of the reduced second order approximation we next compare the evolution of
\rho_{S,t}
and that of
\mu_{t}
with the coarse-grained approximation discussed in
(
lidar2020lecturenotestheoryopen
, eq. (332))
, i.e.
\tilde{\rho}_{S,t}\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
and

\dot{\tilde{\rho}}_{S,t}=-i[H_{S},\tilde{\rho}_{S,t}]+\gamma(\tau)\mathcal{D}_{\sigma_{s}}(\tilde{\rho}_{S,t})

(28)

where the constant
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
is specified later.

The bath coupling coefficients are going to be defined as
\lambda_{k}=\sqrt{J(\omega_{k})}

tamascelli2020quantum
where
J(\omega)
is the bath spectral density and is chosen to be

J(\omega)=\Lambda\omega_{c}^{1-s}\omega^{s}e^{-\frac{\omega}{\omega_{c}}}

(29)

where
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is an overall constant,
\omega_{c}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the bath cut-off frequency and
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the Ohmicity parameter which determines three cases of interest, i.e. Ohmic (
s=1
), sub-Ohmic(
s<1
) and super-Ohmic (
s>1
)
PhysRevB.92.195143
.

V.3
Ising spin chain

Although the idea of approximating a large model with a smaller one that is LTV is common in the setting of a bipartite system-environment couple, this is far from the only case where such an approach is useful. More in general, the same approaches can be adapted to cases where one wants to describe the evolution of only a small set of variables of interest of the original model. For this reason, in this section we showcase the use of the same methods presented before to reproduce the probability distribution in the standard basis.

In order to show that the proposed method works also in cases that are not bi-partite we here consider a spin-chain model. More specifically we consider a model composed of
N_{B}
spins, i.e.
\mathcal{H}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n}
where
n=2^{N_{B}}
whose dynamics is driven by the Ising Hamiltonian

H=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{B}}h\sigma_{z}^{(j)}+A\sum_{j=1}^{N_{B}-1}\sigma_{x}^{(j)}\sigma_{x}^{(j+1)}.

(30)

In this example, we consider
\mathcal{P}
to be the CPTP projector on the diagonal
\mathcal{P}(\rho)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left|j\middle>\middle<j\right|\rho\left|j\middle>\middle<j\right|
which can be factorized into
\mathcal{R}(\rho)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left|j\middle>\middle<j\right|\rho\left|j\right>
and
\mathcal{J}(\eta)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left|j\middle>\middle<j\right|\left<j\right|\eta
. Note that, in such a case,
\eta=\mathcal{R}(\rho)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a probability distribution, i.e.
\eta_{i}\geq 0
and
{\bm{1}}^{T}\eta=1
where
{\bm{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector of all ones. This implies that
\mathcal{F}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

As an initial condition we consider
\rho_{0}=\left|0\middle>\middle<0\right|
. Trivially,
\mathcal{P}(\rho_{0})=\rho_{0}
.

Numerically, we verified that
\mathcal{F}_{(1)}=\mathcal{F}_{(3)}=0
while
\mathcal{F}_{(2)}
is graphically represented in Fig.
7
where one can verify that
\mathcal{F}_{(2)}
is Metzler (a matrix
A
is Metzler if
A_{i,j}\geq 0
for all
i\neq j
) and is such that
{\bm{1}}^{T}\mathcal{F}_{(2)}=0
. Those two conditions are the classical equivalent to the properties characterizing a Lindblad generator and in fact imply that
e^{\mathcal{F}_{(2)}t}
is a stochastic matrix for all
t\geq 0
. This directly implies that
\mathbb{T}e^{\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{F}_{s,2}ds}
is CPTP, or stochastic in this case, for all
t\geq 0
.

VI
Conclusions and outlook

In this work, we explored a novel method for approximating the dynamics of a large autonomous system projected onto a given subspace with a time-dependent generator. The problem is inspired by the derivation of TCL ME in quantum physics but has been treated here in the general case of linear autonomous systems.

The main contribution of this work consists of a novel method for computing the coefficients of the time-dependent model that is polynomial in time and that approximates the evolution of the projected dynamics for small times. Importantly, the coefficients of the polynomial can be determined using a recursive formula.

Benefits of the proposed reduction method include: the method does not require to move into a rotating frame, making the calculations simpler; the iterative form of the coefficients allows for easy calculations of the terms; for lower orders the method offers an advantage with respect to truncating the exponential, as shown in Sec.
III.1
; the method does not rely on a perturbation on a small parameter
\varepsilon
allowing for analysis of systems in strong coupling regimes; when the original dynamics is purely Hamiltonian, the reduced time-dependent model at the lowest two orders is ensured to generate a CPTP two-parameter semigroup at all times. All of this comes at the cost of having a model that approximates the original evolution in the short-time regime.

Future work shall focus on:
i) extending Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
to the case of analytic generators;
ii) lifting the assumption
Px_{0}=x_{0}
;
iii) generalize the results presented here to include control inputs (both linear and bilinear);
iv) apply the proposed procedure to other quantum models in the strong coupling regime to study models that were not accessible before and further test the capabilities of the method;
v) combine the classical model reduction methods and the time-dependent model reduction proposed here to see if the combination of the two methods can allow to improve the accuracy of the reduced model. Note that a first step in this direction has been taken in the physics literature by
breuer2006
;
breuer2007
, where, including extra degrees of freedom, has been shown to improve the accuracy of the derived TCL ME.

Given the numerical examples of the central spin model presented in Sec.
V.2
we do not expect the reduced model to retain the CP property for orders higher than
N=3
. However, it is possible that, in the Hamiltonian case, for
N=3
one can prove that the evolution of the reduced model is necessarily CP at all times, as none of the simulation carried out while preparing this work contradicted this statement.
