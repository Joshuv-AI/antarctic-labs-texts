Unmasking Facial Melanosis: A Prospective Clinico-Dermoscopic Study
Authors: Sheethal K Jose, Priya Sara Kuryan, Diya Katrina Koikkara, Gauri Dinesh Mahabal, Leni George, C.V Dincy Peter, Susanne A. Pulimood, Meera Thomas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionVarious entities of acquired facial melanosis present with considerable clinical overlap, posing diagnostic challenges. Dermoscopy is being increasingly used in its diagnosis.ObjectivesTo assess dermoscopic and clinicopathological features of facial melanosis in Fitzpatrick skin types IV–VI.MethodsA prospective cross-sectional study of clinical and dermoscopic features of facial melanosis—lichen planus pigmentosus (LPP), pigmented contact dermatitis (PCD), and facial acanthosis nigricans (FAN)—was conducted. Melasma, periorbital hyperpigmentation, and nevus of Ota were excluded.ResultsFifty-six patients were included: 31 (55.4%) with LPP, 18 (32.1%) FAN, and seven (12.5%) PCD. Clinically, LPP showed significantly more involvement of face (55.2 ± 22.2%) (P=0.029), especially the chin (29, 93.5%) (P=0.006). Dermoscopy revealed more than one pattern of pigment structures in 54 (96.4%) patients. Predominant patterns in LPP were reticular (21, 67.7%), peppered (15, 48.4%), and annular (13, 41.9%). There was an overlap of patterns like annular, annular-granular, and reticular in both LPP and PCD. Peppered (15, 48.4%) was significantly more in LPP (P=0.023), while linear dots were seen exclusively in LPP (4, 12.9%); smudged was significantly more in PCD (6, 85.7%) and FAN (15, 83.3%) (P=0.003). Concentric circles (16, 88.9%) and parallel lines (14, 77.8%) were significantly more in FAN (P<0.001 each), while crypts-gyri (10, 55.6%) and papillary projections (8, 44.4%) were seen exclusively in FAN. Combination of dots and clods was also significantly more in LPP (26, 83.9%; P=0.007), whereas blue-gray pigmentation (19, 61.3%) was observed exclusively in LPP.ConclusionDistinct dermoscopic features aid in differentiating acquired facial melanosis. Blue-gray dots and clods in peppered, reticular, and linear dots patterns favor LPP, while concentric circles, parallel lines, crypts-gyri, and papillary projections suggest FAN. Recognition of these patterns may obviate biopsy in cosmetically sensitive areas.

Introduction

Acquired facial melanosis is a common cosmetic problem with considerable psychological morbidity due to its chronic course and absence of an effective therapy. Though conditions like melasma are readily diagnosed, other entities overlap both clinically and histopathologically. There has been a lot of recent literature targeted towards clearly defining these clinically overlapping entities. Acquired dermal macular hyperpigmentation (ADMH) encompasses conditions with dermal pigment incontinence (PI), with or without active interface dermatitis. This includes lichen planus pigmentosus (LPP), ashy dermatosis, Riehl’s melanosis (RM), pigmented contact dermatitis (PCD), and idiopathic macular eruptive pigmentation (IMEP). Recently, the term “acquired macular pigmentation of uncertain etiology” (MPUE) has also been described [1–3].

Within ADMH, LPP and PCD characteristically affect the face. Another common condition that has been increasingly reported is facial acanthosis nigricans (FAN). In early stages, and when presenting with diffuse and extensive involvement of the face, it can be challenging to differentiate FAN from other causes of facial melanosis clinically. Even though histopathology may be diagnostic, patients may not be keen on a biopsy from the face. Dermoscopy is an important noninvasive diagnostic technique, and there are studies describing the general dermoscopic features in LPP/ADMH. However, there is a paucity of comprehensive research on differentiating the entities under ADMH. Early accurate diagnosis enhances management, as treatments differs between these conditions. For LPP, prompt systemic therapy can prevent progression and improve outcome [4].

Objectives

Primary objective of the study was to describe the clinical profile and characterize the dermoscopic patterns in acquired facial melanosis in Fitzpatrick skin types IV to VI-focusing on LPP, PCD, and FAN. Dermoscopic and histopathological features were also correlated. As a secondary objective, we attempted to differentiate the dermoscopic features in the various subtypes.

Materials And Methods

Study Design

A prospective descriptive study was conducted from March 2018 to February 2019 after approval from the institutional review board (IRB no. 11183/2018).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Adult patients (>18 years) presenting with facial hyperpigmentation to dermatology outpatient clinic of a tertiary care hospital in South India were screened. Patients with melasma, periorbital hyperpigmentation, and nevus of Ota were excluded.

Clinical Evaluation

A detailed history and examination included symptoms, extent, color, pattern of pigment distribution, and extra-facial involvement along with BSA. Prior treatment details were documented, including systemic or topical therapies, as well as procedural interventions like chemical peeling and LASERs. Patients using topicals in the immediate past discontinued their use for at least five days before inclusion in the study. Patients were also off systemic therapy for at least three months before inclusion.

Laboratory Evaluation

All patients underwent tests to rule out systemic causes of hyperpigmentation, including Hb, ANA, TSH, and Vitamin B12 levels. A patch test using the Systopic standard series was done in suspected PCD.

Dermoscopic Evaluation

A non-polarized videodermoscope, FotoFinder Systems GmbH (software version from 2.1), was used at 20x and 50x magnifications. Dermoscopy was performed from all quadrants of the face if there were extensive lesions or from involved areas.

Dermoscopy was performed by the same investigator (SK), and images were stored in the Fotofinder. The archived images were analyzed by two investigators (DP and SK) together. The findings were the consensus reached between the two investigators.

Basic descriptive dermoscopic structures described were dots/clods/lines/circles, while color of pigmentation were brown, gray, and blue-gray.

Dermoscopy was followed by a biopsy of the most representative site or latest area of involvement on the face. The final diagnosis was made based on the clinical presentation and histopathology. The patients were classified as LPP, PCD, FAN, or miscellaneous/unclassified. The dermoscopic features of these entities were noted and compared. Severity of PI was compared with dermoscopic findings.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was done using SPSS software (version 16). Categorical data were summarized as counts and percentages. Numerical variables were summarized as mean and standard deviation, if normally distributed, and median with range/interquartile range, if non-normally distributed. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square test. Continuous variables across disease groups and severity grades were compared via independent t-test and ANOVA.

Results

Of the 64 patients recruited, eight cases were excluded due to associated systemic causes of hyperpigmentation, nonspecific histopathology, or an unclassified clinical diagnosis. Thus, 56 patients were included: 31 (55.4%) LPP, 18 (32.1%) FAN, and seven (12.5%) PCD patients. The mean age of patients was 43.6 ± 12.6 years. The average percentage of face involvement was 49.3 ± 23.1%.

Overview Of Clinical Profiles

The clinical profile of patients is summarized in Table 2. Females constituted more than 80% of LPP and PCD: 25 (80.6%) and six (85.7%), respectively. Thyroid disorder was noted in seven (22.6%) cases of LPP, significantly higher than the other groups (P=0.040). History of long-term use of cosmetics was reported in 20 (64.5%) cases of LPP and in all patients of PCD. However, a patch test was done only in two patients, with negative results.

Clinically, all the patients with LPP had gray pigmentation, with almost three quarters of them having slate gray-color (23, 74.2%). Blue-gray color, seen in eight (25.8%) cases, was noted exclusively in LPP. Pigmentation was diffuse in more than 80% of the patients across all three groups. Blotchy pigment was significantly more in LPP (11, 35.5%, P=0.007), while velvety appearance was seen only in three (16.7%) cases of FAN (Figure 1). Extent of facial involvement was highest in LPP (55.2 ± 22.2%), followed by FAN (46.6 ± 22.6%) and PCD (30.6 ± 18.5%) (P=0.029).

Eighteen (32.1%) patients gave a history of use of topical treatments, including topical corticosteroids previously. Of these, 10 (32.3%) were from the LPP group, six (33.3%) from FAN, and two (28.6%) from PCD. Only a few patients continued these agents in the immediate past, but all discontinued them for at least five days prior to dermoscopy and biopsy. Also, 10 (17.9%) patients had history of treatment with systemic agents—four (7.1%) each with dapsone and methotrexate, and three (5.4%) patients with systemic steroids.

Comparison Of Dermoscopic Features

Dermoscopic features of the three different entities are summarized and compared in Table 3.

Basic Dermoscopic Structures and Color

More than one color of pigment structure was seen in 32 (57.1%) patients (Figure 2). Blue-gray pigment structures were seen exclusively in LPP, while brown color was seen in 11 (35.5%) cases of LPP, which was lesser than in the other two groups, the difference being statistically significant (P<0.001). On comparing with the other groups, combination of dots and clods was significantly more in LPP (P=0.007) (Figure 3). In the FAN group, the presence of dots alone was seen in six (33.3%) cases, which was statistically significant (P=0.018). Also, brown dots were seen in six (33.3%). Pigment structures were arranged regularly in 15(48.4%) cases of LPP, whereas they were irregularly distributed in the majority of FAN (14, 77.8%) and PCD (5, 71.4%) (P=0.201). Pigment deposition was peri-follicular and peri-eccrine in all patients. Follicular plugs were present in 13 (72.2%) FAN, four (57.1%) PCD, and 15 (48.4%) cases of LPP. They are more commonly seen in the face due to the higher density of hair follicles.

Dermoscopic Patterns of Pigment Deposition

More than 95% of cases (54, 96.4%) had more than one pattern of pigment deposition (Figure 4). There was also considerable overlap in the pigment patterns between the groups. Among LPP cases, reticular pattern was seen in 21 (67.7%), peppered in 15 (48.4%), annular in 13 (41.9%), smudged in 12 (38.7%), annular-granular in six (19.4%), and linear dots in four (12.9%) (Figure 5). Skin creases were pale/spared in four (12.9%). Also, four (12.9%) cases showed white dots (Table 4).

In PCD, common patterns were reticular (7, 100%), smudged (6, 85.7%), and annular (5, 71.4%) (Figure 6), while concentric circles (16, 88.9%), smudged (15, 83.3%), parallel lines (14, 77.8%), crypts-gyri (10, 55.6%), and papillary projections (8, 44.4%) were the common patterns in FAN (Figure 7).

On comparing different groups, reticular pattern was predominantly seen in LPP and PCD than in FAN, values being statistically significant (P<0.001). Peppered was absent in PCD, but on comparing LPP and FAN, it was significantly more in LPP (P=0.023). Linear dots were seen exclusively in LPP. Annular pattern was not observed in FAN, and it was lower in LPP compared to PCD, which was statistically significant at 5%. Concentric circles and parallel lines were significantly more frequent in FAN, while crypts-gyri and papillary projections were seen exclusively in FAN. Smudged appearance was seen significantly more frequently in PCD and FAN (P=0.003)

Histopathology

The main features in LPP were epidermal flattening (18, 58.1%), PI (31, 100%), which was predominantly moderate in severity (26, 83.9%), interface change (21, 67.7%), and basal cell pigmentation (11, 35.5%). PCD showed hyperkeratosis (5, 71.4%), PI (5, 71.4%), predominantly mild in severity (3, 42.9%), and focal interface change and basal cell pigmentation (4, 57.1% each).

There was a significant correlation between the color of pigment structures on dermoscopy and severity of PI; brown was associated with absent or mild PI (P<0.001) and blue-gray with moderate PI (P=0.005). However, dermoscopic patterns showed no significant association with severity of PI.

Discussion

The incidence of acquired facial melanosis is on the rise. According to a recent Delphi census published in the Indian Journal of Dermatology, Venereology and Leprology, ADMH has been accepted as a suitable term to describe LPP and related entities like erythema dyschromicum perstans, ashy dermatosis, RM, and PCD [2]. Characteristic dermoscopic features in entities of ADMH, if delineated, can help avoid a facial biopsy, used to guide treatment, and also to assess treatment response.

In our study, accentuation of pseudonetwork was seen in 87.1% of LPP, 94.4% of FAN, and all cases of PCD. This is due to pronounced hyperpigmentation in the flattened rete ridges in facial skin, interrupted by the surface openings of adnexa, which is also aggravated by chronic sun exposure and ageing [9,10]. It presents as a light brown, irregular network and corresponds to increased melanin in basal keratinocytes [11]. It was described in 64.9% LPP patients in the study by Pirmez et al. and 42% in the study by Sharma et al [12,13].

Presence of fine or coarse blue-gray dots or clods is related to pigment in dermal melanophages. In our study, 61.3% LPP patients presented with blue-gray clods or/and dots. In the study by Pirmez et al., speckled blue-gray dots were described in 35.1% patients, while blue-gray dots arranged in circles were seen in 18.9% [12]. There was a regular arrangement of pigment structures in 48.4% of our LPP cases, while it was seen in 22.2% FAN and 28.6% PCD cases. This can help distinguish LPP from other conditions that may show irregular or patchy pigment structures. In the study by Devanda et al., globules were regular in 25.9% cases [14].

In our study, reticular pattern was significantly more frequent in the LPP and PCD groups (67.7% and 100%, respectively), while peppered was significantly more frequent in LPP (48.4%), and linear dots were seen exclusively in LPP. Though annular (41.9%) and annular-granular (19.4%) were less common in LPP, all these patterns represent a continuous spectrum on dermoscopy that corresponds to PI. According to Gungor et al. and Mendez et al., diffuse peppering pattern is seen in the early phase of LPP, progressing and converging over time to form reticular, linear, and cobblestone patterns [4,15], while in the study by Chamli et al., reticular and diffuse were more prevalent in the early stages, and blotches were indicative of long-standing disease [16]. Reticular pattern was described in 7% and 25.9% of LPP patients, respectively, in the studies by Sharma et al. and Devanda et al. Peppering was described in 48.1% of patients in the study by Devanda et al. They also described linear/hem pattern (59.3%), arciform (37%), and incomplete reticular (39.5%) [13,14]. In the study by Vinay et al. on ADMH, reticular was seen in 29.4%, Chinese letter in 41.2%, and diffuse pattern in 33.3% [3]. These differences in patterns might be due to the inclusion of extra-facial sites in the other studies, differences in the duration, severity, and the dynamic evolution of disease.

Sparing of skin creases was seen in 12.9% of our LPP and 16.7% FAN cases due to reduced friction in skin furrows, as described previously by Gungor et al [15]. Also, the white dots seen in 12.9% LPP cases represent prominent pale follicular or eccrine opening on a dark pigment background and indicate areas spared amid dense dermal melanophages in severe cases [4,15].

In PCD, accentuation of pseudo-network was observed in all our patients, consistent with studies by Shen et al. and Wang et al [17,18]. Other dermoscopic features were combination of dots and clods (42.9%), telangiectasias (71.4%) and follicular plugs (57.1%). According to Wang et al., gray dots/granules, and telangiectasia were described in all patients, followed by slight flour-like scales (53.3%), follicular keratotic plugs, and perifollicular whitish halo (46.7% each). Perifollicular whitish halo, which corresponds to perifollicular fibrosis and floury scales, reported more prominently over the neck, were absent in our patients [17–19]. Prasad et al. reported exaggerated pseudo-reticular pigmentation, patchy pigmentation, dots, globules, fine scales, perifollicular halos, and follicular plugs [20].

In our patients with PCD, reticular (100%) was also the most common pattern, as in LPP, followed by smudged (85.7%) and annular (71.4%). However, the difference from LPP was mainly in the color of pigment deposition. It was predominantly blue-gray (61.3%) in LPP. while brown (100%) and gray (57.1%) were seen in PCD, reflecting more prominent PI and deeper pigment deposition in LPP. Superimposition of brown globules has been described previously in PCD by Ankad BS et al [21].

In FAN, classic crypts-gyri pattern were seen in 55.6%, while concentric circles (88.9%), smudged (83.3%), and parallel lines (77.8%) were more common. Papillary projections and brown dots were seen in 44.4% and 33.3%, respectively. Studies describing dermoscopy of FAN are limited and are mostly case reports. Pardeshi et al. reported sulci-crista cutis in all patients. Other findings were papillary projections (83.5%), hyperpigmented dots (46.6%), and crypts (29.1%) [22]. Global arrangement of ridges and furrows more prominent on non-polarized dermoscopy have been described as “barren land or crocodile skin-like appearance”. Polarized imaging allows better visualization of hyperpigmented dots and streaks [23,24]. Shah et al. reported crista-sulcus cutis and hyperpigmented dots in crista cutis in all patients and brown globules and perifollicular pigmentation in 80% of cases. Perifollicular pigmentation could be comparable to concentric circles seen in our cases (88.9%) [25,26].

In our study, brown pigment was associated with absent/mild PI and blue-gray with moderate PI, similar to the study by Chamli et al. Dermoscopic patterns in our study did not correlate with PI severity as in the study by Devanda et al [14]. However, Chamli et al. found severe PI associated with blotches, and Vinay et al. reported reticular and diffuse patterns to late stage of disease and severe PI [3,16].

Conclusion

Previous dermoscopic studies of ADMH have predominantly used metaphoric terminologies. In contrast, in this series, we have utilized standardized descriptive terminology defined by the International Dermoscopy Society to characterize acquired facial melanosis in skin of color. Another highlight of the study is the focus on melanosis involving only the face, as this addresses potential differences between dermoscopy of face and non-facial sites. We observed that LPP predominantly showed a combination of blue-gray dots and clods. Among the common patterns, reticular and peppered were significantly more common in LPP, while linear dots were seen exclusively seen in LPP, but they did not correlate with the severity of PI; PCD displayed patterns analogous to LPP, but with brown-gray pigment deposition. This underscores that the pigment color rather than pattern will help to differentiate the two conditions. Though FAN can mimic LPP and PCD clinically at times, the distinctive dermoscopic features of concentric circles, parallel lines, and crypts-gryi reliably confirm the diagnosis. Presence of parallel lines or concentric circles also suggest a diagnosis of FAN even without classic crypts-gyri. Thus, these dermoscopic features will facilitate in differentiating the common subtypes of facial melanosis and guide targeted management. Multicenter studies with uniform dermoscopic descriptions and follow-up will enhance our understanding of dermoscopy of acquired facial pigmentary disorders and help develop standardized dermoscopy-based diagnostic and prognostic guidelines.

Limitations

The number of patients in the different subtypes, especially in PCD, was low, limiting overall comparison and generalizability. As this was a descriptive study, the available number of patients were included in the study, and the association presented may lack power. Direct comparison of our findings with other case series and studies was not possible, as the dermoscopic terminologies and clinical inclusion criteria varied. It was a single-center study.
