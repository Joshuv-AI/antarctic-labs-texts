Costello Syndrome Associated With Somatic Mosaicism of Rare p.Gly13Asp HRAS Variant: Expanding the Phenotypic Spectrum
Authors: Jovan Lalosevic, Katarina Djordjevic, Mirjana Gajic‐Veljic, Sonja Pavlovic, Vladimir Gasic, Marina Andjelkovic, Milos Nikolic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCostello syndrome (CS) is a rare RASopathy, mostly caused by de novo heterozygous pathogenic variants in the HRAS gene. Over 80% of cases involve the germline p.Gly12Ser variant, resulting in a fairly uniform phenotype of neuro‐cardio‐facio‐cutaneous involvement with an increased risk of malignancy. Consequences of other rare HRAS variants are less well understood due to the limited number of reported cases.MethodsAn adult, young woman was referred due to sparse, slow‐growing scalp hair, Blaschko‐linear hyperpigmentation, acanthosis nigricans, palmoplantar hyperkeratosis, and joint hyperlaxity. Molecular, imaging, and detailed laboratory studies were performed.ResultsAlthough initial clinical exome‐ and whole‐exome sequencing (WES) were inconclusive, indicating possible mosaicism, subsequent WES from hair‐derived DNA samples revealed somatic mosaicism for the rare HRAS p.Gly13Asp variant. Brain MRIs showed a cerebral cavernoma, while cardiological evaluation, urinalysis, abdominal, and pelvic ultrasound were unremarkable. Nevertheless, she remains under close follow‐up.ConclusionAmong the ten reported individuals carrying the p.Gly13Asp variant, our patient is only the second with confirmed mosaicism and the fifth mosaic CS case described to date. This case expands the phenotypic spectrum of CS and highlights the need for multi‐tissue analysis in attenuated or atypical presentations to ensure a correct diagnosis, oncological risk assessment, and informed genetic and reproductive counseling.

Introduction

Costello syndrome (CS) is a rare genetic disorder with multisystemic manifestations, including severe failure to thrive, short stature, coarse facial features, cardiac abnormalities such as tachyarrhythmia and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, developmental delay, and a predisposition to papillomata and malignant tumors (Gripp et al. 2019). CS is a part of the RASopathies, a group of neuro‐cardio‐facio‐cutaneous syndromes caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in genes encoding components of the ubiquitous RAS/MAPK (Rat Sarcoma virus/Mitogen‐activated Protein Kinase) signaling pathway, resulting in gain‐of‐function effects and pathway upregulation. CS is most commonly caused by de novo germline variants in the HRAS gene (Ferrito et al. 2024; Gripp et al. 2019). Despite its rarity, with an estimated prevalence ranging from 1:380,000 to 1:1,290,000 live births (Abe et al. 2012; Giannoulatou et al. 2013), more available molecular diagnosis had led to an increasing number of genotype–phenotype studies. Over 80% of patients harbor the recurrent p.Gly12Ser missense variant, associated with the classic CS phenotype (Gripp et al. 2019), and most of them present during infancy with a recognizable constellation of systemic findings. Rarely, CS patients remain undiagnosed until later in life due to attenuated or atypical clinical manifestations (Sol‐Church et al. 2009). This variability may be attributed to the lower pathogenic potential of other germline HRAS variants (Leoni et al. 2022). However, postzygotic HRAS variants arising early during embryogenesis should also be considered in these instances. Mosaic CS has been reported in four patients to date, with a clinical spectrum ranging from an oligosymptomatic toddler (Bertola et al. 2017) to a classic phenotype indistinguishable from the germline form (Gripp et al. 2006; Sol‐Church et al. 2009).

Case Report

A 21‐year‐old woman was referred due to thin and slow‐growing hair since birth, unresponsive to prolonged topical 5% minoxidil treatment. At 11 months, areas of hyperpigmentation appeared, followed by focal and linear hyperkeratosis on the palms and soles. From the age of 14 years, she had several peri‐ and intranasal papillomata that were interpreted as viral warts and as such removed. Other personal, medical, and family history were unremarkable. She was born at term to non‐consanguineous parents following an uncomplicated pregnancy, with a birth weight of 3350 g. No macrocephaly, feeding difficulties, or failure to thrive were noted during infancy. Menarche occurred at 11 years of age and cycles were regular thereafter. Motor and cognitive development were normal, without the need for special education, and at the time of presentation she was excelling in her university studies. Peripheral blood chromosomal karyotype analysis, performed at 12 years of age, revealed a normal female karyotype (46, XX). Although she had been initially managed under the presumptive diagnosis of systematized epidermal nevus and hypotrichosis, the constellation of findings raised suspicion of an underlying syndromic disorder.

Initial Clinical Evaluation and Diagnostic Work‐Up

Clinical examination revealed sparse, brittle, light brown hair with interspersed linear dark strands (Figure 1A–C), areas of Blaschko‐linear and mottled hyperpigmentation on the trunk and extremities (Figure 1D,E), acanthosis nigricans on the neck and in the axillary folds (Figure 2A,B), and linear palmar hyperkeratosis and plantar hyperkeratosis at the pressure points (Figure 2C–F). Except for mild hypertelorism, prominent earlobes, and cutis hyperelastica of the face, no other craniofacial anomalies were present (Figure 1A). Hyperextension of the fingers and wrists was noted (Figure 2G,H). She was 154 cm tall (−2.18 SD for the adult height of women in our population (Popovic et al. 2013)) and her weight was 55 kg. Neurological examination was normal. Ophthalmological assessment identified myopia and mild astigmatism. Laboratory investigations, along with urine analysis were within reference range. Thyroid function testing showed elevated TSH levels with normal FT4, suggestive of subclinical hypothyroidism.

Molecular Analysis

Genomic DNA was isolated from whole blood using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) and subsequently analyzed by next‐generation sequencing (NGS). Clinical exome sequencing analysis (CES) and whole‐exome sequencing analysis (WES) were performed using Illumina TruSight One panel and the Illumina DNA Prep with Exome 2.5 Enrichment protocol (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), respectively. Analysis of sequencing‐by‐synthesis was performed on Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Variants were annotated, filtered, and classified using VarSome (Saphetor, Lausanne, Switzerland).

HRAS c38G>A; pGly13Asp heterozygous variant was revealed and classified as pathogenic using the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines (Richards et al. 2015). The variant was reported to be present in 8% of alleles, pointing out a deviation from VAF (variant allele frequency) of 50% indicating heterozygosity. This allelic imbalance was considered a consequence of mosaicism. Additional WES analyses on DNA isolated from the light, thin hair and normally pigmented hair were conducted. Namely, 4–5 strands of hair with visible roots were selected. The hair strands, with their roots preserved, were cut and placed into a tube. After that, DNA was isolated using QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). The same HRAS variant was detected in DNA isolated from the lighter, thin hair (VAF of 35%), whereas DNA from the thick, normally pigmented hair contained only the wild‐type allele and variant allele was not detected at all, confirming the presence of somatic mosaicism.

Post‐Diagnostic Workup and Follow‐Up

Following the molecular diagnosis of Costello syndrome, a further diagnostic workup was performed. Brain MRI revealed a focal lesion consistent with a cavernoma in the left frontal periventricular region, associated with a small venous angioma, without signs of hemorrhage. The patient was evaluated by a neurosurgeon, who recommended only follow‐up with annual MRI. Dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DEXA) was performed and revealed decreased bone mineral density consistent with osteopenia, accompanied by laboratory‐confirmed vitamin D deficiency. Supplementation with cholecalciferol and oral calcium was initiated. Cardiological evaluation, including ECG and echocardiography, abdominal and pelvic ultrasounds were unremarkable. Annual dermatological, cardiological, abdominal ultrasound and brain MRI assessments along with semiannual urine analysis have been continued, with no abnormalities detected during a two‐year follow‐up period.

Discussion

Pathogenic HRAS variants in CS almost predominantly involve glycine at residue 12, while alterations at residue 13 are rare and linked to an attenuated phenotype (Bertola et al. 2017; Gripp et al. 2011). The p.Gly13Asp variant has been reported in ten CS patients so far, all of them exhibiting early failure to thrive and short stature. Nine of the patients had developmental delay; nevertheless, the single case without cognitive impairment revealed somatic mosaicism. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias were present in eight cases (Bertola et al. 2017).

Craniofacial features observed in typical CS include macrocephaly, curly and sparse hair, prominent eyebrows and eyelashes, full nasal tip, thick earlobes, and a wide mouth with full lips (Bessis et al. 2024; Gripp et al. 2019). Less prominent facial dysmorphism, as reported in our case, was also noted in previously reported cases of p.Gly13Asp (Bertola et al. 2017), as well as p.Gly13Cys variant (Gripp et al. 2011). Additional finding, almost exclusively reported with pathogenic residue 13 variants, is sparse, slow‐growing hair, not requiring haircuts and resembling loose anagen hair syndrome (Bertola et al. 2017; Bessis et al. 2024; Gripp et al. 2011) compared to curly hair usually present in the classic phenotype. The direct phenotypic effect of the pathogenic variant in our patient is demonstrated by the short and thin hair shafts, harboring the mutant allele, compared to the adjacent longer, thicker, and normally pigmented hair harboring the wild‐type allele. Similarly, the presence of irregular hyperpigmented streaks interposed with areas of lighter, unaffected skin on the trunk and extremities, is indicative of mosaicism. Given that cutaneous hyperpigmentation is a known feature of CS, more frequently associated with pathogenic residue 13 variants (Bessis et al. 2024), it is likely that the regions of unaffected skin correspond to wild‐type cells, whereas the hyperpigmented areas represent clones of cells harboring the variant. Keratinization disorders, such as palmoplantar keratoderma and acanthosis nigricans, are particularly useful in distinguishing CS from other RASopathies (Bessis et al. 2024).

Skin and joint hyperlaxity are described in a minority of CS patients, most of which are harboring p.Gly12Ser (Gripp et al. 2019), except for one patient harboring p.Gly13Cys. Our patient also demonstrated this phenotype. Nevertheless, our patient is the first reported case associated with p.Gly13Asp variant, further adding to the variability of clinical features.

Our patient had hypothyroidism and osteopenia associated with laboratory‐confirmed vitamin D deficiency. Although subclinical hypothyroidism affects approximately 3%–5% (Yoo and Chung 2021) and osteopenia up to 40% (Xiao et al. 2022) of the adult general population, their co‐occurrence is clinically relevant and consistent with previously reported endocrine manifestations of CS including thyroid dysfunction, growth hormone deficiency and impaired bone mineralization (Gripp et al. 2019; Leoni et al. 2014), supporting regular endocrine and bone density surveillance even in early adulthood. Notably, our patient had menarche at a typical age, in contrast to most females with CS, who exhibit delayed or incomplete puberty or amenorrhea, often due to central hypogonadism (White et al. 2005). Normal pubertal development in our patient suggests intact reproductive function, accompanied by a potential risk of transmitting the pathogenic HRAS variant to offspring, as evidenced by a previously reported case of a father with p.Gly12Ser HRAS somatic mosaicism and mild phenotype, who passed a variant through the germline to his son with classic CS (Sol‐Church et al. 2009). Therefore, in case of pregnancy in our patient, prenatal counseling is mandatory.

Normal neurocognitive development in our patient may reflect either a lower pathogenic potential of the p.Gly13Asp variant or the protective effect of mosaicism, warranting further investigation in larger cohorts. Similarly, the only previously reported case of mosaic p.Gly13Asp variant involved a 4‐year‐old girl who exhibited normal neurodevelopmental milestones (Bertola et al. 2017). In contrast, two reported individuals with the mosaic p.Gly12Ser variant presented with significant developmental delays and intellectual disability (Gripp et al. 2006; Sol‐Church et al. 2009).

Cavernomas and venous angioma observed on the brain MRI of our patient have not been previously reported in CS. Given that cerebral cavernous malformations are found in only 0.4%–0.8% of the general population (Mouchtouris et al. 2015), their occurrence in our patient is unlikely to be incidental, and together with other vascular malformations previously described in CS, such as cutaneous haemangiomas and a renal angioma (Abe et al. 2012), may point to a broader vascular phenotype in CS, potentially driven by RAS/MAPK pathway upregulation. More commonly, CNS anomalies in CS include posterior fossa crowding due to progressive cerebellar enlargement, Chiari malformation, and optic nerve atrophy (Gripp et al. 2019).

Papillomas in CS are benign epithelial proliferations that typically present on the nasal alae and anterior nares, emerging from infancy to early adulthood and affecting approximately 72% of individuals (Siegel et al. 2012). According to medical records, our patient had several lesions consistent with CS‐related papillomas, interpreted as viral warts and removed either by curettage or electrocautery. While initial theories of CS‐papillomas suggested link to HPV, murine models support a non‐viral etiology, implicating hyperactivation of the RAS/MAPK pathway due to pathogenic HRAS variant, leading to benign papillomas, some of which may progress to squamous cell carcinoma (Brown et al. 1986). Likewise, risk for malignancies is the highest in CS among all RASopathies, with a predisposition to embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma and neuroblastoma in childhood, and bladder cancer in adolescence and early adult life, particularly in CS patients harboring p.Gly12Ala (Gripp et al. 2019). Prevalence of papillomas in ten reported individuals harbouring p.Gly13Asp variant is similar to individuals with p.Gly12Ser. However, no malignancies have been reported in individuals carrying the p.Gly13Asp or p.Gly13Cys HRAS variants (Bertola et al. 2017; Gripp et al. 2011), indicating that the transforming potential of HRAS is dependent on the site of amino acid substitution, as previously hypothesized (Fasano et al. 1984).

Somatic alterations in the genes coding for proteins of the RAS/MAPK pathway are associated with a heterogeneous group of diseases known as mosaic RASopathies (Carli et al. 2022). As noted, somatic pathogenic HRAS variants have been identified in mosaic forms of Costello syndrome (Bertola et al. 2017; Girisha et al. 2010; Gripp et al. 2006; Sol‐Church et al. 2009), but also in isolated malformations affecting the skin, such as nevus sebaceous, keratinocytic epidermal nevi, melanocytic nevi, nevus spilus, and woolly hair nevus. Moreover, HRAS mosaicism is observed in true mosaic RASopathies, which are presumably lethal in non‐mosaic form, such as epidermal nevus syndromes, Schimmelpenning‐Feuerstein‐Mims syndrome (linear sebaceous nevus syndrome), cutaneous skeletal hypophosphatemia syndrome, and phacomatosis pigmentokeratotica (Carli et al. 2022; Ono et al. 2023). The recurrent p.Gly12Ser variant of CS has recently been identified in a patient with Schimmelpenning‐Feuerstein‐Mims syndrome (Ono et al. 2023). Our patient's presentation did not align with any of the aforementioned conditions due to constellation of ectodermal findings. The phenotypic variability observed across these disorders could reflect not only the specific type of HRAS variant but also the timing and anatomical distribution of the postzygotic mutational event. While early embryonic mutational events may result in widespread systemic involvement, as seen in mosaic CS, later‐occurring events may lead to more localized, skin‐limited manifestations. Furthermore, significant clinical heterogeneity is observed among three reported cases of CS patients with mosaic p.Gly12Ser HRAS variant (Girisha et al. 2010; Gripp et al. 2006; Sol‐Church et al. 2009), regardless of the percentage of variant alleles, highlighting the complexity of genotype–phenotype correlations in mosaic RASopathies.

Conclusion

Low‐level detection of HRAS variants in leukocyte‐derived DNA should not preclude consideration of CS, particularly in patients with suggestive phenotypic features and Blaschko‐linear skin involvement. Multi‐tissue molecular analysis is therefore essential in attenuated or atypical presentations. In our case, limiting the analysis to leukocyte‐derived DNA would have resulted in a false‐negative outcome, thereby missing the pathogenic HRAS variant, with the patient's risk for malignancy and other systemic complications related to CS, as well as the risk for offspring, being overlooked. Our patient is the second reported case of mosaic CS caused by p.Gly13Asp HRAS variant, presenting with mild phenotype with no pre‐ and perinatal complications, normal neurocognitive development, and without malignancy, but with unique cutaneous findings, joint hyperlaxity and cerebrovascular malformations not previously reported with this variant. Given the rarity of the p.Gly13Asp HRAS variant and somatic mosaicism in CS patients, among whom marked variability in severity was noted, long‐term follow‐up is required to strengthen genotype–phenotype correlations and ultimately improve genetic counseling and clinical guidance.
