Ultra‐Low‐Field Portable Magnetic Resonance Imaging Feasibility and Safety in Pediatric and Neonatal Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation: A Single‐Center Year‐Long Experience
Authors: Jessica S Wallisch, Asdis Finnsdottir Wagner, John M Daniel, Allison Taber, Maura Sien, Sarah Foster, Nathan Artz, Timothy P Zinkus, Hung‐Wen Yeh, Jose A Pineda, Patrick M Kochanek, Sherwin S Chan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite improving outcomes, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) remains associated with high rates of acute brain injury, impacting survival and outcomes. Clinical imaging modalities, including computed tomography and standard‐field magnetic resonance imaging (≥1.5 Tesla), have either limited early detection of ischemic‐type injuries or are incompatible with ECMO, causing diagnostic delays. A Food and Drug Administration–cleared ultra‐low‐field portable magnetic resonance imaging (pMRI; 0.064 Tesla) has shown promise in acute brain injury detection and use with MRI‐unsafe equipment, but pediatric ECMO data are limited.MethodsThis institutional review board–approved, single‐center study was conducted at a quaternary children’s hospital. Neonatal and pediatric patients on ECMO from September 2023 to August 2024 were screened. Enrolled subjects underwent pMRI using Swoop (Hyperfine, Guilford, CT). Feasibility (resource use, concurrent equipment) and safety metrics (ECMO flow/vitals, cannula displacement) were collected along with patient/ECMO variables and all head imaging. Two blinded pediatric neuroradiologists independently reviewed images.ResultsOf 41 screened patients, 20 were enrolled, 16 had pMRI attempted, and 11 completed full imaging (T1, T2, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery, and diffusion‐weighted imaging). Median staff required for pMRI was 6 (interquartile range, 5–7) versus 7 (interquartile range, 7–7) for head computed tomography. Median pMRI positioning and imaging time was 66 (interquartile range, 61–70) minutes versus 75 (interquartile range, 70–79) minutes for head computed tomography with intrahospital transport. One subject had a ≥20% but clinically nonsignificant decrease in blood pressure; no safety events were attributable to pMRI. A total of 70% to 75% of image sets were of diagnostic quality.ConclusionspMRI is feasible in pediatric ECMO with no clinically relevant complications, lower resource use, and generation of diagnostic quality images in our cohort.Registration InformationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique Identifier: NCT06074406.Keywords: acute brain injury, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, pediatric, portable MRI, ultra‐low‐field magnetic resonance imagingSubject Categories: Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), Clinical Studies, Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Emergency Cardiac Care, Ischemic Stroke, Intracranial Hemorrhage

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

This study expands the experience of ultra‐low‐field portable magnetic resonance imaging in pediatric patients on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), where it was found to be feasible without clinically relevant changes in ECMO support and produced diagnostic quality images in a majority of our cohort.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Ultra‐low‐field portable magnetic resonance imaging has the potential to expand time‐sensitive neuroimaging options for pediatric patients on ECMO with less interruptions of critical care therapies and decreased resource use, while eliminating risks associated with intrahospital transport and radiation exposure.

Significant advances in extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) management and technology have expanded its use and improved survival rates, yet rates of poor neurologic outcomes remain unchanged following this level of support. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 There are considerable risks to the development of acute brain injury (ABI) in this population across the continuum of care (before ECMO, during cannulation, and during ECMO support) due to a myriad of patient, disease, and treatment factors. Hypoxia, hypotension, and cardiac arrest (which occurs in 40% of pediatric patients on ECMO) are among the known factors implicated in the pre‐ECMO period, with alterations in cerebral blood flow, thromboembolic events, and use of systemic anticoagulation being well‐described risks during ECMO support. The totality of these risk factors results in half of pediatric ECMO survivors having poor neurologic outcomes and disability. 5 , 8

There are different types of ABI that occur in this population, including global hypoxic–ischemic/anoxic, focal cerebrovascular (ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke), and other intracranial hemorrhagic injuries. The ability of clinicians to diagnose these different types of ABI is limited by multiple factors, including (1) most pediatric patients on ECMO require sedation, limiting the neurologic examination; (2) significant limitations to noninvasive neuromonitoring; (3) risks of invasive neuromonitoring; and (4) lack of sensitivity in available imaging modalities for early hypoxic–ischemic/anoxic injuries and focal ischemic stroke. Head ultrasound is an imaging modality that can be easily obtained at the bedside in patients aged <1 year with an open fontanelle, but ultrasound tends to miss ischemic and posterior fossa injuries, with only a 41% sensitivity (94% positive predictive value and 36% negative predictive value) for ABI on ECMO. 9 Computed tomography (CT) can be used across all ages of pediatric patients on ECMO but adds significant risks and logistical challenges related to intrahospital transport and uses ionizing radiation. 10 , 11 , 12 Furthermore, CT’s detection of early ischemia is inferior to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), with only 27.3% sensitivity in acute pediatric arterial ischemic stroke, which leads to delays in management. 13 , 14 While some institutions have access to portable CT, availability in pediatrics remains limited, and evidence to date does not support improved diagnostic accuracy or a reduction in radiation exposure compared with conventional head CT. 15 , 16 , 17 Traditional MRI is the gold standard to diagnose hypoxic–ischemic and cerebrovascular injuries, but it is incompatible with ECMO. 18 It is estimated that less than half of pediatric patients on ECMO receive head imaging with CT or a postdecannulation MRI, yet 21% to 60% of those who receive imaging have acute neuroimaging abnormalities. 8 , 19 , 20 This leaves clinicians with diagnostic uncertainty during a critical time window, when ABI is suspected, resulting in delayed diagnosis and missed opportunity for neuroprotective or directed interventions. 21 , 22 , 23 Patients might also receive unnecessary empiric treatment that also carries risks such as hyperosmolar therapy or changes to anticoagulation management in cases of suspected ABI without early neuroimaging confirmation. 9 , 13 , 14 , 24 , 25 Additionally, MRI is a source of information that can guide neuroprognostication, which is severely limited or delayed in the setting of extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation. 26 , 27

Ultra‐low‐field portable MRI (pMRI) is a Food and Drug Administration–approved imaging modality that has fewer patient support equipment limitations and eliminates ionizing radiation exposure and the need for transport, and experience with its use is rapidly expanding in adult ABI, including after cardiac arrest, stroke, and intracerebral hemorrhage. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 However, there is limited experience in pediatric patients because of the need for programming modifications when imaging the developing brain (ie, differences in brain tissue composition in neonates). 33 , 34 There is even more limited published experience with the use of ultra‐low‐field pMRI in ECMO, with only 2 small adult case series and a multicenter prospective observational cohort study of 50 adult subjects. 35 , 36 , 37 Pediatric experience lags, with 1 reported case series of 4 subjects. 38 The primary aim of this study was to expand the feasibility and safety data for use of pMRI in pediatric patients on ECMO.

METHODS

Study Design

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. This single‐center cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Children’s Mercy Hospital (STUDY00002824), and the participants’ legally authorized representative(s) gave written informed consent. The study was conducted at a quaternary free‐standing children’s hospital with high ECMO volume and Extracorporeal Life Support Organization Platinum Center of Excellence designation. Neonates and children (aged 0 to <18 years) admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (ICU), pediatric ICU, or cardiac ICU and receiving venovenous or venoarterial ECMO support between September 2023 and August 2024 were prospectively screened and enrolled. Subjects were excluded for pregnancy, active implants (permanent pacemaker or defibrillator, deep brain stimulator, cochlear implant, vagal nerve stimulator, programmable ventriculoperitoneal shunt), and MRI‐incompatible surgical hardware or shrapnel.

Study Procedure

Ultra‐low‐field portable MRI imaging was completed with Swoop (device version 1.8 with software 8.7 beta; Hyperfine, Guilford, CT), which has a static magnetic field strength of 0.064 Tesla. The imaging protocol included T1‐weighted, T2‐weighted, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery, and diffusion‐weighted imaging (with calculated apparent diffusion coefficient) using the standard product sequences excepting modified sequences for subjects aged <1 year. 39 Scanning procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and coordinated with bedside staff and ECMO teams to avoid clinical care interference with goal scan time occurring within 72 hours of ECMO cannulation, as this would capture injuries occurring in the precannulation, pericannulation, and immediate postcannulation periods where the largest changes occur in cerebral blood flow and oxygenation. Radiology research coordinators who are trained radiologic technologists transported the pMRI to the bedside of enrolled subjects. Subjects were positioned into the pMRI head coil by a team of bedside staff (critical care nurses, respiratory therapists, intensivists) and ECMO team members (perfusionists and specialists). The ECMO team monitored arterial blood pressures, flows, oxygenation, and support devices per clinical protocol for the duration of the scanning procedure. Images were reviewed the same day by the clinical neuroradiologist for new or unexpected findings that required communication to the clinical team; otherwise, images and full reports were not available for clinical care.

Data Collection

Data were collected on patient variables (age and weight at cannulation, sex, race and ethnicity, ICU location, survival), ECMO variables (type and indication for ECMO, cannula type and location, pump type and circuit size, cannulation and decannulation dates), and pMRI variables (rate of scan completion, reason for lack of completion, timing of scan, sequences run, reason examination ended early if applicable). Data were also collected on imaging procedure metrics (positioning/prep time, scan duration, total imaging procedure time, number of staff required for positioning, concurrent ICU therapies/devices), rates of adverse or safety events during study interventions, and results of clinical head imaging obtained during the study period. All pMRI image sequences obtained were independently assessed by 2 blinded pediatric neuroradiologists for image quality (artifact presence/severity and regional artifact patterns by sequence), and all complete sets of imaging sequences were evaluated for diagnostic utility and detection of ABI (present/absent/indeterminate for acute hemorrhage, ischemia, cerebral edema, midline shift, and herniation). Data were recorded and stored in Research Electric Data Capture version 14.6.8.

Outcomes

The primary feasibility outcome was defined as rate of imaging completion with all sequences (T1, T2, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery, and diffusion‐weighted imaging) relative to the number of scans attempted. Secondary feasibility outcomes included total time for positioning and imaging, the number of staff required for positioning, and rates of concurrent therapies that were paused. Primary safety outcomes were modeled after the SAFE‐MRI ECMO (Assessing the Safety and Feasibility of Bedside Portable Low‐Field Brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging in Patients on ECMO) study 36 and defined as change in ECMO cannula positioning requiring repositioning by surgeon, ≥20% decrease in ECMO flow, ≥20% decrease in mean arterial pressure, or ≥10% in pulse oximetry.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as median with interquartile range and frequency (%). The pMRI image quality was descriptively dichotomized into acceptable levels of artifact (defined as none, minimal, or moderate with some but no significant effect on diagnostic quality) or high level of artifact (defined as severe or extensive). Considering skewed marginal distributions, interrater reliability was determined by Gwet’s AC1 for binary outcomes and linearly weighted AC2 for trinomial outcomes in R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the irrCAC package version 1.0. 40 Descriptive analysis was conducted in SPSS version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY).

RESULTS

Patient Demographics and ECMO Details

All patients on ECMO at Children’s Mercy Hospital over a 1‐year period beginning in August 2023 (n=41) were screened for study eligibility. A total of 18 ECMO patients comprising 20 ECMO courses were enrolled in the study (48.78% enrollment rate), with 2 subjects being enrolled twice due to a second ECMO cannulation (Figure 1). The median age of our cohort was 4.21 months (interquartile range [IQR], 0.16–5.85), with a median weight of 4.55 kg (IQR, 3.20–7.91). Half of the subjects enrolled were admitted to the cardiac ICU. pMRI Feasibility

Imaging with pMRI was attempted in 16 of 20 enrolled subjects (80%). Reasons imaging was not attempted included competing clinical needs (1), patient instability (1), study withdrawal before imaging (1), and study team withdrawal due to need for safety clarification of temporary pacing wires (1). The majority of subjects in which a scan was attempted (9 [56.25%]) were scanned <72 hours following cannulation, with 5 subjects (31.25%) scanned <24 hours from cannulation. The median ECMO duration at the time of pMRI was 41.83 hours (IQR, 19.78–106.43). Two subjects only had a localizer image obtained due to inability to bend the cannulas adequately for positioning in the head coil. Eleven of 14 (78.57%) subjects were able to complete the full imaging sequence protocol, with reasons for early imaging termination being a sequence malfunction during image acquisition (1) and subject standard clinical care needed unrelated to pMRI (2). Safety

During image acquisition, 1 patient had a predefined safety event with ≥20% decline in mean arterial pressure but remained within the clinical goals without requiring intervention or early study procedure termination. One patient required ECMO arterial cannula repositioning 8 days following pMRI; however, the cannula positioning was stable on subsequent chest radiograph and echocardiography obtained on the same day after pMRI imaging. No adverse events or serious adverse events were attributed to study interventions.

Resource Use: pMRI Relative to CT

Half of the subjects (10 [50%]) traveled to CT during study enrollment. A majority (7 [70%]) of CT scans were obtained for head imaging. Two of the head CTs (28.57%) were completed during study coordinator working hours and available for tracking. The median time outside the ICU was 26.5 (IQR, 21–32) minutes, with median total preparation, imaging, and travel time of 75 (IQR, 66–84) minutes (Table 2). Both head CTs used 7 staff members, which was more resource intense compared with pMRI. Two subjects had continuous renal replacement therapy interrupted, and 1 patient had their electroencephalogram leads temporarily removed before travel for CT.

Image Quality and Detection

pMRI image artifact was considered acceptable by both pediatric neuroradiologists in, respectively, 75.0% and 69.6% of the full sequence examinations and had good interrater reliability with Gwet’s AC1 0.82 (95% CI, 0.67–0.97; P<0.001; Table 3). When present, artifacts were rated as ipsilateral to cannula placement in 71.4% and 61.4% of image sets with Gwet’s AC2 0.85 (95% CI, 0.74–0.96; P<0.001; Table S1). Of the subjects who had complete pMRI, 2 were diagnosed with acute hemorrhage on clinical imaging, with 1 confirmed by pMRI and 1 read as indeterminate on pMRI 6 days later (Figure 3, Table 4). Ischemic injury was not diagnosed with clinical imaging but was detected in 1 subject on pMRI and indeterminant in 8 subjects (4 of them were indeterminate by the second rater) on pMRI. pMRI detection rates by each neuroradiologist and interrater reliability are shown in Tables S2 through S6.

DISCUSSION

This study completed pMRI in the largest cohort of pediatric patients on ECMO to date. We demonstrated expanded feasibility with similar preparation plus imaging time but less resource intensity with lack of transport out of the ICU as compared with the currently clinically available modality of head CT. The majority (68.75%) of imaging attempts were completed with all planned sequences. Imaging was deemed safe in our cohort, with no subjects having clinically relevant changes to ECMO flow, mean arterial pressure, or oxygenation during study procedures, nor did we experience any migration of ECMO cannulas attributable to study interventions. Our exploratory analysis of image quality suggests that while a majority of images produced by pMRI have some degree of artifact, 70% to 75% of image sets had acceptable diagnostic quality for interpretation by 2 independent blinded pediatric neuroradiologists. Only 1 subject with a complete imaging set (9.10%) had extensive artifact that was considered nondiagnostic. Two subjects with incomplete imaging protocols were also found to have ≥1 sequences with extensive artifact.

Our study evaluated similar safety metrics to the SAFE MRI ECMO study, which evaluated the use of pMRI in 50 adult subjects on ECMO. 35 , 41 Except for a single nonclinically relevant decline in mean arterial pressure, subjects in our smaller cohort did not experience any adverse events or predefined safety outcomes attributable to study interventions, unlike the 3 (6%) reported by Cho et al. One subject required an arterial cannula repositioning that was deemed unrelated to study interventions, as it occurred 8 days after pMRI imaging with evidence of stable cannula positioning on radiography and echocardiography immediately following the study intervention. It was noted that this subject underwent intrahospital transport to obtain a head CT on the day before observing a change in arterial cannula position via radiography, which was then confirmed on echocardiography. While there is a temporal association with intrahospital transport, the true pathogenesis or pathogeneses of the cannula displacement could not be determined. Nevertheless, as this is a known complication of position changes and transport on ECMO, 42 , 43 and this requires a much larger patient and circuit movement, it could be hypothesized that the risks of intrahospital transport are greater than those for pMRI.

Adult and pediatric ECMO populations have different risk factors and patterns of ABI, cannulation sites, and brain water content requiring modifications in the approach to imaging. The only prior published experience with pMRI in pediatric ECMO was in 4 peripherally cannulated subjects with no adverse events while obtaining only T1‐ and T2‐weighted sequences, resulting a shorter scan duration without the ability to detect acute ischemia. 38 Therefore, while the work of Sabir et al was fundamental to exploring the use of pMRI in pediatric ECMO, our study builds upon their findings by increasing the cohort size, using a more complete pMRI protocol that included diffusion‐weighted imaging, additional granularity of feasibility and safety outcomes, and assessment of image quality and diagnostic detection. Our study identified technical limitations in 2 subjects that might indicate that not all pediatric and neonatal patients on ECMO can undergo imaging with pMRI. Both subjects had venoarterial ECMO neck cannulas with one‐quarter‐inch circuit tubing that was unable to flex adequately for positioning in the head coil, resulting in clipped localizer images; thus, no further sequences were obtained. As other successfully imaged study subjects had the same neck cannulas, circuit size, and lower weight at cannulation, further work will need to be undertaken to understand the factors contributing to this limitation.

We met our goal to obtain imaging with pMRI within 72 hours of cannulation in a majority of subjects (56.25%), with a median time to scan of 41.83 hours. We identified this goal time frame due to the vulnerability and higher risk of ABI during the pre‐ and pericannulation time periods. 2 , 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 As we prioritized clinical care needs during this feasibility pilot, the scan time was subject to downtime between patient care, coordination, availability of all team members required to move a patient on ECMO, and the research coordinators’ work hours and other responsibilities. Nonetheless, the fact that 31.25% of imaged subjects’ scans were obtained in <24 hours from cannulation provides proof of concept that pMRI could be used to better delineate and ascribe patterns of ABI within the peri‐ and early postcannulation time frame or when clinical suspicion for ABI arises. Our feasibility data will inform future efforts to determine optimal timing of pMRI imaging and guide implementation strategies. Based on the findings in this cohort, we anticipate that pMRI may be able to augment currently available imaging modalities for pediatric patients on ECMO by raising suspicion for or confirming early ABI such as cerebral edema, ischemia, or hemorrhage. In other instances, being able to rule out the presence of midline shift and herniation is also valuable (as consistently done by both imaging reviewers). While pMRI is unlikely to replace standard postdecannulation MRI, it may allow for more timely detection of ABI while avoiding intrahospital transport and radiation exposure. The availability of pMRI may also increase the feasibility of obtaining postdecannulation imaging in patients who are not considered stable for transport outside the ICU. 8

As this was a single‐center cohort study conducted over 1 year, our cohort size is limited. Our institution averages 40 to 50 ECMO courses annually; thus, the number of subjects screened was typical. Our enrollment was limited by not only exclusion criteria but also inability to approach families/surrogate decision makers due to language barriers (2), timing of presence at the bedside (2), clinical instability (2), and rapid decannulation or death (3). Our study team’s effort to avoid interrupting clinical care and the standard research coordinator work hours impacted the timing of scan attempts and completion. Many of these limitations would not be a factor if this imaging modality becomes standard of care; however, these findings need to be validated in larger multicenter studies that are adequately powered to determine if pMRI can detect ABI earlier and lead to changes in patient management. As our study imaging sequence protocols are modified for children aged <1 year, 39 this limits generalizability for institutions with standard commercial imaging sequences that were developed for adults. It is important to note that the resources needed for bedside pMRI compared with intrahospital transport to CT in a radiology suite are not interchangeable. While we believe this was an important component of assessing the feasibility of pMRI, workflows may vary across institutions, limiting the generalizability of our findings. While other single‐center reports have shown that similar time was required to perform portable head CT compared with conventional head CT, 48 our findings regarding workflow may not apply to the minority of pediatric centers that have portable CT capabilities.

Finally, this pilot study was not powered for safety or image detection outcomes. At a 7.7% cannula displacement prevalence in our cohort, we calculate the 95% CI to be 1.2% to 36.0%. Subjects in our study cohort had a high short‐term survival rate with overall all‐cause mortality of 26.32%, which is consistent with our center’s Extracorporeal Life Support Organization registry data of 29.70%. This could skew our cohort’s prevalence of ABI and therefore impact rates of pMRI detection. This study conducted pMRI only at a single time point after informed consent was obtained and therefore was not triggered by the same indications for obtaining standard clinical head imaging. Thus, we cannot make conclusions about the detection of ABI relative to standard head imaging. Importantly, the pediatric neuroradiologists were blinded to clinical diagnoses and other available head imaging while reviewing study images for the presence of ABI. Standard clinical workflow includes reviewing clinically available data and comparison images, and it is possible that this could impact the rates of detection, further limiting our ability to compare diagnostic yield between pMRI and standard imaging in our cohort. Notably, our institution had 3 patients on ECMO declared deceased by neurologic criteria (1.7%), a total of 23 patients with ischemic brain injury (12.7%), and a total of 21 patients with hemorrhagic brain injury (11.6%) over the same 3‐year span as the reference mortality data (2020–2023), with the caveat that such findings included postdecannulation imaging that would have occurred after the comparison pMRI.

CONCLUSIONS

In this single‐center cohort, pMRI is feasible and less resource intense than intrahospital transport to CT for pediatric patients on ECMO. Image quality was adequate in the majority of pMRI studies despite artifact, which was mostly ipsilateral to the ECMO cannula. Future work will expand safety data and determine the diagnostic yield of ultra‐low‐field portable MRI for ischemic and hemorrhagic ABIs in pediatric patients with ECMO. This has the potential to significantly improve neuroimaging and neurocritical care for pediatric patients on ECMO by allowing for earlier and more accurate diagnosis followed by better‐informed decision‐making and treatment with the goal of improving neurologic outcomes and survival.
