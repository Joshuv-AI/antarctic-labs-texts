Fully automated free-breathing cardiac magnetic resonance imaging at 3T: A prospective randomized study of image quality, efficiency, and workflow burden
Authors: Keyi Li, Wenli Zhou, Xinling Yang, Lin Chen, Xuan Qin, Chen Cui, Kai Yang, Weipeng Yan, Wenhao Dong, Lele Liu, Jialin Song, Jing An, Jens Wetzl, Michaela Schmidt, Shihua Zhao, Gang Yin, Minjie Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
abstratcBackgroundAutomation in cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) scans holds the potential to improve examination efficiency and workflow consistency. Prospective clinical evidence validating automated scan workflows in routine CMR practice remains limited.MethodsIn this prospective randomized study, consecutive patients referred for non-stress CMR were assigned to either an automated or a manual free-breathing scanning workflow. The fully automated workflow integrated automated plane prescription of multiple steps required for successful image acquisition. The primary endpoint was total examination time; secondary endpoints included plane prescription accuracy, image quality scores, scanner idle time, and technologist workload.ResultsOf 255 screened patients, 221 were included (automated, n = 109; manual, n = 112). All examinations were diagnostically adequate. The automated and manual workflows showed a similarly low incidence of plane prescription misalignment, corresponding to 19.3% (21/109) and 17.9% (20/112) misalignment events per examination, respectively, with no significant difference between groups (0.19 vs. 0.18 events per examination, P = 0.780). No significant differences were observed across imaging planes or technologist experience levels, and image quality scores were comparable between workflows (2.74 ± 0.67 vs. 2.69 ± 0.70, P = 0.547). However, the automated scanning workflow significantly reduced total examination time (19.16 ± 2.32 vs. 21.25 ± 2.25 min, P < 0.001) and scanner idle time (7.80 ± 1.80 vs. 10.12 ± 2.03 min, P < 0.001), with consistent savings across all experience levels. Operator workload was also substantially lower with automated scanning, evidenced by fewer mouse clicks and keystrokes (both P < 0.01).ConclusionsAn automated CMR scanning workflow improves examination efficiency and reduces operator workload without compromising image quality or plane prescription accuracy, supporting its integration in routine clinical CMR practice.

Background

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) is widely regarded as the gold-standard imaging modality for assessing cardiac structure and function [1], [2]. However, CMR scanning is intrinsically complex, with its technical and clinical competency requirements necessitating practitioners to undergo specialized training [3]. In practice, CMR demands substantially more human–machine interactions compared to other non-CMR scans, placing a heavy cognitive load on operators regarding spatial orientation and procedural execution [4], [5]. Consequently, variability in operator skill and subjective judgment often lead to suboptimal reproducibility, plane misalignment, and the need for repeated acquisitions. These inefficiencies not only prolong examination times, but also represent a primary bottleneck to the broader clinical integration of CMR.

Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning, offers a promising solution for automating CMR workflows, specifically in scan plane prescription and sequence execution [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]. Recent studies have demonstrated that AI-based landmark detection can automatically identify key cardiac structures, enabling fully automated slice planning without manual intervention [11], [12], [13], [14]. Validation studies have shown significant reductions in scan and inter-sequence idle times while maintaining diagnostic image quality [15]. Nevertheless, most existing studies have been confined to 1.5 T Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) systems and breath-holding imaging protocols, leaving the systematic evaluation of automated CMR performance under free-breathing conditions at 3 T insufficient. Free-breathing strategies are particularly beneficial for patients with arrhythmia, elderly, and pediatric populations, and are therefore of growing clinical importance [16], [17]. Moreover, the effect of automated scanning on technologist interaction burden, such as mouse click frequency, has not been quantitatively assessed.

Accordingly, this prospective study aims to evaluate a comprehensive free-breathing automated CMR workflow on a 3T system, encompassing scan setup, cine, flow measurement, T1/T2 mapping, perfusion, and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). By comparing automated and manual workflows in terms of reliability, examination time, and technologist workload, this study seeks to provide objective evidence supporting the clinical implementation of intelligent automated CMR scanning.

Methods study population

Study design and population

This prospective study consecutively enrolled patients undergoing comprehensive free-breathing CMR imaging at our institution between March 2025 and November 2025.

Exclusion criteria comprised (1) MRI contraindications, including the presence of cardiac pacemakers or metallic implants; (2) congenital heart defects associated with abnormal anatomical arrangement or cardiac position, such as transposition of the great arteries or dextrocardia. Eligible participants were randomized to one of two acquisition workflows: an automated (software-driven) workflow or a manual (technologist-driven) workflow. Randomization was performed in a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated random sequence with permuted blocks of variable sizes. The allocation sequence was generated by an independent statistician who was not involved in participant enrollment or image acquisition. Group assignment was implemented through a centralized web-based randomization system after confirmation of eligibility and acquisition of informed consent, thereby ensuring allocation concealment until the moment of assignment. Participants were enrolled by a research coordinator, and the assigned workflow was subsequently communicated to the technologist responsible for the scan [18], [19].

On the day of examination, additional exclusion criteria were applied to patients, which were: (1) required protocol modification due to severe arrhythmias; (2) incomplete image acquisition or missing imaging planes; or (3) failure to complete the examination (e.g., due to claustrophobia). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital and the National Center for Cardiovascular Diseases. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study (Ethics approval number: 2022–1770).

CMR acquisition protocol

All CMR examinations were performed on a 3T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Vida, Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany) using an 18-channel body coil.

In the fully automated group, image acquisition was performed using a Fuwai-integrated automated workflow based on the AI Cardiac Scan Companion (AICSC) [15], [20] research package and implemented on the commercial myExam Cardiac Assist platform (Siemens Healthineers). In contrast to myExam Cardiac Assist, which mainly supports automatic slice positioning for standard cardiac views, the research package further enabled AI-based workflow extensions, including automatic positioning and planning, automatic inversion time selection, slice propagation across sequences, and protocol automation with customized sequence ordering and parameter optimization, thereby enabling a fully automated free-breathing CMR examination.

This integrated platform enabled the fully automated execution of our previously described free-breathing cardiac imaging protocols [21]. The workflow began with the acquisition of coronal and transversal localizer scans. The system then automatically positioned the isocenter at the transversal center of the detected left ventricle and set the adjustment volume to the detected whole-heart bounding box. The center of this bounding box was subsequently used to align all following scan slice groups, ensuring heart-centered imaging. Standard long-axis (LAX) and short-axis (SAX) views were prescribed automatically. For LGE imaging, inversion times (TI) were automatically determined using the previously described Auto TI module, which analyzes signal intensities in the myocardium and blood pool in a TI scout sequence [9]. Fixed TI increments were applied to account for the delay between TI scout and actual LGE acquisitions. Throughout the scan, the operator could manually adjust parameters if needed; however, only safety-relevant steps—such as confirming complete contrast agent administration — required mandatory user interaction before proceeding.

In the manual group, technologists with experience in CMR manually prescribed all standard cardiac views (LAX and SAX). Both groups utilized identical imaging sequences, and all acquisitions were carried out under free-breathing conditions. Recorded variables

Demographic and clinical data were collected for all participants, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), body surface area (BSA), heart rate, and disease category, while cardiac functional parameters which were obtained outside the CMR study included left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV), and stroke volume (SV). Patients were stratified according to heart rhythm (irregular rhythm defined as atrial fibrillation or premature contractions; all others classified as regular rhythm) and technologist experience in CMR (<2 years, 2–10 years, and >10 years).

Time recording and workflow analysis

All timestamps were extracted directly from the scanner to ensure objective measurement of time intervals. The following time components were recorded: examination time, including pre-contrast examination time (from the first localizer to the last native image), perfusion time (duration of the perfusion sequence), post-contrast examination time (from the end of perfusion imaging to the completion of the examination), and total examination time (from examination start to completion). The idle phase was defined as non-acquisition intervals between consecutive sequences, including planning confirmation, system adjustments, shimming, and communication with the patient. Total idle time, idle time before contrast administration, and idle time after contrast administration were calculated separately.

Assessment of plane prescription quality

All examinations were supervised by a radiologist with more than 10 years of experience in cardiovascular imaging. Acquired images were transmitted in real-time to a reporting workstation for the immediate review of every cardiac view in both groups. If abnormal execution or suboptimal positioning was identified, the operator was instructed to manually correct and repeat the corresponding sequence.

Plane prescription misalignment was recorded according to standard imaging orientation, including 2-chamber, 4-chamber, short-axis, left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), and left ventricular coronal (LV-C) views. An examination was defined as misalignment-free if no plane prescription misalignment occurred throughout the entire workflow and no rescanning was required. The incidence of misalignment was compared between the automated and manual groups, both overall and stratified by imaging orientation.

After completion of all examinations, two independent cardiovascular radiologists with more than 10 years of experience performed a blinded assessment of plane prescription quality. Each view was scored using the following scale: 1 = unacceptable plane prescription, requiring re-planning; 2 = acceptable with mild angulation deviation; 3 = accurate plane prescription with no adjustment required [15].

Evaluation of technologist workload

To quantify technologist workload, the system automatically recorded the total number of mouse clicks and keystrokes for each examination. For both automated and manual scanning workflow groups, mouse click counts and keystroke counts were recorded from the start to the end of each examination.

Statistical analyses

Data management and statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27.0, IBM Corp, Armonk, New York). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables are summarized as median (25th–75th percentile). Continuous variables were compared using the independent-samples Student’s t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages, and group differences were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test with Yates’ continuity correction, or Fisher’s exact test when any expected cell count was fewer than five. Inter-rater agreement for the binary assessment of plane prescription misalignment (yes/no) between two independent blinded readers was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa (κ). Between-group comparisons of plane prescription misalignment rates between the two methods were performed using Pearson’s chi-square test with Yates’ continuity correction (or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were <5). Inter-rater agreement for plane prescription quality scores was assessed using Cohen’s weighted kappa. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 255 consecutive patients were screened for non-stress CMR, 23 were excluded prior to imaging. After randomizing the remaining 232 patients, a further 11 were excluded due to severe arrhythmia, incomplete imaging, or scan interruption. The final analysis included 221 patients, with 109 assigned to the automated CMR group and 112 to the manual CMR group (Fig. 1).

Discussion

In this prospective free-breathing 3T CMR study, automated and manual scanning workflows showed no significant differences in rates of plane prescription misalignment or plane prescription quality score distributions, regardless of imaging orientation or technologist experience. Crucially, the automated workflow provided a decisive operational advantage, significantly reducing total examination time, system idle time, and operator workload, as quantified by a substantial decrease in mouse clicks and keystrokes.

With the rapid advancement of AI, an increasing number of AI-based applications have been integrated into cardiac imaging workflows [7]. However, the literature on fully automated CMR scanning workflows remains limited, with prior work predominantly focused on 1.5T systems [20], [22]. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive study to evaluate an end-to-end, free-breathing CMR workflow on a 3T system. In contrast to previous studies that concentrated on validating individual imaging sequences [8], [23], we systematically assessed AI performance across the entire clinical examination, offering a more representative evaluation of real-world practice. The inclusion of a relatively large cohort of 221 patients further strengthens the statistical robustness and generalizability of our findings.

The results of this study demonstrate that the automated workflow reduced the mean total examination time by 2.09 minutes and scanner idle time by 2.32 min. This efficiency gain is rooted in the automation of several labor-intensive steps. Specifically, AI-based algorithms automatically performed cardiac localization and optimized key acquisition parameters, such as field of view and slice positioning, based on individual subject characteristics including cardiac size, morphology, and heart rate. Furthermore, the optimal TI was automatically determined without the need for manual adjustment. As a result, time spent on manual planning, operator confirmation, and repeated mouse clicks to proceed between scanning steps was largely eliminated, leading to a more streamlined and efficient workflow.

Following the protocol described by Yang et al. (2025) [21], cine imaging was performed after perfusion in this study. Because LGE imaging requires a waiting period of approximately 10 min after contrast administration to achieve optimal contrast, the post-contrast examination time was relatively fixed. Therefore, the time-saving benefits of the automated workflow were primarily concentrated in the pre-contrast phase.

This study specifically focused on an automated scanning workflow under free-breathing conditions at 3 T. Previous studies have demonstrated that free-breathing CMR can be applied to a broader patient population while maintaining high image quality [21], [24], although most prior work was conducted on 1.5T systems using breath-hold sequences. Owing to its higher signal-to-noise ratio and spatial resolution, validation of AI algorithms in free-breathing environments at 3T is of particular importance. In addition, unlike many previous studies, no age restrictions were applied, with patient ages ranging from 14 to 83 years, including pediatric, elderly, and frail patients who are often unable to cooperate with breath-holding. Unlike prior 1.5T breath-hold studies, our findings demonstrate that workflow automation retains robustness under high-field, free-breathing conditions, where operator variability is intrinsically greater.

It is noteworthy that although the automated scanning workflow did not demonstrate superiority over manual planning in terms of accuracy, the comparable performance suggests that AI algorithms have reached a level equivalent to expert manual scanning. This differs slightly from Glessgen et al. (2025), who reported lower error rates with automated planning [15]. This discrepancy likely arises from our more heterogeneous cohort and the inherent variability of free-breathing conditions, where the primary benefit of AI shifted from improving accuracy to reducing operational burden.

By introducing mouse click and keystroke counts as objective metrics, we quantitatively demonstrated that the automated scanning workflow significantly reduced human–machine interactions. Beyond improving throughput, reducing repetitive manual tasks like slice adjustment and Auto-TI determination may mitigate technologist fatigue and lower the risk of human error. This allows clinicians to shift their focus toward patient safety and image quality monitoring. These metrics add a novel dimension to the evaluation of AI, complementing traditional measures of examination time and error rates.

Notably, a key advantage of automation lies in its ability to reduce dependence on highly experienced technologists, enhance workflow consistency in high-throughput imaging centers, and support the standardization and scalable deployment of CMR examinations. In routine clinical practice, manual scan planning is inherently operator-dependent and subject to variability related to experience level, workload, and fatigue. By delivering performance comparable to expert technologists across all experience strata, the automated workflow effectively mitigates operator-dependent variability and promotes reproducible scan quality. This capability is particularly relevant for large-volume centers and institutions facing workforce constraints, where consistent image quality and efficient throughput are essential. From a broader perspective, such automation represents a critical enabling step toward standardized, protocol-driven CMR acquisition, facilitating multicenter harmonization and wider clinical adoption of CMR as a scalable imaging modality.

Overall, this study validated the robustness of performance of an AI-based automated scanning workflow throughout the entire CMR examination under high-field, free-breathing conditions. Automated planning achieved image quality and positioning accuracy comparable to manual workflows, while offering meaningful advantages in efficiency and reduced technologist workload. Compared with prior studies conducted at 1.5T, our results extend the applicability of AI-assisted workflows to more complex imaging environments and support their potential for broader clinical translation in high-field CMR practice.

Although the present study demonstrates that the fully automated workflow can shorten examination time, reduce the number of required breath-holds, and lessen technologist effort, the current work is limited to the stage of automated image acquisition. Automated post-processing and quantitative interpretation were not within the scope of this initial investigation. Accordingly, image-derived outputs, including quantitative cardiac function and structure, myocardial strain, T1 and T2 mapping, extracellular volume fraction and LGE were not systematically compared in the present analysis. We fully acknowledge the importance of these downstream analyses, particularly because robust and reproducible post-processing remains essential for broader clinical adoption and longitudinal patient management. Future work will therefore focus on extending the current acquisition pipeline toward a fully automated end-to-end workflow, integrating automated post-processing of functional, structural, and tissue characterization parameters, and comparing these measurements against established third-party software platforms. In addition, inline post-processing may provide an important technical foundation for whole-heart tissue characterization, thereby enabling more comprehensive and reproducible assessment beyond conventional slice- or region of interest-based analysis. Such efforts will be important to determine whether the proposed approach can evolve from automated image acquisition to truly comprehensive, fully automated CMR examination and analysis.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the automated scanning workflow was provided by a single vendor, and algorithm performance may depend on specific hardware and software configurations. To mitigate potential bias, stratified analyses according to technologist experience and imaging orientation demonstrated consistent results. Second, because current AI algorithms may struggle with significant anatomical distortions, patients with complex congenital heart disease were not included. Thirdly, while the system automates the majority of the process, human supervision remains necessary for final verification. Further optimization of AI algorithms and multicenter studies are warranted to validate the robustness and reproducibility of this workflow across different platforms. Finally, a key limitation of this study is that it evaluated only the feasibility of automated image acquisition. Whether this workflow can be extended to fully automated post-processing and quantitative tissue characterization remains to be determined.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this prospective study demonstrates that an automated CMR scanning workflow significantly enhances procedural efficiency and reduces technologist workload without compromising image quality or plane prescription accuracy. By streamlining complex tasks under free-breathing conditions, this intelligent workflow addresses key bottlenecks in CMR clinical practice. These findings provide strong objective evidence to support the broader clinical translation and standardization of automated scanning in routine CMR.
