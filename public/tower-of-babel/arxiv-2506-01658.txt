An Efficient and Interpretable Autoregressive Model for High-Dimensional Tensor-Valued Time Series
Yuxi Cai, Lan Li, Yize Wang, and Guodong Li Department of Statistics and Actuarial Science, University of Hong Kong

Abstract
In autoregressive modeling for tensor-valued time series, Tucker decomposition, when applied to the coefficient tensor, provides a clear interpretation of supervised factor modeling but loses its efficiency rapidly with increasing tensor order. Conversely, canonical polyadic (CP) decomposition maintains efficiency but lacks a precise statistical interpretation. To attain both interpretability and powerful dimension reduction, this paper proposes a novel approach under the supervised factor modeling paradigm, which first uses CP decomposition to extract response and covariate features separately and then regresses response features on covariate ones. This leads to a new CP-based low-rank structure for the coefficient tensor. Furthermore, to address heterogeneous signals or potential model misspecifications arising from stringent low-rank assumptions, a low-rank plus sparse model is introduced by incorporating an additional sparse coefficient tensor. Nonasymptotic properties are established for the ordinary least squares estimators, and an alternating least squares algorithm is introduced for optimization. Theoretical properties of the proposed methodology are validated by simulation studies, and its enhanced prediction performance and interpretability are demonstrated by the El Ni n ~ \tilde{\text{n}} o-Southern Oscillation example.



Keywords
: autoregression, high-dimensional time series, nonasymptotic properties, sparsity, tensor decomposition

Introduction

With the rapid advancement of information technology, multi-dimensional or tensor-valued data are now ubiquitous, and particularly, many of them are time-dependent. Examples can be found in various fields, such as neuroscience
(
Zhou et al., 2013
;
Li and Zhang, 2021
)
, digital marketing
(
Bi et al., 2018
;
Hao et al., 2021
)
, economics and finance
(
Chen et al., 2022
;
Wang et al., 2024
)
and climatology
(
Bahadori et al., 2014
;
Xu et al., 2018
)
.
It is arguably the most important task to conduct supervised learning on these data, say tensor autoregression
(
Li and Xiao, 2021
;
Wang et al., 2022
)
or more general tensor-on-tensor regression
(
Lock, 2018
;
Raskutti et al., 2019
)
. However, the corresponding responses and covariates may both be tensors with high order and high dimension. This ends up with scenarios where the number of parameters to estimate is substantially larger than the available sample size.
Meanwhile, these data often possess hidden low-dimensional structures that can be leveraged to improve model interpretability and estimation efficiency at the same time.

As an illustrative example, a few oceanic variables from specific regions are found to be closely associated with the El Ni
\tilde{\text{n}}
o-Southern Oscillation (ENSO), the most prominent phenomenon of contemporary natural climate variability
(
Sarachik and Cane, 2010
;
Zhou and Zhang, 2023
)
.
The data, which include seven-layer ocean temperature anomalies in the upper 150 meters, are observed over time, forming a three-way tensor-valued time series with dimensions 51 (
longitude
)
\times
41 (
latitude
)
\times
7 (
variables
).
The high dimensionality of the data makes its direct modeling and forecasting formidable since even a simple autoregressive model of order one would involve
(51\times 41\times 7)^{2}=14637^{2}
parameters, an overwhelming number that far exceeds the observed time points. On the other hand, research indicates that climate conditions of the so-called “El Ni
\tilde{\text{n}}
o basin” region affect, and are affected by, those of other regions in different ways, especially before the onset of an El Ni
\tilde{\text{n}}
o event
(
Ludescher et al., 2013
)
. The El Ni
\tilde{\text{n}}
o basin is approximately around the equatorial Pacific corridor; see Figure
1
for an illustration. Yet, the literature has not reached a consensus on its precise location.
It is crucial for a successful inference tool to correctly identify the low-dimensional structure, and this can be achieved by low-rankness-induced approaches in a coherent way
(
Zhou et al., 2013
;
Feng et al., 2021
)
.

Figure 1:
The grid points represent the geographical locations where ocean temperature data are being considered. One possible boundary of the El Ni
\tilde{\text{n}}
o basin is delineated by dashed lines, with grid points within this region highlighted in red.

There are two extensively used methods in the literature for adding low-rank constraints to tensors: Tucker and canonical polyadic (CP) decomposition; see
Kolda and Bader, 2009
.
Tucker decomposition
(
Tucker, 1966
)
has been widely applied to various tensor regression settings, including scalar-on-tensor and tensor-on-tensor models, due to its numerical stability
(
Yu and Liu, 2016
;
Li and Zhang, 2017
;
Li et al., 2018
;
Gahrooei et al., 2021
;
Han et al., 2022
)
.

Wang et al., 2024
considered Tucker decomposition to tensor autoregressive time series models, leading to a nice interpretation of supervised factor modeling; see also
Wang et al., 2022
and
Huang et al., 2025
.
Specifically, they first assume coefficient tensors to have a high-order singular value decomposition (HOSVD), a special Tucker decomposition. Some loading matrices can then be moved to the left-hand side of autoregression or regression to summarize responses into low-dimensional factors or features, and the others will summarize covariates into another few features.
The response features capture all the predictable components, while the covariate ones encapsulate all the driving forces, thereby facilitating model explanations.
The above physical interpretation can help us study the ENSO example. However, Tucker decomposition will lose its efficiency in compressing tensors very quickly as the order of tensors increases, say greater than four or five
(
Oseledets, 2011
;
Cai et al., 2022
)
.
This issue is particularly prominent when dealing with autoregression for tensor-valued time series, where the coefficient tensor has a minimum order of six.

In the meanwhile, CP decomposition
(
Harshman, 1970
)
has been well recognized for its efficiency in dimension reduction
(
Kolda and Bader, 2009
)
.

Guo et al., 2012
and
Zhou et al., 2013
considered CP decomposition for scalar-on-tensor regression problems, followed by many others
(
Guhaniyogi et al., 2017
;
Zhang et al., 2019
;
Hao et al., 2021
;
Feng et al., 2021
)
. A nice interpretation can be achieved since CP decomposition can extract principal components or features from covariates
(
Cai et al., 2022
)
.
For instance, in the application to neuroimaging analysis, the model can identify the relevant brain regions to a clinical response
(
Zhou et al., 2013
)
.
Besides, CP decomposition has also been employed to effectively reduce the parameter dimension of tensor-on-tensor regression models
(
Lock, 2018
;
Llosa-Vite and Maitra, 2023
;
Billio et al., 2024
)
, however, its statistical interpretation becomes vague.
In contrast to Tucker decomposition, the loading matrices of CP decomposition are generally not orthogonal
(
Han et al., 2024
)
. This makes it difficult to move some of them to the left-hand side of regression to extract features from tensor-valued responses.
Consequently, the resulting model lacks the interpretation of feature extraction from both responses and covariates. The limitation of CP decomposition in the settings of tensor-on-tensor regression, and hence tensor autoregression, suggests a pressing need to develop a suitable model that is not only more interpretable but can also dramatically compress parameter dimension.

Beyond Tucker and CP decompositions, several works have explored alternative low-rank structures for coefficients in tensor autoregression
(
Li and Xiao, 2021
)
and tensor-on-tensor regression
(
Llosa-Vite and Maitra, 2023
;
Si et al., 2024
)
. Despite these efforts, none have successfully fulfilled the objective of attaining both interpretability and powerful dimension reduction. The first contribution of this paper is to propose a novel CP-based low-rank structure to the coefficient tensor in tensor-on-tensor regression models and adapt the idea to autoregressive models for tensor-valued time series. The design leads to a supervised factor modeling interpretation, i.e., first use CP decomposition to extract response and covariate features separately and then regress response features on covariate ones. It also inherits the parsimonious structure of CP decomposition, and hence both the interpretability and estimation efficiency can be achieved.

Moreover, in the literature on high-dimensional time series, another widely used method is to assume that coefficients are sparse and then apply sparsity-inducing regularized estimation; see, e.g.,
l_{1}
-regularization for vector autoregressive models
(
Basu and Michailidis, 2015
;
Davis et al., 2016
)
and vector autoregressive moving average models
(
Wilms et al., 2023
;
Zheng, 2024
)
.
There is even a much bigger literature on sparsity-inducing regularization methods for regression models
(
Tibshirani, 1996
;
Fan and Li, 2001
;
Davis et al., 2016
;
Raskutti et al., 2019
;
Bertsimas and Parys, 2020
)
.
On the one hand, despite its powerful performance in prediction, this type of methods may fail to represent underlying complex structures in data
(
Basu et al., 2019
)
.
For the ENSO example, the oceanic conditions of the entire region are likely to change in a concerted manner, driven by climate conditions of the El Ni
\tilde{\text{n}}
o basin.
It is hard to identify this influential region by solely relying on sparsity regularization, whereas the low-rankness may capture this driving force in a more coherent way
(
Zhou et al., 2013
;
Feng et al., 2021
)
.
On the other hand, an exact low-rank assumption can be limited, leaving the method susceptible to model misspecification or heterogeneous signals
(
Agarwal et al., 2012
;
Basu et al., 2019
;
Cai et al., 2023
;
Bai et al., 2023
)
.
It hence may be more reasonable to assume that the coefficient tensor has a low-rank plus sparse structure. This leads to the second contribution of this paper: we designate a coefficient tensor with CP-based low-dimensional structure to model the associations that are collaboratively driven by latent features, while using a sparse one to account for heterogeneous signals or potential model misspecifications arising from an exact low-rank assumption.

These two main contributions are detailed in Sections
2.1
and
2.2
. Besides,
this paper also proposes the corresponding high-dimensional estimation in Section
2.3
, and adopts the alternating least squares (ALS) algorithm
(
de Leeuw et al., 1976
;
Zhou et al., 2013
)
to search for the estimates, which casts the update of a single block at each iteration into a traditional linear regression problem; see Section
2.4
for details.
Theoretically, the non-asymptotic error bounds are given in Section
3
for high-dimensional autoregression as well as regression settings. Section
4
conducts the simulation experiments to assess the finite-sample performance of the proposed models, and their effectiveness is further validated by the ENSO example in Section
5
. Finally, Section
6
provides a concise summary and discussion to conclude the paper, and all technical proofs are deferred to the Supplementary Material.

2
Methodology

2.1
CP-based feature extraction in tensor-on-tensor regression

This subsection first illustrates how to construct a tensor-on-tensor regression model with CP decomposition used to extract features from responses and covariates separately, forming the basis for an interpretable and efficient tensor autoregressive model in the next subsection.

We start by considering a scalar-on-tensor regression model with the coefficient tensor having a CP decomposition
(
Guo et al., 2012
;
Zhou et al., 2013
;
Guhaniyogi et al., 2017
;
Feng et al., 2021
)
,

y_{t}=\left\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\right\rangle+\varepsilon_{t}\hskip 8.53581pt\text{with}\hskip 8.53581pt\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}=\sum_{r=1}^{R_{x}}g_{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{r}^{(1)}\circ\boldsymbol{v}_{r}^{(2)}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{v}_{r}^{(m)},

where
y_{t},\varepsilon_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
are the response and the error term, respectively,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}\times\cdots\times p_{m}}
is the covariate,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}\times\cdots\times p_{m}}
is the coefficient tensor,
R_{x}
is the CP-rank,
\boldsymbol{v}_{r}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}}
with
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}^{(j)}_{r}\rVert_{2}=1
for each
1\leq r\leq R_{x}
and
1\leq j\leq m
, and
\circ
denotes the outer product.
The model can be rewritten into

y_{t}=\sum_{r=1}^{R_{x}}g_{r}f^{\mathrm{covariate}}_{r,t}+\varepsilon_{t}\quad\text{with}\quad f^{\mathrm{covariate}}_{r,t}=\left\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{r}^{(1)}\circ\boldsymbol{v}_{r}^{(2)}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{v}_{r}^{(m)},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\right\rangle,

(2.1)

where
\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{covariate}}_{t}=(f^{\mathrm{covariate}}_{1,t},\dots,f^{\mathrm{covariate}}_{R_{x},t})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{x}}
are features extracted from covariates
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}
, and
the loading tensors
\boldsymbol{v}_{r}^{(1)}\circ\boldsymbol{v}_{r}^{(2)}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{v}_{r}^{(m)}
are of the same shape as
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}
but may not be orthogonal with each other
(
Kolda and Bader, 2009
)
.
From model (
2.1
), the scalar response is modeled as a linear combination of covariate features, which in turn makes the feature extraction a supervised process.
These features can provide a reasonable approximation to many low-dimensional signals
(
Zhou et al., 2013
)
.
For example, when
y_{t}
is a clinical response and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}
is a brain image, these CP-structured loadings can help recover regions of interest in the brain related to a disease. In the ENSO example, features may summarize the driving forces of climate change, while loadings can help identify the location of El Ni
\tilde{\text{n}}
o basin.

However, for time series models, both responses and covariates are observed tensors from the same variable, and hence it is necessary to consider a more general tensor-on-tensor regression model,

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}=\left\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\right\rangle+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t},

(2.2)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times q_{2}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
are the response and the error term, respectively, and the covariate
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}
has the same shape as in model (
2.1
). The problem caused by high dimensionality is more pronounced in this model as now the coefficient tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}\times p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}}
.
While, the efficiency and interpretability brought by covariate feature extraction in (
2.1
) motivates us to consider feature extraction from responses as well.
Specifically, we may extract
R_{y}
features, denoted by
\boldsymbol{f}_{t}^{\mathrm{response}}=(f_{1,t}^{\mathrm{response}},\dots,f_{R_{y},t}^{\mathrm{response}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{y}}
, from
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}
, with each feature of the form:

f_{r,t}^{\mathrm{response}}=\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{r}^{(1)}\circ\boldsymbol{u}_{r}^{(2)}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{u}_{r}^{(n)},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}\right\rangle\quad\text{where }\boldsymbol{u}^{(i)}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}}\text{ and }\lVert\boldsymbol{u}^{(i)}_{r}\rVert_{2}=1

(2.3)

for all
1\leq r\leq R_{y}
and
1\leq i\leq n
.
The loading tensors
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{C}$}_{r}=\boldsymbol{u}_{r}^{(1)}\circ\boldsymbol{u}_{r}^{(2)}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{u}_{r}^{(n)}
are not necessarily orthogonal to each other, but we require them to be non-degenerate, i.e.,
\sum_{r=1}^{R_{y}}c_{r}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{C}$}_{r}=0
must imply that
c_{1}=\cdots=c_{R_{y}}=0
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}=\boldsymbol{U}_{n}\odot\cdots\odot\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\prod_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\times R_{y}}

and

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}=\boldsymbol{V}_{m}\odot\cdots\odot\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\prod_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\times R_{x}}
,
where
\odot
denotes the Khatri-Rao product,
\boldsymbol{U}_{i}=\left(\boldsymbol{u}^{(i)}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{u}^{(i)}_{R_{y}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}\times R_{y}}
for
1\leq i\leq n
, and
\boldsymbol{V}_{j}=\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{(j)}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{v}^{(j)}_{R_{x}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}\times R_{x}}
for
1\leq j\leq m
.
It can be verified that

\boldsymbol{f}_{t}^{\mathrm{response}}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t})
and
\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{covariate}}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}^{\top}\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t})
.

We next attempt to design a low-dimensional structure to model (
2.2
) such that both response and covariate feature extraction are supervised by the predictive relationship between their resulting features.
We propose to assume that the sequential matricization of
\mathscr{A}
has the following decomposition:

[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}]_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}^{\top},

(2.4)

and then model (
2.2
) can be reformulated into

\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t})=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}^{\top}\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t})+\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t})\hskip 8.53581pt\text{or}\hskip 8.53581pt\boldsymbol{f}_{t}^{\mathrm{response}}=\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{covariate}}_{t}+\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{\mathrm{CP}},

(2.5)

where
\boldsymbol{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{y}\times R_{x}}
is the coefficient matrix, and
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{\mathrm{CP}}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t})
.
In other words, it admits a regression form of response features

\boldsymbol{f}_{t}^{\mathrm{response}}
on covariate ones
\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{covariate}}_{t}
, where
\boldsymbol{f}_{t}^{\mathrm{response}}
summarizes all the predictable components and
\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{covariate}}_{t}
captures all the driving forces; see a similar supervised factor modeling argument in
Huang et al., 2025
and
Wang et al., 2024
. Besides, model (
2.5
) also inherits the parsimonious structure of CP decomposition: it has a complexity of
d_{\mathrm{LR}}=R_{y}R_{x}+R_{y}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}+R_{x}\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}
, which only grows linearly with the tensor order and thereby maintaining estimation efficiency even when
n
and
m
are large.

Remark 1
.

In many works of tensor-on-tensor regression, the coefficient tensor
\mathscr{A}
is assumed to have multilinear low ranks, i.e.,
\mathrm{rank}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{(i)}\right)\leq r_{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq n+m

(
Yu and Liu, 2016
;
Gahrooei et al., 2021
;
Han et al., 2022
;
Wang et al., 2022
)
. We can then have a Tucker decomposition of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}\times_{i=1}^{n+m}\boldsymbol{U}_{i}
, where
\boldsymbol{U}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}\times r_{i}}
and
\boldsymbol{U}_{n+j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}\times r_{n+j}}
are standardized to be orthonormal loading matrices for
1\leq i\leq n
,
1\leq j\leq m
, and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{n+m}}
is the core tensor. Putting this decomposition back into model (
2.2
), we have

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}=\left\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}\times_{i=1}^{n+m}\boldsymbol{U}_{i},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\right\rangle+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{or}\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}\times_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{U}_{i}^{\top}=\left\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\times_{j=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{U}_{n+j}^{\top}\right\rangle+\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{\mathrm{Tucker}},

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{\mathrm{Tucker}}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t}\times_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{U}_{i}^{\top}
.
If regarding
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}\times_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{U}_{i}^{\top}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\times_{j=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{U}_{n+j}^{\top}
as the response and covariate features, respectively, the above model then offers an interpretation of supervised factor modeling, which directly assuming
\mathscr{A}
to have a CP decomposition does not provide.
However, the Tucker-based method will lose its efficiency very quickly as the order of coefficient tensors increases, say greater than four or five
(
Oseledets, 2011
)
. It has a complexity of
d_{\mathrm{Tucker}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n+m}r_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}r_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}r_{n+j}
that is exponential in tensor orders.
For model (
2.2
) with the simplest tensor-valued observations, i.e.,
n=m=3
, the coefficient tensor
\mathscr{A}
already has an order of six, where Tucker decomposition is doomed to be inefficient.

2.2
Efficient and interpretable autoregressive time series model

This subsection adopts ideas in Section
2.1
to the autoregressive (AR) time series model,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}=\left\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-1}\right\rangle+\left\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{2},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-2}\right\rangle+\cdots+\left\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{P},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-P}\right\rangle+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t},

(2.6)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times q_{2}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
is the observed tensor,
P
is the order,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}\times q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
with
1\leq k\leq P
are coefficient tensors, and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times q_{2}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
is the error term.

We first consider the case with
P=1
, and the coefficient tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{1}
can be directly assumed to have the decomposition in (
2.4
) by letting
m=n
and
p_{j}=q_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq n
. It then leads to an AR model,
\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t})=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}^{\top}\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-1})+\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t})
, and its interpretable form:

\underbrace{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t})}_{\boldsymbol{f}_{t}^{\mathrm{response}}}=\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\underbrace{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}^{\top}\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-1})}_{\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{covariate}}_{t-1}}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t}).

(2.7)

Here the high-order tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}
is summarized into
R_{y}
features,
\boldsymbol{f}_{t}^{\mathrm{response}}
, each of which is extracted as in (
2.3
). And the information from
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-1}
is compressed into
R_{x}
features, denoted by
\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{covariate}}_{t-1}
, and each of them is obtained as in (
2.1
). The coefficient matrix
\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{y}\times R_{x}}
characterizes the relationship between response features
\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{response}}_{t}
and covariate features
\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{covariate}}_{t-1}
.

For a general order
P
, we extend model (
2.7
) and consider the following form:

\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t})=\sum_{k=1}^{P}[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{k}]_{n}\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-k})+\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t})\quad\text{with}\quad[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{k}]_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{k}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}^{\top},

(2.8)

whose interpretation can be revealed by multiplying
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}
to both sides:

\boldsymbol{f}_{t}^{\mathrm{response}}=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\boldsymbol{G}_{k}\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{covariate}}_{t-k}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t})\quad\text{with}\quad\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{covariate}}_{t-k}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}^{\top}\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-k}).

For the sake of parsimony, the matrices
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}
are shared across all
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{k}
’s, i.e., we apply a single operation to extract response features and use the same loading,
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}
, to extract features from the preceding variables
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-k}
’s. The different matrices
\boldsymbol{G}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{y}\times R_{x}}
’s allow flexible relationships between response and covariate features at different lags. Note that model (
2.8
) has a complexity of
d_{\mathrm{AR}}=PR_{y}R_{x}+(R_{y}+R_{x})\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}
, achieving both the interpretability and efficiency.

Remark 2
.

We may alternatively treat the temporal dimension as an additional tensor mode, and stack covariates
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-1},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-P}
and their coefficients
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{1},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{P}
into new tensors
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}\times P\times q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
, respectively. The resulting model is exactly the same as in (
2.2
), and therefore, our low-dimensional structure can just be applied as in (
2.5
) with
m=n+1
,
p_{1}=P
and
p_{j}=q_{j-1}
for
2\leq j\leq m
. This alternative model consists of
d_{\mathrm{ALR}}=R_{y}R_{x}+PR_{x}+(R_{y}+R_{x})\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}
parameters, while it leads to a more complex interpretation for the temporal dynamics of time series. Recovering the AR form from model (
2.5
), each coefficient
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{k}
can be expressed as

[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{k}]_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\right)^{-1}\bar{\boldsymbol{G}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}^{\top}\quad\text{with}\quad\bar{\boldsymbol{G}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{D}_{k},

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{G}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{y}\times R_{x}}
is the difference compared to (
2.8
), and
\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{x}\times R_{x}}
is a diagonal matrix. The form suggests a further constraint that all
\bar{\boldsymbol{G}}_{k}
’s shall share the same column space. This is less intuitive, and we prefer the dimension reduction at (
2.8
).

Remark 3
.

In addition to supervised models, the literature often investigates the low-rank structures of high-dimensional time series using unsupervised factor models
(
Stock and Watson, 2011
;
Bai and Wang, 2016
;
Chen et al., 2022
;
Han et al., 2024
)
, while an extra dynamic structure, such as vector AR models, has to be imposed on latent factors to enable forecasting, leading to the so-called dynamic factor model
(
Stock and Watson, 2011
)
,

\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t})=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{f}_{t}+\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\hskip 8.53581pt\text{and}\hskip 8.53581pt\boldsymbol{f}_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{f}_{t-k}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},

where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{\prod_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\times r}
is the factor loading matrix,
\boldsymbol{f}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
contains the
r
factors from
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}
,
\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
’s are the coefficient matrices for the latent process,
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{\prod_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
are error terms. Compared to our model (
2.8
), both the response and covariate factors here are extracted with identical loading matrices, which limits the flexibility of the forecasting model.

Finally, the exact low-rank assumption can be restrictive, leaving the method susceptible to model misspecification or heterogeneous signals, while the sparsity is another prevalent way to restrict the parameter space
(
Agarwal et al., 2012
;
Basu et al., 2019
;
Cai et al., 2023
;
Bai et al., 2023
)
.
It hence may be more reasonable to assume that the coefficient tensor has a low-rank plus sparse structure, which in the autoregression setting can be

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\left\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-k}\right\rangle+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t}\quad\text{with}\quad\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{k}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{\mathrm{S}}_{k}.

(2.9)

The tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}
has a low-rank structure, dedicated to modeling the associations that are collaboratively driven by latent features, whereas
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{\mathrm{S}}_{k}
is sparse to account for heterogeneous signals or the potential model misspecifications brought by the exact low-rank assumption. Here we may assume the sequential matricization of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}
to have a decomposition,
[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}]_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{k}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}^{\top}
, as in (
2.8
), and consider the elementwise sparsity for
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{\mathrm{S}}_{k}
.

2.3
High-dimensional estimation

We first consider the AR
(P)
model at (
2.8
) with
[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{k}]_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{k}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}^{\top}
.
Since the matrix
\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\right)^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{y}\times R_{y}}
has a full rank and keeps unchanged at different lag
k
, without loss of generality, we can reparameterize the model into
\boldsymbol{G}_{k}:=\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{k}
and
[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{k}]_{n}:=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\boldsymbol{G}_{k}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}^{\top}
.
Moreover, as in Remark
2
, we stack covariates
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-1},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-P}
and coefficients
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{1},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{P}
into tensors
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t}
and
\mathscr{A}
, respectively, for the sake of convenience.

For an observed time series
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{1},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{T}\}
, denote
\boldsymbol{Y}=\left(\vectorize({\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{T}}),\vectorize({\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{T-1}}),\dots,\vectorize({\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{P+1}})\right)

\in\mathbb{R}^{Q\times(T-P)}
, and

\boldsymbol{X}=\left(\vectorize({\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{T}}),\vectorize({\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{T-1}}),\dots,\vectorize({\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{P+1}})\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{PQ\times(T-P)}
, where
Q=\prod_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}
.
The ordinary least squares (OLS) estimation for AR
(P)
models has the loss function of

\mathcal{L}_{T}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$})=\frac{1}{T-P}\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\left\lVert\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t})-[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}]_{n}\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{T-P}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Y}-[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}]_{n}\boldsymbol{X}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

We first define the parameter space of model (
2.8
).
For the CP-structured loadings
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}
, their parameter space can be summarized into

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}\left(\underline{d},R\right)=\{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{\prod_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\times R}:\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\boldsymbol{U}_{n}\odot\cdots\odot\boldsymbol{U}_{1},\;&\boldsymbol{U}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}\times R},\;\\
&\lVert\boldsymbol{u}^{(i)}_{r}\rVert_{2}=1,\;\forall 1\leq i\leq n,1\leq r\leq R\},\end{split}

where
\underline{d}=\{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\}
.
For each
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\in\mathcal{S}\left(\underline{d},R\right)
, the above definition specifies its structure as the Khatri-Rao product of a series of matrices whose columns are unit-norm, ensuring that each column of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
has the form of a vectorized CP rank-1 tensor, i.e.,
\vectorize(\boldsymbol{u}^{(1)}_{r}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{u}^{(n)}_{r})
. We are now ready to define the parameter space that
\mathscr{A}
lies in:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\underline{q},P,R_{y},R_{x})=\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}\times P\times q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}:&[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}]_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}{\boldsymbol{G}}(\boldsymbol{I}_{P}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}^{\top}),\\
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}&\in\mathcal{S}(\underline{q},R_{y}),\;{\boldsymbol{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{y}\times PR_{x}},\;\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}\in\mathcal{S}(\underline{q},R_{x})\},\end{split}

where
\underline{q}=\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\}
,
\boldsymbol{I}_{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times P}
is an identity matrix,
{\boldsymbol{G}}=({\boldsymbol{G}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{G}}_{P})
, and
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product.
As a result, the OLS estimator of model (
2.8
) can be defined below,

\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}}=\argmin\mathcal{L}_{T}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$})\hskip 8.53581pt\text{subject to}\hskip 8.53581pt\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\underline{q},P,R_{y},R_{x}).

(2.10)

We next consider the low-rank plus sparse model at (
2.9
). Note that the coefficients
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}
’s can also be rearranged into a new tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}\times P\times q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}}
such that
[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}]_{n}=([\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{\mathrm{L}}_{1}]_{n},\dots,[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{\mathrm{L}}_{P}]_{n})
; and similarly for
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{\mathrm{S}}_{k}
’s, we can get
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{S}}
. By our assumptions,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\underline{q},P,R_{y},R_{x})
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{S}}
has an element-wise sparsity.
However, there is an inherent identifiability issue in the estimation of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{S}}
since when
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}
is also sparse or
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{S}}
is also low-rank, the two components cannot be distinguished from each other. To make them identifiable, we follow
Agarwal et al., 2012
,
Basu et al., 2019
and
Bai et al., 2023
to similarly impose a mild assumption that
\left\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\alpha_{\mathrm{L}}/(PQ^{2})
. The term
\alpha_{\mathrm{L}}
is the so-called radius of nonidentifiability, bounding the “spikeness” of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}
.
We define estimators for model (
2.9
) as:

\begin{split}\left(\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}}_{\mathrm{L}},\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)&=\argmin{\mathcal{L}}_{T}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{S}})+\lambda\left\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{S}}\right\rVert_{1}\\
&\hskip 8.53581pt\text{subject to}\hskip 8.53581pt\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\underline{q},P,R_{y},R_{x})\hskip 8.53581pt\text{and}\hskip 8.53581pt\left\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\alpha_{\mathrm{L}}/(PQ^{2}).\end{split}

(2.11)

Remark 4
.

To gain an intuition of
\alpha_{\mathrm{L}}
, we consider
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}
with
\left\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\approx 1
. Then setting
\alpha_{\mathrm{L}}\approx P^{1/2}Q
induces that
\left|[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}]_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n},k,j_{1},\dots j_{n}}\right|\approx P^{-1/2}Q^{-1}
for all
1\leq i_{l},j_{l}\leq q_{l}
,
1\leq l\leq n
, and
1\leq k\leq P
, which implies that the information is evenly distributed across all the entries. In contrast, when
\alpha_{\mathrm{L}}\approx PQ^{2}
, it is possible that there is a spiky entry with all the mass, leading to an extremely sparse case.

Remark 5
.

For the AR
(P)
model in Remark
2
or the more general tensor-on-tensor regression model (
2.5
), given observations
\{(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
, the loss function can be defined as
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{LR}}_{T}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t})-[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}]_{n}\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t})\rVert_{2}^{2}/T
. We further define the parameter space of
\mathscr{A}
:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\mathrm{LR}}(\underline{q},\underline{p},R_{y},R_{x})=\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}\times p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{m}}:&[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}]_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}{\boldsymbol{G}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}^{\top},\\
&\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\in\mathcal{S}(\underline{q},R_{y}),{\boldsymbol{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{y}\times R_{x}},\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}\in\mathcal{S}(\underline{p},R_{x})\},\end{split}

where
{\boldsymbol{G}}
is a reparameterization of
\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}
in (
2.5
) and
\underline{p}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}
. As a result, the corresponding OLS estimator can be given below,

\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}}_{\mathrm{LR}}=\argmin\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{LR}}_{T}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$})\hskip 8.53581pt\text{subject to}\hskip 8.53581pt\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\mathrm{LR}}(\underline{q},\underline{p},R_{y},R_{x}).

(2.12)

2.4
Algorithms

This subsection first introduces an algorithm to search for the low-rank estimate in (
2.10
), while the primary challenge lies in handling the CP-based low-rank constraint.
Denote by
\mathcal{M}_{n}(\cdot)
the reversed transformation of sequential matricization in mode
n
, i.e.,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}=\mathcal{M}_{n}([\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}]_{n})
.
Note that for any
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\underline{q},P,R_{y},R_{x})
, there exists a decomposition
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}=\mathcal{M}_{n}\{(\boldsymbol{U}_{n}\odot\cdots\odot\boldsymbol{U}_{1})\boldsymbol{G}[\boldsymbol{I}_{p}\otimes(\boldsymbol{V}_{n}\odot\cdots\odot\boldsymbol{V}_{1})]^{\top}\}
, where
\boldsymbol{U}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}\times R_{y}}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{j}\times R_{x}}
are matrices with unit-norm columns for
1\leq i,j\leq n
, and
\boldsymbol{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{y}\times R_{x}}
.
As a result, the loss function in (
2.10
) can be rewritten as
\mathcal{L}_{T}(\boldsymbol{U}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{U}_{n},\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{V}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{V}_{n})\vcentcolon=\mathcal{L}_{T}(\mathcal{M}_{n}\{(\boldsymbol{U}_{n}\odot\cdots\odot\boldsymbol{U}_{1})\boldsymbol{G}[\boldsymbol{I}_{p}\otimes(\boldsymbol{V}_{n}\odot\cdots\odot\boldsymbol{V}_{1})]^{\top}\})
, and we then can optimize the loss function with respect to
\boldsymbol{U}_{i}
’s,
\boldsymbol{G}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{j}
’s.

This paper considers an alternating least squares algorithm
(
de Leeuw et al., 1976
;
Zhou et al., 2013
)
for these individual blocks,
\boldsymbol{U}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{U}_{n},\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{V}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{V}_{n}
, leading to an alternating update on each of them while keeping the other blocks fixed.
Importantly, the update of each block is cast into a traditional linear regression problem. Specifically, when updating
\boldsymbol{U}_{i}
, it is equivalent to find the OLS estimate for the following regression problem:

\begin{split}\vectorize([\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t}]_{(i)})&=\boldsymbol{X}_{t,i}\vectorize(\boldsymbol{U}_{i})+\vectorize([\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t}]_{(i)})\\
\text{with}\hskip 8.53581pt\boldsymbol{X}_{t,i}=[(\boldsymbol{U}_{n}\odot\cdots\odot\boldsymbol{U}_{i+1}&\odot\boldsymbol{U}_{i-1}\odot\cdots\odot\boldsymbol{U}_{1})\mathrm{Diag}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})]\otimes\boldsymbol{I}_{q_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q\times q_{i}R_{y}}\end{split}

being the design matrix and
\boldsymbol{U}_{i}
being unknown parameters. Here
\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}=\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{I}_{p}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x}^{\top})\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{y}}
, and
\mathrm{Diag}(\cdot)
takes its argument to form a diagonal matrix.
Similarly, when updating
\boldsymbol{V}_{j}
, we can rewrite model (
2.8
) as

\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t})=\boldsymbol{X}_{t,j}\vectorize(\boldsymbol{V}_{j})+\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t})\hskip 8.53581pt\text{with}\hskip 8.53581pt\boldsymbol{X}_{t,j}=\sum_{r=1}^{R_{x}}\boldsymbol{b}_{r}^{\top}\otimes(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}_{r}\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}^{(j,r)}{}^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{Q\times q_{j}R_{x}},

where
\boldsymbol{b}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{x}}
is the basis vector with the
r
-th entry being one and the remaining being zeros,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}_{r}=(\boldsymbol{G}_{1,r},\dots,\boldsymbol{G}_{P,r})\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{y}\times P}
,
\boldsymbol{G}_{k,r}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{y}}
denotes the
r
-th column of
\boldsymbol{G}_{k}
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}^{(j,r)}=(\boldsymbol{y}_{t-1}^{(j,r)},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}_{t-P}^{(j,r)})\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{j}\times P}
, and
\boldsymbol{y}_{t-k}^{(j,r)}=[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t-k}]_{(j)}(\boldsymbol{v}^{(n)}_{r}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{v}^{(j+1)}_{r}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}^{(j-1)}_{r}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{v}^{(1)}_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{j}}
. When updating
\boldsymbol{G}
, model (
2.8
) can be rewritten as

\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t})=\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\vectorize(\boldsymbol{G})+\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t})\hskip 8.53581pt\text{with}\hskip 8.53581pt\boldsymbol{X}_{t}=[\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{I}_{P}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x})]\otimes\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q\times PR_{y}R_{x}}.

The OLS estimate for each of these subproblems has a closed form.
Besides, define the column-wise normalization operation as
\mathrm{ColNorm}(\boldsymbol{U})=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{D}
for any matrix
\boldsymbol{U}
where
\boldsymbol{D}
is diagonal with
\boldsymbol{D}_{ii}=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
denoting the
i
-th column of
\boldsymbol{U}
.
For simplicity of notations, denote
\{\boldsymbol{U}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
\{\boldsymbol{U}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{i-1}
,
\{\boldsymbol{U}_{l}\}_{l=i+1}^{n}
as
\boldsymbol{U}_{1\leq i\leq n}
,
\boldsymbol{U}_{l<i}
,
\boldsymbol{U}_{l>i}
, respectively. Similar notations apply to
\boldsymbol{V}
.
The full estimation procedure is encapsulated in Algorithm

.

We next construct an algorithm to search for the estimates in (
2.11
). Following
Cai et al., 2023
, we propose Algorithm
2
that involves three major steps: trimming, updating the estimate of the sparse tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{S}}
and that of the low-rank tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}
. To start with, we define a trimming operator
\mathrm{Trim}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$},\zeta)
which trims each entry of
\mathscr{A}
, denoted as
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n},k,j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}
, to
\zeta\cdot\text{sign}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n},k,j_{1},\dots,j_{n}})
if its magnitude is larger than
\zeta
, and does nothing otherwise.
At each iteration, the trimming operation is first applied to
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}
to ensure the low-rank and sparse components are identifiable. Then keeping
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}
fixed, the search of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{S}}
becomes solving a linear regression problem with a Lasso penalty, for which many optimization algorithms exist, such as the coordinate descent
(
Hastie et al., 2015
)
. Subsequently, fixing
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{S}}
, we can update
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{L}}
using the same routine as in Algorithm
1
, with the loss function adjusted to

Lastly, we consider the selection of hyperparameters and initializations of the algorithms. In Algorithm
1
, the autoregressive order
P
and ranks
(R_{y},R_{x})
need to be selected. For computational efficiency, the hold-out method is employed, where the data is split into training and validation sets, each with
T_{\mathrm{train}}
and
T_{\mathrm{val}}
samples. We first use Algorithm
1
to fit our low-rank model on the training set; and then conduct a rolling forecast on the validation set, i.e., fit the model to historical data with the ending point iterating from
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{T_{\mathrm{train}}}
to
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{T_{\mathrm{train}}+T_{\mathrm{val}}-1}
and then make one-step-ahead prediction for each iteration. The parameters
P,R_{y},R_{x}
are selected by minimizing the mean squared error of predictions on the validation set over the ranges
1\leq P\leq P^{\mathrm{max}}
,
1\leq R_{y}\leq R_{y}^{\mathrm{max}}
and
1\leq R_{x}\leq R_{x}^{\mathrm{max}}
for some predetermined upper bounds.
For Algorithm
2
, we set the radius of non-identifiability
\alpha_{\mathrm{L}}=P^{1/2}Q
for simplicity and use the same hold-out method to select
P,R_{y},R_{x}
and the penalty strength
\lambda
, where a predetermined candidate set is considered for
\lambda
as well.
Given these hyperparameters, we follow
Zhou et al., 2013
to adopt a random initialization, i.e., we randomly initialize
\boldsymbol{U}_{1\leq i\leq n}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{1\leq j\leq n}
and normalize their columns to get
\boldsymbol{U}^{(0)}_{1\leq i\leq n}
and
\boldsymbol{V}^{(0)}_{1\leq j\leq n}
, while
\boldsymbol{G}^{(0)}
is obtained through
\boldsymbol{G}^{(0)}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{G}}\mathcal{L}_{T}(\boldsymbol{U}_{1\leq i\leq n}^{(0)},\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{V}_{1\leq j\leq n}^{(0)})
. Note that the algorithm should be run for multiple initializations to locate a better stationary point.

3.1
Tensor-on-tensor regression

As a preliminary discussion for tensor autoregression, this subsection first establishes the nonasymptotic property of the low-rank estimator
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}}_{\mathrm{LR}}
in (

Prior to its presentation, we introduce the following assumptions, where we denote

and
\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{E}$}_{t})

and

Assumption 1

(Regression inputs)
.

Covariates
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\}
are i.i.d. with
\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})=\boldsymbol{0}
,
\mathrm{var}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{x}
and
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian distribution. There exist constants

,
where the two quantities
c_{x}
and

depend on the dimensions of
p_{j}
’s, and they may shrink to zero or diverge to infinity as the dimensions increase.

Assumption 2

(Regression errors)
.

Error terms

are i.i.d. and, conditional on
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}

\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
has mean zero and follows
\kappa^{2}
-sub-Gaussian distribution.

Assumption 3
.

The matrix
\boldsymbol{G}
has its operator norm upper bounded by a constant
g
with
g>0
.

The first two sub-Gaussian assumptions on the covariates and error terms are common in literature
(
Wainwright, 2019
;
Si et al., 2024
)
. Assumption
3
imposes an upper bound for the matrix
\boldsymbol{G}

\boldsymbol{G}

R_{y}
and

Denote by
d_{\mathrm{LR}}=R_{y}R_{x}+R_{y}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}+R_{x}\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}
the model complexity. Theorem

gives the nonasymptotic error upper bound.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
-
3
hold, and the true coefficient tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{\mathrm{LR}}^{*}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\mathrm{LR}}(\underline{q},\underline{p},R_{y},R_{x})

If the sample size

\displaystyle\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{\widehat{A}}$}_{\mathrm{LR}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{*}_{\mathrm{LR}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}

to have a Tucker decomposition with Tucker ranks

3.2
Tensor autoregression

This subsection considers the tensor autoregression setting that is specified in model (
2.6

Assumption 4

(Autoregression errors)
.

For the error term, let
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
. Random vectors
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\}

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{min}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$})=\min_{|z|=1}\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}}(z)\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}(z))\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{\mathrm{max}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$})=\max_{|z|=1}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}}(z)\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}(z)).

\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{I}_{Q}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}\\

6
Conclusion and discussion

Tucker and CP decompositions are commonly used to impose low-rank constraints on tensors in the literature, and they can be applied to the coefficient tensor in autoregressive models for tensor-valued time series. While Tucker decomposition offers a nice interpretation of supervised factor modeling, its efficiency in dimension reduction diminishes very quickly as tensor order increases. In contrast, CP decomposition remains efficient but only provides a vague statistical interpretation. To attain both interpretability and efficiency, this paper proposes a new approach under the supervised factor modeling paradigm by using CP decomposition to summarize high-order response and covariate tensors into features and then performing a regression on these latent features. This leads to a novel CP-based low-rank structure for the coefficient tensor.
Moreover, to account for heterogeneous signals or potential model misspecifications due to an exact low-rank assumption, this paper also considers a low-rank plus sparse model, incorporating an additional sparse coefficient tensor.
High-dimensional estimation is provided for both models, and their non-asymptotic properties are established. An alternating least squares algorithm is suggested to search for the estimates.
Finally, as demonstrated through the ENSO example, our models surpass existing methods in prediction accuracy while effectively extracting interpretable features from tensor-valued time series data.

The proposed methodology in this paper opens avenues for three potential extensions. Firstly, for our tensor low-rank autoregressive model, a sample size of
T\gtrsim\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}
is required for achieving the estimation consistency.
However, real applications, such as time-course gene expression data analysis
(
Lozano et al., 2009
)
, may not always meet this requirement.
To address this issue, we may impose sparsity to the response loading matrices
\{\boldsymbol{U}_{i}\}
and covariate loadings
\{\boldsymbol{V}_{j}\}
, which can further reduce the dimension and relax the sample size requirement.
Secondly, it is of interest to make a statistical inference on estimated tensor coefficients, such as constructing confidence intervals for specific entries or loading subspaces
(
Cai et al., 2020
;
Xia et al., 2022
)
, which we defer to future investigations.
Finally, we may extend the supervised factor modeling framework to utilize the tensor ring decomposition
(
Zhao et al., 2016
)
for feature extraction. Compared to CP-based structures, tensor ring decomposition may lead to enhanced representation ability and numerical stability while maintaining a linear growth in model complexity with respect to the tensor order.

Definition 1

(Gaussian width, Definition 7.5.1 of
Vershynin, 2018
)
.

The Gaussian width of a subset
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as

\omega(T)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in T}\langle\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\quad\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I}_{n}).

Definition 2

(Talagrand’s
\gamma_{\alpha}
functional, Definition 8.5.1 of
Vershynin, 2018
)
.

Let
(T,d)
be a metric space. A sequence of subsets
\left(T_{k}\right)_{k=0}^{\infty}
of
T
is called an admissible sequence if the cardinalities of
T_{k}
satisfy
|T_{0}|=1
and
|T_{k}|\leq 2^{2^{k}}
for all
k\geq 1
. The
\gamma_{\alpha}
functional of
T
is defined as

\gamma_{\alpha}(T,d)=\inf_{(T_{k})}\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{k/\alpha}d(t,T_{k})

where the infimum is with respect to all admissible sequences.

Lemma 3

(
Regression deviation bound
)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
hold and
T\gtrsim d_{c}d_{\mathrm{LR}}
, we have

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\mathrm{LR}}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle\leq C\kappa\sigma\sqrt{d_{c}d_{\mathrm{LR}}/T}

with probability at least
1-\exp\left\{-cd_{c}d_{\mathrm{LR}}\right\}
,
where
d_{\mathrm{LR}}=R_{y}R_{x}+R_{y}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}+R_{x}\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}
,

, and
C,c
are some positive constants.

Proof.

For a fixed matrix
\boldsymbol{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q\times\prod_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}}
, one can check that, conditional on
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
,
\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle
is a sub-Gaussian random variable with parameter
\kappa^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle\right)\right]=

\displaystyle\prod_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle\right)\right]

(B.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{e}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle\right)|\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\prod_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(C_{1}\lambda^{2}\kappa^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(C_{2}\lambda^{2}T\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\right)

(B.4)

holds for any

,
where the equality in (
B.3
) is due to the independence in Assumptions
1
and
2
, and the inequality in (
B.4
) is a direct consequence of Lemma
11
.

\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\mathrm{LR}}^{\prime}
, we thus have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l})\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle\geq u\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l})\boldsymbol{x}_{t}/T\rangle\right)\right]}{\exp(\lambda u)}

\displaystyle\exp\left(C_{2}\lambda^{2}\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}/T-\lambda u\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\min\left(\frac{u^{2}}{C_{3}^{2}\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}/T},\frac{u}{C_{3}\kappa\sigma\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}/T}\right)\right\},

B.4

\leq\exp(-u^{2}),\end{split}

(B.5)

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\mathrm{LR}}^{\prime},d_{1})\leq\left(C_{3}\kappa\sigma/T\right)\gamma_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\mathrm{LR}}^{\prime},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}})\leq\left(C_{6}\kappa\sigma/T\right)\omega^{2}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\mathrm{LR}}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Diam}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\mathrm{LR}}^{\prime})=2C_{3}\kappa\sigma/\sqrt{T},\quad\mathrm{Diam}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\mathrm{LR}}^{\prime})=2C_{3}\kappa\sigma/T.

Combined with (

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\mathrm{LR}}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle\geq C_{7}\kappa\sigma\frac{\omega(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\mathrm{LR}}^{\prime})+u}{\sqrt{T}}\right\}\leq\exp(-u^{2}).

Vershynin, 2018
) and Lemma

\displaystyle\omega(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\mathrm{LR}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{8}\int_{0}^{2}\sqrt{\log\ \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\mathrm{LR}}^{\prime},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}},\epsilon)}\mathrm{d}\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{8}\int_{0}^{2}\left\{\left(4R_{y}R_{x}+2R_{y}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}+2R_{x}\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\right)\log\left(C_{9}\frac{(m\vee n)(R_{y}\wedge R_{x})\sqrt{R_{y}R_{x}}}{\epsilon}\right)\right\}^{1/2}\mathrm{d}\epsilon

\displaystyle C_{10}\left(R_{y}R_{x}+R_{y}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}+R_{x}\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\right)^{1/2}\left[\log\left((m\vee n)(R_{y}\wedge R_{x})\sqrt{R_{y}R_{x}}\right)\right]^{1/2}.

Taking

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\mathrm{LR}}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle\leq C\kappa\sigma\sqrt{d_{c}d_{\mathrm{LR}}/T}\right\}\geq 1-\exp(-cd_{c}d_{\mathrm{LR}}).

∎

T-P\gtrsim\kappa^{4}\kappa_{U,B}^{2}d_{\mathrm{AR}}d_{c}/\kappa_{L,A}^{2}
, we have

\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\kappa_{L,A}}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{T}\leq 2\sqrt{\kappa_{U,A}}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{\mathrm{F}},

for all
\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}
with probability at least
1-2\exp\left\{-cd_{\mathrm{AR}}d_{c}\right\}
, where
d_{\mathrm{AR}}=PR_{y}R_{x}+(R_{y}+R_{x})\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}
,
d_{c}=\log(nP^{1/2}R_{y}R_{x}^{1/2})
,
\kappa_{U,A}=C_{e}/\mu_{\mathrm{min}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{\ast})
,
\kappa_{L,A}=c_{e}/\mu_{\mathrm{max}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{\ast})
,
\kappa_{U,B}=C_{e}/\mu_{\mathrm{min}}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\ast})
and
c
is a positive constant.

Proof.

Recall that
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}:=\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}/{T_{0}}
is the empirical norm.
It is sufficient to show that
\sup_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}\leq 2\sqrt{\kappa_{U,A}}
and
\inf_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}\geq\sqrt{\kappa_{L,A}}/4
hold with high probability.

Recall the VAR(
P
) representation of the autoregressive model as
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}+\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
, where we denote
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{t})
,
\boldsymbol{A}=[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}]_{n}
, and
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\vectorize(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{t})
for convenience. Note that
\boldsymbol{A}=(\boldsymbol{A}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{A}_{P})
where
\boldsymbol{A}_{k}=[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}_{k}]_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q\times Q}
and
Q=\prod_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}
. The VAR(
P
) representation can be further written into an VAR(1) form,
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}
, and hence the VMA(
\infty
) representation of
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{B}^{j}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t-j}
or
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{e}
, where

\boldsymbol{B}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}_{1}&\boldsymbol{A}_{2}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{A}_{P-1}&\boldsymbol{A}_{P}\\
\boldsymbol{I}_{Q}&\boldsymbol{O}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{O}&\boldsymbol{O}\\
\boldsymbol{O}&\boldsymbol{I}_{Q}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{O}&\boldsymbol{O}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{O}&\boldsymbol{O}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{I}_{Q}&\boldsymbol{O}\end{bmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{P}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}_{PQ}&\boldsymbol{B}&\boldsymbol{B}^{2}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{B}^{{T_{0}}-1}&\cdots\\
\boldsymbol{O}&\boldsymbol{I}_{PQ}&\boldsymbol{B}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{B}^{{T_{0}}-2}&\cdots\\
\boldsymbol{O}&\boldsymbol{O}&\boldsymbol{I}_{PQ}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{B}^{{T_{0}}-3}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\cdots\\
\boldsymbol{O}&\boldsymbol{O}&\boldsymbol{O}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{I}_{PQ}&\cdots\end{bmatrix},

\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}=(\boldsymbol{e}_{t-1}^{\top},\boldsymbol{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{0})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{PQ}
,
\boldsymbol{e}=(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{{T}}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{{T}-1}^{\top},\dots,\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{P+1}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{PQ{T_{0}}}
and
\boldsymbol{z}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{{T}}^{\top},\boldsymbol{x}_{{T}-1}^{\top},\dots,

\boldsymbol{x}_{P+1}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{PQ{T_{0}}}
. Moreover, by Assumption
4
,
\boldsymbol{e}=\boldsymbol{D}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, where
\boldsymbol{D}=\boldsymbol{I}_{P{T_{0}}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}^{1/2}
,
\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t}=(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}^{\top},\boldsymbol{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{0})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{PQ}
,
\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{{T}-1}^{\top},\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{{T}-2}^{\top},\dots,\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{P}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{PQ{T_{0}}}
.

To use the conclusion of Lemma
9
, we need to verify that the mixed tail condition is satisfied with
\boldsymbol{W}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}
. For any
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}
, we have

\left|\left(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\right)-\left(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\right)\right|=\left|\left(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\right)\right|.

We can rewrite
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}
as

\begin{split}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}&=\frac{1}{T_{0}}\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{T_{0}}\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{T_{0}}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\otimes\boldsymbol{M})\boldsymbol{z}\\
&=\frac{1}{T_{0}}\boldsymbol{e}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\otimes\boldsymbol{M})\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{e}=\frac{1}{T_{0}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\otimes\boldsymbol{M})\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{D}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\\
&=\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\top}(\frac{1}{T_{0}}\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\otimes\boldsymbol{M})\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{D})\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Q}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\end{split}

(B.7)

where
\boldsymbol{M}=\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\otimes\boldsymbol{M})\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{D}/T_{0}
.
Note that

\begin{split}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}&=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\\
&=\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\leq 2\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}.\end{split}

We would provide upper bound for
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Q}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}
and
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Q}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
.

\begin{split}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Q}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}&=\left\lVert\frac{1}{{T_{0}}}\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\otimes\boldsymbol{M})\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{D}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}\leq\frac{1}{{T_{0}}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{D}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{P}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\otimes\boldsymbol{M}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{{T_{0}}}C_{e}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top})\left\lVert\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\frac{2}{{T_{0}}}C_{e}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top})\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}.\\
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Q}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}&=\left\lVert\frac{1}{{T_{0}}}\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\otimes\boldsymbol{M})\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{D}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{{T_{0}}^{2}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{D}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}^{4}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{P}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}^{4}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\otimes\boldsymbol{M}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{{T_{0}}^{2}}C_{e}^{2}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top})^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\frac{4}{{T_{0}}}C_{e}^{2}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top})^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.\end{split}

(B.8)

Next we derive a concentration inequality for
\left|\left(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\right)\right|
. By the Hanson-Wright inequality in Theorem 6.2.1 of
Vershynin, 2018
, together with (
B.7
) and (
B.8
), we have for any
u>0
,

\displaystyle\hskip 15.0pt\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\left(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\right)\right|\geq u\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Q}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}-\mathbb{E}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Q}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right)\right|\geq u\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}-\mathbb{E}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right)\right|\geq u\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left\{-c\min\left(\frac{u}{\kappa^{2}\left\lVert\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}\right\rVert}_{\mathrm{op}},\frac{u^{2}}{\kappa^{4}\left\lVert\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left\{-c\min\left(\frac{u}{2\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}/{T_{0}}},\frac{u^{2}}{\left(2\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}/\sqrt{{T_{0}}}\right)^{2}}\right)\right\},

(B.9)

where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is the non-zero part of
\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
with independent sub-gaussian entries,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}
is the corresponding submatrix of
\boldsymbol{Q}
, and
\kappa_{U,B}=C_{e}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top})
. Note that the last inequality uses the fact that
\left\lVert\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\lesssim\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Q}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
and
\left\lVert\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{F}}}\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Q}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}
.

This concentration inequality satisfies the condition of Lemma
9
with
d_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})=2c^{\prime}\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}/{T_{0}}
and
d_{2}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})=2c^{\prime\prime}\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}/\sqrt{{T_{0}}}
. We have the following concentration bound for the empirical norm of
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
by directly applying Lemma
9
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}}\left|\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\right|>C\left(\gamma_{2}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime},d_{2})+\gamma_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime},d_{1})+u\mathrm{Diam}_{2}({\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}})+u^{2}\mathrm{Diam}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime})\right)\right\}\\
\leq 2\exp(-u^{2}).\end{split}

(B.10)

Note that
\omega(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime})
is the gaussian-width of
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}
. By Theorem 8.6.1 of
Vershynin, 2018
and Eq.(46) and Lemma 2.7 of
Melnyk and Banerjee, 2016
, we have

\begin{split}\gamma_{2}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime},d_{2})&=\left(2c^{\prime\prime}\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}/\sqrt{{T_{0}}}\right)\cdot\gamma_{2}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}})\leq\left(c_{2}\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}/\sqrt{{T_{0}}}\right)\cdot\omega(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}),\\
\gamma_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime},d_{1})&=\left(2c^{\prime}\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}/{T_{0}}\right)\cdot\gamma_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}})\leq\left(c_{1}\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}/{T_{0}}\right)\cdot\omega^{2}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}),\\
\mathrm{Diam}_{2}({\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}})&=4c^{\prime\prime}\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}/\sqrt{{T_{0}}},\quad\mathrm{Diam}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime})=4c^{\prime}\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}/{T_{0}}.\end{split}

Thus we can rewrite (
B.10
) as

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}}\left|\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\right|>c_{2}^{\prime}\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}\frac{u+\omega(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime})}{\sqrt{{T_{0}}}}+c_{1}^{\prime}\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}\frac{u^{2}+\omega^{2}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime})}{{T_{0}}}\right\}\leq 2\exp(-u^{2}).

When
{T_{0}}\gtrsim\left(u+\omega(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime})\right)^{2}
, the inequality can be reduced to

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}}\left|\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\right|>c_{3}\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}\frac{u+\omega(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime})}{\sqrt{{T_{0}}}}\right\}\leq 2\exp(-u^{2}).

(B.11)

We then provide an upper and lower bound for
\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}
. By the spectral measure of ARMA process in
Basu and Michailidis, 2015
, we have
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}\{\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top})\}\geq\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e})/\mu_{\mathrm{max}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{\ast})=\kappa_{L,A}
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\{\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top})\}\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e})/\mu_{\mathrm{min}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{A}$}^{\ast})=\kappa_{U,A}
. Then

\kappa_{L,A}\leq\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{T_{0}}\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\mathrm{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{I}_{Q}\otimes\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top})\mathrm{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top})\right)\leq\kappa_{U,A},

(B.12)

since
\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=1
.

Combining (
B.11
) and (
B.12
), we get the concentration inequality for
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}
with

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left\{\kappa_{L,A}-c_{3}\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}\frac{u+\omega(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime})}{\sqrt{T_{0}}}\leq\inf_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\leq\kappa_{U,A}+c_{3}\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}\frac{u+\omega(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime})}{\sqrt{T_{0}}}\right\}\\
&\hskip 350.0pt\geq 1-2\exp(-u^{2}).\end{split}

(B.13)

By the Dudley’s inequality (see e.g., Theorem 8.1.10 of
Vershynin, 2018
) and Lemma
1
(b), we have

\displaystyle\omega(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\int_{0}^{2}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}},\epsilon\right)}\mathrm{d}\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\int_{0}^{2}\left(4PR_{y}R_{x}+2(R_{y}+R_{x})\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\right)^{1/2}\left[\log\left(\frac{C_{2}n(R_{y}\wedge PR_{x})\sqrt{PR_{y}R_{x}}}{\epsilon}\right)\right]^{1/2}\mathrm{d}\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\left(4PR_{y}R_{x}+2(R_{y}+R_{x})\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\right)^{1/2}\left[\log(nR_{y}\sqrt{PR_{x}})\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle=C_{4}\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{AR}}{d_{c}}},

(B.14)

where
d_{\mathrm{AR}}=PR_{y}R_{x}+(R_{y}+R_{x})\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}
and
d_{c}=\log(nP^{1/2}R_{y}R_{x}^{1/2})
.
By letting
u=\omega(\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime})
and
T_{0}\gtrsim\kappa^{4}\kappa_{U,B}^{2}d_{\mathrm{AR}}d_{c}/\kappa_{L,A}^{2}
, (
B.13
) can be reduced to

\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{\kappa_{L,A}}{16}\leq\inf_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\right\rVert_{T}^{2}\leq 4\kappa_{U,A}\right\}\geq 1-2\exp\left\{-cd_{\mathrm{AR}}d_{c}\right\}.

By spectral measure of ARMA process in
Basu and Michailidis, 2015
, we could replace
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top})
by
1/\mu_{\mathrm{min}}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\ast})
where
\boldsymbol{B}^{\ast}(z)\vcentcolon=\boldsymbol{I}_{PQ}-\boldsymbol{B}^{\ast}z
and
\mu_{\mathrm{\min}}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\ast})=\min_{|z|=1}\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{B}^{\ast}}(z)

\boldsymbol{B}^{\ast}(z))
. Thus, the proof is completed.
∎

Lemma 5

(
Autoregression deviation bound
)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
3
,
5
,
4
hold and
T-P\gtrsim d_{\mathrm{AR}}d_{\mathrm{c}}
, we have

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{T-P}\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle\leq C_{1}\kappa^{2}\kappa_{U,B}^{\prime}\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{AR}}d_{c}/(T-P)}

with probability at least
1-\exp\left\{-C_{2}d_{\mathrm{AR}}d_{c}\right\}
, where
\kappa_{U,B}^{\prime}=C_{e}/{\mu^{1/2}_{\mathrm{min}}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\ast})}
,
d_{\mathrm{AR}}=PR_{y}R_{x}+(R_{y}+R_{x})\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}
,
d_{c}=\log(nP^{1/2}R_{y}R_{x}^{1/2})
, and
C_{1},C_{2}
are some positive constants.

Proof.

To use the conclusion of Lemma
9
, we first define
\boldsymbol{W}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})=\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle/T_{0}
. For any
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\prime}
, we have

\begin{split}&\left|\frac{1}{T_{0}}\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle-\frac{1}{T_{0}}\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle\right|=\left|\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle\right|.\end{split}

where
\boldsymbol{M}=(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})/T_{0}
with
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}/T_{0}
.

We then provide an upper bound for
\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle\right)\right]
, for any positive
\lambda
.
By Assumption
4
and Lemma
10
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{t},\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\rangle\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(C^{\prime}\lambda^{2}\kappa^{2}\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(C^{\prime}\lambda^{2}\kappa^{2}C_{e}\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\|\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right],

(B.15)

where every entry of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
is mean-zero and i.i.d.
\kappa^{2}
-sub-Gaussian.
By a similar argument in Lemma
4
, we can rewrite

\sum_{t=P+1}^{T}\|\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\otimes\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M})\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{D})\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

(B.16)

where
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}=(\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\otimes\boldsymbol{M})\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{D}
. The upper bounds for
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}
and
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
are

\begin{split}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}&=\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\otimes\boldsymbol{M})\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{D}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{D}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{P}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\otimes\boldsymbol{M}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}\\
&\leq C_{e}^{1/2}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top})\left\lVert\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\frac{1}{{T_{0}}}C_{e}^{1/2}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top})\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}.\\
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}&=\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\otimes\boldsymbol{M})\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{D}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{D}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{P}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\otimes\boldsymbol{M}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\\
&=C_{e}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top})\left\lVert\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{0}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{{T_{0}}}C_{e}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{P}^{\top})\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.\end{split}

(B.17)

Let
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
be the non-zero part of
\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
with independent sub-gaussian entries,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{1}
be the corresponding submatrix of
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}
, we have
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
. Combining this equation with (
B
) and (
B.16
), by Lemma

.

-measurable,
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable, and every entry of
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
is mean-zero and i.i.d.
\kappa^{2}
-sub-Gaussian distributed. Then for any
\lambda>0
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\rangle\right)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(C\lambda^{2}\kappa^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right],

where
C
is some positive constants.

Proof.

By the tower rule, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\rangle\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\rangle\right)\mid\mathcal{F}_{T-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\langle\boldsymbol{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\rangle\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\langle\boldsymbol{w}_{T},\boldsymbol{e}_{T}\rangle\right)\mid\mathcal{F}_{T-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(c^{\prime}\lambda^{2}\kappa^{2}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{T}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\langle\boldsymbol{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\rangle\right)\right]\right],

where the above inequality follows from the the fact that
\langle\boldsymbol{w}_{T},\boldsymbol{e}_{T}\rangle\mid\mathcal{F}_{T-1}
is mean-zero and
\kappa^{2}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{T}\|^{2}_{2}
-sub-Gaussian, since the entries in
\boldsymbol{e}_{T}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
is mean-zero and i.i.d.
\kappa^{2}
-sub-Gaussian. Thus we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\langle\boldsymbol{w}_{T},\boldsymbol{e}_{T}\rangle\right)\mid\mathcal{F}_{T-1}\right]\leq\exp\left(c^{\prime}\lambda^{2}\kappa^{2}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{T}\|_{2}^{2}\right)
.
Then induction on
t
gives the conclusion.
∎

Lemma 11
.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is a mean zero, sub-gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

with
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq\kappa
. Let
\boldsymbol{M}
be an
m\times n
matrix. We have

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\right]\leq\exp\left(C\kappa^{2}\lambda^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\right)\hskip 10.0pt\text{provided }|\lambda|\leq\frac{c}{\kappa\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}},

where
C
and
c
are some positive constants.

Proof.

This proof is similar to the proof of Hanson-Wright inequality in Section 6.2 of
Vershynin, 2018
.
Let
\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a standard Gaussian random vector, i.e.
\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I}_{m})
, and independent with
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\boldsymbol{g}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{g}}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\boldsymbol{g}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}/2\right)\right],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\boldsymbol{g}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{g}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\boldsymbol{g}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)\mid\boldsymbol{g}\right]\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(C\kappa^{2}\lambda^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\right],

where
C
is a positive constant. Comparing these two lines, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}/2\right)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(C\kappa^{2}\lambda^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\right].

Expressing
\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
through its singular value decomposition
\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}^{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{\top}
, where
s_{i}
are the singular values of
\boldsymbol{M}
. We can write

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\boldsymbol{g}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}^{2}\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{i},\boldsymbol{g}\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{i},\boldsymbol{g}\rangle.

By rotation invariance of the normal distribution,
\left(\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{i},\boldsymbol{g}\rangle\right)_{i=1}^{m}
is still a standard normal random vector, which is denoted by
\boldsymbol{g}^{\prime}
. By independence we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(C\kappa^{2}\lambda^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\right]&=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(C\kappa^{2}\lambda^{2}s_{i}^{2}g_{i}^{\prime 2}\right)\right]\\
&\leq\prod_{i=1}^{m}\exp\left(C^{\prime}\kappa^{2}\lambda^{2}s_{i}^{2}\right)\\
&=\exp\left(C^{\prime}\kappa^{2}\lambda^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{2}\right)\\
&=\exp\left(C^{\prime}\kappa^{2}\lambda^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\right)\end{split}

provided that
\lambda^{2}\kappa^{2}s_{i}^{2}\leq c^{\prime}
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,m
, where the inequality follows from Proposition 2.7.1 in
Vershynin, 2018
for the sub-exponential random variable
{g_{i}^{\prime}}^{2}
. Note that
\max_{i}s_{i}=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{op}}}
, so the lemma is proved.
∎
