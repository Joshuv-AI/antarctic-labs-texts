Modular lab-on-chip platform with integrated a-Si:H sensors for real-time bioluminescence monitoring in bioreactors under microgravity conditions
Authors: Lorenzo Nardi, Dirk Jonker, Vahid Omrani, Parsa Abbasrezaee, Sahithi Sriramoju, Emani Venkata Sai Siva Sankam, Domenico Caputo, Nicola Lovecchio, Giulia Petrucci, Alessio Di Serafino, Francesca Costantini, Donato Calabria, Elisa Lazzarini, Han Gardeniers, Mara Mirasoli, Augusto Nascetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-term deep space missions present critical health challenges due to prolonged exposure to space radiation and microgravity, leading to significant physiological risks for astronauts. Galactic cosmic rays, in particular, can penetrate conventional shielding and produce harmful secondary particles, emphasizing the need for innovative in-situ solutions to study the effects of these environmental conditions on astronauts’ health and mitigate the risks. We have developed an advanced lab-on-chip system designed for real-time monitoring and precise environmental control, which allows us to detect changes in living cells throughout a long-term space mission. The system is equipped with integrated photo and thermal sensors based on hydrogenated amorphous silicon functional layers and resistive heating elements for continuous environmental monitoring and precise temperature regulation. By using a modular and adaptable fabrication approach—combining laser-cut polymethyl methacrylate components, adhesive bonding, and thin-film integration—our design enables rapid prototyping and customization for various mission needs. Our platform features a tapered culture chamber that employs both passive and active fluid management techniques to maintain stable liquid positioning. This is achieved through feedback-controlled pressure modulation, addressing the unique challenges of fluid dynamics in space. We validated the system through a series of simulations and experiments demonstrating effective fluid management and accurate environmental control, essential for future biological research in space. Our results underscore the potential of this low-power, automated, and highly compact lab-on-chip solution to advance the study of space radiation effects and contribute to safer, long-duration crewed missions.Keywords: Lab-on-chip, Micro-incubator, Hydrogenated amorphous silicon photosensors, MicrogravitySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

The space environment includes various forms of ionizing radiation, such as particles from solar wind, solar particle events (SPEs), and coronal mass ejections, as well as highly charged galactic cosmic rays (GCRs). Long-term deep space missions expose astronauts to cumulative risks due to space radiation and microgravity, conditions that can lead to muscle atrophy, immune system suppression, metabolic alterations, and an elevated cancer risk, posing a limit to extended crewed missions 1. While shielding can mitigate exposure to SPEs, it is largely ineffective against GCRs, whose high energy and complex trajectories allow them to penetrate shielding and generate secondary particles. Given the lack of terrestrial conditions that replicate the deep space radiation spectrum, in-situ studies are essential to understanding space radiation’s impact on human biology and developing tailored protective measures for astronauts 2–5. To address these challenges, recent and upcoming biological space missions have investigated radiation effects on microorganisms through nanosatellites.

O/OREOS 3U CubeSat was developed by the Small Spacecraft Division at NASA Ames Research Center, with the purpose of studying how micro-organisms survive and adapt to the stresses of the space environment through the SESLO (Space Environment Survivability of Living Organisms) experiments 6.

Recent developments have further highlighted the role of microfluidic and lab-on-chip systems for autonomous biological experimentation in space environments9,10. Traditional missions rely on miniaturized conventional instrumentation, which ensures reliability but limits the type and quality of experiments that can be performed on nanosatellites. These systems, though small, require high energy consumption, often incompatible with the limited resources of CubeSat. In particular, the use of fluidic cartridges and bulky instruments (such as microscopes or spectrometers) requires costly thermal control and reduces the number of replicable experiments. Furthermore, the need to incubate different cells in separate chambers increases complexity and power consumption. These limitations represent an obstacle to missions beyond low Earth orbit, unless larger satellites are used11.

As an alternative to conventional methods, lab-on-chip (LoC) systems offer a highly integrated, compact, and low-power solution, ideal for CubeSat missions. In addition to ensuring automation and precise environmental control, LoCs enable in situ analysis of radiation effects on biological samples, with real-time monitoring via miniaturized sensors, avoiding the use of bulky instrumentation 12.

The ALCYONE project adopts an innovative approach based not on the miniaturization of existing technologies, but on the modular combination of micro technologies, advanced electronics, and bioanalytical methodologies. This strategy enables the creation of a microgravity-compatible platform, optimized for cell distribution and nutrient exchange, and designed to ensure high experimental reproducibility even in space.

Materials and methods

Design

The final aim of ALCYONE project is the development of a new LoC incubator able to accommodate four different genetically engineered cell cultures with localized thermal control and monitor their status by detecting the expression of bioluminescent reporters by means of on-chip hydrogenated amorphous silicon photosensors. In particular, prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells have been genetically modified by introducing a luciferase-based reporter system in response to damage induced by ionizing radiation (Fig. 1).

By maintaining the independence of fluidic, thermal, and sensing modules, the system achieves several key advantages. Both simulation and experimental validations demonstrate the system’s ability to manage liquid positioning and maintain uniform and precise thermal conditions. The platform thus represents a significant advancement over traditional miniaturized analytical systems, combining automation, compactness, and low power consumption tailored specifically to the rigorous demands of space applications.

Thin-film deposition selected for sensors and heaters fabrication, and adhesive bonding for robust assembly, further complements the modular approach, facilitating swift iterations and allowing adaptation to specific mission needs. Laser cutting in PMMA was selected for fabricating the fluidic layer due to its superior edges and walls quality, higher dimensional accuracy, and enhanced capability in reproducing complex and curved geometries compared to conventional CNC machining.

One significant challenge in designing microfluidic pattern arises from the size constraints typical for LoC devices. For instance, the volume and height restrictions listed in Table 1, assuming a gas-to-liquid filling ratio of 2:1 and a cylindrical shape, would yield a cell culture chamber radius that is always larger than the height of the cell culture chamber. The disc-like geometry that arises when Rccc > > Hccc contrasts sharply with rod-like shapes, such as those used in well plates, familiar to biologists. In microgravity, the disc-like shape can exacerbate fluid dynamics issues 13, complicating filling 14, draining 15,16, and conducting experiments 17. Specifically, uncontrolled liquid pinning may obstruct channels or cause inconsistent filling, affecting experimental reproducibility. These fluid dynamics issues are foreseen to significantly affect experimental reproducibility and overall device functionality. Addressing these challenges is crucial for ensuring the reliable performance of the cell culture system in space environments.

To overcome these challenges, we propose a tapered cell culture chamber design (Fig. 1). This design leverages the tendency of water to creep into confined spaces on hydrophilic substrates 18,19, with the driving capillary force influenced by channel dimensions and taper angle 20. By ensuring that the liquid volume pins in the desired location 21,22, the tapered chamber aims to enhance the reliability and consistency of experiments conducted within the LoC device.

Fabrication

Thin-film sensors and actuator

Hydrogenated amorphous silicon (a-Si:H) is uniquely suited for microfluidic integration due to its low-temperature deposition (< 200 °C), which enables direct fabrication on temperature-sensitive substrates like glass and polymers (e.g., PMMA, PDMS), alongside its tunable electronic properties and compatibility with standard lithography. In our work, we leveraged a-Si:H thin films to monolithically integrate three key functional elements on a single microfluidic chip:

Microheater: operating via Joule heating, where the sheet resistance of the a-Si:H layer, tunable via deposition conditions, allows precise, low-power local heating for thermal actuation or gradient generation.

Microheater: operating via Joule heating, where the sheet resistance of the a-Si:H layer, tunable via deposition conditions, allows precise, low-power local heating for thermal actuation or gradient generation.

Photosensors: based on the photoconductive effect in a p-i-n structure, where absorbed photons generate electron–hole pairs separated by an internal field to produce a measurable photocurrent for on-chip fluorescence or bioluminescence.

Photosensors: based on the photoconductive effect in a p-i-n structure, where absorbed photons generate electron–hole pairs separated by an internal field to produce a measurable photocurrent for on-chip fluorescence or bioluminescence.

Thermal sensor: exploiting the linear temperature dependence of the forward-bias voltage in a p-i-n diode, governed by the thermal activation of carriers and defect states in the bandgap, enabling real-time thermal monitoring with millisecond response.

Thermal actuation

Thermal actuation is fundamental in the ALCYONE LoC system, as it enables the creation of a suitable microenvironment to sustain cell viability and allow accurate biological investigation. Moreover, efficient heating is critical as it is essential to meet the low-power consumption requirements of space hardware like a dedicated cell incubator. According to the system requirements, achieving uniform temperature distribution across the cell culture chambers and enabling precise temperature control are key design drivers, along with the possibility to individually control the four chambers at potentially different temperatures. The ALCYONE heating system consists of four circular heaters, which are designed to uniformly heat one of the four circular reactors (chambers), each with a diameter of 8 mm. This arrangement guarantees consistent and uniform heating across the entire surface of each reactor, which is crucial for maintaining stable temperature conditions 23. The heaters featured symmetric arms extending from the main heating area, creating an overall symmetrical structure. However, the advancement introduced with the asymmetric heater semi-circular arms, which are rotated with respect to the chip orientation to ensure better temperature uniformity. The symmetric configuration led to temperature gradients due to the proximity of the chambers, with the two closest ones having a centre-to-centre distance of 10 mm, while the two farthest being 16 mm apart. This proximity resulted in uneven heat distribution, prompting the asymmetric design to minimize thermal crosstalk and provide more stable temperatures. This configuration electrically connects heaters in the four-terminal interface design, allowing for accurate resistance measurements in real-time through the Kelvin sensing method, enabling precise temperature monitoring.

Sensors

In the broader context of the ALCYONE mission, these sensors are not merely instruments but are the keystones in the project’s objective to extend biological research beyond Low Earth Orbit (LEO). A schematic view of the chip layout is shown in Fig. 3. They represent a critical step towards achieving full autonomy in experimental setups for small-scale, remote missions, removing the dependency on larger infrastructures or external instruments. Beyond their contribution to system compactness, the monolithic integration of sensors with the microfluidic layer enhances noise detectivity and improves data efficiency, making this solution particularly well-suited for CubeSat-class missions.

ALCYONE sensors’ chip includes several a-Si:H photodiodes. These photosensors are specifically designed to enable real-time monitoring of cellular responses via bioluminescence detection. The half-donut geometry of each sensor is optimized to enhance photon capture from the bioluminescent cell cultures within the incubation chamber. The sensor chip features a total of eight photodiodes (two per each of the four chambers).

Manufacturing process

The fabrication of the heaters involved several key steps to ensure the desired geometry and electrical properties. The process began with cleaning the glass substrate using a piranha solution to remove organic residues and to prepare the surface for subsequent deposition. A multi-layer Cr/Al/Cr/Al/Cr stack was then deposited on the glass substrate using physical vapour deposition (PVD). The thicknesses of the layers were carefully controlled, following this configuration: 30 nm of chromium, 40 nm of aluminium, 30 nm of chromium, 280 nm of aluminium, and a final 30 nm of chromium. After the deposition, a lithography process was performed using a mask that defined the geometry of the heaters. This was followed by etching the entire stack, ensuring that the heating elements were precisely aligned with the design.

A second lithography step was then conducted to cover the structure’s pads, protecting them during the next etching phase. This etching targeted the first two layers of chromium and aluminium (Cr/Al), leaving the pads intact while ensuring that the central part of the heaters increased in resistance. This was designed to focus heat generation on the central region of the heater, as planned during the project phase.

The photodiode and the temperature sensor are identical a-Si:H p-i-n structures, deposited in parallel during the same fabrication run (Fig. 4). The only difference lies in the presence of a patterned metal layer on the ITO transparent contact of the temperature sensor, which acts as a light shield to suppress photo response. Aside from this, the device structure is quite conventional and requires a set of five photomasks for its patterning. Microfluidic chip

The microfluidic chip was fabricated using a three-layer sandwich architecture. The method was developed to bond a PMMA substrate on which the microchannel network was fabricated via laser cutting (2.5 mm thick) and two PMMA foils (100 μm thick) using ARcare® 90106NB double-sided adhesive tape (140 μm thick, excluding protective liners). Adhesive-based bonding approaches have already been successfully implemented in several space biological microfluidic systems, including GeneSat-1 24, PharmaSat 25, O/OREOS 6, BioSentinel 8, and APHRODITE, demonstrating the feasibility of PSA-based multilayer microfluidic architectures for space applications.

Prior to device fabrication, cutting parameters were optimized for each laser cutter, considering material variability. The engraving process was optimized to achieve the desired width and depth, ensuring sufficient clearance for tubing. Width was controlled by adjusting the dimensions in the laser cutter software, while depth was modified by altering laser power and cutting speed.

PMMA substrates were cut, and the adhesive tape was trimmed. The tape was aligned along the side of the PMMA substrate and applied gradually using the backside of a plastic ruler to ensure even pressure and avoid bubbles. This was repeated on the opposite side of the substrate. Subsequently, the assembled PMMA and double-sided adhesive layers were cut via laser cutting. Then, 5 × 5 cm foils were cut using a dicing saw. After cutting, the protective liner was removed from the foils, which were then rinsed with DI water, isopropanol, and dried with nitrogen. The second liner was left in place in order to protect the surface.

Then, the protective liner was removed from one side of the adhesive tape, and the foil was carefully aligned with the edge of the PMMA substrate, initially maintaining an inclined angle to prevent premature sticking. Once aligned, the angle was reduced until contact was made. The foils were then manually pressed using light pressure, swiping from the center outward to avoid excessive pressure on the cell culture chambers, which lack supporting structures and are prone to fractures. This was done for both sides of the PMMA substrate.

Establishing a reliable connection between the fluidic network and the external instrumentation is essential for LoC device functionality. The fluidic connections made to microfluidic chips can be broadly classified into top-port connections and side-port connections. Top-port connections are positioned on top of the chip and are oriented perpendicular to the flow direction inside the microfluidic channel. In contrast, side-port connections are extensions of the microfluidic channel and are oriented parallel to the flow direction (Fig. 5). A specific requirement of our design is to ensure maximum clearance at the top and bottom of the LoC device for electrical connections and clamping. This necessitates that only the side of the chip be accessible for fluidic interfacing. Moreover, combining PMMA with glass makes the fabrication of a top-port connection difficult. Although it is possible to tap a thread into glass, it requires specialized techniques and tools.

ALCYONE LOC assembly and its fluidic network.

To achieve a reliable connection, a dedicated insert is fabricated, allowing fluid or gas transfer across scales. A robust side-port connection is achieved by positioning an insert at the chip’s side edge, into which a fluidic tube is inserted and sealed with adhesive. The assembled LoC device was secured with the fluidic connections positioned upright relative to Earth’s gravitational pull. Several pieces of 1/16″ outer diameter PTFE tubing, cut to uniform lengths, were prepared. The tubing was inserted into the port until it reached the edge of the insert. A two-component epoxy glue (Araldite 2012) was mixed on a blank silicon wafer using a disposable applicator and promptly applied to the chip/tube interface. The assembly was left to dry for 24 h before removing the chip from its fixed position. These connections are easy to fabricate, mechanically robust, and maintain leak-tight integrity under typical flow conditions used in the ALCYONE LoC fluidic setup (up to 1000 μL/min), ensuring reliable operation under dynamic conditions. The fluidic interface was designed to ensure mechanical robustness, leak-tight sealing, and compatibility with biological samples, with preliminary validation indicating suitability for further qualification toward space applications.

Experimental set-up

The electronic front-end includes the electrical interface to the lab-on-chip, the photocurrent readout board, the temperature sensor readout board, and the heater driver board. The temperature readout board also acts as the system controller, coordinating the other boards, collecting data and providing a USB interface. A custom C + + graphical user interface is used to operate the system and visualize the acquired data. A sandwich-type architecture was developed with the LoC positioned between two printed circuit boards (PCBs). Each of the boards uses spring-loaded contacts (Samtec SIB-122-02-F-S-LC) to implement the electrical connection with the chip pads and routes each line toward conventional cable connectors to interface with the control and readout electronics. According to the chip structure, the bottom board implements the interface with the on-chip sensors, both optical and temperature ones, providing the required sensor bias and readout lines (Fig. 6).

To achieve precise control over the contact line position in the tapered section of the incubator, we developed a fluidic system that simultaneously measures and regulates pressure, flow rate, and contact line position. This setup is essential for maintaining stable and reproducible conditions within the cell culture environment. The fluidic system was controlled via LabView software, which communicated with an OB1 MK3 + pressure controller from Elveflow. The pressure-driven setup employed in this study is intended for ground-based validation, while the flight implementation will rely on miniaturized and integrated actuation solutions compatible with space constraints. Pressure was applied to airtight liquid storage tubes, and the flow rate was determined by the hydraulic resistance of the external circuit.

To manage hydraulic resistance, a MUX valve controller from Elveflow was used, in combination with a 3/2 valve, enabling switching between a low-impedance path for rapid filling and a high-impedance path for precise flow regulation. The latter was introduced through a 20 cm long PEEK tube with a 65 μm internal diameter, calculated based on Hagen-Poiseuille principles. Pressure sensors were positioned at two key locations: one at the exit of the pressure controller to monitor overall system pressure, and one just upstream of the microfluidic chip to assess differential pressure. Figure 6 shows the schematic diagram of the experimental setup.

The LoC device is positioned beneath a microscope. The meniscus (interface between the gas and liquid) is usually easy to observe. because of a large difference between the refractive indices of both media and due to the usage of transparent materials, e.g. glass and PMMA. A machine vision camera (FLIR Blackfly S USB3) was connected to the microscope at the top viewport with a C-mount. Illumination was achieved by a dimmable LED ring for microscopes. Two PID loops were programmed in LabVIEW. One PID loop regulates the pressure applied to the fluidic inlet based on the flow measured. The second PID loop regulates the pressure applied to the gas inlet and outlet channels based on the measured contact line position. The contact line position was established using a Sobel filter and an edge detection algorithm based on a projection of the pixel intensity integrated.

Results and discussion

Sensors characterization

The devices were characterized by means of current–voltage measurement of the a-Si:H diode characteristics under dark conditions using a Keythley 236 Source Measurement Unit in a thermally controlled probe station. In addition, the spectral response in the visible range of two photodiodes in each chip was measured using a Quantum Yield test setup based on two Keythley 236 Source Measurement Units, a monochromator and a reference crystalline silicon photodiode. All the four prototype chips gave positive results with very good quality of the a-Si:H sensors and high process yield (Fig. 7).

In addition, the characterization of the temperature sensors has also been performed by measuring their current–voltage curves in dark condition at different temperatures. Figure 7 reports the current–voltage curves measured between -0.5V and 0.8V varying the temperature between 25 °C and 40 °C, which is the target operating range for the ALCYONE LoC. where I0 is the reverse saturation current, and VT is the thermal voltage equal to kT/q with k Boltzmann constant, T the absolute temperature and q the electron charge.

As expected, in the entire investigated range, the reverse current increases exponentially with the temperature. Furthermore, in the forward region, at fixed current, the voltage decreases with temperature. In particular, when biased in the exponential region the diode can be used as temperature sensor because voltage linearly decreases with temperature. To evaluate the sensor temperature performance, the voltage across the diode as a function of temperature at 10nA forward biasing current is reported. From this experiment, we infer that the sensor sensitivity is equal to -3.3mV/ °C. This value is in excellent agreement with those of other temperature sensors fabricated by UNIROMA1 research group.

The mean value of the I–V characteristic of the a-Si:H temperature sensors integrated on the chip, measured in the range from − 0.5 V to 1 V, with standard deviation, is reported. In the reverse bias region, the current remains very low with values on the order of 10−13–10−12 A. Moreover, the broad exponential trend in the forward bias region ensures accurate temperature monitoring. Finally, the small standard deviation observed between the different sensors indicates a good uniformity of the devices across the chip, confirming the high reproducibility of the fabrication process and the reliability of the integrated temperature sensing system.

Fluidic experiments and simulation

In the first set of experiments, an automated filling process was executed. The results include photographs alongside the measurement data collected during the experiments. The filling process began by filling the chamber through the low-impedance channel up to a predetermined pixel position, which was scaled to the length of the channel (Low Z filling on). This was achieved by switching valve V1 to the ON state (1) and setting the actuation pressure at the fluidic inlet (P3) to 30 mBar. A position PID controller was used to establish a closed-loop feedback system that measured the meniscus position and adjusted the valve state and inlet pressure accordingly. Once the target position was reached, the valve was switched to the high-impedance OFF state (Low Z filling off), and the PID control for regulating the flow was activated (Flow PID on). At this stage, P3 was adjusted to achieve a designated flow rate on FS1.

The hydraulic resistance across the tapered section is lower than that of the outlet channel; therefore, the direction of the flow was initially into the tapered section. At this point, the position PID was turned ON (Position PID on). This PID controller regulated the gas pressures at the gas inlet (P1) and outlet (P2), using the measured pixel position for feedback. Once the position PID was active, the flow direction changed from the tapered section toward the fluidic outlet, and the PID maintained the meniscus position. During the experiment, the gas pressure at P2 was set to be 10 mBar higher than at P1. This means that only one PID loop is used to control the pressure on P2, P1 is simply 10 mBar low. This also means that there is a gas flow across the meniscus. This gas flow is observed to be essential in avoiding the formation of condensation at the gas side of the meniscus. However, this also means that the liquid may evaporate at an increased rate at the meniscus, as the partial pressure of the water vapour in the gas section is expected to be gas flow-rate dependent. Consequently, there must be flow into the tapered section and into the outlet, for the meniscus position to be maintained and for water to flow into the outlet. Keeping the meniscus for an extensive amount of time reverses the curvature from an advancing front to a relaxed or possibly receding state (Meniscus relaxed). The possibility of the receding state may be due to evaporation of water at the meniscus.

We observed that once the liquid flowed steadily through the outlet, it was essential to keep the meniscus advancing through the outlet tubing while maintaining consistent hydrostatic pressures. This required minimizing significant changes in tubing elevation to avoid instability. For instance, a photograph shows instability caused by a rapid liquid drop through a large (~ 5 cm) height difference, which pulled the liquid out of the tapered chamber (Instability). The PID controller could not compensate for this effect, since the gas pressure at the outlet channel could not provide a relative negative pressure. To prevent such occurrences, a fixed pressure was applied to the fluidic outlet, resulting in active backflow of liquid from the outlet to the tapered section. This approach not only allowed the tapered channel to fill more quickly than solely through the high-impedance section, but also effectively pushed bubbles back into the tapered section. Following the instability, a droplet detached from the receding liquid meniscus but reattached after refilling (Contact with detached droplet). Upon reaching the target position for the second time, we increased the target position twice to extend the filling length of the channel (Increase position). This demonstrated the stability of the system by maintaining the position for over a minute (Position Maintained).

In another set of experiments, the goal was to show precise retraction of the liquid meniscus to simulate film positioning under constant flow conditions. The idea behind this is to mimic actions such as pipetting or continuous supply of nutrients and removal of waste. The experiment begins with a steady-state condition, where the liquid fills a section of the culture chamber. Over time, the meniscus is retracted in discrete steps, allowing it to stabilize briefly between each step. Figures 8 and 9 display the results of these experiments. On an extended timescale of 7 min, pressures and flows show large transients, but the mean values are more or less constant over the length of the experiment. However, the position of the meniscus is changed by approximately 5 mm. Two events are observed where a relatively large change in measured pressure occurs. As shown, a spike in applied pressure to P1 and P2 resulted in a spike in the flow rate observed in FS2 and a relatively large decrease in position. We hypothesize that this is due to the nature of the PID and control system. Although we attempted to tune the PID parameters, a trade-off had to be reached, ultimately leading to an underdamped system.

In the Navier–Stokes equations, the dominant body force is gravity, and in the energy equation the convective term often includes contributions from natural convection, which is driven by the density variations and the gravitational force term from the Navier–Stokes equations (often coupled via the Boussinesq approximation). Similarly, the convective term of the transport equation can be significantly influenced by gravity-driven flows. In microgravity conditions, the gravitational body force becomes negligible compared to other forces, fundamentally altering the fluid dynamics. Consequently, surface tension forces and capillary effects become much more significant and dictate the shape of fluid interfaces, and the wetting characteristics of fluids on surfaces 26,27.

Accurate modelling of fluid behaviour is therefore crucial for optimizing device design and performance. Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) plays a key role in simulating both single-phase and multiphase flow behaviour. Single-phase Flow Modelling was used to predict dynamics in confined geometries, optimizing channel design, flow velocity, and pressure distribution; while Multiphase Flow Modelling addressed interactions between different phases, critical for lab-on-a-chip applications. This has significant implications for the design of containment systems, gas exchange membranes, and ensuring stable culture environments. The results of the chamber filling process in Fig. 10 demonstrate the correct behaviour of the different phases. In this model, the red phase represents the gas phase and the blue phase represents the liquid phase. Their interaction was investigated using the Level Set method implemented in COMSOL Multiphysics 28,29.

Thermal simulation

This section presents the simulation and experimental validation of the thermal module of ALCYONE LoC platform. The heat transfer simulation was developed using a multi-physics approach, coupling thermal and electrical phenomena. Heaters were modelled as Joule heating elements, with the central part designed to be more resistive than the connection pads to prevent heating of the latter. Heat generation was focused in the main heating area of the chambers, avoiding unnecessary heating of the surrounding regions. Then, to evaluate the simulation performance and validate temperature uniformity across the ALCYONE, a series of thermal measurements was conducted using a FLIR A325 thermocamera.

The simulated design (Fig. 11) consisted of two glass layers, each 1.2 mm thick, and a microfluidic structure. The top glass layer hosted the heaters, while the bottom layer was bare glass, which represents the layer that will accommodate the photo and temperature sensors. Between these two layers, a PMMA section was placed, where the chambers were formed. These chambers were filled with water during the simulation and significantly influenced the heat transfer within the system, making their inclusion crucial for accurately modelling thermal behaviour. Simulations were conducted under ambient conditions (20 °C), with external natural convection in air applied to all surfaces of the system to account for heat exchange with the environment (on Earth). The electrical properties of the heaters were modelled using conductivity values of σCr = 1 × 106 S/m and σAl = 3 × 107 S/m for the heater materials. Additionally, the resistance at room temperature was 26.3 Ω for each pair of heaters 30,31 (Table 3).

The four incubation chambers are intended for cultivating microorganisms with specific temperature requirements: 25 °C for cyanobacteria, 30 °C for yeast, and 37 °C for Escherichia coli and mammalian cells. Also independent control of the four chambers is feasible. Simulations were conducted by setting all four chambers to the same temperature in three different scenarios: 25 °C, 30 °C, and 37 °C. First scenario: Targeting 25 °C.

1.9 Volts was applied to the heater, and the simulation results showed a highly uniform distribution, ranging from 24.64 °C to 25.16 °C. The thermographic images (Fig. 12) show an average temperature of 25.1 °C across all chambers, with a maximum recorded temperature of 25.3 °C and a minimum of 24.9 °C.

Temperature distribution on the four chip chambers experimentally measured through a FLIR thermocamera. The plots are ordered from top to bottom to display the outcomes of Scenarios 1 through 4, respectively.

These results confirm the heaters’ capability to maintain a stable temperature profile with a deviation of only ± 0.2 °C from the set point. The narrow temperature range (24.9 °C to 25.3 °C) highlights the heater’s effectiveness in achieving a low-temperature gradient across the device at this setting.

Second scenario: Targeting 30 °C.

2.7 Volts was applied to the heater. The system maintained uniform heating, with values ranging from 29.27 °C to 30.33 °C. When the chambers were set to a target temperature of 30 °C, the thermocamera data recorded an average temperature of 30.1 °C, with localized maximum and minimum temperatures of 30.5 °C and 29.8 °C, respectively.

This results in a slight deviation of ± 0.3 °C, slightly higher than at 25 °C, but still within acceptable limits for biological applications.

Third scenario: Targeting 37 °C.

3.6 Volts was applied to the heater, with values spanning from 35.76 °C to 37.55 °C. The uniformity of the temperature distribution remained consistent, with deviations still within acceptable limits for biological applications. The thermographic data reveals a more pronounced temperature range, with a maximum of 37.8 °C and a minimum of 36.7 °C, giving an average temperature of approximately 37.2 °C.

The deviation here is ± 0.5 °C, slightly larger than in the lower temperature scenarios, which can be attributed to higher thermal energy and heat transfer dynamics at elevated temperatures. Nevertheless, the results indicate that the LoC device maintains stability with a limited temperature gradient (36.7 °C to 37.8 °C), ensuring precise conditions for high-temperature biological experiments.

Fourth scenario: Targeting 25 °C and 37 °C.

4.56 Volts was applied to the heater. In this scenario, two chambers were set to 25, while the other two were set to 37 °C. The chambers set to 25 °C ranged from a minimum of 23.65 °C to a maximum of 26.87 °C. In the chambers set to 37 °C, the temperature spanned from 34.76 °C to 37.84 °C. The thermocamera data recorded an average temperature of 25.2 °C in the lower chambers, with a range from 24.8 °C to 25.4 °C, resulting in a deviation of ± 0.3 °C from the target of 25 °C. In the upper chambers set at 37 °C, the measured average was 37.3 °C, with temperatures ranging from 36.8 °C to 37.7 °C, yielding a deviation of ± 0.4 °C.

This simulation and experiment demonstrated that, although there was a slight reduction in temperature uniformity compared to previous simulations with uniform heating, the deviations in both sets of chambers remained within tolerable limits. These results confirm that the system can still function adequately when different temperatures are applied to adjacent chambers.

In summary, the thermal simulations and measurements validate the ALCYONE system’s robust thermal control, maintaining uniform temperature distributions within ± 0.5 °C across a wide temperature range. This performance affirms the device’s suitability for applications requiring precise temperature regulation, such as in-situ biological research under space conditions.

Preliminary biological validation

To provide a validation of our platform with biological samples, a set of experiments was conducted at ENEA laboratories using HEK293T cells expressing a luciferase (Luc2P) reporter system. Both 2D and 3D cell cultures were exposed to gamma radiation at different dose levels (0.2, 0.5, 1.5, and 3 Gy), and the resulting bioluminescence (BL) signal was measured using the ALCYONE setup and benchmarked against a commercial luminometer (GlowMax). Cells were cultured in L-15 medium, selected for its suitability in CO₂-independent conditions, and mixed with a luciferase assay reagent (Bright-Glo™) prior to measurement. The same protocol was applied to both the reference instrument and the ALCYONE system to ensure direct comparability of the acquired signals.

Figure 13 shows the BL signal measured in the ALCYONE chamber for 2D cell cultures exposed to 3 Gy of gamma radiation. A clear transient increase in the signal is observed, with a pronounced peak at 0.94 pA occurring within the first minutes of acquisition, followed by a gradual decay. The response is consistently detected across multiple sensing channels (S2 and S6), confirming the capability of the system to capture biologically induced luminescence signals within the microfluidic environment.

Bioluminescence (BL) signal measured by the ALCYONE platform for HEK293T 2D cell cultures exposed to 3 Gy gamma radiation. The signal shows a clear transient peak followed by a decay, consistently detected across multiple sensing channels.

The observed signal behaviour is in agreement with the expected biological response and with measurements obtained using the reference instrument, indicating that the ALCYONE platform is able to reliably detect radiation-induced cellular activity. These results demonstrate the feasibility of performing in-situ biological measurements directly within the microchamber, providing a first validation of the integrated sensing approach under realistic experimental conditions.

Conclusions

This study presents a novel LoC system optimized for real-time monitoring of different cell cultures in space environments, potentially providing new data for critical advancements for long-term deep space missions. The presented design integrates a-si:H photosensors and temperature sensors with resistive heating elements, enabling autonomous and precise control over environmental conditions, which is essential for maintaining cellular health. By addressing the challenges of fluid dynamics in microgravity through a tapered culture chamber and feedback-controlled pressure modulation, we have demonstrated effective fluid positioning and stability, paving the way for robust biological experimentation in space through a cell incubator with the presented design.

The modular fabrication approach, combining PMMA laser cutting, adhesive bonding, and thin-film integration, facilitates rapid prototyping and adaptation for various mission-specific requirements. Our successful characterization of the thermal and photosensors confirms the system’s potential for low-power, efficient, and high-sensitivity applications. The thermal simulations showed uniform heating and minimized cross-chamber thermal crosstalk, ensuring reliable temperature regulation, all confirmed by the thermocamera measurements. Ultimately, this LoC platform represents a promising, compact solution for studying space radiation effects on different biological systems, but its applications and some of the technological elements presented in this work can go even beyond this scope.
