General Coded Computing: Adversarial Settings
Parsa Moradi, Hanzaleh Akbarinodehi, Mohammad Ali Maddah-Ali

Abstract
Conventional coded computing frameworks are predominantly tailored for structured computations, such as matrix multiplication and polynomial evaluation. Such tasks allow the reuse of tools and techniques from algebraic coding theory to improve the reliability of distributed systems in the presence of stragglers and adversarial servers. This paper lays the foundation for general coded computing, which extends the applicability of coded computing to handle a wide class of computations. In addition, it particularly addresses the challenging problem of managing adversarial servers. We demonstrate that, in the proposed scheme, for a system with N N servers, where 𝒪 ⁡ ( N a ) \mathcal{O}(N^{a}) , a ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) a\in[0,1) , are adversarial, the supremum of the average approximation error over all adversarial strategies decays at a rate of N 6 5 ​ ( a − 1 ) N^{\frac{6}{5}(a-1)} , under minimal assumptions on the computing tasks. Furthermore, we show that within a general framework, the proposed scheme achieves optimal adversarial robustness, in terms of maximum number of adversarial servers it can tolerate. This marks a significant step toward practical and reliable general coded computing. Implementation results further validate the effectiveness of the proposed method in handling various computations, including inference in deep neural networks.

Besides its limited capacity to handle a wide range of computation tasks, conventional coded computing has several additional shortcomings.
These include numerical instability when performing computations over real numbers, inefficiency in handling or leveraging approximate computing, and high
recovery thresholds
, which require a large number of non-failed or non-straggling servers for successful computation. Extensive research efforts have been undertaken to address these challenges, with each approach aiming to resolve specific issues

. However, to effectively address these challenges, it is necessary to revisit the concept of coded computing, starting with its foundational design principles.

In this paper, we lay the foundation of
General Coded Computing
, which is not solely rooted in the principles of error correction coding but also draws on concepts from approximation and learning theory. The effectiveness of this new approach is validated through numerical evaluations and theoretical guarantees. Our focus is particularly on addressing adversarial behavior, which serves as a significant complement to our team’s earlier efforts in mitigating the effects of stragglers for general computations
[
1
,
6
]
. This task is especially challenging, as managing adversarial behavior has not been sufficiently addressed within the areas of approximation and learning theory.

Consider the task of computing
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\ldots,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{K})
, for some general function
\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[-M,M]^{m}
, and input data points
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
K,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
M\in\mathbf{R}_{+}
. The master node embeds the input data points into an encoding function

\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(.)
, and distributes the samples of
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(.)
, as the coded inputs, to worker nodes, which compute

\mathbf{f}(.)
of these coded symbols. Some of these servers are adversaries. At the decoding side, we recover a decoding function
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{d}}(.)
from the results of the worker nodes. However, instead of selecting the encoder and decoder from polynomials, like Reed-Solomon and Lagrange coding,
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(.)
and
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{d}}(.)
belongs to the set of functions with bounded first and second-order derivatives, i.e., the Second-order Sobolev spaces. We show that while such a smoothness condition is sufficiently restrictive to introduce redundancy in the coded symbols and mitigate the impact of adversarial worker nodes, it remains flexible enough to accommodate general computation tasks, with minimal assumptions. We use the properties of Sobolev spaces, as Reproducing Hilbert Kernel Spaces (RHKS), to efficiently form the encoding and decoding functions. We prove that in a system with
N
worker nodes, where
\mathcal{O}(N^{a})
,
a\in[0,1)
, are adversarial, the maximum approximation error of the proposed scheme, taken over all possible adversarial actions, decays at a rate of
N^{\frac{6}{5}(a-1)}
, under the mild assumption that the computing function belongs to the second-order Sobolev space. Additionally, we prove that within this framework, the proposed scheme is optimal in the sense that no other scheme can tolerate a greater number of adversarial worker nodes. Implementation results further validate the effectiveness of the proposed method in handling complex computations, such as inference in deep neural networks.

II
Problem Formulation

Let
\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[-M,M]^{m}
, be a function where
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\triangleq[f_{1}(\mathbf{x}),f_{2}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,f_{m}(\mathbf{x})]
, and
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[-M,M]
for some
m,d\in\mathbb{N}
,
M\in\mathbb{R}
, and
i\in[m]
. The function
\mathbf{f}(\cdot)
can represent an arbitrary function, ranging from a simple scalar function to a complex deep neural network.

Consider a system consisting of a master node and
N\in\mathbb{N}
worker nodes. The master node is interested in the approximate results of computing
\{\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
in a distributed setting, leveraging the set of
N
worker nodes, for
K\in\mathbb{N}
data points
\{\mathbf{x}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, where each
\mathbf{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The set of worker nodes is partitioned into two sets: (1) the set of
honest nodes
, denoted by
\mathcal{D}
, which strictly execute any assigned task, (ii) the set of
adversarial nodes
, denoted by
\mathcal{B}
, which produce arbitrary outputs. We assume that
|\mathcal{B}|\leq\gamma
, for some
\gamma\leq N
.
We assume that all adversarial worker nodes are controlled by a single entity, referred to as the
adversary
.
Throughout this paper, we adopt the following three-step framework for coded computing:

1.

Encoding:
The master node forms an encoding function
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
\mathbf{x}_{k}\approx\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(\alpha_{k})
, where
\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\dots<\alpha_{K}\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
are fixed and predefined constants. The accuracy of the above approximation is determined during the encoder design process, as will be described later. It then evaluates
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(\cdot)
on another set of fixed points
\{\beta_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, where
\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}<\dots<\beta_{N}\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
. For each
n\in[N]
, the master node computes the coded data point
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n}=\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(\beta_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and sends it to worker node
n
,
n\in[N]
. Note that each coded data point is a combination of all the original points
\{\mathbf{x}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
.

2.

Computing:
Every honest worker node
n\in\mathcal{D}
computes
\mathbf{f}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n})=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(\beta_{n}))
and sends it back to the master node. On the other hand, an adversarial worker node
n\in\mathcal{B}
may send arbitrary values, still within the acceptable range
[-M,M]^{m}
in an attempt to avoid detection and exclusion.
Thus, in summary, worker node
n
sends
\overline{\mathbf{y}}_{n}
to the master node as follows:

\overline{\mathbf{y}}_{n}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{f}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n})&\text{if }n\in\mathcal{D},\\
\star\in[-M,M]^{m}&\text{if }n\in\mathcal{B}.\end{cases}

3.

Decoding:
Upon receiving the results from the worker nodes, the master node forms a decoder function
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{d}}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, such that
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{d}}(\beta_{n})\approx\overline{\mathbf{y}}_{n}
, for
n\in\mathcal{D}
. Of course, the master node is unaware of the set
\mathcal{D}
, which is one of the reasons that determining
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{d}}
is challenging.
It then computes
\hat{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}_{k}):=\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{d}}(\alpha_{k})
as an approximation of
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
for
k\in[K]
. If the master node can effectively mitigate the influence of adversarial worker nodes, it expects the approximation
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{d}}(\alpha_{k})\approx\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(\alpha_{k}))\approx\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
to hold.

Recall that
|\mathcal{B}|\leqslant\gamma
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}
denote the set of all possible strategies that the adversary can employ. The adversary chooses its attack to maximize the approximation error of the computation, leveraging full knowledge of the input data points, the computation function
\mathbf{f}(\cdot)
,
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
,
\{\beta_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, and and the scheme used to design the encoder and decoder functions
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(\cdot)
and
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{d}}(\cdot)
. The effectiveness of the proposed coded computing scheme is evaluated using the
average approximation error
, defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\hat{f}}):=\mathop{\sup}_{\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}}\frac{1}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{f}}(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\right\rVert^{2}_{2}.

(1)

The objective is then to find
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}\left(\Omega\right)
and
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{d}}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}_{m}\left(\Omega\right)
such that minimize
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\hat{f}})
in (
1
). The proposed approach raises several important questions:
(i) Does constraining the encoder and decoder to second-order Sobolev spaces (i.e., functions with bounded first and second derivative norms) impose sufficient restrictions to ensure robustness of the distributed system against adversarial attacks? (ii) If so, what is the maximum number of adversaries the scheme can tolerate while maintaining its effectiveness?
(iii) How fast does the average estimation error converge to zero as a function of
N
and
\gamma
?

We note that minimizing (
1
), subject to
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}\left(\Omega\right)
and
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{d}}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}_{m}\left(\Omega\right)
, is a challenging task for several reasons. First, the assumptions on the function
\mathbf{f}(\cdot)
are minimal; we only require that
\mathbf{f}(\cdot)
has bounded first and second derivative norms.
Second, designing these functions involves addressing the complexities of infinite-dimensional optimization, as both the encoder and decoder are elements of Sobolev spaces. Third, optimizing the encoder and decoder cannot be treated independently. The objective function
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\hat{f}})
in (
1
) arises from the composition of three functions: the encoding function, the computation task, and the decoding function, necessitating a joint optimization approach.
Lastly, the supremum in (
1
) is taken over
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}
, representing all possible adversarial behaviors. This requirement to optimize against worst-case adversarial strategies significantly increases the complexity of the problem.

III
Proposed Scheme

Considering the difficulty of optimizing
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\hat{f}})
in (
1
), we first establish an upper bound for
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\hat{f}})
as the sum of two terms:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\hat{f}})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(a)}}}{{=}}\sup_{\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}}\frac{1}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\|\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{d}}(\alpha_{k})-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(\alpha_{k}))+\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(\alpha_{k}))-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(b)}}}{{\leqslant}}\underbrace{\mathop{\sup}_{\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}}\frac{2}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{d}}\left(\alpha_{k}\right)-\mathbf{f}\left(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}\left(\alpha_{k}\right)\right)\right\rVert^{2}_{2}}_{\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{dec}}(\hat{\mathbf{f}})}+\underbrace{\frac{2}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\lVert\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(\alpha_{k}))-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\right\rVert^{2}_{2}}_{\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{enc}}(\hat{\mathbf{f}})},

(2)

where (a) follows from adding and subtracting the term
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(\alpha_{k}))
, and (b) is due to the AM-GM inequality.

The first term in (
2
),
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{dec}}(\mathbf{\hat{f}})
, represents the error of the decoder function in predicting the target function
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(\cdot))
at the points
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, which differ from its training points
\{\beta_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, for which the master node has partial access (except from adversarial inputs) to the value of
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(\cdot))
.
Thus, in the terminology of learning theory, it would represents the generalization error of the decoder function.
In contrast, the second term,
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{enc}}(\mathbf{\hat{f}})
, serves as a proxy for the encoder’s training error, as it captures the encoder’s error on
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, after passing through
\mathbf{f}(\cdot)
. These two terms are not independent, as the target function of the decoder is
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}(\cdot))
, which depends on the encoder function. Consequently, (
2
) reveals an inherent interplay between the encoder and decoder functions, further complicating the optimization of (
1
).

III-A

Decoder Design

Recall that it is important for the decoder function to achieve both a low generalization error and high robustness to adversarial inputs. Therefore, we propose to find
\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{d}}
, using the following regularized optimization:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{d}}=\underset{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}_{m}\left(\Omega\right)}{\operatorname{argmin}}\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{n=1}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}\left(\beta_{n}\right)-\mathbf{\overline{y}}_{n}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{\textrm{d}}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert^{2}_{{L}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)},

(3)

where
\left\lVert\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert_{{L}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}
denotes the
\ell_{2}
norm of the vector-valued function
\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(\cdot)
. The first term in (
3
) represents the mean squared error, which
controls the accuracy
of the decoder function. The second term serves as a regularization term, promoting smoothness of the decoder function over the interval
\Omega
and thereby
controlling generalization and robustness
. The parameter
\lambda_{\textrm{d}}
, known as the smoothing parameter, determines the relative weighting of these two terms.

Using the representer theorem
[
22
]
, it can be shown that the optimal decoder
\mathbf{u}^{*}(\cdot):=[u_{1}^{*}(\cdot),\dots,u_{m}^{*}(\cdot)]
in (
3
) can be expressed such that each element
u_{i}^{*}(\cdot)
is a linear combination of the basis functions
\{\phi(\cdot,\beta_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N}
, where
\phi(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the kernel function associated with
\mathcal{H}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)
.
The solution to (
3
) belongs to a class of spline functions known as
second-order smoothing splines

[
23
,
24
]
. The coefficient vectors for
\mathbf{u}^{*}_{i}
, corresponding to
\{\phi(\cdot,\beta_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N}
, can be efficiently computed in
\mathcal{O}(Nm)
by utilizing B-spline basis functions
[
24
,
25
]
.

In practice, the hyper-parameter
\lambda_{\textrm{d}}
in (
3
) is typically determined using cross-validation
[
23
,
24
]
. However, we perform a theoretical analysis to determine the optimal value of
\lambda_{\textrm{d}}
that minimizes an upper bound for (
1
). Specifically, we show that if the maximum number of adversarial worker nodes is
\mathcal{O}(N^{a})
with
a\in[0,1)
, then the optimal value of
\lambda_{\textrm{d}}
is
\mathcal{O}(N^{\frac{8}{5}(a-1)})
.

III-B

Encoder Design

We will show that
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{dec}}
can be bounded above by an expression, which can serve as a regularization term in searching for the encoder function (see Theorem
3
). Consequently, the encoder function can be determined by the following optimization:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{e}}=\underset{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}\left(\Omega\right)}{\operatorname{argmin}}\frac{C}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}\left(\alpha_{k}\right)-\mathbf{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}+\lambda_{\textrm{e}}\psi\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{u}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}\left(\Omega\right)}\right),

where where
\lambda_{\textrm{e}}
is a function of
(\lambda_{\textrm{d}},N,M,\gamma)
,
C
depends on the Lipschitz constant of the computing function, and
\psi(\cdot)
is a monotonically increasing function in
[0,\infty)
. Applying the representer theorem
[
22
]
, one can conclude that, similar to the decoder function, the encoder function can also be represented as a linear combination of
\{\phi(\cdot,\alpha_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
.

IV
Theoretical Guarantees

In this section, we analyze the proposed scheme from theoretical viewpoint. For simplicity of exposition, in this conference paper, we focus on one-dimensional case in which
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Also, we assume that
\Omega=[0,1].

First, we present the following theorem, which demonstrates that if
\gamma=\mathcal{O}(N)
, no encoder and decoder functions can achieve adversarial robustness:

Theorem 1
.

(Impossibility Result)

In the proposed coded computing framework, assume
\gamma=\mu N
for some
\mu\in(0,1)
. Then, there exists some
f
with bounded first and second derivative, for which there is no
u_{\textrm{e}},u_{\textrm{d}}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)
such that:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{R}(\hat{f})=0.

Next, we demonstrate that the proposed scheme achieves adversarial robustness when
\gamma=o(N)
. More precisely, the next theorem establishes an upper bound for the approximation error as a function of
\lambda_{\textrm{d}}
,
N
,
M
, and
\gamma
, providing insights into the adversarial robustness of the proposed scheme. Subsequently, we utilize this theorem to design the encoder.

Theorem 2
.

Consider the proposed coded computing scheme with
N
worker nodes and
at most
\gamma
colluding worker nodes. Assume decoder points are equidistant, i.e.
\beta_{i}=\frac{i}{N}
for
i\in[N]
and
C_{\lambda}N^{-4}<\lambda_{\textrm{d}}\leqslant 1
for constant
C_{\lambda}>0
. If
\left\lVert f^{\prime}\right\rVert_{{L}^{\infty}\left(\Omega\right)}\leqslant\nu
and
\left\lVert f^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert_{{L}^{\infty}\left(\Omega\right)}\leqslant\eta
, then:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\hat{f})\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{1}\frac{M^{2}\gamma^{2}}{N^{4}}+C_{2}\frac{M^{2}\gamma^{2}}{N^{2}}\lambda_{\textrm{d}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(e^{\left({\sqrt{2}}\lambda_{\textrm{d}}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\right)}+C_{3}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(C_{4}\lambda_{\textrm{d}}^{\frac{3}{4}}+C_{5}N^{-3}\right)\left\lVert(f\circ u_{\textrm{e}})^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert^{2}_{{L}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}

\displaystyle+\frac{2\nu^{2}}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}(u_{\textrm{e}}(\alpha_{k})-x_{k})^{2},

(4)

where
C_{i}
s are constants.

Theorem
2
holds for all
u_{\textrm{e}}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)
. However, these bounds do not directly offer a method for designing
u_{\textrm{e}}(\cdot)
, mainly because of the inherent complexity of the composition function
f\circ u_{\textrm{e}}
. Inspired by
[
6
]
, the following theorem establishes an upper bound for the approximation error in terms of
\left\lVert u_{\textrm{e}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}
.

Theorem 3
.

Consider the proposed scheme. Suppose that the computing function satisfies
\left\lVert f^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert_{{L}^{\infty}\left(\Omega\right)}\leqslant\eta,\left\lVert f^{\prime}\right\rVert_{{L}^{\infty}\left(\Omega\right)}\leqslant\nu
. Then, there exists
\lambda_{\textrm{e}}>0
, which depends on
(\lambda_{\textrm{d}},N,M,\gamma,\nu,\eta)
, and a monotonically increasing function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
, independent of the scheme’s parameters, such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\hat{f})\leqslant\frac{2\nu^{2}}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}(u_{\textrm{e}}(\alpha_{k})-x_{k})^{2}+\lambda_{\textrm{e}}\cdot\psi\left(\left\lVert u_{\textrm{e}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}\right).

(5)

The above theorem provides the foundation for the proposed approach outlined in Section
III-B
to design the encoder function. Finally, the following corollary demonstrate the convergence rate of the proposed scheme:

Corollary 1
.

(Convergence Rate)

Let
\gamma=\mathcal{O}(N^{a})
for
a\in[0,1)
and
\lambda_{\textrm{d}}=\mathcal{O}(N^{\frac{8}{5}(a-1)})
. Then, the average approximation error
\mathcal{R}(\hat{f})
of the proposed scheme converges to zero at a rate of at least

\mathcal{O}(N^{\frac{6}{5}(a-1)})
.

Remark 1
.

(
Optimality)
According to Theorem
1
and Corollary
1
, the proposed scheme achieves optimal robustness in terms of the number of adversarial worker nodes.

Finally, the following theorem states that, under mild assumptions on
\left\lVert u_{\textrm{e}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}
, an upper bound for the approximation error can be established, leading us to the design of the encoder function:

Theorem 4
.

Consider the proposed scheme under the same assumptions as Theorems
2
and
3
. There exists
E>0
, depending only on the encoder regression points
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and the input data points
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, such that:

(i)

If
\left\lVert u_{\textrm{e}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}\leqslant E
, then:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\hat{f})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2\nu^{2}}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}(u_{\textrm{e}}(\alpha_{k})-x_{k})^{2}+\lambda_{\textrm{e}}\left(D_{1}(E)+D_{2}(E)\int_{\Omega}\left(u_{\textrm{e}}^{\prime\prime}(t)\right)^{2}\,dt\right),

(6)

where
D_{1}(E)
and
D_{2}(E)
are functions of
E
.

(ii)

If
u_{e}^{*}(\cdot)
is the minimizer of (
6
), then
\left\lVert u_{e}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}\leqslant E
.

This result demonstrates that the optimal encoder function, which minimizes the upper bound in Theorem
4
, is a second-order smoothing spline, offering higher computational efficiency.

V
Experimental Results

In this section, we evaluate the performance of the proposed scheme in different adversarial settings. Two types of computing functions are considered: a one-dimensional function
f_{1}(x)=x\sin(x)
and the LeNet5 neural network
[
26
]
, a high-dimensional function
\mathbf{f}_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{1024}\to\mathbb{R}^{10}
trained for handwritten image classification.
Equidistant points are used for both the encoder and decoder. The scheme’s approximation error is quantified as empirical approximation of
\mathrm{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}}[\mathcal{R}(\hat{\mathbf{f}})]
, averaged over 20 repetitions for each
N
.

The adversary’s strategy is defined as modifying the values of
f(u_{\textrm{e}}(\beta_{i}))
for all
\beta_{i}
near
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. Specifically, the adversary changes
\frac{\gamma}{K}
values around each
\alpha_{i}
for
i\in[K]
to maximum acceptable value
M
.
As shown in Figure
1
, for
\gamma=N^{0.8}
, the average approximation error for LeNet5 network converges at a rate of
N^{-0.35}
, which is smaller than the theoretical upper bound of
N^{-0.24}
given by Corollary
1
. Similarly, for
\gamma=N^{0.5}
, the approximation error converges at rates of
N^{-0.85}
for
f_{1}(x)
and
N^{-1.35}
for the LeNet5 network, both smaller than the theoretical upper bound of
N^{-0.6}
. Additionally, a rate of
N^{-1.39}
is achieved with
\gamma=50
and
f_{1}(x)
, with a theoretical upper bound of
N^{-1.2}
.

Proof Sketch

Therefore, we assume that

Let

P(x)&\text{if }x\in[\alpha_{\text{min}},\alpha_{\text{max}}].\end{cases}

Appendix A
Preliminaries: Sobolev spaces and Sobolev norms

Let
\Omega
be an open interval in
\mathbb{R}
and
M
be a positive integer. We denote by
{L}^{p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)
the class of all measurable functions
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
that satisfy:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|g_{j}(t)|^{p}\,dt\ <\infty,\quad\forall j\in[M],

(11)

where
g(\cdot)=[g_{1}(\cdot),\dots,g_{M}(\cdot)]^{T}
. The space
{L}^{p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)
can be endowed with the following norm, known as the
{L}^{p}
norm:

\displaystyle\|g\|_{{L}^{p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)}:=\left(\sum^{M}_{j=1}\int_{\Omega}|g_{i}(t)|^{p}\,dt\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

(12)

for
1\leqslant p<\infty
, and

\displaystyle\|g\|_{{L}^{\infty}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)}:=\max_{j\in[M]}\sup_{t\in\Omega}|g_{j}(t)|.

(13)

for
p=\infty
. Additionally, a function
g:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
is in
{L}_{\textrm{loc}}^{p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)
if it lies in
{L}^{p}(V;\mathbb{R}^{M})
for all compact subsets
V\subseteq\Omega
.

Definition 1

(
Sobolev Space
)
.

The
Sobolev space

\mathbb{W}^{m,p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)
is the space of all functions
g\in{L}^{p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)
such that all weak derivatives of order
i
, denoted by
g^{(i)}
, belong to
{L}^{p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)
for
i\in[m]
. This space is endowed with the norm:

\displaystyle\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathbb{W}^{m,p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)}:=\left(\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{{L}^{p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)}^{p}+\sum^{m}_{i=1}\left\lVert g^{(i)}\right\rVert_{{L}^{p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

(14)

for
1\leqslant p<\infty
, and

\displaystyle\|g\|_{\mathbb{W}^{m,\infty}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)}:=\max\left\{\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{{L}^{\infty}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)},\max_{i\in[m]}\left\lVert g^{(i)}\right\rVert_{{L}^{\infty}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)}\right\},

(15)

for
p=\infty
.

Similarly,
\mathbb{W}_{\textrm{loc}}^{m,p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)
is defined as the space of all functions
g\in{L}_{\textrm{loc}}^{p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)
with all weak derivatives of order
i
belonging to
{L}_{\textrm{loc}}^{p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)
for
i\in[m]
.
\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathbb{W}_{\textrm{loc}}^{m,p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)}
and
\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathbb{W}_{\textrm{loc}}^{m,\infty}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)}
are defined similar to (
14
) and (
17
) respectively, using
{L}_{\textrm{loc}}^{p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)
instead of
{L}^{p}\left(\Omega\right)
.

Definition 2
.

(
Sobolev Space with compact support
): Denoted by
\mathbb{W}_{0}^{m,p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)
collection of functions
g
defined on interval
\Omega=(a,b)
such that
g(a)=\mathbf{0},g^{\prime}(a)=\mathbf{0},\dots,g^{(m-1)}(a)=\mathbf{0}
and
\left\lVert g^{(m)}\right\rVert_{{L}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}<\infty
. This space can be endowed with the following norm:

\displaystyle\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathbb{W}^{m,p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)}:=\left\lVert g^{(m)}\right\rVert_{{L}^{p}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)},

(16)

for
1\leqslant p<\infty
, and

\displaystyle\|g\|_{\mathbb{W}^{m,\infty}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)}:=\left\lVert g^{(m)}\right\rVert_{{L}^{\infty}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)},

(17)

for
p=\infty
.

The next theorem provides an upper bound for
{L}^{p}
norm of functions in the Sobolev space, which plays a crucial role in the proof of the main theorems of the paper.

(Theorem 7.34,
[
31
]

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{W}^{m,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{m}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)

\displaystyle:=\widetilde{\mathbb{W}}^{m,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}^{m}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{W}_{0}^{m,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}\right),

(26)

are Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHSs).

The full expression of the kernel function of
\mathcal{H}^{m}\left(\Omega\right)
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{m}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)
and other equivalent norms of Sobolev spaces can be found in
[
33
, Section 4]
. For
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{m}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)
the kernel function is as follows:

\displaystyle R(t,s)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{t^{j}s^{j}}{j!^{2}}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{(t-x)_{+}^{m-1}(s-x)_{+}^{m-1}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\,dx,

(27)

where
(\cdot)_{+}
is positive part function.

Appendix B
Smoothing Splines

Consider the data model
y_{i}=f(t_{i})+\epsilon_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
, where
t_{i}\in\Omega=(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{i}]=0
, and
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{i}^{2}]\leqslant\sigma_{0}^{2}
.
Assuming
f\in\widetilde{\mathbb{W}}^{m,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)
, the solution to the following optimization problem is referred to as the smoothing spline:

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{\lambda,n,m}[\mathbf{y}]:=\underset{g\in\widetilde{\mathbb{W}}^{m,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)}{\operatorname{argmin}}\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\left(g\left(t_{i}\right)-y_{i}\right)^{2}+\lambda\int_{\Omega}\left(g^{(m)}(t)\right)^{2}\,dt,

(28)

where
\mathbf{y}=[y_{1},\dots,y_{n}]
. Based on Proposition
1
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{m}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right):=\widetilde{\mathbb{W}}^{m,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)
with the norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\widetilde{\mathbb{W}}^{m,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)}
is a RKHS for some kernel function
\phi(\cdot,\cdot)
. Therefore for any
v\in\widetilde{\mathbb{W}}^{m,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)
, we have:

\displaystyle v(t)=\langle v(\cdot),\phi(\cdot,t)\rangle_{\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{m}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)}.

(29)

It can be shown that
\phi(t,s)=R^{P}(t,s)+\phi_{0}(t,s)
where
\phi_{0}(t,s)
is kernel function of
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{m}\left(\Omega\right)
and
R^{P}(t,s)
is a null space of
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{m}\left(\Omega\right)
which is the space of all polynomials with degree less than
m
.

The solution of (
28
) has the following form
[
24
,
35
]
:

\displaystyle u^{*}(\cdot)=\sum^{m}_{i=1}d_{i}\zeta_{i}(\cdot)+\sum^{n}_{j=1}c_{j}\phi_{0}(\cdot,t_{j}),

(30)

where
\left\{\zeta_{i}(\cdot)\right\}_{i=1}^{m}
are the basis functions of the space of polynomials of degree at most
m-1
. Substituting
u^{*}
into (
28
) and optimizing over
\mathbf{c}=[c_{1},\dots,c_{n}]^{T}
and
\mathbf{d}=[d_{1},\dots,d_{m}]^{T}
, we obtain the following result
[
24
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{\lambda,n,m}[\mathbf{y}](\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Q}\left(\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{Q}+\lambda\mathbf{\Gamma}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{y},

(31)

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{P}^{T}\mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{P}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{T}\mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{y},

(32)

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}\left(\mathbf{P}^{T}\mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{P}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{T}\mathbf{L}^{-1}\right)\mathbf{y},

(33)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{n\times(n+m)}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{P}_{n\times m}&\bm{\Sigma}_{n\times n}\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Gamma}_{(n+m)\times(n+m)}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{0}_{m\times m}&\mathbf{0}_{m\times n}\\
\mathbf{0}_{n\times 1}&\bm{\Sigma}_{n\times n}\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle\bm{P}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\zeta_{j}(t_{i}),

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}(t_{i},t_{j}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Sigma}+n\lambda\mathbf{I}.

(36)

Equation (
31
) states that the smoothing spline fitted on the data points
\mathbf{y}
is a linear operator:

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{\lambda,n,m}[\mathbf{y}](\mathbf{z}):=\mathbf{A_{\lambda}}\mathbf{z},

(37)

for
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\mathbf{A_{\lambda}}:=\mathbf{Q}\left(\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{Q}+\lambda\mathbf{\Gamma}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Q}^{T}
.

To characterize the estimation error of the smoothing spline,
|f-\mathbf{S}_{\lambda,n,m}(\mathbf{y})|
, we need to define two variables quantify the minimum and maximum consecutive distance of the regression points
\{t_{i}\}^{n}_{i=1}
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{max}}:=\underset{i\in\{0\}\cup[n]}{\max}\left\{t_{i+1}-t_{i}\right\},\quad\Delta_{\textrm{min}}:=\underset{i\in[n-1]}{\min}\left\{t_{i+1}-t_{i}\right\},

(38)

where boundary points are defined as
(t_{0},t_{n+1}):=(a,b)
. The following theorem offers an upper bound for the
j
-th derivative of the smoothing spline estimator error function in the absence of noise (
\sigma_{0}=0
).

Theorem 6
.

(
[
36
, Theorem 4.10]
)
Consider data model
y_{i}=f(t_{i})
with
\{t_{i}\}^{n}_{i=1}
belong to
\Omega=[a,b]
for
i\in[n]
. Let

\displaystyle L=p_{2(m-1)}(\frac{\Delta_{\textrm{max}}}{\Delta_{\textrm{min}}})\cdot\frac{n\Delta_{\textrm{max}}}{b-a}\frac{\lambda}{2}+D(m)\cdot\left({\Delta_{\textrm{max}}}\right)^{2m},

(39)

where
p_{d}(\cdot)
is a degree
d
polynomial with positive weights and
D(m)
is a function of
m
. Then for each
j\in\{0,1,\dots,m\}
and any
f\in\mathbb{W}^{m,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)
, there exist a function
H(m,j)
such that:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(f-\mathbf{S}_{\lambda,n,m}(\mathbf{y})\right)^{(j)}\right\rVert_{{L}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}\leqslant H(m,j)\left(1+\left(\frac{L}{(b-a)^{2m}}\right)\right)^{\frac{j}{m}}\cdot L^{\frac{(m-j)}{m}}\cdot\left\lVert f^{(m)}\right\rVert^{2}_{{L}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}.

(40)

Note that
\left(f-\mathbf{S}_{\lambda,n,m}(\mathbf{y})\right)^{(0)}:=f-\mathbf{S}_{\lambda,n,m}(\mathbf{y})
.

Substituting (
33
) and (
32
) into (
30
), one can conclude that:

\displaystyle u^{*}(x)=\bm{\zeta}(x)^{T}\mathbf{M_{1}}\mathbf{y}+\bm{\phi_{0}}(x)^{T}\mathbf{M_{2}}\mathbf{y},

(41)

where
\bm{\phi_{0}}(x):=[\phi_{0}(x,t_{1})\dots\phi_{0}(x,t_{n})]^{T}
,
\bm{\zeta}(x):=[\zeta_{1}(x)\dots\zeta_{m}(x)]^{T}
, and matrices
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are defined as
\mathbf{M_{1}}:=\left(\mathbf{P}^{T}\mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{P}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{T}\mathbf{L}^{-1}
and
\mathbf{M_{2}}\,:=\,\mathbf{L}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}\left(\mathbf{P}^{T}\mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{P}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{T}\mathbf{L}^{-1}\right)
. Thus, there exists a weight function, denoted by
G_{n,\lambda}(x,t)
, such that:

\displaystyle u^{*}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{n,\lambda}(x,t_{i})y_{i},

(42)

where
G_{n,\lambda}(x,t)
depends on the regression points
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and the smoothing parameter
\lambda
.

Obtaining an explicit expression for
G_{n,\lambda}(x,t)
is challenging due to the complexity of the inverse matrix operation and the kernel of
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{1}\left(\Omega\right)
in general cases. However, it has been shown that the weight function
G_{n,\lambda}(x,t)
can be approximated by a well-defined kernel function
K_{\lambda}(x,t)
under certain assumptions about the regression points
{t_{i}}_{i=1}^{n}

[
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
.

In an early work,
[
27
]
proposed the following kernel function:

\displaystyle\kappa(u)=\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(\frac{-|u|}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\sin\left(\frac{|u|}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right).

(43)

[
27
]
demonstrated that for
t
sufficiently far from the boundary, the following holds:

\displaystyle\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}q(t)^{-\frac{1}{4}}G_{n,\lambda}\left(t+\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}q(t)^{-\frac{1}{4}}x,t\right)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\frac{\kappa(x)}{q(t)},

(44)

where
q(t)
represents the asymptotic probability density function of the regression points.

According to (
44
), as
n
increases, the smoothing spline behaves like a kernel smoother with a bandwidth of
\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}q(t)^{-\frac{1}{4}}
. However, this approximation was later found to be insufficient for establishing certain asymptotic properties
[
28
]
.

Consider the continuous formulation of the smoothing spline objective over the domain
\Omega=[0,1]
:

\displaystyle g^{*}(\cdot):=\underset{g\in\widetilde{\mathbb{W}}^{m,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)}{\operatorname{argmin}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(f(t)-g(t)\right)^{2},dt+\lambda\int_{0}^{1}\left(g^{\prime\prime}(t)\right)^{2},dt.

(45)

It can be shown that the solution to (
45
) is characterized by a unique Green’s function
G_{\lambda}(x,t)
, satisfying:

\displaystyle h(x):=\int_{0}^{1}G_{\lambda}(x,t)f(t),dt.

(46)

[
29
]
proposed an alternative approximated kernel function for equidistant regression points, i.e.
t_{i}=\frac{i}{n}
, which addresses the asymptotic issues of (
44
):

\displaystyle K_{\lambda}(x,t)=

\displaystyle\left(2\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)^{-1}e^{-\frac{|x-t|}{\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}}}\left(\sin\frac{|x-t|}{\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}}+\cos\left(\frac{x-t}{\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\left(2\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)^{-1}\left(\Phi\left(\frac{x+t}{\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}},\frac{x-t}{\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}}\right)+\Phi\left(\frac{1-x}{\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}}+\frac{1-t}{\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}},\frac{1-x}{\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}}-\frac{1-t}{\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}}\right)\right),

(47)

where,
\Phi(u,v):=e^{-u}\left(\cos(u)-\sin(u)+2\cos(v)\right)
. Note that the absolute values of the kernels in (
43
) and (
47
) decay exponentially to zero as the distance
|t-z|
increases. The bandwidth of the kernels are
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}})
.

Lemma 3
.

For the the kernel function proposed in (
47
), there exist a constant
\tau
such that:

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq x,t\leq 1}\left|K_{\lambda}(x,t)\right|\leqslant\tau\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}.

(48)

Proof.

From (
47
), and using the bounds
|\sin(x)|\leqslant 1
and
|\cos(x)|\leqslant 1
, we obtain:

\displaystyle K_{\lambda}(x,t)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)^{-1}e^{-\frac{|x-t|}{\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}}}+4\left(\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)^{-1}\left(e^{-\frac{(x+t)}{\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}}}+e^{-\frac{(2-x-t)}{\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}}}\right)\

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)^{-1}+8\left(\sqrt{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)^{-1}\

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{9}{\sqrt{2}}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}.

∎

The following lemma that characterize the relationship between their proposed kernel
K_{\lambda}(x,t)
and continuous Green function
G_{\lambda}(x,t)
:

Lemma 4
.

[
29
, Theorem 4.1]

For
0<\lambda\leqslant 1
, there exists constant
C
such that:

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq x,t\leq 1}\left|K_{\lambda}(x,t)-G_{\lambda}(x,t)\right|\leq C\frac{e^{-\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}\right)}}{\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}}.

(49)

According to Lemma
4
, if
\lambda\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, the proposed kernel converges exponentially to the true Green’s function.
Moreover, with an appropriate choice of
\lambda
, in the case of uniform regression points, it can be ensured that the weight function
G_{n,\lambda}(x,t)
defined in (
42
) converges to the Green’s function
G_{\lambda}(x,t)
:

Lemma 5
.

[
30
, Theorem 2.1]
For uniformly distributed regression points in
[0,1]
, if
n\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}>C_{0}
for some constant
C_{0}>0
, then there exist positive constants
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
such that:

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq x,t\leq 1}\left|G_{n,\lambda}(x,t)-\mathrm{G}_{\lambda}(x,t)\right|<\frac{\mu_{1}n^{-1}}{1-\mu_{1}n^{-1}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}}\mathrm{e}^{-{\mu_{2}\left|t-x\right|}{\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}}}.

(50)

We now present our main lemma, which characterizes the relationship between the weight function
G_{n,\lambda}(x,t)
and the approximated kernel
K_{\lambda}(x,t)
:

Lemma 6
.

For uniformly distributed regression points in
[0,1]
, if
n\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}>C_{0}
for some constant
C_{0}>0
and
0<\lambda\leqslant 1
, then there exist positive constants
\mu_{3}
and
\mu_{4}
depending on
C_{0}
, such that:

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq x,t\leq 1}\left|G_{n,\lambda}(x,t)-K_{\lambda}(x,t)\right|<\frac{\mu_{3}}{n}e^{\left(-\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}|x-t|\right)}+\mu_{4}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}e^{\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}\right)}.

(51)

Proof.

Lemma
6
can directly derive from Lemma
4
and Lemma
5
and using the fact that:

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq x,t\leq 1}\left|G_{n,\lambda}(x,t)-K_{\lambda}(x,t)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{0\leq x,t\leq 1}\left|G_{n,\lambda}(x,t)-G_{\lambda}(x,t)|+|G_{\lambda}(x,t)+K_{\lambda}(x,t)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{0\leq x,t\leq 1}|G_{n,\lambda}(x,t)-G_{\lambda}(x,t)|+\sup_{0\leq x,t\leq 1}|G_{\lambda}(x,t)+K_{\lambda}(x,t)|

(52)

∎

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem
2

Recall from (
2
) and (
10
) that
\mathcal{R}(\hat{f})\leqslant\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{enc}}(\hat{f})+\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{dec}}^{a}(\hat{f})+\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{dec}}^{g}(\hat{f})
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{dec}}^{a}(\hat{f})

\displaystyle=4\mathop{\sup}_{\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}}\mathop{\sup}_{z}\left|u_{\textrm{d}}\left(z\right)-u_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}(z)\right|^{2},

(53)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{dec}}^{g}(\hat{f})

\displaystyle=4\mathop{\sup}_{z}\left|u_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}\left(z\right)-f(u_{\textrm{e}}(z))\right|^{2},

(54)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{enc}}(\hat{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left|f(u_{\textrm{e}}(\alpha_{k}))-f(x_{k})\right|^{2}.

(55)

Let us begin with
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{enc}}
. Since
|f^{\prime}|\leqslant\nu
,
f(\cdot)
is
\nu
-Lipschitz property. Thus, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{enc}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left(f(u_{\textrm{e}}(\alpha_{k}))-f(x_{k})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left(|f(u_{\textrm{e}}(\alpha_{k}))-f(x_{k})|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left(\nu\cdot|u_{\textrm{e}}(\alpha_{k})-x_{k}|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\nu^{2}}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}(u_{\textrm{e}}(\alpha_{k})-x_{k})^{2}.

(56)

Next, we continue to
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{dec}}^{g}(\hat{f})
. As noted earlier,
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{dec}}^{g}(\hat{f})
quantifies the how generalized the decoder function is. To apply the results from Theorem
6
, it is necessary to ensure that
f\circ u_{\textrm{e}}\in\mathbb{W}^{2,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)
.

Lemma 7
.

[
6
, Lemma 3]

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
\nu
-Lipschitz continuous function with
\left\lVert f^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert_{{L}^{\infty}\left(\Omega\right)}\leqslant\eta
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open interval. If
u_{\textrm{e}}\in\mathbb{W}^{2,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)
then
f\circ u_{\textrm{e}}\in\mathbb{W}^{2,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)
.

Define the function
h(t):=u_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}(t)-f(u_{\textrm{e}}(t))
. By Lemma
7
, and under the assumption that both
u_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}
and
f\circ u_{\textrm{e}}
belong to the Sobolev space
\mathbb{W}^{2,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)
, it follows that
h\in\mathbb{W}^{2,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)
. The subsequent lemmas derive upper bounds for
\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{{L}^{\infty}\left(\Omega\right)}
and
\left\lVert h^{\prime}\right\rVert_{{L}^{\infty}\left(\Omega\right)}
by utilizing the fundamental properties of Sobolev spaces.

Lemma 8
.

If
\Omega=(a,b)
, then:

\displaystyle\|h\|^{2}_{{L}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}\leqslant\left\|h^{\prime}\right\|^{2}_{{L}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}\cdot\left(x_{0}^{2}-x_{0}(a+b)+\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{2}\right)

(57)

Proof.

Assume
\exists\,x_{0}\in\Omega:h\left(x_{0}\right)=0
. Therefore,
|h(x)|=\left|\int_{x_{0}}^{x}h^{\prime}(x)\,dx\right|
for
x\in\left[x_{0},b\right)
. Thus

\displaystyle|h(x)|=\left|\int_{x_{0}}^{x}h^{\prime}(x)\,dx\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(a)}}}{{\leqslant}}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\left|h^{\prime}(x)\right|\,dx\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(b)}}}{{\leqslant}}\left(\int_{x_{0}}^{x}1^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{x_{0}}^{x}|h^{\prime}(x)|^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(58)

where (a) and (b) are followed by the triangle and Cauchy-Schwartz inequalities respectively. Integrating the square of both sides over the interval
[x_{0},b)
yields:

\displaystyle\int_{x_{0}}^{b}|h(x)|^{2}\,dx\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{}}}{{\leqslant}}\int_{x_{0}}^{b}\left(z-x_{0}\right)\cdot\left(\int_{x_{0}}^{b}\left|h^{\prime}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)\,dz\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(a)}}}{{\leqslant}}\int_{x_{0}}^{b}\left(z-x_{0}\right)\,dz\cdot\int_{a}^{b}\left|h^{\prime}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx,

(59)

where (a) follows by
x_{0}\geqslant a
. On the other side, we have the following for every
x\in\left(a,x_{0}\right]
:

\displaystyle|h(x)|=\left|\int_{x}^{x_{0}}h^{\prime}(x)\,dx\right|\leqslant\int_{x}^{x_{0}}\left|h^{\prime}(x)\right|\,dx\leqslant\left(\int_{x}^{x_{0}}1^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\left(\int_{x}^{x_{0}}\left|h^{\prime}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(60)

Therefore, we have a similar inequality:

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{a}^{x_{0}}|h(x)|^{2}\,dx\leqslant\int_{a}^{x_{0}}\left(x_{0}-x\right)\,dx\cdot\int_{a}^{b}|h^{\prime}(x)|^{2}\,dx.

(61)

Using (
59
) and (
61
) completes the proof:

\displaystyle\|h\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{x_{0}}|h(x)|^{2}\,dx+\int_{x_{0}}^{b}|h(x)|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|h^{\prime}\right\|_{{L}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}\cdot\left(\int_{a}^{x_{0}}\left(x_{0}-x\right)\,dx+\int_{x_{0}}^{b}\left(x-x_{0}\right)\,dx\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|h^{\prime}\right\|_{{L}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}\cdot\left(x_{0}\left(x_{0}-a\right)-\left(\frac{x_{0}^{2}-a^{2}}{2}\right)+\left(\frac{b^{2}-x_{0}^{2}}{2}\right)-x_{0}\left(b-x_{0}\right)\right)

(62)

\displaystyle=\left\|h^{\prime}\right\|_{{L}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}\cdot\left(x_{0}^{2}-x_{0}(a+b)+\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{2}\right).

(63)

Thus, if
x_{0}
exists, the proof is complete. In the next step, we prove the existence of
x_{0}\in\Omega
such that
h\left(x_{0}\right)=0
. Recall that
h(t)=u_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}(t)-f(u_{\textrm{e}}(t))
and
u_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}(\cdot)
is the solution of (
9
). Assume there is no such
x_{0}
. Since
h\in\mathbb{W}^{2,2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)
, then
h(\cdot)
is continuous. Therefore, if there exist
t_{1},t_{2}\in\Omega
such that
h(t_{1})<0
and
h(t_{2})>0
, then the intermediate value theorem states that there exists
x_{0}\in(t_{1},t_{2})
such that
h(x_{0})=0
. Thus,
h(t)>0
or
h(t)<0
for all
t\in\Omega
. Without loss of generality, assume the first case where
h(t)>0
for all
t\in\Omega
. It means that
u_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}(t)>f(u_{\textrm{e}}(t))
for all
t\in\Omega
. Let us define

\beta^{*}:=\underset{\beta\in[N]}{\operatorname{argmin}}\,u_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}(\beta)-f(u_{\textrm{e}}(\beta)).

Let
\bar{u}_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}(t):=u_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}(t)-u_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}(\beta^{*})
. Note that
\int_{\Omega}\left(\bar{u}_{\textrm{d}}^{0^{\prime\prime}}(t)\right)^{2}\,dt=\int_{\Omega}\left(u_{\textrm{d}}^{0^{\prime\prime}}(t)\right)^{2}\,dt
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in[N]}\left[u_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}\left(\beta_{v}\right)-f\left(u_{\textrm{e}}\left(\beta_{v}\right)\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in[N]}\left[\bar{u}_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}\left(\beta_{v}\right)+u_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}(\beta^{*})-f\left(u_{\textrm{e}}\left(\beta_{v}\right)\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in[N]}\left[\bar{u}_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}\left(\beta_{v}\right)-f\left(u_{\textrm{e}}\left(\beta_{v}\right)\right)\right]^{2}+N\cdot u_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}(\beta^{*})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+2u_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}(\beta^{*})\sum_{v\in[N]}[\bar{u}_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}(\beta_{v})-f(u_{\textrm{e}}(\beta_{v}))]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(a)}}}{{\geqslant}}\sum_{v\in[N]}\left[\bar{u}_{\textrm{d}}^{\circ}\left(\beta_{v}\right)-f\left(u_{\textrm{e}}\left(\beta_{v}\right)\right)\right]^{2},

(64)

where (a) follows from
