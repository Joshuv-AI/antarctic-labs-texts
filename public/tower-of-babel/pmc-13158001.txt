Non‐Randomised Comparative Analysis and Cost‐Minimisation Analysis of Macrophage‐Modulating Cream on Mildly Infected Diabetic Foot Ulcers: A Real‐World Preliminary Report
Authors: Chia‐Ding Shih, Asad Sayeeduddin, Sean Mauch, Sofia Sakellaridi, Erianthe Ortega, Emily R Rosario
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are serious complications of diabetes, characterised by impaired healing driven in part by pro‐inflammatory macrophage activity. ON101, a topical macrophage‐modulating cream, promotes M1‐to‐M2 transition and improves wound healing. We prospectively enrolled patients with Wagner Grade 2 DFUs and mild infection per the Infectious Diseases Society of America criteria to receive ON101 with weekly follow‐up. Exclusions included dialysis dependence and inadequate arterial perfusion. The primary endpoint was a 4‐week healing outcome. The secondary analysis compared direct costs to a 1:1 retrospective standard of care cohort matched on wound locations and natural history. A total of 12 cases were analysed (mean age: 65.8 years in the ON101 group; 64.6 years old in the control group, p = 0.957). No adverse events occurred. The mean paired difference at the 4‐week healing outcome of ON101 was an 87% reduction (95% bootstrap CI: 9.0–244.0) versus the control group. Direct cost per wound healed was approximately 19% lower with ON101. Subjects receiving ON101 demonstrated a significantly faster wound‐healing rate within 4 weeks. The direct cost difference between ON101 and SOC mirrored lower resource use. A larger, randomised study with longer follow‐up is required to determine the robustness, generalisability and durability of these preliminary results.Keywords: diabetes mellitus, diabetic foot ulcers, macrophage activity, peripheral neuropathy, topical cream, wound care, wound‐care products, wound healing

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a global phenomenon affecting numerous adults worldwide. It is estimated that one in nine adults is living with diabetes [1]. In the United States alone, over 38 million Americans are affected [2]. Aside from its high prevalence, DM is a progressive condition when it is not controlled. The common complications include retinopathies, nephropathies, neuropathy and angiopathy. The downstream effects of these complications, especially the latter three, can lead to a non‐healing wound on the foot, otherwise known as diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) [3]. The pathomechanics of DFU results from an increase in neuromuscular imbalance, as chronic hyperglycaemia alters both the sensory and motor nervous systems [4]. This then causes weakening of the intrinsic muscles of the foot, leading to digital deformities and prominent bony prominences. Constant peak pressures and increased shear forces can lead to skin breakdown, eventually developing into full‐thickness ulcerations. If left untreated, DFU can manifest into non‐healing ulcers that are subject to infections and subsequent amputations. It is estimated that upwards of 34% of diabetics have a risk of acquiring a DFU in their lifetime [5]. Recurrence of these ulcerations is also common. Studies have shown a 65% chance of recurrence in 5 years after the index DFU heals [6].

When treating DFU, there is a standard of care (SOC) that is set for each patient. This includes assessing lower extremity perfusion, infection control, glycaemic control, serial debridement, offloading areas of peak pressure, management of co‐morbidities and wound dressings to maintain a moist healing environment [7]. However, many DFUs become chronic non‐healing wounds. Such an observation was thought to stem from the dysregulation of M1 and M2 macrophages [7]. Specifically, M2 macrophages are shown to have anti‐inflammatory potential, which is greatly reduced in DFUs. To our knowledge, there is no product on the US market that targets the macrophage‐modulating pathways. Macrophage modulation has shown promising results that help rebalance the immune microenvironment by reducing pro‐inflammatory M1 macrophages and increasing anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophages [8]. Although advanced wound‐care products, such as allografts, embryonic products and enzymatic debriders, are also utilised in cases of chronic DFUs, they do not provide a similar mechanism to promote wound healing.

One such product that modulates macrophages is the ON101 topical cream, which has demonstrated promising efficacy for DFU healing [9]. The proprietary formulation inhibits M1 macrophages and activates M2 macrophages, which upregulates VEGF to promote angiogenesis and enhances TGF‐β‐mediated recruitment of regenerative stem cells to stimulate fibroblast proliferation and promotes collagen synthesis via hydroxyproline pathways to facilitate extracellular matrix deposition [10]. Through these mechanisms, ON101 rebalances the wound microenvironment by modulating the M1 and M2 polarisation and enabling the transition from inflammation to proliferation, thereby promoting ulcer healing. As a result, when compared to traditional absorbent wound‐care products or other advanced therapies (i.e., negative pressure wound therapy, flap, skin substitutes and silver foam dressings), a significantly higher healing incidence has been noted in the experimental group [8, 11]. Cost‐effectiveness analysis based on the Markov model also demonstrates cost savings from the Asian healthcare perspective [12, 13]. While the previous randomised controlled trial and real‐world study demonstrate significant wound healing after ON101 treatment, the use of ON101 in an outpatient real‐world setting in the United States is not well known. In addition, the efficacy and cost associated with the concurrent use of ON101 on mildly infected DFU are not well established. To investigate the effect of ON101 on the above conditions, the primary goal of the study will be to examine the wound healing rate of individuals with chronic DFUs that are mildly infected. The secondary goal of the study is to estimate the potential cost savings from ON101 compared to the SOC in a US outpatient setting.

Methods

Study Cohort

Patients who presented with Wagner Grade 2 DFUs and mild infections, as classified by the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) Grade 2, were prospectively recruited at the wound clinic of Casa Colina Hospital and Centers for Healthcare, Pomona, California. A mildly infected DFU would consist of at least two of the following criteria per IDSA:

The inclusion criterion was wound size ≤ 12 cm2 at enrolment. Exclusion criteria included exposed bone, untreated osteomyelitis, necrotising fasciitis, intra‐muscle abscess, extensive necrosis or gangrene, untreated peripheral arterial disease, dialysis, active malignancy, HIV/AIDS, psychiatric conditions affecting compliance or abnormal laboratory values (i.e., liver function > 3× ULN, WBC < 3.0 or > 12.0 × 109/L, albumin < 2.5 g/dL, haemoglobin ≤ 100 g/L). Inadequate perfusion, which was evaluated by Doppler, ultrasound, ABI, TcPO2 or toe pressure, also led to exclusion. Written consent was obtained before a 1‐week pre‐trial run‐in.

Enrolled ON101‐treated subjects were followed prospectively for 16 weeks with weekly physician visits. Wounds were photographed and measured before and after debridement at each weekly follow‐up visit. For the comparative cost analysis, a retrospective SOC control group was identified post hoc from the hospital's patient records from January 2023 to June 2025. Patients aged 18 years or older who received DFU wound care under the primary investigator's care, based on ICD‐10 code, were screened. Match criteria were considered in the following order: (1) wound location/nature history of the ulcer, (2) wound size, (3) co‐morbidities that may influence wound healing (i.e., peripheral arterial disease and its treatment status) and (4) age. The primary objective was the wound healing rate at 4 weeks. The Institutional Review Board at Casa Colina Hospital and Centers for Healthcare (IRB# 00002372) approved the study.

Statistical and Exploratory Pragmatic Cost‐Minimisation Analysis

The effect of healing was determined by the following measures: percent reduction at Week 4, healing slope and normalised slope as the primary aim is to investigate how quickly ON101 may heal wounds as compared to SOC. Week‐4 follow‐up: if not available, the day closest to 28 days from the visit was chosen for the analysis. The Kaplan–Meier curve was used to estimate the healing probability between the two groups.

A pragmatic cost‐minimisation analysis was performed from a payer perspective. To ensure methodological transparency without disclosing proprietary pricing, we report resource utilisation based on the observed time‐to‐healing in both groups. Unit costs for clinical services (Evaluation and Management, CPT 99214; Debridement, CPT 97597) were standardised using the CMS 2025 Physician Fee Schedule.

Given the small sample size and matched study design, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for treatment effects were estimated using nonparametric bootstrap resampling of paired differences (10 000 iterations). For each outcome, within‐pair differences between ON101 and SOC were calculated and bootstrap samples were generated with replacement. Two‐sided 95% CIs were derived using the empirical percentile method (2.5th and 97.5th percentiles). This approach avoids distributional assumptions and is appropriate for small, heterogeneous datasets. All analyses were performed using MATLAB, with custom scripts for data processing, trapezoidal integration for AUC, Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests and exact permutation p value calculations. A p < 0.05 was considered significantly different between the two groups.

Results

Patient Population and Characteristics

A total of 12 clinical cases, consisting of six matched pairs, were utilised for this study. The experimental group received ON101 treatment, while the control group received standard of care based on current wound care practices in the wound clinic at Casa Colina Hospital and Centers for Healthcare. Six SOC cases matched the six ON101‐treated subjects based on the criteria. The wound locations and characteristics were the plantar fourth metatarsal head DFU; the lateral fifth metatarsal DFU; DFU complicated by post‐surgical infections; DFU with exposed tendon; DFU on the distal foot status post transmetatarsal amputation and toe DFU with concerns of underlying bone infection. There was no significant difference in sex between the two groups. The average ages for the experimental and control groups were 65.84 and 64.67 years, respectively (p = 0.957). Both groups carried significant comorbidity burdens, including diabetes, peripheral arterial disease, hypertension and coronary arterial disease. The overall cohort characteristics were summarised in Table 1. All wounds healed in both cohorts, although two from the ON101 treatment group and four from the control group healed beyond the study period of 16 weeks. There was no limb loss or mortality during the study period.

Baseline Wound Characteristics and Healing Trajectories

The baseline wound duration before any treatment averaged 34 weeks for the experimental group, with a range of 15–74 weeks. For the control group, the wound duration before treatment averaged 31 weeks, with a range of 3–135 weeks. In terms of the wound size, the average baseline wound size was 0.86 cm2 (0.40–1.44 cm2) for the experimental group, while the control group's was 2.49 cm2 (0.96–5.1 cm2). The ON101 group's wound healing rate at Week 4 showed a significantly greater reduction in wound size (p = 0.0312) and higher healing efficiency, reflected by the normalised healing slope relative to baseline (p = 0.0312) (Figure 1). At Week 4, ON101 demonstrated greater wound‐area reduction compared with matched SOC controls. The mean paired difference in percent reduction was 87.0 (95% bootstrap CI: 9.0–244.0), with a median difference of 32.7. Similarly, the normalised healing slope favoured ON101, with a mean paired difference of 0.0074 (95% bootstrap CI: 0.0012–0.0158) and a median difference of 0.0069. These findings indicate a consistent directional benefit of ON101 across both healing metrics. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis projects the time to heal is roughly half for the ON101 group versus the control group, as noted in Figure 2.

Wounds under ON101 treatment had a significantly faster healing rate (p = 0.0312) as demonstrated by the adjusted slope and % wound reduction at Week 4. Time to closure (p = 0.125) and unadjusted healing slope (p = 0.4688) were not significantly different between the two groups, possibly due to the small sample size and a wide range of baseline wound size.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis projects a drastic difference between the ON101 and the control groups. The symbols in the red line indicate the two subjects in the control group that did not achieve full closure.

Exploratory Pragmatic Cost‐Minimisation Analysis

Using a cost‐per‐wound‐healed framework adapted from prior work, ON101 was associated with an approximate 19% reduction in the overall direct cost per wound healed compared with SOC. The primary driver of cost savings was the 57.6% reduction in healing duration in the ON101 group, which significantly decreased the cumulative costs associated with repeated outpatient visits and debridement procedures (Table 3). Given the pilot nature of this study (n = 9), these economic findings are considered exploratory. This economic difference tracked lower cumulative resource utilisation, notably fewer evaluation‐and‐management (E&M) visits and debridement procedures. This is consistent with the observed clinical trajectory in which participants showed early improvement under physician‐directed use and weekly follow‐up.

Discussion

Overall, ON101 demonstrated a promising, safe and effective treatment option for mildly infected DFUs. The ON101 group demonstrated significantly greater wound size reduction at Week 4 (p = 0.0312) and higher healing efficiency, as reflected by the normalised healing slope relative to baseline (p = 0.0312). Furthermore, there was a projected cost saving (i.e., 19% reduction in the overall direct cost per wound) in the ON101 group. Overall, subjects under ON101 treatment all had substantial improvement after treatment despite the chronicity of the wound and mild infection status. These findings suggested that ON101 would be a cost‐effective and safe option for challenging hard‐to‐heal DFUs.

Hard‐to‐heal DFUs posed increased risks for infection and subsequent amputations, which both incurred healthcare dollars. A definition of a hard‐to‐heal wound is any wound that fails to improve 40%–50% of the wound size in 4 weeks of SOC [14]. However, several factors, including age, cardiovascular disease, kidney function, pressure, nutritional status or infection, may delay wound healing. In the present cohort, all wounds were classified as hard‐to‐heal. A common pathway to manage hard‐to‐heal DFUs will likely rely on advanced wound‐care products such as allograft, xenograft and other synthetic products [15]. The mechanism of action from the graft was primarily to increase the recruitment of growth factors to improve the wound healing environment. Such a method to manage the wound has been shown to be costly, with questionable effects [15]. If the graft application requires being in the operating room, the cost is even higher and the process to schedule the case is lengthy with inherent perioperative risks, especially when patients have multiple co‐morbidities.

Generally speaking, topical treatment is widely accepted by patients and providers. However, current topical options for hard‐to‐heal DFUs focus on primarily antibacterial properties and enzymatic debridement. For superficial DFUs, antibacterial treatment may be sufficient. Nonetheless, when DFUs become chronic and hard‐to‐heal, applying topical antibiotic ointment may not help and can increase the risk of resistance. The wounds that become chronic and the healing that is stagnant are due to Macrophages 1 and 2 dysregulation [16]. This dysregulation causes the wound cycles in the inflammatory phase of the wound healing process; therefore, the wound would become chronic. Unlike ON101, treatments that are mentioned above do not modulate the macrophage regulation [8]. Our findings in the present trial are aligned with the previous ON101 randomised controlled trial suggesting the consistent efficacy and safety of ON101 for DFU treatment. In our trial, all subjects demonstrated significant improvement within 4 weeks of the ON101 treatment and healed eventually as opposed to weeks and months of SOC, which may not achieve a complete closure. Given its ease to use and promising effects, ON101 is likely a viable option for other chronic wounds in addition to DFUs based on its mechanism of action.

As mentioned above, treatment of DFU is associated with numerous financial burdens, with an estimated over $8659 of diabetic foot care expenditure per patient [17]. Patients on Medicare were found to have reimbursements of around $33 000 per patient [8, 17]. Moreover, DFUs were found to contribute over $1.9 billion in costs to the emergency room department nationwide and nearly $8.8 billion in costs to the inpatient departments. In the United States alone, upwards of $13 billion is spent annually on the management of diabetic foot disease [17]. In cases where limb salvage was not possible, pedal or proximal amputations, the costs are estimated to be $43 800 and $66 215, respectively. This cost is not only associated with the procedure itself but also includes post‐operative management, post‐operative rehabilitation and extended stays at care facilities [17]. Furthermore, the quality‐adjusted life year (QALY) after amputation, as well as the loss of workforce, added additional cost to society.

In our cost analysis using a cost‐per‐wound‐healed framework adapted from prior work, ON101 was associated with an approximate 19% reduction in direct cost per wound healed compared with standard dressings without advanced therapy. This pragmatic measure is not as rigorous as a full cost‐effectiveness model, but the savings—driven by fewer evaluation visits and debridement procedures—mirror the clinical trajectory of earlier improvement under physician‐directed use. Notably, the ON101 group included patients with mildly infected DFUs, whereas the control group comprised only non‐infected ulcers. In that sense, our observed economic advantage may underestimate ON101's true value; a like‐for‐like comparison could reveal even greater savings given the higher baseline complexity of infected wounds.

These clinic‐level findings are consistent with published health‐economic evaluations. For instance, in Taiwan, ON101 improved healing and reduced downstream complications while being cost‐effective in the base case and cost‐saving in high‐risk subgroups. In Singapore, ON101 with general wound care was cost‐saving based on the Markov model for incremental cost‐effectiveness ratios (ICERs) and QALYs [12]. ON101 appeared to yield a 1.5 QALY gain and lower healthcare costs (−$16 237) compared to the SOC. In the case of uncontrolled diabetes (i.e., HbA1c > 9%), ulcer duration > 6 months and ulcer size greater than 5 cm2, ON101 with general wound care had ICERs of −$161 963, −$181 726 and −$199 130 per QALY gained, respectively. Framed against the broader wound‐care burden—$22.5 billion in US Medicare costs annually and $148.65 billion globally—even modest reductions in cost per healed wound translate into meaningful system‐level savings [18]. Overall, the evidence suggests that ON101 may deliver both clinical and economic value by accelerating closure and compressing resource utilisation in routine multidisciplinary practice.

Limitations

There are a few limitations in this small trial. First, the small sample size may be under‐powered, so the results may be subjected to Type II error. Second, not all co‐morbidities were matched between the two groups. Although the experimental group had a worse clinical presentation, such as a mild infection, at the beginning of the trial, the experimental group still demonstrated a faster healing rate than the control group. Third, it is worth noting that the design of the hybrid study—prospectively enrolling for the experimental cohort and retrospective selection for SOC controls—increased the risk of selection and information biases. Matching was performed post hoc, which further limited causal inference. Nonetheless, wound location and the natural history of the ulcer were the primary considerations, as treatment and offloading strategies were expected to be similar between groups. The strict matching criteria narrowed the eligible control pool, which limited the selection process and may affect generalisability, but they were applied to reduce clinically meaningful differences between groups. Fourth, the wound base between the experimental and control groups was not similar at baseline; however, the primary objective was 4‐week wound healing rates, which demonstrated significantly faster healing in the experimental group. Lastly, the study lacks a long‐term follow‐up period. This is important to address in future studies, given that DFUs have high recurrence rates due to underlying and often unaddressed biomechanical or anatomical deformities in the setting of peripheral neuropathy.

Conclusion

DFUs are a common complication associated with the DM patient population. Many modalities are available for treatment. SOC wound‐care products have been the mainstay; however, these products have not been shown to modulate the macrophage pathway that is present in chronic ulcerations. Particularly in the United States, there is no current topical medication that is used to alter this pathway. ON101 has been used internationally and has shown to increase anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophage activity while decreasing pro‐inflammatory M1 activity. Our clinical trial was amongst the first documented in the country using such a product and showed promising results. Faster wound healing and increase in cost savings were noted in the experimental ON101 group. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the extremely small sample size, non‐randomised hybrid study design, short 4‐week observation period and the exploratory nature of the analyses. Given the observed benefits, it may serve as a first‐line therapy to promote wound closure and reduce economic burden; however, larger, randomised studies with longer follow‐up are required to determine the robustness, generalisability, and durability of these preliminary results.
