Real-world treatment patterns and clinical outcomes in patients with emerging estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1)-mutated ER+ metastatic breast cancer in the U.S., 2018-2024
Authors: Jane L Meisel, Clara Chen, Ayush Kris, Meng Ru, Timothy Pham, James Roose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeClinical studies have highlighted the potential utility of monitoring for Estrogen receptor alpha 1 (ESR1) mutation (m) (ESR1m) emergence during first-line therapy (1L) for metastatic breast cancer (mBC). However, less is known about the real-world practice for patients with emergent ESR1m before progression and their outcomes. This study aimed to characterize real-world treatment patterns among patients with mBC who underwent ESR1m mutation testing during first-line (1L) therapy, and to evaluate the association between ESR1m detection during 1L and clinical outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective, observational cohort study used the US-based, deidentified Flatiron Health Research Database and included patients diagnosed with ER+/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HER2-) metastatic BC between 2018 and June 2024. rwOS and rwPFS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier methodology and compared after propensity score matching. Clinical outcomes were evaluated from the time of first ESR1 test during 1L therapy to minimize time-related bias.Results7772 patients who initiated 1L therapy were included. Treatment patterns in 1L were comparable between patients with (n = 240) and without (n = 1095) ESR1m detected during 1L. Among patients tested for ESR1m mutations during 1L, detection of ESR1m was associated with significantly shorter rwPFS and OS from the time of testing, both before and after propensity score matching (rwPFS hazard ratio [HR] 0.68 [95%CI: 0.48–0.96], rwOS HR: 0.58 [95%CI: 0.36–0.95]).ConclusionsDetection of ESR1m during 1L was associated with poorer clinical outcomes. These findings highlight the clinical relevance of ESR1m emergence prior to progression and underscore the need for strategies to better identify and manage patients with emerging endocrine resistance.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10549-026-08061-w.Keywords: Running title, Treatment Patterns and outcomes in ESR1m mBC

Introduction

Female breast cancer (BC) is the second most common cancer globally, and BCs that express hormone receptor (HR+, most commonly estrogen receptor [ER+]), but not human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2-), are the most common BC subtype, representing approximately 70% of all BC cases [1, 2]. Standard first-line (1 L) therapy for HR+ /HER2- mBC includes combining endocrine therapy (ET), such as an aromatase inhibitor, and a cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 inhibitor (CDK4/6i); however, most patients will eventually have disease progression and subsequent therapies are less effective and may be more poorly tolerated compared with 1 L therapies [2]. Resistance to ET is associated with activating mutations in the ESR1 gene that encodes ER⍺, the main isoform of the ER, but are rare at metastatic BC diagnosis [3–5]. ESR1 mutations can be detected in approximately 40% of patients with recurrent disease previously treated with AI or who are resistant to ET, and among those with exposure to ET in the 1 L setting [6–9]. Although ESR1 mutations confer resistance to estrogen-depleting aromatase inhibitors, these tumors may still respond to selective estrogen-receptor degraders (SERDs), which induce the degradation of the ER. Previous work has shown that ESR1 mutations are detectable in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) at a median of approximately 6 months before disease progression [10].

Results from two recent clinical studies demonstrate that modifying endocrine therapy upon detection of ESR1m in ctDNA prior to disease progression can improve progression-free survival [11, 12]. The proof-of-concept phase 3 PADA-1 study (NCT03079011) demonstrated that switching from AI to the SERD fulvestrant while maintaining the CDK4/6i palbociclib when an ESR1 mutation was detected in ctDNA significantly improved PFS, resulting in a 39% reduction in risk [11]. The phase 3, double-blind, randomized SERENA-6 trial (NCT04964934) evaluated the efficacy and safety of switching from an AI to the next-generation oral SERD (ngSERD) camizestrant, while maintaining a CDK4/6i, after the emergence of ESR1m in ctDNA during 1 L therapy in patients without radiologic progression. An interim analysis of this trial demonstrated a statistically significant and clinically meaningful benefit for the camizestrant combination. These findings suggest that ESR1m emergence during first-line therapy may represent a clinically relevant decision point. Although these clinical studies indicate that switching from AI to SERD or next-generation oral SERD (ngSERD) such as camizestrant while maintaining CDK4/6i upon detection of ESR1m delays progression, real-world data on how ESR1m are detected and how these findings translate into clinical outcomes during first-line therapy, particularly prior to progression, remain limited. This study aimed to characterize treatment patterns and to evaluate the association between ESR1m detection during first-line therapy and subsequent clinical outcomes from the time of testing, within the pre-progression setting.

Methods

Study design, data source, and population

This retrospective, observational cohort study used the US-based, deidentified Flatiron Health Research Database (FHRD) [13]. Adult patients with a confirmed diagnosis of ER+/HER2- mBC from 1/1/2018 to 6/30/2024 were included in the study (Fig. 1) Eligible patients were diagnosed with mBC (either de novo, or recurrent), were age 18 or older at mBC diagnosis, diagnosed with ER-positive/HER2-negative BC, based on the ER and HER2 test results closest to mBC diagnosis among results reported anytime up to 60 days after mBC diagnosis. Patients were excluded if they had evidence of another non-breast cancer diagnosis in the 90 days before mBC diagnosis date, had evidence of receiving a clinical study drug prior to or after mBC diagnosis, or if they had evidence indicating missing treatment data at the start of therapy for mBC. Data were captured from initial diagnosis until the end of the patient’s record, death, or the date of data cutoff (September 30, 2024), whichever was earliest.

Study design diagram

Variables and endpoints

Patient demographic and clinical characteristics were captured in structured EHR data (e.g., Race, Ethnicity, Sex, Birth Year) and abstracted from unstructured data (e.g., Group Stage, Biomarker information for the primary study population). Neoadjuvant/adjuvant therapy was inferred based on structured medication orders and administrations and abstracted oral therapy data from before mBC. First-line therapy for mBC and later lines of therapy were derived from structured medication orders and administrations and abstracted oral therapy data using business rules. Details regarding therapy class assignments are presented in the Supplemental Methods. Lines of therapy in the FHRD are derived from structured medication orders and administrations in the EHR as well as abstracted oral therapy data. In general, line advancement (e.g., from 1L to 2L) indicates the addition of one or more antineoplastic drugs to a regimen more than 28 days (more than 60 days, for the addition of CDK4/6i) after it begins, with allowed substitutions (e.g., substitution of anastrozole for letrozole or vice versa). As a result, although line advancement often follows clinical progression, it may also reflect therapy changes unrelated to disease progression, including (but not limited to) toxicity, financial burden, and patient choice.

Clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes (rwOS and rwPFS) were evaluated from the earliest ESR1 test in 1L for eligible patients among those with and without ESR1m detected in the earliest test. rwOS and rwPFS were estimated as the time from earliest ESR1 test to death (for rwOS) and to progression or death (for rwPFS). This approach was used to align the start of follow-up with mutation assessment and to minimize time-related bias. Patients whose tumors were never tested for ESR1m, or who progressed, initiated second-line (2L) therapy, or died before their tumor was tested for ESR1m were not included. This restriction was applied to ensure that outcomes were evaluated in patients at risk of ESR1m detection during 1L therapy.

Statistical methods/analyses

Patient demographic and clinical characteristics, and treatment patterns were summarized using descriptive statistics. Frequencies and percentages were used to describe the categorical variables. Means, standard deviations, medians, interquartile ranges (IQR), and minimum and maximum values were calculated for continuous variables, as applicable. Treatment patterns were also summarized graphically. No imputation was conducted. The number of patients for whom data was missing is reported. ESR1m status was determined based on available testing results from routine clinical practice, which may include both tissue- and blood-based assays.

Real-world overall survival (rwOS) and progression-free survival (rwPFS) were estimated for patients with an ESR1m detected at first ESR1 test during 1L (time zero) before progression using the Kaplan-Meier methodology and compared to those without an ESR1m detected at first ESR1 test using 1:1 propensity score matching on key clinical and treatment-relevant covariates that may be associated with both ESR1m detection and outcomes (time from 1L start to first ESR1 test result, ER expression level, presence of liver metastases, age, duration of prior AI therapy, menopausal status). Median estimates and 95% confidence intervals were reported. In addition, 6, 12, 18, and 24-month survival estimates and hazard ratios were presented for each outcome.

Bias due to missing data was minimized via collection of information from unstructured sources (i.e., review of clinical notes). Clinical outcomes are measured from the earliest ESR1 test to prevent bias that would result from using the start date of 1 L as time zero and classifying patients as having ESR1m based on testing during 1 L (i.e., after time zero). In such an analysis, patients who did not live or remain on 1 L therapy long enough to have ESR1m detected would be classified in the not-ESR1m group. Although patients may have been tested more than once, only the first ESR1 test result reported during 1 L and prior to progression was used as the index date and to define the exposure groups (ESR1m, not ESR1m). This was done to improve comparability of groups with respect to time from 1 L initiation to earliest ESR1 test (as tests that detect ESR1m would otherwise tend to occur later in 1 L), and to avoid immortal time bias that would arise from classifying patients based on mutation status determined after the start of follow-up. Statistical analyses were implemented using R statistical software version 4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [14].

Results

Among patients with ESR1 testing during first-line therapy, detection of ESR1m occurred in a minority of patients and was associated with differences in subsequent clinical outcomes.

Participants and patient characteristics

This study included 8581 eligible patients, of whom 7772 (91%) initiated 1L therapy; ESR1m testing during 1L was observed in 17% of patients, indicating that testing was not routinely performed in this setting, 1086 were eligible for rwOS and rwPFS analyses. The median age of patients was 63 years of age, 63% were White, 11% Black, 2% Asian, 12% Other Race, and Race was Unknown for 12%. 5% were Hispanic or Latino, 75% received care in the Community (i.e., non-Academic) setting, and 16% had evidence of liver metastases at mBC diagnosis (Table 1). Among the 7,772 (91%) patients who initiated 1L, ECOG was 0–1 for 52% of patients, 2 + for 11%, and Unknown for 37% (n = 2,908) at 1L start date. Median time from 1L start to first ESR1 test was 7.4 months. Median follow-up from mBC diagnosis was 18 months.

Treatment patterns

Treatment patterns in 1L were comparable regardless of ESR1m status, with AI plus CDK4/6i being the most common regimen overall (731/1335; 55%) (Table S1), as well as in patients with (140/240; 58%) and without (591/1095; 54%) a mutation detected during 1L therapy (Figs. 2 and 3). Second-line therapy was observed during the follow-up period for a much higher share of patients in whose tumor an ESR1m was detected, 84% versus 52% (Figs. 2 and 3). Patients with an ESR1m detected during 1L therapy were more likely to receive CDK4/6i + fulvestrant or elacestrant as a second-line (2L) therapy (Fig. 2). A higher proportion of patients with ESR1m detected during 1L initiated second-line therapy during follow-up compared with those without ESR1m detection (35/152 [23%] vs. 83/918 [9%]). Among patients tested during 1L, ESR1m detection defined a subgroup with distinct clinical trajectories, including higher rates of treatment transition and shorter survival outcomes.

Clinical outcomes

Detection of ESR1m at the time of first testing during first-line therapy was associated with significantly shorter rwPFS and OS compared with no mutation detected. Of the 1065 patients eligible for rwPFS analysis, an ESR1m was detected in 104 (9.8%) patients and not detected in 961 (90.2%). Of 1086 patients eligible for rwOS analysis, an ESR1m was detected in 105 (9.7%) patients and not detected in 981 (90.3%) (Table 2). Patients with an ESR1m detected on their earliest test reported during 1L therapy had shorter median rwPFS and median rwOS compared with those without an ESR1m detected (Table 2; Fig. 4). These findings remained consistent after 1:1 PSM, with shorter rwPFS and OS observed among patients with ESR1m detected. Median rwPFS (7.7 mos [95% CI: 5.7–11.4] vs. 13.6 mos [95% CI: 9.9–16.5]; hazard ratio [HR] 0.68 [95% CI: 0.48–0.96]) and median rwOS (32.2 mos [95% CI: 21.1-NR] vs. NR [95% CI: 35.4-NR], HR: 0.58 [95% CI: 0.36–0.95]) were shorter in patients with an ESR1m detected during 1L. (Table 2; Fig. 5). Covariates were balanced after 1:1 propensity score matching, with SMD < 0.15 for all key covariates (Supplemental Figure S1). Median rwPFS following ESR1m detection was approximately 7.7 months, indicating a relatively short interval to progression after mutation detection.

Discussion

Key results

In this real-world study, detection of ESR1m during first-line therapy was associated with shorter progression-free and overall survival, consistent with ESR1m as a marker of endocrine resistance. Notably, these findings were observed from the time of mutation detection prior to clinical/radiologic progression, highlighting a clinically relevant pre-progression phase during which outcomes remain poor.

AI plus CDK4/6i was the most common 1L treatment regardless of ESR1m detection. Among patients who initiated 2L, those with ESR1m detected during 1L were more likely to receive CDK4/6i + fulvestrant or elacestrant monotherapy in 2L compared to those without ESR1m detected during 1L. A considerably higher share of patients with ESR1m detected initiated 2L during the follow-up period, consistent with ESR1m being associated with shorter time to progression.

Patients whose tumors developed ESR1m had poorer outcomes (rwPFS and rwOS) measured from the time of first ESR1 test in 1 L. These associations persisted after propensity score matching based on key covariates, which yielded balanced groups for comparison. This is consistent with ESR1m being a driver of endocrine resistance which suggests that patients with ESR1m may benefit from new treatment strategies that address endocrine resistance. Although routine serial testing was not conducted in our real-world study sample, rwPFS estimates for patients with ESR1m detected at first test during 1 L in this study (7.7 months [95% CI: 5.7, 11.4 months]) are comparable to the time from ESR1m to progression reported for the control group of SERENA-6 (median PFS 9.2 months), in which patients with ESR1m detected remained on CDK4/6i + AI, and consistent with previous findings indicating that ESR1m can be detected approximately 6 months before disease progression [10, 12]. Clinical trials such as PADA-1 and SERENA-6 have demonstrated that modifying endocrine therapy upon detection of ESR1m prior to progression can improve outcomes. These findings warrant further research to evaluate how such approaches related to outcomes in real-world clinical practice as strategies for ESR1m testing and treatment modification continue to evolve.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. In this cohort of 1L ER+/HER2- mBC patients, only 17% of patients’ tumors were tested for ESR1m in this study, and the reasons for testing are not known. In routine clinical practice ESR1m detection often occurs as part of broader genomic testing rather than longitudinal monitoring of ESR1, which may influence the timing and context of mutation detection. ESR1 testing may have been conducted to test for alterations in other genes for which targeted therapies are indicated, such as BRCA1, BRCA2, PIK3CA, AKT1, and/or PTEN. The ESR1 tests considered in this study reflected real-world testing patterns, and included tests of liquid and tissue specimens, tests of samples of varying quality, and testing conducted by both numerous commercial vendors (e.g., Guardant, Foundation Medicine, Caris Life Sciences) as well as in-house testing services. This variability in samples, sample types, and assays may have resulted in variable test sensitivity/specificity and limits of detection, which could have resulted in misclassification of ESR1 test results.

Patients were classified based on whether an ESR1m was detected in a test with result reported during 1 L and prior to real-world progression to better understand clinical outcomes in the remainder of 1 L. Real-world progression (rwP), which reflects clinician documentation of disease worsening rather than RECIST-based criteria, was used to determine whether ESR1 testing and detection during 1 L occurred before progression. Because rwP may not align perfectly with RECIST-based progression, some patients’ may have been misclassified as having ESR1m detected prior to progression, and rwPFS estimates may differ from what would have been observed had RECIST-based progression been available [15]. Patients may not have been monitored as regularly as in a prospective clinical study, and progression may not be captured in all instances where disease worsened, either because it was not detected, or because it was not documented in the EHR. In such cases some patients may have already progressed at the time an ESR1m was detected, though this limitation is mitigated because advancement to 2 L therapy would be expected in such instances. Lines of therapy in this study were derived from structured and abstracted treatment data using standard rules, and as such it was not possible to determine why treatment was changed. Although results suggest that a greater share of pts with ESR1m detected advanced to 2 L therapy, limitations of derived line of therapy and use of clinician documented progression preclude attributing this to ESR1m.

Although patients with and without ESR1m detected at first test during 1L were comparable with respect to key covariates after 1:1 propensity score matching, unmeasured or residual confounding could have affected comparative analyses of rwPFS and rwOS.

These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that ESR1m is associated with worse survival outcomes [6, 10, 16, 17]. Detecting ESR1m in 1 L before progression highlights the potential to guide treatment strategies to more effectively treat ER+/HER2- with emergent ESR1m.

Patients included in this study are from both community and academic practices in the U.S. Only patients whose tumors were tested for ESR1m during 1L and before radiologic progression—approximately 14% of all pts with 1L in the sample–could be included in analyses of rwPFS and rwOS. If these patients are not representative, then results may not generalize to a broader population.

While these findings underscore the clinical relevance of ESR1m emergence, the interpretation of their implications for testing strategies or treatment modification in routine practice requires further investigation, as approaches to integrating ESR1m detection into first-line management are still evolving.

Conclusion

Detection of ESR1m during first-line therapy was associated with poorer clinical outcomes. These findings define a clinically relevant pre-progression phase characterized by emerging endocrine therapy resistance and unfavorable outcomes. Further research is warranted to understand how ESR1m detection and subsequent treatment strategies may be integrated into routine practice as this area continues to evolve.
