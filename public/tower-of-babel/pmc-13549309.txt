Neutrophil-Percentage-to-Albumin Ratio Outperforms High-Sensitivity C-Reactive Protein in Predicting Mortality in Acute Heart Failure: Evidence from a Nationwide Prospective Cohort
Authors: Zhanyuan Chen, Yu Wei, Yaoyao Wang, Rui Zhu, Haoran Ji, Yaxin Liu, Jianfang Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio (NPAR) is a novel inflammatory marker. However, its prognostic role in acute heart failure (AHF) remains unclear.MethodsThis post hoc analysis included 4622 patients with AHF from a nationwide prospective cohort in China. The outcomes were all-cause mortality (ACM), cardiovascular mortality (CVM), major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), and hospitalization for HF (HHF). Multivariable Cox regression was used to assess the associations between NPAR and outcomes. The predictive performance of the models was evaluated using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index), 4-year time-dependent continuous net reclassification improvement (cNRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 4.61 years, higher NPAR and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) showed positive graded associations with mortality. Compared with the lowest NPAR quartile, the hazard ratios (95% CIs) for the second, third, and fourth quartiles were 1.29 (1.12–1.47), 1.38 (1.20–1.58), and 1.74 (1.52–1.99) for ACM, and 1.30 (1.10–1.53), 1.39 (1.18–1.63), and 1.72 (1.46–2.03) for CVM, respectively (all P < 0.001). Similar graded associations were observed for 1-year MACE and HHF. Adding NPAR to the primary multivariable model improved the predictive ability for ACM (C-index from 0.693 to 0.701; cNRI 9.90%; IDI 1.20%) and CVM (C-index from 0.705 to 0.712; cNRI 9.30%; IDI 1.00%) (all P < 0.001). NPAR outperformed hs-CRP for mortality prediction, whereas their combination did not further improve model performance.ConclusionNPAR was positively associated with risks of ACM, CVM, MACE, and HHF and provided incremental prognostic value for mortality beyond hs-CRP. NPAR may be useful for prognostic risk assessment in AHF and warrants further investigation as a potential alternative to hs-CRP.Keywords: inflammation, biomarker, neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, acute heart failure, prognosis

Background

As the end stage of various cardiovascular diseases, heart failure (HF) has emerged as a major challenge to global public health due to its escalating prevalence, multi-morbidity, and high rates of rehospitalization and mortality.1,2 Therefore, effective risk stratification for HF is crucial, as it guides personalized treatment and optimizes the allocation of limited healthcare resources. Inflammation represents a promising candidate marker for such risk stratification.3

Even in the absence of infection, inflammatory activation is common in patients with HF, especially in those with acute HF (AHF), and plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of HF.4 It is closely associated with various adverse outcomes, including hospitalization for HF and mortality.5 A wide range of indicators have been validated to assess inflammation in HF, such as interleukins, galectin-3, tumor necrosis factor-α, soluble Toll-like receptor 2, C-reactive protein (CRP), and high-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP).6 Among them, CRP,7,8 hs-CRP,9,10 and derived hematologic indices such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio,11 platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio,12 and systemic immune-inflammation index,13 are widely used in clinical practice and their prognostic value in HF has been well ascertained due to their accessibility and low cost.

The neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio (NPAR), defined as the ratio of neutrophil percentage to serum albumin, is a novel, economical, and readily accessible derived inflammatory marker. Several previous studies have confirmed its prognostic value in various diseases,14–18 including chronic HF (CHF).19,20 Moreover, NPAR has demonstrated prognostic performance superior to that of CRP in specific cohorts, such as patients with CHF,21 coronary artery ectasia,22 and peritoneal dialysis.23 However, its prognostic value in AHF and its potential role as an alternative to hs-CRP for prognostic assessment remain uncertain.

Accordingly, based on a nationwide, multicenter, prospective cohort, we conducted the first large-scale post hoc analysis to assess the associations of NPAR with multiple clinical outcomes and evaluate its prognostic utility in patients with AHF.

Patients and Methods

Study Design, Participants, and Follow-Up

The present study was a post hoc analysis based on the China Patient-Centered Evaluative Assessment of Cardiac Events Prospective Heart Failure (China PEACE 5p-HF) Study, which has been described previously.24 Briefly, this nationwide, prospective, multicenter study consecutively enrolled 4907 patients aged ≥18 years who were hospitalized primarily for AHF between August 2016 and May 2018. Participants were recruited from 52 hospitals (48 tertiary and 4 secondary hospitals) across 20 provinces in China. After excluding 32 patients who died during the index hospitalization and 253 whose NPAR was unavailable at admission, 4622 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure S1). Among these patients, 387 (8.37%) had missing serum uric acid, 359 (7.77%) had missing body mass index (BMI), 296 (6.4%) had missing waist circumference, 120 (2.60%) had missing hs-CRP, and 101 (2.19%) had missing N-terminal pro-B type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), whereas the remaining analysis variables had less than 1% missingness or were complete. We employed multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) in the R software to impute the missing values.

The participants were followed up at 1st, 6th, and 12th months after index discharge and thereafter annually until death, loss to follow-up, or the censoring date. If the participants were not available for the scheduled face-to-face interview, they would be telephone-interviewed by the trained staff at the national coordinating center. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The China PEACE 5p-HF Study conforms with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, was approved by the Ethics Committees of Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences Fuwai Hospital (Reference number: 2016–770), and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02878811).

Data Collection and Definitions

Data on sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, smoking status, and alcohol intake) were collected by physicians during the index hospitalization. Information on clinical characteristics at admission (height, weight, and waist circumference), New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, comorbidities (stroke, ischemic heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, anemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], cancer, and history of HF), and medication (renin-angiotensin system inhibitors [RASIs], mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists [MRAs], beta-blockers, and diuretics) was obtained from medical records of the index hospitalization. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was measured according to standard echocardiography. Blood samples were taken within 48 hours after admission and were assayed at the central laboratory for serum creatinine, NT-proBNP, hs-CRP, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol, triglyceride, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. Hemoglobin, neutrophil percentage, uric acid, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and serum albumin were assayed at local laboratories on admission. NPAR was defined as the ratio of neutrophil percentage (%) to serum albumin concentration (g/dL), with both components assayed at local laboratories at admission.

The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chinese version of the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease formula. NPAR was divided into quartiles (< 15.3, 15.3–17.6, 17.7–20.4, and ≥ 20.5). ln(NPAR) and ln(hs-CRP) represent the logarithmic transformations of both variables. NT-proBNP was stratified by tertiles into < 865, 865–2495, and ≥ 2496 ng/L. Hypertriglyceridemia was defined as triglyceride ≥2.3 mmol/L and hyperuricemia as uric acid > 420 μmol/L. HF was categorized into three subtypes based on LVEF as heart failure with preserved (HFpEF, LVEF ≥ 50%), mid-range (HFmrEF, LVEF 41–49%), and reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF, LVEF ≤ 40%). Renal function was categorized as eGFR < 60 and ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73m2. Anemia was defined as hemoglobin < 130 g/L in men or < 120 g/L in women. Comorbidities were identified by incorporating discharge diagnoses, laboratory results, and medical history.

Assessment of Outcomes

The primary outcomes were all-cause mortality (ACM) and cardiovascular mortality (CVM) within the whole follow-up duration. The secondary outcomes were major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and hospitalization for HF (HHF) within the 1-year follow-up duration. CVM encompassed sudden cardiac death and deaths due to heart failure, acute myocardial infarction (AMI), stroke, or other cardiovascular causes. MACE was defined as a composite of CVM, non-fatal AMI, and non-fatal stroke. Mortality data were ascertained via structured interviews with patients’ relatives, review of death certificates, and the national death registry. All data were centrally adjudicated by trained clinicians at the National Center for Cardiovascular Diseases.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) as appropriate according to their distributions assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Across ordered NPAR quartiles, trends were assessed using Cuzick’s test for continuous variables, the Cochran–Armitage test for binary variables, and the Mantel–Haenszel chi-square test for ordinal variables.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for the association of NPAR and hs-CRP with adverse outcomes, adjusted for age, gender, BMI, smoking and drinking status, stroke, ischemic heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, history of HF, anemia, LVEF, NYHA functional class, eGFR, NT-proBNP, uric acid, and triglyceride. These covariates were prespecified based on clinical relevance and adjustment strategies used in previous studies from the China PEACE 5p-HF cohort.25,26 The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Dose-response relationships of NPAR and hs-CRP with adverse outcomes were evaluated using restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots placed to ensure model flexibility. The correlation between NPAR and hs-CRP was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Effect modification was assessed by including a multiplicative interaction term between NPAR as a continuous variable and each prespecified covariate. Subgroup analyses were performed to estimate the association between NPAR and mortality across populations stratified by key covariates. The incremental value of NPAR in predicting mortality was evaluated by calculating Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) and compared with those of hs-CRP, serum albumin, and neutrophil percentage, either individually or in combination. At the prespecified 4-year prediction horizon, time-dependent continuous net reclassification improvement (cNRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated to quantify the incremental predictive value of NPAR. Model calibration, prediction error, and clinical utility were further assessed using calibration plots, Brier scores, and decision-curve analysis (DCA), respectively. Given the unclear mechanisms underlying the association between NPAR and mortality, mediation analyses were performed to investigate the potential mediating roles of hs-CRP and the blood urea nitrogen-to-creatinine ratio (BUN/Cr).

To assess the robustness of the findings, we performed the following sensitivity analyses: (1) examining the time-dependent association between NPAR and adverse outcomes at each follow-up time point; (2) calculating time-dependent C-index for the multivariable model in predicting mortality; (3) re-assessing the association between NPAR and the adverse outcomes after excluding patients with concurrent cancer or COPD; (4) additionally adjusting the multivariable Cox model for major medications; (5) using LASSO–Cox regression to separately select key covariates for ACM and CVM, followed by comparison of predictive performance between models with and without NPAR; and (6) using competing risk models where appropriate.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was defined as two-sided P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

The mean age of the cohort was 65 ± 14 years. Patients with lower NPAR tended to be younger and had higher BMI, waist circumference, LVEF, eGFR, lipid levels, and serum albumin, lower NYHA functional class, NT-proBNP, hs-CRP, BUN, and HbA1c, lower prevalences of ischemic heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, COPD, and anemia, and higher prevalences of smoking, alcohol consumption, and the use of diuretics, RASIs, MRAs, and beta-blockers. No significant differences in the prevalence of stroke or cancer or in uric acid levels were observed across NPAR quartiles (Table 1). Baseline characteristics did not differ between the final analytic cohort and those excluded, except for the prevalence of ischemic heart disease and the use of RASIs, MRAs, and diuretics (Table S1). NPAR exhibited a moderate positive correlation with ln(hs-CRP) (rs = 0.422, P < 0.001) (Figure S2).

Associations of NPAR and Hs-CRP with Adverse Outcomes

During a median follow-up of 4.61 years (IQR, 4.10–4.95), a total of 2156 all-cause deaths (47%) and 1499 cardiovascular deaths (32%) were recorded. Within the first year after index discharge, 1162 MACE (25%) and 1492 HHF (32%) events occurred.

NPAR showed positive graded associations with ACM and CVM. Compared with the lowest NPAR quartile, the fully adjusted HRs (95% CIs) for the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th NPAR quartiles were 1.29 (1.12–1.47), 1.38 (1.20–1.58), and 1.74 (1.52–1.99) for ACM, and 1.30 (1.10–1.53), 1.39 (1.18–1.63), and 1.72 (1.46–2.03) for CVM, respectively (all P < 0.001). Similar graded associations were observed for 1-year MACE and HHF. When modeled continuously, the associations between NPAR and adverse outcomes remained robust. Specifically, each 5-point increase in NPAR was associated with 24%, 22%, 14%, and 8% higher risks of ACM, CVM, 1-year MACE, and 1-year HHF, respectively. Similarly, hs-CRP showed positive graded associations with ACM and CVM. However, hs-CRP was associated with 1-year MACE but not with 1-year HHF (Figure 1).

Incremental Predictive Value of NPAR

Incorporating NPAR as a continuous variable into the primary multivariable models improved predictive ability for ACM and CVM. Specifically, C-indices increased from 0.693 to 0.701 for ACM and from 0.705 to 0.712 for CVM (both P < 0.001) (Figure 3). Calibration curves showed general agreement between predicted and observed risks for both the primary and NPAR models. Adding NPAR slightly reduced the Brier scores for ACM (0.203 to 0.199) and CVM (0.183 to 0.181), with modest net benefit on DCA (Figure S3). At the 4-year prediction horizon, the time-dependent cNRI was 9.90% for ACM and 9.30% for CVM, and the time-dependent IDI was 1.20% for ACM and 1.00% for CVM (all P < 0.001) (Table 2). Furthermore, NPAR outperformed hs-CRP, albumin, and neutrophil percentage in predicting mortality risk (all P < 0.050) and performed comparably to their combination. However, adding hs-CRP to NPAR model did not further improve prognostic performance (Figure 3). These findings were consistent after logarithmic transformation of both hs-CRP and NPAR (Figure S4).

Subgroup Analysis

The association between NPAR (continuous) and ACM was modified by diuretic use (P for interaction = 0.028), while the association between NPAR and CVM was modified by eGFR and diuretic use (P for interaction = 0.030 and 0.008, respectively). Specifically, diuretic users demonstrated lower HRs (95% CI) as compared with non-users [1.20 (1.13–1.27) vs 1.31 (1.21–1.42) for ACM; 1.17 (1.08–1.25) vs 1.33 (1.20–1.47) for CVM]. Patients with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73m2 exhibited lower HR (95% CIs) relative to those with eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73m2 [1.13 (1.03–1.23) vs 1.29 (1.19–1.39)]. No significant interactions were found between NPAR and other covariates for either ACM or CVM (Figure 4).

Mediation Analyses

Mediation analyses suggested that hs-CRP accounted for 12.77% of the association between NPAR and ACM (β = 0.006, P < 0.001) and 15.56% of the association between NPAR and CVM (β = 0.007, P < 0.001), whereas the BUN/Cr accounted for 6.25% of the association with ACM (β = 0.003, P < 0.001) and 8.16% of the association with CVM (β = 0.004, P < 0.001) (Figure 5).

Sensitivity Analyses

Time-dependent effect analyses revealed the associations between NPAR and mortality, MACE, and HHF waned but remained consistently significant as follow-up period extended (Table S2). Time-dependent C-index analyses showed additive predictive value of NPAR for both ACM and CVM across the entire follow-up period (Table S3). The associations remained significant by excluding patients with cancer and COPD, where inflammation frequently coexisted (Tables S4 and S5). Additional adjustment for RASIs, MRAs, beta-blockers, and diuretics did not materially alter the estimates (Table S6). Fine-Gray competing risk models reiterated the association between NPAR and CVM, 1-year MACE, and 1-month HHF (Table S7). Finally, after separate covariates selection and model fitting for ACM and CVM using LASSO–Cox regression, higher NPAR remained associated with increased risks of both outcomes. Comparisons of predictive performance between models with and without NPAR were also consistent with the primary analyses (Figures S5, S6 and Table S8).

Discussion

In this post hoc analysis of a nationwide, multicenter, prospective cohort, we observed a sustained positive dose-response association between NPAR and all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, MACE, and HHF in patients with AHF. NPAR demonstrated incremental predictive value for mortality superior to that of hs-CRP, albumin, and neutrophil percentage alone but comparable to their combination. These findings highlight the potential of NPAR as a novel marker for risk stratification in patients with AHF and support its further evaluation as a potential alternative to hs-CRP.

As a novel inflammatory marker, the application of NPAR in the AHF population remains underexplored. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale post hoc analysis of nationwide prospective cohort data to comprehensively examine the associations between NPAR and various adverse clinical outcomes in patients with AHF. Our findings demonstrated that NPAR was independently associated with multiple short- and long-term adverse outcomes in patients with AHF, including all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, major adverse cardiovascular events, and hospitalization for HF, whereas hs-CRP was more strongly associated with mortality. Moreover, we observed a moderate positive correlation between NPAR and hs-CRP, suggesting that NPAR may partly reflect systemic inflammatory activity in AHF. Previous studies have identified NPAR as an independent risk factor for adverse outcomes in various non-cardiovascular and cardiovascular conditions, such as COPD,14 stroke,15 cancer,16 kidney disease,23 myocarditis,17 atrial fibrillation,18 and CHF.19–21 By focusing specifically on the AHF population, our study extends the applicability of NPAR and supports its potential role as an integrative inflammatory and prognostic marker.

It is worth noting that the association between NPAR and mortality, as well as its prognostic performance, was strongest within the first year of follow-up. The association attenuated thereafter, but remained significant throughout the whole follow-up period, supporting the sustained prognostic relevance of NPAR. Therefore, NPAR may be useful for prognostic risk assessment in AHF and may help identify patients at increased risk of adverse events potentially related to acute or chronic inflammation and nutritional impairment. This interpretation was further supported by the reclassification and discrimination analyses, which showed that incorporating NPAR into mortality prediction models provided statistically significant but modest improvements in discrimination and risk reclassification.

As a multidimensional composite index, NPAR has shown better prognostic performance than CRP in several studies. Kurkiewicz et al21 and Karasu et al22 demonstrated that NPAR was superior to CRP in predicting mortality and disease progression in patients with advanced heart failure and coronary artery ectasia, respectively. Furthermore, in the non-cardiovascular population, Xu et al23 reported that NPAR also exhibited better predictive ability for mortality than CRP in patients with peritoneal dialysis. These findings collectively suggest that NPAR may offer broader prognostic information than individual biomarkers. A similar pattern was observed in our study. Specifically, NPAR showed better prognostic performance than hs-CRP, neutrophil percentage, and albumin individually, as well as combinations of hs-CRP with either albumin or neutrophil percentage, and performed comparably to the combination of these markers. Additionally, incorporating hs-CRP into the model containing NPAR did not provide further incremental prognostic value. These findings support NPAR as a potential alternative to hs-CRP for prognostic assessment. However, its clinical equivalence to hs-CRP has not yet been established and warrants external validation across a wider spectrum of populations.

Several potential explanations may account for this pattern. First, a single biomarker approach primarily reflects one pathophysiological domain. As AHF is a heterogeneous syndrome with diverse phenotypes, relying solely on an individual indicator for risk stratification and prediction of adverse outcomes may be limited.27,28 NPAR may integrate information related to both inflammation and nutritional status, thereby providing a broader representation of the inflammatory and nutritional burden associated with adverse outcomes in AHF. Second, individual biomarkers may be influenced by infection, volume overload, and hepatic or renal dysfunction,29 resulting in substantial biological variability. Simple combinations of multiple biomarkers may not fully account for their interactions and may remain susceptible to the limitations of each component. Overall, NPAR may reflect the combined influence of inflammation, nutritional metabolism, neuroendocrine activation, and oxidative stress.30,31 Whether driven by an elevated neutrophil percentage,32,33 decreased serum albumin,34,35 or both, a high NPAR may indicate a greater inflammatory burden and/or reduced nutritional reserve, thereby identifying patients at increased risk of adverse outcomes.

Given the heterogeneity in etiology, mechanisms of myocardial injury, and prognosis across HF phenotypes, we conducted subgroup analyses across three LVEF categories. The association between NPAR and mortality was generally consistent across LVEF levels, suggesting consistency across different HF phenotypes. Similarly, no statistically significant interactions were observed according to age, sex, NYHA functional class, history of HF, NT-proBNP level, or usage of medications to improve myocardial remodeling, further supporting the consistency of the observed association across these subgroups. Notably, the association between NPAR and mortality was attenuated among patients receiving diuretic therapy. This heterogeneity may be related to differences in congestion severity, volume status, treatment indication, or other clinical characteristics that may influence the components of NPAR.36 The association also appeared stronger among patients with lower NT-proBNP levels, possibly because inflammatory and nutritional abnormalities captured by NPAR contributed relatively more to prognosis when hemodynamic stress was less pronounced.

Subsequent mediation analyses indicated that hs-CRP and the BUN-to-creatinine ratio accounted for part of the association between NPAR and mortality, suggesting that inflammatory activation and volume-status dysregulation may represent potential pathways underlying the observed association. Nevertheless, more than 80% of the association was not explained by these measured mediators, suggesting that additional processes, such as nutritional impairment, immunometabolic dysregulation, and oxidative stress, may also be involved. Given the observational design and potential residual confounding, the mediation findings should be interpreted cautiously and require further validation.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a post hoc analysis, and the exclusion of patients who died during hospitalization or whose NPAR was unavailable may introduce selection bias. Second, neutrophil percentage and serum albumin were measured locally, whereas hs-CRP was measured centrally; inter-laboratory variability may undermine the robustness of our findings. Third, residual confounding, inherent to observational design or attributable to infection, liver disease, corticosteroid use, hemodilution, and other inflammatory or nutritional conditions which were unavailable, was unavoidable. Finally, because the present analysis was restricted to Chinese patients, the generalizability of our findings to populations outside China may be limited, although the cohort was nationally representative.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this post hoc analysis of a nationwide, multicenter prospective cohort showed that higher NPAR was associated with increased risks of both short- and long-term adverse outcomes in patients with AHF. NPAR showed better prognostic performance than hs-CRP alone and provided modest incremental prognostic information for mortality. Given its wide availability and low cost, NPAR may be useful for risk stratification and prognostic assessment and warrants further investigation regarding its potential role as an alternative to hs-CRP.
