Immunoglobulin G4–related disease (IgG4-RD) is a systemic, immune-mediated fibro-inflammatory condition capable of affecting virtually any organ system, often mimicking other inflammatory or neoplastic processes. This case presentation is particularly notable for several reasons, distinguishing it from many previously reported cases and broadening the known spectrum of IgG4-RD, thereby emphasizing the disease’s protean manifestations and the complex diagnostic and therapeutic challenges it presents.

Illustrative Case

A 23-year-old female with no significant past medical history presented with progressively worsening low back pain that began 4 months before presenting to our clinic. Initially, she attributed her symptoms to physical activity, but the pain persisted despite conservative measures and was later exacerbated after a trip. Initial MRI with contrast (Fig. 1) revealed a left psoas mass. Figure 1B demonstrates mild enhancement of the vertebral body but a preserved disc space, arguing against primary osteomyelitis at this stage. Axial views (Fig. 1C and D) confirmed the enhancing mass extending along the psoas muscle. However, C-reactive protein and the erythrocyte sedimentation rate were within normal ranges. The patient was referred to the neurosurgery service, where the lesion was deemed indeterminate, and a core needle biopsy was performed by radiology. Pathologic analysis revealed dense fibrosis and a lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate (Fig 2A and B) with an increased number of IgG4-positive plasma cells (Fig. 2C), consistent with IgG4-RD. The patient was started on high-dose prednisone (40 mg daily), with initial improvement. However, on tapering, her pain recurred, prompting treatment escalation to rituximab. Subsequent imaging performed 3 months posttreatment showed significant regression of the mass (Fig. 3). The patient’s pain improved markedly. However, she presented with severe worsening back pain 4 weeks later. Repeat MRI imaging performed 4 months after initial treatment demonstrated new, extensive involvement of the disc and adjacent vertebral bodies (Fig. 3C) with frank abscess formation in the psoas muscle (Fig. 3B). This distinct interval change, specifically the new discitis and osteomyelitis shown in Fig. 3C, prompted a second biopsy. A second biopsy and culture of the lesion identified methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus, and the patient was diagnosed with a superimposed bacterial infection. She was afebrile on presentation but had a leukocyte count of 13.9 × 103/µL. She was neurologically intact, and we felt that surgical intervention was not necessary. Rather, the left psoas abscess was aspirated by interventional radiology, and a pigtail catheter was placed. The patient showed improvement that was confirmed with imaging (Fig. 4). The patient was treated with 8 weeks of intravenous cefazolin, followed by a 4-week course of oral cephalexin. Final follow-up MRI obtained 10 months after initial presentation (Fig. 5) demonstrated resolution of the abscess and improved signal changes at L1–2. Figure 5A shows the collapse of the disc space consistent with resolved osteomyelitis, while Fig. 5B confirms the continued regression of the IgG4 mass and absence of abscess fluid. Her pain level was markedly improved. She resumed her normal activities and was planning to start as a 1st-year medical student. She will continue to be vigilantly followed. Discussion

Observations

This case highlights a complex presentation of IgG4-RD involving the vertebral disc, further complicated by a superimposed bacterial infection. Several aspects of this case stand out when compared with other reported instances of IgG4-RD.

First, the patient’s young age and sex as a 23-year-old female are a significant differentiating factor. IgG4-RD typically exhibits a male predominance with a peak onset in middle-aged to older individuals, often between 61 and 70 years.1 For instance, a detailed analysis of 125 patients with biopsy-proven IgG4-RD showed an average age at evaluation of 55.2 years and an average age at onset of 50.3 years, with 61% of patients being male.2 Similarly, other specific case reports of spinal involvement involve patients aged 60 or 62 years.3,4 The presentation in a young female adult underscores that IgG4-RD, although rare, should be considered across a broader age demographic than commonly appreciated.

Second, while spinal involvement in IgG4-RD, frequently manifesting as hypertrophic pachymeningitis leading to spinal cord compression, has been documented, the imaging evidence in this case explicitly highlighted involvement of the vertebral disc alongside prior inflammation of the vertebral bodies, specifically leading to discitis/osteomyelitis.3 While IgG4-related disease of the bone has been noted as a target organ, explicit reporting of disc involvement with discitis/osteomyelitis in IgG4-RD with a mass effect is a less common finding within the reported neurological manifestations.1,3 One report claimed to be the “first case in literature for IgG4-RD of the extradural tissue causing spinal compression,” demonstrating spinal compression by an extradural mass, but did not specify disc involvement leading to discitis/osteomyelitis.3 Our case, therefore, contributes to the expanding understanding of the varied anatomical involvement of IgG4-RD within the spine.

Perhaps most critically, our case was uniquely complicated by a superimposed methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus bacterial infection, resulting in a left psoas abscess and discitis/osteomyelitis. This occurred after initial improvement with glucocorticoids and subsequent worsening of symptoms despite rituximab treatment. While glucocorticoids are the first-line treatment for IgG4-RD, often yielding excellent initial responses, relapse rates are high, and rituximab is used for refractory or relapsing cases.2,4 However, the described worsening in our patient was not due to primary disease progression or relapse of the IgG4-RD itself, but rather a severe infectious complication, a crucial differentiation from other reported cases where treatment failure is typically related to the refractory nature of IgG4-RD. The presence of leukocytosis despite the patient being afebrile, followed by a positive culture, underscores this complication. This observation is particularly important as it highlights a potential pitfall in the management of IgG4-RD. Another case of spinal extradural involvement highlighted a dramatic response to systemic steroids with no recurrence after 5 years, implying an uncomplicated course.3 This case underscores that clinical deterioration in IgG4-RD patients on immunosuppressive therapy necessitates a thorough re-evaluation, actively ruling out concurrent infections rather than solely considering the disease‘s refractory nature or relapse.

Lessons

A comprehensive diagnostic approach and exclusion of mimickers, especially infections, is necessary. IgG4-RD’s diverse manifestations mean that it can mimic many other conditions, including neoplastic, infectious, and other inflammatory diseases, which require exclusion for optimal treatment.1 This case vividly illustrates the importance of not solely relying on an initial diagnosis of IgG4-RD when symptoms worsen, especially under immunosuppressive therapy. The recurrence of pain and mass regrowth after initial steroid response and despite rituximab prompted a second biopsy and culture, which was crucial in identifying the bacterial infection. This emphasizes that worsening symptoms may not always be due to refractory IgG4-RD but can be a complication, such as a superimposed infection, particularly when patients are on immunomodulating agents.

Broadening the understanding of spinal involvement in IgG4-RD is also necessary. While pachymeningitis is a known neurological manifestation of IgG4-RD, the involvement of the vertebral disc and bodies with a mass effect and subsequent discitis/osteomyelitis, as seen in our patient, expands the recognized ways that IgG4-RD can affect the spine. It reinforces that IgG4-RD can cause “diffuse or localized swelling or masses in single or multiple organs.”1,5 This observation highlights the need for clinicians to consider IgG4-RD in the differential diagnosis of complex spinal mass lesions, even those involving disc space.

Vigilance for superimposed infections during immunosuppressive therapy is vital. Glucocorticoids are the first-line treatment for IgG4-RD and are often very effective, with rituximab used for refractory or relapsing cases.2,4 However, as this case demonstrates, these treatments, while effective for IgG4-RD, can increase the risk of infections. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for infectious complications when patients on immunosuppression for IgG4-RD experience clinical deterioration, even if typical signs of infection, like fever, are absent, as our patient was afebrile despite leukocytosis and abscess formation. Early identification and treatment of such complications, as with the aspiration and antibiotic therapy for the abscess in this patient, are critical for patient recovery and preventing further morbidity.

Multidisciplinary management must be pursued in complex cases. Our patient required neurosurgical intervention for diagnosis along with medical management for IgG4-RD and interventional radiology and infectious disease management for the abscess. This highlights that optimal care for complex IgG4-RD presentations, particularly those involving the CNS and infectious complications, often necessitates a collaborative, multidisciplinary approach.

The findings from this single case report might not be generalizable to all patients with IgG4-RD. The rarity of IgG4-RD, particularly its atypical manifestations and complications, means that large-scale studies are challenging. However, these observations are highly relevant to clinical practice, emphasizing the broad and sometimes atypical presentations of IgG4-RD. They provide critical insights into diagnostic challenges and highlight the importance of considering superimposed infections as a cause of worsening symptoms in immunosuppressed patients with IgG4-RD, which can significantly alter management and improve patient outcomes.
