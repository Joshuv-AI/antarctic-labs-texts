Intrinsic non-Hermitian topological phases
Ken Shiozaki

Abstract
We study the interplay of non-Hermitian topological phases under point- and line-gap conditions. Using natural homomorphisms from line-gap to point-gap phases, we distinguish extrinsic phases, reducible to Hermitian or anti-Hermitian line-gapped phases, from intrinsic phases, which are genuinely non-Hermitian without Hermitian counterparts. Although classification tables for all symmetry classes were already presented in earlier work, the present paper develops a unified formulation and provides explicit computations for all internal symmetries.



1
Introduction

Non-Hermitian quantum systems have recently attracted broad interest across condensed matter physics, open quantum systems, photonic and acoustic platforms, and mechanical metamaterials, providing a setting for novel phenomena absent in Hermitian systems (see reviews
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
).
Compared with Hermitian systems, non-Hermitian systems exhibit several distinctive features: eigenvalues are generally complex, right and left eigenvectors differ, and Hamiltonians may fail to be diagonalizable, giving rise to exceptional points.
Moreover, spectral properties often show strong boundary dependence, which is called the non-Hermitian skin effect
[
6
]
, where bulk spectra under periodic and open boundary conditions differ drastically and an extensive number of eigenstates accumulate at the boundary. In such cases, the bulk-boundary correspondence must be reformulated in terms of the generalized Brillouin zone
[
6
,
7
]
.

These intrinsic properties of non-Hermitian systems require new topological principles, motivating systematic studies of gap conditions and classification schemes for non-Hermitian Hamiltonians.
One approach to non-Hermitian topology is based on the
point-gap
condition, where the spectrum avoids a reference point in the complex plane
[
8
]
.
The point gap gives rise to non-Hermitian-specific topological invariants, such as spectral winding numbers.
Alternatively,
line gaps
with respect to the real or imaginary axis
[
9
]
relate non-Hermitian phases to Hermitian topological insulators and superconductors, thereby connecting non-Hermitian classifications to those for Altland–Zirnbauer (AZ) classes
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
.

This naturally raises the question: what phenomena are genuinely intrinsic to non-Hermitian systems, i.e., not realizable within Hermitian settings?
For instance, gapless edge states of a Chern insulator remain stable under non-Hermitian perturbations, indicating their essentially Hermitian origin.
By contrast, the non-Hermitian skin effect emerges from the spectral winding number that vanishes in Hermitian systems and therefore represents a genuine non-Hermitian topological phenomenon
[
14
,
15
]
.

In this work, we propose a systematic framework to classify such intrinsic non-Hermitian topological phases by subtracting line-gap phases from point-gap phases. Since every line-gapped Hamiltonian is also point-gapped, this defines natural homomorphisms from line-gap to point-gap classifications. We identify the image of these maps with extrinsic phases, while point-gap phases outside the image are defined as intrinsic non-Hermitian topological phases.

Explicit examples of intrinsic non-Hermitian phases have been reported in various dimensions and symmetry classes: one- and two-dimensional
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
skin effects with a reciprocal symmetry
[
15
]
, an isolated exceptional point appearing on the surface
[
16
]
and magnetic field induced non-Hermitian skin effect in three dimensions
[
17
,
18
]
, boundary states associated with PT symmetry breaking trajectories
[
19
,
20
]
, and momentum-space defect structures
[
21
]
.
Systematic analyses of intrinsic non-Hermitian phases are also discussed in
[
20
,
19
]
.
As a related development, the recently established detachability of gapless boundary states in Hermitian systems
[
22
]
has been interpreted as an instance of imaginary-line-gap extrinsic phases
[
23
,
24
]
.

In this paper, we present a unified formalism that incorporates crystalline symmetries and provides explicit computations for all internal symmetry classes. In particular, we obtain classification results for all 38 independent non-Hermitian symmetry classes, distinguishing extrinsic from intrinsic phases (see Tables
5
–
11
).
While the classification results already appeared in Supplemental Material of Ref.
[
15
]
, the present work develops a comprehensive formulation together with detailed computations for internal symmetries.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
In Sec. 2 we review basic ingredients of non-Hermitian free-fermion systems, including the definition of symmetry, the Hermitization procedure, and gap conditions.
In Sec. 3 we introduce point-gap and line-gap topological phases and formulate the natural homomorphisms from line-gap to point-gap classifications.
Sec. 4 presents explicit computations of these homomorphisms for all internal symmetry classes and provides the resulting classification tables.
We summarize our findings and discuss future directions in Sec. 5.

2
Some generalities

In this section, we review several basic ingredients of non-Hermitian free-fermion systems: the definition of symmetry, the Hermitization procedure, and gap conditions.

2.1
Symmetry

We begin with symmetries of non-Hermitian matrices
H_{\bm{k}}
defined over a general parameter space
X
, with
{\bm{k}}\in X
.
Let
G
be a finite group, and denote its left action on
X
by

G\times X\ni(g,{\bm{k}})\mapsto g{\bm{k}}\in X
.
In a non-Hermitian setting, the Hamiltonian is not necessarily Hermitian, and symmetry transformations may involve complex conjugation, transpose, or their combination, the Hermitian conjugate.
To distinguish these possibilities, we introduce two homomorphisms

\displaystyle\phi,\kappa:G\to\mathbb{Z}/2=\{\pm 1\},

(1)

and adopt the following notation for a complex number or a matrix
M
:

\displaystyle M^{\phi_{g}}=\begin{cases}M&(\phi_{g}=1),\\
M^{*}&(\phi_{g}=-1),\end{cases}

(2)

\displaystyle M^{\phi_{g},\kappa_{g}}=\begin{cases}M&(\phi_{g}=1,\kappa_{g}=1),\\
M^{*}&(\phi_{g}=-1,\kappa_{g}=1),\\
M^{\top}&(\phi_{g}=-1,\kappa_{g}=-1),\\
M^{\dagger}&(\phi_{g}=1,\kappa_{g}=-1).\end{cases}

(3)

For a family of non-Hermitian matrices on
X
, we consider unitary matrices
u_{\bm{k}}(g)
and
v_{\bm{k}}(g)
, defined continuously on
X
, that satisfy the symmetry constraint

\displaystyle u_{\bm{k}}(g)\,H_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{g},\kappa_{g}}\,v_{\bm{k}}(g)^{\dagger}=H_{g{\bm{k}}},\quad g\in G.

(4)

Note that, unlike in the Hermitian case, it is not required that
u_{\bm{k}}(g)=v_{\bm{k}}(g)
.
For
g,h\in G
, comparing two decompositions,

\displaystyle H_{gh{\bm{k}}}

\displaystyle=u_{\bm{k}}(gh)H_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{gh},\kappa_{gh}}v_{\bm{k}}(gh)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}u_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,u_{\bm{k}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}\,H_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{gh},\kappa_{gh}}\left(v_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,v_{\bm{k}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}\right)^{\dagger}&(\kappa_{g}=1),\\
u_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,v_{\bm{k}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}\,H_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{gh},\kappa_{gh}}\left(v_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,u_{\bm{k}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}\right)^{\dagger}&(\kappa_{g}=-1),\end{cases}

(5)

we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{\bm{k}}(gh)^{\dagger}\,u_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,u_{\bm{k}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}\,H_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{gh},\kappa_{gh}}=H_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{gh},\kappa_{gh}}\,v_{\bm{k}}(gh)^{\dagger}\,v_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,v_{\bm{k}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}&(\kappa_{g}=1),\\
u_{\bm{k}}(gh)^{\dagger}\,u_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,v_{\bm{k}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}\,H_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{gh},\kappa_{gh}}=H_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{gh},\kappa_{gh}}\,v_{\bm{k}}(gh)^{\dagger}\,v_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,u_{\bm{k}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}&(\kappa_{g}=-1).\end{cases}

(6)

For simplicity, we assume that (
6
) imposes only trivial constraints.
In other words, we suppose there exists a
U(1)
phase
z_{\bm{k}}(g,h)
such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{\bm{k}}(gh)^{\dagger}\,u_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,u_{\bm{k}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}=\left(v_{\bm{k}}(gh)^{\dagger}\,v_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,v_{\bm{k}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}\right)^{\dagger}=z_{\bm{k}}(g,h)&(\kappa_{g}=1),\\
u_{\bm{k}}(gh)^{\dagger}\,u_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,v_{\bm{k}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}=\left(v_{\bm{k}}(gh)^{\dagger}\,v_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,u_{\bm{k}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}\right)^{\dagger}=z_{\bm{k}}(g,h)&(\kappa_{g}=-1),\end{cases}

(7)

holds.
The
U(1)
phase
z_{\bm{k}}(g,h)
must then satisfy the following cocycle condition depending on
{\bm{k}}\in X
:

\displaystyle z_{h,l}({\bm{k}})^{\phi_{g}}\,z_{gh,l}({\bm{k}})^{-1}\,z_{g,hl}({\bm{k}})\,z_{g,h}(l{\bm{k}})^{-1}=1,\quad g,h,l\in G,\ {\bm{k}}\in X.

(8)

2.2
Hermitization

For a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H_{\bm{k}}
, we double the matrix size and define the Hermitian Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}:=\begin{pmatrix}&H_{\bm{k}}\\
H_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}&\end{pmatrix}_{\sigma},

(9)

where the subscript
\sigma
indicates the block structure associated with the doubled internal degrees of freedom.
We denote by
{\sigma}_{\mu}

(\mu=0,x,y,z)
the Pauli matrices acting on this doubled space.
The corresponding doubled symmetry operators are defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{\bm{k}}(g):=\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}u_{\bm{k}}(g)&\\
&v_{\bm{k}}(g)\\
\end{pmatrix}&(\kappa_{g}=1),\\[5.16663pt]
\begin{pmatrix}&u_{\bm{k}}(g)\\
v_{\bm{k}}(g)&\\
\end{pmatrix}&(\kappa_{g}=-1),\end{cases}\quad g\in G,

(10)

so that the symmetry relations (
4
) and (
7
) can be written more compactly in the Hermitianized form:

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{\bm{k}}(g)\,\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{g}}\,\tilde{u}_{\bm{k}}(g)^{\dagger}=\tilde{H}_{g{\bm{k}}},\quad g\in G,

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,\tilde{u}_{\bm{k}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}=z_{\bm{k}}(g,h)\,\tilde{u}_{\bm{k}}(gh),\quad g,h\in G.

(11)

The doubling in (
9
) and (
10
) also implies the chiral symmetry

\displaystyle{\sigma}_{z}\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}{\sigma}_{z}=-\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}},

(12)

together with the algebraic relation between
\tilde{u}_{\bm{k}}(g)
and the chiral operator,

\displaystyle{\sigma}_{z}\tilde{u}_{\bm{k}}(g)=\kappa_{g}\,\tilde{u}_{\bm{k}}(g){\sigma}_{z},\quad g\in G.

(13)

Conversely, the conditions (
11
), (
12
), and (
13
) imply the original non-Hermitian symmetry relation (
4
).
Thus, the topological properties of a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H_{\bm{k}}
can be studied equivalently through its Hermitianized counterpart
\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}
.

2.3
Point-gap condition

For a family of non-Hermitian Hamiltonians
H_{\bm{k}}
, we would like to define a notion of topologically stable classification.
Since any two Hamiltonians
H_{\bm{k}},H_{\bm{k}}^{\prime}
can be linearly interpolated while preserving the symmetry (
4
) as

\displaystyle(1-t)H_{\bm{k}}+tH_{\bm{k}}^{\prime},\quad t\in[0,1],

(14)

no nontrivial classification can be obtained without an additional constraint.
To obtain a meaningful classification, we impose gap conditions as described below.

2.3.1
Singular-value gap

We can regard a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H_{\bm{k}}
as a family of linear maps

H_{\bm{k}}:{\cal H}_{1}\to{\cal H}_{2}
acting between two
N
-dimensional Hilbert spaces

{\cal H}_{1}\cong{\cal H}_{2}\cong\mathbb{C}^{N}
.
Under independent basis changes
U_{1},U_{2}
of
{\cal H}_{1},{\cal H}_{2}
, the Hamiltonian transforms as

\displaystyle H_{\bm{k}}\mapsto U_{1}^{\dagger}H_{\bm{k}}U_{2}.

(15)

Note that the set of singular values

\displaystyle{\sigma}(H_{\bm{k}})=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\,\big|\,\det\!\left(\lambda{\bf 1}_{N}-H_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}H_{\bm{k}}\right)=0\right\}

(16)

is invariant under the transformation (
15
).
If
{\cal H}_{1}
and
{\cal H}_{2}
are regarded as distinct physical spaces, it is natural to introduce a gap condition that is invariant under such basis changes.
To this end, consider a subset of the nonnegative real axis given by a single interval

I=[a,b]
,
0\leq a<b
, or by a disjoint union of intervals

I=[a,b]\cup[c,d]\cup\cdots
, with
0\leq a<b<c<d<\cdots
.
We then impose the singular-value gap condition

\displaystyle\left(\bigcup_{{\bm{k}}\in X}\sigma(H_{\bm{k}})\right)\cap I=\emptyset.

(17)

As a special case, taking
I=[0,\epsilon]
with some
\epsilon>0
, or equivalently requiring

\displaystyle\det H_{\bm{k}}\neq 0\quad\text{for all }{\bm{k}}\in X,

(18)

is called the point-gap condition
[
8
]
.

Since the condition
\det H_{\bm{k}}=0
is equivalent to
\det\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}=0
, the point-gap condition for a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H_{\bm{k}}
is equivalent to the usual gap condition for the Hermitianized Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}
, i.e., that
\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}
has no zero eigenvalues.

2.3.2
Constraint of symmetry matrices

If a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H_{\bm{k}}
satisfies the point-gap condition, then it is invertible for all
{\bm{k}}\in X
.
This imposes strong restrictions on the possible symmetry matrices
u_{\bm{k}}(g),v_{\bm{k}}(g)
.
In fact
[
25
]
,

\displaystyle\text{there exists a continuous family of invertible matrices $M_{\bm{k}}$ such that\ }v_{\bm{k}}(g)=M_{g{\bm{k}}}^{-1}\,u_{\bm{k}}(g)\,M_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{g},\kappa_{g}}.

(19)

(The Hamiltonian
H_{\bm{k}}
itself provides an example of such a family
M_{\bm{k}}
.)
In particular, for the subgroup
G_{\bm{k}}\cap\ker\kappa=\{g\in G\mid g{\bm{k}}={\bm{k}},\ \kappa_{g}=1\}
, this relation means that
u_{\bm{k}}(g)
and
v_{\bm{k}}(g)
are unitarily equivalent at fixed points
g{\bm{k}}={\bm{k}}
.
Conversely, if (
19
) fails to hold for the symmetry matrices, then
H_{\bm{k}}
never satisfies the point-gap condition over all of
X
, and the gap must close at some point
{\bm{k}}\in X
.

A similar argument applies to Hermitian systems independently of non-Hermitian ones.
Namely, if a Hermitian Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}
satisfies the symmetries (
11
), (
12
), (
13
) and has no zero eigenvalues for all
{\bm{k}}\in X
, then the symmetry operators
u_{\bm{k}}(g),v_{\bm{k}}(g)
defined by (
10
) necessarily satisfy (
19
).
Conversely, if the symmetry operators fail to satisfy (
19
), the Hermitian Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}
must become gapless somewhere.

2.3.3
Choice of symmetry matrices

In this work, we focus on genuinely non-Hermitian phases with point gaps that cannot be understood as Hermitian or anti-Hermitian systems.
Therefore, we always impose the point-gap condition, and the possible symmetry matrices
u_{\bm{k}}(g),v_{\bm{k}}(g)
cannot be chosen arbitrarily.

Without pursuing full generality in this paper, we fix the choice as follows.
For each group element
g\in G
, introduce a homomorphism

\displaystyle c:G\to\{\pm 1\},

(20)

and take

\displaystyle v_{\bm{k}}(g)=c_{g}\,u_{\bm{k}}(g),\quad g\in G.

(21)

Then the definition of the symmetry (
4

\displaystyle u_{\bm{k}}(g)\,H_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{g},\kappa_{g}}\,u_{\bm{k}}(g)^{\dagger}=c_{g}\,H_{g{\bm{k}}},\quad g\in G.

(22)

Apart from the conjugations specified by
\kappa_{g}

Each element

2.4
Line gaps

Introducing line-gap conditions allows us to distinguish, among point-gapped topological phases, which phases can be understood in terms of Hermitian systems.
The symmetry relation (
22
) relates eigenvalues in quartets
(E,E^{*},-E,-E^{*})
.
It is therefore natural to consider the following two types of line gaps
[
9
]
.
The spectrum of a matrix
M
is denoted by

{\rm Sp}(M)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\;|\;\det(\lambda{\bf 1}_{N}-M)=0\}
.

Real line gap.

We say that
H_{\bm{k}}
has a real line gap if its spectrum avoids the imaginary axis, namely,

\displaystyle\left(\bigcup_{{\bm{k}}\in X}{\rm Sp}(H_{\bm{k}})\right)\cap i\mathbb{R}=\emptyset.

(24)

Imaginary line gap.

We say that
H_{\bm{k}}
has an imaginary line gap if its spectrum avoids the real axis, namely,

\displaystyle\left(\bigcup_{{\bm{k}}\in X}{\rm Sp}(H_{\bm{k}})\right)\cap\mathbb{R}=\emptyset.

(25)

Note that in the above we chose the imaginary axis
i\mathbb{R}
and the real axis
\mathbb{R}
as the reference lines.
In the absence of symmetries relating all of
E
,
-E
,
E^{*}
, and
-E^{*}
, the reference line can in general be shifted.
For example, if only TRS-type symmetry is present, then the spectrum is symmetric with respect to the real axis.
In this case there is no distinguished reference point along the real direction, and one may instead take
a+i\mathbb{R}=\{\,a+iy\mid y\in\mathbb{R}\,\}
as the reference line for the real line gap, with any
a\in\mathbb{R}
.

An important fact is that:

Lemma 2.1

((Anti-)Hermitization)
.

If a Hamiltonian
H_{\bm{k}}
has a real line gap, then it can be continuously deformed, while preserving both the real line gap and the symmetry (
22
), into a Hermitian Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}_{\bm{k}}
with
H^{\prime}_{\bm{k}}=H^{\prime}_{\bm{k}}{}^{\dagger}
.
Similarly, if
H_{\bm{k}}
has an imaginary line gap, then it can be continuously deformed, while preserving both the imaginary line gap and the symmetry (
22
), into an anti-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}_{\bm{k}}
with
H^{\prime}_{\bm{k}}{}^{\dagger}=-H^{\prime}_{\bm{k}}
.

The proof follows the argument in Appendix D of Ref.
[
1
]
, so we only outline it here.
If
H_{\bm{k}}
has a real line gap, introduce closed contours
C_{\pm}
encircling all eigenvalues with
\Re(E_{\bm{k}})>0
and
\Re(E_{\bm{k}})<0
, respectively.
Define the projectors
P^{\pm}_{\bm{k}}:=\oint_{C_{\pm}}\frac{dz}{2\pi i}\frac{1}{z-H_{\bm{k}}}
.
The flattened Hamiltonian
Q_{\bm{k}}=P^{+}_{\bm{k}}-P^{-}_{\bm{k}}
has eigenvalues
\pm 1
and preserves the symmetry (
22
)
1
1

1

Because the symmetry operators satisfy
v_{\bm{k}}(g)=\pm u_{\bm{k}}(g)
, the group action
H\mapsto u_{\bm{k}}(g)\,H_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{g},\kappa_{g}}\,v_{\bm{k}}(g)^{\dagger}
extends, up to a sign, to any matrix function
f(H_{\bm{k}})
. In particular, one finds
u_{\bm{k}}(g)\left(\frac{1}{z-H_{\bm{k}}}\right)^{\phi_{g},\kappa_{g}}v_{\bm{k}}(g)^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{z\mp H_{g{\bm{k}}}}
. If
v_{\bm{k}}(g)=\pm u_{\bm{k}}(g)
does not hold, then it is not guaranteed that one can Hermitize (or anti-Hermitize) while preserving both the line gap and the symmetry
[

]
.
.
Decompose
Q_{\bm{k}}=H^{\prime}_{\bm{k}}+iH^{\prime\prime}_{\bm{k}}
, where
H^{\prime}_{\bm{k}}=\frac{Q_{\bm{k}}+Q_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}}{2},H^{\prime\prime}_{\bm{k}}=\frac{Q_{\bm{k}}-Q_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}}{2i}
. The linear interpolation
(1-t)Q_{\bm{k}}+tH^{\prime}_{\bm{k}}
,
t\in[0,1]
, preserves both the real line gap and the symmetry (

For the imaginary line gap, the same construction applies after replacing
H_{\bm{k}}\mapsto iH_{\bm{k}}

3
Intrinsic and extrinsic non-Hermitian topological phases

In this section, we define point-gap topological phases as well as real- and imaginary-line-gap topological phases, and formulate homomorphisms from line-gap phases to point-gap phases.

3.1
Symmetry

Based on Sec.
2
, we now re-summarize the symmetry structure adopted for non-Hermitian systems in this work.

Let
X
be the parameter space,
G
a finite group, and write
g{\bm{k}}
for the left
G
-action on
X
.
Let
\phi,c,\kappa:G\to\{\pm 1\}
be homomorphisms, and let
z_{\bm{k}}(g,h)
be a 2-cocycle satisfying (
8
).
The
G
-symmetry of a Hamiltonian
H_{\bm{k}}
is defined by

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{\bm{k}}(g)\,H_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{g},\kappa_{g}}\,u_{\bm{k}}(g)^{\dagger}=c_{g}H_{g{\bm{k}}},\quad g\in G,\\
u_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,u_{{\bm{k}}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}=z_{\bm{k}}(g,h)\,u_{\bm{k}}(gh),\quad g,h\in G,\end{cases}

(26)

where
u_{\bm{k}}(g)
are continuous unitary matrices on
X
.
Within the framework of
K
-theoretic classification, the symmetry matrices
u_{\bm{k}}(g)
are not fixed; instead, one considers all possible forms of
u_{\bm{k}}(g)
satisfying (
26
) for a given 2-cocycle
z_{\bm{k}}(g,h)
.
Thus, the data specifying a non-Hermitian system consist of the finite group
G
, the parameter space
X
, the left action

\displaystyle\alpha:G\times X\to X,\quad(g,{\bm{k}})\mapsto g{\bm{k}},

(27)

the homomorphisms
\phi,c,\kappa:G\to\{\pm 1\}
, and the 2-cocycle

z\in Z^{2}_{\rm group}(G,C(X,U(1)_{\phi}))
, where

\displaystyle(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z)

(28)

collectively define a non-Hermitian system
2
2

2

The group cocycle is defined as follows.
Let
C(X,U(1)_{\phi})
denote the abelian group of continuous
U(1)
-valued functions
f:X\to U(1)
, with pointwise multiplication
(ff^{\prime})_{\bm{k}}=f_{\bm{k}}f^{\prime}_{\bm{k}}
.
The left and right actions of
G
on
C(X,U(1)_{\phi})
are given, for
g\in G
, by

(g\cdot f)_{\bm{k}}=f_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{g}}
and
(f\cdot g)_{\bm{k}}=f_{g{\bm{k}}}
.
Then
z_{\bm{k}}
is a 2-cocycle in the group cochain complex
C^{*}_{\rm group}(G,C(X,U(1)_{\phi}))
.
.

3.2
Point-gap and line-gap topological phases

As noted in Sec.
2.3.1
, the existence of a point gap in
H_{\bm{k}}
is equivalent to the doubled Hermitian Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}
being gapped.
We can therefore adopt the well-established
K
-theoretic definition of topological phases
[
12
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
.

We redefine the symmetry operators for the doubled Hermitian Hamiltonian (
9
) as

\displaystyle U_{\bm{k}}(g):=u_{\bm{k}}(g)\otimes({\sigma}_{x})^{\frac{1-\kappa_{g}}{2}},\quad g\in G.

(29)

With this choice, the symmetry of the Hermitianized Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}
can be summarized as follows.

Definition 3.1

(Point-gap topological phase)
.

The classification group
K_{\rm P}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z)
of point-gap topological phases is defined as the
K
-group of gapped Hermitian Hamiltonians satisfying symmetry constraitns:

\displaystyle K_{\rm P}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z):\quad\begin{cases}U_{\bm{k}}(g)\,\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{g}}\,U_{\bm{k}}(g)^{\dagger}=c_{g}\tilde{H}_{g{\bm{k}}},\quad g\in G,\\
U_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,U_{{\bm{k}}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}=z_{\bm{k}}(g,h)\,U_{\bm{k}}(gh),\quad g,h\in G,\\
\sigma_{z}\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}\sigma_{z}=-\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}},\\
U_{\bm{k}}(g){\sigma}_{z}=\kappa_{g}{\sigma}_{z}U_{\bm{k}}(g),\quad g\in G.\end{cases}

(30)

Two remarks are in order.

The original symmetry matrices
u_{\bm{k}}(g),v_{\bm{k}}(g)
of the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H_{\bm{k}}
need not be expressible in the form of (
26

In general, they are defined via the restriction
U_{\bm{k}}(g){\sigma}_{z}=\kappa_{g}{\sigma}_{z}U_{\bm{k}}(g)
in (

Thus, the definition (
30
) encompasses a broader class of symmetries in non-Hermitian systems, including but not limited to those of the form (
26
).

Using the chiral symmetry
{\sigma}_{z}
, one can reinterpret (
30
) as the symmetry of a Hermitian Hamiltonian defined over the suspension of the parameter space.
Define the Hamiltonian on the suspension
SX=X\times[0,\pi]/(X\times\{0\}\cup X\times\{\pi\})
by

\displaystyle\tilde{H}^{\prime}_{{\bm{k}},\theta}:=\cos\theta\,\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}+\sin\theta\,\sigma_{z}.

(31)

Then
\tilde{H}^{\prime}_{{\bm{k}},\theta}
satisfies the symmetry constraints, and we get an alternative expression of the classification group
K_{\rm P}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z)
:

\displaystyle K_{\rm P}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z):\quad\begin{cases}U_{\bm{k}}(g)\,(\tilde{H}^{\prime}_{{\bm{k}},\theta})^{\phi_{g}}\,U_{\bm{k}}(g)^{\dagger}=c_{g}\tilde{H}^{\prime}_{g{\bm{k}},\,c_{g}\kappa_{g}\theta},\quad g\in G,\\
U_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,U_{{\bm{k}}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}=z_{\bm{k}}(g,h)\,U_{\bm{k}}(gh),\quad g,h\in G,\end{cases}

(32)

with the additional
G
-action on
\theta
given by
\theta\mapsto c_{g}\kappa_{g}\theta
.
If the
G
-action leaves a base point
{\bm{k}}_{*}\in X
fixed, then by suspension isomorphism the
K
-groups defined by (
30
) and (
32
) agree up to contributions from the base point.

We now define the classification groups for real- and imaginary-line-gap topological phases.
By Lemma
2.1
, if
H_{\bm{k}}
has a real line gap, it can be continuously deformed, while preserving both the real line gap and the symmetry (
26
), into a Hermitian Hamiltonian with
H_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}=H_{\bm{k}}
.
For the doubled Hermitianized Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}
, the Hermitian condition
H_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}=H_{\bm{k}}
is equivalent to imposing an additional chiral symmetry
{\sigma}_{y}\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}{\sigma}_{y}=-\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}
, or equivalently an additional
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry
{\sigma}_{x}\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}{\sigma}_{x}=\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}
.

Definition 3.2

(Real-line-gap topological phase)
.

The classification group
K_{\rm L_{r}}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z)
of real-line-gap topological phases is defined as the
K
-group of gapped Hermitian Hamiltonians satisfying

\displaystyle K_{\rm L_{r}}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z):\quad\begin{cases}U_{\bm{k}}(g)\,\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{g}}\,U_{\bm{k}}(g)^{\dagger}=c_{g}\tilde{H}_{g{\bm{k}}},\quad g\in G,\\
U_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,U_{{\bm{k}}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}=z_{\bm{k}}(g,h)\,U_{\bm{k}}(gh),\quad g,h\in G,\\
\sigma_{z}\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}\sigma_{z}=-\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}},\\
U_{\bm{k}}(g){\sigma}_{z}=\kappa_{g}{\sigma}_{z}U_{\bm{k}}(g),\quad g\in G,\\
\sigma_{y}\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}\sigma_{y}=-\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}},\\
U_{\bm{k}}(g)(i{\sigma}_{y})=\kappa_{g}(i{\sigma}_{y})U_{\bm{k}}(g),\quad g\in G.\end{cases}

(33)

Indeed, when
\{{\sigma}_{z},\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}\}=\{{\sigma}_{y},\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}\}=0
, the doubled Hamiltonian can be written as
\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}=H_{\bm{k}}\otimes{\sigma}_{x}
, with
H_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}=H_{\bm{k}}
.
The corresponding symmetry relation for
H_{\bm{k}}
, determined by (
29
), is

\displaystyle K_{\rm L_{r}}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z):\quad\begin{cases}u_{\bm{k}}(g)\,H_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{g}}\,u_{\bm{k}}(g)^{\dagger}=c_{g}H_{g{\bm{k}}},\quad g\in G,\\
u_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,u_{{\bm{k}}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}=z_{\bm{k}}(g,h)\,u_{\bm{k}}(gh),\quad g,h\in G,\end{cases}

(34)

which coincides with the standard symmetry condition in Hermitian systems.

Similarly, by Lemma
2.1
, if
H_{\bm{k}}
has an imaginary line gap, it can be continuously deformed, while preserving both the imaginary line gap and the symmetry (
26
), into an anti-Hermitian Hamiltonian with
H_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}=-H_{\bm{k}}
.
For the doubled Hermitianized Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}
, the anti-Hermitian condition is equivalent to imposing an additional chiral symmetry
{\sigma}_{x}\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}{\sigma}_{x}=-\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}
, or equivalently an additional
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry
{\sigma}_{y}\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}{\sigma}_{y}=\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}
.
Indeed, when
\{{\sigma}_{z},\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}\}=\{{\sigma}_{x},\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}\}=0
, one can write
\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}=h_{\bm{k}}\otimes{\sigma}_{y}
with
h_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}=-h_{\bm{k}}
.

Definition 3.3

(Imaginary-line-gap topological phase)
.

The classification group
K_{\rm L_{i}}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z)
of imaginary-line-gap topological phases is defined as the
K
-group of gapped Hermitian Hamiltonians satisfying

\displaystyle K_{\rm L_{i}}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z):\quad\begin{cases}U_{\bm{k}}(g)\,\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{g}}\,U_{\bm{k}}(g)^{\dagger}=c_{g}\tilde{H}_{g{\bm{k}}},\quad g\in G,\\
U_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,U_{{\bm{k}}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}=z_{\bm{k}}(g,h)\,U_{\bm{k}}(gh),\quad g,h\in G,\\
\sigma_{z}\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}\sigma_{z}=-\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}},\\
U_{\bm{k}}(g){\sigma}_{z}=\kappa_{g}{\sigma}_{z}U_{\bm{k}}(g),\quad g\in G,\\
\sigma_{x}\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}}\sigma_{x}=-\tilde{H}_{\bm{k}},\\
U_{\bm{k}}(g){\sigma}_{x}={\sigma}_{x}U_{\bm{k}}(g),\quad g\in G.\end{cases}

(35)

By redefining the Hamiltonian as
H\mapsto iH
, one obtains an alternative symmetry constraint suitable for imaginary-line-gap topological phases:

\displaystyle K_{\rm L_{i}}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z):\quad\begin{cases}u_{\bm{k}}(g)\,H_{\bm{k}}^{\phi_{g}}\,u_{\bm{k}}(g)^{\dagger}=\phi_{g}\,c_{g}\,\kappa_{g}\,H_{g{\bm{k}}},&g\in G,\\
u_{h{\bm{k}}}(g)\,u_{{\bm{k}}}(h)^{\phi_{g}}=z_{\bm{k}}(g,h)\,u_{\bm{k}}(gh),&g,h\in G.\end{cases}

(36)

It should be noted that chiral-type symmetries (
g\in G
with
(\phi_{g},c_{g},\kappa_{g})=(1,-1,-1)
) effectively act as unitary-type symmetries.

3.3
Intrinsic and extrinsic

It is clear that if a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H_{\bm{k}}
has either a real or an imaginary line gap, then it also has a point gap.
Thus, real- and imaginary-line-gap topological phases are always well-defined as point-gap topological phases.
This gives rise to the natural homomorphisms

\displaystyle f_{\rm r}:K_{\rm L_{r}}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z)\to K_{\rm P}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z),

(37)

\displaystyle f_{\rm i}:K_{\rm L_{i}}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z)\to K_{\rm P}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z).

(38)

These maps are concretely defined by simply forgetting the additional chiral symmetry.

Definition 3.4

(Homomorphisms from line-gap to point-gap phases)
.

The map
f_{\rm r}
is defined by forgetting the chiral symmetry
\sigma_{y}
in the relations (
33
).
Similarly, the map
f_{\rm i}
is defined by forgetting the chiral symmetry
\sigma_{x}
in the relations (
35
).

Explicit computations will be presented in the next section.

Among point-gap topological phases, those that can be deformed into real-line-gap or imaginary-line-gap topological phases while preserving the point gap are precisely the images of
f_{\rm r}
and
f_{\rm i}
.
We refer to these as real-line-gap extrinsic topological phases and imaginary-line-gap extrinsic topological phases, respectively.

Definition 3.5

(Extrinsic topological phases)
.

Within the point-gap classification group
K_{\rm P}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z)
, the subgroup of real-line-gap extrinsic topological phases is

\displaystyle{\rm im\,}f_{\rm r}={\rm im\,}[f_{\rm r}:K_{\rm L_{r}}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z)\to K_{\rm P}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z)].

(39)

Likewise, the subgroup of imaginary-line-gap extrinsic topological phases is

\displaystyle{\rm im\,}f_{\rm i}={\rm im\,}[f_{\rm i}:K_{\rm L_{i}}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z)\to K_{\rm P}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z)].

(40)

We define the line-gap extrinsic topological phases as the sum

\displaystyle{\rm im\,}f_{\rm r}+{\rm im\,}f_{\rm i}=\{\,x+y\mid x\in{\rm im\,}f_{\rm r},\ y\in{\rm im\,}f_{\rm i}\,\}.

(41)

Consequently, point-gap topological phases that do not lie in either image
{\rm im\,}f_{\rm r}
or
{\rm im\,}f_{\rm i}
are unique to non-Hermitian systems.
We call these intrinsic topological phases.

Definition 3.6

(Intrinsic topological phases)
.

Within
K_{\rm P}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z)
, the intrinsic topological phases are in the complement set

\displaystyle K_{\rm P}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z)\setminus({\rm im\,}f_{\rm r}+{\rm im\,}f_{\rm i}).

(42)

Equivalently, the classification of intrinsic phases is given by the quotient group

\displaystyle K_{\rm P}(G,X,\alpha,\phi,c,\kappa,z)/({\rm im\,}f_{\rm r}+{\rm im\,}f_{\rm i}).

(43)

4.1
54 internal symmetry classes

We first derive the set of independent internal symmetry classes.
Since we are concerned only with internal symmetries, we may take the Hamiltonian
H

The symmetry conditions, for a finite group

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u(g)\,H^{\phi_{g},\kappa_{g}}\,u(g)^{\dagger}=c_{g}H,\quad g\in G,\\

Our interest is in counting the number of inequivalent symmetry classes that close within an irreducible representation.

4.4
When both
f_{\rm r}
and
f_{\rm i}
are
\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}

As shown in the previous section, the homomorphisms from line-gap to point-gap phases
f_{\rm r},f_{\rm i}
can be independently obtained via suspension from the
0
-dimensional results.
However, their
relative relation
is not fixed in general.
This ambiguity occurs when both
f_{\rm r},f_{\rm i}
take the form
\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z},\ n\mapsto(n,n)
.
Once a basis of
\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}
is fixed, the maps
n\mapsto(n,n)
and
n\mapsto(n,-n)
are distinct homomorphisms.
Thus, in such cases, the relative relation between
f_{\rm r}
and
f_{\rm i}
must be determined separately in each spatial dimension.

The relevant cases are:
(i) odd dimensions for symmetry classes containing A+S as a subgroup, and
(ii) even dimensions for symmetry classes containing AIII+S
-
as a subgroup.

4.4.1
Odd dimensions: A+
S

For the symmetry class A+
S
, the Hamiltonian
H({\bm{k}})
can be written as

\displaystyle H_{\bm{k}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&h_{1}({\bm{k}})\\
h_{2}({\bm{k}})&0\end{pmatrix}_{\sigma},\qquad S={\sigma}_{z},

(238)

where
h_{1}({\bm{k}}),h_{2}({\bm{k}})
are independent.
In odd spatial dimensions
d=2n+1
, the winding numbers

\displaystyle W^{(2n+1)}_{i}=\frac{n!}{(2\pi i)^{n+1}(2n+1)!}\int{\rm tr\,}\bigl[h_{i}^{-1}dh_{i}\bigr]^{2n+1},\quad i=1,2,

(239)

characterize the
\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}
classification of point-gap phases.
With the real line gap, one may assume
H_{\bm{k}}
Hermitian without closing the gap, so that
h_{2}({\bm{k}})^{\dagger}=h_{1}({\bm{k}})
.
With the imaginary line gap, one may assume
H_{\bm{k}}
anti-Hermitian, so that
h_{2}({\bm{k}})^{\dagger}=-h_{1}({\bm{k}})
.
In both cases, the winding numbers satisfy
W^{(2n+1)}_{2}=-W^{(2n+1)}_{1}
.
Hence both homomorphisms coincide:
f_{\rm r}=f_{\rm i}
.
This can also be understood from the fact that class A+
S
does not involve complex-conjugation symmetries.
In the complex energy plane, the real and imaginary axes are not special lines; they are related by a continuous
90^{\circ}
rotation
H_{\bm{k}}\mapsto iH_{\bm{k}}
.

With an additional TRS commuting with
S
(AI+S
+
and AII+S
+
), the TRS
(T\otimes{\sigma}_{0})K
acts on each off-diagonal block
h_{i}({\bm{k}})
as
Th_{i}({\bm{k}})^{*}=h_{i}(-{\bm{k}})T,TT^{*}=\pm 1
.
For AI+S
+
and AII+S
+
, the point-gap
K
-groups in
d=1,5
are
\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}
, characterized by the winding numbers (
239
), and thus
f_{\rm r}=f_{\rm i}
as in A+S.

With an additional PHS anticommuting with
S
(D+S
-
and C+S
-
), the PHS
(C\otimes{\sigma}_{y})
acts on each off-diagonal block
h_{i}({\bm{k}})
as
Ch_{i}({\bm{k}})^{T}=-h_{i}(-{\bm{k}})C,CC^{*}=\pm 1
.
For D+S
-
and C+S
-
, the point-gap
K
-groups in
d=3,7
are
\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}
, characterized again by (
239
), and thus
f_{\rm r}=f_{\rm i}
.

4.4.2
Even dimensions: AIII+
S_{-}

For the symmetry class AIII+
S_{-}
, the Hamiltonian
H({\bm{k}})
is given by two Hermitian blocks
h_{i}({\bm{k}})

(i=1,2)

\displaystyle H({\bm{k}})=\begin{pmatrix}0&h_{1}({\bm{k}})\\

with

where

With the real line gap,

With the imaginary line gap,

f_{\rm r}
and

With an additional TRS commuting with both
S,{\Gamma}
(BDI+S
+-
and CII+S
+-

241

are the same as for AIII+S
-
.

With an additional TRS commuting with
S
but anticommuting with
{\Gamma}
(DIII+S
+-
and CI+S
+-

(T\otimes{\sigma}_{0})K
acts on each block, imposing Hermitian TRS
Th_{i}({\bm{k}})^{*}=h_{i}(-{\bm{k}})T,TT^{*}=\pm 1
.
In
d=0,4

\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}
, characterized by (
241

behave as in AIII+S
-
.

5
Summary and Outlook

In this work, we have formulated a general framework for classifying non-Hermitian topological phases.
By defining homomorphisms from real- and imaginary-line-gap phases to point-gap phases, we identified
intrinsic non-Hermitian topological phases as those that cannot be reduced to Hermitian counterparts.
We computed these homomorphisms explicitly for all 54 internal symmetry classes. The resulting tables provide a systematic account of intrinsic and extrinsic phases.

The present formulation establishes the algebraic structure that underlies intrinsic non-Hermitian
topology and clarifies its relation to Hermitian classifications. The results show that intrinsic phases
arise in a broad range of symmetry settings and dimensions, and include phenomena without Hermitian
analogs such as the non-Hermitian skin effect.

Several directions remain for future work. One is to extend the present framework to systems with
crystalline symmetries, where spatial symmetries impose further constraints on possible intrinsic phases.
Another is to investigate the interplay with interacting systems and stability beyond free-fermion models.
It is also of interest to study concrete physical realizations of the intrinsic phases identified here,
in photonic, acoustic, or other experimental platforms. These problems go beyond the present work and
are left for future studies.
