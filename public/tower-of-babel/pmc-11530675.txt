Association between hearing loss and gout based on National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey and Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Xiaopeng Fu, Xin Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract Hearing loss(HL) represents a significant public health concern. This study aimed to determine the association between hearing loss and gout and to elucidate the underlying causative mechanisms. Data for this study were obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and Mendelian randomization (MR) basic databases. Initially, baseline characteristics of individuals with and without gout were compared. A nonlinear relationship between pure tone audiometry (PTA) values and gout prevalence was confirmed through restricted cubic spline (RCS) curve analysis. Subsequently, hearing loss was categorized into different levels based on PTA values, and multiple logistic regression analysis was employed to calculate the impact of varying degrees of hearing loss on the risk of gout. Finally, MR analysis was conducted to further elucidate the causal relationship between hearing loss and gout. A total of 3,258 individuals were included in this study, with a gout prevalence of 3.7%. Significant differences were observed between the gout group and the non-gout group in variables such as age, gender, blood uric acid level, BMI, hypertension, and diabetes. RCS curve analysis revealed a significant nonlinear relationship between PTA values and gout risk, particularly when PTA values exceed a specific threshold, where the curve flattens. Based on different levels of hearing loss derived from PTA values, multiple logistic regression analysis demonstrated that mild and moderate hearing loss significantly increased the risk of gout, remaining statistically significant after adjusting for covariates (odds ratio (OR) = 2.10–3.48, P < 0.05). MR analysis further confirmed the causal relationship between hearing loss and gout. Inverse variance weighting (IVW) was employed as the primary method, revealing that both individuals with hearing difficulties (OR = 0.98, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.96–0.999, P = 0.012) and those without hearing impairment (OR = 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.04, P = 0.012) exhibited a significant causal relationship with gout. Goodness-of-fit tests and sensitivity analyses were used to verify the reliability of the results. Hearing loss has a significant causal relationship with an increased risk of gout, providing a new perspective for the prevention and management of gout. Introduction

Optimal auditory health is widely acknowledged as a crucial marker of overall well-being and life quality1, with numerous studies demonstrating a strong correlation between hearing loss and various health issues, such as cognitive decline, depression, and social isolation2. Nevertheless, in contemporary society, the incidence of hearing loss is escalating, emerging as a significant public health concern3. Data from the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) reveal that the prevalence of hearing loss is particularly pronounced among the elderly4. Thus, identifying risk factors for hearing loss is paramount for enhancing auditory health and preventing associated diseases5. Auditory health may be influenced by a myriad of factors, including sociodemographic status, health condition, personal behavior, psychological state, and environmental elements6. Hearing impairment has been linked to diabetes and cardiovascular disease7,8; however, the correlation between the severity of hearing loss and gout has not been adequately explored.A retrospective analysis involving 520 individuals demonstrated a potential link between hearing loss, hyperuricemia, and gout, with sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL) correlating with elevated blood uric acid levels9. Another retrospective cohort study utilizing US Medicare claims data10 revealed that among 1.71 million elderly individuals aged 65 and above, gout was significantly associated with an increased risk of hearing loss, with gout patients exhibiting a markedly higher risk of sudden hearing impairment compared to those without gout. Additionally, a South Korean retrospective study indicated that hyperuricemia was linked to an elevated risk of sudden hearing loss, further corroborating the potential association between gout and hearing impairment11. Some studies suggest that genetic factors may underlie the connection between hearing loss and gout, though these findings predominantly come from case reports and lack systematic verification12,13. A notable limitation of these studies is their focus on hearing impairment and deafness as outcome variables rather than on gout, and due to the cross-sectional and retrospective nature of these studies, causality cannot be inferred. Consequently, robust evidence supporting a causal relationship between hearing loss and gout remains insufficient, underscoring the need for further research to investigate causal risk factors between hearing loss and gout in real-world contexts. The U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a comprehensive national survey aimed at evaluating the health and nutritional status of U.S. residents. The NHANES database includes data on pure tone audiometry (PTA)14, allowing researchers to assess various degrees of hearing loss and investigate the epidemiological association between hearing loss and gout. Mendelian randomization (MR) is an epidemiological approach that employs genetic instrumental variables (IVs) to infer the causal effect of exposure factors on outcomes15. The MR method effectively mitigates the impact of residual confounding and reverse causation biases, as genetic variation is randomly assigned during gamete formation and remains unaffected by environmental or lifestyle factors16. This study integrates NHANES data with MR analysis methods, facilitating detailed stratification research on different levels of hearing loss while providing causal inference. The comprehensive application of dual evidence ensures the reliability and robustness of the results.Against this backdrop, this study employed cross-sectional data from NHANES covering the period from 2007 to 2018 to assess varying degrees of hearing loss using PTA values. A multifactorial analysis was conducted to investigate the relationship between hearing loss and gout. Concurrently, MR analysis was utilized to elucidate the causal effects of these risk factors. These findings may offer novel insights into the prevention of hearing loss and gout.

Materials and methods

Data source

This study utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), an interagency series of studies designed to assess the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. We screened core variables such as gout, pure tone audiometry (PTA), and the degree of hearing loss. Additionally, to control for potential confounding factors, variables such as age, gender, race, body mass index (BMI), fasting blood glucose, uric acid levels, and complications were considered. Ultimately, data were extracted from 49,667 participants from public databases, with 3,258 adults selected for a cross-sectional study.

The genetic association data for Mendelian randomization analysis were sourced from the UK Biobank via the IEU OpenGWAS project database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/), including genetic data from 2017–2018 for hearing difficulty (ID: ukb-a-257) with over 323,000 individuals, individuals with no hearing difficulty (ID: ukb-d-2247_0) with over 346,000 participants, and gout data for 462,933 participants (ID: ukb-b-13251). Specific information is provided in the supplementary file. All analytical data were derived from publicly available databases, and no additional informed consent was required.

Covariates and outcome

The covariates for the cross-sectional study include demographic data, physical examination results, laboratory test values, and questionnaire data from NHANES (2007–2018). These covariates encompass age, gender, race, BMI, blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, uric acid levels, lifestyle-related factors (such as smoking and drinking habits), and self-reported health status. For more details, see Table 1 and Figure Notes. This comprehensive background information controls for potential confounding factors, ensuring an accurate assessment of the relationship between hearing loss and gout.

Classification of hearing loss (HL)

In this study, the assessment of hearing impairment was based on the pure-tone average (PTA) obtained from audiometric tests conducted by NHANES. PTA is an average calculated by measuring the subject’s air conduction hearing thresholds at specific frequencies (500 Hz, 1 kHz, 2 kHz, 4 kHz). This process is performed in a soundproof mobile audiometry room using calibrated equipment. Conducted by trained technicians according to NHANES guidelines, hearing loss is categorized into six levels based on the WHO grading standards (2021 version)17: from level 0 (< 20 dBHL, normal hearing) to level 5 (≥ 81 dBHL, profound hearing loss/total deafness). These levels reflect the ability to hear sounds in both quiet and noisy environments.

Determination of gout

In this study, the diagnosis of gout was determined by a combination of self-reported physician diagnosis, measurement of blood uric acid levels, and gout-specific medication use, as referenced in previous research18. Participants were asked, “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had gout?” to self-report whether they had been diagnosed with gout. Serum uric acid (SUA) levels were used as a secondary diagnostic criterion, with hyperuricemia defined as SUA levels ≥ 420 µmol/L (7 mg/dL)19. Additionally, the use of gout medications, such as Allopurinol and Febuxostat, was examined to ensure accurate identification of gout cases in the study.

Exposure factors and outcomes in MR analysis

To further investigate the potential causal relationship between hearing impairment and gout, this study employed Mendelian randomization analysis (MR). Hearing impairment was analyzed as a binary variable (Yes/No), using data from the UK Biobank (ID: ukb-a-257 for hearing difficulty, ID: ukb-d-2247_0 for no difficulty). Gout was similarly treated as a binary outcome, based on self-reported physician diagnosis (ID: ukb-b-13251). Genetic variants (SNPs) closely associated with hearing loss and gout were selected as instrumental variables.

Statistical analysis

This study compared the baseline characteristics of gout and non-gout populations. Descriptive statistical analysis of baseline characteristics was performed using the gtsummary package of R software20. Differences in continuous variables were assessed based on the normality of data distribution using independent samples t-test or Wilcoxon rank sum test, with results expressed as mean (standard deviation [SD]) or median (interquartile range). Comparisons of categorical variables were conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, with results presented as frequencies (percentage [n%]). To explore the potential nonlinear relationship between the hearing impairment severity and the risk of gout, this study utilized the restricted cubic spline (RCS) method21. RCS can illustrate complex nonlinear trends among variables in the model, providing a flexible approach to explore the relationship between PTA and gout risk. RCS analysis was implemented using the rms package in R. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each variable affecting hearing impairment, adjusting for identified potential confounders. The goodness of fit of the model was evaluated by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, while the overall model test and prediction accuracy were assessed. For Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with hearing loss were selected as instrumental variables based on a genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10–8) and pruned for linkage disequilibrium (R2 < 0.001). Missing data were handled in the original GWAS datasets. The primary MR estimator used was the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method to assess the causal effect of hearing loss on gout, supplemented by MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode methods22. MR analysis relies on three core assumptions: relevance, independence, and exclusion restriction23. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran's Q statistic, and pleiotropy was examined using the MR-Egger intercept test. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to verify the robustness of the results. All statistical analyses were conducted in R using the TwoSampleMR package, with two-sided P values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Inclusion of individual baseline data

This study initially included 49,667 individuals from the NHANES data spanning 2007 to 2018. After excluding 20,667 individuals with unavailable PTA data and gout prevalence, 19,600 individuals with missing hearing loss data, and 6,142 individuals with other important covariates missing (such as complications, smoking, and drinking status), 3,258 participants were ultimately included. The overall prevalence of gout in the analyzed population was 3.7%. Univariate analysis revealed significant differences between gout and non-gout populations in variables such as age, gender, blood uric acid levels, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, and hearing loss. Additionally, while lifestyle indicators such as drinking habits, smoking status, and education level showed differences, these differences did not reach statistical significance. The baseline characteristics of the included participants are detailed in Table 1.

Logistic regression and nonlinear PTA relationships

To further investigate the potential relationship between the degree of hearing impairment and the risk of gout, this study first employed the restricted cubic spline (RCS) method to analyze the nonlinear relationship between PTA and the risk of gout. The RCS analysis results revealed that as PTA levels increase, the risk of gout exhibits a significant nonlinear increasing trend. Particularly when PTA exceeds a certain threshold, the RCS curve gradually flattens. This finding indicates a complex nonlinear correlation between the degree of hearing loss and the increased risk of gout. The RCS results are depicted in Fig. 1. Based on the RCS analysis results and existing clinical guidelines for hearing impairment24, we categorized PTA into different levels of hearing loss and subsequently conducted a multivariable logistic regression analysis to examine the relationship between each level and the risk of gout. This analysis adjusted for all identified potential confounders, including demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, and lifestyle habits. The model’s goodness of fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, which indicated a good fit. After adjusting for covariates, the analysis identified an independent effect of the hearing impairment severity on gout risk. Notably, mild and moderate hearing loss were found to significantly increase the risk of gout and remained statistically significant after adjusting for various covariates, suggesting that these degrees of hearing loss may serve as important predictors of gout risk. Although the increased risk associated with moderate and profound hearing loss did not fully reach statistical significance in some models, a clear risk trend was observed across all levels of hearing impairment, with mild to profound hearing loss showing statistically significant associations in certain models. MR analysis

In the Mendelian randomization analysis of this study, 21 SNPs were selected as instrumental variables (IVs) for hearing difficulty, and 23 SNPs were selected as IVs for no hearing difficulty. The F values of all selected IVs were ≥ 10, thereby avoiding weak instrumental bias25. Inverse variance weighting (IVW) was used as the primary method, and the results indicated a significant causal relationship between hearing impairment and gout (OR = 0.97935, P = 0.012), compared to individuals without hearing impairment. Additionally, the results from other methods, such as simple mode and weighted mode, also supported the main findings, with consistent directionality of results. See Figs. 2 and 3 for details.

Sensitivity analysis of MR results

Sensitivity analysis was conducted to verify the robustness of the MR results, including Cochran’s Q statistic and MR-Egger intercept test. The Cochran’s Q test results did not reveal significant heterogeneity, and the MR-Egger intercept test did not indicate the presence of horizontal pleiotropy, thereby enhancing the reliability of our study findings (see the supplementary file). Additionally, through leave-one-out analysis, we examined the impact of individual SNP removal on the overall analysis results. The findings demonstrated that regardless of which SNP was removed, the analysis results remained stable, indicating that our MR estimation possesses high reliability. The sensitivity analysis results are shown in Fig. 4.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated risk factors linking hearing loss and gout using data from NHANES participants from 2007 to 2018. Additionally, we applied the Mendelian randomization (MR) method to evaluate the causal effect of hearing loss on gout.We conducted an RCS analysis based on the PTA value. After adjusting for various confounding factors, the PTA value demonstrated a significant nonlinear relationship with the risk of gout. When the PTA value exceeded a specific threshold, the curve flattened, indicating that the impact of hearing loss on the risk of gout tends to stabilize beyond a certain point. Based on this, we referred to the diagnostic criteria for hearing loss and performed multifactor logistic regression analysis under different models. The results showed that, after adjusting for covariates, the degree of hearing loss independently affected the risk of gout. Specifically, both mild and moderate hearing loss were associated with a significantly increased risk of gout, and this association remained statistically significant after adjusting for various covariates. This suggests that both mild and moderate hearing loss may serve as important predictors of gout risk. Although the increased risk associated with moderate and profound hearing loss did not fully reach statistical significance in all models, a clear risk trend was still observed. Therefore, the severity of hearing impairment may be directly related to an increased risk of gout. To our knowledge, this is the first large-sample study to investigate the causal association between hearing loss and gout in a real-world population and at the genetic level. Sensitivity analyses validated the reliability of our findings. This study provides direct evidence of a causal relationship between varying degrees of hearing loss and gout. The findings are based on a large-scale cross-sectional investigation and further supported by Mendelian randomization analysis. Hearing loss constitutes a significant public health concern, intricately linked to numerous health complications25. Prior research has postulated that potential mechanisms through which hearing loss may influence gout include: the social isolation and psychological stress engendered by hearing loss, which may precipitate metabolic disorders, thereby elevating the risk of gout; and elevated uric acid levels, which may detrimentally impact the vasculature of the inner ear, resulting in hearing impairment13,26.Regarding the impact of hearing loss on the onset of gout, this study concentrates on gout as the outcome rather than hearing loss. The cross-sectional study results indicate that the degree of hearing loss can serve as a risk predictor for the onset of gout. We posit that early and mid-term intervention and timely treatment of hearing loss may help prevent hyperuricemia from progressing to gout. Additionally, genetic factors may play a pivotal role in the relationship between hearing loss and gout27,28. The MR analysis in this study confirmed the causal relationship between hearing loss and gout. Notably, this study utilized PTA values from the NHANES database and verified the significant association between hearing loss and gout risk through multivariate logistic regression analysis across different models. This finding offers new insights into the prevention and treatment of hearing loss and underscores the importance of hearing health management in reducing the risk of gout. However, due to the nature of cross-sectional studies, the associations found do not necessarily indicate causation29. Although the results of the MR analysis support the hypothesis that hearing loss contributes to gout, further longitudinal studies are required to verify this finding and elucidate its underlying mechanisms. Additionally, studies in diverse populations should explore the relationship between hearing loss and gout to uncover potential racial and gender differences.The principal advantage of this study lies in its integration of a large-sample cross-sectional survey with GWAS data. NHANES, a nationally representative survey, offers extensive multi-year sample data, providing real-world evidence of the association between varying degrees of hearing loss and gout. To address the limitations of cross-sectional studies, we further elucidated the causal relationship and resolved the inherent issue of reverse causality of confounding factors by employing MR analysis. The combined application of NHANES and MR analyses furnishes reliable and unbiased estimates of the causal effect of hearing loss on gout.This study also has certain limitations. The diagnosis of gout is based on self-reported data, and despite comprehensively considering medication and other factors, self-selection, memory, and recall biases may still be present. Additionally, the populations in the NHANES and GWAS databases are predominantly of European and American ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other ethnicities. Future studies should focus on including more diverse populations through multi-ethnic cohorts and global biobanks to validate these findings and explore potential ethnic differences in the association between hearing loss and gout. Another limitation is that we did not investigate the specific biological mechanisms underlying the association between hearing loss and gout. It is hypothesized that chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation associated with gout may contribute to auditory system damage, particularly through vascular and neuroinflammatory pathways30. Elevated uric acid levels, characteristic of gout, could lead to microvascular impairment in the inner ear, affecting hearing function. Further research is needed to explore these potential mechanisms by examining inflammatory markers, vascular health, and metabolic pathways. Additionally, animal models and molecular studies could help to clarify the direct impact of hyperuricemia on auditory function.

Conclusions

Ultimately, we conclude that hearing loss is significantly associated with an increased risk of gout, and this association is causal. These findings offer new insights into the prevention and management of gout. Focused attention and timely treatment of hearing loss, particularly mild and moderate hearing loss, may significantly reduce the risk of gout and prevent hyperuricemia from progressing to gout.
