Assessing future invasion risk by Solanum viarum Dunal in Northwestern Himalaya to inform early detection and management
Authors: Saima Jan, Abdul Rahim PP, Mujtaba Aamir Bhat, Safikur Rahman, Zishan Ahmad Wani, Arif Tasleem Jan, Yashwant S Rawat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the potential distribution patterns of invasive species under changing climatic conditions is important for biodiversity conservation and ecological risk assessment. The present study aims to predict the current and future distribution of Solanum viarum Dunal in Jammu and Kashmir, India, using species distribution modelling approach. The MaxEnt model demonstrated excellent performance, with an Area under Curve (AUC), True Skill Statistics (TSS) and Boyce Index values of 0.96, 0.77 and 0.84, respectively, indicating high predictive accuracy. Mean annual air temperature (Bio1) emerged as the most influential variable, contributing 55% to the predicted distribution of S. viarum. Under current climatic conditions, 12.38% and 1.39% of the area fall under medium and high suitability classes, respectively, across the Jammu region, whereas most of the Kashmir Valley falls within low suitability zones. Future climate projections indicate a notable increase in the potential distribution of S. viarum with a varied extent of suitable habitat under different future climatic scenarios. Under the SSP245 scenario, the species is projected to experience a range change of 29.03% by 2050, increasing to 160.84% by 2070. Under the SSP585 scenario, the suitable area is predicted to undergo range expansion of 131.89% and 58.99% by 2050 and 2070, respectively. Furthermore, expansion of medium and high suitability areas within protected areas is projected under both climatic scenarios, with a more pronounced increase under SSP245 by 2070 (~ 169%). The projected expansion of S. viarum highlights the need for proactive management planning. Integrating model outputs with local ecological knowledge, land-use information, and systematic monitoring is likely to enhance the effectiveness of management interventions. Introduction

Invasive alien plants are species that have been introduced by humans outside their natural geographic ranges, where they cause economic losses, environmental damage, or harm to human, animal, and plant health1,2. These species often possess traits such as rapid growth, high reproduction success, and strong competitive ability, outcompeting native plants by increasing their populations and spreading into new areas3. Anthropogenic disturbances coupled with climate change are the major factors that alter habitat conditions and accelerate the spread of these species by creating new opportunities for their invasion4. Invasive species tolerate a wide range of climates and have strong dispersal abilities, allowing them to expand their ranges quickly with changing environmental conditions1,5. As a result, invasive plants pose a significant threat to native biodiversity by changing the structure of plant communities and disturbing ecosystem functioning6,7. Further, these species cause disruption of plant–pollinator interactions, reduced agricultural productivity, increased management and restoration costs, and adverse effects on human health and local livelihoods8–14. With the increasing ecological and socioeconomic impacts of invasions, the field of invasion biology has gained more attention in recent years15. Plant invasions represent a cross-boundary conservation challenge, requiring coordinated management and policy responses informed by robust scientific evidence16. Ricciardi et al.17 identified four key priorities for improving invasion science: better prediction of how non-native species behave and impact ecosystems in new environments, understanding of how climate change and other drivers together influence invasions, strengthening taxonomic expertise for accurate detection and risk assessment, and greater international cooperation in biosecurity to manage invasions arising from global dispersal networks. Building on these priorities, effective invasion management requires timely monitoring and region-specific risk evaluation, particularly in biodiversity-rich and socially vulnerable areas where invasion impacts can be most severe18.

The Indian Himalayan Region (IHR), a part of Himalayan Biodiversity Hotspot, has about 41% of its land under forests, accounting for nearly one-third of the India’s total forest cover19. The region supports nearly half of India’s flowering plant species with around 30% endemics, highlighting its high ecological significance20. However, plant invasions are increasingly recognized as a major threat to these fragile mountain ecosystems, which are already vulnerable to climate change, habitat alteration, and growing human pressures21. Urbanization, expanding road networks, and unsustainable tourism have further accelerated the spread of invasive species across the Himalaya22,23. However, despite the high invasion risk and the region’s exceptional biodiversity, scientific information on the diversity, spatial distribution, ecological drivers, and impacts of invasive plants in the IHR remains limited23. This lack of reliable data highlights the urgent need for detailed research and better monitoring tools to understand and manage plant invasions in this fragile region. Species Distribution Modelling (SDM) has emerged as an important approach for invasion research, as it helps predict the potential distribution of species based on environmental conditions24. SDM allows researchers to identify suitable habitats, detect emerging invasion hotspots, and project how species may expand under future climate scenarios, providing valuable guidance for early intervention and management25. Thus, the present study aims to assess the potential distribution of Solanum viarum Dunal in Jammu and Kashmir (J&K) under current and future climatic scenarios. The study aims to assess potential range expansion, contraction, and persistence of S. viarum using range change analyses. In addition, the study evaluates the extent of invasion within protected areas under both current and future conditions to assess the potential implications for conservation priorities. The findings of the study provide valuable insights for early intervention, habitat management, and conservation planning aimed at limiting the spread of S. viarum in vulnerable Himalayan landscapes.

Methodology

Study area

J&K is situated in the north-western part of the Indian Himalaya (Fig. 1). It encompasses major physiographic divisions, including the Jammu plains, the Pir Panjal range, the Kashmir Valley, and the Greater Himalayan region, which collectively form key watersheds, draining into the tributaries of the Indus River system26. Topographically, it exhibits distinct physiographic heterogeneity, shaped predominantly by the Himalayan orogeny. Climatically, J&K exhibits strong spatial variability driven by altitudinal gradients and orographic influences. The southern and southwestern parts experience a subtropical climate with hot summers and relatively cool winters, receiving the bulk of their precipitation during the southwest monsoon27. In contrast, the Kashmir Valley remains largely shielded from the monsoon due to the barrier effect of the Pir Panjal range, resulting in a temperate climate28. J&K is characterized by marked altitudinal variation and climatic heterogeneity, resulting in a wide spectrum of vegetation formations. The vegetation types range from subtropical scrublands and broad-leaved forests in the Jammu plains and adjoining foothills to temperate mixed and conifer-dominated forests at intermediate elevations, where species of Pinus, Cedrus, Abies and Picea are prominent, followed by subalpine shrub communities, alpine grasslands and sparsely vegetated zones beyond the upper tree limit29,30. This complex vegetation mosaic sustains considerable biological diversity, encompassing numerous angiosperms, gymnosperms, pteridophytes and medicinally important plant taxa, many of which exhibit restricted distributions30. However, biodiversity in the region is facing increasing pressure from human-induced and environmental drivers, notably deforestation, habitat fragmentation, overharvesting, intensive grazing, climate change, and changes in land use31,32. These drivers collectively diminish forest integrity, modify species assemblages and create disturbed environments that facilitate ecological degradation33.

Target species

Solanum viarum Dunal, a member of the family Solanaceae, is a perennial shrub native to South America34 and has since become widely distributed across the globe. The plant is easily recognizable by its dense, sharp spines typically 1–2 cm long and varying from white to yellowish that cover both stems and leaves (Fig. 2). The alternately arranged leaves are prominently lobed with a grey-green pubescent upper surface and a lighter greenish-white underside35. Flowers are white with cream-colored filaments that develop into distinctive berries, which are green with white mottling when unripe and turn bright yellow upon maturation. The spherical fruits, approximately 2–3 cm in diameter, contain a remarkably high seed load, typically ranging from 190 to 385 seeds per fruit, which are dispersed by livestock, wild animals, and hydrochory36. Collectively, the plant’s morphological features, high fecundity, and adaptability enable it to invade agricultural landscapes and natural ecosystems posing significant ecological and management challenges37. It has become extensively invasive as a result of increased international trade, climate change, and land-use change38. The United States Department of Agriculture classified it as a troublesome invasive weed in 15 states in 2012, while Mexico listed it as a quarantine pest in 2018, followed by its inclusion in Chile’s A1 quarantine pest category in 201939. It causes significant biodiversity loss, health impacts, and economic costs across different ecosystems, raising serious concern despite its medicinal value in India39.

Species distribution modelling

Occurrence data

Extensive field surveys were conducted between 2022 and 2024 across the study area to record the occurrence of S. viarum. Species presence locations were documented using a Global Positioning System (GPS), and representative plant specimens were collected along with field photographs to ensure accurate morphological identification. In total, 40 geo-referenced presence points were collected through field observations. Occurrence points of S. viarum were also downloaded from Global Biodiversity Information Facility40 and the primary occurrence point dataset was further supplementary with 3 records representing the study area from this source. Thus, a total of 43 occurrence points were collected that were carefully screened to ensure spatial and taxonomic accuracy, as species occurrence data often contain inherent biases and potential errors41. Records lacking spatial coordinates, exhibiting evident identification inaccuracies, or representing dubious distributional information were excluded. To further minimize spatial autocorrelation and potential model overfitting caused by clustered sampling in easily accessible areas42, spatial rarefaction procedure was applied. Occurrence points were filtered using a 1 × 1 km grid resolution, consolidating multiple nearby records into single representative points using SDMtoolbox in ArcGis 10.8. Following this refinement process, 39 spatially rarefied occurrence points were retained for subsequent species distribution modelling (Table S1).

Environmental data

Nineteen bioclimatic variables representing baseline climatic conditions for the period 1981–2010 were acquired from the WorldClim version 2.1 database (https://www.worldclim.org/), at a spatial resolution of approximately 1 km (30 arc-seconds). To evaluate potential future shifts in species distribution under changing climatic conditions, projections for two time periods (2050 and 2070) of the HadGEM3-GC31-LL were obtained from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6). The model was selected because previous studies have identified it as the best performing CMIP6 model for simulating seasonal precipitation patterns over the Western Himalayan region43. Furthermore, several studies conducted in the Himalayan region have used HadGEM3-GC31-LL for habitat suitability and species distribution modelling under future climate change scenarios44–46. Two Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) scenarios, SSP245, representing a moderate emission trajectory, and SSP585, representing a high-emission or pessimistic scenario were selected for each time period. Topographic variables including elevation, slope, and aspect were derived from the Digital Elevation Model (DEM) downloaded at 30 arc-second resolution from the WorldClim database (https://www.worldclim.org/). All environmental layers were standardized to a common spatial resolution and coordinate reference system (WGS 1984) using ArcMap 10.8. The processing workflow included resampling, clipping, and masking of raster layers to align precisely with the study area boundary. To minimize multicollinearity and improve model efficiency, a Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed among the 22 environmental variables (19 bioclimatic and 3 topographic). Multicollinearity among predictor variables can substantially compromise model reliability and interpretability47,48. Among pairs of variables showing high correlation (|r| > 0.7), the variable with greater ecological relevance or higher contribution to model performance was retained. Following this filtering procedure, six key predictors were selected for the final MaxEnt modelling: mean annual temperature (Bio1), temperature seasonality (Bio4), annual precipitation (Bio12), precipitation of driest quarter (Bio17), precipitation of warmest quarter (Bio18), and slope.

MaxEnt modelling

In the present study, species distribution modelling for S. viarum was carried out using the Maximum Entropy algorithm (MaxEnt, version 3.4.4 downloaded from https://biodiversityinformatics.amnh.org/open_source/maxent/). MaxEnt is widely regarded as one of the most robust approaches for modelling species distributions, particularly when presence-only data are available49,50. In this study, the MaxEnt model was executed using the auto-feature setting. Ten replicate runs were performed under a cross-validation framework to assess the robustness and stability of the model. The model was trained using 70% of the occurrence records, while the remaining 30% were used for testing. A total of 10,000 background points were used, along with a maximum iteration limit of 2000, a default prevalence of 0.5, and the 10th percentile training presence threshold rule. Variable importance was evaluated using the jackknife test48. Model performance was assessed using the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve, and Area Under the Curve (AUC). AUC values close to 1 indicate high discriminatory power, reflecting the model’s ability to reliably distinguish between suitable and unsuitable habitats51. For further model validation, True Skill Statistics (TSS) was calculated following Allouche et al.52 and the Boyce Index was computed using the ‘ecospat’ package in R software following Serva et al.53. The continuous habitat suitability output from MaxEnt (ranging from 0 to 1) was reclassified into discrete suitability categories to facilitate interpretation. Suitability values were grouped into three classes using probability thresholds of < 0.40 (low suitability), 0.40–0.75 (medium suitability), and > 0.75 (high suitability). The classified maps were projected to equal area coordinate system, and the area corresponding to each suitability category was subsequently calculated. Further, the classified suitability maps for current and future scenarios were overlaid with the protected area shapefile, and the area falling inside and outside protected areas was also subsequently calculated. In addition, to assess how the distribution of S. viarum may shift under future climates, the habitat suitability maps for current and projected scenarios were converted into binary form using a threshold of 0.5. These binary maps were subsequently compared using a raster calculator, with each future projection evaluated against the current distribution to quantify spatial changes in suitability, following Wani et al.48. Range shifts were summarized into four categories: Loss, areas suitable under current conditions but projected to become unsuitable; Gain, areas currently unsuitable that are projected to become suitable in the future; Present, areas remaining suitable across both periods; and Absent, areas unsuitable under both conditions. Results

Model performance and variable importance

The MaxEnt model demonstrated excellent predictive performance, with an AUC value of 0.96 ± 0.02 (Fig. 4). Further, excellent model performance was depicted by a TSS of 0.77 and a Boyce Index of 0.84. The variable importance assessment revealed that Bio1 (mean annual air temperature) was the dominant predictor, accounting for approximately 55% of the total contribution to the distribution of S. viarum. This variable also showed the highest permutation importance, emphasizing its strong influence on model behavior (Table 1).

Predicted distribution of Solanum viarum under current climatic conditions

Under the current climatic conditions, the potential distribution of S. viarum in Jammu and Kashmir shows a marked regional pattern (Fig. 6). The species is predicted to occur predominantly across the Jammu region, whereas most of the Kashmir Valley falls within low suitability zones. 86.22% of the study area is fall under low suitability, while 12.38% and 1.39% of the area falls under medium and high suitability, respectively (Table 2). Spatially, the high suitability areas are concentrated in the central and northwestern parts of Udhampur district, the southeastern region of Reasi, the southern and southeastern areas of Ramban, northern Jammu, northwestern Doda, eastern Rajouri adjoining Reasi and portions of southern Kishtwar bordering Doda. Medium suitability areas include central and southern Rajouri, central Kathua, Samba, Poonch, central and northwestern Jammu, central and northern Mirpur, and the western and southern parts of Muzaffarabad. Protected area overlay analysis indicated that, under current climatic conditions, approximately 2,506.7 km² of the protected area network in Jammu and Kashmir is predicted to encompass habitats of high and medium suitability for the species (Table 2). Notably, Nandni Wildlife Sanctuary, Surinsar–Mansar Wildlife Sanctuary, Sheshacharya Conservation Reserve, and Thein Conservation Reserve were identified as protected areas containing suitable habitats.

Predicted distribution of Solanum viarum under future climatic scenarios

Future climate projections indicate notable increase in the potential distribution of S. viarum in J&K with varied extent of suitable habitat under different future climatic scenarios (Fig. 7).Under SSP245 climatic scenarios, the medium and high suitable areas show moderate increase to 12.73% and 1.59%, respectively for the year 2050. However, for the year 2070, a drastic increase has been predicted with medium and high suitability increases up to 18.87% and 3.22%, respectively (Table 2). Under the SSP585 climatic scenarios, the medium and high suitable areas show drastic change for the year 2050 with increase up to 18.03% and 2.80% respectively. For the year 2070, under this pessimistic scenario, medium and high suitable areas increase up to 13.43% and 2.12%, respectively. Further, the overlay analysis indicated a marked increase in medium and high suitability areas of S. viarum within protected areas under future scenarios. Under SSP245, the extent of medium and high suitability habitat within protected areas is projected to increase to 5,191.21 km² by 2050 and further to 6,326.38 km² by 2070. Similarly, under the SSP585 scenario, medium and high suitability areas for S. viarum within protected areas are predicted to expand to 6,744.01 km² by 2050, and 5,252.22 km² by 2070.In addition to the expansion of suitable habitats within protected areas that are currently projected to harbour S. viarum, several new protected areas including Jasrota Wildlife Sanctuary, Sudh Mahadev Conservation Reserve, and the Surinsar-Mansar Conservation Reserve are projected to harbour suitable habitats for the species in the future (Table 2).

Range change dynamics

Predictions under future climatic scenarios indicate substantial shifts in the geographic range of S. viarum relative to its current distribution. Under the SSP245 scenario, the species is projected to experience an overall range change of 29.03% by 2050, increasing to 160.84% by 2070. Similarly, under the SSP585 scenario, S. viarum is predicted to undergo range changes of 131.89% and 58.99% by 2050 and 2070, respectively. Spatially explicit projections reveal distinct patterns of habitat gain, loss, and persistence across districts (Fig. 8). By 2050 under SSP245, habitat gains are projected in parts of southern Kathua, central and southeastern Udhampur, northern Samba, central and northwestern Rajouri, and northern Poonch. In contrast, limited habitat losses are predicted in central Poonch, Mirpur, and parts of Ramban and Kishtwar, while areas such as Reasi, central and northwestern Udhampur, parts of Jammu, and central Kathua are expected to remain stable. Under SSP585 for the same period, gains extend toward southern Kathua, northern Samba, northeastern Jammu adjoining Udhampur, and western, southwestern, and central Rajouri, as well as eastern Mirpur, with losses remaining minimal and confined to isolated pockets of Ramban and Kishtwar. By 2070 under SSP245, habitat gains become more pronounced, particularly in southern Kathua, northern Samba, northeastern Jammu bordering Udhampur, southern Reasi, extensive areas of Rajouri (central and northern), eastern Mirpur, and northern Poonch. Habitat losses remain minor and are restricted to parts of Ramban, Kishtwar, and central Kathua. Under SSP585 by 2070, gains are predicted in southern Kathua, central and southeastern Udhampur, northern Samba, central and northwestern Rajouri, and northern Poonch, while losses remain limited to parts of central Poonch, Mirpur, Ramban, and Kishtwar. Regions such as Reasi, central and northwestern Udhampur, Jammu, and central Kathua are projected to remain stable. Overall, these results indicate that S. viarum is likely to undergo substantial range expansion and spatial redistribution under future climate scenarios, with the magnitude and spatial pattern of change varying across emission pathways and time horizons.

Discussion

Species distribution modelling (SDM) is extensively applied in areas such as reconstructing historical range dynamics, analyzing biodiversity patterns, habitat restoration, delineating species distributions and ecoregions, and forecasting the potential impacts of future environmental change24,54. Further, SDMs plays a crucial role in invasive species management by identifying spatial patterns of invasion risk and highlighting areas that may require targeted monitoring or preventive action55,56. In this study, MaxEnt model was employed to predict the habitat suitability and range dynamics of an invasive plant species, S. viarum in J&K. Model performance was robust as indicated by high values of the AUC, TSS and Boyce Index, reflecting excellent predictive accuracy53,57–59. The variable importance analysis identified mean annual air temperature (Bio1) as the most influential predictor, contributing approximately 55% to the final model. Mullahey et al.60 demonstrated that S. viarum exhibits maximal seed germination under warm fluctuating temperatures and shows rapid vegetative development under warm conditions, indicating a strong physiological affinity for warmer climates. The response curve reveals that the habitat suitability of S. viarum remains extremely low at lower temperature values, indicating that cooler climates are unfavorable for the species. A sharp increase in suitability is observed beyond approximately 14–16 °C, suggesting a critical thermal threshold for establishment and growth. Overall, the curve demonstrates that S. viarum is a warm-adapted species, with temperature acting as a major limiting factor for its distribution. This pattern is consistent with the sensitivity of S. viarum to low temperatures and its ability to regenerate and germinate under warmer conditions61,62. The model revealed a clear spatial pattern under current climatic conditions, with the Jammu region exhibiting substantially higher habitat suitability than the Kashmir Valley. Warmer subtropical climate and lower elevations in the Jammu landscape likely facilitate the establishment of S. viarum. Further, the species is more prevalent in the disturbed habitats like road sides and wastelands63. This trend is consistent with broader invasion patterns reported for India, where alien plants show higher prevalence in low-elevation and disturbed foothill regions64,65.

Under current climatic scenarios, S. viarum is predicted to have narrow suitability in J&K, with most suitable habitats in the sub-tropical climates. Kashmir Valley has been predicted to be mostly unsuitable for the species. However, under all future climatic scenarios, the species is predicted to undergo a drastic range change with a significant increase in suitable areas. Waheed et al.66 also predicted an increase in the suitability of S. viarum under future climate scenarios in Pakistan based on Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs). Although range gains are projected under both future climatic scenarios and time periods, the particularly large overall range change under SSP245 by 2070 suggests that S. viarum may transition from an extended establishment phase to accelerated expansion as climatic conditions become increasingly favourable. Empirical and theoretical studies in invasion ecology indicate that invasive species often experience a lag between initial establishment and rapid spread, driven by demographic, genetic, and environmental processes67–69. During such lag periods, populations may persist at low densities while accumulating genetic diversity, or overcoming environmental constraints before entering a phase of rapid expansion70,71. Climate change can further act as a key trigger by relaxing physiological constraints and creating novel suitable habitats, effectively shortening lag periods and facilitating rapid spread1. Similar climate-driven expansion patterns have been reported for other invasive weeds, including Parthenium hysterophorus and Lantana camara, with both species projected to expand their potential distribution under future climatic conditions as warming temperatures enhance habitat suitability and broaden their ecological ranges72,73. Thus, early detection and rapid response are critical for preventing the large scale spread of invasive plants74.

A key concern emerging from this study is the increasing climatic suitability of S. viarum within existing protected areas of the region. Although expansion of medium and high suitability areas within protected areas is projected under both climatic scenarios, the increase is more pronounced under SSP245 by 2070, with a substantial rise of approximately 169%. Protected areas currently affected, such as Nandni Wildlife Sanctuary and Surinsar-Mansar Wildlife Sanctuary may experience further encroachment under future climatic conditions. Moreover, model projections indicate that suitability is likely to extend into additional protected areas, including Jasrota Wildlife Sanctuary, Gambhir Mughlian Goral Conservation Reserve, and Sudh Mahadev Conservation Reserve. The potential establishment of S. viarum within these ecologically sensitive landscapes poses substantial challenges for biodiversity conservation. Invasive plant species within protected areas can out-compete native flora, alter habitat structure, and undermine conservation objectives75. Given that a considerable proportion of the protected area network is projected to fall within medium to high suitability areas under future scenarios, targeted surveillance, early detection, and timely management interventions will be essential76. As S. viarum is more frequently associated with disturbed and degraded habitats63, habitat restoration and minimizing anthropogenic disturbances especially with protected areas may substantially reduce its establishment and spread. In areas where populations are already established, physical removal and periodic weeding before flowering and seed set may help suppress local spread and reduce propagule pressure. Regular monitoring and rapid eradication of newly established populations, especially along roadsides, forest edges, and disturbed sites, should also be prioritized for effective long-term management. Furthermore, identification of high-risk zones can facilitate early detection, targeted eradication, and habitat restoration efforts. Therefore, integrating SDM outputs with local ecological knowledge, land-use information, and systematic monitoring is likely to enhance the effectiveness of management interventions77. Moreover, climate-resilient conservation strategies must explicitly consider the increasing vulnerability of protected areas to biological invasions. The findings of this study provide a strong scientific basis for developing early detection and rapid response (EDRR) strategies to limit the further spread of S. viarum and maintain conservation priorities, while recognizing that the predictions represent potential rather than realized distributions.

Limitations of the study

The MaxEnt model was implemented using default parameter settings and auto-feature selection without additional tuning of feature classes. Previous studies have shown that parameter optimization can improve model performance and reduce the risk of overfitting78,79. Furthermore, considering the relatively smaller geographical extent of the study area, the use of a single well-performing Global Circulation Model (GCM) was considered appropriate for regional-scale ecological forecasting. Nevertheless, the use of multiple GCMs could further improve the representation of climatic uncertainty in future studies. The modelling framework also relied primarily on bioclimatic and topographic variables, while other potentially important factors such as soil parameters, land-use change, biotic interactions, and human footprints were not included. The inclusion of these variables could improve model accuracy by providing a better representation of habitat conditions and environmental factors influencing species distribution. Studies have revealed that models with biotic, topographic and climatic variables show better performance80. In addition, dispersal constraints and migration buffers were not considered, thereby assuming unlimited dispersal potential of the species under future climate scenarios. Previous studies have shown that incorporating dispersal processes can improve the ecological realism and accuracy of species distribution projections81,82. Consequently, the predicted distribution patterns should be interpreted as potential climatic suitability rather than exact future realized distributions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the effectiveness of MaxEnt modelling in predicting the potential distribution of S. viarum under current and future climatic scenarios. The strong influence of climatic predictors in shaping its distribution highlights the climatic sensitivity of the species and its potential for rapid expansion under warming conditions. Current projections indicate that S. viarum is largely restricted to the Jammu region; however, future scenarios suggest substantial range expansion, with increasing suitability across multiple districts and several protected areas. The projected encroachment of S. viarum into key conservation landscapes poses significant ecological risks, as invasive species can undermine native biodiversity, alter ecosystem functioning, and challenge protected area management objectives. Importantly, identifying future invasion hotspots, including newly vulnerable protected areas, provides a critical opportunity for early detection, rapid response, and long-term monitoring. Overall, this work underscores the value of integrating species distribution modelling into invasive species management and conservation planning. As climate change continues to reshape ecological niches, proactive, climate-informed management strategies supported by robust predictive tools will be essential to safeguard biodiversity and maintain ecosystem resilience in the region.
