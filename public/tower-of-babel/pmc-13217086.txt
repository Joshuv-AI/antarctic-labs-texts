Incidence, predictors, and outcomes of malignant cerebral edema after acute basilar artery occlusion thrombectomy
Authors: Xiangjun Xu, Thanh N Nguyen, Zhongming Qiu, Yingjie Xu, Zhixin Huang, Wei Li, Wen Sun, Yapeng Guo, Junfeng Xu, Shunfu Jiang, Hai Hu, Fang Xie, Guangui Yang, Hongmei Xu, Guoqiang Xu, Xianjun Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMalignant cerebral edema (MCE) following basilar artery occlusion (BAO) thrombectomy is a critical, yet understudied, complication.ObjectiveTo investigate the incidence, predictors, and clinical impact of MCE in patients with BAO after endovascular thrombectomy (EVT).MethodsIn this retrospective analysis of the national multicenter PERSIST Registry (2422 patients with BAO treated with EVT recruited from 65 Chinese centers, 2015–2022), MCE was defined as Jauss score ≥4 on follow-up imaging. Multivariable logistic regression identified predictors of MCE and its association with 90-day outcomes. A favorable outcome was defined as achieving a modified Rankin Scale score of 0–3 at 90 days.ResultsA total of 1883 patients who met the eligibility requirements were included. MCE occurred in 39.6% (746/1883) of patients. Independent predictors included admission systolic blood pressure ≥160 mm Hg (aOR=1.26, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.54), prolonged procedure time (aOR=1.01, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.01), BATMAN score ≥7 (aOR=0.68, 95% CI 0.55 to 0.84), and vertebral V4 occlusion (vs proximal BA: aOR=0.68, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.89). Successful reperfusion reduced MCE risk (aOR=0.57, 95% CI 0.42 to 0.75). Diabetes mellitus exhibited an inverse association (aOR=0.78, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.99). MCE predicted lower odds of favorable outcome (aOR=2.41, 95% CI 1.94 to 3.01; P<0.001) and higher mortality (aOR=1.85, 95% CI 1.51 to 2.27; P<0.001) at 90 days.ConclusionsMCE complicates 39.6% of patients with BAO after EVT and portends catastrophic outcomes despite successful recanalization. Shortening the procedure time, increasing the likelihood of first-pass success, and optimal admission blood pressure management might be targets to decrease MCE for patients with BAO undergoing EVT.

Introduction

Recent randomized controlled trials demonstrated that combining endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) with standard medical therapy produced superior functional outcomes compared with standard medical therapy alone in patients presenting with acute basilar artery occlusion (BAO).1 2 However, up to 54–77% of patients die or survive with significant disability despite treatment.14 Malignant cerebral edema (MCE), a devastating complication after reperfusion, occurring in 10–25% of cases with 80% mortality, significantly worsens prognosis following EVT in acute anterior circulation large vessel occlusion stroke.5 6 Less attention has been paid to post-EVT MCE in patients with BAO. Studies to date were of limited sample size or were performed in select patient populations from randomized trials.7 8

For patients with cerebellar infarction with swelling, the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association recommends that prompt suboccipital craniectomy with dural expansion should be performed in patients exhibiting neurological deterioration.9 Therefore, timely recognition and prediction of patients at high risk of MCE and early initiation of decompressive surgery may further improve patient outcomes. Several biomarkers to predict MCE in patients with large vessel occlusion in the anterior circulation have been identified.5 10 11 However, due to differences in the histological, anatomical, and autoregulation between the anterior and posterior circulation,12 findings derived from anterior circulation stroke may not be generalizable to the posterior circulation. Hence, further studies specifically focusing on posterior circulation stroke are warranted.

Therefore, leveraging a multicenter registry, we evaluated the impact of MCE on EVT outcomes in patients with BAO and explored its predictors.

Methods

Study design and participants

The PERSIST Registry (Posterior Circulation Ischemic Stroke; ChiCTR2000033211) was a two-stage, multicenter, retrospective study of consecutively recruited patients exhibiting an acute, symptomatic, radiologically verified BAO, with 65 participating stroke centers in China, between December 2015 and June 2022. Details of the enrollment process for this study have been published previously.13 This study was approved by the ethics committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC) in Hefei, China, and local institutional review boards of each center also gave approval. Patient consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study. This study adheres to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) statement.14

Patients were included based on the following criteria: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) patients with confirmed BAO underwent EVT; (3) time interval from last known well to BAO confirmation was within 24 hours; (4) pre-stroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score ≤3; (5) For age ≤80 years, posterior circulation Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (pc-ASPECTS) ≥6; for age >80 years, pc-ASPECTS ≥8. Exclusion criteria included: (1) baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score <10; (2) lost to 90 days follow-up. Patients were dichotomized into the non-MCE group and the MCE group according to whether they had MCE or not.

Procedures and data collection

Baseline clinical characteristics, radiological profiles, procedural parameters, and clinical outcomes were systematically collected according to standardized criteria. Stroke etiology was classified according to the TOAST criteria (Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment). The collateral circulation status was quantified by the basilar artery on CT angiography (BATMAN) score, defining a BATMAN score ≥7 as good collateral circulation and BATMAN score <7 as poor collateral circulation.15 A favorable outcome was defined as a mRS score 0–3 at 90 days, unfavorable outcome was defined as a mRS score 4–6 at 90 days, and successful recanalization as modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction grades (mTICI)2 b, or 3.

MCE was defined by the Jauss scale ≥4 from non-contrast CT within 72 hours after EVT.16 The Jauss scale ranges from 0 to 9 and was calculated by adding the following items: (1) degree of fourth ventricle compression: 0=no compression, 1=unilateral compression, 2=midline shift, 3=invisible; (2) degree of quadrigeminal cistern compression: 0=no compression, 1=mild ipsilateral asymmetric compression to the infarct, 2=moderate with bilateral compression, 3=severe bilateral compression resulting in invisible quadrigeminal cistern; (3) degree of dilatation of the temporal horn of the lateral ventricle: 0=normal, 1=mild, 2=moderate, 3=severe.17 All imaging was assessed by two trained interventionalists who were blinded to the clinical information. Potential discrepancies were settled in a consensus reading.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared between patients with and without MCE using Student’s t-test for normally distributed data (assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test) or the Mann-Whitney U test for non-parametric comparisons; categorical variables were analyzed with the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test when expected frequencies fell below 5. Statistical modeling of MCE predictors was performed through a two-stage analytical approach. Variables demonstrating marginal associations (P<0.10) in preliminary univariate analyses were subsequently incorporated into multivariate regression models using backward elimination for optimal variable selection. Furthermore, we conducted stratified analyses to elucidate differential risk patterns in MCE progression among patient cohorts achieving successful recanalization, with particular emphasis on identifying potential clinical implications of therapeutic heterogeneity. For all analyses, a two-tailed value of P≤0.05 was considered significant. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 26.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, USA) software was used for all statistical analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics and incidence of MCE

Among 2422 patients in the PERSIST Registry, 539 did not meet the eligibility criteria (372 patients with baseline NIHSS score <10, 90 patients with baseline pc-ASPECTS <6, 52 patients lost to 90-day follow-up, 23 patients with baseline pc-ASPECTS <8 in patients aged >80 years, and 2 patients aged <18 years). Finally, 1883 patients (541 women (28.7%), median (interquartile) age 65 (56–73) years, median (interquartile) pc-ASPECTS, 9 (8–10)) were included in the present study. Among these patients, 762 (40.5%) achieved a favorable outcome at 90 days, and 776 (41.2%) patients died. The flow chart for this study is displayed in figure 1.

Patients were divided into the MCE (n=746) and non-MCE group (n=1137). The incidence of MCE among patients with BAO treated by EVT was 39.6% (746/1883). Comparative analyses revealed significant differences between the two groups (table 1); online supplemental table 1 lists more details of the results after classifying NIHSS score, BATMAN, and systolic blood pressure (SBP) as continuous data. Significant differences were seen in age (median age 66 vs 65 years, P=0.027), proportions of patients with admission SBP ≥160 mm Hg (34.7% vs 29.5%, P=0.020), admission NIHSS score (median 25 vs 23, P=0.008), admission Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score (median 7 vs 9, P<0.001), admission fasting blood glucose (median: 6.9 vs 6.7 mmol/L, P=0.037), proportions of patients with diabetes mellitus (19% vs 23%, P=0.038), and proportions of patients with BATMAN score ≥7 (26.9% vs 36.2%, P<0.001). Differences in onset to puncture time, number of passes (median: 1 vs 1, P=0.001), procedure time, and occlusion site were detected between the MCE and non-MCE groups (all P<0.05).

Predictors for malignant cerebral edema

As shown in figure 2A, multivariate analysis showed that the baseline GCS score (aOR=0.91, 95% CI 0.89 to 0.94; P<0.001), admission SBP ≥160 mm Hg (aOR=1.26, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.54; P=0.031), BATMAN score ≥7 (aOR=0.68, 95% CI 0.55 to 0.84; P<0.001), the number of passes (aOR=1.10, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.21; P=0.043), and recanalization status (aOR=0.57, 95% CI 0.42 to 0.75; P<0.001) were significantly associated with MCE. Compared with patients with vertebral V4 segment occlusion, patients with proximal BA occlusion were less likely to develop MCE (aOR=0.68, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.89; P=0.006). Unexpectedly, patients with diabetes mellitus were less likely to develop MCE (aOR=0.78, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.99; P=0.042).

The impact of malignant cerebral edema on outcome

As displayed in table 1, patients with MCE were less likely to achieve favorable outcomes, demonstrating lower rates of 90-day mRS 0–3 and a reduced median 90-day mRS score when compared with the non-MCE group (non-MCE vs MCE: 90-day mRS≤3, 50.7% vs 24.9%, P<0.001; mRS, 3 (2–6) vs 6 (4–6), P<0.001). Univariate analysis of favorable outcome and death at 90 days is reported in online supplemental tables 2 and 3. In multivariate analysis with adjustment for confounders, MCE was significantly associated with unfavorable outcome at 90 days (aOR=2.41 (95% CI 1.94 to 3.01), P<0.001) (table 2), and increased mortality at 90 days (aOR=1.85 (95% CI 1.51 to 2.27), P<0.001) (online supplemental table 4). The distribution of the mRS score at 90 days according to MCE or non-MCE is shown in figure 3.

Subgroup and sensitivity analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore whether there were differences in the incidence and predictors of MCE development among patients who had successful reperfusion. Of these patients, 37.8% (623/1650) experienced MCE. Univariate analysis of MCE in the group with successful reperfusion is listed in online supplemental table 5.

In multivariate analysis, except for the history of diabetes mellitus (aOR=0.80 (95% CI 0.62 to 1.03), P=0.088), other predictors were unchanged in multivariate analysis adjusted for confounding factors (admission NIHSS score, admission fasting blood glucose and TOAST type) (figure 2B).

Discussion

In the present study, MCE was independently associated with increased proportions of unfavorable outcomes and mortality, highlighting its devastating impact despite successful recanalization. Admission SBP ≥160 mm Hg, prolonged procedure times, and poor collateral status emerged as factors associated with MCE, while proximal BA occlusions paradoxically conferred protection compared with distal V4 lesions. Unexpectedly, the inverse association between diabetes mellitus and risk of MCE challenges anterior circulation paradigms, suggesting distinct pathophysiological mechanisms in the formation of posterior circulation edema.

This study showed that 39.6% (746/1883) of patients with BAO undergoing EVT developed MCE. This incidence is higher than previous studies.5818 A prior study showed that 21.0% (135/643) of patients with large vessel occlusion stroke developed MCE in the anterior circulation.5 However, the reported incidence of MCE in the BASILAR (The EVT for Acute Basilar Artery Occlusion Study) prospective study was 36.2%.7 The discrepancy might be due to differences in the anatomical and autoregulation response between the anterior and posterior circulation.12 19 In contrast to the anterior cranial fossa, the limited posterior fossa space makes it more susceptible to rapid development of mass effect and brainstem compression.19 Furthermore, the posterior circulation is characterized by decreased sympathetic innervation, more vulnerable endothelial dysfunction, elevated oxygen metabolism in the visual cortex leading to neurovascular coupling, and more vulnerable blood–brain barrier integrity, resulting in a lower autoregulatory capacity.12 Endothelial dysfunction and blood–brain barrier damage have been shown to accelerate the formation of brain edema.17 Recently, Holstein-Rønsbo et al20 found that the dynamic changes of vasoconstriction and dilatation mediated by cerebral autoregulation are an important driver of glymphatic clearance, which constitutes a critical pathogenic mechanism underlying post-stroke edema formation.21 Our findings reinforced the notion that the presence of MCE predicts a reduced chance of favorable outcome and an increased mortality in patients with BAO after EVT.5 6 Considering the higher incidence of MCE, intensively monitoring for the early recognition of MCE, and timely evaluation for decompressive craniectomy may be helpful to improve outcomes.

We observed that the admission SBP ≥160 mm Hg was associated with MCE formation in patients with BAO after EVT in the present study. A lower autoregulatory capacity in the posterior circulation leaves patients more vulnerable to blood pressure changes, thus more likely to develop MCE. Previous trials failed to demonstrate that intensive blood pressure control after EVT had a positive influence on outcome in the anterior circulation.22 However, Cao et al23 demonstrated that a higher admission SBP (≥140 mm Hg) negatively affected clinical outcomes in patients with BAO. This discrepancy suggests that the divergent characteristics of the anterior and posterior circulation might have differential blood pressure optimization strategies. Future research should place greater emphasis on strategies for management of periprocedural blood pressure in patients with BAO.

Our findings revealed both an inverse correlation between the number of recanalization attempts, procedure time, and the development of MCE, which was consistent with findings in the anterior circulation.24 Alexandre et al similarly demonstrated that successful reperfusion at first pass was associated with better outcomes in posterior circulation stroke.25 The mechanism might be that the increased number of passes leads to aggravated endothelial cell injury and increases distal embolism causing infarct tract growth and collateral circulation failure.24 26 Additionally, the longer procedure time might lead to the progression of ischemia and futile recanalization.27 Hence, increasing the likelihood of first-pass success might alleviate post-EVT MCE and thus improve favorable outcomes in patients with BAO.25

Consistent with results in the anterior circulation, good collateral circulation and successful reperfusion can reduce the incidence of brain edema.5 Recently, Broocks et al28 found that patients with BAO with initially poor collaterals might be more inclined to develop post-EVT MCE. Poor collateral circulation not only results in greater lesion growth, but also faster development of early edema.11 Unexpectedly, we found that a history of diabetes mellitus was negatively associated with MCE. However, this association was not present in those with successful reperfusion. Moreover, in contrast to previous studies, we did not observe a positive correlation among fasting blood glucose levels and pronounced edema.5 10 The potential explanations for the variable finding are as follows: on the one hand, a lower density of sympathetic innervation in the posterior circulation would lead to attenuated stress reactivity in the neuroendocrine axis and minimize the pathological consequences of stress-mediated hyperglycemia.12 29 On the other hand, chronic hyperglycemia could induce the downregulation of glucose transporters and upregulate the threshold of stress hyperglycemia. Non-diabetic individuals exhibit a higher propensity for immunological impairment and microcirculatory dysfunction, thus leading to MCE formation.29 Successful recanalization in patients may potentiate redox-mediated cellular responses, correlated with admission hyperglycemic states.30 Those results might be caused by chance, highlighting the need for further research incorporating various blood glucose parameters to confirm this hypothesis.

The current study was unable to examine the surgical indications and timing for MCE as only 18 patients (2.4%) underwent surgical treatment. Key knowledge gaps persist regarding the optimal therapeutic window for posterior fossa decompression—while American Heart Association guidelines advocate early intervention, our data reveal 50% mortality even with MCE diagnosis within 72 hours, questioning whether earlier detection (<24 h) could alter a patient’s trajectory. Randomized trials comparing early decompression versus medical management are urgently needed. Moreover, the pathophysiological basis for the protective effect of diabetes remains unclear—whether it reflects true neuroprotection or residual confounding requires prospective studies with detailed glycemic profiling. Lastly, trials are necessary to investigate whether the application of antiedema medications (such as glibenclamide, methylprednisolone) can mitigate MCE in patients with BAO after thrombectomy in the future.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s strengths include its large sample size, multicenter design, and rigorous adjudication of MCE using standardized imaging criteria. The granular procedural data (eg, thrombectomy attempts, BATMAN collaterals) provide novel insights into the technical determinants of MCE. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, selection bias might be unavoidable due to the non-randomized retrospective study design. It should be particularly noted that we only included patients with pc-ASPECTS ≥6 (≥8 for elderly patients) and excluded those with NIHSS scores <10. The enrollment criteria were based on populations proved to benefit from endovascular therapy in current randomized controlled trials,1 2 but this might limit the generalizability of our findings to patients with BAO with lower pc-ASPECTS or lower NIHSS scores. Furthermore, despite adjusting for key variables, residual confounding probably persists—particularly for the diabetes paradox. The exclusion of patients lost to follow-up (n=52) might underestimate mortality rates. Second, given the lack of comprehensive preoperative neuroimaging (CTP or MRI) for all participants, we were unable to incorporate baseline infarct volume—a known correlate of brain edema formation—in our study parameters. Third, the imaging evaluation was not performed by an independent imaging core laboratory. Finally, the Chinese cohort limits generalizability to other populations with differing stroke etiologies.

Conclusion

In patients with BAO undergoing EVT, MCE occurred in approximately 40% of cases and independently predicts catastrophic outcomes. Elevated admission SBP, prolonged procedures, and poor collaterals are modifiable risk factors, while proximal BA occlusions confer protection. These findings advocate protocolized blood pressure control, expedited thrombectomy, and collateral evaluation to reduce MCE risk. Future research must clarify the role of surgical decompression and neuroprotection in this high-risk subgroup.
