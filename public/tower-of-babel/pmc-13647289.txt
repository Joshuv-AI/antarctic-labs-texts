Integrative machine learning approaches for enzyme kinetic parameter prediction

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prediction of enzyme kinetic parameters, particularly and , is rapidly evolving from physics-based modeling toward data-driven artificial intelligence (AI) approaches, driven by expanding biochemical databases and advances in machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL). We focus on AI-based enzyme kinetic parameter prediction, with an emphasis on and , while also considering related parameters, such as and . The key findings reveal that: (i) Representation learning increasingly relies on pretrained protein language models, while structural embeddings often show limited additional performance gains over sequence-based approaches; (ii) Model performance is highly data-dependent: classical ML regressors can outperform DL architectures on small datasets when using comparable feature representations, though multimodal feature integration can improve generalization; and (iii) Multitask frameworks for the joint prediction of and may provide synergistic benefits, although the magnitude of the improvement may be limited. Future progress will require standardized benchmarks, mechanistically informed model architectures, uncertainty-aware prediction, and robust out-of-distribution validation to improve biochemical relevance, interpretability, and real-world utility.Keywords: enzyme engineering, kinetic parameter prediction, artificial intelligence, benchmark datasets, protein language models

Introduction

Enzymes catalyze the majority of biochemical reactions in living systems, and their quantitative kinetic properties determine how efficiently metabolic, synthetic, and regulatory processes proceed [1–3]. Quantitative characterization of enzyme kinetics—particularly parameters such as the turnover number (), Michaelis constant (), inhibition constant (), and catalytic efficiency ()—is critical for rational enzyme engineering, metabolic network modeling, and drug discovery [4, 5]. Specifically, denotes the maximum number of substrate molecules converted per enzyme active site per unit time under substrate-saturating conditions, whereas is the substrate concentration at which the initial reaction velocity reaches half of and represents an apparent kinetic constant influenced by both substrate binding and catalytic turnover [6]. Together, these parameters enable quantitative prediction of reaction rates across varying substrate concentrations, making them indispensable for optimizing biocatalytic processes.

Despite their importance, experimental determination of kinetic parameters remains labor-intensive, condition-sensitive, and difficult to scale across the enormous diversity of enzyme–substrate pairs. Classical steady-state and presteady-state kinetic assays, including spectrophotometric, fluorometric, stopped-flow, calorimetric, and chromatographic measurements, usually require purified enzymes, well-defined substrates, and carefully controlled reaction conditions, limiting scalability [7, 8]. While high-throughput screening (HTS) platforms have improved throughput, many assays report relative activity or endpoint signals rather than fully resolved kinetic constants, and their outputs are often difficult to compare across substrates, laboratories, and reaction conditions [9, 10]. Consequently, computational prediction of enzyme kinetics has emerged as a complementary strategy, offering rapid estimates to guide experimental prioritization, fill gaps in metabolic models, and support enzyme discovery workflows [11–15].

Early computational approaches relied on mechanistic modeling [e.g. transition-state theory and molecular dynamics (MD)] or statistical regression using handcrafted features to predict relative enzyme activities or selected kinetic parameters [16, 17]. However, these methods struggled to generalize across diverse enzyme families or capture nonlinear relationships between sequence, structure, substrate chemistry, and assay conditions. The development of machine learning (ML)—particularly deep learning (DL)—has reshaped the field by enabling representation learning from raw sequences, molecular graphs, structural data, and experimental metadata [18, 19]. Pretrained protein language models (pLMs), such as ESM [20] and ProtT5 [21], combined with molecular fingerprints, SMILES encoders, or graph neural networks (GNNs) [22] for substrate representation, have improved performance in predicting , and related parameters. Nevertheless, critical challenges persist regarding heterogeneous data curation, incomplete assay-condition metadata, enzyme and substrate redundancy, inconsistent training/testing splits, limited model interpretability, insufficient feature representation, and independently modeled predictions for kinetic parameters.

This review provides a critical synthesis of computational strategies for artificial intelligence (AI)-based enzyme kinetic parameter prediction. It introduces the conceptual foundations of physics-based and AI-based modeling approaches and clarifies the distinction between kinetic parameter prediction and related tasks, such as relative activity prediction, enzyme function annotation, and thermostability modeling. The paper then analyzes the AI-based prediction pipeline through four interdependent pillars: data curation, evaluation methodologies, feature representation, and model architecture, aiming to identify fundamental limitations and propose targeted solutions. The overall structure of this review is summarized in Fig. 1. Our analysis reveals that current approaches face persistent challenges regarding data scarcity, limited feature representation, independently modeled labels, lack of uncertainty quantification, and limited interpretability. We further argue that current performance gains are often constrained not only by model architecture, but also by data quality, train-test split design, biochemical context, benchmark reliability, and uncertainty estimation. Previous reviews on enzyme engineering, protein property prediction, pLMs, and AI-assisted enzymology have provided broad perspectives on enzyme-related modeling, but often treat kinetic parameter prediction as one downstream application [23–26]. In contrast, this Review centers on enzyme kinetic parameter prediction as a condition-dependent biochemical regression problem with distinctive data, benchmarking, representation, and reliability challenges. To our knowledge, this is one of the first reviews to systematically examine AI-based enzyme kinetic parameter prediction by integrating perspectives on biochemical representation, benchmark design, leakage-aware evaluation, uncertainty quantification, and kinetic-parameter interdependence.

From hypothesis-driven modeling to data-driven kinetic parameter prediction

From the perspective of enzyme engineering, key descriptive indicators of enzymatic activity, including relative activity and kinetic parameters, serve as critical descriptors of enzymatic functionality. Traditional experimental characterization of enzyme activity remains prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, and labor-intensive, particularly when screening thousands of enzyme variants or enzyme–substrate pairs [27]. While relative activity measurements (e.g. fluorescence-based [28, 29] or deep mutational scanning (DMS)-based [30] protein fitness measurements) are rapid and widely used in enzyme engineering workflows, they typically lack the quantitative detail provided by kinetic parameters. Unlike relative activity, the accurate prediction of enzymatic kinetic parameters represents a critical challenge for both fundamental enzymology and applied biocatalyst engineering. Kinetic parameters are essential for quantitative comparison of natural enzymes and for detailed modeling in metabolic engineering [31, 32]. Therefore, the accurate prediction of enzymatic kinetic parameters (, , and ) requires models to learn quantitative enzyme–substrate relationships that remain valid across diverse enzymes, substrates, and experimental conditions [33]. This methodological gap has driven the evolution of computational strategies from hypothesis-driven physical models to modern data-driven paradigms.

Physics-based approaches

Early computational methods focused on mechanistic hypotheses about enzyme function, often prioritizing thermodynamic stability or coarse-grained activity metrics. Key strategies included:

Transition-state modeling: Quantum mechanical (QM) calculations of reaction coordinates and activation energies are limited by computational cost and uncertain success rate [34]. They are often used in combination with molecular mechanics (QM/MM) to understand enzymatic reaction processes qualitatively [35]. While QM methods demonstrate potential for kinetic parameter estimation, their lack of generalizability across heterogeneous reaction systems impedes practical implementation [36].

Transition-state modeling: Quantum mechanical (QM) calculations of reaction coordinates and activation energies are limited by computational cost and uncertain success rate [34]. They are often used in combination with molecular mechanics (QM/MM) to understand enzymatic reaction processes qualitatively [35]. While QM methods demonstrate potential for kinetic parameter estimation, their lack of generalizability across heterogeneous reaction systems impedes practical implementation [36].

Molecular modeling: QM/MM, MD simulation [37, 38] and molecular docking [37, 39] can be used to assess substrate/product binding poses and near-attack conformations (NACs) [40], which may correlate with experimental values and substrate/product selectivity in selected cases. NAC-based analyses have been applied to interpret enzyme activity in several systems [41].

Molecular modeling: QM/MM, MD simulation [37, 38] and molecular docking [37, 39] can be used to assess substrate/product binding poses and near-attack conformations (NACs) [40], which may correlate with experimental values and substrate/product selectivity in selected cases. NAC-based analyses have been applied to interpret enzyme activity in several systems [41].

Conservation analysis: Position-Specific Scoring Matrices (PSSMs) derived from multiple sequence alignments [42] can identify evolutionarily conserved residues, implicitly assuming that conservation correlates with catalytic importance [43]. From statistical analysis of several DMS datasets [30], PSSM scores can correlate with protein fitness [15], but they do not directly encode substrate identity, assay conditions, or absolute kinetic constants.

It is also hypothesized that enzyme activities are co-evolution driven for substrate binding residues and thermostability driven for catalysis site distal residues [51]. Such methods achieved moderate success in enzyme mutation design but remain insufficient for generalizable prediction of , , or across diverse enzyme families and substrates.

Data-driven paradigms

The growth of enzyme activity data and mutational fitness profiles—exemplified by large-scale resources like the ProteinGym benchmark [52] integrating DMS [30] datasets—has driven rapid adoption of DL and ML approaches for predicting relative enzymatic activity. These approaches can be roughly divided into two broad categories: pretrained models used for zero-shot prediction and supervised models trained for fitness prediction.

Pretrained models for zero-shot prediction: Frameworks such as ESM-2 [53], ProtT5, ProteinMPNN [54], ESM-IF [55], and LigandMPNN [56] learn statistical or structural constraints of natural proteins from large-scale sequence and/or structure data. Sequence-based pLMs such as ESM-2 and ProtT5 capture evolutionary and statistical patterns in natural protein sequences, whereas inverse-folding and structure-conditioned models such as ProteinMPNN, ESM-IF, and LigandMPNN identify sequences or residues compatible with a given protein structure. These models have been applied to protein design and optimization, including improvements in protein stability, expression, and function [57], but they generally do not explicitly model quantitative catalytic efficiency metrics. Several of these models can also be used in a zero-shot setting for protein fitness or mutational-effect prediction and have been evaluated on DMS benchmarks such as ProteinGym [52].

To make the raw data interpretable by ML algorithms, biological and chemical inputs must be appropriately encoded. Early approaches used one-hot encoding or PSSM profiles, while recent models leverage pretrained pLMs such as ESM-1b [20], ProtBERT, and ProtT5 [21] to capture residue-level semantics and evolutionary features. For substrates, molecular fingerprints (e.g. MACCS [66] and Morgan [67]), pretrained molecular encoders such as MolT5 [15, 68], SMILES Transformer [13, 69], and Mole-BERT [70], as well as graph representations, are commonly applied [14]. When available, 3D structural information—including contact maps or features encoded by graph-based models—can also be integrated [14, 69], where interaction-aware representations are typically modeled through feature fusion and attention mechanisms.

These representations serve as input to various learning architectures. Classical regressors such as random forest (RF), gradient boosting, and extra trees provide competitive performance [13, 69, 71], especially on smaller datasets. For more complex interaction modeling, deep neural networks, recurrent models (e.g. LSTM), and GNNs are commonly adopted. GNNs are particularly useful for capturing molecular graph structures and protein structure information [14, 69]. Recently, Transformer-based models have gained attention for modeling long-range dependencies across sequences. Some pipelines adopt multimodal Transformer architectures with attention-based fusion layers and auxiliary environmental features [11]. In addition, multitask frameworks may jointly predict related parameters such as and , as demonstrated by MPEK [11]. Such strategies may improve predictive consistency across related kinetic parameters, although their benefits depend on data quality, task relationships, and evaluation design.

Model evaluation is typically performed using multiple regression metrics, including mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), the coefficient of determination (), and Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC). For applications such as enzyme mining and directed evolution, ranking-based metrics—such as Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (SCC), top-N accuracy, and the area under the ROC curve—are also used to assess the prioritization capability of models. Some recent frameworks incorporate mutational scanning benchmarks to evaluate the model’s ability to distinguish between activity-enhancing and deleterious mutations, a feature especially valuable in industrial and therapeutic enzyme engineering [15].

In addition, to ensure a fair assessment of model generalization, many recent pipelines enforce sequence identity-based data splitting (e.g. ) to limit similarity between training and test sets. Such partitioning strategies reduce data leakage and provide more realistic evaluations of model performance in practical enzyme discovery scenarios [15, 71]. Experimental measurement

The construction of accurate models for predicting enzyme kinetic parameters relies heavily on comprehensive, high-quality, and well-curated datasets derived from experimental measurements. Traditionally, parameters such as and are obtained through classical enzyme kinetics experiments, including initial-rate measurements under steady-state conditions and various HTS techniques. These experimental results are then aggregated into major enzymology databases such as BRENDA [61] and SABIO-RK [62], which serve as primary sources of kinetic data. However, these databases often exhibit heterogeneity in terms of data format, substrate specificity, organism diversity, and experimental conditions. Moreover, issues such as data sparsity, missing annotations, and inconsistent units present additional challenges for downstream modeling. (1) Optical screening techniques

Optical methods, including fluorescence, luminescence, and absorbance, offer rapid detection in multi-well formats. Fluorescence-based screening is widely used for its sensitivity and compatibility with microplate automation.

(2) Droplet-based microfluidics

Droplet microfluidics creates isolated microreactors for enzyme reactions, enabling ultra-HTS at nanoliter scale [77, 78]. Fluorescence detection allows real-time monitoring of activity inside droplets.

(3) FACS

FACS enables single-cell resolution in enzyme screening by sorting based on fluorescence intensity. It is effective for detecting rare active variants using genetically encoded reporters.

(4) Chromatographic and coupled assays

HPLC and LC-MS are used when optical detection is not feasible, offering sensitive quantification of substrates or products. Coupled reactions generate optically detectable signals when direct measurement is impractical [7, 75].

Assay formats generate different types of readouts and therefore differ in their suitability for machine-learning labels. Databases for kinetic parameters

Among curated resources, BRENDA [61, 79] is one of the most comprehensive enzyme information systems, containing extensive literature-derived annotations of , , and , together with experimental metadata such as temperature, pH, organism, EC number, and UniProt accessions. SABIO-RK [62] complements BRENDA with reaction-centric annotations that include kinetic rate laws and substrate–catalyst relationships under defined conditions.

Other databases provide essential contextual information: UniProt offers functional annotations and enzyme classifications; KEGG contributes pathway-level context; and structural repositories such as PDB [80] enrich 3D structural understanding. Additionally, ChEMBL [81, 82] provides inhibition data useful for predicting , although such records require careful filtering to distinguish enzymatic inhibition constants from broader ligand-binding or cell-based activity measurements.

Despite the growing volume of data, enzyme kinetic datasets remain sparse and imbalanced. A disproportionate number of entries focus on a limited subset of enzymes (e.g. transferases and dehydrogenases), while most EC classes remain underrepresented [83, 84]. Moreover, inconsistencies in data granularity, update frequency, and experimental verification across sources pose challenges for data harmonization and cross-database integration, for which expert validation could be important [84].

Collectively, the integration of experimental, computational, and literature-derived data forms a robust foundation for DL-based modeling of enzyme kinetic parameters. It enhances model generalizability and supports broader applications across enzyme discovery, engineering, and systems biology. Data preprocessing and normalization

Robust and generalizable prediction of enzyme kinetic parameters critically depends on the quality and consistency of the input data. Raw entries derived from heterogeneous experiments often exhibit discrepancies in units, assay conditions, and annotation completeness, necessitating thorough data preprocessing prior to model training [13, 69, 83, 84].

A key initial step is the harmonization of measurement units. Parameters such as are typically converted to millimolar (mM), and is standardized to s, enabling meaningful comparisons across studies and enzyme systems. To reduce noise and eliminate erroneous records, outlier filtering is applied to exclude values with biologically implausible magnitudes—such as negative entries or extreme upper limits—which often originate from experimental artifacts or data curation errors. For heavy-tailed distributions like and , log-transformation is commonly used to stabilize variance and facilitate downstream learning in nearly all DL-based models [11, 13–15, 69, 83, 85]. Condition-based filtering further enhances dataset reliability by retaining only entries measured under physiologically relevant conditions—e.g. pH and temperature within acceptable ranges—thereby reducing confounding effects and improving biological validity [13].

Dataset consolidation is performed to merge duplicated enzyme–substrate pairs and complete missing metadata fields (e.g. EC numbers and organism names) using reliable curated cross-references. This ensures internal consistency and semantic completeness of the dataset. A critical yet often overlooked challenge in public kinetic databases is the imbalance in enzyme–substrate pair representation, where well-studied enzymes and model organisms dominate [83, 84]. This introduces strong bias and limits model generalization. To address this, techniques such as sequence de-redundancy, sample reweighting, and frequency-based subsampling are applied to rebalance the training distribution. Recent frameworks such as CatPred [14], CataPro [15], and TurNuP [71] have increasingly addressed data redundancy and generalization through improved dataset construction and more rigorous evaluation strategies. In addition, partitioning strategies used during model evaluation are critical for avoiding data leakage and assessing true generalizability.

In summary, systematic preprocessing—comprising unit normalization, outlier filtering, assay-based selection, de-redundancy, and balanced splitting—is indispensable for stabilizing model training, improving reliability, and enabling more generalizable enzyme kinetic parameter predictions.

Inherent biases of public enzyme kinetic datasets

Enzyme kinetic parameter datasets exhibit significant nonuniformity, with pronounced clustering in specific categories. For instance, an over-representation of enzymatic data linked to certain labeled “substrates”—such as NADP+/NADPH, NAD+/NADH, and ATP—collectively accounts for over one-sixth of the total dataset [12, 13, 15]. Notably, these molecules may function as cofactors or cosubstrates rather than the primary substrate of interest in many reactions. Treating cofactors as substrates in this context obscures the genuine diversity of enzyme–substrate interactions. Consequently, models trained on such data may fail to adequately learn substrate-specific features, resulting in protein-centric predictions [33] that are overly dependent on the enzyme protein sequence and less responsive to variations in the chemical structure of the primary substrate. This can reduce the model’s ability to predict how kinetic parameters change when the same enzyme acts on distinct primary substrates.

Moreover, substantial variability exists in the environmental parameters of kinetic assays, including enzyme concentration, reaction temperature, ionic strength (or cofactor concentration), and pH. Training a generalized model that relies solely on enzyme sequences and substrate/product information (while excluding experimental conditions) may lead to overfitting or poor generalization due to incompatibility between heterogeneous data sources [13, 71]. Therefore, these key environmental parameters should be taken into account for model building. However, these critical experimental parameters are frequently missing from the dataset. For example, TurNuP uses a carefully curated dataset with comprehensive substrate-product annotation comprising 4271 entries [71]. Similarly, the extended EF-UniKP framework incorporates temperature and pH data, yet only 636 entries include pH values and 572 include temperature values [13]. Such incompleteness introduces biases and restricts the applicability of the model. These effects extend beyond reduced dataset coverage and can directly shape learned representations and model behavior. When kinetic records are dominated by well-studied enzyme families or common cofactors, models may learn enzyme-family priors or cofactor-associated shortcuts rather than true enzyme–substrate specificity. As a result, rare substrate scaffolds, uncommon cofactors, and condition-dependent effects may be underweighted during training. Missing pH, temperature, cofactor-state, or assay-protocol metadata further forces models to average over heterogeneous biochemical contexts, which can yield apparently strong performance under random or weakly controlled splits but reduced reliability under scaffold-, enzyme-, or condition-shifted evaluation. These biases may also lead to overly confident predictions in poorly represented regions of enzyme or chemical space, particularly when uncertainty estimates are not calibrated against substrate rarity, enzyme-family coverage, or assay-context completeness. Different approaches, ranging from data imputation and condition-aware modeling to constrained experimental parameter ranges, have been developed to mitigate biases arising from imbalanced, heterogeneous, or incomplete data.

Inappropriate training/testing dataset construction affects model generalizability

Early prediction models often overlooked data distribution issues, leading to overly optimistic performance evaluations. For instance, studies such as DLKcat demonstrated that models trained on datasets with high sequence similarity between training and test sets exhibited artificially inflated accuracy [33, 85]. However, their predictive capability deteriorated significantly for enzymes with <60% sequence identity to the training data, resulting in unreliable estimates for novel enzymes or mutants. This reflects a fundamental flaw in neglecting data distribution biases, where models “memorized” training samples rather than learning generalizable patterns [33]. Subsequent studies have increasingly emphasized the importance of similarity-aware dataset partitioning, though current methods predominantly focus on sequence similarity thresholds (e.g. 40% identity) [15, 71]. Empirical evidence shows that models perform well when test and training sets share high sequence similarity, but this approach fails to address structural or functional heterogeneity. For example, enzymes with divergent sequences but conserved active-site structures may exhibit similar catalytic behaviors, yet such cases remain underrepresented in current frameworks.

Dataset bias in enzyme kinetic parameter prediction can be broadly categorized into enzyme-family bias, substrate/cofactor bias, experimental-condition bias, and split-induced leakage. Enzyme-family bias may be mitigated through EC-stratified sampling or sequence-cluster-based sampling, particularly when specific EC classes or model enzymes are overrepresented. Substrate/cofactor bias should be addressed by distinguishing primary substrates from cofactors or cosubstrates, especially for frequently occurring molecules such as NAD(P)H and ATP. For experimental-condition bias, performance should be reported separately on subsets with complete and incomplete metadata, including pH, temperature, and ionic strength. To clarify benchmark construction, splitting strategies for enzyme kinetic parameter prediction can be divided into several complementary settings. A random split mainly evaluates interpolation within the observed data distribution. However, identical or highly similar enzymes, substrates, or reactions may appear in both the training and test sets, which can lead to an overestimation of model performance. A sequence-disjoint split separates enzymes according to sequence similarity, for example using a sequence identity threshold of , and evaluates generalization to unseen or weakly homologous enzymes. A substrate-disjoint split excludes test substrates from the training set and assesses prediction for new chemical entities. A reaction-disjoint split separates reaction types or enzyme–substrate transformations between the training and test sets and evaluates transfer to unseen catalytic reactions. An EC-disjoint split separates EC classes or categories and tests generalization to new functional classes. These strategies examine different types of generalization, including unseen enzymes, substrates, reactions, and enzyme classes. Future benchmarks should, therefore, report results under several splitting settings rather than relying only on random or sequence-based splits.

Evaluation metrics for kinetic prediction models

Ranking-based and application-relevant metrics

In many realistic scenarios, ranking quality may be more important than absolute numerical accuracy. For enzyme variant optimization, models are often used to prioritize beneficial mutants from a large candidate library; therefore, metrics such as SCC, top-N hit rate, top-N enrichment, or the number of experimentally validated positive variants among the top-ranked candidates can directly reflect practical utility. Such metrics are particularly relevant for in silico directed evolution, where the main objective is to enrich improved variants within a limited experimental budget rather than to predict exact kinetic values for all candidates.

For enzyme discovery, another important scenario is the comparison of different enzymes catalyzing the same reaction or acting on the same substrate. In this case, evaluation should consider whether a model can correctly rank candidate enzymes within the same reaction context. Conversely, for enzyme engineering, evaluation may focus on whether a model can distinguish activity-enhancing and deleterious mutations within the same protein background. These within-reaction or within-protein evaluations are more aligned with practical use cases than global metrics computed over heterogeneous enzyme families.

Given that kinetic parameters often span several orders of magnitude (e.g. from to s), most evaluations are performed on log-transformed scales to reduce skew and improve comparability. However, global correlation values can still be misleading if the test set contains strong enzyme-family, substrate, or reaction-class biases. Therefore, performance should ideally be reported across a hierarchy of benchmark splits, including random, sequence-disjoint, substrate-disjoint, reaction-disjoint, and EC-disjoint partitions. Such stratified reporting would clarify whether a model mainly performs interpolation within familiar enzyme–substrate space or can extrapolate to new enzymes, new substrates, new reactions, and new functional classes.

In summary, a rigorous evaluation framework should integrate error-based, correlation-based, ranking-based, and application-oriented metrics. Metrics such as MAE, RMSE, and capture absolute predictive accuracy, whereas PCC and SCC highlight fidelity in trend or ranking. The inclusion of , top-N enrichment, and within-protein or within-reaction ranking further grounds model performance in biologically meaningful and experimentally actionable settings. In this review, we report MAE, RMSE, , and PCC across multiple models and dataset partitions whenever available, while also emphasizing ranking-based and out-of-distribution (OOD)-oriented evaluations when they are relevant to enzyme discovery or engineering.

Feature representation in enzyme kinetic parameter prediction models

The accurate prediction of enzyme kinetic parameters hinges on effective feature representation strategies that transform biological and chemical entities into machine-interpretable inputs. Feature encoding directly impacts a model’s ability to capture biochemical patterns, generalize across enzyme families, and infer catalytic mechanisms. This section systematically examines representation paradigms for three core components: enzymes, substrates/products, and reaction or experimental conditions. We trace the evolution from early handcrafted descriptors to modern pretrained embeddings and graph-based representations, highlighting how advances in pLMs and molecular GNNs have improved representation learning in recent kinetic prediction models. The interplay between representation fidelity, computational efficiency, and biological interpretability remains central to balancing predictive accuracy with mechanistic insights in kinetic parameter modeling.

Enzyme feature representation

Enzyme representation is a fundamental component in modeling kinetic parameters, as it determines how sequence-level or structural-level information is transformed into machine-interpretable input features. Early studies employed simple encodings, such as one-hot vectors or physicochemical descriptors derived from amino acid composition, including AAindex, hydrophobicity, net charge, and isoelectric point [23]. While these approaches offer interpretability, they lack the capacity to capture long-range dependencies or contextual information embedded within protein sequences and structures.

Other representations such as k-mer-based embeddings and handcrafted domain-specific features (PSSMs and ProtParam-derived attributes) are also commonly used. These methods improved performance in traditional ML models but remained dependent on expert knowledge and extensive preprocessing [87–90].

Recent developments have evolved toward pLMs for protein sequence representation, including ESM, ProtBERT, ProtT5, and UniRep, which are trained on large-scale protein sequences using self-supervised learning objectives. These models generate contextualized amino acid embeddings that capture hierarchical sequence features and have been shown to enhance downstream predictive tasks [91], including the estimation of and values. pLMs have been widely adopted in frameworks such as CatPred, MPEK, CataPro, NNKcat [83], DEKP [69], and UniKP [13], where they provide a unified sequence representation for downstream fusion with substrate/product or structural features and graphs [14, 69]. Substrate and product feature representation

Substrates and products are in most cases small molecules with diverse chemical properties spanning vast chemical space. Early approaches relied on manual feature extraction using molecular fingerprints, such as MACCS keys, Morgan (circular) fingerprints, and PubChem substructure patterns, which encode molecular patterns into fixed-length binary or continuous vectors. These descriptors, while efficient and interpretable, often fail to capture the full chemical complexity of small molecules, especially stereochemistry, protonation states, and electronic effects.

More recent studies have employed SMILES-based encodings, treating the substrate’s SMILES string as a molecular “language”. This enabled the application of text-based models, such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs), 1D convolutional neural networks (1D-CNNs), and Transformer architectures, to learn data-driven representations directly from the substrate’s linear notation [11, 13, 15].

A more expressive alternative is to represent small molecules as molecular graphs, where atoms are treated as nodes and bonds as edges. Message Passing Neural Networks (MPNNs) and variants such as Directed MPNNs (D-MPNNs) [95] have been adopted in recent models such as CatPred. These models can learn atom- and bond-level topological context directly from molecular graphs, making them suitable for capturing chemical properties relevant to catalysis. Notably, our analysis suggests a consistent pattern across contemporary DL architectures for enzyme kinetic parameter prediction: pretrained and graph-based representation models are generally more expressive than traditional molecular fingerprints. However, unlike protein sequences, small molecule representation models face inherent challenges in capturing analogous evolutionary or functional context across diverse molecular species. Their feature encoding must simultaneously address two critical aspects: (i) the vastness of chemical space encompassing structurally divergent molecules, and (ii) the high-dimensional correlations among chemically analogous compounds. Current molecular fingerprinting methods and graph-based models still struggle to fully reconcile these dual requirements. Although protein folding space has been increasingly sampled by structural prediction and sequence databases, the chemical space relevant to enzyme catalysis remains highly expansive. Existing small molecule pretraining paradigms may not comprehensively cover the scaffold diversity of enzymatic substrates. Consequently, when encountering novel molecular substrates or products beyond their training distribution, current models may demonstrate a pronounced “protein-centric” predictive bias during practical applications. This phenomenon may partly stem from the disproportionate reliance on protein feature extraction relative to chemical and reaction-context modeling in many architectures.

Reaction and condition features

In addition to enzyme and substrate representations, many recent studies have incorporated reaction and condition features that capture experimental or biological context, which can significantly affect enzymatic activity [13, 69, 83, 84]. These include reaction conditions such as pH, temperature, and ionic strength, as well as organismal information, Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers, and compartmental localization.

Reaction conditions are typically encoded as normalized numerical variables. For instance, temperature and pH values are scaled and directly input as continuous features. Some models, such as MPEK, use radial basis function (RBF) [96, 97] encodings to enhance the resolution of condition-sensitive effects.

Categorical information like EC numbers or species identity is commonly represented via one-hot encoding or learned embedding vectors. EC numbers, in particular, are useful for guiding the model toward class-specific patterns of enzyme–substrate interaction and kinetics, although they may also introduce functional-class leakage if training and test splits are not carefully designed. Organism-level features can also capture phylogenetic or expression-related variability, although they are often underutilized due to limited annotation availability. Incorporating reaction and condition features can improve model accuracy and robustness, especially when predicting kinetic parameters under variable environmental or physiological conditions. These context-aware features are increasingly seen as essential for real-world deployment of enzyme kinetic parameter prediction models.

In summary, effective representation of enzymes, substrates, products, and contextual factors plays a pivotal role in enabling accurate and generalizable predictions of enzyme kinetic parameters. The evolution from handcrafted descriptors to data-driven, learned embeddings—powered by pLMs and graph-based molecular encoders—has substantially improved the model’s ability to capture biochemical complexity and mechanistic specificity. Moreover, the integration of auxiliary features such as reaction conditions and phylogenetic information adds critical biological context, further enhancing predictive robustness. As the field progresses, the design of unified, multimodal representation frameworks that incorporate sequence, structure, and contextual data is expected to drive the next wave of advances in enzyme kinetic parameter modeling.

Summary and perspectives on feature representations

In summary, we systematically review contemporary representation methodologies for proteins, substrates/products, and catalytic conditions, documenting the paradigm shift from hand-engineered features to pretrained representations. However, it is critical to note that current DL models for enzyme kinetic parameter prediction face significant challenges arising from the highly clustered nature of training data, including concentrated enzyme family distributions and narrowly clustered substrate/product categories [83]. These data sparsity patterns can lead to over-optimized model performance on predominant data clusters while causing underperformance on rare biochemical instances. Future model development should focus on dual advancements: (i) enhanced molecule data diversification strategies to better capture the biological diversity of substrates/products in pretrained molecular language or graph-based models, and (ii) synergistic optimization of input representations to improve generalization across the full spectrum of enzymatic activities.

Model architectures for enzyme kinetic parameter prediction

With the rapid advancement of bioinformatics and computational biology, ML—particularly DL—has emerged as an important tool for predicting enzyme kinetic parameters, offering scalable modeling strategies and reducing experimental screening burden. DL is especially useful due to its capacity to extract complex hierarchical features and handle high-dimensional, heterogeneous inputs, making it an increasingly valuable approach in enzyme kinetic parameter prediction research [17].

In the following sections, we first review fundamental learning paradigms commonly employed for enzymatic parameter prediction, and then summarize recent methodological advances by highlighting representative models that exemplify these developments.

Learning paradigms for enzyme kinetic parameter modeling

Early efforts employed classical algorithms such as support vector machines, RFs, and deep neural networks [99–101] to predict enzyme-related properties or EC numbers. Although EC numbers provide useful functional annotations, they should be distinguished from direct prediction of quantitative kinetic parameters such as and . More recent approaches leverage compound–protein interaction (CPI)-like models and DL architectures to directly estimate kinetic parameters [102]. These advances are part of the broader shift in ML from rule-based systems to parameterized learning frameworks [103]. Depending on the nature and availability of labeled data, different ML paradigms can be applied. Models are trained to learn a mapping from molecular representations—such as amino acid sequences, SMILES strings, or molecular graphs—to kinetic outputs, using regression-based loss functions. For example, Borger et al. [31] applied a linear regression model to estimate values for eight metabolites across a diverse set of enzyme-organism systems, achieving an RMSE of 1.01. In a separate study, Yan et al. [104] constructed a feedforward backpropagation neural network to predict the of cellobiose-catalyzed glucosidases, attaining a PCC exceeding 0.6 on a validation set comprising 12 glucosidases. Later, widely used architectures include CNNs, RNNs, and Transformer-based models, which are well suited to capturing complex biochemical patterns and nonlinear interactions in enzymatic systems. More recently, additional DL models have been developed and are discussed in detail in the following sections.

Benchmark analyses reveal a critical constraint: with limited curated entries for and , conventional ensemble methods can outperform simple deep neural networks. This underscores the potential advantage of classical ensemble methods over purely complexity-driven architectures under data scarcity, emphasizing the need for hybrid strategies that balance expressive power with sample efficiency.

MPEK also shows potential in predicting mutant enzyme variants, generalizing to unseen reactions, and distinguishing preferred from alternative substrates. Bias-resistant and out-of-distribution-aware modeling approaches

While recent DL models—such as DLKcat, UniKP, and MPEK—have demonstrated architectural and predictive advances, they remain constrained by a recurring limitation: heavy reliance on training data distributions and susceptibility to overfitting. These models are often trained on relatively small and partially redundant datasets, frequently with high sequence or substrate similarity across training and test splits [33]. This overlap can artificially inflate performance metrics and impair generalization to novel enzyme–substrate combinations.

For instance, DLKcat incorporates multimodal learning with CNNs and GNNs, but its original benchmark splitting strategy did not explicitly enforce sequence-disjoint training and test sets. UniKP [13] enhances representation learning using pretrained protein and molecular language models, yet relies on conventional benchmark splits that lack strict data partitioning. Similarly, MPEK integrates multitask modeling and environmental condition encoding, but still draws its training and test sets from potentially overlapping biological sources.

In various related applications, including protein function annotation [105, 106] and the prediction of protein-ligand binding affinity [107, 108], a high degree of sequence similarity between proteins in the training and test sets often results in inflated performance estimates and introduces systematic biases into model evaluation. These patterns highlight the need for standardized, low-redundancy benchmark datasets and rigorous evaluation practices to ensure model generalizability.

To ensure robustness, models must learn generalizable patterns instead of overfitting to noise or redundancy in the training data [33]. Such patterns are reflected in latent representations learned by the model and can often be visualized in lower-dimensional projection spaces to facilitate interpretation [109]. TurNuP (Turnover Number Prediction) was developed to address the limited generalization ability of prior models in predicting by leveraging a biologically diverse and strictly filtered dataset. In contrast to models such as DLKcat and UniKP, TurNuP utilizes a dataset with identical enzyme sequences excluded across the training and test sets and model performance further evaluated across different levels of sequence similarity. TurNuP employs a fine-tuned ESM-1b-based protein representation to encode enzyme sequences and uses differential reaction fingerprints to represent complete enzymatic reactions, including substrates and products. TurNuP is evaluated across enzyme sequence-similarity strata and on reactions absent from the training set, providing a more challenging assessment of model generalization. The model demonstrates consistent performance across these challenging evaluation settings, highlighting its ability to generalize to dissimilar enzymes and previously unseen reactions.

CatPred proposes a unified DL framework for predicting , , and from in vitro biochemical data. Built on CatPred-DB—a benchmark dataset aggregated from BRENDA and SABIO-RK—it retains low-frequency enzyme–substrate pairs while minimizing aggressive filtering. The CatPred architecture integrates ESM-2 embeddings for enzyme sequences, substrate graphs encoded via D-MPNNs, and optionally, 3D structural features. In contrast to DLKcat and UniKP, which adopt point estimation, CatPred applies probabilistic regression to simultaneously predict value and uncertainty. Moreover, while TurNuP evaluates generalization to low-homology enzymes, CatPred explicitly evaluates model performance under strict sequence identity thresholds () and provides query-specific uncertainty estimates. By supporting multi-parameter prediction, incorporating uncertainty estimates, and enabling rigorous OOD evaluation, CatPred addresses multiple limitations of previous models and offers a more interpretable and robust framework for enzyme kinetic parameter modeling.

CataPro, on the other hand, creates 10-fold datasets based on enzyme sequence similarity clusters for , , and / prediction. With pretrained pLM features for enzyme sequences and pretrained substrate embeddings, CataPro demonstrates improved performance over DLKcat and UniKP in the reported benchmarks and has also been applied to industrial enzyme discovery and engineering for vanillin synthesis. CataPro places particular emphasis on leakage-aware evaluation through sequence-cluster-based data partitioning rather than relying solely on larger training datasets. Its datasets were constructed from BRENDA and SABIO-RK and partitioned by CD-HIT-based clustering with a sequence identity threshold of 0.4 to reduce sequence-level overlap between cross-validation folds.

Together, these models represent a new generation of bias-resistant modeling strategies that emphasize data diversity, uncertainty modeling, and biologically meaningful evaluation. Their methodological rigor enhances robustness and real-world applicability, although interpretability remains an open challenge.

Summary and perspectives on model architecture

Recent advancements in DL have led to the rapid evolution of modeling architectures for enzyme kinetic parameter prediction. This review categorizes these developments into key paradigms, including multi-task learning frameworks, bias-resistant architectures, and Transformer-based encoders. Representative models—such as DLKcat, UniKP [13], CatPred [14], MPEK [11], CataPro [15], and TurNuP [71]—demonstrate diversity in input feature types, neural module design, and evaluation protocols. Despite these advances, several challenges remain: structural embeddings have yet to show consistent superiority over sequence-based representations; multitask models may fail to exploit the context-dependent relationship between and ; and many models struggle to generalize under stringent conditions, such as enzyme sequence identity thresholds . Collectively, while prediction accuracy and generalizability have improved in several benchmarks, future efforts must strive to balance model complexity, scalability, interpretability, uncertainty calibration, and data efficiency. Achieving this balance will be key to developing robust and biologically grounded predictive systems applicable across diverse enzymatic contexts.

Challenges and future directions

Despite notable advances in applying DL to enzyme kinetic parameter prediction, several persistent challenges remain that limit model reliability, biological interpretability, and real-world utility.

First, limitations in both dataset construction and benchmarking practices continue to undermine model evaluation. Public databases such as BRENDA and SABIO-RK often lack standardized metadata, including key assay conditions like pH, temperature, and ionic strength. This leads to noisy inputs and hampers the learning of robust enzyme–substrate–condition relationships. Furthermore, the available data are highly imbalanced, overrepresenting a small number of enzyme families [83], while underrepresenting novel or less-studied enzymes. Compounding this, many studies rely on random data partitioning without enforcing sequence dissimilarity thresholds, resulting in potential data leakage and inflated performance metrics [13, 33, 85]. Moreover, evaluation strategies remain overly reliant on overall PCC, while neglecting per-target or enzyme-family-based performance, ranking-oriented metrics, and the coefficient of determination , which provides a stricter assessment of both trend agreement and prediction error [15, 33].

Second, the integration of heterogeneous biochemical features into predictive frameworks remains suboptimal. Although pLMs and AlphaFold2 have enabled widespread access to high-quality sequence and structural data, these typically reflect only one static conformation and may not capture biologically active or ligand-bound states. Structure-based embeddings thus often contribute limited or inconsistent predictive gains [15], especially when catalytic behavior is regulated by conformational dynamics or allosteric effects. On the substrate side, simple descriptors such as SMILES strings, molecular fingerprints, or molecular graphs may miss reactive, stereochemical, protonation-state, or 3D features critical to enzymatic recognition.

Third, many models treat kinetic parameters as isolated targets, ignoring their mathematical relationships and potential biochemical coupling. In practice, , , and are mathematically related, but the statistical and mechanistic relationships between and are context-dependent. Multitask frameworks like MPEK suggest that leveraging shared latent representations across these outputs can enhance prediction consistency, although the benefit of joint modeling depends on task relatedness, data quality, and loss design.

Finally, current models are generally opaque and lack interpretability. Most DL approaches function as black boxes, offering numerical predictions without insight into the underlying biochemical mechanisms or residue-level determinants. This hinders their utility in hypothesis generation, enzyme engineering, or knowledge-driven model refinement. Although attention mechanisms and attribution methods are gaining traction in related fields, their systematic application in enzyme kinetic parameter modeling is still in its infancy.

Standardized benchmarking and evaluation. Future efforts should begin with the construction of high-quality benchmark datasets that provide complete and standardized metadata, including pH, temperature, buffer composition, protein solubility, substrate concentrations, cofactors, and assay formats. To reduce data leakage and overfitting, enzyme sequences and substrate molecules should be clustered based on appropriate similarity thresholds (e.g. sequence identity for proteins) to enforce nonredundant splits. Benchmarks should reflect a broad distribution of enzyme classes and kinetic ranges and include OOD subsets to assess robustness under realistic deployment scenarios. In parallel, evaluation protocols must go beyond simple correlation metrics. While PCC is commonly reported, more informative criteria such as SCC, RMSE, , top-k accuracy, top-N enrichment, and uncertainty calibration should be incorporated. For generative or sampling-based models, frameworks should also assess the biological plausibility and functional realism of predicted variants. A centralized community benchmark—featuring shared datasets, metrics, baseline models, and clearly defined split strategies—would enable fair comparisons and accelerate progress.

Multimodal biochemical representation. Accurate modeling of enzyme kinetic parameters requires the joint consideration of heterogeneous biochemical factors, including enzyme sequence, 3D structure, substrate/product features, cofactors, and environmental conditions [11, 13]. Beyond sequence and structural representations for independent molecular entities, interaction-aware representations of enzyme–substrate or enzyme–product complexes could provide additional information for kinetic parameter prediction. Such features may be derived from biomolecular structure prediction methods such as AlphaFold-3 [110], molecular docking [39], or other interaction modeling approaches, although their reliability will depend on the accuracy of complex structures, ligand poses, and relevant catalytic conformations.

Future models could employ architectures with shared encoders and hierarchical or graph-structured decoders that reflect known mathematical relationships and potential mechanistic links among kinetic parameters. Dynamic loss balancing and constraint-based regularization may further enhance prediction consistency and biological plausibility.

Enhancing model interpretability. Beyond improving accuracy, future models should provide interpretable predictions that offer mechanistic insights. This can be achieved by integrating attention mechanisms, residue-level attribution methods (e.g. integrated gradients and saliency maps), or perturbation-based analyses to highlight input features most responsible for predicted outputs. Interpretability should be benchmarked using curated DMS datasets, enzyme variants with measured kinetic changes, or experimentally verified active-site and substrate-recognition residues [15], providing a pathway from black-box prediction to biologically meaningful discovery and rational enzyme design.

Collectively, these findings support an integrative ML perspective that connects improved data curation, multimodal biochemical representation, biologically consistent modeling, rigorous evaluation, uncertainty estimation, and model interpretability for enzyme kinetic parameter prediction. Future models that incorporate these directions are expected to be not only more accurate, but also more generalizable, better calibrated, and biologically informative—ultimately facilitating the rational design and engineering of enzymes in both research and industrial contexts.
