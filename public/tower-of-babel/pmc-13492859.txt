Designing, psychometric, and application of measurement instrument for Self-Care Literacy in Prevention of Prostate Cancer (SCL-PCa): A mixed-method study protocol
Authors: Ali Ramezankhani, Mohammad Reza Razaghi, Amir Kavousi, Tayebeh Marashi, Ahmad Moradi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Prostate cancer is one of the most common cancers in men worldwide. Prostate cancer prevention requires self-care; however, there seems to be insufficient information about men’s self-care literacy, and almost no studies have addressed this issue. Therefore, this study aimed to design and psychometric evaluation of a measurement instrument for Self-Care Literacy in Prevention of Prostate Cancer (SCL-PCa) in men over 40 years old.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This mixed-method study will adopt a sequential exploratory approach (qualitative–quantitative) and will be conducted in Iran. In the qualitative phase, participants will include urologists, oncologists, nurses, and men aged 40 and older, selected via purposive sampling with maximum variation. Data will be collected through in-depth, semi-structured face-to-face interviews and analyzed using content analysis with MAXQDA 20 (VERBI Software, Berlin, Germany). The initial items for the instrument will be developed in this phase. In the second (quantitative) phase, the instrument’s psychometric properties, including face, content, construct validity, and reliability (internal consistency and stability), will be evaluated.CONCLUSIONS:The validated and reliable instrument developed in this study will address the existing gap in assessing prostate cancer-related self-care literacy. Furthermore, it will provide a foundation for designing effective educational interventions and targeted health planning in future research.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most common cancer diagnosis among men and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. In its early stages, PCa may not produce any symptoms and often has an indolent course, which may only require active monitoring.[1] Lowering the risk by limiting high-fat foods, increasing the intake of vegetables and fruits, and performing more exercise is possible.[2] In 2020 alone, there were more than 1.4 million new cases of PCa worldwide.[3] By 2040, the incidence of PCa is estimated to increase to nearly 2.3 million new cases and 740,000 deaths, primarily due to population growth and aging.[4] The effect of self-care on improving health and lessening costs has been proven in numerous studies.[5] Self-care literacy encompasses the knowledge, motivation, and skills of individuals, families, and communities to enhance and sustain health, prevent illness, and manage health challenges—both independently and with the assistance of healthcare providers.[6] Actively participating in one’s health and wellness through self-care offers multiple advantages, such as improved choices, enhanced care, and greater value.[7] The scope of self-care in this definition includes health promotion, disease prevention and control, self-medication, providing care to dependent persons, seeking hospital/specialist care if necessary, and rehabilitation, including palliative care.[8] Self-care is a broad term that encompasses related concepts such as self-treatment, self-management, self-efficacy, self-monitoring, self-help, patient empowerment, consumer empowerment, and personal care.[9] Self-care literacy equips people with cost-effective strategies, empowering them to find, understand, assess, and use information. This allows for informed decisions and better health management, ultimately helping to maintain and improve their quality of life.[6] By accessing and using available health information and resources, individuals can take control of their well-being, leading to better health management and disease prevention.[10] Projections show that by 2040, PCa will significantly increase in prevalence in Iran, posing major challenges to the healthcare system.[11] PCa is currently one of the major health concerns worldwide.[12] In addition to innate factors, environmental factors such as differences in dietary habits, increased body mass index (BMI), and obesity are also strongly associated with PCa.[13] Despite the importance of measuring self-care literacy in PCa prevention with a dedicated tool, no studies were found in this area. In this regard, due to the lack of appropriate tools in this field, we decided to design and validate a tool to measure self-care literacy in prostate cancer prevention (SCL-PCa) using quantitative and qualitative methods.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study aims to conceptualize self-care literacy to inform the development and testing of the constructs for the Self-care Literacy in Prevention of Prostate Cancer (SCL-PCa) Questionnaire. This research is a sequential exploratory mixed methods study with qualitative and quantitative sequencing design [Figure 1]. Studies utilizing a mixed-methods approach are often grounded in the philosophical framework of pragmatism. According to this perspective, integrating both qualitative and quantitative methods enhances the understanding of the phenomenon under investigation. One common design within this approach is the sequential exploratory design, which involves a two-phase process. In the qualitative phase, researchers collect and analyze qualitative data to develop an initial version of the instrument, while the quantitative phase assesses the instrument’s psychometric properties.[14]

Diagram of study design steps

Phase 1: Qualitative study

This phase will be done to identify dimensions and to define the concept of SCL-PCa with in-depth interviews. The inductive conventional content analysis method will be employed to clarify this concept.

Study participants and sampling

The research environment includes comprehensive health centers, offices, urology and oncology departments of hospitals, and participants’ workplaces or homes in Khuzestan, Iran in 2024. Participants in the qualitative section will include healthcare providers such as urologists, oncologists, nurses, health education and promotion specialists, men over 40 years of age, and people with PCa (with maximum diversity in age, marital status, education level, specialty, and work experience) who will be selected using purposive sampling methods. The sample size process will continue until data saturation.[15] Participants will determine the schedule, duration, and place of the interview. Inclusion criteria for healthcare providers will include at least one year of work experience, and for healthy men over 40 years old, at least elementary literacy and willingness to participate in the study are necessary.

Data collection tools and techniques

Data is collected through in-depth and semi-structured interviews after explaining the research objectives to the participants and obtaining their informed consent. An interview guide is used to conduct the interview. The interview guide will be developed based on a literature review and expert consultations, then finalized upon approval by the research team to ensure clarity and relevance. During the interview, the researcher will use probing questions such as “Can you explain this further?” and “Can you give an example of this?” etc., by the initial responses of the individuals and the interview guide.

All interviews are conducted using an audio recorder and then transcribed verbatim. Field notes are utilized alongside the interviews. Each interview will last a maximum of 60 minutes, and the participant is free to withdraw from the interview at any time. Data collection continues until data saturation, signifying that no new themes or codes emerge from the data during the interviews, and existing codes are fully explored.[15] However, three additional interviews will be conducted to provide further confirmation.

Data analysis

Conventional qualitative content analysis is used to analyze the data.[16] While conducting and recording the interviews, the researcher simultaneously regularly transcribes the data and codes the recorded sentences and phrases. Final categories and themes are then extracted from these codes. Data analysis will be conducted concurrently with data collection using conventional qualitative content analysis, adhering to the five-step approach outlined by Graneheim and Lundman: 1) conducting interviews and reviewing them immediately afterward, 2) thoroughly reading the entire text to develop a general understanding of its content, 3) identifying meaning units and assigning initial codes, 4) grouping similar initial codes into broader categories, and 5) identifying the main themes within these categories.[1718] The interview texts and codes will be organized using MAXQDA 20 (VERBI Software, Berlin, Germany).

Rigor and trustworthiness

The four criteria proposed by Guba and Lincoln—credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability—will be applied to ensure data accuracy.[1920]

To increase credibility, measures such as long-term engagement with the topic, peer debriefing, and member re-checks will be used. Stepwise replication by the research team and review of the data by independent individuals will be considered to achieve dependability. To ensure confirmability, expert researchers will examine the accuracy of the entire research process.

An accurate and clear description of the context, the participants, sampling with maximum variation, the sampling process, data collection, and data analysis will be applied to enhance the transferability.

Second phase: Quantitative study

In this phase of the research, psychometric evaluation will be conducted to determine the validity and reliability of the instrument designed to measure the SCL-PCa. Validity will be assessed using face, content, and construct validity, while reliability will be determined using stability and internal consistency.

Face validity

Face validity is related to the appearance and apparent attractiveness of an instrument, which may affect the instrument’s acceptability by respondents.[21] The face validity of this study will be assessed by quantitative and qualitative methods. To calculate the quantitative face validity of the instrument, the item effect will be used. For this purpose, a 5-point Likert scale will be used for each item in the instrument. Participants will assign a value to each item from 1 = not at all important to 5 = extremely important.[22] The item impact score is calculated by the following formula: Impact score = Frequency (%) ×Importance. An impact score above 1.5 indicates that the item is suitable for later analysis and will be retained.[2324]

For qualitative face validity, 10 participants will be asked to comment on each item’s level of difficulty, appropriateness, and ambiguity. At the same time, four experts and linguists will comment on grammar and style. The comments will be incorporated into the instrument. The time needed to complete the instrument will also be estimated.

Content validity

In the qualitative content validity section, 10 experts in urology, oncology, and instrument design will be asked to express their opinions on the content of the instrument constructs in terms of grammar, use of appropriate words, placement of items in the appropriate location, and appropriate scoring.[24] Quantitative content validity is assessed in two ways: Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI). The CVR for each item is calculated using Lawshe’s formula. Ten experts evaluate whether an item is essential for operational the concept (essential = 3; useful but not essential = 2; not essential = 1).[2526] The following formula will be used to calculate CVR: (CVR = (ne-N/2)/(N/2)), in which “Ne” is the number of experts evaluating “essential” and “N” is the total number of experts. The 10 experts were referred to in evaluating the CVR, with 0.62 regarded as acceptable.[27]

To calculate the CVI, 10 experts who participated in determining the CVR are asked to rate each item’s relevance using a 4-point Likert scale (e.g., 1: not relevant; 2: somewhat relevant; 3: relevant; and 4: quite relevant).[2829] The CVI score is calculated by dividing the sum of favorable scores (ranking 3 or 4) by the number of experts. Items with a CVI above 0.79 are suitable and will be retained.[27]

Construct validity

Factor analysis is a key method for assessing construct validity and the predictability of variables.[30] In this study, construct validity will be evaluated using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). In this study, a multistage cluster sampling method was used to select samples from men over 40 years of age. The sample size for each item designed for the instrument is 3–10 samples. EFA is performed using the principal components method with varimax rotation. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett’s tests are used to assess sampling adequacy and the appropriateness of the data for factor analysis. A KMO value greater than 0.9 indicates excellent sampling adequacy.[3132] Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity should have significant results (P < 0.05).[23]

The purpose of the CFA is to assess a factor structure suggested by the EFA and to validate it. The root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), goodness of fit index (GFI), normed fit index (NFI), and comparative fit index (CFI) will be used to fit the model. An RMSEA index of less than 0.08 indicates a good fit, also for GFI, NFI, and CFI, a value of more than 0.9 indicates a good fit for the model.[33]

Statistics

In this study, all statistical analyses will be conducted by using the SPSS software. The maximum error accepted in all tests is 5%.

Reliability

Two methods of determining the reliability of the instrument are used: internal consistency and stability. Internal consistency will be evaluated by various statistical indicators such as Cronbach’s alpha (α), McDonald’s omega (Ω), and the average inter-item correlation (AIC) for the instrument and its subscales. Coefficient values of α and Ω exceeding 0.70 or higher is considered acceptable.[34] Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) will be used to assess the stability. ICC will be calculated at two-week intervals among a sample of 30 men over 40 years of age. ICC values between 0.7 and 0.8 indicate acceptable stability.[35]

Ethical considerations

All participants will complete the written informed consent form for participation. They will be fully informed about the aims of the study. Participation is completely voluntary, and participants can withdraw from the study at any stage. Note-taking or audio recording will be done with the permission of the participants, and the audio files will be deleted after the research. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (Reference No: IR.SBM.PHNS.REC.1402.109).

Discussion

This study represents a pioneering effort to develop a standardized and reliable tool for assessing self-care literacy in the context of PCa prevention, marking an innovative step in men’s health research.

Health literacy and self-care literacy play a critical role in disease prevention, particularly in the case of cancers. In modern societies, where access to information has become faster and more convenient, individuals’ ability to understand and utilize health-related information can significantly reduce the risk of developing diseases. In this regard, self-care literacy empowers individuals to make more informed decisions about their health and, through effective risk management, lowers the likelihood of disease onset.

A key aspect of advancing health and self-care literacy lies in designing tools that enable researchers to accurately and scientifically evaluate individuals’ levels of self-care literacy. In this study, a mixed-method approach (qualitative-quantitative) was employed, allowing for an in-depth exploration of concepts related to self-care literacy through qualitative interviews, followed by the quantitative measurement of these concepts. This comprehensive approach enhances the validity and reliability of the developed tool, ensuring that the final instrument covers all dimensions of self-care literacy.

Conclusion

The instrument developed in this study can help identify strengths and weaknesses in men’s knowledge and skills related to PCa prevention. Furthermore, it can serve as a standardized instrument in future research, aiding in the design of educational interventions and the promotion of preventive behaviors.
