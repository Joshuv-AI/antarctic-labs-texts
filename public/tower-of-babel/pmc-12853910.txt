A proteogenomic gene signature defines prognostic subgroups highlighting PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway as a therapeutic vulnerability in myeloid malignancies
Authors: Fan He, Shuyang Lin, Bei Gao, Varun Ramesh, Alexander B Kim, Tim Kong, Daniel A C Fisher, Christopher T Letson, Molly Brakhane, Mary Fulbright, Yanbo Yu, Marco Sardiello, Jorge Di Paola, Stephen M Sykes, Stephen T Oh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMyeloid malignancies, including acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN), exhibit overlapping pathophysiology. Chronic MPNs can transform into secondary AML (sAML), which is associated with poor prognosis and limited treatment options. However, the process and prognostic significance of leukemic transformation remain incompletely understood.MethodThrough a two-sample bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis, we showed that genetic liability to MPN significantly predicts the risk of developing AML, establishing MPN as the precursor to leukemia. To identify mediators of this risk, we integrated population-level plasma proteomics data, identifying 55 proteins associated with MPN. Upon integrative analysis with the BEAT-AML cohort, we developed a prognostic proteogenomic gene signature, showing that higher expression of CDCP1, CRISP3, and DCXR, alongside lower MPO levels, correlates with worse AML outcomes. We further performed pharmacogenomic analysis to identify vulnerability to PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway inhibition in high-risk AML. In vitro and in vivo experiments validated the efficacy of mTOR inhibition in myeloid malignancies.ResultsThis gene signature effectively stratified patients by risk, with significant survival differences across the BEAT-AML and TCGA-LAML cohorts, and revealed immune alterations in high-risk groups, including elevated monocyte prevalence and cytokine signaling activity. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) further suggested enrichment of these genes in progenitor cells and AML blasts. Drug sensitivity predictions suggested that high-risk AML patients may be particularly responsive to PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway inhibitors. Consistently, we observed upregulation of the genes in cell line models harboring MPN and AML mutations, which was suppressible via dual PI3K/mTOR inhibitor omipalisib. The efficiency of PI3K/mTOR inhibition in myeloid malignancies was further corroborated by results from multiple in vivo models.ConclusionTogether, our findings revealed shared molecular features across MPN and AML, identified a prognostic gene signature for risk stratification, and provided rationale for PI3K/mTOR inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy in myeloid malignancies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02568-3.Keywords: Proteogenomic, Prognostic model, Myeloid malignancy, Pharmacogenomics, PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a clonal hematopoietic disorder marked by a deficiency of functional myeloid-lineage cells and an excess of undifferentiated myeloblasts. AML is the most common type of leukemia in adults, with a highly variable prognosis, diverse clinical features, and poor outcomes, representing a major global health burden [1]. Despite recent advances in the molecular profiling of AML, the development of a robust prognostic model, guided by population-level omic data, remains underexplored [2]. Identification of prognostic biomarkers is critical to enhancing risk stratification, clinical decision-making, and patient outcomes.

Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are characterized by abnormal proliferation of blood cells and share multiple features with AML, including clonal hematopoiesis and altered bone marrow microenvironments. MPN can progress to secondary AML, which is associated with poor prognosis and limited treatment options. Studies have shown that hyperactive inflammatory signaling, such as NF-κB and IL1 overproduction, drives clonal expansion in MPNs and predicts poor outcomes in AML [3–9]. Despite their shared molecular features, the mediator of MPN conferred risk to AML remains incompletely understood.

The proteome manifests the intricate pathological dynamics underlying disease progression, particularly in hematological diseases [10]. Population-level omic-scale characterization of myeloid malignancy offers unprecedented insights into the disease mechanism and clinical heterogeneity, surpassing what can be inferred from genomic mutations alone. We hypothesized that genetic liability to MPN predicts the risk of developing AML, and this increased risk is, in part, mediated through alterations in the plasma proteome.

As shown in the schematic diagram (Fig. S1), we first established the genomic relationship between MPN and AML via mendelian randomization (MR). Next, we employed cis protein quantitative trait loci (cis-pQTL) as an initial screening tool to identify proteins that are associated with MPN (Fig. S1A). We then validated these biomarkers in the BEAT-AML cohort, deriving a 4-gene proteogenomic signature through Lasso-Cox regression (Fig. S1B), which is consistent with the TCGA-LAML cohort. The proteogenomic signature is also enriched in myeloid progenitors and malignant cells. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), mutation profiling, and pharmacogenomics corroborated the functional significance of the signature (Fig. S1C). We further investigated targeted therapies against myeloid malignancies using cell lines, patient samples, and murine models (Figs. S1D-E). Our findings underscore how the orchestration of genetic predisposition, multiomic characterization, and therapeutic evaluation in cellular and animal models can effectively elucidate disease risk and inform treatment strategy in myeloid malignancies.

Results

MR analyses reveal proteins associated with myeloid disease risks

To test the hypothesis that genetic liability to MPN underlies the risk of developing AML, we first performed bidirectional MR to interrogate the relationship between MPN and AML. Genetically predicted MPN risk was consistently associated with increased AML risk across various MR methods (Figs. 1A and B, Supplemental Tables 1–2), with no evidence of heterogeneity, pleiotropy, or outliers (Supplemental Table 3). Conversely, there was no evidence supporting reverse causality from AML to MPN, supporting a unidirectional relationship between these two myeloid malignancies (Fig. 1A, Supplemental Table 4). To minimize confounding due to nearby correlated variants (linkage disequilibrium), we conducted a genome-wide Bayesian colocalization analysis to identify loci where AML and MPN are likely influenced by the same genetic variant. This analysis highlighted rs10974960 as a shared SNP (H4 = 1; Fig. 1C, Supplemental Table 5), which maps to the JAK2 locus—a gene frequently mutated in MPN and sAML patients [11, 12]. After establishing the relationship between MPN and AML, we further screened for plasma proteins that may contribute to the leukemic progression of MPN.

Our cis-pQTL analysis from population-level plasma proteome data sourced from the UK Biobank (UKB) identified 55 candidate proteins associated with increased MPN risk (P < 0.05, OR > 1.0; Fig. 1D, Supplemental Tables 6 and 7). While the protective proteins may also be biologically relevant (P < 0.05, OR < 1.0), our subsequent investigations focused on the risk-conferring proteins. These candidates were prioritized for prognostic evaluation in subsequent analysis with AML. Details of the GWAS datasets used are provided in Supplemental Table 8. Given that this analysis was designed as a hypothesis-generating screen, we did not apply multiple-testing correction. We reasoned that stringent correction methods, such as Bonferroni adjustment, would be overly conservative and obscure biologically meaningful signals. Thus, we perform downstream analyses with the 55 risk proteins. Together, these findings supported a proteogenomic axis linking the inherited risk of MPN as a precursor to leukemic transformation and proteomic alterations as biomarkers and mediators of this process.

Construction of a proteogenomic gene signature for prognosis prediction in the BEAT-AML cohort

Identification of candidate proteins alone is insufficient to provide prognostic information or deeper biological insights due to the inherent limitation of GWAS in lacking individual-level clinical information. To address this, we interrogated two independent AML cohorts with transcriptome data and individual-level clinical information available to inform the complex disease dynamics of myeloid malignancy. We first evaluated the prognostic value of the 55 candidate proteins in AML using Lasso-Cox and Log-rank survival analyses in the BEAT-AML cohort. Four proteins were selected to build a proteogenomic gene signature from the 55 candidate proteins that significantly predicted survival outcome in the BEAT-AML cohort based on conserved results from both Lasso-COX and Log-rank analyses. (Fig. 1E). AML patients with low expression of CDCP1, CRISP3, and DCXR, but high expression of MPO, had a significant survival advantage (Fig. 2A). While MPO is a recognized differentiation marker where lower expression correlates with poor AML survival [13], the roles of CDCP1, CRISP3, and DCXR in AML remain undefined. To explore the cellular origin and biological relevance of our proteogenomic risk signature, we investigated the expression pattern of these genes across hematopoietic cell types. Re-analysis of our previously published scRNA-seq results of CD34+ and CD14+ bone marrow cells from two healthy donors revealed significant enrichment of MPO, CDCP1, and DCXR in myeloid progenitor cells when compared to hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and mature monocytes (Figs. S2A-S2D). DCXR was expressed across multiple types of myeloid progenitor cells (Fig. S2B and S2E), whereas MPO was highly enriched in granulocyte-macrophage progenitor (GMP; Figs. S2C and S2F). Analysis of the normal hematopoiesis with AML datasets from Bloodspot (https://www.fobinf.com/) showed enrichment of MPO in GMP and DCXR in several myeloid progenitors compared to HSCs and mature CD14+ monocytes (Figs. S2G and S2H), consistent with our scRNA-seq results. This dataset also showed significantly elevated expression of MPO and DCXR in AML cells compared to HSCs (Fig. S2G and S2H), suggesting their roles in myeloid differentiation and AML pathogenesis.

Building on these insights, we next evaluated the prognostic significance of the proteogenomic signature. To determine the combined prognostic significance of these four genes, we developed a risk score signature using Lasso-Cox regression (Figs. S3A and S3B). The risk score was calculated as follows: Risk score = [MPO * (−0.04101012)] + [CRISP3 * (0.03653976)] + [CDCP1 * (0.03794669)] + [DCXR * (0.04982226)]. Patients were dichotomized into low-risk (n = 185) and high-risk (n = 186) groups based on the median threshold of the risk score, with survival rates inversely related to risk score (Fig. S3C). ROC analysis indicated that the risk score had moderately strong predictive accuracy, with an AUC of 0.7 (Fig. 2B). Time-dependent AUC values for 2-, 10-, and 25-month overall survival were 0.68, 0.66, and 0.64, respectively, suggesting consistent prognostic value across time points (Fig. 2C). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed a median survival of 21.9 months for low-risk versus 11.9 months for high-risk patients (log-rank P < 0.0001, Fig. 2D), signifying more aggressive disease progression in the high-risk patients.

To explore associations with clinical features, we performed univariate Cox regression (Fig. 2E). Features with P < 0.05 were incorporated into multivariate analysis, suggesting the risk score as an independent prognostic factor with the highest hazard ratio (HR: 3.01 [1.2–7.6]; Fig. 2F). Aalen’s additive regression indicated an increasing impact of the risk score on hazard rate over time (Fig. S3D). Furthermore, we created a nomogram that integrates the risk score with other independent prognostic factors. Higher total points predict worse survival, providing a quantitative survival prediction tool for clinicians. A mock patient with total points of 266 is shown in blue dots as an example (Fig. 2G). Calibration and parameter distribution plots are included in the supplemental materials (Figs. S3E and S3F).

Validation of the proteogenomic gene signature in the TCGA-LAML cohort

To validate the accuracy of our proteogenomic gene signature, we tested the model independently in the TCGA-LAML cohort, which included 130 AML patients. High-risk patients (n = 65) had a significantly lower survival rate (p < 0.0001) and a notably shorter median survival time (10.1 months vs. 47.7 months for low-risk patients) compared to low-risk patients (n = 65), underscoring the robustness of the gene signature (Fig. 3A). Consistently, an overall AUC of 0.74, along with 1-, 2-, and 3-year survival AUCs of 0.73, 0.77, and 0.77, respectively, highlights the robust prognostic performance of the model over time (Figs. 3B and C). The prognostic value of individual genes in TCGA-LAML is shown in Fig. S4A. Stratifying patients by risk scores revealed a clear survival advantage in the low-risk group (Fig. S4B).

To identify clinical variables associated with overall survival, we performed univariate Cox proportional hazards regression for each clinical and biological factor. HRs, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values were calculated for all variables (Fig. 3D). Age, ELN2017, and risk score were found to be statistically significant (p < 0.05) and were therefore included in the multivariate analysis. In the multivariate model, risk score emerged as a strong independent predictor of poor survival with the largest effect size (Fig. 3E).

We subsequently examined the relationship between risk scores and clinicopathological parameters. Analysis across French-American-British (FAB) and ELN2017 classifications revealed significantly elevated risk scores in patients with ELN2017 poor-risk status and those classified as FAB M0 or M5—subtypes known to be associated with poor prognosis in AML (Figs. 3F and S4C). Consistently, there was enrichment of ELN2017 poor-risk category in patients classified as FAB M0/M5 groups (Fig. S4D). Importantly, the risk score also showed significant correlations with age, platelet count, ELN2017 staging, monocyte percentage, and bone marrow cellularity, suggesting that the proteogenomic gene signature reflects biological processes such as aging, leukemogenesis, and myelopoiesis (Fig. 3G, Supplemental Table 9). Collectively, these findings indicated that our proteogenomic gene signature not only established an AML prognostic model but also aligned closely with established clinicopathological features.

The proteogenomic gene signature differentiates AML patients with distinct immune landscapes and transcriptional profiles

The plasma proteome plays a central role in mediating the disease microenvironment, particularly in hematological malignancies [10]. Upon establishing the prognostic relevance of our gene signature, we next evaluated its association with the immune microenvironment in AML. We first applied the ESTIMATE algorithm to estimate the fraction of stromal and immune cells in tumor samples based on gene expression profiles to infer tumor purity [14]. Significantly higher immune and estimate scores indicated an altered microenvironment in high-risk AML (Fig. S5A). To further dissect the immune cell alteration, we complemented ESTIMATE with the CIBERSORT algorithm to infer the immune cell composition in both high and low risk groups [15]. Monocytes emerged as the most abundant cell type in AML samples, with their prevalence further increasing in high-risk patients (Figs. 4A and B, and S5B). Additionally, monocyte counts were negatively correlated with lymphoid lineage cells such as resting NK cells, resting memory CD4 T cells, CD8 T cells, and naive B cells (Figs. S5C and S5D). These results suggest that the tumor microenvironment of the high-risk group is more heterogeneous than low risk group, with aberrant lineage expansion toward monocyte cell populations.

Interestingly, we and others have previously demonstrated the central role of monocytes and chronic inflammation in myeloid neoplasms [6, 8, 9, 16–18]. Building on these findings, we interrogated the transcriptomic dynamics of AML patients stratified by our proteogenomic risk score. Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis between high- and low-risk TCGA-LAML patients identified 1,793 DEGs (p < 0.05, |log2 fold change| ≥ 0.5). Of these, 1,319 genes were significantly up-regulated, and 474 were down-regulated in the high-risk group (Fig. 4C and Supplemental Table 10). Notably, GSEA revealed activation of multiple inflammatory pathways in high-risk AML samples, including interferon signaling, NF-κB, and IL6-JAK-STAT3 pathways (Fig. 4D). The activation of these pathways, commonly observed in MPNs [3, 16], supports the hypothesis that hyperinflammation may represent a shared pathogenic molecular feature across myeloid malignancies and is echoed by our proteogenomic signature. Consistently, gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed upregulation of genes involved in cytokine production and leukocyte activation in the high-risk group (Fig. 4E). In addition, KEGG pathway analysis corroborated the enrichment of cytokine and chemokine signaling pathways among high-risk patients (Fig. 4F). In sum, the proteogenomic gene signature suggests the activation of inflammatory pathways in high-risk AML, offering insights into shared mechanisms across the disease spectrum of myeloid malignancies.

The proteogenomic gene signature separates AML patients with distinct mutation landscapes

To further investigate the molecular foundation of risk stratification based on the proteogenomic signature, we analyzed the somatic mutational landscape associated with high- and low-risk groups. Interestingly, we observed a distinct gene mutation pattern that may contribute to the divergent pathophysiology observed. DNMT3A and RUNX1 mutation frequencies were significantly different between the two groups (Figs. 5A and S6A). DNMT3A was the most frequently mutated gene in the high-risk group (37%), compared to only 11% in the low-risk group (Figs. S6B and S6C). This observation aligns with previous studies reporting that DNMT3A mutations are associated with poor prognosis in AML [19]. Interestingly, DNMT3A mutated clonal hematopoiesis has been implicated in promoting systemic inflammatory conditions, including periodontitis, arthritis, and bone loss. Notably, these pathological conditions can be reversed by treatment with the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin [20]. Similarly, RUNX1 mutations were significantly more frequent in high-risk patients (Figs. 5A and S6A), consistent with previous reports that RUNX1 mutations are predictive of poor prognosis in AML [21]. In contrast, CEBPA mutations showed a strong trend toward enrichment in the low-risk group (Figs. S6B and S6C), supporting prior evidence that CEBPA mutations are linked to favorable prognoses [22]. Together, these findings highlight how our proteogenomic signature reflects the genomic heterogeneity of AML and is consistent with known survival outcomes associated with the adverse mutations.

Pharmacogenomic analysis reveals differential vulnerability to PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway inhibition in high-risk AML

Given the distinct transcriptomic programs observed between high- and low-risk groups, we hypothesized that these subgroups may exhibit differential vulnerability to specific classes of therapeutics. To assess potential clinical drug response, we utilized the oncoPredict package, which integrates patient gene expression profiles with large-scale pharmacogenomic data derived from thousands of cancer cell lines to predict sensitivity to hundreds of anti-cancer compounds [23]. We first assigned each patient to high- or low-risk based on the proteogenomic gene signature. Drug responses were then predicted by aligning individual patients’ transcriptomes to the reference sensitivity data curated from GDSC (Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer), as implemented in oncoPredict package.

Using this approach, we identified 46 compounds with significantly different predicted responses between high- and low-risk groups (Fig. 5B and Supplemental Table 11). These compounds primarily targeted the PI3K/AKT/mTOR, apoptosis, and ERK/MAPK signaling pathways (Fig. 5C). Notably, high-risk AML patients exhibited greater predicted vulnerability to six out of seven inhibitors targeting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway (Fig. 5D), implicating the pathological dependency of high-risk AML on the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. In contrast, compounds targeting apoptosis and ERK/MAPK signaling pathways showed better predicted efficacy in low-risk AML patients (Figs. S6D and S6E). Combined with our earlier findings of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway activation in MPNs [16, 24], these results suggest that PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling may represent a common feature of myeloid neoplasms and a promising therapeutic target.

PI3K/AKT/mTOR Inhibition disrupts oncogenic signaling in AML cells

To interrogate the interplay between the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and the functional relevance of our proteogenomic gene signature, we next examined their modulation by driver mutations and PI3K/mTOR inhibition. Notably, overexpression of MPLW515L and FLT3-ITD genes in the Ba/F3 murine cell line significantly upregulated DCXR at both RNA and protein levels (Figs. 6A and B), which echoes our scRNA-seq and Bloodspot analyses (Figs. S2A-H). Moreover, treatment with omipalisib, a potent dual PI3K/mTOR inhibitor, decreased DCXR and MPO levels in both Ba/F3 and THP-1 human leukemic cells (Figs. 6B and C).

Next, we explored the therapeutic effects of PI3K/mTOR inhibitors in myeloid leukemia cell lines (HEL, U937, MOLM13, MV4-11). To broaden our assessment of the PI3K/mTOR inhibition, we included another inhibitor, sapanisertib, in our subsequent assays. Treatment with the mTOR inhibitors omipalisib and sapanisertib significantly inhibited AML cell proliferation (Figs. 6D and E). Additionally, PI3K/mTOR inhibitors inhibited oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and glycolysis, as evidenced by a decrease in oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) in Seahorse assays of MV4-11 cells (Figs. 6F and G). Both mTOR inhibitors induced apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner, as shown by an increase in apoptotic cells in MV4-11 and MOLM13 cells (Figs. 6H and S6F).

Mechanistically, we corroborated the on-target inhibition of p-mTOR, p-Akt, and p-S6 by the mTOR inhibitors in AML cell lines U937 and MV4-11 cells with minimal effects on P-ERK. (Fig. 6I). Furthermore, mTOR inhibitors reduced colony formation in the colony-forming unit (CFU) assay from MPN and sAML patients, suggesting their effects on inhibiting hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell (HSPC) proliferation and differentiation (Figs. 6J, S7A, and S7B). Taken together, our findings suggest that mTOR inhibition reduces disease-associated molecular features in cell lines.

PI3K/AKT/mTOR suppression is efficacious in vivo for treating myeloid malignancies

To further investigate the therapeutic effects of mTOR inhibition in vivo, we employed the MLL-AF9 model, a commonly used AML murine model known for its aggressive progression and rapid death [25](Fig. 7A). Treatment with omipalisib was initiated two weeks following transplantation of MLL-AF9 GFP tagged transduced splenocytes into recipient mice, which led to significantly prolonged survival in these mice (Fig. 7B) and notably ameliorated disease progression. Specifically, omipalisib treatment led to significantly reduced white blood cell (WBC), monocyte, and neutrophil counts based on HEMAVET CBC (complete blood count) reading, compared to the vehicle group (Figs. 7C-E).

Additionally, as assessed by flow cytometry, Omipalisib-treated mice exhibited a substantially reduced GFP+ ratio and a preserved proportion of CD45.1+ residual immune cells at day 28 (Figs. 7F and S7C), suggesting that omipalisib selectively targets AML cells engraftment while sparing residual immune cells. Further, omipalisib also led to a significant reduction of splenomegaly without affecting body weight at the study endpoint (Figs. 7G and S7D), further suggesting its therapeutic potential in AML.

To investigate the transcriptomic impact of PI3K/mTOR inhibition in vivo, we performed bulk RNA-sequencing of femur and tibia bone marrow cells from vehicle control (NC) and omipalisib-treated (OMI) mice. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation between NC and OMI samples, indicating a distinct transcriptional response to treatment (Fig. 7H). Differential gene expression analysis identified 1292 genes upregulated and 1969 genes downregulated in the NC group relative to OMI (Fig. 7I). Notably, several inflammation-related genes, including Tnf, Cxcl10, Ccl4, and Ccl5, were significantly downregulated following omipalisib treatment (Fig. 7J). GSEA demonstrated significant enrichment of the mTORC1 signaling pathway and multiple pro-inflammatory signaling pathways—such as interferon and TNF responses—in the NC group (Figs. 7K, S7E, and S7F). These results not only corroborate the on-target inhibition of mTOR by omipalisib but also highlight inflammation as a key axis modulated by mTOR signaling in the leukemic bone marrow microenvironment.

To evaluate the broader applicability of mTOR inhibition in myeloid malignancies, we assessed the impact of omipalisib on a JAK2V617F knock-in mouse MPN model (Fig. S7G). Omipalisib was well-tolerated by these mice, with no significant changes in body weight or liver size over the course of four weeks of treatment (Figs. S7H and S7I). Importantly, omipalisib treatment resulted in significant improvements in MPN disease features, including decreased WBC counts and reduced splenomegaly (Figs. S7J-S7L). A trend toward a reduced platelet count was also observed (Fig. S7M). However, hematocrit (HCT) levels were not significantly affected by omipalisib treatment (Fig. S7N).

In summary, omipalisib treatment in vivo is efficacious across both MPN and AML murine models. These results underscore the importance of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis in sustaining the malignant cell state and support its potential as a therapeutic target for myeloid malignancies.

Discussion

MR has emerged as a powerful tool in epidemiology, offering the ability to infer relationships while minimizing confounding and addressing reverse causality. MR has been successfully applied to identify risk factors and therapeutic targets in a range of complex diseases, but without prognosis information and subsequent validation [26–28]. In this study, we harnessed MR to interrogate the association between MPN and AML, and identified proteins predictive of myeloid disease risk. The prioritized proteins were then used to build prognostic models and discover actionable targets, exemplifying an integrative framework that bridges genomic association with translational applications.

Most previous prognostic models for AML have relied on predefined gene sets, such as aging, metabolism, inflammation, and immunity [29–33]. While informative, these approaches may introduce bias and inadequately capture disease heterogeneity. In contrast, our proteogenomic approach assumes no prior knowledge of specific functional pathways, deriving candidate biomarkers directly from genetic predisposition and plasma protein levels (Fig. S1). Integrating the candidate proteins with transcriptomic data and individual-level clinical information from independent AML cohorts, we developed and validated a four-gene prognostic signature, comprising MPO, CDCP1, CRISP3, and DCXR. Grounded on population-level proteogenomic data, this prognostic model stratified AML patients robustly across two independent cohorts (BEAT-AML and TCGA-LAML).

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway regulates key cellular processes, including proliferation, metabolism, apoptosis, and autophagy, and its dysregulation has been implicated in AML and other malignancies [34–36]. Aberrant activation, driven by oncogene activation or tumor suppressor loss, promotes malignant transformation and disease progression [37]. Our previous work demonstrated the interplay between cellular metabolism, PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, and inflammation in MPNs, with therapeutic effects of α-ketoglutarate mediated through inhibition of both PI3K/AKT/mTOR and inflammatory pathways [16]. The therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway has been extensively explored across hematologic malignancies. Since the approval of idelalisib, a first-in-class PI3K inhibitor, for relapsed follicular lymphoma in 2014, additional agents such as copanlisib, duvelisib, and umbralisib have been developed [38, 39]. However, clinical application has been limited by toxicity and modest efficacy [39]. For instance, although the PI3Kδ inhibitor parsaclisib showed promising symptom improvement in myelofibrosis, the subsequent phase 3 trial failed to meet its primary endpoint [40].

mTOR proteins belong to the PIKK (phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase-related kinase) superfamily, sharing conserved structural domains essential for cellular signaling [41]. Omipalisib is a highly potent and selective dual inhibitor of PI3K and mTOR, offering comprehensive blockade of this pathway [42]. High-risk AML patients identified by our gene signature exhibited heightened predicted sensitivity to compounds targeting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, suggesting a therapeutic vulnerability that could be exploited for precision treatment. Using AML cell lines and murine models, our results suggest that omipalisib exerts significant anti-leukemic effects in myeloid malignancies. Consistent with our findings, recent studies have also highlighted the anti-leukemic potential of omipalisib, supporting its promise as a targeted therapy for AML patients [43].

Several limitations exist in our study. First, while MR offers a robust approach to infer causality, it relies on several key assumptions. Although we applied multiple sensitivity analyses to mitigate these concerns, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Second, the population-level plasma proteomic data may not fully capture disease-specific protein alteration since it is not a direct comparison between patients versus healthy donors. Additionally, the proteomic changes do not always correspond to transcriptomic alterations. Finally, omipalisib treatments were conducted in cell lines and murine models. While informative, these experiments may not fully recapitulate human AML biology or drug response in clinical settings.

Conclusion

Collectively, our findings uncovered the association between MPN and AML, established a proteogenomic prognostic model, and validated mTOR inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy for myeloid malignancy.

Materials and methods

Reagents

The reagents used in this study have been listed in Supplemental Table 12.

Cell culture

MOLM13, HEL, MV4-11, U937, THP-1, and Ba/F3 cells were obtained from ATCC and maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin. The Ba/F3 cells were additionally supplemented with 0.5ng/mL murine IL-3. All cell lines were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 and regularly tested for mycoplasma. All cells were maintained in this standard culturing condition unless otherwise specified.

Data collection

The transcriptomic dataset and associated clinical information of the TCGA-LAML cohort were acquired from the UCSC Xena website (https://xena.ucsc.edu/). The transcriptomic dataset and associated clinical information of the BEAT-AML cohort were acquired from supplementary data of the original publication [44].

Two-sample MR

Two-sample MR was performed as previously described [45]. Briefly, the GWAS was filtered for SNPs with P-value < 5e-8 and clumped by using the online clumping (European Ancestry, R2 >0.001 and window size = 10,000 kb) function of the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.5.7). Bidirectional analyses were performed using the Inverse variance weighted (IVW), Weighted median, weighted mode, simple mode, and MR Egger.

Plasma-proteome-wide MR

Summary-level statistics for p-QTL of 2,923 plasma proteins from European ancestry were downloaded from the UK biobank (http://ukb-ppp.gwas.eu) [46]. Cis-pQTL were extracted by restricting the SNPs to be within ± 1 Mb of the transcription starting site of the protein-coding gene using the hg38 reference genome. Local clumping based on linkage disequilibrium was performed using the ieugwasr package (version 0.1.5) with Plink reference genome from European Ancestry (R2 >0.001 and window size = 10,000 kb). Next, strong instruments were extracted from the clumped cis-pQTL data by filtering for significant SNPs with P-value < 5e-6 and F-statistics > 10. Subsequently, a two-sample MR analysis was performed with the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.5.7) by individually analyzing each plasma protein as the exposure and myeloproliferative neoplasm as the outcome. Based on the IVW method, proteins with OR >1 and P-value < 0.05 were selected as the prognostic biomarkers for subsequent validation in the BEAT-AML cohort.

Bayesian colocalization

Genome-wide Bayesian colocalization was performed with the Coloc package (version 5.2.3) by imputing shared causal SNPs between MPN and AML GWAS with eQTL as the reference map. A posteriori probability of H4 >0.75 indicates that the causal SNP common to both phenotypes is statistically significant [47].

Construction and validation of the prognostic signature for AML

To explore the prognostic significance of these MPN-related genes in AML patients, the BEAT-AML cohort was used as a training dataset to identify MPN-related genes with P-values less than 0.05 by both univariate Cox regression and Kaplan-Meier analyses. DEG analysis, immune cell type inference, and potential drug susceptibility prediction

The Limma package was applied to identify the DEGs between the high- and low-risk groups. The immune, stromal, and estimate scores were calculated based on transcriptome data using the estimate package. The proportions of 22 immune cell types were calculated using the CIBERSORT algorithm. The cell proportions were compared between low- or high-risk samples using t-test, and pearson correlation was assessed between the top 10 enriched proportions. The OncoPredict package is used for drug susceptibility prediction, examining the differences of sensitivities to chemotherapy and molecular-targeted compounds between high- and low-risk patients in the TCGA-LAML cohort.

MTT assay

To assess cell viability of MOLM13, MV4-11, HEL and U937, 2 × 10³ cells per well were seeded into 96-well flat-bottom plates in six technical replicates. Cells were treated with the indicated small-molecule inhibitors. To minimize edge effects due to evaporation, empty wells were filled with PBS. After 72–96 h of incubation under standard culture conditions, AlamarBlue reagent was added directly to the culture media, and plates were returned to the incubator for an additional 2–4 h. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a BioTek microplate reader. Wells containing media and AlamarBlue without cells were included as background controls. Percent viability was calculated relative to untreated control wells after background subtraction. All conditions were performed in six technical replicates and repeated in at least two independent biological experiments.

Western blot

1 × 106 cells per well were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with the indicated inhibitors for 1 h. Cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS (800 g, 5 min) and lysed in Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad) supplemented with EDTA, protease, and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Lysates were vigorously vortexed, boiled, and loaded onto the NuPAGE Novex Gel Electrophoresis System (Thermo Fisher) for electrophoretic separation. Proteins were transferred onto methanol-activated nitrocellulose membranes using the Bio-Rad semi-dry transfer system. Following transfer, membranes were processed according to standard immunoblotting protocols. Signal was detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system. The antibody source and concentration have been specified in Supplemental Table 12.

CFU assay

MF and sAML patient PBMCs were thawed, filtered, and enriched for CD34 + by the Miltenyi Automacs system. Cells were seeded at 1,000 cells per 1.1 mL in semi-solid culture MethoCult H4034 (StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) with the indicated inhibitors. Each condition was plated in duplicate. Colonies were counted 7 days after seeding. Images were taken by the StemVision Hematopoietic colony counter (StemCell Technology). The colonies were manually quantified by a blinded researcher following StemCell Technology’s colony classification recommendations. Deidentified patient information is included in Supplemental Table 13.

Seahorse assay

Bioenergetics of MV4-11 cells treated with indicated inhibitors for two hours (1 × 105/well) were determined by Seahorse XFe96 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The Oxygen consumption rates (OCR) was determined by using the Cell Mito Stress kit (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol.

Mice irradiation

Irradiation was performed using an X-RAD 320 system (Precision X-ray, Madison, CT, USA). The irradiation settings were 320 kV and 12.5 mA, with a source-to-surface distance (SSD) of 50 cm and an offset of 1.5 cm. A variable dose was applied using an F2 filter composed of 1.5 mm aluminum (Al), 0.25 mm copper (Cu), and 0.75 mm tin (Sn).

Secondary MLL-AF9 AML mice model

Retrovirus-mediated MLL-AF9-expressing GFP + splenocytes (CD45.2) were a kind gift of Dr. Stephen M. Sykes. 0.5 × 106 splenocytes were transplanted into sub-lethally irradiated (one dose of 450 cGY with 4 h of rest prior to injection) CD45.1 recipient mice (female C57BL/6J, age 6–8 weeks). For 2 weeks following the transplant, mice were caged with water containing 0.5 mg/mL TMP-SMX. After 16 days, recipient mice were randomly grouped and treated with 1 mg/kg of omipalisib (n = 7) or 10% HPBCD w/v in PBS (vehicle) (n = 8) by oral gavage daily Monday through Friday. The drug was weighed and dissolved in 10% HPBCD w/v in PBS with sonication. Mice survival and complete blood count were monitored by a blinded researcher. The peripheral blood chimerism was evaluated by flow cytometry tests of CD45.2 and GFP. Death or WBC reaching 50 × 103/µl is considered an “event” in the leukemia-free survival analysis. The mice were sourced from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and maintained under standard housing conditions compliant with Washington University Department of Comparative Medicine facility, and with ad libitum access to food and water.

JAK2V617F mice model

The mice model was performed as previously described [24]. Briefly, whole bone marrow was collected from the femurs, sternum, and tibias of CD45.2+ JAK2V617F donor mice. 0.5 × 106 bead-enriched Kit+ cells from the donor mice were transplanted via tail vein injection into CD45.1+ lethally irradiated recipient mice (female C57BL/6J, age 6–8 weeks) (two separate doses of 550cGY with 4 h rest between doses). For 2 weeks following the transplant, mice were caged with water containing 0.5 mg/mL TMP-SMX. 4 weeks post-transplant, recipient mice were randomly grouped and treated with vehicle (10% HPBCD w/v in PBS, n = 6) or omipalisib (0.5 mg/kg, n = 8) by oral gavage daily Monday through Friday. The drug was weighed and dissolved in 10% HPBCD w/v in PBS with sonication. Complete blood counts of the peripheral blood of transplanted mice were monitored by a blinded researcher with the Hemavet every week. Mice were sacrificed after 4 weeks of treatment. Body, spleen, and liver weights were recorded. The mice were sourced from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and maintained under standard housing conditions of the Washington University Department of Comparative Medicine facility with ad libitum access to food and water.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism and R software. Two-tailed Student’s t-test and Pearson’s correlations were performed as indicated. All relevant assays were performed independently at least 3 times. A p-value of 0.05 or less was considered significant.
