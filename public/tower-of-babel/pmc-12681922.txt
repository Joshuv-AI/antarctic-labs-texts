Enhancing Prediction of Cancer Therapy-Related Cardiomyopathy From Surveillance Echocardiograms
Authors: Kasey J Leger, Kayla L Stratton, Ritu Sachdeva, Saro H Armenian, Aarti H Bhat, Patrick M Boyle, Lindsay A Edwards, Lillian R Meacham, Shanti Narasimhan, Paul C Nathan, Karim T Sadak, Surbhi Sharma, William L Border, Wendy M Leisenring, Eric J Chow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly echocardiographic indicators of cardiac remodeling may enhance cardiomyopathy risk prediction in childhood cancer survivors (CCS).ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to assess whether influential echocardiographic measures can be combined to develop a robust cardiomyopathy risk prediction model in CCS.MethodsMulticenter retrospective study of ≥1-year CCS with digitally archived surveillance echocardiograms, enrolled cardiomyopathy cases (left ventricular [LV] fractional shortening ≤28% or LV ejection fraction ≤50% on ≥2 occasions) and noncases (≥5-year CCS who maintained fractional shortening ≥ 30% and ejection fraction ≥55% without initiation of cardiac medications). Echocardiograms were centrally quantitated in a blinded fashion. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression identified the most influential 2-year predictors of cardiomyopathy among 27 echocardiographic parameters. Logistic regression was used to generate ORs with 95% CIs. Estimates were applied to the training and test data sets to generate area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUC).ResultsData from 146 CCS (52 cases; 94 noncases) with a median follow-up of 9.3 years post-cancer diagnosis and a total of 281 echocardiograms were included. A set of 7 echocardiographic measures were identified as the most influential predictors, with AUC of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.74-0.89) and 0.85 (95% CI: 0.74-0.95) in the training and test data sets, respectively. LV end-systolic dimension (ORmm: 1.2; 95% CI: 1.1-1.4), apical 4-chamber longitudinal strain (OR%: 1.2; 95% CI: 1.0-1.3), and septal A’ velocity (ORcm/s: 1.3; 95% CI: 1.1-1.6) were strongly predictive of cardiomyopathy. AUCs were similar if cancer treatment exposures were included.ConclusionsEarly abnormalities in echocardiographic parameters of structure and function predict subsequent cardiomyopathy in CCS and can identify high-risk survivors who warrant early intervention.Key words: cardiomyopathy, cancer survivorship, echocardiography, risk prediction

Cardiomyopathy is a common and significant complication of cancer therapy, occurring in 5% of childhood cancer survivors (CCS) by 45 years of age with 50-year cumulative incidence of >10% in higher-risk subgroups.1,2 Following exposure to cardiotoxic cancer therapy, primarily in the form of anthracycline chemotherapy and thoracic radiation, cardiac injury can precipitate a cascade of adverse cardiac remodeling pathways. This adverse remodeling often progresses silently, leading to thinning of the left ventricular (LV) wall, enlargement of LV diameter, increase in LV wall stress, and ultimately asymptomatic and eventually symptomatic cardiovascular dysfunction.3 Echocardiogram surveillance is recommended in at-risk CCS; however, standard measures of LV function such as LV fractional shortening (LVFS) or ejection fraction (LVEF) on which interventions are based are typically late manifestations of progressive cardiomyopathy.4 Initiation of medications targeted at cardiac remodeling after established decline in LVEF/FS has limited utility in long-term reversal of cardiac dysfunction.5

Given that pharmacologic interventions are most likely to be successful in halting cardiomyopathy progression and preserving long-term cardiac function when initiated early in the disease course, early identification of those at risk is essential.6,7 Cardiomyopathy risk prediction models have been developed that incorporate baseline clinical factors and treatment exposures, such as sex, age at cancer treatment, anthracycline dose, and receipt of cardiac radiation.2,8 Although these models are helpful in guiding risk-based surveillance, they do not account for individual heterogeneity in sensitivity to cardiotoxic therapies and/or pathologic remodeling patterns. Integration of echocardiographic measures of cardiac structure and function following cancer treatment may allow enhanced prediction of cardiomyopathy risk.4,9

Using data from five centers derived from 50 cardiomyopathy cases and 50 noncases, our group previously identified a measurable decline in echocardiographic parameters of cardiac function and remodeling up to 4 years before onset of cardiomyopathy in CCS.10 We have expanded this work to include more centers across the US National Cancer Institute–supported Children’s Oncology Group with a greater diversity of patients and to increase the number of cases and noncases. As a natural progression of our earlier work, in this expanded data set, we assessed whether there are specific echocardiographic measures capable of informing a robust risk prediction model of cardiomyopathy in CCS.

Methods

Patients and settings

The Children’s Oncology Group is a member of the US National Cancer Institute–supported National Clinical Trials Network and includes most of the pediatric oncology centers across the United States and Canada.11 This retrospective, multi-institutional study was conducted across participating Children’s Oncology Group sites with protocol ALTE11C2 open (Supplemental Table 1). Eligible patients were previously diagnosed with cancer at an age <21 years, at least 1 year beyond the end of initial cancer therapy and had available echocardiographic images from routine surveillance for therapy-related cardiomyopathy. Cardiomyopathy cases were defined by 2-dimensional echocardiography as having either a LVFS ≤28.0% or LVEF ≤50.0%, on at least 2 occasions, with at least 1 of those measurements occurring after completion of cancer therapy. Instances in which these LVEF/FS criteria were met only once were also accepted if the qualifying measurement occurred after the completion of cancer therapy and led to the initiation of chronic medical therapy for cardiomyopathy.

Noncases were similarly diagnosed with cancer at an age <21 years, at least 1 year off-therapy, and with available surveillance echocardiographic images. In addition, noncases must have survived at least 5 years, maintained an LVFS ≥30.0% and LVEF ≥55.0% per their institutional echocardiographic reports and remained without clinical concerns for cardiomyopathy during the available follow-up period. Noncases were excluded if they were on any antihypertensive medications during the follow-up. Patients with known congenital heart anomalies (except patent foramen ovale) or underlying genetic syndrome associated with abnormal cardiovascular development were excluded from this analysis. The Institutional Review Boards at all participating institutions approved the study procedures with waiver of consent.

Data collection

The medical records of all patients were reviewed with demographic and cancer diagnosis information, cumulative anthracycline agent doses and radiotherapy exposures, and the presence of other cardiometabolic conditions (ie, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, valvular and pericardial disease, ischemic heart disease, and chronic lung disease) abstracted.10 Dates of cardiomyopathy diagnosis and last follow-up were determined based on reports from the institution, as were the reported dates of echocardiograms, and height and weight at time of each echocardiogram, which were then used to calculate body surface area using the Haycock formula. Any missing body surface areas were interpolated based on temporally adjacent records with complete height and weight information for the patient.

Archived echocardiographic images obtained for clinical care of cases and noncases were deidentified and submitted in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format to a central core laboratory at Children’s Healthcare of Atlanta for offline analysis, using a vendor-neutral software analysis package (TOMTEC Corporation). Comprehensive echocardiogram quantitation was performed by a single blinded research sonographer. Global longitudinal strain (GLS) was attempted; however, measurable in only 10% of the submitted echocardiograms due to inadequacy in any of the apical 2-, 3-, and 4- chamber views that are needed for global estimations. Echocardiograms date from 2004 to 2020 with the majority dating from before 2015. However, given robust correlation between GLS and apical 4-chamber longitudinal strain (A4LS), this latter measure was incorporated as a surrogate for GLS.10 A recent evaluation of interobserver variability using intraclass correlation (ICC) in the core laboratory determined moderate (ICC: 0.5-0.75) to good (ICC: 0.76-0.9) interobserver reliability across 18 echocardiographic parameters (data not shown).

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics are presented with 25th-75th percentiles [Q1-Q3] and count percentage and were compared across groups using Wilcoxon 2-sample test for continuous measures and chi-square test for categorical variables (or Fisher exact test where cell counts <5). Central echocardiogram quantitation was performed for 1,041 echocardiograms (522 case, 519 noncase studies). As the goal of this analysis was to determine which echocardiographic parameters would be predictive of cardiomyopathy within the subsequent 2 years, echocardiograms at or after cardiomyopathy diagnosis were excluded (n = 273). In the absence of cardiomyopathy, if 2 years of follow-up from the last echocardiogram was not available, that echocardiogram was also excluded (n = 142). Of the 43 routine echocardiographic parameters measured, given variable image quality, 18 parameters had >25% missingness for either cases or noncases, including biplane LVEF (Supplemental Table 2). As a result, we decided a priori to focus the analysis on the 25 parameters remeasured in ≥75% of studies, plus 2 measures specifically of interest based on prior analyses (LV and Doppler tissue imaging–derived myocardial performance indices),12, 13, 14 thus excluding 345 echocardiograms with missing data for at least 1 of the 27 echocardiographic measures of interest. As a result, 281 echocardiograms (92 case and 189 noncase studies) with complete data were available for the main analysis (Supplemental Figure 1). The distribution of timing of cancer diagnosis varied slightly between excluded and included survivors with those excluded more frequently diagnosed in the most remote (1985-1999) or most recent (2010-2017) time periods compared to those included. Otherwise, the 2 groups were similar.

Due to the large number of echocardiographic parameters relative to the number of echocardiograms, and the possibility of multicollinearity, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used to determine the best set of predictors of cardiomyopathy using the glmnet software package in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).15,16 Similar to the standard logistic regression models, these employed a logit link, but added a penalty term for the beta coefficients based on the sum of their absolute values multiplied by a tuning parameter (lambda). Coefficients represent change in log-odds due to a 1-U change in the echo parameter.

Patients (along with their echocardiograms) were randomly assigned into a training (approximately 85% of the sample) and testing (remaining sample) data set. For the training data set of 235 echocardiograms, 10-fold cross-validation, using minimization of deviance, was used to identify the optimal (ie, smallest) value of the lambda to penalize and shrink some coefficients to zero.

All models included patient sex and age at study as potential predictors. Since Z-scores were not available for all 27 echocardiographic parameters, Haycock body surface area was included in all models. Total anthracycline dose (doxorubicin-equivalent in mg/m2)17 and chest radiation dose (cGy), as continuous measures, were added as predictors of cardiomyopathy in a subanalysis. As we have previously shown, biplane LVEF is frequently not available from retrospective pediatric echocardiograms due to missing 2-chamber views or other technical issues.18 However, a priori, biplane LVEF was included in a subanalysis to determine if its inclusion provided any additional predictive value beyond the other 27 measures. Estimates from the LASSO models were applied to the training and test data sets to generate receiver operating characteristic curves and derive corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values, with 95% CIs based on 2,000 bootstraps.

Since the use of the LASSO balances tradeoffs between increasing bias while decreasing variance, standard errors for LASSO estimates do not provide reliable CIs. Due to this, additional unpenalized logistic regression models were also evaluated, allowing us to generate ORs and 95% CIs for each selected parameter in relation to the risk of developing cardiomyopathy. These logistic regression model estimates were applied to the training and test data to generate AUCs to ensure predictive accuracy and discrimination remained similar to AUC estimates derived from the LASSO models. Notably, when the basic and treatment models were fit using the subset of echocardiograms available for the model with LVEF, AUC estimates were nearly identical to those of the models presented with the larger data set. All analyses were performed using R (v4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and SAS (v9.4, SAS Institute Inc).

Results

The final data set of 281 analyzed echocardiograms represented 146 patients including 52 cardiomyopathy cases and 94 noncases (Central Illustration) (Table 1). Patients were previously treated for a variety of malignancies including hematologic (40.4% leukemia, 18.4% lymphoma) and solid tumors (23.3% sarcoma, 17.8% other). The median age at cancer diagnosis was 6.5 years (Q1-Q3 3-11); the median age at cardiomyopathy diagnosis was 15 years (Q1-Q3 13-17). Years of follow-up after cancer diagnosis ranged from 1.7 to 20.7 years, with 45.2% of patients having follow-up of 10 years or more. Notably, >50% of patients belonged to a racial or ethnic minority group. The median number of echocardiograms per patient used in this analysis was 2 (max = 6). The median interval between consecutive echocardiograms was 2.0 years (Q1-Q3 1.0-2.6), and for cases, the median time between the last predictive echocardiogram and subsequent cardiomyopathy was 0.8 years (Q1-Q3 0.3-1.4 years). Cases were slightly older at cancer diagnosis and last follow-up than noncases (median age 18 vs 17 years). As expected, cases had a higher median anthracycline exposure (300 vs 200 mg/m2) than noncases.

Within the training set (235 echocardiograms), LASSO regression identified the 7 most influential 2-year predictors of cardiomyopathy based on determining the optimal value of the shrinkage parameter, lambda, for each model (lambda of 0.027-0.028) (Supplemental Figure 2). Selected parameters varied slightly when treatment exposures (n = 232 echocardiograms) and treatment exposures plus LVEF (155 echocardiograms) were included, although consistently included lower mitral inflow and greater septal velocities and higher (less negative) A4LS in predicting the increased cardiomyopathy risk (Table 2). A4LS appeared most influential across models based on the standardized beta coefficients (ie, 0.46-0.49). Higher LV end-systolic dimension (LVESD) appeared influential in the basic model with and without treatment exposures (standardized beta coefficients 0.34-0.39) but was not selected in the model that included LVEF.

The resulting AUCs based on the LASSO models ranged from 0.80 (95% CI: 0.71-0.89) to 0.83 (95% CI: 0.76-0.89) (Table 4, Figure 1). The addition of LVEF did not markedly improve the performance, although the data set with analyzable LVEF was much smaller. Discussion

This longitudinal study of 146 CCS identified a set of 7 echocardiographic parameters highly predictive of subsequent cardiomyopathy within 2 years. The accuracy of this prediction model was robust (AUC: 0.82-0.85) and was maintained in both the training and test data sets. Model performance was not clearly improved with inclusion of cancer treatment exposures or LVEF. Notably, LVESD, A4LS, and diastolic parameters (mitral inflow and septal velocities) were most influential in predicting subsequent cardiomyopathy.

Prediction of late cardiomyopathy after childhood cancer therapy has been extensively studied and resulted in relatively robust risk prediction models incorporating cancer treatment exposures, age at cancer treatment, and more recently the presence of cardiovascular comorbidities in survivorship.2,8 However, these models do not take into account early biologic indicators of cardiovascular stress and remodeling that precede global declines in LV function.3 Serial surveillance echocardiograms are the standard of care for CCS exposed to cardiotoxic anthracyclines or cardiac radiation.19 However, cardiology referral and pharmacologic intervention is often reserved for those survivors who have developed abnormal LVEF or LVFS.19,20 Early identification, referral, and intervention before the onset of global LV dysfunction may prevent progressive cardiomyopathy.21

LVESD has been recognized as an important predictor of outcomes in children with existing dilated cardiomyopathy wherein increased LVESD correlates with more severe disease progression, need for cardiac transplant, and higher mortality risk.22,23 Although data informing longitudinal relationship of LVESD along with LVEF are scarce, chamber dimension relative to LV wall thickness has been commonly linked to cardiomyopathy progression in the CCS population.3 Our findings indicate a higher risk for cardiomyopathy with increasing LV dilation, although this predictor was not impactful in models incorporating LVEF. However, the number of echocardiograms with measurable LVEF was fewer, which may have limited our ability to identify predictors for that model with the same precision.

Strain echocardiography, particularly GLS, has gained significant attention as a potential predictor of cardiotoxicity risk due to its high sensitivity in detecting regional changes in LV function among cancer patients and survivors.24, 25, 26, 27 GLS has been recommended in both pediatric and adult cardiology guidelines for cardiotoxicity monitoring,4,28 supported by data linking GLS to future declines in LVEF, including findings from 2 recent studies on CCS.9,29 In a retrospective analysis of children receiving high-dose anthracycline therapy, GLS declines of ≥15% during treatment were strongly associated with abnormal LVEF in survivorship.29 Similarly, an analysis of 5-year CCS in the St. Jude Lifetime cohort found that GLS decline predicted future cardiomyopathy and enhanced existing cardiotoxicity risk prediction models.9 Our study similarly found that longitudinal strain was predictive of subsequent cardiomyopathy, albeit with the use of single plane strain (A4LS). Our group has previously shown that A4LS strongly correlates with GLS and reliably distinguishes patients with normal vs abnormal GLS. As such, A4LS may be a useful surrogate for GLS in studies where multiview strain quantification is not feasible due to foreshortened windows or missing data.14,18

Although the majority of studies indicate that declines in GLS precede and predict subsequent LVEF declines, a recent study of nearly 1,400 5 year Dutch CCS highlighted a more complex relationship between these 2 measures.25 In this study, 20% of survivors with normal LVEF were found to have abnormal GLS. However, of those with abnormal LVEF, 39% maintained a normal GLS. GLS was more strongly associated with radiation therapy, whereas LVEF was linked to anthracycline dose. These results suggest that GLS and LVEF may reflect differing pathophysiology after childhood cancer therapy and underscore the need for further research to clarify their relationship and improve their prognostic value in CCS.

Diastolic dysfunction is an important predictor of heart failure progression in the general population where it has been associated with an increased risk for major adverse cardiac events and all-cause mortality.30,31 In the CCS population, diastolic dysfunction occurs largely in the setting of systolic dysfunction, whereas isolated diastolic dysfunction has been reported to occur in <5% of CCS with preserved LVEF and up to 9% in survivors with abnormal GLS.32 Diastolic function may be more commonly impacted in survivors exposed to cardiac radiation related to myocardial fibrosis.3 Adults survivors of pediatric allogeneic stem cell transplant have exhibited a decline in echocardiographic indices of diastolic function as they age, with exposure to total body irradiation conferring the highest risk for long-term diastolic dysfunction.33 Inconsistent findings regarding prevalence and predictive value of diastolic dysfunction in CCS cohorts are likely in part related to discordance in definitions and grading of diastolic dysfunction.4 Updated 2016 American Society of Echocardiography and European Association of Echocardiography recommendations were developed to provide a consistent stepwise hierological assessment of diastolic parameters to improve diagnostic accuracy across studies and in clinical practice.34 In contrast to adult patients, the more classic interim stages of diastolic dysfunction (impaired relaxation, psuedonormalization, and reversible restrictive) are seldom seen. Rather, we often see parameters reflecting decreased ventricular compliance such as mitral inflow (E) and septal (A’) show subtle abnormalities relatively early on.4,35,36 This seems to be the case in this cohort. This suggests that other echo parameters of ventricular compliance such as atrial strain may prove helpful as well.

Although individual echocardiographic predictors such as LVESD, strain, and diastolic parameters have proven value in predicting cardiomyopathy, their sensitivity and accuracy are limited when used in isolation.4 Our study suggests that a composite model incorporating these and other echocardiographic parameters may better identify CCS at risk of developing subsequent cardiomyopathy. With further work to define and validate optimized cut-points for each echocardiographic measure, an echocardiogram-based composite risk score could be developed and employed to stratify survivors by cardiomyopathy risk. This pathophysiology informed risk score could identify with greater precision those who would benefit most from early intervention with cardiac remodeling medications to halt progressive cardiomyopathy; on the flip side, burdensome echocardiographic monitoring may be less frequent or avoided altogether in those at the lowest risk for cardiomyopathy. This multiparameter approach holds significant potential for guiding early interventions and improving long-term outcomes in CCS and represents a potential improvement on other proposed risk algorithms incorporating only treatment exposures or a single echocardiogram-based measure.1,9

The strengths of this study include its longitudinal capture of echocardiographic images across multiple sites, which is relatively rare in studies of CCS to date, which are often cross sectional. Subjects were racially and ethnically diverse (>50% racial/ethnic minority representation) and represent a more contemporary study population in contrast to cohorts of adult-aged CCS; thus, findings may be more generalizable to survivors treated in recent eras.

Study Limitations

This study also has several limitations. First, this longitudinal data set relies on older archived echocardiograms, many with unmeasurable parameters of interest such as LVEF. However, it is reassuring that the models restricted to echocardiograms with analyzable LVEF performed similarly to those without LVEF. Furthermore, the limitations in analyzability observed in this study reflect those encountered in clinical practice where inadequate windows in pediatric patients frequently lead to missing parameters. Thus, the composite model is based on measurements that are feasible in the “real-world” setting. Recently the American Society of Echocardiography published guidelines detailing a standardized approach to noninvasive cardiac imaging in childhood cancer patients to ensure comprehensive assessment of LV and right ventricular function, valve function, and the pericardium.4 These guidelines will support improvements in comprehensive assessment of LV function in clinical and research realms in the future. In addition, although the majority of clinical echocardiograms obtained in the CCS population are performed for guideline-directed cardiomyopathy surveillance in asymptomatic individuals, it is possible that some of the echocardiograms included in the data set could have been performed to evaluate symptoms of cardiac dysfunction and therefore selecting for higher risk patients. Although this may risk introduction of bias, we feel this is unlikely because our risk prediction model only included echocardiograms preceding cardiomyopathy diagnosis wherein those with LVEF and/or left ventricular shortening fraction in the normal range are unlikely to have had cardiac symptoms. Given that our analyses excluded echocardiograms with a large amount of missing data and was therefore restricted to higher quality echocardiograms, it is possible that our model performance may be overly optimistic. We recognize that in clinical practice, inadequate echocardiogram windows related to body habitus, rib spacing or technical limitations may preclude the ability to measure all echocardiographic parameters. However, for this reason we restricted our modeling to the parameters that are most consistently feasible in clinical practice and therefore more reliable for risk prediction in clinical practice. Finally, given analytic constraints of the current sample size, the cardiomyopathy prediction models identified herein are based on echocardiographic parameters at a single point in time and do not account for change over time. Our models also did not allow identification of specific parameter cut-points assessed using more sophisticated performance metrics. Rather, this analysis served as proof of principal that the most influential echo parameters could be identified and continuous values combined together for robust prediction. Ongoing work within the Children’s Oncology Group will expand on the current data set adding serial images from additional cases and noncases to inform future analyses that can incorporate changes of these key parameters over time, along with estimating the predictive performance of specific parameter cutoffs with greater precision.

Conclusions

A subset of echocardiographic measures including increased end-systolic volume, reduced longitudinal strain, and diastolic dysfunction may be useful in predicting subsequent cardiomyopathy in CCS. Further work is needed to identify specific cut-points or trends over time that may identify survivors at the highest risk who warrant early intervention to prevent progressive cardiomyopathy.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: In CCS, serial echocardiograms are standard of care for cardiomyopathy surveillance. A combination of echocardiographic measures of LV structure and function robustly predict onset of cardiomyopathy in this population within 2 years irrespective of cancer treatment exposures.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Future studies using an expanded data set should seek to identify specific cut-points or trends in echocardiographic measures of LV structure and function that can be combined to identify CCS at the highest cardiomyopathy risk that may benefit from early intervention.
