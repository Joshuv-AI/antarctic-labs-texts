Quotients of Poisson boundaries, entropy, and spectral gap
Samuel Dodds, Alex Furman

Abstract
. Poisson boundary is a measurable Γ \Gamma -space canonically associated with a group Γ \Gamma and a probability measure μ \mu on it. The collection of all measurable Γ \Gamma -equivariant quotients, known as μ \mu -boundaries, of the Poisson boundary forms a partially ordered set, equipped with a strictly monotonic non-negative function, known as Furstenberg or differential entropy. In this paper we demonstrate the richness and the complexity of this lattice of quotients for the case of free groups and surface groups and rather general measures. In particular, we show that there are continuum many unrelated μ \mu -boundaries at each, sufficiently low, entropy level, and there are continuum many distinct order-theoretic cubes of μ \mu -boundaries. These μ \mu -boundaries are constructed from dense linear representations ρ : Γ → G \rho:\Gamma\to G to semi-simple Lie groups, like PSL 2 ​ ( 𝐂 ) d \textbf{PSL}_{2}({\mathbf{C}})^{d} with absolutely continuous stationary measures on 𝐂 ^ d \hat{\mathbf{C}}^{d} .

1.2.
Our approach

Consider the simple real Lie group
G=\textbf{SO}(3,1)
that is essentially
\textbf{Isom}(\mathbf{H}^{3})
,
and consider the space
\textbf{Hom}^{Z}(\Gamma,G)
of homomorphisms

\rho:\Gamma\ {\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}\ G

with Zariski dense image. In fact, since
G
has rank one, and
\mathbf{H}^{3}
is hyperbolic,
being Zariski dense is equivalent to saying
\rho(\Gamma)
is unbounded and non-elementary.
In these circumstances the measure
\mu
on
\Gamma
defines a random walk on
G

that has a unique stationary measure
\nu_{\rho}\in\textbf{Prob}(\partial\mathbf{H}^{3})
and that

(\partial\mathbf{H}^{3},\nu_{\rho})
is a
\mu
-boundary. We shall denote it
(B_{\rho},\nu_{\rho})
.

The idea is to vary
\rho\in\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}(\Gamma,G)
in order to construct a large family
of
\mu
-boundaries

.
It seems plausible that the entropy

would depend continuously
on the representation
\rho
in all cases.
While the associated
\mu
-stationary measures
\nu_{\rho}
do vary continuously
in the weak-* topology on the sphere
S^{2}=\partial\mathbf{H}^{3}
,
this fact alone does not seem to be sufficient to show that the associated

Hence we focus on the situations where the stationary measure
\nu_{\rho}

is in the
G
-invariant measure class, and has a
\log
-integrable

with respect to the
\textbf{SO}(3)
-invariant measure on the
2
-sphere
S=\partial\mathbf{H}^{3}
.
In these cases we are able to prove that the entropy

varies

\rho
.

The problem of achieving

for the stationary measure
\nu_{\rho}

in the Lebesgue class,
has been recently considered by Bourgain
[
7
]
and
Benoist–Quint
[
4
]
.
We revisit and simplify these approaches in Section

.
For Theorem

we need to study stationary measures
for
\rho_{1}\times\cdots\times\rho_{d}:\Gamma\to G=\textbf{PSL}_{2}({\mathbf{C}})^{d}
.
The need to achieve regularity for the stationary measures forces us to work in the

.

Notations and some Preliminary facts

2.1.
Stationary spaces,
\mu
-harmonic functions, and Poisson boundary

A probability space
(S,\eta)
with a measurable, measure-class preserving action
of
\Gamma
is called

\mu
-

if
\mu*\eta=\eta
, where

\mu*\eta:=\sum_{g\in\Gamma}\mu(g)\cdot g_{*}\eta.

Given a
\mu
-stationary
\Gamma
-space
(S,\eta)
and a function

define a bounded function

by the formula

(2.1)

F(g)=\int_{S}f\,{\textrm{d}}g_{*}\eta=\int_{S}f(x)\cdot\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}g_{*}\eta}{\,{\textrm{d}}\eta}(x)\,{\textrm{d}}\eta(x)

The assumption that
\eta

\mu
-stationary implies that
F
satisfies the following

\mu
-
mean value property
:

(2.2)

F(g)=\sum_{g^{\prime}\in\Gamma}\mu(g^{\prime})\cdot F(gg^{\prime})\qquad(g\in\Gamma).

Functions satisfying this property are called
\mu
-

,
and

\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{\mu}(\Gamma):=\left\{\left.{F\in\ell^{\infty}(\Gamma)}\ \right/\ {F\ \textrm{satisfies\ }(\ref{e:muMVP})}\right\}

denotes the space of all bounded
\mu
-harmonic functions on
\Gamma
.

Formula (
2.1
) defines a positive, normalized, linear map

{\mathscr{FP}}_{S}:L^{\infty}(S,\eta){\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{\mu}(\Gamma)

that is sometimes called Furstenberg–Poisson Transform.
There exists a unique
\mu
-stationary
\Gamma
-space
(B,\nu)
for which

{\mathscr{FP}}_{B}:L^{\infty}(B,\nu){\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{\mu}(\Gamma)

is an isomorphism.
In fact, this
(B,\nu)
is the von Neumann spectrum of the commutative von Neumann algebra

L^{\infty}(B,\nu)\cong\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{\mu}(\Gamma)
, where
\nu
corresponds to the evaluation
functional

on
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{\mu}(\Gamma)
.
This special stationary
\Gamma

(B,\nu)
is called the
Poisson

or
Furstenberg–Poisson boundary
of
(\Gamma,\mu)
.

A
\mu
-stationary
\Gamma
-space
(S,\eta)
is called a
\mu
-

if

{\mathscr{FP}}_{S}
is an embedding. The Poisson boundary

is the
universal

\mu
-boundary:
for any
\mu
-boundary
(B^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})
there is a unique
\Gamma
-equivariant measurable quotient map

p:(B,\nu){\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}(B^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})\qquad p_{*}\nu=\nu^{\prime}

corresponding to the embedding

.

The lattice of boundaries

We are interested in understanding the partially ordered set (POSet)

of all
\mu
-boundaries

(B^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})\geq(B^{\prime\prime},\nu^{\prime\prime})

The partial order is most transparent in the last two points of view as

More precisely.

2.3.
Entropy

Let us now assume that
\mu
on
\Gamma
has a finite entropy
H(\mu)<\infty
.
Here
H(\mu)
denotes the entropy of the countable partition of the probability space
(\Gamma,\mu)

into atoms:

(2.3)

H(\mu):=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}-\mu(\gamma)\cdot\log\mu(\gamma)<+\infty

with the convention that
0\cdot\log 0=0
.
For measures with finite entropy, there is a notion of asymptotic entropy
of the
\mu
-random walk on
\Gamma
,
introduced by Avez
[
1
]
and studied by Kaimanovich–Vershik
[
21
]
, as the limit

{\textbf{h}_{\textbf{RW}}}(\Gamma,\mu):=\inf_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}H(\mu^{*n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}H(\mu^{*n})

where
\mu^{*n}
denotes the
n
th convolution power of
\mu
. The existence of the limit
is a consequence of the subadditivity
H(\mu^{*(n+m)})\leq H(\mu^{*n})+H(\mu^{*m})
,
that follows from the general properties of the entropy function and the
fact that the product map
\Gamma\times\Gamma\to\Gamma
,

(g_{1},g_{2})\mapsto g_{1}\cdot g_{2}
, maps
\mu^{*m}\times\mu^{*n}
to
\mu^{*(m+n)}
.

Let
(S,\eta)
be a
\mu
-stationary
\Gamma
-space.
The boundary entropy, also called Furstenberg entropy, is defined by

h_{\mu}(S):=\int_{\Gamma}\int_{S}-\log\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}g_{*}^{-1}\eta}{\,{\textrm{d}}\eta}\,{\textrm{d}}\eta\,{\textrm{d}}\mu

We recall the basic ”soft” properties (cf.
[
13
]
*Chapter 3):

•

h_{\mu}(S)\geq 0
with
h_{\mu}(S)=0
iff
\Gamma\curvearrowright(S,\eta)
is measure-preserving.

•

h_{\mu}(S)\leq{\textbf{h}_{\textbf{RW}}}(\Gamma,\mu)
and this maximum is achieved by the Poisson boundary:

h_{\mu}(B)={\textbf{h}_{\textbf{RW}}}(\Gamma,\mu)
.

•

h_{\mu}
is monotonic: if
S_{1}\to S_{2}
then
h_{\mu}(S_{1})\geq h_{\mu}(S_{2})

and the inequality is strict unless
S_{1}\to S_{2}
is relatively probability measure preserving.

•

Any
\mu
-stationary
(S,\eta)
is also
\mu^{*n}
-stationary for
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
and

h_{\mu^{*n}}(S)=n\cdot h_{\mu}(S).

Specializing to
\mu
-boundaries, we remark that relatively probability
measure preserving map
B_{1}\to B_{2}
between
\mu
-boundaries has to be an isomorphism.
This implies that
h_{\mu}
is a
strictly monotonic
map on
\textbf{Bnd}(\Gamma,\mu)

taking values in
[0,{\textbf{h}_{\textbf{RW}}}(\Gamma,\mu)]
.

2.4.
Integrability conditions on the measure
\mu

Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group with a generating set
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
.
The associated word length
|-|_{A}
on
\Gamma
is defined by

|\gamma|_{A}:=\min\left\{\left.{n}\ \right/\ {\gamma\in(A\cup A^{-1})^{n}}\right\}

with
|e|_{A}=0
by convention.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\Gamma
.
We say
\mu
is
generating
\Gamma
as a group
if
\Gamma=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}(\textbf{supp}(\mu)\cup\textbf{supp}(\mu)^{-1})^{n}
.
We say that
\mu

\Gamma
as a semi-group
if

\Gamma=\{e\}\cup\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}(\textbf{supp}(\mu))^{n}
.
Clearly the latter condition implies the former.
A probability measure
\mu
on
\Gamma
has
finite first moment
if

(2.4)

\sum_{\gamma}\mu(\gamma)\cdot|\gamma|_{A}<\infty.

We say that
\mu
has

if there exists
\epsilon>0

so that (
1.3
) holds, i.e.

\sum_{\gamma}\mu(\gamma)\cdot e^{\epsilon\cdot|\gamma|_{A}}<\infty.

For a measure
\mu
on a finitely generated group
\Gamma

the conditions of having finite support,
having finite exponential moment,
having finite first moment, and having finite entropy are progressively weaker.
We shall need the following:

Lemma 2.2
.

If
\mu
has finite exponential moment, then any convolution power
\mu^{*n}

and any finite convex combination of convolution powers
has a finite exponential moment.

Proof.

It suffices to show that if
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
both satisfy (
1.3
)
then so does
\mu_{1}*\mu_{2}
.
Consider the positive integrable functions

f_{i}\in\ell^{1}(\Gamma)
given by

f_{i}(\gamma)=\mu_{i}(\gamma)\cdot e^{\epsilon|\gamma|_{A}}
for
i=1,2
.
Consider their convolution
f_{1}*f_{2}
and estimate

\begin{split}f_{1}*f_{2}(\gamma)&=\sum_{x\cdot y=\gamma}f_{1}(x)\cdot f_{2}(y)=\sum_{x\cdot y=\gamma}\mu_{1}(x)\cdot e^{\epsilon|x|_{A}}\cdot\mu_{2}(y)\cdot e^{\epsilon|y|_{A}}\\
&=\sum_{x\cdot y=\gamma}\mu_{1}(x)\cdot\mu_{2}(y)\cdot e^{\epsilon(|x|_{A}+|y|_{A})}\\
&\geq\sum_{x\cdot y=\gamma}\mu_{1}(x)\mu_{2}(y)\cdot e^{\epsilon|\gamma|_{A}}=\mu_{1}*\mu_{2}(\gamma)\cdot e^{\epsilon|\gamma|_{A}}\end{split}

Since its is well known (Fubini argument) that
\|f_{1}*f_{2}\|_{1}\leq\|f_{1}\|_{1}\cdot\|f_{2}\|_{1}
,
we deduce that
\mu_{1}*\mu_{2}
has finite exponential moment, in fact
with respect to the same exponent
\epsilon>0
.
The statement about convex combinations is obvious.
∎

2.5.
Semi-simple Lie groups

Let
G
be a connected semi-simple real Lie group with finite center,

K<G
a maximal compact subgroup,

P<G
a minimal parabolic, and let
S=G/P
be the flag variety.
The Iwasawa decomposition
G=K\cdot P
shows that
K
acts transitively on
S
,
hence
S=K/M
where
M=K\cap P
. We denote by
m
the unique
K
-invariant
probability measure on
S
.

We will need to describe a ”norm”
\mathcal{N}:G{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}[1,\infty)
satisfying

\mathcal{N}(g_{1}g_{2})\leq\mathcal{N}(g_{1})\cdot\mathcal{N}(g_{2})

and
\mathcal{N}(g)=1
iff
g\in K
.
There are several natural options to do so, including the following:

\mathcal{N}_{G/K}(g)=e^{d_{G/K}(K,gK)},\qquad\mathcal{N}_{\textbf{Ad}}(g)=\|\textbf{Ad}g\|,\qquad\mathcal{N}_{\textbf{RN}}(g)=\|\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}g_{*}m}{\,{\textrm{d}}m}(x)\|_{\infty}

where
d_{G/K}
is the
K
-invariant distance on the symmetric space
G/K
associated
with the Killing form, and
\|-\|:\textbf{Ad}G\to[0,\infty)
is the operator norm.
These norms are equivalent in the following sense: there exist positive constants

c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}>0
so that for all
g\in G

\mathcal{N}_{\textbf{RN}}(g)\leq\mathcal{N}_{G/K}(g)^{c_{1}}\leq\mathcal{N}_{\textbf{Ad}}(g)^{c_{2}}\leq\mathcal{N}_{\textbf{RN}}(g)^{c_{3}}

We will be using the
\mathcal{N}_{\textbf{RN}}
norm, and the above relation allow one
to translate these statements to other norms.

Let
\Gamma
be a group generated by a finite set
A
.
Let
G
be a semi-simple real Lie group, and consider the space

\textbf{Hom}(\Gamma,G)

of all representations
\rho:\Gamma{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}G
.
Since any representation
\rho:\Gamma{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}G
is determined
by its values
\rho(a)
on the generators
a\in A
, the space

\textbf{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
can be viewed as an algebraic subvariety of
G^{A}
;
called the
representation variety
.
The Hausdorff topology on
\textbf{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
is inherited from
G^{A}
;
we have
\rho_{i}\to\rho
iff
\rho_{i}(a)\to\rho(a)
for all
a\in A
.
Denote

\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}(\Gamma,G):=\left\{\left.{\rho\in\textbf{Hom}(\Gamma,G)}\ \right/\ {\rho(\Gamma)\ \ \textrm{is\ Zariski\ dense\ in\ }G}\right\}.

2.8.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space, and

The

Given a finite set

we have the associated unitary operators

on
L^{2}_{0}(S,m)

and denote by

\kappa_{C}:=1-\sup\left\{\left.{\left\|\frac{1}{2k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}(f\circ c_{i}+f\circ c_{i}^{-1})\right\|}\ \right/\ {f\in L^{2}_{0}(S,m),\ \|f\|=1}\right\}

[

]

In fact, with the exception of the circle any simple compact Lie group

admits such a collection of elements (
[
2
]

3.
Regularity of stationary measures

The purpose of this section is to describe an explicit argument that allows
us to construct a subset

\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}_{\textbf{sg}}(\Gamma,G)\subset\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}(\Gamma,G)

of representations
\rho:\Gamma{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}G
with a unique
\mu
-stationary
measure
\nu_{\rho}
on
S
with
\nu_{\rho}\sim m
.
The basic idea is inspired by the work of Benoist–Quint
[
4
]

(see also earlier work of Bourgain
[
7
]
).
However our argument is more direct and thus is easier to quantify.

Consider a finitely generated group
\Gamma
with a generating set
A
,
and let
B\subset\Gamma
be a finite subset, where we may assume
B\subset A
.
Let
G
be a connected semi-simple real Lie group with trivial center,
and
S=G/P=K/M
with the
K
-invariant probability measure as in §
2.5
.
In the Theorem
3.2
below we consider representations
\rho:\Gamma{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}G

such that
\rho(B)
is contained in the compact subgroup
K
has a spectral
gap

\kappa_{\rho(B)}>0

on
L^{2}_{0}(S,m)
, while at the same time
\rho(A)
is sufficiently close to
K
,
but is not contained in
K
.
We use the following parameter
\alpha_{\rho}\geq 0
to gauge closeness to
K
:

(3.1)

\alpha_{\rho}:=\max_{g\in\rho(A)\cup\rho(A)^{-1}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\textbf{RN}}(g).

Note that
\alpha(\rho)=0
iff
\rho(A)
is contained in
K
.
Since
\Gamma
is generated by
A
the latter is equivalent to

\rho(\Gamma)\subset K
.

Remark 3.1
.

Let
G
be a connected real Lie group of rank one,
a maximal compact subgroup

is maximal as a closed subgroup.
Thus for
\rho\in\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}_{\textbf{sg}}(\Gamma,G)
with

the image
\rho(\Gamma)
is topologically dense in

.
In particular this implies to
G=\textbf{SO}(3,1)
and
K=\textbf{SO}(3)
.

Theorem 3.2

(Good stationary measure)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group generated by a finite set
A
,
and let
B
be a finite subset in
\Gamma
.
Let
\mu\in\textbf{Prob}(\Gamma)
be generating as a semi-group
and having a finite exponential moment.
This data determines a constant
{\textbf{C}_{\textbf{sg}}}>0
so that the following holds.

Let
G
be a semi-simple Lie group
G
,
m
be the
K
-invariant
probability measure on
S=G/P
,
\rho:\Gamma{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}G
a representation with
Zariski dense image in

, such that
\rho(B)\subset K

has a positive spectral gap
\kappa_{\rho(B)}>0
on

, while

0<\alpha_{\rho}<{\textbf{C}_{\textbf{sg}}}\cdot\kappa_{\rho(B)}.

Then the
\Gamma
-action

has a unique
\mu
-stationary measure
\nu_{\rho}
,
this measure is in the
G
-invariant measure class, and has density

\phi_{\rho}=\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}}{\,{\textrm{d}}m}\qquad\textrm{with}\qquad\int_{S}\phi^{2}\,{\textrm{d}}m<\infty,\qquad\int_{S}\phi^{-\tau}\,{\textrm{d}}m<\infty

for some
\tau>0
.
In particular,
\log\phi_{\rho}\in L^{p}(S,m)
for all
p\in[1,\infty)
.

Notation 3.3
.

We denote by
\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}_{\textbf{sg}}(\Gamma,G;\mu,A,B)\subset\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}(\Gamma,G)

the set of representations satisfying conditions of Theorem
3.2
.

We start with a general setting.
Let
(X,m)
be a probability space,
L^{2}(X,m)
denote the real Hilbert space of square integrable functions,

\mathbb{1}\in L^{2}(X,m)
denote the constant
1
function,
and its orthogonal compliment
\mathbb{1}^{\perp}
by

L^{2}_{0}(X,m)=\left\{\left.{f\in L^{2}(X,m)}\ \right/\ {\int_{X}f\,{\textrm{d}}m=0}\right\}.

We shall also denote by
L^{2}_{+}(X,m)=\left\{\left.{f\in L^{2}(X,m)}\ \right/\ {f\geq 0}\right\}
the cone of non-negative square
integrable functions.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(X,m)
be a probability space,
\kappa>0
and
p\in(0,1)
be constants, and

P
and
Q
bounded operators on
L^{2}(X,m)
satisfying:

(1)

L^{2}_{+}(X,m)
is invariant under
P^{*}
and
Q^{*}
,

(2)

P\mathbb{1}=Q\mathbb{1}=\mathbb{1}
,

(3)

\|P^{*}f\|\leq(1-\kappa)\|f\|
for all
f\in L^{2}_{0}(X,m)
,

(4)

\|Q^{*}\|<1+\kappa p/(1-p)
.

Then the dual
T^{*}
of the operator
T=tP+(1-t)Q
has a unique fixed vector

T^{*}\phi=\phi\qquad\textrm{with}\qquad\left\langle{\phi},{\mathbb{1}}\right\rangle=1

And
\phi
is positive:
\phi\in L^{2}_{+}(X,m)
.

Proof.

Consider the affine hyperplane

H=\mathbb{1}+L^{2}_{0}(X,m)=\left\{\left.{f\in L^{2}(X,m)}\ \right/\ {\left\langle{f},{\mathbb{1}}\right\rangle=1}\right\}

and a closed convex subset
H_{+}=H\cap L^{2}_{+}(X,m)
in it.
Note that
H
and
H_{+}
are
T^{*}
-invariant.
Indeed,
H
is
T^{*}
-invariant due to assumption (2), because

\left\langle{T^{*}f},{\mathbb{1}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{f},{T\mathbb{1}}\right\rangle=p\left\langle{f},{P\mathbb{1}}\right\rangle+(1-p)\left\langle{f},{Q\mathbb{1}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{f},{\mathbb{1}}\right\rangle.

By assumption (1) the cone
L^{2}_{+}(x,m)
is invariant under
T^{*}=pP^{*}+(1-p)Q^{*}
,
hence
H_{+}
is
T^{*}
-invariant as well.

For any
f,g\in H_{+}
we have
f-g\in L^{2}_{0}(X,m)
and therefore, using assumption (3), we have

\begin{split}\|T^{*}(f-g)\|&\leq p\|P^{*}(f-g)\|+(1-p)\|Q^{*}(f-g)\|\\
&\leq p(1-\kappa)\|f-g\|+(1-p)\|Q^{*}\|\cdot\|f-g\|\\
&=(p(1-\kappa)+(1-p)\|Q^{*}\|)\cdot\|f-g\|.\end{split}

Assumption (4) implies that the constant
q=p(1-\kappa)+(1-p)\|Q^{*}\|

satisfies
q<1
and therefore
T^{*}
is a
q
-contraction
on the complete spaces
H
and
H_{+}
.
Hence
T^{*}
has a unique fixed point
\phi\in H

and furthermore
\phi\in H_{+}
.
∎

We shall use the following elementary statement.

Lemma 3.5
.

Given
\epsilon>0
for all
x\in[0,\epsilon/2]

\sup_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\ \frac{e^{nx}-1}{e^{\epsilon n}}\leq\frac{2}{\epsilon}\cdot x.

Proof.

\frac{e^{nx}-1}{e^{\epsilon n}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-\epsilon n}\cdot\frac{n^{k}x^{k}}{k!}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k!}{\epsilon^{k}n^{k}}\cdot\frac{n^{k}x^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{x}{\epsilon}\right)^{k}=\frac{x}{\epsilon-x}\leq\frac{2x}{\epsilon}.

∎

-stationary because

(3.3)

\phi(x)=T^{*}\phi(x)=\sum_{\gamma}\bar{\mu}(\gamma)\cdot\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}\rho(\gamma)_{*}m}{\,{\textrm{d}}m}(x)\cdot\phi(\rho(\gamma).x).

Since
\nu=\mu*\nu=\mu*\mu*\nu=\dots
the measure
\nu
is stationary for all convolution powers of
\mu
and their convex combinations, such as
\bar{\mu}
. Hence we have

\bar{\mu}*\bar{\nu}=\bar{\nu},\qquad\bar{\mu}*\nu=\nu.

But
\textbf{supp}(\bar{\mu})\supset\textbf{supp}(\mu)
generates
\Gamma
,
thus by Theorem
2.3
there is only one

\bar{\mu}
-stationary measure. Hence
\nu=\bar{\nu}
.

Since
\phi\in L^{2}(S,m)
by construction, it remains to show that
\phi^{-\tau}\in L^{1}(S,m)

for some
\tau>0
.
To this end, notice that equation (
3.3
) implies that

\begin{split}\phi(x)&\geq\frac{p}{2k}\cdot\sum_{g\in\rho(B)\cup\rho(B)^{-1}}\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}g_{*}m}{\,{\textrm{d}}m}(x)\cdot\phi(g.x)\\
&\geq r\cdot\max_{g\in\rho(B)\cup\rho(B)^{-1}}\phi(g.x)\end{split}

where
r>0
takes into account both
t/2k
but also
the essential minimum of the Radon–Nikodym
derivatives of the finitely many elements
g
participating in the sum.

We shall now use the spectral gap assumption
\kappa_{\rho(B)}>0
on
L^{2}_{0}(S,m)
.
Fix some
\delta>0
so that the set
E=\left\{\left.{x\in S}\ \right/\ {\phi(x)>\delta}\right\}
has positive measure
m(E)>0
.
Denote by
E_{0}=E
and let
E_{n}
denote the set
x\in X
for which
there exists
g\in\rho(B)\cup\rho(B)^{-1}
so that
gx\in E_{n-1}
.
Note that the inequality

\phi(x)\geq r\cdot\max_{g\in\rho(B)\cup\rho(B)^{-1}}\phi(g.x)

implies that for a.e.
x\in E_{n}
we have
\phi(x)\geq\delta\cdot r^{n}
.
The spectral gap assumption
\kappa_{\rho(B)}>0
implies that
m(S\setminus\bigcup E_{n})=0

and moreover there exists
\lambda<1
and
C
so that
m(E_{n})<C\lambda^{n}

(see Zimmer
[
30
]
, or
[
12
]
*Lemma 4.5).
Taking
\tau>0
so that
\lambda<r^{\tau}
, and thereby
\lambda\cdot r^{-\tau}<1
, we deduce

\int_{S}\phi^{-\tau}\,{\textrm{d}}m\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C\lambda^{n}(\delta r^{n})^{-\tau}=C\delta^{-\tau}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\lambda\cdot r^{-\tau})^{n}<\infty.

This proves the claimed integrability bounds.
This completes the proof of Theorem
3.2
.
∎

4.
Continuity of Entropy

Recall notation
3.3
for the set of representations
\rho:\Gamma{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}G

satisfying Theorem
3.2

\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}_{\textbf{sg}}(\Gamma,G;\mu,A,B)\subset\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}(\Gamma,G).

The goal of this section is to prove that the map

\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}_{\textbf{sg}}(\Gamma,G;\mu,A,B)\ {\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}\ \textbf{Bnd}(\Gamma,\mu)\ {\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{h_{\mu}}}}\ {\mathbf{R}}_{+},\qquad\rho\ \mapsto\ h_{\mu}(B_{\rho},\nu_{\rho}),

associating to a representation
\rho
the entropy of the corresponding boundary

(B_{\rho},\nu_{\rho})
, is continuous.
This continuity, described in Theorem
4.1
below,
can be shown for a slightly
broader family

\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}_{\textbf{ac}}(\Gamma,G;\mu)\supset\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}_{\textbf{sg}}(\Gamma,G;\mu,A,B)

of representations
consisting of all representations with image
\rho(\Gamma)

being Zariski dense in
G
,
and such that the unique
\mu
-stationary measure
\nu_{\rho}
on
S=G/P

is in the
G
-invariant measure class
[m]
,
where
\phi=\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}/\,{\textrm{d}}m
and
f=\log(\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}/\,{\textrm{d}}m)

are in
L^{2}(S,m)
.
We note that Theorem
3.2
establishes the inclusion:

\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}_{\textbf{sg}}(\Gamma,G;\mu,A,B)\subset\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}_{\textbf{ac}}(\Gamma,G;\mu)

and as a consequence
h_{\mu}(\nu_{\rho})
varies continuously
over
\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}_{\textbf{sg}}(\Gamma,G;\mu,A,B)
.

Theorem 4.1

(Continuity of entropy)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group,
\mu
a generating measure with finite first moment,
G
a semi-simple real Lie group.
Then the entropy map

\rho\in\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}_{\textbf{ac}}(\Gamma,G;\mu){\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}{\mathbf{R}}_{+},\qquad\rho\mapsto h_{\mu}(B_{\rho},\nu_{\rho})

is continuous with respect to the topology inherited from
\textbf{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
.

The first observation concerns weak-* convergence of stationary measures in
\textbf{Prob}(S)
,
S=G/P
.
More precisely

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\rho_{n}\in\textbf{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
,
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
, be a sequence
converging to a limit representation
\rho\in\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}(\Gamma,G)
.
For
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
let
\nu_{n}\in\textbf{Prob}(S)
be a
\rho_{n}(\mu)
-stationary measure, i.e.
a measure satisfying

\nu_{n}=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\rho_{n}(\gamma)_{*}\nu_{n}.

Then
\nu_{n}
weak-* converges to
\nu_{\rho}\in\textbf{Prob}(S)

– the unique
\rho(\mu)
-stationary measure.

Proof.

Since
\textbf{Prob}(S)
is weak-* compact, it suffices to show that:
if a subsequence
\{\nu_{n_{i}}\}
weak-* converges to some
\nu\in\textbf{Prob}(S)
,
then necessarily
\nu=\nu_{\rho}
.

Let
f\in C(S)
be a continuous function. For any
\gamma\in\Gamma
we have

\int_{S}f\circ\rho(\gamma)\,{\textrm{d}}\nu=\lim_{i\to\infty}\int_{S}f\circ\rho_{n_{i}}(\gamma)\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{n_{i}}

where all the terms are bounded by
\|f\|
.
Taking the average over
\gamma\in\Gamma
with distribution
\mu
,
and using
\rho_{n_{i}}(\mu)
-stationarity of
\nu_{n_{i}}
,
we get

\begin{split}\int_{\Gamma}\int_{S}f\circ\rho(\gamma)\,{\textrm{d}}\nu\,{\textrm{d}}\mu(\gamma)&=\lim_{i\to\infty}\int_{\Gamma}\int_{S}f\circ\rho_{n_{i}}(\gamma)\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{n_{i}}\,{\textrm{d}}\mu(\gamma)\\
&=\lim_{i\to\infty}\int_{S}f\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{n_{i}}=\int_{S}f\,{\textrm{d}}\nu.\end{split}

Hence
\nu
is
\rho(\mu)
-stationary.
Since
\rho\in\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}(\Gamma,G)
, there is only one
\rho(\mu)
-stationary measure

\nu_{\rho}
. Therefore
\nu=\nu_{\rho}
.
∎

Hence the map
\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}(\Gamma,G){\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}\textbf{Prob}(S)
,
\rho\mapsto\nu_{\rho}
, is weak-* continuous.
It is not clear how this fact can be used towards continuity of the entropy

(4.1)

h_{\mu}(\nu_{\rho})=\int_{\Gamma}\int_{S}-\log\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}\rho(\gamma)^{-1}_{*}\nu_{\rho}}{\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}}(\xi)\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}(\xi)\,{\textrm{d}}\mu(\gamma)

due to possibly irregular dependence on
\rho
of the integrand.

To overcome this difficulty we make the following observations.
There exists an everywhere defined
continuous
cocycle

\sigma_{0}:G\times S{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}{\mathbf{R}}

that represents the
m
-a.e. defined Radon–Nikodym cocycle

\sigma_{0}(g,\xi)=-\log\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}g^{-1}_{*}m}{\,{\textrm{d}}m}(\xi).

For
\rho\in\textbf{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
we have
|\sigma_{0}(\rho(a),\xi)|\leq\alpha_{\rho}

for
m
-a.e.
\xi\in S
.
Therefore

\|\sigma_{0}(\rho(\gamma),-)\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha_{\rho}\cdot|\gamma|_{A}.

Thus we have the estimate

(4.2)

\int_{\Gamma}\int_{S}|\sigma_{0}(\rho(\gamma),\xi)|\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}(\xi)\,{\textrm{d}}\mu(\gamma)\leq\alpha_{\rho}\cdot\left(\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\mu(\gamma)\cdot|\gamma|_{A}\right)<\infty.

The following general lemma is well known
(cf.
[
14
]
), but for the reader’s convenience
we include here its simple proof.
We consider an abstract setting that involves
a
\Gamma
-space
M
with a
\mu
-stationary measure
\nu
.

Lemma 4.3

(Cohomology lemma)
.

Let
(M,\nu)
be a
(\Gamma,\mu)
-stationary space,
and let
\eta
be a measure on
M
in the measure class
of
\nu
with
\log\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}\nu}{\,{\textrm{d}}\eta}\in L^{1}(\nu)
.
Denote by
\sigma_{\nu},\sigma_{\eta}:\Gamma\times M{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}{\mathbf{R}}
the
\nu
-a.e. defined measurable cocycles

\sigma_{\nu}(g,\xi)=-\log\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}g_{*}^{-1}\nu}{\,{\textrm{d}}\nu}(\xi),\qquad\sigma_{\eta}(g,\xi)=-\log\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}g_{*}^{-1}\eta}{\,{\textrm{d}}\eta}(\xi).

and assume
\sigma_{\eta}\in L^{1}(\Gamma\times M,\mu\times\nu)
.
Then
\sigma_{\nu}\in L^{1}(\Gamma\times M,\mu\times\nu)
and

\int_{\Gamma}\int_{M}\sigma_{\nu}(g.\xi)\,{\textrm{d}}\nu(\xi)\,{\textrm{d}}\mu(g)=\int_{\Gamma}\int_{M}\sigma_{\eta}(g.\xi)\,{\textrm{d}}\nu(\xi)\,{\textrm{d}}\mu(g).

Proof.

For every
g\in\Gamma
we have an a.e. identity

\begin{split}\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}g^{-1}_{*}\eta}{\,{\textrm{d}}\eta}(\xi)&=\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}g^{-1}_{*}\eta}{\,{\textrm{d}}g^{-1}_{*}\nu}(\xi)\cdot\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}g^{-1}_{*}\nu}{\,{\textrm{d}}\nu}(\xi)\cdot\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}\nu}{\,{\textrm{d}}\eta}(\xi)\\
&=\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}\eta}{\,{\textrm{d}}\nu}(g.\xi)\cdot\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}g^{-1}_{*}\nu}{\,{\textrm{d}}\nu}(\xi)\cdot\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}\nu}{\,{\textrm{d}}\eta}(\xi)\end{split}

and therefore

\sigma_{\eta}(g,\xi)=\sigma_{\nu}(g,\xi)+f(g.\xi)-f(\xi).

where
f(\xi)=\log(\,{\textrm{d}}\nu/\,{\textrm{d}}\eta)(\xi)
.
We observe that

\int_{\Gamma}\int_{M}(f\circ g-f)\,{\textrm{d}}\nu\,{\textrm{d}}\mu=\int_{M}f\,{\textrm{d}}\mu*\nu-\int_{M}f\,{\textrm{d}}\nu=0.

This proves the Lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Consider
\rho\in\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}_{\textbf{ac}}(\Gamma,G)
,
let
\nu_{\rho}
be the associated
stationary measure on
S
, and let
\phi=\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}/\,{\textrm{d}}m

and
f=\log\phi
.
By our assumption
\phi,f\in L^{2}(S,m)
, and so Cauchy–Schwarz
inequality implies that
f\in L^{1}(S,\nu_{\rho})
because

\int_{S}|f|\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}=\int_{S}|f|\cdot\phi\,{\textrm{d}}m<\infty.

In view of (
4.2
) it follows that
Lemma
4.3
with
\nu=\nu_{\rho}
and
\eta=m

applies, and so

h_{\mu}(\nu_{\rho})=\int_{\Gamma}\int_{S}\sigma_{0}(\rho(\gamma),\xi)\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}(\xi)\,{\textrm{d}}\mu(\gamma).

Let
\rho_{n},\rho
be a representations in
\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}_{\textbf{ac}}(\Gamma,G;\mu)
so that
\rho_{n}\to\rho

in
\textbf{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
.
Let
\nu_{\rho_{n}}
,
\nu_{\rho}
be the associated stationary measures on
S
.
By Lemma
4.2
we have weak-* convergence
\nu_{\rho_{n}}\to\nu_{\rho}
.

Since
\sigma_{0}:G\times S{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}{\mathbf{R}}
is a continuous map,
for every
\gamma\in\Gamma

we have convergence as
n\to\infty

\int_{S}\sigma_{0}(\rho_{n}(\gamma),\xi)\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho_{n}}(\xi)\quad{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}\quad\int_{S}\sigma_{0}(\rho(\gamma),\xi)\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}(\xi).

Since
\sup_{n}\alpha_{\rho_{n}}<\infty
estimate (
3.1
) justifies
(dominated convergence) the limit

\int_{\Gamma}\int_{S}\sigma_{0}(\rho_{n}(\gamma),\xi)\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho_{n}}(\xi)\,{\textrm{d}}\mu(\gamma)\quad{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}\quad\int_{\Gamma}\int_{S}\sigma_{0}(\rho(\gamma),\xi)\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}(\xi)\,{\textrm{d}}\mu(\gamma).

In view of Lemma
4.3
we deduce

h_{\mu}(B_{\rho_{n}},\nu_{\rho_{n}})\quad{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}\quad h_{\mu}(B_{\rho},\nu_{\rho})

that proves the Theorem.
∎

5.
Estimating Entropy range

So far we established that every
\rho\in\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}_{\textbf{sg}}(\Gamma,G;\mu,A,B)
gives rise to a
boundary
(B_{\rho},\nu_{\rho})
which is non-trivial, and therefore has
a strictly positive entropy
h_{\mu}(B_{\rho},\nu_{\rho})>0
.
The goal of this section is to give an estimate for this entropy
in terms of the quantity

\alpha_{\rho}=\max_{\gamma\in A\cup A^{-1}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\textbf{RN}}(\rho(\gamma))

that gauges the non-triviality of the boundary
(B_{\rho},\nu_{\rho})
.
We focus on
G=\textbf{SO}(3,1)
,
K=\textbf{SO}(3)
, and
S=S^{2}
.

Theorem 5.1

(Entropy estimate)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group,

\mu
be a probability measure on
\Gamma

with finite exponential moment, so that
\textbf{supp}(\mu)
generates
\Gamma
as a semi-group.
Let
G=\textbf{SO}(3,1)
,
K=\textbf{SO}(3)
,
S=S^{2}
and
B\subset\Gamma
be a finite subset
with
\rho_{0}(B)\subset K
having a spectral gap
\kappa_{\rho(B)}>0
.
Then there is
{\textbf{c}_{\textbf{ent}}}>0
,
L
and
\alpha_{0}>0
so that

{\textbf{c}_{\textbf{ent}}}\cdot(\alpha_{\rho})^{2}\leq h_{\mu}(B_{\rho},\nu_{\rho})\leq L\cdot\alpha_{\rho}.

for all
\rho\in\textbf{Hom}^{\textbf{Zd}}_{\textbf{sg}}(\Gamma,G;\mu,A,B)
with
0<\alpha_{\rho}\leq\alpha_{0}
.

The upper estimate follows from Lemma
4.3
and formula
(
4.2
) where

L=\sum_{\gamma}\mu(\gamma)\cdot|\gamma|_{A}.

The challenge in establishing the lower estimate is that the definition
(
4.1
) of
h_{\mu}(B_{\rho},\nu_{\rho})
(or Lemma
4.3

for that matter) involves measure
\nu_{\rho}
which is not known explicitly.
To overcome this difficulty we use the quasi-regular
unitary representation

\pi_{\rho}:\Gamma{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}U(L^{2}(S,\nu_{\rho}))

given by

(\pi_{\rho}(\gamma)f)(x)=\left(\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}\rho(\gamma)^{-1}_{*}\nu_{\rho}}{\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}}(x)\right)^{1/2}\cdot f(\rho(\gamma)^{-1}x).

We denote by
\pi_{\rho}(\mu)
the contraction on
L^{2}(S,\nu_{\rho})
given by the average of unitary operators

\pi_{\rho}(\mu)=\int_{\Gamma}\pi_{\rho}(\gamma)\,{\textrm{d}}\mu(\gamma),

and by
|\pi_{\rho}(\mu)|_{\rm sp}\leq 1
the spectral radius of this contraction.

Lemma 5.2
.

With the notation above we have the estimate

h_{\mu}(S,\nu_{\rho})\geq-2\log|\pi_{\rho}(\mu)|_{\rm sp}

in terms of the spectral radius.

Proof.

Using convexity of
f(t)=-\log(t)
we get

\begin{split}h_{\mu}(S,\nu_{\rho})&=\int_{\Gamma}\int_{S}-\log\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}\rho(\gamma)^{-1}_{*}\nu_{\rho}}{\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}}\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}\,{\textrm{d}}\mu\\
&=2\cdot\int_{\Gamma}\int_{S}-\log\left(\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}\rho(\gamma)^{-1}_{*}\nu_{\rho}}{\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}\,{\textrm{d}}\mu\\
&\geq-2\cdot\log\left(\int_{\Gamma}\int_{S}\left(\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}\rho(\gamma)^{-1}_{*}\nu_{\rho}}{\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,{\textrm{d}}\nu_{\rho}\,{\textrm{d}}\mu\right)\\
&=-2\cdot\log\left\langle{\pi_{\rho}(\mu)\mathbb{1}},{\mathbb{1}}\right\rangle\geq-2\log\|\pi_{\rho}(\mu)\|.\end{split}

Applying this argument to convolution powers
\mu^{*n}
we get

h_{\mu}(S,\nu_{\rho})=\frac{1}{n}h_{\mu^{*n}}(S,\nu_{\rho})\geq-2\frac{1}{n}\log\|\pi_{\rho}(\mu^{*n})\|=-2\log\|\pi_{\rho}(\mu)^{n}\|^{1/n}.

Passing to the limit as
n\to\infty
we deduce the claimed inequality with spectral radius.
∎

Our next observation is that since
\nu_{\rho}
is in the same measure class as
m
,
there is a unitary isomorphism
L^{2}(S,\nu_{\rho}){\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\cong}}}L^{2}(S,m)

that intertwines
\pi_{\rho}
with the unitary representation
\pi\circ\rho

from (
2.6
), and so
\pi_{\rho}(\mu)
on
L^{2}(S,\nu_{\rho})

is isomorphic to the operator
P_{\mu}=\pi\circ\rho(\mu)
on
L^{2}(S,m)

(P_{\mu}f)(\xi)=\int_{\Gamma}\left(\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}\rho(\gamma)_{*}m}{\,{\textrm{d}}m}(\xi)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot f\left(\rho(\gamma)^{-1}\xi\right)\,{\textrm{d}}\mu(\gamma).

As in the proof of Theorem
3.2
, we choose a convex combination
\bar{\mu}

(5.1)

\bar{\mu}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\cdot\mu^{*n_{i}}

of convolution powers of
\mu
, so that
\bar{\mu}\geq p\cdot\mu_{0}

and
\bar{\mu}(a)\geq q
for all
a\in A
, where
\mu_{0}
is the uniform distribution
on
B\cup B^{-1}
, and
p>0
and
q>0
are some constants.
Since
\pi\circ\rho
preserve the cone of positive functions,
the spectral radii of
P_{\mu}
and
P_{\bar{\mu}}
are attained by positive functions,
and one can see that

\left|{P_{\bar{\mu}}}\right|_{\textbf{sp}}\geq F\left(\left|{P_{\mu}}\right|_{\textbf{sp}}\right),\qquad\textrm{where}\qquad F(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\cdot t^{n_{i}}.

The function
F
is strictly convex with
F(1)=1
, hence

F\left(\left|{P_{\mu}}\right|_{\textbf{sp}}\right)\geq 1-F^{\prime}(1)\cdot(1-\left|{P_{\mu}}\right|_{\textbf{sp}})

where the constant
F^{\prime}(1)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}p_{i}
depends only on the fixed data

(\Gamma,\mu)
and
A,B\subset\Gamma
.
In view of Lemma
5.2
, and using the elementary inequality
-\log(1-x)>x
for
x\in(0,1)
,
we have

(5.2)

\begin{split}h_{\mu}(\nu_{\rho})&\geq-2\cdot\log\left(\left|{P_{\mu}}\right|_{\textbf{sp}}\right)\geq 2\left(1-\left|{P_{\mu}}\right|_{\textbf{sp}}\right)\\
&\geq\frac{2}{F^{\prime}(1)}\cdot\left(1-\left|{P_{\bar{\mu}}}\right|_{\textbf{sp}}\right)\geq\frac{2}{F^{\prime}(1)}\cdot(1-\|P_{\bar{\mu}}\|).\end{split}

It remains to get a linear lower estimate on the spectral gap

1-\|P_{\bar{\mu}}\|
on
L^{2}(S,m)
in terms of the quantity

\alpha_{\rho}=\max_{a\in A}\ \log\mathcal{N}_{\textbf{RN}}(\rho(a)).

We have
\bar{\mu}=p\cdot\mu_{0}+(1-p)\cdot\mu^{\prime}
for some
0<p<1
,
where
\mu_{0}
is a uniform distribution on
B\cup B^{-1}
, and
\mu^{\prime}
is some probability
measure on
\Gamma
, where
\mu^{\prime}(a)\geq q/(1-p)\geq q
fpr all
a\in A
.
We have

P_{\bar{\mu}}=p\cdot P_{\mu_{0}}+(1-p)\cdot P_{\mu^{\prime}}.

Denote
\epsilon=1-\|P_{\bar{\mu}}\|
and let
f\in L^{2}(S,m)
be a unit vector
with
\|P_{\bar{\mu}}f\|\geq 1-2\epsilon
. We may assume
f
to be positive
f\geq 0
.
Since

1-2\epsilon\leq\|P_{\bar{\mu}}f\|\leq p\cdot\|P_{\mu_{0}}f\|+(1-p)\cdot\|P_{\mu^{\prime}}f\|,

while
\|P_{\mu_{0}}f\|\leq 1
and
\|P_{\mu^{\prime}}f\|\leq 1
, it follows that

(5.3)

1-\frac{2\epsilon}{p}\leq\|P_{\mu_{0}}f\|\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad 1-\frac{2\epsilon}{1-p}\leq\|P_{\mu^{\prime}}f\|.

Write
f=f_{0}+(1-\delta)\cdot\mathbb{1}
with
f_{0}\in L^{2}_{0}(S,m)
.

Claim
.

\delta<25(p\kappa_{0})^{-2}\cdot\epsilon^{2}<C_{1}\epsilon
.

Proof.

We have
\|f_{0}\|=\sqrt{1-(1-\delta)^{2}}=\sqrt{2\delta-\delta^{2}}\leq\sqrt{2\delta}

and so
\|P_{\mu_{0}}f_{0}\|\leq(1-\kappa_{0})\|f_{0}\|\leq(1-\kappa_{0})\sqrt{2\delta}
.
Hence by triangle inequality

\begin{split}1-\frac{2\epsilon}{p}&\leq\|P_{\mu_{0}}f_{0}\|+(1-\delta)\leq(1-\kappa_{0})\sqrt{2\delta}+(1-\delta)\\
&\leq 1-(\sqrt{2}-1)\kappa_{0}\sqrt{\delta}\end{split}

using
\delta<\sqrt{\delta}
. Thus

\sqrt{\delta}<2\epsilon/(\sqrt{2}-1)p\kappa_{0}<(5/p\kappa_{0})\cdot\epsilon.

∎

Using the second inequality (
5.3
), for any
a\in A
we have

\begin{split}1-\frac{2\epsilon}{1-p}&\leq\left\langle{P_{\mu^{\prime}}f},{f}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle{P_{\mu^{\prime}}\mathbb{1}},{\mathbb{1}}\right\rangle+2\delta\\
&\leq\mu^{\prime}(a)\cdot\left\langle{\pi(\rho(a))\mathbb{1}},{\mathbb{1}}\right\rangle+(1-\mu^{\prime}(a))+2\delta\\
&=1-\mu^{\prime}(a)\cdot(1-\left\langle{\pi(\rho(a))\mathbb{1}},{\mathbb{1}}\right\rangle)+2\delta.\end{split}

This gives

\epsilon+(1-p)\delta>\frac{q}{2}\cdot\left(1-\left\langle{\pi(\rho(a))\mathbb{1}},{\mathbb{1}}\right\rangle\right)
, and in particular

(5.4)

1-\|P_{\bar{\mu}}\|=\epsilon>c_{2}\cdot\left(1-\left\langle{\pi(\rho(a))\mathbb{1}},{\mathbb{1}}\right\rangle\right)

for some constant
c_{2}
that depends only on
\Gamma
,
\mu
,
\kappa_{0}
.

We now specialize to
G=\textbf{SO}(3,1)
and
K=\textbf{SO}(3)

or, equivalently,
G=\textbf{PSL}_{2}({\mathbf{C}})
and
K=\textbf{SU}(2)
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
g\in G
have
\log\mathcal{N}_{\textbf{RN}}(g)=t
.
Then

\left\langle{\pi(g)\mathbb{1}},{\mathbb{1}}\right\rangle=\frac{t}{2\sinh(t/2)}\leq 1-\frac{t^{2}}{25}

for
0<t\leq 1
.

Proof.

Since the constant function
\mathbb{1}
is invariant under
K
,
the value of
\left\langle{\pi(g)\mathbb{1}},{\mathbb{1}}\right\rangle
is bi-
K
-invariant
and can be reduced to a diagonal
g
.
Viewing
G
as
\textbf{PSL}_{2}({\mathbf{C}})
acting on the Riemann sphere
\hat{\mathbf{C}}={\mathbf{C}}\cup\{\infty\}

we may assume

S=\prod_{j=1}^{d}S_{j}
.

Let
J
be a subset of
\{1,\dots,d\}
, denote by
S_{J}=\prod_{j\in J}S_{j}
and

\textbf{pr}_{J}:S\to S_{J}
the corresponding quotient, and let
\nu_{J}=(\textbf{pr}_{J})_{*}\nu

on

be the corresponding quotient. Such a measure is absolutely continuous
with respect to

where

are the
K_{j}
-invariant
probability measures on the Riemann spheres

over

.
Applying Lemma

we deduce that

h_{\mu}(S_{J},\nu_{J})=\sum_{j\in J}h_{\mu}(S_{j},\nu_{j})=\sum_{j\in J}t_{j}.

Denoting
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{d}
the indicator function of
J
and by
C^{\omega}_{s,\textbf{t}}

the quotient
(S_{J},\nu_{J})
of
C_{s,\textbf{t}}=(S,\nu)
, we can rewrite the above
formula in the form

in line with the statement of Theorem

.
Lemma
7.2
.(6) allows us to apply Proposition

and deduce that measurable
\Gamma
-equivariant quotients of

are
G
-equivariant. This shows that the collection

,
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{d}
,
is the
complete
list of measurable
\Gamma
-quotients of
C_{s,\textbf{t}}
.
This collection forms a
d
-cube
\{0,1\}^{d}
with

C_{s,\textbf{t}}=C^{(1,\dots,1)}_{s,\textbf{t}}
and
C^{(0,\dots,0)}_{s,\textbf{t}}=\{*\}

the trivial boundary.

Finally, Proposition
6.2
combined with Lemma

.(6)
guarantees that all the non-trivial quotients

\{C^{\omega}_{s,\textbf{t}}\},\qquad(s\in(0,1],\ \omega\in\{0,1\}^{d}\setminus(0,\dots,0))

are distinct. Moreover, in all cases the
\Gamma
-action is essentially free.

This completes the proof of Theorem
B
, assuming Theorem

that we discuss in the next section.

8.
Spectral Gap for Products and Disjointness

Let
C
be a finite set,
K
a compact groups with Haar measure
m
,
and let
\tau:C\to K
be a map.
In this section, a
spectral gap

\kappa_{\tau}
for such a map
\tau:C\to K

refers to

\kappa_{\tau}=1-\sup\left\{\left.{\|Pf\|}\ \right/\ {f\in L^{2}_{0}(K,m),\ \|f\|=1}\right\}

for the self-adjoint Markov operator

Pf(x)=\frac{1}{2|C|}\left(\sum_{c\in C}f(\tau(c).x)+\sum_{c\in C}^{k}f(\tau(c)^{-1}.x)\right).

This section is devoted to addressing the following problem.

Problem

(Spectral Gap Disjointness)
.

Given a compact group
K
, a finite set
C
and
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
,
construct maps
\tau_{i}:C\to K
,
1\leq i\leq n
, so that the diagonal embedding

\tau_{1}\times\cdots\times\tau_{n}:C\ {\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}\ K\times\cdots\times K

has a spectral gap in the product
K^{n}
. Give a lower bound on this spectral gap
in terms of
n
and gaps
\kappa_{\tau_{1}},\dots,\kappa_{\tau_{n}}
.

Fix
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
. Let
\Delta:K\to K^{n}
denote the diagonal embedding,
\Delta(k)=(k,\dots,k)
. For an
n
-tuple
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
in
K^{n}
we denote the embedding

\Delta^{\bar{a}}(k)=(k^{a_{1}},\dots,k^{a_{n}}),\qquad\textrm{where}\qquad k^{a}=aka^{-1}.

Let
B
be a finite subset of
K
with a spectral gap
\kappa_{B}>0
,
and
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
be a
n
-tuple in
K^{n}
.
Double the finite set
B
by taking

C=B\times\{1,2\}

and consider the embeddings
\tau_{i}:C{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}K
given by

(8.1)

\tau_{i}(b,1)=b,\qquad\tau_{i}(b,2)=b^{a_{i}}\qquad(1\leq i\leq n,\ b\in B).

The Markov operator
P
on
L^{2}_{0}(K^{n},m^{n})
corresponding to this embedding

\bar{\tau}=\tau_{1}\times\cdots\times\tau_{n}:C=B\times\{1,2\}\ {\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}\ K^{n}

is the average
P=\frac{1}{2}(P_{1}+P_{2})
of two self-adjoint Markov operators

P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, corresponding to the maps

\Delta:B\ {\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}\ K^{n},\qquad\Delta^{\bar{a}}:B\ {\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}\ K^{n}.

Our approach applies to a general simple compact Lie group

, but we shall focus
on

(or rather its double cover

),
because this is sufficient
for our purposes, while some technical arguments in the proof are more

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
B
be a finite subset in

with a spectral gap
\kappa_{B}>0
.
For

there exists a
n
-tuple
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
in

so that taking

and maps
\tau_{i}:C\to K^{n}
,

,
as in (

) the Markov operator

.

8.1.
General approach

Consider the action of
K^{n}
on itself by left translations, and
the corresponding unitary representation of
K^{n}
on
L^{2}(K^{n},m^{n})

and on the orthogonal complement to constants:

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}_{0}(K^{n},m^{n}).

We have a finite subset
B\subset K
with a spectral gap
\kappa_{B}>0

on
L^{2}_{0}(K,m)
, and some element
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
in
K^{n}

that will be chosen later to ensure that
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
are well separated in
K
.
Associated with these choices there are two self-adjoint Markov operators:

P_{1}
and
P_{2}
on
K^{n}
, defined by the subsets

\Delta(B),\qquad\Delta^{\bar{a}}(B)

in
K^{n}
. Since the constants are preserved by these operators,

P_{1}
and
P_{2}
act on
\mathcal{H}
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
denote the subspaces of invariants for subgroups

\Delta(K)
and
\Delta^{\bar{a}}(K)
, respectfully.
More precisely:

(8.2)

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{1}&:=\left\{\left.{f\in\mathcal{H}}\ \right/\ {\forall k\in K,\ f(kx_{1},\dots,kx_{n})=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})}\right\},\\
\mathcal{H}_{2}&:=\left\{\left.{f\in\mathcal{H}}\ \right/\ {\forall k\in K,\ f(k^{a_{1}}x_{1},\dots,k^{a_{n}}x_{n})=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})}\right\}.\end{split}

Lemma 8.2
.

For
i=1,2
the splitting
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{i}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\perp}

is
P_{i}
-invariant, where
P_{i}
is the identity on
\mathcal{H}_{i}
,
and
P_{i}
is a
(1-\kappa_{B})
-contraction on
\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\perp}
.

Proof.

We focus on the case
i=1
, as
i=2
follows by applying
the conjugation by
\bar{a}\in K^{n}
.
Since
\Delta(B)\subset\Delta(K)
,
the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is
P_{1}
invariant
and
P_{1}
is the identity on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
.
Being self-adjoint,
P_{1}
preserves
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\perp}
.

The projection
K^{n}\to\Delta(K)\backslash K^{n}
has fibers that
can be identified with
K\cong\Delta(K)
, and the orthogonal
projection
\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
f\mapsto\bar{f}
, given by

\bar{f}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\int_{K}f(kx_{1},\dots,kx_{n})\,{\textrm{d}}m(k),

is by integration over these fibers.
Thus
f\in\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\perp}
iff the disintegration

f=\int_{\Delta(K)\backslash K^{n}}f_{y}

of
f
along these fibers has

a.e.
The Markov operator
P_{1}
preserves each one of these fibers,
acting as
(1-\kappa_{B})
-contraction on each

. Thus

f\in\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\perp}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\|P_{1}f\|\leq(1-\kappa_{B})\cdot\|f\|

which is precisely the claim that
P_{1}
is
(1-\kappa_{B})
-contraction on
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\perp}
.
∎

Define the
angular separation
of the subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
by

(8.3)

\delta(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}):=1-\sup\left\{\left.{\frac{|\left\langle{f_{1}},{f_{2}}\right\rangle|}{\|f_{1}\|\cdot\|f_{2}\|}}\ \right/\ {f_{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{i}\setminus\{0\}}\right\}.

(Note that
\mathcal{H}_{1}\cap\mathcal{H}_{2}=\{0\}
is necessary but not sufficient
for positive separation in infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces).

Lemma 8.3
.

Suppose
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
are self-adjoint Markov operators on
\mathcal{H}
,
where
P_{i}
fixes point-wise subspace
\mathcal{H}_{i}
vectors, and acts as
a
(1-\kappa)
-contraction on
\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\perp}
, and assume that

\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
have positive angular separation
\delta=\delta(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})>0
.
Then the average
P=\frac{1}{2}(P_{1}+P_{2})
is a
(1-\kappa\cdot\delta^{2}/36)
contraction on
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Take arbitrary
f\in\mathcal{H}
with
\|f\|=1
. Since
\|Pf\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|P_{1}f\|+\frac{1}{2}\|P_{2}f\|
,
while
\|P_{i}f\|\leq 1
, it suffices to show that

\min_{i=1,2}\|P_{i}f\|\leq 1-\frac{\kappa\cdot\delta^{2}}{18}.

Write
f=f_{1}+g_{1}=f_{2}+g_{2}
with
f_{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{i}
and
g_{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\perp}
.
Then
1=\|f\|^{2}=\|f_{i}\|^{2}+\|g_{i}\|^{2}
for
i=1,2
.
We first note that
f
cannot be very close to both
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
.
More precisely, the claim is that

\max(\|g_{1}\|,\|g_{2}\|)>\frac{\delta}{3}.

Indeed, otherwise
\|g_{i}\|\leq\delta/3
and
\|f_{i}\|\geq\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}/9}
,
and we get

\begin{split}1=\left\langle{f},{f}\right\rangle&=\left\langle{f_{1}+g_{1}},{f_{2}+g_{2}}\right\rangle\\
&\leq|\left\langle{f_{1}},{f_{2}}\right\rangle|+|\left\langle{f_{1}},{g_{2}}\right\rangle|+|\left\langle{g_{1}},{f_{2}}\right\rangle|+|\left\langle{g_{1}},{g_{2}}\right\rangle|\\
&\leq(1-\delta)\cdot\|f_{1}\|\cdot\|f_{2}\|+\|g_{1}\|+\|g_{2}\|+\|g_{1}\|\cdot\|g_{2}\|\\
&\leq(1-\delta)\cdot(1-\frac{\delta^{2}}{9})+\frac{\delta}{3}+\frac{\delta}{3}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{9}\leq 1-\frac{\delta}{3}+\frac{\delta^{3}}{9}<1\end{split}

which is absurd.

Assuming
\|g_{1}\|\geq\delta/3
we will show
\|P_{1}f\|\leq 1-\kappa\delta^{2}/18
.
The argument for the other case is identical.
Since
\|f_{1}\|=\sqrt{1-\|g_{1}\|^{2}}
,
P_{1}f_{1}=f_{1}
,
P_{1}g_{1}\perp f_{1}
,
and
(1-\kappa)^{2}\leq 1-\kappa
, we have

\begin{split}\|P_{1}f\|^{2}&=\|f_{1}\|^{2}+\|P_{1}g_{1}\|^{2}\leq(1-\|g_{1}\|^{2})+(1-\kappa)^{2}\cdot\|g_{1}\|^{2}\\
&\leq 1-\|g_{1}\|^{2}+(1-\kappa)\cdot\|g_{1}\|^{2}=1-\kappa\cdot\|g_{1}\|^{2}\\
&\leq 1-\frac{\kappa\cdot\delta^{2}}{9}.\end{split}

For
t\in(0,1)
we have
\sqrt{1-t}<1-t/2
. Hence we get

\|P_{1}f\|<1-\kappa\cdot\delta^{2}/18
.
As was pointed out at the beginning of the proof this estimate gives

\|Pf\|\leq 1-\frac{\kappa\cdot\delta^{2}}{36}

as required.
∎

The next step for the proof of Theorem
8.1
is to choose

\bar{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in K^{n}
to ensure that the subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
\mathcal{H}_{2}

have a positive angular separation, more precisely

\delta(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})\geq\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}\ ??

for some constant

.
To this end we identify
\mathcal{H}_{i}
with

as follows:
the space
\mathcal{H}_{1}
consists of functions of the form

where
\phi\in L^{2}_{0}(K^{n-1},m^{n-1})
. Similarly, a general

is obtained from
\psi\in L^{2}_{0}(K^{n-1},m^{n-1})
be the formula

\begin{split}g(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})&=\psi((x_{1}^{a_{1}})^{-1}\cdot x_{2}^{a_{2}},\dots,(x_{1}^{a_{1}})^{-1}\cdot x_{n}^{a_{n}})\\
&=\psi(a_{1}x_{1}^{-1}(a_{1}^{-1}a_{2})x_{2}a_{2}^{-1},\dots,a_{1}x_{1}^{-1}(a_{1}^{-1}a_{n})x_{n}a_{n}^{-1})\\
&=\psi^{\prime}(x_{1}^{-1}c_{2}x_{2},\dots,x_{1}^{-1}c_{n}x_{n}),\end{split}

where

for

and
\psi^{\prime}
is

by
(a_{1},\dots,a_{1})
and from the right by

Since the maps

L^{2}_{0}(K^{n-1},m^{n-1}){\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}\mathcal{H}_{1},\ \phi\mapsto f,\qquad L^{2}_{0}(K^{n-1},m^{n-1}){\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits}}\mathcal{H}_{2},\ \psi\mapsto g

and

\begin{split}\left\langle{f},{g}\right\rangle&=\int_{K^{n}}\phi(x_{1}^{-1}x_{2},\dots,x_{1}^{-1}x_{n})\cdot\bar{\psi}^{\prime}(x_{1}^{-1}b_{2}x_{2},\dots,x_{1}^{-1}b_{n}x_{n})\,{\textrm{d}}x_{1}\dots\,{\textrm{d}}x_{n}\\
&=\int_{K^{n-1}}\phi(y_{2},\dots,y_{n})\cdot\left(\int_{K}\bar{\psi}^{\prime}(c_{2}^{x}\cdot y_{2},\dots,c_{n}^{x}\cdot y_{n})\,{\textrm{d}}x\right)\,{\textrm{d}}y_{2}\dots\,{\textrm{d}}y_{n}\\
&=\left\langle{\phi},{Q_{\mu}\psi^{\prime}}\right\rangle\end{split}

where

are unit vectors in

, and

is the

given by the convolution with the probability measure
that describes the distribution of

\mu=\int_{K}\delta_{(c_{2}^{x},\dots,c_{n}^{x})}\,{\textrm{d}}m(x).

Hence the for all

,

with

by the operator norm of

on

.
Thus the angular separation between
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
in

is bounded from below by this spectral gap:

\delta(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})\geq 1-\|Q_{\mu}\|.

8.2.
A spectral gap of
Q_{\mu}

We consider
\mu

etc. on
H=K^{n}

.
These are probability measures on

More generally, let

We denote by

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
L<H
be a closed subgroup of a compact group
H
. Then

(1)

The space
H^{L}
is a compact space.

(2)

The closed convex subspace
\textbf{Prob}(H)^{L}\subset\textbf{Prob}(H)

is closed under convolution.

(3)

There is a natural identification
\textbf{Prob}(H^{L})\cong\textbf{Prob}(H)^{L}
.
In particular,
\textbf{Prob}(H)^{L}
is a convex weak-* compact with
extreme points
\left\{\left.{\mu_{x}}\ \right/\ {x\in H^{L}}\right\}
.

(4)

For any
x,y\in H^{L}
there is a unique
measure
\omega_{x,y}\in\textbf{Prob}(H^{L})
so that

\mu_{x}*\mu_{y}=\int_{H^{L}}\mu_{z}\,{\textrm{d}}\omega_{x,y}(z).

Proof.

(1)
L
is a compact group being a closed subgroup in a compact group
H
.
It acts on
H
by conjugation, hence the space
H^{L}
of the orbits of this action
is Hausdorff, and since
H
is compact while
H\to H^{L}
is continuous,
H^{L}
is a compact space.

(2) For
\mu,\eta\in\textbf{Prob}(H)^{L}
and
\ell\in L
we have

(\mu*\eta)^{\ell}=\int_{H}\int_{H}\delta_{(h_{1}h_{2})^{\ell}}\,{\textrm{d}}\mu(h_{1})\,{\textrm{d}}\eta(h_{2})=\int_{H}\int_{H}\delta_{h_{1}^{\prime}h_{2}^{\prime}}\,{\textrm{d}}\mu^{\ell}(h_{1}^{\prime})\,{\textrm{d}}\eta^{\ell}(h_{2}^{\prime})=\mu*\eta

because
(h_{1}h_{2})^{\ell}=h_{1}^{\ell}h_{2}^{\ell}
.

(3) The identification
\textbf{Prob}(H^{L})\cong\textbf{Prob}(H)^{L}
follows from the fact that
H^{L}

is Hausdorff and that any
L
-orbit
x=h^{L}
has the form
L/L_{0}
for a closed subgroup

L_{0}=C_{L}(x)
and therefore carries a unique
L
-invariant probability measure
\mu_{x}
.
By Riesz representation theorem every
\eta\in\textbf{Prob}(H)^{L}=\textbf{Prob}(H^{L})

decomposes uniquely as an integral over its extreme points

, i.e. as
an integral of
\mu_{x}
.

(4) By (2)
\mu_{x}*\mu_{y}\in\textbf{Prob}(H^{L})
and by (3) it decomposes uniquely as
an integral of
\mu_{z}
,
z\in H^{L}
.
∎

The group

\Phi(k)\in[0,\pi]

. In fact, we have a homeomorphism

\textbf{SO}(3)^{\textbf{SO}(3)}\ {\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\Phi}}}\ [0,\pi].

For
\phi\in[0,\pi]

\mu_{\phi}
on

-sphere

.

for

.

The group of unit quaternions is

Lemma 8.5

(Implicit in Archimedes !)

For
\alpha,\beta\in[0,\pi]
we have

\mu_{\alpha}*\mu_{\beta}=\frac{1}{2\sin\alpha\sin\beta}\cdot\int_{\overline{\alpha-\beta}}^{\overline{\alpha+\beta}}\mu_{\phi}\cdot\sin(\phi)\,{\textrm{d}}\phi.

In particular, for
\alpha\in(0,\pi/2]

\mu_{\alpha}*\mu_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2\sin^{2}\alpha}\cdot\int_{0}^{2\alpha}\mu_{\phi}\cdot\sin(\phi)\,{\textrm{d}}\phi.

Proof.

Multiplication of quaternions can be expressed as

(r_{1}+\mathbf{v}_{1})\cdot(r_{2}+\mathbf{v}_{2})=\left(r_{1}r_{2}-\left\langle{\mathbf{v}_{1}},{\mathbf{v}_{2}}\right\rangle\right)+\left(r_{1}\mathbf{v}_{2}+r_{2}\mathbf{v}_{1}+\mathbf{v}_{1}\times\mathbf{v}_{2}\right).

In particular, the scalar part of the products
p\cdot q
of two unit quaternions

p=\cos\alpha+\sin\alpha\cdot\mathbf{u},\qquad q=\cos\beta+\sin\beta\cdot\mathbf{w}

with unit vectors
\mathbf{u}
,
\mathbf{w}
, is given by

(8.5)

\cos\alpha\cdot\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\cdot\sin\beta\cdot\left\langle{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle.

The distribution of
\left\langle{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle
for
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{w}

being chosen independently and uniformly over
S^{2}
, is the same as the distribution
of a projection of a single random
\mathbf{u}\in S^{2}
to a fixed axis.
In his famous computation of surface area for
S^{2}
Archimedes (!)
showed that this projection is uniformly distributed over
[-1,1]
.

The extreme cases of
\left\langle{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle=\pm 1
correspond to
the value of
\cos(\alpha\pm\beta)
for (
8.5
).
Therefore the scalar part of
p\cdot q
is a uniform distribution
over the interval with endpoints

\cos(\alpha-\beta)=\cos(\overline{\alpha-\beta}),\qquad\cos(\alpha+\beta)=\cos(\overline{\alpha+\beta}),

while
\Theta(p\cdot q)
distributes over the interval with endpoints

\overline{\alpha-\beta}
and
\overline{\alpha+\beta}

with density
\,{\textrm{d}}\cos\phi=-\sin(\phi)\,{\textrm{d}}\phi
, appropriately rescaled.
The rescaling is by

\cos(\overline{\alpha-\beta})-\cos(\overline{\alpha+\beta})=\cos(\alpha-\beta)-\cos(\alpha+\beta)=2\sin\alpha\sin\beta.

∎

In
\textbf{SO}(3)
the angle of rotation
\Phi(k)
can be expressed as the maximal displacement

\Phi(k)=\max_{\xi\in S^{2}}d(k\xi,\xi)

of points on the unit 2-sphere.
This implies that
\Phi(k_{1}^{-1}k_{2})
is a bi-invariant metric on
\textbf{SO}(3)
.
It follows that
\Phi(p^{-1}q)
is a bi-invariant metric on
{\mathbb{H}_{1}}
.
For
\alpha>0
we denote by

U_{\alpha}=\left\{\left.{p\in{\mathbb{H}_{1}}}\ \right/\ {\Phi(p)<\alpha}\right\}

the open
\alpha
-neighborhood of the identity in
{\mathbb{H}_{1}}
, and by

m_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{m(U_{\alpha})}\cdot m|_{U_{\alpha}}

the normalized restriction of the Haar measure to this neighborhood.

Lemma 8.6
.

The Haar measure
m
on
{\mathbb{H}_{1}}
and
m_{\alpha}
are both conjugation invariant and given by

m=\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{2\sin^{2}\phi}{\pi}\cdot\mu_{\phi}\,{\textrm{d}}\phi,\qquad m_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{m(U_{\alpha})}\cdot\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{2\sin^{2}\phi}{\pi}\cdot\mu_{\phi}\,{\textrm{d}}\phi,

where

m(U_{\alpha})=\frac{\alpha-\sin(\alpha)\cdot\cos(\alpha)}{\pi}=\frac{4}{3\pi}\cdot\alpha^{3}+O(\alpha^{5}).

In particular,
\mu_{\alpha}^{*2}=\mu_{\alpha}*\mu_{\alpha}
and
m_{2\alpha}

are in the same measure class, and
the Radon-Nikodym derivative is

\frac{\,{\textrm{d}}(\mu_{\alpha}^{*2})}{\,{\textrm{d}}(m_{2\alpha})}(\phi)=\left(\frac{8\alpha}{3}+O(\alpha^{3})\right)\cdot\frac{1}{\sin\phi}\cdot 1_{(0,2\alpha)}(\phi).

Proof.

Unit quaternions
{\mathbb{H}_{1}}
can be identified with the
3
-sphere
S^{3}
,
and the Haar measure is the normalized sphere-volume.

Lemma 8.7
.

For any

\Theta(p_{i}^{-1}p_{j})\geq\frac{1}{100\cdot n^{\frac{1}{3}}}
