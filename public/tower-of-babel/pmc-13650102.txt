The Expanding Role of GLP-1 Receptor Agonists and Dual GIP/GLP-1 Agonists in Dermatology: From Immunometabolism to Clinical Practice
Authors: Caterina M. Giorgio, Carola Romanò, Mariachiara Arisi, Mariateresa Rossi, Marina Venturini, Sara Mezzana, Laura Grigolato, Benedetta Galli, Maria M. Nicoletti, Cesare Tomasi, Gaetano Licata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) and dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide/glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists) have rapidly changed the management of obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus. Beyond their effects on weight reduction and glycemic control, these agents exert pleiotropic effects on inflammation, adipose tissue biology, endothelial function and tissue repair, properties of growing relevance to dermatology, where several chronic inflammatory diseases are closely linked to obesity, insulin resistance and systemic low-grade inflammation. Current evidence suggests potential clinical benefit in psoriasis and hidradenitis suppurativa, encouraging signals regarding wound healing and a generally favorable cutaneous safety profile, although cutaneous adverse events, including injection site reactions, hypersensitivity, alopecia and rare autoimmune eruptions, have been increasingly reported. This review summarizes the immunometabolic rationale, current clinical evidence, the dermatologic safety profile and future perspectives of GLP-1RAs and dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists in dermatology. Although available data remain heterogeneous and largely observational outside psoriasis and hidradenitis suppurativa, these therapies may represent a new class of systemic immunometabolic modulators with practical implications for inflammatory, surgical and aesthetic dermatology.

Introduction

The therapeutic landscape of obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus has been profoundly transformed by glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) and, more recently, dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide/glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists. Initially developed as metabolic drugs, these agents are now recognized as systemic modulators of inflammation, adipose tissue remodeling, vascular biology and tissue repair. This broader biologic profile has important implications for dermatology, where many inflammatory skin diseases are closely linked to metabolic dysfunction, obesity, insulin resistance and chronic low-grade systemic inflammation [1–5].

Adipose tissue functions as an active immune-endocrine organ capable of producing adipokines, cytokines and lipid mediators that shape immune responses; in obesity, adipocyte hypertrophy and macrophage infiltration promote a persistent low-grade inflammatory state (“meta-inflammation”) that amplifies pathways central to cutaneous inflammation. This immunometabolic framework is particularly relevant to psoriasis and hidradenitis suppurativa, two disorders in which metabolic syndrome, obesity and systemic inflammation are common and clinically meaningful, and in which metabolic dysfunction appears to influence disease severity, therapeutic response and comorbidity burden. The availability of drugs capable of simultaneously reducing body weight, improving insulin resistance and attenuating inflammatory signaling has therefore generated considerable interest in their dermatologic potential [3, 5–8].

At the same time, the increasing use of GLP-1RAs and dual incretin agonists has created new diagnostic and management challenges for dermatologists. Cutaneous adverse reactions may range from common injection site reactions to rare but clinically important immune-mediated eruptions. Hair shedding related to rapid weight loss, facial volume depletion associated with pharmacologic weight reduction and perioperative considerations in patients receiving incretin-based therapies are also becoming increasingly relevant in daily dermatologic practice [9, 10].

This review provides a concise synthesis of the current evidence on GLP-1RAs and dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists in dermatology, focusing on immunometabolic mechanisms, therapeutic applications, cutaneous adverse events, wound healing, aesthetic implications and future clinical perspectives.

Methods

A focused literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE during 2026 to identify clinically relevant evidence on the dermatologic implications of GLP-1RAs and dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists. The search strategy combined terms related to incretin-based therapies and dermatologic outcomes, including “GLP-1 receptor agonists,” “semaglutide,” “liraglutide,” “dulaglutide,” “tirzepatide,” “GIP/GLP-1 agonists,” “psoriasis,” “hidradenitis suppurativa,” “atopic dermatitis,” “wound healing,” “diabetic foot ulcers,” “cutaneous adverse events,” “bullous pemphigoid,” “hair loss” and “perioperative outcomes,” using appropriate Boolean operators. Clinical studies and review articles addressing the mechanisms, therapeutic applications, safety profile or dermatologic outcomes of these agents were considered for inclusion. Studies not directly relevant to the dermatologic scope of the review were excluded. Reference lists of relevant publications were additionally screened to identify pertinent studies not captured by the initial database search. Given the narrative nature of the review, the search was intended to provide a broad and clinically relevant synthesis of the available literature rather than to follow a formal systematic review methodology.

Discussion

Immunometabolic Basis of GLP-1 Receptor Agonists in Dermatology

The concept of immunometabolism recognizes that metabolic pathways actively regulate immune cell differentiation, inflammatory signaling and tissue homeostasis rather than representing an independent biologic system. This paradigm is particularly relevant in dermatology, where chronic inflammatory skin diseases frequently coexist with obesity, insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome, suggesting that systemic metabolic dysfunction actively contributes to cutaneous inflammation rather than simply accompanying it [11, 12].

Adipose tissue occupies a central position within this network. Once considered merely an energy storage organ, it is now recognized as a metabolically active endocrine and immunologic compartment composed of adipocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages and multiple lymphocyte subsets. Under physiologic conditions, adipose tissue maintains an anti-inflammatory microenvironment characterized by regulatory T cells, alternatively activated (M2) macrophages and anti-inflammatory adipokines such as adiponectin. Progressive adipocyte hypertrophy disrupts this balance through hypoxia, extracellular matrix remodeling and oxidative stress, promoting monocyte recruitment and polarization toward pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages. The resulting increase in tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-6, together with activation of nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling, establishes a persistent state of low-grade systemic inflammation that extends far beyond adipose tissue itself [13–15].

These metabolic alterations directly influence the skin. Obesity enhances activation of the IL-23/Th17 pathway, amplifies macrophage activation and promotes endothelial dysfunction—mechanisms central to inflammatory dermatoses. Leptin, resistin and visfatin stimulate inflammatory signaling, whereas reduced adiponectin removes an important endogenous brake on immune activation. Consequently, obesity not only increases the prevalence of diseases such as psoriasis and hidradenitis suppurativa but may also contribute to greater disease severity, reduced response to biologic therapy and increased cardiovascular comorbidity [16, 17].

GLP-1 receptor agonists appear to interfere with several components of this network. Beyond reducing caloric intake and body weight through central appetite regulation, GLP-1 signaling attenuates inflammatory activity by suppressing NF-κB activation and downstream production of TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6, while improving endothelial nitric oxide bioavailability and limiting oxidative stress. Experimental models further suggest modulation of inflammasome activation and macrophage polarization toward reparative phenotypes, providing a biologic explanation for anti-inflammatory actions that appear to extend beyond weight loss alone [1, 18, 19].

Dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists add a further dimension to this biology. Tirzepatide consistently produces greater reductions in body weight and visceral adiposity than currently available GLP-1RAs, raising the possibility that superior metabolic improvement translates into greater attenuation of systemic inflammation; experimental evidence also suggests complementary effects of GIP receptor activation on adipose tissue remodeling and cytokine production, although the relative contribution of direct GIP signaling versus enhanced metabolic efficacy remains incompletely understood [1, 20, 21].

An important caveat concerns receptor localization. While pancreatic β-cells remain the primary target of GLP-1 signaling, receptor expression has also been demonstrated in cardiovascular tissue, kidneys, the central nervous system and several immune cell populations; in contrast, receptor expression in human skin remains less clearly defined. Available evidence suggests that some cutaneous effects may derive from indirect regulation of immune cells and adipose tissue rather than from widespread expression in resident keratinocytes or fibroblasts, and studies evaluating psoriatic skin have suggested that apparent receptor positivity may reflect inflammatory immune cell infiltrates rather than constitutive epidermal expression. This distinction supports the concept that many dermatologic effects of GLP-1RAs result from systemic immunometabolic reprogramming rather than direct pharmacologic stimulation of skin cells [1, 3, 22, 23].

Taken together, these observations position GLP-1 receptor agonists as systemic immunometabolic therapies rather than exclusively metabolic drugs, providing a coherent biologic rationale for their expanding application across inflammatory skin diseases and chronic wound repair (Fig. 1). Therapeutic Applications in Dermatology

Psoriasis

Among inflammatory skin diseases, psoriasis currently represents the condition for which the strongest clinical evidence supporting GLP-1 receptor agonists has been generated. The close association between psoriasis and obesity has long suggested that metabolic dysfunction contributes directly to disease pathogenesis rather than representing a simple comorbidity through chronic activation of the IL-23/Th17 axis, increased circulating TNF-α, IL-6 and leptin, reduced adiponectin and persistent macrophage activation—mechanisms that contribute to keratinocyte hyperproliferation and maintenance of chronic plaque inflammation [3, 13, 14].

Early clinical observations described rapid improvement of psoriatic lesions following initiation of liraglutide in obese patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus, in some cases preceding clinically significant weight loss. These reports generated the hypothesis that incretin-based therapies might exert direct immunomodulatory effects independent of their metabolic benefits, a possibility subsequently supported by translational studies demonstrating reductions in inflammatory biomarkers and modulation of invariant natural killer T-cell activity [5, 7, 24].

The most robust prospective evidence currently available derives from an open-label randomized clinical trial evaluating semaglutide in obese patients with concomitant type 2 diabetes mellitus and psoriasis. Compared with standard diabetes management, semaglutide produced significantly greater reductions in Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) scores, along with marked improvements in Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI). Treatment was accompanied by significant reductions in circulating IL-6 and C-reactive protein, supporting the hypothesis that clinical improvement reflects attenuation of systemic inflammatory activity rather than weight loss alone. Although limited by a relatively small sample size and short follow-up period, this trial currently represents the strongest prospective evidence supporting the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists in psoriasis [25].

Real-world studies and systematic reviews have largely confirmed these findings, with most patients experiencing clinically meaningful reductions in disease severity together with improved quality of life and metabolic parameters. Nevertheless, available evidence remains heterogeneous in study design, patient selection, concomitant systemic therapies and duration of follow-up, and most published studies enrolled obese individuals with diabetes or metabolic syndrome, making it difficult to determine whether similar benefits would occur in metabolically healthy patients with psoriasis [5, 8, 11].

Regarding dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists, they represent the next frontier in this field. Their superior efficacy in reducing body weight and visceral adiposity raises the possibility of greater anti-inflammatory activity than currently achievable with GLP-1 receptor agonists alone, although direct dermatologic data remain extremely limited; ongoing trials combining tirzepatide with biologic therapies for psoriatic disease may clarify whether dual agonism provides additive benefit beyond metabolic optimization [1, 20].

The most relevant clinical evidence to date comes from the phase 3b TOGETHER-PsO randomized clinical trial, which evaluated concomitant ixekizumab and tirzepatide in 274 adults with moderate-to-severe psoriasis and overweight or obesity [26]. At week 36, 27.1% of patients receiving ixekizumab plus tirzepatide simultaneously achieved PASI 100 and at least 10% weight reduction compared with 5.8% of patients receiving ixekizumab alone. Moreover, complete skin clearance (PASI 100) was achieved by 40.6% and 29.0% of patients, respectively, while 69.2% of participants receiving combination therapy achieved at least 10% weight reduction compared with 9.1% in the ixekizumab group. These findings provide the first randomized clinical evidence that combining a highly effective biologic treatment with a dual incretin agonist may simultaneously improve psoriasis outcomes and metabolic risk in patients with obesity or overweight. Importantly, however, the study was not designed to determine whether the additional improvement in psoriasis was mediated primarily by weight loss, improved metabolic status or direct immunomodulatory effects of GIP/GLP-1 receptor activation [26].

Current evidence cannot definitively distinguish weight-dependent from weight-independent therapeutic effects, and findings should be framed accordingly: available data indicate that GLP-1 receptor agonists may improve psoriasis severity in selected populations rather than establishing a direct disease-modifying effect. Future studies should incorporate mediation analyses, standardized inflammatory biomarkers and carefully matched weight loss comparators to resolve this question.

Similarly, for dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists, comparative studies directly evaluating tirzepatide against GLP-1 receptor agonists at comparable levels of weight loss are lacking, making it difficult to determine whether dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor activation confers an intrinsic immunomodulatory advantage. Future randomized trials should incorporate standardized dermatologic endpoints, longitudinal inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers, and appropriate weight loss comparators.

Hidradenitis Suppurativa

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is another disease in which immunometabolism has emerged as a major pathogenic determinant. Obesity is one of the strongest risk factors for HS, contributing through mechanical stress, altered microbiome composition, insulin resistance and chronic systemic inflammation; adipose tissue-derived cytokines amplify TNF-α, IL-17 and IL-23 signaling, while metabolic syndrome and smoking further increase the inflammatory burden [9, 27].

Clinical evidence supporting GLP-1 receptor agonists in HS has expanded rapidly over the past 3 years, progressing from isolated case reports to retrospective cohort studies and, more recently, systematic reviews showing consistently favorable trends across multiple outcomes, including Hurley stage progression, DLQI scores, inflammatory markers and healthcare utilization [28, 29].

The largest available systematic review, encompassing more than 60,000 patients across observational studies, demonstrated clinically meaningful improvements in disease severity together with reductions in C-reactive protein and glycated hemoglobin. Notably, improvement in HS activity was not always proportional to weight loss, suggesting that at least part of the therapeutic benefit may derive from direct modulation of inflammatory pathways rather than reduced body mass alone. Substantial heterogeneity among included studies, the absence of randomized trials and variability in outcome measures, however, currently preclude definitive conclusions regarding efficacy [28].

Mechanistically, GLP-1 receptor agonists appear well suited to HS because they simultaneously target several components of disease pathogenesis—suppression of TNF-α, NF-κB and IL-17 signaling, improvement of insulin resistance and modulation of macrophage polarization—effects that may complement rather than replace currently available biologic therapies. Combination treatment integrating biologic cytokine inhibition with systemic metabolic modulation through incretin-based therapy may represent one of the most promising future applications [9, 30].

Current European guidelines acknowledge the potential role of GLP-1 receptor agonists in obese patients with HS while emphasizing that available evidence remains preliminary. These agents should therefore presently be regarded as promising adjunctive therapies in appropriately selected patients rather than established disease-modifying treatments; large randomized controlled trials using validated HS-specific endpoints remain an important unmet need.

Regarding dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists in hidradenitis suppurativa, current clinical evidence remains limited to case-based observations, including a recent report describing marked improvement in severe hidradenitis suppurativa following tirzepatide treatment [31]. Although these findings are consistent with the strong association among obesity, insulin resistance and systemic inflammation in hidradenitis suppurativa, they remain insufficient to establish therapeutic efficacy or a causal relationship [32].

Wound Healing, Diabetic Foot Ulcers and Perioperative Outcomes

Delayed wound healing, particularly in patients with diabetes mellitus, results from persistent inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, impaired angiogenesis, oxidative stress and defective keratinocyte migration—processes that are simultaneously influenced by incretin-based therapy, raising interest in GLP-1RAs as potential adjuncts to tissue repair beyond their metabolic effects [9, 15, 33].

Experimental studies provide substantial biologic plausibility for a direct role in wound healing. In diabetic animal models, liraglutide accelerates wound closure, enhances granulation tissue formation and promotes collagen deposition while reducing inflammatory infiltrates through activation of PI3K/Akt signaling, increased endothelial nitric oxide synthase activity and enhanced vascular endothelial growth factor expression, resulting in improved angiogenesis and perfusion. Macrophage polarization toward reparative (M2) phenotypes appears to be a central mechanism, with reduced TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 and increased IL-10 and transforming growth factor-β favoring fibroblast activation and collagen synthesis. GLP-1 receptor agonists additionally reduce oxidative injury by activating antioxidant defenses and suppressing NADPH oxidase activity, thereby preserving cellular viability within the wound bed [9, 15].

One particularly intriguing observation is that some reparative effects appear to occur independently of classical GLP-1 receptor signaling. Animal studies evaluating liraglutide in diabetic wound models have demonstrated accelerated wound closure through mechanisms involving myosin-1c (Myo1c) and dedicator of cytokinesis 5 (Dock5), suggesting that incretin-based therapies may activate alternative intracellular pathways relevant to epidermal regeneration. Although these findings require confirmation in human tissue, they indicate that the regenerative properties of GLP-1RAs may extend beyond their endocrine actions [15].

Clinical evidence remains more limited than experimental data but is steadily expanding, particularly for diabetic foot ulcers. Narrative and systematic reviews consistently report improved microvascular function, reduced inflammatory biomarkers and enhanced tissue oxygenation associated with GLP-1RA therapy, but robust randomized trials specifically evaluating complete ulcer healing, healing time or recurrence remain lacking. GLP-1RAs should therefore currently be viewed as agents that may optimize the biologic environment for wound repair rather than as primary wound-healing therapies [12, 15].

The perioperative setting provides additional, increasingly reassuring evidence. Large propensity score-matched cohort studies involving tens of thousands of surgical procedures have shown that perioperative GLP-1 receptor agonist use is not associated with increased surgical site infection rates, and several investigations have reported significantly lower rates of wound dehiscence, postoperative hematoma and hospital readmission among GLP-1RA users compared with matched diabetic controls. Procedure-specific data in free-flap breast reconstruction have similarly shown lower rates of wound complications, surgical site infection and dehiscence among users versus non-users—a particularly informative finding given that microsurgical reconstruction is one of the most demanding models of wound healing [16].

Not all surgical settings, however, have produced uniformly favorable results. A retrospective analysis of abdominal panniculectomy reported increased rates of delayed wound healing among GLP-1RA users despite lower rates of postoperative seroma. However, treated patients also had more severe obesity and higher rates of diabetes and comorbidity than controls, and nutritional deficiencies associated with rapid weight loss may have partly offset any biologic benefit. Current evidence, therefore, does not justify broad conclusions about impaired healing; rather, it emphasizes individualized perioperative assessment based on procedure type and patient-specific risk factors [16, 34].

A related practical concern is delayed gastric emptying induced by GLP-1 receptor agonists, which has raised anesthesiologic concern regarding aspiration risk during deep sedation or general anesthesia. Initial recommendations favored temporary discontinuation before surgery, particularly for long-acting weekly formulations; more recent multidisciplinary guidance instead favors an individualized approach that balances aspiration risk against the metabolic consequences of treatment interruption, considering procedure type and gastrointestinal symptoms. For dermatologic surgery performed under local anesthesia—including most excisional and flap procedures—current evidence does not support routine discontinuation of GLP-1RA therapy. Management should instead be individualized according to anesthetic requirements when sedation or general anesthesia is planned [17].

Overall, available evidence supports a cautiously reassuring view: rather than impairing tissue repair, GLP-1 receptor agonists appear capable of improving several biologic pathways fundamental to regeneration, with large clinical cohorts demonstrating neutral-to-favorable perioperative outcomes. Definitive recommendations, however, await prospective dermatologic and surgical trials that use standardized wound-healing endpoints, particularly for diabetic foot ulcers and reconstructive surgery.

Other Inflammatory Dermatoses

Compared with psoriasis and hidradenitis suppurativa, evidence supporting GLP-1 receptor agonists in other inflammatory dermatoses remains limited, derived predominantly from case reports, small observational studies and large epidemiologic databases rather than disease-specific trials.

Atopic dermatitis has attracted attention because obesity and metabolic dysfunction have been implicated in selected disease phenotypes. However, the largest comparative real-world study evaluating GLP-1 receptor agonists did not identify a significant difference in incident atopic dermatitis compared with dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors after adjustment for confounders, and controlled evidence of therapeutic efficacy in established disease is currently lacking [17, 35].

Similarly, current evidence does not support a consistent association between GLP-1 receptor agonists and lichen planus, vitiligo, morphea, dermatomyositis, systemic sclerosis or pyoderma gangrenosum: large population-based analyses have generally not demonstrated significant differences in incidence compared with active comparators, while isolated case reports describe both improvement and disease onset after incretin exposure—observations that likely reflect disease rarity, limited statistical power and the inherent limitations of pharmacovigilance data [18, 35].

Interest has also emerged regarding connective tissue diseases and granulomatous disorders, supported by mechanistic plausibility (macrophage activation, T-cell polarization and cytokine modulation) but not by adequately powered clinical trials. At present, their role should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence-based.

Overall, the strongest dermatologic evidence remains confined to psoriasis and hidradenitis suppurativa. For most other inflammatory dermatoses, current data primarily provide reassurance that GLP-1 receptor agonists do not appear to substantially increase disease incidence while highlighting the need for prospective, disease-specific studies.

Dermatologic Adverse Events Associated with GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

Despite a generally favorable safety profile, GLP-1 receptor agonists have increasingly been associated with a broad spectrum of cutaneous adverse events. Most reactions are uncommon and mild, but an expanding number of pharmacovigilance reports indicate that dermatologists should be familiar with both expected and unusual manifestations. Importantly, the overall incidence of severe dermatologic toxicity remains very low, and in most patients, treatment can be continued following appropriate evaluation [36].

Injection Site Reactions

Injection site reactions are the most frequently encountered dermatologic complication, usually manifesting as transient erythema, edema, pruritus or localized induration shortly after subcutaneous administration. When biopsied, lesions generally show superficial perivascular lymphocytic infiltrates with variable eosinophils, compatible with delayed hypersensitivity. Most resolve spontaneously without treatment discontinuation, although persistent nodules have occasionally been reported with long-acting formulations; rotating injection sites and appropriate technique significantly reduce their frequency [36].

Hypersensitivity Reactions

Hypersensitivity reactions range from localized delayed-type reactions to generalized urticaria, angioedema and, exceptionally, anaphylaxis. True IgE-mediated allergy appears uncommon, although delayed T-cell-mediated hypersensitivity has been documented by positive intradermal and patch testing in isolated patients. Cross-reactivity among different GLP-1 receptor agonists remains poorly characterized, and successful switching from one molecule to another has been reported in selected cases, suggesting incomplete immunologic overlap among individual compounds [19].

Autoimmune Blistering Disease (Bullous Pemphigoid)

Bullous pemphigoid is one of the most clinically relevant autoimmune blistering diseases potentially associated with incretin-based therapy. While the association between dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors and bullous pemphigoid is well established, evidence involving GLP-1 receptor agonists is considerably weaker; sporadic cases have nonetheless been described following treatment with semaglutide, dulaglutide and liraglutide. Proposed mechanisms include immune dysregulation, altered antigen presentation and modification of basement membrane immune tolerance secondary to systemic immunometabolic change. Given the widespread prescription of these agents, clinicians should consider medication history in newly diagnosed autoimmune blistering disorders, although current evidence does not support routine discontinuation in the absence of a convincing temporal association [20].

Paradoxical Inflammatory Eruptions

Isolated reports describe psoriasiform dermatitis, eczematous eruptions, erythema annulare centrifugum-like lesions and granulomatous dermatitis. Because obesity itself is a chronic inflammatory state and many treated patients receive multiple concomitant medications, establishing causality remains difficult. In several reports, lesions resolved after drug withdrawal and recurred on rechallenge, supporting a causal relationship, whereas in others, continuation of therapy did not worsen disease activity [21, 36].

Hair Disorders

Hair disorders have received particular attention since the widespread use of semaglutide and tirzepatide for obesity treatment, with diffuse telogen effluvium increasingly reported during periods of rapid weight loss. Current evidence suggests that nutritional change, caloric restriction, protein deficiency and the physiologic stress of substantial weight reduction are more likely responsible than direct pharmacologic toxicity, consistent with similar hair shedding observed after bariatric surgery and other intensive weight loss interventions. Most cases develop approximately 3 to 4 months after rapid weight reduction and gradually resolve with nutritional optimization and weight stabilization [23, 37].

Facial Volume Loss (“Ozempic Face”)

The so-called “Ozempic face,” characterized by apparent facial volume loss and accentuated wrinkles following substantial weight reduction, is not a direct dermatologic adverse effect of GLP-1 receptor activation but rather a predictable consequence of rapid loss of facial subcutaneous adipose tissue, analogous to changes long recognized after bariatric surgery or marked caloric restriction. Dermatologists increasingly encounter patients seeking cosmetic correction through fillers, biostimulatory agents or energy-based procedures after pharmacologically induced weight loss, making aesthetic counseling an increasingly relevant component of patient management during GLP-1RA therapy.

Rare Immune-Mediated Reactions

Rarely reported reactions include fixed drug eruption, acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis, leukocytoclastic vasculitis and eosinophilic dermatoses. Evidence remains confined to isolated case reports, precluding reliable estimation of incidence or identification of specific risk factors, and causal relationships cannot always be definitively established because of concomitant medications and underlying systemic disease [36].

Overall, current evidence indicates that GLP-1 receptor agonists have a favorable dermatologic safety profile given their rapidly expanding clinical use. Most cutaneous adverse events are uncommon, reversible and manageable without permanent discontinuation; continued pharmacovigilance nonetheless remains essential as millions of patients worldwide are expected to receive these agents for obesity, diabetes and future immunometabolic indications and as exposure expands across progressively younger and healthier populations.

Future Perspectives and Conclusions

The rapid evolution of incretin-based therapies has opened a new field at the intersection of endocrinology, immunology and dermatology. Medications initially developed for glucose regulation are now recognized as systemic immunometabolic modulators with potential relevance to multiple skin diseases, and dermatologists will increasingly encounter both the beneficial and adverse cutaneous consequences of GLP-1 receptor agonists and next-generation incretin therapies [1, 2].

Among inflammatory dermatoses, psoriasis and hidradenitis suppurativa currently offer the most compelling rationale, supported by experimental, translational and observational data suggesting reduced disease activity through both weight-dependent and direct immunomodulatory mechanisms. However, randomized controlled trials are lacking for HS, and for psoriasis the relative contribution of weight loss versus direct immune modulation remains unresolved. Future studies integrating clinical outcomes with molecular biomarkers and lesional tissue analyses will be needed to identify patients most likely to benefit [5, 31].

For other inflammatory skin diseases, current epidemiologic data are generally reassuring regarding disease incidence, while isolated case reports continue to describe uncommon immune-mediated adverse events; these should be interpreted cautiously, recognizing that widespread drug exposure inevitably increases the absolute number of coincidental temporal associations. Robust pharmacovigilance systems, standardized diagnostic criteria and prospective dermatology registries—incorporating clinical photography, biopsy findings and standardized causality assessment—will become increasingly important as prescription volumes continue to expand worldwide [36].

One of the most important scientific priorities is to distinguish weight-dependent from weight-independent biologic effects. Because nearly all available clinical studies involve obese or diabetic populations undergoing substantial weight reduction, future randomized trials should incorporate causal mediation analyses, carefully matched weight loss comparators and comprehensive metabolic phenotyping to isolate the independent contribution of incretin signaling [1, 2].

In future clinical trials, careful patient selection and stratification by obesity, insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, baseline inflammatory burden and disease severity will be essential for identifying the immunometabolic phenotypes most likely to benefit. Prospective studies should also incorporate circulating inflammatory cytokines, adipokines, metabolic parameters and, whenever feasible, lesional tissue biomarkers to identify predictors of therapeutic response and disentangle the relative contributions of metabolic improvement and direct immunomodulatory effects. Finally, long-term studies are needed to assess the durability of dermatologic benefit, treatment persistence, recurrence after treatment discontinuation and long-term cutaneous safety, particularly as these therapies are increasingly used to manage chronic metabolic diseases.

Comparative trials should also evaluate GLP-1 receptor agonists and dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists both as monotherapies and in combination with established biologic treatments. In psoriasis and hidradenitis suppurativa, studies comparing biologic therapy alone with biologic therapy plus incretin-based treatment could determine whether metabolic modulation provides additional clinical benefit beyond cytokine-targeted therapy. Importantly, trials should incorporate appropriate weight loss comparators and mediation analyses to distinguish effects attributable to weight reduction from those related to direct immunometabolic signaling.

Parallel advances in molecular biology, including single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics and multiplex immunohistochemistry, are expected to clarify whether GLP-1 receptors are functionally expressed in keratinocytes, fibroblasts, melanocytes and dermal adipocytes—knowledge essential for understanding both therapeutic opportunities and adverse cutaneous reactions [9].

The emergence of dual and triple incretin agonists (combining GLP-1, GIP and glucagon receptor agonism) further expands this research horizon. Whether these next-generation agents will produce greater benefit or different adverse event profiles in inflammatory skin disease remains unknown, providing an unprecedented opportunity to study how modulation of multiple metabolic pathways influences cutaneous immunity [2, 28].

Finally, integrating circulating cytokine and adipokine profiles, metabolomic analyses and advanced skin imaging into future trials may enable the identification of predictive biomarkers and immunometabolic endotypes most likely to benefit from incretin therapy—a precision medicine approach that would minimize unnecessary treatment exposure while maximizing dermatologic benefit [9].

Conclusions

GLP-1 receptor agonists have evolved from antidiabetic medications into multifunctional immunometabolic therapies with important implications for dermatology. Current evidence supports beneficial effects in psoriasis and hidradenitis suppurativa, with encouraging data on wound healing and an overall favorable dermatologic safety profile, despite the emergence of uncommon immune-mediated adverse reactions. Significant knowledge gaps remain regarding receptor biology, optimal patient selection and long-term dermatologic outcomes, and addressing these questions will require close collaboration among dermatologists, endocrinologists, immunologists and translational scientists. As incretin therapeutics continue to expand beyond diabetes into obesity and other chronic diseases, dermatology is poised to become one of the specialties most profoundly influenced by this rapidly evolving field.
