SIRT5-mediated regulation of BMP2 succinylation enhances osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and promotes tibial fracture healing
Authors: Hongji Gu, Buhan Liu, Yue An
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) play a crucial role in bone repair and regeneration. The sirtuin family member sirtuin 5 (SIRT5) has been implicated in various cellular processes, including the regulation of osteogenic differentiation. This study aimed to investigate the role of SIRT5 in osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and its impact on fracture healing.MethodsBMSCs were characterized and induced to undergo osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation. The effects of SIRT5 modulation on osteogenic differentiation were assessed through alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining, Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining, and osteogenic genes expression analysis. The role of SIRT5 in regulating bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2) succinylation and stability was explored using Western blot, immunoprecipitation, and co-immunoprecipitation. A rat tibial fracture model was used to evaluate the in vivo effects of SIRT5 and BMP2 modulation on fracture healing, assessed by Micro-CT and histological analysis.ResultsSIRT5 overexpression enhanced osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, as evidenced by increased ALP activity and mineralization. It also upregulated the expression of osteogenic markers such as osteopontin (OPN), runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), osterix (OSX), and BMP2. Conversely, SIRT5 knockdown inhibited these processes. Mechanistically, SIRT5 was found to regulate BMP2 succinylation, affecting its stability and function. In vivo, overexpression of SIRT5 in a rat tibial fracture model significantly improved bone volume fraction, trabecular number, and thickness, while reducing trabecular separation. The beneficial effects of SIRT5 overexpression were counteracted by BMP2 knockdown, highlighting the importance of BMP2 in mediating SIRT5’s actions on bone healing.ConclusionSIRT5 promotes osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and enhances fracture healing by modulating BMP2 succinylation. These findings suggest that SIRT5 could be a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of bone fractures and other skeletal disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04808-2.Keywords: Tibial fracture healing, Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, Osteogenic differentiation, SIRT5, BMP2, Succinylation

Introduction

Fracture healing is a complex biological process that involves a series of coordinated cellular events, including inflammation, repair, and remodeling, ultimately leading to the restoration of bone integrity [1]. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) play a pivotal role in this process due to their multipotentiality and ability to differentiate into osteoblasts, the cells responsible for bone formation [2]. The osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs is a critical determinant of fracture healing outcomes, with the potential to enhance bone regeneration and improve healing times [3, 4]. Understanding the molecular mechanisms that govern BMSC differentiation is therefore essential for developing therapeutic strategies to promote efficient bone repair.

Bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2), a member of the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) superfamily, has been extensively studied for its osteoinductive properties [5, 6]. BMP2 is a key regulator of BMSCs osteogenic differentiation, as it activates intracellular signaling pathways that lead to the transcription of osteoblast-specific genes [5, 7]. The importance of BMP2 in fracture healing is underscored by its ability to induce ectopic bone formation and enhance bone regeneration at sites of injury [8]. However, the clinical application of BMP2 is limited by its short half-life and rapid degradation, necessitating the development of strategies that can enhance its stability and bioavailability at the fracture site [9].

Sirtuin 5 (SIRT5), a NAD+-dependent desuccinylase, has emerged as a critical epigenetic regulator in various cellular processes, including the modulation of signaling pathways involved in cell differentiation and metabolism [10, 11]. Recent evidence suggests that SIRT5-mediated succinylation plays a significant role in the regulation of osteogenic differentiation [12, 13]. By desuccinylating specific lysine residues on target proteins within the BMP2 signaling cascade, SIRT5 may either activate or repress the signaling pathway, which in turn affects the commitment of BMSCs to the osteoblastic lineage. This modulation is crucial for the orchestration of bone formation during fracture healing, highlighting the potential of SIRT5 as a novel therapeutic target for enhancing bone regeneration. The present study aims to investigate the mechanism by which SIRT5-mediated succinylation modification regulates BMP2 signaling and promotes BMSCs osteogenic differentiation, contributing to the understanding of the molecular underpinnings of fracture healing and providing insights into the development of targeted therapies for bone repair.

Materials and methods

Identification of BMSCs

Human BMSCs were purchased from ATCC (PCS-500-012), and cultured in were cultured in complete growth medium consisting of 89% DMEM/F12 (Gibco, 21331020), 10% FBS (Gibco, A5670801), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122) under an atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2 at 37℃. BMSCs were identified by flow cytometry using antibodies CD105-PerCP-Cy5.5, CD73-APC, and CD90-PE for positive markers, and CD45-PE and CD34-PE for negative markers (all purchased from eBioscience, the catalog number is MA5-17691 and 25-0349-42). Cells were detached using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, 25200072), centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in PBS (Gibco, AM9624) for staining. Aliquots of 50 µL of cell suspension containing approximately 106 cells were incubated with the antibody cocktails for 20 min on ice in the dark. After incubation, an additional 1 mL of staining buffer was added to each tube, and the cells were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with PBS before being resuspended in 100 µL of buffer for flow cytometry analysis on a BD FACSCalibur system using CellQuest software (BD Biosciences).

Osteogenic induction, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining, and Alizarin red S (ARS) staining of BMSCs

For osteogenic induction, the culture medium was supplemented with 50 µg/ml Ascorbic Acid (Sigma-Aldrich, A4034), 10 mM β-Glycerophosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, G9891), and 100 nM Dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich, D4902). Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, J19943.K2) for 15 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with a staining solution containing 2 mg/mL Naphthol AS-MX Phosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, N4875) and 0.5 mg/mL Fast Red TR Salt (Sigma-Aldrich, 368881) in 0.1 M Tris buffer (pH 8.2) for 30 min at 37 °C. The reaction was stopped by washing with PBS, and the cells were examined under a microscope for the presence of red-colored ALP-positive areas.

Additionally, cells were stained with 0.1% ARS solution (pH adjusted to 4.1–4.3 with 10% ammonium hydroxide) (Sigma-Aldrich, A5533) for 10 min at room temperature. Excess dye was removed by washing with PBS, and the cells were examined for the presence of red-orange calcium deposits.

Adipogenic differentiation and oil red O staining of BMSCs

For adipogenic differentiation, BMSCs were induced with DMEM containing 10% FBS, 0.5 mM 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (Sigma-Aldrich, I7018), 0.5 µM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich, 265005), and 60 µM indomethacin (Sigma-Aldrich, 405268) for 3 days, followed by culture in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1 µg/mL insulin for an additional 3 days. Cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature to preserve cell morphology. After fixation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove any residual culture medium. Cells were then treated with 60% isopropanol (Sigma-Aldrich, 278475) for 5 min to dehydrate and enhance the permeability of cell membranes. The cells were stained with Oil Red O Staining Solution (Sigma-Aldrich, MAK560) for 15 min at room temperature. The staining solution was prepared by mixing ORO Stain A and ORO Stain B in a 3:2 ratio, followed by a 20–40 min culturing to allow the solution to clarify. Excess stain was removed by washing with 60% isopropanol, and cells were then rinsed with PBS to remove unbound dye. After staining, cells were mounted with an aqueous mounting medium and examined under a light microscope to visualize lipid droplets stained red by Oil Red O.

Transduction of BMSCs with lentiviral vectors

For lentiviral vector production, 293T packaging cells (ATCC, CRL-3216) were seeded at 3.8 × 106 cells per 10 cm tissue culture plate in DMEM nd incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for approximately 20 h. Lentiviral vectors (GeneCopoeia) carrying SIRT5/BMP2 overexpression or shRNA targeting SIRT5 were co-transfected with psPAX2 packaging and pMD2.G envelope plasmids into 293T cells. The transfection mix was added to the 10 cm plate containing the 293T cells and incubated for 18 h, after which the media was replaced and the cells were incubated until virus harvest at 48, 72, and 96 h post-transfection. The viral supernatant was centrifuged at 2100 rcf for 5 min to pellet packaging cells, filtered through a 0.45 μm polyethersulfone filter, and stored at − 80 °C. The ViraBind™ Lentivirus Concentration and Purification Kit (Cell Biolabs, VPK-091) was used for concentration, resulting in highly purified lentivirus. BMSCs were transduced with the harvested lentivirus. Post-transduction, cells were incubated for 72 h to allow for gene expression changes before further analysis.

Preparation and grouping of rat tibial fracture model

Rats were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane (C153359, Aladdin). A longitudinal incision, approximately 1.5 cm in length, was made on the lateral aspect of the left lower limb at the tibial region, and the skin was carefully dissected to expose the tibialis anterior muscle, which was then peeled back layer by layer to reveal the mid-shaft of the tibia. Following this, a punch was used to create a hole, and a Kirschner wire was fully inserted into the medullary cavity of the tibia for intramedullary fixation. The tibia was then transected horizontally at a depth of approximately 0.5 cm below the tibial tuberosity to induce a fracture. The surgical site was irrigated and closed with a layered suture technique. Postoperatively, rats received daily intraperitoneal injections of penicillin for three consecutive days to prevent infection. Subsequently, rats with tibial fractures were randomly assigned to five different groups. We injected 100 µL lentiviral vector (a lentiviral titer of 1 × 109 transducing units per mL) at the fracture site. After 4 weeks, the experimental animals were euthanized by inhaling isoflurane and cervical dislocation. The left tibia of each group of rats was isolated and the attached soft tissues such as muscles were removed. The Kirschner wire in the tibia was removed from the proximal end for subsequent testing.

Micro-CT detection

Tibial samples were fixed in a dedicated cylinder and scanned with a Micro-CT system using parameters set to 70 kVp X-ray, collecting 1000 images at 180° intervals. The built-in CT evaluation software of the machine was utilized to analyze the newly formed callus tissue at the fracture site, generating three-dimensional images and callus morphological parameters. These parameters included bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), which were used to assess the microstructure of the newly formed bone.

HE staining

After fixing the tibial samples (3 from each group), they were decalcified in a 0.5 mol/L EDTA solution for 4 weeks. After decalcification, conventional dehydration, and other treatments, the slices were embedded in paraffin. Coronal section (5 mm/slice) were taken from the fracture site and incubated with lignin staining solution for 10 min, hydrochloric acid ethanol differentiation for 20 s, PBS decolorization for 40 s, and eosin staining solution for 1 min before sealing. The tissue morphology at the fracture site was observed under an optical microscope.

Gene expression analysis

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (RT-qPCR) was used for gene expression analysis. Total RNA was extracted from BMSCs and bone marrow tissue of rats using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and concentration of RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with RNA Integrity Number values above 8.0 being considered acceptable for further analysis. Complementary DNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems) following the supplier’s instructions. The reaction was carried out in a Veriti Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems) at 25 °C for 10 min, followed by 37 °C for 120 min, and a final step at 85 °C for 5 min. Specific primers for SIRT5, OPN, RUNX2, OSX, and BMP2 were designed using Primer-BLAST (NCBI) and synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies. Primer sequences were as follows: human SIRT5 forward: 5’-TGGAGGAGGTTGACAGAGAGC-3’, reverse: 5’-CTGCTGGGTACACCACAGA-3’; human osteopontin (OPN) forward: 5’-GAAGTTTCGCAGACCTGACAT-3’, reverse: 5’-GTATGCACCATTCAACTCCTCG-3’; rat OPN forward: 5’-GCTGAAGCCTGACCCATCT-3’, reverse: 5’-TCCCGTTGCTGTCCTGAT-3’; human runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) forward: 5’-CCGCCTCAGTGATTTAGGGC-3’, reverse: 5’-GGGTCTGTAATCTGACTCTGTCC-3’; rat RUNX2 forward: 5’-CCCAACTTCCTGTGCTCC-3’, reverse: 5’-AGTGAAACTCTTGCCTCGTC-3’; human osterix (OSX) forward: 5’-TTTGCTCCCCTTAATCCAGCC-3’, reverse: 5’-CCTGGCAATTAGGGCAGTCG-3’; rat OSX forward: 5’-CCCTCTGCGAGACTCAACA-3’, reverse: 5’-TGCCCACCACCTAACCAA-3’; human BMP2 forward: 5’-TTCGGCCTGAAACAGAGACC-3’, reverse: 5’-CCTGAGTGCCTGCGATACAG-3’; and rat BMP2 forward: 5’-TAAGTTCTGTCCCTACTGATG-3’, reverse: 5’-AAAGACCTGCTAATCCTCA-3’. RT-qPCR was performed using the PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on a QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Each 20 µL reaction contained 10 µL of SYBR Green Master Mix, 1 µL of each primer (10 µM), and 2 µL of cDNA template. The thermal cycling conditions were 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, normalizing to the housekeeping gene GAPDH.

Subcellular fractionation

Subcellular fractions were prepared from approximately 2 × 107 BMSCs using the Pierce Subcellular Protein Fractionation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 78840) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and sequentially lysed with Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents I and II to obtain the cytoplasmic fraction. The pellet was then extracted with Membrane Extraction Reagent (MER) to yield the membrane/organelle (including mitochondrial) fraction, followed by nuclear protein extraction using Nuclear Extraction Reagent (NER). All centrifugation steps were performed at 12,000×g for 5–10 min at 4 °C. Protease and phosphatase inhibitors were added fresh to all buffers. Fraction purity was verified by Western blotting.

Western blot, immunoprecipitation, and co-immunoprecipitation

BMSCs were lysed using RIPA Lysis Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 89901) containing Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche, 4693116001) and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche, 04906837001) to ensure complete lysis and prevent degradation of proteins. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce, 23227) to ensure equal loading of proteins for Western blot analysis. Primary antibodies are listed as follows: SIRT5 (Abcam, ab259967, 1:1000), β-actin (Abcam, ab8227, 1:1000), OPN (Abcam, ab307994, 1:1000), RUNX2 (Abcam, ab192256, 1:1000), Osx (Abcam, ab209484, 1:1000), BMP2 (Abcam, ab214821, 1:1000), IL-6 (Abcam, ab259341, 1:1000), TNF-α (Abcam, ab205587, 1:1000), IGF-1 (Alomone Labs, ANT-046-200UL, 1:200).

For enrichment of BMP2 protein, we utilized the Protein G Magnetic Beads (Invitrogen, 88802) and a specific BMP2 antibody (Abcam, ab284387, 1:30) that recognizes an internal region of human BMP2. The antibody was precoated onto the magnetic beads according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE using the NuPAGE Novex 4–12% Bis-Tris Protein Gel (Invitrogen) and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad) using the Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad). Nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, 126615) in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were incubated with the primary BMP2 antibody (Abcam, ab284387, 1:1000) at a dilution of 1:1000 overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a dilution of 1:5000 for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized using the Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad) and imaged on a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

To assess the succinylation levels of BMP2, we used a Succinyl-Lysine (K) Antibody (PTM Biolabs, PTM-401) which specifically recognizes succinylated lysine residues. The membrane was stripped using the Restore Western Blot Stripping Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and reprobed with the succinylation antibody following the same protocol as above.

Prediction of potential succinylation sites

The Generic and Species-specific Succiniylation Sites Prediction Server (GPSuc, http://kurata14.bio.kyutech.ac.jp/GPSuc/index.php) was used to predict the potential succinylation sites of human BMP2 protein.

The potential lysine-succinylation sites of human BMP2 (UniProt ID: P12643) were predicted using the Generic and Species-specific Succinylation Sites Prediction Server (GPSuc, http://kurata14.boto.kyutech.ac.jp/GPSuc/index.php) [14]. We retained lysines with a high-stringency threshold (FPR ≤ 1%, score ≥ 7.5) and additionally filtered for solvent-exposed residues (ASA ≥ 20%) using NetSurfP-2.0 to prioritize functionally modifiable sites.

Mutation of BMP2 mRNA was achieved with the help of Quickchange Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies). As for protein stability, cycloheximide (CHX, 50 mg/mL; S7418, Selleck Chemicals; Houston) was added, and the samples were collected at 0, 8, 16, and 24 h, and the cell proteins were extracted. The expression of BMP2 was detected by western blotting analysis.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

For the dual-luciferase assay, a 2.0-kb fragment of the human BMP2 promoter was amplified from BMSC genomic DNA and cloned into the KpnI/HindIII sites of pGL4.20[luc2] (Promega) to generate pGL4-BMP2-promoter-luc; pRL-TK (10 ng/well, Promega) served as the transfection control. Passage-3 hBMSCs (2 × 10⁴ cells/well, 96-well plate) were co-transfected with 100 ng reporter plasmid plus either empty pLVX, LV-SIRT5 overexpression vector, or sh-SIRT5 knockdown construct using Lipofectamine 3000 (DNA: lipid 1:2.5, Invitrogen) in six replicates per group. After 48 h, cells were lysed with 50 µL passive lysis buffer (Promega Dual-Luciferase Kit) and Firefly/Renilla activities were measured on a SpectraMax i3x reader; Firefly values were normalized to Renilla (F/R ratio) and expressed as fold-change versus empty-vector control (set to 1.0).

Statistics

The Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA was used to analyze the differences between two or among multiple groups. All data shown represent the results obtained from triplicated independent experiments with standard deviation of the mean (mean ± SD). The values of P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs

We initially induced osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation in BMSCs to assess their differentiation capabilities. The ALP staining of BMSCs post-osteogenic induction was displayed in Fig. 1A, where a distinct increase in ALP activity was observed, indicating the onset of osteogenic differentiation. The staining revealed a homogeneous distribution of purple-colored deposits, confirming the presence of ALP-positive cells. Meanwhile, the ARS staining of the mineralized extracellular matrix in BMSCs following osteogenic differentiation was performed. The presence of red-orange nodules in the culture indicated calcium deposition, a hallmark of osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 1B). After adipogenic induction, the Oil Red O staining of BMSCs showed that lipid-laden adipocytes were stained red, confirming the successful differentiation of BMSCs into adipocytes. The staining intensity and distribution provided a visual representation of lipid accumulation within the cells (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, the phenotypic characterization of BMSCs was conducted using flow cytometry. The flow cytometry results indicated that BMSCs expressed the surface markers CD105, CD73, and CD90 positively, while CD45 and CD34 were negative, confirming the mesenchymal stem cell phenotype of the cultured cells (Fig. 1D).

A positive correlation between SIRT5 expression and osteogenic differentiation

After 14 days of osteogenic induction, the ARS staining revealed an increase in red-orange mineralized nodules, indicating active mineralization and matrix deposition within the BMSCs. Quantitative analysis of the ARS staining showed a significant increase, which correlates with the extent of mineralization (Fig. 2A). Quantitatively, the ALP activity was also measured and found to be significantly elevated in induced BMSCs compared to controls (Fig. 2B), suggesting a successful osteogenic differentiation process. During osteogenic differentiation, the expression of SIRT5 at the mRNA and protein levels were also measured. SIRT5 mRNA levels increased progressively with the duration of osteogenic induction, as determined by qRT-PCR (Fig. 2C). Figure 2D illustrated the protein expression of SIRT5 by western blot, which also exhibited an upward trend with extended induction times (Fig. 2D). These data indicated a positive correlation between SIRT5 expression and osteogenic differentiation.

SIRT5 effects on osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs

To further investigate the regulatory effects of SIRT5 on osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, SIRT5 expression was downregulated or upregulated, respectively. The qPCR and western blot results demonstrated a significant reduction in SIRT5 expression in the shSIRT5 group compared to the shNC group, confirming the efficiency of SIRT5 knockdown. There is a marked increase in SIRT5 expression in the SIRT5 group compared to the vector group, indicating successful overexpression of SIRT5 (Fig. 3A and B). Knockdown of SIRT5 significantly inhibited cell viability of BMSCs, while overexpression of SIRT5 significantly enhances it. The SIRT5 group showed significantly higher viability than the vector group (Fig. 3C). The ARS staining intensity and the corresponding quantitative analysis revealed that SIRT5 knockdown inhibited mineralization, while overexpression promoted it (Fig. 3D and E). Similarly, ALP staining and its quantitative analysis showed that SIRT5 knockdown reduces ALP activity, and overexpression increased it, indicating the impact of SIRT5 on early osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 3F and G). Additionally, knockdown of SIRT5 significantly reduces the mRNA expression of osteogenic markers (OPN, RUNX2, Osx, and BMP2) compared to the shNC group while overexpression of SIRT5 exerted the opposite way (Fig. 3H). The blots and the corresponding densitometric analysis confirmed the qPCR results, demonstrating that SIRT5 knockdown inhibited the expression of these osteogenic markers while upregulation of SIRT5 elevated levels these osteogenic markers (Fig. 3I).

SIRT5 regulation of BMP2 protein succinylation and stability

SIRT5 prevents the degradation of target proteins through desuccinylation modification, thereby enhancing their stability [15]. The global succinylation levels in BMSCs transfected with sh-NC, sh-SIRT5, Vector, or SIRT5 using a succinylation-specific antibody were detected. Western blot analysis revealed that knockdown of SIRT5 increased overall succinylation levels, while its overexpression decreased them, indicating SIRT5’s role in regulating succinylation (Fig. 4A). The results of co-immunoprecipitation followed by Western blot analysis demonstrated the BMP2 succinylation levels. The data showed that SIRT5 knockdown enhanced BMP2 succinylation and reduced BMP2 protein levels, whereas SIRT5 overexpression had the opposite effect, suggesting SIRT5’s direct influence on BMP2 succinylation and stability (Fig. 4B). Figure 4C depicted the results of an immunoprecipitation assay in BMSCs, demonstrating the interaction between SIRT5 and BMP2 (Fig. 4C). The findings indicated a physical association between these two proteins, which may be crucial for the regulation of BMP2 function. We then predicted potential succinylation sites on BMP2 using GPSuc, Among the these high-score lysines, Lys-272 and Lys-297 ranked highest and are located in the solvent-exposed loop region of mature BMP2. Subsequently, we validated the succinylation sites of BMP2 by constructing wild-type (WT) and mutant BMP2 (K272R or K297R). Arginine (R) simulates the succinylation state of lysine by introducing similar sizes and charges, which helps to understand the functional consequences of succinylation. Compared with WT BMP2, the K272R mutant exhibited a significant increase in protein abundance and a marked reduction in succinylation, whereas the K297R mutant showed decreased protein levels and enhanced succinylation (Fig. 4D). These data indicate that Lys-272 is the primary succinylation site that governs BMP2 stability, while Lys-297 may serve as an auxiliary or context-dependent modification residue. BMP2 protein stability regulated by SIRT5 was detected by treating cells with CHX to inhibit protein synthesis. Western blot analysis of BMP2 protein levels at 0, 4, 8, and 12 h post-CHX treatment suggested that SIRT5 knockdown promoted BMP2 protein degradation, while SIRT5 overexpression inhibited it, highlighting SIRT5’s role in maintaining BMP2 stability (Fig. 4E and F). SIRT5 over-expression increased BMP2-promoter-driven luciferase activity, whereas SIRT5 knock-down reduced it, confirming that SIRT5 enhances BMP2 transcription (Figure S1). Additionally, under continuous sh-SIRT5, cells expressing BMP2-K272R displayed prolonged half-life, restored ALP/ARS mineralisation and downstream RUNX2/OSX/OPN levels, whereas the K272Q remained unstable and osteo-incompetent. These single-residue data demonstrate that SIRT5 drives osteogenesis chiefly via direct Lys-272 desuccinylation of BMP2, not by simply increasing BMP2 transcription (Figure S2).

BMP2 overexpression reverses the effects of SIRT5 knockdown in BMSCs

The qPCR and western blot results for BMP2 mRNA expression in BMSCs transfected with either a control vector or a BMP2-overexpressing vector revealed a significant increase in BMP2 expression in the BMP2 group compared to the vector group, confirming the successful overexpression of BMP2 (Fig. 5A). Knockdown of SIRT5 significantly reduced cell viability, while overexpression of BMP2 significantly reversed this reduction, indicating that BMP2 overexpression can counteract the negative effects of SIRT5 knockdown on cell viability (Fig. 5B). The ARS staining intensity and the corresponding quantitative analysis showed that SIRT5 knockdown inhibited mineralization, and BMP2 overexpression significantly reversed this effect (Fig. 5C and D). Similarly, ALP staining and its quantitative analysis demonstrated that SIRT5 knockdown reduced ALP activity, and BMP2 overexpression increased it, indicating that BMP2 overexpression can restore the osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs that is diminished by SIRT5 knockdown (Fig. 5E). Knockdown of SIRT5 significantly reduces the mRNA and protein expression of OPN, RUNX2, OSX, and BMP2 compared to the sh-NC group. However, overexpression of BMP2 significantly reversed these reductions (Fig. 5F and G).

The impact of SIRT5 and BMP2 modulation on tibial fracture healing in rats

Micro-CT scans of tibial fractures at four weeks post-injury revealed significant differences in bone healing among the various treatment groups. Overexpression of SIRT5 significantly increased BV/TV, Tb. N, and Tb. Th, while decreasing Tb.Sp. In contrast, knockdown of BMP2 in the group co-injected with SIRT5-overexpressing lentivirus and BMP2-knockdown lentivirus, negated the positive effects of SIRT5 overexpression on these parameters, suggesting that BMP2 is a critical mediator of SIRT5’s actions on bone healing (Fig. 6A–D). The HE staining provides a detailed view of the bone tissue structure and the extent of healing. Briefly, the model group showed some proliferation, and after overexpression of SIRT5, the proliferation partially decreased, while knocking down BMP2 significantly reversed the partial effect of overexpression of SIRT5 (Fig. 6E). To assess whether SIRT5-mediated fracture healing was secondary to systemic changes, we measured serum IL-6, TNF-α and IGF-1 at 4 weeks. Compared with the model group, SIRT5 overexpression reduced IL-6 and TNF-α levels and elevated IGF-1 levels; these effects were partially reversed when BMP2 was simultaneously silenced (Fig. 6F). Overexpression of SIRT5 upregulated the expression of BMP2, OPN, RUNX2, and Osx. However, co-knockdown of BMP2 with SIRT5 overexpression led to a downregulation of these proteins, further supporting the role of BMP2 in mediating the effects of SIRT5 on bone healing (Fig. 6G and H).

Discussion

Under normal circumstances, the process of fracture healing is divided into three main stages: hematoma stage, callus formation stage (including cartilage and callus stage), and remodeling stage [1, 16]. In the stage of callus formation, stem cells around the fracture line gradually differentiate into chondrocytes, forming cartilage callus. BMSCs are cells isolated from the bone marrow, with low immunogenicity and multipotent differentiation potential [17]. Experimental studies have shown that BMSCs can differentiate into various human tissue cells in vitro to repair corresponding tissues, and are also the preferred seed cells for bone tissue engineering [18]. During the process of bone defect repair, the migration of BMSCs to the bone defect site and their differentiation into osteoblasts promote bone formation, which is of great significance for bone repair [19–21]. Promoting the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs is of great significance in fracture healing.

BMP2 plays an extremely important role in the recovery of tibial fractures [22, 23]. As a powerful signaling molecule for inducing osteogenic differentiation, BMP2 can promote the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells towards osteoblasts, accelerating and enhancing the process of fracture healing [24, 25]. Firstly, BMP2 affects cell proliferation, migration, differentiation, and extracellular matrix synthesis by activating specific signaling pathways such as Smad dependent pathways and other non classical signaling pathways [26, 27]. These effects collectively promote the formation of new bone tissue, which is crucial for the repair of fracture sites. Therefore, understanding and utilizing the function and regulatory mechanism of BMP2 is of great significance for improving fracture healing outcomes, shortening rehabilitation time, and preventing complications. The current study provides compelling evidence that the sirtuin family member, SIRT5, plays a pivotal role in the regulation of osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs, particularly through its influence on BMP2 signaling. Our findings indicate that overexpression of SIRT5 leads to a decrease in the succinylation levels of BMP2, a post-translational modification that has been previously implicated in the regulation of protein function and stability. By inhibiting succinylation, SIRT5 may enhance the activity of BMP2, a key osteoinductive growth factor that is essential for the proliferation and differentiation of BMSCs into osteoblasts. Although SIRT5 was originally annotated as a mitochondrial sirtuin, accumulating proteomic and cell-biological evidence indicates that a fraction of the protein resides in the cytosol and nucleus under physiological conditions. Chen et al. first demonstrated SIRT5 immunoreactivity in mitochondria, cytosol and nuclei of cells [13], while large-scale mass-spectrometry surveys consistently recovered SIRT5 peptides in cytosolic fractions prepared from mouse liver and human cell lines [28]. These data support the concept that SIRT5 can escape the mitochondrial import machinery or undergo retrograde translocation, thereby gaining access to non-mitochondrial substrates. Thus, despite the fact that BMP2 is not a mitochondria-associated protein, its succinylation status—and consequently its stability and bioactivity—can still be shaped by the desuccinylase activity of SIRT5 localized in the cytosolic compartment.

The promotion of osteogenic differentiation by SIRT5 overexpression is further supported by the increased expression of BMP2 target genes, such as OPN, RUNX2, and OSX, which are critical for bone formation. These genes are known to be involved in the maturation of osteoblasts and the deposition of extracellular matrix, processes that are fundamental to the healing of fractures. Our results suggest that the SIRT5-mediated increase in BMP2 signaling could be a therapeutic strategy to enhance bone repair, particularly in conditions where the natural healing process is compromised.

Moreover, the observation that SIRT5 overexpression enhances fracture healing is consistent with previous studies that have highlighted the importance of sirtuins in cellular stress resistance and metabolic homeostasis [29, 30]. SIRT5, in particular, has been shown to modulate cellular metabolism and mitochondrial function, which are crucial for the energy demands of bone formation and remodeling [12]. The interplay between SIRT5 and BMP2 may represent a novel axis through which metabolic and anabolic pathways converge to regulate bone homeostasis. Yin et al. recently reported that SIRT5-driven desuccinylation of UQCRC2 disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis and blunts osteogenesis in senescent BMSCs [31], seemingly conflicting with our pro-osteogenic findings. This discrepancy is readily explained by the divergent cellular contexts: Yin et al. employed aged rat BMSCs (24–28 months), whereas our study and the independent work of Liu et al. [32] used young cells (10-week rat BMSCs, human donor BMSCs, or mouse embryonic fibroblasts). Liu et al. further demonstrated that SIRT5 potentiates BMP9-induced bone regeneration in young MEFs by de-acetylating and de-malonlylating HIF-1α, thereby stabilizing the transcription factor. A similar age-dependent switch has been documented for SIRT1, which promotes osteogenesis in young but not senescent BMSCs [33]. Collectively, these data establish that SIRT5 exerts context-dependent dualism: pro-osteogenic in young cells (targeting BMP2 or HIF-1α) and anti-osteogenic in aged cells (targeting UQCRC2). Future studies must systematically dissect how age- and metabolism-associated cues dictate SIRT5 substrate selectivity, and leverage this knowledge to design staged or controllable SIRT5-modulatory strategies for precision bone regeneration.

Moreover, the precise molecular mechanisms by which SIRT5 regulates BMP2 succinylation and the subsequent impact on osteogenic differentiation warrant further investigation. It is possible that SIRT5 interacts with other regulatory proteins or enzymes that directly influence the succinylation status of BMP2. Identifying these interacting partners could provide additional targets for modulating bone formation and could lead to the development of new therapeutic approaches for the treatment of bone-related diseases.

In conclusion, our study uncovers a novel role for SIRT5 in the regulation of BMP2 signaling and osteogenic differentiation, with implications for the management of fracture healing. The findings suggest that SIRT5 could be a potential therapeutic target for enhancing bone repair and that modulating its activity may offer a new strategy for the treatment of bone fractures and other skeletal disorders. Future research should focus on elucidating the underlying mechanisms and exploring the therapeutic potential of SIRT5 in preclinical and clinical settings.

Although our data favour a BMP2-centred mechanism, we cannot exclude direct desuccinylation of RUNX2, OSX or OPN by SIRT5. IP-MS-based succinylome profiling coupled with site-specific mutagenesis will therefore be used in future work to identify the full substrate spectrum of SIRT5 in BMSCs; confirmation of transcription-factor targeting would add an extra regulatory layer to the SIRT5-osteogenesis network and help explain the genome-wide amplification of osteogenic signals observed upon SIRT5 overexpression.
