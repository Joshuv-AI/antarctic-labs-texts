Complex dynamic transformations and strange attractors in a tri-trophic predator-prey system
Ju Kang, Yiyuan Niu Xin Wang

Abstract
Understanding predator-prey interactions is a fundamental issue in ecology, and the complex dynamics they induce are highly significant for maintaining community stability and self-organising biodiversity. Here, we investigate complex dynamical behaviors and bifurcation structures in a tri-trophic food web comprising a basal prey, an intermediate predator, and an omnivorous top predator. By combining Jacobian analysis with Hopf bifurcation theory, we derive explicit stability conditions and identify thresholds for oscillatory onset at the coexistence equilibrium. Further, we employ Shilnikov’s theorem to establish criteria for the emergence of homoclinic orbit. Numerical simulations uncover a rich repertoire of dynamics, including stable limit cycles, Shilnikov homoclinic attractors, strange attractors, period-doubling cascades (with period-2 and 3 windows), chaotic bursts, and crisis-induced intermittency. These regimes are highly sensitive to the omnivore’s foraging strategy: minor parameter shifts can destabilize oscillations or precipitate full system collapse. Our results highlight how omnivory and interaction structure critically modulate ecosystem complexity and resilience, offering new insights into the mechanisms that govern stability in multitrophic systems. Keywords: Hopf bifurcation, Shilnikov homoclinic attractors, period-doubling bifurcation, strange attractors, chaos

1. Introduction

Predator-prey interactions lie at the heart of population ecology, offering a powerful lens through which to understand
the dynamic balance of natural systems. The pioneering work of Lotka
[
1
,
2
]
and Volterra
[
3
,
4
]
introduced mathematical formalism to this field, yielding the now-classic Lotka-Volterra model, one of the most enduring frameworks in theoretical ecology.
Holling’s influential contribution
[
5
]
extended this paradigm by incorporating functional responses,
capturing key aspects of predator behavior often absent in earlier models. Since then, a proliferation of increasingly sophisticated models has sought to capture the rich, often nonlinear dynamics of predator-prey systems, revealing layers of complexity previously unaccounted for
[
6
]
.

While traditional research on interspecies interactions has largely focused on two-species predator-prey models,
often yielding stable equilibria or limit cycles, recent efforts have shifted toward tri-trophic systems, where far richer and less predictable dynamics emerge. In particular, extensions involving ratio-dependent functional responses, Allee effects, and fear-induced costs have illustrated how behavioral and demographic factors jointly shape prey reproduction and mortality
[
7
,
8
]
.
Three-species models, whether structured as two prey with one predator or one prey with two predators, have been shown to produce a striking variety of population-level behaviors: periodic oscillations, multi-stable attractors, and even deterministic chaos
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
.
These emergent dynamics often include structured periodicity and coexisting long-term states, hinting at deeper underlying nonlinear mechanisms. Yet, despite this progress, key questions remain: under what conditions can such systems give rise to complex, self-organized phenomena, such as Shilnikov homoclinic or strange attractors in well-mixed environments with omnivorous interactions? Addressing this challenge is crucial for understanding the full range of dynamical possibilities in tri-trophic ecological networks, and it forms the central aim of this study.

In this work, we study a tri-trophic predator-prey model incorporating omnivory into the classical Lotka-Volterra framework,
with a Holling-II functional response
[
2
,
4
]
.
By allowing the top predator to consume both species below it, we reveal a rich tapestry of dynamical behaviors, including Shilnikov homoclinic structures, strange attractors, and chaotic transients. Our findings demonstrate that even subtle changes in the omnivore’s foraging strategy can destabilize the system, triggering extinction cascades or chaotic shifts. Moreover, we show that stochastic fluctuations, arising from environmental variability or intrinsic noise, can further undermine long-term coexistence.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2.1
introduces the model and its assumptions. Sections
2.2
-
2.5
investigate the emergence of complex dynamics: multi-periodic oscillations, homoclinic bifurcations,
chaotic windows, intermittent regimes, and crisis-induced transitions.
In Section
3
, we synthesize the implications of our results and outline directions for future research.

2. Results

2.1. Tri-trophic predator-prey system

We analyze a tri-trophic predator-prey system comprising a basal prey, an intermediate predator, and a top omnivorous predator.
Building on the Hastings-Powell model
[
13
]
, we incorporate a Holling-II functional response to capture
nonlinear predation dynamics. This yields the following system of differential equations describing population interactions:

\begin{cases}\dfrac{d\tilde{R}}{d\tau}=\tilde{R}_{0}{\tilde{R}}(1-{\tilde{R}}/K_{0})-f_{1}({\tilde{R}}){\tilde{C}}_{1}-f_{2}({\tilde{R}}){\tilde{C}}_{2},\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{d\tilde{C}_{1}}{d\tau}=w_{1}f_{1}({\tilde{R}}){\tilde{C}}_{1}-f_{3}({\tilde{C}}_{1}){\tilde{C}}_{2}-D_{1}{\tilde{C}}_{1},\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{d\tilde{C}_{2}}{d\tau}=w_{2}f_{2}({\tilde{R}}){\tilde{C}}_{2}+w_{3}f_{3}({\tilde{C}}_{1}){\tilde{C}}_{2}-D_{2}{\tilde{C}}_{2}.\\[6.0pt]
\end{cases}

(1)

the term
f_{i}(X)
denotes a Holling-II functional response:
f_{i}(X)=A_{i}X/(B_{i}X+1),~i=1,2,3
,
where
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
represent the capture rate and saturation rate, respectively.
The variables
\tilde{R},{\tilde{C}}_{1}
and
{\tilde{C}}_{2}
correspond to the population densities of the prey, the intermediate predator, and the top omnivore.
Each consumer
{\tilde{C}}_{i}
experiences a per capita mortality rate
D_{i}
,
and converts consumed biomass into predator biomass with efficiency
w_{i}
.
In the absence of predation, the prey population grows logistically,
characterized by an intrinsic growth rate
R_{0}
and a carrying capacity
K_{0}
.
To reduce parameter complexity and facilitate analysis, we nondimensionalize the system via the substitutions

R={\tilde{R}}/K_{0},C_{1}=A_{1}{\tilde{C}}_{1},C_{2}=A_{2}{\tilde{C}}_{2},t=\tau
,
yielding the following dimensionless formulation of Eq. (
1
):

\begin{cases}\dfrac{dR}{dt}=r{R}(1-{R})-\dfrac{R{C}_{1}}{\alpha_{1}R+1}-\dfrac{R{C}_{2}}{\alpha_{2}R+1}=g_{1}(R,C_{1},C_{2}),\\[8.0pt]
\dfrac{dC_{1}}{dt}=\dfrac{\alpha_{3}R{C}_{1}}{\alpha_{1}R+1}-\dfrac{\alpha_{4}{C}_{1}C_{2}}{\alpha_{5}{C}_{1}+1}-\alpha_{6}{C}_{1}=g_{2}(R,C_{1},C_{2}),\\[8.0pt]
\dfrac{dC_{2}}{dt}=\dfrac{\alpha_{7}R{C}_{2}}{\alpha_{2}R+1}+\dfrac{\alpha_{8}{C}_{1}C_{2}}{\alpha_{5}{C}_{1}+1}-\alpha_{9}{C}_{2}=g_{3}(R,C_{1},C_{2}).\\[8.0pt]
\end{cases}

(2)

The dimensionless parameters introduced in the rescaled system are defined as:

r=\tilde{R}_{0},\alpha_{i}=B_{i}K_{0}~(i=1,2);\alpha_{3}=w_{1}A_{1}K_{0},\alpha_{4}=A_{3}/A_{2},\alpha_{5}=B_{3}/A_{1},\alpha_{6}=D_{1},\alpha_{7}=w_{2}A_{2}K_{0},\alpha_{8}=w_{3}A_{3}/A_{1},\alpha_{9}=D_{2}
.
Together, the parameters
r
and
\alpha_{i}~(i=1,...,9)
characterize the local dynamics of the nondimensional system (
2
).

From a biological standpoint, every solution of system (
2
) with strictly positive initial conditions remains non-negative
for all time, ensuring ecological feasibility of the model.
The system admits five biologically meaningful equilibrium points:

E_{0}=(0,0,0)
,
E_{1}=(1,0,0)
,

E_{2}=\left(\frac{\alpha_{9}}{\alpha_{7}-\alpha_{2}\alpha_{9}},0,\frac{\alpha_{7}(\alpha_{7}-(1+\alpha_{2})\alpha_{9})r}{(\alpha_{7}-\alpha_{2}\alpha_{9})^{2}}\right)

E_{3}=\left(\frac{\alpha_{6}}{\alpha_{3}-\alpha_{1}\alpha_{6}},\frac{\alpha_{3}(\alpha_{3}-(1+\alpha_{1})\alpha_{6})r}{(\alpha_{3}-\alpha_{1}\alpha_{6})^{2}},0\right)

and
E^{*}=(R^{*},C_{1}^{*},C_{2}^{*})
(
E^{*}
see Eq. (
S1
)). Our analysis focuses on the interior equilibrium
E^{*}
, which represents the simultaneous persistence of the prey, intermediate predator,
and top omnivore. Local asymptotic stability of

is guaranteed if the Routh-Hurwitz conditions

2.4. Period-doubling bifurcation leads to chaos

Beyond the Hopf bifurcation, the predator-prey system (
2

as the bifurcation parameter

This cascade characterized by successive doublings of the orbit period leads to the emergence of increasingly complex periodic dynamics
and ultimately to deterministic chaos

This route to chaos exemplifies a well-known nonlinear dynamic transition and highlights the intricate bifurcation structure
inherent in tri-trophic ecological systems.

To numerically investigate the period-doubling bifurcation, we treat the prey growth rate

Simulation results, presented in Fig.

2.5. Strange attractors

The concept of a strange attractor was first introduced through the Lorenz system,
where it served as a hallmark of nonlinear dynamics, illustrating how deterministic systems can evolve into complex,
aperiodic, and highly sensitive trajectories.
In our analysis, bifurcation structures and the emergence of a Shilnikov homoclinic attractor indicate that the predator-prey system (
2
) is also capable of generating rich chaotic dynamics.
These findings suggest that ecological systems, even with relatively simple interactions,
can support attractor structures as intricate as those found in classical models of chaos.

The predator-prey system (
2
) further exhibits self-organization into a strange attractor,
enabling long-term species coexistence within a chaotic regime
(see Figs.
4
,
5
,
S6
,
S7
).
To illustrate system’s sensitivity to initial conditions, a hallmark of deterministic chaos,
we conducted a perturbation analysis by initializing the system with two nearly identical states.
Specifically, simulations were started with
R_{0}=0.5
,
C_{10}=0.5
,
C_{20}=0.5
and
R_{0}=0.5
,
C_{10}=0.5001
,
C_{20}=0.5
.
As shown in Fig.
4
b, even this minute difference (
\Delta C_{10}=0.0001
)
leads to pronounced divergence in species trajectories (
\Delta R
,
\Delta C_{1}
,
\Delta C_{2}
),
providing compelling numerical evidence of sensitive dependence on initial conditions.
Additionally, the system’s aperiodic nature is evident in the irregular time series (see Figs.
4
a, c,
S6
)
and is further represented in the Poincaré map (see Fig.
4
d),
which reveals a non-repeating, scattered point cloud consistent with the geometric structure of a strange attractor.
Collectively, these results confirm the presence of deterministic chaos in system (
2
) and demonstrate that
complex population dynamics can emerge even in structured, rule-based ecological models.

3. Discussion

Predator-prey interactions form a cornerstone of ecological theory, yet the mechanisms enabling complex,
self-organized population dynamics in well-mixed biotic environments remain poorly understood.
In this study, we explore a tri-trophic predator-prey system and uncover a rich tapestry of nonlinear dynamical behaviors.
Through bifurcation analysis, we identify key transitions marked by Hopf bifurcations, period-doubling cascades,
Shilnikov homoclinic orbits, and strange attractors.
These phenomena give rise to diverse population outcomes, including stable fixed points, limit cycles, quasi-periodic regimes,
homoclinic excursions, chaotic bursts, and fully developed deterministic chaos.
As bifurcation parameters are varied, the system traverses a hierarchy of dynamical regimes shifting between equilibrium,
periodicity, multi-periodicity, and chaos highlighting the intricate architecture underpinning ecological self-organization.

The complex dynamics observed in the tri-trophic predator-prey system (
2
) emerge from intricate interactions
among all three trophic levels.
When the omnivorous predator ceases to consume the intermediate predator, potentially due to environmental shifts,
the system effectively reduces to a two-predator, one-prey framework.
This structural simplification profoundly alters the system’s dynamical landscape.
To elucidate the functional role of each species, we first examine a regime characterized by strange attractors
(see Figs.
4
and
5
), then consider a modified scenario where the omnivore no longer preys on the middle predator.
As shown in Fig.
S8
a-b, this alteration triggers the extinction of the omnivore
C_{2}
,
and the system rapidly collapses due to the failure of the remaining species to coexist.
This outcome is consistent with the competitive exclusion principle, which posits that no two predators can stably coexist
when competing for a single prey resource
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
.
More generally, sustainable coexistence in ecological communities typically requires that
predator diversity does not exceed prey diversity
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
.
These findings underscore the critical stabilizing role of omnivory in maintaining complex population dynamics.
Even in systems capable of supporting chaotic and multi-scale behavior, a shift in trophic interaction,
such as the loss of a feeding link can precipitate systemic collapse, highlighting the delicate balance underlying ecological resilience.

Stochastic fluctuations pose a significant threat to species coexistence, particularly in ecological systems
governed by deterministic oscillations
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
.
In our tri-trophic predator-prey model (
2
), complex deterministic dynamics,
such as multi-periodic or chaotic attractors enable coexistence under idealized conditions.
However, as illustrated in Fig.
S8
c-d, even modest stochastic perturbations can destabilize these regimes,
leading to extinction cascades and structural collapse.
This tension reveals a central paradox: the dynamical richness that enables coexistence also amplifies sensitivity to noise.
Explaining how ecological systems evolve robustness to such perturbations remains an open and pressing challenge.
In particular, understanding why oscillatory coexistence is so fragile to noise,
a phenomenon observed across both theoretical models and empirical systems,
may hold the key to bridging dynamical complexity and ecological stability.

can be decomposed into the following form,

(\lambda^{2}+\varpi^{2})(\lambda+a_{3})=0

(S6)

Thus,

\lambda_{1,2,3}=\pm\sqrt{a_{0}}i,-a_{2},

(S7)

if
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
are positive and
a_{0}=a_{1}a_{2}
at bifurcation point
r=r_{0}
.

The general form of the roots of the eigenequation (
S3
) are

\lambda_{1,2}=\mu(r)\pm i\delta(r),\lambda_{3}=-a_{2}(r)

(S8)

Now, to determine the transformation conditions, we need to substitute
\lambda_{1}=\mu(r)+i\delta(r)
into (
S6
), we have

(\mu^{2}-\delta^{2}+2i\mu\delta+a_{1})(\mu+i\delta+a_{2})=0

(S9)

Differentiating
\mu
and
\delta
with respect to
r
, and separating the real
and imaginary parts, we obtain

\beta_{1}(r)\dot{\mu}(r)-\beta_{2}(r)\dot{\delta}(r)+\beta_{3}(r)=0,\\
\beta_{2}(r)\dot{\mu}(r)-\beta_{1}(r)\dot{\delta}(r)+\beta_{4}(r)=0

(S10)

where
\beta_{1}=3\mu^{2}-3\delta^{2}+a_{1}+2a_{2}\mu
,
\beta_{2}=6\mu\delta+2\delta a_{2}
,
\beta_{3}=\dot{a}_{2}\mu^{2}-\dot{a}_{2}\delta^{2}+\dot{a}_{1}\mu+\dot{a}_{0}
,
\beta_{4}=\dot{a}_{1}\delta+2\dot{a}_{2}\delta\mu
.
Hence, putting
r=r_{0}
, we have

\dfrac{d\mu}{dr}\arrowvert_{r=r_{0}}=\dfrac{\dot{a}_{2}a_{1}-\dot{a}_{0}+{a}_{2}\dot{a}_{1}}{2a_{1}+2{a}_{2}^{2}}\neq 0

(S11)

if
\dot{a}_{2}a_{1}-\dot{a}_{0}+{a}_{2}\dot{a}_{1}\neq 0
, then, the predator-prey system (
2
) undergoes Hopf bifurcation around the coexistent equilibrium
E^{*}
, when the parameter
r
crosses the critical value
r=r_{0}
.

III. Homoclinic orbit analysis

In general, the eigenenquation (see Eq. (
S3
)) of the Jacobian
J(E^{*})
at
E^{*}
can be reduced to the following

\Lambda^{3}+p\Lambda+q=0.

(S12)

where

\lambda=\Lambda-a_{2}/3,~p=a_{1}-a_{2}^{2}/3,~q=a_{0}-a_{1}a_{2}/3+2a_{2}^{3}/27.

(S13)

Denote

\Delta=\left(\frac{q}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{p}{3}\right)^{3}.

(S14)

When
\Delta>0
, then Eq. (
S12
) has a real root
\Lambda_{3}
and a pair of complex roots
\Lambda_{1,2}=\mu\pm i\delta
with

\begin{cases}\Lambda_{3}=\sqrt[3]{-\frac{q}{2}+\sqrt{\Delta}}+\sqrt[3]{-\frac{q}{2}-\sqrt{\Delta}},\\[8.0pt]
\mu=-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt[3]{-\dfrac{q}{2}+\sqrt{\Delta}}+\sqrt[3]{-\frac{q}{2}-\sqrt{\Delta}}\right),\\[8.0pt]
\delta=-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt[3]{-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}+\sqrt{\Delta}}-\sqrt[3]{-\frac{q}{2}-\sqrt{\Delta}}\right).\end{cases}

(S15)

It follows that the roots of Eq. (
S3
) are

\lambda_{1}=\mu+i\delta-\frac{1}{3},~\lambda_{2}=\mu-i\delta-\frac{1}{3},~\lambda_{3}=\Lambda-\frac{1}{3}.

(S16)

Since
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}=-a_{0}<0
. To ensure that the coexistent equilibrium
E^{*}
is a saddle focus with
\text{Re}(\lambda_{1})>0
, the following condition should be satisfied:

\left(\sqrt[3]{-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}+\sqrt{\Delta}}+\sqrt[3]{-\frac{q}{2}-\sqrt{\Delta}}\right)<-\dfrac{2}{3}

(S17)

If
\Delta>0
and Eq. (
S17
) are satisfied, in which
\Delta
is given by Eqs.
S13
and
S14
, then predator-prey system (
2
) has a homoclinic orbit
[
17
]
.
