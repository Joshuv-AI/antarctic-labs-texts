Diagnosing morphology-vitality relationships through multifractal urban form metrics and explainable machine learning
Authors: Tingting Wen, Junjie Zhang, Zhen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding how urban morphology is associated with urban vitality is important for sustainable urban regeneration, yet conventional density-based indicators often fail to capture cross-scale heterogeneity in three-dimensional urban form. This study proposes a morphology-vitality diagnostic framework that integrates elevation-weighted multifractal analysis with explainable machine learning. Taking the main urban area of Jinan, China, as a case study, we derived multifractal metrics from building footprint and height data and constructed a composite vitality index using population, point-of-interest, and road-network indicators. XGBoost models and SHAP interpretation were used to examine the nonlinear association between morphological descriptors and urban vitality, while additional robustness analyses were conducted for the vitality index, feature sets, multifractal parameters, and threshold detection. The results show that the morphological heterogeneity index Δα is a key explanatory variable for vitality variation and remains highly ranked across alternative model settings. Baseline comparisons indicate that multifractal descriptors provide additional predictive information beyond conventional morphological indicators. A partial-dependence-based segmented regression further identifies a stable sensitive interval around Δα ≈ 1.82, with bootstrap and spatial-block robustness tests supporting the consistency of this threshold. Rather than implying direct causality, the proposed mismatch indicator is interpreted as a diagnostic measure of deviation between morphology-based predicted vitality and observed vitality. The framework contributes to SDG-oriented urban regeneration by providing a quantitative tool for identifying areas where morphological structure and functional performance are unevenly aligned.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55314-1.Keywords: Multifractal analysis, Explainable machine learning, Urban form, Sustainable urban regeneration, Urban vitalitySubject terms: Environmental social sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

As urbanization increasingly shifts from outward expansion to the regeneration and optimization of existing built environments, understanding the relationship between urban form and urban vitality has become an important issue for sustainable urban development1. This transition is closely related to the objectives of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 11, which emphasizes inclusive, safe, resilient and sustainable cities; SDG 9, which highlights resilient infrastructure and innovation; and SDG 13, which calls for enhanced urban resilience under climate-related risks2,3. In mature urban areas, functional vitality is shaped by socio-economic activities, service provision, mobility patterns, and the spatial context in which these processes occur4. However, physical urban form usually evolves more slowly than population movement, land-use restructuring, and functional demand, which may result in uneven alignment between morphological structure and observed vitality5,6. Therefore, clarifying how urban morphology is associated with functional vitality can provide useful diagnostic information for regeneration prioritization and spatial governance7,8. Existing studies have shown that urban vitality is closely related to population density, land-use mix, accessibility, and other built-environment attributes9–13. Nevertheless, how cross-scale morphological heterogeneity can be quantitatively represented and how such representations contribute to the predictive diagnosis of vitality variation remain insufficiently examined14,15.

Current quantitative research on urban vitality has increasingly relied on multi-source urban data to characterize the spatial distribution and formation mechanisms of functional activity16–19. Indicators such as population density, point-of-interest density and diversity, land-use mix, transportation accessibility, road-network configuration, nighttime light, and street-view perception have been widely used to represent different dimensions of vitality20–23. These studies have substantially improved the measurement of urban functional performance and have shown that vitality is jointly associated with demographic concentration, service supply, mobility conditions, and environmental quality24,25. However, in many existing frameworks, urban form is still represented mainly by aggregate or planar indicators, or is introduced as a contextual variable rather than as an object of structural diagnosis26. This limits the ability to explain why urban areas with similar density, land-use composition, or accessibility may exhibit different levels of vitality27. In particular, high-density built environments often contain complex combinations of building height, volume distribution, spatial clustering, and local fragmentation, which cannot be fully captured by conventional two-dimensional indicators28,29. Therefore, a more refined representation of three-dimensional morphological heterogeneity is needed to examine how the structural organization of urban form contributes to the spatial differentiation of vitality30,31.

From a morphological perspective, traditional urban form studies have commonly relied on two-dimensional aggregate indicators, such as building density and floor area ratio, to describe the physical structure of urban areas32,33. These indicators are intuitive and easy to compare across spatial units, but they tend to compress complex three-dimensional configurations into single-scale summary statistics34, and as a result, they may overlook the internal organization of building volumes, the coexistence of compact and sparse patterns, and the nested variations of height and density across scales35. This limitation can lead to a “density illusion”: urban units with similar average density or floor area ratio may differ substantially in their vertical structure, local clustering, spatial fragmentation, and functional context36–38. For high-density built environments, such differences are particularly important because vitality is not only associated with the amount of built space, but also with how built volumes are distributed and organized39. Therefore, moving beyond planar and average-based indicators is necessary for constructing a more sensitive representation of urban morphology and for examining its association with vitality variation.

Multifractal theory provides a suitable analytical framework for describing complex spatial systems and multi-scale structures40. Unlike traditional fractals, which primarily describe overall spatial filling characteristics41, multifractal analysis uses a spectrum of scaling exponents to capture the heterogeneous distribution of spatial measures across different density levels42. This makes it particularly useful for representing urban fabrics in which compact building clusters, sparse low-density areas, and vertically differentiated structures coexist43. In urban studies, multifractal methods have been applied to characterize urban form, land-price distribution, population agglomeration, and other spatial phenomena, demonstrating their capacity to reveal cross-scale heterogeneity and spatial polarization44–47. However, most existing applications remain focused on the statistical description of urban morphology itself. Less attention has been paid to whether multifractal descriptors provide additional explanatory information for functional urban outcomes, such as vitality, when compared with conventional morphological indicators48,49. Furthermore, the physical interpretation of multifractal metrics remains relatively abstract, which limits their direct use in planning-oriented diagnosis. Integrating multifractal indicators with explainable machine learning may help bridge this gap by linking cross-scale morphological descriptors with observable vitality variation and by identifying which morphological features contribute most strongly to model predictions50–53.

To address these gaps, this study develops a diagnostic framework for examining the association between three-dimensional urban morphology and urban vitality. Taking the main urban area of Jinan, China, as a case study, we construct an elevation-weighted multifractal indicator system based on building footprint and height data, and integrate it with multi-source vitality indicators and explainable machine learning. The objectives of this study are threefold. First, we assess whether multifractal descriptors can capture cross-scale morphological heterogeneity that is not fully represented by conventional density-based indicators. Second, we examine the nonlinear association between morphological heterogeneity and urban vitality, with particular attention to the threshold-like response of the key metric Δα. Third, we develop a morphology-vitality mismatch indicator as a diagnostic measure of the deviation between morphology-based predicted vitality and observed vitality, rather than as direct evidence of causal constraint or functional overload. The contribution of this study lies in three aspects: methodologically, it combines elevation-weighted multifractal analysis, baseline model comparison, spatial-block validation, and SHAP interpretation to improve the transparency of morphology-vitality modelling; theoretically, it shifts the interpretation of urban form from average density to cross-scale structural heterogeneity; and practically, it provides a quantitative diagnostic tool that may support SDG-oriented urban regeneration by identifying areas where morphological structure and functional performance are unevenly aligned.

Data and methodology

Study area and research framework

Study area

This study focuses on the central urban area of Jinan City in Shandong Province (Fig. 1), China, encompassing five central administrative districts: Lixia, Shizhong, Huaiyin, Tianqiao, and Licheng. The selection of this area is based on Jinan’s spatial development strategy of "Central Dispersal, Eastern Expansion, Western Advancement, Southern Control, and Northern Crossing" as well as its profound historical and geographical context. The urban form here exhibits typically heterogeneous characteristics, where historic neighborhoods and modern high-density building clusters are interwoven. At the same time, traditional courtyard layouts and the natural spring water network are interwoven. These multi-level and heterogeneous spatial elements collectively constitute a complex system with significant self-organizing features, providing a suitable empirical case for employing multifractal methods to characterize the structure of the urban fabric.

Research framework

This study develops a morphology-vitality diagnostic framework that integrates elevation-weighted multifractal analysis, multi-source vitality measurement, machine-learning prediction, and explainable interpretation. The overall workflow is illustrated in Fig. 2, and consists of five main steps. First, multi-source spatial data, including building footprints and heights, population data, point-of-interest data, and road-network data, were collected, cleaned, and aligned to a unified spatial analysis framework. Local window units were then constructed to ensure consistent spatial correspondence between morphological indicators and vitality-related variables. Second, an elevation-weighted multifractal analysis was conducted using three-dimensional building morphology data. Building height was used as a proxy for vertical mass, and multifractal metrics were extracted to describe planar filling, spatial aggregation, and cross-scale morphological heterogeneity. These metrics included generalized dimensions, singularity-spectrum parameters, and the spectrum-width indicator Δα. Third, a composite vitality index was constructed using indicators related to population demand, functional intensity, and accessibility. To address the uncertainty associated with equal weighting, alternative weighting strategies and leave-one-indicator-out tests were further used to examine the robustness of the vitality index. Fourth, machine learning models were used to estimate the association between morphological features and the composite vitality index. Spatial-block cross-validation was adopted to reduce the potential influence of spatial autocorrelation on model evaluation. Baseline comparisons among conventional morphological indicators, multifractal indicators, and combined feature sets were conducted to assess the incremental value of multifractal descriptors. SHAP analysis was then used to interpret the relative contribution and response pattern of each morphological feature. Finally, threshold detection and mismatch diagnosis were performed. A partial-dependence-based segmented regression was used to identify the sensitive interval of Δα, and bootstrap and spatial-block robustness tests were conducted to examine threshold stability. The mismatch indicator was defined as the deviation between morphology-based predicted vitality and observed vitality. It was interpreted as a diagnostic indicator of uneven alignment between morphological structure and functional performance, rather than as direct evidence of causal constraint or functional overload. Residual analysis was further used to discuss the remaining unexplained variation and the possible role of omitted socio-economic, behavioral, and micro-scale environmental factors.

Data and vitality index construction

Data sources and vitality indicator system

Urban vitality is a multidimensional concept that reflects the intensity and diversity of human activities, service functions, and spatial interactions within the built environment54. Because direct human-activity data such as mobile-phone signaling, consumption records, and fine-grained street-activity observations are not always publicly available and may raise issues of privacy, temporal consistency, and spatial comparability, this study constructed an operational vitality proxy using reproducible multi-source urban data (Table 1). Specifically, the composite vitality index was derived from three dimensions: population demand, functional intensity, and spatial accessibility55–57.

Population demand was represented by population density, which reflects the potential demand for urban services and daily activities. Functional intensity was measured using POI density and POI Shannon entropy, representing the concentration and diversity of urban functions, respectively. Spatial accessibility was described using opportunity accessibility, road density, and intersection density, reflecting the ability of a unit to connect with surrounding opportunities and support movement-based interactions. Together, these six indicators provide a reproducible representation of urban vitality from the perspectives of demand, supply, and connectivity. All indicators were calculated within the same local spatial window units used for morphological analysis. Building morphology data, population data, POI data, and road-network data were cleaned, projected to a unified coordinate system, and spatially aligned. Empty units without valid building coverage were excluded to ensure that each sample contained both morphological information and vitality-related attributes. This spatial alignment ensured that the dependent variable, Svitality, and the morphological predictors were calculated for the same analytical units.

Urban morphology characterization using multifractal analysis

To overcome the limitations of conventional two-dimensional metrics, such as density and floor area ratio, in characterizing vertical-dimensional morphological heterogeneity32,36,58, this study establishes an elevation-weighted multifractal analysis procedure (Fig. 3). These included a narrower q range, a denser scale sequence, and alternative fitting intervals that excluded either small or large scales. The detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Across these configurations, the model performance remained generally stable, and Δα consistently ranked as the most important morphological feature in the SHAP analysis. Although the regression performance decreased moderately when small scales were excluded, Δα remained the top-ranked feature across all tested configurations. These results indicate that the main findings were not driven by a single arbitrary choice of multifractal parameters.

Mismatch indicator: construction and interpretation

Modelling and validation

This study employs the XGBoost regression algorithm to estimate the nonlinear association between morphological features and the composite vitality index (Svitality). All models were re-estimated under a unified modelling workflow, including consistent sample selection, feature-set construction, target-variable standardization, hyperparameter tuning, and spatial-block cross-validation.

To examine the incremental value of multifractal descriptors, four feature-set configurations were compared: conventional morphological indicators only, conventional indicators plus Δα, multifractal indicators only, and combined conventional and multifractal indicators. In addition, multiple machine-learning algorithms, including Ridge regression, Random Forest, Extra Trees, HistGradientBoosting, GradientBoosting, and XGBoost, were tested as supplementary robustness checks to assess whether the modelling results were sensitive to algorithm selection.

Subsequently, the SHAP method was applied to interpret the trained XGBoost model, quantifying the relative contribution and response pattern of each morphological feature to the prediction of Svitality. To further examine whether the importance of Δα was dependent on a specific algorithm, SHAP-based feature-importance analyses were also conducted for the alternative models.

To further examine the efficacy of morphological features in discriminating between “high-vitality” and “low-vitality” extreme states, a polarized binary classification task was designed as a supplementary analysis. Within each fold of the spatial-block cross-validation, samples were categorized based on the Svitality quantiles of the training set: the top and bottom 30% were assigned to the positive (high-vitality) and negative (low-vitality) classes, respectively, whereas the intermediate 40% were excluded to enhance class separability. Under this binary classification setup, the Area Under the Curve (AUC) and Average Precision (AP) were used as evaluation metrics. This task complements continuous regression evaluation by testing whether morphological features can distinguish extreme vitality states under spatially separated validation conditions.

When M > 0, the observed vitality of a unit is lower than the level predicted by its morphological features. When M < 0, the observed vitality is higher than the morphology-based prediction. These two situations are interpreted as relative deviations between morphological structure and functional performance, rather than as direct evidence of causal constraint, unrealized potential, or functional overload.

In this study, P is used as a diagnostic indicator for identifying spatially uneven alignment between morphological structure and functional performance.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Spatial distribution patterns

The composite vitality index Svitality exhibits a clear spatial differentiation pattern within the study area (Fig. 4a). High-vitality units are mainly concentrated in the continuously built-up central districts and functionally mixed areas, reflecting relatively strong population demand, functional intensity and accessibility. In contrast, low-vitality units are frequently distributed in peripheral urban areas and functionally homogeneous zones, indicating weaker composite vitality conditions. This distribution is generally consistent with the typical center–periphery structure of Jinan.

Further analysis shows that the morphology-vitality mismatch indicator M displays a spatially differentiated pattern, with relatively negative values more frequently observed in traditional core districts such as Lixia and Shizhong, and relatively positive values more frequently observed in peripheral and newly developed areas (Fig. 4b). According to the definition in Section "Definition of the mismatch indicator", negative M values indicate that observed vitality is higher than the morphology-based predicted level, whereas positive M values indicate that observed vitality is lower than the morphology-based predicted level. This suggests that the relationship between urban morphology and vitality is spatially structured, although part of the variation may also be related to socio-economic, behavioral, historical, and policy-related factors not included in the morphology-based model.

Model performance and feature contribution

Model performance and baseline comparison

Following the modelling workflow described in Section "Modelling and validation", all model results were re-estimated under a unified spatial-block cross-validation framework, with consistent sample selection, target-variable standardization, feature-set construction, hyperparameter tuning, and evaluation metrics. The updated baseline comparison results are summarized in Table 4. The XGBoost model using the combined feature set achieved stable predictive performance, with a regression R2 of 0.697 ± 0.090, an RMSE of 0.474 ± 0.078, a classification AUC of 0.979 ± 0.011, and an AP of 0.963 ± 0.040. The polarized classification results indicate that morphological features provide strong discriminative information for distinguishing high- and low-vitality states under spatially separated validation conditions. The regression results further suggest that morphological indicators contain substantial diagnostic information for explaining the spatial variation of Svitality. However, the model should not be interpreted as fully determining urban vitality, because a considerable proportion of variation may still be associated with socio-economic, behavioral, temporal, and micro-environmental factors not included in the current morphology-based framework.

To examine the incremental value of multifractal descriptors, four feature-set configurations were compared: conventional morphological indicators only, conventional indicators plus Δα, multifractal indicators only, and combined conventional and multifractal indicators. Additional algorithm comparisons were conducted to examine whether the modelling results were sensitive to algorithm selection. As shown in Fig. 6, Ridge regression showed relatively weaker predictive performance, whereas tree-based ensemble models, including Random Forest, HistGradientBoosting, GradientBoosting, Extra Trees, and XGBoost, produced more stable observed–predicted relationships. Among them, XGBoost achieved competitive regression performance and was retained as the main modelling framework because of its nonlinear fitting ability and compatibility with SHAP- and PDP-based interpretation. Detailed numerical results of the algorithm-level robustness comparison are provided in Supplementary Table S3. These results suggest that the identified morphology-vitality relationships were not dependent on a single model specification.

SHAP-based feature contribution

To examine whether the feature-importance pattern was dependent on a specific modelling algorithm, SHAP-based interpretation was conducted across multiple machine-learning models. As shown in Fig. 7, the figure presents the SHAP summary plots derived from different models and the corresponding feature-ranking comparison. The tested models included Ridge regression, Random Forest, Extra Trees, GradientBoosting, and XGBoost. To provide a numerical summary of this robustness analysis, the top three features and the rank of Δα for each model are reported in Supplementary Table S4. Across the models shown in Fig. 7f, Δα consistently ranked among the top two predictors, indicating that the importance of morphological heterogeneity was not dependent on a single model specification. Across the alternative models, Δα consistently remained among the most highly ranked predictors, indicating that its strong influence was not an artifact of a single model structure. Although the SHAP results of different models showed some variation in the ranking of secondary features, the overall importance pattern was broadly consistent. In particular, tree-based ensemble models produced more stable feature-importance structures than the linear Ridge baseline. Combined with the model-performance comparison reported in Section "Model performance and baseline comparison", XGBoost was selected as the main model for subsequent interpretation because of its competitive predictive performance, nonlinear fitting ability, and compatibility with SHAP- and PDP-based analysis. Therefore, the following interpretation mainly focuses on the SHAP decomposition results of the XGBoost model.

The SHAP decomposition results of XGBoost model are shown in Fig. 7e. Among all morphological features, Δα exhibits the highest mean absolute SHAP value, indicating that cross-scale morphological heterogeneity contributes most strongly to the prediction of Svitality. Planar filling remains an important morphological descriptor, but its predictive contribution is lower than that of Δα. This result indicates that vitality variation is not only associated with the amount of built space, but also with how building volumes are distributed across scales. Compared with D0, which mainly reflects the degree of planar coverage, Δα captures the spectrum width of building-volume distribution and therefore represents the coexistence of compact clusters, sparse components, and vertical-density fluctuations. Its high SHAP importance suggests that such cross-scale heterogeneity provides additional predictive information beyond conventional density-based indicators.

Nonlinear response of Δα and threshold robustness

The relationship between the morphological heterogeneity index Δα and the model-predicted vitality response was not strictly linear, but exhibited a threshold-like nonlinear pattern (Fig. 8). To avoid relying solely on visual interpretation, a partial-dependence-based segmented regression approach was used to identify the point at which the response slope changed most markedly. Specifically, the partial dependence profile of Δα was first derived from the trained XGBoost model, and a two-segment linear regression was then fitted to the profile by minimizing the residual sum of squares across candidate breakpoints.

Nonlinear response and robustness of the Δα threshold: (a) PDP curve and segmented regression; (b) Bootstrap distribution of threshold estimates; (c) Threshold robustness across spatial-block splits; (d) Slope comparison before and after the breakpoint.

The full-sample PDP-based segmented regression identified a breakpoint at Δα = 1.821, with a PDP fitting R2 of 0.921. The estimated slope before the breakpoint was close to zero, whereas the slope after the breakpoint increased to 0.376, indicating a marked change in the model-predicted vitality response. To examine the robustness of this threshold estimate, two additional tests were conducted. First, bootstrap refitting of the model and PDP analysis produced a mean threshold estimate of 1.834 and a median estimate of 1.836, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.778–1.884. Second, spatial-block split robustness analysis produced an overall mean threshold estimate of 1.838, with an interval of 1.740–1.884. The mean threshold estimates under the 4 × 4, 5 × 5, and 6 × 6 spatial-block settings were 1.817, 1.852, and 1.831, respectively. Detailed results of the bootstrap-based and spatial-block threshold robustness tests are provided in Supplementary Table S5. These results indicate that the nonlinear response was consistently located around Δα ≈ 1.82, although this value should be interpreted as a case-specific sensitive range rather than as a universal threshold.

When Δα is within the approximate range of 1.7–2.0, the partial dependence curve showed a relatively rapid increase in predicted Svitality, suggesting that variations in cross-scale morphological heterogeneity were more strongly associated with changes in the model-predicted vitality level within this interval. Beyond this sensitive range, the response tended to flatten, indicating that further increases in morphological heterogeneity may not correspond to proportional increases in predicted vitality. This pattern suggests a nonlinear and potentially saturating association between morphological heterogeneity and vitality prediction.

Although the threshold analysis identifies a sensitive interval in the morphology-based prediction model, Δα alone cannot fully explain the observed variation in urban vitality. Other morphological dimensions, as well as non-morphological factors such as functional composition, accessibility, demographic structure, historical context, and policy orientation, may also contribute to local differences. Therefore, the threshold analysis should be understood as identifying a sensitive interval in the morphology-based prediction model, rather than as direct evidence that modifying Δα would necessarily lead to a specific increase in urban vitality.

Discussion

Diagnostic interpretation of morphology-vitality mismatch

This study further examines the relationship between multifractal indicators and the model-based predicted vitality, and the standardized mismatch indicator P (Fig. 9). Rather than treating the mismatch indicator as direct evidence of causal constraint or functional overload, we interpret it as a diagnostic measure of the deviation between observed vitality and the vitality level predicted from morphological features. This interpretation is consistent with the cross-sectional and observational nature of the data.

Relationships between multifractal indicators and vitality mismatch.

As shown by the predicted vitality curves in Fig. 9, several multifractal indicators, including D0, Δα, and D1, exhibit positive associations with morphology-based predicted Svitality over certain value ranges. This suggests that planar filling, distributional uniformity, and cross-scale heterogeneity all contain useful information for predicting vitality variation. In particular, Δα shows a more pronounced nonlinear response, which is consistent with the SHAP results and the threshold analysis reported in Sections "Model performance and feature contribution" and "Nonlinear response of Δα and threshold robustness". The standardized mismatch indicator P further reveals that the relationship between morphology-based prediction and observed vitality is not spatially or structurally uniform. When P is greater than 0.5, observed vitality is lower than the morphology-based predicted level, indicating a relatively positive mismatch. Such areas may correspond to locations where morphological conditions appear favorable in the model, but observed functional activity remains comparatively weak. When P is lower than 0.5, observed vitality is higher than the morphology-based predicted level, indicating a relatively negative mismatch. Such areas may reflect locations where socio-economic activity, service concentration, accessibility advantages, historical accumulation, or policy factors contribute to higher observed vitality than expected from morphology alone.

Therefore, the mismatch pattern should not be interpreted as a direct measurement of unrealized potential or functional overload. Instead, it provides a way to identify where morphology-based predictions and observed functional performance diverge. These divergences are useful because they highlight areas where additional non-morphological factors may need to be examined. For example, positive mismatch areas may require further investigation of land-use activation, service provision, transport connection, and population demand, whereas negative mismatch areas may require attention to local congestion, spatial pressure, or possible dependence on non-morphological advantages.

Overall, the mismatch analysis shows that urban morphology provides a stable but partial diagnostic signal for understanding vitality variation. The segmented response of P along different morphological dimensions indicates that the morphology-vitality relationship is uneven and context-dependent. This supports the use of multifractal morphology as a supplementary tool for urban regeneration diagnosis, while also emphasizing that morphology-based modelling should be combined with socio-economic, behavioral, and policy-related evidence before drawing planning conclusions.

Morphological heterogeneity and possible explanatory pathways

The results show that Δα is the most influential morphological feature in the SHAP analysis and exhibits a stable nonlinear response in the threshold analysis. This indicates that cross-scale morphological heterogeneity contains important predictive information for explaining the spatial variation of Svitality. However, Δα should not be interpreted as a direct causal driver of vitality. Rather, it can be understood as a synthetic descriptor of how building volumes, heights, and densities are unevenly distributed across scales.

From a morphological perspective, a larger Δα reflects a wider singularity spectrum, indicating that compact high-density components, sparse low-density components, and vertically differentiated structures coexist within the same local window. This type of spatial configuration may be associated with urban vitality through several possible pathways. First, moderate morphological heterogeneity may support functional diversity by accommodating different building types, development intensities, and activity spaces. Second, variations in building height and density may correspond to differences in street interfaces, service concentration, and pedestrian-scale spatial experience. Third, cross-scale clustering may influence accessibility and interaction opportunities by shaping the spatial proximity between population demand and functional supply. At the same time, excessively high morphological heterogeneity may not necessarily correspond to higher vitality. In some cases, a very wide Δα may reflect fragmented spatial organization, abrupt transitions between high-rise clusters and low-density areas, or discontinuities in the built environment. Such patterns may increase coordination costs, reduce spatial legibility, or weaken the continuity of pedestrian and service networks. This provides a possible explanation for the nonlinear and saturating response observed in the PDP analysis. Nevertheless, these pathways remain interpretive hypotheses derived from morphology-based modelling and should be further tested using additional data, such as street-view perception, pedestrian flow, mobile-phone signaling, or longitudinal land-use change.

Therefore, the contribution of Δα lies not in replacing conventional indicators such as density, accessibility, or land-use mix, but in complementing them by capturing cross-scale structural variation that average-based indicators may overlook. This interpretation is consistent with the baseline comparison, where adding Δα to conventional morphological indicators substantially improved model performance. It also suggests that urban vitality is better understood as the outcome of interactions between morphological structure and non-morphological factors, rather than as a result determined by morphology alone.

Planning implications for diagnostic urban regeneration

The morphology-vitality diagnostic framework proposed in this study provides a supplementary perspective for spatial-priority assessment in stock-based urban regeneration. Compared with approaches that rely mainly on short-term functional performance indicators, such as population concentration, POI density, or traffic accessibility, this framework introduces multifractal morphological metrics and a standardized mismatch indicator to help identify areas where morphological structure and observed vitality are unevenly aligned. This is relevant to SDG-oriented urban regeneration, particularly SDG 11 on sustainable cities and communities, SDG 9 on resilient infrastructure and innovation, and SDG 13 on urban resilience and adaptation. However, the proposed framework should be used as an auxiliary diagnostic tool rather than as a standalone basis for planning decisions.

At the implementation level, the standardized mismatch indicator P can be used to support preliminary spatial screening:

For areas with relatively positive mismatch values (P > 0.5), observed vitality is lower than the morphology-based predicted level. These areas may require further investigation into whether weak functional activity is related to insufficient service provision, limited land-use activation, poor public-space quality, or inadequate transport connection. In such cases, low-intensity interventions, such as improving pedestrian accessibility, supplementing community services, enhancing public-space quality, and introducing compatible functions, may help improve the alignment between morphological conditions and functional performance.

In such cases, low-intensity interventions, such as improving pedestrian accessibility, supplementing community services, enhancing public-space quality, and introducing compatible functions, may help improve the alignment between morphological conditions and functional performance.

For areas with relatively negative mismatch values (P < 0.5), observed vitality is higher than the morphology-based predicted level. These areas should not be simply interpreted as overloaded spaces, but they may warrant closer examination of whether high functional intensity is supported by adequate spatial organization, public facilities, and mobility infrastructure. The nonlinear response of Δα also provides useful diagnostic information. The threshold analysis suggests that the morphology-vitality relationship is more sensitive around Δα ≈ 1.82 in the Jinan case. Areas within this range may deserve closer attention because small differences in cross-scale morphological heterogeneity are associated with relatively large changes in model-predicted vitality. However, this threshold should not be directly transferred to other cities or scales without recalibration. In practical planning, it should be interpreted as a case-specific reference for identifying morphology-sensitive areas rather than as a universal design standard. Overall, the proposed framework can support urban regeneration by helping planners identify where morphological structure, functional performance, and spatial governance priorities may require further coordination. Its practical value lies not in replacing conventional planning assessment, field investigation, or policy judgement, but in providing an additional quantitative layer for evidence-based diagnosis and targeted spatial intervention. Although the threshold robustness tests reported in Supplementary Table S5 indicate that the Δα breakpoint was relatively stable across bootstrap refitting and spatial-block validation, this value should be interpreted as a case-specific diagnostic reference rather than as a universal planning standard or a prescriptive intervention threshold.

Limitations and future research

Although this study demonstrates the usefulness of combining elevation-weighted multifractal indicators with explainable machine learning for morphology-vitality diagnosis, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, the composite vitality index used in this study is an operational proxy rather than a complete measurement of urban vitality. It was constructed from population density, POI-based functional indicators, and road-network accessibility indicators because these data are reproducible, spatially continuous, and comparable across the study area. However, more direct representations of human activity, such as nighttime light, mobile-phone signaling, consumption records, pedestrian flow, and street-level activity observations, were not included. This limitation may affect the ability of the model to capture temporal fluctuations, informal activities, and fine-grained behavioral patterns. Future research could integrate these data sources to construct a more comprehensive and temporally dynamic vitality representation, while also addressing data privacy, spatial resolution, and temporal consistency issues.

Second, the analysis is based on cross-sectional observational data. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as predictive associations and diagnostic patterns rather than causal evidence. Although the SHAP analysis, baseline comparison, and threshold robustness tests indicate that Δα contains important predictive information, they do not prove that changing Δα would directly cause a corresponding change in urban vitality. Future studies could incorporate longitudinal building morphology, land-use change, planning intervention records, or quasi-experimental designs to examine the temporal co-evolution between urban morphology and functional vitality.

Third, the explanatory power of morphology-based modelling remains partial. The revised model performance and residual analysis show that multifractal and conventional morphological indicators can explain a meaningful proportion of vitality variation, but part of the residual variation remains spatially structured. This unexplained component may be associated with socio-economic conditions, demographic composition, land-use policy, historical accumulation, commercial operation, public-space quality, and micro-scale environmental perception. Therefore, the proposed framework should be regarded as a structural diagnostic supplement rather than a complete explanatory model of urban vitality.

Fourth, the multifractal analysis is influenced by parameter settings, including raster resolution, q range, scale sequence, fitting interval, sliding-window size, and quality-control thresholds. The sensitivity analysis reported in Supplementary Table S2 suggests that the main role of Δα was stable across alternative multifractal parameter settings, but the quantitative model performance still varied when certain scale ranges were excluded. To reduce this uncertainty, this study reported the baseline parameter settings and conducted sensitivity tests using alternative q ranges and scale sequences. The results showed that the main findings, especially the high importance of Δα, remained stable across different configurations. Nevertheless, the optimal parameter settings may vary across cities with different urban densities, block structures, topographic conditions, and development histories. Future work should develop a more systematic calibration procedure for applying multifractal urban morphology analysis across different spatial contexts.

Finally, this study focuses on the main urban area of Jinan, and the identified Δα sensitive interval should be regarded as case-specific. The threshold around Δα ≈ 1.82 reflects the morphology-vitality relationship under the spatial scale, data structure, and urban development context of this case. It should not be directly transferred to other cities or planning scales without recalibration. Comparative studies across multiple cities, different urban regeneration stages, and different topographic settings are needed to evaluate the generalizability of the proposed framework.

Conclusion

This study developed a morphology-vitality diagnostic framework by integrating elevation-weighted multifractal urban form indicators with explainable machine learning. Using the main urban area of Jinan as an empirical case, the study examined how morphological characteristics are associated with the spatial differentiation of urban vitality.

The results show that multifractal indicators can capture cross-scale morphological heterogeneity that is not fully represented by conventional density-based indicators. Baseline comparisons further indicate that adding multifractal descriptors, especially Δα, improves model performance compared with conventional morphological indicators alone. SHAP interpretation consistently identifies Δα as the most important morphological feature, suggesting that the spatial differentiation of vitality is associated not only with the amount of built space, but also with the cross-scale organization of building height, density, and volume distribution.

The nonlinear analysis further reveals a threshold-like response between Δα and morphology-based predicted vitality. In the Jinan case, the sensitive interval is located around Δα ≈ 1.82, as supported by PDP-based segmented regression, bootstrap refitting, and spatial-block robustness tests. This threshold should be interpreted as a case-specific diagnostic reference rather than as a universal planning standard. The result suggests that moderate changes in cross-scale morphological heterogeneity may be associated with more pronounced variation in predicted vitality within this interval, while the response tends to become weaker when Δα exceeds this range. The mismatch indicator M provides an additional diagnostic perspective by measuring the relative deviation between morphology-based predicted vitality and observed vitality. Areas with positive or negative mismatch values should not be directly interpreted as having unrealized potential or functional overload. Instead, they indicate where morphological structure and observed functional performance are unevenly aligned, and where further investigation of land use, accessibility, service provision, population demand, public-space quality, or policy context may be needed.

Overall, this study contributes to urban morphology and vitality research in three aspects. Methodologically, it combines elevation-weighted multifractal analysis, spatial-block validation, baseline comparison, threshold robustness testing, and SHAP interpretation into a reproducible diagnostic workflow. Theoretically, it shifts the interpretation of urban form from average density toward cross-scale structural heterogeneity. Practically, it provides a supplementary quantitative tool for SDG-oriented urban regeneration, helping planners identify areas where morphological structure and functional performance require further coordination. Nevertheless, the framework should be used as a diagnostic supplement rather than a standalone decision-making tool, and future studies should incorporate longitudinal data, direct human-activity indicators, and cross-city comparisons to further test its transferability.
