Status and key determinants of integrated farming systems in coastal regions of eastern India: a comparative assessment
Authors: Subrat Pattanaik, Arati Priyadarshini, Khitish Kumar Sarangi, Abhiram Dash, Ashis Ranjan Udgata, Himansu Sekhar Gouda, Anmol Panda, Pedda Ghouse Peera Sheikh Kulsum, Kamlesh Kumar Acharya, Utpal Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoastal agriculture in India, where it serves as an important economic pillar, operates under escalating climate variability, fragmented landholdings, and risks in marketing and socio-economic conditions. Integrated farming systems (IFS) provide a system-based approach for smallholder farmers to improve resource-use efficiency, diversify income streams, and become more resilient to climatic conditions and other forms of risk. This study outlines the current status and determinants of IFS adoption in coastal Odisha using primary data gathered from 208 farm respondents selected through a multi-stage random sampling design across three coastal districts during 2020–2023. Principal Component Analysis included nine socio-economic variables that were used to describe five latent components that explained 86.67% of the cumulative variance. Probit regression analysis (McFadden’s pseudo-R2 = 0.712; 95.95% correctly classified) indicates that access to institutional credit (marginal effect = 0.286), education (0.174), market connectivity (0.213), labour availability, and family type significantly increase the adoption probability (p < 0.05), whereas age and operational landholding exert negative effects. Economic comparison revealed significant differences in gross income among farming systems for marginal farmers (F = 124.63, p < 0.01) and medium farmers (F = 9.89, p < 0.01), and in variable costs for marginal farmers (F = 247.57, p < 0.01) and small farmers (F = 15.56, p < 0.01). Tukey’s post-hoc analysis further confirmed relative economic differentiation among farming system models. The Crop–Livestock–Pisciculture–Resource Generating (vermicompost and FYM)–Mushroom model, integrated for additional income generation and resource recycling, demonstrated the highest economic performance, whereas Crop–Livestock systems were more suitable for smaller holdings. The findings underscore the need for targeted credit mechanisms, specially designed location specific extension services, and promoting region-specific IFS models aligned with agro-ecological conditions and farm-size categories for enhancing livelihood resilience and aligning with broader sustainability targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49440-z.Keywords: Coastal integrated farming systems, Livelihood diversification, Sustainability, Agro-climatic resilienceSubject terms: Environmental economics, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Environmental economics, Environmental impact, Socioeconomic scenarios, Sustainability

Introduction

As of 2023–24, India’s agriculture spans 219.16 million hectares and remains highly vulnerable to natural calamities, affecting 2.3–4.6% of the cropped area annually1,2 with extreme years reporting losses of up to 7% of the total area3. In India, 86.08% of farms are owned and operated by smallholder farmers with an average land holding of 1.08 hectares4. Approximately 52% of agricultural land is owned by such farmers and the rest is possessed by medium and large farmers. The mean size of landholdings has declined significantly over time from 1.89 ha in 1970–71 to 0.95 ha in 2015–16 largely due to the fragmentation driven by population growth and land demand. The mean size of operational land holding is 1.25 ha4. Being an agrarian and rural state, 83% of Odisha’s population lives in rural areas, while agriculture accounted for almost 48% of the entire workforce in Odisha in 2019–20 of which 92% belonged to the small and marginal category4. The fragmentation along with recurring natural disasters has contributed to rising fallow land (1.7 million ha in 2015–16), including 0.7 million hectares of permanent fallows, thereby constraining agricultural productivity and sustainability targets.

Historically, Indian farmers engaged in diversified and integrated production systems; however, the expansion of single-crop cultivation and specialized enterprise models following the Green Revolution and economic liberalization during the late 1960s and 1990s has increasingly proven inadequate for sustaining livelihoods in disaster-prone coastal regions such as Odisha. Floods, droughts, and salinity intrusion remain persistent threats to agricultural stability in coastal districts5. Declining farm sizes, price volatility, rising input costs, and labour shortages during the peak agricultural season further intensify the vulnerability of smallholder households6.

Although Integrated farming systems (IFS) have been widely studied in India, most empirical investigations are concentrated in inland and rainfed agro-ecological regions, with comparatively limited attention to coastal zones characterized by salinity intrusion, high input costs, and extreme weather variability. Consequently, empirical evidence on the effectiveness of IFS adoption in addressing these distinct coastal challenges remains limited. In particular, systematic analysis of the socioeconomic determinants of IFS adoption and the comparative economic performance of alternative IFS configurations across farm-size categories in coastal eastern India remains limited. This evidence gap constrains the development of targeted, region-specific policy interventions aimed at promoting sustainable and diversified agricultural systems along India’s coastlines6,7.

Against this backdrop, IFS presents a resilience-oriented and scalable production framework7–10. By adopting a diversified farming approach that integrates crop production, livestock, fisheries, poultry, and mushroom cultivation (Fig. 1), IFS minimizes the dependency on external inputs while diversifying income sources11–13. These systems facilitate resource optimization from multiple components and improved resource efficiency through recycling of by-products reducing production costs and environmental footprint in a region prone to resource scarcity8,14,15. As emphasized by FAO16, ‘there is no waste’, and ‘waste is merely a misallocated resource that has the potential to transform into a valuable resource for the production of another product’ in IFS.

In coastal eastern India, particularly Odisha, IFS models adopting a diversified approach that connects crop production with integrating crops with poultry, dairy, mushroom and agroforestry have demonstrated complementary interactions among system components17–20. Such integrated interactions have been reported to enhance economic stability and ecological resilience in climate-vulnerable environments. IFS coincide with various Sustainable Development Goals, namely, SDG 1, which aims to end all forms of poverty in the world, SDG 2, which aims to reduce the risk of food insecurity, there by enhancing income diversification and food security6,11,21,22. IFS also supports SDG 8, which focuses on decent work and economic growth, SDG 12, which focuses on responsible consumption and production, SDG 13, which focuses on climate change, as well as SDG 15, which focuses on life on land, through efficient resource utilization and sustainability23. The NITI Aayog Expert Committee also suggested IFS as a viable agricultural option which was tasked with finding ways to double farmers’ income24.

Despite policy support and documented advantages, the large-scale adoption of IFS remains limited particularly among smallholder farmers. To ensure income and nutritional security, the need for the implementation of such strategies at the regional level is important. Accordingly, this study aims to:

Methodology

Study area and sampling design

The study was carried out in the coastal districts viz., Puri, Khordha, and Bhadrak of Odisha, India in regions highly prone to natural disasters especially cyclones. The districts were selected due to their varying agro-climatic conditions, access to marine and brackish water resources, and presence of state-sponsored IFS projects. The sample farmers were selected using a multi-stage random sampling approach. For the initial phase, the districts were selected through stratified random sampling. On the basis of physical targets for implementation of IFS set by the Government of Odisha for 2022–23, seven coastal districts of Odisha were divided into 3 strata (high, medium and low), one district was randomly selected from each stratum, viz., Puri, Khordha and Bhadrak. This sampling strategy aligns with established practices in agricultural research and provides sufficient coverage of the targeted population. According to the agro-climatic zones of Odisha, Bhadrak district comes under North Eastern Coastal Plain and Khordha and Puri district comes under East & South-Eastern Coastal Plain respectively ensures geographic and socio-economic diversity. During the second stage, the blocks were determined using Stratified Random Sampling; blocks were further stratified on the basis of Net Area Sown and were categorized into low, medium and high Net Area Sown groups. In the third stage, the villages (two villages from each block were selected) ended up being chosen via simple random sampling from each block. As per secondary data provided by various line departments the data was collected. A total of 208 farmers were identified as the sample respondents using the probability proportionate sampling approach (Fig. 2).

Diagrammatical depiction of location of study area.

Data collection

The primary data was obtained from farmers through a survey method utilizing a meticulously designed and pretested questionnaire in lieu with the research objectives. To make sure the information gathered from the farmers was appropriate and pertinent, the sample farmers were individually contacted to record the data. The primary data gathered for the research work were related to 2020–21 to 2022–23 agricultural years. The secondary data was collected from project reports of Farm information and advisory center (FIAC) of selected districts; reports of AICRP on Integrated Farming System, OUAT, Bhubaneswar; Directorate of Economics and Statistics, Govt. of Odisha; published magazines, journals, books and from internet sources.

Ethical considerations and participant consent

All procedures carried out in this study complied with the relevant guidelines and regulations. This research work was developed with collaborative academic insights from domain experts at Vellore Institute of Technology. Ethical approval for this socioeconomic study involving farmer participants was granted by the Research Ethics-cum-Advisory Committee of the College of Agriculture, OUAT, Bhubaneswar, Odisha, India (Ref. No. 1405/CA., dated 06.04.2021). All respondents provided informed consent prior to their inclusion in the survey participants were clearly explained about the study purpose, the voluntary nature of their participation, and the confidentiality of their answers. Only individuals who provided verbal and/or written consent were included in the study.

Statistical analysis

Correlation analysis

To examine relationships between adoption and socioeconomic or demographic variables, Pearson’s correlation was performed using R software (4.3.1) with ggplot227 and corrplot28. The significance of correlations was tested at three levels (p < 0.10; p < 0.05; and p < 0.01). Coefficients were interpreted based on magnitude: values closer to ± 1 indicated stronger relationships. Strongly correlated variables were further analyzed using PCA and Probit regression to address multicollinearity.

Principal component analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed to minimize redundancy/dimensionality among the independent variables such as access to credit, market facilities, and frequency of extension visits, which were highly correlated. Principal components with eigenvalues greater than 1 (Kaiser’s criterion) and a cumulative variance exceeding 80% were retained. Variable loadings were analyzed to understand the contribution of each variable to the principal components. Variables with higher loadings on a component were identified as significant contributors to that dimension29. The derived components were interpreted based on their factor loadings and subsequently incorporated into the Probit regression model to evaluate their influence on the likelihood of adoption.

Core components of the integrated farming system currently in practice

The Integrated farming system in coastal Odisha is a perfect example of sustainable agricultural approach by combining diverse components such as crop production, fisheries, livestock and poultry management, mushroom and waste recycling. This approach utilizes the resources of the region, thus promoting productivity and strengthening the region as well as the environment. In the Fig. 3, under the crop production component, respondents cultivate a variety of crops (field + horticultural). Fruit cultivation, particularly mango and banana, serves as a supplementary source of income for the respondents. Under the dairy farming component, respondents rear cattle that supply milk and other dairy products, thereby enhancing the nutritional intake of the farm household. The respondents raise both dual-purpose birds for backyard poultry farming and commercial breeds for laying eggs. Respondents raise fish in a mixed-species system and harvest fish three times per year after approximately 20 days in between. Mushroom cultivation includes paddy straw mushrooms (Volvariella volvacea) from March to mid-November and oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus ostreatus) from mid-November to February, often grown on bamboo racks in shaded areas. Composting crop residues and animal manure produces organic fertilizer, promoting farm sustainability and income diversification.

Prominent IFS prevalent in coastal regions of eastern India, Odisha

Given its diverse agro-climatic conditions, an extensive coastline of approximately 480 km, and frequent exposure to natural shocks such as cyclones and floods, Odisha offers significant potential for integrated farming, particularly in enhancing food, nutritional, and economic security for small and marginal farmers. The data reveal that the majority engage in “Only Crop” farming, followed by “Crop-Livestock” and “Crop-Livestock-Resource Generating (vermicompost and FYM)” systems. The proportion of marginal farms involved in “Only crop”, “Crop-Livestock”, and “Crop-Poultry” was determined to be 28.15%, 22.96%, and 10.37%, respectively. Among medium farmers, the highest proportion (34.78%) was observed under the “Crop–Pisciculture–Livestock–Mushroom–Resource Generating (vermicompost and FYM)” system.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to identify the key determinants influencing the adoption of Integrated farming systems. In Fig. 5A and B/Supplementary Table 1, the loadings plot highlights the contribution of variables to the principal components. For PC1, socio-economic factors such as Family type (X5), Market Access (X8), and Credit Availability (X7) show high positive loadings. The scree plot illustrates that most of the variance in the data set can be explained by the first four principal components (PC1–PC4). For example, PC1 explains just more than half of the total variance (45.298%). PC2 captures 16.98% of the variance, primarily driven by X1 (Owned Land) with a strong positive correlation. PC3 with 9.578% variance, is characterized by strong correlations with farm income (X4) and extension visits (X9). PC4 is closely associated with the variability in farm income and extension support, similar to PC3. Finally, PC5 is dominated by 6.486% of the total variance and emphasizes the importance of labor availability (X6). It is evident that the first five principal components account for 86.67% of the total variance and effectively capture all the determinants that influence Integrated farming systems (IFS) adoption.

Impact of integrated farming system on farm revenue in India and Odisha

Effect of integrated farming system over single enterprise that have been implemented all across India

As demonstrated in Table 4, IFS has the potential to increase the net income of a particular enterprise, thereby enhancing relative economic efficiency. About 2383% increase in net income was recorded in Crop + Horticulture + Dairy + Goat + Poultry + Vermicompost35 over mono-crop/single enterprise i.e., Sugarcane (Table 6). IFS systems encompass a variety of land-based enterprises (Rice–brinjal + mushroom + poultry) generated net returns of Rs. 77,310 compared to conventional rice cropping alone (Rs. 19,210)36. By implementing strategies to recycle end products and residues from diverse components of the system, IFS was able to decrease production expenses and attain a higher net income. Additionally, a recent study highlighted that integrating crops and livestock boosted resource-use efficiency, contributing to income stability and fostering a diversified livelihood for farmers37. Applying Integrated Farming Systems (IFS) can effectively reduce input costs, especially for essential inputs such as fertilisers, insecticides, and herbicides. This is achieved by optimising resource utilisation and applying integrated pest and nutrient management strategies. With IFS’s increased product diversity, particularly in the dairy and poultry industries, small and marginal farmers might potentially earn money every day.

At least one pair of farming methods has a gross income that differs significantly from the other.

Discussion

Prominent IFS prevalent in coastal regions of eastern India, Odisha

The observed dominance of Only Crop farming systems among marginal and small farmers suggests that a considerable share of farmers in the coastal regions of Odisha still depend primarily on conventional crop-based agriculture. This may be attributed to limited land availability, resource constraints, and lower investment capacity among smaller landholders, which restricts their ability to diversify into more complex integrated systems. However, the presence of diversified models such as Crop–Livestock, Crop–Poultry, and Crop–Pisciculture–Livestock indicates a gradual transition toward integrated farming approaches aimed at improving resource utilization and farm income stability.

The higher prevalence of Crop–Pisciculture–Livestock–Mushroom–Resource Generating systems among medium farmers suggests that farmers with relatively larger landholdings and better access to resources are more capable of adopting multi-component farming systems. Such integrated systems enable better recycling of farm residues and diversification of farm enterprises, which may enhance overall farm productivity and economic resilience. These findings highlight the potential of Integrated farming systems in coastal agro-ecosystems, where diversification of enterprises can help farmers cope with climatic uncertainties and improve livelihood sustainability.

Region-specific Integrated Farming System (IFS) models across coastal India further demonstrate considerable diversity. For instance, the livestock–fish–horticulture-based IFS model reported in Salem, Goa38. In Thanjavur, Tamil Nadu (East Coast Plains and Hills), cropping systems (0.44 ha), horticulture (0.10 ha), dairy (1 crossbred cow and calf), fishery with azolla, poultry, goatery, and vermicomposting, contributing to improved income and resource-use efficiency. Similarly, synthesized IFS models documented earlier in regions such as Raigad, Maharashtra and North and South Goa of West Coast Plains and Ghats include combinations of cropping systems with dairy, poultry, goatery, and horticulture, as well as crop–livestock–fishery systems in Srikakulam and coastal Odisha39,40. Other models integrate field crops with dairy, horticulture, poultry, and composting components in regions like West Bengal, Kerala, and Maharashtra, reflecting location-specific enterprise diversification40,41. These examples underline that diversified integration of enterprises within IFS significantly improves productivity, profitability, and livelihood resilience in coastal farming systems. The findings presented for the distribution of farming systems across landholding categories align with the results reported by the author in Table 2.

Correlation matrix of key factors affecting the adoption of integrated farming systems (IFS)

The availability of credit also has a positive correlation with market access and labour availability, which supports the role of financial inclusion in the stability of farming operations. Land ownership, on the other hand, has no significant correlation with the adoption of IFS, which indicates that land ownership has no significant influence in the adoption of integrated farming systems. The positive correlation among labour availability, access to credit, market access, and family type highlights the importance of socio-economic enablers in facilitating the adoption of integrated farming practices. These variables form a closely associated group, indicating that households with better access to labour and institutional support are more likely to adopt diversified farming systems. The relationship between family type and labour availability suggests that family structure plays a role in ensuring workforce availability for managing multiple farm enterprises, which is consistent with findings that family labour and employment generation significantly contribute to farm income and enterprise diversification42.

Conversely, age negatively correlates with variables such as access to market and extension visits, as well as education, farm income, family type, labour availability, and access to credit, indicating that older farmers may have relatively lower engagement with institutional and market-related services. Similar patterns have been reported where education, experience, and extension contact significantly enhance adoption behaviour and livelihood outcomes in IFS, while age and landholding often show weaker or non-significant relationships43,44. The availability of credit also shows a positive correlation with market access and labour availability, which further supports the role of financial inclusion and infrastructural support in strengthening farming systems. Land ownership and farm income, however, exhibit relatively weaker associations with other variables, suggesting a limited direct influence on the adoption of IFS, which aligns with earlier evidence indicating that landholding size may not significantly determine awareness or adoption of IFS practices44.

Principal component analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is widely used in agricultural systems research to reduce dimensionality and identify key socio-economic and structural drivers underlying farm-level decision-making45. Previous studies applying PCA in related domains such as crop diversification and farming system typology have demonstrated its effectiveness in capturing dominant sources of variation and addressing multicollinearity among variables46. In the present study, PCA results revealed that PC1 exhibited high positive correlations with family type, market access, labour availability and credit availability, suggest that these variables collectively represent a “Socio-economic Resource Factor” influencing Integrated Farming System (IFS) adoption. For instance, farmers with larger family type, greater market and credit facility access tend to be associated with adoption of integrated farming practices. This reveals that socio-economic facilitators are of great importance in developing integrated farming diversification. Likewise, PC2 represents “Land Ownership Factor” defined by owned land. This suggests that larger landholdings are more likely to be associated with greater potential for adopting multiple farming practices in integrated farming. This reveals that land availability is also a potential facilitator for diversification in integrated farming practices. PC3 represents “Economic and Advisory Support Factor” defined by farm income and extension visits. This reveals that economic stability is of great importance in integrated farming practices. Moreover, PC4 could be defined as “Self-reliance vs. Institutional Dependency Factor,” which captures variability related to income and extension support. However, the negative relationship between farmers with less dependency on external extension visits could be associated with developing self-sufficiency in integrated farming practices. PC5 represents “Labour Accessibility Factor,” which reflects additional variation in labour availability in integrated farming practices.

The linkage between access to credit and access to market shows a strong positive correlation, signifying that farmers with better access to credit facilities also tend to have better access to market facilities. A cluster of positive associations is observed among family type, labour availability, access to credit, and market access, indicating the combined role of socio-economic factors in shaping farming decisions. A moderate negative association is observed between age and variables such as frequency of extension visits and access to market, suggesting that older farmers tend to have relatively lower engagement with extension and market-related services compared to younger farmers. Education exhibits relatively weak and mixed relationships with other variables, indicating no clear directional influence. Farm income and land ownership also show weaker associations with other factors. The presence of strong correlations among variables such as access to credit and access to market indicates possible multicollinearity. Therefore, the application of PCA becomes essential to reduce redundancy in the dataset and identify the major factors influencing IFS adoption.

Determinants of identified integrated farming systems of coastal regions of eastern India, Odisha

The influence of family type on participation in integrated farming systems requires careful interpretation. While joint family systems are often assumed to provide greater labour availability, this may not always translate into effective on-farm labour supply. In many cases, a considerable proportion of household members are engaged in non-farm or service-sector employment, often in nearby urban areas, thereby limiting their direct involvement in agricultural activities. This may explain the observed relationship between family type and IFS adoption in the study area. Having access to credit facilities enables farmers to finance the initial costs of setting up and managing various IFS components, especially resource-intensive enterprises such as livestock rearing and pisciculture. Access to credit has been identified as a strong and direct determinant of IFS adoption, facilitating investment and operational efficiency34.

Similarly, improved access to markets helps farmers reap economic benefits from diversified enterprises, thereby encouraging farmers to diversify and add multiple components within farming systems. The positive influence of farm size on IFS adoption can be attributed to the ability of larger holdings to allocate space for multiple enterprises, reducing income risks and enabling diversification. If farmers are educated, they are able to understand and reap the benefits of this long-term system and are able to utilize it50,51.

The negative correlation between age and IFS adoption implies that older farmers show a lower likelihood of adopting new and labour-intensive systems of agriculture because they are more accustomed to traditional monoculture practices. Younger farmers, being more risk-takers and flexible, have a higher likelihood of adopting IFS due to its economic and environmental advantages. Similar findings have been reported earlier, highlighting the role of age and economic factors in farm diversification decisions47–49. Furthermore, Integrated farming system provides huge scope in reducing the need for fertilizer and enhancing income per acre through reusing manure available on the farm33,50. The use of PCA and Probit analysis helped identify the determinants of IFS adoption, which included socio-economic enablers like access to credit facilities and market facilities, land ownership, and labor. This further reiterates the importance of developing strategies to improve socio-economic enablers for IFS adoption among smallholder farmers.

The study revealed that FS-II (Crop-Livestock-Pisciculture-Mushroom) is the most promising IFS model for small-scale farmers, in which various factors are involved in improving the recycling of resources, income stability, and efficient utilization of resources. This is helpful in increasing income through diversification of income even with low landholding size. FS-I (Crop-Livestock) is a highly adaptable IFS model for marginal and medium-scale farmers with low management intensity and stable economic benefits. It is a balanced model of productivity and management feasibility.

From the policy point of view, the scaling up of these models requires the development of enabling conditions such as access to water resources, availability of credit, development of market infrastructure, and training of the farmers through extension programs. Region-specific interventions such as the development of climate-resilient fish ponds in cyclone-prone areas, development of cold storage facilities for perishables, and training of the farmers through existing extension programs. FS-II, having been proven to be economically efficient and robust, has the scope for scaling up in the coastal regions of India, namely West Bengal, Andhra Pradesh, and Tamil Nadu, and similar agro-climatic zones worldwide. Similarly, FS-I has the scope for providing an alternative model for small and marginal farmers in the rain-fed and fragmented land resource regions, which can offer sustainable income diversification and resource efficiency.

Policy implications

The eastern coastal belt of India, which includes the state of Odisha, is particularly vulnerable to natural calamities, and therefore, the need for promoting integrated farming systems cannot be overemphasized. The performance of the integrated farming systems across the districts of Puri, Khordha, and Bhadrak varies, which can be explained by the agro-climatic conditions, availability of resources, and market accessibility. For instance, the district of Puri has shown high potential for the integration of pisciculture, but the area faces the problems of cyclonic storms and saline water. Similarly, the district of Khordha has shown high profitability in the area of livestock and mushrooms, mainly because of the proximity to urban market, but the area faces the problems of high input costs and fragmented land holdings. The district of Bhadrak has shown the highest adoption of diverse integrated farming systems, mainly because of the availability of brackish water, but the area faces the problems of poor extension and market facilities.

These results highlight the importance of developing region-specific policies. Developing climate-resilient infrastructure for Puri, input subsidies and land consolidations for Khordha, and developing market infrastructure and extension services for Bhadrak are essential for formulating effective policies. Financial inclusion, market development, and sustainable agriculture practices are critical for developing effective policies to drive widespread adoption of IFS. Developing IFS models for disaster-prone regions, with government partnerships with financial institutions, can be effective in developing climate-resilient solutions using subsidies and low-interest loans. Developing local market infrastructure, processing facilities, cooperatives, and cold storage facilities can be effective in reducing losses and ensuring remunerative prices for farmers.

The research is in line with the existing policies and programs at the national and state levels, such as the NITI Aayog’s initiative on ‘Doubling Farmers’ Income,’ PMKSY, and the Odisha government’s program on ‘Climate Smart Agriculture.’ Adoption of water-efficient models of IFS will ensure the optimization of irrigation facilities, which will be in line with the objectives of PMKSY. Expanding financial inclusion initiatives such as Kisan Credit Cards (KCC) for providing targeted financial support to IFS enterprises will help address key constraints to the adoption of the technology. Region-specific initiatives such as the development of cyclone-resistant infrastructure for fish ponds in Puri, land consolidation and cooperative farming in Khordha, and the development of extension services and cold storage facilities in Bhadrak would help in replicating promising IFS models like FS-II and FS-I in coastal and similar agro-climatic conditions.

Conclusion

This study evaluates the economic performance and determinants of Integrated farming systems (IFS) adoption among farm households in the coastal districts of Odisha based on farm-level survey data. The results show that diversified farming systems generate higher economic returns and improve resource-use efficiency compared to conventional crop-based systems. The probit regression analysis reveals that education, institutional credit, labor availability, market access, and family labor are significant determinants influencing the adoption of Integrated farming systems by farm households in Odisha, whereas older farmers have a lower likelihood of adopting integrated farming systems. The study reveals that limited institutional credit is a major constraint for adoption by farm households in Odisha. The study recommends that integrated farming enterprises like livestock rearing, fishpond development, and mushroom production require a certain level of investment in terms of funds for establishing integrated farming units by smallholder farmers. Hence, there is a need to provide a specific loan facility under the Kisan Credit Card (KCC) for multi-enterprise farming, not only for seasonal crop farming. Providing medium-term credit for livestock infrastructure, small fish ponds and mushroom production units would enable farmers to diversify their farming systems.

Market access significantly influences adoption decisions, suggesting that farmers with greater market connectivity are more likely to adopt diversified farming systems when reliable marketing channels exist for products such as milk, fish and mushrooms. Establishing local aggregation centres and strengthening Farmer Producer Organizations (FPOs) in coastal districts can reduce marketing costs and improve price realization for diversified farm products. The positive effect of education on adoption suggests that farmers require better technical knowledge to manage multiple enterprises within integrated farming systems. Agricultural extension services should therefore expand training programs on integrated nutrient management, livestock–crop integration and farm waste recycling. Demonstration farms and farmer field schools can help disseminate practical knowledge of integrated farming practices among smallholders. Labour availability and family type were also found to positively influence adoption, highlighting the labour-intensive nature of diversified farming systems. This suggests that labour-saving technologies and mechanized tools suitable for small farms should be promoted to reduce management constraints and encourage wider adoption.

Overall, it is evident from the above findings that integrated farm systems can enhance farm income stability and efficiency in coastal areas vulnerable to natural calamities. For scaling up integrated farm systems, there is a need to develop policy interventions to enhance credit access, market infrastructure, and technical training programs related to integrated farm management. The findings have shown that integrated farm systems can enhance farm income stability and efficiency in coastal farm systems.
