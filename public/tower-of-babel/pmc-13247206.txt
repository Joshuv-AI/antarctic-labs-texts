The KRT15 and KRT81 complex promotes lenvatinib resistance in thyroid cancer by upregulating DGKB mediated lipid metabolism
Authors: Yunjun Wang, Yu Zhang, Dan Zhao, Yi Wu, Tian Liao, Yu Wang, Jun Xiang, Qi Chen, Tuanqi Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLenvatinib resistance is a major clinical obstacle in the treatment of radioiodine-refractory papillary thyroid cancer (PTC). Clarification of the molecular mechanisms of this resistance is of utmost importance to devise effective therapeutic strategies. We investigated the role of Keratin 15 (KRT15) in lenvatinib resistance through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo studies. Tumor and normal thyroid tissues were analyzed for KRT15 expression and correlation with patient survival. Metabolic profiling was performed to investigate KRT15-dependent alterations in lipid metabolism, namely fatty acid oxidation (FAO). Mechanistic investigations explored the interaction between KRT15, Keratin 81 (KRT81), and Diacylglycerol Kinase B (DGKB). The therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway was evaluated using shRNA-mediated knockdown and pharmacological inhibition. KRT15 overexpression was associated with unfavorable clinical prognosis in thyroid cancer patients. We identified that KRT15 interacts with KRT81 to constitute a regulatory complex, which induces DGKB upregulation. The KRT15-KRT81-DGKB axis controls metabolic reprogramming by upregulating key FAO enzymes (CPT1A and ACOX1), resulting in increased cellular energetics and survival against therapeutic stress. Inhibition of this pathway successfully restored lenvatinib sensitivity in resistant cells. This study illustrates a novel mechanism of cytoskeletal proteins involvement in metabolic adaptation of drug-resistant thyroid cancer cells. Introduction

The incidence of papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) has shown a consistent increasing trend globally during the past three decades, presenting a significant healthcare burden1,2. In PTC, radioiodine-refractory tumors represent a particularly challenging-to-treat subset with unfavorable prognosis and limited treatment choices3. Following the failure of conventional radioiodine therapy, lenvatinib has emerged as a standard second-line treatment with action against a variety of kinases including VEGFRs and FGFRs4. While clinical trials have documented significant progression-free survival benefit in lenvatinib-treated patients, therapeutic resistance in most patients arises sooner or later, and the disease progresses with poor clinical outcomes5.

The present understanding of lenvatinib resistance mechanisms comprises a variety of pathways, including alternative receptor tyrosine kinase activation, non-canonical angiogenesis, and target gene mutation6. In thyroid cancer, resistance to kinase inhibitors has been attributed to alterations in MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways, as well as epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition7. These known mechanisms, however, fail to fully explain the heterogeneous nature of resistance development, and more importantly, adaptation of cancer cells to targeted therapy at such a high rate. The failure of existing mechanistic models suggests the existence of additional, as yet unknown, regulatory factors in lenvatinib resistance.

Recent advances in cancer biology have highlighted two novel paradigms in therapeutic resistance: metabolic reprogramming and non-canonical functions of cytoskeletal proteins. Metabolic adaptation, and more specifically alterations in lipid metabolism, has emerged as a central determinant of therapeutic resistance in a wide range of cancers8–10. At the forefront of this process is diacylglycerol kinase B (DGKB), a major enzyme that regulates lipid signaling and cellular metabolic regulation2,11. DGKB expression levels are closely correlated with disease progression and response to treatment in a wide range of cancers, largely through its control of membrane composition and receptor trafficking12,13. However, the exact role of DGKB-mediated metabolic reprogramming in lenvatinib resistance is unclear.

In parallel to metabolic adaptations, cytoskeletal proteins, and in particular keratins, have demonstrated critical regulatory functions in addition to their structural role. Keratin 15 (KRT15), primarily expressed in basal keratinocytes and epithelial stem cells, has been recognized as a significant regulator of PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin pathways in a variety of malignancies8. These pathways are particularly overlapping with known resistance mechanisms in PTC, suggesting a potential role of KRT15 in therapeutic resistance. Similarly, Keratin 81 (KRT81), while primarily described by its hair follicle expression, has been implicated in tumor progression and chemoresistance through its role in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition14. The possible cross-talk between these keratins and their influence on metabolic regulation, and more specifically in thyroid cancer resistance, represents a critical gap in the literature15.

Our preliminary data have revealed an interesting correlation between KRT15 expression and DGKB activity in lenvatinib-resistant thyroid cancer cells. The observation suggests a previously unrecognized link between cytoskeleton organization and metabolic reprogramming in drug resistance development. Furthermore, we have found alterations in the expression patterns of both KRT15 and KRT81 in resistant cells, indicating a possible synchronized response to therapeutic stress.

The aim of this study is to elucidate a new mechanism of lenvatinib resistance in thyroid cancer, namely the KRT15, KRT81, and DGKB-mediated metabolic reprogramming regulatory axis. We hypothesize that KRT15 upregulation and interaction with KRT81 is an adaptive response that causes resistance through DGKB-mediated metabolic reprogramming. Elucidation of this new resistance mechanism could yield important information on potential targets for overcoming lenvatinib resistance in thyroid cancer patients.

Results

KRT15 is strongly upregulated in lenvatinib-resistant thyroid cancer cells and significantly facilitates drug resistance

To elucidate the mechanisms of lenvatinib resistance in thyroid cancer, lenvatinib-resistant TPC-1-len and B-CPAP-len cell lines were established by gradual drug exposure and exhibited significant resistance compared with parental cells (Fig. S1A–B). Transcriptome analysis revealed the striking upregulation of KRT15 in resistant cells compared with their parental counterparts (Fig. 1A), suggesting a potential critical role for KRT15 in the development of drug resistance. This dramatic increase was also supported by analysis of the TCGA-THCAdeg dataset (Fig. 1B), indicating the clinical relevance of this observation in human thyroid cancer. Consistently, KRT15 expression was markedly elevated in thyroid tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 1C–E), further implicating KRT15 in the development of thyroid cancer. Interestingly, lenvatinib treatment itself increased the expression of KRT15 in TPC-1 and B-CPAP thyroid cancer cell lines (Fig. 1F–G), raising the possibility of an adaptive cellular response in which tumor cells overexpress KRT15 as a counter-regulatory measure to potentially blunt the effect of the drug and survive under drug pressure. This observation also raises the possibility of KRT15 as a potential marker of acquired resistance to lenvatinib. Functional assays demonstrated that KRT15 plays a direct role in facilitating drug resistance. Overexpression of KRT15 significantly elevated its mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 1H–I), while KRT15 knockdown resulted in a marked decrease (Fig. 1J–K). Importantly, upregulation of KRT15 led to reduced sensitivity to lenvatinib, as evidenced by increased cell viability under drug treatment (Fig. 1L). Conversely, silencing KRT15 restored lenvatinib sensitivity in resistant cells (Fig. 1M). Collectively, these results indicate that KRT15 upregulation not only marks, but also functionally drives lenvatinib resistance in thyroid cancer cells, representing a key adaptive strategy by which tumor cells circumvent the effects of lenvatinib.

KRT15 facilitates thyroid cancer cell proliferation and migration and aids in the aggressive phenotype of lenvatinib resistance

To further clarify the overall role of KRT15 in thyroid cancer, and in the context of lenvatinib resistance, we first analyzed its impact on fundamental cellular behaviors involved in tumor growth. Overexpression of KRT15 significantly increased both cell proliferation and migration in TPC-1 and B-CPAP cell lines (Fig. 2A–D), suggesting a role for KRT15 in tumor growth and metastasis, characteristics of aggressive cancers. Given the observed upregulation of KRT15 in lenvatinib-resistant cells, we next questioned whether KRT15 contributes to their aggressive behavior. Indeed, knockdown of KRT15 in resistant TPC-1 and B-CPAP cell lines resulted in a significant inhibition of both cell proliferation and migration (Fig. 2E–H). These findings suggest that KRT15 plays a role in maintaining the aggressive characteristics of lenvatinib-resistant thyroid cancer cells, implicating it as a significant contributor to this resistance mechanism and a promising target for anti-metastatic therapy in drug-resistant tumors. KRT15′s ability to promote proliferation and migration in resistant cells likely underpins the heightened aggressiveness typically observed in drug-resistant cancers, potentially enabling tumor growth and spread even under conditions of drug treatment.

KRT15 regulates lipid metabolism by modulating fatty acid oxidation in lenvatinib-resistant thyroid cancer cells

Our initial transcriptomic profiling revealed a remarkable correlation between KRT15 and lipid metabolism pathways in lenvatinib-resistant thyroid cancer cells (Fig. 3A), suggesting a putative metabolic reprogramming process mediated by KRT15 in drug resistance. To investigate this further, we found that knockdown of KRT15 in resistant TPC-1 and B-CPAP cell lines significantly reduced both total cholesterol and triglyceride levels (Fig. 3B–C), indicating a critical role for KRT15 in lipid homeostasis in these cells. Consistent with this, we observed a remarkable reduction in lipid accumulation upon knockdown of KRT15 in both resistant cell lines, as revealed by Nile Red staining (Fig. 3D). To elucidate the mechanisms underlying this, we examined the expression of key enzymes in fatty acid oxidation, a process known to be crucial for energy production and survival in cancer cells. Notably, the mRNA and protein levels of key enzymes in fatty acid oxidation, including CPT1A, CPT1B, ACOX1, and ACOX2, were generally reduced upon KRT15 knockdown in both resistant cell lines (Fig. 3E–F).

Furthermore, we assessed the functional effect of these metabolic alterations by measuring cell proliferation and viability under various conditions. The proliferative defect caused by KRT15 knockdown was rescued partially by treatment with GW501516, a PPARα/δ agonist that enhances fatty acid oxidation (Fig. 3G). Similarly, GW501516 treatment partially restored the resistance of KRT15-depleted cells to lenvatinib (Fig. 3H). These collective findings demonstrate that KRT15 plays a significant role in regulating lipid metabolism in lenvatinib-resistant thyroid cancer cells, potentially by influencing fatty acid oxidation. This suggests that KRT15 may confer a metabolic advantage on drug-resistant cells, potentially by ensuring sufficient energy supply through enhanced fatty acid oxidation, which is crucial for survival under lenvatinib stress.

DGKB, a downstream effector of KRT15, mediates lipid metabolism and contributes to lenvatinib resistance

To further dissect the molecular mechanisms downstream of KRT15, our gene expression analysis revealed that KRT15 knockdown most significantly reduced DGKB expression, and other lipid metabolism genes including PNLIPRP3, CYP3A5, SRD5A2, HSD17B3, OLAH, PTGS2, ELOVL4, HSD11B1, DGKI, and LIPG were downregulated to varying degrees (Figs. 4A, S2A). The connection between KRT15 and DGKB was also supported by a positive correlation between their expression levels in TCGA data (Fig. 4B). Similarly, we verified that both DGKB mRNA (Fig. 4C) and protein (Fig. 4D) expression were dramatically reduced in lenvatinib-resistant cells when KRT15 was knocked down, suggesting DGKB is a downstream effector of KRT15. To explore the functional role of DGKB, we constructed DGKB overexpression, having first verified that DGKB mRNA expression was significantly higher in both TPC-1 and B-CPAP cell lines compared to normal controls (Fig. S2B). Successive knockdown of DGKB by two independent shRNAs significantly inhibited cell proliferation in both TPC-1 and B-CPAP cell lines over 5 days as reflected by OD450 values (Fig. S2C).

To examine whether DGKB is involved in the impact of KRT15 on cell proliferation and lipid metabolism, rescue experiments were performed. Overexpression of DGKB partially rescued the proliferation of KRT15-silenced resistant cells (Fig. 4E), indicating that DGKB is one of the mediators of KRT15′s impact on cell growth. Moreover, DGKB overexpression also partially rescued the reduction of total cholesterol and triglyceride content (Fig. 4F–G), and lipid accumulation (Fig. 4H) caused by KRT15 silencing, indicating a role of DGKB in KRT15-mediated regulation of lipid metabolism. Consistent with these findings, the repression of lipid metabolism-related proteins by KRT15 knockdown was also partially reversed by DGKB overexpression (Fig. 4I). KRT15 knockdown significantly decreased the expression of CPT1A, CPT1B, ACOX1, and ACOX2 mRNA, and DGKB overexpression in KRT15-silenced cells partially restored the expression of these genes (Fig. S2D–K). These results collectively demonstrate that DGKB is a key downstream effector of KRT15, mediating its impact on lipid metabolism and hence, lenvatinib resistance in thyroid cancer cells.

KRT15 and KRT81 synergistically control DGKB expression in lenvatinib-resistant thyroid cancer cells

To look for potential upstream regulatory mechanisms of DGKB, we used five online databases to predict potential interacting partners of KRT15 and identified Keratin-81 (KRT81) as a potential candidate (Fig. 5A). Molecular docking prediction revealed direct interaction between KRT15 and KRT81 (Fig. 5B), which was subsequently confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation assays (Fig. 5C). Following confirmation of KRT81 overexpression in TPC-1-Len and B-CPAP-Len cells (Fig. S3A, B), we discovered that KRT81 overexpression indeed increased DGKB mRNA expression (Fig. 5D) and protein levels (Fig. 5E). Notably, co-overexpression of KRT15 and KRT81 resulted in synergistic increase in DGKB expression at both mRNA (Fig. 5F) and protein levels (Fig. 5G), suggesting that KRT15 and KRT81 jointly regulate DGKB expression in lenvatinib-resistant thyroid cancer cells.

In vivo knockdown of KRT15 inhibits tumor growth and regulates lipid metabolism in a xenograft model

To validate our in vitro findings in a more physiologically pertinent setting, we examined the function of KRT15 in vivo in a xenograft model of lenvatinib-resistant thyroid cancer. Tumor-bearing mice from KRT15-knockdown resistant cells displayed much slower tumor growth than control mice (Fig. 6A–C), revealing the significant contribution of KRT15 to tumor growth in vivo. Consistent with our in vitro findings, tumor tissue analysis revealed reduced expression of both KRT15 and its downstream effector DGKB in the knockdown group (Fig. 6D–E). Furthermore, we discovered decreased total cholesterol and triglyceride content in tumors with KRT15 knockdown compared to the control group (Fig. 6F–G), revealing that KRT15 influences lipid metabolism in vivo.These in vivo findings strongly validate our in vitro results, supporting the contribution of KRT15 to tumor growth and lipid metabolism, most likely through regulating the expression of DGKB, thereby establishing KRT15 as an attractive therapeutic target for lenvatinib-resistant thyroid cancer.

High expression of KRT15 and DGKB is related to late stage in human thyroid cancer tissues, suggesting clinical significance

To ascertain the clinical relevance of our findings, we examined the expression of KRT15 and DGKB in a panel of human thyroid cancer tissues. KRT15 expression was significantly increased in early and late stage thyroid cancer tissues compared to normal thyroid tissues (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, KRT15 expression was also increased in late stage tumors compared to early stage tumors, implicating a role in disease progression and in the acquisition of more aggressive and drug-resistant phenotypes.

Discussion

Keratins have been broadly implicated in tumor progression and therapeutic resistance through dynamic cytoskeletal remodeling processes16,17. Our work significantly broadens this canvas by identifying KRT15 as a central mediator of lenvatinib resistance in thyroid cancer, with marked upregulation in resistant cell populations—a finding strongly corroborated by clinical evidence from both the TCGA-THCAdeg dataset and patient-derived samples. Moreover, the observation that KRT15 expression peaks in ATC—the most aggressive and undifferentiated subtype (Fig. S3C,D)—corroborates the link between KRT15 upregulation and the acquisition of a high-grade, drug-resistant phenotype. Through meticulous functional characterization using both gain- and loss-of-function experimental paradigms, we demonstrated that KRT15 overexpression alone is sufficient to confer lenvatinib resistance, while its targeted inhibition effectively resensitizes resistant cells to treatment.

This result places KRT15 as a candidate determinant in acquired drug resistance mechanisms, though the complete extent of its regulatory functions warrants further investigation. Notably, KRT15-overexpressing thyroid cancer cells exhibited enhanced proliferative and migratory capacities While TPC-1 cells displayed higher basal motility than B-CPAP cells, this observation is consistent with previous studies18 attributing the difference to distinct genetic drivers: the RET/PTC rearrangement in TPC-1 cells establishes a motility-favoring mesenchymal phenotype, whereas BRAF-mutant B-CPAP cells exhibit suppressed 2D motility due to RHOA-mediated signaling and strong epithelial adhesiveness. Despite these intrinsic differences, KRT15 consistently promoted aggressive behaviors in both genetic backgrounds. This observation aligns with accumulating evidence implicating cytoskeletal protein dysregulation in aggressive cancer phenotypes across a spectrum of malignancies: in hepatocellular carcinoma, disrupted cytoskeletal dynamics directly affect invasive behavior and therapeutic response; in polyploid giant cancer cells, heightened cytoskeletal stiffness mediated through RhoA-ROCK1 signaling pathways facilitates both migration and chemoresistance; and in breast cancer, specific cytoskeletal protein signatures are powerfully predictive of metastatic potential19–22. These complementary results across a variety of cancer types validate our findings while suggesting that cytoskeletal proteins may represent a conserved mechanism in treatment resistance.

Our preliminary investigations suggest a potential connection between KRT15 and lipid metabolism in the specific context of resistance to lenvatinib. Reductions in cholesterol and triglyceride levels and downregulation of several fatty acid oxidation (FAO) enzymes (CPT1A, CPT1B, ACOX1, and ACOX2) were noted upon KRT15 knockdown in resistant cells. While these results suggest a connection between KRT15 and lipid metabolic pathways in thyroid cancer cells under therapeutic stress, the precise molecular mechanisms involved in this connection remain to be elucidated. Changes in lipid metabolism have been described in other drug-resistant cancer models, including increased fatty acid oxidation in resistance to antiangiogenic drugs, increased lipid synthesis and uptake in metastatic cells resistant to therapy, and disrupted cholesterol metabolism affecting membrane properties in resistant cancer cells23–26. Collectively, these reports underscore the potential importance of metabolic reprogramming in resistance mechanisms, although the exact involvement of KRT15 in these processes must be mechanistically investigated.

Further investigation identified DGKB as a downstream target that was considerably regulated by KRT15. Interestingly, our results suggest DGKB’s potential involvement in playing a key role in sustaining proliferation and lipid metabolism alterations in lenvatinib-resistant cells, perhaps through modulation of pathways intersecting with VEGFR and FGFR signaling networks—the direct targets of lenvatinib. Rescue experiments corroborated evidence that DGKB overexpression partially rescued the proliferative and metabolic phenotype disrupted by KRT15 knockdown, suggesting its functional relevance. Earlier reports have implicated DGKB in lipid signaling and membrane dynamics including its involvement in the regulation of TASK potassium channels through diacylglycerol signaling, mediating cancer radioresistance through DGAT1-dependent lipotoxicity, and regulating mTORC1 and lipogenic metabolism in tumor cells27–29. Our findings extend these observations by suggesting a potential role for DGKB in therapeutic resistance through lipid metabolism regulation, although the partial rescue effect observed upon DGKB restoration indicates the likely involvement of additional downstream factors regulated by KRT15.

In a novel finding, we found an interaction between KRT15 and KRT81. Our preliminary data suggest that these keratins interact to form a complex that can positively regulate the expression of DGKB, with potential cooperative transcriptional regulation. Though the exact molecular mechanism of this interaction has to be elucidated, this observation is indicative of a unique cytoskeletal-metabolic regulatory network at the core of resistance mechanisms in thyroid cancer. Such keratin complex formation could have implications in cancer biology more broadly, particularly in cancer treatment resistance, where they could serve as scaffolds for metabolic enzymes like DGKB, though this is a hypothesis that should be experimentally validated.

Our study has several major limitations that must be mentioned. Although our findings highlight the involvement of KRT15 in DGKB regulation and lipid metabolism, in particular, in the context of drug resistance, the precise molecular mechanisms, including transcriptional regulation, remain unclear and require investigation. Additionally, extensive clinical validation is required, for example, large-scale, multi-center prospective studies with comprehensive patient survival analysis, treatment response, and biomarker analysis to fully determine the prognostic and predictive value of KRT15 and DGKB. Additionally, although we demonstrated the impact of KRT15 on cellular proliferation, migration, and lipid metabolism, a series of fundamental questions remain unanswered. Specifically, the mechanism by which KRT15-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming contributes to the survival of drug-resistant cells must be further defined. Additionally, it is uncertain whether this metabolic shift has a primary effect on membrane dynamics, bioenergetic processes, or other cellular processes. Additionally, the general importance of KRT15-KRT81 interaction in tumor formation and therapeutic resistance warrants further exploration. Addressing these knowledge gaps through rigorous mechanistic studies and exploring targeted therapeutic strategies, including the development of specific inhibitors against the KRT15-DGKB pathway or combination therapy with lenvatinib, are required to enhance therapeutic outcomes.

In conclusion, our study reveals a new mechanism of lenvatinib resistance in thyroid cancer by demonstrating that KRT15 and KRT81 can form a functional complex that controls DGKB expression and metabolic adaptation. This new cytoskeletal-metabolic axis not only improves our understanding of resistance mechanisms but also offers the potential for therapeutic targeting. Future studies should be aimed at confirmation of these findings in larger patient populations and the development of targeted approaches to inhibit this resistance pathway, potentially through direct inhibition of KRT15-KRT81 interactions or their downstream effects on DGKB-mediated metabolic reprogramming.

Conclusions

This study identifies a novel KRT15-KRT81-DGKB axis that drives lenvatinib resistance in thyroid cancer through metabolic reprogramming of lipid metabolism. Our findings not only enhance the understanding of resistance mechanisms to targeted therapies but also reveal potential therapeutic targets and biomarkers for improving treatment outcomes in thyroid cancer patients. The discovery of this cytoskeletal-metabolic regulatory network provides new insights into the complex adaptations cancer cells undergo to survive therapeutic pressure and suggests that targeting this pathway may be a promising strategy to overcome drug resistance.

Materials & methods

Sample collection

Tumor tissues (n = 10) and corresponding normal tissues (n = 10) were collected from 10 patients diagnosed with PTC at Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center from December 1, 2020, to June 30, 2023. Diagnoses were confirmed through cytological and pathological tests. The detailed clinical characteristics of the patients are listed in Table S3. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (Approval No: 050432–4-1911D), all experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The samples were stored at − 80 ℃ before being used in the experiments.

Cell culture and transfection

TPC-1 and B-CPAP cells were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, Grand Island, NY) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 100 U/mL Penicillin–Streptomycin (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Small hairpin (sh)-KRT15, Overexpression (oe)-KRT15, oe-DGKB, oe-KRT81 and negative control (oe-NC and sh-NC) were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China). The cells were transfected using the hieff-TransTM liposomal-transfection reagent (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The shRNAs targeting KRT15 were cloned into the pLKO.1-EGFP-puro vector. The target sequences were as follows: shKRT15-1, 5′-TGAAGCCGAAGTATCTCAGCT-3′; and shKRT15-2, 5′-TAGCCAATACTTCAAGACCAT-3′.

Construction of lenvatinib-resistant cell lines

The lenvatinib-resistant cells were constructed as previously described30. Briefly, TPC-1 and B-CPAP cells were treated with varying doses of lenvatinib (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) for 3 days. The 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) of the two cells was measured via cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) regent (Meilunbio, Dalian, China). Subsequently, cells were cultured in lenvatinib concentrations below the IC50, with weekly increments of 1 μM until reaching 10 μM. Finally, lenvatinib-resistant cell lines were obtained by incubating the cells with 10 μM lenvatinib for 6 months. The established resistant cell lines were expanded and cryopreserved. To verify the identity of the cell lines after the long-term selection process, Short Tandem Repeat (STR) profiling was performed. The results confirmed that the resistant cells matched the genetic profile of their respective parental lines without cross-contamination (Supplementary Data).

Real‐time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

TRIzol reagent was used to extract total RNA from tissues and cells. After quantification, total RNA was reverse transcribed to construct cDNA using hiScript-II 1st strand cDNA synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR-master-mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was used to perform qRT-PCR. Relative RNA expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primers used in the study were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China). The primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

The tissues and cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher) and centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 30 min to remove insoluble substances. The protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (MeilunBio). Subsequently, the proteins were separated using SDS-PAGE (Sangon, Shanghai, China) and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA) through electrophoresis. The membranes were blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against KRT15, DGKB, KRT81, CPT1A, CPT1B, ACOX1, and ACOX2. GAPDH and β-actin were used as loading controls. After washing, the membranes were incubated with Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (1:3000, Abcam) and Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (1:3000, Abcam) secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Finally, protein bands were visualized using an electrochemiluminescence (ECL) program (Tanon, Shanghai, China). Detailed information regarding the antibody manufacturers, catalog numbers, and dilutions is listed in Table S2.

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

Co-immunoprecipitation assay was performed with a commercial IP kit from Beaver (Cat. No. 22202-100). The process began with the preparation of cell lysates, which were incubated overnight with the corresponding antibodies at 4 °C. Protein A/G magnetic beads were added to the mix on the following day and incubated at 4 °C for 3 h. The complexes were washed four times with IP binding buffer. Samples were denatured by boiling at 100 °C for 10 min in 1 × loading buffer. The protein components were then resolved and examined by Western blot analysis.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

TPC-1, TPC-1 lenvatinib resistance (TPC-1-Len), sh-NC TPC-1-Len, and sh-KRT15 TPC-1-Len cell lines were used for transcriptome sequencing in this study. Each treatment group contained 3 replicates.RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher), followed by library construction and transcriptome sequencing at OE Biotech (Shanghai, China). Sequencing was performed using the Illumina novaseq-6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA; https://www.illumina.com/).

Differential expression gene analysis was performed using DESeq2 (version 1.38.3; https://bioconductor.org/packages/DESeq2/) implemented in R (version 3.2.0; https://www.r-project.org/)31. The threshold for evaluating DEGs in TPC-1 and TPC-1-Len cells included the Q value < 0.05 and |log fold change (FC)|> 3. For sh-NC TPC-1-Len and sh-KRT15 TPC-1-Len cells, the thresholds included the Q value < 0.05 and |log FC|> 1. The KEGG database was employed for the enrichment analysis of DEGs32,33.

Identification of DEGs in the TCGA database

Gene expression profiles and clinical information of patients with thyroid carcinoma (THCA) were downloaded from TCGA. The limma package (version 3.54.2; https://bioconductor.org/packages/limma/) implemented in R (version 3.2.0; https://www.r-project.org/) was used to identify TC-related DEGs with thresholds including a Q value < 0.05 and |log FC|> 320. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed via GSEA software (version 4.3.2; Broad Institute; https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/)34.

Cell viability assay

Cells (2 × 103 cells/well) were placed in 96-well plates and incubated for 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 days, respectively. Then, CCK-8 solution (10 µL/well, Meilunbio) was added, and the cells were cultured for 3 h. The absorbance was subsequently measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax iD3 multifunctional Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices).

Colony formation assay

Cells were inoculated at a density of 1 × 103/well and gently mixed to ensure uniform dispersion. The culture was terminated upon the frequent observation of visible colonies. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were rinsed twice with PBS (Meilunbio). The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Meilunbio). Crystal violet (0.1%, Solarbio, Beijing, China) was added to stain colonies for 10–20 min. The colony count was performed under a microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Wound healing assay

Cells were scratched upon reaching 90% confluence. The scratched monolayer was incubated in a serum-free medium (Corning, NY, USA) for 24 h. Cell migration into the wound area was monitored using a microscope (Olympus), and images were captured to analyze wounded areas. Wound Healing Rate = (Scratch area 0 h—scratch area 24 h)/scratch area 24 h.

Transwell assay

Transwell chambers (Corning) were used to assess the migration capability of cells. Cells were seeded in serum-free medium (Corning), and nutrient-rich solution was added to the lower chamber. After 48 h of incubation, migrated cells were fixed in 4% formaldehyde (Meilunbio) and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio). Photomicrographs were captured via a microscope (Olympus).

Enzyme-linked-immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Supernatant from the mouse tumor tissue homogenates and cells were collected. Triglyceride (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) levels were determined using Human ELISA-TG and ELISA-TC kits (Meibiao, Nanjing, China) in cells and Mouse ELISA-TG and ELISA-TC kits (Meibiao) in mouse tumor tissue.

Nile red staining assay

Cells were cultured on Glass-Slides and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Meilunbio). Subsequently, the cells were stained with Nile red staining solution (1 mL, Beyotime Biotechnology), and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Images were captured and analyzed using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Xenograft mice models establishment

An experimental model for xenotransplantation was established using male BALB/C nude mice weighing 18–20 g. The mice were housed in animal facilities under controlled conditions with optimal temperature, humidity, and a consistent light–dark cycle. Newly acquired mice were acclimated to the animal facility for one week prior to experimentation. Each experimental group (sh-NC and sh-KRT15) consisted of 5 mice. TPC-1 cells in each treatment group (5 × 107 cells/mL, 200 μL) were inoculated through subcutaneous injection in the right forelimb axilla. The tumor growth was monitored regularly, and tumor volume reached approximately 50 mm3. After 22 days, mice were anesthetized with 2–3% isoflurane in oxygen. Tumors were excised and photographed under anesthesia, followed by humane euthanasia via cervical dislocation while the mice remained deeply anesthetized.The tumors’ volume and weight were recorded, and the tumor tissue was collected for analysis. All animal procedures were approved by the IACUC of the Experimental Animal Center, Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (Approval No: FUSCC-IACUC-S2024-0733), and reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines to minimize animal suffering.

Immunohistochemistry assay (IHC)

The tumor tissues were fixed with 4% formaldehyde solution (Meilunbio). Paraffin-embedded tissues were deparaffinized in Xylene (Sinopharm, Shanghai, China) and rehydrated with ethanol (Sinopharm). The sections were uniformly coated with a 3% BSA solution (Solarbio) and incubated at room temperature for 30 min to block non-specific binding. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies against anti-KRT15 and anti-DGKB dilutions is listed in Table S2.

After washing, sections were treated with either Anti-Rabbit IgG (1:1000, Abcam) or Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (1:1000, Abcam) secondary antibodies for 1 h. The chromogen was developed by adding a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB, DAKO, Shanghai, China) solution. Following DAB staining, the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, mounted with coverslips, and examined under a microscope (SAGA, Suzhou, Jiangsu, China). Additionally, a tissue microarray (TMA) containing 58 cancer-adjacent and 58 tumor tissues of patients with PTC was purchased from OUTDO Biotechnology (Shanghai, China) and analyzed.

Statistical analysis

The data were presented as Mean ± Standard Deviation (SD). The GraphPad Prism 8 software (version 8.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA; https://www.graphpad.com/) and SPSS 26.0 (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA; https://www.ibm.com/) were used for visualization and data analysis, respectively. A two-group comparison was conducted using the t-test, while a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for comparisons among multiple groups, followed by the post-hoc Tukey test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
