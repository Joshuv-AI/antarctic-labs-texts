An optimal experimental design approach to sensor placement in continuous stochastic filtering
Sahani Pathiraja, Claudia Schillings, Philipp Wacker

Abstract
Sequential filtering and spatial inverse problems assimilate data points distributed either temporally (in the case of filtering) or spatially (in the case of spatial inverse problems). Sometimes it is possible to choose the position of these data points (which we call sensors here) in advance, with the goal of maximising the expected information gain (or a different metric of performance) from future data, and this leads to an Optimal Experimental Design (OED) problem. Here we revisit an interpretation of optimising sensor placement as an integration with respect to a general probability measure ξ \xi . This generalises the problem of discrete-time sensor placement (which corresponds to the special case where the probability measure is a mixture of Diracs) to an infinite-dimensional, but mathematically more well-behaved setting. We focus on the continuous-time stochastic filtering setting, whose solution is governed by the Zakai equation. We derive an expression for the Fréchet derivative of a general OED utility functional, the key to which is an adjoint (backwards in time) differential equation. This paves the way for utilising new gradient-based methods for solving the corresponding optimisation problem, as a potentially more efficient alternative to (semi-)discrete optimisation methods, e.g. based on greedy insertion and deletion of sensor placements.

Main idea, prior work, and contributions

This work focuses on parameter estimation problems with either a temporal dependency (a filtering or data assimilation problem) or a spatial varying parameter (more commonly called an inverse problem). 1.1
Setup of guiding example: Monitoring logistic growth

We will use the following simple one-dimensional parameter estimaton problem as a guiding example throughout the manuscript, as a way of motivating ideas and notation.

Guiding example

We consider an unknown parameter which we model as a time-independent state
X(t)=x_{0}
, which we interpret as the rate of an logistic growth process of a concentration
Z(t)=g(X(t),t)=\frac{\exp(x_{0}t)}{z_{0}^{-1}-1+\exp(x_{0}t)}
, where the observation map is
g(x,t)=\frac{\exp(xt)}{z_{0}^{-1}-1+\exp(xt)}
, with a fixed and known
z_{0}\in(0,1)
.

Very generally, our goal will be to find the best experimental setup such that we can learn as much as possible about the value of the unknown growth parameter
x_{0}
. As a starting point (although we will generalise this considerably), we assume that we collect exactly one observation of form

Y=g(X_{\tau},\tau)+\varepsilon,

(1.1)

where
\tau
is the time at which we measure the concentration
z
. This problem has a closed form solution: If we use D-optimality (see, e.g.,
[
Puk06
]
) as a criterion for optimal experimental design, we should be choosing
\tau=\frac{\log(z_{0}^{-1}-1)}{x_{0}}
(note that this ignores prior information on
x_{0}
). Intuitively, this is the time at which the “fan of trajectories” spreads out the most, so pointwise observation at this time is bound to give a high amount of information about the underlying parameter.

This approach does not generalise well to optimising several evaluation times
\tau_{i}
where we collect data
Y_{i}=g(X_{\tau_{i}},\tau_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}
simultaneously, and becomes either a high-dimensional problem with a lot of unusable symmetry (since permutation of the
\tau_{i}
leads to the same outcome), or to a tricky discrete optimisation problem (if we define a finite set of viable time points
T
in advance, and we are to pick an optimal subset).

Our goal in this manuscript is to relax the data acquisition process from the discrete case of individual observations to continuous-time monitoring, but allowing for a time-inhomogeneous “sensor density”. Put succinctly, we start with (
1.1
), which can be thought of as an observation model with sensor schedule
\xi=\delta_{\tau}
, a Dirac measure at position
t=\tau
. Then we are going to generalise this to the case of several pointwise observations
Y_{i}=g(X_{t_{i}},t_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}
(with possibly variable observational noise strength depending on a parameter
\alpha_{i}
), which corresponds to a sensor distribution of
\xi=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\delta_{t_{i}}
, an empirical probability measure at times
t_{i}
such that its total mass
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=1
sums to one. Finally, we will replace
\xi
by a general probability distribution, usually assuming that
\xi
has a Lebesgue density. We think of
\xi
as the experimental design variable and will call it the
sensor schedule
. The fact that
\xi
is a probability measure represents bounded resources. After setting up an optimality criterion (measuring how well the experiment allows us to learn about underlying parameters or states, depending on the context), this will allow us to formulate and solve the associated optimalisation problem of finding the optimal sensor schedule
\xi
.

Before discussing the continuous time case, we remind ourselves of a few basic concepts from stochastic filtering, see for example
[
BC09
]
for a comprehensive overview.

1.2
Background on Stochastic filtering

We denote all variables in this section with a tilde, such as
\tilde{X}(t)
, since we will derive modified versions of these formulae later on.

Nonlinear continuous-time filtering problem

We consider a latent stochastic process
\tilde{X}(t)
(the signal) with infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{L}
and data (the observation) given by

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{Z}(t)=g(\tilde{X}(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\,\Gamma^{1/2}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(1.2)

The conditional distribution of
\tilde{X}(t)
given the path
\{\tilde{Z}(s)\}_{s\leq t}
is given by
\tilde{q}_{t}
and evolves according to the Kushner-Stratonovich equation

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{q}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{L}^{\star}\tilde{q}_{t})(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\tilde{q}_{t}(x)\left(g(x)-\bar{g}_{t}\right)^{\top}\Gamma^{-1}(t)\left(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{Z}(t)-\bar{g}_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right),

where
\bar{g}_{t}=\int g(x)q_{t}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
. It is often easier (computationally less intensive and numerically more stable) to work with an unnormalised density
\tilde{p}_{t}
instead, which is governed by the Zakai equation

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{p}_{t}(x)=(\mathcal{L}^{\star}\tilde{p}_{t})(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\tilde{p}_{t}(x)g(x)^{\top}\Gamma^{-1}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{Z}(t),

(1.3)

where we can always compute the normalised density via
\tilde{q}_{t}(x)=\tilde{p}_{t}(x)/\int\tilde{p}_{t}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y
from
\tilde{p}_{t}
. We find that numerical stability can be improved further by tracking the unnormalised log-density: This makes sure that (taking the exponential),
\tilde{p}_{t}(x)
is always non-negative, which is not the case for naive implementations of (
1.3
). Using a simple Itō-type computation, we have
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\log\tilde{p}_{t}(x)={p_{t}(x)}^{-1}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{p}_{t}(x)}-\tfrac{1}{2}{p_{t}(x)}^{-2}{(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p_{t}(x))^{2}}
, so

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\log\tilde{p}_{t}(x)=\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\star}\tilde{p}_{t}(x)}{\tilde{p}_{t}(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{2}\left\|g(x)\right\|_{\Gamma(t)}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+g(x)^{\top}\Gamma^{-1}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{Z}(t).

(1.4)

It is also possible to derive a similar form of the logarithmic Kushner-Stratonovich equation, which is omitted here.

Linear continuous-time filtering problem.

If the signal and observation dynamics have linear and Gaussian structure,

\begin{split}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{X}(t)&=L\tilde{X}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\Sigma^{1/2}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B_{t}\\
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{Z}(t)&=H\tilde{X}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\Gamma^{1/2}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t},\end{split}

(1.5)

then the conditional distribution of
\tilde{X}(t)
given the path
\{\tilde{Z}(s)\}_{s\leq t}
is given by
\tilde{p}_{t}=\mathcal{N}(\tilde{m}(t),\tilde{C}(t))
, where

\begin{split}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{m}(t)&=L\tilde{m}(t)+\tilde{C}(t)H^{\top}\Gamma(t)^{-1}\left(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{Z}(t)-H\tilde{m}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)\\
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{C}(t)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}&=L\tilde{C}(t)+\tilde{C}(t)L^{\top}+\Sigma(t)-\tilde{C}(t)H^{\top}\Gamma^{-1}(t)H\tilde{C}(t)\end{split}

(1.6)

These are the celebrated Kalman-Bucy filtering equations.

Nonlinear and linear discrete-time filtering problem.

We can also accommodate the setup where observations are not collected continuously, but at discrete time steps: In this case we will use
y_{i}
for the discrete measurements obtained via

Y_{t_{i}}=g(X_{t_{i}})+\Gamma^{1/2}(t_{i})\varepsilon_{t_{i}}.

(1.7)

Filtering is then done by evolving the filtering distribution according to the Fokker-Planck equation, and then assimilating data momentarily (and discontinuously) as they arrive, via Bayes’ law. If the signal and observation dynamics are linear, then we recover the usual Kalman (discrete-time) filter.

1.3
Sensor placement as an optimal experimental design task

All of these filtering settings mentioned take for granted a given way of information acquisition. Either, continuously (in the case of continuous-time filtering in the form
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{Z}(t)=g(\tilde{X}(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\Gamma^{1/2}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}
), or at given discrete timepoints (in the case of discrete-time filtering in the form
Y_{i}=g(\tilde{X}_{t_{i}})+\Gamma^{1/2}(t_{i})\varepsilon_{i}
).

We can imagine, though, that conscious design of the observational setup (or schedule) can provide more informative data, e.g. by contracting the filtering distribution more strongly.

If observations arrive at discrete time steps, it is conceivable that there are time steps which are more suitable than others. An obvious example for this is when the observational noise covariance
\Gamma(t)
is time-dependent: If we pick observational time points
t_{i}
where
\Gamma(t_{i})
is small, we will likely get more informative data. Or, in our guiding example of logistic growth, observation times
\tau\approx 0
or
\tau\gg 10
are likely to not provide much information, either, since
g(X_{\tau},\tau)
is essentially a deterministic value (
0
, or
1
, respectively) regardless of the value of
x_{0}
.

If observation is performed continuously, then this is less obvious: How can we observe “better than continuously?” We need to keep in mind, though, that even continuous observation of the form (
2.6
) does not translate into perfect information: Data is collected continuously, but it is simultaneously perturbed by measurement noise. We can now imagine concentrating our “observational attention” towards times where, for example,
\Gamma(t)
is small in comparison (or, as in our guiding example, towards times where we expect to learn more about the signal process). This leads to data collection
more strongly concentrated
on some intervals than others. We can imagine changing from “uniform observation” (which is the standard set-up for stochastic filtering) to “observation with variable sensor strength” (mentally replacing a uniform distribution with, e.g., a Gaussian distribution). We model variability in sensor strength with a probability measure
\xi
, which we call the
sensor schedule
. Intuitively,
\int_{s}^{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi
quantifies the fraction of “sensor budget” allocated to the time interval
[s,t]
.

Optimal sensor design for stochastic filtering

2.1
Preamble: Allocating a sensor budget

To get ourselves familiar with the general idea of sensor placement, let’s consider the following straightforward setup where we are trying to optimise spending a “sensor budget” between two fixed sources of data. Assume we want to infer the value of an unknown parameter
X\in\mathbb{R}
and we have two ways of getting information about
X
,

\displaystyle W_{1}

\displaystyle=X+\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{1}}}\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle W_{2}

\displaystyle=X+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{2}}}\varepsilon_{2}.

This means that we assume that
\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is standard normal observation error, and the data
W_{i}
arises from perturbing the unknown
X
with additive Gaussian noise scaled by a standard deviation
\gamma_{i}
depending on
i
, and additionally on a term
\alpha_{i}
that models a budgetary constraint, where we say that

\alpha_{i}\geq 0\quad\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=1.

(2.1)

Let’s for concreteness assume that
\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}
, i.e., the first datapoint is inherently more reliable than the second datapoint.
Obviously, letting both
\alpha_{i}\to+\infty
would be optimal from a statistical perspective (since
W_{i}\to X
is going to converge to a perfect observation of
X
), but the budget constraints forbid this. One way to interpret this budgetary constraint is (noting that
\alpha_{i}^{-1}
is a variance-type quantity, so
\alpha_{i}
is a precision-type quantity) as an upper bound on
total precision
.

The question now is,
how should we set
\alpha_{i}
(in alignment with the budgetary constraints (
2.1
)) such that our information about
X
is maximised. One way to think about the quality of data is the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), which in this case (up to a constant) is
\gamma_{i}/\sqrt{\alpha_{i}}
for the
i
-th datapoint.

The most straightforward way of solving this question is to make sure that the likelihood
\mathbb{P}(W_{1},W_{2}|X)
is as sharply peaked as possible. A quick calculation using quadratic expansion shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W_{1},W_{2}|X)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(W_{1}|X)\mathbb{P}(W_{2}|X)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\gamma_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\gamma_{2}^{2}}\right]\,\left(X-\frac{\alpha_{1}/\gamma_{1}^{2}W_{1}+\alpha_{2}/\gamma_{2}^{2}W_{2}}{\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\gamma_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\gamma_{2}^{2}}}\right)^{2}\right).

Making the likelihood as sharply peaked as possible amounts to the maximisation problem

\displaystyle\max\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\gamma_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\gamma_{2}^{2}},\quad\alpha_{i}\geq 0,\,\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=1,

which can be seen to be solved by
\alpha_{1}=1
and
\alpha_{2}=0
(because of our assumption that
\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}
).

The way
W_{i}
is defined is slightly inconvenient: For
\alpha_{2}\to 0
(which is the optimal choice),
W_{2}\to\pm\infty
(with unclear sign). By multiplying both sides by a factor of
\alpha_{i}
, we get the following, completely equivalent, model:

Y_{i}:=\alpha_{i}W_{i}=\alpha_{i}X+\gamma_{i}\sqrt{\alpha_{i}}\varepsilon_{i},

(2.2)

which doesn’t show this behaviour. Since
\alpha_{i}
is, at time of observation, a fixed and deterministic number, it does not matter whether we work with
W_{i}
or
Y_{i}
,
2
2

2

and the Bayesian posterior would be identical, since
\mathbb{P}(X|W_{1},W_{2})=\mathbb{P}(X|Y_{1},Y_{2})
but
Y_{i}
has bounded second moments for all possible budgetary allocations
\alpha_{i}
. Also, the SNR remains
\gamma_{i}/\sqrt{\alpha_{i}}
under this constant rescaling.

We will now generalise these ideas to the setting where we observe a time-dependent quantity

(rather than a constant value

), and then take a continuous-time limit, starting our consideration with the form (

) right away.

Derivation of filtering equations with inhomogeneous sensor density

We consider a latent stochastic process with an infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{L}
. The most general setting in this context will be a diffusion process
X(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for all
t\in[0,T]
,
3
3

3

We assume from now on that the filtering problem happens on a fixed time interval, i.e. we can anticipate data to arrive within

with

\xi=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\delta_{t_{i}}(t).

(2.5)

In (

Following the standard approach in going from discrete to continuous time in stochastic filtering, we now define a cumulative observation process as
Z(t)=\sum_{i:t_{i}\leq t}y_{t_{i}}
which is characterised as

\displaystyle Z(t)-Z(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i:s<t_{i}\leq t}\alpha_{i}g(X_{t_{i}})+\sum_{i:s<t_{i}\leq t}\sqrt{\alpha_{i}}\,\Gamma^{1/2}(t_{i})\varepsilon_{t_{i}}=\int_{s}^{t}g(X(r))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi(r)+\Delta_{\xi}(t)-\Delta_{\xi}(s),

\Delta_{\xi}(t):=\sum_{i:t_{i}\leq t}\sqrt{\alpha_{i}}\,\Gamma^{1/2}(t_{i})\varepsilon_{t_{i}}
. The object
\Delta_{\xi}(t)
is a Gaussian random process such that
\Delta_{\xi}(s)
and
\Delta_{\xi}(t)-\Delta_{\xi}(s)
are independent for
s\leq t
. The Kolmogorov extension theorem states that
\Delta_{\xi}(t)
is uniquely characterised by its finite-dimensional distributions, and we can define:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\xi
be a probability measure on
([0,T],\mathcal{B}([0,T]))
and
\Gamma:[0,T]\to\mathcal{M}^{+}
a time-dependent covariance matrix. Then we define
\Delta_{\xi}
as the unique Gaussian process satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\Delta_{\xi}(t))

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{\xi}(r)\Delta_{\xi}(t)^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i:t_{i}\leq\min\{r,t\}}\alpha_{i}\Gamma(t_{i})=\int_{0}^{\min\{r,t\}}\Gamma(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi(s).

Remark 2.2
.

This setting encompasses two important special cases:
\xi=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\delta_{t_{i}}(t)
(empirical measure, discrete probability distribution) and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi(t)=\xi(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
(absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure).

We can write both of these versions notationally as
\Delta_{\xi}(t)=\mathcal{N}(0,\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi(u))
, where we informally write
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Delta_{\xi}(t)
for the infinitesimal increments.

Definition 2.3

(Scheduled filtering problem)
.

We call inference of
X(t)
,
t\in[0,T]
from observations
Z(t)
, where

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X(t)

\displaystyle=b(X(t),t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(X(t),t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B_{t}

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z(t)

\displaystyle=g(x(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi(t)+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Delta_{\xi}(t)

the scheduled filtering problem, where we call the probability measure
\xi
the sensor schedule.

If
\xi
has a density (notationally identified with
\xi(t)
), then by the Itō isometry,
\Delta_{\xi}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\xi(u)}\,\Gamma^{1/2}(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(u)
for standard Brownian motion
W
, so we can also write
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Delta_{\xi}(t)=\sqrt{\xi(t)}\Gamma^{1/2}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(t)
. Then
Z(t)
is a diffusion process of form

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z(t)=g(X(t))\xi(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\xi(t)}\,\Gamma^{1/2}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(t).

(2.6)

Remark 2.4
.

It is tempting to view (
2.6
) as a time-rescaling of the usual measurement process in stochastic filtering,

\displaystyle dZ(t)=g(X(t))dt+\Gamma^{1/2}(t)dW(t).

(2.7)

This interpretation does not seem to hold in an obvious way, due to the dependence of
\tilde{Z}(t)
on the signal process
\tilde{X}(t)
, as can be seen by the calculations in appendix
C
.

Whether we think of the sensor schedule
\xi
as being a singular or absolutely continuous measure, we can write the Kushner-Stratonovich equation for the filtering problem (
2.6
) formally as

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}q_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{\star}q_{t}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\xi(t)^{-1}\,q_{t}(x)\left(g(x)\xi(t)-\bar{g}_{t}\xi(t)\right)^{\top}\Gamma^{-1}(t)\left(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z(t)-\bar{g}_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi(t)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{\star}q_{t}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+q_{t}(x)\left(g(x)-\bar{g}_{t}\right)^{\top}\Gamma^{-1}(t)\left(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z(t)-g_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi(t)\right).

We will prefer to work with the Zakai equation instead,

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{L}^{\star}p_{t})(x)+p_{t}(x)g(x)^{\top}\Gamma(t)^{-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z(t)

which governs an unnormalised density
p_{t}(x)
where
q_{t}(x)=p_{t}(x)/\int p_{t}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y
. The logarithmic form of the Zakai equation is given by

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\log p_{t}(x)=\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\star}p_{t}(x)}{p_{t}(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{2}\left\|g(x)\right\|_{\Gamma(t)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi(t)+g(x)^{\top}\Gamma^{-1}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z(t)

(2.8)

The probability measure
\xi
represents an
experimental design
governing the “observational attention” (or “sensor schedule”) to specific subsets of the observational time horizon, either as specific points at which to measure the signal (in the discrete-time setting), or (in the continuous-time setting) as a continuous concentration of “observational attention” on the full interval. The fact that
\xi
is a probability measure (and thus integrates to
1
) represents a budget limitation.

We continue with our guiding example from above.

Guiding example

Since
X(t)=x_{0}
is constant, the generator is
\mathcal{L}=0
. The generalised measurement model with sensor schedule
\xi
(assumed to have a Lebesgue density, also written as
\xi(t)
) yields an observation process

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z(t)=g(X(t),t)\xi(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\gamma\sqrt{\xi(t)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}

(2.9)

For example, if
\xi(t)=\delta_{\tau}(t)
, then this (informally) collapses to the pointwise observation problem via
y=\exp(-x_{0}\cdot\tau)+\gamma\epsilon
, where
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and where
z(s)=0
for
s<\tau
, and
z(s)=y
for
s\geq\tau
. The log-Zakai equation is

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\log p_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}g(x,t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z(t)-\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}g(x,t)^{2}\xi(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(2.10)

The next figure shows how the observation process
Z(t)
responds to a specific choice of
\xi

\xi=\mathrm{Exp}(1)
, and the right column featuring a uniform
\xi=\mathrm{Unif}[2,3]

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z

2.3
The optimal experimental design problem

We now pose an OED problem by associating a utility
U
with desirable properties of
p_{T}
. This could be, for example, the KL-divergence between
p_{T}
and
p_{0}
(as a measure for contraction, which we typically want to be as large as possible). An alternative choice is the variance of
p_{T}
(also as as a measure of contraction, where we typically want the variance to be as small as possible). Other utilities are of course possible, and inspiration may be taken from other classical measures of uncertainty known to the literature of OED
[
Puk06
]
.

In any case, the utility will be a function of the posterior distribution which in turn depends on the choice of sensor design
\xi
. Choosing a design leading to an optimal utility defines our optimal experimental design problem.

To fix notation, let
\mathcal{L}
be the infinitesimal generator of the latent process
X(t)
and
U_{1}:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}
be a utility function to be minimised. The OED problem that we want to solve is finding the best design
\xi
with density
\xi
such that the expected utility is minimised:

\displaystyle\min_{\xi\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}U_{1}(p),\quad\text{ where }p_{t}\text{ solves }

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{\star}p_{t}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+p_{t}(x)\left(g(x)-\bar{g}_{t}\right)^{\top}\Gamma^{-1}(t)\left(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z(t)-\bar{g}_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi(t)\right),\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z(t)

\displaystyle=g(X(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi(t)+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Delta_{\xi}(t),

where
\Delta_{\xi}
was defined in definition
2.1
.

Remark 2.5
.

The expectation is to be taken across path realisations of
X(t)
and
Z(t)
, similar to how EIG in standard OED contexts is computed by taking the expectation with respect to both parameters and data.

Remark 2.6
.

In practice we will use the log-form of the Zakai equation instead of the Kushner-Stratonovich equation.

A suitable candidate for the utility function here would be to choose
U_{1}(p)=-\alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{T}\mid\mid q_{0})-\int_{0}^{T}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{t}\mid\mid q_{0})
, where
q_{t}
is the normalised version of
p_{t}
. This optimisation problem strives to maximise a certain combination (mediated by
\alpha>0
) of the expected information gain between the final filtering distribution
q_{T}
and the prior
q_{0}
, and a similar integral-type term for the full filtering path.

Guiding example

We will now convince ourselves that choosing a suitable sensor schedule
\xi
can indeed make a difference in terms of the utility gained. Here we choose
U_{1}(p)=-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{T}\mid\mid q_{0}),
which measures the information gain about the unknown parameter
x_{0}
after assimilating the full data
Z(t)
governed by (
2.9
), via solving the Zakai equation (
2.10
). Since we are still yet to develop the machinery for optimising
U_{1}
in the next section, we just compare four different choices of
\xi
, where
\xi_{i}=\mathcal{N}(1.5+i,0.5)
, for
i=0,1,2,3
. The top left figure shows these four candidate sensor schedules
\xi_{i}
, with the resulting observation paths shown in the top right figure. We can clearly see how the paths of
Z(t)
have “increased activity” in areas where
\xi_{i}
supports probability mass. If we solve the log-Zakai equations for each of these observational paths, we get the corresponding final-time filtering distributions
p_{T}
(lower left figure). We can see that setting
0
and
3
(blue and red) have relatively little posterior contraction (as compared to the prior distribution shown with a dashed line). Settings
1
and
2
(yellow and green) seem to have collected more information from the data assimilation procedure. This is summarised in a plot of the value utility function for these four settings, which shows that these settings indeed lead to the largest KL-divergence among the one compared.

We note that the specific outcomes for
Z(t),p_{T},
and the value of the utility are path-dependent, and the utility would strictly speaking need to be averaged over many such runs so we get a better (lower variance) estimate for
\mathbb{E}U_{1}(p)
, but we forgo this for the sake of simplicity.

We now consider the special case where the system dynamics and the observation operator are linear, and all errors of stochasticity are Gaussian. This yields much simpler weighted Kalman-Bucy equations.

2.4
Linear Gaussian case

If the signal has linear and Gaussian dynamics, and the observations are of the form (
2.6
), with
g(x)=Hx
for a matrix
H
,

\begin{split}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X(t)&=L(t)X(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\Sigma^{1/2}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B_{t}\\
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z(t)&=\xi(t)HX(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\xi(t)}\Gamma^{1/2}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t},\end{split}

(2.11)

for
t\in[0,T]
, then
X(t)|\{Z(s)\}_{s\leq t}
follows a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(m(t),C(t))
for all
t
, with corresponding mean
m(t)
and covariance
C(t)
evolving according to the Kalman-Bucy equations

\begin{split}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(t)&=L(t)m(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+C(t)H^{\top}\Gamma(t)^{-1}\left(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z(t)-Hm(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi(t)\right)\\
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}C_{\xi}(t)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}&=L(t)C_{\xi}(t)+C_{\xi}(t)L(t)^{\top}+\Sigma(t)-\xi(t)C_{\xi}(t)H^{\top}\Gamma^{-1}(t)HC_{\xi}(t)\end{split}

(2.12)

We now proceed in setting up the OED problem of optimal sensor scheduling. For simplicity, we consider the case where the utility is a function of the filtering covariance alone, and where
\xi
is absolutely continuous with density, in which case we want to solve

\displaystyle\min_{\xi\in\mathcal{P}}U_{2}(C_{\xi})

From now on we will suppress the dependence of
C_{\xi}
on
\xi
notationally.

We want to note here that in this specific case, the utility is a deterministic quantity in terms of
\xi
, independent of the actual observational data (since the Riccati equation governing
C
does not react to neither the actual underlying path of the latent state
X(t)
, nor the observation
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z(t)
), so there is no need to take the expectation over possible realisations of this data.

A suitable utility function here arises from
U_{2}(C)=\mathrm{trace}(C(T))
, so the optimisation problem is aiming towards minimising the variance of the Gaussian filtering distribution at the final time
T
.

3
Solving the OED problem using adjoint derivatives

We have now formulated the OED problem of finding the best sensor schedule
\xi
. What remains to be done is to derive an expression for the gradient of the utility function with respect to
\xi
, so we can set up efficient numerical methods. We will do this now, with the help of the machinery of “adjoints”, well-known in the optimal control community
[
GS02
,
GG06
]
. A recapitulation of this concept can be found in appendix
B
, and now we will begin with the linear and Gaussian case, and then derive the gradient in the more general nonlinear case afterwards.

3.1
Linear Gaussian case

Lemma 3.1
.

We consider a utility
U_{2}(C)=U_{\mathrm{final}}(C(T))+\int_{0}^{T}U_{\mathrm{int}}(C(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
for some smooth functions
U_{\mathrm{final}},U_{\mathrm{int}}
, where

\displaystyle\dot{C}(t)=f(C(t),\xi(t),t),\qquad C(0)=C_{0}

(3.1)

is a matrix-valued ODE. Then the gradient of
U_{2}
with respect to
\xi
can be evaluated via
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}U_{2}(\xi)=\eta
where

\displaystyle\eta(t)=(\partial_{\xi}f(C(t),\xi(t),t))^{\star}\Lambda(t)

and

\displaystyle-\dot{\Lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=(\partial_{C}f(C(t),\xi(t),t))^{\star}\Lambda(t)+\partial_{C}U_{\mathrm{int}}(t,C(t)),

\displaystyle\Lambda(T)

\displaystyle=\partial_{C}U_{\mathrm{final}}(C(T))

Proof.

We set
H(\dot{C},C,\xi,t)=\dot{C}(t)-f(C(t),\xi(t),t)
(which means that
H=0
characterises solutions
C
of (
3.1
)). This defines a Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}(\dot{C},C,\xi,\Lambda,\Omega)

\displaystyle=U_{\mathrm{final}}(C(T))+\int_{0}^{T}U_{\mathrm{int}}(t,C(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{T}\left\llangle\Lambda(t),H(\dot{C},C,\xi,t)\right\rrangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\llangle\Omega,C(0)-C_{0}\rrangle-\nu\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{T}\xi(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-1\right)-\int_{0}^{T}\mu(t)\xi(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

for a non-negative Lagrange multiplier
\mu
,

where
\Lambda\in\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathcal{M})
and
\Omega\in\mathcal{M}
are matrix-valued Lagrangian multipliers and
\nu
is a real-valued Lagrangian multiplier. Note that the inner product here is
\llangle M,N\rrangle=\sum_{ij}M_{ij}N_{ij}=\mathrm{trace}(M^{\top}N)
. Now we can compute the Fréchet derivative of the Lagrangian with respect to the design
\xi
as (we write
H(\dot{C},C,\xi,t)=H
for convenience)

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}(\dot{C},C,\xi,\Lambda,\Omega)[h]=\llangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{C}U_{\mathrm{final}}(C(T)),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(T)[h]\rrangle+\int_{0}^{T}\llangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{C}U_{\mathrm{int}}(t,C(t)),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(t)[h]\rrangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\left\llangle\Lambda(t),\partial_{\dot{C}}H\left[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}\dot{C}(t)[h]\right]+\partial_{C}H\left[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(t)[h]\right]+\partial_{\xi}H[h]\right\rrangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle-\langle\Omega,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(0)[h]\rangle-\nu\int_{0}^{T}h(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{T}\mu(t)h(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

Now we use the fact that
\partial_{\dot{C}}H=I
, and
\partial_{C}H=-\partial_{C}f,
and
\partial_{\xi}H=-\partial_{\xi}f
:

\displaystyle=\llangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{C}U_{\mathrm{final}}(C(T)),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(T)[h]\rrangle+\int_{0}^{T}\llangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{C}U_{\mathrm{int}}(t,C(t)),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(t)[h]\rrangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{T}\llangle\Lambda(t),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}C(t)[h]\rrangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\left\llangle\Lambda(t),\partial_{C}f\left[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(t)[h]\right]+\partial_{\xi}f[h]\right\rrangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\llangle\Omega,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(0)[h]\rrangle-\int_{0}^{T}h(t)(\nu+\mu(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

Integration by parts (with respect to
t
) on the second integral, and using
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(0)=0
yields

\displaystyle=\llangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{C}U_{\mathrm{final}}(C(T)),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(T)[h]\rrangle+\int_{0}^{T}\llangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{C}U_{\mathrm{int}}(t,C(t)),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(t)[h]\rrangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\left[\llangle\Lambda(t),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(t)[h]\rrangle\right]_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\llangle\dot{\Lambda}(t),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(t)[h]\rrangle+\left\llangle\Lambda(t),\partial_{C}f\left[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(t)[h]\right]+\partial_{\xi}f[h]\right\rrangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{T}(\nu+\mu(t))h(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\llangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{C}U_{\mathrm{final}}(C(T))-\Lambda(T),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(T)[h]\rrangle+\int_{0}^{T}\llangle\dot{\Lambda}(t)+[\partial_{C}f]^{\star}\Lambda+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{C}U_{\mathrm{int}}(t,C(t)),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}C(t)[h]\rrangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\llangle\Lambda(t),\partial_{\xi}f[h]\rrangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{T}(\nu+\mu(t))h(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

Now we set
\Lambda(T)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{C}U_{\mathrm{final}}(C(T))
,
-\dot{\Lambda}(t)=(\partial_{C}f)^{\star}\Lambda(t)+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{C}U_{\mathrm{int}}(t,C(t)),
and use the fact that
\partial_{\xi}f:T_{\xi}\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{M}
, so
(\partial_{\xi}f)^{\star}:\mathcal{M}^{\star}\to T_{\xi}^{\star}\mathcal{P}
, and thus (since we can identify the Euclidean space
\mathcal{M}
with its dual)

\int_{0}^{T}\llangle\Lambda(t),\partial_{\xi}f[h]\rrangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}(\partial_{\xi}f)^{\star}[\Lambda(t)]\,h(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

which simplifies the Lagrangian derivative to
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}\mathbf{L}(\dot{C},C,\xi,\Lambda,\Omega)[h]=\int_{0}^{T}\left\{(\partial_{\xi}f)^{\star}[\Lambda(t)]-\nu-\mu(t)\right\}\,h(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}(\dot{C},C,\xi,\Lambda,\Omega)=(\partial_{\xi}f)^{\star}[\Lambda(t)]-\nu-\mu(t)

where
\nu,\mu(t)
are chosen to satisfy the constraints on
\xi
to be a valid probability density.

∎

Lemma 3.2
.

We consider the linear, Gaussian setup (
2.11
) with Kalman-Bucy equations (
2.12
) and set
U:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
to be a utility function. We abbreviate
A(t):=H^{\top}\Gamma(t)^{-1}H
. Then the gradient of
V(\xi):=U_{\mathrm{final}}(C(T))+\int_{0}^{T}U_{\mathrm{int}}(C(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
is given by

\displaystyle\delta_{\xi}V(\xi)(t)

\displaystyle=-\llangle\Lambda(t),C(t)A(t)C(t)\rrangle

and where

\displaystyle-\dot{\Lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=L^{\top}\Lambda(t)+\Lambda(t)L-\xi A(t)C(t)\Lambda(t)-\xi\Lambda C(t)A(t)+\partial_{C}U_{\mathrm{int}}(t,C(t))

\displaystyle\Lambda(T)

\displaystyle=\partial_{C}U_{\mathrm{final}}(C(T)).

In particular:

•

If
U(M)=\mathrm{trace}(M)
, then
\Lambda(T)=I
.

•

If
U(M)=\log\det(M)
, then
\Lambda(T)=2C^{-1}(T)-C^{-1}(T)\circ I
.

Proof.

In this proof, we will sometimes drop dependency on
t
notationally if convenient. Since
f(\dot{C},C,\xi,t)=LC+CL^{\top}+\Sigma-\xi(t)CAC
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{kl}}{\partial C_{ij}}

\displaystyle=L_{ki}\delta_{lj}+\delta_{ik}L_{lj}-\xi(t)\delta_{lj}(CA)_{ki}-\xi(t)\delta_{ki}(AC)_{jl}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{kl}}{\partial\xi}

\displaystyle=(-C(t)AC(t))_{kl}

Then from lemma
3.1
,

\displaystyle\llangle\Lambda,\partial_{C}fM\rrangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{kl}\Lambda_{kl}\sum_{ij}\frac{\partial f_{kl}}{\partial C_{ij}}M_{ij}=\sum_{ij}M_{ij}\underbrace{\sum_{kl}\frac{\partial f_{kl}}{\partial C_{ij}}\Lambda_{kl}}_{[(\partial_{C}f)^{\star}(\Lambda)]_{ij}}=\llangle(\partial_{C}f)^{\star}(\Lambda),M\rrangle,

which leads to

\displaystyle-\dot{\Lambda}_{ij}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{kl}\frac{\partial f_{kl}}{\partial C_{ij}}\Lambda_{kl}+(\partial_{C}U_{\mathrm{int}}(t,C(t)))_{ij}=\sum_{kl}\left[L_{ki}\delta_{lj}+\delta_{ik}L_{lj}-\xi\delta_{lj}(CA)_{ki}-\xi\delta_{ki}(AC)_{jl}\right]\Lambda_{kl}+(\partial_{C}U_{\mathrm{int}}(t,C(t)))_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}L^{\top}_{ik}\Lambda_{kj}+\sum_{l}\Lambda_{il}L_{lj}-\sum_{k}(CA)^{\top}_{ik}\Lambda_{kj}-\sum_{l}\Lambda_{il}(AC)^{\top}_{lj}+(\partial_{C}U_{\mathrm{int}}(t,C(t)))_{ij}

\displaystyle=\left[L^{\top}\Lambda+\Lambda L-\xi(CA)^{\top}\Lambda-\xi\Lambda(AC)^{\top}\right]_{ij}+(\partial_{C}U_{\mathrm{int}}(t,C(t)))_{ij}

Which means that (using
A=A^{\top},C=C^{\top}

\displaystyle-\dot{\Lambda}=L^{\top}\Lambda+\Lambda L-\xi AC\Lambda-\xi\Lambda CA+\partial_{C}U_{\mathrm{int}}(t,C(t))

The gradient of

is then given by
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}V(\xi)[h]=\int_{0}^{T}\llangle\Lambda(t),C(t)A(t)C(t)\rrangle h(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,
so

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{\xi}V(\xi)(t)=-\llangle\Lambda(t),C(t)A(t)C(t)\rrangle,

where

3.2
Nonlinear case

For simplicity, we are going to focus on the case where the hidden signal

Lemma 3.3
.

We assume that the hidden signal is a stochastic process

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X(t)=f(X(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\Sigma^{1/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B_{t}

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z(t)=g(X(t))\xi(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\xi(t)}\,\Gamma^{1/2}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(3.2)

We further assume that we have a utility which is a function of the posterior distribution after assimilation of all the data, i.e.

V(\xi)=\mathbb{E}U(p),

(3.3)

where
p_{t}
is the filtering distribution of
X(t)|\{Z(s)\}_{s=0}^{t}

5
Conclusion

We have shown how the problem of optimal sensor placement can be applied to continuous stochastic filtering, and how the gradient of an utility functional can be recovered using the adjoint method. We demonstrated in a few simple cases that this allows us to find provably optimal experimental designs. There are several avenues for future work, e.g.,

•

Rather than performing a simple projected gradient descent (which ignores the fact that the utility function is an expectation of a random variable), optimisation algorithms more adapted to this stochastic optimisation setup can be explored, such as the SPSA algorithm
[
Spa87
]
.

•

Embedding probability distributions in a manifold structure allows development of gradient flow algorithms based on Wasserstein or Rao-Fisher flow. The former would allow application of particle methods.

•

In practice, the Zakai (or Kushner-Stratonovich equation) is rarely solved computationally, instead, tools like the particle filter are used. Extending the theory in the manuscript to developing an adjoint expression for the gradient of a utility functional depending on a particle filter instead of the Zakai equation would be a very interesting avenue for future research.

•

We started with the problem of finding a single (or several finitely many) observation points, and then relaxed this setting to smooth sensor schedules
\xi
in the form of a probability distribution. At this point it is unclear how optimality of the smooth density translates into optimality of discrete observation points (e.g., obtained via sampling from this
\xi
).

•

We focussed on finding the optimal sensor distribution in advance, but of course incoming data will continuously provide more information, so
adaptive
sensor placement on the fly has the potential to improve on this (with the limitation that computing time has to keep up with the speed of incoming data).

•

A large class of interesting sensor placement problems are built on spatial domains, with sensors being placed at spatial locations (rather than ordered points in time). Generalising the current theory to the spatial setting would be very interesting.

Appendix C
Time rescaling does not yield a scheduled filtering problem

In this section we quickly study the question whether the scheduled filtering problem (
2.6
) can be viewed as a simple time rescaling of a standard observation process (and we answer this to the negative). Given a so-called base process,

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y(s)=g(Y(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B(s)

construct a time changed process
Z
such that

\displaystyle Z(t)=Y(r(t))

where
r(t)
may be either a deterministic or stochastic time change. In our context, taking
r(t)
to be a deterministic time change makes the most sense, i.e.
r(t)
such that
\frac{dr(t)}{dt}=\xi(t)
with
\xi
a deterministic function of time. Then

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z(t)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y(r(t))

\displaystyle=g(Y(r(t)))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r(t)+\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B(r(t))

\displaystyle=g(Z(t))\xi(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\sqrt{\xi(t)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(t)

where
W(t)
is a standard Brownian motion in time scale
t
independent from
B(t)
. In our case, we instead want to consider a time change of the observation process, which itself depends on another stochastic process
X
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y(s)=g(X(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B(s)

doing a deterministic time change in a similar way yields

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z(t)

\displaystyle=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y(r(t))=g(X(r(t)))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r(t)+\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B(r(t))

\displaystyle=g(X(r(t)))\xi(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\sqrt{\xi(t)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(t)

which is not consistent with (
2.6
) as the signal process is evaluated at time
r(t)
rather than
t
.
