Management of Norovirus Infection in Solid Organ Transplant Recipients: A Scoping Review
Authors: Thida Maung Myint, Jonathan Hand, Adam Lowe, Camille N. Kotton, Sharon C-A Chen, Justin Beardsley, Armando Teixeira-Pinto, Germaine Wong, Samuel Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background.Norovirus infection causes significant morbidity in solid organ transplant (SOT) recipients, yet few treatments are available, and evidence for efficacy is sparse. In this scoping review, we identify and evaluate potential interventions for managing norovirus infections in SOT recipients.Methods.We searched electronic databases from inception to July 6, 2025. Eligible studies were analyzed for participants’ characteristics, intervention types, and reported outcomes.Results.After screening 245 abstracts, 58 studies were included (1 randomized controlled trial, 27 cohort studies, 5 case series and 25 case reports), mainly from the United States. Transplant types included kidney (n = 36), liver (n = 12), cardiac (n = 12), pulmonary (n = 7), pancreas (n = 6), small bowel (n = 7), and multiorgan (n = 13) transplants. The most frequently reported primary outcome was resolution of gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms. Interventions were diverse: immunosuppression modification (n = 14), nitazoxanide (n = 6), IVIG (n = 3), oral immunoglobulin (n = 10), combination of these (n = 19), fecal transplant (n = 2), supportive management (n = 4), and others not classified (n = 5). Limited quality evidence for the resolution of gastrointestinal symptoms was reported for immunosuppression modification (n = 7/14), nitazoxanide (n = 4/6), IVIG (n = 2/3), oral immunoglobulin alone (n = 10/10), fecal transplant (n = 2/2), supportive treatment (n = 4/4), and a combination of treatments (n = 10/19). A lack of clinical improvement was described in 13 of 58 studies.Conclusions.A wide range of interventions has been used to manage norovirus infections in SOT recipients; however, the evidence is limited to observational studies, and the findings are uncertain. High-quality randomized controlled trials are needed to establish treatment efficacy and safety.

INTRODUCTION

Norovirus is a single-stranded RNA virus from the Caliciviridae family,1 with the GII.4 recognized as the most common cause of outbreaks of norovirus infection globally.2-4 The disease is highly contagious and is a leading cause of acute gastroenteritis in people of all ages and settings,5 causing >20 million episodes of gastroenteritis and >200 000 deaths every year, worldwide.6,7 It poses a significant financial burden, with estimates exceeding $4 billion in direct health system costs and $60 billion in societal costs yearly, and a high burden of disease disability-adjusted life years.7-9 This impact is attributed to healthcare expenses and loss of productivity, highlighting the need for effective prevention and control measures to mitigate its spread.8 Infections in immunocompetent hosts typically resolve within a few days. However, disease may be severe and sometimes prolonged in immunocompromised patients, including solid organ transplant (SOT) recipients, causing major complications such as acute kidney injury, allograft dysfunction, and occasionally death.10-13

Despite the disease’s severity, preventive and therapeutic measures are lacking. Noroviruses are a genetically and antigenically diverse group of RNA viruses, which makes it challenging to develop diagnostics and vaccines.14 In addition, there are currently no targeted antiviral treatments for norovirus gastroenteritis, and cases are typically managed with supportive therapy, including rehydration, electrolyte replacement, and immunosuppression reduction. Very few randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have analyzed the efficacy and safety of interventions to manage norovirus in SOT recipients.15 Furthermore, in vivo and in vitro data are lacking, because human norovirus is only known to infect humans; there are no robust and reproducible models available.16,17 Well-designed, adequately powered RCTs are ultimately required to assess the safety and efficacy of interventions for managing norovirus infections in SOT recipients. As a necessary first step, we aimed to conduct a scoping review to systematically map and evaluate the existing literature on such interventions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews and the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis were used to guide this work.

Inclusion Criteria

Eligible studies reported data on SOT recipients with a diagnosis of norovirus infection based on nucleic acid amplification testing of stool samples, such as real-time polymerase chain reaction. We searched databases from inception to July 2025, without language restrictions, accepting study designs including systematic reviews, RCTs, cohort studies, case series, case reports, and letters to the editor or editorials with relevant data. Reports on patients who did not have an SOT were ineligible.

Search Strategy

We developed the search strategy in Medline, reviewed it according to the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies guidelines, and adapted it for use in Embase, CINAHL, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. We also assessed the reference lists of studies included to identify relevant articles. Appendix 1 (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A868) highlights the search strategy used in this scoping review.

Evidence Selection and Screening

Two researchers (T.M.M. and A.L.) independently screened titles and abstracts to determine their inclusion based on the eligibility criteria. If any discrepancies could not be resolved, a final decision was made by a third party (G.W.). Following this, the same pair of researchers independently screened all full-text articles for inclusion. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion between the 2 researchers. A third party (G.W.) made a final decision on the disparity if the disagreements could not be resolved.

Data Extraction

A spreadsheet was developed to capture standardized data from included studies, including authorship, publication year, study characteristics, study design, country of study and setting. Participant information, including age, sex, and sample size, was also collected, along with the study objectives: specifically, the population, intervention, comparator, and outcome. Data were extracted by a single researcher (T.M.M.) and verified by a second (A.L.).

Data Analysis, Summary, and Presentation

Data were analyzed with STATA version 19 and RStudio.18,19 Demographic data were presented descriptively as mean and SD or median and interquartile range, depending on the data distribution.

INTERVENTIONS TO TREAT NOROVIRUS INFECTIONS

Monotherapies

Immunosuppression Modification Alone

Overall, 14 studies (n = 791) reported modifications in immunosuppression among individuals diagnosed with norovirus infection. These studies included recipients of kidney, heart, lung, liver, pancreas, intestine, and multiorgan SOTs, as well as hematopoietic stem cell transplants, in both adults and children. Of these, the most common immunosuppressive changes were the reduction or withdrawal of antiproliferative agents (4 studies) and tacrolimus (3 studies). Other changes included conversion from mycophenolate to azathioprine (2 studies), conversion to mammalian target of rapamycin (2 studies), and conversion from tacrolimus to cyclosporine (1 study). Three studies did not specify the reduction or modification of immunosuppressive drugs.

Across all studies, responses to immunosuppression modification varied. Six studies reported resolution within weeks to 12 mo,20-23,27,29 whereas 7 found improvement to be inconsistent, often transient, or absent.24,25,28,30,66-68 In a cohort study of 39 kidney transplant recipients (29 with norovirus), reducing or withdrawing antiproliferative agents (with 6 switching from tacrolimus to cyclosporine) resulted in symptom resolution. However, the time to resolution was not reported. Similar results were observed in other studies.21,22,25 In a larger cohort of 280 patients with norovirus infection comprising both adult and pediatric SOT and hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients, an inconsistent response with immunosuppression reduction was reported.24 There were no reported cases of acute rejection or de novo donor-specific antibody formation after reducing immunosuppressive doses. Conversely, a single case study reported de novo donor-specific antibody development after withholding mycophenolate for >1 wk.30

Nitazoxanide Alone

High-quality evidence assessing the effects of nitazoxanide for the treatment of norovirus in SOT is sparse. Six studies (n = 130) included kidney, heart, lung, liver, intestine, and multiorgan transplant recipients24,31-35 analyzed the benefits and harms of nitazoxanide for treating norovirus infections. A single RCT (n = 31 SOTs, nitazoxanide = 16 and placebo = 15) found no evidence of benefits over placebo in time to clinical resolution or viral shedding.32 A cohort study of 91 transplant recipients reported sustained resolution in 35.2%, transient improvement in 52.7%, and no change in 12.1%.24 Three case reports described transient improvement in GI symptoms among heart and kidney transplant recipients.31,34,35

IVIG Alone

Three studies (n = 54) evaluated IVIG alone in SOT recipients with norovirus infections. In a single-cohort study (n = 18), all participants achieved GI symptom resolution, but 10 (56%) remained stool-positive for norovirus polymerase chain reaction. Duration, dose, and time to resolution of symptoms were not reported.45 A multicenter observational study (n = 34 SOTs) reported no improvement in 14.7%, sustained resolution in 29.4%, and temporary improvement in 55.9% of all participants.24 One case report described treatment failure with a combination of IVIG and fresh frozen plasma in a kidney transplant recipient who developed hemolytic uremic syndrome and died.69 However, none of the studies explicitly included hypogammaglobulinemia as an inclusion criterion, adding uncertainty to its severity and impact on treatment response.

Oral and Enteral Immunoglobulin Alone

Ten studies (n = 196) assessed oral (7 studies; n = 193) and enteral immunoglobulin (3 studies; n = 3) for the treatment of norovirus-associated diarrhea in SOT recipients. Across these studies, improvement in GI symptoms was described in all recipients. In most studies, immunoglobulin was administered orally, citing prior evidence reporting the stability of IgG through proteolytic digestion in the stomach and upper intestinal tract, as well as concerns regarding the risks associated with enteric tube placement in highly immunosuppressed patients.36,70 Enteral immunoglobulin was given in 3 case reports, with clinical improvement observed in all 3 patients.42-44 One patient developed abdominal pain after a third dose of nasojejunal enteral immunoglobulin, which resolved with conservative management.42

There was considerable variability in the doses and timing of treatment, ranging from 25 mg/kg every 6 or 8 h for 2 d37 to 100–300 mg/kg/d for 1–5 d.38 Resolution of diarrhea was reported in most studies.36,39,40,42,44 In 4 studies, clinical improvement occurred within 2–7 d of treatment initiation.36,37,41,43 One study reported a broader time to response, with improvement observed within 4–21 d for acute diarrhea, and 28–90 d for chronic cases.38 A single case series (n = 4) reported variable responses, with no change in symptoms reported in 1 (25%), temporary improvement in 3 (75.0%), and none had sustained resolution.40

Fecal Microbiota Transplant

Two case series (n = 5) reported fecal microbiota transplant (FMT) for the management of chronic norovirus gastroenteritis in SOT recipients.60,71 Improvement and resolution of symptoms were reported in all patients in both studies.

Combined Therapies

Immunosuppression Modification and Nitazoxanide

Combined management with immunosuppression modification and nitazoxanide was reported in 5 studies (n = 199), comprising 4 case reports in kidney and liver transplant recipients38-41 and 1 cohort study (n = 195) in kidney, liver, heart, lung, and pancreas transplant recipients.46 Most case reports reported symptom resolution. In a cohort of 195 SOT recipients, nitazoxanide was associated with greater symptom improvement and shorter time to improvement. However, no benefit was observed with immunosuppression modification alone.46

Immunosuppressive Therapy Modification, Nitazoxanide and Other Combination Therapies

Across all studies, different combinations of immunosuppressive therapy modification with adjunctive therapies have been trialed for norovirus-related diarrhea in SOT recipients, with mixed outcomes. A single case report found that symptom improvement occurred only after reducing mycophenolate doses.57 A combination of immunosuppression adjustments, nitazoxanide, and oral immunoglobulin was assessed in 5 studies (n = 234), and most followed a stepwise approach of supportive care, immunosuppression reduction, then nitazoxanide, or oral IgG.47-49,72,73 Clinical responses were inconsistent, with some reporting uncertain benefits,72,73 others limited improvement,49 and a few documenting symptom resolution.47,48 Two larger cohort studies (N = 272) assessed the modification of immunosuppression with nitazoxanide and IVIG, showing efficacy in 1 study but inconclusive benefits in the other.50,58 Smaller studies (N = 42) suggested that immunosuppression adjustment combined with oral IgG was associated with symptom improvement within 2–7 d.51-53 Overall, the evidence was limited to retrospective cohorts and case reports, which restricted the certainty of these findings.

Nitazoxanide and Other Adjunctive Therapies

The evidence base for nitazoxanide-based combination therapy in norovirus infection among SOT recipients remains sparse and inconclusive. A single case report in an intestinal transplant recipient described an unfavorable outcome, with no clinical or virologic response to combined nitazoxanide and oral Ig therapy.59 Similarly, a small retrospective cohort study evaluating the use of nitazoxanide and IVIG in 4 intestinal transplant recipients with norovirus infection found that symptoms were prolonged, and efficacy could not be ascertained.56 A larger study (N = 152) assessing the effectiveness of nitazoxanide, IVIG, and antimotility treatment suggested that nonintestinal transplant recipients required more frequent or prolonged use of combination treatment compared with intestinal SOTs.71

Other Treatments

Supportive Management

A total of 4 studies (n = 7) reported symptom resolution with supportive management (intravenous fluids with or without loperamide and dimenhydrinate) in kidney and multiorgan SOT recipients with norovirus-associated diarrhea.62-65 One case report described a patient who successfully underwent kidney transplantation after bilateral lung transplantation, with resolution of symptoms occurring approximately 10 wk after kidney transplantation.65

Other Studies not Classified

Three studies (n = 13) investigated the use of breast milk, favipiravir, and metronidazole for the management of norovirus-associated diarrhea in SOT recipients. These studies have found responses to treatment varied from temporary and sustained symptom resolution to no improvement.24,49,74 The other 2 case reports (n = 3) reported rare complications of norovirus infection, such as agranulocytosis and elevated aminotransferases.75,76 All had shown improvement with resolution of norovirus with trials of various treatments (modification of immunosuppression, IVIG, and ceftriaxone); however, the efficacy of these treatments was uncertain, as they were given after failure of other treatments.

DISCUSSION

This scoping review identified 1 RCT, 27 cohort studies, 5 case series, and 25 case reports evaluating interventions for norovirus infections in SOT recipients. The most frequently analyzed interventions were immunosuppressive therapy modification, nitazoxanide, immunoglobulin (intravenous or oral), combination regimens, FMT, and supportive care. Symptom resolution was most consistently reported with oral immunoglobulin, FMT, and supportive care, whereas the literature on other interventions was more heterogeneous, showing variable effectiveness. In addition, the smaller studies reported higher treatment success rates than the larger cohort studies, reflecting publication and selection biases that may overestimate treatment effects.

Overall, we found insufficient high-quality evidence to conclude with certainty that any of the described interventions are effective in treating norovirus infection in SOT recipients. The included studies exhibited substantial methodological heterogeneity, with inconsistent comparisons, variable outcome measures, and limited data on harms. Most reports were case studies, presenting descriptive or narrative findings, which are inherently susceptible to a higher risk of bias due to their selective nature, absence of systematic methodology, and limited generalizability.77 The existing literature was unable to address likely differences in treatment response relating to patient factors, such as degree of immunosuppression, acute versus chronic persistent infections, or advanced stages of disease. Moreover, comparisons with control groups were not reported in any of the observational studies. Given the limitations of the available evidence, it was not possible to explore and summarize the estimates of potential complications arising from any of the proposed treatments.

The transferability of the findings from these studies into clinical practice needs to be taken with caution. First, many small, observational studies reported highly varied outcomes. For example, no specific time to symptom resolution was documented in >80% of these studies. Second, there was minimal data on adverse effects for any of the interventions proposed. Third, it is unclear whether the resolution of symptoms, such as abdominal pain and diarrhea, indicates eradication of norovirus, with implications for infection control.

A key feature that distinguishes norovirus infections in SOT recipients is their tendency to become chronic and refractory, in contrast to the acute, self-limiting course observed in immunocompetent individuals. Most published studies of therapeutic strategies have focused on hospitalized transplant recipients with chronic norovirus infections. The true incidence of acute self-limited gastroenteritis in this population remains unclear. A single study of 60 hospitalized kidney transplant recipients reported that nearly 50% had acute norovirus infection,45 a proportion that is likely to be higher when outpatient episodes are included. Furthermore, it is unclear whether early adjustment of immunosuppression in the acute setting (eg, withholding mycophenolate) prevents the illness from progressing to a chronic infection. Effective therapeutic strategies should therefore aim to both prevent progression to chronic disease and halt established chronic or refractory infections.

The strength of this review lies in its comprehensive and up-to-date synthesis of the published evidence, conducted through a thorough and rigorous electronic database search. By including all available studies to date, we have provided the most complete overview of therapeutic strategies for norovirus infection in SOT recipients. However, our study has several limitations. First, the heterogeneity in study designs, population characteristics, types of interventions, and outcomes reporting precluded the derivation of pooled summary estimates that could be reliably applied in clinical practice. Second, most included studies were small, retrospective cohorts or single case reports, limiting both internal validity and generalizability. Third, we were unable to formally assess for reporting or publication bias without pooling the data, which is particularly important given that smaller studies are more likely to overestimate the actual treatment effects. Finally, the absence of standardized measures and inconsistent follow-up times further limited the ability to conclude effectiveness across studies. While considering the above-mentioned limitations, for all acute norovirus infections, we recommend supportive treatment followed by modification of immunosuppression if the infection does not resolve within a few days. For chronic persistent infections, despite modification of immunosuppression, we should consider combination treatments with immunoglobulins and nitazoxanide.

Accordingly, well-designed and adequately powered RCTs are urgently needed to establish the efficacy and safety of interventions for norovirus in SOT recipients. These trials should seek to compare commonly used strategies, such as nitazoxanide and immunoglobulin preparations, with immunosuppression modification against supportive care. In addition, microbiota-targeted therapies should be trialed to assess their effect on regulating microbial dysbiosis, which may be associated with norovirus infections. Standardized outcome measures, including both clinical symptom resolution and virological clearance, should be consistently reported, alongside data on adverse outcomes. Notably, studies should differentiate between acute, self-limited infections and chronic or refractory disease to inform stage-specific management. Multicenter collaboration, standardized trial protocols, and integration of patient-reported outcomes will be critical to generate robust evidence that can be appropriately translated into clinical practice, guidelines, and policies. CONCLUSIONS

Our review identified a range of strategies that may offer benefits in managing norovirus infections among SOT recipients. However, the supporting evidence remains inconclusive and limited. Alternative interventions, including therapies targeting microbiota, warrant further investigations. There is a pressing need to evaluate the efficacy and safety of these novel interventions in RCTs to establish the effectiveness and safety of these approaches, strengthen the certainty of evidence, and address current knowledge gaps. Such trials should use standardized definitions, outcome measures, and intervention protocols to ensure that findings are robust, comparable, and directly translatable across diverse clinical settings worldwide.
