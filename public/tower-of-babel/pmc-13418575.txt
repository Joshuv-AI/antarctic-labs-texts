Click Before You Pipette: Building Foundational Pipetting Skills and Confidence With an Interactive Prelaboratory Simulation
Authors: Maurizio Costabile, Beth Loveys
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCorrectly setting and reading a manual air displacement micropipette is an essential yet challenging skill for life‐science students. The incorrect delivery of volumes affects both experimental outcomes and data interpretation, potentially reducing student confidence in the laboratory. To address this issue, we developed and evaluated an interactive simulation using Articulate Storyline. Our novel approach isolated and addressed the critical cognitive task of reading a micropipette dial from the physical skill of using the tool. This intervention offers a uniquely scalable and cost‐effective solution compared to the use of physical micropipettes. Existing training often focuses on the physical action, leaving a gap for a scalable digital tool that allows students to first master the cognitive challenge of reading the dial. Using a prepost mixed methods design, we measured student perceptions of the approach and changes in student performance across confidence, unit conversion, and micropipette dial reading accuracy. Students at two independent universities showed significant improvements in self‐rated confidence and accuracy in reading micropipette scales after using the simulation. Student feedback was highly positive regarding the benefits of the simulation in learning these skills and enhancing their self‐efficacy. This simulation is an ideal preparatory tool within a blended learning framework, significantly improving student confidence and accuracy. By mastering the cognitive prerequisite in a safe, virtual environment, students are better prepared for effective hands‐on practice in a face‐to‐face setting.

Introduction

The development of practical laboratory skills is a fundamental objective of undergraduate science education. These motor skills are essential for translating abstract theoretical knowledge into tangible, real‐world applications, preparing students for future careers in research, clinical diagnostics, and industry [1]. Among the array of foundational techniques in biochemistry and molecular biology, one of the most critical which requires proficiency in its use is the manual air displacement micropipette. The correct use of a micropipette is noted as an authentic assessment activity [2, 3, 4] and accurate, precise, and repeatable delivery of given volumes of liquid is foundational to many experimental procedures [5, 6].

Despite its ubiquitous nature, students frequently find mastering the correct use of a micropipette to be a significant challenge. The process involves multiple cognitive and technical steps: firstly, selecting the most appropriate micropipette for a given volume, correctly reading and setting the dial, (which often vary between manufacturers), performing any necessary unit conversions (e.g., milliliters to microliters), and finally repeatedly executing the proper physical technique [7, 8]. This multifaceted task can induce a high cognitive load, leading to common errors such as incorrect volume settings, inconsistent liquid aspiration, and cross‐contamination [9]. Such errors can compromise experimental integrity [10, 11], lead to wastage of expensive reagents and contribute to student anxiety, diminishing their confidence in the laboratory setting [12]. A key reason for this difficulty is that most assessments and training address the procedural act of pipetting. An inaccurate result in the “Pipette Olympics” [13, 14] could stem from multiple sources, poor plunger technique, incorrect tip immersion, or the cognitive error of misreading the dial among other human and environmental factors [15]. Our work targets this critical upstream activity: the cognitive task of correctly interpreting and setting the micropipette dial. By decoupling this mental task from the physical, motor skill, we aim to address a foundational point of failure before a student is required to use a micropipette.

To bridge the gap between theory and practice, educational technologies such as interactive computer simulations offer a powerful and effective pedagogical solution. Well‐designed simulations provide a “safe‐to‐fail” environment where students can practice complex procedures without the risk of breaking equipment or wasting materials [16]. This approach allows for unlimited, self‐paced repetition, a key factor in developing kinaesthetic skills, and can provide immediate, formative feedback that is often difficult for an educator to deliver to each student simultaneously in a large laboratory class [17]. A simulation allows students to master the cognitive task of reading a micropipette dial in a virtual environment before a laboratory session. This could reduce their intrinsic cognitive load during in‐person practicals, freeing up mental resources to focus on the physical technique and the broader experimental context. This approach aligns with the blended virtual‐physical laboratory model proposed by de Jong et al. [16], where a virtual tool serves as preparatory stage. While others have developed innovative physical solutions, from tactile training aids [1] to at‐home kits [18, 19, 20], these face limitations in scalability, cost, and logistics. Our study introduces a simulation‐based approach to teach students how to read and set a laboratory micropipette, developed using the widely available e‐learning tool, Articulate Storyline. We position this as a powerful and highly scalable preparatory tool within a blended laboratory curriculum. This approach is infinitely scalable, and highly transferable for other educators, representing a uniquely practical and accessible solution.

This study was guided by the following questions: 1: Can a brief, interactive simulation significantly improve student confidence in unit conversion and reading micropipette dials? 2: Does the simulation lead to a measurable reduction in errors in reading micropipette dials? and 3: What aspects of the simulation do students find most and least helpful?

Materials and Methodology

Student Cohort

The simulation was trialed across two Australian Universities. At the University of South Australia (UniSA), the simulation was implemented into a second year Biochemistry course (BIOL 2014; 102 students) taken by Laboratory Medicine, Biomedical Science, Food and Nutrition, and Pharmaceutical Science students. At the University of Adelaide (UoA), it was implemented into second year Agricultural Biochemistry (AGRIC 2500WT) and Agricultural Genetics (AGRIC 2510WT) courses taken by Agricultural Science and Viticulture and Oenology students (95 students total). Most students had prior exposure to using micropipettes during their first‐year biology courses (UniSA: 90%; UoA: 80%); note that first‐year Chemistry courses used only bulb pipettes, graduated pipettes, and measuring cylinders. However, the extent of this experience varied considerably, ranging from minimal use (2–3 times) to regular laboratory application (including students working in external laboratory settings). Despite this prior exposure, informal observations by instructors indicated persistent difficulties in accurately reading and setting pipette volumes.

Preparation of the Simulation

The simulation was created using Articulate Storyline 3 and hosted on the course learning management platform (Moodle or Canvas respectively) as a SCORM package. Storyline was chosen as it is widely available, allows for the creation of targeted, interactive tutorials without needing specialized programming skills, ensuring the methodology is accessible and transferable to the broader educational community. The simulation covered how volumes are displayed across micropipettes available in our laboratory settings (P20, P200, P1000, and P5000). Formative questions were provided for students to test their understanding of both reading a micropipette scale as well as converting between volume units. The ~20‐min module began with a brief tutorial on how different micropipette dials (e.g., P20, P200, P1000, P5000) represent volumes, highlighting the meaning of the red numbers or lines. This included explicit instruction on the minimum and maximum volume ranges for each micropipette, emphasizing that accurate liquid handling requires selecting volumes within the calibrated operating range of the instrument. Students then engaged in an interactive practice mode where they were given a target volume and had to “click” to adjust a virtual micropipette dial to the correct setting, receiving immediate feedback. The module concluded with a formative quiz.

Outline of the Simulation

The simulation employs a scaffolded learning pathway designed to guide users from foundational knowledge to practical application and assessment. Upon launching the program, users are introduced to the main interface (Figure 1), showing a laboratory pipette in action. The instructional phase begins by presenting essential information, with a diagram illustrating how to read the volume on a specific micropipette, using a P5000 (5 mL) as an example, showing the importance of the plunger's dial (Figure 2). Following this initial instruction, students can apply this information to actively practice their understanding by manipulating a virtual micropipette's volume setting using increment (+) and decrement (−) controls, allowing for immediate reinforcement of the concept (Figure 3). To validate their comprehension, learners are then presented with an assessment question that requires them to correctly identify both the micropipette type and the specific volume displayed on a static image (Figure 4). This structured progression ensures that users first acquire the necessary knowledge before applying and being tested, fostering a more robust understanding of correct pipetting technique.

Timing of Implementation

In Week 2 of the 13‐week semester, students completed a prequestionnaire to establish baseline data on demographics, prior experience in using a pipette, and their ability to convert units and read pipette scales. After the questionnaire had been completed, the interactive simulation was made available to students prior to their first hands‐on laboratory session involving pipetting. During an introductory practical, students were provided with the rationale for the simulation and were encouraged to complete the ~20‐min module before the following practical session. In Week 4, the postquestionnaire was delivered to gage changes in student confidence and ability. The performance‐based questions in the postquestionnaire were identical to those in the pre‐questionnaire, allowing for a direct assessment of performance changes.

The interactive simulation was designed to be a preparatory tool that allowed students to master the cognitive components of using a micropipette in a self‐paced, formative environment. In this instance, we did not teach students how to choose the best pipette for a given volume (e.g., to deliver 150 μL, should they use a P200 or a P1000?). This important principle is covered in the first face‐to‐face practical (Week 3) where students deliver 50, 100, and 200 μL using a P200 pipette and then report on the relationship between volume delivered and the error in volume that was delivered.

Questionnaire

Student confidence was assessed using a 5‐point Likert scale (1, Strongly Disagree; 2, Disagree; 3, Neutral; 4, Agree; 5, Strongly Agree) on three statements: 1, I am confident in my ability to use an automatic pipette; 2, I am confident in my ability to convert between mL and μL; and 3, I am confident in my ability to correctly read the volume on a pipette dial. Performance questions assessed four pipette volumes (P10, P20, P200, and P1000). The P5000 was not included in the performance assessment as it is less frequently used in these teaching laboratories and its exclusion helped keep the questionnaire to a manageable length to support response rates. The full pre‐ and postquestionnaires can be found in Files S1 and S2, respectively.

Data Analysis

All quantitative data were collated and analyzed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.1.2, GraphPad Software LLC). A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all inferential tests. The specific analytical approaches for the different data types are detailed below.

Analysis of Student Confidence

Changes in student self‐reported confidence were assessed using responses on a 5‐point Likert scale. Although Likert data are ordinal, a paired‐samples t‐test was used to evaluate pre‐ and postsimulation differences within each cohort, as this approach is considered appropriate for symmetric, 5‐point scales with sample sizes of this magnitude [21]. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation. To explore potential baseline differences between degree programs, pre‐simulation data from independent groups were compared using an independent samples t‐test. To explore potential baseline differences in confidence among students from different degree programs (e.g., Laboratory Medicine vs. Biomedical Science), presimulation data from these independent groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Analysis of Pipette Reading Performance

Student performance on tasks requiring them to read a micropipette dial was scored as “Correct” or “Incorrect.” To determine whether the proportion of students answering correctly changed significantly after using the simulation, a McNemar's test, which is specifically designed for analyzing paired nominal data, was performed. The results of this analysis are presented as the percentage of students who made an error on each task, allowing for a clear visualization of the reduction in error rates.

Qualitative Analysis

Free‐text responses provided by students in the postsimulation questionnaire were analyzed using a thematic analysis approach [22]. This structured qualitative analysis was used to contextualize the quantitative results and provide deeper insight into the student learning experience.

Use of AI

The authors utilized generative AI tools (Google Gemini) to assist in the initial stages of the literature search and to generate preliminary summaries of relevant articles. All sources identified and summarized by the AI were subsequently reviewed and verified by the authors, and the final synthesis and interpretation of the literature were conducted entirely by the authors.

Baseline Confidence

At both UniSA and UoA, student use of laboratory micropipettes was introduced in first‐year biology, while they used bulb pipettes, graduated pipettes, and measuring cylinders in their first year Chemistry courses. Despite most students having some prior experience in using a micropipette, they reported confidence slightly above the neutral midpoint of the scale. On a 5‐point Likert scale (1, Strongly disagree to 5, Strongly agree), the mean ± SD student confidence in using an automatic micropipette was 3.835 ± 0.82 (n = 85) (UniSA) and 4.036 ± 1.26 (n = 56) (UoA), with only minor differences observed between cohorts. When asked to rate their confidence in converting between units of volume, the mean score was 3.671 ± 0.83 (UniSA) and 3.304 ± 1.67 (UoA), again indicating a response only slightly higher than neutral, and lastly, when asked to rate their confidence in correctly reading an automatic micropipette, mean confidence scores were 3.655 ± 0.96 (UniSA) and 3.692 ± 1.3 (UoA), indicating moderate levels of confidence, slightly above the neutral midpoint of the scale, across key aspects of laboratory micropipette use in both cohorts. This information is important, as it may explain many of the challenges faced by students during their laboratory studies. We compared two cohorts to identify if any differences existed to the initial responses. The only significant difference was noted for Question 3 in the post analysis, with a p value of 0.0361 (Students t‐test) noted. Due to the similarity between cohorts, the data were pooled for all future analyzes.

Baseline Performance

We next examined the ability of students to correctly convert between volume units and correctly interpret pipette dial images. As has been reported in other studies, student confidence in these two parameters did not align with their actual performance on skills‐based questions, revealing a significant gap between perception and competence. In unit conversion tasks, 67.9% (57 of 84) of students correctly converted 3280 μL to mL (3.28 mL), and 79.8% (67 of 84) correctly converted 5.234 mL to μL (5234 μL). Consistent with both authors' experiences in the teaching laboratory, performance was weakest when interpreting pipette dials from images. Error rates were substantial for the smallest and largest volume pipettes: the P10 (set to 2.5 μL) had a 40.8% error rate, and the P1000 (set to 320 μL) had a 49.7% error rate. Students performed better with mid‐range pipettes, with a 14.1% error rate for the P20 (set to 15.0 μL) and a 15.5% error rate for the P200 (set to 128 μL). These baseline results demonstrate a clear need for targeted instruction, particularly in reading P10 and P1000 pipette dials.

Following the 1‐week intervention, 61 students (71.7% of the UniSA cohort) and 81 (85% UoA) completed the postsimulation questionnaire. The results showed a significant improvement across measured domains (Table 2). For Question 1, we initially noted a significant increase in student confidence for the UniSA cohort, however after pooling the data with the UoA students, the difference did not reach statistical significance. For Question 2, confidence in correctly converting between units of volume, and Question 3, confidence in correctly reading a pipette, significant improvements in confidence were noted.

Postsimulation Performance

This boost in student confidence in Questions 2 and 3 was mirrored by significant improvements in student performance across all metrics. Accuracy on both unit conversion tasks increased to over 91%, an improvement of over 23% for μL to mL conversion and 13% for mL to μL conversion. Most notably, error rates in reading pipette dials decreased across all four pipette volumes tested (Table 3). For the P10 pipette, the error rate fell from 40.8% to 7.5%. For the P1000, the error rate fell from 49.7% to 27.4%. Smaller but still significant gains were seen for the P20 (6.1% reduction) and P200 (10.6% reduction). These data confirm that the simulation was highly effective in addressing the specific knowledge gaps identified in the baseline assessment.

Student Qualitative Feedback

To contextualize the quantitative findings, we performed a thematic analysis of open‐ended student comments from both questionnaires. This provided a deeper understanding of students' initial anxieties and the specific benefits they derived from the simulation. A total of 48 responses were received, and a total of six themes were identified for the question relating to the “Best aspects of the simulation”. These included: Practice and Repetition (12 comments, 25%): Students appreciated the ability to practice repeatedly without the risk of damaging equipment, allowing them to refine their skills and reinforce learning through repetition. This desire for safe practice likely contributed to the significant increase in confidence observed in our quantitative data. Visual Aids and Interactivity (10 comments, 20.8%): Visual elements, such as pipette scales, helped students better understand how to read the pipette scale, as laboratory equipment can be confusing (e.g., “visuals of the pipette,” “visual demonstration”). Clear and Simple Design (11 comments, 22.9%): The comments emphasized that the simulation was easy to use, clear, and beginner‐friendly, with straightforward instructions and explanations. Preparation and Familiarity (9 comments, 18.8%): Many students valued being able to practice and prepare before the Face‐to‐Face laboratory, which helped them feel more confident and comfortable with the processes (e.g., “get prepared before participating in the lab,” “can be familiar with the steps”). Examples and Explanations (9 comments, 18.8%): The provision of multiple examples and clear explanations was noted as beneficial for solidifying students' understanding and building confidence. Skill Development (7 comments, 14.6%): The simulation helped students develop specific lab skills such as unit conversions and choosing the correct pipette for a given volume.

Discussion

This cross‐institutional study demonstrates that a purpose‐built, interactive simulation is a highly effective tool for enhancing both confidence and competence of undergraduate biochemistry students in the foundational laboratory skill of pipetting. Our findings show statistically significant improvements in students' self‐rated confidence across two key domains: converting units of volume and correctly reading a pipette dial. This increased confidence was confirmed by a significant reduction in the percentage of errors students made when asked to read pipette dials in a post‐simulation assessment. These quantitative improvements were strongly verified by qualitative feedback, in which students overwhelmingly endorsed the simulation as a valuable preparatory tool.

The success of this simulation can be interpreted through the lens of Cognitive Load Theory [23], with its design principles aligned with established practices for effective multimedia learning [24]. While previous work has focused on assessing the outcome of the complete pipetting action [14], our simulation isolates the cognitive step of reading and setting the dial. This allows students to achieve mastery in a formative environment, reducing cognitive load when they later handle a physical pipette. Our approach provides a direct, targeted intervention to solve the exact problem noted by Goach et al. [12], of low student confidence in setting the volume on a pipette. It serves as the cognitive complement to psychomotor‐tactile tools like those described by Bernstein [1] and Lewis et al. [25], which connect the feel of the plunger to the volume.

A significant point of novelty in our work is its methodological accessibility and scalability. In contrast to approaches requiring the logistical coordination and expense of at‐home kits [18, 19, 20, 26] or the manual creation of physical training aids [1], our simulation was developed using Articulate Storyline. This approach makes our intervention infinitely scalable at no cost per student, a critical advantage in high‐enrolment courses. Furthermore, where scalable assessment might require laboratory equipment such as a spectrophotometer [27], our tool requires only a web browser for scalable training, making it accessible to a wider range of institutions and for independent student practice.

Our findings strongly support the role of this simulation as an ideal preparatory tool within a blended learning framework. Drawing on the framework of de Jong et al. [16], we reinforce the power of a combined virtual‐physical model. The simulation serves as the “virtual” preparatory stage, ensuring that valuable hands‐on “physical” laboratory time is spent learning the key aspects of a laboratory, rather than learning how to read a dial.

Finally, this study moves from diagnosis to solution. Previous research established that setting the pipette correctly is a primary challenge [12]. We have presented a targeted digital intervention but also provided quantitative evidence of its efficacy. The significant improvements observed in our pre‐ and posttest data across all tested volumes demonstrate that a brief (~20 min), interactive simulation can effectively and rapidly close this specific knowledge gap.

Our results are consistent with a growing body of literature advocating for the use of digital tools to supplement traditional laboratory teaching [28]. For instance, Towns et al. [9] implemented a “digital pipetting badge” system that used online videos and assessments to improve student hand skills. This approach was also validated by Bancroft et al. [29], who similarly found that a digital badge module significantly enhanced student confidence and skill. Similarly, Sostarecz et al. [8] demonstrated that visualizing data from their own pipetting improved students' technique and accuracy, an approach also effectively applied by Matsumoto and Ohtani [30] using a color wheel activity. Our study contributes to this field by affirming the efficacy of an interactive simulation, which goes beyond passive video‐watching by requiring active student engagement and decision‐making. The cross‐institutional nature of our deployment also strengthens the generalizability of our findings, confirming that such tools are effective across different student cohorts and institutional contexts. Furthermore, the overwhelmingly positive student feedback regarding the simulation's value as a preparatory tool supports the broader consensus that virtual and physical laboratories should be viewed as synergistic components of a modern, blended science curriculum [16, 28].

The qualitative feedback provided valuable insights into the specific features that students found most beneficial. The ability to practice repeatedly without fear of damaging expensive equipment was a recurrent theme, highlighting the simulation's role in reducing laboratory‐associated anxiety. Students also praised the clear visual aids and multiple examples, which helped demystify what is often a confusing piece of equipment. The constructive feedback received, such as the request for more complex scenarios and clearer explanations of subtler details (e.g., the meaning of the red line on a P20 dial), provides a clear roadmap for future iterations of the simulation. This feedback underscores the importance of an iterative design process in educational technology, where student input is used to continually refine and improve the learning experience.

Based on our findings, we recommend that science educators implement a short, mandatory pre‐laboratory simulation focused on the cognitive aspects of pipetting. This approach requires minimal resources, can be deployed through any standard LMS, and can free up valuable in‐person lab time for focusing on psychomotor technique and higher‐order experimental questions.

Despite the strongly positive results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the assessment of student confidence relied on self‐reported Likert scale data. Second, our performance metric is a proxy for overall motor proficiency. Crucially, the study demonstrated improvement on a virtual task but did not directly measure the transfer of this cognitive skill to the physical act of setting a real pipette through a gravimetric analysis, which remains the gold standard for assessing pipetting skill [5]. Third, the absence of a control group means we cannot entirely rule out the influence of confounding variables, such as peer instruction or instructor guidance during the introductory lab. Future research will address these limitations by incorporating objective, quantitative measures of pipetting accuracy (e.g., gravimetric analysis) and measuring long‐term skill retention.

In conclusion, this study provides strong evidence that a brief, targeted, and interactive simulation can significantly improve undergraduate students' skills and confidence in using a laboratory micropipette. By providing a safe, accessible, and repeatable learning experience, such digital tools effectively prepare students for in‐person practicals, allowing them to engage with hands‐on work more confidently and successfully. For educators, simulations represent a scalable and resource‐efficient method to enhance foundational skill acquisition, ultimately fostering a more positive and productive laboratory learning environment. Future work will focus on refining the simulation based on student feedback and expanding this pedagogical model to other essential laboratory techniques.
