Unveiling risk factors and predicting osteoporosis through bone density based aging model: a community-based cohort in Guangdong, China
Authors: Jinhong Tan, Jijun Zhu, Yongtao He, Zhaohao Fan, Fuqiang Cai, Haowen Zhuang, Yanhua Hu, Kangyan Liu, Qiancheng Li, Bo Feng, Yushi Guo, Gan Li, Bin Wang, Junfang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoporosis is a progressive skeletal disorder influenced by multiple clinical and lifestyle factors. Early identification of individuals at high risk is essential for prevention and personalized management. This study aimed to identify key determinants of osteoporosis and to establish a bone density based aging model to evaluate deviations in bone health.MethodsThis retrospective study included 4,275 adults from Shunde District, Guangdong, China (mean age 60.62 years, 67.36% female). Bone mineral density (BMD) was measured at the lumbar spine (L2-L4) and proximal femur sites (femoral neck and trochanter) using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). Participants were classified as normal, osteopenic, or osteoporotic according to WHO and Chinese guidelines. Univariable and multivariable regression models were applied to assess the associations between clinical and lifestyle factors and osteoporosis risk. A bone density aging model was developed using support vector regression to estimate bone density age, and bone density age acceleration (BDAA) was calculated as a marker of deviation from age-matched bone health.ResultsRisk analyses identified age, sex, body mass index, exercise frequency, OSTA score, and post-menopausal years (in women) as significant risk factors for osteoporosis. The bone density aging model showed good predictive performance (mean absolute error = 5.716 years, R² = 0.145). Higher BDAA was positively correlated with osteoporosis risk across bone health categories, including individuals without clinical diagnosis. In addition, sex-specific analyses showed that BDAA was elevated in male with insufficient exercise or smoking, and in female with osteopenia and osteoporosis, with the increase being more pronounced in osteoporosis patients, particularly under the influence of diet and alcohol consumption.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that the bone density based biological aging models can effectively capture deviations in bone health, improve early identification and personalized risk stratification of osteoporosis. This approach may facilitate and support the development of precision medicine strategies in osteoporosis prevention and management.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09298-0.

Background

Osteoporosis is a prevalent metabolic bone disease marked by decreased bone density and an elevated risk of fractures, primarily affecting older adults and post-menopausal women [1–3]. As the global population continues to age, the incidence of osteoporosis is rising, presenting a significant public health challenge. This condition leads to considerable morbidity, mortality, and economic burden worldwide. Despite its widespread prevalence and severe consequences, osteoporosis is frequently underdiagnosed and undertreated, highlighting a pressing need for more effective screening and diagnostic tools [4].

Numerous studies have identified key risk factors for osteoporosis, including age, hormonal changes [5], body mass index (BMI) [6], physical activity [7], dietary habits [8], smoking status [8, 9], lipid metabolism [10], and alcohol consumption [8]. However, existing approaches to osteoporosis risk prediction, such as the Osteoporosis Self-Assessment Tool for Asians, often fail to integrate these multifactorial risks, even including the presence of comorbidities such as stroke [11], hypertension [12], HIV infection [13], chronic kidney disease [14] and diabetes [15], liver disease [16] and others [17]. The interactions among these variables and their collective impact on osteoporosis risk are poorly understood, especially when considering the differing risk profiles between men and women.

With the advent of machine learning, predictive modeling for osteoporosis risk has seen significant advancements. Recent studies have explored the application of machine learning algorithms for osteoporosis risk prediction, demonstrating superior accuracy in identifying individuals at high risk for fractures and bone density loss [18–20]. These data driven approaches have been validated in large cohorts, providing a robust foundation for developing decision support systems and preclinical screening tools for osteoporosis [21, 22].

Despite substantial progress in predicting osteoporosis risk and identifying related factors, current research still lacks a systematic characterization of the extent to which bone health in high-risk individuals deviates from the age-matched average. This concept is analogous to biological aging, which has been assessed by various omics-based biological age clocks [23–26]. Previous studies have demonstrated that multiple biological age measures are associated with age-related diseases and may provide predictive value for osteoporosis risk [27, 28]. However, the application of omics technologies is limited by high cost and the requirement of invasive procedures such as blood collection. In contrast, there remains no reliable framework for quantifying bone health deviation and its association with osteoporosis risk using bone mineral density (BMD) or other more accessible indicators of bone health. Moreover, the sex-specific characteristics of such deviations, as well as the influence of lifestyle factors on accelerated bone loss, have yet to be systematically investigated.

To address these gaps and considering that osteoporosis is not a uniform process but site-dependent, with varying degrees of involvement across different skeletal regions, this study followed the recommendations of the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Primary Osteoporosis (2022) and measured BMD at major axial skeletal sites using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). Based on these measurements, we not only analyzed the risk factors associated with osteoporosis, but also constructed and validated a bone density aging model, serving as a metric analogous to biological age clocks, to assess individual risk by capturing deviations in bone health. Furthermore, we investigated the associations between this metric and osteoporosis risk across different demographic and lifestyle factors.

Methods

Clinical study subjects

We conducted a retrospective observational study including 4,275 individuals evaluated for BMD at Lunjiao Hospital, Foshan, China, from July 2019 to July 2024.

This study population consisted of Shunde District individuals representing different bone density levels. All participants were unrelated community-dwelling adults, and no family members were included within or between the training and validation cohorts.

BMD was measured at the lumbar spine (second (L2), third (L3), and fourth (L4) vertebrae) and at the proximal femur, including the femoral neck (Fem.Neck) and trochanter (Troch) regions, using DXA with the XR-800 system (Norland, USA) in fully automatic mode. Bone area demarcation was automatically performed by the system and confirmed by experienced technicians. The device was calibrated daily according to the manufacturer’s instructions to ensure measurement accuracy. For the lumbar spine, measurements were restricted to L2–L4 based on the XR-800 system default settings. For the femur, the left hip was assessed for all participants, and the dominant leg was not specifically considered. T-scores were calculated based on the international reference population database provided with the DXA system. To account for site-specific variability, the lowest T-score among all measured skeletal sites was used to classify participants into bone density status groups, reflecting the most severe bone density loss at any skeletal site.

According to WHO and Chinese “Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Primary Osteoporosis (2022)”, the diagnostic cutoffs are widely accepted in clinical practice: normal (T-score ≥ −1.0), osteopenia (T-score between − 1.0 and − 2.5), and osteoporosis (T-score ≤ −2.5) [29]. Based on these criteria, participants were classified as normal bone density (NOP, N = 684), osteopenia (N = 1,723), or osteoporosis (OP, N = 1,868).

For the univariable and multivariable analyses of osteoporosis risk factors, only the osteoporosis group (OP) and the normal bone density group (NOP) were included, to specifically compare individuals with a clear diagnosis of osteoporosis against those with normal bone density. Osteopenia participants were not included in these analyses, as this group represents a gray zone between normal and osteoporotic bone density, and including them could reduce the clarity and interpretability of the results.

The study was a retrospective analysis of samples collected between 2019 and 2024. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Lunjiao Hospital in 2024 (Approval No. Lunjiao Hospital Ethics [2024] No. 008), and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Participant characteristics and lifestyle factors

To minimize potential bias and ensure data quality, detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied in this study (Supplementary Fig. 1). Individuals were included if they were aged 40 years or older, were permanent residents of Shunde District, and met the criteria set by the Chinese Osteoporosis Diagnosis Guidelines. Participants must have complete DXA measurements of the lumbar spine (L2-L4), femoral neck, and trochanter regions [30]. All participants provided informed consent. Exclusion criteria included individuals with a history of metabolic bone diseases other than osteoporosis, current use of medications affecting bone metabolism (e.g., corticosteroids), or those with severe liver or kidney diseases, and malignancies. Additionally, individuals who had previously received osteoporosis treatment were excluded.

Comorbidities were recorded from medical history, including hypertension, diabetes, and other past medical conditions. Lifestyle factors were assessed through standardized questionnaires, including smoking status, alcohol consumption, exercise frequency, and dietary habits. Smoking status was categorized as: never smoked, quit, current smoker, and other, with the duration of smoking recorded. Alcohol consumption was classified as: never drank, quit, occasional drinking, frequent drinking, and other. Exercise frequency was categorized as: no exercise, sometimes, everyday, and other. Dietary habits were assessed using dietary recall and categorized as: balanced diet, meat-based, vegetarian, and other. All collected variables were included in subsequent statistical analyses. The characteristics of all samples are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1) with relevant packages for machine learning and regression modeling. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of categorical and continuous variables. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations (SD).

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine pairwise associations between continuous and categorical variables. Because certain variables exhibited strong correlations, these highly correlated features were not included simultaneously in subsequent multivariable regression analyses to avoid multicollinearity.

For univariable analysis, two-sample t-tests were applied to compare continuous variables between the NOP group and the OP group. Fold change (FC) values, defined as the mean of the OP group divided by the mean of the NOP group, and the corresponding p-values were calculated to evaluate differences across the entire sample as well as within gender-stratified subgroups.

For multivariable analysis, logistic regression models were used to investigate the associations between osteoporosis risk and relevant features. Analyses were conducted for the overall sample and separately by gender. To minimize the impact of strong correlations on model fitting, features were divided into two groups for analysis. Odds ratios (OR), regression coefficients (β), and the corresponding p-values were reported.

Osteoporosis risk scoring model

During the model development stage, we performed propensity score matching based on age and sex between the normal bone density group and the osteoporosis group, selecting 500 subjects with normal bone density and 500 patients with osteoporosis to construct the training set. A total of 23 machine learning algorithms, including logistic regression, naive Bayes, k-nearest neighbors, decision trees, random forests, and several other advanced methods, were trained using five-fold cross-validation repeated 50 times to ensure robust and reliable results. The best-performing model was then selected and applied to the entire sample to predict osteoporosis risk. The input features included bone density measurements from the L2, L3, L4 vertebrae, femoral neck, and trochanter regions. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with the AUC reflecting the model’s ability to distinguish osteoporosis patients from individuals with normal bone density. Additionally, the model was validated on an independent test set consisting of the remaining 184 normal bone density subjects and their matched 184 osteoporosis patients to further confirm its predictive ability.

Bone density based aging model

Using the normal bone density samples from the training set of the Osteoporosis risk scoring model, we developed a bone density age clock to quantify deviations in individual bone health relative to the age-matched average. Specifically, five bone density features from the L2, L3, and L4 vertebrae, femoral neck, and trochanter regions were used as input variables. Multiple regression learners were applied, and five-fold cross-validation was performed with 50 repetitions to ensure robust results. The best-performing model was then selected as the final bone density age clock and subsequently applied to predict bone density age for the entire sample. Model performance was evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE), R-squared (R²), and the Pearson correlation coefficient between predicted bone age and chronological age. MAE reflects the average magnitude of the difference between predicted and actual age, with smaller values indicating higher prediction accuracy; R² represents the proportion of variance in chronological age explained by the model, with values closer to 1 indicating better model fit; and the correlation coefficient measures the linear relationship between predicted and actual age, with higher values reflecting stronger agreement. The predictive performance of the model was further validated in the independent test set consisting of 184 normal bone density subjects to assess the effectiveness of bone density age prediction. Taken together, these metrics provide a comprehensive evaluation of the model’s accuracy, goodness-of-fit, and consistency, supporting its reliability as a metric for quantifying deviations in bone health.

Multivariable analysis of osteoporosis risk factors

We compared the predictive performance of five classifiers using five-fold cross-validation based on all risk factors for osteoporosis. The detailed results can be found in Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 3. After considering evaluation metrics such as AUC, sensitivity, specificity, false negative rate (FNR), and false positive rate (FPR), we selected logistic regression for the multivariable analysis of osteoporosis risk factors.

For the analysis of the entire population with normal bone density and the osteoporosis group (sample size of 684:1868), we focused on gender together with other features that showed no significant pairwise correlations, as determined by Pearson correlation analysis. The results indicated that male had a lower risk of osteoporosis than females (β = −1.933, p < 2 × 10−16). Additionally, age (β = 0.067, p = 1.17 × 10−8), reduced exercise frequency (β = −0.248, p = 0.008), and lower OSTA (β = −0.459, p < 2 × 10−16) were significant risk factors across the entire sample (see Supplementary Table 4).

Furthermore, we performed separate multivariable analyses using logistic regression for males and females. To avoid the impact of strong correlations on model fitting, features were divided into two groups for analysis (Supplementary Table 5). For the male population (sample size of NOP: OP = 140:579), Group 1 (Fig. 2A) included age, SBP, BMI, exercise frequency, diet, smoking status, drinking frequency, and history of hypertension and diabetes. Group 2 (Fig. 2B) included SBP, OSTA, exercise frequency, diet, smoking status, drinking frequency, and history of hypertension and diabetes. The analysis indicated that age (β = 0.058, p = 8.46 × 10−5), reduced exercise frequency (β = −0.601, p = 0.0026), lower BMI (β = −0.246, p = 3.87 × 10−7), and lower OSTA (β = −0.356, p = 1.39 × 10−11) were significant risk factors for osteoporosis in males. Similarly, for the female population (sample size of NOP: OP = 140:579), Group 1 (Fig. 2C) included age, SBP, BMI, exercise frequency, diet, smoking status, drinking frequency, post-menopausal years, and history of hypertension and diabetes. Group 2 (Fig. 2D) included SBP, OSTA, exercise frequency, diet, smoking status, drinking frequency, post-menopausal years, and history of hypertension and diabetes. The analysis showed that age (β = 0.165, p < 2 × 10−16), post-menopausal years (β = 0.087, p = 1.25 × 10−6), lower BMI (β = −0.211, p = 2.92 × 10−15), and lower OSTA (β = −0.589, p < 2 × 10−16) were significant risk factors for osteoporosis in females. Osteoporosis risk scoring model prediction performance

Among the machine learning models evaluated, the random forest classifier demonstrated the best cross-validation performance, with an accuracy of 98.29% (SD = 0.15%) and an AUC of 0.9976 (SD = 0.0003) over 50 repetitions (Supplementary Table 6). When tested on an independent dataset, the random forest model achieved an accuracy of 97.5% and an AUC of 0.998, confirming its high predictive power for identifying osteoporosis risk.

Bone density aging model prediction performance

In the regression analysis for the bone density aging model, the support vector machine (SVM) regression model exhibited the strongest performance during cross-validation, with a correlation coefficient of 0.3016 (SD = 0.0159), an R2 of 0.0913 (SD = 0.0098), and an MAE of 6.0259 years (SD = 0.0538) (Supplementary Table 7). On the independent test set, the SVM model further improved its performance, with a correlation coefficient of 0.381, an R2 of 0.145, and an MAE of 5.716 years, demonstrating its effectiveness and robustness in predicting bone density age.

Bone density age acceleration analysis

Bone density age acceleration (BDAA) was defined as the residual from a linear regression of predicted bone density age on chronological age. The regression model was fitted using normal bone density individuals in training set and then applied to all participants, with the residual for each individual calculated as the difference between their predicted bone density age and the value predicted by the regression. This residual serves as a measure of the extent to which an individual’s bone health deviates from the age-matched average. Osteoporosis risk for all participants was simultaneously predicted using the established bone density classifier. Linear regression analysis revealed a significant positive association between BDAA and osteoporosis risk probability. Bone density age acceleration analysis in female populations

Among males, osteoporosis patients who exercised occasionally or daily exhibited significantly higher BDAA compared to other health status groups (all p ≤ 1.11 × 10⁻⁶). Additionally, among males with normal bone density, non-exercisers had significantly higher BDAA than exercisers (p = 3.4 × 10⁻²). In the BMI categories, underweight, normal weight, or overweight male osteoporosis patients displayed significantly elevated BDAA (all p ≤ 2.42 × 10⁻⁸). Regarding diet, male osteoporosis patients with either balanced or meat-based diets showed significantly higher BDAA (all p ≤ 2 × 10⁻¹⁶). Smoking status also had a significant effect: currently smoking male osteoporosis patients had higher BDAA than males in other bone health groups (all p ≤ 9.04 × 10⁻³), and higher than non-smokers or former smokers with osteoporosis (p ≤ 4.19 × 10⁻⁴). Furthermore, across different histories of hypertension and diabetes, drinking frequency, and current blood pressure levels, male osteoporosis patients consistently exhibited higher BDAA than other bone health groups (all p ≤ 1.67 × 10⁻³). Notably, among males with normal blood pressure, individuals with osteopenia also showed significantly higher BDAA than those with normal bone density (p = 2.04 × 10⁻²). Discussion

This study highlights critical risk factors for osteoporosis, such as age, BMI, and physical activity, while introducing bone density based age acceleration as a novel predictor of osteoporosis risk. Our univariable and multivariable analyses revealed that both men and women share common risk factors, with post-menopausal years being a notable additional risk for women. Moreover, bone density based age acceleration showed a consistent and strong correlation with osteoporosis risk across the study population, positioning it as a valuable biomarker for early detection.

The significant association between age, BMI, and reduced exercise frequency with osteoporosis risk underscores well-established knowledge in the field. Age-related bone loss is a major factor driving osteoporosis, while maintaining a healthy BMI and regular physical activity are protective against this condition. Our results also highlighted gender-specific differences: post-menopausal years were a significant factor in females, aligning with the well-known role of estrogen in maintaining bone density. These findings reaffirm previous studies linking hormonal changes in women to osteoporosis [5] and strengthen the argument for targeted interventions during and after menopause. Chronic stress also plays a significant role, as recent studies suggest that stress-induced bone loss is mediated by a GABAergic neural circuit in the ventromedial hypothalamus [31].

This study makes a novel contribution by introducing bone density age acceleration as a predictor of osteoporosis risk. Previous research has shown that telomere shortening, a widely recognized indicator of biological aging, is associated with osteoporosis [32],and cellular senescence has been implicated in bone aging and osteoporosis development, suggesting that senescence markers may help predict bone health deterioration [33]. However, our analysis extends this understanding by demonstrating that individuals with osteoporosis show significantly accelerated bone density aging, even compared to those with osteopenia or density. This observation suggests that bone density age, as modeled by machine learning algorithms, may be a more sensitive and specific early biomarker for osteoporosis, allowing for earlier identification of at-risk individuals before clinical symptoms manifest. Moreover, the use of residuals from linear regression models to quantify bone density age acceleration introduces a new tool for assessing the deviation of biological aging from chronological aging, similar to the widely recognized epigenetic-based age acceleration [34], which could be crucial for precision medicine approaches to osteoporosis prevention.

The observed correlation between bone density age acceleration and osteoporosis risk likely reflects the cumulative effects of various lifestyle, genetic, and physiological factors influencing bone health. For instance, excessive caffeine intake, which has been shown to increase the risk of osteoporosis by affecting calcium clearance [35]. Aging, reduced physical activity, and low BMI all contribute to decreased bone remodeling and bone mineral density, leading to accelerated bone aging. In females, post-menopausal hormonal changes further exacerbate bone density loss, accounting for the significant acceleration observed in this group. These findings align with the bone remodeling theory, which suggests that the balance between bone formation and resorption shifts with age and hormonal changes, leading to osteoporosis [5]. Our data suggest that bone density age acceleration captures this shift, potentially offering a mechanistic link between lifestyle factors and biological aging of bones.

BDAA also exhibited sex-specific risk patterns in both men and women. In females, BDAA was significantly elevated in individuals with osteopenia and osteoporosis, particularly under the influence of lifestyle factors such as dietary habits and alcohol consumption, indicating that post-menopausal hormonal changes may increase female susceptibility to lifestyle related bone deterioration. In males, BDAA was significantly higher in those with insufficient physical activity and smoking, suggesting that lifestyle interventions targeting men, including increasing exercise and smoking cessation, may help slow bone aging and reduce the risk of osteoporosis.

Recent genetic studies provide additional insights that could further enhance the predictive power of bone density age acceleration models. For example, research has identified the POLR2A gene as having a protective effect against post-menopausal osteoporosis [36]. Similarly, the discovery of the Men1 gene as a key regulator of age-related osteoporosis, along with the development of a new animal model for studying the disease [37], underscores the potential for integrating genetic data into future models.

Emerging therapeutic strategies also offer exciting opportunities to complement the predictive power of bone density age acceleration. For instance, synthetic biology-based therapies, such as bacterial extracellular vesicles displaying BMP-2 and CXCR4, have shown potential in ameliorating bone degeneration [38]. Additionally, extracellular vesicles from apoptotic bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (ApoEVs) have been shown to carry regenerative signals that can mitigate osteoporosis [39], opening new avenues for treatment. These therapies, alongside interventions targeting mitochondrial homeostasis in bone cells [40] and senolytic therapies aimed at reducing the load of senescent cells in post-menopausal women [41], could complement early detection efforts by targeting the underlying biological processes that drive bone aging and osteoporosis. Furthermore, targeted therapies that modulate immune cell recruitment to bone matrix, such as those involving CXCL9 and CXCR3 [42], offer targeted approaches to reducing excessive bone resorption while preserving normal turnover. These strategies align well with the concept of bone density age acceleration, capturing early biological shifts and enabling timely interventions to prevent bone loss and reduce fracture risk.

While our findings strongly support the role of BDAA as a predictor of osteoporosis, alternative explanations should also be considered. Genetic factors [43], which were not accounted for in this study, may influence both bone density age and osteoporosis risk, and environmental factors such as calcium and vitamin D intake were not directly assessed [44]. Future studies should integrate genetic information to better understand the interaction between genetics, clinical factors, and lifestyle determinants in osteoporosis risk prediction. Certain lifestyle and clinical factors potentially affecting bone health, such as medication use, occupational exposures, and nutrient supplementation, were not comprehensively assessed. Furthermore, lifestyle factors including physical activity, diet, smoking, and alcohol consumption were collected at a single time point, which may not fully reflect long-term exposure or habitual behaviors, potentially affecting the precision of osteoporosis risk modeling. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and longitudinal studies are needed to confirm whether bone density age acceleration can serve as a reliable early marker for predicting future osteoporosis risk. Additionally, this study was conducted in a single center and included only adults from the Shunde District, which may limit generalizability to other populations or ethnic groups [45]. DXA measurements were limited to the L2–L4 vertebrae and the left hip. The dominant leg and other skeletal sites were not assessed, which may affect the representativeness of the measurements. Although the large sample size partially mitigates potential statistical limitations, formal sample size estimation or power calculation was not performed. In addition, while BDAA provides informative quantification of deviations in skeletal health, it has not yet been directly linked to clinical fracture outcomes or other functional endpoints. If validated, bone density age acceleration could be integrated into clinical screening protocols, allowing for more personalized and timely interventions to prevent or slow the progression of osteoporosis.

Conclusions

This study systematically identified key clinical and lifestyle factors associated with osteoporosis and demonstrated the utility of a bone density based aging model in evaluating skeletal aging. Age, reduced exercise frequency, lower body mass index, and lower OSTA scores were consistently significant risk factors across both genders, with post-menopausal years further contributing to risk in females. Females exhibited a higher overall risk of osteoporosis than males. The bone density aging model effectively predicted bone density age and revealed that osteoporosis patients showed significant bone density age acceleration compared to controls, with correlations observed for exercise, BMI, diet and smoking. These findings highlight the potential of bone density based biological age assessment for early identification of high risk individuals and personalized intervention strategies, providing a framework to enhance precision medicine approaches in osteoporosis prevention and management. Our results are specific to a community-based cohort in Shunde District, Guangdong, China.
