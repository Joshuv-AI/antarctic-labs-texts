Comparison of the efficacy of pentazocine and tramadol for reducing emergence agitation in children after general anesthesia: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Weilian Ren, Bingqi Wang, Baoyu Han, Hao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmergence agitation (EA) is a common postoperative complication in pediatric anesthesia that can lead to serious safety events and increased healthcare burden. Both pentazocine and tramadol are frequently used for postoperative analgesia in children, but their comparative effectiveness in preventing EA remains unknown. This study aimed to compare the efficacy of a single intravenous dose of tramadol versus pentazocine for reducing EA in children undergoing tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included 240 children aged 2–12 years who underwent elective tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy under general anesthesia with remimazolam induction. Patients received either pentazocine 0.4 mg/kg or tramadol 1 mg/kg intravenously immediately after surgery. The primary outcome was the incidence of EA, defined as a Pediatric Anesthesia Emergence Delirium (PAED) scale score ≥ 10 at any time during the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) stay. Secondary outcomes included PAED score trajectories over time, PACU stay duration, postoperative pain (FLACC scale), and adverse events (nausea/vomiting, respiratory depression). Statistical analyses primarily used multivariable logistic regression; secondary and sensitivity analyses (linear mixed models, generalized estimating equations, spline-based models, and Cox regression) were performed to assess robustness and are detailed in the Supplementary Material.ResultsThe unadjusted incidence of EA was 0.8% (1/120) in the tramadol group and 14.2% (17/120) in the pentazocine group (relative risk 17.00, 95% CI: 2.30-125.73, p < 0.001). Multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for potential confounders, showed that the pentazocine group had higher odds of EA (OR = 12.61, 95% CI: 2.33-234.43, p = 0.017), although the wide confidence intervals reflect the low event rate. Linear mixed models suggested a significant group-by-time interaction (p < 0.001), with higher PAED scores in the pentazocine group at 5 and 10 min but convergence by 20 min. Exploratory time-to-event analyses (Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression) suggested that the pentazocine group had a slower agitation resolution rate compared with the tramadol group (HR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.56–0.96, p = 0.025), consistent with faster resolution in the tramadol group. However, these results should be interpreted cautiously due to fixed-time assessments. PACU stay was shorter in the tramadol group (median 32 vs. 34 min, p < 0.001). Adverse event rates were low and comparable between groups. Extended analyses (GEE, spline-based models) consistently supported the primary findings (see Supplementary Material).ConclusionIn this retrospective cohort, tramadol was associated with a lower incidence of EA and faster recovery compared with pentazocine, without an observed increase in short-term adverse events. The adjusted odds ratio of 12.61 has an extremely wide 95% CI, reflecting statistical instability; this estimate should not be interpreted as a precise effect size. Due to the very low event rate (one event in the tramadol group) and other limitations (retrospective design, potential residual confounding), these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and suggestive rather than definitive. Further prospective studies are needed to confirm whether tramadol offers a clinically meaningful advantage over pentazocine in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07410-4.Keywords: Anesthesia, Emergence delirium, Tramadol, Pentazocine, Pain

Introduction

Emergence agitation (EA) represents a frequent and clinically significant complication in pediatric anesthesia practice. It is characterized by a constellation of behavioral disturbances during the emergence phase from general anesthesia, including involuntary crying, thrashing, disorientation, inconsolability, and a lack of purposeful eye contact or interaction with caregivers [1]. This phenomenon poses risks to the child (accidental extubation, surgical site disruption, falls), prolongs PACU stay, increases nursing workload and costs, and causes psychological distress for both child and parents [2, 3]. Understanding EA pathogenesis and developing effective preventive strategies are central to improving pediatric anesthesia quality and safety.

The reported incidence of EA varies widely, from 10% to 80% [4]. This variability is likely due to differences in study populations, surgical procedures, anesthetic regimens, and assessment criteria [5–7]. Preschool-aged children (2–5 years) are at highest risk for EA, possibly due to central nervous system immaturity, fear of unfamiliar environments, and limited communication skills [8, 9]. Furthermore, certain types of surgery, particularly otorhinolaryngologic procedures (notably tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy), ophthalmic surgeries, and head and neck operations, have been consistently associated with a higher incidence of EA [8, 10–13].

The etiology of EA is multifactorial, involving various risk factors. Key contributors include acute postoperative pain, preoperative anxiety, the rapid washout of volatile anesthetics (especially sevoflurane and desflurane), the use of anticholinergic agents, the child’s inherent temperament, and a history of previous surgery or anesthesia [14, 15]. Among these factors, postoperative pain is widely recognized as one of the most critical drivers of EA. Adequate postoperative analgesia significantly reduces EA incidence and severity, a cornerstone of EA management strategies [16, 17]. However, analgesic choice involves more than efficacy; sedative properties, central nervous system effects (anxiolytic or psychotomimetic), and pharmacokinetics in children profoundly influence emergence smoothness [18, 19].

Tramadol is an atypical centrally acting analgesic with a unique dual mechanism of action. It exerts analgesic effects by (1) weak µ-opioid receptor agonism via its parent compound and active metabolite M1, and (2) inhibiting serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake, thereby enhancing descending inhibitory pathways [20]. This dual mechanism provides tramadol with effective analgesia and potential anxiolytic/mood-stabilizing effects, theoretically promoting smoother emergence [21, 22]. In contrast, pentazocine is a mixed agonist-antagonist opioid acting primarily as a κ-receptor agonist with partial µ-receptor antagonism [23]. Its analgesic efficacy is well-established, but κ-receptor agonism is frequently associated with a range of psychotomimetic side effects, including dysphoria, anxiety, hallucinations, and nightmares, which have been extensively documented in adult patients [23]. In children, these adverse psychological effects may be difficult to articulate and can directly manifest as postoperative agitation, crying, and inconsolability, thereby potentially exacerbating the risk of EA [24, 25]. Although both drugs are widely used for acute postoperative pain in children due to their efficacy and mild respiratory effects, no study has directly compared their efficacy in preventing pediatric EA.

Therefore, this single-center, retrospective cohort study aims to compare the effects of a single postoperative intravenous dose of tramadol versus pentazocine on emergence agitation in children aged 2–12 years undergoing tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy under general anesthesia. We selected this specific surgical population due to its high incidence of EA and the uniformity of the procedure, which helps to control for confounding factors [11, 26, 27]. The primary outcome of this study is the incidence of EA, defined as a Pediatric Anesthesia Emergence Delirium (PAED) scale score ≥ 10 [28]. Secondary outcomes include: the dynamic trajectory of PAED scores over time (at 5, 10, 15, and 20 min post-PACU admission), the duration of PACU stay, postoperative pain scores (assessed by the FLACC scale), and the incidence of drug-related adverse events (nausea/vomiting and respiratory depression) [29]. Using statistical methods (linear mixed models, generalized estimating equations, and multivariable regression), we aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the two drugs in preventing EA [30]. Our goal is to provide evidence-based guidance for rational analgesic selection in pediatric patients, optimizing emergence management and anesthesia care quality [5].

Methods

Study design and ethical statement

This study was designed as a single-center, retrospective, observational cohort study and was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [31]. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Children’s Hospital Affiliated to Shandong University (also known as Jinan Children’s Hospital) (Approval No. SDFE-IRB/T-2026034). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants, and assent was obtained from the children where applicable. All patient data were de-identified prior to analysis to protect patient privacy.

Study population and data source

Setting and time period

The study was conducted at the Department of Anesthesiology and the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) of Jinan Children’s Hospital. Electronic medical records from the hospital information system were systematically reviewed to identify all eligible children between January 1, 2022, and June 30, 2024. Data extracted included anesthesia records, PACU nursing records, and medication orders.

Inclusion criteria

Patients were included if they met all of the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 2 years and ≤ 12 years (calculated on the date of surgery); (2) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification I or II [32]; (3) underwent elective tonsillectomy with or without adenoidectomy under general anesthesia, with an expected surgical duration < 2 h; (4) received a standardized general anesthesia protocol: induction with remimazolam 0.2 mg/kg, propofol 2 mg/kg, sufentanil 0.2 µg/kg, rocuronium 0.3 mg/kg, ondansetron 0.15 mg/kg, and penehyclidine 0.01 mg/kg; anesthesia was maintained with continuous infusion of propofol 0.05 mg/kg/min and remifentanil 0.2 µg/kg/min, plus sevoflurane 2.5% inhaled; no other sedative or hypnotic agents (e.g., midazolam, ketamine, dexmedetomidine) were administered beyond the specified protocol; (5) received a single intravenous dose of either pentazocine 0.4 mg/kg or tramadol 1 mg/kg after the completion of surgery, before entering the PACU with a laryngeal mask airway in place, with clear documentation of the medication. The choice of tramadol or pentazocine was made by the attending anesthesiologist based on routine clinical practice; no randomization was performed.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) age < 2 years or > 12 years; (2) ASA classification ≥ III, or a pre-existing definitive diagnosis of neurodevelopmental delay, epilepsy, autism spectrum disorder, psychiatric or behavioral abnormalities, or severe communication difficulties; (3) use of any other sedative medication that could significantly affect consciousness or behavior (e.g., dexmedetomidine, continuous propofol infusion, benzodiazepines, ketamine, clonidine) within 24 h before surgery, intraoperatively, or during the PACU stay; (4) a known history of allergy to pentazocine or tramadol; (5) PACU nursing records with severe lack of detail or overly brief descriptions of the child’s state of consciousness and behavior (e.g., only “stable” or “awake” recorded, with no specific behavioral descriptions), rendering retrospective PAED scoring impossible; (6) contradictory or missing key information in the medical records, preventing confirmation of medication administration or outcome assessment.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was based on the primary outcome, the incidence of emergence agitation (EA), for a two-group comparison. Based on literature review and preliminary data from our center, the expected EA incidence was estimated at approximately 20% for the pentazocine group and 5% for the tramadol group [33–35]. With a two-sided α of 0.05 and a power of 80% (β = 0.20), the sample size required per group was calculated using the standard formula for comparing two independent proportions. The calculation yielded a minimum requirement of 73 patients per group. To account for an anticipated 10% rate of missing data, the sample size was increased, resulting in a need for 80 patients per group. The final study cohort comprised 240 patients, with 120 patients in each group, exceeding the minimum required sample size and thus providing adequate statistical power [36].

Variable definitions and data collection

Baseline characteristics

Baseline data for all included patients were extracted from the electronic medical record system, including: age, sex, weight, ASA classification, type of surgery, and anesthesia time (minutes).

Outcome variable definitions

(2) Secondary Outcomes: (1) Severity of agitation, represented by the total PAED score; (2) Temporal trajectory of agitation, assessed by PAED scores recorded at four distinct time points: 5, 10, 15, and 20 min after PACU admission; (3) Recovery efficiency, measured by the total duration of PACU stay (minutes); (4) Postoperative pain, assessed using the FLACC scale (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability) [29], with the highest pain score during the PACU stay recorded; (5) Adverse drug events, including the occurrence of nausea, vomiting, and respiratory depression (defined as respiratory rate < 12 breaths/min or SpO₂ <90%) newly observed during the PACU stay [37].

Retrospective PAED scale scoring method

Due to the retrospective design of this study, PAED scores were derived retrospectively based on behavioral descriptions documented in the PACU nursing records. Prior to data collection, two researchers (R1, R2) collaboratively developed a standardized “Nursing Record Text-to-PAED Score Conversion Coding Manual.” This manual systematically mapped common descriptive phrases found in nursing records (e.g., “violent crying, inconsolable by holding or verbal comfort,” “vacant stare, does not look at parents,” “thrashing limbs, requiring restraint”) to the five domains of the PAED scale (eye contact, purposeful actions, awareness of surroundings, restlessness, inconsolability) and assigned corresponding scores (0–4) [38]. After receiving unified training, the two researchers, blinded to the patients’ group allocation (pentazocine or tramadol), independently scored the PAED items for each time point based on the text in the nursing records. The nursing records were anonymized and did not contain any information about the analgesic administered, ensuring complete blinding. To assess inter-rater reliability, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for the total scores was calculated on a randomly selected 10% of the cases. The calculated ICC was 0.92 (95% CI: 0.87–0.95), indicating good reliability. An ICC > 0.80 was considered acceptable; otherwise, retraining and re-evaluation were conducted. The final score for each time point was taken as the average of the two researchers’ scores [39] Therefore, these reconstructed PAED scores should be considered semi-quantitative approximations rather than validated measurements.

Statistical analysis

Statistical software

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.3). Graphical visualizations were generated using the ggplot2 package (version 3.3.5). All tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Clinical data analysis

Continuous variables were first tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between two groups using Student’s t-test; non-normally distributed data were expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages (n, %) and compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when any expected cell count was less than 5. Baseline characteristics compared between groups included age, sex, weight, ASA classification, and anesthesia time.

Primary analysis

The primary outcome (incidence of emergence agitation, defined as PAED score ≥ 10) was analyzed using multivariable logistic regression. The model included treatment group (pentazocine vs. tramadol) as the independent variable, adjusting for prespecified covariates: age, sex, weight, ASA classification, anesthesia time, and FLACC pain score. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). This analysis was defined as the main analysis for the primary hypothesis.

Secondary and sensitivity analyses

To explore secondary outcomes (PAED trajectories, time to agitation resolution, adverse events) and to assess the robustness of the primary findings, we performed several additional analyses. These included: linear mixed models for repeated PAED measurements; generalized estimating equations (GEE) as a sensitivity analysis for the mixed model; spline-based mixed models to flexibly model nonlinear time trajectories; subgroup analysis by age group (2–5 years vs. 6–12 years) with interaction testing; and Poisson regression for adverse events with PACU time as an offset. Given the small number of EA events and the exploratory nature of these analyses, detailed results are presented in the Supplementary Material (Tables S1-S4, Figures S1-S4).

Justification for multiple secondary models

Although the total number of EA events is small, we performed several complementary secondary analyses (linear mixed models, GEE, spline-based models, subgroup interaction, Poisson regression) to assess the robustness of the primary logistic regression findings. These models are intended as sensitivity analyses to test whether the primary conclusion changes under different statistical assumptions, not for definitive inference. Given the limited event count, results from these complex models should be interpreted with caution, and the primary conclusion is based on the simpler multivariable logistic regression.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 240 children undergoing tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy under general anesthesia were enrolled in this study, with 120 patients in the tramadol group and 120 in the pentazocine group. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Age was normally distributed and did not differ significantly between groups (tramadol: 6.24 ± 2.23 years; pentazocine: 5.71 ± 2.12 years; t = 1.83, df = 238, p = 0.069). Sex distribution was comparable, with 77 males (64.2%) in the tramadol group and 75 males (62.5%) in the pentazocine group (χ² = 0.08, df = 1, p = 0.803). Weight was also similar between groups (tramadol: 26.92 ± 11.81 kg; pentazocine: 26.01 ± 12.73 kg; t = 0.85, df = 238, p = 0.423). ASA classification showed no difference, with 90.8% of patients classified as ASA I in both groups (Fisher’s exact test, p = 1.000). Anesthesia time was also comparable (tramadol: 25.0 ± 4.3 min; pentazocine: 24.62 ± 4.51 min; t = 0.83, df = 238, p = 0.416). All patients underwent the same surgical procedure, ensuring comparability. These data confirm that the two treatment groups were generally well-balanced with respect to all measured baseline covariates, providing a solid foundation for subsequent comparative analyses.

Incidence of emergence agitation - primary analysis

The unadjusted incidence of emergence agitation (EA, defined as PAED score ≥ 10) was 0.8% (1/120) in the tramadol group and 14.2% (17/120) in the pentazocine group (relative risk = 17.00, 95% CI: 2.30-125.73, p < 0.001). To adjust for potential confounders, we performed multivariable logistic regression including age, sex, weight, ASA class, anesthesia time, and FLACC score. The pentazocine group had significantly higher odds of EA compared with the tramadol group (OR = 12.61, 95% CI: 2.33-234.43, p = 0.017). FLACC pain score was also a significant predictor (OR = 2.62, 95% CI: 1.25–5.49, p = 0.010). The extremely wide confidence intervals reflect the very low event rate (only one event in the tramadol group); therefore, this estimate should be interpreted with great caution and not as a precise effect size. Results of the multivariable model are shown in Table 2. A sensitivity analysis without FLACC adjustment yielded results directionally consistent with the primary analysis (Table S8).

Trajectory of PAED scores and secondary analyses

Inter-rater reliability for retrospective PAED scoring was high (ICC = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.87–0.95). Linear mixed model analysis of PAED scores over time (adjusted for FLACC, age, and sex) revealed a significant group-by-time interaction (p < 0.001). At 5 min post‑admission, PAED scores were significantly higher in the pentazocine group than in the tramadol group (estimated difference = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.19–0.96, p = 0.003). The decrease from 5 to 10 min was smaller in the pentazocine group (interaction estimate = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.01–0.74, p = 0.043), indicating delayed early resolution. By 20 min, scores converged (interaction estimate = -0.73, 95% CI: -1.09 to -0.36, p < 0.001). FLACC pain score was a strong predictor across all time points (estimate = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.44–1.12, p < 0.001). Detailed model outputs (standard errors, t values, etc.) are provided in Supplementary Table S6 (linear mixed model) and Figure S4 (raw data with mean ± 95% CI).

Sensitivity analyses using generalized estimating equations (GEE) and spline-based mixed models confirmed these findings (Supplementary Tables S1-S2, Figure S1). Subgroup analysis by age (2–5 vs. 6–12 years) showed no heterogeneity of treatment effect (interaction p = 0.991, Supplementary Table S3). Poisson regression for adverse events, with PACU time as an offset, detected no significant difference between groups (RR = 1.82, 95% CI: 0.33–9.92, p = 0.490; Supplementary Table S4). Time-to-event analyses (Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression) suggested that the pentazocine group had a slower agitation resolution rate compared with the tramadol group (log-rank p < 0.001; HR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.56–0.96, p = 0.025), consistent with faster resolution in the tramadol group. However, because PAED scores were assessed only at fixed time points, the data are interval-censored; therefore, these results should be interpreted as exploratory only. Full details are provided in the Supplementary Material (Table S7, Figure S6).

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes are summarized in Table 3. FLACC pain scores (median 0 in both groups) showed a statistically significant difference in distribution (p < 0.001), largely due to a few non-zero values. Clinically, this indicates that although most children in both groups had no pain, a small subset of patients in the pentazocine group experienced clinically meaningful pain (FLACC ≥ 4), which may have contributed to their higher early agitation scores. PACU stay duration was significantly shorter in the tramadol group (median 32 min, IQR 30-34.25 vs. 34 min, IQR 32–36; p < 0.001). Adverse events (nausea/vomiting and respiratory depression) were rare and comparable between the two groups (p = 1.000 for both comparisons), indicating a similar short‑term safety profile.

Discussion

This study provides the first systematic comparison of pentazocine and tramadol for the prevention of emergence agitation (EA, defined as PAED score ≥ 10) in children undergoing tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy. In this retrospective study, tramadol was associated with a lower incidence of EA and faster recovery compared with pentazocine, with a comparable short-term safety profile. Given the low event rate and other limitations, these findings should be interpreted as suggestive rather than definitive. Linear mixed models revealed distinct temporal trajectories. Patients receiving pentazocine had higher agitation scores during the early PACU period, suggesting delayed resolution, although scores converged by 20 min. FLACC pain score was a strong predictor of agitation severity across all models, reaffirming the role of adequate postoperative analgesia in EA prevention. Sensitivity analyses (e.g., generalized estimating equations, spline-based modeling, subgroup analysis) consistently supported the robustness of these findings (see Supplementary Material).

Comparison with previous studies

Direct comparisons between pentazocine and tramadol for pediatric EA prevention are scarce. Previous studies have shown that tramadol was associated with a reduction of EA in children [21, 22], consistent with our findings. Data on pentazocine in children are limited, but adult studies suggest psychotomimetic effects (e.g., dysphoria, anxiety) that may increase EA risk [40–43]. Our study is the first to systematically observe higher EA rates in children receiving pentazocine, likely related to its κ-receptor agonist-induced adverse effects.

However, alternative explanations for the large observed difference should be considered. First, the single event in the tramadol group (0.8%) could be a chance finding given the low overall EA rate in this cohort. Second, unmeasured differences in perioperative pain management or preoperative anxiety between groups may have contributed to the disparity, despite multivariable adjustment. Third, misclassification bias from retrospective PAED scoring could have affected the two groups differently. Therefore, while tramadol appears promising, these findings require confirmation in prospective studies.

Pharmacological mechanisms underlying the differences

The distinct pharmacological profiles of the two drugs likely explain the observed differences. Tramadol, an atypical opioid, provides analgesia through µ-receptor agonism and monoamine reuptake inhibition, with additional anxiolytic effects that may facilitate smooth emergence [44]. Pentazocine, a κ-receptor agonist, produces analgesia but is associated with dysphoria and hallucinations, possibly mediated by κ-induced inhibition of dopamine release [45–47]. The higher early PAED scores in the pentazocine group may reflect these psychotomimetic effects, while later convergence could reflect drug metabolism or adaptation to the awakening environment [35]. Notably, pain consistently predicted agitation across both groups. This finding suggests that tramadol’s analgesic effect does not fully account for its advantage, leaving room for potential non-analgesic contributions [48–50].

Role of pain and clinical implications

The consistent finding that FLACC pain score strongly predicts PAED scores reaffirms that postoperative pain is a primary driver of EA [5, 51, 52]. Although median pain scores were identical between groups, the difference in distribution highlights that pain may be underestimated in some children. From a clinical perspective, agents with dual mechanisms (e.g., tramadol) that address both pain and anxiety may more effectively prevent EA by targeting its multifactorial etiology [53]. Administering tramadol intraoperatively or immediately postoperatively may offer greater EA prophylaxis than pentazocine, given its faster onset and lower psychotomimetic potential.

Safety and clinical significance

Both drugs exhibited low and comparable adverse event rates, providing preliminary safety evidence for their short-term use in children. The nausea/vomiting rates were lower than reported averages [54–56], possibly due to the surgical type and anesthetic regimen. The single case of respiratory depression in the pentazocine group warrants caution, though not statistically significant. Due to low event rates, the study’s power to detect between-group differences was limited, necessitating larger studies to confirm safety [57]. Although the 2-minute difference in median PACU stay was statistically significant, its clinical relevance is uncertain. A difference of this magnitude is unlikely to be meaningful in routine clinical practice, especially given the other limitations of the study [58, 59].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include its relatively large sample size, head-to-head comparison of two commonly used analgesics, and we performed several complementary secondary analyses (linear mixed models, GEE, spline-based models, etc.) as a comprehensive statistical exploration. Detailed results of these secondary analyses are provided in the Supplementary Material. However, limitations must be acknowledged: the retrospective design may introduce selection and information bias; anesthesiologists may have preferentially selected tramadol or pentazocine based on their perception of the child’s risk for EA or expected postoperative behavior, introducing confounding by indication that cannot be fully adjusted for with the available covariates. Propensity score methods were considered but were not feasible due to the extremely low event count (which would lead to unstable models); residual confounding by unmeasured factors (e.g., preoperative anxiety, parental presence, clinician preference, intraoperative opioid dosing) cannot be excluded, and propensity score matching was not performed due to the extremely low event rate (which would lead to unstable models); therefore, causal interpretations should be avoided [60]; It is important to emphasize that the PAED scale was originally validated for real-time bedside assessment; our retrospective reconstruction yields only approximate scores and carries a substantial risk of misclassification, particularly for items such as eye contact and awareness of surroundings; therefore, these scores should be considered semi-quantitative estimations rather than precise measurements [61]; limited power for rare events; inclusion of only one surgical procedure limits generalizability [62]; lack of dose standardization; and short follow-up precluding assessment of long-term outcomes [63]. Among these, a particularly critical limitation is the extremely low event rate for the primary outcome, with only one event observed in the tramadol group. This leads to unstable odds ratio estimates and wide confidence intervals. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and suggestive rather than definitive, and strong causal language has been avoided.

Future research directions

Future research should include prospective randomized trials to confirm our findings and explore optimal dosing regimens [64], along with mechanistic studies to elucidate tramadol’s anxiolytic pathways [20], and long-term follow-up to assess behavioral outcomes [65]. Pharmacoeconomic evaluations and development of personalized prediction models integrating preoperative traits and pharmacogenomics will guide precision prophylaxis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in this retrospective cohort study, compared with pentazocine, tramadol was associated with a lower incidence of emergence agitation after general anesthesia and a shorter PACU stay, without an observed increase in short-term adverse event risk. However, due to the extremely low event rate (only one event in the tramadol group) and potential residual confounding, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in prospective studies. The associated confidence intervals are very wide, indicating that the magnitude of the observed association is highly uncertain. Tramadol exhibits a smoother recovery trajectory during the early emergence phase, likely attributable to its unique dual mechanism of analgesia and anxiolysis. Despite the inherent limitations of its retrospective design, the consistency of findings across multiple sensitivity analyses provides reliable evidence for clinical decision-making. For children undergoing tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy, tramadol may be preferred for EA prevention, particularly in ambulatory surgery settings. It is important to emphasize that the PAED scale was originally validated for real-time bedside assessment. Our retrospective reconstruction yields only approximate scores and carries a substantial risk of misclassification, particularly for items such as eye contact and awareness of surroundings.
