An interferonopathy associated with a monoallelic variant in TRIM28
Authors: Sonia Melo Gomes, Ebun Omoyinmi, Ying Hong, Gillian Rice, Luis Seabra, Vincent Bondet, Darragh Duffy, Glenn Anderson, Florence Adams, Fionnuala McMorrow, Charalampia Papadopoulou, J A Melo Gomes, Paul Brogan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn this work, we describe the clinical and functional changes in a family with a novel type I interferonopathy phenotype which we suggest could be associated with a TRIM28 variant, presenting with chilblains, skin ulceration, lipodystrophy and calcinosis. TRIM28 has multiple roles in regulating human health and disease, which include DNA repair and anti-viral response. The latter includes negative regulation of IRF7, one of the master regulators of the interferon response.MethodsWhole exome sequencing and targeted panel sequencing was performed on DNA extracted from peripheral blood. Functional work included gene (qPCR), protein (WB) and cytokine (MSD and SIMOA) expression studies. Transfection studies of siRNA in control Human Dermal Fibroblast Cells (HDFCs) using RNAiMAX and of wild-type protein in patient HDFCs using FuGENE HD were also performed.ResultsAnalysis of WES data according to a dominant model with incomplete penetrance revealed TRIM28 c.A623G; p.Y208C in the proband of Family A and his mildly affected mother. Gene expression studies revealed persistent elevation of interferon inducible proteins including increased gene as well as protein expression of IRF7. Cytokine measurements revealed a marked elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines particularly IFNβ in affected individuals. A good clinical and functional response to treatment with baricitinib was documented, however the proband subsequently died of septic complications. TRIM28-siRNA transfection into control HDFCs resulted in changes in gene, protein and cytokine expression similar to those seen in the proband. Transfection of a wild-type pEGFP-TRIM28 plasmid into the proband’s HDFCs achieved partial rescue of the cellular phenotype.DiscussionWe describe a novel and severe interferonopathy potentially associated with an autosomal dominant TRIM28 variant with an incomplete penetrance disease model, presenting with skin ulceration, calcinosis, high interferon scores, and upregulation of IRF7. Pathogenicity was supported by TRIM28 gene silencing in healthy HDFCs. Introduction

Type-I interferonopathies emerged as a group of auto-inflammatory diseases around two decades ago and are characterized by a high interferon (IFN) signature as a common pathogenic mechanism, which frequently involves aberrant DNA sensing. Since the identification of the first monogenic defects related to Aicardi-Goutières Syndrome (AGS), nearly 40 different genotypes have been identified in association with these diseases thus far [1].

This ever-expanding group of diseases consists of complex genetic disorders resulting from disruption in the homeostatic control of the normally very tightly regulated IFN responses in immune host cells. Whilst encompassing a wide range of phenotypes [1], the clinical presentation of type-I interferonopathies can show a significant degree of overlap between different diseases, an indication of their common pathogenic mechanisms, albeit with varying grades of phenotypic expression and severity [2]. Examples of common clinical phenotypes in interferonopathies are vasculitic skin lesions, other vasculopathies, cerebral calcifications and variable pulmonary disease [3]. Despite being considered as autoinflammatory diseases, in some instances there may be some spillover into autoimmunity as fluctuating titers of antinuclear antigen (ANA) and other autoantibodies are frequently seen, although these do not show any correlation with disease severity nor with immune complex deposition-mediated tissue damage [1, 3]. Indeed, autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and juvenile dermatomyositis (JDM) also have a high IFN-related gene expression as well as overlapping clinical features, and have been designated autoimmune interferonopathies [4].

TRIM28, Tripartite Motif Containing 28, also known as KRAB-associated protein 1 (KAP1) and transcription intermediary factor 1β (TIF1β)(Fig. 1-L), has a major role in health and disease [5], being involved in transcriptional coregulation, DNA damage repair, regulation of retroviral replication, autophagy and cancer [5–7]. TRIM28 also has a major role in response to viral infections, via modulation of host immune response as well as directly suppressing viral gene expression [5–7]. One example of modulation of host immune response is negative regulation of Interferon Regulatory Factor(IRF)-7 in response to viral infections, through its small ubiquitin related modifiers (SUMO) E3 ligase activity. In fact, TRIM28 was the first SUMO E3 ligase recognized as specific for IRF7 [8]. TRIM-28 mediated sumoylation of IRF7 is increased in viral infections to control the interferon response. As such, overexpression of TRIM28 inhibits IRF7 transactivation, whilst TRIM28 knockdown results in the contrary, with heightened IFN signalling and antiviral responses. The RBCC and middle domains, corresponding to amino acid residues 1-617, were shown to be esssential for interaction with IRF7 by immunoprecipitation studies [8].

IRF7 belongs to a family of transcription factors involved in regulation of innate and adaptive immune responses, including anti-viral response and immune cell differentiation [9]. IRF7 is closely related to IRF3 and has an important role in feedback amplification of the IFN-α/β response, inclusively being named the master regulator of IFN-I production [10, 11]. In addition, IRF7 has been linked to Epstein-Barr virus latency and has a role in cell differentiation, regulation of oncogenesis and apoptosis [11, 12]. IRF7 also has a role in autoimmune diseases associated with increased IFN-I production such as SLE [11] and systemic sclerosis [13].

In this work, we describe a novel interferonopathy phenotype in two related patients characterized by chilblains, joint contractures skin ulceration, lipodystrophy and calcinosis. We suggest that this phenotype could be caused by a loss of function mutation in TRIM28 with autosomal dominant transmission and variable penetrance, through impaired regulation of IRF7 leading to an uncontrolled IFN-I response.

Methods

Patients

Patients were recruited at GOSH with informed consent and assent as appropriate. Demographics, clinical and treatment-response data were collected from clinical records.

Genetic studies

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) library preparation was performed with the Ilumina Nextera Rapid Capture Exome Library Preparation Kit according to manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced in Hiseq 1000 or NextSeq 500 platforms. Reads were aligned to GRCh38 using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner [14] annotation was performed using wANNOVAR [15] and variants were filtered in-house. The targeted gene panel (TGP) used in this study was the Vasculitis and Inflammation Panel, as previously described [16]. Sanger based DNA sequencing was used for variant confirmation.

Functional experiments

RNA extraction and quantification, c-DNA synthesis, SYBR green qPCR reaction were performed as previously described [17]. Quantitect primers (Qiagen) were used for qPCR reactions.

The interferon score (IS) was performed in peripheral blood as previously described [18].

Cytokine measurements

Cytokine quantification assays were performed by using a sandwich assay, VPLEX assay and Interferon Combo, from MesoScale Diagnostics (MSD), according to manufacturer’s instructions. Single molecular array (SIMOA) digital ELISA technology to was performed to measure IFN-α as previously described [19].

Protein expression

Protein expression was analysed by Western Blot (WB). Protein lysate extraction, protein quantification and WB were performed as previously described [17, 20]. The list of antibodies used is provided in Supplementary Table-1.

Electron microscopy

Electron microscopy studies were performed on skin biopsies as previously described [21].

Transfection of siRNA in HDFCs

Gene silencing experiments were performed using RNAiMAX (cat no. 13778-075, Thermo Fisher Scientific). HDFCs were seeded at 60–80% confluency and incubated in reduced serum transfection medium (Opti-Mem, cat no 31985062, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The RNAiMAX lipofectamine reagent was incubated with 10µM of siRNA for TRIM28 (kit of TRIM28 human siRNA duplexes, cat no SR306853, Origene) or control scrambled siRNA for 20 min at RT and then added to cells. Cells were incubated with the transfection reagents for 48 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2, after which supernatants were collected for cytokine measurements, and cells collected for either RNA or protein extraction. Evaluation of knockdown efficiency of siRNA transfection on TRIM28 expression was performed by qPCR.

Transfection of plasmid DNA in HDFCs

Transfection of plasmid DNA in HDFCs was performed using the FuGENE HD transfection reagent (cat no. E231A, Promega). The plasmid DNA used for transfection was the pEGFP-TRIM28 plasmid (cat no. 45568, Addgene) [8]. As the respective vector backbone was not available, a vector constructed in-house was used for control (third generation lentiviral vector with pCCL backbone – pCCL-EFS-eGFP) (Supplementary Fig. 1) [22]. Fugene HD transfection reagent and plasmid DNA (pEGFP-TRIM28 plasmid or empty vector) were incubated at a ratio of 3.5:1 (7 µl of transfection reagent and 2 µg of plasmid DNA per well of a 6 well-plate) in 100uL of Opti-Mem medium for 15 min at RT after vortexing. The FuGENE HD/ DNA complex was then added to the medium of plated HDFCs (seeded at 60–80% confluency) and mixed by pipetting. Cells were harvested and supernatant collected following a 48 h incubation at 37 °C, 5% CO2. WB was performed to assess transfection efficiency and TRIM28 overexpression in HDFCs.

Statistical analysis

Numeric data were expressed as mean (standard error of mean) unless otherwise specified. Comparative statistical analyses (ANOVA/T-test) were performed using GraphPad Prism Software version 9.

Results

Family A – clinical presentation

The proband in Family A was Portuguese. The mother of the proband had a history of rheumatic fever with valvulopathy, and chilblains in her twenties.

Symptoms started in the proband before the age of 2-years with recurrent erythematous cutaneous lesions on face, lower limbs and extremities, with ulcerations leading to tissue loss on the helix of the ears. The skin lesions progressed to the trunk and shoulder area, accompanied by Raynaud’s phenomenon, livedo reticularis and arthralgia with limitation of movement and contractures. At 13-years-old, he had multiple joint contractures, generalized cutaneous erythematous/hyperpigmented lesions (worsening with the cold) and lipodystrophy. Blood tests showed increased ESR (mean: 56 mm/h; range: 49–68 mm/h) and CRP (10-30 mg/L; range < 10 mg/L), normal biochemistry, complement levels, and negative autoantibodies apart from an intermittently positive ANA (1/320). The skin biopsy was suggestive of vasculitis with necrosis in the dermis and hypodermis. MRI of the thighs showed signs of lipodystrophy. The presentation evolved with flares of skin ulceration and articular symptoms controlled with anti-inflammatories, nifedipine and oral prednisolone. At 22-years-old there was a significant clinical worsening, with ulcerated lesions on hands, feet, face and ears, and joint contractures leading to disability (Fig. 1).

Azathioprine and methotrexate were tried without benefit. A severe sensory-motor polyneuropathy was detected on electromyogram and neuroimaging (MRI/CT brain) showed prominence of cerebral sulci and basal ganglia calcifications (Fig. 1G-I). There was also dysphagia requiring a PEG insertion due to unsafe swallow.

The skin ulcerations lead to areas of bone exposure with others suggestive of calcinosis with ulceration (Fig. 1-F).

Whole exome sequencing results

WES was performed on the immediate family. Analysis of a disease model of incomplete penetrance revealed the NM_005762.3(TRIM28):c.623 A > G; Chr19(GRCh38):g.58,547,412 A > G; p.Y208C variant in exon 4 of TRIM28.

DNA Sanger sequencing confirmed the heterozygous TRIM28 c.623 A > G; p.Y208C variant in the proband and mother. The maternal grandmother and uncle also carried the c.623 A < G; p.Y208C variant (Supplementary Figure-2) but were asymptomatic. The clinical and laboratory features of the family members presenting the TRIM28 c.623 A < G; p.Y208C variant are summarised in Supplementary Table 2. This variant was predicted as deleterious according to SIFT, Polyphen-2 and Mutation Taster, and well conserved according to the PhyloP score (2.7; conserved > 1.6). The variant is present in GnomAD [23] but is extremely rare, counting a total of 5 (heterozygous) alleles in the database with a resulting allele frequency of 3.0984e-06.

Polymorphisms in other pro-inflammatory genes may contribute to modulate the phenotype

Polymorphisms in known inflammatory genes present in the proband were analysed by Sanger sequencing in family members with the TRIM28 variant (Supplementary Figure-3), revealing a predominance of certain subsets of genes related to monogenic interferonopathies in the proband, including IFIH1, proteasome genes, and DDX58 as well as DNASE1 and ITGAM.

Expression of interferon stimulated genes is increased at gene and protein levels

Gene expression studies (qPCR) for the proband and mother showed increased expression of ISGs such as C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10), stimulator of interferon response cGAMP interactor 1 (STING1), IFN-β and IFN-γ in the proband and in the mother (at lower level). There was no significant variation in expression of TRIM28 or activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) (Fig. 2-A).

There was a marked increase in the expression of IRF7, but not of IRF3, in proband-A and increase in the mother and other WT/p.Y208C family members (Fig. 2-B-C). These results are compatible with a loss of a specific inhibitor of IRF7. Gene expression of IL-1B and IL-6 was also increased in the proband (Fig. 2-D).

In addition, ultra-structure analysis via electron microscopy of a skin biopsy in the proband showed the presence of tubulo-reticular inclusions (Fig. 1-J-K), a marker of interferon stimulation, even in seemingly unaffected tissue on normal histology.

Protein expression studies in PBMCs showed increased protein expression of IRF7 in the proband and mother (Fig. 2-G), but not in other family members (Fig. 2-I). Phosphorylated signal transducer and activator of transcription 2 (P-STAT2) was also increased in proband-A (Fig. 2-H).

IFNa is elevated in proband-A and mother

Cytokine levels were elevated in proband-A compared to healthy control except for IL-10 and were normal in other family members (Fig. 2-J-P).

Levels of IFN-α assessed by SIMOA assay were increased in the proband and the mother (II-5) but normal in all other family members (Fig. 2-F).

Anti-TIF1-β antibodies are absent in serum

TRIM28 is also referred to as TIF1-β. As anti-TIF1-β autoantibodies have been described in JDM we tested for these in Family A, with negative results (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Clinical and functional response to treatment with the janus kinase inhibitor baricitinib

Treatment with baricitinib [24, 25] (6 mg/day orally) was started and the response evaluated at 1, 3 and 6 months, on clinical and functional levels. Clinical endpoints included photographic documentation of skin lesions, blood tests and disease activity, assessed using the STING-associated vasculopathy with onset in infancy (SAVI) disease activity rating scale [26] (given the clinical similarity between phenotypes). Biomarker assessment of functional response to treatment included IS, cytokine measurements, gene and protein expression studies.

There was a marked improvement in skin lesions over-time (Supplementary Fig. 5), including healing of deep ulcerations on both wrists at 6 months of treatment. The disease activity score also improved (Supplementary Table-3).

The laboratory and functional response also showed significant improvements: increase in haemoglobin levels and decrease in ESR, CRP, IS, expression of IRF7 and levels of inflammatory cytokines (Supplementary Fig. 6). More specifically, the interferon score completely normalised, reducing from 21.8 pre-treatment to 7.83 at 1month, 2.73 at 3 months and 0.33 at 6 months of treatment, reflecting the clinical improvement. Unfortunately, despite marked improvement on baricitinib, the patient ultimately died at around 7 months of immunosuppressive treatment due to septic complications of an infected heel ulcer with bone exposure. The use of JAK-inhibitors is linked to an increased risk of infectious adverse events, including opportunistic infections [27]. The proband’s deep skin ulcerations constituted potential points of entry for infection, thus conferring an additional risk.

Discussion

In this work, we describe a family with a novel and potentially severe type-I IFN phenotype with WES analysis revealing the presence of a rare mono-allelic variant in TRIM28 with incomplete penetrance. Clinically, the phenotype was characterized by overlapping features of AGS, proteasome-associated autoinflammatory syndromes (PRAAS) and SAVI.

The joint contractures, lipoatrophy/lipodystrophy and muscle atrophy are reminiscent of PRAAS, whilst the intracranial calcifications are usually present not only in PRAAS but also AGS, the same being true for soft tissue calcinosis; white matter changes are usually seen in different subtypes of AGS, with polyneuropathy being more frequently linked for example to ADAR1 or RNASE2HB mutations; the severity of the skin ulcerations with loss of substance is characteristic of SAVI, although the proband did not present the typical facial rash; the clinical presentation in the mother on the other hand (with chilblains only) could be reminiscent of familial chilblain lupus associated to TREX1 mutations. Given the severe type I interferonopathy phenotype, treatment with baricitinib was initiated, unfortunately at a time where the proband had already accrued significant damage. JAK inhibitors block interferon response by inhibiting the phosphorylation of the JAK signalling pathway and are the most widely used treatment options for type I interferonopathies albeit with varying clinical responses. Other potential therapeutic options are IFN blockade, either directed to the free molecules or their receptor (IFNAR), or the use of reverse-transcriptase inhibitors which block the reverse transcription of endogenous retro-elements. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation has been performed in very rare instances with variable outcomes but does not correct the non-haematopoietic component of these diseases [28].

Functional data showed a markedly raised IFN-I signature, increased expression of IRF7 and good clinical and functional response to JAK inhibition with baricitinib. TRIM28 knock-down experiments in control HDFCs recapitulated the cellular phenotype observed in patient’s cells.

The construction of an incomplete penetrance disease model in Family A suggested TRIM28 (c.A623G; p.Y208C) as a variant of interest, on the premise that the variant could cause a LOF effect and increased expression of IRF7. Arguments in favour of this variant having a pathogenic role include its rarity in population databases [23, 29] and a damaging prediction by several prediction algorithms, which are respectively a moderate and supporting criteria for pathogenicity according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/ Association for Molecular pathology (ACMG/AMP) classification [30]. More speciafically, this variant is present at extremely low frequencies in control databases (PM2) meeting Sherloc criteria [31] for pathogenicity for AD inheritance (present in ≤ 8 alleles in population databases) and is located in a highly conserved amino acid residue, with several in silico prediction models supporting a deleterious effect (PP3). Although these are not sufficient on their own to classify this variant as pathogenic, mainly due to the fact that only one family is known to be affected thus far, additional supporting data is available. GnomAD shows evidence for loss of function intolerance in TRIM28. This evidence includes a high probability of loss of function intolerance (pLI) score of 1 (with scores closer to 1 (≥ 0.9) indicating that the gene or transcript cannot tolerate truncating variation including haploinsufficiency due to heterozygous variants) and a low loss of function observed/expected upper bound fraction (LOEUF) of 0.165, corresponding to a gene percentile 1. This means that TRIM28 is among the 1% of genes with highest predicted LOF constraint. The number of observed missense variants in TRIM28 is also lower than expected, indicating increased constraints. Increased genetic constraint signifies intolerance to mutational changes [23, 29].

This report highlights a common challenge when exploring potential novel monogenetic causes of disease; to date incomplete disease penetrance or clinical non-penetrance has been described in several interferonopathies, mainly those associated with a dominant mode of transmission. Common examples are dominant negative mutations in TREX1, COPA, as well as the p.G1007R mutation in ADAR1, and GOF mutations in IFIH1. Regarding COPA mutations, the estimated rate of clinical non-penetrance is of about 30%, with asymptomatic carriers having normal or only mildly raised IS [1]. In contrast, IFIH1 mutations have been associated with a rate of clinical non-penetrance of 13.5%, with the vast majority of clinical asymptomatic individuals having documented raised IS [32]. In recessive interferonopathies the potential rate of non-penetrance is not known, as asymptomatic siblings are usually not genotyped, despite variable expression being described in AR forms of AGS for example [1].

One of the possibilities to explain these differences in penetrance is cumulative genetic burden, where variants in other immune-related genes may modulate the phenotype and contribute to disease risk [33]. Interestingly, there was a relative increase in the proband for polymorphisms in genes related to other monogenic interferonopathies, such as IFIH1, DDX58, PSMB4, PSMB9 and PSMB11 (encoding a catalytic subunit of the proteasome but not yet associated with human disease), as well as ITGAM (several polymorphisms associated with risk of SLE) and DNASE1 (related to a form of monogenic SLE but in which a raised IS has not yet been documented). Two of the variants identified in IFIH1 have been described as disease modifying: the p.A946T polymorphism (found in heterozygous state in the proband), which has been shown to be a GOF risk variant associated with increased production of IFN-I, increased risk of autoimmunity and a modifying effect on other autoimmune disease risk alleles, and the p.H843R (homozygous in the proband, heterozygous in the mother (II-4) and uncle (II-5), another risk allele which together with p.A946T constitute a haplotype described in association with increased risk of diabetes mellitus type I and psoriasis [34]. One of the common polymorphisms identified in DNASE1 (p.R244Q, present in homozygous state in the proband and heterozygous in other members of family A) has also been described as a risk factor for SLE and associated with autoantibody production in SLE patients [35]. Similarly, three of the ITGAM variants found in the proband have been associated with risk of SLE in Caucasian populations. A study showed that these variants (p.R77H/p.A858V/p.P1146S) can independently affect neutrophil adhesion and phagocytosis, but also tend to occur in strong linkage disequilibrium, suggesting that they co-segregate as a haplotype, acting concertedly in increasing the risk for SLE [36]. Furthermore, these three variants were shown to be associated with increased levels of type-I interferon in lupus patients, with TLR-stimulated macrophages carrying these variants showing increased basal levels of IRF7 and IFNB [37], the exact same mechanism proposed in this novel interferonopathy. These variants segregate with disease penetration in Family A.

We also highlight the TMEM173/STING1 HAQ haplotype (p.R71H/p.G230A/p.R293Q) in heterozygous state in the proband and in homozygous state in the mother (II-4). HAQ has been suggested to be a LOF allele, with only around 10% of the European population being HAQ/HAQ. Furthermore, HAQ/HAQ has been associated with decreased IFN-α production following vaccinia virus inoculation in Europeans [38]. Although there is conflicting data on this subject [39], as some studies describe HAQ/HAQ as being a TMEM173/STING1 null allele, it’s presence could potentially contribute to the lower (although still raised) IFN-α levels in the mother than in the proband, as well as absence of increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, since the high levels of the latter in the proband could result at least partially from STING-mediated NF-κB activation. A recent study showed that the presence of the HAQ allele in heterozygous form co-segregated with non-penetrance of COPA syndrome in a cohort of 35 individuals with COPA mutations, thus showing that the HAQ allele could dominantly dampen COPA-dependent STING signalling [40]. The proposed functional mechanism affecting disease penetrance were differences in STING trafficking [40] with conformational changes induced by the HAQ allele causing structural rearrangements in binding orientation between COPA and STING [41].

Thus, variants in other genes occurring frequently in the general population may contribute to modify phenotypes and even confer complete clinical protection from a severe monogenic disorder [40]. Common variants or alleles that are usually filtered out during bioinformatic analysis may impact penetrance in other monogenic disorders [33, 40, 42]. We speculate that the ITGAM variants present in the proband could contribute to the severity of the phenotype.

Other factors may also contribute to intrafamilial variation in penetrance and expressivity. The asymptomatic carriers could have other protective variants that have not been identified as these individuals were not sequenced by WES. Other possibilities include epigenetic modifications and environmental exposures, the so-called “exposome”, which includes exposure to infections, possibly immunizations, microbiome variations, diet, metabolism and radiation, with the age when the exposure occurred also being important [33]. Gene methylation studies to assess eventual epigenetic changes would also be of obvious interest in this context. Autosomal random monoallelic expression could also have a role in phenotypic variability, with a recent study showing that asymptomatic carriers’ T-cells express predominantly the wild-type allele unlike symptomatic patients with the same genotype [43].

A further possibility to explain the apparent incomplete/non-penetrance would be if this was in fact an AR disease and a second, perhaps non-coding variant in TRIM28 had been missed by WES. Moreover, TRIM28 knock-out mouse models are embryonically lethal [44], which seems to suggest that severe defects could be incompatible with life.

Interestingly, anti-TIF1β/(TRIM28) autoantibodies have been identified as specific antibodies in dermatomyositis, a disease that also features calcinosis and high IS, and showed clinical correlation with amyopathic dermatomyositis or mild myopathy [45–47]. JDM quite often overlaps clinically with monogenic interferonopathies. High IS have also been demonstrated in blood, muscle and skin of these patients in levels that can overlap some interferonopathies, particularly SAVI [48]. However, the physiopathology of the disease itself or of the increased IS is not yet properly understood. There is mounting evidence of the role of JAK inhibitors in the treatment of JDM even in refractory cases [49–51].

Myositis specific antibodies (MSA) were shown to correlate with certain disease presentations and complications. TIF1(λ)/TRIM33 is another member of the TRIM family and is also a frequent MSA substrate in JDM. Anti TIF1(λ)/TRIM33 antibodies are present in 15–35% of patients with JDM and have been linked to a prolonged disease course, amyopathic or milder muscle disease but more severe skin involvement, including increased risk for ulceration and lipodystrophy [52]. Anti TIF1-β/TRIM28 antibodies more specifically are not routinely tested and supposedly less frequent. The role of MSA in JDM disease pathogenesis is still speculative despite providing association with clinical phenotypes and useful prognostic information [53, 54]. It is interesting to note that in our proposed model, a genetic loss-of-function in a MSA target could be causing an interferonopathy phenotype. This may raise additional research questions regarding possible etiopathogenic links.

With this work, we demonstrated a pattern of increased IFN-I dysregulation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production, but also a clear link to the proposed mechanism of action that a LOF mutation in TRIM28 could cause a loss of a specific inhibitor of IRF7 and therefore decreased inhibition of a master regulator of interferon production [10]. An upregulated expression of IRF7 has been documented in the proband at gene and protein level and to a lesser extent also in other family members carrying the p.Y208C variant. We hypothesize that this variant, located in the RBCC domain of the TRIM28 protein, could compromise TRIM28 E3ligase sumoylation activity and/or its interaction with IRF7. Additional studies should be performed to explore these still unanswered questions such as assessment of interaction of mutated TRIM28 with IRF7 by immunoprecipitation, assessment of the effect of mutated TRIM28 on inhibition of IRF7’s transactivation activity compared to wild-type [8] and test its effect on the negative regulation of aggresome formation and subsequent suppression of viral proliferation [7].

The knock-down experiments in control HDFCs recreated similar changes to those observed in patient cells, suggesting that TRIM28 insufficiency/LOF might be associated to the patient’s phenotype. The attempt to rescue the cellular phenotype by transfection of wild-type TRIM28 yielded only partial rescue, which could be suggestive of a dominant negative effect of this variant [55]. However, we cannot exclude haploinsufficiency as the mechanism driving LOF of the mutated TRIM28 with other causes such as need for stoichiometric balance or experimental or technical limitations for example accounting for the partial rather than total rescue [56]. Although all the functional work thus far supports a potentially pathogenic role of this variant, additional transfection studies focusing on inserting p.Y208C in healthy donor cells would still be necessary to firmly confirm this hypothesis and the variant’s pathogenic role.

Conclusion

Herein, we described a novel, severe IFN-I phenotype which we suggest could be associated with a rare variant in TRIM28, compatible with a dominant (negative or haploinsufficiency) model with incomplete/non-penetrance. Functional studies conducted thus far are limited but support the hypothesis of a loss of inhibition of IRF7 causing increased IFN-I and pro-inflammatory cytokine production. The proposed pathway summary is exemplified in Fig. 5. Whilst further functional work would be valuable in confirming this hypothesis, we suggest that clinicians should explore TRIM28 variants in undiagnosed IFN-I phenotypes presenting with early-onset chilblain lesions, skin ulceration and calcinosis, accompanied by high IS and IRF7 upregulation.
