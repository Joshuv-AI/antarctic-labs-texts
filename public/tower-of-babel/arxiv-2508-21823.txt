A new characterization of the holographic entropy cone Preprint: BRX-TH-6726
Guglielmo Grimaldi, Matthew Headrick, and Veronika E. Hubeny

Abstract
Entanglement entropies computed using the holographic Ryu-Takayanagi formula are known to obey an infinite set of linear inequalities, which define the so-called RT entropy cone. The general structure of this cone, or equivalently the set of all valid inequalities, is unknown. It is also unknown whether those same inequalities are also obeyed by entropies computed using the covariant Hubeny-Rangamani-Takayanagi formula, although significant evidence has accumulated that they are. Using Markov states, we develop a test of this conjecture in a heretofore unexplored regime. The test reduces to checking that a given inequality obeys a certain majorization property, which is easy to evaluate. We find that the RT inequalities pass this test and, surprisingly, only RT inequalities do so. Our results not only provide strong new evidence that the HRT and RT cones coincide, but also offer a completely new characterization of that cone. A video abstract is available at https://youtu.be/stYAA9BwJj8 .

3
Saturation of inequalities and null reductions

We seek to test whether the HRT formula obeys the same set of inequalities as the RT formula. Our strategy is to construct non-static configurations that saturate a given sHEI, and then perturb the bulk geometry slightly to try to violate the inequality. (In entropy space, saturating an inequality means the entropy vector is sitting on a facet of the holographic entropy cone. A violation would correspond to the vector being pushed out of the cone.) Writing the sHEI in the form
\text{LHS}\geq\text{RHS}
, there are two natural ways a configuration of HRT surfaces can saturate the inequality:

1.

The HRT surfaces that calculate the entropies on the LHS are the same as the surfaces that calculate those on the RHS. This kind of saturation is robust under perturbations of the bulk. Under a small perturbation of the metric, the same surfaces will continue to calculate the two sides, so the inequality will continue to be saturated.
7
7

7

An exception may occur if the configuration happens to be sitting on a phase transition for one of the sides. It would be interesting to explore whether this can be a source of tests for the covariant validity of RT inequalities.
Such a configuration therefore does not pose a threat to the validity of the inequality.

2.

Different HRT surfaces appear in LHS and RHS, but they happen to have the same total area. Then, we can try to change the metric in order to increase the area of the surfaces in RHS without changing those in LHS, leading to a violation of the inequality. Naively it seems we will always be able to make a counterexample in this way. However, the perturbation may violate the null energy condition (NEC) and therefore be illegal. Indeed, the examples we will study have the feature that they saturate NEC, so some small perturbations of the metric do violate it. The question then is whether there exist NEC-preserving perturbations that lead to a counterexample to the given inequality. This seems to be an excellent place to test for violations, and we will focus on this second class of configurations.

When evaluating
\mathbf{Q}
on a light-cone configuration, there is a fundamental difference between the terms that include the central region (say
A
) and those that don’t. Namely, for a composite region containing
A
, and therefore containing
p
, the state has the form of a Markov state, which means that the modular Hamiltonian takes a particularly simple form

; on the other hand, for a region not containing
A
, the state is degenerate. We will be more specific about both cases below. Because of this distinction, we partition
\mathbf{Q}
accordingly,

\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}+\overline{{\mathbf{Q}}_{\downarrow A}}\,,

(18)

where
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
includes all the terms that contain
A
and
\overline{{\mathbf{Q}}_{\downarrow A}}
includes the rest. The fact that
\mathbf{Q}
is balanced means that the sum of the coefficients in
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
vanishes. We also have:

Lemma 1
.

Both
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
and
\overline{{\mathbf{Q}}_{\downarrow A}}
are balanced.

Proof.

Consider a region other than
A
, say
B
. Since
\mathbf{Q}
is superbalanced, the sum of the coefficients of the terms in
\mathbf{Q}
containing both
A
and
B
vanishes. These are the same as the terms in
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
containing
B
. So
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
is balanced. Since the sum of the coefficients of all the terms in
\mathbf{Q}
containing
B
vanishes, so does the sum of the coefficients of the terms containing
B
but not
A
. These are the terms in
\overline{{\mathbf{Q}}_{\downarrow A}}
containing
B
. So
\overline{{\mathbf{Q}}_{\downarrow A}}
is also balanced.
∎

We will now argue that
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
and
\overline{{\mathbf{Q}}_{\downarrow A}}
separately vanish on
\vec{\mathsf{S}}_{\rm vac}
. Actually, for
\overline{{\mathbf{Q}}_{\downarrow A}}
, we will show something stronger: it vanishes on
\vec{\mathsf{S}}_{\psi}
for any finite-energy state
\psi
. Thus, its vanishing is simply a consequence of the light-cone configuration, while that of
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
depends on the state being the vacuum. We begin with
\overline{{\mathbf{Q}}_{\downarrow A}}
.

For each term in
\overline{{\mathbf{Q}}_{\downarrow A}}
, the corresponding region is null and therefore does not have a well-defined entropy, as its proper size in the null direction is less than the UV cutoff; in holography, this is reflected in the fact that the HRT surface is degenerate and hugs the boundary.
8
8

8

One might be tempted to assign such null regions an entropy of 0. However, this is not correct, as can be seen by considering a null triangle with sides
A,B
.
AB
is a spacelike region and therefore has a positive entropy. If we assigned entropy 0 to
A
and
B
, we would get a contradiction with subadditivity. Instead, we should simply say that it has an undefined entropy. We thank T. Takayanagi for pointing this out.
We can regulate their entropies by making
L
slightly spacelike. The key fact we will use is that, for a composite region, the entropies add up: the MIs all vanish, as the size of each region is very small compared to their separation; in holography, the (degenerate) HRT surface is the union of those of the elementary regions. Since
\overline{{\mathbf{Q}}_{\downarrow A}}
is balanced, it vanishes on
\vec{\mathsf{S}}_{\psi}
. Note that this statement does not depend on the state
\psi
(for finite-energy states); indeed, in holography the degenerate HRT surfaces hugging the boundary are insensitive to the metric in the interior of the bulk.

Having shown that

\overline{{\mathbf{Q}}_{\downarrow A}}(\vec{\mathsf{S}}_{\psi})=0

(19)

for any state, we will henceforth focus on the more interesting quantity
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
, which we call the
null reduction
of
\mathbf{Q}
on
A
. The null reduction on any given region is a linear projection operator on the dual entropy space.

We now argue that

\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}(\vec{\mathsf{S}}_{\rm vac})=0\,.

(20)

For each term in
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
, we can appeal to the Markov property, which says that a region
X
on a null cone containing the vertex has the following modular Hamiltonian
Casini:2017roe
:

H_{\rm vac}(X)=2\pi\int_{S^{d-2}}d\Omega\int_{0}^{\lambda_{X}(\Omega)}d\lambda\,\lambda^{d-1}\,\frac{\lambda_{X}(\Omega)-\lambda}{\lambda_{X}(\Omega)}\,T_{\lambda\lambda}\,,

(21)

where
\Omega
is the angle on
L
,
\lambda
the affine parameter along the null ray with
p
at
\lambda=0
,
\lambda_{X}(\Omega)
the value of
\lambda
at the boundary of
X
, and
T_{\lambda\lambda}
the stress tensor component in the
\lambda
direction. This implies that the entropy can be written in the following form:

\mathsf{S}(X)=\int_{S^{d-2}}d\Omega\,s(\lambda_{X}(\Omega))\,,

(22)

where
s
a function that can be read off from (
21
) but whose form is not important for us. Denoting the non-central regions by
B_{1},\ldots,B_{{\sf N}-1}
,
we can write (
22
) as

\mathsf{S}(X)=\int_{R_{0}}d\Omega\,s(\lambda_{A}(\Omega))+\sum_{B_{i}\not\in X}\int_{R_{i}}d\Omega\,s(\lambda_{A}(\Omega))+\sum_{B_{i}\in X}\int_{R_{i}}d\Omega\,s(\lambda_{i}(\Omega))\,,

(23)

where
R_{i}\subset S^{d-2}
is the set of angles subtended by
B_{i}
(
i=1,\ldots,n
),
R_{0}:=S^{d-2}\setminus(\cup_{i=1}^{{\sf N}-1}R_{i})
is the set of angles not subtended by any of the
B_{i}
, and
\lambda_{i}(\Omega)
is the outer boundary of
B_{i}
. For the null-reduced information quantity
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
, we then have

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}(\mathsf{S}_{\rm vac})

\displaystyle=\sum_{X}q_{X}\mathsf{S}(X)

\displaystyle=\int_{R_{0}}d\Omega\left(\sum_{X}q_{X}\right)s(\lambda_{A}(\Omega))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{i}\int_{R_{i}}d\Omega\left(\sum_{X\not\ni B_{i}}q_{X}\right)s(\lambda_{A}(\Omega))+\sum_{i}\int_{R_{i}}d\Omega\left(\sum_{X\ni B_{i}}q_{X}\right)s(\lambda_{i}(\Omega))

\displaystyle=0\,,

(24)

where
q_{X}
is the coefficient of
X
in
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
, and the last equality follows from the fact that
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
is balanced, so each sum in large parentheses vanishes.

The above argument did not refer to holography and holds in any CFT. However, it is instructive to see why
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}(\vec{\mathsf{S}}_{\rm vac})=0
in holography. Since we are in the vacuum, the bulk is AdS, and all the HRT surfaces lie on
\mathscr{L}=\partial I_{\rm bulk}^{+}(p)
, the future light cone in the bulk of
p
. In fact, each HRT surface intersects every null generator of
\mathscr{L}
exactly once. Furthermore,
\mathscr{L}
has vanishing expansion, so the area element is constant along any geodesic. So any geodesic makes a contribution to
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
proportional to the sum of all the coefficients in
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
, which (again by balance) vanishes. See figure
2
for a visualization of the HRT surfaces that saturate MMI as an example.
9
9

9

To be slightly more careful, we should take care of the UV regulator. We are assuming a regulator that, for every null ray of
\mathcal{L}
, either includes or excludes the entire ray. For example, we could use a bulk cutoff surface that is a timelike hyperplane in Poincaré coordinates that intersects the boundary on a spacelike boundary hyperplane containing
p
. The important point is that, while the entropy vector
\vec{\mathsf{S}}_{\rm vac}
depends on the regulator, since
\mathbf{Q}
is superbalanced,
\mathbf{Q}(\vec{\mathsf{S}}_{\rm vac})
does not.

In the next section, we will perturb the bulk metric to try to violate the inequality. Before doing so, in the rest of this section, we will present some examples and general properties of null reductions of superbalanced information quantities. We will see that they are quite interesting objects in their own right.

3.3
Null reduction: Examples

To gain some intuition about null reductions, in this subsection we will give a few examples of null reductions of sHIQs.

Example 1
.

Consider MMI:

\mathbf{Q}_{\text{MMI}}=\mathsf{S}(AB)+\mathsf{S}(BC)+\mathsf{S}(AC)-\mathsf{S}(A)-\mathsf{S}(B)-\mathsf{S}(C)-\mathsf{S}(ABC)\,

(25)

Its three null reductions are

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{{}^{\text{MMI}}}_{\downarrow A}=\mathsf{S}(AB)+\mathsf{S}(AC)-\mathsf{S}(A)-\mathsf{S}(ABC)

\displaystyle={\sf I}(B:C|A)

(26)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{{}^{\text{MMI}}}_{\downarrow B}=\mathsf{S}(AB)+\mathsf{S}(BC)-\mathsf{S}(B)-\mathsf{S}(ABC)

\displaystyle={\sf I}(A:C|B)

(27)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{{}^{\text{MMI}}}_{\downarrow C}=\mathsf{S}(BC)+\mathsf{S}(AC)-\mathsf{S}(C)-\mathsf{S}(ABC)

\displaystyle={\sf I}(A:B|C)\,.

(28)

Notice that each null reduction is a non-negative information quantity, by SSA.

Example 2
.

Consider the following primitive sHIQ on five regions

\mathbf{Q}^{[5]}=\mathsf{S}(ABC)+\mathsf{S}(ABD)+\mathsf{S}(ACD)+\mathsf{S}(ADE)+\mathsf{S}(CDE)\\
-\mathsf{S}(C)-\mathsf{S}(AB)-\mathsf{S}(AD)-\mathsf{S}(DE)-\mathsf{S}(ACDE)-\mathsf{S}(ABCD)\geq 0\,.

(29)

Its null reductions are:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{[5]}_{\downarrow A}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{S}(ABC)+\mathsf{S}(ABD)+\mathsf{S}(ACD)+\mathsf{S}(ADE)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\mathsf{S}(AB)-\mathsf{S}(AD)-\mathsf{S}(ACDE)-\mathsf{S}(ABCD)

\displaystyle={\sf I}(C:D|AB)+{\sf I}(C:E|AD)

(30)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{[5]}_{\downarrow B}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{S}(ABC)+\mathsf{S}(ABD)-\mathsf{S}(AB)-\mathsf{S}(ABCD)

\displaystyle={\sf I}(C:D|AB)

(31)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{[5]}_{\downarrow C}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{S}(ABC)+\mathsf{S}(ACD)+\mathsf{S}(CDE)-\mathsf{S}(C)-\mathsf{S}(ACDE)-\mathsf{S}(ABCD)

\displaystyle={\sf I}(B:D|AC)+{\sf I}(A:DE|C)

(32)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{[5]}_{\downarrow D}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{S}(ABD)+\mathsf{S}(ACD)+\mathsf{S}(ADE)+\mathsf{S}(CDE)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\mathsf{S}(AD)-\mathsf{S}(DE)-\mathsf{S}(ACDE)-\mathsf{S}(ABCD)

\displaystyle={\sf I}(B:C|AD)+{\sf I}(A:C|DE)

(33)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{[5]}_{\downarrow E}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{S}(ADE)+\mathsf{S}(CDE)-\mathsf{S}(DE)-\mathsf{S}(ACDE)

\displaystyle={\sf I}(A:C|DE)

(34)

Again, each expression is non-negative by SSA.

Based on these two examples, the reader may be tempted to guess that null reductions of a sHIQ is always a positive sum of CMIs. This is not true, as it can be seen in the following example:

Example 3
.

Here is another 5-party primitive sHIQ:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{Q}^{[8]}=&\,\mathsf{S}(AD)+\mathsf{S}(BC)+\mathsf{S}(ABE)+\mathsf{S}(ACE)+\mathsf{S}(ADE)+\mathsf{S}(BDE)+\mathsf{S}(CDE)\\
&-\mathsf{S}(ABDE)-\mathsf{S}(ACDE)-\mathsf{S}(BCE)-\mathsf{S}(AE)-\mathsf{S}(DE)-\mathsf{S}(A)-\mathsf{S}(B)-\mathsf{S}(C)-\mathsf{S}(D)\,.\end{split}

(35)

Null reducing on
E
, we find

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{[8]}_{\downarrow E}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{S}(ABE)+\mathsf{S}(ACE)+\mathsf{S}(ADE)+\mathsf{S}(BDE)+\mathsf{S}(CDE)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\mathsf{S}(ABDE)-\mathsf{S}(ACDE)-\mathsf{S}(BCE)-\mathsf{S}(AE)-\mathsf{S}(DE)

(36)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Q}^{[8]}+{\sf I}(B:C)+{\sf I}(A:D)\,.

(37)

While the null reduction in this case is not a sum of CMIs, it is still an sHIQ. Indeed, we find this to be a general feature. Based on extensive tests (see sec.
5
), we conjecture that the null reductions of any superbalanced sHIQ are sHIQs. We also find that the converse statement holds: a superbalanced information quantity is an sHIQ
only if
all of its null reductions are.

3.4
Null reduction & tripartite form

We can make more precise claims about the structure of a null reduced information quantity by utilizing the so-called “tripartite form” of the sHIQ from (
16
). As we shall see momentarily, this form is particularly well-suited to the present context, since we can analyze what happens to
\mathbf{Q}
under null reductions by considering what happens to each term individually.
Suppose for concreteness that we are null-deducing on the party
A
, and let us consider a single term in the tripartite form,
{\sf I}_{3}(X:Y:Z|W)
, where the
X
,
Y
,
Z
, and
W
are arbitrary (possibly composite) disjoint subsystems, at most one of which contains
A
. Since
{\sf I}_{3}(X:Y:Z)
is symmetric under permuting its arguments, there are three distinct possibilites for which subsystems can contain the central region
A
:

1.

A
is contained in one of
X
,
Y
or
Z
,

2.

A
is contained in
W
.

3.

A
is not contained in any of
X
,
Y
,
Z
,
W
.

In the first case, suppose that
A\subseteq X
. Then

\displaystyle\begin{split}-{\sf I}_{3}(X:Y:Z|W)_{\downarrow A}&=-{\sf I}_{3}(WX:Y:Z)_{\downarrow A}+{\sf I}_{3}(W:Y:Z)_{\downarrow A}\\
&=-{\sf I}_{3}(WX:Y:Z)_{\downarrow A}\\
&={\sf I}(Y:Z|WX)\end{split}

(38)

where in the first line we rewrote the conditional tripartite information as a difference of two tripartite information quantities as in (
17
), in the second line we dropped the second term in which
X
, and therefore
A
, is absent, and in the third line we used (
26
) to actually perform the null reduction. Similarly if
A\subseteq Y
, then
-{\sf I}_{3}(X:Y:Z|W)_{\downarrow A}={\sf I}(X:Z|WY)
, and if
A\subseteq Z
, then

-{\sf I}_{3}(X:Y:Z|W)_{\downarrow A}={\sf I}(X:Y|WZ)
. In all cases the party containing
A
joins the conditioned-on argument in the conditional mutual information.

We can re-express this result more conveniently, by recognizing that the CMI generated by null reduction (
38
) is in fact the original conditional tripartite information term plus another CMI,

{\sf I}(Y:Z|XW)=-{\sf I}_{3}(X:Y:Z|W)+{\sf I}(Y:Z|W),

(39)

so by SSA,
the effect of this null reduction can only increase the value of the term.

In the second case, when
A\subseteq W
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}-{\sf I}_{3}(X:Y:Z|W)_{\downarrow A}&=-({\sf I}(Y:Z|WX)-{\sf I}(Y:Z|W))_{\downarrow A}\\
&=-{\sf I}_{3}(X:Y:Z|W),\end{split}

(40)

we simply get back the original conditional tripartite information. Here the first line comes from conditioning the following identity on
W
,

-{\sf I}_{3}(X:Y:Z)={\sf I}(X:Z|Y)-{\sf I}(X:Z),

(41)

and the second line follows from noticing that every terms will contain
W
so the null reduction operator acts as the identity.

In the third case, when
A\nsubseteq XYZW
, the null reduction kills the entire term:

-{\sf I}_{3}(X:Y:Z|W)_{\downarrow A}=0
. This renders the result simple, but since
{\sf I}_{3}(X:Y:Z|W)
is not sign-definite, it is no longer clear whether this operation increases or decreases the value of the original term. It would be interesting to examine more carefully how the tripartite form terms in the sHEI interface with each other, so as to be able to prove that the combined effect of any null reduction necessarily increases the value of sHEI in full generality. We leave this for future explolations.

4
The majorization test

Let us recap what we have done so far. We have considered configurations that saturate holographic entropy inequalities. We have learnt that configurations that are most susceptible to potentially being violated under a bulk perturbation are the light-cone configurations from subsection
3.1
. We have seen that when evaluated on such configurations, the sHEIs reduce to their null reduction on some given party. The question that remains to be answered is: can we violate such an inequality by perturbing the bulk slightly? To answer the question we first need to understand how these surfaces look on the bulk light-cone
\mathscr{L}
, and then how their areas change under bulk metric perturbations. Restricting bulk perturbations to those that respect the null energy condition will lead us to the majorization test.

4.1
HRT surfaces

The metric for the Poincaré patch of AdS
d+1
(
d\geq 2
) can be written

ds^{2}=\frac{1}{\cos^{2}\theta}\left(-2du\,dv-u^{2}\,dv^{2}+d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\Omega_{d-2}^{2}\right),

(42)

where
u\in(-\infty,0)
,
v\in\mathbb{R}
,
\theta\in[0,\pi/2)
,
\Omega\in S^{d-2}
. These are related to the usual Poincaré coordinates by

z=-\frac{\cos\theta}{u}\,,\qquad x^{0}=-\frac{1}{u}+v\,,\qquad\vec{x}=-\frac{\sin\theta}{u}\hat{x}(\Omega)\,,

(43)

where
\hat{x}(\Omega)
is the unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
at the angle
\Omega
. With
p
at
z=x^{0}=x^{i}=0
, its future light cone
\mathscr{L}
is at
v=0
, with
\theta,\Omega
specifying the null ray and
u
an affine parameter along the ray. The boundary is at
\theta=\pi/2
, and the boundary light cone
L
with the same vertex
p
is at
v=0
, with
\Omega
specifying the null ray and
u
a (non-affine) parameter along the ray. (
\lambda=-1/u
is an affine parameter for the boundary ray.) Extending both the bulk and boundary light cones until they reconverge at the point
q
on the boundary, as shown in figure
2
, extends
u
to cover the whole real line.

Given a boundary region
X\subset L
containing
p
, with boundary at
u=u^{*}(\Omega)
, the HRT surface
\gamma_{X}
lies on
\mathscr{L}
, intersecting each ray exactly once, and can therefore be expressed as a function
u(\theta,\Omega)
obeying the boundary condition
u(\pi/2,\Omega)=u^{*}(\Omega)
. This function is determined by the following elliptic PDE on the hemisphere parametrized by
\theta,\Omega
:
10
10

10

Note that the PDE (
45
) is regular at
\theta=0
, where it locally reduces to the flat Laplacian in spherical coordinates in
d-1
dimensions. Also, for
d\neq 3
, (
45
) can be written
\tilde{\nabla}^{2}u=0
, where
\tilde{\nabla}
is the scalar Laplacian with respect to the following metric on the hemisphere:

d\tilde{s}^{2}=(\cos\theta)^{-(d-1)/(d-3)}(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta d\Omega_{d-2}^{2})\,.

(44)

\partial_{\theta}\left(\frac{\sin^{d-2}\theta}{\cos^{d-1}\theta}\partial_{\theta}u\right)+\frac{\sin^{d-4}\theta}{\cos^{d-1}\theta}\nabla^{2}_{\Omega}u=0\,,

(45)

where

is the Laplacian on

. The important feature of the PDE (

u
.
Because of this linearity, the solution

is a linear functional of the boundary function

. Furthermore, this functional is strictly monotone: If
u^{*}_{1}\geq u^{*}_{2}
for all

Given a light-cone configuration of boundary regions with central region

(

(46)

where

We now define the functions

(48)

Note that, since

4.2
Bulk perturbations

Let’s now perturb the bulk and ask how the entropy vector for our light-cone configuration changes. Specifically, since the HRT surface is extended in the
\theta
and
\Omega

\delta g_{\mu\nu}=\epsilon hg_{\mu\nu}\quad(\mu,\nu=\theta,\Omega)\,,

(49)

where
h
is a function of
u,\theta,\Omega
. Since
\gamma_{X}
is extremal, the change in its area
|\gamma_{X}|
at first order in
\epsilon
is simply due to the change in the metric at its coordinate position, which is:

\delta|\gamma_{X}|=\epsilon\int d\theta\,d\Omega\,\frac{\sin^{d-2}\theta}{\cos^{d-1}\theta}\,h\,,

(50)

Henceforth, we abbreviate
(\theta,\Omega)
as
\tilde{\Omega}
and
d\theta\,d\Omega\,\sin^{d-2}\theta/\cos^{d-1}\theta

d\tilde{\Omega}
, so (

\delta|\gamma_{X}|=\epsilon\int d\tilde{\Omega}\,h\,.

(51)

Define the function
\vec{s}(\tilde{\Omega})

s(\tilde{\Omega})_{X}:=\begin{cases}h(u_{X}(\tilde{\Omega}),\tilde{\Omega})/4G_{\rm N}\,,\quad&A\in X\\
0\,,\quad&A\not\in X\end{cases}\,.

(52)

We then have, from (

\delta\vec{\mathsf{S}}=\epsilon\int d\tilde{\Omega}\,\vec{s}(\Omega)\,.

(53)

Now let
\mathbf{Q}

11
11

11

There is no loss of generality in assuming integrality of the coefficients. We then have:

\mathbf{Q}(\vec{\mathsf{S}}_{\psi})=\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}(\vec{\mathsf{S}}_{\psi})=\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}(\delta\vec{\mathsf{S}})+O(\epsilon^{2})=\epsilon\int d\tilde{\Omega}\,\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}(\vec{s}(\tilde{\Omega}))+O(\epsilon^{2})\,,

where in the first equality we used (

\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}

A couple of notational housekeeping items will make our task less laborious going forward. First, from (

\tilde{\Omega}

\mathscr{L}
, make independent contributions. Therefore, we henceforth fix a value of
\tilde{\Omega}
and drop the explicit dependence on it. We should therefore think of

\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathsf{S}(X_{n})-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathsf{S}(Y_{n})\,,

are those appearing with negative coefficients. The same region

\mathsf{S}(X)
has coefficient greater than 1 in
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
, and similarly for the negative coefficients. From the fact that the total of the coefficients of
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
vanishes, we know that the number

\sum_{n=1}^{N}h(u_{X_{n}})\geq\sum_{n=1}^{N}h(u_{Y_{n}})\,.

(56)

Recall that, for any region

,

is given by (

Let us see how (

Example 4
.

The null reduction on

Example 5

30
)):

\mathbf{Q}^{[5]}_{\downarrow A}=\mathsf{S}(ABC)+\mathsf{S}(ABD)+\mathsf{S}(ACD)+\mathsf{S}(ADE)\\
-\mathsf{S}(AB)-\mathsf{S}(AD)-\mathsf{S}(ACDE)-\mathsf{S}(ABCD)\,.

(59)

The inequality (
56
) then takes the following form:

h(a+b+c)+h(a+b+d)+h(a+c+d)+h(a+d+e)\\
\geq h(a+b)+h(a+d)+h(a+c+d+e)+h(a+b+c+d)\,.

(60)

4.3
Null energy condition & majorization

Let us now take a closer look at (
56
).
The arguments
u_{X_{n}}
appearing on the left-hand side are all distinct from the ones
u_{Y_{n}}
appearing on the right-hand side, else they would have cancelled in
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
(more precisely, they are distinct as functions of the
b_{i}
, which means they have distinct values for generic values of the
b_{i}
). Therefore we can easily choose a function
h
that violates (
56
); for example, we can choose
h
to be negative at some
u_{X_{n}}
and zero at all other
u_{X_{n}}
s and at all
u_{Y_{n}}
. It would therefore appear to be very easy to construct a non-static counterexample to any sHEI.

We know the argument in the previous paragraph must be incorrect, because it is known that HRT entropies obey MMI
Wall_2014
;
Bousso:2024ysg
. The resolution to this puzzle is that the bulk metric is not arbitrary: since we are working in classical Einstein gravity, it must obey the Einstein equation with matter obeying the null energy condition. Without these conditions, it is indeed easy for the HRT formula to violate MMI (and indeed subadditivity, SSA, and many other consistency conditions
Callan:2012ip
).

Specifically, the Einstein equation and NEC constrain the function
h
defining our perturbation (
49
) via the focusing lemma, which says that the expasion
\theta
of a null geodesic congruence obeys

\theta^{\prime}\leq-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}\,,

(61)

where the derivative is with respect to the affine parameter. The expansion is the logarithmic derivative of the area element; in our case, from (
51
), the area element is
1+\epsilon h
, so

\theta=\epsilon h^{\prime}+O(\epsilon^{2})\,,

(62)

so focusing implies that
h
is concave.

Now return to the MMI example (
58
). Recalling that
b
and
c
must be positive, the two arguments of
h
appearing on the left-hand side,
a+b
,
a+c
, lie in between those appearing on the right-hand side
a
,
a+b+c
, with the same mean value
a+(b+c)/2
. This implies that the right-hand side values
majorize
the left-hand side ones,

(a,a+b+c)\succ(a+b,a+c)

(63)

(See appendix
A
for the definition of majorization and its key properties.) Together with the concavity of
h
, (
63
) implies that (
58
) is true: the MMI inequality is safe (as we already knew).

For the
\mathbf{Q}^{[5]}
example (
60
), again we see that the arguments on the left- and right-hand sides have the same mean value, namely
a+(2b+2c+3d+e)/4
. The equality of the mean values is a consequence of balance of the null-reduced information quantity, so it will always hold. Again, for any positive
b,c,d,e
, the arguments on the right-hand side majorize those on the left-hand side:

(a+b,a+e,a+b+c+d+e)\succ(a+b,a+d+d,a+b+e)\,.

(64)

And, again, together with the concavity of
h
, this implies that (
60
) is true. So this inequality, for this kind of light-cone configuration, is safe.

The general theorem relating majorization to inequalities obeyed by concave functions is the following:

Theorem 1

(Karamata)
.

Given numbers
l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}
and
r_{1},\ldots,r_{N}
such that
\sum l_{n}=\sum r_{n}
,
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{N})\succ(l_{1},\ldots,l_{N})
if and only if, for any concave function
h
,

\sum_{n=1}^{N}h(l_{n})\geq\sum_{n=1}^{N}h(r_{n})\,.

(65)

For a given information quantity, we thus need to test whether the quantities
u_{Y_{n}}
from the RHS majorize the ones
u_{X_{n}}
from the LHS. Actually, since the term
u_{A}
appears in all of the
I_{n}
s and
J_{n}
s, it has no effect on the majorization condition.
12
12

12

In the version of the majorization test given in the introduction, for ease of presentation a positive quantity corresponding to the central region is included. Again, since by definition the central quantity appears in every term in the null-reduced inequality, whether such a quantity is included or not has no effect on the majorization property.
Thus, defining

x_{n}:=\sum_{B_{i}\in X_{n}}b_{i}\,,\qquad y_{n}:=\sum_{B_{i}\in Y_{n}}b_{i}\qquad(n=1,\ldots,N)\,,

(66)

is it sufficient to check whether

(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})\succ(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\,.

(67)

(Note that if the term
\mathsf{S}(A)
appears in
\mathbf{Q}
, and therefore in
\mathbf{Q}_{\downarrow A}
, then the corresponding
x_{n}
or
y_{n}
will be 0.) If (
67
) holds for all positive numbers
b_{i}
, then
\mathbf{Q}(\mathsf{S}_{\psi})\geq 0
, at first order in
\epsilon
, for any light-cone configuration with the given central region, and we say that
\mathbf{Q}

passes the majorization test for
A
.

In fact, we can reverse the logic to show that the converse holds: By the Karamata theorem, if there exists a set of positive numbers
b_{i}^{\prime}
such that (
67
) is false, then, for any number
u_{A}^{\prime}
, there exists a concave function
h
such that (
56
) is false. Furthermore, for any given value
\tilde{\Omega}^{\prime}
of
\tilde{\Omega}
, there exists a light-cone configuration with central region
A
such that
u_{A}(\tilde{\Omega}^{\prime})=u_{A}^{\prime}
and
b_{i}(\tilde{\Omega}^{\prime})=b_{i}^{\prime}
, which can be found by starting with any light-cone configuration and multiplying the functions
u^{*}(\Omega)
,
b_{i}^{*}(\Omega)
by appropriate numbers. For this configuration,
\mathbf{Q}(\mathsf{S}_{\psi})<0
. Therefore the inequality is safe against this type of counterexample if and only if it passes the majorization test for
A
.

We say that
\mathbf{Q}

passes the majorization test
if it does so for every elementary region. By definition, the majorization test is invariant under
S_{\sf N}
permutations of the parties, but not under
S_{{\sf N}+1}
permutations involving the purifer.

5
Conjectures and evidence

In this section we will present four conjectures concerning null reductions and the majorization test, and give strong empirical evidence in favor of each of them. For all four of them, we assume
\mathbf{Q}
is a superbalanced information quantity.

Conjecture 1
.

If
\mathbf{Q}
is an sHIQ, then it passes the majorization test.

Conjecture 2
.

If
\mathbf{Q}
passes the majorization test, then it is an sHIQ.

Conjectures
1
and
2
are the central claims of this paper. They tie majorization theory with objectives (1) and (2) listed in the introduction: conjecture
1
guarantees
robustness
of sHEIs in the HRT regime, while conjecture
2

characterizes
sHEIs through majorization.

Conjecture 3
.

If
\mathbf{Q}
is an sHIQ then all of its null reductions are sHIQ.

Conjecture 4
.

If all of the null reductions of
\mathbf{Q}
are sHIQs, then it is an sHIQ.

These two conjectures represent some kind of self-consistency condition on the dual entropy cone.

Conjecture
1
:

We tested a large number of known sHIQs, finding that they all passed the majorization test. Two strategies were employed: analytic and numerical. The analytic strategy consisted of showing that the inequality (
73
) holds for arbitrary
t
and arbitrary positive
b_{i}
. As an example, for MMI null-reduced on the region
A
, we wish to show that

(b+c,0)\succ(b,c)\,.

(68)

If
b\leq c
, we have three cases:
t\in(-\infty,b]
,
t\in[b,c]
,
t\in[c,\infty)
. In each case, (
73
) is easily confirmed.
13
13

13

Note that in this simple case, majorization is also immediately evident from the definition in (
70
).
This method quickly becomes tedious to do by hand, but can be implemented in
Mathematica
using
FullSimplify
; see appendix
B
for details. The analytic method was applied to the 1877 known
{\sf N}=6
primitive sHIQs (but not their images under permutations involving the purifier).

For large inequalities, this analytic method becomes too slow. We can instead carry out the majorization test numerically, by directly implementing the definition (
70
) for random values of the
b_{i}
; again, see appendix
B
for details. This method can find counterexamples but cannot prove that the test is passed. However, finding no counterexample after many random draws constitutes convincing evidence. Furthermore, the test is extremely fast. For example, testing all 1877 known
{\sf N}=6
primitive sHIQs
Hernandez-Cuenca:2023iqh
with 100 random draws for each null reduction took only about a minute on a standard laptop, versus several days for the analytic method. The numerical method was applied to the toric inequalities
Czech:2024rco
up to
{\sf N}=13
.

The analytic and numerical results taken together constitute overwhelming evidence in favor of conjecture
1
.

Conjecture
2
:

This conjecture is supported by testing its contrapositive: a superbalanced information quantity that is not an sHIQ fails the majorization test. To construct non-sHIQs, we subtracted superbalanced primitive sHIQs from each other. Specifically, we wrote code to do the following:

1.

Pick a random pair of primitive sHIQs
\mathbf{Q}_{i}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{j}
.

2.

Permute the
{\sf N}
parties plus the purifier in
\mathbf{Q}_{i}
,
\mathbf{Q}_{j}
independently with randomly drawn permutations
\pi,\sigma\in S_{{\sf N}+1}
to obtain new quantities
\pi(\mathbf{Q}_{i})
and
\sigma(\mathbf{Q}_{j})
. This is done to democratically test the conjecture on all corners of dual entropy space, avoiding potential selection biases.

3.

Pick a random integer
n\in[4,10]
and construct the quantity

\mathbf{Q}_{\text{false}}:=n\pi(\mathbf{Q}_{i})-\sigma(\mathbf{Q}_{j}).

(69)

Then, we perform the majorization test on
\mathbf{Q}_{\text{false}}
using the random numerical method described above. Using the know
{\sf N}=6
primitive sHIQs, we performed more than one million random draws, in every case seeing a failure of the majorization test.

Conjecture
3
:

This conjecture was tested by showing that all null reductions of all 372 superbalanced
{\sf N}=5
sHIQs are sHIQs by directly checking that each null reduction sits inside the dual cone. See appendix
B
for some details.

Conjecture
4
:

Similarly to conjecture
2
, this conjecture is supported by testing its contrapositive: constructing a false superbalanced information quantity
\mathbf{Q}_{\text{false}}
, checking that at least one of its null reductions is
not
an sHIQ. Since the HEC is known completely only up to
\mathsf{N}=5
, we tested the conjecture in this regime. First, we construct
\mathbf{Q}_{\text{false}}
in the same way as in conjecture 2. Then, we check whether all of its null reductions sit inside the
\mathsf{N}=5
dual cone, by checking whether they can be written as conical combinations of the 372 primitive sHIQs. We tested this for hundreds of randomly generated examples of
\mathbf{Q}_{\text{false}}

Appendix A
Review of majorization

Consider two
N
dimensional real vectors
\vec{x}
and
\vec{y}
ordered component wise, i.e. such that
x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\dots\geq x_{N}
and
y_{1}\geq y_{2}\geq\dots\geq y_{N}
. If the sum of the first
k
components of
\vec{y}
is greater than the sum of the first
k
components of
\vec{x}
for all
k=1,\dots,N-1
and if the sums are equal for
k=N
, then we say that the vector
\vec{y}
majorizes the vector
\vec{x}
(in notation
\vec{y}\succ\vec{x}
). More compactly,

\vec{y}\succ\vec{x}\quad\text{if}\quad\begin{cases}\sum_{n=1}^{k}y_{n}\geq\sum_{n=1}^{k}x_{n},&\text{if }k=1,2,\dots,N-1\\
\sum_{n=1}^{N}y_{n}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}.\end{cases}

(70)

Majorization comes with an ordering of the components, if the two vectors are initially not ordered then one vector majorizes the other if it majorizes it once the components are ordered. Intuitively, majorization is a statement about how much more “spread out” the components of a vector are with respect to another vector; the vector
\vec{x}=(1,5)
majorizes the vector
\vec{y}=(3,4)
, since once we order their components we have
5\geq 3
and
5+1=3+3=6
.

There are many equivalent definitions of majorization. One is through doubly stochastic matrices: the vector
\vec{y}
majorizes the vector
\vec{x}
if and only if
\vec{x}
is in the convex hull of all the vectors obtained from permuting the components of
\vec{y}
. This is expressed formally as the existence of a doubly stochastic matrix
\mathsf{D}
(i.e. a square matrix whose rows and columns sum to 1) such that

\vec{x}=\mathsf{D}\vec{y}.

(71)

Another equivalent definition is through inequalities of concave (or convex) functions. The vector
\vec{y}
majorizes
\vec{x}
if and only if, for all concave functions
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, one has

\sum_{n=1}^{N}h(x_{n})\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}h(y_{n})\,.

(72)

The inequality is reversed for convex functions, since if
h
is concave then
-h
is convex. This definition of majorization is extremely powerful, since it means that, to guarantee that
\vec{x}\succ\vec{y}
, it suffices to check that (i)
\sum_{n}x_{n}=\sum_{n}y_{n}
and that (ii) for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\max(0,x_{n}-t)\geq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\max(0,y_{n}-t)\,.

(73)

This is the definition we will use use to prove the majorization test for our holographic entropy inequalities.
