Empirical Bayes for Data integration
Paul Rognon-Vael, Pompeu Fabra University and David Rossell, Pompeu Fabra University

Abstract
. We discuss the use of empirical Bayes for data integration, in the sense of transfer learning. Our main interest is in settings where one wishes to learn structure (e.g. feature selection) and one only has access to incomplete data from previous studies, such as summaries, estimates or lists of relevant features. We discuss differences between full Bayes and empirical Bayes, and develop a computational framework for the latter. We discuss how empirical Bayes attains consistent variable selection under weaker conditions (sparsity and betamin assumptions) than full Bayes and other standard criteria do, and how it attains faster convergence rates. Our high-dimensional regression examples show that fully Bayesian inference enjoys excellent properties, and that data integration with empirical Bayes can offer moderate yet meaningful improvements in practice.



Keywords:
Data integration, Transfer learning, Empirical Bayes, Frequentist properties, Variable selection

1.
Introduction

Data integration is the task of combining multiple datasets or sources of information into a single analysis.
It has the potential to improve inference and/or predictions,
particularly in settings where data are scarce relative to the problem’s complexity (number of parameters, models or questions being considered).
This is a standard statistical problem that is receiving renewed attention within the context of transfer learning.
Therein, one wishes to transfer findings obtained from a setting where one has large or cheaply obtained data, to another setting where data are scarcer or costlier.
We discuss the role of empirical Bayes methodology for data integration.
In contrast to parameter estimation or prediction problems, which have been better studied, we focus on structural learning.
By structural learning we refer to model selection tasks where one seeks to learn about structural features of the data-generating truth, say whether a parameter is zero, and we use variable selection as a canonical example.

There are two main scenarios where one may seek to integrate data, illustrated in Figure
1
.
The first (left panel) is when one has full access to multiple datasets. For example, one wishes to study multiple cancer types, and one has a separate dataset for each cancer type.
Or, one has data from multiple schools and wants to learn common traits for low academic performance.
We refer to this scenario as
data integration with full data
.
There is a well-developed literature for this setting. A popular approach is to use hierarchical models that share information across datasets, see for instance
Gelman and
Hill 2007
for a monograph on mixed effects models.
For data integration in covariance models see
Avalos-Pacheco et al. 2022
;
Chandra
et al. 2024
, in clustering see
Lock and
Dunson 2013
and in functional data analysis see
Yang
et al. 2015
.
See also
Jacob
et al. 2017
;
Nott
et al. 2023
and references therein for data integration strategies when the assumed model is misspecified.

Our main focus is in the second scenario (Figure
1
, right), which occurs when one is interested in a single dataset
\bm{y}
, and one only has full access to that data. One also has other information that could be useful, for example summaries of previous datasets (denoted
\bm{Z}=(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{q})
in Figure
1
).
This second scenario is probably more common and certainly more challenging, because one does not have full access to the previous data and the provided summaries may be poor.
An application that we illustrate later is a gene expression study in human colon cancer, where one has a list of genes that were found to be important in mice. The data analyst has full access to the human data, but only the list of genes from the mice experiment.
We refer to this scenario as
data integration with meta-covariates
,
i.e. with summaries
\bm{Z}
that may be informative as to what parameters are more likely to play an important role in the main data of interest.
Although this scenario received less attention than data integration with full data, there is a literature on the topic.
To mention some examples,
Stingo
et al. 2011
;
Cassese
et al. 2014
proposed Bayesian variable selection for gene expression where prior inclusion probabilities depend on biological knowledge and meta-covariates.

Chen
et al. 2021
predicted disease outcomes by allowing LASSO penalties to depend on functional annotations.
In causal analysis,
Belloni et al. 2012
;
Antonelli and
Dominici 2021
;
Papaspiliopoulos et al. 2025

penalized the inclusion of control covariates using their degree of association with the treatment of interest.
See
van de
Wiel et al. 2019
for an overview on the use of meta-covariates in regression.
Beyond regression, in Gaussian graphical models
Peterson
et al. 2016
and
Jewson et al. 2023
used node and edge covariates to drive prior edge inclusion probabilities.
And in factor models,
Schiavon
et al. 2022
used meta-covariates to determine non-zero loadings.

Bayesian statistics provide a natural framework to integrate prior data such as meta-covariates into the analysis pipeline.
In principle it suffices to set a prior on the parameters of interest (
\bm{\theta}
in Figure
1
, right panel) that depends on the meta-covariates
\bm{Z}
and some hyper-parameters
\bm{\omega}
.
There may be situations where an expert is willing to set such a prior subjectively. For example a biologist believes that if a gene is known to be involved in gastrointestinal cancer, then it has 0.9 prior probability of also being involved in colorectal cancer.
Alas this situation is rather rare: experts do not have such a precise knowledge of
\bm{\omega}
, and it is cumbersome to specify such a prior when there are
q>1
meta-covariates.
This important because, if the prior grossly failed to reflect the true effect of
\bm{Z}
(i.e., the expert provided a poor prior for
\bm{\omega}
), then one may obtain worse inference than if
\bm{Z}
were not used at all.

Our goal is to explain how empirical Bayes can be a useful strategy for data integration.
By modeling the relation between the meta-covariates
\bm{Z}
and the parameters
\bm{\theta}
governing the observed data
\bm{y}
,
one may learn
\bm{\omega}
and hence integrate
\bm{Z}
in a way that’s less sensitive to misspecification.
We remark that empirical Bayes can be seen as a frequentist method where the model-fitting criteria are based on Bayesian thinking, and as such it can help reconcile long-standing debates between frequentist and Bayesian inference, see for example
Efron 2024
for a recent discussion.

The paper is structured as follows.
Section
2
reviews basic notions related to empirical Bayes and to the frequentist validity of Bayesian methods, in the more common setting of data integration with full data.
We also point to some literature discussing the benefits of empirical Bayes, which largely focus on parameter estimation and the full data setting.
In Section
3
we outline an empirical Bayes framework for data integration with meta-covariates.
For concreteness we focus on variable selection, but the setup extends directly to more general settings.
Section
4
summarizes the scarce theory on structural learning with meta-covariates.
In particular, we discuss how a simple form of meta-covariate (partitioning the parameters into blocks) leads to consistent model selection in settings where it is otherwise mathematically impossible (milder sparsity or betamin conditions), and that it improves consistency rates.
By model selection (sometimes referred to support recovery) we mean identifying the subset of covariates truly associated to
\bm{y}
.
We claim no methodological novelty for Sections
2
-
3
.
They are however not just a survey, rather they reflect the views of the authors on using empirical Bayes for data integration.
Similarly, the main novelty in Section
4
is adapting results from
Rognon-Vael et al. 2025
to a Bayesian setting.
In Section
5
we present some new results that relate to computational algorithms to estimate the hyper-parameters
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
.
A practical challenge is that evaluating the log marginal likelihood
p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\omega})
requires a sum over models (e.g.
2^{p}
models in regression). We prove that its gradient can be evaluated in a linear cost in
p
in fairly general settings, propose an Expectation-Maximization (EM,
Dempster
et al. 1977
) algorithm, for which we derive simplified gradients and hessians.
In Section
6
we present simulated examples and an application to colon cancer data.
These illustrate that fully Bayesian inference enjoys excellent properties and that its results are often fairly aligned with those from empirical Bayes. However in some settings, including our colon cancer example, there can be moderate but non-negligible differences between the two frameworks.
Section
7
concludes.

2.
Empirical Bayes

We start by discussing a purely frequentist interpretation of Bayesian inference.
The interpretation holds exactly when the prior matches a certain data-generating truth, and asymptotically under certain settings.
We discuss how this motivates the use of empirical Bayes as an attempt to improve the frequentist properties of Bayesian inference,
and explain how fully Bayesian inference cannot attain such an improvement.
We end by mentioning pitfalls of empirical Bayes and how to ameliorate them.

To interpret Bayesian inference from a frequentist standpoint, it may be easiest to consider an example.
Consider a setting where repeated experiments are conducted, with data from experiment
b
generated as
\bm{y}^{(b)}\sim p(\bm{y}^{(b)}\mid\bm{\theta}^{(b)})
, and where the data-generating parameters
\bm{\theta}^{(b)}
are drawn from some distribution
p(\bm{\theta})
.
The usual frequentist setting envisions conducting repeated studies with the same exact parameter
\bm{\theta}^{(1)}=\bm{\theta}^{(2)}=\ldots
(i.e.
p(\bm{\theta})
is a point mass), whereas here we envision a frequentist setting where each experiment has its own data-generating
\bm{\theta}^{(b)}\sim p(\bm{\theta})
.
In a Bayesian analysis, one also specifies a prior
\pi(\bm{\theta})
, which in general will not match
p(\bm{\theta})
.
For example, a pharmaceutical company conducts repeated experiments over time to assess the efficacy of drugs
b=1,2,\ldots
.
One observes the number of cured patients
y^{(b)}\sim\mbox{Beta}(n^{(b)},\theta^{(b)})
and the parameter of interest is the curing probability
\theta^{(b)}
.
Suppose that, unknown to the company, truly
\theta^{(b)}\sim\mbox{Beta}(2,11)
independently across
b
(i.e.,
p(\bm{\theta})
is given by this Beta distribution).
Figure
2
shows an illustration for
n=30
and
n=100
.
For simplicity it assumes that
n^{(1)}=n^{(2)}=\ldots=n
, and that the experiments are independent (given
\theta^{b}
).
The left panels show the
\mbox{Beta}(2,11)
density, two realizations
\theta^{(b)}=0.1
and
0.35
from this
p(\theta)
, and the corresponding Binomial probability mass functions.
Suppose that the data analyst sets independent
\mbox{Beta}(a,b)
priors to each
\theta^{(b)}
, denoted
\pi(\theta^{(b)})
earlier, for some
a,b>0
. The right panels show the posterior distributions
\pi(\theta^{(b)}\mid y^{(b)})
for two putative
y^{(b)}
if the data analyst were to use the correct
\mbox{Beta}(2,11)
prior (black solid lines), where by correct we mean matching the data-generating truth. These posteriors are the frequentist distribution of the
\theta^{(b)}
’s conditional on the observed
y^{(b)}
, that is
\pi(\theta^{(b)}\mid y^{(b)})=p(\theta^{(b)}\mid y^{(b)})
where
p(\theta^{(b)}\mid y^{(b)})\propto p(y^{(b)}\mid\theta^{(b)})p(\theta^{(b)})
. No Bayesian interpretation is needed:
(\theta^{(b)},y^{(b)})
have a frequentist distribution,
p(\theta^{(b)}\mid y^{(b)})
is a well-defined frequentist object,
and posterior inference is well-calibrated from a frequentist standpoint.

Figure
2
also shows another posterior that only has a Bayesian interpretation, obtained by setting a
\mbox{Beta}(4,9)
prior (dashed grey lines).
The new posterior no longer has a direct frequentist interpretation under the data-generating
p(\bm{\theta})
,
but as
n
grows (bottom panels) both posteriors converge to the same answer by virtue of the Bernstein-von Mises theorem (see
van der
Vaart 1998
, Theorem 10.1, for example).
That is, even if one sets the wrong (in a frequentist sense) prior, when certain limiting results apply the posterior asymptotically has a valid frequentist interpretation.
Alas, such limiting theorems typically apply to regular finite-dimensional models, and high-dimensional settings where
\mbox{dim}(\bm{\theta}^{(b)})\gg n
must be handled with more care.

The empirical Bayes framework, proposed by
Robbins 1992
, replaces a fully Bayesian prior
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\omega})
(possibly completed by a hyper-prior
\pi(\bm{\omega})
) by a data-based prior
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\hat{\bm{\omega}})
,
where
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
is usually obtained via marginal likelihood or variations thereof (see Sections
3
and
5
).
The hope is that
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\hat{\bm{\omega}})
is close to the data-generating
p(\bm{\theta})
and hence gives better frequentist properties than
\pi(\bm{\theta})
.

Robbins 1992
considered parameter estimation settings akin to Figure
2
,
where one learns about
\bm{\theta}^{(b)}
by using data
\bm{y}^{(b^{\prime})}
from other experiments
b^{\prime}\neq b
,
by letting
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
and hence also
\pi(\bm{\theta}^{(b)}\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}})
depend on such
\bm{y}^{(b^{\prime})}
.
For a review of empirical Bayes in estimation see
Carlin and
Louis 2000
(Chapter 3)
and for theory see
Butucea and
Comte 2009
;
Efron 2016
.
As mentioned earlier, here we are more interested in data integration and structural learning than in estimation.

Under suitable assumptions, if
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\omega})
is a parametric prior then asymptotically
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
maximizes the prior density of the data-generating
\bm{\theta}^{*}

(
Petrone
et al. 2014
)
. See
Rousseau and
Szabo 2017
for non-parametric extensions.
A natural question is whether a fully Bayesian framework, one where
\bm{\omega}
is not estimated from data, shares such a frequentist interpretation. The answer is that not always.
In some settings the empirical Bayes posterior
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}})
asymptotically matches
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{y})
from a fully Bayesian analysis
(
Petrone
et al. 2014
)
, extending the Bersntein-von Mises argument given above, whereas in others empirical and full Bayes cannot be reconciled.
In Section
3
we discuss intuitively why empirical and full Bayes may give different answers in our data integration setting, in Section
4
we give precise conditions when this occurs, and in Section
6
we show via examples that the differences are usually not large but that they can be non-negligible.
See also
Scott and
Berger 2010
for a discussion in the multiple testing and variable selection settings.

We remark that there are also pitfalls to empirical Bayes.
A first concern for Bayesians is that, by setting a data-dependent
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\hat{\bm{\omega}})
, Bayesian updating is no longer coherent. By coherence we refer to the standard desideratum that, if one observes the data
\bm{y}=(\bm{y}_{1},\bm{y}_{2})
as two separate batches, the posterior
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{y})\propto p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\theta})\pi(\bm{\theta})
is equal to the posterior under the sequential updating

\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{y})\propto p(\bm{y}_{2}\mid\bm{\theta})\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid y_{1})
.
If
\pi(\bm{\theta})
is replaced by
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\hat{\bm{\omega}})
, where
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
depends on
\bm{y}
, such coherence no longer holds.
The second concern is more critical in that it also applies to non-Bayesians. The empirical Bayes estimate
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
may collapse onto degenerate solutions, e.g. where
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\hat{\bm{\omega}})
assigns prior probability 0 or 1 to a parameter being zero, resulting in poor inference.
We discuss some practical solutions to such degeneracies in Section
3
.

3.
Framework

We lay out an empirical Bayes framework for data integration via meta-covariates.
The framework is not new, see
van de
Wiel et al. 2019
and references therein.
Consider a setting where the main data records an outcome
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
n
individuals
and
p
covariates
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
For each covariate
j=1,\ldots,p
, one also has a vector of
q
meta-covariates
\bm{z}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
.
We denote by
\bm{X}
the
n\times p
covariates matrix with
i^{th}
row equal to
\bm{x}_{i}
, and by
\bm{Z}
the
p\times q
metacovariates matrix with
\bm{z}_{j}
as its
j^{th}
row.
We remark that the framework can be easily extended to other settings such as graphical models or factor models
(
Peterson
et al. 2016
;
Schiavon
et al. 2022
;
Jewson et al. 2023
)
, but for simplicity here we focus on regression.

Before proceeding, we discuss some examples to make ideas concrete.
Suppose that one wants to study the association between colon cancer survival (
y_{i}
) and gene expression (
\bm{x}_{i}
), and one has three lists of genes found to be associated to gastrointestinal, endometrial and ovarian cancer survival.
Here,
\bm{z}_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{3}
indicates whether gene
j
is included in these 3 lists.
The meta-covariates
\bm{z}_{j}
may contain further information,
such as the biological pathways that each gene is known to be associated with,
or whether the covariate corresponds to clinical variable, known risk factors, genomic data, etc.
As another example, the director of a school wishes to identify socio-demographic covariates
\bm{X}
associated with academic performance
\bm{y}
. The director has reports giving estimated covariate effects
\bm{Z}
in other schools, but does not have access to the full data from those schools.

3.1.
Empirical vs. full Bayes

As discussed, the key idea is to set a prior

\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{Z},\bm{\omega})
that depends on the meta-covariates
\bm{Z}
via hyper-parameters
\bm{\omega}
,
and where
\bm{\omega}
is estimated via empirical Bayes.
For simplicity we denote
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\omega}):=\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{Z},\bm{\omega})
.
We are primarily interested in structural learning where one considers (possibly many) different models for
\bm{\theta}
.
We denote such models generically by
\bm{\gamma}\in\Gamma
, where
\Gamma
is the set of possible models.
For example, in variable selection we have
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{p})^{T}
regression parameters
and the models are indexed by
\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{p})\in\{0,1\}^{p}
, where
\gamma_{j}=\mbox{I}(\theta_{j}\neq 0)
is an inclusion indicator for covariate
j=1,\ldots,p
.
One may also have parameters that are present in all models, such as the error variance or covariates that one forces to be included in the model, but we omit these from the notation for simplicity.
Formally, each model
\bm{\gamma}
has its own likelihood
p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\theta},\bm{\gamma})
, and the joint fully Bayesian model is

\displaystyle p(\bm{y},\bm{\theta},\bm{\gamma})=p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\theta},\bm{\gamma})\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\gamma})\pi(\bm{\gamma}),

where
\pi(\bm{\gamma})
is some given model prior.
Using Bayes formula, posterior model probabilities are given by
p(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y})\propto p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})\pi(\bm{\gamma})
, where

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})=\int p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\theta},\bm{\gamma})d\Pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\gamma})

is the so-called marginal likelihood of model
\bm{\gamma}
, and
\Pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\gamma})
the prior measure on the parameters under
\bm{\gamma}
.

To achieve data integration within full Bayes, one would replace
\pi(\bm{\gamma})
by some
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})
that depends on the meta-covariates
\bm{Z}
, and then set a hyper-prior
\pi(\bm{\omega})
.
For example, one may set prior inclusion probabilities
\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{\omega})
that depend on the
j^{th}
parameter’s covariates
\bm{z}_{j}
via a logistic regression with linear predictor
\bm{z}_{j}^{T}\bm{\omega}
.
The issue is that such a fully Bayesian framework gives posterior probabilities
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y})\propto p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})\pi(\bm{\gamma})
, where

\displaystyle\pi(\bm{\gamma})=\int\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})d\Pi(\bm{\omega}).

By definition
\pi(\bm{\gamma})
does not depend on
\bm{y}
, it is a quantity defined a priori, and hence it cannot take advantage of learning
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
from
\bm{y}
.
That is, although such
\pi(\bm{\gamma})
depends on meta-covariates, this dependence is set entirely a priori.
For example, suppose that one wants to set different prior inclusion probabilities
\pi(\gamma_{j}=1)
for each
j
, but one wishes to learn them from the data, and sets an exchangeable
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})
.
This task it not possible because
\pi(\bm{\gamma})
is exchangeable (it’s a mixture of exchangeable
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})
).

Here we consider an empirical Bayes framework where one sets
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\hat{\bm{\omega}})
that depends on
\bm{Z}
and where
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
is learned from the observed
\bm{y}
.
Since
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\hat{\bm{\omega}})
may be quite different from the
\pi(\bm{\gamma})
used in a fully Bayes setting,
in settings where the (marginal) likelihood
p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})
does not fully dictate inference even asymptotically (e.g. high-dimensional settings where prior regularization can be critical), the empirical and full Bayes solutions may not match as
n
grows.
In other situations where the combination of
p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})
and
p(\bm{\gamma})
are sufficiently informative, the fully Bayesian solution matches the empirical Bayes solution asymptotically
(
Petrone
et al. 2014
)
.

We remark that in the framework above only the model prior
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})
depends on
\bm{Z}
, but it is also possible to consider a prior on parameters
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\gamma},\bm{\omega})
that depends on
\bm{\omega}
.
For example,
Jewson et al. 2023
considered a spike-and-slab graphical model where the slab’s mean and variance depend on
\bm{Z}
.
The theory of Section
4
can be extended to such settings, but the computational algorithms in Section
5
get more involved, and in particular some of the simplified gradients given there no longer apply.

3.2.
Hyper-parameter estimation in empirical Bayes

A possible strategy to obtain
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
is to maximize the log-marginal likelihood

\displaystyle\log p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\omega})=\log\left(\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma},\bm{\omega})\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})\right).

However in our setting maximizing
p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\omega})
may lead to degenerate priors. Specifically,
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
may lead to prior inclusion probabilities
\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}})
that are either 1 or 0 for all covariates
j
, see Section
5.1
.
Practical fixes include constraining the support of
\bm{\omega}
such that the degeneracies cannot occur,
considering two-step solutions where one does not fully maximize
\log p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\omega})

or setting a minimally informative prior on
\bm{\omega}
.
The theory in Section
4
discusses such a two-step solution, which we use there because it facilitates theoretical analysis.
The results therein show that, for the particular case where one has a single meta-covariate and a particular initial choice of
\bm{\omega}
, the two-step solution asymptotically avoids degeneracies.
More generally however, we advocate for setting minimally informative priors on
\bm{\omega}
that avoid the degeneracies for finite
n
and in more general settings (e.g. more than one covariate), by setting zero prior density to problematic
\bm{\omega}
values.
We next discuss such a possible minimally informative prior, which is based on the idea that prior inclusion probabilities should not be excessively close to 0 or 1, and which we use for our examples in Section
6
.

Suppose that
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\pi(\gamma_{j}\mid\bm{\omega})
and that
\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{\omega})=m_{j}(\bm{\omega})
for some invertible function
m_{j}
.
By default, take the inverse logit function
m_{j}(\bm{\omega})=1/(1+e^{-\bm{z}_{j}^{T}\bm{\omega}})
, for its interpretability and because it leads to some computational simplifications (Section
5
).
Consider the prior
\bm{\omega}\sim N({\bf 0},g\bm{V})
for some known
\bm{V}
where,
taking inspiration from the popular Zellner prior, by default we set
\bm{V}=(\bm{Z}^{T}\bm{Z}/p)^{-1}
.
The scaling by
p
facilitates interpretability: if the columns in
\bm{Z}
(other than the intercept) have zero column means, then
\bm{V}
is equal to the sample precision (inverse covariance) matrix of
\bm{Z}
multiplied by
g
.

We next discuss how to set a minimally informative default for
g
.
The idea is to set
g
such that
\pi(\bm{\omega})
is unlikely to generate a value of
\bm{\omega}
that leads to some prior inclusion probabilities that are very close to 0 or 1.
In more detail, note that the prior inclusion probabilities
m_{j}(\bm{\omega})
are random, since they depend on
\bm{\omega}
.
We propose setting
g
such that all
m_{j}(\bm{\omega})\in(0.001,0.999)

with prior probability
P\left(m_{j}(\bm{\omega})\in[0.001,0.999]\right)\geq 0.95
.
Consider for example the inverse logit function, we then seek

\displaystyle 0.95\leq P\left(m_{j}(\bm{\omega})\in[0.001,0.999]\right)\Rightarrow g\leq\frac{1}{v_{j}}\left(\frac{\log(0.001/0.999)}{\Phi^{-1}(0.05/2)}\right)^{2},

where
\Phi^{-1}
is the standard normal quantile function,
v_{j}=\bm{z}_{j}^{T}\bm{V}\bm{z}_{j}
, and we used that
\bm{z}_{j}^{T}\bm{\omega}\sim N(0,gv_{j})
.
We hence take
g
by replacing
v_{j}
above by
\max_{j=1}^{p}v_{j}
.

4.
Theory for variable selection consistency

We consider a simplified setting where one has a high-dimensional linear regression
\bm{y}\sim N(\bm{X}\bm{\theta},\phi I)
,
where
\phi>0
is the error variance,
and a single discrete meta-covariate
z_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,B\}
that splits covariates
j=1,\ldots,p
into
B
blocks.
Letting
\gamma_{j}=\mbox{I}(\theta_{j}\neq 0)
as before, we consider a model prior

\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\mbox{Bern}(\gamma_{j};\omega_{z_{j}})
,
where
\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{B})\in[0,1]^{B}
are prior inclusion probabilities for each block.
The number of covariates
p
can grow with
n
.
To ease the exposition and proofs, we assume that
\phi
is known and that one sets Zellner’s prior

\displaystyle\pi(\bm{\theta}_{\bm{\gamma}}\mid\bm{\gamma})=N(\bm{\theta}_{\bm{\gamma}};{\bf 0},g\phi(\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}/n)^{-1}),

where
\bm{\theta}_{\bm{\gamma}}=\{\theta_{j}:\gamma_{j}=1\}
are the non-zero parameters under model
\bm{\gamma}
,

\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}
the corresponding columns of
\bm{X}
and
g>0
a given prior dispersion.
Our results can be extended to unknown
\phi
and to other Gaussian priors by placing eigenvalue conditions on
\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}
, for example those listed in Sections 2.3-2.4 of
Rossell 2022
.
In particular, one could consider a block-dependent prior dispersion, but we do not do this here for brevity and because its main role would be to induce block-dependent sparsity, which is already achieved by
\bm{\omega}
.
Throughout, we assume that one constrains attention to models such that
\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}
has full column rank for any
\bm{\gamma}\in\Gamma
, and that the true model lies in the set of considered models.

We provide two main results. Theorem
1
in Section
4.1

states that, when
\bm{\omega}
satisfies certain conditions, the posterior distribution on models
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})
concentrates on
\bm{\gamma}^{*}=(\gamma_{1}^{*},\ldots,\gamma_{p}^{*})
, where
\gamma_{j}^{*}=\mbox{I}(\theta_{j}^{*}\neq 0)
and
\bm{\theta}^{*}
is the data-generating parameter.
Theorem
1
hold under milder conditions than those required when setting equal prior inclusion probabilities in all blocks
\omega_{1}=\ldots=\omega_{B}

(obviously, since we consider a strictly wider set of
\bm{\omega}
values), and also than those required by the Beta-Binomial model prior of
Scott and
Berger 2010
, and by any standard (non block-dependent)
L_{0}
criterion.
The interest of Theorem
1
is that it gives the best possible consistency result attainable by integrating the meta-covariates
\bm{Z}
, if an oracle were to set
\bm{\omega}
to an ideal value.
Theorem
2
in Section
4.2
shows that, under slightly stronger conditions, one can obtain
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
that also leads to consistent model selection under milder conditions than under
\omega_{1}=\ldots=\omega_{B}
, a Beta-Binomial model prior, and standard
L_{0}
criteria.

4.1.
Improved consistency with data integration

We denote by
p_{b}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mbox{I}(z_{j}=b)
the number of variables in block
b
,
by
p_{\bm{\gamma},b}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\gamma_{j}\mbox{I}(z_{j}=b)
the number of those selected by
\bm{\gamma}
,
and by
s_{b}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\gamma_{j}^{*}\mbox{I}(z_{j}=b)
the number of truly active variables in block
b
.
We denote by
\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}
the subset of columns of
\bm{X}
selected by
\bm{\gamma}
(i.e., such that
\gamma_{j}=1
),
and
\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}^{*}\setminus\bm{\gamma}}
those selected by
\bm{\gamma}^{*}
but not
\bm{\gamma}
.
Under the priors set above, simple algebra shows that

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{\pi(\bm{\gamma}^{*}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})}{\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})}=\exp\left\{-\frac{gnW_{\bm{\gamma}\bm{\gamma}^{*}}}{2\phi(1+gn)}\right\}\prod_{b=1}^{B}\left(\frac{\omega_{b}}{(1+gn)^{1/2}(1-\omega_{b})}\right)^{p_{\bm{\gamma}^{*},b}-p_{\bm{\gamma},b}}

where
W_{\bm{\gamma}\bm{\gamma}^{*}}=\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\gamma}}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\gamma}^{*}}^{T}\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}^{*}}^{T}\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}^{*}}\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\gamma}^{*}}

and
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\gamma}}=(\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}})^{-1}\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}\bm{y}
is the least-squares estimate.
The first term on the right-hand side in (
1
) compares the sum of squared residuals between
\bm{\gamma}
and
\bm{\gamma}^{*}
,
and hence rewards goodness-of-fit, whereas the second term is a complexity penalty.
Its logarithm is
\sum_{b=1}^{B}(p_{\bm{\gamma},b}-p_{\bm{\gamma}^{*},b})\kappa_{b}
, where

(2)

\displaystyle\kappa_{b}=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+gn)+\log(1/\omega_{b}-1)

plays a key role in our conditions, which we now state.

A1.

The number of blocks
B
is constant.

A2.

For each block
b
, there exists
f_{b}\to\infty
(as
n\to\infty
) such that for sufficiently large
n
,

\kappa_{b}\;=\;\log(p_{b}-s_{b})+f_{b}
.

A3.

For each block
b
, there exists
g_{b}\to\infty
such that for sufficiently large
n
,

\sqrt{\frac{(1-\nu)n\phi^{-1}\rho(\bm{X})}{6}}{\theta_{\min,b}^{*}}\,-\,\sqrt{\kappa_{b}}\;=\;\sqrt{\log(s_{b})}+g_{b},

where
\theta_{\min,b}=\min_{z_{j}=b,\theta_{j}^{*}\neq 0}|\theta_{j}^{*}|
,

\nu:=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\max_{b}\log(p_{b}-s_{b})/\kappa_{b})\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
,

(3)

\rho(\bm{X})=\min_{\bm{\gamma}:\bm{\gamma}\not\supseteq\bm{\gamma}^{*}}\lambda_{\min}\big(\tfrac{1}{n}\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}^{*}\setminus\bm{\gamma}}^{\top}\left(I-\bm{P}_{\bm{\gamma}}\right)\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}^{*}\setminus\bm{\gamma}}\big),

\lambda_{\min}
denotes the smallest eigenvalue,
and
\bm{P}_{\bm{\gamma}}=\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}})^{-1}\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}
is the usual projection matrix onto the column span of
\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}
.

Assumption A2 requires
\kappa_{b}
to be large enough, that is that the prior penalizes sufficiently complex models.
A3 is a betamin condition on the smallest non-zero parameter
\theta_{\min,b}^{*}
in block
b
.
The quantity
\rho(\bm{X})
is non-negative and relates to how distinguishable non-overfitted models
\bm{\gamma}
are from
\bm{\gamma}^{*}
. More specifically,
\tfrac{1}{n}\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}^{*}\setminus\bm{\gamma}}^{T}\left(I-P_{\bm{\gamma}}\right)\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}^{*}\setminus\bm{\gamma}}
is the sample covariance of the residuals when regressing
\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}^{*}\setminus\bm{\gamma}}
on
\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}
,
and
\rho(\bm{X})=1
in a simplest orthonormal case where
\bm{X}^{T}\bm{X}=nI
.
Assumptions A2-A3 are fairly minimal.
For orthonormal designs A2-A3 are similar to Assumptions A4-A5 in
Rognon-Vael et al. 2025
, which the authors showed to be near-necessary.
Also, when applied to the
B=1
blocks setting, these conditions are very similar (and in some settings, slightly weaker) than those required by
L_{0}
criteria that know the true model size analyzed in
Wainwright 2009
.
Further,
Rognon-Vael et al. 2025
(Sections 4.3 and S7) showed that A2-A3 match necessary conditions for consistent model recovery in a wide range of regimes, and that A2-A3 are milder than those required when one uses standard
L_{0}
criteria or a Beta-Binomial model prior.

Theorem 1
.

If Assumptions A1 holds and
\bm{\omega}
satisfies A2 and A3,
then
\lim_{n\to\infty}E\left[\pi(\bm{\gamma}^{*}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\right]=1
.

Theorem
1
shows that
\pi(\bm{\gamma}^{*}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})
converges to 1 in the
L_{1}
sense.
Its proof also provides bounds for the convergence rate, which we omit here for simplicity.
By Proposition 1 in
Rossell 2022
, such
L_{1}
convergence rates bound the frequentist probability of a wrong model selection, as well as type I and II error probabilities.

4.2.
Consistency for empirical Bayes

Theorem
1
gives a range of block-based
\bm{\omega}
leading to consistent model selection.
We now show that it is possible to obtain a data-based
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
that also leads to consistent model selection.
We analyze the following two-step procedure.

(1)

Set
\hat{\omega}_{b}^{(0)}=1/(p+1)
for
b=1,\ldots,B
, so that

\kappa_{b}^{(0)}=\log(p)+\frac{1}{2}\log(1+gn)
.

(2)

Set
\hat{\omega}_{b}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{p_{b}}\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}}^{(0)})
,
and
\kappa_{b}^{(1)}=\log(1/\hat{\omega}_{b}^{(0)}-1)+\frac{1}{2}\log(1+gn)
.

This procedure can be seen as first taking
\hat{\omega}_{b}^{(0)}=1/(p+1)
in all blocks, which is a sparse choice assuming a constant prior expected number of active covariates as
p
grows.
If one sets
\hat{\omega}_{b}^{(0)}=c/(p+1)
for constant
c
, our theory continues to hold with minor modifications.
In Step 2, one obtains
\hat{\omega}_{b}^{(1)}
by taking the average posterior inclusion probability in block
b
, given the data and
\hat{\bm{\omega}}^{(0)}
. Formally, Step 2 is a fixed-point iteration aimed at setting
\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}\log p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\omega})={\bf 0}
, see the discussion after Theorem
3
in Section
5.1
.

The consistency of the two-step procedure can be established under slightly stronger conditions than A2-A3, called A4-A6 below, but still milder than those attainable by a model prior that does not use the meta-covariates.
To discuss these assumptions, we introduce a notion of small, intermediate and large truly active signals
|\theta_{j}^{*}|/\sqrt{\phi}
in each block,
denoted
S_{b}^{S}
,
S_{b}^{I}
, and
S_{b}^{L}
respectively.
Let
\bar{\lambda}
be the largest eigenvalue of
\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}^{*}}^{T}\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}^{*}}

and
\kappa_{b}
as defined in (
2
), then

\displaystyle S_{b}^{S}(\kappa_{b}):=\left\{j:z_{j}=b,\theta_{j}^{*}\neq 0,\sqrt{n\bar{\lambda}}|\theta_{j}^{*}|/\sqrt{\phi}=o\big(\sqrt{\kappa_{b}}\big)\right\}

\displaystyle S_{b}^{L}(\kappa_{b}):=\left\{j:z_{j}=b,\theta_{j}^{*}\neq 0,\sqrt{\frac{(1-\nu)n\rho(\bm{X})}{6\phi}}|\theta_{j}^{*}|\,-\,\sqrt{\kappa_{b}}\;=\;\sqrt{\ln(s_{b})}+g_{b}\right\}.

\displaystyle S_{b}^{I}(\kappa_{b}):=\left\{j:z_{j}=b\right\}\setminus\big(S_{b}^{L}(\kappa_{b})\cup S_{b}^{S}(\kappa_{b})\big).

We are now ready to state the conditions.
Condition A4 imposes a mild bound on number of truly active covariates.
A5 is a slightly stronger version of A3, requiring that the number of large signals is not too small,
where as A6 requires that the number of small signals is not too large.
Both conditions are stated in terms of
\kappa_{b}^{(0)}
defined in Step 1.
Intuitively, if there were too many small signals or too few large signals relative to
\kappa_{b}^{(0)}
, then
\hat{\omega}_{b}^{(1)}
would be a poor estimator of the proportion of truly active covariates in block
b
, and the posterior would not be guaranteed to concentrate on
\bm{\gamma}^{*}
.

A4.

The total number of truly active covariates
\sum_{b=1}^{B}s_{b}=o(\sqrt{1+gn})
.

A5.

For each block
b
, there exists
a_{b}\to\infty
such that for sufficiently large
n
,

\sqrt{\frac{(1-\psi)n\phi^{-1}\rho(\bm{X})}{6}}{\theta_{\min,b}^{*}}-\sqrt{\log\bigg(\frac{p_{b}}{|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa_{b}^{(0)})|}-1\bigg)+\frac{1}{2}\log(1+gn)}\;=\;\sqrt{\log(s_{b})}+a_{b}.

where
\psi=\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(1+\max_{b}\log(p_{b}-s_{b})/\big(\log(p_{b}/s_{b}-1)+\log(1+gn)/2\big)\Big)
.

A6.

For each block
b
, it holds that
|S^{I}_{b}(\kappa_{b}^{(0)})|=O(1)
and
|S^{S}_{b}(\kappa_{b}^{(0)})|=O(p_{b}-s_{b})
.

Theorem 2
.

If Assumptions A1, A4, A5 and A6 hold,
then
\lim_{n\to\infty}E\left[\pi(\bm{\gamma}^{*}\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}}^{(1)})\right]=1
.

5.
Computational framework

We discuss computational algorithms to obtain the empirical Bayes estimate

\hat{\bm{\omega}}=\arg\max_{\bm{\omega}}\log\pi(\bm{\omega}\mid\bm{y})=\arg\max_{\bm{\omega}}\log p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\omega})+\log\pi(\bm{\omega})=

(4)

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\bm{\omega}}\log\left(\sum_{\bm{\gamma}}p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})\right)-\frac{1}{2g}\bm{\omega}^{T}\bm{V}^{-1}\bm{\omega},

where
(g,\bm{V})
are as in Section
3.2
. Directly maximizing (
4
) poses a computational challenge,
because it involves a prohibitive sum over all models
\bm{\gamma}
, e.g.
2^{p}
in regression.
We next discuss some strategies that render this problem tractable.

In Section
5.1
we provide results
showing that maximizing (
4
) is amenable to stochastic gradient methods, as long as one can quickly evaluate
p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})
for any given model
\bm{\gamma}
.
We remark that
p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})
is only available in closed-form for specific models such as linear or additive regression with Gaussian outcomes.
However, in practice one can use Laplace approximations and recent extensions like the approximate Laplace approximation
(
Kass
et al. 1990
;
Rossell
et al. 2021
;
Rossell and
Rubio 2021
)
, which allow to quickly approximate
p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})
in popular models such as generalized linear and generalized additive models.
Hence, the results in Section
5.1
can be useful for a relatively wide model class.
Section
5.2
extends the results of Section
5.1
to the setting of an EM algorithm
that eases somewhat the computational burden of the stochastic gradients from Section
5.1
.
Finally, in Section
5.3
we discuss how to go beyond a point estimate
\hat{\bm{\omega}}

to obtain a full posterior
\pi(\bm{\omega}\mid\bm{y})
,
and briefly discuss strategies for the case where
p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})
cannot be easily computed.
We remark that
\log\pi(\bm{\omega}\mid\bm{y})
is typically multi-modal, hence gradient-based methods find a local mode,
whereas sampling
\pi(\bm{\omega}\mid\bm{y})
may be more effective at exploring multiple modes.

5.1.
Gradient-based methods

Our main results are as follows.
First, Theorem
3
shows that under fairly general conditions the gradient of (
4
) can be evaluated as a sum over only
p
terms.
We then derive an EM algorithm to obtain a local mode for (
4
)
that also only requires sums over
p
terms (Corollary
1
), and brings some further simplifications.
Finally, Proposition
1
shows that in an important particular case where the meta-covariates
\bm{Z}
partition the parameters into blocks, the M-step is available essentially in closed-form.
For example, the partition could be given by a list of covariates found to be relevant in a prior study, or by clustering the covariates into blocks using
\bm{Z}
.
More specifically, the main implication of Theorem
3
is that, if
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})
factors,
then
\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}\log p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\omega})
can be evaluated as a sum over
p
terms.
Theorem
3
is not limited to
\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{\omega})
given by the logit function:
one may one may parameterize
\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{\omega})
using an arbitrary function
m_{j}()
, including non-parametric choices.
Theorem
3
is a novel result that is related to Proposition 3.1 in
Papaspiliopoulos et al. 2025
and Lemma S0.1 in
Rognon-Vael et al. 2025
, but is more general as these authors considered different settings to ours and only the logit function.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\propto p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})
for
an arbitrary model prior
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})
. Then

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}\log p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\omega})=E_{\bm{\gamma}}\left[\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}\log\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}\right]=\sum_{\bm{\gamma}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}\log\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega}).

If
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\mbox{Bern}(\gamma_{j};m_{j}(\bm{\omega}))
for a given differentiable
m_{j}(\bm{\omega})
, then

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}\log p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\omega})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}m_{j}(\bm{\omega})}{m_{j}(\bm{\omega})[1-m_{j}(\bm{\omega})]}\left[\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})-\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{\omega})\right].

Further,
\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}\log p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\omega})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bm{z}_{j}\left[\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})-\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{\omega})\right]

for the inverse logit function
m_{j}(\bm{\omega})=(1+e^{-\bm{z}_{j}^{T}\bm{\omega}})^{-1}
.

Beyond computation, Theorem
3
provides intuition for the empirical Bayes solution, and for the degeneracies associated to maximizing
p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\omega})
mentioned in Section
3.2
.
Said interpretation is simplest when
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\mbox{Bern}(\gamma_{j};m_{j}(\bm{\omega}))
and
m_{j}(\bm{\omega})
is the inverse logit function. Then, setting
\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}\log p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\omega})={\bf 0}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bm{z}_{j}\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{\omega})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bm{z}_{j}\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}).

That is, the inner-products between the columns of
\bm{Z}
and the vector of prior inclusion probabilities

(\pi(\gamma_{1}=1\mid\bm{\omega}),\ldots,\pi(\gamma_{p}=1\mid\bm{\omega}))
must be equal to the inner products with the posterior inclusion probabilities.
For example, if
\bm{Z}
divides covariates into
B
blocks, this implies that the average prior and posterior inclusion probabilities within each block must be equal.
This is on the one hand intuitive, one sets higher prior inclusion probabilities in the blocks where the corresponding posterior probabilities are higher.
On the other hand the expression for the gradient highlights the degeneracy problem: if
\bm{\omega}
is such that all
\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{\omega})
is equal to either 1 or 0 for all
j
, then
\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})=\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{\omega})
and
\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}\log p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\omega})={\bf 0}
.
This issue can be addressed by setting a minimally informative
\pi(\bm{\omega})
(Section
3.2
).

Theorem
3
enables the use of stochastic gradient methods where one uses samples
from
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})
to estimate
\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}\log p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\omega})
.
In principle, for each newly considered
\bm{\omega}
one must obtain new samples from the corresponding
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})
.
This is less cumbersome than it might first appear.
First,
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\propto p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})
where
p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})
does not depend on
\bm{\omega}
. Hence, once
p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})
is first computed, it can be stored and re-used for all
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})
.
Second, if one has samples from
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})
one may use sequential Monte Carlo to sample from

\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{\prime})
, and the associated weights are simply
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega}^{\prime})/\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})
.
Third, we next derive an EM that allows one to do multiple updates of
\bm{\omega}
using a single set of samples from
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})
at each EM iteration.

5.2.
Expectation-Maximization algorithm

Consider an EM algorithm setting where the likelihood for the full data
(\bm{y},\bm{\gamma})
is given by

p(\bm{y},\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})\propto p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})
,
and where
\bm{\gamma}
are the latent variables in the usual definition of the EM algorithm.
The algorithm requires an E-step where one obtains the expected full data log-likelihood with respect to
\bm{\gamma}
,
given the data
\bm{y}
and the current parameter estimate
\hat{\bm{\omega}}^{(k)}
.
This requires obtaining
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}}^{(k)})
and, if an exact computation is too costly (one cannot enumerate all models), one may use an stochastic EM algorithm where
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}}^{(k)})
is replaced by the frequency of posterior samples visiting model
\bm{\gamma}
.
Given such
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}}^{(k)})
, the M-step seeks to maximize

\displaystyle E_{\bm{\gamma}}\left[\log p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})+\log\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}=\hat{\bm{\omega}}^{(k)}\right]+\log\pi(\bm{\omega})=c+f(\bm{\omega})+\log\pi(\bm{\omega}),

where
c
is a constant that does not depend on
\bm{\omega}
and

\displaystyle f(\bm{\omega})=\sum_{\bm{\gamma}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}}^{(k)})\log\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega}).

This expression is analogous to that in Theorem
3
, replacing
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})
by
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}}^{(k)})
.
Also similar to Theorem
3
,
Corollary
1
gives an expression to evaluate
\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}f(\bm{\omega})
using only
p
operations
when
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})
factors.

Corollary 1
.

If
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\mbox{Bern}(\gamma_{j}\mid m_{j}(\bm{\omega}))
, then

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}f(\bm{\omega})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}m_{j}(\bm{\omega})}{m_{j}(\bm{\omega})[1-m_{j}(\bm{\omega})]}\left[\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}})-\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{\omega})\right].

In the particular case
m_{j}(\bm{\omega})=1/(1+e^{\bm{z}_{j}^{T}\bm{\omega}})
, then

\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}f(\bm{\omega})=\bm{Z}^{T}[\hat{\bm{\pi}}-{\bf m}(\bm{\omega})]

and

\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}^{2}f(\bm{\omega})=-\bm{Z}^{T}\bm{D}(\bm{\omega})\bm{Z}
where

\hat{\bm{\pi}}=(\pi(\gamma_{1}\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}}),\ldots,\pi(\gamma_{p}\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}}))^{T}
,

{\bf m}(\bm{\omega})=(m_{1}(\bm{\omega}),\ldots,m_{p}(\bm{\omega}))^{T}
and

\bm{D}(\bm{\omega})=\mbox{diag}(m_{1}(\bm{\omega}),\ldots,m_{p}(\bm{\omega}))
.

Briefly, each M-step requires solving
\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}f(\bm{\omega})=\bm{A}[\hat{\bm{\pi}}-{\bf m}(\bm{\omega})]={\bf 0}
,
where
\bm{A}
is the
q\times p
matrix with
j^{th}
column equal to
[\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}m_{j}(\bm{\omega})]/(m_{j}(\bm{\omega})[1-m_{j}(\bm{\omega})])
,

{\bf m}(\bm{\omega})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
the vector with
j^{th}
entry equal to
m_{j}(\bm{\omega})
,
and
\hat{\bm{\pi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
the vector with
j^{th}
entry
\pi(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}})
.
One may use any standard optimization algorithm, and in our examples we used Newton-Raphson.
When
m_{j}(\bm{\omega})
is the inverse logit function, it is easy to see that the objective function of the M-step is strictly concave and therefore the maximum, if it exists, is unique.

Finally, we consider in Proposition

an important particular case where the M-step has a particularly simple solution.
The result holds when
\bm{Z}
has
q
unique and linearly independent rows.
This occurs when
\bm{Z}
divides the covariates into
q
blocks,
and when the rows of
\bm{Z}
are clustered into
q
blocks, and one replaces
\bm{Z}
by the mean of the assigned cluster.
The solution requires a univariate function
h()
that can be easily evaluated using Newton’s method.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\bm{U}
be the sub-matrix containing the unique rows of the
p\times q
matrix
\bm{Z}
.
Suppose that
\bm{U}
has
q
rows and rank
q
,
m_{j}(\bm{\omega})=1/(1+e^{-\bm{z}_{j}^{T}\bm{\omega}})
,
and
\bm{\omega}\sim N({\bf 0},g(\bm{Z}^{T}\bm{Z}/p)^{-1})
.
Then

where

\tilde{\bm{Z}}=\bm{Z}\bm{U}^{-1}
and

5.3.
Other computational methods

An alternative to obtaining a point estimate
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
maximizing the posterior
\pi(\bm{\omega}\mid\bm{y})
is to approximate this posterior with MCMC. That is, one performs fully Bayesian inference using
\pi(\bm{\omega}\mid\bm{y})
, obtains
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
as some sensible posterior summary such as the posterior mean,
and finally uses a second MCMC run to approximate
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}})
.
Here we do not discuss the many options available in the MCMC literature,
but rather outline strategies that are relatively simple to implement.

A first option is to use the gradients derived in Section
5.1
within stochastic gradient MCMC (see
Nemeth and
Fearnhead 2021
for a review).
Alternatively, it is often simple to design an MCMC algorithm to sample either from
\pi(\bm{\gamma},\bm{\omega}\mid\bm{y})
or from
\pi(\bm{\theta},\bm{\gamma},\bm{\omega}\mid\bm{y})
.
For example, this strategy was used by
Jewson et al. 2023
in Gaussian graphical model setting. Briefly, given some initialization
(\bm{\gamma}^{(0)},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})
,
one may use a block Gibbs sampler where its iteration
l=1,2,\ldots
proceeds as follows:

(1)

Sample
\bm{\gamma}^{(l)}\sim\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(l-1)})\propto p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega}^{(l-1)})
.

(2)

Sample
\bm{\omega}^{(l)}\sim\pi(\bm{\omega}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\gamma}^{(l)})\propto\pi(\bm{\gamma}^{(l)}\mid\bm{\omega})\pi(\bm{\omega})
.

Upon convergence,
\bm{\omega}^{(l)}
is by definition a sample from
\pi(\bm{\omega}\mid\bm{y})
.
One may set
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
to the posterior mean
L^{-1}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\bm{\omega}^{(l)}
(possibly after discarding a burn-in period),
or to some Monte Carlo estimate of the posterior mode.
Step 1 requires sampling from the posterior of the model
\bm{\gamma}
given hyper-parameters
\bm{\omega}=\bm{\omega}^{(l-1)}
.
This step has the same complexity as sampling
\bm{\gamma}
in a fully Bayesian setting where no data integration is performed.
Step 1 is easy to implement as long as one can compute or approximate
p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})
, as is the case for generalized linear and generalized additive models, for example (see the opening of Section
5
for some discussion).
Step 2 is typically also easy.
First, note that
\bm{\omega}
is often low-dimensional and that

\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}\log\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})
is usually easy to obtain (else, one can use automatic differentiation), enabling the use of gradient-based methods such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo.
Second, if
\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\omega})=\prod_{j}\mbox{Bern}(\gamma_{j};m_{j}(\bm{\omega}))
where
m_{j}
is the inverse logit (or some other suitable) function, Step 2 requires sampling from the posterior of a logistic regression model where the observed outcomes are
\gamma_{1}^{(l)},\ldots,\gamma_{p}^{(l)}
, for which a pletora of algorithms are available.

If marginal likelihoods
p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma})
in Step 1 are hard to approximate, one could alternatively sample

\bm{\theta}^{(l)}\sim\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{y})
using PDMPs such as the sticky Zig-Zag sampler
(
Bierkens et al. 2023
)
,
and subsequently obtain
\bm{\gamma}^{(l)}
as a deterministic function of
\bm{\theta}^{(l)}
.
Briefly, PDMPs only require being able to evaluate the likelihood
\pi(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\theta})
,
and can handle situations where
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\omega})
is such that some singletons have non-zero prior probability.
For example, in variable selection we have non-zero
\pi(\theta_{j}=0\mid\bm{\omega})
and,
after sampling
\bm{\theta}^{(l)}\sim\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})
, one simply computes
\gamma_{j}^{(l)}=\mbox{I}(\theta_{j}^{(l)}\neq 0)
.

If one only seeks a point estimate
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
, there’s yet another strategy.
One can replace the model selection prior
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\omega})
, which places positive probability to zero parameter values, by a continuous prior that sets high probability close to zero
(
George and
McCulloch 1993
)
.
Typically this is achieved by replacing Dirac measures at 0 by Gaussian or Laplace distributions that have zero mean and small variance.
In this setting it is possible to devise an EM algorithm to find a local mode of

\pi(\bm{\theta},\bm{\omega}\mid\bm{y})

(
Rockova and
George 2014
;
Avalos-Pacheco et al. 2022
)
.
Briefly, the algorithm views
\bm{\gamma}
as a latent variable and
defines a complete-data likelihood
p(\bm{y},\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{\theta},\bm{\omega})
,
similarly to the EM algorithm outlined in Section
5.2
,
except that the parameters are now
(\bm{\theta},\bm{\omega})
rather than only
\bm{\omega}
.
This strategy can be very fast computationally, because the EM updates do not involve integrals over
\bm{\gamma}

and they may be even available in closed-form.
Alas, the value of
\bm{\omega}
featuring in the mode of
\pi(\bm{\theta},\bm{\omega}\mid\bm{y})
is different from the mode of
\pi(\bm{\omega}\mid\bm{y})
. Whereas the latter has been well-studied theoretically (see Section
2
), the former lacks theoretical guarantees (to our knowledge).
Unless such guarantees are provided, we do not recommend this strategy.

Examples

To assess the improvements in inference brought by data integration,
we compared the performance of our empirical Bayes framework relative to an analogous framework where
\bm{Z}
only contains the intercept.
We also considered a fully Bayesian counterpart that sets a Beta-Binomial model prior
(
Scott and
Berger 2010
)
, which in our experience is an excellent default,
and three penalized likelihood methods.
The latter are the LASSO
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
, adaptive LASSO (ALASSO,
Zou 2006
) and SCAD
(
Fan and Li 2001
)
,
and we set their penalization parameters with 10-fold cross-validation
using functions mylars and ncvreg in R packages parcor 0.2.6 and ncvreg 3.15.0 (respectively).

For our empirical Bayes methodology we used the EM algorithm from Section
5
, which we implemented in function modelSelection_eBayes in R package mombf, using 1,000 full Gibbs iterations (i.e.
1000p
model updates) to estimate log-gradients and hessians at each hyper-parameter value
\bm{\omega}
.
For fully Bayesian inference under the Beta-Binomial prior we used function modelSelection, also at mombf.
In both cases, we used 5,000 full Gibbs iterations to explore the model space.
Regarding the prior on the coefficients, we set a product MOM prior with default prior dispersion (
g=1/3
, after the columns in
\bm{X}
are standardized to unit sample variance)
(
Johnson and
Rossell 2012
;
Rossell and
Telesca 2017
)
.
As a sensitivity analysis, we also obtained results using Zellner’s unit information prior. For clarity of the figures and space constraints, Zellner’s prior results are in the supplement.
The prior on the error variance was set to a (minimally informative) inverse gamma(0.01, 0.01).
The R code to reproduce our analyses is at

In molecular biology it is common to use animal models (e.g. mice) to study genomic mechanisms such as gene expression.
Findings from such studies often serve as a basis to study human diseases, for example to assess whether genes that played key roles in the animal model are also important in humans.
Here we assess the performance of our empirical Bayes methodology in such a transfer learning task (from animal models to humans) in colon cancer.

Specifically,
Calon et al. 2012
compared mice where gene TGFB was silenced to mice where it was not, found a list of 172 genes that were associated to TGFB, and showed that TGFB plays a crucial role in colon cancer progression.
We consider a colon cancer dataset analyzed in
Rossell and
Telesca 2017

p=1,000

n=262

Both TGFB and the other genes were standardized to zero mean and unit variance.
Given the importance of TGFB in colon cancer, it is of interest to understand how its expression is associated to other genes.
To this end, we used our methodology to regress TGFB on the 1,000 genes, using as a meta-covariate the binary indicator of whether the gene was in the mouse shortlist or not.
Hence,
\bm{Z}
is a
1000\times 2
matrix containing the intercept and said indicator (to be specific, coded with a sum-to-zero constraint, so that it is orthogonal to the intercept).

As a purely exploratory analysis, TGFB showed stronger correlations with genes inside the mouse shortlist than with genes outside, see Figure
5
(left).
While this suggests that the mouse findings may have value for humans, these correlations only measure marginal association, and are not concluding evidence that the mouse study helped identify genes that are predictive of TGFB (i.e., conditionally associated with TGFB, given all other genes).
To assess this rigorously, we performed Bayesian variable selection with our empirical Bayes framework.
For comparison, we repeated the exercise under a Beta-Binomial prior, which does not use the mouse data.
We report results when using the pMOM prior on parameters, the results for Zellner’s prior are almost identical and are in the supplement.
The estimated empirical Bayes prior inclusion probabilities were 0.032 for genes in the mouse list and 0.015 for those outside.
Figure
5
(right) shows the posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) for all genes using empirical Bayes and using the Beta-Binomial.
While the PIP for most genes was close to 0 under both, there are non-negligible differences for genes with large PIPs.
Table
1
shows genes with PIP
>
0.5 under either analysis.
Empirical Bayes assigned higher PIPs to all these genes, e.g. 2 had PIP
>

These differences in PIP are not huge, but they can have practical implications.
For example, if one sets a stringent PIP threshold such as 0.95, only empirical Bayes would find a gene associated to TGFB.
This gene is CILP, a cartilage intermediate layer protein that has been independently found to be associated to colon cancer progression

(
Wang et al. 2023

At a 0.9 threshold a second gene emerges, GAS1. This is a growth-arrest specific gene that is associated to colon cancer metastatis

.
Note that both CILP and GAS1 were in the mouse shortlist, as well as HIC1 and ESM1, but not KCNJ5-AS1.

While this discussion already suggests that the empirical Bayes findings are supported by the biological literature, we conducted two more analyses to further assess that using the mouse-based meta-covariates gave better inference.
First, we used leave-one-out cross-validation to assess the accuracy of the Bayesian model averaging predictions.
Specifically, we computed the
R^{2}

obtaining
R^{2}=

That is, adding the mouse data gave slightly higher out-of-sample predictive accuracy.
Second, there is the question of whether the estimated

is significantly different from 0.
To assess this in an independent fashion from our Bayesian analysis, we used the hyper-parameter estimates from the leave-one-out exercise to obtain a jackknife confidence interval. Said interval was (0.68, 0.82), and all the estimated
\omega_{2}
were
>
0.64, providing strong evidence that truly
\omega_{2}\neq 0
and that the mouse findings are informative for humans.

7.
Discussion

Bayesian inference is naturally suited to incorporate previous data or knowledge into the current analysis.
Fully Bayesian inference is a powerful framework that is particularly well-suited to this task when one is willing to set a moderately informative prior linking the current and previous data or parameters.
In most situations full Bayes enjoys excellent properties. In particular, as
n
grows its solution often matches that of empirical Bayes and enjoys a frequentist interpretation as conditional probabilities on parameters given the data, under repeated sampling of both data and parameters.
There are situations when full and empirical Bayes do not match asymptotically, these are somewhat extreme settings where the prior plays a critical role.
In our data integration context, this occurs for example when parameters are divided into two blocks and one is much sparser than the other.
From a theoretical viewpoint, it is interesting that the mathematical conditions for model selection consistency are milder when integrates data. As future work, it would be interesting to consider more complex models than linear regression, and cases where
\bm{Z}
contains many meta-covariates. It would also be interesting to compare our results to those obtained when doing data integration with full data.
From a practical viewpoint, in most of our examples the benefits of data integration were moderate but non-negligible.

Performing data integration via empirical Bayes has a computational cost, due to adding more layers to the model.
We discussed simple strategies that rendered computations feasible in our examples, but more work in this area is needed.
Particularly as one considers transfer learning in large models, where data integration seems particularly promising.

\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}f(\bm{\omega})+\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}\log\pi(\bm{\omega})={\bf 0}
.
Plugging in the expression for
\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}f(\bm{\omega})
and
\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}\log\pi(\bm{\omega})=-\frac{1}{g}\bm{V}^{-1}\bm{\omega}
,
where
\bm{V}^{-1}=(\bm{Z}^{T}\bm{Z})/p
, we seek
\bm{\omega}
such that

\displaystyle\bm{Z}^{T}[\hat{\bm{\pi}}-{\bf m}(\bm{\omega})]-\frac{1}{gp}\bm{Z}^{T}\bm{Z}\bm{\omega}=0\Leftrightarrow\bm{Z}^{T}\left[{\bf m}(\bm{\omega})+\frac{1}{gp}\bm{Z}\bm{\omega}\right]=\bm{Z}^{T}\hat{\bm{\pi}},

where
m_{j}(\bm{\omega})=1/(1+e^{-\bm{z}_{j}^{T}\bm{\omega}})
.
The
q\times q
matrix
\bm{U}
contains the unique rows in
\bm{Z}
and is full-rank, hence
\tilde{\bm{Z}}=\bm{Z}\bm{U}^{-1}
is a matrix whose unique rows equal the identity matrix.
Define
\tilde{\bm{\omega}}=\bm{U}\bm{\omega}
and consider the problem of finding
\tilde{\bm{\omega}}
such that
\tilde{\bm{Z}}^{T}[\tilde{{\bf m}}(\tilde{\bm{\omega}})-(gp)^{-1}\tilde{\bm{Z}}\tilde{\bm{\omega}}]=\tilde{\bm{Z}}^{T}\hat{\bm{\pi}}
, where
\tilde{m}_{j}(\tilde{\bm{\omega}})=1/(1+e^{-\tilde{z}_{j}^{T}\tilde{\bm{\omega}}})
.
We first show how to obtain
\tilde{\bm{\omega}}
, and subsequently that one can find
\bm{\omega}
from
\tilde{\bm{\omega}}
.

To obtain
\tilde{\bm{\omega}}
, intuitively this is simple because the subset of unique rows in
\tilde{\bm{Z}}
is the identity matrix.
Specifically, the
j^{th}
equation in
\tilde{\bm{Z}}^{T}[\tilde{{\bf m}}(\tilde{\bm{\omega}})-(gp)^{-1}\tilde{\bm{Z}}\tilde{\bm{\omega}}]=\tilde{\bm{Z}}^{T}\hat{\bm{\pi}}
is

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mbox{I}(\tilde{z}_{ij}=1)\left[\frac{1}{1+e^{-\tilde{\bm{z}}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\bm{\omega}}}}+\frac{\tilde{\bm{z}}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\bm{\omega}}}{gp}\right]=\tilde{\bm{z}}_{.j}^{T}\hat{\bm{\pi}}\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+e^{-\tilde{\omega}_{j}}}+\frac{\tilde{w}_{j}}{gp}=\frac{\tilde{\bm{z}}_{j}^{T}\hat{\bm{\pi}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mbox{I}(\tilde{z}_{ij}=1)}=\frac{\tilde{\bm{z}}_{.j}^{T}\hat{\bm{\pi}}}{\tilde{\bm{z}}_{.j}^{T}\mathbf{1}},

where
\tilde{\bm{z}}_{.j}
is the
j^{th}
column in
\bm{Z}
,
and we used that
\tilde{\bm{z}}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\bm{\omega}}=\tilde{\omega}_{j}
, since
\tilde{\bm{z}}_{il}=1
for
l=j
and
=0
otherwise.
We hence have that
\tilde{\omega}_{j}=h(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{.j}^{T}\hat{\bm{\pi}}/\tilde{\bm{z}}_{.j}^{T}\mathbf{1},gp)
,
where
h(a,c)
denotes the solution (in terms of
w
) to
1/(1+e^{-w})+w/c=a
.
For the case
c=\infty
,
h(a,\infty)=\log(a/(1-a))
.
More generally, the solution to this univariate equation can be easily found via Newton-Raphson.

To complete the proof, define
\hat{\bm{\omega}}=\bm{U}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{\omega}}
,
so that
\tilde{\bm{Z}}\tilde{\bm{\omega}}=\bm{Z}\bm{U}^{-1}\bm{U}\hat{\bm{\omega}}=\bm{Z}\hat{\bm{\omega}}
,
and therefore
{\bf m}(\hat{\bm{\omega}})=\tilde{{\bf m}}(\tilde{\bm{\omega}})
.
By definition of
\tilde{\bm{\omega}}
we have that

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{Z}}^{T}[\tilde{{\bf m}}(\tilde{\bm{\omega}})-\frac{\tilde{\bm{Z}}\tilde{\bm{\omega}}}{gp}]=\tilde{\bm{Z}}^{T}\hat{\bm{\pi}}\Rightarrow(\bm{U}^{-1})^{T}[\bm{Z}^{T}{\bf m}(\hat{\bm{\omega}})-\frac{\bm{Z}\hat{\bm{\omega}}}{gp}]=(\bm{U}^{-1})^{T}\bm{Z}^{T}\hat{\bm{\pi}}

which implies that
\bm{Z}^{T}[{\bf m}(\hat{\bm{\omega}})-(gp)^{-1}\bm{Z}\hat{\bm{\omega}}]=\bm{Z}^{T}\hat{\bm{\pi}}
, as we wished to prove.

Lemma 6

\int_{0}^{1}P\left(W>c\log\left(\frac{h}{1/u-1}\right)\right)du\;\leq\;2a+\int_{a}^{1-a}P\left(W>c\log\left(\frac{h}{1/u-1}\right)\right)du.

Take

for some
\psi\in(\alpha,1)
. By Lemma
6

\underline{u}=a

\bar{u}=1-a

\int_{0}^{1}P\left(W>c\log\left(\frac{h}{1/u-1}\right)\right)du\;\leq\;\frac{2}{1+h^{\psi}e^{-(\eta+\mu)/c}}+\frac{1-2a+\log(h^{\psi}e^{-(\eta+\mu)/c})}{h^{\psi}}.

12.1.2.
Bound on individual expected posterior model probability

Lemma 8
.

For any
\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}\subseteq\bm{\gamma}^{*}
and
\bm{\gamma}\neq\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}
, denote
A_{\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}}:=\nu\Delta(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})+\tfrac{1-\nu}{6}\mu(\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}),\bm{\gamma})
(

(
7

\nu\in(1/2,1]

A_{\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}}>0
, and
|\bm{\gamma}\setminus\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}|=o(A_{\bm{\gamma}})
. For any
\psi\in(0,1)
and every
n
large enough,

E\left(\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\right)\;\;\leq\;\;e^{-\psi A_{\bm{\gamma}}}.

Proof.

For any
\bm{\gamma}\neq\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}
, since
\sum_{\bm{\gamma}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})=1
, we have the following bound on the posterior probability

(20)

\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})=\frac{\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})}{\sum_{\delta}\pi(\delta\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})}=\bigg[1+\sum_{\delta:\delta\neq\bm{\gamma}}\frac{\pi(\delta\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})}{\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})}\bigg]^{-1}\leq\bigg[1+\frac{\pi(\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})}{\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})}\bigg]^{-1}

where the last inequality follows from
\sum_{\delta:\delta\notin\{\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}\}}\pi(\delta\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})/\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\geq 0
.
By (
20
) and using that for any random variable
Z\geq 0

E(Z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}P(Z>u)du
, we have that

E\left[\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\right]\leq\int_{0}^{1}P\left(\bigg[1+\frac{\pi(\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})}{\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})}\bigg]^{-1}\geq u\right){\rm d}u.

and inverting and taking logarithm on both sides of the inequality within the probability in the right-hand side, we get

(21)

E\left[\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\right]\leq\int_{0}^{1}P\left(\log\frac{\pi(\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})}{\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})}\leq\log\Big(\frac{1}{u}-1\Big)\right){\rm d}u.

Simple algebra shows that

(22)

\frac{\pi(\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})}{\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})}=\exp\left\{-\frac{gn(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}\bm{X}_{\bm{\gamma}}\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\gamma}}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}}^{T}\bm{X}_{\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}}^{T}\bm{X}_{\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}}\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}})}{2\phi(1+gn)}\right\}\prod_{b=1}^{B}\left(\frac{\omega_{b}}{(1+gn)^{1/2}(1-\omega_{b})}\right)^{p_{\tilde{\bm{\gamma}},b}-p_{\bm{\gamma},b}}

Inputting (
22
) in (
21
), and using the notation in (
6
) and (
7
), we have

\displaystyle E\left[\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}P\left(\frac{gnL(\tilde{\bm{\gamma}},\bm{\gamma})}{2(1+gn)}+\Delta(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})\leq\log{(\tfrac{1}{u}-1)}\right){\rm d}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}P\left(-\frac{gnL(\tilde{\bm{\gamma}},\bm{\gamma})}{2(1+gn)}-\Big(\Delta(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})-\log{(\tfrac{1}{u}-1)}\Big)\geq 0\right){\rm d}u.

The second step of the proof is to use the union bound to upper bound the probability in the integrand above.
By adding and resting
\phi^{-1}\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})}^{T}\bm{X}_{\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})}^{T}\bm{X}_{\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})}\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})}
to

L(\tilde{\bm{\gamma}},\bm{\gamma})=L(\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}),\bm{\gamma})-L(\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}),\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}).

For any
\nu\in\mathrm{R}
,

\displaystyle-\frac{gnL(\tilde{\bm{\gamma}},\bm{\gamma})}{2(1+gn)}-\Big(\Delta(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})-\log{(\tfrac{1}{u}-1)}\Big)\;=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{gnL(\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}),\bm{\gamma})}{2(1+gn)}-\log{\Bigl(\frac{e^{\nu\Delta(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})}}{\tfrac{1}{u}-1}\Bigl)}\right)

\displaystyle-\Bigl(\frac{gnL(\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}),\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})}{2(1+gn)}+(1-\nu)\Delta(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})\Bigl).

Observe that for any random variables
U
,
V
, and any
\epsilon,\nu^{\prime}\geq 0
, the event
\{U-V\geq 0\}
implies
\{U\geq\nu^{\prime}\epsilon\}\cup\{V<\nu^{\prime}\epsilon\}
. Let

. Take

and
\nu^{\prime}=\tfrac{1}{6}({1-\nu})

. We then have

\displaystyle\{U\geq\nu^{\prime}\epsilon\}\;

\displaystyle=\;\left\{\frac{gnL(\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}),\bm{\gamma})}{2(1+gn)}\geq\log{\Bigl(\frac{e^{\nu\Delta(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})}}{\tfrac{1}{u}-1}}\Bigl)+\nu^{\prime}\mu(\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}),\bm{\gamma})\right\}

\displaystyle=\;\left\{\frac{gnL(\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}),\bm{\gamma})}{2(1+gn)}\geq\log{\Bigl(\frac{e^{A_{\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}}}}{\tfrac{1}{u}-1}}\Bigl)\right\}

\displaystyle\{V<\nu^{\prime}\epsilon\}\;

\displaystyle+P\Bigl(\frac{gnL(\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}),\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})}{2(1+gn)}<\nu^{\prime}(\mu(\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}),\bm{\gamma})-6\Delta(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}))\Bigl).

The third and final step of the proof is to upper bound each of the terms in the right-hand side of (
23
). The intuition is that both
\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}
and
\bm{\gamma}
are nested within
\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})

\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})=\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}

\bm{\gamma}\not\subset\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}

1

23

\bm{\gamma}\supset\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}

\displaystyle P\left(\frac{gnL(\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}),\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})}{2(1+gn)}<\nu^{\prime}\mu(\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\gamma},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}),\bm{\gamma})\right)

(25)

as we wished to prove.

, we have the decomposition

(43)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{b}}\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})=\frac{\sum_{z_{j}=b}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}|j\in\bm{\gamma}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})}{p_{b}}=A_{b}\,+\,C_{b}.

To show the lower bound , we decompose
A_{b}

(44)

\displaystyle A_{b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\sum_{z_{j}=b}\frac{I(j\in\bm{\gamma}_{b})}{p_{b}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\sum_{j|z_{j}=b,j\in S^{L}_{b}(\kappa)}\frac{I(j\in\bm{\gamma}_{b})}{p_{b}}+\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\sum_{j|z_{j}=b,j\not\in S^{L}_{b}(\kappa)}\frac{I(j\in\bm{\gamma}_{b})}{p_{b}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa)|}{p_{b}}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})+\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\sum_{j|z_{j}=b,j\not\in S^{L}_{b}(\kappa)}\frac{I(j\in\bm{\gamma}_{b})}{p_{b}}.

where the last equality follows from
I(j\in\bm{\gamma}_{b})=1
for all
j\in S^{L}(\kappa)
when
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)
. The righmost term above and
C_{b}
are nonnegative, then by the linearity of the expectation

E\bigg(\frac{1}{p_{b}}\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\bigg)\geq\frac{|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa)|}{p_{b}}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}E(\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})).

By Lemma
9
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}E(\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}))=1
. It follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}E\bigg(\frac{1}{p_{b}}\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\bigg)\geq\frac{|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa)|}{p_{b}}\quad\text{ for every }j=1,\ldots,b.

We now prove the upper bound. Recall that
\mathcal{T}(\kappa)
by definition includes models that have no small signals, i.e. all parameters are in
S^{L}(\kappa)\cup S^{I}(\kappa)
. That is,
for all
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)
, we have that
I(j\in\bm{\gamma}_{b})=0
for all
j\in\{j|z_{j}=b,j\not\in(S^{L}_{b}(\kappa)\cup S^{I}_{b}(\kappa))\}
. Hence,
A_{b}
in (
44
) satisfies

\displaystyle A_{b}

\displaystyle=\frac{|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa)|}{p_{b}}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})+\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\sum_{j\in S^{I}_{b}(\kappa)}\frac{I(j\in\bm{\gamma}_{b})}{p_{b}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa)|}{p_{b}}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})+\frac{|S^{I}(\kappa)_{b}|}{p_{b}}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})

where the inequality follows from
\sum_{j\in S^{I}_{b}(\kappa)}I(j\in\bm{\gamma}_{b})\leq|S_{b}^{I}(\kappa)|
for all
\bm{\gamma}
. By (
43
), we then have

(45)

\frac{1}{p_{b}}\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\leq\frac{|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa)|+|S^{I}(\kappa)_{b}|}{p_{b}}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})+C_{b}.

Moreover, for every
j=1,\ldots,b
,
C_{b}
satisfies

(46)

C_{b}=\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\not\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\sum_{z_{j}=b}\frac{I(j\in\bm{\gamma}_{b})}{p_{b}}\leq\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\not\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})

where the inequality follows from
\sum_{z_{j}=b}\frac{I(j\in\bm{\gamma}_{b})}{p_{b}}\leq 1
for all
\bm{\gamma}
.
Taking expectations in (
45
) and (
46
) gives

E\bigg(\frac{1}{p_{b}}\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\bigg)\leq\frac{|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa)|+|S^{I}(\kappa)_{b}|}{p_{b}}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}E(\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}))+\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\not\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}E(\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})).

By Lemma
9
, we have on one hand
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}E(\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}))=1
and, on the other hand,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\not\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa)}E(\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}))=0
. It follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}E\bigg(\frac{1}{p_{b}}\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\bigg)\leq\frac{|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa)|+|S^{I}(\kappa)_{b}|}{p_{b}}=\frac{s_{b}-|S^{S}_{b}(\kappa)|}{p_{b}}\quad\text{for every }j=1,\ldots,b,

which proves the upper bound.
∎

12.2.
Proof of Theorem
1

Theorem
1
is a particular case of Lemma
9
. Observe that under assumption A3,
S^{L}(\kappa)=\bm{\gamma}^{*}
and
S^{S}(\kappa)=S^{I}(\kappa)=\emptyset
. It follows that
\mathcal{T}(\kappa)=\bm{\gamma}^{*}
. Assumptions A1 and A2 are also assumed to hold and, trivially,
|S^{I}(\kappa)|=O(1)
and
|S^{S}_{b}(\kappa)|=O(p_{b}-s_{b})
for every
b=1,\ldots,B
, then by Lemma
9
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\neq\bm{\gamma}^{*}}E\left(\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega})\right)=0
.

12.3.
Proof of Theorem
2

The proof strategy is to show that Assumptions A1, A2 and A3 hold to apply Theorem
1
. Recall that Theorem
2
makes Assumptions A1, A4, A5 and A6, hence it suffices to show that A2 and A3 hold.
We first derive a convenient decomposition of the empirical Bayes penalties
\kappa^{(1)}_{b}
. The second step of the proof is to show that, with probability going to 1, these
\kappa^{(1)}_{b}
satisfy Assumption A2. The third step consists in showing that Assumption A5 implies Assumption A3 for
\kappa^{(1)}_{b}
. The convergence of
E\left(\pi(\bm{\gamma}^{*}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(1)})\right)
then follows Theorem
1
.

In Step 1, from the choice
\omega^{(0)}
and the ensuing penalty
\kappa^{(0)}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{b}}\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{p_{b}}\sum_{z_{j}=b}\,\,\sum_{\bm{\gamma}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})I(j\in\bm{\gamma})

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{p_{b}}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})\sum_{z_{j}=b}I(j\in\bm{\gamma})

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{s_{b}}{p_{b}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}^{*}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})+\sum_{\bm{\gamma}|\bm{\gamma}\neq\bm{\gamma}^{*}}\frac{|\bm{\gamma}_{b}|}{p_{b}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)}).

Using that
\pi(\bm{\gamma}^{*}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})=1-\sum_{\bm{\gamma}|\bm{\gamma}\neq\bm{\gamma}^{*}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})
, we get

\frac{1}{p_{b}}\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})=\frac{s_{b}}{p_{b}}+\sum_{\bm{\gamma}|\bm{\gamma}\neq\bm{\gamma}^{*}}\frac{|\bm{\gamma}_{b}|-s_{b}}{p_{b}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)}).

Consider the decomposition of the sum in the right-hand side above between the sum over models
\bm{\gamma}
that contain more parameters than
\bm{\gamma}^{*}
in block
b
and the sum over those that contain less parameters than
\bm{\gamma}^{*}
in block
b
. Denote

O_{b}^{(0)}:=\sum_{\bm{\gamma}||\bm{\gamma}_{b}|>s_{b}}\frac{|\bm{\gamma}_{b}|-s_{b}}{p_{b}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})\quad\text{and}\quad U_{b}^{(0)}:=\sum_{\bm{\gamma}||\bm{\gamma}_{b}|<s_{b}}\frac{s_{b}-|\bm{\gamma}_{b}|}{p_{b}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)}).

We have

(47)

\frac{1}{p_{b}}\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})=\frac{s_{b}}{p_{b}}\,\,+\,\,O_{b}^{(0)}-\,\,U_{b}^{(0)}.

Observe that we have the following decomposition of Step 2 penalties

\kappa^{(1)}_{b}=\log(p_{b}-s_{b})+\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{1+gn}}{s_{b}}\Big)+\log\Bigg(\frac{p_{b}-\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})}{p_{b}-s_{b}}\Bigg)+\log\Bigg(\frac{s_{b}}{\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})}\Bigg).

By (
47
), it follows that

(48)

\kappa^{(1)}_{b}=\log(p_{b}-s_{b})+\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{1+gn}}{s_{b}}\Big)+\log\Bigg(1-\frac{p_{b}(O^{(0)}_{b}-U^{(0)}_{b})}{p_{b}-s_{b}}\Bigg)+\log\Bigg(\frac{s_{b}}{\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})}\Bigg),

completing the first step of the proof.

We continue with the second step of the proof: showing that the
\kappa^{(1)}_{b}
’s satisfy Assumption A2 with probability going to 1. Recall that Assumption A2 states that there exists
f_{b}\to\infty
(as
n\to\infty
) such that for every sufficiently large
n
,

\kappa_{b}\;=\;\log(p_{b}-s_{b})+f_{b}.

Since
U_{b}^{(0)}
is nonnegative, a lower bound on
\kappa^{(1)}_{b}
is

(49)

\kappa^{(1)}_{b}\geq\log(p_{b}-s_{b})+\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{1+gn}}{s_{b}}\Big)+\log\Bigg(1-\frac{p_{b}O^{(0)}_{b}}{p_{b}-s_{b}}\Bigg)+\log\Bigg(\frac{s_{b}}{\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})}\Bigg).

Plugging in the definition of
O^{(0)}_{b}
, we have that

\frac{p_{b}O^{(0)}_{b}}{p_{b}-s_{b}}=\sum_{\bm{\gamma}||\bm{\gamma}_{b}|>s_{b}}\frac{|\bm{\gamma}_{b}|-s_{b}}{p_{b}-s_{b}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})\leq\sum_{\bm{\gamma}||\bm{\gamma}_{b}|>s_{b}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})

where the inequality follows from
(|\bm{\gamma}_{b}|-s_{b})/(p_{b}-s_{b})\leq 1
for all
\bm{\gamma}
.
Note that if
\bm{\gamma}
is such that
|\bm{\gamma}_{b}|>s_{b}
, then
\bm{\gamma}\not\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})
(this follows immediately from the definition of
\mathcal{T}(\kappa)
in (
9
)) and therefore
\sum_{\bm{\gamma}||\bm{\gamma}_{b}|>s_{b}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})\leq\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\not\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})
. Moreover,
\kappa^{(0)}
satisfies Assumption A2 and the assumptions of Lemma
9
are met for
\kappa^{(0)}
.
Then, by Lemma
9
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\not\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})}E(\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)}))=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}||\bm{\gamma}_{b}|>s_{b}}E(\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)}))=0
. It follows that
\frac{p_{b}O^{(0)}_{b}}{p_{b}-s_{b}}
vanishes in probability and so does
\log\Big(1-\frac{p_{b}O^{(0)}_{b}}{p_{b}-s_{b}}\Big)
. By Assumption A4, we also have that
\log(\sqrt{1+gn}s_{b}^{-1})\to\infty
.
Then, to show that Assumption A2 holds with probability going to 1, it is enough to show that with probability going to 1,
\log(s_{b}/\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)}))
is nonnegative. Observe that all assumptions in Lemma
10
are also met for
\kappa^{(0)}
. By (
45
) and (
46
) in the proof of Lemma
10
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})}{p_{b}}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa^{(0)})|+|S^{I}(\kappa^{(0)})_{b}|}{p_{b}}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{p_{b}}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\not\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)}).

By Lemma
9
,
\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})
and
\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\not\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})
converge in probability to 1 and 0 respectively. We then have that, with probability going to 1,

\frac{\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})}{p_{b}}\leq\frac{|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa^{(0)})|+|S^{I}(\kappa^{(0)})_{b}|}{p_{b}},

which implies, with probability going to 1,

\log\Big(\frac{s_{b}}{\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})}\Big)\geq\log\bigg(\frac{s_{b}}{|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa^{(0)})|+|S^{I}(\kappa^{(0)})_{b}|}\bigg)\geq 0.

We then obtain that, with probability going to 1,

(50)

\kappa^{(1)}_{b}\geq\log(p_{b}-s_{b})+\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{1+gn}}{s_{b}}\Big)

and that the
\kappa^{(1)}_{b}
satisfy Assumption A2, completing the second part of the proof.

For the third and final part of the proof,
we now show that Assumption A5 implies Assumption A3 for the
\kappa_{b}^{EB}
with probability going to 1. Recall that Assumption A3 for the
\kappa_{b}^{EB}
states that for each block
b
there exists
g_{b}\to\infty
such that for large enough
n
,

\sqrt{\frac{(1-\nu)n\phi^{-1}\rho(\bm{X})}{6}}{{\theta_{\min,b}^{*}}}\,-\,\sqrt{\kappa^{(1)}_{b}}\;=\;\sqrt{\log(s_{b})}+g_{b}.

where
\nu
takes value

(51)

\nu=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+\max_{b}\frac{\log(p_{b}-s_{b})}{\kappa^{(1)}_{b}}\Big).

Observe that Assumption A5 and Assumption A3 take the same form. To show that Assumption A5 implies Assumption A3 for the
\kappa_{b}^{EB}
with probability going to 1, it suffices to show that the following two inequalities

(52)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{(1-\nu)n\phi^{-1}\rho(\bm{X})}{6}}{\theta_{\min,b}^{*}}\,

\displaystyle\geq\,\sqrt{\frac{(1-\psi)n\phi^{-1}\rho(\bm{X})}{6}}{\theta_{\min,b}^{*}},

(53)

\displaystyle-\,\sqrt{\kappa^{(1)}_{b}}\,

\displaystyle\geq\,-\sqrt{\log\bigg(\frac{p}{|S^{L}(\kappa^{(0)})|}-1\bigg)+\frac{1}{2}\log(1+gn)}

hold with probability going to 1 for
\nu
as in (
51
) and
\psi=\frac{1}{2}\big(1+\max_{b}\frac{\log(p_{b}-s_{b})}{\log(p_{b}/s_{b}-1)+0.5\log(1+gn)}\big)
(defined in Assumption A5). We first show (
52
) holds with probability going to 1 and then that (
53
) does too.

By (
50
), with probability going to 1,

\frac{\log(p_{b}-s_{b})}{\kappa^{(1)}_{b}}\leq\frac{\log(p_{b}-s_{b})}{\log(p_{b}-s_{b})+\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{1+gn}}{s_{b}}\Big)}=\frac{\log(p_{b}-s_{b})}{\log(p_{b}/s_{b}-1)+0.5\log(1+gn)}.

It follows that:

\nu=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+\max_{b=1,\ldots,b}\frac{\log(p_{b}-s_{b})}{\kappa^{(1)}_{b}}\Big)\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+\max_{b}\frac{\log(p_{b}-s_{b})}{\log(p_{b}/s_{b}-1)+0.5\log(1+gn)}\Big)=\psi

and (
52
) holds with probability going to 1.

We now upper bound
\kappa^{(1)}_{b}
to show (
53
) holds with probability going to 1. Observe that

\log\Big(\frac{\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})}{s_{b}}\Big)=\log\left(1+\frac{\sum_{z_{j}=b}\pi(\bm{\gamma}_{j}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})-s_{b}}{s_{b}}\right)=\log\Big(1+\frac{p_{b}(O^{(0)}_{b}-U^{(0)}_{b})}{s_{b}}\Big).

where the second equality follows from (
47
).
Plugging this expression into (
48
), and using that
O_{b}^{(0)}\geq 0
, we have that

(54)

\kappa^{(1)}_{b}\leq\log\big(p_{b}-s_{b}\big)+\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{1+gn}}{s_{b}}\Big)+\log\bigg(\frac{1+\frac{p_{b}}{p_{b}-s_{b}}U^{(0)}_{b}}{1-\frac{p_{b}}{s_{b}}U^{(0)}_{b}}\bigg).

We split the sum in
U^{(0)}_{b}
between models in
\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})
and those not in
\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})
.

U_{b}^{(0)}=\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})||\bm{\gamma}_{b}|<s_{b}}\frac{s_{b}-|\bm{\gamma}_{b}|}{p_{b}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})+\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\not\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})||\bm{\gamma}_{b}|<s_{b}}\frac{s_{b}-|\bm{\gamma}_{b}|}{p_{b}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)}).

If
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})
, then by definition
|\bm{\gamma}_{b}|\geq|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa^{(0)})|
and thus
s_{b}-|\bm{\gamma}_{b}|\leq s_{b}-|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa^{(0)})|
. A bound on
s_{b}-|\bm{\gamma}_{b}|
for
\bm{\gamma}\not\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})
is simply
s_{b}-|\bm{\gamma}_{b}|\leq s_{b}
. It follows that

\displaystyle U^{(0)}_{b}\leq\frac{s_{b}-|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa^{(0)})|}{p_{b}}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})||\bm{\gamma}_{b}|<s_{b}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})+\frac{s_{b}}{p_{b}}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\not\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})||\bm{\gamma}_{b}|<s_{b}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})

By Lemma
9
,
\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})||\bm{\gamma}_{b}|<s_{b}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})
and
\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\not\in\mathcal{T}(\kappa^{(0)})||\bm{\gamma}_{b}|<s_{b}}\pi(\bm{\gamma}\mid\bm{y},\bm{\omega}^{(0)})
converge in probability to 1 and 0 respectively. We then get that, with probability going to 1,

\displaystyle\frac{p_{b}}{p_{b}-s_{b}}U^{(0)}_{b}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{s_{b}-|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa^{(0)})|}{p_{b}-s_{b}}

(55)

\displaystyle\frac{p_{b}}{s_{b}}U^{(0)}_{b}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{s_{b}-|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa^{(0)})|}{s_{b}}.

By the bounds above and (
54
), we have that with probability going to 1,

\displaystyle\kappa^{(1)}_{b}

\displaystyle\leq\log\big(p_{b}-s_{b}\big)+\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{1+gn}}{s_{b}}\Big)+\log\Bigg(\frac{1+\frac{s_{b}-|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa^{(0)})|}{p_{b}-s_{b}}}{1-\frac{s_{b}-|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa^{(0)})|}{s_{b}}}\Bigg)

(56)

\displaystyle=\log\big(p_{b}-s_{b}\big)+\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{1+gn}}{s_{b}}\Big)+\log\Bigg(\frac{\frac{p_{b}-|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa^{(0)})|}{p_{b}-s_{b}}}{\frac{|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa^{(0)})|}{s_{b}}}\Bigg)

(57)

\displaystyle=\log\big(p_{b}/|S^{L}_{b}(\kappa^{(0)})|-1\big)+\frac{1}{2}\log(1+gn)

which shows (
53
) holds with probability going to 1 and that
Assumption A5 implies Assumption A3 holds for the
\kappa^{(1)}_{b}
with probability going to 1.

Since Assumptions A2 and A3 hold with probability going to 1, by Theorem
1
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}E\left[\pi(\bm{\gamma}^{*}\mid\bm{y},\hat{\bm{\omega}}^{(1)})\right]=1
, as we wished to prove.
