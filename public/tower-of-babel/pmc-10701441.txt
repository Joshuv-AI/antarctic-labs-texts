Beyond ENO1, emerging roles and targeting strategies of other enolases in cancers
Authors: Jiaojiao Ni, Yihui Huang, Chaoqun Li, Qian Yin, Jieer Ying
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Aerobic glycolysis is a hallmark property of cancer metabolism. Enolase is a glycolytic enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of 2-phosphoglycerate into phosphoenolpyruvate. In mammals, enolases exist in three isoforms, encoded by the genes ENO1, ENO2, and ENO3. The altered expression of enolases is a common occurrence in various types of cancer. Although most published studies on enolases have predominantly focused on the role of ENO1 in cancer, ENO2 and ENO3 have recently emerged as crucial regulatory molecules in cancer development. Significant progress has been made in understanding their multifaceted roles in oncogenesis. In this comprehensive review, we provide an overview of the structure, subcellular localization, diagnostic and prognostic significance, biological functions, and molecular mechanisms of ENO2 and ENO3 in cancer progression. The importance of enolase in cancer development makes it a novel therapeutic target for clinical applications. Furthermore, we discuss anticancer agents designed to target enolases and summarize their anticancer efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo studies.

Introduction

Although the tricarboxylic acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation generate more ATP, cancer cells tend to use the less efficient process of glycolysis. This metabolic strategy results in the abundant production of pyruvate and lactate, even in the presence of abundant oxygen, a phenomenon commonly recognized as aerobic glycolysis or the Warburg effect. Extensive research has explored the roles of glycolysis-related genes in cancer progression and has yielded promising targeted agents. These genes include lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), glucose transporter (GLUT), pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), and enolases (ENOs).

Enolase is a glycolytic metalloenzyme responsible for catalyzing the interconversion of 2-phosphoglycerate into phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), a crucial step in the ninth step of glycolysis, and enolase has recently been a research focus.1 This enzymatic reaction is reversible and influenced by the concentration of available substrates in the environment.2,3 Enolases are highly conserved among species, and three isotypes have been identified in mammals: α-enolase (ENO1), β-enolase (ENO3), and γ-enolase (ENO2). All three isotypes form homodimers, with two magnesium ions noncovalently bound to the active site.

Enolase overexpression has been observed in various cancers and is frequently associated with an unfavorable prognosis. Beyond their established role in glycolysis, enolases exhibit diverse biological functions in cancer development (Figure 1). As moonlighting proteins, enolases exert nonglycolytic functions, including gene regulation, protein synthesis, and the activation of cascade signaling pathways.4 Moreover, enolases play pivotal roles in critical processes such as the immune response, host‒pathogen interactions, chemotherapy resistance, and the cellular stress response. As the most extensively studied isoform, the functions and mechanisms of ENO1 in cancer development have been well characterized and reviewed in previous studies.4,6,7 Although there have been limited investigations into the roles of ENO2 and ENO3 in tumors, research has progressed rapidly in recent years. Therefore, we have compiled a comprehensive review that encompasses the structure, subcellular localization, diagnostic and prognostic significance, biological functions, and molecular mechanisms of ENO2 and ENO3 in cancer progression. In addition, we have investigated novel anticancer agents targeting enolases and have provided an overview of their anticancer efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo settings.

Structure and subcellular localization of enolases

Active enolases are composed of dimers formed by noncovalently linked α, β, or γ subunits, each encoded by distinct genes. α-Enolase, encoded by the ENO1 gene, is widely expressed in most tissues. β-Enolase, encoded by the ENO3 gene, is primarily expressed in muscle tissues. γ-Enolase, also known as neuron-specific enolase (NSE), is encoded by the ENO2 gene and is localized mainly in the cytoplasm of neurons and neuroendocrine cells. The latter are found in various tissues, including the lung, pancreas, gastrointestinal tract, and thyroid gland.8,9 In addition, the catalytic activity of enolases relies on the presence of Mg2+, which serves as a natural cofactor.10

The three enolase isoforms share similar structural features while retaining their distinct characteristics. Each enolase subunit consists of two domains: a smaller N-terminal domain, which extends from amino acid 1–134, and a larger C-terminal domain encompassing residues 143–434.11 The expression of enolase undergoes dynamic changes during developmental, physiological, and pathological conditions. Enolase is primarily located in the cytoplasm of cells, where it promotes glycolysis. Moreover, enolases have also been observed in the nucleus, cell membrane, and extracellular space, indicating diverse cellular functions beyond glycolysis.

α-Enolase serves as a plasminogen receptor on the membrane surface of various cells, such as neurons and monocytes.12,13 Furthermore, α-enolase binds to and activates plasminogen through the protease degradation pathway, subsequently regulating various biological processes, including pathogen invasion, inflammation, and tumor metastasis.14 In addition, α-enolase can be localized in the nucleus, where it plays a crucial role as an alternative translation product for c-Myc binding protein 1.15 β-Enolase is usually located in the bare regions of actin thin filaments, known as the I band of the sarcomere, to supply the ATP required for muscle contraction.16 Moreover, β-enolase can translocate to a wide range of subcellular sites to meet specific functional demands.

NSE also exhibits diverse subcellular localizations under different physiological and pathological conditions. Scientists investigated the subcellular distribution of NSE in primary and metastatic cerebral tumor cells using immunoelectron microscopy.17 The results revealed the presence of NSE in the cytoplasm and on cell membranes. Concerning the mechanism, the C-terminal peptide of NSE plays a pivotal role in subcellular distribution.18 Specifically, γ1-syntrophin, a brain-specific scaffold protein, binds the C-terminal end of NSE through its PDZ domain, facilitating its translocation to the cell membrane. This interaction has been confirmed by immunoprecipitation, surface plasmon resonance, and fluorescence colocalization studies.18

NSE was found to be expressed in the nucleus of breast epithelial and urothelial cells when exposed to Cd2+ and As+, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for chronic environmental ion exposure.19,20 Interestingly, low levels of NSE expression appeared to favor more nuclear localization, which needs further investigation.20 In addition, intracellular NSE is released into the blood or cerebrospinal fluid when neuronal injury occurs, making it a biomarker for assessing brain damage. The levels of NSE detected in body fluids can predict the prognosis of neurological disorders, including ischemic stroke, cerebral hemorrhage, and seizures.21,22

Role of ENO2 in cancer

Nervous system tumors

NSE has been established as a serum marker for tumors of neuronal lineage, such as glioblastoma (GBM), astrocytoma, medulloblastoma, and ependymoma.23,24,25 For instance, NSE expression levels in neuroblastoma patients showed a positive correlation with tumor stages and were increased significantly when recurrence occurred.26 Likewise, NSE could serve as a marker for monitoring relapse and predicting chemotherapy response in a patient with recurrent medulloblastoma.25

Among nervous system tumors, ENO2 has been most extensively studied in glioma. Compared with normal tissues, GBM tissues exhibited upregulated ENO2 expression, which correlated with a poor prognosis.27 ENO2 was also upregulated in GBM cells under conditions of cellular stress, such as serum starvation and hypoxia. Knockdown of ENO2 using small interfering RNA has been shown to suppress the migration of GBM cells and sensitize them to hypoxia, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy.27 In another study, researchers implanted GBM cells harboring ENO2 or scrambled small hairpin RNA into the right frontal cortex of NOD/SCID mice to establish intracranial GBM xenograft models. The results showed that ENO2 knockdown significantly extended mouse survival by 7.2%.28

Tumors can also delete or affect adjacent genes, known as passenger genes, when deleting tumor suppressor genes. This phenomenon is referred to as collateral lethality.29 The deletion of passenger genes may upregulate the expression of redundant isoforms, making it a promising target for therapeutic strategies. In the case of GBM, this phenomenon involves ENO1 and ENO2. Both ENO1 and ENO2 are expressed in GBM, with ENO1 being the predominant isoform.30 When homozygous deletion of ENO1 along with the chromosome 1p36 tumor suppressor locus occurs, it induces upregulation of ENO2 expression to compensate. Consequently, the selective knockdown of ENO2 effectively inhibits the growth and survival of ENO1-deleted GBM cells but not ENO1-intact cells, providing an effective treatment strategy for ENO1-deleted GBM.30

Lung cancers

Small cell lung cancer

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a deadly tumor subtype accounting for approximately 15% of all lung cancers. Elevated levels of NSE were observed in SCLC cell lines compared to cell lines derived from other types of lung cancers.31 NSE levels in the serum of SCLC patients were significantly higher than those in adenocarcinoma or squamous cell carcinoma patients.32 The diagnostic efficiency of NSE for SCLC ranges from 0.8554 to 0.894.33,34 Moreover, serum NSE levels are significantly correlated with tumor size, stage, and distant metastasis in SCLC patients.

In addition, serum NSE levels increased in SCLC at diagnosis, decreased after remission, and rebounded during relapse. These findings indicate that NSE can serve as a valuable biomarker for monitoring treatment and predicting relapse in SCLC patients.35,36 In terms of prognostic value, a meta-analysis showed that elevated NSE levels are associated with worse overall survival (OS) (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.78) and progression-free survival (PFS) (HR = 1.50).35 Moreover, elevated NSE levels both at baseline and during treatment were associated with a worse prognosis in advanced SCLC patients receiving chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy.36,37,38

Several studies have explored the biological functions and mechanisms of NSE in SCLC development. NSE positively regulates cell proliferation, drug resistance, spherical colony formation, tumor growth, and stem cell–like characteristics of SCLC cells.39 Further mechanistic experiments revealed that NSE downregulated NBL1 expression and activated the bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2)/Smad/ID1 pathway in SCLC cells.39 Another study revealed that NSE promoted migration, invasion, metastasis, and the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in SCLC cells by activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.40 In addition, NSE was found to be positively regulated by the LINC00657-miR-93-5p axis to promote SCLC invasion and EMT, revealing the upstream regulatory factors of NSE in SCLC.41

Non-SCLC

Non-SCLC (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 80%–85% of all lung cancers, with relatively low malignancy. Early diagnosis and surgical treatment of NSCLC markedly extend the survival time of patients. Although NSE is specifically overexpressed in SCLC with neuroendocrine features, NSE expression also holds clinical diagnostic value in NSCLC to some extent. A study involving 448 NSCLC patients found that increased NSE levels were present in 32% of the patients.42 Serum NSE levels in NSCLC patients were significantly higher than those in patients with benign lung diseases, with a diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of 66.67% and 78.69%, respectively.43

In terms of prognostic value, most studies have shown that high NSE expression is a poor prognostic factor in NSCLC patients. A prospective study of 621 NSCLC patients revealed that elevated NSE levels (>12.5 ng/mL) predicted poor outcomes (HR = 1.52).44 Similarly, another study involving 448 NSCLC patients revealed that the serum NSE level was an independent predictor of survival.45 A recent study analyzed 363 NSCLC patients and identified NSE as an independent prognostic factor for short PFS (HR = 1.81) and OS (HR = 1.76).46

Furthermore, a series of studies have documented the predictive value of NSE in NSCLC treatment. For instance, NSE expression levels were significantly associated with the objective response of NSCLC patients receiving standard chemotherapy.47 In targeted therapy, epidermal growth factor-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) are common drugs for NSCLC. One study observed significantly shorter PFS and OS in patients with high NSE levels treated with EGFR-TKIs.48 Similarly, another study revealed that negative NSE expression at baseline was significantly associated with improved PFS in EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients treated with EGFR-TKIs.49

The use of immunotherapy in NSCLC has made great progress in the past decade.50 However, conclusions regarding the correlation of serum NSE levels with patients’ immunotherapy response and clinical outcomes are controversial. One study revealed that NSE was insufficient to predict the efficacy of nivolumab in NSCLC patients.51 In another study, NSCLC patients whose NSE levels decreased after 6 weeks (HR = 0.619) or 12 weeks (HR = 0.578) of immunotherapy treatment appeared to have longer OS.45

Neuroendocrine neoplasms

Neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) represent a diverse group of malignant tumors and are broadly divided into two types: well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs). NSE is present mainly in the cytoplasm of neuroendocrine cells beyond neurons.52 NSE serves as a valuable biomarker for identifying neuroendocrine differentiation in various cancers.53,54 In two cohorts with a total of over 200 NEN patients, the sensitivity and specificity values of NSE were 39%–43% and 65%–73%, respectively, for distinguishing NENs from non-NENs.55,56 Next, we describe the specific roles of NSE in each type of NEN.

Gastroenteropancreatic (GEP) neuroendocrine neoplasms (GEP-NENs)

The incidence of gastroenteropancreatic (GEP) NENs has steadily increased in recent decades.57 GEP-NENs are distributed mainly in the small intestine and colon (approximately 80%), with the rest in the pancreas and stomach.58 NSE levels can be used to predict disease prognosis and treatment response in GEP-NEN patients. For high-grade GEP-NEN patients, one study revealed that NSE levels exceeding 21 ng/mL predicted poor OS (HR = 4.610).59 Similarly, in patients with GEP-NEC, high NSE levels were linked to worse median OS (HR = 2.77).60 In addition, extensive research has explored the role of NSE in specific GEP-NEN types.

In intestinal NETs, NSE staining is positive, but the correlation between NSE expression level and treatment response has rarely been reported.61,62 Pancreatic NETs (panNETs) account for approximately 1%–2% of all pancreatic tumors.63 A study from the Phase III RADIANT-3 study showed that elevated baseline NSE levels were correlated with shorter OS in panNET patients (HR = 0.36).64 In addition, high serum NSE levels were found to be associated with disease progression in patients with pancreas and small intestine NENs.65 Further multivariate Cox analysis indicated that NSE concentrations were an independent prognostic factor for PFS in NEN patients.65

Gastric NENs (g-NENs), although relatively rare, have increased in incidence over the past decade, partly due to improved diagnostic techniques and greater awareness of the disease.66,67 Similar to other NENs, g-NENs exhibit positive staining for neuroendocrine markers, including chromogranin A (CgA) and NSE. A cohort study revealed that the overall positive rate of NSE expression in g-NEN patients was 40%.68 g-NENs are conventionally classified into four main types with distinct pathological characteristics, and type I and IV tumor cells are positive for NSE.69 The specific role of NSE in each subtype of g-NENs needs further study.

Neuroendocrine prostate cancer

Neuroendocrine prostate cancer (NEPC) usually expresses neuroendocrine markers, including CgA, NSE, and synaptophysin, which distinguish it from adenocarcinoma.70,71 Neuroendocrine differentiation is associated with treatment failure and poor outcomes in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer.72 Elevated serum NSE levels have been reported in castration-resistant prostate cancer and are correlated with a poor prognosis, indicating the presence of neuroendocrine differentiation under androgen deprivation therapy.73,74 Moreover, analysis of the public dataset GSE59986 revealed that ENO2 began to increase at 3 weeks postcastration and was dramatically upregulated in relapsed and NEPC cells.75,76 In addition, the NEPC xenograft mouse model confirmed that ENO2 was strongly upregulated in LNCaP-derived NEPC xenografts compared to their adenocarcinoma counterparts, suggesting the important role of ENO2 in NEPC progression.76 However, the specific function and underlying mechanism of ENO2 in NEPC remain unclear and require more in-depth research.

Other NENs

NSE has also demonstrated its relevance in other rare NENs. In Merkel cell carcinoma (MCC), a primary NEN of the skin, NSE has been investigated as a serum biomarker.77 A study involving 48 MCC patients revealed a positive correlation between NSE levels and the extent of the disease.78 Moreover, NSE expression could serve as a marker of disease progression (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve = 0.89) and as a predictor of immunotherapy response in MCC.78 In another study involving 63 MCC patients, elevated NSE levels during the disease course were significantly correlated with recurrence risk and MCC-specific death.79 Small cell carcinoma of the urinary bladder (SCCB) is an extremely rare NEN with aggressive clinical features and a poor prognosis, accounting for only 0.3%–1.0% of all bladder cancers. Notably, the serum NSE value at diagnosis has been identified as an independent prognostic factor for SCCB (HR = 18.52).80

Other nonneuronal and nonneuroendocrine cancers

Positivity for ENO2 has also been observed in several nonneuronal and non-NETs, especially malignant tumors of the digestive system (Table 1). In colorectal cancer (CRC), the upregulation of ENO2 in tumor tissues was correlated with a poor prognosis in CRC patients. Further experiments revealed that ENO2 promoted the migration, invasion, and metastasis of CRC cells by activating the YAP1-induced EMT process.81 Interestingly, ENO2 was also upregulated in BRAF V600E-mutated CRC and predicted a poor prognosis.82 Further in vitro studies revealed that BRAF V600E-mutated CRC cells exhibited greater dependency on ENO2 compared to wild-type cells. The clinical significance, functions, and mechanisms of ENO2 in various tumors

In gastric cancer (GC), ENO2 was identified as a downstream target of the METTL3/hepatoma-derived growth factor (HDGF) axis, which promoted glycolysis in GC cells. High ENO2 expression in GC was correlated with a poor prognosis, as indicated by the Kaplan-Meier plotter data.83 In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), high ENO2 expression was also associated with a poor prognosis based on bioinformatics analysis.84,85 Notably, the expression levels of ENO2 exhibited a strong correlation with those of immune checkpoints and immune cell markers, suggesting its potential role in tumor immune infiltration.85 Further research is urgently needed to elucidate the function and molecular mechanism of ENO2 in GC and HCC.

Enzyme activity and cellular metabolism can be influenced by acetylation status. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), one study revealed a novel mechanism by which acetylation of the ENO2 protein inhibited its enzymatic activity.86 High ENO2 expression correlated with metastasis and a poor prognosis in PDAC patients. ENO2 promoted metastasis and glycolysis in PDAC cells. Moreover, ENO2 was deacetylated at the K394 site by HDAC3 in PDAC cells. Insulin growth factor 1 (IGF-1) decreased K394 acetylation and stimulated ENO2 activity, making IGF-1 inhibitors a promising strategy for preventing PDAC progression.86 Overall, ENO2 has significant diagnostic and prognostic value in various tumors, as shown in Table 1. However, its function and mechanism in cancer development remain elusive and need further experimental exploration.

Role of ENO3 in cancer

ENO3 as an oncogenic gene

Compared with ENO2, there are considerably fewer studies on the role of ENO3 in tumors. ENO3 is primarily expressed in skeletal muscle cells, where it may promote muscle development and regeneration. This suggests the potential significance of ENO3 in soft tissue sarcoma. The diagnostic value of ENO3 in tumors was first reported in rhabdomyosarcoma in 1984.103 ENO3 staining was positive in 86% of rhabdomyosarcomas but negative in neuroblastomas, fibrosarcomas, Wilms sarcomas, and osteosarcomas, underscoring its sensitivity as a marker for muscle differentiation in rhabdomyosarcoma.103

Multiple studies have reported that ENO3 is also distributed on various tissues beyond muscles, with most studies focusing on CRC. Several bioinformatics studies have explored the glycolysis-related gene signature in CRC and identified that ENO3 is upregulated in CRC, predicting a poor prognosis in patients.104,105 Regarding its function and mechanism, a recent study revealed that ENO3 promoted the proliferation and migration of CRC cells. Further RNA sequencing and pathway enrichment analysis revealed that ENO3 enhanced cell glycolysis, which was validated by detecting the levels of lactate and ATP.106

STK11 mutant lung cancer accounts for ?30% of lung adenocarcinomas (LUADs).107 STK11 is considered a tumor suppressor gene in various cancers, and its mutation usually leads to loss of function.108,109 STK11 mutation has been reported to directly upregulate the expression of ENO3 in LUAD. Knockdown of ENO3 inhibited cell proliferation and decreased viability in STK11-mutated lung cancer cells but not in STK11-wild-type lung cancer cells.110 This highlights the potential of ENO3 as a therapeutic target for LUAD patients harboring STK11 mutations.

ENO3 as a tumor suppressor gene

ENO3 has exhibited tumor-suppressive properties in other cancers. In PDAC, two bioinformatics studies have constructed prognostic signatures based on metabolic reprogramming and hypoxia-stemness.111,112 Subsequent results showed that ENO3 was downregulated in PDAC tissues compared to normal tissues. More important, low ENO3 expression was found to be related to a poor prognosis in PDAC patients.111,112 Similarly, a recent study constructed a prognostic risk model in NSCLC by integrating differentially expressed hypoxia-related genes.113 ENO3, as one core gene among the nine genes in this model, was associated with a better prognosis in NSCLC patients (HR = 0.885). Intriguingly, ENO3 was negatively correlated with most immune cell types, although this correlation requires verification by more experiments.113

Regarding HCC, ENO3 has emerged as a tumor suppressor. ENO3 was downregulated in HCC tissues compared to normal tissues, and low ENO3 expression was correlated with a poor prognosis in HCC patients.114,115 Mechanistically, ENO3 repressed the proliferation, migration, invasion, tumor growth, and lung metastasis abilities of HCC cells by inhibiting the EMT process and Wnt/β-catenin pathway.114

Enolase-targeted therapies

Considering the important role of enolases in tumors, therapeutic strategies targeting enolases have attracted widespread attention (Figure 2). Over the years, several ENO1 inhibitors have shown promising results in pharmacological studies and preclinical trials.4 Inhibitors targeting ENO2 are also being actively developed, with some demonstrating antitumor efficacy in preclinical studies. For ENO3, there is currently a lack of specific inhibitors in tumors. Targeted therapeutic strategies of enolase in various tumors

AP-III-a4

AP-III-a4, also known as ENOblock, was discovered during screening for small molecules that are toxic to tumor cells under hypoxic conditions.117 It is a nonenzymatic active site inhibitor that directly binds to enolases as a nonsubstrate analog.117 AP-III-a4 can induce glucose uptake and inhibit PEP carboxykinase expression in hepatocytes and kidney cells, suggesting its potential antidiabetic effects.118 As a pan inhibitor of enolases, AP-III-a4 has been widely used in elucidating the roles of enolases in cancer development, particularly ENO1. Overall, AP-III-a4 inhibits the survival, migration, and invasion abilities of cancer cells and induces cell apoptosis.101,119,120

Studies have also clarified the specific functions and mechanisms of AP-III-a4 in various cancers.117 In GC, AP-III-a4 inhibited the glycolysis levels and stemness of GC cells, increasing their sensitivity to cisplatin.119 AP-III-a4 also significantly induced remission in a xenograft mouse model of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Mechanistically, AP-III-a4 inhibits the ENO2-activated AKT and ERK pathways in HNSCC cells.101 In multiple myeloma (MM), the interaction between plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) and MM cells upregulated ENO1 in both cell types.120 ENO1 inhibition by AP-III-a4 activated pDCs and restored T and natural killer cell–mediated tumor cell killing. Previous studies have shown that pDC-MM interactions also upregulate PD-L1, providing a rationale for combined therapy. The combination of AP-III-a4 and anti-PD-L1 antibody enhanced T cell–mediated cancer cell lysis more effectively than either agent alone.120

Moreover, AP-III-a4 exhibits synergistic antitumor effects when combined with radiation therapy.121 ENO1 enhances radiotherapy resistance in breast cancer through modulating mitochondrial homeostasis to reduce the production of reactive oxygen species and inhibit cell apoptosis. AP-III-a4 restores the sensitivity of tumor cells to radiation therapy. Further results showed that the combination of AP-III-a4 and radiation produces a considerable synergy both in vitro and in vivo.121

Phosphonoacetohydroxamate and HEX

Phosphonoacetohydroxamate (PhAH) and HEX are two well-studied and commercially available inhibitors. PhAH is a potent enolase inhibitor that acts as a transition-state analog.122 PhAH is a pan-enolase inhibitor with slightly greater potency against ENO1 than against ENO2. Due to the collateral lethality principle,29 PhAH is selectively toxic to ENO1-deleted GBM cells instead of ENO1-intact cells.30

In recent years, HEX and its prodrug POMHEX have been rapidly developed.123 HEX is a substrate-competitive inhibitor and displays 4-fold greater specificity against ENO2 than against ENO1.123 POMHEX is a cell-permeable pivaloyloxymethyl (POM) prodrug of HEX with higher cell and blood‒brain barrier permeability. Both HEX and POMHEX have exhibited longer drug exposure in primate plasma with a considerable safety profile, indicating their potential for clinical application.123 Mechanistically, POMHEX interferes with the formation of anaplerotic pyruvate from glucose, leading to glycolysis inhibition and cytotoxicity.124 In terms of anticancer efficacy, POMHEX exhibited low-nanomolar potency against ENO1-deleted GBM cells in vitro and exerted dramatic antineoplastic effects in ENO1-deleted intracranial orthotopic tumors and subcutaneous xenografts.123,124 More prodrugs of HEX are being developed, and their anticancer efficacy has been examined, illustrating the potential for further clinical translation.125

Specific antibody and short peptide

Using antibodies against enolase represents a promising targeted treatment strategy. In vivo adoptive transfer of anti-ENO1 antibody in mice markedly inhibited the lung metastasis and bone metastasis of human A549 lung cancer cells.14 In addition to directly affecting tumor cells, ENO1-targeted antibodies also inhibit tumor progression by regulating the tumor microenvironment. For instance, HuL227, a novel anti-ENO1 antibody, reduced the growth of subcutaneous PC-3 xenografts. Beyond that, it also affected monocyte recruitment, suppressed the secretion of prometastatic cytokines, and hindered vascular endothelial growth factor–induced angiogenesis.126 Furthermore, short peptides with a high binding affinity have also attracted much attention. One such peptide, pHCT74 (SSMDIVLRAPLM), was found to specifically bind to ENO1. Lipid nanoparticles containing pHCT74 displayed robust anticancer effects in animal models of CRC and PC.127,128

Enolase inhibitors produced by microorganisms

Enolase inhibitors can also be found among natural antibiotics. For instance, SF2312 is a natural phosphonate antibiotic produced by the actinomycete Micromonospora. In 2016, SF2312 was first identified as a potent enolase inhibitor.129 SF2312 showed similar half-maximal inhibitory concentration values toward ENO1 and ENO2, but displayed a stronger inhibitory effect on ENO2 at high concentrations. Similar to HEX, SF2312 blocked glycolysis and demonstrated strong selective toxicity toward ENO1-deleted versus ENO1-rescued glioma cells.129 Recently, the structural basis of enolase inhibition by SF2312 has been elucidated by several studies through molecular docking and X-ray crystallography.130,131,132

In addition, some phage-encoded proteins have been identified as enolase inhibitors. For instance, GP60 was identified as a phage-encoded enolase inhibitor protein (PEIP) of bacteriophage SPO1 for Bacillus subtilis. Interestingly, PEIP-expressing bacteria exhibited slower growth, thinner cell walls, and safranin color in Gram staining, owing to impaired peptidoglycan synthesis.133 Mechanistically, GP60 limited accessibility to the enolase catalytic site by triggering a cascade of conformational changes. Given the high conservation of enolases across species, PEIP also inhibited human ENO1 activity, although its inhibitory effect was much weaker.133 The potential implications of PEIP and its derivatives in human tumors remain to be explored further.

ENO1 DNA vaccine

DNA-based vaccines have emerged as an efficient option for immunotherapy. ENO1 has been reported to induce autoantibody production in various cancers, including breast cancer, lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, head and neck cancer, and melanoma.134 The expression levels of anti-ENO1 autoantibodies were correlated with cancer development and treatment efficacy. Moreover, ENO1 can generate peptide fragments that stimulate tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes to secrete cytokines, such as interferon gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-α).135 Similarly, ENO1 was able to stimulate healthy autologous T cells to proliferate, secrete IFN-γ, and lyse PDAC cells but not normal cells.136 Notably, ENO1-specific T cells inhibited the growth of PDAC cells in immunodeficient mice, indicating that ENO1 could serve as a tumor-associated antigen (TAA).136

To test this hypothesis, scientists injected and electroporated ENO1 plasmid into Kras(G12D)/Cre mice and Kras(G12D)/Trp53(R172H)/Cre mice, a genetically engineered mouse model of pancreatic cancer.137 Subsequently, specific antibodies induced by the ENO1 DNA vaccine promoted the antitumor immune response and extended the survival time of the mice. Further experiments found that ENO1 vaccination reduced the numbers of myeloid-derived suppressor cells and T regulatory cells while promoting T helper 1 and T helper 17 responses. This highlights the potential of the ENO1 DNA vaccine as an innovative immunotherapy for pancreatic cancer patients.137

Recent research has explored the combined application of the ENO1 DNA vaccine and chemotherapy in pancreatic cancer. Analysis of plasma from pancreatic cancer patients revealed that chemotherapy increased the number of TAAs and their ability to activate complement-dependent cytotoxicity.138 Moreover, chemotherapy enhanced the T cell response to ENO1, one of the most recognized TAAs. In a mouse model of pancreatic cancer, combining chemotherapy with the ENO1 DNA vaccine had a synergistic effect and strongly impaired tumor progression, providing evidence for the potential of combined therapies in cancer treatment.138

Limitations and challenges

Despite the promising results described above, limitations and challenges still must be addressed before enolase inhibitors can be translated into clinical drugs for in vivo use (Figure 2). One major challenge is developing enolase inhibitors with high selectivity for cancer cells without affecting normal cells. For instance, ENO1 is the sole isoform expressed in red blood cells (RBCs). Pan-enolase inhibition can result in anemia, which is consistent with human Mendelian data.139 PhAH, as a pan-enolase inhibitor, caused a decrease in the proportion of RBCs in the blood.123 Maximizing inhibitor specificity for ENO2 may help avoid potential off-target effects. Recently, more prodrugs of enolase inhibitors with high selectivity have been identified for further development.125,140

Another challenge is the potential problems of primary and secondary drug resistance. Not all cancer cells rely equally on enolases for their survival and growth due to the complexity and heterogeneity of cancer. Cancer cells may adapt to enolase inhibitors by activating compensatory pathways, acquiring mutations, or upregulating alternative metabolic enzymes. Combination therapies may be necessary to address this resistance and have shown synergistic effects in some studies. However, determining the optimal combinations and timing of treatment and patient stratification for treatment selection is a complex process and requires more studies. Moreover, exploratory studies on biomarkers for enolase-targeted therapy are still lacking.

Overall, although targeting enolases offers potential advantages in cancer therapy, it is essential to address these limitations and challenges related to specificity, toxicity, resistance, and patient stratification. Considering the limited data currently available, the development of enolase inhibitors for clinical use may take time.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Overall, enolases play a critical role in cancer development through both glycolytic and nonglycolytic functions. In addition to ENO1, recent studies have also found important roles for ENO2 and ENO3 in different tumors. NSE, also called ENO2, has been used as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for certain types of tumors, particularly NETs. Furthermore, ENO2 exerts diverse biological functions in tumor progression, including increased glycolysis, cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, chemoresistance, and radiotherapy resistance (Figure 1). The currently known biological functions of ENO3 differ across tumors, and more research is needed.

In view of previous studies, enolases have emerged as promising drug targets for cancer therapy. Preclinical studies have shown that enolase-targeted therapies suppress tumor growth and metastasis in a variety of cancer types, including GBM, prostate, and pancreatic cancer (Table 2). In addition, combination with enolase-targeted therapy, such as the ENO1 DNA vaccine, synergistically enhances the efficacy of chemotherapy. With continued research and development, enolase-targeted therapies hold great potential as novel anticancer agents.
