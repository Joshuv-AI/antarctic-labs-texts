An improved particle swarm optimization framework with Jacobian guidance for inverse kinematics of non-Pieper robots
Authors: Saidong Yang, Ruilong Ren, Debo Liu, Lu Mi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For non-Pieper robotic manipulators that lack closed-form analytical solutions, inverse kinematics (IK) is typically solved using numerical or optimization-based methods. However, conventional Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) algorithms often suffer from premature convergence, unstable performance near singular configurations, and limited trajectory smoothness. To address these challenges, this paper proposes an improved PSO framework that integrates a nonlinear inertia weight strategy with Jacobian pseudo-inverse guidance. The nonlinear inertia weight employs a two-phase adaptation mechanism, enhancing global exploration in the early iterations and improving local exploitation later, thus avoiding stagnation. The Jacobian guidance introduces gradient-based directional information, which accelerates convergence and improves robustness under singular conditions. Furthermore, a quaternion-based pose error formulation combined with a joint continuity penalty ensures smooth trajectory tracking and avoids orientation singularities. Experimental results on benchmark functions and two representative non-Pieper manipulators demonstrate that the proposed method achieves superior convergence accuracy, improved stability, and better trajectory continuity compared with multiple PSO variants. The results confirm the effectiveness and generality of the proposed framework for solving IK problems of non-Pieper robots.

Introduction

The rapid growth in demand for intelligent manufacturing and safe production has led collaborative robots (cobots) to gradually capture a larger market share compared to industrial robots, owing to their superior attributes of small mass, low power consumption, and ease of operation. Cobots demonstrate irreplaceable advantages in complex operational scenarios, such as flexible assembly1,2 and medical surgical procedures3. For operators, the primary concern often lies in the trajectory the robot can execute within Cartesian space. However, robots are fundamentally driven through joint servos, necessitating the transformation of Cartesian space trajectory points into joint space via inverse kinematics (IK). Consequently, IK solutions play a pivotal role in trajectory planning and motion control.

Current IK solution methodologies primarily encompass numerical and closed-form solutions, each with inherent limitations. Closed-form solutions exist only for robots satisfying the Pieper’s criterion4. This method derives joint expressions directly from the robot’s kinematic equations, offering high computational efficiency. However, its applicability is restricted, and solution accuracy is constrained by the precision of the kinematic model. In contrast, numerical iterative methods approximate the IK solution through iterative computations, generally achieving higher solution accuracy. Yet, these methods demand substantial computational resources and fail to yield solutions when the robot approaches singular configurations. Some scholars have used the pseudo-inverse of the Jacobian matrix to solve IK problems, in some instances, resulted in joint velocities exceeding 180°/s, triggering emergency braking.

For redundant robots5, traditional analytical methods are inapplicable, while numerical iterative techniques face a fundamental trade-off between computational efficiency and solution optimality. Within this context, intelligent optimization algorithms present a novel paradigm for addressing such challenges. Indeed, robot IK constitutes a nonlinear problem where a pose may correspond to multiple valid joint angle combinations. Formulating the IK problem as a minimization task-finding the joint angles that minimize the distance to a desired target pose—provides a viable solution framework. Optimization-based IK approaches also offer significant advantages in terms of generality and independence from specific kinematic model constraints, attracting considerable research interest. Among these, the Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) algorithm has been widely adopted for IK solving due to its simplicity, few tuning parameters, and rapid convergence. However, the standard PSO algorithm encounters specific challenges when applied to the IK of redundant robots, including premature convergence, loss of particle diversity in high-dimensional search spaces leading to entrapment in local optima, and inadequate handling of dynamic constraints on particle states.

Consequently, numerous scholars have proposed enhancements to the PSO algorithm6,7. Mustafa Ayyıldız8 analyzed optimization results for a 4-degree-of-freedom (DOF) robot’s IK, determining that the Quantum-behaved PSO (QPSO) algorithm offered more effective solutions. DENG et al.9 utilized an iterative process based on the objective function for IK solving, optimized via PSO with an adaptive inertia weight strategy to adjust particle velocity, achieving accurate IK solutions for two types of serial robots. Serkan DERELİ10 compared the Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) algorithm11, Firefly Algorithm (FA), and PSO for robot IK, validating the efficacy of swarm intelligence algorithms. Hsu-Chih Huang and Thomas Joseph Collins et al.12 employed PSO for the IK of a 7-DOF manipulator. Serkan Dereli et al.13 applied QPSO to the IK of a 7-DOF robot, utilizing wave functions for particle position and velocity updates, thereby reducing positional error. LIU YIYANG14 introduced a similarity concept, updating the inertia weight based on the distance between a particle and the global best particle. They also implemented parallel sub-swarms with information exchange between sub-swarms via their best particles, effectively enhancing population diversity. Liu et al.15 partitioned the swarm into two parallel sub-swarms to strengthen global search capability, successfully solving the IK for a UR5 manipulator. Zhao et al.16 employed an adaptive strategy to update the PSO inertia weight and introduced position coefficients to bound particle velocities, reducing both positional and orientational errors in the IK solution. Sebastian Starke et al.17 integrated genetic algorithm operators within PSO, enabling higher precision IK solutions across different robot models. An et al.18 leveraged the global best solution from the previous iteration to guide particle movement, demonstrating improved algorithm stability through manipulator IK validation. Arabinda Ghosh et al.19 proposed a modified position update mechanism confining particles to feasible joint ranges, subsequently improving position error accuracy for a 7-DOF manipulator. Cai et al.20 developed a Simulated Annealing PSO (SA-PSO) hybrid, exploiting SA’s ability to escape local optima to enhance PSO’s global search, achieving high pose accuracy for a 6-DOF manipulator IK.

Evidently, the particle velocity update strategy significantly influences the algorithm’s convergence speed and optimization precision. Therefore, this paper proposes a novel PSO variant featuring a nonlinear inertia weight strategy for velocity updates and the integration of asynchronous learning factors to enhance information exchange between individual particles and the collective swarm. Concurrently, the IK problem is formulated as a fitness function based on the robot’s end-effector position and orientation error, tailored to the operational task. The performance of the proposed improved PSO algorithm is then rigorously evaluated by solving this objective function and compared against several existing PSO variants.

To address the limitations of existing optimization-based IK solvers, this paper proposes an improved Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) framework that integrates a nonlinear inertia weight strategy with Jacobian guidance. The main contributions are as follows:

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 details the kinematic modeling of both redundant robots and robots not satisfying Pieper’s criterion, along with the formulation of the fitness function. Section 3 introduces the principles of the standard PSO algorithm and elaborates on the proposed improvement strategies. Section 4 presents the experimental validation, employing standard benchmark functions and IK solving for various robot models to verify the effectiveness of the improved PSO algorithm. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper.

Modeling of Robot inverse kinematics problem

Robot model establishment

Cobots are commonly categorized by their number of serial joints, with six-degree-of-freedom (6-DOF) and seven-degree-of-freedom (7-DOF) configurations being prevalent. Representative models include the UR5e, the FANUC CRX-10iA21, and the YuMi 14,000 ABB robot22( Fig. 1). The latter two models, which do not satisfy Pieper’s criteria, are particularly significant for studying IK solution methodologies.

IK addresses the critical problem of determining the set of joint angles required to achieve a desired end-effector pose. Consider a welding application scenario: a robot performing linear welding requires trajectory points defined in Cartesian space. Translating this trajectory into executable motion necessitates solving the IK problem to obtain the corresponding joint angle sequences. Therefore, the IK problem can be formally posed as a mathematical optimization problem.3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f^{ - 1}}\left( {{{\mathbf{T}}_{{\mathrm{t}}\arg {\mathrm{et}}}}} \right)={\mathbf{q}}\left( {{\theta _1},{\theta _2}, \ldots ,{\theta _m}} \right)$$\end{document}

Problem formulation

Since the robots analyzed in this work (CRX-10iA and YuMi 14000) do not satisfy Pieper’s criterion, analytical solutions do not exist. Consequently, numerical iterative or optimization-based methods are essential for finding suitable joint angle configurations. The objective of IK solving is to find a Joint angle combination such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{T}}_{{\mathrm{cur}}}}$$\end{document}minimizes the error relative to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{T}}_{{\mathrm{t}}\arg {\mathrm{et}}}}$$\end{document}, while simultaneously adhering to the joint angle limits. The homogeneous transformation matrix incorporates both R and P, while other parameters remain constant. To reduce the complexity of the objective function, R and P are separated; consequently, Eq. 4 is often simplified to Eq. 5.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_1}=\left\{ \begin{gathered} {{\mathbf{q}}_{\hbox{min} }} \leqslant {\mathbf{q}} \leqslant {{\mathbf{q}}_{\hbox{max} }} \hfill \\ \hbox{min} \left\| {{{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathrm{t}}\arg {\mathrm{et}}}} - {{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathrm{cur}}}}} \right\|+\hbox{min} \left\| {{{\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathrm{t}}\arg {\mathrm{et}}}} - {{\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathrm{cur}}}}} \right\| \hfill \\ \end{gathered} \right.$$\end{document}

A common practice involves decomposing R into Roll-Pitch-Yaw (RPY) angles, reducing the orientation representation from 9 parameters to 3, thereby shrinking the optimization search space. However, this approach suffers from gimbal lock when the Pitch angle approaches ± 90°. In this singular configuration, the Roll and Yaw axes become aligned, causing the robot to lose one rotational degree of freedom, and rendering the Jacobian matrix rank-deficient. To circumvent this issue and prevent angular discontinuities during optimization-especially relevant when joint angle ranges exceed 360°23, this paper employs quaternions to represent robot orientation. Quaternions inherently avoid gimbal lock and provide a continuous, singularity-free representation.

Furthermore, to assign distinct priorities to position and orientation during the optimization, different weighting factors are introduced. Particle Swarm Optimization and Improvement Strategies

Algorithm fundamentals

Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), a heuristic algorithm inspired by avian collective behavior, was introduced by Eberhart and Kennedy in 199524. It enhances search efficiency through information exchange between individual particles and the swarm collective. Due to its structural simplicity and robust optimization capability, PSO has gained significant research adoption across diverse domains. Consequently, numerous variants have emerged to augment its performance.

In the standard PSO algorithm, each particle possesses its current position and velocity. Enhancement strategies

Standard PSO’s fixed parameters limit performance. Leveraging its gradient-descent characteristics, dynamic parameter adjustment improves convergence and precision. Notable improvement strategy include:

This strategy primarily introduces local interaction rules inspired by starling flocking behavior to enhance population diversity. Furthermore, through the local interaction term, particles are influenced by neighboring particles, achieving a balance between global exploration and local exploitation25. (4) Multi-Population PSO (MPPSO).

The Multi-Swarm PSO strategy26 partitions the population into distinct sub-swarms that conduct independent searches. During each iteration, sub-swarm information exchange and synchronized historical optimal solution updates collectively enhance global exploration capability while Avoiding suboptimal solutions.

Comprehensive analysis reveals that PSO exhibits gradient-descent-like optimization behavior, demonstrating robust convergence even for high-dimensional nonlinear problems. Leveraging the principles of linear inertia weight and empirical insights, this paper proposes a Nonlinear Weight PSO (NWPSO) variant. This method preserves the advantages of linear strategies while accommodating the algorithm’s dual-phase convergence characteristics: rapid initial convergence and late-stage local optimum avoidance. The thresholds ωz and tz preserve ω’s linear decay trajectory while enabling phase-dependent adaptation:

To accelerate convergence for IK problems, the manipulator Jacobian matrix J is leveraged. This matrix defines the differential mapping between joint space and Cartesian space.14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{D}}_p}={\mathbf{J}}{{\mathbf{D}}_\theta }$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{D}}_p}={\left[ {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} {dx}&{dy}&{dz}&{drx}&{dry}&{drz} \end{array}} \right]^{\mathrm{T}}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{D}}_\theta }$$\end{document} denotes joint differential motion.

When a target pose is given, the joint adjustment direction that drives the end-effector along the steepest error descent gradient can be computed. To address Jacobian rank deficiency and prevent numerical instability, the pseudo inverse of the Jacobian matrix is used to update the joint angle.15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {\mathbf{q}}={{\mathbf{J}}^T}{\left( {{\mathbf{J}}{{\mathbf{J}}^T}+\lambda {\mathbf{I}}} \right)^{^{{ - 1}}}}{\mathbf{e}}$$\end{document}

The error term e in the manuscript represents the pose error in operational space, including both position and orientation components. Specifically, e can be interpreted as the difference between the target and current pose over consecutive timesteps, analogous to a velocity. The mapping between operational space and joint space is given by:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{\dot {x}=J\dot {q}}}$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{\dot {x}}}$$\end{document} is the operational space velocity, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{\dot {q}}}$$\end{document} is the joint space velocity, and J is the Jacobian matrix.

The representation of the terminal pose X is as follows:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X=\left[ {{\mathbf{P}},\left[ {{q_0},{q_1},{q_2},{q_3}} \right]} \right]$$\end{document}

Where P is the position vector, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left[ {{q_0},{q_1},{q_2},{q_3}} \right]$$\end{document} is the unit quaternion equivalent of the rotation matrix R.

Then, the error in continuous time can be expressed as:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{V}}=\left[ {{\mathbf{\dot {P}}},\left[ {{{\dot {q}}_0},{{\dot {q}}_1},{{\dot {q}}_2},{{\dot {q}}_3}} \right]} \right]=\left[ {{{\mathbf{V}}_E},\left[ {{{\dot {q}}_0},{{\dot {q}}_1},{{\dot {q}}_2},{{\dot {q}}_3}} \right]} \right]$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{V}}_E}$$\end{document} is the linear velocity vector of the end-effector.

The angular velocity vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{\omega }}_E}$$\end{document} is obtained from the quaternion derivatives as:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{\omega }}_E}=2\left[ {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} { - {q_1}}&{{q_0}}&{ - {q_3}}&{{q_2}} \\ { - {q_2}}&{{q_3}}&{{q_0}}&{ - {q_1}} \\ { - {q_3}}&{ - {q_2}}&{{q_1}}&{{q_0}} \end{array}} \right]\left[ {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} {{{\dot {q}}_0}} \\ {{{\dot {q}}_1}} \\ {{{\dot {q}}_2}} \\ {{{\dot {q}}_3}} \end{array}} \right]$$\end{document}

The velocity vector in Cartesian space can be transformed to the joint space:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{\dot {q}}}={{\mathbf{J}}^T}{\left( {{\mathbf{J}}{{\mathbf{J}}^T}+\lambda {\mathbf{I}}} \right)^{ - 1}}\left[ \begin{gathered} {{\mathbf{V}}_E} \hfill \\ {{\mathbf{\omega }}_E} \hfill \\ \end{gathered} \right]$$\end{document}

Note that the error vector e used in Eq. 15 is directly related to the Cartesian velocity (i.e., e can be seen as the desired Cartesian displacement to be achieved in one iteration).

Integrating the PSO velocity update with this Jacobian-based guidance direction yields the hybrid particle update formulation for IK:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{ \begin{gathered} \mathcal{v}_{{iD}}^{{t+1}}=\omega * \mathcal{v}_{{iD}}^{t}+{c_1}{r_1}(p_{{iD}}^{t} - \mathcal{x}_{{iD}}^{t})+{c_2}{r_2}(p_{{gD}}^{t} - \mathcal{x}_{{iD}}^{t})+{{\mathbf{J}}^T}{\left( {{\mathbf{J}}{{\mathbf{J}}^T}+\lambda {\mathbf{I}}} \right)^{ - 1}}{\mathbf{e}} \hfill \\ \mathcal{x}_{{iD}}^{{t+1}}=\mathcal{x}_{{iD}}^{t}+\mathcal{v}_{{iD}}^{{t+1}} \hfill \\ \end{gathered} \right.$$\end{document}

Where λ is a damping coefficient that prevents matrix singularities. A larger λ increases the stability of the Jacobian inverse but reduces accuracy, a smaller λ improves accuracy but may cause larger joint velocities27,28. λ is typically tuned via simulation or empirical experience. It can be set to 0 within the robot’s normal working range but should be increased near singularities. In simulations, λ is adjusted for trajectories that may encounter singularities (e.g., 0.01, 0.05, 0.1) by observing joint velocities and end-effector errors. In this paper, λ ranges from 0.01 to 0.1.

Given the stochastic nature of optimization algorithms, each function is executed over 30 independent runs. Mean performance metrics are reported in Table 5 to assess algorithmic stability.

Data in Table 5 demonstrate that while all PSO variants converge towards near-optimal solutions across unimodal and multimodal functions, significant performance differences exist. The proposed NWPSO algorithm outperforms other variants in convergence precision for most functions. This superiority stems from two key design aspects:

Nonlinear Inertia Weight Adaptation: Empirical analysis indicates algorithms frequently converge prematurely between iterations 20–30. Continuous trajectory algorithm validation

Comparative validation at randomly generated points demonstrates that the NWPSO algorithm exhibits strong optimization performance. In practical applications, robots typically operate along trajectories. When the motion is planned as a continuous trajectory, Eq. 7 is adopted as the fitness function. It considers not only the minimum pose difference between two points but also the pose error between adjacent trajectory points along the trajectory. Given the trajectory-dependent nature of practical robotic operations, a spatial linear path was generated to evaluate NWPSO’s waypoint optimization performance. The trajectory initialization procedure commenced from the initial joint-space configuration [0, 0.7854, -0.5236, 0.1, 0, 1.22217, 0]rad, where forward kinematics computed the starting end-effector pose Tstart. Cartesian displacements (Δx, Δy, Δz) along the end-effector frame axes defined the linear trajectory path. Continuous target poses were subsequently generated via cubic polynomial interpolation using MATLAB’s Robotics Toolbox ctraj function. NWPSO optimized joint solutions for each target pose with Eq. 7 as the fitness function, recording optimal solutions throughout the process. Simulation results are presented in Fig. 5.

Optimization-based methods overcome this limitation: by reformulating the fitness function to minimize pose errors between actual and target end-effector configurations, they generalize to arbitrary redundant robots.

Conclusion

This study addresses forward and inverse kinematics (IK) analysis for nonstandard robot—specifically those violating Piper criteria and redundant architectures. To solve the IK problem for such robots, target end-effector poses in Cartesian space were converted into homogeneous transformation matrices via forward kinematics. Pose error functions between current and target configurations were subsequently formulated and resolved through optimization algorithms. The principal contributions are threefold:

The proposed NWPSO introduces intermediate thresholds for inertial weight and iteration count to modulate convergence behavior. This dual-phase adaptation accelerates convergence velocity while preventing local optima entrapment and enhancing solution precision. Validation on standard benchmark functions confirmed NWPSO’s superiority in both convergence speed and accuracy over state-of-the-art alternatives.Specialized manipulator IK resolution was transformed into an optimization problem by constructing fitness functions based on pose errors. Multi-algorithm comparative simulations demonstrate that optimization-based approaches not only effectively solve IK for nonstandard robots but also enable rapid deployment across diverse kinematic structures.Integration of Jacobian pseudo-inversion (Eq. 15) guides particles along gradient-optimal descent directions. Experimental validation on CRX-10iA and YuMi 14,000 manipulators demonstrates that the enhanced algorithm achieves superior accuracy (mean fitness 0.0348) and stability (variance 0.0019) compared to other methods, establishing a novel paradigm for IK solution in specialized robotic systems.
