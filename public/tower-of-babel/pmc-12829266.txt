Lived experiences of peer learning to improve the professional capabilities of hospital managers: a phenomenological study
Authors: Sedighe Sadat Tabatabaei Far, Milad Ahmadi Marzaleh, Maedeh Moradi Farahani, Fatemeh Jafari, Faezeh Sarooeyeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study explored hospital managers’ lived experiences with peer learning as a constructivist educational approach in healthcare management. It aimed to elucidate how peer learning contributes to managerial development and to identify contextual factors that either facilitate or hinder its effectiveness in healthcare organizations.Results descriptionPeer learning significantly improved decision-making, managerial performance, inter-organizational interactions, and continuous updating of specialized knowledge. Exchanging real-world experiences enhanced critical thinking, professional attitudes, and self-efficacy among managers. Facilitating factors included strong organizational support, sharing successful practices, and benchmarking against high-performing hospitals. However, effectiveness was limited by weak teamwork culture, the absence of systematic management development strategies, restricted structured interactions, and organizational/structural barriers. Additional obstacles included limited managerial authority, deficiencies in succession planning, and inadequate coordination of directors’ assemblies by upstream institutions. Overall, peer learning proved highly valuable for developing managerial competencies, yet realizing its full potential requires eliminating structural obstacles and implementing targeted participatory policies and mechanisms (e.g., learning communities and succession planning programs).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07616-2.Keywords: Peer learning, Hospital management, Managerial competencies, Professional development, Healthcare organizations, Experiential learning, Organizational support, Leadership skills, Constructivist approach

Introduction

One of the important approaches to learning is constructivism, and one of the methods used in this approach, widely used in medical education, is peer learning [1]. Peer learning involves learners at the same age group and at a similar learning level, often sharing comparable experiences. In health settings, peer learning enhances self-confidence, presentation skills [2], teamwork, responsibility, development of critical thinking skills [3], increased opportunities to ask questions, and increased intergroup cooperation in transferring health-related information [4, 5]. In healthcare, skill development in critical situations is enhanced by observing and practicing alongside experienced peers to identify best practices [6–8]. One reason for the limited learning in healthcare settings within the country is insufficient reliance on and ineffective use of peer learning. There is also a lack of opportunities to gain clinical experience and learn best practices in healthcare centers [9]. For example, learning from peers has a significant impact on changing attitudes, increasing clinical skills, and gaining successful experiences while working in nursing [10].

Human resource training, particularly for managers, is a key strategy for developing human capital and adapting to change [11]. Healthcare organizations are not an exception to this issue. Among them, hospitals are complex and challenging institutions that have the most capital, specialized knowledge and skills, and equipment in the health sector. In many countries, hospitals account for over 50% of health sector financial resources; in Iran, this reaches about 75%, highlighting the critical importance of effective hospital management [12].

Several strategies are being implemented to improve professional skills in hospitals. Peer learning is one of the fundamental topics in developing and improving performance [13, 14]. In the peer training approach, knowledge, skills, and behaviors are shared by individuals or groups who have similar characteristics, such as age, gender, experience, or culture, and possess the influence to guide the target group [15].

Hospital managers’ professional capabilities are vital for efficient healthcare performance. In this study, the monthly managers’ assembly meetings were utilized as the platform to facilitate a structured peer learning process. While the agenda items often involved policy and coordination—functions typical of such committees—the core intervention was the guided, collaborative reflection on these shared challenges. The premise is that while these administrative structures exist in hospitals, they often lack a deliberate focus on knowledge sharing. The meetings created a formal space for managers to learn from each other’s experiences and problem-solving approaches, thereby embedding peer learning within the existing administrative routine. As no qualitative research has explored peer learning among Iranian hospital managers, this study aimed to explore their lived experiences of peer learning to enhance professional capabilities. It also sought to improve healthcare management quality and enhance the performance and effectiveness of hospitals and healthcare organizations.

Method

Study type and study setting

This study was conducted in 2025 in Iran using a qualitative, cross-sectional phenomenological approach to describe the conditions and the phenomenon. The research was conducted at the teaching hospitals of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences in Iran. The study was carried out across the 10 major teaching hospitals affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. Data were collected either in person or by phone.

Study population and participants

The research population included both current and former managers of teaching hospitals affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Science. Twelve managers were interviewed, with the sample size determined by theoretical saturation. The sampling method was purposive and snowball, starting with the managers of the studied hospitals as the initial sample. If additional information was needed, the snowball sampling method was used to conduct further interviews. The inclusion criterion was having at least a bachelor’s degree. The exclusion criteria included the participants’ lack of readiness to participate and lack of interest in participating in the study.

Data collection tools and process

Each manager was asked in-depth and semi-structured questions about peer learning to improve the professional skills of hospital managers. The main questions included: “Would you describe your experience with peer learning to enhance the professional capabilities of hospital managers?” and “Has your attendance at joint meetings of managers helped you learn? How?” Based on the participants’ responses, follow-up questions were also asked; the complete set of interview questions is provided in supplementary file 1. The interviews lasted between 30 and 60 min and took place in a quiet setting. They were ended once data saturation was reached. This study leveraged the monthly administrative meetings as a structured platform for peer learning. The methodology focused specifically on the facilitated peer-to-peer knowledge exchange and collaborative problem-solving that occurred within these meetings, treating this interaction as the core learning intervention, distinct from the formal administrative agenda. Data saturation was operationally defined as the point where no new codes or themes emerged from consecutive interviews. This criterion was met during the final 2–3 interviews, which consistently yielded only repetitions of previously identified codes.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection, beginning with the first interview. The hermeneutic method guided the analysis, which was done through four main phases. First, all interviews were transcribed, capturing each participant’s experience. Next, statements from each participant were coded to identify distinct aspects. Then, similar codes were grouped, and each group was assigned an appropriate title. Finally, these categories were organized based on theoretical similarities, revealing the relationships among the core themes of data interpretation [16].

After each interview, the transcript was written and reviewed multiple times to gain a comprehensive understanding. An interpretive summary was then prepared for each transcript to uncover and extract the underlying meanings. Reporting, writing, and describing the phenomenon.

Finally, a complete report is presented for each theme, code, and subcategory, accompanied by a discussion. The report begins with an introduction outlining the research area, objectives, or specific research questions. Next, the research methodology, its rationale, and process are explained. In the findings section, the extracted themes are presented, defined, and supported by participant quotes, and their relationships are discussed. The discussion section places the themes within the context of existing literature and studies, offering suggestions for improving practice and directions for future research.

Data analysis was conducted manually. Also, reductionist phenomenology (bracketing) was used in the sense of abandoning the researcher’s assumptions. Further texts and sources were reviewed after data analysis. This includes the use of reflexive journals by the research team and the strategic involvement of interviewers from non-management backgrounds to mitigate domain-specific biases. These measures were implemented to ensure that the findings genuinely emerged from the participants’ data rather than the researcher’s assumptions.

Validity and reliability

To ensure the accuracy and rigor of the qualitative data, we applied Guba and Lincoln’s criteria [20]. The study examined trustworthiness, transformability, dependability, and confirmability. To ensure credibility (internal validity), we employed prolonged engagement, direct observation, peer debriefing, member checks, and triangulation. Transferability (external validity) was achieved by providing thick, contextual descriptions of the study and using systematic inductive and deductive coding procedures. Dependability (reliability) was established by maintaining an audit trail of the interview data and reflecting on the stability of the findings. Finally, confirmability (objectivity) was secured by having methodology experts, familiar with qualitative research, review a sample of the interview transcripts to verify the accuracy of the analysis process. We believe these concrete measures robustly address the application of the trustworthiness criteria. Member checking was conducted by sharing emergent findings with participants to validate our interpretations, and peer debriefing was undertaken through regular discussions with disinterested colleagues to challenge the analysis.

Ethical considerations

All ethical considerations related to qualitative interview-based studies were observed in this study. Before conducting the interviews, the study purpose, data usage, and participants’ rights were fully explained to the managers, and written informed consent was obtained. Participation was voluntary, and participants were free to continue or withdraw at any point during the interview process. All collected information was kept confidential, and the final report avoided naming or identifying participants.

Discussion

Our findings can be better understood through the lens of social learning theory and constructivist perspectives, which emphasize the social and interactive nature of learning. In line with Bandura’s concept of modeling and vicarious learning [21], Hospitals’ managers described observing their peers’ approaches to complex managerial challenges and subsequently adapting these strategies within their own contexts. This process reflects learning through imitation, reflection, and contextual adaptation rather than passive knowledge transfer. Similarly, consistent with Vygotsky’s notion of scaffolding and collaborative dialogue [22], Peer interactions created opportunities for participants to construct meaning collectively, negotiate perspectives, and gradually build higher levels of competence. These dialogical exchanges served not only to reinforce existing knowledge but also to challenge assumptions and stimulate reflective inquiry—an aspect less emphasized in traditional hierarchical learning structures. At the same time, our findings extend these theoretical models by highlighting the importance of mutual trust, openness, and reflective dialogue as enabling conditions for professional growth. In this sense, peer learning among hospital managers appears to operationalize the core mechanisms of social and constructivist learning theories while also suggesting their adaptation to the context of managerial practice [23–26].

In a related study, managers reported positive attitudes toward peer learning, noting that peer pairs simultaneously gained conceptual knowledge and collaboration skills. Also, managers observed that these pairs showed high levels of independence and continued to support each other even after the intervention ended [27]. These results match our findings and highlight the strengths of this learning method, such as improved decision-making and planning, as well as the development and enhancement of managerial performance. Additionally, it fosters better managerial interactions and communication, along with ongoing training and updating of managerial knowledge. Yet, these outcomes also invite critical reflection. The apparent increase in independence and ongoing collaboration raises the question of whether the observed changes stem primarily from the peer-learning structure itself or from broader organizational contexts that encourage collegial behavior. Without examining institutional culture or motivational factors, attributing these improvements solely to peer learning may risk oversimplification. Moreover, while the method enhances communication and continuous professional updating, there remains a need to evaluate its scalability and long-term integration into managerial training programs.

The results indicate that peer learning in Iranian hospital management offers significant contexts of transformation to enhance managerial performance and organizational structures. Participants stressed that informal peer interactions improved managerial skills, decision-making, critical thinking, and problem-solving in complex settings. Four main themes were identified as key outcomes:

First, organizational and scientific support appear indispensable to the success of peer learning initiatives. Previous studies emphasize that access to institutional resources, dedicated time, skilled facilitators, and supportive leadership are vital to sustaining such programs [28]. Support from universities, higher education centers, and professional associations also plays a key role in transferring specialized knowledge and providing the necessary scientific content. While this argument is compelling, it invites a critical question: Can peer learning truly flourish in the absence of structural incentives or recognition within the organization? Merely providing logistical support may not be enough unless it is coupled with a broader culture that values continuous learning. The active involvement of universities and professional associations reinforces this process, but the challenge lies in bridging academic frameworks with the often fast-paced realities of managerial practice.

Second, peer learning contributes to more systematic managerial processes and evidence-informed decision-making. Through the exchange of experiences and joint analysis of comparable cases, hospital managers cultivate the capacity to evaluate options critically and make context-sensitive choices [29]. This shows that peer learning is not limited to the transfer of information but provides a platform for developing critical thinking and collective decision-making. However, this outcome presupposes that participants engage reflexively rather than simply replicating others’ methods. The process should, therefore, be seen not as the passive transmission of information, but as a dynamic arena for questioning assumptions and refining collective reasoning.

Third, a notable effect of peer learning is strengthening interpersonal and inter-organizational relationships. These connections often extend beyond formal sessions, giving rise to informal support networks that sustain professional exchange and collaboration [30, 31]. Such networks can facilitate rapid knowledge transfer and inter-organizational cooperation during crises. Yet, informal networks also raise equity concerns—those with stronger social capital may benefit disproportionately, while others remain on the periphery. A more intentional design of inclusive mechanisms could mitigate this limitation.

Fourth, peer learning provided increased feedback and learning from others’ experiences. By hearing success stories, failures, and how colleagues have faced challenges, managers can better identify their strengths and weaknesses and adopt more effective approaches. This experiential model, aligned with reflective and pragmatic learning, has strong potential to enhance professional behavior across managerial levels [32]. Still, its effectiveness depends on psychological safety and trust within the group; without them, honest reflection may be replaced by superficial exchanges.

While peer learning benefits hospital management, this study identified challenges that limit its effectiveness and sustainability. In Iranian hospitals, it lacks a formal role due to absent policies, limited resources, and low awareness among senior managers. To address this, health education systems should revise professional development policies to build a structured framework for peer learning. The recommended strategies include programs that foster horizontal interactions, create learning communities, support experience exchange, and strengthen peer-to-peer initiatives.

Key barriers to peer learning among Iranian hospital managers include challenges in management and decision-making, weak interpersonal and organizational interactions, ineffective teamwork, and shortcomings in forming functional managerial assemblies. Additionally, structural and organizational limitations further hinder the process, as reported in similar contexts [33, 34]. These challenges show that to effectively use peer learning, simply providing educational opportunities or facilitating meetings is insufficient. Rather, more comprehensive measures are required, including organizational structure reform, enhancement of managers’ soft skills, promotion of a participatory culture, and reduction of systemic barriers. An integrated approach combining individual empowerment with structural change can maximize the impact of peer learning on health system management [35].

A key strength of this study is its exploration of peer learning from managers’ perspectives. Using a phenomenological approach, it captured real experiences, emotions, and perceptions, revealing hidden aspects of learning within complex organizational settings. Its focus on Iran’s cultural and organizational context adds practical relevance. Limitations include the qualitative design, small sample size, and reliance on self-reported data, which may affect generalizability. However, the study provides a valuable foundation for future research on professional learning in health system management.

Conclusion

Peer learning may serve as a valuable avenue for enhancing hospital managers’ professional competencies, particularly by nurturing more informed decision-making, strengthening systemic and strategic modes of thinking, fostering collaborative engagement, and promoting reflective learning and a sense of self-efficacy. Drawing on a phenomenological approach, this study delved into the lived experiences of hospital managers, shedding light on the nuanced ways in which social interactions and reflective processes shape their professional growth and learning trajectories. The insights derived from this exploration may offer meaningful guidance to policymakers and educators seeking to design more contextually grounded, interaction-oriented professional development programs within Iran’s health management system.
