Poncelet property of planar elliptic integrable Kepler billiards
Daniel Jaud, Lei Zhao

Abstract
We consider the integrable dynamics of a Kepler billiard in the plane bounded by a branch of a conic section focused at the Kepler center. We show that in this case, for non-zero-energy orbits, the lines of consecutive second orbital foci along a billiard trajectory are all tangent to a fixed circle. Based on this observation we analyse in details the integrable dynamics of a planar Kepler billiard inside or outside an elliptic reflection wall, with the Kepler center occupying one of its foci. We identify the associated elliptic curve on which the dynamics is linearized, and the shift defined thereon. We also discuss explicit conditions on n n -periodicity using Cayley’s criteria.



Keywords:
billiards, central potential, Kepler problem, Poncelet porism

14H70, 37C79, 37J99, 37N05

Introduction

The classical Poncelet theorem asserts that in the plane, giving a pair of conic sections, a polygon inscribed in one conic section and circumscribing another always appears in an infinite family of such polygons
[
15
]
. A modern review due to Griffiths and Harris
[
9
]
explained that this theorem is ultimately linearized on an elliptic curve with a shift of finite order defined thereon. Starting with any point on the elliptic curve, by successive application of the shift we get an orbit of finite order in the complex domain. When the whole orbit is real, then the shift is real and this corresponds to a polygon having the prescribed property. Shifting the initial point in the real part we get an infinite family of polygons with the same property. The link of the Poncelet porism to billiard dynamics is described in
[
16
]
.

Billiard systems in the plane are much studied in the research field of Dynamical Systems, both with and without a potential. Recently many studies on the integrability and dynamics of billiards in a Kepler-Coulomb potential, often referred to as Kepler billiards, have been carried out. In
[
4
]
, Felder integrated out the planar Kepler billiard with a line as the wall of reflection, whose integrability is due to Gallavotti and Jauslin
[
7
]
, in terms of elliptic curves with a direct computation. Some conditions on periodic points has been computed in this work as well. Conditions on
n
-periodicity as well as the topology of such a system have been subsequently studied in
[
8
]
. Many more integrable Kepler billiards in the plane are pointed out in
[
17
,
18
]
.

The aim of this article is to initiate analysis on the dynamics of integrable Kepler billiard systems with a branch of a (non-degenerate) conic section as reflection wall in the plane. In this system the particle moves on one side of the reflection wall in a Kepler potential field whose center is at a focus of the conic section. As first observed in
[
7
]
for the line case and later also in the cases with elliptic or hyperbolic reflection walls
[
17
,
18
,
14
]
, along a billiard trajectory, the second orbital foci all lie on a circle centered at the second focus of the reflection wall.

In the case with a line reflection wall, Felder
[
4
]
has analyzed the integrable dynamics via the use of elliptic curves and elliptic function. Analogously it is natural to expect a link between the integrable dynamics of these billiard-type systems with elliptic curves. It is theoretical possible to identify this elliptic curve by direct parametrizing the elliptic boundary and the (elliptic) Keplerian orbits. We nevertheless adapt a different way based on a simple but for us unexpected geometric fact: Along a Kepler billiard orbit, the lines containing consecutive second orbital foci are all tangent to a circle, that we call the
foci-caustic circle
. Therefore by considering how the second orbital foci is iterated we reduce the analysis into the much familiar, “standard” situation of the Poncelet porism. In this situation the underlying elliptic curve, the shift, and the condition for
n
-periodicity are exclusively discussed in
[
9
,
10
]
. An application of these classical works yields our desired results. We carry out the detailed analysis in this article for Kepler problem with negative energy with an elliptic boundary. We leave the detailed analysis of several other cases in our future upcoming works.

In a recent preprint
[
1
]
the authors showed in particular that at high energy, the only integrable Kepler billiards inside an ellipse are those with the Kepler center getting placed at a focus. Through the Hooke-Kepler correspondence, the billiard systems under our study are also directly related to centered Hooke billiard inside an ellipse, for which some analysis are available
[
2
,
11
,
6
]
.

We organize this article as follows: In Section
2
we set up our systems under study and discuss the foci-circle, a notion first introduced in
[
13
]
. We complete the discussion for the parabolic case, in which the foci-circle should be replaced by a
foci-line
. In Section
3
we analyse the geometric properties of the iterated second foci and show the existence of the foci-caustic circle. The billiard dynamics is now reduced to the dynamics of line segments tangent to a circle with end-points in another circle. The associated elliptic curve, the corresponding shift, and the relevant real part are computed in Section
4
. Conditions on
n
-periodicity are computed in
5
, in which we also include some explicit discussions for small
n
.

2
Integrable Kepler billiards and some of their geometrical properties

We consider a billiard system in the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, for which the particle moves under the influence of a Kepler(-Coulomb) potential field and gets reflected at a branch of a conic section
\mathbf{K}
with a focus at the Kepler center
F
. Let
F^{\prime}
be the second focus of the conic section boundary: This leaves out only the case of a parabola but include two important degenerate cases of a circle for which
F=F^{\prime}
and a line for which
F^{\prime}
is mirrored from
F
through the line (the case implicitly investigated by Boltzmann
[
3
]
).

We set
F^{\prime}=O
as the origin of our coordinate system. We put the Kepler center at
F=(-2c_{\mathbf{K}},0)
, so
c_{\mathbf{K}}\geq 0
is the linear eccentricity. The semi major axis of
\mathbf{K}
is denoted by
a_{\mathbf{K}}
. The eccentricity of
\mathbf{K}
is
e_{\mathbf{K}}=c_{\mathbf{K}}/a_{\mathbf{K}}
. The degenerate case of a circle and a line correspond respectively to
c_{\mathbf{K}}=0
and
a_{\mathbf{K}}=\infty
, two cases of no eccentricity.

The Keplerian orbits of the underlying system are conic section branches with a focus at
F
. At elastic reflections the total energy is preserved, which gives us a conserved quantity of the system. We consider the cases that the total energy is non-zero. The orbital semi major axis is thus well-defined and is invariant under reflections. We denote it by
a
. The orbit is now completely determined by its second focus up to orientation. Thus to a billiard trajectory of Keplerian arcs reflecting at
\mathbf{K}
there is an associated sequence of second foci
F_{1}F_{2}\dots
, which depends on the Kepler billiard trajectory and can be finite or infinite.

A major geometrical fact on the integrability of this mechanical billiard system is the following, which is established in
[
7
,
4
,
17
,
18
]
:

Proposition 2.1
.

The sequence of second foci associated to a Kepler billiard trajectory lies on a circle centered at
F^{\prime}
.

The circle is called the
foci-circle
of the system, and is denoted by
S(F^{\prime},R)
for which
R>0
is the radius. The quantity
R
is another conserved quantity of the system. For a graphical representation of the setup see Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
Setup of the system with elliptic boundary and foci-circle on which all consecutive second Kepler foci lie.

The parabolic case needs to be considered separately, as it possesses only one focus. We set
R^{\prime}=2\,c_{\mathbf{K}}-R
, which is naturally conserved in the elliptic/hyperbolic boundary cases. We can rewrite the above proposition in the following form to include the parabolic boundary case:

Proposition 2.2
.

The sequence of second foci associated to a Kepler billiard trajectory lies on a circle centered at
F^{\prime}
in the elliptic/hyperbolic boundary cases.
For a parabolic boundary with focus
F
the second Kepler billiard foci lie on a line perpendicular to its axis of symmetry.

Proof.

We complete the proof for both parabolic cases starting with the one enclosing
F
.

Call
F
the focus of the parabolic boundary equal to the Kepler force center,
F_{1}
the second orbital focus,
P_{1}
the point of reflection and
\ell_{P_{1}}
the tangent line at
P_{1}
to the parabolic boundary. Then the mirror point
F_{P_{1}}
of
F
with respect to
\ell_{P_{1}}
is such that
F_{P_{1}}P_{1}
is parallel to the symmetry axis of the parabolic boundary. The next second orbital focus
F_{2}
lies on two circles, one centered at
P_{1}
with radius
|F_{1}P_{1}|
and the other one centered at
F_{P_{1}}
with radius
|F_{P_{1}}F_{1}|
. The first follows from optical definition of conic section and the second follows from a theorem of
[
7
]
when the reflection is considered to happen along
\ell_{P_{1}}
. As both circles are symmetric with respect to the line
F_{P_{1}}P_{1}
, the point
F_{2}
is just the mirror point of
F_{1}
and thus
F_{1}F_{2}
is perpendicular to the major axis of the parabolic boundary. Thus under iterations all
F_{i}^{\prime}s
lie in the same line perpendicular to the symmetry axis of the parabolic boundary. See Fig.
2
for a graphical representation of the proof.
This can be interpreted as the limiting case of a hyperbolic boundary with
F^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty
and
c_{\mathbf{K}}-a_{\mathbf{K}}=\hbox{const}.
In this case the curvature of the foci-circle tends to zero with center tending toward
\infty
, and in the limit we get the foci-line.
∎

Figure 2:
Foci line construction for the parabolic boundary.

The existence of
R
(respectively
R^{\prime}
) provides a conserved quantity independent of the total energy, thus makes these billiard systems integrable. This allows us to make detailed analysis of their (regular) dynamics.

3
The foci-chords and the foci-caustic circle

3.1
The Foci-Caustic Circle

Associated to a foci sequence
F_{1}F_{2}\dots
there is a sequence of line segments
F_{1}F_{2},F_{2}F_{3},\dots
that we call the
foci-chords
. The lines containing them are denoted by
\ell(F_{i}F_{i+1}),i=1,2,3\dots
respectively.
We have

Theorem 3.1
.

If the reflection wall is a focused ellipse or a branch of a focused hyperbola, then along a Kepler billiard trajectory, the iterated line of foci-chords
\ell(F_{1}F_{2}),\ell(F_{2}F_{3}),\dots
are tangent to the circle
S(x_{0},|r_{0}|)
, called the
foci-caustic circle
, in which
x_{0}=x_{0}(a,R)
and
r_{0}=r_{0}(a,R)
are given by quadratic functions in terms of the semi major axis
a
of the Kepler orbits and the radius
R
of the foci-circle.

For illustration see Fig.
3
.

Figure 3:
Foci-chords tangent to the foci-caustic circle.

Thus the iterated foci-chords satisfy the usual setting of the Poncelet porism: The line containing the foci-chords are all tangent to the foci-caustic circle and have their ends in the foci-circle.

To proceed we distinguish cases: for the reflection boundary, we write
(\mathbf{E}),(\mathbf{P}),(\mathbf{H}_{F^{\prime}}),(\mathbf{H}_{F})
respectively for the cases of elliptic boundary, parabolic boundary, hyperbolic branch enclosing
F^{\prime}
, and hyperbolic branch enclosing
F
. We write respectively
(\mathcal{E}),(\mathcal{H}_{F^{\prime}}),(\mathcal{H}_{F})
for orbit types: elliptic orbits, hyperbolic orbits enclosing
F^{\prime}
, and hyperbolic orbits enclosing
F
. Note that hyperbolic orbits enclosing the empty focus
F^{\prime}
appear when the Kepler-Coulomb center is repulsive.

The case
(P)
is special as we know that there is a line containing all the
F_{i}^{\prime}s
along a billiard trajectory. Consequently the foci-chords all lie in the same line, and the notion of foci-caustic is not well-defined in this case.

Proof.

We consider the case that an orbit with second focus
F_{1}
is reflected to an orbit with second focus
F_{2}
at the point
P_{1}\in\mathbf{K}
. We assume
\mathbf{K}
is not parabolic.

We set

|FP_{1}|=A_{1},\quad|F^{\prime}P_{1}|=B_{1},\quad|F_{1}P_{1}|=C_{1}^{+},\quad|F_{2}P_{1}|=C_{1}^{-}.

Depending on the type of
\mathbf{K}
we have by its two-foci definition

(\mathbf{E}):A_{1}+B_{1}=2a_{\mathbf{K}};

(\mathbf{H}_{F^{\prime}}):A_{1}-B_{1}=2a_{\mathbf{K}};

(\mathbf{H}_{F}):B_{1}-A_{1}=2a_{\mathbf{K}}.

Then for the orbits we have as well

(\mathcal{E}):A_{1}+C_{1}^{+}=A_{1}+C_{1}^{-}=2a;

(\mathcal{H}_{F^{\prime}}):A_{1}-C_{1}^{-}=A_{1}-C_{2}=2a;

(\mathcal{H}_{F}):A_{1}-C_{1}^{+}=A_{1}-C_{1}^{-}=-2a.

So we have in all cases

C_{1}^{+}=C_{1}^{-}:=C_{1},

and we obtain the following relationships in different cases:

(\mathbf{E},\mathcal{E}):C_{1}-B_{1}=2(a-a_{\mathbf{K}});

(\mathbf{E},\mathcal{H}_{F^{\prime}}):C_{1}+B_{1}=2(a_{\mathbf{K}}-a);

(\mathbf{E},\mathcal{F}):C_{1}+B_{1}=2(a+a_{\mathbf{K}});

(\mathbf{H}_{F^{\prime}},\mathcal{E}):C_{1}+B_{1}=2(a-a_{\mathbf{K}});

(\mathbf{H}_{F^{\prime}},\mathcal{H}_{F^{\prime}}):C_{1}-B_{1}=2(a_{\mathbf{K}}-a);

(\mathbf{H}_{F^{\prime}},\mathcal{H}_{F}):C_{1}-B_{1}=2(a_{\mathbf{K}}+a);

(\mathbf{H}_{F},\mathcal{E}):C_{1}+B_{1}=2(a+a_{\mathbf{K}});

(\mathbf{H}_{F},\mathcal{H}_{F^{\prime}}):B_{1}-C_{1}=2(a_{\mathbf{K}}+a);

(\mathbf{H}_{F},\mathcal{H}_{F}):B_{1}-C_{1}=2(a_{\mathbf{K}}-a).

To continue, we denote the polar angle of
P_{1}
by
\varphi
. The direction
F^{\prime}P_{1}
is thus given by the unit vector
(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)
. As mentioned in the proof of
[
14
, Thm 2]
,
F_{1},F_{2}
are symmetric with respect to
F^{\prime}P_{1}
. Thus the line
\ell(F_{1}F_{2})
is perpendicular to
F^{\prime}P_{1}
and is given by an equation of the form

x\cos\varphi+y\sin\varphi+k=0,

which has signed distance
k
to
F
.

Write
h=|F_{1}F_{2}|/2
. By construction of the foci-circle, we have

k^{2}+h^{2}=R^{2}.

(1)

Applying Pythagorean theorem to
P_{1}
,
F_{1}
and the middle point

{S=(-k\cos(\varphi),-k\sin(\varphi))}
of
F_{1}F_{2}
, we have the formula

(B_{1}+k)^{2}+h^{2}=C_{1}^{2}.

(2)

We thus get from (
1
) and (
2
) that

k=\dfrac{C_{1}^{2}-B_{1}^{2}-R^{2}}{2B_{1}}.

In any of the nine cases listed above we have

C_{1}^{2}-B_{1}^{2}-R^{2}=(C_{1}-B_{1})(C_{1}+B_{1})-R^{2}

=2(\pm a_{\mathbf{K}}\pm a)\cdot(2(\pm a_{\mathbf{K}}\pm a)\pm 2B_{1})-R^{2}=4(a_{\mathbf{K}}\pm a)^{2}-R^{2}\pm 4(a_{\mathbf{K}}\pm a)B_{1},

with a corresponding choice of signs, with the note that the signs are chosen in the same way in the expressions
(\pm a_{\mathbf{K}}\pm a)
.

So we have

k=\dfrac{4(a_{\mathbf{K}}\pm a)^{2}-R^{2}}{2B_{1}}\pm 2(a_{\mathbf{K}}\pm a).

As
P_{1}
lies on
\mathbf{K}
we have

B_{1}=\frac{a_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}\pm c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}}{a_{\mathbf{K}}\pm c_{\mathbf{K}}\cdot\cos\varphi},

in which the sign in the numerator is
+
when
\mathbf{K}
is a hyperbolic branch, and is
-
when
\mathbf{K}
is an ellipse; the sign in the denominator is
-
for
\mathbf{H}_{F}
and is
+
for the other two cases.

So we can now write

k=\dfrac{(4(a_{\mathbf{K}}\pm a)^{2}-R^{2})(a_{\mathbf{K}}\pm c_{\mathbf{K}}\cdot\cos\varphi)}{2(a_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}\pm c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2})}\pm 2(a_{\mathbf{K}}\pm a)

and thus the equation of the line
\ell(F_{1}F_{2})
can be written as

\Bigl(x{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}+}\dfrac{(4(a_{\mathbf{K}}\pm a)^{2}-R^{2})c_{\mathbf{K}}}{2(a_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}\pm c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2})}\Bigr)\cos\varphi+y\,\sin\varphi+\dfrac{a_{\mathbf{K}}(4(a_{\mathbf{K}}\pm a)^{2}-R^{2})}{2(a_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}\pm c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2})}\pm 2(a_{\mathbf{K}}\pm a)=0,

in which the choice of the sign is the same in the expressions
(a_{\mathbf{K}}\pm a)
.

Now if we write the above equation as

(x-x_{0})\cos\varphi+y\sin\varphi+r_{0}=0

then this line, and as well as any of the other lines
\ell(F_{i}F_{i+1})\dots
, is tangent to the circle
S(x_{0},r_{0})
centered at
x_{0}
with radius
|r_{0}|
, for which both
x_{0}
and
r_{0}
are uniquely determined by the reflection boundary
\mathbf{K}
and the fixed conserved quantities
(a,R)
.

∎

3.2
The Foci and Foci-caustic Circles: The
(\mathbf{E},\mathcal{E})
Case

We consider the
(\mathbf{E},\mathcal{E})
case in more details. Remind that as focused ellipses intersect at most at two points, the particle may move either inside or outside
\mathbf{E}
and leads to the same dynamics up to a time reversal
[
14
]
.

3.2.1
The Expressions of
x_{0},r_{0}
and the Parameter Range

We have

x_{0}=-\dfrac{(4(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})^{2}-R^{2})c_{\mathbf{K}}}{2(a_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}-c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2})},\,~r_{0}=\dfrac{(4a_{\mathbf{K}}(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})^{2}-a_{\mathbf{K}}R^{2})}{2(a_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}-c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2})}+2(a-a_{\mathbf{K}}).

(3)

Once the elliptic boundary
\mathbf{K}
of type
(\mathbf{E})
is given, we have

Proposition 3.2
.

To effectively define a Kepler billiard system, the conserved quantities
(a,R)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
has to satisfy

2a>a_{\mathbf{K}}-c_{\mathbf{K}}>0,\,\,|2a_{\mathbf{K}}-2a|\leq R\leq 2a+2c_{\mathbf{K}}.

For fixed
a_{\mathbf{K}}>c_{\mathbf{K}}>0
, we call such a pair of
(a,R)

admissible
.

Proof.

The Hill’s region of the Kepler problem with semi major axis
a
is the disk
D(F,2a)
centered at
F
, which has to contain some point
\mathbf{K}
and in particular its pericenter, which has distant
a_{\mathbf{K}}-c_{\mathbf{K}}
from
F
. We thus have the first condition.

Also the Hill’s region
D(F,2a)
has to contain the second orbital focus, so it has to intersect the foci circle
S(F^{\prime},R)
. This is equivalent to say that the closest point from
S(F^{\prime},R)
to
F
is in
D(F,2a)
. We thus get

-2a\leq 2c_{\mathbf{K}}-R\leq 2a

and thus

2(c_{\mathbf{K}}-a)<R<2(a+c_{\mathbf{K}}).

On a point of reflection
P
, if a corresponding second orbital focus is
F_{1}
, then we have by the two-foci definition of ellipses

|FP|+|F^{\prime}P|=2a_{\mathbf{K}},~~|FP|+|F_{1}P|=2a,

so we have

R=|F^{\prime}F_{1}|\geq 2|a-a_{\mathbf{K}}|.

As

c_{\mathbf{K}}-a<a_{\mathbf{K}}-a\leq|a-a_{\mathbf{K}}|

we get the second condition. The combined conditions are represented in Fig.
4
.
∎

This condition is also sufficient, in the sense that for any value of
(a,R)
satisfying Prop.
3.2
, there exist billiard trajectories of elliptic Keplerian orbits with these parameter values. This follows from the following analysis on orbits of the second foci, which asserts that for all these parameter values there are orbits of the second foci
\{F_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
and the following simple observation: If
F_{i}
and
F_{i+1}
are both real, then by the previous construction,
F_{i}
is reflected to
F_{i+1}
by the line perpendicular to
F_{i}F_{i+1}
and pass their middle point. Moreover by construction, this line is tangent to
\mathbf{K}
and thus in particular the corresponding elliptic Keplerian orbits are reflected to each other at the point of tangency. So the analysis in the sequel faithfully reflects the dynamics of the Kepler billiard system.

Gray regions displaying the admissible values (
a,R
) for physical reflections with the elliptic boundary.

3.2.2
Scenarios of the Two Circles

We fix an admissible
a>(a_{\mathbf{K}}-c_{\mathbf{K}})/2
and increase
R
from
0
. We came across different scenarios.

When
R=0
, we have

x_{0}=-\dfrac{2(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})^{2}c_{\mathbf{K}}}{(a_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}-c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2})},r_{0}=\dfrac{2a_{\mathbf{K}}(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})^{2}}{(a_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}-c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2})}+2(a-a_{\mathbf{K}}).

As
a_{\mathbf{K}}>c_{\mathbf{K}}
, we have
r_{0}>|x_{0}|
and thus the degenerate
S(F^{\prime},0)=F^{\prime}
lies inside
S(x_{0},r_{0})
, unless
a=a_{\mathbf{K}}
.

When
a\neq a_{\mathbf{K}}
, we increase
R
from
0
: When
R
is sufficiently small, the foci-caustic circle lies inside the foci-circle. This is the prohibited case, as no lines intersecting
S(F^{\prime},R)
can be tangent to
S(x_{0},r_{0})
.

This situation does not change till
R=2|a-a_{\mathbf{K}}|
, for which there hold
x_{0}=0,r_{0}=2(a-a_{K})>0
. In this case the two circles
S(F^{\prime},R)
and
S(x_{0},r_{0})
coincide. So we have
R>2|a-a_{\mathbf{K}}|
for admissible
R
as in Prop.
3.2
.

Moreover by (
3
), for
R>2|a-a_{\mathbf{K}}|
, always we have
x_{0}>0
.

For
R>2|a-a_{\mathbf{K}}|
, we consider the quantity

x_{0}+|r_{0}|-R.

If
r_{0}>0
, then this quantity is

x_{0}+r_{0}-R=\dfrac{(4(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})^{2}-R^{2})}{2(a_{\mathbf{K}}+c_{\mathbf{K}})}+2(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})-R

=\dfrac{(2(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})-R)(2(a+c_{\mathbf{K}})-R)}{2(a_{\mathbf{K}}+c_{\mathbf{K}})}.

If
r_{0}<0
, then it is

x_{0}-r_{0}-R=-\dfrac{(4(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})^{2}-R^{2})}{2(a_{\mathbf{K}}-c_{\mathbf{K}})}-2(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})-R

=-\dfrac{(2(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})+R)(2(a-c_{\mathbf{K}})-R)}{2(a_{\mathbf{K}}-c_{\mathbf{K}})}.

We distinguish two cases:

Case 1
:
a-c_{\mathbf{K}}\geq|a-a_{\mathbf{K}}|
, or equivalently
2a\geq a_{\mathbf{K}}+c_{\mathbf{K}}
, since we have
a_{\mathbf{K}}>c_{\mathbf{K}}>0
.

In this case, we have
x_{0}+|r_{0}|<R
for
2|a-a_{\mathbf{K}}|<R<2(a-c_{\mathbf{K}})
. As
x_{0}>0
, geometrically this implies that
S(x_{0},r_{0})
lies inside
S(F^{\prime},R)
.

In the particular case
r_{0}=0
, the foci-caustic circle degenerates into a point, and the corresponding foci-orbit are
2
-periodic. This is given by

r_{0}:=\dfrac{(4a_{\mathbf{K}}(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})^{2}-a_{\mathbf{K}}R^{2})}{2(a_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}-c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2})}+2(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})=0,

so

R=R_{2}:=2\sqrt{(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})(a-c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}/a_{\mathbf{K}})}.

As
c_{\mathbf{K}}<a_{\mathbf{K}}
, we have
R_{2}>2|a-a_{\mathbf{K}}|
. We also have
R_{2}<2|a-c_{\mathbf{K}}|
, which follows from the inequality

(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})(a-c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}/a_{\mathbf{K}})<(a-c_{\mathbf{K}})^{2},

which after expansion is equivalent to

-a_{\mathbf{K}}-c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}/a_{\mathbf{K}}<-2c_{\mathbf{K}},

which holds true multiplying both sides by
a_{\mathbf{K}}
and then completing the square.

We thus have
2|a-a_{\mathbf{K}}|<R_{2}<2(a-c_{\mathbf{K}})
.

When
R=2(a-c_{\mathbf{K}})
, we have

r_{0}=\dfrac{(4a_{\mathbf{K}}(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})^{2}-4a_{\mathbf{K}}(a-c_{\mathbf{K}})^{2})}{2(a_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}-c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2})}+2(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})=\dfrac{2a(c_{\mathbf{K}}-a_{\mathbf{K}})}{a_{\mathbf{K}}+c_{\mathbf{K}}}<0,

x_{0}=\dfrac{2c_{\mathbf{K}}(2a-a_{\mathbf{K}}-c_{\mathbf{K}})}{a_{\mathbf{K}}+c_{\mathbf{K}}}.

So in this case we have
x_{0}-r_{0}=x_{0}+|r_{0}|=R
, meaning that the two circles are tangent to each other, with
S(x_{0},r_{0})
lies inside
S(F^{\prime},R)
.

Note that for this parameter value there exists a particular focal reflection property
[
14
]
: The ellipses tends monotonically to the degenerate limit of a collinear bouncing orbit at the pericenter of the boundary ellipse along its major axis. Now this property has an almost trivial geometrical proof: The iterated (oriented) foci-chords are well-defined outside the point of tangency of these two circles, and tend to the point of tangency.

For
2(a-c_{\mathbf{K}})<R<2(a+c_{\mathbf{K}})
, the two circles

4.2
Determinant and different cases of real forms

The sign of the normalized determinant of the quadratic equation

For

The frequency of the mapping is given by

For almost all admissible choices of the orbital semi major axis

With the explicit formula it is possible to do concrete computations. For our purpose we are nevertheless content to have the following non-explicit, “soft” argument.

5.1

We follow the classical argument presented in

As in

Proposition 5.1

In the

Proof.

We transform the problem into the equivalent mechanical billiard problem of an attractive Hooke center lying at the center of an elliptic reflection wall, still denoted by

\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C},z\mapsto z^{2}.

See

17

14

for details. In particular, the negative of the energy of the Kepler problem appears as the string constant in the corresponding Hooke problem having normalized energy 1.
The relevant corresponding cyclic configurations space is the symmetric cyclic configuration space

\tilde{G}(\mathbf{E},n)=\{z_{1},-z_{1},z_{2},-z_{2},\dots,z_{n},-z_{n},z_{i}^{2}\neq z_{j}^{2}\,\,\,\forall\,\,i\neq j\},

in which

The energy being given, by assumption

lies entirely in the corresponding Hill’s region of the planar Hooke problem. Thus the corresponding Jacobi metric is a non-degenerate Riemannian metric in the region bounded by

, and the billiard system is equivalent up to a time change to the billiard system defined by geodesics of the Jacobi metric. We denote by

and thus gives an

corresponds to such an

∎

For

-periodic orbits the condition is

The second equation is cubic in

and it does not seem easy to make a detailed analysis.

The equation

R=|x_{0}|,

can be solved explicitly as

R=\frac{a_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}-c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}}{c_{\mathbf{K}}}\cdot\left(1+\sqrt{1+\frac{4c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})^{2}}{(a_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}-c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2})^{2}}}\right).

As the parameter range is given by

2a>a_{\mathbf{K}}-c_{\mathbf{K}}>0,\,\,|2a_{\mathbf{K}}-2a|\leq R\leq 2a+2c_{\mathbf{K}},

We have

|2a_{\mathbf{K}}-2a|\leq\frac{a_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}-c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}}{c_{\mathbf{K}}}\cdot\left(1+\sqrt{1+\frac{4c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}(a-a_{\mathbf{K}})^{2}}{(a_{\mathbf{K}}^{2}-c_{\mathbf{K}}^{2})^{2}}}\right)\leq 2a+2c_{\mathbf{K}}.

For the inequality on the left, after squaring both sides we easily see that it always holds. For the inequality on the right, after squaring both sides and simplifying the expressions, we obtain the much simplified equivalent form giving restriction for the shape of the reflection wall:

3\,c_{\mathbf{K}}\geq a_{\mathbf{K}}.

Appendix A
Computation of integrals

In the computation of periods there appear the integrals of the type

\int_{a}^{+\infty}\dfrac{d\tilde{t}}{\sqrt{4\tilde{t}^{3}-g_{2}\tilde{t}-g_{3}}}=\int_{a}^{+\infty}\dfrac{d\tilde{t}}{\sqrt{4(\tilde{t}-e_{1})(\tilde{t}-e_{2})(\tilde{t}-e_{3})}}.

Here we indicate a standard procedure to reduces this computation to Jacobi’s elliptic integrals, see e.g.
[
12
]
. We shall not need to express the periods as well as other related integrals in elliptic integrals but this might be useful for analysis of different questions.

Indeed we can set
\hat{t}=\tilde{t}-e_{1}
to transform the above integral into the form

\int_{a-e_{1}}^{+\infty}\dfrac{d\tilde{t}}{\sqrt{4\hat{t}(\hat{t}+e_{1}-e_{2})(\hat{t}+e_{1}-e_{3})}}.

Further we set

\hat{t}=\dfrac{e_{2}-e_{1}}{\check{t}}
so
d\hat{t}=-\dfrac{e_{2}-e_{1}}{\check{t}^{2}}d\check{t}
to transform the integral into the form

\int_{0}^{\frac{e_{2}-e_{1}}{a-e_{1}}}\dfrac{(e_{2}-e_{1})d\check{t}}{\check{t}^{1/2}\sqrt{4(e_{2}-e_{1})((e_{2}-e_{1})(1-\check{t}))((e_{2}-e_{1})+(e_{1}-e_{3})\check{t})}}.

Choosing the branch of the square root for
(e_{2}-e_{1})^{1/2}
as the analytic continuation of the positive square root on the positive real axis and further with the coordinate change
\check{t}=s^{2}
we get

\dfrac{1}{(e_{2}-e_{1})^{1/2}}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\frac{e_{2}-e_{1}}{a-e_{1}}}}\dfrac{ds}{\sqrt{(1-s^{2})\Bigl(1-\dfrac{e_{1}-e_{3}}{e_{1}-e_{2}}s^{2}\Bigr)}}=\dfrac{1}{(e_{2}-e_{1})^{1/2}}F\Bigl(\sqrt{\frac{e_{2}-e_{1}}{a-e_{1}}},k\Bigr),

in which
F(x,k)
denotes the incomplete elliptic integral of the first kind in Jacobi form, and

k^{2}=\dfrac{e_{1}-e_{3}}{e_{1}-e_{2}},

which allows to evaluate the period, the shift and the frequency in terms of Jacobi’s elliptic integrals.
