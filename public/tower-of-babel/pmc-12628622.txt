Assessment of depression, anxiety, and stress levels among undergraduate dental students in Türkiye during endodontics education: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Betul Aycan Uysal, Elif Burcak Katircioglu, Azra Tuana Donmez, Sude Gul Kalkan, Mohammad Javad Ranjbar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental education is highly demanding, and endodontics is considered one of the most stressful disciplines due to its technical complexity. Psychological distress, including depression, anxiety, and stress, may negatively affect students’ performance and well-being. This study aimed to assess these psychological outcomes among dental students in Türkiye during endodontic training.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted using the validated Turkish version of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) among undergraduate dental students. Descriptive statistics and chi-square tests were applied to analyze associations with age, sex, year of study, and university type (p < 0.05).ResultsA total of 448 students participated. Female students reported significantly higher levels of stress and anxiety (p = 0.001). Older and more advanced students, especially in the fourth year, had significantly increased levels of depression, anxiety, and stress (p < 0.05). In contrast, second-year students consistently reported lower distress levels. No significant differences were found between public and private universities (p > 0.05).ConclusionsDepression, anxiety, and stress increase as students progress through dental education, with marked gender differences. The findings highlight the heightened vulnerability of students during endodontic training and underscore the need for targeted psychological support during this critical phase.Keywords: Depression, Anxiety, Stress, Dental students, Endodontic training, DASS-21

Background

Dental education demands not only academic rigor but also intensive clinical training, coupled with the development of fine motor skills and hand–eye coordination, to ensure accurate and effective patient care [1]. Among the various disciplines within dentistry, endodontics is consistently identified by students as one of the most stressful and technically challenging fields. This perception stems from multiple factors, including the morphological complexity of the root canal system, the critical nature of treatment outcomes, the intricacy of endodontic procedures, and a general lack of confidence among students [2, 3].

In addition to these technical and cognitive demands, students frequently encounter difficulties such as patient absenteeism, delays, inconsistencies in preclinical and clinical instruction, variability in technical approaches, and insufficient or inconsistent faculty supervision during procedures [4]. In the preclinical phase, students are introduced to complex manual techniques via artificial models, often under strict time constraints and continuous performance assessments. For those who have not yet developed adequate manual dexterity or confidence, this environment can lead to increased anxiety and stress [5]. Upon entering the clinical phase, students may experience a heightened psychological burden due to fears of making irreversible errors in real patients, the unpredictability of clinical situations, and pressure to meet faculty expectations [6].

In Türkiye, preclinical endodontic training begins in the second year of dental faculties, during which students perform endodontic procedures on extracted human teeth or resin models with root canals. In the third year, training progresses to performing endodontic treatments on phantom heads designed to simulate the patient’s oral cavity. Clinical endodontic training typically commences in the fourth year and continues into the fifth year, which is the final year. Students actively participate in patient care during their fourth and fifth years of education and are expected to independently perform all stages of a root canal treatment from start to finish. Moreover, differences between public and private dental faculties may influence the depth of clinical experience. Public dental faculties often have higher patient volumes, which can provide students with broader clinical exposure. In contrast, private universities may offer more individualized supervision but potentially fewer patient interactions. These institutional variations can affect students’ clinical competence, confidence, and psychological well-being, which is an important consideration in interpreting study outcomes.

Some studies have demonstrated that dental students are at increased risk for psychological distress compared with their peers in other health-related disciplines [7, 8]. A range of psychometric instruments have been employed to evaluate such conditions, including the Perceived Stress Scale [9], the General Health Questionnaire [10], and the Student Stress Inventory [11]. Among these scales, the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21), developed by Lovibond and Lovibond [12], is widely utilized to assess depression, anxiety, and stress within student populations. Several studies have specifically applied this scale to dental students [13–16].

To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has employed the DASS-21 to evaluate psychological distress specifically among students undertaking endodontic training. Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess the levels of depression, anxiety, and stress in undergraduate dental students during both the preclinical (2nd and 3rd years of study) and clinical (4th and 5th years of study) phases of endodontic education. The alternative hypothesis of the present study was that students in higher academic years of endodontic training would exhibit increased levels of depression, anxiety, and stress.

Materials and methods

This survey study was performed at the Health Science University, Faculty of Hamidiye Dentistry, Department of Endodontics. Ethical approval was obtained from the xxx University Ethics Committee (no: 25–297). Clinical trial number was not applicable. A power analysis was conducted with the program package G*Power 3.1.2 (University of Duesseldorf, Duesseldorf, Germany) to determine the sample size. An alpha level of 0.05 and a power of 0.80 were assumed when one-tailed tests were used to establish the significance of correlations [16]. The results revealed that 295 respondents were needed. The survey was distributed through a cover letter explaining the study’s objectives and providing a link to the survey hosted on Google Forms (Google, USA). The letter also emphasized that participation was voluntary. The survey link was distributed to the endodontics departments of 12 dental faculties (including both public and private institutions) across Türkiye. Faculty members were asked to share the survey with students currently enrolled in endodontic training who met the inclusion criteria. A total of 2080 dental students were sent. Data collection spanned a three-month period, from March to May 2025.

The inclusion criteria were as follows:

Dental students who had completed preclinical or clinical endodontic training at most within the past week or were still undergoing preclinical or clinical endodontic training (in order to determine whether their levels of stress, anxiety, and depression were related to their recent educational experiences);

Dental students who had completed preclinical or clinical endodontic training at most within the past week or were still undergoing preclinical or clinical endodontic training (in order to determine whether their levels of stress, anxiety, and depression were related to their recent educational experiences);

Enrollment in the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, or 5th year of the dental program;

Enrollment in the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, or 5th year of the dental program;

Age between 18 and 25 years;

Age between 18 and 25 years;

Did not undergo cognitive or behavioral therapy;

Did not undergo cognitive or behavioral therapy;

Not currently using antidepressant medications.

Not currently using antidepressant medications.

The exclusion criteria for this study were as follows: students who had not received preclinical or clinical endodontic training within the past week; students enrolled in the first year of the dental program, those who had already graduated, or those not actively participating in the academic curriculum; individuals under the age of 18 or over 25; students currently undergoing cognitive or behavioral therapy; those using antidepressant, anxiolytic, or other psychotropic medications; individuals with a previously diagnosed psychiatric disorder that could influence their responses to the DASS-21; and those who submitted incomplete or inconsistently answered surveys.

The survey consisted of two parts: the first collected demographic data, including age, sex, year of study, and type of institution (private or public university); the second part comprised the Turkish version of the DASS-21 survey. Although the DASS-21 is a general psychometric instrument, in this study, students were instructed to respond based on their recent experiences during preclinical or clinical endodontic training. The DASS-21 includes three subscales designed to measure self-reported negative emotional states of depression, anxiety, and stress experienced during the previous week. Scores for each subscale were categorized into severity levels—normal, mild, moderate, severe, and extremely severe—according to cutoff points suggested by Lovibond [12]. The validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the DASS-21 have been previously established [17].

Statistical analysis

The SPSS (IBM SPSS for Windows, ver.26) statistical package program was used for the analyses.

Descriptive statistics for the variables in the study are expressed as numbers (n) and percentages (%). The chi-square test was used to assess relationships between categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study evaluated the levels of depression, anxiety, and stress among undergraduate dental students during their endodontic training. To the best of our knowledge, no previous research has specifically used the DASS-21 scale to assess psychological distress in endodontics education. The DASS-21 is a standardized psychometric instrument designed to measure depression, anxiety, and stress in the general population, but it is not specific to endodontic training. In the present study, the survey was not modified or adapted to reflect endodontic procedures. Participants were instructed to complete the survey based on their recent experiences related to endodontic training.

A study in Australia showed that dental students reported higher levels of depression and anxiety than students in other health-related fields [13]. This outcome emphasizes the universal nature of professional stressors in dentistry. Dental students often begin one-on-one patient care early in their education. This responsibility comes before their clinical skills are fully developed, increasing anxiety levels [18]. Many students view endodontic procedures as the most complex and stressful among dental treatments [4]. For these reasons, this study focused on assessing depression, anxiety, and stress during endodontic training.

Babar et al. reported that dental students in public universities in Malaysia had higher stress levels than those in private institutions [19]. In contrast, our study found no statistically significant differences in stress, anxiety, or depression scores between students from public and private universities (p >0.05). This suggests that psychological distress among dental students may not depend on the type of institution. The similarity of endodontic curricula and widespread use of a standard educational model among endodontic instructors in Türkiye may explain this result. Additionally, the absence of statistically significant differences may be explained by compensatory institutional factors. For instance, students in public universities are typically exposed to a higher volume of patients, potentially alleviating stress associated with fulfilling clinical quotas. In contrast, private university students may benefit from more favorable clinical infrastructure and increased instructor availability, offering more individualized guidance. These contrasting institutional advantages may have served as balancing factors.

Previous research has shown that clinical dental students experience significantly higher stress levels than those in preclinical phases [20]. This finding partially aligns with the results of this study. Notably, fourth-year students reported the highest levels of depression, anxiety, and stress. This year marks a substantial transition into intensive clinical training. Students are expected to perform direct patient care and execute complex endodontic procedures in most dental faculties across Türkiye. The elevated levels of psychological distress observed in this group, especially within the extremely severe category, highlight the emotional burden associated with clinical responsibility, performance pressure, and the technical complexity of endodontics. Accordingly, the alternative hypothesis of the present study was accepted, except for the levels of depression, anxiety, and stress in fifth-year students. Interestingly, a slight reduction in distress was noted among final-year students. This decrease may be attributed to factors such as increased clinical competence and greater familiarity with endodontic procedures gained during earlier years. Fifth-year students are often more experienced in endodontics and thus better prepared to handle challenges. They tend to perform treatments more efficiently and manage clinical patient quotas with greater ease. This can alleviate some of the stress related to clinical workload. The reduction may also reflect psychological adaptation to academic demands or a sense of relief and motivation due to the proximity of graduation. However, it is also plausible that some students experience emotional disengagement or burnout. This may paradoxically result in lower self-reported stress levels despite ongoing internal strain. Moreover, one might expect psychological distress to increase in the final year due to career-related concerns, national examinations, and uncertainties about professional life after graduation. This apparent contradiction underscores the complex and dynamic nature of psychological well-being during dental education.

Importantly, high levels of psychological distress were observed across all academic years. Notably, severe levels of anxiety and stress were reported by more than 20% of participants. These results indicate that such psychological challenges are not limited to specific phases of dental education but appear to be pervasive throughout the curriculum. Potential contributing factors include cognitive overload, lack of adequate time for rest and reflection, pressure to meet academic deadlines, and uncertainty in clinical decision-making.

These findings highlight the need for continuous, structured mental health support throughout all years of dental training. Educators may consider implementing more process-oriented, student-centered approaches to reduce psychological strain.

It is important to acknowledge that students from different academic years (2nd to 5th) were included in this study, and their overall academic workload and external stressors may vary. While the inclusion criteria ensured recent exposure to endodontic training, concurrent courses or responsibilities unrelated to endodontics may have influenced the DASS-21 scores. This represents a potential confounding factor and should be considered when interpreting the results.

Students aged 21 years and older presented moderate to extremely severe stress levels (p = 0.019). Fourth-year students demonstrated the greatest psychological burden across all categories, which may partly explain why older students reported significantly greater stress, anxiety, and depression—an expected outcome given their increasing academic and clinical responsibilities.

In terms of sex, Lugassy et al. reported no significant differences in depression, anxiety, or stress between male and female students [21]. However, Basudan et al. [15], along with Uraz et al. [22] and Aboalshamat et al. [23], reported that female students were more prone to psychological distress. In line with these findings, the present study revealed that female students experienced significantly greater stress and anxiety than male students did (p = 0.001), although no statistically significant difference was observed in depression scores (p = 0.193). This may suggest that while female students experience greater stress and anxiety, they might be more capable of managing these emotions before they progress to depression. Additionally, the greater proportion of female respondents (67.9%) than male students (32.1%) may have influenced these outcomes. Biological and social factors, such as hormonal fluctuations, menstrual cycles, and gender-specific cultural expectations, should also be considered when these findings are interpreted.

These results underscore the importance of implementing comprehensive mental health support systems within dental faculties. The demanding nature of dental education can lead to burnout due to its continuous need for performance, precision, and attention to detail. Universities should promote healthier academic environments by offering resources such as time management workshops, stress-reduction programs, mindfulness training, and coping strategies tailored to the unique challenges faced by dental students [24]. Regular mental health screenings, stress management seminars, and access to psychological counseling services may also serve as effective interventions [25].

This study has several limitations. The relatively low response rate (21.5%) constitutes a major limitation and may reduce the representativeness of the findings. Non-participation may have been due to academic workload, scheduling conflicts, or avoidance associated with psychological distress. Students experiencing higher distress may have been more motivated to respond, potentially inflating prevalence estimates, whereas those experiencing severe distress may have avoided participation, leading to underestimation. Consequently, the actual levels of depression, anxiety, and stress among dental students may differ from those reported here. In addition, this study did not investigate the specific endodontic procedures that students found most challenging, focusing instead on the overall psychological experience during training. Lifestyle-related factors, such as housing conditions and personal habits, which can significantly affect emotional well-being, were also not assessed. Future studies should address these contextual variables, include procedure-specific analyses of stressors in endodontics education, and employ longitudinal designs to provide deeper insights into how mental health challenges evolve throughout the dental curriculum.

Conclusion

Compared with male students, female students experienced significantly greater stress and anxiety, highlighting the need for gender-sensitive mental health support. No significant differences were found across university types, indicating that personal and academic factors outweigh institutional factors. Older students and those in higher years—especially fourth-year students—reported greater psychological distress, reflecting increased demands during clinical training. Second-year students presented lower levels of depression, anxiety, and stress, suggesting that the psychological burden increases with academic progress.
