Risk factors and clinical outcomes of late-onset severe ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome in advanced maternal age women undergoing fresh embryo transfer
Authors: Ying Huang, Jiangman Gao, Tian Tian, Lixue Chen, Rong Li, Rui Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSince young age is an established risk factor for ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS), most research has consequently focused on early-onset OHSS in this patient population. With increasing fertility demands among advanced maternal age (AMA) populations, their reproductive health and assisted reproductive outcomes have garnered significant clinical attention. However, there are few reports on late-onset OHSS in AMA patients, and its risk factors and impact on pregnancy outcomes remain unknown.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 13,040 fresh embryo transfer cycles with positive serum hCG results in women aged ≥ 35 years from January 2010 to December 2021. Patients were stratified based on the occurrence of late-onset severe OHSS. Pregnancy and neonatal outcomes were analyzed and multivariable regression analysis was used to identify risk factors of OHSS in advanced-age women.ResultsWithin this cohort, 142 patients were diagnosed with late-onset severe OHSS. Both long and ultra-long COH protocols were associated with a significantly higher risk of OHSS compared to the antagonist protocol. Lower total Gn dosage, higher trigger-day E2 level and greater number of good quality embryos were also independent risk factors. Patients with OHSS had significantly lower biochemical pregnancy rate (1.4% vs. 6.7%, P < 0.05), ectopic pregnancy incidence (0 vs. 3.4%, P < 0.05) and early miscarriage rate (10.0% vs. 19.0%, P < 0.05), but higher clinical pregnancy rate (98.6% vs. 93.3%, P < 0.05), and live birth rate (84.3% vs. 73.0%, P < 0.05). The Neonatal and delivery outcomes in singleton live births were comparable between the two groups. The clinical severity of OHSS during hospitalization (manifested as hepatic dysfunction, hypoalbuminemia, ascites, or pleural effusion) showed no significant impact on live birth outcomes.ConclusionElevated trigger-day E2 levels and increased good-quality embryos independently predict late-onset severe OHSS risk in AMA women, without compromising pregnancy outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01848-3.Keywords: OHSS, Advanced maternal age, Fresh embryo transfer, Independent risk factors, Pregnancy outcomes

Introduction

Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) is an iatrogenic complication arising from controlled ovarian hyperstimulation (COH) during assisted reproductive technology (ART), characterized by cystic ovarian enlargement and fluid shift from the intravascular to the third space due to increased capillary permeability. Clinical features include abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, ascites, edema, and, in severe cases, hydrothorax or hydropericardium. OHSS is classified as mild, moderate, or severe based on symptom severity and laboratory findings [1]. According to the occurrence time, OHSS is further categorized into early-onset and late-onset forms. Early-onset OHSS, typically triggered by exogenous human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), occurs within 9 days post-hCG administration, while late-onset OHSS, caused by endogenous hCG produced by the trophoblasts of implanted embryos, and develops 10 or more days post-hCG. The latter is less predictable, tends to be more severe, and often requires hospitalization [2, 3].

Risk factors for OHSS include polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), prior OHSS history, high anti-müllerian hormone (AMH) levels, young age, low body mass index (BMI), elevated serum estradiol (E2) levels and retrieval of >15 ~ 18 oocytes during and after COH [4]. Due to the higher incidence of OHSS in younger patients [5], most existing research has focused on this population, resulting in a lack of systematic analysis of independent risk factors for OHSS in advanced maternal age (AMA) women (≥ 35 years old). As childbearing age is postponed, the demand for ART in AMA patients continues to increase. The reproductive physiological characteristics of advanced-age patients differ significantly from those of younger individuals. As women age, their ovarian reserve gradually diminishes. Declining ovarian reserve leads to reduced follicular recruitment, making OHSS less likely to occur theoretically. However, some AMA women still exhibit an exaggerated response to ovarian stimulation due to high doses of exogenous gonadotropins (Gn) or individual sensitivity differences, leading to severe OHSS [6, 7]. AMA is often associated with metabolic abnormalities, such as insulin resistance, and impaired vascular function [8], which may further aggravate OHSS progression. Notably, one study found that patients over 40 hospitalized with severe OHSS were at higher risk of life-threatening complications [9]. To prevent severe OHSS in AMA women, it is necessary to re-evaluate whether traditional OHSS high-risk factors are applicable to this population.

Elective freeze-all strategy is widely adopted in ART treatment for OHSS prevention. Theoretically, with increasing age, oocyte quality and developmental potential may decline [10, 11], and embryos may become less tolerant to cryopreservation-induced damage. Whether the “freeze-all” strategy, aimed at preventing OHSS, is suitable for AMA patients remain uncertain. The aim of this retrospective cohort study is to analyze data on late-onset severe OHSS in AMA patients to identify potential risk factors and assess the clinical outcomes. The findings are expected to provide a theoretical foundation for optimizing clinical decision-making in this growing patient population.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

This study retrospectively analyzed the medical records of patients aged ≥ 35 years who underwent in vitro fertilization/intracytoplasmic sperm injection (IVF/ICSI) with fresh embryo transfer at the Center for Reproductive Medicine, Peking University Third Hospital between January 2010 and December 2021(n = 41,476). Only patients who achieved a positive hCG result following IVF-/ICSI-fresh ET cycles were included (n = 14,536). Cycles involving in vitro maturation, mild stimulation protocol, natural cycle, luteal phase stimulation, or rescue ICSI were excluded. A total of 13,040 cycles met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Within this cohort, 142 patients were diagnosed with severe late-onset OHSS based on clinical manifestations of ascites, hydrothorax, or dyspnea, combined with abnormal laboratory findings in hematocrit, creatinine, or alanine aminotransferase levels, according to previously established diagnostic criteria [12, 13]. The remaining “non-severe OHSS” patients were defined as the control group, encompassing both those with no OHSS and those with mild or moderate forms, as the retrospective design and potential underreporting due to variations in symptom tolerance precluded reliable distinction between non-severe OHSS cases and unaffected patients.

COH, oocyte retrieval, fertilization and fresh ET

COH protocol and initial Gn dosages were individualized based on comprehensive clinical assessment, including patient age and ovarian reserve parameters. The study incorporated four distinct stimulation protocols: long GnRH agonist protocol, ultra-long GnRH agonist protocol, GnRH antagonist protocol, and short protocol.

In long GnRH agonist protocol, pituitary down-regulation was induced during the mid-luteal phase through either daily subcutaneous administration of 0.1 mg triptorelin acetate, or a single intramuscular injection of 1.3–1.8 mg triptorelin depot. Successful suppression was confirmed via serum hormone assessment 14 days post-initiation. In ultralong GnRH agonist protocol, a single 3.75 mg dose of either triptorelin or leuprorelin acetate was administered intramuscularly on menstrual cycle day 1. Following 28–30 days suppression period, ovarian stimulation was initiated using recombinant FSH (rFSH) or human menopausal gonadotropin (HMG), with doses tailored to individual patient characteristics. In GnRH antagonist protocol, ovarian stimulation commenced on cycle day 2 with daily rFSH injections. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone antagonist co-treatment (ganirelix or cetrorelix acetate) was initiated on stimulation day 5. Antagonist administration was maintained through the day of final oocyte maturation trigger with human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG). In the short protocol, ovulation induction injections are typically initiated on day 2 or 3 of the menstrual cycle and are continued for approximately 7 to 10 days. Follicular development was monitored through serial transvaginal ultrasonography performed at 2–3-day intervals, with subsequent dose adjustments based on ovarian response.

Final oocyte maturation was triggered when at least two leading follicles reached ≥ 18 mm in diameter. Transvaginal ultrasound-guided oocyte retrieval was subsequently performed 36–38 h post-trigger to optimize oocyte yield and maturity. Oocyte fertilization was performed either through conventional IVF or ICSI based on sperm quality parameters. Three to five days later, embryonic development and quality were assessed, and 2–3 cleavage embryos or 1–2 blastocysts were transferred (a maximum of 2 embryos were transferred after 2014). Good-quality embryos were defined as 5–8 cells with less than 30% fragments and of even size. In clinical practice, trigger-day E2 levels and oocyte yield serve as critical indicators for elective embryo freezing to prevent OHSS. The established criteria include trigger-day E2 ≥ 15,000 pmol/L, retrieval of ≥ 20 oocytes, or a combination of trigger-day E2 ≥ 10,000 pmol/L and ≥ 15 oocytes retrieved.

Luteal phase support

For fresh embryo transfer cycles, standardized luteal phase support (consists of daily 90 mg vaginal progesterone gel plus oral dydrogesterone 10 mg twice daily) was initiated on the day of oocyte retrieval and maintained through the 10th week of gestation.

Diagnosis and treatment for late-onset severe OHSS

The diagnostic criteria for late-onset severe OHSS and its treatment was performed according to the guideline reported by American Society for Reproductive Medicine in 2016 [12]. The management of OHSS followed established clinical protocols, involving intravenous fluid resuscitation, administration of human albumin, anticoagulation therapy, and therapeutic paracentesis when indicated. During hospitalization, routine monitoring of blood parameters, liver and renal function, ascites, and pleural effusion was performed regularly to assess the severity of the condition and guide prognosis.

Normal hepatic function was established as serum ALT and AST levels < 40 U/L, with transaminase elevations categorized as mild (< 5×upper limit of normal value [ULN]), moderate (5–15×ULN), or severe (> 15×ULN). Hypoalbuminemia is classified based on serum albumin levels (mild: <60 g/L; moderate: <30 g/L; severe: <20 g/L). Fluid accumulation was quantified ultrasonographically: ascites severity was determined by distribution and maximal depth (mild: 1–2 regions < 2 cm; moderate: 2–5 regions ≤ 5 cm; severe: more than 5 regions > 5 cm), while pleural effusion volume (mL) [90×maximal height (cm)] was stratified as mild (< 500 mL), moderate (500–1000 mL), or severe (> 1000 mL).

Outcome measures and definition

Biochemical pregnancy was defined as a serum hCG level ≥ 25 mIU/mL on the 13th-14th day after ET, without subsequent ultrasonographic evidence of a gestational sac. Clinical pregnancy confirmation required ultrasonographic visualization of a gestational sac, while ectopic pregnancy was diagnosed when implantation occurred outside the uterine cavity. Pregnancy loss was categorized as miscarriage before 24 gestational weeks, further stratified into early pregnancy loss (< 12 weeks) and late pregnancy loss (12–24 weeks). Multiple pregnancy was identified by the detection of two or more gestational sacs on ultrasound, with its incidence derived from the proportion of multiple pregnancies among all clinically confirmed pregnancies. The fetal reduction rate represented the ratio of reduction procedures performed to the total multiple pregnancies. Live birth was defined as delivery of a viable neonate at or beyond 24 weeks’ gestation. The live birth rate (LBR) was calculated as live births per clinical pregnancy. Preterm delivery referred to birth occurring before 37 completed gestational weeks, with its frequency calculated among live births. Low birth weight was classified as a neonatal weight below 2500 g at delivery, whereas macrosomia referred to a birth weight exceeding 4000 g (applied only to singleton live births).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed with SPSS version 26.0 software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were expressed as percentages n (%). Normality assumptions were assessed for all continuous variables, with normally distributed data analyzed using one-way ANOVA, whereas non-normally distributed variables were examined using the Kruskal-Wallis nonparametric test. Intergroup comparisons were conducted using chi-square tests, with Pearson’s chi-square applied when all expected cell counts exceeded 5, and Fisher’s exact test employed when any expected cell count was below 5. To account for potential confounding effects from baseline characteristics, we performed adjusted multivariable logistic regression analyses. The selection of covariates was based on significant findings from univariate screening. Results were expressed as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to determine a cutoff value to accurately predict OHSS occurrence. Statistical significance was established at a P value of < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics between the late-onset OHSS group and the control group

The prevalence rate of late-onset severe OHSS was 0.34% (142/41,476) in this population. Comparation of demographic and clinical characteristics (Table 1) revealed that women in the late-onset severe OHSS group (referred to as the OHSS group following) were significantly younger than controls (36.56 ± 1.75 vs. 37.32 ± 2.25, P < 0.001), with a higher proportion of PCOS diagnoses (13.4% vs. 6.2%, P < 0.001). Regarding ovarian reserve parameters, the OHSS group demonstrated significantly lower baseline FSH (5.92 ± 2.30 vs. 7.81 ± 6.80, P < 0.001) and E2 levels (146.15 ± 97.46 vs. 199.48 ± 422.5, P < 0.05), higher LH (4.27 ± 3.26 vs. 3.63 ± 2.96, P < 0.05) and AMH levels (3.84 ± 1.96 vs. 2.13 ± 1.96, P < 0.001), and higher AFC (12.54 ± 5.16 vs. 8.89 ± 4.69, P < 0.001) compared to controls. In terms of stimulation protocols, the OHSS group showed significantly lower utilization of antagonist and short protocols, but greater preference for long protocols (P < 0.05). At trigger day, significantly elevated E2 (11479 ± 5788 vs. 7557 ± 5053, P < 0.001) and progesterone levels (2.84 ± 1.47 vs. 2.62 ± 19.25, P < 0.05) were observed in OHSS group, and LH levels remained comparable between groups. Compared to the control group, the total Gn doses used in the OHSS group was significantly lower (2522 ± 969 vs. 3271 ± 1293, P < 0.001), and the rates of half-IVF fertilization was higher (4.9% vs. 1.8%, P < 0.05). Laboratory outcomes significantly favored the OHSS group, with higher oocyte yield (14.48 ± 4.84 vs. 9.63 ± 5.15, P < 0.001), 2PN counts (9.39 ± 3.76 vs. 5.84 ± 3.52, P < 0.001), transferable embryos (6.92 ± 4.13 vs. 3.79 ± 2.96, P < 0.001), and good quality embryos (6.88 ± 3.83 vs. 4.07 ± 2.96, P < 0.001). Numbers of embryos transferred after 2014 was significantly more in the OHSS group than that in the control group (1.93 ± 0.25 vs. 1.85 ± 0.36, P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in infertility duration, primary infertility rates, BMI, stimulation duration, or transferred embryo stage between two groups.

Potential risk factors for late-onset severe OHSS

Variables with statistically significant differences in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate regression analysis. Due to substantial missing data in AMH measurements and its strong correlation with AFC, only AFC was retained in the multivariable logistic regression analysis. The final model adjusted for the following variables: maternal age, basal serum levels of FSH, E2, and LH; AFC; COH protocol; Gn dosage; trigger-day E2 and LH levels; insemination method; number of oocytes retrieved, good-quality embryos, and embryos transferred; and causes of infertility (with or without PCOS). For analysis, trigger-day E2 levels and Gn dosage were log-transformed prior to statistical evaluation.

The results showed that ovarian stimulation protocol, total Gn dosage, E2 levels on trigger day, and number of high-quality embryos were the independent risk factors for OHSS occurrence. Compared to the antagonist protocol, both the long and ultra-long protocols demonstrated significantly higher OHSS incidence rates (aOR 1.86, 95%CI 1.11–3.12, P < 0.05; aOR 2.60, 95%CI 1.14–4.73, P < 0.05, respectively), whereas the short protocol showed a markedly reduced OHSS occurrence (aOR 0.19, 95%CI 0.05–0.82, P < 0.05). In advanced-age patients, lower Gn requirements, elevated trigger-day E2 levels, and greater number of good quality embryos show increased susceptibility to OHSS (aOR 0.32, 95%CI 0.20–0.50, P < 0.001; aOR 2.28, 95%CI 1.54–3.37, P < 0.001; aOR 1.10, 95%CI 1.03–1.16, P < 0.05, respectively) (Table 2). Besides, as risk profiles might differ across age groups, we stratified the population into two subgroups: 35–37 years (n = 107 in OHSS group) and ≥ 38 years (n = 35 in OHSS group). In both subgroups, lower total Gn dose, higher trigger-day E₂ levels, and greater good-quality embryos were independent risk factors for OHSS—consistent with the overall cohort. However, the ovarian stimulation protocol was a significant risk factor only in the 35–37-year group, not in those aged ≥ 38 (Supplemental Table 1).

ROS curve to predict the occurrence of late-onset severe OHSS

Based on multiple logistic regression analysis, previous reports, and our clinical experience, we plotted ROC curves for E2 levels on trigger day and number of good quality embryos to determine the cut-off values for predicting late-onset severe OHSS occurrence. As shown in Fig. 2, the area under the curve was 0.715 and 0.726 respectively (P < 0.001), indicating some potential for predicting the occurrence of late-onset severe OHSS in AMA women. We took cutoff values of 8775 mmol/L and 5.5 for E2 levels on trigger day and number of good quality embryos at which the sensitivity was 68.1% and 64.8%, and specificity was 67.4% and 73.4% respectively.

Pregnancy outcomes between the late-onset OHSS group and the control group

Patients in the OHSS group exhibited a lower biochemical pregnancy rate (1.4% vs. 6.7%, P < 0.05), higher clinical pregnancy rate (98.6% vs. 93.3%, P < 0.05), reduced ectopic pregnancy incidence (0 vs. 3.4%, P < 0.05), decreased early miscarriage rate (10.0% vs. 19.0%, P < 0.05), and improved live birth rate (84.3% vs. 73.0%, P < 0.05), but elevated multiple pregnancy rate (39.3% vs. 18.6%, P < 0.001) compared to the control group. No significant differences were observed in late miscarriage, fetal reduction rate, preterm delivery, or congenital malformation rates, as well as in gestational hypertension or gestational diabetes mellitus. In single live birth, the gestational age at delivery, macrosomia, low birth weight, cesarean section rate, birth weight, and body length showed comparable outcomes between groups (Table 3).

Hospitalizations of OHSS patients

In this cohort of 142 patients with late-onset severe OHSS, hepatic dysfunction was observed in 62% (moderate-to-severe 7.7%), while hypoalbuminemia occurred in 40.8% (severe 4.2%). Ascites was universally present (100%), with severe cases predominating (82.4%) and 73.9% requiring paracentesis. Pleural effusion developed in 32.4% (moderate-to-severe 23.2%), with a 3.5% thoracentesis rate. Notably, no thrombotic complications occurred. Seven patients (4.9%) required rehospitalization for OHSS, and two patients underwent laparoscopic adnexectomy due to early pregnancy ovarian torsion (Fig. 3A).

Clinical features of OHSS and its severity impact on pregnancy outcomes. A Clinical manifestations of the OHSS patients. B Impact of OHSS severity on live birth rate

To further evaluate the impact of OHSS severity during hospitalization on pregnancy outcomes, we compared live birth rates among different subgroups. As shown in Fig. 3B, although patients with abnormal hepatic function had lower live birth rates than those with normal hepatic function, the difference was not statistically significant (90.6% vs. 80.5%, P = 0.111). Additionally, neither OHSS-related readmission, nor the presence of pleural effusion or hypoalbuminemia, nor the severity of ascites showed any significant effect on live birth rate.

Discussion

As we known, this is the first study to analyze the risk factors and pregnancy outcomes for late-onset severe OHSS following ART treatment in AMA women. Trigger-day E2 levels and the number of good-quality embryos were identified as independent risk factors for late-onset severe OHSS. No detrimental impact of OHSS on pregnancy outcomes or maternal-neonatal wellbeing was observed in our study, and no severe complications occurred in the OHSS population.

Previous data have shown that the incidence of moderate and severe OHSS in China was 1.14% [5], which was comparable to or slightly lower than reported rates in other countries [14–18]. Severe OHSS prevalence among patients undergoing IVF has been reported to range between 0.1% and 2% [19], with age being a significant predictive factor—younger patients are generally more susceptible to OHSS than older individuals [14]. Among patients >35 years old included in this study, the observed late-onset severe OHSS rate in fresh embryo transfer cycles was 0.34%, although strategies for OHSS prevention have been implemented in routine clinical practice.

As the suggestion of Practice Committee of the American Society for Reproductive Medicine in 2024, it is recommended that patients with elevated AMH levels, PCOS, and anticipated high oocyte yields increased risk for OHSS, and using GnRH antagonists COH protocol, GnRH agonist to trigger oocyte maturation, and freeze-all cycle could reduce the risk of moderate-to-severe OHSS [3]. However, existing literatures does not clearly differentiate between risk factors for early versus late onset OHSS [3, 13, 20], with most established predictors being specific to early-onset OHSS. While fresh embryo transfer remains clinically necessary for certain patient populations to reduce the costs and treatment duration associated with embryo cryopreservation, effective predictors for late-onset OHSS in fresh transfer cycles remain elusive. Consistent with previous findings [13], our results confirmed that the antagonist protocol markedly decreases the risk of late onset OHSS, whereas elevated estradiol (E2) levels substantially increase OHSS susceptibility in women of advanced age. Unexpectedly, a negative correlation was observed between Gn dosage and OHSS incidence. The inverse relationship might be attributed to clinicians’ dose reduction in patients with favorable ovarian reserve or drug sensitivity during controlled ovarian stimulation. Furthermore, multivariate analysis revealed that neither maternal age, AFC, nor the number of oocytes retrieved constituted independent risk factors for late-onset severe OHSS. Intriguingly, the number of good-quality embryos emerged as a significant predictive marker. This finding suggests that in AMA women, embryo quality may serve as a more sensitive indicator of OHSS risk than traditional markers. ROC curve analysis identified more than 5.5 good-quality embryos or E2 ≥ 8775 pmol/L as optimal predictive thresholds for late-onset severe OHSS in AMA patients, which was significantly lower than conventional clinical thresholds as 12,000–18,000pmol/l (3,500–5,000 pg/mL) [3]. This might be related to the fact that patients with markedly high E2 levels underwent complete embryo cryopreservation without fresh embryo transfer. Nonetheless, these thresholds may serve as a reference for patient management and early risk stratification in clinical practice.

Our data demonstrated notably favorable pregnancy outcomes in OHSS patients, characterized by significantly lower rates of ectopic pregnancy and early miscarriage, alongside higher clinical pregnancy rate and live birth rate. The elevated multiple pregnancy rate further supports enhanced embryo implantation potential in this population. Collectively, these results are likely attributable to underlying factors such as higher ovarian reserve and better embryo quality, rather than being a direct consequence of OHSS itself. Late-onset OHSS is primarily initiated by endogenous hCG secreted by a viable early pregnancy, meaning that its occurrence may be considered an early clinical indicator of a pregnancy with high developmental potential. Thus, the improved clinical pregnancy and live birth rates likely reflect this positive prognostic background. It has also been suggested that the abundant production of VEGF secreted by luteinized granulosa cells [21] in OHSS patients may contribute to endometrial angiogenesis and implantation capacity [22], while further studies are needed to clarify its specific role. However, our findings also provide clinical reassurance that the occurrence of late-onset severe OHSS does not appear to adversely affect embryonic development or implantation, nor does it increase the miscarriage risk. This is a valuable message for addressing a common concern among patients diagnosed with OHSS. In our study, no ectopic pregnancy was observed in the OHSS group, which might be explained by the specific characteristics of our study population. Given that ectopic pregnancies are generally associated with lower serum hCG levels, the relatively reduced hCG secretion in these cases may be insufficient to induce severe OHSS. Furthermore, as the clinical presentation of OHSS generally precedes the diagnosis of ectopic pregnancy, the significantly lower incidence of ectopic pregnancy in our OHSS cohort compared to controls (0% vs. 3.4%) suggests that the emergence of severe late-onset OHSS might be a clinical indicator of intrauterine pregnancy, with a minimal likelihood of ectopic gestation. However, it should be noted that the occurrence of severe late-onset OHSS is relatively uncommon in AMA populations, the sample size of the OHSS group in our study was limited. Additionally, although rare, several case reports have described OHSS associated with tubal ectopic pregnancy [23–25]. Therefore, while no ectopic pregnancy was observed in our OHSS cohort, this does not entirely preclude the possibility, and clinical vigilance remains necessary for differential diagnosis in such cases.

Furthermore, the potential association between pregnancies complicated by OHSS and adverse perinatal outcomes remains inadequately studied. Some studies suggest that there was no significant difference in perinatal outcomes between patients with OHSS and without OHSS [26–28], while others indicate a possible link to higher risks of preterm birth and low birth weight [20, 29–31]. Our retrospective cohort study showed comparable pregnancy-related complications (including gestational hypertension and gestational diabetes mellitus) and perinatal outcomes in AMA women with and without OHSS, suggesting that the occurrence of OHSS has no impact on maternal-fetal health. This observation alleviates previous concerns regarding potential developmental sequelae associated with ovarian hyperstimulation.

As for the clinical safety of severe OHSS in AMA women, our cohort observed no major complications such as thromboembolic events or renal failure. All the patients had ascites, and subset of them presented with concurrent clinical symptoms, such as abnormal hepatic function, hypoproteinemia, and hydrothorax in our study. The impact of various OHSS symptoms on the outcomes of IVF-ET remains poorly studied and inconclusive in the current literature. Ma et al. demonstrated that the presence of hydrothorax did not significantly affect miscarriage rates when compared to cases with ascites alone [32]. Our data showed that various symptoms of OHSS and their severity had no impact on live birth rate and miscarriage rate. After hospitalization, all OHSS-affected patients attained complete clinical recovery, demonstrating the safety of ART in this population when properly managed.

The tolerance of embryos to cryopreservation is closely associated with their quality [33], which tends to decline with increasing maternal age [34, 35]. Live birth rates following a Freeze-All strategy were shown to decrease significantly with age, particularly among women aged 35–37 and 38–40 [36]. Additionally, FET was associated with significantly lower clinical pregnancy rates compared to fresh embryo transfer in AMA patients [37]. Some studies demonstrated that frozen embryo transfer was associated with substantially higher risk of hypertensive disorders [38] and pre-eclampsia [39]. These considerations present a considerable clinical dilemma: whether to accept a certain risk of OHSS with fresh transfer—a strategy that shortens time-to-pregnancy and reduces psychological and financial burdens—or to pursue a Freeze-All approach that eliminates OHSS risk. Our data indicated that the occurrence of OHSS does not adversely affect pregnancy outcomes or increase perinatal risks after proper management, supporting the viability of fresh embryo transfer in selected AMA patients. However, it should be noted that our study predominantly included lower-risk patients, as those at highest risk for OHSS often underwent elective freeze-all cycles, which may introduce selection bias. These findings highlight the importance of individualized risk assessment and treatment planning. High-risk patients should be counseled to remain vigilant for OHSS symptoms and seek prompt medical attention if needed.

However, there were still several limitations in our research: (1) As a retrospective single-center study, the generalizability of findings may be constrained; (2) The analysis included only OHSS cases that occurred despite standard preventive measures, potentially introducing selection bias; (3) The relatively small sample size of advanced-age OHSS patients may affect the precision of our risk threshold calculations; (4) The inclusion of patients with mild or moderate OHSS within the control group, due to the inability to reliably distinguish them from true non-OHSS cases, may have biased the results toward the null, thereby potentially diluting the observed associations.

Conclusion

The level of E2 on trigger day and a higher number of good-quality embryos were independent risk factors for late-onset severe OHSS in women of advanced age. Despite the occurrence of OHSS, it does not compromise pregnancy outcomes or increase perinatal and neonatal risks after appropriate treatment.
