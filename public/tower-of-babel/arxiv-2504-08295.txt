1.
Introduction

The sum of positive divisors function
\sigma(F)
is defined by

\sigma(F)=\sigma_{1}(F)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid F\\
d>0\end{subarray}}{d}.

The abundancy index of
F
is defined by

I(F)=\frac{\sigma(F)}{F}.

Note that,
\sigma(F)
, and
I(F)
are both multiplicative.
Let
F
and
E
be two distinct positive integers such that
I(E)=I(F)
then we say
E
and
F
are friends of each other. A number that has no friends is called a solitary number. Some numbers, such as
10,14,15,\text{and}~20
, are believed to be solitary, however no proofs confirming their solitary nature are available to date.
10
is the smallest positive integer suspected to be solitary. A positive integer
F
is said to be a friend of
10
if
I(F)=9/5
that is
\sigma(F)=\frac{9}{5}F
. In order to find one friend
F
of 10, many authors gave necessary conditions for the existence of
F
. J. Ward
[
9
]
proved that,
F
is an odd square positive integer with
\omega(F)\geq 6
and
5
is the smallest positive divisor of
F
. In
[
4
]
, the authors improved J.Ward’s result by proving that in order to become a friend of
10
,
F
must have
\omega(F)\geq 7
, additionally they established some necessary properties of
F
. In
[
5
]
necessary upper bounds for the second, third, and fourth smallest prime divisors of
F
have been proposed, further, the author in
[
6
]
generalized the paper
[
5
]
and established general upper bounds for all prime divisors of a friend of
10
. In this paper, we establish some relations between the divisors of friends of
10
. Note that, any friend of
10
can be written as
F=5^{2a}\cdot Q^{2}
(where
Q
is an odd positive integer coprime to
15
, and
a\geq 1
) then

I(F)=\frac{\sigma(F)}{F}=\frac{\sigma(5^{2a}\cdot Q^{2})}{5^{2a}\cdot Q^{2}}=\frac{\sigma(5^{2a})\cdot\sigma(Q^{2})}{5^{2a}\cdot Q^{2}}=\frac{9}{5}

that is

(1)

\displaystyle\sigma(5^{2a})\cdot\sigma(Q^{2})=9\cdot{5^{2a-1}}\cdot{Q^{2}}.

Further, if we set
a=a_{1}
,
p_{1}=5
, and
Q={\displaystyle\prod_{i=2}^{\omega(F)}p_{i}^{a_{i}}}
(
p_{i}>5
for all
2\leq i\leq\omega(F)
are prime numbers) then from (
1
) we have

\sigma(5^{2a_{1}})\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{\omega(F)}\sigma(p_{i}^{2a_{i}})=9\cdot 5^{2a_{1}-1}\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{\omega(F)}p_{i}^{2a_{i}}.

A well-known open problem relating to the abundancy index is the existence of odd perfect numbers (an odd positive integer having abundancy index
2
is called an odd perfect number). Inspired by the papers
[
3
,
7
]
on odd perfect numbers where the authors essentially discussed the exponents of prime divisors of an odd perfect number, we give a similar type of result for a friend of
10
,

Theorem 1.1
.

If
F=5^{2a_{1}}\cdot{\displaystyle\prod_{i=2}^{\omega(F)}p_{i}^{2a_{i}}}
(
p_{1}=5
) is a friend of 10, then not all
a_{i}
are congruent to
13
modulo
27
.

Motivating by the above theorem we prove the following theorem which is even better as we can observe that, if
a_{i}\equiv 13\pmod{27}
holds for all
1\leq i\leq\omega(F)
then it would imply that
a_{i}\equiv 13\pmod{3}
i.e.,
a_{i}\equiv 1\pmod{3}
holds for all
1\leq i\leq\omega(F)
, thus Theorem
1.1
follows from Theorem
1.2
(recall contrapositive statements).

Theorem 1.2
.

If
F=5^{2a_{1}}\cdot{\displaystyle\prod_{i=2}^{\omega(F)}p_{i}^{2a_{i}}}
(
p_{1}=5
) is a friend of 10, then not all
a_{i}
are congruent to
1
modulo
3
.

As a consequence of Theorem
1.2
we have an alternative proof of the fact that not all
a_{i}=1
, which can also be deduced from Theorem 1.9
[
4
]
(the authors
[
4
]
proved that if
F=5^{2a}\cdot Q^{2}
is a friend of
10
then
Q
must be non squarefree and thus the fact follows).

In number theory, congruence relations play a fundamental role as they provide a structured and systematic way to analyze mathematical problems. The following two theorems establish relationships between the divisors of
F
through specific congruence relations,

Theorem 1.3
.

If
F=5^{2a}\cdot Q^{2}
is a friend of 10, then
\sigma(5^{2a})+\sigma(Q^{2})\equiv 6\pmod{8}
if and only if
a
is even.

Theorem 1.4
.

If
F=5^{2a}\cdot Q^{2}
is a friend of 10, then
\sigma(5^{2a})+\sigma(Q^{2})\equiv 2\pmod{8}
if and only if
a
is odd.

We may now ask: why are the congruence relations in Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
important? We provide an example to understand how they play a significant role in finding a friend of

10
. If
F=5^{2}\cdot Q^{2}
is a friend of
10
, then from Theorem
1.4
we have

\sigma(5^{2})+\sigma(Q^{2})\equiv 2\pmod{8}

which is basically

\sigma(Q^{2})\equiv 3\pmod{8}

that is
\sigma(Q^{2})=8t+3
for some
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Since
F
is friend of
10
we have

(2)

\displaystyle Q^{2}=\frac{5\cdot 31}{9\cdot 25}\cdot\sigma(Q^{2})=\frac{31}{45}\cdot\sigma(Q^{2}),

since
Q^{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we must have

\sigma(Q^{2})\equiv 0\pmod{45}

putting
\sigma(Q^{2})=8t+3
we get

8t+3\equiv 0\pmod{45}

that is

t\equiv 39\pmod{45},

thus
t=45t^{\prime}+39
for some
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, using (
2
) we obtain

\displaystyle Q^{2}=248t^{\prime}+217

therefore it follows that the form of
F
must be
F=25\cdot(248t^{\prime}+217)=6200t^{\prime}+5425
for some
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

The next theorem provides a lower bound for a friend
F
of

10
, depending on the exponents of the prime divisors of

F
.

Theorem 1.5
.

If
F=5^{2a_{1}}\cdot{\displaystyle\prod_{i=2}^{\omega(F)}p_{i}^{2a_{i}}}
(
p_{1}=5
) is a friend of 10, then
F
is strictly larger than
\frac{25}{81}\cdot{\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\omega(F)}(2a_{i}+1)^{2}}
.

In
[
4
]
, the authors gave an upper bound (see Corollary 1.11) for a friend
F=5^{2a}\cdot Q^{2}
of 10: if
\Omega(Q)\leq K
then
F<5\cdot 6^{(2^{\omega(F)}-1)^{2}}<5\cdot 6^{(2^{K-2a+1}-1)^{2}}
, where
\Omega(Q)
is the total number of prime divisors of
Q
. As a corollary of Theorem
1.5
we can give a lower bound for
F
depending on
\omega(F)
only.

Corollary 1.6
.

If
F
is a friend of
10
then

F>625\cdot 9^{\omega(F)-3}.

Proof.

If
F=5^{2a_{1}}\cdot{\displaystyle\prod_{i=2}^{\omega(F)}p_{i}^{2a_{i}}}
(
p_{1}=5
) is a friend of 10, then not all
a_{i}=1
( by Theorem
1.2
) i.e., there exists some
a_{j}
such that
a_{j}\geq 2
, thus it implies from Theorem
1.5
that

F>\frac{25}{81}\cdot 25\cdot 9^{\omega(F)-1}=625\cdot 9^{\omega(F)-3}.

∎

Throughout this paper, we use
p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{\omega(n)}
to denote prime numbers and we consider
a,a_{1},\dots,a_{\omega(F)}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.

Proof of The Main Results

Before we start proving the results, it is useful to state some of the elementary properties of the abundancy index.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
2
,
10
]
)
.

(a)

I(F)
is weakly multiplicative, that is, if
F
and
G
are two coprime positive integers then
I(FG)=I(F)I(G)
.

(b)

If
\alpha,F
are two positive integers and
\alpha>1
. Then
I(\alpha F)>I(F)
.

(c)

If
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
,
p_{3}
,…,
p_{n}
are
n
distinct prime numbers and
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
,…,
\alpha_{n}
are positive integers then

\displaystyle I\biggl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\biggl)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\biggl(\sum_{j=0}^{\alpha_{i}}p_{i}^{-j}\biggl)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}+1}-1}{p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}(p_{i}-1)}.

(d)

If
p_{1}
,…,
p_{n}
are distinct prime numbers and if
q_{1}
,…,
q_{n}
are distinct prime numbers such that
p_{i}\leq q_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
then for positive integers
l_{1},l_{2},...,l_{n}
we have

\displaystyle I\biggl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{l_{i}}\biggl)\geq I\biggl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}^{l_{i}}\biggl).

(e)

If
F={\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}}
, then
I(F)<{\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-1}}
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
4
]
, Theorem 1.3)
.

Let
p,q
be two distinct prime numbers with
p^{k-1}\mid\mid(q-1)
where
k
is some positive integer. Then
p
divides
\sigma(q^{2a})
if and only if
2a+1\equiv\ 0\pmod{f}
where
f
is the smallest odd positive integer greater than
1
such that
q^{f}\equiv 1\pmod{p^{k}}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

For any positive integer
a
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma(5^{2a})\equiv\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1\pmod{8}&\text{if~}a\equiv 0\pmod{4}\\
7\pmod{8}&\text{if~}a\equiv 1\pmod{4}\\
5\pmod{8}&\text{if~}a\equiv 2\pmod{4}\\
3\pmod{8}&\text{if~}a\equiv 3\pmod{4}\\
\end{array}\right..

Proof.

Since
a
is a positive integer, we can write
a=4q+r
for some
q,r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},0\leq r\leq 3
, therefore
\sigma(5^{2a})=\frac{5^{2a+1}-1}{4}=\frac{5^{8q+2r+1}-1}{4}
. Let

\displaystyle\sigma(5^{2a})=\frac{5^{8q+2r+1}-1}{4}\equiv c(q,r)\pmod{8}~~~~~~~\text{where}~~c(q,r)~~\text{depends on }~q,r.

Then we can write

\displaystyle 5^{8q+2r+1}\equiv 4c(q,r)+1\pmod{32}.

Since
5^{8}\equiv 1\pmod{32}
we have,

\displaystyle 5^{2r+1}\equiv 4c(q,r)+1\pmod{32}.

This shows that
c(q,r)
is independent of
q
i.e.,
c(q,r)=c(r)
only depends on
r
. Thus we have

\displaystyle c(r)\equiv\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1\pmod{8}&\text{if~}r=0\\
7\pmod{8}&\text{if~}r=1\\
5\pmod{8}&\text{if~}r=2\\
3\pmod{8}&\text{if~}r=3\\
\end{array}\right..

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3

Let
F=5^{2a}\cdot Q^{2}
be a friend of 10. Assume that
a
is even. Then from Proposition
2.3
either
\sigma(5^{2a})\equiv 1\pmod{8}
or
\sigma(5^{2a})\equiv 5\pmod{8}
. Suppose that
\sigma(5^{2a})+\sigma(Q^{2})\equiv k\pmod{8}
, where

. Since

is a friend of 10, we have

(3)

\displaystyle\sigma(5^{2a})\cdot\sigma(Q^{2})=9\cdot 5^{2a-1}\cdot Q^{2}\equiv 5\pmod{8}~~\text{as $Q^{2}$ is odd and}~a\in\mathbb{Z^{+}}.

Now we consider the following two cases,

Case-1
: If
\sigma(5^{2a})\equiv 1\pmod{8}
we have

\sigma(Q^{2})\equiv k-1\pmod{8},

using (
3
) we get

k-1\equiv 5\pmod{8}

that is

k\equiv 6\pmod{8}.

It follows that, for
\sigma(5^{2a})\equiv 1\pmod{8}

\sigma(5^{2a})+\sigma(Q^{2})\equiv 6\pmod{8}
.

Case-2
: If
\sigma(5^{2a})\equiv 5\pmod{8}

) we have

5\sigma(Q^{2})\equiv 5\pmod{8}.

Since
\sigma(Q^{2})\equiv k-5\pmod{8}
we get

that is

k\equiv 6\pmod{8}.

This shows that, for
\sigma(5^{2a})\equiv 5\pmod{8}
,
\sigma(5^{2a})+\sigma(Q^{2})\equiv 6\pmod{8}
.

Conversely, let
\sigma(5^{2a})+\sigma(Q^{2})\equiv 6\pmod{8}
. If possible, assume that
a
is odd positive integer. Then from Proposition
2.3
either
\sigma(5^{2a})\equiv 3\pmod{8}
or
\sigma(5^{2a})\equiv 7\pmod{8}
.

: If
\sigma(5^{2a})\equiv 3\pmod{8}

Case-4
: If
\sigma(5^{2a})\equiv 7\pmod{8}
