Biophysical drivers of tropical tree diversity and carbon stocks across an urban gradient in Mexico
Authors: Armando Navarrete-Segueda, Abril Soria-Nambo, M Lourdes González-Arqueros, Guadalupe Cornejo-Tenorio, Elizabeth Chávez-García, Guillermo Ibarra-Manríquez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrban and peri-urban green spaces are shaped by a complex interaction between anthropogenic management and underlying biophysical heterogeneity, which gives them particular community attributes (structure and diversity) and soil functions. Consequently, vegetation-based assessments alone often fail to capture the multidimensionality of these socioecological systems. Here, we examined the extent to which biophysical constraints, such as precipitation, topography, soil properties, and the structure of urban forests, differentially affect terrestrial carbon (C) stocks within tropical urban and peri-urban green spaces. We tested hypotheses regarding the importance of diversity in native and non-native plant species, as well as the effect of soil variation among sites. We compared woody species richness (0D), dominance (2D), composition, structure, and tree aboveground biomass (AGB), as well as soil organic carbon, across a mosaic of green spaces in the mid-sized city of Morelia, Michoacán state, Mexico. Using principal components analysis (PCA) and analysis of similarities (ANOSIM), we identified significant correlations between biophysical heterogeneity and community-level patterns. Elevated carbon density in urban areas is related to the presence of larger individuals of non-native species. Instead, native species shaped the peri-urban carbon stock through a greater number of individuals. Soil organic carbon stocks showed no significant differences between urban and peri-urban sites. This study advances the understanding of urban ecosystems, highlighting the need for integrative indicators that account for tropical environmental quality. Our findings contribute to a framework for assessing the performance of green infrastructure, with implications for climate adaptation, biodiversity conservation, and urban landscapes.Keywords: Spatial heterogeneity, Urban forests, Green-spaces monitoring, Urban tree biodiversity, Urban soils

Introduction

Urban forestry research has advanced our understanding of the socio-environmental contributions of urban green spaces by examining how natural elements (e.g., flora, fauna, soil) mediate ecosystem functions that support human well-being and sustainable development within the urban landscape (Stroud et al., 2022; Flores et al., 2022; Dobbs et al., 2023; Oertel et al., 2024). The term green space refers to vegetated areas in both urban and peri-urban contexts that may include built-environment features. Depending on their physical characteristics and functional uses, vegetation composition and structure of green spaces can be classified into multiple categories, such as parks, gardens, or urban forests (McIntyre et al., 2000; Taylor and Hochuli, 2017; Jones et al., 2022).

Research on the contribution of green spaces to human well-being in urban (i.e., urban green spaces, UGS) and peri-urban (i.e., peri-urban green spaces, P-UGS) contexts has expanded globally in recent decades. However, the literature remains disproportionately focused on the Northern Hemisphere, with relatively few comprehensive assessments in Latin America (Dobbs et al., 2019; Veerkamp et al., 2021; Pinto et al., 2022). In high-latitude regions and Asia, assessments of urban ecosystem services (ES) have frequently relied on vegetation-based approaches using high-resolution remote sensing data (e.g., normalized difference vegetation index) as proxies of ES supply (Chang et al., 2017; Cortinovis and Geneletti, 2020; Liu and Russo, 2021; Reyes-Riveros et al., 2021; Graça et al., 2022; Stroud et al., 2022; Meng, 2023; Rodrigues Ferreira Barbosa et al., 2024). These analyses could be complemented by field surveys that characterize plant community attributes, including species richness, species diversity, and tree community structure (McKinney, 2008; Flores et al., 2022; Lokatis and Jeschke, 2022). Together with soil properties, these vegetation metrics serve as key indicators of ecosystem function, as their spatial variability influences the type and magnitude of regulating, provisioning, and cultural ecosystem services in urban spaces (de Groot et al., 2010; Quijas et al., 2012; Yao et al., 2025). Among soil-related variables, soil organic carbon (SOC), particularly in the top 30 cm, is critical due to its strong associations with multiple ecosystem services derived from urban soils (Calzolari et al., 2016, 2020; Bünemann et al., 2018; Ungaro et al., 2022).

Latin American studies on ES have developed a trajectory grounded in a combination of field- and remote-sensing-based methodologies (Ordoñez Barona et al., 2020; Muñoz-Pacheco and Villaseñor, 2022; Bakhtsiyarava et al., 2024). Most research has been conducted in urban areas of South America (Argentina, Brazil, Chile, and Colombia), with a primary focus on urban parks and regulating services such as temperature and flooding mitigation, as well as biodiversity support, mainly for avian species. In contrast, other types of green spaces within the urban context, such as roadside trees, gardens, and forest fragments, have received comparatively little attention despite their biophysical heterogeneity and the potential implications of this variability for ES supply (Dobbs et al., 2019; Muñoz-Pacheco and Villaseñor, 2022; Flores et al., 2022).

Urbanization is widely associated with biodiversity loss and reduced carbon stocks (Seto et al., 2012; Chang et al., 2024), yet recent studies suggest that this trend is not universal. It has been shown that public management policies can support higher tree densities, biomass accumulation, and carbon stocks comparable to those in P-UGS, especially when large, mature individuals are promoted in cities (Schafer et al., 2019; Benítez et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Moreno et al., 2024). Species origin (native vs. exotic) also influences diversity patterns and their contributions to total carbon storage outcomes (Shrestha et al., 2024; Jevon et al., 2025). In addition, recent evidence highlights that urban soils may provide ES at levels comparable to those of adjacent natural ecosystems (Eldridge et al., 2024), though their role is still understudied, especially in Latin American cities, where the supply of ES varies substantially due to differences in biophysical conditions and governance systems (de la Barrera et al., 2016; Dobbs et al., 2018; Sobrinho Soares et al., 2021; Pouladi et al., 2023; González-Arqueros et al., 2025).

Spatial heterogeneity in landscape factors (e.g., soil, slope, rainfall) modulates how human pressures affect plant communities, ultimately shaping the ecological capacity of ES supply (Gupta and Ahmad, 1999; Zhao et al., 2014; Ruas et al., 2022; Hou et al., 2023). The knowledge of these biophysical factors serves as proxies for vegetation attributes (structure and diversity) and ecosystem functions, namely, processes of exchange of energy and matter among biophysical factors that support plant and animal life, as biomass production (Hector and Bagchi, 2007; Tilman et al., 2014; Greinert, 2015; Pickett et al., 2017; Schram-Bijkerk et al., 2018; Calzolari et al., 2020; Sharma et al., 2022). Yet, beyond the vegetation, the contribution of other key biophysical factors (e.g., soil, topography) to the quality of life of urban inhabitants remains poorly understood (Stroud et al., 2022; Pouladi et al., 2023).

While urban and peri-urban forests are shaped by human interventions, the specific structure of each green space is constrained by the underlying biophysical conditions (Pickett et al., 2017). Thus, vegetation-only assessments overlook key biophysical complexities of the green space mosaic (Stevenson et al., 2023). Despite increasing recognition of the role of UGS and P-UGS in supporting multiple ecosystem services (Arcos-LeBert et al., 2021; Muñoz-Pacheco and Villaseñor, 2022; Xie et al., 2024; Augustinus et al., 2024), critical knowledge gaps remain regarding how key biophysical indicators, such as tree community structure, diversity, and soil organic carbon, vary across these green space types in tropical regions, and how such variation may shape the ecological capacity for ES supply (Pereira et al., 2024).

To address this gap, we examined how woody plant diversity, structural composition, and SOC vary across urban and peri-urban green spaces as key indicators of their ecological capacity to supply ES. While carbon storage -through both aboveground biomass and SOC- is the primary ES quantified in this study, we also consider vegetation support itself as a regulating ecosystem service, in line with established frameworks (Quijas et al., 2012; Balvanera et al., 2014). In this context, SOC and vegetation attributes are not only indicators of carbon stocks but also foundational components that can modulate a myriad of ecological processes and associated services, such as habitat provisioning, thermal regulation, and soil fertility (Zhang et al., 2024; Jevon et al., 2025). Accordingly, this study addresses the following questions: (1) To what extent do components of biophysical heterogeneity (namely, plant community structure and diversity, climate, topographic slope, and soil properties) co-vary across UGS and P-UGS? (2) How do differences in species composition between UGS and P-UGS, particularly the replacement of native species by non-native ones, influence aboveground carbon storage potential? (3) Does the stock of soil organic carbon (SOC) differ significantly between UGS and P-UGS? These questions collectively aim to elucidate the interactions among vegetation structure, biodiversity, and carbon dynamics across urban and peri-urban landscapes as categorical proxies.

Biophysical heterogeneity drives species diversity in ecosystems (Stein et al., 2014). It has been hypothesized that this effect extends to ecosystem functioning in urban landscapes (Pickett et al., 2017). Accordingly, we expect a strong correlation between biophysical heterogeneity components (i.e., plant community structure and diversity, precipitation, topographic slope, and soil properties) across UGS and P-UGS (Hypothesis 1). It has been demonstrated that there are significant differences in plant community structure and diversity between UGS and P-UGS, largely due to the replacement of native species with non-native ones (Filazzola et al., 2019; Lambert and Donihue, 2020; Lokatis and Jeschke, 2022). Nevertheless, we expect native species to store more carbon, based on the evidence from Shrestha et al. (2024) and Jevon et al. (2025) (Hypothesis 2). Finally, according to Eldridge et al. (2024), we expect no differences in SOC stocks between the two types of green spaces (Hypothesis 3).

The aforementioned hypotheses were empirically evaluated in UGS and P-UGS of the medium-sized tropical city of Morelia, Michoacán, Mexico. This city is situated on the Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt, at the northern limit of the Neotropical region. We adopt the terms UGS and P-UGS not as distinct categories of green infrastructure, but as spatial designations within the broader urban–peri-urban continuum. Both categories may encompass similar types of green infrastructure (e.g., parks, gardens), yet differ in their urbanization intensity and exposure to socio-environmental pressures. This distinction follows recent calls in urban ecology to differentiate typological classifications of green infrastructure from the spatial gradients they occupy (Taylor and Hochuli, 2017; Jones et al., 2022; Paul et al., 2025). We aimed to assess green space variability by examining the complex relationships among plant community attributes (structure and diversity), soil properties, topography, and climate across urban and peri-urban environments.

Rather than controlling for green space typology across zones, our aim was to capture the actual biophysical variability of urban and peri-urban landscapes as it is spatially expressed in the study area. Indeed, certain green space types (e.g., riparian corridors) are disproportionately located in flat, densely urbanized areas, while others (e.g., remnant forests) are primarily found in steep, peri-urban slopes. This asymmetric distribution reflects the spatial imprint of land-use history and urban expansion. Therefore, rather than artificially controlling for typology, we sampled across this variability to evaluate how biophysical drivers (soil, topography, vegetation) shape ecological function and carbon dynamics across the full urban-peri-urban mosaic.

Materials and methods

Study Area

Morelia city is located in the north of the state of Michoacán (Fig. 1). It covers approximately 97.1 km² and has a population of around 849,000. Approximately 95% of the population resides within the urban zone, resulting in an average population density of 56 inhabitants per ha. However, within the core urbanized area, a relatively flat surface, population density increases markedly, reaching values of up to 181 inhabitants per ha (INEGI, 2020; Bollo Manent et al., 2022; IMPLAN, 2022, 2023).

The biophysical landscape of UGS and P-UGS is characterized by topographic variations. The elevation ranges from 1900 to 2200 m. The green spaces within the urban area host various vegetation types, including parks and gallery forests, interspersed among buildings at a density ranging from 61 to 180 dwellings per ha. The green spaces within the peri-urban area are located in transitional zones between urban and rural areas of the city and exhibit a moderate degree of anthropization, including livestock, unpaved roads, logging, and resin extraction from Pinus spp. trees. The Morelia region has been shaped by volcanic activity, characterized by small-volume volcanic structures dating from the Late Pliocene to the present (Pereznegrón and Corona-Chávez, 2024). These features define the region’s topographic heterogeneity. According to INEGI (2007) and based on ISSS/ISRIC/FAO (1998) classification system, the predominant soil units are Phaeozems and Leptosols. In Morelia, the warm-temperate climate with a dry winter (Cw) is dominant according to the Köppen climate classification (García, 2004). Based on climatological data reported by the National Water Commission (SMN-CAN, 2023) for the reference period 1981–2020, the average maximum temperature occurs in May (21.5 °C), while the average minimum temperature occurs in January (15.5 °C).

Biophysical Characterization and Sampling

We assumed that a stratified sampling design would provide more accurate estimates of urban forest diversity and structure than a non-stratified design (Jin and Yang, 2020). Therefore, the first sampling type was employed based on UGS size, shape, and dominant species (Jones et al., 2022; Santillán-Gaona, 2024). These typological criteria were selected for nesting of the biophysical elements (Table 1). The UGS categories were grouped into equal numbers of sampling plots to capture the full range of biophysical variability within these systems, allowing direct comparison with P-UGS. This approach enabled consistent and reproducible categorization, thereby supporting robust comparisons and maximizing the representation of landscape heterogeneity.

To collect data on soil and vegetation across the green spaces, 34 sampling sites were established. Each site comprised four plots measuring 2 × 50 m, encompassing a total sampled area of 400 m² per site. Each plot had a minimum linear separation of 50 m. The separation between sites was, on average, 0.91 km and 5.9 km for the urban and peri-urban environment, respectively. Sampling locations were selected within UGS and P-UGS. To capture spatial heterogeneity, sampling sites were distributed across the city’s quadrants. Only green spaces that met the following criteria were considered: presence of unsealed soil, at least one tree, and a minimum linear distance of 50 m to accommodate the transect. In the peri-urban fringe, sampling focused on areas with forest cover, as large portions of the peripheral landscape have been deforested or converted to agriculture, thereby limiting the availability of sites. Access permissions were obtained from the Instituto Municipal de Planeación de Morelia and the respective management institutions for each site.

For the topographical analysis, a digital elevation model (DEM) was derived from the 1:50,000-scale contour lines of the E15A23 chart of INEGI (2017), and a slope layer was generated. We also generated maps of precipitation and evaporation. To accurately interpolate and strengthen the climate surface, we used meteorological data recorded by weather stations for the period 1991–2020, with elevations ranging from 1831 to 2285 m a. s. l. Yearly average precipitation (mm yr–1) and evaporation maps (mm yr–1) were generated with Inverse Distance Weighting interpolation through ArcMap (ESRI ®), which is a mathematical (deterministic) function that assumes that closer values are more related than farther values (Chen and Liu 2012).

Slope values were measured directly in the field using a clinometer at each sampling plot. The trees and shrubs were censused and identified to the species level, recording their total height and crown width (N-S orientation); the diameter of the trunk at breast height (DBH; 1.3 m) was measured only for trees. When trees exhibited multiple stems at 1.3 m above ground level, each stem was measured separately. Additionally, the presence of parasitic or epiphytic organisms, specifically Tillandsia spp. (Bromeliaceae) and mistletoes, were recorded for each tree individual.

Soil properties were determined from a composite surface-soil (0-30 cm) sample collected at six points per plot, spaced 10 m apart. Before sample collection, vegetation residues, such as grass and leaf litter, were removed from the soil surface. Additionally, at each collection point, three undisturbed soil samples were taken using a 100 ml cylinder to determine their bulk density. In the laboratory, soil samples were analyzed according to the guidelines of the Soil Science Division Staff (2017) and Soil Survey Staff (2011). The percentage of water content was determined by the gravimetric method, the bulk density by the cylinder method, and the texture by the hydrometer method. The pH and electrical conductivity were measured in water using the electrometric method and a conductivity meter (HI 9812-5 from HANNA Instruments), respectively.

Data Analysis

To assess biophysical variation (i.e., precipitation, relief slope, soil properties, woody community structure —individuals’ density, trunk diameter, height, and diversity, effective number of species—Table S1) between UGS and P-UGS, we first made comparisons using t-tests when the variables met the assumptions of normality and Mann-Whitney tests when they did not. Specifically for the plant community, we compared the density (number of individuals in a 400 m2 area) and the effective number of species (species richness 0D to dominant species 2D) as defined by Jost (2006).

We assessed biophysical relationships across the UGS and P-UGS. Initially, we performed a principal component analysis (PCA) to examine relationships among plant structure, climate, topography, and soil physicochemical properties across the green spaces studied (Table S1). To complement this analysis, we examined the relationship between a biophysical gradient derived from a PCA of climate, soil, and topographic variables and the distribution of woody species richness and abundance. Second, to evaluate whether species distribution reflects the urban-peri-urban stratification, a permutational multivariate analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) was performed. This technique is a highly effective non-parametric method for testing hypotheses about group differences (Somerfield et al., 2021). We set UGS and P-UGS as factors and the community distance matrix as the response variable in the function “adonis2”. We obtained the p-value by running 999 permutations. A non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) ordination was performed using the function “metaMDS” to visualize the ANOSIM results. The functions “decostand” and “vegdist” were used to compute the average dissimilarity matrix based on the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity measure for density data. All analyses were performed using the R program (R Core team 2024).

We conducted independent t-tests after confirming normality and homogeneity of variances to evaluate differences in carbon stocks between aboveground biomass and topsoil in UGS and P-UGS. Finally, the carbon stocks of each species were quantified to determine their contributions to the total carbon sink, with species clustered by native or non-native status.

Results

We identified UGS and P-UGS in contrasting topographies. The first ones were situated on relatively flat terrain (2.9%) and mean elevations ranging from approximately 1890 to 1950 m, whereas the latter sites were located in strongly inclined relief (18.7%) and higher elevations (2000–2200 m), with steep areas that exceeded slopes of 30% (Fig. 2a, b and Table 2). The mean annual precipitation (MAP) and evaporation in Morelia exhibited a marked gradient (Fig. 2c). Although they do not present significant differences, three zones can be recorded. Most UGS were developed in a zone with MAP values close to 800 mm yr–1, decreasing to 656 mm yr–1 in the northeast, whereas in the southeast region, MAP was > 1000 mm yr–1. On the contrary, P-UGS were found in the three precipitation conditions described previously (Fig. 2c). Both types of green spaces were established in areas with evaporation rates > 1500 mm yr–1, although this climatic variable was higher in UGS (Fig. 2d). Except for tree height and number of individuals, significant differences were observed in all vegetation structural and diversity variables. Urban Green Space exhibited higher values across all diversity metrics and in some traits associated with tree size, including height, DBH, and crown width (Table 2). However, the highest individual density was recorded in P-URB, where it was mostly influenced by shrub density (Table 2). Most soil physicochemical properties differed between the two urban green space types; however, SOM, SOC, and bulk density did not differ significantly.

Significant differences were observed in above-ground biomass C (C-AGB) stock. The highest C stock was recorded in UGS (Fig. 5), where non-native species contributed the most to the C sink (Fig. 6A), despite their relative abundance being comparable to that of native species (Fig. 6B). Discussion

The Biophysical Environment and Green Spaces

As expected, we found strong relationships between variables that shape biophysical heterogeneity between UGS and P-UGS. The first two axis of the PCA indicated a gradient of relief and soil, in which the green spaces are clearly distributed (Table 3 and Fig. 3). Based on Arreygue Rocha et al. (2002), the flat relief in which the UGS are located and the urban matrix that contains them is associated with lake deposits from the Holocene (0.01 My), while the rugged relief (in which the peri-urban areas are developed) is associated with volcanic materials from the Miocene (2.6 My). Therefore, the differences in the relief of green spaces are influenced by geological variations.

Our results are consistent with previous studies (Pardo García et al., 2019; Chakraborty et al., 2022), which suggests that the spatial distribution of urban development and the peri-urban environment exhibit distinct spatial configurations. The relationship between flat relief-urban centers and rugged relief-peri-urban forest cover in Morelia has been reported in other Latin American cities (Zambrano Andrade et al., 2019), since rugged relief makes it challenging to build urban infrastructure, and also changes land use for agriculture (Pardo García et al., 2019).

The differences in soil properties observed between UGS and P-UGS are consistent with those reported in multiple studies (Riddle et al., 2022; Ungaro et al., 2022). Regarding texture variations, the findings align with those of Stumpe et al. (2023) and Enescu et al. (2022), who found that soil texture varies according to land use, with peri-urban forest soils being sandier. The high level of stoniness in urban soils also supports findings from earlier studies (Cram et al., 2008; Jordán et al., 2017; Ruiz et al., 2020). Jim (1998) noted that soils in UGS exhibit characteristics associated with human management, particularly due to the incorporation of construction-derived fill materials. The coarser textures and greater fragment content of soils in UGS (Fig S1 and Table 2) compared with their peri-urban counterparts appear to decouple soil properties from the underlying geology, indicating that urban land use in UGS imprints distinctive pedological signatures on these green spaces (Scharenbroch et al., 2005; Lehmann and Stahr, 2007). These sandy, fragment-rich soil matrices are characterized by reduced bulk volume and low water-holding capacity (Batjes, 1996; Jim, 1998), which likely accounts for the lower moisture levels recorded in the study sites. Such conditions can constrain tree growth (Layman et al., 2016). However, irrigation practices commonly implemented in UGS (Fig. S2) may mitigate these limitations, potentially explaining the observed positive relationship between rock fragment volume and tree size (height and DBH) (Fig. 3).

The volume of coarse fragments, primarily composed of construction debris, may contribute to the release of calcium carbonates into UGS soils (Prado et al., 2020), thereby increasing both pH and electrical conductivity (Table 2 and Fig. 3). Textural variation did not influence soil bulk density. The apparent uniformity of this property could be associated with the content of organic soil matter (SOM), for which, unexpectedly, no differences were detected among sampled soils. This aligns with Pickett et al. (2011), who explained that soil organic matter reflects the ecological homogenization produced by urbanization. In this regard, extensive research has documented that such homogenization leads to simplified soil habitats, which in turn result in reduced SOM and SOC cycling in tropical and subtropical urban environments (e.g., Ossola et al., 2016; Delgado-Baquerizo et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2025).

The observed lack of SOC variation contrasts with findings from tropical and subtropical regions, where differences in SOM and SOC between urban and peri-urban soils have been reported (Tang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2022). Notably lower SOM and SOC contents are typically expected in UGS compared to P-UGS (Yoon et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2022; Park et al., 2025), presumably due to urbanization pressures that disrupt carbon cycling in urban soils (Lorenz and Lal, 2015; Jackson et al., 2017). Furthermore, SOC in UGS is predominantly composed of labile fractions (Mondragón et al., 2022; Park et al., 2025), which may promote faster SOC turnover, particularly in tropical and subtropical environments (Furquim and Almeida, 2022). However, soils in UGS often consist of a mixture of construction debris and “natural” soil materials extracted from surrounding P-UGS (Cram et al., 2008; Calzolari et al., 2020), as observed in Morelia (Fig. S1). Accordingly, the concentrations of SOC and SOM in the UGS soils may reflect traits inherited from ex-situ pedogenetic processes that occurred in P-UGS, the original source of the soils. Moreover, Tang et al. (2021) reported that SOM content varies across different types of green spaces in subtropical Asia. Similarly, Mondragón et al. (2022) found that the SOC of soils in a Colombian UGS varies with differences in dominant tree species. These findings suggest the importance of categorizing green spaces (e.g., parks, roads, gardens) to better capture variability in SOC and SOM dynamics.

The second urban-peri-urban green spaces gradient identified through PCA reflects a variation in water availability, primarily driven by differences in precipitation and soil moisture. The slightly higher precipitation observed in the hilly region may be related to topography, which enhances water retention by forming ravines (Bailey, 2009). This orographic influence on precipitation may be further amplified by the expansion of urban infrastructure. UGS typically exhibit lower levels of both precipitation and soil moisture (Table 2), likely due to altered evaporation dynamics (Chen et al., 2022) and intensified urban heat island effects (Shahfahad et al., 2022). Although precipitation did not differ significantly, it contributed moderately to the variability explained by the second axis of the PCA. This reflects slight spatial differences in rainfall across the gradient, particularly in areas with elevation-driven microclimatic variation. However, this variability was insufficient to produce strong predictive patterns at the scale of analysis. Therefore, while precipitation may indirectly influence carbon dynamics, our results suggest that it plays a secondary role relative to vegetation structure and soil properties in this urban context.

According to Jim (1998), edaphoclimatic constraints, such as those found in peri-urban soils, may play a significant role in the establishment and growth of individual trees. Ali et al. (2019) and Sterck et al. (2021) identified climatic variation in water availability as a key regulator of biomass accumulation in tropical tree vegetation. Consequently, a larger tree size would be expected in P-UGS. However, the impact of climatic water availability may not be evident in UGS, where management practices such as irrigation mitigate water deficit (Fig. S2). As a result, tree parameters such as height, diameter, and canopy width are greater in UGS. These results highlight an important research gap: under low-precipitation conditions, the influence of irrigation in shaping vegetation structure and ecosystem services remains insufficiently understood. Global assessments indicate that irrigation practices in UGS are rarely monitored or systematically reported (Esperon-Rodriguez et al., 2025). Modeling of soil moisture in UGS shows that supplementary irrigation can mitigate drought stress and modify thermal comfort outcomes (Gobatti et al., 2025). However, in the context of increasing global water scarcity, such practices may also reduce the species’ drought sensitivity and hinder adaptive responses to long-term hydric stress (Guo et al., 2021; Rosenberger et al., 2025). Future research should therefore aim to quantify irrigation regimes and examine their interactions with precipitation deficits to improve understanding of biomass accumulation and soil carbon dynamics in urban systems.

Our results regarding vegetation structure and composition are consistent with former studies that report significant differences between UGS and P-UGS (McKinney 2006, 2008; Lambert and Donihue 2020; Lokatis and Jeschke 2022). However, these differences are more consistently observed in species composition across other tropical and subtropical American studies, whereas structural patterns remain variable (Freitas et al., 2020; Meléndez-Jaramillo et al., 2023; Salamanca-Fonseca et al., 2024). The higher density of individuals in P-UGS, characterized by hilly relief, is consistent with Cram et al. (2008), who conducted a study in Mexico City. Likewise, Zhao et al. (2014) found a correlation between land use and topography in a tropical city in China, where forests are typically found at the highest elevations and on steeper slopes. Regarding the steep slope-tree density relationship, Edwards et al. (2019) suggest that the conservation of biodiversity and tree cover during the Anthropocene will be greater in areas with this type of environmental factor. When we compared with flat areas, such topographic conditions typically yield lower economic returns, particularly in agricultural productivity or the profitability of gray infrastructure development. Consequently, higher tree density tends to be preserved or, in some cases, actively restored (Busch and Ferretti-Gallon, 2017). In addition, UGS in Latin America constitute complex and heterogeneous systems in which vegetation patterns are strongly shaped by social dynamics (Dobbs et al., 2023). However, consistent with findings from other studies conducted in Latin American UGS and P-UGS (Bakhtsiyarava et al., 2024), our results indicate that vegetation community parameters, specifically height and diameter, are influenced by biophysical factors, including topography, soil properties, and climatic conditions.

The observed differences in species diversity between green spaces (Table 2) align with our initial hypothesis. The lower diversity in P-UGS may be attributed, as Guerra-De la Cruz and Galicia (2017) argued, to historical reforestation practices in Mexico that have predominantly relied on a limited number of species. Consistent with this pattern, a global trend of reforestation and plantation schemes commonly deploy few species, often monocultures, leading to reduced plant diversity and stand homogenization (Liu et al., 2018). Likewise, multiple authors have reported increased species diversity in urban spaces, primarily driven by the introduction of exotic species (McKinney, 2008; Lokatis and Jeschke, 2022). In line with the above, the ordination results are consistent with those reported by Freitas et al. (2020), who observed shifts in plant composition between UGS and P-UGS in Brazil. Such a result is not unexpected, as urban landscapes often support plant assemblages with no analog in natural ecosystems (Filazzola et al., 2019; Lambert and Donihue, 2020; Lokatis and Jeschke, 2022).

Carbon Stocks between Urban and Peri-urban Green Spaces

The finding that urban vegetation stores greater amounts of C supports other studies worldwide (Moussa et al., 2019; Jevon et al., 2025). Consistent with the findings of Richter et al. (2020) and Nero et al. (2018), a relatively small number of urban tree species in Morelia contributed disproportionately to the total C-AGB stock. The elevated C-AGB stock in UGS was primarily influenced by tree sizes, as measured by DBH (Table 2). In this regard, Zhang et al. (2024) reported that plant community structure strongly influences carbon storage in UGS. This effect of large trees is consistent with findings by Liu and Li (2012) in Shenyang, China, Belo Horizonte, Brazil (Fernandes Barros et al., 2024), and Quito, Ecuador (Rodríguez and Cuesta, 2024).

Differing to our expectations, non-native species stored more C-AGB than native species in UGS (Fig. 6), a result that contrasts with Jevon et al. (2025), who found that native species were generally larger than non-native ones. This result may reflect a legacy effect (Hope et al., 2003) associated with historical planting initiatives in UGS, whereby non-native species, such as those belonging to the genera Casuarina, Eucalyptus and Ficus have attained large population sizes, as documented by Moreno et al. (2024) and Mancilla Morales et al. (2024) in Peru and in Metropolitan Area of Mexico City, respectively. However, this trend differed in peri-urban sites, where native species accounted for the largest share of C-AGB stocks. As indicated Liu and Li (2012), tree density is another key factor influencing carbon storage; in these sites, higher native abundance was associated with greater C storage (Fig. 6). Contrary to patterns commonly reported in urban areas worldwide (Dangulla et al., 2020; Bayón et al., 2021) and Latin America (dos Santos et al., 2010; Guevara et al., 2024), the city of Morelia exhibits a comparable proportion of native and non-native species (Fig. 6B), largely attributable to the high abundance of Fraxinus uhdei trees. Nevertheless, as previously noted, non-native species tend to attain larger sizes; thus, in the studied urban context, tree size exerted a stronger influence on ecosystem attributes than tree density.

The absence of significant differences in SOC stocks between UGS and P-UGS aligns with our expectations and is consistent with previous studies (Cambou et al., 2018; Eldridge et al., 2024). The recorded SOC stock, however, contrasts with topsoil (30 cm) global carbon patterns reported by Chien and Krumins (2022). As Xu et al. (2024) indicate, tropical urban soils respond to urbanization in a pattern opposite to that observed globally. Nevertheless, according to the same study, higher SOC stock values would be expected. In this context, the SOC densities (kg m2) recorded in the present study are comparable to those reported by Richter et al. (2020) for Berlin and other cities across Europe, Asia, and the United States of America (0-20 cm). The absence of significant differences, on the other hand, may be influenced by the sampling approach. In this regard, several studies (Pouyat et al., 2006; Edmondson et al., 2012; Richter et al., 2020) have shown that SOC stock estimates can vary considerably when deeper soil layers (up to 100 cm) are included and when green space types (e.g., residential, gardens, parks, commercial zones, etc.) are stratified in a particular analysis. Thus, deeper soil horizons should be considered to better capture actual variability. The large variability in soil sampling depths across studies complicates interpretation, particularly given that soils are key providers of ES globally. In urban environments, the management of soil functions constitutes a pivotal axis for the design and implementation of nature-based solutions (Daily, 1997; Ronchi, 2018; Delibas et al., 2021). Therefore, standardized protocols for soil sampling and SOC assessment should be prioritized to improve comparability and interpretation across studies.

Methodological frameworks used to assess UGS contributions to human wellbeing vary widely, encompassing approaches based on primary data collection, remote sensing, and the use of existing databases (Romero-Duque et al., 2020; Muñoz-Pacheco and Villaseñor, 2022; Flores et al., 2022). Research designs that classify urban landscape according to dominant infrastructure into green (e.g., parks, gardens) or blue (e.g., rivers, lakes) (Li et al., 2017; Romero-Duque et al., 2020) provide a useful framework for capturing the complexity of UGS. Such approaches facilitate the integration of ecosystem service delivery and its contributions to human well-being (Kremen 2005).

Limitations

Despite its contributions, our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the spatial scope and sample size were constrained to a single medium-sized tropical city (Morelia, Mexico) with 34 sampling sites, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other urban contexts or larger geographic scales. Additionally, the distribution of green spaces across UGS and P-UGS is uneven, reflecting underlying land-use patterns. This asymmetry may confound zone-based comparisons due to typological and historical differences. Although such heterogeneity aligns with our aim of examining ecological contrasts along the urban gradient, it may limit the comparability of certain metrics. Nevertheless, the stratified sampling design by green space type employed in this study enabled a more efficient capture of urban forest biophysical heterogeneity despite the limited sample size, and this approach can be replicated and adapted to other urban landscapes (Jin and Yang, 2020). Second, our framework focused on biophysical attributes and did not explicitly incorporate socio-cultural or economic variables that can strongly mediate urban vegetation patterns and carbon dynamics. Factors such as land-use history, governance models, and local management practices were not measured here, yet they likely influence the distribution of ecosystem services in urban landscapes. Integrating such socio-ecological metrics into future research would enhance the explanatory power of our approach and its applicability across diverse socio-environmental contexts. Regarding our native non-native species comparison, we recognize that the concept of “nativeness” is not absolute in urban environments, where species may not have evolved under urban conditions but are often classified as native based on their pre-urban distribution within a broader biogeographic region (Kaplan et al., 2022). Rather than implying evolutionary adaptation to urban settings, our classification of species as native or non-native follows established floristic references and management frameworks that are commonly used in urban forestry and conservation planning. Finally, while we assessed proxies of ecosystem services (carbon storage and biodiversity), we did not evaluate other service dimensions (e.g., cultural or regulating services) or direct benefits to local communities. Future studies should therefore explore additional ecosystem service metrics and possibly employ longitudinal or multi-city comparative designs to build on our findings. These efforts would help address the limitations outlined above and deepen our understanding of how biophysical heterogeneity drives urban ecosystem services in tropical regions.

Conclusions

Consistent with our initial hypotheses, the results demonstrate that biophysical components covary across the studied city’s urban landscape, influencing woody plant community structure, diversity, and green space composition. These components also play a key role in regulating the terrestrial carbon sink and shaping the characteristics of the species assemblages involved. The relationship between biophysical heterogeneity and species diversity has been extensively demonstrated in natural environments, offering a valuable framework for mosaic-level ecological assessments. Such assessments are particularly relevant for human-dominated landscapes, yet they have often been neglected in urban contexts.

Our study introduces novel analytical tools that incorporate biophysical indicators to assess urban biophysical conditions. Our biophysical characterization and quantification of green spaces in Morelia city allowed us to identify landscape-scale parameters and potential reference indicators for assessing the functioning of urban socio-ecosystems under strong anthropogenic pressure. Our approach provides a foundational framework for identifying landscape-scale parameters and potential reference indicators for assessing socio-ecosystem functions.
