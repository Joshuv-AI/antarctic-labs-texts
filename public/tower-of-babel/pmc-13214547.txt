Nanoadjuvant-integrated organic biomaterials for immune engineering: Mechanisms, design strategies, and translational applications
Authors: Shi Tang, Ling Lv, Na Liu, Xuewen Xu, Qasim Khan, Shasha Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune enhancement is a central objective in modern immunotherapy, vaccination, and regenerative medicine; however, achieving a powerful, sustained immune response while reducing overall systemic toxicity remains a major challenge. Organic biomaterials (OBs) are effective immunobioengineering platforms characterized by biocompatibility, tunable physicochemical properties, and the ability to deliver therapeutic cargo at localized, sustained doses; however, most exhibit low intrinsic immunostimulatory activity. Integrating nanoscale adjuvants into OB matrices has therefore emerged as an effective strategy to amplify immune activation by regulating immune recognition, cellular uptake, intracellular trafficking, and downstream signaling pathways. This review presents a mechanistic framework for nanoadjuvant-integrated OBs, focusing on biointerface-mediated immune sensing, complement activation, cellular internalization pathways, and antigen processing and presentation. We have summarized the major classes of natural and synthetic biomaterials, representative nanoadjuvant platforms, and key nanoengineering parameters, including particle size, surface chemistry, mechanical properties, and spatiotemporal release kinetics, that govern immune potency, polarization, and safety. Particular emphasis is placed on molecular mechanisms involving pattern recognition receptor signaling, inflammasome activation, cytokine regulation, immunometabolic reprogramming, and epigenetic remodeling of immune cells. Finally, we have discussed the translational potential of these systems for cancer immunotherapy, vaccination, regenerative medicine, and immune-inflammatory diseases, while highlighting key challenges related to immunotoxicity, biodistribution, manufacturing scalability, and regulatory translation. We have also provided mechanistic insights and rational design principles for the development of next-generation nanoadjuvant biomaterials for immune engineering.Keywords: Nanoadjuvants, Organic biomaterials, Immune cell enhancement, Immuno-bioengineering, Antigen presentation, Cross-presentation, Vaccine delivery, Immunometabolism

Introduction

Immune enhancement: background and clinical rationale

Immune cells not only enable host defense against pathogens but also play essential roles in cancer surveillance, tissue repair, and the maintenance of inflammatory homeostasis [1]. However, achieving controlled and durable immune activation remains a major challenge in many therapeutic contexts. Recent clinical breakthroughs, such as immune checkpoint blockade, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy, and next-generation vaccines, have demonstrated that a suitable increase in immune activity can yield long-term, and in some cases curative, treatment outcomes [2]. Despite these achievements, immune enhancement remains inconsistent among patients. Poor responses to many treatments result from inadequate immune activation, poor immune cell infiltration, defective antigen presentation, or progressive immune exhaustion [3].

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is typically characterized by hypoxia, metabolic competition, suppressive cytokines, and the recruitment of regulatory immune cells, all of induce an immunosuppressive milieu. Similarly, chronic inflammatory diseases and infections may perpetuate immune dysregulation – either hyperactivation of immune cells that leads to tissue destruction, or hyporesponsive cells that prevent disease clearance [[4], [5], [6]]. In addition, patients exhibit heterogeneous immune responses due to differences in genetic background, age, microbiome composition, disease stage, and prior treatments. This in turn translates to a substantial disparity in the delivery of high-quality, long-term immune-enhancing effects in clinical practice [7,8].

Effective immune enhancement must be applied in a controlled and context-dependent manner. For instance, immunostimulatory agents often result in off-target inflammation, cytokine-mediated toxicity, and autoimmunity when administered systemically [9]. Furthermore, some immune agonists have short half-lives and low stability, which necessitates multiple doses. These limitations can be overcome by developing platforms that enable localized, sustained, and programmable immune activation to optimize immune responses, and minimize systemic exposure and chronic toxicity [[10], [11], [12]].

Organic biomaterials in immuno-bioengineering

Organic biomaterials (OBs) have transformed the field of immuno-bioengineering on account of their biocompatibility, controllable chemistry, and dynamic interactions with biological systems. Natural polymers like chitosan, alginate, collagen, gelatin, and hyaluronic acid contain bioactive motifs that enhance tissue integration, migration, and immune cell adhesion include [13,14]. On the other hand, synthetic polymers like (poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), polyethylene glycol (PEG), and polycaprolactone (PCL) offer advantages such as predictable degradability, mechanical stability, and scalability. These materials can be used to formulate hydrogels, porous scaffolds, injectable depots, microspheres, and particulate carriers for a myriad of applications, including vaccination, cancer immunotherapy, tissue regeneration, and inflammation control [15,16].

OBs can be used in immune-based applications as spatiotemporal regulators of immune signaling. They can protect antigens, nucleic acids, or cytokines from rapid degradation, allow sustained or controlled release of the cargo, and target immune responses to specific organs, such as tumors, lymphoid organs, or wounds [17,18]. Additionally, the mechanical properties, surface chemistry, and degradation by-products of OBs can influence immune cell behavior by regulating macrophage polarization, dendritic cell (DC) maturation, immune cell infiltration, and inflammatory resolution [[19], [20], [21]].

However, most OBs elicit a low innate immunostimulatory response and may also induce immune tolerance or a foreign body response, depending on the formulation and implantation environment. This has necessitated the incorporation of immunostimulatory elements, particularly nanostructures and adjuvants, into organic matrices to promote immune responses and biocompatibility. As a result, current efforts are focused on adapting hybrid immuno-biomaterials to achieve a balance between immune activation, tissue compatibility, and long-term safety [22,23].

Nanostructures as adjuvants: significance and rationale

Adjuvants are used in vaccine formulations to enhance antigen immunogenicity and promote adaptive immune responses. In this review, we have uses the term “nanoadjuvant” in a broader immune-engineering context to describe nanoscale materials that enhance immune activation through mechanisms such as improved antigen delivery, modulation of innate immune signaling pathways, facilitation of antigen presentation and cross-presentation, and regulation of immune-cell recruitment and polarization. When incorporated into organic biomaterial matrices, these nanoscale components function not only as classical vaccine adjuvants, but also as programmable elements capable of modulating immune responses in cancer immunotherapy, regenerative medicine, and immune-inflammatory diseases.

Nanoparticles have attracted considerable attention as immune adjuvants due to their ability to enhance immunity at the physical and molecular level, which is not achievable with soluble preparations. Nanoscale materials have large surface-to-volume ratios and can be readily engineered by controlling size, shape, surface charge, and ligand density. In addition, these materials can readily interact with immune cells and immune receptors [[24], [25], [26]]. Nanostructures can stimulate uptake of antigens through DCs and macrophages, control intracellular trafficking, and augment antigen processing and cross-presentation – which are key steps in the activation of cytotoxic T cell responses [27]. Furthermore, recent studies have demonstrated that nanoscale biointerfaces can regulate immune activation through receptor-mediated recognition and controlled antigen presentation mechanisms [28].

Apart from delivering antigens, nanoadjuvants can proactively regulate innate immune signaling by engaging pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), increasing cytokine production, and promoting immune polarization [29]. The display of multivalent antigens, regulated intracellular routing, and long-term retention at sites of immune activation further enhance the magnitude and longevity of responses. Nanoadjuvants with improved biodistribution, cellular uptake pathways, intracellular fate, and safety profiles have been designed in recent years [25,30]. In addition, nanoadjuvants incorporated into hydrogels, microspheres, or scaffolds can provide long-term, localized immune stimulation at targeted sites while minimizing systemic exposure and off-target toxicity. In the context of cancer immunotherapy, local tumor immune activation can remodel the tumor-suppressive microenvironment without causing systemic inflammation [[31], [32], [33], [34]]. Furthermore, nanostructures can be designed as multifunctional vectors that enable targeting, imaging, stimuli-responsive delivery, and combination immunotherapy. Overall, the integration of nanostructures into OB matrices represents a versatile and clinically relevant class of immune-modulating platforms that combine delivery specificity with immunological efficacy [35,36].

Scope and organization of the review

The purpose of this review is to present a mechanistic and translational view of combining OB matrices with nanoadjuvant components to regulate immune recognition, cellular uptake, intracellular processing, signaling activation, and immune cell fate decisions [37,38]. Briefly, we have provided an overview of the immune system and the early immune recognition events that occur at these biointerfaces. In addition, the natural and synthetic OBs used to fabricate immunomodulatory matrices, and the different classes of nanostructures employed as adjuvants have been described. We have also highlighted the impact of specific design parameters, including nanoscale architecture, surface chemistry, mechanical properties, degradation behavior, and spatiotemporal release, on their immune activity, safety, and intracellular behavior [39,40]. Lastly, we have discussed the translational applications in cancer immunotherapy, vaccination, regenerative medicine, and inflammatory diseases, and address key challenges related to toxicity, biodistribution, scalability, reproducibility, and regulatory translation. This review is intended to guide readers from foundational concepts to clinically applicable design plans, and to define the next directions for accurate and intelligent immune-enhancing biomaterials [41].

Immune system basis for biomaterial-driven enhancement

Innate immune activation relevant to biomaterial responses

The innate immune system is the first line of defense against pathogens, and mainly comprises of physical barriers, chemical defenses, and immune cells including macrophages, DCs, neutrophils, and natural killer (NK) cells. Macrophages have the capacity to differentiate into pro-inflammatory (M1-like) and pro-healing (M2-like) phenotypes depending on environmental cues, and mediate tissue regeneration, inflammation, and anti-tumor immune responses. Neutrophils are involved in early inflammatory cascades, while DCs integrate danger signals to prime immune cells against foreign antigens (Fig. 1a) [42,44,45]. Innate immune cells can also rapidly sense biomaterials following their implantation or injection through phagocytosis, cytokine secretion, and antigen uptake [44]. These early interactions are highly sensitive to biomaterial characteristics, including surface chemistry, dimensions, charge, mechanical hardness, and degradation behavior, which control the recruitment, activation, and polarization of immune cells.

The ability to regulate and localize innate immune activation is a key property of biomaterial-mediated immune enhancement. Biomaterials serve as “depots” of antigens and immunostimulatory molecules, and release them in a sustained manner, thereby encouraging DC recruitment and maturation, followed by antigen processing and presentation to T cells [46]. The development of a localized immune niche not only enhances the intensity and duration of native activation, and also enables regulated resolution (Fig. 2a). Moreover, OB nanostructures can mimic pathogen-related physical and molecular characteristics, which promote their uptake by innate immune cells via engagement of PRRs. A combination of these interventions elicits robust innate immune responses and suppresses systemic inflammation, foreign body responses, and tissue damage [48].

Adaptive immunity and antigen-specific priming

Adaptive immunity facilitates antigen-specific recognition and long-term immune memory, and is therefore necessary for successful long-term vaccination and cancer immunotherapy. The adaptive immune response is generally triggered by the uptake of antigens by DCs and macrophages, and the presentation of antigenic peptides on cell membranes via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and class II molecules [49,50]. MHC class II presentation stimulates CD4+ helper T cells, which orchestrate immune responses by producing cytokines and facilitating B-cell antibody production. MHC class I presentation, including cross-presentation, triggers CD8+ cytotoxic T cells to kill infected or malignant cells (Fig. 1b) [43,51,52].

Biomaterials enhance adaptive immune priming by stabilizing antigens, regulating antigen release kinetics, and targeting them to professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs). Furthermore, the nanoadjuvants in the OBs can increase lymph node trafficking and APC targeting depending on their size, surface chemistry, and biomimetic structure (Fig. 2b and c) [47,53]. Notably, nanoadjuvant systems can regulate intracellular antigen trafficking and facilitate endosomal escape, and promote effective cross-presentation to CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2d) [47,54]. Biomaterial design, in turn, determines whether adaptive responses are steady and protective or feeble, tolerogenic, or exhausted. Localized and regulated stimulation supports vigorous effector cell growth and immune memory while reducing immune-associated toxicity [55,56].

Key immune endpoints for immune enhancement

Immune enhancement is defined not by increased inflammation solely, but by functional outcomes – mainly innate immune activation, maturation of APCs, cytokine polarization, cytotoxic effector function, and long-term immune memory – in relation to the therapeutic goal. In the context of innate immunity, DC maturation enhances antigen uptake and promotes the production of balanced cytokines that do not cause excessive toxicity but facilitate effective priming [57,58]. On the other hand, adaptive immunity is boosted through increased CD8+ T-cell expansion, enhanced antibody responses, and sustained memory T- and B-cell development (Table 1) [99,100].

Biological materials enable systematic optimization of these endpoints by rationally controlling delivery, mechanics, and spatiotemporal signaling. Release kinetics determine whether immune activation is transient or sustained, and material architecture defines how the immune system penetrates the material and interacts with the encapsulated cells [101,102]. The nanostructures also enhance immune responses by increasing cell uptake, lymph node access, and intracellular trafficking, thereby improving the effectiveness and quality of antigen presentation and cross-priming (Fig. 2b–d) [103]. Notably, immune enhancement should be clinically assessed by the quality of response rather than its magnitude – i.e., the production of polyfunctional effector cells and the maintenance of immune memory are more significant than transient inflammatory responses [104].

Immunosuppression and immune escape in disease microenvironments

Various diseases are associated with repressed immunity, which makes immune augmentation particularly challenging in clinical settings. For instance, tumors create an immunosuppressive microenvironment characterized by hypoxia, low pH, metabolic competition, and the production of inhibitory cytokines, such as TGF-β and IL-10 [105]. In addition, immunosuppressive populations, such as regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), also infiltrate the tumor microenvironment (TME), and restrain antigen presentation and inhibit cytotoxic T cell activity (Fig. 1a). As in chronic infections and inflammatory disorders, immune exhaustion in the TME is driven by ongoing antigen exposure, leading to a dysfunctional immune response despite activation [106,107].

Immune enhancement measures based on biomaterials aim to overcome these effects by modulating the local immune landscape. OBs may be engineered to provide immunostimulatory signals to suppressive tissues, enhance immune cell recruitment and activation, while reducing systemic exposure [35,108]. While nanoadjuvants enhance local immune signaling, controlled release systems maintain immune stimulation long enough to overcome immune shutdown and immune evasion (Fig. 2a–c) [47]. Furthermore, biomaterials can also be used to deliver checkpoint inhibitors, cytokines, or metabolic modulators in vivo to specifically target immunosuppressive pathways. Therefore, the key to translating nanoadjuvant OBs into clinically relevant immune-enhancing therapies is o overcome immune suppression [109,110].

Biointerface events governing early immune sensing of biomaterials

Protein adsorption and nanobiointerface formation

When biomaterials and nanostructures come into contact with biological fluids, they are immediately coated with serum- and tissue-derived proteins, resulting in the formation of a dynamic nanobiointerface (Fig. 3a), which actively controls receptor interactions, cellular adhesion, and immune activation cues by shaping immune cell interactions with and perception of the material [[111], [112], [113]]. Typically, the adsorbed protein corona is composed of albumin, fibrinogen, immunoglobulins, complement components, and extracellular matrix proteins, and the relative abundance of the different proteins depends heavily on the surface chemistry, hydrophobicity, charge, and roughness of nanoscale features (Table 2) [172,173] (see Table 3).

Nanostructures also regulate the formation of biointerfaces by increasing surface area and providing curvature-dependent binding forces that selectively enrich or deplete specific protein classes. It is important to note that protein adsorption is often associated with conformational changes, such as partial unfolding, that contribute to immune recognition [116,174]. Immunogenic cells can make contact with the nanoparticle-protein corona complex via non-specific interactions mediated by physicochemical characteristics (e.g., size, charge, aggregation state), or the recognition of opsonins and immunogenic proteins (Fig. 3a). These interactions create emergent properties at the nano-bio interface that ultimately determine early immune responses [175,176].

In the case of nanoadjuvant OBs, initial recognition events are especially decisive, as they determine downstream immune activation. Protein adsorption can stimulate or inhibit immunostimulatory properties and enhance immune cell uptake, or suppress immunostimulatory properties and weaken the effect of adjuvants [177]. Moreover, the protein corona is dynamic and responds to competitive exchange, enzymatic degradation, and mechanical forces in vivo. This temporal evolution can modify the biodistribution, immune-cell specificity, and inflammatory potential of OB nanostructures, particularly when applied to injectable nanosystems. To rationally design biomaterials that enhance immune responses, precise control is required over surface characteristics and the presentation of nanostructures to direct biointerface assembly and achieve desired immune responses without inducing unwanted immune clearance or inflammation [178,179].

Complement activation, opsonization, and immune recruitment

Complement activation is a potent innate immune response that often follows protein adsorption on the biomaterial. It may occur via the classical, lectin, or alternative pathways, and is driven by the surface chemistry of the biomaterial, as well as the composition of the adsorbed protein layer (Fig. 3b–Table 2) [111,180]. The activation of these pathways culminates in C3 cleavage, resulting in the production of opsonins such as C3b and iC3b, which tag the biomaterials for immune recognition and enhance their phagocytosis by macrophages and DCs. Simultaneously, the anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a recruit and activate neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages at the biomaterial interface [181,182]. Complement-mediated recruitment in turn can facilitate antigen presentation and immune priming by enhancing APC density and local inflammation. Nevertheless, complement activation is not carried out in isolation [111]. Rather, it is closely associated with coagulation and contact activation systems, which entail platelet adhesion, fibrin deposition, thrombin generation, and von Willebrand factor binding at the biomaterial surface. These cascades interact to amplify both inflammatory signals and vascular responses, thereby increasing immune cell recruitment and the risk of tissue destruction and excessive inflammation (Fig. 3b) [[183], [184], [185]].

From a translational perspective, complement overactivation is a challenge as it may lead to rapid clearance of nanoadjuvants, excessive inflammation, tissue damage, and hypersensitivity. Thus, it is imperative to ensure that complement opsonization is sufficient to promote immune cell uptake and activation, without reducing tolerability and functional longevity [186]. Surface shielding, charge modulation, and controlled ligand presentation can achieve predictable immune recruitment, reduce pathological inflammation, and also minimize inter-patient variation in immune responses [19,45].

Foreign body response, fibrosis, and chronic inflammation

The foreign body response (FBR) is a chronic manifestation of early biointerface-activated immune responses, and a significant impediment to the long-term functionality of biomaterials. After the first wave of protein adsorption and immune cell recruitment, macrophages adhere to the biomaterial surface and can fuse to form multinucleated giant cells at foreign-body sites [187,188]. This in turn triggers fibroblast stimulation and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, resulting in the formation of a fibrotic capsule that physically isolates the biomaterial from the surrounding tissue. Fibrotic encapsulation limits nutrient diffusion, reduces drug release efficacy, and impedes immune-cell infiltration, ultimately diminishing the therapeutic potential of immune-enhancing biomaterials (Table 2) [140].

FBR-related chronic inflammation is particularly problematic for nanoadjuvant systems intended for chronic or repeated use. Constant complement activation, cytokine signaling, and macrophage activation may favor tissue damage, implant failure, and immune exhaustion. Notably, these changes result from chronic biointerface signaling rather than isolated immune events (Fig. 3b) [19,189]. Biomaterial characteristics, including stiffness, porosity, degradation dynamics, and surface nanotopography, play a critical role in determining whether FBR remains controlled or progresses to fibrosis. Based on this, effective immune-enhancing OBs should be formulated to suppress fibrotic reactions while maintaining desirable immune activation [108,145].

Immune tolerance versus persistent activation outcomes

The biointerface events discussed above can culminate in immune tolerance, controlled immune activation, or persistent inflammation. Immune tolerance and minimal inflammation are often required to maintain the biocompatibility of tissue-engineering scaffolds or long-term drug depots [34,190]. By contrast, cancer immunotherapy platforms and vaccines require regulated immune stimulation to facilitate DC maturation and effective T-cell priming. This balance is governed by early biointerface cues, including protein corona composition, complement activation strength, and immune cell recruitment kinetics (Fig. 3) [23,191,192]. Nanoadjuvant OBs can achieve localized immune activation, which enhances antigen presentation and immune recruitment without causing systemic inflammation [23,193]. At the same time, the surface chemistry, degradation patterns, and temporal cues of these OBs can be modulated to constrain chronic inflammation and foreign-body encapsulation. Studies show that immune responses depend on the order and duration of biointerface-mediated stimuli; coupling early memory activation along with immune resolution cues can prevent chronic inflammation [21,194]. Therefore, mechanistic knowledge of biointerface events is necessary to predict the outcomes of immune processes and construct biomaterial systems that induce or suppress immune responses in a context-specific manner [195].

OBs as immunomodulatory matrices

Natural OBs (polysaccharides, proteins, ECM-based)

Natural OBs have been widely studied in immuno-bioengineering as they resemble components of the native ECM, and possess intrinsic bioactivity that facilitates cell adhesion, migration, and tissue integration. Dextran, hyaluronic acid, alginate, and chitosan are routinely used to fabricate injectable hydrogels, wound dressings, microspheres, and vaccine depots due to their biocompatibility, and tunable gelation and degradation [196]. These materials elicit immune cell responses through charge-dependent interactions, receptor binding, and cytokine signaling. Notably, the cationic features of chitosan facilitate antigen loading and immune cell recruitment. On the other hand, hyaluronic acid can modulate macrophage polarization depending on its molecular weight and degradation, thereby affecting inflammatory and regenerative responses [197].

Biomaterials based on proteins and ECMs, such as collagen, gelatin, fibrin, and decellularized ECM scaffolds, contain structural cues and biochemical ligands that regulate immune cell behavior. By simulating native microenvironments, these matrices facilitate immune cell invasion and strengthen the resolution of inflammation during tissue repair. ECM-mimetic systems can also be used to rebuild local, tissue-specific niches that promote immune compatibility, functional recovery, and regeneration, rather than immune activation (Fig. 4) [198,199]. Nevertheless, natural biomaterials are inherently heterogenous given their biological complexity; thus, variations in the source, purification methods, and batch consistency of these biomaterials can lead to variable immune responses. Furthermore, residual immunogenic components or contaminants may inadvertently trigger inflammatory responses; therefore, it is imperative to process and tune the function to achieve predictable, reproducible immunomodulatory effects [200].

OB-based immunomodulatory matrices in tissues and disease. Natural and synthetic biomaterial systems, such as microspheres and hydrogels, exemplify surface engineering, sustained release, tissue-specific immune regulation, macrophage polarization, and inflammation repair for regenerative medicine in neural, musculoskeletal, and reproductive microenvironments [198].

Synthetic OBs (polyesters, PEG-based systems, hydrogels)

Synthetic OBs are highly designable, reproducible, and scalable, and therefore of special interest for clinical translation. Biodegradable polyesters such as PLGA, PLA, and PCL have broad applications in immune engineering as they can be fabricated into microspheres, nanoparticles, and scaffolds with well-defined degradation and release kinetics [15]. These systems can be particularly useful for long-term delivery of antigens, cytokines, and immunomodulatory drugs. In addition to vaccination, polyester microspheres are increasingly used to modulate immune responses in regenerative and inflammatory environments through local, sustained, but not acute, immunostimulation (Fig. 4) [201].

PEG-based systems and synthetic hydrogels provide additional control over immune interactions by enabling surface shielding, crosslinking density, and adjustable mechanical properties. PEGylation reduces extravasation and clearance of non-specific proteins, favoring sustained residence and sustained immunomodulation [195]. It is also possible to design synthetic hydrogels that respond to local cues, such as pH, enzyme activity, or oxidative stress, thereby enabling the timely release of immune cues in response to tissue damage or inflammation. Such materials are particularly effective in reducing excessive inflammation, suppressing immune hyperactivity, and stimulating tissue repair, as demonstrated in microsphere-based systems (Fig. 4) [202]. Nonetheless, as synthetic biomaterials typically do not inherently elicit immunostimulatory signals, they are generally functionalized or complexed with immunostimulatory cargos to induce desired immune responses [18].

Degradation behavior and immune microenvironment remodeling

One of the most important factors determining the long-term immune responses to OBs is their degradation behavior. The degradation of the biomaterials alters their mechanical stability and porosity, which in turn affects the release of the encapsulated immunomodulators [21]. In addition, the byproducts of degradation can directly affect the local immune landscape by altering the pH, redox status, or metabolite levels. For instance, the acidic products generated during PLGA degradation have been shown to inflammatory processes. Conversely, enzymatically damaged natural matrices can support the progressive migration of immune cells and the resolution of inflammation [203].

Remarkably, degradation-initiated immune remodeling can also be harnessed to favor tissue repair. Transient processes that alternate between early inflammatory and later regenerative or immunosuppressive conditions are achieved using sustained or stimulus-responsive degradable biomaterials [204]. Controlled degradation has the potential to provide immune signals on therapeutically relevant time scales, as exemplified by degradable microspheres that polarize macrophages toward pro-regenerative phenotypes (Fig. 4). In addition, the degradation of biomatrices can reveal new functional properties or elicit secondary signals in the presence of nanoadjuvants or immune-regulatory drugs, thereby enhancing immune programming. Thus, biomaterial degradation is not only a significant design variable for modulating the immune microenvironment but also a major clearance mechanism [205,206].

Advantages and limitations in immune engineering

The use of OBs in immune engineering offers several advantages, including biocompatibility, structural biomimicry, and local immune regulation. Their flexibility enables fabrication of various platforms, such as injectable depots, microspheres, and implantable scaffolds, for vaccination, tissue regeneration, and the treatment of chronic inflammation [207,208]. Organic matrices safeguard fragile immune cargos, provide greater stability, and enable in vivo delivery profiles that support long-term immune control. Moreover, the mechanics and architecture of these matrices can be designed to control immune cell infiltration, polarization, and functional persistence without complete dependence on molecular signaling. These characteristics make OBs particularly well suited to precision immunomodulation strategies that are both effective and safe (Fig. 4) [209,210].

Despite these benefits, there are certain constraints in using OB matrices. A variety of OBs are weakly immunostimulatory and may require combining with nanoadjuvants or immune-active ligands to elicit robust immune responses. Certain formulations may cause foreign-body reactions, fibrosis, or chronic inflammation if poorly adapted to the biological environment. Natural biomaterials are also heterogenous and prone to contamination, whereas synthetic systems typically require extensive functionalization to favor the desired immune interactions. Moreover, immune responses may differ across tissues and disease conditions, which further complicates translation [195]. The most promising immunomodulatory platforms are hybrid systems that combine organic biomaterial matrices, controlled-release microspheres, nanoadjuvants, and immune-regulatory agents to elicit predictable, tissue-specific, and clinically translatable immune responses [21].

Nanostructures as adjuvants: types and functional roles

Inorganic nanostructures as immune adjuvants

Inorganic nanostructures have been extensively studied as immune adjuvants owing to their physicochemical stability, composition, and material properties. As shown in Fig. 5a, this group comprises metallic and metal-oxide nanoparticles (e.g., gold, silver, iron oxides), mineral-based systems (e.g., calcium phosphate), and carbon-based nanomaterials (e.g., graphene, fullerenes, carbon nanotubes) [211,213]. These platforms are characterized by rigid cores, large surface areas, tunable surface charge, optical responsiveness, field-strength effects, and redox-active properties, which, when combined, make them distinct from softer organic systems [214].

(a) Overview of organic, inorganic, carbon-based, hybrid, and bioinspired nanostructures, highlighting key physicochemical features that govern immune interactions, including surface charge, mechanical strength, optical or field-responsive behavior, redox activity, biodegradability, intracellular delivery, and controlled release [211]. (b) Representative biomedical and industrial application domains of nanobiomaterials, illustrating their roles in diagnostics, cancer therapy, pharmacology and medicine, and analytical chemistry [212].

Inorganic nanostructures have been shown to enhance immune responses by increasing antigen adsorption, and by presenting antigens at high density and in multivalent form at the nanobiointerface. Their rigidity and surface biochemistry can facilitate antigen uptake by APCs and induce innate immune responses via stress-related signaling pathways, inflammasome interactions, or reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated mechanisms [215]. In particular, carbon-based nanomaterials are characterized by high mechanical strength, large specific surface area, and the ability to modulate oxidative processes, thereby influencing innate immune sensing. Therefore, inorganic platforms tend to exhibit adjuvant properties despite the absence of other molecular agonists [211,216,217].

When incorporated into organic biomaterial matrices, inorganic nanoadjuvants can elicit sustained, localized immune responses and enhance mechanical integrity. Mineral-like particles can act as antigen depots that release antigens over time, and mesoporous silica systems enable high drug loading and co-delivery of antigens and immunostimulatory cues [23,218]. However, the stability of these structures raises concerns regarding long-term persistence, accumulation, and cytotoxicity. Translational application of inorganic nanoadjuvants at the design-synthesis level necessitates a balance between immune strength and safety, controlled by size, dose, and degradation characteristics [25].

Organic nanostructures and self-assembled nanoadjuvants

Organic nanostructures, including lipid-based systems (liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles), polymeric nanostructures (polymeric nanoparticles, micelles, polymersomes, core-shell nanospheres), and protein-based assemblies such as core-shell nanoparticles, ferritin nanoparticles, and virus-like particles, are the most biocompatible and clinically advanced type of immune adjuvant (Fig. 5a). These systems are characterized by modifiable surfaces, membrane fusion, intracellular delivery, biodegradability, and release control [219].

Organic nanoadjuvants primarily stimulate the immune response by shielding antigens, nucleic acids, and immune agonists from degradation, and facilitating their uptake by antigen-presenting cells. Their particulate disposition facilitates endocytic internalization and regulates intracellular trafficking, enabling antigen processing and, in most instances, cross-presentation [220]. Organic nanostructures can be used in vaccine development to replicate the size and structure of pathogens, eliciting robust antibody and T-cell responses, while demonstrating favorable safety profiles. Repetitive antigen display is also observed in protein-based nanostructures and virus-like particles, which are particularly effective at eliciting B-cell responses [221].

The nanoadjuvants are self-assembled, which provides further design flexibility through non-covalent interactions, such as hydrophobic assembly, electrostatic complexation, and peptide organization. Using these methods, the strength of immune activation, release kinetics, and intracellular fate can be optimally regulated without aggressive crosslinking using harsh chemicals. By incorporating OBs, nanostructures can serve as both delivery vehicles and immune modulators that enable sustained, localized immune stimulation. However, the shortcomings in their performance may be attributable to protein exchange, lipid redistribution, or premature in vivo disassembly, underscoring the importance of formulation strategies such as surface shielding or structural stabilization [222].

Hybrid and bioinspired nanostructures for immune enhancement

Hybrid nanostructures are fabricated by combining organic and inorganic materials to achieve multifunctionality that is not attainable with either material alone. These systems primarily consist of inorganic cores with organic shells, polymer-mineral composites, and nanomaterials within hydrogels or scaffolds (Fig. 5a) [223,224]. Inorganic layers are used to provide stability, optical or magnetic functionality, and high loading capacity, whereas organic layers provide biocompatibility, targeting, and immune regulation [225].

Staged immune signaling, in which initial innate activation is followed by prolonged antigen exposure through antigen release (or degradation), is particularly well suited to hybrid platforms. Bio-mimetic nanostructures can be further enhanced with biological motifs, such as cell membranes, extracellular vesicles, and ECM-inspired architectures [223,226]. Membrane-coated nanoparticles and exosome-like vesicles take advantage of their endogenous biological identity to stimulate interactions between immune cells or regulate immune recognition, but use filamentous and nanofiber-based nanostructures to stimulate antigen-presenting activity. Although these systems appear to have strong immune-shaping potential, their complex structure and composition preclude large-scale manufacturing, standardization, and regulatory approval [227,228].

Functional roles: immune activation, delivery, and immune shaping

Nanoparticles can support three interrelated functions in the field of immune-engineering – immune activation, delivery enhancement, and immunomodulation. Nanoadjuvants enhance innate immune activation by increasing antigen uptake, pattern recognition, and cytokine signaling, thereby facilitating immune priming [229]. Second, they improve delivery by stabilizing cargo, regulating release and sequencing, and enhancing the accessibility of immune cells and lymphoid tissues. Third, nanoscale structures can modulate the immune response by regulating antigen-presenting pathways, immunopolarization, and the balance between pro-inflammatory and immunoregulatory responses [37]. When incorporated into organic biomaterial matrices, nanoadjuvants can achieve spatiotemporal immune programming by accumulating at the injection site and releasing immune cues in a regulated manner. The localized release minimizes systemic toxicity while maintaining immune stimulation to support effector function and memory development. The translational potential of nanostructure-enabled immune modulation in diagnostics, cancer therapeutics, pharmacology, and other biomedical applications has been illustrated in Fig. 5b [26,212]. Design parameters governing nanoadjuvant activity in OBs

Size, shape, surface charge, and topography effects

Nanoscale physicochemical parameters, including particle size, shape, surface charge, and surface topography, strongly influence biodistribution, immune organ recruitment, and interactions with immune cells (Fig. 6a) [259,261]. Particle size has a crucial effect on lymphatic drainage, tissue retention, and uptake by APCs. Smaller nanostructures tend to be trafficked to the lymph node and are efficiently internalized by the resident DCs and macrophages, thereby promoting efficient immune priming. By contrast, larger particles are more likely to remain at the injection or implantation site and form local immune depots, which provide long-term antigen exposure and sustained innate activation [262]. Particle shape also controls immune responses by changing membrane contact geometry, phagocytic efficiency, and intracellular trafficking signatures. Prolonged membrane wrapping or ineffective phagocytosis caused by non-spherical geometries, such as rods, fibers, or plate-like structures, can result in distinct cytokine profiles and immune activation/tolerization [262,263]. The size and shape of OBs also influence their circulation time and retention at target sites, thereby regulating the duration of exposure of immune cells to adjuvant cues [264,265].

Surface charge and nanotopography control initial immune recognition mechanisms through protein adsorption, opsonization, and receptor interactions at the nanobiointerface. Positively charged nanomaterials often exhibit increased cellular uptake via electrostatic interactions with negatively charged cell membranes. Nevertheless, such a benefit is typically accompanied by increased cytotoxicity, membrane perturbation, and non-specific inflammation. On the other hand, neutral or slightly negative surfaces are likely to minimize unwanted immune clearance and off-target organ retention, consistent with their biodistribution and safety profiles (Fig. 6b) [172,260]. Innate immune sensing may be further enhanced by nanotopographical features, including surface roughness, porosity, and curvature, which mimic pathogen-associated physical features, cytoskeletal remodeling, inflammasome activation, and downstream signaling. Taken together, rational optimization of the size, shape, charge, and topography of OBs enables precise control of immune targeting, activation strength, and safety effects (Table 5) [293].

Surface chemistry, ligand density, and molecular patterning

Surface chemistry, along with physical form, is a defining factor in the immunogenicity, modulation, or evasion of nanoadjuvants. The functional groups and surface coatings of OBs influence hydrophobicity, electrostatic interactions, and hydrogen bonding, thereby shaping protein corona structure and the accessibility of immune receptors [294]. PEGylation, zwitterionic coating, and charge modulation are among the most commonly used surface-engineering strategies to enhance host compatibility, minimize non-specific accumulation, and extend circulation/residence times (Fig. 6b) [295]. These stealth methods are particularly relevant for avoiding rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocytic system and for controlling unwanted immune responses [296].

Surface functionalization with immune-stimulatory or targeting ligands enables selective interactions with immune cells and tissues. Carbohydrates, peptides, antibodies, or small molecules can serve as ligands that activate DCs, induce phagocytosis by macrophages, or target lymphoid tissues. Of particular concern are ligand density and molecular patterning, since immune receptors recognize multivalent clustering rather than single-ligand interactions. Moderate to high ligand density can increase receptor occupancy and amplify signaling pathways; on the other hand, very high density can lead to overstimulation, immune exhaustion, or clearance. Mimicking microbial epitope distances, also known as patterning, has been shown to enhance antigen recognition and adaptive priming capacity [297].

Spatial patterning of ligands also contributes to immune modulation by skewing responses toward particular polarization states, e.g., Th1-biased cytotoxic immunity or more regulatory phenotypes, depending on therapeutic goals. Moreover, specific ligands can be incorporated to selectively target certain subsets of immune cells, such as DC subsets or macrophage phenotypes. By combining rational control of surface chemistry, ligand density, and molecular architecture, nanoadjuvant systems can precisely modulate immune responses without compromising safety or translational stability [298].

Mechanical cues: stiffness, viscoelasticity, and porosity

The mechanical properties of biomaterials, including stiffness, viscoelasticity, and porosity, actively regulate immune cell behavior. Mechanotransduction signaling in immune cells regulates adhesion, migration, polarization, and cytokine release in response to mechanical stimuli. For example, substrate stiffness can induce macrophage polarization to pro-inflammatory or pro-healing phenotypes [299]. In addition, the motility and antigen-presenting efficiency of DCs have been shown to improve in matrices with optimal viscoelasticity. These biomechanical cues work in concert with biochemical signals and may induce dramatic changes in immune responses, even when the nanoadjuvant preparations remain constant [300].

Porosity and microarchitecture also exert additional control over immune cell infiltration, spatial distribution, and the diffusion of nutrients and oxygen. Extremely porous matrices with overlapping pore networks facilitate the migration and infiltration of immune cells, long-term antigen presentation, and long-term immune priming [301]. Conversely, thick or poorly connected structures may hinder immune invasion, promote fibrotic encapsulation, and reduce treatment efficacy, consistent with the safety factors (Fig. 6b). Nanoscale biochemical signal interactions with macroscale mechanical signal responses elicit immune responses when nanoadjuvants are ensconced within mechanically tuned matrices. Therefore, optimization of nanoadjuvants and matrix mechanics can achieve effective yet controlled immune response with minimal chronic inflammation [195].

Spatiotemporal control: degradation and release kinetics

The most significant advantage offered by nanoadjuvant OBs is the high spatiotemporal control of immune stimulation. Degradation kinetics of the OBs influence the release of immune cargos and the formation of the local immune niche during treatment [108]. It is necessary to control the retention time and targeted release of the cargo in order to achieve immunological objectives, such as long-term priming, tumor-specific activation, and immune organ recruitment (Fig. 6a). Rapid degradation may result in transient immune activation insufficient to generate memory, whereas slow degradation may lead to prolonged inflammation and an exaggerated response to foreign bodies, thereby complicating clearance [302,303].

The release dynamics, i.e., timing and sequence, also determine immune activation. The early release of chemokines or danger signals can recruit APCs, which subsequently present antigens and promote T cell priming, expansion, and differentiation. Nanoadjuvants enable the delivery of multiple cargos at varying rates, either in stages or sequentially, or unlock immunostimulatory surfaces through progressive degradation. Furthermore, stimuli-responsive systems can achieve more controlled release in the presence of acidic pH, oxidative stress, and enzymes, which increases targeting specificity and minimizes off-target effects [304].

Nanoadjuvant OBs enable programmed immune amplification with controlled degradation and release, thereby balancing potency and safety. This spatiotemporal programmability is best exemplified in vaccines and immunotherapies, where the immunological duration and stimulation site significantly affect efficacy, durability, and tolerability. In this regard, kinetic degradation and release are key design factors that can be incorporated into a nanoadjuvant biomaterial system for clinically viable immunogenic therapies.

Cellular uptake and intracellular processing of nanoadjuvant systems

Endocytosis pathways and immune cell-specific uptake

DCs and macrophages are the major cellular targets of nanoadjuvant systems as they are physiologically adapted to take up particles. However, internalization of nanostructures and their associated cargos can adversely affect the immune cells, and therefore influence their efficacy. Nanoadjuvant OBs are taken up by immune cells through various pathways, including clathrin-mediated endocytosis, caveolin-dependent uptake, macropinocytosis, and phagocytosis, each with distinct molecular machinery and downstream trafficking pathways (Fig. 7b) [306]. The uptake mechanism and the extent of internalization depend on particle size, shape, and surface chemistry. Aggregated or larger nanostructures tend to be phagocytosed, whereas smaller nanoadjuvants usually enter cells via clathrin-mediated or caveolin-associated pathways, resulting in distinct intracellular fates.

(b) Major endocytic pathways governing nanostructure internalization, highlighting clathrin-mediated, caveolin-dependent, macropinocytic, and phagocytic routes, their molecular machinery, and convergence on endosomal-lysosomal trafficking that shapes antigen processing and immune outcomes [306].

The specificity of immune cells also influences nanoadjuvant uptake and the ensuing immune effects. For instance, DCs can recognize specific ligands on the surface of OBs and internalize them via receptor-mediated endocytosis, thereby facilitating efficient antigen presentation. In contrast, macrophages internalize particles via non-specific adherence to scavenger receptors, macropinocytosis, or opsonization-mediated phagocytosis, leading to rapid clearance or polarization. Other immune cells, such as neutrophils and B cells, interact with nanostructures via more specific processes, including rapid engulfment and B cell receptor-mediated uptake. Consequently, immune-enhancing responses are not only determined by the uptake of the nanoadjuvant, but also the type of immune subset that internalizes the adjuvant. The rational design of nanoadjuvants should thus align with the uptake pathways and targets of the desired immune cells to maximize activation while minimizing premature clearance or hyperinflammation.

Endosomal escape, lysosomal routing, and intracellular fate

Following internalization, nanoadjuvants reach various intracellular locations, and their fate depends on the intensity of immune activation. Internalized nanoscale structures can be trapped by endosomes, transported to lysosomes for degradation, recycled to the plasma membrane, and exocytosed from the cell (Fig. 7a) [305,307]. For most nanoadjuvants, early endosomal-to-late endosomal and lysosomal trafficking exposes the cargo to acidic and proteolytic environments, leading to their degradation. Lysosomal processing facilitates the breakdown of antigens for presentation by MHC class II molecules and stimulates CD4+ T cells. Nevertheless, excessive lysosomal degradation can be a serious impediment in immune applications that require cytotoxic immunity, e.g., cancer immunotherapy [308].

Endosomal escape control is thus a guiding design goal for nanoadjuvant systems to promote CD8+ T cell responses. Nanostructures with pH-responsive polymers, membrane-active peptides, proton-sponge effects, or redox-sensitive release mechanisms can escape lysosomal degradation by translocating to the cytosol or destabilizing endosomal membranes, and access intracellular compartments that are vital to cross-presentation (Fig. 7a) [305]. Although these methods can enhance the effectiveness of cytosolic delivery and immune potency, excessive membrane disruption may lead to cytotoxicity or inflammatory stress. Intracellular fate is therefore the fine line between maintaining antigen integrity and cellular viability, which is directly correlated to the design of nanoadjuvants, and immune efficacy and safety [25].

Antigen processing and cross-presentation efficiency

The mechanistic interface between nanoadjuvant uptake and adaptive immune activation is antigen processing. In the classical pathway, antigens degraded within endolysosomal compartments are loaded onto MHC class II molecules and presented to CD4+ helper T cells. By contrast, cross-presentation enables exogenous antigens to be presented on MHC class I molecules, thereby activating CD8+ cytotoxic T cells. The efficiency of cross-presentation is highly sensitive to the ability of the antigens to avoid endosomal degradation and access cytosolic or non-degradative routes (Fig. 7a and b). It is a fundamental process in anti-tumor and antiviral immunity, and its effectiveness is limited with soluble antigens [309].

Nanoadjuvant systems can stabilizing antigens and facilitate their cytosolic delivery, thus steering intracellular trafficking towards pathways favorable to MHC class I presentation. This process is further promoted by the incorporation of nanoadjuvants into OBs, which maintains antigen availability and supports the recruitment and maturation of DCs. Additionally, nanoscale structures can be used to co-deliver immune agonists to upregulate antigen-processing machinery and costimulatory signaling. Since the efficiency of cross-presentation is determined by antigen form, degradation rate, and trafficking dynamics, the ability to design effective nanoadjuvants relies on mechanistic insights into intracellular processing mechanisms that produce cytotoxic responses and long-lasting immune memory [310].

Trafficking-driven differences in immune outcomes

Intracellular trafficking is not merely a transport process; it also shapes immune outcomes by determining which receptors are occupied, which signaling pathways are activated, and which antigen-presenting pathways are most frequently used. As shown in Fig. 7a, endosomal contents, such as nanoadjuvants, may selectively activate endosomal PRRs, thereby promoting MHC class II presentation and favoring helper T cell responses and antibody production [311]. In comparison, nanoadjuvants that gain intracellular access or interact with intracellular membranes can activate cytosolic immune sensors and promote MHC class I cross-presentation, thereby boosting CD8+ T cell responses.

Immune polarization and safety are also affected by trafficking. The continued accumulation of lysosomes can cause cellular stress and inflammatory signaling, and the cytosolic release of inflammatory agonists can further increase the risk of toxicity. The timing of trafficking also plays a critical role: early endosome signaling may drive immune activation, whereas chronic antigen persistence favors sustained priming and memory development. Thus, trafficking control should also be considered in the design of nanoadjuvant OBs, with intracellular fate goal and therapeutic objectives in mind. By combining uptake pathway selection, endosomal escape mechanisms, and regulated intracellular trafficking, nanoadjuvant systems can present immune signals at their respective cellular locations with high efficacy and minimal adverse inflammatory effects [33,312].

Molecular mechanisms of immune enhancement

PRR signaling (TLR/NLR/STING) and inflammasome pathways

PRRs are the key molecular readers of nanoadjuvant biomaterials, which are recognized by immune cells to activate innate immune responses. Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are either expressed on the plasma membrane or localized to endosomal compartments. They can interact directly with adjuvant-associated ligands or be indirectly activated by cellular stress following uptake [30]. Such interactions stimulate inflammatory cytokine production and maturation of APCs by activating NF-|human|- and IRF-dependent signaling cascades. In addition, coordinated PRR signaling strengthens antigen processing, costimulatory molecule expression, and lymph node-directed immune priming at the tissue level, which underlies effective adaptive responses (Fig. 8a) [313,315].

Molecular mechanisms underlying immune enhancement by nanoadjuvant biomaterials. (a) Systems-level integration of PRR-mediated innate sensing, antigen uptake and presentation, lymph node trafficking, inflammasome-associated cytokine release, and coordinated activation of T and B lymphocytes [313]. (b) Convergent danger-sensing pathways leading to NLRP3 inflammasome activation, including disruption of ion homeostasis, mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS signaling, and lysosomal damage, culminating in caspase-1 activation, IL-1 family cytokine maturation, and pyroptotic inflammatory responses [314].

Cytosolic PRRs, specifically NOD-like receptors, are also key players in the immune augmentation mechanism mediated by nanoadjuvants. Some nanomaterials activate the NLRP3 inflammasome via lysosomal destabilization following particulate uptake, perturbation of ion homeostasis (particularly K+ efflux and Ca2+ flux), or mitochondrial stress and ROS production [316]. These unique danger signals all center on NLRP3 assembly, caspase-1 activation, and IL-1 cytokine maturation, thereby amplifying local inflammation and solidifying DC activation. The intersection of these pathways and their downstream effects is illustrated in Fig. 8b [317].

The STING pathway is another cytosolic sensing axis that is particularly relevant in cancer immunotherapy, where cytosolic DNA sensing triggers robust type I interferon production and cytotoxic T cell cross-priming. STING signaling may be augmented by promoting cytosolic delivery of agonists using nanoadjuvant systems, or by increasing signal duration in APCs. Together, the TLR, NLR, and STING pathways constitute a unified PRR network through which nanoadjuvant biomaterials enhance immune activation. The pathway signaling nodes and immune outcomes are outlined in Table 6 [354].

Key molecular pathways engaged by nanoadjuvant biomaterials.

Cytokine/chemokine regulation and immune polarization control

Cytokines and chemokines are key regulators of the recruitment, activation, and functional polarization of immune cells. Nanoadjuvant biomaterials indirectly regulate cytokine production by depleting inflammatory mediators that facilitate T cell and B cell activation, and by modulating the magnitude, timing, and localization of PRR and inflammasome signaling. For example, inflammasome-induced cytokines like IL-1β amplify the local immune response and orchestrate innate and adaptive responses (Fig. 8a) [313,355]. Furthermore, IL-12, TNF-α, and IFN-γ are biased toward Th1 and cytotoxic T cells, which are essential for anti-tumor and anti-viral immunity, whereas IL-10 or TGF-β dominance can lead to regulatory or tolerogenic immune responses that are beneficial in autoimmune or inflammatory diseases but deterimental in cancer immunotherapy. Tuning the balance among cytokines, rather than merely maximizing inflammatory response, is the key to effective immune enhancement [356].

Immune spatial organization and trafficking are also dependent on chemokine signaling. Biomaterial depots can generate local chemokine gradients that attract DCs, macrophages, and effector lymphocytes to vaccination sites, tumors, or damaged tissue. Nanoadjuvant systems promote cell-cell interactions by enhancing immune cell localization and optimizing priming efficacy, as they combine antigen persistence with PRP-mediated chemokine production. Table 6 summarizes the key cytokine-chemokine axes and related polarization patterns in immunity.

Immunometabolism, ROS/redox signaling, and immune training

Immune activation is strongly linked to cellular metabolism, and the effects of nanoadjuvant biomaterials on immune activity are partly mediated by alterations in metabolic and redox conditions. The internalization of nanostructures may modulate mitochondrial function, nutrient metabolism, and oxygen delivery, which in turn determines whether immune cells adopt a glycolytic effector phenotype or an oxidative, memory-related status. Given that mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS generation strengthen inflammasome activation, these metabolic shifts are closely associated with endogenous danger signaling (Fig. 8b) [314,357,358].

Moderate levels of ROS function as signaling intermediates that stimulate antigen processing, PRR signaling, and cytokine production, whereas higher concentrations can trigger pyroptotic cell death or chronic inflammation. Therefore, a nanoadjuvant formulation should maintain a balance between immunostimulatory redox signaling, and cellular viability and longevity. Notably, biomaterials also elicit metabolic and redox cues that can drive epigenetic remodeling, linking acute activation to a prolonged immune response [359].

Prolonged or chronic exposure to such metabolic stress signals can result in trained immunity, in which innate immune cells acquire memory-like characteristics through permanent metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming. This effect provides a mechanistic explanation for the long-term immune-boosting effects of nanoadjuvant systems, and underscores the importance of regulating this phenomenon to prevent maladaptive immune priming. Representative metabolic pathways and markers of trained immunity are summarized in Table 6 [360].

Transcriptional and epigenetic reprogramming

Immune cell phenotypes are stabilized by transcriptional and epigenetic remodeling. PRRs and inflammasome signaling activate transcription factors, including NF-κB, IRFs, STATs, and AP-1, which coordinate the expression of cytokines, costimulatory molecules, antigen-presenting machinery, and chemokine receptors. The choice of whether immune cells adopt inflammatory, cytotoxic, regulatory, or memory states is dictated by these transcriptional programs, thereby linking early innate sensing to the ultimate adaptive response (Fig. 8a).

The functional states of immune cells are also maintained by epigenetic regulation of genes involved in activation programs. This can be achieved through alterations in chromatin accessibility, histone modifications, and expression of non-coding RNAs. Nanoadjuvant systems that induce persistent or repeated stimulation can elicit long-lasting immune memory; however, excessive or uncontrolled epigenetic remodeling can lead to chronic inflammation or autoimmunity. Table 6 summarizes transcriptional regulators and epigenetic features associated with the immune-enhancing effects of nanoadjuvants [361].

Immune cell-specific enhancement strategies

DC maturation and antigen presentation

DCs are professional APCs that integrate innate sensing and adaptive immune responses. The maturation of DCs is accompanied by the upregulation of MHC class I and II molecules, increased expression of costimulatory molecules, and secretion of cytokines that promote T cell priming [362]. Nanoadjuvant biomaterials support these mechanisms by stabilizing antigens, facilitating uptake, and targeting intracellular trafficking pathways that link cross-presentation. Nanoparticles can coordinate the activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells by enhancing MHC-dependent antigen presentation (Fig. 9a) [363,365].

Immune cell-specific enhancement strategies enabled by nanoadjuvant biomaterials. (a) Modulation of adaptive and innate immune cells through enhanced antigen presentation, DC maturation, macrophage reprogramming, and activation of T and NK cells, alongside regulation of immunosuppressive pathways [363]. (b) Passive and active targeting strategies for delivering nanoadjuvants to tumor-resident immune cells and inflamed tissues, including systemic and local administration routes and receptor-mediated immune cell targeting [364].

In addition to molecular activation, spatial targeting has a very powerful impact on DC activity. Nanoadjuvant systems can be designed to target tumor-resident DCs or inflamed tissues through passive accumulation or active receptor-mediated interaction (Fig. 9b) [364,366]. Local delivery of nanoadjuvants, via intra-tumoral injection or depot-based release, can enhance the interactions between DCs and antigens and reduce systemic exposure. The combination of these targeting methods increases priming efficiency in lymphoid tissues and improves the quality of T cell priming. Notably, DC-based methods are centered on regulated activation, since overstimulation can trigger systemic inflammation [367].

Macrophage polarization and microenvironment remodeling

Macrophages are highly versatile immune cells that control inflammation, immune suppression, and tissue remodeling by adopting pro-inflammatory or suppressive phenotypes in response to environmental cues. Nanoadjuvant biomaterials have the potential to induce macrophage polarization by modulating surface chemistry, mechanical signals, and the immunomodulatory cargo. Furthermore, nanostructures can induce macrophage reprogramming toward pro-inflammatory or immune-protective phenotypes, for example, by the specific delivery of regulatory nucleic acids (Fig. 9a) [206,363].

The delivery route and localization of nanoadjuvants also influence their ability to target macrophages – either passively by taking advantage of vascular permeability or actively via receptor-mediated interactions between immune cells at the site of inflammation (Fig. 9b) [368]. Biomaterials enable local reprogramming of immunosuppressive microenvironments by targeting nanoadjuvants to macrophage-rich niches. This rearrangement increases cytokine production, alters ECM organization, and enhances the infiltration of effector lymphocytes. Given that macrophages are early responders to biomaterials, early polarization is a decisive factor in the success of therapeutic responses [139].

T cell activation, expansion, and exhaustion

T cells are central to long-lasting anti-tumor and anti-viral responses. Nanoadjuvant biomaterials facilitate T cell activation indirectly by enhancing DC-mediated antigen presentation, and directly by promoting cytokine profiles that support T cell survival and expansion. Nanostructure-based immunomodulation promotes CD4+ helper and CD8+ cytotoxic T cell responses, supports immune synchronization, and enhances target cell killing (Fig. 9a) [369].

T cell function in the tumor or inflamed tissues can be optimized through localized or actively targeted delivery strategies (Fig. 9b), which also limit systemic exposure to cytokines and prevent chronic overstimulation, a major cause of T cell exhaustion. Biomaterial systems can maintain T cell cytotoxicity and promote the development of functional memory populations by regulating antigen dose, release kinetics, and microenvironmental cues [370].

NK cell activation and cytotoxic responses

NK cells elicit antigen-independent rapid cytotoxicity and augment adaptive immunity, particularly against tumors that evade T cell recognition. Nanoadjuvant biomaterials can enhance NK activity either indirectly by stimulating DCs and macrophages, or directly by providing NK-activating cytokines, leading to early cytotoxic pressure on malignant cells (Fig. 9a) [371]. Furthermore, nanoadjuvants targeting inflamed or tumor-associated sites can enhance accumulation of NK cells at these sites. Platforms for immune recruitment, scaffold systems, and local depots can be used to maintain NK cell activation and prevent off-target inflammation. Since overstimulation of NK cells can cause tissue damage, it is necessary to deliver cytokines locally and regulate their release [22]. In sum, NK cell-specific interventions enable nanoadjuvant biomaterials to expand their therapeutic applications through rapid immune interactions, thereby strengthening immune surveillance [372].

Therapeutic applications: immunotherapy and vaccination

Cancer immunotherapy and TME targeting

Although immunotherapy has transformed the field of oncology, its clinical application is often limited by the immunosuppressive TME. Tumors limit immune infiltration due to defective vasculature, thickened extracellular matrices, and repressive cytokine networks, while also enriching for Tregs and MDSCs [373]. Nanoadjuvant OBs can overcome the immunosuppressive TME by enabling local delivery of immunostimulatory signals. The diversity of nanocarrier designs, targeting strategies, and combination strategies aimed at spatially confining immune activation through DC activation, macrophage reprogramming, and cytotoxic T cell priming are summarized in Fig. 10a [374,377].

(a) Design principles and delivery strategies of nanooncologic vaccines, including nanocarrier platforms, targeting mechanisms, and integration with combination therapies [374]. (b) Immune activation cascade induced by nanovaccines, highlighting DC maturation, antigen presentation, activation of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, memory formation, and tumor cell killing [375]. (c) Lymph node–targeted nanovaccine workflows showing coordinated delivery of antigens and adjuvants, DC activation, antigen-specific CD8+ T cell expansion, and therapeutic tumor eradication [376].

In addition to direct immune activation, biomaterial systems may actively remodel the TME by continuously delivering immune agonists, enhancing immune cell recruitment, and controlling physical and biochemical barriers [378]. Immunologically cold tumors, in particular, benefit from local delivery strategies, such as intra-tumoral injection, post-resection implantable scaffolds, or lymph node-targeted systems. Nanoadjuvant biomaterials can also improve the efficacy of immunotherapies by enhancing immune infiltration and responsiveness to checkpoint blockade, create chemokine gradients, and minimize stromal resistance [[379], [380], [381], [382]].

Infectious disease vaccines and next-generation adjuvant systems

Vaccination has been one of the most effective medical interventions, although most infectious diseases lack potent vaccines due to antigen instability, low cellular immunity, or insufficient protection. OBs and nanoadjuvant OBs can overcome these limitations by improving antigen stability, enhancing delivery to antigen-presenting cells, and controlling immune stimulation. Nanoadjuvants stimulate the immune system to generate a powerful antibody and T cell response by replicating pathogen-like particulate structures [383].

Continued release of antigens into biomaterial depots also contributes to affinity maturation and long-term immune memory. Fig. 10c depicts the mechanistic processes underlying these long-term responses, mainly lymph node targeting, DC uptake, and antigen-specific expansion of CD8+ T cells [384]. Notably, nanoadjuvant systems are highly modular, enabling rapid responses to emerging threats and supporting alternative delivery routes, such as intranasal and transdermal administration, thereby facilitating the design of next-generation vaccines [33].

Combination regimens and immune monitoring endpoints

Due to the failure of single-modality immunotherapy and vaccination strategies to overcome diverse immune resistance mechanisms, combination therapy has become an essential component of immunotherapy, particularly vaccination. Nanoadjuvant OBs provide a versatile delivery platform of combination regimens through co-delivery of antigens, immune agonists, checkpoint inhibitors, cytokines, and metabolic modulators. Biomaterial platforms have the potential to coordinate immune stimulation with tumor injury or pathogen exposure, thereby improving therapeutic synergy and reducing toxicity by regulating the spatiotemporal delivery of biomaterials [385].

Immune monitoring is also required because clinical translation aims to determine efficacy and safety as reliably as possible. The relevant endpoints include DC maturation, cytokine production, expansion of antigen-specific T lymphocytes, memory formation, and immune infiltration into target tissues [386]. Furthermore, exhaustion markers, inflammatory toxicity, and long-term immune durability can be used to streamline the dosing regimen and determine the clinical outcomes. A reciprocal relationship between rational regimens and potent immune detectors will help translate nanoadjuvant biomaterials into useful immunotherapies and vaccination [387].

Therapeutic applications: regeneration and immune-inflammatory disorders

Immunomodulatory scaffolds for tissue repair and regeneration

Tissue repair and regeneration are closely linked to inflammation, debris clearance, angiogenesis, and ECM remodeling. In regenerative medicine, the therapeutic goal is not maximal immune activation, but rather immune modulation that enhances healing without promoting fibrosis and chronic inflammation [388]. OBs including collagen, gelatin, hyaluronic acid, alginate, silk, and artificial hydrogels are increasingly being used as scaffolds to guide tissue formation and cell behavior through structural support and biochemical signaling. These scaffolds can be used with particle-based or cell-based delivery systems and custom-engineered cargos to actively regulate immune cell recruitment and activity at the injury site (Fig. 11a) [389]. These scaffolds may also be adapted to induce pro-healing macrophage phenotypes, regulate cytokine balance, and mediate angiogenesis and tissue remodeling. Furthermore, OB scaffolds can coordinate immune activation with regenerative schedules owing to their tunable chemical and physical properties, such as porosity, stiffness, surface topology, and degradation kinetics [390]. The gradual replacement of the scaffold by native tissue is facilitated by controlled degradation, thereby avoiding the long-term FBR. Some downstream functional outcomes of these design decisions, such as reduced inflammation, improved host integration, and tissue repair, are shown in Fig. 11b. Immune-informed scaffold design, in combination, facilitates regeneration-sustaining immune programming, improves tissue healing, and reduces fibrosis and implant failure [45].

Wound healing, anti-infection immunity, and immune-guided repair

Wound healing is a complex process that begins with acute inflammation to prevent infection and remove debris, and continues with resolution and tissue repair. Any disruption in this immune process can result in non-healing wounds, chronic diseases, or scarring [391]. Nanoadjuvants based on OBs can be employed in wound repair to enhance local immunogenicity, antimicrobial efficacy, and tissue strength. The dressing of wounds and hydrogels may also be structured to attract immune cells, deliver antimicrobial agents, and maintain a moist environment, thereby enhancing regeneration [392].

During pathological wound healing, hemostasis and inflammation are replaced by proliferation and remodeling, which can lead to chronic wounding, fibrosis, or immunological rejection of implants (Fig. 11c) [393]. Nanoparticles can enhance the efficacy of wound-healing scaffolds by increasing drug stability, extending drug release, and targeting pathogens. Biomaterials that combine immune stimulation with antibiofilm activity have been particularly useful in situations where the biomaterials fail to control immune responses and vascularization, such as in infected or diabetic wounds. Notably, immune-mediated repair entails balancing antimicrobial inflammation with tissue-protective reactions to prevent unnecessary tissue damage and eradicate pathogens [389].

Autoimmune diseases and tolerance-inducing biomaterial strategies

Autoimmune diseases are characterized by inappropriate activation of immune responses against self-antigens, leading to chronic inflammation and tissue destruction. In this regard, the therapeutic goal is immune tolerization as opposed to immune activation [354]. It is possible to design OBs that deliver autoantigens in tolerogenic forms, favor regulatory immune responses, and inhibit pathogenic effector responses. Scaffold systems or particulate systems can contain antigens without high levels of inflammatory stimuli, and promote regulatory T cell induction and immune retraining rather than activation [394].

Nanoparticles are an important component of tolerance-inducing biomaterial strategies, as they enable targeted delivery to APCs and control release kinetics and surface signaling. These design principles reduce the risk of accidental PRR activation and the amplification of inflammatory responses [232]. Biomaterial-based tolerance platforms could reduce systemic immunosuppression by providing sustained, regionally localized immune regulation to restore long-term immune balance. They are particularly appealing for chronic autoimmune disorders that involve long-lasting immune reprogramming, rather than short-term symptom management [395].

Chronic inflammation control and immune homeostasis restoration

Various diseases and conditions are associated with chronic inflammation, such as metabolic diseases, cardiovascular disease, neuroinflammatory diseases, and fibrotic pathologies. In contrast to acute inflammation, chronic inflammatory conditions persist due to unresolved inflammation, ongoing tissue injury, or impaired regulation of cytokine signaling [21]. Biomaterial-based strategies aim to correct immune homeostasis by regulating inflammatory processes, facilitating resolution, and tissue repair. OBs can deliver anti-inflammatory factors, modulate cytokine gradients, and provide a physical scaffold that attenuates inflammatory stress at the affected site [19].

The presence of nanostructures in these biomaterials enables control of chronic inflammation through efficient delivery of immune regulators and stimulus-responsive release in response to inflammatory signals, such as ROS, enzymes, and acidic pH. Notably, immune homeostasis can be restored by balancing immunosuppression, which can increase the risk of infection, with immune activation that induces long-term inflammatory signals that cause tissue damage [195]. The nanoadjuvant OBs provide a multifunctional platform that enables sustained modulation, localized delivery, and microenvironment-responsive control to treat immune-inflammatory disorders over an extended period [359].

Clinical bioengineering strategies and translation roadmap

Safety, immunotoxicity, biodistribution, and clearance

Since immune enhancement can readily transition to immunotoxicity in the event of excessive, systemic, or uncontrolled activation, safety considerations are critical for the successful clinical translation of nanoadjuvant-integrated OB systems. Common risks include uncontrollable cytokine release, complement-dependent hypersensitivity reactions, unwanted polarization of the immune response, and chronic inflammatory responses that can lead to fibrosis or tissue damage [37]. Moreover, nanomaterials can cause oxidative stress, membrane perturbation, or trigger inflammasome activation when their physicochemical properties, including size, surface charge, or degradability, are not optimized. Therefore, the development of immune-enhancing platforms should be guided by safety-by-design principles that balance immunopotency with spatial confinement, dose minimization, and immune-activation reversibility [396].

Biodistribution and clearance profiles are also critical issues, as nanomaterials can accumulate in the liver, spleen, and lymphoid tissues, potentially resulting in long-term toxicity. OBs exhibit biodegradability and regulated release kinetics, enabling controlled clearance and reduced systemic exposure [25]. However, immunological compatibility should also be considered when degradation products are used, since acidic residues or agents that are not removed promptly may contribute to inflammation or destabilize tissues. Preclinical testing should therefore encompass long-term pharmacokinetics, organ accumulation, immunotoxicity studies, and long-term histopathological studies [139]. Animal models of disease and the dosing regimens used in these models must be well chosen to avoid overestimating safety or efficacy. Lastly, effective translation would involve demonstrating that immune augmentation is local, controllable, and reversible as needed [397].

Manufacturing scale-up, reproducibility, and GMP constraints

Even exceptionally potent nanoadjuvant biomaterials have failed in clinical trials due to a lack of large-scale reproducibility. Nanoparticles are highly sensitive to variations in synthesis conditions, including mixing rate, temperature, solvent composition, and processing time, which can affect size distribution, surface chemistry, and cargo loading efficiency. Furthermore, the natural variability of natural polymers can lead to inconsistent immune responses that are undesirable in the clinical context [398]. Scalable manufacturing, in turn, presupposes a high level of process control, standardization of characterization methods, and validated quality measurements, which ensure the reproducibility of results across batches [399].

The guidelines for good manufacturing practice (GMP) are highly restrictive with respect to the sourcing of raw materials, traceability, sterility, and documentation. The most important challenge is sterilization, as not all nanoadjuvants and biomaterial matrices can withstand heat, radiation, or chemical agents. Loss of bioactivity or undesirable changes in release behavior should also be avoided under long-term storage conditions, without aggregation or associated loss of bioactivity [400]. These limitations can be addressed by considering scale-up early in the design of materials and formulations, ensuring that fabrication processes are reproducible, and selecting packaging methods suitable for clinical applications. The core issue is the correlation between material innovation and manufacturing feasibility, which enables successful translation in the laboratory [401].

Regulatory considerations and clinical evaluation metrics

The regulatory acceptance of nanoadjuvant OBs is often complex because these systems are integrated into drugs, biologics, and medical devices. They are divided by the mode of action, which are mostly immune modulation, controlled delivery, or structural support. Such a hybrid nature imposes regulatory constraints that require a comprehensive study of material structure, degradation, pharmacology, and immunological safety. Regulatory agencies typically stipulate the characterization of particle size, surface properties, purity, endotoxin concentration, stability, and discharge dynamics, and the use of approved assays to demonstrate consistent immune function [25].

The clinical evaluation measures are intended to provide evidence of therapeutic efficacy and immune safety. Immune cell invasion, antigen-directed T cell expansion, cytokine production, and extended tumor control are endpoints relevant to cancer immunotherapy. On the other hand, neutralization capacity, antibody titer, memory, and challenge protection are important endpoints for vaccines. Immune markers in regenerative medicine must be directly related to tissue repair, including angiogenesis, less fibrosis, and functional regeneration [402]. Furthermore, adverse immune effects, such as systemic inflammation, autoimmune reactions, and chronic foreign body reactions, also need to be assessed. Standardizing immune readouts and translational biomarkers will enable cross-study comparisons and support dose selection, thereby reducing late-stage clinical failure [19].

Future perspectives

Future-generation nanoadjuvant OBs will strive to develop adaptive smart systems that respond to dynamic biological conditions, such as pH, ROS, enzymes, and mechanical stress, and deliver immune signals with high precision to limit off-target immune reactions. Multi-functional nanostructures that monitor immune responses in real time (e.g., immune modulation, imaging, or biosensing) can facilitate adaptive treatment therapies. These developments represent a broader shift from generalized immune stimulation toward programmable immune engineering contingent on the disease environment [403]. Emerging studies have also demonstrated the potential of next-generation nanoengineered biomaterials that dynamically modulate immune responses through programmable nanoarchitectures and stimuli-responsive interfaces, offering promising directions for precision immune engineering and translational immunotherapy [404].

Immunogenicity arising from genetic background, microbiome composition, disease progression, and prior treatments is also a challenge for precision immunoengineering, as it varies among individual patients. Future biomaterial systems will be able to exploit patient antigens, immune-profiling data, and adaptive dose regimens to optimize therapeutic responses. The optimization of nanobiomaterial architecture will be enhanced by computational modeling and AI-guided design, as these approaches will link physicochemical criteria to immune pathways. In any case, the functional sophistication, manufacturability, and regulatory feasibility should be harmonized to ensure successful translation. The second step in this area will be an interdisciplinary approach, an evaluation process, and a more translational strategy that merges design, safety, and clinical usability at the most advanced design phases [405].

Conclusion

Integrating nanostructures into OB matrices represents a powerful and versatile strategy for enhancing immune cell function across diverse therapeutic contexts. The ability to elicit localized, sustained, and programmable, high-potency immune responses through the combination of the biocompatibility and structural tunability of organic matrices with the immunomodulatory potency of nanoadjuvants is achievable in these hybrid systems, but remains challenging with soluble agents alone. The control of immune cell fate is a complex process involving the regulation of biointerface events, cellular uptake, intracellular trafficking, and downstream signaling pathways.

At the molecular level, nanoadjuvant biomaterials regulate signal transduction by PRRs, inflammasome activation, cytokine polarization, immunometabolism, and transcriptional and epigenetic remodeling. At the cellular level, they promote DC maturation, macrophage re-education, T cell priming, T cell persistence, and innate cytotoxicity. It is important to note that these effects are highly design-sensitive and dependent on nanoscale quantities, matrix mechanics, and spatiotemporal release kinetics, which govern the types of immune responses (productive, tolerogenic, or pathologically inflammatory). This places considerable emphasis on designing materials based on immunity rather than on empirical principles.

Translational nanoadjuvant OBs are highly promising in cancer immunotherapy, immunization, regenerative medicine, and the treatment of chronic inflammatory diseases, as they can modulate immunity at the site of action without causing systemic toxicity. However, the clinical use should be carefully considered in the context of immunotoxicity, biodistribution, clearance, manufacturing reproducibility, and regulatory classification. The lack of a relationship between laboratory innovations and clinical applications necessitates a safety-by-design approach to ensure that immunological objectives and fabrication plans are reproducible.

Overall, nanoadjuvant-integrated organic biomaterials represent a rapidly evolving platform for immunomodulation with broad therapeutic potential. Continued advances in nanoengineering, biomaterials science, and systems immunology are expected to enable more precise control of immune activation, polarization, and memory formation. Future research should focus on developing predictive design principles, improving biosafety profiles, and establishing scalable manufacturing strategies to facilitate clinical translation. Such efforts will be essential for realizing the full potential of nanoadjuvant biomaterials in next-generation immunotherapy and regenerative medicine.
