Sulphanilamide degradation by undoped and copper doped ZnO, and ferromagnetic properties of fresh, aged, and heat-treated aged ZnO
Authors: Daphne Mary John, Sreerag Kaaliveetil, Yadhu J Nair, S Sruthy, P Lasya, Ramanujam Brahmadesam Thoopul Srinivasa Raghava, G Sivasubramanian, KM Sreedhar, KM Sreekanth
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work looks into how well Cu-doped zinc oxide works as a photocatalyst when exposed to visible light to break down sulphanilamide. The synthesized samples were characterized by XRD and then FTIR techniques for structural besides compositional analysis. The approximate value of bandgap found out by NBE values of PL spectra showed a decrease in bandgap with doping. Deconvoluted PL spectra revealed the presence of radiative recombination pathways associated with zinc interstitial and zinc vacancy in addition to band-to-band recombination in all samples. FESEM images showed a nanoflake shape for pure ZnO, while Cu-ZnO had a pseudo-spherical shape. SQUID-VSM was adopted to understand the magnetic nature of freshly prepared, heated, and unheated aged samples. Ferromagnetic behavior was observed with saturation magnetization of 5.56 × 10−4, 2.88 × 10−4, and 2.78 × 10−4 emu/g for freshly prepared, heated, and unheated aged samples, respectively. A 2 % Cu-doped sample exhibited the highest efficiency of 92.3 % towards the degradation of sulphanilamide. Since we got better efficiency for 2 % Cu-ZnO, we should further decrease the doping percentage and check whether we will get even higher efficiency in the future. The photocatalytic degradation efficiency of ternary ZnO compounds, codoped ZnO, should be evaluated, yet another future scope.Keywords: Zinc oxide, Ferromagnetism, Photocatalytic degradation, Drug, Electron-hole recombination

Introduction

Wastewater treatment is an essential part of modern society to sustain life. As the years go by, more and more pollutants have been detected in water sources, which has become a crucial concern [1]. Pharmaceuticals and personal care products come under a large section of pollutants called emerging organic contaminants, a term that is used to describe unregulated organic pollutants. Sulphanilamide with the chemical formula C6H8N2O2S is a widely used antibacterial drug belonging to sulphonamide or sulpha drugs and has been reported in the groundwater of Indian cities like Varanasi and Ramnagar in Uttar Pradesh [2]. Sulphanilamide is one of the pharmaceuticals in the list of emerging organic pollutants given by ALS Global [3]. Despite the low amount of these medications in the environment, they can gather in different organisms through the food chain, potentially be hazardous to people besides aquatic life, and lead to pathogenic bacteria developing drug resistance [4].

Different water treatment methods have been employed to address this issue, which are classified into two sections: physicochemical process and biological process. Main physicochemical processes include advanced oxidation, electrochemical, and coagulation-flocculation. Biological processes utilize enzymes, yeast, fungi, algae, or bacteria to treat the contaminated water [5]. However, the biological process has several limitations; for example, the initial cost of equipment (bioreactors) may be very high and can only degrade biodegradable compounds [6]. The advanced oxidation process (AOP) is much cheaper to implement than all other physicochemical processes. In AOP, highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (HO•) and superoxide anion radicals (O2•−) react with the organic and inorganic contaminants to achieve complete degradation. Typical features of HO• include nonselectivity with high reactivity besides powerful oxidizing capacity, E0 = +2.8 eV [7]. The different methods of AOP are Fenton-based, ozone-based, photocatalysis, and electrochemical. Among these, photocatalysis is cheaper and more effective in degrading organic pollutants. Photocatalysis involves the presence of a light source and a catalyst to produce HO• and O2•−. Homogeneous photocatalysis and heterogeneous photocatalysis are two classifications of photocatalysis.

In heterogeneous photocatalysis, solid catalysts, which are highly photoactive, usually semiconductors, are used to perform photocatalytic reactions [8]. A photocatalyst is usually a semiconductor material like ZnO that can produce electron-hole pairs upon photon absorption. Because of their excellent chemical stability, lack of toxicity, and appropriate band-edge configuration to redox potential of H2O, metal oxide semiconductors with band edges have been employed widely as photocatalysts. However, the major drawbacks of these semiconductors are their wide bandgap (UV region) and high recombination rate. This can be overcome by doping the base materials to tune their properties, making them useful in the visible region and lowering their recombination rate [9]. Doping ZnO with copper has been reported to introduce a deep acceptor level in the bandgap. This leads to a reduction in the bandgap and reduces the recombination rate. Cu2+ has an ionic radius (73 p.m.) very similar to that of Zn2+ (74 p.m.), which makes it suitable for doping [10].

In this work, we tried to modify the properties of ZnO (bandgap and recombination rate) by doping it with copper. Out of the many methods for synthesizing ZnO and doped ZnO, we adopted precipitation and coprecipitation methods [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. ZnO at nanolevel exhibits room temperature ferromagnetism, and this can be improved by doping with transition elements like Mn, Co, Fe, etc., called diluted magnetic semiconductors, which are used in various applications such as spintronics [15]. Here, we tried to investigate the magnetic behavior of freshly prepared, heated, and unheated aged ZnO nanoparticles. The samples' structural, morphological, optical, and magnetic properties were analyzed using various techniques. We identified the pharmaceutical sulphanilamide as an organic pollutant for testing the photocatalytic activity of copper doped ZnO NPs, which is less studied. Our study on copper doped ZnO for sulphanilamide degradation under sunlight fills a notable gap in the literature, where most studies have focused on the photocatalytic degradation of other pharmaceutical pollutants like ibuprofen and tetracycline. A comparative study was brought out on how the photocatalytic degradation of the sulphanilamide drug varies with three different dopant concentrations. This study, as stated elsewhere, also focused on the investigation of the magnetic properties of fresh samples, heat-treated aged samples, and aged samples of ZnO, which forms the novelty of the work as it is also less studied.

Resources and approaches

Resources utilized

Zn(CH3COO)2.2H2O, Cu(CH3COO)2.H2O, NaOH, cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), acetone, all from Merk, and deionized water were used in this work. Sulphanilamide (AnalaR NORMAPUR® analytical reagent) used in this study is from Rankem.

Preparation of undoped then Cu-doped ZnO nanoparticles

The precipitation approach was followed to produce pristine ZnO, where Zn(OH)2 was first produced by adding a solution of NaOH to an aqueous solution of the zinc salt under constant stirring at room temperature, after the addition of 0.5 g of CTAB, a surfactant preventing agglomeration of particles. The Zn(OH)2 precipitate was filtered over a Whatman filter paper and dried at 150 °C for 2 h in a silica crucible, after washing with deionized water, then with acetone. The obtained product was powdered, followed by annealing at 250 °C for 3 h, yielding ZnO. The coprecipitation method was adopted to prepare Cu-doped ZnO, where a solution of NaOH was added to a solution containing both Zn(CH3COO)2.2H2O and Cu(CH3COO)2.H2O under stirring at room temperature after adding 0.5 g of CTAB. The rest of the procedure is identical to that adopted to produce ZnO. We synthesized 2, 5, then 10 mol% Cu-doped ZnO, represented as 2 % Cu-ZnO, 5 % Cu-ZnO, then 10 % Cu-ZnO, respectively [[8], [9], [10]].

Characterization methods

ZnO nanostructure was investigated by means of XRD (Bruker AXS D8 ADVANCE Diffractometer with Cu-K alpha radiation λ = 1.5406 Å, 1°/min step size in the scan range of 30–80°) for phase identification. FESEM was used to analyze surface morphology (Carl Zeiss Germany, Gemini 300) and then elemental composition by EDS. Absorption spectra were investigated by a UV–Vis. spectrometer (JASCO UV–Vis–NIR spectrometer, model name: V-670). Functional groups were analyzed through FTIR (BRUKER ALPHA II, Germany). Optoelectronic properties of semiconductors were investigated by photoluminescence spectroscopy (RF-6000, Spectrofluorometer, SHIMADZU). Magnetic properties were investigated using a commercial Quantum Design Superconducting Quantum Interference Device-Vibrating Sample Magnetometer (SQUID-VSM) at a constant temperature and with a field range of ± 7 T.

The presence of hexagonal wurtzite ZnO was confirmed with file JCPDS-890510. Every peak in the XRD pattern corresponds to wurtzite ZnO [17]. All peaks were indexed to the corresponding plane by comparing with the JCPDS file. The obtained peaks are sharp besides intense, showing the high crystallinity of samples.

The intensity of the (101) peak is high compared to other peaks, which indicates that the preferred orientation of synthesized ZnO NPs is ⟨101⟩ orientation. From the XRD pattern, we can understand that there is no change in the crystal structure of ZnO NPs upon doping with copper. For 2 % Cu-ZnO and 5 % Cu-ZnO, no additional peaks due to Cu, CuO, or any binary zinc-copper phase are observed. This might be because the majority of Cu atoms have substituted into the ZnO lattice, causing a strain in the system for 2 and 5 % doping while the solubility limit is exceeded for 10 % doping, leading to the formation of secondary phases or defects that do not affect the primary lattice of ZnO [18]. Optical properties

Optical properties of ZnO besides Cu-ZnO NPs were analyzed by photoluminescence spectroscopy. The emission spectrum of ZnO besides Cu-ZnO NPs at an excitation wavelength λexc = 280 nm is given in Fig. 3. There is a decrease in the intensity of PL spectra as the doping percentage increases. This is because of the reduced radiative recombination in doped samples. Cu doping introduces additional defect states within the ZnO crystal lattice. These defects function as traps for the photogenerated electrons and holes. Instead of recombining radiatively and emitting light, the charge carriers are captured by these defects, leading to non-radiative recombination. This results in a reduction in PL intensity because less light is emitted. The defects introduced by Cu doping provide pathways for non-radiative recombination, where the energy of recombining electron-hole pairs is released as heat rather than light. This reduces the overall PL intensity. Deconvolution of the emission peak was done by fitting multiple peaks with the gaussian curve, Fig. 4. The fit was completed with R2 = 0.99 and χ2 = 10−15, and multiple peaks exist because of the transitions between defect states [27]. The potential transitions causing these emission peaks are also represented schematically in Fig. 4. The energy levels of defective states were determined from the data existing in different literature (Table 3).

For all samples, emission corresponding to zinc interstitial and zinc vacancy was detected, other than band-to-band recombination [28]. But there is an additional peak in the case of 5 % and 10 % Cu-ZnO. For 5 % Cu-ZnO, this arises from the transition between Zni and Cu + states. For 10 % Cu-ZnO, an extra peak may correspond to the transition between the t2 state of Cu2+ and Cu + states. So, there is an additional radiative recombination pathway for 5 % and 10 % Cu-ZnO. This transition was not visible in 2 % Cu-ZnO because of low doping. As the doping percentage increases, the PL intensity decreases, Fig. 3. This means that there is a decrease in radiative recombination when the doping percentage increases. Near bandgap emission values can be used to calculate the bandgap of different samples, even though this may not give the accurate bandgap value due to the Stokes shift. The variation in NBE for additional doping percentage is given in Fig. 5. From this, the bandgap decreases as doping percentage increases.

Plot showing variation in near band gap emission values with doping percentage.

The continuous shift in the near-band-edge (NBE) photoluminescence (PL) emission observed in the samples with increasing copper doping can be accredited to bandgap narrowing, the creation of defect states and non-radiative recombination centers, and the formation of impurity bands due to copper incorporation. The interaction between the 3d state of copper and the conduction band of ZnO leads to bandgap narrowing. The bandgap narrowing increases with concentration of copper, shifting the NBE emission to longer wavelengths (redshift). In the present study, a clear redshift in NBE can be observed as doping concentration increases from 2 to 10 %. With an increase in copper doping concentration, more defect states, such as oxygen vacancies and interstitial copper, are created in the ZnO lattice. These defects can act as non-radiative recombination centers, which trap charge carriers and reduce the overall PL intensity. As copper doping concentration increases, density of these defect states also increases, which explains why intensity decreases as doping concentration increases from 2 to 10 %. Copper ions can form an impurity band close to the conduction band edge at a higher doping percentage. This band can contribute to redshift by allowing radiative recombination from these impurity levels rather than the conduction band, further lowering the energy of the emitted photons [29].

The surface morphology of NPs plays an essential role in photocatalysis. The shape that produces the largest surface area is preferred for photocatalytic application. Since photocatalysis only happens once the substrate gets adsorbed onto the surface of the NPs, a larger surface area would provide many more reduction sites, increasing the rate of photocatalytic degradation. For 2 % Cu-ZnO NPs, particle size is below 100 nm in all three dimensions. The particle size of pure ZnO is below 100 nm and is only in two dimensions. So, it is possible that 2 % Cu-ZnO exhibits a larger surface area for a given sample than pure ZnO. But to be sure, we must consider the agglomeration between particles.

Magnetic properties

M − H loop for as-prepared besides aged ZnO samples before and after heating was analyzed using a commercial Quantum Design Superconducting Quantum Interference Device-Vibrating Sample Magnetometer (SQUID-VSM) at a constant temperature and with a field range of ± 7 T, and the diamagnetic characteristic is removed carefully. In Fig. 9, we have plotted the M − H curve for fresh, aged, and heated aged ZnO to see how the magnetic nature varies. Saturation magnetization (Ms) for the latest batch of ZnO was found to be 5.56 × 10−4 emu/g, while 2.88 × 10−4 and 2.78 × 10−4 for aged samples after and before heat treatment, respectively. Remanent magnetization (Mr) was observed as 7.61 × 10−5, 3.38 × 10−5, and 3.38 × 10−5 emu/g, and coercivity (Hc) as 124.33, 102.89, and 95.75 Oe for fresh samples, heat-treated aged samples, and aged samples, respectively. A significant decrease in Ms and Mr was observed in the aged samples. This kind of observation is important when it comes to the application of ZnO nanostructures. Both heat-treated aged samples and aged samples produced the same value for Mr but different values for Hc, the heat-treated aged samples being the highest. The ferromagnetic behavior of ZnO nanoparticles is often attributed to defects such as oxygen vacancies, zinc interstitials, and dopants. Over time, these nanoparticles can undergo aging, significantly altering their structural, optical, and magnetic properties with particle size variation [31]. Major factors that contribute to the change in magnetic behavior are surface chemistry changes like the formation of hydroxyl groups from the atmosphere, reorganization of defects, dopant diffusion, and aggregation of particles, which alter the effective particle size and surface area, influencing the defect density and magnetic interactions [32]. On heating the samples, the water molecules trapped inside the structure are released, thereby showing an increase in its saturation magnetization, Fig. 9.

M − H curve for (a) fresh ZnO, (b) heated aged ZnO, (c) aged ZnO, and the inset shows the enlarged view of the hysteresis.

The magnetic behavior of fresh, aged, and heat-treated aged ZnO samples can be scientifically explained by considering the role of defects, mainly oxygen vacancies and zinc interstitials. The fresh ZnO sample exhibits a high saturation magnetization of 5.56 × 10⁻⁴ emu/g and remanent magnetization of 7.61 × 10⁻⁵ emu/g. This behavior can be ascribed to the presence of a higher density of defects, such as oxygen vacancies and zinc interstitials, which cause long-range ferromagnetic ordering via defect-induced bound magnetic polarons (BMPs). These vacancies and interstitials act as centers for unpaired spins, which interact with neighboring spins, causing enhanced ferromagnetism. Aged ZnO before heat treatment exhibits a lower saturation magnetization, 2.78 × 10⁻⁴ emu/g, and remanent magnetization, 3.38 × 10⁻⁵ emu/g, compared to fresh ZnO. This decrease can be credited to the aging process, where atmospheric exposure leads to the formation of hydroxyl groups on the surface, which passivate oxygen vacancies and zinc interstitials, thereby lowering the density of magnetic defects. Heat-treated aged ZnO exhibits a slight recovery in saturation magnetization, 2.88 × 10⁻⁴ emu/g, but remains lower than that of fresh ZnO. The heat treatment removes surface-adsorbed species, such as water molecules, and partially restores some of the oxygen vacancies by restructuring the defect distribution. However, it does not fully restore the original defect density, which does explain why magnetic properties do not return to what was seen in the fresh samples. The coercivity value for fresh ZnO is the highest, 124.33 Oe, while aged and heat-treated aged samples show reduced coercivity, 95.75 and 102.89 Oe, respectively. The reduction in coercivity with aging is due to the decrease in defect density, which weakens the pinning of magnetic domain walls. Heat treatment increases the coercivity slightly by restoring some defects, which help to pin the domain walls more effectively than in the aged sample without treatment [33,34].

Photocatalytic degradation

Sulphanilamide was chosen as the pharmaceutical pollutant for testing the degradation efficiency of ZnO besides Cu-ZnO because of its widespread use in medical treatment and its persistence in water sources, making it a relevant target for environmental remediation. Pharmaceuticals, including sulphanilamide, are increasingly found in water bodies due to ineffective wastewater treatment methods. These pollutants cause significant risk to aquatic ecosystems and human health. Photocatalytic degradation using metal oxides like copper doped ZnO has been shown to effectively break down such pollutants under visible light, making it an ideal candidate for this study [35,36]. A solution of 3 ppm of sulphanilamide was prepared, and 50 mg of photocatalyst was added. Photocatalysis was done under sunlight. The reduction in the absorption peak of sulphanilamide solution using 2, 5, and then 10 mol% Cu-ZnO as photocatalysts is shown in Fig. 10. The photocatalysis using all different doping percentages of ZnO was done simultaneously so that any change in light intensity would equally affect all three photocatalytic reactions.

Graph showing the reduction in sulphanilamide peak due to photocatalytic activity of (a) pure ZnO, (b) 2 %, (c) 5 %, then (d) 10 % Cu-ZnO.

Average Global Horizontal Irradiance (GHI) at the location of the experiment was 812 ± 39.52 W/m2. After every 15 min, 3.5 ml of sulphanilamide solution was pipetted out of the solution to perform the UV–Vis analysis. Each time, the intensity of absorption at λmax = 258 nm of sulphanilamide was observed. As degradation proceeds, the intensity was found to decrease. The degradation efficiency of different doping percentages is shown in Fig. 11. At the end of 165 min, 2 % Cu-ZnO could degrade 92.3 % of sulphanilamide in the solution, while 5 % and 10 % Cu-ZnO, respectively, could only degrade 63.9 and 49.3 % sulphanilamide. This indicates that the degradation efficiency decreases as the doping percentage of Cu increases, and 2 % is the optimal level of doping to attain maximum degradation. The degradation efficiency of pure ZnO was analyzed another day with a GHI of 681 ± 63.95 W/m2. The degradation in the UV–Vis peak and degradation efficiency of ZnO are shown in Fig. 12. After 75 min of photocatalysis, 36.6 % of sulphanilamide degradation was achieved. Since the light intensity was different while doing the photocatalysis of doped and pure ZnO, it is difficult to compare results obtained with pure ZnO and Cu-ZnO.

Plot showing degradation efficiency of different doping concentration.

Degradation efficiency of ZnO.

Even though the intensity of light was less during photocatalysis using pure ZnO, it showed better efficiency after 75 min when compared to 5 % Cu-ZnO (33.42 % after 75 min) and 10 % Cu-ZnO (26.8 % after 75 min). For 2 % Cu-ZnO, the degradation efficiency after 75 min was 53.37 %, larger than that of pure ZnO. However, it cannot indicate the better efficiency of 2 % Cu-ZnO because the light intensity differed in the two cases. It indicates that a high Cu doping can decrease ZnO's photocatalytic degradation efficiency. From PL spectra, it could be understood that increasing the doping percentage increased the nonradiative recombination pathways for the electron. This might be the cause of the reduction in degradation efficiency. Cu doping introduces additional defect states or impurity levels within the ZnO bandgap. At higher doping levels, these defects function as non-radiative recombination centers where photogenerated electron-hole pairs can recombine without contributing to photocatalytic reactions. This reduces the number of free charge carriers available to participate in photocatalysis, thereby decreasing the photocatalytic degradation efficiency. In undoped ZnO, photogenerated electrons and holes can migrate to the surface to participate in redox reactions that degrade pollutants. However, with high Cu doping, the increased number of recombination centers causes these charge carriers to recombine before reaching the surface, further reducing the efficiency of the photocatalytic process. The nominal doping percentage of Cu has been shown to increase the photocatalytic efficiency of ZnO. In this case, the nominal doping percentage of Cu shows much better efficiency than the higher doping percentage. This might be either because, at nominal doping percentage, the defect state density is extremely low and does not produce extra recombination pathways compared to pure ZnO or could be the presence of more zinc interstitial in pure and 2 % Cu-ZnO as found from the FTIR spectrum. Oxygen vacancy and zinc interstitials are found to promote O2 adsorption, and there will be a strong interaction between photo-induced electron bound by zinc interstitial and adsorbed O2 [37]. This will produce O2•– radicals that enhance photocatalytic degradation.

Mechanism of photocatalytic activity

In the photocatalytic degradation of sulphanilamide using ZnO and copper doped ZnO as a photocatalyst, sunlight excites electrons from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB), creating electron-hole pairs: electrons with negative charge (e–) in the CB and holes with positive charge (h+) in the VB.

These electron-hole pairs migrate to the surface of the photocatalyst, where they participate in redox reactions. Electrons in the CB reduce oxygen molecules adsorbed on the surface of the photocatalyst to form superoxide anion radicals, O2•–, while holes in the VB oxidize water or hydroxide ions (OH⁻) on the surface of the photocatalyst to generate hydroxyl radicals, •OH.

Low levels of Cu doping, as in 2 % doping, can improve photocatalytic efficiency because Cu2⁺ act as electron traps, reducing electron-hole recombination and allowing more free radicals, •OH and O2•–, to form. But, at higher doping levels, as in 5 % and 10 % doping, Cu2⁺ introduces more defect states and non-radiative recombination centers, which facilitate electron-hole recombination without contributing to the photocatalytic degradation reaction.

The O2•– and •OH radicals generated in the previous step participate in the degradation of sulphanilamide.

The 2 % copper doped ZnO exhibits the highest efficiency because it balances defect formation and charge carrier separation. Higher copper doping causes excessive recombination, reducing the available radicals for sulphanilamide degradation. The sunlight intensity (Global Horizontal Irradiance, GHI) affects degradation rates. Higher GHI leads to higher electron-hole pair generation, which increases the overall degradation rate [35,36].

Summary and future scope

Pure ZnO and different mol% Cu-ZnO NPs were produced by precipitation and coprecipitation techniques, respectively. The XRD analysis revealed the contraction of lattice parameters as doping percentage increased and confirmed the hexagonal wurtzite structure for all as-prepared solid materials. The intensity of PL spectra was found to decrease as the doping percentage increased, indicating a decrease in radiative recombination. FESEM revealed that pure ZnO has a nanoflake-like shape, while 2 % Cu-ZnO exhibited a pseudo-spherical form. The aging effect on the ferromagnetic properties of ZnO nanoparticles is a complex interplay of surface chemistry, defect dynamics, dopant behavior, and particle aggregation. Understanding these processes is crucial for optimizing the long-term stability and performance of ZnO-based ferromagnetic materials for practical applications. Photocatalytic degradation of sulphanilamide was done using all the samples under solar irradiance. 2 % Cu-ZnO displayed the highest efficiency among all doped samples. ZnO has better efficiency than 5 % Cu-ZnO and 10 % Cu-ZnO. This was attributed to zinc interstitials and their role in producing more O2•– radicals.

For each sample, the concentration of O2•– radical and OH• radical produced during photocatalytic reactions should be evaluated. This is required to confirm that zinc vacancies can promote the adsorption of O2 and pay more O2•– radicals, which improves the efficiency of photocatalysis. Since we got better efficiency for 2 % Cu-ZnO, we should further decrease the doping percentage and check whether we will get even higher efficiency in the future. Dopants that can bring a much greater reduction in bandgap should be used such that they could absorb more percentage of visible light. The photocatalytic degradation efficiency of ternary ZnO compounds, codoped ZnO, should be evaluated, yet another future scope.
