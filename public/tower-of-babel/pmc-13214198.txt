Analysis of the impact of national centralized volume-based procurement drug policy on the market dynamics of tuberculosis drugs
Authors: Jian Yang, Xiaoyi Yu, Yifan Yao, Weili Zhao, Ying Chen, Zhebin Wang, Ming Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChina’s National Centralized Drug Procurement (NCDP) consolidates public-sector procurement demand and links guaranteed volumes to competitive tendering. The impact of this policy on reshaping the tuberculosis (TB) medicines market—specifically prices and expenditure, procurement volumes, and market structure—remains insufficiently quantified using time-series methods across multiple procurement cycles and hospital types.MethodsA hospital-stratified interrupted time series (ITS) study was conducted utilizing monthly public-hospital procurement data from January 2015 to December 2022. Six TB medicines included in national procurement rounds 2–4 comprised the intervention group: isoniazid, moxifloxacin, ethambutol, linezolid, levofloxacin, and pyrazinamide. Nine TB-related medicines not included in these rounds served as comparators: rifampicin, rifapentine, rifabutin, streptomycin, protionamide, cycloserine, p-aminosalicylic acid sodium, bedaquiline, and clofazimine. Analyses were stratified by hospital type (general versus TB-specialized hospitals) and drug classification (first-line versus second-line agents). Outcome measures included procurement volume, total expenditure and cost per defined daily dose (DDDc), market concentration, and the number of active manufacturers in hospital procurement. Segmented regression analysis estimated immediate level and subsequent slope changes. Sensitivity analyses with alternative comparator specifications assessed robustness and parallel trends assumptions.ResultsFollowing NCDP implementation, hospital procurement costs for TB medicines declined substantially across both hospital types. In general hospitals, expenditure decreased significantly. DDDc reductions were dramatic: linezolid decreased from approximately 800 Renminbi (RMB) to below 100 RMB; moxifloxacin from 99 RMB to 20 RMB; levofloxacin from 24 RMB to 9 RMB. Similar patterns were observed in specialized hospitals. Market structure effects exhibited heterogeneity: first-line drugs showed increased concentration with declining manufacturer participation, while second-line drugs demonstrated expanded supplier participation and decreased Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI). Sensitivity analyses confirmed robustness across alternative comparator specifications.ConclusionsThe NCDP achieved substantial price and expenditure reductions for TB medicines whilst inducing heterogeneous effects on market structure—increased concentration for first-line drugs but expanded supplier participation and decreased market concentration for second-line drugs. TB-specialized hospitals, with higher baseline concentration and fewer suppliers, face heightened supply chain vulnerability. Given NCDP’s design—where hospitals independently select among winning suppliers and may procure non-winning products—policymakers should implement differentiated approaches: multi-winner tender designs to preserve supplier diversity for first-line drugs; strategic stockpiles and supplier qualification programs for specialized hospitals; and continued volume-price guarantees for second-line agents such as linezolid and moxifloxacin. Regular monitoring of market structure indicators across hospital types is essential to balance price efficiency with supply resilience.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14668-y.

Background

In the global effort to end tuberculosis (TB), the “Global Plan to End TB 2023–2030” released by the Stop TB Partnership emphasizes addressing TB challenges through drug optimization, diagnostic technology improvement, and procurement strategy innovation [1]. The plan highlights the critical role of the Global Drug Facility (GDF) in reducing prices of key medicines such as bedaquiline (achieving price reductions of up to 55%) [1]. Against this international backdrop, China’s National Centralized Drug Procurement (NCDP) policy—a core component of healthcare reform implemented in 2018—aims to reduce drug costs and enhance accessibility through bulk purchasing. However, this policy has also raised concerns regarding increased market concentration, supply chain resilience, and potential impacts on long-term supplier participation. Internationally, GDF-procured TB drug prices serve as the primary benchmark for affordable access, with pooled procurement across over 160 countries achieving dramatically lower unit costs than individual country domestic markets.

The NCDP, also known as volume-based procurement (VBP), aggregates public-sector demand and negotiates large-scale, multi-year purchasing contracts directly with pharmaceutical manufacturers, correlating guaranteed purchase volumes with competitively bid prices [2–5]. Operationally, each hospital reports its expected procurement volume for each generic name, and these volumes are aggregated nationally to form the procurement base. Pharmaceutical manufacturers submit competitive bids, and winning suppliers are selected based on price ranking: the number of winners per generic name is determined by the number of qualified applicants (e.g., 1–2 winners for ≤ 3 applicants, 3–6 winners for 4–9 applicants, and up to 10 winners for ≥ 13 applicants), with agreed procurement volumes set at 50–80% of the base depending on the number of winners [2–5]. Critically, while hospitals must prioritize winning products and complete their agreed procurement volumes, they retain autonomy in two respects: first, when multiple suppliers win for the same generic name, each hospital independently selects which winning supplier(s) to purchase from; second, for the remaining volume beyond the agreed quota, hospitals may procure non-winning products of the same generic name—including originator brands—at prevailing market prices. This institutional design means that hospitals with different patient populations, clinical priorities, and formulary structures may develop divergent supplier selection patterns, resulting in heterogeneous procurement structures across hospital types even under the same centralized policy. The primary objectives are to reduce drug costs, promote rational medicine use, and optimize resource allocation within the public healthcare system. Since its initiation, evaluation studies have verified that NCDP has resulted in substantial price reductions (typically 40–90%) for winning-bid drugs, significantly alleviating healthcare costs for hospitals and patients [2–5]. These evaluations have spanned multiple therapeutic categories—including cardiovascular, antidiabetic, antibiotic, and antineoplastic drugs—demonstrating consistent price reduction effects [5–16]. However, studies have also revealed heterogeneous impacts on market structure: while some drug categories experienced increased supplier participation following NCDP implementation, others showed market consolidation with reduced manufacturer numbers [6, 8, 12]. Despite this extensive literature, the impact of NCDP on TB medicines remains largely uninvestigated.

Tuberculosis presents unique challenges for pharmaceutical markets compared to other disease areas. TB drugs are characterized by narrow profit margins, limited numbers of active manufacturers, and low commercial attractiveness, resulting in reduced willingness among enterprises to invest in research and development (R&D) of new anti-TB medicines [17]. Unlike cardiovascular and oncology drugs, where large patient populations and higher margins attract extensive manufacturer participation, the economic incentives for TB drug production and innovation are substantially weaker. As the challenge of drug-resistant TB becomes increasingly severe globally, ensuring sustainable drug supply and continued pharmaceutical innovation in this therapeutic area is critical for achieving global TB elimination targets.

Market structure significantly influences pharmaceutical industry dynamics and policy outcomes. According to the Structure-Conduct-Performance (SCP) framework from industrial organization theory [18, 19], market concentration—commonly measured by the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)—reflects the degree of competition and affects corporate decision-making regarding production, pricing, and innovation [20–25]. Research across multiple countries has demonstrated that market concentration impacts drug pricing, procurement patterns, and R&D investment [22–26]. In China, studies have shown that centralized procurement significantly affects drug procurement and expenditure, with procurement volumes of targeted drugs increasing while related expenditures decrease simultaneously [26]. These findings underscore the importance of examining how NCDP reshapes market structure within specific therapeutic markets.

A critical but understudied dimension of TB drug markets is the procurement-level heterogeneity between hospital types. In China’s healthcare system, TB care is delivered through two distinct institutional streams: general hospitals, which serve broader patient populations and procure anti-TB drugs alongside a wide range of other medicines; and TB-specialized hospitals, which focus exclusively on tuberculosis diagnosis and treatment within China’s vertical disease control structure. Under the NCDP framework, hospitals autonomously choose among winning suppliers and may procure non-winning and originator products beyond the agreed volume quota. These institutional differences in clinical focus directly shape procurement patterns. General hospitals, which primarily manage drug-susceptible TB in respiratory departments alongside other conditions, typically lack drug-resistant TB agents such as bedaquiline and maintain broader formularies with purchases distributed across more suppliers. TB-specialized hospitals, which concentrate on drug resistance testing and management of multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) with treatment courses of 18–24 months, represent high-volume, narrow-spectrum purchasers of anti-TB drugs—a profile that attracts greater manufacturer engagement and investment in formulary access, thereby influencing supplier selection dynamics. These divergent procurement structures under the same NCDP policy have not been systematically examined.

Existing research has mainly examined price reductions and procurement volume changes following individual NCDP rounds. Few studies have explored how the policy influences market concentration, supplier participation, and overall expenditure for TB drugs over multiple procurement cycles. Moreover, there is limited evidence on whether these reforms have strengthened or weakened supplier participation and market concentration within the TB pharmaceutical market, and no studies have examined differential policy effects between general and specialized hospitals. Given the pivotal role of TB treatment in China’s public health system and the growing challenge of drug-resistant TB, a comprehensive, time-series assessment of the policy’s effects across different hospital types is warranted.

To fill these gaps, the present study employs a multi-batch interrupted time series (ITS) design using monthly hospital procurement data from January 2015 to December 2022. We analyze six TB drugs included in the second to fourth NCDP rounds (isoniazid, moxifloxacin, ethambutol, linezolid, levofloxacin, and pyrazinamide) and compare them with nine non-procured comparators. By examining changes in procurement volume, expenditure, market concentration (HHI), and the number of active manufacturers, this study provides an integrated assessment of how NCDP reshapes the TB medicines market.

Our analytical approach incorporates three key innovations. First, we stratify analyses by hospital type—general versus TB-specialized hospitals—to capture heterogeneous policy impacts across different hospital types and drug categories that aggregate analyses would obscure. Second, we stratify by drug classification—first-line versus second-line agents—recognizing that these drug categories serve different patient populations and face different procurement dynamics. Third, we conduct extensive sensitivity analyses using alternative comparator specifications to assess the robustness of findings and validity of the parallel trends assumption underlying ITS designs.

Our contribution is fourfold. First, we move beyond single-batch or single-outcome assessments to provide a multi-outcome, multi-batch ITS analysis of the TB drug market under NCDP. Second, we quantify how centralized purchasing reshapes market structure—via HHI and supplier counts—thereby illuminating potential trade-offs between price efficiency and long-run supplier diversity. Third, by stratifying analyses by hospital type and drug classification, we reveal differential policy effects that have important implications for targeted policy interventions. Fourth, by contrasting NCDP-included TB drugs with contemporaneous comparators not subject to early rounds and conducting sensitivity analyses with alternative comparator sets, we strengthen causal interpretation of observed changes. Collectively, the findings aim to inform future procurement strategies that balance price efficiency with supply resilience and sustainable supplier participation.

Methods

Study design

We used an ITS design covering the period from January 2015 to December 2022 to analyse changes in hospital procurement volumes and spending, and market concentration on the tuberculosis drugs included in Antituberculosis medicines of the WHO Model Lists of Essential Medicines and of actual drug purchases catalogue in Chinese Medical Economic Information (CMEI) database in 2022 [27, 28]. In the 2023 WHO Model Lists of Essential Medicines, antituberculosis medicines include 23 drugs (isoniazid, ethambutol, pyrazinamide, rifampicin, ethionamide, moxifloxacin, rifabutin, rifapentine, amikacin, amoxicillin + clavulanic acid, bedaquiline, clofazimine, cycloserine, terizidone, delamanid, protionamide, levofloxacin, linezolid, meropenem, imipenem+cilastatin, p-aminosalicylate sodium, pretomanid and streptomycin), while the actual drug purchases catalogue in CMEI database does not include ethionamide, terizidone, delamanid, and pretomanid, which means these drugs are not used in hospitals in China during this period. Definitions of key terms and detailed dataset variable descriptions are provided in the Supplementary Appendix (Glossary of Key Terms; Table S1. Dataset Variable Descriptions).

The sample includes six tuberculosis medications that were included in the second to fourth round of the national centralized volume-based procurement, which constituted the intervention group. During this period, bedaquiline, clofazimine, and rifabutin were in a state of complete monopoly (supplied by a single manufacturer). Amoxicillin+clavulanate, although included in the second batch of NCDP, was excluded from the analysis because it is classified as a WHO Group C companion agent rather than a core anti-TB medicine, and its primary market is driven by general bacterial infections with extremely sparse procurement data in TB-specialized hospitals. Consequently, the comparison group comprised nine medications not included in these procurement rounds (details see Table 1). The intervention group included two medications from the second national centralized volume-based procurement policy (isoniazid, moxifloxacin), two from the third national centralized volume-based procurement policy (ethambutol, linezolid), and two from the fourth national centralized volume-based procurement policy (levofloxacin, pyrazinamide). For the stratified ITS analysis, comparators were selected based on clinical similarity: first-line intervention drugs (isoniazid, ethambutol, pyrazinamide) were compared with non-NCDP first-line drugs (rifampicin, rifapentine), while second-line intervention drugs (moxifloxacin, linezolid, levofloxacin) were compared with all non-NCDP second-line drugs (protionamide, cycloserine, amikacin, streptomycin, p-aminosalicylate sodium). Imipenem+cilastatin and meropenem, being carbapenems used primarily for extensively drug-resistant TB, were included only in sensitivity analyses to test robustness of findings.

Given the staggered implementation of NCDP across multiple rounds, we employed drug-specific ITS models rather than aggregated analysis. Each intervention drug was analyzed with its own model, with the post variable coded as 1 from the specific NCDP entry date for that drug (April 2020 for Round 2, October 2020 for Round 3, May 2021 for Round 4). This approach allows precise estimation of policy effects while accounting for the fact that drugs entered NCDP at different times. Comparator drugs were analyzed over the same time periods to ensure temporal alignment.

Data source

Outcome measures

The study assessed the following outcome measures:

Hospital Procurement Volume (DDDs): We adopted the “Defined Daily Dose” (DDD) recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) as the standardized measurement unit for drug procurement volume [29]. This indicator is mainly targeted at typical patients and is defined as the average daily dose of a certain drug when it is used for its primary indication. In this study, we calculated the number of DDDs purchased by public hospitals on a monthly basis to track the actual scale of drug procurement.

Expenditure: In terms of expenditure, we simultaneously calculate the total monthly purchase amount in Renminbi (RMB, Chinese Yuan) and the cost per defined daily dose (DDDc). This indicator can clearly reflect the actual impact of the national centralized procurement policy on the drug expenditure at the hospital level, and is helpful for evaluating the policy effect from an economic perspective [29].

Market Concentration: Market concentration was assessed using the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), a widely used metric to evaluate supplier concentration within a market [30–43]. The HHI is calculated by summing the squares of the market shares of each firm within the market [31–33]. Market concentration was measured using the.

Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), calculated as the sum of squared expenditure shares of all manufacturers for each drug in each month. Expenditure-based rather than volume-based HHI was used because it better captures market power dynamics and is more directly relevant to procurement cost containment objectives. The HHI can range from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating higher market concentration and reduced supplier diversity, potentially approaching a monopoly [33]. Conversely, values closer to 0 suggest a highly diversified market with broad supplier participation. Interpretation followed established thresholds: HHI < 0.15 represented unconcentrated market, 0.15 ≤ HHI ≤ 0.25 represented moderately concentrated market. HHI > 0.25 represented highly concentrated market.

Number of Manufacturers: The number of manufacturers involved in supplying each TB drug was tracked to evaluate the diversity of suppliers in the market before and after the NCDP intervention.

The defined daily doses (DDDs): It refers to the number of standard daily doses for each tuberculosis (TB) drug, calculated according to the dosage regimens specified in manufacturers’ product labels and approved by the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) [29]. For interrupted time series (ITS) analysis, we compiled data encompassing monthly hospital procurement expenditures and the aggregate volume (expressed in DDDs) of medications procured from January 2015 to December 2022 [44]. The DDD values and WHO drug classification for all study drugs are listed in the Supplementary Appendix (Table S2. Defined Daily Dose (DDD) Values and Drug Classification).

Statistical analysis

Statistical model

We employed segmented regression analysis for interrupted time series (ITS) to quantify the immediate level change and sustained slope change resulting from China’s National Centralized Drug Procurement (NCDP) policy on monthly hospital procurement outcomes for tuberculosis (TB) medicines across provinces in mainland China (excluding Tibet, Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan due to data unavailability). Assessed outcomes encompassed procurement volume (measured in defined daily doses, DDDs), procurement expenditure (total spending and defined daily dose cost, DDDc), market concentration (Herfindahl–Hirschman Index, HHI), and the number of active manufacturers. Monthly data spanned the period from January 2015 to December 2022.

We employed two complementary analytical approaches. First, for the main stratified analysis (Tables 3 and 4), we used a controlled ITS design: separate segmented regression models were estimated for the NCDP intervention group and the non-NCDP comparator group independently within each hospital type. Each model comprises four terms: an intercept (β₀, baseline level), a continuous time variable (β₁, pre-policy secular trend), a binary post-policy indicator (β₂, immediate level change), and a time × post interaction (β₃, trend change following implementation). This approach allows visual and qualitative comparison of NCDP drug trajectories against comparator drug trajectories. For multi-drug groups, expenditure and DDDs were aggregated by summation to reflect total market volume, while DDDc, HHI, and manufacturer counts were averaged to reflect market-level characteristics. Second, for the sensitivity analyses (Tables 5 and 6), we used a DID-ITS panel model that formally tests the differential policy effect. Each NCDP drug was paired with its comparator(s) in a stacked panel, with group interaction terms (δ₂ = group × post, δ₃ = group × time × post) capturing the NCDP drug’s change relative to its comparator. Pre-intervention parallel trends were assessed via the group × time interaction in the pre-policy period (p > 0.05 indicating satisfied).

Comparator selection and sensitivity analyses

Given clinical and epidemiological differences between first-line and second-line TB treatments, we conducted sensitivity analyses using alternative comparator sets restricted to drugs with similar clinical indications, price levels, and procurement patterns. Pre-intervention parallel trends were assessed via interaction tests in the pre-policy period. Results are reported for the main comparator and alternative sets. To ensure the reliability of the policy evaluation results, this study conducted robustness tests by constructing multiple sets of counterfactual scenarios. Statistical analysis software

All models were estimated using Python (scipy, numpy) with ordinary least squares (OLS) and iterative Prais-Winsten generalized least squares (GLS) correction for AR(1) autocorrelation. The full statistical model specification is described in the Supplementary Appendix (Sect. 1.2).

Patient and public involvement

In this study, solely the information regarding tuberculosis medications of patients was incorporated, and all such information was anonymized. Neither patients nor the general public were engaged in this study. All data have been subjected to anonymization and do not encompass any personally - identifiable details. Furthermore, the database does not contain any individual patient - level data or patient identifiers. Individual hospital identities were additionally anonymized during our analysis. Given that this study only entailed a secondary analysis of aggregated procurement data devoid of any patient - level information, ethical approval was not requisite in accordance with national regulations.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Figure 1, Panels A and B show the ranking of anti-tuberculosis drug market concentration in national and specialized hospitals from 2015 to 2022. Analysis of the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) reveals a sharp contrast: specialized hospitals have much higher market concentration than the overall hospital sample. The average HHI of specialized hospitals ranged from 0.61 to 0.73, while that of the overall sample was only 0.42 to 0.47, with a gap of 0.19 to 0.28. This shows extreme concentration in specialized hospital markets, where a few suppliers dominate. At the drug level, in specialized hospitals, bedaquiline, clofazimine and rifabutin had an HHI of 1.0, indicating complete monopoly, and other drugs like amoxicillin had HHI values from 0.94 to 0.98, showing oligopoly. In contrast, the overall hospital market is more fragmented, with most drugs’ HHI values ranging from 0.3 to 0.7, indicating diversified supplier participation. This shows that while there are some monopolistic categories in the tuberculosis drug market, most drugs maintain supplier diversity. The comparison of supplier numbers further highlights market differentiation. In the overall hospital sample, each drug has 16.6 to 20.7 suppliers on average, showing a highly diversified supplier market. In specialized hospitals, each drug has only 2.6 to 3.3 suppliers, less than 1/6 to 1/8 of the overall market, dominated by a few core enterprises. This disparity reflects supply-chain vulnerability and potential risks in specialized hospitals. Dynamically, the overall hospital market’s HHI decreased slightly from 0.46 to 0.44 between 2015 and 2022, indicating more decentralized supplier participation. Specialized hospitals, however, had a U-shaped fluctuation: HHI dropped from 0.73 to 0.61 from 2015 to 2019, then rebounded to 0.71 from 2020 to 2022. The rebound in specialized hospitals is related to the launch of the new drug bedaquiline, suggesting that innovative drugs can reshape the market in the short term and intensify concentration, while also showing the need for cutting-edge solutions in specialized treatment.

The second row, representing sales rankings, shows distinct patterns between national (Panel C) and specialized (Panel D) hospital samples. From the perspective of changes in sales ranking, the overall sample hospitals and the sample specialized hospitals present completely different market patterns. The overall market of the sample hospitals has demonstrated a high degree of stability and concentration. Meropenem has consistently ranked first in sales (with a standard deviation of 0), and high-value antibacterial drugs such as moxifloxacin, levofloxacin and imipenem+cilastatin have long occupied the top five positions. The sales share of the top three drugs has remained stable at 68% to 76%. It shows the overall continuous demand for high-end anti-tuberculosis drugs in large sample hospitals. In contrast, the sales ranking of sample specialized hospitals shows a significant dynamic change feature. In the early stage (2015–2017), levofloxacin and moxifloxacin were dominant. However, with the passage of time, the market structure underwent a fundamental transformation. Cycloserine rose rapidly and took the top spot after 2018. Hospital-stratified ITS analysis for first-line anti-TB drugs

For first-line drugs (isoniazid, ethambutol, pyrazinamide), the stratified analysis revealed distinct patterns between hospital types (see Table 3; Fig. 2; pre-intervention baseline characteristics in Supplementary Appendix Table S4.Pre-intervention Baseline Market Structure Indicators by Hospital Type). In general hospitals, NCDP implementation was associated with significant reductions in expenditure across all three procurement batches: Second Batch (β = -2,538,024 RMB, p < 0.001), Third Batch (β = -249,389 RMB, p < 0.01), and Fourth Batch (β = -983,474 RMB, p < 0.01). The DDDc (cost per defined daily dose) decreased significantly in both hospital types, with general hospitals showing immediate level changes of -1.13 RMB (p < 0.001), -0.39 RMB (p < 0.001), and − 0.97 RMB (p < 0.001) for the Second, Third, and Fourth Batches, respectively.

Market concentration (HHI) showed heterogeneous responses. In general hospitals, HHI increased significantly following NCDP implementation for Second Batch drugs (β = +0.12, p < 0.001), indicating market consolidation. However, Fourth Batch drugs showed a non-significant decrease in HHI (β = -0.04). Specialized hospitals exhibited similar patterns but with higher baseline HHI values (0.42–0.94 vs. 0.17–0.26 in general hospitals), reflecting their more concentrated supplier base.

The number of active manufacturers declined in general hospitals following NCDP, with significant reductions observed for Third Batch drugs (β = -1.37, p < 0.05). In contrast, specialized hospitals maintained relatively stable supplier numbers, though from a much lower baseline (1–4 manufacturers vs. 11–27 in general hospitals).

Hospital-stratified ITS analysis for second-line anti-TB drugs

For second-line drugs (moxifloxacin, linezolid, levofloxacin), the policy effects were more pronounced (See Table 4; Fig. 3). In general hospitals, moxifloxacin (Second Batch) showed dramatic expenditure reductions (β = -87,356,235 RMB, p < 0.001), accompanied by significant DDDc decreases (β = -27.68 RMB, p < 0.001). Linezolid (Third Batch) demonstrated the most striking price reduction. In general hospitals, while the immediate level change was not significant (level change β = +25.40, not significant), the trend change was steep (β = -14.81 RMB/month, p < 0.001), representing a sustained price decline from approximately 800 RMB to below 100 RMB over the post-policy period. In specialized hospitals, the immediate level change was β = -226.22 RMB (p < 0.001) with a sustained trend change of -8.77 RMB/month (p < 0.001).

Notably, the number of manufacturers increased significantly for second-line NCDP drugs in general hospitals: moxifloxacin (β = +1.85, p < 0.05), linezolid (level change β = +0.57, not significant; trend change + 0.23 per month, p < 0.05), and levofloxacin (β = +0.42, not significant). This contrasts with the pattern observed for first-line drugs and suggests that NCDP may have different effects on market structure depending on the initial market structure and drug category.

In specialized hospitals, policy effects were generally consistent with general hospitals but often with smaller magnitudes, likely reflecting the lower baseline volumes and the specialized nature of TB-specialized hospitals.

Sensitivity analysis with alternative comparator groups for first-line drugs

To assess the robustness of our findings and address concerns about comparator group validity, we conducted comprehensive sensitivity analyses using alternative comparator sets across both general and specialized hospitals (Tables 5 and 6). For each intervention drug, we tested the main analysis against two alternative specifications; the comparator selection strategy is detailed in Table 2. We formally tested the parallel trends assumption for each drug-comparator-hospital combination in the pre-intervention period.

In general hospitals, parallel trends were satisfied (p > 0.05) for DDDs in most specifications, supporting the validity of the ITS design for volume outcomes. Full parallel trends test results for all drug-outcome-hospital combinations are reported in the Supplementary Appendix (Table S5. Parallel Trends Test Results — All Outcomes, Both Hospital Types). However, the assumption was violated for a substantial proportion of outcomes: in Table 5 (first-line drugs), parallel trends were satisfied for 37 of 90 drug-outcome-hospital combinations (41%), while in Table 6 (second-line drugs), only 27 of 90 (30%) satisfied the assumption. Violations were concentrated in DDDc, HHI, and manufacturer count outcomes, where intervention and comparator drugs had divergent pre-intervention trajectories reflecting their fundamentally different market positions. Findings for these outcomes should therefore be interpreted as associations consistent with a policy effect rather than as causally identified impacts. DDDs outcomes showed substantially higher rates of parallel trends satisfaction, supporting stronger causal interpretation for procurement volume effects.

For isoniazid in general hospitals, the main analysis showed non-significant DDDs level changes (+ 108.6 K DDDs) with negative trend changes (-13.2 K DDDs/month), indicating stable procurement patterns. Expenditure reductions were significant (-2.06 M RMB, p < 0.001) alongside substantial DDDc decreases (-2.28 RMB, p < 0.001) and HHI increases (+ 0.17, p < 0.001). Alternative comparators (S1: Pyrazinamide; S2: Rifampicin alone) yielded consistent patterns: DDDs changes ranged from − 3.1 K to + 108.6 K (all non-significant), while expenditure reductions ranged from − 1.45 M to -2.06 M RMB (all p < 0.001).

In specialized hospitals, isoniazid showed non-significant DDDs changes across all specifications: main analysis (-7.9 K DDDs), S1 (-7.6 K DDDs), and S2 (-5.2 K DDDs), with parallel trends satisfied for DDDs outcomes. Expenditure reductions were significant (-268 K to -486 K RMB, p < 0.01), while DDDc reductions were observed but varied in significance (-1.58 to -2.20 RMB). HHI increased significantly in main analysis and S2 (+ 0.17 to + 0.29, p < 0.05 to p < 0.001).

For ethambutol (Round 3) in general hospitals, DDDs showed positive but non-significant level changes across specifications (+ 216.7 K to + 273.7 K DDDs), with parallel trends satisfied. DDDc reductions were significant (-0.55 to -1.48 RMB, p < 0.05 to p < 0.001), and HHI increased (+ 0.01 to + 0.16). In specialized hospitals, ethambutol DDDs showed positive level changes (+ 16.8 K to + 23.5 K DDDs, non-significant), with expenditure reductions ranging from − 48 K to -317 K RMB.

Pyrazinamide (Round 4) in general hospitals demonstrated mixed DDDs effects: positive level changes in main analysis and S1 (+ 41.1 K to + 46.3 K DDDs) but negative in S2 (-43.8 K DDDs, with significant negative trend − 12.8 K DDDs/month, p < 0.01). DDDc reductions were robust (-0.68 to -1.47 RMB, not significant to p < 0.05), and expenditure decreased significantly (-1.03 M to -1.46 M RMB, p < 0.05 to p < 0.001). Specialized hospital DDDs effects were negative but non-significant (-7.4 K to -52.4 K DDDs), with expenditure reductions of -168 K to -420 K RMB.

Sensitivity analysis with alternative comparator groups for second-line drugs

For second-line drugs, parallel trends were more consistently violated in general hospitals, particularly for expenditure and DDDs outcomes (Table 6). This reflects the distinct supplier participation patterns of second-line agents, which serve different patient populations (primarily MDR-TB) and have different demand drivers compared to first-line treatments. Detailed parallel trends results are in the Supplementary Appendix (Table S5. Parallel Trends Test Results — All Outcomes, Both Hospital Types).

Moxifloxacin in general hospitals showed heterogeneous DDDs effects across specifications: negative level changes in main analysis and S2 (-107.2 K to -128.6 K DDDs) but positive in S1 with levofloxacin comparator (+ 405.7 K DDDs). Despite these DDDs variations, expenditure reductions were consistently dramatic (-34.3 M to -41.0 M RMB, all p < 0.001), DDDc decreased substantially (-27.82 to -37.60 RMB, all p < 0.001), HHI declined (-0.09 to -0.10, p < 0.05), and manufacturer counts showed positive level changes (+ 1.57 to + 1.82, not significant to p < 0.05) with significant positive trend changes. In specialized hospitals, moxifloxacin DDDs showed small negative changes with parallel trends satisfied (-1.9 K to -3.4 K DDDs), alongside significant expenditure reductions (-47 K to -1.29 M RMB), DDDc decreases (-26.45 to -54.91 RMB, p < 0.001), and manufacturer increases (+ 2.03 to + 2.91, p < 0.01 to p < 0.001).

Linezolid exhibited substantial DDDs increases in general hospitals: +8.9 K to + 44.0 K DDDs in main analysis and S1 (with parallel trends satisfied), but negative changes in S2 with carbapenem comparators (-27.3 K DDDs). The most pronounced effects were on DDDc: in general hospitals, level changes were non-significant but trend changes were steep (-14.84 to -19.42 RMB/month, all p < 0.001), representing approximately 80–90% price decreases over the post-policy period. HHI showed non-significant level changes but significant negative trend changes (-0.01/month, p < 0.05 to p < 0.01), and manufacturer counts showed non-significant level changes but significant positive trend changes (+ 0.42 to + 0.46/month, p < 0.001). In specialized hospitals, linezolid DDDs effects were heterogeneous: negative in main analysis and S1 (-3.1 K to -5.6 K DDDs) but positive in S2 with carbapenem comparators (+ 3.1 K DDDs, p < 0.001). DDDc reductions in specialized hospitals were even more striking (-315 to -353 RMB, all p < 0.001), HHI decreased substantially (-0.23 to -0.33, p < 0.05 to p < 0.01), and manufacturers increased (+ 2.14 to + 2.80, p < 0.01 to p < 0.001).

Levofloxacin demonstrated robust positive DDDs level changes in general hospitals across all specifications (+ 729.5 K to + 799.3 K DDDs), with parallel trends satisfied in most cases. Expenditure reductions were significant (-27.9 M to -33.2 M RMB, all p < 0.001), and DDDc decreased consistently (-8.84 to -23.64 RMB, p < 0.001). HHI effects were mixed (+ 0.04 to -0.03), and manufacturer counts initially increased (+ 3.28 to + 3.78, not significant) with negative trend changes (-1.69 to -1.72/month, p < 0.001). In specialized hospitals, levofloxacin DDDs showed smaller and mixed effects: positive in main analysis and S1 (+ 3.7 K to + 10.5 K DDDs) but negative in S2 (-1.0 K DDDs), with significant negative trend changes in some specifications (-1.5 K to -2.0 K DDDs/month, p < 0.05 to p < 0.001). Expenditure reductions ranged from − 527 K to -1.55 M RMB (p < 0.001), and DDDc decreased by -4.04 to -42.57 RMB.

Robustness of main findings

DDDs effects showed notable heterogeneity across drugs, reflecting the complex interplay between price reductions, clinical guidelines, and supplier participation patterns. For first-line drugs, DDDs changes were generally modest and non-significant, suggesting that NCDP primarily affected pricing rather than procurement volumes. In general hospitals: isoniazid (-3.1 K to + 108.6 K DDDs, p > 0.05), ethambutol (+ 216.7 K to + 273.7 K DDDs, p > 0.05), and pyrazinamide (-43.8 K to + 46.3 K DDDs, mixed significance). Specialized hospitals showed similar patterns with smaller magnitudes reflecting their lower baseline volumes.

Second-line drugs exhibited more heterogeneous DDDs responses. Moxifloxacin showed negative level changes in most specifications (-107.2 K to -128.6 K DDDs in general hospitals), potentially reflecting substitution toward other fluoroquinolones following price equalization. Linezolid DDDs increased modestly (+ 8.9 K to + 44.0 K DDDs in general hospitals), possibly indicating improved access following dramatic price reductions. Levofloxacin demonstrated the most consistent DDDs increases (+ 729.5 K to + 799.3 K DDDs in general hospitals), suggesting that NCDP-induced price reductions facilitated expanded procurement. Importantly, parallel trends for DDDs were satisfied in most first-line drug specifications and many second-line specifications, supporting valid causal interpretation for volume outcomes.

Significant expenditure reductions were observed across all comparator specifications and both hospital types, representing the most robust finding. In general hospitals: isoniazid (-1.45 M to -2.06 M RMB, all p < 0.001), ethambutol (-184 K to -752 K RMB, mixed significance), pyrazinamide (-1.03 M to -1.46 M RMB, p < 0.05 to p < 0.001), moxifloxacin (-34.3 M to -41.0 M RMB, all p < 0.001), linezolid (+ 1.1 M to + 13.3 M RMB level change with significant negative trend changes of -1.77 M to -2.34 M RMB/month, all p < 0.05), and levofloxacin (-27.9 M to -33.2 M RMB, all p < 0.001). In specialized hospitals: isoniazid (-268 K to -486 K RMB, p < 0.01), ethambutol (-48 K to -317 K RMB), pyrazinamide (-168 K to -420 K RMB), moxifloxacin (-47 K to -1.29 M RMB), linezolid (-319 K to + 718 K RMB level change), and levofloxacin (-527 K to -1.55 M RMB, p < 0.001). A comprehensive summary of robustness findings across all specifications is provided in the Supplementary Appendix (Part III).

DDDc decreases were robust across specifications, with particularly strong effects for second-line drugs. In general hospitals: isoniazid (-0.71 to -2.28 RMB, p < 0.01 to p < 0.001), ethambutol (-0.55 to -1.48 RMB, p < 0.05 to p < 0.001), pyrazinamide (-0.68 to -1.47 RMB, mixed significance), moxifloxacin (-27.82 to -37.60 RMB, p < 0.001), linezolid (-315 to -353 RMB in specialized hospitals, p < 0.001; level changes in general hospitals were non-significant but with steep trend declines of -14.84 to -19.42 RMB/month, p < 0.001), and levofloxacin (-5.90 to -8.84 RMB, p < 0.05 to p < 0.001). In specialized hospitals, DDDc reductions were often larger in magnitude: isoniazid (-1.58 to -2.20 RMB), ethambutol (-0.22 to -0.46 RMB), moxifloxacin (-26.45 to -54.91 RMB, p < 0.001), linezolid (-339 to -388 RMB, p < 0.001), and levofloxacin (-4.04 to -42.57 RMB). The greater DDDc reductions in specialized hospitals likely reflect higher baseline prices in specialized hospitals.

The divergent HHI effects between first-line and second-line drugs were replicated across hospital types and comparator specifications. First-line drugs consistently showed HHI increases indicating market consolidation: isoniazid (+ 0.03 to + 0.29), ethambutol (-0.19 to + 0.15, mixed), pyrazinamide (-0.05 to + 0.10). Second-line drugs predominantly showed HHI decreases indicating expanded supplier participation: moxifloxacin (-0.14 to -0.17 in general hospitals; +0.11 to + 0.15 in specialized hospitals reflecting different baseline conditions), linezolid (-0.07 to -0.11 in general hospitals; -0.21 to -0.36 in specialized hospitals, all p < 0.001), and levofloxacin (mixed effects: -0.41 to + 0.12 depending on comparator and hospital type). The more pronounced HHI decreases for linezolid in specialized hospitals suggest that NCDP particularly expanded supplier participation in hospital types that previously had highly concentrated markets.

The divergent effects on manufacturer counts—decreases for first-line drugs and increases for second-line drugs—were consistently replicated across comparator specifications and hospital types. In general hospitals, first-line drug manufacturer counts declined: isoniazid (-4.74 to -5.78, p < 0.001), ethambutol (-0.30 to -1.97, not significant to p < 0.05), and pyrazinamide (-0.42 to -1.48, not significant). Second-line drugs showed manufacturer increases: moxifloxacin (+ 1.57 to + 1.82, not significant to p < 0.05; with S1 showing − 8.18, p < 0.05, reflecting comparator-specific effects), linezolid (+ 0.26 to + 1.11, not significant; with significant positive trend changes + 0.42 to + 0.46/month, p < 0.001), and levofloxacin (+ 3.28 to + 3.78, not significant). In specialized hospitals, the effects were more muted given lower baseline manufacturer counts: moxifloxacin (+ 2.03 to + 2.91, p < 0.01 to p < 0.001), linezolid (+ 2.14 to + 2.80, p < 0.01 to p < 0.001), while first-line drugs showed minimal changes.

In conclusion, the hospital-stratified sensitivity analyses corroborate that NCDP implementation was significantly associated with substantial decreases in drug expenditure and unit costs across diverse hospital types and comparator specifications, while DDDs effects were more heterogeneous reflecting drug-specific market responses to price changes. The divergent impacts on market structure—characterized by heightened concentration for first-line drugs and expanded supplier participation for second-line drugs—were consistently detected in both general and specialized hospitals, irrespective of the analytical approach. This replication across hospital types with fundamentally different baseline market structure indicators (HHI 0.17–0.26 for first-line drugs, 0.07–0.63 for second-line drugs vs. 0.42–0.94; manufacturers 11–27 vs. 1–4) provides strong evidence that these are authentic policy effects rather than artifacts of comparator selection or setting-specific confounding. The findings underscore the importance of differentiated policy approaches for different drug categories and hospital types to optimize the balance between cost containment, procurement volume optimization, and supply chain resilience.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study provides the first comprehensive, hospital-stratified interrupted time series (ITS) analysis of the National Centralized Drug Procurement (NCDP) policy’s impact on the tuberculosis (TB) drug market in China. Our analysis reveals substantial heterogeneity in policy effects across hospital types, drug classifications, and market outcome measures. We note at the outset that the strength of causal inference varies by outcome: pre-intervention parallel trends were satisfied for the majority of procurement volume (DDDs) specifications but violated for most price (DDDc) and market structure (HHI, manufacturer count) outcomes. Accordingly, we present DDDs effects as our most causally credible findings, while price and market structure effects are presented as associations that are consistent with, but not causally identified as, NCDP impacts.

The stratified analysis revealed substantial differences in procurement market structure between general hospitals and TB-specialized hospitals. Pre-intervention baseline characteristics illustrating this heterogeneity are presented in the Supplementary Appendix (Table S4. Pre-intervention Baseline Market Structure Indicators by Hospital Type). General hospitals—which serve broader patient populations with diversified disease profiles—maintained diversified supplier bases with baseline HHI values of 0.17–0.26 for first-line drugs and 0.07–0.63 for second-line drugs, with an average of 11–27 active manufacturers participating in hospital procurement per drug category. In contrast, TB-specialized hospitals—which operate within China’s vertical disease control structure serving exclusively TB patients—exhibited substantially higher baseline market concentration (HHI 0.42–0.94) and fewer active suppliers in procurement (1–4 manufacturers). This institutional heterogeneity reflects the interaction between NCDP’s policy design and divergent hospital characteristics. Under NCDP, winning suppliers and prices are determined centrally, but hospitals retain autonomy in selecting among winners and may procure non-winning products (including originator brands) for their remaining volume. General hospitals—with diverse patient populations and broader formularies—exercise this autonomy across a wide range of suppliers, while specialized hospitals—as high-volume, narrow-spectrum purchasers of anti-TB drugs that attract focused manufacturer engagement—develop more concentrated supplier relationships. These differences have important implications for how NCDP reforms differentially affect hospital types with varying degrees of supply chain vulnerability.

The NCDP achieved substantial and consistent price reductions across all included TB drugs and both hospital types. In general hospitals, expenditure decreased significantly for all procurement batches: Second Batch first-line drugs (β = -2,538,024 RMB, p < 0.001), Third Batch (β = -249,389 RMB, p < 0.01), and Fourth Batch (β = -983,474 RMB, p < 0.01). The cost per defined daily dose (DDDc) declined dramatically, with linezolid showing the most striking reduction—from approximately 800 RMB to below 100 RMB. In specialized hospitals, linezolid DDDc showed an immediate level change of β = -226.22 RMB (p < 0.001) and a sustained trend change of -8.77 RMB/month (p < 0.001); in general hospitals, the level change was non-significant but the trend decline was steep (β = -14.81 RMB/month, p < 0.001). Moxifloxacin DDDc decreased from 99 RMB to 20 RMB, and levofloxacin from 24 RMB to 9 RMB. In specialized hospitals, similar price reduction patterns emerged: isoniazid expenditure decreased by -418,134 RMB (p < 0.001), and linezolid DDDc decreased by -353.33 RMB (p < 0.001). These price reductions were consistent across sensitivity analyses using alternative comparator specifications, confirming the robustness of NCDP’s cost-containment effects across both hospital types.

Procurement volume (DDDs) effects showed notable heterogeneity reflecting the complex interplay between price reductions, clinical guidelines, and supplier participation patterns. For first-line drugs, DDDs changes were generally modest and non-significant in both hospital types, suggesting that NCDP primarily affected pricing rather than procurement volumes. In general hospitals: isoniazid (-3.1 K to + 108.6 K DDDs, p > 0.05), ethambutol (+ 216.7 K to + 273.7 K DDDs, p > 0.05), and pyrazinamide (-43.8 K to + 46.3 K DDDs, mixed significance). For second-line drugs, levofloxacin demonstrated the most consistent DDDs increases (+ 729.5 K to + 799.3 K DDDs in general hospitals), suggesting that NCDP-induced price reductions facilitated expanded procurement. Linezolid DDDs increased modestly (+ 8.9 K to + 44.0 K DDDs), potentially indicating improved access following dramatic price reductions. Importantly, parallel trends for DDDs were satisfied in most first-line drug specifications, supporting valid causal interpretation for volume outcomes.

Policy effects on market structure exhibited notable heterogeneity between drug categories and hospital types. For first-line drugs (isoniazid, ethambutol, pyrazinamide), market concentration in hospital procurement increased following NCDP implementation. In single-group ITS analysis (Table 3), HHI rose by + 0.12 (p < 0.001) for Second Batch drugs in general hospitals and + 0.15 (p < 0.01) in specialized hospitals, with manufacturer numbers declining by -1.17 (not significant) for isoniazid and − 1.37 (p < 0.05) for ethambutol. The DID-ITS sensitivity analysis (Table 5) confirmed these patterns with larger effect sizes: isoniazid HHI + 0.17 (p < 0.001) and manufacturer count − 5.78 (p < 0.001) relative to comparators, and ethambutol manufacturer count − 1.97 (p < 0.05). This consolidation pattern suggests that competitive pressure from price reductions may have reduced the number of suppliers actively participating in hospital procurement, though it does not necessarily indicate that manufacturers exited the market entirely or ceased production. In contrast, second-line drugs (moxifloxacin, linezolid, levofloxacin) demonstrated increased supplier participation in hospital procurement post-NCDP: manufacturer counts rose significantly for moxifloxacin (+ 1.85, p < 0.05 in general hospitals; +2.35, p < 0.001 in specialized hospitals), linezolid (+ 0.57, not significant, with significant trend increase + 0.23/month, p < 0.05, in general hospitals; +2.14, p < 0.01 in specialized hospitals). This divergent pattern indicates that NCDP’s effects on market structure depend critically on initial market structure, baseline pricing, and commercial attractiveness of specific drug categories.

The sensitivity analyses corroborated these findings across alternative comparator specifications and both hospital types. Expenditure reductions were robust for isoniazid (range: -1.45 M to -2.06 M RMB in general hospitals, -268 K to -486 K RMB in specialized hospitals), moxifloxacin (-34.3 M to -41.0 M RMB in general hospitals, -47 K to -1.29 M RMB in specialized hospitals), and levofloxacin (-27.9 M to -33.2 M RMB in general hospitals, -527 K to -1.55 M RMB in specialized hospitals). DDDc decreases remained consistent for linezolid (-174 to -225 RMB in general hospitals, -339 to -388 RMB in specialized hospitals) and moxifloxacin (-38 to -51 RMB in general hospitals, -26 to -55 RMB in specialized hospitals). The divergent market structure effects—increased concentration for first-line drugs and expanded supplier participation for second-line drugs—were replicated across hospital types and analytical approaches, suggesting these are genuine policy effects rather than artifacts of comparator selection or setting-specific confounding. Detailed robustness findings are summarized in the Supplementary Appendix (Part III).

More broadly, the sensitivity analyses demonstrate that the observed policy effects are robust across alternative analytical specifications, strengthening confidence in these findings as a basis for policy recommendations. The parallel trends assessment revealed that while the ITS design assumptions were satisfied for volume outcomes (DDDs) in most specifications, they were violated for some price and market concentration outcomes—suggesting that causal interpretation should be more cautious for HHI and DDDc findings. The replication of findings across both general and specialized hospitals, with their fundamentally different baseline market structure indicators, provides additional evidence that these are authentic policy effects rather than artifacts of comparator selection or setting-specific confounding.

An important consideration for interpreting these findings is the temporal overlap between NCDP Round 2 implementation (April 2020) and the COVID-19 pandemic. Most drugs showed 15–29% declines in procurement volume during the early pandemic period, with patterns normalizing by April–June 2020. Critically, formal statistical testing confirmed that the COVID-19 procurement shock was comparable across hospital types: general hospitals experienced a mean year-over-year DDDs decline of − 17.3% versus − 18.1% in specialized hospitals (paired t-test p = 0.917; Wilcoxon p = 0.685), and expenditure changes were similarly non-differential (− 18.4% vs. − 16.3%, paired t-test p = 0.798). Within each hospital type, the COVID shock did not differ significantly between intervention and comparator drug groups (general: p = 0.534; specialized: p = 0.731). Detailed results are reported in the Supplementary Appendix (Table S7 and Part IV, Sect. 4.1). Nevertheless, Round 2 findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution given this temporal overlap.

Implications for drug access and innovation

From an immediate effect perspective, NCDP’s role in enhancing TB drug affordability is clear and substantial. The reduction in procurement prices directly alleviates the financial pressure on hospitals and improves patient access to necessary medications [48]. The dramatic price reductions observed for second-line drugs—particularly linezolid (approximately 80–90% reduction) and moxifloxacin (approximately 80% reduction)—have meaningful implications for the treatment of drug-resistant TB, where high medication costs have historically posed significant barriers to guideline-concordant therapy.

However, contextualizing these reductions against international procurement benchmarks reveals that substantial room for further price improvement remains. Analysis of international procurement transaction data from the Global Fund Price and Quality Reporting (PQR) database reveals that despite NCDP’s substantial price reductions, China’s post-NCDP hospital procurement prices for second-line TB drugs remain markedly higher than prices achieved through the Stop TB Partnership’s Global Drug Facility (GDF) [49]. For linezolid, China’s post-NCDP DDDc (approximately 112 RMB, or US$15.78) was 46-fold higher than the GDF median procurement price (US$0.34 per DDD based on 2022–2024 transaction data); for moxifloxacin, the ratio was 19-fold (US$2.84 vs. US$0.15); and for levofloxacin, 25-fold (US$1.21 vs. US$0.05). In contrast, first-line drugs achieved near-international price parity: ethambutol’s post-NCDP DDDc was 0.7-fold the GDF benchmark, and pyrazinamide reached 1.1-fold, suggesting that NCDP has been highly effective for low-cost, high-volume generics [49]. The persistent price gap for second-line drugs reflects the fact that China, as a GDF-ineligible country, negotiates drug prices independently without reference to international procurement benchmarks [50, 51]. The GDF’s recent achievement—reducing the complete BPaLM regimen for drug-resistant TB to below US$300 per treatment course through competitive generic tendering —highlights the magnitude of potential savings [52]. These findings suggest that incorporating international reference pricing, particularly GDF benchmark prices, into NCDP’s price negotiation framework could yield further substantial reductions for second-line anti-TB drugs, thereby improving affordability for drug-resistant TB treatment.

Beyond immediate affordability considerations, the relationship between centralized procurement and pharmaceutical innovation warrants consideration, though it lies beyond the scope of the present study. Theoretical considerations suggest that significant compression of generic drug prices may reduce profit margins and potentially dampen research and development incentives—particularly for therapeutically challenging areas such as MDR-TB where commercial returns are already limited [53–55]. However, multiple countervailing factors—including government research funding, regulatory incentives such as accelerated approval pathways, and market exclusivity provisions—may partially offset margin compression for innovators. Future research specifically designed to assess innovation outcomes is needed to establish any causal relationship between centralized procurement and pharmaceutical research investment in the TB therapeutic area.

Market concentration and supplier dynamics

The price reductions observed following NCDP implementation were associated with substantial changes in the supplier landscape of the TB drug market, with differentiated effects across drug categories and hospital types. Our hospital-stratified analysis reveals that the relationship between centralized procurement and market structure is more nuanced than previously understood.

For first-line TB drugs—characterized by low baseline prices, narrow profit margins, and established presence of multiple generic manufacturers—NCDP implementation was associated with increased market concentration (noting that parallel trends were violated for most HHI and manufacturer count outcomes, requiring cautious interpretation) in hospital procurement. In general hospitals, HHI increased by + 0.12 (p < 0.001) for Second Batch isoniazid (Table 3), + 0.04 (p < 0.01) for Third Batch ethambutol, and − 0.04 (not significant) for Fourth Batch pyrazinamide, while manufacturer numbers in procurement declined (isoniazid: -1.17, not significant, Table 3; ethambutol: -1.37, p < 0.05, Table 3; pyrazinamide: -0.12, not significant, Table 3). In specialized hospitals, similar patterns emerged: isoniazid HHI increased by + 0.17 (p < 0.05), and ethambutol HHI increased by + 0.15 (main analysis). This consolidation pattern reflects the interplay between NCDP’s competitive tendering mechanism and the low-margin characteristics of first-line drugs. As fewer manufacturers successfully bid at the reduced price levels, hospitals’ choice among winning suppliers narrowed; meanwhile, the minimum usage ratio requirements for winning products further constrained hospitals’ ability to maintain diverse non-winning suppliers in their procurement, leading to increased concentration in hospital-level purchasing [56–59].

It is important to note that our data capture hospital procurement concentration rather than total market structure. A decrease in the ‘number of manufacturers’ in hospital procurement data reflects fewer vendors winning bids or actively participating in procurement, not necessarily that manufacturers have exited the market entirely or ceased production. Some suppliers may continue serving retail pharmacies, private clinics, or other distribution channels not captured in our hospital-based dataset.

Notably, second-line drugs exhibited contrasting dynamics. For moxifloxacin, linezolid, and levofloxacin—which had higher baseline prices and greater profit potential even after NCDP-induced reductions—NCDP facilitated new market entry and business expansion in hospital procurement. In general hospitals, manufacturer counts increased significantly: moxifloxacin (+ 1.85, p < 0.05), linezolid (+ 0.57, not significant; trend + 0.23/month, p < 0.05), and levofloxacin (level change + 0.42, not significant; trend change − 2.11/month, p < 0.001). In specialized hospitals, similar patterns were observed: moxifloxacin (+ 2.35, p < 0.001) and linezolid (+ 2.14, p < 0.01). Concurrently, HHI decreased for these drugs: moxifloxacin (-0.09, p < 0.05 in general hospitals), linezolid (-0.08, not significant in general hospitals; -0.33, p < 0.01 in specialized hospitals). This finding indicates that NCDP’s guaranteed volume commitments and transparent pricing attracted new manufacturers to seek winning-bid status for these higher-margin drugs. Because hospitals independently select among multiple winners, the entry of new suppliers directly translated into more diversified hospital-level procurement. In specialized hospitals, where concentrated TB caseloads—including drug-resistant TB patients requiring agents such as bedaquiline, linezolid, and moxifloxacin that are largely absent from general hospital formularies—represent commercially attractive, narrow-spectrum markets for anti-TB drug manufacturers, this effect was particularly pronounced, as manufacturers actively invested in gaining formulary access to these high-volume specialized hospitals.

The hospital-stratified analysis further revealed that TB-specialized hospitals face heightened supply chain vulnerability compared to general hospitals. With baseline HHI values of 0.42–0.94 for first-line drugs and 0.31–0.85 for second-line drugs (compared to 0.17–0.26 and 0.07–0.63 respectively in general hospitals) and only 1–4 active first-line drug suppliers in procurement (compared to 11–27 in general hospitals), with second-line drugs showing 1–11 suppliers in specialized vs. 1–132 in general hospitals, specialized hospitals operate with limited supplier redundancy. The more pronounced HHI decreases for linezolid in specialized hospitals (-0.33 vs. -0.08 in general hospitals) suggest that NCDP may have particularly benefited these previously concentrated markets by attracting new supplier participation. However, for first-line drugs where margins are narrower, any further market consolidation in these hospital types could significantly threaten treatment continuity. These baseline differences are quantified in the Supplementary Appendix (Table S4. Pre-intervention Baseline Market Structure Indicators by Hospital Type).

These dual trends underscore the necessity for differentiated procurement strategies tailored to the distinct supplier participation patterns of different TB drug categories. For first-line drugs with mature generic markets and low margins, policy design should prioritize supplier diversity preservation and market entry facilitation to prevent excessive consolidation. For second-line agents with higher value and commercial potential, the current NCDP framework appears to effectively balance price efficiency with supplier participation.

Policy and supply chain implications

Our findings carry important implications for TB drug procurement policy design in China and potentially for similar centralized purchasing systems internationally. The documented heterogeneity in policy effects—across hospital types, drug classifications, and market outcomes—suggests that uniform procurement approaches may produce unintended consequences that warrant targeted policy interventions.

First, the substantial heterogeneity between general and specialized hospitals indicates that procurement frameworks should incorporate institution-specific considerations. TB-specialized hospitals, with their higher baseline market concentration (HHI 0.42–0.94 for first-line drugs, 0.31–0.85 for second-line drugs) and fewer active suppliers in procurement (1–4 manufacturers for first-line drugs, 1–11 for second-line drugs), face greater supply chain fragility than general hospitals. Although NCDP’s institutional design allows hospitals to choose among multiple winning suppliers and to procure non-winning products, specialized hospitals’ narrow drug portfolios and concentrated caseloads inherently limit the pool of suppliers willing to compete for their formulary access. Our data demonstrate that while NCDP expanded supplier participation for second-line drugs in both hospital types, the absolute number of suppliers in specialized hospitals remains limited. Policy interventions for specialized hospitals should prioritize supply security mechanisms—including strategic stockpiles, supplier qualification programs, and contingency procurement arrangements—particularly for medicines critical to drug-resistant TB treatment regimens where supply disruptions could have severe clinical consequences.

Second, the divergent market structure effects between first-line and second-line drugs suggest that differentiated procurement strategies are warranted. For first-line drugs showing post-NCDP concentration increases (isoniazid HHI: +0.12, p < 0.001, Table 3; manufacturer count: -5.78, p < 0.001, Table 5), policy mechanisms should actively preserve supplier diversity. This could include multi-winner tender designs that allocate procurement volumes across multiple qualified manufacturers, preventing single-source dependency and maintaining competitive market structures for essential, low-margin TB medicines.

Third, the successful expansion of supplier participation for second-line drugs suggests that NCDP’s volume-price guarantee mechanism can effectively attract market entry when profit margins remain viable. For moxifloxacin, linezolid, and levofloxacin, manufacturer counts increased significantly despite substantial price reductions (DDDc decreases of 50–90%). This finding indicates that centralized procurement can achieve dual objectives—cost reduction and supplier diversity enhancement—when structured appropriately. Policymakers should consider extending similar volume-guarantee mechanisms to newer anti-TB agents and newly approved anti-TB agents to encourage market participation while maintaining affordability.

Fourth, continuous monitoring of market structure indicators should be institutionalized within the NCDP evaluation framework. Regular tracking of HHI, manufacturer counts in procurement, and supplier concentration across hospital types would enable early detection of concerning consolidation trends, allowing timely policy adjustments before supply vulnerabilities materialize into actual drug shortages. Integrating these market surveillance activities with clinical drug-resistance monitoring and patient outcome tracking would provide a comprehensive evidence base for ongoing policy optimization in TB care. Together, these four measures—institution-specific supply security, differentiated procurement design, volume-guarantee mechanisms for newer agents, and systematic market surveillance—would help ensure that centralized procurement achieves sustained price efficiency without compromising supply resilience.

Limitations

First, our analysis relies on hospital-level aggregated purchasing data from the CMEI database, which captures public hospital procurement but does not include private medical institutions, retail pharmacies, or other non-hospital procurement channels. This data source limitation may lead to an incomplete assessment of total TB drug market structure. Specifically, the observed decrease in number of manufacturers in hospital procurement data reflects fewer vendors actively participating in hospital tenders, not necessarily that manufacturers have exited the market entirely or ceased production. Some suppliers may continue serving alternative distribution channels not captured in our dataset. A detailed discussion of this limitation is provided in the Supplementary Appendix (Part IV, Sect. 4.2).

Second, the hospital-level aggregation precludes analysis of individual patient treatment outcomes and clinical indicators. We cannot directly assess whether the observed price reductions translated into improved medication adherence, treatment completion rates, or clinical outcomes. This data limitation affects the depth and practical value of translating research findings into clinical practice recommendations.

Third, the study period (2015–2022) primarily captures short- to medium-term effects of centralized procurement policies. Long-term impacts on TB drug resistance patterns, manufacturer R&D investment decisions, and market structure evolution require extended observation periods beyond our current data availability.

Fourth, NCDP implementation for Round 2 drugs (April 2020) coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, introducing potential confounding from pandemic-related disruptions to healthcare delivery and procurement patterns. Although formal testing confirmed that the overall COVID-19 shock was statistically comparable across hospital types (paired t-test p = 0.917 for DDDs, p = 0.798 for expenditure; Supplementary Appendix Table S7), the stratified analysis revealed a marginally significant difference for intervention drugs specifically (general: -12.9% vs. specialized: -25.0%; paired t-test p = 0.038; Wilcoxon p = 0.063), warranting cautious interpretation of Round 2 findings in specialized hospitals.

Fifth, the parallel trends assumption—critical for valid ITS inference—was violated for some price (DDDc) and market concentration (HHI) outcomes (see Supplementary Appendix Table S5. Parallel Trends Test Results — All Outcomes, Both Hospital Types for full results), though it was generally satisfied for volume (DDDs) outcomes. This suggests that causal interpretation should be more confident for procurement volume effects than for price and market structure effects, where secular trends may confound policy impact estimates.

Sixth, certain study drugs — most notably levofloxacin, a fluoroquinolone widely prescribed for urinary tract infections, respiratory infections, and other non-TB conditions — are not used exclusively for tuberculosis treatment. Our hospital-level procurement data do not differentiate between TB-related and non-TB prescribing, which may overestimate TB-specific procurement volumes, particularly in general hospitals. This limitation is less pronounced in TB-specialized hospitals, and may partially explain the substantially larger DDDs changes observed for levofloxacin in general hospitals (+ 729.5 K to + 799.3 K DDDs) compared to specialized hospitals (+ 3.7 K to + 10.5 K DDDs).

Seventh, our analysis focuses on a specific subset of TB drugs (6 intervention drugs, 9 comparator drugs) that entered NCDP during Rounds 2–4. The findings may not generalize to other TB medications, particularly newer agents such as bedaquiline and delamanid that have different market structures and clinical applications.

Policy implications and recommendations

This study highlights the need for continuous monitoring and differentiated approaches within the NCDP framework. Based on our empirical findings, we offer the following policy recommendations:

First, implement multi-winner procurement mechanisms for first-line TB drugs. Our data demonstrate significant market concentration increases following NCDP implementation: isoniazid HHI increased by + 0.12 (p < 0.001, Table 3), and manufacturer counts in hospital procurement declined by 4.74–5.78 (p < 0.001, Table 5). To prevent excessive consolidation, procurement frameworks should allocate volumes across multiple qualified manufacturers, maintaining supplier diversity for essential medicines where single-source dependency poses supply security risks. The interpretation of HHI and manufacturer count metrics is discussed further in the Supplementary Appendix (Part IV, Sect. 4.3).

Second, establish targeted supply security mechanisms for TB-specialized hospitals. Our hospital-stratified analysis revealed that specialized hospitals operate with substantially higher baseline concentration (HHI 0.42–0.94 for first-line drugs, 0.31–0.85 for second-line drugs) and fewer active suppliers (1–4 first-line drug manufacturers, 1–11 s-line drugs) compared to general hospitals (HHI 0.17–0.26 first-line drugs, 0.07–0.63 s-line drugs; 11–27 and 1–132 manufacturers respectively). Given the critical role of these institutions in treating drug-resistant TB, policy interventions should include strategic stockpiles, emergency procurement protocols, and supplier qualification programs specific to specialized hospital needs.

Third, maintain and extend volume-price guarantee mechanisms for second-line anti-TB drugs. The observed expansion of supplier participation for moxifloxacin (+ 1.85 manufacturers, p < 0.05), linezolid (significant positive trend of + 0.23 manufacturers/month, p < 0.05), and levofloxacin (+ 0.42 manufacturers, not significant) demonstrates that NCDP can achieve simultaneous price reduction and supplier diversity enhancement when commercial viability is preserved. Similar mechanisms should be considered for newer agents entering the procurement system to encourage market entry while ensuring affordability.

Fourth, future NCDP rounds covering TB drugs should incorporate international price benchmarking, particularly against GDF procurement prices, as a reference point for price negotiations. Our analysis indicates that while NCDP has brought first-line drug prices to near-international parity (0.7–4.1× GDF benchmark), second-line drugs remain 19–46× above GDF procurement prices despite substantial domestic reductions. Given that the GDF achieves these prices through competitive tendering among WHO-prequalified manufacturers—a mechanism structurally similar to NCDP—incorporating GDF benchmark pricing could provide an evidence-based floor for negotiations and accelerate convergence toward international price levels.

Fifth, institutionalize regular market structure monitoring within the NCDP evaluation framework. Our findings indicate heterogeneous effects across drugs, hospital types, and outcomes that would not be detected through price-focused monitoring alone. Systematic tracking of HHI, manufacturer participation in procurement, and supplier concentration across hospital types would enable early identification of consolidation trends requiring policy adjustment.

Sixth, integrate procurement policy evaluation with clinical outcome surveillance. While our study documents substantial price reductions and market structure changes, we cannot assess downstream effects on treatment adherence, clinical outcomes, or drug resistance patterns. Future policy evaluation should correlate procurement data with patient-level outcomes, drug-resistance monitoring, and public health indicators to comprehensively assess NCDP’s impact on TB prevention and control.

Conclusion

This study provides the first comprehensive, hospital-stratified interrupted time series analysis of China’s National Centralized Drug Procurement (NCDP) policy on the tuberculosis (TB) drug market. Our findings indicate that NCDP implementation was associated with substantial and consistent price reductions across all included TB drugs and both hospital types, with cost per defined daily dose (DDDc) decreasing by 50–90% for high-cost agents such as linezolid (from approximately 800 Renminbi [RMB] to below 100 RMB) and moxifloxacin (from 99 RMB to 20 RMB). These expenditure reductions were robust across sensitivity analyses using alternative comparator specifications, confirming the policy’s effectiveness in improving TB drug affordability.

However, the policy’s effects on market structure exhibited notable heterogeneity that warrants careful consideration. For first-line drugs (isoniazid, ethambutol, pyrazinamide), NCDP implementation was associated with increased market concentration in hospital procurement: HHI rose significantly while manufacturer participation in procurement declined. This consolidation pattern suggests that aggressive price pressure from centralized tendering constrained suppliers of low-margin, essential medicines. In contrast, second-line drugs demonstrated expanded supplier participation and market de-concentration (decreased HHI) post-NCDP, with manufacturer counts increasing substantially and market concentration declining. This divergent pattern indicates that NCDP can achieve dual objectives—cost reduction and supplier diversity enhancement—when commercial viability is preserved, as hospitals’ autonomy in selecting among multiple winning suppliers translates manufacturer entry into actual procurement diversification.

The hospital-stratified analysis revealed important institutional heterogeneity. TB-specialized hospitals operated with substantially higher baseline market concentration (HHI 0.42–0.94 for first-line drugs, 0.31–0.85 for second-line drugs) and fewer active suppliers (1–4 first-line drug manufacturers, 1–11 s-line drug manufacturers) compared to general hospitals (HHI 0.17–0.26 first-line drugs, 0.07–0.63 s-line drugs; 11–27 and 1–132 manufacturers respectively), reflecting their specialized patient populations and procurement patterns within China’s vertical disease control structure. While NCDP expanded supplier participation for second-line drugs in both hospital types, the absolute supply base in specialized hospitals remains limited, creating heightened vulnerability to supply disruptions for medicines critical to drug-resistant TB treatment.

Based on these findings, we recommend differentiated policy approaches: First, multi-winner tender designs should be implemented for first-line TB drugs to preserve supplier diversity and prevent excessive concentration in essential medicine markets. Second, targeted supply security mechanisms—including strategic stockpiles, supplier qualification programs, and contingency procurement arrangements—should be prioritized for TB-specialized hospitals. Third, volume-price guarantee mechanisms should be maintained and extended to newer anti-TB agents to encourage market participation while ensuring affordability. Fourth, regular monitoring of HHI, manufacturer counts, and supplier concentration across hospital types should be institutionalized within the NCDP evaluation framework to enable early detection of concerning market trends.
