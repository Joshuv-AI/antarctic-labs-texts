A longitudinal study of early postoperative symptom cluster trajectories in patients with gynecologic malignancies
Authors: Liu Jiarui, Li Xia, Wang Zhe, Zhang Yan, Chang Huixia, Yan Hong, Yan Shurong, Liu Mengzhen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore the dynamic evolution of symptom clusters in patients with gynecologic malignancies during the early postoperative period and identify key transition points and influencing factors, providing evidence for precision symptom management in clinical nursing.MethodsA longitudinal study was conducted among 324 patients using the MDASI-PeriOp-GYN on postoperative days 1 (T1), 5 (T2), and 7 (T3). Exploratory factor analysis identified symptom clusters at each time point, and growth mixture modeling (GMM) was applied to examine trajectory patterns. Latent profile analysis (LPA) and network analysis were performed at T2 to identify patient subgroups, influencing factors, and core symptoms.ResultsFive symptom clusters were identified: disease behavior, gastrointestinal, endocrine, neurological, and emotional. The emotional cluster, independent at T1 and T3, merged with the disease behavior cluster at T2. GMM indicated that all clusters declined from T1 to T2, followed by divergence after T2. LPA identified high- and low-symptom subgroups. Patients with ovarian cancer and those with KPS_1 were more likely to belong to the high-symptom group. Network analysis revealed “poor appetite” as the most central symptom at T2.ConclusionPostoperative day 5 (T2) represents a critical transition point in symptom evolution. Ovarian and KPS_1 are at higher risk for severe symptoms, and “poor appetite” plays a key driving role. Targeted assessment and intervention at T2 may reduce symptom burden and improve recovery outcomes in patients with gynecologic malignancies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04221-0.

Introduction

Gynecological malignancies, including cervical, ovarian, and endometrial, are among the most common tumors affecting the female reproductive system. According to the Global Cancer Statistics 2022 [1], these cancers account for a substantial proportion of the global disease burden in women. In China, their incidence and mortality have been steadily increasing, posing a major public health challenge [2]. Although advances in diagnosis and treatment have improved outcomes, surgery remains the primary treatment for early-stage disease [3]. However, patients often experience multiple concurrent physical and psychological symptoms after surgery, including pain, nausea, fatigue, low mood, and sleep disturbance. These symptoms can interact to form complex symptom clusters, which significantly hinder postoperative recovery [4].

Symptoms, defined as an individual’s subjective perception and response to changes in physiological, psychological, or social functioning [5], are inherently multidimensional [6]. In 2001, Dodd et al. introduced the concept of “symptom clusters,” describing the stable co-occurrence of two or more symptoms within the same patient [7]. Subsequent research has emphasized their synergistic effects [8], dynamic nature [9, 10], and amplified clinical impact [11]. Recognizing and managing symptom clusters has thus become a key focus in oncology nursing research [12–15], providing a systems-based framework for precision symptom management.

Nevertheless, most studies to date have been cross-sectional [16], limiting the ability to capture how symptom clusters evolve over time. The early postoperative phase—a critical window for recovery—has received little longitudinal attention, despite evidence showing that symptom experiences exhibit strong temporal dynamics [17] and individual variability [18]. Incorporating time as a key dimension is therefore essential to improve the accuracy of cluster identification and inform timely interventions.

Recent advances in longitudinal modeling, such as Growth Mixture Modeling (GMM), enable the identification of heterogeneous symptom trajectories and provide new insights into their dynamic evolution [19]. Building on theoretical frameworks such as the Symptom Experience Model [20] and the Symptom Management Dynamic Model [18], this study applied a longitudinal design to examine symptom cluster trajectories in patients with gynecologic malignancies. Data were collected during the early postoperative period, specifically on postoperative days 1, 5, and 7. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was used to identify the structure and stability of symptom clusters at each time point. Growth Mixture Modeling (GMM) was then applied to delineate their trajectory patterns. Guided by these results, we identified postoperative day 5 (T2) as a potential turning point. Latent profile analysis (LPA) and symptom network analysis were subsequently conducted to classify patient subgroups and identify core symptoms.

Drawing on prior evidence of a “biphasic” recovery pattern, we hypothesized that postoperative day 5 (T2) marks the lowest symptom burden and serves as a key transition point in symptom cluster evolution. This study aims to elucidate the dynamic trajectories of symptom clusters, identify high-risk subgroups, and provide evidence-based guidance for precision symptom management in gynecologic oncology.

Materials and methods

Participants

The data for this study were derived from a previously published study by Zhang et al. [4], which focused on the translation and validation of the Perioperative Module of the Anderson Symptom Assessment Scale. This was a longitudinal study that used a convenience sampling method to recruit 324 patients from a tertiary cancer hospital between July and October 2019. Inclusion criteria were as follows:(1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) a confirmed diagnosis of cervical, ovarian, or endometrial cancer; (3) provision of informed consent; (4) scheduled for surgery; and (5) sufficient language and reading ability. Exclusion criteria included: (1) presence of psychiatric disorders; (2) concurrent malignancies at other sites; (3) severe complications; and (4) participation in other clinical trials that could potentially influence this study. This included trials involving symptom-controlling medications. This study adopted the same inclusion and exclusion criteria. This study is a secondary analysis of pre-existing, openly accessible clinical data. The use of these data for secondary analysis was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University (Approval No. NO.KYYJ-2025-285). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national regulations.

Instruments

The Demographic and Clinical Information Questionnaire was self-designed by the researchers. It collected patients’ demographic characteristics (e.g., age, marital status, and education) and disease-related variables (e.g., cancer diagnosis and type of surgery).

The MDASI-PeriOp-GYN was used to collect symptom information from patients with gynecological malignancies preoperatively and on postoperative days 1, 5, and 7. For this study, we further incorporated a gynecologic malignancy–specific module, which included 9 symptom items, and a core cancer module, which included 13 symptom items. Symptom severity was assessed using these modules on postoperative days 1 (T1), 5 (T2), and 7 (T3).

Methods

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27.0), Mplus (version 8.3), and R (version 4.0.5).

Categorical data were summarized as counts and proportions. For continuous variables, those following a normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and assessed using independent-sample t tests. Variables with a skewed distribution were expressed as the median (P25, P75) and compared with the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test. Comparisons of categorical variables were conducted with the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on applicability.

To identify symptom clusters at different postoperative time points, EFA was conducted. To ensure the stability of the analysis, only symptoms with a frequency greater than 20% were included [21]. Principal component analysis with varimax rotation was used for factor extraction. The criteria for factor retention were: eigenvalue > 1, factor loadings > 0.4, and each factor including at least two items with significant loadings [22].

Based on the symptom clusters identified by EFA, GMM and LPA were conducted separately using Mplus. GMM was employed to identify longitudinal trajectory classes of symptom clusters, whereas LPA was used to detect cross-sectional latent profiles. For both models, we started with a single class and gradually increased the number of classes. Model fit was evaluated using unified criteria. Information criteria included AIC, BIC and aBIC; lower values indicate better fit. Among them, BIC was used as the primary decision criterion [23]. We applied the LMR and BLRT tests. A P < 0.05 indicated that the K-class model fitted significantly better than the K–1-class model [24]. Classification accuracy was assessed with entropy (0–1); higher values indicate better accuracy. Entropy ≥ 0.8 was considered excellent (> 90% correct classification) [25]. Class feasibility was ensured by requiring that the estimated probability of each latent class exceed 5%. In summary, model selection followed the decision hierarchy of prioritizing BIC minimization, requiring significant LMR and BLRT results, preferring entropy ≥ 0.80, and rejecting solutions with latent classes representing < 5% of the sample. After selecting the optimal model, we assigned each participant to the trajectory or profile class with the highest posterior probability.

After classifying participants using LPA, univariate analyses were conducted to screen for variables associated with high- and low-symptom burden groups, followed by logistic regression to identify independent predictors.

In addition, network analysis was conducted on the symptom set at T2 using R. Data were imported via the readxl package, and symptoms were categorized into four groups: disease behavior, gastrointestinal, endocrine and neurological. We constructed an undirected weighted network with the estimateNetwork() function in bootnet. The EBICglasso method and Spearman correlation coefficients were combined for estimation. In the network, nodes represented symptoms, and the thickness of edges reflected the strength of the relationships between symptoms. Centrality indices were calculated to assess the importance of each symptom within the network, including strength, closeness, and betweenness [26]. Network stability was evaluated by bootstrap. A correlation stability coefficient (CS) > 0.25 was deemed acceptable, and CS > 0.5 indicated good stability [27]. All statistical tests considered a P < 0.05 as indicative of statistically significant differences.

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 324 patients were included, with ages ranging from 22 to 75 years (mean ± SD: 51.95 ± 9.79 years). Of these, 316 were Han Chinese and 8 were from ethnic minority groups. Detailed information is presented in Additional file 1.

Trajectory classes of postoperative symptom clusters in patients with gynecologic malignancies

The emotional symptom cluster had merged into the disease-related behavior cluster at T2 and was therefore excluded from this analysis. For each symptom cluster, GMM was fitted based on patients` postoperative symptom severity scores. The number of candidate classes varied by cluster. We fitted 1–3-class models for the disease-behavior and endocrine symptom clusters, and 1–4-class models for the gastrointestinal and neurological symptom clusters. Model selection considered multiple indicators, including BIC, BLRT, entropy, and class sample sizes. The results indicated that a two-class model was optimal for the disease behavior (BLRT < 0.05) and endocrine symptom clusters (LMR-LRT < 0.05). For the gastrointestinal symptom cluster, some subgroups had < 5% of the sample. Consequently, a one-class model was selected. Characteristics of postoperative symptom cluster trajectories in patients with gynecologic malignancies

For each symptom cluster, latent classes were defined using T1 as the reference point. Since all classes showed a declining trend from T1 to T2, class labels were primarily based on the T2–T3 trajectories. In the disease behavior symptom cluster, C1 (13.7%) exhibited a marked increase from T2 to T3. It was labeled the “initial decline–subsequent rise” group. C2 (86.3%) showed a more gradual increase and was labeled the “initial decline–gradual rise” group (Fig. 1a). The gastrointestinal symptom cluster contained only one class. This class was labeled the “initial decline–subsequent rise” group (Fig. 1b). In the endocrine symptom cluster, C1 (35.2%) increased from T2 to T3 and was labeled the “initial decline–subsequent rise” group. In contrast, C2 (64.8%) continued to decrease and was labeled the “continuous decline” group (Fig. 1c). The neurological symptom cluster displayed three trajectories. C1 (28.5%) was labeled “initial decline–gradual decline.” C2 (15.2%) was labeled “initial decline–subsequent rise,” and C3 (56.3%) was labeled “rapid initial decline–gradual decline” (Fig. 1d).

Identification of latent classes of symptom clusters at T2 in patients with gynecologic malignancies

Based on the above analysis, the trajectories of symptom cluster subgroups showed apparent differentiation after T2. To statistically examine whether T2 represented a turning point in postoperative symptom evolution, linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were used. The results revealed a significant main effect of time across all symptom clusters (p < 0.001), and pairwise comparisons indicated that symptom intensity generally reached its lowest level at T2, followed by partial rebound or stabilization from T2 to T3. Detailed information is presented in Additional file 3. Therefore, symptom severity data at T2 were used for latent profile analysis (LPA) to further explore potential symptom patterns in postoperative patients.

Models with one to four classes were fitted in this study. The results showed that as the number of classes increased, AIC, BIC, and aBIC values gradually decreased, indicating improved model fit. In terms of statistical indicators, the LMR and BLRT for the two-, three-, and four-class models were all significant (P < 0.05), suggesting that models with additional classes provided a significantly better fit than their preceding models. Regarding classification accuracy, the two-class model had the highest entropy (Entropy = 0.964). The class proportions were relatively balanced (67.9% and 32.1%). Additionally, the line plot displayed clear and distinguishable symptom patterns. We evaluated model fit, classification precision, and clinical interpretability. On this basis, the two-class model was regarded as optimal. Network analysis of T2 symptom set in patients with gynecologic malignancies

Based on the aforementioned findings, a symptom network analysis was conducted to further identify core symptoms at T2. Considering that the sample sizes of the high- and low-symptom groups were insufficient for stable network estimation, the two groups were combined for analysis (Fig. 4a). Centrality analysis indicated that “poor appetite” had the highest centrality in the network (Fig. 4b). The CS coefficient was 0.596, suggesting good network stability. Radar plots were subsequently generated for the two groups separately. These plots showed that “poor appetite” scored highest in the high-symptom group (Fig. 4c). Further comparison using violin plots revealed that patients in the high-symptom group had significantly higher “poor appetite” scores than those in the low-symptom group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4d). The results from multiple analytical approaches were consistent. This indicates that “poor appetite” is the core symptom for high-symptom patients at T2.

Discussion

Dynamics and clinical significance of symptom clusters

This study identified five symptom clusters in postoperative patients with gynecologic malignancies: disease behavior, gastrointestinal, endocrine, neurological, and emotional clusters. The composition of these clusters changed dynamically over time. Notably, the emotional symptom cluster appeared independently at T1 and T3 but merged into the disease behavior cluster at T2. This suggests that emotional distress in the mid-postoperative period may interact with somatic symptoms. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that women with higher levels of sadness and depression often experience greater symptom burden [28] and that fatigue and depression can exacerbate each other bidirectionally [29]. Therefore, nurses should pay special attention to emotional distress at T2, which may be masked when symptom clusters merge.

To address this, structured psychological screening should be incorporated into routine postoperative assessments at T2. Brief and validated instruments, such as the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) or the emotional items of the MDASI, can help identify patients at risk for anxiety or depression. Patients who screen positive should receive timely, targeted support through an integrated approach that combines emotional and physical care.

Evidence-based interventions, including mindfulness-based stress reduction [30] and acceptance and commitment therapy [31], can help alleviate sadness and depression. In addition, aerobic exercise, respiratory muscle training, balance-focused exercises, and Tai Chi may reduce fatigue and promote recovery [32]. A comprehensive biopsychosocial intervention model may prevent escalation of hidden emotional distress, reduce overall symptom burden, and facilitate smoother postoperative recovery.

T2 as a turning point of symptom trajectories and identification of high-risk populations

In the introduction, we hypothesized that postoperative day 5 (T2) might represent a symptom nadir and indicate trajectory divergence. The results support this hypothesis. GMM and LMM analyses showed that symptom clusters reached their lowest point at T2 before diverging: some continued to decline, while others rebounded. This pattern aligns with the “biphasic pattern” reported by Meyer et al. in patients with gynecologic malignancies [33]. Our study further pinpointed T2 as a critical turning point, representing not only transient symptom relief but also a key phase for predicting subsequent symptom rebound.

LPA analysis revealed that patients with ovarian cancer and those with KPS_1 experienced a higher symptom burden. These findings are consistent with previous reports: Vistad et al. found that declines in self-rated functional status among ovarian cancer patients were closely associated with greater symptom distress [34], and Portenoy et al. reported a positive correlation between KPS scores and symptom count [35]. Together, these results indicate that patients with ovarian cancer and impaired functional status constitute a high-risk population for symptom rebound after T2.

Although univariate analysis indicated that cancer stage, prior treatment and type of surgery were significantly associated with symptom burden, these variables were not retained in the multivariate model. This may reflect overlapping effects with disease type or functional status, or limited variability within the current sample. Nevertheless, disease severity and surgical complexity may still contribute to symptom heterogeneity and postoperative symptom rebound. Future studies with larger, multicenter samples are needed to confirm these potential influences.

Operationally, T2 should be incorporated as a structured assessment point in perioperative nursing pathways. Nurses should perform focused symptom reviews at this stage, emphasizing appetite, fatigue, and sleep quality, along with functional assessment using KPS. High-risk patients—especially those with ovarian cancer or KPS_1—should receive intensified follow-up, which may include nutritional counseling, early mobilization guidance, and individualized symptom self-monitoring.

Identification and clinical significance of the core symptom “poor appetite” at T2

Given that T2 represents the turning point in postoperative symptom trajectories, we conducted a symptom network analysis at this time point. The analysis revealed that “poor appetite” had the highest centrality, highlighting its core role within the overall symptom network. This finding aligns with previous symptom network studies in patients with gynecologic malignancies [36]. Further subgroup analysis showed that patients in the high-symptom group had significantly higher “poor appetite” scores than those in the low-symptom group. Our study also found that ovarian cancer patients were more likely to belong to the high-symptom group. Previous research has reported that ovarian patients often experience poor appetite, which can lead to malnutrition, exacerbate symptom burden [37], and prolong postoperative hospital stay [38]. Collectively, these findings suggest that “poor appetite” functions as a core symptom in the high-symptom group at T2 and acts as a pivotal driver of overall symptom burden and postoperative recovery.

In clinical practice, early identification and management of “poor appetite” are crucial. Individualized nutritional support—including nutritional risk screening, dietary adjustments, and enteral or parenteral supplementation—can improve patients’ energy intake and nutritional status [39]. Pharmacological interventions, such as olanzapine or glucocorticoids, can alleviate cancer-related anorexia and associated symptoms [40]. Non-pharmacological interventions, including acupoint PC6 stimulation [41], aromatherapy [42] and ginger supplementation [43], have demonstrated efficacy. To strengthen clinical applicability, network-derived core symptoms such as “poor appetite” can be incorporated into routine perioperative nursing assessment. For example, nurses may conduct daily appetite scoring to detect early deterioration and initiate timely nutritional evaluation or supportive interventions as needed. Integrating core symptoms into postoperative monitoring pathways can support proactive symptom management, reduce overall symptom burden, and facilitate enhanced recovery.

Taken together, a multidimensional nursing strategy that integrates nutritional support, pharmacological, and non-pharmacological interventions can improve appetite, reduce overall symptom burden, and promote postoperative recovery, thereby enhancing patients’ quality of life.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. It was conducted in a single tertiary hospital, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Symptom data were self-reported, possibly introducing recall bias. Although variables such as disease stage, surgical type, anesthesia duration, and comorbidities were included in preliminary analyses, they were not retained as independent predictors in the final models. This may be due to multicollinearity or overlapping effects with strongly correlated clinical variables such as diagnosis and KPS. Nonetheless, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be excluded. The dataset was complete with no missing values, but future studies should assess missing data mechanisms and perform sensitivity analyses to ensure model robustness. Moreover, this study focused only on the postoperative period; future research should explore whether preoperative interventions—such as physical preparation, nutrition, and psychological support—could influence postoperative symptom trajectories.

Conclusions

This longitudinal study identified T2 (postoperative day 5) as a critical time point for symptom rebound and heterogeneity in patients with gynecologic malignancies. Patients with ovarian and KPS_1 were at higher risk for elevated symptom burden, while “poor appetite” played a central role within the symptom network at T2. These findings provide evidence-based guidance for time-specific nursing assessments and targeted symptom interventions.

Building upon these results, our team will conduct interventional trials targeting “poor appetite” at T2 to evaluate their impact on postoperative recovery. In addition, future research should investigate whether preoperative interventions—such as physical conditioning, nutritional optimization, patient education, and psychological support—can reduce the intensity of postoperative symptom clusters. Integrating preoperative and postoperative care may enhance surgical tolerance, accelerate recovery, and improve patients’ overall quality of life.
