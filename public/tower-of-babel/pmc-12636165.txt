Editorial stances on large Language models in leading nursing publications: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Xing Zhou, Guidi Su, Limei Zhu, Yaoping Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rapid integration of large language models (LLMs) into scholarly publishing has created an urgent need for clear standards. This study aims to comprehensively analyze the editorial stances of leading nursing publications regarding the use of LLMs in manuscript preparation and peer assessment.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of the top 50 nursing publications according to their journal impact factor. Each publication’s website was systematically evaluated for directives concerning LLM use in authorship, content generation, image creation, and peer assessment. Journal metrics were also extracted to assess any correlation with policy adoption.ResultsOf the 50 publications, 35 (70%) had explicit LLM-related directives. A strong point of agreement permits the use of LLMs for content generation (97%) but prohibits LLM authorship (94%). However, a significant divergence was found regarding AI-generated images, with 52% of publications prohibiting their use. Guidance on LLM use in peer assessment was also inconsistent, with 49% of publications prohibiting it. Policy adoption varied significantly by publisher (ranging from 20% to 100%). No statistical association was found between policy existence and journal impact metrics (p > 0.05).ConclusionsLeading nursing publications exhibit a fractured landscape on LLM use. While foundational agreement exists on authorship and content generation, critical areas like image creation and peer assessment lack consistent standards. This ambiguity underscores the need for a more unified, transparent framework to guide ethical and responsible LLM integration in nursing scholarship.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04102-9.

Introduction

The advent of large language models (LLMs) such as Chat Generative Pre-trained Transformer (ChatGPT) has profoundly reshaped the landscape of scholarly communication [1]. These generative tools offer significant advantages, including enhancing writing efficiency, overcoming language barriers for non-native English speakers, and assisting in the summarization of complex information [1, 2]. A survey of over 1,600 researchers revealed that LLMs are already being widely used to assist with brainstorming, writing, and literature searches [3]. However, this rapid integration is not without peril. Concerns regarding the potential for generating inaccurate or fabricated information, the risk of plagiarism, and fundamental questions about academic integrity and originality have prompted urgent discussions across the scientific community [4, 5].

Large language models are a sophisticated form of artificial intelligence (AI) built upon deep learning principles. Typically, they utilize a transformer architecture, which enables them to process and understand complex relationships within sequential data, such as text [6]. These models are pre-trained on vast quantities of data, which often encompassing billions of documents from the internet, books, and scholarly articles, allowing them to learn intricate patterns, grammar, and contextual nuances of human language [7]. Their core function is to generate human-like text in response to user queries or “prompts”. The public release of OpenAI’s ChatGPT in late 2022, a prominent example of an LLM-powered chatbot, attracted substantial global attention and accelerated the integration of these tools into various professional domains, including healthcare and nursing [6, 8].

The rapid proliferation of LLMs has spurred a significant body of academic literature across healthcare disciplines. Broad scoping reviews have mapped the potential applications, challenges, and future directions of LLMs in medicine and healthcare integration [9, 10]. Within nursing, the response has been similarly vigorous, with numerous scoping and systematic reviews synthesizing the current evidence. A substantial portion of this emerging literature has focused on the role of LLMs in nursing education, exploring their potential as tools for curriculum development, simulating patient encounters, and providing personalized student feedback [11–13]. These studies also highlight concurrent challenges, such as the risks to academic integrity and the potential for over-reliance on technology, which may hinder the development of critical thinking skills [12, 13].

While these reviews provide a crucial overview of opinions and applications, a systematic analysis of the official editorial directives being established by scholarly publications themselves, which act as the gatekeepers of nursing science, is currently lacking. This gap is of critical importance, as the formal stances adopted by leading journals directly influence the standards of scholarly conduct and the integrity of scientific communication. The absence of a clear benchmark of these directives creates significant ambiguity for the entire nursing academic community [14]. Authors operate without clear guidance on permissible use and disclosure, while reviewers face uncertainty regarding the ethical use of these tools in their assessments, a situation fraught with confidentiality and integrity risks [15]. Ultimately, the directives from these influential publications will set the precedent for transparency and accountability in nursing scholarship for years to come, demanding robust policy development and inclusive governance to ensure ethical integration [16]. Understanding the current landscape of these formal norms, including the extent of their adoption, their points of agreement, and their areas of divergence, is therefore an essential and timely endeavor.

Without a systematic map of the current policy landscape, authors risk violating guidelines unknowingly, reviewers are left without clear ethical frameworks for their assessments, and the nursing discipline as a whole misses a critical opportunity to proactively shape the responsible integration of AI. This study was designed to fill this pressing knowledge gap.

Therefore, this study was designed to answer the following research questions:

Methods

This study employed a descriptive cross-sectional design. This methodological approach is optimal for capturing a “snapshot” of the prevalence, characteristics, and distribution of specific phenomena within a defined population at a single point in time [17]. In this investigation, the study sample consisted of the top 50 nursing publications, and the phenomenon under study was the existence and nature of their editorial directives concerning LLMs. The data collection was conducted on a specific date to ensure a standardized, cross-sectional view of the landscape. Our overall methodological framework was also informed by established practices from comparable investigations into scholarly publication directives [18–22]. Approval from an Institutional Review Board was not required for this study, as it did not involve human or patient data and was based exclusively on publicly accessible information.

An initial list of publications was identified from the “Nursing” category in the 2023 edition of Clarivate’s Journal Citation Reports (JCRs). Inclusion criteria were: (1) indexed in the Science Citation Index Expanded or Social Sciences Citation Index; and (2) ranked within the top 50 by 2023 Journal Impact Factor (JIF). The top 50 journals were selected for this analysis because, as the most influential publications in the field, their editorial stances often serve as benchmarks that establish scholarly norms and influence practices across the wider academic community.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) publications whose scope was not exclusively dedicated to nursing science, practice, or education, such as interdisciplinary publications that also publish non-nursing research; and (2) publications without publicly accessible guide for authors in English. The final list of publications for analysis was determined through a rigorous, multi-step process. Initial data extraction was conducted independently by two authors (Xing Zhou and Guidi Su) using a standardized data collection form to ensure consistency. This form included fields for all variables presented in Table 1, such as policies on authorship, textual content, visuals, and peer assessment. Following the independent extraction, the two authors cross-verified each other’s data to check for accuracy. Any discrepancies in the extracted data or disagreements in policy interpretation were resolved through discussion with a third author (Limei Zhu), who served as an adjudicator to reach a final decision. This systematic approach was designed to minimize bias and ensure the reliability of the collected information. A complete list of the 50 journals included in the analysis, along with direct web links to their respective editorial policy pages, is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Two researchers independently evaluated each publication’s website between February 4, 2025, and February 7, 2025. This specific and narrow timeframe was chosen to ensure a standardized, cross-sectional view of the landscape, capturing the journals’ policies at a single, consistent point in time. The process for each journal involved a two-stage approach to ensure a thorough search for relevant directives. Initially, researchers manually navigated to and meticulously reviewed key sections of the website where such directives are typically located, including pages titled “Instructions for Authors”, “Guide for Authors”, “Editorial Policies”, and “Publishing Ethics”.

Following this manual review, to ensure comprehensive coverage and locate directives that might be housed elsewhere on the site, researchers utilized the website’s internal search function, where available. We used the following keywords for this search: artificial intelligence, AI, large language model, LLM, ChatGPT, generative AI, assist, writing, and peer review. If this systematic search yielded no relevant results, the publication was classified as “without specific directives on LLM use”.

For each publication, we extracted general characteristics, including the publication title and publisher. To provide a multi-dimensional perspective on scholarly impact, a range of journal metrics were collected, including CiteScore, Source Normalized Impact per Paper (SNIP), SCImago Journal Rank (SJR), Journal Impact Factor (JIF), and Journal Citation Indicator (JCI). These were extracted from the Scopus and Web of Science (WoS) databases. The primary purpose for collecting these metrics was to test for a potential association between a journal’s scholarly impact and its likelihood of having adopted a directive on LLM use, addressing one of our primary research questions. Specifically, we hypothesized that publications with higher impact metrics (e.g., JIF, CiteScore) would be more likely to have established explicit LLM policies compared to those without such policies.

We then documented the specifics of each publication’s LLM directives, focusing on four key areas: (1) LLM attribution in authorship; (2) LLM utilization for textual content; (3) incorporation of LLM-created visuals; and (4) LLM engagement in peer assessment. This allowed for a detailed and standardized comparison of stances across all publications included in the study.

We performed the analysis using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) (Version 26.0). We reported qualitative data as frequencies and percentages and presented quantitative data as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). We assessed the normality of quantitative data using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. We used the Mann-Whitney U test to compare non-parametric quantitative data between groups. We also used the Chi-square test to compare qualitative data. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Our final analysis included the top 50 nursing publications based on the 2023 JCR rankings. Of these, we found that 35 (70%) had an explicit policy regarding the use of LLM in manuscript preparation. A detailed breakdown of the LLM policy information for each of the 50 publications is presented in Table 1.

Among the 35 publications with an LLM policy, we observed variations in the scope and specificity of the guides. Regarding authorship, the guidance was largely consistent. Of the 35 publications, 33 (94%) explicitly prohibited the inclusion of LLM tools as authors, while the remaining 2 did not address this specific point. This prohibition was often justified in the directives by stating that authorship requires accountability for the work, a responsibility that non-human entities cannot assume. A total of 34 publications (97% of those with a policy) permitted the use of LLM for drafting or editing text. However, this permission was almost universally contingent upon a mandatory disclosure, requiring authors to specify in the manuscript which LLM was used and for what purpose.

Policies concerning LLM-generated images were less common. Twenty-three of the 35 publications (66%) provided guidance on this topic. Among these, there was a notable split: 12 publications (52%) prohibited LLM-generated images, while 11 (48%) permitted their use. Journals prohibiting AI-generated images typically cited concerns over copyright and the potential for data manipulation, whereas those permitting them required clear disclosure and verification of originality. Guidance on the use of LLM in the peer review process was also varied. Of the 35 publications with LLM policies, 17 (49%) explicitly prohibited reviewers from using LLM tools to evaluate manuscripts. The primary rationale provided for this prohibition was the critical need to maintain the confidentiality of unpublished manuscripts, as uploading content to external LLM platforms constitutes a breach of confidentiality. The other 18 publications (51%) did not have an available policy on this matter.

The 50 publications included in our study were published by seven main publishers. Elsevier was the most prominent, publishing 20 (40%) of the publications, followed by Wiley (30%), Lippincott Williams & Wilkins (16%), and Sage (8%). We observed significant variation in LLM policy adoption rates across the four publishers with more than one publication in the study. Sage had implemented an LLM policy across all of its publications (100%), and Elsevier demonstrated a similarly high adoption rate of 95%. In contrast, Lippincott Williams & Wilkins had an adoption rate of 75%, while Wiley had the lowest rate at 20%.

We compared journal metrics between publications with and without LLM-use policies to determine if there was any association between policy adoption and journal impact. The analysis revealed no statistically significant differences across any of the metrics examined. The median CiteScore for publications with AI policies was 4.10 (IQR: 3.70–5.90), compared to 5.90 (IQR: 4.00-7.20) for those without (P = 0.18). Similarly, no significant differences were found for SNIP (P = 0.35), SJR (P = 0.34), JIF (P = 0.80), or JCI (P = 0.12). These results are summarized in Table 2.

Discussion

Our analysis of the top 50 nursing publications reveals a “fractured landscape” regarding the use of large language models in scholarly publishing. While a substantial majority (70%) of leading publications have established explicit directives, the landscape is characterized by areas of strong agreement alongside significant divergence and silence on critical issues. This inconsistency creates a fragmented and unpredictable landscape for authors, reviewers, and editors navigating this new technological era.

The most consistent finding in our study is the near-universal prohibition of LLM authorship (94%), which is mirrored by the widespread permission (97%) for LLMs to assist in content generation. This dual-sided agreement largely aligns with guidance from major ethics bodies like ICMJE and COPE, which underscore that authorship entails accountability and responsibility, qualities that a non-human entity cannot possess [21, 23]. It reflects a pragmatic view within the nursing academic community: LLMs are accepted as sophisticated writing aids, analogous to grammar checkers or statistical software, but are firmly denied the status of intellectual contributors. This distinction is crucial for maintaining the principle of human accountability for the entire scholarly work [5].

However, this widespread agreement fractures when moving to more complex applications. The stark 52% vs. 48% split on the permissibility of LLM-generated images highlights a major point of contention. This divergence likely reflects a tension between the potential of LLMs to create high-quality, illustrative figures and deep-seated concerns about data fabrication, copyright, and the verifiability of visual information [24]. An even more concerning gap exists in the realm of peer assessment. The fact that nearly half of publications with policies prohibit LLM use by reviewers, while the other half remain silent, leaves a critical part of the scholarly validation process in a state of uncertainty. This silence is particularly problematic given the profound confidentiality and integrity risks associated with uploading manuscript data to external LLM platforms [15].

Beyond the immediate inconsistencies our data reveal, our findings resonate with a broader and more complex ethical discussion concerning the integration of LLMs into healthcare. The concerns over confidentiality in peer review, which our results highlight, are a microcosm of the larger challenge of data privacy and security [20]. As LLMs are often trained on vast, opaque datasets and operate on third-party servers, their use in any scholarly or clinical context poses risks to sensitive information, a challenge repeatedly emphasized in recent literature [20, 25]. Furthermore, a significant blind spot in the directives we analyzed is the risk of perpetuating or even amplifying algorithmic bias [26]. It is well-documented that LLMs trained on historical data can reproduce and scale existing societal biases, potentially exacerbating health disparities in nursing care if not carefully mitigated [7, 26]. The issue of accountability, which journals address superficially by prohibiting LLM authorship, becomes far more complex in practice. The “black box” nature of these models raises profound questions about whether liability rests with the developer, the institution, or the individual nurse when an AI-assisted decision leads to an adverse outcome [16]. Finally, and perhaps most centrally for the nursing profession, there is a nascent but critical concern that over-reliance on these tools could lead to the deskilling of core scholarly and clinical competencies, and a dehumanization of care, thereby eroding the humanistic foundations of nursing [14, 16].

Our findings also demonstrate that the adoption of these directives is not uniform but is significantly influenced by the publisher. The vast difference in adoption rates between publishers like Sage (100%) and Wiley (20%) strongly suggests that these are top-down, corporate-level decisions rather than initiatives from individual publication editorial boards. This pattern, where publication directives on LLM use show notable variations in scope, specificity, and enforcement and appear more influenced by the publisher than by individual publication characteristics like prestige or field, has been observed in other medical disciplines as well [18, 22]. This indicates that publishers are currently the primary drivers shaping the normative framework for LLM use, leading to a fragmented, rather than unified, landscape.

Interestingly, we found no statistical association between the existence of an LLM policy and any of the journal impact metrics analyzed. This important negative finding challenges the assumption that higher-impact publications are necessarily more proactive in establishing ethical guardrails for emerging technologies. Several factors may explain this. First, as a recent phenomenon, the adoption of LLM directives may not yet have translated into discernible effects on submission behavior or citation patterns [27]. Second, some high-impact publications might be intentionally adopting a cautious “wait-and-see” approach, preferring to observe how community norms evolve before codifying strict rules. Third, the homogeneity of our sample, which exclusively included top-ranked publications, might obscure potential differences. It is plausible that if a broader range of publications, including those with lower impact factors, were included, a correlation might emerge. Finally, as some studies on reporting guides have shown, policy adoption is not always correlated with a publication’s prestige but can be driven by other factors, such as the publisher’s philosophy or the specific culture of an editorial board [18, 28]. This lack of correlation underscores that a publication’s impact factor is not a reliable proxy for its leadership in establishing ethical frameworks for LLMs.

This study has several limitations. First, as a cross-sectional analysis, it provides a snapshot of a rapidly evolving landscape; publication directives are likely to change. Our study was also limited to the top 50 nursing publications by JIF, and our findings may not be generalizable to publications with lower impact factors or in different disciplines. Finally, our keyword-based search strategy, though comprehensive, may have missed directives that used non-standard terminology.

Conclusion

The landscape of LLM directives in leading nursing publications is characterized by significant divergence alongside areas of strong agreement. While there is strong, near-universal agreement on prohibiting LLM authorship and permitting text-editing assistance, significant divergence and ambiguity exist in critical domains such as the use of LLM-generated images and the role of LLMs in peer assessment. This inconsistency, largely driven by publisher-level decisions rather than a unified community effort, creates an uncertain environment for scholars. The lack of correlation between policy adoption and publication impact suggests the field is still in the early stages of adaptation. There is an urgent need for the nursing academic community, in collaboration with publishers and ethics bodies, to develop and endorse clear, comprehensive, and standardized norms to ensure scholarly integrity and effectively guide authors and reviewers in this new era of generative technology.
