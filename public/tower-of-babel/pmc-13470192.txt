Unravelling the genomic potential of sponge-associated Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 from Indonesia for mannooligosaccharide production
Authors: Nanik Rahmani, Annisyia Zarina Putri, Hana Nadhifah, Lina Herliana, Rike Rachmayati, Eva Agustriana, Siti Eka Yulianti, Nuryati, Elvi Yetti, Akhirta Atikana, Anisa Fitri Rahayu, Emma Rochima, Shanti Ratnakomala, Puspita Lisdiyanti, Yopi, Bambang Prasetya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research aims to show the promising capacity of Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 to produce high β-mannanase enzymes and generate mannooligosaccharide (MOS) such as mannobiose, mannotriose, mannotetraose and mannopentaose when exposed to mannan polymers. Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 was isolated from the sponge (Rhabdastrella globostellata) Put4 obtained from the marine waters of Putus Island in Bitung, North Sulawesi, Indonesia. The characterization results showed that the peak enzyme activity was achieved at 50 mM sodium acetate, 6.0 pH, and 60 °C temperature on the seventh day of production with a value of 155.77 ± 3.21 U/mL. The SDS-PAGE and zymograms also showed that the size of the enzyme molecule was approximately ±34.8–49.1 kDa. Moreover, whole-genome sequencing was conducted to identify the genetic basis of MOS-synthesizing capabilities in the selected strain, followed by functional annotation of genes encoding mannan degradation and associated functions. The results showed an 8,248,862 Mb complete draft genome of the strain which comprised 111 predicted gene models. Gene annotation also provided important information about the location and function of protein-encoding genes. A total of 6 mannan degradation-related genes encoding mannanase-related metabolism were identified and the three-dimensional structures were predicted using AlphaFold 3. This characterization and modeling further enhanced the bioprospecting and development of this strain which exhibited efficient mannose metabolism. The results showed Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 as a promising microorganism for the future bioproduction of MOS which were discovered to have the capability of serving as a potential prebiotic substance to enhance digestion and promote health.Keywords: Bioprospecting, Mannanase, Mannooligosaccharide, Marine actinomycetes, Whole-genome sequencing

Introduction

Oligosaccharides are very important to several biological systems and have attracted the attention of significant research and development over the last twenty years due to certain substantial physiological features considered advantageous to human health.1, 2 For example, the enzymatic process for food and feed industries as reported by Chauhan et al.3 Oligosaccharides are most beneficial when generated enzymatically through polysaccharide breakdown endo-enzymes. Among all, mannooligosaccharide (MOS) are a novel class of oligosaccharide and classified as emerging ones compared to the other oligosaccharides, i.e. fructooligosaccharides (FOS), galactooligosaccharides (GOS), and xylooligosaccharides (XOS).4

Enzyme β-Mannanase (1,4-β-D-mannan mannohydrolase, EC 3.2.1.78) hydrolyze 1,4-manno-glycosidic bonds and releasing MOS with various types of polymerizations.3 The MOS are able to resist digestion and exhibits significant physiological properties such as prebiotics and dietary fiber.5 It has also been found to significantly impact gut flora due to the ability to promote bacterial growth and suppress pathogenic microorganisms. The attributes show that MOS is a valuable source of prebiotics.6

A bioprospecting and characterization method is needed on new marine actinomycetes that synthesize large amounts of β-mannanase to produce MOS. Identification of a novel microorganism with high β-mannanase production capacity is proposed as a good first step in improving hemicellulose hydrolysis to generate MOS. Therefore, this study aim for bioprospecting marine actinomycetes from Indonesian water, with purposes to determine their potential as producer of mannanase enzymes and MOS. Additionally, the study also investigate the genetic basis and capabilities for mannanase enzyme production in a selected sponge-associated actinomycetes, Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3, through whole-genome sequencing, followed by functional annotation of genes encoding the enzymes for potential MOS synthesis, and protein prediction using AlphaFold3.

Materials and methods

Sources of microorganisms, materials, and chemicals

This study tested a total of 21 actinomycetes (Supplementary material Table S1), derived from marine samples of Lembeh Strait, North Sulawesi, Indonesia, for their mannanolytic activity. These 21 isolates were deposited in the Indonesian Culture Collection (InaCC), National Research and Innovation Agency (NRIA). The sugar palm fruit samples of this study were obtained from local farmers in Tasikamalaya, Indonesia. The ivory nut and mannooligosaccharide (MOS) standards, including mannobiose (M2), mannotriose (M3), mannotetraose (M4), mannopentaose (M5), and mannohexaose (M6), were obtained from Megazyme (Wicklow, Ireland). Whereas standards for Locust Bean Gum (LBG) and Mannose (M1) were obtained from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Culture conditions of marine actinomycete for the bioprospecting of β-mannanase enzyme producer

The actinomycetes were precultures through in a glucose basal marine broth medium, consisting of 0.4% yeast extract, 0.1% malt extract, 0.4% d-glucose, and 3% Marine ART SF-1. The broth media was formulated in 100 mL distilled water. Single actinomycetes colony was transferred into 10 mL of basal marine broth medium and precultured under control conditions, incubated at 28 °C and 190 rpm for three days of incubation. Further culture procedure was implemented by transferring 1 mL of the preculture isolates into 10 mL LBG basal marine broth medium, containing 0.4% yeast extract, 1.0% malt extract, 3% (w/v) Marine ART SF-1, and 2% LBG. This culture was incubated at 28 °C and 190 rpm for seven days. Sampling was obtained by taking 1.5 mL of cultures on day 4 and day 7 of incubation. The samples were centrifuged for 20 min, at 12,000 rpm and temperature 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was transferred into a sterile 1.5 mL tube (Eppendorf) and kept at 4 °C until further use.

Measurement of β-mannanase activity

The activity of β-mannanase was determined using a previously published method.7 The supernatant from previous cultures (5 μL) was inoculated onto an LBG agar plate, incubated at 37 °C for 3 days. The LBG agar contains 0.5% LBG and 1.7% agar. During the incubation period, each plate was treated with 0.2% Congo red solution. The plates were subsequently washed three times using 1 M NaCl solution and 0.5% (v/v) acetic acid solution. Each washing procedure is followed by a 3-min of agitation. The β-mannanase activity was confirmed by formation of a clear zone surrounding each of the actinomycetes colony.

Whole-genome sequencing, library preparation, assembly, and annotation of Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3

Among Streptomyces isolates tested in this study, a sponge-associated Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 exhibited the highest mannanase activitiy and was therefore selected for further characterization. The genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted using the ZymoBIOMICS DNA kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The quality of genomic DNA was assessed prior to sequencing using multiple complementary approaches. DNA purity was evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, yielding A260/280 and A260/230 ratios of 1.80 and 1.35, respectively. DNA concentration was measured using the Qubit dsDNA HS assay, providing sufficient input DNA (>5 μg) for library preparation. DNA integrity was assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis and TapeStation analysis, revealing high-molecular-weight DNA (>40 kb) with a DNA Integrity Number (DIN) of 7.5, indicating high-quality DNA suitable for long-read sequencing. Detailed DNA quality assessment results are provided in Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Fig. S1. Sequencing was performed using an Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) PromethION 2 SOLO platform with an R10.4.1 flow cell (FLO-PRO114M) and the SQK-NBD114–24 library preparation kit. The NTC/a negative control was not included during library preparation and sequencing. Library preparation followed the manufacturer's protocol using high-molecular-weight genomic DNA. Sequencing runs were controlled using MinKNOW v24.02.10, and basecalling was performed using Dorado v7.3.9 in high-accuracy (HAC) mode.8, 9

Sequencing data analysis and construction of phylogenomic tree

Raw Nanopore reads were quality-filtered using Filtlong v0.2.1, and sequencing quality was assessed using NanoPlot v1.40.2.10 A minimum quality threshold of Q ≥ 10 was applied for read filtering. After filtering, 15,766 reads (∼300 Mb) were retained. The filtered dataset showed a mean read length of ∼19 kb, with an N50 of ∼21 kb and maximum read lengths exceeding 100 kb. The mean read quality score was Q16.6. Overall, the read length distribution was enriched for long reads (>10 kb), and the quality distribution indicated that the majority of reads exceeded Q15, supporting the suitability of the dataset for high-quality de novo genome assembly. De novo genome assembly was performed using Flye v2.8.3,11 followed by multiple rounds of polishing with Racon v1.5.012 and Medaka v1.7.2 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). Read mapping was conducted using Minimap2 v2.24,13 and assembly quality was evaluated using QUAST v5.0.214 and Qualimap v2.2.2.15 Genome annotation was performed using Prokka v1.14.62,16 and genome visualization was carried out using Proksee.17 Genome completeness and contamination were assessed using BUSCO v5.4.418 and CheckM v1.2.2.19 Phylogenomic analysis was conducted using the Type (Strain) Genome Server (TYGS),20 including genome-based distance calculations using GBDP and digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) estimation.21 Phylogenetic trees were constructed using FASTME22 and visualized using PhyD3.23 Detailed descriptions of software tools, parameters, and workflow are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Functional annotation and structural analysis of mannan-related proteins

Functional annotation of protein-coding sequences (7122 proteins) from the Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 genome was performed using the EggNOG-mapper server.24 Carbohydrate-Active enZymes (CAZy) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) identifiers were extracted from the annotated dataset.25, 26

Metabolic pathway reconstruction focused on mannose metabolism was conducted using the KEGG Mapper server.26 Five protein-coding genes associated with mannan metabolism were selected for further structural analysis. Structural similarity and three-dimensional (3D) models of selected proteins were analyzed using AlphaFold27 and compared with available structures in the Protein Data Bank (PDB).28 Predicted models were superimposed on experimental structures to assess structural similarity.

Characterization of β-mannanase enzyme derived from Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3

Cultivation and production of β-mannanase from Streptomyces BLC 17–3

The production of β-mannanase from marine actinomycetes in this study was conducted following previously published protocol.29, 30 A single colony of actinomycetes was pre-cultured using ISP2 broth medium for three days incubation at temperature 28 °C and 190 rpm. Afterwards, the preculture was transferred into 30 mL modified ISP2 broth medium containing 1% malt extract (w/v), 3% Marine ART SF-1 (w/v), 0.4% yeast extract (w/v), 0.4% d-glucose (w/v), and 2% LBG in 100 mL Erlenmeyer flasks. Fermentation was performed for seven days incubation, at temperature 28 °C and 190 rpm, in a rotary shaker (Bioshaker, BR-43FH, Taitec). Sampling was done after an interval of 24 h, in which the supernatants were collected from the culture by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm, temperature 4 °C, for 15 min. The collected samples (supernatant) were stored at 4 °C, while the pellets were frozen after 3 days.

Growth curve of β-mannanase enzyme from Streptomyces BLC 17–3

The enzyme activity growth curve of β-mannanase enzyme from Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 was measured using the previously published methodology.29 The crude enzymes data activity was obtained from 0 to 168 h. Each experiment was conducted with two replicates.

Assay of β-mannanase activity from Streptomyces BLC 17–3

The β-mannanase activity assay of Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 was conducted using the previously published procedure.7 Substrate of 0.5% LBG was prepared in a 50 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 6.0). For enzymatic activity assay, substrate (250 μL) was mixed with crude enzyme (250 μL) and incubated at 30 °C for 15 min. A control experiment was conducted by mixing substrate (250 μL) and milli-Q (250 μL). The blank was obtained by carrying 500 μL of 50 mM sodium acetate buffer. The enzymatic process was ended by giving 500 μL of Dinitrosalicylic Acid (DNS) solution to the mixture and heating them at 100 °C for 10 min. Afterwards, the reaction tube cooled down in cold water for 10 min prior to measurement of the optical density (OD). The OD of each sample was measured using a spectrophotometer at wavelength 540 nm.

Characterization of pH and temperature on β-mannanase activity of Streptomyces BLC 17–3

The impact of pH on enzyme activity was conducted using the LBG as a substrate. The optimum pH was measured at 30 °C for 15 min, over the pH range from 3 to 10, using 50 mM sodium citrate (3.0–5.0), 50 mM sodium acetate (4.0–6.0), 50 mM sodium phosphate (6.0–8.0), 50 mM tris-HCl (7.0–9.0), and 50 mM glycine- NaOH (8.0–10.0). The optimal temperature on enzymatic activity was investigated at range temperature from 30 to 90 °C on the selected optimum pH. The assay resulted as an activity relative to the enzymatic value, obtained by either the optimum pH or the optimum temperature.

Effect of metal ions and chemicals on β-mannanase activity of Streptomyces BLC 17–3

The effect of metal ions and chemical reagents on enzyme activity was determined by incubating the enzyme in 50 mM sodium acetate (pH 6), containing LBG (0.5%, w/v), in the presence of 1 mM metal ions and chemicals. Each enzyme was incubated with metal ions and chemicals for one hour at room temperature before the addition of LBG to initiate the reaction. The activity of an enzyme without the addition of metal ions and chemicals was defined as 100%. The metal ions and chemicals used in this study were PEG-6000 [Merck; Jakarta, Indonesia], KCl, CaCl2, MnCl2.4H2O, ZnCl2, Fe2SO4.7H2O, MgSO4.H2O, CuSO4, HgCl2 [Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA]. Methanol, Ethanol, and Isopropanol were obtained from Emsure, ACS [Darmstadt, Germany], Triton X-100 [Merck; Jakarta, Indonesia], SDS [Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA], and EDTA [Sigma-Aldrich; St. Louis, MO, USA].

SDS-PAGE and zymogram analysis of β-mannanase enzyme from Streptomyces BLC 17–3

SDS-PAGE was performed to estimate the molecular weight of the target protein, conducted using 12% (w/v) polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, following published protocol.31 Sample buffer was prepared using 0.001% bromophenol blue, 10% SDS, 10% glycerol, 100 mM mercaptoethanol, and 100 mM Tris pH 8.0. The crude samples were denatured at 95 °C for 5 min. The protein bands were stained using Coomassie brilliant blue G-250.

The zymogram procedure was conducted following previous protocol,29 with modification on the use of LBG as substrate. The zymogram was stained in a multi-stage staining procedure with 2.5% Triton X-100. Staining was also performed in Milli-Q water. The incubation was performed in a buffer solution, at the selected optimum pH and optimum temperature. The gel was stained with Congo Red at room temperature for 30 min. Afterwards, the gel was immersed in a mixture of 1 M NaCl. Acetic acid (0.05%) was also used as cleaning solution.

Hydrolysis of mannan polymer using β-mannanase from Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 and the analysis of hydrolysis product using thin-layer chromatography

The hydrolysis of mannan polymer in this study was conducted following the previous protocol.7 The crude enzyme of β-mannanase derived from Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 was incubated in various substrates including the commercial types, such as ivory nut, galactomannan, and LBG. Local substrates were also used in this study, including sugar palm fruit and porang potato. All experiment was conducted in a 50 mM sodium acetate solution at a pH 6.0. The substrates were added to the enzyme in a proportion of 1:1 (v/v) and a reaction volume of 20 mL in a 100 mL Erlenmeyer flask, incubated at 40 °C. Reaction analysis samples were obtained at 0, 1, 4, 24, 48, and 72 h.

The identification of β-mannanase hydrolysis pattern, using thin-layer chromatography (TLC), was conducted following the previous protocol.7 Samples were spotted on Silica gel 60 F plates (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) at various time points, i.e. 0, 1, 4, 24, 48, and 72 h. The mixture spraying solution was prepared in ratio of 2:1:1, consist of 1-butanol, glacial acetic acid, and MiliQ water, respectively. The TLC plate was air-dried and placed in the oven at 120 °C for 10 min, prior to spraying solution treatment.

Results

Screening of β-mannanase derived from marine actinomycetes

All the 21 marine actinomycetes in this study were tested for β-mannanase activity. The activity was measured as a clear zone, which was observed as a lighter appearance of purple-white colour after staining at 4 °C for 24 h. The clear zone of the marine actinomycetes in this study was observed at pH 5.0 (Fig. 1a), 6.0 (Fig. 1b), and 7.0 (Fig. 1c). The highest value was measured at 27.0 mm. Notably, the highest β-mannanase activity was observed in Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3. Therefore, this strain was selected for further characterization, including whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and enzymes identification.

Characterization of β-mannanase enzyme

The growth of β-mannanase activity showed a peak value of 155.77 U/mL after 168 h of culture (Fig. 6). Additionally, the β-mannanase enzyme characterization, conducted using 0.5% LBG (w/v) as a substrate, at 30 °C, indicate the optimal activity at pH 6.0 of sodium acetate with 40.19 U/mL (Fig. 7a). The current study observes the optimum β-mannanase activity at temperature 60 °C when assayed at pH 6.0, with enzyme activity 154 U/mL (Fig. 7b).

An overview of the effect of metal ions on β-mannanase activity is shown in Fig. 8a. The activity of β-mannanase in this study was increased by >100% with metal ions such as KCl (126%), MgSO4.7H20 (128%), Fe2SO4.7H2O (157%), CaCl2 (182%), and MnCl2.4H2O (247%) at 1 mM concentration (Fig. 8a). However, ZnCl2 ions led to a significant drop in activity levels (0%). Further characterization of chemical components showed that the most significant activity inhibition was observed in methanol, followed by Triton X-100, isopropanol, and ethanol. Meanwhile, the activity decreases by inducing Cu2+, Zn2+ and Hg2+.

SDS-PAGE and zymogram analysis from the selected isolate

The target bands associated with β-mannanase enzyme in the culture samples of this study are successfully distinguished by SDS-PAGE and zymogram (Fig. 8b and c, respectively), at the estimated molecular weight of the target enzyme molecule between 34.8 and 49.1 kilodaltons (kDa). The number and the intensity of protein bands for many strains exhibited a distribution size, ranging from 20 to 60 kDa (Fig. 8b). Based on the monomeric structure, zymography confirmed the protein as β-mannanase (Fig. 8b).

Hydrolysis properties of β-mannanase enzyme from the selected isolate

The hydrolysis mechanism was evaluated to show the role and practical usage of the β-mannanase. This was done by the use of β-mannanase enzyme to hydrolyze various mannan polymers, including ivory nut, galactomannan, LBG, sugar palm fruit, and porang potato. TLC analysis showed that the main components of products of Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 were mannobiose (M2), mannotriose (M3), mannotetraose (M4), and mannopentaose (M5). This reflected that β-mannanase enzyme could act as producer of MOS on the substrates. In addition, the main products of this mannan hydrolysis were M2 and M3 without the presence of mannose in any of the four specific substrate hydrolysates.

The commercial substrate in the form of ivory nut underwent a hydrolysis process, which mainly contributed to the formation of M2, M3, M4, and M5 (Fig. 9a). The last products, M2, M3 and a minor M4, were observed to be liberated separately from galactomannan (Fig. 9b). Further, the end products produced by LBG after 24 and 72 h were M2-M3 and M4-M5, respectively (Fig. 9c). However, mannose observed to be absent from this study. The products of hydrolysis of sugar palm fruit substrate were mainly M4 with modest amounts of M2, M3 and M4 (Fig. 9d.) At the same time, porang flour strongly contributed to the production of M2, M3, M4 and a minor amount of M5 (Fig. 9e).

Discussion

All the 21 marine actinomycetes in this study were tested for β-mannanase activity. The β-mannanase activity of Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 reach the highest peak at the optimal time for enzyme production. This study indicates the highest reading of 155 U/mL of β-mannanase enzyme at day seven of fermentation. This result is similar with activity reported from Penicillium occitanis Pol6, with activity 4.5 U/mL.32 Additionally, the incubation period activity curve of Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 was also indicate similar pattern during the production of β-mannanase enzyme. The result indicates similar optimum condition for production of β-mannanase from Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 compared to other study.

The whole genome characterization of Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 in this study aims to determine genes related to carbohydrate degradation enzyme, especially mannanase. Therefore, the annotation prediction and lookup comparison of the whole genome data showed some genes related to mannanase production. Long-read sequencing was employed to enable high-contiguity genome assembly, particularly important for Streptomyces genomes with high GC content and repetitive regions. Although hybrid approaches combining short- and long-read data are commonly used to improve base-level accuracy, the long-read dataset in this study provided sufficient coverage to generate a highly contiguous assembly (3 contigs, N50 = 8.11 Mb). Assembly polishing and quality assessment further confirmed the high completeness and accuracy of the genome, indicating that long-read sequencing alone was sufficient for reliable genome reconstruction. The KEGG database comprises several biological pathways, as well as data on genetic and biochemical systems, that systematically analyze the metabolic process and functions of gene products in cells. The database can be used to investigate the complex biological behaviors of genes, in which genes pathway information can be used to understand microbial function and biological process.33, 34 The metabolic pathway of carbohydrate mannose metabolism within Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 can be determined to understand the production of mannanase enzymes. Among of 111 annotated genes (Tabel 2), 61 were glycoside hydrolase (GH) family, 25 were glycosyltransferase (GTs), 11 were carbohydrate-binding module (CBMs), 7 were carbohydrate esterase (CEs), 6 were auxiliary activities (AA), and 1 was polysaccharide lyase (Pls).

The genome analysis showed that endo-1,4-β-mannanases [EC 3.2.1.78] hydrolyzed the internal β-1,4-linkage of mannan backbone by a retaining double-displacement mechanism and were classified as glycoside hydrolase families 5 (GH5), 26 (GH26), and 113 (GH113) in CAZy database.35 Among of 6 mannan degradation-related genes encoding mannanase-related metabolism, DOCAKMJM_06882 was predicted to be GDP-mannose 4,6-dehydratase (GMD) which converted GDP-D-mannose to D-rhamnose. The majority of the members in this enzyme subfamily are known to form homodimers but further research is necessary to determine possible exceptions. For example, recent research showed that GMD in bacteria, specifically in Pseudomonas aeruginosa exhibited tetrameric formation similar to the trend identified in Arabidopsis thaliana plant.36 The tetrameric GMD in P. aeruginosa (PDB ID: 1RPN) showed 56% sequence identity compared to the DOCAKMJM_06882 in Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 as presented in Table 3. There is currently no information on the existence of DOCAKMJM_06882 as a homodimer or tetramer formation. However, the superimposition of the protein predicted through AlphaFold with GMD in P. aeruginosa (1RPN) showed a high degree of similarity with a longer structure on the last alpha-helix and an unstructured region at the C-terminal region. The GMP predicted model also showed an extensive unstructured region at the N-terminus which exhibited a low predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) value.

The analysis further identified two mannose-6-phosphate isomerases (MPI) which were encoded as DOCAKMJM_01328 and DOCAKMJM_04284. The structures were found to be 33% and 41% similar to mannose-6-phosphate isomerase in Salmonella enterica (2WFP) as presented in Table 2.37 A superimposition of 2WFP with DOCAKMJM_01328 showed that MPI from Salmonella enterica had extensive structure at the N-terminus region consisting of three beta-sheets and one short alpha-helix. This was different from the predicted DOCAKMJM_01328 which had a longer C-terminus of an unstructured region in Fig. 5. The trend showed that the MPI-predicted structures in Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 were similar to the patterns identified in other bacteria solved. An additional gene was also identified and encoded as DOCAKMJM_04283 in Fig. 5 but the predicted structure was not similar to the current MPI solved. There is a need to subject the model to validation through structural and biochemical analyses.

The predicted protein structures and PDB models were superimposed to assess the structural similarity. The results presented in Fig. 5 showed that DOCAKMJM_00387 (beta-mannosidase) and DOCAKMJM_01326 (mannan endo-1,4-beta-mannosidase) had 82% and 63% PDB sequence identity respectively with the published GH domain structure from Streptomyces sp. Meanwhile, DOCAKMJM_06882 was predicted as a GDP-mannose 4,6-dehydratase (GMD) with 56% PDB sequence identity to GMD structure in P. aeruginosa. The analysis also identified DOCAKMJM_01328, DOCAKMJM_04284, and DOCAKMJM_04283 as mannose-6-phosphate isomerases (MPI). It was observed that DOCAKMJM_01328 and DOCAKMJM_04284 were 33% and 41% similar to the structure of MPI in Salmonella enterica. However, DOCAKMJM_04283 did not exhibit any similar sequence identity with the available MPI structure. The prediction of these three-dimensional structures was also required to be confirmed through structural and biochemical research. The results further showed the enrichment of genes that exhibited high bio-prospecting potential for the bioproduction of MOS in the future.

The whole-genome sequencing data showed that Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 had two distinct beta-mannanase encoded enzymes (EC 3.2.1.78), including DOCAKMJM_00387 (beta-mannosidase) and DOCAKMJM_01326 (mannan endo-1,4-beta-mannosidase). Table 3 shows that both beta-mannanases exhibit 82% and 63% sequence identity respectively with the catalytic domain of mannanase in previously solved Streptomyces sp. (4FK9). Moreover, the superimposition of DOCAKMJM_00387 (beta-mannosidase) with the published structure of the catalytic domain of the supposed glycoside hydrolase family 5 (GH5) in mannanase (4FK9) showed a high degree of similarity and a strong superimposing ability. AlphaFold also predicted the presence of a secondary structure in the C-terminus of beta-mannosidase that exhibited a high degree of similarity to the fibronectin type-III domain (Fn3) and the carbohydrate-binding type-2 domain (CBM2) as depicted in Fig. 5. This prediction was supported by a very high pLDDT score. Previous research also reported that Streptomyces sp. had several variants of β-mannanase enzyme. It was observed that the full-length enzyme comprised a GH (catalytic domain), a Fn3 domain, and a CBM2.38 The analysis of DOCAKMJM_01326 (mannan endo-1,4-beta-mannosidase) prediction also showed a congruence with the GH5 catalytic domain of 4FK9 after superimposition. However, the N-terminus exhibited an extended unstructured region with a low pLDDT score. The results also showed that the C-terminus had shorter beta-sheets and an alpha-helix with a very high pLDDT (Fig. 5).

CAZy are necessary for the biosynthesis and degradation of carbohydrates.39 Therefore, Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 strain was cultured to continue the characterization process with the aim of determining the potential as a producer of mannanase enzyme and mannooligosacharide (MOS). The growth of β-mannanase activity showed a peak value of 155.77 U/mL after 168 h of culture (Fig. 6). Additionally, the β-mannanase enzyme characterization, conducted using 0.5% LBG (w/v) as a substrate, at 30 °C, indicate the optimal activity at pH 6.0 of sodium acetate with 40.19 U/mL (Fig. 7a). Moreover, the optimal pH range for was between 5.0 and 9.5 in line with the specific values reported in several previous research for different isolates of Streptomyces. Studies on Streptomyces galbus NR exhibited optimal activity at pH 6.5,40 while Streptomyces sp. S27 recorded the highest level of activity at pH 7,41 and Streptomyces ipomoea CECT 3341 showed optimal activity at pH 7.5.42

The current study observes the optimum β-mannanase activity at temperature 60 °C when assayed at pH 6.0, with enzyme activity 154 U/mL (Fig. 7b). However, the activity gradually decreased afterward. This result was similar with the activity of mannanase derived from Bacillus licheniformis,43 Penicillium freii,44 and Bacillus subtilis subsp. inaquosorum CSB31. However, some other microorganisms indicate β-mannanase activity at temperatures exceeding 60 °C, for example the activity of B. subtilis WY3445 and Aspergillus niger.46 This study suggests the optimum condition of pH 6 and temperature 60 °C for production of mannanase.

The activity of β-mannanase in this study was increased by >100% with metal ions such as KCl (126%), MgSO4.7H20 (128%), Fe2SO4.7H2O (157%), CaCl2 (182%), and MnCl2.4H2O (247%) at 1 mM concentration (Fig. 8a). However, ZnCl2 ions led to a significant drop in activity levels (0%). Further characterization of chemical components showed that the most significant activity inhibition was observed in methanol, followed by Triton X-100, isopropanol, and ethanol. The highest activity was observed in Mn2+ and the trend was consistent with the results obtained for B. subtilis BE-9, in which a significant increase in activity due to the presence of Mn2+. Furthermore, the addition of Triton X-100 and Ca2+ in Paenibacillus sp. HY-8 also reported to enhance the activity of the β-mannanase.47 Meanwhile, the activity decreases by inducing Cu2+, Zn2+ and Hg2+.

Study indicates the auto-oxidation of cysteine to intramolecular disulfide bridges or sulfenic acid was catalyzed by Cu2+ ions.48 Moreover, Hg2+ ions had a total effect of inhibition of the β-mannanase activity compared to all other metal ions.49 Study also reported in the activity of β-mannanase in Enterobacter ludwigii MY271 that was inhibited by the presence of Hg2+ 50. Thus, the inhibition of activity in the current study was probably attributed to the existence of a critical sulfhydryl group within the enzyme.

The addition of methanol, isopropanol, and ethanol enhanced the activity in this study. Study also reported an increasing activity due to the presence of methanol (132.7 ± 0.049), ethanol (140.5 ± 0.019), and propanol (140.9 ± 0.008) in Bacillus sp. CSB39.51 Other study reported the induction of methanol as an important role in the activity of P. pastoris.52 Nevertheless, the current study indicates he supplementation of EDTA did not cause any perceptible effects on the activity of β-mannanase, suggesting that the enzyme was not a metalloenzyme. These results showed significance of certain metals and other additional substances, such as methanol, isopropanol, and ethanol, for any industrial setting.

The target bands associated with β-mannanase enzyme in the culture samples of this study are successfully distinguished by SDS-PAGE and zymogram (Fig. 8b and c, respectively), at the estimated molecular weight of the target enzyme molecule between 34.8 and 49.1 kilodaltons (kDa). The number and the intensity of protein bands for many strains exhibited a distribution size, ranging from 20 to 60 kDa (Fig. 8a). Based on the monomeric structure, zymography confirmed the protein as β-mannanase (Fig. 8b). The fraction profiles also showed several bands for β-mannanase activity and the migration patterns reflected molecular weights that ranged from 30 to 50 kDa while one band was >124 kDa. This was possible because endo-β-mannanases GH5, 26, and 113 typically exhibited a molecular weight range of 35–130 kDa.53, 54 The results of this study indicates diverse types of β-mannanase with several bands was identified under denaturing conditions. A strong signal of approximately 34.8–49.1 kDa was observed in one sample, while the other samples showed a weak signal. The molecular sizes of mannanase derived from Streptomyces genus was within the range of 35 kDa. This result in line with the study on Streptomyces sp. CS428 that showed molecular weight of 35.0 kDa55 and the study on Streptomyces sp. S27 that recorded molecular weight of 37.2 kDa.41

The hydrolysis mechanism was evaluated to show the role and practical usage of the β-mannanase. This was done by the use of β-mannanase enzyme to hydrolyze various mannan polymers, including ivory nut, galactomannan, LBG, sugar palm fruit, and porang potato. TLC analysis showed that the main components of products of Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 were mannobiose (M2), mannotriose (M3), mannotetraose (M4), and mannopentaose (M5). This reflected that β-mannanase enzyme could act as producer of MOS on the substrates. In addition, the main products of this mannan hydrolysis were M2 and M3 without the presence of mannose in any of the four specific substrate hydrolysates.

The commercial substrate in the form of ivory nut underwent a hydrolysis process, which mainly contributed to the formation of M2, M3, M4, and M5 (Fig. 9a). The last products, M2, M3 and a minor M4, were observed to be liberated separately from galactomannan (Fig. 9b). Further, the end products produced by LBG after 24 and 72 h were M2-M3 and M4-M5, respectively (Fig. 9c). However, mannose observed to be absent from this study. The products of hydrolysis of sugar palm fruit substrate were mainly M4 with modest amounts of M2, M3 and M4 (Fig. 9d) At the same time, porang flour strongly contributed to the production of M2, M3, M4 and a minor amount of M5 (Fig. 9e).

MOS is identified as a nondigestible oligosaccharide composed of M2, M3, M4.56 M2 and M3 can be used as prebiotics in the improvement of animal gut health.57 According to,58 MOS was also prone to bacterial degradation within the human fecal microbiota to promote the growth of advantageous populations. In addition, recent study offered data that probiotic Lactobacillus sp. exhibited stronger preferences toward M2 and M3 than functional oligosaccharides with greater degrees of polymerization.59 Another positive prebiotic effect on both the tested isolates was F6 which was 61-alpha-D-galactosyl-beta-D-mannobiose. One of the potential explanations of the phenomenon is that oligosaccharides with galactose substitutions have a high prebiotic index and can easily be acted on by α-galactosidases found in Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium isolates.60 In another study, the prebiotic properties of MOS derived by enzymatic treatment of copra meal were identified and the growth of L. reuteri KUB-AC5, L. crispatus JCM 5810, Bifidobacterium bifidum TISTR 2129, and B. animalis subsp. animals TISTR 2194 were promoted by defatted copra meal hydrolysate.61 Moreover, cassia gum MOS was generated to increase the development of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacteria. It was also observed that MOS-derived cassia gum had the highest prebiotic effect on L. reuteri ATCC 23272 than the others.62 Thus, suggest the importance of MOS for production of prebiotics. Previous study reported the implementation of One-Factor-At-A-Time (OFAT), combined with Central Composite Design (CCD) of Response Surface Methodology (RSM), to investigate the activity of β-mannanase produced by the Streptomyces sp. strain BLC 17–363. Current study investigates the potential of Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 to produce mannooligosaccharides (MOS). Notably, this recent study is also the first to report the whole genome sequence of Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3. The comprehensive analysis of marine actinomycetes in this study contributes to discovering candidate genes that are responsible for production of important enzymes. The knowledge from this study can be further implemented to develop targeted recombinant enzymes from marine actinomycetes, for examples recombinant β-mannanase, for industry and biotechnology applications.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the bioprospecting process identified one isolate as Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 obtained in a marine sponge that could be potentially useful in β-mannanase activity. The Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 also possessed a high level of β-mannanase activity and was classified as Streptomyces vinaceusdrappus with a molecular weight of about 34.8–49.1 (kDa). The isolate showed the best enzymatic performance at a pH sodium acetate of 6 and 60 °C. It also largely generated prebiotic MOS of mannobiose (M2), mannotriose (M3), mannotetraose (M4) and mannopentaose (M5) when subjected to five mannan polymers. Moreover, the genome of the strain had six genes from glycoside hydrolase families (GH5) and two from the glycoside hydrolase families (GH26). The genome sequencing analysis also identified a total of six mannan degradation-related genes that encoded mannanase-related metabolism among CAZy.

The 3D prediction structure conducted using AlphaFold showed that Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 had beta-mannosidase (DOCAKMJM_00387), mannan endo-1,4-beta-mannosidase (DOCAKMJM_01326), and GDP-mannose 4,6-dehydratase (DOCAKMJM_06882). The results showed that two mannose-6-phosphate isomerases (MPI) in the form of DOCAKMJM_01328 and DOCAKMJM_04284 had a similar structure to the MPI published. Meanwhile, DOCAKMJM_04283 (beta-mannosidase) and DOCAKMJM_01326 (mannan endo-1,4-beta-mannosidase) did not show any similarity. This characterization and modeling further enhanced the bioprospecting and development of recombinant β-mannanase enzymes from Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 that exhibited efficient mannose metabolism. The trend showed Streptomyces sp. BLC 17–3 as a promising microorganism for the future bioproduction of MOS identified to be a potential prebiotic substance to enhance digestion and promote health.
