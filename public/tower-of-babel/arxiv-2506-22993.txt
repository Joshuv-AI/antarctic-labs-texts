Prediction Gaps as Pathways to Explanation: Rethinking Educational Outcomes through Differences in Model Performance
Javier Garcia-Bernardo, Eva Jaspers, Weverthon Machado, Samuel Plach, Erik Jan van Leeuwen

Abstract
Social contexts—such as families, schools, and neighborhoods—shape life outcomes. The key question is not simply whether they matter, but rather for whom and under what conditions. Here, we argue that prediction gaps—differences in predictive performance between statistical models of varying complexity—offer a pathway for identifying surprising empirical patterns (i.e., not captured by simpler models) which highlight where theories succeed or fall short. Using population-scale administrative data from the Netherlands, we compare logistic regression, gradient boosting, and graph neural networks to predict university completion using early-life social contexts. Overall, prediction gaps are small, suggesting that previously identified indicators, particularly parental status, capture most measurable variation in educational attainment. However, gaps are larger for girls growing up without fathers—suggesting that the effects of social context for these groups go beyond simple models in line with sociological theory. Our paper shows the potential of prediction methods to support sociological explanation.

Specifically, the prediction gap helps us understand three key issues. First, it indicates whether there are important interactions or non-linear relationships that are overlooked by traditional models. For example, if growing up without a father disproportionately affects already disadvantaged children
[
113
,
114
,
115
]
, we expect models that can automatically identify such interactions—like gradient boosting—to outperform simpler models such as logistic regression unless we explicitly include the necessary interaction terms. Second, the prediction gap reveals whether commonly used aggregate measures of social context, such as average education level of peers’ parents or average neighborhood education levels, adequately capture the true influence of these social contexts. If we observe a significant gap between traditional models that use aggregated measures and graph neural networks—which directly model an individual’s embededdness in social contexts—it indicates that important effects are not well-captured by our current operationalization of social contexts. Third, by comparing predictability across subpopulations (instead of across models), we uncover for whom the impact of social contexts may be understudied. For instance, if we find larger predictability for students with migration backgrounds, this indicates that the effect of social contexts is more complex for these subpopulations. This would highlight the need to investigate these groups specifically and contextualize these findings in light of sociological theories. It would also emphasize the importance of tailoring policy accordingly.

The recent surge in data availability and prediction methods has prompted researchers to incorporate machine learning and other computational approaches into social science
[

. However, focusing solely on prediction can lead researchers to prioritize finding successful prediction problems rather than improving our understanding of the world
[

. This has led some researchers to believe that predictive modeling could drive social sciences toward an atheoretical approach
[
118
,
122

. Here, we show that predictive methods can be used to advance sociological theory, rather than undermine it. If we identify interactions between social contexts and individual outcomes that are consistent with our theories, it strengthens their validity. If not, it challenges us to revise them to explain these empirical patterns.

We find a modest overall prediction gap between logistic regression models and more complex models (gradient boosting and graph neural networks), suggesting that previously identified
indicators, particularly parental status, capture most measurable variation in educational attainment. Once parental background is accounted for, other social contexts, such as extended family, neighborhoods, and schools, add little independent predictive power.
Importantly, we find that prediction gaps are large among children with absent fathers, especially girls and those with less-educated mothers. Examining predictability across different subpopulations, we find that disadvantaged students are also generally less predictable. At age 11, it is harder to accurately predict whether disadvantaged students will complete university than whether privileged students will
not
complete university. Predictability is especially low for children growing up in less-educated families and for the least affluent students of Moroccan or Turkish background. This suggests that for these groups social contexts may operate in more complex ways. While our prediction analyses do not directly establish causality, they highlight empirical patterns that can guide the refinement of theory and the adjustment of empirical models in confirmatory studies.

This article makes important contributions to three bodies of literature. First, we speak to the intergenerational mobility literature by confirming that parental socioeconomic status remains the strongest predictor of educational success, and showing that the relationship is weaker for disadvantaged students. Second, we contribute to the promising emerging literature investigating the relationship between social capital and mobility. Our findings that social contexts do not add much predictability in the pooled sample suggest that social contexts may operate primarily as mediators for the persistence of intergenerational advantage. Third, we advance the computational social science literature by showing that machine learning prediction methods can support sociological theory-building rather than undermining it.

The Educational System in the Netherlands

The Dutch educational system separates students into different academic and vocational pathways at an early age (Fig.
3
). Primary education continues until age 12, after which students transition to secondary education tracks determined by standardized tests, teacher recommendations, and parental input. The three main secondary tracks are pre-vocational secondary education (VMBO), senior general secondary education (HAVO), and pre-university education (VWO). These tracks significantly shape students’ subsequent educational and career opportunities. Students who complete VMBO typically continue to senior secondary vocational education (MBO), HAVO graduates progress to universities of applied sciences (HBO), and VWO graduates have direct access to research universities (WO).

Dutch students from highly educated or affluent families are disproportionately placed in academic (VWO) tracks and subsequently access university education at higher rates, even when controlling for prior achievement (Fig.

Previous research has further demonstrated how parental involvement and cultural capital intensify these outcomes: middle-class families often strategically influence placement decisions and navigate the educational system more effectively, while disadvantaged families may face greater institutional barriers

Prediction Gaps Reveal Hidden Complexity in the Social Sciences

Traditional quantitative research in the social sciences typically follows a deductive logic: researchers start with a theory, derive hypotheses, and then test these using statistical models and empirical data
[
128
]
. An alternative increasingly recognized in the recent literature is abductive inference
[
122
,
129
]
—an approach focused on identifying and explaining surprising empirical patterns that existing theories do not fully anticipate. In this section, we explain how predictive modeling techniques can enable researchers to systematically surface such patterns, providing evidence for revising or extending theory and adjusting or focusing more standard empirical models.

Social contexts are inherently complex. Many of their effects unfold through interactions—between individuals and their environments or across interacting settings such as schools and families—that standard statistical models are not able to fully capture. Machine learning methods can reveal relationships that are not easily captured through conventional approaches. For instance,
[
129
]
show that long-term declines in income mobility are largely explained by increased female labor force participation—an insight that challenges dominant theories emphasizing early-life factors such as parental characteristics. Similarly,
[
130
]
demonstrates how machine learning models trained on the same variables as a linear model can outperform them by capturing hidden interactions and non-linearities. Numerous studies have shown that flexible machine learning models outperform traditional statistical models in predicting key individual outcomes, such as high school dropout
[
131
,
132
]
and unemployment
[
133
]
.

In this paper, we argue that “prediction gaps”—the differences in
out-of-sample
predictive performance between simple statistical models and more flexible machine learning models—can guide sociological research in three areas: improving model specification, evaluating the operationalization of social contexts, and uncovering heterogeneity across subpopulations. The insights generated in those three areas can subsequently be used for abductive inference and to guide new confirmatory studies.

Model specification: Interactions and non-linearities

Every statistical model modeling an outcome variable (e.g. completing university) can be written with the formula
y=f(X)
, where
y
is the outcome variable,
X
is a vector of predictor variables, and
f(\cdot)
is a function that maps inputs
X
to a prediction of
y
. This function
f(X)
may be simple (as in linear regression) or complex (as in a neural network), but in all cases it expresses the model’s best guess at how inputs relate to outcomes.

In this paper, we start by comparing two different specifications of
f(X)
: a simple logistic regression model, which assumes linear effects and no interactions, and a gradient boosting model, which flexibly learns interactions and nonlinearilties from the data.
Both models use the same individual-level variables (e.g., parental income and education, or migration background) to predict the probability of completing university. A significant prediction gap between the models unveils in this case important interactions or nonlinearities that the linear model fails to capture (see also
[
118
,
130
]
). Prediction gaps are a useful tool for abductive inquiry: when these interactions and linearities align with theoretical expectations, they provide evidence in favor of specific theories and help us improve model specification, reducing inference errors. When they are unexpected, they suggest areas where theory might need to be revised.

The operationalization of social contexts

Every predictive model makes assumptions not only about the function
f
—how inputs relate to outcomes—but also about the structure of the social contexts
X
. In most traditional models,
X
includes individual-level variables like parental income or educational background. To incorporate social context, researchers often aggregate features from the environment, such as the average income in a neighborhood or the share of highly educated parents in a school. These are then included as additional covariates to statistical models, preserving the individual-level structure of the data.

However, this approach treats social contexts as isolated, static properties. In reality, social contexts interact with each other and with individual characteristics, producing complex and layered effects. Traditional models struggle to incorporate this complexity, as they require variables to be defined at the level of the individual observation.

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) offer a solution by fundamentally changing how both
X
and
f
are defined. Introduced in 2008
[
134
]
, GNNs are a class of models specifically designed to operate over data structured as graphs—i.e., networks. In our case, the graph represents the population-scale network linking individuals to others in their households, extended families, schools, and neighborhoods. At the same time, the function
f(\cdot)
becomes a more complex process: GNNs predict outcomes not just from a person’s own characteristics, but by recursively aggregating information from their social contexts (see Section
4
).

Here, we compare a gradient boosting model—where
X
consists of social contexts aggregated at the individual-level—to a GNN, which uses a graph-based representation of
X
and a relational function
f
able to capture complex network interactions. A prediction gap between these models suggests that conventional operationalizations (e.g., including mean neighborhood education as a covariate) fail to fully capture the structure and influence of social contexts. For example, segmented assimilation theory suggests that migrant children may do better in school when they live near highly educated migrant neighbors who offer support and act as role models
[
135
]
. Boosting models may miss this kind of pattern unless we explicitly include it—for instance, by adding the share of highly-educated migrant neighbors. In contrast, GNNs can pick up these connections automatically. These gaps again provide a basis for abductive inference: they reveal that social mechanisms are either underspecified or entirely overlooked in traditional models, offering new directions for sociological theory.

Comparing the predictability of subpopulations

Finally, comparing predictive performance across subpopulations allows us to examine whether social contexts impact educational attainment similarly for everyone. Lower predictability in a group reveals more complex life trajectories, greater unmeasured heterogeneity, or distinct social processes shaping outcome
[
136
]
, all sociologically relevant processes.
For instance, the observation that Asian immigrant students in the United States exceed predictions set by social reproduction theory spurred new theoretical
[
107
]
and explanatory
[
137
]
work on cultural frames and parental expectations.

Data and Methods

Data

We use population-scale administrative data from Statistics Netherlands (CBS). Access to these data is facilitated by ODISSEI (Open Data Infrastructure for Social Science and Economic Innovations). These longitudinal microdata cover all residents of the Netherlands and are linkable across registers (e.g., tax authority, registrations, education) using anonymized personal identifiers. This enables us to jointly analyze the impact of household, extended family, school, and neighborhood contexts on long-term educational outcomes.

4.1.1
Cohort Construction

Our study cohort includes all children who completed the final year of primary school (grade 8) in 2010—when the social contexts data start—and transitioned to secondary school in 2011. We use demographic characteristics and information on social contexts (nuclear and extended family, schools, and neighborhoods) observed in 2010, when children were approximately age 11–12. The outcome is whether an individual had completed university by 2023 (approximately age 24–25). We excluded 4,734 individuals with a missing educational attainment (this happens for example when the person dies) and 1,272 individuals who were absent from the Netherlands for more than 1 year of the 13 year period studied. The final sample size is 188,011 individuals.

We then reconstructed children’s early-life social environments by linking them to their nuclear and extended family members, school classmates, and neighborhood residents using CBS population registries. This is possible through the network files
[
138
,
139
]
, which connect individuals to household members, extended family members, schoolmates, and neighbors.

4.1.2

Since we are mostly interested in the prediction ability of the models, we include a large number of variables across six thematic domains: individual, nuclear family, household, extended family, school, and neighborhood. Preprocessing

We ensured a fair comparison between models by using the same data and processing. Since Graph Neural Network can be highly affected if the variables have widely different scales, all continuous variables were standardized or normalized using the following transformations:

• Rank
: All income variables were transformed to ranks, as typical in the literature
[
129
]
. This also enables us to model both positive and negative incomes when large outliers are present. Parental income ranks are based on the full Dutch population. Household, school and neighborhood ranks are relative to the sample studied.

• Min-max scaling:
Applied to birth year, migration generation, household income size, and number of siblings and grandparents.

• Log transformation
: Applied to school urbanity to reduce skew.

• Z-score standardization
: Applied to school class size and log school urbanity.

Several variables had a few missing values (see Table
1
) and two variables had a large share of missing values. These are parental education (father and mother), where information is largely missing for older cohorts (Section
in Appendix), and for the receipt of child benefit (WPO gewicht), which is missing for students attending special education. For those two, we created a corresponding binary indicator variable to flag the original missingness.

Graph Neural Network Variables

For the Graph Neural Network (GNN), we encode the same information into the heterogeneous network structure representing the embeddedness of individuals within social contexts. The GNN input consists of two node types:
child
(students) and
person
(e.g., parents, neighbors, peers), each with its own set of features:

• Child node features
: Birth year, migration generation, gender (male), disability indicator, special education (SBO) indicator, receipt of child benefit (WPO gewicht), WPO weight (child benefit) and parent-known indicators. Household features included at the child level: mean and total household income; number of known grandparents, indicator for co
-
municipal grandparents. School features: class size, average class size, log
-
urbanicity, share of parents reporting income.

• Person node features
: Birth year, migration generation, gender (male; imputed to 0.5 if missing; educational attainment (university completed in 2010), missingness indicator for educational attainment, income rank (filled with 0 if missing), missingness indicator for income.

To keep the GNN architecture relatively simple, we include school and household characteristics—such as class size, average household income, and urbanity—directly as input features on the child nodes, rather than including ‘school nodes” in the network and relying on GNN to infer them. We also add binary indicators for whether each parent is known, because the architecture we use (GraphSAGE with mean and max aggregation) makes it difficult to reliably count the number of connected nodes, such as siblings or household members. By including these features explicitly, we ensure the model has access to important contextual information that might otherwise be lost and make the statistical models rely on different information.

Statistical Models and Prediction Approach

We use models of increasing complexity to investigate to what extent the prediction gap is driven by missing interactions and nonlinearities, or by limitations in how we operationalize social contexts (Fig.
2
).

4.2.1
Logistic Regression

Our baseline model is a logistic regression, which models the log-odds of university completion with a linear combination of predictors:

(1)

\log\left(\frac{P(y=1\mid X)}{1-P(y=1\mid X)}\right)=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}X_{1}+\beta_{2}X_{2}+\dots+\beta_{p}X_{p}

This model is interpretable and widely used in the social sciences. It assumes additive, linear effects and cannot capture complex interactions or nonlinearities without explicitly specifying them. We use the standard implementation from the
scikit-learn
Python package
[
141
]
.

Our predictors consist of individual characteristics (e.g., migration background, gender, education, parental income) and aggregated contextual variables (e.g., average neighborhood income, share of educated neighbors or school peers).

4.2.2
Gradient Boosting Trees

Gradient boosting is a machine learning method that builds a sequence of decision trees, each one correcting the errors of the previous. This creates a highly flexible function that can capture complex, high-order interactions and nonlinearities without requiring researchers to specify them manually.

We use a standard implementation (
Histogram-based Gradient Boosting Classification Tree)
from
scikit-learn
, trained on the same individual-level features as the logistic regression. Formally, the prediction is modeled as:

(2)

\log\left(\frac{P(y=1\mid X)}{1-P(y=1\mid X)}\right)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\beta_{m}h_{m}(X)

where each
h_{m}(X)
is the prediction of a shallow decision tree, and
\beta_{m}
is a learned weight.

4.2.3
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) offer a fundamentally different approach compared to traditional models, both in how they define the function
f(\cdot)
and how they structure the input space
X
. Rather than treating individuals as isolated units with fixed features, GNNs represent individuals as nodes within a social graph. The GNN input consists of two node types:
child
(students) and
person
(e.g., parents, neighbors, peers), each with its own set of features. Each node is connected to others via edges that represent social contexts between the type of nodes. We model five different relationships between the two types of nodes (Fig.
2
A): shared classrooms (child:child), parents (child:person), children (person:child), parents or children (person:person), neighbors (person:person).

In this framework, the input
X
includes not only an individual’s own characteristics, but also the features of their social connections. The function
f(\cdot)
is redefined as a recursive aggregation process over the network. We use the
GraphSAGE
architecture
[
142
]
without sampling, from the
pytorch-geometric
Python package.

Formally, for each of the five relationships, the process unfolds in recursive steps over
K=2
convolutional layers. For each node
v
and each of the two layers,
k
, we compute an embedding
h_{v}^{(k)}
. We set an initial embedding
h_{v}^{(0)}
for each node
v
to the individual-level variables such as age, gender, or migration background (Section
4.1.2
).

For a layer
k
, first, each node
v
aggregates information from its neighbors in a given social relationship
s
(e.g., schoolmates, neighbors) in a hidden vector
h_{v}^{(k,s)}
. Let
\mathcal{N}^{s}(v)
denote the set of neighbors of node
v
in relationship
s
. Then set

(3)

h_{v}^{(k,s)}=\text{AGGREGATE}^{(k)}\left(\left\{h_{u}^{(k-1)}:u\in\mathcal{N}^{s}(v)\right\}\right)

Here,
\text{AGGREGATE}^{(k)}
is a function that aggregates embeddings from neighbors in relationship
s
. We use the same aggregation function for each GNN layer, which is either the
mean
or the concatenated
mean
and
max
, depending on the relationship (see Appendix Section
).

Second, the node updates its own embedding by combining the aggregated information received from its neighbors with its prior embedding. This is done separately for each social relationship
s
, and then merged:

(4)

h_{v}^{(k)}=\text{UPDATE}^{(k)}\left(h_{v}^{(k-1)},\bigoplus_{s}h_{v}^{(k,s)}\right)

Here,
\bigoplus_{s}
is a cross-type aggregation function across social relationships, and
\text{UPDATE}^{(k)}
is function that combines the resulting vector with the embedding vector of the previous layer. We use the same standard update function for each GNN layer: a fully connected layer, followed by a ReLU layer providing nonlinearity. The cross-type aggregation function is either
mean
or
cat
(concatenation), depending on the social contexts used (see Section
5.2
and Appendix Section
).

After two rounds of message passing (aggregation and update), the final node embeddings
h_{v}^{(K)}
are used as input to a logistic regression that predicts whether the student completes university:

(5)

\log\left(\frac{P(y=1)}{1-P(y=1)}\right)=\beta h^{(K)}.

This GNN-based approach is especially well suited for modeling complex and overlapping social environments. By incorporating the structure of relationships and recursively combining information across direct and indirect connections, the model can capture higher-order effects—such as effects of academically high-achieving peers, or the clustering of disadvantage within schools or neighborhoods—that would need to be explicitly operationalized and included in other type of statistical models.

4.2.4
Ensuring a fair comparison between models

To ensure fair comparisons between models, we use the same data (see Section
4.1.2
) and apply the same fitting and evaluation procedure to all approaches. Each model is trained to minimize the log-loss (also known as binary cross-entropy loss), which is the standard function in traditional logistic regression. This loss function penalizes incorrect predictions more strongly when the model is more confident in them, encouraging models to produce calibrated probabilities rather than just correct classifications.

The log-loss is defined as

(6)

\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[y_{i}\log(\hat{y}_{i})+(1-y_{i})\log(1-\hat{y}_{i})\right],

where
y_{i}\in{0,1}
is the true outcome for individual
i
(i.e., 0=not completing university, 1=completing university) and
\hat{y}_{i}
is the model’s predicted probability that the individual completes university.

Highly flexible models, such as Gradient Boosting and Graph Neural Networks, are prone to overfitting, where a model appears highly accurate simply because it has memorized the data it was trained on, rather than having learned patterns that apply more generally. To avoid this, we evaluate
out-of-sample
prediction ability. We randomly split the dataset into a training set (80% of the data) and a test set (20%, 37,603 observations). The models are trained only on the training set, and their performance is evaluated on the held-out test set. The test set provides a clean benchmark for assessing how well the model performs on unseen data, thereby offering a more honest evaluation of predictive accuracy. We construct confidence intervals by performing 1,000 bootstrap resamples of the test dataset.

By keeping the loss function, data inputs, and train/test split constant across models, we can attribute the observed differences in performance to the model’s ability to capture complexity which helps predict university graduation—such as interactions or network structure—rather than differences in data access or overfitting.

Statistical models, especially highly flexible machine learning approaches such as gradient boosting trees and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), have several settings or
hyperparameters
that must be specified prior to training. These hyperparameters control how complex and flexible the learned function
f(X)
can be. Optimally tuning these hyperparameters is crucial: Overly simple models may miss relevant complexity, while overly complex models may overfit the training data, leading to poor generalization to unseen cases. To systematically determine optimal hyperparameters for our GNN, we used an automated optimization procedure via Optuna, a Python package designed for efficient hyperparameter optimization. This is detailed in the Appendix Section
.

4.2.5
Comparing the Prediction Ability

Evaluating how well models predict university completion is not straightforward when there are imbalances in university completion. For some groups, success is nearly guaranteed: for instance, over 89% of children with parents with post-graduate degrees attend university. In these cases, predicting success is easy and uninformative. A good model is one that can also identify the few children who do
not
follow expected trajectories—those who fail despite advantage, or succeed despite disadvantage.

Similarly to
[
143
]
, we use the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) as our main measure of predictive performance. MCC is a balanced metric that captures how well a model correctly identifies both positive and negative outcomes
[
144
]
. Unlike accuracy or F1 score, it remains reliable even when one outcome is much more common than the other. Mathematically, it is defined as:

(7)

\text{MCC}=\frac{TP\cdot TN-FP\cdot FN}{\sqrt{(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)}}

Here,
TP
refers to true positives,
TN
to true negatives,
FP
to false positives, and
FN
to false negatives. The MCC ranges from
-1
(perfectly wrong predictions), to
0
(no better than chance), to
1
(perfect prediction). Although not intuitive, MCC is equivalent to a Pearson correlation for binary outcomes.

The prediction gap between models

We first predict university completion by 2023 (when most students are age 24–25) from demographic characteristics and early-life social contexts (nuclear family, extended family, schools, neighborhoods) observed in 2010, when students were about 11–12. We compare three models of increasing complexity: logistic regression (Linear), gradient boosting (Boost), and graph neural networks (GNNs), evaluating performance with the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), a measure robust to outcome imbalance that is equivalent to Pearsons correlation for binary outcomes.

Small prediction gaps are driven by parental characteristics

The limited gaps across models suggest that predictions hinge on a few strong predictors common to all approaches. To examine this further, we assess how prediction accuracy improves when social contexts are added sequentially. As expected given the extremely dominant role of parental background in shaping educational outcomes
[
93
]
, parental characteristics yield the largest gain in MCC—rising from 19% to 32%—when added to a baseline model including only basic demographics (birth year, gender, migration generation, disability status) (Fig.
4
B). Adding household, extended family, school, and neighborhood information produces only marginal additional improvements, reaching about 35%. This suggests that parental background captures most of the measurable variation in educational outcomes. Notably, these smaller incremental gains from additional contexts are consistently modest across all modeling strategies (Fig.
4
B), suggesting that interactions and nonlinearities overall do not greatly improve prediction.

Principal Component Analysis of the GNN embeddings confirms this dominance. PCA is a dimensionality reduction technique that is able to combine the information of the embeddings into fewer variables while capturing as much of the remaining variability as possible. Because GNN embeddings represent each student’s position within their social context—and serve as input into a logistic regression in the model’s final prediction—examining which dimensions explain the most variation helps us understand what the model has learned. If one dimension accounts for most of the variance in the embeddings and aligns closely with parental background, this suggests that parental background is the most important factor shaping the model’s predictions. That is exactly what we observe. Three components explain nearly 90% of the variance in the embeddings (67.6%, 15.5%, and 6.0%, respectively). The first dimension correlates strongly with the embedding when only features related to the nuclear family are used (82% correlation), and with parental education and income (51–53%). Even in a flexible, network-based model, parental background remains the primary determinant (see Appendix
for full tables and methodology).

Importantly, these findings do not imply that schools and neighborhoods are unimportant; rather, they suggest that much of the influence of family background may be mediated by school and neighborhood social contexts. Wealthier, better-educated families place children into advantaged contexts
[

,
127

. So, once parental characteristics are accounted for, these social contexts add little independent predictive power.

Prediction gaps reveal unequal effects of social contexts for girls with absent fathers

Although overall prediction gaps are small, they reveal meaningful variation across subgroups. We find the largest prediction gaps (between GNN and logistic regression) among students whose fathers were deceased or absent from administrative records (Fig.

This suggests that the social contexts of children without fathers relate to educational attainment differently—and in more complex ways, which more flexible models partly capture—compared to those of children with fathers.

These insights call for more flexible theories of educational inequality that attend to heterogeneity in how social processes unfold.

Conclusion

Educational attainment remains one of the most critical determinants of life chances, shaping individuals’ income, health, and social mobility across generations. Yet the pathways through which social contexts influence educational outcomes are profoundly complex and often hidden. In this paper, we leverage the concept of
prediction gaps
—differences in model performance across statistical methods of varying complexity—to probe the intricacies of social context effects. Our central theoretical innovation is to reframe prediction errors not as random statistical noise, but as sociologically meaningful signals. By identifying subpopulations in which prediction gaps occur, we reveal for whom social contexts may operate differently, pointing toward new directions for explanatory research.

Using population-scale registry data from the Netherlands, we systematically compare logistic regression, gradient boosting trees, and graph neural networks to assess how well early-life social contexts predict college completion. Our findings show that while conventional indicators of parental socioeconomic status capture much of the measurable variance in educational attainment, critical prediction gaps remain. These gaps overall are rather small, but they are much larger for some subgroups—especially for girls growing up without fathers. Although the interaction between gender and father absence has been documented in the United States (e.g.,
[
112
]
), our study extends this finding to the Netherlands, suggesting the cross-national relevance of gendered family dynamics in shaping educational trajectories.

A key contribution of this paper is demonstrating the potential of graph neural networks for sociological inquiry. Applying GNNs to population-scale data, we show that network-based models yield additional predictive gains over traditional statistical models—especially for some groups in society—indicating that individual embeddedness within overlapping social contexts carries important—and previously obscured—information. While we focus on educational attainment, this approach opens new avenues for other types of network data, such as social networks affecting opinion dynamics, collaboration networks in science and innovation studies, or professional networks influencing career trajectories.

In addition, our results show that educational outcomes are significantly less predictable for disadvantaged groups, such as students from families with lower levels of educational attainment and non-Western migration background. This unpredictability suggests the presence of compounding disadvantages and unmeasured contextual factors—such as parenting styles, social networks, and community resources. Predictability is key to effective policy, since the results of interventions should ideally be predicted accurately across groups. Policies addressing educational inequalities should therefore consider tailored strategies that account for complex interactions among individual and contextual characteristics. For example, our study highlights the need to better understand the mechanisms for educational attainment in children from low-education and low income as well as Moroccan or Turkish backgrounds—exactly groups which would benefit from effective policies. This is especially true in the context of rising inequality, polarization, and anti-immigrant sentiment
[
147
]
.

Our study has three important limitations, each opening avenues for further research. First, our outcome variable—university completion—is dominated by parental characteristics. While this aligns with classical theories of social reproduction, it likely obscures other pathways of influence, such as peer effects or neighborhood dynamics. In this case, some of these variables might not increase prediction much but still be important mediators for the intergenerational persistence of advantage. Future research could expand by analyzing outcomes less tightly coupled to family background—such as labor market trajectories or adult health outcomes—to disentangle additional layers of social context influence and explore mediation effects.

Second, the scope of our analysis is constrained by data limitations. We focus on a single cohort and operate within a “small data” setting by machine learning standards, which, compounded by a long prediction horizon, restricts the ability to model rare or evolving interactions. Future work could extend these insights by examining different stages of the life course—for instance, by analyzing cohorts at later educational stages and predicting transitions to bachelor’s, master’s or PhD programs, where non-parental social contexts are likely to be more predictive of the outcomes.

Third, important sociological mechanisms are unmeasured in administrative data. Parenting styles, cultural norms, genetics, friendships, and local institutional interactions are absent from our models. This likely contributes to the observed persistent unpredictability, particularly among disadvantaged groups. However, if these mechanisms are indirectly captured through observable behaviors (e.g., parenting styles reflected in enrollment in religious or alternative schools; cultural values visible through patterns of residential mobility), machine learning models could still detect their imprints. While we do not directly investigate why certain subpopulations—such as non-Western immigrants from disadvantaged families—are more difficult to predict, identifying these groups provides valuable insight into areas that require further research for policy interventions and sociological inquiry.

In conclusion, by explicitly linking where models succeed or fail to the underlying complexity of social processes, we provide a framework to challenge and refine existing theories of educational inequality. Rather than treating machine learning as an alternative to sociological theorizing, we advocate for its use as a complementary tool—one that can help illuminate the unequal influence of social contexts on individual outcomes—eventually shaping sociological theory and policy-making.
