Effects of in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation, and environmental pollution on the risk of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in adulthood: a large scale prospective cohort study
Authors: Jianing Li, Guoxian Li, Ze Ma, Ziqing Sun, Yujie Shi, Zexin Lou, Hanqing Zhao, Mengtong Sun, Yu Wang, Qida He, Miao Jiang, Yueping Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere has been limited research to date comprehensively assessing the effects of in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation and environmental pollution on the incidence of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).MethodsThe participants in the current study were drawn from the UK Biobank cohort. 274,330 participants were included for analyses of in utero tobacco exposure and incident risk of COPD, and 306,080 were analyzed for age of smoking initiation and incident risk of COPD. In utero tobacco exposure was determined by maternal smoking during pregnancy. According to smoking habits, participants were classified as non-smokers, late-onset smokers (age of smoking initiation ≥ 15 years), or early-onset smokers (age of smoking initiation < 15 years). Environmental pollution is classified into low, medium, and high Environmental Pollution Score (EPS) groups. A Cox proportional hazard regression model was used to evaluate the effects of in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation, and environmental pollution on COPD incidence. The combined effects and interactions among in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation, and environmental pollution on COPD incidence were also examined.ResultsOver a median follow-up period of 12.96 years, 9054 and 10,301 participants were diagnosed with COPD based on in utero tobacco exposure and age of smoking initiation, respectively. Hazard ratios (HRs; 95% confidence intervals [CIs]) of COPD risk among early-onset smokers with in utero tobacco exposure, participants with in utero tobacco exposure and high EPS, early-onset smokers with high EPS, and early-onset smokers with high EPS and in utero tobacco exposure were 3.92 (3.54, 4.31), 1.25 (1.15, 1.35), 3.49 (3.11, 3.91), and 3.81 (3.26, 4.46), compared with non-smokers without in utero tobacco exposure, participants without in utero tobacco exposure and low EPS, non-smokers with low EPS, and non-smokers with low EPS and without in utero tobacco exposure, respectively. Furthermore, additive interactions among late-onset smokers and intermediate/high EPS were observed [relative excess risk due to the interaction (95% CI): 0.26 (0.07, 0.45) and 0.34 (0.15, 0.52)].ConclusionsOur findings suggest that in utero tobacco exposure, early-life smoking, and environmental pollution independently and jointly increase the incidence of COPD.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-04009-w.

Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a chronic lung disease associated with high morbidity and mortality [1–3], imposing a considerable economic burden on the healthcare system. The global cost of COPD is projected to reach $4.326 trillion between 2020 and 2050 [4]. More than 350 million people worldwide are affected by COPD, accounting for the third highest proportion of deaths globally [4], and these numbers are expected to gradually increase. Despite extensive efforts to identify and mitigate the risk factors of COPD, the impact of risk factors on COPD development remains incompletely understood. Therefore, further efforts to explore risk factors associated with COPD are crucial.

Several factors may influence the development of COPD [5]. Accumulated evidence suggests that exposure to toxic particles, especially from tobacco smoking, increases the risk of developing COPD [6]. Preclinical studies have shown that the link between prenatal tobacco smoke exposure and COPD risk via a mouse model. Pregnant mice were exposed to 3–10 cigarettes daily from 7 days pre-mating to birth, and their offspring exhibited reduced ciliated cells, impaired alveolar development, and upregulated EGFR signaling [7]. In another study, researchers used C57Bl/6J mice to test combined tobacco smoke exposure effects [8]. First, pregnant mice were exposed to cigarette smoke before and during pregnancy. Then, the offspring were re-exposed to smoke during adolescence (postnatal days 21–49). Finally, they measured lung outcomes, finding the combined exposure caused persistent lung function decline, alveolar simplification, and airway remodeling. Numerous studies have explored the relationship between tobacco exposure in early life and COPD incidence. For instance, a meta-analysis showed that smoking during pregnancy can increase the likelihood of developing COPD in adulthood [9]. A United States (US) cross-sectional survey of adults also illustrated that early-onset smokers demonstrate greater susceptibility to developing COPD than late-onset smokers [10]. However, previous studies often had small sample sizes and rarely considered the combined effects of in utero tobacco exposure and age of smoking initiation. Therefore, an in-depth study on the combined effects of in utero tobacco exposure and age of smoking initiation on COPD incidence in a large prospective cohort is crucial.

In addition to tobacco exposure, ambient conditions have been identified as major contributors to the development of COPD [11, 12]. Wang et al. established a clinically relevant rat model of COPD [13]. They exposed these COPD rats to real-world PM2.5 pollution (weeks 9–16), simulating actual human exposure scenarios. They found that pollutants exacerbated lung function impairment, promoted mucus/collagen deposition, and elevated levels of pro-fibrotic factors. The European Air Pollution Effects Cohort Study Project has shown that high concentrations of NO2 and PM10, as well as increased traffic near residential areas, are associated with impaired lung function in adults [14]. Relevant meta-analyses have indicated that exposure to ambient air pollutants correlates with a higher risk of COPD [15]. A prospective cohort study also demonstrated that air pollutants elevate the risk of COPD onset [16]. Although studies have separately explored the effects of tobacco exposure and environmental pollution on the incidence of COPD [17, 18], the combined effects of in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation, and environmental pollution on COPD incidence remain unknown.

A review of the literature revealed that prenatal tobacco exposure is associated with increased nicotine craving in childhood, which may lead to earlier smoking initiation during adolescence [19]. Additionally, smoking may alter an individual’s metabolic response to air pollution [20]. Long-term exposure to polluted air may exacerbate smoking-related lung damage, with all three factors collectively increasing health risks [21]. Mechanistically, exposure to tobacco components during fetal development (in utero tobacco exposure) may make offspring more prone to accelerated cessation of lung maturation and enhanced airway susceptibility, thereby amplifying the harmful effects of early smoking and later exposure to environmental air pollution [22]. These exposures share common pathways, including oxidative stress, epigenetic regulation of lung development genes, and persistent airway inflammation [23–25]. Therefore, we believe that revealing the combined effects of in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation, and environmental pollution on the risk of developing COPD is meaningful and necessary, which can provide a theoretical basis for formulating phased intervention strategies, such as smoking control during pregnancy, smoking cessation among adolescents, and environmental governance.

This study examined the effect of in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation, and environmental pollution on the incident risk of COPD. The potential interactions among these risk factors were also investigated.

Materials and methods

Study population

The participants in this study were sourced from the United Kingdom (UK) Biobank, a large public database consisting of over half a million individuals recruited throughout the UK during 2006–2010. The cohort comprised individuals with baseline ages ranging from 40 to 69 years [26]. All enrolled participants were instructed to complete their baseline assessments at one of the 22 assessment centers located throughout England, Wales, and Scotland, complete a self-reported touchscreen questionnaire collecting detailed personal exposure information, and undergo a physical examination [26]. Following approval by the Northwest Multi-Center Research Ethics Committee, the UK Biobank study was conducted in accordance with ethical standards, with informed consent from all participants.

The study population data were obtained from the UK Biobank under Application Number 68,136. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, we excluded participants with any type of pulmonary disease at baseline (n = 9840), as well as those without available data on smoking during pregnancy (n = 68,000) and age of smoking initiation (n = 73,642). Additionally, individuals without data on covariates were excluded (specifically, the in utero tobacco exposure group excluded 150,246 people, and 112,854 were excluded from age of smoking initiation group). Thus, 274,330 participants were analyzed for in utero tobacco exposure and incident risk of COPD, and 306,080 were analyzed for age of smoking initiation and incident risk of COPD. To estimate the joint effects and interactions among in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation, and environmental pollution, participants without environmental pollution data were excluded, resulting in 250,423 and 279,756 participants in the two analyses, respectively. Ultimately, 247,880 participants were analyzed for in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation, environmental pollution, and incident risk of COPD.

Assessment of in utero tobacco exposure and age of smoking initiation

In utero tobacco exposure was ascertained according to maternal smoking status during pregnancy. Participants were categorized as non-smokers, late-onset smokers (age of smoking initiation ≥ 15 years), and early-onset smokers (age of smoking initiation < 15 years) based on their smoking habits [27].

Assessment of environmental pollution

The environmental pollution score (EPS) was constructed using eight environmental pollutants: NOX, NO2, PM2.5, PM10, PM2.5−10, noise, traffic intensity, and distance to the road [28]. Supplementary Table 1 shows the relationship between each pollutant and COPD risk. All pollutants were estimated using data from a small regional health statistics unit, part of the European Union project on environmental determinants of health within the biohealth sector [28]. In 2010, land use regression (LUR) was implemented to derive spatial estimates of particulate matter (PM2.5, PM2.5−10, PM10), nitrogen oxides (NOx, NO2) and noise exposure in a European air pollution cohort [29]. Additionally, distance to the road and traffic intensity were determined using data from the 2009 Ordnance Survey Meridian 2 road network, which captures the local road infrastructure of that period. In line with approaches used in prior research, the estimated values from 2009 to 2010 were treated as a proxy to quantify participants’ chronic, long-term exposure to environmental pollutants [30]. Noise was assessed by weighting, whereas traffic intensity and distance to roads were analyzed using logarithmic transformations [28]. Additionally, EPS was categorized into low, intermediate, and high groups. The formula is as follows [28]:

Outcome assessment

The primary outcome of this study was COPD incidence. We diagnosed COPD using the International Classification of Diseases Version 10 (J41-J44). Hospital records, primary care data, and self-reported health conditions served as sources of relevant information [31]. Participant follow-up duration was determined from the baseline date to the first diagnosis of COPD, end of the study (February 27, 2022), or death, whichever occurred first.

Assessment of covariates

The covariates included age, sex, ethnicity, Townsend deprivation index (TDI), body mass index (BMI), educational level, income, alcohol intake frequency, physical activity, sleep duration, dietary pattern, sedentary time, handgrip strength, family history of COPD, and pack-years of smoking, which were collected using touchscreen questionnaires and physical measurements at baseline. The details were described in Supplementary data.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized according to in utero tobacco exposure (yes or no) and age of smoking initiation (non-smokers, late-onset smokers, and early-onset smokers). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are expressed as means along with their standard deviations (SD), whereas those with a skewed distribution are depicted as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR).

Multivariate-adjusted Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Assessment of the proportional hazards assumption was conducted using Schoenfeld residual analysis. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, and ethnicity, whereas model 2 was further adjusted for income, educational level, and TDI. Model 3 accounted for adjustments for BMI, physical activity, dietary patterns, alcohol intake frequency, family history of COPD, sedentary time, sleep duration, grip strength, and pack-years of smoking.

To examine the joint effects of in utero tobacco exposure and age of smoking initiation, the participants were divided into six groups. The reference group comprised individuals without in utero tobacco exposure and non-smokers. To analyze the joint effects between in utero tobacco exposure and EPS, all participants were categorized into six groups, and individuals in the reference group were those without in utero tobacco exposure and with low EPS. Investigating the joint effects of age of smoking initiation and EPS resulted in nine groups, with the reference group consisting of participants who were non-smokers and in the low EPS group. Furthermore, when exploring the joint effects of in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation, and EPS, participants without in utero tobacco exposure, with low EPS, and non-smokers were considered the reference group. Additionally, we estimated the interactions between in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation, and EPS. Relative excess risk due to the interaction (RERI) and attributable proportion due to the interaction (AP) were used to evaluate the results. If there were no additive interactions, the 95% CI of RERI and AP would be zero [32].

Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the stability and reliability of the findings. First, participants diagnosed with COPD within the first two years after baseline were excluded to minimize potential reverse causation. Second, a new weighted EPS was developed based on the relevant literature [33], including NOx, NO2, PM2.5, PM10, and PM2.5–10.5, which can exclusively discover relationships between air pollution and COPD incidence. Third, multiple imputations were conducted to address missing covariates, and the relationships between exposure and outcomes were re-evaluated. Fourth, FEV1/FVC was used to define the onset of COPD. Fifth, we further adjusted for prematurity and occupational exposure factors to account for their potential effects.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and two-sided P values were used for all tests. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the in utero tobacco exposure group and the age of smoking initiation group. Analysis of in utero tobacco exposure involved 274,330 participants, of whom 84,974 were exposed. For the age of smoking initiation analysis, the largest group consisted of non-smokers, followed by late-onset and early-onset smokers. In the analysis of in utero tobacco exposure, individuals exposed to in utero tobacco were more likely to be male, white, have lower educational levels, lack adequate physical activity, ≥ 20 pack-years of smoking, and have a family history of COPD compared with those without such exposure. A total of 306,080 participants were included in the analysis of age of smoking initiation. Compared to non-smokers, individuals who had a higher TDI, lower income levels, higher BMI, higher hand grip strength, shorter sleep duration, and longer sedentary duration were mostly early-onset smokers.

In utero tobacco exposure and incident risk of COPD

Over a median follow-up period of 12.96 years, 9054 participants were diagnosed with COPD (Supplementary Table 2). In model 1, individuals exposed to in utero tobacco had a higher risk of COPD compared with those without such exposure, with an HR of 1.49 (95% CI, 1.44, 1.54). In the fully adjusted model, the HR (95% CI) slightly decreased but remained robust at 1.14 (95% CI, 1.09, 1.18).

Age of smoking initiation and incident risk of COPD

In total, 10,301 participants were diagnosed with COPD during a median follow-up time of 12.96 years (Supplementary Table 2). Generally, the risk of COPD increases gradually from non-smokers to early-onset smokers. Notably, compared with non-smokers, late- and early-onset smokers had a significantly higher risk of developing COPD, with HR (95% CI) of 2.67 (2.51, 2.84) and 3.46 (3.20, 3.74) respectively, in the fully adjusted model.

Environmental pollution and incident risk of COPD

The associations between the eight environmental pollutants and COPD incidence were detailed in Supplementary Table 1, which indicates that all eight environmental pollutants increased the incidence of COPD. Moreover, the intermediate/high EPS group had an increased risk of COPD compared with the low EPS group (Supplementary Table 3).

In utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation and incident risk of COPD

A relationship between in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation, and COPD incidence was observed (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 4). Specifically, individuals exposed to in utero tobacco who were early-onset smokers had the highest risk of COPD incidence, with an HR (95% CI) of 3.92 (3.54, 4.31), compared with non-smokers without in utero tobacco exposure. Additive interactions between in utero tobacco exposure and age of smoking initiation were not observed [RERI (95%CI), 0.09 (−0.09, 0.28) and 0.43 (−0.01, 0.87)] (Table 2). However, a multiplicative interaction was found between these two exposures and incident COPD (P interaction = 0.0236).

In utero tobacco exposure, EPS and incident risk of COPD

Associations between in utero tobacco exposure, EPS, and incident risk of COPD were observed (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 5). In utero tobacco exposure combined with high EPS levels was associated with an increased risk of COPD. Participants with in utero tobacco exposure and high EPS had the highest risk of COPD incidence, with an HR (95% CI) of 1.25 (1.15, 1.35), compared with participants without in utero tobacco exposure and with low EPS. Nevertheless, no significant additive or multiplicative interactions between in utero tobacco exposure and EPS were observed concerning the diagnosis of COPD [RERI (95%CI), −0.10 (−0.23, 0.03) and − 0.04 (−0.17, 0.08), P interaction=0.5810] (Table 3).

Age of smoking initiation, EPS, and incident risk of COPD

Both the joint effects and additive interactions between age of smoking initiation and EPS were investigated for their associations with COPD incidence (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 5). Individuals who initiated smoking earlier and had high EPS were more likely to be diagnosed with COPD. The HR (95% CI) for COPD incidence was 3.49 (3.11, 3.91) among individuals who were early-onset smokers and had high EPS, compared with non-smokers with low EPS. Importantly, additive interactions were observed among participants who were late-onset smokers and had intermediate/high EPS [RERI (95%CI), 0.26 (0.07, 0.45) and 0.34 (0.15, 0.52)] (Table 3). Moreover, multiplicative interactions between age of smoking initiation and EPS were not tested (Pinteraction = 0.4577).

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis results demonstrated the robustness of the study findings. After excluding participants who developed COPD within two years of the start of follow-up, recalculating EPS, interpolating covariates, defining COPD onset using FEV1/FVC, and adjusting for prematurity and occupational exposure, the results remained consistent with the main findings (Supplementary Tables 6–11).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, in utero tobacco exposure and age of smoking initiation independently and jointly increased COPD incidence in adulthood. Furthermore, EPS was also associated with COPD incidence, and individuals with in utero tobacco exposure, smoking during childhood/adolescence, and living in heavily polluted environments generally had a higher risk of COPD. Notably, interactions between late-onset smokers and moderate/high EPS levels were observed.

In the present study, we observed a significant positive association between in utero tobacco exposure and COPD in adults. Another study that used questionnaires to ask patients with COPD about their mothers’ smoking status during pregnancy showed that maternal smoking negatively affected children’s lung function and promoted the development of COPD [34]. Furthermore, Bartal showed that in utero tobacco exposure and passive smoking in early childhood were causative factors for COPD [35]. Our findings were also consistent with those of a previous meta-analysis concluding that in utero tobacco exposure may induce COPD in adulthood [36]. Similarly, our study also revealed associations between early-onset smokers and late-onset smokers and an increased risk of COPD, which was consistent with the findings of a cross-sectional survey of adults in the US [27]. Additionally, studies by Chinese scholars have shown that an earlier onset of smoking is positively correlated with an increased prevalence of COPD [37]. Notably, this research fills a critical knowledge gap by exploring EPS in relation to COPD, showing that more severe environmental pollution is linked to an elevated prevalence of COPD [38, 39].

This study provides novel insights into the literature on the joint effects of in utero tobacco exposure and age of smoking exposure on COPD incidence. Our results showed that participants with in utero tobacco exposure and smoking in early life had a higher risk of developing COPD. This may be due to impaired lung development from in utero tobacco exposure and further lung damage from early-life smoking, both of which contribute significantly to COPD development [40]. Additionally, we found joint effects of in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation, and EPS on COPD incidence. Specifically, individuals with in utero tobacco exposure or smoking in early life, and those in areas with high EPS, had the highest risk of COPD. There are several possible explanations for this observation. First, both tobacco exposure and environmental pollution can cause oxidative stress and disrupt intracellular balance, thereby accelerating the development of COPD [41]. Moreover, smoking may cause persistent inflammatory responses in the lungs and other tissues [42]. Air pollution further stimulates the inflammatory response and exacerbates lung and airway inflammation, thereby increasing the risk of COPD [43]. Cigarettes contain substances such as nicotine and carbon monoxide, which irritate and damage the respiratory system [44]. In areas with severe environmental pollution, air contains a higher concentration of harmful gases such as SO2, NOX, and O3, which can also stimulate and damage the respiratory tract, leading to airway remodeling and an increased risk of COPD [45]. PM2.5’s small particle size allows deep penetration into alveoli, where it induces persistent oxidative stress, activates alveolar macrophages, and triggers epithelial-mesenchymal transition-mechanisms that drive airway remodeling and parenchymal destruction independent of gaseous pollutant exposure [46–48]. This aligns with epidemiological evidence confirming PM2.5 as an independent risk factor for COPD, even after adjusting for gaseous pollutants and smoking [39, 49]. The early stages of life represent a critical period for lung growth and development [50]. Early life tobacco exposure, such as in utero tobacco exposure and smoking during childhood and adolescence, causes permanent, irreversible damage [51]. EPS primarily regulates adult lung function and cannot repair lung damage caused by early life tobacco exposure. Therefore, regardless of EPS levels, existing developmental defects increase the risk of COPD to a similar degree.

Notably, interactions between late-onset smoking and intermediate/high EPS levels were observed. For instance, late-onset smoking exhibited significant additive interactions with high EPS on COPD incidence. The RERI (95% CI) was 0.34 (0.15, 0.52), which suggests that the combined effects of late-onset smoking and high EPS on COPD incidence are greater than the sum of their individual effects. This indicates a synergistic relationship between the two factors and their combined presence results in a higher risk of COPD than a single factor. The AP (95% CI) was 0.13 (0.06, 0.20), indicating that 13% of the excess risk of COPD can be attributed to the interaction of late-onset smoking and high EPS. The findings of additive interactions have important clinical implications and public health significance. The number of participants who started smoking late in life and had high EPS levels was relatively high. The incidence of COPD can be significantly reduced through combined interventions targeting smoking control legislation, air purification, and occupational protection. In summary, the discovery of additive interactions suggests that comprehensive interventions targeting tobacco exposure and environmental pollution may be more effective in reducing COPD risk than interventions targeting either factor alone, underscoring the need for an integrated approach to intervening in COPD. In contrast, no interaction between early-onset smoking and EPS categories was observed, possibly because of the inadequate study population. The number of early-onset smokers was lower than that of late-onset smokers, which may have reduced the statistical efficacy and resulted in negative results. Nonetheless, the specific reasons for this discrepancy remain unclear, and future work must address the causal relationships underlying these observations.

Notably, joint effects of the three exposure factors—in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation, and EPS—on COPD incidence were observed. First, tobacco exposure in utero and smoking during early life may affect the development and function of the immune system, thereby increasing susceptibility to environmental pollutants [52]. Dysregulation of the immune system may lead to an abnormal response to airway inflammation, thereby increasing the risk of COPD [53]. Furthermore, in utero exposure to tobacco may affect gene expression and epigenetic modifications in the fetus, increasing the sensitivity to smoking in early life, environmental pollution, and the risk of COPD [54]. Tobacco exposure in pregnant women, children, and adolescents poses a significant public health challenge. Cumulative studies indicated that early-life smokers were more likely to become heavy smokers, thereby increasing their risk of COPD in adulthood [55, 56]. Therefore, our findings underscore the need for targeted interventions, including legal designations, social campaigns, and family education to reduce the prevalence of smoking among pregnant women, children, and adolescents. Urgent action is required to protect the environment and mitigate the impact of environmental pollution on COPD through the formulation of environmental regulations and public health initiatives.

In sensitivity analysis, we defined COPD onset as FEV1/FVC < 0.7 and observed discrepancies with the primary findings in the manuscript. However, this may be attributable to the following reasons. First, the ICD-10-based analysis included 274,330 and 306,080 participants, while the spirometry subgroup was limited to 10,148 and 10,290 participants, respectively. This larger sample size for the ICD-10 analysis was critical for achieving sufficient statistical power. Second, it is worth noting that FEV1/FVC is commonly used to define baseline COPD incidence. In prospective cohort studies, ICD-10 is more frequently used to define COPD incidence [57–59].

The advantages of our study are as follows. First, this is the first epidemiological study to comprehensively evaluate the relationships between in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation, EPS, and incident risk of COPD in a large prospective cohort. Second, the study’s large sample size ensured the reliability of the relationships between in utero tobacco exposure, age of smoking initiation, EPS, and COPD incidence. Third, unlike previous studies that focused on the relationship between a single environmental pollutant and COPD, our study used a composite indicator that included eight pollutants for a more comprehensive assessment of environmental pollution. However, it is important to acknowledge that this study has some limitations. First, in the current study, COPD incidence was defined using ICD-10 codes. Future research may explore using spirometry data to define the onset of COPD. Second, the collection of exposure information relied on self-reported methods and retrospective data, which introduced the potential for misclassification bias and may have further reduced the hazard ratio. Third, considering that the UK Biobank predominantly comprises individuals of European ancestry, caution should be exercised when generalizing the conclusions to other ethnic groups. Fourth, air pollution is a complex and dynamic mixture containing numerous toxic and harmful substances [60, 61], making it challenging to determine the effects of each component. Nonetheless, the LUR method employed by the UK Biobank remains an effective measurement approach [62]. Future studies should measure air pollution at multiple time points to investigate the influence of changes in air pollution on pulmonary diseases such as COPD [63]. Finally, unmeasured or uncorrected biases may persist, despite adjusting for potential confounding biases.

Conclusions

This large prospective cohort study provided evidence that in utero tobacco exposure, early-life smoking, and environmental pollution were independently and jointly positively associated with an elevated risk for COPD. Considering the preventable nature of tobacco, it is crucial to avoid it as early as possible, particularly in individuals residing in heavily polluted environments. Future studies should collect more comprehensive information on early-life tobacco exposure and environmental pollution, and further explore their combined influence on COPD.
