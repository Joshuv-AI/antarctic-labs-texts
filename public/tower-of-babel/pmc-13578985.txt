Decoding the bioactive peptides and metabolic fingerprints of whey derived from dairy fan processing by-products
Authors: Wenli Tao, Zhenzhu Li, Wei Du, Yue Wang, Ruonan Dai, Yanan Shi, Hong Li, Qiuling Tan, Aixiang Huang, Guangqiang Wei, Yufang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Whey contains abundant nutrients and bioactive compounds with health benefits, but functional peptides and metabolites in Dairy fan whey (Whey generated during dairy fan processing, DFW) remain underexplored. Therefore, this study identifies DFW nutritional profile and bioactivity via peptidomics, nontargeted metabolomics, and in vitro experiments. The results showed that a variety of bioactive peptides were identified, including antioxidant peptides and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitory peptides. Four antioxidant peptides interact with Keap1 via hydrogen bonds and salt bridges, thereby exerting antioxidant effects and regulating the Keap1-Nrf2/ARE pathway. In vitro assays confirmed that DFW scavenged DPPH, ABTS, and hydroxyl radicals, demonstrating strong antioxidant activity. Additionally, 919 metabolites were identified in DFW and conventional whey (W), including 15 characteristic compounds such as 4-aminobutyric acid, histamine, and pyridoxine. These characteristic metabolites exhibit antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunoregulatory activities. Compared with W, DFW contains more characteristic nutrients. Overall, this study provides a theoretical basis for the high-value utilization of DFW.

Introduction

Whey is rich in proteins, lactose, essential amino acids, vitamins, and minerals (León-López et al., 2022). Among them, whey protein-mainly composed of lactoglobulin, lactoalbumin, immunoglobulin, and lactoferrin-supports immune system regulation (Saint-Sauveur et al., 2008), exhibits antibacterial and antiviral activities, and helps prevent osteoporosis (García-Martínez et al., 2024). Whey also contains diverse beneficial small-molecule metabolites, including amino acids and their derivatives, bioactive peptides, lactic acid, citric acid, and other organic acids. These components regulate blood glucose, blood pressure, and lipid metabolism, and help maintain intestinal health (Fekete et al., 2016). Previous studies show that whey protein supplementation can significantly increases lean muscle mass and promotes fat loss, offering significant nutritional benefits for fitness populations and older adults at risk of muscle loss (Verreijen et al., 2015). Overall, whey is a high-quality nutrient source that supports human health.

Large amounts of byproduct whey are generated during the processing of cheese and other dairy products. To improve its utilization, researchers have investigated the nutritional components of whey. Deshmukh et al. (2024) produced the prebiotic galacto-oligosaccharides from lactose-rich whey via enzymatic hydrolysis. Juthi et al. (2025) investigated the use of cheese whey in whey protein powder and the effects of milk source on its properties. Zaman et al. (2023) developed a whey-sugarcane functional beverage using whey as a raw material. Studies also show that the carbon sources in whey can be converted into sustainable biofuels and biochemicals through fermentation (Ma et al., 2024). In addition, whey nutrients can be completely recovered from whole whey or its fractions for animal feed using different processes (Guirguis et al., 1993). Li et al. (2024) employed proteomics combined with simulated gastrointestinal digestion and molecular docking to reveal compositional differences and potential biological activities of whey proteins from yak, cattle, and cattle-yak milk. Zhao (2023) used multi-omics approaches to identify anti-inflammatory peptides in a Binglangjiang buffalo whey hydrolysate and elucidate their mechanisms in vitro. However, differences in milk source and processing also alter the composition of byproduct whey.

As one of the three major ethnic dairy products in Yunnan (milk cake, dairy fan, and butter), dairy fan is known as “Chinese cheese.” It is rich in protein, fat, and amino acids, with a unique flavor that is widely appreciated by local consumers. Raw dairy fan is the only stretched cheese in China, produced from raw milk through heating, acidification, curdling, blanching, stretching, shaping, and natural drying (Liao et al., 2021). During dairy fan processing, the byproduct,diary fan whey (Whey generated during dairy fan processing，DFW) accounts for 85–95% of the total milk volume (Bernabucci et al., 2015). In recent years, the rapid growth of a dairy fan industry in Dali, Yunnan, has led to a marked increase in byproduct whey. According to incomplete statistics, Dali produces approximately 9000–13,500 tons of whey annually, most of which is not effectively treated or utilized. Instead, most whey is discharged as wastewater or buried underground, with only a small portion used for breeding and animal husbandry. Its low utilization rate contributes to environmental pollution (Zandona et al., 2021). However, the characteristic nutrients of dairy fan whey remain unclear, limiting its effective use.

Therefore, this study aims to use non-targeted metabolomics and peptideomics to systematically characterize DFW, reveal its peptide and metabolic profiles, identify the characteristic bioactive peptides and functional metabolites, and elucidate their mechanisms of action. The study findings could provide a theoretical basis for the sustainable transformation and high-value utilization of byproduct whey.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and preparation

DFW and W (raw milk is Chinese Holstein cattle milk supplied by the Dali Eryuan Dairy Factory) were the materials employed in this investigation. Fig. 1 illustrates the traditional production process of dairy fan. Fresh raw milk (pH 6.4–6.8) was added to spontaneously fermented sour whey (pH 3.5–4.5), which had been preheated to approximately 65 °C, to induce acid-mediated coagulation of the milk casein. After coagulation, approximately one-third of the separated whey was removed. The remaining curd was repeatedly folded, scalded, and stretched using an iron clip until a smooth, elastic, and fibrous texture was obtained. The curd was then crushed into a rectangular sheet, rolled around a wooden stick, and manually wound onto a drying rack. After ventilation and drying, the product was collected as dairy fan (Jiang et al., 2021). The whey remaining after dairy fan production was collected as the whey by-product sample. Eight batches of whey, a by-product generated during the production of dairy fan, were collected separately. The batches were then pooled in pairs to form four groups for subsequent analysis. Each biological replicate was injected once for analysis. According to the classical whey extraction method, the milk was adjusted to pH 4.6–4.8 and allowed to stand at 25 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was centrifuged at 1500 ×g for 20 min using a TLG20M centrifuge (Changsha Magissen Instrument Co., Ltd., China). The two freshly prepared whey samples, without prior storage, were pre-frozen at −80 °C and subsequently freeze-dried at −50 °C under a vacuum of 15 Pa using a freeze dryer (FD-1 A-50, Billion, China). The resulting lyophilized powders were collected and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.Fig. 1Dairy fan production process.Fig. 1

Identification of bioactive peptides in whey

Sample treatment

One milligram of freeze-dried powder was suspended in 100 μL of 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) aqueous solution and homogenized by ultrasonication. The homogenate was centrifuged at 14,000 ×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was then subjected to ultrafiltration using a 10 kDa molecular-weight-cutoff membrane (UFSC40001, Millipore, USA) at 13,500 ×g for 10 min. The resulting filtrate was used for peptide quantification with a NanoDrop 2000C UV–visible spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The filtrate was desalted using a C18 solid-phase extraction column (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Briefly, the column was first washed with acetonitrile and then equilibrated with 0.1% TFA aqueous solution. The ultrafiltrated sample was subsequently loaded onto the column, and the retained peptides were eluted with 70% acetonitrile.

Liquid chromatography-tandem Mass spectrometry data acquisition

Each fraction was separated using an Easy nLC (ThermoFisher Scientific，USA) nanoflow HPLC system. Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid 84% acetonitrile. The column was equilibrated with 95% A. The sample was loaded via an autosampler onto a Thermo Scientific EASY column (100 μm × 2 cm, 5 μm, C18) and separated on a second Thermo Scientific EASY column (75 μm × 10 cm, 3 μm, C18) at 250 nL/min. The liquid-phase gradient was: 0–110 min, linear gradient of B from 0 to 35% B; 110–118 min, 35–100% B; 118–120 min, 100% B.

Samples were separated via chromatography and analyzed using a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo-Fisher Scientific, USA). The analysis time was 120 min; detection was positive ion mode; the parent ion scan range was 300–1800 m/z; the main mass spectrometry (MS) resolution was 70,000 at m/z 200; the automatic gain control target was 3e6; the maximum injection time was 10 ms; and the dynamic exclusion time was 40.0 s. Peptides and fragment mass-to-charge ratios were acquired as follows: 10 MS2 scans were obtained after each full scan. The normalized collision energy (NCE) was 30 eV; the underfill ratio was 0.1%; the isolation window was 2 m/z; the MS2 resolution was 17,500 at 200 m/z; and the MS2 activation type was HCD. For manual review of the MS/MS spectra, peptide identifications were retained only when they met the following criteria: accurate precursor-mass measurement, sufficient fragment-ion coverage, consistency between the observed and theoretical fragment ions, an acceptable signal-to-noise ratio, and the absence of mutually conflicting evidence. Assignments with insufficient fragment-ion support or ambiguous spectral interpretation were excluded. MaxQuant (version 1.5.5.1) was used to identify the peptide sequences by searching against the Bos taurus protein database downloaded from UniProt. The specific parameters were as follows: a precursor ion mass tolerance of 20 ppm, a fragment ion mass tolerance of 0.1 Da, a maximum of two missed cleavages, and an FDR of ≤0.01.

Molecular docking analysis

Differentially expressed peptides were identified using a fold-change threshold of >1.20 for upregulated peptides or < 0.83 for downregulated peptides, together with a p-value <0.05. The novelty and functions of the peptide compounds were evaluated using the BIOPEP (https://biochemia.uwm.edu.pl/bioprep/start_bioprep.php) and MBPDB (http://MBPDB.nws) databases. The potential antioxidant activity of the peptides was predicted using the PeptideRanker server (http://distilldeep.ucd.ie/PeptideRanker/). The Keap1 protein crystal structure (PDB ID: 2FLU) was obtained from the PDB database provided and utilized for docking. PyMOL 2.5.5 was used to generate the 3D structure of peptide. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina 1.2.3 software (Eberhardt et al., 2021). The receptor protein was processed using PyMOL 2.5.5 prior to docking by eliminating small molecules, water, and salt ions. The crystal ligand was then enclosed in the docking box. All processed ligands and receptor proteins were converted to the PDBQT format required for AutoDock Vina 1.2.3 docking using ADFRsuite 1.03 (Ravindranath et al., 2015). During docking, exhaustiveness was set to 32, and all other parameters were kept at default values. The binding conformation was defined as the docking pose with the highest score. Docking findings were visualized using PyMOL 2.5.5.

Determination of antioxidant activity of whey

DPPH, ABTS, and hydroxyl radical scavenging assays were used to evaluate the antioxidant activity of DFW. The specific operation was based on the methods of Wang, Mao, et al. (2024) and Wang, Zhao, et al. (2024). The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) is the effective concentration at which DPPH, ABTS, and hydroxyl radical scavenging reach 50%. In this study, all experiments were conducted in triplicate.

After preparing a 0.1 mmol/L DPPH solution in 95% ethanol, three groups were established: experimental (50 μL sample + 50 μL DPPH), control (50 μL water +50 μL DPPH), and blank (50 μL sample + 50 μL anhydrous ethanol). Each group was mixed and incubated in the dark for 30 min, and absorbance was measured at 517 nm. The DPPH radical scavenging rate was calculated using the following formula:DPPH radical scavenging activity%=1−Asample−AblankAcontrol×100

To prepare the ABTS radical cation working solution for the scavenging experiment, equal volumes of 7.0 mmol/L ABTS solution and 2.45 mmol/L potassium persulfate were mixed and incubated in the dark for 16 h. The ABTS radical cation solution was diluted 20–30 fold with PBS to an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 at 734 nm. In a 96-well plate, 50 μL of the sample solution was mixed with 150 μL of the ABTS radical cation solution. The mixture was then incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 6 min. Using 50 μL of distilled water as the blank, absorbance was measured at 734 nm. The ABTS radical scavenging rate was calculated using the following formula:ABTS radical scavenging activity%=1−Acontrol−AsampleAcontrol×100

Following the approach of Huang et al. (2017), solutions of 9 mmol/L salicylic acid, 9 mmol/L FeSO₄, and 8.8 mmol/L H₂O₂ were prepared. Three groups were established: experimental (25 μL sample + 25 μL salicylic acid +25 μL FeSO4 + 25 μL H2O2 + 25 μL water), control (equal volume of distilled water replacing the sample), and blank (equal volume of distilled water replacing the sample and H2O2). Following a 15 min reaction at 37 °C, absorbance was measured at 510 nm. The rate of hydroxyl radical scavenging was determined using the following formula:Hydroxylradical scavenging activity%=1−Asample−AblankAcontrol×100

Analysis of small-molecule metabolites of whey

Four independent biological samples were analyzed for each group, including four DFW samples and four W samples. After weighing 25 mg of sample into a centrifuge tube, two homogenization beads and 500 μL extraction solvent (methanol:acetonitrile:water = 2:2:1, v/v/v) containing an isotopically labeled internal standard were added (Isotopic internal standards were listed in Supplementary Material 1). The mixture was vortexed for 30 s (Shanghai Jingxin Technology Co., Ltd., China), homogenized for 4 min at 35 Hz, and sonicated in an ice-water bath (Shenzhen Redbond Electronics Co., Ltd., China) for 5 min. This procedure was performed three times. After standing at −40 °C for 1 h, 300 μL was added to a 96-well filter plate. For 3 min, the filter plate–collection plate assembly was gradually compressed to 6 psi using a positive pressure apparatus. The filtrate was then collected and analyzed.

For polar metabolites, a Vanquish ultra-high-performance liquid chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used in this study. The target compounds were chromatographically separated using a liquid chromatography column (Waters Corporation, USA; 2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm) (Wang et al., 2016). In liquid chromatography, the mobile phases were acetonitrile (B) and water containing 25 mmol/L ammonia and 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate (A). The sample tray temperature was 4 °C, and the injection volume was 2 μL. The control software (Xcalibur 4.4, Thermo) allows the Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific，USA) to acquire MS and MS/MS data. The following are the specific parameters: sheath gas flow 50 Arb; auxiliary gas flow 15 Arb; capillary temperature 320 °C; MS/MS resolution 15,000; full MS resolution 60,000; NCE 20/30/40; spray voltage −3.4 kV (negative) or 3.8 kV (positive). Metabolomics data were processed using SIMCA software (version 18.0.1; Sartorius Stedim Data Analytics AB, Umeå, Sweden), including logarithmic (LOG) transformation followed by centering (CTR). Differential metabolites were selected based on the criteria of a fold change (FC) < 0.5 or > 2.0 and a p-value <0.05.

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation from at least three independent experiments. Statistical significance was assessed using Duncan multiple comparison test, with p < 0.05 considered significant. Data analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 27.0.1. Metabolite profiles, peptide chain lengths, peptide molecular masses, and antioxidant activities were visualized using Origin 2021.

Results and discussion

Functional bioactive peptide Spectrum of whey

Liquid chromatography-tandem MS was used to identify and evaluate components of DFW and W with molecular weights <3 kDa (Fig. 2A & 2B). In total, 3559 and 2884 peptides were detected in DFW and W, respectively. The higher peptide count in DFW suggests that it contains more bioactive peptides than W. Additionally, Fig. 2C displays the peptide molecular weight and length. Peptides in DFW and W contained 3–25 amino acid residues. Their molecular weights ranged from 331.21 to 3101.53 Da (DFW) and 331.21 to 3113.74 Da (W) (Fig. 2D & 2E). The molecular weight distributions were similar, with DFW molecular weight slightly lower than that of W. According to the BIOPEP database, DFW peptides exhibit 26 biological activities, including angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibition, ACE2 inhibition, α-glucosidase inhibition, antibacterial, antiamnestic, anticancer, antidiabetic, anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, antithrombotic, binding, dipeptidyl peptidase III inhibition, dipeptidyl peptidase IV inhibition, glutamate carboxypeptidase II inhibition, glutamate carboxypeptidase inhibition, hypotensive, hypouricemic, immunomodulatory, tripeptidyl peptidase II inhibition, lactocepin inhibition, neprilysin inhibition, neuropeptide activity, regulating, renin inhibition, stimulating, and other activities (Fig. 2G & 2H). Fig. 2F illustrates the diverse health-related biological activities among the discovered bioactive peptides. Major activities included dipeptide peptidase IV inhibition (DFW: 4005; W: 3218), dipeptide peptidase III inhibition (DFW: 2440; W: 1906), α-glucosidase inhibition (DFW: 1531; W: 1145), anticancer (DFW: 495; W: 383), anti-inflammatory (DFW: 565; W: 436), and blood pressure regulation (DFW: 926; W: 766). DFW had higher counts than those of W and exhibited greater health-related biological activity, largely due to ACE inhibition associated with diary fan processing. Helal et al. (2023) showed that ACE and DPP-IV-inhibitory peptides are the most prevalent bioactive peptides released from Parma cheese whey during natural and inoculated fermentation. This suggests that microorganisms widely hydrolyze whey proteins, promoting the release of peptides with diverse biological activities. According to Sutantawong et al. (2024), 1187 peptides were identified in in vitro gastric digests, of which 61 exhibited biological activity, indicating that whey proteins generate bioactive peptides during digestion. Given that the acid solution used to prepare dairy fan was naturally fermented from whey, DFW contained more and a greater diversity of bioactive peptides than W. Microbial enzymes promote the production of additional bioactive peptides during fermentation. In whey production, heat and acid act together: heating denatures proteins, unfolds their structure, increases peptide chain exposure to water, and accelerates peptide bond hydrolysis (O Loughlin et al., 2013). In conclusion, under combined acid-heat treatment and microbial fermentation, DFW releases a greater number and diversity of bioactive peptides than W, indicating higher health-promotion value and offering a crucial theoretical foundation for the high-value use of whey.

Analysis of antioxidant mechanism of whey characteristic Peptides from DFW

PeptideRanker was used to screen characteristic peptides (score > 0.7) from the 51 upregulated peptides identified in DFW and W for antioxidant analysis. Then BIOPEP-UWM was used to predict peptide functional activity. Four potential antioxidant peptides were identified: FLPYPYYAKPA, FPKYPVEPF, LGPVRGPFPII, and MPFPKYP.

Molecular docking was used to evaluate interactions between the four DFW-derived peptides (FLPYPYYAKPA, FPKYPVEPF, LGPVRGPFPII, and MPFPKYP) and the antioxidant target Keap1 protein, to assess their antioxidant activity and mechanism. The four peptides had strong interactions with the Keap1 (Fig. 4, lower left). The binding energies were −7.004, −7.454, −6.778, and −7.671 kcal/mol, respectively. Lower binding energy indicates stronger ligand–receptor interactions. Previous studies report that the antioxidant peptide VAPFPEVFGK from walnut protein binds to Keap1 with a binding energy of −8.9 kcal/mol (Chen et al., 2024), while LSDLHAHKL from chicken hemoglobin shows a binding energy of −8.546 kcal/mol (S. Wang et al., 2024). Dimethyl fumarate (DMF) activates the ARE pathway by disrupting Nrf2-Keap1 binding and exhibits a binding energy of −3.0 kcal/mol (Zheng et al., 2024). The four peptides showed lower binding energies to Keap1 than that of DMF, suggesting stronger affinity for the Keap1 target and potential antioxidant activity.Fig. 4Molecular docking analysis of four DFW-derived peptides (FLPYPYYAKPA, FPKYPVEPF, LGPVRGPFPII, and MPFPKYP) with the Keap1 protein. Furthermore, the Keap1-Nrf2/ARE pathway represents the primary cellular defense mechanism against oxidative stress (Nan et al., 2024). Antioxidant peptides mitigate oxidative damage by binding to the Kelch domain of Keap1, thereby promoting dissociation and activation of Nrf2 and Keap1 (Adinolfi et al., 2023). Fig. 4 illustrates that FLPYPYYAKPA binds to Keap1 at Arg483, Arg415, Arg380, Tyr525, Ser555, and Tyr334, while FPKYPVEPF binds to Keap1 at Arg415, Arg380, Arg483, and Ser363. Additionally, LGPVRGPFPII interacts with Keap1 at Arg380, Arg415, and Tyr334 while MPFPKYP binds to Keap1 at Arg415, Arg483, Tyr334, Arg380, Tyr572, and Ser602. Collectively, these findings indicate that all four peptides rely on the polar anchoring mechanism of Arg415 and Arg380 within the Keap1-Nrf2 binding pocket, forming key salt bridges or hydrogen bonds that stabilize the peptide positioning and provide antioxidant activity. Consistently, Tonolo et al. (2020) reported that the milk-derived peptide K-8-K (KVLPYPEK) forms hydrogen bonds with Keap1residues Arg380, Ser363, Ser383, Asn382, Asn387, and Tyr334. Furthermore, the milk tofu-derived peptide NY9 (NQFLPYPYY) binds key regions of the Keap1, including Leu365, Val465, Gly464, Leu557, and Thr560, through π-alkyl interactions, hydrophobic contacts, and hydrogen bonding, thereby exhibiting antioxidant activity (Wang et al., 2025).

Overall, these findings indicate that FLPYPYYAKPA, FPKYPVEPF, LGPVRGPFPII, and MPFPKYP may bind to the Nrf2-binding region within the Keap1-Kelch domain, thereby potentially disrupting the Keap1-Nrf2 interaction and exerting antioxidant effects. In future studies, we will conduct an in-depth investigation into bioactive peptides. Following chemical synthesis, their antioxidant activity, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitory activity, and other bioactivities will be validated via in vitro experiments.

Antioxidant activity of whey

The above studies demonstrate the presence of antioxidant peptides in DFW; therefore, the antioxidant activity of whey was evaluated. The determination of DPPH, ABTS, and hydroxyl radical scavenging capacities is widely used to evaluate antioxidant activity (Wei et al., 2023). The results of this study showed that, with increasing whey concentration, the scavenging activities of DFW and W against DPPH, ABTS, and hydroxyl radicals increased progressively (Fig. 5A-C). DFW exhibited considerably higher antioxidant activity than W, except for hydroxyl radical scavenging at 8 mg/mL. The relatively higher hydroxyl radical scavenging observed in W may be attributed to acetic acid treatment, which creates an acidic environment at high concentration and enhances metal ion chelation, thereby improving hydroxyl radical scavenging efficiency (Fadda et al., 2018). The IC50 values of DFW for DPPH, ABTS, and hydroxyl radical scavenging were 3.37, 10.59, and 5.62 mg/mL, respectively, while those of W were 10.61, 14.97, and 4.76 mg/mL. IC50 values for DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging were lower in DFW than in W (Fig. 5D). Additionally, whey protein hydrolysate has an IC50 of 22.79 mg/mL, indicating that DFW demonstrates stronger DPPH free radical scavenging activity (Khadka et al., 2024). These findings suggest that DFW has higher antioxidant potency. This effect may be attributed to microbial metabolism during natural fermentation, which promotes the breakdown of whey proteins into low-molecular-weight bioactive peptides. Previous studies have demonstrated that these peptides generally exhibit enhanced electron transfer capacity, hydrogen-donating ability, and free radical scavenging activity (Corrochano et al., 2018). Metabolomics-based analysis of whey metabolic profile

In addition to abundant bioactive peptides, DFW contains various metabolites that may act synergistically with these peptides to exert functional effects. Processing steps, including acid treatment, blanching, and protein coagulation, may also influence the composition of small-molecule metabolites in DFW. Therefore, untargeted metabolomic analysis was conducted to identify the characteristic small-molecule metabolites in DFW.

Overall, 919 metabolites were identified from DFW and W samples (Fig. 6A). These metabolites were classified into seven categories, including 207 lipids, 111 carbohydrates, 112 organic acids and derivatives, 247 amino acids and derivatives, 9 steroids and derivatives, 94 phenylpropanoids, and 139 other metabolites. Amino acids and low-molecular-weight peptides were typically present in dairy byproducts due to protein and macromolecular degradation during cheese making (Piraino et al., 2007). Therefore, the metabolites identified in both samples were predominantly amino acids and their derivatives, as well as lipids, consistent with the high protein and lipid contents of milk. To produce dairy fan, acid treatment and blanching hydrolyze macromolecular proteins under combined heat and acid conditions, yielding substantial amounts of amino acids and low-molecular-weight peptides. Blanching also induces casein precipitation and coagulation. While most casein was used in dairy fan production, residual casein and fat remained in DFW, contributing to the abundance of amino acids and their derivatives in its metabolite profile. Lactose is the primary carbohydrate in milk. The high lactose content of whey serves as an energy source and a precursor for prebiotics (Fara et al., 2020). Citric acid, lactic acid, pyruvic acid, and other organic metabolites formed during fermentation and processing are naturally present in milk (Sameen et al., 2010). Phenylpropanoids and steroids may also originate from feed intake or dairy cow metabolism (Modica, 2018).Fig. 6Classification of DFW and W (A) metabolites. (B) PCA score plot. To further differentiate between DFW and W, 15 characteristic metabolites were selected from 561 differential metabolites using the OPLS-DA model. The characteristic metabolites exhibited VIP values >1.2, indicating a strong contribution to the classification of both sample groups. The DFW group showed greater enrichment of nutritionally active compounds than that of the W group (Fig. 7C & 7D). Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), histamine, dimethylglycine, sn-glycerol-3-phosphate, phenylalanine, pyridoxine, cordycepin, theobromine, theophylline, xanthine, indole-3-propionic acid, capric acids, tricaprin, α-naphthoflavanone, and methyl hydrogen fumarate were identified as characteristic metabolites of DFW. GABA is widely incorporated into functional food and health products, with reported roles in sleep improvement, stress relief, blood pressure regulation, growth hormone secretion, liver and kidney function, immune modulation, antioxidant activity, and antiaging effects (Zhang et al., 2024). Histamine is a key immune mediator involved in gastric acid secretion, allergic responses, and neurotransmitter modulation. Immune function is dependent on histamine homeostasis (Smolinska et al., 2022). Szczyrba et al. (2024) reported that histamine was detected in bovine milk samples, with its concentration being influenced by the heat-treatment method, fat content, and storage duration after opening. As a byproduct of choline metabolism, dimethylglycine may indirectly support cardiovascular health by regulating homocysteine levels. Previous studies indicated that it may also contribute to antioxidant activity and immune modulation (Liu et al., 2021). Sn-glycerol-3-phosphate regulates insulin secretion and influences appetite, hepatic steatosis, glucose and lipid metabolism, and metabolic stress responses primarily via the ChREBP-FGF21 pathway (Al-Mass et al., 2022). Medium-chain fatty acids such as tricaprin and capric acid are rapidly metabolized in the liver into energy or ketone bodies. Compared with long-chain fatty acids, they are more readily absorbed and digested and are frequently used in weight management and energy supplementation. They may also reduce fat accumulation and support cardiovascular protection (Jadhav & Annapure, 2023). Theobromine and theophylline are xanthine derivatives with bronchodilatory, diuretic, and central nervous system-stimulating properties (Monteiro et al., 2016). Theobromine, commonly found in chocolate, exhibits antioxidant, cardiovascular, and mood-enhancing effects, while theophylline is primarily used in the treatment of asthma and bronchiectasis (Franco et al., 2013). Pyridoxine, a vitamin B6 derivative, is essential for erythropoiesis, neurotransmitter synthesis, and amino acid metabolism. Maintenance of immune and nervous system function is crucial for normal physiological homeostasis (Xu et al., 2025). Cordycepin, an adenosine analog, has nutritional and physiological benefits, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, immunomodulatory, hypolipidemic, enhanced glucose metabolism, and antitumor activities (Ashraf et al., 2020).

The results showed that DFW was enriched with a diverse range of potentially bioactive metabolites and exhibited a higher overall nutritional quality than W. Furthermore, Gao et al. (2024) employed LC–MS-based untargeted metabolomics to characterize the metabolic changes occurring during the fermentation of whey by Kluyveromyces marxianus. In total, 151 differential metabolites were identified, predominantly belonging to the categories of amino acids and peptides, fatty acids and their conjugates, and carbohydrates and their derivatives. As the fermentation time increased, substantial changes were observed in multiple classes of metabolites, as well as in amino acid and lipid metabolic pathways. These findings also suggest that the metabolite profile of DFW may be influenced by both the composition of the raw materials and the processing conditions. Fu et al. (2026) compared the proteolytic degradation patterns and peptide-release profiles of bovine and goat whey protein concentrates during in vitro gastrointestinal digestion. A total of 4434 peptides were identified, with the goat whey digest and bovine whey digest containing substantial numbers of peptides unique to each digest, respectively. The findings indicate that whey proteins from different milk sources possess distinct peptide-release potentials. Further in vitro studies are required to validate the bioactivities of these peptides and to comprehensively evaluate the nutritional quality of DFW. In this study, the peptides and small-molecule metabolites in whey were systematically analyzed. Fig. 8 presents the characteristic nutritional fingerprint of DFW. Dairy fan, a traditional ethnic dairy product originating from Dali, Yunnan, is produced using a unique processing technique that enriches its whey by-product, DFW, with diverse nutrients conferring potential health benefits. This study demonstrates that DFW has considerable potential for further development and utilization, which could enhance the added value of whey by-products. Conclusion

In conclusion, 4012 and 3169 peptides were identified in DFW and W, respectively. Overall, 1451 differential peptides and 26 biological activities were identified. These peptides included anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, uric acid-lowering, and other beneficial physiologically bioactive peptides, with antioxidant and ACE inhibitory peptides representing a major proportion. In general, DFW contained more putative bioactive peptides than W. The antioxidant mechanisms of four characteristic peptides were also examined. The results showed that these peptides bind to Keap1 via salt bridges and hydrogen bonds, thereby regulating the Keap1-Nrf2/ARE pathway and exerting antioxidant effects. The antioxidant activity of DFW was confirmed using DPPH, ABTS, and the hydroxyl radical scavenging assay. In total, 919 metabolites, including 561 differential metabolites and 15 characteristic differential metabolites, were detected in DFW and W using metabolomics. The primary functional roles of these metabolites include immune enhancement, anti-inflammatory activity, antioxidant effects, and cardiovascular protection. These findings clarify the characteristic nutritional fingerprint of DFW, a byproduct of whey processing, and highlight its functional bioactive peptides and characteristic metabolites. In addition to providing new insights into the nutritional value of whey byproducts, these findings serve as a valuable resource for the high-value production and utilization of whey.
