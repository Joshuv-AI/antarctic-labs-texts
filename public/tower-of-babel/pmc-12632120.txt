Genomic islands and plasmid borne antimicrobial resistance genes drive the evolution of high-risk, ST-131 uropathogenic E. coli NS30
Authors: Arun Sai Kumar Peketi, Valakunja Nagaraja, Eswarappa Pradeep Bulagonda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUrinary tract infections (UTIs) caused by Uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC) belonging to global strains such as ST-131 pose a significant health challenge. To understand the evolutionary landscape and molecular mechanisms defining ST-131 UPEC, the complete genome of E. coli NS30 was generated and analyzed.ResultsThe complete genome assembly of E. coli NS30, belonging to high-risk ST-131, C2 subclade, revealed a chromosome and two plasmids. A large conjugative plasmid, pNS30-1, harboured a multi-drug resistance (MDR) cassette within a Tn402-like class 1 integron, which was functionally demonstrated to be transferable. Comparative genomic analysis identified four distinct genomic islands (GIs) that are absent in its closest ST-131 neighbour. Two of these, including a novel pathogenicity island (PAI), were acquired from other E. coli lineages, harbouring Virulence factors (VFs) and efflux pump genes. The remaining two GIs are phage-like elements contributing to genome plasticity.ConclusionsE. coli NS30 is distinct from the other ST-131 UPEC genomes by the acquisition of novel GIs. The presence of GIs, virulence factors and AMR genes in a conjugative MDR plasmid has driven its evolution into a formidable uropathogen with a high potential to spread resistance and virulence traits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12308-z.

Background

Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs) are one of the most common causes of outpatient infections across the globe affecting 50–60% of women at least once in their lifetime [1, 2]. An approximate 404 million UTI cases and 236,790 deaths were reported worldwide in a single calendar year [1]. Among various bacteria responsible for UTIs, Escherichia coli is one of the most frequently reported organisms. Uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) demonstrate a remarkable ability to colonize the urinary tract [3]. Virulence Factors (VFs) encoded by genes located on Pathogenicity Islands (PAIs) are responsible for the colonization and infection potential of the strain [4, 5].

ST-131 UPEC belonging to O25:H4 serotype, are often associated with recurrent UTI cases globally [6]. The clade C2 of ST-131 is linked to persistent UTI and septicemia [7]. The emergence of antimicrobial-resistant (AMR) ST-131 E. coli has led to higher rates of treatment failure [8]. The prevalence of AMR genes, such as blaCTX-M, blaTEM, blaSHV, blaOXA, blaKPC, blaNDM, catB3, aab-6’-Ib-cr among ST-131 has been linked to this phenomenon [8]. These genes confer resistance to beta-lactams, extended spectrum beta-lactams, chloramphenicol, aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones, respectively [9]. Typically, a majority of these genes are plasmid-borne and are flanked by insertion sequences such as ISEcp1, IS903D and IS26 [10], facilitating their mobility and integration into the bacterial genome.

The genome size of E. coli is highly variable. The sizes of E. coli genomes deposited into the NCBI Refseq database, ranged from 4.6 Mbp in the laboratory strain K-12 [11] to 7.6 Mbp in the human fecal isolate RH-045-CS. This variation is attributed to the insertion/deletion of large chromosomal regions known as GIs [12]. PAIs are a subclass of GIs that carry an array of virulence factors [13]. The maintenance of GIs is often linked to the presence of Toxin-Antitoxin (TA) systems within these regions [14, 15]. TA systems are widespread across bacterial chromosomes, plasmids, and PAIs. TA modules are implicated in the stabilization of GIs, transposons, and Integrative and Conjugative Elements (ICEs), playing a critical role in bacterial evolution and pathogenicity.

Several studies have documented the diversity and genomic features of ST-131 strains [16–18], suggesting that recombination is a major driver of evolution among them. However, limited information is available on the role of GI migrations and interaction of ST-131 with other strain types. Unambiguous documentation of phylogenomic origins of the elements contributing to the emergence of ST-131 is needed. To address these, we performed phylogenomic analysis of a new MDR uropathogenic E. coli strain NS30. We have analysed its complete genome for the presence of prophages, AMR genes, plasmids and demonstrated its conjugal transfer. Among UPEC belonging to ST-131 strain type, NS30 presented a unique genomic signature by its acquisition of GIs leading us to explore the evolutionary path of NS30 to become a successful uropathogen.

Methods

Bacterial isolation, identification and antibiotic susceptibility test (AST)

NS30 bacterial strain was isolated from the mid-stream urine of a 63-year-old female patient suffering from renal calculi at a tertiary care hospital in India on 20th September 2017. Identification and AST of the isolate were done by Vitek-2 (GN-280) and GN-281 AST card respectively, as per CLSI guidelines [19]. The organism was identified as E. coli.

DNA extraction and whole genome sequencing (WGS)

A single colony of the bacteria was inoculated in 3 ml of MH broth (Muller Hinton broth, Himedia—M391) and incubated overnight at 37 °C and 180 rpm. DNA was extracted using Macherey Nagel Nucleospin Tissue DNA extraction kit (Cat no—740,952) according to Manufacturer’s instructions. Short read and long read sequences were obtained by Illumina and Nanopore sequencing respectively. Short read Illumina sequencing was employed as described previously [15], with minor modifications. Briefly, libraries were prepared using NEBNext Ultra II DNA library preparation kit (E7645S). The pooled libraries were analyzed using Qubit2 and Agilent bioanalyzer DNA 1000 kit (5067–1504). Multiplexed libraries were sequenced using Illumina HiSeq X. Bcl2fastq (v2.2, RRID:SCR_015058) was used to demultiplex the sequences. The sequencing libraries for long read sequencing were prepared using SQK-LSK09 ligation sequencing kit [20]. Data was collected from the FLO-MIN106 flow cell. Base calling was performed using Guppy (v5.0.1), with high-accuracy base calling model. LongQC, nanoplot and Fastp were used for QC checking and adapter trimming for both long and short reads respectively [21–23].

Genome assembly and annotation

The trimmed and QC passed reads were assembled by Unicycler [24] into a hybrid assembly. QUAST was used to check the quality of the hybrid assembly. Busco (v3.0.2) [25] completeness check of the genome was carried out using “gamaproteobacteria_odb9” dataset. The hybrid assembly was annotated by Prokka [26] using ‘compliant’ and ‘addgenes’ flags under default settings. A custom concatenated E. coli Genbank file was generated using all the available complete and chromosome level assemblies. This was parsed as a reference using “proteins” flag in Prokka to annotate the E. coli NS30 genome. AMR gene data was obtained using SraX [27] with RGI-CARD database [28]. Virulence factors were analyzed using Abricate [29] with VFDB database [30]. Genomes were visualized using Artemis Genome Viewer [31]. The circular map of NS30’s chromosome was plotted using the Pycirclize tool (Version 0.1.0) [32].

Phylogenetic analysis, genome alignment, comparative genome (CG) analysis and MLST

Whole-genome-based phylogeny was inferred using SANS serif [33]. Default settings of the tool were used to infer the phylogeny. Whole genome phylogeny was preferred over MLST based, as it considers the entire genome of all isolates to build a phylogeny unlike MLST, which utilizes the sequence of only few genes. For ST-131 population structure analysis, all ST-131 genomes were aligned to EC958 reference, as mentioned in earlier studies [34, 35] and SNPs were called using snippy [36]. The core SNP phylogeny was generated using snippy-core while recombination was predicted and masked using Gubbins [37]. The final recombination-masked phylogeny was inferred using Iqtree [38]. Further, the fimH gene typing was performed using fimtyper [39]. Phylogenetic tree was visualized using Interactive Tree of Life (iToL)v6 [40]. ProgressiveMauve [41] was employed to perform whole genome alignment and the alignments were exported using PyGenomeViz v1.6.1 [42]. GIs were predicted using Treasure island [43]. Multi Locus Sequence Typing (MLST) of the assemblies was determined using pubMLST [44]. Serotype was deduced using SeroType finder [45] and Phyloclade was assigned by Clermon Typing web server [46]. Plasmid analysis was carried out by Plasmid Finder [47] and transposon analysis by Tnfinder [48]. Similar/identical GIs were identified by NCBI-BLAST using default settings [49]. Top three NCBI BLAST hits were chosen for comparison, unless mentioned otherwise. Genegraphics [50] tool was used to visualize the aligned GIs. Pairwise nucleotide comparisons were generated using clincker [51].

Conjugal transfer

Horizontal transferability of AMR genes encoded by E. coli NS30 was assessed after broth mating with a recipient strain E. coli J53AziR, a sodium-azide resistant and plasmid free strain, as described previously [52]. Briefly, exponentially growing cultures of E. coli NS30 and E. coli J53AziR (500 µl each, 0.6 at OD600nm) were mixed and incubated for 12 h without shaking. Four random transconjugant colonies were selected on MacConkey agar (Himedia-M082) plates supplemented with Ampicillin—100 µg/ml (SRL—61,314), Ciprofloxacin—8 µg/ml (TCI – C2510) and Sodium Azide—100 µg/ml (Merck—822,335). Sub-culturing in LB broth (Himedia—M1245) supplemented with Ampicillin (100 µg/ml), Ciprofloxacin (8 µg/ml) and Sodium Azide (100 µg/ml) was carried out. DNA from these cultures was extracted and subjected to WGS. The transfer of plasmids and AMR genes was confirmed by WGS data analysis.

Results

General characteristics of NS30 genome

The de novo assembly of E. coli NS30 genome resulted in three contigs representing one chromosome (5,150,956 bp) and two plasmids, namely pNS30-1 (124,906 bp) and pNS30-2 (4,082 bp). The hybrid genome assembly of NS30 had a total length of 5,279,944 bp and an average sequencing depth of 585X. BUSCO analysis yielded a 100% coverage of single copy orthologs indicating a high level of contiguity and completeness. NS30 genome has 4,982 protein encoding genes, 22rRNA genes spread across 7 operons, each containing one of 16S, 23S and 5S rRNA and an additional copy of 5S ribosomal rRNA and 89 tRNA genes.

Diversity of E. coli strains

To evaluate the genetic architecture of E. coli NS30, in the context of UPEC strains, we first carried out a whole genome based phylogenetic analysis of 3,157 complete and chromosome level assemblies of E. coli retrieved from NCBI-Refseq database (as of 15th August 2023, Fig. 1a). Assessment of the overall population structure in the phylogeny, revealed that strain types such as ST38 and ST11 represented clonal expansions, which are represented by monophyletic clades. In comparison, ST131 genomes displayed a remarkable diversity. While exhibiting a larger spread in a non-clustering pattern, ST131 genomes were represented by multiple clades with limited branching. This pattern is representative of the heterogeneity that exists in the ST131 pan genome probably driven by acquisition of GIs. Implementation of Heap’s law on the pan genome data of ST131 resulted in a gamma value of 0.258, indicating that ST131 E. coli has an open pan genome. The NS30 genome was phylogenetically adjacent to E. coli H105, GN02770, GN03344, KE21 and C102 genomes (Fig. 1a). Of these, E. coli H105 isolated from a vaginal swab of a patient in Germany emerged as the closest phylogenetic neighbour of NS30 with an Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) of 99.84%. Among these five strains, E. coli GN03344, KE21 and H105 belong to ST-131 whereas E. coli GN02270 and C102 belong to ST-69 and ST-295, respectively. ST69 and ST295 E. coli were also reported to be causative agents of UTIs and other infections [53, 54]. Comparative analysis with the H105 genome revealed the presence of four unique genomic regions in NS30, which are absent in H105 (Fig. 1b). According to the pubMLST Achtman classification, the study strain belongs to the ST-131 strain type. Recombination masked core SNP phylogeny placed NS30 under the sub-clade C2 (Supplementary Fig. 1) within the ST-131 phylogeny. NS30 harbours a plethora of VFs and TA systems.

Uropathogens rely on VFs for the successful establishment of an infection. VFs ensure bacterial adhesion, infection and survival, especially in nutrient-deprived conditions. Urinary tract is one such niche conducive for successful adhesion, biofilm formation and colonization by the bacteria [55]. NS30 possessed 190 VFs involved in adherence, secretion systems, invasion, motility, immune modulation, exotoxin, invasion, motility, and nutritional/metabolic factor regulation. Among the various adherence factors in pathogenic E. coli only fim operon encoding for fimbriae was intact in the strain, whereas pap and afa operons which encode for pylonephritis-associated pili and afimbrial adhesins respectively were incomplete in the organism. Intact flg and fli operons were found which encode motility factors. Siderophores aid bacteria in the acquisition of iron, a vital metal ion required for bacterial survival. Operons such as sit, iuc, chu, ent, fep and ybt, responsible for siderophore function and iron metabolism are found in NS30 genome along with the master regulator, ferric uptake regulator encoded by fur. Ag43 is a well-characterized VF involved in biofilm formation and agglutination of bacteria [56]. Two copies of ag43 were present in the NS30 chromosome located in GI-1 and GI-4, along with extra copies of fimC, fimD in GI-4.

TA systems are ubiquitous small operons that play a crucial role in the maintenance and segregation of MGEs such as plasmids, GIs and prophages [57, 58]. NS30 harboured 20 TA systems, 16 of which are located on its chromosome and four on the larger plasmid pNS30-1. The 16 chromosomal TA systems consisted of two type I, eight type II, four type IV and one type V systems. Four pairs of yeeVU, a type IV TA system, were located in the chromosome of NS30. Two of them were located within GI-1 and GI-4. Type II TA systems are well-known for their association with plasmids and are responsible for maintaining plasmid stability through post-segregational killing [59]. All four TA systems located on the larger plasmid pNS30-1 belonged to type II. Surprisingly, the NS30 genome possessed two copies of the Type II toxin parE, one each on its plasmid pNS30-1 and the chromosome. The corresponding anti-toxin parD was absent in both loci. Normally, the toxin and antitoxin genes exist together as antitoxin negates the toxic effects of the toxin by binding to it in the case of type II TA modules. Upon comparison with functional ParE from TA database (TADB), it was apparent that both the copies of parE in NS30 harboured multiple non-synonymous mutations, thus accounting for the existence of the non-functional orphan parEs. These mutant toxins are likely the vestigial remnants of previous horizontal transactions between different strains of E. coli. Such remnant ParE toxins were found in other E. coli genomes as well. The large contingent of TA systems present in NS30 may imply multiple events of Horizontal Gene Transfers (HGTs) into the chromosome of NS30 over the course of evolution.

pNS30-1 is a conjugative F1A type plasmid

Among the two plasmids present in the genome, pNS30-1 is a 123 kb conjugative plasmid belonging to the IncF1A incompatibility group (Fig. 2a) [60]. pNS30-2 is the smaller plasmid with a size of 4 kb, harbouring only three ORFs. Among these three, one ORF encoded the replication initiation protein and other two genes encoded for MobQ and MobC relaxases. These three genes form the core genome of IncQ plasmids and are essential for plasmid replication [61]. The larger plasmid pNS30-1 harboured 143 annotated CDS, including the four TA systems and multiple AMR genes: blaOXA-1, sul1, aadA5, aac-6`-Ib-cr, catB3 and dfrA17 (Fig. 2b). True to the nature of F plasmids, pNS30-1 harboured all the genes required for conjugal transfer. These include tra operon genes, which are responsible for the formation of F pili and transfer of plasmid DNA during conjugation. To test the transferability of pNS30-1, conjugal transfer experiments were carried out using a sodium-azide resistant, plasmid-free E. coli J53AziR recipient. WGS analysis of four random transconjugants confirmed the presence of pNS30-1, while pNS30-2 was absent in them (Supplementary data 1). Vitek 2 analysis identified E. coli NS30 as a multidrug resistant (MDR) strain exhibiting resistance to Ampicillin, Ciprofloxacin, Nalidixic acid and Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Multiple AMR genes in pNS30-1 are organized within a Tn402 type class I integron. Flanked by IS26 transposases, this integron possesses the ability to disseminate AMR genes [62]. Since the integron can stably integrate into the bacterial chromosome, we screened the genomes of Enterobacteriaceae for its presence. A highly similar, but inverted homolog of the integron was found in the chromosome of Morganella morganii. A similar cassette with minor variations was found in the chromosome of another E. coli. Similar cassettes were observed in the chromosomes of Proteus mirabilis, Salmonella enterica and Klebsiella pneumoniae. The presence of this integron in the chromosome of E. coli ST-542 genome YSP8-1 is a pointer to the integron’s ability to integrate chromosomally among E. coli populations (Fig. 2b).

Comparative resistome and virulome analysis of ST-131 E. coli

To understand the diversity of the AMR genes among ST-131 E. coli, we carried out comparative resistome analysis on 226 ST-131 isolates (Supplementary Table 1). The most frequently occurring AMR genes were sul1 (117/226), tetA (108/226) and aadA5 (104/226). NS30 harboured both sul1 and aadA5, but did not harbour tetA. blaOXA-1 was present only in 32 ST-131 genomes. A comparative virulome analysis involving 25 ST-131 UPEC was carried out to gain insights into the evolution of virulence among these strains. A conserved nature of virulome across all the studied ST131 UPEC was observed. All ST-131 isolates harboured the hemolysin E toxin gene hlyE. It encodes for a pore-forming toxin which can lyse mammalian erythrocytes [63]. Similar to NS30, 23 of the 25 tested ST-131 UPEC isolates lacked a complete pap operon. E. coli S65EC was the only ST-131 UPEC isolate harbouring a complete pap and alpha hemolysin operons. The absence of a complete pap operon suggests the reliance of ST131 strains on fimbriae encoded by fim operon for adhesion to host epithelial cells.

Structural features of GIs integrated into E. coli NS30 chromosome

Next, to assess the origins of the NS30 genome, its GIs were analyzed. GIs play crucial roles in bacterial evolution, virulence, antimicrobial resistance and niche specific adaptations [64]. In its pan genome, NS30 harbours 35 GIs. Four of these GIs are unique acquisitions relative to the closest phylogenetic neighbour E. coli H105 (Fig. 1b). These four GIs were named GI-1, GI-2, GI-3 and GI-4 and respectively. A detailed summary of their size, location, key genes, and proposed origins is provided in Table 1. Along with ORFs of unknown function, these GIs harboured TA systems, prophages, VFs, efflux pumps and IS elements. To understand the phylogenetic origins of these GIs and their contribution in shaping the NS30 as an MDR uropathogen, comparative analysis was carried out with E. coli genomes harbouring similar/identical GIs. The absence of GI-1 and GI-4 in the analyzed 226 ST-131 genomes, indicates that the genome of NS30 has acquired these GIs from other strain types. Among the four unique GIs in NS30 genome, GI-1 is the largest and is 57.5-kb long, spanning from 45,076 bp to 102,615 bp. GI-1 is inserted into a specific tRNA-Ser locus, which is post-translationally converted to tRNA-Sec [65]. The GI consisted of IS elements, emrE, qacN efflux pump genes, yeeV/yeeU type IV TA system and several ORFs of unknown function. Surprisingly, GI-1 was absent in all the five phylogenetic neighbours of NS30 studied. Based on CG analysis, the phylogenetic origins of GI-1 were traced to E. coli SCPMO-B-9427, FDAARGOS_1283, and SCU-176 genomes, belonging to ST678, ST144 and ST550 respectively. These genomes were phylogenetically distant to NS30 (Fig. 1a). While ST144 and ST550 are often associated with UTI, ST678 is frequently reported in Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS) [66, 67]. There are two copies of IS3 and one copy of IS4 elements present in GI-1 sequences of the compared genomes. NS30’s GI-1 additionally possessed one copy of each of these transposases, further increasing the mobility of GI-1 from NS30 to other bacteria. The second GI, GI-2 is an Enterobacteria phage mEp460. It is a 42.6-Kb long lambdoid phage spanning from 2,055,020 bp—2,097,673 bp on NS30 chromosome. It is integrated adjacent to a tRNA-Ser locus. This phage is disseminated among E. coli spread across clinical, seawater and marine environments [68, 69]. The dicB gene in lambdoid phages confers superinfection immunity [70]. It functions as a protective mechanism for host bacteria from additional phage infections by inhibiting the expression of inner membrane receptor proteins [70]. However, dicB is found to be insertionally inactivated by an IS3 transposase in GI-2 of NS30. Comparison of NS30’s GI-2 with homologs from E. coli IPCEC42, C410P1 and O25b:h4, indicated its conserved nature, except for dicB being functional in the latter genomes. A notable point is the presence of two pairs of attR and attL sites flanking GI-2 in all the four genomes. GI-4 is a 44.9-kb PAI. It is located between 4,570,302 bp—4,615,293 bp in NS30 genome and is inserted into a tRNA-Phe locus. A xerD recombinase was identified at the 5’ of the GI-4, adjacent to the aforementioned tRNA. Adherence related genes such as papB, papX, fimC, AfaD, Afa-III and fimD and VFs like ag43 were found in GI-4, along with a type IV TA system yeeV/yeeU. The fim genes found in GI-4 are in addition to those found in the intact fim operon harboured by NS30. Presence of these pathogenicity contributors in GI-4 suggests that PAIs play an important role in uropathogenesis of the strain. Deletion of GI-4 homologs from other UPEC resulted in reduced pathogenicity, confirming the contribution of this PAI to the pathogenic potential of bacteria [72]. These deletion mutants failed to establish UTI in murine urinary tracts and showed reduced adherence to human uroepithelial cells [72]. As GI-4 was absent from all the analyzed ST-131 isolates, we explored its origin and traced it to a few ST38 genomes, namely E. coli GTEN-97, RIVM_C036569 and RIVM_C019217. Surprisingly, ST38 belongs to Enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC). ST38 gained its prominence as an emerging strain by contributing to the spread of blaOXA-244 [73]. Homologs of GI-4 from the aforementioned ST38 genomes harboured multiple IS elements, while GI-4 in NS30 possessed two of these IS elements (IS66 and IS31) (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 5) [74]. Upon further investigation, it was apparent that GI-4 is a part of 110 Kb long genomic region, typical to ST38 genomes. Adjacent to the tRNA-Phe, this 110 Kb region comprised two adjacent GIs, with two distinct tyrosine-type recombinase integrases. A parDE TA module separated both these GIs. Only the proximal part of this 110 Kb genomic region, comprised of GI-4 and the orphan parE toxin migrated into NS30 (Fig. 6b). Discussion

Rapid emergence and dissemination of drug-resistant strains of bacteria has been a daunting challenge for the global clinical fraternity. Evolution of MDR strains severely impacts the viability of available treatment regimens. Multiple reasons contribute to the emergence of drug-resistant pathogens. While developing new treatment regimens and discovery of new drugs for infectious diseases remain high-priority research, efforts are simultaneously being made to trace the origins of new MDR strains. In this manuscript, we have investigated the origin of ST131 MDR UPEC strain NS30, isolated from a tertiary care hospital.

E. coli ST131 are known for their global spread and their association with AMR gene transmission. From Fig. 1a, it is evident that, unlike many other STs, the ST131 strains are spread across the phylogenetic spectra of E. coli, indicating the diversity in its pan genome. Thus, in contrast to other STs, shorter clades and multiple branches of origins are seen, indicating large scale genomic rearrangements. Accordingly, a substantial variation in the genomic architecture of NS30 is seen when compared to its nearest phylogenetic neighbour E. coli H105, an already characterized uropathogen. While retaining a major set of features of the H105 genome, NS30 has evolved into a unique ST-131 strain by acquiring GIs from other strain types. Some of these acquired features could contribute to the robust transmissibility of these GIs from NS30 to other genomes, potentially aiding in the spread of virulence and AMR.

Presence of a conjugative MDR plasmid has been a characteristic feature of ST-131 UPEC strains. NS30 also harboured an MDR plasmid pNS30-1 which carried multiple AMR genes. While pNS30-1 belongs to the F1A incompatibility group, other ST131 UPEC harbour F-plasmids of different incompatibility groups or other conjugative plasmids. Notably, AMR genes were predominantly plasmid-encoded among all the analyzed ST-131 strains, ensuring their robust transmissibility. However, variations are found in the number and type of AMR genes carried by them among ST-131 strains. In NS30, as in many other ST-131 strains, all the AMR genes are a part of Tn402 type class I integron which is stably integrated into the plasmid. The stable integration of integron carrying AMR cassette into the plasmid is a signature trait of ST-131 strains. However, the presence of Tn402 integron chromosomally in other E. coli strains and members of Enterobacteriaceae indicates alternate modes of transmission. The association of an AMR integron with F plasmids or other conjugative plasmids raises the tantalizing possibility of a persistent inheritance and transmission. The integration into F plasmids or its variants appears to be a ‘fail safe’ option for the integrons to ensure their definitive spread across bacteria.

The emergence of NS30 was also investigated using the GI acquisition events as markers of HGT mediated evolution. Presence of an intact superinfection immunity conferring gene dicB in lambdoid phage mEp460 among genomes carrying both GI-2 and GI-3 suggests that GI-3 acquisition preceded the infection and integration of mEp460. Further, co-occurrence of GI-2 and GI-3 having the two phages in many ST-131 UPEC genomes suggests that their acquisition preceded the acquisition of other GIs. The insertional inactivation of dicB in NS30’s GI-2 indicates that NS30 is amenable to further phage infections. The absence of GI-1 in any of the analyzed ST-131 genomes suggests that the acquisition of GI-1 is a later event in NS30’s evolution.

In contrast to other GIs of NS30, GI-4 harboured multiple VFs, which may contribute to the uropathogenic potential of the strain. It also has a distant origin which could be traced to ST38 strains. EAEC ST38 strains carrying blaOXA-244 gene were reported in 2016 in Europe. Unlike ST131, ST38 genomes present a true clonal population structure in their phylogeny. All ST38 genomes trace back to a single branch on the E. coli phylogeny and are characterized by long branches and extended branching patterns (Fig. 1a). Also, the pangenome of ST38 is less diverse compared to that of ST131. Since GI-4 of NS30 was traced to ST38, its arrival in NS30 genome must have been a recent event in the evolution of NS30 (Fig. 7). Absence of GI-4 in other ST131 UPEC strains further substantiates this hypothesis. Most notably, the adherence genes are found in the proximal GI of PAI found in ST38 strains. This proximal GI was probably acquired by the NS30 genome (Fig. 6b). Instead of acquiring the entire PAI consisting of both the GIs, only the necessary and adequate part of it has been acquisitioned, in the emergence of NS30 genome. Conclusions

Exploring the origin and emergence of the E. coli NS30 strain revealed the continuous diversification and evolution of ST131 genomes in contrast to several other monophyletic ST types. Emergence of the new strain can be attributed to acquisitions of GIs and MGEs. Different GIs from UPEC, EAEC and Entero-Invasive as well as Extra-intestinal pathogenic E. coli appear to mobilize into recipient genomes to evolve into new drug-resistant variants. Such a facile mobilization of genomic elements due to the inadequate “immigration control” has facilitated the expansion of the pan genomes of the MDR strains of E. coli. Their porous borders enable facile acquisition of new GIs and MGEs as exemplified in the case of NS30. The functional conjugative plasmids aid in further expansion of the already open pan genome of ST-131 making it a reservoir of AMR genes and VFs. A comprehensive analysis of substantial number of ST-131 genomes should aid in understanding genome plasticity and pan genome variations in these clinically relevant MDR strains of E. coli.
