Association between cumulative symptom burden and cognitive impairment among breast cancer survivors: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Xiaotong Li, Kaitlin Lampson, Kevin T Liou, Yuelin Li, Susan Q Li, Tim Ahles, James Root, Jun J Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI) is a multifaceted symptom associated with various comorbid conditions, complicating management. This study aims to evaluate cumulative symptom burden and its impact on both subjective and objective CRCI in breast cancer survivors.MethodEnrollment data were analyzed from a randomized clinical trial involving women with stage 0–III breast cancer, free of oncology disease, reporting moderate or greater CRCI and insomnia. Subjective CRCI was measured using the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy–Cognitive Function (FACT-Cog), and objective CRCI with the Hopkins Verbal Learning Test–Revised (HVLT). Comorbid symptoms (insomnia, fatigue, pain, anxiety, depression) were assessed with validated self-report tools. A Cumulative Symptoms Score (CSS) was calculated to quantify symptom burden. Multivariable linear regression was used to examine associations with CRCI, adjusting for co-variables.ResultsAmong 260 participants (mean age 56.6 years), 21% were non-White and 10% Hispanic. Fatigue (90%) was the most common, followed by insomnia (70%), pain (45%), anxiety (35%), and depression (12%). Higher CSS was significantly associated with worse subjective CRCI after adjustment for age and chemotherapy (Coef. = -3.0, 95% CI: -3.6 to -2.4, p < 0.001). Each comorbid symptom was correlated with subjective CRCI (all p < 0.001). CSS was not associated with objective cognitive performance (p = 0.58), although insomnia correlated with HVLT scores (r = -0.20; p < 0.001).ConclusionCumulative symptom burden was associated with subjective but not objective CRCI. Comprehensive symptom management has the potential to improve subjective appraisal of CRCI. Sleep disturbance may be a therapeutic target for improving objective outcomes.Keywords: Breast cancer, Cognitive impairment, Fatigue, Insomnia, Survivorship, Symptom management

Introduction

Cancer related cognitive dysfunction (CRCI), involving symptoms such as memory loss, attention deficits, slowed processing speed, and executive function difficulties [1, 2], affects 60–75% of breast cancer survivors [1, 3]. Over half of breast cancer survivors experience persistent CRCI [1, 4, 5], lasting over 10–20 years after treatment completion [6, 7]. This negatively impacts the quality of life among breast cancer survivors, particularly in their daily tasks and social relationships [2, 6]. Of more concern, CRCI is associated with an up to 40% increase in risk for the discontinuation of hormonal therapy in breast cancer survivors [8], increasing the risk of cancer recurrence and mortality [9, 10]. However, CRCI still remains inadequately managed and insufficiently discussed among clinicians and patients [11], primarily due to limited knowledge, challenges in diagnosis, and the lack of evidence-based interventions [12].

Effectively addressing CRCI requires recognizing its complex and multifaceted nature [13], as it may be influenced by and frequently coexist with other cancer-related symptoms, such as insomnia, fatigue, pain, anxiety, and depression. Among these, insomnia is a prominent comorbidity [14], affecting up to 62% of breast cancer survivors [15]. A recent study (N = 1072) demonstrated a significant association between CRCI and insomnia in breast cancer survivors, with 72% of participants with severe insomnia reporting moderate or greater cognitive impairment [16]. CRCI is considered a major consequence of insufficient sleep, impacting psychomotor and cognitive speed, vigilance and executive attention, working memory, and executive abilities [17]. Additionally, other physical and psychological symptoms, such as pain, fatigue, anxiety, and depression are all common among breast cancer survivors and have been shown to be associated with both cognitive impairment and sleep problems independently [15, 18, 19].

Although associations between CRCI and individual symptoms have been reported, less is known about the cumulative impact of these co-occurring symptoms on cognitive outcomes. Furthermore, current epidemiological studies evaluating CRCI and its accompanying symptoms have largely been conducted in the general breast cancer survivor population, rather than among patients who report clinically significant insomnia and cognitive impairment, a subgroup that may experience particularly high symptom burden and is more likely to seek clinical care. In addition, limited research has evaluated the relationship between cumulative symptom burden and both subjective and objective measures of cognition within this population.

Evaluating cognitive conditions and symptom burden among breast cancer survivors reporting insomnia and CRCI is crucial for identifying symptom profiles within this population and improving the efficiency of CRCI diagnosis through targeted symptom screening [20]. Furthermore, exploring the impact of comorbid symptoms on CRCI may help clinicians leverage existing interventions that target co-occurring symptoms to indirectly alleviate cognitive complaints. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the prevalence of both subjective and objective CRCI, as well as comorbid symptoms, among breast cancer survivors with self-reported insomnia and CRCI. Additionally, we sought to assess the impact of cumulative symptom burden on subjective and objective CRCI. Our findings may contribute to identifying distinct symptom profiles within this population and to the development of novel, targeted interventions for managing CRCI.

Methods

Study design, participants, and procedures

The current study was conducted using enrollment data from an ongoing 3-arm parallel randomized controlled trial ENHANCE (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04837820; Registration Date: April 2021), which started in 2021. It compares acupuncture against sham acupuncture and a wait list control among breast cancer survivors with insomnia related CRCI. The institutional review board at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center approved this trial (IRB number: 20–124).

Adult survivors with a diagnosis of stage 0 to III breast cancer were eligible to participate if they met the following criteria: (1) English-speaking; (2) free of oncologic disease by clinical examination or history (3) reported moderate or greater perceived CRCI as indicated by a score of “quite a bit” or “very much” on at least one of the two items that specifically assess concentration and memory problems on the EORTC QLQ-C30 [version 3.0]) [21]; (4) reported a worsening of cognitive function since their cancer diagnosis by answering “Yes” to all of the following questions: a) Do you think or feel that your memory or mental ability has gotten worse since your cancer diagnosis? b)Do you think your mind isn’t as sharp now as it was before your cancer diagnosis? c) Do you feel like these problems have made it harder to function on your job or take care of things around the home? (5) reported insomnia symptoms, identified as a score of eight or greater on the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) [22].

We excluded patients if they (1) completed active cancer treatment (surgery, chemotherapy, and/or radiation therapy) within one month prior to study enrollment; (2) had their most recent breast cancer diagnosis more than 10 years ago; (3) were diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease, vascular dementia, Parkinson disease, or other organic brain disorders; (4) had a score of ≥ 10 on the Blessed Orientation-Memory-Concentration (BOMC) screening instrument [23]; (5) had pre-existing, uncorrectable visual or auditory impairment that would preclude ability to complete the assessments; (6) reported a primary psychiatric disorder not in remission; (7) changed their hormonal or targeted therapies for the past 4 weeks or planned to initiate or change them in the coming 8 weeks; (8) altered the dose of somnogenic medications (e.g., hypnotics, sedatives, antidepressants) in the past 8 weeks prior to study enrollment.

After the initial screening, trained research staff conducted interviews with potential participants to confirm their eligibility. Eligible participants provided informed consent and completed enrollment assessments, which included demographics (e.g., age, race, education), clinical characteristics (e.g., cancer stage, years since diagnosis, cancer treatments), and symptom burden measures (e.g., insomnia, CRCI, pain). This analysis included 260 participants who completed enrollment assessment.

Study variables

Outcomes

Patient-reported subjective cognitive impairment: We used the perceived cognitive impairment (PCI) subscale from the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy–Cognitive Function (FACT-Cog), version 3, to assess CRCI by patient self-report. FACT-Cog is one of the most widely recognized and commonly used self-reported instruments in both research and in clinical settings to evaluate cognitive function in adult cancer patients [24, 25]. The FACT-Cog’s 37 items yield four subscales: perceived cognitive impairment, perceived cognitive abilities, impact on quality of life, and comments from others [25, 26]. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 “Never” or “Not at all” to 4 “Several times a day” or “Very much” over the past 7 days. For this analysis, we focused on the total score of the PCI subscale (Cronbach’s α 0.94) [27], which ranges from 0 to 72, with lower score indicating greater perceived cognitive impairment. A clinically meaningful cutoff score suggesting potential perceived cognitive impairment in female breast cancer survivors is below 54 [28] (subjective CRCI).

Standardized objective cognitive performance: The Hopkins Verbal Learning Test – Revised (HVLT-R) [29] was used to provide a standardized measure of cognitive performance. The HVLT-R is a widely-used verbal learning and memory test that is recommended by the International Cognition and Cancer Task Force (ICCTF) guidelines and has high validity and test-retest reliability [30, 31]. The HVLT-R assesses participants’ free-recall of a list of 12 words from three semantic categories and thus provides a more objective assessment of CRCI thought to be unaffected by patients’ appraisal of their cognitive lapses. Participants repeat this free-recall activity for three subsequent trials, and then once again after a delay period of 20–25 min. The test has six equivalent forms available; to mitigate practice effects, three different forms were selected for this trial and administered at the following timepoints: Week 0, Week 10, and Week 26. For this analysis, we focused on the “Delayed Recall” score (range: 0–12; Cronbach’s α 0.94) [32, 33], which is the number of words recalled in the single delay trial. We used published norms[34] provided in the HVLT-R/BVMT-R Software Portfolio (Version 1.10.031; Psychological Assessment Resources, Lutz, FL) to convert raw Delayed Recall scores to age-matched T-scores. T-scores below 40 were considered to indicate below average cognitive performance (objective CRCI).

Covariates

Insomnia – Insomnia Severity Index (ISI): The ISI, a 7-item self-report screen for insomnia, was used to assess patient sleep difficulties with Cronbach’s α of 0.90 [35]. The ISI has been validated in cancer populations [35], and clinically-meaningful cutoffs have been established for scores, which range from 0 to 28: no or mild insomnia: 0–14; moderate insomnia: 15–21; and severe insomnia: 22–28 [36].

Fatigue – Brief Fatigue Inventory (BFI) Worst Fatigue: The BFI is a valid self-report questionnaire with Cronbach’s α of 0.96 [37]. It consists of 9 items, including one item that assesses a participants’ worst fatigue over the past 24 h on a scale of 0 (no fatigue) to 10 (as bad as you can imagine) [37]. The BFI Worst Fatigue score (range:0–10) is a commonly-used proxy for patient fatigue severity, and is recommended by the recent American Society of Clinical Oncology [38] and Society for Integrative Oncology guidelines for the assessment of cancer-related fatigue [39]. Clinically meaningful cutoffs have been established: no or mild fatigue: 0–3; moderate fatigue: 4–6; and severe fatigue: 7–10 [37].

Pain: Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System – Global Health (PROMIS – Global Health) Pain Item: The PROMIS Global Health scale is a 10-item scale that includes a single-item measure for pain intensity. The pain item asks participants to rate their average pain over the past 7 days on a scale from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable), with the following cutoffs: 0–3 (none to mild), 4–6 (moderate), and 7–10 (severe). This item has been validated for use in diverse populations, [40] demonstrates good test-retest reliability, [41] and has shown adequate responsiveness in detecting clinically meaningful changes in pain in longitudinal studies [42].

Anxiety – Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale – Anxiety (HADS-A): The HADS scale, which has been validated in several populations, including cancer patients. It is a widely-used self-report questionnaire that assesses anxiety and depression with Cronbach’s α of 0.83 (anxiety subscale) and 0.79 (depression subscale) [43, 44]. The HADS-A subscale consists of 7 items and is scored from 0 to 21. Clinically meaningful score cutoffs have been established: no or mild anxiety: 0–7; moderate anxiety: 8–10; severe anxiety: 11–21 [45].

Depression – Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale – Depression (HADS-D): The HADS-D subscale is a 7-item questionnaire (score range: 0–21) that has been validated for assessing depression in the cancer population [46]. The clinically meaningful cutoffs for this subscale are: no or mild depression: 0–7; moderate depression: 8–10; and severe depression: 11–21 [45].

Cumulative Symptom Burden– Cumulative Symptoms Score (CSS): The CSS was calculated based on the severity of the five comorbid symptoms described above: insomnia, fatigue, pain, anxiety, and depression. A score was assigned to each symptom based on the cutoff values for severity as described above: 0 = no or mild, 1 = moderate, and 2 = severe. For each patient, we calculated a total score by summing the severity scores of their individual comorbid symptoms, which reflects the patient’s cumulative symptom burden. The cumulated symptom score ranges from 0 to 10 with higher scores indicating a greater symptom burden.

Demographic and Clinical Factors: We collected demographic and clinical data at enrollment from patients to characterize the study population and identify potential confounders. Demographic variables included age, race, ethnicity, education, employment, and marital status. Clinical variables included cancer stage, years since cancer diagnosis, the history of chemotherapy and radiation, and the current use of hormone therapy.

Statistical analysis

Prior to data modeling, descriptive analyses were performed to summarize patients’ subjective cognitive impairment, objective cognitive performance, and other comorbid symptoms. Pearson correlations were used to examine the relationship between each comorbid symptom and cognition. The strength of correlation was interpreted using the following cut-off points: 0.0 to 0.1 indicates a negligible correlation, 0.1 to 0.39 a weak correlation, 0.4 to 0.69 a moderate correlation, 0.7 to 0.89 a strong correlation, and 0.9 to 1.0 a very strong correlation [47].

A central concept in this study is the use of the CSS cumulative symptom scores which represents the cumulative severity of five common comorbid symptoms, as an important covariate cognitive outcomes. The rationale for using the CSS, rather than individual symptoms one at a time, rests on a common finding in the symptom cluster literature[48–50] that comorbid symptoms typically cooccur at a high prevalence in cancer survivors. Our own descriptive analyses also showed the same pattern. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Differences in subjective and objective cognitive outcomes across demographic and clinical characteristics were evaluated using independent-samples t-tests for binary variables and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for variables with more than two categories. Univariate linear regression models were fitted by regressing the cognitive outcomes on the cumulated symptom score as the covariate. Additionally, multivariate regression models were fitted to include potential covariates, including clinical variables (e.g., previous cancer treatments and cancer stage) and other characteristics (e.g., age and ethnicity) at enrollment. These covariates were identified a priori in the ENHANCE study protocol based on their potential associations with cognitive outcomes in breast cancer survivors. Characteristics associated with cognitive outcomes at a significance level of p < 0.2 in univariable analyses were considered for inclusion as independent variables in the multivariable linear regression model. Clinical judgment was also applied in the selection process to account for collinearity; for example, when two characteristics were highly associated (e.g., cancer stage and chemotherapy usage), only one was included in the model. A sensitivity analysis additionally included education as a covariate in the multivariable model for objective cognitive performance. All analyses were two-sided with a p-value of less than 0.05 for statistical significance. Cytoscape software[51] was used to visualize the prevalence and correlations of the symptoms. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 26; IBM Corp).

Results

Participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. Among the 260 participants, the mean age was 56.6 years (standard deviation [SD] = 10.4). The majority were White (203, 79.0%), non-Hispanic (233, 90%), and had at least a college education (195, 77.0%). More than half (134, 52.8%) were employed full-time at enrollment. A total of 59.2% of participants had stage 0–I breast cancer, with a mean time since diagnosis of 4.7 years (SD = 2.8). Additionally, 53.5% had undergone chemotherapy, 69.2% had received radiation therapy, and 62.7% were receiving hormonal therapy (tamoxifen or aromatase inhibitors) at the time of enrollment.

Subjective and objective cognition at enrollment

All 260 participants completed the FACT-Cog PCI assessment, with a mean PCI score of 36.0 (SD = 14.0). A total of 230 participants (88.5%) had a PCI score below 54, indicating subjective CRCI that met clinical significance. The HVLT delayed recall assessment was completed by 241 participants, with a mean score of 46.0 (SD = 12.7). Among them, 73 participants (30.3%) had a delayed recall T score below 40, indicating objective CRCI. There was no significant difference in PCI scores between participants with objective CRCI (PCI = 36.2, SD = 13.1) and those without (PCI = 35.8, SD = 15.5) (p = 0.84).

Among the 241 participants who completed both the FACT-Cog PCI and HVLT delayed recall assessments, 65 (27.0%) met criteria for both subjective and objective CRCI, while 148 (61.4%) met criteria for subjective CRCI only. Eight participants (3.3%) demonstrated objective CRCI without clinically significant subjective CRCI, and 20 (8.3%) met criteria for neither subjective nor objective CRCI.

Comorbid symptoms and their correlation with subjective and objective CRCI

Among all participants, fatigue was the most common comorbid symptom, with 90.0% reporting moderate to severe severity. More than half of the participants experienced moderate to severe insomnia (70.0%), followed by pain (45.2%), anxiety (35.4%), and depression (11.5%).

All comorbid symptoms were significantly correlated with subjective CRCI (Fact-Cog PCI scores) (all p < 0.001). Among them, fatigue (r = -0.40), insomnia (r = -0.41), and depression (r = -0.40) showed a moderate negative correlation, while anxiety (r = -0.39) and pain (r = -0.25) indicated a weak, negative correlation. However, only insomnia was significantly correlated with objective CRCI (HVLT delayed recall), showing a weak negative correlation (r = -0.20, p = 0.001). Cumulative symptom burden and its association with subjective and objective CRCI

A total of 259 participants completed all assessments of comorbid symptoms, allowing for the calculation of a CSS score. Among them, the mean CSS was 4.6 (SD = 2.3).

Univariable linear regression analysis revealed a statistically significant association between CSS and FACT-Cog PCI (Coef. = -3.3, 95% CI = -3.9 to -2.7, p < 0.001). To explore potential demographic and clinical factors affecting FACT-Cog PCI, univariable analyses (Table 1) identified possible differences in FACT-Cog PCI based on age, ethnicity, years since cancer diagnosis, cancer stage, chemotherapy history, and current hormonal therapy (all p < 0.20). In multivariable linear regression models including these factors, age and chemotherapy were significantly associated with FACT-Cog PCI (both p < 0.05), indicating that these patient characteristics may impact subjective cognitive function (Table 2).

Discussion

Cancer-related cognitive impairment remains a common and challenging problem for cancer survivors and evidence-based solutions are still limited. While insomnia has been previously found to be associated with CRCI, this study also evaluated the influence of additional comorbid symptoms among breast cancer survivors with self-reported insomnia and cognitive impairment who seek care through a clinical trial. Results showed that cumulative symptom burden was significantly associated with subjective—but not objective—CRCI, suggesting that managing comorbid symptoms may be a promising strategy to improve perceived cognitive function. Given that only insomnia was corelated with objective CRCI, targeted treatment of insomnia has the potential to improve objective cognitive functions in women with breast cancer.

Our study contributes to the growing body of literature showing CRCI is a multifactorial symptom. We identified a high symptom burden: all comorbid symptoms assessed—fatigue, insomnia, pain, anxiety, and depression—were common, with prevalence rates two to four times higher than those reported in the general breast cancer survivors [52]. Each of these symptoms was strongly associated with subjective CRCI, consistent with previous findings [12, 53]. Notably, we also assessed cumulative symptom burden to evaluate its overall impact on cognitive function—a novel approach in this context—which also showed a strong association with subjective CRCI. Previous research suggests that psychological and physical symptoms may increase the mental effort required for daily tasks for cancer patients, thereby negatively affecting patients’ perception of their cognitive functioning [54]. These findings highlight that not only individual symptom but also the overall comorbid symptom burden significantly shapes subjective cognitive experience in this cohort of breast cancer survivors with co-occurring CRCI and insomnia.

We also observed that older age was associated with better subjective cognitive function, a finding that is consistent with some previous studies and may reflect differences in symptom perception across age groups [55, 56]. Older survivors may be more likely to attribute cognitive changes to normal aging, whereas younger survivors often face greater work and family demands and may therefore be more aware of cognitive difficulties [55]. Interestingly, chemotherapy was no longer associated with subjective CRCI after accounting for cumulative symptom burden, suggesting that cognitive complaints may be influenced by the broader burden of co-occurring symptoms beyond the direct effects of chemotherapy alone. Similarly, hormonal therapy was not associated with cognition in our cohort. Although endocrine therapy has been linked to cognitive symptoms in some studies, findings have been inconsistent, and the magnitude of cognitive effects may vary according to treatment type, duration, and patient characteristics [57].

In contrast, no significant association was observed between symptom burden and objective CRCI; only insomnia showed a significant correlation. Our findings differ from the only study we identified that examined the influence of accumulated indicators on CRCI, which reported a significant association with objective cognition [58]. Notably, that study used the Deficit Accumulation Frailty Index (DAFI) to measure accumulated indicators, which primarily includes items related to chronic diseases (e.g., diabetes), functional impairments (e.g., difficulty walking), and clinical abnormalities, in addition to some symptoms [59 ]. These indicators tend to reflect long-term physiological vulnerability or biological aging rather than patient-reported symptom experience, which may account for their different associations with objective CRCI. However, consistent with previous research, [16, 60] insomnia appears to play a significant role in objective cognitive function. Sleep is essential for normal cognitive processes, such as memory consolidation [61, 62]. Neurobiological studies suggest that insomnia may increase the risk of cognitive decline by contributing to reduced gray matter volume and compromised white matter integrity in the brain—factors associated with impairments in attention, memory, visuospatial ability, executive function, and verbal memory [63]. The distinct contributors to subjective and objective CRCI highlight the need for tailored approaches in their assessment and management.

Our study also confirms previous research showing that only a subset of patients with subjective cognitive complaints exhibits objective, and that the severity of these two are often not related [12]. This discrepancy may partly stem from the way each type of cognition is assessed [64]. Subjective cognitive measures typically reflect the everyday cognitive challenges patients face. In contrast, objective cognition is evaluated through structured, time-limited neuropsychological tests conducted under optimal conditions, that may reduce the influence of real-life stressors [65]. Furthermore, many neurocognitive batteries were originally designed to detect more severe impairments, such as dementia, which affect functional independence. [65, 66] While cancer survivors with sufficient cognitive reserve may still score within the normal range on these tests, even experiencing real-life difficulties. Additionally, brain imaging studies have shown that some treatment-related abnormalities—such as reduced fractional anisotropy associated with cognitive decline—can return to baseline levels within 3–4 years after treatment [67]. However, comorbid symptoms often persist for many years and may continue to contribute to subjective CRCI [52], independent of measurable changes in cognitive function. It is important to note that both subjective and objective measures are valuable when evaluating CRCI, as they assess different aspects of cognitive functioning and are influenced by distinct factors.

Our study provides valuable insights into both research and clinical practice. First, to adequately recruit patients with objectively measured CRCI for future studies, researchers may need to screen approximately three times as many patients with self-reported moderate to severe cognitive concerns. Second, the symptom patterns identified in our study suggest that CRCI requires a multicomponent management approach. Given the high prevalence of comorbid symptoms—and their strong association with subjective cognitive impairment— evidence-based comprehensive strategies (such as cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia [CBT-I], exercise interventions for fatigue) that target co-occurring symptom clusters may help alleviate perceived cognitive impairment, especially in the absence of definitive treatments[68]. Third, since only insomnia was significantly correlated with objective CRCI, addressing sleep disturbances may offer a promising approach to improving objective cognitive function—this warrants further investigation.

Our study has several limitations. First, the data were drawn from the enrollment visit of a clinical trial evaluating an intervention for CRCI, so the recruited population may have reported more cognitive complaints and comorbid symptoms than those in population-based surveys. However, this cohort represents the patients most in need of care and is commonly seen in clinical practice. Second, our study sample included individuals with insomnia and CRCI, so findings may differ from those with CRCI alone. However, both insomnia and CRCI are highly prevalent and closely associated in breast cancer survivors [16]. Third, most participants had a college-level education or higher. Given that higher education levels may influence neurocognitive test performance [69], results should be interpreted with caution when applied to populations with lower educational level. Fourth, we included only the HVLT-R as a measure of objective cognitive function. Although this is a well-established and validated test, future studies should consider incorporating additional objective assessments to evaluate other objective cognitive domains. Fifth, multiple comparisons were performed when evaluating associations between individual symptoms and cognitive outcomes. Although most statistically significant findings remained highly significant (p < 0.001), these results should be interpreted cautiously and warrant confirmation in future studies. Sixth, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding the direction of the association between cumulative symptom burden and subjective CRCI. Shared underlying distress or other unmeasured factors may contribute to both symptom burden and perceived cognitive impairment. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify these relationships. Seventh, the evaluation of symptom burden is complex. In this study, we used a simple summative score to reflect the cumulative severity of symptoms, which may not fully capture the complexity of symptom burden. Finally, this study was conducted at a large, urban, well-resourced academic cancer center. All participants were English-speaking, and most were white and not Hispanic. As such, our findings may not be generalizable to other settings or patient populations.

Despite these limitations, this study extends previous research by evaluating subjective and objective cognitive function in relation to symptom burden among breast cancer survivors with co-occurring CRCI and insomnia. By focusing on this highly symptomatic cohort, our findings are directly relevant to patients who actively seek clinical care and may be most likely to benefit from interventions targeting symptom clusters. The findings underscore the distinct influences of comorbid symptoms on perceived versus objective cognitive function, highlighting the importance of individualized, symptom-informed approaches to care. These insights can inform the development of more targeted interventions and guide future research aimed at improving both subjective and objective cognitive outcomes in women with breast cancer.
