Fuzzy logic and deep learning approach for automated white blood cell detection and classification via multi-CNN feature fusion
Authors: Taseer Ullah, Khalil Ullah, Parvez Khan, Olga Brikova, Alexander Voznesensky, Alexander Eroshkin, Ibrar Hussain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhite blood cells (WBCs) are vital components of the human immune system, responsible for defending the body against pathogens and infections. The classification and detection of white blood cells (WBC) are indispensable for the diagnosis of hematologic diseases such as leukemia, but manual classification is time-consuming and susceptible to subjectivity. This paper introduces a new framework that integrates multi-CNN feature fusion with fuzzy logic-based Evaluation Based on Distance from Average Solution (EDAS) for automatic classification and detection of WBCs. The method combines aspects of DenseNet121, MobileNetV2, and ResNet101 to obtain varied morphological features, tackling intraclass variation and imaging inhomogeneity. A fuzzy EDAS model prioritizes the fused features for better interpretability and robustness in clinical decision-making. Using the Kaggle Blood Cell Images dataset of 8013 images of neutrophils, eosinophils, monocytes and lymphocytes, the suggested approach obtained an overall precision of 99.79%, precision, sensitivity and F1 scores greater than 99.70% for all types of WBC. In contrast to individual CNN models (see, e.g., DenseNet121: 95.28%, ResNet101: 81.85%), the combined model performs much better, especially for difficult classes such as neutrophils. The fuzzy EDAS method also guarantees robust model classification in an uncertain situation and is therefore appropriate for clinical settings. It minimizes diagnostic delay, improves scalability in low-resource scenarios, and facilitates quick screening for blood diseases. Future development will address real-time implementation and verification on varied datasets to ensure generalizability.Subject terms: Cell biology, Computational biology and bioinformatics

Introduction

White blood cells (WBCs) are important in the immune response and their proper classification is vital for the diagnosis of hematological conditions. Early and correct diagnosis is essential in hematology, where the white blood cell count (WBC) is particularly responsible for the identification of infections, disorders of the immune system, and blood cancers. Leukocytes, also known as white blood cells (WBCs), are immune cells that protect the body against pathogens that may enter the body from various sources. The classification of WBCs is crucial for diagnostic purposes, such as infections, autoimmune disorders, or hematologic malignancies. Hematological malignancies, such as leukemia, are blood-related cancers that require accurate WBC classification. The major problem of this classification is that it is based on a manual examination of blood smears by hematologists, which results in subjectivity and variability between observers.

Manual interpretation of blood smears, although common practice, is susceptible to variation between observers and is not scalable. In addition, the manual method is not scalable, requires resources, and can contribute to diagnostic delays, particularly in high-throughput clinical settings. In recent years, machine learning and deep learning methods have dramatically transformed medical image analysis, providing automatic tools for complex diagnostic tasks. Significant progress has been made in the automatic detection and classification of WBCs from microscopic images using computational algorithms. They promise potential improvements in diagnostic accuracy, workflow of healthcare professionals, and timely interventions in patients. WBCs are the main cellular components of the human immune system and cell classification, and accurately counting WBC samples is important to facilitate the diagnosis and treatment of different diseases. Traditionally, WBC evaluation was done by expert pathology pathologists with blood smear microscopy1. But still this is a time-consuming and cumbersome task since it depends on man power to identify a subjective view of the individual assessor’s experience.

With the emergence of deep learning, AI algorithms have been promising in automating image-based medical tasks. Medical science has adapted to the use of automated systems instead for the detection and classification of WBC using computer vision, machine learning2,3. Recent advances in computer vision and deep learning provide the scope of automatic detection and classification to be more accurate with higher precision for detecting WBCs. Deep neural networks have shown the best performance in machine learning, compared with other approaches for segmentation and classification of medical images such as microscopic blood smear image. These deep learning techniques can alleviate the time-consuming and labor-intensive nature of conventional manual analysis, resulting in more efficient, accurate, and reproducible outputs4. This paper comprehensively combines three CNN architectures (DenseNet121, ResNet101, and MobileNetV2) for complementary feature extraction and utilizes a fuzzy EDAS-based multi-criteria assessment to rank performances comprehensively. Although ensemble learning is put in place, the application of fuzzy logic towards explainable performance comparison based on clinical measures constitutes a new contribution within the context of WBC subtype classification.

To address limitations in WBC classification, we propose a novel multi-CNN feature fusion system enhanced by fuzzy EDAS.

Related work

Recently, automated WBC classification has become a topic of interest for medical imaging and machine learning communities due to its potential use to transform healthcare diagnostic practices13. White Blood Cells (WBCs) are also called leukocytes and play a very important role in the human immune system, protecting our body from any kind of infection or foreign invaders. White blood cells come in several varieties, each with different functions and attributes. The main types of WBC include neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes (after extrication in the tissues-macrophages), eosinophils and basophils13. Neutrophils, the most abundant subgroup of white blood cells, are primarily responsible for fighting bacterial infections through their ability to phagocytize and release antimicrobial molecules. In addition, lymphocytes (a core subset of the cellular component of the immune system) comprising T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells (NK) are essential for many functions in adaptive or innate immunity- including recognition / clearance of pathogens, immunoglobulin production, and regulatory responses mediated by cytokines14.

Monocytes are phagocytic leukocytes that play a role in the removal of foreign particles and antigens by scavenging cellular debris, dead cells, or activated immune complexes13. Eosinophils and basophils are associated with allergic reactions, parasitic infections, and inflammatory responses. Eosinophils are known to release toxic proteins during parasite infection, such as basophil-derived products such as histamine and other inflammatory mediators15,16. However, from the perspectives of hematology, immunology, and medical imaging, extensive studies have been performed to classify and characterize White Blood Cells. The gold standard for WBC analysis is manual differential counting by trained hematologists and automated hematology analyzers utilizing optical and impedance-based approaches14. However, the methods are labor intensive, time consuming, and susceptible to draw variability between observers, as well as being highly subjective14,17. Progress in the field of computational vision algorithms, when combined with machine and deep learning techniques, provides a platform to objectively assess white blood cells automatically using microscopic images16,17. For example, by processing digital images of blood smears using artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms, researchers have been able to develop automated systems that accurately identify and classify white blood cell populations17,18. In 2020 Moussa et al. introduced a classification of white blood cells using deep features extracted from convolutional neural network (CNN) models, of which results were shown13. Similarly, Sajjad et al. in 2020 in leukemia cell detection and classification/ segmentation for blood smear images through a computer-aided system based on machine learning19.

In addition, researchers investigated using different image analysis methods to segment white blood cells from microscopic images and obtain more accurate classification systems with the support of algorithms such as K-means clustering19 or modified watershed method. These advances illustrate the promise of AI-based methods for transforming what is currently a highly manual process into data-rich tools that can be used to diagnose patients in clinical scenarios. Manually collecting and registering large amounts of data sets to train Convolutional Neural Network models can be a very time consuming, expensive, and labor intensive process25. Proposed methodology

This study adopts a stepwise approach to develop and test a deep learning-based WBC classification system. Describes the rationale, including the description of the data set, preprocessing, model structure, training strategies, evaluation criteria, and ranking of the fuzzy logic-based model. The strategy leverages deep learning and transfer learning to enhance CNNs’ discriminative ability, aiming at accurate and reliable WBC classification. The proposed methodology utilizes annotated WBC image data sets from various sources to improve model adaptability to different WBC types. Following normalization, image enhancement enhances the diversity of the dataset and addresses class imbalances. CNN-based architectures, including DenseNet-121, MobileNetV2, ResNet-101, and a combined model, are used for feature extraction and classification due to their proven effectiveness in biomedical image classification13.

Transfer learning with pre-trained models adjusts network weights for WBCs-specific features, optimizing model performance. The effectiveness of the method is evaluated using classification metrics such as Precision, Recall, F1 Score, Sensitivity, and Specificity, assessing the model’s ability to distinguish types of WBC and generalize to unseen data. Rigorous experiments with validation procedures ensure reliability and reproducibility, while parameter validation is conducted using validation data. Finally, the fuzzy logic-based EDAS approach is used to rank the model’s performance for classifying different classes in WBCs.

Dataset description

This study uses the publicly available Kaggle Blood Cell Images dataset (https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/paultimothymooney/blood-cells), comprising 8013 expertly labeled white blood cell (WBC) images, classified into four classes: neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, and eosinophils (for detailed dataset statistics, please refer to Table 1). Each image has a resolution of 320240 pixels, and the dataset includes images captured at varying magnifications and resolutions to reflect the variability of practical clinical imaging. Data were partitioned into training sets 70%, validation sets 15%, and tests sets 15%. To address class imbalance, stratified sampling and weighted cross-entropy loss were used, avoiding simple replication or deletion, while future studies will explore more sophisticated techniques such as SMOTE and focal loss. Despite its suitability for developing and benchmarking WBC classification models, the use of a single-source dataset presents limitations in terms of generalizability. As part of ongoing research, future efforts will focus on integrating datasets from multiple clinical centers with various staining protocols and applying domain adaptation methods to enhance the robustness and reliability of the model in varying diagnostic environments.

Pre-processing

To enhance the robustness and generalization of the proposed model, a comprehensive data augmentation and preprocessing pipeline was implemented. Initially, all images were normalized by rescaling the pixel values to the range [0, 1] to ensure consistency of brightness under heterogeneous imaging conditions. Sharpening filters were applied to improve contrast and reduce noise. To address the limited size and class imbalance typical in medical image datasets, a set of augmentation techniques was used: horizontal and vertical flips, ±15 rotations, ±10% zoom, and brightness adjustments, with more aggressive augmentation applied to underrepresented classes. These transformations expanded the diversity of the training data without the need for extensive manual labeling. For class balancing, stratified sampling was used in conjunction with a limited oversampling of minority classes to prevent overfitting, while future work will explore advanced strategies such as SMOTE and focal loss. This preprocessing framework ensures high-quality and standardized input for deep learning models, ultimately contributing to improved and reliable classification of all types of WBC.

Image preprocessing and augmentation

All images were resized to 2242243 pixels. The preprocessing included histogram equalization, Gaussian filtering, and ImageNet-based channel normalization. Data augmentation strategies encompassed random rotations (±20°), horizontal/vertical flips, zoom (0.8–1.2), and brightness/contrast jittering. These steps improve generalizability and mitigate overfitting, especially in minority classes.

The Blood Cell data set has a good distribution balance in four primary kinds of white blood cells, which is highly beneficial for classification and detection applications in medical imaging. Specifically, the dataset contains 2260 images of eosinophils, 2243 lymphocytes, 2238 monocytes, and 2259 neutrophils. This almost uniform representation facilitates training models without substantial class imbalance, resulting in a more robust and generalized performance across cell types. The well-balanced composition of the data set is most suitable for deep learning tasks that seek to automate blood smear examination and enhance diagnostic precision in hematology. MobileNetV2

The mobile computer vision model MobileNetV2 is widely deployed in different fields to classify images in medical applications as well as face-based systems and objects. Real-time classification applications find MobileNetV2 to be their preferred selection due to its ability to perform accurately while maintaining quick processing speeds31. Lightweight and low-latency models are suitable for mobile embedded applications. They used fewer convolutions using depth-wise separable convolutions. Lightweight image features extractors that do not consume much computation and can run on low-resource environments.

DenseNet121

The DenseNet121 deep convolutional neural network functions for efficient distribution of features while achieving superior classification capabilities. Huang et al.32 introduced this neural network to the Densely Connected Convolutional Networks family because it adds new connections to improve the information flow between the layers of the network. Applying dense connections among nearly all layers to improve information flow and gradient conspiracy. Facilitates the network to learn rich and diverse features using dense connectivity, which further makes information smoother from one layer to another in the entire network.

ResNet101

The ResNet101 deep neural network belongs to the Residual Networks family, which was developed to tackle training difficulties in very deep neural network systems. The research by He et al.33 presented ResNet101 which implements residual connections to enhance gradient flow that enables training networks of hundreds of layers. This uses skip or residual connections to solve the vanishing gradient problem and allows intense network training. Easy training of a deeper network that is very powerful in extracting deep features from the images. The CNN models extract features and are then input into a fully connected (FC) layer, which consists of 1000 neurons. Scales down the dimensions of our features, thereby facilitating feature compression.

Feature fusion and classification

Feature vectors from each model (1000 dimensional) are concatenated into a 3000-dimensional vector. This is processed through a dense layer with dropout (0.5) and batch normalization. No dimensionality reduction was used, although future work will evaluate PCA and attention mechanisms.

Boosting feature extraction with merged multi-CNN models

In this approach, the output from fully connected layers of the three CNN models is concatenated to get a multi-CNN feature vector of size 3000 (1000 features for each model). The Multi-CNN feature vector is then inputted into the classification layer through the softmax layer, for identification of WBC classes. Performance evaluation with classification metrics and fuzzy EDAS

Fuzzy EDAS ranks models by evaluating their performance relative to an average solution across multiple clinical metrics, such as precision, precision, recall, F1 score, and specificity, while incorporating fuzzy logic to manage uncertainty inherent in medical decision making. In this study, WBC classification models were assessed using these traditional performance metrics, and further analyzed through the Fuzzy EDAS framework to provide a multi-criteria, interpretable ranking. These metrics offer a quantitative evaluation of the ability of the models to accurately and consistently detect different types of WBC, while the EDAS-based ranking adds an additional layer of interpretability for model selection in clinical contexts.

Traditional classification metrics

The evaluation of model performance depends fundamentally on the classification metrics consisting of precision, recall, F1 score, and accuracy, each highlighting different aspects of performance. Precision measures true positives relative to all positive predictions, while recall evaluates true positives against actual positives34,35. The F1 score balances precision and recall, providing an overall efficiency metric, and accuracy calculates the ratio of correct predictions to total cases36. These metrics are widely applied in various domains, guiding researchers and practitioners in selecting and refining models for specific applications. The confusion matrix provides a tabular representation of the model’s predictions vs. true labels over different classes. It enables us to analyze in more depth the performance of our model (e.g. we can see which types of error it did - False Positives, False Negatives). Together, these metrics give us a complete picture of how our model performs in different dimensions with respect to classification.

Training configuration

DenseNet121 facilitates deep feature reuse, ResNet101 extracts hierarchical semantics, and MobileNetV2 offers lightweight computation. These models were chosen to combine depth, efficiency, and complementary feature diversity for robust fusion. All models were trained with Adam Optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.0001. To avoid overfitting and promote generalization, early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs was employed, alongside a ReduceLROnPlateau learning rate scheduler. The models were trained for a maximum of 100 epochs with a batch size of 16. A dropout layer with a rate of 0.5 was used before the final classification layer to regularize the network and mitigate overfitting, particularly given the relatively small dataset size. The weighted cross-entropy loss function was used to handle class imbalance, assigning higher importance to underrepresented classes. Table 4 presents the hyperparameter setting.

Fuzzy EDAS approach

The Fuzzy Evaluation Based on Distance from Average Solution (Fuzzy EDAS) is a multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) technique that evaluates model performance based on their proximity to an ideal solution across multiple clinical metrics. In this study, Fuzzy EDAS is used to classify the classification performance of four WBC detection models using five criteria: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-Score, and Specificity. This technique incorporates fuzzy logic to address uncertainty in the evaluation of medical models and provides a more interpretable and balanced performance ranking, especially in healthcare applications where trade-offs between sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy are critical37. Figure 4 presents the eight-step workflow of the Fuzzy EDAS (Evaluation based on Distance from Average Solution) method, used for multi-criteria decision-making. It begins with forming a decision matrix and normalizing the data, followed by assigning fuzzy weights to each performance metric. An average solution is then calculated to serve as a benchmark. The positive and negative distances from this average are computed, aggregated using fuzzy logic, and then defuzzified to crisp values. Finally, the models are ranked on the basis of their overall performance across all metrics. This process ensures balanced, interpretable, and clinically relevant model evaluation.

Interpretability and multi-criteria evaluation

In this study, fuzzy evaluation based on distance from average solution (Fuzzy EDAS) is used as a multi-criteria post-training decision support framework to classify white blood cell (WBC) classification models based on five clinical performance metrics: precision, precision, recall, F1 score, and specificity. By integrating fuzzy logic, EDAS handles uncertainty in performance evaluation and facilitates nuanced, interpretable comparisons between models, which is essential in clinical settings where trade-offs between metrics like sensitivity and specificity are critical. Although EDAS does not directly influence model training, it aggregates diverse evaluation criteria into a single assessment score, supporting robust model selection beyond simple accuracy metrics. The complete mathematical formulation is detailed in Sect. 4.3, and the eight-step workflow is shown in Fig. 5. This interpretability-driven approach improves transparency, informs clinical applicability, and lays the foundation for future integration of fuzzy decision logic within training pipelines. In future work, the fuzzy logic component will be integrated into the training phase to enhance interpretability and explainability. Given the limitations of the current study, we propose its incorporation into the training loop, for example, as a fuzzy-weighted loss function, to enable dynamic optimization across multiple metrics. However, it is important to note that while Fuzzy EDAS facilitates multi-metric evaluation, its interpretability from a clinician’s perspective remains unvalidated. To ensure clinical relevance and adoption, future work will include user studies with practicing hematologists to assess the utility, usability, and trustworthiness of EDAS-based outputs in the real-world. This will help determine whether the fuzzy evaluation framework aligns with clinical decision-making processes and provides meaningful support in diagnostic workflows.

This study presents a comprehensive evaluation of the automated detection and classification of four subtypes of white blood cells (WBC) using machine learning and deep learning techniques. Details data sets, model architectures, training configurations, and evaluation metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. Furthermore, the study explores the clinical applicability of automated WBC classification, emphasizing its potential to improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce clinician workload, and facilitate timely medical interventions, thus contributing to the advancement of computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) systems in healthcare.

Fuzzy EDAS based model performance

Using the fuzzy EDAS methodology, the analysis evaluates the performance of DenseNet121 compared to MobileNetV2, ResNet101 and a combined model for classifying white blood cell types, specifically lymphocytes, monocytes and neutrophils. In this evaluation framework, lower rank values indicate better classification performance, with rank 1 representing the highest accuracy. DenseNet121 and the Combined Model excel in monocyte classification, both achieving the top rank. For neutrophil classification, MobileNetV2 and ResNet101 emerge as the best performers, each achieving rank 1. However, lymphocyte classification proves challenging for all models, with none showing strong performance; the Combined Model delivers the most favorable results among them. The complementary strengths of DenseNet121 and MobileNetV2 suggest a promising combination for medical diagnostics: DenseNet121 excels in monocyte detection, while MobileNetV2 shows superior performance in identifying neutrophils. ResNet101 is particularly effective in neutrophil classification, but demonstrates reduced precision with monocytes and lymphocytes. Overall, the Combined Model presents a balanced yet inconsistent performance: it performs strongly on monocytes but struggles in several neutrophil classification cases. To improve diagnostic precision, an optimal strategy would involve taking advantage of DenseNet121 for monocyte detection, MobileNetV2 or ResNet101 for neutrophils, and the Combined Model for lymphocytes.

Conclusion and future work

This study presents a composite framework that fuses multi-CNN architectures with a fuzzy EDAS-based evaluation layer to improve both classification performance and interpretability in the recognition of white blood cell (WBC) subtypes. Rather than relying solely on conventional metrics such as precision, the proposed system evaluates clinical performance across multiple axes–precision, recall, specificity, and F1-score–providing a more clinically meaningful assessment. The integration of DenseNet121, ResNet101, and MobileNetV2 allows the model to capture various morphological cues, while fuzzy EDAS introduces a structured, multi-criteria evaluation mechanism. This dual approach enables improved diagnostic robustness, particularly in scenarios with class ambiguity or intraclass variation. Importantly, the framework is validated through cross-validation, statistical testing, and ablation studies to establish reproducibility and model integrity.

Although high accuracy was achieved on the Kaggle data set, the generalizability of the system to real-world clinical environments remains limited by the lack of demographic, staining, and equipment diversity in the data set. Furthermore, the fuzzy EDAS layer is currently used only as a post-hoc evaluator and not as part of the learning process. To strengthen the practical relevance and originality of future directions, we propose several targeted extensions. These include implementing a fuzzy-weighted loss function to embed interpretability into the training loop, conducting clinician-involved studies comparing Fuzzy EDAS with visual explainability tools like Grad-CAM and LIME, and applying domain-specific normalization methods for cross-center data harmonization. Such steps go beyond established approaches and are directly aligned with real-world clinical deployment challenges.

Future work will focus on three core directions: (1) integrating fuzzy logic directly into the model’s training process through neuro-fuzzy architectures to optimize across multiple clinical metrics in real time; (2) expanding the data set to include multi-center, multi-device images to better reflect real-world hematological conditions; and (3) linking classification with WBC segmentation and incorporating explainability tools such as Grad-CAM to support clinical trust and adoption. By bridging high-performance deep learning with interpretable decision-making, this work moves a step closer to deployable, clinician-aligned AI for hematology.
