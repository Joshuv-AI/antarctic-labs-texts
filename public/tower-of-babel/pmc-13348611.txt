Rimegepant for the acute treatment of migraine: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Melissa Istoc, Basit A. Chaudhry, Rune H. Christensen, Haidar M. Al-Khazali, Messoud Ashina, William K. Karlsson, Håkan Ashina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo determine the efficacy and tolerability of a single 75 mg oral dose of rimegepant compared with placebo for the acute treatment of migraine attacks in adults.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines, with the protocol prospectively registered in PROSPERO (Identifier: CRD420251042015). Comprehensive searches of CENTRAL, ClinicalTrials.gov, EMBASE, EUCTR, PubMed, and WHO ICTRP were performed from database inception to December 2025, without language restrictions. Supplementary sources were also reviewed, including regulatory and sponsor materials such as FDA and EMA submissions and entries in the Pfizer and Biohaven clinical trial registries. Eligible studies were double-blind randomized controlled trials enrolling adults (≥ 18 years) with migraine (with or without aura) who received a single 75 mg oral dose of rimegepant compared with placebo for the acute treatment of migraine attacks. The co-primary outcomes were pain freedom at 2 h post-dose and sustained pain freedom from 2 to 24 h post-dose. Tolerability outcomes included all non-serious adverse events (AEs) following dosing, whereas safety outcomes comprised serious adverse events. Risk of bias was evaluated using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool, and certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE framework. Pooled risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using a random-effects model, and heterogeneity was quantified using the I² and τ² statistics.ResultsSeven double-blind RCTs (one phase II, five phase III, one phase IV) comprising 6,734 participants met the inclusion criteria. Two hours post-dose, 19.8% of participants receiving rimegepant and 10.7% receiving placebo achieved pain freedom (RR, 1.93; 95% CI, 1.51–2.47; P < 0.001; NNT = 10). Sustained pain freedom from 2 to 24 h was achieved by 14.2% of those receiving rimegepant, compared with 6.3% receiving placebo (RR, 2.39; 95% CI, 1.75–3.27; P < 0.001; NNT = 11). AEs occurred in 14.3% of rimegepant-treated and 12.7% of placebo-treated participants (RR, 1.14; 95% CI, 1.00–1.30; P = 0.056; NNH = 55). Risk of bias was judged as low across the included trials.ConclusionsRimegepant 75 mg provides modest yet clinically meaningful efficacy with favorable short-term tolerability for the acute treatment of migraine attacks. These findings support its use as an effective treatment option for adults with inadequate response, contraindications, or intolerance to first- or second-line therapies.Clinical trials numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02388-x.

Introduction

The acute treatment of migraine aims to achieve rapid, complete, and sustained pain relief while minimizing the need for rescue headache medication [1]. Despite a wide range of available options, [2] including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and triptans, many patients experience suboptimal efficacy, recurrence of head pain, or adverse effects that limit continued use [3]. These limitations underscore the need for novel agents with distinct mechanisms and improved tolerability profiles [4].

Rimegepant is an oral antagonist of the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) receptor, [5] a central mediator in migraine pathogenesis [6]. It is the first member of the gepant drug class approved for both acute and preventive migraine treatment [7], offering a unique approach to disease management. Individual phase II, III, and IV randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have shown that a single 75 mg oral dose of rimegepant improves pain freedom at 2 h compared with placebo [8–14]. However, these RCTs varied somewhat in design, population characteristics, geographic scope, and outcome selection [8–14].

Therefore, a systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted of randomized controlled trials evaluating the efficacy and short-term tolerability of rimegepant for the acute treatment of migraine attacks. In contrast to previous reviews, this analysis adheres to the methodological recommendations of the International Headache Society (IHS) for trials assessing acute migraine therapies in adults [15]. By incorporating all guideline-recommended efficacy and tolerability endpoints where available, the present synthesis provides a balanced and standardized appraisal of the current evidence base.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [16]. The protocol was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; Identifier: CRD420251042015).

Search strategy

A systematic search was performed from database inception to December 17, 2025, using a PICO framework to identify double-blind RCTs evaluating oral rimegepant 75 mg versus placebo for the acute treatment of migraine. The following electronic databases were searched: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), European Union Clinical Trials Register (EUCTR), World Health Organization (WHO) International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP), and ClinicalTrials.gov. The complete search strategy is available in the Supplemental Appendix 1. To ensure comprehensive coverage, additional sources of unpublished and regulatory data were examined. These included submissions and reports from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and its European Public Assessment Report (EPAR), the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA), the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA), the British National Formulary (BNF), the Bundesinstitut für Arzneimittel und Medizinprodukte (BfArM), and the Agence Nationale de Sécurité du Médicament et des Produits de Santé (ANSM). Sponsor-maintained trial repositories from Pfizer and Biohaven were also searched to identify unpublished datasets. Reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews were screened manually, and no language restrictions were applied.

The full electronic search strategies for each database, including Boolean search strings, date of execution, and registry search procedures, are provided in the Supplemental Appendix 1 to allow reproducibility.

Each domain was rated as low risk, some concerns, or high risk. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (RHC). An overall risk-of-bias judgment was generated algorithmically following Cochrane recommendations.

Publication bias was visually assessed using funnel plots, where asymmetry between effect size and precision suggested potential bias. Formal statistical testing (Egger’s regression) was not performed due to fewer than ten included studies.

The certainty of evidence for critical outcomes was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [22]. Evidence from randomized controlled trials was initially rated as high certainty and downgraded based on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

Data analysis

All quantitative analyses were conducted using R, version 4.4.3, with the meta and metafor packages. For dichotomous outcomes, pooled risk ratios (RRs) were estimated with a random-effects model using the inverse-variance method, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for pooled effect estimates calculated using the Hartung–Knapp adjustment. Between-study variance (τ²) was estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML), with CIs for τ² derived using the Q-profile method. Absolute risk differences were used to calculate the number needed to treat (NNT) and the number needed to harm (NNH). Efficacy outcomes were analyzed using the intention-to-treat population (ITT), whereas safety outcomes were analyzed using the safety population, resulting in different pooled denominators for efficacy and safety analyses.

Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated with Cochran’s Q, τ², and the I² statistic, with I² values of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Because the operational definition of the modified ITT (mITT) population differed slightly across trials (typically including participants who received study medication and had evaluable post-dose outcome data), sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate whether pooling these populations affected the robustness of the results. To further assess robustness, a leave-one-out analysis was conducted excluding either the single phase II trial [8], or the single phase IV trial [14]. All tests were two-sided, with an alpha level of 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

The systematic search identified 1,182 records across all databases. After removal of duplicates and ineligible reports, 131 unique records were screened by title and abstract, and 39 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility (Fig. 1). No additional eligible studies were identified through regulatory databases, sponsor trial registries, or reference list screening, although supplementary information was identified for five studies [8–11,13]. Ultimately, seven double-blind RCTs met the inclusion criteria [8–14], comprising one phase I [8], five phase III, [9–13] and one phase IV study [14]. Together, these trials enrolled 6,734 participants to the pooled analysis, with 3,300 assigned to rimegepant 75 mg and 3,434 assigned to placebo. Four investigations were conducted in the United States [8–11], one was carried out in East Asia [12], one in Japan [13], and one encompassed multiple countries [14]. All trials enrolled adults with episodic migraine, treated a single attack of moderate-to-severe intensity, and permitted the use of rescue headache medication after 2 hours. Baseline characteristics were similar between treatment groups: the mean age ranged from about 38 to 42 years, 80–90% of participants were female, and the mean body mass index was about 30 kg/m2 where reported. Furthermore, most participants had a diagnosis of migraine without aura. Independent review of sponsor and regulatory datasets confirmed consistency with the published data. Pain freedom at 2 h post-dose

The proportion of participants who achieved pain freedom 2 h after dosing was 19.8% in the rimegepant group and 10.7% in the placebo group. The pooled RR was 1.93 (95% CI, 1.51–2.47; P < 0.001; Fig. 2; Table 2), representing an absolute therapeutic gain of 10.0% and an NNT of 10. Between-study heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 57.1%; τ² = 0.038), and Cochran’s Q test indicated statistically significant heterogeneity (P = 0.030).

Sustained pain freedom from 2 to 24 h post-dose

Rimegepant was also superior to placebo for sustained pain freedom from 2 to 24 h post-dose, achieved by 14.2% of participants receiving rimegepant and 6.3% of those receiving placebo (RR, 2.39; 95% CI, 1.75–3.27; P < 0.001; NNT = 11; Fig. 3; Table 2). Between-study heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 55.2%; τ² = 0.060), and Cochran’s Q test indicated statistically significant heterogeneity (P = 0.037).

Other efficacy outcomes

One hour after dosing, 7.7% of participants treated with rimegepant achieved pain freedom, as compared with 4.1% of those receiving placebo; however, no statistically significance difference was observed (RR, 1.61; 95% CI, 0.61–4.21; P = 0.101; NNT = 40). Rimegepant was associated with a higher rate of freedom from the most bothersome symptom at 2 h (41.4% vs. 29.8%; RR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.28–1.49; P < 0.001; NNT = 9). In addition, the proportion of participants reporting pain relief at 2 h was greater with rimegepant than with placebo (56.5% vs. 41.5%; RR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.27–1.49; P = 0.001; NNT = 6).

Rimegepant also reduced the use of rescue medication within 24 h after dosing, with 15.0% of participants requiring additional therapy, as compared with 30.0% in the placebo group (RR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.43–0.60; P < 0.001; NNT = 7). Detailed results of all secondary and tertiary outcomes, together with corresponding forest plots, are provided in Table 2 and Supplemental Appendices 4–9.

Tolerability

The occurrence of any AEs was reported in 14.3% of rimegepant-treated and 12.7% of placebo-treated participants (RR, 1.14; 95% CI, 1.00–1.30; P = 0.056; Fig. 4). Furthermore, no significant differences were observed between rimegepant and placebo for SAEs (RR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.24–3.96; P = 0.962). All other specific adverse events were rare and did not differ significantly between rimegepant and placebo (Table 3).

Risk of bias and GRADE assessment

Assessment with the Cochrane RoB-2 tool indicated an overall low risk of bias across all seven included trials (Supplemental Appendix 10). Randomization procedures, blinding methods, and outcome reporting were adequately described in each study.

Certainty of evidence according to GRADE was considered high for the co-primary outcomes and most secondary outcomes, but low for pain freedom at 1 h and moderate for AEs (Supplementary Appendix 11).

Publication bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plots showed a slight asymmetry (Supplemental Appendix 12), indicating a small risk of publication bias. Because only seven trials were available, interpretation of the funnel plots should be regarded as exploratory, and Egger’s regression test was not conducted.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed using complete-case data from the mITT, with full results provided in Supplemental Appendix 4. Outcomes were consistent with those of the primary analysis, although the estimates were slightly more precise and less heterogeneous. Pain freedom at 2 h remained statistically significant (RR, 1.91; 95% CI, 1.51–2.43; P < 0.001; NNT = 9; I² = 55.0%; τ² = 0.034; P = 0.038), and comparable findings were observed for sustained pain freedom from 2 to 24 h (RR, 2.37; 95% CI, 1.77–3.18; P < 0.001; NNT = 11; I² = 51.7%; τ² = 0.050; P = 0.053).

In an additional sensitivity analysis excluding the only phase II trial, the results remained materially unchanged. Pain freedom at 2 h (RR, 1.91; 95% CI, 1.42–2.57; P = 0.003; NNT = 10; I² = 62.7%; τ² = 0.046; P = 0.020) and sustained pain freedom from 2 to 24 h (RR = 2.24; 95% CI, 1.64–3.05; P = 0.001; NNT = 13; I² = 47.8%; τ² = 0.038; P = 0.088) both remained significant. Findings for secondary outcomes were likewise consistent across analyses (Supplemental Appendix 5).

Furthermore, we conducted sensitivity analyses excluding the phase IV study [14]. This study enrolled participants unsuitable for triptans due to lack of efficacy, intolerability, or cardiovascular contraindications. The analyses showed no major differences in the co-primary endpoints (Supplemental Appendix 6). Pain freedom at 2 h remained significant (RR, 1.82; 95% CI, 1.45–2.28; P = 0.001; NNT = 11; I² = 44.7%; τ² = 0.020; P = 0.108). Sustained pain freedom from 2 to 24 h was also unchanged (RR, 2.36; 95% CI, 1.62–3.43; P = 0.002; NNT = 11; I² = 60.9%; τ² = 0.071; P = 0.026). Secondary outcomes also continued to favor rimegepant. Absence of the most bothersome symptom at 2 h was higher (RR, 1.36; 95% CI, 1.27–1.46; P < 0.001; NNT = 9). Pain relief at 2 h also improved (RR, 1.35; 95% CI, 1.27–1.44; P < 0.001; NNT = 7). In terms of SAEs (RR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.24–3.96; P = 0.962) and AEs (RR, 1.15; 95% CI, 0.98–1.35; P = 0.068; NNH = 50) rimegepant demonstrated a favorable short-term tolerability profile. Forest plots are available in the Supplemental Appendix 6.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of seven double-blind RCTs enrolling 6,671 participants demonstrates that a single 75-mg oral dose of rimegepant is efficacious for the acute treatment of migraine. Two hours after dosing, 19.8% of participants achieved pain freedom compared with 10.7% receiving placebo, yielding a RR of 1.93 and an NNT of 10. Sustained pain freedom from 2 to 24 h was also superior with rimegepant (RR, 2.39; NNT = 11). Rimegepant also improved secondary efficacy endpoints, including pain relief at 2 h, freedom from the most bothersome symptom, and reduced use of rescue headache medication within 24 h. Adverse events were generally infrequent and mild. The overall incidence of AE was not significantly higher with rimegepant compared to placebo. Individual AEs occurred rarely and did not differ significantly between groups. Collectively, these data confirm that rimegepant provides modest pain relief with a favorable short-term tolerability profile.

Existing evidence and added value

This meta-analysis consolidates and strengthens the existing evidence base by integrating all available phase II, III, and IV data. Each included RCT demonstrated superiority of rimegepant over placebo for the prespecified co-primary endpoints, [8–14] and the pooled estimates substantially improve statistical precision while confirming the consistency of treatment effects across populations.

Importantly, this synthesis includes the recent phase IV randomized trial conducted in adults unsuitable for triptan treatment, [14] representing the first prospective evaluation of rimegepant in this clinically relevant subgroup. Although this trial differed from the phase II–III studies with respect to population characteristics and endpoint hierarchy, [8–13] its inclusion did not materially alter pooled estimates for pain freedom at 2 h or sustained pain freedom from 2 to 24 h as confirmed by sensitivity analysis. These findings support the consistency of rimegepant’s acute efficacy across distinct migraine populations, including those who do not benefit from triptans.

These results are also in close agreement with a 2024 network meta-analysis encompassing 137 randomized trials, which demonstrated that rimegepant and ubrogepant were superior to placebo [23] However, both oral gepants were generally less efficacious than certain triptans (i.e., eletriptan, rizatriptan, sumatriptan, and zolmitriptan) in achieving pain freedom at 2 h post-dose [23]. The proportions of participants becoming pain-free to rimegepant of 19.8% aligns well with the network meta-analytic estimate, [23] reinforcing confidence in our effect estimate. The present incorporation of both ITT and complete-case mITT data also provides a transparent synthesis that extends beyond individual trial reports. Moreover, our explicit quantification of NNT provides a more interpretable framework for clinical decision-making and guideline development.

On another note, recent real-world survey data have reported higher rates of rapid pain freedom at 2 h post-dose among participants with migraine who were treated with gepants [24]. Although these observations appear promising, they are limited by non-randomized designs and expectancy bias. The consistency of the controlled data presented here underscores that randomized evidence remains the most reliable estimate of true therapeutic effect and highlights the need for pragmatic trials to evaluate performance in real-world settings.

Clinical relevance and magnitude of effect

Rimegepant provides modest yet clinically meaningful pain freedom at 2 h after dosing, with an NNT of 10 and an absolute benefit of 10%. This equates to one additional pain-free responder for every 10 patients treated compared with placebo. The effect was consistent across trials, although moderate heterogeneity was observed (I² = 57%). The magnitude of benefit observed in this analysis is comparable with previous evaluations of CGRP receptor antagonists used for acute migraine treatment. Recent reviews have reported NNTs of approximately 9 for rimegepant, 11 for zavegepant, and 12 for ubrogepant for achieving pain freedom at 2 h post-dose [25, 26]. These estimates are broadly comparable to those reported for simple analgesics such as naproxen or paracetamol [27, 28], whereas triptans typically demonstrate lower NNT values reflecting greater efficacy [29, 30]. Consequently, the modest absolute benefit observed for rimegepant should be interpreted in the broader context of treatment sequencing and patient preferences. In clinical practice, gepants may be particularly valuable for patients who have an inadequate response to triptans, experience intolerable adverse effects, or have contraindications to vasoconstrictive therapies.

Beyond its modest acute efficacy, rimegepant’s broader clinical value lies in its pharmacological versatility. As the first gepant approved for both acute and preventive migraine treatment [7, 31], it enables flexible, individualized regimens in which intermittent dosing for acute attacks can transition seamlessly into preventive use. This dual-purpose mechanism represents a meaningful therapeutic advance, uniting rapid symptom relief and sustained disease control within a single treatment approach.

Recent studies further support the clinical effectiveness and safety of rimegepant across diverse patient populations. A post hoc subgroup analysis of three randomized trials demonstrated that rimegepant significantly improved pain freedom and freedom from the most bothersome symptom at 2 h in Black or African American adults compared with placebo, with a safety profile comparable to that observed in the overall trial populations [32]. These findings highlight the consistency of rimegepant’s therapeutic effect across demographic subgroups and reinforce the robustness of the efficacy estimates observed in the present meta-analysis. In addition, recent narrative reviews summarizing mechanism-based migraine therapies have emphasized the emerging role of CGRP receptor antagonists as flexible treatment options bridging acute and preventive migraine management [5, 25, 31].

The moderate heterogeneity observed in the co-primary efficacy outcomes likely reflects a combination of methodological and clinical differences across trials. Variations in study design, particularly between the phase II dose-ranging trial and later phase III and IV studies [8, 14], along with differences in geographic distribution, baseline patient characteristics, and prior treatment exposure, may all contribute to variability in effect estimates. In addition, subtle differences in outcome definitions and endpoint hierarchies may have influenced treatment response. Importantly, sensitivity analyses excluding either the phase II or phase IV study produced consistent results with reduced heterogeneity, indicating that these factors do not materially affect the overall conclusions.

A similar degree of heterogeneity was noted in some tolerability outcomes, particularly for overall adverse event rates. This heterogeneity might reflect differences in sample size, geographic recruitment, and background event rates, as well as variation in adverse event definitions and reporting thresholds across trials. Differences in patient populations, including comorbidities and prior medication use, could also influence reporting patterns. Nevertheless, most adverse events were infrequent and effect estimates were imprecise, suggesting that these variations are unlikely to meaningfully alter the overall interpretation of rimegepant’s favorable short-term safety profile.

Implications for clinical practice and future research

Current evidence suggests that rimegepant may be an appropriate treatment option for patients who do not respond adequately to, cannot tolerate, or have contraindications to commonly used acute therapies such as non-opioid analgesics or triptans [33]. Given its higher cost and modest comparative efficacy, first-line use is not currently warranted.

However, rimegepant offers clear advantages in specific clinical contexts. Its lack of vasoconstrictive activity and currently no evidence suggesting a risk of medication-overuse headache make it potentially suitable for patients with cardiovascular comorbidities, frequent attacks, or a high risk of medication-overuse headache, especially those with high-frequency episodic or chronic migraine [34, 35]. In these populations, earlier introduction might be clinically justified, offering both reliable acute pain relief and a reduction in migraine recurrence through its preventive mechanism.

Future research should extend beyond single-attack paradigms to evaluate long-term and real-world consistency of efficacy across multiple attacks and more diverse patient groups, including adolescents and older adults. Comparative trials with triptans and NSAIDs, together with cost-effectiveness and patient-preference studies, are essential to define rimegepant’s relative clinical and economic value. Leveraging prospective registries and adaptive trial designs will further strengthen evidence on its sustained safety and durability of response. Ultimately, future therapeutic frameworks should integrate rimegepant’s dual acute and preventive functions into flexible, personalized treatment strategies, supported by real-world monitoring and health-economic evaluation.

Strengths and limitations

This meta-analysis represents the most comprehensive synthesis to date of RCT data evaluating rimegepant for the acute treatment of migraine. Its principal strengths lie in its methodological rigor, adherence to a pre-registered protocol, and application of contemporary standards for evidence synthesis, including PRISMA guidelines and Cochrane RoB-2 methodology. The inclusion of only double-blind RCTs minimizes risk of selection, performance, and detection bias. In addition, the use of random-effects modeling, sensitivity analyses across ITT and complete-case mITT populations, and independent verification of unpublished datasets collectively enhance the robustness and reproducibility of the findings.

The large, pooled sample of 6,734 participants provided statistical power to detect clinically meaningful differences across primary and secondary outcomes, while the consistent direction and magnitude of effects across studies lend further credibility to the conclusions. The analysis also benefits from the inclusion of geographically distinct populations, which strengthens generalizability and external validity.

Nonetheless, several limitations merit consideration. First, the number of eligible trials was small, precluding formal assessment of small-study effects and limiting the precision of heterogeneity estimates. Second, all trials evaluated the treatment of a single migraine attack, restricting inferences regarding cumulative efficacy across repeated attacks or long-term use. Third, most studies enrolled participants with episodic rather than chronic migraine, and subgroup analyses by aura status or sex were unavailable. Fourth, the operational definition of the mITT population varied slightly across trials, which may introduce differential missingness across studies. However, sensitivity analyses using both ITT and mITT datasets produced consistent effect estimates, suggesting that this variability did not materially influence the pooled results. Lastly, indirect comparisons with other acute treatments, such as triptans or NSAIDs, were beyond the scope of this analysis. Notwithstanding these limitations, the results provide a reliable and clinically relevant estimate of rimegepant’s short-term efficacy and tolerability for the acute treatment of migraine attacks.

Conclusions

Rimegepant 75 mg is an effective and well-tolerated oral drug for the acute treatment of migraine attacks, producing reduced rescue medication use compared with placebo. The favorable short-term tolerability profile suggests that rimegepant may represent a useful option for individuals in whom commonly used acute treatments such as non-opioid analgesics or triptans are ineffective, poorly tolerated, or contraindicated. These findings consolidate the evidence base for gepants as a distinct and valuable therapeutic class in modern migraine management.
