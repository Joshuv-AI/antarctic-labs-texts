Analysis of differential mechanisms in flavonoid synthesis in the exocarp of virescens and nigrescens fruits and development of KASP molecular markers
Authors: Ruimin Zhang, Xiaoyu Liu, Jerome Jeyakumar John Martin, Xinyu Li, Lixia Zhou, Rui Li, Dengqiang Fu, Qihong Li, Hongxing Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOil palm is an important tropical woody oilseed crop. Its fruit is rich in flavonoids, which possess high nutritional value and exhibit antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial activities. The molecular mechanism underlying flavonoid biosynthesis in oil palm remains unclear. In this study, we performed LC-MS/MS and RNA-Seq analyses to investigate the molecular regulatory mechanism of flavonoid accumulation in oil palm exocarp. Exocarps from virescens (FS) and nigrescens (FT) at 95, 125, and 185 days after pollination were used as experimental materials to characterize the metabolome and transcriptome at different developmental stages. Our objectives were to identify flavonoid metabolite types, key biosynthetic genes, and related biological metabolic pathways in oil palm exocarp.ResultsA total of 274 flavonoids were identified in FS and 275 in FT. Across the three developmental stages, the flavonoid content in FT was higher than that in FS. The differentially accumulated metabolites were mainly enriched in three pathways: flavonoid biosynthesis, flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, and anthocyanin biosynthesis. Combined metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses allowed the reconstruction of the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway and the identification of enzyme genes and metabolites involved in flavonoid synthesis. For FS, six enzyme genes and seven differentially accumulated metabolites were involved. For FT, eight enzyme genes and nine metabolites were involved. This study preliminarily developed 9 pairs of KASP molecular markers (K2–K10). In field tests using 30 FS individuals and 30 FT individuals, these markers were used to evaluate the consistency between genotypes and corresponding phenotypes.The identification accuracy reached 100% within the tested population.These 9 marker pairs are closely linked to the EgDFR gene and can effectively distinguish FS from FT.ConclusionsThis study clarifies the flavonoid biosynthesis mechanism in the exocarp of oil palm fruits and develops markers associated with flavonoid biosynthesis. It helps improve the ornamental value of oil palm, and allows the identification of variety purity and type at the seedling stage. This shortens the breeding cycle of oil palm. It provides a resource basis for the breeding and utilization of oil palm varieties, the screening of high‑flavonoid germplasm, and the development of related products.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-08826-x.Keywords: Oil palm, Flavonoid, Transcriptomics, metabolomics, Kompetitive Allele-Specific PCR molecular marker

Background

Overview of oil palm and biological value of flavonoids

Elaeis guineensis Jacq., commonly known as oil palm, is a perennial arborescent species belonging to the genus Elaeis in the family Arecaceae, and ranks as the oil crop with the highest oil production globally [1]. Flavonoids, as secondary metabolites widely present in plants, possess various biological activities and nutritional values such as stress resistance, antibacterial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties [2, 3]. For example, soybeans contain high levels of flavonoids, demonstrating tolerance [4]. Flavonoids in citrus not only regulate oxidative stress, but also inhibit the occurrence and development of tumors in humans, which is crucial for the development of the pharmaceutical industry [5].Some studies have shown that oil palm is rich in flavonoids [6], and increasing the flavonoid content in oil palm may enhance the antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anticancer effects of palm oil while improving its nutritional value. Understanding the synthesis of flavonoids in oil palm and exploring the key genes of flavonoid synthesis are of great significance to the expansion of palm oil consumption market.

Classification and biosynthetic pathway of flavonoids

Currently, analyzing the synthesis pattern of plant flavonoids based on combined transcriptome and metabolome analysis has become a hot direction in the field of plant secondary metabolism research. In view of the oxidation degree of the C - ring, the connection position of the B - ring, and the open or closed situation of the C - ring, flavonoids are mainly grouped as flavones, flavonols, flavanones, anthocyanins, isoflavones, chalcones, and so on [7–10]. Flavonoid synthesis begins within the phenylpropane metabolic pathway. PAL promotes the deamination of phenylalanine to generate trans-cinnamic acid. Then, C4H makes use of CYP450 enzymes to transform trans-cinnamic acid into p-coumaric acid through hydroxylation [11]. 4CL assists in the binding of p - coumaric acid to CoA to make p - coumaroyl - CoA [12]. In the central part of flavonoid synthesis, CHS acts on p - coumaroyl - CoA to produce chalcone. It is the core enzyme in flavonoid synthesis and decides the flow of flavonoid production. CHI takes part in the isomerization of chalcone to get naringenin. Flavone Synthase II (FNSII) makes possible the production of flavones from naringenin. Flavonol Synthase (FLS) creates flavonols by carrying out the oxidation of dihydroflavonols. F3H adds hydroxyl groups so as to produce dihydroflavonols. DFR and ANS help with the synthesis of anthocyanins, which give plants red, blue, or purple coloration [13]. FLS and LAR and so on. Play a role in the making process of catechins and proanthocyanidins [10].

Research progress of key enzyme genes in flavonoid biosynthesis

Numerous enzyme genes in the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway have been widely studied, including HCT, CHS, F3H, UFGT, DFR, FG2, FLS, and ANR [14–18].For example, UFGT is essential for anthocyanin biosynthesis, acylation, glycosylation, and flavonol synthesis in plants [19]. In tobacco flowers, RNA interference (RNAi)-mediated post-transcriptional gene silencing inhibits the expression of NtDFR-1 and NtDFR-2. Pink-flowered plants with silenced DFR show higher flavonol contents than white-flowered plants, suggesting that DFR suppression increases flavonol accumulation [20]. In poplar, genes including BZ1, FG2, ANS, and IF7MAT are positively or negatively correlated with 13 flavonoid differential metabolites during anthocyanin biosynthesis. Additionally, FLS and DFR compete for the common substrate dihydroflavonol to regulate flavonoid metabolism [21, 22]. Qian et al. reported that in Acer truncatum, downregulation of C4H, CHS, and other genes and upregulation of FLS reduce anthocyanin biosynthesis. Meanwhile, upregulation of the downstream gene ANR promotes the conversion of anthocyanins to proanthocyanidins and increases total flavonoid content, indicating that ANR is a key gene for breeding high-flavonoid varieties [23]. Studies on flavonoid-regulated red pulp formation in grapes have revealed that the ANS gene may be related to the red pulp phenotype [24]. At present, studies on flavonoid biosynthesis-related genes and metabolic pathways in the exocarp of oil palm fruits are limited. Only a few reports have focused on flavonoid content determination and exocarp anthocyanin biosynthesis in oil palm [25, 26].

Experimental materials, objectives and scientific hypothesis

In this study, integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses of flavonoids were conducted in the exocarps of virescens (FS) and nigrescens (FT) at three developmental stages: 95 d (FS1/FT1), 125 d (FS2/FT2), and 185 d (FS3/FT3). The purposes were to find differential metabolites and genes that might play vital roles in flavonoid biosynthesis in the oil palm exocarp. At the same time, explore flavonoid - synthesis - associated genes in the exocarp of oil palm fruits, clarify the flavonoid synthesis signaling pathway, and identify and verify the regulatory mechanisms of key enzyme genes in the flavonoid synthesis pathway. Furthermore, during fruit development, the exocarp color changes from purplish black to purplish red and then to orange-red in virescens (FS), and from green to yellowish green and then to orange-yellow in nigrescens (FT).We hypothesized that the distinct fruit color differences between FS and FT oil palm are caused by the differential accumulation of flavonoids, which is attributed to the differential expression of key genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis. Besides, single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) loci were screened from transcriptomic data. Kompetitive Allele - Specific PCR (KASP) assays were used to see if the gene EgDFR could tell the difference between the oil palm varieties FS and FT.

Scientific significance and application value of this study

Virescens (FS) and nigrescens (FT) are two oil palm forms with obvious differences and dynamic changes in exocarp color. Their fruit color variations are closely related to the accumulation of flavonoids and anthocyanins. Elucidating the differential mechanisms of flavonoid biosynthesis between them can not only clarify the molecular basis of fruit color formation in oil palm, but also provide theoretical support for the breeding of high-flavonoid and high-quality oil palm varieties. This is of great agronomic and application value for improving the nutritional quality of palm oil and promoting the high-quality development of the oil palm industry.

Plant materials

Collection LocationThe research base of oil palm at the Coconut Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences, located in Wenchang City, Hainan Province.

Plant Materials Exocarps of two oil palm cultivars, Elaeis guineensis virescens (FS) and Elaeis guineensis nigrescens (FT).

Storage: Samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis (Table 1).

(5) After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min, the supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane, and transferred into a sample vial for UPLC-MS/MS analysis (Table 2).

Experimental Software

UPLC combined with MS/MS was employed for qualitative and quantitative analysis. Mass spectral data were processed and analyzed utilizing Analyst 1.6.3 software and Multi Quant 3.0.3 software. Differentially accumulated metabolites were screened using thresholds of |log₂FC| ≥ 1 (FC ≥ 2 or FC ≤ 0.5) and VIP ≥ 1. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS 26.0 software. The FDR were used for multiple testing correction.

RNA Seq Method

RNA quantification and qualification

RNA degradation and contamination was monitored on 1% agarose gels. RNA purity was checked using the NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA). RNA concentration was measured using Qubit® RNAAssay Kit in Qubit®2.0 Flurometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA). RNAintegrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000Assay Kit of the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA).

Library preparation for Transcriptome sequencing

Atotal amount of 1 µg RNAper sample was used as input material for the RNA sample preparations. Sequencing libraries were generated using NEBNext®UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina®(NEB, USA) following manufacturer’s recommendations and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. Briefly, mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo attached magnetic beads. Fragmentation was carried out using divalent cations under elevated temperature in NEBNext First Strand Synthesis Reaction Buffer(5X). First strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primer and M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase(RNase H-). Second strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. Remaining overhangs were converted into blunt ends via exonuclease/polymerase activities. After adenylation of 3’ends of DNA fragments, NEB Next Adaptor with hairpin loop structure were ligated to prepare for hybridization. In order to select cDNA fragments of preferentially 250 ~ 300 bp in length, the library ragments were purified with AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA). Then 3 µl USER Enzyme (NEB, USA) was used with size-selected, adaptor-ligated cDNA at 37 °C for 15 min followed by 5 min at 95 °C before PCR. Then PCR was performed with Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerase, Universal PCR primers and Index (X) Primer. Atlast, PCR products were purified (AMPure XP system) and library quality was assessed on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system.

Clustering and sequencing

The clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumia) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After cluster generation, the library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina platform and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated.

Data analysis

Data quality control

Use fastp v 0.19.3 to filter the original data, mainly to remove reads with adapters; when the N content in any sequencing reads exceeds 10% of the base number of the reads, remove the paired reads; when any sequencing reads When the number of low-quality (Q < = 20) bases contained in reads exceeds 50% of the bases of the reads, this paired reads will be removed. All subsequent analyses are based on clean reads.

Reads mapping to the reference genome

Download the reference genome and its annotation files from the designated website, use HISAT v2.1.0 to construct the index, and compare clean reads to the reference genome.

New transcript prediction

Use StringTie v1.3.4d for new gene prediction. StringTie applies network streaming algorithms and optional de novo to splice transcripts. Compared with Cufflinks and other software, StringTie can splice a more complete and accurate transcript, and the splicing speed is faster.

Quantification of gene expression levels

Use featureCounts v1.6.2 /StringTie v1.3.4d to calculate the gene alignment and FPKM. FPKM is currently the most commonly used method to estimate gene expression levels.

Difference analysis

Differential expression analysis between the two groups was performed using DESeq2 v1.22.1. The FDR was calculated by adjusting P-values using the Benjamini & Hochberg method. FDR and |log2foldchange| were used as thresholds for identifying significantly differentially expressed genes.

Differential gene enrichment analysis

The enrichment analysis is performed based on the hypergeometric test. For KEGG, the hypergeometric distribution test is performed with the unit of pathway; for GO, it is performed based on the GO term.

Differential AS analysis

Use rMATS v3.1.0 to analyze variable splicing events, including five alternative splicing events: SE, RI, MXE, A5SS, andA3SS.

SNPanalysis

Use GATKv4.1.9.0 to analyze the variant sites, and use annovar to annotate the variant sites.

Differential gene protein interaction analysis

The protein interaction analysis of differentially expressed genes is based on the STRING database of known and predicted protein-protein interactions. For the species existing in the database, we construct the network by extracting the target gene list from the database; otherwise, use diamond v0.9.24.125 to compare the target gene sequence with the selected reference protein sequence, and then according to the selected reference species Know the interaction to build a network.

GSEA

Use gsea-3.0.jar for gene set enrichment analysis.

WGCNA

Use WGCNAv1.69 for weighted gene co-expression network analysis.

Integrated metabolomics and transcriptomics analysis

Pathways related to flavonoid genes and metabolites were screened from KEGG (https://www.genome.jp/kegg). The criteria were as follows: pathways with no less than 25 entries and ranking among the top 25 based on P-values, with FDR correction performed using the Benjamini & Hochberg method to avoid Type I errors.

We have clarified the sample size used for correlation analysis. The correlation analysis between metabolome and transcriptome was performed using all biological replicates (n = 9), with a threshold of R > 0.80 and FDR < 0.05.

Determination of total flavonoid content

Ultrasound-assisted extraction was used. Fresh samples (0.5 g) were ground into powder in liquid nitrogen, and extracted with 75% ethanol at a solid–liquid ratio of 30:1.Ultrasonic extraction was conducted for 20 min and repeated three times.The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 5 min. A standard curve was prepared using rutin (0–1 mg/mL) as the standard. The standard curve equation was obtained with rutin concentration (mg/mL) as the abscissa and absorbance as the ordinate.

Total flavonoid content was determined by the NaNO₂‑AlCl₃ colorimetric method. A 0.5 mL sample was placed in a test tube. First, 50 µL of 5% NaNO₂ was added, and the mixture was reacted at room temperature for 6 min. Then, 100 µL of 10% AlCl₃ was added and reacted for 5 min. Finally, 0.5 mL of 1 mol/L NaOH was added. A 150 µL aliquot was loaded, and absorbance was measured at A500 nm(Table 3).

DNA Extraction from Oil Palm Leaves

(1) Weigh 50–100 mg of oil palm exocarp, grind into powder with liquid nitrogen, and transfer to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube.

(2) Add 400 µL Buffer PCL and 8 µL β-mercaptoethanol, vortex to mix. Incubate in a 65 °C water bath for 45 min until the sample is completely lysed.

(3) Add 200 µL Buffer PP, mix thoroughly by inversion, and place at − 20 °C for 5 min.

(4) Centrifuge at 10,000 rpm for 5 min at room temperature, and transfer the supernatant to a new 1.5 mL centrifuge tube.

(5) Add 500 µL chloroform, mix by inversion, centrifuge at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at room temperature, and collect the supernatant.

(6) Add an equal volume of isopropanol, invert 5–8 times to mix thoroughly, leave at room temperature for 2–3 min, centrifuge at 10,000 rpm for 5 min at room temperature, and discard the supernatant.

(7) Add 1 mL 75% ethanol, invert to rinse for 1–3 min, centrifuge at 10,000 rpm for 2 min, and discard the supernatant. Repeat this step once.

(8) Open the lid and invert the tube at room temperature for 5–10 min until residual ethanol evaporates completely.

(9) Dissolve the extracted DNA in 50–100 µL TE Buffer. Store the DNA at − 20 °C.

Primer design and KASP assay protocol

Based on the SNP loci of the EgDFR gene identified from transcriptome sequencing data, sequences spanning 100 bp upstream and downstream of each SNP were extracted for KASP primer design. Primers were successfully designed for all 10 loci (Appendix Table 2).

DNA samples were quantified using agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop ND‑2000 spectrophotometer. DNA concentrations were adjusted according to the species tested.

A preliminary KASP assay was first performed on 24 DNA samples. If the preliminary genotyping results were successful, formal large‑scale KASP validation was then conducted on the remaining 36 DNA samples.

First, DNA concentrations were adjusted to 5–50 ng according to the sample type, diluted into 96‑well plates, and re‑quantified using a NanoDrop 2000.

Next, KASP validation was performed on the normalized DNA samples. Master mix was prepared according to the table provided. During preparation, reagent names and volumes were carefully verified following the manufacturer’s instructions. The mixture was gently mixed by pipetting 5 times and briefly centrifuged at 3000 rpm (Table X).

Then, 2.57 µL of the prepared master mix was dispensed into the corresponding wells of a 384‑well plate, followed by 2.5 µL of normalized DNA. NTC reactions were included at designated positions, containing 2.57 µL master mix and 2.5 µL RNase‑free water.

The plate was sealed with specialized sealing film and tightly pressed using a plate sealer. The 384‑well plate was briefly centrifuged at 1200 rpm at 4 °C to collect samples at the bottom of each well. Any bubbles were removed by gentle tapping, followed by another brief centrifugation at 1200 rpm.

Finally, the plate was removed and subjected to end‑point fluorescence scanning and genotyping (Tables 7 and 8).

Fluorescence detection and analysis

Following the completion of KASP amplification, fluorescence signal values were read, and sample genotypes were called and clustered. Samples with the allele linked to the FAM fluorescent tag were detected as one color, corresponding to homozygous wild-type plants without base mutations. Samples with the allele linked to the VIC fluorescent tag were detected as another color, corresponding to homozygous mutant plants with base mutations. Samples showing an intermediate color, with signals detected from both FAM and VIC probes, were identified as heterozygous mutant plants.

Method for validating the accuracy of KASP genotyping results

The accuracy of KASP genotyping was calculated by comparing the field phenotypic survey results of green-fruited and black-fruited oil palm samples with the corresponding KASP genotyping results.

Result

Changes in flavonoids content of oil palm exocarp

Flavonoids represent a class of crucial secondary metabolites in plants and serve as one of the primary pigments implicated in the formation of plant colors. During the three developmental stages of virescens (FS) and nigrescens (FT) fruits, the exocarp colors show significant differences. In FS, the exocarp is green at FS1, yellow-green at FS2, and orange-yellow at FS3. In FT, the exocarp is purple-black at FT1, purple-red at FT2, and orange-red at FT3(Fig. 1A). The total flavonoid contents (TFC) were: FS1 (1.368 mg/g), FS2 (3.133 mg/g), FS3 (1.923 mg/g) for the FS genotype; FT1 (4.322 mg/g), FT2 (3.701 mg/g), FT3 (2.683 mg/g) for the FT genotype. At the same developmental stage, FT1, FT2 and FT3 had 3.16-, 1.18- and 1.40-fold higher TFC than FS1, FS2 and FS3, respectively(Fig. 1B).

A Oil palm fruits at three periods (95d,125d,185d) in FS and FT. B Total exocarp flavonoid content of oil palm fruits at three periods (95d,125d,185d) in FS and FT

Notably, the total flavonoid content in FT1, FT2, and FT3 was 3.16, 1.18, and 1.4 times that in FS1, FS2, and FS3, respectively. Throughout the developmental stages, the total flavonoid content in FT was higher than that in FS. The content trend in FT showed a continuous decrease, while that in FS increased first and then decreased. The content change trends of FS and FT were consistent with the relative flavonoid content trends in the metabolome.

Comparative flavonoid metabolome analysis of two oil palm fruit types

Flavonoid classification in the exocarp of oil palm fruits at various developmental stages

Nine flavonoid subclasses were detected in the oil palm exocarp during flavonoid accumulation.These included chalcones, flavanones, dihydroflavonols, anthocyanins, flavones, flavonols, flavanols, isoflavones, and other flavonoids (Fig. 2). Comparison between FS and FT showed that FT had higher contents of 8 flavonoid subclasses, such as anthocyanins, flavonols and dihydroflavonols.Only the flavone content was higher in FS than in FT.

Dynamic changes in the relative contents of nine flavonoids in the exocarp of oil palm fruits at different developmental stages

Relative content analysis of flavonoids in the exocarp of oil palm fruits during various developmental periods

In this study, we analyzed the flavonoid metabolome in the exocarp of FS and FT oil palm.A total of 274 and 275 flavonoids were identified in FS and FT, respectively.

In FS, the relative content of flavonoids first increased and then decreased.Flavones and flavonols were the dominant components at all three developmental stages:44.74% and 38.46% at FS1,67.50% and 9.13% at FS2,and 71.30% and 21.62% at FS3. In FT, the flavonoid content decreased continuously.Anthocyanins and flavonols were dominant at FT1 (21.17% and 46.26%) and FT2 (19.45% and 47.96%).At FT3, flavones (23.76%) and flavonols (44.70%) were the main components (Fig. 3A).The flavonoid contents in the FT exocarp were significantly higher than those in FS at all three developmental stages (Fig. 3B).

A Percentage content of various flavonoids in the exocarp of fruits at three developmental periods in FS and FT. B Changes in exocarp flavonoid content of fruits during three developmental periods in FS and FT

In summary, flavones and flavonols were the most abundant flavonoids in FS during the three developmental stages. In FT, flavonols and anthocyanins were dominant at FT1 and FT2, whereas flavones and flavonols were the most abundant at FT3.

Statistical analysis of differential flavonoid metabolites in the exocarp of oil palm fruits at different developmental stages

Metabolomic data analysis was performed on two types of oil palm fruits. Based on the screening criteria (variable importance in projection, VIP ≥ 1; Fold Change ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5), a total of 186 significantly differential metabolites were identified in FS, and 194 DEMs were identified in FT. In FS, 34 DEMs were shared between FS1 vs. FS2 and FS1 vs. FS3, 14 between FS1 vs. FS2 and FS2 vs. FS3, and 99 between FS2 vs. FS3 and FS1 vs. FS3. In FT, 30 DEMs were shared between FT1 vs. FT2 and FT1 vs. FT3, 30 between FT1 vs. FT2 and FT2 vs. FT3, and 116 between FT2 vs. FS3 and FT1 vs. FS3 (Fig. 4A-B). To identify key differential metabolites, the top 20 metabolites with the highest |log₂FC| values were selected by comparing FS and FT at the same developmental stage.Epicatechin and hesperetin-7-O-glucoside were common to all three comparison groups(Fig. 4C-E).

Develop molecular markers for the EgDFR gene

Through the screening of transcriptome data and the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, it was found that the gene EgDFR (LOC105048473) exhibited abnormal expression in virescens and 30 nigrescens individuals. NR annotation revealed that this gene encodes the DFR in oil palm. As a key component of the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, it is linked with the biosynthesis of multiple anthocyanins. In consequence, the differences in its expression levels and site mutations may be closely correlated with the color change of the exocarp as oil - palm fruit goes through development and ripening. To investigate this issue, 10 SNP loci of the DFR gene were screened from the transcriptome data. Primers were designed for these 10 SNP loci, designated as K1-K10. Young leaves were randomly collected from 30 virescens and 30 nigrescens individuals in the field, which were used as experimental materials for KASP molecular marker analysis. The aim of this experiment was to verify whether K1-K10 could serve as specific primers for distinguishing FS and FT. The results showed that except for marker K1, the % accuracy of KASP genotyping for markers K2-K10 was 100%(Table 9; Fig. 12). This outcome confirms that these molecular markers are able to accurately tell apart virescens and nigrescens oil palm. This study demonstrates that EgDFR, a key gene involved in flavonoid synthesis in oil palm fruit, plays a critical role in exocarp pigment formation in the two fruit types.However, given the relatively small sample size in this study, further validation using an expanded and independent population is warranted.

Discussion

Secondary metabolites, flavonoids, that are of great importance, are widely found in vegetables, fruits, and medicinal plants in nature [27]. Flavonoids taken from plants show effects related to anti - hypertension, anti - oxidation, anti - inflammation, and anti - cancer [28, 29]. The high level of flavonoids in oil palm not only improves the nutritional value of palm oil but also has important physiological effects, like anti - oxidation and wound healing, in the development of the oil palm [30]. This study found that with the development and ripening of oil palm fruits, the flavonoid content in the exocarps of FT and FS fruits respectively showed a downward trend and a trend of increasing first and then going downwards, which was consistent with the change trend of the relative flavonoid content in the metabolomics data.

Currently, metabolomics has been widely employed in studies on plant flavonoids.Metabolomic analysis has also been extensively applied in various crops, including rice, purple rice and Torreya grandis [31–33]. Flavonoid metabolomics was conducted on the exocarps of FS and FT oil palm fruits. A total of 9 flavonoid subclasses were detected, including flavanones, flavanonols, anthocyanins, etc., which are similar to the main flavonoids identified in Ricinus communis [34]. In FS, flavonoids and flavonols are the main components, including 134 kinds of glycosides, which is similar to tartary buckwheat seeds and Chinese torreya fruits [35, 36]. The levels of anthocyanin in FT drop step - by - step, similar to what prior studies have shown about the decrease of anthocyanin accumulation in pineapple peel with fruit ripening [37]. From the above - shown results, it can be inferred that the difference in flavonoid content between the exocarps of FS and FT fruits may result in the different exocarp colors. In particular, the exocarp of FT contains abundant anthocyanins, which is similar to the finding that anthocyanin accumulation leads to purple pigmentation in Dendrobium orchids [38].

In recent years, transcriptomic technology has been widely used to screen key candidate genes involved in plant flavonoid biosynthesis.It has also been widely applied in various crops such as Coreopsis tinctoria and pumpkin [39–40]. In FT, eight enzyme genes including HCT-1, F3H-1, and ANR, and nine metabolites such as Isovitexin, Flavosativaside, and Baimaside were related to flavonoid biosynthesis. In FS, F3H-1 showed a significant positive correlation with Flavosativaside and Trifolin. In FT, the differential gene F3H-1was strongly negatively associated with Isovitexin and Vitexin, but strongly positively correlated with Baimaside. F3H-2 exhibited a significant positive association with Vitexin-2’’-rhamnoside. The F3H gene in melon can promote the accumulation of flavonoids, flavanones, flavonols and isoflavones, which is similar to this study [41]. A transcriptional and metabolic analysis of five sunflower varieties revealed that UFGT was highly expressed in red flowers, which was associated with anthocyanin synthesis [42]. The current study, UFGT affects the synthesis of Chrysanthemin and Taxifolin, and the enzyme gene UFGT-2 is significantly negatively correlated with the differential metabolite Taxifolin. Treating sorghum seeds with Citric acid can upregulate ANR and promote the accumulation of flavonoids [43]. When ANR expression is high in the exocarp, the accumulation of (-)-Epicatechin and (-)-Epiafzelechin goes up. A research on flavonoid synthesis in Ginkgo biloba showed that genes including DFR, HCT, and FG2 may take part in the acetylation of flavonoid biosynthesis, thereby impacting flavonoid synthesis [44]. In addition, the DFR gene is also associated with changes in anthocyanin accumulation and pigmentation in crabapple leaves [45]. Studies of Amaryllidaceae alkaloids have shown that CYP73A and C3’H are important components in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis [46]. If HCT or C3’H are deficient, it leads to problems in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis [47]. In the biosynthetic route of flavonoids in the exocarp of oil - palm fruits, CYP73A, C3’H, and HCT play critical roles in phenylpropanoid synthesis. DFR affects the making of Pelargonidin − 3 - O - rutinoside, Epiafzelechin, and Afzelechin. FG2 is engaged in the biosynthesis of flavones and flavonols, in line with earlier work. In addition, the ANR gene (LOC105035639) showed significant differential expression between FS and FT.The relative content of (-)-Epicatechin in FT was consistent with the FPKM expression pattern of ANR.(-)-Epicatechin is a precursor of anthocyanin biosynthesis.Anthocyanin is a key pigment that determines color variation in plants.

The KASP technology is a type of competitive allele-specific PCR technique. Specific primers bind to target SNP loci, and fluorescent signals are used to distinguish different genotypes. It features high flexibility, low cost, and strong accuracy, and has been applied in various crops such as wheat, rice, and radish [48–50]. In the study of the EgPSY2 gene in oil palm, our research group identified 10 SNP mutation loci and developed 3 pairs of KASP molecular markers. These markers achieved 100% accuracy and could effectively distinguish between the two oil palm varieties, virescens and nigrescens [51]. Although the K2-K10 markers achieved 100% genotyping accuracy in this study, the sample size was relatively small. Further validation using a larger and independent population is needed in future research. To date, no molecular markers associated with the DFR gene and fruit color have been reported. This study is the first to develop DFR-based KASP markers for these two oil palm varieties. This method can be applied to other oil palm varieties or related species in the future. In addition, exploring the linked SNPs between DFR and genes in other pathways will help establish a multi-gene KASP genotyping system. This will provide a more efficient basis for breeding oil palm varieties with high flavonoid content.

Conclusions

In summary, this study employed combined metabolomic and transcriptomic analysis.We investigated flavonoid accumulation patterns and the correlations between DEGs and DEMs in oil palm FS and FT across three developmental stages. The results revealed that flavonoid accumulation differed greatly between FS and FT. This accumulation difference may be related to the expression differences of genes involved in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway.Among the 27 DEGs associated with flavonoid biosynthesis (such as HCT-3, CHS, and FG2), 20 DEGs (including HCT-1, ANS, and F3H) showed higher expression levels in FT than in FS. The high expression of these genes may be associated with the higher flavonoid content in FT. However, the causal relationship between them has not been verified by gene functional validation. Through correlation analysis of genes and metabolites, we identified six DEGs potentially related to flavonoid synthesis (HCT-2, HCT-3, DFR-1, DFR-3, CYP73A-1, C3’H) and seven DEMs (Trifolin, Prunin, Vitexin, etc.) in FS.In FT, eight enzyme genes potentially related to flavonoid synthesis (HCT-1, F3H-1, ANR, FG2-2, etc.) and nine metabolites (Isovitexin, Flavosativaside, Baimaside, etc.) were identified. The above correlations between genes and metabolites are only based on correlation analysis. Their specific regulatory mechanisms need further verification. In addition, this study preliminarily identified the EgDFR gene associated with flavonoid biosynthesis using KASP technology. The K2-K10 markers achieved 100% accuracy in distinguishing oil palm exocarp phenotypes.These results provide a reliable molecular marker basis for the rapid identification of oil palm exocarp phenotypes. Notably, only 30 FS and 30 FT individuals were used in this study. The sample size was relatively limited, no validation was conducted in an independent population, and field-grown samples may be potentially affected by environmental variations. In future work, we will expand the sample size and include oil palm materials with different genetic backgrounds.We will further verify the stability and universality of these markers.This will provide stronger support for their practical application in oil palm molecular breeding.
