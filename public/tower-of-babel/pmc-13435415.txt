A brain-penetrant chimeric protein combines direct glioma targeting with myeloid cell-mediated immune reprogramming
Authors: Elisabetta Mori, Elisa De Santis, Marta Scalera, Julia Meissel, Elena Ellmeier, Alessandra Flori, Luca Menichetti, Gudula Schmidt, Antonino Cattaneo, Stefano Angiari, Mario Costa, Eleonora Vannini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlioblastoma (GB) is the most aggressive primary brain tumor, characterized by poor prognosis, profound immunosuppression, and limited response to current therapies. To overcome these barriers, we developed CTX-CNF1, a brain-penetrant recombinant protein that integrates direct glioma targeting with myeloid cell–mediated immune reprogramming.MethodsCTX-CNF1 was generated by fusing Chlorotoxin (CTX), which enables selective delivery across the blood–brain barrier, with Cytotoxic Necrotizing Factor 1 (CNF1), a bacterial protein with antitumor and immunostimulatory properties. Therapeutic efficacy was evaluated in immunocompetent orthotopic GB mouse models using survival analysis, MRI, motor assessment, and immunophenotyping.ResultsSystemic CTX-CNF1 administration was well tolerated and significantly prolonged survival, reduced tumor burden, and preserved motor function. A substantial proportion of treated animals achieved complete tumor regression and resisted tumor re-challenge, consistent with the induction of durable antitumor immunity. Mechanistically, CTX-CNF1 promoted a pro-inflammatory reprogramming of tumor-associated myeloid cells and enhanced CD8⁺ T-cell recruitment and cytotoxicity, thereby counteracting GB-associated immunosuppression. Therapeutic efficacy was confirmed across distinct glioma models. In combination settings, CTX-CNF1 selectively enhanced the activity of immune checkpoint blockade in a context-dependent manner.ConclusionsCTX-CNF1 represents a dual-action therapeutic strategy that combines tumor targeting with immune modulation and effective brain delivery. By coupling direct antitumor effects with remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment, CTX-CNF1 supports the development of combinatorial approaches to overcome therapeutic resistance in GB and other immune-cold tumors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08287-8.

Background

Although significant progress has been made in understanding Glioblastoma (GB), effective therapies that ensure long-term survival are still lacking [1]. GB is the most aggressive primary brain tumor in adults, with a median overall survival of approximately 8 months, and remains one of the major challenges in neuro-oncology [2]. Current treatment strategies—maximal safe resection followed by radiotherapy and temozolomide chemotherapy—are only partially effective, underscoring the urgent need for novel and effective therapeutic approaches ([3–4]).

Despite the introduction of advanced modalities such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), cancer vaccines, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)– T or NK cells, and oncolytic virotherapy, a clear standard of care has not emerged [3]. These therapies have indeed shown only partial benefit and are often constrained by high cost and limited accessibility [3]. The failure of current treatments is largely attributable to intrinsic features of GB, including (i) high intratumoral heterogeneity, which limits the efficacy of targeted therapies ([5–6]); (ii) an immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironment dominated by immunosuppressive tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and poor T-cell activation [7–9]; and (iii) the blood–brain barrier (BBB), which restricts drug delivery to the brain and contributes to systemic toxicity at therapeutic doses [10]. Moreover, successful brain tumor treatments should also aim at protecting neuronal cells, to prevent functional deficits and cognitive deterioration, in order to maintain a patients’ quality of life as good as possible [11–14].

In recent years, our group has investigated the bacterial protein cytotoxic necrotizing factor 1 (CNF1), showing its ability to reduce glioma mass and protect peritumoral neural tissue from functional impairment ([11–12, 15]). CNF1 also exhibits immunostimulatory properties, promoting pro-inflammatory cytokine release from myeloid cells ([16–17]), but its translational potential is limited by the inability to cross the BBB ([18–19]). To overcome this limitation, we engineered CTX-CNF1 [19], a chimeric protein that combines CNF1 with chlorotoxin (CTX), a peptide capable of crossing the BBB and selectively targeting glioma cells [20–24]. CTX-CNF1 retains the antitumor and immunomodulatory properties of CNF1 while gaining brain selectivity and delivery efficiency through CTX [19]. Importantly, a previous study showed that intravenously administered CTX-CNF1 selectively accumulates in GB tissue while remaining undetectable in surrounding brain regions, supporting its ability to cross the BBB and target tumor cells in vivo [19].

This study provides a preclinical evaluation of CTX-CNF1 in immunocompetent orthotopic GB models, demonstrating that its dual mechanism—direct glioma targeting and myeloid cell-mediated immune reprogramming—yields durable antitumor responses. These findings support CTX-CNF1 as a promising, brain-penetrant immunotherapy for GB.

Materials and methods

Protein design, expression, and purification

The CTX sequence (36 amino acids, 108 nucleotides) was cloned into the pGEX2TGL + 2-CNF1 vector using BamHI–BglII restriction sites to generate an N-terminal CTX extension on cytotoxic necrotizing factor 1 (CNF1). Correct cloning was verified by Sanger sequencing [19]. GST-tagged CTX-CNF1 and its catalytically inactive mutant (C866S), referred as vehicle, were expressed in E. coli BL21, induced at OD₆₀₀ ≈ 0.6 with 0.2 mM IPTG for 6 h, and purified on glutathione-Sepharose columns. Proteins were eluted, lyophilized, and stored at − 80 °C. The mutant served as a catalytically inactive vehicle control. Protein production was carried out in Prof. Gudula Schmidt’s laboratory (University of Freiburg, Germany). Endotoxin contamination was minimized, and Polymyxin B (PB) 50 µg/ml (Sigma-Aldrich; 30 min) was used in in vitro assays to neutralize residual LPS traces.

Cell culture

Murine glioma cells

Murine GL261 and CT-2 A syngeneic glioma cells were grown monolayer in complete Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM/F12, Sigma-Aldrich) containing 10% Fetal Calf Serum (FCS, ThermoFisher Scientific), 4.5 g/L glucose (Sigma-Aldrich), 2 mM L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich), 100 UI/ml penicillin (Sigma-Aldrich) and 100 mg/ml streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Media was changed three times per week once 80–90% confluency was reached [25]. GL261 cells were a kind gift of Dr. Carlo Sala, CNR Neuroscience Institute of Milan, whereas CT- 2 A cells were purchased by Sigma-Aldrich.

Bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs)

Twelve-week-old C57BL/6J mice were euthanized by CO₂ followed by cervical dislocation. Bone marrow was flushed from femurs, tibias, and hip bones with DMEM and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. After red blood cell (RBC) lysis with RBC Lysis Buffer 1X (ThermoFisher Scientific; 3 min), cells were seeded in 10 cm dishes in DMEM containing 10% FCS, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1% penicillin–streptomycin and 20 ng/ml of mouse Macrophage Colony-Stimulating Factor (M-CSF, BioLegend), and incubated for 6 days at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. On day 6, cells were collected, washed, and re-plated at 5 × 10⁵ cells/ml in fresh medium. Cells were polarized into M0 (unstimulated), M1 (+ LPS 100 ng/ml, Sigma-Aldrich, +IFN-γ 20 ng/ml, BioLegend), or M2 (+ IL-4 20 ng/ml, BioLegend) phenotypes. After 24 h, macrophages were collected, washed, and re-plated in fresh medium in the presence of CTX-CNF1 or its inactive mutant (vehicle control) at a concentration of 25 nM. PB (50 µg/ml) was added before treatment for 30 min to neutralize residual endotoxin. Supernatants and RNA were collected for analysis after 24 h.

Human macrophages

Buffy coats from healthy donors were obtained from the Transfusion Medicine Department, Medical University of Graz, under informed consent and anonymized processing (Ethical approval reference provided in Compliance section). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated as in [26]. CD14⁺ monocytes were purified using the classical human Monocyte Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) and plated at 1 × 10⁶ cells/ml in 24-well plates in RPMI-1640 (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% FCS, 2.5 mM GlutaMAX (Sigma-Aldrich), 125 U/ml penicillin–streptomycin, and 100 ng/ml human M-CSF (BioLegend). 1 ml of M-CSF-containing fresh medium was added on day 3. After 6 days, macrophages were polarized into M0, M1, or M2 phenotypes using the same cytokine concentrations as for BMDMs, then treated for 24 h with CTX-CNF1 or mutant protein (25 nM). PB (50 μg/ml) was added before treatment for 30 min to neutralize residual endotoxin. Supernatants and RNA were collected after 24 h.

T-cell isolation and activation

CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells were purified from mouse spleens using magnetic negative selection kits (CD4 + T and CD8a + T Cell Isolation Kit, mouse respectively; Miltenyi Biotec). Cells were stimulated with plate-bound anti-CD3 (1 µg/ml, BioLegend) and anti-CD28 (2 µg/ml, BioLegend) antibodies in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FCS, 55 µM 2-mercaptoethanol (ThermoFisher Scientific), and antibiotics, as described in [27]. Cells were stimulated for 72 h in the presence of CTX-CNF1 or the inactive mutant (25 nM). Activation markers and cytokines were analyzed by flow cytometry as described below.

Flow cytometry

After 3 days of activation, cells were stained with anti-mouse CD62L (FITC), CD44 (APC), and CD25 (APC/Cyanine7) antibodies (BioLegend). For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were collected, washed, and re-stimulated with Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate PMA (50 ng/ml, Sigma-Aldrich), ionomycin (1 µg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich), and brefeldin A (10 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) for 4 h. Cells were then collected, fixed/permeabilized using Fixation Buffer and Permeabilization Buffer 1X (BioLegend) and stained for IFN-γ (APC), granzyme B (FITC), and TNF-α (APC/Cyanine7). Samples were acquired on a LSRFortessa X-20 (BD) and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (BD).

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR

RNA was isolated using PureLink RNA columns (PureLink® RNA Mini Kit, ThermoFisher Scientific) following manufacturer’s instructions, quantified using the NanoDrop ND-1000 (ThermoFisher Scientific), and reverse-transcribed using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (ThermoFisher Scientific). Gene expression was evaluated by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) using the PowerUp SYBR Green (ThermoFisher Scientific), on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR Bio-Rad system. Relative expression was calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method. 18 S rRNA (BMDMs) or Hypoxanthine Phosphoribosyltransferase 1 (HPRT, human macrophages) were used as housekeeping genes.

Cytokine quantification (ELISA)

IL-6 and IL-12 concentrations were measured using DuoSet ELISA kits (R&D Systems), following manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was read at 450 nm (SunriseTM, TECAN), background-subtracted, and cytokine concentrations were determined by interpolation from standard curves using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad).

Animals and ethical statement

Adult (≥ postnatal day 60) male and female C57BL/6J mice were used. Animals were maintained under a 12 h light/dark cycle with food and water ad libitum. All procedures followed EU Directive 2010/63/EU and were approved by the Italian Ministry of Health (Authorization 1204/2020-PR; prot. B4BB8.30, 02/10/2020). Mice were randomized to treatment groups after MRI confirmation, and all motor testing, imaging, and histological analyses were performed by investigators blinded to the experimental conditions. No sex-related differences were observed; data were pooled accordingly.

Orthotopic glioma induction and CTX-CNF1 treatment

Mice were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine (100/10 mg/kg, i.p.) and injected stereotaxically with 40,000 GL261 cells (20,000 cells/µL PBS) or of 50,000 CT-2 A cells (25,000 cells/µL PBS; [28]) into the left primary motor cortex (1.75 mm lateral to midline, 0.5 mm anterior to bregma, 0.9–1.0 mm deep). While the striatum is commonly used for GB cells implantation, the primary motor cortex was deliberately selected to enable longitudinal assessment of tumor-induced motor deficits and treatment-associated recovery through behavioral testing (e.g., Grip Strength and Grid Walk). This anatomical location allows a sensitive and functionally relevant evaluation of neurological impairment, enabling correlation of tumor progression with motor dysfunction and therapeutic response within the same animals. This approach has been previously validated [12]. Cell suspension was delivered using a Hamilton syringe and automated pump (KdScientific). Post-surgery, animals were warmed (37 °C) and received saline i.p. (1 mL, 0.9% NaCl). At day 12 or 7 post-injection (for GL261 and CT-2 A injected animals respectively), GB engraftment was confirmed by MRI and mice were randomized to receive CTX-CNF1 or its catalytically inactive mutant (C866S), (80 nM, 20 µL; 231.84 ng, intravenous injection in the tail-vein) weekly for three weeks. For immunofluorescence analyses, a separate cohort of mice received a single intravenous administration of CTX-CNF1 or vehicle at day 16 post-tumor induction and was sacrificed 48 h later. Animals were monitored daily for weight loss and euthanized if body weight decreased ≥ 30%. Long-term survivors were re-challenged on day 180 with 40,000 GL261 cells injected into the contralateral motor cortex and monitored as above.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

Tumor growth was monitored weekly using a Bruker Avance III HD 7T system with a ¹H Quadrature Volume Coil (30 mm inner diameter). Mice were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine (100/10 mg/kg, i.p.) before imaging. Sequences: FLASH (anatomical localization): FOV 20 × 20 mm; matrix 128 × 128 (156 × 156 μm resolution); slice 1 mm; flip angle 30°; T2-weighted TURBORARE (tumor imaging): FOV 20 × 20 mm; matrix 256 × 256 (78 × 78 μm resolution); slice 0.5 mm; scan time 8 min. Mice were kept at 37 °C via warm airflow and respiration monitored with a pillow sensor (SA Instruments). MRI sessions lasted ≈ 30 min. Tumor area was quantified on coronal slices using Mango software (v4.1, University of Texas), and volumes were computed in voxels.

Motor performance

Motor function was evaluated using Grip Strength and Grid Walk tests which are effective and non-invasive measures [12, 29]. Baseline measurements were obtained twice before tumor implantation and repeated twice weekly from day 10 post-injection. Tests were conducted between 10:00 and 12:00 to minimize circadian effects, and results were normalized to each animal’s baseline.

Missteps were counted from video recordings with a mirror under the grid. Affected limb error (%) = (wrong steps / total steps) × 100.

Anti-CD8 and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) administration

GL261-bearing mice were treated i.p. with anti-PD-1 (200 μg/mouse, BioXcell, BE0146) or anti-PD-L1 (200 μg/mouse, BioXcell, 217 BE0101) antibodies twice per week starting from day 12 until day 26 after tumor induction. Anti218 CD8 (250 μg/mouse, BioXcell, BE0117) antibody was given i.p. twice per week starting from day 11 and until day 25 after tumor induction. CT-2A-bearing mice were treated i.p. with anti-CTLA4 (200 μg/mouse, BioXcell, 216 BE0164), anti-PD-1 (200 μg/mouse, BioXcell, BE0146) or anti-PD-L1 (200 μg/mouse, BioXcell, 217 BE0101) antibodies, twice per week starting from day 7 until day 21 after tumor induction. Anti-CD8 (250 µg/mouse, BioXcell, BE0117) antibody was given i.p. twice per week starting from day 11 and until day 25 after tumor induction.

Immunofluorescence and imaging

For immunofluorescence analyses, a separate cohort of mice received a single intravenous administration of CTX-CNF1 or its catalytically inactive mutant (vehicle) at day 16 post-tumor induction and was sacrificed 48 h later (day 18). Animals were perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich). This time point (48 h post-treatment) was selected to assess early immune cell infiltration and microenvironmental changes during tumor progression, prior to the development of late-stage disease and extensive tissue disruption, thereby enabling a more accurate evaluation of treatment-induced immune modulation. Brains were post-fixed for 4 h at 4 °C, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, and sectioned at 50 μm. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies against CD4, CD8, perforin, granzyme B, TMEM119, F4/80, GFAP, CD11c, and arginase-1, followed by appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch) and Hoechst (1:500). Antigen retrieval (Tris–EDTA, pH 9.0, 95 °C, 5 min) was performed for selected antibodies, as described in the Supplementary Methods.

Imaging was performed using a Zeiss Axio Observer microscope equipped with an AxioCam MRm for widefield acquisition (223.82 × 167.70 μm) or a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope with Airyscan 2 (159.73 × 159.73 μm). For each animal, 6–8 fields were acquired and analyzed. Cell quantifications were performed by an investigator blinded to the experimental conditions and averaged per animal. More detailed information can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad). Two-group comparisons used unpaired two-tailed t-tests; ≥3 groups were analyzed with one-way ANOVA and post-hoc tests as appropriate. Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used for longitudinal motor tests. Survival data were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests. Data distribution was tested for normality; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Exact n values and test used for each experiment are specified in the figure legends.

Results

CTX-CNF1 treatment increases survival of glioma-bearing mice, consistently reducing GB mass and restoring motor functions

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the chimeric protein CTX-CNF1 in GB-bearing mice, we implemented a clinically relevant protocol [30]. At day 0, GL261 cells were implanted orthotopically into the mouse primary motor cortex, a common site for GB development in patients [31], allowing functional assessment of tumor-associated motor impairment. After MRI-confirmed diagnosis (12 days after tumor induction) mice were randomly assigned to receive either CTX-CNF1 or the inactive mutant (C866S), referred as vehicle, administered intravenously once a week for three weeks (231.84 ng/dose; Fig. 1A). Efficacy was assessed through survival, tumor burden, and motor performances (Grip Strength and Grid Walk tests, Fig. 1B-G).

We first observed that CTX-CNF1 treatment significantly prolonged survival in GB-bearing mice (Fig. 1B), and tumoral mass progressively decreased following treatment (Fig. 1C-D). Notably, over 50% of CTX-CNF1-treated animals were still alive 180 days post-tumor induction, with MRI confirming complete tumor eradication (Fig. 1D), in comparison to an average survival of only 28 days in the control group (Fig. 1B), Additionally, while control animals displayed clear motor deficits, CTX-CNF1 led to a significant recovery of motor function, without inducing systemic toxicity, as evidenced by the two motor tests (i.e., grid walk and grip strength), and by the stable body and organ weights (Fig. 1E-G, Suppl. Figure 1).

CD8 + T cells contribute to the therapeutic effects of CTX-CNF1 treatment

Given the marked survival benefit induced by CTX-CNF1 treatment, we investigated the possible involvement of the immune system in this effect. We initially focused on T cells, which are crucial mediators of anti-tumor immune responses and have been previously shown to be important for control of GB growth ([32–33]). Immunofluorescence analyses were performed 48 h after a single administration of CTX-CNF1 or its catalytically inactive mutant (hereafter referred to as “vehicle”) (Fig. 2A), at an early stage of tumor progression (day 18 post-implantation). While CD4 + cell numbers remained unchanged between groups (Fig. 2B-C), CTX-CNF1-treated mice exhibited a significant increase in CD8+ T cells in the peritumoral region (Fig. 2D). Notably, a higher proportion of these CD8+ cells expressed granzyme B (GzmB) (Fig. 2E, G) and perforin (Fig. 2F, H) in the tumor of CTX-CNF1-treated mice, suggesting enhanced cytotoxic activity. To assess the functional relevance of CD8+ cells in CTX-CNF1-mediated efficacy, we depleted them using an anti-CD8 antibody prior to treatment (Fig. 2I). In the absence of CD8+ cells, CTX-CNF1 failed to improve survival, and treated animals showed outcomes comparable to vehicle controls (Fig. 2J). These findings demonstrate that CD8+ T cells are essential for the therapeutic effect of CTX-CNF1. Surprisingly, in vitro assays showed that CTX-CNF1 does not directly activate CD4+ or CD8+ T cells. Indeed, the chimeric protein did not significantly alter the expression of surface activation markers or the production of key effector cytokines and cytotoxic molecules (Suppl. Figure 2). These data suggest that increase in CD8+ T cell infiltration and cytotoxic activity in the GB microenvironment may arise indirectly, via CTX–CNF1 effects on other immune subsets.

CTX-CNF1 shifts bone-marrow derived macrophages (BMDM) phenotype towards a pro-inflammatory profile

Because macrophages dominate the GB immune landscape and often exhibit anti-inflammatory phenotypes [9, 32], we tested whether CTX–CNF1 could repolarize macrophages, thereby facilitating T cell recruitment and activation. Murine bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDMs) (M0, M1-like, M2-like) were treated in vitro with CTX–CNF1 or its catalytically inactive mutant (i.e., vehicle) for 24 h. Following treatment with CTX-CNF1, we observed a consistent upregulation of pro-inflammatory genes (Il1b, Il6, Il12) and a downregulation of the anti-inflammatory gene Il10 across all polarization states in CTX-CNF-1-treated cells (Fig. 3B-D). ELISA experiments confirmed an increased secretion of IL-6 and IL-12 in M0 macrophages treated with CTX-CNF1 (Fig. 3E).

Importantly, similar effects were observed in human macrophages, where CTX-CNF1 enhanced IL1b and IL6 expression in M0, M1, and M2 phenotypes, supporting the translational relevance of our findings (Suppl. Figure 3). Collectively, these data indicate that CTX-CNF1 promotes a pro-inflammatory shift also in human macrophages.

CTX-CNF1 treatment effectively reprograms tumor-associated myeloid cells in vivo, promoting a shift toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype

To validate our in vitro findings in an in vivo setting, we performed immunohistochemical analyses of tumor-associated myeloid cells 48 h after a single administration of CTX-CNF1 or its catalytically inactive mutant (vehicle) in GL261-bearing mice. Notably, the catalytic subunit of CNF1 was found to colocalize extensively with F4/80⁺ myeloid cells within the tumor microenvironment, supporting a direct interaction and preferential engagement of CTX-CNF1 with tumor-associated myeloid populations (Fig. 4B). No significant differences were observed in the number of TMEM119⁺ resident microglia between groups (Fig. 4C, G), indicating that CTX-CNF1 does not affect the resident microglial compartment at this early time point. In contrast, CTX-CNF1-treated mice showed a reduction in F4/80⁺ myeloid cells in the peritumoral region (Fig. 4D, H), a feature associated with improved prognosis in GB [33]. As F4/80 does not discriminate between resident microglia and infiltrating macrophages, these analyses refer to the overall tumor-associated myeloid compartment. Accordingly, F4/80⁺ cells were interpreted as representing the global tumor-associated myeloid population rather than a specific subset. In line with the in vitro results, CTX-CNF1 treatment was associated with a phenotypic shift toward a pro-inflammatory profile in vivo, as evidenced by a decrease in F4/80⁺ Arginase1⁺ cells (Fig. 4E, I) and an increase in F4/80⁺ CD11c⁺ cells (Fig. 4F, J). These data indicate that CTX-CNF1 not only targets tumor-associated myeloid cells but also functionally reprograms them toward a pro-inflammatory state within the GB microenvironment. Importantly, these changes are consistent with the increased CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and cytotoxic activity observed following CTX-CNF1 treatment (Fig. 2), supporting a mechanistic link between myeloid cell reprogramming and enhanced T cell–mediated anti-tumor immunity.

CTX-CNF1 treatment protects from tumor re-challenge and boosts immune checkpoint therapy efficacy

Over 50% of CTX-CNF1-treated GL261-bearing mice survived beyond 180 days without tumor recurrence (Fig. 1B), suggesting the potential induction of long-term immune protection. To directly assess this, we re-implanted GL261 cells into the contralateral motor cortex of cured mice and used age-matched controls for comparison (Fig. 5A). Mice previously treated with CTX-CNF1 were markedly less susceptible to tumor re-growth, indicating the potential establishment of tumor-specific immune memory (Fig. 5B).

We further evaluated whether combining CTX-CNF1 with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) could enhance therapeutic efficacy. 12 days post tumor induction mice were randomized into four treatment groups: CTX-CNF1 + anti–PD-1, CTX-CNF1 + anti–PD-L1, anti–PD-1 alone, or anti–PD-L1 alone (Fig. 5C). While ICI monotherapy had no significant effect, as expected [7, 34], the CTX-CNF1 + anti–PD-1 combination significantly improved survival, with 75% of mice remaining tumor-free at 100 days. A modest survival benefit was also observed in the CTX-CNF1 + anti–PD-L1 group (20% survival at 100 days; Fig. 5D). These findings support the role of CTX-CNF1 as an immunotherapeutic agent capable of enhancing checkpoint blockade efficacy and promoting durable anti-tumor immunity.

CTX-CNF1 treatment prolongs survival in a more aggressive CT-2 A GB model

To determine whether the therapeutic effects of CTX-CNF1 extend beyond the GL261 model, we evaluated its efficacy in the more aggressive CT-2 A mesenchymal-like model [35–36]. Following tumor implantation and MRI confirmation of tumor establishment, mice were treated intravenously with CTX-CNF1 or the inactive mutant (C866S; i.e., vehicle) at 7, 14, and 21 days post-tumor induction (Fig. 6A). CTX-CNF1 significantly prolonged survival of CT-2 A–bearing mice and reduced tumor burden compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 6B–D). In addition, CTX-CNF1-treated mice showed a modest attenuation of motor impairment compared to controls, as indicated by a reduced error rate in the Grid Walk test (Suppl. Figure 4). Together, these findings indicate that CTX-CNF1 retains antitumor activity in an independent and more aggressive immunocompetent GB model.

To explore potential combinatorial strategies, CT-2 A–bearing mice were treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors in combination with CTX-CNF1 (Fig. 6E). Among the tested conditions, the combination of CTX-CNF1 with anti-CTLA4 resulted in increased survival compared to anti-CTLA4 monotherapy, whereas combinations with anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 did not provide additional benefit over single treatments (Fig. 6F). Notably, the CT-2 A model is considered relatively less responsive to immune checkpoint blockade, as reported in previous studies [35, 37]. Therefore, these results suggest that CTX-CNF1 can enhance the activity of selected checkpoint inhibitors in this model, although the extent of the combinatorial effect appears context-dependent.

Discussion

Glioblastoma (GB) remains one of the most lethal and treatment-resistant brain tumors, characterized by profound heterogeneity, a highly immunosuppressive microenvironment, and limited penetration of systemic therapies due to the blood–brain barrier (BBB) [3, 5–7, 38]. Current standard treatments, including surgery, radiotherapy, and temozolomide chemotherapy provide only modest survival benefit and are frequently associated with neurological impairment [3, 39]. Despite major advances in immunotherapy for other solid tumors, these approaches have not achieved durable benefit in GB, largely because of its low immunogenicity, the dominance of suppressive myeloid populations, and poor T-cell infiltration [9, 38]. These limitations highlight the urgent need for therapeutic strategies that combine direct tumor activity, immune modulation and effective brain delivery.

In this context, we developed CTX-CNF1, a chimeric protein that combines the antitumor and immunostimulatory properties of cytotoxic necrotizing factor 1 (CNF1) with the glioma-targeting and BBB-penetrant features of chlorotoxin (CTX) [19]. CTX is known to preferentially bind GB cells through interactions with membrane-associated complexes, including matrix metalloproteinases, supporting its tumor selectivity. Previous studies demonstrated that intravenously administered CTX-CNF1 selectively accumulates in glioma tissue while remaining undetectable in surrounding brain regions, supporting both its ability to cross the BBB and its GB selectivity in vivo [19]. Building on these observations, our results demonstrate that systemic CTX-CNF1 administration significantly prolongs survival in the immunocompetent GL261 model, inducing complete tumor regression in a large proportion of animals (Fig. 1B-D). This effect occurred in the absence of detectable systemic toxicity and with preserved motor function, supporting a favourable therapeutic index (Fig. 1E-G Fig. Suppl. 1). The use of a motor cortex implantation model further enabled functional assessment of neurological impairment, providing clinically relevant readout beyond tumor burden and survival.

Mechanistically, CTX-CNF1 appears to exert a dual mode of action supported by converging lines of evidence. First, it directly induces glioma cell senescence and death, thereby reducing tumor mass [19]. Second, it remodels the tumor immune microenvironment by promoting a pro-inflammatory state in tumor-associated myeloid cells and enhancing CD8 + T cell recruitement and cytotoxicity (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Importantly, CD8 + T cell depletion abrogated the therapeutic benefit of CTX-CNF1, demonstrating that T cell-mediated immunity is required for its antitumor activity (Fig. 2J). These findings support a model in which myeloid reprogramming and T cell activation act in concert to sustain tumor control. Notably, GL261-GB-treated mice resisted tumor re-challenge, suggesting the potential establishment of long-term, tumor-specific immune protection (Fig. 5B). While CNF1 immunostimulatory properties have been proposed [40–43], this is, to our knowledge, the first in vivo demonstration of GB immune reprogramming by CNF1. Our data are consistent with recent evidence that bacterial-derived components can sensitize GB to checkpoint blockade [44], positioning CTX-CNF1 within an emerging class of bacterial-derived immunomodulators with translational potential.

The antitumor activity of CTX-CNF1 was further validated in the more GB aggressive CT-2 A model, indicating that its efficacy is not restricted to a single GB model (Fig. 6). CT-2 A tumors, characterized by increased aggressiveness, stem-like features and reduced immunogenicity [28, 45], remained responsive to CTX-CNF1 treatment, which significantly prolonged survival and reduced tumor burden. Combination studies revealed context-dependent interactions with immune checkpoint inhibitors. In this model, CTX-CNF1 enhanced the efficacy of anti-CTLA-4, whereas no additional benefit was observed with anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 (Fig. 6F). These findings indicate that CTX-CNF1 enhances specific checkpoint pathways rather than broadly potentiating immune checkpoint blockade, depending on the tumor immune context.

CTX-CNF1 dual activity on GB cells and on the immune system may help explain the differential response to checkpoint blockade observed across models. In the GL261 model, anti–PD-1 may benefit from direct activation of the T cell compartment, which is enhanced following CTX-CNF1–mediated myeloid reprogramming. In contrast, anti–PD-L1, primarily targeting tumor-associated ligands, may be partially redundant in a context where tumor cells are already modulated by CTX-CNF1 [46]. In the CT-2 A model, the stronger efficacy observed with anti-CTLA-4 suggests that the optimal checkpoint partner for CTX-CNF1 may depend on the immune context of the tumor microenvironment. Further studies will be required to define how CTX-CNF1 reshapes immune landscapes across GB subtypes and how this influences responsiveness to distinct checkpoint inhibitors.

From a translational perspective, CTX-CNF1 integrates brain penetration, tumor selectivity and immune modulation within a single therapeutic platform. Unlike antigen-specific approaches such as CAR-based therapies [47], CTX-CNF1 is antigen-agnostic and may therefore overcome tumor heterogeneity. Moreover, its ability to induce durable antitumor responses in GB-GL261 model while preserving neurological function highlights its potential clinical relevance. Together, these features support the development of CTX-CNF1 as a combinatorial strategy for the treatment of immune-cold CNS tumors.

Limitations of the study

Although these findings provide robust preclinical evidence, several limitations should be considered in the context of the overall strength and consistency of the observed therapeutic effects. While myeloid cell activation is strongly implicated in the observed antitumor effects, its precise contribution as a primary driver of tumor eradication, as opposed to a secondary consequence of glioma cell senescence, remains to be further defined. The CD8⁺ T-cell depletion experiment was conducted with a limited sample size and should therefore be interpreted as a proof-of-concept supporting the requirement of CD8⁺ T cells for CTX-CNF1 efficacy.

Further studies will be required to fully characterize the pharmacokinetic profile, biodistribution, and safety of systemic CTX-CNF1 administration. Previous evidence indicates that CTX-CNF1 can cross the blood–brain barrier and selectively accumulate in tumor tissue [19]; however, a quantitative assessment of BBB penetration and systemic distribution is still needed.

Endotoxin levels in CTX-CNF1 preparations were not directly quantified. Nevertheless, CTX-CNF1 and the catalytically inactive mutant control were produced and purified using identical procedures, suggesting comparable levels of any residual endotoxin contamination. Therefore, the observed biological effects are unlikely to be attributable to endotoxin and instead depend on the catalytic activity of CTX-CNF1. In addition, isotype-matched control antibodies were not included for immune-targeting treatments (anti-CD8 and immune checkpoint inhibitors), which should be taken into account when interpreting these results.

Finally, although tumor resistance upon re-challenge suggests the induction of immune memory, the identity, durability, and specificity of these responses remain to be clarified. Ongoing studies using radiolabelling and in vivo imaging are expected to provide a more comprehensive characterization of CTX-CNF1 distribution and pharmacokinetics.

Implications and future directions

Future studies should expand these findings through in-depth immunophenotyping to identify the cellular mediators of long-term immunity, as well as validation in human macrophage–glioma co-culture systems and in patient-derived or humanized models. Further work is also required to define the pharmacokinetic profile, quantitative biodistribution, BBB permeability, and potential off-target effects of CTX-CNF1, which will be critical to support its clinical development. Importantly, the robust antitumor efficacy observed across distinct glioma models, together with its dual tumor-targeting and immunomodulatory activity, supports CTX-CNF1 as a promising candidate for translational development. Ultimately, the integration of CTX-CNF1 with immune checkpoint blockade or other immunomodulatory strategies may represent a promising approach to overcome therapeutic resistance in glioblastoma and other refractory solid tumors.
