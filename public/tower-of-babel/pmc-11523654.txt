Anaemia among lactating adolescents (15–19 years) in India: a repeated cross-sectional analysis of the NFHS data (2005–2021)
Authors: Ananya Kundu, Aditya Singh, Sumit Ram, Rakesh Chandra, Arabindo Tanti, Shivani Singh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnaemia continues to be a pressing public health concern in India. Despite the high burden of anaemia among lactating adolescents in the country, research addressing this concern among this population remains scarce. Hence, this study attempted to examine the prevalence and determinants of anaemia among lactating adolescents in India.MethodsWe used data from three latest rounds of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS) carried out in 2005-06, 2015-16, and 2019-21 and analysed a pooled sample of 10,689 lactating adolescents aged 15–19 years from these three rounds. Bivariate statistics was used to analyse the prevalence of anaemia according to the background characteristics of lactating adolescents. Further, binary multivariate logistic regression was carried out to examine the factors associated with anaemia in the study population.ResultsThree in every five lactating adolescents from the pooled sample were found to have anaemia (66.98% in NFHS-3, 61.08% in NFHS-4 and 66.27% in NFHS-5). Lactating adolescents with a higher education had a 30% lower likelihood [adjusted odds ratio (AOR): 0.70, 95% CI: 0.49–1.01, p = 0.050] of being anaemic compared to those with no education. The likelihood of being anaemic among the poorest quintile was 41% (AOR: 1.41, 1.12-1.77; p = 0.003) higher than those from the richest quintile. Lactating adolescents from the Scheduled Tribe category had higher odds (AOR: 1.29, 95% CI: 1.01–1.46, p = 0.001) of anaemia than those categorised as ‘Others’. Those living in rural areas were 22% more likely to suffer from anaemia (AOR: 1.22, 95% CI: 1.00-1.26, p = 0.050) compared to their urban counterparts. Additionally, underweight lactating adolescents were 15% more likely (AOR: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.05–1.26, p = 0.002) to be anaemic than those with normal BMI. Odds of being anaemic were higher among the ones receiving supplementary nutrition during pregnancy (AOR: 1.18, 95% CI: 1.08–1.29, p < 0.001) in contrast to those not receiving.ConclusionThe consistently high prevalence of anemia among lactating adolescents, especially among those with lower education level, rural residence, underweight status, and belonging to ST category and poorest quintile, underscore the urgent need for targeted public health interventions to address and reduce anemia in this population.

Introduction

Anaemia is a significant public health concern across the globe. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines anaemia as a condition characterized by a reduction in the number of red blood cells below a specified threshold, leading to diminished oxygen-carrying capacity [1–3]. This disorder disproportionately affects women, with recent estimates indicating that 31.2% of women worldwide experience anaemia, compared to 17.5% of men [4]. The prevalence of anaemia among women is particularly high in developing nations. Notably, in 2021, South Asia’s anaemia prevalence was 43.0%, slightly higher than the 39.4% observed in Sub-Saharan Africa [4].

Among all age groups, lactating adolescents (aged 15–19 years) in developing nations are notably more vulnerable to anaemia due to various factors such as poor socioeconomic conditions, inadequate diets, and limited healthcare access [5, 6]. The onset of adolescence, marked by physical changes including menarche, often coincides with increased iron needs and heightened susceptibility to anaemia [5–7]. Socio-cultural practices such as early marriages, pregnancies, and lactation further contribute to the prevalence of anaemia in this demographic [4, 8, 9]. Additionally, factors such as age, lower body weight, socioeconomic status, and environmental conditions conducive to parasite infestations further compound the risk of anaemia and iron deficiency during this critical developmental phase [1, 5, 8, 9].

For lactating adolescents who breastfeed, the nutritional demands are increased to support foetal growth, maternal tissue changes, metabolism, and breast development [1, 8, 10]. However, inadequate dietary intake among women in developing countries exacerbates the risk of anaemia during this critical period [1, 8, 10]. Anaemia in lactating adolescents not only poses risks to maternal health but also increases the likelihood of adverse outcomes such as preterm birth and low birth weight [11], which in turn rise the risk of developing anaemia among the new born babies in the first six months of life [12]. Moreover, maternal anaemia is associated with a higher incidence of postpartum depression and maternal depletion syndrome, which includes complications like preeclampsia and preterm premature rupture of membranes [13, 14]. Ensuring that lactating adolescents are free from anaemia is crucial because exclusive breastfeeding (EB) not only enhances neurocognitive functions in children but also reduces the likelihood of disorders such as diarrhoea, upper respiratory infections, and obesity later in the child’s life [10].

When compared to other developing countries, India has reported a higher prevalence of anaemia among lactating adolescents [7, 8]. Despite advancements in mother and child healthcare and nutritional awareness efforts over the past ten years [15], anaemia still affects pregnant and postpartum women in poor and middle-income countries, including India. While previous studies have extensively explored anaemia prevalence and risk factors among female adolescents, they have often overlooked a significant demographic: adolescents who lactate. Anaemia among these lactating adolescents not only affects their health, but also poses threat to the health of newborns born to such anaemic lactating adolescents. Despite being one of the populations most at risk for anaemia, adolescents (15–19 years) who are lactating has not been the sole focus of studies conducted on a national scale, which highlights the research gap.

Therefore, this study aims to fill the gap by focusing on this overlooked yet critical demographic, utilizing multiple rounds of NFHS data from 2005 to 2021 to examine anaemia prevalence among lactating adolescents in India and the factors associated with it. The findings could assist policymakers in developing targeted interventions and policies to improve anaemia outcomes in this population and enhance maternal and child health nationwide.

Data and methods

Data source

The data was drawn from three rounds of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS) conducted in the year 2005-06, 2015-16 and 2019-21. The NFHS is a cross-sectional survey that collects data on a range of demographic, nutritional and public health issues. A total of 124,385, 699,686, and 716,273 women aged 15–49 years participated in NFHS-3, NFHS-4, and NFHS-5, with response rates of 95%, 97%, and 97% respectively. A stratified two stage sample design was used by NFHS [16–18]. Sample sizes for the current study were 1801, 4860, and 4028 from NFHS-3, NFHS-4, and NFHS-5 respectively (pooled sample size: 10689). The Biomarker Schedule of NFHS contained information on haemoglobin levels (Hb) for the adolescents aged 15–19 years. Figure 1 demonstrates the process of selection of the sample for this study.

Anaemia measurement

Blood samples for anaemia testing were not obtained the authors but by experts under the stewardship of NFHS from eligible lactating adolescents between the ages of 15 and 19 years with their consent. From a finger prick or heel prick, a drop of blood was used to extract blood samples. On-site analysis of Hb concentration was performed using a HemoCue Hb 201 + analyser [16–18].

Outcome variable

According to World Health Organisation (WHO) criteria, anaemic status of females could be divided into severe, moderate, and mild anaemia [4, 19]. Anaemia for pregnant and lactating women is defined by haemoglobin concentration (Hb) below 12.0 g/dL. Further, severe, moderate, and mild anaemia in pregnant and lactating women refers to Hb levels below 7.0 g/dL, 7.0–9.9 g/dL, 10.0–10.9 g/dL respectively. The present study did not consider the three categories of anaemia and created a dichotomous variable ‘anaemia’ with two categories: anaemic (Hb < 12.0 g/dL) and not anaemic (Hb > 12.0 g/dL).

Exposure variables

Based on extensive review of literature and availability of data, numerous sociodemographic, geographic, and obstetric variables were considered to predict anaemia prevalence among lactating adolescents [1, 5, 8, 20]. To exemplify, level of education was found be a significant determinant of anaemia among the lactating adolescents. Body Mass Index (BMI) was also significantly correlated with anaemia. Statistical analysis

The data was analyzed using Stata 17 statistical software [21]. The ‘svyset’ command was used to adjust for the clustering of the survey. The analysis used sampling weights (importance weight: iw) and therefore, weighted estimates were presented. Cross-tabulation was used to compute the prevalence of anaemia among the population in relation to their background characteristics. The effects of exposure variables on the outcome variable were examined using binary logistic regression model. Prior to including the predictor variables in the regression model, Chi-squared test was employed to examine the significance of statistical association between the exposures and the outcome variable. The regression model included only the exposures that were found statistically significant (p < 0.10) in the Chi-squared test. Adjusted Odds ratios (AOR) with p-values and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used to express the outcomes of the logistic regression model. Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) analysis was run to check the multicollinearity among the independent variables. The values of VIF were below 2 which suggested that the multicollinearity was not an issue in our case.

Results

Sample characteristics of the study population

Table 2 displays the sociodemographic profile of the pooled sample used in this study. More than three out of five of the lactating adolescents had secondary education while lactating adolescents with no education made up around 22% of the respondents. Around 41% of the respondents belonged to Other Backward Classes (OBC), whereas SC and ST categories constituted about 26.72% and nearly 13.88% of the respondents respectively. Almost 40% of the population belonged to the poorest wealth quintile whereas those belonging to the richest wealth quintile comprised less than 5% of the total respondents. Distribution of the sample by sociodemographic profile showed that more than four in every five lactating adolescents belonged to rural areas. The eastern region of the country alone shared more than two-fifth of the total population (43.89%), followed by central region (15.91%). A higher proportion of the respondents had normal BMI (57.86%). Most of the lactating adolescents had only one child (85.01%). Among the respondents, more than half of them utilized public health facility as the place of delivery. More than three in every four lactating adolescents had bought or were given iron tablets or syrup during pregnancy. More than half of the lactating adolescents did not take supplementary nutrition from Anganwadi during pregnancy.

Differentials in prevalence of anaemia among lactating adolescents

Table 3 presents the differentials in the prevalence of anaemia among the respondents. About two third lactating adolescents among the pooled population were observed to be anaemic. Prevalence of anaemia was nearly 70% in lactating adolescents with primary education against 49% in lactating adolescents having higher education. More than seven in every ten adolescents belonging to the ST category were found to have anaemia whereas prevalence was comparatively lower among lactating adolescents from ‘others’ category. A remarkably higher prevalence of anaemia was found among the lactating adolescents from the poorest households (70%) than those hailing from the richest households (53.83%). Rural lactating adolescents reported a higher prevalence of anaemia (64.94%) compared to their urban counterparts. 71.82% lactating adolescents from the eastern region of the country were anaemic versus 56.79% from the western region of the country. When compared to lactating adolescents with a normal BMI, those who were underweight had a considerably higher prevalence of anemia, nearly 68%. On the other hand, anaemia prevalence was 51.24% in overweight adolescents. Lactating adolescents who delivered their newborns at home had higher anaemia levels (nearly 67%). Respondents who did not buy or receive iron tablets or syrup during pregnancy had slightly higher prevalence of anaemia compared to those who had (63.82% versus 62.79%).

Estimates from the logistic regression analysis

Adjusted odds ratios obtained from the logistic regression model are presented in Table 4. The variables included education, social group, residence, region, BMI, supplementary nutrition, iron intake during pregnancy and others. Lactating adolescents who obtained higher education were 30% less likely [Adjusted odds ratio (AOR): 0.70, 95% CI: 0.49–1.01] to suffer from anaemia than those with no education. This finding was statistically significant at a 10% significance level. Lactating adolescents from ST background were 29% (1.29, 1.01–1.46) more likely to be anaemic than those belonging to ‘Others’ category.

The likelihood of having anaemia was 22% higher (1.22, 1.00-1.26) among rural lactating adolescents in reference to their urban counterparts. This finding was statistically significant at a 10% significance level. The likelihood of anaemia was found to be higher by 15% (1.15, 1.05–1.26) among the underweight lactating adolescents in comparison to those with normal BMI. To the contrary, those who were overweight had 44% less odds of being anaemic (0.56, 0.46–0.67). Lactating adolescents from the poorest wealth quintile were 41% (1.41, 1.12-1.77) more likely to be anemic than those from the richest wealth quintile. Respondents who bought or were given iron tablets or syrup during pregnancy appeared to be 10% less likely (0.90, 0.54–1.12) to have anemia than those who did not. However, this relationship was neither statistically significant at 5% nor at 10% significance level. Controlling all other variables, the odds of being anaemic increased over the study period.

Discussion

The present study investigated prevalence and correlates of anaemia among a pooled sample of 10,689 adolescents who were lactating during the study period (2005–2021). Findings indicate that nearly two-third of the lactating adolescents were anemic. While the national anaemia rates have remained consistent from 2005 to 06 to 2019-21, notable differences were found based on various background characteristics of the lactating adolescents. Key predictors of anaemia identified in this study include age, social group, educational status, body mass index, rural residency, intake of iron tablets or syrups, as well as supplementary nutrition during pregnancy.

Lactating adolescents aged 15–19 years were found to experience a significantly high burden of anaemia. This may be attributed to their limited purchasing power during pregnancy and lactation, which restricts their ability to obtain essential vitamins, proteins, and supplements [1, 22–24]. Lactating adolescents with less favorable demographic status, such as those without formal education and ST adolescents were more likely to develop anaemia. Findings from this study are supported by prior studies [1, 5, 25]. Due to the large blood loss associated with menstruation and the fact that nutrients are lost from the mother’s body through breast milk, lactating adolescents need adequate amounts of nutrition. Therefore, lactating adolescents, are more susceptible to anaemia. Numerous earlier studies have highlighted such a finding [5, 12, 15, 20, 26]. This study shows that ST lactating adolescents develop higher odds of having anaemia, which is corroborated by several past researches [5, 27, 28]. They have historically been underserved by health care, disproportionately afflicted by undernourishment, and have suffered from socioeconomic deprivation.

Many prior researches conducted in India and other nations have confirmed that adolescents from the underprivileged and poor households are more prone to have anaemia than those from affluent backgrounds [5, 20, 29, 30]. Several factors, including the accessibility and affordability of foods high in iron, folic acid, and vitamins, significantly add to the issue. The findings of the previous investigations showed that anaemia is very common in many rural parts of our country [28, 31]. In this study, lactating adolescents belonging to the rural areas had higher odds of developing anaemia, while the risk was minimal among the urban residents. Since lactating adolescents from rural background generally get married and have children in their late adolescence, it raises the danger of adolescent anaemia. Furthermore, inaccessibility to health facilities, lack of knowledge of maternal and obstetric issues, and lack of nutrition advice possibly contribute to the higher prevalence of anaemia in rural lactating adolescents.

This study revealed that underweight lactating adolescents had higher odds of being anaemic, highlighting the need for adequate nutritional support to alleviate the burden of anaemia. These findings align with previous studies conducted in India and Bangladesh [5, 9, 32, 33]. Lack of iron, folic acid, various micronutrients, vitamins, and minerals, which had been shown to be a cause of maternal and infant morbidity and death, may be the cause of the anaemia in the underweight lactating adolescents.

The findings of this study indicated that lactating adolescents who had received any supplementary nutrition from an Anganwadi were more likely to be anaemic than those who had not. Lactating adolescents have poor maternal nutritional status frequently owing to health difficulties and early pregnancy make their health fragile and are unable to acquire optimal nutrition, even though they receive the same level of maternal health and nutrition services as adult pregnant women [3]. It might be because impoverished lactating adolescents who are already struggling with nutritional guidance and supplementary nutrition offered by frontline health workers are the target groups. However, several studies concluded that lactating adolescents receiving supplementary nutrition, had lesser chance to be exposed to anaemia [8, 20, 34, 35]. Adequate nutrition ensures maternal and foetal health, thereby reducing complications associated with anaemia [1, 20, 34, 36].

The Government of India (GoI) has launched various initiatives to combat anaemia, particularly targeting young children and women of reproductive age. The National Nutritional Anaemia Control Programme (NNACP), established in 1970, aims to reduce the prevalence and incidence of anaemia among women of reproductive age through Primary Health Centres and Health Sub Centres [37]. This programme focuses on three key strategies: promoting the regular consumption of iron-rich foods, providing high-risk groups with iron and folate supplements, and identifying and treating cases of severe anaemia [38].

In 2011, the National Iron Plus Initiative (NIPI) was introduced to offer free folic acid and iron supplements to women of reproductive age, including those who are pregnant or lactating [37]. More recently, the Anaemia Mukt Bharat strategy (2018) aims to decrease anaemia prevalence among children, adolescents, and women aged 15–49 by 3% points annually from 2018 to 2022 [38]. To enhance these efforts, it is recommended that the government and policymakers expand initiatives like Anaemia Mukt Bharat and NIPI to include lactating adolescents. Further research is needed to tailor health interventions for this demographic while considering their sociodemographic context. Additionally, implementing regular nutritional and health assessments in schools could help identify and address anaemia among students, potentially reducing anaemia rates during pregnancy and lactation.

When evaluating the findings, it is important to keep in mind the limitations of the current study. Firstly, due to the cross-sectional nature of the data gathered for this study, the results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships. Secondly, because the model used in this study only took into account the data from the NFHS-3, NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 dataset, it is possible that important predictor variables like blood pressure, diabetes, folate, vitamin B12, or vitamin A intake, as well as the effect of exercise on anaemia, were left out and may have introduced omitted variable bias. It is advised that these shortcomings be addressed in follow-up researches to offer a more precise and thorough understanding of anaemia among the lactating adolescents in India.

Conclusion

Anaemia among lactating adolescents remained constant from 2005 to 2021 despite the fact that India was the first developing nation to put in place a national program to prevent anaemia [1]. The study identified that lactating adolescents from rural areas, those in the ST category, and underweight individuals, were particularly at risk. To address this, targeted interventions should focus on these high-risk groups. For the high-risk population, it is also recommended that nutritious foods be provided through the Mid-Day Meal (MDM) programme. Additionally, as a long-term solution, it is important that adolescents be made aware against getting pregnant at this vulnerable age as it could endanger not only their health, but also could yield negative foetal and neonatal outcomes.
