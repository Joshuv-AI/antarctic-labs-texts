Porcine decellularized splenic matrix hydrogel-based vesicle sustained-release System: A dual-regulatory biomaterial for accelerated wound healing via angiogenesis and immune remodeling
Authors: Peng Peng, Siyuan Fan, Peisheng Liu, Xiaoyao Huang, Zilin Xuan, Anqi Liu, Meiling Wu, Bingdong Sui, Bin Ye, Sha Zhang, Hao Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWound healing involves a complex cascade of regenerative processes, particularly angiogenesis and immunomodulation, the orchestration of which is highly important for efficient repair of tissue defects. However, there is currently a lack of feasible strategies to coordinate angiogenesis and immunomodulation during cutaneous wound management, leading to non-healed skin defects a persistent clinical challenge. In this study, by harnessing the natural immunomodulatory property of splenic matrix and the intrinsic angiogenic effects of apoptotic vesicles (ApoVs), we developed a composite system loading ApoVs derived from stem cells of human exfoliated deciduous teeth (SHED-ApoVs) onto a porcine decellularized splenic matrix-based hydrogel (pDSMG). This pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs system maintains sustainable release of functionalized ApoVs to boost angiogenesis in the early phase of wound healing. Furthermore, the system also preserves the pDSMG capacity of immunomodulation, such as promoting M2 macrophage polarization, Treg differentiation and anti-inflammatory cytokines. With these synergistic effects, pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs creates an optimal regenerative microenvironment and significantly accelerates cutaneous wound healing, which is mechanistically mediated through inhibiting the glycolytic metabolism. Collectively, an innovative bioactive material system of pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs has been established, which safeguards efficient wound healing through concerted actions on angiogenesis and immunomodulation. With translational advantages of pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs, this approach holds potential for clinical wound management.Keywords: Porcine decellularized splenic matrix hydrogel, Apoptotic vesicles, Cutaneous wound healing, Angiogenesis, Immune remodeling

Introduction

Cutaneous wound healing is a highly orchestrated physiological process involving multiple programmed but overlapping stages, including hemostasis, inflammation, cell proliferation, and tissue remodeling, as well as the coordinated interactions of various cell types [1]. While minor injuries often resolve readily in healthy individuals, difficult-to-heal wounds cause chronic pain and functional impairment on patients, remaining highly challenging in clinical practice [2,3]. Angiogenesis, re-epithelialization and immunomodulation are critical steps in wound healing and represent crucial targets for accelerating the healing process. On one hand, angiogenesis provides the wound area with oxygen and nutrients, thereby supporting adjacent proliferating cells and promoting epithelial regeneration. This process forms the material basis for wound healing and involves the synergistic action of multiple growth factors and cytokines [4]. Our previous study has demonstrated that CD31+ endomucin (EMCN)+ vessels play a pivotal role in promoting skin wound healing [5]. On the other hand, the immune response, while clearing pathogens and necrotic tissues, regulates the intensity and duration of inflammatory responses, influencing the healing process [6,7]. Research has shown that the polarization state of macrophages is crucial for coordinating the various stages of wound healing [[8], [9], [10]], and the metabolic communication between macrophages and other cell types collectively orchestrates tissue repair [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) also suppress inflammatory responses and promote tissue regeneration by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) and interleukin-10 (IL-10) [[15], [16], [17]]. Therefore, developing therapeutic strategies that synergistically modulate immune responses, promote angiogenesis, and facilitate re-epithelialization is imperative for advancing wound healing.

Decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM), a distinct category of biomaterials, possesses an excellent capability of promoting tissue regeneration [18]. Critically, dECM sourced from animal tissues and organs effectively preserves their bioactive components and native architecture [19]. These native biomolecules work in concert to modulate host immune responses, mitigate excessive inflammation, and actively direct progenitor cells toward functional tissue repair, hence demonstrating broad applicability across various regenerative scenarios [20,21]. Therapeutics leveraging these biomaterials have been clinically utilized to enhance post-trauma tissue regeneration [[22], [23], [24]].

The spleen, as a crucial peripheral immune organ, performs dual functions of blood filtration and immune defense, while restoring hematopoietic function partially under pathological stress [25,26]. With its unique vascular distribution and highly organized structure, the spleen shows significant therapeutic promise for modulating inflammatory responses and promoting tissue regeneration [27]. As the structural framework of spleen, ECM supports its intricate microvascular network and mediate cell-matrix interactions to maintain microenvironment homeostasis. We innovatively developed a porcine decellularized splenic matrix hydrogel (pDSMG), which has emerged as a promising wound dressing offering advantages in efficiency, comfort, and cost-effectiveness. Following decellularization, the splenic tissue preserved the innate structural constituents of the extracellular matrix. After subsequent lyophilization and mechanical grinding, the resulting pDSMG exhibited retention of its essential biochemical profile along with partial preservation of its native microstructural topology. Unlike dECM derived from skin or liver, pDSMG offers a unique combination of effective immunomodulation, enhanced vascularization, and efficient re-epithelialization, alongside an optimal structural framework for sustained delivery of bioactive molecules. Collectively, pDSMG may represent a multifunctional dressing that reshapes damaged tissue environment, providing an effective strategy for chronic cutaneous wounds regeneration.

Notably, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)-derived apoptotic vesicles (ApoVs) have demonstrated therapeutic effects comparable or even superior to those of live cells across multiple disease models [[28], [29], [30]]. We specifically selected ApoVs from stem cells of human exfoliated deciduous teeth (SHED) due to their well-documented superior paracrine potency. Originating from a highly regenerative and neuro-vascular rich niche, SHED exhibit a uniquely favorable cytokine profile that enhances proliferation, differentiation, and the regulation of inflammation and immune responses compared to other mesenchymal stem cells [31,32]. Our prior work established that SHED-ApoVs significantly enhance the angiogenic capacity of endothelial cells within inflammatory microenvironments and can ameliorate hypersensitivity via immunomodulation [33,34], making them an ideal candidate to address the dual challenges of angiogenesis and immune dysregulation in chronic wounds. Despite these promising findings, clinical translation of ApoVs remains limited by their instability and rapid degradation [35]. Novel sustained release system is required to prolong the functional persistence of SHED-ApoVs and optimize their therapeutic efficacy.

While dECM hydrogels from tissues like skin or liver have been explored as vesicle delivery platforms, they often primarily function as passive scaffolds or provide tissue-specific structural cues. For instance, skin dECM excels in supporting re-epithelialization but may lack potent immunomodulatory signals, whereas liver dECM, rich in pro-angiogenic factors, often suffers from suboptimal mechanical stability [36,37]. In contrast, the spleen, as a primary immune organ, provides a dECM that is inherently and uniquely rich in immunomodulatory cues, making it an ideal matrix to actively remodel the inflammatory wound microenvironment. Furthermore, its specific porous microstructure is advantageous for sustained biomolecule delivery. By integrating this immunomodulatory pDSMG with SHED-ApoVs—vesicles known for their exceptional capacity to drive angiogenesis and mitigate inflammation—we propose a rationally designed, bilateral regenerative strategy.

This system is engineered to simultaneously and synergistically address the two most critical barriers to efficient healing: dysregulated immunity and inadequate vascularization, thereby advancing beyond conventional dECM-based delivery concepts. This composite dressing combines favorable translational attributes, including scalable sourcing, facile fabrication, and clinical applicability with synergistic bioactivity. Beyond its intrinsic regenerative properties, pDSMG functioned as a sustained-release scaffold, prolonging SHED-ApoVs’ pro-angiogenic activity and amplifying therapeutic efficacy. In vivo experiments demonstrated that pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs-treated wounds healed significantly faster, with reduced wound size and enhanced skin structure reconstruction. Critically, it polarized macrophages toward an M2 phenotype, promoted Treg differentiation, and attenuated inflammation via upregulated anti-inflammatory cytokines. Mechanistically, pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs exerted immunomodulatory effects via suppression of glycolytic metabolism. In summary, pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs represents a novel bioinspired hydrogel system that achieves spatiotemporally modulated wound healing acceleration through synergistic orchestration of immunomodulation and tissue regeneration. This study delivers a translatable innovative strategy for clinical wound management and establishes a novel therapeutic insight in regenerative medicine via multi-targeted microenvironmental reprogramming.

Material and methods

Preparation of porcine decellularized splenic matrix-based hydrogel (pDSMG)

Fresh porcine spleens were obtained from a local slaughterhouse. The connective tissue membrane on the visceral surface of the spleen was removed and the spleen was rinsed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Serviceble, China). Cut the spleen into small pieces and rinsed them with PBS three times (Fig. S1A). For decellularization, the splenic tissues were sequentially placed into the following solutions, with stirring at 300 rpm under room temperature (Fig. S1B): a PBS solution containing 0.25 % trypsin (6 h); 70 % ethanol (10 h); 3 % H2O2 (15 min); a mixed solution containing 1 % Triton X-100 and 0.26 % ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)/0.69 % Tris (24 h, change after 6 h); and a solution containing 0.1 % peracetic acid and 4 % ethanol (2 h). Finally, the decellularized tissues were lyophilized using a vacuum freeze dryer (Fig. S1C).

The porcine decellularized spleen matrix (pDSM) was ground into powder, digested in the pepsin-containing (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis MO, USA) solution (1 mg/ml, 0.01N HCl, mass ratio of pepsin: pDSM = 1:10) for 48 h, neutralized to pH 7.4 with 0.1N NaOH solution and 10 × PBS, and then gelled at 37 °C. Pluronic F-127 hydrogel(PF-127, P2443, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was prepared as previously reported and used as a control material for comparison with the pDSMG [38].

Identification and characterization of pDSMG

Evaluation of decellularization

To evaluate the extent of decellularization, appropriately sized fresh porcine spleens and pDSM were collected and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 6 h. Then the samples were dehydrated using a gradient ethanol series and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded tissues were first cut into 4 μm sections, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subsequently stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (3803098, Leica, Germany) and Masson's trichrome stain (BA4079A, Baso, China) separately. Additionally, sections were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, ab104139, Abcam, UK). The images were captured using a fluorescence continuous scanning digital section microscope with a full slide scanning system (VS200, Olympus, Japan).

For DNA quantitative analysis, fresh porcine spleen tissues and pDSM were ground in liquid nitrogen. The total DNA of the samples was extracted and purified according to the instructions of Universal Genomic DNA Kit (CW2298S, CWBIO, China). Subsequently, the DNA concentrations were measured by NanoDrop™ One spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The pDSMG samples were fixed with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde for 30 min and dehydrated with graded ethanol solutions. Before SEM imaging, the samples were lyophilized and coated with a thin layer of gold-palladium alloy by a sputter-coater. Then scanned the samples under SEM to characterize the microscopic morphology of pDSMG.

Rheological properties

The rheological behavior of pDSMG was analyzed by rheometer (Anton Paar, USA). The pre-gel samples were placed in the test chamber of the rheometer, and the heating program was set to increase the temperature from 4 °C to 37 °C in compression and oscillatory shear according to the methods previously described [39]. Parameters such as the storage modulus (G′) and the loss modulus (G″) were measured to determine the hydrogel transition temperature and rheological properties of pDSMG.

Stem cells from human exfoliated deciduous teeth-derived apoptotic vesicles (SHED-ApoVs) isolation and characterization

Preparation of SHED-ApoVs

SHED were isolated according to previously established protocols [34]. Briefly, dental pulp tissues were obtained from exfoliated deciduous teeth provided by the School of Stomatology, Fourth Military Medical University, with ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (License No. KQ-YJ-2023-162). Primary cultures were maintained in α-MEM basal medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) or 10 % exosome-depleted FBS (164222, Procell, China). SHED-ApoVs were generated following published methodologies [34]. Briefly, SHED were first cultured in complete medium until reaching 80 % confluence. The medium was then removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were incubated in exosome-depleted serum medium until full confluence was achieved. Before apoptosis induction, the medium was replaced with fresh exosome-depleted serum medium. Then SHED was treated with staurosporine (HY-15141, MedChemExpress, 0.5 μM) for 12 h. Apoptosis was confirmed using a standard apoptosis assay. Culture supernatants were sequentially centrifuged at 800 g for 10 min to remove intact cells, followed by 2000 g for 10 min to eliminate debris. The resulting supernatant was ultracentrifuged at 16,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C to pellet ApoVs. Then, the pellet was resuspended in PBS and subjected to an additional centrifugation step under identical conditions to ensure purity.

Identification of SHED-ApoVs

Morphological analysis was performed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM-1400FLASH, JEOL, Japan). Western blot was employed to identify ApoV-specific protein markers. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was conducted with a ZetaVIEW instrument (Particle Metrix, Germany) after diluting ApoVs in PBS to an appropriate concentration.

Preparation of hydrogel composite

Purified SHED-ApoVs were embedded into either pDSMG or PF-127 thermosensitive hydrogel as described previously. The concentration of SHED-ApoVs was set at 20 μg/mL for in vivo studies and 10 μg/mL for in vitro assays [33,34]. The mixture was blended and stored at 4 °C until no precipitate was observed. The resulting composites, denoted as the pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs group and the SHED-ApoVs/PF-127 group were thus prepared for further use.

Wound healing model in vivo

C57BL/6 experimental mice, aged 8–10 weeks, were obtained from the Animal Laboratory Center, Fourth Military Medical University and divided into four groups: (1) the “pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs” group, (2) the “pDSMG” group, (3) the “SHED-ApoVs” group (SHED-ApoVs resuspended in PF-127 hydrogel), and (4) the “PBS” group. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Ethical Committee of the School of Stomatology, Fourth Military Medical University (license number: IACUC-2025- kq-051).

To construct the mouse skin injury model, full-thickness circular wounds with approximately 12 mm in diameter were made on the shaved backs of anesthetized mice. Each wound was topically treated with one of the four materials (100 μL) to fully cover the wound area. Dressings (TegadermTM, 3M, USA) were then applied to the wounds and removed after 24 h. Wound healing was assessed by photographic measurement of wound size on 2,4,7 and 10 days post-operation, followed by subsequent analyses.

Histology assay

Wound skin samples were collected and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, followed by dehydration and paraffin embedding. The embedded samples were then sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm for further analysis on days 4, 7, and 10 after modeling. The sections were stained with H&E and Masson's trichrome stain. Micrographs were acquired using a fluorescence continuous scanning digital section microscope with a full slide scanning system (VS200, Olympus, Japan).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on 4 μm paraffin-embedded sections. The slides were dewaxed, hydrated, and subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval using Tris-EDTA (C1038, Solarbio, China). Afterward, the sections were blocked with 5 % goat serum albumin (AR0009, Boster, China) at room temperature for 1 h. Primary antibodies were then incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were subsequently washed with PBS three times and sealed using a mounting medium containing DAPI. Micrographs were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM, Nikon A1, Japan) analyzed with ImageJ.

Immunocytochemistry (ICC) staining was performed on RAW264.7 cells and activated T cells. The cells were collected and seeded into confocal dishes at a density of 1 × 104 cells/mL. Activated T cells were seeded into confocal dishes pre-coated with Poly-D-Lysine (R30150, Yuan Ye, China). Once the cells had adhered to the bottom of the dishes, the culture medium was removed and the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min. The cells were then permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 (T8787, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 10 min and blocked with goat serum at room temperature for 1 h. The primary antibody was added and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the fluorescent secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h at room temperature and the samples were sealed with medium containing DAPI. The images were captured using a spinning-disk confocal microscope (IXplore SpinSR, Olympus, Japan) and the relative fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software.

SHED-ApoVs were labeled using the PKH26 Red Fluorescent Cell Linker Kit (MIDI26, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, they were mixed with pDSMG and applied to establish skin wound healing model. Tissue samples were harvested after 7 days for frozen embedding and sectioning. The sections were subjected to DAPI staining and mounted. Microscopic imaging was then performed to observe and analyze the degradation of ApoVs within the composite in the skin wound model.

The antibodies used were as follows: Anti-CD31 antibody (FAB3628G, R&D Systems, USA; diluted 1:100), Anti-alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) antibody (ab7817, Abcam, UK; diluted 1:100), Anti-EMCN antibody (14-5851-82, Invitrogen, USA; diluted 1:200), Anti-F4/80 antibody (ab16911, Abcam, UK; diluted 1:200), Anti-iNOS antibody (ab178945, Abcam, UK; diluted 1:200), Anti-IL-6 antibody (12912s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; diluted 1:200), Anti- Arginase 1(Arg1) antibody (93668s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; diluted 1:200), Anti-CD4 antibody (11948s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; diluted 1:200), Anti-CD25 antibody (11948s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; diluted 1:200), Anti-Foxp3 antibody (22228-1-AP, Proteintech, China; diluted 1:200), Anti-TGF-β antibody (3711s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; diluted 1:200), Anti-TNF-α, antibody (SC-52746, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA; diluted 1:200), Anti-collagen type I alpha 1 chain (COL1A1) antibody (#P02452, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; diluted 1:200), Alexa Fluor 594 Donkey anti-Mouse secondary antibody (A21203, Invitrogen, USA; diluted 1:200), Alexa Fluor 594 Donkey anti-Rat secondary antibody (A21209, Invitrogen, USA; diluted 1:200), Alexa Fluor 594 Donkey anti-Rabbit secondary antibody (A21207, Invitrogen, USA; diluted 1:200), Alexa Fluor 488 Donkey anti-Rabbit secondary antibody (A21206, Invitrogen, USA; diluted 1:200)

Small animal in vivo imaging

SHED-ApoVs were labeled with DiR iodide (Yeasen Biotechnology, China) at a dosage of 2 μL per mouse. The labeled SHED-ApoVs were then incorporated into either pDSMG or PF-127 hydrogel and administered to skin defect wounds. For induction of anesthesia, mice were placed in an induction chamber and maintained under inhalation of 2.5 L/min oxygenated isoflurane. Changes in fluorescence intensity at the wound site were monitored at day 1,3,4 and 5 post-operation using IVIS lumina XRMS Series 2 (PerkinElmer, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions.

Release kinetics assessment of SHED-ApoVs in vitro

To determine whether pDSMG encapsulation enhances the intrinsic stability of SHED-ApoVs, PKH26-labeled SHED-ApoVs were mixed with pDSMG, using PF-127 hydrogel as a control. The mixtures were plated in six-well plates and incubated in 2 mL of PBS at 37 °C. From day 1 to day 6, 100 μL of supernatant was collected daily, and a fluorescence microplate reader (Bioteck, USA) was applied to detect the released vesicles by exciting at 551 nm and detecting emission at 567 nm.

Cell culture

Human adult Cancer Type keratinocyte (HaCaT)

Human adult Cancer Type keratinocyte (HaCaT, IM-H225, IMMOCELL, China) were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium with high glucose (DMEM-HG, Gibco, USA) supplemented 10 % FBS, and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (PS, Gibco, USA). All cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO2.

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs)

HUVECs (CP-H082, Pricella, China) were cultured in Endothelial Cell Medium (ECM, ScienCell, USA) supplemented with 5 % FBS, 1 % PS, 25 U/mL heparin sodium salts (ScienCell, USA), and 1 % endothelial cell growth supplement (ECGS) (ScienCell, USA). All cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO2.

RAW264.7 cells

RAW264.7 cells (CL-0190, Pricella, China) were cultured in DMEM-HG, supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % PS. To induce polarization of RAW264.7 cells into the M1 phenotype, LPS was added at a concentration of 100 ng/mL and incubated for 24 h.

Splenic lymphocytes (PBMCs)

C57 mice were euthanized by overdose of anesthetic and then their spleens were removed and gently ground in PBS. Splenic lymphocytes (referred to as activated T cells in the text) were isolated using a 70 μm cell strainer. The cells were then centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. Resuspended and lysed cells with red blood cell lysis buffer (AC0081M, AccuRef, China) at room temperature for 15 min. Centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min and discarded the supernatant. Total isolated splenic lymphocytes were rinsed with PBS and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % FBS Plate-bound anti-CD3 (2 μg/mL) and soluble anti-CD28 (2 μg/mL) monoclonal antibodies were added to stimulate the splenic lymphocytes for two days in preparation for further experiments.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA of collected cells was extracted with TRIzol (Life Technologies, United States) and measured with a Nanodrop 2000 UV–visible spectrophotometer. Total RNA ranging from 20 to 100 ng/mL was utilized to create cDNA through a high capacity of cDNA reverse transcription kit (Analytik Jena, USA), following the provided guidelines. SYBR Green probes specific for Tnf-α (TNF-α), Tgf-β (TGF-β), Il-6 (IL-6), Il-10 (IL-10), Arg1 (ARG-1), Inos (iNOS), Foxp3 (FOXP3), Vegf (VEGF) and Mmp9 (MMP9) were used in quantitative real-time PCR analysis of cDNA. The C1000 Touch PCR System (Bio-Rad, United States) was used for the analysis. The relative gene expressions of (Tnf-α, Tgf-β, Il-6, Il-10, Arg1, Inos, Foxp3, Vegf and Mmp9) were tested by qRT-PCR.

Cell scratch assay

Materials were pre-coated into the wells of six-well plates to a uniform thickness of 0.5 mm separately and solidified in an incubator at 37 °C. Culture-Inserts(81176, ibidi, Germany)were then adhered to the bottom of the wells of 6-well plates. HaCaT were seeded into both reservoirs at a density of 5 × 104 cells and incubated with corresponding materials for 6 h. Following cell attachment, the inserts were removed and the mediums were replaced with DMEM-HG. Cell migration was monitored in real-time via microscopy at predetermined time intervals (0 h, 12 h, and 24 h).

For the endothelial cell migration assay, HUVECs were seeded onto material-pre-coated six-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well. Once the cells reached 95 % confluence, uniform scratches were created at the bottom of the wells. The scratched areas were photographed at regular intervals (0 h, 12 h, and 24 h) in fixed fields of view. Data analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Co-culture assay in vitro

All four groups were pre-coated in six-well plates separately with a thickness of 0.5 mm and then solidified in an incubator at 37 °C. M1 macrophages and activated T cells were collected and inoculated into the precoated six-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated for 24 h (Fig. S6A). The PBS group served as the control. The polarization and functional changes of the samples were investigated.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Elisa)

The culture supernatants from each sample were collected, and the release levels of inflammatory factors were measured according to the user manual provided with the ELISA kits. The following reagents were used: Mouse TNF-α ELISA Kit (88-7324-88, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA), Mouse TGF-β ELISA Kit (88-8350-86, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA), Mouse IL-6 ELISA Kit (88-7064-88, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA), Mouse IL-10 ELISA Kit (88-7105-88, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA), Mouse IL-4 ELISA Kit (88-7044-88, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA), Mouse VEGF ELISA Kit (EK283, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) and Mouse MMP9 ELISA Kit (SEA553Mu, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA).

Flow cytometry

The polarization of RAW264.7 macrophages and activated T cells was evaluated by flow cytometry after co-culture with pDSMG. Cells were collected and stained with the corresponding antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C. The following flow cytometry antibodies were used: PE anti-mouse F4/80 antibody (123110, Biolegend, USA), APC anti-mouse CD206 antibody (17-2061-82, eBioscience, USA), APC anti-mouse iNOS antibody (17-5920-82, eBioscience, USA), APC anti-mouse Arg-1 antibody (17-3697-82, eBioscience, USA), PE anti-CD4 antibody (MA5-17451, eBioscience, USA), APC anti-CD25 antibody (17-0251-82, eBioscience, USA), Anti-Foxp3 antibody (48-5773-82, eBioscience, USA). After washing, the cells were resuspended in PBS and analyzed using a Flow Cytometer (Thermo Fisher). Data were analyzed by FlowJo.

The Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (BMS500FI-100, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) was utilized to assess the apoptotic SHED according to manufacturer's instructions. Annexin-V fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) and propidium iodide (PI) combined working solution were prepared. Harvested cells were resuspended and incubated with the working solution for 15 min at 4 °C in the dark. After staining, cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer, and the percentage of Annexin V positive cells was determined.

Western blot

Western blot analysis was performed as previously reported [40]. Briefly, RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (Beyotime, China) was added to the samples on ice to extract the total protein. The protein concentrations were determined according to the instructions provided by the BCA kit (Beyotime, China) and adjusted to a uniform level. The proteins were then separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, IL00010). The membranes were blocked with 5 % non-fat milk powder dissolved in TBST buffer (Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween 20) at room temperature for 1–2 h. They were then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in the blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C and washed three times with TBST buffer. Membranes were blocked with 5 % non-fat milk powder dissolved in TBST buffer (Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween 20) at room temperature for 1–2 h. Incubated the membranes with primary antibodies diluted in the blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C and washed them with sufficient TBST buffer three times. Then membranes were incubated with corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Washed membranes was reacted with enhanced chemiluminescence kit (AP0081M, AccuRef, China) and detected using a chemiluminescent imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). Data analysis was performed by ImageJ software.

The antibodies used were as follows: Anti-Arg1 antibody (93668, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; diluted 1:1000), Anti-iNOS antibody (ab178945, Abcam, UK; diluted 1:1000), Anti-IL-6 antibody (12912s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; diluted 1:1000), Anti-Foxp3 antibody (22228-1-AP, Proteintech, China; diluted 1:1000), Anti-TGF-β antibody (3711s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; diluted 1:1000), Anti-TNF-α antibody (SC-52746, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA; diluted 1:500), Anti-GAPDH antibody (M3029, Immunoway, USA; diluted 1:1000), Anti-CD63 antibody (SC-5275, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA; diluted 1:1000), Anti-CD9 antibody (A24130, ABclonal, China; diluted 1:1000), Anti-TSG101 antibody (A24130, ABclonal, China; diluted 1:1000), Anti-GM130 antibody (#610822, BD Pharmingen, USA; diluted 1:1000), Anti-Caspase3 antibody (#9662, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; diluted 1:1000), Anti-Cleaved-Caspase3 antibody (#9664, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; diluted 1:1000).

Mass spectrometry-based protein identification

Total proteins from pDSMG were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. After centrifugation, the proteins in the resulting supernatant were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The obtained protein bands were transferred into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube and rinsed twice with ultrapure water. Proteins were then enzymatically digested to produce smaller peptides and then the proteomic data analysis was performed by Shanghai Luming Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

L - lactic acid detection

Cells were collected to measure their lactic acid content using the L-Lactic Acid (L-LA) Content Detection Kit (BC5345, Solarbio, China), following the instructions provided. The relative expression of lactic acid was quantified by normalizing the data to the expression levels of the control group.

Seahorse metabolic assays

Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), reflecting glycolysis, was measured using a Seahorse Extracellular Flux Analyzer (XFe96, Agilent, USA) according to the manufacturer's instruments. Macrophages were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well in a 96-well Seahorse XFe96 Cell Culture Microplate (W26224, Agilent, USA) and grown to 80–90 % confluency at the time of measurement. Activated T cells were seeded at a density of 8 × 104 cells/well in a 96-well Seahorse XFe96 Cell Culture Microplate, which was pre-coated with Cell-TakTM (354240, Corning, USA) before measurement and allowed to adhere for 30 min. Analysis was performed using the XFe96 analyser as per manufacturer's instructions.

Tube formation assay

Matrigel (354234, Corning, USA) was evenly spread in a 96-well plate at a volume of 60 μL per well and allowed to solidify at 37 °C. HUVECs were harvested using a trypsin-EDTA solution and resuspended in the supernatants of each sample. The cell count was determined using a cell counter (Denovix, USA). A cell suspension containing 60 μL (3 × 104 cells) was gently added onto the solidified Matrigel in each well. The plate was then incubated in a 5 % CO2 humidified atmosphere at 37 °C. Images were taken at 2, 4, and 6 h in the same area using an optical microscope and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0). The two-tailed Student's t-test and one way ANOVA were employed to evaluate differences in means between two groups.

Results

Preparation and characterizations of pDSMG and SHED-ApoVs

After decellularization, the pink-ish porcine spleen turned white-ish and translucent. Then the pDSM was lyophilized, ground into powder and digested with pepsin (Fig. 1A). After pH neutralization and ionic balancing, the resulting pre-gel solution was incubated at 37 °C for about 20 min to form pDSMG (Fig. 1E). Both H&E and DAPI staining confirmed the absence of nuclei in the decellularized spleen tissues (Fig. 1B–C). Likewise, cellular DNA content was decreased to 5.94 ng/μl compared to that of the native porcine spleen (204.83 ng/μl), implicating a successful decellularization (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, SEM characterization revealed that pDSMG exhibited the typical structure of hydrogel (Fig. 1F). Rheological analysis showed that the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G’’) of pDSMG pre-gel solution increased rapidly and stabilized after temperature elevation to ∼ 13 °C, reflecting the hydrogel formation (Fig. 1G).

After apoptosis induction, flow cytometry analysis of Annexin V/PI double staining showed an elevated percentage of apoptotic SHED (Fig. 1SD-E), and subsequently, SHED-ApoVs were isolated. TEM analysis of SHED-ApoVs displayed classical double-membrane spherical structure (Fig. 1H). NTA showed a particle size range of 400–500 nm, consistent with the size of apoptotic vesicles (Fig. 1I). Western blot analysis indicated that SHED-ApoVs expressed common markers of extracellular vesicles (EVs), such as CD63, CD9 and TSG101, but failed to express exosomes-specific marker GM130. Compared to uninduced SHED, SHED-ApoVs expressed specific apoptosis-associated marker cleaved-caspase3 (Fig. 1J). These findings verifed that the ApoVs derived from SHED in this study exhibited classic characteristics of high-purity apoptotic vesicles originating from MSCs.

pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs promoted wound healing in vivo

A full-thickness skin defect model was established on the dorsum of mice to sequentially evaluate the effects of different treatments on wound healing. Representative macroscopic images captured at distinct time points presented and the wound healing rate revealed that the pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs treatment significantly accelerated wound healing, particularly during the early phase of wound healing (Fig. 2A–C). Initially, the SHED-ApoVs group exhibiting a superior wound healing rate compared to the pDSMG group at the 2-day post-injury time point. However, by day 4 post-injury, this trend was reversed with the pDSMG group surpassed SHED-ApoVs group in terms of wound healing rate (Fig. 2C).

During cutaneous wound healing, epithelial thickness transitions from initial proliferative repair to subsequent restoration of normal tissue thickness and function. Thus, its dynamic changes serve as a reliable indicator, reflecting the phase and progression of the repair process. H&E staining and Masson's trichrome staining performed 10 days post-treatment revealed that the pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs group exhibited almost complete wound healing, characterized by the smallest wound widths, thinnest regenerative epithelial thickness, and the most abundant dermal tissue and hair follicles. In contrast, fewer appendages were regenerated in the other groups (Fig. 2D–I). Notably, the epithelial thickness in the pDSMG group was greater than that in the SHED-ApoVs group (Fig. 2D, E, H), implying that pDSMG treatment more effectively promotes wound epithelialization.

Next, IF staining of CD31 and α-SMA was performed to explore the vascular conditions among the four groups. CD31 is used as endothelial cells marker, while α-SMA was expressed during the later stage of vascular maturation, reflecting the differentiation of vascular smooth muscle cells. The data demonstrated that the pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs group exhibited significantly higher relative areas and numbers of newly formed blood vessels (CD31+/α-SMA+) compared to other groups. Meanwhile, the pDSMG group exhibited superior pro-angiogenic effects compared to the SHED-ApoVs group at day 10 post-injury (Fig. 2J–L). Furthermore, evaluations based on histology and hematology provided preliminary evidence for the material's low immunogenicity (Fig. S3, Table S2 and S3).

These findings revealed that pDSMG accelerated wound healing and its efficacy was further enhanced when combined with SHED-ApoVs.

pDSMG/ApoVs promoted re-epithelialization and angiogenesis at early phases of wound healing

Given that the pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs treatment significantly accelerated the early stages of wound healing, we further evaluated the wound healing conditions at day 4 post-treatment. When compared with other groups, the pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs group exhibited relatively smaller wound sizes. However, no significant differences were observed between the pDSMG group and the SHED-ApoVs group (Fig. 3A). Histological staining revealed that the pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs group exhibited a significantly more elongated neo-epithelial layer compared to other groups. Additionally, the neo-epithelial layer in the pDSMG group was longer than that in the SHED-ApoVs group (Fig. 3B). Subsequently, a scratch assay was conducted on HaCaT to further assess the migratory-promoting potential of the four groups (Fig. 3C). The application of pDSMG significantly enhanced the migratory capacity of HaCaT. Moreover, the combination of pDSMG with SHED-ApoVs demonstrated an optimized effect in promoting HaCaT migration (Fig. 3D and E). The aforementioned data suggest that pDSMG, particularly the pDSMG combined with SHED-ApoVs, exhibits robust re-epithelialization potential in both in vivo and vitro experiments.

Then, IF staining was performed to evaluate CD31 and EMCN double-positive vessels, which were identified as H-type vessels. These vessels play a pivotal role in initiating tissue regeneration by rapidly extending into the injured regions and reconstructing the vascular network. The results demonstrated that the SHED-ApoVs enhanced the area and number of CD31 and EMCN double-positive vessels, compared with pDSMG group. In this process, pDSMG exerted a more pronounced pro-epithelialization capacity, while SHED-ApoVs displayed a stronger involvement in early-stage angiogenesis.

Compared with the SHED-ApoVs and PBS groups, the pDSMG group significantly accelerated wound closure and re-epithelialization (Fig. 4A–B).

The magnified histological image revealed that the pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs group effectively established an orderly epithelial layer, facilitated the regeneration of appendages beneath the scar, and promoted collagen deposition. Multifaceted mechanisms of pDSMG in promoting wound healing

To further elucidate whether pDSMG could retain and sustainably release SHED-ApoVs directly onto injured tissues, thereby prolonging their pro-angiogenic activity, the stability and degradation rate of SHED-ApoVs were detected. In vivo fluorescence imaging showed that the fluorescence intensity in the pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs group decayed at a significantly slower rate compared to the control group. By day 4, detectable fluorescence signal was still observed in the pDSMG group while that in the PF-127 group diminished rapidly and was nearly undetectable by day 3 (Fig. 5A). Meanwhile, at 7 days post-injury, the pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs group displayed a distinct band-like distribution of PKH26+ cells along the superficial wound region, with uniform coverage of the lesion surface (Fig. 5B). The release kinetics assays also revealed that the pDSMG group exhibited a markedly slower decline in SHED-ApoVs concentration compared to the PF-127 group (Fig. 5C). The intrinsic stability of SHED-ApoVs was maintained by pDSMG, as shown by NTA and Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analyses (Fig. S4). These observations highlight the capacity of pDSMG to sustainably deliver ApoVs, thereby effectively prolonging their local retention and bioactivity. Proteomics sequencing analysis was performed to elucidate the protein profile of pDSMG. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of biological processes revealed that the top 16 enrichment pathways were predominantly involved in wound healing-related pathways, including “keratinization,” “innate immune response,” and “platelet aggregation” (Fig. S2A). Additionally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis identified the top 19 enrichment pathways, which encompassed “ECM-receptor interaction,” “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway,” “platelet activation,” and “vascular smooth muscle contraction” (Fig. 5D).

The scratch assay performed on HUVECs demonstrated that pDSMG more effectively accelerated the migration of HUVECs compared to SHED-ApoVs alone. Furthermore, when pDSMG was combined with SHED-ApoVs, a synergistic effect was observed, resulting in a significant enhancement of HUVECs migration at the early time point of 12 h (Fig. 5E–F). pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs modulated macrophage polarization in vitro

Macrophages are typically capable of polarizing into either the pro-inflammatory M1 or the reparative M2 phenotype. RAW264.7 macrophages could be triggered into M1 phenotype in the presence of lipopolysaccharide (LPS). The polarization and function of M1-type macrophages were detected after co-cultivation with each group for 24 h to further investigate their immunomodulatory ability in vitro.

Macrophage polarization serves as a key indicator of inflammatory responses, with elevated CD206 expression representing a hallmark of the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. Flow cytometry showed that after 24 h of co-culture with pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs, the proportion of F4/80+CD206+ macrophages increased significantly from 12.93 % to 73.42 % (Fig. 6A and B). ICC staining and flow cytometry further confirmed a notable reduction in iNOS positive M1 macrophages, accompanied by the accumulation of Arg-1 expression, indicating that pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs treatment promoted macrophage M2 polarization and alleviated immune responses (Fig. 6C–J). Notably, the pDSMG group also exhibited considerable immunomodulatory capability compared to the SHED-ApoVs and PBS group. Additionally, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs treatment upregulated the mRNA expression of M2 markers (Arg1, Il-10, Tgf-β) while downregulated M1 markers (Il-6, Inos, Tnf-α), suggesting suppression of M1 phenotypic differentiation and facilitation of the M2 state (Fig. 6K–P). Consistent with the gene expression trends, western blot analysis showed that the increased protein expression of ARG1 and TGF-β, alongside decreased levels of TNF-α and iNOS (Fig. 6Q). Beyond polarization markers, cytokine secretion levels also reflect the inflammatory state of macrophages. ELISA results showed that pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs treatment elevated anti-inflammatory cytokine levels while reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 6R–U), indicating attenuated immune activation.

pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs promoted tregs differentiation in vitro

Given the crucial role of T cells in cell-mediated immunity, particularly the ability of Tregs to modulate immune responses through anti-inflammatory cytokines secretion, we further investigated whether pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs was involved in the differentiation and function of T cells. Flow cytometry analysis revealed an increased percentage of Tregs from 2.16 % to 17.25 % through CD4, CD25 and Foxp3 staining (Fig. 7A), indicating that pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs dressing promoted the differentiation of conventional T cells into Tregs. This finding was supported by immunocytochemistry (ICC), which showed an elevated proportion of CD4+Foxp3+ Tregs following treatment (Fig. 7C–E). Consistent with these observations, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs treatment upregulated the expression of Foxp3, Il-10, and Tgf-β, while significantly downregulating Il-6 and Tnf-α, suggesting a shift toward an anti-inflammatory T cell phenotype (Fig. 7F–J). Western blot analysis further confirmed increased protein expression of TGF-β and Foxp3, along with decreased TNF-α levels (Fig. 7K).

Elisa results showed that the pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs treatment group exhibited significantly elevated levels of VEGF and MMP9 in the supernatant, suggesting enhanced angiogenic activity and extracellular matrix remodeling capacity (Fig. 8I,L). Scratch assay was then applied to evaluate the immunomodulatory and angiogenic properties of pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs. HUVECs were cultured on matrigel with different conditioned medium. Discussion

Wound healing is an intrinsic repair process of the organism in response to injury, which involves complex cellular and molecular interactions and holds significance in maintaining health [[41], [42], [43]]. While minor skin wounds heal within days, larger injuries caused by trauma, acute diseases, or major surgeries may take weeks to heal [8]. This discrepancy highlights the remarkable challenge of accelerating wound healing, which remains a global conundrum [[44], [45], [46]]. Strategic delivery of therapeutic biological agents offers a promising approach, driving breakthroughs in advanced wound treatment [[47], [48], [49]]. In this study, we innovatively developed pDSMG from readily available sources of the porcine spleen, which was then leveraged to load SHED-ApoVs and release them in a sustained mode. Through establishing the pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs system, we created a multifunctional wound dressing with excellent biocompatibility and significant clinical translation potential. Indeed, as demonstrated here, pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs accelerated cutaneous wound healing remarkably in mice. Future work will be need to verify the effects in pre-clinical experiments in large animals.

Effective wound healing necessitates a precisely coordinated sequence of phases: immunomodulation, neovascularization, re-epithelialization, and appendage formation [50,51]. Particularly, the multifunctional therapeutic properties of pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs in accelerating wound healing and stimulating skin tissue regeneration were revealed in orchestrating immunological and angiogenic responses. Notably, while both individual components of pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs exhibited therapeutic effects on skin wounds, their mechanisms differed yet were interconnected in the composite system. Specifically, ApoVs significantly promoted endothelial cell migration during early wound healing and demonstrated inherent pro-angiogenic activity, while pDSMG demonstrated more potent regulatory effects on macrophages and Treg differentiation. Intriguingly, synergistic application of pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs exerted a concerted action on immunomodulation through inhibiting the glycolytic metabolism, which then indirectly strengthened angiogenesis in the formed anti-inflammatory environment. It remains to dissect the detailed molecular signals responsible for these processes. Future work should employ proteomic analysis of the pDSMG and SHED-ApoV cargo, coupled with receptor blockade experiments, to identify the key ligand-receptor pairs and signaling pathways responsible for this synergy.

The spleen is a gatekeeper of systemic immunity where immune responses against blood-borne pathogens are initiated and sustained, harboring substantial reservoirs of macrophages and lymphocytes [52]. ECM sculpts microanatomical niches in the spleen that make diverse contributions to physiological spleen functions and regulate the homeostasis of immune cells [53]. While conventional hydrogels often act merely as passive carriers for cytokines and growth factors [50], pDSMG intrinsically exhibited remarkable immunomodulatory capacity in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, pDSMG demonstrated exceptional mechanical performance due to its 3D porous architecture as an excellent carrier for sustained release of SHED-ApoVs. These superior properties are likely attributable to the structural complexity and diverse repertoire of bioactive factors preserved within the naturally derived decellularized matrix components [[54], [55], [56]]. Together with our findings, emerging evidence suggests that decellularized splenic ECM can be used in regenerative medicine [[57], [58], [59], [60]] and possesses chemotactic signaling [26,61,62], which has been successfully employed to support hepatic regeneration [63]. Our study thus extends the horizon of applying decellularized splenic ECM and further provides supportive proof of its therapeutic potential in skin wounds. The potent immunomodulatory activity revealed here prompts further investigation into whether pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs can effectively treat other inflammation-driven conditions, such as autoimmune skin disorders or fibrotic wounds.

MSC-derived ApoVs possess distinct advantages including low immunogenicity, convenient storage, and suitability for drug delivery engineering [28]. Accumulating evidence highlights their promising therapeutic potential in tissue regeneration and wound repair [29]. Meanwhile, ApoVs inherit parental MSC-specific biomolecules and also show source tissue-related properties. For example, our previous investigations demonstrate that SHED-ApoVs mediate TUFM transfer to activate endothelial cell autophagy, driving neovascularization in the ischemic microenvironment during dental pulp regeneration [33]. Moreover, our previous work indicates that SHED-ApoVs can ameliorate hypersensitivity of the immune system via inducing CD8+ T cell apoptosis through calcium overload and mitochondrial dysfunction [34]. Therefore, SHED-ApoVs hold potential to comprehensively modulate the wound healing process. Despite their therapeutic potential as signaling agents, ApoVs face limitations due to rapid clearance within pathological microenvironments and susceptibility to degradation, which require advanced delivery strategies to enhance bioavailability. In this regard, pDSMG provides a sustained-release platform to extend the bioavailability of SHED-ApoVs, enabling synergistic enhancement of wound healing. To enhance clinical translation, future efforts will focus on optimizing the delivery formulation, such as developing an injectable or sprayable version of the composite, and evaluating its stability as a lyophilized powder for storage.

Advances in molecular and systems biology have revealed that immune cells undergo profound metabolic reprogramming during defensive responses, which plays a crucial regulatory role in their differentiation, activation, and effector functions [6,64]. Under stress conditions, pro-inflammatory T cells and M1-polarzied macrophages rapidly switch from an oxidative phosphorylation-dominated quiescent state to a glycolysis-driven hypermetabolic state, generating sufficient ATP and metabolic intermediates to support cell proliferation and effector molecules secretion [16,65]. Conversely, Tregs and M2 macrophages preferentially utilize fatty acid oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation to support their anti-inflammatory and reparative functions [16,66]. Conversion between these two metabolic modes reflects cellular adaptation to different pathological states. In this study, co-culture with pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs significantly suppressed the glycolytic activity in macrophages and T cells, indicating the composite system modulates immune cell function by suppressing their pro-inflammatory metabolic state. This immunomodulatory effect is potent and warrants notice because of its wide application potential for treating inflammatory diseases. Future studies will be expected to test the efficacy more broadly. The profound immunometabolic reprogramming effect warrants validation in more complex, chronic wound models, such as in diabetic or aged mice, and a critical safety and efficacy assessment in a large animal model as a prerequisite for clinical application.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed an innovative bioactive wound dressing, pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs, by integrating pDSMG ApoVs. pDSMG provided exceptional mechanical support and intrinsic bioactivity due to its preserved native ECM composition while simultaneously functioning as a sustained-release platform for therapeutic SHED-ApoVs. SHED-ApoVs specifically enhanced early angiogenesis, particularly type H vessel formation. The composite synergistically accelerated wound healing in murine models, promoting rapid wound closure, enhanced vascularization, re-epithelialization, functional appendage regeneration, and organized collagen deposition. Mechanistically, pDSMG/SHED-ApoVs regulated immune cell metabolic reprogramming, suppressing pro-inflammatory glycolysis. This synergy between the versatile natural biomaterial hydrogel and the therapeutic ApoVs creates a multifaceted system with robust practicality and outstanding clinical translation prospects, not only for advanced wound healing but also for broader applications in immune-related disorders and regenerative medicine.
