Breast cancer progression in the presence of treated and untreated left ventricular dysfunction
Authors: Celine Civati, B K Goovaerts, S Van Laere, S Van den Bogaert, J Ott, B van Berlo, VFM Segers, GW De Keulenaer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent studies have shown that heart failure (HF) and left ventricular (LV) dysfunction are associated with enhanced tumor growth. However, whether treating LV dysfunction mitigates its impact on cancer progression remains unknown. We hypothesized that HF treatments would attenuate tumor growth in a rodent model of post-myocardial infarction (MI)-induced LV dysfunction, and that different pharmacological agents (carvedilol, enalapril, and empagliflozin) might exert distinct effects on tumor progression.MethodsMI was induced in female BALB/c mice, leading to LV dysfunction. Mice with LV ejection fraction < 40% two weeks after MI received daily vehicle or one of three HF treatments by gavage. Two weeks later, 2 × 105 4T1 metastatic breast cancer cells were injected, with a three-week follow-up. The effects of HF treatments on cancer progression were assessed by analyzing: (i) tumor size in vivo over 22 days, (ii) lung metastasis development and (iii) gene expression levels in tumor tissue by RNA sequencing.ResultsEnalapril reversed several MI-induced transcriptomic changes in tumor tissue. Empagliflozin reduced primary tumor growth, while carvedilol reduced metastatic clusters. These effects were absent in control mice without MI, and neither drug directly affected cultured 4T1 cells. Transcriptomic analysis revealed treatment-specific inflammatory pathway regulation. Notably, carvedilol and empagliflozin restored MI-suppressed IFN-γ expression in tumors, accompanied by increased STAT1 expression in 4T1 cells.ConclusionSpecific HF treatments can mitigate cancer growth in a mouse model of MI-induced LV dysfunction, with outcomes varying by treatment. These benefits occurred only with LV dysfunction, suggesting they result from changes in HF pathophysiology rather than direct drug effects on tumor cells. Restoration of immune signaling may contribute to these effects.Keywords: LV dysfunction, Breast cancer, Heart failure treatment, Reversed cardio-oncology

Background

Heart failure (HF) and cancer are the two most common causes of death worldwide [1].

Despite their distinct clinical manifestations, these two diseases exhibit several common risk factors, including smoking, obesity or diabetes, and share several underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, such as inflammation and immune dysregulation [2–4]. Nevertheless, HF and cancer have conventionally been considered to be separate entities. Among the various forms of HF, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) is a condition in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body’s needs. HFrEF is characterized by impaired systolic function and it is often accompanied by significant comorbidities, resulting in poor quality of life and prognosis [5]. Ischemic heart disease, especially myocardial infarction, is the leading cause of HFrEF [6].

In the past 40 years, therapeutic advances in HFrEF have improved survival [7]. Recent epidemiological studies have shown that the number of patients diagnosed with both HF and cancer is rising [8–10]. In the years following diagnosis, patients with HFrEF have an increased risk of developing cancer compared to the general population, even after adjusting for shared risk factors [11, 12]. Several rodent studies suggest a direct link between HF and cancer, indicating that a cardiac dysfunction/failure may promote cancer [13–20]. Different underlying mechanisms have been proposed, including the secretion of soluble factors from the failing heart, immune cell reprogramming, microbial dysbiosis and neurohumoral imbalances [13–16]. Understanding the links between HF and cancer is crucial because it may lead to therapies that can uncouple them.

To date, HFrEF is treated with a combination of drugs targeting different pathways including β-blockers, angiotensin-converting-enzyme (ACE)-inhibitors, angiotensin-II receptor blockers, sodium-glucose-transporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and neprilysin inhibitors [21]. The direct effects of these HF treatments on cancer growth, independent of HF, have been studied both in vitro and in vivo, yielding conflicting results [22–24]. However, whether these drugs affect cancer growth in conditions of LV dysfunction/failure and hence uncouple HF and cancer has never been studied.

In this study, we aimed to investigate triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) progression in mice with MI-induced LV dysfunction [25, 26]. By using three treatments targeting different pathways, we studied the distinct effects of HF treatments on cancer growth in conditions of LV dysfunction. We evaluated three key aspects of tumor biology: primary tumor growth, metastatic lung cluster formation, and transcriptomic changes analyzed through RNA sequencing (Fig. 6).

Central illustration. In this study, we used a mouse model combining LV dysfunction, HF treatments, and metastatic breast cancer to assess whether HF treatments mitigate cancer progression in a post-MI rodent model and if treatment choice influenced this effect. Our results showed that cancer progression can be analyzed at multiple levels, with HF treatments affecting each differently. Empagliflozin reduced tumor growth, carvedilol decreased metastasis, and all treatments uniquely altered the tumor transcriptome. Notably, these effects appeared only in the presence of a post-MI dysfunctional heart, suggesting the treatments influence cancer progression primarily through HF pathophysiology rather than a direct effect on cancer cells

Methods

Study approval

All animal experiments were approved by the Ethical Committee of the University of Antwerp (ECD file 2023-27) and conformed to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, 8th edition published by the US National Institutes of Health in 2011, and with the European Communities Council Directive 2010/63/EU for the protection of animals used for experimental purposes. All mice were kept on standard chow and water ad libitum, at a temperature of 22 °C and 50% humidity, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All mice were daily monitored for any signs of distress throughout the experiment.

Surgical induction of MI

Eleven-week-old female Balb/cJ mice (Charles River, 028) were used to replicate common clinical scenarios of mammary tumors. Mice were randomized into control (Ctrl) or MI groups. The MI group underwent left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) ligation (64 animals), while Ctrl group underwent a sham procedure (13 animals) [27, 28]. Buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg, Produlab Pharma) was administered 15 min before surgery. Anesthesia was induced with 8% sevoflurane (SevoFlo, Zoetis) and maintained with 4.5% sevoflurane till the end of the procedure. Mice were ventilated using a small-animal respirator (MiniVent, Harvard Apparatus). Next, an incision of 15 mm was made on the left thorax and the thoracic cavity was opened at the third intercostal space. The LAD was permanently ligated 2 mm below the left atrium using a 8/0 polypropylene monofilament suture (Ethicon, F1894). After ligation, the thorax was closed with 6/0 polypropylene monofilament suture (Ethicon, F1841), and the skin was closed with 5/0 polyamide 6 suture (Ethilon, F2412H). A second dose of buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg) was given 6–8 h post-surgery. Two days after surgery, a cardiac ultrasound was performed to confirm the success of the procedure, by evaluating the akinetic segments of the left ventricle via parasternal long-axis B-mode imaging.

Cardiac ultrasound

All mice underwent a cardiac ultrasound in the week prior the operation to assess baseline left ventricular function. Cardiac ultrasounds were performed at two, four, six and seven weeks after surgery. All ultrasound assessments were performed using the Vevo F2-LAZRX (FUJIFILM VisualSonics) and UHF57x probe. Mice were anesthetized with 3.5% isoflurane (Alvira, BE-V512222) and maintained with 1.5% isoflurane. Parasternal long-axis B-mode images and M-mode images were obtained and analyzed using VevoLAB software (FUJIFILM VisualSonics, Version 5.7.1). Measurements were obtained through analysis of the parasternal long-axis B-mode, of which the EF (ejection fraction) and the left ventricular end-systolic (LVESV) and end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) were determined by tracing the endocardial border [29]. Data acquisitions, measurements and analyses were conducted blinded.

HF treatments

Mice with an EF < 40%, two weeks after surgery, were randomized to receive vehicle or one of the three HF treatments: enalapril (20 mg/kg, MCE, HY-B0331A), carvedilol (10 mg/kg, MCE, HY-B0006), or empagliflozin (15 mg/kg, MCE, HY-15409) [21, 30–33]. All treatments were administered once daily by oral gavage, consistently performed in the morning at approximately the same time each day.

Metastatic breast cancer induction

All mice with and without MI were inoculated with cancer four weeks after the surgical procedure. Anesthesia was induced with 8% sevoflurane (SevoFlo, Zoetis) and maintained with 4.5% sevoflurane. 200 µl of 4T1 tumor cell suspension (1 × 106 cells/mL) was injected into the left 4th mammary fat pad of the animal using a 25G needle. Tumor growth was measured in vivo with the help of a digital caliper. Length and width of the tumor were measured blindly from day 8 to day 21 after injection to calculate tumor volume at different time points. On day 22, the tumor was excised under anesthesia (induced at 8% maintained 4.5% with sevoflurane); thereafter, the skin was closed with 5/0 (Ethilon, F2412H). The tumor was weighted and its length, width and depth were measured to estimate its final weight and volume. Tumor volume was calculated as V = (L × W2)/2. At day 28, mice were euthanized with sodium pentobarbital (200 mg/kg, i.p.); serum samples were collected via the retrobulbar sinus, hearts and lungs were weighted and collected.

Metastatic breast cancer model without the presence of MI

This experiment had a similar design to the previous in vivo study, without the presence of cardiac surgery or cardiac ultrasound procedures. Only carvedilol (10 mg/kg) and empagliflozin (15 mg/kg) were included as treatments.

Cells

4T1 cells (ATCC CRL-2539) were cultured according to the provider’s instructions [25]. Briefly, cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 (ThermoFisher Scientific, 11875093) supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (ThermoFisher Scientific, 10270106) and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (10,000 units/ml) at 37° in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Treatments

All the treatments were dissolved in vehicle: distilled water and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), control wells were only treated with vehicle.

Cell viability assay

A WST-1 assay was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 4T1 cells were seeded at a density of 1.0 × 103 cell/100 µL in supplemented RPMI-1640 medium in 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One). After 6 h, the medium was replaced with 100 µl RPMI-1640 0.1% FBS. After 24 h, 10 µL of various treatment combinations were added to the plate, including: isoprenaline 1 µg/mL, carvedilol, carvedilol + isoprenaline, or empagliflozin. Each of the two medications was tested at three different concentrations 0.1 µM, 1 µM, 10 µM. Each condition was tested in triplicate. Twenty-four hours after the treatment, 11 µL of cell proliferation reagent WST-1 (Roche Diagnostic) was added in each well, the cells were incubated at 37 °C protected from light. After 4 h, absorbance was measured using a microplate reader at 450 nm, with a reference wavelength of 690 nm. A similar experiment was used to determine interferon gamma (IFN-γ) effect on 4T1 cell viability. Briefly, 4T1 cells were seeded at a density of 5.0 × 10³ cells/100 µL in supplemented RPMI-1640 medium in 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One). After 24 h, cells were treated with recombinant murine IFN-γ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 315-05-100UG) at final concentrations of 1, 10, 25, 50, and 100 ng/mL for 48 h. Cell viability was assessed with the WST-1 assay.

Migration assay (scratch assay)

4T1 cells were seeded at a density of 4 × 105 cell/mL in supplemented RPMI-1640 medium in 12-well plates (Greiner Bio-One). After 6 h, the initial medium was replaced to 1 mL RPMI-1640 0.1% FBS per well and the cells were incubated for 18 h at 37 °C. The monolayer was scraped in three different locations using a P200 pipette tip to create three scratches [34]. The medium was replaced to remove cell debris and treatments were added straight after. Cells were treated with empagliflozin (10 µM) or carvedilol (10 µM). Cell migration was documented at 0, 3, 6, 9, 12 and 24 h post-scratch using the CELENA S Digital Imaging System and wound closure was quantified using ImageJ.

Histology and immunostaining

Hearts, tumors, and lungs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and cut into 5 μm sections. Images were acquired with an Olympus BX43 microscope (Olympus Stream Motion Software) with a 20x (heart and tumor) or 10x magnification (lungs) and analyzed with ImageJ 2.14.0 software. For quantification of cardiac interstitial fibrosis, Masson’s Trichrome staining was used. The percentage of fibrotic tissue was expressed as the ratio of positively stained fibrotic area (blue) to the total tissue area in the image. Four images per slide were taken in fixed locations of the remote LV and the result was expressed as a mean of the four individual images. Total fibrotic area was measured to ensure that all MI mice had comparable scarring at the selected cutting point where interstitial fibrosis was assessed. To detect and analyze lung metastasis, both Haematoxylin and eosin stain (H&E) and minichromosome maintenance complex component 2 stain (MCM2) were performed. H&E staining was used to visually count the number of metastatic clusters present in the tissue and MCM2 staining was used to calculate the ratio of positive MCM2 high proliferative cells (brown) on total lung tissue area (blue). MCM2 staining was also performed on tumor tissue. Four pictures per slide were taken in fixed locations of the tumor section and the amount of high proliferating cells was expressed as a ratio of positive stained cells (brown) to the total tissue area (blue) and the result was expressed as a mean of the four individual pictures. Quantification was performed by a person blinded to the treatment protocol.

RNA sequencing and data preprocessing

Twenty tumor samples divided in five separate groups were analyzed with RNA sequencing: group 1 = Ctrl; group 2 = MI/veh; group 3 = MI/car; group 4 = MI/emp; group 5 MI/ena. Total RNA was extracted from the tissue samples using the Nucleospin RNA (Macherey-Nagel, 740955.250) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality control and sequencing libraries were prepared, and paired-end sequencing was performed by Azenta Life Sciences, using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating an average of 10 million paired-end reads per sample. Raw sequencing data were returned in FASTQ format. The reads were processed by trimming low-quality bases and adaptor sequences using Trimmomatic (v0.39) and aligned to the human and mouse reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2 (v2.1.0). Raw count data were filtered by retaining only genes with at least 10 counts in at least 20% of the samples, which identified 23,960 informative genes. Next, raw count data were normalized using negative binomial models using the BioC-package DESeq2. The design matrix was set up to only consider the treatment conditions (i.e., Ctrl vs. MI/veh vs. MI/car vs. MI/emp vs. MI/ena). Finally, data were subjected to variance stabilizing transformation prior to further analysis (i.e., vst-function in DESeq2). Global expression themes were analyzed using unsupervised hierarchical clustering (UHC; R-package pheatmap) and principal component analysis (PCA). For UHC analysis, the Manhattan distance was used to calculate the dissimilarity matrix, and clustering was performed using Ward linkage. For PCA, data were median centered and scaled to unit variance. Additional removal of technical noise, identified following the first round of unsupervised analysis, was performed using the BioC-package sva. Data were corrected using the fsva-function with the number of surrogate variables set to 6. Differential expression analysis was performed using generalized linear models (BioC-package limma), and results were visualized using volcano plots and upset plots (R-package ComplexUpset). Genes with false discovery rate adjusted p-values inferior to 10% were considered significant. Finally, vectors of log2 fold changes were inspected for enrichment of the hallmark gene sets (Molecular Signatures Database; Broad Institute) using the BioC-package fgsea and results were visualized using dotplots. Hallmarks with a false discovery rate adjusted p-values inferior to 10% were considered significant.

RT-qPCR analysis of gene expression in 4T1 cells and tumor tissue

RNA was extracted from tumors from mice with and without MI using Nucleospin RNA (Macherey-Nagel, 740955.250) and from 4T1 cells, previously treated with 50 ng/ml or 100 mg/ml IFN-γ for 24 h, using Nucleospin RNA XS (Macherey-Nagel, 740902.50) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For cDNA synthesis, total RNA was added to a mixture containing buffer with random hexamers and reverse transcriptase enzyme (TaqMan reverse transcription reagents, Applied biosystems, N8080234) after which samples were incubated for 10 min at 25 °C, 30 min at 48 °C, and 5 min at 95 °C. RT-qPCR was performed on QuantStudio 3 Real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) using Taqman Universal PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, 4304437) and Taqman primers according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Settings were as follows: 2 min at 95 °C followed by 10 min at 95 °C, 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, and 1 min at 60 °C. RT-qPCR data from tumors were normalized using both glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase 1 (HPRT) as housekeeping genes, whereas data from 4T1 cells were normalized using HPRT alone. Expression levels were calculated using the comparative cycle method and expressed as fold change (FC) to appropriate controls. The following TaqMan primers were used (Thermo Fisher Scientific): GAPDH (Mm99999915_g1); IFN-γ (Mm01168134_m1); HPRT (Mm00446968_m1) STAT1 (Mm01257286_m1) and STAT3 (Mm01219775_m1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10. Data are presented as the mean value of biological replicates (unless stated otherwise) ± SD. Data was checked for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. P value < 0.05 was considered the threshold for statistical significance and all exact P values are provided, when possible, within each figure together with the statistical test performed for each experiment. Comparison between groups was performed using unpaired t-test (two-tailed) or one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test for non-parametric analysis. For the graphs including repeated measurement one-way ANOVA was conducted separately for each time point in individual graphs, and the corresponding significance was added to the graphs. For RT-qPCR statistical analysis were performed on ΔCt and significance was added to the fold change (FC) graphs. For the RNA sequencing, genes with false discovery rate adjusted p-values inferior to 10% were considered significant. Hallmarks with a false discovery rate adjusted p-values inferior to 10% were considered significant.

Results

Effect of enalapril, empagliflozin and carvedilol on LV function and histology

First, we studied the effects of HF treatments on LV function (Fig. 1b–d). At weeks four and five, the MI/emp group showed significant improvements in EF, LVEDV, and LVESV compared to the MI/veh group. The other treatments did not improve these parameters. The total fibrotic area, consisting mostly of scar tissue, was comparable among MI groups (Fig. 1f), indicating that infarct sizes were similar between groups. The interstitial fibrotic area was higher in MI mice compared to the Ctrl group, but the MI/car group showed a significantly lower amount of fibrosis than the MI/veh group (Fig. 1g). Heart weight, normalized to body weight (Fig. 1h), was significantly increased in MI/veh when compared to Ctrl, and was not significantly altered by HF treatments.

After excision of the tumors on day 22 (Fig. 2a, b), no significant differences in tumor volume and weight were observed when comparing Ctrl and MI/veh groups. Only the MI/emp group had significantly lighter and smaller tumors when compared to the MI/veh group. No differences were observed among groups when quantifying the amount of positive stained MCM2 high proliferating cells with a x20 magnification (Fig. 2d). Also, no differences were observed among the groups for lung weight when normalized to body weight (Fig. 2e). No significant differences were observed when comparing Ctrl group with MI/veh, but the MI/car group showed a significant decrease in both the number of metastatic lung clusters and the ratio of high proliferating cells in the lungs. Effect of empagliflozin and carvedilol on cancer progression in mice without MI

Next, we studied whether carvedilol and empagliflozin induced similar effects on tumor growth and metastasis in mice without a previous MI, and therefore without LV dysfunction. In the absence of LV dysfunction, empagliflozin had no significant effect on tumor weight, volume, growth, or amount of high proliferating cells in the tumor when compared to vehicle (Fig. 3b). Similarly, carvedilol had no effects on the ratio of high proliferating cells in the lungs or on the number of metastatic clusters when compared to vehicle. (Fig. 3f, g).

Effect of empagliflozin and carvedilol on tumor cell proliferation and migration in culture

Next, we investigated the direct in vitro effects of carvedilol and empagliflozin on 4T1 cells proliferation and migration (Fig. 3h). Empagliflozin did not affect cell proliferation when compared to the Ctrl (Fig. 3i). Similarly, carvedilol did not reduce cell proliferation, even not after simultaneously stimulating the cells with isoprenaline at low doses (Fig. 3j). Only at the highest concentration (10µM), carvedilol attenuated proliferation of 4T1 cells, potentially due to toxicity. Finally, neither empagliflozin nor carvedilol reduced 4T1 cell migration compared to Ctrl (Fig. 3k).

Effect of enalapril, empagliflozin and carvedilol on the tumor transcriptome assessed by RNA sequencing

The heat map (Fig. 4a) represents the expression levels of differentially expressed genes across samples, with red indicating upregulation and blue indicating downregulation. Hierarchical clustering of the samples reveals distinct transcriptional signatures associated with each treatment condition. The Ctrl and MI/veh groups form separate clusters, suggesting strong differences between control and post-MI states. Among the treated groups, MI/car, MI/emp, and MI/ena exhibit partially overlapping yet distinct clustering patterns, indicating that each treatment induces specific transcriptomic alterations. To explore global transcriptional differences among treatment groups, we performed PCA on the gene expression data (Fig. 4b), which revealed that MI/veh and Ctrl groups form distinct clusters; MI/car, MI/emp, and MI/ena each induce unique transcriptional shifts, though with some overlap. The clustering suggests that carvedilol may have a more homogenous effect, while empagliflozin and enalapril show more dispersed variation. We first assessed whether gene expression levels in the tumors was influenced by the presence of LV dysfunction. Tumors from MI/veh mice showed 408 upregulated and 872 downregulated genes compared to Ctrl (Fig. 4c). Specifically, 401 genes were uniquely upregulated and 866 genes were uniquely downregulated in the HF/veh group (Fig. 4h, i), The overlap in significantly up or downregulated genes between the MI/veh group and one of the treatment groups (MI/ena, MI/car, and MI/emp) was minimal, further emphasizing the distinct gene and pathway activation patterns between untreated and treated HF animals. Compared to MI/veh, MI/ena treatment led to the upregulation of 1383 genes and downregulation of 1418 genes (Fig. 4d), with 874 genes uniquely upregulated and 1013 uniquely downregulated (Fig. 4h, i). Similarly, MI/car treatment resulted in the upregulation of 1063 genes and downregulation of 808 genes (Fig. 4e), with 577 uniquely upregulated and 454 uniquely downregulated. Notably, MI/car exhibited considerable gene overlap with HF/ena, sharing 285 upregulated and 263 downregulated genes, whereas its overlap with HF/emp was more limited, with only 84 upregulated and 27 downregulated genes (Fig. 4h, i). Meanwhile, MI/emp treatment led to the upregulation of 626 genes and downregulation of 392 genes (Fig. 4f), with 318 uniquely upregulated and 227 uniquely downregulated in this group. (Fig. 4h, i). The distinct effects on gene expression were also reflected in the differential regulation of HALLMARK pathways across the groups (Fig. 4g). Notably, MI/ena reversed numerous pathways that were downregulated in MI/veh compared to Ctrl. Moreover, almost all the pathways that were upregulated by MI/ena were similarly upregulated in MI/emp and MI/car (Fig. 4g). In addition to these overlapping pathways, MI/car group uniquely regulated several other pathways involved in cell proliferation and invasion (MYC target V1/V2 and E2F target), apoptosis and immune response. On the contrary MI/emp uniquely regulated several pathways involved in adipogenesis, fatty acid metabolism, glycolysis, and xenobiotic metabolism (Fig. 4g). Among the most significantly altered pathways, the presence of MI appears to trigger several that are involved in inflammation and innate adaptive immunity among which IFN-γ (Fig. 4g).

MI and HF therapies alter IFN-γ expression on tumor tissue

The levels of IFN-γ were assessed by RT-qPCR in tumor tissue of treated and untreated mice with or without LV dysfunction. In the MI/veh group, IFN-γ expression was significantly downregulated compared to Ctrl mice (Fig. 5a). Treatment with either carvedilol or empagliflozin significantly increased IFN-γ expression, restoring it to levels comparable to the Ctrl group, whereas enalapril had no significant effect (Fig. 5a). In the absence of MI, no differences in IFN-γ expression were observed among carvedilol, empagliflozin, and vehicle groups (Fig. 5b).

Discussion

Recent studies have shown that HF directly promotes cancer growth [13–15]. This observation is clinically significant, as the number of individuals diagnosed with both HF and cancer continues to rise, highlighting the need for treatments that mitigate the impact of HF on tumor progression [9]. In this study, we investigated whether HF treatments would influence cancer growth in a rodent model of post-MI LV dysfunction, or in other words, whether HF drugs would uncouple HF and cancer (Fig. 6 Central Illustration). Collectively, our findings support this hypothesis while also highlighting that the outcome depends on how the dysfunctional heart is treated. Whereas enalapril only partially reversed the MI-induced changes in tumor transcriptome without affecting the tumor growth or the metastatic spread, empagliflozin reduced primary tumor expansion, and carvedilol suppressed metastatic spread to the lungs. Notably, both empagliflozin and carvedilol fully reversed the pathways downregulated in the MI/veh group, particularly those involved in inflammation and immune response. Importantly, the effects of empagliflozin and carvedilol on cancer progression were observed only in the post-MI mouse model.

In our study, carvedilol and enalapril failed to improve LV function after MI. While this might seem unexpected, it is still consistent with previous studies showing that beta blockers can fail to improve LV function in a LAD ligation model or that enalapril demonstrated benefits only after eight weeks of treatment [33, 35, 36]. In contrast, our study employed a relatively short treatment duration and also included a waiting period of two weeks after MI before initiating HF therapy, which may also have influenced the outcomes [35–37]. This approach allows for the completion of post-MI remodeling, ensuring that any observed effects are not confounded by ongoing early-phase remodeling. Consequently, if compounds exert their primary effects during the early remodeling phase, bypassing this phase might explain the lack of improvement in LV function. Empagliflozin was the only treatment to significantly improve EF, LVEDV and LVESV, however carvedilol demonstrated a significant reduction of interstitial fibrosis in the remote zone of the LV.

Importantly, in our model, we did not detect a direct effect of MI-induced LV dysfunction on primary breast tumor growth or metastasis spread, which somehow contrasts with previous studies [13–15]. Nevertheless, RNA sequencing of tumor tissue from post-MI animals revealed that LV dysfunction influences cancer at the level of gene expression and pathway regulation. Notably, MI led to a downregulation of several immune-related inflammatory pathways within the tumor tissue, including IFN-γ, a key cytokine involved in promoting antitumor immunity and activating cellular immune responses [38]. These transcriptional changes suggest that while tumor size may remain unchanged, MI can still drive meaningful alterations within the tumor microenvironment suggesting that tumor growth alone may not be a sensitive marker for detecting HF-induced effects on cancer. The discrepancy between our findings and those of Tani and colleagues, who also used 4T1 tumor cells and observed more pronounced MI-induced changes in tumor growth, may stem from differences in study design. In our study, 4T1 cells were injected four weeks after MI rather than two weeks, primarily capturing the effects of chronic LV dysfunction rather than the inflammatory phase of post-infarct remodeling.

A crucial result of this study was that HF treatments (empagliflozin and carvedilol) influenced cancer progression only in post-MI mice, and not in healthy controls. This finding suggests that the observed effects are driven by mechanisms intrinsic to the pathophysiology of HF, rather than by direct actions of the treatments on tumor cells. This was further endorsed by the lack of effects of these drugs on cell proliferation and migration in cell culture experiments. Although empagliflozin also improved LV function, it is unlikely that a mere hemodynamic improvement explains the effects of this drug on primary breast cancer growth, as this mechanism has been previously excluded by Meijers and colleagues [13].

A striking observation of this study was that carvedilol reduced both the number of metastatic clusters and the total amount of MCM2 positive cells in mice with MI. The relationship between the β-adrenergic system and cancer progression remains controversial in the literature [39–42], but it is well-established that neural signaling plays a crucial role in both solid tumors and certain hematological malignancies [43]. Our results are consistent with those from Chang and colleagues, showing that propranolol decreased metastatic spread, but not primary tumor growth, in anthracycline-induced LV dysfunction in mice [41]. These findings suggest that the effects of β-adrenergic blockade are more pronounced when the β-adrenergic system is activated, for instance due to MI or chemotherapy [41–45].

RNA sequencing of primary tumors revealed that enalapril partially reversed the downregulated pathways observed in the MI/veh group. In contrast, empagliflozin additionally modulated cellular metabolism-related pathways, while carvedilol affected pathways associated with replication and proliferation. Both empagliflozin and carvedilol upregulated distinct components of innate and adaptive immune responses, including IFN-γ signaling. IFN-γ expression in tumor tissue was significantly restored in mice treated with carvedilol or empagliflozin compared to vehicle, whereas enalapril had no significant effect. In vitro, treatment of 4T1 cells with IFN-γ led to a marked upregulation of STAT1 without altering STAT3 levels. STAT1, primarily activated by IFN-γ, functions as a tumor suppressor by promoting growth arrest, apoptosis, and antigen presentation via enhanced MHC expression [46]. In contrast, STAT3, often constitutively activated in cancer, drives oncogenic processes such as proliferation, angiogenesis, invasion, and immune evasion [47–49]. These findings suggest selective activation of tumor-suppressive and pro-inflammatory pathways through STAT1, without engaging the immunosuppressive and oncogenic signaling typically associated with STAT3.

Immune reprogramming has already been proposed as a possible explanation of the correlation between HF and cancer progression [50, 51]. Specifically, IFN- γ supports the activation of the proinflammatory response and thus could enhance anti-tumor immunity [38]. IFN-γ demonstrated in previous studies to affect tumor phenotype, growth, and metastasis development when injected in IFN-γ−/− mice with 4T1 breast cancer inducing cancer cell death and apoptosis through the activation of the JAK-STAT1 [52], moreover it also decreased cancer stem cells in a 4T1 mouse model [53]. In conclusion, these preliminary findings indicate that the immune reprogramming occurring after MI could modify the tumor microenvironment of 4T1 metastatic breast cancer leading to a reduction of IFN-γ which has previously been demonstrated to result in higher tumor progression [54–56]. On the other hand, the treatment of the dysfunctional heart with empagliflozin and carvedilol mediates an increase in IFN-γ levels in the tumor microenvironment suggesting IFN-γ being one of the possible factors that leads to a reduction in tumor growth and metastasis spread of 4T1 cancer.

Provocatively, these findings together suggest that in some patients with HF and cancer, HF treatment may be tailored to a specific cancer type or stage of progression.

Conclusion

HF medications with different mechanisms of action had distinct effects on TNBC progression when the two diseases coexisted. These effects varied from isolated effects on the tumor transcriptome, to effects on the expansion of the primary tumor and on metastatic spread in the lungs. Our findings underline the key role of immune signaling, particularly IFN-γ, in shaping the tumor microenvironment in the context of HF. MI-induced LV dysfunction suppressed IFN-γ expression in tumors, potentially promoting cancer progression. Empagliflozin and carvedilol reversed this effect, suggesting they may counteract HF-driven immune alterations that favor tumor growth. These observations together contributed to understand the link between HF and cancer and suggest that HF treatments may uncouple HF and cancer in a therapy-specific manner also through immune response modulation in the tumor microenvironment.
