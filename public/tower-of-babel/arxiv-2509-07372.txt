On the edge eigenvalues of sparse random geometric graphs
Xiucai Ding, Yichen Hu

Abstract
In this paper, we study the edge eigenvalues of random geometric graphs (RGGs) generated by multivariate Gaussian samples in the sparse regime under a broad class of distance metrics. Previous work on edge eigenvalues under related setups has relied on methods based on integral operators or the Courant–Fischer min–max principle with interpolation. However, these approaches typically require either a dense regime or sampling distributions that are compactly supported and non-vanishing, and therefore cannot be generalized to our setting. We introduce a two-step smoothing–matching argument. First, we construct a smoothed empirical operator from the given RGG. We then match its edge eigenvalues to those of a continuum limit operator via a counting argument. We show that, after proper normalization, the first few nontrivial edge eigenvalues of the RGG converge with high probability to those of a differential operator, which can be computed explicitly through a simple second-order linear partial differential equation. To the best of our knowledge, these are the first results on the edge eigenvalues of RGGs generated from samples with unbounded support and vanishing density functions.

Introduction

Random geometric graphs (RGGs)
[
25
,
51
]
have been widely employed in modern data science to uncover latent geometric structures underlying complex datasets, wherein each node is associated with a feature vector and edges are established based on geometric information–especially the pairwise distances between these features–rather than being formed randomly according to prescribed probabilities. For example, RGGs provide a tractable framework for modeling latent spatial structures in network analysis, where edges are formed based on underlying geometric spatial relationships. This framework has found broad applications in areas such as wireless networks
[
33
]
, social networks
[
37
]
, biological networks
[
5
,
27
,
36
,
52
]
, community detection
[
18
,
50
]

[
28
]

[
17
]
, Laplacian Eigenmaps (LE)
[
3
]
, and Isomap
[
60
]
, are widely used to extract the underlying geometric structures. These algorithms typically begin by constructing a random geometric graph in which edges are formed based on pairwise distances between samples, providing a discrete approximation of the underlying manifold.

Broadly speaking, RGGs can be constructed as follows. Suppose we observe a point cloud
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
consisting of
n

d

\mathbf{x}_{i}

\mathbf{x}_{i}

is defined as

\mathbf{K}(i,j)=\mathbf{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})g(\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}/r_{n}),

(1.1)

where the precise assumptions on

For practical application of RGGs, many methodologies and algorithms rely crucially on the choice of

\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{L}_{rw}

Motivated by these challenges, this paper studies the edge eigenvalues of

for general classes of kernel functions

for

,
42

Our contributions and novelties

In this paper, we study the edge eigenvalues of random geometric graph generated by
d
-dimensional
\operatorname{i.i.d.}
multivariate Gaussian samples in the sparse regime. Unlike the bounded support and regular density assumptions commonly imposed in the literature—which typically ensure that the resulting graphs have only one connected component—the unbounded support and vanishing density of Gaussian samples will lead to multiple connected components. Consequently, the matrix (
1.2
) can exhibit several trivially small eigenvalues. In this context, by edge eigenvalues we refer to the non-trivial ones. In Theorem
2.2
, we show that, after proper normalization, the first few non-trivial edge eigenvalues of (
1.2
) converge with high probability to deterministic limits, which are characterized by the eigenvalues of the continuum limit operator defined in (
2.7
). Furthermore, in Proposition
2.4
, we show that these limits can be computed explicitly by solving a second-order linear partial differential equation (PDE) (cf. (
3.2
)).

Different techniques have been employed to study problems with related setups in the literature. However, none of these methods can be directly applied or readily generalized to address our problem. In the dense regime,
[
1
]
use integral operators to study the limiting behavior of the edge eigenvalues. However, in our sparse regime, since
r_{n}
tends to zero, we cannot construct such valid integral operators using their approaches. On the other hand, in the sparse regime with compact support and bounded densities, Courant–Fischer min–max principle is utilized to link the eigenvalues of RGG to those of some differential operators (e.g.,
[
11
,
15
,
31
,
61
]
). This argument relies on interpolations that crucially require bounded eigenfunctions or bounded support, neither of which holds in our Gaussian setup. Consequently, it is difficult to construct an appropriate interpolation to carry out the min-max framework in our setup, if not impossible.

Unlike the dense regime studied through the lens of integral operators in
[
1
]
, we investigate the eigenvalues of the RGG via a differential operator that emerges as the continuum limit of the graph. Rather than employing the min–max principle and interpolation as in
[
11
,
15
,
31
,
61
]
, to link the eigenvalues of the random geometric graph to the limit operator, we propose a two-step smoothing-matching approach. Specifically, we construct a smoothed empirical operator based on the random geometric graph and then match the first few non-trivial edge eigenvalues of this empirical operator to those of the limit operator. A crucial aspect of our approach is to show the closeness between the empirical operator and the limit operator on some carefully chosen functions. This is achieved by detailed analysis of an orthonormal decomposition the limit operator based on the physicist’s Hermite polynomials
[
59
]
and a truncation argument.

While this Hermite-based decomposition is specific to Gaussian distribution, our approach is adaptable to other distributions: operators associated with other sampling distributions that admit
orthonormal bases can, in principle, be treated by replacing Hermite polynomials with the corresponding basis to carry out our arguments. On the other hand, we point out that in bounded-support domains, the truncation argument becomes unnecessary and our approach applies with minor modification. As future work, we plan to explore the statistical applications discussed at the end of Section
2.1
and extend our approach to high-dimensional regimes.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
presents our main results and outlines the proof strategies. In Section
3
, we analyze and compute the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the limiting differential operators. Section
4
contains the proofs of the main results. Additional details are provided in the appendices.

Main results

Let

\mathcal{N}_{d}(\bm{0},\Sigma)

\displaystyle\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},\cdots,\sigma_{d}^{2}),\quad 0<\sigma_{i}<\infty,\quad 1\leqslant i\leqslant d.

(2.1)

Recall (
1.1

. Moreover, for

\displaystyle m_{l}\coloneq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{i}|^{l}h(\lVert\bm{u}\rVert_{p})\mathrm{d}\bm{u},\quad\bm{u}=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{d})^{\top},

for any

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}\coloneq\frac{2m_{0}}{m_{2}\,r_{n}^{2}}\mathbf{L}_{rw}.

Throughout the paper, we list the eigenvalues of

\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{L})\leqslant\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{L})\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{L}).

We point it out that according to classic spectral graph theory (see, e.g.,

For

\displaystyle\varrho(\mathbf{x})=\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}x_{i}^{2}\right).

Based on

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}=\left\{f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\biggm|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{2}(\mathbf{x})\varrho^{2}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{1}(\mathbf{x})f_{2}(\mathbf{x})\varrho^{2}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},\quad f_{1},f_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}},

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}\coloneq\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle},\quad f\in{\mathcal{F}}.

With the above notations in place, we define the weighted Laplace–Beltrami operator on

\displaystyle\Delta_{\varrho}f=-\frac{1}{\varrho^{2}}\,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\bigl(\varrho^{2}\nabla f\bigr).

As we will show in Theorem

\mu_{1}(\Delta_{\varrho})\leqslant\mu_{2}(\Delta_{\varrho})\leqslant\cdots

.

The density function of

\varrho^{*}(\mathbf{x})=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}^{-1}\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}x_{i}^{2}\right)=\left[(2\pi)^{-d/2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}^{-1}\right]\varrho(\mathbf{x}),

which differs from

One can also construct another Hilbert space

,

First,

can be relaxed into more general functions. In fact, as
\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}/r_{n}\geqslant 0

[0,1]

Second, the condition (

. On the one hand, if

is too large, each
\mathbf{x}_{j}
connects too many neighbors, which obscures the local geometry around
\mathbf{x}_{j}
. On the other hand, if
r_{n}
is too small, it is difficult to connect sufficient neighbors to capture enough information of the data. This tradeoff in the choice of

can be empirically visualized in Figure

than those of the existing literature like
[

,
31
]

]

. Technically, the larger lower bound on

in our setup required by (

when

Third, in this paper, we focus on Gaussian samples with diagonal population covariance matrices. On the one hand, for the ease of statement, we assume that

is diagonal so that the induced weighted Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta_{\varrho}
can be studied via

Proof strategy

Before discussing our proof strategy, we first point out that when the density function
\varrho
of
\{\mathbf{x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
has compact support and is bounded from above and below, the edge eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}
have been studied in the literature, for example,
[
11
,
15
,
31
]
. However, none of those critical assumptions hold in our setup. Consequently, the techniques developed in those papers cannot be carried over to study our problem. For example, when
\varrho
has unbounded support, the infinity transportation distances used in
[
11
,
31
]
become infinity. As a result, the errors introduced by transportation-based interpolation between the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{L}
and the eigenfunctions of
\Delta_{\varrho}
in
[
11
,
31
]
will also become infinity, which indicates that the idea of
[
11
,
31
]
cannot be applied. For another instance, in
[
15
]
, the interpolation-based errors rely on the
\ell_{\infty}
norm of the eigenfunctions of
\Delta_{\varrho}
. However, in our setup that
\varrho
has unbounded support, as can be seen in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, the
\ell_{\infty}
norm of the eigenfunctions of
\Delta_{\varrho}
will be unbounded, and the interpolation-based error bound will diverge so that the idea in
[
15
]
cannot be applied. Therefore, new ideas are needed to study
\mathbf{L}
in our setup.

In our first step, unlike
[
15
,
31
]
, we will construct a suitable empirical operator to connect
\mathbf{L}
and
\Delta_{\varrho}
. Similar ideas have been used in
[
4
,
11
,
14
,
17
,
26
,
35
,
56
,
57
]
. However, the ideas from the aforementioned works cannot be applied to our setup as
\operatorname{supp}(\varrho)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Especially, the empirical operator directly based on
\mathbf{L}
, denoted as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}f(\mathbf{x})\coloneq\frac{2m_{0}}{m_{2}r_{n}^{2}}\frac{\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})g(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})(f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_{j}))}{\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})g(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})},

(2.14)

is not well-defined for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}>r_{n}
for all
j=1,\cdots,n
. In order to address this issue, we instead construct an empirical operator based on a smoothed version of
\mathbf{L}
. Specifically, we smooth the hard cut-off
\bm{1}_{[0,1]}(\cdot)
with a sigmoid function and construct a smoothed affinity matrix

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}(i,j)=\sig\left(\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}^{2})\right)g^{*}(\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}/r_{n}),\quad\sig(t)=(1+\exp(-t))^{-1},

(2.15)

where
g^{*}\in C^{2}([0,\infty))
satisfies
g\equiv g^{*}
on
[0,1]
and
0<g^{*}(y)\leqslant\sup_{y\geqslant 0}g^{*}(y)<\infty
for
y\in[0,\infty)
.
The corresponding scaled random-walk Laplacian matrix is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}\coloneq\frac{2m_{0}}{m_{2}r_{n}^{2}}(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}),

(2.16)

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}=\operatorname{diag}(\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}(i,i))
with
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}(i,i)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}(i,j)
.
As we will show in Lemma
C.6
, for suitably chosen large
\alpha_{n}
,
\sig\left(\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}^{2})\right)
sharply approximates
\bm{1}_{[0,1]}(\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})
.
Together with the boundedness of
g^{*}(\cdot)
, the entries of
\mathbf{K}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}
are close with high probability regardless of whether
\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}
or
\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}>r_{n}
, which implies that
\mathbf{L}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}
are also close in operator norm.

Then, we construct an empirical operator
\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{F}}
based on
\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}
defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}f(\mathbf{x})\coloneq\frac{2m_{0}}{m_{2}r_{n}^{2}}\frac{\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sig(\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}^{2}))g^{*}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})(f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_{j}))}{\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sig(\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}^{2}))g^{*}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})}.

(2.17)

There are several advantages of using (
2.17
). Firstly, compared to (
2.14
), (
2.17
) is well-defined for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Secondly, it can be shown that except for at most one diverging eigenvalue, the remaining eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}
coincide (see Lemma
B.5
or
[
62
, Proposition 9]
). Moreover, we can show that the edge eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}
are bounded from above. Consequently, this builds a one-to-one correspondence between the edge eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}
. As a result, we build the connection between the edge eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}
and those of
\mathbf{L}
.

The rest of the work remains to connect the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}
to those of
\Delta_{\varrho}
. Existing literature provides two possible strategies,
[
11
,
15
,
31
]
apply the min-max theorem by interpolations based on optimal transport or heat kernel while
[
26
,
55
,
57
]
aim at controlling
\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}-\Delta_{\varrho}\rVert
. The ideas from the aforementioned works require either compact support or bounded
\ell_{\infty}
norm of the eigenfunctions of
\Delta_{\varrho}
and they often assume the eigenvalues of
\Delta_{\varrho}
are simple. However, none of the assumptions are satisfied in our setup. In our paper, we propose an eigenvalue-counting-based method. For any fixed integer
M_{0}>0
, let
\mu_{1}^{*}<\mu_{2}^{*}<\cdots<\mu_{M_{0}}^{*}
denote the first
M_{0}
distinct non‑trivial eigenvalues of
\Delta_{\varrho}
, which are separated by a constant gap (see Theorem
3.1
). For arbitrary small
\varepsilon>0
, we can split
(\delta,\mu_{M_{0}}^{*}+\varepsilon)
with
\delta
in (
2.8
) into the following three possible types of intervals:
(\mu_{j}^{*}-\varepsilon,\mu_{j}^{*}+\varepsilon)
for
j=1,\cdots,M_{0}
,
[\mu_{j}^{*}+\varepsilon,\mu_{j+1}^{*}-\varepsilon]
for
j=1,\cdots,M_{0}-1
and
(\delta,\mu_{1}^{*}-\varepsilon]
. We can show that for small
\delta
in (
2.8
),
K_{0}=\oo(n)
with high probability, and at least one of the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}
is located in each
(\mu_{j}^{*}-\varepsilon,\mu_{j}^{*}+\varepsilon)
with high probability. Moreover, none of the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}
falls into
\cup_{j=1}^{M_{0}-1}[\mu_{j}^{*}+\varepsilon,\mu_{j+1}^{*}-\varepsilon]
or
(\delta,\mu_{1}^{*}-\varepsilon]
with high probability. In a second step, we will use a counting formula (Theorem
A.10
) to show that
\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}
and
\Delta_{\varrho}
have the same number of eigenvalues (with multiplicities) inside each
(\mu_{j}^{*}-\varepsilon,\mu_{j}^{*}+\varepsilon)
with high probability. The key ingredient to establish the results is to show that for some properly chosen testing functions
\{\varphi_{j,l}\}
, with high probability,

\displaystyle\lVert(\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}-\Delta_{\varrho})\varphi_{j,l}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}=\oo(1).

(2.18)

To build such a result, we utilize a bias-variance-type argument by introducing a deterministic operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n}f(\mathbf{x})\coloneq\frac{2}{r_{n}^{d+2}m_{2}\varrho(\mathbf{x})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})g(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})(f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{y}))\varrho(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y},

(2.19)

which can be regarded as a close approximation of
\mathbb{E}\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}
. Then by careful and detailed analysis of the eigenfunctions of
\Delta_{\varrho}
, we can show that
\lVert(\mathcal{T}_{n}-\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n})\varphi_{j,l}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}=\oo(1)
with high probability and
\lVert(\mathcal{T}_{n}-\Delta_{\varrho})\varphi_{j,l}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}=\oo(1)
. This completes our proof.

3
Spectrum of
\Delta_{\varrho}

In this section, we study the spectrum of
\Delta_{\varrho}
in (
2.7
) with
\varrho
in (
2.4
). The results provide the testing functions used in the proof of Theorem
2.2
and the limits of the first few non-trivial eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}
in Proposition
2.4
. The main results are stated in Theorem
3.1
below. Recall that the physicist’s Hermite polynomials
[
59
]
are defined as, for
j\in\mathbb{N}
,

H_{j}(x)=(-1)^{j}e^{x^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{j}}{\mathrm{d}x^{j}}e^{-x^{2}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Theorem 3.1
.

For the weighted Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta_{\varrho}
on
({\mathcal{F}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
in (
2.5
), its spectrum is discrete and consists of eigenvalues
\sum_{i=1}^{d}2(k_{i}-1)/\sigma_{i}^{2}
with corresponding orthonormal eigenfunctions

\prod_{i=1}^{d}\psi_{i,k_{i}-1}(x_{i}),\quad k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{+},

where

\psi_{i,k_{i}-1}(x_{i})=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}(k_{i}-1)!2^{k_{i}-1}\sigma_{i}}\right)^{1/2}H_{k_{i}-1}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i}),

and
H_{k_{i}-1}
is the
(k_{i}-1)
-th physicist’s Hermite polynomial.

Remark 3.2
.

For any eigenvalue
\lambda
of
\Delta_{\varrho}
, its multiplicity equals the dimension of

\operatorname{span}\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{d}\psi_{i,k_{i}-1}(x_{i}):\sum_{i=1}^{d}2(k_{i}-1)/\sigma_{i}^{2}=\lambda,\quad k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\right\}.

We can see that zero eigenvalue is always simple. Moreover, when
d=1
, all eigenvalues of
\Delta_{\varrho}
are simple, as in Example
2.5
. When
d\geqslant 2
, some of the eigenvalues can be identical. Furthermore, for any finite index sequence
\{(k_{1},\cdots,k_{d})\}
, the associated eigenvalues are bounded from above. In addition, by our assumption on
\delta
in (
2.10
),
\mu_{2}(\Delta_{\varrho})>\delta
.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
relies on the observation that the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
\Delta_{\varrho}
can be constructed using the solutions of
d
ordinary differential equations, which are all the Hermite differential equations
[
59
]
up to change of variables. Moreover, the analytical properties of the eigenfunctions can be studied using the confluent hypergeometric functions
[
49
, Chapter 13]
.

Proof.

To solve the eigenvalue problem, we seek solutions to

\displaystyle\Delta_{\varrho}f=\lambda f,\quad f\in{\mathcal{F}}.

(3.1)

Recalling
\varrho(\mathbf{x})
in (
2.4
), we can rewrite (
3.1
) as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{2x_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}+\lambda f=0,\quad f\in{\mathcal{F}},\quad f(\mathbf{x})\not\equiv 0.

(3.2)

According to (
A.3
), to solve (
3.2
), it suffices to solve

\displaystyle\mathsf{f}_{i}^{\prime\prime}(x_{i})-\frac{2x_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\mathsf{f}_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})+\lambda_{i}\mathsf{f}_{i}(x_{i})=0,\quad\mathsf{f}_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{i},\quad\mathsf{f}_{i}(x_{i})\not\equiv 0,\quad i=1,\cdots,d,

(3.3)

where
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}=\lambda
and
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
is the Hilbert space defined as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{i}\coloneq\left\{\mathsf{f}_{i}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\biggm|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathsf{f}_{i}^{2}(x_{i})\exp\left(-x_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x_{i}<\infty\right\},

(3.4)

with corresponding inner product

\displaystyle\langle\mathsf{f}_{i},\mathsf{g}_{i}\rangle_{i}\coloneq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathsf{f}_{i}(x_{i})\mathsf{g}_{i}(x_{i})\exp\left(-x_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x_{i},\quad\mathsf{f}_{i},\mathsf{g}_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{i},

(3.5)

and induced norm

\displaystyle\lVert\mathsf{f}_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}_{i}}=\sqrt{\langle\mathsf{f}_{i},\mathsf{f}_{i}\rangle_{i}}.

(3.6)

We begin solving (
3.3
). First, we find the general solution to (
3.3
). By Theorem
A.1
, it suffices to find two linearly independent solutions
\mathsf{f}_{i,1}
and
\mathsf{f}_{i,2}
and any solution
\mathsf{f}_{i}
to (
3.3
) can be written as a linear combination
c_{i,1}\mathsf{f}_{i,1}+c_{i,2}\mathsf{f}_{i,2}
for some
c_{i,1},c_{i,2}\in\mathbb{R}
. We start seeking solutions that can be represented by the following power-series expansion:

\displaystyle\mathsf{f}_{i}(x_{i})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}b_{i,j}x_{i}^{j},

(3.7)

where
\{b_{i,j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
are coefficients ensuring that
\mathsf{f}_{i}
has an infinite radius of convergence and is therefore analytic on
\mathbb{R}
. By Cauchy–Hadamard theorem and Weierstrass
M
-test
[
54
]
, this implies that the series in (
3.7
) converges uniformly on every compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
. Combining with the analyticity of
\mathsf{f}_{i}
on
\mathbb{R}
, we further write:

\displaystyle\mathsf{f}_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}jb_{i,j}x_{i}^{j-1},\quad\mathsf{f}_{i}^{\prime\prime}(x_{i})=\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}j(j-1)b_{i,j}x_{i}^{j-2}.

Substituting the above expansion into (
3.3
) and matching coefficients of
x_{i}^{j}
gives, for all
j\geqslant 0
,

b_{i,j+2}=\frac{2j/\sigma_{i}^{2}-\lambda_{i}}{(j+2)(j+1)}b_{i,j}.

To find two linearly independent solutions to (
3.3
), it suffices to impose some initial conditions so that we can find two linearly independent solutions to (
3.3
) in terms of (
3.7
). Without loss of generality, we assume that

\mathsf{f}_{i}(0)=1,\mathsf{f}_{i}^{\prime}(0)=0\text{ and }\mathsf{f}_{i}(0)=0,\mathsf{f}_{i}^{\prime}(0)=1.

This yields two linearly independent functions—an even
\mathsf{f}_{i,1}
and an odd
\mathsf{f}_{i,2}
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{f}_{i,1}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\lambda_{i}}{2!}x_{i}^{2}-\frac{(4/\sigma_{i}^{2}-\lambda_{i})\lambda_{i}}{4!}x_{i}^{4}-\cdots,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathsf{f}_{i,2}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=x_{i}+\frac{(2/\sigma_{i}^{2}-\lambda_{i})}{3!}x_{i}^{3}+\frac{(6/\sigma_{i}^{2}-\lambda_{i})(2/\sigma_{i}^{2}-\lambda_{i})}{5!}x_{i}^{5}+\cdots.

(3.9)

By Cauchy–Hadamard theorem
[
54
]
, both
\mathsf{f}_{i,1}(x_{i})
and
\mathsf{f}_{i,2}(x_{i})
have infinite radius of convergence. So
\mathsf{f}_{i,1}
and
\mathsf{f}_{i,2}
are two linearly independent solutions to (
3.3
) in terms of (
3.7
). Combining the above discussion, we see that
\{\mathsf{f}_{i,1},\mathsf{f}_{i,2}\}
forms a fundamental system and any solution
\mathsf{f}_{i}
to (
3.3
) can be written as a linear combination
c_{i,1}\mathsf{f}_{i,1}+c_{i,2}\mathsf{f}_{i,2}
for some
c_{i,1},c_{i,2}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Second, we determine which of these solutions belong to
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
. We analyze the asymptotics of
\mathsf{f}_{i,1}(x_{i})
and
\mathsf{f}_{i,2}(x_{i})
as
|x_{i}|\rightarrow\infty
. By Remark
A.4
, Lemma
A.6
and Remark
A.7
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathsf{f}_{i,1}(x_{i})\sim\frac{\Gamma(1/2)}{\Gamma(-\lambda_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}/4)}e^{x_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{i}^{2}}|x_{i}/\sigma_{i}|^{-\lambda_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}/2-1}\quad(|x_{i}|\rightarrow\infty),

when
\lambda_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}\notin\{0,4,8,\cdots\}
. Hence, we have

\mathsf{f}_{i,1}^{2}(x_{i})\exp\left(-x_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)\sim e^{x_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{i}^{2}}|x_{i}/\sigma_{i}|^{-\lambda_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}-2},

which diverges as
|x_{i}|\rightarrow\infty
. Combining with (
3.4
), this implies that
\mathsf{f}_{i,1}\notin{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
when
\lambda_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}\notin\{0,4,8,\cdots\}
. On the other hand, when
\lambda_{i}=4s/\sigma_{i}^{2}
for
s\in\{0,1,2,\cdots\}
,
\mathsf{f}_{i,1}
becomes
H_{2s}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i})
(up to normalization), where
H_{2s}
is the
2s
-th physicist’s Hermite polynomial
[
59
]
and has degree
2s
. This implies that
\mathsf{f}_{i,1}(x_{i})
is square-integrable with respect to the measure
e^{-x_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{i}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x_{i}
. Consequently,
\mathsf{f}_{i,1}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
if and only if
\lambda_{i}=0,4/\sigma_{i}^{2},8/\sigma_{i}^{2},\cdots
. Similarly,
\mathsf{f}_{i,2}(x_{i})
is square-integrable with respect to the measure
e^{-x_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{i}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x_{i}
if and only if
\lambda_{i}=2/\sigma_{i}^{2},6/\sigma_{i}^{2},10/\sigma_{i}^{2},\cdots
. This yields
H_{1}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i}),H_{3}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i}),H_{5}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i}),\cdots
(up to normalization). Combining the two cases, the only
\lambda_{i}
admitting solutions in
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
are
0,2/\sigma_{i}^{2},4/\sigma_{i}^{2},\cdots
. Moreover, the corresponding eigenfunctions of (
3.3
) are
\{H_{m}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i})\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
, up to normalization.

Next, we normalize
\{H_{m}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i})\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
to form a complete orthonormal basis of
({\mathcal{F}}_{i},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{i})
to address the eigenvalue problem (
3.3
). By the orthogonality property of Hermite polynomials
[
59
]
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{l}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i})H_{s}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i})e^{-x_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{i}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x_{i}=\sqrt{\pi}2^{l}l!\sigma_{i}\bm{1}_{\{l=s\}}.

Normalizing each
H_{m}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i})
yields the orthonormal eigenfunctions:

\displaystyle\psi_{i,m}(x_{i})=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}m!2^{m}\sigma_{i}}\right)^{1/2}H_{m}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i}),\text{ for }m=0,1,\cdots.

(3.10)

This shows that the solutions to (
3.3
) are
2(k_{i}-1)/\sigma_{i}^{2}
with corresponding orthonormal eigenfunctions
\psi_{i,k_{i}-1}(x_{i})
for
k_{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

Now we construct the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
\Delta_{\varrho}
using those of (
3.3
). By the arguments below Theorem
A.1
,
\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{d}\psi_{i,k_{i}-1}(x_{i})\right\}_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
constitutes a complete orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{F}}
. Combining with the fact that each
\prod_{i=1}^{d}\psi_{i,k_{i}-1}(x_{i})
is a solution to (
3.2
) with
\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{d}2(k_{i}-1)/\sigma_{i}^{2}
, we have that (
3.2
) is solvable if and only if

\lambda\in\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}2(k_{i}-1)/\sigma_{i}^{2}\right\}_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}},

and any solution
f(\mathbf{x})
to (
3.2
) satisfies

f(\mathbf{x})\in\operatorname{span}\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{d}\psi_{i,k_{i}-1}(x_{i}):\sum_{i=1}^{d}2(k_{i}-1)/\sigma_{i}^{2}=\lambda,\quad k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\right\}.

Consequently, the spectrum of
\Delta_{\varrho}
on
({\mathcal{F}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
is discrete and consists of eigenvalues
\sum_{i=1}^{d}2(k_{i}-1)/\sigma_{i}^{2}
with corresponding orthonormal eigenfunctions
\prod_{i=1}^{d}\psi_{i,k_{i}-1}(x_{i})
for
k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. This completes our proof.
∎

) on

∎

The rest of the section leaves to prove Theorem

in (

and

Bounding

In light of the relation between the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}_{rw}
in (

) and
\mathbf{L}
in (
2.3
), we will work directly with

. For convenience of notation, denote

\displaystyle\xi(d)\coloneq(d+2)^{2}/2.

(4.1)

The proof relies on our observation that the majority of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}_{rw}
, denoted as
\{\lambda_{l}^{*}\}_{l=1}^{n}
, will be around 1 in the sense that
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}(1-\lambda_{l}^{*})^{2}
can be bounded by
\OO(n^{-1/\xi(d)})
with high probability.
Consequently, it is impossible to have more than
n^{1-2/(2\xi(d)+\eta)}
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}_{rw}
deviating significantly below 1, which yields the
n^{1-2/(2\xi(d)+\eta)}
upper bound for

.

Proof.

Recall
\mathbf{L}_{rw}
in (
1.2
). We denote the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}_{rw}
as follows,

\lambda_{1}^{*}\leqslant\lambda_{2}^{*}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\lambda_{n}^{*}.

We can see that for
l=1,\cdots,n
,
\lambda_{l}^{*}=\frac{m_{2}r_{n}^{2}}{2m_{0}}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{L})
, with
m_{0},m_{2}
as in (
2.2
) and
\mathbf{L}
in (
2.3
). Therefore, showing
K_{0}\leqslant n^{1-\frac{2}{2\xi(d)+\eta}}
is equivalent to showing that the number of eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}_{rw}
smaller than or equal to
\frac{m_{2}\delta}{2m_{0}}r_{n}^{2}
is less than or equal to
n^{1-\frac{2}{2\xi(d)+\eta}}
, where
\delta
is in (
2.8
).
We now prove
K_{0}\leqslant n^{1-\tfrac{2}{2\xi(d)+\eta}}
by contradiction. We assume to the contrary that, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle K_{0}>n^{1-\tfrac{2}{2\xi(d)+\eta}}.

(4.2)

The assumption (
4.2
) indicates that for all
l\leqslant n^{1-\tfrac{2}{2\xi(d)+\eta}}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{l}^{*}\leqslant\frac{m_{2}\delta}{2m_{0}}r_{n}^{2}.

(4.3)

Recall
\mathbf{K}
in (
1.1
) and
\mathbf{D}
in (
1.2
). Applying (
C.2
) to
\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{K}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}|\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{K})|^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}|(\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{K})_{js}|^{2},

(4.4)

where
\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{K})
is the
l
-th eigenvalue of
\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{K}
and
(\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{K})_{js}
is the
(j,s)
-th entry of
\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{K}
. Observe that the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{K}
are
\{1-\lambda_{l}^{*}\}_{l=1}^{n}
and
(\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{K})_{js}=\mathbf{K}(j,s)/\mathbf{D}(j,j)
. Therefore, (
4.4
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}(1-\lambda_{l}^{*})^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\mathbf{K}(j,s)}{\mathbf{D}(j,j)}\right)^{2}.

(4.5)

For sufficiently large
n
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}(1-\lambda_{l}^{*})^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{\lfloor{n^{1-2/(2\xi(d)+\eta)}}\rfloor}(1-\lambda_{l}^{*})^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant n^{-\tfrac{2}{2\xi(d)+\eta}}/2,

(4.6)

where in the last step we used (
4.3
) and the fact that
\frac{m_{2}\delta}{2m_{0}}r_{n}^{2}=\oo(1)
.
Under the boundedness of
g(\cdot)
in Theorem
2.2
, there exist
C_{1},C_{2}
such that
0<C_{1}\leqslant g(y)\leqslant C_{2}
for any
y\in[0,1]
. This indicates that for any
\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{x}_{s}
,

\displaystyle C_{1}\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{s}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})\leqslant\mathbf{K}(j,s)\leqslant C_{2}\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{s}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}).

(4.7)

Denote the number of points connected to
\mathbf{x}_{j}
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}_{j}\coloneq\sum_{s=1}^{n}\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}_{s}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})\geqslant 1.

(4.8)

Then we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\mathbf{K}(j,s)}{\mathbf{D}(j,j)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\left(\frac{C_{2}\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{s}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})}{C_{1}\mathsf{d}_{j}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{2}^{2}}{C_{1}^{2}n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mathsf{d}_{j}},

(4.9)

where the first step is from (
4.7
). Combining (
4.5
), (
4.6
) and (
4.9
) gives,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mathsf{d}_{j}}\geqslant\frac{C_{1}^{2}}{2C_{2}^{2}}n^{1-\tfrac{2}{2\xi(d)+\eta}}.

(4.10)

To contradict (
4.10
), on the other hand, we will show that with probability
1-\oo(1)
,
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mathsf{d}_{j}}=\OO(n^{-1/(\xi(d))})=\oo(n^{-2/(2\xi(d)+\eta)})
. Before we proceed, we define a bulk region
B_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

\displaystyle B_{n}\coloneq\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left[-\sigma_{i}\sqrt{\log n/(d^{2}/4+d+1)},\sigma_{i}\sqrt{\log n/(d^{2}/4+d+1)}\right],

(4.11)

where
\prod
denotes Cartesian product for sets, and set the corresponding tail region
T_{n}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{n}
. Therefore, we split all points
\mathbf{x}_{j}
into the bulk points in
B_{n}
and the tail points in
T_{n}
. To show
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mathsf{d}_{j}}=\oo(n^{-2/(2\xi(d)+\eta)})
, we will show that there are only
\OO(n^{1-1/(\xi(d)}))
tail points, and that for any
\mathbf{x}_{j}
in the bulk region,
\mathsf{d}_{j}\geqslant n^{1/2}
.

First, we show that there are only
\OO(n^{1-1/(\xi(d))})
points in the tail region.
Denote
p_{n}\coloneq\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}\in T_{n})
, where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})^{\top}\sim\mathcal{N}_{d}(\bm{0},\Sigma)
with
\Sigma
in (
2.1
). Recall (
4.1
). We have that

\displaystyle p_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}\left\{x_{i}\notin\left[-\sigma_{i}\sqrt{\log n/(d^{2}/4+d+1)},\sigma_{i}\sqrt{\log n/(d^{2}/4+d+1)}\right]\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{x_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\right|\geqslant\sqrt{\log n/(d^{2}/4+d+1)}\right)\leqslant 2d\exp\left(-\frac{1}{d^{2}/2+2d+2}\log n\right)=2dn^{-1/(\xi(d))},

(4.12)

where in the second step we used the union bound and in the third step we used the fact that
x_{i}/\sigma_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and the Gaussian tail bound
[
63
, Section 2.1.2]
that for
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and any
t\geqslant 0
,

\mathbb{P}(|z|\geqslant t)\leqslant 2\exp\left(-t^{2}/2\right).

On the other hand, we have that

\displaystyle p_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}\left\{x_{i}\notin\left[-\sigma_{i}\sqrt{\log n/(d^{2}/4+d+1)},\sigma_{i}\sqrt{\log n/(d^{2}/4+d+1)}\right]\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{x_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\right|\geqslant\sqrt{\log n/(d^{2}/4+d+1)}\right)\gtrsim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log n}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{d^{2}/2+2d+2}\log n\right)\geqslant n^{-1/(d^{2}/2+2d)},

(4.13)

where in the third step we the Gaussian tail bound
[
30
, Section 7.1]
that
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and any
t\geqslant 2
,

\mathbb{P}(|z|\geqslant t)\geqslant\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}(t^{-1}-t^{-3})\exp\left(-t^{2}/2\right)\geqslant t^{-1}\exp(-t^{2}/2)/3.

Moreover, since
\mathbf{x}_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\operatorname{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}_{d}(\bm{0},\Sigma)
, we have that

\bm{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j}\in T_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\operatorname{Ber}(p_{n}),\quad j=1,\cdots,n.

Now we proceed to control the summation of
\bm{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j}\in T_{n})
,
j=1,\cdots,n
.
We have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j}\in T_{n})-np_{n}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}np_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{np_{n}}{12}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-Cn^{1-1/(d^{2}/2+2d)}\right)=\oo(1),

where in the first step we used Lemma
C.1
, and in the second step we used (
4.13
) and
C
is some absolute positive constant. Consequently, with probability
1-\oo(1)
, there are at most
1.5np_{n}\leqslant 3dn^{1-1/(\xi(d))}=\OO(n^{1-1/(\xi(d))})
points in the tail region
T_{n}
.

Second, we show that with probability
1-\oo(1)
, for all
\mathbf{x}_{j}\in B_{n}
,
\mathsf{d}_{j}\geqslant n^{1/2}
. Fix one
\mathbf{x}_{j_{0}}\in B_{n}
, we have that for all
j\neq j_{0}
,
\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{j_{0}}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})|\mathbf{x}_{j_{0}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\operatorname{Ber}(q_{n,j_{0}})
,
where

\displaystyle q_{n,j_{0}}\coloneq\int_{\lVert\mathbf{x}_{j_{0}}-\mathbf{x}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sigma_{i}^{-1}\phi\left(\frac{x_{j_{0},i}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

(4.14)

and
\phi(\cdot)
is the density of standard Gaussian distribution.
For sufficiently large
n
and any
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})^{\top}
such that
\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j_{0}}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}
,

\left|\frac{x_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\right|\leqslant\left|\frac{x_{j_{0},i}+r_{n}}{\sigma_{i}}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{\log n/(d^{2}/4+d+1)}+r_{n}/\sigma_{i}\leqslant\sqrt{\log n/(d^{2}/4+d)},\quad i=1,\cdots,d,

where in the second step we used (
4.11
) and in the last step we used (
2.9
). Combining with (
4.14
), we have that

\displaystyle q_{n,j_{0}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{\lVert\mathbf{x}_{j_{0}}-\mathbf{x}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sigma_{i}^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{d^{2}/2+2d}\log n\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\asymp r_{n}^{d}n^{-1/(d/2+2)},

(4.15)

where in the last step we used the fact that
\int_{\lVert\mathbf{x}_{j_{0}}-\mathbf{x}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\asymp r_{n}^{d}
. Hence, conditioning on
\mathbf{x}_{j_{0}}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{j\neq j_{0}}\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{j_{0}}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})-(n-1)q_{n,j_{0}}\right|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}(n-1)q_{n,j_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\frac{(n-1)q_{n,j_{0}}}{12}\right)\leqslant 2\exp(-C_{3}n^{1-\tfrac{d}{d+4}-\tfrac{2}{d+4}})=2\exp(-C_{3}n^{2/(d+4)})=\oo(n^{-2}),

where in the first step we used Lemma
C.1
and the fact that
0\leqslant\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{j_{0}}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})|\mathbf{x}_{j_{0}}\leqslant 1
, in the second step we used (
4.15
) and (
2.9
) and
C_{3}
is some absolute positive constant. Since the conditional probability bound in (
4.11
) is free of
\mathbf{x}_{j_{0}}
, it holds unconditionally for any
\mathbf{x}_{j_{0}}\in B_{n}
. Consequently, by taking the union bound over at most
n
points
\mathbf{x}_{j}
such that
\mathbf{x}_{j}\in B_{n}
, we have that for sufficiently large
n
, with probability
1-\oo(n^{-1})
, for all
\mathbf{x}_{j}
such that
\mathbf{x}_{j}\in B_{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}_{j}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{s\neq j}\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{s}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})\geqslant\frac{1}{2}(n-1)q_{n,j}\gtrsim n^{2/(d+4)},

(4.16)

where the second step is from (
4.11
) and the last step is from (
4.15
) and (
2.9
).

Combining the above discussion, we have that with probability
1-\oo(1)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mathsf{d}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\{j:\mathbf{x}_{j}\in T_{n}\}}\frac{1}{\mathsf{d}_{j}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\{j:\mathbf{x}_{j}\in B_{n}\}}\frac{1}{\mathsf{d}_{j}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\{j:\mathbf{x}_{j}\in T_{n}\}}1+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\{j:\mathbf{x}_{j}\in B_{n}\}}\frac{1}{n^{2/(d+4)}}

\displaystyle=\OO(n^{-1/(\xi(d)))})+\OO(n^{-2/(d+4)})=\OO(n^{-1/(\xi(d)))}),

(4.17)

where in the second step we used (
4.8
) and (
4.16
) and the last step is from the fact that for

,
d^{2}/2+2d+2\geqslant(d+4)/2
. Note that for sufficiently large
n
, (
4.17
) contradicts (
4.10
) that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mathsf{d}_{j}}\gtrsim n^{-2/(2\xi(d)+\eta)}.

Therefore, our assumption (
4.2
) cannot hold.
Consequently, we have that with probability
1-\oo(1)
,
K_{0}\leqslant n^{1-2/(2\xi(d)+\eta)}
. Recall (
4.1
) that
\xi(d)=(d+2)^{2}/2
. This completes our proof.
∎

Proof of (
2.11
)

Recall
\mathbf{L}
in (
2.3
),
\Delta_{\varrho}
in (

),
\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}
in (

\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}

.

Proof.

We begin by comparing the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}
. Recall
\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}
in (
2.16
), which is a smoothed version of
\mathbf{L}

\lambda_{1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}})\leqslant\lambda_{2}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}})\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\lambda_{n}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}})

B.1
, we have that whenever
\alpha_{n}

, for sufficiently large
n
, with probability

which is a finer approximation of

\lVert(\sL_{n}-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n})\varphi\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}

Proof.

Recall from Theorem
3.1
,

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{x})=C_{\varphi}\prod_{i=1}^{d}H_{k_{i}}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i}),\quad k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}\in\mathbb{N},

(4.37)

where
C_{\varphi}
is some constant and
\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{d}
are as in (
2.8
).

Control of the bias part.

Recall (
4.33
), (
4.34
) and (
4.35
). By Lemma
B.2
,
\mathcal{T}_{n}\varphi(\mathbf{x})
can be represented as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n}\varphi(\mathbf{x})=\frac{2C_{\varphi}}{m_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{0}\leqslant\bm{s}\leqslant\bm{k}\\
\bm{s}\neq\bm{k}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\frac{(-1)^{|\bm{\alpha}|}}{\bm{\alpha}!2^{|\bm{\alpha}|}}\mathsf{C}_{\bm{k},\bm{s},\bm{\alpha}}\bm{\sigma}^{-\bm{\gamma}}r_{n}^{|\bm{\gamma}|-2}\Lambda_{\bm{\gamma}}(r_{n};\bm{\sigma},g),

where
\bm{\gamma}=\bm{k}-\bm{s}+\bm{\alpha}
. We point out that by symmetry, for (
4.34
),
\Lambda_{\bm{\gamma}}(r_{n};\bm{\sigma},g)=0
whenever there exists some odd
\gamma_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,d
, and that for all
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,

\displaystyle|\Lambda_{\bm{\gamma}}(r_{n};\bm{\sigma},g)|\leqslant\int_{\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{p}\leqslant 1}g(\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{p})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}\lesssim m_{0},

(4.38)

where in the first step we used the fact that
|z_{i}|\leqslant\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{p}\leqslant 1
for all
i=1,\cdots,d
and in the second step we used (
2.2
) and our assumption on
g(\cdot)
in Theorem
2.2
.
Moreover, observe that when
|\bm{\gamma}|\geqslant 4
,
r_{n}^{|\bm{\gamma}|-2}\Lambda_{\bm{\gamma}}(r_{n};\bm{\sigma},g)=\OO(r_{n}^{2})
. Since
|\bm{\gamma}|>0
and all terms with odd
|\bm{\gamma}|
yield zero, we can decompose
\mathcal{T}_{n}=\mathsf{M}+\mathsf{R}
, where the main part
\mathsf{M}
collects all terms with
|\bm{\gamma}|=2
and the remainder part
\mathsf{R}
collects all terms with
|\bm{\gamma}|\geqslant 4
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{2C_{\varphi}}{m_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{0}\leqslant\bm{s}\leqslant\bm{k},\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\\
\bm{s}\neq\bm{k},|\bm{\gamma}|=2\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{|\bm{\alpha}|}}{\bm{\alpha}!2^{|\bm{\alpha}|}}\mathsf{C}_{\bm{k},\bm{s},\bm{\alpha}}\bm{\sigma}^{-\bm{\gamma}}r_{n}^{|\bm{\gamma}|-2}\Lambda_{\bm{\gamma}}(r_{n};\bm{\sigma},g),

(4.39)

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{2C_{\varphi}}{m_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{0}\leqslant\bm{s}\leqslant\bm{k},\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\\
\bm{s}\neq\bm{k},|\bm{\gamma}|\geqslant 4\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{|\bm{\alpha}|}}{\bm{\alpha}!2^{|\bm{\alpha}|}}\mathsf{C}_{\bm{k},\bm{s},\bm{\alpha}}\bm{\sigma}^{-\bm{\gamma}}r_{n}^{|\bm{\gamma}|-2}\Lambda_{\bm{\gamma}}(r_{n};\bm{\sigma},g).

(4.40)

For the main part
\mathsf{M}
,
\Lambda_{\bm{\gamma}}(r_{n};\bm{\sigma},g)\neq 0
only when there is a unique index
i
such that
\gamma_{i}=2
and
\gamma_{j}=0
for all
j\neq i
. In that case,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\bm{\gamma}}(r_{n};\bm{\sigma},g)

\displaystyle=\int_{\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{p}\leqslant 1}g(\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{p})\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{r_{n}^{2}z_{i}^{2}}{4\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)z_{i}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{p}\leqslant 1}g(\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{p})(1+\OO(r_{n}^{2}))z_{i}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}=m_{2}(1+\OO(r_{n}^{2})),

(4.41)

where in the second step we used the fact that
\exp(-t)=1+\OO(t)
uniformly for
0\leqslant t\leqslant 1
, and in the last step we used (
2.2
).
The condition
\gamma_{i}=2
arises either from
s_{i}=k_{i}-1,\alpha_{i}=1
or from
s_{i}=k_{i}-2,\alpha_{i}=0
. Then we proceed to discuss these two cases. When
s_{i}=k_{i}-1,\alpha_{i}=1
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\bm{k},\bm{s},\bm{\alpha}}=-2x_{i}\left(\prod_{j\neq i}H_{k_{j}}(x_{j}/\sigma_{j})\right)2k_{i}H_{k_{i}-1}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i})/\sigma_{i}=-2x_{i}\frac{\partial\mathsf{H}_{\bm{k}}(\mathbf{x}/\bm{\sigma})}{\partial x_{i}},

(4.42)

where in the first step we used the fact that
H_{0}\equiv 1,H_{1}(x)=2x
and in the second step we used the identity (
C.4
). When
s_{i}=k_{i}-2,\alpha_{i}=0
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\bm{k},\bm{s},\bm{\alpha}}=-H_{k_{i}-2}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i})\left(\prod_{j\neq i}H_{k_{j}}(x_{j}/\sigma_{j})\right)2k_{i}(k_{i}-1)=-\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathsf{H}_{\bm{k}}(\mathbf{x}/\bm{\sigma})}{\partial x_{i}^{2}},

(4.43)

where in the last step we applied (
C.4
) twice. Recall
\mathsf{M}
in (
4.39
). Combining (
4.41
), (
4.42
) and (
4.43
), we have that

\displaystyle|\mathsf{M}-\Delta_{\varrho}\varphi(\mathbf{x})|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{2C_{\varphi}}{m_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{1}{2}2x_{i}\frac{\partial\mathsf{H}_{\bm{k}}(\mathbf{x}/\bm{\sigma})}{\partial x_{i}}\sigma_{i}^{-2}m_{2}(1+\OO(r_{n}^{2}))-\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathsf{H}_{\bm{k}}(\mathbf{x}/\bm{\sigma})}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}\sigma_{i}^{-2}m_{2}(1+\OO(r_{n}^{2}))\right)-\Delta_{\varrho}\varphi(\mathbf{x})\right|

\displaystyle=\OO(r_{n}^{2})\widetilde{\varphi}(\mathbf{x}),

(4.44)

where in the last step we used (
3.2
) and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varphi}(\mathbf{x})=C_{\varphi}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\mathsf{H}_{\bm{k}}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}\right|+\left|\frac{2x_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{H}_{\bm{k}}}{\partial x_{i}}\right|\right).

We point out that
\widetilde{\varphi}
is the summation of the absolute values of
2d
polynomials. Therefore, by the triangle inequality,
\lVert\widetilde{\varphi}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}<\infty
.

We now control the remainder
\mathsf{R}
with
|\bm{\gamma}|\geqslant 4
. Recall (
4.40
). We have that

\displaystyle|\mathsf{R}|

\displaystyle\lesssim r_{n}^{2}\sum_{\bm{0}\leqslant\bm{s}\leqslant\bm{k}}\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\frac{1}{\bm{\alpha}!2^{|\bm{\alpha}|}}|\mathsf{C}_{\bm{k},\bm{s},\bm{\alpha}}|\bm{\sigma}^{-\bm{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\leqslant r_{n}^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left\{\sum_{s_{i}=0}^{k_{i}}\sum_{\alpha_{i}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}!2^{\alpha_{i}}}\left|H_{s_{i}}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i})H_{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i})\right|\binom{k_{i}}{s_{i}}2^{k_{i}-s_{i}}\sigma_{i}^{-(k_{i}-s_{i}+\alpha_{i})}\right\},

where in the first step we used (
4.38
) and in the second step we used the factorization structure of (
4.34
) and (
4.35
). Recall
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}
in (
2.6
) and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{{\mathcal{F}}_{i}}
in (
3.6
).
Then we have

\displaystyle\lVert\mathsf{R}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle\lesssim r_{n}^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\biggl\lVert\sum_{s_{i}=0}^{k_{i}}\sum_{\alpha_{i}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}!2^{\alpha_{i}}}\left|H_{s_{i}}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i})H_{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i})\right|\binom{k_{i}}{s_{i}}2^{k_{i}-s_{i}}\sigma_{i}^{-(k_{i}-s_{i}+\alpha_{i})}\biggr\rVert_{\mathcal{F}_{i}}

\displaystyle\leqslant r_{n}^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left\{\sum_{s_{i}=0}^{k_{i}}\sum_{\alpha_{i}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}!2^{\alpha_{i}}}\binom{k_{i}}{s_{i}}2^{k_{i}-s_{i}}\sigma_{i}^{-(k_{i}-s_{i}+\alpha_{i})}\bigl\lVert H_{s_{i}}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i})H_{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i}/\sigma_{i})\bigr\rVert_{\mathcal{F}_{i}}\right\}

\displaystyle\lesssim r_{n}^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left\{\sum_{s_{i}=0}^{k_{i}}\sum_{\alpha_{i}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}!2^{\alpha_{i}}}\sqrt{(s_{i}+1)!\alpha_{i}!}(2\sqrt{2})^{s_{i}+\alpha_{i}}\binom{k_{i}}{s_{i}}2^{k_{i}-s_{i}}\sigma_{i}^{-\alpha_{i}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant r_{n}^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left\{\sum_{s_{i}=0}^{k_{i}}\binom{k_{i}}{s_{i}}2^{k_{i}-s_{i}}(2\sqrt{2})^{s_{i}}\sqrt{(s_{i}+1)!}\sum_{\alpha_{i}=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\sqrt{2}/\sigma_{i})^{\alpha_{i}}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{i}!}}\right\}=\OO(r_{n}^{2}),

(4.45)

where in the second step we used the triangle inequality, in the third step we used Lemma
C.4
and the fact that
\sigma_{i}^{-(k_{i}-s_{i})}
is bounded and in the last step we used the fact that by ratio test,

\sum_{\alpha_{i}=0}^{\infty}(\sqrt{2}/\sigma_{i})^{\alpha_{i}}/\sqrt{\alpha_{i}!}<\infty
,
and that
k_{1},\cdots,k_{d}
are fixed integers.

Combining (
4.44
) and (
4.45
), we have that

\displaystyle\lVert(\mathcal{T}_{n}-\Delta_{\varrho})\varphi\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle=\lVert\mathsf{M}-\Delta_{\varrho}\varphi+\mathsf{R}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}\leqslant\lVert\mathsf{M}-\Delta_{\varrho}\varphi\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}+\lVert\mathsf{R}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}\leqslant\OO(r_{n}^{2})\lVert\widetilde{\varphi}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}+\OO(r_{n}^{2})=\OO(r_{n}^{2}),

(4.46)

which completes our proof of the bias part.

Control of the variance part.
Set the bulk region

\displaystyle B_{1}\coloneq\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left[-\sigma_{i}\sqrt{2(1+\beta)\log n},\sigma_{i}\sqrt{2(1+\beta)\log n}\right],

(4.47)

and denote
\bm{1}_{B_{1}}(\mathbf{x})=\bm{1}(\mathbf{x}\in B_{1})
, where
\beta>0
is any fixed small constant. Recall (
4.36
).
By the triangle inequality, it suffices to show
\lVert(\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n})\varphi\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}=\oop(1)
and
\lVert(\mathcal{T}_{n}-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n})\varphi\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}=\oo(1)
. We start with the proof of the first term. Recall
\mathsf{s}(\cdot)
and
g^{*}(\cdot)
as in (
2.15
).
For
j=1,\cdots,n
, set

\displaystyle w_{j}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{s}\left(\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}^{2})\right)g^{*}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})(\varphi(\mathbf{x})-\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{j})),

(4.48)

\displaystyle v_{j}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{s}\left(\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}^{2})\right)g^{*}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}/r_{n}).

(4.49)

For convenience of notation, denote

\displaystyle N_{1}\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}

\displaystyle,\quad N_{2}\coloneq\int_{\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}}g(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})(\varphi(\mathbf{x})-\varphi(\mathbf{y}))\varrho(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y},

(4.50)

\displaystyle D_{1}\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}v_{j}

\displaystyle,\quad D_{2}\coloneq\int_{\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}}g(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})\varrho(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y},

(4.51)

and

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}\coloneq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}/(2\sigma_{i}^{2})\right)^{1/2}.

(4.52)

Our argument relies on the following decomposition

\displaystyle\frac{m_{2}r_{n}^{2}}{2m_{0}}\lVert(\widetilde{\mathbf{\mathcal{L}}}_{n}-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n})\varphi\rVert_{{\mathcal{F}}}\leqslant

\displaystyle\biggl\lVert\left(\frac{N_{1}}{D_{1}}-\frac{N_{1}}{D_{2}}\right)\bm{1}_{B_{1}}\biggr\rVert_{{\mathcal{F}}}+\biggl\lVert\left(\frac{N_{1}}{D_{1}}-\frac{N_{1}}{D_{2}}\right)\bm{1}_{B_{1}^{\complement}}\biggr\rVert_{{\mathcal{F}}}+

\displaystyle\biggl\lVert\left(\frac{N_{1}}{D_{2}}-\frac{N_{2}}{D_{2}}\right)\bm{1}_{B_{1}}\biggr\rVert_{{\mathcal{F}}}+\biggl\lVert\left(\frac{N_{1}}{D_{2}}-\frac{N_{2}}{D_{2}}\right)\bm{1}_{B_{1}^{\complement}}\biggr\rVert_{{\mathcal{F}}}

\displaystyle\coloneq

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{1}+\mathsf{E}_{2}+\mathsf{E}_{3}+\mathsf{E}_{4}.

(4.53)

In what follows, we will show that
\mathsf{E}_{i}=\oop(r_{n}^{2})
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant 4
.

(i)

Control of
\mathsf{E}_{4}
.
By the triangle inequality,

\bigl\lVert\mathsf{E}_{4}\bigr\rVert_{{\mathcal{F}}}\leqslant\bigl\lVert\left(N_{2}/D_{2}\right)\bm{1}_{B_{1}^{\complement}}\bigr\rVert_{{\mathcal{F}}}+\bigl\lVert\left(N_{1}/D_{2}\right)\bm{1}_{B_{1}^{\complement}}\bigr\rVert_{{\mathcal{F}}}.

Recall
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
in (
4.36
),
N_{2}
in (
4.50
) and
D_{2}
in (
4.51
). For the first term,

\displaystyle\bigl\lVert(N_{2}/D_{2})\bm{1}_{B_{1}^{\complement}}\bigr\rVert_{{\mathcal{F}}}\asymp r_{n}^{2}\lVert\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}\varphi\bm{1}_{B_{1}^{\complement}}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}\leqslant r_{n}^{2}(\lVert\mathcal{T}_{n}\varphi\bm{1}_{B_{1}^{\complement}}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}+\lVert(\mathcal{T}_{n}-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n})\varphi\rVert_{\mathcal{F}})=\oo(r_{n}^{2}),

(4.54)

where in the last step we used Lemma
B.3
and (
4.47
).
Next, we control the second term. Denote

\displaystyle i_{0}\equiv i_{0}(\mathbf{x},\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d})\coloneq\arg\max_{i}\{|x_{i}/\sigma_{i}|\}.

(4.55)

We can see that whenever
\mathbf{x}\notin B_{1}
,
|x_{i_{0}}|\geqslant\sigma_{i_{0}}\sqrt{2(1+\beta)\log n}
. Then with probability
1-\oo(1)
, for all
j=1,\cdots,n
and
\mathbf{x}\notin B_{1}
,

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}\geqslant|x_{i_{0}}|(1-1/\sqrt{1+\beta})\geqslant\frac{(1-1/\sqrt{1+\beta})}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\sigma_{i_{0}}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}x_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\geqslant\zeta\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma},

(4.56)

where in the first step we used Lemma
C.5
, in the second step we used (
4.55
) and
\zeta>0
is some fixed constant independent of
\mathbf{x}
. Therefore, for sufficiently large
n
, with probability
1-\oo(1)
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{s}\left(\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}^{2}))}\leqslant\frac{1}{1+\exp(\alpha_{n}(\zeta\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{2}-r_{n}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp(-\alpha_{n}(\zeta\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{2}-r_{n}^{2}))\leqslant\exp(-\alpha_{n}\zeta\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{2}/2),

(4.57)

where in the second step we used (
4.56
) and the last step is from the fact that for sufficiently large
n
,
r_{n}^{2}\leqslant\zeta\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{2}/2
.
Moreover, recalling (
4.37
) and (
4.55
), for
\mathbf{x}\notin B_{1}
, we have that with probability
1-\oo(1)
,

\displaystyle|\varphi(\mathbf{x})|,|\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{j})|\lesssim\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i_{0}}/\sigma_{i_{0}}|^{k_{i}}\lesssim\prod_{i=1}^{d}\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{k_{i}}=\OO(\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{|\bm{k}|}).

(4.58)

Recall
N_{1}
in (
4.50
) and that
g^{*}(\cdot)
is bounded. Then we have that

\displaystyle N_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathsf{s}(\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}^{2}))g^{*}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})(\varphi(\mathbf{x})-\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{j}))

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(|\varphi(\mathbf{x})|+|\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{j})|)\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha_{n}\zeta}{2}\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{|\bm{k}|}\exp\left(-2\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{2}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha_{n}\zeta}{4}\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{2}\right)\leqslant\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{|\bm{k}|}\varrho^{2}(\mathbf{x})\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha_{n}\zeta}{8}|x_{i_{0}}/\sigma_{i_{0}}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{|\bm{k}|}\varrho^{2}(\mathbf{x})\exp(-\alpha_{n}\zeta\log n/4)\leqslant\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{|\bm{k}|}\varrho^{2}(\mathbf{x})n^{-5},

(4.59)

where in the second step we used (
4.57
), in the third step we used (
4.58
) and in the last but one step we used that
|x_{i_{0}}/\sigma_{i_{0}}|>\sqrt{2(1+\beta)\log n}
.
On the other hand, for
\mathbf{x}\notin B_{1}
and
\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}
, we have that

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert_{\Sigma}\leqslant\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}+\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{\Sigma}=\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}+\OO(r_{n})\leqslant\sqrt{2}\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}.

(4.60)

Recall
D_{2}
in (
4.51
). (
4.60
) indicates that

\displaystyle D_{2}=\int_{\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}}g(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})\exp(-\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{2})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\gtrsim r_{n}^{d}\exp(-2\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{2}).

(4.61)

Therefore, with probability
1-\oo(1)
,

\displaystyle\biggl\lVert\left(\frac{N_{1}}{D_{2}}\right)\bm{1}_{B_{1}^{\complement}}\biggr\rVert_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}^{\complement}}\left(\frac{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{|\bm{k}|}\varrho^{2}(\mathbf{x})n^{-5}}{r_{n}^{d}\varrho^{2}(\mathbf{x})}\right)^{2}\varrho^{2}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=n^{-10}r_{n}^{-2d}\int_{B_{1}^{\complement}}\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{2|\bm{k}|}\varrho^{2}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle n^{-10}r_{n}^{-2d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{2|\bm{k}|}\varrho^{2}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\oo(r_{n}^{4}),

(4.62)

where in the first step we used (
4.59
) and (
4.61
), and in the last step we used (
2.9
) and the fact that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\Sigma}^{2|\bm{k}|}\varrho^{2}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}<\infty
.
Combining (
4.54
) and (
4.62
), we conclude that
\mathsf{E}_{4}=\oop(r_{n}^{2})
.

(ii)

Control of
\mathsf{E}_{2}
.
Recall
N_{1}
in (
4.50
) and

in (
4.51

with

.

(iii)

Control of
\mathsf{E}_{3}
.
Recall
w_{j}
in (
4.48
).
We have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\lVert\mathsf{E}_{3}\rVert_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbf{x}\in B_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}-N_{2}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{D_{2}^{2}}\varrho^{2}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\OO(r_{n}^{-2d})\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbf{x}\in B_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}-N_{2}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\OO(r_{n}^{-2d})\int_{\mathbf{x}\in B_{1}}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}-N_{2}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\OO(r_{n}^{-2d})\int_{\mathbf{x}\in B_{1}}\frac{1}{n}(\mathbb{E}w_{j}^{2}-N_{2}^{2})+\frac{n-1}{n}\mathbb{E}w_{j}(\mathbb{E}w_{j}-N_{2})+\frac{n+1}{n}N_{2}(N_{2}-\mathbb{E}w_{j})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\OO(r_{n}^{-2d})\int_{\mathbf{x}\in B_{1}}\frac{1}{n}(\mathbb{E}w_{j}^{2}-N_{2}^{2})+2|\mathbb{E}w_{j}-N_{2}+N_{2}||\mathbb{E}w_{j}-N_{2}|+2|N_{2}||N_{2}-\mathbb{E}w_{j}|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\OO(r_{n}^{-2d})\int_{\mathbf{x}\in B_{1}}\frac{1}{n}(\mathbb{E}w_{j}^{2}+N_{2}^{2})+2|\mathbb{E}w_{j}-N_{2}|^{2}+4|N_{2}||N_{2}-\mathbb{E}w_{j}|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

Consider the following eigenvalue problem for second order linear partial differential equations:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{2x_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}+\lambda f=0,

Lemma B.5

Proof.

Suppose

\displaystyle\frac{2m_{0}}{m_{2}r_{n}^{2}}\left(\varphi^{*}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{j})\varphi^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{j})}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{j})}\right)=\lambda^{*}\varphi^{*}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle\left|g(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})-g^{*}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})\mathsf{s}\left(\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}^{2})\right)\right|\leqslant\frac{\max_{t\geqslant 0}g^{*}(t)}{1+\exp(\alpha_{n}|r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}^{2}|)}.

(C.9)

Proof.

We split our proof into two cases
\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}
and
\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}>r_{n}
.

Case 1.
When
\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}
, since
\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})=1
and
g(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})=g^{*}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|g(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})-g^{*}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})\mathsf{s}\left(\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}^{2})\right)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|g^{*}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})-\frac{g^{*}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})}{1+\exp\left(-\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}^{2})\right)}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{g^{*}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})\exp\left(-\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}^{2})\right)}{1+\exp\left(-\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}^{2})\right)}\leqslant\frac{\max_{t\geqslant 0}g^{*}(t)}{1+\exp(\alpha_{n}|r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}^{2}|)}.

Case 2.
When
\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}>r_{n}
, since
\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})=0
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|g(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})\bm{1}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n})-g^{*}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})\mathsf{s}\left(\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}^{2})\right)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{g^{*}(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})}{1+\exp\left(-\alpha_{n}(r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}^{2})\right)}\leqslant\frac{\max_{t\geqslant 0}g^{*}(t)}{1+\exp(\alpha_{n}|r_{n}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}^{2}|)}.

This completes our proof.
∎

Recall
B_{1}
as in (
4.47
). In the following lemma, we provide a uniform approximation of

\int_{\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}}g(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})\varrho(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

Lemma C.7
.

Uniformly for any
\mathbf{x}\in B_{1}
,

\int_{\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}}g(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})\varrho(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}=m_{0}r_{n}^{d}\varrho(\mathbf{x})(1+\oo(1))

Proof.

Applying a change of variable that
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}+r_{n}\mathbf{z}
gives

\displaystyle\int_{\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}}g(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})\varrho(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}=r_{n}^{d}\int_{\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{p}\leqslant 1}g(\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{p})\varrho(\mathbf{x}+r_{n}\mathbf{z})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}.

(C.10)

Moreover, uniformly for all
\mathbf{x}\in B_{1}
,

\displaystyle\varrho(\mathbf{x}+r_{n}\mathbf{z})/\varrho(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}((x_{i}+r_{n}z_{i})^{2}-x_{i}^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\exp(-\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|\OO(r_{n}))=\exp(\oo(1))=1+\oo(1),

where in the third step we used (
4.47
). Note this also indicates that uniformly for
\mathbf{x}
such that
|x_{i}|\leqslant C\log n
for
i=1,\cdots,d
and any large fixed constant
C>0
,

\displaystyle\varrho(\mathbf{x}+r_{n}\mathbf{z})=\varrho(\mathbf{x})(1+\oo(1)).

(C.11)

Combining with (
C.10
), we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}\leqslant r_{n}}g(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{p}/r_{n})\varrho(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}=r_{n}^{d}m_{0}\varrho(\mathbf{x})(1+\oo(1)).

This completes the proof.
∎
