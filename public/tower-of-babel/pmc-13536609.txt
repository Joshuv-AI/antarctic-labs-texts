Ocular manifestations of expanded dengue syndrome: a systematic review with meta-analysis
Authors: Daniel Peñaherrera-Vásquez, Luis Fuenmayor-González, German Fierro, Jaime Fernando Andrade M., Silvia Jessica Guarderas-Muñoz, Sylvia Campoverde, Geovanny Chuchuca-Carrión, Josué Rivadeneira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOcular manifestations are under-recognized complications of expanded dengue syndrome, with no robust frequency estimates available.MethodsA systematic review was conducted following PRISMA guidelines. Primary studies reporting the frequency of ocular manifestations consistent with expanded dengue syndrome (uveitis, maculopathy, retinal hemorrhage, retinal vasculitis, and optic neuropathy) in patients with confirmed dengue diagnosis were included. Six databases were searched (PubMed, Scopus, Embase, WoS, BIREME–BVS, and SciELO), supplemented by manual searching of reference lists and gray literature, with no date, language, or geographic restrictions. Risk of bias was assessed using the JBI critical appraisal checklist for prevalence studies. Pooled frequency estimates were calculated using a random-effects model with Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran's Q, I2, and Tau2. Subgroup analyses were performed based on time of onset of ocular manifestations relative to dengue symptom onset (early < 7 days vs. late ≥ 7 days). Funnel plot asymmetry and Egger's test were used to assess reporting bias in analyses, including ≥ 10 studies.ResultsEight studies (n = 1,394 patients) were included. Pooled frequency estimates were maculopathy 6% (95% CI 3–10%; I2 = 28.9%, τ2 = < 0.01) and retinal hemorrhage 8% (95% CI 3–14%; I2 = 55.9%, τ2 = < 0.01). Maculopathy frequency was higher beyond 7 days from symptom onset (10% vs. 5%), a pattern consistent with, but not confirmatory of, an immune-mediated pathophysiology.ConclusionsMaculopathy, retinal hemorrhage, and optic neuropathy are relevant complications of expanded dengue syndrome. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for these ocular manifestations, particularly during the recovery phase.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41182-026-01051-0.

Introduction

Dengue fever is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as an acute febrile illness lasting 2–7 days, accompanied by at least two of the following: headache, retro-orbital pain, myalgia, arthralgia, rash, hemorrhagic manifestations, or leukopenia, in a patient from or traveling to an endemic area [1]. The WHO classifies dengue into three categories of increasing severity dengue without warning signs, dengue with warning signs, and severe dengue the latter defined by significant plasma leakage leading to shock, severe bleeding, or severe organ involvement [1–3].

Expanded dengue encompasses atypical clinical presentations in which the disease extends beyond its classical manifestations to affect organs not typically involved in uncomplicated infection, including the nervous system, liver, kidneys, and heart, among others [4]. Since expanded dengue manifestations are not included in the WHO's classification of dengue severity, their attribution to dengue infection relies on a combination of criteria, including laboratory-confirmed dengue infection (PCR, IgM, or NS1 antigen detection), a temporal relationship between systemic dengue onset and the emergence of the atypical manifestation, and exclusion of alternative causes [4]. Notably, such organ-specific complications have been documented not only in the context of dengue hemorrhagic fever but also in patients who present without plasma leakage, underscoring that severe end-organ involvement is not exclusive to the hemorrhagic phenotype [3]. These manifestations may emerge at any phase of the illness and are variably associated with hemodynamic compromise, pre-existing comorbidities, coinfections, or direct immune-mediated tissue injury, collectively reflecting the heterogeneous and often unpredictable clinical continuum of expanded dengue syndrome [4, 5].

Despite the breadth of organ systems recognized under the expanded dengue framework, the WHO classification does not comprehensively address ophthalmic involvement, which remains an undercharacterized dimension of the syndrome, despite having a plausible and well-grounded pathophysiological basis [4]. Ocular injury in dengue is driven by the breakdown of the blood–ocular barrier, resulting from antibody-dependent enhancement, a phenomenon in which pre-existing non-neutralizing antibodies from a prior dengue infection facilitate viral entry into Fc-receptor-bearing cells and amplify viral replication, together with complement activation and the release of inflammatory mediators, which collectively promote vascular leakage, tissue injury, and ischemia [6, 7].

These mechanisms translate into a broad spectrum of funduscopic findings, predominantly affecting the posterior pole and manifesting as retinal hemorrhages, macular edema, foveolitis, vasculitis, and optic neuropathy, which may arise during the acute febrile phase or emerge weeks after systemic resolution [7]. Notably, ocular features in dengue fever are a rarely investigated aspect of the expanded dengue syndrome, and their clinical significance is frequently underestimated in routine practice [8].

Despite growing clinical recognition of these manifestations, the available evidence is inconsistent and lacks robust frequency estimates, limiting our understanding of their true epidemiological burden. The epidemiological characterization of ocular manifestations in the context of expanded dengue fever is essential for establishing strategies for early diagnosis and preventing permanent visual impairment.

The aim of this study was to estimate the frequency of ocular manifestations associated with expanded dengue fever. As a secondary objective, we sought to evaluate the duration of dengue illness as a contributing factor to the incidence of these ocular manifestations.

Methods

This manuscript has been prepared in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [9]. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO on April 9, 2026, ID: CRD420261364929.

Study design

Systematic review.

Eligibility criteria

Primary studies including cross-sectional studies; prospective or retrospective cohort studies; baseline data from randomized controlled trials and non-randomized controlled studies, that describe patients with confirmed dengue diagnosis by NS1 antigen, IgM antibodies, or polymerase chain reaction (PCR), will be included if they report the frequency of at least one ocular manifestation considered characteristic of expanded dengue [4], provided that each individual study defined and applied its own criteria to support a causal attribution of the ocular findings to dengue infection, including a temporal association between systemic dengue onset and the emergence of the ocular manifestation, and exclusion of alternative causes for the ocular findings (pre-existing ocular disease, trauma, or unrelated systemic comorbidities).

The WHO framework for expanded dengue broadly recognizes three categories of ocular involvement: macular hemorrhage, impaired visual acuity, and optic neuritis [4], without providing detailed diagnostic criteria for these entities. Because these categories are too broad to be operationalized as discrete, clinically confirmed pathological outcomes, we incorporated more specific diagnostic definitions proposed in subsequent ophthalmological literature [5]. This operationalization also included uveitis and retinal vasculitis which, although not explicitly named among the WHO’s ocular categories, are consistent with the WHO’s broader definition of expanded dengue as an atypical, organ-specific manifestation extending beyond the classical clinical picture [4].

Accordingly, within this study, the ocular manifestations of expanded dengue comprise: uveitis, maculopathy in any of its forms (including cystoid macular edema, diffuse retinal thickening, foveolitis, hemorrhagic maculopathy or subretinal yellow dots), retinal hemorrhage of vasculopathic origin (distinguished from thrombocytopenic hemorrhage by its morphology flame-shaped, dot, blot, or preretinal and its localization in the posterior pole, with thrombocytopenia not being a required condition), retinal vasculitis (vascular sheathing, angiographic leakage, or late hyperfluorescence of vessel walls), and optic neuropathy (disc edema, disc hyperemia, peripapillary hemorrhages, visual field defect, relative afferent pupillary defect, or visual acuity loss associated with disc abnormalities) [4, 5] (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Ocular manifestations of dengue and expanded dengue syndrome

No linguistic, geographic, or date-of-publication restrictions were applied. Studies whose results were reported exclusively as the number of eyes were excluded, since the unit of analysis in this review is the patient. Studies reporting exclusively manifestations considered characteristic of nonexpanded classic dengue will be excluded, such as retro-orbital pain, conjunctival injection, subconjunctival hemorrhage, or peripheral petechial retinal hemorrhage attributable solely to thrombocytopenia, if none of these coexist with any of the ocular outcomes of interest described above.

Conference abstracts lacking complete data, case reports, letters to the editor, expert opinions, commentaries, editorials, and narrative reviews were excluded. Systematic reviews were not considered primary sources; however, their reference lists were screened as part of the manual search strategy.

Information sources

The databases Medline (PubMed), Scopus, Embase, Web of Science (WoS), and the libraries BIREME–BVS and SciELO were searched. In addition, a manual search was performed, including cross-referenced citations from the reference lists of included studies and gray literature. If an article cited another study that appeared relevant based on its title, we retrieved and assessed that study according to our eligibility criteria. This process was repeated iteratively until no additional eligible studies were identified [10].

Search strategy

Sensitive search strategies were developed and adapted for each information source, using MeSH, DeCS, and Emtree terms, combined with free-text terms and the Boolean operators OR and, following the PO acronym (population [P]: patients with confirmed dengue infection; outcome [O]: ocular manifestations of expanded dengue) (Appendix Supplementary 1).

The search strategy was independently peer-reviewed according to the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) checklist [11]. The search was last updated on April 20, 2026.

Selection process

Articles were managed using Rayyan Software (Rayyan Systems Inc., Cambridge, MA, USA) [12]. Authors JR, DP-V and GF independently screened the titles and abstracts of retrieved records. Subsequently, full texts of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and independently assessed by the same authors. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus, and if necessary, a third reviewer (LF-G) was consulted.

Data collection and analysis

Data extraction was performed independently by at least two investigators (DP and GF) using a standardized and pre-piloted data extraction form. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus, with involvement of a third reviewer (JR) when necessary.

Data items

Variables extracted from each included study were organized into three domains. First, study-level characteristics were recorded, including first author, year of publication, country of origin, study design, data source, number and type of study centers, recruitment method, sampling strategy, study period (start and end dates), and sample size estimation. Given that some included studies originated from the same country, these variables were used to assess the possibility of overlapping patient populations by comparing recruitment institutions and enrollment periods across studies. This verification confirmed that all eight included studies were conducted at distinct institutions, supporting compliance with the independence assumption of the random-effects model.

Second domain, population-level variables were extracted, comprising the number of participants, sex, age (reported as mean or median, with standard deviation [SD] and range where available). Third level, clinical and diagnostic variables were collected, including the method used to confirm dengue infection (PCR, IgM, ELISA, or NS1 antigen detection), the diagnostic method used to identify each ocular manifestation (fundoscopy, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, optical coherence tomography, fluorescein angiography, or automated perimetry), and the time elapsed from dengue onset to diagnosis of ocular involvement (reported as mean or median, with SD and range where available). For each outcome, diagnostic protocols were additionally categorized as "high-sensitivity protocols" (systematic use of OCT, fluorescein angiography, or automated perimetry in all enrolled patients) or "standard protocols" (fundoscopic or structural examination with selective use of advanced testing only in patients with abnormal baseline findings).

Primary outcome

Ocular manifestations were defined according to the WHO criteria (Fig. 1), while the specific definition of each pathology was considered according to the diagnostic criteria reported by each study.

Study risk-of-bias assessment

Risk of bias (RoB) for individual studies was assessed using the JBI critical appraisal checklist for prevalence and incidence studies [13–15]. This instrument evaluates nine aspects: (D1) adequacy of the sampling frame, (D2) recruitment method, (D3) sample size, (D4) study subjects and setting, (D5) coverage, (D6) diagnostic methods, (D7) reliability and standardization of measurements, (D8) statistical analysis, and (D9) response rate.

Two reviewers (DP-V and JR) independently evaluated each item, marking it as "yes," "no," or "unclear." Responses of "yes" were considered as low risk of bias and "no" as high risk. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved by consensus. RoB summary plots across domains were generated using the ROBVIS R package v.0.3.0.9001 [13].

Effect measures

The frequency of each ocular manifestation will be expressed as a proportion with its corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). When data are not reported in an included study, the corresponding authors will be contacted via email to request the missing information.

Synthesis method

A narrative synthesis of the results, including frequency tables and measures of central tendency and dispersion. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.4.2 and the meta for package.

The pooled frequency was estimated using a random-effects model to account for between-study heterogeneity. To stabilize inter-study variance, the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation was applied to the proportion data [16]. Including studies reporting the frequency of at least one ocular manifestation consistent with the expanded dengue definition, provided that a minimum of three studies were available for each outcome.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed by estimating Cochran’s Q, Tau2, and I2. Cochran’s Q p value < 0.10 was considered indicative of significant heterogeneity. I2 values were interpreted as follows: 0–40% not important, 30–60% moderate, 50–90% substantial, and 75–100% considerable heterogeneity. Tau2 was used to measure the variance of the true effect sizes across studies. The 95% prediction intervals (95% PI) were estimated when five or more studies were available [17].

Subgroup analyses were performed based on the time elapsed from dengue symptom onset to the diagnosis of ocular manifestations, using a cutoff of 7 days to define early (< 7 days) and late (≥ 7 days) presentations. This threshold was selected based on the WHO dengue classification framework, which defines the recovery phase as beginning around day 7, coinciding with the peak of immune complex circulation and the predominance of immune-mediated mechanisms underlying expanded dengue ocular manifestations [2]. The sensitivity of the diagnostic protocol was not previously specified in the study protocol as a subgroup variable and, given the small number of studies per stratum, was reported narratively in the Results rather than by a formal subgroup meta-analysis.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted for each outcome, including a leave-one-out approach and an overall frequency estimate assuming a predefined I2 of 10% [18]. Given that conventional two-step transformation methods, such as the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation, may yield suboptimal confidence interval coverage, lack of intuitive interpretation, and biases particularly in scenarios involving sparse data, extreme event rates, or a small number of included studies [19] an additional sensitivity analysis was conducted using a one-step generalized linear mixed model (GLMM), as described by Lin and Chu [20]. This model employed a logit link function and a binomial distribution, directly modeling the counts of events and non-events without ad hoc continuity corrections or variance-stabilizing transformations. Random intercepts were included at the study level to account for between-study heterogeneity. For highly sparse datasets, asymmetric confidence intervals were calculated directly from the exact logit limits to avoid estimation artifacts.

Meta-regression to explore potential sources of heterogeneity was planned; however, in accordance with established methodological guidance, this analysis was to be performed only if at least ten studies were available per outcome [21].

Reporting bias assessment

To objectively assess small-study effects, for analyses, including ≥ 10 primary studies [22], funnel plot asymmetry was evaluated both through visual inspection of the funnel plot and statistically using Egger's test (p ≤ 0.05) [22]. However, given that no outcome in this review included the minimum number of studies required for these formal statistical tests, publication bias was additionally assessed qualitatively, considering the likely direction and structural sources of under-reporting, such as differential access to ophthalmologic evaluation across study settings and geographic regions.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of the evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach, adapted for meta-analyses of proportions, in which comparative designs are not applicable [23]. As this review addressed a question of frequency rather than comparative effectiveness, the randomized controlled trial framework used in conventional GRADE assessments does not apply. Cross-sectional design constitutes the most appropriate study design to address questions of frequency and was, therefore, assigned an initial rating of high certainty, which was subsequently adjusted according to the domains of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, and imprecision. Risk of bias was assessed using the JBI critical appraisal checklist. Inconsistency was evaluated according to the magnitude of between-study heterogeneity (I2). Indirectness was assessed by evaluating the correspondence between the population, exposure, and outcome of the included studies and those specified in the review question. Imprecision was assessed according to the width of the 95% confidence interval, exceeding ± 10 percentage points, together with the 95% prediction interval and overall sample size [23]. Publication bias was not formally assessed, as no outcome included the minimum of 10 studies required for Egger's test. Domains used to rate up certainty (large effect, dose–response gradient) were not applicable to this review and were, therefore, not evaluated.

Results

The electronic search identified 1,407 articles. After screening, 529 duplicates were removed. A total of 878 articles were assessed by title and abstract, resulting in 18 full-text articles evaluated for eligibility. Of these, 4 reports were excluded due to incorrect population and 6 due to incorrect outcome. The final sample comprised 8 articles with a total of 1,394 participants included in the quantitative synthesis [24–31] (Fig. 2). Of the 1,249 patients for whom sex was reported, 769 were female (61.6%), while one study did not report sex distribution [27]. Across the included studies, the reported mean or median patient age ranged from 30 to 40.1 years (Table 1).

The diagnostic method used to confirm dengue infection was NS1 antigen detection and/or IgM serology in 6 studies (75%) [24, 25, 27–29, 31]. The time from dengue symptom onset to ocular manifestation ranged from 4.1 to 8 days across the included studies. Diagnostic methods used to identify ocular manifestations also varied considerably across studies, ranging from fundoscopy alone to protocols incorporating optical coherence tomography, fluorescein angiography, and automated perimetry (Table 1). All eight included studies (100%) performed a baseline ophthalmologic examination in the entire enrolled population, regardless of the presence of visual symptoms; where used, advanced confirmatory testing (OCT, fluorescein angiography, or automated perimetry) was reserved for patients with abnormal findings on baseline examination.

For maculopathy, three studies [27, 28, 31] applied high-sensitivity testing (systematic OCT) to all enrolled patients, reporting frequencies ranging from 5.5% to 9%, while two studies applied standard fundoscopic examination first, reserving high-sensitivity testing for patients with abnormal baseline findings, reporting frequencies of 10% and 3.3%, respectively. For retinal hemorrhage, all five contributing studies relied on standard examination techniques, with no study employing OCT, FFA, or other high-sensitivity imaging for this outcome. For optic neuropathy, two studies [26, 28] employed automated perimetry (HFA 24–2), reporting frequencies of 8.4% and 4.2%, respectively, whereas the remaining study [30] relied on structural examination alone, reporting a frequency of 0.1% (Table 1).

Figure 3 presents the RoB assessment for the 8 included studies. In the domain-specific assessment, two domains were identified as presenting a notable proportion of high-risk judgments. The sample size justification domain (D3) stood out as the most consistently problematic, with 6 out of 8 studies (75%) [24, 26–28, 30, 31] receiving an unfavorable rating, primarily attributable to the absence of formal sample size calculations. Similarly, the coverage domain (D5) yielded high-risk judgments in 2 studies (25%) [27, 30], largely related to incomplete reporting of recruitment strategies.Fig. 3Risk of bias of included studies. Maculopathy

Five studies encompassing a total of 551 participants. The pooled frequency of maculopathy was 6% (95% CI 3–10%). Low heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I2 = 28.9%, τ2 = < 0.01, p = 0.22). A 95% PI was calculated, ranging from 1% to 13%; given the minimum number of studies (n = 5), this interval should be interpreted with caution (Fig. 4A).Fig. 4Pooled frequency of expanded dengue-associated maculopathy. Pooled frequency of expanded dengue-associated maculopathy. A Overall estimate. B Subgroup analysis by time of onset

A subgroup analysis was conducted by time elapsed. Studies in the ≥ 7 day subgroup (studies: 2; n: 193) yielded a pooled frequency of 10% (95% CI 9.3–10.2%), with no heterogeneity detected (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0, p = 0.99). In contrast, the < 7 day subgroup (studies: 2; n: 240) showed a lower pooled frequency of 5% (95% CI 5–6%), with negligible heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0, p = 0.97). The test for subgroup differences was statistically significant (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4B).

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was subsequently performed to further assess the robustness of the pooled estimate. The omission of the Su et al. study [25] produced the most notable shift, eliminating heterogeneity entirely (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0) and reducing the pooled estimate to 4.8%, identifying it as the primary contributor to between-study variability. Across all remaining iterations, the pooled estimate remained stable (range 4.8–7%).

A GLMM-based sensitivity analysis was conducted to validate this pooled frequency estimate. The GLMM showed a pooled frequency of 6.36% (95% CI 3.91–8.81%), closely matching the primary Freeman–Tukey estimate of 6% (95% CI 3–10%). The model revealed low heterogeneity across studies (random-intercept variance = 0.05; LR test p = 0.29).

As this outcome included only five studies, the predefined threshold for meta-regression was not met, and this analysis was, therefore, not performed.

A sensitivity analysis restricted to studies with low risk of bias in the critical domains (D3, sample size, and D5, coverage) was attempted. However, of the 5 studies included in the maculopathy analysis, only one study [25] met low risk of bias in both domains, while the remaining four studies [26–28, 31] presented high risk in D3, and one of them [27] also in D5. Because only one study met the eligibility criteria for this filter, it was not possible to generate a pooled estimate restricted to low-risk studies; the single remaining study reported a maculopathy frequency of 10% (95% CI 6–16%), a figure consistent with the overall pooled estimate 6% (95% CI 3–10%) but not formally comparable, as it constitutes a single-study estimate.

Retinal hemorrhage

Five studies encompassing a total of 489 participants. The pooled frequency of retinal hemorrhage was 8% (95% CI 3–14%). Moderate heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I2 = 55.9%, τ2 = < 0.01, p = 0.05). A 95% PI was calculated, ranging from 1% to 21%; as this estimate is based on the minimum threshold of five studies, it should be interpreted with caution (Fig. 5A).Fig. 5Pooled frequency of dengue-associated retinal hemorrhage. A subgroup analysis was conducted, studies in the < 7 day subgroup (studies: 3; n: 338) yielded a pooled frequency of 9% (95% CI 0–26%), with substantial heterogeneity detected (I2 = 77.1%, τ2 = < 0.01, p = 0.01). In contrast, the ≥ 7 day stratum corresponds to a single-study estimate (not pooled), which reported a frequency of 6% (95% CI 1–20%). The test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (p = 0.87) (Fig. 5B).

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to try to explain the heterogeneity. The omission of Kapoor et al. study [24] produced the most notable shift, substantially reducing heterogeneity from 55.9% to 11.4% (τ2 = < 0.001), identifying it as the primary contributor to between-study variability. Across all remaining iterations, the pooled estimate remained stable (range: 6.3–9.5%).

A GLMM-based sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of this estimate. The GLMM estimated a pooled frequency of 7.86% (95% CI 4.42–11.31%; random-intercept variance = 0.13; LR test p = 0.10), consistent with the primary estimate of 8% (95% CI 3–14%).

As this outcome also included only five studies, the predefined threshold for meta-regression was not met, and this analysis was, therefore, not performed.

A sensitivity analysis restricted to studies with low risk of bias in the critical domains (D3, sample size, and D5, coverage) was attempted. However, of the 5 studies included in the retinal hemorrhage analysis, only one study [29] met low risk of bias in both domains, while the remaining four studies [24, 26–28, 30, 31] presented high risk in D3. Because only one study met the eligibility criteria for this filter, it was not possible to generate a pooled estimate restricted to low-risk studies; the single remaining study reported a retinal hemorrhage frequency of 14% (95% CI 8–22%), a figure higher than the overall pooled estimate 8% (95% CI 3–14%) but not formally comparable, as it constitutes a single-study estimate.

Optic neuropathy

Three studies reported this outcome. The highest frequency was reported in a population with a mean age of 34.3 years, diagnosed by PCR, with 10 of 118 patients affected (8.4%) [26]. The lowest frequency was reported in a population with a mean age of 32 years, diagnosed by IgM serology, with 1 of 600 patients affected (0.1%) [30]. Detailed characteristics of these studies are described in Table 1.

Other manifestation

Regarding ocular manifestations, one study reported uveitis in a cohort of 600 patients, with 6 cases identified, corresponding to a frequency of 1.0% [30]. Ocular vasculitis was reported in two studies: the first, conducted in a cohort of 600 patients, identified 3 affected individuals (0.5%) [30], while the second, carried out in a smaller cohort of 95 patients, reported 8 cases, yielding a notably higher frequency of 8.4% [28]. The considerable variation in the frequency of ocular vasculitis between these two studies may reflect differences in the populations studied, diagnostic criteria applied, or the clinical settings in which patients were recruited.

Assessment of publication bias

The evaluation of publication bias was not conducted, because fewer than ten studies were included in the analyses. According to established methodological recommendations [32], Egger’s regression test should not be applied when the number of studies is below this threshold, as the test has limited statistical power and may yield unreliable or spurious results under such conditions. However, a qualitative assessment of publication bias was performed. The near total absence of studies from Latin America and Africa, regions with high dengue endemicity but limited access to ophthalmologic evaluation, suggests a structurally driven form of under-reporting. These considerations suggest that the qualitative risk of publication bias in this field is high, and that the pooled frequency estimates reported here likely represent an underestimate of the true burden of ocular involvement in dengue.

Certainty of evidence assessment

Two outcomes were considered critical for this review: maculopathy and retinal hemorrhage.

For maculopathy (5 studies, 551 participants), the certainty of evidence started at high and was downgraded by two levels for risk of bias, as 4 of the 5 included studies, accounting for the majority of the information contributing to the pooled estimate, presented high risk of bias in the sample size domain (D3), representing a very serious concern. Inconsistency was not downgraded, as low heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 28.9%). Indirectness was not downgraded. Imprecision was not downgraded, as the 95% confidence interval width (3–10%) did not exceed the predefined 20-percentage-point threshold. Publication bias was not assessed, as fewer than 10 studies were available for this outcome. Overall, the certainty of the evidence for maculopathy was rated as low.

For retinal hemorrhage (5 studies, 489 participants), the certainty of evidence started at high and was downgraded by two levels for risk of bias, for the same reasons described above, and by one additional level for inconsistency, given the moderate heterogeneity observed across studies (I2 = 55.9%). Indirectness was not downgraded. Imprecision was not downgraded, as the 95% confidence interval width (3–14%) did not exceed the predefined threshold. Publication bias was not assessed, as fewer than 10 studies were available for this outcome. Overall, the certainty of the evidence for retinal hemorrhage was rated as very low.

Discussion

The present study identified a maculopathy frequency of 6% and retinal hemorrhage in 8% of the evaluated patients. These figures are notably lower than those reported in a substantial portion of the published literature, a discrepancy that is likely attributable to methodological differences in study design. Some series from endemic regions have reported ocular involvement in up to one quarter of hospitalized dengue patients [33]. However, this study was not eligible for inclusion in the present meta-analysis, as it reported a cumulative frequency of composite fundus findings, rather than the discrete, clinically defined entities of maculopathy, retinal hemorrhage, and optic neuropathy required by our eligibility criteria. This distinction likely accounts, at least in part, for the observed difference in reported frequencies: composite outcomes that aggregate multiple nonspecific fundus findings under a single frequency figure tend to yield higher estimates than the discrete, individually quantified entities reported in the present study.

In addition, studies conducted in dedicated ophthalmology settings may be subject to referral bias, as patients with overt ocular symptoms are more likely to be selected for evaluation [33], whereas the studies included in this review systematically assessed unselected patients admitted for dengue fever. Notably, all eight studies included in the present meta-analysis were hospital-based, enrolling patients admitted for dengue fever; therefore, the discrepancy in reported frequencies is unlikely to be explained by differences between hospital-based and population-based sampling.

To date, two major efforts to synthesize the available evidence on dengue-associated ophthalmic involvement have been published. The first, a systematic review of 29 articles encompassing 686 patients, identified blurring of vision and scotoma as the most frequent symptoms [8]. The second, a meta-analysis of 17 studies encompassing 1276 patients, reported an overall frequency of ocular manifestations of 23.99%, with blurred vision and scotomas noted in 55.54% and 36.15% of cases, respectively [34]. Notably, neither of these analyses were conducted within the framework of the WHO-defined expanded dengue syndrome, nor did they systematically evaluate ophthalmic involvement as an organ-specific complication within that clinical classification [8, 34]. Moreover, both works aggregate data from cohorts in which the primary outcomes were visual symptoms and imaging findings rather than distinct, clinically confirmed pathological entities.

In contrast, the present study specifically reports discrete conditions, such as maculopathy, retinal hemorrhage, and optic neuropathy, based on clinical confirmation. As detailed in the Methods, these entities were operationalized from the WHO's broader ocular categories using more specific definitions from subsequent ophthalmological literature [5], and the pooled estimates reported here should be interpreted within this expanded, literature-based framework rather than the WHO's original, narrower categorization. It must be acknowledged that this grouping, particularly for maculopathy, aggregates clinically and pathophysiologically heterogeneous entities under a single term, which may contribute to the heterogeneity observed in this outcome. However, this reflects how the included primary studies themselves reported their findings, collectively classifying these presentations as "maculopathy" rather than as separate, individually quantifiable entities.

The temporal relationship between the onset of dengue and the development of ophthalmic complications has been consistently described in the literature and has important pathophysiological implications. Dengue-associated maculopathy typically appears approximately 7 days after the onset of systemic symptoms [6], a pattern that is not random. This delay raises the possibility of an immune-mediated pathophysiology rather than a direct viral cytopathic effect, as a 1-week interval corresponds to the onset of antibody production, immune complex deposition, and autoantibody generation [2, 7]. This immune-mediated pathophysiology has been more extensively characterized elsewhere, encompassing antibody-dependent enhancement, whereby pre-existing non-neutralizing antibodies from a prior dengue infection facilitate viral entry into Fc-receptor bearing cells and amplify viral replication; complement activation, which further promotes vascular injury; immune complex deposition within ocular tissues; and cytokine-mediated vascular leakage, collectively contributing to blood–ocular barrier breakdown alongside a potential contribution from direct viral cytopathic injury [7].

It is important to clarify that it is the duration of the disease itself, rather than the mere capacity to tolerate it, that may suggest an immune-mediated origin; confirming true immunological tolerance would require the direct measurement of immune mediators in the intraocular fluid, which was beyond the scope of the included studies. Among patients in whom maculopathy was identified seven or more days after symptom onset, the frequency reached 10%, compared to 5% in those in whom it was detected within the first 7 days. This difference should be interpreted within the context of these previously established immune-mediated mechanisms, rather than being presented as a largely novel observation. On the other hand, retinal hemorrhage was identified in 9% of patients during the first 7 days after the onset of symptoms, a percentage that decreased to 6% after this period.

It should also be acknowledged that the temporal subgroup comparisons (< 7 days vs. ≥ 7 days) for both maculopathy and retinal hemorrhage were based on very few studies per arm (two per arm for maculopathy; three vs. a single-study estimate for retinal hemorrhage). With such a small number of studies per subgroup, these comparisons lack the degrees of freedom necessary to isolate the effect of time of onset from other study-level differences, such as country, diagnostic protocol sensitivity, dengue serotype, or disease severity spectrum. Both temporal subgroup findings should, therefore, be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of a true underlying temporal effect.

Across the two outcomes for which a pooled estimate was retained, the absolute difference between the Freeman–Tukey and GLMM estimates did not exceed 0.4 percentage points for maculopathy and retinal hemorrhage [19, 20]. Part of this concordance likely reflects the moderate size of the pooled proportions and the absence of extremely small or extremely large individual studies, conditions under which the two approaches would be expected to diverge more visibly. However, the sample sizes ranged from 33(31) to 600(30) participants and the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation is known to introduce bias under this degree of imbalance; that the transformation-free GLMM produced similar values is reassuring, though it does not fully exclude some residual distortion. The numerical agreement between the two statistical methods also speaks only to computational consistency, not to the broader validity of the pooled estimates, which remains constrained by the heterogeneous sensitivity of diagnostic protocols across studies, possible spectrum bias, the under-representation of Latin American and African cohorts, and the limited number of studies available per outcome.

The leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted separately for maculopathy and hemorrhagic retinopathy, and revealed a consistent pattern across both analyses: in each case, the exclusion of one study led to a substantial reduction in heterogeneity, suggesting that this pattern was not driven by a single source of data but rather reflects a study-specific phenomenon in each pooled estimate. This behavior may be attributable to the intrinsic characteristics of each respective study, particularly their low risk of bias and narrower confidence intervals, which assigned them greater statistical weight within the pooled analysis, thereby exerting a stronger influence on overall heterogeneity.

Certain limitations must be acknowledged. Notably, the reported frequency of each ocular manifestation appeared to vary according to the sensitivity of the diagnostic method employed [7]. For maculopathy, the two studies relying on selective, abnormality-triggered OCT reported the widest range of frequencies (10% and 3.3%), while the three studies applying OCT systematically to all patients reported a narrower range (5.5–9%); this pattern suggests that diagnostic sensitivity alone does not fully account for the observed variability in maculopathy frequency, and that other sources of heterogeneity, such as study-level differences in population or diagnostic threshold, are also likely to be at play. In contrast, a clearer pattern was observed for optic neuropathy: the two studies employing dedicated automated perimetry reported considerably higher frequencies (4.2% and 8.4%) than the single study relying solely on structural examination (0.1%), reinforcing the likelihood that functional testing detects subclinical optic nerve involvement that structural assessment alone fails to capture. For retinal hemorrhage, all contributing studies relied on standard visualization techniques, consistent with this manifestation being readily identifiable without advanced multimodal imaging, and diagnostic sensitivity is, therefore, unlikely to explain the heterogeneity observed for this outcome [7]. Together, these patterns identify diagnostic protocol sensitivity as an important, unmeasured source of heterogeneity for maculopathy and optic neuropathy specifically, which could not be formally tested through subgroup meta-analysis given the small number of studies per stratum.

These findings suggest that less sensitive diagnostic protocols may substantially underestimate the true burden of ocular involvement in dengue, particularly for outcomes such as optic neuropathy that require functional rather than purely structural assessment. Together with the predominance of hospital-based cross-sectional designs and the lack of formal sample size justifications in most included studies, which introduce potential selection and precision biases, these factors likely result in pooled estimates that reflect the frequency of clinically detected ocular complications rather than the true frequency of ocular involvement among patients with dengue.

It is important to note that, of the 8 studies included in this review, only two [25, 29] presented low risk of bias simultaneously in both domains identified as most problematic (D3, sample size, and D5, coverage). This finding underscores the cross-cutting methodological fragility of the available evidence on dengue-associated ocular manifestations: the vast majority of studies lacked a formal sample size calculation and/or an adequate description of recruitment strategies, which limits the ability to contrast the robustness of the pooled estimates against a subset of methodologically high-quality studies.

The small number of eligible studies (n = 8) and the moderate-to-substantial heterogeneity observed in some outcomes limit the precision and generalizability of the pooled estimates. In addition, the scarcity of studies from Latin America and Africa restricts the global applicability of these results. This is particularly concerning given that these regions combine high dengue endemicity with limited access to ophthalmologic consultation and multimodal imaging, conditions under which many patients with dengue are never referred for a retinal examination, regardless of systemic severity. This structural gap likely reflects a form of publication bias compounding detection bias: centers with limited ocular evaluation capacity, disproportionately concentrated in under-resourced, high-endemicity regions, are less likely to generate publishable data on this outcome. Consequently, this bias most likely results in an underestimation, rather than an overestimation, of the true frequency of ocular involvement in dengue, although its exact magnitude cannot be quantified with the available evidence. A related limitation is that, even where ophthalmologic services are available, the severity of the systemic disease may have prevented a complete ocular examination in some patients, further contributing to the underestimation of ocular manifestations observed in this review. Future prospective, population-based studies employing standardized diagnostic protocols and explicit temporal characterization of ocular manifestations are needed to refine these estimates and inform clinical practice guidelines.

Conclusions

This systematic review demonstrates that maculopathy, retinal hemorrhage, and optic neuropathy are clinically relevant complications of expanded dengue syndrome. Pooled frequency estimates were 6% for maculopathy and 8% for retinal hemorrhage, while optic neuropathy showed highly variable individual frequencies (0.1–8.4%) that precluded a reliable pooled estimate. The temporal subgroup analysis is consistent with, but does not confirm, an immune-mediated pathophysiology, with a higher frequency of maculopathy observed beyond 7 days from symptom onset. These findings highlight the importance of ophthalmological evaluation into the clinical management of dengue patients, particularly during the recovery phase. Given the limited number of available studies and the heterogeneity observed across outcomes, further high-quality, prospective research is warranted to consolidate the evidence and guide the development of clinical practice recommendations.
