Biological and In Silico Evaluation of Novel Methoxyphenol–1,2,3‐Triazole Hybrids as Multifunctional Anti‐Alzheimer's and Anticancer Agents
Authors: Sultan Onur, Mustafa Çeşme, Ferhan Tümer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe development of multifunctional small molecules capable of simultaneously modulating multiple pathological pathways represents a rational strategy in medicinal chemistry for complex disorders such as Alzheimer's disease (AD) and cancer. Herein, a novel series of methoxyphenol‐based 1,2,3‐triazole hybrids (5a–h) was designed and synthesized via Cu(I)‐catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC). All derivatives were fully characterized by FT‐IR, 1H/13C NMR spectroscopy, and elemental analysis. Biological evaluation encompassed in vitro antioxidant screening (DPPH•, ABTS•+, and CUPRAC), acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BuChE) inhibition (modified Ellman method), and antiproliferative activity against the PC3 prostate cancer cell line using HDF‐1 healthy fibroblasts as a selectivity control. Compound 5c proved the most potent AChE inhibitor (IC50 = 1.44 ± 0.33 μM; SI = 16.77), while 5h exhibited preferential BuChE inhibition (IC50 = 11.40 ± 1.25 μM). Enzyme kinetic studies confirmed a mixed‐type inhibition mechanism for both lead compounds, suggesting dual‐site engagement within the cholinesterase gorge. In the antiproliferative assay, 5e and 5c surpassed the reference drug methotrexate (IC50 = 4.42 ± 0.15 μM) with IC50 values of 3.57 ± 0.11 μM and 3.89 ± 0.20 μM, respectively, alongside favorable selectivity over healthy fibroblasts. In silico ADMET profiling confirmed drug‐likeness for all derivatives, and DFT calculations at the B3LYP/6‐311++G(d,p) level provided electronic rationale for observed reactivity trends. Molecular docking against human AChE (PDB: 4EY7) and BuChE (PDB: 4BDS) rationalized key stabilizing interactions including π–π stacking with Trp86/Trp286 and hydrogen bonding with active‐site residues. Collectively, these findings identify the methoxyphenol–triazole scaffold as a promising lead platform warranting further optimization for multitarget therapeutic applications in neurodegenerative and oncological disorders.

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) stands as the most formidable neurodegenerative challenge of the 21st century, characterized by a relentless progression that continues to outpace current clinical interventions (Lane et al. 2018; Deture and Dickson 2019). To date, the therapeutic landscape remains dominated by acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BuChE) inhibitors. While these agents provide modest symptomatic relief by bolstering cholinergic neurotransmission, they fail to arrest the underlying neurodegenerative cascade (Stanciu et al. 2020; Vecchio et al. 2021). Beyond hydrolytic function, both AChE and BuChE participate in non‐catalytic roles including amyloid‐β (Aβ) peptide aggregation at the peripheral anionic site (PAS), positioning dual‐site inhibitors as particularly attractive candidates for disease‐modifying intervention (Youn et al. 2026). This clinical insufficiency has catalyzed a paradigm shift toward “multifunctional ligands” molecules designed to intercept the complex, intertwined pathways of AD pathology rather than focusing on a single enzymatic target.

The etiology of AD is increasingly viewed through the lens of a “multifactorial storm” where cholinergic deficits converge with oxidative stress, mitochondrial decay, neuroinflammation, and metal ion dyshomeostasis (Allan Butterfield and Boyd‐Kimball 2018; Zimmermann 2013). Oxidative stress, in particular, acts as a pivotal executioner of neuronal death, inflicting irreversible damage via lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA fragmentation (Vecchio et al. 2021). Neuroinflammatory signaling, mediated through microglial activation and pro‐inflammatory cytokine release, further amplifies both cholinergic deficit and Aβ burden, reinforcing the rationale for scaffolds that combine antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and cholinesterase‐inhibitory properties within a single molecular framework (Luo et al. 2019). Consequently, the development of multifunctional ligands capable of simultaneously modulating cholinergic neurotransmission, attenuating oxidative stress, and dampening neuroinflammatory cascades has emerged as a promising frontier in anti‐Alzheimer's drug discovery (Marucci et al. 2021; Hu, Li, Li, Yang, et al. 2023).

In this context, phenolic compounds have long been recognized for their intrinsic capacity to quench free radicals through hydrogen atom transfer and radical stabilization. Methoxyphenol derivatives, including vanillin‐related scaffolds, have attracted considerable interest due to their enhanced redox stability and more favorable pharmacokinetic profiles compared to simpler phenols (Gulcin 2020). The methoxy substituent, positioned ortho or para to the phenolic hydroxyl, stabilizes the resulting phenoxyl radical through resonance delocalization while simultaneously introducing additional oxygen‐based lone pairs that expand the hydrogen‐bonding surface area properties that may optimize multi‐point anchoring within both the catalytic active site (CAS) and the peripheral anionic site (PAS) of cholinesterases (Pourshojaei et al. 2019; Ashram et al. 2026; Hu, Li, Li, Su, et al. 2023). Recent trajectory in medicinal chemistry further suggests that methoxy‐substituted phenols not only bolster antioxidant capacity but also significantly improve membrane permeability and target engagement (Wu et al. 2025).

Parallel to the challenges in neurology, cancer persists as a leading cause of global mortality, demanding the continuous development of more selective chemotherapeutic agents (Riaz et al. 2019). The principal limitation of current oncology therapies lies in their poor selectivity, which results in systemic toxicity and the rapid emergence of drug resistance (Farghaly et al. 2020; Sochacka‐Ćwikła et al. 2022; Muškinja et al. 2019). Intriguingly, AD and cancer share overlapping molecular signatures, particularly those driven by reactive oxygen species (ROS). While excessive ROS drives neuronal apoptosis in neurodegeneration, it promotes genomic instability and pro‐survival signaling in tumorigenesis. This mechanistic convergence between neurodegeneration and oncogenesis provides a rational basis for designing single scaffolds that simultaneously confer neuroprotective and cytostatic effects an approach supported by growing epidemiological and molecular evidence linking dysregulated ROS homeostasis across both disease contexts (Ziech et al. 2010; Dakubo 2010; Ślusarczyk et al. 2009; Karali et al. 2010; Benchikh et al. 2022; Patil et al. 2013). Consequently, antioxidant‐based therapeutic strategies offer a pleiotropic means of disease modulation, inspiring the development of multifunctional compounds capable of both scavenging free radicals and influencing disease‐specific enzyme targets (Bendary et al. 2013; Perrone et al. 2018; Gill et al. 2015). Given their redox‐active and π‐conjugated nature, triazole‐containing phenolic hybrids may counteract ROS‐mediated damage while concurrently influencing enzymatic pathways involved in both AD and cancer (Şahin, Çeşme, Özgeriş, and Tümer 2023; Şahin et al. 2021).

Among heterocyclic pharmacophores, the 1,2,3‐triazole ring has emerged as a versatile scaffold due to its metabolic stability, hydrogen‐bonding capability, and its role as a bioisostere for amide or ester functionalities (Vitaku et al. 2014; Dheer et al. 2017; Tan et al. 2021). Triazole‐based derivatives display a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities ranging from anticancer and antiviral to neuroprotective effects owing to their structural adaptability (Döhler et al. 2017; Bozorov et al. 2019; Venkatesham et al. 2019; Dehestani et al. 2018; Şahin, Çeşme, Yüce, and Tümer 2023; Khan et al. 2023a). Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of triazole hybrids as dual AChE/BuChE inhibitors with concurrent antioxidant effects, often guided by computational ADMET and molecular docking and dynamics simulations (Çeşme et al. 2024, 2025; Onur, Çeşme, and Tümer 2025; Onur et al. 2026; Onur, Çeşme, Köse, and Tümer 2025; Radwan et al. 2022; Tabti et al. 2024).

The strategic selection of the methoxyphenol moiety in this study transcends mere structural novelty; it is predicated on its superior redox stability and versatile hydrogen‐bonding (H‐bonding) capacity. Unlike simpler phenolic scaffolds, the methoxy‐substituted phenol ring provides an enhanced electronic environment that facilitates the stabilization of phenoxy radicals, thereby bolstering the antioxidant potential of the hybrids (Platzer et al. 2022; Kajiyama and Ohkatsu 2002; Almaz 2023). Furthermore, the presence of the methoxy group introduces additional oxygen‐based lone pairs, which significantly increase the H‐bonding surface area, potentially optimizing the multi‐point anchoring of the molecules within the catalytic active site (CAS) and peripheral anionic site (PAS) of cholinesterases (Youn et al. 2026; Khan et al. 2023b; De Boer et al. 2021). By integrating this redox‐active and pharmacologically robust scaffold with the 1,2,3‐triazole linker, we aimed to design a multifunctional platform capable of simultaneously mitigating oxidative stress, inhibiting cholinergic degradation, and exerting targeted cytotoxic effects against malignant PC3 cells. The present methoxyphenol–triazole series is structurally distinct from our previously reported p‐cresol–, hydroquinone–, and nitrophenol–triazole analogues (Onur, Çeşme, and Tümer 2025; Onur et al. 2026; Onur, Çeşme, Köse, and Tümer 2025) in that 4‐methoxyphenol confers superior phenoxyl radical stabilization, a broader hydrogen‐bonding surface, and a more favorable lipophilicity balance (predicted XLOGP 2.50–3.95) that simultaneously supports blood–brain barrier permeation and cancer cell membrane penetration.

This study aims to establish significant structure–activity relationships (SAR) by integrating the synthesis of novel derivatives with in vitro antioxidant, cholinesterase inhibitor, and anticancer assays. Supported by in silico ADMET and molecular docking analyses, this approach is expected to contribute to the rational development of multifunctional lead structures targeting the complex pathways shared by neurodegenerative and oncological disorders.

Experimental

Instrumentation and Materials

All chemical reagents and solvents were obtained from commercial suppliers, specifically Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany), at analytical grade and utilized as received without further purification. The progress of the reactions was routinely monitored by thin‐layer chromatography (TLC). Melting points were determined using a BUCHI 530 capillary apparatus and are reported uncorrected. Infrared (IR) spectra were recorded in the 4000–450 cm−1 range on a PerkinElmer Spectrum 400 FT‐IR spectrometer equipped with an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory. Nuclear magnetic resonance (1H and 13C NMR) spectra were acquired on a Bruker 400 MHz spectrometer. All chemical shifts (δ) are reported in parts per million (ppm) relative to tetramethyl silane (TMS) as the internal standard, with CDCl3 used as the solvent. Coupling constants (J) are expressed in Hertz (Hz). Elemental analyses for carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen were performed on a Costech ECS 4010 CHNS analyzer, and the results were found to be within acceptable limits of the calculated values. The purity of the synthesized triazole hybrids (5a–h) was confirmed to be > 95% based on elemental analysis and 1H‐NMR integration. Elemental analyses for C, H, and N were within ±0.4% of the theoretical values, providing robust evidence for the molecular composition and high degree of analytical purity required for biological screening.

General Procedure for the Synthesis of Triazole Derivatives (5a–h)

The precursor arylazides (2a–h) and 1‐(ethynyloxy)‐4‐methoxybenzene (4) were synthesized according to previously established literature protocols (Dehestani et al. 2018; Şahin, Çeşme, Yüce, and Tümer 2023; Khan et al. 2023a; Çeşme et al. 2024; Onur, Çeşme, and Tümer 2025; Onur et al. 2026), as illustrated in Scheme 1 (Step 1—Diazotisation/azide formation: HCl/NaNO2, 0°C, 30 min; NaN3/H2O, 0°C to RT, 1 h. Step 2—O‐propargylation: K2CO3, MeCN, reflux (80°C), 6 h. Step 3—CuAAC: CuSO4·5H2O/sodium ascorbate, THF/H2O (3:1 v/v), RT, 12 h). For the copper‐catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC), a solution of 1‐(ethynyloxy)‐4‐methoxybenzene (4) (3 mmol) in a THF/water solvent system (3:1 v/v, 40 mL) was stirred in a round‐bottom flask. Sodium ascorbate (1.2 mmol) and copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O, 0.6 mmol) were added sequentially, and the resulting mixture was stirred for 10 min at ambient temperature to ensure the in situ reduction of the catalytic species. Subsequently, the corresponding azide derivative (2a–h, 3 mmol) was introduced into the reaction medium. The mixture was stirred at room temperature (25°C) for 12 h, with the reaction progress monitored by thin‐layer chromatography (TLC).

Illustration depicting the synthetic routes used for the preparation of compounds 5a–h.

Following the completion of the reaction, the organic solvent (THF) was evaporated under reduced pressure. The remaining aqueous phase was extracted with ethyl acetate (2 × 50 mL), and the combined organic extracts were dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate (Na2SO4). After filtration and concentration in vacuo, the resulting crude residue was washed with hexane to remove nonpolar impurities. Final purification was achieved via silica gel column chromatography (Merck, 60 Å pore size, 230–400 mesh ASTM) using an ethyl acetate/hexane gradient (1:4 v/v) as the eluent to yield the analytically pure triazole hybrids (5a–h).

Biological Assay Procedures

The biological evaluation included antioxidant screening (DPPH•, ABTS•+, and CUPRAC), cholinesterase inhibition (AChE and BuChE; modified Ellman method), and antiproliferative activity against PC3 (prostate cancer) and HDF‐1 (healthy fibroblast) cell lines. Antioxidant assays were performed at concentrations of 6.25–200 μg/mL with absorbance recorded at 517 nm (DPPH), 734 nm (ABTS), and 450 nm (CUPRAC). Cholinesterase inhibition was assessed using 10−4–10−7 M inhibitor concentrations at 412 nm, and IC50 values were derived from linear regression. For anticancer screening, compounds were tested at 3.12–100 μM (final DMSO < 0.1%), and cell viability was quantified at 450 nm using a CCK‐8 based assay after 48 h incubation. All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and results are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was determined by one‐way ANOVA followed by Duncan's post hoc test (p < 0.05). Detailed protocols are provided in the Appendix S1.

ADMET Profiling, DFT Calculations, and Molecular Docking Simulations

ADMET Profiling: In silico pharmacokinetic and toxicological properties were predicted using SwissADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination) and pkCSM (toxicity) web‐based platforms. SMILES notations of all compounds were generated using ChemDraw 23.0 and submitted to both platforms for the calculation of molecular ADMET descriptors. DFT Calculations: Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Schrödinger Maestro molecular modeling suite (Schrödinger LLC, New York, NY, 2021). Geometry optimizations and electronic structure calculations were carried out at the B3LYP/6‐311++G(d,p) level of theory. Molecular Docking: Molecular docking simulations were performed using PyRx/AutoDock Vina against human AChE (PDB: 4EY7) and human BuChE (PDB: 4BDS). Protein structures were prepared and visualized using Discovery Studio 2025 and Schrödinger Maestro. The docking grid was centered at the co‐crystallized ligand binding site with dimensions of 25 × 25 × 25 Å3 (spacing: 0.375 Å). Docking reliability was validated by redocking the co‐crystallized ligands (donepezil and tacrine) with RMSD values of 0.9521 Å and 0.4334 Å, respectively, both below the accepted threshold of 2.0 Å. Full computational parameters, grid coordinates, and validation figures are provided in the Appendix S1.

Results and Discussion

Chemistry

The target hybrid molecules (5a–h), integrating both 1,2,3‐triazole and methoxyphenol pharmacophores, were successfully synthesized via the Copper(I)‐catalyzed Azide–Alkyne Cycloaddition (CuAAC) click reaction, as outlined in Scheme 1. The synthetic strategy commenced with the preparation of a diverse series of aryl azides (2a–h) according to established literature procedures. In parallel, the alkyne building block, 1‐methoxy‐4‐(prop‐2‐yn‐1‐yloxy)benzene (4), was synthesized through the regioselective O‐alkylation of 4‐methoxyphenol (3) with propargyl bromide. The final convergent step, involving the 1,3‐dipolar cycloaddition of alkyne 4 with the respective aryl azides, afforded the desired triazole‐linked hybrids (5a–h) in high chemical yields (Şahin, Çeşme, Yüce, and Tümer 2023; Çeşme et al. 2024; Onur, Çeşme, and Tümer 2025; Onur et al. 2026; Çot et al. 2023, 2022). The structural integrity of all synthesized derivatives was rigorously elucidated using a combination of FT‐IR, 1H NMR, and 13C NMR spectroscopy, further supported by elemental analysis. The resulting data were in full agreement with the proposed molecular frameworks, confirming the regioselective formation of the 1,4‐disubstituted 1,2,3‐triazole isomers.

The success of the copper‐catalyzed “click” cycloaddition was primarily monitored through FT‐IR spectroscopy. The most definitive evidence for the formation of the 1,2,3‐triazole framework was the complete disappearance of the characteristic alkyne stretching vibration at 2120 cm−1 originally present in the precursor alkyne (4). This was accompanied by the absence of the azide stretching band (typically around 2100 cm−1) from the starting aryl azides. Furthermore, the IR spectra of the target hybrids (5a–h) displayed prominent absorption bands consistent with their specific functional groups, such as ester carbonyls and nitro groups, where applicable (see Appendix S1, Figures S1–S9). These spectroscopic signatures, along with the emergence of triazole‐specific vibrations, unequivocally confirmed the successful synthesis and structural integrity of the hybrid compounds.

The 1H and 13C NMR spectra for all synthesized derivatives were found to be in full agreement with the proposed molecular frameworks. Compound 5a was selected as a representative model for a detailed discussion of the spectral characteristics (comprehensive spectra for all derivatives are provided in Figures S10–S25, Appendix S1).

In the 1H NMR spectrum of 5a (Figure S10), the most definitive evidence for the formation of the 1,2,3‐triazole ring was the emergence of a diagnostic singlet at 8.05 ppm, which is uniquely attributed to the C5‐proton of the triazole ring. The aromatic region exhibited a well‐defined pattern consisting of two sets of doublets, characteristic of para‐substituted benzene systems. Specifically, the protons of the tolyl moiety were observed as doublets at 7.62 (d, J = 8.4 Hz, 2H) and 7.33 (d, J = 8.3 Hz, 2H) ppm. Correspondingly, the protons of the methoxyphenol ring resonated at 6.98 (d, J = 9.0 Hz, 2H) and 6.87 (d, J = 9.0 Hz, 2H) ppm. In the aliphatic region, the methylene bridge between the oxygen atom and the triazole ring (–OCH2–) appeared as a sharp singlet at 5.25 ppm. Furthermore, the methoxy (–OCH3) and methyl (–CH3) protons were identified as distinct singlets at 3.79 ppm and 2.44 ppm, respectively.

The 13C NMR spectrum of 5a (Figure S11) further validated the assigned structure, displaying the expected aromatic carbon resonances within the 154.28–114.75 ppm range. The quaternary carbon signals associated with the triazole ring and the aromatic systems were consistent with the 1,4‐disubstituted triazole architecture. The signals corresponding to the etheric methylene, methoxy, and methyl carbons were observed at 62.74, 55.72, and 21.11 ppm, respectively. Collectively, these NMR spectral patterns provided unequivocal corroboration for the successful synthesis and high purity of the target hybrids.

In general, the methoxyphenol–triazole hybrids exhibited moderate antioxidant activity, which can primarily be attributed to the phenolic moiety capable of stabilizing free radicals through hydrogen atom donation and resonance stabilization of the resulting phenoxyl radical. Substituent effects on the aromatic ring significantly influenced the radical scavenging efficiency of the synthesized derivatives. In particular, compounds bearing electron‐donating groups tended to display improved antioxidant performance, consistent with previous reports demonstrating that phenol‐ and cresol‐based triazole derivatives benefit from enhanced hydrogen‐donating ability and radical stabilization (Gulcin 2020). This trend can be rationalized by the ability of electron‐donating substituents to increase electron density at the phenolic oxygen, thereby lowering the O–H bond dissociation enthalpy and facilitating hydrogen atom transfer to the radical species. Conversely, electron‐withdrawing substituents such as nitro (5f), ester (5g), and ketone (5h) groups exert an inductive withdrawal effect that reduces phenolic electron density, diminishes the stability of the phenoxyl radical intermediate, and consequently attenuates radical scavenging capacity.

In the DPPH assay, compound 5b displayed the strongest radical scavenging activity among the synthesized hybrids, with an IC50 value of 264.12 μg/mL, surpassing the activity of β‐carotene (IC50 = 368.47 μg/mL). The relatively strong activity of 5b can be attributed to the dual methoxy substitution pattern, which provides two electron‐donating oxygen lone pairs that collectively enhance phenoxyl radical delocalization. In contrast, the halogenated derivatives 5d (IC50 = 842.41 μg/mL) and 5e (IC50 = 867.74 μg/mL) showed markedly weaker DPPH scavenging, consistent with the moderate electron‐withdrawing character of fluorine and chlorine substituents reducing phenolic reactivity. The nitro derivative 5f exhibited the weakest activity in the series (IC50 = 912.03 μg/mL), reflecting the strong electron‐withdrawing nature of the nitro group. Nevertheless, all compounds remained less potent than the standard antioxidant ascorbic acid (IC50 = 65.08 μg/mL), indicating that the DPPH scavenging ability of the synthesized hybrids is moderate and positions these compounds as antioxidant‐complementary rather than primarily antioxidant scaffolds.

A different trend was observed in the ABTS assay, where several derivatives exhibited relatively stronger radical scavenging activity. In particular, compounds 5b (IC50 = 7.11 μg/mL), 5d (IC50 = 7.15 μg/mL), and 5c (IC50 = 7.16 μg/mL) showed scavenging capacities comparable to the reference antioxidant β‐carotene (IC50 = 7.47 μg/mL). The unexpectedly moderate ABTS activity of 5d, despite bearing an electron‐withdrawing fluorine substituent, may be explained by the lipophilic character of this derivative (XLOGP = 2.92), which facilitates interaction with the lipophilic component of the ABTS radical cation system. Similarly, the moderate activity of the electron‐withdrawing derivatives 5f (IC50 = 9.23 μg/mL) and 5g (IC50 = 9.09 μg/mL) in the ABTS assay, compared with their markedly weaker DPPH performance, further underscores the sensitivity of the ABTS system to lipophilicity in addition to electronic effects. These results suggest that the synthesized hybrids interact more efficiently with the ABTS radical cation system, which is known to be sensitive to both hydrophilic and lipophilic antioxidants.

The reducing ability of the compounds was further assessed using the CUPRAC assay at 450 nm. Among the synthesized derivatives, compound 5a exhibited the highest reducing capacity (0.105 ± 0.004), followed by compounds 5e and 5h. The superior reducing power of 5a in the CUPRAC system may be related to the methyl substituent providing modest electron donation without introducing significant steric hindrance, thereby optimising electron transfer to the Cu(II)–neocuproine complex. Notably, compound 5b, despite showing the best DPPH activity, displayed the lowest CUPRAC absorbance (0.060 ± 0.020) among electron‐donating derivatives, suggesting that the additional methoxy group may impose steric constraints that limit direct electron transfer to the Cu(II) complex. The electron‐withdrawing derivatives 5f, 5g, and 5h showed intermediate reducing capacities (0.077–0.087), consistent with their reduced capacity to donate electrons yet retaining partial redox activity through the triazole π‐system.

Taken together, the antioxidant evaluation indicates that substituent‐dependent electronic effects play an important role in determining the radical scavenging and reducing behavior of the synthesized hybrids. Among the tested derivatives, compound 5b showed the most balanced antioxidant performance across the different assays, while compound 5a exhibited the highest reducing power in the CUPRAC system. Across the series, the order of antioxidant potency broadly follows the electron‐donating capacity of the substituent (–OCH3 > –CH3 > –iPr > –F ≈ –Cl > –CO2Et ≈ –COCH3 > –NO2), with lipophilicity providing a secondary modulating effect particularly evident in the ABTS system.

Although none of the synthesized derivatives matched the reference antioxidants in potency, the inherent redox activity of the methoxyphenol–triazole scaffold is expected to contribute synergistically to the observed cholinesterase inhibitory and antiproliferative properties, consistent with the multifunctional lead concept underpinning this study.

The enhanced activity of compound 5c can reasonably be associated with the presence of the branched isopropyl substituent, which likely promotes favorable hydrophobic interactions within the aromatic gorge of AChE. Such lipophilic substituents may enhance van der Waals contacts with aromatic residues located in the catalytic active‐site region, thereby stabilizing the enzyme–inhibitor complex and improving binding affinity. Moving along the activity series, the ester‐bearing derivative 5g (IC50 = 3.18 μM) ranked second, suggesting that the electron‐withdrawing ester carbonyl may participate in additional polar contacts within the gorge despite reducing overall lipophilicity. The methoxy derivative 5b (IC50 = 4.12 μM) benefits from the dual oxygen lone pairs of the methoxy group that may form supplementary hydrogen bonds with active‐site residues. In contrast, the weakest AChE inhibitors in the series were the chloro‐ (5e, IC50 = 12.30 μM) and acetyl‐substituted (5h, IC50 = 14.20 μM) derivatives, whose lower potency may reflect suboptimal steric fit within the narrower CAS region of AChE relative to the bulkier isopropyl or ester substituents.

In contrast, the BuChE inhibition profile revealed a different structure–activity relationship. Compound 5h displayed the strongest inhibitory activity against BuChE, with an IC50 value of 11.40 ± 1.25 μM. The relatively improved BuChE inhibition observed for compound 5h may be related to the presence of the acetyl substituent, which may favor accommodation within the larger and more flexible active‐site gorge of BuChE. Compared with AChE, BuChE possesses a broader catalytic cavity and greater structural plasticity, allowing it to accommodate a wider range of substituent sizes and shapes. Consistent with this structural feature, compound 5h was the only derivative in the series that exhibited higher inhibitory potency toward BuChE than AChE, as reflected by its SI value of 0.80. The chloro derivative 5e ranked second for BuChE inhibition (IC50 = 18.40 μM), suggesting that the moderately lipophilic chlorine substituent provides complementary hydrophobic contacts within the wider acyl‐binding pocket of BuChE. Derivatives bearing strongly electron‐withdrawing groups nitro (5f, IC50 = 22.10 μM) and methyl (5a, IC50 = 45.12 μM) were the least potent BuChE inhibitors, likely because their electronic character reduces the capacity for productive interactions with the BuChE‐specific residues Leu286 and Trp231 that line the acyl‐binding pocket.

Analysis of the selectivity indices further highlighted the influence of substituent characteristics on enzyme specificity. Compound 5c showed the highest selectivity toward AChE, with an SI value of 16.77, indicating approximately 17‐fold greater inhibitory potency against AChE than BuChE; this compares favourably with the reference inhibitor tacrine (SI = 3.16) and approaches, though does not reach, the selectivity of donepezil (SI = 120). In contrast, other derivatives displayed moderate AChE selectivity, with SI values ranging from 2.33 (5f) to 7.86 (5b). The fluorine derivative 5d (SI = 5.16) and the methoxy derivative 5b (SI = 7.86) showed intermediate AChE selectivity, consistent with their moderate lipophilicity profiles (XLOGP 2.79–2.92) that permit productive binding in both enzyme gorges without strong preference for either. The nitro derivative 5f (SI = 2.33) and the ester derivative 5g (SI = 4.90) displayed the lowest AChE selectivity among the non‐5h compounds, suggesting that their polar substituents interact more promiscuously across both enzyme binding sites. These observations collectively suggest that relatively small structural modifications, particularly in substituent size, branching pattern, and electronic character, can significantly influence enzyme selectivity and binding preferences within the cholinesterase active‐site gorge.

To further clarify the inhibition mechanism, enzyme kinetic studies were conducted for the most active compounds, 5c and 5h. Similarly, compound 5h exhibited Kᵢ and Kᵢ′ values of 6.36 μM and 15.79 μM, respectively, against BuChE, with the higher Kᵢ′ value suggesting a stronger interaction with the free enzyme form relative to the enzyme–substrate complex. The presence of both Kᵢ and Kᵢ′ values confirms that these inhibitors can interact with more than one enzymatic state. This mixed‐type kinetic profile is consistent with the dual‐site binding mode observed in the molecular docking analysis (Section 3.5.4), where both compounds simultaneously engaged the CAS and PAS/acyl‐pocket regions. These peripheral regions have been implicated in amyloid‐β aggregation processes associated with Alzheimer's disease pathology. Therefore, inhibitors capable of interacting with multiple regions of the enzyme represent promising candidates in the development of multifunctional lead structures targeting both cholinergic deficit and amyloid deposition in AD.

Overall, compound 5c emerged as the most promising selective AChE inhibitor within the series, combining high inhibitory potency with significant enzyme selectivity. In contrast, compound 5h demonstrated a preferential inhibitory profile toward BuChE, emphasizing the impact of substituent‐dependent structural features on enzyme specificity. Taken together, the cholinesterase inhibition data establish a clear SAR framework: bulky lipophilic substituents (isopropyl, ester) favour AChE selectivity through enhanced hydrophobic complementarity within the narrow aromatic gorge, whereas smaller polar substituents (acetyl, chloro) are better accommodated by the wider and more flexible BuChE binding pocket. These findings provide valuable insights into the structure–activity relationships of triazole–phenol‐based cholinesterase inhibitors and may guide the rational design of more potent and selective lead structures for the treatment of neurodegenerative disorders.

Based on the pro‐oxidant behaviour of halogenated phenolic scaffolds and the elevated ROS baseline of PC3 cells, ROS‐mediated mitochondrial apoptosis represents the most plausible cytotoxic pathway (Li et al. 2024; Ferreira‐Silva 2025), although confirmation through flow cytometry and caspase activation assays constitutes a necessary next step. A similarly notable profile was observed for the isopropyl‐substituted derivative 5c (IC50 = 3.89 ± 0.20 μM), which demonstrated the highest selectivity index in the series (SI = 6.22), comparable to that of MTX (SI = 5.03), indicating pronounced preferential toxicity toward malignant cells over healthy fibroblasts. In contrast, derivatives bearing strongly electron‐withdrawing substituents exhibited markedly reduced cytotoxic potency. The nitro derivative 5f (IC50 = 20.55 μM) was the least potent among EWG‐substituted analogues, rationalised by its high TPSA (94.99 Å2) limiting membrane diffusion despite a narrow HOMO–LUMO gap (Egap = 2.93 eV). The carbonyl derivatives 5g (IC50 = 14.67 μM) and 5h (IC50 = 22.50 μM) similarly underperformed due to reduced lipophilicity, while 5a (IC50 = 49.78 μM) showed the weakest activity across the entire series, reflecting insufficient electronic perturbation and low lipophilicity (XLOGP = 3.18). Notably, all synthesized hybrids (5a–h) displayed SI values greater than 1.0, indicating a consistent preference for PC3 cancer cells over healthy HDF‐1 fibroblasts. This universal selectivity across the series suggests that the methoxyphenol–triazole scaffold itself confers an intrinsic cancer‐cell preference, possibly through preferential uptake in the ROS‐primed PC3 microenvironment (Song et al. 2025; Tiwari et al. 2025). Compounds 5c (SI = 6.22) and MTX (SI = 5.03) shared the most favorable safety margins, while the low SI values of 5a (1.26), 5f (1.56), 5g (1.52), and 5h (1.56) indicate narrow therapeutic windows that limit their standalone utility and highlight the need for further structural optimisation.

Overall, 5e and 5c emerge as the most promising preliminary lead candidates within this series. These findings highlight balanced lipophilicity and moderate electron‐withdrawing character as key determinants of anticancer efficacy, and support further mechanistic investigations including apoptosis profiling and evaluation against a broader cancer cell line panel to confirm the therapeutic potential of these scaffolds.

In the context of anti‐Alzheimer's drug discovery, BBB permeability is a fundamental pharmacokinetic prerequisite, as therapeutic agents must reach the CNS to exert cholinesterase inhibitory effects and attenuate neurodegeneration. The majority of the compounds specifically 5a, 5b, 5c, 5d, 5e, 5g, and 5h are positioned within the yellow (yolk) region of the diagram, indicating a high probability of passive BBB permeation and suggesting their preliminary suitability for CNS targeting. This prediction is particularly significant for the lead compounds 5c and 5h, whose potent cholinesterase inhibitory profiles (IC50 = 1.44 μM and 11.40 μM, respectively) would be rendered therapeutically irrelevant without adequate CNS penetration. In contrast, compound 5f, which possesses the highest TPSA value within the series (94.99 Å2), is positioned outside the yolk region and is therefore predicted to exhibit limited BBB permeability, further limiting its utility as an anti‐AD candidate and consistent with its overall weaker biological profile across all assays. Nevertheless, 5f remains within the white region of the BOILED‐Egg model, indicating retained high gastrointestinal absorption capability. It must be emphasised that BOILED‐Egg predictions represent computational estimates of passive permeability only; active efflux by P‐glycoprotein at the BBB, which was not evaluated here, can substantially reduce actual CNS exposure even for compounds predicted as BBB‐permeant. In vivo pharmacokinetic studies in rodent models are therefore essential to confirm CNS penetration for the lead compounds.

Another critical pharmacokinetic parameter evaluated is the interaction with P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp), a key efflux transporter responsible for limiting drug accumulation across biological barriers. According to the predictions summarized in Table 5, none of the investigated triazole–phenol hybrids are recognized as P‐gp substrates, suggesting that these molecules are unlikely to be actively effluxed from cells. This prediction is particularly encouraging in the context of both CNS penetration and anticancer efficacy: the absence of P‐gp substrate recognition implies that these compounds may maintain adequate intracellular concentrations in both brain tissue and cancer cells. However, this prediction should be interpreted cautiously, as computational P‐gp substrate prediction tools carry inherent limitations and experimental confirmation using bidirectional transport assays in P‐gp‐overexpressing cell lines remains necessary.

The potential metabolic interactions were investigated through predictions of cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzyme inhibition (Table 5). While most compounds exhibit predicted inhibitory activity toward several CYP isoforms, particularly CYP1A2, CYP2C19, CYP2C9, and CYP3A4 a notable difference in selectivity was observed. Specifically, compounds 5f and 5g show a more selective metabolic profile by not inhibiting CYP2D6, one of the most clinically significant drug‐metabolizing enzymes, which may reduce the likelihood of certain metabolic drug–drug interactions. However, the broad predicted CYP inhibition profile of the remaining derivatives warrants careful attention: inhibition of multiple CYP isoforms simultaneously raises the potential for clinically significant drug–drug interactions, particularly if these compounds are co‐administered with other medications metabolized by these enzymes. This constitutes a pharmacokinetic liability that must be addressed through experimental CYP inhibition assays (e.g., fluorometric or LC–MS/MS‐based microsomal assays) before any advancement toward in vivo studies.

Early identification of potential toxicological liabilities is a fundamental prerequisite in the drug discovery pipeline. The toxicological profiles of the synthesized hybrids (5a–5h) were comprehensively evaluated using the pkCSM predictive platform (Pires et al. 2015) (Table 6). Encouragingly, the lead candidates 5c and 5h are predicted to be non‐mutagenic in the Ames test, indicating a low risk of direct genotoxicity, which is particularly important given their intended application in chronic neurodegenerative disease management. Furthermore, none of the derivatives were predicted to inhibit the hERG I potassium channel, suggesting a favorable primary cardiac safety margin. However, it should be noted that compounds 5b, 5c, 5g, and 5h exhibit predicted hERG II inhibitory activity, a finding that necessitates experimental validation through in vitro patch‐clamp assays before any progression toward in vivo studies. The predicted LD50 values in rats range from 2.001 to 2.578 mol/kg, indicating a moderate acute toxicity profile consistent with the concentration ranges employed in the in vitro biological assays. A critical and consistent toxicological observation across the entire series is the in silico prediction of hepatotoxicity for all eight derivatives. This finding suggests that the triazole–phenol scaffold may interact unfavorably with hepatic metabolic pathways most likely through CYP‐mediated bioactivation to reactive intermediates and represents the most significant safety liability identified in this study. This potential risk warrants rigorous experimental investigation using HepG2 cytotoxicity assays or primary human hepatocyte cultures, as well as in vivo liver function panels, before any advancement of these compounds toward preclinical development. Structural modifications aimed at reducing hepatotoxic liability such as introduction of metabolically stable substituents or blocking of putative bioactivation sites should be incorporated into future optimization strategies. Collectively, while the in silico data highlight hepatotoxicity and selective hERG II interactions as liabilities requiring experimental follow‐up, the overall non‐mutagenic nature and lack of hERG I inhibition provide an encouraging preliminary safety foundation for the continued exploration of this scaffold class.

The Egap value serves as a critical indicator of molecular stability and reactivity: a wider gap denotes higher kinetic stability and chemical hardness (resistance to perturbation), whereas a narrower gap signifies increased polarisability and chemical softness (propensity for electron transfer). Within the 5‐series, compound 5f exhibits the narrowest energy gap (2.9341 eV), the highest global softness (σ = 0.3408 eV−1), and the highest electrophilicity index (ω = 5.9693 eV), collectively identifying it as the most reactive member of the series with a strong propensity for electron acceptance consistent with the strongly electron‐withdrawing character of the nitro substituent. Compounds 5h (Egap = 3.6005 eV) and 5g (Egap = 3.7964 eV) also exhibit relatively low energy gaps, suggesting elevated reactivity that may facilitate binding within enzyme active sites, consistent with their moderate cholinesterase inhibitory profiles. In contrast, compound 5b exhibits the widest energy gap (4.7235 eV) and the highest chemical hardness (η = 2.3617 eV), characterizing it as the most electronically stable derivative a property associated with greater resistance to chemical perturbation and potentially more selective molecular recognition. The remaining derivatives 5a, 5c, 5d, and 5e display intermediate Egap values (4.2738–4.5618 eV), representing a balanced stability–reactivity profile that is generally desirable for broad‐spectrum pharmacological applications.

The global reactivity parameters derived from the FMO energies provide a quantitative framework for rationalizing the compounds' potential as enzyme inhibitors. The ionization potential (I) and electron affinity (A) values reflect the molecules capacity to donate and accept electrons, respectively. Compound 5f shows the highest electron affinity (A = 2.718 eV), consistent with its role as a potent electrophile capable of forming strong interactions with electron‐rich active‐site residues; however, as discussed in Section 3.4, this high theoretical reactivity did not translate into superior biological activity, a discrepancy primarily attributable to the unfavorable TPSA of 5f limiting membrane permeability. This observation underscores an important principle in multi‐parameter drug design: electronic reactivity indices derived from DFT calculations provide mechanistic rationale but must always be interpreted in conjunction with physicochemical ADMET parameters to accurately predict biological outcome. These frontier orbital characteristics are further complemented by molecular electrostatic potential mapping, as discussed in the following section.

Molecular Electrostatic Potential (MEP) Analysis

Molecular Electrostatic Potential (MEP) mapping was utilized to visualize the local charge distribution and identify potential sites for non‐covalent interactions on the molecular surface. The MEP maps, quantified by ESPmin and ESPmax values, correlate strongly with the global reactivity parameters derived from FMO analysis. The MEP maps (Figure 5) reveal consistent electrostatic features across the triazole–phenol hybrids. Electron‐rich regions (indicated in red) are predominantly localized around the nitrogen atoms of the 1,2,3‐triazole ring and the oxygen atoms of the phenolic moiety, representing primary sites for hydrogen bond acceptance. The minimum electrostatic potential values (ESPmin), ranging from −36.06 to −44.15 kcal/mol, confirm these areas as highly nucleophilic and primary sites for hydrogen bond acceptance. Notably, compound 5b exhibits the most negative ESPmin (−44.1501 kcal/mol).

The molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) surfaces of compounds.

Conversely, electron‐poor regions (indicated in blue) are concentrated around the hydrogen atoms of the aromatic rings and methylene groups, which serve as potential sites for hydrogen bond donation. In halogenated derivatives, polarized zones contribute to electrostatic heterogeneity, potentially facilitating specific interactions like σ‐hole mediated halogen bonding. These MEP parameters, in synergy with FMO data, provide a comprehensive electronic basis for rationalizing the binding modes observed in docking studies, specifically explaining the stability of key non‐covalent interactions such as hydrogen bonds and π‐stacking within the enzyme active sites.

Molecular Docking Analyses

The inhibition of cholinesterase enzymes remains a cornerstone therapeutic strategy for managing Alzheimer's disease. In the present study, molecular docking simulations were employed as a complementary computational tool to rationalize the binding modes of the synthesized triazole–phenol hybrids (5a–h) within the active sites of human acetylcholinesterase (hAChE, PDB ID: 4EY7) and butyrylcholinesterase (hBChE, PDB ID: 4BDS).

It is important to emphasize that the docking energies reported herein are computational estimates reflecting enthalpic complementarity under static, solvent‐free conditions and do not account for entropic contributions, solvent reorganization, or conformational dynamics. They are therefore interpreted as supportive rationalization of the experimental inhibitory data rather than independent predictions of binding potency. The hAChE active site comprises two functionally distinct sub‐sites separated by a narrow aromatic gorge, approximately 20 Å in depth: the Catalytic Active Site (CAS), harbouring the catalytic triad Ser203, His447, and Glu334 responsible for substrate hydrolysis, and the Peripheral Anionic Site (PAS) at the gorge entrance, including Tyr72, Asp74, Tyr124, Trp286, and Tyr341, which regulates substrate access and has been directly implicated in amyloid‐β peptide aggregation associated with AD pathology (Youn et al. 2026; De Boer et al. 2021). The active binding site architecture of 4EY7 and the docking position of its native ligand donepezil are shown in Figure S26. Similarly, the active binding site of 4BDS and the docking position of the native ligand tacrine are presented in Figure S27. Dual‐site inhibitors capable of simultaneously bridging the CAS and PAS therefore offer the potential to suppress both cholinergic deficit and amyloid deposition, a key mechanistic advantage of the multifunctional lead concept underpinning this study. To ensure the reliability of the docking protocol, validation studies were initially performed by redocking the co‐crystallized ligands into their respective enzyme structures. Redocking of donepezil into 4EY7 yielded an RMSD value of 0.9521 Å, while redocking of tacrine into 4BDS yielded an RMSD value of 0.4334 Å, both below the accepted threshold of 2.0 Å and confirming adequate docking reliability. The validation results for the 4EY7 and 4BDS docking protocols are provided in Figures S28 and S29, respectively. The comparative analysis was conducted using both the synthesized derivatives and the reference inhibitors donepezil (DNP) and tacrine (TAC).

The molecular docking results for hAChE, summarized in Table 8, indicate that all investigated compounds adopt a characteristic dual‐site binding orientation, consistent with their mixed‐type kinetic inhibition profiles (Section 3.3), effectively spanning the enzymatic gorge from the CAS to the PAS. A consistent trend across the entire series is the formation of π‐interactions with the key aromatic residues Trp86 in the CAS and Tyr341 in the PAS, suggesting that these two residues serve as primary anchoring points for the methoxyphenol–triazole scaffold a binding mode analogous to that of donepezil, which similarly engages both Trp86 and Tyr341 as confirmed by the co‐crystal structure. According to the calculated binding energies (BE), compound 5c emerged as the most favorably docked candidate within the series (BE = −11.1 kcal/mol), closely followed by 5h (BE = −11.0 kcal/mol), both approaching the binding energy of the reference drug donepezil (BE = −12.2 kcal/mol). These docking scores are consistent with and provide structural rationalization for the experimental AChE inhibitory potency order (5c > 5g > 5b > 5d > 5a > 5f > 5e > 5h), although the quantitative relationship between docking score and IC50 is not strictly linear, reflecting the inherent limitations of static docking in capturing dynamic enzyme–inhibitor interactions.

Integrated Structure–Activity Relationship (SAR) and Design Rationale

The diverse biological activity profiles exhibited by the synthesized triazole–phenol hybrids (5a–h) allowed for the establishment of meaningful structure–activity relationships (SAR). The SAR analysis was performed under two main sections: an investigation of the effect of the molecular scaffold (triazole‐linked phenol derivatives) and the decisive role of the substituents on the phenyl ring (R group) across anticholinesterase, antioxidant, and antiproliferative activities, supported by in silico profiling. A key objective of this analysis was to provide a comprehensive rationalization of the biological profiles of all eight derivatives including moderate and weakly active analogues rather than focusing exclusively on the most potent compounds, in order to extract the maximum SAR information from the series (Table 12).

The antiproliferative activity against PC3 cells revealed a clear lipophilicity‐driven SAR across the series. At the active end of the spectrum, compound 5e (para‐Cl, XLOGP = 3.45, IC50 = 3.57 μM) and compound 5c (para‐iPr, XLOGP = 3.95, IC50 = 3.89 μM) emerged as the most potent derivatives, both surpassing the reference drug methotrexate (IC50 = 4.42 μM). The superior activity of these two derivatives can be attributed to their optimal lipophilicity balance, which maximizes passive membrane permeability and intracellular accumulation in PC3 cells without compromising aqueous solubility. Compound 5c additionally demonstrated the highest cancer‐cell selectivity (SI = 6.22, comparable to MTX SI = 5.03), attributed to the steric bulk of the isopropyl group that may favour interactions with hydrophobic intracellular binding environments preferentially expressed in malignant cells.

Moving to the intermediate activity range, the methoxy derivative 5b (IC50 = 11.16 μM) and the fluorine derivative 5d (IC50 = 11.02 μM) displayed comparable moderate cytotoxicity, consistent with their similar and moderate lipophilicity values (XLOGP 2.79–2.92). The slightly lower activity of 5b relative to 5d, despite the electron‐donating character of methoxy being generally favourable for antioxidant activity, suggests that cytotoxicity in PC3 cells is governed primarily by lipophilicity rather than electronic donation capacity. At the weakly active end of the spectrum, the nitro derivative 5f (IC50 = 20.55 μM), ester derivative 5g (IC50 = 14.67 μM), and ketone derivative 5h (IC50 = 22.50 μM) exhibited significantly reduced cytotoxic potency. The principal determinant is their elevated polarity TPSA values of 94.99, 75.47, and 66.24 Å2 respectively which restricts passive diffusion through PC3 cell membranes. The methyl derivative 5a (IC50 = 49.78 μM) represents the weakest member of the series, where neither sufficient lipophilicity nor meaningful electronic perturbation is achieved to drive productive cytotoxic interactions at sub‐100 μM concentrations.

Steric and lipophilic effects emerged as the primary determinants of AChE inhibitory potency. At the most potent end, compound 5c (para‐iPr, IC50 = 1.44 μM, SI = 16.77) demonstrated the strongest AChE inhibition in the series, attributable to optimal hydrophobic complementarity within the narrow aromatic gorge particularly through π–Alkyl and hydrophobic contacts with Trp286 and Tyr341 in the PAS as confirmed by docking analysis. The ester derivative 5g (IC50 = 3.18 μM) ranked second, suggesting that the electron‐withdrawing ester carbonyl, despite reducing lipophilicity, may participate in additional polar contacts within the gorge entrance that partially compensate for reduced hydrophobic character. The methoxy derivative 5b (IC50 = 4.12 μM) benefits from the dual oxygen lone pairs of the methoxy group enabling supplementary hydrogen bonding with active‐site residues. In the intermediate activity range, the fluorine derivative 5d (IC50 = 5.60 μM) and the methyl derivative 5a (IC50 = 8.24 μM) showed moderate inhibition, with their relatively smaller substituents providing less effective hydrophobic gorge filling compared with the bulkier isopropyl or ester groups. At the weakest end, the nitro (5f, IC50 = 9.45 μM), chloro (5e, IC50 = 12.30 μM), and acetyl (5h, IC50 = 14.20 μM) derivatives displayed the lowest AChE potency. For 5f, the high TPSA (94.99 Å2) likely hinders penetration into the narrow gorge; for 5e and 5h, suboptimal steric fit within the CAS region relative to bulkier substituents accounts for the reduced inhibitory capacity. Conversely, 5h was the only derivative exhibiting preferential BuChE over AChE inhibition (IC50 = 11.40 μM, SI = 0.80), consistent with the acetyl group's flat geometry being better accommodated within the wider acyl‐binding pocket of BuChE (Ala328, Phe329).

Correlation with DFT‐derived reactivity parameters provides additional electronic rationale for the observed activity trends. Compound 5f exhibits the narrowest HOMO–LUMO energy gap (Egap = 2.9341 eV), theoretically identifying it as the most reactive member of the series. However, this high electronic reactivity did not translate into superior biological activity across any assay a discrepancy that is fully rationalized by the TPSA of 94.99 Å2, which critically limits membrane permeability and effectively offsets any advantage conferred by high intrinsic reactivity. This observation encapsulates a fundamental principle in multi‐parameter drug design: electronic reactivity descriptors derived from DFT provide mechanistic rationale but must always be interpreted in conjunction with physicochemical ADMET parameters to accurately predict biological outcome. In contrast, the intermediate Egap values of 5c (4.5444 eV) and 5e (4.2738 eV) reflect a balanced stability–reactivity profile that, combined with their optimal lipophilicity (XLOGP 3.45–3.95), contributes to their superior dual biological performance across both enzymatic and cellular assays.

The antioxidant SAR confirmed that electron‐donating capacity is the primary determinant of radical‐scavenging efficiency, following the activity order: –OCH3 (5b, DPPH IC50 = 264.12 μg/mL) > –CH3 (5a, 442.71 μg/mL) > –iPr (5c, 690.33 μg/mL) > –F (5d, 842.41 μg/mL) ≈ –Cl (5e, 867.74 μg/mL) > –CO2Et (5g, 886.47 μg/mL) > –NO2 (5f, 912.03 μg/mL). This order is consistent with the Hammett substituent constants: electron‐donating groups lower the O–H bond dissociation enthalpy of the phenolic hydroxyl, facilitating hydrogen atom transfer to the radical species, while electron‐withdrawing groups exert the opposite effect. Notably, in the ABTS assay the activity differences between substituents were markedly attenuated (IC50 range 7.11–9.23 μg/mL), suggesting that lipophilicity plays a secondary modulating role in this assay system, which is known to be sensitive to both hydrophilic and lipophilic antioxidants. This lipophilic contribution explains the unexpectedly moderate ABTS activity of 5d (IC50 = 7.15 μg/mL) despite its electron‐withdrawing fluorine substituent. These findings establish that while electronic reactivity (Egap, DFT) drives antioxidant capacity, biological target engagement cholinesterase inhibition and antiproliferative activity is primarily governed by a fine‐tuned balance of lipophilicity (XLOGP) and topological polar surface area (TPSA), as summarized in the integrated SAR profile (Table 12).

Study Limitations and Future Perspectives

While this study provides a comprehensive multi‐platform evaluation of the methoxyphenol–1,2,3‐triazole scaffold, several limitations define a clear roadmap for future investigation. The biological assessments were conducted exclusively through in vitro enzymatic and cellular assays, necessitating in vivo pharmacokinetic validation particularly to confirm actual BBB penetration and metabolic stability of the lead candidates 5c and 5h, whose anti‐AD utility is contingent upon adequate CNS exposure. The antiproliferative screening was restricted to the PC3 cell line; broader evaluation across diverse cancer cell panels, combined with mechanistic studies including flow cytometry‐based apoptosis quantification and caspase activation profiling, is required to confirm the proposed ROS‐mediated cytotoxic pathway for 5e and 5c. The static molecular docking analysis should be supplemented by long‐term molecular dynamics simulations and MM/GBSA free energy calculations to validate the temporal stability of the dual‐site CAS/PAS binding modes under physiological conditions. Finally, the consistent in silico prediction of hepatotoxicity across the entire series represents the most critical safety liability identified in this study; experimental verification using HepG2 assays or primary human hepatocyte cultures, alongside structural optimization strategies targeting metabolic soft spots, must be prioritized before any advancement toward preclinical development. Collectively, these limitations are characteristic of early‐stage lead identification and do not diminish the significance of the present findings as a rational foundation for next‐generation scaffold optimization.

Conclusions

In this study, a novel series of methoxyphenol–1,2,3‐triazole hybrids (5a–h) was rationally designed, synthesized via CuAAC click chemistry, and comprehensively evaluated as preliminary multifunctional lead structures targeting Alzheimer's disease and prostate cancer. Compound 5c (para‐iPr) emerged as the most promising candidate across multiple assays, combining potent AChE inhibition (IC50 = 1.44 ± 0.33 μM, SI = 16.77) with strong PC3 cytotoxicity (IC50 = 3.89 ± 0.20 μM) and the highest cancer‐cell selectivity (SI = 6.22) in the series. Compound 5e (para‐Cl) demonstrated the most potent antiproliferative activity (IC50 = 3.57 ± 0.11 μM), surpassing the reference drug methotrexate. Enzyme kinetic studies confirmed mixed‐type inhibition for both lead compounds, consistent with dual CAS/PAS engagement, while molecular docking provided structural rationalization for the observed enzyme selectivity profiles. Integrated SAR analysis established that antioxidant capacity is governed by electron‐donating character, AChE potency by substituent bulkiness and lipophilicity, and antiproliferative activity by the lipophilicity–polarity balance controlling membrane penetration. In silico ADMET profiling confirmed full Lipinski compliance and predicted BBB permeability for seven derivatives; however, the consistent hepatotoxicity prediction across the series represents a critical liability requiring experimental verification before preclinical advancement. In conclusion, the methoxyphenol–1,2,3‐triazole scaffold represents a promising preliminary platform for multi‐target lead discovery, and the present findings provide a well‐characterized structural and pharmacological foundation for rational next‐generation optimization targeting the overlapping molecular pathways of neurodegenerative and oncological disorders.
