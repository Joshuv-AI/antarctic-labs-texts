Comparison theorems in Hermitian geometry I
Xiaokui Yang

Abstract
. This paper develops second variational formulas and index forms in the context of Hermitian geometry. Building upon these analytical foundations, we establish results analogous to classical theorems in Riemannian geometry, including Myers’ theorem, Laplacian comparison theorems and volume comparison theorems.



1.
Introduction

Let
(M,g,J)
be a Hermitian manifold, where
g
is a Riemannian metric and
J\in\mathrm{End}(T_{\mathbb{R}}M)
is an integrable complex structure compatible with
g
. In the study of Hermitian geometry, several canonical metric compatible affine connections play fundamental roles. The most prominent connections include:

(1)

The Chern connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{Ch}}
: The unique connection preserving both the Hermitian metric and the holomorphic structure of
M
.

(2)

The Strominger-Bismut connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
(
[
Str86
,
Bis89
]
): A geometrically natural connection that emerges in heterotic string theory and plays a crucial role in the study of complex non-Kähler geometry.

(3)

The restricted Levi-Civita connection
\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{LC}}
: The projection of the complexified Levi-Civita connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{LC}}
to the holomorphic tangent bundle
T^{1,0}M
.

Remarkably, when
(M,g,J)
is Kähler, these connections coincide, reflecting the special geometric properties of Kähler manifolds. Recent research has significantly advanced our understanding of these connections, particularly in applications to string theory and the study of non-Kähler Calabi-Yau manifolds. In this paper, we establish new extensions of fundamental results from Riemannian geometric analysis to the framework of Hermitian geometry, with particular emphasis on the Strominger-Bismut connection. Our work bridges important gaps between these two geometric theories while revealing new phenomena to the Hermitian setting. Let
(M,g,J)
be a Hermitian manifold and let
\omega_{g}
denote the fundamental

2
-form associated with the Hermitian structure
(g,J)
:

\omega_{g}(X,Y)=g(JX,Y)

for
X,Y\in T_{\mathbb{R}}M
. It is well-known that the Strominger-Bismut connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
on the real tangent bundle
T_{\mathbb{R}}M
of a Hermitian manifold
(M,g,J)
is characterized by the following relation:

(1.1)

g(\nabla_{X}^{\mathrm{SB}}Y,Z)=g(\nabla_{X}^{\mathrm{LC}}Y,Z)+\frac{1}{2}\left(d\omega_{g}\right)(JX,JY,JZ),

where
\nabla^{\mathrm{LC}}
is the Levi-Civita connection of
(M,g)
. A key feature of this connection is that its torsion tensor

(1.2)

T^{\mathrm{SB}}(X,Y,Z):=g(\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{X}Y-\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{Y}X-[X,Y],Z)

is totally skew symmetric, i.e.
T^{\mathrm{SB}}\in\Omega^{3}(M)
. This property makes it particularly significant in complex non-Kähler geometry and theoretical physics. Moreover, since
T^{\mathrm{SB}}
is totally skew-symmetric, for any
X\in\Gamma(M,TM)
, one can seee clearly that

(1.3)

\nabla^{\text{SB}}_{X}X=\nabla^{\text{LC}}_{X}X.

In particular, if
\gamma
is a geodesic with respect to the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla^{\text{LC}}
, then it is also a geodesic with respect to the Strominger-Bismut connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
. Consequently, we will not distinguish between these connections when discussing geodesics in subsequent analysis.

The first main result establishes the second variation formula for the energy functional of unit speed geodesics in a Hermitian manifold
(M,g,J)
. This extends classical Riemannian variational principles to the complex-geometric setting, where the Strominger-Bismut connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}

plays a crucial role in characterizing the torsion-modified curvature effects.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(M,\omega_{g})
be a Hermitian mainfold and
\gamma(t):[a,b]\to M
be a unit speed geodesic. Then the second variation of the energy of
\gamma
is

(1.4)

\displaystyle\frac{{\partial}^{2}E(\alpha)}{{\partial}s_{1}{\partial}s_{2}}\bigg|_{s_{1}=s_{2}=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\left\{\left\langle\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}V,\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}W\right\rangle+T^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(V,\gamma^{\prime},\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}W\right)dt-R^{\mathrm{SB}}(V,\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},W)\right\}dt

\displaystyle+\left.\left\langle\left.\left(\overline{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}s_{1}}}\alpha_{*}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}s_{2}}\right)\right)\right|_{s_{1}=s_{2}=0},\gamma^{\prime}\right\rangle\right|_{t=a}^{t=b},

where
V
and
W
are variational vector fields of
\alpha
, with

\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}
and
\overline{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}
representing the connections
induced on
\gamma^{*}TM
and
\alpha^{*}TM
respectively via the
Strominger-Bismut connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
on
(TM,g,J)
. Moreover, if
\alpha
is a proper variation of
\gamma
, then

(1.5)

\frac{{\partial}^{2}E(\alpha)}{{\partial}s_{1}{\partial}s_{2}}\bigg|_{s_{1}=s_{2}=0}=\int_{a}^{b}\left\{\left\langle\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}V,\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}W\right\rangle+T^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(V,\gamma^{\prime},\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}W\right)-R^{\mathrm{SB}}(V,\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},W)\right\}dt.

While the energy functional
E(\alpha)
and its second variation
\frac{{\partial}^{2}E(\alpha)}{{\partial}s_{1}{\partial}s_{2}}
are intrinsically determined by the Riemannian metric

g
, the explicit form of the second variation formula depends crucially on the choice of connection. In Hermitian geometry, this leads to multiple meaningful expressions:

(1)

The Levi-Civita connection yields the classical Riemannian formulation;

(2)

The Chern connection provides a natural complex-geometric perspective;

(3)

The Strominger-Bismut connection reveals torsion effects in the variational structure.

Our approach utilizes the Strominger-Bismut connection to highlight the interplay between metric variations and the Hermitian torsion. In Riemannian geometry, the index form
I_{\gamma}:\Gamma(\gamma^{*}TM)\times\Gamma(\gamma^{*}TM)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is classically defined using the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{LC}}
:

(1.6)

I_{\gamma}(V,W)=\int_{a}^{b}\left\{\left\langle\widehat{\nabla}^{\text{LC}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}V,\widehat{\nabla}^{\text{LC}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}W\right\rangle-R^{\text{LC}}(V,\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},W)\right\}dt.

We reformulate it by using the Strominger-Bismut connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(M,\omega_{g})
be a Hermitian mainfold and
\gamma(t):[a,b]\to M
be a unit speed geodesic. Then for any smooth vector fields
V
and
W
along
\gamma
, the index form can be written as

\displaystyle I_{\gamma}(V,W)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\left\{\left\langle\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}V,\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}W\right\rangle+T^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(V,\gamma^{\prime},\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}W\right)-R^{\mathrm{SB}}(V,\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},W)\right\}dt

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{1}{2}T^{\mathrm{SB}}(V,W,\gamma^{\prime})\right|_{t=a}^{t=b}.

In particular,

(1.7)

I_{\gamma}(V,V)=\int_{a}^{b}\left\{\left\langle\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}V,\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}V\right\rangle+T^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(V,\gamma^{\prime},\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}V\right)-R^{\mathrm{SB}}(V,\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},V)\right\}dt.

The index form constitutes a fundamental analytical tool in geometric variational theory, with particularly profound applications in comparison theorems for both Riemannian and Kähler manifolds. As applications of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
, we derive results analogous to classical theorems in Riemannian geometry, including Myers’ theorem and various comparison theorems. Recall that
the
real Ricci curvature of the Strominger-Bismut connection
is defined to be

(1.8)

\mathrm{Ric}^{\mathrm{SB}}(X,Y):=\sum_{i,\ell=1}^{2n}g^{i\ell}R^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x^{i}},X,Y,\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x^{\ell}}\right),

where
X,Y\in T_{\mathbb{R}}M
.
It is well-known that the first Bianchi identity fails to hold for
R^{\mathrm{SB}}
, resulting in
\mathrm{Ric}^{\mathrm{SB}}

not necessarily being symmetric. It is well-known that Ricci curvature tensors derived from the Levi-Civita connection and the Strominger-Bismut connection possess intrinsically different geometric properties, making them generally incomparable. For detailed discussions on the comparative analysis of these and other Ricci curvatures, we refer to
[
LY12
]
,
[
LY17
]
, and
[
WY25
]
.
In the Hermitian setting, the holomorphic Ricci curvature serves as the complex analogue of the real Ricci curvature and is defined by

(1.9)

\mathfrak{Ric}^{\mathrm{SB}}(V,\overline{V}):=\sum_{i,\ell=1}^{n}h^{i\overline{\ell}}R^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}z^{i}},\overline{V},V,\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}{{\overline{z}}^{\ell}}}\right)

where
V\in\Gamma(M,T^{1,0}M)
. The following theorem is a Hermitian counterpart to Myers’ theorem by using the holomorphic Ricci curvature of the Strominger-Bismut connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(M,\omega)
be a complete balanced Hermitian manifold of complex dimension
n
. Suppose that there exists some constant
K>0
such that the holomorphic Ricci curvature of the Strominger-Bismut connection satisfies

(1.10)

\mathfrak{Ric}^{\mathrm{SB}}(V,\overline{V})\geq(2n-1)K|V|^{2},

for each
V\in\Gamma(M,T^{1,0}M)
.

(1)

M
is compact and

\mathrm{diam}(M,\omega)\leq\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{K}}.

(2)

The fundamental group of
M
is finite.

(3)

The volume comparison theorem holds:

(1.11)

\mathrm{Vol}(M,\omega)\leq\mathrm{Vol}\left(\mathbb{S}^{2n}\left(1/\sqrt{K}\right),g_{\mathrm{can}}\right).

The proof of Theorem
1.3
demonstrates that both the Laplacian comparison theorem and the local volume comparison theorem remain valid under the curvature condition (
1.10
). In the Kähler case (
\omega
Kähler), Theorem
1.3
is classical, and the manifold
M
is known to be simply connected. However, when

\omega
is merely balanced, the question of whether
M
retains simple connectivity remains open. On the other hand, using perturbation methods (e.g.
[
LY17
]
and
[
WY25
]
), one can easily construct non-Kähler Hermitian metrics satisfying
\mathfrak{Ric}^{\mathrm{SB}}>0
while their Levi-Civita Ricci curvature need not be positive.‌

Holomorphic sectional curvature is also a fundamental concept in complex geometry that carries rich geometric information, sharing important relationships with both Ricci curvature and holomorphic bisectional curvature of complex manifolds. The holomorphic sectional curvature
\mathrm{HSC}^{\mathrm{SB}}
of the Strominger-Bismut connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
is defined as

(1.12)

\mathrm{HSC}^{\mathrm{SB}}(X):=R^{\mathrm{SB}}(JX,X,X,JX)/|X|^{4}.

The following result demonstrates a structural analogy to the foundational theorems established by Sygne and Tsukamoto (
[
Tsu57
]
), extending their framework within the Hermitian geometric setting.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
(M,\omega)
be a complete Hermitian manifold. Suppose that the holomorphic sectional curvature of the Strominger-Bismut connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
satisfies

\mathrm{HSC}^{\mathrm{SB}}\geq K

for some constant
K>0
. Then
M
is compact and

\mathrm{diam}(M,\omega)\leq\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{K}}.

Moreover,
M
is simply connected.

In particular, we establish the following result, which parallels Weinstein’s theorem on positive Riemannian sectional curvature and extends the Kähler setting in
[
NZ18
]
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
(M,\omega_{g})
be a compact Hermitian manifold with positive holomorphic sectional curvature
\mathrm{HSC}^{\mathrm{SB}}>0
. Then every isometry
F:(M,g)\rightarrow(M,g)
has a fixed point.

2.
Curvature notions

Let
(M,g,J)
be a Hermitian manifold. There is a unique affine connection

\nabla:\Gamma(M,T_{\mathbb{R}}M)\times\Gamma(M,T_{\mathbb{R}}M)\rightarrow\Gamma(M,T_{\mathbb{R}}M)

satisfying

(1)

\nabla
is compatible with
g
and
J
;

(2)

The torsion tensor

T(X,Y,Z):=g(\nabla_{X}Y-\nabla_{Y}X-[X,Y],Z)

is totally anti-symmetric, i.e.
T\in\Omega^{3}(M)
.

This connection is called the
Strominger-Bismut connection
and it is denoted by
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
. Let
\omega_{g}
denote the fundamental

2
-form associated with the Hermitian structure:

\omega_{g}(X,Y)=g(JX,Y),\ \ X,Y\in T_{\mathbb{R}}M.

The following foundational formulation will be employed throughout our work:

Lemma 2.1
.

The Strominger connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
on
(M,g,J)
is given by

(2.1)

g(\nabla_{X}^{\mathrm{SB}}Y,Z)=g(\nabla_{X}^{\mathrm{LC}}Y,Z)+\frac{1}{2}\left(d\omega_{g}\right)(JX,JY,JZ),

where
\nabla^{\mathrm{LC}}
is the Levi-Civita connection on
(M,g)
and the torsion of
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
is

(2.2)

T^{\mathrm{SB}}(X,Y,Z)=\left(d\omega_{g}\right)(JX,JY,JZ).

Proof.

It follows from a straightforward computation. ∎

The
curvature tensor

R^{\mathrm{SB}}
of the Strominger-Bismut connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
is defined as

(2.3)

R^{\mathrm{SB}}(X,Y,Z,W)=\left\langle\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{X}\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{Y}Z-\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{Y}\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{X}Z-\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{[X,Y]}Z,W\right\rangle.

The following lemma describes the essential curvature property of Strominger-Bismut connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
:

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
X,Y,Z,W\in\Gamma(M,T_{\mathbb{C}}M)
, it holds that

(2.4)

R^{\mathrm{SB}}(X,Y,Z,W)=-R^{\mathrm{SB}}(Y,X,Z,W)=-R^{\mathrm{SB}}(X,Y,W,Z).

Proof.

The first identity follows from the definition. The second identity can be derived from the relation
R^{\mathrm{SB}}(X,Y,Z,Z)=0
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle R^{\mathrm{SB}}(X,Y,Z,Z)=\left\langle\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{X}\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{Y}Z-\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{Y}\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{X}Z-\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{[X,Y]}Z,Z\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X\left\langle\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{Y}Z,Z\right\rangle-\left\langle\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{Y}Z,\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{X}Z\right\rangle-Y\left\langle\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{X}Z,Z\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{X}Z,\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{Y}Z\right\rangle-\frac{1}{2}[X,Y](|Z|^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X\left(\frac{1}{2}Y(|Z|^{2})\right)-Y\left(\frac{1}{2}X(|Z|^{2})\right)-\frac{1}{2}[X,Y]|Z|^{2}=0.

The proof is completed.
∎

The curvature tensor
R^{\mathrm{SB}}

exhibits key deviations from the symmetric properties of the Riemannian curvature tensor:

(1)

the first Bianchi identity fails to hold for
R^{\mathrm{SB}}
.

(2)

R^{\mathrm{SB}}(X,Y,Z,W)\neq R^{\mathrm{SB}}(Z,W,X,Y)
in general.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any
X\in\Gamma(M,T_{\mathbb{R}}M)
, it holds that

(2.5)

\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}_{X}X=\nabla^{\mathrm{LC}}_{X}X.

In particular, if
\gamma

\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}\gamma^{\prime}=0

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
be a smooth curve and

(2.6)

E(\gamma):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}|\gamma^{\prime}(t)|^{2}dt

\alpha:[a,b]\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to M

(2.7)

\frac{d}{ds}\bigg|_{s=0}E(\alpha(\bullet,s))=-\int_{a}^{b}\left\langle V,\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}\gamma^{\prime}\right\rangle dt.

Proof.

It follows from the well-known result

(2.8)

\frac{d}{ds}\bigg|_{s=0}E(\alpha(\bullet,s))=-\int_{a}^{b}\left\langle V,\widehat{\nabla}_{\frac{d}{dt}}^{\text{LC}}\gamma^{\prime}\right\rangle dt

and the fact that
\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}\gamma^{\prime}=\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{LC}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}\gamma^{\prime}
.
∎

This is equivalent to

(2.16)

{{}^{\mathrm{SB}}T}_{ij}^{k}=h^{k\overline{\ell}}\left(\frac{{\partial}h_{i\overline{\ell}}}{{\partial}z^{j}}-\frac{{\partial}h_{j\overline{\ell}}}{{\partial}z^{i}}\right),\quad{{}^{\mathrm{SB}}T}_{\overline{ij}}^{k}=0,

and

(2.17)

\ {}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{ij}^{k}=h^{k\overline{\ell}}\frac{{\partial}h_{i\overline{\ell}}}{{\partial}z^{j}},\quad^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{\overline{j}i}^{k}=2\ ^{\mathrm{LC}}\Gamma_{\overline{j}i}^{k}=h^{k\overline{\ell}}\left(\frac{{\partial}h_{i\overline{\ell}}}{{\partial}\overline{z}^{j}}-\frac{{\partial}h_{i\overline{j}}}{{\partial}\overline{z}^{\ell}}\right),\quad^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{i\overline{j}}^{k}=\ ^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{\overline{i}\overline{j}}^{k}=0.

On the other hand, the condition
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}J=0
implies that
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
preserves both
T^{1,0}M
and
T^{0,1}M
. Hence,

(2.18)

R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{ijk\ell}=R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{i\overline{j}k\ell}=R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\overline{i}jk\ell}=R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\overline{i}\overline{j}k\ell}=0,\quad R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{ij\overline{k}\overline{\ell}}=R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{i\overline{j}\overline{k}\overline{\ell}}=R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\overline{i}j\overline{k}\overline{\ell}}=R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\overline{i}\overline{j}\overline{k}\overline{\ell}}=0.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(M,\omega)
be a Hermitian manifold. For any
X,Y\in\Gamma(M,T_{\mathbb{C}}M)
, the complexification of the real Ricci curvature of
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
defined in (
1.8
) is

(2.19)

\mathrm{Ric}^{\mathrm{SB},{\mathbb{C}}}\left(X,Y\right)=h^{i\overline{\ell}}R^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}z^{i}},X,Y,\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\overline{z}^{\ell}}\right)+h^{\ell\overline{i}}R^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\overline{z}^{i}},X,Y,\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}z^{\ell}}\right).

In particular,

(2.20)

\mathrm{Ric}^{\mathrm{SB},{\mathbb{C}}}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}z^{p}},\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\overline{z}^{q}}\right)=h^{\ell\overline{i}}R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\overline{i}p\overline{q}\ell}=h^{\ell\overline{i}}R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{p\overline{i}\ell\overline{q}}.

Proof.

The first identity follows from a complexification process. Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ric}^{\mathrm{SB},{\mathbb{C}}}\left(X,Y\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,\ell=1}^{n}\left(g^{i\ell}R^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x^{i}},X,Y,\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x^{\ell}}\right)+g^{i,\ell+n}R^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x^{i}},X,Y,\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x^{\ell+n}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+g^{i+n,\ell}R^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x^{i+n}},X,Y,\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x^{\ell}}\right)+g^{i+n,\ell+n}R^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x^{i+n}},X,Y,\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x^{\ell+n}}\right)\right).

By using complexification relations

\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x^{i}}=\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}z^{i}}+\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\overline{z}^{i}},\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i+n}}=\sqrt{-1}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{i}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}^{i}}\right),\quad h^{i\overline{j}}=2\left(g^{ij}-\sqrt{-1}g^{i,n+j}\right)

and similar computations as in
[
LY17
, Lemma 7.1]
one gets (
2.19
). The second identity follows from the fact that
R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{ij\overline{k}\overline{\ell}}=0
.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
(M,\omega)
be a balanced Hermitian manifold, i.e.
d\omega^{n-1}=0
. For any real vector field
X=X^{i}\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}z^{i}}+\overline{X}^{i}\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\overline{z}^{i}}\in\Gamma(M,T_{\mathbb{R}}M)
, we have

(2.21)

\operatorname{Ric}^{\mathrm{SB}}(X,X)=2\left(h^{i\overline{\ell}}R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{i\overline{j}k\overline{\ell}}\right)X^{k}\overline{X}^{j}.

Proof.

Let
X,Y\in\Gamma(M,T_{\mathbb{R}}M)
. One can write them as

X=X^{1,0}+X^{0,1}\quad\quad Y=Y^{1,0}+Y^{0,1}

where
X^{1,0},Y^{1,0}\in\Gamma(M,T^{1,0}M)
and
X^{0,1}=\overline{X}^{1,0}
,
Y^{0,1}=\overline{Y}^{1,0}
. By the relation (
2.19
) and (
2.18
), we deduce that

(2.22)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}^{\mathrm{SB}}(X,Y)=\operatorname{Ric}^{\mathrm{SB},{\mathbb{C}}}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,\ell=1}^{n}\left[h^{i\overline{\ell}}R^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{i}},X^{1,0}+X^{0,1},Y^{1,0},\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}^{\ell}}\right)+h^{\ell\overline{i}}R^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}^{i}},X^{1,0}+X^{0,1},Y^{0,1},\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\ell}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,\ell=1}^{n}2\operatorname{Re}\left[h^{i\overline{\ell}}\left(R^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{i}},X^{0,1},Y^{1,0},\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}^{\ell}}\right)+R^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{i}},X^{1,0},Y^{1,0},\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}^{\ell}}\right)\right)\right].

In particular, we obtain

(2.23)

\operatorname{Ric}^{\mathrm{SB}}(X,X)=2\mathrm{Re}\left[\left(h^{i\overline{\ell}}R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{i\overline{j}k\overline{\ell}}\right)X^{k}\overline{X}^{j}+\left(h^{i\overline{\ell}}R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{ijk\overline{\ell}}\right)X^{k}X^{j}\right].

Since
\ {}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{ij}^{\overline{k}}=0
, by complexification of the curvature formula, we have

(2.24)

h^{i\overline{\ell}}R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{ijk\overline{\ell}}=\frac{{\partial}{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{jk}^{i}}{{\partial}z^{i}}-\frac{{\partial}{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{ik}^{i}}{{\partial}z^{j}}-{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma^{i}_{jp}{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma^{p}_{ik}+{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma^{i}_{ip}{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma^{p}_{jk}.

Moreover, by using (
2.17
),

(2.25)

\frac{{\partial}{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{jk}^{i}}{{\partial}x^{i}}=-{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{pi}^{i}{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{jk}^{p}+h^{i\overline{\ell}}\frac{{\partial}^{2}h_{j\overline{\ell}}}{{\partial}z^{i}{\partial}z^{k}},\quad\frac{{\partial}{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{ik}^{i}}{{\partial}x^{j}}=-{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{pj}^{i}{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{ik}^{p}+h^{i\overline{\ell}}\frac{{\partial}^{2}h_{i\overline{\ell}}}{{\partial}z^{j}{\partial}z^{k}}.

Similarly, by (
2.16
),

(2.26)

\frac{{\partial}{}^{\mathrm{SB}}T_{ij}^{i}}{{\partial}x^{k}}=-{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{pk}^{i}{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{ij}^{p}+{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{pk}^{i}{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma_{ji}^{p}+h^{i\overline{\ell}}\frac{{\partial}^{2}h_{i\overline{\ell}}}{{\partial}z^{j}{\partial}z^{k}}-h^{i\overline{\ell}}\frac{{\partial}^{2}h_{j\overline{\ell}}}{{\partial}z^{k}{\partial}z^{i}}.

By (
2.24
), (
2.25
) and (
2.26
), we obtain

(2.27)

h^{i\overline{\ell}}R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{ijk\overline{\ell}}=-\frac{{\partial}{}^{\mathrm{SB}}T_{ij}^{i}}{{\partial}x^{k}}+{}^{\mathrm{SB}}T_{ip}^{i}{}^{\mathrm{SB}}\Gamma^{p}_{jk}.

On the other hand, by the Bochner-Kodaira formula
[{\overline{\partial}}^{*},L]=\sqrt{-1}(\partial+[\Lambda,\partial\omega])
,

(2.28)

\overline{\partial}^{*}\omega=\sqrt{-1}\Lambda(\partial\omega)=\sqrt{-1}h^{s\overline{q}}\left(\frac{\partial h_{s\overline{q}}}{\partial z^{k}}-\frac{\partial h_{k\overline{q}}}{\partial z^{s}}\right)dz^{k}=\sqrt{-1}{}^{\mathrm{SB}}T_{sk}^{s}dz^{k}.

Here we assume

is compact. In general, if

, by using

, we obtain

for each

and so

(2.29)

h^{i\overline{\ell}}R^{\mathrm{SB}}_{ijk\overline{\ell}}=0.

By using a similar computation, one can show that

is a Hermitian matrix (see also
[

, Corollary 1.8]
on the formula of

∎

3.

In this section, we formulate the second variation formula and the index form by using the Strominger-Bismut connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
. In particular, we prove Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
.

Let
\gamma:[a,b]\to M

(M,g)

\alpha:[a,b]\times(-\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{1})\times(-\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{2})\to M

\gamma(t)

V
and
W

\alpha(t,0,0)=\gamma(t)

I_{\gamma}(V,W)=\int_{a}^{b}\left\{\left\langle\widehat{\nabla}^{\text{LC}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}V,\widehat{\nabla}^{\text{LC}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}W\right\rangle-R^{\text{LC}}(V,\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},W)\right\}dt.

.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\partial}^{2}}{{\partial}s_{1}{\partial}s_{2}}\left|\alpha_{*}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}\right)\right|^{2}=\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}s_{1}}\left\langle\overline{\nabla}_{\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}s_{2}}}^{\mathrm{SB}}\alpha_{*}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}\right),\alpha_{*}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}\right)\right\rangle=\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}s_{1}}\left\langle\overline{\nabla}_{\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}}^{\mathrm{SB}}\alpha_{*}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}s_{2}}\right),\alpha_{*}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}\right)\right\rangle

since the torsion term

\displaystyle\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}s_{1}}\left\langle\overline{\nabla}_{\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}}^{\mathrm{SB}}\alpha_{*}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}s_{2}}\right),\alpha_{*}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\overline{\nabla}_{\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}s_{1}}}^{\mathrm{SB}}\overline{\nabla}_{\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}}^{\mathrm{SB}}\alpha_{*}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}s_{2}}\right),\alpha_{*}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\left\langle\overline{\nabla}_{\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}}^{\mathrm{SB}}\alpha_{*}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}s_{2}}\right),\overline{\nabla}_{\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}s_{1}}}^{\mathrm{SB}}\alpha_{*}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}\right)\right\rangle.

By using the curvature formula of

, one has

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\partial}^{2}}{{\partial}s_{1}{\partial}s_{2}}\left|\alpha_{*}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle-\left\langle\overline{\nabla}_{\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}s_{1}}}^{\mathrm{SB}}\alpha_{*}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}s_{2}}\right),\overline{\nabla}_{\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}}^{\mathrm{SB}}\alpha_{*}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}\right)\right\rangle.

Proof of Theorem

\displaystyle I_{\gamma}(X(s),W(s))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}\left(\left\langle\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{LC}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}X(t),\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{LC}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}W(t)\right\rangle-R^{\mathrm{LC}}(X(t),\gamma^{\prime}(t),\gamma^{\prime}(t),W(t))\right)dt.

Since
\left(\mathrm{Hess}\ r\right)(\gamma^{\prime}(b),\gamma^{\prime}(b))=0
, one obtains

(4.4)

\Delta_{g}r(q)=\sum_{i=2}^{2n}I_{\gamma}(X_{i}(b),X_{i}(b)).

We define

Z_{i}(t)=f(t)e^{\mathrm{SB}}_{i}(t)

with
f(t)=\frac{\mathrm{sn}_{k}(t)}{\mathrm{sn}_{k}(b)}
for
i=2,\cdots,2n
. Then
Z_{i}(a)=X_{i}(a)=0
and
Z_{i}(b)=X_{i}(b)
. This implies

(4.5)

\Delta_{g}r(q)=\sum_{i=2}^{2n}I_{\gamma}(X_{i}(b),X_{i}(b))\leq\sum_{i=2}^{2n}I_{\gamma}(Z_{i}(b),Z_{i}(b)).

For
2\leq i\leq 2n
, by Theorem
1.2
,

\displaystyle I_{\gamma}(Z_{i}(b),Z_{i}(b))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{b}\left\{\left\langle\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}Z_{i},\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}Z_{i}\right\rangle+T^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(Z_{i},\gamma^{\prime},\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}Z_{i}\right)-R^{\mathrm{SB}}(Z_{i},\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},Z_{i})\right\}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{b}\left(|f^{\prime}(t)|^{2}-f^{2}(t)R^{\mathrm{SB}}(e^{\mathrm{SB}}_{i},\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},e^{\mathrm{SB}}_{i})\right)dt.

On the other hand, we have the estimate

\sum_{i=1}^{2n}R^{\mathrm{SB}}(e^{\mathrm{SB}}_{i},\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},e^{\mathrm{SB}}_{i})\geq(2n-1)K.

Hence

(4.6)

\Delta_{g}r(q)\leq(2n-1)\int_{0}^{b}\left(|f^{\prime}(t)|^{2}-Kf^{2}(t)\right)dt=(2n-1)\frac{\mathrm{sn}^{\prime}_{K}(r)}{\mathrm{sn}_{K}(r)}.

It is well-established that the Bishop-Gromov volume comparison theorem follows from the Laplacian comparison theorem and Myers’ diameter estimate under Ricci curvature bounds. We adopt an analogous approach in our setting, leveraging the identical geodesic structures and exponential maps between two frameworks. Let
p\in M
and
U=M\setminus\mathrm{cut}(p)
. Let
B(p,\delta)
be the
metric ball centered at
p
with radius
\delta
, and
g_{K}

be the constant curvature metric. Consider the trivialization map in the normal coordinates on the domain of injective radius

\Sigma(p)
,

{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2n-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Phi}}{{\rightarrow}}T_{p}M\backslash\{0\}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\backslash\{0\}\rightarrow U\backslash\{p\}

given by
\Phi(\rho,\omega)=\rho\omega
,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}_{g}\left(B(p,\delta)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}_{g}\left(\exp_{p}(B(0,\delta)\cap\Sigma(p))\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2n-1}}\int_{0}^{\delta}\chi_{\Sigma(p)}\cdot\sqrt{\det g}\circ\Phi(\rho,\omega)\cdot\rho^{2n-1}\cdot d\rho d{\mathrm{vol}}_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2n-1}}.

For each fixed
\omega\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}

the volume density ratio is defined as

(4.8)

\lambda(\rho,\omega)=\frac{\rho^{2n-1}\sqrt{\det g}\circ\Phi(\rho,\omega)}{\mathrm{sn}_{K}^{2n-1}(\rho)}.

By using the Laplacian comparison result (
4.6
), one has

(4.9)

{\partial}_{r}\log\left(r^{2n-1}\sqrt{\det g}\right)=\Delta_{g}r\geq(2n-1)\frac{\mathrm{sn}_{K}^{\prime}(r)}{\mathrm{sn}_{K}(r)}={\partial}_{r}\log\left(\mathrm{sn}_{K}^{2n-1}(r)\right).

Hence, the volume density ratio

\lambda(\rho,\omega)
is decreasing in
\rho\in(0,\delta)
and
\lim\limits_{\rho\rightarrow 0}\lambda(\rho,\omega)=1
.
If
K=1/R^{2}>0
,

\frac{\mathrm{Vol}_{g}(B(p,\delta))}{\mathrm{Vol}_{g_{k}}(B(p,\delta))}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}({\mathbb{S}}^{2n-1})}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2n-1}}\left(\frac{\int_{0}^{\delta}\chi_{\Sigma(p)}\cdot\rho^{2n-1}\sqrt{\det g}\circ\Phi(\rho,\omega)d\rho}{\int_{0}^{\delta}\chi_{B(0,\pi R)}\cdot\mathrm{sn}_{K}^{2n-1}(\rho)d\rho}\right)d\mathrm{vol}_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2n-1}}.

By first part of Theorem
1.3
,
\mathrm{diam}(M,g)\leq\pi R
. Therefore,
\Sigma(p)\subset B(0,\pi R)
and

\widetilde{\lambda}(\rho,\omega)=\frac{\chi_{\Sigma(p)}\cdot\rho^{2n-1}\sqrt{\det g}\circ\Phi(\rho,\omega)}{\chi_{B(0,\pi R)}\cdot\mathrm{sn}_{K}^{2n-1}(\rho)}

is nonincreasing in
\rho
. Hence
\frac{\mathrm{Vol}_{g}(B(p,\delta))}{\mathrm{Vol}_{g_{K}}(B(p,\delta))}

is nonincreasing in
\rho
. On ther other hand,

\lim\limits_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathrm{Vol}_{g}(B(p,\delta))}{\mathrm{Vol}_{g_{K}}(B(p,\delta))}=1
,
we deduced that
\mathrm{Vol}_{g}(B(p,\delta))\leq\mathrm{Vol}_{g_{K}}(B(p,\delta))
. When
\delta
approaches
\pi/\sqrt{K}
, one deduces that
\mathrm{Vol}(M,\omega)\leq\mathrm{Vol}\left(\mathbb{S}^{2n}\left(1/\sqrt{K}\right),g_{\mathrm{can}}\right)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Suppose
\gamma:[0,L]\to M
is a unit speed minimal geodesic with length
L>\pi/\sqrt{K}
.
Let
\alpha:[a,b]\times(-\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{1})\times(-\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{2})\to M
be a
proper
second variation of
\gamma
with variational vector fields
V
and
W
.
In particular, we choose

(4.10)

V(t)=W(t)=\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{L}\right)J\gamma^{\prime}(t).

where
J
is the complex structure of
(M,\omega)
. A simple calculation shows that

(4.11)

\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}\left(J\gamma^{\prime}(t)\right)=J\left(\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}\gamma^{\prime}(t)\right)=0.

Hence, by Theorem
1.1
, we obtain

0\leq\frac{{\partial}^{2}E(\alpha)}{{\partial}s_{1}{\partial}s_{2}}\bigg|_{s_{1}=s_{2}=0}=\int_{0}^{L}\left\{\left\langle\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}V,\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}W\right\rangle+T^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(V,\gamma^{\prime},\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}W\right)-R^{\mathrm{SB}}(V,\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},W)\right\}dt.

Moreover, since
T
is totally skew-symmetric,

(4.12)

T^{\mathrm{SB}}\left(V,\gamma^{\prime},\widehat{\nabla}^{\mathrm{SB}}_{\frac{d}{dt}}W\right)=\frac{\pi}{L}\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{L}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{L}\right)T^{\mathrm{SB}}(J\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},J\gamma^{\prime})=0.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\partial}^{2}E(\alpha)}{{\partial}s_{1}{\partial}s_{2}}\bigg|_{s_{1}=s_{2}=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi t}{L}\right)-\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi t}{L}\right)R^{\mathrm{SB}}(J\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},J\gamma^{\prime})\right)dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi t}{L}\right)-\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi t}{L}\right)K\right)dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi t}{L}\right)\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}-K\right)dt<0.

This is a contradiction. Hence, the diameter of
M
is bounded by
\pi/\sqrt{K}
.

Moreover,
suppose
\pi_{1}(M)
is not trivial.
Let
\gamma:[0,\ell]\to M
be a unit speed closed geodesic representing a nontrivial element in
\pi_{1}(M)
that has minimum length in the free homotopy class of
\gamma
. Let
V(t)=J\gamma^{\prime}(t)
and define a variation
\alpha:[0,\ell]\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to M
of
\gamma(t)
as

\alpha(t,s)=\exp_{\gamma(t)}(sV(t)).

Recall that geodesics for
\nabla^{\mathrm{LC}}
and
\nabla^{\mathrm{SB}}
are the same and so exponential maps of them are the same.
It is easy to see that
\alpha
is a smooth variation of

by loops in the free homotopy class, i.e.

\alpha(t,0)=\gamma(t),\ \ \alpha(0,s)=\alpha(\ell,s),\ \ \forall s\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon).

(4.14)

\frac{{\partial}^{2}E(\alpha)}{{\partial}s^{2}}\bigg|_{s=0}\geq 0.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
F:(M,g)\rightarrow(M,g)
be an isometry. Suppose to the contrary that

\ell>0
. Suppose
\delta_{F}
takes its minimum at point
p\in M
. Let
\gamma:[0,\ell]\rightarrow M

Pet16

\gamma:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow M

In particular,
\gamma^{\prime}(\ell)=F_{*}\gamma^{\prime}(0)
.

We employ a setup similar to that in the previous paragraph. Let
V(t)=J\gamma^{\prime}(t)
and define a variation
\alpha:[0,\ell]\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to M

\gamma(t)

V(\ell)=J\gamma^{\prime}(\ell)=J\circ F_{*p}(\gamma^{\prime}(0))=F_{*p}(J\gamma^{\prime}(0))=F_{*p}(V(0)).

\alpha(\ell,s)=F\circ\alpha(0,s).

1.4
) in Theorem
1.1
