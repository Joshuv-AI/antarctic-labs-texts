Loss of NCOA6 protein causes aggressive prostate cancer with EGFR overexpression and dependency
Authors: Yonghong Liu, Zhangwei Tong, Qianxing Mo, Xiaohui Peng, Antrix Jain, Sung Yun Jung, Patricia Castro, Mu Yang, Lan Liao, Roberto Mantovani, Fen Wang, Michael M. Ittmann, Jianming Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Loss of tumor suppressors can drive carcinogenesis while simultaneously creating actionable therapeutic vulnerabilities. Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is frequently overexpressed in prostate cancer (PCa), yet EGFR inhibitors have shown limited clinical benefit, and the basis for this variability remains unclear. Here, analysis of 1,719 human PCa specimens reveals frequent downregulation of the nuclear receptor coactivator 6 (NCOA6), particularly in metastatic disease. Genetic loss of NCOA6 accelerates tumor growth in human PCa xenografts, induces prostate tumorigenesis in mice, and cooperates with Pten haploinsufficiency to drive aggressive metastatic PCa. Mechanistically, NCOA6 forms a repressive complex with nuclear transcription factor Y (NFY) at a distal EGFR enhancer, blocking p300 recruitment and limiting EGFR transcription. Loss of NCOA6 derepresses EGFR, causing EGFR overexpression and rendering both androgen-sensitive and castration-resistant PCa highly dependent on EGFR signaling and exquisitely sensitive to EGFR inhibition. These findings identify NCOA6 as a potent tumor suppressor and a clinically relevant biomarker to stratify PCa patients most likely to benefit from EGFR-targeted therapy.

Introduction

Prostate cancers (PCa) in men are common and heterogenous1. Indolent prostate tumors are often overtreated, but aggressive PCa remains lethal although therapies targeting the androgen receptor signaling are initially effective2. Identification and characterization of PCa drivers and suppressors will provide insights into PCa development, improve diagnosis and treatment of aggressive PCa, and avoid overtreatment of indolent prostate tumors. Prostate carcinogenesis is caused by overexpression (OE) and gain-of-function mutation of proto-oncogenes such as TMPRSS2-ERG, PIK3CA, KRAS, NCOA2, EZH2, and MYCN3–6, or by downregulation and loss-of-function mutation of tumor suppressor genes such as PTEN, NKX3.1, p53, SPOP and RB14,7–12. EGFR, a receptor tyrosine kinase, is a protooncogene in the ERBB family, and it plays critical roles in epithelial survival, proliferation, migration, and carcinogenesis13,14. Upon EGF binding, EGFR dimerizes and becomes activated through transphosphorylation, leading to phosphorylation and activation of its downstream signaling components, including JAK/STAT3, PI3K/AKT, and MEK/ERK1/213,14. Aberrant activation of EGFR signaling has been observed in PCa and associated with PCa progression, metastasis, relapse, and poor prognosis15–19. In PCa both inherited and somatic EGFR mutation and amplification are rare, with an amplification rate of 0.29% and a total incidence of 1.22%20. However, EGFR OE is common, which is observed in 18% of European American (EA) PCa and as many as 45% of African American (AA) PCa. EGFR OE is also a stable marker of PCa metastasis21,22. Because AA men possess 1.6-fold and 2.4-fold higher risks than EA men to develop PCa and die of PCa23,24, the higher frequency of EGFR OE in AA PCa has been considered as a contributing factor to the AA PCa disparity21. However, the key regulators and underlying molecular mechanisms for EGFR OE in PCa are still unclear. Furthermore, although increased EGFR is known to drive PCa progression, clinical trials for treating unselected PCa patients with EGFR inhibitors have only achieved limited outcomes25–27, highlighting a need in knowledge and biomarkers for diagnosing PCa vulnerable to anti-EGFR therapies.

NCOA6 (AIB3, ASC-2, PRIP, NRC, TRBP and RAP250) was initially discovered as a coactivator for nuclear receptors such as RAR, PPAR, TR, GR and VDR28–33, and certain other transcription factors (TFs) such as CREB, AP-1, NF-κB, SRF, CEBPα, E2F1, and p5334–37. However, the role of NCOA6 in transcriptional repression and its interactive and functional relationships with the nuclear transcription factor Y (NFY) have not been studied. NFY is a trimeric complex of NFYA, NFYB, and NFYC coded by three different genes essential for cell surviving. NFYB and NFYC form a dimer via their histone-fold domains, which binds to the DNA sugar-phosphate backbone, similarly to H2A/H2B histones within the nucleosome; NFYA provides sequence-specificity, contacting the CCAAT motif located in specific promoters and enhancers, to determine the target genes’ specificity of the trimeric complex38–40. NFY binding is an early transcriptional regulation event critical for histone methylation and acetylation41. The NFY complex can interact with p300 and HDACs to activate or repress transcription42,43. Regulatory relationships between NFY, NCOA6, and EGFR expression, and their integrated roles in PCa, are still unclear.

Gene expression is regulated by TFs and coregulators44. A coregulator can work with multiple TFs and profoundly reprogram genes for cell proliferation, differentiation and transformation44–47. NCOA6 is broadly expressed and required for placenta, heart and liver development, and for the uterus to receive embryo implantation48–52, indicating its essential role in embryonic development and pleiotropic physiological functions in adulthood. Certain overexpressed coregulators such as NCOA3, CTNNB1 and YAP1 are oncogenic factors44–47. NCOA6 (AIB3) was also initially considered as a possible oncogene due to its amplification in breast cancer cells28. However, no clear data from any large cohort of breast tumors have been reported to demonstrate that NCOA6 protein is frequently overexpressed in breast cancer. In contrast to the speculated promotive role of NCOA6 in breast cancer, heterozygous knockout (KO) of Ncoa6 in mice with MMTV-PyMT (polyoma middle T)-induced mammary gland tumorigenesis significantly slowed down tumor initiation and growth, suggesting NCOA6 is a breast cancer suppressor53. Furthermore, KO of MLL3, a component of the NCOA6 protein complex, in a heterozygous Tp53 KO background in mice caused ureter epithelial tumors, because NCOA6 (ASC-2) indirectly interacts with p53 through 53BP1 to bring MLL3 to p53-associated chromatin sites, increasing H3K4me3 to enhance p53-mediated expression of tumor suppressor genes36. The tumor suppressive role of NCOA6 was also suggested by favorable prognosis in renal tumors with high NCOA6 protein54. Intriguingly, opposite results have also been reported. In liver cancer, high NCOA6 protein was associated with poor prognosis54. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), high NCOA6 expression was associated with poor survival, and knockdown of NCOA6 decreased PDAC cell proliferation55. NCOA6 was also shown to be a part of NLRP3 inflammasome, which could be implicated in tumor-promoting inflammation56. These studies suggest that NCOA6 can be either a tumor suppressor or a tumor-promoting factor, which may be dependent on cancer types. Up to date, the role of NCOA6 in prostate development and PCa remains to be defined.

In this study, we find that NCOA6 protein, but not its mRNA, is frequently downregulated in human prostate tumors. KO of Ncoa6 alone and on a Pten-heterozygous genetic background in the mouse prostate causes high-grade prostate intraepithelial neoplasia (HGPIN) and aggressive PCa, respectively. KO of NCOA6 in human PCa cells also accelerates cell proliferation and tumor growth. We also find that NCOA6 KO induces EGFR OE and addiction, which render these PCa cells particularly vulnerable to anti-EGFR therapy. Mechanistically, NCOA6 represses PCa through interacting with NFY TF associated with a distant EGFR enhancer identified in this study to prevent EGFR OE.

Results

NCOA6 protein is frequently downregulated in human PCa

Analysis of TCGA-PRAD datasets (Zuley et al. 2016, the Cancer Genome Atlas prostate adenocarcinoma collection (TCGA-PRAD) (V4), https://doi.org/10.7937/K9/TCIA.2016.YXOGLM4Y) revealed that genetic alterations including mutations, deletions and amplifications in the NCOA6 gene are infrequent, occurring in only 1.8% of prostate tumors. There is no significant difference in NCOA6 mRNA expression levels between prostate normal tissues and tumors (Fig. S1). However, previous studies demonstrated that mRNA-protein correlations in PCa tissues are generally weak and transcriptomic profiling alone may overlook critical protein-level changes that are functionally relevant to tumor progression and therapeutic response57. In our pilot study, we examined the relationship between NCOA6 mRNA and its protein expression levels in 20 human prostate tumors and 15 normal prostate tissues, including 14 pairs of patient-matched tumors and normal tissues. In these 14 paired samples, the expression levels of NCOA6 mRNA were similar in matched tumor and normal tissues, while NCOA6 protein was significantly decreased in 6 tumors compared with their matched normal tissues. Only one tumor had higher NCOA6 protein compared with its matched normal tissue. A number of tumors and normal tissues showed high NCOA6 mRNA with low NCOA6 protein or low NCOA6 mRNA with high NCOA6 protein (Fig. S2a). Linear correlation analysis revealed that there is no positive correlation relationship between NCOA6 mRNA and protein expression levels in these human prostate tumors, normal prostate tissues, or the mixed pool of both (Fig. S2b).

Given the poor correlation of NCOA6 mRNA with its protein expression, we first focused on determining changes of NCOA6 protein expression in PCa cells versus their adjacent non-cancerous prostate epithelial cells (PECs) by immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of NCOA6 in tissue microarrays prepared from 201 EA and 187 AA patients. High and moderate expression levels of NCOA6 protein were detected in non-cancerous PECs, including both basal and luminal epithelial cells. Whereas, variable intensities of nuclear NCOA6 immunoreactivity were detected among both EA and AA primary PCa cells (Fig. S3). Importantly, negative or low expression levels of NCOA6 protein were frequently detected in PCa cells in different tissue cores or same tissue cores of EA and AA PCa tissues containing adjacent normal PECs with high NCOA6 protein expression (Fig. 1a). We also examined NCOA6 protein in metastatic PCa specimens of 41 regional lymph nodes obtained at surgery, as well as 2 bones and one lung obtained from distant metastatic sites. We detected negative or very weak NCOA6 immunoreactivities in the majority of metastatic PCa cells in these PCa metastases (Fig. 1a). Overall, negative or low levels of NCOA6 were detected in 73% of metastatic PCa, 48% of AA primary PCa, and 42% of EA primary PCa, but only in 19% of AA normal prostates and 11% of EA normal prostates (Fig. 1b). Accordingly, the average NCOA6 immunoreactivity scores (IRS) were significantly decreased in both AA and EA PCa compared with their matched adjacent normal prostate tissues, and the average NCOA6 IRS was the lowest in PCa metastases. Interestingly, the NCOA6 IRSs in AA normal and primary PCa were also slightly lower than that in EA normal and primary PCa, respectively (Fig. 1c). To validate above findings, we further examined NCOA6 protein expression in a large PCa cohort with 1287 PCa tissue microarray (TMA) cores. In these TMAs, 566 tumor tissue cores contain adjacent non-cancerous PECs. In many of these cores, NCOA6 immunoreactivity is significantly lower in PCa cells versus adjacent non-cancerous prostate luminal epithelial cells (Fig. S4a). High, moderate and low NCOA6 protein levels in non-cancerous luminal epithelial cells were detected in 45%, 48% and 7% of specimens, respectively. However, high, moderate and low NCOA6 protein levels in PCa cells were detected in 9%, 48% and 44% of samples, respectively, showing a significant decrease in the number of samples with high NCOA6 protein expression (Fig. S4b). Accordingly, the average IRS of NCOA6 in PCa is significantly lower than that in non-cancerous luminal epithelial cells (Fig. S4c). Additionally, correlation analysis revealed no significant association of NCOA6 protein expression with PCa Gleason scores. These results confirmed that NCOA6 protein is indeed frequently downregulated in PCa versus non-cancerous prostate luminal epithelial cells.

Knockout of NCOA6 strongly promotes PCa development, growth and metastasis

To determine the effect of NCOA6 loss in PCa, we knocked out NCOA6 in both 22Rv1 and MDA-PCa-2b PCa cell lines58–60 (Fig. S5a). In culture without any treatment, these KO cells showed no significant increases in growth rates. However, after these cells were inoculated to SCID mice, the xenografts derived from both types of KO cells grew much faster than the xenografts derived from their matched control cells, because NCOA6 KO cells in tumors were significantly more proliferative as indicated by in vivo BrdU incorporation (Fig. 1d–f and Fig. S5b–d).

To define the role of NCOA6 in prostate carcinogenesis, we generated Ncoa6PC−/− mice with PEC-specific Ncoa6 KO by breeding Ncoa6f/f and ARR2PBi-Cre mice49,61. In the prostate of Ncoa6PC−/− mouse, NCOA6 protein is absent in PECs (Fig. 2a). Young Ncoa6PC−/− mice showed normal prostatic morphogenesis. However, by 18 months of age, the Ncoa6PC−/− mouse prostates were much heavier than the control Ncoa6f/f mouse prostates (Fig. 2b). Most of the control Ncoa6f/f mouse prostates were morphologically normal; only 31% of these control mice developed prostate epithelial hyperplasia (PEH) due to aging. In contrast, 86% of Ncoa6PC−/− mice developed PEH and low-grade prostate intraepithelial neoplasia (LGPIN), and 14% of these mice developed HGPIN, which are non-invasive cancers (Fig. 2c, d). We also generated PtenPC−/−;Ncoa6PC−/− mice with PEC-specific homozygous KO of both Pten and Ncoa6, and compared their prostate tumorigenesis with PtenPC−/− mice with only PEC-specific Pten KO. The prostates of PtenPC−/−;Ncoa6PC−/− mice were significantly heavier than those of PtenPC−/− mice starting at 6 weeks of age, and their differences were more significant at ages of 15 and 18 weeks (Fig. S6c). At 18 weeks of age, PtenPC−/− mice developed HGPIN and localized prostate tumors, while PtenPC−/−;Ncoa6PC−/− mice developed large invasive prostate tumors (Fig. S6d). Moreover, to assess the effect of NCOA6 deficiency in the development of castration-resistant PCa (CRPC), we castrated 9-month-old PtenPC+/−;Ncoa6PC−/− and PtenPC+/− mice, as well as 18-week-old PtenPC−/−;Ncoa6PC−/− and PtenPC−/− mice bearing prostate tumors to deplete their androgen produced in testes, and examined their prostate tumor weights at different time points following castration. The average prostate tumor weight of PtenPC+/−; Ncoa6PC−/− mice was about 3-fold heavier than that of the PtenPC+/− control mice at the castration time point, but the ratio was increased to 5 and 4 folds at week 1 and 13 post-castration, respectively (Fig. S7a). Similar profile was also observed from castrated PtenPC−/−; Ncoa6PC−/− mice versus PtenPC−/− mice at week 13 post-castration (Fig. S7b). Collectively, these results demonstrate that NCOA6 is a potent suppressor of PCa initiation, growth, progression and metastasis.

Knockout of NCOA6 strongly activates EGFR signaling pathways and EGF-stimulated PCa cell growth by causing robust EGFR overexpression

To understand the mechanism of NCOA6 as a PCa suppressor, we profiled NCOA6-regulated genes. By comparing differentially expressed mRNA transcripts, we identified 4833 and 1216 NCOA6-downregulated, and 3437 and 1625 NCOA6-upregulated protein-coding genes in the mouse prostate tumors and human 22Rv1 PCa cells, respectively (Fig. S8a, b, Suppl. Data 1, 2, and related profiling data accessible at GEO #: GSE155757). We further knocked in a 3xFLAG tag onto the C-terminus of NCOA6 in 22Rv1 cells (Fig. S8c, d), and performed ChIP-Seq assay using anti-FLAG antibody. We identified 10293 NCOA6-associated genes (Fig. S8e, Suppl. Data 3, and related profiling data accessible at GEO #: GSE155757), and 47 significantly enriched TF-binding motifs, including the CCAAT motif for the trimeric NFYA/B/C TF38,63 (Table S1). Integrated analysis of the above transcriptome and ChIP-Seq data revealed 130 genes associated with and downregulated by NCOA6 (Table S2, & Fig. S8f), and 134 genes associated with and upregulated by NCOA6. (Table S3, & Fig. S8f). Certain genes known to promote PCa progression or metastasis, such as EGFR, SNAI2, FKBP5, and NEAT116,64–66, are in the list of NCOA6-repressed genes. These 264 potential NCOA6 target genes are enriched in 21 signaling pathways, including the EGFR and its downstream ERK-mTOR and PI3K-AKT pathways that can promote PCa development and metastasis (Fig. S8g)17,67–72.

Separate gene set enrichment analyses also identified the EGFR pathway with enriched genes upregulated in NCOA6 KO mouse and human PCa cells (Fig. 4a). The expression of EGFR mRNA was significantly increased in the prostate tumor tissues of PtenPC+/−; Ncoa6PC−/− mice with Ncoa6 KO versus the control prostate tissues of PtenPC+/− mice, and in the NCOA6 KO human 22Rv1 and MDA-PCa-2b PCa cell lines versus their control cells (Fig. 4b). EGFR protein was also robustly elevated in the Ncoa6 KO primary prostate tumors and metastatic tumor cells in the lymph nodes and lungs of PtenPC+/−;Ncoa6PC−/− mice versus the control tissues of PtenPC+/− mice (Fig. 4c, d). NCOA6 represses an EGFR distant enhancer (EGFR-dEn) by preventing p300 recruitment

N6BS1/2 coincidentally overlapped with a H3K27ac-enriched chromatin region, suggesting that N6BS1/2 are located in an EGFR-dEn (Fig. S11b). Indeed, deletion of this EGFR-dEn region strikingly reduced both basal and upregulated EGFR mRNA and protein expression, though still detectable after long-time exposure of Western blotting, resulting in EGF ineffective on activating EGFR singling pathways in both NCOA6 wild type (WT) and KO PCa cells (Fig. S11c, d). Nevertheless, NCOA6 KO did not significantly affect the recruitments of histone deacetylases HDAC1/2/3 to these regions (Fig. S12a, b). NCOA6 is recruited to EGFR-dEn by forming a complex with NFY that binds to CCAAT motifs in N6BS1/2 to prevent EGFR overexpression

Mass spectrometry analysis of NCOA6-coprecipitated proteins identified 107 NCOA6-associated proteins, including all three NFYA/B/C subunits (Supplementary Data 4, related mass spectrometry datasets accessible at MassIVE repository MSV000102930). NFY is the only NCOA6-associated TF with its CCAAT binding motif enriched in the NCOA6-binding peaks identified by ChIP-Seq (Fig. 6e). The N6BS1 and N6BS2 regions also contain one and two NFY-binding motifs (NFYBM1/2/3), respectively (Fig. 6a). Furthermore, NCOA6 forms a protein complex with NFYA/B/C in both 22Rv1 and MDA-PCa-2b cells (Fig. 6f and Fig. S13a). NFYA/B/C bind N6BS1/2 within the EGFR-dEn region independent of NCOA6 (Fig. 6g and Fig. S13b). Knockdown of NFYA, NFYB or NFYC significantly decreases NCOA6 recruitments to N6BS1/2 (Fig. 6h–i), indicating that NFY is required for NCOA6 recruitments. Moreover, mutation of the CCAAT NFYBM1 significantly impaired NFY recruitment to N6BS1 and NCOA6 recruitment to both N6BS1 and N6BS2, and mutations of NFYBM2, NFYBM3, or NFYBM2&3 significantly impaired recruitments of NFY and NCOA6 to both N6BS1 and N6BS2, suggesting that all three NFYBMs are required for effective recruitments of the NFY/NCOA6 complex to N6BS1/2 in the EGFR-dEn region (Fig. S14a, b). Mutations of NFYBM1, NFYBM2, NFYBM3 or NFYBM2&3 robustly increased p300 recruitment and H3K27ac levels at both N6BS1 and N6BS2 regions (Fig. S14b), and also robustly upregulated EGFR mRNA and protein expression (Fig. S14c, and Fig. 6j). These results indicate that the NCOA6/NFYA/B/C complex is recruited to N6BS1/2 in the EGFR-dEn region through NFYA/B/C binding to NFYBM1/2/3 and these recruitments are required for NCOA6 to prevent EGFR OE.

The trimeric NFYA/B/C TF is essential for cell survival. PCa cells with sufficient knockdown or KO of NFYA/B/C do not survive long enough for longer-time experiments, making it hard to define the specific role of NFY–NCOA6–EGFR axis in cell proliferation by using cells with sufficient NFY knockdown or KO. Alternatively, we generated NCOA6 WT and KO 22Rv1 stable cell lines with or without OE of FLAG-tagged NFYA, NFYB, NFYC or NFYA/B/C (Fig. S15a). In 22Rv1 cells, NFYA and NFYB are abundant and NFYC is quite low (Fig. 6f), and NFYB and NFYC first form a stable dimer, and then form trimer with NFYA75,76. Thus, in NCOA6 WT 22Rv1 cells, NFYA OE did not change EGFR expression, NFYB or NFYC OE moderately downregulated EGFR, and only NFYA/B/C co-OE significantly downregulated EGFR (lanes 1-5 in Fig. S15a). However, in NCOA6 KO 22Rv1 cells OE of NFYA, NFYB or NFYC did not affect EGFR OE, and only co-OE of all three subunits slightly downregulated EGFR OE, suggesting an essential role of NCOA6 in NFY-mediated prevention from EGFR OE (lanes 6-10 in Fig. S15a). Similar EGFR expression patterns were also observed in EGF-treated NCOA6 wild type and KO 22Rv1 cells (lanes 12–21 in Fig. S15a). With vehicle treatment, OE of NFYA/B/C individually or together did not change the proliferation of NCOA6 WT and KO 22Rv1 cells (blue bars in Fig. S15b). In EGF-treated 22Rv1 NCOA6 WT cells, NFYA OE had no effect, and NFYB or NFYC OE showed a weak but insignificant effect on cell proliferation versus control cells. Only co-OE of NFYA/B/C together significantly inhibited the proliferation of EGF-treated NCOA6 WT cells. These could be due to the low NCOA6 protein level in the cells, since only a proportion of NFY trimers on enhancers could be associated with available NCOA6 when NFYs were overexpressed. In EGF-treated 22Rv1 NCOA6 KO cells, OE of NFYs individually or together did not significantly alter cell proliferation, suggesting that NCOA6 plays an important role in NFY TF-mediated inhibition of EGFR expression and EGF-stimulated cell proliferation (Fig. S15b).

Erlotinib treatment eliminates NCOA6-deficient primary and metastatic PCa in PtenPC+/−;Ncoa6PC−/− mice

To test whether NCOA6-deficient PCa depends on the overexpressed EGFR to grow and metastasize, we treated 52-week-old PtenPC+/− and PtenPC+/−;Ncoa6PC−/− mice for 3 weeks with placebo or erlotinib, an FDA-approved EGFR inhibitor for treating lung and pancreatic cancers77–79. In placebo- and erlotinib-treated PtenPC+/− mice, the prostates were small and had similar weights (Fig. 7a–c). The total and phosphorylated EGFR, STAT3, AKT and MEK were comparable at low or undetectable levels (Fig. S16a). The proliferation and apoptosis rates of PECs were also low and comparable (Fig. S16b–d). These PtenPC+/− mice did not have any metastasis. NCOA6 knockout and EGFR inhibition cause synthetic death of both androgen-sensitive and castration-resistant human PCa cells in SCID mice

We inoculated NCOA6 control and KO MDA-PCa-2b PCa cells into SCID mice, and treated the mice harboring ~500 mm3 volume of tumors derived from these cells with placebo or erlotinib for 22 days. We also injected NCOA6 control and KO 22Rv1 PCa cells into SCID mice, castrated the mice at day 21 post cell inoculation, and treated these mice with placebo or erlotinib for 22 days. As expected, the placebo-treated tumors derived from NCOA6 KO MDA-PCa-2b and 22Rv1 cells grew about two times faster than the placebo-treated tumors derived from their matched control cells. Interestingly, erlotinib treatment moderately decreased the growth of the tumors derived from the androgen-sensitive control MDA-PCa-2b PCa cells by ~20% (Fig. 7f, g), but the tumors derived from the castration-resistant control 22Rv1 cells were completely resistant to erlotinib treatment (Fig. 7h, i). Strikingly, erlotinib treatment inhibited the growth of tumors derived from both NCOA6 KO MDA-PCa-2b and 22Rv1 cells by ~75% (Fig. 7f–i). Erlotinib treatment inhibited EGFR activity (phosphorylation) without significant effect on EGFR protein levels in tumors derived from control cell lines with low EGFR and NCOA6 KO cell lines with high EGFR (Fig. S17a and Fig. S18a). In agreement with the tumor growth data, erlotinib treatment moderately decreased the proliferation and increased the apoptosis of control MDA-PCa-2b tumor cells (Fig. S17a–c), but had no effects on the proliferation and apoptosis of control 22Rv1 tumor cells (Fig. S18a–c). Importantly, erlotinib treatment dramatically suppressed proliferation and increased apoptosis of both MDA-PCa-2b and 22Rv1 NCOA6 KO tumor cells (Fig. S17a–c, S18a–c). These results indicate that NCOA6 deficiency significantly promotes the growth of both androgen-sensitive PCa and CRPC cells, and simultaneously creates EGFR addiction, rendering these PCa very vulnerable to EGFR inhibitor.

Discussion

A tumor suppressor is commonly downregulated, mutated or deleted in cancer, and loss of a tumor suppressor induces cancer development and progression80. PTEN, NKX3.1, SPOP and p53 are well-established PCa suppressors, and their functional losses mainly caused by mutations, deletions or transcriptional cessation81. In mice, knockout of Pten and Nkx3.1 causes slow-growing invasive PCa and prostatic epithelial hyperplasia, respectively10,62; KO of Spop or Trp53 does not induce obvious prostatic epithelial overgrowth82,83; and KO of Pten and Nkx3.1, Spop or Trp53 synergistically promotes PCa development82–84. In this study, we discovered that NCOA6 protein is downregulated in primary and metastatic PCa. KO of NCOA6 accelerated the growth of xenografts derived from human PCa cells in SCID mice. In genetically engineered mouse models, KO of Ncoa6 causes HGPIN, and KO of Ncoa6 in a Pten heterozygous background results in fast-growing, lethal metastatic PCa. These results indicate that NCOA6 is a potent PCa suppressor whose protein is frequently downregulated in human PCa, and that the PCa suppressor function of NCOA6 is stronger than p53, NKX3.1 and SPOP in mouse models.

NCOA6 is a pleiotropic transcriptional coregulator that most often functions as a coactivator by serving as a scaffold for nuclear receptors and certain other TFs, enabling assembly of coactivator machinery and chromatin-activating activities, including interactions with CBP/p300 and incorporation into NCOA6 (ASC-2) containing complexes (ASCOM) harboring the H3K4 methyltransferases MLL3/MLL4 to promote activating histone marks at target loci85–87. As a coactivator, NCOA6 can either promote cancer growth by enhancing the function of a cancer-facilitating TF or repress cancer growth by enhancing the function of a cancer-suppressing TF. For example, NCOA6 enhances estrogen receptor-regulated target gene expression and estrogen signaling is commonly implicated in estrogen-promoted tumor growth88, while it coactivates PPARγ/RXR and p53 transcriptional activities to repress mammary gland and ureter epithelial carcinogenesis, respectively36,53. On the other hand, accumulating evidence supports a broader “coregulator/integrator” view in which NCOA6 can restrain transcription in a context- and locus-specific manner by modulating the composition and/or activity of recruited cofactor assemblies rather than acting as a dedicated enzymatic writer itself, thereby buffering transcriptional outputs across diverse signaling pathways36,85. Consistent with this integrator model, our data identify an NFY–NCOA6 axis that functions atypically as a *brake* on enhancer activity: NFY and NCOA6 form complex that associates with NFY-binding motifs in an EGFR-dEn identified in this study to prevent EGFR OE (without extinguishing basal expression), because KO of NCOA6 or deletion of NFY-binding motifs de-represses EGFR and is accompanied by increased p300 recruitment, H3K27ac level, and p-S5-Pol2 recruitment at the enhancer; moreover, NFY OE in NCOA6-deficient cells only slightly reduces EGFR levels, indicating that NCOA6 is a key limiting component for enhancer restraint. Together, these findings suggest a mechanistic mode distinct from canonical NCOA6 coactivation via ASCOM/MLL3/4-linked H3K4 methylation and instead support a model in which NFY-NCOA6 restricts enhancer hyperacetylation (p300 gain) to maintain EGFR expression within a physiological range85–87. NFY-NCOA6 possibly plays a role to prevent other TF-mediated p300 recruitment to the enhancer. Identification of specific TFs that recruit p300 to the EGFR-dEn will further uncover the mechanisms for EGFR OE in PCa cells.

In normal prostate, NCOA6 was detected in both basal and luminal epithelial cells (LECs), while EGFR was detected in LECs by IHC. In the examined human cancer cells, which are thought transformed from LECs, both NCOA6 and EGFR were expressed. These observations suggest that NCOA6 does not repress physiological level of EGFR expression in basal cells or transformation-induced EGFR expression in PCa cells, which could be attributed to the activity of other EGFR enhancers. In addition to our finding showing NCOA6 loss causes EGFR overexpression, there may be other factors whose changes induce EGFR overexpression in PCa. The complexity of molecular mechanisms governing cell type-specific expression of EGFR in normal tissues and its induction and overexpression in cancer cells should be far beyond that discovered in this study, and thus, additional studies need to be carried out in the future.

Loss of a tumor suppressor, such as PTEN, often results in activation of cancer-driving pathways, such as the PI3K-AKT pathway89,90. EGFR is a protooncogene, and its signaling activates ERK1/2, AKT and STAT3 to promote PCa67–70. EGFR is overexpressed in a subset of human PCa and its OE is associated with PCa grade, relapse, metastasis and poor prognosis17,71,72. Our results demonstrate that NCOA6 loss-mediated EGFR OE strongly activated STAT3, ERK1/2, and AKT in cultured PCa cells, PCa cell-derived xenograft tumors and mouse spontaneous prostate tumors, and that the EGFR signaling pathway is the major PCa-driving pathway identified in the NCOA6 KO mouse prostate tumors. Our findings explained a complex molecular mechanism for EGFR OE in PCa for the first time, and demonstrated that NCOA6 loss mainly promotes PCa development and progression through EGFR OE-mediated activation of its downstream oncogenic pathways.

We found that the EGFR protein levels and the percentage of EGFRhigh PCa are significantly higher in NCOA6low PCa versus NCOA6moderate and NCOA6high PCa, suggesting that NCOA6 downregulation may be a causative factor for the frequent EGFR OE in human PCa. Importantly, we also found that patients with NCOA6lowEGFRhigh PCa exhibit a significantly shorter survival time in both EA and AA cohorts, indicating that the NCOA6lowEGFRhigh expression profile is associated with poor survival. Some previous studies have reported that AA men have the highest PCa incidence and death rates versus EA men and others23,24. Previous study reported that EGFR OE occurs more frequently in AA PCa versus EA PCa and may drive AA PCa disparity21. We found that the average levels of NCOA6 protein in AA normal prostates and PCa are lower than that in EA normal prostates and PCa, respectively. Future studies will be needed to investigate whether NCOA6 downregulation plays a role in driving AA PCa disparity.

Erlotinib is an FDA-approved EGFR inhibitor for treating NSCLC and pancreatic cancer77. Erlotinib has also been tested for treating unselected PCa patients in phase I/II clinical trials, which only offered limited clinical benefits as monotherapy, with modest prostate-specific antigen (PSA) declines and progression-free survival benefits, prompting a shift toward combination strategies with chemotherapy or androgen receptor pathway inhibitors. Despite these challenges, EGFR is a biomarker of bone metastasis and remains an attractive target, and future trials should be facilitated by biomarker-driven patient selection to enhance clinical benefit22,25–27,91. We found that although both MDA-PCa-2b and 22Rv1 control cells express EGFR, only tumors derived from MDA-PCa-2b control cells moderately respond to erlotinib treatment, while tumors derived from 22Rv1 control cells are completely resistant to erlotinib treatment. Clinically, EGFR inhibitors have shown limited efficacy in unselected prostate cancer populations despite frequent EGFR overexpression. These findings indicate that additional molecular determinants modify EGFR dependency. Importantly, our study showed that erlotinib treatment killed both primary and metastatic Ncoa6 KO PCa cells with EGFR OE in PtenPC+/−;Ncoa6PC−/− mice, and also effectively inhibited the growth of androgen-sensitive xenograft tumors developed from NCOA6 KO MDA-PCa-2b cells and the growth of castration-resistant xenograft tumors developed from NCOA6 KO 22Rv1 cells by 75%. These results indicate that KO of NCOA6 in PCa cells not only causes EGFR OE but also renders PCa cells dependent on EGFR signaling to survival and growth, and thus creates a particular therapeutic vulnerability of the aggressive NCOA6low/EGFRhigh PCa to anti-EGFR therapies. A successful example of such kind of synthetic death therapy is to treat BRCA1-deficient breast and ovarian cancers with PARP inhibitor92. We speculate that the NCOA6low/EGFRhigh expression profile may be useful as a pair of biomarkers to select PCa cases sensitive to anti-EGFR therapies.

Interestingly, MDA-PCa-2b cancer cells are PTEN-deficient, whereas 22Rv1 cancer cells are PTEN-intact, raising a question whether loss of PTEN is necessary for erlotinib to work. Loss of PTEN, a prevalent alteration in advanced prostate cancer, constitutively activates the PI3K–AKT pathway and can cooperate with receptor tyrosine kinases such as EGFR to enhance survival signaling. Our data suggest that NCOA6 loss transcriptionally de-represses EGFR, establishing EGFR addiction even in PTEN-intact PCa cells. Although PTEN deficiency may further amplify downstream PI3K–AKT signalling, NCOA6 loss represents a parallel, transcriptional mechanism converging on EGFR rather than an alternative to PTEN loss.

In summary, NCOA6 is a potent PCa suppressor frequently downregulated in human PCa. NCOA6 restricts EGFR OE in PCa cells by the NCOA6/NFY complex-mediated restriction of the EGFR-dEn activity. Loss of NCOA6 or NFY binding to EGFR-dEn causes EGFR OE and development of fast-growing metastatic PCa, which simultaneously creates an EGFR addiction in these PCa and an opportunity for anti-EGFR therapies (Fig. 8). Methods

Human prostate and PCa tissue microarrays (TMAs)

Frozen human prostate tumors (n = 20) and normal tissues (n = 15) stored at −80 oC, including 14 pairs of patient-matched specimens, were obtained from the Human Tissue Acquisition & Pathology Core at Baylor College of Medicine (BCM). All of these specimens were deidentified, and no clinical and personally identifiable information of patients were provided. Three sets of human prostate tumor TMAs were obtained from the Human Tissue Acquisition & Pathology Core at Baylor College of Medicine (BCM) without any patient’s personal identification information provided. The first set of TMAs with individual patient-matched prostate tumor and adjacent normal prostate tissue specimens were collected from 216 European American (EA) patients and 194 African American (AA) patients. One prostate tumor core and one normal prostate tissue core from each patient were included in this set. A few cores missed prostate or prostate tumor tissues. NCOA6 and EGFR immunoreactivity scores (IRSs) were obtained from 195 normal prostate tissue cores and 201 prostate tumor cores from EA patients, and 177 normal prostate cores and 187 prostate tumor cores from AA patients. The second set of TMAs had primary and metastatic PCa specimens from 44 patients with unknown patient racial information. NCOA6 and EGFR IRSs were obtained from 44 PCa metastasis cores including 41 lymph node, 1 lung, and 2 bone metastases. The third TMA set was prepared from 1712 human primary PCa specimens with Gleason scores but without associated metastasis and survival information available. After IHC staining for NCOA6 with the third TMA set, 1287 tumor tissue cores had PCa cells, and 566 out of these 1287 cores had clear adjacent normal or non-cancerous prostate tissues for data collection. All clinical samples were collected with informed consent at Baylor College of Medicine-affiliated hospitals with the approval of the Baylor College of Medicine Institutional Review Board (H14435 and H11658).

Genetically engineered mouse models

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of BCM. All mice were housed in the Transgenic Mouse Facility of BCM conditioned at 12 h light/dark cycle at 7 am on and 7 pm off, 22 ± 2 oC, and 60% ± 5% humidity. The Ncoa6-floxed (Ncoa6f/f) mouse line was previously generated in our laboratory49. The Pten-floxed (Ptenf/f) mouse line was initially generated in Dr. Hong Wu’s laboratory, and was obtained from Jackson Laboratory62,93. The ARR2PBi-Cre transgenic mice were previously generated in Dr. Fen Wang’s laboratory61. The Ncoa6f/f, Ptenf/f, and ARR2PBi-Cre mouse lines with mixed genetic backgrounds were backcrossed with pure C57BL/6 J mice for six generations. ARR2PBi-Cre;Ncoa6f/f (Ncoa6PC−/−) mice were generated by crossbreeding the backcrossed ARR2PBi-Cre and Ncoa6f/f mice. ARR2PBi-Cre;Ptenf/+ (PtenPC+/−) and ARR2PBi-Cre;Ptenf/f (PtenPC−/−) mice were generated by crossbreeding the backcrossed ARR2PBi-Cre and Ptenf/f mice. ARR2PBi-Cre;Ptenf/+;Ncoa6f/f (PtenPC+/−;Ncoa6PC−/−) and ARR2PBi-Cre;Ptenf/f;Ncoa6f/f (PtenPC−/−;Ncoa6PC−/−) mice were generated by crossbreeding the backcrossed PtenPC+/− and Ncoa6f/f mice. Since only male mice have a prostate, only male ARR2PBi-Cre, Ncoa6PC−/−, PtenPC+/−, PtenPC+/−;Ncoa6PC−/−, PtenPC−/−, and PtenPC−/−;Ncoa6PC−/− mice were used in experiments. Genomic DNA was prepared from a small piece of ear tissue, and subjected to genotype analysis by PCR using wild type and mutant allele-specific oligonucleotide primers as reported in original publications cited above.

Mouse tissue dissection and processing

Male mice were euthanized following complete anesthesia with isoflurane. Euthanized mice were transcardially perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) until the liver became pale. Tissue samples including prostates, lumbar lymph nodes, lungs, and kidneys were collected for experimental analysis. Weights of the prostates and kidneys were measured to determine the relative prostate weight. Each one of the four paired prostate lobes was snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until use. The remaining prostate lobes, lumbar lymph nodes, and lungs were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution at 4 °C overnight. Fixed samples were washed with PBS overnight, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, IHC staining, and double immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were sectioned at a thickness of 4 µm. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol solutions. For H&E staining, rehydrated sections were stained in Harris hematoxylin solution (S216, Poly Scientific R&D Corp) for 2 min and counterstained in eosin Y solution (586X-75, EMD) for 1 min. Sections were dehydrated in ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with DPX Mountant (06522, Sigma).

For IHC staining, rehydrated sections were immersed in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) or 1 mM EDTA buffer (pH 8.0), and heated in a high-pressure cooker for 5 min for antigen retrieval. Sections were incubated with 10% hydrogen peroxide solution in water for 10 min at room temperature to block endogenous peroxidase activity, and washed in PBS with Tween 20 (PBST) buffer once for 5 min. Sections were incubated in a blocking buffer with 10% normal donkey serum at room temperature for 1 h. Sections were incubated with primary antibody diluted in a blocking buffer with 5% normal donkey serum at 4 °C overnight, and washed 4 times in PBST buffer. Sections were incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h, and washed 4 times with PBST buffer. Sections were incubated with VECTASTAIN ABC reagent (PK-6100, Vector Laboratories) at room temperature for 30 min to amplify the signal and washed 4 times with PBST buffer. Sections were incubated with DAB peroxidase substrate solution (SK-4103, Vector Laboratories) until appropriate staining signals developed. Sections were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin solution (S216, Poly Scientific R&D Corp) for 1 min, differentiated with 0.25% (v/v) hydrochloric acid in 70% ethanol for 3 seconds, and blued with 10% saturated lithium carbonate solution (26754-4B, Electron Microscopy Sciences) for 20 s. Counterstained sections were dehydrated in ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with DPX Mountant. The primary and secondary antibodies used in IHC staining experiments were listed in Table S4. IHC images were taken by using a ZEISS AXIOSKOP2 microscope mounted with a SPOT RT3 camera using the matched SPOT imaging software (version 5.0). The brightness and contrast of IHC images were uniformly adjusted, and the uneven noise background was subtracted by using the ‘Subtract Background’ tool in ImageJ94.

For double IF staining, background-blocked sections were incubated with a primary antibody at 4 °C overnight and washed 4 times in PBST buffer. Sections were incubated with a secondary biotinylated antibody at room temperature for 1 h and washed 4 times with PBST buffer. Signals were amplified and developed with the TSA-Alexa Fluor 488 kit (T20932, Thermo Fisher). Then, sections were blocked with Fab fragments at room temperature for 1 h and incubated with a blocking solution containing 10% donkey serum at room temperature for 1 h. Blocked sections were incubated with another primary antibody at 4 °C overnight and washed 4 times in PBST buffer. Sections were incubated with a secondary biotinylated antibody at room temperature for 1 h and washed 4 times with PBST buffer. Signals were amplified and developed with the TSA-Alexa 594 kit (T20935, Thermo Fisher). Sections were counterstained with 4′, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) solution for 5 min, washed twice with PBST, and mounted with water-soluble Fluoromount-G mounting medium (0100-01, SouthernBiotech). The primary and secondary antibodies used in double IF staining experiments were listed in Table S4. Images were taken under a fluorescence microscope with UV lighting and appropriate filters using a SPOT RT3 camera.

Immunoreactivity scoring

The immunoreactivities of NCOA6 and EGFR IHC on human prostate and PCa TMAs were scored according to a previously established scoring system95. Specifically, the intensity of immunoreactivity for NCOA6 in the nucleus and for EGFR in the cell membrane in the prostate epithelial and tumor cell compartment was scored on a scale of 0 (Negative), 1 (Weak), 2 (Moderate), and 3 (Strong). The percentage of positively stained cells in the same compartment was scored on a scale of 0 (0%), 1 ( < 10%), 2 (10-50%), 3 (50-80%), and 4 ( > 80%). The IRS of NCOA6 or EGFR for each specimen was obtained by multiplying the intensity score by the percentage score, giving a score range from 0 to 12.

Cell lines

MDA-PCa-2b human PCa cells were obtained from ATCC. 22Rv1, C4-2 and VCaP human PCa cells and HEK293T human embryonic kidney cells were initially obtained from ATCC by our Tissue & Cell Culture Core at BCM and subsequently made available to this study. The cell lines were authenticated by SRT profiling and confirmed being mycoplasma free by using MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit in the Tissue & Cell Culture Core. MDA-PCa-2b cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium (10-090-CV; Corning) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10437028; Thermo Fisher), 10 ng/ml EGF (AF-100-15; PeproTech), 0.005 mg/ml insulin (I0516; Sigma), and 1% penicillin (10000 IU)/streptomycin (10000 µg/ml) solution (30-002-CI; Corning). 22Rv1 and C4-2 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (12-702 F; Lonza) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin (10000 IU)/streptomycin (10,000 µg/ml) solution. HEK293T and VCaP cells were cultured in DMEM medium (10-013-CV; Corning) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin (10,000 IU)/streptomycin (10,000 µg/ml) solution. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 cell culture incubator.

Cell transfection, generation of knockdown and overexpression cell lines, and CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing

For knocking down NFYA, NFYB, and NFYC mRNAs, the GIPZ lentiviral vector was used to express each shRNA. Two different shRNAs were used to target each mRNA molecule. The sequences of shRNAs used to knocking down NFYA, NFYB and NFYC mRNAs were listed in Table S5. Lentiviral particles were produced by transfecting HEK293T cells with the GIPZ lentiviral vector for expressing a specific shRNA, and the pMD2.G and psPAX2 plasmids for expressing lentiviral packaging proteins. Lentivirus-containing medium was collected 3 days after transfection and used to infect 22Rv1NCOA6-3FLAG #67 cells in culture. Infected cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 complete medium containing 5 µg/ml puromycin for 7 days to select stable shRNA-expressing cells. Survived individual clones were isolated, expanded, and screened by RT-qPCR and Western blotting for determining the knockdown efficiency of NFYA, NFYB or NFYC. Two best clones for each single knockdown of NFYA, NFYB or NFYC were established for experiments. Control cell lines were generated by infecting 22Rv1NCOA6-3FLAG #67 cells with lentiviral particles for expression of non-targeting shRNA.

To generate NFYA, NFYB, NFYC or NFYA/B/C OE cell lines, the FLAG-tagged pGenLenti lentiviral plasmids for expressing NFYA (OHu09041), NFYB (OHu05282), and NFYC (OHu07557) were obtained from GenScript. Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with a lentiviral expression plasmid and pMD2.G and psPAX2 plasmids for packaging. Lentivirus-containing medium was collected 3 days after transfection. Individual medium was used to transduce 22Rv1 control and NCOA6 KO (KO4) cell lines for NFYA, NFYB or NFYC OE, while their mixture was used to transduce these cells for co-OE of NFYA/B/C. Transduced cells were selected in RPMI-1640 complete medium containing 5 µg/ml puromycin for 7 days to establish stable OE cell lines. Parallel empty control cell lines were generated using the empty pGenLenti lentiviral plasmid.

For CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing96, DNA oligonucleotides coding for single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designed by using an online CRISPR Design Tool (http://crispr.mit.edu). The sense and antisense strands of DNA oligonucleotides for each sgRNA were synthesized, annealed into double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) fragment, and ligated into the PX459 plasmid (48139, Addgene) for expressing both Cas9 and sgRNA. Two plasmids were constructed for disrupting NCOA6, with one targeting exon 5 and the other targeting exon 6. These two plasmids were separately introduced into MDA-PCa-2b, C4-2, VCaP, and 22Rv1 cells by using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Thermo Fisher). For inserting the 3FLAG coding sequence between the last a.a. codon and the STOP codon of NCOA6, single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) donor template with short homologous arms and the plasmid for co-expressing Cas9 and sgRNA targeting a site near the STOP codon of NCOA6 were co-introduced into 22Rv1 cells by using Lipofectamine 3000. For mutating the NFY binding sites located in the EGFR distant enhancer (EGFR-dEn), plasmids for expressing Cas9 and site-specific sgRNAs were constructed and introduced into the 22Rv1NCOA6-3FLAG #100 cells by using Lipofectamine 3000 reagents. The sequences of DNA oligonucleotides coding for all sgRNAs and ssDNA donor templates were summarized in Table S5. The transfected cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 complete medium for one day and then treated with 5 µg/ml of puromycin (P8833, Sigma) for 2 days to select cells with transient expression of Cas9 and sgRNA. Selected cells were seeded into 150 mm dishes at a density of 200 cells per dish to grow individual clones. After culturing for 2−3 weeks, individual clones were picked up and expanded in 24-well dishes (1 clone per well). Positive clones were identified by PCR-based genotyping, DNA sequencing, and Western blot analysis.

MTS cell viability assay

Parental, control and NCOA6 KO cell lines derived from MDA-PCa-2b, 22Rv1, C4-2 and VCaP human PCa cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2,000 cells per well. Cells were cultured for 1, 3, or 5 days in the presence or absence of EGF (100 ng/ml). To measure cell viability, 20 µl CellTiter 96 Aqueous One Solution (G3580, Promega) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. Empty control, and NFYA, NFYB, NFYC, and NFYA/B/C OE cell lines derived from 22Rv1 control and NCOA6 KO4 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells per well. Cells were cultured for 3 days in the presence or absence of EGF (100 ng/ml). To measure cell viability, 10 µl CellTiter 96 Aqueous One Solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a Synergy HT plate reader (BioTek).

Xenograft tumor growth assay

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the animal protocols approved by the IACUC of BCM. The control (Ctrl) and NCOA6 knockout #1 (KO1) and knockout #3 (KO3) cells derived from MDA-PCa-2b cells were suspended in Matrigel (356231, BD Bioscience) at a concentration of 5 × 107 cells per ml. Cell suspensions with 5×106 cells in 0.1 ml Matrigel were subcutaneously injected into the left dorsal flank of male SCID mice aged 20 weeks. The Ctrl, NCOA6 KO2, and NCOA6 KO4 cells derived from 22Rv1 cells were suspended in Matrigel (356231, BD Bioscience) at a concentration of 2 × 107 cells per ml. Cell suspensions with 2 × 106 cells in 0.1 ml Matrigel were subcutaneously injected into the left dorsal flank of male SCID mice aged 20 weeks. The length and width of xenograft tumors were measured with a caliper every 7 days after injection and every 2 days after treatment initiation. The tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: Volume = 0.5 x (Length x Width2). The maximal tumor size permitted under our approved animal protocol was 3 cm in diameter in order to study metastasis, and no tumor in any of our experiments reached this limit. Mice used in experiments of Figs. 1d, 7h and Fig. S5b were euthanized once any mouse reached a predefined humane endpoint, including a maximum tumor diameter of 2.5 cm, a maximum tumor-ulceration diameter of 0.2 cm, a 20% body-weight loss from baseline, or a significant decline in overall health condition. Mice used in the experiment of Fig. 7f were euthanized on day 22 after treatment initiation, the earliest time point when any mouse under the treatment reached the same predefined humane endpoints described above. All xenograft tumors were collected for characterization, and lungs of all mice were examined macroscopically for evidence of metastasis.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells and male mouse prostate tissues and tumors using TRIzol reagent (15596018, Thermo Fisher). Two µg of total RNA was reversely transcribed into cDNA using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (4368813, Thermo Fisher). Real-time qPCR analysis was performed using gene-specific primers and the matched TaqMan probes designed in the Roche Universal ProbeLibrary Assay Design Center. The 18S ribosomal RNA, GAPDH, or β-actin RNA was used as internal controls for normalization. The primers and the matched probes were summarized in Table S5. For RNA-Seq assay, total RNA was extracted from mouse prostate tissues and tumors using TRIzol reagent followed by further purification with the RNeasy Mini Kit (74106, Qiagen).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), Western blotting (WB), and mass spectrometry (MS) analysis

Cells were lysed with a RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 150 mM sodium chloride, 1% NP40, 0.25% sodium deoxycholate) supplemented with 1% (v/v) protease inhibitor cocktail solution (50 µM PMSF, 10 µM pepstatin A, 20 µM leupeptin, 100 µM benzamidine, 50 µM bestatin) (P3100, GenDEPOT) and 1% (v/v) phosphatase inhibitor cocktail solution (sodium fluoride, sodium orthovanadate, sodium pyrophosphate, sodium glycerophosphate) (P3200, GenDEPOT). Total protein concentration was measured using bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (23225, Thermo Fisher) and adjusted to 2 µg/µl. For Co-IP and WB assays, 40 µl of total cell extract with 80 µg total protein was diluted with 40 µl RIPA buffer to a total volume of 80 µl as an input control. One ml total cell extract (2 mg total protein) was incubated with 1 µg of primary antibody and 20 µl of protein A/G-coupled agarose beads (sc-2003, Santa Cruz Biotechnology). The negative control for Co-IP was concurrently set up by incubating the same amount of total cell extract with 1 µg of non-immune IgG and 20 µl of protein A/G-coupled agarose beads. After overnight incubation at 4 °C, beads were washed with the RIPA buffer 4 times and suspended in 80 µl RIPA buffer. The co-precipitated proteins on the beads and the input control samples were mixed with 20 µl of 5x SDS-PAGE reducing sample buffer (786-701, G-Biosciences) and boiled 10 min. Proteins in equal sample volumes were separated in SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (1620115, Bio-Rad). Protein-transferred nitrocellulose membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h, incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, washed 4 times in PBST, incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h, and washed 4 times in PBST. The activity of the bound HRP was detected using the enhanced chemiluminescent (ECL) Western blotting substrates (32106, Pierce). The primary and secondary antibodies used in Co-IP and WB assays were listed in Table S4.

For Co-IP and MS analysis, 22Rv1 cells from ten 150 mm cell culture dishes at 90-100% confluency were harvested and lysed in the RIPA buffer supplemented with 1% (v/v) protease inhibitor cocktail and 1% (v/v) phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. Chromatin DNA was sheared by sonication, and total cell extracts were cleaned by centrifugation at 75,000 g for 20 min. Ten mg protein in a part of cleaned cell extracts was incubated with 1 µg of anti-NCOA6 antibody (HPA004198, Sigma) and 20 µl of protein A/G-coupled agarose beads. The negative control for Co-IP was concurrently set up by incubating 10 mg protein in another part of cleaned cell extracts with 1 µg of non-immune rabbit IgG (sc-2027, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and 20 µl of protein A/G-coupled agarose beads. NCOA6 immuno-affinity beads were washed, boiled in 1X NuPAGE LDS sample buffer (NP0007, Invitrogen), and subjected to NuPAGE 10% Bis-Tris Gel (NP0301BOX, Invitrogen). The protein bands were visualized with Coomassie Brilliant blue-stain and sliced into gel pieces according to molecular sizes. The gel pieces were destained and subjected to in-gel digestion using trypsin (T9600, GenDepot). The digested peptides were collected, vacuum dried and resuspended in loading solution containing 5% methanol and 0.1% formic acid. A nano-LC 1000 system (Thermo Scientific) coupled to Q Exactive Plus (Thermo Scientific) Mass Spectrometer was used to analyze the peptides. An in-housed precolumn of 2 cm × 100 µm size (Reprosil-Pur Basic C18, 3 µm, Dr.Maisch GmbH, Germany) and an in-housed 50 mm × 150 µm analytical column packed with C18 beads (Reprosil-Pur Basic C18, 1.9 µm, Dr. Maisch GmbH, Germany) were used to enrich and separate the peptides. The peptides were eluted by using 75 min of discontinuous gradient of 4-26% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid at a flow rate of 800 nl/min. The mass spectrometer operated in the data-dependent acquisition mode acquiring fragmentation spectra of the top 50 strongest ions and under the direct control of Xcalibur software (Thermo Scientific). Obtained MS/MS spectra were searched against target-decoy human refseq database in Proteome Discoverer 1.4 interface (Thermo Fisher) with Mascot algorithm (Mascot 2.4, Matrix Science). The precursor mass tolerance was confined within 20 ppm with fragment mass tolerance of 0.02 dalton and a maximum of two missed cleavage allowed. Dynamic modification of oxidation of methionine was allowed. The peptides identified from mascot result file were validated with 5% false discover rate (FDR). Gene quantification was performed using the label-free, intensity-based absolute quantification (iBAQ) approach by ‘gpGrouper’ algorithm97.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) followed by qPCR analysis

ChIP assays were performed using the SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Magnetic Beads) (9005S, Cell Signaling Technology) by following the manufacturer’s instructions. Antibodies used for ChIP assays were listed in Table S4. Real-time qPCR analysis was performed using primers and the matched TaqMan probes designed by the software of Roche Universal ProbeLibrary Assay Design Center. The primers for qPCR analyses were listed in Table S5.

Erlotinib treatment of mouse models

Captisol powder (NC-04A-160154, Captisol Technology) was dissolved in sterilized Milli-Q water to prepare 15% (w/v) Captisol solution as vehicle. Erlotinib HCI (OSI-744) powder (S1023, Selleckchem) was dissolved in 15% Captisol solution at a concentration of 10 mg/ml. Fifty-two-week-old male PtenPC+/− and PtenPC+/−;Ncoa6PC−/− mice were orally fed with either 15% Captisol solution (5 ml/kg body weight/day) as placebo control or erlotinib solution (50 mg/kg body weight/day) for 21 consecutive days. Mice were euthanized and tissue samples were collected 1 day after the last treatment. Xenograft SCID mouse models were established as described in a preceding section. Male SCID mice with 0.5 cm3 size xenograft tumors derived from control (Ctrl) and NCOA6 knockout (KO1/3) MDA-PCa-2b cells were orally fed once every other day for a total period of 3 weeks with placebo (15% Captisol solution, 5 ml/kg body weight) or erlotinib solution (50 mg/kg body weight). Male SCID mice inoculated with control (Ctrl) and NCOA6 knockout (KO2/4) 22Rv1 cells on day 1 were surgically castrated on day 21, and orally fed with same dosages of placebo or erlotinib solution from day 27 through day 48. The treated SCID mice were euthanized and their tumor and tissue samples were collected on day 49 for histopathological and biochemical analyses. By day 49, no tumor diameter exceeded 3 cm and no mice showed significant body-weight loss or signs of significant pain.

Microarray-based RNA profiling assay

RNA samples were isolated from 22Rv1 parent cells and 22Rv1-derived control cells, and four NCOA6 knockout cell lines derived from 22Rv1 cells. Sample quality was confirmed by checking with a Nanodrop Spectrophotometer and an Agilent Bioanalyzer Nano Chip. Fifty ng of total RNA was processed using the Agilent’s RNA Spike-Ins kit. The samples were amplified and labeled with Cy3 by using the Agilent Quick Amp Labeling Kit (p/n 5190-2331, Agilent). The Cy3-labeled cRNA samples were purified using Qiagen RNeasy mini spin columns and assayed on Nanodrop. After the samples were fragmented, 0.6 μg fragmented sample and hybridization mix were loaded onto each Agilent G3 Human Gene Expression v3 8x60K Expression arrays (G4851C, Agilent). The slides were hybridized in Agilent Hybridization Chamber at 65 °C at a rotation rate of 10 rpm for 17 h. The hybridized slides were washed with the Agilent Expression Wash Buffer Sets 1 and 2. Dried slides were scanned with the Agilent Scanner (G2565BA, Agilent). Data extraction and quality assessment of the microarray data were performed using Agilent Feature Extraction Software (version 11.0.1.1).

Microarray data were analyzed using the Bioconductor “limma” software package (http://bioconductor.org)98. Data background was corrected using the “normexp” method with an offset of 16 added to the intensities99. The background-corrected data were then log2-transformed and quantile-normalized. Moderated t-statistics was used to determine whether genes were differentially expressed between control cell lines (22Rv1 parent and vector control cell lines) and NCOA6 knockout cell lines. Benjamini-Hochberg method was used to estimate false discovery rate (FDR). FDR ≤ 0.05 and fold change ≥ 1.5 were considered statistically significant.

RNA-Seq analysis

RNA samples isolated from 4 prostates of PtenPC+/− and 4 prostates of PtenPC+/−;Ncoa6PC−/− mice were used for RNA-Seq analysis. All samples passed quality control as assayed by a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer and an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. RNA samples were quantified using Picogreen, and 250 ng of total RNA was used to construct each double-stranded DNA library. ERCC RNA Spike-In Controls were added to each sample. The fragmented and 3’ poly (A)-selected portion of total RNA together with random primers were used to generate cDNAs. In the process of second strand synthesis, dTTP was replaced with dUTP to quench the second strand during amplification and thereby achieve strand specificity. The created cDNA fragments were blunt-ended, attached with an adenosine to the 3’ end, and ligated with unique adapters to the end. The ligated products were amplified with 15 PCR cycles. The resulting libraries were quantitated using a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer, and the fragment sizes were assessed with an Agilent Bioanalyzer. To determine the concentration of adapter-ligated fragments, the libraries were quantitated by qPCR using the Applied Biosystems ViiA7 Real-Time PCR System and a KAPA Library Quant Kit. Sixteen individual libraries were equimolarly pooled at a concentration of 29 pM. The pooled library was loaded to three lanes of a high-output flow cell and amplified by bridge amplification using the Illumina cBot instrument. PhiX Control v3 adapter-ligated library was spiked-in at 2% by weight to ensure balanced diversity and to monitor clustering and sequencing performance. A paired-end 100 cycle run was used to sequence the flow cell on a HiSeq 2500 instrument with V4 chemistry. At a later date, a second pool of 16 additional sample libraries was loaded to three lanes of a second high-output flow cell and loaded with the same conditions and chemistry.

The paired-end reads were mapped to the mouse genome (UCSC mm10) using STAR with NCBI RefSeq genes as the reference. 100. STAR generated read counts for each gene were used as the measurement of gene expression. DESeq2 was used to analyze the gene-based read counts to detect differentially expressed genes between the groups of interest101. The FDR of the differentially expressed genes was estimated using Benjamini-Hochberg method. FDR ≤ 0.05 and fold change ≥ 1.5 were considered statistically significant.

ChIP-Seq profiling and data analysis

DNA samples were obtained from 22Rv1 parent cells and 22Rv1-derived NCOA6-3FLAG knock-in cell lines with two biological repeats (#67 and #100) by ChIP using FLAG antibody and magnetic beads. Sample quality checks were performed by using the NanoDrop Spectrophotometer, Invitrogen Qubit 2.0 DNA quantitation assay, and Agilent Bioanalyzer. The Rubicon ThruPlex DNA-Seq library preparation system (p/n R400523, protocol QAM-328-001) was used to prepare ChIP-Seq libraries for sequencing on the Illumina NGS sequencing instruments. One ng of ChIP’d DNA was used for generating a double-stranded DNA library. The genomic DNA fragments were blunt-ended and then the 5’ end of the fragments were ligated with stem-loop adapters with blocked 5’ end, leaving a nick at the 3’ end. Then, the 3’ end was extended during library synthesis, and Illumina-compatible indexes were incorporated with 11 amplification cycles. The fragments were purified by using the AMPure XP Bead Purification System. Fragments with a size below 200 bp and unincorporated PCR reagents and nucleotides were removed. The resulting libraries were quantitated by using the NanoDrop ND-1000 Spectrophotometer and the fragment sizes were assessed with the Agilent Bioanalyzer. To determine the concentration of adapter ligated fragments, the libraries were quantitated by qPCR using the Applied Biosystems ViiA7 TM Real-Time PCR System and a KAPA Library Quant Kit. Fourteen pM of equimolarly pooled library was loaded to two lanes of a TruSeq HiSeq Rapid v2 Single Read flow cell and amplified by bridge amplification using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencing instrument. PhiX Control v3 adapter-ligated library was spiked-in at 2% by weight to ensure balanced diversity and to monitor clustering and sequencing performance. A single-end 50 cycle run was used to sequence the flow cell on a HiSeq Sequencing System in Rapid Mode with v2 chemistry.

The sequence reads were mapped to the reference genome hg19 using software BWA102. Duplicate reads were removed using Picard tools (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Software MACS and iSeq were used for peak calling103,104. Since the peaks were highly overlapped, the results generated by iSeq were used. For the iSeq method, a region with the posterior probability > 0.9 was claimed to be a binding region.

The TDF files of NCOA6 ChIP-Seq data were generated by using software igvtools105,106. The bigWig file of H3K27ac ChIP-Seq data was generated in ENCODE project and downloaded from UCSC website107. The binding peaks of TDF and bigWig files were visualized using the software Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV)105,106.

The online software tool MEME-ChIP was employed to discover TF binding motifs in the ±200 bp regions around the predicted NCOA6-binding peaks identified by ChIP-Seq analysis108. A motif with an E-value ≤ 0.05 was considered as a significantly enriched motif.

Heatmaps and Venn diagrams

The normalized intensity of differentially expressed genes identified by microarray analysis and the reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM) of differentially expressed genes from RNA-Seq analysis were transformed into Z-Scores. The Z-Scores were plotted as Heatmaps by using the software package of “ComplexHeatmap” in RStudio109. Venn diagrams were plotted by using the software package of “VennDiagram” in RStudio110.

Pathway and gene set enrichment analyses

Differentially expressed genes were identified by comparing gene expression profiles between sample groups of interest. Pathway enrichment analysis of these differentially expressed genes was performed using the web-based software tool DAVID Bioinformatics Resources111. p-value ≤ 0.1 was considered statistically significant.

For gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), three input files including an expression data file, a phenotype data file, and a gene set database are required. Normalized intensities of all probes obtained from microarray analysis and RPKMs of all genes obtained from RNA-Seq analysis were used as expression data files. Phenotype data files were generated based on comparisons between groups of interest. Gene sets deposited in Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB) were used as gene set database. Based on the GSEA guideline, P-value ≤ 0.05 and FDR q-value ≤ 0.25 were considered statistically significant112,113.

Statistical analysis

Data from 3 or more independent samples or experiments were presented as Mean ± SEM. Two-tailed Student’s t-test and One-Way ANOVA test were used to calculate the P-value for comparing the means of two and more groups of data, respectively. Chi-Square test was used to calculate the P-value for comparing the distributions of countable data groups. Log-rank test was used to calculate the p-value for comparing the overall survival data groups. The difference was considered statistically significant if p-value ≤ 0.05.
