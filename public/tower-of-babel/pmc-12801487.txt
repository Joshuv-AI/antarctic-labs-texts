Clinical features and treatment outcomes of Mycobacterium colombiense pulmonary disease: a retrospective study
Authors: Yuanjie Li, Yuan Wang, Yaoxi Chen, Shuang Zhao, Zhixin Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMycobacterium colombiense (M. col), a rare member of the Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), primarily infects immunocompromised individuals and often causes disseminated disease with high mortality. Clinical understanding of its manifestations and outcomes remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study at a single tertiary center in Sichuan, China, reviewing records of patients diagnosed with nontuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) from 2009 to 2024. Data collected included demographics, clinical symptoms, imaging findings, laboratory results, treatment regimens, and outcomes. Comparative analyses were performed between patients with M. col pulmonary infection and those with other MAC pulmonary infections.ResultsAmong 299 NTM-PD cases, 134 (44.8%) were diagnosed with MAC pulmonary infection, including 11 (3.7%) confirmed as M. col pulmonary infection. Compared to other MAC infections, patients with M. col exhibited higher rates of disseminated disease (36.4% vs. 7.3%, p < 0.05), fever (45.5%), dyspnea (72.7%), and concurrent infections (72.7% vs. 37.4%, p < 0.05), along with elevated eosinophil counts and lower hemoglobin levels. Imaging findings and antibiotic susceptibility showed no significant differences compared to other MAC infections. Treatment outcomes were comparable between the two groups when guideline-based regimens were followed.ConclusionM. col demonstrates a predilection for extrapulmonary dissemination and polymicrobial infections, exhibiting distinct clinical and laboratory features compared to other MAC subspecies, but responds similarly to standard MAC antibiotic therapy.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12294-3.

Background

Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), comprising more than 190 species and subspecies, include pathogenic strains capable of causing infections in individuals of all ages, with manifestations occurring in both pulmonary and extrapulmonary sites [1]. Mycobacterium colombiense (M. col) is a rare NTM that belongs to the Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC). As an opportunistic pathogen, M. col primarily infects immunocompromised individuals, particularly those with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection [2]. Clinical manifestations typically include respiratory symptoms (e.g., fever, productive cough, dyspnea) and lymphadenopathy [3]. Due to its nonspecific clinical features, M. col infections are frequently misdiagnosed or overlooked, resulting in delayed treatment and high mortality rates [2–4].

Given the limited clinical understanding of M. col, we retrospectively analyzed the clinical characteristics and outcomes of patients with M. col pulmonary infection to clarify its distinct presentations and prognosis.

Methods

Patient collection

We conducted a retrospective study of patients with NTM pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) at West China Hospital of Sichuan University, a national tertiary referral center in southwestern China, between March 2009 and June 2024. This institution routinely receives complex and refractory referrals from across Sichuan province and nationwide. From this cohort, we identified all patients with pulmonary infection caused by M. col. Demographic, clinical, therapeutic, and outcome data were systematically collected. The diagnosis of NTM-PD was strictly based on the 2020 ATS/IDSA guidelines [1]. Patients with incomplete medical records or indeterminate pathogen identification were excluded (Fig. 1).

Clinical evaluation

Two independent investigators (LYJ and CYX) collected clinical variables, including (1) demographic characteristics, (2) symptoms, (3) laboratory data, (4) treatment regimens, and (5) clinical outcomes. To ensure standardized radiological assessment, all imaging studies were independently reviewed by two pulmonologists (WY and ZS). Any diagnostic discrepancies were resolved by a senior pulmonologist (QZX).

Laboratory testing procedures

Clinical specimens were pretreated using standard N-acetyl-L-cysteine/NaOH decontamination method and then cultured using both the automated BD BACTEC™ MGIT™ 960 system (Mycobacteria Growth Indicator Tube) and conventional Löwenstein-Jensen slants until visible growth was detected. Species identification from positive cultures was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) [5]. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values for antimicrobial agents were determined using Mycobacterium Drug Susceptibility Löwenstein-Jensen Medium (Yinke, China). Anti-mycobacterial susceptibility was assessed based on MIC breakpoints, following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M24S 2018 guidelines. For antimicrobials not covered by CLSI recommendations for MAC, such as cefoxitin and imipenem, breakpoints established for other NTM (e.g., Mycobacterium kansasii) or Mycobacterium tuberculosis, as referenced in CLSI M24-A3 and relevant literature, were applied. The specific metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) workflow for patient samples in our hospital includes: sample collection, reception, nucleic acid extraction, DNA processing (enzymatic digestion and bead beating), RNA processing (viral enrichment technology), library construction, high-throughput sequencing, data analysis, and report interpretation. The primary sequencing platforms used were BGI (formerly Beijing Genomics Institute) and Illumina systems [6]. The diagnostic threshold for NTM was defined as ≥ 3 sequence reads. All experiments were conducted in the microbiology laboratory of our hospital.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons for categorical variables were conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected counts were less than five. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data or Student’s t-test for normally distributed data. Baseline characteristics were compared between the M. col group and other MAC groups. Imaging findings and laboratory data were evaluated for group differences. Drug susceptibility results for M. col and other MAC isolates were categorized according to clinical guidelines. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3).

Given the limited sample size of the M. col cohort (n = 11), all statistical comparisons involving this group are considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. P-values are provided for descriptive purposes and should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, the focus is on describing the clinical characteristics and observed trends rather than making definitive statistical inferences.

Results

Epidemiological characteristics

Among 321 patients with NTM-PD, 22 cases lacked species identification. Of the remaining 299 patients, 134 (44.8%) were diagnosed with MAC infections, including 11 (3.7%) definitively identified as M. col infections (Figure S1). The M. col cohort consisted of 4 females and 7 males, with a median age of 61 years (range: 31–82). Comparative analysis of epidemiological characteristics between M. col and other MAC infections showed no statistically significant differences in demographic features, underlying comorbidities, or chronic pulmonary diseases—specifically bronchiectasis, immunologic disorders, or acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics

Among the 11 patients with M. col infection, 4 (36.4%) developed disseminated disease involving extrapulmonary sites (bones/joints, lymph nodes, and skin). This proportion was numerically higher than that observed in other MAC patients (9/123, 7.3%; p < 0.05), whose extrapulmonary manifestations also included intracranial and intra-abdominal infections. The most common respiratory symptoms in M. col patients were cough (81.8%), expectoration (72.7%), dyspnea (72.7%), and fever (45.5%). Compared to other MAC patients, those with M. col infection showed trends toward higher frequencies of fever (p < 0.05) and dyspnea (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Examination and diagnosis

Analysis of pulmonary CT findings in 124 MAC patients revealed predominantly bilateral involvement (57.3%). Based on characteristic imaging patterns, patients were categorized into three radiological subtypes: bronchiectasis, cavity, and nodular/patchy opacity. Comparative analysis indicated similar imaging manifestations between M. col and other MAC pulmonary infections (Table 2). Laboratory evaluation of M. col patients (n = 11) showed reduced hemoglobin levels (median: 101 g/L) (Table 2). White blood cell counts were generally normal or elevated (median: 6.0 × 10⁹/L), with only one case of leukopenia. CD4 + T-cell count assessment in six patients (median: 291 cells/µL) identified three cases of cellular immunodeficiency, all in patients with isolated pulmonary infection. Compared to other MAC patients, those with M. col infection exhibited trends toward higher eosinophil counts (p < 0.01), lower hemoglobin levels (p < 0.01), and increased rates of co-infection with other pathogens (72.7% vs. 37.4%, p < 0.05).

All M. col cases were definitively diagnosed through pathogenic testing using smear microscopy and microbial culture. Clinical specimens included sputum, BALF, lung tissue, and purulent discharge (Table S1). Notably, among 321 patients with NTM infections, mNGS was performed on clinical samples from 160 individuals, detecting NTM in 149 cases (93.1%). All mNGS reports were available within 48 h of sample submission, significantly reducing the time to pathogen identification.

Treatments and prognosis

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed on 49 MAC isolates, including six M. col isolates. Amikacin, rifampin, and rifabutin demonstrated the lowest MIC values (Figure S1). Analysis of M. col susceptibility to nine antibiotic classes revealed similar patterns to other MAC species (Figure S1).

Of 134 MAC patients, 41 completed full antibiotic courses with concurrent symptom relief, radiographic improvement, and microbiological clearance. This included 5 of 11 M. col patients meeting treatment success criteria. Treatment duration for M. col pulmonary infection appeared comparable to other MAC infections (Table 2). Following diagnosis, all M. col patients received antibiotic therapy (typically ethambutol, macrolides, rifampin, and amikacin). Only one patient discontinued treatment due to adverse effects. Ten patients completed 3–24 months of therapy and showed clinical improvement (Table S2). In the asymptomatic patient, 9-month follow-up revealed reduced nodules, improved pleural thickening and effusion on CT, and normalized hemoglobin levels (Fig. 2).

Discussion

M. col, first isolated from HIV-infected patients in Colombia in 2006, is a slow-growing, non-pigmented, acid-fast bacillus that belongs to the MAC and exhibits unique phenotypic features [7–9]. Its pathogenic mechanisms, including the ability to modulate apoptosis and inhibit phagosome-lysosome fusion, may contribute to a heightened virulence particularly evident in immunocompromised hosts [10]. Despite its established taxonomic position, the clinical profile and management of M. col infection remain inadequately characterized due to its rarity and the predominance of case-based reports in the literature.

Our study represents one of the largest clinical series specifically focused on pulmonary infections caused by M. col. In our cohort, MAC accounted for 44.8% of all NTM isolates, while M. col represented 3.7% of all NTM pulmonary infections and 8.2% of MAC pulmonary infections. The discrepancy between the proportion and previously reported prevalence rates of MAC isolates may be attributed to differences in study settings [11–13]. A nationwide survey by Liu et al. demonstrated that MAC constituted 34.1% of all NTM isolates in China, with marked regional variations: higher MAC proportions were observed in southern and coastal areas, whereas inland regions showed a lower prevalence of MAC [14]. This geographic heterogeneity is further supported by a global systematic review by Zweijpfenning et al., which confirmed strong regional differences in MAC isolation frequencies across continents and even within countries, reinforcing that local epidemiology significantly influences NTM species distribution [15]. Moreover, studies focusing on specific immunocompromised populations, such as individuals co-infected with HIV in Chengdu, reported high prevalence rates of MAC (52.5%) and M. kansasii (27.1%), suggesting that host immune status also plays a critical role in shaping the NTM pathogen spectrum [16]. As a national tertiary referral center, our hospital admits many patients with complex, refractory, or culture-negative diseases. According to statistics, over 80% of the patients in our cohort were referrals, while fewer than 20% were initially diagnosed at our hospital. This patient selection bias, combined with the widespread application of mNGS in such challenging cases, likely enhances the detection of rare NTM species and mixed infections. Consequently, our cohort may be enriched with non-MAC NTM species, which reduces the relative proportion of MAC and provides a unique opportunity to characterize less common pathogens such as M. col.

Our study indicates that the host susceptibility profile for M. col infection—including common comorbidities such as bronchiectasis, autoimmune diseases, and AIDS—shows no significant differences compared to other MAC species. This finding establishes the classic pathogenic niche of M. col within the MAC family. Although early case reports often associated it with severe disseminated infections, particularly in patients with advanced AIDS, suggesting a potential preference for specific immunocompromised hosts, our cohort data demonstrate that its susceptibility spectrum is in fact broad and non-specific, consistent with other MAC members [4, 13]. Notably, M. col infection was associated with a significantly higher rate of disseminated disease (36.4% vs. 7.3% in other MAC infections), frequently involving bones, joints, lymph nodes, and skin. This finding underscores the systemic potential of M. col and aligns with earlier reports of its propensity to cause extrapulmonary manifestations [2, 3, 17]. Furthermore, patients with M. col infection presented more frequently with fever and dyspnea, and exhibited pronounced hematological abnormalities including anemia and eosinophilia, suggesting a heightened inflammatory response or possible hypersensitivity component. A particularly intriguing finding was the high rate of co-infection with other pathogens (72.7%), significantly greater than that observed in other MAC patients. This may partly explain the elevated eosinophil counts and more complex clinical presentations among M. col patients. The reasons for this association remain unclear but could relate to underlying immune dysregulation or environmental exposure facilitating polymicrobial colonization.

Radiologically, M. col pulmonary infections were indistinguishable from those caused by other MAC species, indicating that imaging alone cannot guide species-specific diagnosis. This reinforces the necessity of advanced microbiological techniques for accurate identification. In our cohort, mNGS provided rapid and reliable detection, with results available within 48 h. This aligns with growing evidence supporting the utility of mNGS in diagnosing rare or fastidious pathogens, especially in cases of mixed infection or when conventional methods yield negative or ambiguous results [4, 18]. However, its limitations include high costs and the potential detection of non-pathogenic or colonizing microorganisms.

Drug susceptibility testing revealed that M. col isolates were generally susceptible to first-line antimycobacterial agents including macrolides, aminoglycosides, and rifamycins, with no significant differences in resistance patterns compared to other MAC species. All patients who completed a course of combination therapy—typically including a macrolide, ethambutol, a rifamycin, and often an aminoglycoside—experienced clinical improvement. These findings support the use of standard MAC treatment regimens for M. col infection, consistent with previous reports [19–21]. However, the optimal duration of therapy and the role of adjunctive interventions remain uncertain and warrant further investigation.

In terms of clinical treatment, although the sample size of the M. col infection group was limited, the treatment duration showed no significant difference compared to other MAC infection groups. We also reported an unusual case of asymptomatic pulmonary infection in an immunocompetent individual whose radiographic abnormalities resolved spontaneously without therapy. This suggests that M. col may, in certain contexts, behave as an indolent colonizer rather than an invasive pathogen. In such cases, careful watchful waiting may be a reasonable alternative to immediate treatment, particularly when drug toxicity or patient tolerance is a concern. More importantly, all 11 patients with M. col infection showed clinical improvement. This finding contrasts significantly with the high mortality rates reported in previous literature, which may be attributed to the fact that earlier studies primarily focused on disseminated infections and immunocompromised patient populations [2].

Several limitations should be considered. The small sample size, though reflective of the rarity of M. col infection, precludes definitive conclusions regarding its clinical course and optimal management. The single-center, retrospective design also introduces potential selection bias, which may be further amplified by our hospital’s status as a national referral center where complex and rare cases are more likely to be referred. Additionally, the clinical application of mNGS may have increased the detection rate of mixed infections. Future multicenter prospective studies incorporating larger patient numbers and standardized treatment protocols are needed to validate these findings and refine strategies for diagnosis and therapy.

Conclusions

In this exploratory study, pulmonary disease caused by M. col, while relatively rare, appeared to demonstrate a greater propensity for dissemination and more distinct laboratory findings compared to disease caused by other MAC species. However, it exhibited similar antibiotic susceptibility profiles and comparable treatment outcomes. Molecular diagnostic techniques like mNGS were crucial for rapid pathogen identification. Treatment should adhere to MAC guidelines while incorporating individualized management strategies. Given the small cohort size, these findings must be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, requiring validation in larger, prospective studies.
