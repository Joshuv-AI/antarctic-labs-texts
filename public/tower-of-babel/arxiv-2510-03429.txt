Abstract
. We discuss interrelations between: Cohn localizations of full square matrices; a Leavitt localization of a row; and the Jacobson quasi-inverses of quasi-regular elements. The latter Jacobson localizations appear naturally and easily in rings which are Hausdorff topological spaces with respect to an ideal topology, pointing out also a connection to specific Gabriel localizations. As a main result and an application we develop a factorization theory for free polynomials with non-zero augmentation over a field. This is inspired by a factorization theory given in the joint work with Mantese for polynomials with constant in non-commutative variables. The basic tool of this research is the localization of a free group algebra by a row of free generators, that is, the Fox algebra of a free group. Hence link modules, that is, Sato modules become naturally modules over Fox algebras, proving a uniqueness and inducing a bijective correspondence between factorizations and composition chains. This is a very first step in a structure theory of matrices over either free algebras or group algebras of free groups with coefficients in a field, or more generally in a principal ideal domain.

1.
Introduction

In an informative, interesting work
[
20
]
Farber and Vogel used topological ideas and methods on link modules to show that the rational closure (inside the power series ring) of the group algebra of a free group of a finite rank is a universal Cohn localization by the set of matrices
1+M
, where
M
runs over all square matrices with entries from the augmentation ideal. In this approach, although it was not explicitly stated, they used heavily the fact that the row of free generators can be inverted easily using Fox derivatives, making possibly a unified module theoretic treatment of link modules. In that way, link modules are also modules over the algebra of Fox derivatives, consequently link modules are naturally modules over the associated Leavitt localization.
On the other hand, in computer science rational closures were introduced by Schützenberger and Nivat (cf. the book
[
30
]
of Solomaa and Soittola) for non-commutative power series algebras over semirings where power series without constant are clearly quasi-regular and have quasi-inverses in the Jacobson sense. In this work we explore these aspects to a larger class of rings and make their interrelations transparent.

of a free algebra

contains all copies of

in

defined by points

of the affine space

with invertible coordinates

sending each

. Properties of rational elements can be deduced easily from the general theory of either rational power series developed in

or Cohn’s localization
[

]

R^{\rm rat}_{I}

be the subalgebra of
\hat{R}
generated by

and the subgroup of

generated by

; Then define

. Assume that

be the subalgebra of
\hat{R}
generated by
R_{l}
and the subgroup of
1+\hat{I}
generated by
1+I_{l}
. Put
I_{l+1}=R_{l+1}\cap\hat{I}
. It is obvious from this construction that

Proposition 3.1
.

For a transfinitely nilpotent ideal
I\lhd R
, the rational closure

is the subalgebra
\bigcup\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}R_{l}
.

Put

and let

be the two-sided ideal of
\bar{R}
generated by
I
. Then we have trivially

\bigcap\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}{\tilde{I}}^{l}=0

. The canonical isomorphisms

show that

is also a completion of

with respect to the ideal topology defined by
\tilde{I}
. This means that

is the rational closure of itself with respect to the ideal
\tilde{I}
. Hence the following claim is verified.

Proposition 3.2
.

The rational closure is an idempotent functor on rings
R
with a transfinitely nilpotent ideal. That is, for
\tilde{R}=R^{\rm rat}_{I}
and its ideal
\tilde{I}
generated by

one has
\bigcap\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}{\tilde{I}}^{l}=0

.

For

let

Then the rational closure

Proposition 3.3

For an arbitrary positive integer

whose entries belong to

. By symmetry, the same claim holds for rows

and equations

where

and

have entries in
R^{\rm rat}_{I}
and
\hat{I}\cap R^{\rm rat}_{I}

Proof.

The uniqueness of a solution follows from

and the fact that

is a unit in the matrix algebra

in view of

and the obvious equality

(1-Q)^{-1}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}Q^{i}\quad(Q^{0}=1)
.

To finish the proof we use induction on

. Put
Q=(q_{ij})
where all

are quasiregular elements of
\hat{R}
contained in
R^{\rm rat}_{I}
. In case

we have

whence the claim holds trivially. Assume now by induction the claim for all integers
<l
(
\geq 2

\vdots\\
z_{l}\end{pmatrix},P=\begin{pmatrix}p_{1}\\
\vdots\\

Substituting this value of

in the first

whence the induction hypothesis implies that all

and so

belong to

∎

Let

It is clear that the notion of a rational closure and results of this section can be treated in the same manner for a more general case when

4.
Cohn’s localization

An ingenious idea in Cohn’s localization inspired by criteria of rational power series by Schützenberger and Nivat, see Section
3
and
[
14
, Notes and Comments on Chapter 7]
, is to invert matrices instead of elements. If
M
is an
n\times m
matrix over an algebra
R
then an
m\times n
matrix
N
over
R
is said to be an
inverse
of
M
if both
MN
and
NM
are the identity matrix 1
of size
n\times n
and
m\times m
, respectively. Let
\Sigma
be a set of (not necessarily square) matrices over
R
. An algebra homomorphism
f\colon R\rightarrow S
is said to be
\Sigma
-
inverting
if every matrix in
\Sigma
is mapped by
f
to an invertible matrix over
S
. Therefore one can define the
Cohn quotient ring

R_{\Sigma_{f}}(S)
of
R
in
S
via
f
as a subring (subalgebra) of
S
generated by
f(S)
and entries of
(f(M))^{-1}\,(M\in\Sigma)
. We often write
R_{\Sigma}(S)
when
f
is understood. We use this terminology for this notion which is originally called

\Sigma
-
rational closure
in
[
15
]
, to avoid confusion. Let
\Sigma_{f}
be the set of all (not necessarily square) matrices over
R
such that their images via
f
are invertible over
f(R)\subseteq S
. If
S
is a ring with IBN, an abbreviation for
invariant basis number
, then matrices from
\Sigma_{f}
must be square matrices. This fact emphasizes the specific classes
\Sigma
consisting of square matrices. Recall that

Definition 4.1
.

A ring has IBN if modules generated freely by
n
and
m
elements, respectively, are isomorphic if and only if
n=m
.

Since invertibility of a square matrix is unaffected by permuting rows and columns, one can assume without loss of generality in the case of square matrices that any matrix in
\Sigma
still lies in
\Sigma
after permutations of either rows or columns. Moreover, if square matrices
A,B\in\Sigma
, then for any matrix
C
of suitable size,
\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
0&B\end{pmatrix}\in\Sigma_{f}
by

\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
0&B\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}A^{-1}&-A^{-1}CB^{-1}\\
0&B^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Consequently, the same argument in the first part of the proof to Proposition
3.5
where
Q
runs over matrices
1-M\,(M\in\Sigma)
, implies immediately the following description of
R_{\Sigma}(S)
(cf. also
[
14
, Theorem 7.1.2]
and
[
15
, Theorem 7.1.2]
for results of the most general form).

Proposition 4.1
.

For a
\Sigma
-inverting homomorphism
f\colon R\rightarrow S
by a set
\Sigma
of square matrices over an algebra
R
the Cohn quotient algebra
R_{\Sigma}(S)
is the set of entries of a unique solution
Z
of a matrix equation

AZ=P

where
A
runs over square matrices from
f(\Sigma_{f})
and
P
runs over columns of suitable size over
f(R)
. A symmetric claim holds by using row vectors. As an
R
-module
R_{\Sigma}(S)
is generated by entries of inverse matrices from
f(\Sigma_{f})
.

By this way, for square matrices, rational closures appear as rings of quotients whose elements are entries of vectors
Z
satisfying Ore’ expression
Z=A^{-1}P
or the Cramer’s Rule in
[
15
, Proposition 7.1.5]
.

Remark 4.2
.

Put
I=\ker f
in Proposition
4.1
. Then

1+M_{I}\subseteq\Sigma_{f}
. Hence inverting
1+M_{I}
makes the images of
1+I
invertible, or makes matrices from
I
to have a quasi-inverse with respect to the circle operation of Jacobson. Hence in certain good cases, inverting matrices can be carried out by inverting elements successively as in Proposition
3.1
by using the reductive proof of Proposition
3.3
. As an obvious example, any algebra is her own Cohn quotient ring, even her universal Cohn localization with respect to
\Sigma=1+M_{J}
where
J
denotes its Jacobson radical.

Definition 4.3
.

An algebra homomorphism
\jmath\colon R\rightarrow R_{\Sigma}
is called the
universal
\Sigma
-inverting homomorphism
and
R_{\Sigma}
is called the
universal Cohn localization of
R
by inverting
\Sigma
if
\jmath
is
\Sigma
-inverting and any
\Sigma
-inverting homomorphism
f\colon R\rightarrow S
factors uniquely through
\jmath
, that is, there is a unique algebra homomorphism
g\colon R_{\Sigma}\rightarrow S
satisfying
g\jmath=f
. Hence, as an algebra

is generated by
\jmath(R)
and entries in inverses of matrices from
\jmath(\Sigma)
. In the other words, the rational closure of
R
in
R_{\Sigma}
is
R_{\Sigma}
itself.

It is a well-known result of Cohn
[
15
, Section 7.2]
that the universal
\Sigma
-inverting homomorphism
\jmath\colon R\rightarrow R_{\Sigma}
exists and is unique up to
R
-preserving isomorphism because the construction presented there holds also for not necessarily square matrices.
In this case
\jmath
is obviously an (algebra) epimorphism in the catogorial sense by a uniqueness of the inverse whence Morita’s theory (cf.
[
27
]
and
[
28
]
) of localization is applicable, too.

If
R
has
IBN
, then any invertible square matrix cannot be a product of non-square matrices. If one requires that a Cohn’s localization
R_{\Sigma}
is an algebra with IBN, then it is necessary that

has IBN, and
\Sigma
is a set of square matrices which cannot be written as products of non-square matrices. Such a square matrix is called by definition a
full matrix
.
R_{\Sigma}
is called a

of

with respect to
or
by

\Sigma
if
\Sigma
is a set of full matrices over

. For example, one can consider a free algebra
A
and let
\Sigma
be the set of all nonzero elements. Therefore there are, in general, several one-to-one
\Sigma
-inverting homomorphisms such that the corresponding Cohn quotient rings are pairwise non-isomorphic. As an obvious consequence of the universality one has the following useful and quite simple test to decide when a ring is a subring of its strictly universal Cohn localization.

Proposition 4.2

Let

be a set of full square matrices over an algebra

-inverting homomorphism, i.e., if

.

At the first glance it seems difficult to use Proposition

because it is not easy to find

together with an injective

-inverting homomorphism.
However, if

Let

5.
Leavitt localization and module type

It is obvious that all finitely generated free modules over the column-finite, infinite

over any ring

without IBN there is a unique pair

with smallest possible integers

such that modules generated freely by

of

) shows that

does not have IBN and the module type of

is a pair

with (
1\leq p\leq m,q\leq n;p\leq q-1
). The computation of the module type of a ring without IBN is not an easy job in general. However, in particular cases, the computation is essentially equivalent to the description of an associated Grothendieck group. It turns out that a localization of
an
1\times n
matrix, i.e. a row, is easier and more well-understood then one of an
m\times n
matrix with
1\leq m-1\leq n-2
. We investigate in this section only a localization of a row. By definition a
Leavitt localization
is a universal inversion of a row. In what follows, we shall present the construction of the Leavitt localization in certain cases.

Inverting universally a row
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
over an algebra

yields an algebra
L(R)
, called
the Leavitt algebra of

R

generated over

R

by

x^{*}_{1},\dots,x^{*}_{n}
satisfying the so-called
Cuntz-Krieger equalities
for a left and a right inverse

(3)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x^{*}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x^{*}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&\dots&x_{n}\end{pmatrix}=I_{n}\in R_{n}\iff x^{*}_{i}x_{j}=\delta_{ij}\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm(CK1)}

and

(4)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x^{*}_{i}=1\quad\quad\text{\rm(CK2)},

respectively.

Let
\imath\colon R\rightarrow L(R)
be the associated canonical map from
R
into
L(R)
. As an easy but important consequence we have first for all integers
l\in\mathbb{N}

(5)

\displaystyle 1=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x^{*}_{i}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}x^{*}_{j}\right)x^{*}_{i}=\cdots=\sum\limits_{|b|=|c|=l}bc^{*}=1\in\imath(R),

where
c^{*}=x^{*}_{i_{l}}\cdots x^{*}_{i_{1}}
for
c=x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{l}}
. It is worth to keep in mind that
R
is not necessarily a subalgebra of
L(R)
although we use the same letters for elements of both
R
and
\imath(R)\subseteq L(R)
. For example,
\imath
is not one-to-one if

is either the Jacobson, i.e., the Toeplitz algebra of one-sided inverses, or more generally, the Cohn algebra of one-sided inverses of a row
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. In this case,
L(R)
is either the Laurent polynomial ring or the classical Leavitt algebra
L_{K}(1,n)
for
n\geq 2
, respectively. In fact, in
L(R)
the sum
\sum x_{i}\imath(R)
(denoted
\sum x_{i}R
according to our notation) is direct and free of rank
n
over
\imath(R)
. Hence,
\sum x_{i}R=\oplus x_{i}R\cong{{{}^{n}R}}
with the trivial left annihilator is necessary for
R
being canonically embedded in
L(R)
. Moreover, if we assume additionally that the right ideal of
R
generated the
x_{i}
is a two-sided ideal, then for any non-zero element
\gamma\in L(R)
, there is a finite product
b
of the
x_{i}
such that
0\neq\gamma b\in R=\imath(R)
.

This observation shows elements in
L(R)\,\,(R\in\{A,\Gamma,\Lambda\})
can be written in a ”quasi-normal” form as linear combinations

(6)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}c^{*}_{i}\quad(r_{i}\in R;\quad c_{i}\in M_{X})

in view of the fact that for the group algebra
\Lambda

(7)

\displaystyle x^{*}_{i}t^{-1}_{j}=x^{*}_{i}-x^{*}_{i}(t_{j}-1)t^{-1}_{j}=x^{*}_{i}-\begin{cases}-t^{-1}_{i}&\text{if}\qquad j=i,\\
0&\text{if}\qquad j\neq i\end{cases}.

If now a right ideal

of

generated by elements

is a two-sided ideal having trivial left annihilator and free of rank n, then a Leavitt localization

L(R)
by the row
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
can be constructed as follows (see also
[
4

). Examples for such algebras are the algebras

considered in Section

. Let
I=\sum x_{i}R=\oplus x_{i}R
be the right ideal generated by
x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}
. Then

is a two-sided ideal which is free of rank
n
by assumption. Therefore the ideal topology defined by

is a perfect Gabriel topology and one can form a (flat epimorphic right) ring of quotients

Q(R)=\mathop{\underrightarrow{\mathrm{lim}}}\limits_{l\in\mathbb{N}}\Hom_{R}(I^{l},R)

of

by
[

[

for basic facts on rings of quotients, Gabriel topologies and perfect epimorphisms. If

0&\text{if}\qquad j\neq i\end{cases}
, then as elements of

the column

\begin{pmatrix}x^{\star}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x^{\star}_{n}\end{pmatrix}
is the inverse of the row
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
. Therefore from the definition of

there is a canonical epimorphism

by sending

. It is immediate that for a non-zero element
\gamma\in L(R)
and a finite product

of the

with

is also a non-zero element of

. Since

is clearly a subring of

and the restriction of

Theorem 5.1
.

For
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in R\,\,(n\geq 2)
let
\imath\colon R\rightarrow L(R)
a Leavitt localization of
R
by inverting universally the row
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\imath(x_{i})\imath(R)
is free over
\imath(R)
of rank
n
on the
\imath(x_{i})

\imath\colon R\rightarrow L(R)
is one-to-one, then
\sum x_{i}R=\oplus x_{i}R
is a free right
R
-module of rank
n
on the
x_{i}
with trivial left annihilator. Conversely, if

is a two-sided ideal free of rank

on the
x_{i}

is a ring of right quotients of

with respect to the
I

is an isomorphism whence the canonical map

is one-to-one.

Proof.

One needs only to verify the last claim. If there is a nonzero element

, then there is
b=x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{l}}
such that

. Therefore

, a contradiction. Hence

is one-to-one, whence it is an isomorphism.
∎

for

although the latter is not defined outside

for

of power series we have unfortunately neither Schreier bases nor a Schreier-Lewin formula to compute rank of finitely generated essential one-sided ideals of

as
\Gamma

is not a fir. Hence we need further arguments. For the sake of completeness we present a proof which work at the same time for all three cases over an arbitary commutative ring

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
R
be any one of the algebras
A,\Lambda,\Gamma
. Then the module type of
L(R)
is
(1,n)
if
n\geq 2
.

Proof.

We use a description of
L(R)
as a flat epimorphic right ring
Q(R)
of quotients by Proposition
5.1
. We already know
Q(R)^{n}\cong Q(R)_{Q(R)}
. Hence
it suffices to verify that
n-1
is a divisor of
l-1
if

L(R)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}L(R)=\bigoplus\limits_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}L(R)\quad(\alpha_{i}\in L(R))

where
\ann_{L(R)}\alpha_{i}=\{\beta\in L(R)\,|\,\alpha_{i}\beta=0\}=0
.
By passing to factor of
\mathfrak{k}
by a maximal ideal of
\mathfrak{k}
which is a field, one can assume without loss of generality that
\mathfrak{k}
is a field. From the definition
there is an integer
m\in\mathbb{N}
big enough such that as an element of
Q(R)
, each
\alpha_{i}\,(i\in\{1,\cdots,l\})
is represented as a right
R
-module homomorphism
\alpha_{i}\colon{\bar{R}}^{m}\rightarrow R
, where
\bar{R}
denotes the kernel of the augmentation
\epsilon\colon R\rightarrow\mathfrak{k}
. This implies that
R_{i}=\alpha_{i}{\bar{R}}^{m}
is a right ideal of
R
isomorphic to
{\bar{R}}^{m}
. Hence
R_{i}
is a free right
R
-module of rank
n^{m}
. Consequently,
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}R_{i}=J
is a free right
R
-module of rank
ln^{m}
. Now we compute rank of

using a different method. The equality
R\subseteq L(R)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}L(R)
implies

x_{j}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}\beta_{ij}\quad\beta_{ij}\in L(R)

for each index

together with uniquely determined elements
\beta_{ij}\in L(R)
where
x_{j}=t_{j}-1
in the case of
\Lambda
.
Therefore by the definition of
L(R)
there is a positive integer

big enough that all
\beta_{ij}{\bar{R}}^{N}\in{\bar{R}}^{m}
where

. This shows

Consequently,

is a right ideal of

of finite codimension

the rank of

is

by the Schreier-Lewin formula
[

]
.
Comparing these two values for the rank of

one obtains that

is a divisor of

and so the proof is complete in case of

and
\Lambda

For the power series algebra

[

\Gamma
is a local ring, the rank of

is just the dimension of the
\mathfrak{k}
-space

denotes the Jacobson radical of

. Choose

big enough such that

. Passing to the finite-dimensional local factor algebra

which form a generating set of
\tilde{J}
. Hence the rank of

is precisely the rank of the projective cover of

which is a free module over
\tilde{\Gamma}
. On the other hand, let

. Then

and

by the well-known Noetherian Isomorphism Theorems. This implies that the rank of a free module

is

by the Schreier-Lewin formula. Moreover, the rank of

is the dimension of the
\mathfrak{k}
-space

and so of the
\mathfrak{k}
-dimension of

where
\tilde{A}=A/\bar{A}^{N+1}

and

denote the Jacobson radical of
\tilde{A}
and

is again

where

is the codimension of

. This shows that

is still a divisor of

in this case as well, and so our proof is complete.

Module type and Sato modules

Algebras
R
together with a two-sided ideal

form a natural class of augmented rings with an interesting connection to link modules by works of Sato
[
31
]
and Faber and Vogel
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
. In what follows,
(R,I)
is a pair of an algebra

with a two-sided ideal
I\lhd R
which is a free right
R
-module on
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and has trivial left annihilator. Thus
R
is an augmented ring with the augmentation
\epsilon\colon R\rightarrow R/I=S

l\in\mathbb{N}
the power
I^{l}_{R}
is a free right
R
-module of rank
n^{l}
on the set of monomials of length
l
in the
x_{i}
’s. Furthermore,
S_{R}
has the projective dimension 1 with a free resolution

(8)

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow{{}^{n}R}\xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\cdots x_{n}\end{pmatrix}}R\overset{\epsilon}{\longrightarrow}S\longrightarrow 0

where
{{}^{n}R}
denotes the free right
R
-module of column vectors of length
n
together with the scalar product
(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})\begin{pmatrix}r_{1}\\
\vdots\\
r_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}r_{i}\in I
.

Inspired by the notion of link module
[

and the homology theory of groups we define

Definition 6.1

A left

for every

-isomorphism

satisfying

(9)

\displaystyle(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})\in M^{n}\longmapsto\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}m_{i}=m\in M.

If a weak Sato module

called a

Together with examples arising from geometric topological investigation the most natural algebraic examples for Sato modules are left

This means that the

-endomorphisms of

Proof.

First we show

7.
Prime factorizations in free group algebras

It is a famous result of Cohn
[

that both the free algebra and the group algebra of a free group are firs, hence UFD’s. In this section, inspired by the approach presented in

we discuss the factorization problem of elements of the group algebra

of the free group

generated by

is assumed to be a field. Since we treat Fox derivations of free group algebras in Section

by using power series algebras, it is worth to deal with free group algebras in their own context. This is why the next consideration is necessary.

Put
t_{i}-1=x_{1},y_{i}=t^{-1}_{i}-1=-x_{i}t^{-1}_{i}

contains the free associative algebra
{\mathfrak{k}}\langle t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\rangle={\mathfrak{k}}\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle=A

is transfinitely nilpotent and free of rank

over
\Lambda
. Therefore
\Lambda
is an augmented algebra by the augmentation

together with the standard partial derivatives

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}_{i}=-t_{i}\partial_{i}\Longleftrightarrow\partial_{i}=-t^{-1}_{i}\bar{\partial}_{i}.

is a maximal left ideal of

∎

We are now in position to state the first main result of this section.

Theorem 7.19
.

For any two polynomials
\gamma,\lambda\in\Lambda
there is a uniquely determined comonic polynomial
\delta\in\Lambda
of length at most
m=\min\{|\gamma|,|\lambda|\}
called the
greatest common divisor
of
\gamma,\lambda
with the property that
\delta
is a generator (uniquely determinedup to a unit) of the left ideal of
L(\Lambda)
generated by
\gamma
and
\lambda
, that is,
L(\Lambda)\gamma+L(\Lambda)\lambda=L(\Lambda)\delta

Proof.

The uniqueness of
\delta
is a consequence of the equality
L(\Lambda)\delta\cap\Lambda=\Lambda\delta
granted by lemma
7.2
and the fact that
\Lambda
is a domain. By Lemma
7.1
and the simple induction on a number of generators which are free comonic polynomials, one can assume without loss of generality that
\gamma,\lambda
are comonic and neither of them is contained in the left ideal of
L(\Lambda)
generated by the other. We use induction on
m=\min\{|\gamma|,|\lambda|\}
. The case
m=1
holds by Proposition
7.18
. Assume now the statement for any two free comonic polynomials such the the minimum of their length is at most
m-1\,(m\geq 2)
and consider free comonic polynomials
\gamma,\,\lambda
with
m=\min\{|\gamma|,|\lambda|\}
. Without loss of generality one can assume
m=|\gamma|
.

By Proposition
7.6
, Theorem
7.10
and the induction hypothesis one can also assume additionally that
|\lambda|=m=|\gamma|
. Put
J=L(\Lambda)\gamma+L(\Lambda)\lambda
. We have to show that
J
is a principal left ideal of
L(\Lambda)
generated by some comonic polynomial of
\Lambda
of length at most
m
. According to our notation,
{{}_{\gamma}}V
is a finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{k}
-subspace of
\Lambda
spanned by all partial derivatives
\partial_{w}\gamma\,(w\in F)
including
\gamma=\partial_{\emptyset}\gamma
. Then

L(\Lambda)=L(\Lambda)\gamma+\Lambda=L(\Lambda)\gamma+\Lambda({{}_{\gamma}}V)
holds
by Lemma
7.2
and Theorem
7.10
. Since
J/L(\Lambda)\gamma
is a
L(\Lambda)
-submodule of
\coker\gamma
, by Theorem
7.10
there is a uniquely determined finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{k}
-submodule
P
of
{{}_{\gamma}}V
satisfying
J=L(\Lambda)\gamma+\Lambda P
.

If there is a comonic free polynomial
\mu\in P\setminus L(\Lambda)\gamma
of length at most
m-1
, then the induction hypothesis implies
J\supseteq J_{1}=L(\Lambda)\gamma+L(\Lambda)\mu=L(\Lambda)\gamma_{1}
for some comonic free polynomial
\gamma_{1}
of length at most
m-1
. Hence the equality
J=J_{1}+L(\Lambda)\lambda=L(\Lambda)\gamma_{1}+L(\Lambda)\lambda
implies again by the induction hypothesis that

is a left principal ideal generated by some comonic polynomial
\delta
of length at most
|\gamma_{1}|\leq m-1
.

Consequently, it remains to consider the case when
P
admits a
\mathfrak{k}
-basis consisting of free comonic polynomials of length
m
modulo
L(\Lambda)\gamma
.
In this case there exists by the equality (
23
) an index
i\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
such that
\lambda_{1}=z_{i}\star_{\gamma}\lambda\notin\Lambda\gamma
and
|\lambda_{1}|=m
holds. Moreover, by Lemma
7.1
one can again assume that
\lambda_{1}
is comonic. Then
\lambda_{1}
is a special polynomial by Lemma
7.5
.
Consequently, all strictly maximal words of
\lambda_{1}=-\epsilon(\lambda)\partial_{i}\gamma+\partial_{i}\lambda
begin with
t^{-1}_{i}
. By replacing
\gamma
with
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda
with
\gamma
we consider the left ideal
J_{1}=L(\Lambda)\lambda_{1}+L(\Lambda)\gamma\subseteq J
with
|\gamma|=m=|\lambda_{1}|
. Put
P_{1}=J_{1}\cap{{}_{\lambda_{1}}V}
. As in the first part of the proof one can assume again without loss of generality that none of
\lambda_{1}
and
\gamma
is contained in the left ideal of
L(\Lambda)
generated by the other and
P_{1}
has a
\mathfrak{k}
-basis consisting of free comonic polynomials of length
m
. For the further argument it is worth to keep in mind that all partial derivatives
\partial_{t}\gamma\,(t\in\{t_{1},\dots,t_{n},t^{-1}_{1},\dots,t^{-1}_{n}\})
may be of the length
m
. Namely, it is the case when
|\eta(\gamma)|=2n
. Again by the equality (
23
) one can assume that there is an index
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
\gamma_{1}=z_{j}\ast_{\lambda_{1}}\gamma\notin L(\Lambda)\lambda_{1}
with
|\gamma_{1}|=m
. Then
\gamma_{1}
is a free special polynomial by Lemma
7.5
. Let
H=L(\Lambda)\lambda_{1}+L(\Lambda)\gamma_{1}\subseteq J_{1}
. Then we have
H=L(\Lambda)(t_{i}\lambda_{1})+L(\Lambda)(t_{j}\gamma_{1})
with
|t_{i}\lambda_{1}|=m=|t_{j}\gamma_{1}|
. Furthermore all partial derivatives
\partial_{w}t_{i}\lambda_{1}
and
\partial_{w}t_{i}\gamma_{1}
of

because

and
\gamma_{1}
are special with

and

L(\Lambda)\lambda_{1}+L(\Lambda)(z_{1}\ast_{\lambda_{1}}\gamma_{1})=H_{1}
is a principal left ideal of
L(\Lambda)
generated by a comonic polynomial
\alpha
of length at most

is also a left principal ideal of

generated by some comonic polynomial

of length at most

is also a left principal ideal of
L(\Lambda)
generated by an appropriate comonic polynomal

of length at most

in view of the induction hypothesis. In summary we have again from the induction hypothesis that

is a left principal ideal of

generated by a free comonic polynomial of length at most

whence the proof is complete.
∎

It is clear that Theorem

provides an algorithm to find a greatest common divisor of any two free polynomials in the case of a finite field
\mathfrak{k}
. However, as one can see from the proof that this way is not very economical, namely it goes very slowly to the end. Namely, one has to reduce a length step by step via both a positive and a negative power of each free variable
t_{i}\,(i=1,\dots,n)
. For further comments and remarks concerning the way to find greatest comon divisors we refer to
[
5
]
.

As obvious consequence of Theorems
7.10
and

we have immediately

Corollary 7.20
.

Any proper left ideal of
L(\lambda)
which intersects
\Lambda
non-trivially, is a principal left ideal of
L(\Lambda)
generated by a uniquely

or can be generated by a free comonic polynomial of

.

For the thorough discussion of factorizations in domains we need a notion of similarity.
Note that two polynomials
a
and
b
of an algebra
C
are said to be
similar
if the corresponding left modules
C/Ca,C/Cb
are isomorphic, or equivalently, the corresponding right modules
C/aC,C/bC
are isomorphic. Consequently, in a domain
C
any generator

of a stricly cyclic module
C/Ca
is similar to
a
. This one sided property leads to the paradise of elements similar to
a
, provides a unified way to control factorizations of a polynomials.

We are now able to characterize free comonic irreducible polynomials via Sato modules over the Leavitt localization
L(\Lambda)
and their smallest lattices which are finite-dimensional modules over
\Lambda^{*}
which can be generated by
2n
elements.

Theorem 7.21
.

Let
\pi\in\Lambda
be a free comonic polynomial which is not a unit and
L(\Lambda)
the Fox algebra of
\Lambda
of Fox derivatives, that is, the Leavitt localization of
\Lambda
inverting the row
(t_{1}-1=x_{1},\dots,t_{n}-1=x_{n})
where
\Lambda
is the group algebra of a free group of rank
n\geq 2
over a field
\mathfrak{k}
. Let
{{}_{\pi}}U
be the smallest lattice of
\coker\pi
generated by images of partial derivatives of
\pi
. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

\pi
is irreducible;

(2)

\coker\pi=\Lambda/\Lambda\pi
is a simple
L(\Lambda)
-module;

(3)

{{}_{\pi}}U
is a simple
\Lambda^{*}
-module which is finite-dimensional over
\mathfrak{k}
.

Proof.

The equivalence
(2)\Longleftrightarrow(3)
is already verified in Theorem
7.9
. Therefore it is enough to see the equivalence
(1)\iff(2)
.

First let
\pi\in\Lambda
be a comonic irreducible polynomial. To see that
\coker\pi\cong L(\Lambda)/L(\Lambda)\pi
is a simple
L(\Lambda)
-module, it is enough to show
L(\Lambda)\pi+L(\Lambda)\gamma=L(\Lambda)
for every
\gamma\in\Lambda\setminus\Lambda\gamma
. By Lemma
7.1
it is enough to restrict to the case when
\gamma
is comonic. By Theorem
7.19
we have
L(\Lambda)\pi+L(\Lambda)\gamma=L(\Lambda)\delta
for a uniquely determined comonic polynomial
\delta\in\Lambda
. Hence
\delta
is a right factor of
\pi
by Proposition
6.1
. Therefore
\delta
must be a unit in view of the fact that
\pi
is irreducible. Hence
L(\Lambda)/L(\Lambda)\pi
is a simple
L(\Lambda)
-module.

Conversely, assume that
L(\Lambda)/L(\Lambda)\pi
is a simple
L(\Lambda)
-module. We have to show that
\pi
is irreducible. Namely, if
\pi
is reducible, i.e.,
\pi=\alpha\beta
for
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
which are non-units. Then
\alpha
and
\beta
are also comonic because
\pi
is comonic. Since
\Lambda
is a domain,
\beta
defines an injective
\Lambda
-homomorphism
\beta\colon\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda\beta
sending
\Lambda\alpha\mapsto\Lambda\pi\colon\rho\alpha\mapsto\rho\alpha\beta
for every polynomial
\rho\in\Lambda
. Consequently,

\beta
induces an isomorphism
\Lambda/\Lambda\alpha\cong\Lambda\beta/\Lambda\pi
(cf. also
[
8
, Lemma 1]
) whence
\Lambda\beta/\Lambda\pi
is a proper submodule of

\Lambda/\Lambda\pi
which by the indirect assumption contradicts to the simplicity of the
L(\Lambda)
-module
\coker\pi=\Lambda/\Lambda\pi
in view of the equality (
24
).
∎

Theorem
7.21
has the following nice consequence by Corollary
7.13
.

Corollary 7.22
.

Let
\pi\in\Lambda
be a free comonic irreducible polynomial and
U={{}_{\gamma}}U
the smallest lattice of
\coker\pi
which is a finite-dimensional

-module. Then the endomorphism rings
\End\left({}_{L(\Lambda)}\coker\pi\right)
and
\End\left({}_{D_{2n}}U\right)
are isomorphic finite-dimensional skew algebras over
\mathfrak{k}
.

Proof.

Since
\coker\pi
is a simple
L(\Lambda)
-module and
{{}_{\pi}}U
is its smallest lattice, any nonzero endomorphism of
\coker\pi
is an automorphism whence it leaves
{{}_{\pi}}U
invariantly. Consequently,
\End\left({}_{L(\Lambda)}\coker\pi\right)
embeds in
\End({{}_{\pi}}U)
. On the other hand, any
\Lambda^{*}
-endomorphism of
{{}_{\pi}}U
over extends uniquely to an
L(\Lambda)
-endomorphism of
\coker\pi
by Corollary
7.13
which shows immediately the isomorphism

\End\left({}_{L(\Lambda)}\coker\pi\right)\cong\End\left({}_{D_{2n}}U\right)\cong\End\left({}_{\Lambda^{*}}U\right)
and so the proof is complete.
∎

\End({{}_{\pi}}U)
is called the
(division) finite-dimensional algebra
associated to a free comonic irreducible polynomial
\pi\in\Lambda
. Theorem
7.21
suggests a question of central
importance, namely, to characterize free comonic irreducible polynomials

such that

is non-commutative.
We are now in position to transfer the factorization theory of ordinary polynomials in non-commuting variables developed together with F. Mantese in

to free polynomials as follows. First recall that for any free comonic polynomial

the smallest lattice

of

-module. In particular,

Using this assignment the main goal of this work is to show that a study of a free comonic polynomial

over

or more precisely, over
