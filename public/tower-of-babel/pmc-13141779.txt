The iron-responsive transcription factor HapX drives iron starvation adaptation and virulence in Talaromyces marneffei
Authors: Artid Amsri, Patcharin Thammasit, Kritsada Pruksaphon, Jutatip Supato, Huamei Wei, Yuanji Teng, Joshua D Nosanchuk, Monsicha Pongpom, Sirida Youngchim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•HapX is essential for iron-limited growth in Talaromyces marneffei.•Deletion of hapX impairs siderophore biosynthesis and disrupts iron-responsive gene regulation.•Loss of HapX reduces conidiation, conidial germination, and stress tolerance (cell wall, membrane, and nitrosative stress).•The ΔhapX mutant shows increased susceptibility to amphotericin B.•HapX is required for intracellular survival and full virulence in infection models.Keywords: Talaromyces marneffei, HapX transcription factor, Iron homeostasis, Stress response, Virulence, Antifungal susceptibility

Author summary

Talaromycosis is a severe opportunistic infection caused by the thermally dimorphic fungus Talaromyces marneffei and is a major threat to immunocompromised individuals in endemic regions. During infection, the fungus survives and replicates within host phagocytic cells, where it encounters iron limitation and host-imposed stresses. Because iron is essential for fungal growth yet restricted by the host, precise regulation of iron homeostasis is critical for pathogenic success.

HapX is a conserved iron-responsive transcription factor in many fungi, but its function in T. marneffei has not been characterized. Here, we show that HapX is required for adaptation to iron starvation in both mold and yeast phases. Loss of HapX impaired siderophore production, reduced conidiation and germination, and altered expression of iron-regulated genes. Beyond iron metabolism, the ΔhapX mutant displayed increased sensitivity to cell wall and membrane stress, reduced tolerance to nitrosative stress in the yeast phase, and enhanced susceptibility to amphotericin B. Importantly, HapX was required for optimal survival within phagocytic amoebae and for full virulence in an invertebrate infection model. Our findings demonstrate that HapX integrates iron homeostasis with stress adaptation and intracellular survival, thereby promoting pathogenic fitness in T. marneffei. These results highlight iron-responsive regulation as a key determinant of fungal virulence and a potential target for antifungal strategies.

Introduction

Iron is an essential micronutrient for virtually all living organisms due to its central roles in respiration, DNA synthesis, electron transport, and numerous enzymatic reactions (Haas, 2012). The ability of iron to cycle between ferrous (Fe²⁺) and ferric (Fe³⁺) states underlies its biological importance, but this same redox activity also renders excess iron highly toxic through the generation of reactive oxygen species via iron-mediated oxidative stress (Oberegger et al., 2025). Consequently, microorganisms must tightly regulate iron acquisition, utilization, and storage to maintain cellular homeostasis and ensure survival across diverse environmental conditions.

During infection, iron availability constitutes a main point of competition between the host and invading pathogens, as hosts actively restrict iron access through nutritional immunity mechanisms, thereby imposing a critical limitation on microbial proliferation (Hoffmann et al., 2025; Murdoch and Skaar, 2022). In response, pathogenic fungi have developed high-affinity iron acquisition systems, including reductive iron assimilation (RIA) and siderophore-mediated iron uptake, to overcome iron limitation in host niches (Haas, 2012; Pasricha et al., 2016). Thus, disruption of these iron acquisition pathways or their regulatory networks frequently leads to impaired growth and attenuated virulence, revealing iron homeostasis as a critical determinant of fungal pathogenicity (Lopez-Berges et al., 2012; Rocha et al., 2025; Schrettl et al., 2010).

In filamentous fungi, transcriptional regulation plays a dominant role in iron homeostasis. Two evolutionarily conserved transcription factors, the GATA-type repressor SreA and the basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factor HapX, function in a mutually negative feedback loop to coordinate iron-responsive gene expression (Misslinger et al., 2021). Under iron-replete conditions, SreA represses genes involved in high-affinity iron uptake and suppresses hapX expression to prevent iron overload. In contrast, during iron starvation, HapX represses sreA and iron-consuming metabolic pathways while activating iron acquisition systems, thereby reallocating limited iron to essential cellular processes (Gsaller et al., 2014). This regulatory circuit has been extensively characterized in Aspergillus fumigatus and is largely conserved across diverse fungal species (Oberegger et al., 2025).

In addition to its function in mediating responses to iron starvation, HapX has been implicated in iron detoxification and the regulation of stress adaptation (Brandon et al., 2015). In several fungi, HapX contributes to resistance against iron excess by activating vacuolar iron sequestration and supports oxidative stress tolerance (Gsaller et al., 2014). Importantly, HapX has been identified as a virulence determinant in various fungal pathogens, including A. fumigatus (Schrettl et al., 2010), Candida albicans (Hsu et al., 2011), Cryptococcus neoformans (Jung et al., 2010), Paracoccidioides brasiliensis (Rocha et al., 2025), and Fusarium oxysporum (Lopez-Berges et al., 2012). However, the contribution of HapX to virulence is not universal across fungi. In the dermatophyte Arthroderma benhamiae, HapX is essential for iron homeostasis but dispensable for infection of keratinized host tissues, demonstrating species-specific adaptation of iron regulatory networks to distinct pathogenic lifestyles (Krober et al., 2016).

Talaromyces marneffei is a thermally dimorphic fungal pathogen endemic to Southeast and East Asia and northeastern India, causing opportunistic systemic mycosis in immunocompromised individuals, especially patients with HIV/AIDS (Hu et al., 2013; Limper et al., 2017; Sethuraman et al., 2020). Infection is initiated by inhalation of environmental conidia, which are subsequently engulfed by phagocytic cells and differentiate a temperature-dependent morphological transition into pathogenic fission yeast cells (Pruksaphon et al., 2023). Within macrophages and other phagocytic host cells, T. marneffei encounters a restrictive intracellular environment characterized by nutrient limitation, oxidative stress, and restricted iron availability (Pongpom et al., 2017). Therefore, effective intracellular survival and replication require coordinated metabolic remodeling and adaptive stress response mechanisms.

Previous studies in T. marneffei have demonstrated the importance of iron acquisition systems for growth under iron-limited conditions and pathogenic fitness. Transcriptional regulators such as SreA, AcuK, and AcuM influence iron metabolism, carbon utilization, and virulence-associated phenotypes in this fungus (Amsri et al., 2021; Pasricha et al., 2016; Wangsanut et al., 2024). However, despite the role of HapX in iron homeostasis and virulence in other pathogenic fungi, its function in T. marneffei has not been characterized. In particular, it remains unclear how HapX contributes to iron-responsive transcriptional regulation across iron-replete and iron-depleted conditions, how it affects fungal growth, morphology, and stress tolerance, and whether HapX-mediated iron adaptation is required for intracellular survival and virulence in this dimorphic pathogen.

In this study, we investigated the role of the hapX gene in T. marneffei using an integrated genetic, transcriptional, physiological, and infection-based approach. By employing hapX deletion (ΔhapX) and complemented (ΔhapX::hapX⁺) strains, we examined HapX-dependent transcriptional regulation under varying iron conditions and evaluated its effects on fungal morphology, growth, and stress susceptibility. To assess the contribution of HapX to host-associated fitness, we analyzed fungal survival within phagocytic amoeba as an evolutionarily relevant model of intracellular predation and performed G. mellonella infection assays to evaluate virulence in vivo.

Materials and methods

Strains, media and growth conditions

T. marneffei ATCC18224 (FRR2161) was used as the wild-type (wt) strain in this study. The ∆hapX mutant (∆hapX, pyrG⁺) and the complemented strain (∆hapX::hapX⁺, pyrG⁺) were generated from the uracil auxotrophic G816 strain (ΔligD pyrG⁻ niaD⁻) as previously described (Boyce et al., 2012; Bugeja et al., 2012). The G816 strain was maintained on Aspergillus minimal medium (ANM) supplemented with 5 mM uracil and 10 mM (NH₄)₂SO₄, whereas the wt, ∆hapX mutant, and complemented strains were cultured on ANM without uracil (Table 1). The conidia were collected from a 10 day old culture by scraping the colony surface and resuspending them in a sterile normal saline-tween solution (0.1 % v/v tween 40, 0.85 % w/v NaCl). The suspension was filtered through sterile glass wool to remove the mycelia. The conidial concentration was determined using a hemocytometer.

Talaromyces marneffei hapX gene deletion and complementation

To investigate the role of hapX in T. marneffei, the hapX open reading frame plus 2.2 kb upstream and downstream flanking regions were amplified from the genomic DNA of FRR2161 strain with hapX-Apa I (F) and hapX-Sac I (R) primers. A PCR reaction was performed using a Phusion Hot start High-Fidelity DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Espoo, Finland). The amplification was started at 98 ∘C for 2 min; followed by 35 cycles of 98 ∘C for 10 s, 57 ∘C for 30 s, 72 ∘C for 3.5 min, a final extension at 72 ∘C for 10 min, and hold 4 ∘C. A 4.56 kb product was purified by using GeneJET Gel extraction kit (Thermo Scientific, California, USA). The purified product and pGEM-T vector (Promega) were cut with ApaI and SacI restriction enzyme and purified. They were ligated together in a ligation reaction 1:1 molar ratio (insert: vector) and T4 DNA ligase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The ligation mixtures were plated and selected on a Luria-Bertani (LB) agar containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin. To generate a hapX deletion construct, a pGEM-T plasmid backbone containing 5′ and 3′ flanking regions without hapX gene was amplified by inverse PCR with Inverse-hapX-PstI and Inverse-hapX-SmaI primers. A PCR reaction was performed using a Phusion® Hot start High-Fidelity DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Espoo, Finland). The amplification was started at 98 ∘C for 2 min; followed by 35 cycles of 98 ∘C for 10 s, 65 ∘C for 30 s, 72 ∘C for 2 min, a final extension at 72 ∘C for 10 min , and hold 4 ∘C. A 6.1 kb product was purified by using GeneJET Gel extraction kit (Thermo Scientific, California, USA). After purification, it was ligated with an Aspergillus nidulans pyrG (AnpyrG) selectable marker, which was cut from a pAB4626 vector. Subsequently, the hapX deletion plasmid was transformed into Escherichia coli NEB10β and isolated. A linear fragment of hapX gene deletion construct plasmid was amplified with hapX-ApaI (F) and hapX-SacI (R) primers, and the linear DNA was used for transformation into T. marneffei G816 protoplasts (Bugeja et al., 2012).

To construct the complemented strain, the PCR product of the hapX gene was amplified using NotI-hapX (F) and NotI-hapX (R) primers, then digested with NotI restriction enzyme. The NotI fragment from the PCR product of the hapX gene was ligated into NotI-digested and dephosphorylated pAB4626 vector, yielding the pChapX. The plasmid pChapX was then transformed in E. coli NEB10β and isolated. The primers are described in supplementary table S1. 5-fluororotic acid was used to select the spontaneous pyrG mutant (uracil auxotroph) from the hapX deletion mutant strain, and then the mutant was complemented by the pChapX vector containing the hapX gene plus the selectable marker (pyrG) gene.

HapX sequence and motif analysis

To assess HapX conservation in T. marneffei in comparison with other pathogenic fungi, gene structures of hapX orthologs from A. fumigatus, A. nidulans, C. albicans (hap43), P. brasiliensis, and H. capsulatum were visualized using the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS) (https://gsds.gao-lab.org/) based on genomic and coding sequences retrieved from NCBI. HapX orthologs were identified by BLASTP searches using A. fumigatus HapX as the query sequence. Conserved protein motifs were analyzed using the MEME Suite (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme), and multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis were performed using Clustal Omega.

Conserved domains and interaction-related features were analyzed using InterProScan and Pfam (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/search/sequence/). Putative HapX-centered functional association networks were inferred using the STRING database to support functional interpretation. Prediction of HapX target genes in T. marneffei was performed by scanning 2-kb upstream regions of annotated genes in the T. marneffei ATCC18224 genome for the conserved HapX-CCAAT-binding complex (CBC) motif (N5CCAATN30). Upstream sequences were received from NCBI, and motif localization was manually examined using SnapGene Viewer 6.1.1.

Morphological characterization and growth analysis of the ΔhapX deletion strain

Macroscopic morphologies of the T. marneffei wt, ΔhapX mutant, and complemented strains were examined under conditions supporting both mold and yeast growth. Each strain was cultured on ANM agar and incubated at 25 °C or 37 °C for 7 days to induce the mold and yeast phases, respectively. Colony morphologies were recorded using a stereomicroscope (Drawell, China).

Microscopic examination of the mold phase was performed using a slide culture technique. Briefly, all strains were cultivated on ANM agar at 25 °C for 12 days, and hyphal morphology was observed under a bright field microscope (Nikon Eclipse 50i, Tokyo, Japan). For analysis of yeast-phase morphology in liquid culture, conidia were inoculated into ANM broth and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 12 days. Cells exhibiting yeast-like morphology were subsequently observed and imaged using light microscopy. Cell wall morphology was further assessed using 0.1 % (w/v) calcofluor white (CFW) staining (fluorescent brightener 28, Sigma-Aldrich). Stained samples were examined using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse 50i, Tokyo, Japan).

To evaluate the role of hapX in fungal growth and conidiation, 10⁶ conidia of each strain were spotted onto ANM agar and incubated at 25 and 37 °C for up to 12 days. Colony diameters of six independent colonies per strain were measured at day 3, 5, 7, and 12 throughout the incubation period. Conidial production was quantified at day 12 by harvesting conidia via cotton swab scraping and resuspending them in sterile normal saline supplemented with Tween 40.

Germination assays were performed by inoculating 10⁶ conidia of each strain into 300 μL of ANM broth and incubating the cultures at 25 °C or 37 °C with continuous shaking at 150 rpm. Germination was monitored at 3 h intervals for up to 24 h by microscopic examination.

Investigation of growth in iron-deplete and iron-replete conditions

To evaluate the role of HapX in iron-dependent growth and stress tolerance, conidia of T. marneffei wt, ΔhapX mutant, and complemented strains were prepared and quantified using a hemocytometer. For growth on solid media, conidial suspensions of each strain were adjusted to 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL, and 5 μL aliquots were spotted onto ANM agar supplemented with iron or various stressors. Iron-dependent growth was assessed under three conditions: normal condition (ANM), iron-depleted condition (ANM lacking iron supplementation), and iron-repleted condition (ANM supplemented with 100 μM ferric ammonium citrate). Then, plates were incubated at 25 °C or 37 °C for up to 12 days, and colony size and morphology were monitored and photographed after 3 days of incubation. For liquid growth assays, conidia were cultured in ANM broth under the same conditions, after which fungal biomass was harvested by vacuum filtration through 0.45 μm membrane filters, dried at 60 °C for 7 days, and quantified by measuring biomass dry weight.

Stress and antifungal susceptibility assays

Growth and stress susceptibility of T. marneffei wt, ΔhapX mutant, and complemented strains were examined using solid and liquid culture assays. Conidia were harvested, quantified using a hemocytometer, and adjusted to a final concentration of 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL. For solid-medium assays, 5 μL aliquots of conidial suspensions were spotted onto ANM agar supplemented with oxidative, nitrosative, or cell wall stressors. Oxidative stress was induced using hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂; 1 to 4 mM). Nitrosative stress was induced using sodium nitrite (NaNO₂; 4 mM). Cell wall and membrane stressors were induced using sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS; 0.004, 0.008, 0.016, and 0.032 % w/v) or congo red (CR; 100, 200, 300, and 400 μg/mL). Plates were incubated at 25 °C or 37 °C for up to 12 days, and colony size and morphology were recorded and photographed after 3 days of incubation. In addition, amphotericin B (10 μg/mL) and itraconazole (0.1 μg/mL) were utilized for antifungal sensitivity testing. For liquid growth assays, conidia were inoculated into ANM broth at a final concentration of 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL and exposed to the same stress conditions. Cultures were incubated at 25 °C for 3 days to promote the mold phase or at 37 °C for 5 days to induce the yeast phase. Fungal biomass was harvested by vacuum filtration through 0.45 μm membrane filters, dried at 60 °C for 7 days, and growth was quantified by measuring dry biomass weight.

Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR)

T. marneffei ATCC18224, ΔhapX, and ΔhapX::hapX+ strains were prepared to 107 conidia/ml in 100 mL iron-free ANM broth supplemented with different iron concentration; 0, 10, and 100 μM ferric ammonium citrate. The cultures were incubated at either 25 °C for 3 days or 37 °C for 5 days before harvesting the fungal cells.

For stress treatment experiments, all strains were initially grown in ANM broth at 25 °C or 37 °C for 48 h. Cultures were then exposed to the following stress conditions for 2 h: SDS (0.01 %, w/v) or Congo red (CR; 300 µg/mL) for cell wall stress, hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂; 0.5 %, v/v) for oxidative stress, and sodium nitrite (NaNO₂; 4 mM) for nitrosative stress. After that, the cultures were collected by centrifugation at 4500 rpm for 10 min, and the RNA was extracted from the cells.

The total RNA was extracted from the cells by using a TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The mycelia were mechanically broken with 0.5 mm glass beads in a bead beater (Biospec, Bartlesville, OK, USA). Total RNA was isolated following the manufacturer’s protocol. One microgram of the isolated total RNA was converted to cDNA using Maxime RT PreMix (LiliF Diagnostics, Korea). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the SensiFAST™ SYBR Lo-ROX Kit (Bioline Ltd, UK) on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Primer sequences for selected genes are provided in Supplementary Table S2. The relative expression level of each gene was tested in the following conditions: one cycle of 95 °C, 60 sec; followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C, 60 sec and 60 °C, 60 sec. Gene expression levels were normalized to the actin gene and analyzed using the 2−ΔCt method. For each strain, log2 fold change values were calculated relative to the corresponding normal iron condition, and expression levels were compared between the wt and ΔhapX strains under different iron and stress conditions. All experiments were performed with three biological replicates, each analyzed in technical triplicate.

Survival of T. marneffei inside phagocytic (amoeba) cells

To investigate survival of T. marneffei ΔhapX mutant inside of phagocytic cells, the intracellular survival assay was modified as previously described (Pruksaphon et al., 2022). Acanthamoeba castellanii strain 30,234 was used for the amoeba infection model. The cells were maintained in a peptone yeast glucose (PYG) broth at 28 °C. The amoeba were removed from T-75 tissue culture flask, washed with PBS, and counted with a hemocytometer. The cells were suspended to 106 cells/mL in PBS. They were added to 6 well plates at 106 cells/mL, and allowed to adhere for 2 h at 28 °C before the addition of fungal cells, T. marneffei wt, ΔhapX mutant, and complemented strains at MOI as 10. Then plates were spun at 500 rpm for 10 min to conidia exposure to the amoebas and then the samples were incubated at 28 °C. After allowing phagocytosis to occur for 2 h, the media containing non-phagocytosed conidia was removed. Then, the cells were rinsed three times with PBS. To verify percentage of phagocytosis, amoebas were treated by 1 % triton X-100 to get intracellular conidia. 100 μL of internalized conidia was diluted and plated on SDA agar. The plate was then incubated at 28 °C for colony forming unit (CFU) count. The percentage of phagocytosis was determined by comparing the number of conidia internalized at 2 h with the initial inoculum, according to the formula below:

% Phagocytosis = (CFU internalized at 2 h / CFU inoculum added)×100

% Phagocytosis = (CFU internalized at 2 h / CFU inoculum added)×100

Moreover, the CFUs harvested from cell lysates at two hours were used as the initial inocula that acted as the baseline values for intracellular survival analysis. CFUs harvested at 24 h were used for calculation of the percentage recovery of fungal conidia inside amoebas. The percentage of fungal recovery was calculated as:

% Recovery = (CFU at 24 h / CFU at 2 h)×100

% Recovery = (CFU at 24 h / CFU at 2 h)×100

All experiments were performed in triplicates.

Galleria mellonella infection and virulence assay

To determine the potential virulence of the various strains, G. mellonella killing assay was performed. Larvae, weighing between 300 and 350 mg, were used for all experiments. Sample size was determined using an online tool (https://clincalc.com/stats/samplesize.aspx) for a chi-squared test comparing two proportions (control survival = 75 %, treatment survival = 25 %, α = 0.05, power = 0.8), resulting in an estimated requirement of 15 larvae per group. To test comparative virulence, T. marneffei wt, ΔhapX mutant, and complemented strains were tested, with PBS (vehicle only) and methanol (lethal) as control groups. Larvae (15 per group, with three independent replicates; total of 225 larvae) were inoculated with 20 μL of conidia suspension containing 1 × 10⁷ cells or 20 μL of PBS or methanol (25 %). Following inoculation, larvae were incubated at 37 °C for up to 10 days, and survival was monitored every 24 h. Larval death was defined as the absence of movement in response to tactile stimulation with a sterile pipette tip. To observe the in vivo morphology of the fungus, infected larvae were dissected and stained with 0.1 % (w/v) CFW. Stained tissues were examined using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse 50i, Tokyo, Japan) and Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan). To quantify fungal burden, hemolymph was extracted from larvae at 5 days post-infection. Larvae were chilled on ice for 10 min, after which hemolymph was collected and serially diluted in 1× PBS. Fungal burden per larva was determined by counting CFUs on SDA following incubation at 28 °C for 72 h. Furthermore, to assess host immune responses, total hemocyte counts were determined from hemolymph samples. Hemolymph was smeared onto glass slides, air-dried, and stained using Wright-Giemsa stain. Hemocyte populations were examined and counted under a light microscope, and hemocyte types were classified based on morphological characteristics.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using tests appropriate to each experiment, including two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple-comparison test, two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple-comparison test, chi-square test, and log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test for survival analysis. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, P < 0.001, or P < 0.0001. Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10 and IBM SPSS Statistics 29.

Results

HapX is required for iron-dependent growth in Talaromyces marneffei

To characterize the bZIP transcription factor HapX in T. marneffei, a HapX homolog was identified by BLASTP, sharing 30.32 % overall amino acid identity with HapX from A. fumigatus. Sequence analysis revealed conserved structural features characteristic of HapX family transcription factors, including an N-terminal region previously reported to mediate interaction with the CCAAT-binding complex, a conserved bZIP DNA-binding domain, and multiple cysteine-rich motifs containing a putative iron-sensing motif (CGFCN₅CNC) (Supplementary Figures S1 and S2) (Gsaller et al., 2014).

To investigate the functional role of HapX, a ∆hapX deletion mutant was generated by replacing the coding sequence of hapX (PMAA_083210) with the pyrG selectable marker. A complemented strain was subsequently constructed by reintroducing hapX into the mutant background (Supplementary Figure S3). Under normal growth conditions, no detectable differences in colony diameter or colony morphology were observed among the wt, ∆hapX mutant, and complemented strains during the mold phase at 28 °C at each assessed time point (days 3, 5, 7, and 12). Similarly, growth of the three strains was comparable during yeast phase cultivation at 37 °C (Supplementary Figure S4). These results indicate that deletion of hapX did not affect growth of T. marneffei under standard growth conditions in either morphotype.

In A. fumigatus, HapX has been implicated in the regulation of iron homeostasis (Lopez-Berges et al., 2021). To determine whether HapX plays a similar role in T. marneffei, growth of the wt, ∆hapX mutant, and complemented strains was examined under defined iron conditions in both mold and yeast phases. During iron restriction, the ΔhapX mutant exhibited a significant reduction in growth during mold-phase cultivation, as evidenced by reduced colony expansion and decreased biomass (Fig. 1A–E). A comparable iron-dependent growth defect was also observed during yeast-phase growth (Fig. 2A–E). In addition to impaired growth, iron limitation resulted in pronounced alterations in colony morphology in the ΔhapX mutant, characterized by thinner and more translucent colonies compared with the grayish-green pigmentation and velvety to powdery surface of the wt and complemented strains. Moreover, microscopic examination revealed no obvious differences in hyphal or yeast cell morphology between the ΔhapX mutant and control strains under any tested condition (Fig. 1B, 2B, and Supplementary Figure S5). The altered macroscopic colony appearance under iron-deficient conditions suggested defects in conidiation. Consistent with this observation, quantitative analysis demonstrated that the ΔhapX strain produced significantly fewer conidia than both control strains (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, conidial germination assays revealed a markedly reduced germination rate in the ΔhapX mutant compared with the wt and complemented strains in both morphotypes (Fig. 1G and 2F). Importantly, iron repletion restored colony morphology, biomass production, conidiation, and germination efficiency in the ΔhapX mutant to levels comparable to those of the wt and complemented strains, indicating that the observed phenotypic defects were specifically associated with iron limitation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that HapX is required for iron-dependent growth and developmental processes in T. marneffei.

HapX is required for iron-dependent transcriptional regulation and modulates iron acquisition in T. marneffei

To investigate the role of HapX in iron-dependent transcriptional regulation across the dimorphic forms of T. marneffei, the expression levels of key iron-related genes (hapX, sreA, sidA, sidC, sidF, sidX, ftrA, and fetC) were examined by qRT-PCR in both mold (28 °C) and yeast (37 °C) phases. Transcript levels under low-iron and high-iron conditions were normalized to those under normal conditions and expressed as log2 fold change. Data represent the mean of three independent biological replicates and are presented as heat maps comparing the wt and ΔhapX mutant strains (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figure S6–7). Under low-iron conditions, the wt strain showed increased log2 fold changes in hapX expression together with elevated expression of siderophore biosynthesis genes (sidA, sidC, sidF, and sidX) in both phases. In contrast, the ΔhapX mutant exhibited lower expression levels of these siderophore biosynthesis genes than the wt strain in both mold and yeast phases. Consistent with these transcriptional patterns, siderophore production was significantly reduced in the ΔhapX mutant compared with the wt strain. In the mold phase under low-iron conditions, genes encoding components of the reductive iron assimilation (RIA) system, ftrA and fetC, displayed higher expression in the wt strain, whereas their expression in the ΔhapX mutant remained close to baseline. In the yeast phase, expression of ftrA and fetC showed only minor changes in both strains. Under high-iron conditions, iron-responsive genes in the wt strain showed minimal changes in both morphological phases. In the ΔhapX mutant, sreA expression was higher than that in the wt strain during the mold phase. By contrast, in the yeast phase, expression of sreA increased to similar levels in both strains. Collectively, these data demonstrate that loss of HapX alters iron-responsive transcriptional programs under both iron-limiting and iron-replete conditions in T. marneffei.

Loss of HapX impairs cell wall and membrane stress tolerance in T. marneffei

Whether loss of HapX is associated with altered tolerance to cell wall and membrane stress was further examined by comparing growth of the wt, ΔhapX, and complemented strains under different concentrations of Congo red and SDS in both mold and yeast phases. Under cell wall stress conditions, the ΔhapX mutant showed concentration-dependent growth defects relative to the wt and complemented strains in both forms. These defects in the ΔhapX mutant were apparent as reduced colony diameter on solid media and decreased dry weight in liquid cultures (Fig. 4). Moreover, SDS exposure revealed phase-specific differences in membrane stress tolerance. In the mold phase, the wt and complemented strains tolerated SDS concentrations up to 0.016 %, whereas the ΔhapX mutant showed no detectable growth at this concentration. In the yeast phase, growth differences among strains were observed at lower SDS levels, with the ΔhapX mutant displaying reduced growth at 0.008 % compared with the wt and complemented strains (Fig. 5).

Disruption of hapX impairs nitrosative but not oxidative stress tolerance in T. marneffei

To determine whether HapX contributes to oxidative stress resistance, the wt, ΔhapX, and complemented strains were exposed to various concentrations of hydrogen peroxide in both mold and yeast phases. We found that no significant differences in growth were observed among the strains under this condition, indicating that HapX is not required for oxidative stress tolerance in T. marneffei (Supplementary Figure S8). Consistently, the expression of sodA, a key oxidative stress-responsive gene, showed no substantial difference between the wt and ΔhapX strains under oxidative stress condition.

Furthermore, upon exposure to sodium nitrite, no significant growth differences were detected among the wt, ΔhapX, and complemented strains in the mold phase. In contrast, in the yeast phase, the ΔhapX mutant exhibited significantly reduced growth compared with the wt and complemented strains (Fig. 6A–B). Under combined nitrosative and iron-depleted conditions, the ΔhapX mutant showed a more pronounced growth defect in both mold and yeast phases. Microscopic examination further revealed that the mutant population contained a higher proportion of swollen conidia and ungerminated spores compared to the wt and complemented strains, indicating impaired germination under these stress conditions (Fig. 6C–D and Supplementary Figure S9). Given the increased sensitivity of the ΔhapX mutant to nitrosative stress in the yeast phase, we next examined the expression of sakA, a key MAP kinase in the high-osmolarity glycerol (HOG) pathway. Under nitrosative stress conditions, sakA expression was strongly induced in the wild-type yeast phases. In contrast, this induction was significantly reduced in the ΔhapX mutant, indicating a defect in stress-responsive signaling (Fig. 7, Fig. 8).

Disruption of hapX alters antifungal susceptibility in T. marneffei

We further examined the effect of hapX deletion on fungal growth in the presence of an antifungal agent. Changes in drug susceptibility were assessed by spot dilution assays, comparing the ΔhapX mutant with the wt and complemented strains under amphotericin B and itraconazole exposure. In the mold phase, the ΔhapX mutant exhibited reduced growth relative to the wt and complemented strains. In the yeast phase, growth was not detected in the ΔhapX mutant, whereas the wt and complemented strains retained detectable growth under the same conditions (Fig. 9A–B). A similar trend was observed under itraconazole treatment, where the ΔhapX mutant showed increased sensitivity compared with the wt and complemented strains in both morphotypes (Fig. 9C–D).

The hapX deletion strain exhibits reduced survival within phagocytic amoeba cells

To determine whether HapX contributes to the survival of T. marneffei within phagocytic cells, amoebae were co-incubated with conidia of the wt, ΔhapX mutant, complemented strains at 28 °C. After co-incubation, fungal survival was quantified by determining colony-forming units recovered from non-phagocytosed and phagocytosed fungal populations. At 2 h post co-incubation, representing the baseline, CFU counts were comparable among strains (wt: 9.77 × 10⁵ cells/mL; ΔhapX: 9.78 × 10⁵ cells/mL; complemented strain: 9.74 × 10⁵ cells/mL). After 24 h, survival of the ΔhapX mutant decreased significantly (41.73 %) compared with the wt (52.72 %) and complemented strains (61.19 %) (Fig. 10). Therefore, these findings demonstrate that HapX does not alter conidial uptake, but the transcription factor contributes to intracellular survival of T. marneffei within phagocytic amoeba cells.

Virulence attenuation upon hapX deletion in Galleria mellonella

To further evaluate the role of HapX in fungal virulence under non-mammalian host-relevant conditions, a G. mellonella infection model was utilized at 37 °C, a temperature corresponding to the pathogenic form of T. marneffei. Larvae were injected with conidia of wt, ΔhapX mutant, or complemented strains. Then, survival rate was monitored daily until 10 days. Larvae injected with PBS served as a negative control, and 25 % methanol was used as a toxicity control (Fig. 11A). Compared to larvae infected with the ΔhapX mutant, infection with the wt or complemented strains resulted in a more rapid larval mortality, with all larvae dying by day 10 (Fig. 11B). In contrast, 80 % of larvae infected with the ΔhapX mutant were alive at day 10. Consistent with the survival data, quantification of fungal burden revealed significantly reduced CFU recovery from larvae infected with the ΔhapX mutant compared with those infected with the wt or complemented strains (Fig. 11C). Hemocyte responses of larvae were also examined. Total hemocyte counts were significantly increased in larvae infected with the wt or complemented strains compared with the PBS control, whereas counts were similar between PBS treated and ΔhapX mutant-infected animals (Fig. 11D). Differential hemocyte analysis showed that plasmatocytes were the predominant population, accounting for approximately 80–90 % of total hemocytes, followed by granulocytes at 10 %, with no significant differences in subtype proportions among the wt, ΔhapX mutant, and complemented strain (Fig. 11E–F). To assess fungal morphology in vivo, fungal cells recovered from infected larvae at day 5 post-infection were stained with calcofluor white and examined by fluorescence microscopy, exhibiting a mixture of fission yeast and elongated yeast-like morphologies across all strains (Fig. 12 and supplementary figure S10), with no obvious differences observed among the wild-type, ΔhapX mutant, and complemented strains. Collectively, these results demonstrate that loss of HapX leads to significantly attenuated virulence in T. marneffei, as evidenced by increased host survival, reduced fungal burden, and altered host immune responses.

Discussion

Iron homeostasis is a central determinant of fungal survival and pathogenicity, particularly for intracellular pathogens that must adapt to iron-restricted host environments. In this study, we demonstrate that the bZIP transcription factor HapX plays a critical role in coordinating iron-dependent growth, stress adaptation, intracellular survival, and virulence in the thermally dimorphic pathogen T. marneffei. Collectively, our findings support a model in which HapX functions as a key regulator of iron-responsive transcriptional programs that contribute to pathogenic fitness in both T. marneffei mold and yeast phases.

Deletion of hapX from T. marneffei resulted in significant growth defects under iron-limited conditions in both forms, whereas growth under iron-replete conditions remained largely unaffected. This phenotype is consistent with the conserved role of HapX as an iron starvation-responsive transcriptional regulator reported in A. fumigatus (Schrettl et al., 2010), P. brasiliensis (Rocha et al., 2025), and Fusarium oxysporum (Lopez-Berges et al., 2012). Importantly, the absence of major morphological abnormalities in the ΔhapX mutant further suggests that HapX influences metabolic and physiological adaptation rather than morphogenetic programs in T. marneffei.

In addition, the defects in conidiation and reduced germination efficiency observed in the ΔhapX mutant indicate that HapX also contributes to developmental fitness, likely by ensuring sufficient iron availability during energy-intensive processes such as spore formation and outgrowth. Consistent with this, loss of HapX led to altered expression of multiple iron-responsive genes, particularly those involved in siderophore biosynthesis. Under iron starvation, the ΔhapX mutant exhibited significantly reduced expression of sidA, sidC, sidF, and sidX in both mold and yeast phases. Consequently, siderophore production was decreased. These results suggest a positive regulatory role of HapX in siderophore-mediated iron acquisition in T. marneffei. In several pathogenic fungi, HapX operates within an antagonistic regulatory circuit with the GATA-type transcription factor SreA to maintain iron homeostasis. In P. brasiliensis, loss of HapX resulted in inappropriate upregulation of sreA under iron-limited conditions, thereby disrupting the iron starvation response (Rocha et al., 2025). Similarly, in A. nidulans, SreA represses hapX under iron-replete conditions, whereas HapX represses sreA during iron starvation (Haas et al., 1999; Hortschansky et al., 2007). Consistent with this regulatory framework, our previous work demonstrated that deletion of sreA in T. marneffei results in increased extracellular siderophore production, confirming its role as a negative regulator of iron acquisition (Amsri et al., 2022). Together, these observations support a model in which HapX promotes iron acquisition both by activating iron-responsive genes and by counteracting SreA-mediated repression under iron-limited conditions. Additional support for this model may be inferred from promoter motif and functional association analyses. Several HapX-responsive genes appear to harbor CCAAT motifs in their upstream regions, suggesting potential regulation through the CCAAT-binding complex (Supplementary Table S3) (Moye-Rowley, 2022). In parallel, STRING-based functional association analysis suggests coordinated clustering of HapX-associated genes involved in iron metabolism and iron-dependent cellular processes (Supplementary Figure S11). We acknowledge that these predictive and associative approaches do not constitute direct experimental validation of regulatory interactions and are therefore presented as supportive evidence only. Future studies employing genome-wide transcriptomic profiling and chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) will be necessary to experimentally delineate the direct transcriptional targets and mechanistic regulatory networks governed by HapX.

In addition to its role in iron acquisition, HapX may also contribute to stress adaptation in T. marneffei, particularly in maintaining cell wall integrity. Deletion of hapX increased sensitivity to cell wall and membrane disrupting agents such as congo red and SDS, suggesting iron-dependent regulation may influence pathways involved in cell wall biosynthesis or remodeling. Consistent with these phenotypes, expression analyses of key cell wall integrity pathway genes, including fks1, chsB, and mpkA, were altered in the ΔhapX mutant compared to the wild-type strain under stress conditions. These findings suggest that HapX may influence the regulation of cell wall integrity pathways, potentially through iron-dependent processes. Similarly, studies in other fungi, including P. brasiliensis and F. oxysporum, have shown that HapX negatively regulates genes related to cell wall biogenesis under iron-limited conditions. In P. brasiliensis, HapX silencing resulted in derepression of glucan and chitin modifying enzymes and increased glucan content, which was proposed to help maintain cell integrity during iron starvation (Rocha et al., 2025).

In contrast to its effects on cell wall and membrane stress tolerance, loss of HapX selectively compromised resistance to nitrosative stress, while oxidative stress tolerance remained largely unaffected. Consistent with this observation, expression of sodA was not markedly altered, whereas expression of sakA, a central component of the HOG pathway, was affected under nitrosative stress conditions, particularly in the yeast phase. This is in agreement with the phenotype showing increased sensitivity of the ΔhapX mutant to nitrosative stress in yeast but not in the mold form, suggesting a morphotype-specific role of HapX in stress adaptation. Notably, although the ΔhapX mutant exhibited reduced colony growth under NaNO₂ stress conditions, microscopic analysis revealed that both wt and mutant strains were capable of forming elongated yeast-like cells. Instead, the mutant displayed a higher proportion of conidia and swollen cells, indicating impaired germination or delayed developmental progression rather than a defect in phase transition. This phenotype was further more severe under combined iron-limiting and nitrosative stress conditions, supporting a role for HapX in coordinating stress adaptation and cellular development under conditions relevant to the host. In fungi, detoxification of nitric oxide is primarily mediated by flavohemoglobins, which convert NO to nitrate and whose activity depends on heme and iron availability (Gardner, 2025). Moreover, regulation of flavohemoglobin expression in fungal systems is typically mediated by specific transcriptional regulators, rather than by global stress response pathways (Merhej et al., 2015). Given the central role of HapX orthologs in regulating iron homeostasis through interaction with the CCAAT-binding complex, the nitrosative stress sensitivity observed in the ΔhapX mutant might be attributable to impaired iron-dependent NO detoxification, rather than a direct involvement of HapX in nitric oxide sensing (Furukawa et al., 2020; Oberegger et al., 2025). Moreover, the phase-specific susceptibility to nitrosative stress may suggest that HapX-mediated coordination of iron availability and flavohemoglobin-dependent RNS detoxification is particularly important for protecting the pathogenic yeast form against host-derived reactive nitrogen species encountered within phagocytic cells. However, the mechanism by which HapX influences flavohemoglobin function and nitrosative stress resistance requires further investigation.

Consistent with this interpretation, the ΔhapX mutant exhibited significantly reduced survival within phagocytic amoeba. Free-living amoebae have been proposed as environmental training grounds for pathogens including dimorphic fungi (Price et al., 2024; Pruksaphon et al., 2022; Steenbergen et al., 2004), and impaired survival in this model suggests that HapX-dependent iron adaptation contributes to resistance against intracellular killing mechanisms. These findings support the concept that iron homeostasis regulators not only mediate nutrient acquisition but also enhance tolerance to host-imposed stresses during intracellular residence. Moreover, attenuated virulence of the ΔhapX mutant in the G. mellonella infection model further demonstrates the importance of HapX for pathogenicity. Reduced larval mortality and fungal burden, together with altered hemocyte responses, indicate that HapX is required for effective proliferation and immune evasion in vivo. Notably, reduced virulence associated with ΔhapX deletion has also been reported in several other pathogenic fungi, indicating that HapX plays a conserved role in promoting fungal pathogenicity (Peng et al., 2020; Schrettl et al., 2010). Additionally, the increased susceptibility of the ΔhapX mutant to amphotericin B, together with its enhanced sensitivity to the azole antifungal itraconazole, suggests that disruption of iron homeostasis may affect membrane integrity and function, thereby increasing susceptibility to membrane-targeting antifungal agents. Although the precise mechanisms remain to be defined, these findings are consistent with a link between iron regulation and fungal membrane homeostasis. Given that T. marneffei encounters iron limitation, nitrosative stress, and host-derived membrane-targeting defenses during infection, the combined defects observed in the ΔhapX mutant likely contribute to reduced fitness in the host environment.

An important limitation of the present study is that the global transcriptional landscape and the direct genomic targets of HapX were not examined. Although phenotypic analyses revealed clear defects in stress tolerance and virulence-associated traits, the extent to which these effects result from direct versus indirect regulation by HapX remains unresolved. In particular, genes involved in cell wall remodeling, nitrosative stress response, and iron-dependent metabolic pathways were not systematically assessed at the transcriptome level. Thus, further investigation using RNA sequencing under defined iron and stress conditions will be required to comprehensively identify HapX-responsive genes and regulatory networks. In parallel, chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by ChIP sequencing will be essential to determine direct HapX binding sites and to distinguish primary targets from downstream effects. Together, these approaches will provide mechanistic insight into how HapX integrates iron homeostasis with stress adaptation in T. marneffei.

Conclusion

This study shows that HapX is an important regulator of iron homeostasis and pathogenic fitness in T. marneffei. Deletion of hapX caused clear growth defects under iron-limited conditions in both mold and yeast phases. The mutant also showed reduced siderophore production and altered expression of iron-related genes, supporting a conserved role of HapX in the iron starvation response. In addition, loss of HapX increased sensitivity to cell wall and membrane stress, reduced tolerance to nitrosative stress in the yeast phase, and increased susceptibility to amphotericin B. These phenotypes suggest an association between HapX function and fungal stress tolerance under iron-limited or host-relevant conditions. Importantly, the ΔhapX mutant showed reduced survival inside phagocytic amoebae and significantly lower virulence in a G. mellonella infection model. Overall, our findings demonstrate that HapX links iron regulation with stress adaptation and intracellular survival, which together contribute to virulence in T. marneffei. Therefore, HapX-dependent regulatory pathways may represent potential targets for the development of new antifungal strategies against T. marneffei and other dimorphic fungal pathogens.
