Haloferax mediterranei R4 for bioremediation of desalination and textile wastes: a step towards their valorisation
Authors: Iraide Sáez-Zamacona, Rosa María Martínez-Espinosa, Guillermo Grindlay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHaloferax mediterranei has shown strong potential for bioremediating brines contaminated with nitrogenous compounds, oxychlorides, and metals, making it a promising candidate for treating saline wastewater. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate its capacity to bioremediate industrial wastewaters (IWWs) from desalination and textile industries. After characterising IWWs and formulating suitable media, growth was compared with two control media: an optimal medium (NH4+, 20% salts) and a control medium providing basic nutrients and ionic strength (NO3−, 15% salts). Raw IWWs were heterogeneous and unsuitable per se for optimal growth, requiring supplementation with minimal essential nutrients (C, N, P, and Fe). H. mediterranei grown in IWWs media exhibited reduced growth and increased cell pigmentation, indicating stress. Particularly, textile residues induced tenfold higher carotenoid production than the optimal medium and only fourfold less than the carotenoid production medium. Carotenoid extract composition showed variations within different IWWs, but bacterioruberin revealed as the main natural pigment in all cases. Elemental biomass analysis showed Fe and Zn accumulation, as metalloprotein cofactors for stress tolerance, and Ca, related to exopolysaccharide production. Despite the stress, H. mediterranei effectively removed 60–90% of the NO3− in the wastes—among the higher removal rate values reported for matrices under comparable salinities and initial NO3− loads. While further optimisation of waste mixtures is needed to improve nutrient balance and eliminate the need for supplementation, our findings suggest that IWWs from both industries can support H. mediterranei growth, enabling a cost-effective, industrially viable bioremediating strategy with the added value from pigment production.Key points Haloferax mediterranei removed up to 90% of nitrates from industrial wastes (IWWs)IWWs bioremediation enhanced carotenoid production, a valuable biomoleculeExtremophile-based strategy enables circular reuse of high-salinity IWWsSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-025-13640-w.

Introduction

Industrial development has brought clear advancements, improving living standards and productivity of the processes (Rani et al. 2019). However, this progress has resulted in considerable environmental costs, particularly waste generation and mismanagement of residues (Singh et al. 2014; Kaushik et al. 2021). Among these, industrial wastewater represents a major environmental challenge because of its large volume and complex composition. Such effluents often contain mixtures of pollutants that can interact in unpredictable and toxic ways. Therefore, effective management requires tailored treatment approaches specific to each industry (Ranade and Bhandari 2014; Crini and Lichtfouse, 2019). In recent years, industries have started to actively consider wastewater recycling to promote sustainable development, improve water management, and mitigate health risks (Crini and Lichtfouse, 2019). Therefore, industries must secure a water supply and fully treated wastewater from high water-consuming sectors to enable its reuse. This need is particularly pressing in water-scarce regions such as the Mediterranean Basin (Noto et al. 2023), not only as a measure to protect ecosystems but also to contribute to water resource conservation. To address freshwater scarcity, desalination techniques have become more crucial (Gude 2017). However, this technique not only produces fresh water but also generates a waste byproduct: brine. This waste, together with the concentrated salts, can also contain concentrated contaminants (e.g. metals and metalloids, oxychlorides, N species) (Panagopoulos et al. 2019). Parallel to this, the textile, clothing, and footwear industries, key contributors to the global economy, are also among the largest generators of industrial wastewater (Imran et al. 2015) . Thus, their adverse effects are particularly significant in arid areas (Colin et al. 2016). Their effluents are characterised by strong colour, high salinity, high temperature, variable pH, and high DOC (Mantzavinos and Psillakis 2004). Moreover, many synthetic azo dyes and their metabolites are toxic, carcinogenic, and mutagenic, posing a serious risk to human health (Nilsson et al. 1993). In addition, dyes are challenging to treat with conventional chemical and physical procedures (Lim et al. 2010) due to their complex molecular structures. Consequently, the need for effective waste treatment, both for desalination and textile wastes, is crucial to ensure environmental protection and water security.

Industrial effluents are traditionally treated using physical, chemical, or biological methods, either separately or in combination (Ranade and Bhandari 2014). However, physicochemical approaches often generate secondary waste, and not all technologies are compatible with some residues; for instance, high salt wastes can lead to the corrosion of infrastructures. Within this context, microbial bioremediation has emerged as a promising and socially accepted alternative for wastewater treatment (Vidali 2001). Unlike conventional methods, bioremediation uses living organisms (i.e. microorganisms) to remove or transform pollutants into harmless substances, offering a clean, efficient, and environmentally friendly solution (Perpetuo et al. 2011; Rani et al. 2019; Kaushik et al. 2021). Despite challenges in maintaining optimal growth conditions, microbial adaptability is the keystone of bioremediation, as many organisms can metabolise diverse pollutants even under extreme conditions thanks to their enzymatic plasticity (Oger and Cario 2013). This metabolic flexibility allows bioremediation to operate under aerobic, anaerobic, or facultative conditions, and to use various pollutants as a source of energy (Hassan et al. 2017; Koul et al. 2021). This versatility makes bioremediation not only effective but also widely applicable across industries. Although bacteria have been the most employed microorganisms in bioremediation, their efficiency decreases markedly under extreme environmental conditions, such as those found in hypersaline wastewaters. Under such conditions, haloarchaea offer a valuable alternative due to their remarkable physiological plasticity and high salt tolerance. A particularly promising candidate is Haloferax mediterranei, which was first isolated from coastal salt ponds in Santa Pola, Alicante, Spain (Rodríguez-Valera et al. 1980) and has aroused scientific interest in the field of bioremediation due to its ability to thrive in polluted hypersaline environments. It can metabolise high concentrations of nitrates, nitrites, hydrocarbons, and oxychloride compounds (Nájera-Fernández et al. 2012; Oren et al., 2014; Matarredona et al. 2021), making possible the complete removal of those pollutants. Under aerobic conditions, it integrates nitrogen compounds into biomolecules like proteins and nucleic acids. In contrast, under anaerobic conditions, it uses them for respiration, releasing nitrogen gas as a byproduct thanks to denitrification (Nájera-Fernández et al. 2012). Beyond nitrogen removal, H. mediterranei has shown potential for heavy metal remediation (i.e. Cd and Cu) (Llorca and Martínez-Espinosa 2022; Sáez-Zamacona et al. 2023) and serves as a robust producer of commercially valuable biomolecules, such as carotenoids, bioplastics, and selenium nanoparticles (Antón et al. 1988; Giani et al. 2021; Guijarro-Ramírez et al. 2024). Recent investigations have made it possible to valorise candy residues by growing H. mediterranei cells on cultures derived from them, aiming to produce carotenoids (i.e. bacterioruberin (BR)) (Giani et al. 2021) and PHAs (Simó-Cabrera et al. 2024). This makes H. mediterranei particularly relevant for treating high-salinity waste because, unlike the bacteria commonly used in bioremediation, it can thrive under high salt concentrations where bacterial growth is inhibited (Meerbergen et al. 2017).

All the aspects discussed highlight the dual role of H. mediterranei not only as a bioremediation agent but also as a valuable tool for converting industrial waste into commercially relevant biomolecules. Thus, the integration of H. mediterranei into industrial wastewater treatment aligns with the principles of circular economy. In this strategy, waste is viewed as a resource rather than a byproduct. To this end, the main objective of this research was to evaluate the growth of H. mediterranei in different wastewaters derived from human industrial activities: desalination plant waste and textile industry waste. Moreover, it was intended to assess its bioremediation potential. Additionally, it was aimed to evaluate the suitability of these wastes to be used as growth media for H. mediterranei to produce highly marketed biomolecules, particularly natural pigments. This is, to the best of the knowledge of the authors, the first study exploring the potential of H. mediterranei for bioremediation of real textile and desalination wastes, while simultaneously producing carotenoids as valuable byproducts.

Materials and methods

Industrial wastewaters, reagents, and preparation of dilutions

Three samples coming from two industrial wastewaters were used: one sample from a desalination plant and two samples from a textile industry, the last two differing in the treatment employed by the industry to treat the waste.

The residue from the desalination plant (DPR) used comes from the desalination plant of the University of Alicante, which is located on the campus of San Vicent del Raspeig (Alicante, SE Spain) (38.387070°N, 0.519135°W) (https://sstti.ua.es/es/infraestructuras-de-apoyo/planta-de-tratamiento-de-aguas.html). This plant is supplied with saline water from an aquifer, and desalination is carried out through a reverse osmosis process. While the desalinated water is used for the irrigation of green areas on the campus, the generated waste (i.e. the brine) is discharged into the environment.

Regarding textile samples (TIR), two different textile wastewaters coming from a textile industry located in Villena (Alicante, Spain) (38.640125°N, 0.885987°W) were collected and assessed (https://texathenea.com/). Wastewater corresponded to the effluent after a filtration of coarse particles and a first biological treatment (TIR) (both containing high salt concentrations compared to tap water) and the raw effluent (TIRraw).

Regarding the dilutions prepared (to optimise the culture media recipes, etc.), these were prepared using high purity water with a resistivity > 18 MΩ from a Direct-Q 3 V water purification system (Millipore Inc., Paris, France) and all the dilutions were done by weight on an analytical balance to guarantee a higher accuracy. All reagents used in the present work are displayed in Table A.1 of the Appendices.

Characterisation of industrial wastewaters

To identify the composition of the wastewaters, a physicochemical characterisation was first carried out. The composition of the optimum growth medium served as a reference for later optimisation of the different residue-containing culture media. Parameters measured for the characterisation were (i) conductivity (and its equivalence in salinity), (ii) pH, (iii) BOD5, (iv) HCO3− and CO32−, (v) organic and inorganic ions, and (vi) elemental composition. Before any analysis, all industrial wastewaters were filtered through a 0.45-µm pore size hydrophilic nylon membrane filter (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) in a vacuum filtration system to avoid interferences from suspended particles.

For BOD5, measurement was done by measuring the difference between oxygen content at time 0 and 5 days (indicating biochemical oxygen consumption at 5 days) after sample incubation in darkness at 20 °C. This was done by measuring oxygen content using the pocket oxygen meter FireStingGO2 (Pyroscience). Carbonates (CO32−) and bicarbonates (HCO3−) concentrations were determined through volumetric titration using phenolphthalein and methyl orange as indicators and HCl 0.1 M as a titration agent. Regarding organic and inorganic ions, they were determined through ionic chromatography by first filtering residues through a 0.22-µm pore size filter (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Ionic chromatography was conducted using Metrohm, 850 ProfIC AnCat-MCS ionic chromatography with conductometric detection (operating conditions of the equipment are displayed in Table A.2 of Appendices). Finally, elemental analysis of the wastewaters was performed both by Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES) (Agilent 720 ICP-OES equipment (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA)) and Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectroscopy (ICP-MS) (Agilent 8900× ICP-MS equipment (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA)) (operating conditions of the equipment are displayed in Table A.3 of Appendices).

Culture media for Haloferax mediterranei R4 growth

Different culture media were prepared: (i) the preinoculum culture media, (ii) optimal medium for cellular growth (NH4+ as N source and 20% w/v of salts), (iii) control minimum mineral medium (NO3− as N source and 15% of salts), (iv) DPR culture media, and (v) TIR culture media. Three replicates of each culture (except for the preinoculum) were prepared to have statistically representative data. The strain Haloferax mediterranei R4 was isolated for the first time in Santa Pola’s salt ponds (Rodríguez-Valera et al. 1980) and, since then, it has been maintained by several research groups at the University of Alicante working with haloarchaea. It is kept at −80 °C in a storage solution containing 80% glycerol and 6% salt water and will be available upon request.

Preinoculum

To cultivate the haloarchaeon H. mediterranei R4, a preinoculum was prepared to later inoculate all the different culture media. Preinoculum for culture media inoculation was prepared with 20% w/v salt water (Rodríguez-Valera et al. 1980), 50 mM MOPS buffer, 15 mM ammonium chloride (NH4Cl), 1 mM inorganic phosphate (Na2PO4·12H2O – NaH2PO4·2H2O) (i.p.), 0.5% w/v glucose, and 5 mg L−1 iron chloride (FeCl3). pH of the culture media was adjusted to 7.3 using hydrochloric acid (HCl) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) before autoclaving (21 min at 121 °C). The last three components of culture media (FeCl3, i.p., and glucose) were added through microfiltration with 0.22-µm pore size sterile filters after autoclaving as per the optimised protocol previously reported (Martínez-Espinosa et al. 2001). Preinoculum was grown under optimal conditions in an incubator at 42 °C and 170 rpm to facilitate optimum oxygenation (aerobic conditions) of the culture media. Unless otherwise stated, media were prepared as just explained, and these were considered standard/optimal growth conditions. The preinoculum was grown until it reached the last part of the exponential phase (O.D.600 nm = 4.4) to reach the highest content of metabolically active cells. When that phase was reached, preinoculum was stored in a sterilised Falcon tube and stored at 4 °C. All media were inoculated using the same preinoculum.

Culture media

Two control media were prepared with different N sources (NH4+ and nitrate (NO3−)) and salt percentages (20% w/v and 15% w/v). The assimilation of NH4+ and its incorporation into amino acids is more bioenergetically favourable for H. mediterranei than the assimilation of NO3− as two more reactions (reduction of nitrate to nitrite and further reduction of nitrite to ammonium) are required compared to NH4+ assimilation (Esclapez et al. 2014a). Hence, control culture media with NH4+ were considered “optimal” media (OM), while media with NO3− were considered “control minimum mineral” media (CM). The final concentrations of nitrogen sources were 15 mM of NH4Cl (OM) and 15 mM of KNO3 (~930 mg kg−1) (CM). Moreover, control media were prepared at 15% w/v salt water to minimise reagents to evaluate the potential upscaling of the recipe for further applications based on circular economy approaches. The rest of the components and incubation conditions were the same as those explained for the preinoculum.

Supplementation of residue media

As part of the circular economy, it was aimed to use a lower variety and quantity of reagents. In terms of maintaining a constant pH of the culture media, it is necessary to buffer the different media. For that, three different buffers were tested whose working pH range included the optimum pH for H. mediterranei growth (pH = 7.3), according to Good’s buffer list (Good et al., 1972). Selected buffers were, from the cheapest and simplest to the more expensive and complex: NaH2PO4-Na2HPO4 buffer (pH = 7.3, 50 mM), TRIS-HCl (50 mM), and MOPS (50 mM). Moreover, according to the physicochemical characterisation of the different wastewaters, different supplementations were considered using the minimum quantity and variety of different nutrients to ensure the growth of H. mediterranei in culture media prepared with the wastewaters. Once supplemented, the residue culture media were inoculated by adding 500 µL of preinoculum in optimal conditions to 100 mL of media (0.5% v/v of the volume of the media). Before inoculation, the preinoculum was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and then it was resuspended in the same volume with the fresh culture media that was going to be inoculated. Later, the H. mediterranei R4 strain was grown under previously defined standard growth conditions.

Assessment of the cellular growth and other responses under bioremediation conditions of Haloferax mediterranei

The cellular growth of H. mediterranei grown in wastewater-based cultures was assessed through measuring the parameters: (i) adaptation phase, (ii) growth rate (µ), and (iii) maximum optical density value (O.D.600 nm) reached at the stationary phase. These parameters were determined from periodic growth monitoring of the microorganism by optical density measurements using the Cary 60 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) at a wavelength of 600 nm (Giani et al. 2021) (O.D.600 nm). When cultures reached an O.D.600 nm > 1, aliquots were diluted using 20% w/v salt water. The adaptation phase is calculated as the time the microorganism takes to start to grow (threshold established at 0.3 of the absorbance value to define the start of growth). The growth (µ) rate was calculated as the slope of the exponential phase in a semi-log graph.

Another physiological parameter measured was carotenoid production. Carotenoids are highly marketed biomolecules due to their biotechnological, medical, and industrial applications and are produced under stressful conditions by H. mediterranei (Giani et al. 2021). Therefore, once the microorganism was grown in the different media, the carotenoid content of the cellular pellet was assessed by measuring the absorbance of carotenoid extract through a UV-Vis spectrophotometer, as in the study conducted by Giani and co-workers (2021). Briefly, cell pellets were harvested at the end of the exponential phase by centrifugation (45 min, 7,830 rpm, 4 °C) and stored frozen overnight. Pellets were then resuspended in cold pure acetone (1:10, v/v) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After incubation, samples were centrifuged, and carotenoid content in the supernatant was quantified at 494 nm using a Cary 60 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), following Giani et al. (2021). Concentrations were normalised to both cell dry weight and culture volume to enable comparison with the optimum values reported by Giani et al. (2021) for the same haloarchaeon. For detailed identification of extract composition, carotenoids were analysed by high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS). HPLC analyses were conducted using a Zorbax Extend-C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.8 µm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) on an Agilent 1200 Series system. Compound identification was confirmed by an Agilent 6490 Triple Quadrupole LC/MS system equipped with an electrospray ionisation (ESI) jet stream source operating in positive scan mode over an m/z range of 300–900 with 0.1 atomic mass unit resolution. The system was operated and data processed using MassHunter Workstation Software (version B.05.00; Agilent Technologies). Instrumental parameters were set as follows: capillary voltage, 3000 V; gas flow, 11 L min⁻1; gas temperature, 290 °C; sheath gas flow, 12 L min⁻1; sheath gas temperature, 300 °C; and nebulizer pressure, 35 psi. The relative abundance of each carotenoid was assessed by comparing the peak area of each carotenoid in the different samples. The production of these natural pigments provides information about the cellular stress status and makes it possible to explore the valorisation of the wastewater to produce biomolecules that are highly marketed.

The bioremediation potential of the microorganism was analysed by measuring NO3− and other N species content at the beginning and the end of the incubation. NO3− concentration was estimated by the UV NO3− quantification method (Goldman and Jacobs, 1961) and NO2− concentration estimation was done using the diazo-coupling method described by Snell and Snell (1949) and NH4+ determination was conducted following the Nesslerization method (Jeong et al. 2013). All the N species determinations were done in the culture media before inoculation and after cellular growth (in this case by harvesting the cells by centrifugation and collecting the supernatant for nitrogenous compounds measurements).

Finally, the variations in the elemental profile of the cellular pellet of H. mediterranei were measured through the means of ICP to understand how exposure to different wastewaters affects the biomass composition. For that, cell pellets were harvested at the end of the exponential phase by centrifugation (45 min, 4 °C, 7,830 rpm). After centrifugation, the pellet was washed twice with 15% NaCl solution and washed samples were then oven-dried at 90 °C for 48 h to obtain the dry mass. For digestion, 1.5 mL of 69% HNO₃ was added, and the samples were incubated in a sand bath at ~70 °C for 6 h to ensure complete digestion. The digests were then diluted to 20 g with ultrapure H2O to achieve a final concentration of 5% w/w. Aliquots of this solution were further diluted (1:10 and 1:100) while maintaining the HNO3 concentration at 5% w/w for ICP analysis. For method validation, and in the absence of certified reference materials, recovery tests were carried out, obtaining quantitative recoveries in all cases.

Data analysis

R software (R Core Team 2020) was used as a statistical program for data analysis. To observe the possible effect of the different residues on the growth of H. mediterranei, different analyses of variance (ANOVA) were carried out for the variables maximum O.D.600 nm and growth rate (µ). ANOVAs were also carried out for each of the elements measured to determine the variations in the elemental profile of H. mediterranei concerning the exposure to the different residues, as well as for carotenoid production in each medium. Consequently, every ANOVA followed the same linear model with “Culture media” as a fixed factor with 5 levels (OM, CM, DPR+NaCl, DPR+salts, and TIR+salts). Other media were not considered, as no growth was detected (see “Results” part). Before any ANOVA, homogeneity of variance and normality of data were tested with Cochran’s C-test and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. When ANOVA showed significant differences (p-value < 0.05), a post hoc test was performed using the Tukey HSD test. To do the statistical analysis, it should be highlighted that elements with concentrations below the limit of detection were not considered in the study because, even if their detection was not possible, it does not mean they are not present in the samples.

Results

Physicochemical characterisation of wastewaters

In the present work, three different wastewaters coming from two different industrial activities (textile and desalination water plant) were characterised to evaluate their suitability to be used as part of the culture media for the growth of H. mediterranei. To this end, conductivity (and its equivalence in salinity), pH, BOD5, HCO3− and CO32−, organic and inorganic ions content, and the elemental composition were analysed. Besides, the suitability of wastewaters was evaluated by comparing the data towards an optimal culture medium.

The analysed residues displayed high heterogeneity in their physicochemical properties (Table 1). Nevertheless, all had low electrical conductivity, indicating a low salt concentration compared with the optimal salinity for H. mediterranei growth (20.0 ± 0.2 mS/cm). In particular, DPR and TIR contained only 3.3% and 0.5% of the required salts, respectively. All wastewaters exhibited higher BOD5 values but markedly lower phosphate (PO43−) concentrations than the optimal medium, below 0.5% of the amount required for optimal growth (48,000 ± 2000 mg kg⁻1). Among the three residues, DPR had a slightly more alkaline pH, but still neutral, together with an elevated boron (B) content (5.6 times higher than the OM). No other hazardous metals were detected. After pretreatment by the company, the textile wastewater (TIR) showed a neutral pH (the raw wastewater, TIRraw, was basic). Comparing both textile samples, carbon content decreased by 99.92%, whereas nitrate (NO3−) concentration increased 92-fold, reaching 1170 mg kg⁻1.

Optimisation of culture media

According to the results obtained from the characterisation of the wastewaters (Table 1), residues (DPR and TIR) were supplemented with the essential chemical components to theoretically guarantee an optimum cellular growth. Preliminary experiments were conducted with TIRraw effluent, but growth was not observed. Therefore, several attempts were conducted to determine the cause of growth inhibition; nevertheless, no successful outcomes were obtained, and the residue was discarded.

To establish this supplementation, the optimised recipe for H. mediterranei growth in minimum culture media was used as a reference. It was decided to supplement them with salts, C source, PO43−, and Fe, as these are the critical parameters that ensure the growth of the haloarchaeon. There was no need for N source supplementation as residues already contained NO3− concentrations in the range of the N requirements of this strain (Martínez-Espinosa et al. 2001). Regarding salt supplementation, previous works with H. mediterranei as a model organism revealed that NaCl concentrations below 15% are critical for cellular growth. Preliminary growth monitoring of TIR cultures demonstrated that the cells were stressed due to low NaCl concentrations and to lack of elements (Ca, K, and Mg). Consequently, it was decided to supplement the different residue media with NaCl and with the mixture of salts of the saltwater described by Rodríguez-Valera and co-workers (1980). Additionally, NaCl was added to guarantee a final concentration of 15% w/v of salts, as it is a percentage that minimises the amount of reagent used and ensures the growth of the microorganism (Matarredona et al. 2021). Another parameter that should be considered when culturing is the media pH. MOPS was the buffer used in these culture media, as results revealed that both phosphate and TRIS-HCl buffers were unsuitable as pH decreased from 7.3 to 4.3 and salt precipitates were formed, respectively. Summarising, wastewater-based culture media were supplemented with a C source (glucose), PO43−, Fe source, MOPS buffer, and either NaCl or a mixture of salts of the saltwater.

Growth curves and stress response of Haloferax mediterranei cells

Growth curves and stress response (based on cellular pigmentation) of H. mediterranei grown in different media were monitored to understand how the microorganism behaves when it is grown in the presence of different wastewaters.

H. mediterranei grown in optimal conditions showed an adaptation phase (Lag phase) of 16.5 h and a growth rate of 0.199 ± 0.006 h−1, and it reached the maximum O.D.600 nm at 4.73 ± 0.09 (Fig. 1). Compared to the optimal medium, the control media had 3.7 times longer adaptation phase (16.5 h vs. 61 h) (Fig. 1). Moreover, the growth rate decreased to 0.0681 ± 0.0003 h−1 (around 66% of reduction), and the maximum O.D.600 nm decreased to 3.43 ± 0.15 (around 27.5% of reduction). However, as the maximum O.D.600 nm reached decreases by less than 30%, it was still considered to be grown in quite optimal conditions. As regards wastewater media, H. mediterranei grew less in wastewater-based cultures supplemented only with NaCl than in media supplemented with the mixture of salts of the salt water (Fig. 1). Specifically, this happens for DPR media as media containing NaCl showed a decrease in both growth rate and O.D.600 nm of 64% and 56%, respectively, compared to the media supplemented with the mixture of saltwater. Moreover, an intensification of this behaviour was observed for the TIR media, as in the ones supplemented with NaCl H. mediterranei could not grow. Regarding culture media containing NO3− as the N source (CM, DPR, and TIR), the two DPR media had 0.66 times shorter adaptation phase than the control and TIR media. However, DPR supplemented with salts showed a reduction of 48.53% and 25.44% for both the maximum O.D.600 nm and the growth, respectively, compared to the control medium. Comparing the control and TIR supplemented with salt media, differences in the growth response are not so clear (Fig. 1 and Table 2). No statistically significant differences were observed regarding growth rate (Table 2) or for the adaptation phase (61 h in both cases). Interestingly, when cultures reached the stationary phase, cell pellets showed a significantly more intense pinkish colour compared to the optimal (Fig. 2). This was especially noticeable for TIR media supplemented with salts. When carotenoid production was quantified, pellet colour intensification was found to be directly correlated with carotenoid production, mainly the C50 BR and its isomers all accounting for up to 80% (w/w) of the total carotenoids isolated from the cells (Giani et al. 2022). TIR supplemented with salts yielded the highest carotenoid content, reaching nearly 0.025 mg/g of biomass dry weight. Regarding elemental variation, Fe and Zn exhibit a clear trend when H. mediterranei is cultivated in different industrial wastewaters. Fe and Zn concentrations were higher in DPR (Fig. 5B). Discussion

This work has evaluated the potential use of industrial brines from desalination and textile processes as culture media for the haloarchaeon Haloferax mediterranei R4. The study was designed to explore whether these saline residues, typically considered problematic wastes, could instead be integrated into a circular bioeconomy framework by addressing both the challenges of bioremediation under high-salinity conditions and the possibility of generating valuable biomolecules, thus valorising these residues.

IWWs employed in the present work showed a high heterogeneity in terms of physicochemical characteristics. However, most of the parameters did not show any differences between different residues and were within the levels set by the responsible authorities (EPA, 2009), a priori being harmless to the environment. While EPA drinking water limits are included for comparative purposes, they are not directly applicable to industrial wastewater. Discharge standards for effluents are established separately and may vary depending on national or regional regulations. Nevertheless, some differences were found when comparing the IWWs with CM. IWWs showed high BOD5 levels, but those were lower than the threshold established for urban wastewaters (25 mg O2 L−1) according to Spanish legislation (Real Decreto 509/1996, 2015). Additionally, elevated boron levels in DPR were consistent with its origin in a B-rich aquifer (Chillón, 1996). Importantly, no hazardous metals exceeded regulatory thresholds (EPA, 2009), confirming that these residues were not toxic to H. mediterranei with respect to metals.

Among the textile residues, TIRraw initially appeared attractive due to its high carbon content, but its basic pH and chemically complex profile made it less suitable for growth. Even though buffering could theoretically mitigate pH, the overall complex matrix inhibited the growth of H. mediterranei in preliminary tests, suggesting the presence of an unidentified compound that negatively impacts cellular physiology and metabolism. In contrast, TIR provided a simpler matrix enriched in NO3− containing values well above drinking water thresholds set by the EPA (2009). While such concentrations would be problematic in terms of environmental discharge, they represent a readily available nitrogen source for microbial growth. This makes TIR far more promising than TIRraw as a basis for culture medium optimisation. Consequently, TIRraw was discarded and TIR was used for subsequent experiments. Overall, considering the characteristics of the different wastewaters, it was concluded that none of the residues per se was suitable for the growth of H. mediterranei and consequently, minimal supplementations were applied to optimise cellular growth.

Upon optimisation of the culture media, H. mediterranei was grown in the different media. Growth experiments revealed that deviations from optimal conditions, such as lower salinity, different nitrogen sources, or the use of wastewaters as culture media, resulted in extended Lag phases, reduced growth rates, and lower maximum O.D.600 nm. As previously described in the literature, cultures containing exclusively NO3− exhibited longer Lag phases than those grown with NH4−, reflecting the higher metabolic cost of NO3− assimilation (Esclapez et al. 2014a). However, the two DPR media displayed a shorter adaptation phase compared to the control (also containing NO3−). The origin of this behaviour is unclear. A possible explanation could rely on the presence of some unidentified compounds that may help the microorganisms to grow. The reduction in the maximum O.D.600 nm in some IWW media may be related to nutrient limitation, as already reported in other studies (Elser et al. 1995; Demoling et al. 2007; Prest et al. 2016). Specifically, DPR contained approximately 20% of NO3− required for the optimal growth of H. mediterranei. Consequently, cultures enter the stationary phase sooner as microorganisms start to grow until resources are depleted. Supplementation with artificial saltwater mixtures enhanced growth compared to media supplemented with NaCl, suggesting that additional ions, such as K+ and Mg+2, are essential for optimal cellular function. However, when nutrients are highly available, the growth of the microorganisms is usually enhanced (Elser et al. 1995; Demoling et al. 2007; Prest et al. 2016). Contrary to DPR, TIR contained a higher NO3− concentration than the control medium (1170 mg kg⁻1 vs. 930 mg kg⁻1), explaining the increased O.D.600 nm observed due to greater nutrient availability.

Moreover, when cultures reached the stationary phase and a cellular pellet was obtained, stress responses were evident through pigmentation changes. In several cultures, particularly TIR with salts, intense colouration corresponded to elevated carotenoid accumulation (BR and its isomers). This is consistent with carotenoid production under stressful conditions previously reported (Giani et al. 2021). This suggests that H. mediterranei responds to suboptimal growth conditions, such as lower salinity and a shift in N source, by enhancing carotenoid synthesis as a stress response mechanism. When the wastewater-based media are compared to the control media, the impact of the stress response becomes more visible, especially for TIR supplemented with salts, achieving the highest carotenoid content and, thus, indicating that this condition led to the highest stress level. This increase suggests the presence of additional, yet unidentified, stressors in the TIR medium that may be acting synergistically to trigger stronger carotenogenesis in H. mediterranei. Although carotenoid production did not reach levels observed in their optimised production media (10 mg L−1 compared to 40 mg L⁻1) (Giani et al. 2021), it remained substantially higher than in OM and CM, demonstrating the potential for valorisation of these residues through stress-induced carotenogenesis. Moreover, the value achieved is only four times lower than the one obtained in the optimised carotenoid production media. Along with differences in carotenoid production in the different IWWs, qualitative changes in the extracts composition have also been observed. In the IWWs-derived extracts, BR remained the major carotenoid, generally comparable to the level observed in CM. However, the relative abundances of MABR and BABR varied depending on the type of residue. Analysis of BR isomer composition in the present study showed similar patterns previously observed (Giani et al. 2022), as only all-trans-BR and 9-cis-BR isomers were present. In the present study, all-trans-BR accounted for 90% and 9-cis-BR accounted for 10% of the total BR content of the extract for optimal growth media. This suggests that, under optimal growth conditions (no induction of carotenogenesis), BR production seems to be largely dominated by all-trans-BR. In contrast, CM displayed a more diverse isomeric profile, with reduced all-trans-BR and increased contributions of the cis isomers, indicating that even under control conditions without added residues, minor shifts in isomer distribution can occur. These changes in the composition of BR isomers are also observed when growing the microorganism in the IWWs media. It has already been reported that changes in nutrient sources drive shifts in isomeric composition of BR (Giani et al. 2022), suggesting a possible modulation of the carotenoid extracts by the nature of media or residues used. These results underscore the importance of nitrogen source and nutrient availability in H. mediterranei physiology, with carotenoids acting as protective molecules stabilising the cellular structure under stress conditions (Giani et al. 2021). Different carotenoid profiles found in the different extracts for varied IWWs suggest that each extract could have a different industrial application. However, it should be highlighted that residues vary from one batch to another, so carotenoid profile might also change. Moreover, although characterisation of carotenoid profile is essential for some applications (i.e. biomedicine, food), it is not as critical for others (paintings), which makes their industrial applicability even broader, considering that their composition might change.

Beyond growth and stress responses, the bioremediation potential of H. mediterranei was evaluated in terms of N compounds removal. NO3− removal efficiencies reached levels substantially higher than the 60% previously reported (Nájera-Fernández et al. 2012). This highlights the high potential of this microorganism to be used for NO3− removal of industrial wastewaters. When compared with salt-tolerant bacteria, such as Halomonas sp. W07, they can efficiently remove NO3− (100% of removal) up to 8% salts but lose efficiency entirely at high salinities (16% salts) (Ke et al. 2025). Taking this into consideration, H. mediterranei displays a clear ecological advantage. Its optimal growth in media with up to 25% salt and its ability to maintain high NO3− removal efficiency even at 15% salinity highlight haloarchaea as better candidates than bacteria for the remediation of hypersaline effluents. In DPR supplemented with NaCl, where NH4+ was abundant, growth was unexpectedly limited. However, this medium showed to accumulate NO2−, which has been shown to be toxic for H. mediterranei at high concentrations (higher than those reported as toxic for most of the microbial strains tested) (Nájera-Fernández et al. 2012). Although detailed mechanistic insights were beyond the scope of the present work, further investigation of NO2− stress responses and the interplay between NO3− and NH4+ assimilation will be essential to fully exploit the bioremediation potential of this haloarchaeon.

The growth efficiency, high NO3− removal rates, and survival in IWWs can be attributed to metabolic modifications occurring within the cell. It has been previously reported that transition metals such as Fe and Zn are essential for the physiology of all organisms, acting as cofactors in metalloproteins (Kaur et al. 2006), constituting more than 50% of total proteins (Degtyarenko 2000). In H. mediterranei, the observed accumulation of these metals, along with Ca and Mn, reflects targeted metabolic adaptations rather than incidental uptake. Fe may support the formation of Fe–S clusters, structures known to stabilise proteins under oxidative or ionic stress in both E. coli and haloarchaea such as H. volcanii (Waldron and Robinson 2009; Zafrilla et al. 2010). Similarly, Zn likely contributes to the activity of redox enzymes, including glucose dehydrogenase, helping cells manage oxidative challenges (Esclapez et al. 2014b). Ca has been studied in some haloarchaea species to act as a metal binder in the exo-mucopolysaccharide (EPS) layer (Srivastava and Kowshik 2013), a characteristic external structure. This layer is also found in H. mediterranei, where EPS production increases under stress conditions, such as elevated carbon content (Antón et al. 1988). This suggests that H. mediterranei increases EPS production as a stress response, leading to higher Ca concentrations due to its role in EPS composition. Similarly, the increase in Mn may help the cells cope with oxidative stress, both by directly reducing harmful reactive oxygen molecules and by enhancing the activity of protective enzymes like superoxide dismutase (Culotta and Daly 2013). The increase of Mn has also been reported in previous studies as a heavy metal stress response (Sáez-Zamacona et al., 2023). Together, these patterns support the view that metal accumulation is not incidental but reflects an adaptive response of H. mediterranei to the chemical complexity of wastewater environments.

All these findings together indicate that H. mediterranei responds to the specific chemical and ionic composition of industrial residues by adjusting growth dynamics, NO3− assimilation/respiration, and stress-related pathways, including carotenoid production and selective metal accumulation (Ca, Fe, Mn, Zn). Such physiological adaptations reflect the capacity of this microorganism to cope with suboptimal nitrogen sources, high salinity, and nutrient limitations.

Conclusions

The current work has assessed the viability of employing industrial wastewaters (desalination plant and textile industry residues) as culture media for Haloferax mediterranei to remediate the wastes and to evaluate the valorisation of these residues to produce highly marketed biomolecules like natural pigments. The results reveal that, although wastewaters have a heterogeneous composition and per se are not suitable for the growth of H. mediterranei, their supplementation with salts, carbon source, and other essential nutrients significantly improve their suitability as a culture medium. However, the growth response was negatively affected, resulting in decreased growth rates and maximum O.D.600 nm, except for TIR residues due to high NO3− content. As the stress response mechanism is not unique and is mediated by a complex mechanism, different stress responses have been detected in H. mediterranei when it is grown in different residual media, including an increase in carotenoid production and stress tolerance metal accumulation. The stress caused by growth conditions in residue-based cultures was confirmed with carotenoid content quantification, which was higher than in both optimal and control media. This enhanced carotenoid production not only indicates a physiological response to environmental stress but also underscores the biotechnological value of cultivating H. mediterranei under such conditions. Carotenoids are well-known for their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and photoprotective properties, making them highly desirable in pharmaceutical, nutraceutical, and cosmetic applications. Thus, the ability of H. mediterranei to synthesise elevated levels of these valuable pigments in non-ideal, residue-based media reveals as a sustainable approach to producing high-value compounds while contributing to environmental remediation. Although BR and its isomers have been identified as predominant in carotenoids extracts obtained from cells grown under the conditions here established, more efforts should be made shortly to carry out an accurate differentiation of those carotenoids that constitute up to 20% of the carotenoid extract. This differentiation could help to find new potential applications of carotenoids from haloarchaea in biotechnology. Additionally, when assessing variations in the elemental profile, it was found that H. mediterranei accumulates Ca, Fe, and Zn intracellularly. Ca may be accumulated because of a possible increment of EPS production, of which Ca is an essential element acting as a metal binder, whereas Fe and Zn are accumulated due to their essential role as cofactors of metalloproteins related to stress tolerance. Moreover, Mn is also accumulated in DPR+salts residue, as a key element of stress tolerance. Even though most results reveal the stress conditions the microorganism is facing while being grown in the wastewater media, H. mediterranei was able to remove between 60 and 90% of NO3− present in the wastewaters, thus confirming its potential role in bioremediation processes. These findings indicate that, although H. mediterranei does not reach optimal growth in IWWs media, it can still survive, remove contaminants, and produce carotenoids of potential interest for pharmaceutical and medical applications. This work should be regarded as a proof-of-concept, demonstrating the potential to couple wastewater reuse with biomolecule production, though further optimisation is required before industrial application to guarantee culture stability and competitive valorisation of residues decreasing the need for culture supplementation and increasing the production of carotenoids. The results presented in this study are promising, although there are still critical issues that need to be addressed, such as finding the right mix of wastes to obtain enough nutrients without the need for further artificial supplementation. Whereas residues containing C and N seem to be easy to find (candy industry and textile/irrigation water, respectively), Fe, P, and high salt–containing residues might be a bit harder to get. Moreover, future studies should also address scalability challenges, including reactor design, oxygenation strategies, and integration with existing treatment systems, to translate laboratory findings into feasible industrial processes.
