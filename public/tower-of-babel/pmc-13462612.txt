Spatial lipid-barrier coupling in knee osteoarthritis: a mechanism-based perspective centered on the infrapatellar fat pad
Authors: Yuwu Liu, Ming Chen, Ruhua Yong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKnee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a heterogeneous whole-joint disorder in which mechanical loading, aging, metabolic stress, synovitis, microvascular dysfunction, matrix injury, subchondral remodeling, pain-related neurovascular change, and impaired repair interact over time. The infrapatellar fat pad (IFP) is increasingly recognized as an active synovio-adipose tissue, yet IFP inflammation, lipid disturbance, hypoxia, angiogenesis, matrix catabolism, and subchondral change are often considered parallel abnormalities rather than as a spatially organized propagation process. This Perspective proposes an IFP-centered spatial lipid-barrier coupling framework for a biologically enriched KOA subset. The central claim is not that lipid quantity alone drives KOA, but that lipid-derived signals become mechanistically meaningful when they appear in vulnerable barrier-defined spaces and co-localize with permeability injury, hypoxia, inflammatory execution, oxidative-matrix injury, or impaired repair. In this model, lipid-spatial mislocalization is a candidate threshold event, lipid-cytokine-hypoxia-barrier coupling is the progression engine, and barrier-defined propagation links the IFP, synovium, synovial cavity, cartilage matrix, osteochondral interface, and subchondral marrow-adipose compartment. Current evidence supports active IFP biology, IFP-synovium communication, circulation-to-joint-fluid access, multi-compartment lipid disturbance, HIF/VEGF and FFA-NOX/ROS pathways, and subchondral marrow involvement. However, source-resolved spatial co-localization and temporal ordering remain unproven. The framework should therefore be retained only if a reproducible lipid-barrier signature predicts progression, pain biology, or treatment response beyond synovitis, body mass index, malalignment, bone marrow lesions, and radiographic severity.Keywords: ApoB, barrier-defined propagation, hypoxia, infrapatellar fat pad, knee osteoarthritis, microvascular permeability, spatial lipid-barrier coupling

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is no longer regarded as isolated cartilage wear but as a multifactorial whole-joint disorder involving cartilage, synovium, subchondral bone, menisci, ligaments, periarticular muscle, local microcirculation, pain-related neurovascular remodeling, systemic metabolism, aging, inflammatory signaling, and impaired repair.1–3 This heterogeneity creates a persistent interpretive problem: multiple upstream risks can produce overlapping late structural phenotypes, whereas the local events that convert risk exposure into self-sustaining joint degeneration remain incompletely defined.

The infrapatellar fat pad (IFP) is anatomically and mechanically positioned to influence this whole-joint environment. It lies adjacent to the patellar tendon, patellofemoral compartment, synovial lining, anterior joint cavity, and cartilage-facing interfaces; contains adipocytes, microvessels, immune cells, stromal cells, fibrovascular tissue, extracellular matrix (ECM), and sensory innervation; and deforms dynamically during knee motion.4–7 Imaging and microcirculatory studies further suggest that IFP morphology, signal intensity, and local perfusion can change in KOA-relevant settings.8–10

A growing body of human, ex vivo, histological, biochemical, imaging, and single-cell work has moved the IFP from a passive cushion concept to an active synovio-adipose tissue concept. These studies indicate that the IFP can produce inflammatory mediators, adipokines, lipid-derived signals, growth factors, matrix-remodeling outputs, and pain-relevant signals that interact with synovium, cartilage, and other articular tissues.11–16 These observations justify placing the IFP within KOA pathobiology, but they do not by themselves explain why disease persists, why repair becomes inefficient, or why single-axis interventions often fail to modify established disease.

Three unresolved questions motivate the present framework. First, focal mechanical stress, aging, obesity, dyslipidemia, metabolic dysfunction, inflammatory joint states, and microvascular injury may converge on similar local abnormalities, but the shared local threshold event remains unclear. Second, KOA can continue after the initiating trigger weakens, implying that local tissue architecture may acquire a self-maintaining memory. Third, an adipose-vascular lesion centered on the IFP-synovial interface must be anatomically connected to synovial fluid, cartilage, subchondral marrow, and pain pathways if it is to influence whole-joint progression.

This Perspective proposes an IFP-centered spatial lipid-barrier coupling framework for a biologically enriched KOA subset. Spatial lipid-barrier coupling is used here to describe a threshold-sensitive, self-reinforcing, and partially hysteretic interaction among lipid-spatial mislocalization, inflammatory execution, hypoxic-vascular remodeling, oxidative-matrix injury, barrier failure, and impaired repair. The framework is not a clinical diagnosis and does not require the IFP to be the universal origin of KOA. Its purpose is to test whether some knees enter a self-reinforcing state in which lipid-spatial mislocalization and barrier injury regenerate one another over time.

Methods: Evidence acquisition and interpretive synthesis

This article is a mechanism-based Perspective/Hypothesis rather than a systematic review. We used a targeted narrative evidence selection approach to identify representative studies that support, challenge, or operationalize the proposed spatial lipid-barrier coupling framework. The goal was to synthesize mechanistic plausibility, evidence boundaries, and testable criteria rather than to estimate pooled effects.

PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus were searched up to May 2025 using combinations of the following terms: KOA, IFP, synovium, synovial fluid, lipid metabolism, triglyceride, apolipoprotein, free fatty acid, lipidomics, hypoxia, VEGF, oxidative stress, NOX, inflammation, cartilage matrix, subchondral bone, bone marrow lesion, and osteochondral interface.

Inclusion criteria were peer-reviewed original studies or high-quality reviews addressing OA whole-joint pathobiology, IFP biology, synovial-fluid proteomics or lipidomics, lipid metabolism, hypoxia/VEGF signaling, oxidative injury, cartilage matrix catabolism, barrier dysfunction, or subchondral marrow remodeling. Human, animal, ex vivo, in vitro, imaging, and omics studies were considered when they had direct mechanistic relevance to the proposed framework. Case reports, conference abstracts, non-peer-reviewed materials, non-English full-text articles, and studies unrelated to joint pathology or lipid-inflammatory mechanisms were excluded.

Evidence was weighted conceptually by proximity to the core spatial lipid-barrier claim. Direct human spatial or longitudinal evidence was considered strongest; paired tissue-fluid, imaging, lipidomic, or proteomic studies were considered supportive; and generic lipid, inflammation, hypoxia, oxidative-stress, or endothelial-barrier studies were used only for mechanistic plausibility. Because this is a Perspective rather than a systematic review, no formal risk-of-bias scoring or meta-analysis was performed.

Core thesis: Spatial lipid-barrier coupling is defined by lipid location, not by lipid abundance

The defining feature of an IFP-centered spatial lipid-barrier coupling phenotype is not the presence of lipid-rich tissue. The normal IFP is adipose tissue and contains lipid by definition. The central issue is whether TG-rich or ApoB-linked lipid cargo, lipid droplets, FFAs, oxidized lipid products, or lipid-processing reporters appear in spaces where they are normally restricted, compartmentalized, or efficiently cleared. These spaces include perivascular regions, IFP and synovial interstitium, synovio-adipose interfaces, cartilage-facing borders, the bounded synovial cavity, osteochondral interfaces, and subchondral marrow-adipose regions. Lipid-derived signals are therefore informative only when their location, neighboring barrier injury, and downstream tissue response are considered together.

This spatial emphasis separates the framework from broader lipid or inflammation concepts. Conventional lipotoxicity can explain cellular injury from FFAs or oxidized lipids without requiring a defined anatomical leakage or retention site. Synovitis-centered models can explain cytokines, VEGF, effusion, pain, and matrix catabolism without requiring lipid-barrier co-localization. Mechanical overload and metabolic OA models remain essential, but they do not by themselves establish a local lipid-barrier mechanism.

Accordingly, the framework should satisfy a restrictive discriminating rule: lipid-derived signals must spatially co-localize with barrier injury and must predict progression, pain biology, or treatment response beyond synovitis, BMI, malalignment, BMLs, and radiographic severity. Cytokine elevation, VEGF activation, ROS generation, cartilage catabolism, obesity, serum dyslipidemia, or IFP size change can support the model only when embedded in a reproducible lipid-barrier pattern. Without this rule, the framework would become a broad relabeling of familiar KOA mechanisms rather than a testable spatial-barrier hypothesis.

Why the IFP is a plausible amplification hub

The IFP should not be considered intrinsically pathogenic. Under physiological conditions, it likely contributes to accommodation of motion, local lubrication, force distribution, and protection of adjacent structures. The relevant issue is pathological transformation under mechanical, vascular, metabolic, or inflammatory stress.

Mechanical vulnerability is a central reason for focusing on the IFP. Its location between the patellar tendon, patellofemoral compartment, synovium, and anterior joint cavity exposes it to deformation, impingement, shear, and pressure fluctuation during motion.6,7 Altered patellofemoral tracking, extensor-mechanism dysfunction, quadriceps weakness, knee instability, synovial thickening, effusion, osteophytes, or reduced anterior joint space may convert a physiological cushion into a repeatedly compressed compartment.

Mechanical stress can be translated into vascular and metabolic vulnerability. Recurrent compression or impingement may produce focal perfusion instability, intermittent hypoxia-reperfusion, endothelial stress, HIF/VEGF activation, and permeability change. Microcirculatory and MRI-based observations support the idea that the IFP is a dynamic tissue whose perfusion and signal features can change in KOA-relevant states.8–10 Aging, obesity, dyslipidemia, and adipose dysfunction can further reduce endothelial, glycocalyx, mitochondrial, stromal, and repair reserve.17–19 In this interpretation, mechanical abnormality does not compete with the spatial lipid-barrier model; it may be one upstream route by which the IFP becomes permissive to lipid spatial mislocalization.

The adipose-vascular-immune composition of the IFP makes this vulnerability biologically consequential. Adipocytes provide lipid substrate; microvessels provide potential leakage routes; macrophages and stromal cells provide cytokine and matrix-remodeling capacity; endothelial and perivascular structures regulate permeability; sensory and neurovascular elements contribute to pain and remodeling; and proximity to synovium and the bounded synovial cavity allows local signals to enter a broader whole-joint axis. The IFP is, therefore, best viewed as an anatomically privileged amplification hub. Synovial vascular leakage, joint-cavity lipid processing, cartilage-barrier injury, and subchondral marrow-adipose activation may also initiate or amplify spatial lipid-barrier coupling in selected contexts. Mechanism I: Lipid-spatial mislocalization as the candidate threshold event

From lipid excess to lipid misplacement

The first conceptual shift is from lipid excess to lipid misplacement. Lipid-related abnormalities have been reported across several KOA-relevant compartments. IFP explants show altered fatty-acid and oxylipin release in OA-relevant settings, paired IFP and synovial-fluid studies show distinct fatty-acid signatures, and serum-synovial comparisons indicate relationships between systemic and synovial-fluid lipoprotein components.20–22 Synovial fluid and synovial membrane lipidomic studies identify lipid alterations, and OA cartilage has abnormal lipid distributions and tissue lipid changes.23–28 These findings support multi-compartment lipid disturbance, but they do not by themselves identify the initiating tissue source.

The proposed framework becomes specific only when lipid signals are assigned to space and interpreted according to the likely source. TG-rich or ApoB-linked cargo is most informative as a possible marker of vascular escape, tissue-fluid entry, circulating lipoprotein access, or failed compartmental restriction. FFAs are more likely to reflect local lipolysis, adipocyte stress, damaged-cell release, inflammatory lipid processing, or incomplete clearance. Oxidized lipid products and lipid-peroxidation adducts are downstream injury reporters and effectors. Lipid droplets may reflect cellular storage, stress adaptation, macrophage uptake, or failed lipid handling. This hierarchy prevents the model from becoming a nonspecific statement that lipid metabolism is abnormal in KOA.

Spatial priority and source interpretation

Spatial priority should be specified before data are analyzed. Perivascular enrichment within the IFP or synovium of TG/ApoB-linked signals would provide the strongest support for a vascular escape interpretation, especially if paired with albumin/fibrinogen extravasation, endothelial-junction disruption, glycocalyx injury, or macrophage lipid uptake. Synovial-fluid lipid and apolipoprotein signals provide strong evidence of circulation-accessible joint-fluid convergence, but they are source-ambiguous because they may arise from circulating lipoprotein entry, IFP release, synovial lipid remodeling, cartilage matrix injury, subchondral marrow coupling, or combinations of these routes. Cartilage-facing border enrichment would support matrix-barrier transfer, whereas osteochondral or BML-adjacent enrichment would support distal lipid-barrier restart within marrow-adipose or osteochondral compartments.

The synovial cavity deserves special weight because it is not an ordinary interstitium; it is a bounded, plasma-accessible joint-fluid space. Comparative serum-synovial studies show relationships between serum and synovial-fluid lipoprotein components, supporting lipoprotein-related entry into joint fluid. 22 OA synovial-fluid studies also detect plasma proteins, apolipoprotein-related proteins, complement-related proteins, and coagulation-related proteins, consistent with circulation-to-cavity access through a permeability-regulated synovial interface.29–35 This is currently stronger evidence for circulation-to-joint-fluid access than for proven human IFP perivascular TG/ApoB leakage. Therefore, synovial fluid should be treated as a powerful convergence readout but a weak source-resolving marker unless paired with tissue-spatial data.

Mechanism II: The lipid-cytokine-hypoxia-barrier injury loop

Local lipid processing converts misplacement into cellular injury

The progression engine of spatial lipid-barrier coupling is mutual regeneration rather than simple coexistence. Lipid misplacement supplies abnormal lipid substrate, but pathological amplification depends on how that substrate is processed within vulnerable tissue spaces. Local lipolysis, lipid-droplet turnover, oxidative modification, and incomplete clearance can generate FFAs, oxylipins, oxidized lipid products, lipid-peroxidation adducts, and redox-active intermediates. These signals may act on macrophages, adipocytes, endothelial cells, synovial lining cells, fibroblast-like stromal cells, chondrocytes, and perivascular cells.

Unlike circulating FFAs, which are diluted, albumin-buffered, and systemically cleared, local processing of spatially misplaced lipid may create a compartmentalized FFA burden within vulnerable tissue spaces. In the adipose-rich IFP, hypoxic, mechanical, or inflammatory stress may further amplify this burden through adipocyte lipolysis, lipid-droplet remodeling, CD36/FABP-mediated uptake, macrophage lipid loading, or stress-related lipid release, making downstream FFA-sensitive inflammatory and oxidative activation more likely.

Mechanistic evidence supports this execution layer. FFAs can behave as proinflammatory mediators, modulate TLR4 activation, induce inflammation, impair vascular reactivity, and amplify catabolic responses in chondrocytes under palmitate-rich or hyperlipidemic conditions.36–40 In this framework, these findings do not imply that FFAs alone cause KOA. Rather, they support the more specific prediction that locally concentrated and poorly cleared FFAs, when spatially coupled to barrier vulnerability, hypoxia, and inflammatory cells, can convert lipid misplacement into cellular injury.

Cytokine, oxidative, and hypoxic execution axes

Cytokines, HIF/VEGF signaling, and FFA-NOX/ROS activity should be interpreted as execution axes rather than standalone definitions of the proposed lipid-barrier coupling phenotype. They become mechanistically relevant only when spatially linked to lipid mislocalization, barrier vulnerability, and tissue injury.

Cytokines form the inflammatory execution layer. IL-6, TNF-related signals, CCL2, VEGF, macrophage-rich inflammation, fibroinflammatory programs, MMPs, and ADAMTS can convert lipid processing into immune recruitment, endothelial stress, ECM remodeling, pain-related inflammatory signaling, and barrier vulnerability. IFP cytokine production, IFP-derived IL-6 and soluble IL-6 receptor, histologic and molecular IFP abnormalities, COMP-associated remodeling, and IFP-synovium communication support the plausibility of the IFP as a cytokine-active compartment.11–16

The FFA-NOX/ROS axis provides the oxidative-matrix route. Locally concentrated FFAs and oxidized lipid products may activate NOX2/NOX4-related ROS production and inflammatory TLR4/NF-κB, JNK, p38, or ERK signaling.36–41 ROS and inflammatory mediators can injure endothelial cells, stromal cells, synovial cells, chondrocytes, and ECM structures.42–45 Through lipid peroxidation, 4-HNE/MDA adduct formation, MMP13, ADAMTS4/5, COMP-associated remodeling, and collagen-aggrecan fragmentation, this axis may loosen local tissue architecture and create matrix-degradation-enabled propagation spaces.

Hypoxia is both an upstream permissive state and a regenerated product of the loop. In a compressed or microvascularly unstable IFP, low-flow conditions may occur early. Lipid processing, cytokine-driven inflammation, ROS-mediated endothelial dysfunction, interstitial edema, mitochondrial stress, and ECM damage can further disturb oxygen delivery and consumption. HIF activation and VEGF release may then increase vascular permeability, angiogenic remodeling, and possibly neurovascular ingrowth. Joint-specific studies link VEGF or HIF-related pathways to KOA severity, cartilage destruction, and OA-associated pain mechanisms.46–51

Barrier instability and structural lock-in

Barrier instability is the structural output of the loop. Relevant barriers include microvascular endothelium and glycocalyx, synovial vascular interfaces, IFP interstitial ECM, cartilage matrix, and the osteochondral interface. Oxidant stress can impair endothelial function, and lipid peroxidation with antioxidant imbalance has been reported in synovial fluid from patients with knee OA.42,43 Hyperlipidemic environments can injure chondrocytes through oxidative stress, and IFP-derived or synovial-fluid inflammatory signals can be linked to matrix catabolism, macrophage/neutrophil-related inflammation, and cartilage degradation.40,44,45 These findings support the plausibility of a lipid-barrier loop, but they remain indirect unless barrier injury and lipid-derived signals are mapped together in the same tissue regions.

Once barriers become unstable, disease may become less dependent on the original trigger and more dependent on damaged local architecture. Permeable microvessels, loosened ECM, lipid-retaining interstitial spaces, macrophage-rich inflammatory niches, hypoxic-vascular remodeling, and impaired repair may recreate the conditions for renewed lipid entry, local FFA accumulation, cytokine production, oxidative injury, and matrix damage. This transition can be conceptualized as structural lock-in: early leakage, cytokine activation, and hypoxia may be reversible, whereas repeated cycles may establish a self-maintaining state in which barrier failure and lipid processing regenerate one another over time.

Candidate molecular sequence linking lipid mislocalization to matrix-barrier failure

A more granular molecular sequence is needed to make the framework testable. One candidate sequence can be organized into four linked layers. First, a lipid-handling layer emerges when local TG/ApoB-linked cargo entry or lipid-droplet remodeling creates a spatial lipid substrate, while LPL, ATGL, HSL, CD36, FABP4, and related programs increase local FFA or lipid-intermediate availability.52,53 Second, an inflammatory-sensing layer is activated when saturated FFAs, oxylipins, oxidized phospholipids, and 4-HNE/MDA adducts engage TLR4, CD36, NLRP3, NF-κB, JNK, p38, ERK, and NOX2/NOX4 pathways. Third, an oxidative-hypoxic amplification layer upregulates IL-6, CCL2, TNF-related signals, VEGF, ANGPT2, NOX/ROS activity, and pain-relevant inflammatory mediators. Fourth, a matrix-barrier failure layer develops through VE-cadherin disruption, glycocalyx shedding, albumin/fibrinogen leak, chondrocyte stress, MMP13, ADAMTS4/5, COMP remodeling, and collagen-aggrecan fragmentation. The weakened barrier then permits renewed lipid access or local lipid processing, closing the loop.

This sequence is intentionally provisional. It is not necessary that every marker appear in every knee. What matters is whether a reproducible module emerges in which lipid location, barrier injury, cellular execution, and matrix damage are spatially and temporally coupled. A knee with IL-6 elevation alone, VEGF elevation alone, lipid droplets alone, or matrix fragments alone should not be labeled as lipid-barrier coupling-enriched unless these signals align within the predicted lipid-barrier architecture.

Mechanism III: Barrier-defined compartmental propagation

A local IFP-centered event becomes relevant to the whole-joint KOA only if it gains anatomical reach. This propagation should not be understood primarily as bulk TG or FFA transport from the IFP to distant joint structures. IFP-derived lipid material may enter adjacent synovial or joint-fluid spaces, but distal cartilage or subchondral marrow involvement is more plausibly driven by signal-first propagation. In this model, biological signals travel across barrier-defined compartments, create new barrier-permissive sites, and allow local lipid entry, FFA generation, and lipid-barrier injury to restart within those sites.

The anatomical propagation axis includes the IFP, synovium, bounded synovial cavity, cartilage matrix, osteochondral interface, and subchondral marrow-adipose compartment. These spaces are connected but not equivalent. IFP and synovial interstitial territories act as local amplification niches; the synovial cavity functions as a joint-level convergence space; cartilage matrix acts as an avascular ECM-rich barrier-transfer structure; and the osteochondral interface links synovial fluid, cartilage-derived, and marrow-derived signals.54,55

The anatomical axis should be distinguished from the functional barrier-opening axes. Propagated hypoxic, angiogenic, inflammatory, oxidative, and matrix-derived signals may activate HIF/VEGF-related permeability and remodeling programs, together with oxidative-matrix injury programs, at newly vulnerable sites. Thus, TG/ApoB leakage is best viewed as a local consequence of signal-induced barrier permissiveness rather than as the primary carrier of whole-joint propagation.

Existing evidence supports this signal-convergence model. OA synovial fluid contains plasma-derived, apolipoprotein-related, complement/coagulation-related, and inflammatory proteins, while IFP-synovium studies support active macrophage-, fibroblast-, and APOE-related communication.11–16,29–33,45 Cartilage matrix injury and osteochondral-interface remodeling further support biological coupling among synovial fluid, cartilage-derived, and marrow-derived signals.54,55 These signals may prime distal compartments and create barrier-permissive states before overt TG/ApoB leakage or FFA accumulation becomes detectable.

Cartilage matrix and the osteochondral interface are not passive recipients. Once catabolic matrix remodeling, collagen-aggrecan damage, and matrix-fragment release weaken cartilage-barrier function, synovial-fluid signals and osteochondral or marrow-derived signals may become more strongly coupled. Cartilage degeneration can therefore be interpreted not only as local matrix wear, but also as a matrix-barrier manifestation of propagated hypoxic, oxidative, inflammatory, and lipid-processing stress.

Subchondral marrow is best described as circulation-accessible but source-ambiguous. Propagated signals may first induce local marrow-adipose, vascular, and remodeling responses; once activated, subchondral marrow may develop its own lipid-permissive state through local vascular permeability, marrow adipocyte stress, inflammatory lipolysis, ischemia-reperfusion injury, or osteochondral transport. Cancellous bone lipid changes, serum cholesterol/TG associations with later BML development, subchondral bone-derived IL-6, synovitis-BML coupling, BML histology, and dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI support the biological relevance of this compartment.54–61 Tracer and metabolic studies further support the plausibility that circulating TG-rich lipoprotein-derived particles or fatty-acid substrates can reach marrow-related compartments.62–64

The model would be strengthened if signal gradients, matrix-barrier injury, local TG/ApoB leakage, and lipid-barrier signatures appear sequentially from IFP/synovial regions toward cartilage, osteochondral, and marrow-adipose compartments. It would be weakened if distal cartilage or marrow changes occur independently of lipid-barrier, inflammatory, oxidative, angiogenic, or hypoxic propagation patterns.

Evidence hierarchy and current boundaries

The evidence should be interpreted hierarchically. The strongest current support is not for proven TG/ApoB leakage within human IFP tissue, but for plausible component-level support and several strong links that make the hypothesis testable. The decisive gap is source-resolved spatial co-localization and temporal ordering. The model requires demonstration that TG/ApoB-linked cargo or lipid-derived reporters are enriched in prespecified vulnerable regions, co-localize with endothelial leak, albumin/fibrinogen extravasation, macrophage lipid uptake, hypoxia, oxidative injury, matrix injury, or osteochondral barrier disruption, and precede later progression, pain trajectory, or treatment response.

First, human and experimental studies support the premise that the IFP is biologically active. The IFP can produce cytokines, adipokines, inflammatory mediators, matrix-remodeling outputs, and pain-relevant signals, and single-cell evidence supports coordinated IFP-synovium biology, including APOE-related signaling that may connect lipid handling, macrophage/stromal states, and OA pathology.11–16 These findings support the IFP as an active execution and amplification compartment rather than an inert fat depot.

Second, circulation-to-joint-fluid access is supported at the synovial-fluid level. Serum-synovial relationships involving lipoprotein components and the detection of plasma proteins in OA synovial-fluid support the concept that plasma-derived proteins and lipoprotein-related components can enter the bounded synovial cavity through synovial permeability routes.22,29–35 This is the strongest current evidence for a circulation-accessible lipid/protein reaction space in KOA.

Third, multi-compartment lipid disturbance is supported across IFP, synovial fluid, synovial membrane, cartilage, and cancellous bone.20 xref ref-type="bibr" rid="bibr21-1759720X261462643"/>–28,56 Barrier vulnerability is also supported across joint compartments: plasma proteins in OA synovial fluid, inflammatory and vascular features in IFP and synovium, oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in synovial fluid, hyperlipidemic chondrocyte injury, osteochondral transport, and subchondral marrow perfusion or histologic remodeling.29–33,40,42–45,54–61 Historical bone-lipid tracer and metabolic studies additionally support skeletal and marrow access to circulating TG-rich lipoprotein-derived particles and fatty-acid substrates.62–64 These data are compatible with spatial lipid-barrier coupling, but they do not prove source, directionality, or temporal precedence. Operational definition of a lipid-barrier coupling-enriched KOA subset

For research purposes, a lipid-barrier coupling-enriched subset should be defined by convergent evidence rather than by IFP size, obesity, pain severity, radiographic grade, or a single cytokine level. The operational definition should combine local lipid location, co-localized barrier or matrix-barrier injury, cellular execution, barrier-defined anatomical reach, and longitudinal or intervention-based discriminating value.

Five operational domains are most useful. First, there should be a local lipid signal in prespecified spatial regions, such as perivascular IFP or synovium, synovio-adipose interface, synovial cavity, cartilage-facing border, osteochondral interface, or BML-adjacent marrow-adipose regions. Second, the lipid signal should co-localize with barrier or matrix-barrier injury, including albumin/fibrinogen leak, endothelial-junction disruption, glycocalyx injury, Ang-2/Tie2 imbalance, cartilage permeability, matrix-fragment release, or osteochondral-interface abnormality. Third, there should be cellular execution activity, such as macrophage lipid uptake, IL-6, CCL2, TNF-related signals, VEGF, NOX/ROS, 4-HNE/MDA, MMPs, ADAMTS, COMP, or fibro-inflammatory programs. Fourth, the pattern should show barrier-defined anatomical reach rather than assumed bulk lipid spread. Fifth, the combined signature should provide added value through temporal precedence, progression prediction, pain-trajectory association, or treatment-response discrimination beyond conventional KOA variables.

Supportive but non-defining features include IFP focal signal alteration, impingement-prone anatomy, altered fat-pad microcirculation, local perfusion instability, obesity, dyslipidemia, aging, prior injury, synovitis, effusion, BMLs, malalignment, KL grade, and pain severity. These features may enrich sampling and subtype identification, but they should remain modifiers rather than standalone criteria. A defensible research phenotype requires convergent evidence of lipid-derived signals in prespecified spaces, co-localized barrier or matrix-barrier injury, cellular execution or tissue injury, and added temporal, prognostic, or treatment-response value beyond conventional KOA variables. Supportive features such as IFP MRI abnormality, synovitis, effusion, BMLs, or pain severity may enrich the phenotype but should not independently define it.

Validation strategy

Spatial validation

Spatial studies should be prioritized because the hypothesis is fundamentally spatial. IFP, synovium, synovial fluid, cartilage-interface tissues, and subchondral marrow samples should be paired when feasible. Candidate approaches include spatial lipidomics, mass spectrometry imaging, multiplex immunostaining for ApoB or lipid adducts, lipid-droplet mapping, endothelial-junction markers, glycocalyx or permeability markers, albumin/fibrinogen leak, 4-HNE/MDA adducts, macrophage lipid uptake markers, and prespecified region-of-interest analysis. Useful regions include perivascular, interstitial, adipocyte-adjacent, synovial-interface, cartilage-facing, osteochondral-interface, and BML-adjacent areas.

The strongest spatial test would show that lipid-derived reporters are enriched in prespecified vulnerable regions compared with matched internal controls and that these lipid signals co-localize with barrier leak, endothelial or matrix injury, macrophage lipid uptake, hypoxia, oxidative injury, or matrix catabolism. A weaker but still useful test would show paired tissue-fluid gradients, such as serum-synovial-tissue TG/ApoB relationships coupled to local leak markers and tissue injury.

Longitudinal validation

Longitudinal studies should test ordering rather than association alone. The key prediction is that lipid mislocalization and barrier vulnerability precede or predict cytokine execution, hypoxic amplification, matrix injury, pain trajectory, structural progression, or treatment response beyond age, sex, BMI, malalignment, Kellgren-Lawrence grade, baseline pain, synovitis, and BMLs. Repeated synovial-fluid sampling, serial MRI, dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI, tissue-spatial sampling from staged disease, and mediation or change-point models could help test whether lipid-barrier signatures occur upstream of progression rather than merely after late tissue destruction.

Temporal testing should also distinguish early permissive states from late lock-in. Early disease may show reversible lipid-barrier coupling and a stronger response to mechanical unloading, metabolic correction, or barrier stabilization. Established disease may show a weaker response to single-axis intervention because lipid handling, cytokine execution, hypoxia, oxidative-matrix injury, and barrier failure have become mutually regenerative.

Intervention and treatment-response validation

Intervention-enriched studies should test whether a lipid-barrier coupling-enriched phenotype responds differently to mechanical unloading or decompression, metabolic lipid reduction, cytokine modulation, antioxidant or NOX-targeted strategies, HIF/VEGF or permeability modulation, barrier stabilization, or macrophage-repair reprogramming. A positive result would not require every intervention to work; it would require the phenotype to show mechanistically coherent proof-of-response.

For example, if a lipid-barrier phenotype predicts improvement in synovial-fluid TG/ApoB, 4-HNE/MDA, IL-6/CCL2, VEGF, albumin/fibrinogen leak, cartilage matrix fragments, or pain trajectory after a barrier- or lipid-targeted intervention, this would support the framework. Conversely, if the signature fails to add predictive value beyond synovitis, BMLs, BMI, malalignment, KL grade, and baseline pain, the framework should not be retained as a separate mechanistic category.

Evidence boundaries, competing explanations, and falsification criteria

What current evidence does not yet prove

Current evidence provides plausible component-level support and several strong links, especially IFP biological activity and circulation-to-synovial-cavity access. It does not yet prove that TG/ApoB-rich cargo accumulates in human IFP perivascular or interstitial regions as an initiating threshold event. It does not prove that TG/FFA enrichment in IFP or subchondral marrow is circulation-derived, because local adipocyte lipolysis, marrow-adipose remodeling, inflammatory lipolysis, cell death, macrophage lipid handling, and tissue-specific lipid remodeling remain plausible sources. It also does not prove that lipid mislocalization precedes cytokine execution, hypoxia, barrier injury, and progression in the predicted order.

This limitation should be explicit without weakening the purpose of the hypothesis. The evidence gap is not the absence of lipid abnormalities or barrier vulnerability. The decisive gap is source-resolved spatial co-localization and temporal ordering: TG/ApoB-rich cargo or lipid-derived reporters must be shown in prespecified vulnerable regions together with endothelial leak, albumin/fibrinogen extravasation, macrophage lipid uptake, hypoxia, matrix injury, and later progression.

Competing explanations

Several competing explanations must remain viable. General synovitis can produce cytokines, VEGF, effusion, pain, and matrix catabolism without requiring lipid-spatial mislocalization. Conventional lipotoxicity can explain cellular injury from FFAs or oxidized lipids without requiring a defined anatomical leakage event. Mechanical overload can explain cartilage injury, synovitis, IFP stress, and subchondral remodeling without requiring circulating lipid entry. Obesity and metaflammation can produce systemic inflammatory and lipid abnormalities without a source-resolved local threshold event. Late-stage tissue destruction can release lipids and plasma proteins secondarily. Subchondral-origin models can explain marrow-adipose activation, BMLs, pain, and osteochondral remodeling without making the IFP the initiating node. Finally, IFP abnormalities may be secondary responders to a broader diseased-joint environment rather than initiating or amplifying drivers.

The spatial lipid-barrier coupling framework is useful only if it can be distinguished from these alternatives. In practice, that means it must identify a reproducible lipid-barrier pattern with spatial specificity, temporal relevance, and added predictive or therapeutic value. If it cannot do so, the framework should be narrowed to a descriptive module within inflammatory or metabolic KOA rather than retained as a distinct mechanism.

Falsification criteria

The spatial lipid-barrier coupling framework should be narrowed or rejected if it fails discriminating tests. If spatial studies do not detect TG/ApoB-linked cargo or lipid-derived reporter signals in vulnerable IFP, synovial, cartilage-interface, or subchondral marrow regions, the lipid-spatial mislocalization component should be weakened. If TG/ApoB signals do not co-localize with albumin/fibrinogen leak, endothelial-junction injury, glycocalyx disruption, macrophage lipid uptake, or matrix injury, the circulation-to-tissue leakage interpretation should be weakened. If lipid droplets or FFAs localize only to late cell death or generalized tissue destruction, they should be interpreted as secondary injury products rather than threshold events.

If longitudinal studies show lipid abnormalities only after advanced tissue destruction, the threshold-event interpretation should be rejected. If lipid-barrier coupling-enriched signatures do not predict progression, pain biology, or treatment response beyond synovitis, BMLs, BMI, malalignment, radiographic grade, and baseline pain, the framework should not be retained as a separate mechanistic label. If subchondral-origin, synovitis-only, or mechanical-overload models explain the same outcomes with equal or greater predictive power and no lipid-barrier contribution, the IFP-centered spatial lipid-barrier coupling model should be downgraded.

Translational implications

If validated, the spatial lipid-barrier coupling framework would support mechanism-based subgrouping rather than a universal KOA treatment strategy. Early or permissive-stage disease may require correction of mechanical impingement, metabolic susceptibility, microvascular instability, and barrier vulnerability before structural lock-in develops. Established lipid-barrier coupling states would probably require a combination strategy rather than single-axis intervention: reducing lipid substrate, limiting excessive cytokine execution, alleviating hypoxia and improving microcirculation, reducing oxidative-matrix injury, stabilizing vascular and matrix barriers, and preserving macrophage clearance and repair functions.

At present, no diagnostic label, disease-modifying osteoarthritis drug, or targeted clinical therapy specific to this proposed phenotype can be recommended on the basis of this hypothesis alone. These implications remain provisional until a lipid-barrier coupling-enriched phenotype predicts differential biological or clinical response.

The most practical near-term use is research enrichment. A lipid-barrier signature could be used to stratify patients in imaging-biomarker cohorts, synovial-fluid studies, tissue-spatial studies, and proof-of-mechanism interventions. The framework would be useful if it identifies a subset with distinct spatial signatures, progression risk, pain biology, or intervention response. It would be unnecessary if it merely renames synovitis, lipotoxicity, mechanical overload, metabolic OA, or late-stage degeneration.

Conclusion

This mechanism-based Perspective proposes that a biologically enriched subset of KOA may be amplified by spatial lipid mislocalization, local lipid processing, inflammatory and hypoxic-vascular signaling, oxidative-matrix injury, and barrier-defined propagation centered on the IFP as an anatomically privileged amplification hub. The novelty of the model lies in a spatial lipid-barrier rule: lipid-derived signals become mechanistically meaningful only when they occur in vulnerable spaces, co-localize with barrier injury, and add predictive or therapeutic value beyond standard KOA variables. KOA progression may depend less on lipid quantity than on lipid location, barrier state, and compartmental propagation. Current evidence supports the plausibility of this framework, but source-resolved spatial proof and temporal ordering remain required. The framework should therefore be treated as a falsifiable research construct whose value depends on identifying a reproducible lipid-barrier phenotype with distinct progression risk, pain biology, or treatment response.
