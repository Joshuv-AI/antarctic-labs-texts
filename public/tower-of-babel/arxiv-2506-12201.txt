Functional Multi-Reference Alignment via Deconvolution
O. Al-Ghattas, A. Little, D. Sanz-Alonso, M. Sweeney

Abstract
This paper studies the multi-reference alignment (MRA) problem of estimating a signal function from shifted, noisy observations. Our functional formulation reveals a new connection between MRA and deconvolution: the signal can be estimated from second-order statistics via Kotlarski’s formula, an important identification result in deconvolution with replicated measurements. To design our MRA algorithms, we extend Kotlarski’s formula to general dimension and study the estimation of signals with vanishing Fourier transform, thus also contributing to the deconvolution literature. We validate our deconvolution approach to MRA through both theory and numerical experiments.

This paper studies the functional multi-reference alignment (MRA) problem of estimating a signal function from shifted, noisy observations. 1.2
Literature review

1.2.1
Discrete MRA

In the discrete formulation of the MRA problem, the goal is to recover an unknown signal
vector

f\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
from
N
noisy, randomly shifted observations:

\displaystyle y_{n}[j]

\displaystyle=f\big[j-\zeta_{n}]+\eta_{n}[j],\qquad 1\leq j\leq J,\quad 1\leq n\leq N,

(1)

where translations of indices are defined modulo
J
. Each shift
\zeta_{n}
is drawn independently from a probability distribution on
\{0,1,\ldots,J-1\}
, and the noise vectors
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{N}
are independent, isotropic Gaussian vectors with variance
\sigma^{2}
.

The MRA model arises in a variety of applications, including structural biology and image processing
[
24
,
44
,
45
,
50
,
52
,
56
,
18
,
29
,
60
]
. In particular, it serves as a simplified model for cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), where the goal is to reconstruct a molecular structure from noisy, randomly rotated projections. As noted in
[
9
]
, “[MRA] … represents a special case of cryo-EM, corresponding to the scenario in which the molecule’s axis of rotation is known, but its rotation angle is not.” More broadly, MRA belongs to a class of models where a latent signal is transformed by a group action and possibly projected or downsampled
[
10
,
3
,
16
]
.

Numerous algorithmic strategies have been developed to solve MRA. When the noise level is moderate,
synchronization
-based methods —which estimate the shifts and then align the observations— are often effective
[
8
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
17
,
19
,
20
,
47
,
54
,
59
]
. However, synchronization fails in the high-noise regime, motivating alternative approaches such as expectation-maximization (EM)
[
2
,
23
,
7
]
, the method of moments
[
32
,
37
,
53
,
1
]
, and the method of invariants
[
8
,
14
,
21
,
35
,
34
]
.

The identifiability and sample complexity of discrete MRA depend critically on the properties of the shift distribution. If the distribution is
aperiodic
, the signal
f
can be recovered from the first and second empirical moments using a spectral method, with sample complexity
N\gtrsim\sigma^{4}

[
2
]
. In contrast, if the shift distribution is
periodic
—e.g., uniform on
\{0,\ldots,J-1\}
—higher-order moments are typically required. (An important exception is when the signal is sparse
[
15
,
31
,
30
]
). A common approach in this case is to estimate the bispectrum of
f
(which requires
N\gtrsim\sigma^{6}
samples), and then invert it; see
[
46
]
for theoretical guarantees using Jennrich’s algorithm. Recent work
[
25
]
provides precise error bounds for bispectrum inversion with explicit dependence on signal length and frequency structure. The high-dimensional setting has also been studied: for instance,
[
49
]
shows that for Gaussian signals, a phase transition in sample complexity occurs at the threshold
J/(\sigma^{2}\log J)=2.

Closely related to the discrete MRA problem, and moving towards a functional formulation, one may consider estimating the Fourier coefficients
f^{\text{ft}}[j],

1\leq j\leq J,
of a
band-limited
signal function
f
from Fourier domain observations of the form

y^{\text{ft}}_{n}[j]=e^{-ij\zeta_{n}}\,f^{\text{ft}}[j]+\eta^{\text{ft}}_{n}[j],\qquad 1\leq j\leq J,\qquad 1\leq n\leq N.

This
phase shift model
has been studied for instance in
[
9
,
25
]
.

1.2.2
Functional MRA

Our study of functional MRA is motivated by several limitations of the discrete formulation given in (
1
), both as a model for practical applications and in terms of its theoretical analysis. We outline these issues below.

In many of the applications that motivate MRA, such as cryo-EM, the latent signal is more naturally modeled as a continuous
function
rather than a finite-dimensional vector. In such cases, it is important to understand how properties of the noise affect sample complexity independently of the discretization level
J
. For example, while the results in
[
25
]
are minimax optimal for the discrete model (
1
), their analysis does not extend meaningfully to the limit
J\to\infty
, as the limiting signal is not square-integrable. Similarly, the inversion guarantees in
[
46
]
degrade as the signal length
J
increases.

Moreover, the white noise assumption in (
1
) forces the noise to blow up as
J\to\infty
, which in turn necessitates that the noise level
\sigma\to 0
as
J\to\infty
for recovery to remain possible
[
49
,
25
]
. Beyond this technical issue, white noise is often an unrealistic modeling choice. In many applications, including cryo-EM, the noise is known to exhibit spatial correlations
[
33
,
13
,
36
]
. As resolution increases, the assumption that pixelwise noise is uncorrelated becomes increasingly implausible, and a Gaussian process model with short-range correlations (small lengthscale) may offer a more accurate representation.

These limitations motivate us to recast (
1
) within a
functional
framework. This formulation not only accommodates natural smoothness assumptions on the underlying signal but also permits the exploitation of derivative information in the Fourier domain, which is central to our deconvolution methodology. In this vein, the functional model elucidates a deep connection between MRA and the classical statistical problem of deconvolution from replicated measurements
[
41
,
40
,
22
,
39
]
, wherein one seeks to identify the distribution of a latent variable from repeated, conditionally independent noisy observations —possibly under unknown noise. This connection enables us to leverage established inferential techniques, most notably Kotlarski’s identity
[
48
,
38
]
, which guarantees identifiability of the latent distribution from the joint law of the noisy replicates.
Our results illustrate that the insights gained from discrete MRA do not always apply to functional MRA. For example, our analysis and numerical experiments suggest that the sample complexity for solving functional MRA with a uniform shift distribution is no worse than with an aperiodic shift distribution.

We note for completeness that in order to analyze the impact of random dilations,
[
58
,
35
,
34
]
also formulate a functional model with white noise; however, theoretical guarantees are derived only for the relevant Fourier invariants, and inversion is done with existing discrete algorithms which lack theoretical guarantees in the functional context.

MRA: functional setting and deconvolution algorithm

2.1
Problem setting

Let
D:=[-1,1]^{d}
and let
f:D\to\mathbb{R}
be a square-integrable function supported on
\bigl[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\bigr]^{d}
. Our goal is to estimate the signal
f
from
N
independent observations
\{y_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
of the form

\displaystyle y_{n}(t)=f(t-\zeta_{n})+\eta_{n}(t),\qquad t\in D,\qquad n=1,\ldots,N.

(2)

Here,
\{\zeta_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
are independent copies of a centered random vector
\zeta
with density
p_{\zeta}
supported on
\bigl[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\bigr]^{d},
and
\{\eta_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
are independent copies of a centered square-integrable Gaussian process
\eta
on
D
with known covariance function
k_{\eta}.
Thus, each observation
y_{n}
represents a shifted, noisy measurement of the signal
f.
We assume that the shifts
\{\zeta_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
and measurement errors
\{\eta_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
are all independent. We will let
y(t)=f(t-\zeta)+\eta(t)
denote a generic observation.
Note that we do not assume periodicity of the signal
f,
nor cyclic shifts; due to the compact support of the signal and shifts, all observations are supported in the domain
D.

1

In our numerical examples, we use a slightly different convention and take
D=[-2,2]^{d}
and the signal and shifts to be supported on
[-1,1]^{d}.
See Figure

for illustrative signals and observations in dimension
d=1
.

2.2
Identifiability via second-order statistics in the Fourier domain

We will work in the Fourier domain, where the observations take the form

\displaystyle y^{\text{ft}}_{n}(\omega)

\displaystyle=e^{-i\omega\cdot\zeta_{n}}f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)+\eta^{\text{ft}}_{n}(\omega),\qquad\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad n=1,\dots,N.

(3)

Here,
f^{\text{ft}}(\omega):=\int_{D}f(t)e^{-it\cdot\omega}\,dt
and
\eta^{\text{ft}}_{n}(\omega):=\int_{D}\eta_{n}(t)e^{-it\cdot\omega}\,dt
denote the Fourier transforms of
f
and
\eta_{n}
. As before, we denote by
y^{\text{ft}}
and
\eta^{\text{ft}}
a generic observation and measurement error in Fourier domain.

Our deconvolution approach to MRA is based on the fact that the autocorrelation function
r_{y^{\text{ft}}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[y^{\text{ft}}(\omega_{1})\overline{y^{\text{ft}}(\omega_{2})}\bigr]
of the noncentered process
y^{\text{ft}}

satisfies

\displaystyle r_{y^{\text{ft}}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=f^{\text{ft}}(\omega_{1})\overline{f^{\text{ft}}(\omega_{2})}p^{\text{ft}}_{\zeta}(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})+k_{\eta^{\text{ft}}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=:\Psi(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})+k_{\eta^{\text{ft}}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}),\qquad\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
k_{\eta^{\text{ft}}}
is the covariance function of
\eta^{\text{ft}}
. Note that since
f,p_{\zeta}
have compact support, it follows that
f^{\text{ft}},p^{\text{ft}}_{\zeta}\in C^{\infty};
consequently, we also have that
\Psi\in C^{\infty}.

The following identity shows that
f^{\text{ft}}
is completely determined by a nonlinear transformation of
\Psi.
For
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d},v\in\mathbbm{C}^{d}
we abuse notation slightly and write
u\cdot v
to mean
u\cdot\Re(v)+iu\cdot\Im(v),
where
\Re(v),\Im(v)
are the real and imaginary parts of
v,
respectively.

Theorem 1
(Identification).

Let
p_{\zeta}\in L^{2}(D)
be the probability density function of a centered random variable
\zeta
with Fourier transform
p^{\text{ft}}_{\zeta}

and let
f\in L^{2}(D)

have Fourier transform
f^{\text{ft}}
satisfying
f^{\text{ft}}(0)=1.

Define

\Psi(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}):=f^{\text{ft}}(\omega_{1})\overline{f^{\text{ft}}(\omega_{2})}p^{\text{ft}}_{\zeta}(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}),\qquad\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(4)

and assume that
\Psi
is nowhere vanishing on its diagonal (i.e.
\Psi(\omega,\omega)\neq 0
for any
\omega
). Then,

\displaystyle f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\nabla_{1}\Psi(\alpha\omega,\alpha\omega)}{\Psi(\alpha\omega,\alpha\omega)}\cdot\omega~d\alpha\right),

(5)

where
\nabla_{1}
indicates the gradient with respect to the first argument.

Proof.

The assumption that
\Psi
is nowhere vanishing on its diagonal implies that
f^{\text{ft}}
is nowhere vanishing. Note that since
f\in L^{2}(D)
and
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact, we have that
f^{\text{ft}}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).
Further,
f^{\text{ft}}(0)=1
by assumption. Therefore, Lemma
23
implies

\displaystyle f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\nabla f^{\text{ft}}(\alpha\omega)}{f^{\text{ft}}(\alpha\omega)}\cdot\omega\,d\alpha\right),\quad\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(6)

Noting that
p^{\text{ft}}_{\zeta}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),
direct calculation shows that

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla f^{\text{ft}}(\alpha\omega)}{f^{\text{ft}}(\alpha\omega)}=\frac{\nabla_{1}\Psi(\alpha\omega,\alpha\omega)}{\Psi(\alpha\omega,\alpha\omega)}-\frac{\nabla p^{\text{ft}}_{\zeta}(0)}{p^{\text{ft}}_{\zeta}(0)}.

Finally,
observe that
\nabla p^{\text{ft}}_{\zeta}(0)=0
since
\zeta
is centered by assumption.

Remark 2
.

The setting where
f^{\text{ft}}
is allowed to vanish (allowing
\Psi(\omega,\omega)
to vanish as well) will be considered in Section
4
.
We assume
f^{\text{ft}}(0)=1
only for ease of presentation: If
f^{\text{ft}}(0)=c\neq 0,
the constant
c
can be estimated by
N^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}y^{\text{ft}}_{n}(0)
, and the data can then be rescaled accordingly.

2.3
Main algorithm

We introduce a three-step approach to estimate the signal
f
:

(a)

Estimate
\Psi(\omega,\omega),
for instance by
\widehat{\Psi}(\omega,\omega):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}y^{\text{ft}}_{n}(\omega)\overline{y^{\text{ft}}_{n}(\omega)}-k^{\text{ft}}_{\eta}(\omega,\omega);

(b)

Estimate
f^{\text{ft}}
by plugging in the estimate of
\Psi
into (
5
);

(c)

Deconvolve the estimate of
f^{\text{ft}}
to obtain an estimate of
f
.

There is significant flexibility in the implementation of each step. Algorithm
1
outlines the method that we will analyze in Section
3
and test numerically in Section
5
. First, we estimate
\Psi
using the sample moment estimator
\widehat{\Psi},
and also introduce a regularized estimator
\widetilde{\Psi}.
Second, we estimate
f^{\text{ft}}
by plugging in
\widehat{\Psi}
and
\widetilde{\Psi}
in the numerator and denominator in (
5
), respectively. Third, as is standard in the deconvolution literature, we estimate
f
by regularizing the inverse Fourier transform of
\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}
with a user-chosen kernel function.

Algorithm 1

MRA via Deconvolution

1:

Input:
Data
\{y_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N};
Covariance function
k_{\eta};
Kernel
K;
Bandwidth parameter
h>0.

2:

Estimate
\Psi
:
Set

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}(\omega,\omega):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}y^{\text{ft}}_{n}(\omega)\overline{y^{\text{ft}}_{n}(\omega)}-k^{\text{ft}}_{\eta}(\omega,\omega),\qquad\widetilde{\Psi}(\omega,\omega):=\frac{\widehat{\Psi}(\omega,\omega)}{1\land\sqrt{N}|\widehat{\Psi}(\omega,\omega)|}.\vskip-12.80365pt

3:

Estimate
f^{\text{ft}}
:
Set

\displaystyle\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}(\omega):=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\nabla_{1}\widehat{\Psi}(\alpha\omega,\alpha\omega)}{\widetilde{\Psi}(\alpha\omega,\alpha\omega)}\cdot\omega~d\alpha\right).

4:

Deconvolve:
Set

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(t):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\omega\cdot t}\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}(\omega)K^{\text{ft}}(h\omega)\,d\omega.

5:

Output
: Approximation
\widehat{f}
to the hidden signal
f.

2.4
Deconvolution perspective and Kotlarski’s identity

Theorem
1
reveals a fundamental connection between functional MRA and deconvolution. In this subsection, we elaborate on this connection by framing the theorem as a multivariate generalization of Kotlarski’s lemma
[
38
,
48
]
. We begin by reviewing the
replicated measurements framework

[
41
, Section 2.6.3]
, a generalization of classical density deconvolution widely studied in econometrics.

In standard density deconvolution, the goal is to recover the density of a real-valued latent variable
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
from i.i.d. observations of
Z=X_{0}+X_{1}
, where
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
are independent. Without knowledge of the distribution of
X_{1}
, there is lack of identifiability. This issue disappears in the presence of
replicated measurements
, where we observe
N
independent copies
\{(Z_{n,1},Z_{n,2})\}_{n=1}^{N}
of the random vector
(Z_{1},Z_{2})
defined by

\displaystyle Z_{1}:=X_{0}+X_{1},\quad Z_{2}:=X_{0}+X_{2},

with
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
independent, identically distributed, and independent of
X_{0}
. The joint characteristic function of
(Z_{1},Z_{2})
is then

\displaystyle\psi(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\mathbb{E}[\exp(i\omega_{1}Z_{1}+i\omega_{2}Z_{2})]=\varphi_{1}(\omega_{1})\varphi_{1}(\omega_{2})\varphi_{0}(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}),

(7)

where
\varphi_{0}
and
\varphi_{1}
denote the characteristic functions of
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
, respectively. The key insight that allows us to connect MRA and deconvolution is that the function
\Psi
defined in (
4
) has the same structure (up to a sign flip) as the function
\psi
in (
7
), with
f^{\text{ft}}
playing the role of
\varphi_{1}
and
p^{\text{ft}}_{\zeta}
playing the role of
\varphi_{0}.

Kotlarski’s identity
[
48
, Lemma 1]
provides a powerful identification result in the deconvolution setting (leveraged for instance in
[
40
,
22
,
39
]
): if
\psi
is nowhere vanishing, then the joint distribution of
(Z_{1},Z_{2})
identifies the distributions of
X_{0},X_{1},X_{2}
up to location shifts. Moreover, an explicit formula (see
[
48
, Remarks 2.1.11]
) for the characteristic function of
X_{0}
is given by

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}(\omega)=\exp\left(i\omega\,\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]+\int_{0}^{\omega}\frac{\partial_{1}\psi(0,\omega^{\prime})}{\psi(0,\omega^{\prime})}\,d\omega^{\prime}\right),\quad\omega\in\mathbb{R},

(8)

with
\varphi_{1}
recovered via

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(\omega)=\frac{\psi(\omega,0)}{\varphi_{0}(\omega)}.

(9)

In settings where the target of estimation is
\varphi_{1}
,
[
26
]
proposes to use the direct expression

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(\omega)=\exp\left(i\omega\,\mathbb{E}[X_{0}]+\int_{0}^{\omega}\frac{\partial_{1}\psi(\omega^{\prime},-\omega^{\prime})}{\psi(\omega,-\omega^{\prime})}\,d\omega^{\prime}\right),\quad\omega\in\mathbb{R}.

(10)

It is natural to assume that
X_{1},X_{2}
are centered as they are most often interpreted as random noise. We can then estimate the density of
X_{0}
(resp.
X_{1}
) as follows:

(a)

Estimate the joint characteristic function
\psi,
e.g. by the sample version
\widehat{\psi}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}):=N^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\exp(i\omega_{1}Z_{n,1}+i\omega_{2}Z_{n,2}).

(b)

Estimate
\varphi_{0}
(resp.
\varphi_{1}
) by plugging in
\widehat{\psi}
into (
8
) (resp. (
9
) or (
10
)).

(c)

Deconvolve the estimate of
\varphi_{0}
(resp.
\varphi_{1}
) to estimate the density of
X_{0}
(resp.
X_{1}
).

Notice that this three-step procedure is completely analogous to our deconvolution approach to MRA in Subsection
2.3
.

Deconvolution via Kotlarki’s identity in the multivariate setting is, however, less well developed.

[
48
, Theorem 3.1.1]
extends Kotlarski’s identification result to random elements in a separable Hilbert space, but without providing an explicit estimator. In the context of errors-in-variables regression,
[
51
]
derives several Kotlarski-type results in the univariate case and notes —without proof— that multivariate extensions may be obtained via path integrals
[
51
, Footnote 11, p. 49]
.

Theorem
1
and its proof address this gap, delivering a constructive multivariate generalization of Kotlarski’s lemma. A slight adaptation of the proof yields the following corollary:

Corollary 3
(Multivariate Kotlarski).

Let
X_{0},X_{1},X_{2}
be independent integrable random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and define

\displaystyle Z_{1}:=X_{0}+X_{1},\quad Z_{2}:=X_{0}+X_{2}.

Let
\varphi_{k}(\omega):=\mathbb{E}[e^{i\omega\cdot X_{k}}]
for
k=0,1,2
, and define

\displaystyle\psi(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}):=\mathbb{E}[e^{i\omega_{1}\cdot Z_{1}+i\omega_{2}\cdot Z_{2}}]=\varphi_{0}(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})\varphi_{1}(\omega_{1})\varphi_{2}(\omega_{2}),\quad\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

If
\psi
is nowhere vanishing, then

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}(\omega)=\exp\left(-i\omega\cdot\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\nabla_{1}\psi(0,\alpha\omega)}{\psi(0,\alpha\omega)}\cdot\omega\,d\alpha\right),\quad\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(11)

In the univariate case (
d=1
), the change of variable
\omega^{\prime}=\alpha\omega
in the integral component of (
11
) recovers the classical identity in (
8
). An identical argument can also be used to establish the multivariate version of (
10
),

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-i\omega\cdot\mathbb{E}X_{0}+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\nabla_{1}\psi(\alpha\omega,-\alpha\omega)}{\psi(\alpha\omega,-\alpha\omega)}\cdot\omega\,d\alpha\right)\,,\qquad\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(12)

3.1
Error bound for Algorithm
1

In this subsection, we derive a high-probability bound on the deviation
\|\widehat{f}-f\|_{\infty}.
To prove our main result, Theorem

Throughout, we will work with the following standing assumption:

Assumption 4
(Standing assumption).

Let
H^{s}(D)
be a Sobolev space of order
s>d/2
on the domain
D=[-1,1]^{d}.

(i)

The signal
f
satisfies
f\in H^{s}(D)
and is supported on
[-1/2,1/2]^{d}
with
f^{\text{ft}}(0)=1
.

(ii)

The noise
\eta

(iii)

The shift
\zeta
is a centered random variable supported on
[-1/2,1/2]^{d}.

As noted in Remark

, the assumption that
f^{\text{ft}}(0)=1

Lemma 5
(Concentration of
\widehat{\Psi}
).

Let

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}(\omega,\omega):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}y^{\text{ft}}_{n}(\omega)\overline{y^{\text{ft}}_{n}(\omega)}-k_{\eta^{\text{ft}}}(\omega,\omega)\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\nabla_{1}\Psi}(\omega,\omega):=\nabla_{1}\widehat{\Psi}(\omega,\omega).

There exist positive universal constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that, for any
\tau\geq 1,
it holds with probability at least
1-c_{1}e^{-c_{2}\tau}
that

\displaystyle\sup_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\Psi}(\omega,\omega)-\Psi(\omega,\omega)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\rho_{N}\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\nabla_{1}\widehat{\Psi}(\omega,\omega)-\nabla_{1}\Psi(\omega,\omega)\|_{2}\lesssim\rho_{N},

where, with
r_{y}(t,t^{\prime}):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[y(t)y(t^{\prime})\bigr]
denoting the autocorrelation function of
y
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{N}:=\|r_{y}\|_{\infty}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{N}}\lor\frac{\tau}{N}\lor\frac{\Gamma(y)}{\sqrt{N}}\lor\frac{\Gamma(y)^{2}}{N}\right),\qquad\Gamma(y):=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in D}y(t)]}{\|r_{y}\|_{\infty}^{1/2}}.

(13)

Next, we analyze the recovery of the signal in Fourier domain.
The following assumption generalizes to a multivariate setting the commonly used
ordinary smoothness
assumption in the deconvolution literature; see for example
[
28
,
40
,
22
,
39
]
. We make explicit the dependence on
\|f\|_{\infty},
as this quantity will appear in our notion of signal-to-noise ratio in Subsection
3.2
.

Assumption 6
(Smoothness).

The signal
f
has non-vanishing Fourier transform
f^{\text{ft}}.
Further, there exist positive constants

\beta,\omega^{\star}\geq 1
and positive universal constants
C\geq c
such that

\displaystyle c\|f\|_{\infty}\|\omega\|_{2}^{-\beta}\leq|f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)|\leq C\|f\|_{\infty}\|\omega\|_{2}^{-\beta}\qquad\text{ for all }\quad\|\omega\|_{2}\geq\omega^{\star}.

In Section
4
, we will show that
the non-vanishing assumption for
f^{\text{ft}}
can be relaxed, but doing so requires a more intricate procedure and theoretical analysis. We remark further that, in principle, all our results extend to alternative formulations of
super smoothness
considered in the aforementioned literature; however, we do not investigate this extension in this work.

Theorem 7
(Recovery in Fourier domain).

Under Assumption
6
with smoothness parameter
\beta
, let
h\in(0,1)
satisfy
\rho_{N}\lesssim\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}h^{3\beta+1}
. Then, there exist positive universal constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that for any
\tau\geq 1,
it holds with probability at least
1-c_{1}e^{-c_{2}\tau}
that

\displaystyle\sup_{\omega\in[-h^{-1},h^{-1}]^{d}}\left|\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}(\omega)-f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)\right|\lesssim\frac{\rho_{N}h^{-3\beta-1}}{\|f\|_{\infty}}.

If in addition
\sup_{\omega\in[-h^{-1},h^{-1}]^{d}}\frac{\|\nabla f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)\|_{2}}{|f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)|}\lesssim 1,
then, for any
h\in(0,1)
that satisfies
\rho_{N}\lesssim\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}h^{2\beta+1}
, it holds with the same probability that

\displaystyle\sup_{\omega\in[-h^{-1},h^{-1}]^{d}}\left|\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}(\omega)-f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)\right|\lesssim\frac{\rho_{N}h^{-2\beta-1}}{\|f\|_{\infty}}.

Finally, to analyze the recovery error in the spatial domain, we place the following assumption on the kernel:

Assumption 8
(Deconvolution kernel).

For a positive even integer
p
, the kernel
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies:

(i)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(t)\,dt=1.

(ii)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}t^{\nu}K(t)\,dt=0
for all multi-indices
\nu\in\mathbbm{N}^{p}
with
1\leq|\nu|\leq p-1.

(iii)

t\mapsto t^{\nu}K(t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all multi-indices
\nu\in\mathbbm{N}^{p}
with
|\nu|=p.

(iv)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|t\|_{2}^{p}\,|K(t)|\,dt\leq C_{K}
where
C_{K}
if a finite constant allowed to depend on
K,d
, and
p.

(v)

K^{\text{ft}}(\omega)=0
for all
\|\omega\|_{\infty}>1.

We give an example of a family of kernels satisfying Assumption
8
in Appendix
B
. We are now ready to state our main result concerning recovery in space. Our proof technique borrows from the deconvolution literature, see e.g.
[
39
, Lemma A.3]
and
[
41
]
for related one-dimensional analyses, which achieve similar rates for deconvolution problems.

Theorem 9
(Recovery in spatial domain).

Let
f
satisfy Assumption
6
with smoothness parameter
\beta>d
and let
K
be a kernel satisfying Assumption
8
with
p>\beta-d
and bandwidth parameter
h
. Then, there exist positive universal constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that, for all
\tau\geq 1,
it holds with probability at least
1-c_{1}e^{-c_{2}\tau}
that the estimator
\widehat{f}(t)
in Algorithm
1
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\widehat{f}-f\|_{\infty}\lesssim\rho_{N}\|f\|_{\infty}^{-1}h^{-3\beta-d-1}+\|f\|_{\infty}h^{\beta-d},

where
\rho_{N}
is defined in (
13
).
Choosing
h=h^{*}\asymp\left(\frac{\rho_{N}}{\|f\|^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4\beta+1}}
thus yields an estimator
\widehat{f}
with relative error satisfying

\displaystyle\frac{\|\widehat{f}-f\|_{\infty}}{\|f\|_{\infty}}\lesssim\left(\frac{\rho_{N}}{\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{\beta-d}{4\beta+1}}.

Proof.

Theorem
7
shows that the event
\mathsf{E}_{h,\tau}
on which
\sup_{\omega\in[-h^{-1},h^{-1}]^{d}}|\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}(\omega)-f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)|\lesssim\rho_{N}\|f\|_{\infty}^{-2}h^{-3\beta-1}
satisfies
\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{E}_{h,\tau})\geq 1-c_{1}e^{-c_{2}\tau}.
Now, note that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{f}-f\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in D}\left|\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\omega\cdot t}\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}(\omega)K^{\text{ft}}(h\omega)\,d\omega-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\omega\cdot t}f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)\,d\omega\right|

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|K^{\text{ft}}(h\omega)||\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}(\omega)-f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)|\,d\omega+\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)||K^{\text{ft}}(h\omega)-1|\,d\omega

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt=:\textbf{(I)}+\textbf{(II)}.

Conditional on
\mathsf{E}_{h,\tau},
we have

(I)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[-h^{-1},h^{-1}]^{d}}|K^{\text{ft}}(h\omega)||\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}(\omega)-f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)|d\omega\leq\frac{C_{K^{\text{ft}}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[-h^{-1},h^{-1}]^{d}}|\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}(\omega)-f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)|d\omega

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{K^{\text{ft}}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\times 2h^{-d}\sup_{\omega\in[-h^{-1},h^{-1}]^{d}}|\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}(\omega)-f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)|\lesssim\rho_{N}\|f\|_{\infty}^{-1}h^{-3\beta-d-1},

where the first equality holds since

K^{\text{ft}}
is supported on
[-1,1]^{d}
by Assumption
8
(v),
for the first inequality we use that
\sup_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}K^{\text{ft}}(\omega)\leq C_{K^{\text{ft}}}
for some positive finite constant
C_{K^{\text{ft}}}
since
K
has compact support, and in the last inequality we use Assumption
6
. Next, for
(II)
, we have by Assumption
6
that

(II)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\|\omega\|_{2}^{-\beta}|K^{\text{ft}}(h\omega)-1|d\omega

\displaystyle=\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\left(\int_{[-h^{-1},h^{-1}]^{d}}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus[-h^{-1},h^{-1}]^{d}}\right)\|\omega\|_{2}^{-\beta}|K^{\text{ft}}(h\omega)-1|d\omega

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[-h^{-1},h^{-1}]^{d}}\|\omega\|_{2}^{-\beta}|K^{\text{ft}}(h\omega)-1|d\omega+\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\|\omega\|_{2}>h^{-1}}\|\omega\|_{2}^{-\beta}d\omega

\displaystyle=:\textbf{(A)}+\textbf{(B)}.

In the second equality, since
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus[-h^{-1},h^{-1}]^{d}
, we have
\|h\omega\|_{\infty}=h\|\omega\|_{\infty}>hh^{-1}=1,
and so by Assumption
8
(v)
|K^{\text{ft}}(h\omega)-1|=|0-1|=1.
The second inequality follows since
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus[-h^{-1},h^{-1}]^{d}\subset\{\omega:\|\omega\|_{2}>h^{-1}\}.
To control
\textbf{(A)},
we note that the
p
-th order remainder version of Taylor’s expansion of
K^{\text{ft}}(t)
about
t=0
yields

\displaystyle K^{\text{ft}}(h\omega)=1+\sum_{|\nu|=p}\frac{D^{\nu}K^{\text{ft}}(\xi)}{\nu!}(h\omega)^{\nu},

where
\xi
is a point on the line segment connecting the origin and
h\omega,
and we have used that all partial derivatives up to order
p-1
vanish by Assumption
8
(ii). Note that for any
\omega
,
D^{\nu}K^{\text{ft}}(\omega)=(-i)^{|\nu|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}t^{\nu}K(t)e^{-it\cdot\omega}dt
, which by Assumption
8
(iii) exists and is finite, and further is continuous in
\omega.
Therefore,
D^{\nu}K^{\text{ft}}
must be bounded on the compact set
[-1,1]^{d}.
Further, for
|\nu|=p
,

\displaystyle|(h\omega)^{\nu}|\leq h^{p}\prod_{j=1}^{d}|\omega_{j}|^{\nu_{j}}\leq h^{p}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\|\omega\|_{2}^{\nu_{j}}=h^{p}\|\omega\|_{2}^{p}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\textbf{(A)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\infty}h^{p}\int_{[-h^{-1},h^{-1}]^{d}}\|\omega\|_{2}^{p-\beta}d\omega=\|f\|_{\infty}h^{p+\beta-p-d}\int_{[-1,1]^{d}}\|\omega\|_{2}^{p-\beta}d\omega\asymp\|f\|_{\infty}h^{\beta-d},

where the final equality holds whenever
p>\beta-d.
To see this, denote the
d
-dimensional Euclidean ball of radius
R>0
by
B_{d}(R).
Then,

\displaystyle\int_{B_{d}(R)}\|\omega\|_{2}^{p-\beta}d\omega=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}\int_{0}^{R}r^{p-\beta+d-1}dr=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}\frac{R^{p-\beta+d}}{p-\beta+d}

if
p-\beta+d>0.
Now, since
B_{d}(1)\subset[-1,1]^{d}\subset B_{d}(\sqrt{d}),
it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{d}(1)}\|\omega\|_{2}^{p-\beta}d\omega\leq\int_{[-1,1]^{d}}\|\omega\|_{2}^{p-\beta}d\omega\leq\int_{B_{d}(\sqrt{d})}\|\omega\|_{2}^{p-\beta}d\omega,

and so if
p-\beta>-d,
we have

\displaystyle\int_{[-1,1]^{d}}\|\omega\|_{2}^{p-\beta}d\omega\asymp 1.

For
\textbf{(B)},
provided that
\beta>d
, by switching to polar coordinates we have

\displaystyle\textbf{(B)}=\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}\int_{h^{-1}}^{\infty}r^{d-\beta-1}dr\lesssim\|f\|_{\infty}h^{\beta-d}.

It follows that
\textbf{(II)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\infty}h^{\beta-d}.

3.2
Signal-to-noise ratio and model parameters

In much of the existing literature on MRA, results are formulated in terms of a
signal-to-noise ratio
; see, for example,
[
46
,
2
,
31
]
. While the functional MRA framework considered in this work differs significantly from these prior settings, we introduce a notion of signal-to-noise ratio
(\mathsf{snr})
that is tailored to the functional version of the MRA problem addressed here. Namely, we define

\displaystyle\mathsf{snr}:=\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\|k_{\eta}\|_{\infty}^{1/2}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in D}\eta(t)]}.

(14)

We now illustrate our results in the challenging
high-noise
regime, where
\|k_{\eta}\|_{\infty}^{1/2}\geq\|f\|_{\infty}
. Throughout this subsection, we adopt the framework of Theorem
9
and assume we are in the high-noise setting. Proofs for all results stated in this subsection are deferred to Appendix
B.5
.

Corollary 10
(High-noise regime).

Under the assumptions of Theorem
9
, suppose that
\|k_{\eta}\|^{1/2}_{\infty}\geq\|f\|_{\infty}
and
N\gtrsim\Gamma(y)^{2}\lor\tau
; then, it holds with probability at least
1-c_{1}e^{-c_{2}\tau}
that

\displaystyle\frac{\|\widehat{f}-f\|_{\infty}}{\|f\|_{\infty}}\lesssim\left(\|k_{\eta}\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{N}}\lor\frac{\mathsf{snr}^{-1}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)^{\frac{\beta-d}{4\beta+1}}.

Remark 11
.

Corollary
10
implies that, in the high-noise regime, it suffices to take
N\gtrsim\mathsf{snr}^{-2}
samples to ensure recovery of the signal function in relative supremum norm. Note further that since
\beta>d
, then as
\beta\to\infty
(increasing smoothness of the underlying signal) the bound becomes
(\mathsf{snr}^{-1}/\sqrt{N})^{1/4}.
Finally, we remark that in the high-noise regime, it is necessarily true that
\mathsf{snr}<1
since
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in D}\eta(t)]\geq\|f\|_{\infty}
as shown in the proof of Corollary
10
.

If the covariance function
k_{\eta}
of the noise process
\eta
belongs to a parametric family, then, for a fixed signal
f,
we may study the scaling of
\mathsf{snr}
with the parameters of
k_{\eta}.
For example, suppose that
\eta
has squared-exponential covariance function

\displaystyle k_{\eta}^{\text{SE}}(t,t^{\prime})=\sigma^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\|t-t^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}}\right),\qquad t,t^{\prime}\in D,

(15)

which satisfies Assumption
4
with arbitrary Sobolev order
s.
Then, the noise becomes stronger as the scale parameter
\sigma>0
increases, and more spatially uncorrelated as the lengthscale parameter
\lambda>0
decreases; if all other quantities are fixed, the high-noise regime holds provided that
\sigma
is sufficiently large, or that
\lambda
is sufficiently small. The following two corollaries quantify the individual effect of large
\sigma
and small
\lambda
in the sample complexity. In Corollary
12
(resp. Corollary
13
) we treat
\|f\|_{\infty}
and
\lambda
(resp.
\|f\|_{\infty}
and
\sigma
) as constants. Their proofs follow from Corollary
10
, using the scalings of
\mathsf{snr}
with
\sigma
and
\lambda:
namely,
\mathsf{snr}^{-1}\asymp\sigma^{2}
and
\mathsf{snr}^{-1}\asymp\sqrt{\log\lambda^{-d}}
for sufficiently small
\lambda.

Corollary 12
(High-noise, large-
\sigma
regime).

For all
\sigma
sufficiently large, the following holds.
For any
\tau\geq 1
, if
N\geq\tau
, it holds with probability at least
1-c_{1}e^{-c_{2}\tau}
that

\displaystyle\frac{\|\widehat{f}-f\|_{\infty}}{\|f\|_{\infty}}\lesssim\left(\sigma^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{N}}\right)^{\frac{\beta-d}{4\beta+1}}.

Corollary 13
(High-noise, small-
\lambda
regime).

For all
\lambda
sufficiently small, the following holds.
If
N\gtrsim\log\lambda^{-d},
it holds with probability at least
1-\lambda^{d}
that

\displaystyle\frac{\|\widehat{f}-f\|_{\infty}}{\|f\|_{\infty}}\lesssim\left(\frac{\log\lambda^{-d}}{N}\right)^{\frac{\beta-d}{8\beta+2}}.

Remark 14
.

Corollary
12
establishes that it is sufficient to choose
N\gtrsim\sigma^{4}

to guarantee accurate recovery of the underlying signal. While one might be inclined to draw comparisons with the discrete formulation of the MRA problem —where
N\gtrsim\sigma^{6}
samples are required in the case of periodic translations
[
46
]
, and
N\gtrsim\sigma^{4}
in the aperiodic setting
[
2
]
—it is important to note that our analysis pertains to the continuous variant of the problem and employs a distinct error metric, namely the relative supremum norm error. From a modeling viewpoint, the small-lengthscale regime is of particular interest and not captured by previous finite-dimensional analyses of MRA: in the limit as
\lambda\to 0,

k_{\eta}^{\text{SE}}
formally approximates a white noise model with no spatial correlations.
Relevant to Corollary
13
, the papers
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
show that the lengthscale fundamentally governs the difficulty of estimating the covariance operator of a Gaussian process. In a similar spirit, Corollary
13
shows that accurate recovery can be achieved with
N\gtrsim\log\lambda^{-d}
samples. Similar conclusions hold for a more general family of isotropic, positive, differentiable, and strictly decreasing covariance functions that decay to zero at infinity, including Matérn covariance functions —see
[
5
, Assumption 2.7]
.

4
Extension for vanishing Fourier transform

This section extends the procedure in Section
2
and the theory in Section
3
to the case where
f^{\text{ft}}
may vanish.

4.1
Algorithm: vanishing Fourier transform

Motivated by Algorithm
1
, here we propose a four-step procedure with the following outline:
(a) Estimate
\Psi.

(b) Estimate the zeros of
f^{\text{ft}}.

(c) Estimate
f^{\text{ft}}
by plugging the estimates obtained in (a) and (b) into a generalized Kotlarski’s formula.
(d) Deconvolve the estimate of
f^{\text{ft}}
to obtain an estimate of
f
.

We focus on the one-dimensional setting (
d=1
) and leverage a generalization of Kotlarski’s formula derived in
[
27
]
.
Specifically, let
h>0
be a bandwidth parameter and let
k(h^{-1})
denote the number of zeros of
f^{\text{ft}}
on the interval
[0,h^{-1}]
, which we denote by
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k(h^{-1})}
. Supposing that
k(h^{-1})<\infty
and that the zeros are simple (i.e. of multiplicity one),
[
27
]
shows that, for any fixed
\omega\in[0,h^{-1}]
,

\displaystyle f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\,(-1)^{k(\omega)}\prod_{i=1}^{k(\omega)}\exp\left(\int_{\xi_{i-1}+\varepsilon}^{\xi_{i}-\varepsilon}\frac{\partial_{1}\Psi(\xi,\xi)}{\Psi(\xi,\xi)}d\xi\right)\exp\left(\int_{\xi_{k(\omega)}+\varepsilon}^{\omega}\frac{\partial_{1}\Psi(\xi,\xi)}{\Psi(\xi,\xi)}d\xi\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\,(-1)^{k(\omega)}\exp\left(\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}\frac{\partial_{1}\Psi(\xi,\xi)}{\Psi(\xi,\xi)}d\xi\right),

(16)

where
k(\omega)
denotes the number of zeros on
[0,\omega],

\xi_{0}=0
for convenience, and

\displaystyle\omega_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=[0,\omega]\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{k(h^{-1})}(\xi_{i}-\varepsilon,\xi_{i}+\varepsilon)\,.

(17)

Fixing a small
\varepsilon
in the right-hand side of Equation
4.1
would in principle yield a good approximation to
f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)
for
\omega\in[0,h^{-1}]
. However, two key challenges arise:

(a)

The number
k(\omega)
of zeros and their locations
\xi_{i}
are unknown; and

(b)

The values of the integrand
\frac{\partial_{1}\Psi(\xi,\xi)}{\Psi(\xi,\xi)}
are not directly observable.

To address these challenges, we will estimate the zeros and the integrand from data. The idea is to define, for a small fixed
\varepsilon
and
\omega\in[0,h^{-1}],
the estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}_{\varepsilon}(\omega)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\widehat{k}(\omega)}\exp\left(\int_{\widehat{\omega}_{\varepsilon}}\frac{\partial_{1}\widehat{\Psi}(\xi,\xi)}{\widetilde{\Psi}(\xi,\xi)}d\xi\right),

(18)

where
\widehat{k}(\omega)
and
\widehat{\omega}_{\varepsilon}
are estimators of
k(\omega),\omega_{\varepsilon}
, respectively, and the functions
\widehat{\Psi}
and
\widetilde{\Psi}
are as defined in Algorithm
1
.
We then extend the definition of
\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
to
\omega\in[-h^{-1},0]
using conjugate symmetry, and set
\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}_{\varepsilon}(\omega)=0
for
\omega\notin[-h^{-1},h^{-1}]
.
Given the estimator
\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}_{\varepsilon}
, we estimate the underlying signal via deconvolution as in Algorithm
1
. We now turn to the problem of estimating the zeros of
f^{\text{ft}}
, which are needed to compute both
\widehat{k}(\omega)
and
\widehat{\omega}_{\varepsilon}.
To this end, we let
Z_{g}
denote the set of zeros of a function
g
, and
Z_{g}^{\omega}=Z_{g}\cap[0,\omega]
. Additionally, we let
P(\omega)=\Psi(\omega,\omega)=|f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)|^{2}
denote the power spectrum of the hidden signal, and let
\widehat{P}(\omega)=\Re\widehat{\Psi}(\omega,\omega)
be the real part of its empirical approximation as defined in Algorithm
1
.
To construct (
18
), we need to estimate
Z_{f^{\text{ft}}}^{h^{-1}}
, the zeros of
f^{\text{ft}}
on the interval
[0,h^{-1}]
, or, equivalently,
Z_{P}^{h^{-1}}
. We exploit the observation that when the zeros of
f^{\text{ft}}
are isolated and simple, the corresponding zeros of
P^{\prime}
exhibit linear crossings, in contrast to the quadratic behavior of the zeros of
P
. This motivates a practical approach for estimating
Z_{f^{\text{ft}}}^{h^{-1}}
: we identify the zeros of
\widehat{P}^{\prime}
and retain only those for which the value of
\widehat{P}
is sufficiently small. Specifically, for a threshold
{\mathcal{T}}>0
, we define the approximate zero set as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{T}}^{h^{-1}}

\displaystyle:=\Bigl\{\widehat{\xi}\in Z_{\widehat{P}^{\prime}}\,:\,\widehat{P}(\widehat{\xi})<{\mathcal{T}}\ ,\widehat{\xi}\in[0,h^{-1}]\Bigr\}

(19)

and let
\widehat{\xi}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\xi}_{\widehat{k}(h^{-1})}

denote the elements of
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{T}}^{h^{-1}}
arranged in increasing order, with
\\
\widehat{k}(h^{-1}):=|\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{T}}^{h^{-1}}|
.
We then use the estimated zeros to define
\widehat{k}(\omega)
and
\widehat{\omega}_{\varepsilon}
.
The full procedure is summarized in Algorithm
2
.

Algorithm 2

MRA via Deconvolution (Vanishing Case,
d=1
)

1:

Input:
Data
\{y_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N};
Covariance function
k_{\eta};
Kernel
K
; Bandwidth parameter
h>0
; Windowing parameter
\varepsilon
; Threshold
{\mathcal{T}}
.

2:

Estimate
\Psi
:
Set

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}(\omega,\omega):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}y^{\text{ft}}_{n}(\omega)\overline{y^{\text{ft}}_{n}(\omega)}-k^{\text{ft}}_{\eta}(\omega,\omega),\qquad\widetilde{\Psi}(\omega,\omega):=\frac{\widehat{\Psi}(\omega,\omega)}{1\land\sqrt{N}|\widehat{\Psi}(\omega,\omega)|}.

3:

Estimate zeros:
Set
\widehat{P}(\omega):=\Re\widehat{\Psi}(\omega,\omega)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{T}}^{h^{-1}}

\displaystyle:=\Bigl\{\widehat{\xi}\in Z_{\widehat{P}^{\prime}}\,:\,\widehat{P}(\widehat{\xi})<{\mathcal{T}}\ ,\widehat{\xi}\in[0,h^{-1}]\Bigr\}\,,\qquad\widehat{k}(h^{-1}):=|\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{T}}^{h^{-1}}|,

and let
\widehat{\xi}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\xi}_{\widehat{k}(h^{-1})}
be the increasingly ordered elements of
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{T}}^{h^{-1}}.

4:

Estimate
f^{\text{ft}}
:
For
\omega\in[0,h^{-1}]
, set

\displaystyle\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}_{\varepsilon}(\omega)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\widehat{k}(\omega)}\exp\left(\int_{\widehat{\omega}_{\varepsilon}}\frac{\partial_{1}\widehat{\Psi}(\xi,\xi)}{\widetilde{\Psi}(\xi,\xi)}d\xi\right),\,\quad\widehat{\omega}_{\varepsilon}:=[0,\omega]\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{\widehat{k}(h^{-1})}(\widehat{\xi}_{i}-\varepsilon,\widehat{\xi}_{i}+\varepsilon)\,.

For
\omega\in[-h^{-1},0]
, extend via conjugate symmetry. For
\omega\notin[-h^{-1},h^{-1}],
set
\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}_{\varepsilon}(\omega)=0.

5:

Deconvolve:
Set

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(t):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\omega t}\widehat{f}^{\text{ft}}_{\varepsilon}(\omega)K^{\text{ft}}(h\omega)\,d\omega.

6:

Output
: Approximation
\widehat{f}
to the hidden signal
f.

4.2
Theoretical guarantees: vanishing Fourier transform

Here, we analyze Algorithm
2
under our standing Assumption
4
and the following relaxed version of Assumption
6
. For notational brevity we will henceforth omit the superscript on the zero sets, with the assumption that all zero sets of functions are intersected with
[0,h^{-1}]
.

Assumption 15
(Vanishing model).

(a)

(Decay with oscillation)
Assume
f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)\neq 0
for
|\omega|\leq 1
while for all
|\omega|\geq 1

\displaystyle f^{\text{ft}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=o_{f}(\omega)\cdot\omega^{-\beta}\,\quad\text{with}\quad|o^{(\ell)}_{f}(\omega)|\leq C\|f\|_{\infty}\quad\text{for}\quad\ell=0,1,2

for a potentially oscillating function

6
Conclusions

This paper has introduced a novel deconvolution framework for functional MRA, establishing a connection to Kotlarski’s identity and extending it to multidimensional and vanishing-frequency settings. By formulating MRA in function space, our approach avoids issues arising in the discrete model as resolution increases, and enables recovery guarantees for non-bandlimited signals. Our theory provides error bounds in both Fourier and spatial domains, including in high-noise regimes, and accommodates signals with vanishing Fourier transforms —a significant advance over prior work. Numerical experiments confirm the practical effectiveness of our estimators, demonstrating accurate estimation for a variety of signals.

Lemma 23
(Path-wise exponential representation).

Suppose that
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbbm{C}
is a continuously differentiable function and
let
\Re\xi,\Im\xi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the real and imaginary parts of
\xi
, respectively, so that
\xi=\Re\xi+i\Im\xi.
Then for any
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which
\xi
is non-vanishing on the straight-line path from
\omega_{1}
to
\omega_{2},
which we denote by
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbbm{C}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\xi(\omega_{2})}{\xi(\omega_{1})}=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\nabla\xi(\gamma(\alpha))}{\xi(\gamma(\alpha))}\cdot(\omega_{2}-\omega_{1})d\alpha\right),

where
\nabla\xi(\omega)\cdot\omega:=\nabla\Re\xi(\omega)\cdot\omega+i\nabla\Im\xi(\omega)\cdot\omega
.

Proof.

Fix
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which the stated assumption holds and let
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the straight line path from
\omega_{1}
to
\omega_{2}
(i.e.
\gamma(\alpha)=(1-\alpha)\omega_{1}+\alpha\omega_{2}
). Let
\mathcal{L}(\alpha):=\log_{c}\xi(\gamma(\alpha))
, where
\log_{c}
denotes the continuously chosen branch of the complex logarithm along the path
\alpha\mapsto\xi(\gamma(\alpha)),
with initial condition
\mathcal{L}(0)=\log\xi(\omega_{1}).
Note that this is well-defined since
\xi\circ\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbbm{C}
is continuously differentiable and non-vanishing. Further,
\mathcal{L}
is smooth on
[0,1].
We then have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\alpha}\mathcal{L}(\alpha)=\frac{\nabla\xi(\gamma(\alpha))}{\xi(\gamma(\alpha))}\cdot(\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}).

(22)

The above can be justified as follows. Write

\displaystyle\log_{c}\xi=\log|\xi|+i\arg(\xi),

where
|\xi|
is the magnitude and
\arg(\xi)
is the continuously chosen argument of
\xi
along the curve. Below, all functions are evaluated at
\gamma(\alpha)
. Standard calculation then yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\alpha}\log|\xi|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\xi|}\frac{d}{d\alpha}|\xi|=\frac{\Re\xi\frac{d}{d\alpha}R+\Im\xi\frac{d}{d\alpha}\Im\xi}{\Re\xi^{2}+\Im\xi^{2}}=\frac{(\Re\xi\nabla\Re\xi+\Im\xi\nabla\Im\xi)\cdot(\omega_{2}-\omega_{1})}{\Re\xi^{2}+\Im\xi^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\alpha}\arg(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\Re\xi\nabla\Im\xi-\Im\xi\nabla\Re\xi)\cdot(\omega_{2}-\omega_{1})}{\Re\xi^{2}+\Im\xi^{2}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\alpha}\log|\xi|+i\frac{d}{d\alpha}\arg(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\Re\xi\nabla\Re\xi+\Im\xi\nabla\Im\xi+i\Re\xi\nabla\Im\xi-i\Im\xi\nabla\Re\xi)\cdot(\omega_{2}-\omega_{1})}{\Re\xi^{2}+\Im\xi^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{((\nabla\Re\xi+i\nabla\Im\xi)(\Re\xi-i\Im\xi))\cdot(\omega_{2}-\omega_{1})}{R^{2}+\Im\xi^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nabla\Re\xi+i\nabla\Im\xi}{\Re\xi+i\Im\xi}\cdot(\omega_{2}-\omega_{1})=\frac{\nabla\xi}{\xi}\cdot(\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}).

Now, integrating both sides of (
22
) yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(1)-\mathcal{L}(0)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{d\alpha}\mathcal{L}(\alpha)d\alpha=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\nabla\xi(\gamma(\alpha))}{\xi(\gamma(\alpha))}\cdot(\omega_{2}-\omega_{1})d\alpha,

and so the final result follows by exponentiating both sides of the previous display.

B.1.2
Sub-Gaussian processes

Definition 25
(Sub-Gaussian process).

A stochastic process
\{X(t):t\in D\}
whose sample paths lie almost surely in the Sobolev space
H^{s}(D)
is said to be sub-Gaussian if there exists a positive constant
K
such that, for every bounded linear functional
L:H^{s}(D)\to\mathbb{R}
with
\|L\|\leq 1
, the scalar random variable
L(X)
is
K
sub-Gaussian. In other words, the Orlicz norm satisfies

\|L(X)\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\inf\left\{m>0:\mathbb{E}\left[e^{L(X)^{2}/m^{2}}\right]\leq 2\right\}\leq K.

Lemma 26
(
y
is sub-Gaussian).

Under Assumption
4
, the process
y(t)
defined in (
2
) is sub-Gaussian.

Proof.

For any bounded linear functional
L:H^{s}(D)\to\mathbb{R},
we have that

\displaystyle|L(f(\cdot-\zeta))|\leq\|L\|\|f(\cdot-\zeta)\|_{H^{s}(D)}=\|L\|\|f\|_{H^{s}(D)}<\infty,

where the last equality holds since the Sobolev norm is invariant under shifts, since both signal and the shifted signal are supported on
D
. It follows that, for
\|L\|\leq 1,

|L(f(\cdot-\zeta))|
is bounded by a constant independent of
L
and is therefore sub-Gaussian. Further, since
\eta
is a Gaussian process supported on
H^{s}(D)
,
s>d/2,
it is sub-Gaussian. Therefore,

is a sum of two sub-Gaussian processes and so is itself a sub-Gaussian process.

B.1.3
Properties of Fourier transformed processes

In this section, we recall some properties of the Fourier transform of a stochastic process. Throughout, we consider a generic separable sub-Gaussian process
\{X(t):t\in D\}
supported on
H^{s}(D)
with
s>d/2
,
and
D=[-1,1]^{d},
with mean function, autocorrelation function, and covariance functions
m_{X}(t)
,
r_{X}(t,t^{\prime}),
and
k_{X}(t,t^{\prime})
respectively. The Fourier transformed process is then:

\displaystyle X^{\text{ft}}(\omega)=\int_{D}X(t)e^{-i\omega\cdot t}dt,\qquad\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The following lemma shows that the mean function of the transformed process is the Fourier transform of the mean function of the original process. Similarly, the autocorrelation and covariance functions are the Fourier transforms of the autocorrelation and covariance functions of the original process up to a flip in sign of the second argument.

Lemma 27
(Properties of Fourier transformed process).

For
\omega,\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Fourier transformed process
\{X^{\text{ft}}(\omega):\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
has the following properties:

(a)

Mean function:
m_{X^{\text{ft}}}(\omega)=m^{\text{ft}}_{X}(\omega);

(b)

Autocorrelation function:
r_{X^{\text{ft}}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=r^{\text{ft}}_{X}(\omega_{1},-\omega_{2});

(c)

Covariance function:
k_{X^{\text{ft}}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=k^{\text{ft}}_{X}(\omega_{1},-\omega_{2}).

Proof.

For the mean, we have

\displaystyle m_{X^{\text{ft}}}(\omega):=\mathbb{E}[X^{\text{ft}}(\omega)]=\int_{D}\mathbb{E}[X(t)]e^{-i\omega t}dt=\int_{D}m_{X}(t)e^{-i\omega t}dt=m^{\text{ft}}_{X}(\omega).

The second equality is justified by Fubini’s theorem. To verify this, note that the Sobolev embedding theorem guarantees that all point evaluation functionals have unit norm when
s>d/2
. Since
X
takes values in
H^{s}(D)
, we can express
X(t)
as
\mathcal{E}_{t}(X),
where
\mathcal{E}_{t}
is the evaluation functional at
t
. The sub-Gaussian property of the process
Z
then immediately implies that each
X(t)
is a sub-Gaussian random variable i.e. that
\|X(t)\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K.

This implies (see e.g.
[
57
, Proposition 2.5.2]
) that for any
p,

\|X(t)\|_{L_{p}}\lesssim c_{p}K
for a constant
c_{p}
depending only on
p
. In particular, for
p=1,
we have
\mathbb{E}|X(t)|\leq c_{1}K.
Since
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact, we have

\displaystyle\int_{D}\mathbb{E}|X(t)|dt\leq(\sup_{t\in D}\mathbb{E}|X(t)|)\text{vol}(D)<\infty.

For the autocorrelation, we have

\displaystyle r_{X^{\text{ft}}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X^{\text{ft}}(\omega_{1})\overline{X^{\text{ft}}(\omega_{2})}]=\iint_{D\times D}\mathbb{E}[X(t)X(t^{\prime})]e^{-i\omega_{1}t}e^{i\omega_{2}t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime}dt

\displaystyle=\iint_{D\times D}r_{X}(t,t^{\prime})e^{-i\omega_{1}t}e^{i\omega_{2}t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime}dt=r^{\text{ft}}_{X}(\omega_{1},-\omega_{2}),

where the second equality again follows by Fubini since

\displaystyle\iint_{D\times D}\mathbb{E}|X(t)X(t^{\prime})|dt^{\prime}dt

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{D\times D}\|X(t)\|_{L_{2}}\|X(t^{\prime})\|_{L_{2}}dt^{\prime}dt

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}^{2}\sup_{t\in D}\|X(t)\|^{2}_{\psi_{2}}(\text{vol}(D))^{2}<\infty,

where the first inequality holds by Cauchy-Schwarz, and the second by properties of sub-Gaussian variables. The proof for the covariance function follows similarly and is omitted for brevity.

B.1.4
Autocorrelation estimation for sub-Gaussian processes

Suppose now that we have access to
N
i.i.d. copies of
X(\cdot),
denoted
X_{1}(\cdot),\dots,X_{N}(\cdot)
, and that we wish to estimate the autocorrelation function
r_{X}.
The natural estimator is the sample autocorrelation function

\displaystyle\widehat{r}_{X}(t,t^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}X_{n}(t)X_{n}(t^{\prime}).

We then have the following high-probability supremum-norm bound.

Lemma 28
(Concentration of

There exist positive universal constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that, for any

(23)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma(X):=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in D}X(t)]}{\|r_{X}\|_{\infty}^{1/2}},\qquad\|r_{X}\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{t\in D}r_{X}(t,t).

First, we deal with the centered case. We write the quantity of interest as a product empirical process indexed by the class of linear evaluation functionals:

\displaystyle\sup_{s,t\in D}|\widehat{r}_{X}(t,t^{\prime})-r_{X}(t,t^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=\sup_{s,t\in D}\left\lvert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{n}X_{n}(t)X_{n}(t^{\prime})-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}(t)X_{n}(t^{\prime})]\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{f,g\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{n}f(X_{n})g(X_{n})-\mathbb{E}[f(X)g(X)]\right\rvert,

(24)

where

is the family of evaluation functionals. By Theorem
[
43
, Theorem 1.13]
, with probability at least
1-c_{1}e^{-c_{2}\tau}
, (

) is bounded above by

\displaystyle d^{2}_{\psi_{2}}(\mathcal{F})\left(\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{N}}\lor\frac{\tau}{N}\lor\frac{\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F},\psi_{2})}{\sqrt{N}d_{\psi_{2}}(\mathcal{F})}\lor\frac{\gamma^{2}_{2}(\mathcal{F},\psi_{2})}{Nd^{2}_{\psi_{2}}(\mathcal{F})}\right),

where

denotes Talagrand’s generic complexity

and we write

which completes the proof.

In the non-centered case, write

\displaystyle f(X_{n})g(X_{n})

\displaystyle=\bar{f}(X_{n})\bar{g}(X_{n})+\bar{g}(X_{n})\mathbb{E}f(X_{n})+\bar{f}(X_{n})\mathbb{E}g(X_{n})+\mathbb{E}f(X_{n})\mathbb{E}g(X_{n}).

By the triangle inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{f,g\in\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(X_{n})g(X_{n})-\mathbb{E}[f(X_{n})g(X_{n})]\right\rvert\leq\sup_{f,g\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bar{f}(X_{n})\bar{g}(X_{n})-\mathbb{E}[\bar{f}(X_{n})\bar{g}(X_{n})]\right\rvert

Combining this with

yields that with probability at least

\textbf{(II)}\lesssim d_{\psi_{2}}(\mathcal{F})\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F},\psi_{2})/\sqrt{N}.

Suppose instead that we were given the Fourier transformed observations,

B.3
Kernels satisfying Assumption

Here, we give a simple example of a class of kernels satisfying Assumption

. As is standard (see e.g.
[
39
]
for the univariate case), we specify the Fourier transform of the kernel. Let

be the Fourier transform of a univariate kernel that is even

[-1,1]

(p+2)

\displaystyle(K^{\text{ft}}_{0})^{(\ell)}(0)=\begin{cases}1\qquad\text{if}\qquad\ell=0,\\
0\qquad\text{if}\qquad\ell=1,\dots,p-1,\end{cases}

where
(K^{\text{ft}}_{0})^{(\ell)}
denotes the
\ell

K_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\omega t}K^{\text{ft}}(\omega)d\omega,
and define the multivariate kernel
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as the tensor product kernel with components

that is,
K(t)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}K_{0}(t_{j}).
As we will show,

satisfies Assumption
8
. Standard choices for

are

\displaystyle K^{\text{ft}}_{0}(\omega)=\begin{cases}1\qquad&\text{if }\qquad|\omega|\leq c_{0},\\

which is commonly referred to as the flat-top kernel. Now, we prove that

(i)-(v).

(i)

By the Fourier inversion theorem,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{0}(t_{j})dt_{j}=K^{\text{ft}}_{0}(0)=1.
Therefore

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(t)dt=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{0}(t_{1})dt_{1}\right)^{d}=1.

(ii)

For any multi-index
\nu
with
1\leq|\nu|\leq p-1,
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}t^{\nu}K(t)dt=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}}t_{j}^{\nu_{j}}K_{0}(t_{j})dt_{j},

and so the assumption holds if at least one of the univariate moments is zero, i.e. if
\int_{\mathbb{R}}t_{j}^{\nu_{j}}K_{0}(t_{j})dt_{j}=0
for some

1\leq\nu_{j}\leq p-1.
Note that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}t_{j}^{\nu_{j}}K_{0}(t_{j})dt_{j}=i^{-v_{j}}(K^{\text{ft}}_{0})^{(v_{j})}(0)=0

(iii)

By

|K_{0}(t_{j})|=o(|t_{j}|^{-p-2})

|t_{j}|\to\infty.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|t^{\nu}K(t)|dt=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|t_{j}^{\nu_{j}}||K_{0}(t_{j})|dt_{j}<\infty,

since each

(iv)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|t\|^{p}_{2}|K(t)|dt

|P^{\prime}(\omega_{5})|=|P(\widehat{\xi}_{i})-P(\xi_{i})|/|\widehat{\xi}_{i}-\xi_{i}|
. But
P(\xi_{i})=0
, so by Lemma
30
there exists a constant
C>0
such that

|P(\widehat{\xi}_{i})|=|P^{\prime}(\omega^{*})|\cdot|\widehat{\xi}_{i}-\xi_{i}|\leq\frac{C}{h^{2\beta}\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}}\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}\rho_{N}={C\cdot h^{-2\beta}}\rho_{N}.

Finally,
\widehat{P}(\widehat{\xi}_{i})<P(\widehat{\xi}_{i})+\rho_{N}<({h^{-2\beta}C}+1)\rho_{N}
. Since by (

, it must be that

.

(VI)
We conclude by defining
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{T}}:=\{\widehat{\xi}\in Z_{\widehat{P}^{\prime}}\,:\,\widehat{P}(\xi)<{\mathcal{T}}\}
. We began by showing that for any element
\xi\in Z_{P}
there is a unique element
\widehat{\xi}\in Z_{\widehat{P}^{\prime}}
such that
|\xi-\widehat{\xi}|\lesssim\delta
, and now we have shown that every element of
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{T}}
is in
\cup_{\xi_{i}\in Z_{P}}(\xi_{i}-\check{\varepsilon},\xi_{i}+\check{\varepsilon})
and thus is
\delta
away from a unique element of

and the lemma is proved.
