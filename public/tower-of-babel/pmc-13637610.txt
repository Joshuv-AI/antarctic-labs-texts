An alternate mechanism for the emergence of localized oscillatory patterns in networked reaction–diffusion systems
Authors: Zhi-Chao Xue, Xiaofeng Luo, Li Li, Yongli Song, Stefano Boccaletti, Charo I del Genio, Yicheng Zhang, Gui-Quan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLocalized oscillatory patterns, confined to finite spatial domains or subsets of nodes in a network, have been observed in cortical neurons, metacommunity ecosystems, and chemical experiments.Previous studies have shown that wave bifurcations (oscillatory Turing instabilities) in undirected networked reaction–diffusion systems can induce such localized oscillations. Here, we propose an alternative mechanism for their emergence, namely Hopf-type instabilities acting on a subcritical Turing branch. Unlike wave bifurcations, this mechanism does not require interactions amongst at least three components. We further demonstrate its robustness in both the Brusselator and FitzHugh–Nagumo models in undirected Barabási–Albert, Erd˝os–Rényi, and Watts–Strogatz networks. This work extends the universality of such patterns in networked systems and provides a theoretical foundation for the control of collective dynamical phenomena in complex systems.Keywords: complex network, localized oscillatory patterns, networked system, subcritical branch

INTRODUCTION

The formation of spatiotemporal patterns is a central problem in the study of complex systems and nonlinear dynamics [1–3]. The idea of using reaction–diffusion systems to explain pattern formation was first introduced by Turing in 1952, providing a prototypical framework for diffusion-driven instabilities [4]. Although the theory was initially developed from a purely theoretical perspective, its validity was not experimentally confirmed until the early 1990s, when Castets et al. first observed stationary chemical patterns [5], followed by the experimental realization of Turing patterns in the chlorite–iodide–malonic acid reaction system [6,7]. These pioneering experiments established diffusion-driven instability as a general mechanism for spontaneous pattern formation and stimulated extensive research across disciplines, such as ecology [8] and biology [9]. Within this classical reaction–diffusion framework, Turing instabilities produce stationary spatial structures, whereas Hopf instabilities give rise to temporal oscillations. Together, they underpin a broad spectrum of spatiotemporal behaviors and provide the theoretical foundation for understanding self-organization in complex systems.

In many real-world systems, however, spatial organization is inherently discrete and heterogeneous rather than continuous [10]. Representative examples include metapopulation ecosystems, where habitats are represented as discrete patches connected by migration pathways [11–15], and epidemic spreading across transportation or mobility networks, where long-range movement occurs through heterogeneous connections [16–18]. These systems motivate a network-based representation of space, in which nodes correspond to spatial units and links describe migration or mobility pathways. From this perspective, networked reaction–diffusion models provide a natural complement to classical continuous reaction–diffusion systems, extending the study of pattern formation to spatially discrete domains while retaining the same reaction–diffusion principles.

Over the past decade, advances in network science have revealed that network topology plays a fundamental role in shaping dynamical processes across diverse complex systems. In biological and ecological networks, transient amplification has been shown to provide a more informative predictor of extinction risk than asymptotic stability [19]. More broadly, structural heterogeneity, clustering, and temporal interactions have been identified as key determinants of collective behavior, evolutionary dynamics, and network controllability [20–25]. Recent studies in network neurodynamics further demonstrate that the interplay between network architecture and nonlinear dynamics governs transitions among multiple neural states through attractor-based mechanisms [26,27]. These findings underline the distinct advantages of complex networks in capturing structural heterogeneity and temporal variability. Consequently, reaction–diffusion systems have been extended to complex network topologies [10,28–34], highlighting the crucial role of network structure in pattern formation.

Notably, globally coherent states are not the only instances of spatiotemporal patterns that can emerge in complex systems. In fact, localized oscillatory patterns, in which temporal oscillations are confined to a subset of nodes or regions, are a somewhat common occurrence, having been observed in a large number of situations, including granular media, physical reaction–diffusion systems, neuronal networks, and metapopulation systems [35–46].

In continuous media, localized patterns with an oscillatory background have been widely studied, and are generally interpreted as the result of Turing–Hopf interactions. Nonetheless, only a few works have considered the emergence of patterns involving only a subset of nodes in networked reaction–diffusion systems. The mechanism suggested to cause the phenomenon is that of wave bifurcations (oscillatory Turing instabilities) [47], which, however, requires at least three interacting components, similarly to what happens in continuous systems [4,48,49]. This fundamental requirement restricts the applicability of the oscillatory Turing framework to systems with three or more dynamical variables, leaving the origin of localized oscillatory patterns in the broad class of two-component reaction–diffusion systems largely unexplored.

However, in many real-world systems, the number of dynamical components is intrinsically constrained by the underlying biological or physical mechanisms. For example, in cortical neuroscience, the interactions between excitatory and inhibitory neuronal populations constitute the minimal framework for describing large-scale brain dynamics [50]. Likewise, in epidemiology, the spread of infectious diseases without permanent immunity is naturally captured by the two-component Susceptible–Infected–Susceptible model [51]. In such systems, introducing additional dynamical variables is often neither biologically meaningful nor physically justified. These considerations motivate a fundamental question: can localized oscillatory patterns arise in networked reaction–diffusion systems with only two interacting components? Addressing this question would extend the applicability of localized oscillation theory while preserving the mechanistic consistency and interpretability of low-dimensional models.

Here, we propose an alternative mechanism for the occurrence of localized oscillatory patterns in two-dimensional reaction–diffusion systems on undirected networks. Specifically, we show that such states can be generated by Hopf-type instabilities acting on a subcritical Turing branch. This mechanism overcomes the component-number limitation inherent to traditional oscillatory Turing instability theory. Moreover, by applying our approach to different systems across three types of networks, we show the consistent emergence of these localized oscillatory patterns, highlighting their universality.

Further increasing r leads to a Turing bifurcation point (TBP1) at , where a subcritical Turing branch composed of numerous stationary patterns appears. The transition from HB1 to TBP1 marks a crucial regime shift where the system progresses from purely temporal oscillations toward space-dependent, diffusion-driven instabilities. One of the corresponding representative states is shown in Fig. 2c with R=0. As r varies along this branch, it undergoes a sequence of fold points, forming a classical snaking structure and hysteresis loops [56–58]. Note that the existence of this structure requires that the Turing bifurcation is subcritical.

As the parameter r now enters the Hopf instability region illustrated in Fig. 1, we detect a series of Hopf bifurcation points (SHB1–SHB6) along the subcritical Turing branch, marking the loss of stability of the stationary Turing patterns. These bifurcations arise from the interaction between the underlying Turing pattern and the Hopf instability, causing the originally stationary spatial patterns to undergo temporal oscillations. Consequently, distinct spatiotemporal states emerge from these bifurcation points. The first four of them are global oscillatory patterns, with one example shown in Fig. 2d with (R=1). In these states, all the nodes oscillate with similar periods, while retaining a spatially heterogeneous distribution. In contrast, the last two patterns are localized oscillatory patterns, of which one example is illustrated in Fig. 2e. In these states, only a small subset of nodes, such as nodes 21 and 28, sustain periodic oscillations, while most nodes exhibit only weak fluctuations or remain nearly stationary with (0< R=0.06667< 1).

To elucidate the relation between the stationary patterns along the snaking structure and the spatiotemporal patterns on the new branches reported in Fig. 2, first consider the steady-state patterns near the Hopf bifurcation points SHB1–SHB6 (Fig. 3a1–f1). It indicates that, as the number of differentiated nodes increases, the pattern gradually evolves toward a fully developed Turing pattern, similarly to the successive addition of wavelengths in continuous systems, eventually leading to a periodic state [56]. Specifically, as the solution branch traverses successive left folds of the snaking structure, one additional differentiated node is incorporated at each left fold. The parameter interval enclosed by each pair of adjacent fold points defines the existence range of the corresponding localized state. Also, the spatiotemporal patterns inherit the spatial structure of their steady-state counterparts, but acquire oscillatory dynamics due to the Hopf instability. For the first four patterns, all nodes, including the few differentiated ones, exhibit strong, synchronized oscillations. Conversely, as the last two patterns approach a fully developed Turing state, oscillations persist only in a very small subset of nodes, such as node 20 alone, thereby producing localized oscillatory patterns.

Mechanism validation

To verify that the phenomena described above and observed on BA networks are not specific to this network topology, we study the Brusselator also on Erdős–Rényi random graphs (ER) [61] and Watts–Strogatz small-world networks (WS) [62]. In both cases, we build networks of 30 nodes. For ER graphs, we use connection probability 0.3; for the WS networks, we connect each node to its 8 nearest neighbors, and rewire with probability 0.3. All other parameters are chosen consistently with the results shown above and reported in Fig. 2.

The results, illustrated in Fig. 4, show that varying the network structure induces no significant change in the observed bifurcation behavior, highlighting its robustness across types of network. Specifically, we observe that, as the control parameter r is increased, the system undergoes a Hopf bifurcation followed by a Turing one. Along the snaking structure evolving from the Turing bifurcation, additional Hopf bifurcations emerge, giving rise to global oscillatory patterns as well as localized oscillatory patterns. Thus, in both ER graphs (Fig. 4a) and WS networks (Fig. 4b), the branch structures exhibit essentially the same behavior as those obtained on BA networks. The details of the spatiotemporal patterns associated with these branches are also consistent with those shown in Fig. 2, and are therefore not displayed again here.

We further confirm that this phenomenology persists in larger networks, as illustrated in Fig. 5 for BA, ER, and WS networks with N=1000. In fact, the emergence of localized oscillatory patterns is most pronounced in the BA network, where oscillatory nodes are fewer but display larger amplitudes due to pronounced degree heterogeneity. In contrast, the ER and WS networks, characterized by relatively uniform degree distributions, exhibit a more homogeneous distribution of oscillatory nodes. One possible explanation for this phenomenon is that high-degree nodes experience stronger diffusive coupling, which stabilizes them near the steady state, whereas weakly connected, low-degree nodes are more susceptible to Hopf oscillations, leading to localized oscillatory patterns. Moreover, Material S3 presents results for BA networks with different sizes (N=100, 500, and 5000), all of which exhibit the same qualitative localized oscillatory structures, demonstrating that the underlying mechanism is robust with respect to network size.

CONCLUSIONS

In this work, we propose an alternative mechanism for the emergence of localized oscillatory patterns in networked two-dimensional reaction–diffusion systems, namely Hopf-type instabilities acting on a subcritical Turing branch. This mechanism overcomes the restriction of the classical wave bifurcation (oscillatory Turing instability), which requires at least three interacting components. Combining normal-form analysis with numerical continuation, we uncover the underlying branch structure and reveal the transition from global oscillations to localized patterns as system parameters vary. Moreover, we demonstrate that the emergence of such patterns is robust across different network sizes and types, as well as different reaction–diffusion systems.

Notably, the normal form coefficients we generate are more than just determinants of the bifurcation type. Rather, they are directly related to the topological heterogeneity of the network via the spectral decomposition inherent in our analysis. The critical cubic coefficient , which dictates subcriticality (), explicitly incorporates the fourth spectral moment via the term in Equation (S38). On a 1000-node regular lattice (with 8 nearest neighbors), the delocalized eigenvectors () yield and a vanishingly small . Conversely, topological heterogeneity induces severe eigenvector localization, concentrating the critical mode amplitude () onto a small active subset of K nodes, while leaving the remaining nodes near zero. Under this localized state, the fourth moment scales as , which remains non-vanishingly large in the thermodynamic limit. For the three N=1000 network topologies shown in Fig. 5, our calculations yield (BA), 0.7482 (ER), and 0.5650 (WS), while takes the values -0.6116, 0.7939, and -0.6244, respectively. This quantitative jump, which amplifies by more than 300 times compared with the regular lattice, drastically enhances the direct cubic projection term . Such a substantial order-of-magnitude discrepancy in the spectral moments is sufficient to reverse the sign of . The emergence of localized oscillatory patterns is therefore not an intrinsic property of the model itself, but rather a direct consequence of the interplay between nonlinear dynamics and the spectral signature of topological heterogeneity.

Also, the emergence of localized oscillatory patterns marks a transition from global synchrony [65,66] to asynchronous dynamics amongst network nodes, indicating that a single system can intrinsically support a diversity of spatiotemporal states, including complete synchrony, strong disorder and partial synchrony. From an applied perspective, this intrinsic multistability may offer a theoretical basis for future strategies to sustain healthy synchrony or suppress pathological synchronization, such as cognitive performance [67] and abnormal neural synchrony in Parkinson’s disease and epilepsy [68]. Moreover, our findings may provide new perspectives for understanding localized spreading phenomena in heterogeneous networked systems. For example, in epidemiological networks, the combined effects of topological heterogeneity, eigenvector localization, and uneven intervention intensity may naturally induce localized outbreak dynamics confined to specific regions or subnetworks [69]. Therefore, clarifying the formation mechanisms and bifurcation structures of localized steady and oscillatory patterns may help reveal the dynamical origin of recurrent regional outbreaks, and further provide theoretical guidance for the development of targeted intervention and control strategies in realistic spreading systems.
