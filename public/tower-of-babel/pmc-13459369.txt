Prevalence, knowledge of long-term effects, and associated factors of alcohol consumption among adults in the Hohoe Municipality, Volta Region - Ghana
Authors: Abraham Zormelo, Reginald Enam Gibril Attidigah, Joshua Elorm Amehavi, Daniel Kwabla Ahiagbede, Wisdom Kwami Takramah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlcohol consumption is a significant and growing public health concern in Ghana, where it contributes to rising rates of non-communicable diseases, social dysfunction, and economic burden. Despite its impact, municipality-level data on alcohol use among the general adult population remains scarce, creating an evidence gap that limits the development of targeted, context-specific interventions. Prior studies in Hohoe Municipality have been restricted to youth and tertiary student populations, leaving the broader adult population unexamined.AimThis study aimed to determine the prevalence of current and lifetime alcohol consumption, assess knowledge of the long-term health effects of alcohol, and identify sociodemographic and behavioral factors associated with alcohol consumption among adults in Hohoe Municipality, Volta Region, Ghana.MethodA quantitative cross-sectional design was used among 318 adults aged 18 years and above in Hohoe Municipality. Participants were recruited from residential households and alcohol-selling points in Hohoe Municipality using stratified random sampling and a balloting procedure. A structured questionnaire was administered face-to-face by trained interviewers. Current alcohol consumption was defined as consuming at least one standard drink in the 30 days preceding the survey. Knowledge of the long-term effects of alcohol was assessed, scored dichotomously and summed into a composite score, with the sample median used to classify participants into high and low knowledge groups Data were analyzed using Stata Version 17. Descriptive statistics summarized the sample, and chi-square tests examined bivariate associations. Binary and multivariable logistic regression were used for inferential analysis, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals reported.ResultsOf the 318 participants, 122 (38.4%) were current alcohol consumers; those who had consumed alcohol in the past 30 days while an additional 48 (15.1%) were former consumers. Combined, 170 participants (53.5%, 95% CI: 48.0–59.0%) reported any lifetime alcohol exposure, representing the study’s overall lifetime prevalence. Among the 56.2% of male participants and 50.6% of female participants who were consumers, sex was not a statistically significant predictor of consumption (p = 0.323). Local gin (akpeteshie) was the most consumed beverage (47.6%). Knowledge of the long-term effects of alcohol was low, as only 53.5% of respondents demonstrated high knowledge of chronic consequences, despite 85.6% acknowledging general health harm. Accessibility (55.3%) and peer influence (18.9%) were the primary self-reported drivers of consumption. Ewe ethnicity (aOR = 5.6; 95% CI: 1.97–15.95; p = 0.001), Guan ethnicity (aOR = 3.9; 95% CI: 1.06–14.55; p = 0.039), and Islamic religious affiliation (aOR = 0.22; 95% CI: 0.05–0.90; p = 0.036) were the significant independent predictors of alcohol consumption. Ethnicity likely reflects the influence of cultural norms, community-level alcohol availability, and social practices rather than any inherent characteristic.ConclusionAlcohol consumption is prevalent among adults in Hohoe Municipality, with over half reporting lifetime exposure and more than a third identified as current consumers. Knowledge of specific long-term health risks remains limited, particularly awareness of recommended drinking limits. Ethnicity and religion are the strongest independent predictors of consumption, pointing to the central role of sociocultural context in shaping drinking behavior. These findings call for multi-level, culturally sensitive public health responses combining targeted health education, regulatory action on alcohol accessibility, and engagement with community and faith-based structures to reduce alcohol-related harm and support Ghana’s national NCD prevention agenda.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28072-y.Keywords: Alcohol consumption, Prevalence, Knowledge, Long-term effects, Associated factors, Hohoe Municipality, Volta Region, Ghana

Introduction

Alcohol consumption is deeply embedded in cultural, religious, and social practices worldwide and remains a major public health concern. It is a leading behavioral risk factor affecting individuals across all demographic groups. The World Health Organization (WHO) has stated that there is no completely safe level of alcohol consumption in relation to cancer risk, given its psychoactive and dependence-producing properties and its association with a wide range of adverse health outcomes [1]. Globally, harmful alcohol use contributes substantially to morbidity and mortality, accounting for a significant proportion of the disease burden and imposing considerable social and economic costs [1].

The most recent WHO Global Status Report on Alcohol and Health estimates that approximately 32.5% of the global population equating to 2.4 billion people currently consumes alcohol, with higher prevalence among men (39%) than women (25%) [20]. Alcohol-related causes account for 2.2% of deaths among females and 6.8% among males annually [20]. Global per capita consumption was estimated at 6.2 L of pure alcohol among individuals aged 15 years and above, with 26% attributed to unrecorded consumption [4]. While high-income countries such as the United States, Canada, and those in Western Europe report high prevalence of alcohol use, recent trends indicate a gradual decline attributable to effective policy interventions, increased public awareness, and stronger regulatory measures [5–7]. In contrast, many low- and middle-income countries are experiencing rising consumption, driven by rapid urbanization, economic transitions, and aggressive alcohol marketing [7].

In sub-Saharan Africa, alcohol consumption is characterized by significant variability across countries, with an average per capita consumption of 6.3 L; slightly below the global average yet frequently accompanied by hazardous drinking patterns such as binge drinking and consumption of unregulated local brews [8]. These patterns pose serious public health challenges, as alcohol is causally linked to over 200 disease conditions, including liver cirrhosis, cancers, cardiovascular diseases, mental health disorders, and infectious diseases such as tuberculosis and HIV [9, 10]. Furthermore, alcohol contributes substantially to injuries, including road traffic accidents, violence, and suicide, particularly among younger populations [11]. It remains a leading risk factor for premature mortality, especially among individuals aged 20–39 years, accounting for approximately 13.5% of deaths in this age group [4, 18].

In Ghana, alcohol consumption is an emerging public health concern, reflecting broader trends observed in low- and middle-income settings. Evidence suggests increasing consumption across different population groups, influenced by sociocultural norms, the availability of locally brewed beverages such as akpeteshie, and changing socioeconomic condition [14]. Existing studies, however, have largely focused on specific subpopulations. A national-level analysis reported a lifetime prevalence of 23% among adults aged 50 years and above [17], while studies among youths and tertiary students in Hohoe Municipality reported prevalence rates of 43% and 39.5%, respectively [1, 14]. Although these findings provide valuable insights, their limited scope means they may not adequately represent alcohol consumption patterns in the general adult population. These subpopulation-specific findings, while informative, underscore the urgent need for a comprehensive study targeting the broader adult population in Hohoe Municipality, where alcohol consumption patterns, associated knowledge, and contributing factors remain poorly characterised at a community-wide level. More recent evidence continues to reinforce this concern; a 2021 cross-sectional study by Aboagye et al. among tertiary students in Hohoe Municipality reported a current use prevalence of 49.1%, with peer influence and academic stress identified as key contributing factors, further highlighting the need for updated, population-wide data [1].

Hohoe Municipality warrants particular attention as a study setting for several reasons beyond the absence of population-level data. The municipality is one of the recognized production area for akpeteshie, the locally distilled gin that is both highly affordable and deeply embedded in the ceremonial and social life of the predominantly Ewe population, including funerals, festivals, and traditional rites. These structural and cultural conditions create an environment in which alcohol is unusually accessible and socially normalized, making Hohoe an epidemiologically important setting for understanding the drivers and burden of alcohol use at the municipal level.

Beyond prevalence, understanding knowledge of the long-term health effects of alcohol is important because the relationship between awareness and behavior is not straightforward. Studying prevalence and knowledge together allows us to test whether high consumption coexists with poor knowledge, a pattern that has direct implications for how interventions should be designed. If knowledge deficits explain high consumption, then health education is the primary lever; if knowledge and consumption co-occur, structural and normative approaches must complement educational efforts. Behavioral frameworks such as Social Cognitive Theory [5] guided variable selection in this study: individual-level variables (age, sex, knowledge) capture personal and cognitive determinants; interpersonal variables (marital status, peer influence) capture relational influences; and community and structural variables (ethnicity, religion, employment, accessibility of alcohol) capture the broader social and environmental context. Evidence from high-income countries suggests that general awareness of harm does not reliably translate into safer consumption for instance, awareness of alcohol’s link to cancer remains low even where general harm is widely acknowledged [14, 19]. This pattern is what the present study examines in Hohoe’s specific context and empirical evidence on population-level knowledge of long-term alcohol-related health risks in Ghana, particularly at the municipal level, also remains limited.

In Hohoe Municipality, available studies have predominantly examined alcohol use among specific groups, with little attention given to the general adult population and their awareness of long-term health consequences, a gap that hampers the design of effective, context-specific NCD prevention interventions. Alcohol consumption is further shaped by a complex interplay of individual and societal factors, including age, gender, socioeconomic status, cultural norms, peer influence, and the policy environment [3, 10], best understood within a socio-ecological framework that recognizes health behaviors as products of multiple levels of influence.

Given the increasing burden of alcohol-related harm and the limited population-wide data available for Hohoe Municipality, this study aims to assess the prevalence of current and lifetime alcohol consumption among adults, which is reported in the prevalence subsection of the results; knowledge of long-term health effects associated with alcohol use, presented in the knowledge subsection; and identify sociodemographic and behavioral factors associated with alcohol consumption, examined through binary and multivariable logistic regression and reported in the associations subsection. A community-based random sample of 318 adults was recruited from residential households and alcohol-selling points within the municipal capital. The findings are intended to contribute to the development of targeted public health interventions, inform municipal-level health policies, and support Ghana’s National Policy for the Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases [3].

Materials and methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in Hohoe Municipality, one of the 261 Metropolitan, Municipal, and District Assemblies (MMDAs) in Ghana, situated within the Volta Region. Covering approximately 1,172 square kilometers, which constitute approximately 5.6% of the region’s land area, the municipality is centrally located at longitude 00 15’E to 00 45’E and latitude 6° 45’N to 7° 15’N, with Hohoe serving as the administrative capital. The municipality shares borders with Afadjato South District to the north, South Dayi District to the south, Ho Municipal and Ho West Districts to the west, and the Republic of Togo to the east (Fig. 1). According to the 2021 Population and Housing Census, the population was approximately 114,472, comprising 54,893 males and 59,579 females. The economy is predominantly agricultural, with crops such as cocoa, rice, plantain, and cassava cultivated by local farmers. The healthcare infrastructure includes a regional hospital, approximately fourteen health centers, and CHPS zones and compounds.

Study design

This research employed a quantitative, analytical cross-sectional design to assess the prevalence of alcohol consumption, knowledge of its long-term health effects, and associated factors among adults in Hohoe Municipality. Data were collected at a single point in time using Kobo Collect, a mobile data capture platform, enabling efficient and standardized recording of responses across all study sites. While this design is well-suited for estimating prevalence and identifying associations at a population level, it is inherently limited in its ability to establish causal relationships, as exposure and outcome were measured simultaneously. Additionally, as data were collected through face-to-face self-report interviews, the findings are subject to recall bias and social desirability bias as participants may have under-reported alcohol consumption or over-reported knowledge due to perceived social expectations. Findings should therefore be interpreted as associative rather than causal, and with these reporting limitations in mind.

Sampling was conducted within Hohoe, the municipal capital, using a simple random sampling technique. The municipality was stratified into residential communities and alcohol-selling points (e.g., bars, drinking spots, and liquor shops). Within each stratum, sampling units households and alcohol-selling points were enumerated using existing community maps and local authority registers. A proportional allocation approach was used to determine the number of participants drawn from each stratum: of the 318 participants, 212 (66.7%) were recruited from residential households and 106 (33.3%) from alcohol-selling points. This allocation was based on the relative population size of each stratum within the municipal capital, ensuring that neither habitual drinkers (likely over-represented at selling points) nor non-drinkers (likely over-represented in households) would dominate the sample, thereby reducing selection bias.

At each selected household or alcohol-selling point, all adults aged 18 years and above who had resided in the municipality for at least three months were considered eligible. One participant was selected per household to prevent within-household clustering effects. To randomly select this individual from among eligible household members, balloting was employed: slips of paper labelled “YES” or “NO” were prepared such that only one “YES” slip was included per household unit. Each eligible household member drew a slip, and the individual selecting “YES” was enrolled. At alcohol-selling points, where individual patrons rather than household units were the sampling unit, the same balloting procedure was applied in a ratio corresponding to the number of participants required from that location. This approach ensured that selection was random and independent of the interviewer’s discretion, supporting the reproducibility of the sampling process.

A “current alcohol consumer” was defined as any participant who reported consuming at least one standard drink of any alcoholic beverage in the last 30 days preceding the survey, consistent with the WHO definition of current drinking used in the Global Status Report on Alcohol and Health [18]. A “former consumer” was defined as a participant who reported having previously consumed alcohol but not in the past 30 days. Participants who had never consumed alcohol were classified as “non-consumers.” These definitions were operationalized through questionnaire items SB1 and SB1.1 respectively and are used consistently throughout this manuscript.

Data collection procedure and instrument validation

The data collection instrument was a structured questionnaire developed for this research in English and translated into a local dialect (Ewe, Twi) verbally. The questionnaire comprises four sections: socioeconomic and demographic factors, the prevalence of alcohol consumption, knowledge of the long-term effects of alcohol, and factors influencing alcohol use.

The questionnaire was developed drawing on previously validated instruments used in comparable alcohol consumption studies in Ghana and sub-Saharan Africa, including items framework and prior local studies [14]. Content validity was assessed through expert review by public health specialists and faculty members at the Fred N. Binka School of Public Health, who evaluated the relevance, clarity, and completeness of items.

Following expert review, the instrument was pretested in a community outside the main study area with 20 participants to assess clarity, flow, and cultural appropriateness. Feedback from the pretest was used to refine question wording and response options before the main data collection commenced.

Trained interviewers administered the questionnaire by reading questions aloud to participants who understood English but translated verbally to Ewe/Twi for other participants and recorded responses accordingly by selecting the suitable options. Pretesting was conducted in a community to refine the instrument before the main data collection. Data were collected electronically via Kobo Collect, with subsequent data management and analysis performed via Excel and STATA version 17.

Statistical analysis

The data were exported to STATA version 17 for cleaning and analysis. Data cleaning involved identifying and handling missing data, outliers, and inconsistencies through standard procedures including listwise deletion for variables with minimal missingness and standardization of categorical responses.

A composite score representing “knowledge of the long-term effects of alcohol” was constructed from five items in Section C of the questionnaire (SC1–SC5). These five items were selected to represent the key dimensions of long-term alcohol-related health knowledge identified in the literature: (SC1) awareness of recommended drinking limits; (SC2) recognition that alcohol adversely affects the body when abused; (SC3) general perception of alcohol consumption as risky; (SC4) awareness of specific alcohol-related diseases (stroke, cancer, heart disease); and (SC5) knowledge of the physical effects of alcohol on the body. While five items represent a relatively brief scale, each item was designed to tap a conceptually distinct and non-overlapping dimension of long-term health knowledge, ensuring content coverage without redundancy. This approach is consistent with similarly brief knowledge scales used in Ghanaian and sub-Saharan African health behavior studies [14]. Each item was scored dichotomously (1 = positive response; 0 = negative response or “don’t know”), and scores were summed to produce a total composite score ranging from 0 to 5. The median score of the sample was then used as the cut-off point to categorize participants into “low knowledge” (below the median) and “high knowledge” (at or above the median) groups. The median was preferred over an arbitrary threshold because it reflects the actual distribution of knowledge within this specific population, ensuring roughly equal group sizes and enhancing the statistical power of subsequent analyses.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, with frequency distributions for categorical variables. The outcome variable alcohol consumption (current/former consumer consumer vs. non-current consumer) was binary, making logistic regression the appropriate analytical method. Binary logistic regression was first performed to examine the unadjusted association between each independent variable and alcohol consumption. Variables significant at p < 0.20 in bivariate analysis, along with variables identified a priori as theoretically relevant based on the literature, were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. The following covariates were included in the adjusted model: age group, sex, ethnicity, religion, marital status, educational level, and employment status. This approach guarded against excluding potential confounders despite marginal bivariate significance. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with VIF > 10 considered indicative of problematic collinearity; no significant multicollinearity was detected. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test; a non-significant result (p > 0.05) was taken as indicating adequate model fit. Results are reported as crude odds ratios (cOR) and adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Health and Allied Sciences (UHAS-REC) in Ghana with Protocol Identification Number (ID: UHAS-REC A.4[083] 23–24). Permissions were also secured from local health authorities such as District Health Directorate and community elders after the study objectives were explained.

Human ethics and consent to participate

An informed consent form with a clear explanation of the procedure was read to participants before each interview. Before issuing the questionnaires, all respondents were presented with the option of declining to answer any of the questions. Furthermore, respondents were made aware of the fact that they could withdraw from the study at any point in time. Respondents’ safety was ensured, and confidentiality of responses was also ensured. Respondents indicated their consent by signing or thumbprinting the informed consent form. Confidentiality was maintained by anonymizing the data and securely storing all records in password-protected files accessible only to the principal investigator and authorized personnel. The study adhered to ethical standards to ensure participant safety and data privacy.

All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

The majority (53.8%, n = 171) were aged 30–59 years, followed by those aged 18–29 years (38.7%, n = 123) and those aged 60 years and above (7.6%, n = 24). Most participants identified as Ewe (75.8%, n = 241) and Christian (79.9%, n = 254). Regarding marital status, 42.5% (n = 135) were married and 33.7% (n = 107) were single. Association between demographic characteristics and prevalence of alcohol consumption

With respect to alcohol consumption by socio-demographic category, age was not a statistically significant predictor (p = 0.161), though participants aged 60 years and above showed the highest proportion of high-prevalence consumers (70.8%), compared to the 30–59 age group (53.8%) and the 18–29 age group (49.6%). Among all 318 participants, 162 (50.9%) were male and 156 (49.1%) were female. When examining prevalence of alcohol consumption within each sex, 91 of 162 males (56.2%) were consumers compared to 79 of 156 females (50.6%); however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.323). Notably, ethnicity and religion were the only socio-demographic variables with statistically significant associations with alcohol consumption (both p < 0.001). Among ethnic groups, the Ewe group had the highest proportion of high-prevalence consumers (61.4%), while the “Other” category had the lowest (12.9%). Among religious groups, Muslims had the lowest proportion of high-prevalence consumers (23.9%), while those practicing African Traditional Religion had 66.7%. Marital status (p = 0.161), employment status (p = 0.503), and educational level (p = 0.774) showed no statistically significant associations.

Prevalence of alcohol consumption

Three prevalence figures appear in this study, and each refers to a distinct measure of alcohol use. Of the 318 participants, 122 (38.4%, 95% CI: 33.2–43.8%) reported currently consuming alcohol at the time of the survey (Table 2, item SB1), defined as at least one standard drink in the 30 days preceding the interview. Among the 196 (61.6%) who reported not currently drinking, 48 (24.5%, 95% CI: 18.6–31.2%) reported having consumed alcohol previously, classified as former consumers. When current and former consumers are combined to capture any lifetime alcohol exposure, 170 of 318 participants (53.5%, 95% CI: 48.0–59.0%) had consumed alcohol at some point. This combined figure of 53.5% is used as the overall lifetime prevalence of alcohol use for this study, as it most comprehensively reflects the burden of alcohol exposure in the population (Fig. 2, N = 318). All subsequent knowledge and behavioral analyses apply to this group of N = 170. The majority of all respondents (82.4%, N = 318) expressed concern about safe drinking practices, while 17.7% reported no such concern (Fig. 3, N = 318).

Knowledge of the long-term effects of alcohol

Table 2 presents knowledge-related responses among the 170 participants with any alcohol exposure. A large majority (95.9%, n = 163) were unaware of the recommended drinking limits, indicating a substantial gap in knowledge about safe consumption thresholds. Despite this, 85.6% (n = 143) acknowledged that alcohol can adversely affect the human body when abused. Furthermore, 67.7% (n = 115) were aware that alcohol can cause serious health conditions such as stroke, cancer, and heart disease. When asked about their general perception of alcohol, 68.8% (n = 117) considered it risky, while 24.1% (n = 41) viewed it as beneficial, and 7.1% (n = 12) were unsure, highlighting mixed opinions on alcohol’s impact on health and well-being.

Regarding effects of alcohol use, (Fig. 5, N = 170) illustrates the perceived physical effects of alcohol on the human body. Drowsiness was the most widely recognized effect (91%), followed by slurred speech (88%), injuries such as falls and burns (65%), and chronic diseases including cancer, heart disease, stroke, and brain and liver damage (63%).

Perceived effects of alcohol on the human body (N = 170)

When the knowledge items were aggregated into a composite score (Figure 6, N=170), 53.5% (95% CI: 46.0–61.0%) of participants were classified as having high knowledge of the long-term effects of alcohol, while 46.5% (95% CI: 39.0–54.0%) were classified as having low knowledge. The composite score was derived from five questionnaire items (SC1–SC5) assessing awareness of recommended limits, knowledge that alcohol harms the body if abused, risk perception, knowledge of specific diseases, and awareness of acute physical effects. Each item was scored dichotomously (1=Yes; 0=No), and the median score served as the classification cut-off.

Factors associated with alcohol consumption

Table 3 presents the self-reported influences and motivations for alcohol use among the 170 participants. Easy accessibility of alcohol was the most frequently cited influence on drinking decisions (55.3%, n = 94), followed by peer influence (18.9%, n = 32), curiosity (14.2%, n = 24), other influences (6.5%, n = 11), and parental influence (5.3%, n = 9). Most participants (71.8%, n = 122) felt that alcohol satisfied their needs, while 25.3% (n = 43) disagreed. A majority (64.1%, n = 109) found alcohol to be affordable and easily accessible, and 62.9% (n = 107) reported feeling safe drinking alcohol.

Factors associated with alcohol consumption

Reasons for alcohol consumption

The primary reason for alcohol consumption among respondents is fun, with 49.4% of individuals citing fun as their main motivation, indicating that social enjoyment is the most common driver of drinking (Fig. 7, N = 170). Using alcohol as an appetizer is also a significant factor influencing 25.3% of the respondents, suggesting that it is often consumed in a context related to meals or increasing appetite. Stress release is another notable reason, with 12.9% of individuals drinking alcohol to cope with stress. A smaller portion of the population (9.4%) drinks for other reasons, such as medication and menstrual cramps. Finally, family problems are the least common reason, influencing only 2.9% of the respondents, indicating that using alcohol as a coping mechanism for family-related issues is relatively uncommon.

Participants aged 60 years and above showed a non-significant trend toward higher odds of consumption compared to the 18–29 reference group (cOR = 2.47, 95% CI: 0.96–6.37, p = 0.062). Sex was also non-significant (cOR = 1.25, 95% CI: 0.80–1.94, p = 0.323).

Compared to the Akan group (reference), Ewes had significantly higher odds of consuming alcohol in both the unadjusted model (cOR = 4.51, 95% CI: 1.71–11.85, p = 0.002) and after full adjustment (aOR = 5.6, 95% CI: 1.97–15.95, p = 0.001). Guans also showed significantly elevated odds in the adjusted model (aOR = 3.9, 95% CI: 1.06–14.55, p = 0.039). The “Other” ethnic group did not differ significantly from Akans in either model (cOR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.10–1.70, p = 0.225; aOR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.18–3.99, p = 0.849).

It should be noted that ethnicity in this context likely operates as a proxy for a constellation of cultural, geographic, and social exposures including ceremonial drinking norms, community-level alcohol availability, and shared social practices rather than as a biological or inherent characteristic.

Religion was equally a significant predictor. Compared to those practising African Traditional Religion (reference), Muslims had significantly lower odds of alcohol consumption in both the crude (cOR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.04–0.55, p = 0.004) and adjusted models (aOR = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.05–0.90, p = 0.036). Christians had lower odds than the reference group, but this was not statistically significant in either model (cOR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.22–2.04, p = 0.486; aOR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.16–1.69, p = 0.279).

Marital status, employment status, and educational level were not statistically significant predictors of alcohol consumption in either the crude or adjusted analyses (all p > 0.05).

Discussion

This discussion interprets the findings of the study on the prevalence, knowledge, and factors associated with alcohol consumption among adults in Hohoe Municipality, drawing on relevant local and international literature. The discussion is organized around four thematic areas: prevalence patterns, knowledge of long-term effects, the role of ethnicity and religion, and factors associated with consumption, followed by a statement of study limitations and policy implications. Data were collected from 318 respondents aged 18–70 years. The majority of the respondents were in the 30–59 years age group (53.8%) category, followed by those aged 18–29 years (38.7%), the youngest age group and 60 years and above, are the least represented, comprising (7.6%) of the sample, respectively. The gender distribution is nearly balanced; 50.9% of the males who participated in this study were slightly more common than 49.1% of the females because more men met at the drinking locations and were willing to openly talk about their drinking behavior. In terms of ethnicity, a significant majority belong to the Ewe ethnic group (75.8%) because Hohoe Municipality is largely made up of the Ewes, while smaller proportions belong to Akan (7.2%), Guan (7.2%), and other ethnic groups, such as Sisala, Hausa, Kotokoli and Dagomba (9.8%).

Prevalence of alcohol consumption

This study found that 53.5% of adults in Hohoe Municipality consumed alcohol or had any lifetime alcohol exposure (combining current and former consumers), with 38.4% reporting active current consumption. This prevalence is notably higher than figures reported in comparable Ghanaian studies, and the divergences are instructive. Osei-Bonsu et al. (2017) reported a prevalence of 43% among youth aged 15–35 years in Hohoe-Tokorni, while Tachi et al. (2020) reported a lifetime prevalence of 23% among adults aged 50 years and above [16]. The higher prevalence observed here is likely explained by methodological differences rather than a genuine secular increase in consumption: this study recruited from a broader adult age range (18–70 years) and from both residential households and alcohol selling points, capturing individuals who may not be reached in households. The elevated prevalence relative to older adult studies also suggests that alcohol consumption peaks in middle adulthood in this population, consistent with global epidemiological trends [18]. These divergences in sampling strategy should caution against direct numerical comparisons across studies.

The male predominance in alcohol use, though not statistically significant in this study (p = 0.323), is consistent with global patterns in which men consistently report higher rates of alcohol consumption than women [18]. The near-parity observed here may reflect the broader sampling strategy, which included alcohol-selling points where both men and women were captured, compared with studies relying solely on household sampling that may under-represent female drinkers. The high proportion of occasional drinkers (61.2%) among consumers suggests that heavy or dependent drinking may not yet be the dominant pattern, though even occasional drinking carries long-term health risks that warrant public health attention [4].

Knowledge of long-term effects of alcohol

While 85.6% of respondents acknowledged that alcohol is harmful when abused, only 53.5% demonstrated high knowledge of its specific long-term health consequences, and a striking 95.9% were unaware of recommended drinking limits. This distinction is important as general awareness of harm does not translate into actionable knowledge. The literature consistently shows that this awareness knowledge gap is a poor predictor of behavior change. Cotter et al. (2013), in a large Australian survey, found that fewer than 48% of adults knew alcohol was carcinogenic despite widespread awareness of its general harms. Similarly, the U.S. HINTS survey found that while over 79% recognized alcohol’s link to liver disease, fewer than 40% were aware of its carcinogenic potential [14]. These findings from high-income settings mirror what was observed in Hohoe, suggesting that the gap between general awareness and specific risk knowledge is a cross-cutting challenge, not one unique to low-resource contexts.

Critically, however, the relationship between knowledge and drinking behavior must not be oversimplified. The present study found that 53.5% of participants had high knowledge of long-term effects and yet 53.5% were also active or former consumers, indicating substantial co-occurrence of knowledge and consumption. This reflects the well-established health behavior literature which shows that knowledge alone is an insufficient driver of behavior change [15] which means that improving health is necessary but not sufficient when structural barriers such as alcohol affordability and social norms actively support consumption [21]. Socioecological models of health behavior emphasize that individual knowledge operates within a web of social norms, economic pressures, cultural practices, and environmental availability that can override cognitive awareness. In Hohoe Municipality, where alcohol is affordable, culturally embedded, and widely available, knowledge improvements alone are unlikely to yield meaningful reductions in consumption without corresponding structural and normative interventions.

The role of ethnicity and religion in alcohol consumption

The most substantively important finding of this study is that ethnicity and religion were the only statistically significant sociodemographic predictors of alcohol consumption. Ewes had over five times the odds of consuming alcohol compared to Akans (aOR = 5.6, 95% CI: 1.97–15.95, p = 0.001), and Guans had nearly four times the odds (aOR = 3.9, 95% CI: 1.06–14.55, p = 0.039). These associations should be interpreted with analytical caution. Ethnicity in this context is best understood as a proxy for a constellation of cultural, geographic, and social exposures that co-vary with ethnic identity including ceremonial norms around alcohol use, community-level availability of akpeteshie, shared social practices, and intergenerational transmission of drinking behavior rather than as an inherent or biological characteristic. Among Ewe communities, alcohol particularly akpeteshie plays a recognized role in communal life, including funerals, festivals, and customary rites. Within these settings, participation in communal drinking may carry social expectations, and abstention can sometimes be perceived as disengagement from community practices. The higher odds of consumption observed among Ewes in this study are consistent with these contextual patterns, as documented in the Ghanaian literature [1], and the present quantitative findings add epidemiological weight to those observations without implying that all Ewes drink or that drinking is culturally prescribed.

The finding that Muslims had significantly lower odds of alcohol consumption (aOR = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.05–0.90, p = 0.036) compared to African Traditional Religion practitioners is consistent with the well-documented role of Islamic prohibition (haram) on alcohol in structuring abstinence behaviors [2, 13]. This is a divergence from the pattern seen among Christians, who, despite being the majority group in the sample and in Ghana broadly, did not differ significantly from the African Traditional Religion reference group (aOR = 0.52, p = 0.279). This suggests that religious identification per se is less determinative than the specific doctrinal and community enforcement mechanisms around alcohol within a given faith tradition. Islam’s explicit prohibition, reinforced by community accountability, appears to produce a measurable protective effect in this population. These findings carry clear implications for intervention design: faith-based organizations particularly Islamic community structures represent underutilized but potentially high impact partners for alcohol harm reduction programmes in religiously diverse municipalities like Hohoe.

Factors associated with alcohol consumption

Beyond ethnicity and religion, the most commonly cited influence on drinking decisions was easy accessibility of alcohol (55.3%), followed by peer influence (18.9%) and curiosity (14.2%). These findings align with Allison (2023) and Frimpong-Mansoh (2013), who identified social functions, peer pressure, and appetite stimulation as primary drivers of alcohol use in Ghanaian contexts. What the present study adds to this literature is a quantified picture of the relative weight of these factors in Hohoe specifically, where the ready availability of affordable akpeteshie appears to be a primary structural driver. The finding that 64.1% of participants found alcohol affordable and easily accessible, and that 71.8% reported it satisfied their needs, points to a demand-side dynamic reinforced by supply-side accessibility, a combination that substantially limits the effectiveness of knowledge-based interventions alone and points to the need for regulatory and environmental strategies alongside education.

Limitations

Some limitations to be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; the associations identified between sociodemographic factors and alcohol consumption reflect co-occurrence at a single point in time and cannot establish directionality or temporality. Second, alcohol consumption and knowledge were assessed through face-to-face self-report interviews, which are subject to both recall bias; participants may not accurately remember past consumption and social desirability bias participants may under-report consumption or over-report knowledge in the presence of an interviewer. Third, although the sampling design included both residential households and alcohol selling points to reduce selection bias, the inclusion of alcohol-selling points may nonetheless have enriched the sample with heavier or more regular drinkers relative to the general population. This potential over-representation of drinkers should be considered when generalizing the prevalence estimates. Fourth, sampling was conducted within the municipal capital, limiting generalizability to rural areas of the municipality These limitations should be addressed in future longitudinal and mixed-methods research.

Policy and practice implications

The findings of this study have concrete implications for public health action at the municipal level and align with Ghana’s National Policy for the Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases 2022–2026, which identifies alcohol as a priority risk factor for NCD burden reduction [12]. Given that accessibility and affordability are the primary structural drivers of consumption, the Hohoe Municipal Assembly and the Ghana Food and Drugs Authority should consider strengthening enforcement of existing alcohol licensing regulations, including restrictions on the density of alcohol retail outlets and the sale of akpeteshie in unlicensed premises. The Ghana Health Service’s Health Promotion Division should develop targeted community health education campaigns that go beyond general awareness messaging to address the specific long-term risks of alcohol particularly its links to cancer, liver disease, cardiovascular conditions, and stroke using locally appropriate media and community structures. Given the significant role of ethnicity and religion, culturally tailored and faith sensitive approaches are essential; engagement with traditional leaders, community elders, and Islamic community organizations should be central to any intervention strategy. Finally, the high prevalence among the 30–59 age group suggests that workplace health programmes and community-based screening for hazardous drinking warrant consideration as priority action areas for the Hohoe District Health Directorate. Effective action will require multi-sector collaboration across health, policy, community leadership, and faith institutions, given the structural and sociocultural complexity of alcohol use in this setting.

Conclusion

Alcohol consumption represents a significant and growing public health burden in Hohoe Municipality, with over half of the adult population (53.5%) reporting lifetime alcohol use and 38.4% identified as current consumers. These figures are notably higher than those reported in earlier Ghanaian subpopulation studies, likely reflecting the study’s broader age range and mixed sampling frame rather than a true secular increase. The burden this places on the prevention of alcohol-related non-communicable diseases including liver cirrhosis, cardiovascular disease, stroke, and cancer which are already straining health systems across sub-Saharan Africa, underscores the need for targeted public health action at the municipal level.

A critical finding is that knowledge of the long-term health consequences of alcohol remains limited. Although 85.6% of consumers were generally aware that alcohol causes harm when abused, 95.9% were unaware of recommended drinking limits. This knowledge gap does not operate in isolation. It is compounded by high alcohol accessibility, affordability, and embedded sociocultural drinking norms. Multi-level public health strategies that address structural drivers alongside awareness deficits are therefore required, moving well beyond information campaigns alone.

A notable contribution of this study is its empirical demonstration that ethnicity and religion are the strongest independent predictors of alcohol consumption in this population. This finding provides epidemiological justification not merely aspirational reasoning for culturally and religiously sensitive intervention frameworks. Approaches that engage traditional community leaders, leverage the protective role of religious networks, and are designed with sensitivity to the sociocultural diversity of Hohoe’s population are essential for effective programme design. Crucially, realizing these approaches will require sustained multi-sector collaboration across health services, local government, community leadership, and faith institutions.

Taken together, these findings offer a locally grounded, evidence-based foundation for alcohol policy and health promotion in Hohoe Municipality. They are also relevant to comparable municipalities across the Volta Region and Ghana where similar cultural, religious, and socioeconomic dynamics shape drinking behavior. The study further contributes to the evidence base needed to support the implementation of Ghana’s National Policy for the Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases 2022–2026 and is consistent with the broader goals of reducing premature mortality from NCDs as outlined in global health frameworks.
