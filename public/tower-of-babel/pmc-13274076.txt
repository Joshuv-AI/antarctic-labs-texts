Development and validation of a comprehensive in vitro organ model for androgenetic alopecia
Authors: Shizhao Liu, Wenzhen Li, Botian Jiang, Haoyang Li, Jian Chen, Zhexiang Fan, Zhiqi Hu, Qian Qu, Yong Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMale androgenetic alopecia (AGA) is the most common hair loss disorder. However, research on AGA remains limited, primarily due to the lack of clinical samples and imperfect AGA models incapable of replicating the androgen sensitivity of AGA.MethodsAn AGA organ model was established by optimizing the concentration of dihydrotestosterone (DHT) and simulating AGA pathology through organotypic culture. The model’s effectiveness was evaluated using histology, immunohistochemistry, and RNA-seq analysis, focusing on hair follicle (HF) growth, proliferation, apoptosis, key signaling molecules in AGA, stem cell state, and androgen receptor (AR) alterations.ResultsWe determined the optimal DHT concentration for our AGA organ model at 5 × 10− 6 mol/L. The model demonstrated inhibited HF growth and accelerated entry into the catagen phase, with a 24% reduction in matrix cell proliferation and a 31% increase in apoptosis. Key molecular changes included decreased β-catenin and IGF-1 expression and elevated TGF-β1, DKK1, p21, and AR levels. The expression of the dermal papilla (DP) cell marker versican was reduced, while the follicular stem cell marker K15 remained unchanged. Moreover, the model successfully replicated the partial reversal of DHT’s effects by minoxidil or AR antagonist bicalutamide. RNA-seq analysis revealed alterations in the Wnt/β-catenin and TGF-β signaling pathways and inflammatory responses in the AGA model.ConclusionsOur study presents a novel human in vitro AGA model that closely mirrors the clinical features of AGA in terms of morphology and molecular pathology. Beyond mechanistic research, this model provides a translationally relevant platform for drug evaluation and personalized therapeutic development. By preserving native androgen sensitivity and pathological microenvironment, this work lays a foundation for discovering new regenerative treatments for AGA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-026-01176-4.

Background

AGA is a prevalent condition causing hair thinning and baldness, primarily affecting men’s psychological well-being and quality of life. Its etiology involves a complex interplay between genetics, aging, lifestyle, environmental factors and androgen levels [1, 2]. However, the exact pathophysiology remains elusive, presenting challenges for effective treatment [3, 4]. Clinically, it is observed that AGA primarily affects HFs in the frontal scalp, while those in the occipital region remain largely unaffected. Current AGA therapies, including topical and oral medications [5, 6], platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections [7, 8], low-level laser treatment [9], and hair transplantation [6], offer only temporary improvement or delayed progression. Moreover, treatment responses vary considerably among individuals, highlighting the urgent need for novel models that can more accurately replicate the disease and support stable drug testing.

Currently, various models such as animal [10], cellular [11], and human tissue [12, 13] models have been developed to study AGA, yet they all exhibit significant shortcomings. In 1988, Uno et al. and in 1994, Mills et al. used stumptailed macaques to research the effects of topical minoxidil and the localization of AR in the scalp [14, 15]. However, research involving macaques declined in the 21st century due to ethical concerns, regulatory restrictions, and high costs [16]. Subsequently, Hideoki Ogawa [17] et al. utilized C3H/HeN transgenic mice, and Catherine C. Thompson [18] et al. developed AR transgenic mice, which reduced the costs and space requirements for creating models. Nonetheless, apart from primates [19], these animals do not naturally exhibit hair loss and cannot replicate the varied androgen sensitivity of HFs in different regions of AGA patients. Experiments on these models only reflect the damage due to androgen excess and significantly differ from human AGA, making results less convincing. Cell models primarily work by adding androgens like DHT to extracted occipital DP cells [20–22] or by transfecting them with AR [23] or other genes to mimic AGA characteristics. However, cell models still diverge from AGA due to genetic profile changes, loss of microenvironment, and various characteristics, and they cannot simulate the HF’s microenvironment within the whole organism. Among human tissue models, including scalp samples [24] and HFs [12] from patients, organ culture of HFs is becoming a research focus due to its ability to accurately reflect natural HF behavior and provide a quick, high-throughput platform for drug screening and testing. Miaojian Wan modeled AGA by adding DHT to occipital HFs, confirming that DHT regulates hair growth via the Wnt/β-Catenin pathway [25]. However, there are doubts about this method since occipital HFs are not sensitive to DHT under physiological conditions, possibly leading to non-physiological hormone dosing and failing to accurately reflect the pathology of androgen-sensitive AGA HFs. To address the limitations of existing models, we propose a novel approach that utilizes DHT-treated frontal HFs obtained from patients to recapitulate the in vitro characteristics of AGA. This method enables the investigation of disease mechanisms using patient-specific follicular units and facilitates the testing of individualized therapeutic responses—an essential step toward personalized and precision medicine for AGA.

Looking ahead, the key advantage of our AGA organ model lies in its use of human follicles from the frontal region at optimized DHT concentrations, enabling faithful recapitulation of the AGA-specific microenvironment. This establishes a controlled, reproducible platform for screening new therapeutic interventions, thereby advancing precision medicine in hair loss management. Moreover, this disease-relevant organ culture system provides a framework for investigating similar degenerative processes in other androgen-sensitive organs and for developing regenerative therapies across a spectrum of diseases.

Methods

Organ culture

Human HFs were harvested from the frontal and occipital regions of 10 AGA patients (aged 25–36) as previously described [26]. An informed consent form was obtained from all donors, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nanfang Hospital. HFs were isolated under a stereo microscope. For organ culture, 2 HFs per well were placed in a 24-well plate containing 500 µL of Williams’ E medium supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine, 10 µg/mL insulin, 10 ng/mL hydrocortisone, 10 ng/mL adrenaline, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The cultures were maintained at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2 for up to seven days [25–27]. The medium was completely renewed every 2 days, and photographs were taken every day under a stereomicroscope to document hair follicle growth.

The hair cycle score was defined as follows: anagen = 100, early catagen = 200, mid catagen = 300, late catagen = 400, and telogen = 500 [28].

Tissue histology and immunohistochemistry

HFs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and sectioned. Histological analysis was performed using Hematoxylin and Eosin staining to assess morphological changes. For immunofluorescence, permeabilization was achieved by treatment with 0.025% Triton X-100 in PBS, followed by blocking with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4℃. Then samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor-488 conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with 4’-6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Immunostaining intensity was assessed quantitatively by using ImageJ software.

RT-qPCR

Ten hair follicles per group were used for total RNA isolation. Total RNA was isolated from HFs using Animal Total RNA Isolation Kit and cDNA was synthesized using Invitrogen Superscript cDNA Synthesis Kits and quantified in real-time PCR using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene expression levels were quantified by the 2ΔΔCT method.

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from frontal hair follicles of the control and AGA model (5 × 10− 6 mol/L DHT) groups using TRIzol reagent. Sequencing libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (150 bp paired‑end). Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were quantified with featureCounts, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 with |log₂FC| ≥ 1 and padj < 0.05. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler (padj < 0.05). A protein‑protein interaction network was constructed using the STRING database and visualized with Cytoscape.

Statistics

The statistical software used was SPSS software (SPSS 22.0; IBM). p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Optimal DHT concentration of AGA in vitro organ culture

To faithfully replicate the impact of androgens on HFs in the microenvironment of AGA patients, we aimed to identify an optimal DHT concentration that inhibits the growth of AGA-affected frontal HFs without affecting occipital HFs in vitro. Frontal and occipital HFs from male AGA patients were cultivated for 7 days in media with DHT concentrations ranging from 10− 4 to 10− 8 mol/L, and compared to a DHT-free control medium. The results indicated that DHT concentrations below 10− 6 mol/L did not significantly affect the growth or the cycle of either frontal or occipital hair (Fig. 1). In contrast, concentrations of 10− 5 mol/L or higher consistently inhibited the elongation of both frontal (day 3 10− 5 mol/L: 0.073 mm vs. control: 0.140 mm, p < 0.0001) and occipital HFs(day 3 10− 5 mol/L: 0.068 mm vs. control: 0.158 mm, p < 0.0001) from the first to the seventh day of culture. The highest tested concentration of DHT (10-4mol/L) was lethal to HFs within the first day of cultivation. Observations of the hair growth cycle stages corroborated these findings (Fig. 1). By day 7, the proportion of HFs entering the catagen phase was higher with 10− 5 mol/L DHT than with the control or any lower DHT concentration. The mean hair cycle score also increased with 10− 5 mol/L DHT (Fig. 1).

Further experiments between 10− 5 mol/L and 10− 6 mol/L showed that a concentration of 5 × 10-6 mol/L DHT suppressed the elongation of frontal HFs(day 3: 0.083 mm vs. control: 0.123 mm, p < 0.0001) and increased the proportion entering the catagen phase, along with a higher mean hair cycle score. There was no significant change in the elongation, catagen phase proportion, or mean hair cycle score for the occipital HFs at this concentration. Consequently, we hypothesize that 5 × 10-6 mol/L DHT is the most suitable concentration for our AGA model (Fig. 2).

Inter-patient variability in the AGA model

To assess inter-individual reproducibility, we analyzed hair follicle (HF) responses from 10 different AGA patients. Although the inhibitory effect of 5 × 10⁻⁶ mol/L DHT on frontal HFs was consistent across all donors, the degree of inhibition varied. On day 7, relative to the control group, the inhibition of HF growth ranged from 12% to 35% (p < 0.0001), and the reduction in the proportion of anagen HFs ranged from 8% to 33% (p < 0.0001) (Table S1).

Comparison of physical parameters of HFs in the AGA organ model

On the third day after modelling with the addition of 5 × 10-6mol/L DHT, HE staining results revealed that the frontal HFs were thinner compared to the occipital HFs. Both types of HFs exhibited complete HF morphology, with intact bulbs containing fully-developed papilla. There was a significant difference in the diameter of the HFs between the frontal and occipital regions (Frontal HFs 0.359 mm ± 0.099 vs. Occipital HFs 0.478 mm ± 0.029, p < 0.001). The addition of DHT did not result in any noticeable changes in the diameter of the frontal and occipital HFs (Fig. 3a).

Proliferation and apoptosis of hair matrix keratinocytes in AGA organ model

Upon determining the optimal DHT concentration, we aimed to examine cellular proliferation and apoptosis in the in vitro AGA organ model. Considering that prolonged culture of frontal HFs results in an excessive shift to the resting phase, our findings are based on data from the third day of organ culture. Quantitative analysis of Ki-67 and TUNEL staining on day 3 revealed that DHT significantly reduced proliferation(number of Ki-67+ cells/number of DAPI+ cells×100%; Fig. 3b) and increased apoptosis(number of Tunel+ cells/number of DAPI+ cells×100%; Fig. 3c) in frontal HFs.In particular, DHT significantly reduced the number of Ki-67 + frontal matrix keratinocytes by 20% (p < 0.05), and enhanced the number of Tunel+ frontal matrix keratinocytes by 167% (p < 0.001) compared to HFs incubated with Williams E medium alone. Notably, no significant impact of DHT on occipital HFs was observed at this concentration (Fig. 3b and c).

Changes in key signaling molecule pathways in the AGA organ model

Having established the effects of DHT on proliferation and apoptosis, we next examined its impact on key signaling pathways. Wnt signaling is recognized as one of the most critical regulatory targets in the pathogenesis of AGA. In AGA-affected HFs, androgens bind to AR in dermal papilla cells, weakening downstream signals of the Wnt signaling pathway [29, 30]. This process promotes the breakdown of β-catenin and impedes its transfer into the cell nucleus, suppressing the expression of β-catenin-dependent genes related to HF growth and cell differentiation [29, 31]. As a consequence, HFs fail to enter the growth phase normally, contributing to AGA symptoms. In our AGA organ model, the expression level of β-catenin in hair matrix cells decreased by 45% (p < 0.001). Conversely, in occipital HFs, there were no significant changes in β-catenin expression (Fig. 4a). Additionally, on the first day of culture, DKK1 expression in frontal HFs was four-fold higher than that in occipital HFs (Fig. 4b), which is consistent with the findings by Kwack et al. [32]. Furthermore, we observed a 58% increase in the Wnt pathway inhibitor DKK1 expression in DP cells of the frontal HFs after three days of DHT treatment (p < 0.001). Importantly, DKK1 expression remained unchanged in the dermal papilla cells of occipital HFs (Fig. 4b). Regardless of the HF location, DKK1 expression on the third day of culture was higher than on the first day, indicating that organ culture affects the expression of follicular DKK1 (Fig. 4b).

Previous literature indicates that TGF-β1 and IGF-1 play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of AGA. TGF-β1, an inducer of the catagen phase, inhibits HF cell proliferation and induces apoptosis, with increased expression in AGA [33, 34]. IGF-1, a protein similar to growth hormone, facilitates the progression of AGA by influencing the HF cycle and promoting HF miniaturization, with its expression declining in AGA [35]. The expression of TGF-β1 was increased by 76% (p < 0.001) in the AGA organ model (Fig. 5a). Immunoreactivity was observed in the outer root sheath, and to a lesser extent, in the inner root sheath. Additionally, after modeling, mRNA levels of IGF-1 were reduced by 44% and those of TGF-β1 were increased by 42% in the entire frontal HFs (Fig. S1). In contrast, neither inducer was significantly altered in DHT-treated occipital HFs.

HF aging is a key pathological process in AGA [36], and P21 is a molecule associated with cellular aging [37]. Our study revealed that p21 expression significantly increased in the frontal HFs three days post-DHT treatment, whereas its expression in occipital HFs was unaffected (Fig. 5b). Regardless of location, p21 expression was higher on the third day of culture compared to the first (Fig. 5b).

These findings suggest that treating with 5 × 10-6 mol/L DHT for in vitro AGA modeling is consistent with the pathological changes associated with AGA.

Stem cell marker expression in AGA organ models

Our findings reveal that versican immunoreactivity was observed in the hair bulb, but more weakly, in the outer root sheath. Additionally, the expression levels of the DP cell marker versican in frontal DP cells were generally lower compared to those in the occipital HFs (Fig. 6a). After DHT treatment, the expression of versican was notably suppressed in the frontal HFs but not in the occipital ones (Fig. 6a). It is noteworthy that by the third day of cultivation, the levels of versican expression in the HFs from both the frontal and occipital regions were observed to be lower than those recorded on the first day (Fig. 6a).Treatment with DHT did not significantly affect the expression of the hair follicle stem cell marker K15 in the HFs of both the frontal and occipital regions, suggesting that changes in hair follicle stem cells may not be significant in the AGA organ model (Fig. 6b).

The expression of AR in the AGA organ model

To assess AR expression in AGA organ models, we employed immunohistochemistry (data not shown) and immunofluorescence, with the latter yielding superior results. Consistent with published results, AR immunoreactivity was mainly detected in DP, and the fluorescence intensity of the frontal HFs was much higher than that of the occipital HFs (Fig. 7). AR expression has been shown to be significantly higher in DP of DHT-treated frontal HFs than in the DP of control frontal HFs indicating that DHT stimulated AR expression (Fig. 7a). Collectively, these findings map the expression of AR to HFs in Organ-cultured frontal HFs and demonstrate that this expression is androgen sensitive, similar to that in patients with AGA.

The AR inhibitors bicalutamide and minoxidil can partially reverse the effects of DHT treatment

To further investigate the efficacy of blocking androgen receptor (AR) in rescuing anagen inhibition in this model, the following treatments were administered: no DHT, DHT alone, DHT in combination with the AR antagonist bicalutamide(DHT + bi), and DHT in combination with minoxidil(DHT + mi), a drug commonly used for hair loss treatment. The results showed that hair regrowth in frontal HFs treated with DHT plus bicalutamide or minoxidil was faster compared to those treated with DHT alone, but still slower than the control group (Fig. 8a-c). This implies that the delayed hair regrowth effect caused by DHT can be partially reversed by using an AR antagonist. Furthermore, it was observed that the proportion of HFs entering the catagen three days after modeling was higher in comparison to the other groups. The partial reversal of DHT-induced hair growth inhibition by minoxidil, a first-line FDA-approved drug, validates the predictive power of our model for pro-regenerative compounds. More importantly, the similar effect observed with the AR antagonist bicalutamide highlights the potential of targeting the androgen signaling pathway as a regenerative strategy and provides a platform for screening novel therapeutics with higher efficacy and fewer side effects.

RNA-seq analysis of the model

To assess the precise alterations in the AGA model, we performed a transcriptome analysis on frontal HFs group and frontal HFs exposed to 5 × 10− 6mol/L DHT group. This RNA-seq analysis uncovered distinctive patterns that highlight changes in Wnt/β-catenin and TGF-β signaling pathway and inflammation response within the AGA organ culture.

Table S2 lists the differentially expressed genes between the control group and the AGA model group. Among all differentially expressed genes, FRMPD3 exhibited the highest fold change of upregulation. As a scaffold protein harboring both FERM and PDZ domains, FRMPD3 is positioned at the membrane–cytoskeleton interface where it may orchestrate multiple pathogenic axes in AGA. The FERM domain mediates integrin signaling and cell-extracellular matrix interactions that are critical for DP microvascular stability, a process that has already been shown to be disrupted in AGA through AR-mediated paracrine signaling [38]. Meanwhile, its PDZ domain participates in the assembly of junctional complexes that sustain epithelial–mesenchymal crosstalk, a process progressively disrupted during follicular miniaturization [39, 40]. Furthermore, given that perifollicular fibrosis, which is potentially driven by adipocyte-to-myofibroblast transition [41] and TGF-β/Smad signaling [42], requires extensive cytoskeletal remodeling, FRMPD3 may also modulate fibrotic progression.

Table S3 and S4 present the top 30 upregulated/downregulated Gene Ontology (GO) terms and the top 30 upregulated/downregulated KEGG pathways when comparing the AGA model to the control group. GO analysis revealed inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway within the AGA model group, evidenced by decreased expression of Wnt5a and increased expression of the Wnt pathway inhibitor, DKK1(Table S2). Additionally, KEGG pathway analysis indicated that other follicle development-relevant pathways such as the TGF-β signaling pathway were upregulated in the AGA model group (Table S4). Furthermore, to elucidate the functional associations between differentially expressed genes, we utilized the STRING database to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, which revealed a significant cluster of mainly upregulated proteins involved in the transmission of the SHH and SOX14 pathways. Notably, this cluster includes PITX2, a key transcription factor regulating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, suggesting suppression of this pathway in the AGA model (Fig. S2). It is also significant that genes associated with inflammatory response, such as TNFSF14, are upregulated in the AGA model(Table S2). Additionally, several inflammation-promoting KEGG pathways, including the NF-κB pathway, were found to be upregulated, aligning with the follicular alterations observed in AGA(Table S4).

Discussion

AGA is a common, progressive hair loss disorder [5, 43]. Currently, there is growing interest in finding an appropriate model to unveil the mechanisms of AGA and to explore strategies to counteract its progression. The challenge lies in the inability to conduct direct human experiments on AGA patients, along with the inadequacies of current models. Hence, we established a frontal follicle-based AGA organ model, which accurately reflects the pathological characteristics of AGA and is vital for researching AGA and testing treatment methods.

This study established an AGA organ model using frontal HFs to evaluate the effects of DHT on hair growth, proliferation, apoptosis, stem cell dynamics, AR expression, and AGA-related pathology. Gradient experiments with DHT in vitro revealed that a concentration of 5 × 10− 6 mol/L, after three days of treatment, inhibited the growth of frontal HFs and induced their entry into the regression phase, whereas occipital HFs remained unaffected. This differential response replicates the clinical manifestation of AGA.

At the molecular level, our model successfully recapitulated key signaling imbalances characteristic of AGA. Previous research has shown that AGA progression is closely linked to the dysregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [26, 44, 45]. IGF-1 promotes HF proliferation and growth [27, 46], and TGF-β1 is associated with initiating the catagen stage in the hair growth cycle [47, 48]. Our findings demonstrate increased TGF-β1 and DKK1 and decreased IGF-1 and β-catenin expression, aligning with patterns observed in AGA development. Such alterations of HF cycle inducers may even affect follicular aging [49, 50]. Studies point to the significant role of DP cell [49] and hair follicle stem cell [51, 52] aging in AGA pathogenesis, accelerated significantly by oxidative stress and androgen effects. Aged DP cells may contribute to slower hair growth, while HFSCs show impaired proliferative capacity [36]. However, in our AGA organ model, the primary site of p21 expression was not in the DP cells or HFSC regions but concentrated in the middle segments of the HF, especially the outer root sheath and inner root sheath. This may relate to tissue-specific responses, local environmental differences, and variations in the cell cycle. This observation highlights an intrinsic limitation of the ex vivo model: the gradual “culture-induced aging” or stress response, as evidenced by rising DKK1 and p21 levels, might compound or mask the specific effects of DHT over longer culture periods. Therefore, while our 7-day model is suited to study the transition from anagen to catagen, extrapolating findings to chronic, long-term androgen exposure (over months to years) should be done with caution.

In the progression of AGA, alterations in HF microenvironment and cell signaling impact both dermal and epidermal stem cells [53–55]. Key among these alterations is a decrease in versican [13], a proteoglycan vital for HF structure, observed in our AGA organ model. This reduction could compromise the microenvironment, impairing essential interactions between DP cells and matrix keratinocytes that maintain the hair growth phase. Despite these changes, the expression of the epithelial stem cell marker K15 remained stable, suggesting that AGA’s impacts on hair growth may stem from altered signaling pathways and environments supporting stem cell proliferation, rather than a depletion of the epidermal stem cell pool. This observation aligns with documented patterns in AGA-afflicted HFs.

AR expression is elevated in AGA and plays a crucial role in the disease process. Studies by Chong Hyun Won et al. [21] have shown that knocking out AR can promote hair growth in DHT-induced AGA mouse models. These effects of DHT were partially reversed by AR inhibitors like finasteride and minoxidil [56–58]. By blocking AR, finasteride may interfere with DHT’s ability to induce detrimental changes in HF dynamics [56], thus potentially mitigating hair loss associated with elevated androgen levels. These findings also confirm that our AGA model successfully replicates the features of AGA-afflicted HFs. To assess the gene-level changes within our AGA model, we conducted a transcriptome analysis of both the AGA models and control groups. The sequencing revealed changes in the Wnt/β-catenin, NF-κB and TGF-β signaling pathways.

AGA, while not an autoimmune disease, shows compromised immune privilege in HFs, affecting normal function. Notably, research reveals increased expression of genes related to inflammation, stress, and fibrosis, particularly in the hair bulb and bulge area [59], corroborated by evidence of fibrosis from Hu Zhiqi’s work [60]. Our studies also indicate heightened inflammatory responses, including NF-κB pathway activation, suggesting profound immunological shifts within AGA HFs. Future treatments might therefore focus on anti-inflammatory strategies to address these immune-related alterations in AGA.

Beyond elucidating disease mechanisms, our AGA organ model holds significant promise for advancing precision medicine in AGA management. Current treatment strategies largely rely on a trial-and-error approach, with considerable inter-patient variability in response to frontline therapies such as finasteride and minoxidil. Leveraging patient-derived frontal HFs, our experimental framework can be adapted into a personalized drug testing platform. In a future clinical workflow, a small punch biopsy (yielding 20–30 HFs) could be obtained from a patient’s frontal scalp during a routine clinic visit. These HFs would be isolated, cultured in our AGA model system (as described in Methods), and exposed to a panel of therapeutic agents (e.g., minoxidil, finasteride, low-level laser therapy, or novel compounds) for 7 days. The readouts, including hair shaft elongation, hair cycle stage, and molecular markers (e.g., Ki-67, β-catenin), would provide direct, patient-specific evidence of therapeutic efficacy. This would empower clinicians to select the most effective treatment for that individual, thereby shifting from empirical to evidence-based, personalized regimens. Practical barriers, such as cost and turnaround time, would need to be optimized, but this model provides a translatable framework for doing so.

While our in vitro AGA organ model reflects many of the pathological characteristics of AGA, it has certain limitations that must be acknowledged. Our model cannot address factors such as hormone levels regulated by various physiological factors, including health status and hormonal cycles. However, compared to other models, our model uses HFs from AGA patients’ frontal regions with appropriate DHT concentrations, accurately reflecting the fundamental characteristics of diseased HFs.

Although this study focuses on AGA, the experimental paradigm we established—using patient-derived diseased tissue to construct an in vitro organ model that recapitulates key pathological features—has broad applicability. This model enables the evaluation of interactions among proliferation, apoptosis, senescence, and inflammation within a 3D organ context, serving as a prototype for studying other hormone-related organ disorders (e.g., prostate hyperplasia) or chronic degenerative diseases driven by genetic and inflammatory factors.

Conclusions

In conclusion, current evidence shows that our AGA organ model aligns with AGA in aspects like regulating HF growth, proliferation, apoptosis, and pathological changes. Although its in vitro nature limits direct extrapolation to in vivo conditions, these observations provide insights for further AGA research and drug screening and may open doors for novel therapeutic interventions. Future studies should further unravel the complex interplay between androgens and other signaling pathways within HFs affected by AGA. Beyond hair loss research, the experimental framework presented here—using patient-derived diseased tissue to construct a disease-relevant organ culture system—holds promise for broader application as a paradigm for studying organ degeneration and regeneration in other related diseases.
