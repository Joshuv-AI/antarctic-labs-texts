Flipped classroom combined with case-based learning enhances ventilator management training for non-anesthesiology medical undergraduates: a randomized controlled study
Authors: Su-Mei Liu, Peng Ou, Hui Li, Zhao-Lan Hu, Ru-Yi Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUndergraduate education constitutes a fundamental component of higher education and serves as a crucial indicator of institutional quality. Consequently, the reform of medical curricula, especially in the field of anesthesiology, is imperative. Such reform must effectively cater to both specialty trainees and non-specialty students on rotation, thereby enhancing the clinical relevance and educational impact of the discipline. This study aimed to evaluate the teaching effectiveness of a flipped classroom (FC) combined with case-based learning (CBL) approach in the ventilator management clerkship for non-anesthesiology undergraduate students.MethodsA total of 164 non-anesthesiology undergraduates (Class of 2021, five-year clinical medicine program) from Xiangya School of Medicine, Central South University, were enrolled. Using a random number table based on pre-existing clerkship groups, students were assigned to either the FC combined with CBL (FC-CBL) group (n = 87) or the traditional lecture-based learning (LBL) group (n = 77). The FC-CBL group completed pre-class video modules covering respiratory mechanics principles and ventilation mode recognition. During class, students worked in small groups to analyze typical cases of patients undergoing general anesthesia across three phases (induction, maintenance, and emergence), engaging in hands-on ventilator parameter adjustment training. Teaching satisfaction was assessed using a Likert 5-point scale. Learning outcomes were evaluated multidimensionally, including theoretical assessment, practice skills testing, and teaching satisfaction survey.ResultsCompared to the LBL group, the FC-CBL group demonstrated significantly higher total theoretical scores (90.0 [80.0, 100.0] vs. 100.0 [80.0, 100.0], P = 0.024), total operational skill scores (80.0 [60.0, 95.0] vs.90.0 [80.0, 95.0], P = 0.016), and total teaching satisfaction scores (27.0 [24.2, 30.0] vs.30.0 [26.0, 30.0], P = 0.049). In the modular theoretical assessment, the FC-CBL group scored significantly higher than the LBL group in the “Basic Knowledge” module (30.0 [20.0, 40.0] vs. 40.0 [30.0, 40.0], P = 0.02).ConclusionThe FC-CBL model, as an innovative teaching approach, effectively enhances theoretical knowledge, operational skills, and teaching satisfaction among non-anesthesiology undergraduates learning ventilator management, meriting wider application in anesthesiology clinical clerkships.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08380-4.Keywords: Undergraduate Education, Flipped Classroom, Case-Based Learning, Ventilator, Teaching Evaluation

Introduction

Undergraduate education has emerged as a crucial component of higher education and serves as a significant indicator for assessing the quality and standards of higher educational institutions [1]. The reform of undergraduate medical education is of paramount importance, with anesthesia curriculum reform being particularly significant as it should encompass not only anesthesia majors but also non-anesthesia students [2–4]. Currently, most teaching innovations in anesthesiology primarily target anesthesia specialty students [5, 6], while anesthesiology faculty actually engage with a much broader audience - including non-specialty students during their clinical rotations, internships, and clerkships. Implementing pedagogical reforms for these non-specialty students represents a crucial pathway to enhance the influence and reach of the anesthesiology discipline.

Proficiency in mechanical ventilation represents a core clinical competency in anesthesiology and critical care, directly impacting patient safety during the perioperative period. Traditional lecture-based learning (LBL) often fails to equip students with the ability to translate complex ventilator principles into dynamic clinical decision-making, increasing the risk of operational errors [7]. Compounded by limited hands-on exposure during clerkships, students frequently lack of opportunities to develop systematic clinical reasoning skills. Undergraduate medical education forms the foundation of physician training. Prior studies suggest case-based learning (CBL) enhances clinical skill acquisition and learners satisfaction compared to conventional methods [8], while the flipped classroom (FC) model—shifting knowledge transmission to pre-class activities and focusing in-class time on application—promotes learner engagement and active participation [9, 10]. Previous study showed that the flipped classroom combined with CBL (FC-CBL) [11] instructional approach is anticipated to enhance the learning motivation of international students, augment the appeal of teaching, and promote interaction in the classroom. Concurrently, the integration of the FC-CBL teaching methodology is expected to bolster the assessment of international students’ engagement both in and out of the classroom, as well as in online learning environments. Nevertheless, robust evidence regarding the integration of FC with CBL specifically for ventilator management training remains limited in the Chinese medical education context.

This study designed and implemented an innovative FC-CBL curriculum for ventilator management. Through structured pedagogical redesign and multidimensional evaluation, we aimed to address critical limitations of traditional teaching: dissociation of theory and practice, passive learner roles, and insufficient contextualization within clinical workflows.

Methods

Participants

164 fifth-year clinical medicine undergraduates (Class of 2021) from Xiangya School of Medicine were enrolled. Students were randomized by pre-existing 12 clerkship groups into LBL (n = 77) or FC-CBL (n = 87) cohorts. Groups were comparable in baseline characteristics (gender distribution: LBL 38 M/39F, FC-CBL 41 M/46F; P > 0.05). Ethics approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of The Second Xiangya Hospital (Approval No.: LYEC2025-0168), with written informed consent from all participants. Undergraduates operate ventilators on mannequins rather than patients. This study was conducted in strict accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Randomization and blinding

In this study, students were assigned to 12 clerkship groups based on their clinical rotation schedule. Cluster randomization was performed on these groups using a random number table. While the students were unaware of their group assignments, it was not feasible to ensure baseline equivalence between groups in terms of prior academic performance or comprehension ability.

Learning objectives

Upon completion, students should be able to: select appropriate ventilator modes and parameters for the induction, maintenance, and emergence (including extubation) phases of general anesthesia; Interpret arterial blood gas (ABG) results—including recognition of normal values, acid-base disturbances, and electrolyte imbalances; and adjust ventilator settings accordingly.

Curriculum content

Content adhered to the Xiangya School of Medicine syllabus, covering essential ventilator theory and standardized operational protocols. Both groups received identical content volume and contact hours, delivered by an attending anesthesiologist with more than five years of experience.

Instructional methods

The FC + CBL method was structured into two phases. Before class, students learned on their own using the WeChat platform and online videos about respiratory mechanics and ventilation modes. They also had some early online discussions. During the in-class phase, small groups analyzed sequential clinical cases integrated with hands-on ventilator training on mannequins, facilitated by attending anesthesiologists who provided immediate feedback. The case-based discussions centered on a 55-year-old female (160 cm, 60 kg, previously healthy) undergoing an appendectomy under general anesthesia and covered three critical phases: (1) Induction: selecting the initial ventilation mode and parameters post-intubation with a focus on safety; (2) Maintenance: interpreting abnormal arterial blood gas results and adjusting ventilator settings to manage intraoperative challenges; and (3) Emergence: assessing weaning criteria and selecting appropriate modes for safe extubation. As the depth of anesthesia changed, leading to variations in respiratory status, corresponding adjustments in ventilator mode and parameters were required.

In summary, this sequential design separated knowledge acquisition from application, using realistic cases to develop the competencies measured in our outcomes directly. After the assessment, the instructor briefly summarized both student groups. Additionally, the cases from the FC-CBL group were shared with the LBL group, and video materials from the FC-CBL group were distributed via the WeChat platform for all students to review (Fig. 1).

Outcome measures

The teaching effectiveness evaluation in this study included three aspects: theoretical assessment, practice skills testing, and teaching satisfaction survey.

Theoretical assessment

A modular closed-book written exam was conducted to evaluate students’ mastery of three core areas in ventilator application: “basic knowledge,” “clinical application skills,” and “safety and risk prevention awareness.” The exam consisted of 10 multiple-choice questions, with a scoring distribution of 40 points for “basic knowledge,” and 30 points each for “clinical application skills” and “safety and risk prevention awareness.” The exam paper (Supplementary Material 1) was jointly designed by three instructors, including the course lecturers.

Practical skills assessment

A standardized operational assessment (Supplementary Material 2) was conducted in a simulated scenario, evaluating performance in “safety verification,” " rationality of mode selection”, “initial parameter settings,” “correct ventilator connection,” and “dynamic parameter adjustment.” Two trained examiners (not involved in teaching) independently scored the performance based on a unified grading rubric, with the final score being the average of the two evaluations.

Teaching satisfaction survey

Upon completion of the training, an anonymous questionnaire survey was conducted with a self-designed Likert 5-point scale [12–15]. The scale covered six dimensions: “teaching method”, “knowledge mastery”, “operational confidence”, “clinical thinking”, “course load” and “teacher guidance” comprising a total of six items (e.g., “The lesson increased my interest in learning”, " Through this course, I can more clearly understand the principles of adjusting ventilator parameters under different conditions”). Response options: 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree. The total score was the sum of all item scores (range: 6–30 points). The questionnaire was distributed via the “Wenjuanxing” platform (Supplementary Material 3). The concise scale’s internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Analysis of the collected data via Wenjuanxing yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.915, indicating satisfactory reliability.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). The normality of all continuous variables was first assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For variables that followed a normal distribution, data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and group comparisons were conducted using the independent samples t-test. For variables that did not meet the normality assumption, data were presented as median (interquartile range), and comparisons were performed using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

This study enrolled 164 undergraduate students from the Class of 2021 majoring in non-anesthesiology disciplines at Xiangya School of Medicine, Central South University. Participants were randomly allocated by clerkship groups into either the LBL group or the FC + CBL group. Exclusion criteria included: (1) participants who declined to participate in the study, and (2) participants who withdrew during the intervention period. Upon course completion, all students underwent theoretical knowledge testing, practical skills assessment, and teaching satisfaction survey (Fig. 2).

The impact of FC-CBL on the mastery of theoretical knowledge among Non-Anesthesiology undergraduate students

Students in the FC-CBL group achieved significantly higher total theoretical scores than those in the traditional LBL group (LBL group vs. FC-CBL group, median [IQR]: 90.0 [80.0, 100.0] vs. 100.0 [80.0, 100.0]; U = 2699.5, P = 0.024) and “basic knowledge” module (30.0 [20.0, 40.0] vs. 40.0 [30.0, 40.0], U = 2693.5, P = 0.020). The non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was employed as the Shapiro-Wilk test indicated the data deviated from a normal distribution (W < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in Clinical Application or Safety/Risk Mitigation modules (P > 0.05; Table 1). Therefore, FC-CBL can significantly improve theoretical knowledge of ventilator application in undergraduate non-anesthesiology students.

Effects of FC-CBL on ventilator technical skills in Non-Anesthesiology undergraduate students

Clinical clerkships are not just about theoretical knowledge, but more crucially about developing hands-on practical skills. FC-CBL students achieved higher total skills scores (80.0 [60.0, 95.0] vs. 90.0 [80.0, 95.0], U = 2627.5, P = 0.016, Table 2) and superior performance in safety checks (10.0 [0.0, 10.0] vs. 10.0 [10.0, 10.0], U = 2673.5, P = 0.002, Table 2), mode selection (10.0 [10.0, 10.0] vs.10.0 [10.0, 10.0], U = 2933.5, P = 0.023, Table 2). No differences were found in initial settings, circuit assembly or dynamic parameter adjustment (P > 0.05; Table 2). The FC-CBL group approach comprehensively enhances ventilator operation competency in non-anesthesiology undergraduates, effectively transforming theoretical knowledge into practical skills and potentially laying a solid foundation for future clinical practice.

Assessment of teaching satisfaction levels among Non-Anesthesiology undergraduate students

Regarding the satisfaction survey, we distributed questionnaires via Wenjuanxing to students to ascertain their level of satisfaction with the FC-CBL and LBL. A total of 148 valid questionnaires were collected (70 in the LBL group and 78 in the FC-CBL group), with an effective response rate of 90.24%. The FC-CBL group scored significantly higher than the LBL group in satisfaction ratings for the dimensions of “knowledge mastery,” (4.0 [4.0, 5.0] vs. 5.0 [4.0, 5.0], U = 2322, P = 0.001, Table 3) as well as in overall satisfaction (27.0 [24.2, 30.0] vs. 30.0 [26.0, 30.0], U = 2245, P = 0.049, Table 3). The FC-CBL group showed a trend toward higher ratings in “operational confidence” and “teaching method” compared to the LBL group, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (Table 3). There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in satisfaction ratings for the dimensions of “clinical thinking,” “course load,” or “teacher guidance” (P > 0.05, Table 3). The findings demonstrate that FC-CBL significantly improves instructional satisfaction while maintaining the original learning workload.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that an integrated FC-CBL approach significantly enhances ventilator management competencies among non-anesthesiology undergraduates. It’s due to the synergistic design: FC shifts foundational knowledge acquisition (e.g., respiratory physiology, waveform interpretation) to self-paced pre-class study, reducing intrinsic cognitive load during contact hours [16, 17]. The time liberated from class is then dedicated to CBL, where authentic clinical scenarios—such as managing difficult ventilation during induction, correcting intraoperative acid-base disturbances, or assessing extubation readiness—serve as anchors for deliberate practice [8]. This establishes a “preparatory study to contextualized application with immediate feedback” learning cycle, effectively mitigating the “low-exposure, high-stakes” challenge inherent in the acquisition of non-specialized skills.

Research has demonstrated [11] that the FC model overcomes spatiotemporal constraints in knowledge delivery by utilizing pre-class learning analytics (e.g., video viewing patterns) to enable instructors to identify knowledge gaps and provide targeted in-class guidance. CBL enhances analytical and decision-making competencies through authentic clinical scenarios, transforming instructors into facilitators while students achieve knowledge transfer via case discussions and role-playing activities. The integrated FC-CBL approach has proven effective in pathophysiology [11] and nephrology education [18], significantly improving learning motivation, instructional engagement, and clinical case analysis proficiency. The current study establishes the superior efficacy of the FC-CBL model in enhancing mechanical ventilation education for non-anesthesiology undergraduates. The FC-CBL cohort demonstrated significantly better performance than the traditional LBL group across multiple metrics: comprehensive theoretical knowledge (particularly in fundamental concepts), practical skills assessment, and critical operational competencies (including safety verification, ventilation mode selection, and dynamic parameter adjustment).

Mechanistically, the FC component facilitates foundational knowledge acquisition through pre-class multimedia resources (e.g., respiratory mechanics animations and waveform interpretation tutorials), effectively reducing intrinsic cognitive load during classroom sessions - a design principle aligned with cognitive load theory’s optimization of working memory [19]. The liberated classroom time enables immersive CBL sessions anchored in authentic clinical scenarios (e.g., difficult airway management intraoperatively, abnormal blood gas interpretation during maintenance, and respiratory function evaluation during emergence). These simulations require students to troubleshoot alarms, adjust ventilator settings, and resolve clinical dilemmas, thereby reinforcing conceptual understanding while developing clinical reasoning skills [20]. This synergistic integration establishes a closed-loop pedagogical framework comprising pre-class cognitive preparation, in-situ case-based application, and real-time competency feedback. This innovative paradigm effectively addresses the pedagogical challenge of teaching high-stakes clinical skills to non-specialists with limited prior exposure, establishing a replicable model for competency-based medical education.

Teaching satisfaction serves as a crucial indicator for evaluating the acceptance of educational reforms [21, 22]. In this study, students in the FC-CBL group demonstrated significantly higher satisfaction levels regarding knowledge acquisition, and overall scores surpassing those of the LBL group. These findings indicate strong endorsement of the FC-CBL model from the students. This approach effectively enhances students’ learning motivation and cultivates their self-directed learning capabilities. Although it requires greater instructor investment in case design, video production, and classroom facilitation, the improvements in teaching effectiveness are substantial. Implementing such educational innovations among non-anesthesiology medical undergraduates represents a vital strategy for enhancing their perioperative critical care management skills and expanding the influence of anesthesiology as a discipline.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, its single-institution design limits generalizability. Second, while cluster randomization by clerkship group was a practical approach, it was not feasible to ensure baseline equivalence between groups in terms of prior academic performance or comprehension ability, which could potentially confound the results. Third, the outcomes were measured immediately after the intervention. Therefore, future multi-center studies with longitudinal follow-up are needed to assess the long-term skill retention and the translational impact on patient care. Finally, for the teaching satisfaction survey, using a single item per dimension was a pragmatic choice to minimize respondent burden and maintain a high response rate; however, this approach limits the depth of insight for each dimension and is a recognized methodological limitation of the study.

Conclusion

The FC-CBL model significantly improves theoretical knowledge, operational proficiency, and learners’ satisfaction in ventilator management training for non-anesthesiology medical students. We recommend its adoption within anesthesiology clerkships and analogous high-stakes clinical skill domains.
