Toxoplasma gondii infection is associated with changes in chronic outcomes in traumatic brain injury
Authors: Gershon Spitz, Tamara L Baker, Stuart J McDonald, Amelia J Hicks, Rachael Knott, Mujun Sun, Jake Mitchell, Gavin Gan, Natasha Krishnadas, Terence J O’Brien, William T O’Brien, Meng Law, Jennie L Ponsford, Christopher Rowe, Sandy R Shultz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic brain injury (TBI) can lead to lasting neurological and emotional effects. Latent Toxoplasma gondii (T. gondii) infection, prevalent worldwide, may exacerbate these outcomes by altering immune and neurochemical pathways. This cross-sectional observational study investigated whether chronic T. gondii infection is associated with structural brain differences and long-term outcomes in survivors of moderate-to-severe TBI ≥ 10 years post-injury. 89 TBI survivors (≥10 years post-injury) were recruited from a tertiary-centre database; 35 (39%) tested positive for latent T. gondii via plasma IgG. Thirty-two age- and sex-matched controls without TBI were included (25% T. gondii-seropositive). Nonparametric regression analyses adjusted for age, sex, and intracranial volume, with false discovery rate corrections applied. Primary outcomes were MRI-based measures of white matter microstructure, cortical thickness, and subcortical volumes. Secondary outcomes included neuropsychological assessments of anxiety, depression, cognition, and TBI blood biomarkers. Within the TBI group, T. gondii-positive individuals had reduced fibre density and increased diffusivity in the posterior corpus callosum, increased cortical thickness (insula, cuneus), and reduced brainstem volume. They also reported higher anxiety and mediation analysis showed brainstem volume partially mediated the link between infection and anxiety. Within controls, T. gondii infection was not significantly associated with anxiety, cognition, white matter microstructure, or blood biomarkers. However, controls with T. gondii infection did have increased cortical thickness in the left inferior temporal gyrus and reduced volume in the left caudate compared to their uninfected counterparts. Taken together, latent T. gondii infection may alter brain structure and exacerbate anxiety in chronic TBI. These findings support considering infection status in TBI prognostics and call for further research into its mechanistic and clinical implications.Subject terms: Psychiatric disorders, Neuroscience

Introduction

Toxoplasma gondii (T. gondii) is an obligate intracellular parasitic protozoan that infects a wide array of warm-blooded animals, including approximately one-third of humans worldwide [1–3]. Transmission occurs primarily via ingestion of contaminated water or food harbouring oocysts, consumption of undercooked meat containing tissue cysts, or exposure to cat faeces. Once acquired, T. gondii typically establishes a lifelong latent infection by persisting within host tissues – particularly in the brain – in the form of dormant cysts [4–6]. Although acute infections can be managed with antiparasitic therapies, these interventions do not eradicate the infection which eventually transitions into a chronic latent phase. There is some evidence from general population studies suggests that latent T. gondii infection may influence brain structure and neuropsychiatric function. Epidemiological studies have linked T. gondii seropositivity to increased risk of schizophrenia, anxiety, suicidal behavior, and alterations in personality and impulsivity [7–10]. Although neuroimaging studies in humans remain limited, a UK Biobank analysis found that higher T. gondii antibody titers were associated with reduced total grey matter volume, though no associations were observed with specific regional volumes or white matter [10]. While most chronically infected individuals remain clinically asymptomatic, emerging evidence suggests that latent T. gondii infection may modify neurological vulnerability to other brain insults, including traumatic brain injury (TBI) [11, 12].

TBI is a significant health concern worldwide, with millions of individuals affected each year, leading to a broad spectrum of short- and long-term neurological deficits [13]. These injuries often result in lasting cognitive impairment, increased susceptibility to neurodegenerative diseases, and heightened risk of psychiatric disorders [14–16]. TBI initiates a cascade of inflammatory responses, cellular damage, and subsequent neural repair mechanisms, which can alter brain function and structure [17–19].

Recent preclinical studies have found that chronic T. gondii infection – defined as the latent phase marked by the persistence of tissue cysts in the brain following an acute infection – can interact with TBI-induced alterations and influence outcomes [12, 20]. This interaction may stem from the parasite’s capacity to modulate the immune system, particularly through cytokine production, and its influence on neurochemical pathways [6, 12, 21]. Experimental models have demonstrated that chronic T. gondii infection leads to increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α within the brain [20]. These cytokines are also elevated in response to TBI, where they play a crucial role in the immune response to injury but may additionally contribute to secondary neurotoxicity and exacerbate neuronal damage [5]. The overlapping immune activation raises the possibility that a concomitant chronic T. gondii infection could amplify neuroinflammation and neuropathology, thereby worsening neurobehavioral outcomes or delaying recovery, as supported by murine studies showing that infected mice exhibit more pronounced inflammatory responses and neuropathological changes after TBI compared to non-infected controls [12, 20].

Despite these intriguing connections and potential clinical implications, the relationship between T. gondii infection and TBI remains largely unexplored in humans. To our knowledge, no previous study has systematically examined the association between latent T. gondii infection and long-term neural, cognitive, and emotional outcomes following moderate-to-severe TBI. In this study, we investigated the association between latent T. gondii infection and neural outcomes, as well as cognitive and emotional function, in a cohort of individuals more than 10 years post moderate-to-severe TBI. We employed a multimodal approach by examining the relationship between T. gondii infection and brain microstructure, in addition to brain morphometry (i.e., cortical thickness and subcortical volume) via neuroimaging, along with assessments of blood biomarkers, cognitive performance, and neuropsychiatric symptoms. We hypothesised that latent T. gondii infection would be associated with alterations in brain microstructure and morphometry, differences in blood biomarkers reflective of neural injury, and impairments in cognitive and emotional function relative to uninfected TBI survivors. By integrating neuroimaging data with cognitive and behavioral assessments, this study aims to characterise the potential neurological and psychosocial effects of latent T. gondii infection following TBI, providing novel insights into its implications for long-term brain health and function.

Methods

Study population

Participants for this cross-sectional observational study were recruited between January 2018 and March 2021 from a pre-existing database of individuals who had previously undergone inpatient rehabilitation at Epworth HealthCare in Melbourne, Australia. Prior to any research activities, all participants, or their legal representatives, provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the Austin Health Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/17/Austin/202). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

From this database, eligible participants were selected for a study focusing on chronic neuropathology in an ageing cohort of adults with moderate-to-severe TBI. Eligibility criteria were being 40 years or older at enrolment (to capture an ageing population where long-term neuropathological changes may be more apparent), having sustained a single moderate-to-severe TBI (as documented in medical records and classified by the Mayo classification system) [22], being at least 16 years old at the time of injury, and at least 10 years post-injury. Exclusion criteria were any pre-existing neurological conditions and insufficient cognitive, English language, or functional capacity to participate in in-person assessments, which comprised blood draws, neuropsychological testing, MRI, and PET scans. This specific cohort of aging, chronic TBI participants has been described in previous studies [23–25]. These previous papers confirmed that this study cohort does not differ from the larger database in key demographics and clinical variables, including sex, age at injury, injury severity (measured by post-traumatic amnesia duration and Glasgow Coma Scale score), and injury mechanism.

Although the primary focus of this study was on the interaction between T. gondii and TBI, control participants were also included for exploratory analyses of the potential consequences of T. gondii infection in the absence of TBI. For control recruitment, participants were required to be aged 40 years or older, with no history of TBI, loss of consciousness, or any other neurological conditions in line with the TBI exclusion criteria, and without severe psychological conditions. They also needed to possess adequate English language skills and sufficient cognitive capacity for study participation. To ensure comparability with the TBI cohort, controls were matched on age, sex, and estimated premorbid IQ using the MatchIt package in R [26].

T. gondii infection status

Chronic infection status was determined with a Human T. gondii IgG ELISA Kit (Creative Diagnostics®, USA, DEIA-JY2121) and acute infection status was determined with a Human T. gondii IgM ELISA Kit (Creative Diagnostics®, USA, DEIA380) [27]. Plasma was collected from K₂-EDTA tubes (7.5 mL S-Monovette, Sarstedt, Cat. No. 01.1605.008) containing prostaglandin E1 (33 ng/mL of whole blood, Sapphire Biosciences) to inhibit platelet activation. The plasma was sequentially centrifuged at room temperature: initially at 200 g for 10 min, followed by 800 g for 15 min, and finally at 3200 g for 30 min (‘max g’). After each centrifugation step, platelet-rich plasma was pooled, with the final ‘max g’ plasma snap-frozen within two hours of collection and stored in vapor-phase liquid nitrogen. Prior to analysis, plasma samples were thawed and diluted 1:100 with the dilution reagent provided with the respective kits. Samples were then processed as per manufacturer instructions. Optical density values were obtained via a FLUOstar® Omega microplate reader and interpreted as per manufacturer guidelines.

Blood biomarker procedures

Blood biomarker procedures adhered to the protocol established by the Australian Imaging, Biomarkers, and Lifestyle (AIBL) Study as described above. Plasma samples were thawed and biomarker quantification was conducted on the Single Molecule Array (Simoa) HD-X platform, using the ‘Neurology 4-Plex B’ assay to measure GFAP, UCH-L1, NfL, and total tau concentrations. P-tau181 concentrations were assessed with the ‘P-tau181 Advantage V2’ assay. Each sample was measured in duplicate, yielding mean coefficients of variation as follows: 2.8% for GFAP, 5.4% for NfL, 6.2% for tau, 28.5% for UCH-L1, and 5.4% for P-tau181. Of the total samples, 32 out of 147 had UCH-L1 levels below the lower limit of detection (LLOD) of 1.90 pg/mL and were assigned concentrations equal to the LLOD. Participants who tested positive for IgM antibodies were excluded, as concurrent seropositivity may indicate acute or recently acquired infection, which is characterised by a different immunological profile compared to a chronic, latent infection. One TBI participant tested positive for IgM antibodies and was therefore excluded from all subsequent analyses. The final TBI sample comprised of 89 participants after this exclusion.

MRI acquisition

All imaging was performed on a 3T Siemens Magnetom Skyra scanner (Erlangen, Germany). T1-weighted images were acquired using a three-dimensional magnetisation-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: resolution = 1 × 1 × 1 mm, repetition time (TR) = 2300 ms, echo time (TE) = 3.65 ms, field of view = 208 × 240 × 256 mm, flip angle = 9°, number of slices = 208, and slice thickness = 1 mm. Diffusion-weighted images were acquired with the following parameters: resolution = 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.5 mm, 71 diffusion-weighted volumes (b = 3000 s/mm²) and one non-diffusion-weighted volume (b = 0 s/mm²), TR = 8400 ms, TE = 110 ms, field of view = 150 × 240 × 240 mm, and 59 axial slices. A reverse phase-encoded image was also acquired for distortion correction.

White matter microstructure

Preprocessing of the diffusion-weighted imaging data was conducted using QSIPrep (version 0.21.4) [28], a robust, automated pipeline specifically designed for diffusion magnetic resonance imaging preprocessing. This process involved denoising of raw diffusion-weighted images, correction of Gibbs ringing artefacts, and adjustments for motion and eddy currents using FSL’s eddy tool. Susceptibility-induced distortions were corrected using reverse phase-encoded b0 images, and bias field correction was performed to address intensity inhomogeneities across the images.

Following preprocessing, fibre orientation distributions were reconstructed using QSIRecon, leveraging a single-shell, three-tissue pipeline for robust modelling of tissue microstructure. Tissue-specific response functions for white matter, grey matter, and cerebrospinal fluid were estimated using a method analogous to the ‘Dhollander’ approach [29]. These response functions were then used to generate fibre orientation distributions via the multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution algorithm. To improve the accuracy of fibre orientation distribution estimation, the pipeline incorporated global intensity normalisation and calibration steps, ensuring reliable comparisons across participants. The resulting reconstructions provided high-resolution insights into tissue-specific microstructural properties, facilitating advanced analyses of diffusion metrics across the cohort.

An unbiased, study-specific white matter fibre orientation distribution template was created using non-linear registration of white matter fibre orientation distributions from 50 TBI participants and 50 controls drawn from the broader cohort of participants in the parent study. The current study includes a subset of these individuals [23–25]. Individual fibre orientation distributions were registered to this template, and fixels were segmented and reoriented to align with the template.

Whole-brain probabilistic tractography was performed on the fibre orientation distribution template to enable connectivity-based fixel enhancement for statistical analysis. Using the iFOD2 algorithm, 20 million streamlines were initially generated and subsequently filtered to 2 million streamlines using spherical-deconvolution informed filtering of tractograms to reduce reconstruction biases. Key fixel metrics derived included fibre density and log-transformed fibre cross-section, representing microstructural and macrostructural properties within white matter fibre bundles. Additionally, traditional diffusion tensor imaging metrics – fractional anisotropy, apparent diffusion coefficient, axial diffusivity, and radial diffusivity – were calculated.

White matter segmentation was performed using TractSeg, a deep learning-based framework, to parcellate the fibre orientation distribution template into 52 predefined white matter tracts [30]. Average fixel-based and tensor-based metrics were then computed for each participant across these tracts, providing comprehensive microstructural and macrostructural assessments of white matter integrity.

Cortical thickness estimation

Cortical thickness was estimated using FreeSurfer version 6.0 [31]. Lesion masks were manually delineated on T1-weighted MRI images to identify damaged brain regions. These lesion masks were then processed using the Virtual Brain Grafting (VBG) package [32]. VBG replaces lesioned areas with a synthetic “virtual brain,” ensuring more accurate cortical parcellation by mitigating errors introduced by discontinuities or missing tissue. This preprocessing step significantly improved the reliability of subsequent segmentation and parcellation processes.

Cortical parcellation was performed using the Destrieux atlas within FreeSurfer, which segments the cortex into anatomically defined regions. For each region, cortical thickness values were extracted. In addition to cortical measures, subcortical volumes were also computed to provide a comprehensive assessment of brain structure. Subcortical volumes were derived from FreeSurfer’s automated segmentation (aseg) and included bilateral lateral ventricles, inferior lateral ventricles, cerebellum white matter, cerebellum cortex, thalamus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, hippocampus, amygdala, nucleus accumbens, ventral diencephalon, and the brainstem. To account for inaccuracies arising from lesion overlap, regions with partial lesions underwent an additional adjustment procedure.

For regions where lesions overlapped with specific cortical or subcortical areas, the percentage of lesion overlap was calculated as follows: Lesion Overlap Percentage=(Lesion Overlap VoxelsRegion Volume Voxels)×100. This percentage was then used to adjust the cortical thickness or subcortical volume to provide a more accurate estimate of the structure in question. The adjusted value was calculated using the formula: Adjusted corticalthicknesssubcorticalvolume=Originalthicknessvolume×(1−Lesion overlap percentage). For example, a cortical thickness of 2.0 mm with 50% lesion overlap was adjusted to 1.0 mm. This correction ensured that cortical thickness and subcortical volume values reflected the proportion of intact tissue within the lesioned regions.

By integrating these steps, this approach allowed for reliable estimation of cortical and subcortical structural metrics while minimising segmentation errors introduced by lesions. This methodology also enhanced the comparability of results across participants with varying lesion sizes and distributions, improving the robustness of statistical analyses.

Cognitive assessment

Participants completed a comprehensive battery of cognitive tests designed to assess memory, processing speed, and executive functions [24]. Verbal memory was evaluated using the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test, which measured immediate memory (the sum of Trials 1–5), short-delay recall (Trial 6), and long-delay recall (Trial 7), along with the Wechsler Memory Scale Logical Memory I and II tests. Working memory, attention, and processing speed were assessed with the Digit Span (forward and backward) and Digit Symbol Coding tasks. Visual memory was examined using the Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure Test, which included a copy trial and delayed recall assessments at 3 and 30 min. Additionally, verbal fluency was measured using the Controlled Oral Word Association Test, while processing speed and cognitive flexibility were evaluated with the Trail Making Test Parts A and B.

Clinical evaluations

Participants completed a research interview to provide demographic information and medical history. Injury-related information (Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores, duration of post-traumatic amnesia (PTA), CT results) was obtained from medical records. PTA was measured prospectively using the Westmead Post Traumatic Amnesia Scale. Time since injury was defined as the time between injury and clinical evaluation. Participants completed cognitive tests of memory, processing speed, and cognitive control. Functional outcome was assessed with the Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended (GOSE). Premorbid IQ was assessed using the Wechsler Test of Adult Reading (WTAR). The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was administered as the primary measure of anxiety and depression symptoms.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.2), unless stated otherwise. The primary analyses assessing the impact of T. gondii infection on TBI outcomes were conducted within TBI participants. Analyses assessing the impact of T. gondii infection in control participants and comparing TBI and control participants are reported separately in the final subsections of the Results. Group-by-infection interaction analyses were not conducted given the small control sample (n = 32, 8 T. gondii seropositive). Nonparametric permutation testing, using the permuco package [33], was used to address violations of normality in analyses of white matter integrity and cortical thickness. Nonparametric regression analyses for cortical thickness and subcortical volumes accounted for age, sex, and estimated total intracranial volume. For white matter microstructure, models accounted for age and total brain volume; the latter was included to account for the substantial variability in global brain atrophy across the chronic TBI sample, and because fixel-based metrics such as fibre cross-section are related to brain size. To adjust for multiple comparisons, a False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied to all group-effect p-values. Mediation analyses were performed using the mediation package in R [34]. The indirect effects (average causal mediation effects; ACME) and direct effects (average direct effects; ADE) were estimated using 10,000 bootstrapped permutations to ensure robust confidence intervals. The mediator models tested the role of white matter tracts and brain regions significantly associated with T. gondii infection in explaining the relationship between T. gondii infection and emotional distress outcomes. Blood marker concentrations were log-transformed to address skewed distributions, and group differences in plasma concentrations were analysed using linear regression, controlling for age and sex. Additionally, linear regression models examined the relationship between chronic T. gondii infection, cognition, and emotional distress, while adjusting for age, sex, and premorbid IQ.

Results

This study included 89 participants (73% male) aged 40–85 years, with a history of a single moderate-to-severe TBI. The average age at study enrolment was 58.1 years (SD = 11.6; Table 1), with participants recruited approximately 22 years post-injury on average (SD = 6.6; range: 10–33 years). The most common cause of injury was car accidents (57%), followed by pedestrian incidents involving motor vehicles (16%), motorcycle accidents (8%), falls (6%), and bicycle accidents (6%).

Most participants experienced severe TBI: 98% had PTA lasting more than one day (M = 30.4 days; range: 1–140 days), 64% had a GCS score between 3–8, 87% had abnormal CT scans at the time of injury, and 48% had visible focal lesions on MRI at study inclusion. Despite the severity of their injuries, 57% achieved a good recovery, as indicated by a GOSE score of 7–8 at the time of clinical assessment.

Chronic T. gondii infection was identified in 39% (n = 35) of TBI participants. Those with a chronic T. gondii infection were significantly older (M = 61.9 years, SD = 12.00) compared to participants without an infection (M = 55.6 years, SD = 10.7, t(87) = 2.56, p = 0.012). No significant differences were observed between the groups on other demographic or injury-related variables.

Thirty-two control participants were comparable to individuals with TBI in age (M = 58.1 years), sex distribution (34% female), and education (M = 12.3 years). Chronic T. gondii infection was identified in 25% of controls (n = 8). There were no significant demographic differences between controls with and without chronic T. gondii infection (p > 0.05).

Association between T. gondii infection and white matter microstructure in TBI participants

White matter microstructure was assessed using traditional DTI metrics (i.e., FA, ADC, AD, and RD) and advanced fixel-based measures (i.e., FD and log-FC). To account for age differences across infection groups, chronological age and total intracranial volume were included as covariates in all nonparametric regression analyses. Significant associations between chronic T. gondii infection and alterations in white matter microstructure were observed (Fig. 1). These effects were predominantly found in the right hemisphere tracts and posterior corpus callosum regions. Chronic T. gondii infection was associated with increased AD, ADC, and RD, alongside reduced FA, FD, and log-FC.

Association between T. gondii infection and brain morphology in TBI participants

We next examined the associations between chronic T. gondii infection and brain morphology using nonparametric regression analyses, controlling for chronological age, total intracranial volume, and sex. Significant associations were observed between T. gondii infection and both cortical and subcortical brain regions (Fig. 2). Chronic T. gondii infection was associated with increased cortical thickness in specific regions, including the long gyrus and central sulcus of the insula (G Ins lg and S cent ins), the anterior circular sulcus of the insula (S circular insula ant), and the cuneus gyrus (G cuneus). These findings suggest a notable impact of T. gondii infection on cortical morphology within the insular and occipital lobes. In contrast, a reduction in brainstem volume was also observed, indicating a significant subcortical effect of chronic T. gondii infection. These findings highlight the insula and cuneus as key cortical regions showing increased thickness in response to T. gondii infection, alongside a reduction in brainstem volume, suggesting potential structural changes spanning both cortical and subcortical regions.

Associations between T. gondii infection and emotional distress in TBI participants

We examined the association between chronic T. gondii infection and emotional distress, as assessed using the HADS (Fig. 3). Regression analyses were conducted while controlling for age, sex, and premorbid IQ. Our results indicated that individuals with chronic T. gondii infection exhibited significantly higher overall HADS scores (Mean = 10.8) compared to those without infection (Mean = 8.0), bT.gondii = 2.76, SE = 1.38, p = 0.048 CI95% [0.02,5.50]. Further analysis revealed that this overall difference was primarily driven by elevated anxiety symptoms. Specifically, individuals with chronic T. gondii infection reported significantly higher anxiety scores (Mean = 6.5) compared to those without infection (Mean = 4.6), bT.gondii = 1.93, SE = 0.85, p = 0.027, CI95% [0.23,3.62]. In contrast, no significant differences were observed in depression symptoms between infected (Mean = 4.3) and non-infected individuals (Mean = 3.4), bT.gondii = 0.84, SE = 0.74, p = 0.258, CI95% [−0.62,2.30].

Chronic T. gondii infection is associated with increased emotional distress in TBI participants.

This figure illustrates the results of regression analyses investigating the relationship between chronic T. gondii infection and emotional distress, adjusting for age, sex, and premorbid IQ. The analyses revealed that individuals with chronic T. gondii infection exhibited higher levels of overall emotional distress, primarily driven by elevated anxiety symptoms, with no significant differences observed in depression levels.

Brainstem mediates the relationship between T. gondii infection and anxiety symptoms in TBI participants

Given the observed associations between chronic T. gondii infection and brain white matter integrity, we explored whether disruptions in white matter tracts mediated the relationship between T. gondii infection and emotional distress, including anxiety and depression. Mediation analyses were conducted with individual white matter tracts significantly associated with T. gondii infection serving as mediators. However, no significant indirect effects were observed for any of the examined white matter tracts.

To further examine potential mediation, we assessed brain regions with statistically significant associations with chronic T. gondii infection. Of the tested regions, the brainstem emerged as the only significant mediator, with a notable indirect effect (ACME = − 0.109, 95% CI [−0.258, −0.014], p = 0.015, Fig. 4). This suggests that changes in brainstem volume partially mediates the relationship between T. gondii infection and anxiety symptoms. Additionally, the brainstem exhibited a significant direct effect (ADE = 0.360, 95% CI [0.132, 0.579], p = 0.004), indicating that T. gondii infection influences anxiety both directly and indirectly through changes in brainstem integrity. Other brain regions, including the long gyrus and central sulcus of the insula (p = 0.487), the anterior circular sulcus of the insula (p = 0.928), and the cuneus (p = 0.119), did not exhibit significant indirect effects. These findings highlight the brainstem as a key mediator in the link between T. gondii infection and emotional distress, specifically symptoms of anxiety.

In this model, the indirect effect (ab) was significant (ab = −0.109, 95% CI[−0.258, −0.014], p = 0.015), suggesting that changes in brainstem integrity partially mediate the relationship between T. gondii infection and anxiety symptoms. The direct effect (c′) remained significant (c′ = 0.360, 95% CI[0.132, 0.579, p = 0.004), indicating that T. gondii infection has a substantial direct impact on emotional outcomes independent of the brainstem. The total effect (c) of T. gondii infection on emotional outcomes was significant (c = 0.252, 95% CI[0.031, 0.463], p = 0.039), with approximately 43.3% of this effect mediated through changes in brainstem integrity. The model was adjusted for age, gender, and estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV).

Associations between T. gondii infection and blood biomarkers in TBI participants

We examined the association between the blood biomarkers measuring neuronal, axonal, and astrocytic pathology and T. gondii infection using regression, controlling for age and sex. There were no statistically significant associations between T. gondii infection and any of the blood biomarkers: P-tau181 (bP-tau = −0.03, SE = 0.09, p = 0.746, CI95% [−0.20, 0.14]), tau (btau = −0.05, SE = 0.10, p = 0.627, CI95% [−0.26, 0.16]), UCH-L1 (bUCH-L1 = −0.06, SE = 0.25, p = 0.824, CI95% [−0.56, 0.45]), GFAP (bGFAP = −0.04, SE = 0.11, p = 0.714, CI95% [−0.18, 0.26]), and NfL (bNfL = −0.05, SE = 0.10, p = 0.591, CI95% [−0.15, 0.26]).

Associations between T. gondii infection and cognition in TBI participants

We investigated the relationship between cognition and T. gondii infection using regression analyses controlling for age, sex, and premorbid IQ. A wide range of cognitive domains were assessed, including information processing speed, verbal and visual memory, working memory, and mental flexibility. The results indicated that T. gondii infection was not significantly associated with performance on any of the cognitive tests: Digit Span (Forward and Backward), Digit Symbol Coding, Logical Memory (I and II), RAVLT (immediate and delayed recall), ROCF (copy, 3-min, and 30-min delay), COWAT, or Trail Making Tests (TMT A and B).

Associations between chronic T. gondii infection and outcome measures in control participants

In control participants, chronic T. gondii infection was not significantly associated with global outcome measures, including HADS total scores, anxiety and depression subscales, blood biomarkers, white matter microstructure, or cognitive performance (all p > 0.05). However, region-specific differences emerged: controls with chronic infection exhibited significantly greater cortical thickness in the left inferior temporal gyrus (padjusted = 0.005) and reduced volume in the left caudate (padjusted = 0.021) compared with uninfected controls.

TBI versus control comparisons for T. gondii-affected brain regions

To examine whether the brain regions associated with T. gondii infection within the TBI participants also differed between the TBI and control groups, we conducted follow-up group comparisons using the same nonparametric permutation regression framework and covariates (age, sex, and intracranial volume for grey matter; age and brain volume for white matter).

For white matter, 35 of 40 tract-measure combinations that were associated with T. gondii status within TBI also showed significant differences between TBI and control groups (pFDR < 0.05; Table 2), with TBI participants showing reduced FA and FD alongside increased ADC, AD, and RD. For grey matter, brainstem volume was significantly reduced in TBI relative to controls (b = −2256.8, SE = 493.8, pFDR < 0.001; Table 2). The remaining cortical regions, the long gyrus and central sulcus of the insula, the anterior circular sulcus of the insula, and the cuneus, did not significantly differ between groups (all pFDR > 0.10; Table 2).

Discussion

This study investigated the association between latent T. gondii infection and long-term neural, cognitive, and emotional outcomes in individuals with moderate-to-severe TBI. By integrating neuroimaging, emotional distress measures, cognitive assessments, and blood biomarkers, we aimed to determine whether T. gondii modulates recovery in this population. Our findings demonstrate significant structural alterations in both cortical and subcortical regions, alongside heightened anxiety symptoms in infected individuals. These results offer novel insights into how chronic T. gondii infection may interact with TBI-related vulnerabilities to influence long-term outcomes.

A primary finding of this study was the significant alteration in white matter microstructure in T. gondii-infected individuals, particularly in the posterior corpus callosum and right hemisphere tracts. These changes, characterised by reduced fibre density and increased diffusivity metrics, indicate compromised white matter integrity. These findings align with preclinical evidence showing that T. gondii-infected mice subjected to TBI have worse white matter integrity than their uninfected counterparts. Notably, these white matter changes in the infected mice occurred in the presence of exacerbated neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, suggesting that these may be important contributory mechanisms in this process [12]. Given that TBI frequently induces diffuse axonal injury and disrupts interhemispheric connectivity [25, 26], chronic T. gondii infection may further exacerbate these microstructural deficits. Follow-up comparisons between TBI and control participants revealed that most white matter tracts associated with T. gondii infection within TBI were also significantly reduced in the TBI group relative to controls. This indicates that the parasite’s effects converge on tracts that are also vulnerable to injury-related damage, consistent with an exacerbation model in which chronic infection compounds TBI-induced microstructural deficits. It remains unclear, however, whether this exacerbation is primarily an early event – amplifying vulnerability in already compromised white matter – or whether the parasite exerts an ongoing, degenerative influence over time. Future longitudinal studies are necessary to disentangle these possibilities.

We also observed reduced brainstem volume in T. gondii-infected individuals. The brainstem is a key integrator of autonomic regulation, arousal, and stress responses through its connections with the amygdala, insula, and prefrontal regions [35, 36]. Consistent with previous studies, T. gondii-infected individuals reported significantly higher levels of emotional distress, driven by elevated anxiety scores [28, 29]. Mediation analysis revealed a significant indirect effect of T. gondii infection on emotional distress via brainstem volume, suggesting that compromised brainstem integrity partly explains the link between infection and anxiety. Brainstem volume was also significantly reduced in TBI participants relative to controls, indicating that the brainstem is already vulnerable to injury-related atrophy. The additional reduction associated with T. gondii infection suggests that the parasite compounds this existing deficit, with the further volume loss partially mediating the observed elevation in anxiety symptoms. This finding underscores the brainstem’s role within anxiety circuitry, as it processes sensory input and autonomic responses central to fear and distress. In TBI populations, these effects may be compounded by disruptions to the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and prefrontal-limbic circuitry, which are commonly impacted by injury. Importantly, latent T. gondii infection has been linked to anxiety and reduced grey-matter volume in the general population [9, 10]. Our control group was likely underpowered to detect such effects, and we cannot rule out that the anxiety association observed in our TBI participants partly reflects a main effect of infection. However, the identification of brainstem volume as a significant mediator, a structure reduced in TBI relative to controls and previously found to be affected by T.gondii cysts and lesions [37, 38], suggests that TBI-related neuropathology may provide a substrate through which the effects of infection on anxiety are amplified. Further supporting the notion that infection and injury may interact to worsen neurobiological outcomes, Buh et al. [39] found that serum levels of S100B – a biomarker of astroglia injury and/or activation [40] – were acutely elevated in TBI patients who were T. gondii-seropositive.

The observed increases in cortical thickness in the insula and cuneus may offer further insights into T. gondii’s potential impact on anxiety-related neural circuitry. While cortical atrophy is a hallmark of chronic TBI, increased cortical thickness may reflect maladaptive neuroplasticity driven by chronic neuroinflammation. Similar patterns have been reported in COVID-19 patients, where reactive gliosis and cellular reorganisation contribute to counterintuitive increases in cortical thickness [27]. Importantly, follow-up analyses revealed that the insula and cuneus did not significantly differ between TBI and control groups, indicating that the increased cortical thickness observed in T. gondii-infected TBI participants cannot be attributed to an exacerbation of existing injury-related pathology. Rather, these changes may reflect a distinct infection-driven process affecting regions that are otherwise structurally intact following TBI. The insula, a hub for interoception, emotional regulation, and sensory integration, is heavily implicated in anxiety disorders [41, 42]. Structural changes in the insula may reflect heightened reactivity or maladaptive remodelling, contributing to exaggerated emotional responses observed in T. gondii-infected individuals. Similarly, increased thickness in the cuneus, a region responsible for early visual processing, has been linked to sensory hyper-reactivity, which can amplify perceptual stress and emotional distress. Combined with the brainstem findings, T. gondii infection appears to influence regions central to emotional and sensory processing, potentially underpinning the observed anxiety phenotypes.

Chronic T. gondii infection was not significantly associated with exacerbated cognitive impairment or with blood-based biomarkers in our cohort. Although our TBI sample exhibited notable cognitive deficits, the presence of chronic infection did not further deteriorate these functions. It is possible that the cognitive impairment in our moderate-to-severe TBI cohort was sufficiently pronounced such that any additional impact of T. gondii was not readily detectable with our current assessment tools. Alternatively, compensatory neural mechanisms or the limited sensitivity of our cognitive tests might have masked subtle effects of the infection. Similarly, the absence of significant associations with peripheral blood biomarkers (GFAP, NfL, P-tau, UCH-L1, and tau) suggests that these markers may not capture the specific pathophysiological processes linked to chronic T. gondii infection in the chronic phase of TBI. It may be that the neurodegenerative impact of the infection might have been more pronounced earlier in the post-injury period—resulting in transient elevations of these biomarkers—while later assessments reflect a more stable state. Given the established associations between these biomarkers and structural brain changes, including in our own work, a more nuanced, longitudinal investigation is required to elucidate the temporal dynamics of T. gondii-related neurodegeneration in TBI survivors.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional, observational design precludes definitive causal inferences regarding the interplay between chronic T. gondii infection and TBI outcomes. A significant limitation is the uncertainty regarding the timing of infection relative to TBI occurrence and clinical sampling; it remains unclear whether T. gondii infection preceded the injury, was acquired post-injury, or if its impact has varied over time. Second, the relatively small control group sample limits our ability to examine whether the observed neural and emotional alterations are specific to an interaction between TBI and infection or reflect effects of the infection in isolation. Additionally, the modest overall sample size – particularly within subgroups – may have reduced our statistical power to detect subtle differences in cognitive performance and blood-based biomarkers. The reliance on peripheral biomarkers is also a limitation as it may not fully capture central nervous system-specific pathophysiological processes associated with T. gondii-related neurodegeneration. Furthermore, the spatial resolution of the MRI protocol used in this study (1 mm for T1-weighted; 2.5 mm for diffusion-weighted imaging) does not permit visualisation of individual T. gondii tissue cysts (~5–60 µm in diameter) [43, 44], precluding direct examination of the relationship between cyst location and regional white matter changes. Although future research is required to investigate whether white matter is directly altered by T. gondii cysts, previous histological studies in murine models have demonstrated that bradyzoite cysts reside almost exclusively within neurons in grey matter regions [43, 44]. As such, the observed white matter changes in this study may be more likely attributable to downstream processes associated with chronic infection, including neuroinflammation, astrocytic reactivity, disruption of perineuronal nets, and altered synaptic integrity, which can affect structural connectivity beyond the immediate site of cyst residence [45]. It should also be noted that the white matter changes observed in our study were predominantly lateralised to the right hemisphere. Considering the heterogeneous nature of TBI and lack of evidence for a T. gondii hemispheric preference, this finding was unexpected and requires further investigation. Notwithstanding these limitations, the robust effect sizes observed in key neural and emotional outcomes underscore the potential importance of T. gondii infection as a critical modifier of TBI pathology, warranting further investigation in larger, longitudinal studies with direct measures of neuroinflammation.

In conclusion, this study provides novel evidence that chronic T. gondii infection is associated with altered brain structure and heightened anxiety symptoms in long-term TBI survivors. Future research should prioritise longitudinal studies to clarify the timing of infection relative to TBI and elucidate the causal pathophysiological mechanisms linking T. gondii infection, neuropathology, and emotional outcomes. If inflammation is confirmed as a central mediator, research should explore the utility of anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory interventions for mitigating the long-term consequences of infection after TBI. By highlighting the interplay between chronic T. gondii infection, cortical and subcortical brain structure, and anxiety outcomes, this study underscores the importance of considering infections as modifiers of TBI recovery and how this influences prognostic and treatment strategies.
