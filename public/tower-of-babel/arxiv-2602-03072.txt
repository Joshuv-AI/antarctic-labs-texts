Towards weak stratification for logics of definitions Last modified: August 10, 2026
Nathan Guermond

Abstract
The logic of definitions is a family of logics for encoding and reasoning about judgments, which are atomic predicates specified by inference rules. A definition associates an atomic predicate with a logical formula, which may itself depend on the predicate being defined. This leads to an apparent circularity which can be resolved by interpreting definitions as monotone fixed-point operators on terms, and which is enforced by imposing a stratification condition on definitions. In many instances, it is useful to consider definitions in which the predicate being defined appears negatively in the body of its definition. In the logic 𝒢 \mathcal{G} , underlying the Abella proof assistant, this is not allowed due to the stratification condition. One such application violating this condition is that of defining logical relations, which is a technique commonly used to prove properties about programming languages. Tiu has shown how to relax this stratification condition to allow for a broader body of definitions including that needed for logical relations. However, he only showed how to extend a core fragment of 𝒢 \mathcal{G} with the weakened stratification condition, resulting in a logic he called LD . In this work we show that the weakened stratification condition is also compatible with generic (nabla) quantification and general induction. The eventual aim of this work is to justify an extension of the Abella proof assistant allowing for such definitions.

The logic of definitions is a family of logics for encoding and reasoning

When specifying definitions, one notices that the body of a definition is allowed to depend on the predicate being defined, which may lead to an apparent circularity. Indeed, failing to impose restrictions on the types of definitions we are allowed to make can lead to inconsistencies. For example, allowing the definition

In this work, we take this idea further by showing that the weakened stratification condition is also compatible with the nabla quantifier and inductive definitions in a logic we call
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
. The eventual aim of this work is to justify an extension of the Abella proof assistant

allowing for weakly stratified definitions. Since Abella is based on the logic
\mathcal{G}
introduced in
[
7

, fully justifying this extension would require further extending
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}

The nabla quantifier (
\nabla
), first introduced by Miller and Tiu in
[
13
]
and refined in
[
18
]
, was introduced in order to reason about names, which are modeled by

, and freshness, which is modeled by the nabla quantifier. For a judgment
\nabla x.J
to be derivable means that
J[n/x]
has a derivation
\Pi[n/x]
for a generic nominal constant

not appearing in
J
. In particular, this allows us to conclude that
J[t/x]
is uniformly derivable by the derivation
\Pi[t/x]
for any term
t
. This differs from the meaning of a universally quantified judgment
\forall x.J
, which asserts that
J[t/x]
has a derivation
\Pi_{t}
for every term
t
, but cannot guarantee the uniformity of the derivations.

In a similar vein, inductive definitions were introduced by Momigliano and Tiu in
[
15
]
to allow us to reason about the least fixed-point of a definition. For example, to model the least fixed-point
p
of an operator
B
(eg. the operator for
list
in (
1
)), it should be the case that for any predicate
S
such that

\forall X.B\ S\ X\supset S\ X

is provable (ie.
S
is a pre-fixed-point of
B
), and for which
S\ t\supset C
is provable, then
p\ t\supset C
should be provable. In
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
we will introduce a general rule capturing this idea. This is in contrast to Tiu’s
LD
, which supports natural number induction, but does not support induction in this general sense.

In order for a logic to be useful, we must first show that it is
consistent
, which means that not all formulas are provable. To show consistency of
LD
, Tiu first constructs a ground version of the logic,
\text{LD}_{\infty}
, which means that no eigenvariables (free variables) appear in the derivations. He then defines an interpretation of
LD
into
\text{LD}_{\infty}
, which shows that if
\text{LD}_{\infty}
is inconsistent, then
LD
must be as well. This reduces the problem to proving the consistency of
\text{LD}_{\infty}
which he does by using the cut elimination technique. The aim of this method is to show that any derivation making use of the
cut
rule can be transformed into a
cut-free
derivation, which allow us to locally decide the provability of certain formulas, including
\bot
. This is done by defining a
cut reduction
relation, which can be shown to be well founded. The technique for doing this is known as the Tait-Martin-Löf reducibility technique
[
16
,
10
]
, which was adapted to the logic of definitions by McDowell and Miller
[
11
]
. Following Tiu’s work, we define a ground version of
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
called
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
and prove cut-elimination for the latter, thus allowing us to conclude that
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
is consistent.

Our main contribution is to show that
LD
extended
with nabla quantification and inductive definitions is consistent,
which we show by adapting Tiu’s method of eliminating cut in a ground logic.
Doing this required resolving
weak stratification with inductive definitions using the ideas
of Momigliano and Tiu
[
15
]
, which assume strict stratification
on both generic and inductive definitions. Our results show that this
can be done so long as the strict stratification condition is
maintained on inductive definitions. We show by means of a counterexample
that allowing weakly stratified inductive definitions may lead to an
inconsistency.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows. In section
2
, we introduce the logic
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
, which we wish to prove consistent. In section
3
, we give examples of definitions requiring weak stratification, as well as a counter example justifying the restriction to strict stratification for inductive definitions. In section
4
we introduce a ground version of the logic,
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
. We show in section
4.1
that
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
may be interpreted in
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
, allowing us to conclude that the consistency of
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
may be reduced to the consistency of
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
. In section
5
, we define the cut reduction relation on ground derivations, and in section
6
we introduce the intermediate notion of reducibility and prove that all ground derivations are reducible. We put these results together to prove the cut elimination theorem in
6.3
. In section
7
we show how to apply this result to obtain consistency of
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
, as well as an application to proof search. We conclude in section
8
with a discussion of related work and future directions.

2
The logic
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}

In this section we define the logic
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
, which is an extension of the first order sequent calculus implicitly parametrized by a set of definitions
\mathcal{D}
and a set of inductive definitions
\mathcal{I}
. First, we define the syntax of terms and formulas in the logic, and what constitutes a valid sequent. Second, we describe a core fragment of the logic, consisting of logical inference rules, followed by the structural, axiom, and multicut rules. Then, we specify what constitutes a valid set of definitions
\mathcal{D}
, as well as the inference rules associated with
\mathcal{D}
. Finally, we specify what constitutes a valid set of inductive definitions
\mathcal{I}
, with its associated inference rules. The logic
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
, which is implicitly parametrized by the sets
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{I}
, is precisely summed up at the end of this section. We observe that
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
is an extension of Tiu’s
LD
with nabla quantification and inductive definitions. It can also be viewed as a core fragment of the logic
\mathcal{G}

[
7
]
, which underlies Abella, extended with more permissive definitions.

2.5

3
Examples

We have already seen that inconsistencies may appear in the logic of definitions as soon as the predicate being defined appears negatively in the body of its definition. A sufficient condition to prevent this is the stratification condition (
3
), but the aim of this work is weaken this to the weak stratification condition (
2
) to allow for a broader body of definitions. In this section, we will see examples of definitions which are weakly but not strictly stratified, thus demonstrating the usefulness of such definitions. Our first example (from
[
19
]
) gives a simple but illustrative example for defining even numbers. Our second example motivates weak stratification as a means of encoding logical equivalence, a special case of a more general method to prove properties about programs known as logical relations
[
9
]
. Finally, we give a counter-example demonstrating that weak stratification is not a sufficient condition for inductive definitions to be consistent, thus justifying our restriction on inductive definitions.

In addition to the examples given in this section, we note that weakly stratified definitions may be used to encode the Gödel-Gentzen translation, as well as Kolmogorov’s double negation translation of classical logic in intuitionistic logic, as described in
[
19
]
. As pointed out in
[
3
]
, another useful application of such definitions is for the formalization of Tait-style
[
16
,
8
]
strong normalization arguments. A more elaborate example application of a logical relations argument may be found in
[
20
]
, which implicitly uses weak stratification to define a step-indexed logical equivalence relation to prove correctness of compiler transformations for functional programs.

3.1
Even numbers

We consider the following definition recognizing all even natural numbers by first specifying the following signature over the base type
\mathrm{tm}
:

We then specify the definition clauses as follows

appears negatively in the body of its own definition. However, it can be weakly stratified by assigning the levels

for any ground

and

4.1

in
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}

The goal is now to define an interpretation of
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
in
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}

\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
to the derivability of a ground sequent in
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}

If

\mathcal{Y}
-grounding substitution

to be empty. We only show the cases for

and

\forall\mathcal{R}

4.2
The consistency of
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
implies consistency of

As a result of the grounding lemma, we obtain the following

5
Cut reductions for
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}

We now define the cut reduction relation on derivations in
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
. This specifies how the cut rule may be propagated upwards in a derivation tree. In section
6
we will see how this relation can be used to obtain cut-free derivations.

Specifically, we define a
reduction
relation between derivations, following closely
the reduction relation in
[
11
]
. The
redex
, that is the derivation to be reduced, is always a derivation
\Xi
ending with the multicut rule

{\frac{{\begin{array}[]{c}\Pi_{1}\\
\Delta_{1}\vdash B_{1}\end{array}}\quad\cdots\quad{\begin{array}[]{c}\Pi_{n}\\
\Delta_{n}\vdash B_{n}\end{array}}\quad{\begin{array}[]{c}\Pi\\
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n},\Gamma\vdash C\end{array}}}{\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n},\Gamma\vdash C}\quad\textsc{right=$\textit{mc}$ }}

We refer to the formulas
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
in the multicut as

. If a left or right rule introduces a cut formula, we say the rule is
principal
. If
\Pi
reduces to
\Pi^{\prime}

\Pi^{\prime}
is a

of
\Pi
, and we may write
\Pi\to\Pi^{\prime}
.

Specifically, the reduction relation relates a derivation
\Xi
ending with the multicut rule

\Xi^{\prime}
, which may again end with a multicut which we will see later is always of smaller complexity. The reduction relation is specified by case analysis on the last rule of

and
\Pi_{i}
for a chosen cut formula

Essential cases
:

An essential case occurs when

\Pi
ends with a left introduction rule. For example, here is the case when
\Xi

\Pi_{1}

\Pi
ends with
\wedge\mathcal{L}i

If

and

{\frac{\Delta_{1}\vdash B_{1}^{1}\Pi_{1}^{1}\quad\Delta_{1}\vdash B_{1}^{2}\Pi_{1}^{2}}{\Delta_{1}\vdash B_{1}^{1}\wedge B_{1}^{2}}\quad\textsc{right=$\wedge\mathcal{R}$ }}{\frac{B_{1}^{i},B_{2},\ldots,B_{n},\Gamma\vdash C\Pi^{\prime}}{B_{1}^{1}\wedge B_{1}^{2},B_{2},\ldots,B_{n},\Gamma\vdash C}\quad\textsc{right=$\wedge\mathcal{L}i$ }}

{\frac{{{\begin{array}[]{c}\Pi_{1}^{i}\\

A left commutative case occurs when

Left multicut case

This is the case in which
\Pi_{i}

\Pi

\Pi_{i}

\Pi

We now state and sketch a proof of the main lemma needed to prove normalizability of derivations in

Case
n=0
: In this case
\Xi
reduces to
\Pi
, so it suffices to show that
\Pi
is reducible. We use the following trick: for any derivation
\Psi
with height less than
0pt(\Pi)
, induction hypothesis
C
applies to the nullary multicut
mc(\Psi)
, and since this reduces to
\Psi
it must be that
\Psi
is reducible. It is then shown by case analysis on the last rule of
\Pi
that
\Pi
is reducible.

Case
n>0
:
We proceed by subordinate induction on the

(4)
\alpha_{i}
-reducibility of
\Pi_{i}
for
i=1\ldots n
, and proceed by case analysis on the reduction of
\Xi
. We argue that this applies to each of the cases described in Section
5
.

Essential cases
:
In each of these cases, the reduction decreases the height of the major premise derivation
\Pi
, allowing us to apply the induction hypothesis
C
. We show the
\wedge\mathcal{R}
/
\wedge\mathcal{L}i
case below as example:

If
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi
are

{\frac{\Delta_{1}\vdash B_{1}^{1}\Pi_{1}^{1}\quad\Delta_{1}\vdash B_{1}^{2}\Pi_{1}^{2}}{\Delta_{1}\vdash B_{1}^{1}\land B_{1}^{2}}\quad\textsc{right=$\wedge\mathcal{R}$ }}{\frac{B_{1}^{i},B_{2},\ldots,B_{n},\Gamma\vdash C\Pi^{\prime}}{B_{1}^{1}\land B_{1}^{2},B_{2},\ldots,B_{n},\Gamma\vdash C}\quad\textsc{right=$\wedge\mathcal{L}i$ }}

then the reduction

is reducible from the induction hypothesis

because
\Pi_{1}^{i}
is reducible and
0pt(\Pi^{\prime})<0pt(\Pi)
.

Inductive cases
:

For this case to hold, we notice first that the reduction decreases the height of the major premise derivation
\Pi
, so by induction hypothesis
C
it suffices to show that the unfolded derivation
\mu(\Pi_{i},\Pi_{S})
is reducible whenever
\Pi_{i}
is. This step depends on showing that for any reducible derivation
\Upsilon

mc
(\Upsilon,\textsc{Init})
is reducible, which crucially depends on induction hypothesis
C
.

Left commutative cases
:

In the case of a left commutative reduction, the multicut is pushed above the last rule of a minor premise
\Pi_{i}
. In particular, the new multicut involves a major premise
\Pi_{i}^{\prime}
of
\Pi_{i}
, which is reducible because
\Pi_{i}
is reducible. Thus the new multicut
mc
(\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{i}^{\prime},\ldots,\Pi_{n},\Pi)
is reducible by the induction hypothesis
C
.

Right commutative cases
:

In the case of a right commutative reduction, the multicut is pushed above the last rule of the major premise
\Pi
, so the height of the major premise is reduced. Thus the new multicut
mc
(\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{n},\Pi^{\prime})
is reducible by the induction hypothesis
C
.

Structural cases
:

In the case of a structural reduction, the height of the major premise is reduced. So even though the number of cut formulas may increase if
\Pi
ends with a contraction, we may use induction hypothesis
C
.

Left multicut case
:

Similarly to the left commutative cases, the minor premise
\Pi_{i}
reduces to a reducible derivation
\Pi_{i}^{\prime}
, thus the reduct
mc
(\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{i}^{\prime},\ldots,\Pi_{n},\Pi)
is reducible by the induction hypothesis
C
.

Right multicut case
:

In this case, the multicut in the major premise
\Pi
is merged with the multicut in
\Xi
, but since the height of the major premise derivation decreases, induction hypothesis
C
may be applied to obtain the reducibility of the reduct.

Left axiom case
:

In this case, the number of cut formulas decreases, allowing us to apply induction hypothesis
C
.

Right axiom case
:

In this case, the multicut
mc
(\Pi_{1},\Pi)
reduces to a nominal renaming
\Pi_{1}^{\prime}
of
\Pi_{1}
. Since renaming preserves reducibility, and
\Pi_{1}
is reducible, the reduct is reducible.

∎

6.3
Cut elimination

We now proceed to show how the multicut rule may be eliminated from any
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
derivation.

Corollary 1
.

Every derivation
\Pi
in
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
is reducible.

Proof.

Consider the nullary multicut
mc(\Pi)
, which is reducible by Lemma
5
. Since
mc(\Pi)
reduces to
\Pi
it follows that
\Pi
is reducible.
∎

Lemma 6

(Normal form lemma)
.

If a derivation
\Pi
of a sequent
\Gamma\vdash C
is normalizable, then there exists a cut-free derivation
\hat{\Pi}
of
\Gamma\vdash C
, which we call a
normal form
for
\Pi
.

Proof.

By induction on normalizability. If
\Pi
ends with a cut, first note that there necessarily exists a reduction
\Pi\to\Pi^{\prime}
, and furthermore that
\Pi^{\prime}
is normalizable. By the induction hypothesis,
\Pi^{\prime}
has a cut-free normal form
\hat{\Pi}^{\prime}
, which is also a cut-free normal form for
\Pi
.

If
\Pi
ends with any other rule
\circ
, then each of its premise derivations

\left\{{\begin{array}[]{c}\Pi_{i}\\
\Gamma_{i}\vdash C_{i}\end{array}}\right\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}

is normalizable, and therefore has a cut-free normal form
\{\hat{\Pi}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
of the same sequent. It follows that there exists a cut-free derivation
\hat{\Pi}
obtained by applying
\circ
to the premise derivations
\{\hat{\Pi}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
.
∎

Since every derivation in
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
is reducible by Corollary
1
, reducibility implies normalizability by Lemma
4
, and a normalizable derivation has a cut-free normal form by the previous lemma, we obtain our main theorem

Theorem 1

(Cut admissibility for
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
)
.

Every derivation of a ground sequent
\Gamma\vdash C
in
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
admits a cut-free derivation of the same sequent.

Proof.

By the previous observation, any derivation making use of cut is normalizable, and therefore has a cut-free normal form by Lemma
6
.
∎

We now obtain the following important consequences of the cut elimination theorem.

Corollary 2

(Consistency of
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
)
.

\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
is consistent.

Proof.

It suffices to notice by case analysis that there are no cut-free derivations of
\vdash\bot
. Since every derivation has a cut-free normal form by Theorem

,
\vdash\bot
is not derivable in

.
∎

Corollary 3

(Consistency of
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
)
.

\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
is consistent.

Proof.

This follows from the interpretation lemma (Lemma

) and the consistency of
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
.
∎

7.2
Proof search

An important consequence of the cut admissibility theorem is the following. A rule is
invertible
(or
asynchronous
) if the cut-free derivability of its conclusion implies the cut-free derivability of its premises. Furthermore, we say that a clause
H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{0.5}{\mbox{$\Delta$}}}}}{{=}}B
is invertible if
H
is not unifiable with any other clause head
H^{\prime}
. The following corollary gives us completeness guarantees for proof search procedures in
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
.

Corollary 4

(Inversion theorem for
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
)
.

The logic
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
satisfies the following meta-theoretic properties:

1.

The rules

,

,

are invertible.

2.

If a clause

is invertible, then its corresponding
\Delta\mathcal{R}
rule is invertible.

3.

When the context is empty, we obtain the following partial invertability results for the rules

\exists\mathcal{R}
, and
\Delta\mathcal{R}

(a)

If

or

(b)

(c)

and

Proof.

This makes essential use of the cut admissibility property.

For (

), we only show the case of

8
Related and future work

In this work, we’ve shown how to extend the logic
LD
to a new logic
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
with inductive definitions and nabla quantification, which we’ve proven to be consistent. The addition of weak stratification allows us to make definitions commonly used to reason about computer programs, such as logical relations. The eventual aim of this work is to extend the logic
\mathcal{G}
, underlying Abella, with this broader body of definitions.

In another line of work towards this goal, Baelde and Nadathur have developed the
\mu\text{NJ}
calculus
[
3
]
, which is an extension of deduction modulo (see eg. Dowek
[
5
]
) with inductive and co-inductive definitions. Their work similarly shows that the same kind of definitions are permissible, but using a technique involving a rewriting system on propositions. Consistency is obtained by proving strong normalization for
\mu\text{NJ}
.

However, there are several future extensions to this work that seem more promising from the direction of
LD
. First, although
\mu\text{NJ}
includes co-induction, it is not clear how to define nominal abstraction in the natural deduction context, whereas we believe that adding co-induction and nominal abstraction to
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
will not add much complexity to our present work. Adding these features into the logic is a necessary step to fully justify extending the Abella proof assistant.

Second, we desire a cut elimination theorem for our target extension of
\mathcal{G}
. We saw with Corollary
4
that a cut elimination theorem allows us to obtain certain desirable properties about a logic. However, we are presently only able to obtain cut elimination for the ground logic
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}_{\infty}
, and not the logic
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
. Similarly,
\mu\text{NJ}
does not help us in this regard, since the structure of natural deduction derivations is quite different from that of the sequent calculus.

Third, we envision extending the logic to allow for higher order quantification. This would on the one hand allow for much greater generality in the statement of theorems and definitions, and on the other, the increased strength of our logic would allow us to prove consistency theorems for higher order logics. In this regard, the
\mu\text{NJ}
calculus’s introduction of rewriting into the system affects the intensional treatment of syntax that is crucial to preserving our desired interpretation of equality. This is not an immediate concern in a first order system for which rewriting is restricted to propositions. However, this leads to further complications for a higher order system.

In addition to these key research directions, we envision two other possibilities for future work. First, the logics
LD
and
\text{LD}^{\mu\nabla}
both require restricting definition clauses
H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{0.5}{\mbox{$\Delta$}}}}}{{=}}_{\mathcal{X}}B
to those in which
H
must lie in the
L_{\lambda}
pattern fragment. We believe this is not an essential restriction, and that our current proof can be adapted by a slight modification of the ground
\Delta\mathcal{L}
rule to remove this restriction. Finally, we aspire to eventually formalize this work. One could imagine formalizing this work inside the logic we are defining itself, which, in order to define reducibility as we are using it would require a notion of induction compatible with weak stratification. Note that à priori this poses no Gödelian circularities since the logic of definitions is not a single logic, but a family of logics indexed by a set of definitions whose stratification must be proven at the meta-theoretic level.

Appendix C
The reducibility lemma

See
5

Proof.

We proceed first by induction on the

(1) index
\mathrm{Ind}(\Pi)
, then on the

(2) height
0pt(\Pi)
, then by subordinate induction on the

(3) number of cut formulas
n
.

Case
n=0
: In this case
\Xi
reduces to
\Pi
, so it suffices to show that
\Pi
is reducible. We use the following trick: for any derivation
\Psi
with height less than
0pt(\Pi)
, the second induction hypothesis applies to the multicut
mc(\Psi)
, and since this reduces to
\Psi
it must be that
\Psi
is reducible. We can now show
\Pi
is reducible by case analysis on
\Pi
:

Subcase
\supset\!\!\mathcal{R}
: Suppose
\Pi
is a derivation of
\Gamma\vdash A\supset B
with premise derivation
\Pi^{\prime}
, let
\alpha=\mathrm{lvl}(A)
, and let
\Upsilon
be an
\alpha
-reducible derivation of
\Delta\vdash A
, then since
0pt(\Pi^{\prime})<0pt(\Pi)
,
\Pi^{\prime}
is reducible, and furthermore the second induction hypothesis applies to
mc(\Upsilon,\Pi^{\prime})
, so
\Pi
is reducible.

Subcase
\supset\!\!\mathcal{L}
: If
\Pi
ends with
\supset\!\!\mathcal{L}
with major premise derivation
\Pi^{\prime}
and minor premise derivation
\Psi
, then since
0pt(\Pi^{\prime}),0pt(\Psi)<0pt(\Pi)
, both
\Psi
and
\Pi^{\prime}
are reducible, and by the normalization lemma (Lemma
4
)
\Psi
must be normalizable, thus
\Pi
is reducible.

Subcase
\mu\mathcal{L}
: If
\Pi
ends with
\mu\mathcal{L}
with major premise derivation
\Pi^{\prime}
and minor premise derivations
\{\Pi_{\vec{u}}\}_{\mathrm{ground}(\vec{u})}
, then since
0pt(\Pi^{\prime}),0pt(\Pi_{\vec{u}})<0pt(\Pi)
, all
\Pi_{\vec{u}}
and
\Pi^{\prime}
are reducible, and by the normalization lemma (Lemma
4
)
\Pi_{\vec{u}}
must be normalizable, thus
\Pi
is reducible.

Subcase
mc
: If
\Pi
is a multicut

, then each
\Psi_{i}
is reducible since
0pt(\Psi_{i})<0pt(\Pi)
, so the second induction hypothesis applies.

Otherwise: If
\Pi
has premise derivations
\{\Psi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
, then since
0pt(\Psi_{i})<0pt(\Pi)
then
\Psi_{i}
are reducible and so is
\Pi
.

Lemma 12

(Inductive definitions are fixed-point definitions)
.

Given an inductive definition

with inductive invariant
S=B\ p
as follows

\frac{B\ (B\ p)\ \vec{x}\vdash B\ p\ \vec{x}\quad\Gamma,B\ p\ \vec{t}\vdash C}{\Gamma,p\ \vec{t}\vdash C}\quad\textsc{right=$\mu\mathcal{L}$ }

The derivability of
B\ (B\ p)\ \vec{x}\vdash B\ p\ \vec{x}
is obtained from the following sublemma:

Sublemma 13
.

Suppose
B
is an operator of type
\omega\to\omega
such that
p
does not occur negatively in the formula
B\ p\ \vec{x}
, and suppose
S:\omega
satisfies
S\ \vec{t}\vdash p\ \vec{t}
for any
\vec{t}
, then
B\ S\ \vec{x}\vdash B\ p\ \vec{x}
is derivable.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the complexity of
B\ p\ \vec{x}
. We only consider the following cases.

p\ \vec{t}
: In this case,
B\ S\ \vec{x}\equiv S\ \vec{t}
, so the derivation of
S\ \vec{t}\vdash p\ \vec{t}
is obtained by assumption.

B_{1}\ p\ \vec{x}\wedge B_{2}\ p\ \vec{x}
: In this case, the derivation is obtained as follows

\frac{\frac{B_{1}\ S\ \vec{x}\vdash B_{1}\ p\ \vec{x}\hskip 8.19447ptB_{2}\ S\ \vec{x}\vdash B_{2}\ p\ \vec{x}}{B_{1}\ S\ \vec{x},B_{2}\ S\ \vec{x}\vdash B_{1}\ p\ \vec{x}\wedge B_{2}\ p\ \vec{x}}\quad\textsc{Right=$\wedge\mathcal{R}$ }}{B_{1}\ S\ \vec{x}\wedge B_{2}\ S\ \vec{x}\vdash B_{1}\ p\ \vec{x}\wedge B_{2}\ p\ \vec{x}}\quad\textsc{right=$\wedge\mathcal{L}*$ }

where
B_{i}\ S\ \vec{x}\vdash B_{i}\ p\ \vec{x}
are obtained from the induction hypotheses.

B_{1}\ \vec{x}\supset B_{2}\ p\ \vec{x}
: In this case, the derivation is obtained as follows

\frac{\frac{\frac{}{B_{1}\ \vec{x}\vdash B_{1}\ \vec{x}}\hskip 8.19447pt\textsc{right={Init} }\hskip 8.19447pt\frac{B_{2}\ S\ \vec{x}\vdash B_{2}\ p\ \vec{x}}{B_{2}\ S\ \vec{x},B_{1}\ \vec{x}\vdash B_{2}\ p\ \vec{x}}\hskip 8.19447pt\textsc{Right=w$\mathcal{L}$ }}{B_{1}\ \vec{x}\supset B_{2}\ S\ \vec{x},B_{1}\ \vec{x}\vdash B_{2}\ p\ \vec{x}}\quad\textsc{Right=$\supset\!\!\mathcal{L}$, rightskip=3em, leftskip=3em}}{B_{1}\ \vec{x}\supset B_{2}\ S\ \vec{x}\vdash B_{1}\ \vec{x}\supset B_{2}\ p\ \vec{x}}\quad\textsc{right=$\supset\!\!\mathcal{R}$ }

where
B_{2}\ S\ \vec{x}\vdash B_{2}\ p\ \vec{x}
is obtained from the induction hypothesis.

\forall_{\alpha}y.B^{\prime}\ y\ p\ \vec{x}
: In this case, the derivation is obtained as follows

\frac{\left\{\raisebox{-10.00002pt}{$\frac{B^{\prime}\ t\ S\ \vec{x}\vdash B^{\prime}\ t\ p\ \vec{x}}{\forall y.B^{\prime}\ y\ S\ \vec{x}\vdash B^{\prime}\ t\ p\ \vec{x}}\quad\textsc{right=$\forall\mathcal{L}$ }$
}\right\}_{t\in\mathrm{ground}(\alpha)}}{\forall y.B^{\prime}\ y\ S\ \vec{x}\vdash\forall y.B^{\prime}\ y\ p\ \vec{x}}\quad\textsc{right=$\forall\mathcal{R}$ }

where
(B^{\prime}\ t)\ S\ \vec{x}\vdash(B^{\prime}\ t)\ p\ \vec{x}
is obtained from the induction hypothesis, noting that substituting
t
for
y
in
(B^{\prime}\ t)
does not increase the complexity of the formula.
∎

We apply the lemma by choosing
B=B
, and
S=(B\ p)
. Note that the stratification condition on inductive definitions guarantees that
p
does not occur negatively in
B\ p
. Furthermore, the condition on
S
is obtained as follows

\frac{\frac{}{B\ p\ \vec{t}\vdash B\ p\ \vec{t}}\quad\textsc{Right={Init} }}{B\ p\ \vec{t}\vdash p\ \vec{t}}\quad\textsc{right=$\mu\mathcal{R}$ }

∎

D.2
Uniqueness of definition instances

We make the following observation from
[
19
]
which makes use of the requirement that the head
H
of a definition
H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{0.5}{\mbox{$\Delta$}}}}}{{=}}B
be in the pattern fragment.

Lemma 14

(Uniqueness of definition instances)
.

Given a definition
H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{0.5}{\mbox{$\Delta$}}}}}{{=}}_{\mathcal{X}}B
satisfying
\mathrm{defn}(H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{0.5}{\mbox{$\Delta$}}}}}{{=}}B,A,\theta,B_{1})
and
\mathrm{defn}(H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scalebox{0.5}{\mbox{$\Delta$}}}}}{{=}}B,A,\theta,B_{2})
such that
H\rho_{1}=A\theta=H\rho_{2}
, then
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}
. In particular this implies that
B_{1}=B_{2}
.

Proof.

We first recall the domains
\theta:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Y}
and
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{X}
. We can then consider the problem of finding substitution
\rho:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{X}
unifying
A\theta
and
H
. Since
\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{Z}=\emptyset
, and
H
is in the pattern fragment, we know that their exists a unique most general unifier
\rho
(see
[
12
]
).
In particular, since
\rho
is a ground substitution with respect to
\mathcal{X}
, it is the unique solution and thus
\rho=\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}
. Finally, we note that
B_{1}=B\rho_{1}=B\rho_{2}=B_{2}
, as desired.
∎
