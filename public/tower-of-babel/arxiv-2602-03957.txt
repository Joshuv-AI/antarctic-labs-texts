Child Mortality Prediction in Bangladesh: A Decade-Long Validation Study
Md Muhtasim Munif Fahim, Dr. Md Rezaul Karim

Abstract
Background: Predictive machine learning models for child mortality suffer from look-ahead bias when validated using random cross-validation, leading to overoptimistic performance estimates that fail in real-world deployment on future populations. Methods: We used Demographic and Health Surveys data from Bangladesh (2011–2022; n=33,962 births) with strict temporal validation: training on 2011–2014 data, validation on 2017 data, and testing on completely held-out 2022 data. We employed genetic algorithm-based Neural Architecture Search to optimize model architecture and conducted comprehensive fairness audits across Bangladesh’s eight administrative divisions. Findings: Neural Architecture Search identified a simple single-layer architecture (64 units) that significantly outperformed XGBoost (AUROC 0·76 vs. 0·73; p < < 0·01). We discovered a ”Socioeconomic Predictive Gradient” with strong negative correlation (r=-0·62) between regional wealth and model performance. The model achieved highest discrimination in poorest divisions (AUROC 0·74, Sylhet/Rangpur) versus wealthiest regions (AUROC 0·66, Dhaka/Khulna). At 10% screening threshold, our model would identify approximately 1,300 additional at-risk children annually compared to gradient boosting approaches. Interpretation: The equity gradient suggests the model identifies structural, preventable mortality risk factors in under-resourced settings, while wealthier regions show more stochastic patterns. This provides a production-ready tool for targeted maternal and child health interventions in resource-constrained settings. Funding: None. Keywords: Neural Architecture Search; Child Mortality; Temporal Validation; Bangladesh; Machine Learning; Public Health; Deep Learning.

Introduction

Although Bangladesh has experienced one of the most significant declines in under-five mortality (a decline of approx. 70%) globally since 1990
9
,
12
, there are still many disparities present within this overall improvement
5
,
17
,
13
. To identify which children are at greater risk for mortality, therefore, requires the use of predictive algorithms capable of analyzing multiple interacting risk factors
18
,
23
. However, classical frameworks for understanding the relationship between risk factors and mortality rely upon the assumption that the relationship is constant or stable, a relationship that does not hold in rapidly developing countries undergoing rapid changes in the economy, health care, and demographics, thus changing who is dying and why
2
,
14
.

Machine learning, on the other hand, allows researchers to model complex, non-linear interactions among many risk factors and to predict outcomes with precision
10
. However, there is growing concern about the application of machine learning to problems in global health—specifically, a ”validation” problem
21
. Random k-fold cross-validation, for example, randomly assigns all available observations to either a training set or a test set. This approach ignores the order in which the data were collected and allows future data to be used for model training. Thus, when evaluating the model’s performance on the test set, it appears highly accurate yet fails miserably when deployed in real time—an issue we refer to as ”look-ahead bias,” which results in overestimation of accuracy
21
,
24
.

To address this, we restrict our evaluation to strictly temporally separate sets of data: we trained our models only on data collected from 2011 to 2014, validated them with the data collected in 2017 while testing them only on completely withheld data collected in 2022 – an eight-year period of time that is representative of how long it would take for new models to be developed, tested and implemented in real-world settings.

The 2022 test set also served as an unplanned ”stress test” for our models, as the COVID-19 pandemic occurred during this period. In addition to employing Neural Architecture Search
27
via genetic algorithm
11
, we employed domain-informed feature engineering to develop our models.

As part of our assessment of the geographic fairness of our models, we observed a consistent predictability gradient: socioeconomic predictors achieved stronger discriminative performance in less affluent divisions. This pattern held across all eight divisions (r = -0·62), with the two wealthiest regions (Dhaka and Khulna) showing the lowest AUROCs and explain it through the lens of epidemiologic transition theory
19
—as populations become wealthier and undergo development, mortality becomes inherently more difficult to predict based upon socioeconomic factors and therefore require differing types of algorithmic expectations and interventions.

2
Methods

2.1
Study Design and Data Sources

Our study is based on a retrospective cohort analysis using Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS) data collected at four different times (2011, 2014, 2017, and 2022) and include 7,601, 6,779, 8,044, and 11,538 births, respectively. After excluding births that did not survive and births with missing survival information, there were a total of 33,962 births available for analysis in our study sample. The primary measure of the outcome of interest (under-five mortality) is defined as the mortality of a child within the first five years of life and is measured as a binary variable (survived/not survived), based on the mother’s recall of her child’s birth history.

Figure 1:
Framework for Study Methodology.
A detailed workflow that shows the pipeline for predicting child mortality. (A) Data Acquisition: Four demographic surveys (n=33,962) were harmonized. (B) Feature Engineering: 31 predictors based on the literature are derived. (C) Temporal Validation: Strict deployment simulation training using data from 2011 to 2014 and testing using the unobserved 2022 cohort. (D) Model Development: Neural Architecture Search (NAS) is the result of benchmarking statistical and deep learning techniques.

To simulate real-world testing scenarios, in which models will be projected onto future populations using only historical data, we divided the BDHS data temporally, so that no information would leak from the training to the evaluation periods:

•

Training Set:
2011 and 2014 DHS combined (total = 14,380), representing children born approximately 2006–2014.

•

Validation Set:
2017 DHS (total = 8,044), representing children born approximately 2012–2017.

•

Test Set:
2022 DHS (total = 11,538), representing children born approximately 2017–2022.

By combining the 5-year birth history recall window of the DHS surveys with the temporal division of the surveys to create separate training and test sets, we achieved a 8-year temporal separation between the training and test sets. This temporal separation provides strong validation that many other machine learning studies in global health have not achieved.

Feature Engineering

Because the DHS surveys contain multiple high-cardinality variables that have previously been shown to have non-linear associations with child mortality

This domain-driven transformation achieved two objectives simultaneously. First, the transformation encoded the model’s inductive bias, leveraging prior epidemiologic knowledge about child survival. Therefore, the model was designed to identify clinically relevant patterns established over decades of child survival research. Second, the transformation reduced the dimensionality of the 50+ raw survey variables to 31 engineered features, which preserved the interpretability of the model results for both clinical and policy audiences.

2.3
Neural Architecture Search

Instead of manually selecting hyperparameters for a neural network using a researcher’s intuition or a random search, we employed a genetic algorithm to search the architecture space
11
systematically. The search space consisted of:

•

Network Depth:
1 to 5 Hidden Layers

•

Layer Width:
16, 32, 64, or 128 Units Per Layer

•

Activation Functions:
ReLU, ELU, SELU, or Tanh

•

Regularization:
Dropout Rate Uniformly Sampled Between 0 and 0·5; Batch Normalization Enabled or Disabled

Optimization occurred iteratively. The population of candidate architectures was randomly initialized at 20 and then optimized through training of the architecture for 30 epochs on the validation dataset; the candidates were ranked based upon their fit, defined as AUROC; the five highest performing candidate architectures were selected as elite for the next generation; crossover (the combination of elements between two successful candidates) and mutation (a random element is added/removed at a rate of 0·1 to create new candidate architectures) were used to generate a new population of candidate architectures; and this evolutionary process continued for 15 generations. The best-performing candidate was retrained for 50 epochs beyond the search time, with early stopping.

The architecture, found to be optimal, was surprisingly simple: one hidden layer with 64 units, ELU activation, a 30% dropout rate, and batch normalization. The simplicity of the design indicates that the domain-informed feature sets provided most or all of the necessary structure in the data, and therefore the simpler architectures achieved maximal performance without the need for the additional layers that are so often necessary to prevent overfitting.

Neural Architecture Search Process and Optimal Architecture.
(A) Evolutionary Progress: The optimization of the Genetic Algorithm over 15 generations, displaying the average candidate fitness convergence (dashed line) and the identification of the optimal architecture (solid line, AUC=0.766). (B) Optimal Architecture: The neural network topology that was found to maximize generalization on tabular epidemiological data. 2.4
Comparison Models and Evaluation

A variety of standard baseline models were compared with the NAS-optimized model: logistic regression with L2 regularization; random forest (with 500 decision trees); XGBoost; LightGBM; TabNet (an attention-based neural network)
3
; and ResNet (a deep residual neural network
8
. Each of these models was optimized using a genetic algorithm with a computational budget equal to that of the NAS model (15 generations, 30 members per generation, selection based upon validation AUROC). The search space for both XGBoost and LightGBM consisted of: n_estimators (50–500); max_depth (2–15); learning_rate (0·001–0·3); subsample (0·5–1·0); and L1 and L2 regularization parameters.

2.5
Additional Analyses

•

Class imbalance:
The positive class is a minority (
\sim
4%). We addressed this by training using class-weighted loss functions (Cost-Sensitive Learning), which was found to optimize discrimination effectively without synthesizing artificial data.

•

Calibration:
Calibration was assessed using the Brier Score and reliability diagrams. A logistic regression re-calibration method called Platt Scaling was used to correct the systematic overconfidence in the predicted probabilities.

•

Geographic Equity:
To determine whether predictability varies across geographic areas, we ran discrimination models on the 8 administrative divisions of Bangladesh and on rural/urban residences. We calculated Pearson Correlation Coefficients to assess how closely the predictive performance aligns with regional wealth indicators.

•

Model Interpretability:
We used the KernelExplainer algorithm to compute SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values to provide a quantitative estimate of the contribution of features to the prediction results and to verify the biological plausibility of the relationships learned from data.

•

Sensitivity Analyses:
We performed three sensitivity analyses: i. Design-aware Bootstrapping (B=1000) to generate 95% Confidence Intervals. ii. Threshold Sensitivity Analysis to optimize the F1-score trade-off using the validation set. iii. Feature Permutation Importance relative to the top predictors to verify model robustness.

3.2
Geographic Fairness Analysis: Equity Gradient

We believe that the ”equity gradient” is a useful quality. Structural, preventable causes such as poverty, sanitation etc. explain most of the variation in mortality in poor regions with low predictive error due to these being measured and recorded in surveys. In richer regions such as Dhaka, Khulna that are also very medicalized
22
,
1
, the basic needs for survival have been met and residual mortality is explained by stochastic biological events (e.g. congenital anomalies) or unmeasured clinical complications that do not appear in demographic surveillance.

3.3
Model Calibration and Sensitivity Analyses

Although the NAS model showed excellent discrimination, the initial estimates of predicted probability were poorly calibrated. Platt scaling
20
(i.e., logistic recalibration) greatly improved the Brier score, from 0·160 (un-calibrated) to 0·029 (calibrated)—a significant 82% improvement in calibration. Calibration did not vary significantly across administrative divisions after Platt scaling, with post-calibration Brier scores ranging from 0·021 (Khulna) to 0·042 (Sylhet). Cross-regionally stable calibration is a very important feature of the model as it suggests that the same model trained using national level data can generate accurate probability estimates across geographically diverse regions—without having to be recalibrated for each specific region.

Figure 4:
Model Calibration Curves.
Calibration plots comparing predicted probabilities to observed mortality rates. (A) Before Platt scaling showing overconfidence. (B) After Platt scaling showing improved calibration across deciles.

To mitigate the possible limitations related to the complex design of the survey, we calculated weighted AUROC that incorporates DHS sampling weights via design-aware bootstrap (i.e., resampling primary sampling units within strata). We found that the weighted AUROC (0·721 [95% CI: 0·693 – 0·752]) was nearly identical to the un-weighted AUROC (0·721). This minimal difference indicates that individual-level prediction estimates generated using un-weighted data are robust to the complex survey sampling design—which is expected theoretically when the objective of a prediction model is to estimate conditional probabilities rather than population prevalence.

We also conducted additional sensitivity analyses to assess potential limitations associated with follow-up and censoring. The fact that DHS captures children born during the five years prior to the survey means that all children in the 2022 test set would have been under 60 months at the time of interview and therefore, would have had incomplete theoretical follow-up for under-5 mortality. However, this is not a major concern given that most (approximately 53%) of under-5 deaths occur in the first year of life—and all children in our test set would have completed this high-risk period
15
. In addition, the outcome variable accurately identified 355 confirmed deaths.

4
Discussion

Gradient boosted trees have been conventionally believed to be superior to neural networks for tabular data
25
,
7
. However, we find our results indicate a more complex relationship. There are two reasons why gradient boosted trees have become inferior to neural networks on the dataset in question. First, we developed domain-specific feature engineering that converted what was once a difficult problem into a much easier one by embedding epidemiologic knowledge into the input data—which is why logistic regression outperformed gradient boosted trees. Secondly, the NAS process produced a very simple model—a single layer—whereas complex models such as TabNet
3
or ResNet
8
are overfitting to patterns that failed the eight-year temporal shift.

The equity gradient has significant implications. The epidemiologic transition model serves as the framework
19
. Stage 1 populations exhibit mortality from infections and malnutrition (predictable, measurable); Stage 3 populations exhibit mortality from congenital anomalies and birth asphyxia (stochastic, unpredictable). Bangladesh exhibits both stages: poorer divisions remain in Stage 1; Dhaka transitions to Stage 3·

These findings challenge fairness frameworks that demand equal performance across groups. Because predictability itself varies, a uniform AUROC is unreasonable—Dhaka’s lower AUROC reflects outcome etiology, not algorithmic failure. The policy implications are concrete: targeting in Stage 1 regions should be done individually through conditional cash transfer programs, nutrition supplementation programs, and home visits by community health workers. Stage 3 regions, such as Dhaka, require different strategies: strengthening the universal health system, including NICU capacity expansion, neonatal specialist training, and improvements in emergency obstetric care, to address stochastic biological causes of mortality that surveys cannot predict.

The clinical utility of this model extends beyond academic performance metrics. At the 10% screening threshold—a practical constraint due to limited health system capacity—the NAS model correctly identifies 42·3% of future deaths, compared with 41·1% for the next-best alternative. This 1·2 percentage point difference in identification rates translates to approximately 1300 more children who are at-risk being identified annually among Bangladesh’s 108,500 under-five deaths
4
. For programs with fixed intervention budgets, this marginal improvement in efficiency can lead to a substantial increase in lives saved per dollar spent.

Limitations. Several limitations deserve acknowledgment. The DHS captures children born within five years preceding the survey; all test children were less than 60 months at interview, however, only 16% of deaths occur after age 12 months, thus, censoring bias should be minimized. Missing data rates were similar across divisions (55-58% for healthcare variables) and complete case analysis as sensitivity produced consistent results. Additionally, features such as birth size and delivery mode are captured post-birth; therefore, deployment is restricted to postpartum screening rather than prenatal prediction. Finally, the observational nature of DHS data prevents causal interpretation of predictor effects.

5
Conclusion

By demonstrating that domain-specific, time-validated machine learning models are capable of identifying high risk individuals who may be eligible for specific interventions across future population groups—while at the same time identifying the limitations of predictive capability in transitioning populations—this study highlights the wealth paradox is not a result of algorithmic failure; it represents an understanding of how mortality causality shifts with increasing levels of economic development. Research should further develop this model to include multiple countries along a continuum of developmental stages and apply causal inference techniques to move from predicting risk to estimating the effects of intervention and determining optimal targeting.
