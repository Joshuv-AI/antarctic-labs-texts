Development and validation of the Lao Clinical Nurse Competency Scale
Authors: Toyomitsu Tamura, Souksavanh Phanpaseuth, Anousone Sisoulath, Phengdy Inthaphanith, Shikino Kikuchi, Kyoko Koto-Shimada, Kazumi Fujiwara, Nobuko Matsuda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Ministry of Health in the Lao People’s Democratic Republic (Laos) has developed a national nursing competency framework comprising five domains and 43 items, aligned with the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) core competencies. However, nurses’ actual competencies in Laos have not been systematically evaluated.ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop and validate a measurement scale to assess the clinical competencies of nurses working in healthcare facilities and clinical settings in Laos.MethodsA self-administered questionnaire was created based on the National Competency for Licensed Nurses in Laos (NCLN) and distributed to 359 nurses in Vientiane Capital and Champasak Province in October 2022. Responses from 355 nurses were analyzed. Construct validity was examined using exploratory factor analysis (EFA). Structural equation modeling (SEM) was then performed solely to evaluate the goodness of fit of the proposed model, rather than to confirm the factor structure. Content validity was verified by expert review, and reliability was assessed using the Spearman–Brown coefficient, Cronbach’s alpha, and McDonald’s omega (ω).ResultsThe EFA identified five factors, comprising 26 items, which explained 73.2% of the variance. The SEM showed an acceptable model fit: χ2(289) = 698.2, p <0.001; GFI = 0.86; AGFI = 0.83; CFI = 0.94; RMSEA = 0.066. Expert review confirmed the content validity. Reliability was high (Spearman–Brown = 0.934; Cronbach’s α = 0.880; McDonald’s ω = 0.971).ConclusionThe Lao Clinical Nurse Competency Scale (CAS) demonstrated satisfactory validity and reliability. SEM was used only to assess the model’s goodness of fit, and future studies should conduct confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using independent samples to verify construct validity.

Background

The concept of competency as a developmental model for skill acquisition was introduced by Dreyfus (1980) and later applied to nursing by Benner in the “From Novice to Expert” theory (1984). By the 1990s, competency-based approaches were integrated into nursing education (Morcke et al., 2013).

Nursing practice competencies encompass the qualities, skills, and abilities that enable nurses to perform their professional roles effectively (Flinkman et al., 2017). Competency is commonly defined as a nurse’s ability to fulfill an expected role (Schrimmer et al., 2019). It plays a crucial role in leadership development, contributing to organizational growth, effective teamwork, and the promotion of safe nursing practices (Dalla Bernardina & Spiri, 2019). Clinical experience strongly influences competency; nurses with fewer years of practice generally demonstrate lower competency levels, which gradually improve with experience (Vanaki & Memarian, 2009). Consequently, assessing competency is vital to ensuring quality-assured nursing services (Bahreini et al., 2011). Deficiencies in knowledge and skills can directly undermine the quality and safety of patient care (Ajani & Moez, 2011; Conlon et al., 2024; Saifan et al., 2021). Although many countries have developed assessment tools, their use has mostly been limited to high- and middle-income settings and specialized nursing areas (Schrimmer et al., 2019).

At the regional level, the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) introduced a mutual recognition agreement in 2006 to facilitate the mobility of doctors, dentists, and nurses among its ten member states (ASEAN, 2006). In 2009, ASEAN defined five core nursing competencies: 1) ethics and legal practice, 2) professional nursing practice, 3) leadership and management, 4) education and research, and 5) professional, personal, and quality development. These core competencies have been promoted across member states (ASEAN Joint Coordinating Committee on Nursing, 2009), with each country developing its own national frameworks, though no common regional assessment scale exists. Some countries have independently created national competency assessment scales (Feliciano et al., 2019; Juntasopeepun et al., 2019a; Lusmilasari et al., 2025).

The Lao People’s Democratic Republic (Laos), an ASEAN member state, continues to face challenges in health outcomes. The maternal mortality rate remains high at 126 per 100,000 live births (2020), and the infant mortality rate stands at 34.2 per 1,000 live births (2021) (WHO, n.d.). In response, the Ministry of Health (MOH) has prioritized strengthening nursing services through the establishment of a national competency framework (Lao Ministry of Health, 2015). The National Competency for Licensed Nurses in Laos (NCLN) was first introduced in 2013 and revised in 2021 to align with ASEAN’s five core competencies (Koto-Shimada et al., 2023; Lao Ministry of Health, 2022; Tamura et al., 2015) (Table 1).

Additionally, the MOH introduced a licensing and registration system in 2017, which mandates Continuing Professional Development (CPD). To design appropriate CPD programs, however, it is first necessary to assess nurses’ competency levels in clinical practice. Despite these initiatives, no comprehensive competency assessment of nurses has yet been conducted in Laos, nor has a valid and reliable assessment scale been developed.

To address this gap, this study aimed to develop a Competency Assessment Scale (CAS) based on the NCLN framework and to verify its validity and reliability among nurses working in health facilities and clinical settings in Laos. A literature review conducted in PubMed, CINAHL, and ScienceDirect identified approximately 1,610 relevant studies published between 2000 and 2024. However, no previous research focused on the development and validation of a CAS for nurses in Laos. This study therefore provides the foundation for subsequent large-scale assessments of nursing competency, while also informing the design of CPD programs and the strengthening of basic nursing education and policy. Furthermore, it aligns with the global trend toward establishing standardized competency frameworks and assessment tools across countries (Schrimmer et al., 2019).

Methods

This study used a descriptive, cross-sectional, and exploratory design to verify the validity and reliability of the Competency Assessment Scale (CAS) in two stages: CAS creation and CAS evaluation.

CAS Creation

During the development of the CAS, a Lao-language questionnaire was formulated based on the National Competency for Licensed Nurses (NCLN). The instrument consisted of 43 items; each rated on a four-point Likert scale. Brislin’s translation model was applied to conduct forward and back translations (Brislin, 1970). A pilot study was then conducted to compare and reconcile the questionnaire items.

Variables and Measurement Instrument

An anonymous, self-administered questionnaire was used to develop the CAS for nurses in Laos (Nilsson et al., 2018). It included questions on sociodemographic and work-related variables. The 43 items reflected the five domains of the NCLN, namely ethical and legal practices, professional nursing practice, leadership and management, education and research, and professional, personal, and quality development. Responses were rated on a four-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating higher competency.

The scale ranged from 43 to 172, with response options defined as follows: 1) Requires advice from experienced nurses to perform basic nursing procedures; 2) Able to prioritize and provide care but needs advice for difficult cases; 3) Able to manage difficult care but still lacks sufficient knowledge and skills for independent practice; 4) Demonstrates expertise in analyzing, planning, resolving, monitoring, and evaluating health problems, serves as a role model, and provides appropriate care independently.

Translation Process

The NCLN, which served as the theoretical foundation for this study, is available in both Lao and English. Technical terms from the NCLN were used to develop the questionnaire. The initial version was written in English and translated into Lao. Because not all researchers involved were native Lao speakers, English was retained as a working language. Officials from the Ministry of Health (MOH) ensured the accuracy of the translation, and the questionnaire was back-translated to confirm equivalence.

Pilot Test

A cognitive pilot test was conducted with seven nurses working in health facilities and clinical settings in Laos, reflecting the target population of the main survey. Polit and Beck (2017) recommend five to ten participants for pilot testing, which is sufficient to strengthen the reliability of a larger survey. The pilot test in this study assessed the comprehensibility, acceptability, and completion time of the questionnaire.

Face and Content Validation by Experts

After the pilot test, the CAS was reviewed by a panel of experts for adequacy, relevance, and comprehensibility. The panel included one official from the MOH, two nursing educators from the Faculty of Nursing at the University of Health Sciences, and six supervisors from national central hospitals. A total of nine experts were invited to evaluate the accuracy of the translation, the cultural appropriateness of the items, and the overall applicability of the instrument. Feedback from the experts was incorporated to ensure that the CAS was valid and culturally appropriate for use in Laos (Maruyama & Matsunari, 2020).

CAS Evaluation

The CAS was evaluated through a cross-sectional study conducted in Laos in October 2022 to assess its validity and reliability.

Participants

The nursing workforce in Laos includes 6,724 nurses working in clinical settings (WHO, 2020). Participants were selected using a stratified cluster sampling method, stratified by type of health facility and years of experience. Vientiane Capital and one province with a large nursing workforce were selected to capture both urban and rural contexts, accounting for disparities in workforce distribution and resources. The inclusion criteria required nurses to be actively working in a health facility or clinical setting, providing direct nursing care to patients, and to have at least one month of work experience. Nurses in managerial roles, supervisors, educators, and nursing officers were excluded from the study. A minimum sample size of 88 per group was estimated based on an assumed effect size of 0.3, 80 percent power, and α = 0.05. Considering two strata and a design effect of 1.7, the required total sample was 352 for exploratory factor analysis (EFA).

Procedure and Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the National Center for Global Health and Medicine, Japan (NCGM-S-004478-00, 17 August 2022), and the University of Health Sciences, Laos (No. 357/REC, 21 July 2022). Self-administered questionnaires were distributed to nurses in five central hospitals in Vientiane, four district hospitals, one provincial hospital, six district hospitals, and three health centers in Champasak province. The MOH issued official letters requesting participation from facilities. Before data collection, researchers explained the study’s purpose, assured participants that their participation was voluntary and anonymous, and emphasized that they could withdraw their consent at any time without disadvantage.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0.1.0 and IBM SPSS Amos version 28.0.0. Descriptive statistics, including percentages, frequencies, means, and standard deviations (SD), were calculated. Validity was assessed from two perspectives, construct validity and content validity. Construct validity was examined by first checking for ceiling and floor effects. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to assess inter-domain relationships. Sampling adequacy and suitability for factor analysis were tested using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was then conducted on the 43 items using maximum likelihood extraction with PROMAX oblique rotation. Model fit was evaluated with absolute fit indices (Hooper et al., 2008; McDonald & Ho, 2002). Structural equation modeling (SEM) was subsequently employed to assess the overall model fit. Because both EFA and SEM were performed on the same dataset, SEM was applied solely to evaluate the fit, rather than as a confirmatory factor analysis (Liu et al., 2021; Yaghobian et al., 2020).

Content validity was assessed through expert review, which examined the relevance and cultural appropriateness of the items. Reliability was evaluated using the split-half method with the Spearman–Brown coefficient, as well as Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega (ω).

Results

Characteristics of Participants

Self-administered questionnaires were distributed to 359 nurses, and responses from 355 who met the inclusion criteria were analyzed, representing a response rate of 98.8 percent. Of these participants, 63.1 percent (n = 224) were from Vientiane Capital and 36.9 percent (n = 131) from Champasak Province. All participants (100%, n = 355) were Laotian, and 89.0% (n = 316) were women. The mean age was 33.1 years (SD = 8.5), and the mean nursing experience was 10.9 years (SD = 8.5). Competency scores ranged from 44 to 172, with a mean competency level between 2.75 (SD = 0.96) and 3.44 (SD = 0.99).

Fifteen items (Items 1, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 25, 28, 31, 39, 41, 42, and 43) demonstrated a ceiling effect, with mean plus standard deviation values exceeding 4.0, the upper limit of the scale. These items, which related to nurses’ knowledge and skills, were deleted. No floor effects were observed (Table 2).

Content Validity

The content validity of the 26-item, five-factor structure, derived from exploratory and SEM analyses, was verified by experts. The expert panel confirmed that the scale measured nursing competencies based on the NCLN, incorporated the three attributes of competency, was aligned with the ASEAN core competencies, and retained the original number of 43 items without further expansion.

Reliability

The reliability of the CAS was evaluated using several measures. The Spearman–Brown coefficient was 0.934, indicating high split-half reliability. Cronbach’s alpha for the entire scale was 0.880, with values for the five factors ranging from 0.834 to 0.883 (0.837, 0.836, 0.834, 0.863, and 0.883, respectively). McDonald’s omega (ω) was 0.971. Although this value was high, it may reflect inter-item overlap across factors rather than unidimensional reliability.

Discussion

Principal Findings

Using the ASEAN standard core competencies, the Competency Assessment Scale (CAS) was developed as the Lao adaptation of the National Competency for Licensed Nurses (NCLN). The original version comprised 43 items across five domains, but because an evaluation tool that accurately reflected the realities of nursing in Laos was needed, each item underwent rigorous selection and refinement to ensure cultural and contextual appropriateness for health facility settings.

The validity of the CAS was supported by item-level and factor-level analyses. Item analysis revealed ceiling effects in 15 of the 43 items, leading to the exclusion of 2 of 4 items related to legal and ethical nursing practice (50.0 percent), 6 of 14 items related to professional nursing practice (42.9 percent), 3 of 14 items related to leadership and nursing management (21.4 percent), and 4 of 5 items related to professional and personal development (80.0 percent). Items related to nursing research did not show ceiling effects, likely because research is not a common responsibility for most nurses in Laos and is typically considered only by senior nurses (Tamura et al., 2023).

To assess suitability for factor analysis, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were calculated. The KMO value (0.972 > 0.60) and the significant Bartlett’s result (χ2 = 7999.1, p <0.001) confirmed that factor analysis was appropriate for this study (Matsuda & Watanuki, 2022. The factor loadings for the five-factor scale ranged from 0.404 to 0.933. Items with factor loading values of less than 0.4 were removed, and the items were restructured as 26 items (Fang et al., 2022). Various criteria were used to assess model fit. The GFI and AGFI scores of 0.86 and 0.83, respectively, were satisfactory (Matsuda & Watanuki, 2022). The CFI score (> 0.92) was satisfactory (Kueh et al., 2024). The RMSEA value was also acceptable, with a value of less than 0.08 (Kääriäinen et al., 2011). Consequently, construct validity was provisionally supported by the five-factor solution; however, further cross-validation using an independent sample is recommended to confirm the stability of the structure. In addition, experts confirmed the content validity of the CAS. It was found that the selected items covered all measures required for competency assessment and reflected the three attributes defined by Matsutani et al. (2010): understanding patient conditions, practicing people-centered care, and improving the quality of nursing services.

Reliability was also confirmed. Structural equation modeling (SEM) demonstrated an adequate fit with the five-factor model, which was further supported by Spearman–Brown, Cronbach’s alpha, and McDonald's omega (ω). This confirmed strong internal consistency (Fang et al., 2022). These findings highlight the robustness of the scale and its applicability in assessing nursing competencies in Laos. However, because test–retest reliability was not evaluated, the temporal stability of the CAS remains unverified and should be examined in future studies.

Implications

The validity of the results was verified based on statistical analysis. While referring to the NCLN and the factors derived from the reconstructed items, the following labels were assigned: Factor 1 “Ethics and Nursing Practice,” Factor 2 “Leadership and Nursing Management,” Factor 3 “Logical Thinking and Research,” Factor 4 “Community Nursing,” and Factor 5 “Professional Development.” Factor 1, Ethics and Nursing Practice, comprises basic nursing knowledge, techniques, and skills. Medical assistance is a crucial role expected of nurses in Lao (Matsuo et al., 2017), and thus, Factor 1 is essential. Information technology was also included in the 26 items of the five CAS factors (American Association of Colleges of Nursing, 2021). This was particularly emphasized during the coronavirus pandemic (Honda et al., 2023). Because the participants in this survey were nurses working in health facilities, it is also likely that items related to community nursing were extracted independently.

The newly constructed competencies in Factor 4, Community Nursing, were consistent with the government policy of promoting primary health care, as outlined in the Health Sector Reform Strategy 2021–2030 and the Human Resources for Health Strategy by 2030. Factor 4 was identified as a competency required by nurses who are expected to contribute to community-level health promotion and care (Notarnicola et al., 2025).

In comparing these results with those of other ASEAN member states, the findings highlight both similarities and differences. In the Philippines, the original 73-item scale was used with minor modifications, producing Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.89–0.94 and 0.91–0.95, respectively (Feliciano et al., 2019). In contrast, Thailand reduced the number of items and reconfigured the scale into a six-factor model to enhance cultural fit (Juntasopeepun et al., 2019a; Juntasopeepun et al., 2019b). In Laos, an original national version was implemented, and its factor structure was reconfigured by reducing the number of items and introducing a community nursing factor to ensure cultural appropriateness and contextual alignment.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. The survey employed a stratified cluster sampling method across selected areas, which yielded lower measurement accuracy compared to a simple random sampling method and thus limited the representativeness of the findings. Moreover, competency evaluation based on self-assessment is inherently susceptible to potential biases. These methodological constraints had direct implications for assessing community nursing competencies. Restricting participants to clinically based nurses in health facility settings further narrowed generalizability, particularly regarding competencies in the community. To capture the full spectrum of community nursing competencies, future studies should expand the sampling frame to include nurses in primary healthcare and outreach settings and adjust participant selection criteria accordingly.

Another limitation was the approach to verifying validity. Content validity was assessed through expert review, but indices such as the Item-Level Content Validity Index (I-CVI), Scale-Level Content Validity Index (S-CVI), and Average Scale Content Validity Index (S-CVI/Ave) were not computed. Furthermore, only nine experts participated in the review, limiting the evaluation of cross-cultural adaptation. Construct validation ideally should be confirmed using an independent sample rather than reusing the same dataset. However, the EFA and SEM were both applied to the same dataset of 355 nurses. Using a single sample capitalizes on chance variation and artificially improves fit indices, thereby overstating construct validity. Thus, the SEM results in this study should be interpreted strictly as an assessment of model fit rather than as confirmatory evidence of construct validity, and the validity reported in this study should be interpreted with caution.

Reliability verification was also limited. Test–retest procedures were not implemented, and external validity measures, such as temporal stability and reproducibility metrics (e.g., intraclass correlation coefficient), were not calculated due to the time and cost limitations of this study. Increased measurement error may compromise the interpretability of results and the efficacy of future decision-making.

Despite these constraints, this study verified the validity and reliability of the CAS among health facility-based nurses, demonstrating its suitability as a tool for assessing CPD needs. For broader application, particularly within community nursing or CPD contexts, the wording of questionnaire items and the delineation of factors should be reviewed and refined to enhance content relevance and clarity.

Conclusion

This study referenced the National Competency for Licensed Nurses (NCLN) in Laos and verified the validity and reliability of a measurement scale for assessing the current competencies of nurses working in health facilities and clinical settings in Laos. After creating the questionnaire, including the Competency Assessment Scale (CAS), a quantitative survey was conducted in Vientiane Capital and Champasak Province. Responses from 355 nurses were analyzed. The construct and content validity of the CAS were verified, and the results confirmed an adequate fit to the five-factor model, ensuring internal validity. Reliability was demonstrated with strong coefficients for Spearman–Brown, Cronbach’s alpha, and McDonald’s omega (ω). In addition, the ASEAN initiative, which emphasized the measurement of basic nursing knowledge and skills and considered language characteristics, aligned with the Ministry of Health’s intention to assess competency progress among nurses based on the NCLN.

The measurement scale developed in this study enables the systematic assessment of nursing proficiency in Laos. Applying a validated and contextually appropriate scale allows surveys to identify specific areas and items where competency improvement is needed. The findings of this study may serve as a valuable reference for nurses providing care at public healthcare facilities in Laos, supporting them in identifying pathways for enhancing their personal competency. Furthermore, the results contribute to overall improvements in nursing quality through education and health administration, while also providing foundational data for the development of educational programs for new nurses in alignment with national standards currently being developed by the Ministry of Health.

The development of measurement scales generally begins with item creation, a complex process requiring human resources, time, financial investment, and diverse nursing-related information. In low- and middle-income countries, such as Laos, where resources are limited and competency frameworks are still evolving, it is nevertheless feasible to construct reliable measurement tools based on defined items. The scale developed in this study demonstrates this potential. Moreover, because the NCLN was created based on the ASEAN core competency framework, the findings and the CAS developed here may also serve as a valuable reference for neighboring countries undertaking similar efforts in competency assessment.
