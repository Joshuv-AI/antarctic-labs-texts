Anesthetic management of transapical beating-heart septal myectomy in a child with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: a case report
Authors: Honghui Yu, Lin Cheng, Hongbo Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransapical beating‑heart septal myectomy (TA‑BSM) is considered an effective surgical approach for obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (oHCM). However, data regarding its application in pediatric patients remain limited. To date, no cases have been reported detailing the anesthetic management for pediatric oHCM patients undergoing TA‑BSM.Case presentationWe report the case of a 9‑year‑old girl with oHCM who underwent TA‑BSM under general anesthesia. Despite thorough preoperative preparation, she experienced significant perioperative complications, including hypotension and ventricular arrhythmias. Cardiac arrest occurred during rotation of the beating‑heart myectomy device (BMD) to adjust its angle. This critical event was successfully managed through direct epicardial compression, emergency temporary pacemaker implantation, and multidisciplinary collaboration, ultimately resulting in the patient’s favorable recovery. Background

Obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (oHCM) is an inherited cardiomyopathy characterized by thickening of the heart muscle and interventricular septum [1–3]. It often leads to chest pain, palpitations, reduced exercise capacity, worsening dyspnea, and even sudden cardiac death due to dynamic left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction and mitral regurgitation [3–6]. Mitral regurgitation mediated by systolic anterior motion (SAM) and a left ventricular outflow tract gradient (LVOTG) ≥ 30 mmHg represents a typical characteristic of the disease [5–6]. Timely diagnosis and treatment are crucial and may include pharmacotherapy, percutaneous septal ablation, or surgical myectomy to alleviate symptoms [1, 5–6]. Among these, surgical septal myectomy is currently the preferred treatment for symptomatic patients with drug-refractory, severe oHCM [5]. Tongji Hospital has successfully implemented a self-developed minimally invasive transapical beating‑heart septal myectomy (TA‑BSM) procedure for adult patients with oHCM, achieving favorable therapeutic outcomes [6–9]. The primary challenges in the anesthetic management of patients with oHCM include the low incidence rate, which results in limited clinical experience, as well as perioperative LVOT obstruction and exacerbation of myocardial ischemia. Furthermore, there is an increased risk of arrhythmias, heart failure, and even cardiac arrest [1–3]. However, clinical data on pediatric patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy remain even more limited due to underdiagnosis, and clinical anesthesia decisions often rely on experience extrapolated from adult cases. To date, no cases have been reported detailing the anesthetic management of TA‑BSM in pediatric oHCM patients. This article presents the anesthetic and surgical management of a TA‑BSM performed on a 9‑year‑old girl with oHCM at our institution. It summarizes key points and potential risks in the anesthetic management, aiming to provide a reference for the perioperative care of pediatric patients with oHCM.

Case presentation

A 9‑year‑old girl with a height of 140 cm and a weight of 28 kg was admitted to the hospital. She had a history of recurrent upper respiratory tract infections and fevers. She presented with chest tightness and shortness of breath on exertion for more than 3 months. Based on clinical findings, electrocardiographic (ECG) abnormalities, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) results, a diagnosis of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy was considered. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed asymmetric left ventricular hypertrophy consistent with oHCM, severe mitral regurgitation, mild aortic and tricuspid regurgitation, a small pericardial effusion, and left atrial enlargement. The child was taking oral metoprolol tartrate for symptom management. She had a family history of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy; her father was diagnosed with the condition but did not undergo surgery and later died of sudden cardiac death. Preoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) combined with clinical evaluation confirmed the diagnosis of oHCM, with an LVOTG of 101 mmHg (Fig. 1A and B). After multidisciplinary consultation, the patient was scheduled to undergo TA‑BSM under general anesthesia to relieve left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (see Additional file 1).

Preoperative preparation and anesthesia management

To optimize the preoperative status, the following measures were implemented. First, oral metoprolol tartrate (25 mg twice daily) was administered to control heart rate and reduce myocardial contractility. Second, a three-day intravenous volume expansion protocol was carried out, with a daily infusion of at least 1000 mL of compound sodium chloride to ensure adequate intravascular volume. The child was accompanied into the operating room by an anesthesiologist and a nurse, where continuous monitoring of blood pressure (BP), ECG, and oxygen saturation (SpO₂) was maintained. No sedative premedication was administered, as the child had a cheerful personality and had developed a trusting relationship with the medical team. Our primary concern was that premedication might mask early signs of hemodynamic compromise, such as hypotension or worsening of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, especially given the child’s high‑risk family history of sudden cardiac death. As the intravenous cannula placed in the ward was found to be blocked, intravenous induction of anesthesia was not feasible. Instead, the anesthesiologist used a mobile phone to play the child’s favorite cartoon while guiding the child to inhale 5% to 8% sevoflurane through a mask, ensuring a smooth transition into sleep without anxiety. All subsequent procedures were performed under ultrasound guidance: the nurse established intravenous access, and the anesthesiologist cannulated the right radial artery for invasive arterial blood pressure (IBP) monitoring. A slow induction anesthesia protocol was then employed, consisting of the intravenous administration of midazolam 1 mg, propofol 50 mg, sufentanil 28 µg, and cisatracurium 6 mg. One minute after induction, the IBP decreased to 80/51 mmHg; following administration of 20 µg of phenylephrine, the blood pressure recovered to 99/63 mmHg. Subsequently, tracheal intubation was performed under video laryngoscope guidance, and a transesophageal echocardiography probe was placed. Hemodynamics remained stable throughout the intubation process, with no significant ST segment changes observed. Following intubation, an ultrasound-guided puncture of the right internal jugular vein was performed to insert a triple-lumen central venous catheter, enabling continuous intraoperative monitoring of central venous pressure (CVP). At that time, the child’s IBP reading was 104/60 mmHg, with a CVP of 17 mmHg (Fig. 2A). Anesthesia was maintained with inhalation of 2.0% sevoflurane and continuous intravenous infusions of dexmedetomidine (0.4 µg/kg/h), sufentanil (0.2–0.5 µg/kg/h), and propofol (1–4 mg/kg/h). End-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO₂) was carefully maintained between 35 and 40 mmHg. Subsequently, an ultrasound-guided serratus anterior plane block (SAPB) was performed via the superficial approach for postoperative analgesia. A linear array probe was placed horizontally at the level of the fourth and fifth ribs in the midaxillary line, and 20 mL of 0.3% ropivacaine was injected into the fascial plane between the serratus anterior and the latissimus dorsi muscles. Following the block, the child was repositioned horizontally with a slight left lateral tilt.

Intraoperative contingency preparations

Routine contingency protocols for TA-BSM were implemented: defibrillation electrode pads were placed over the right subclavian region and on the left lateral chest wall (posterior to the left anterior axillary line, at the 5th intercostal space); sterile temporary pacing wires, along with a pre-packaged external pulse generator, were pre-positioned on the surgical table for immediate access. Furthermore, the perfusion team had primed the cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) circuit and kept it on standby from the induction of anesthesia, ensuring that, in the event of a severe intraoperative complication (e.g., iatrogenic mitral valve injury), emergency heparinization and immediate initiation of CPB could be performed without delay to safeguard the patient.

Surgical procedure and anesthesia management

Once these preparations were completed, the surgical field was disinfected and draped along the cardiac surgical incision line. Prior to skin incision, TEE was performed to evaluate image quality, cardiac morphology, and hemodynamics in all standard views. Thereafter, an additional 5 µg of sufentanil was administered. Transthoracic ultrasound revealed that the apical beat was located in the 5th intercostal space, which prompted the decision to enter the chest through the 5th intercostal space. The cardiac surgeon incised and suspended the pericardium, after which the anesthesiologist initiated systemic heparinization. After the arterial line was inserted, hemodynamic monitoring showed a left ventricular apical pressure (LVAP) of 133/21 mmHg and an LVOTG of 66 mmHg (Fig. 2B). Before introducing the myocardial rotary resection system through the apical incision, the anesthesiologist maintained mean arterial pressure at 55–65 mmHg using phenylephrine as needed, and heart rate at 50–65 beats/min using esmolol or diltiazem as needed, based on real-time monitoring. Subsequently, the beating-heart myectomy device (BMD) was placed through the apical incision, and the TEE-guided rotary knife was positioned to resect the hypertrophied myocardium within the left ventricle (Fig. 1C and D). During the rotary resection process, premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) increased significantly, so 50 mg of lidocaine and 10 mg of esmolol were administered to reduce cardiac stimulation. Each time the BMD was withdrawn from the cardiac chamber after resection, the anesthesiologist increased the mean arterial pressure to 60–70 mmHg by accelerating fluid infusion or administering a small dose of phenylephrine (see Additional file 2). Following the surgeon’s and echocardiographer’s repeated assessment of the resection extent and effect, and after they had determined the strategy for the subsequent resection—specifically, “where and how to cut next”—the anesthesiologist reinduced controlled hypotension in preparation for the next insertion of the BMD. However, the close contact of the BMD with the myocardium during operation, along with its negative pressure suction effect, led to relative hypovolemia and myocardial irritability. These disturbances clinically manifested as transient hypotension, PVCs, or non-sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT). In response, the anesthesiologist immediately intervened by instructing the surgeon to pause or withdraw the BMD, accelerating fluid infusion, and cautiously administering a low dose of phenylephrine to maintain hemodynamic stability and coronary perfusion.

Intraoperative cardiac arrest: team response and management

During another attempt of this resection procedure, the patient’s blood pressure suddenly dropped to a minimum of 30/15 mmHg. In this early hypotensive phase, the anesthesiologist immediately instructed the surgeon to withdraw the BMD, swiftly administered a 40 µg intravenous bolus of phenylephrine, and simultaneously increased the infusion rate of norepinephrine. The patient’s blood pressure subsequently recovered, and the surgery proceeded. However, as the cardiac surgeon rotated the BMD again to resect the hypertrophic myocardium at the base of the left ventricle, the patient suddenly developed ventricular fibrillation; the arterial waveform disappeared instantaneously, and the heart ceased beating, confirming cardiac arrest. At this critical moment, the cardiac surgeon rapidly removed the BMD, quickly tightened and sutured the apical purse-string, and immediately began open cardiac massage to maintain perfusion of vital organs. With the monitor confirming ventricular fibrillation and undetectable blood pressure, the anesthesiologist promptly adjusted the ventilator settings and charged the defibrillator to 56 J. Once charging was complete, the surgeon immediately performed external electrical defibrillation.

Following defibrillation, cardiac electrical activity was restored, but the ECG showed third-degree atrioventricular block with an extremely slow ventricular escape rhythm, which was unable to maintain effective cardiac output. Concurrently, an assistant implanted a pacemaker lead on the surface of the left ventricle and another on the diaphragm, then rapidly connected the pacemaker. Considering the patient’s diagnosis of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, the team discussed and decided to temporarily withhold epinephrine to avoid exacerbating left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. Instead, they continued open cardiac massage to support circulation. After successful pacemaker capture, the heart rhythm gradually stabilized. Subsequently, phenylephrine was administered to raise blood pressure. The patient’s hemodynamics progressively improved, blood pressure recovered, and resuscitation was successful. Thereafter, the child completed the remaining hypertrophic myocardial resection under temporary pacemaker support.

Postoperative course and follow-up

Postoperatively, the child’s LVOTG decreased significantly from 66 mmHg to 4 mmHg (Fig. 2B and C), indicating effective relief of the obstruction. Additionally, the mitral valve, which had shown severe regurgitation preoperatively, resumed a normal echogenic appearance with good mobility (Fig. 1B and F). Intraoperative blood loss and aspirated fluid totaled 400 mL, and 200 mL of autologous packed red blood cells recovered via the autologous blood recovery system were reinfused by the anesthesiologist. The resected specimen was sent promptly for pathological examination, which revealed histological features consistent with oHCM (Fig. 3A and B). The child recovered rapidly after surgery. The temporary pacemaker was removed on postoperative day 3, and chest pain and tightness resolved completely. Subsequently, the child was successfully discharged on postoperative day 9. Follow-up assessments at 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 1.5 years (July 17, 2025) demonstrated stable cardiac function and complete resolution of all clinical symptoms (see Additional file 3). ECG showed sinus rhythm with complete left bundle branch block (LBBB), while echocardiography revealed mild (1+) regurgitation on the left atrial side of the mitral orifice during systole.

Discussion and conclusions

TA-BSM has been reported as safe and feasible in adult patients with oHCM, with preliminary evidence also supporting its feasibility in pediatric populations [6–10]. Compared with the modified Morrow procedure, it offers real-time guidance via TEE, thereby avoiding the need for reoperation, while also reducing surgical trauma and eliminating the need for cardiopulmonary bypass. Although there are now emerging pharmacological alternatives, such as myosin inhibitors, these were not clinically available in China at the time of this patient’s surgery (December 2023). Taking into account the wishes of both the child and her mother, surgical myectomy was considered a reasonable option at that time.

Despite comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation and meticulous perioperative preparation, this 9-year-old patient with oHCM who underwent TA-BSM experienced sudden cardiac arrest during rotation of the BMD. Following 1.5 min of coordinated resuscitation, the patient was successfully revived but developed third-degree atrioventricular block, necessitating completion of the procedure with temporary pacemaker support. Postoperatively, ECG revealed new-onset complete LBBB. Based on the intraoperative resection extent, the cardiac arrest was postulated to be associated with reduced blood flow from device rotation, as well as compression of the right bundle branch secondary to septal myocardial edema. According to the literature, the incidence of new-onset LBBB following TA-BSM is 63% [11], which is closely related to the anatomical proximity of the left bundle branch to the resection area.

Performing TA-BSM under general anesthesia in pediatric patients with oHCM poses a significant challenge, requiring multidisciplinary collaboration to maintain hemodynamic stability and ensure smooth procedural execution. Based on adult case series, published literature, and institutional experience, this procedure demands close collaboration among anesthesiologists, surgeons, and echocardiography teams. General anesthesia is often recommended, as it helps to reduce peripheral vascular resistance and increase venous capacitance [12–16]. The following recommendations are proposed for perioperative management:

Preoperative management should include the continuation of chronic β-blocker or non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker therapy to maintain a heart rate of 60–80 beats/min, thereby prolonging diastole, reducing the LVOTG, and improving myocardial oxygen supply [12–14]. For oHCM patients with preoperative atrial fibrillation, anticoagulation is recommended. Additionally, age-appropriate psychological support, age- and weight-appropriate fluid management for preoperative volume expansion, and dietary adjustments to minimize intestinal gas (which may interfere with TEE) are also advised.

Before induction, crystalloid or colloid infusion should be administered to optimize ventricular filling, thereby counteracting anesthetic-induced vasodilation and the reduction in preload associated with positive pressure ventilation, both of which may exacerbate LVOT obstruction [13–15]. During anesthesia maintenance, adequate depth of anesthesia should be ensured and myocardial contractility appropriately controlled, with the bispectral index (BIS) maintained between 45 and 55. It is acceptable not to use near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) in the absence of CPB; however, given that controlled hypotension is required in this type of surgery, NIRS is recommended for cerebral oxygen monitoring when conditions permit.

To ensure full preparedness for potential intraoperative emergencies, we recommend implementing several contingency measures. These include the use of cell salvage, placement of defibrillation pads (right subclavian and left lateral chest wall), and ensuring sterile temporary pacing wires with an external pulse generator are immediately available on the back table. From the induction of anesthesia, the CPB circuit should be primed and kept on standby, allowing for rapid emergency heparinization and immediate initiation of bypass in the event of a major complication, such as mitral valve injury, ventricular perforation, or severe bleeding.

TA-BSM presents unique hemodynamic challenges primarily associated with use of the BMD. Device insertion into the small left ventricular cavity may cause endocardial irritation and trigger ventricular irritability, while negative pressure during BMD activation may lead to relative hypovolemia. These disturbances often manifest as transient hypotension, PVCs, or non-sustained VT. Immediate interventions include temporary suspension of device manipulation, rapid volume replacement (crystalloids or colloids, e.g., albumin, hydroxyethyl starch), and cautious administration of phenylephrine (0.5–1 µg/kg). As a pure α₁ agonist, phenylephrine increases afterload, reduces SAM/obstruction, raises BP, and potentially lowers the LVOTG without positive inotropy. Norepinephrine should be infused continuously at a low dose to maintain baseline vascular tone. When systolic BP drops > 20% from baseline, the norepinephrine infusion rate should be increased accordingly. Norepinephrine combines α₁‑mediated vasoconstriction with weak β₁ inotropic support, increasing afterload while preserving cardiac output, and must be titrated against the LVOT gradient to avoid worsening obstruction [13–15]. For frequent arrhythmias, intravenous lidocaine (1 mg/kg) or esmolol (0.5 mg/kg) may be considered.

A phased BP control strategy is important. During device insertion and resection, controlled hypotension (MAP 60–70 mmHg in adults, 55–65 mmHg in children) is often employed to reduce myocardial contractility and septal excursion, optimizing surgical conditions. Upon device withdrawal, MAP should be restored to ≥ 75 mmHg (≥ 65 mmHg for children) to ensure adequate coronary and cerebral perfusion. A slow, regular sinus rhythm (heart rate 60–70 beats/min) is targeted throughout. Inotropes (e.g., dopamine, dobutamine) are generally avoided, and vasodilators (e.g., nitroglycerin, nitroprusside) are used cautiously to preserve preload and afterload, minimising LVOT obstruction risk. This meticulous BP management may offer several advantages: reduced myocardial contraction amplitude, decreased intraoperative bleeding, and improved TEE visualization of the hypertrophied myocardium and LVOT, aiding precise resection while reducing the risk of conduction system injury or iatrogenic damage (e.g., to the mitral valve).

(5)Intraoperative atrial fibrillation may be corrected by direct current cardioversion rather than by pharmacological means [12–13]. If cardiac arrest occurs, the pericardial purse-string suture should be tightened promptly, and direct cardiac compression should be prioritized, combined with defibrillation and temporary pacing as needed. Although epinephrine is recommended by standard CPR guidelines, it should be used with caution in these patients, as it may exacerbate LVOT obstruction and further compromise hemodynamic status [12–16]. When available, alternative vasopressors (e.g., phenylephrine) should be considered, and prompt establishment of cardiopulmonary bypass is recommended if necessary.

This case report has several limitations. First, with only two years of follow-up in a single case, we cannot determine whether the conduction abnormalities will persist, nor can we assume that our findings apply to other patients. Second, although the resection was performed under real-time ultrasound guidance, the optimal views during episodes of hemodynamic instability or immediately after resection were not saved. These images would have provided more intuitive confirmation of the relief of obstruction and SAM, thereby enhancing the educational value of this report. Third, our anesthetic strategy was largely adapted from adult experience. Since hemodynamic responses in children may differ, whether this approach is safe and applicable in pediatric patients requires further validation. Fourth, we cannot definitively distinguish whether the cardiac arrest, transient third-degree atrioventricular block, and new-onset complete left bundle branch block observed in this case were attributable to surgical manipulation, anesthetic management, or the patient’s own anatomical characteristics. Finally, as a case report, we cannot establish causality between any specific anesthetic intervention and the clinical outcomes.

This report demonstrates that effective perioperative multidisciplinary collaboration and meticulous hemodynamic management are important for the safe performance of TA-BSM in pediatric patients with oHCM. The described anesthetic approach provides valuable preliminary experience for this procedure in the pediatric population. Future prospective studies are warranted to further optimize individualised hemodynamic targets based on age and septal thickness.
