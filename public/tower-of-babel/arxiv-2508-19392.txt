Towards New Characterizations of Small Circuit Classes via Discrete Ordinary Differential Equations
Melissa Antonelli, Arnaud Durand, Juha Kontinen

Abstract
Implicit computational complexity is a lively area of theoretical computer science, which aims to provide machine-independent characterizations of relevant complexity classes. One of the seminal works in this field appeared in the 1960s, when Cobham introduced a function algebra closed under bounded recursion on notation to capture polynomial time computable functions ( 𝐅𝐏 \mathbf{FP} ). Later on, several complexity classes have been characterized using limited recursion schemas. In this context, an original approach has been recently introduced, showing that ordinary differential equations (ODEs) offer a natural tool for algorithmic design and providing a characterization of 𝐅𝐏 \mathbf{FP} by a new ODE-schema. In the present paper we generalize this approach by presenting original ODE-characterizations for the small circuit classes 𝐅𝐀𝐂 0 \mathbf{FAC}^{0} and 𝐅𝐓𝐂 0 \mathbf{FTC}^{0} .

By now, computability and complexity theory have a long history, dating back to pioneering studies by Gödel

Historically, time and space complexity for Turing machines were first introduced in an explicit context

More recently, a different approach to complexity, called implicit computational complexity (ICC, for short), has been developed, aiming to describe complexity classes without explicitly referring to a specific machine model.
This alternative perspective has offered remarkable insights into the corresponding classes and has led to related meta-theorems in several domains, from database theory to constraint satisfaction.

In particular, from the 1990s on, several implicit characterizations for multiple classes have been introduced.

2.1.1
From Computability to Complexity

Modern computing machines have been developed in relation to the first attempts to formalize the notion of algorithm and of computable functions.
Accordingly, computational models are inherently linked to function algebras, where a function algebra is an algebra constituted by a set of basic functions and by schemas to construct new functions from the given ones.
For example, the class of primitive recursive functions is the smallest class defined as follows:

\mathcal{PR}=[\mathsf{0},\mathsf{s},\pi^{p}_{i};\circ,\text{PR}]

where
\mathsf{s}(x)=x+1
is the successor function,
\pi^{p}_{i}
is the projection function,
\circ
is function composition and PR is the primitive recursion schema below.

Definition 2.1

(Primitive Recursion, PR)
.

Given
g:\mathbb{N}^{p}\to\mathbb{N}
and
h:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
, the function
f:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
is defined by
primitive recursion
from
g
and
h
if:

\displaystyle f(0,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=g(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle f(x+1,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=h\big(x,\mathbf{y},f(x,\mathbf{y})\big).

Clearly, primitive recursive computation requires a number of steps that is approximately exponential in the length of the input. However, when the recursion schema is restricted in the proper way, several other classes could be defined, including complexity ones.

In 1943, Kalmár defined the class of elementary functions by restricting PR so to allow only limited sums and products
[
35
]
, and, in 1953, Grzegorczyk generalized this work to the study of the classes
\mathcal{E}^{k}
.
About ten years later, Bennett introduced the notion of bounded recursion on notation in the context of rudimentary sets
[
7
]
.
Then, a groundbreaking result in this spirit was established by Cobham, who provided a characterization of
\mathbf{FP}
using the notion of BRN, as defined below, and inspired by Bennett’s recursion
[
7
]
.

Definition 2.2

(Bounded Recursion on Notation, BRN)
.

Given
g:\mathbb{N}^{p}\to\mathbb{N}
,
k:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
and
h_{i}:\mathbb{N}^{p+2}\to\mathbb{N}
, with
i\in\{0,1\}
, a function
f:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
is defined by
bounded recursion on notation
from
g,h_{0},h_{1}
and
k
if

\displaystyle f(0,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=g(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle f(\mathsf{s}_{i}(x),\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=h_{i}\big(x,\mathbf{y},f(x,\mathbf{y})\big)\quad x\neq 0

\displaystyle f(x,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\leq k(x,\mathbf{y})

where
\mathsf{s}_{0}=2x
and
\mathsf{s}_{1}=2x+1
are the binary successor functions.

Observe that if
\ell(k(x,\mathbf{y}))
is polynomial in
\ell(x)+\ell(\mathbf{y})
, then so is
\ell(f(x,\mathbf{y}))
(being
\ell(z)
the length of
z
written in binary, see Notation
2.6
).
This ensures that inner terms do not grow too fast, i.e. that the growth rate is controlled by means of already known functions.
On the other hand, the number of induction steps is kept under control by the binary successor functions, as
\ell(\mathsf{s}_{1}(x))=\ell(\mathsf{s}_{0}(x))=\ell(x)+1
.
It was relying on the BRN schema, that Cobham defined the algebra below:
2
2

2

Actually, Cobham’s algebra is made of functions on words, while we are here presenting its number version, see
[
44
]
.

\mathcal{A}_{Cob}=[\mathsf{0},\mathsf{s}_{0},\mathsf{s}_{1},\pi^{p}_{i},\#;\circ,\text{BRN}],

where
x\#y=2^{\ell(x)\times\ell(y)}
.
It was proven that this function algebra precisely captures the class of poly-time computable functions, i.e.

\mathcal{A}_{Cob}=\mathbf{FP}.

This result has led to several machine-independent characterizations of other complexity classes, even of parallel ones (see Section
2.2
).

2.1.2
From Bounded Recursion to ODEs

Cobham’s work not only led to a variety of new, implicit characterizations for different classes, but also inspired alternative characterizations for
\mathbf{FP}
, such as those based on safe recursion
[
6
]
and ramification
[
36
,
37
]
.
Among them, the proposal presented in
[
13
]
offers the advantages of not imposing an explicit bound on the recursion schema and of not assigning specific roles to variables.
These nice properties are obtained by introducing two original notions in the context of discrete ODEs: the idea of
deriving along specific functions
, so that, if the function is properly chosen, the number of computation steps is kept under control, and a special syntactic form for equations, called
essential linearity
and allowing to control the object size.
It was relying on these two features that poly-time computability is captured.

Discrete ODE Schemas

Classical ODEs have been widely used in applied sciences, serving as a model in several contexts involving continuous phenomena.
When a change occurs incrementally rather than continuously, their counterpart is defined by
Discrete ODEs
or
difference equations
, which are, in turn, capable of modeling discrete changes.
An obvious observation is that a discrete function can be naturally described by its right
discrete derivative
defined as
\Delta\mathbf{f}(x)=\mathbf{f}(x+1)-\mathbf{f}(x)
(also denoted
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(x)
in what follows), and that, in this setting, it is also straightforward to rewrite primitive recursion.

A discrete calculus analogous to differential calculus has been developed (see, e.g.,
[
46
]
), and many techniques and results from classical derivatives generalize to the context of discrete derivatives.
The notion of discrete integral is defined so that, for a function
\mathbf{f}(x)
,

\int^{b}_{a}\mathbf{f}(x)\delta x=\sum^{x=b-1}_{x=a}\mathbf{f}(x)

with the convention that
\int^{b}_{a}\mathbf{f}(x)\delta x=0
when
a=b
and
\int^{b}_{a}\mathbf{f}(x)\delta x=-\int^{b}_{a}\mathbf{f}(x)\delta x
, when
a>b
.
The fundamental theorem for the finite calculus states that, given the function
\mathbf{F}(x)
,

\int^{b}_{a}\mathbf{F}^{\prime}(x)\delta x=\mathbf{F}(b)-\mathbf{F}(a).

Given the analogy between finite and classical calculus, it is also natural to consider a theory of discrete differential equations corresponding to the traditional theory of differential equations.
Discrete ODEs are expressions of the (possibly vectorial) form

\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial x}=\mathbf{h}\big(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})\big)

where
\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial x}
stands for the derivative of
\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})
considered as a function of
x
, when
\mathbf{y}
is fixed, i.e.

\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial x}=\mathbf{f}(x+1,\mathbf{y})-\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y}).

The concept of solution is as expected.
When some initial value
\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{y})
is added, this is called an
Initial Value Problem
(IVP) or
Cauchy Problem
.
An IVP can always be put in integral form
and, if it is
defined as above, it always admits a necessarily unique solution over
\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Since coping with negative numbers on classical models of computation can be done through simple encodings, as in
[
12
]
, we will restrict our treatment to non-negative numbers.

As anticipated, discrete ODEs are convenient tools to define functions.

Definition 2.4

(Discrete ODE Schema)
.

Given the functions
\mathbf{g}

and
\mathbf{h}
, we say that
\mathbf{f}
is defined by
discrete ODE solving from
g
and
h
, if
\mathbf{f}
is the (necessarily unique) solution of the IVP:

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{h}\big(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})\big).

The class of functions that are defined using discrete ODEs instead of primitive recursion is easily seen to correspond to primitive recursive functions.
On the other hand, in order to capture fine-grained complexity measures, some restrictions are needed.

Particularly relevant and studied are discrete linear or affine ODEs, namely restricted discrete ODEs of the form:

\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(x)=\mathbf{A}(x)\cdot\mathbf{f}(x)+\mathbf{B}(x),

where
\mathbf{A}
is a matrix and
\mathbf{B}
a vector.
The corresponding schema is defined as follows:

Definition 2.5

(Linear ODE Schema)
.

Given vectors
\mathbf{G}=(G_{i})_{1\leq i\leq k},\mathbf{B}=(B_{i})_{1\leq i\leq k}
, and matrix
\mathbf{A}=(A_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq k}
, we say that
\mathbf{f}
is obtained by
linear ODE solving from
\mathbf{G},\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, if
\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{Z}^{k}
corresponds to the (necessarily unique) solution of the IVP:

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}(x,\mathbf{y})\times\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})+\mathbf{B}(x,\mathbf{y}).

In
[
13
, Lemma 3.15]
, it is shown that, for matrix
\mathbf{A}
and vectors
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{G}
, the solution of the equation

\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(x,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{A}\big(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{h}(x,\mathbf{y})\big)\times\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})+\mathbf{B}\big(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{h}(x,\mathbf{y})\big)

with initial value
\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y})
is

\sum^{x-1}_{u=-1}\bigg(\prod^{x-1}_{t=u+1}\Big(1+\mathbf{A}\big(t,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(t,\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{h}(t,\mathbf{y})\big)\Big)\bigg)\times\mathbf{B}\big(u,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(u,\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{h}(u,\mathbf{y})\big)

with the conventions that
\mathbf{B}(\cdot,\cdot,-1,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y})
and
\prod^{x-1}_{x}\kappa(x)=1
.
Remarkably, this can be interpreted as an algorithm unrolling the computation of
\mathbf{f}(x+1,\mathbf{y})
from the computation of
\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{f}(x-1,\mathbf{y}),\dots,\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})
in a dynamic programming way.

On Length ODE

It is now possible to introduce the idea of deriving along a given function.

Notation 2.6
.

Let
\ell(x)
denote the length of
x
written in binary, i.e.
\big\lceil\text{log}_{2}(x+1)\big\rceil
, so that, in particular,
\ell(0)=0
and
\ell(1)=1
.
Let
\ell_{2}
be a shorthand for
\ell\circ\ell
, that is
\ell_{2}(x)=\ell(\ell(x))
and, more generally for
i\geq 2
,
\ell_{i}(x)=\ell(\ell_{i-1}(x))
.

Definition 2.7

(
\lambda
-ODE Schema)
.

Let
\mathbf{f},\lambda
and
\mathbf{h}

be functions.
Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\lambda}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\lambda(x,\mathbf{y})}=\mathbf{h}\big(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})\big)

(1)

is a formal synonym of
\mathbf{f}(x+1,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})+\big(\lambda(x+1,\mathbf{y})-\lambda(x,\mathbf{y})\big)\times\mathbf{h}\big(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})\big)
.
When
\lambda(x,\mathbf{y})=\ell(x)
, we call (1) a
length
-ODE.

Intuitively, one of the key properties of the
\lambda
-ODE schema is its dependence on the number of distinct values taken by
\lambda
, that is, the value of
\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})
changes only when the value of
\lambda(x,\mathbf{y})
does.

The computation of the solutions of
\lambda
-ODEs has been fully described in
[
13
]
.
Here, we focus on the special cases of
\lambda=\ell
and
\lambda=\ell_{2}
, which are particularly relevant to characterize circuit classes.
Clearly, the value of
\ell(x)
changes (it increases by 1) when
x
goes from
2^{t}-1
to
2^{t}
.
The value of
\ell_{2}(x)
changes when
x
goes from
2^{2^{t}-1}-1
to
2^{2^{t}-1}
.

Notation 2.8
.

For
u\geq 0
, let us denote by
\alpha(u)=2^{u}-1
the greatest integer whose length is
u
; similarly,
\alpha_{2}(u)=2^{2^{u}-1}-1
is the greatest integer
t
such that
\ell_{2}(t)=u
.

If
\mathbf{f}
is a solution of Equation (1) with
\lambda=\ell
and initial value
\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{y})
, then:

\mathbf{f}(1,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})+\mathbf{h}\big(\alpha(0),\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(\alpha(0),\mathbf{y})\big).

More generally, for all
x
and
\mathbf{y}
:

\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{f}(x-1,\mathbf{y})+\Delta(\ell(x-1))\times\mathbf{h}\big(x-1,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(x-1,\mathbf{y})\big)

where
\Delta(\ell(t-1))=\ell(t)-\ell(t-1)
. Hence, by induction, for any
t\leq x
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathbf{f}(t,\mathbf{y})\quad\quad&\text{if }\ell(x)-\ell(t)=0\\
\mathbf{f}(t,\mathbf{y})+\mathbf{h}(t,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(t,\mathbf{y}))\quad\quad&\text{if }\ell(x)-\ell(t)=1\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}\big(\alpha(\ell(x)-1),\mathbf{y}\big)+\mathbf{h}\big(\alpha(\ell(x)-1),\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(\alpha(\ell(x)-1),\mathbf{y})\big).

Indeed,
\alpha(\ell(x)-1)
is the largest integer whose length is different from
\ell(x)
(in fact equal to
\ell(x)-1
, and consequently,
\Delta(\alpha(\ell(x)-1))=1
). For every
t
strictly in between
\alpha(\ell(x)-1)
and
x
, it holds that
\Delta(\ell(t))=0
and
\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{f}(t,\mathbf{y})
.
Starting from
t=x\geq 1
and taking successive decreasing values of
t
, the first difference such that
\Delta(t)\neq 0
is defined by the biggest
t-1
such that
\ell(t-1)=\ell(x)-1
, i.e.
t-1=\alpha(\ell(x)-1)
.
Hence, by induction it is shown that:

\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})=\sum^{\ell(x)-1}_{u=-1}\mathbf{h}\big(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y})\big),

with
\mathbf{h}(\alpha(-1),\mathbf{y},\cdot)=\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})
and, as seen,
\alpha(u)=2^{u}-1
.
Similarly, if
\mathbf{f}
is a solution of Equation (1) for
\lambda=\ell_{2}
with the same initial value, then, for all
x
and
\mathbf{y}
:

\mathbf{f}(x,\mathbf{y})=\sum^{\ell_{2}-1}_{u=-1}\mathbf{h}\big(\alpha_{2}(u),\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}(\alpha_{2}(u),\mathbf{y})\big),

with
\mathbf{h}(\alpha_{2}(-1),\mathbf{y},\cdot)=\mathbf{f}(0,\mathbf{y})
and
\alpha_{2}(u)=2^{2^{u}-1}-1
.

On Linear Length ODE

A second fundamental concept is that of
(essential) linearity
, adapted so to control the growth of functions defined by ODEs.
To introduce it, we start by formally presenting the notion of
degree for a polynomial expression
, which is a slightly generalized version of the corresponding definition in
[
13

.

2.2
On Parallel Computation

2.2.1
Boolean Circuits and Parallel Complexity classes

We consider parallel complexity measures defined by Boolean circuit computation.
A Boolean circuit is a vertex-labeled directed acyclic graph whose nodes are either input nodes (no incoming edges), output nodes (no outgoing edges) or nodes labeled with a Boolean function in a given set.
The set of functions allowed in a Boolean circuit is given by its
basis
, and the set
\{\neg,\wedge,\vee\}
forms a
universal basis
.
A bounded fan-in Boolean circuit is a circuit over the (standard) basis
\{\neg,\wedge^{2},\vee^{2}\}
, while an unbounded fan-in circuit is defined over the (standard) basis
\{\neg,(\wedge^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(\vee^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\}
.
A Boolean circuit with majority gates allows in addition gates labeled by the function
Maj
, that outputs 1 when the majority of its inputs are 1’s.
A family of circuits (
C_{n}
) is
\mathbf{Dlogtime}
-uniform if there is a TM (with a random access tape) that decides in deterministic logarithmic time the
direct connection language of the circuit
, i.e. such that, given

and
t\in\{\wedge,\vee,\neg\}
, it decides if
a
is of type
t
and
b
is a predecessor of
a
in the circuit (and analogously for input and output nodes).

When dealing with circuits, some resources of interests are
size
, i.e. the number of its gates, and
depth
, i.e. the length of the longest path from the input to the output (see
[
48
]
for more details and related results), as well as the choice of bounding or not the fan-in of gates, in particular for small classes.
In this paper we will focus on two small circuit classes of functions, namely
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
and
\mathbf{FTC}^{0}
, which are the first levels of the hierarchies
\mathbf{FAC}
and
\mathbf{FTC}
.

Definition 2.17
.

For

(resp.,
\mathbf{TC}^{i}

-uniform family of Boolean circuits (resp., circuits including majority gates) of polynomial size and depth

In other words,

In this Section, we provide the first implicit characterization of

Remark 3.1
.

Here, functions can take images in

by padding on the right

zeros (this corresponds to multiply by
2^{\ell(x)-u-1}
) and padding on the left
u+\ell(g(\mathbf{y}))
zeros.

•

Compute bit by bit the disjunction of all values computed above.
Clearly, this is done in constant depth.

∎

Observe that this schema is not as weak as it may seem as, together with the
\mathsf{sg}
function, it is already sufficient to express bounded quantification.

Remark 3.10
.

Let
R\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{p+1}
and
h_{R}
be its characteristic function.
Then, for all
x
and
\mathbf{y}
, it holds that
(\exists z\leq\ell(x))R(z,\mathbf{y})=\mathsf{sg}(f(x,\mathbf{y}))
, where
f
is the solution of the IVP:

\displaystyle f(0,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=h_{R}(0,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}

\displaystyle=f(x,\mathbf{y})+h_{R}\big(\ell(x+1),\mathbf{y}\big)

which is an instance of
\ell
-
ODE
1
.
Intuitively,

f(x,\mathbf{y})\neq 0
when, for some
z
smaller than
\ell(x)
,
R(z,\mathbf{y})
is satisfied (i.e.
h_{R}(z,\mathbf{y})=1
): if such instance exists, our bounded search ends with a positive answer.
Clearly, this is conceptually the same as the treatment of bounded quantification by Clote (see
[
18
]
), although in his function algebra
\mathsf{sg}
is not primitive.

Universally bounded quantification can be expressed in a similar way.
Let
\mathsf{cosg}(x)=1-\mathsf{sg}(x)
and consider
\mathsf{cosg}(f(x,\mathbf{y}))
, for
f
defined substituting the value of
h_{R}
with its co-sign. So,
(\forall z\leq\ell(x))R(z,\mathbf{y})=\mathsf{cosg}(f(x,\mathbf{y}))
for
f
such that:

\displaystyle f(0,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{cosg}\big(h_{R}(0,\mathbf{y})\big)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}

\displaystyle=f(x,\mathbf{y})+\mathsf{cosg}\big(h_{R}(\ell(x+1),\mathbf{y})\big).

We now consider a more general schema, called
\ell
-ODE
2
.
Intuitively it allows multiple left-shifting, such that each “basic operation” corresponds to shifting a given value
of a specific number of digits
, determined by
2^{\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))}
.

Definition 3.11

(Schema
\ell
-ODE
2
)
.

Given
g:\mathbb{N}^{p}\to\mathbb{N}
,
h:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
and
k:\mathbb{N}^{p}\to\mathbb{N}
, the function
f:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
is defined by
\ell
-
ODE
2
from functions
g,h
and
k
if it is the solution of the IVP:

\displaystyle f(0,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=g(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}

\displaystyle=\big(2^{\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))}-1\big)\times f(x,\mathbf{y})+h(x,\mathbf{y}),

where
h(x,\mathbf{y})\in\{0,1\}
and if, for some
x
and
\mathbf{y}
,
h(x,\mathbf{y})=1
, then
k(\mathbf{y})\neq 0
.

Again, we are using the multiplication symbol with a slight abuse of notation as the “multiplication” involved in the computation above is of a special kind, i.e. corresponds to multiplying by
2^{i}
, which is computable in constant depth.
Since this schema is introduced to characterize
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
, the constraint imposing
k(\mathbf{y})\neq 0
, when at some point
h(x,\mathbf{y})
takes value 1, is really essential.
Indeed, as we shall see (Section
3.3
), if we omit it,
\ell
-ODE
2
will be too strong, as able to capture binary counting, which is not in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
.

Observe that
\ell
-ODE
1
is a special case of
\ell
-ODE
2
, such that
\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))=1
and that, also for it, the desired closure property holds.

Proposition 3.12
.

If
f
is defined by
\ell
-
ODE
2
from
g
and
h
in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
, then
f
is in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
.

Proof.

There are two main cases to be taken into account.
If
k(\mathbf{y})\neq 0
the proof is similar to that of Proposition
3.9
.
Indeed, for all
x
and
\mathbf{y}
:

\displaystyle f(x,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\sum^{\ell(x)-1}_{u=-1}\Bigg(\prod^{\ell(x)-1}_{t=u+1}2^{\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))}\Bigg)\times h\big(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum^{\ell(x)-1}_{u=-1}2^{\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))\times(\ell(x)-u-1)}\times h\big(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y}\big)

with the convention that
\alpha(u)=2^{u}-1
,
\prod^{x-1}_{x}\kappa(x)=1
and
h(\alpha(-1),\mathbf{y})=f(0,\mathbf{y})
.
As said, multiplication here corresponds to a left-shifting such that its “basic shifting” corresponds to a left movement of
\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))
digits.
As for the basic case, it can be easily shown that this operation can be implemented by a constant-depth circuit.
Then, analogously to Proposition
3.9
, the outermost iterated sum amounts to concatenation (as, by construction,
h(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y})\in\{0,1\}
).

Concretely, we can construct a constant-depth circuit generalizing the procedure defined for the special case of
\ell
-ODE
1
:

•

In parallel, compute the values of
g(\mathbf{y})
and
h(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y})
, for each
u\in\{0,\dots,\ell(x)-1\}
.
This can be done in constant depth by hypothesis.

•

In one step, shift the value of
g(\mathbf{y})
by padding
\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))\times\ell(x)
zeros on the right, and, for
u\geq 0
, shift all values
h(\alpha(u),\mathbf{y})
by padding on the right
\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))\times(\ell(x)-u-1)
zeros (i.e. multiplying by
2^{\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))\times(\ell(x)-u-1)}
) and by padding on the left
\ell(g(\mathbf{y}))+\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))\times(u+1)-1
zeros.

•

Compute bit by bit the disjunction of all the values above.

In the special case of
k(\mathbf{y})=0
and
h(x,\mathbf{y})=0
, for all
x
and
\mathbf{y}
, it holds that
f(x,\mathbf{y})=g(\mathbf{y})
.
This is clearly computable in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
, as corresponding to computing
g(\mathbf{y})
, which is computable in constant depth by hypothesis.
∎

Let us also introduce a weaker form of
\ell
-ODE
2
, called
wk
-ODE
2
.
Intuitively, this schema is defined by simply omitting the “bit addition” part of
\ell
-ODE
2
.
As we shall see in Section
3.2
, together with
\ell
-ODE
1
(and forthcoming
\ell
-ODE
3
), this schema is expressive enough to capture
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
.

Definition 3.13

(Schema
wk
-ODE
2
)
.

Given
g:\mathbb{N}^{p}\to\mathbb{N}
, the function
f:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
is defined by wk-
ODE
2
from
g
if it is the solution of the IVP:

\displaystyle f(0,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=g(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}

\displaystyle=\big(2^{\ell(\mathbf{y})}-1\big)\times f(x,\mathbf{y}).

The
\ell
-ODE
3
Schema

Let us now consider
\ell
-ODE
3
, intuitively corresponding to (basic) right-shifting operations.

Definition 3.14

(Schema
\ell
-ODE
3
)
.

Given
g:\mathbb{N}^{p}\to\mathbb{N}
, the function
f:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
is defined by
\ell
-
ODE
3
from
g
if it is the solution of the IVP:

\displaystyle f(0,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=g(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}

\displaystyle=-\Bigg\lceil\frac{f(x,\mathbf{y})}{2}\Bigg\rceil

where
\lceil\frac{z}{2}\rceil
is a shorthand for
z-(z\div 2)
.

For
x>0
, the above equation can be rewritten as:

f(x,\mathbf{y})=f(x-1,\mathbf{y})-\Delta\ell(x-1)\times\Bigg\lceil\frac{f(x-1,\mathbf{y})}{2}\Bigg\rceil

where, as seen,
\Delta\ell(x-1)=\ell(x)-\ell(x-1)
.
Observe that, also in this case, we are using
\times
with a slight abuse of notation: indeed, we are dealing with “bit multiplication” and multiplying a number by 0 or 1 can be easily done in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
(and re-written in our setting using the basic conditional function, which, as we will see in Section
3.2
, can be defined in
\mathbb{ACDL}
).
In other words,

\displaystyle f(x,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f(x-1,\mathbf{y})\quad\quad&\text{if }\ell(x)=\ell(x-1)\\
f(x-1,\mathbf{y})-\Big\lceil\frac{f(x-1,\mathbf{y})}{2}\Big\rceil\quad\quad&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f(x-1,\mathbf{y})\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\;&\text{if }\ell(x)=\ell(x-1)\\
\Big\lfloor\frac{f(x-1,\mathbf{y})}{2}\Big\rfloor\quad\quad&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f(x-1,\mathbf{y})\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad&\text{if }\ell(x)=\ell(x-1)\\
f(x-1,\mathbf{y})\div 2\quad\quad&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

A bit more formally,

f(x,\mathbf{y})=\Bigg\lfloor\frac{f(\beta(\ell(x)-1),\mathbf{y})}{2}\Bigg\rfloor=\Bigg\lfloor\frac{f(2^{\ell(x)-1}-1,\mathbf{y})}{2}\Bigg\rfloor=f\big(2^{\ell(x)-1}-1,\mathbf{y}\big)\div 2

where
\beta(\ell(z))=2^{\ell(z)}-1
is the greater integer the length of which is
\ell(z)
, i.e. here
2^{\ell(x)-1}-1
is the greatest integer the length of which is
\ell(x)-1
.
Hence, starting with
x>0
, there are
\ell(x)-1
jumps of values.

Proposition 3.15
.

If
f
is defined by
\ell
-
ODE
3
from
g
in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
, then
f
is in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
.

Proof.

As clarified by the remarks above, relying on Definition
3.14
, and since
(a\div 2^{b})\div 2=a\div 2^{b+1}
, it is easily shown by induction that, for all
x
and
\mathbf{y}
:

\displaystyle f(x,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\Bigg\lfloor\frac{g(\mathbf{y})}{\prod^{\ell(x)-1}_{u=0}2}\Bigg\rfloor

\displaystyle=\Bigg\lfloor\frac{g(\mathbf{y})}{2^{\ell(x)}}\Bigg\rfloor

\displaystyle=g(\mathbf{y})\div 2^{\ell(x)}.

This corresponds to right-shifting
g(\mathbf{y})
a number of times equal to
\ell(x)
, which can be easily implemented by a constant depth circuit.
∎

Alternative ODE Schemas

As a last variant, we consider a schema
\ell
-ODE
4
, which encapsulates both left- and right-shifting (with no bit addition) simultaneously. It will be proved useful to give an alternative characterization of
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
.

Definition 3.16

(Schema
\ell
-ODE
4
)
.

Given
g,k:\mathbb{N}^{p}\to\mathbb{N}
, the function
f:\mathbb{N}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{N}
is defined by
\ell
-
ODE
4
from functions
g
and
k
if it is the solution of the IVP:

\displaystyle f(0,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=g(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}

\displaystyle=\big(2^{\pm\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))}-1\big)\times_{\uparrow}f(x,\mathbf{y})

where
\times_{\uparrow}
is so defined that
\frac{v}{2^{v^{\prime}}}\times_{\uparrow}z=\Big\lceil\frac{v\times z}{2^{v^{\prime}}}\Big\rceil
.

Remark 3.17
.

It is clear that both wk-
ODE
2
and
\ell
-
ODE
3
are special cases of
\ell
-
ODE
4
, in particular such that
\pm\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))=\ell(\mathbf{y})
and
\pm\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))=-1
, respectively.

Remark 3.18
.

The IVPs presented in Example
2.16
are not only instances of
\ell
-
ODE
1
(with
h(x,\mathbf{y})=0
), but also of
\ell
-
ODE
4
, considering
\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}=(2^{+\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))}-1)\times_{\uparrow}f(x,\mathbf{y})
with
k(\mathbf{y})=1
.

Proposition 3.19
.

If
f
is defined by
\ell
-
ODE
4
from
g
and
k
in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
,
then
f
is in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
as well.

Proof.

The proof is by cases:

•

Assume
k(\mathbf{y})=0
.
Then, for any
x
and
\mathbf{y}
,
f(x,\mathbf{y})=g(\mathbf{y})
, which is in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
for hypothesis.

•

Assume
+\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))
.
Then,
f
is defined from
g
and
h
as the solution of the IVP:

\displaystyle f(0,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=g(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}

\displaystyle=\big(2^{\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))}-1\big)\times_{\uparrow}f(x,\mathbf{y})

and the proof is similar to that of Proposition
3.12
.
Indeed, since
\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))>1
, we can simply treat
\times_{\uparrow}
as
\times
(where, as said, we use the standard multiplication symbol
\times
with a slight abuse of notation, as it corresponds to multiplication by a power of 2, i.e. corresponds to left-shifting and can be computed in constant depth).
So for every
x
and
\mathbf{y}
:

f(x,\mathbf{y})=\prod^{\ell(x)-1}_{t=0}2^{\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))}\times g(\mathbf{y})

Concretely, this corresponds to left shifting the value of
g(\mathbf{y})
by padding
2^{\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))\times\ell(x)}
zeros on the right and can be computed in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
.

•

Assume
-\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))
.
Then,
f
is defined from
g
and
h
as the solution of the IVP below:

\displaystyle f(0,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=g(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(x,\mathbf{y})}{\partial\ell}

\displaystyle=\big(2^{-\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))}-1\big)\times_{\uparrow}f(x,\mathbf{y}).

The special case of
\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))=1
precisely corresponds to
\ell
-ODE
3
, while the other cases are natural generalizations of it.
Recall that
(2^{-1}-1)\times_{\uparrow}f(x,\mathbf{y})=-\frac{1}{2}\times_{\uparrow}f(x,\mathbf{y})=-\Big\lceil\frac{f(x,\mathbf{y})}{2}\Big\rceil
, and we have seen that, since
(a\div 2^{b})\div 2=a\div 2^{b+1}
, it is proved by induction that, for all
x
and
\mathbf{y}
:

f(x,\mathbf{y})=\Big\lfloor\frac{g(\mathbf{y})}{2^{\ell(x)}}\Big\rfloor=g(\mathbf{y})\div 2^{\ell(x)}.

This intuitively corresponds to right-shifting
g(\mathbf{y})
a number of times equal to
\ell(x)
and can be computed by a constant depth circuit.
When dealing with
\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))>1
, the procedure is analogous but we consider a sequence of right-shifting such that “basic shifting” is no more of one bit.
The number of digits for of the basic right-shifting is determined by the value of
\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))
, namely

f(x,\mathbf{y})=\Big\lfloor\frac{g(\mathbf{y})}{2^{\ell(x)\ell(y)}}\Big\rfloor
.

∎

3.2
An ODE Characterization of
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}

We define a new class of functions, crucially relying on two of the ODE schemas just introduced:

\mathbb{ACDL}=[\mathsf{0},\mathsf{1},\ell,\mathsf{sg},+,-,\div 2,\pi^{p}_{i};\circ,\ell\text{-ODE}_{2},\ell\text{-ODE}_{3}].

Observe that all its basic functions and (restricted) schemas are natural in the context of differential equations and calculus.
In
\mathbb{ACDL}
, multiplication is, of course, not allowed.
Compared to
\mathbb{LDL}
, the linear-length ODE schema is substituted by the two schemas
\ell
-ODE
2
and
\ell
-ODE
3
, characterized by a very limited form of “multiplication” and, as seen, intuitively capturing left- and right-shifting.

In order to prove that
\mathbb{ACDL}
characterizes
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
we start by providing an
indirect
proof that
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}\subseteq\mathbb{ACDL}
.
This is established by showing that basic functions and schemas defining Clote’s
\mathcal{A}_{0}
(so its arithmetization of log-time bounded RAM) can be simulated in our setting by functions and schemas of
\mathbb{ACDL}
.
Preliminary, observe that some important operations “come for free” by composition.
For instance, the modulo 2 operation is defined as
x\;\mathsf{mod\;2}=x-\big\lfloor\frac{x}{2}\big\rfloor-\big\lfloor\frac{x}{2}\big\rfloor
, while the binary successor functions are expressed in our setting as
\mathsf{s}_{0}(x)=x+x
and
\mathsf{s}_{1}(x)=\mathsf{s}_{0}(x)+\mathsf{1}
(being the constant
\mathsf{1}
and + basic functions of
\mathbb{ACDL}
).

3.2.1
The Smash Function 2
ℓ(x)×ℓ(y)
.

The smash function
x\#y:x,y\mapsto 2^{\ell(x)\times\ell(y)}
can be rewritten as the solution of the IVP below:

\displaystyle f(0,y)

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(x,y)}{\partial\ell}

\displaystyle=\big(2^{\ell(y)}-1\big)\times f(x,y).

Indeed,
f(x,y)=\prod^{\ell(x)-1}_{t=0}2^{\ell(y)}\times 1=2^{\ell(y)\times\ell(x)}
.
This is clearly an instance of
\ell
-ODE
2
(and of
wk
-ODE
2
), such that
g(\mathbf{y})=1
,
k(\mathbf{y})=y
and
h(x,\mathbf{y})=0
.
Recall that, since
h(x,\mathbf{y})=0
, also the case
y=0
is properly captured.

Remark 3.20
.

Observe that the IVP corresponding to the smash function is also an instance of
\ell
-
ODE
4
(namely, such that
g(\mathbf{y})=1
,
\pm\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))=+\ell(k(y))
and
k(\mathbf{y})=y
).

3.2.2
The
\mathsf{BIT}
Function

Intuitively, the function
\mathsf{BIT}(x,y)
returns the
y^{th}
bit in the binary representation of
x
.
In order to capture it, a series of auxiliary functions are needed:

•

the
log most significant part function

\mathsf{msp}(x,y):x,y\mapsto\Big\lfloor\frac{y}{2^{\ell(x)}}\Big\rfloor
, which can be rewritten via
\ell
-ODE
3
.

•

the
basic conditional function

\mathsf{if}(x,y,z)
, returning
y
if
x=0
and
z
otherwise, can be rewritten in our setting by composition, using, in particular, the “shift function”
2^{\ell(x)}\times y
(as seen, defined in
\mathbb{ACDL}
using
\ell
-ODE
1
).

•

the
special bit function

\mathsf{bit}(x,y)
, returning 1 when the
\ell(y)^{th}
bit of
x
is 1, can be rewritten in
\mathbb{ACDL}
due to
\mathsf{msp}
.

•

the
bounded exponentiation function

\mathsf{bexp}(x,y)
, that, for any
y\leq\ell(x)
, returns
2^{y}
, can be obtained relying on functions in
\mathbb{ACDL}
, including, in particular
\mathsf{msp},\mathsf{if}
and
2^{\ell(\cdot)}
together with ODE schemas.

Then, using
\mathsf{bexp}
and
\mathsf{bit}
, the desired function
\mathsf{BIT}
can be rewritten in our setting by composition:

\mathsf{BIT}(x,y)=\mathsf{bit}\big(x,\mathsf{bexp}(x,y)-1\big).

Notably, alternative proofs are possible, but the one proposed here, and based on the introduction of
\mathsf{bexp}
, not only has the advantage of being straightforward, but also avoids the unnatural use of the most significant part function,
\mathsf{MSP}
.

Log most significant part function

The
log most significant part function

\mathsf{msp}(x,y):x,y\mapsto\big\lfloor\frac{y}{2^{\ell(x)}}\big\rfloor
can be rewritten as the solution of the IVP below:

\displaystyle f(0,y)

\displaystyle=y

\displaystyle\frac{f(x,y)}{\partial\ell}

\displaystyle=-\Bigg\lceil\frac{f(x,y)}{2}\Bigg\rceil

which is clearly an instance of
\ell
-ODE
3
, such that
g(\mathbf{y})=y
.

Remark 3.21
.

This is not only an instance of
\ell
-
ODE
3
, but also of
\ell
-
ODE
4
, such that
g(\mathbf{y})=y
,
\pm\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))=-\ell(k(\mathbf{y}))
and
k(\mathbf{y})=1
.

Basic conditional function

We introduce the basic conditional function:

\mathsf{if}(x,y,z)=\begin{cases}y\quad\quad&\text{if }x=0\\
z\quad\quad&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Notice that this function is also crucial to rewrite the CRN schema.
As seen, the “shift function”
2^{\ell(x)}\times y
can be rewritten via
\ell
-ODE
1
(see Example
2.16
).
Thus,
\mathsf{if}(x,y,z)
is simulated in our setting by composition from shift, addition and subtraction:

\mathsf{if}(x,y,z)=\big(2^{\ell(1-\mathsf{sg}(x))}\times y-y\big)+\big(2^{\ell(\mathsf{sg}(x))}\times z-z\big).

Indeed, as desired, if
x=0
, then
\mathsf{sg}(x)=0
and
\ell(\mathsf{sg}(x))=0
, so that
\mathsf{if}(0,y,z)=\big(2^{\ell(1)}\times y-y\big)+\big(2^{\ell(0)}\times z-z\big)=y
;
similarly, for
x\neq 0
,
\mathsf{if}(0,y,z)=\big(2^{\ell(0)}\times y-y\big)+\big(2^{\ell(1)}\times z-z\big)=z
.
Generalizing this definition we can capture the more general conditional function below:

\mathsf{cond}(x,v,y,z)=\begin{cases}y\quad\quad&\text{if }x<v\\
z\quad\quad&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Special bit function

Then, we consider the special bit function
\mathsf{bit}(x,y)
returning 1 when the
\ell(y)^{th}
bit of
x
is 1.
This can be rewritten in
\mathbb{ACDL}
due to
\mathsf{msp}
:

\mathsf{bit}(x,y)=\mathsf{msp}(y,x)-2\times\mathsf{msp}(2y+1,x).

Bounded exponentiation function

Finally, we introduce the function
\mathsf{bexp}(x,y)
, that, for any
y\leq\ell(x)
, returns
2^{y}
.
We start by defining
f_{aux}(t,x,i)
by the
\ell
-ODE
1
schema below:

\displaystyle f_{aux}(\mathsf{0},x,i)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{if}(i,\mathsf{1},\mathsf{0})

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{aux}(t,x,i)}{\partial\ell(t)}

\displaystyle=f_{aux}(t,x,i)+h_{aux}(t,i)

where

h_{aux}(t,i)=\mathsf{if}(\ell(t)-i,\mathsf{1},\mathsf{0})

Observe that, as seen,
\mathsf{if}
can be rewritten in
\mathbb{ACDL}
(while
\ell
and subtraction are basic functions).
Then, for
i\leq\ell(x)
, we obtain
f_{aux}(x,x,i)=2^{\ell(x)-i}
.
The function
\mathsf{bexp}

3.4.1
Direct Completeness for

Let
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a class of circuits of polynomial size

.
We assume that each circuit

is in a special normal form, such that it strictly alternates between
\wedge
and

\wedge
gates, at odd levels all gates are
\vee
gates, and the depth of the circuit
d
is even (so output gates are
\wedge

with vertices of in-degree (resp. out-degree)

In this context we keep all the basic functions of
\mathbb{ACDL}
, but we add a set

, for

,

(these predicates are seen as their characteristic functions) and

. The predicates and functions in

will encode the circuit family.
Intuitively,
m(\ell(x))
(denoted

C_{\ell(x)}
, while

describe the level of gates (and, implicitly, their type):

to negation (of input) gates, and

to
\wedge
and
\vee

More precisely, for integers

with

.
Finally, the predicate

Since we aim to define functions over integers, to simplify unessential technical details, we assume that input gates are numbered from

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce what, to the best of our knowledge, are the first implicit characterizations for small circuit classes based on discrete ODEs. By imposing various restrictions on linearity, we obtain straightforward characterizations for both
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
and
\mathbf{FTC}^{0}
.
The striking simplicity of our schemas and the fact that we can pass from the former class to the latter by simply removing a ‘syntactical’ constraint, i.e. switching from
\ell
-ODE
2
to
\ell
-ODE
{}_{2}^{*}
, suggests that these characterizations could clarify the computational principles underlying the classes considered and enhance our understanding of the relationship between circuit computation and the ODEs machinery.
Indeed, it emerges that the features defining circuit computation and their basic steps are mirrored by the shape and constraints on (linear) equations defining our schemas:
for example, when
A
is a power of two, computation corresponds to shifting (the binary representation of) a number, while
B
being restricted to values 0 or 1 ensures that addition involves only bits, thereby preventing any carry operations; additionally, when
A
is allowed to take value 0, binary counting – which is not in
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
but is characteristic of counting or majority gates of
\mathbf{FTC}^{0}
circuits – is captured.
Notably, this framework is expected to generalize to the investigation of other relevant circuit classes.

For this reason, we view these characterizations as a first step in a broader project aimed at capturing additional small circuit classes.
Indeed, given the novelty of this approach, there are several promising directions for future research.
As mentioned, since the restrictions we have applied to linear-length ODE schemas are surprisingly natural, we believe that a similar analysis could also capture computation corresponding to
k
-BRN and WBRN
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
, paving the way for
uniform
characterizations of the
\mathbf{FAC}
and
\mathbf{FNC}
hierarchies through the lens of ODEs. Investigating intermediate classes, such as
\mathbf{FACC(2)}
(i.e.,
\mathbf{FAC}^{0}
with modulo
2
gates), could provide new insight into the relationship between small circuit classes (with varying notions of uniformity) and counting complexity. Another intriguing avenue for exploration is the generalization of our approach to the continuous setting, specifically the analysis of small circuit classes over the reals, following the path outlined by
[
8
,
9
]
for
\mathbf{FP}
and
\mathbf{FPSPACE}
. A challenging direction for future research would also be to develop logical and proof-theoretical counterparts to ODE-style algebras, such as introducing
natural
rule systems (guided by the ODE design) to syntactically characterize the corresponding classes.
