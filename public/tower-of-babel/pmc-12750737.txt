The ethics of a watch-and-wait diagnosis: an exploratory study on the experiences of patients with stage A chronic lymphocytic leukaemia
Authors: Julia Tinland, Oussama Haddadi, Marie-Pierre Aimée Dann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe diagnosis of chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL) is often fortuitous. Stage A patients do not experience severe symptoms and therefore do not require initial treatment. Instead, they are placed under active surveillance in order to track disease deterioration over time. Around half of these patients will never need treatment. Diagnostic disclosure of Stage A CLL thus leads to a great deal of uncertainty. Accordingly, stage A patients’ lives are sometimes more impacted by their diagnosis than by the disease itself. This study aimed to outline the ethical issues surrounding the diagnosis of potentially indolent CLL in stage A patients under active surveillance in France by investigating their experiences, needs, expectations and self-perceptions.MethodsWe co-constructed an online questionnaire with the French leukaemia and lymphomas patient association ELLyE. Comprising 46 closed, semi-closed and semi-open questions, it focused on stage A patients’ experiences regarding their diagnosis and active surveillance, their encounters with medical professionals, as well as the support they received from family members, friends, non-medical healthcare professionals, and patient associations. We conducted a descriptive analysis to examine the results.ResultsOf the 235 participants included in the study, 46% were women. Mean age was 66 years old. All had been diagnosed between 1982 and 2021 (median year of diagnosis was 2013). Almost three-quarters (72.8%) of the sample did not consider themselves as sick. Two-thirds (66.8%) reported feeling uncertain about their disease progression and the feeling of a constant threat hanging over them. Patients recently diagnosed (i.e., < 5 years) were more likely to experience anxiety, anguish and powerlessness related to this uncertainty than patients diagnosed more than ten years previously. Many participants did not wish for more frequent or longer follow-up consultations with hospital-based specialists. Moreover, some wished to distance themselves as much as possible from anything related to cancer care and to live their lives as normally as possible.ConclusionsOur study highlights that the difficulties patients with stage A CLL encounter tend to remain invisible because of limited interactions with medical professionals. Greater acknowledgment and understanding of these difficulties by these professionals are needed in order to adequately address different needs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-025-01343-w.Keywords: Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, Indolent cancer, Watch-and-wait, Active surveillance, Vulnerability, Uncertainty, Biomedical ethics

Background

Health care professionals’ diagnostic disclosure of a serious illness to a person is a key moment in the latter’s life, representing a profoundly embodied, biographical rupture [1]. For these persons who suddenly become patients, disclosure creates an unexpected break between the before and the after. Many medical professionals’ and patients’ accounts of disease trajectories discuss the various dimensions of this momentous announcement, including how the diagnosis is disclosed, the impact of this disclosure, and, for patients, the belief that their lives will never be the same again. Besides the upheaval and turmoil which it brings to a person’s life, diagnostic disclosure of a serious illness also constitutes a social moment where the doctor-patient relationship is delineated, where the authority of medical professionals is affirmed, where healthcare and support resources are allocated, where pathologising labels are attached and where plans of action are decided on [2].

Diagnostic disclosure of cancer is particularly delicate, as society’s collective imagination that this disease unavoidably leads to suffering and death fosters a great deal of anxiety [3]. However, some diagnosed patients have so-called ‘indolent cancer’; these tumours remain stable over time or evolve so slowly that they may never cause severe symptoms or require any treatment. Accordingly, it is possible that these persons’ lives are more impacted by their diagnosis than by the disease itself [4]. This fact raises significant ethical questions surrounding the responsibilities which healthcare professionals and institutions take on towards the patients concerned.

Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL) is a form of leukaemia consisting of a proliferation of small mature B lymphocytes in the blood, in the bone marrow and in the lymph nodes [5]. It is often diagnosed fortuitously, with up to 80% of patients having no symptoms at the time of diagnosis [5, 6]. There are two recognized systems for staging CLL: the Binet system (stages A to C), which is widely used in Europe, and the Rai system (stages 0 to IV), which is more common in the United States [7]. We shall use the Binet system in the present article.

CLL is most frequently discovered after the results of a routine complete blood count revealed lymphocytosis [6, 7] and led to additional examinations and a differential diagnosis [8]. Accordingly, the CLL diagnosis can come as a complete shock for asymptomatic persons. In France, the focus country of the present study, between 4,000 and 5,000 people are diagnosed with CLL each year [8, 9].

Stage A CLL is usually managed using active surveillance, that is to say a treatment-free ‘watch and wait’ approach, to determine how quickly the disease progresses, if at all [6]. To our knowledge, no study to date has provided evidence (in terms of overall survival or time to first treatment) that treatment at stage A is beneficial [6, 7, 10].

Only patients presenting symptoms of active disease (i.e., stages B or C) meet the criteria for treatment [7, 10]. The individual evolution of CLL is heterogeneous: among stage A patients, approximately half will never need any treatment [8]. This subgroup is considered to have an indolent form of the disease (see above) [7]. In France, the approximate median age of persons diagnosed with CLL is 71 years in men and 74 years in women [8]. Accordingly, many stage A patients with indolent CLL will never be directly impacted, whether in terms of quality of life or life expectancy, and can continue to live a ‘normal’ life.

During the active surveillance phase, which can last from as little as a few months (if CLL progression is rapid and significant, leading to treatment) to several decades (if the disease is indolent) [7], stage A patients generally consult a haematologist at regular intervals to review test results and assess disease progression [6]. However, these intervals may be quite long. For example, CLL patients under active surveillance in France only visit a haematologist once or twice a year [8]. Healthcare teams therefore have very little contact with these patients; the consequence is that they become invisibilised despite living with a great deal of daily uncertainty about their future and about the possibility of having to start treatment [11].

What are the ethical implications of the modalities of diagnostic disclosure of CLL and of active surveillance to someone who has stage A cancer? What moral responsibilities from professionals and institutions to the patients concerned do they give rise to? To answer these two research questions, the present study explored the self-reported experiences of cancer diagnosis, the expectations and the needs of stage A CLL patients on active surveillance living in France. To do this, we developed a patient-researcher co-constructed survey which explored, among other things, whether these patients perceived themselves as sick, the emotions they experienced upon diagnostic disclosure of a watch-and-wait diagnosis, their relationship with hospital settings and with healthcare professionals.

Methods

To investigate patients’ experiences, needs and expectations as completely as possible, we chose to use a participatory approach through close collaboration with the French cancer patient association Ensemble Leucémie Lymphomes Espoir (ELLyE). ELLyE is the largest leukaemia and lymphoma patient association in France. It has the widest network of CLL patients in the country, and thanks to its experience, was able to provide us with pertinent insights into these patients’ lived experiences. Up to four members of ELLyE’s directional board were involved as partner patients at different stages during our study, from conceptualisation to the interpretation of results.

Although a validated questionnaire, such as the FACT-LEUKEMIA scale developed by Cella and colleagues [12], would have provided us with accurate and reliable measures of quality of life specifically related to leukaemia, the scope of our study was much wider. We wished to explore CLL patients’ experiences, needs and expectations in relation to their diagnosis and active surveillance in as great a detail as possible. Accordingly, we chose to co-construct an ad hoc online questionnaire with the patient partners from ELLyE (see supplementary file). In order to ensure that themes could emerge naturally and spontaneously, we did not formulate rigid prior study hypotheses.

Participant consent was obtained through the welcoming message of the questionnaire. Specifically, this message informed potential respondents of the voluntary and anonymous nature of their participation and that participation indicated consent.

Study population

Patients diagnosed with stage A CLL on active surveillance (i.e., with an indolent form) who answered the questionnaire were included in the present analysis. Patients with stage A CLL on treatment and patients diagnosed with stages B or C who mistakenly answered the questionnaire were excluded.

The co-constructed questionnaire

The questionnaire comprised five sections for a total of 46 closed, semi-closed and semi-open questions. Questions with a list of possible answers provided participants with the option to add another answer, and to expand on that answer in free text if they wished. The first section explored patients’ experiences related to CLL diagnosis: (i) when they were diagnosed, (ii) by whom, (iii) whether they were provided support by a coordinating nurse1 up to a few weeks after the diagnosis, (iv) what information they retained after being told of their diagnosis. The second section focused, among other things, on active surveillance and patients’ encounters with medical professionals following diagnosis: (i) how frequently they consulted their haematologist and other medical professionals, (ii) whether they were referred to a psychotherapist, (iii) whether they had agreed to participate in a related clinical trial, (iv) whether they felt the need to use alternative medicine. The third section explored the impact of CLL diagnosis on patients’ lives in detail: (i) whether they considered themselves sick or not, (ii) whether they had to disclose their diagnosis to their health insurance provider, (iii) whether their diagnosis may have derailed plans to buy a house, to travel, etc. and (iv) how they dealt with the uncertainty associated with their situation. The fourth section examined family-, friend-, professional-, and association-based support networks and patients’ use of them. Finally, the fifth section collected sociodemographic data: birth year, gender, professional and familial situation, education and income levels, etc.

Data

The questionnaire was hosted online on LimeSurvey between November 2021 and July 2022. Survey anonymity was guaranteed. The link to the survey was shared one time only to everyone on the ELLyE association’s mailing list.

Analysis

We performed a descriptive analysis of frequency and percentage and bivariate analyses with appropriate tests for qualitative variables (chi2) using the statistical software package Stata (StataCorp LLC, version 17). The answers to the semi-open questions gathered qualitative data in order to provide depth and perspective on the quantitative statistical results obtained.

CLL diagnosed more than 10 years prior to the study (i.e., 1982–2011), more than five years prior (i.e., from 2012 to 2016), and less than five years prior (i.e., after 2017) were labelled ‘long-term’, ‘medium-term’ and ‘recent’, respectively.

Results

Participants’ socio-demographic characteristics

Among the 491 persons who responded to the online survey, 308 provided complete questionnaires. Of the latter, the 235 with stage A CLL on active surveillance (i.e., ‘indolent’ cancer and therefore no treatment) constituted our study sample. 46% of the respondents were women. Year of diagnosis ranged from 1982 to 2021 (median 2013). Ages ranged from 40 to 91 years (mean 66 years): 25% were over 72 years, 50% were between 60 and 72 years, and 25% were under 60 years. Unsurprisingly, a large percentage (63.4%) were retired. Education and income levels varied substantially: 34.9% had an education less than or equal to high school diploma, while 66.8% reported higher education. Moreover, the respondents were distributed throughout metropolitan France. A detailed description of the respondents’ sociodemographic characteristics is presented in Table 1.

Sociodemographic characteristics

Experiences related to the diagnosis of stage A CLL

171 (72.8%) of the participants were asymptomatic at diagnosis; their diagnosis came as a great surprise. 58 mentioned symptoms such as exhaustion, swollen glands, susceptibility to infectious diseases, and headaches. Diagnosis was disclosed by a haematologist for 65.7% of the cases, by general practitioners for 26.2%, and by laboratory technicians for 6%.

The duration of consultations when diagnostic disclosure occurred was less than 15 min for 15.1% of the sample and less than 30 min for 38.2%. We found no link between shorter diagnosis-related consultations and self-reported anxiety, or the desire for longer or more frequent follow-up consultations. Specifically, 80.4% of the sample did not desire more frequent consultations, while 19.6% did (“Since the disease does not seem to be progressing in a worrying way, I do not see the need for it”). Moreover, 66.8% did not desire longer follow-up consultations while 28.9% did (“Yes, I would like to have more explanations each time. For example, why would it not be beneficial to start immunotherapy even without symptoms”).

The vast majority of participants (91.1%) reported that they were not offered a support consultation with a coordinating nurse in the weeks following diagnostic disclosure, despite this being a recommendation in the 2003–2007 French National Plan [13]. The perception was that professionals did not really take into account the concern which some participants felt (“Doctors and haematologists tend to underestimate the difficulties felt when you are ‘just’ a stage A). Many expressed that they would have appreciated this kind of support (“I would have liked to meet with a nurse just after my diagnosis was disclosed”). The moment when diagnostic disclosure took place was very emotionally charged; 44.7% recalled a feeling of anxiety, 32.8% anguish and 31.5% powerlessness. Furthermore, 26.8% recalled a feeling of fear and 22.1% sadness. Some mentioned they felt calm (18.7%), while others felt hopelessness (19.6%).

Experiences of active surveillance and indolent disease

Emotional impact and perceptions of health

Almost half of the sample (45.5%) said they experienced difficulties later on which were related to the announcement of their diagnosis. Of these, 75.7% reported anxiety, 47.7% anguish, 44.86% sleep problems, 20.6% a lack of motivation, and 12.2% anger. Others described difficulties in communicating their diagnosis to their loved ones, misunderstandings or conflicts, professional burn-out or other professional difficulties. The time since diagnosis appeared to be a pertinent factor in terms of current (i.e., at the time of the study) feelings of anguish: people with a medium-term or recently diagnosed CLL were more likely to report current anguish than those with a long-term CLL (p-value = 0.065; see Fig. 1). The direct comparison between ‘long-term’ and ‘recent’ CLL participants was even more striking (p-value = 0.024) (“With each new blood test, the anguish is there […]. Just because you’ve got stage A doesn’t mean it’s not going to get worse”). Interestingly, self-reported current anxiety did not seem to decrease with time post-diagnosis, the way anguish did. A small number of participants with long-term CLL reported anxiety and/or anguish (“I’ve been living with the disease for at least 40 years, with stress and a knot in my stomach”). Moreover, 14% of the respondents (n = 33) were advised to have psychotherapy, 32.77% expressed that they felt they needed it, and 14.5% (n = 34) mentioned they had in fact gone for therapy. Patients with a recent or medium-term CLL were also more likely (p-value = 0.013) to express feeling powerless when faced with a lack of disease progression prevention recommendations than patients with a long-term CLL (“When will treatment begin? Why is nothing being done?”), suggesting that the length of time that had passed since diagnosis may have impacted current experiences of powerlessness (see Fig. 2).

Difficulties experienced following CLL diagnosis

Feelings of powerlessness when faced with the lack of preventive recommendations

Interestingly, 50.6% (n = 119) of our respondents believed that their diagnosis also brought something positive to their lives: a new relationship with time (74%), a new outlook on life (64.7%), stronger relationships with loved ones (38.7%), and new values (36.1%) (“I try not to stress myself about silly things and to remain zen by putting things into perspective”).

Very importantly, 72.8% of the sample did not see themselves as sick. (“No, because the absence of any change [in disease progression] led me not to consider myself as sick”). A majority (60.4%) of the sample considered themselves to be in either good or very good health, while 34.5% considered their health to be average, and 5.1% poor. No participant considered they had very poor health. People with a recent or medium-term CLL were slightly more likely to report poor health (see Fig. 3).

Perception of sickness

Active surveillance

For a majority of the sample (73.2%), active surveillance entailed meeting with their haematologist after routine blood tests every six months (31%) or every year (35.3%). A non-negligible percentage (6.8%) only consulted with their haematologist once every two years.

With regard to their follow-up and care, participants’ primary criticism was a lack of clear information (31.9%) (“explanations are too fast or too technical”). This was followed by unsatisfactory consultations (11.5%) and a disappointing follow-up (8.1%) (“I feel like ​​people who are well [i.e., Stage A patients with indolent cancer] are not of much interest to medical professionals, which I can understand”). Some respondents (6%) also mentioned having difficult relationships with healthcare professionals. (“I’ve got such a strong feeling that I’m annoying my oncologist that I want to see her as little as possible”; “With the haematologist, the less the better”). A very large majority (95.7%) of the sample had sought additional information about CLL, whereas only 3.4% had not. Of the former, 81.3% used the internet, 50.7% asked their haematologists, 44.4% approached patient associations, and 2.7% asked their coordinating nurse. Besides information, participants relied on patient associations for news and to bond with peers with similar experiences (“I learned 10 times more about my disease with this association than with haematologists”). The desire to interact with fellow patients was mentioned on several occasions (“When you’re ill, it’s easier to talk with people going through the same thing. People who aren’t ill panic and loved ones get stressed, only making things worse”).

Sixty-percent (60.1%) of the sample answered they were slightly or very reassured by hospitals as a place of care. One fifth (20.2%) reported that hospital settings made them slightly or very anxious. Of the people who desired more frequent (19.6%) or longer (28.9%) consultations, some said that having them would perhaps lead to a more holistic approach to care (“the importance of life hygiene, diet, sports, the evolution of treatments… Topics that are important for patients’ quality of life. More in-depth information makes it possible to involve patients in their own care and also to take the drama out of certain aspects of the disease”; “I would like longer consultations so that a ‘daily life advice’ section could be included”). These two quotations underline the sometimes very different needs these patients may have.

Life projects and lifestyle

A total of 69.8% of the sample (n = 164) said that their life projects were not impacted by their CLL diagnosis, whereas 28.5% (n = 67) said that they were. Among the latter, 38.8% mentioned that they had had plans to buy a house, 43.3% travel plans, and 26.9% (n = 18) a bank loan, but that the diagnosis had affected these plans. One respondent noted that doctors tended not to “even know the difficulties related to borrowers’ insurance. The AERAS convention2 is not a right to borrow, but a right to obtain a quote for borrowers’ insurance, and this nuance is significant”. In terms of other events which participants linked to their diagnosis, 3 people had gone through divorces or separations, 11 had moved home, 17 had changed their jobs and 9 planned to take early retirement. Although a slight majority of the sample (51.5%) declared that CLL diagnosis had had a limited impact on their life, 66.8% (n = 157) said they felt a constant threat hanging over them.

One-fifth (22.1%) of the sample used alternative medicine in addition to active surveillance (see Table 2). The reasons given for this use included the need for more holistic approaches to their care, better life hygiene, and the ability to fill the gaps which strictly biomedical approaches do not cover. Some declared that they used alternative medicine to improve their chances of survival, despite this approach not currently being officially recognised or recommended as a preventive measure. A substantial number of participants (n = 39) mentioned their use of alternative medicine to their haematologist, most of the latter showing open-mindedness or indifference according to the respondents. One participant said that her haematologist indicated strong opposition to alternative medicine; the participant subsequently changed haematologist.

Relationships with others

Two-thirds (67.2%) of the respondents discussed their CLL diagnosis with loved ones and/or acquaintances (mostly spouses, children, family and friends, and more rarely, colleagues and acquaintances). Several of those who did not discuss their diagnosis with these people cited their fear of being seen differently, and of being looked at with worry or pity—especially by friends—as reasons for not doing so (“I talked to one of my close friends who said ‘hurry up and enjoy life’, like I was going to die imminently. A lack of knowledge… the word leukaemia is synonymous with not having much time to live”; “Monolithic thinking and terror associated with the word ‘cancer’, pity, empathy for others is often inseparable from the fear of one’s own death…etc.”; “At the same time, you’re sick without others seeing you as sick, and that gives you a strange feeling of guilt and injustice…”). The absence of severe symptoms presented an obstacle to discussion, in this sense, as it required the capacity for loved ones to be able to “understand a disease that cannot be seen”. This highlighted how isolating the experience of such a diagnosis was for many participants.

Discussion

Diagnosis as an object of study and the moral responsibilities arising from it

In society’s collective imagination, cancer unavoidably leads to suffering, and death; this fosters a great deal of anxiety [3, 14]. Furthermore, chemotherapy—one of the most common forms of treatment—is often likened to an aggressive poison that destroys the body in the same way it attacks the disease itself [15]. While some tumours are asymptomatic and either progress very slowly or not at all (i.e., indolent form), others, like pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, progress silently and are often diagnosed late, the result being a very negative prognosis [16, 17]. Therefore, being asymptomatic does not mean no risk of disease progression.

Investigating the impact of diagnosis alone, without it being inextricably linked to the impact of treatment is very difficult in oncology. As approximately half of the patients diagnosed with stage A CLL will never need any treatment [8], we used this particular context to better understand patients’ needs and medical professionals’ ethical responsibilities in a population whose quality of life may be more impacted by the diagnosis than by the disease itself. To cite Jutel, “the diagnostic moment irrevocably [shifts] an individual’s life story despite the absence of any material change in condition” [2].

The affirmation of the authority which the medical world places over patients’ experiences is reflected in the decision by medical professionals to disclose a diagnosis of cancer—a disease socially associated with suffering and death—to someone with stage A indolent CLL who is asymptomatic (or does not have severe symptoms) and who does not feel sick. Effectively, haematologists become the purveyors of a medical ‘truth’ that supersedes the power of these lived experiences [18]. Following Esserman and Varma’s line of questioning in their short debate: “Should we rename low risk cancers?” [19], we can question whether the stage A CLL diagnostic category, as it is conceptualised, named and used today, is a fitting classification for these patients. In any case, the decision to diagnose CLL in this way brings with it a great deal of responsibility towards these patients [20, 21].

Dealing with uncertainty and vulnerability

Many stage A patients with potentially indolent CLL find themselves in an unclear and anxiety-inducing situation; Timmermans and Buchbinder call these persons “patients-in-waiting” [22]. A majority (66.8%) of the people we surveyed reported feeling a constant threat hanging over them. Many reported that the lack of recommendations on preventing disease progression, combined with a profound sense of uncertainty [23] and vulnerability [21], led to anxiety, anguish and a feeling of powerlessness. As Jutel puts it, “the certainty of diagnosis does not necessarily predict the outcome of the disease with certainty” [2]. In Mishel’s reconceptualised uncertainty in illness theory, uncertainty is defined as a cognitive state created by the inability to determine the meaning of illness-related events [23].

Moreover, stage A patients with potentially indolent CLL have a more fragile immune system and greater levels of anxiety than the general population, reflecting what Rogers et al. call inherent vulnerabilities (arising from our corporeality, our neediness, our dependence on others, and our affective and social natures) [21]. In addition, they tend to be older and, for some, quite isolated, which the same authors refer to as situational vulnerabilities (caused or exacerbated by one’s personal, social, political, economic, or environmental situation). Even more importantly, the fact that they are labelled as cancer patients but effectively walking the thin line between health and illness, is often misunderstood and invisibilised. Rogers et al. call these pathogenic vulnerabilities (they exacerbate existing vulnerabilities or generate new ones through inadequate responses) [21].

An invisibilised population in need of early support

In our study, invisibilisation was compounded by the fact that under the French healthcare system, participants only saw their haematologist once or twice a year on average, and rarely ever interacted with other medical professionals. The duration between follow-up visits in our study was much longer than in other healthcare systems; for example, in the US, some stage A CLL patients meet their haematologist up to four times a year [6]. Not only does this diminish their invisibilisation, it may also positively impact their experiences of distress and anxiety. This highlights the difficulties in the French system to identify particularly vulnerable patients whose experience of diagnosis is highly distressing and whose care and support needs are not met. Infrequent follow-up consultations also put the responsibility of addressing patients’ distress and uncertainty squarely on the shoulders of the haematologist who makes the diagnostic disclosure [20, 23]. However, this means having sufficient time, training and skills to recognise more acute signs of distress, as well as an institutional organisation and structure that plans and caters for the referral of these patients to other qualified healthcare professionals, including psychotherapists and coordinating nurses (see above). With regard to the latter, official French cancer diagnosis disclosure procedural guidelines provide newly-diagnosed patients with a consultation with a coordinating nurse. This intervention acts as a first window of opportunity for all of the stakeholders involved to acquire, provide and reformulate essential information, to express needs, and to have their voices heard in a quieter, calmer context [24]. This consultation with a coordinating nurse also aims to provide better access to supportive care and to strengthen collaboration with general practitioners. However, as our results show, despite this guideline, very few of our participating stage A CLL patients had been offered such a consultation, and were simply placed on active surveillance when it was found that they had an indolent form of cancer. This would suggest that despite the long-held belief that the diagnostic disclosure of cancer is something very serious [18], the possible negative impact on a patient of disclosing a potentially indolent stage A CLL is not fully recognised and is not a priority for haematologists; this is echoed in the fact that very few of our participants (8,9%) had had a consultation with a coordinating nurse after their diagnosis, despite official French recommendations. This is a problem, especially given the distress and anxiety which recently diagnosed patients may experience. Identifying diagnosed patients who are particularly distressed and isolated as early as possible is essential. In our study, persons with a recent (i.e., < 5 years) CLL diagnosis experienced the most anguish. This highlights the importance of providing adequate support early on.

Similarly, identifying patients who do not consider themselves to be sick and who wish to limit their interactions with the hospital world to avoid being labelled as cancer patients is equally important. Better coordination between haematology departments and general practitioners could lead to a form of graded care for Stage A CLL patients whereby those who desire to stay away from the hospital setting could be followed by their general practitioners as long as their cancer does not progress.

The ethical responsibilities of medical professionals and of the healthcare system more generally are therefore tied to the widely varying needs of Stage A indolent CLL patients, who are put in a position of uncertainty and vulnerability by a watch-and-wait follow-up. Our results also indicate that other actors may also take on some of these ethical responsibilities, including patient associations, who play an essential role in providing information, counselling and support, as well as peer support networks [25].

Limitations

Several study limitations need to be taken into consideration: first, our decision to construct an ad-hoc questionnaire to explore dimensions of patients’ experiences other than their quality of life, prevents comparison with other relevant studies. Second, the invitation to answer the questionnaire was only distributed through the association ELLyE, meaning that only persons who subscribed to its mailing list could take part. Third, the online questionnaire was distributed through one platform (LimeSurvey), meaning that persons without sufficiently high technological literacy were not represented. This is especially pertinent given that the target population tends to be over 60 years old. Finally, we did not collect data on supportive care such as intravenous Immunoglobulin or greater access to vaccinations.

Conclusion

A majority of our stage A CLL participants did not consider themselves to be sick but felt that a constant threat was hanging over them. The anguish, anxiety and feelings of powerlessness triggered by the diagnosis itself was a source of significant distress for some, especially patients recently (< 5 years) diagnosed. While most felt reassured in hospital settings, infrequent consultations with medical professionals regarding their disease left them feeling isolated and uninformed. Many found it difficult to deal with the uncertainty of their situation (disease progression, changes to lifestyle), and the hardship they faced tended to be invisibilised and not acknowledged. All these findings highlight the need to improve early care pathways at diagnosis and immediately after. Organisational actions need to be put in place to ensure that newly-diagnosed patients receive a consultation with a coordinating nurse, as per official French guidelines, and that their needs are genuinely and fully taken into consideration. Medical professionals (haematologists and coordinating nurses) require better training in recognising particularly distressed patients so that they can guide them and refer them to other health professionals including general practitioners and psychologists. Involving patients with experiential knowledge in care could prove very beneficial to all stakeholders.
