Association of physiological parameters with final pH in beef
Authors: Rudney da Silva Maia Júnior, Emanuela Costa Fernandes, Patrícia Fernandes Nunes da Silva Malavazi, Jilssyane Richelle da Silva Souza, Anderson Azevedo Mesquita, Patrícia Gelli Feres de Marchi, Cassio Toledo Messias, Bruna Laurindo Rosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Serum activity of the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase was correlated with final pH.•Age measured by dental chronology was correlated with final pH.•Glucose measured using a portable glucometer did not correlate with the pH and differed from that measured by the laboratory method.•Non-castrated males showed different correlations from castrated males.Keywords: Biomarkers, Blood, DFD, Glucometer, Nellore

Introduction

Dark, firm, and dry (DFD) beef, also known as dark-cut beef, is a common problem in the beef industry (Fuente-García et al., 2022). In Brazil, approximately 4.53% of carcasses contain this type of meat, which causes considerable economic losses to the beef industry (Ferreira et al., 2024; Rosa et al., 2016). Notably, approximately 80% of the Brazilian herd comprises animals of the Nellore breed (Bos taurus indicus) or ringed breeds (Rosanova et al., 2025), which is known for its high reactivity, making the animals highly stressed and difficult to manage. These characteristics contribute to the greater tendency of this breed to produce DFD meat after slaughter (Mota-Rojas et al., 2024).

This problem occurs because of failures in pre-slaughter management, which causes stress in the animals, reducing muscle glycogen reserves and, consequently, preventing the adequate production of lactic acid, which is responsible for the decrease in pH values during the process of transforming muscle into meat (Fuente-García et al., 2022; Grandin, 1980; Ijaz et al., 2022).

In the face of a stressful event, the sympathetic nervous system is activated, in which the animal's body enters a "fight or flight" stage. In response to this event, corticotropin (CRH) is released, which stimulates the pituitary gland to release adrenocorticotropic hormones (ACTH). This hormone induces the adrenal glands to release catecholamines (dopamine, epinephrine, and norepinephrine) and cortisol (Klein, 2020). These substances have several functions in the body, including increasing heart rate, blood pressure, and blood flow to the muscles. Moreover, they raise blood glucose levels through glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, preparing the body to react to the "fight or flight" situation (Klein, 2020; Loudon et al., 2019; Nelis et al., 2022). This process prevents a proper drop in pH to between 5.3 and 5.8 within 24 h of slaughter owing to the depletion of glycogen stores (Hou et al., 2014).

In addition, non-castrated (entire) males tend to exhibit greater stress responsiveness compared to castrated males, which is associated with the action of testosterone on the behavior and reactivity of these animals (George & Rosvall, 2022). Consequently, animals with more reactive behavior may show higher concentrations of cortisol and catecholamines in the bloodstream (Burdick et al., 2011).

Additionally, in situations of muscle damage or intense physical exertion during pre-slaughter management, there is extravasation of the enzymes creatine kinase (CK) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) into the bloodstream. Therefore, these enzymes may constitute potential biomarkers for predisposition to dark-cut meat in cattle (Nelis et al., 2022). Several studies have sought to correlate blood parameters, such as cortisol, glucose, CK, and LDH, with final meat quality in different breeds and species (Choe & Kim, 2014; Chulayo & Muchenje, 2017; Ferreira et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2018; Loudon et al., 2019). However, currently there is no consensus among these studies on which biomarkers are actually correlated with the ultimate pH (pHu), and there are few studies on Nellore cattle that correlate these blood parameters with the final quality of beef.

Therefore, this study aimed to correlate physiological parameters (cortisol, glucose, CK, LDH, and age) with pHu of beef from Nelore cattle, to identify possible differences in these correlations between non-castrated and castrated males, and to investigate the relationship between glucose measured using a portable human glucometer and pHu, in order to assess the feasibility of using this device for early prediction of the animals’ pHu.

Material and methods

This experiment was approved by the Ethics Committee on the Use of Animals (CEUA – UFAC) under protocol number 36/2024 and process number 23107.028235/2024-59 and approved by the member committee.

Description of the study area

The present experiment was carried out in a slaughterhouse with the Federal Inspection Service (S.I.F.), located in the municipality of Rio Branco (9°58′26″ S; 67°48′27″ W), State of Acre, located in the Amazon biome, Northern Brazil. The municipality has an Am type climate, characterized as hot and humid equatorial, according to the Köppen classification, with a temperature of 25.2–32°C and average annual rainfall of 1747 mm (IPAM, 2020).

Pre-slaughter handling

The cattle used in this experiment were slaughtered in accordance with Brazilian legislation, specifically the Regulation of Industrial and Sanitary Inspection of Products of Animal Origin (RIISPOA), of August 18, 2020, and Ordinance No. 365, of July 16, 2021, issued by the Ministry of Agriculture, Livestock, and Supply (MAPA) (Brasil, 2020, 2021). Two hundred cattle (n = 200) were evaluated and divided into two groups: 100 non-castrated Nelore animals (entire) and 100 castrated animals of the same breed.

The animals were transported to the slaughterhouse on the day prior to slaughter using six-axle trucks and were subsequently housed in separate pens for each group. In these pens, they remained at rest until the following day, aiming to reduce transport-induced stress and to allow for the restoration of glycogen reserves. In addition, they were subjected to a 12-hour feed withdrawal period, with free access to water.

On the day of slaughter, the animals were driven by trained personnel to the slaughter area using handling flags in order to minimize stress. In the slaughterhouse, each animal was individually restrained in a stunning box, stunned using a pneumatic captive bolt gun, and subsequently exsanguinated within an interval of up to one minute.

Blood collection

Soon after exsanguination, blood was collected in a sterilized and disposable plastic cup and distributed into previously identified tubes as follows: 4 mL in tubes with a clot activator, in duplicate, and 4 mL in tubes containing sodium fluoride. The tubes were packed in an isothermal box containing Gelox and sent to the laboratory shortly after collection. The remaining amount of blood was used to measure glucose by means of a portable glucose meter (G-Tech Lite®), and for the values obtained by this method, the denomination "Blood Glucose" was adopted.

In the laboratory, the tubes were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to obtain plasma and serum within 60 min of the last collection. After separating the plasma and serum, approximately 1.5 mL of each sample was pipetted and transferred to previously identified microtubes. Plasma obtained from tubes containing sodium fluoride was used to determine glucose levels. To present the values obtained by this method, the denomination "Plasma Glucose" was adopted. Serum from the tubes with clot activator was used for the analysis of cortisol by means of the chemiluminescence method and for measuring CK and LDH.

Laboratory parameters

Serum cortisol was measured in an automated Immulite 2000 (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics, Immulite 2000, New York, USA) using commercial Immulite kits® 2000 Veterinary Cortisol (Glyn Rhonwy, Gwynedd, Wales, UK). The analyses were conducted according to the manufacturer's specifications, based on the principle of competitive immunochemiluminescence in an automated system, with reading and interpretation performed using the analyzer system. The concentrations were automatically determined using the equipment and expressed in ng/mL.

Plasma glucose levels and serum CK and LDH activities, were obtained by spectrophotometric reading in a semi-automatic biochemistry analyzer (Bioplus Bio-200®, Barueri, São Paulo, Brazil), using commercial kits, according to the manufacturer's instructions (Labtest Diagnóstica®, Lagoa Santa, MG, Brazil). Plasma samples were collected in tubes containing sodium fluoride as a glycolytic inhibitor, and the analysis was performed using the colorimetric enzymatic method, with an end-point reaction, and the results were expressed in mg/dL. Enzymes were measured using the kinetic method, and the results are expressed in U/L.

Measurement of pHu and identification of age of animals

Twenty-four hours after slaughter, at the time of shipment, the pHu of the carcasses from which blood had been collected was evaluated. A portable pH meter (AK87, AKSO Produtos Eletrônicos Ltda., Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil) was calibrated using buffer solutions with pH 4.0, 7.0, and 10.0. The pH meter probe was inserted into the Longissimus thoracis (LT) muscle, located between the 12th and 13th ribs, in the A-band of each carcass, and the pH value was recorded after stabilizing the reading. The ages of the animals were determined using the dental chronology method, considering the number of permanent teeth on the label of each carcass evaluated.

Statistical analysis

Data were tabulated in Microsoft Excel (Version 2016) and subjected to descriptive statistical analysis. Differences between groups were assessed using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test, due to the lack of normality and homoscedasticity of the residuals. The same test was applied to compare glucose determination methods. Correlations between variables were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ), considering that the residuals did not follow a normal distribution. Correlation strength was interpreted as weak (ρ < 0.40), moderate (0.40 ≤ ρ < 0.70), or strong (ρ ≥ 0.70). Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi software (Version 2.3), adopting a significance level of 5% (p < 0.05).

Furthermore, the pHu values were higher than those considered ideal for beef (Hou et al., 2014).

The high values of cortisol, blood glucose, and plasma glucose were possibly due to the stress experienced by the animals during pre-slaughter management (Table 1). When faced with a stressful stimulus, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis is activated, promoting the release of cortisol and catecholamines. These hormones act on the liver, stimulating glycogenolysis (degradation of glycogen into glucose) and gluconeogenesis (synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate compounds), resulting in an increase in the concentration of glucose in the bloodstream (Loudon et al., 2019; Klein, 2020).

Although whole males tend to be more reactive to stress, likely due to the effects of testosterone, and therefore generally exhibit higher cortisol and glucose levels than castrated males, the observed result may be related to more stressful pre-slaughter management for castrated males, which led this group to present higher cortisol and glucose levels than whole animals (Table 1). This finding is similar to that of Bolado-Sarabia et al. (2018), who observed that immunocastrated animals were more docile and became slower during management in the slaughterhouse, which resulted in greater use of electric shocks to move them, and consequently, higher cortisol levels. According to these authors, castration is an efficient alternative for reducing physiological indicators associated with stress, such as cortisol, glucose, and CK; however, it should be associated with good pre-slaughter management in the slaughterhouse.

As shown in Table 1, mean CK and LDH values higher than the reference values for bovine species were identified in both groups. CK catalyses the transfer of a phosphate group from ATP to creatine, forming phosphocreatine and ADP, whereas LDH catalyses the conversion of pyruvate to lactate during anaerobic glycolysis process (Daroit & Brandelli, 2008; Matarneh et al., 2018; Nelis et al., 2022). Factors, such as improper handling during loading at the farm, transport, and unloading at the slaughterhouse and water and food restrictions can alter the activities of these enzymes (Chulayo & Muchenje, 2017). Under these conditions, CK and LDH are released into the bloodstream in response to muscle damage or intense physical exertion during pre-slaughter management, and increased levels are indicative of stress (Chulayo & Muchenje, 2017).

Additionally, a significant difference in LDH levels was observed between the non-castrated (whole) and castrated males (Table 2). Whole males have higher levels of testosterone, a hormone that stimulates protein synthesis, muscle hypertrophy, enzyme activity, and muscle energy metabolism, than castrated males (Anaruma et al., 2020; Silva et al., 2025). Therefore, the LDH levels tended to be higher in this category (Table 1), given that this enzyme acts on muscle energy metabolism. Krotkiewski et al. (1980), when evaluating the effect of castration on rats, observed a reduction in LDH levels in castrated animals compared to non-castrated males, and the subsequent administration of testosterone restored the LDH levels.

The mean pHu was 6.26 for whole males and 6.30 for castrated males, high values in relation to the ideal range of 5.3 to 5.8 for beef (Table 1) (Hou et al., 2014). This result may be related to the depletion of muscle glycogen reserves resulting from glycogenolysis due to stressful pre-slaughter management and the intense physical effort to which these animals were possibly subjected. Because of these factors, there is little lactic acid production after slaughter, causing the pHu of meat to be above ideal (Fuente-García et al., 2022; Ijaz et al., 2022; Loudon et al., 2019). This high value is indicative of typical DFD meat (pHu > 6.09), which causes significant losses in the meat-packing industry (Fuente-García et al., 2022; Ferreira et al., 2024; Ijaz et al., 2020).

Comparison of glucose measurement methods

Additionally, the different glucose measurement methods were compared between both groups (Table 3). A statistically significant difference was observed between the methods analyzed (p < 0.001), indicating that the methods resulted in divergent values. This result indicates that despite refrigerating the tubes containing blood and sodium fluoride, the glucose levels had reduced. This finding is similar to that of Chan et al. (1989) who observed a rapid drop in glucose levels in the first hour after collection using tubes containing sodium fluoride. This effect occurs because sodium fluoride acts primarily as an inhibitor of enolase, which slows but does not immediately stop glycolysis. Thus, the initial steps of the glycolytic pathway remain active for some period of time, allowing cells to metabolize glucose even in the presence of sodium fluoride (Mikesh & Bruns, 2008). Therefore, a portable glucometer is a viable and reliable option for glucose measurement in such instances.

Association between physiological factors and pHu in intact and castrated males

Table 4 describes the correlation analyses and the degree of correlation between the variables evaluated in this study for both groups. Cortisol showed a strong positive correlation (p < 0.001) with blood and plasma glucose levels because of the action of this hormone, which stimulates an increase in the availability of substrates used in the process of hepatic gluconeogenesis, resulting in a higher concentration of glucose in the bloodstream (Klein, 2020). Similarly, Jardim et al. (2024), when evaluating behavioral and physiological measures as indicators of animal welfare in cattle, identified a strong positive correlation (p < 0.001) between cortisol concentration, capillary glucose levels (measured using a glucometer), and plasma glucose levels. This finding is also in agreement with a study by Choe and Kim (2014) carried out in pigs, in which the authors observed a correlation between cortisol and blood glucose levels (p < 0.001). Although the mean cortisol and pH values of the analyzed carcasses were high (Table 1), indicating pre-slaughter stress, no correlation was observed between these variables, in contrast to the results described by Lu et al. (2018) and García-Torres et al. (2021).

A strong positive correlation between blood glucose and plasma glucose (p < 0.001) was also observed, as one variable increased, the other also increased (Table 4). This result was expected because both represent the same parameter but are measured by different methodologies. Choe and Kim (2014) identified an association between high glucose values and low pHu in pig carcasses, indicating that glucose measurement soon after slaughter may be a useful parameter for the early identification of dark-cut pork. However, when we evaluated the same association in bovine species, no relationship was observed between these two variables, regardless of the method used to measure glucose levels (Table 4). In another similar study, also carried out with cattle, Lu et al. (2018) observed a positive correlation between glucose values and pHu, that is, as glucose values increased, the pHu of the evaluated carcasses also increased.

A weak positive correlation between CK and LDH (p < 0.05) enzymes was noted and is shown in Table 4, indicating that as the serum activity of one of these enzymes increased, that of the other also increased. This correlation occurs because both enzymes are released into the bloodstream during muscle damage or after intense physical exertion due to stressful pre-slaughter management (Chulayo & Muchenje, 2017; Loudon et al., 2019). This finding is similar to that reported by Chulayo and Muchenje (2017), who observed a correlation between these enzymes. However, no relationship was observed between CK and pHu of the carcasses (Table 4), which differs from the results of Lu et al. (2018) and Loudon et al. (2019).

A weak negative correlation was observed between LDH and plasma glucose (p < 0.05), a moderate negative correlation between LDH and pHu (p < 0.01), and a strong negative correlation between LDH and dental chronology (p < 0.001) (Table 4). LDH and glucose are indicators of animal welfare; therefore, in situations of stress, there is usually an increase in their concentrations in the bloodstream (Bartling et al., 2025; Chulayo & Muchenje, 2017; Lu et al., 2018). However, in this study, a weak negative correlation was observed between these variables (p < 0.05), contradicting the results of Escalera-Valente et al. (2021). Because of the action of adrenergic hormones and cortisol, circulating glucose in these animals is elevated, whereas the glycogen present in the musculature is reduced or depleted (Nelis et al., 2022). Therefore, soon after slaughter, the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, performed by LDH, was reduced, which may explain the negative correlation between LDH and glucose, as presented in Table 4. Another factor that corroborates this finding is the high pH observed in both groups (Table 1), indicating low lactic acid production in these carcasses. However, in this study, no correlation was observed between LDH and blood glucose levels.

LDH is responsible for catalyzing the conversion of pyruvate to lactate during anaerobic glycolysis; therefore, the higher the activity of this enzyme, the greater the lactate production, which reduces the pH. However, the possible lower lactate production due to a lower LDH activity may have resulted in a higher pHu, which would justify the negative correlation observed in Table 4 (Apaoblaza et al., 2020; Matarneh et al., 2018). However, pHu does not depend only on the action of this enzyme, but also on the amount of glycogen present in the muscle, diet, pre-slaughter management, and method of cooling the carcass (Apaoblaza et al., 2020; Kuffi et al., 2018; Lu et al., 2018). This result differs from that of Lu et al. (2018), who report a positive correlation between the variables.

However, the strong negative correlation between LDH and dental chronology (p < 0.001) indicates that this enzyme is inversely proportional to animal age. Stalder et al. (1997) compared the LDH activity in young and old cattle and observed that the activity of this enzyme was higher in young animals. In view of this, there was a tendency for the activity of this enzyme to decrease with age, which would explain the results.

The pHu showed a strong positive correlation with animal dental chronology (p < 0.001) (Table 4). This result indicates that older animals tend to have carcasses with high pH after slaughter, a result similar to that observed by Mendes et al. (2024) who evaluated Nellore cattle. Several factors can contribute to an animal's reactivity, such as previous negative experiences, sex, management, temperament, and genetics (Grandin, 1997; Maffei, 2009). Therefore, this set of factors associated with advancing age in animals tends to contribute to more reactive behavior in cattle (Grandin & Shivley, 2015). Consequently, these animals become more responsive to stressors during pre-slaughter management and, after slaughter, have a greater tendency to have carcasses with high pHu (Lensink et al., 2001; Lu et al., 2018).

Another factor that may have contributed to this result is that, in this experiment, Nellore cattle (Bos taurus indicus) were evaluated, which have the characteristic of being more reactive and temperamental than Bos taurus taurus animals (Mota-Rojas et al., 2024). Nellore cattle are classified as more reactive and produce meat with a smaller rib eye area, darker color, and higher pHu than those classified as calmer (Sant'Anna et al., 2019). In Brazil, an extensive breeding system predominates, with 80% of the animals finishing on pastures (Anaruma et al., 2020). This system is characterized by low human-animal interactions, causing greater reactivity when they are managed compared to those raised under intensive management (Grandin, 1997; Maffei, 2009; García-Torres et al., 2021; Mota-Rojas et al., 2024).

The type of feed also influences the final quality of meat, and cattle fed exclusively on pasture have a higher level of oxidative enzymes and a lower level of glycolytic enzymes than those fed grains, which results in lower lactate production and, consequently, a lower drop in pH after slaughter (Apaoblaza et al., 2020).

When evaluating only the entire male group, cortisol did not present a correlation with blood and plasma glucose levels, which differed from what was observed in castrated males (p < 0.001) (Table 6). The correlation between cortisol and LDH differed between the groups. Intact males showed a weak positive correlation (Table 5), whereas castrated males showed a moderate negative correlation (Table 6). The weak correlation between cortisol and LDH (p < 0.05) may have been influenced by stressors associated with pre-slaughter management, which promoted an increase in cortisol concentrations and, simultaneously, an increase in LDH levels as a result of greater physical exertion and possible muscle injury (Loudon et al., 2019; Klein, 2020; Nelis et al., 2022).

Although a correlation between cortisol and pHu was identified (Table 5), this relationship differs from the findings of Lu et al. (2018), García-Torres et al. (2021), and Ferreira et al. (2026), as the correlation observed in this study was negative, indicating that as one variable increased, the other decreased. This result also differs from that observed in the group of castrated animals, in which no relationship was identified between cortisol and the pHu of the evaluated carcasses (Table 6). Cortisol, when evaluated in isolation, may not constitute a good marker for predicting pHu, since its detection may be limited by the response dynamics of the HPA axis, characterized by relatively slow activation, or by negative feedback mechanisms that can restore basal levels under chronic stress conditions (Nelis et al., 2022). Mormède et al. (2007) reported that glucocorticoid secretion shows considerable variation as a function of diurnal and seasonal factors, feed intake, temperature, and humidity, in addition to the physiological state of the animal. Thus, these factors, together with hormonal differences between intact and castrated males, may have contributed to the difference in the observed results.

The correlation between cortisol and dental chronology variables showed completely distinct results, with intact males presenting a strong negative correlation (p < 0.001) (Table 5), whereas castrated males showed a weak positive correlation (Table 6). Another correlation observed was that all males showed a moderately negative correlation between blood glucose and dental chronology (p < 0.01) (Table 5), which differed from the group of castrated animals, in which this type of correlation was not observed (Table 6). This moderately negative correlation is consistent with the findings of Bourgon et al. (2017), who identified a negative correlation between glucose levels and dental chronology, with younger bulls having higher glucose levels. Another factor that may have contributed to this result is that young bulls tend to have a higher amount of testosterone than older animals (Baharun et al., 2021). This hormone potentiates the HHA axis, resulting in an increased release of catecholamines (Kutlikova et al., 2020). This release generates an increase in blood glucose levels via glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis (Klein, 2020). In contrast, males neutered in the absence of testosterone are less reactive and have more stable blood glucose levels (Bolado-Sarabia et al., 2018).

Fights, horns and mounts resulting from the mixing of whole males from different groups during pre-slaughter management are factors that may have increased the stress of these animals and, consequently, increased circulating glucose levels, resulting in dark-cut meats. This problem occurs due to the hierarchy and dominance among whole males, intensified when animals from different groups are grouped together during pre-slaughter management, resulting in fights to determine a new hierarchy among them (Grandin, 1980; Warriss et al., 1984). In both groups, no correlation was observed between the plasma glucose method and dental chronology. In view of this, further research is needed to identify the factors that explain the lack of correlation between these variables through this method of measuring glucose.

The group of intact cattle showed a strong negative correlation between LDH and pHu (p < 0.001) (Table 5). In contrast, Lu et al. (2018), when evaluating Holstein–Friesian bulls, reported a positive correlation between these variables. Similarly, Ferreira et al. (2026), using Nellore bulls, also observed a positive association between LDH and pHu. On the other hand, in the present study, no relationship was observed between these parameters in castrated males (Table 6).

The pHu also showed a strong positive correlation with dental chronology (p < 0.001) (Table 5). However, in the group of castrated animals, no correlation was observed between these variables (Table 6). Thus, pHu appears to be directly related to the age of intact animals, such that the older the animal, the greater the likelihood that the carcass will produce meat with a high pHu. Muižniece et al. (2020) evaluated the association between age and carcass pHu and observed a relationship between older heifers and higher pH. However, this effect was not observed in bulls.

Ferreira et al. (2026) report that the association between different variables does not necessarily imply a cause-and-effect relationship. Similarly, García-Torres et al. (2021) noted that stress-related biomarkers may respond differently depending on factors such as breed, production system, and pre-slaughter handling. In the combined data analysis (Table 4), considering both groups (castrated and intact), it was possible to observe some correlations between the evaluated variables and pHu. However, when each group was analyzed separately, distinct correlation patterns were identified, as the evaluated categories showed differences both in the type of correlation (positive or negative) and in its strength (Table 5, Table 6). Thus, the association between physiological factors and pHu may vary according to the animal category analyzed.

Conclusions

It was observed that the physiological parameters cortisol, LDH, and dental chronology were correlated with pHu, with significant associations identified only in intact males. These differences may be attributed to hormonal and behavioral factors, as well as to pre-slaughter handling, which influence the evaluated parameters. Thus, the use of these parameters as early predictors of pHu may vary according to the animal category analyzed.

The glucose measured using the different methods did not present a correlation with pHu, and its measurement using a portable glucometer showed a statistical difference in relation to the laboratory method, which is considered the gold standard.
