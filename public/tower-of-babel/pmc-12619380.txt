The role of eCyPA as an inflammatory biomarker for predicting 28-day mortality in ARDS patients
Authors: Juan Chen, Xue Dai, Jing Lv, Meijun Liu, Ewan C. Goligher, Wang Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is the primary manifestation of systemic inflammatory response syndrome in the lungs. Extracellular cyclophilin A (eCyPA) as a novel inflammatory marker, this study aims to investigate the expression of eCyPA in ARDS and its relationship with patient prognosis.MethodsThis retrospective study collected serum samples from adult patients diagnosed with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) upon their admission to ICU. Additionally, venous blood and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid were obtained from a lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced ARDS mouse model. The expression levels of extracellular cyclophilin A in serum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The data collection period extended from December 2021 to December 2023.Binary logistic regression analysis, ROC curve, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis were employed to evaluate the correlation with 28-day all-cause mortality in ARDS patients. An incremental prognostic value analysis was conducted to further investigate the enhancement in predictive value when combined with clinically significant indicators (platelet count and oxygenation index). Additionally, we assessed the dynamic trends of eCyPA and cytokines in serum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid at 0, 1, 3, and 7 days in LPS-induced ARDS mouse models.ResultsA total of 50 patients were enrolled. The serum eCyPA expression level was significantly higher among non-survivors compared to survivors (7.2 ng/ml, interquartile range, IQR, 5.9–8.2 vs. 10.1 ng/ml, IQR 7.5–10.6; p = 0.002), and this finding remained consistent across various subgroups. eCyPA is an effective predictor of 28-day mortality in ARDS patients (AUC = 0.754). When combined with platelet count and oxygenation index, the prognostic value assessment improved the AUC to 0.887 (P < 0.001). In ARDS mice, eCyPA increased continuously over 7 days in serum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, the trend is similar to inflammatory cytokines in ARDS mice.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that eCyPA may serve as a potential biomarker for predicting 28-day mortality in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03406-3.

Background

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), also known as acute lung injury, is a condition characterized by damage to the pulmonary capillary endothelial cells and alveolar epithelial cells following severe infections, trauma, burns, and other precipitating factors. This damage results in refractory hypoxemia and respiratory distress [1]. ARDS is marked by an excessive inflammatory response and has a global mortality rate ranging from 30% to 50% [2, 3]. Despite advancements in critical care and organ support technologies, ARDS continues to pose a significant socioeconomic burden, with no effective therapeutic interventions currently available [4].

Cyclophilin A (CyPA) is an intracellular receptor exhibiting peptidyl-prolyl cis/trans isomerase activity, which is crucial for various cellular functions, including protein folding and transport [5]. Intracellular CyPA can be secreted by multiple cell types, such as vascular smooth muscle cells, macrophages, and endothelial cells, in response to pro-inflammatory stimuli, including hypoxia, infection, or oxidative stress, via autocrine or paracrine mechanisms [6–8]. Extracellular cyclophilin A (eCyPA) is implicated in numerous systemic diseases affecting the cardiovascular, digestive, urinary, and nervous systems, as well as rheumatic immune diseases [9–13]. Recently, eCyPA has attracted attention as a novel inflammatory biomarker among researchers. In a mouse model of systemic inflammation induced by a high-fat diet, significantly elevated levels of eCyPA and CD147 expression were observed in various organ tissues [14]. Furthermore, extensive studies have confirmed the expression of eCyPA in several inflammatory diseases, including vascular inflammation, rheumatoid arthritis, sepsis, and periodontitis [14–17]. eCyPA primarily mediates inflammatory responses by modulating the NF-κB pathway, which enhances the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [18–20]. For instance, some studies indicate that viral infections induce eCyPA via the CD147 receptor, leading to the upregulation of IL-17, IL-6, and IL-1β in lung epithelial cells [21].

The relevance of eCyPA in patients with ARDS is unknown. This study undertook to investigate the expression of eCyPA in ARDS and its relationship to patient outcomes. Additionally we measure eCyPA trajectories during experimental ARDS in murine models.

Methods

Selection of study population and variables

This study was conducted in a single ICU at second affiliated hospital of chongqing medical university. Patients were eligible if they had age >18 years, had been admitted to the ICU >24 h; and met the Berlin definition and diagnostic criteria for ARDS [14]. Patients were excluded if they were pregnant, had undergone radiotherapy or chemotherapy within the last 8 weeks, received immunosuppressive therapy within the last 4 weeks, or were prior recipients of organ transplants.

All patients and their families involved in this study were informed and provided their consent by signing informed consent forms. This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Ethics Approval Number: 2022729), and this study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki while all procedures involving animals were approved by the institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Chongqing Medical University. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Specimen collection and measurement

Processing of human serum samples: 5 ml of venous blood was collected from the cubital vein of the subjects, with samples from patients taken within 24 h of ICU admission. The collected serum samples were centrifuged at 3000 r/min for a minimum of 10 min. The upper serum layer was then transferred to EP tubes and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. Serum levels of eCyPA, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, CCL-2, and CXCL-10 were quantified using ELISA kits (Quanzhou Ruixin Biotechnology, China), and the microplate reader employed was the Rayto RT-6100.

Murine experiments

Collection and Measurement of Mouse Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid: SPF-grade male C57BL/6J mice, aged 8–10 weeks and weighing 20–25 g, were procured from Chongqing Enbi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The mice were housed under standard lighting conditions and provided with ad libitum access to food in the animal facility of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Animal Facility of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, Qualification Certificate No.: SYXK (Yu) 2023-0005). Healthy SPF-grade male C57BL/6J mice were randomly assigned to a control group (0-day group), a 1-day group, a 3-day group, and a 7-day group, following a random number table. After anesthetizing the mice with inhaled anesthetic, secure the mice on a simple operating table. Perform tracheal intubation using an 18 G indwelling needle and instill 5 mg/kg of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to establish an ARDS model. Administer an equivalent volume of sterile saline to the control group. At the time points of 24 h, 72 h, and 1 week, collect whole blood from the mice, allow it to stand at room temperature for 1 h, and then centrifuge at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Collect the serum and store it at −80 °C for future use. Expose the neck, separate the neck vessels and trachea, and attempt to puncture the upper third of the trachea with a needle. Pre-trim the tip of the 18 G indwelling needle into a wedge shape, perform tracheal intubation at the puncture site, and secure it with surgical thread. A recovery rate exceeding 80% is deemed acceptable (aspirate 1 ml of pre-cooled PBS three times). The collected bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was preserved in pre-chilled EP tubes, followed by centrifugation at 1200 r/min for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was stored at −80 °C in an ultra-low temperature freezer for future analysis. The levels of CyPA in serum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, along with the levels of cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, CCL-2, and CXCL-10, were measured using ELISA kits obtained from Quanzhou Ruixin Biotechnology, China. The instrument utilized was a microplate reader: Rayto RT-6100.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measure of this study is the 28-day mortality rate among patients with ARDS. The secondary outcome measures include the duration of mechanical ventilation, the length of stay in the ICU, the necessity for continuous renal replacement therapy, and the administration of vasoactive drugs.

Statistical analysis methods

The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of continuous variables. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), while categorical data were presented as counts (%). Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution were reported as mean ± SD, whereas skewed data were represented as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were described in terms of frequency (percentage). To compare baseline characteristics between the two groups, categorical variables were analyzed using the χ² test, and continuous variables were compared using either the student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on the distribution of the data.

Using the R package ‘rms’, a restricted cubic spline analysis was conducted to explore the potential nonlinear relationship between log2-EASIX and the 28-day survival outcome in patients with ARDS. The cutoff value obtained from the ROC curve was employed to categorize the data into high eCyPA and low eCyPA groups. Additionally, the R packages ‘survminer’ and ‘survival’ were utilized to plot Kaplan-Meier survival curves for the two groups, and the log-rank test was applied to compare the survival curves between these groups.

Conduct subgroup analyses to explore the potential associations of CyPA levels across various demographic and clinical subgroups. These subgroups include age (greater than 65 years vs. less than or equal to 65 years), sex (male vs. female), degree of respiratory failure (oxygenation index greater than 150 vs. less than or equal to 150), overweight status (BMI greater than 24 vs. less than or equal to 24), smoking status, alcohol consumption, presence of hypertension, presence of diabetes, presence of coronary heart disease, and presence of malignant tumors.

Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, along with the area under the curve (AUC) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), were analyzed using the R package ‘pROC’. And then a calibration plot and decision curve analysis (DCA) based on predicted probability were performed for clinical use while using the R package ' rms’and ' ResourceSelection’ and ' pROC’. Reclassification analysis was performed by calculating the continuous net reclassification improvement (cNRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) with the R package ‘PredictABEL’, based on a median follow-up period of 28 days. The cNRI serves as a metric for evaluating enhancements in risk prediction, indicating the net percentage of patients accurately reclassified due to the inclusion of a new marker, regardless of whether they experience the event. A significant (p < 0.05) and positive cNRI is anticipated when an additional marker is associated with the outcome and contributes additively to predicting it [22].

Statistical analyses were conducted using R package (version 2024.04.1), GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.2), and SPSS (version 27). A p-value of less than 0.05 (two-tailed) was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of enrolled patients

This study included a total of 50 participants. The overall average age of enrolled patients was 72 years, with a predominance of males (36/50, 72%). The average oxygenation index was 171, the average SOFA score was 6.06, and the average APACHE II score was 17. The average eCyPA level was 7.61 ng/ml.

Primary outcome

Of the patients enrolled, 29 survived (mortality rate, 42%). Compared to survivors, non-survivors exhibited higher eCyPA levels (survival vs. non-survival: 7.15 (5.88, 8.22) vs. 10.10 (7.49, 10.62); p = 0.002), lower systolic blood pressure, lower oxygenation index, lower platelet count, a higher prevalence of coronary heart disease, and more frequent use of vasoactive drugs (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

In univariate logistic regression, diastolic blood pressure, platelet count, oxygenation index, eCyPA, the presence of comorbid coronary heart disease, and the administration of vasoactive drugs were found to be statistically associated with 28-day mortality in patients with ARDS(S1). Further multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that eCyPA (OR (95% CI): 2.15 (1.13 ~ 4.09), P = 0.020) and the use of vasoactive drugs (OR (95% CI): 51.60 (1.91 ~ 1395.26), P = 0.019) were identified as independent risk factors for 28-day mortality in ARDS patients (Table 2).

The restricted cubic spline (RCS) curve is referenced against the cutoff value derived from the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. In the RCS analysis (Fig. 1a), a significant nonlinear relationship was observed between eCyPA levels and the odds ratio (OR) of 28-day mortality in patients with ARDS (P_nonlinear = 0.216). The eCyPA cutoff value was determined to be 7.61 ng/ml using the ROC curve, categorizing patients into high and low eCyPA groups. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed a significant difference in survival probabilities between these groups (p = 0.020), with the high eCyPA group exhibiting markedly lower survival rates compared to the low eCyPA group (Fig. 1b).

In the high eCyPA group (eCyPA > 7.61 ng/ml) and the low eCyPA group (eCyPA ≤ 7.61 ng/ml), we conducted a further analysis of the relationship between eCyPA expression levels and the inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. The analysis revealed that the median IL-1β level in the high eCyPA group was significantly higher (81.55 [IQR 72.54–99.78]; p < 0.001) compared to the low eCyPA group (50.36 [36.72–68.79]; p < 0.001). Furthermore, the high eCyPA group exhibited elevated levels of TNF-α (85.57 [76.06–99.14] vs. 77.56 [51.49–89.68]; p = 0.041), CXCL10 (165.12 [135.46–191.56] vs. 123.69 [79.85–149.80]; p < 0.001), and IL-6 (51.11 [45.72–62.23] vs. 46.27 [41.18–51.22]; p = 0.052). However, no statistically significant difference in CCL2 was observed between the two groups (p = 0.108) (Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis revealed that across ten predefined subgroups—age (greater than 65 years vs. less than or equal to 65 years), gender (male vs. female), severity of respiratory failure (oxygenation index greater than 150 vs. less than or equal to 150), overweight status (BMI greater than 24 vs. less than or equal to 24), smoking status, alcohol consumption, presence of hypertension, presence of diabetes, presence of coronary heart disease, and presence of malignant tumors—the exploratory analysis demonstrated that the proportional effect of high eCyPA (eCyPA > 7.61 ng/ml) on the 28-day mortality rate in ARDS patients was consistent across all subgroups (all p for interaction ≥ 0.05) (S2).

The parameters assessed upon ICU admission (quantitative variables), including platelet count (AUC = 0.748), oxygenation index (AUC = 0.762), and eCyPA (AUC = 0.754), demonstrated statistically significant correlations in predicting 28-day mortality in patients with ARDS. The results of this study indicated that the incorporation of eCyPA significantly improved the predictive performance for 28-day mortality in ARDS patients compared to models utilizing only conventional clinical indicators (platelet count and oxygenation index). Notably, the combined model that integrated platelet count, oxygenation index, and eCyPA exhibited the highest predictive efficacy for mortality in ARDS patients (AUC = 0.887) (Fig. 3A), and in the calibration plot, the combined model predicted probability approximately matched the actual mortality in ARDS patients (Fig. 3B). What’s more, in the decision curve, the mode l shows higher net benefit than any single parameter (above P/F, eCyPA, PLT) if the threshold probability was greater than 20% (Fig. 3C). The incremental prognostic value was further supported by the continuous Net Reclassification Improvement (cNRI) ([95% CI], 1.1626 [0.7205–1.6047]; p < 0.001) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) ([95% CI], 0.2716 [0.1498–0.3933]; p < 0.001), which validated the superior predictive performance of the combined model over those based solely on individual indicators (Table 3).

Dynamic changes of eCyPA in ARDS mice model

To further explore the dynamic changes of eCyPA during the progression of ARDS, this study measured the expression levels of eCyPA and various cytokines in the serum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of LPS-induced ARDS mouse models. The results indicated that the expression levels of eCyPA gradually increased in the control group (0-day) and at 1, 3, and 7 days post-induction. Similarly, the expression levels of the cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, CCL-2, and CXCL-10 also exhibited an increasing trend over time. Furthermore, the expression levels of eCyPA in the BALF were largely consistent with those observed in the serum (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Extracellular Cyclophilin A (eCyPA), a novel inflammatory biomarker, plays a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of inflammatory diseases. Under various pro-inflammatory conditions, such as necrosis, reactive oxygen species (ROS), lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation, and radiation, CyPA is released extracellularly. As eCyPA, it mediates inflammatory responses through specific binding with CD147, exerting a pronounced chemotactic effect on lymphocytes, monocytes, and neutrophils [23–25]. CyPA promotes inflammation via the NF-κB pathway, as well as the ERK1/2 and MAPK signaling pathways [12, 18]. Research indicates that elevated levels of CyPA not only induce significant chemotaxis of monocytes and neutrophils to inflammatory sites but also activate the MAPK signaling pathway, resulting in increased expression of cytokines and chemokines [21].

Previous studies have demonstrated that retrospective analyses revealed significantly elevated levels of CyPA in the plasma of severe and critical COVID-19 patients, as assessed by ELISA, in comparison to mild cases and healthy individuals. Furthermore, CyPA levels in the lung tissues of COVID-19 patients exhibited a strong positive correlation with CD68, CCL2, and IL-6 [21], suggesting that CyPA is associated with the infection by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) and may serve as a key pro-inflammatory factor in this context. Additionally, the levels of eCyPA in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of patients suffering from ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) were found to be 5–6 times higher than those observed in healthy volunteers. In VILI mice, the levels of eCyPA in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid were positively correlated with the severity of lung injury and the levels of markers indicative of alveolar epithelial permeability [26], indicating the potential utility of eCyPA as a biomarker for alveolar epithelial damage.

The findings of this study indicate a significant correlation between eCyPA levels and 28-day all-cause mortality in patients with ARDS. This association remains consistent across various subgroups. In the ARDS mouse model, eCyPA expression shows a sustained upward trend within the first seven days following the onset of ARDS, and this trend is consistent in both mouse serum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid.These results suggest that eCyPA may serve as a valuable parameter for risk stratification in ARDS patients and could potentially act as an independent risk factor for this condition. Unfortunately, This study did not identify a statistically significant association between eCyPA levels and secondary outcome measures, including the duration of mechanical ventilation, the use of vasoactive drugs, continuous renal replacement therapy, and ICU length of stay, and this may be attributed to various confounding factors such as patients’ financial status, religious beliefs, the size of the study sample, and baseline patient conditions and so on.

In both the high eCyPA group and the low eCyPA group, we conducted a further analysis of the relationship between eCyPA and inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. Our findings indicate that in the high eCyPA group, the expression levels of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and CXCL10 were significantly higher than those observed in the low eCyPA group. In the ARDS mouse model, the dynamic trend of eCyPA over the 7 days following ARDS onset was consistent with the trends of inflammatory cytokines. However, no significant differences were noted in the expression of the aforementioned common clinical inflammatory cytokines between the 28-day survival group and the non-survival group among ARDS patients. This suggests that eCyPA, as a novel inflammatory biomarker, can effectively reflect the inflammatory levels in ARDS patients, akin to traditional clinical inflammatory cytokines, and may offer superior predictive efficacy for 28-day mortality in these patients. The identification of biological phenotypes in patients with Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) has garnered increasing attention. Biomarkers are considered closely related to the clinical manifestations of ARDS and may mitigate misclassification issues, which complicate the identification of clinical phenotypes [27]. Previous studies employing independent latent class models have demonstrated that hyperinflammatory ARDS, in comparison to hypoinflammatory ARDS, exhibits significantly higher levels of plasma inflammatory biomarkers, including IL-6, IL-8, and TNF [28, 29]. Based on the statistical associations observed in this study between eCyPA and the inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNFα, we hypothesize that eCyPA may serve as a novel biomarker for subtyping ARDS.

However, our study reports a sustained inflammatory response one week after LPS instillation in the murine ARDS model. This finding is atypical, as LPS-induced inflammation generally peaks earlier [30]. We propose that this discrepancy may be related to the differing doses of LPS utilized in the experimental design. In the study conducted by Lax S et al., the dose of LPS administered per mouse was 50 µg, whereas in our experiment, the dosage was 5 mg/kg per mouse. Based on this standard, the LPS dose used in our study was nearly double that administered to mice of similar body weight. Consequently, we contend that the overall inflammatory response in the mice of our study was more severe, thereby explaining the prolonged elevation of inflammation. Furthermore, studies have measured the levels of TNF-α, IL-113, and IL-6 in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid on days 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 12, revealing that these inflammatory factors remained continuously elevated in the non-survivor group [31]. Similarly, in patients suffering from COVID-19-related acute respiratory distress syndrome, the non-survivor group exhibited higher levels of IL-6 and IL-10 upon admission compared to the survivor group, with these levels demonstrating a gradual increasing trend over time (days 1, 3, and 5) [32]. Thus, we believe this difference can primarily be attributed to the inconsistent severity of inflammation in ARDS mice.

The oxygenation index and platelet count are closely linked to the prognosis of Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS). The combined action of platelets and fibrinogen mediates endothelial injury, which is considered to significantly contribute to the pathogenesis of critical pulmonary ARDS [33]. Furthermore, some researchers suggest that Mfn2 in megakaryocytes and platelets plays a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial phenotype, which is significantly associated with the 28-day mortality rate in patients with acute lung injury [34]. Regardless of whether one refers to the Berlin definition [35] or the recently proposed “Global ARDS Definition” [36], the core criterion for defining ARDS remains the oxygenation level [37]. Typically, a lower oxygenation index necessitates more complex and intensive treatment [38]. Our study further corroborates these findings, demonstrating that levels of eCyPA, the oxygenation index, and platelet count are influential factors in the 28-day mortality rate of ARDS patients. The incremental prognostic value indicates that the combination of these three factors can significantly enhance predictive efficacy for the 28-day mortality rate in ARDS patients.

Although our study did not find that eCyPA alone, as a novel biomarker, has a significant advantage in predicting the prognosis of ARDS patients compared to other biomarkers, our research revealed that combining eCyPA with traditional biomarkers (such as the oxygenation index and platelet count) significantly enhances the predictive capability for 28-day mortality in ARDS patients. This finding provides a new reference for the study of ARDS prognostic models or subtypes. Moreover, despite extensive research on biomarkers associated with ARDS, there remains a lack of specific therapeutic drugs for its treatment [39]. A study has shown that intratracheal instillation of MM-284, a non-immunosuppressive cyclosporine A derivative that inhibits the extracellular signaling of CypA, can block CypA, improve the survival rate of hyperventilated mice, and alleviate alveolar-capillary barrier dysfunction and epithelial injury [40]. Additionally, Tianzhang Song et al. indicated in a mouse model of sepsis that targeting CypA is beneficial in reducing sepsis-associated lung injury [41]. Furthermore, two phase 3/4 clinical studies have focused on improving the prognosis of COVID-19-infected patients through the combination of CsA(a inhibitors of Cyclophilin A) [42]. Therefore, targeting eCyPA is expected to become a new therapeutic option for ARDS treatment.

However, this study has some limitations: (1) The analysis is retrospective, and there is limited information on the exact causes of death; thus, it remains unclear whether the higher mortality observed in patients with elevated eCyPA is indeed attributable to ARDS itself. (2) This study is a single-center investigation with a small sample size, making the findings applicable only to critically ill patients within the same institution. Further validation of these results through multi-center prospective studies with larger sample sizes is necessary. (3) There is a lack of data on the dynamic changes of eCyPA in clinical ARDS patients throughout the disease course; however, we have addressed this deficiency in our ARDS mouse model.

Conclusion

eCyPA, as a novel inflammatory biomarker, serves as a practical parameter for risk stratification in patients with ARDS and may potentially function as a new inflammatory biomarker for subtyping ARDS.
