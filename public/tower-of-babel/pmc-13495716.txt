High-Throughput Measurement of Biodegradation Rates Using the Clear-Zone Assay
Authors: Sarah H. M. Av-Ron, Natalie D. Mamrol, Dylan J. Walsh, Katharina A. Fransen, Bradley D. Olsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plastic pollution has become a major concern, driving the search for biodegradable-polymer alternatives. Existing standards for measuring biodegradation are often costly, are time-consuming, and lack reproducibility between different laboratories. Previous research demonstrated the development of a new cost- and time-efficient biodegradation test based on the clear-zone assay, enabling polymers to be rapidly classified as biodegradable or nonbiodegradable. This work builds upon that assay, addressing challenges in improving automation, signal-to-noise, and analytical methods to enable a more quantitative comparison between materials and meaningful extraction of biodegradation rates. The sample preparation process was modified to ensure uniform particle size distribution, and changes in sample preparation, data processing, and analysis improved output data accuracy. The design of a fully automated biodegradation monitoring system that runs inside an incubator increased the reproducibility and reliability of data acquisition. These modifications enhance the high-throughput biodegradation assay, enabling an improved accuracy.

Introduction

With the increasing concern about plastic pollution, there is an active search for biodegradable polymer alternatives. − However, predicting the rate of degradation of plastic in the environment is extremely complex. Plastic biodegradation depends on environmental parameters such as temperature, , moisture, light exposure, , microbiome composition, , and pH , in addition to the plastic’s properties like surface-to-volume ratio, , crystallinity, , hydrophobicity, thermal properties, and molecular weight. This has led to the creation of biodegradation testing standards for many different environments such as composting, , marine water, sewage sludge, and soil. Most standards are expensive and time-consuming, requiring equipment for gas monitoring (such as CO2) and testing times of several months. Furthermore, biodegradation tests rarely use standardized microbial inoculum; results from the same standardized test tested in different laboratories are difficult to compare. The limited data and varying results from biodegradation studies have stifled the development of biodegradable polymer alternatives.

Albright and Chai have identified four challenges in polymer biodegradation testing that need to be addressed which are (1) a lack of standardization in polymer biodegradation testing, (2) the long test durations, (3) demanding analytical methods, and (4) a lack of a framework for assessing the overall persistence of polymers. Some studies have made progress regarding these issues. Da Silva et al. developed a new test using a Bartha respirometer to track the progress of mineralization in the samples, which led to a reduction of testing cost and time while reproducing the results of the standard tests regarding polymer biodegradability. Šerá et al. modified biodegradation standards to accelerate the biodegradation process by increasing the temperature, demonstrating a 4-fold increase in degradation rates and comparing results from two different laboratories. While these studies reduce cost or testing time, limitations remain. Da Silva’s method lacks parallelization capabilities since there is a need for one setup per sample, effectively ramping up testing costs to achieve high-throughput biodegradation testing. Šerá’s acceleration method still heavily relies on gas analysis and CO2 quantification, which require expensive equipment, and allows testing to run for up to 2 years for a single sample. These tests simulate polymer biodegradation behavior in natural environments but prevent the high-throughput generation of biodegradation data.

One test that could address all of Albright and Chai’s areas for improvement is the clear-zone assay. The clear-zone assay was first introduced by Spence and Van Niel in 1936 to study the biodegradation of rubber. Microorganisms were added onto Petri dishes made of a bottom layer of agar containing nutrients and a top layer of agar medium containing a milky white suspension of sterile latex. If the bacterium degraded the latex, a clear-zone would be visible around the microorganism colony. Since the clear-zone assay follows the change in opacity in the sample, indicating breakdown of the polymer by enzymes, the biodegradation rate is the local change in opacity with time. A degrading sample that does not change in color has a maximum and minimum level of opacity range, defining a scale of biodegradation. In the context of the clear-zone assay, the biodegradation rate is defined as the percent change in opacity (also referred to as shading) in time. This elegant technique allowed the identification of microorganisms capable of biofragmenting a material of interest. Clear-zone assays were limited to rubber material until 1992 when Augusta applied the technique to a family of poly­(3-hydroxyalkanoates) polymers. Since then, many occurrences of this assay can be found in the literature. − During a clear-zone assay, the colony grows on the polymer-filled agar. The polymer dispersed in the agar gel renders it optically opaque. Enzymes released by the colony diffuse in the gel and break down polymer. This event reduces the size of the polymer particles and consequently, the opacity of the plate decreases. Tracking the decrease in opacity is the degradation rate, which can be monitored, and the method can be adapted to high throughput.

The main advantages of the clear-zone test are its reproducibility (the strain selection that can be made from a constant source), the short test time (∼2 weeks), and low-cost equipment. Though the test only measures biofragmentation and not complete biodegradation (lack of CO2/CH4 monitoring), it allows the effective screening for potential successful chemistries for biodegradability since biofragmentation is often the rate-limiting step in polymer biodegradation. Av-Ron and Fransen demonstrated that these advantages can be leveraged to develop high-throughput biodegradation testing for polymers, screening over three hundred samples in two months. However, the execution of the high-throughput testing continues to be limited by the need to manually move plates from an incubator to a gantry robot used for plate imaging at regular intervals, limiting the time resolution of measurements. Additionally, changes in the physical properties between polymers meant that the sample preparation protocol led to nonuniform polymer particle sizes, making comparison of degradation rates between different chemistries challenging, since biodegradation rate is assumed to be dependent on surface area of a particle. The design of a new sample preparation procedure and a fully automated monitoring system is motivated by the need for accurate enough data to reliably determine the degradation rate of polymers in the clear-zone assay.

Herein, modifications to the previously designed clear-zone assay are proposed to improve accuracy and reproducibility, allowing an increased throughput and quantitative comparison of degradation rates between polymers. First, the sample preparation process was modified to include nanoprecipitation of the polymer in the aqueous phase to guarantee an identical polymer particle size distribution in the agar samples, increase volume and concentration to improve accuracy, and reduce data noise. Second, an automated measurement robot was designed and built specifically to fit inside an incubator to eliminate the need for any human intervention for the full duration of the test. Finally, the monitoring analysis procedures were modified to reduce noise in the data and the need for user judgment. This also enabled a higher measurement frequency, leading to better accuracies for the determination of the biodegradation rate.

Materials and Methods

Materials Used

All polymers used were sourced from a previous study by Av-Ron and Fransen.

Nanoparticle Synthesis

Each nanoparticle batch was made using 20 mg of polymer dissolved in 4 mL of tetrahydrofuran (THF). 16 mL of Milli-Q water was added to a separate 20 mL vial, and polymer solution was transferred to a 5 mL syringe with a 0.21 mm gauge needle. The polymer was added to the water at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. The air-free vial was stirred at a speed of 900 rpm and was vented to allow pressure to equilibrate when adding the THF solution. After addition of THF, the mixture was visibly milky but without macroscopic polymer precipitates, confirming the formation of nanoparticles.

Nanoparticle Size Distribution Analysis

Nanoparticle size distributions were determined through dynamic light scattering. The samples were prepared by adding 0.1 mL of as-prepared nanoparticle suspension to 0.9 mL of Milli-Q water and analyzing through dynamic light scattering. The measurements were conducted with a Wyatt DynaPro NanoStar instrument at a wavelength of 532 nm. Ten measurements were taken for each sample, and the results were reported as an average and standard deviation, as well as the size distribution plot.

Nanoparticle Purification

To remove THF from the nanoparticle solution, the sample was added to a dialysis bag with a pore size of 3.5 kDa. The first dialysis water bath had a volume of 100 mL of Milli-Q water per mL of THF from the nanoparticle solution. The dialysis bags were left for 4 h in the water bath before being transferred to clean water. There were four water changes in total.

Double Concentrated Growth Medium

Double concentrated growth medium was prepared following the ATCC 179 Pseudomonas medium and is composed of a basal medium, solution A, and solution B. The basal medium was prepared by adding 2.56 g of K2HPO4, 2.08 g of KH2PO4, 1.0 g of NH4Cl, and 0.247 g of anhydrous MgSO4 in 500 mL of Milli-Q water. The solution’s pH was adjusted to 6.8 using NaOH and then filter-sterilized. Solution A was prepared by adding 1.0 g of ferric ammonium citrate and 0.1 g of CaCl2 to 100 mL of Milli-Q water and then filter-sterilized. Solution B is a 1.0 M succinic acid solution adjusted to pH 6 with NaOH and filter-sterilized. The microorganism growth medium is composed of 50 μL of solution A and 150 μL of solution B for 5 mL of basal medium, adding 5 mL of sterile water to achieve normal concentrations.

Plating of Nanoparticle Solutions

To obtain the desired polymer concentration for the biodegradation testing, the THF-free nanoparticle solution was reduced to a 4 mL volume. A hot plate was set to 125 °C, and the nanoparticle solution was progressively added to a vial constantly stirring to allow water to evaporate off. If the final volume went below 4 mL, the appropriate volume of sterile Milli-Q water was added. 5 mL of double concentrated growth medium was added to the nanoparticle solution and heated to 60 °C. A Milli-Q water/agarose solution of concentration 0.5 g/mL was sterilized by an autoclave, and 1 mL was added to the hot nanoparticle solution. The solution was then immediately plated with 1.5 mL per well making 5 replicates in a 12-well plate.

Microorganism

Paucimonas lemoignei (P. lemoignei) Jendrossek DSM 7445 [LMG 2207, NCTC 10937] was purchased from ATCC. The strain was grown in a single concentrated growth medium and preserved by cryofreezing in a 30% glycerol solution for future use. For sample inoculation, the strain was grown for at least 48 h in liquid growth medium in a shaker incubator set to 30 °C and 180 rpm, until a measured OD600 of at least 0.4 was reached. Wells of a 12-well plate were inoculated with 0.5 μL of P. lemoignei culture manually deposited in the center of the wells using a pipettor.

Automated Biodegradation System Design

A system to automate the imaging of the wells used in clear-zone tests was designed to fit in a shelf of a 75 L VWR Gravity Convection Incubator. The full build requires access to a mill, laser cutter, and resin 3D printer. The details of the build can be found in the Supporting Information, and all the CAD design for the build and separate parts was made freely accessible. The stepper motors and lights were controlled by an SKR mini, and commands to the SKR mini were sent through a Linux computer using a Python script. The robot parts and assembly were designed using Fusion360. The robot’s acrylic base layer was covered in LED strips. Three spaced layers of white opaque acrylic sheets were used to obtain diffused backlighting along the surface of each single well. The next layer in the robot base was a black acrylic sheet with cutouts to allow light to only shine through the wells of the samples. Finally, the last layer of the base was an acrylic sheet with cutouts to precisely position 12-well plates. The sides of the robot consisted of custom acrylic support structures and 2020 aluminum extrusions with V-wheel sliders to make it an xy-gantry camera system. The stepper motors and LED lights were controlled by an SKR-mini controller, and the full system was controlled with a Python script. All the details for the build and the controls can be found in the Supporting Information, and the exact code scripts were made available.

Incubation and Biodegradation Monitoring

Inoculated well plates were placed in the automated clear-zone assay monitoring robot face down with lids removed. The incubator housing the robot was set to a temperature of 30 °C, and the chamber was humidified with a wet sponge on a tray (4.4 × 2.7 × 0.8 in), maintained within a 20 g range to ensure constant humidity levels and minimize the dehydration of plates. To avoid water accumulations on well plate surfaces, the opening in the back of the incubator (allowing for cable connections with the controller and computer) was left unsealed. Pictures of wells were taken every 30 min turning on the LED lights only during active measurement to minimize any effects from light exposure.

Data Processing and Analysis

For each well of a 12-well plate, the pixel coordinates of the center position were determined. For inoculated wells, the center was set as the center of the grown colony, while for control wells, the center was taken as the center of the illuminated area under the well. Shading curves were extracted corresponding to the average intensity value at a given radius from the well’s center position. Shading curves of six control wells were determined for each sample using the same position in the robot and the same center point as the sample wells. The controls’ shading curves were averaged and used for normalization of degradation samples to obtain the percent degradation curve: 1 d%(t)=100(1−Scontrol−Ssample(t)Scontrol−Ssample(0)) where S designates the shading curves. The result is a percent degradation curve d %. The percent degradation curve at a specific radius is d % r . The local slope of d % r is determined by finding the best linear fit to 20 consecutive data points. The slope of d % r as a function of time is the percent degradation rate curve noted R % d r .

Gel Permeation Chromatography

Polymer molecular weight was determined using size exclusion chromatography on an Agilent 1260 Infinity liquid chromatography system with a Wyatt Technology Optilab rEX refractive index detector or using a Waters liquid chromatography system with a Wyatt Technology Optilab T-rEX refractive index detector and miniDAWN TREOS light scattering detector for polymers soluble in tetrahydrofuran. Molecular weights were determined as the polystyrene standard equivalent molecular weight.

Property Characterization

Thermal stability measurements were investigated by a Thermogravimetric Analysis instrument (TA Instruments Discovery 5500) performed under a nitrogen atmosphere (50 mL/min) in platinum pans with a heating rate of 20 °C/min from 20 to 600 °C. A TA Instruments differential scanning calorimeter (DSC 250), calibrated with high purity indium standards, was used for the study of crystallization and melting of the polyesters. Samples of 5 to 10 mg were loaded and sealed in hermetic aluminum pans and heated at a rate of 10 °C/min to their 2% decomposition temperature, above the equilibrium melting point of the polymer. The samples were then cooled to −50 °C and heated again with a temperature ramp of 5 °C/min and 10 °C/min, respectively. The degree of crystallinity was determined by comparing the ΔH m for a 100% crystalline sample. Polymers 3 and 5 were calculated using ΔH m,100% equal to 28.9 kJ/mol and 124 kJ/g. , Values of ΔH m,100% for the other samples were not reported in the literature. X-ray diffraction (XRD) tests of samples were carried out using SmartLab, Rigaku. XRD sample surfaces were prepared from lyophilized polymer nanoparticle solutions and packed onto a silicon wafer. The X-rays were sourced from Cu–Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm), with an operating target voltage of 45 kV, with continuous scanning ranging acquisition angles from 5° to 60° with a speed of 3.5 deg/min. The degree of crystallinity was calculated from XRD patterns using the relative areas under the crystalline peaks (A c) and amorphous content (A a) using the following equation: 2 Xc=AcAc+Aa

Results and Discussion

Controlling Polymer Particle Size

Since the turbidity of a well in a clear-zone biodegradation assay depends upon particle size, direct comparisons of the biodegradation rate require preparing polymers with nearly identical particle size distributions. Several different methods were tested to obtain a controlled distribution of nanoparticles across a representative set of chemistries. Selected chemistries have different backbone lengths, the presence of heteroatoms along the backbone, side groups, and rings. Mechanical filing using crystal files was attempted but was ineffective due to the tough mechanical properties of the polymers. Cryogrinding was also unsuccessful at the lab scale due to the heat released from the grinder, which led to agglomerated sticky polymer particles, but may be feasible using industrial equipment. Polymer nanoprecipitation was the most reproducible and generally applicable small-scale technique; details on other explored methods can be found in the Supporting Information. For successful nanoprecipitation, two miscible solvents were selected, where the polymer of interest was soluble in one but not the other. For polymers here, water was selected as the non-polymer-soluble solvent, and tetrahydrofuran (THF) was selected as the polymer-soluble solvent for its ability to dissolve a wide range of polyesters. The use of water as the nonsolvent avoided the risk of irreversible aggregation on particle drying or solvent change after precipitation that would be required for other solvents. Overall, nanoprecipitation was used to control the particle size for the subsequent experiments and was optimized for each polymer.

The best parameters for nanoparticle precipitation had to be determined to make the biodegradation method applicable to the widest range of chemistries with the lowest cost. Parameters that have been shown to affect final nanoparticle size distributions are polymer concentration, outlet needle gauge, solvent flow rate, and solvent ratios. Using commercial poly­(1,4-butylene adipate) (PBA) with a M n of 12 kDa, and polycaprolactone diol (PCLdiol) with a M n of 2 kDa, the influence of these different parameters on different polymers was explored. The effect of polymer concentration in THF was studied first. For low concentration of PCLdiol, the average particle size increased with higher concentration but leveled off starting around 4–6 mg/mL (see Supporting Information Figures S1 and S2). For PBA, concentrations above 6 mg/mL led to clumping. Therefore, the nanoparticle precipitation polymer concentration was set to 6 mg/mL to ensure that different chemistries would successfully lead to well-separated nanoparticles, while also minimizing the solvent volume required and waste generated in the following steps. With the same motivation, THF to water ratios were increased from 1:5 to 1:4 as it showed to not change the nanoparticle size (see Supporting Information Figure S3). The inner diameter (ID) of the needle when adding the THF solution to Milli-Q water had minimal impact on the final particle size as shown in Supporting Information Figure S4, though clumping was occasionally observed with larger needle sizes for PCLdiol which motivated using the smallest ID needle0.21 mmamong the tested sizes. After nanoprecipitation, THF was removed through dialysis to make the polymer solution suitable for the clear-zone biodegradation assay as residual THF could impact the growth and behavior of the microorganism and lead to incorrect degradation data. The full process is illustrated in Figure , and the chosen parameters were verified using PBA samples.

The nanoparticle sample preparation process was applied to 15 different polymers listed in Table . The particle sizes of each chemistry were measured after the nanoprecipitation step and after the reduction step once the growth medium had been added to the nanoparticle solution. All the polymers had an APS around 100 ± 20 nm. The APS after the addition of the growth medium varied widely due to the varying thermodynamic properties of the polymers studied. The nanoprecipitation study with commercial PBA showed that after reduction but before addition of the growth medium, the APS was smaller than after nanoprecipitation, which is also the case for 8 of the 15 polymers after the addition of the growth medium. However, other chemistries showed constant or increasing particle sizes after adding growth medium, suggesting that several of the nanoparticles aggregate in the presence of agar. The full particle size distributions of a few such polymers are shown in Figure . Consistent with aggregation processes that are governed by both kinetic and thermodynamic factors, there is a wide variety in the shape of the distributions and in the aggregated particle sizes. It is unclear if aggregation of particles affects biodegradation rates; it would be dependent on whether the space between particles within an aggregate allows for enzymes to diffuse and how much the opacity level is affected by the particle size change (thus affecting apparent biodegradation).

While it helps to standardize particle size, the nanoprecipitation process does introduce some limits that affect high-throughput testing. The requirement of solubility in THF or another suitable solvent reduces the range of polymers that can be processed in this way. From the data set used in the previous study, over 70% of the 662 polyesters were THF insoluble, and many were insoluble in all solvents. Any modification to the solvent system requires significant modifications to the downstream particle purification process to ensure that toxic solvent is removed. Another limitation to this sample preparation process is that only solid samples can be tested, as waxy polymers easily clump. Taking both of these limitations into account, it is estimated that the presented sample preparation process is still applicable to about 15% of polymers, indicating its potential for material format standardization in clear-zone biodegradation assays. There are solutions to extending the method to more chemistries. Hard polymers may be ground into fine powders for addition in the agar solution, though particle sizes would not reach those of nanoprecipitation. For nonsoluble waxy polymers, finer control of their crystallinity could lend them favorable to the grinding solution.

Fully Automated Clear-Zone Assay Biodegradation Monitoring System

Automation of the clear-zone biodegradation assay improves the reliability and data accuracy. To allow flexibility in sampling times beyond what can be performed via vial manual imaging and improve the reliability of imaging, a custom camera gantry system was constructed to automate sample monitoring. Schematics of the design are listed in Figure . As seen in Figure a, the total volume of the robot is just under 0.75 cu ft, with dimensions allowing users to fit two robots in a standard incubator of 2.6 cu ft. The robot was designed to maximize off-the-shelf components and components that could be manufactured via laser cutting or 3D printing, thus making the build as cost-friendly and reproducible as possible. Details on the build and list of parts can be found in the Supporting Information.

Schematic of the custom clear-zone biodegradation monitoring robot. The robot has almost perfect symmetry front to back and left to right (other than the stepper motor heights and belts). A general outside view is shown in (a), while views (b) and (c) are sliced views of the robot seen from the front or the right in respect to (a).

The software of the robot was designed for flexibility. The controls were reduced to a Python script and a csv. file listing the samples to monitor. Because the script runs continuously, the script has to be interrupted to remove or add a sample. At each start of the script, the system checks for new samples and accordingly saves the start time and date of each sample. Thus, even if the script is interrupted, the robot is able to resume the monitoring from where it was interrupted, taking into account the elapsed time of the interruption. The details on how to begin a run on the robot are given in the Supporting Information. To reduce noise during data acquisition, the output of the robot control program is no longer a single picture from the camera but the average of 100 consecutive frames. The noise is computed as the root-mean-square of the difference of consecutive data points: 3 nRMS=∑[S(r+1)−S(r)]2 where S is the shading curve ranging through the full span of radius. The noise for the single frame shading curve is 3.3 bits while the 100 frame-average shading curve is half as much at 1.7 bits (computed from the curves shown in Figure ).

Shading curves of the same well when well monitoring is done with a single frame versus 100 frames averaged, where shading curve noisiness is half that of the single frame shading curve.

Different analyses were run to demonstrate the reliability of the automated system, starting with positioning precision. To test the level of accuracy of the camera’s displacement, the camera was homed and moved to a well in the middle of the robot repeatedly, and the resulting images can be seen in Supporting Information Figure S7. When zooming in on a detail of the image in both the x and y directions, Supporting Information Figure S8 shows that the average positioning of the camera stays within a 5 pixel range, equal to 345 μm, which compared to the 200 pixel (13.8 mm) wide well cutout represents a small positioning error. The second analysis was to demonstrate that the use of normalization with controls can help to compensate for variations in lighting. Control well plates were prepared with just growth medium solution with no polymer and no colony. Figure a shows the shading curve of different positions in the robot of a control well at each position. The shading varies somewhat between positions as the backlighting is not uniform across the imaging surface. However, once the shading curve is subtracted from that of the position in the robot with no well plate (blank), the resulting shading curves shift and overlap within a 5 bit range as shown in Figure b, thus demonstrating that normalization can compensate for nonuniformity in backlighting.

Accuracy Improvements and Analysis Process

The new design of the clear-zone assay biodegradation system allowed for improved accuracy and a significant reduction in time and human intervention. A larger opacity difference between before and after degradation further improved the detection accuracy of the system. To achieve this, the concentration of polymer in the wells was doubled from the previously set value (2 mg/mL instead of 1 mg/mL). The opacity increases in some cases, while for some polymers, it does not change with doubling of concentration (as shown in Supporting Information Figure S21). This may be due to the opacity change being correlated to the diffusivity of the media and not the absorbance of the particles. In the case where particles were already maximally diffusing at 1 mg/mL, the addition of more particles did not change the light transmission behavior through the well. The doubling of concentration led to being closer to maximum diffusivity for more samples; therefore, the concentration of 2 mg/mL was selected for the samples. Furthermore, to increase the opacity further, the wells were filled with 1.5 mL of solution instead of 0.8 mL.

When drying occurs, the concentration in the wells increases, leading to a higher sample opacity. A significant amount of drying led to variations that conflicted with opacity changes due to biodegradation, thus falsifying the data. To quantify this change, the average shading of the control well over the first day of monitoring was compared to the average shading of the 11th day of monitoring (the 11th day was selected as the comparison point as some biodegradation runs finished sooner than others, but all samples were monitored for at least 11 days). The average change in shading between these 2 days at the fixed radius of 60 pixels was 2.5 bits with a standard deviation of 0.7 bits for all of the tested samples. This difference in shading is lower than that from the same control well being measured at different positions in the robot as shown in Figure b, and thus, the effect of drying is neglected for the biodegradation rate determination process.

As the new system has higher accuracy, it was possible to reduce the number of replicates for each polymer. The previous well plate layout allowed for 6 biodegradation replicates and 2 replicates of each control. Extensive testing with more than 1,000 well plates in the previous layout revealed no significant variation between duplicate control wells. This empirical validation supported reducing the number of control replicates to one in the updated design. The new number of replicates was decreased to allow two polymer samples to be tested in a single 12-well plate. The old versus new layouts are shown in Figure . With this updated well-plate layout, using the incubator automated robot, 12 polymer samples can be tested simultaneously.

The process for analyzing the data was slightly modified from the previous system. The colony was still detected by shading differences between the darkness of the colony and the surrounding agar (see Supporting Information Figure S14). It is the only step in the analysis process that requires user interaction with the new system. The sizes of the colonies were determined differently. Instead of finding the largest distance between two pixels of the colony, the new size was designated as the distance between the determined center and the farthest colony edge pixel. This distance is determined by finding the smallest radius of the centered circle that would contain all colony pixels (see Supporting Information Figure S15). The full process details of these steps are shown in a flowchart in Supporting Information Figure S13.

The shading curve extraction was done as previously, finding the average color of the pixels at each radius. The modification in this new process was to shift each shading curve at the same baseline by subtracting them to a control curve: the shading curves of six no polymer wells were extracted from sampling at the same position and using the same center point as determined by the sample well colony. The shading curve of the sample was then subtracted to the average curve of the six controls. Figure illustrates this process. In Figure a, the unmodified shading curves at the six positions are plotted. Figure b shows the shading curves once they are subtracted from the control curve of each position. The 95% confidence interval was calculated, and the margin of error is plotted against each radius in Figure c. The margin of error never exceeds 0.53, indicating that the shading curves from Figure c are confidently shifted to the same baseline.

Unmodified shading curve of a growth medium only well at each position of the robot (a) and subtracted to a 6-control shading curve average (b). The 95% confidence interval of the 6 positions shading curves (c) indicates that this method of data processing allows properly bringing all samples to the same baseline regardless of the position in the robot.

With this method of data processing, the shading curves can be quantitatively compared between wells. Figure shows an example of the shading curves of the different wells present on a sample well plate. As biodegradation occurs, opacity decreases, thus, shading decreases as seen in Figure a. A polymer control well will maintain a high shading value as in Figure c. Similarly, the growth of a colony increases the shading as seen in the grayed zone in Figure a,c. Finally, control wells are properly shifted to zero, as seen in Figure c,d. If the colony was properly centered (Figure a), the 100 pixel radius does not intersect the edge of the backlit area, while for improperly centered colonies (Figure b,d), there are edge effects at large distances from the center. Because of variation in colony position, only information out to 80 pixels is used in analyzing shading curves. Conclusion

This paper reports advances to the clear-zone assay to obtain biodegradation rates. First, the sample preparation method was developed by using polymer nanoprecipitation to obtain controlled particle sizes for different chemistries. Parameters including polymer concentration, solvent ratios, and needle gauge were optimized, and the reproducibility of the nanoprecipitation process was demonstrated. Furthermore, the paper presented a fully automated clear-zone biodegradation monitoring system as well as an analysis process where human intervention was reduced to a single step, both of which significantly reduced the human intervention time required for the monitoring phase and the data analysis. The accuracy improvements of the system included doubling the polymer concentration in the wells, increasing the well solution volume, and averaging multiple frames for data analysis. The developed data analysis method effectively extracted the biodegradation rates. Overall, this research provides a new process for controlling polymer particle size in clear-zone degradation assays and monitoring biodegradation. With this cost-effective system, the degradation rates of many polymers can be determined and could lead to the emergence of relationships between the chemistries and biodegradation.

To achieve broader academic and industrial adoption of this method, further testing is required to make the clear-zone assay into a standard method. The cost comparison with existing biodegradation tests suggests that the proposed system can offer substantial savings in materials and analyst time; however, interlab reproducibility is yet to be tested and duplication of the full system by other groups is needed to guaranty the method’s repeatability with all external variables (operator, automation built, etc.). Future efforts should focus on coordinated validation trials to establish reproducibility and benchmark performance against existing standardized methods. These steps will be essential to support eventual translation into ASTM or ISO testing standards. Future exploration of the clear-zone assay with increased microbial diversity would support extension of this biodegradation method to additional polymer chemistries and allow testing of biodegradability using microbiomes derived from diverse environmental samples. By addressing these validation and standardization pathways, the automated clear-zone biodegradation assay could form the basis for a next-generation, standardized approach for evaluating polymer biodegradability across research and industrial settings.
