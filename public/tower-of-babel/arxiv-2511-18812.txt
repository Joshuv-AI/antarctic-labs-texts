Recovering discontinuous viscosity coefficients for inverse Stokes problems by boundary measurements
Yu Jia, Chengyu Wu, Hao Wu, Jiaqing Yang

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the inverse Stokes problem of determining a discontinuous viscosity coefficient μ \mu in a bounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝ 3 \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3} . By analyzing the singularity of the Dirichlet Green’s functions in H 1 H^{1} -norm and constructing a specifically coupled Stokes-Brinkman system in a localized domain, we prove a global uniqueness theorem that the viscosity coefficient μ \mu can be uniquely determined from boundary measurements.



keywords

inverse problem, Stokes equations, discontinuous viscosity coefficient, Cauchy data

1
Introduction

The inverse problems in hydrodynamics have recently garnered increasing attention due to their significant applications in various fields, such as oceanography, biomechanics, the oil industry, and naval engineering. A detailed investigation of this topic can be found in
[
5
,
2
,
28
,
32
,
23
]
. In this paper, we focus on an inverse Stokes problem involving the recovery of a discontinuous viscosity coefficient from boundary measurements. Mathematically, let
\Omega
be a bounded
C^{2}
-domains in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
filled with an incompressible viscous fluid. Given boundary data
f
, the Stokes equations for the velocity field
\bm{u}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the pressure field
p:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are described as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-{\rm{div}}(\mu(x)\mathcal{D}(\bm{u}))+\nabla p=0&\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\\
{\rm{div}}\bm{u}=0&\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\bm{u}=\bm{f}&\quad\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
\mu
is the viscosity coefficient . The strain tensor and the stress tensor associated with the flow
(\bm{u},p)
are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{2}([\nabla\bm{u}]+[\nabla\bm{u}]^{\top}),

\displaystyle T(\bm{u},p)=-p\mathbf{I}_{3}+\mu\mathcal{D}(\bm{u}),

respectively. Here,
\mathbf{I}_{3}
denotes the identity matrix, and
\nabla\bm{u}
is the matrix function whose
j
th column represents the gradient of the
j
th component of
\bm{u}
. Since
{\rm{div}}\bm{u}=0
, the vector
\bm{f}
must satisfy the standard flux compatibility condition:

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{f}\cdot\bm{n}\,\mathrm{d}s=0,

(1.2)

where
\bm{n}
the outward normal vector on
\partial\Omega
.

The well-posedness of Problem (
1.1
) has been studied using either the variational approach or the integral equation method in the associated function spaces (see, e.g.,
[
33
,
9
]
). We can thus define the Dirichlet-to-Neumann (D-to-N) map as

\displaystyle\Lambda(\bm{f}):=t(\bm{u},p)\quad\text{ on }\partial\Omega,

(1.3)

where
t(\bm{u},p):=T(\bm{u},p)\bm{n}
, and
\Lambda
is considered to act between the space
\bm{H}^{1/2}_{0}(\partial\Omega)
and
\bm{H}^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, with
\bm{H}^{1/2}_{0}(\partial\Omega)
defined as

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{1/2}_{0}(\partial\Omega):=\{\bm{f}\in\bm{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega):\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{f}\cdot\bm{n}\,\mathrm{d}s=0\}.

Furthermore, we define the following Cauchy data set

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mu}:=\{(\bm{f},\Lambda(\bm{f})):\bm{f}\in\bm{H}_{0}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\}.

Throughout this paper, we assume that
\mu
is a discontinuous viscosity coefficient of the form

\displaystyle\mu(x):=\mu_{0}\cdot\chi_{\Omega\backslash\overline{D}}(x)+a(x)\cdot\chi_{\overline{D}}(x),

(1.4)

and satisfies the following conditions:

\rm{i)}

\mu_{0}
is a positive constant;

\rm{ii)}

a(x)\in C^{0,1}(\overline{D})
with
a(x)>0
;

\rm{iii)}

\mu(x)|_{\partial D}\neq\mu_{0}
for any
x\in\partial D
.

Here,
D
is assumed to belong to the class of admissible geometries

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{ad}:=\left\{D\subset\subset\Omega:{D\rm\ is\ open,bounded},\ C^{2}{\rm\ boundary\ and}\ \Omega\backslash\overline{D}{\rm\ is\ connected}\right\}

and
\chi_{\Omega\backslash\overline{D}}(x)
represents the characteristic function in the subdomain
\Omega\backslash\overline{D}
, defined as 1 in
\Omega\backslash\overline{D}
and 0 elsewhere.

The inverse Stokes problem (ISP) is to determine
\mu
from the Cauchy data set
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
. The following theorem presents the main result regarding the global uniqueness of

\mu
.

Theorem 1
.

Assume
\mu_{i}|_{\overline{D_{i}}}\in C^{8}(\overline{D_{i}})
with
D_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{ad}
for
i=1,2
. If
\mathcal{C}_{\mu_{1}}=\mathcal{C}_{\mu_{2}}
, then
\mu_{1}(x)=\mu_{2}(x)
in
\Omega
.

Remark 2
.

If there exists the boundary data
\bm{f}
satisfying certain a priori assumptions, Theorem
1
also holds for local Cauchy data defined on any non-empty open subset of
\partial\Omega
. Furthermore, for the case with a Robin boundary condition
t(\bm{u},p)-\beta\bm{u}=\bm{f}
on
\partial\Omega
(
\beta\geq 0
), we can prove, with slight modifications, that
\mu
is uniquely determined by the local Robin-to-Dirichlet map defined on any non-empty open subset of
\partial\Omega
, without requiring any assumptions on
\bm{f}
.

It is well known that inverse boundary problems can date back to A.P.Calderón
[
6
]
, who investigated the unique determination of
\sigma
in the conductivity equation
{\rm div}(\sigma(x)\triangledown u(x))=0
from the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map. Significant advancements have been made in this direction since then; see fundamental papers like
[
19
,
31
,
16
,
4
,
26
,
27
,
7
]
for results on determining smooth coefficients in
schr{\ddot{o}}dinger
equations or wave equations. Furthermore, we refer to
[
20
,
13
,
18
,
15
,
17
]
and the related references cited therein for results on determining discontinuous coefficients or using partial boundary measurements. However, uniqueness results for the Stokes and Navier-Stokes equations remain relatively scarce, both theoretically and numerically. It was shown in
[
10
]
by Heck
et al.
that a smooth viscosity coefficient
\mu(x)
in the Stokes equations can be uniquely determined from the boundary data by constructing exponentially growing solutions connecting with
\overline{\partial}
-method in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This result was later extended by Li
et al.
to the case of Navier-Stokes equations
[
25
]
using linearization method. In the two-dimensional case, Imanuvilov
et al.
in
[
14
]
and Lai
et al.
in
[
24
]
established similar identifiability results for
\mu(x)
in the Stokes and Navier-Stokes equations, respectively, using different measurements data. Furthermore, if an unknown solids is immersed in an unbounded homogeneous fluid, the inverse problem reduces to determining the shape and location of the solid. In this case, we refer to
[
3
,
1
,
11
]
for related works.

Note that all aforementioned works rely heavily on the assumption that
\mu(x)
is smooth in
\Omega
. Additionally, it is essential to impose the a priori information

\displaystyle\partial^{\alpha}\mu_{1}(x)=\partial^{\alpha}\mu_{2}(x)\quad\forall x\in\partial\Omega,\ |\alpha|\leq 1

(1.5)

for the viscosity coefficients
\mu
. For a detailed discussion in both two-dimensional and three-dimensional cases, we refer to
[
10
,
25
,
24
]
. If
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a bounded convex domain with non-vanishing Gauss curvature on
\partial\Omega
, it was shown in
[
10
]
that the uniqueness can be proved without the assumption (
1.5
).

In this paper, we investigate the inverse Stokes problem of recovering a discontinuous viscosity coefficient
\mu(x)
from boundary measurements
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
. A novel and simple approach will be proposed, where the key technique is to analyze the singularity of the Dirichlet Green’s functions in the
H^{1}
-norm and construct a coupled PDE-system associated with the Stokes and Brinkman equations in a localized domain. With these preparations, we can first prove that
\mu
is uniquely determined near the boundary
\partial\Omega
by choosing a family of special boundary data
f
that behaves like
1/|x-z|
, with
z\in\partial\Omega
. Then, the inverse problem is reduced to determining
D
with its physical property from
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
in a known fluid domain. Through an elaborate analysis conducted in a similar manner, we further prove that
D
and
\mu(x)|_{\partial D}
can be uniquely determined from the boundary measurements
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
. Moreover, based on a special inequality established for two different Dirichlet Green’s functions, we also show the unique determination of
\partial^{\alpha}\mu|_{\partial\Omega}
for
|\alpha|=1
from
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
. These improve all previous results in e.g.
[
10
,
24
,
25
,
14
]
, where
\partial^{\alpha}\mu|_{\partial\Omega}
has to be assumed to be known in advance for
|\alpha|\leq 1
. Finally, the uniqueness of
\mu(x)|_{D}
follows from existing results
[
10
,
24
]
with a denseness discussion. We note that our method is independent of the choice of dimensions, and with slight modifications, the main results in Theorem
1
can be easily extended to the two-dimensional case.

The structure of the paper is outlined as follows. In Section 2, we introduce some useful notations and function spaces in the
H^{1}
-norm sense, as well as the potential theory for Stokes and Brinkman equations. In Section 3, we establish a priori estimates for the Dirichlet Green’s functions and prove the well-posedness of a coupled Stokes-Brinkman system, which plays a key role in the analysis of the inverse Stokes problem and is of interest in its own right. Section 4 is devoted to the detailed proof of the main result.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we first introduce some useful notations and function spaces that are employed throughout the text, and then present the potential theory for the Stokes and Brinkman equations.

2.1
Notations

Let
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain with a
C^{2}
-boundary
\partial\Omega
. The standard scalar Sobolev spaces on
\Omega
and
\partial\Omega
are denoted by
H^{s}(\Omega)
and
H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
, respectively, where
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
H^{0}=L^{2}
. The vector-valued Sobolev spaces on these domains are defined as

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{s}(\Omega):=[H^{s}(\Omega)]^{3},\;\bm{H}^{s}(\partial\Omega):=[H^{s}(\partial\Omega)]^{3},\qquad\qquad s\in\mathbb{R},

and the tensor-valued spaces are given by

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{s}(\Omega)^{3}:=[H^{s}(\Omega)]^{3\times 3},\;\bm{H}^{s}(\partial\Omega)^{3}:=[H^{s}(\partial\Omega)]^{3\times 3},\quad s\in\mathbb{R}.

Additionally, for
q>1
, we define
\bm{L}^{q}(\partial\Omega):=[L^{q}(\partial\Omega)]^{3}
. The inner product for matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is defined by
A:B=\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}A_{ij}B_{ij}
, and the induced norm is given by
|A|=\sqrt{A:A}
. For tensor fields
A,B\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{3\times 3}
, the inner product is defined as

\displaystyle\langle A,B\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{3\times 3}}:=\int_{\Omega}A(x):B(x)dx.

For any smooth solenoidal vector fields
\bm{u},\bm{v}
and scalar function
p
, we have the first Green’s formula

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(-{\rm{div}}(\mu(x)D(\bm{u}))+\nabla p)\cdot\bm{v}dx=-\int_{\partial\Omega}t(\bm{u},p)\cdot\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}s(x)+\int_{\Omega}\mu(x)D(\bm{u}):D(\bm{v})dx.

2.2
Potential theory for Stokes and Brinkman equations

Let
(\mathcal{G}^{\mu,\chi^{2}},\Pi^{\chi^{2}})
be the fundamental solution of the Brinkman system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-{\rm div}(\mu\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}))+\nabla\Pi^{\chi^{2}}+\chi^{2}\mathcal{G}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}=\delta I_{3}&\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
{\rm div}\mathcal{G}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}=0&\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
\end{cases}

(2.1)

in the distribution sense, where
\delta
is the Dirac delta function. The components of the fundamental solution
(\mathcal{G}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}_{ik},\Pi_{i})
are given by(cf.
[
23
]
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}_{ik}(x-y)=\frac{1}{4\pi\mu}\frac{\delta_{ik}}{|x-y|}A_{1}(R)+\frac{1}{4\pi\mu}\frac{(x_{i}-y_{i})(x_{k}-y_{k})}{|x-y|^{3}}A_{2}(R),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\Pi_{i}(x-y)=\frac{x_{i}-y_{i}}{4\pi|x-y|^{3}},\quad i,k=1,2,3,

where
R=\frac{\chi\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\mu}}|x-y|
, and

\displaystyle A_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2e^{-z}(1+z^{-1}+z^{-2})-2z^{-2},

\displaystyle A_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2e^{-z}(1+3z^{-1}+3z^{-2})+6z^{-2}.

The components of the stress and pressure tensors
\mathcal{S}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}
and
\Lambda^{\mu,\chi^{2}}
for the Brinkman equation are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}_{ijk}(x-y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\pi}\bigg\{\delta_{ik}\frac{x_{j}-y_{j}}{|x-y|^{3}}D_{1}(R)+\left(\delta_{kj}\frac{x_{i}-y_{i}}{|x-y|^{3}}+\delta_{ij}\frac{x_{k}-y_{k}}{|x-y|^{3}}\right)\cdot D_{2}(R)

\displaystyle+\frac{(x_{i}-y_{i})(x_{j}-y_{j})(x_{k}-y_{k})}{|x-y|^{5}}D_{3}(R)\bigg\},

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mu,\chi^{2}}(x-y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi}\frac{\delta_{ik}}{|x-y|^{3}}(R^{2}-2)+\frac{3}{4\pi}\frac{(x_{i}-y_{i})(x_{k}-y_{k})}{|x-y|^{5}},

where

\displaystyle D_{1}(z):

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2e^{-z}(1+3z^{-1}+3z^{-2})-6z^{-2}+1,

\displaystyle D_{2}(z):

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-z}(z+3+6z^{-1}+6z^{-2})-6z^{-2},

\displaystyle D_{3}(z):

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-z}(-2z-12-30z^{-1}-30z^{-2})+30z^{-2}.

We define the hydrodynamic single- and double-layer potentials for the Brinkman equation on a closed and connected Lipschitz surface
S
and a bounded domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as follows

\displaystyle(\bm{V}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}_{S})_{k}(x,\bm{\phi})=\int_{S}\mathcal{G}_{kj}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}(x-y)\phi_{j}(y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y),\qquad\qquad\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash S,

\displaystyle(P_{S}^{s})(x,\bm{\phi})=\int_{S}\Pi_{j}(x-y)\phi_{j}(y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y),\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash S,

\displaystyle(\bm{W}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}_{S})_{k}(x,\bm{\varphi})=\int_{S}\mathcal{S}_{jkl}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}(y-x)n_{l}(y)\varphi_{j}(y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y),\quad\;\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash S,

\displaystyle(P_{\mu,\chi^{2};S}^{d})(x,\bm{\varphi})=\mu\int_{S}\Lambda_{jk}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}(x-y)n_{k}(y)\varphi_{j}(y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash S,

and the volume potentials are given by

\displaystyle(\bm{V}_{\Omega}^{\mu,\chi^{2}})_{k}(x,\bm{\phi})=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{G}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}_{kj}(x-y)\phi_{j}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad\qquad\quad\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

\displaystyle(P_{\Omega})(x,\bm{\phi})=\int_{\Omega}\Pi_{j}(x-y)\phi_{j}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

The boundary integral operators corresponding to the Brinkman equation are defined as

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}_{S})_{k}(x,\bm{\phi})=\int_{S}\mathcal{G}_{kj}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}(x-y)\phi_{j}(y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y),\qquad\qquad\quad\;x\in S,

\displaystyle(\bm{K}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}_{S})_{k}(x,\bm{\phi})=\int_{S}\mathcal{S}_{jkl}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}(y-x)n_{l}(y)\phi_{j}(y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y),\quad\qquad x\in S,

\displaystyle(\bm{K}^{\ast,\mu,\chi^{2}}_{S})_{k}(x,\bm{\phi})=\int_{S}\mathcal{S}_{jkl}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}(x-y)n_{l}(x)\phi_{j}(y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y),\qquad\;x\in S,

\displaystyle(\bm{D}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}_{S})_{j}(x,\bm{\phi})=\int_{S}D^{\mu,\chi^{2}}_{jl}(x,y)\phi_{l}(y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y),\qquad\qquad\qquad\;x\in S,

where

\displaystyle D_{jl}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}(x,y)=-\Lambda_{lk}^{\chi^{2}}(x-y)n_{k}(y)n_{j}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{\mu}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\mathcal{S}_{lik}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}(y-x)+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\mathcal{S}_{ljk}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}(y-x)\right)n_{i}(x)n_{k}(y).

We refer the reader to
[
23

12
]
for the jump relations and continuity properties of the layer potentials in classical Hölder or standard Sobolev spaces. In the case of

-spaces, due to the weak singularity of the kernel of
\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}_{S}

\bm{K}_{S}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}
, and

Theorem 3

Let

,
\bm{K}_{S}^{\mu,\chi^{2}}
, and

are bounded compact mappings in

Remark 4

If we take
\frac{\chi\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\mu}}\rightarrow 0
, then one has

3
Some crucial Lemmas

In this section, we present several technical lemmas that are used frequently in the proof of the uniqueness of the inverse Stokes problem. Throughout the following discussion, we denote generic constants using lowercase
c
and uppercase
C
, with or without subscripts, which may take different values in different contexts.

3.1
A priori estimates of the Dirichlet Green’s functions

Define the Dirichlet Green’s function
(G^{\mu}(x,z),r^{\mu}(x,z))
for
z\in\Omega
, which satisfies the following equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-{\rm div}(\mu(x)\mathcal{D}(G^{\mu}(x,z)))+\nabla r^{\mu}(x,z)=\delta(x-z)I_{3}&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
{\rm div}G^{\mu}(x,z)=0&\text{ in }\Omega\backslash\{z\},\\
G^{\mu}(x,z)=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(3.2)

in the distribution sense. For
z_{\ast}\in\partial D
, define

\displaystyle z_{j}:=z_{\ast}+\frac{\sigma}{j}\bm{n}(z_{\ast}),\quad j=1,2,\cdots

with a sufficiently small
\sigma>0
such that
z_{j}\in B(z_{\ast},\sigma)
, where
B(z_{\ast},\sigma)
denotes a small ball with radius
\sigma
centred at
z_{\ast}
.

Firstly, we consider the case with a piecewise constant coefficient
\widetilde{\mu}
, defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}(x):=\mu_{0}\cdot\chi_{\Omega\backslash\overline{D}}(x)+\mu_{c}\cdot\chi_{\overline{D}}(x),

where
\mu_{c}
is a positive constant satisfying
\mu_{c}\neq\mu_{0}
. We have the following regularity estimate of the Dirichlet Green’s function
(G^{\widetilde{\mu}}(x,z_{j}),r^{\widetilde{\mu}}(x,z_{j}))
.

Lemma 5
.

G^{\widetilde{\mu}}(x,z_{j})
has the estimate

\displaystyle\bigg\|G^{\widetilde{\mu},D}(x,z_{j})-\bigg\{\frac{\mu_{c}}{\mu_{c}+\mu_{0}}\mathcal{G}^{\mu_{c}}(x-z_{j})+\frac{\mu_{0}}{\mu_{c}+\mu_{0}}\mathcal{G}^{\mu_{0}}(x-z_{j})\bigg\}\bigg\|_{\bm{H}^{1}(D)^{3}}\leq C

(3.3)

for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
, where
C
is a positive constant independent of
j\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\widetilde{\mu}}(x,z_{j}):=\begin{cases}\mathcal{G}^{\mu_{c}}(x,z_{j})&\text{ for }x\in\overline{D},\\
\mathcal{G}^{\mu_{0}}(x,z_{j})&\text{ for }x\in\Omega\backslash\overline{D}.\end{cases}

for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\bm{U}_{l}(x,z_{j}):=G_{l}^{\widetilde{\mu}}(x,z_{j})-\mathcal{G}_{l}^{\widetilde{\mu}}(x,z_{j})
and
R_{l}(x,z_{j}):=r_{l}^{\widetilde{\mu}}(\cdot,z_{j})-\Pi_{l}(x,z_{j})
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
l=1,2,3
. Then, it is easily founded that
(\bm{U}_{l}(x,z_{j}),R_{l}(x,z_{j}))
satisfy the following Stokes equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-{\rm div}(\mu_{0}\mathcal{D}(\bm{u}))+\nabla p=0&\quad\text{ in }\Omega\backslash\overline{D},\\
{\rm div}\bm{u}=0&\quad\text{ in }\Omega\backslash\overline{D},\\
-{\rm div}(\mu_{c}\mathcal{D}(\bm{v}))+\nabla m=0&\quad\text{ in }D,\\
{\rm div}\bm{v}=0&\quad\text{ in }D,\end{cases}

(3.4)

with boundary conditions

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\bm{u}-\bm{v}=\bm{h}&\quad x\in\partial D,\\
t(\bm{u},p)-t(\bm{v},m)=\bm{g}&\quad x\in\partial D,\\
\bm{u}=\bm{k}&\quad x\in\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(3.5)

where

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\bm{h}=\bm{h}^{(j)}_{l}:=\mathcal{G}_{l}^{\mu_{c}}(x,z_{j})-\mathcal{G}_{l}^{\mu_{0}}(x,z_{j})\in\bm{L}^{q}(\partial D),\quad 1<q<2,\\
\bm{g}=\bm{g}^{(j)}_{l}:=0\in\bm{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D),\\
\bm{k}=\bm{k}^{(j)}_{l}:=G_{l}^{\widetilde{\mu},D}(x,z_{j})-\mathcal{G}_{l}^{\mu_{0}}(x,z_{j})\in\bm{H}_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega),\end{cases}

(3.6)

Clearly, it can be easily verified that

\displaystyle\|\bm{h}^{(j)}_{l}\|_{\bm{L}^{q}(\partial D)}+\|\bm{g}^{(j)}_{l}\|_{\bm{H}^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)}+\|\bm{k}^{(j)}_{l}\|_{\bm{H}_{0}^{1/2}(\partial D)}\leq C

(3.7)

for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
l=1,2,3
.

Now, we seek the solutions
(\bm{u},p)
and
(\bm{v},m)
of (
3.4
)-(
3.5
) in the form
