Intra-osseous and coverage fixation for inferior pole fracture of the patella: a comparative finite element analysis and retrospective cohort study
Authors: Chen-Dong Liu, Sun-Jun Hu, Shi-Min Chang, Chang-Qing Zhang, Yi-Gang Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveInferior pole fracture of the patella (IPFP) is notoriously difficult to manage. Although multiple surgical options are available, the percutaneous cannulated-screw tension-band (PCST) technique remains the conventional choice, despite its high complication rate. The recently introduced technique of tension-band wiring through a cannulated screw with suture anchors (TACS-SA) offers a promising alternative, though its efficacy requires further validation. This study was conceived to compare the biomechanical behaviour and early clinical results of the TACS-SA construct with PCST fixation.MethodsThis study comprises a finite-element analysis (FEA) and clinical investigation. In the FEA, a knee flexion of 45° under 200 N contact stress was simulated. Three fracture models were constructed for the TACS-SA, PCST with a figure-of-eight tension band (EPCST), and PCST with a figure-of-zero tension band (ZPCST). Fracture fragment stability was assessed by measuring mean displacement (MD-FF) and mean stress (MS-FF). The biomechanical strength of the fixation device was evaluated via its mean displacement (MD-IF) and mean stress (MS-IF). The clinical cohort included 43 patients, with 25 undergoing TACS-SA and 18 undergoing EPCST. Recorded parameters included bone union time, radiographic union time, range of motion (ROM), and follow-up duration. At the final follow-up, functional outcomes were evaluated with the Böstman score, KOOS score, and extension lag.ResultsIn the FEA, the TACS-SA models consistently showed lower MD-FF, MS-FF, and MD-IF across all three fragment conditions (p < 0.05), with the MS-IF of TACS-SA being moderate but not significant in the one-fragment condition (p > 0.05). In the retrospective cohort, the TACS-SA group achieved radiographic union earlier than the EPCST group (P < 0.05). Similar results were observed in the KOOS score, bone union period, ROM, Böstman score, and extension lag between the groups (P > 0.05).ConclusionThe TACS-SA technique exhibits superior biomechanical properties and clinical outcomes to the conventional PCST technique when treating IPFP.Keywords: Inferior pole fracture of the patella, Fixation, Tension-band, Suture anchor

Introduction

The patella, as the body’s largest sesamoid bone, plays a vital role in knee flexion and extension. As a key component of the knee extension mechanism, the patella enhances the quadriceps’ moment arm and improves knee stability [1]. The inferior pole, comprising approximately one-fourth to one-third of the patella, is the bony extension merged with the patellar tendon. Mainly composed of cancellous bone and lacking cartilage covering on its surface, the inferior pole is prone to breaking under tensile force. Due to its small size and frequent comminuted morphology, achieving satisfactory clinical outcomes in IPFP is often challenging for surgeons [2, 3].

Currently, no single strategy is universally accepted as the “gold-standard” for patellar-fracture fixation. Surgeons often select from an array of techniques—including partial-pole resection, trans-osseous suturing, conventional or cannulated-screw tension-band wiring, plate osteosynthesis, suture-anchor repair, shape-memory alloy constructs, and diverse cerclage configurations—each of which offers distinct biomechanical advantages while simultaneously introducing specific limitations [4, 5]. Among these options, the PCST technique has gained regional popularity because it converts anterior tensile forces into compressive stress at the fracture site, fostering primary bone healing [6–8]. In 2011, Chang and Ji [2] initially reported this method in treating IPFP. Over a 12-month follow-up period in their study, the case series demonstrated an average range of motion (ROM) of 122.5° and a Böstman score of 28.7, with no implant failures or reduction loss. However, complications were noted: 30% (3/10) of patients experienced knee weakness, and 50% (5/10) reported limited knee function with an average flexion loss of 17°. To this result, Zhang et al. [9] thought that two cannulated screws could not provide sufficient biomechanical support for comminuted IPFP because of the difficulty in accommodating the center of the inferior pole.

In recent years, the surgical management of patellar fractures (including IPFP) has progressively moved toward non-metallic fixation, aiming to reduce implant-related discomfort and late hardware failure [10]. However, non-metallic fixation seems to have insufficient strength when compared to screw fixation [11]. To reconcile the mechanical reliability of metal with the biological advantages of non-metallic constructs, we introduced the TACS-SA technique [12]. Our preliminary retrospective cohort showed encouraging short-term results. These data, however, emanate from a single-arm study; higher-level evidence comparing TACS-SA with the established PCST method is still lacking. So far, biomechanical analyses of IPFP have almost exclusively employed single-fragment surrogates, and data on the non-metallic fixation interface, specifically the suture-anchor–tendon complex, remain sparse. Therefore, the present investigation was designed to provide head-to-head biomechanical and clinical validation, determining whether the hybrid construct offers superior stability, lower complication rates, and improved functional outcomes, thereby establishing an evidence-based alternative for this challenging fracture.

Materials and methods

FEA

Fracture models

In the FEA, knee imaging data were acquired from a healthy volunteer (31-year-old male, 175 cm, 72 kg) with no history of osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, or fracture using a 64-slice CT scanner (Siemens®, Erlangen, Germany) and a Skyra 3.0 T MRI system (Siemens®, Erlangen, Germany). The DICOM data underwent initial processing in Mimics (v21.0, Materialise®, Belgium), involving threshold segmentation, region growing, dynamic region growing, Boolean operations, mask editing, hole filling, and surface smoothing. The patella was segmented based on Hounsfield Unit (HU) values of 130–2009, and the patellar tendon was isolated using grayscale values (GV) of 100–200. After export as STL files, both structures were imported into Geomagic Wrap (v2017, 3D Systems®, USA) for local optimization, denoising, and smoothing. Based on established patellar fracture mapping and IPFP characteristics [3, 13], the inferior pole was artificially divided into one (I), two (II), and three (III) fragment models in SolidWorks (v2022, Dassault Systèmes®, USA).

Experimental models

A previous FEA demonstrated that the “zero” tension-band technique stabilizes both sides of the anterior surface, thereby indirectly securing the middle region. In contrast, the conventional “eight” tension-band primarily fixes the central area only, rendering the “zero” configuration more effective in restricting bilateral displacement [14]. Based on these findings, both tension-band techniques were included as control groups in the present FEA.

Cannulated screws, suture anchors, and wires were modeled in accordance with manufacturer specifications (Smith & Nephew®, Massachusetts, USA). Three experimental models were developed: the TACS-SA, the EPCST, and the ZPCST. Each fixation construct was combined with three basic fracture models, resulting in nine experimental assemblies: (i) EPCST-I, EPCST-II, EPCST-III; (ii) TACS-I, TACS-II, TACS-III; (iii) ZPCST-I, ZPCST-II, ZPCST-III (Fig. 1).

Finite element meshing was performed using Ansys Workbench (v2022, Ansys, Pennsylvania, USA). To ensure computational accuracy and convergence, five mesh sensitivity tests were conducted by adaptively refining the element and node counts until stress variation was below 2%. For instance, mesh sizes ranging from 1.0 to 3.0 mm were evaluated for the patella and patellar tendon, confirming that a 1 mm mesh satisfied the stress deviation criterion. Final mesh sizes were accordingly defined as 1 mm for cannulated screws, 1 mm for suture anchors, 0.4 mm for tension-band wiring, and 0.1 mm for sutures (Fig. 2). Boundary conditions and load settings

Boundary conditions were defined according to previously established methods [15, 16]: (i) All degrees of freedom were constrained at the interfaces between the patella and the quadriceps tendon, and between the patella and the patellar tendon; (ii) Friction coefficients of 0.45, 0.3, and 0.3 were assigned to characterize the interactions between the fragments, the bone and the suture anchors, as well as the bone and the cannulated screws; (iii) The contacts between the suture and the fragments, the suture and the patellar tendon, as well as the cannulated screw and the wire were fixed. The static scenario that the contact stress of 200 N was simulated in this study (Fig. 3), equivalent to the quadriceps tensile of 200 N in similar research based on Newton’s second Law [14, 16, 17]: (i) F3 = F1*Sin20° + F2*Sin35°; (ii) F1*Cos20° = F2*Cos35° (1). The material properties of the patella were based on the empirical formulas in Mimics [18]: Elastic modulus = − 388.8 + 5925 × (− 13.4 + 1017 × HU) (2). According to previous literature [18–21], both bone and implants were modeled as linearly elastic, isotropic, and homogeneous materials. Measurement parameters

To identify the stability of the fragment, the MD-FF and the MS-FF were both measured. In addition, the MD-IF and MS-IF were recorded to compare the biomechanical strength. The results were determined by the mean value of a set of ten points in the target concentrated area of each parameter.

Clinical study

A total of 63 patients diagnosed with IPFP were retrospectively reviewed. Eight cases were excluded on account of loss of follow-up or data deficiency, and 12 cases were excluded for using other surgical techniques. Finally, 43 cases with complete follow-up data were included in this study. Among them, 25 were managed using TACS-SA and 18 with PCST (exclusively EPCST).

Surgical technique

A midline longitudinal incision was made anteriorly. By dissecting the flap under the deep fascia, the fragments became visible. Fracture reduction was maintained by two tower clamps. With the knee flexed at approximately 45°, a guide pin was inserted from the central aspect of the largest fragment toward the superior patellar body, directed perpendicular to the fracture line at the patellofemoral joint surface. Subsequently, a 4.00 mm cannulated screw was then introduced over the guide pin. A stainless steel wire was passed anteriorly and tightened. After that, two anchors preloaded with ultra-braid sutures were placed at 45° on either side of the proximal fragment. Half of the suture strands were used to repair the patellar tendon bilaterally using the Krachow technique; the remaining sutures were utilized to reinforce the reconstruction of the tendon, collateral ligaments, and smaller fragments. At last, the incision was closed, layer by layer. (Fig. 4).

Surgical procedure of the TACS-SA technique. A. Fracture reduction by two tower clamps. B. Implanting the cannulated screw and wire. C. Implanting the suture anchors at an angle of 45°. D. Repairing patellar tendon through the Krachow technique

Rehabilitation protocol

During surgery, knee flexion and extension (0–90 degrees) were initiated. Postoperatively, straight-leg elevation and isometric quadriceps femoris contraction were permitted without any external fixation, starting within two days after surgery. During the first week, knee flexion was limited to 45°, gradually increasing to 60° in the second week. After suture removal in the second week, partial weight-bearing with crutches was encouraged. By the third week, patients progressed to strength-oriented functional exercises. Most patients achieved nearly 90° of extension by the fourth postoperative week. Full range of motion and weight-bearing were recommended at eight weeks, contingent upon radiographic evidence of bone union.

Follow-up indicators

All included patients were ensured with a follow-up period of at least 12 months. During the follow-up, the bone union period, the radiographic union period, the range of motion (ROM), and the follow-up period were recorded. The Böstman score, KOOS score, and the extension lag compared with the healthy leg were evaluated at the final follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was achieved by SPSS (version 22.0, IBM, USA) and Origin (version 2022, OriginLab, USA). In the FEA, differences were examined using one-way variance analysis (ANOVA) within the assurance of the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). In the clinical study, continuous data are given as mean ± SD, and categorical variables are expressed as counts. For continuous variables with normal distributions, the Student’s t-test was employed to compare the data between two groups, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for the variables with non-normal distributions. The chi-square test was used to investigate categorical correlations between variables. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant; the levels of significance were divided into: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Results

FEA results

The stability of the fracture fragment

In the one-fragment model, the MD-FF was 0.0053 ± 0.0001, 0.0048 ± 0.0001, and 0.0054 ± 0.0001 mm in EPCST, TACS-SA, and ZPCST, respectively. And the MS-FF was 1.0340 ± 0.1486, 0.2440 ± 0.0678, 1.6710 ± 0.2375 MPa, respectively. In the two-fragment model, the MD-FF was 0.0057 ± 0.0040, 0.0047 ± 0.0006, 0.0052 ± 0.0020 mm, respectively. And the MS-FF was 0.9310 ± 0.1859, 0.0680 ± 0.1874, and 0.6960 ± 0.2121 MPa, respectively. In the three-fragment model, the MD-FF was 0.0054 ± 0.0029, 0.0047 ± 0.0009, 0.0052 ± 0.0030 mm, respectively. And the MS-FF was 1.0450 ± 0.2112, 0.0400 ± 0.0240, and 0.2960 ± 0.1016 MPa, respectively.

Figure 5 demonstrates that fragment displacement was significantly lower (P < 0.05) in the TACS-SA model than in both control groups across all three fracture types. The difference in displacement between the EPCST and ZPCST models was not statistically significant (P > 0.05), except in the two-fragment condition. As fragment number increased, bilateral instability became more pronounced in the EPCST and ZPCST models, particularly in the EPCST group, indicating its limited ability to stabilize multi-fragment fractures.

As shown in Fig. 6, the TACS-SA model consistently exhibited the smallest stress concentration area. In contrast, the ZPCST model showed the highest peak stress concentration, exceeding that of the EPCST model. Collectively, these findings indicate that the TACS-SA model provides superior stabilization and more effectively restricts fragment displacement compared to the EPCST and ZPCST techniques.

The stability of the internal fixation

In the one-fragment model, the MD-IF was 0.1123 ± 0.1183, 0.0042 ± 0.0012, and 0.4338 ± 0.0315 mm in EPCST, TACS-SA, and ZPCST, respectively. The MS-IF was 5.1370 ± 1.5629, 5.7700 ± 1.1476, 27.1580 ± 2.0735 MPa, respectively. In the two-fragment model, the MD-IF was 0.0499 ± 0.0108, 0.0039 ± 0.0009, 0.5013 ± 0.0138 mm, respectively. The MS-IF was 12.7170 ± 0.7888, 19.4190 ± 2.7418, and 31.4870 ± 3.1593 MPa, respectively. In the three-fragment model, the MD-IF was 0.5957 ± 0.0322, 0.0054 ± 0.0107, and 0.4451 ± 0.0349 mm, respectively. The MS-IF was 5.6950 ± 1.0068, 7.8840 ± 1.3075, 31.3180 ± 2.5292 MPa, respectively.

Figure 7 showcases the internal fixation displacement for each model. The tension-band wire was observed with marked displacement across all configurations, with the ZPCST exhibiting the highest values in the one- and two-fragment models, while the EPCST showed the greatest displacement in the three-fragment condition. Notably, the TACS-SA model consistently demonstrated the least displacement in all three fracture types, with statistically significant differences among the three techniques (P < 0.0001).

The nephograms display the displacement distribution of the internal fixations for the three techniques across three fracture models, and the bar diagrams show the mean displacement of the internal fixations (MD-IF). (Unit: mm)

As shown in Fig. 8, the ZPCST model consistently exhibited the highest peak stress concentration under all conditions. The TACS-SA technique showed intermediate stress levels, whereas the EPCST presented the lowest. However, no statistically significant difference was observed between the TACS-SA and EPCST groups in the one-fragment model (P > 0.05). In both the EPCST and ZPCST models, stress was predominantly concentrated at the upper and lower ends of the tension-band wire. In contrast, the TACS-SA technique displayed greater stress distribution around the cannulated screw, which offers superior mechanical strength compared to the tension-band wire, thereby potentially reducing the risk of implant failure.

Clinical outcomes

A comparative analysis of clinical outcomes was conducted between the TACS-SA group (n = 25) and the EPCST group (n = 18). The mean age was 58.04 ± 1.93 years in the TACS-SA group and 60.50 ± 1.87 years in the EPCST group, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (P = 0.381). Gender distribution was comparable, comprising 14 females and 11 males in the TACS-SA group, and 10 females and 8 males in the EPCST group (P = 0.977). Regarding injury etiology, traffic accidents constituted 4 cases in the TACS-SA group and 6 cases in the EPCST group. Falls represented the most prevalent mechanism, accounting for 19 cases in the TACS-SA group and 11 cases in the EPCST group. Injuries due to quadriceps contraction were recorded in 2 patients in the TACS-SA group and 1 patient in the EPCST group. These differences in injury causes were not statistically significant (P = 0.170). The affected side was the left in 16 patients of the TACS-SA group and 10 patients of the EPCST group, while the right side was affected in 9 and 8 patients, respectively (P = 0.208). Fracture patterns were classified as single (TACS-SA: 6; EPCST: 7) or comminuted (TACS-SA: 19; EPCST: 11), revealing no significant intergroup difference (P = 0.300). The mean bone union period was 8.36 ± 1.35 weeks for the TACS-SA group and 8.50 ± 1.15 weeks for the EPCST group (P = 0.601). However, the radiographic union period was significantly shorter in the TACS-SA group (11.44 ± 1.26 weeks) compared to the EPCST group (12.39 ± 1.09 weeks) (P = 0.042). Functional outcomes, including the mean range of motion (ROM) (TACS-SA: 135.00 ± 5.59°; EPCST: 132.22 ± 6.91°; P = 0.081), Böstman score (TACS-SA: 27.60 ± 2.08; EPCST: 26.94 ± 3.23; P = 0.700), KOOS (TACS-SA: 84.84 ± 6.22; EPCST: 83.06 ± 7.08; P = 0.386), and extension lag (TACS-SA: 3.60 ± 5.31°; EPCST: 5.56 ± 6.62°; P = 0.381), showed no significant differences. The mean follow-up duration was 20.68 ± 5.15 months for the TACS-SA group and 19.89 ± 6.13 months for the EPCST group (P = 0.631). Loss of reduction occurred in one patient in the TACS-SA group and two patients in the EPCST group. (Table 3).

Discussion

Although IPFP accounts for only a small proportion of knee-related fractures, inadequate management is associated with severe complications, such as joint stiffness, weakened knee extension, and patellofemoral arthritis [22]. Non-operative treatment is generally restricted to fractures with displacement < 3 mm and an intact extensor apparatus, or to patients whose comorbidities preclude surgery; all other cases warrant operative stabilization [7, 22].

The tension-band wiring technique, favored for its ability to transform stress and promote bony union, remains the reference standard for patellar fractures [7, 12, 23–25]. However, the comminuted nature typical of IPFP frequently precludes the insertion of two parallel cannulated screws, thereby limiting the applicability of the conventional PCST technique. Under these circumstances, a single cannulated screw may be used to provide axial stability [2, 12, 26, 27]. Suture-anchor fixation is an alternative strategy that permits reliable reconstitution of the extensor mechanism, yet biomechanical investigations in cadaveric models have demonstrated that suture anchors exhibit a significantly lower mean failure load (201 N) than interference screws (229 N) [12, 28, 29]. Other data indicate that suture-anchor constructs are mechanically inferior to screw-based fixation and should be supplemented with additional rigid implants [11, 30]. Consequently, a hybrid internal fixation method—the TACS-SA technique—has been devised to integrate the mechanical advantages of tension-band wiring with the soft-tissue reattachment capability of suture anchors.

Previous biomechanical studies of IPFP have predominantly employed single-fragment surrogates; however, comminuted synthetic models more faithfully reproduce the native mechanical environment [2, 12, 24, 31, 32]. In the present FEA, one-, two-, and three-fragment geometries were therefore generated to correspond with the morphological subtypes described by Liu et al. [13]. Across all fracture complexities, the TACS-SA construct conferred markedly superior interfragmentary stability relative to the PCST; this advantage became progressively more pronounced as fragment number increased. Concomitantly, TACS-SA consistently yielded lower peak von Mises stresses within the osseous segments, implying a more favorable biomechanical milieu for osseous union. The internal fixation implants themselves exhibited intermediate stress magnitudes, denoting acceptable load sharing and a reduced risk of implant-related failure.

Clinical corroboration was obtained through a consecutive cohort comparison. Patients treated with TACS-SA achieved higher mean Böstman and KOOS scores, greater active range of motion, shorter radiographic union times, and lower prevalence of extensor lag than those managed with extended PCST; although these differences did not reach statistical significance. Most importantly, reduction loss occurred in only 4% (1/25) of TACS-SA cases versus 11% (2/18) of EPCST cases, underscoring the superior mechanical reliability of the hybrid construct. Collectively, the biomechanical and clinical data indicate that TACS-SA provides at least equivalent, and potentially superior, efficacy to the reference tension-band technique for the management of inferior-pole patellar fractures.

Contemporary management of PFP is increasingly guided by two complementary biomechanical paradigms: (1) intra-osseous fixation, which achieves stability through interfragmentary compression and osseous interlocking, and (2) coverage fixation, which provides cortical buttress and tendon-to-bone re-attachment [12, 13, 33]. Liu et al. [13] observed that IPFP typically presents as a dominant fragment and multiple tiny fragments; in their cohort, the fracture complex averaged 3.8 fragments arranged in a transverse-comminuted pattern with a 92.1° coronal-plane angle and a 93.0° sagittal-plane angle. These morphometric data underscore the limited reliability of isolated intra-osseous implants in many IPFP patterns. To rationalize implant selection, the authors proposed a four-tier morphological classification: Type I (simple IPFP with one fragment), Type II (comminuted IPFP with two or more fragments), Type III (simple IPFP with a simple patellar body fracture, further divided into III-A with transverse patellar fracture and III-B with longitudinal patellar fracture), and Type IV (comminuted patella fracture with three or more multidirectional fracture lines involving the inferior pole). Intra-osseous fixation is advocated for Types I and III-A because an adequate cancellous surface remains for screw implantation and compression. Conversely, Types II, III-B, and IV exhibit a diminished osseous footprint; exclusive intra-osseous fixation is mechanically precarious in these subgroups, which are better served by coverage constructs used either alone or as part of a hybrid strategy [13]. The TACS-SA technique integrates both philosophies. A cannulated lag screw secures the dominant fragment (intra-osseous component), while tension-band wiring neutralizes tensile forces. Simultaneously, suture anchors re-approximate the patellar tendon and capture small cortical fragments (coverage component), thereby restoring extensor-mechanism continuity. Furthermore, a quantitative FEA mentions that the linear design of the prosthesis is beneficial for reducing peak stress and optimizing the overall biomechanical environment [34]. Thus, this hybrid configuration is applicable to most IPFP subtypes and is particularly advantageous in comminuted patterns where the conventional PCST construct is mechanically inadequate.

This study offers two principal strengths. First, the finite-element models progress from single- to three-fragment geometries that mirror the morphological spectrum encountered clinically, enhancing external validity. Second, by formalising the complementary principles of “intra-osseous fixation” (interfragmentary compression of the main fragment) and “coverage fixation” (capture of tiny cortical shards and tendon re-attachment), we provide a biomechanical framework that guides implant selection when both osseous comminution and soft-tissue disruption are present.

However, our study has several limitations. First, the FEA assumed bone, cartilage, and soft tissues to be homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic continua; although standard for comparative analyses, these findings require validation in cadaveric or synthetic bone models that incorporate visco-elastic and anisotropic properties. Second, the clinical cohort was limited to 43 patients, reflecting the low incidence of IPFP and the practical difficulty of obtaining ≥ 12-month follow-up, as patients typically discontinue attendance once radiographic union is confirmed (3–6 months). We acknowledge that this may affect generalizability and therefore employed distribution-appropriate statistical methods to strengthen the robustness of our inferences. Third, the encouraging numerical trends must be confirmed in adequately powered, prospective, randomized, and blinded trials to minimize selection and observer bias and to establish definitive clinical benefit.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the novel TACS-SA construct confers superior stabilization of both osseous fragments and the fixation construct compared with conventional PCST. Its hybrid, morphology-adapted design is suitable for the full spectrum of IPFP and yields favourable early clinical outcomes.
