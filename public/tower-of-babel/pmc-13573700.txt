Practical considerations for dense longitudinal neuroimaging: Ten simple rules for frequent scanning over short time periods
Authors: E Cross, AB Compton, A Vess, JR Booth, K Kay, S Vinci-Booher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDense longitudinal neuroimaging (DLN) is a precision neuroimaging approach that samples frequently throughout a targeted window of time to characterize individual trajectories of brain change. The approach enables the answering of fundamental questions about the underlying mechanisms of cognitive development and learning. However, acquiring DLN data presents practical challenges that go beyond those associated with acquiring conventional longitudinal data. In this paper, we discuss key practical considerations for designing and executing DLN studies, especially with respect to collecting data from children and other populations with limited scan tolerance. We provide ten simple rules for study design, measurement, recruitment and retention, and compliance to assist research teams seeking to conduct DLN studies.Keywords: Dense, Precision, Longitudinal, Neuroimaging, Best practices, Protocol

Dense longitudinal neuroimaging (DLN) is an emerging approach that acquires multiple brain scans from individual participants throughout a period of rapid change. The approach enables theoretical and applied advancements in studies where understanding neural mechanisms over time are of primary interest, such as studies of development, aging, or intervention response (Vinci-Booher et al., 2025), as an important complement to the large population-scale longitudinal datasets (Volkow et al., 2018; Sudlow et al., 2015). Collecting DLN data requires participants to undergo multiple sessions within a short window of time, sometimes as often as every day for a month (Murata et al., 2024, Pritschet et al., 2020) — a design characteristic that places uncommon burdens on participants and presents unique challenges for research teams. Furthermore, studies of development and intervention response often work with populations that typically experience formidable challenges to research participation, such as low study compliance and high attrition rates. DLN studies can be particularly challenging for these populations, as each participant must complete multiple sessions in short windows of time.

DLN is becoming increasingly common across multiple research domains (Dehaene-Lambertz et al., 2018, Huber et al., 2018, Murata et al., 2024, Pritschet et al., 2020), yet there are currently no best practice guidelines for conducting these labor-intensive studies. Drawing upon our combined experience conducting DLN and other multi-session studies, we provide guidelines for designing and executing a DLN study. We discuss DLN-specific considerations in the form of ten simple rules that cover study design, measurement, recruitment and retention, and compliance that we have found to be key to successfully collecting DLN data (Table 1). Additionally, we cover practical DLN considerations that are also relevant to multi-session studies, large MRI datasets, and populations with limited scan tolerance. Considerations related to analytical and statistical choices for DLN data are beyond the scope of this paper, and we refer the reader to relevant discussions from conventional longitudinal work (King et al., 2018, Li et al., 2025).

We focus our discussion on two populations that stand to benefit greatly from the knowledge learned: young children and specific populations. We use the term ‘specific populations’ to refer to populations for whom standard adult MRI protocols often require modification, such as clinical populations and individuals with atypical developmental histories.

Ten simple rules for DLN study designs

1. Define the study population narrowly and deeply understand its characteristics.

DLN is a precision neuroimaging approach with characteristically small sample sizes that are not intended to be representative of the general population (Dehaene-Lambertz et al., 2018, Flournoy et al., 2024, Huber et al., 2018, Pritschet et al., 2021). Therefore, the selection of the study population should be narrowly defined to a population that exhibits the exact change of interest. More specifically, to select the study population, researchers should consider the expected trajectory of the change and pinpoint a narrow sample that adequately represents the population of interest by excluding populations with extraneous factors that may suppress or alter that trajectory and including populations with factors that promote that trajectory. The aim is to build a detailed model of change in individual participants to provide insight into a change mechanism that future research can evaluate in broader populations (Gell et al., 2024, Naselaris et al., 2021). For example, a researcher might wish to establish if a math intervention causes a reorganization of functional brain responses in children. The researcher could consider recruiting a few children with similar educational and financial backgrounds, without developmental or academic delays, and of similar health, age, and year in schooling, such that the modeling captures the impact of the intervention for that narrowly defined study population. Importantly, the children would need to be sampled throughout a period in their development and learning when math changes can be expected. In this case, a sample might be defined based on age and grade but might be even better defined based on other variables that correlate with the change of interest in the population (e.g., recruiting low-socioeconomic status [SES] first grade children or high-SES kindergarten children). Arguably, only a few participants from that narrowly defined population are needed to ensure some generalizability beyond a single participant; future investigations would be needed to test the bounds of generalization to broader populations.

2. Only use DLN when the scientific question requires high density sampling over time.

DLN is a labor-intensive design, requiring substantial and sustained commitment from the participants and research team. For example, DLN studies have scanned the same adult every day for a month (Murata et al., 2024, Pritschet et al., 2020), every other week for 2 months (Huber et al., 2018), or every other month for a year (Dehaene-Lambertz et al., 2018). Many scientific questions can be answered adequately using less intensive designs, such as cross-sectional and conventional longitudinal neuroimaging designs, and those designs should be considered if they are sufficient to address the scientific question. Scientific questions that require a DLN approach often begin with a question about the mechanism underlying a change of interest, a question that requires a complete model of potentially non-linear trajectories of brain change (Fig. 1A; Vinci-Booher et al., 2025). For example, a researcher might require a DLN design to establish the temporal order of distinct brain changes to support causal inferences about the brain mechanisms of early reading development (Dehaene-Lambertz et al., 2018). To establish this temporal order, the researcher would need to estimate trajectories over time with enough precision to adequately account for nonlinearities and threshold effects. After establishing temporal order, they might go on to use a DLN design to further evaluate potential mechanisms by probing the measured trajectories and observing responses to the manipulation (Thelen and Smith, 1994), eventually leading to effective intervention approaches. As additional examples, DLN should be used when the phenomenon of interest is expected to be transient, such as the temporary brain changes following traumatic brain injury that predict cognitive recovery (Rajesh et al., 2025) or individualized rates of brain change in healthy aging (Elliott et al., 2026).

For example, some studies may need to prioritize the number of participants at the expense of the amount of data per session while other studies may need to prioritize the amount of data per session at the expense of the number of participants. Still others may emphasize sampling density beyond the minimum needed at the expense of both data per session and number of participants.

3. Define the sampling window and sampling frequency based on prior knowledge.

The sampling window and frequency define the sampling density of a DLN study (Vinci-Booher et al., 2025). The window is typically selected first because it defines the specific period of interest (e.g., first grade year for early literacy and numeracy learning). The sampling window should be selected based on prior findings from conventional or accelerated longitudinal studies and should cover the expected window of change as well as any necessary baseline or endline periods. For example, a study of early progression of Parkinson’s disease might define the sampling window as the period of anticipated motor decline identified by prior epidemiological data. The sampling window should be selected in a way that avoids floor or ceiling effects and matches the period of expected change for the sample population (Practical Rule 1). The sampling frequency is typically selected second. The frequency determines the number of samples that will be acquired and should be selected to optimize modeling of the change trajectory. In the same study of Parkinson’s progression, the sampling frequency might be chosen to capture short-term fluctuations in medication response as a complement to tracking the longer-term trajectory of degeneration with conventional longitudinal designs. The more complex the model, the greater the necessary sampling frequency. It can be tempting to set the frequency high, going beyond the frequency necessary for the modeling. However, very high sampling frequencies may not be feasible in terms of time and resources for both participants and the research team alike, leading to retention and compliance issues that ultimately degrade the quality of the dataset (Fig. 1B). In the case of children and specific populations, effectively balancing sampling density with participant feasibility is especially important because these populations may already face substantial barriers to participation.

4. Maintain sufficient sampling density while balancing data per session and number of participants.

Making design decisions for DLN studies requires balancing tradeoffs among at least three dimensions: sampling density, data collected per session, and the number of participants (Fig. 1C; Gell et al., 2024). The first dimension, sampling density, must be set and maintained to ensure that the trajectory of change is adequately measured. How one balances the amount of data collected at each session and the number of participants depends on study goals. For example, a research team planning an analysis of within-participant change trajectories to determine a mechanistic understanding of an intervention response might prioritize the amount of data per session (Gell et al., 2024), whereas a research team planning a growth curve analysis to estimate between-participant differences in within-participant change trajectories may prioritize the number of participants (Curran et al., 2010, Grimm et al., 2011). For any DLN study, however, the sampling density is set at no less than is necessary to model the expected trajectory of change, followed by a study-specific balance between the amount of data collected per session and the number of participants.

The second dimension, the data collected per session, is often streamlined to reduce the data collection required at each session by collecting only the necessary measurements. If the amount of data collected at each session is too large, then sessions may be too burdensome for the participant or research team to repeat often and maintain over the full sampling window. Research teams should err on the side of collecting more data per task rather than collecting less data from more tasks to increase the reliability of the collected measurements. Collecting more data per task increases task-level reliability and should be prioritized up to the point where additional data no longer meaningfully reduce measurement error (Practical Rules 5 and 6), at which point additional session time is better spent on breaks or novel tasks to avoid repetition, boredom, frustration, or fatigue. Only after the reliability of the necessary measurements is secured should the research team consider collecting data from additional tasks. It can be prudent to consider measurements that ‘come for free’, such as video recordings, that generally do not increase participation burden but can increase the amount of data collected at each session. Additionally, collecting data from young children and specific populations typically requires additional breaks to mitigate fatigue or repeating scan sequences due to head motion (Winter et al., 2018). Hence, the amount of data expected from each session is often substantially reduced for these populations relative to what might be expected from other populations.

The third dimension, the number of participants, is often small to allow for the required sampling density given resource limitations that are especially limiting for DLN studies, such as time, cost, and equipment usage (Gratton et al., 2022, Naselaris et al., 2021). DLN studies can be effective with very small sample sizes, even as small as one participant (Pritschet et al., 2021). Exceptions are DLN studies that focus on between-participant differences in within-participant trajectories, and for those studies, the number of participants would need to be prioritized over data per session (Curran et al., 2010, Grimm et al., 2011). In any case, DLN researchers should aim to collect as many participants as possible within the given constraints to ensure some amount of generalizability beyond a single participant.

5. Select high-powered MRI protocols to precisely measure between-session changes.

Brain measurements selected for a DLN study must be able to recover what are often small session-to-session changes within individual participants. To accomplish this, DLN researchers need a multi-pronged approach that optimizes the detection of within-participant change.

First, design and analytic choices should be made with an eye towards high-powered protocols that demonstrate sensitivity within individual participants. For example, participant-specific functional localizers produce greater within-participant effect sizes than anatomical localizers (Nieto-Castañón and Fedorenko, 2012), and sensitivity can be further enhanced by pairing these participant-specific functional localizers with multi-voxel pattern analyses that can be more sensitive than univariate analyses (Davis et al., 2014), single-trial estimates that quantify measurement error (Prince et al., 2022), and optimized protocols that reduce scan time (Marvi et al., 2025). In the least, efforts should be made to quantify reliability for design and analytic choices to determine the threshold for meaningful between-session changes. Selection of the reliability statistic should consider that reducing between-participant variance (Practical Rule 1) can artificially deflate common reliability statistics, such as intra-class correlation (ICC) calculated as a ratio of between-participant variance to total variance, even when the absolute measurement precision is unchanged. Researchers should report sample-independent metrics alongside ICC, such as standard error of the mean (SEM) and minimal detectable change (MDC), to provide an assessment of the underlying measurement precision. When necessary, additional efforts can be made to optimize the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of candidate MRI protocols (including pulse sequence parameters and experimental design). Some simple guidelines include the use of jittering and blocked designs in fMRI stimulation protocols (Parrish et al., 2000; Petersen and Dubois, 2012; Serences, 2004; Wager and Nichols, 2003) and inclusion of a sufficient number of b = 1000 s/mm2 volumes in multi-shell diffusion imaging (Basser et al., 1994, Zhang et al., 2012). Other MRI-based measurements acquired at each session, such as those associated with quantitative imaging (Travis et al., 2019), should be subjected to similar considerations.

Second, experimental procedures should be selected to minimize incidental session effects and ensure consistent engagement from participants. A straightforward way to minimize incidental session effects is to standardize the time of day to reduce variance related to diurnal rhythms (Murata et al., 2024, Pritschet et al., 2021) and the type of day to reduce variance related to pre-visit experiences (e.g., school/work vs. non-school/workdays; (Telzer et al., 2018). Procedures to ensure consistent participant engagement across sessions are less straightforward, but often necessary to minimize incidental session effects related to participant compliance. DLN studies may need to specifically prevent the waning engagement that can come with repeating similar study procedures so often, a consideration that does not exist for conventional longitudinal studies that often have one or more years between sessions. Increasing the novelty of each session can help maintain engagement. DLN studies can increase novelty by allowing variation in any aspect that does not jeopardize the experimental effects of interest (e.g., by reducing test-retest reliability). For example, item-level stimuli are commonly safe to change because the underlying construct does not change (e.g., phonological processing), even if the individual test items differ (e.g., exact words being heard). In addition, using naturalistic stimuli (e.g., complex scenes or videos) (Allen et al., 2022; Marvi et al., 2025) can increase the novelty and engagement at each visit compared to using stereotyped, highly controlled functional localizer paradigms (Stigliani et al., 2015). Furthermore, varying the order of the administration of study tasks can reduce fatigue and minimize unintended carryover effects. Additional efforts supporting measurement variability in the testing environment can be useful as well, including using the same scanner, coil configuration, and study team.

6. Use reliable and sensitive behavioral measures to detect short-term, within-person changes.

Many DLN studies will acquire behavioral measurements simultaneously with scanning to relate behavioral changes to brain changes. A behavioral measure selected for a DLN study must be able to recover what are often small changes within individual participants, making many commonly used behavioral assessments poorly suited for the repeated within-person sampling necessary for dense longitudinal sampling.

One key consideration for selecting a behavioral measure suitable for a DLN study is the measure’s reliability and sensitivity, as initially touched upon in the discussion of sample homogeneity and reliability metrics (Practical Rules 1 and 5). Measures must be sensitive enough to capture subtle change trajectories and precise enough that true change is not obscured by measurement error. For example, a DLN study that measures vocabulary growth in an 18-month-old child would need to detect a small but meaningful growth rate of 5–20 words per month (Fenson et al., 1994, Huttenlocher et al., 1991). The Bayley III Language Composite has a standard error of measurement of 4.4 standard points, yielding a 95% confidence interval that corresponds to approximately + /-8.6 standard points at each timepoint (Albers and Grieve, 2007). A small change of 5–20 words is likely to fall within the bounds of measurement error, making month-to-month detection unlikely. Moreover, standard scores are norm-referenced and such small changes in vocabulary are unlikely to move the child’s score into the next standard deviation, resulting in unchanged scores despite real changes in ability. For this reason, DLN studies may wish to rely on raw scores, such as raw word counts from the MacArthur Bates Cognitive Development Inventories (CDI) that is designed to measure incremental changes within individuals (Fenson et al., 2012). However, efforts should be made to quantify test-retest variability for any behavioral measurement to determine the threshold for meaningful between-session changes.

A second, more practical, key consideration is the number of alternate forms that allow a behavioral assessment to be administered repeatedly in short time frames without substantial practice effects. Many standard assessments provide only a small number of alternate forms (Dunn and Dunn, 2012; Matthews and Davis, 2018; Schrank and Wendling, 2018; Wilkinson and Robertson, 2006), limiting their suitability for DLN studies. One exception is progress monitoring (PM) assessments that are designed specifically for repeated administration and commonly provide dozens of equivalent forms (Deno, 1985, Fuchs et al., 1993, Yeatman et al., 2021). However, PM assessments are often used to track student progress, so it is important to ensure that these tools are not embedded within local schools and intervention programs. In some cases, research teams may need to develop or adapt in-house instruments and may consider making use of planned missing data designs (Rhemtulla and Little, 2012).

Finally, behavioral measures should exhibit maturational stability throughout the sampling window such that the measures reflect the same underlying construct across sampling points. If the construct itself changes over the sampling window, perhaps with age or with an intervention, then longitudinal inferences can become invalid. Expanding on the example provided above, the construct of language comprehension measured by the Bayley III is likely to change across the 18-month age range, whereas the number of known words measured by the CDI likely reflects a stable construct.

7. Recruit through intentional and sustained community engagement.

Given that DLN populations are narrowly defined, only a small proportion of the general population will fit the inclusion criteria. Recruitment efforts should focus on reaching that population specifically rather than advertising broadly (Fig. 2A; Table 2). Distributing advertisements through social media and other platforms that specialize in matching research participants with studies are typically impactful recruitment tactics. Outreach campaigns can be customized, targeting individuals of specific groups (e.g., parents of specific ages, or specific age groups), with specific interests (e.g., science, psychology, kid-friendly), and in specific locations (e.g., by radius to lab location). Recruitment materials should make clear the intensity of the commitment to a DLN study; the more a potential participant understands about the study, the more able they are to make a decision about whether or not to contact the research team about participating. These efforts help to ensure that the individuals who do contact the research team are well-informed, and therefore, more likely to enroll and remain engaged throughout the study. The principal objective behind all additional efforts is to establish a long-term, mutually beneficial partnership between the study population and research team (Sarrasin et al., 2020). There are two key ingredients of effective long-term recruitment efforts: reputation management and regularly scheduled outreach activities. Reputation management refers to oversight of the university, department, and research lab’s public reputations and is largely handled through lab websites, social media accounts, and messaging at local events and media outlets. Regularly scheduled outreach activities bring the research team to members of the study population and include activities such as public lectures and information booths at community events, preferably scheduled at relevant organizations and locations (Table 2). These long-term efforts provide opportunities for the research team to deepen their relationship with a study population through a community partnership that, once established, can help researchers increase the relevance of the research program and more effectively incentivize participation for that community. At the same time, community partnerships help members of the study population better understand the research process and engender trust in the research team, thereby increasing receptivity and interest in research participation broadly.

8. Use a tiered enrollment strategy to ensure long-term compliance and participation.

DLN research teams should screen potential participants thoroughly before they enroll to avoid spending effort and resources for data that will not be usable. One technique for ensuring that participants are clear about the expectations of research participation is to adopt a tiered enrollment strategy that begins with a pre-study interview before advancing to a screening visit and then enrollment in the main study (Fig. 2B). The idea is that the participant is more likely to enroll once they are familiar with the experimental procedures, and that participants who say yes after the pre-study interview or screening visit are more likely to complete the full study because their ‘yes’ is well-informed.

The pre-study interview can be conducted virtually or in person after the prospective participant passes basic screening questions. The research team can describe the study in more detail than standard recruitment materials allow and ask additional screening questions to evaluate fit for the DLN study. The pre-study interview is also a time for the prospective participants to ask questions, an option that is especially important for children and specific populations (Garcini et al., 2022).

The screening visit can be a time for prospective participants to undergo a mock MRI session to ensure that they are able and willing to undergo basic study procedures. The research team might consider including a real MRI scan during the screening visit to evaluate the potential for the participant to provide high-quality MRI data (Allen et al., 2022). Such a screening visit will require a screening consent process that is separate from the consent process for enrollment in the main study. The screening consent process should describe the screening procedures and the criteria that would allow or prohibit the participant from enrolling in the main study.

Once enrolled in the main study, the multiple visits of the study should be scheduled up front to make participation more feasible for participants who are often managing hectic and unpredictable schedules, such as parents with school-aged children and the elderly who may have recurring doctor appointments. Researchers should consider automating calendar invitations, email reminders, text reminders, and email follow-ups for participants. Alternatively, or complementarily, researchers can attach reward-based incentives to specific compliance concerns or by population needs (e.g., sticker charts for children).

Despite these enrollment tactics, participation and compliance in DLN studies may still decline as the study progresses over time, suggesting the need for an exit clause in the consent form to avoid wasteful data collection efforts (Fig. 2C). The exit clause should state that continued participation in the study will be contingent upon compliance and continued eligibility. Two suggestions might be dependability (e.g., the participant must have no more than two consecutively missed timepoints) or task performance (e.g., the participant must maintain above 75% accuracy on in-scanner tasks).

One important compliance consideration is head motion. Head motion is a pervasive compliance issue for conventional longitudinal designs with young children and specific populations (Greene et al., 2016, Power et al., 2012, Satterthwaite et al., 2012, Van Dijk et al., 2012). However, DLN studies may tend to avoid the problem of between-session differences in head motion (Fig. 3). The enrollment process has a selection bias towards participants who are able to complete full sessions and commit to frequent sessions, so DLN studies of young children may tend to select low motion participants. However, both DLN and conventional longitudinal studies may observe increases in head motion across runs within a session, with higher motion in later runs than earlier runs. This is consistent with common guidelines for functional neuroimaging that suggest positioning the functional runs at the beginning of the scan protocol and keeping scan times short to avoid increases in head motion across runs (Engelhardt et al., 2017, Horien et al., 2020).

9. Retain participants through participant-centered supports.

Many retention strategies for DLN are like those used for conventional longitudinal designs, but with an important shift in focus towards increasing ease of participation and developing strong, lasting relationships with participants. Conventional longitudinal designs often focus on maintaining contact over multiple years, accounting for changes of addresses and contact information that often occur across years (Davis et al., 2022). DLN designs, on the other hand, focus on maximizing participant support and establishing a high-quality relationship between the participant and research team (Vinci-Booher et al., 2025).

At minimum, participant support should be responsive to the high demand on the participant’s time by ensuring substantial scheduling flexibility through quick response times and options to reschedule sessions on short or no advanced notice. An effective strategy for some populations is an online self-booking system to allow participants to schedule and even reschedule study sessions at their convenience. Additionally, researchers might help the participant plan around other life events, such as travel or medical procedures. Beyond the minimum, researchers might address persistent needs in the lives of the potential participants, such as dependent-care and transportation assistance during visits (Table 3).

Establishing a high-quality relationship between the participant and research team is another important feature of DLN retention efforts. A strong relationship between the participant and the research team should be established early in the study and maintained throughout the duration of the full study timeline. Note-taking is important for all study types but can be especially essential in DLN studies when remembering personal details (e.g., likes/dislikes) can go a long way towards building and maintaining strong relationships. If a participant has requested specific accommodations in the past, note-taking can help the research team proactively address those needs in future visits. Interactions between the participants and the research team are frequent in DLN designs, and the quality of that relationship is more obvious and impactful in a DLN study design than in a conventional design.

10. Offer effective compensation.

Compensation structures for DLN studies should increase the benefit-to-burden ratio of participation consistent with the intensity of the demand on the participant’s time. Research teams should offer traditional forms of compensation at minimum, such as financial compensation. Financial compensation structures for DLN studies should provide some amount of compensation at every visit with a tiered bonus structure that provides increasingly large bonuses at critical waypoints, such as after each quarter of the sampling window. Providing cash or rechargeable gift cards is more effective than gift cards for frequent compensation. Compensation structures for children also commonly include a toy in addition to financial compensation for the child and caregivers that, again, should be provided at every visit, and scaled such that they might receive the most exciting toys at critical waypoints.

DLN studies offer research teams an opportunity to offer non-traditional forms of compensation that further increase the benefit-to-burden ratio of participation. Non-traditional forms of compensation can also increase access to the study for participants and, therefore, increase flexibility to quickly reschedule study sessions and reduce the burden of participation. For example, providing a regular day camp for children participating in a DLN study offers parents free dependent care and increased flexibility to reschedule scans without hassle. The frequency of the camp could be such that study visits could occur while the child is attending camp, reducing late arrivals that result in data loss and increasing scheduling flexibility. Additionally, a benefit of a camp is that participants have a social incentive for continued study participation because study visits become an occasion to see friends. A second example of non-traditional forms of compensation is to offer enrichment outings for specific populations (e.g., a picnic in the park or a trip to the art museum). Although this second example would not increase flexibility for scheduling scans, it would still increase the benefit of participation by building community and incentive via social structures that translate into increased retention and relevancy of the research program over time.

General practical considerations relevant for DLN studies

Attrition considerations for multi-session study designs

DLN is a multi-session study design, and some practical considerations for DLN studies can be borrowed from practices that work well in other multi-session designs, such as conventional longitudinal neuroimaging that typically samples not more than once per year (Davis et al., 2022, Telzer et al., 2018, Wang et al., 2022, Wiseman et al., 2019) and precision neuroimaging that samples much more frequently but ignores the longitudinal change that is central to DLN (Kupers et al., 2024, Poldrack, 2021). Multi-session studies can experience high attrition, often due to missed or unusable sessions and/or losing participants during the course of the study, similar to DLN studies. Missed sessions and difficulty rescheduling may occur due to miscommunications between the research team and participants, while unusable sessions may occur when there is a lapse in participant compliance or data quality at a particular session (Davis et al., 2022, Luna et al., 2010, Simhal et al., 2021, Tazegul et al., 2015). Additionally, participants may unexpectedly drop out of the study due to a relocation, a new medical procedure or diagnosis that renders them ineligible, or simply a lack of continued interest in participating (Davis et al., 2022, Gell et al., 2024, Poldrack, 2021).

Best practices for minimizing between-session attrition for any multi-session study begin with establishing rapport and maintaining consistent communication with participants throughout the study duration (Davis et al., 2022). A good practice is to regularly communicate study progress and any findings to make clear the impact of their participation. Monitoring for changes in participant contact information in the visit-to-visit support loop is especially important to ensure that participants receive study communications (Fig. 2C).

Data management and sharing considerations for large datasets

DLN datasets are large, and practices associated with managing and sharing large datasets can be applied to DLN datasets. Early planning to ensure that the data sharing infrastructure is scalable and sustainable is essential to ensure that these difficult-to-acquire DLN datasets are reusable and maximally impactful (Kupers et al., 2024). First, large datasets require adequate storage solutions and enough compute to perform basic preprocessing and analyses. Second, data should be organized in simple, widely used formats (e.g., the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS)) (Gorgolewski et al., 2016, Poldrack et al., 2024) that are required by common analysis packages (Esteban et al., 2019) and compatible with widely used sharing platforms (Halchenko et al., 2021, Hayashi et al., 2024, Markiewicz et al., 2021) (Fig. 4). Third, rigorous standardization and documentation procedures should clearly delineate what data was collected during each timepoint with detailed records of changes throughout the progression of the study. Additionally, research teams may consider preparing high-quality pre-processed versions of the DLN data and software tools to facilitate data access and basic data manipulation procedures, thereby sparing users the challenge of processing raw data and lowering the barrier to entry.

Within-session head motion and compliance considerations for young children and specific populations

Neuroimaging studies typically spend substantial effort to minimize head motion, which is higher in children and specific populations than in typical adults (Dosenbach et al., 2017), and to maximize task compliance, which is lower in children and specific populations than in typical adults (Schlund et al., 2011, Simhal et al., 2021). To minimize head motion, studies with children and specific populations often provide a practice session in a mock scanner (Barnea-Goraly et al., 2014, de Bie et al., 2010, Raschle et al., 2009, Woods-Frohlich et al., 2010) to systematically acclimate the participant to the MRI scanner itself (Copeland et al., 2021, Vannest et al., 2014). Once participants are in the scanner, presenting compelling stimuli, (e.g., requested movies; Greene et al., 2018; Vanderwal et al., 2015), and providing real-time motion feedback to the participant and research team can further minimize head motion (e.g., FIRMM technology and a “scanner buddy”; Dosenbach et al., 2017; Greene et al., 2018; Krause et al., 2019). Additional interventions during the MRI scan include regular breaks and check-ins between each scan (Meissner et al., 2020), providing weighted blankets (Horien et al., 2020), and rewards that incentivize stillness (Horien et al., 2020). Practical considerations for neuroimaging studies with these populations have been proposed elsewhere and are widely used for young children (Copeland et al., 2021, Davis et al., 2022, Greene et al., 2016, Hendrix and Thomason, 2022, Horien et al., 2020, Kraus and Horowitz-Kraus, 2022, Thomason, 2009, Vannest et al., 2014) and specific populations (Backhausen et al., 2022, Church et al., 2025, Greene et al., 2016, Horien et al., 2020, Nordahl et al., 2016, Simhal et al., 2021).
