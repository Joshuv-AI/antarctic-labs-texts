Interaction of Obese Body Mass Index and Comorbidities on Inflammatory Profiles and Clinical Outcomes After Traumatic Brain Injury: A TRACK‐TBI Study
Authors: Shawn R Eagle, Mary U Simons, Jason Barber, Nancy Temkin, John K Yue, Ava Puccio, Michael A McCrea, Joseph Giacino, Geoffrey Manley, David O Okonkwo, Lindsay D Nelson, The TRACK‐TBI Investigators
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveObesity has been associated with worse clinical outcomes after mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI; Glasgow Coma Scale score between 13 and 15). Augmented inflammatory response to mTBI appears to be an important modifier of outcomes for the patients with obesity, but the contribution of comorbidity profile has yet to be analyzed. The objective of this study was to examine the association of: 1. comorbidity profile; and 2. the interaction of obesity with comorbidity profile on clinical outcomes and inflammatory blood biomarkers after mTBI.MethodsParticipants were enrolled across emergency departments within 24 h of injury, with clinical outcomes and neurocognitive tests assessed at 2 weeks and 6 months post‐injury (n = 1337). Latent class analysis (LCA) was used to identify comorbidity profiles and mixed‐effect models for the association of obesity and comorbidity profile on inflammatory/clinical outcomes.ResultsUsing LCA, 3 classes of comorbidities were identified: “cardiovascular” (high rates of cardiovascular disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes; n = 287; 21%), “internalizing” (high rates of depression/anxiety, n = 121; 9%), and “healthy” (0%–8% rate of comorbidities; n = 929; 69%). “Healthy” had higher rates of functional recovery at 6 month (31%; p = 0.001) than “cardiovascular” and “internalizing” (15%–26%). Within “healthy,” obesity was associated with higher c‐reactive protein levels on day 1 (ratio = 1.86; p < 0.001), 2 week (ratio = 2.05; p < 0.001) and 6 month (ratio = 2.34; p < 0.001) relative to normal BMI. Obesity was associated with higher IL‐6 levels at 2 week (ratio = 1.36; p = 0.005) and 6 month (ratio = 1.47; p < 0.001) relative to normal BMI within “healthy.”ConclusionsObesity and comorbidity profiles are associated with an inflammatory response with the potential to impact recovery; consideration of systemic health at the time of injury could improve recovery.Keywords: clinical outcomes, comorbidities, inflammation, traumatic brain injury

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a national health concern, with up to 5 million evaluations of adults in United States emergency departments every year [1]. Furthermore, the risk of long‐term functional impairments and/or persistent symptoms following TBI is considerable. Over 50% of patients with TBI and presenting Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score between 13 and 15 (i.e., “mild” TBI [mTBI]) first evaluated at a level 1 trauma center experience functional limitations up to 5 years post‐injury [2, 3]. Globally, an estimated 5.7–7.1 million years of living with disability can be attributed to TBI [4]. Considerable evidence suggests that certain pre‐existing risk factors modify outcomes from mTBI, including biological sex, pre‐injury depression/anxiety, and migraine history [5, 6, 7]. Until recently, there has been a limited appreciation for how systemic health at the time of injury impacts acute response and recovery from mTBI.

Prior studies have shown that obesity is associated with significant elevations in baseline inflammation, detectable even in highly trained competitive athletes (compared to non‐obese athletes) [8]. Higher baseline inflammation predisposes patients with mTBI to larger acute inflammatory spikes, which can affect both short‐ and long‐term outcomes from the injury [9]. Acute elevations in hsCRP and IL‐6 are also predictors of worse 6‐month clinical outcomes following mTBI [10, 11]. Patients with mTBI and comorbid obesity have prolonged elevations in inflammatory markers, which can increase the risk for persistent symptoms, worse quality of life, and functional impairments [12, 13, 14]. Patients with mTBI and comorbid obesity also have prolonged elevations in inflammatory markers, which can increase the risk for persistent symptoms, worse quality of life, and functional impairments [12, 13, 14]. Prior work has shown significant acute elevations for TBI patients with obesity in cytokines like c‐reactive protein (CRP), interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), IL‐8, IL‐10, IL‐18 and IL‐1β compared with patients without obesity [12, 13, 15]. Cytokine concentrations within the first day of mTBI can moderate poorer cognitive performance at 2 weeks and 6‐months post‐injury in the patients with obesity [14]. This evidence has established obesity as a viable risk factor for worse TBI outcomes.

However, these studies have not considered the potential interaction effect between obesity and diagnosed obesity‐related medical conditions on recovery from mTBI. Many obesity‐related medical conditions are common sequelae or risk factors for worse outcomes after TBI (e.g., cardiovascular disease, diabetes, stroke, headache/migraine conditions and sleep disorders) [9]. These comorbidities are all associated with an increased inflammatory profile compared to obesity alone and this increase in inflammation could theoretically worsen outcomes from TBI [10, 16, 17]. While prior studies have examined the effect of individual comorbidities on mTBI outcomes [18], there is a paucity of evidence for how these conditions cluster together and impact outcomes following mTBI. This study examined the effect of: 1. comorbid medical condition profile; and 2. the interaction of obesity with comorbid medical condition profile on inflammatory blood biomarkers and clinical outcomes after mTBI.

Methods

Transforming Research and Clinical Knowledge in Traumatic Brain Injury (TRACK‐TBI) study participants were enrolled at 1 of 18 Level 1 U.S. trauma center emergency departments. Participants were included if they presented to the hospital within 24 h of external force trauma to the head, had a head computed tomography (CT) scan ordered by the treating physician, and met the American Congress of Rehabilitation Medicine's criteria for diagnosis of TBI [19]. Participants returned for follow‐up at 2 weeks and 6 months. Blood was obtained within 24 h, 2 weeks and 6 months post‐injury. Clinical outcome assessments and neurocognitive tests were performed at 2 weeks and 6 months post‐injury.

Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, incarceration, nonsurvivable physical trauma, debilitating pre‐injury mental health disorders (e.g., schizophrenia, bipolar disorder) or neurological disease (e.g., stroke, dementia), and non‐English‐ or Spanish primary language. Participants were categorized into body mass groups from height and weight data taken at hospital intake according to body mass index (BMI) guidelines [20]. Obese (BMI > 30.0) and normal weight participants (BMI = 18.5–24.9) at the time of injury were retained for analysis. From the original TRACK‐TBI dataset (n = 3092), we excluded participants age < 17 years (n = 145), those with a GCS score < 13 (indicating more severe brain injury; n = 716), those with incomplete medical history (n = 45), and those with missing BMI (n = 282). Those with either underweight (< 18.5) or overweight BMI (BMI = 25.0–29.9; n = 521) were excluded from analysis with clinical outcomes, but the average BMIs are reported for each latent class. In the TRACK‐TBI study, only those admitted to the hospital had documented BMI, so participants not admitted to the hospital were also excluded (n = 567). This resulted in an analytic dataset of 1337 participants for the comorbidity profile analysis and 816 for analyses including obese BMI. Participants or legally authorized representatives provided written informed consent to participate after being approached by a member of the research team in the hospital. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained from each participating institution prior to enrollment of human subjects.

Outcome Measures

Demographics and Injury Characteristics

A trained researcher collected relevant demographic, medical history (including history of cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, stroke, cancer, depression, anxiety, migraine, renal and pulmonary disease), and injury information including age, biological sex, race (white, Black, Asian, “Other” or unknown), Hispanic ethnicity (yes/no/unknown), years of education, injury cause (motor vehicle crash occupant, motor cycle collision [MCC], motor vehicle accident as a cyclist or pedestrian, fall, assault, other/unknown), highest level of care (ED only, admitted to hospital floor, admitted to intensive care unit), admission GCS, loss of consciousness (yes/no), post‐traumatic amnesia (yes/no), initial CT scan to detect acute intracranial abnormality (positive vs. negative) [21], prior treated TBI history (none, ED only, admitted to hospital), and insurance status (private, Medicaid, self‐pay, “other”).

Symptom Burden, Quality of Life, Functional Recovery

The Rivermead Post‐Concussion Questionnaire (RPQ) measures the severity of headaches, dizziness, and nausea as well as cognitive, mood, and sleep disturbances and other physical symptoms associated with concussion. Each item is rated on a scale of 0–4, with 0 indicating the symptom was not experienced at all and 4 indicating the symptom was a severe problem within the past 7 days, as compared with preinjury status [22]. The Quality of Life after Brain Injury Overall Scale (QOLIBRI‐OS) scale is a health‐related quality‐of‐life instrument used for TBI patients with six domains and an overall score [23]. The overall score was analyzed in this study, with scores ranging from 0 to 100. Lower scores indicate a worse quality of life. The Glasgow Outcome Scale‐Extended (GOSE) was used to assess functional outcomes specific to TBI [24]. Complete recovery was defined as a GOSE = 8; incomplete recovery was defined as GOSE < 8.

Cognitive Performance

The Trail Making Tests A (TMT‐A) and B (TMT‐B) measure processing speed and executive functioning, respectively [25]. Slower completion time (in seconds) represents worse performance. The Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) measures verbal episodic memory with immediate (range 0–75) and delayed recall (range 0–15) [26]. Higher scores indicate better recall. The Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (4th edition; WAIS‐IV) Processing Index measures psychomotor processing speed (scaled to a normative mean = 100, SD = 15) [27], where higher scores indicate better performance.

Inflammatory Blood Markers

Blood samples were collected on the day‐of‐injury and 2 weeks and 6 months post‐injury. Samples were processed and stored according to the Traumatic Brain Injury Common Data Elements Biospecimens and Biomarkers Working Group consensus recommendations for plasma and serum preparation [28]. Plasma and serum aliquots were prepared for each subject and frozen at −80°C for future analysis. All samples were deidentified using a unique study ID specific to site and subject, and batch‐shipped in temperature‐controlled overnight express freight containers to the TRACK‐TBI Biospecimens Repository at the University of Pittsburgh.

Blinded sample analysis of hsCRP was carried out by a single laboratory (University College Dublin) using the Abbott Architect c8000, MULTIGENT CRP Vario assay with the high‐sensitivity method (CRP16). Serum samples were thawed in batches at room temperature and centrifuged at 10,000 rfc for 10 min at 4°C before testing. Assays were performed in duplicate with a lower limit of quantification of 0.1 mg/L and a reportable range of 0.1–160.0 mg/L. Circulating plasma concentrations (pg/mL) of the cytokines IL‐6, IL‐10, and TNF‐α were measured by SiMoA (Quanterix, Billerica, MA) using the Cytokine 3‐plex A kit at University College Dublin. Temporal trends of these biomarkers were analyzed and reported.

Statistical Analysis

Muthén & Muthén Mplus Version 8.9, Los Angeles, CA was used to conduct a latent class analysis (LCA) using cardiovascular disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, cancer, migraine, renal disease, pulmonary disease, depression, and anxiety (coded 0 for no diagnosis and 1 for diagnosis) as indicator variables on the overall dataset (regardless of obesity status) [29]. There were no missing data among indicators in this analysis. LCA is an exploratory approach in which the researchers derive the optimal number of classes based on evidence from fit statistics and likelihood ratio tests as well as consideration of subgroup size and clinical utility [30].

The following information criteria: Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC [31]), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC [32]), Sample‐size Adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (SABIC [33]), and Consistent Akaike's Information Criterion (CAIC [31]) were considered when selecting models. Statistically, a better model fit is reflected in lower AIC, BIC, SABIC, and CAIC values. The Adjusted Lo‐Mendel‐Rubin Test (LMR [34]) and Bootstrapped Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT [35]) were also considered; a non‐significant LMR demonstrates a more parsimonious model, whereas a significant BLRT indicates a better fit than the previous K‐1 model. Entropy was also considered, which measures separation between profiles (higher entropy indicates more separation). For more detailed information regarding the above‐mentioned information criteria and likelihood ratio tests see Ferguson, Moore, & Hull (2020) [36]. Information criteria and likelihood ratio tests for up to 7 classes were investigated, considering a combination of fit statistics and practice considerations (e.g., size and conceptual distinctions between groups) to determine the most optimal configuration.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for the overall sample and by risk factor class and BMI group, with Kruskal‐Wallis and Fisher's exact tests used to test for statistically significant differences. A series of linear (if the outcome was continuous) or logistic (if the outcome was binary) regression models were built to estimate the effect of risk factor class and/or obesity on clinical outcomes at 2 week and 6 month post‐mTBI and inflammatory markers on day 1, 2 week and 6 month post‐mTBI. Fixed‐effect models were used to assess the effect of risk‐factor classification on outcome. Mixed‐effect models were used to assess the effect of obesity on outcome within each risk‐factor classification, which included an effect for the interaction of risk factor and obesity, and random subject intercepts using an unstructured correlation matrix. Age, sex, ethnicity, insurance, ICU status, CT (positive vs. negative) and TBI history were also included as covariates in all models. All p‐values were interpreted in the context of multiple comparisons using a 5% false discovery rate (FDR) per Benjamini Hochberg. A 2‐sided threshold of p < 0.05 post‐FDR correction was used to determine statistical significance. All regression modeling was carried out using SAS version 9.4 statistical software.

Results

Based on fit statistics and likelihood ratio tests (see Table S1), a 3‐class model was selected. Investigations into proportions of diagnoses across classes resulted in the labeling of the classes as Cardiovascular Risk Factors, Internalizing Risk Factors, and Healthy (see Figure 1). In the Cardiovascular Risk Factors class (N = 287; 21%), 100% of participants were diagnosed with cardiovascular disease, 94% were diagnosed with hypertension, 25% were diagnosed with hyperlipidemia, and 27% were diagnosed with diabetes. In the Internalizing Risk Factors class (N = 121; 9%), 70% of the participants were diagnosed with depression and 68% were diagnosed with anxiety. Lastly, the Healthy Class (N = 929; 69%) contains minimal risk factor diagnoses (ranging from 0% [hypertension] to 8% [pulmonary disease]).

Proportion of sample represented by individual pre‐injury medical conditions in the three‐class solution identified by latent class analysis.

Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Overall, 42.5% of the Cardiovascular Class (n = 122), 24.0% of the Internalizing Class (n = 29) and 23.9% of the Healthy Class (n = 222) had obesity. The average BMI of the Cardiovascular Class (29.8 ± 6.4) was significantly higher than that of the Internalizing (27.0 ± 5.9; p < 0.001) and Healthy Classes (27.0 ± 5.3; p < 0.001).

Comparing Comorbidity Classes on Clinical Outcomes 2 Weeks and 6 Months Post‐Injury

The effect of comorbidity classification on clinical outcomes is presented in Table 2. The Internalizing Class had higher mTBI symptom scores at 2 week (p = 0.003) and 6 month (p = 0.001) than Cardiovascular and Healthy Classes. The Cardiovascular Class had worse TMT‐B time at 6 month (96 s; p = 0.033) than the Internalizing Class (77 s) and Healthy Class (70 s); however, this result did not survive FDR correction. The Cardiovascular Class also had worse 6‐month WAIS‐PSI (99; p = 0.042) compared to the Internalizing Class (102) and Healthy Class (103), but this comparison did not survive FDR correction. The Healthy Class had higher rates of better quality of life at 2 week (54%; p = 0.003) than Cardiovascular (45%) and Internalizing Classes (31%) and at 6 month (74%; p < 0.001) compared to Cardiovascular (58%) and Internalizing Classes (53%). The Healthy Class had higher rates of complete functional recovery at 6 month (31%; p = 0.001) than Cardiovascular (26%) and Internalizing Classes (15%). Healthy and Cardiovascular Classes had higher rates of symptom recovery at 6 month (41%–45%; p < 0.001) compared to the Internalizing Class (17%). No differences were observed between classes of CRP, TNFα, IL‐6, or IL‐10 at any timepoint.

Interaction of Obesity With Comorbidity Classification on Clinical Outcomes

The interaction effect of obesity with comorbidity classification on clinical outcomes after mTBI is presented in Table 3. There were no significant differences in clinical outcomes within risk factor classes for participants with mTBI and obesity in comparison with participants with mTBI and normal BMI. Within the Healthy Class, obesity was associated with increased CRP on day 1 (ratio = 1.86; p < 0.001), 2 week (ratio = 2.05; p < 0.001) and 6 month (ratio = 2.34; p < 0.001) relative to those with normal BMI. Obesity was also associated with increased IL‐6 levels at 2 week (ratio = 1.36; p = 0.005) and 6 month (ratio = 1.47; p < 0.001) relative to those with normal BMI within the Healthy Class. Within the Internalizing Class, obesity was associated with increased CRP on day 1 (ratio = 2.43; p = 0.011), 2 week (ratio = 4.09; p < 0.001) and 6 month (ratio = 1.69; p = 0.045) relative to those with normal BMI; however, only the 2‐week timepoint remained statistically significant after FDR correction.

Discussion

In this national cohort of participants with mTBI and comorbid obesity, significant increases in hsCRP in the acute, subacute and chronic phases of mTBI recovery were noted, even in the obese patients with low self‐reported rates of medical comorbidities (i.e., the Healthy Class). Increases in IL‐6 were also noted in the subacute and chronic phases of recovery in this group. These results demonstrate the systemic effect that obesity at the time of injury can play in post‐mTBI inflammatory response (e.g., even in the absence of pro‐inflammatory comorbidities), which prior research suggests could be a risk factor for poorer clinical recovery [10]. Clinicians treating the patients with mTBI and comorbid obesity should understand that comorbidity profiles are associated with inflammatory response to the injury with the potential to impact mTBI recovery. Comorbidity classes were associated with differences in clinical outcomes such as cognitive function, symptoms and functional outcomes; interestingly, obesity did not interact with comorbidity classes to change these outcomes.

The co‐occurrence of obesity and cardiovascular diseases is known to result in higher inflammatory profiles than obesity or cardiovascular disease in isolation [37]. It was therefore expected that obesity would augment the inflammatory response in patients with mTBI and high rates of cardiovascular disease. One possible explanation for the absence of a significant effect is the lack of current medication data in this study. Several medications for cardiovascular diseases have known anti‐inflammatory effects for hsCRP, IL‐6, and TNFα [38, 39], which could have blunted the inflammatory response to mTBI. Another possibility is that the analyses were underpowered to detect real differences in the participants with obesity, as trends were observed for higher hsCRP in the Cardiovascular Class with obesity on the day‐of‐injury and 6 months post‐injury, but either did not reach the significance threshold or did not survive false discovery corrections (p = 0.033–0.078; Table 3). Future studies with larger samples of patients with cardiovascular diseases and documented medications could shed more light on this population.

Internalizing disorders, such as depression and/or anxiety, have a known pro‐inflammatory phenotype with several meta‐analyses noting higher hsCRP and IL‐6 levels in patients with these conditions compared to those without [40, 41]. Obesity augments the inflammatory profile of patients with depression and/or anxiety; [17, 41] some studies have even suggested that CRP/IL‐6 levels may partially mediate a clinical pathway between obesity and depressive symptoms [16]. This is relevant to the current study because both obesity and internalizing disorders are observed at high rates in patients with TBI [13, 42]. In the present study, TBI participants with high rates of pre‐injury internalizing disorders and comorbid obesity had hsCRP values which were over 4 times higher at 2 weeks post‐injury than those without obesity (Table 3). hsCRP was also elevated for mTBI patients with obesity in this class on the day‐of‐injury (p = 0.011) and 6 months post‐injury (p = 0.045), but this result did not remain significant after false discovery correction. It is possible, for participants in both the internalizing and cardiovascular classes, that a ceiling effect was observed for hsCRP and IL‐6 following trauma due to the higher baseline levels associated with these comorbidities. Prior studies have suggested that both hsCRP and IL‐6 can reach a saturation point in participants with obesity‐ and/or comorbidity‐related baseline inflammation, where post‐traumatic levels can plateau despite increasing injury severity [43, 44, 45]. Future research will be needed to understand the interplay between pre‐injury, pro‐inflammatory conditions and post‐traumatic response from mTBI.

Significant differences were noted between comorbidity classes in several clinical outcomes. Cardiovascular Class members had poorer cognition in several domains compared to the other classes, while Internalizing Class members had higher symptom scores compared to other classes. Cardiovascular diseases can negatively impact cognitive performance through a variety of mechanisms (e.g., reduced blood flow to brain, small vessel damage, atrial fibrillation, blood‐brain barrier disruption, and systemic inflammation) [46, 47, 48], depending on the specific disease. The literature has demonstrated a clear relationship between internalizing disorders and higher symptom burden after mTBI [49, 50]. Obesity did not interact with comorbidity class to worsen these outcomes after correction for multiple comparisons; we may have been underpowered to detect differences between groups. Without longitudinal health information on participants, it is impossible to delineate how long the patient had obesity, when the patient was diagnosed with comorbidities, and how well‐controlled these comorbidities were by pharmacologic or other therapies. Future studies will be necessary to determine the mechanisms linking obesity‐induced comorbidities, obesity‐induced inflammation and clinical recovery from mTBI.

There are clinical implications for this work within the growing evidence base supporting obesity as a risk factor for worse outcomes after TBI. This study, in combination with previous work [12, 13, 14], provides initial support that understanding a TBI patient's comorbidity burden (including obesity) is a critical aspect of the patient's baseline health and inflammatory status, which can impact recovery [18]. Given the clear inflammatory link between obesity and TBI [11, 51] targeting a reduction in circulatory inflammation may be a worthwhile therapeutic target. The treatment effect of anti‐inflammatory pharmacologic and non‐pharmacologic treatments on recovery from TBI in the patients with obesity has not yet been studied. For example, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonist trials have demonstrated a large reduction in inflammatory markers associated with obesity, such as CRP, in human and animal studies [9]. Aerobic exercise programs have also shown efficacy in reducing inflammatory profile in obese patients and improving recovery of patients with TBI [52, 53]. Future work should consider the therapeutic potential of such interventions.

The results of this study are generalizable to adults presenting to U.S. Level 1 trauma centers with mTBI, who present with GCS of 13–15, and who are obese or normal weight. History of diagnosed medical conditions was self‐reported, which may increase the risk of bias. It is possible that those in the Healthy Class had certain comorbidities and were unaware of the diagnoses. BMI was only documented in mTBI patients who were admitted to the hospital, resulting in missing data for those who were discharged directly from the ED. Obesity has known limitations in populations with greater muscle mass. However, recent research in general U.S. populations, such as the cohort analyzed here, suggests that obesity and excess adiposity are in agreement for 98.4% of U.S. adults [54]. Future studies should delineate if increases in certain inflammatory biomarkers (such as hsCRP or IL‐6) are causally associated with clinical outcomes in the patients with obesity.

Conclusion

In this latent class analysis of 1337 patients with mTBI, medical comorbidities clustered into Cardiovascular, Internalizing, and Healthy (i.e., low rate of self‐reported medical conditions) Classes. Obesity was associated with Cardiovascular Class membership, but internalizing and healthy classes had an average overweight BMI at the time of injury. Participants with obesity in the Healthy Class had significantly worse hsCRP and IL‐6 throughout recovery than participants without obesity in the Healthy Class. IL‐6 was also elevated for participants with obesity in the Internalizing Class at 2 week post‐injury compared with those without obesity in the Internalizing Class. Clinicians treating patients with TBI and comorbid obesity should consider obesity‐related comorbidities and the effect these conditions may have on post‐injury inflammatory profile and/or TBI recovery. Understanding the TBI patient's systemic health, in general, may lead to more precise treatment recommendations with a higher likelihood of improved recovery trajectory.
