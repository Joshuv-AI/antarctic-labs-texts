Evaluation of GCF levels of leucine-rich alpha-2 glycoprotein and TNF-α in stage III periodontitis patients: A case–control study
Authors: GK Chetana, Umesh Yadalam, K Vijay Raghava, Divya Alexander, Manjusha Nambiar, Nomitha Prakash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriodontitis results from interactions between oral biofilms and the host immune response, with acute-phase proteins indicating disease presence. This study measured leucine-rich alpha-2 glycoprotein (LRG) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels before and after full-mouth disinfection. Their levels were correlated with clinical parameters.Materials and methodsThis prospective controlled clinical study was conducted between June 2022 and June 2023 at the Department of Periodontology and Implantology, Sri Rajiv Gandhi College of Dental Sciences & Hospital, Bengaluru. Thirty systematically healthy adults (15 healthy controls and 15 with stage III periodontitis) were enrolled. Clinical parameters (PD, CAL, GI, PI, and BOP) were recorded. Gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) was collected. Periodontitis patients received full-mouth disinfection with a chlorhexidine protocol. GCF was re-sampled after six weeks. LRG and TNF-α levels were analyzed using ELISA. Spearman's rank correlation assessed associations between biomarkers and clinical parameters (p < 0.05).ResultsAt baseline, clinical parameters and GCF levels of LRG and TNF-α were significantly higher in stage III periodontitis patients than controls. Both biomarkers correlated with indices at baseline and post-treatment. Six weeks after disinfection, PI, GI, PD, CAL, LRG, and TNF-α levels were significantly reduced (p < 0.05). Exploratory regression analysis identified significant associations between periodontal parameters and GCF biomarker levels.ConclusionPatients with stage III periodontitis exhibited elevated GCF levels of LRG and TNF-α. Full-mouth disinfection significantly reduced their expression. This suggests LRG merits further study as an adjunctive marker of periodontal inflammation. The exploratory design and small sample size preclude conclusions about applicability.Keywords: Acute-phase proteins, Biomarkers, Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, Host–pathogen interaction, Probing depth, Serum protein, Clinical attachment level

Introduction

Periodontitis, a chronic inflammatory disease, is caused by interactions between oral biofilms and the host immune response, leading to progressive destruction of tooth-supporting tissues.1 An exaggerated immune reaction to bacterial challenge triggers the release of inflammatory mediators, such as cytokines and acute-phase proteins, resulting in local and systemic tissue destruction.2, 3, 4 Acute-phase proteins, produced in response to cytokine activity during inflammation, constitute part of the early immune response and reflect its presence and severity.5,6 Their concentrations in biological fluids often parallel disease activity and healing capacity, making them valuable diagnostic and prognostic markers in inflammatory disorders such as periodontitis.6

Leucine-rich alpha-2 glycoprotein (LRG) is a 50 kDa acute-phase protein containing leucine-rich repeats, involved in angiogenesis, cell differentiation, and immune signaling, although its exact function remains unclear.7 It is produced primarily by hepatocytes and neutrophils and is also expressed in inflammatory periodontal lesions.8 Elevated LRG levels have been reported in systemic inflammatory diseases such as asthma, myocardial infarction, and rheumatoid arthritis,9 as well as in the saliva of patients with chronic periodontitis or diabetes mellitus.10 Experimental studies suggest that LRG may contribute to bone destruction via the interleukin-6 (IL-6) pathway, linking it to periodontal bone resorption.11

Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) is a cytokine that contributes to periodontal disease by promoting the release of inflammatory mediators and osteoclastogenesis, leading to tissue destruction.3 Gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) from the gingival sulcus reflects local inflammation and is useful for monitoring disease status and treatment outcomes.9 It contains cytokines, enzymes, antibodies, and acute-phase proteins. Although previous studies have reported elevated LRG levels in periodontal disease, evidence regarding its site-specific expression in gingival crevicular fluid and its response following periodontal therapy remains limited.7

Furthermore, limited evidence exists regarding the relationship between GCF LRG, TNF-α, and clinical periodontal parameters before and after periodontal treatment. Since TNF-α is a key pro-inflammatory cytokine involved in periodontal tissue destruction and is known to influence LRG expression during inflammatory responses3,10, simultaneous evaluation of these biomarkers may provide a better understanding of the local inflammatory changes associated with periodontal disease. While TNF-α primarily reflects pro-inflammatory cytokine activity, LRG may provide complementary information on the host acute-phase inflammatory response. Therefore, combined assessment of these biomarkers may improve the evaluation of periodontal disease activity and treatment response, with LRG serving as a potential adjunctive biomarker of periodontal inflammation.7

Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate and compare GCF levels of LRG and TNF-α between periodontally healthy individuals and patients with stage III periodontitis and to assess their changes following full-mouth disinfection. The secondary objective was to determine the association of these biomarkers with clinical periodontal parameters. We hypothesized that patients with stage III periodontitis would exhibit higher GCF levels of LRG and TNF-α than healthy individuals and that these levels would decrease following periodontal therapy.

Materials and methods

Study design and participant selection

This one-year prospective controlled clinical study included 30 systemically healthy individuals, comprising 15 periodontally healthy participants and 15 patients diagnosed with stage III periodontitis. The study involved a baseline comparison between the healthy and periodontitis groups, followed by an evaluation of the periodontitis group six weeks after full-mouth disinfection.

Data were collected between June 2022 and June 2023. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Sri Rajiv Gandhi College of Dental Sciences and Hospital, Bengaluru, India (Approval No: SRGCDS/2022/335). Participants were recruited from the Department of Periodontology and Implantology of the college.

The sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power v3.1.9.4, based on Keles Yucel et al.7 with an effect size of 0.80, α = 0.05, and 80% power, yielding a requirement of 15 subjects per group. The duration of the study extended over one year due to the limited number of participants meeting the strict eligibility criteria.

All clinical examinations and GCF sample collections were performed by a single trained examiner using standardized instruments and protocols to ensure consistency in clinical measurements. Groups were age- and sex-matched to minimize potential bias. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion. Each participant voluntarily agreed to undergo the procedures described in the study, including clinical examinations and gingival crevicular fluid collection.

Eligibility criteria

Group 1 (healthy controls) comprised adults aged 25–50 years with ≥20 natural teeth (excluding third molars), no clinical attachment loss, probing depth ≤3 mm, bleeding on probing <10%, and no history of periodontitis. Group 2 (stage III periodontitis) included adults meeting the same age and tooth criteria but with clinical attachment loss ≥5 mm, probing depth ≥6 mm, radiographic bone loss to the middle third of the root, and ≤4 teeth lost to periodontitis. Based on the 2017 classification guidelines outlined in the document, stage III periodontitis is characterized by significant damage to the periodontal attachment apparatus, with clinical attachment loss (CAL) of ≥5 mm at the site of greatest loss.12 This stage often involves tooth loss due to periodontitis (≤4 teeth) and is complicated by factors such as probing depths ≥6 mm, vertical bone loss ≥3 mm, and Class II or III furcation involvement. Despite the potential for tooth loss, masticatory function is generally preserved, and complex rehabilitation is not yet required. The management of stage III periodontitis typically involves advanced therapeutic approaches to control disease progression and prevent further tooth loss.12 Exclusion criteria for both groups were systemic illness, recent antibiotic or anti-inflammatory use, periodontal therapy in the past six months, pregnancy or lactation, and tobacco use. The study was matched for gender to ensure balanced groups. Socio-economic and academic status were not formal matching criteria, but all participants were drawn from the same geographic area and institution, which helped reduce variation related to these factors.

Clinical examination

Clinical parameters, namely, probing depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), gingival index (GI), plaque index (PI), and bleeding on probing (BOP) were recorded at six sites per tooth using a UNC-15 probe, examining each tooth at six points around its circumference to measure gum inflammation, plaque accumulation, bleeding, and pocket depth, providing a comprehensive evaluation of periodontal health. Plaque Index (PI, Silness and Löe, 1964) assessed the thickness of plaque along the gingival margin. Gingival Index (GI, Löe and Silness, 1963) measured gingival inflammation based on color, consistency, and bleeding response.2 Bleeding on Probing (BOP, Ainamo and Bay, 1975) indicated bleeding presence within 15 s of gentle probing.13 Probing Depth (PD) was the distance from the gingival margin to the pocket base, and Clinical Attachment Level (CAL) represented the distance from the cementoenamel junction to the pocket base, showing connective tissue loss.1 The periodontal probe used was a UNC 15 model (Deppeler SA, Switzerland). GCF (gingival crevicular fluid) cytokine levels (LRG and TNF-α) were quantified using commercially available sandwich ELISA kits (Human TNF-α Quantikine ELISA Kit, R&D Systems, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Gingival crevicular fluid was collected with microcapillary pipettes from the deepest site in each subject; in healthy controls, non-inflamed sites were selected. On average, six sites per tooth were assessed, and gingival crevicular fluid for biomarker analysis was collected from 15 sites with the deepest probing depth before and after treatment. Sampling was performed one day after the clinical examination to avoid blood contamination. After isolation and gentle supragingival plaque removal, 3 μL of GCF was collected, transferred to sterile vials, and stored at −80 °C.

Intervention

Group 2 (stage III periodontitis) underwent full-mouth disinfection, which included ultrasonic scaling and root planing in two sessions within 24 h, tongue cleaning, and a chlorhexidine (CHX) regimen: 1% CHX gel applied to the tongue for 1 min, 0.2% CHX rinse twice daily for two weeks, subgingival irrigation with 1% CHX gel repeated after eight days, and 0.2% CHX tonsil spray. Group 1 received no therapy. In Group 2, periodontal parameters and GCF resampling was performed at the end of six weeks of the prescribed mouthwash, spray, and tongue-cleaning regimen.

Biochemical analysis

Each GCF sample was diluted with 300 μL phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4), vortexed, and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 min at +4 °C. Supernatants were analyzed using ELISA kits for human LRG and TNF-α. Samples and standards were added to antibody-coated wells, incubated, treated with chromogen, and the absorbance was read at 450 nm. Concentrations were determined from kit-generated standard curves.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Mann–Whitney U test was applied to compare continuous variables between groups, and the Chi-square test was used for categorical variables. Within-group comparisons (pre- and post-FMD) were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Spearman's rank correlation was employed to assess associations between GCF biomarker levels and clinical parameters. Stepwise multiple linear regression was used to examine associations between clinical periodontal parameters and GCF biomarker levels. Given the sample size, these regression models were regarded as exploratory. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline demographic characteristics of study participants are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Both groups were comparable in age and gender distribution (p > 0.05). The mean age was 35.20 ± 4.49 years in the healthy control group and 38.80 ± 8.22 years in the stage III periodontitis group (p = 0.15). All participants were systemically healthy, nonsmokers, and had at least 20 natural teeth.

Clinical parameters

The clinical parameters are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. At baseline, the periodontitis group showed significantly higher scores for all clinical parameters. Following non-surgical periodontal therapy, Group 2 exhibited marked improvements in all parameters. At the sampling sites, PD and CAL also showed significant reductions (from 5.48 to 2.80 mm and from 5.77 to 2.91 mm, respectively; p < 0.001), indicating substantial clinical recovery post-treatment.

GCF biomarkers

As shown in Table 1, baseline GCF levels of LRG and TNF-α were significantly higher in Group 2 (662.07 ± 14.29 ng/mL and 28.19 ± 6.20 pg/mL, respectively) than in Group 1 (639.57 ± 21.17 ng/mL and 14.90 ± 5.05 pg/mL), with p = 0.002 and < 0.001, respectively. Post-treatment, both biomarkers demonstrated a significant decline in Group 2, LRG decreased to 646.65 ± 9.02 ng/mL and TNF-α to 19.28 ± 5.54 pg/mL (p < 0.001), reflecting biochemical improvement consistent with clinical healing.

Correlation analysis

Table 2 shows that all correlations were statistically significant (p < 0.05). At baseline, LRG demonstrated strong positive correlations (rho = 0.59–0.67) with PI (0.64), GI (0.62), BOP (0.59), PD (0.67), CAL (0.66), and sampling site PD and CAL (0.65 each). TNF-α showed similar strong correlations (rho = 0.67–0.75) with the same clinical parameters. Post-treatment, correlations remained strong but were slightly lower (rho = 0.54–0.70), indicating consistent associations between clinical periodontal measures and GCF inflammatory markers before and after therapy.

Regression analysis

Exploratory stepwise regression analysis (Table 3) revealed that at baseline, PD significantly predicted GCF LRG levels (β = 19.742, p = 0.001), explaining 53% of the variance (R2 = 0.53). For GCF TNF-α, clinical attachment level at the sampling site (Site CAL, β = 20.915, p = 0.001) and PD (β = 10.220, p = 0.02) were significant predictors, accounting for 65% of the variance (R2 = 0.65). Post-treatment, Site CAL was a significant negative predictor for both GCF LRG (β = −6.666, p = 0.003, R2 = 0.46) and TNF-α (β = −10.000, p < 0.001, R2 = 0.69).

Discussion

Periodontitis is an inflammatory disease characterized by activation of the host immune–inflammatory response, leading to tissue destruction and influencing treatment outcomes. Conventional clinical measures such as PD, CAL, GI, BOP, PI, and radiographs provide only a static assessment and fail to reflect real-time disease activity or predict future progression, often resulting in delayed diagnosis.3,8,9 Identifying sensitive and clinically relevant biomarkers is therefore critical for early detection and monitoring of periodontal disease. In this study, the control group established baseline levels of leucine-rich alpha-2 glycoprotein and tumor necrosis factor-alpha in periodontal health. Although both are established inflammatory markers, evidence regarding their site-specific expression within the periodontal microenvironment is limited.7 This study aimed to evaluate their local and dynamic changes in gingival crevicular fluid following full-mouth disinfection and to determine whether LRG has the potential to serve as a site-specific biomarker for periodontal inflammation and therapeutic response.

Patients with stage III periodontitis (Group 2) exhibited significantly higher baseline values for PI (1.82 ± 0.41), GI (1.48 ± 0.55), BOP (96.07 ± 11.11%), PD (4.08 ± 0.55 mm), and CAL (4.68 ± 0.98 mm) compared with healthy controls. Correspondingly, GCF levels of LRG (662.07 ± 14.29 ng/mL) and TNF-α (28.19 ± 6.20 pg/mL) were markedly elevated, reflecting heightened local inflammation. Following non-surgical periodontal therapy, Group 2 showed substantial clinical improvement, with reductions in PI (0.54 ± 0.26), GI (0.52 ± 0.26), PD (2.37 ± 0.78 mm), and CAL (2.71 ± 1.09 mm), accompanied by significant declines in LRG (646.65 ± 9.02 ng/mL) and TNF-α (19.28 ± 5.54 pg/mL) (all p < 0.001). These findings indicate a strong correspondence between clinical recovery and biochemical improvement. The healthy control group provided baseline reference values, enabling distinction between physiological and disease-associated elevations in LRG and TNF-α.7

LRG responds directly to TNF-α stimulation, acting as a downstream effector in inflammatory cascades.14 Its levels rise in chronic inflammatory conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis,15 meningitis,14 inflammatory bowel disease.15 These findings suggest LRG may serve as a systemic inflammatory biomarker, a role potentially applicable to chronic periodontitis. The established association between rheumatoid arthritis and periodontitis further supports the hypothesis that LRG may also serve as a potential biomarker in periodontal disease.16

Over the past two decades, GCF has gained recognition as a reliable diagnostic medium in periodontal disease pathogenesis. A recent systematic review further highlighted its utility in staging disease severity.17 Rich in bioindicators, GCF reflects the biochemical status of periodontal tissues. Its diagnostic value is supported by demonstrated associations between protease activity, collagenase levels, and pocket depth.18 This makes it particularly suitable for biomarker analysis in the present study. Since TNF-α directly stimulates LRG production, measuring both markers in GCF provides a clearer understanding of their interplay during the development and treatment of periodontitis.14,19

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay is the most established and reliable technique for biofluid analysis in periodontal research.9 Keles Yucel et al.7 reported increased LRG expression in stage III periodontitis both locally and systemically, with non-surgical therapy significantly reducing GCF and serum LRG levels as measured by ELISA. Similarly, Kurtiş et al.19 and Reis et al.20 analyzed GCF TNF-α using ELISA, supporting its validity for biomarker assessment. In this study, the periodontitis group demonstrated significantly higher baseline PD, CAL, GI, PI, and BOP values at both full-mouth and sampling sites compared with controls (p < 0.05). These results are consistent with Keles Yucel et al.,7 and with Özer et al.21 The elevated levels of LRG and TNF-α at baseline reflect active inflammation, as inflammatory processes are known to upregulate acute-phase proteins such as LRG and cytokines such as TNF-α.14,19 Together, these findings highlight ELISA-based GCF analysis as a sensitive and effective approach for assessing local disease severity.20,22

Baseline GCF TNF-α levels in this study were consistent with previous reports, showing elevated concentrations in chronic periodontitis compared with healthy controls.22 Following full-mouth disinfection, the periodontitis group demonstrated significant clinical improvement at 6 weeks, with reductions across all parameters. Similar clinical outcomes were reported by Apatzidou et al.23 and Knöfler et al.24 at 6 months. GCF LRG levels also declined significantly from baseline to 6 weeks (p < 0.001), corroborating earlier findings after scaling and root planing.7

Although the reduction in GCF LRG levels was statistically significant, the absolute magnitude of change was relatively modest. Therefore, the clinical significance of this reduction remains uncertain and should be interpreted with caution. Larger longitudinal studies are required to determine whether these changes represent clinically meaningful improvements and to establish diagnostic thresholds for the use of LRG in periodontal disease monitoring.

TNF-α contributes to inflammation and tissue damage in periodontitis, and its levels are associated with disease severity.19 TNF-α levels fall after treatment, in parallel with reduced inflammation and clinical healing.22 LRG falls in the same way, indicating a reduced acute-phase response. Together, these findings suggest that both biomarkers may reflect disease activity and treatment response in periodontitis.20

GCF TNF-α levels decreased significantly after treatment, consistent with reports of reductions in both local and systemic measurements following non-surgical periodontal therapy.22 Sampling sites also showed significant CAL gain and PD reduction from baseline to six weeks,7 paralleling clinical improvement. A significant positive correlation was observed between GCF LRG, TNF-α, and clinical parameters (PD, CAL, GI, PI) both before and after treatment,22 supporting their role as indicators of disease activity. Although some studies reported no significant association between GCF TNF-α and specific stages of chronic periodontitis,25 the positive correlation between LRG and TNF-α in this study aligns with earlier findings,7 suggesting that TNF-α may regulate LRG expression in periodontitis.

Quantitatively, at baseline, each 1 mm increase in PD corresponded to a 19.742-unit rise in GCF LRG, while a 1 mm increase in CAL and PD was associated with increases of 20.915 and 10.220 units in GCF TNF-α, respectively. These relationships highlight the tight link between tissue destruction and the inflammatory response in periodontitis.19 Following treatment, at the six-week follow-up, a 1 mm gain in CAL corresponded to decreases of 6.666 units in GCF LRG and 10 units in GCF TNF-α, reflecting resolution of inflammation and clinical healing.22 Collectively, these findings suggest that both LRG and TNF-α are potential biomarkers associated with periodontal disease activity and treatment response. Their combined assessment may provide complementary information regarding periodontal inflammation and treatment response; however, larger longitudinal studies are required before their clinical utility in periodontal diagnosis and monitoring can be established.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The study's strengths include the intensive analysis of GCF samples, enabling accurate evaluation of periodontal inflammation. Simultaneous measurement of LRG and TNF-α offered insights into their relationship with disease progression and treatment response. The use of ELISA enhanced the reliability of biomarker detection, while the inclusion of clinical parameters alongside biomarker levels provided a comprehensive understanding of periodontal changes.

However, the study was limited by its relatively small sample size and short six-week follow-up period, which restricted the assessment of long-term changes in biomarker levels and treatment outcomes. Therefore, the clinical applicability of LRG and TNF-α should be interpreted with caution until further studies with larger populations and longer follow-up periods are conducted.

Furthermore, as the full-mouth disinfection protocol included adjunctive chlorhexidine use, the individual contributions of mechanical debridement and chlorhexidine to the observed reductions in LRG and TNF-α could not be distinguished. Additionally, the exclusion of patients with systemic inflammatory conditions may limit the generalizability of these findings to broader populations. Furthermore, although all clinical measurements were performed by a single trained examiner using standardized procedures, formal examiner calibration and intra-examiner reliability assessment were not performed, which may have introduced measurement variability.

Conclusion

Within the limits of this study, GCF levels of LRG and TNF-α were elevated in patients with stage III periodontitis and decreased significantly six weeks after full-mouth disinfection. Both biomarkers correlated with clinical periodontal parameters before and after therapy. These findings are exploratory and are based on a small cohort with a short follow-up period, so they indicate only that LRG merits further study as a possible adjunctive marker of periodontal inflammation alongside TNF-α. No conclusion can yet be drawn about its clinical applicability. Larger longitudinal studies, including patients with systemic inflammatory conditions, are needed to establish reference values and diagnostic thresholds and to determine whether GCF LRG has any role in periodontal diagnosis or monitoring.
