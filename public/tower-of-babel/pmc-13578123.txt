Contour‐regulated registration framework for Liver CT perfusion images
Authors: Zhan Xu, Yao Zhao, Xinru Chen, Jinyong Lin, Roya Barati, Chaan S Ng, Jinzhong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiver computed tomography perfusion (CTp) imaging enables quantitative assessment of vascular dynamics but is highly susceptible to motion‐induced artifacts arising from breath‐hold variability and involuntary patient motion. These effects cause different portions of the liver to fall within the imaging field of view across time frames and introduce in‐plane deformation, creating substantial challenges for image registration. Misregistration between distinct regions of interest disrupts temporal signal consistency and perfusion estimation, underscoring the need for robust registration strategies tailored for Liver CTp.PurposeTo develop and evaluate a contour‐regulated automated registration framework for correcting motion artifacts and aligning time‐resolved images in Liver CTp series.MethodsForty‐six CTp datasets from 23 patients were analyzed; each dataset consisted of a pre‐contrast static scan, a post‐contrast high temporal resolution cine scan (59–79 time frames), and a post‐contrast low temporal resolution helical scan (8–10 time frames). A 2D liver auto‐segmentation nnU‐Net model was trained on 23 whole liver contours that had been manually delineated on pre‐contrast static images and applied to all 46 cine and helical series. The auto‐segmented contours were used to regulate a two‐stage registration pipeline consisting of affine registration followed by TransMorph‐based deformable registration. The models with different parameters were trained by using intensity‐corrected images on 36 randomly selected cases and tested on the remaining 10 cases. Performance of multiple registration model variants were evaluated against the unregistered series by using mean surface distance (MSD) and 95th percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95) on simplified 3D surfaces reconstructed from manually placed anchor points.ResultsLiver auto‐segmentation achieved mean Dice similarity coefficients of 0.92 for helical scans and 0.89 for cine scans. Structure alignment was improved significantly compared with raw data after registration. The best‐performing model had an auto‐determined contour regularization weighting factor (λS) of 0 or 0.8, reduced the MSD from 5.1 mm in the unregistered data to 1.0 mm and HD95 from 9.7 mm to 3.0 mm in cine scans. In helical scans, MSD decreased from 3.2 mm to 1.0 mm and HD95 from 6.6 mm to 2.9 mm.ConclusionsThe proposed contour‐regulated, two‐stage registration framework improved temporal alignment in dynamic Liver CTp images, providing a robust foundation for subsequent analysis and clinical assessment.Keywords: CT perfusion, deformable, liver registration

INTRODUCTION

Liver cancer remains one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies worldwide. 1 Despite advances in surgical resection, locoregional therapy, and systemic treatments, patient prognosis continues to depend heavily on accurate diagnosis, treatment stratification, and longitudinal monitoring of therapeutic response. Computed tomography perfusion (CTp) has emerged as a promising imaging modality that can provide dynamic information on tissue vascularization and perfusion parameters. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 By capturing time‐resolved volumetric images after contrast administration, CTp can characterize liver tumor hemodynamics and enable quantitative assessment of changes in vascular supply between tissues. However, the clinical value of Liver CTp depends on the accurate alignment of a sequence of rapidly acquired images centered over the tumorous region, which are often compromised by patient motion, inconsistent breath‐holding, and irregular abdominal organ motion, all of which introduce intensity‐based artifacts and spatial inconsistencies.

Typical CTp protocols 5 , 6 for liver imaging include a high temporal resolution cine scan series with 59–79 time frames acquired during a 30 second breath‐hold which is limited by a narrow craniocaudal coverage of approximately 4 cm. This is supplemented by a sequential low temporal resolution helical scan series with 8–10 intermittent short breath‐hold acquisitions over about 10 min, covering up to 10 cm. The limited craniocaudal coverage of the cine scan captures only a fraction of the liver, and uncertainty in breath‐hold position can lead to misalignment of the liver across time frames, such that different liver regions are captured within the imaging field of view. This combination introduces substantial registration challenges that conventional algorithms are unable to fully address. In CTp studies, image registration typically assumes that the fixed and moving images represent the same region of interest (ROI) and iteratively updates a deformation field to maximize voxelwise similarity. 7 When distinct ROIs appear in the same location and then are mis‐registered, however, the resulting errors lead to disrupted temporal signals, inaccurate perfusion maps, and ultimately misinterpretation of post‐contrast liver hemodynamics. Therefore, developing robust and reliable registration strategies for Liver CTp is both a technical necessity and a clinical imperative.

Over the past decade, several registration algorithms have been explored for dynamic CT imaging. Rigid 8 and affine transformations 9 can correct global shifts but are insufficient to address localized deformations of abdominal organs during respiration. Local deformable registration methods, including B‐spline 10 , 11 , optical‐flow 12 , 13 , and diffeomorphic approaches 14 , 15 , can capture more complex motion that could be modeled with smooth, continuous, and topology‐preserving deformations but might break down over ROI mismatches and introduce unrealistic deformation with forced similarity. Recently, deep learning‐based deformable image registration has achieved wide popularity for its reduced computation time and improved robustness. Convolutional neural network–based registration has been a major focus in the field, and both supervised 16 and unsupervised 17 algorithms have been developed based on a U‐Net 18 framework. To consider the long‐range relationship between voxels and regions that are spatially distant from each other, Vision‐Transformer–based models have been developed to consider both local and global similarities between the fixed image and moving image. 19 , 20

Here we present a deep learning–based registration framework specifically tailored for CT imaging of contrast‐agent perfusion in the liver. Specifically, we first constructed a 2D auto‐segmentation model based on an nnU‐Net 21 framework to contour the liver at each time frame of the image series. Then we carried out an affine registration to align the craniocaudal offset, and constructed a contour‐regulated deformable registration framework subsequently for transformation in finer scale.

METHODS AND MATERIALS

Dataset

This retrospective analysis of de‐identified patient CTp scans was approved by the institutional review board of The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center (Protocol # PA12‐0589). A total of 46 CTp datasets from 23 patients were included in this study, with each patient contributing 1 to 3 datasets acquired on different days. Each CTp dataset included three axial scans: A pre‐contrast static scan covering the entire liver with craniocaudal extent of 20–30 cm, a post‐contrast high temporal resolution cine scan with a narrow extent of 4 cm, followed by low temporal resolution helical scan with broader extent of 10 cm. Auto segmentation model

The segmentation model was trained using raw CT images. Liver, heart, bilateral kidneys, pancreas, spleen, and stomach were manually delineated on the pre‐contrast scans of 23 datasets from 23 patients. Compared with the full craniocaudal coverage in the pre‐contrast scans, the cine image volume had a much narrower coverage, providing very limited information along the craniocaudal direction. Therefore, a 2D nnU‐Net model was trained using default configuration for liver auto‐segmentation. 21 Model training and testing were performed on a GPU computing cluster. The segmentation model was trained for 200 epochs, using the Adam optimizer 22 with a learning rate of 10−4.

The auto‐segmentation model was tested on both cine and helical images from the remaining 23 cases, corresponding to 21 patients, as two patients were scanned once and two others were scanned three times. The results were evaluated separately. Segmentation model performance was evaluated in two ways: By inter‐subject performance and by inter‐time‐frame performance. Liver contours were delineated manually on several time frames from each image type and evaluated against the auto‐segmentation by using a Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) 23 matrix. Specifically, six randomly selected time frames from each cine series and three randomly selected time frames from each helical series were manually delineated. To separate the inter‐subject from the inter‐time‐frame performance, a linear mixed‐effects model was used to analyze cine DSC n×6 result matrix, and helical DSC n×3 result matrix as

The fixed intercept β0 represents the overall mean DSC over subjects and time frames, while β1 captures the average changes in DSC per time frame. Because the time frames were randomly sampled rather than ordered temporally, β1 does not have a meaningful temporal interpretation in this study and is included only to account for potential linear trends. The term ui is the subject‐specific random intercept, reflecting inter‐subject variability, and εij is the residual error, representing within‐subject (inter‐time‐frame) variability.

Pre‐registration processing

The presence of air pockets in abdomen creates distinct air‐tissue boundaries which could mislead the registration algorithm causing registration errors. To address this issue, we performed an automated intensity correction of the CT images by filling in air pockets with the soft tissue signal intensity for subsequent registration process. Specifically, a coarse abdominal contour was first obtained by thresholding the raw CT images at −500 Hounsfield units (HU) and selecting the largest connected component. Within this contour, a morphological closing 24 operation (30 mm radius) was used to fill residual air pockets. Finally, voxels within the contour with CT values below −50 HU were set to −50 HU. 24 Representative examples of the raw and intensity‐corrected dynamic images are provided in Figure 2.

Images for the same subject were adjusted to the same dynamic range for display purposes. Detail of intensity correction was illustrated in Section 2.3, and the corrected images were used for downstream registration.

Registration model construction

The registration framework was separated into two sequential steps. First, an affine registration was applied to the intensity‐corrected dynamic images to correct for large craniocaudal displacements caused by breath‐hold variability. In this step, a module (Section 2.4.1) was incorporated to automatically detect and compensate for missing slices in cases of excessive motion. The same transformation matrix was then applied to the automatically segmented liver contours. In the second step, a deformable registration, implemented with the TransMorph framework 20 and a liver‐contour–regulated module (Section 2.4.2), was performed to achieve finer in‐plane alignment to account for deformable soft tissue nature of liver. The complete segmentation and registration workflow is shown in Figure 3.

All 46 datasets, including both cine and helical scans, were first affine‐registered. Subsequently, 36 datasets from 16 patients were selected randomly to develop the deformable registration model, while the remaining 10 datasets from 7 patients were reserved for registration testing.

Affine registration

For craniocaudal alignment, a 3D affine transform was estimated for each moving time frame and initialized using center‐of‐mass alignment to the fixed pre‐contrast image, with liver contour regulation applied. Due to contrast differences between the pre‐contrast image and the post‐contrast series, the performance of this step was limited. The pre‐registered cine and helical image series were therefore further aligned using a self‐registration approach, in which a constrained affine transformation was estimated in which only translation and scaling along the craniocaudal direction were applied, using the volume at the center time point as the reference. The affine alignment was primarily intended to correct superior–inferior displacement and scaling, leaving local in‐plane deformation to a subsequent step described in Section 2.4.2.

To compensate for missing slices, after the craniocaudal alignment, a reference image frame Ir from the aligned image series was automatically selected to identify the missing slices for other frames. Instead of arbitrarily choosing the frame at t = 0 or at the temporal midpoint of the time series, the frame Ir was selected from It as the frame that maximized the summed mutual information (MI) with all other frames, Ik, in the same image series

where N denotes the total number of temporal frames in the image series. For each non‐empty slice at position z identified from the reference frame Ir , if more than 50% voxels in a time frame Ik were zero at the same slice, then this slice was flagged as missing for Ik . The missing slice was compensated by automatically choosing a donor slice from a donor time frame Id that maximized MI at its adjacent non‐zero slices

If the missing slice was completely empty, the donor slice was then directly duplicated to the missing slice after histogram equalization using adjacent slices on Id and Ik. If a slice contained fewer than 50% non‐zero voxels due to empty regions introduced at the image boundary by translational or rotational transformations during affine registration, it was considered a partially missing slice. The missing voxels were reconstructed by solving a Poisson equation for blending with the donor image, where the guidance field was defined by the gradients of the partial missing image.

Deformable registration for in‐plane alignment

The craniocaudal shift–aligned and missing‐slice–compensated cine and helical images were then registered with a TransMorph‐Based deformable registration framework.

The 3D TransMorph 20 framework uses a Swin‐Transformer 25 model that leverages local fine and global coarse features to predict the deformation matrix between the inputted fixed and moving im ages. The output deformation vector field was used to deform the associated liver contour of moving images. The contour‐guided deformation method was introduced in the original report of the TransMorph model

where LMSE,Seg(If,Im,Φ) stands for the fidelity measurement between gradient image of fixed and moving images within the contour of moving images, and λS is the associated contour‐guidance weighting factor that was set between 0 and 1, which was empirically selected to represent different levels of contour guidance. Notably, the contour was updated synergically with the deformation matrix during training.

The craniocaudal shift–aligned and missing‐slice–compensated image series were iteratively self‐registered. Specifically, each image volume Vt was registered to the preceding volume Vt−1. If a volume exhibited severe motion or CT strike artifacts, the reference image was instead manually selected as the most recently registered volume for upcoming volume. This strategy effectively reduced contrast differences between the static reference image and dynamically acquired volumes.

Registration performance was evaluated at λs = 0, 0.2, and 0.8. We further evaluated a hybrid λs strategy in which the contour‐guidance weight was selected from these predefined values according to the temporal consistency of the liver contours. A larger λs value was used when the contours were relatively consistent across time frames, whereas a smaller or zero λs value was used when substantial contour variation was observed. Specifically, the DSC between any two auto‐segmented 2D contours at different time frames for each slice of the moving images was first calculated. Then the smallest DSC was stored for that slice. Finally, the smallest difference in DSCs across all slice locations was defined as the minimum 2D DSC difference of this 3D moving image volume, denoted as ΔDSC. The λS was set in terms of ΔDSC: if ΔDSC was > 0.8, then λS was set to 0.8 or 0.2; if ΔDSC was < 0.8, then λS was set to 0. Then we compared the hybrid λS values = 0.8 or 0, and λS = 0.2 or 0, with the fixed λS value = 0, 0.2, and 0.8.

The registration models were trained in the same computational environment as segmentation model training, using an Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 10−4 and the rate was reduced by half every 10 epochs. Data augmentation on input image pairs was implemented to improve model reliability, including rotation, random flipping, and scaling. All models were trained for a maximum of 200 epochs or until the loss function difference of three consecutive epochs was less than 10−7. The weighting factor for the regulatory loss term R(Φ) was set to λ = 10. We kept the default TransMorph configurations except for changing the image size to 224 × 192 × 160.

Registration testing

To minimize bias arising from potential imperfections in the automatically segmented liver contours in the evaluation of registration, an independent simplified contour volume was manually delineated on a testing image series. The testing dataset included 10 cases from 7 patients, and both cine and helical images were analyzed. Three anatomic anchor points were manually identified on each slice of both the raw and registered image series. These points corresponded to the far‐left, far‐right, and far‐posterior edges of the liver. A simplified closed 3D liver contour volume was then reconstructed by connecting the anchor points across slices using piecewise linear segments. This generated a coarse surface representation for quantitative evaluation. Then, using the contour volume at the initial time frame as the reference, registration accuracy was quantified by measuring the maximum offsets of contour volumes across the entire image series, measured as the mean surface distance (MSD) and the 95th‐percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95). 23 The MSD represented the average surface misalignment, whereas the HD95 captured near‐worst‐case misalignment; smaller values of both metrics indicated better registration performance.

The two‐misalignment metrics were evaluated both on the unregistered image series and on the results from the following eight registration methods:

To determine the overall best‐performing model, we used the non‐parametric Friedman test 26 to compare the metrics measured from the unregistered and six registered image sets, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. Post‐hoc pairwise comparisons between any two sets of images were performed using the Nemenyi test 27 , and a correction for false discovery rate was applied to account for the multiple comparisons.

To assess whether contour‐regularized deformable registration achieved performance equivalent to deformable registration without contour regularization, equivalence testing was performed using the two one‐sided tests (TOST) procedure with 90% confidence intervals for the paired differences in registration metrics. Equivalence margins were prespecified as ± 0.9 mm for MSD and ± 1.8 mm for the HD95, corresponding to approximately one and two image voxels, respectively, based on the in‐plane image resolution and expected boundary localization uncertainty. Equivalence was concluded when the 90% confidence interval of the paired difference was entirely contained within the corresponding prespecified equivalence bounds (TOST, P < 0.05).

With reference to unregistered images, affine registration alone effectively corrected craniocaudal displacement and compensated for missing slices, resulting in noticeable improvement (P < 0.001). Although the original TransMorph‐affine combined with TransMorph‐deformable pipeline reduced image misalignment, its performance was comparable to that of our affine registration alone. In contrast, replacing the TransMorph‐affine component with our affine registration significantly improved registration accuracy (P < 0.001). Following affine registration, deformable registration with contour regularization further improved alignment performance. The 90% confidence intervals for the paired differences between λs = 0.2 and λs = 0 were −0.20 to 0.31 mm for MSD and −1.10 to 1.55 mm for HD95. Both confidence intervals were entirely within the prespecified equivalence bounds, demonstrating equivalence between the two approaches (TOST, P < 0.05). Similarly, the hybrid model combining λs = 0 and 0.2 was also shown to be equivalent to λs = 0 for both metrics (TOST, P < 0.05), with 90% confidence intervals of −0.22 to 0.30 mm for MSD and −1.02 to 1.61 mm for HD95.

At a stronger regularization level, λS= 0.8, the registration accuracy improvement was compared with λS= 0.2. Models with auto‐determined λS values of 0 or 0.8 had the best overall performance among all models (P < 0.01). The auto‐determined λS helped to mitigate the influence of suboptimal auto‐segmented contours, illustrated in Figure 4C. In cases of inconsistent inter‐time‐frame segmentation, the auto‐determined, contour‐free deformable registration provided more stable registration results.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we developed a contour‐regulated sequential registration framework tailored for Liver CTp imaging. Our approach integrated an nnU‐Net‐based liver segmentation model with a TransMorph‐based deformable registration pipeline, regulated by auto‐segmented liver contours to improve alignment accuracy across both cine and helical CTp scans. The proposed framework addressed several key challenges in Liver CTp analysis, including motion‐induced misalignments, intensity variations due to contrast dynamics, and anatomic inconsistencies across time frames.

Accurate registration of Liver CTp images is critical for reliable quantification of perfusion parameters, which are increasingly used in assessing tumor vascularity, treatment response, and disease progression. Unlike approaches that rely on inter‐subject or inter‐modality registration in brain imaging 28 or cardiac imaging 29 , which typically pair static images with full‐craniocaudal‐coverage, this work solved a key challenge of partial liver mismatches caused by from varying craniocaudal coverage. We resolved these challenges by separating segmentation from registration.

Our automatic liver segmentation model, trained with pre‐helical images, achieved good performance while accounting for inter‐subject and inter‐temporal variations. The slightly improved segmentation results in helical images, along with the greater inter‐time‐frame variation compared with between subjects, might result from the immediate post‐injection signal intensity variation. The multi‐ROI segmentation model effectively separated liver from adjacent tissues. Despite the small cohort, we achieved reasonable average DSC values for both cine and helical images. Several dedicated works for liver segmentation using CT images are available from public datasets such as LITS2017 30 , 31 and 3D‐IRCADb‐01. 31 Our future work will integrate those models to enable fully automate segmentation for downstream registration work.

The reliability of the inter‐temporal segmentation performance is important as it directly affects the downstream registration on sequential images. Our model achieved strong stability across both subjects and time frames. Because the segmentation and registration models were trained with the same dataset, data dependency exists between them. To minimize potential cross‐talk and ensure an unbiased evaluation of registration accuracy, we used manually placed anchor points on both the reference and the registered images for independent assessment, rather than relying on segmentation‐derived contours.

Affine registration with donor slice correction, served as a key preprocessing step to improve the temporal alignment of CT perfusion image series prior to deformable registration. Missing slices at certain time frames were compensated with auto‐selected slices from neighboring frames. Breath‐hold position variability and craniocaudal motion often introduce through‐plane misalignment that deformable algorithms alone cannot fully correct. The comparison of registration strategies indicates that affine initialization was a major contributor to the overall improvement in registration accuracy. Replacing TransMorph‐affine with our affine registration significantly improved subsequent deformable registration, suggesting that effective correction of craniocaudal displacement and missing slices provided a more favorable initialization. The affine correction stabilized the overall liver anatomy and reduced large positional errors, whereas donor slice interpolation preserved slice continuity and prevented artifacts from propagating into the deformable stage. Together, these steps achieved consistent liver alignment and enhanced the convergence of deformable registration. Training deep learning–based registration models on image pairs from the same series at different time points is a common approach for dynamic image series such as cine. 32 , 33 We used this strategy in model training, and further applied the dynamic fixed image approach during inference to reduce the impact of contrast difference.

Using liver contour as regulation constraint for registration offered several advantages. First, it directed the registration to focus on the liver region, reducing the influence of surrounding structures such as bowel gas, diaphragm motion, and vascular artifacts. Second, auto‐segmentation promoted temporal consistency within the target region and provided an unbiased term in the loss function. Third, contour‐regulated registration enabled more interpretable and anatomically consistent deformation fields, which are crucial for downstream quantitative analysis. Our results demonstrated that the nnU‐Net model, trained on a limited number of manually delineated scans, generalized well to both cine and helical CTp images.

The two‐step registration process, consisting of affine alignment followed by deformable registration, effectively addressed both global shifts and localized deformations. The initial affine registration, guided by liver masks, corrected craniocaudal displacement caused by breath‐hold variability. The subsequent deformable registration refined the alignment, achieving sub‐voxel accuracy as measured by MSD and HD95. As illustrated in Figure 6, registration reduced temporal fluctuations in tumor‐bed time‐attenuation curves while preserving the enhancement pattern of the aortic input function, suggesting improved temporal consistency for CT perfusion analysis. However, evaluation of perfusion maps, quantitative perfusion parameters, and ROI reproducibility was beyond the scope of this study. Accordingly, this work focuses on the geometric evaluation of deformable image registration, while downstream perfusion validation remains an important direction for future investigation.

However, transformer‐based models are computationally intensive and require careful hyperparameters tuning and although our model performed well on the test set, additional optimization may be necessary for deployment in real‐time clinical workflows. Moreover, the reliance on accurate segmentation masks introduces a potential source of error, as inaccurate segmentation errors could propagate through the registration pipeline. On the other hand, the manual delineation of anchor points is time‐consuming and introduces inter‐ and intra‐evaluator variability, which motivates the use of auto‐feature detection methods 34 , 35 , 36 , or integration of dedicated Liver CT auto‐segmentation system trained on larger multi‐center datasets. Future work could explore joint segmentation‐registration frameworks or incorporate uncertainty modeling to mitigate this limitation. In addition, future studies should assess the relationship between registration accuracy and downstream perfusion quantification.

CONCLUSIONS

We present a contour‐regulated, transformer‐based registration framework for Liver CT perfusion imaging that addresses key challenges in motion and intensity variation. By integrating liver auto‐segmentation with advanced deformable registration, our approach improved registration accuracy and provided a foundation for subsequent analysis.
