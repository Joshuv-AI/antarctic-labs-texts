Development of machine learning models for survival prediction in nasopharyngeal carcinoma using population-based data
Authors: Guoxiang Lin, Qiyan Mo, Lu Han, Shaohan Sun, Zhiqiang Xiao, Weiming Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite significant improvements in treatment efficacy, nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) remains one of the most common and threatening head and neck cancers. This study sought to build a risk stratification model that categorizes NPC patients into distinct prognostic groups based on readily available demographic and clinical variables, with the goal of enhancing patient care and improving survival rates.MethodsData for patients diagnosed with NPC between 2010 and 2018 were obtained from the SEER program. Kaplan–Meier analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression were applied to evaluate the effects of treatment modalities on overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS). Prognostic variables from multivariable Cox models were used to construct nomograms for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS and CSS. Several survival-focused machine learning algorithms, including the random survival forest (RSF), were trained and compared using the concordance index (C-index) and integrated Brier score. An interactive web-based calculator was developed based on the best-performing model.ResultsThe analysis included 9,816 patients diagnosed between 2000 and 2020. Combined radiotherapy and chemotherapy significantly improved survival in locoregionally advanced and advanced NPC compared with other treatment regimens. Cox-based nomograms achieved a C-index of 0.71 (95% CI: 0.70–0.72), outperforming the conventional staging system. The RSF model demonstrated the highest prognostic accuracy, with C-indices of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.71–0.74) for 3-year OS and 0.75 (95% CI: 0.73–0.76) for 5-year OS. The RSF algorithm was implemented as a free online survival prediction tool.ConclusionMachine learning based models, particularly the RSF algorithm, provide improved individualized survival predictions for NPC. While performance was promising, further external validation and inclusion of additional clinical and molecular predictors are needed before clinical adoption. We also developed an interactive web-based survival prediction tool, enabling clinicians and researchers to obtain individualized survival probabilities in real time.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03790-8.Keywords: Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Machine learning algorithm, Survival prediction, Survival probability calculator, Cancer-specific survival

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is an epithelial cancer originating from the mucosal lining of the nasopharynx and ranks as one of the most prevalent head and neck cancers [1]. Although decreasing trends in the morbidity and mortality of NPC have been sporadically reported in some high-risk areas, such as East Asia [2], researchers have reported that among NPC subtypes, the morbidity of early-onset nasopharyngeal carcinoma has shown the fastest increasing trend, with an estimated annual growth percentage of 2.28%, especially in North America [3, 4]. Globally, it is estimated that by 2040, approximately 179,476 new cases of NPC will occur worldwide, reflecting a roughly 34.58% rise from the 133,354 cases reported in 2020 [5]. Despite advances in multidisciplinary treatment, NPC is often diagnosed at advanced stages, leading to a generally poor prognosis [6], and approximately 4–10% of NPC patients present with metastases at their initial diagnosis [1]. Once NPC reaches an advanced stage, survival prospects are grim, with the 5-year survival rate as low as 19.2% for patients diagnosed with advanced NPC even when they are treated with standard first-line radiochemotherapy regimens [7, 8]. It is estimated that the number of NPC deaths worldwide will increase by approximately 42.29%, from 80,008 in 2020 to 113,851 in 2040 [5]. Collectively, a 2% annual decrease in global incidence and mortality rates is needed to halt the increasing NPC burden by 2040 [5].

Owing to its unique anatomical location, the early detection of nasopharyngeal carcinoma is challenging [9]. To improve the quality of medical care, some clinical decision support tools have been developed to help clinicians with the early detection, diagnosis, treatment and prognosis of NPC [10–13]. The prediction of NPC prognosis has been partially validated through extensive follow-up at several medical institutions [10, 14]; however, the overall quality of previous NPC prediction models was less than optimal because of limited sample sizes and special stage systems such as locoregionally advanced NPC (LA-NPC) [15, 16]. For example, Toumi et al. conducted a retrospective study of only 112 patients with metastatic NPC to assess survival and prognostic factors [17]. In addition, these limitations result in an inability to capture the dynamic progression of the disease, provide precise depictions of the varied tumor presentations, and anticipate patient-specific prognoses and therapeutic outcomes [15]. Despite aggressive treatment, a considerable proportion of LA-NPC patients develop distant metastases, which challenges the accuracy of traditional risk stratification [1, 15]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for the development of more accurate prognostic systems that comprehensively and individually assess the disease status of patients and disease prognosis [18, 19].

Machine learning, which is an advanced subfield of artificial intelligence, has emerged as a promising approach to address these challenges [20]. With the ability to analyze extensive datasets [21, 22], discern intricate patterns [23, 24], and perform personalized risk assessments [24, 25], machine learning provides an effective means to surmount these limitations and has the potential to substantially increase prognostic accuracy [20]. While machine learning has shown considerable promise in the field of cervical [26], ovarian [27] and lung cancer [28], its application in the field of NPC is still in its early stages [1, 13]. In particular, significant gaps exist in previous research that need to be filled to create powerful predictive models that can improve outcomes [13]. To address these gaps, we used the SEER database to construct a robust machine learning model. Concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CRT) remains the most effective treatment for NPC and is the guideline-recommended first-line therapy. Other therapeutic approaches are typically administered in combination with CRT or reserved for patients unable to tolerate CRT, Comparing CRT with other therapies helps assess real-world prognostic differences and model performance. We compared survival between NPC patients receiving standard radiochemotherapy and those receiving other therapies. Next, we created a nomogram to predict OS and CSS and identified high-risk patients by NPC clinical stage. Finally, we developed multiple predictive models (e.g., Cox regression, flexible parametric survival, SRF, SCIT, SCRF, AORSF, CoxBoost, GBRT, and gradient boosting with a componentwise linear model) using supervised machine learning. These models were evaluated in a deviation cohort and validated internally for performance.

Materials and methods

Data source

The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program was established by the National Cancer Institute to assess population-based cancer statistics in the United States [29]. This database includes 18 geographic registries, encompasses approximately 30% of the U.S. population, and provides insights into the prevalence of cancer within the U.S. population [21, 30, 31]. This retrospective study cohort initially included patients diagnosed with NPC between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2020. The final study cohort was identified from the exclusion criteria depicted in Fig. 1. Data were obtained to create the case list through SEER ∗ Stat software version 8.3.6 (https://seer.cancer.gov/data/). Since the SEER data were anonymized, an institutional review board approval was not needed [23].

Flowchart of overall study design and patient subject exclusion and selection of NPC from SEER database

Study covariables

Demographic data, including age at diagnosis, sex (female and male), race (white, black, and other), marital status at diagnosis (married, unmarried, and unknown), and socioeconomic indicators (median household income and rural vs. urban), were extracted. Taking inflation into account, the income data (median household income) were adjusted to the 2021 United States Census and categorized into tertiles (high, medium and low) [30]. Tumor-related characteristics included NPC grade (Grade I–IV), T stage (tumor extension), N stage (node extension), M stage (metastasis status) and tumor size (0–30 mm, 30–60 mm, > 60 mm and unknown). Tumors were staged according to the 6th, 7th, and 8th versions of the AJCC (2004–2009, 2010–2017, and 2018, respectively) [32]. All NPCs were categorized into four clinical stages, namely, early-stage (T1N0M0), middle-stage (T0-1N0M0 and T2N0-1M0), locoregionally advanced stage (T0-2N2M0, T3N0-2M0, T4N0-2M0 and TxN3M0) and advanced stage (TxNxM1), on the basis of clinical practice [1]. In accordance with the International Classification of Disease for Oncology third revision (ICD-O-3), all NPCs were classified into four histological subtypes, namely, keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma (KSCC; ICD-O-3 codes 8070 and 8071), differentiated nonkeratinizing carcinoma (DNKC; ICD-O-3 codes 8072 and 8073), undifferentiated nonkeratinizing carcinoma (UNKC; ICD-O-3 codes 8020, 8021 and 8082) and basaloid squamous cell carcinoma (BSCC; ICD-O-3 codes 8083)[32]. The therapeutic patterns of NPC patients were classified as follows: radiotherapy alone (RT alone), chemotherapy (CT alone), surgery (surgery alone), conventional radiotherapy plus chemotherapy (RT + CT) and RT + CT plus surgery (RT + CT + surgery). Variables with > 20% missingness were excluded; those with ≤ 20% missingness were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE).

Data on survival time and cause of death were collected for all participants. Model development was conducted using the R packages mlr3 (version 1.1.0) and mlr3proba (version 0.8.2), which provide a standardized framework for implementing a broad range of survival analysis algorithms. Unless otherwise specified, models were executed with the default hyperparameters defined by their respective underlying R packages. Given that the majority of models attained C-index values exceeding 0.70, further hyperparameter optimization was not pursued.

Nomogram development and evaluation

In accordance with the rules of clinical predictive model establishment, all NPC patients were randomly divided into a training set and a test set at a ratio of 7:3, which kept the distribution of the variable data in both groups consistent [33]. A prognostic nomogram was constructed on the basis of the multivariate Cox analysis of the training cohort, and it was used to predict the 1-, 3- and 5-year OS and CSS by representing the total points for each variable. Individual scores for each patient were determined based on clinicopathological parameters and aggregated to yield a cumulative score. The predictive accuracy of the prognostic model was evaluated against conventional clinical staging using the C-index and Kaplan‒Meier curves [34]. Calibration curve were employed to compare the predicted OS and CSS rates with the actual survival rates, while a decision curve analysis assessed the clinical applicability of the nomogram across a spectrum of threshold probabilities. Risk groups (low, medium, and high) were established on the basis of patient scores, reflecting distinct prognoses.

Model development and validation

Considering that this was a regression problem based on time-to-event (censored) cohort data, we developed survival prediction models. For this study, to ensure the reliability and robustness of the model, we employed fivefold cross-validation. Therefore, patients were randomly divided into five equal parts as follows: one part was used for testing in each development, and the remaining parts were used for training, where the calculation process was repeated five times, and the average performance was calculated. We used fivefold cross-validation, where in each iteration four folds were used for training and the remaining one-fold was used for validation to evaluate model performance. To ensure comparable event rates across training and validation sets, we employed stratified sampling based on the event indicator. The overall event rate in the full dataset was 49.28% (4837/9816). The following table presents the event distribution of training and validation sets from one instance of the fivefold cross-validation, provided for reference only. We observed that both the training and validation sets maintained event distributions that were close to that of the overall dataset. The detailed methodology of model construction and validation is presented in the supplementary material.

Statistical analyses

The continuous variable age was converted into an ordinal categorical variable using the R package “Cutoff” to determine the optimal cutoff value. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean (standard deviation) and compared using the two-tailed t test or the Mann‒Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. To explore potential predictive factors for overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS), we calculated the hazard ratio (HR) and the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) with a Cox regression model [30]. Risk factors for survival in NPC patients were predicted primarily by univariate Cox regression. Univariable logistic regression (P < 0.05) was used to identify candidates for further multivariable Cox analysis. OS and CSS curves were plotted using the Kaplan‒Meier method and analyzed using log-rank analysis [25]. All data were extracted from SEER*Stat 8.4.2, and statistical analyses were performed using STATA (Version 15, StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA) and R (Version 4.1.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The statistical tests used to compare baseline characteristics (Chi-square test for categorical variables and t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables).

Results

A total of 9,816 patients who were diagnosed with NPC between 2000 and 2020 were identified in this study, followed by 668,138 person-years as follows: 3,234 patients diagnosed with KSCC, 2,288 patients diagnosed with DNKC, 1,512 patients diagnosed with UNKC and 88 patients diagnosed with BSCC. Several differences in patient characteristics were identified between the NPC patients who received conventional radiochemotherapy and those who received other therapeutic regimens (Table 1). Compared with patients who received other therapeutic regimens, patients who received conventional radiochemotherapy were more likely to be younger (p < 0.001), American Indian/AK Native or Asian/Pacific Islander (p < 0.001), and in the N1 stage (p < 0.001).

Comparison of survival among NPC patients undergoing different therapeutic patterns

In the entire cohort, compared with CT alone or RT alone, radiochemotherapy significantly improved the OS of NPC patients (Table 2). Moreover, compared with conventional radiochemotherapy, radiochemotherapy plus surgery slightly improved the OS (HR = 0.87; 95% CI [0.76–0.98]; p = 0.027) and CSS (HR = 0.77; 95% CI [0.64–0.93]; p = 0.007) of NPC patients. In addition, compared with CT alone, radiochemotherapy increased the CSS of NPC patients (HR = 1.70; 95% CI [1.46–1.99]; p < 0.001); however, this was not the case for patients who underwent RT alone (HR = 1.13; 95% CI [0.97–1.32]; p = 0.121), which indicates that the addition of chemotherapy did not decrease the risk of death from carcinoma among NPC patients.

Survival outcomes by treatment and factors in NPC

For patients with early-stage NPC, there was no statistically significant difference between patients who received radiochemotherapy and those who received other therapeutic strategies (Supplementary Table S1). For patients with mid-stage NPC, compared with CT alone, traditional radiochemotherapies improved the OS and CSS of patients (Supplementary Table S2). Compared with RT alone, the combination of chemotherapy and radiotherapy did not increase the OS (HR = 1.30; 95% CI [0.96–1.75]; p = 0.094) or CSS (HR = 1.07; 95% CI [0.64–1.80]; p = 0.784) of NPC patients. In patients with NPC in the locoregionally advanced stage, compared with CT alone and RT alone, radiochemotherapy markedly improved the OS and CSS of NPC patients (Supplementary Table S3). Moreover, compared with the conventional radiochemotherapy regimen, surgery alone significantly increased OS (HR = 1.94; 95% CI [1.27–2.95]; p = 0.002) among these patients. For patients with advanced NPC, compared with CT alone and RT alone, radiochemotherapy improved the OS and CSS of NPC patients (Supplementary Table S4). The survival rates of NPC patients who underwent various therapeutic strategies are shown in Fig. 2. Notably, compared with chemotherapy alone or radiotherapy alone, radiotherapy combined with chemotherapy resulted in superior overall and cancer-specific prognoses.

Construction of a nomogram for prognostic assessment among NPC patients

The prognostic variables identified through multivariate Cox regression analysis were incorporated into a nomogram to predict OS. This nomogram enabled the estimation of 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS and CSS probabilities for individual patients (Fig. 3).

Nomogram to predict 1-, 3- and 5-year overall survival (A) and cancer-specific survival (B) in NPC patients

In the training cohort, the bias-corrected C-indices for the nomogram was 0.71 (95% CI: 0.70–0.72), compared to 0.61 (95% CI: 0.60–0.62) for the traditional clinical staging system. In the internal validation cohort, the C-index of the nomogram was 0.72 (95% CI: 0.70–0.74), which was higher than that of the clinical stage. Across all three cohorts, the C-indices of the nomogram consistently outperformed those of the clinical staging system, with p values < 0.001, demonstrating superior predictive accuracy in both training and validation cohorts.

Patients in the training cohort were stratified into three risk groups based on risk score cutoffs derived from multivariate Cox regression: low-risk (≤ 174.3; n = 2,274), intermediate-risk (> 174.3 and ≤ 208.9; n = 1,885), and high-risk (> 208.9; n = 2,711). Significant differences in OS rates were observed among these groups, with 5-year OS rates of 26.7%, 45.9%, and 59.7% for the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, respectively. Comparable findings were observed in the validation cohort, with 5-year OS rates of 25.2%, 46.0%, and 58.8%, respectively. Notably, the high-risk group exhibited significantly poorer 5-year OS compared to the intermediate- and low-risk groups. Kaplan–Meier survival curves demonstrated greater separation among risk groups using the nomogram compared to clinical staging in both the training and validation cohorts (Fig. 4). These results indicate that the nomogram effectively distinguished prognostic outcomes across low-, intermediate-, and high-risk subgroups, with the high-risk group consistently showing a lower median survival time and significant p-values, unlike the traditional clinical staging system.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis of overall survival and cancer-specific survival according to the nomogram stratification in the training cohort (A, B) and validation cohort (C, D)

Calibration analysis revealed that the survival probabilities predicted by the nomogram closely corresponded with observed outcomes (Supplementary Fig. S1), indicating robust model calibration. The DCA further demonstrated that the nomogram provided a significantly greater clinical net benefit compared to the traditional clinical staging system for predicting OS in NPC patients (Supplementary Fig. S2). Additionally, Cox regression analysis prioritized prognostic variables based on their relative weights from stepwise regression. Clinical stage and age group emerged as the two most influential predictors of 3- and 5-year survival outcomes for individual patients.

Model specification

In this study, a commonly used Cox regression was selected as the reference model, and other supervised survival-based machine learning algorithms (the Cox regression model, flexible parametric survival model, SRF, SCIT, SCRF, AORSF, CoxBoost survival analysis, GBRT and gradient boosting with a componentwise linear model) were used to develop prediction models. Additionally, survival-based models can consider patient survival time in the algorithm structure, whereas other popular classification machine learning algorithms (e.g., support vector machines) focus on outcome prediction (death or survival) with less consideration of the contribution of patient survival time. In this study, we describe the prediction models for 3-year and 5-year NPC survival in a national cohort. On the basis of a single-factor analysis of the baseline characteristics of the included patients, we used eleven supervised learning models to predict NPC patient survival. A total of 11 candidate variables were used to develop a predictive model for NPC patient survival on the basis of supervised learning algorithms.

The predictive performance of the models was measured using the C-index and IBS. Values of the overall C-index for each model are presented in Table 3. In this study, the C-index was calculated as the average of 20 iterations of fivefold cross-validation. Similar results were found in the 5-year survival cohort. As shown in Fig. 5, over time, SRF and SCRF consistently exhibited the best C-index throughout the period of investigation across all settings. With respect to the prediction of long-term survival for NPC patients (> = 200 months), compared with other predictive models, the SRF model had a superior C-index. In addition to the C-index plots over time, Fig. 6 presents the prediction error curves for each model based on the IBS [35]. We found that all models performed better than the SCIT model. The values of the SCRF and SRF models were approximately the same. With respect to the prediction of short-term survival among NPC patients, the prediction error of the SCRF model was greater than that of the SRF model; moreover, with respect to the prediction of long-term survival among NPC patients, the prediction error of the SRF model was greater than that of the SCRF model. However, this comes with a computational burden; for example, in the complete case analysis of the 5-year cohort, the average training times for the Cox regression model, flexible parametric survival model, SRF, SCIT, SCRF, AORSF, CoxBoost survival analysis, GBRT and gradient boosting with the componentwise linear model for each iteration were 0.40 s, 34.26 s, 20.72 s, 8.5 s, 289.20 s, 9.73 s, 54.07 s, 63.60 s and 96.60 s, respectively.

Model presentation and development of an online survival calculator

The random forest survival method outperformed other algorithms, using regression trees with bootstrap aggregation and predictor randomization for high predictive accuracy. Based on this, we have developed a web-based NPC survival probability calculator (https://bio.wencode.shop/npctool/) that incorporates the latest 9th Edition TNM classification for NPC, jointly released by the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) and the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC). This tool is designed to assist clinicians and medical researchers in formulating more precise and individualized treatment strategies, scientifically evaluating treatment efficacy and prognosis, and supporting the design and implementation of related clinical studies. The calculator enables users—including clinicians, patients, and researchers—to access 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year overall survival curves from NPC cohorts, interact with predictive models and survival curves, and obtain individualized survival probability estimates for 1–5 years post-diagnosis. The application is powered by a Cox proportional hazards model trained on simulated data, integrating patient characteristics, tumor features, and treatment information to generate time-dependent survival predictions.

The calculator provides the following core functionalities: (1) Patient Data Input:

Users can enter basic patient information, detailed TNM-9 staging for NPC, selected treatment modalities, pathological subtype, and other relevant clinical data. (2) Survival Prediction Results: Generates individualized overall survival curves at 1, 3, and 5 years, based on NPC cohorts derived from the SEER database. (3) Compare Patient Scenarios: Enables comparison of survival outcomes between different patient profiles, providing both prognostic statistics and comparative survival curves over multiple time intervals. (4) Risk Factor Analysis: Allows exploration of how specific risk factors influence survival probabilities. Selecting a variable displays its impact on the survival curve and generates a corresponding correlation heatmap. (5) Data Analysis: Allows users to upload their own survival datasets in CSV or Excel format. The tool automatically generates Kaplan–Meier survival curves and conducts additional survival analyses (Supplementary Fig. S3). Comprehensive user instructions are provided to facilitate effective and accurate use of the calculator in both research and clinical practice.

Discussion

Our investigation into NPC provides valuable insights into the disease's epidemiology, prognosis, and potential for improved prognostic tools. Previous studies highlight a concerning trend: despite declining NPC morbidity and mortality in some regions, a noticeable surge in early-onset cases is occurring, particularly in North America [36]. These findings underscore the evolving nature of NPC and the imperative to develop more effective predictive and treatment strategies.

This study included a significant cohort of 9,816 patients diagnosed with NPC from 2000 to 2020 and offers substantial insights into the therapeutic strategies employed. With 668,138 person-years observed, the study delineated patients across various diagnostic categories, including KSCC, DNKC, UNKC, and BSCC. Notably, a variety of treatment modalities were identified, with radiotherapy plus chemotherapy being the most prevalent. The differences in patient characteristics between conventional radiochemotherapy recipients and those opting for other therapies highlight the nuanced considerations in treatment planning. Compared with monotherapies or radiotherapy alone, radiochemotherapy has emerged as a potent therapeutic intervention that significantly increases the survival of NPC patients[37].

A pivotal contribution of this study lies in the development of a prognostic nomogram for NPC patients, which meticulously encapsulates demographic and tumor-related variables. This ability of the nomogram lies not only in its superior predictive accuracy but also in its ability to discern nuanced risk stratifications that range from low- to high-risk subgroups. The consistency of the performance of the traditional AJCC staging system with that of the nomogram further highlights its potential as a transformative tool in prognostic assessments. The results of the calibration and decision curve analyses collectively reinforce the precision and clinical applicability of the nomogram. The alignment of the calibration curve with actual survival rates underscores the model's reliability, while the decision curve elucidates its tangible clinical benefits, surpassing the utility of conventional staging systems. In addition, the comparative DCA results for models revealed that the Random Survival Forest and XGBoost consistently achieved greater net clinical benefit than the Cox nomogram across a broad range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities (0.1–0.6), suggesting their potential for guiding treatment decisions in moderate-to-high risk scenarios. Conversely, the Cox nomogram maintained comparable or superior net benefit in lower-threshold settings, which may be more suitable for screening or early intervention. This complementary performance highlights the importance of selecting predictive models based on specific clinical contexts and decision thresholds.

The poor prognosis associated with advanced-stage NPC, as evidenced by a disheartening 5-year survival rate of 19.2%, highlights the critical need for precise prognostic tools. Traditional risk stratification methods, which are constrained by limited sample sizes and specific stage systems, have been insufficient for capturing the intricate nuances of NPC progression and diverse manifestations [38]. To address these gaps, in our research, the capabilities of machine learning, an innovative facet of artificial intelligence, is harnessed to formulate a comprehensive and individualized prognostic model. Unlike previous predictive models based on binary classification systems (dead or live), this study focuses first on the application and evaluation of survival-related machine learning models based on time-to-event data and censored observations. The aim of our model, which draws upon the expansive SEER database, is to transcend the limitations of conventional approaches and provide a nuanced assessment of the disease trajectories and prognostic outcomes of patients with NPC. The incorporation of various supervised survival-based machine learning algorithms has enriched the predictive modeling landscape for NPC survival. With methods ranging from Cox regression to gradient boosting, the study offers a comparative evaluation of model performance. Notably, the random forest survival technique stood out, demonstrating superior predictive accuracy, albeit with relatively increased computational demands [39]. The superior performance of the RSF model likely reflects its capacity to model nonlinear relationships, capture complex interactions, and handle multicollinearity. Its top-ranked predictors-stage, tumor size, age, and treatment modality—are consistent with established NPC prognostic factors, ensuring both robust predictive ability and clinical interpretability. While our model demonstrates strong performance using the SEER database, its applicability to other populations with differing healthcare systems, demographics, and resource availability requires further consideration. The SEER database primarily represents the U.S. population, which may not fully capture the diverse clinical realities in regions with different ethnic distributions, healthcare infrastructure, or access to advanced diagnostic and treatment modalities. In particular, in areas where NPC is endemic, such as Southeast Asia, the model's predictive accuracy may be impacted by limited healthcare resources and variations in treatment protocols. External validation and recalibration of the model in these diverse settings are essential for improving its generalizability and ensuring its clinical utility across different populations.

Machine learning algorithms are becoming increasingly prominent for assessing the likelihood of survival of nasopharyngeal carcinoma patients [40]. A review of existing models revealed a reliance on SEER database data, which offered rich patient information [41–47]. Remarkably, we observed that only two studies obtained data from the SEER database [41, 44], which is an extensively utilized cancer registry that furnishes a substantial volume of patient information for research purposes [25, 30, 48]. The extensive utilization of the SEER database has empowered researchers to acquire intricate data on patients with cancers at a national scale [49–51], thereby contributing to the development of more comprehensive and dependable machine learning models [14, 52]. Conversely, other studies utilized data from a single or a limited number of hospitals, as listed in Table 4. Overall, machine learning models surpassed conventional statistical models in terms of the prognosis of nasopharyngeal carcinoma, with C-indices ranging from 0.68 [46] to 0.79 [41]. Unlike previous binary classification approaches, our work pioneers the development of survival-centric machine learning models for prognosis, culminating in an online survival probability calculator based on random forest. The interactive web-based survival prediction tool that uniquely incorporates the latest 9th Edition TNM staging system (2025) for NPC and offers comprehensive functionalities, including individualized survival curves, patient scenario comparisons, risk factor analyses, and customizable data input. These features distinguish our tool from existing NPC prediction models and enhance its translational potential for both clinical practice and research applications. This tool enhances clinical decision-making by providing a dynamic understanding of patient prognosis beyond binary outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this comprehensive study illuminates the intricate landscape of NPC treatment modalities, prognostic insights, and predictive modeling. Radiochemotherapy has emerged as a cornerstone therapeutic strategy, while the integration of advanced predictive tools and machine learning algorithms heralds a new era in NPC management. However, limitations in currently available data constrain model precision. Therefore, future models incorporating comprehensive clinical, molecular, and behavioral data are essential to enhance prediction reliability. Future research should focus on refining these models, validating findings across diverse populations, and translating insights into tangible clinical implementation.
