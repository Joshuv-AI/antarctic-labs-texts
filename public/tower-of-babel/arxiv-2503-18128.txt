A cometary Fluorescence Model for the ν 3 \nu_{3} Vibrational Band of Cyanogen
P. Hardy, P. Rousselot, C. Richard, V. Boudon, X. Landsheere, A. Voute, L. Manceron, F. Kwabia Tchana

Abstract
Cyanogen ( C 2 ​ N 2 \text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} ) has been suspected for a long time to be present in comets and to contribute to the creation of the CN radical. So far no observations with ground-based facilities have managed to detect this species but the Rosetta mission, thanks to in situ observations with the ROSINA mass spectrometer detected this species in the coma of 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko. To investigate its presence from infrared spectra in other comets, we developed a fluorescence model for the ν 3 \nu_{3} fundamental band. From new laboratory high-resolution infrared spectra of cyanogen, we analyzed the region of the ν 3 \nu_{3} band of C 2 ​ N 2 \text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} , centered around 4.63 µ ​ m 4.63\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m} ( 2158 cm − 1 2158\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1} ). In addition to line positions and intensities, molecular parameters for the ground and excited vibrational state were obtained. These parameters allowed us to develop a fluorescence model for cyanogen. Excitation rates of the ν 3 \nu_{3} band of cyanogen in cometary comae are presented. An attempt to detect cyanogen in a high-resolution spectrum of comet C/2022 E3 (ZTF) is discussed.



1
Introduction

Among the limited number of space missions dedicated to cometary science, the Rosetta spacecraft launched in 2004 significantly enhanced the understanding of those small bodies, by conducting a two-year study of the comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko. One of Rosetta’s instruments, the mass spectrometer ROSINA, discovered numerous chemical species in the coma that had never been observed in comets before.

One such species, cyanogen (
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
) is a linear molecule that was previously only observed in the atmosphere of Titan by
[
20
]
before its detection on comet 67P
[
1
]
. Although the CN radical is a prominent feature in cometary optical spectra, its origins (i.e., its corresponding parent species) are still poorly understood. In particular, hydrogen cyanide (HCN) has been shown not to be the only source of cometary CN
[
15
,
16
]
.
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, while being much less abundant than HCN in the coma of comet 67P (
[
16
]
reported
{}{}\mathrm{C}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{N}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}/{}\mathrm{HCN}=0.0018\pm 0.0009
, (1.24-1.74 au)) could still participate in the formation of CN. Its detection in 67P should be extended to other comets to better constrain its abundance in cometary environments.

While the
\nu_{5}
far-infrared band of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is already present in the HITRAN database
[
14
,
13
]
, no data are available at wavenumbers greater than
310\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}
. In particular, one of the fundamental bands of cyanogen, centered at
2158\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}
(
4.63\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
are present.

The paper is organized as follows. After discussing experimental details (Section

2
Experimental details

Seven absorption spectra of cyanogen have been recorded in the range from 1800 to 3500
\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}
using the high-resolution Bruker IFS125HR Fourier transform spectrometer (FTS) located at the LISA facility in Créteil (France). The instrument was equipped with a silicon carbide Globar source, a KBr/Ge beamsplitter and a liquid nitrogen-cooled indium antimonide (InSb) detector. The InSb detector was used in conjunction with an optical filter, with a bandpass of 1800–3400
\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}
, to minimize the size of the interferogram data files and also to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. The FTS was continuously evacuated below
3\times 10^{4}
hPa by a turbomolecular pump to minimize absorption by atmospheric gases. The diameter of the entrance aperture of the spectrometer was set to 1.3 mm to maximize the intensity of infrared radiation falling onto the InSb detector without saturation or loss of spectral resolution. Interferograms were recorded with an 80 kHz scanner frequency and a maximum optical path difference (MOPD) of 250 cm. According to the Bruker definition (resolution = 0.9/MOPD), this corresponds to a resolution of 0.0036
\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}
. The spectra were obtained by Fourier transformation of the interferograms using a Mertz phase correction with a
1\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}
resolution, a zero-filling factor of 2 and no apodization (boxcar option).
The cyanogen (
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
) sample was synthesized in the laboratory by progressive heating of silver cyanide (99%, Sigma Aldrich) in a glass bulb previously evacuated:

2

\Delta>350^{\circ}\text{C}
(-2Ag)
.

(1)

About 5 g of silver cyanide powder was placed in a thoroughly cleaned, grease-free glass bulb and heated gently under high vacuum up to 200°C to eliminate atmospheric gases and adsorbed water vapor. Next, the powder was progressively heated to 300°C where evolution of cyanogen gas slowly started to occur. The first batch, containing traces of carbon dioxide from carbonate impurities, was discarded, further heating to about 400°C completed the reaction and cyanogen was collected in a glass trap on the vacuum line. Finally, a trap-to-trap distillation at about -70°C helped reduce possible remaining impurities (
HCN
,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
or others). The remaining
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
traces were estimated from IR measurements to much less than 0.1%.

A short-path absorption cell (SPAC) made of Pyrex glass and equipped with CsBr windows was used for all the measurements. The SPAC is a White-type multipass cell with a base length of 0.20 m. In this experiment, an optical path of 0.849(2) m was used. The sample pressure in the cell was measured using calibrated MKS Baratron capacitance manometers models 627 (2.6664 hPa full scale) and 628 D (13.332 hPa full scale) characterized by a stated reading accuracy of 0.12%. Considering the uncertainty arising from small variations of the pressure during the recording (
<0.35\%
), we estimated the measurement uncertainty on the pressure to be equal to 0.5%. All the spectra were recorded at a stabilized room temperature of
293\pm 1
K.
The following procedure was used to record the spectra. A background spectrum was first collected while the cell was being continuously evacuated. It was recorded at the same resolution as the sample spectra to ensure proper removal of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
or
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
absorption lines. The infrared gas cell was then passivated several times with the
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
sample. Finally, spectra were recorded for seven different sample pressures of cyanogen. The seven pressures chosen and the number of interferograms recorded and averaged to yield the corresponding spectra are listed in Table
1

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
observed therein to reference wavenumbers available in HITRAN (
[
14
]
) with an rms deviation of 0.00018
\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}
. The absolute accuracy of the measured
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
line positions was estimated as the square root of the sum of squares of the accuracy of the reference
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}

0.0001\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}
) plus the RMS deviation between the observed frequencies and values listed in HITRAN for
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

3
Theoretical aspects

Cyanogen (
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
) is a linear molecule belonging to the
D_{\infty h}
point group, with five fundamental vibrational modes: CN symmetric stretch (
\nu_{1}
, centered at
2330.4859\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}
, with
\Sigma_{g}
symmetry), CC symmetric stretch (
\nu_{2}
, centered at
845.5939\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}
, with
\Sigma_{g}
symmetry, infrared inactive), CN asymmetric stretch (
\nu_{3}
, centered at
2157.8243\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}
, with
\Sigma_{u}
symmetry), and two doubly degenerated CCN bending modes (
\nu_{4}
, centered at
502.7745\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}
, with
\Pi_{g}
symmetry, infrared inactive; and
\nu_{5}
, centered at
233.7225\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}
, with
\Pi_{u}
symmetry). Band-center values come from the analysis of
[
23
]
. Our study is focused on the
\nu_{3}
mode, centered at 2158 cm
-1
.

The rotational energy levels are described as usual by

E(J)=BJ(J+1)-DJ^{2}(J+1)^{2}+HJ^{3}(J+1)^{3}+\dots,

(2)

where
B
is the rotational constant, and
D
and
H
represent centrifugal distortion corrections. The allowed frequencies are obtained as
\nu_{J}=\nu_{0}+E_{u}(J_{u})-E_{l}(J_{l})
, where
\nu_{0}
is the frequency of the pure vibrational transitions, and the subscripts
u
and
l
refer to the upper and lower vibrational levels, respectively. For non-
\Sigma
states, the interaction between rotation and angular momentum components leads to
l
-doubling, resulting in energy splitting given by

\Lambda(J)=qJ(J+1)+q_{D}J^{2}(J+1)^{2}+q_{H}J^{3}(J+1)^{3}+\dots

(3)

Individual line strengths (
S_{J}
) can be expressed in terms of the band strength (
S_{\nu}
, usually given in
\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-2}\text{\,}{\mathrm{atm}}^{-1}
)
[
28
]

S_{J}=\varepsilon_{J}\frac{\nu_{J}}{\nu_{0}}L_{J}\frac{S_{\nu}}{Q_{r}}(e^{-hcE_{l}/kT})(1-e^{-hc\nu_{J}/kT})(1+A_{1}m)^{2}.

(4)

Here,
\varepsilon_{J}
represents the statistical weight,
L_{J}
is the Hönl-London factor, and
Q_{R}
is the rotational partition function. The last term is the first-order Herman-Wallis correction, which is equal to 1 for perfect rigid rotors and
Q
branches. This correction depends on
m
, defined as
J+1
for the
R
branch, and
-J
for the
P
branch. For linear (and diatomic) molecules, the Hönl-London factors for the
P
and
R
branches of
\Sigma-\Sigma
transitions are simply given by
[
19
]

L_{J}=|m|.

(5)

For the main isotopologue (
\hphantom{{}^{\text{14}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{14}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{14}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{14}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{14}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{N}\hphantom{{}^{\text{12}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{C}\hphantom{{}^{\text{12}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{C}\hphantom{{}^{\text{14}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{14}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{14}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{14}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{14}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{N}
, with an abundance of 97.08% reported in
[
14
]
),
\varepsilon_{J}
is equal to 6 for even J values; 3 for odd J values. In practice, these statistical weights lead to an intensity alternation between two consecutive lines in the spectra. The rotational partition function appearing in equation
4
is expressed as

Q_{R}=\sum_{J=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{J}(2J+1)e^{-hcE_{l}(J)/kT},

(6)

while the vibrational partition function is given by

Q_{V}=\prod_{i=1}^{5}\left(1-e^{(-hc\nu_{0,i}/kT)}\right)^{-d_{i}}.

(7)

where
d_{i}
is the degeneracy of the fundamental band
i
, centered at
\nu_{0,i}
. The rotovibrational partition function (
Q
) is the product of
Q_{R}
and
Q_{V}
.
To derive the excitation rates, we followed the development presented in
[
30
]
.
The Einstein coefficient
A_{\nu}
(in
\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
) for a vibrational band
[
10
,
9
]
is defined by :

A_{\nu}=\frac{3.080\times 10^{-8}(T_{\nu}/300)\nu_{0}^{2}S_{\nu}Q_{V}}{\omega},

(8)

where
T_{\nu}
is the temperature (in kelvins) at which the band strength
S_{\nu}
(in
\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-2}\text{\,}{\mathrm{atm}}^{-1}
) is obtained, and
\omega
is the band degeneracy (
\omega=1
for
\nu_{3}
). The constant
3.080\ \times 10^{-8}
The
g
-factor for a fundamental band excited by a blackbody of solid angle
\Omega
at temperature
T_{b}
is then expressed
[
10
]
:

g_{\nu}=\frac{\omega\Omega}{4\pi}A_{\nu}\left(e^{hc\nu_{0}/kT_{b}}-1\right)^{-1}.

(9)

With an excitation by the Sun at 1 au,
T_{b}
=5770 K and
\Omega/4\pi=5.42\times 10^{-6}

[
30
]
.

5
Development of a cometary fluorescence model

5.1
Ground-state population and energy levels

From our analysis, we first computed the energy levels
E_{J}
of cyanogen in the ground state. As a parent species, it is assumed that the initial population of
{}{}\mathrm{C}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{N}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}
is mainly in the ground state. This is because the temperature in the coma is very low (typically a few tens of kelvins), and the sublimation of the parent species from the nucleus to the coma does not excite the molecules to higher vibrational states. Moreover, in a first good approximation, collisions are negligible because the coma is an optically thin environment. The initial population distribution in the ground state follows:

N_{J}=\varepsilon_{J}\ (2J+1)\ e^{-hcE_{J}/kT},

(11)

and the initial fractional population
f_{J}
is the ratio between
N_{J}
and the total partition function
Q
at temperature
T

f_{J}=\frac{N_{J}}{Q}.

(12)

\nu_{3}

The temperature of the coma was estimated by analyzing the intensity distribution of the
\nu_{3}

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, the strongest emission feature in the spectrum. We assumed the gas to be in local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE), and we expect the relative population levels to follow a Boltzmann law. From HITRAN database
[
14
]
and simulation of the fluorescence spectrum in PGOPHER
[
31
]
, we derived
T=56

[
2

T=60
K. The
\nu_{3}
band of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and the simulation are represented in Fig.
6
. We observe that the assumed Boltzmann distribution matches very well the cometary spectrum, confirming the LTE assumption. As
\mathrm{CO_{2}}
is a parent molecule with a short lifetime (
\tau=5\times 10^{5}
s,
[
10
]
), it is more concentrated around the nucleus, and no temperature gradient is expected. The lifetime of
\mathrm{C_{2}N_{2}}
being even shorter, the LTE assumption holds for this molecule as well.

The production rate
Q
(in molecules
\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
) can be expressed as

Q=\frac{4\pi\Delta^{2}F}{\tau ghc\nu f}G,

(25)

where
\Delta
is the geocentric distance (in meters),
F
is the flux of the emission line (in
\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}
),
\tau
is the lifetime (in seconds) of the molecule,
g
is the fluorescence efficiency (in photons per molecule
\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
) of the emission line,
hc\nu
is the energy (in joules) of a photon with a wavenumber
\nu
, and
f
is a geometric term representing the fraction of molecules sampled through the telescope aperture relative to the total number of molecules in the coma. We write
G
the growth factor of the considered species, which is the ratio between the terminal production rate and the nucleus-centered production rate
[
11
]
. In Eq.
25
,
\tau
and
g
both depend on the heliocentric distance (
R_{h}
, expressed in au). However, as
g\propto R_{h}^{-2}
(see Eq.
9
) and
\tau\propto R_{h}^{2}
, their product is independent of the heliocentric distance.

To quantify an upper limit for
Q
(
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
) in comet C/2022 E3 (ZTF), we used the Haser model
[
18
]
, which describes the distribution of chemical species in the coma. It relies on the following hypothesis:

1.

The cometary nucleus is assumed to be spherical, with a radius
R_{0}
.

2.

Parent species are isotropically ejected away from the nucleus, with a constant velocity noted
v_{0}
.

3.

The photodissociation of parent species is given by the law

n=n_{0}\ e^{-t/\tau},

(26)

where
n_{0}
is the number of molecules at time
t=0
, and
\tau
is the photodissociation lifetime of the corresponding molecule.

The density of parent species at a distance
R>R_{0}
from the nucleus is given by

n(R)=\frac{Q}{4\pi v_{0}R^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{R-R_{0}}{\gamma}\right),

(27)

where
Q
is the production rate of the parent species (expressed in molecules
\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
) and
\gamma
is the scale length, defined as
\gamma=v_{0}\tau_{0}
. The column density of molecules,
N(\rho)
, is obtained by integrating
n(R)
along the line of sight:

\displaystyle N(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{Q}{4\pi v_{0}}\exp\left(\frac{R_{0}}{\gamma}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{R^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{R}{\gamma}\right)dz

(28)

,

\displaystyle=\frac{Q}{2\pi v_{0}\rho}\exp\left(\frac{R_{0}}{\gamma}\right)\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\exp\left(-\frac{\rho}{\gamma\cos{\alpha}}\right)d\alpha,

(29)

where we changed the variable of integration for
\alpha
such that
\rho=R\cos{\alpha}
, and
dz=\rho d\alpha/\cos^{2}{\alpha}
(the change of variables is illustrated in Fig.
10
). We write
M(s)
for the total number of molecules for a specific parent species observed through a circular aperture
s
as

M(s)=2\pi\int_{R_{0}}^{R}\rho N(\rho)d\rho,

(30)

where we assumed that the aperture was centered on the nucleus.
R\approx\Delta\times s
is the physical size at the comet given the geocentric distance
\Delta
, with
s
in radian.
The total number of molecules is obtained when
s,R\to\infty
in Eq.
30
:

\displaystyle M(\infty)

\displaystyle=\frac{Q}{v_{0}}\exp{\left(\frac{R_{0}}{\gamma}\right)}\left(\gamma-\int_{0}^{R_{0}}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\exp{\left(-\frac{\rho}{\gamma\cos{\alpha}}\right)}d\alpha d\rho\right),

(31)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{Q\gamma}{v_{0}},\quad\text{since }R_{0}\ll\gamma.

(32)

For a rectangular aperture of dimensions
s_{1}
,
s_{2}
, centered on the nucleus,

M(s_{1},s_{2})=\left(4\int_{0}^{R_{2}}\int_{0}^{R_{1}}N(x,y)dxdy\right)-\left(2\pi\int_{0}^{R_{0}}\rho N(\rho)d\rho\right),

(33)

where
R_{1}\approx\Delta\times s_{1}
,
R_{2}\approx\Delta\times s_{2}
. Again, as the second term is much smaller than the first one, the total number of molecules is approximately

M(s_{1},s_{2})\approx 4\int_{0}^{R_{2}}\int_{0}^{R_{1}}N(x,y)dxdy.

(34)

The geometric factor
f

25

f=\frac{M(s_{1},s_{2})}{M(\infty)}.

(35)

This factor depends on the slit’s dimensions (
3.0^{\prime\prime}\times 3.0^{\prime\prime}

R_{0}
of the cometary nucleus (we assumed
R_{0}=2.5

[
22
]
), the geocentric distance
\Delta

\tau
at the heliocentric distance
R_{h}
(

v_{0}
of the parent species (we chose

\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}

6.3
Perspectives

The mixing ratios measured in comet 67P mentioned above correspond to a
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
/
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
\sim 3\times 10^{-6}
for this comet. Such a ratio, if similar for other comets would imply a minimum water production rate of about
6\times 10^{25}/3\times 10^{-6}\sim 2\times 10^{31}
molec.
\mathrm{s^{-1}}
as the minimum water production rate to allow detection of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
(if considering similar heliocentric and geocentric distances than the ones at the time of comet C/2022 E3 (ZTF) observations). Such a water production rate can be compared to that measured in comet C/1995 O1 Hale-Bopp of about
8.3\times 10^{30}
molec.
\mathrm{s^{-1}}

[
12
]
, i.e., of the same order of magnitude. Such values confirm that
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
could reasonably be detected by infrared spectroscopy with ground-based facilities or infrared space observatories for some comets, if they are bright enough, for small heliocentric and geocentric distances (those at the time of observations of comet C/2022 E3 (ZTF) not being truly optimal). Additionally, advancements in telescope technology will contribute to reducing uncertainties. Instruments like METIS (Mid-Infrared ELT Imager and Spectrograph), planned for installation in 2028 on the 39-meter ESO Extremely Large Telescope (ELT)
[
6
]
, will offer high-resolution spectroscopy (
R\sim 100000
) in the L/M infrared windows.

Such an instrument should make it possible to better understand the production rates of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

7
Conclusions

This study presents a fluorescence model for the
\nu_{3}
vibrational band of cyanogen (
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
), offering a robust tool for analyzing its spectral characteristics in cometary environments. By combining high-resolution laboratory spectra with detailed theoretical modeling, we have, for the first time, derived line-by-line excitation rates for this molecule. The analysis of the
\nu_{3}
band confirms that cyanogen’s fluorescence efficiency is low; however, future bright comets observed either with JWST or ground-based facilities such as METIS are expected to significantly improve the detection and characterization of cyanogen in cometary spectra.

Cyanogen is the second molecule studied as part of the COSMIC project (COmputation and Spectroscopy of Molecules in the Infrared for Comets), following chloromethane (
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{Cl}
,
[
17
]
). Although the ESA/Rosetta mission led to the discovery of approximately 40 new molecules, molecular databases remain incomplete, lacking data on many key species needed to analyze trace elements in cometary spectra effectively. This work highlights the importance of continued laboratory spectroscopy efforts to expand these databases and enhance our understanding of cometary chemistry, and of the origins of our solar system.
