THGB: predicting ligand-receptor interactions by combining tree boosting and histogram-based gradient boosting
Authors: Liqian Zhou, Jiao Song, Zejun Li, Yingxi Hu, Wenyan Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLigand-receptor interaction (LRI) prediction has great significance in biological and medical research and facilitates to infer and analyze cell-to-cell communication. However, wet experiments for new LRI discovery are costly and time-consuming. Here, we propose a computational model called THGB to uncover new LRIs. THGB first extracts feature information of Ligand-Receptor (LR) pairs using iFeature. Next, it adopts a tree boosting model to obtain representative LR features. Finally, it devises the histogram-based gradient boosting model to capture high-quality LRIs. To assess the THGB performance, we compared it with three new LRI prediction models (i.e., CellEnBoost, CellGiQ, and CellComNet) and one classical protein-protein interaction inference model PIPR. The results demonstrated that THGB achieved the best overall predictions in terms of six evaluation indictors (i.e., precision, recall, accuracy, F1-score, AUC, and AUPR). To measure the effect of LR feature selection on the prediction, THGB was compared with four feature selection methods (i.e., PCA, NMF, LLE, and TSVD). The results showed that the tree boosting model was more appropriate to select representative LR features and improve LRI prediction. We also conducted ablation study and found that THGB with feature selection outperformed THGB without feature selection. We hope that THGB is a useful tool to find new LRIs and further infer cell-to-cell communication.Keywords: Ligand-receptor interaction, Feature selection, Tree boosting, Histogram-based gradient boostingSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Classification and taxonomy, Machine learning

Introduction

Cell-Cell Communication (CCC) is involved to many important physiological and pathological signal transduction and is essential to the regulation of biological processes in multicellular organisms. CCC typically involves various protein-protein interactions, especially ligand-receptor interactions (LRIs)1–3. The rapid development in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) techniques has better investigated cellular phenotypic heterogeneity and cell type distribution within complex tissues, promoting CCC inference4–8. Moreover, comprehensively unraveling CCC network through LRIs has emerged as a research hotspot. Consequently, creating a trustworthy and well-arranged LRI resource has immensely value for CCC study9,10. Once the effects of LRI-mediated CCC on patients is decoded, therapeutics targeting CCC pathways will be one of the best options in clinical practice. Thus, numerous efforts have been focused on potential LRI selection by combining diverse biological data and machine learning or text mining techniques11.

Pan et al.12 found a few potential protein-protein interactions (PPIs) through random forest and protein sequences. Tian et al.13 predicted PPIs based on Chou’s pseudo components and wavelet denoising. Guo et al.14 forecasted PPIs by integrating different physicochemical characteristics of amino acids and autocovariance with support vector machine. Recently, deep learning has been increasingly used to PPI prediction due to powerful feature representation ability, for example, Siamese-like convolutional neural network (CNN)15, graph representation learning16, deep neural network17–20. Although many new PPIs have been discovered, numerous PPIs similar to LRIs remain unidentified and unconfirmed.

Furthermore, many researches have been devoted to acquiring LRIs by integrating current data resources and text mining techniques11,21. Ramilowski et al.22 collected 2,422 LRIs from the publicly available databases DLRP, IUPHAR, and HPMR. CellPhoneDB23 offered 1,396 LRIs involving 978 proteins derived from PPI databases UniProt, PDB, InnateDB, the IMEx Consortium, and IUPHAR. Omnipath24 consolidated 14,348 LRIs from CellPhoneDB, HPMR, Guide2Pharma, and Gene Ontology. SingleCellSignalR25 identified 3,251 LRIs by integrating PPIs from UniProt, InnateDB, I2D, IMEx, MatrixDB, MINT, IUPHAR, and IntAct. CellTalkDB4 gained human 3,398 LRIs and 2,033 mouse LRIs from the STRING database. NATMI26 accumulated 2,293 LRIs from STRING, HPRD, and CellPhoneDB. While these tools offer some LRIs, reliable and abundant LRIs aid in minimizing incorrect positive and negative prediction and enhancing the CCC inference precision. Therefore, computational LRI identification is crucial to CCC prediction11.

Although computational approaches have obtained good performance in PPI prediction, accurate prediction of LRIs is still needed10. Here, we propose THGB, a computational tool to predict LRIs by extracting the characteristics of ligands and receptors through the tree-based gradient boosting model27 and classifying unknown LRIs via the Histogram-Based Gradient Boosting28 (HGB) classifier. This study has the following three contributions:

Results

Considering that the co-expression of ligand and receptor has an important impact on cellular communication, here, we present a boosting-based ensemble learning model (THGB) for LRI discovery. THGB consists of the following three steps: (i) feature extraction. LRI features are extracted from corresponding protein sequences. (ii) Feature Selection. The tree-based boosting model is designed to select the important features of LR pair. (iii) LRI classification. The Histogram-Based Gradient Boosting (HGB) model is proposed to classify LR pairs. Experimental settings and evaluation metrics

To measure the THGB performance, we compared it with three representative LRI prediction methods (i.e., CellEnBoost21, CellGiQ29 and CellComNet11) and a classical PPI prediction model PIPR30 under 20 5-fold cross validations on four datasets. To predict new LRIs, CellEnBoost21 integrated Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)-based AdaBoost and Light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM). CellGiQ29 combined gradient boosting neural network and interpretable boosting machine. CellComNet11 ensembled heterogeneous Newton boosting machine and deep neural network. PIPR30 integrated a deep residual RCNN and Siamese network architecture. Their parameters were set to the default values.

For THGB, we set early_stopping=’auto’, loss=“log’loss”, learning_rate=0.3, max_iter=400, max_leavenodes=31, min_samples_leaf=10, and max’bins=255 on Data 1, 2, and 3. We set early_stopping=’auto’, loss=“log’loss”, learning_rate=0.1, max_iter=400, max_leavenodes=31, min_samples_leaf=10, and max’bins=255 on Data 4. Finally, six evaluation metrics, precision, recall, accuracy, F1 score, AUC, and AUPR31–33, were used to measure the model performance.

Moreover, we computed the LRI overlapping ratio between THGB and other CCC analysis tools by the Jaccard index34. As shown in Eq. (1), higher Jaccard index denote the predicted LRIs are more credible.

Performance comparison of THGB and other models

THGB was compared with CellEnBoost21, CellGiQ29, CellComNet11, and PIPR. CellEnBoost used a variety of methods to extract its biological features, including 60 natural vector features, 273 CTD features, 8,420 pseudoKNC features, and 400 monoMonoKGap features. Finally, a ligand/receptor was depicted as a 9,153-dimensional vector. CellGiQ used BioTriangle and extracted 1,890 features for each ligand/receptor based on their sequences. Consequently, each LR pair was represented as a 3,780-dimensional vector based on a concatenation operation. CellComNet extracted biological features including basic kmer, Auto-Cross Covariance, Distance-based Top-n-gram, Pseudo Amino Acid Composition (PseAAC) of Distance-Pairs and reduced alphabet scheme, Parallel Correlation PseAAC, and Series Correlation PseAAC. Finally, one LR pair was represented as a 4,576-dimensional vector. PIPR employed a deep Siamese architecture with residual RCNN and obtained latent semantic features of ligands and receptors.

LRI validation by molecular docking

In this section, we validated unknown but predicted LRIs using molecular docking. 20 LRIs were randomly selected from unknown but predicted interactions in each dataset for molecular docking. The initial 3D structures for both ligands and receptors were acquired using the ZDOCK35 online tool. Subsequently, we performed molecular docking for each unknown but predicted LRI using PDBePISA36 with the default values. Finally, we obtained the binding energy (BE, kcal/mol), interface area (IA, Å2), and hydrogen bonds (HB, Å) for each LRI. LR pairs with binding energy below -4 kcal/mol may be considered potential LRIs. The likelihoods of binding and interaction between the corresponding ligand and receptor increases when their molecular binding energy decreases. Table 4 lists the related information of each LRI with the lowest binding energy among the 20 LRIs on each dataset. The results revealed that the four LR pairs exhibited exceptionally low binding energies, suggesting they are potential LRIs. The other molecular docking results on each dataset have been listed at Tables S1-S4 in Supplementary Materials 1.

Case study

Melanoma is one severe skin cancer40. Its early-stage CCC network construction is very necessary to its treatment. Here, we analyzed its CCC between melanoma cancer cells, B cells, T cells, CAFs, endothelial cells, macrophages, and natural killer (NK) cells. To begin with, we downloaded its scRNA-seq data from the GEO database (accession code: GSE72056)41. Next, we combined the acquired scRNA-seq data to further screen known and predicted LRIs on dataset 1. For LRIs that were not expressed in human melanoma tissues, we excluded them. Consequently, 2,156 LRIs (Supplementary Material 2) were obtained and used to CCC prediction. Finally, we used the three-point estimation method3 to evaluate CCC intensity between the seven cell types described above.

We calculated and ranked CCC intensity between melanoma cells and other cell types. Table 6 gives the CCC intensity rankings between melanoma cells and other cells, the top three “outgoing” and “incoming” LRIs, and the corresponding evidence. The findings revealed that CAFs had the strongest signaling capability with melanoma cells, followed by macrophages. CAFs play a crucial role in cancer invasion42. Macrophages can promote the melanoma spread by interacting with melanoma tumor cells43. The results provided an inference to inhibit the occurrence of melanoma.

Discussion

In multicellular organisms, cellular communication mediated by LRIs is a critical regulatory event. Thus, reliable LRIs contribute to the study of cellular communication. Here, we presented a computational framework THGB to identify reliable LRIs. First, LR features, including amino acid composition (AAC), conjoint triads (CT), pseudo amino acid composition (PseAAC), composition transition and distribution (CTD), and composition associated with k-spaced amino acid pairs (CKSAAP), were extracted based on iFeature. Next, the tree-based boosting model was explored to select the representative LR features from the extracted features. Finally, an HGB model was designed to identify possible LRIs.

To verify the THGB classification performance, we conducted a series of comparative experiments. In comparison with the current three new LRI prediction models (CellEnBoost, CellGiQ, and CellComNet) and one classical deep learning-based PPI inference model PIPR, THGB achieved the optimal performance in most conditions. We also compared our proposed tree boosting-based LR feature selection method with four feature selection methods, i.e., PCA, NMF, LLE, and TSVD. The results showed that the tree boosting model was more appropriate to select representative LR features compared to other four methods. Finally, we conducted ablation study to analyze the effect of feature selection on the LRI classification accuracy. The results demonstrated that the tree boosting model could select representative LR features.

To further evaluate the reliability of the predicted LRIs, we randomly selected 20 LRIs from predictions on each dataset and conducted molecular docking for these LRIs. In addition, we counted the overlapping LRIs and Jaccard index between THGB and five classical CCC tools (CytoTalk, CellChat, CellTalkDB, NATMI, and Connectome). Finally, we used known and predicted LRIs for CCC inference within melanoma tissues and validated the inference results by comparing with four CCC analysis tools (CellPhoneDB, NATMI, iTALK, and CellChat). The results strongly confirmed the accuracy and reliability of the THGB model.

THGB achieved the best LRI identification performance, possibly due to three characteristics: (i) it extracted sufficiently abundant LR features using iFeature; (ii) The tree-based boosting model preserved essential LR features and alleviate redundant features; (iii) The HGB model had better LRI classification accuracy.

Although THGB achieved the best predictions among the current five classification algorithms, its prediction accuracy can still be further boosted. The boosting model31,32 and deep learning model33,50–52 demonstrate optimal classification performance. In the future, we will design new boosting model or deep learning model for learning more representative LR features and further improving LRI prediction. We hope that THGB contributes to the study of LRI-mediated cellular communication.

LRI identification

Each LR pair can be depicted as an H-dimensional vector after conducting feature selection. Here, we adopt the HGB model for LRI classification. The HGB model is a scikit-learn implementation of the gradient boosting similar to LightGBM28. It can accelerate the training process by using histogram approximation, which is especially suitable for large-scale data. Thus, it performs well in processing high-dimensional and large-scale data. In this tree-based model, each LR pair conducts multiple tree processing. In each tree, each LR pair is assigned a weighted leaf. Afterwards, the category of samples is predicted by summing the weights of the leaves. For multi classification problems, a tree is built for each class at each iteration, and the leaf weights are summed. Using the One to Rest method, one sample is taken as one class, and all remaining types of samples are taken as another class. The softmax normalization converts predictions into probabilities.

During building the decision trees, the model expedites node construction through a binning strategy65, which is more efficient than sorting every feature value. The algorithm generates histograms G and H including all feature bin samples, then utilizes these histograms to determine the optimal bin edge for splitting. This split is designed to maximize the gain for the left and right nodes.

To account for samples with missing value, one bin is specifically reserved for them based on missingness incorporated in attributes65. During each split, the model determines the appropriate nodes for sources with missing values. In addition, this method considers the presence or absence of data.
