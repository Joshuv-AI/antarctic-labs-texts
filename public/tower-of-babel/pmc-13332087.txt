Precision oncology in gastric cancer: navigating molecular subtypes, therapeutic targets, and future horizons
Authors: Yanyun Hong, Xiaodong Wang, Hang Yu, Xiaosun Liu, Chao Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastric cancer (GC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally and is characterized by significant inter- and intra-tumoral heterogeneity, which poses major challenges to effective treatment. Although traditional “one-size-fits-all” chemotherapy regimens have improved outcomes, the prognosis for advanced disease remains poor, necessitating a paradigm shift towards personalized medicine. This review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the current landscape of precision oncology in GC. We systematically analyze the clinical implications of major molecular classification systems, particularly The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) subtypes (EBV-positive, MSI-H, GS, and CIN), and their role in guiding therapeutic stratification. The integration of molecular profiling has revolutionized the management of GC. We discuss the evolution of targeted therapies, ranging from established standards, like HER2 inhibition, to emerging targets, including Claudin18.2 and FGFR2, highlighting their potential of overcoming resistance mechanisms. Furthermore, we evaluate the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (PD-1/PD-L1 blockade), specifically in the context of high microsatellite instability (MSI-H) and EBV-positive subtypes, where these have demonstrated robust antitumor activity. Beyond tissue-based markers, this article also explores the expanding role of liquid biopsies, including circulating tumor cells (CTCs) and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), as non-invasive tools for real-time monitoring of disease progression and therapeutic response. Precision oncology represents a transformative approach in GC, moving beyond histology to a molecularly driven treatment framework. However, realizing its full potential requires addressing challenges related to tumor heterogeneity and drug resistance. Future research must focus on validating novel biomarkers and developing synergistic combination strategies to further improve patient survival.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Precision oncology, Molecular subtypes, Targeted therapy, Immunotherapy, Liquid biopsy

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) constitutes a substantial global health challenge, representing the fifth most common malignancy and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. The latest global cancer statistics (GLOBOCAN 2020) annually attributes over one million new cases and approximately 769,000 deaths to GC [1]. The disease burden, accounting for nearly half of the global total, is disproportionately concentrated in Eastern Asia, particularly in China, Japan, and South Korea [2]. Despite gradual incidence rate declines in some Western populations, the absolute number of cases is projected to rise due to aging populations and population growth [3]. Clinically, GC is characterized by an insidious onset and aggressive biological behavior. Approximately 60% to 80% of patients are diagnosed at a locally advanced or metastatic stage, precluding the possibility of curative surgical resection [4]. For decades, the therapeutic landscape for such patients, mostly involving palliative chemotherapy regimens based on fluoropyrimidines and platinum agents, remained largely unchanged. While these treatments offer modest survival benefits, the median overall survival (OS) for patients with metastatic disease is 10–12 months, with a 5-year survival rate of < 10% [5]. This bleak prognosis underscores the urgent necessity to transition from a “one-size-fits-all” empiric approach to a more refined, biomarker-driven strategy, known as precision oncology.

The fundamental obstacle to improving GC outcomes lies in its profound inter- and intra-tumoral heterogeneity. GC is not a single disease entity but a complex collection of molecularly distinct subtypes, each driven by unique genomic alterations [6]. The traditional histological classifications, including the Lauren classification (intestinal and diffuse types), are insufficient for guiding modern targeted therapies. A paradigm shift occurred in 2014 when The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) research network published the comprehensive molecular characterization. This landmark study stratified gastric adenocarcinomas into four clinically relevant molecular subtypes: Epstein-Barr virus-positive (EBV+), microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H), genomically stable (GS), and chromosomal instability (CIN) [7]. Shortly thereafter, the Asian Cancer Research Group (ACRG) proposed a complementary classification system based on gene expression profiles, further validating the molecular diversity of GC [8]. These classifications, which provided the first roadmap for precision medicine, identified that EBV+ tumors harbor extreme DNA hypermethylation and PIK3CA mutations; MSI-H tumors are characterized by a high tumor mutational burden (TMB-H) and immune activation; CIN tumors are enriched for receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) amplifications; and GS tumors, often of the diffuse histology, lack obvious targetable drivers [7, 9].

Guided by this molecular understanding, the past decade has witnessed the evolution of targeted therapies, from a single success story to a rapidly expanding arsenal. The initial breakthrough, which came with the ToGA trial in 2010, established trastuzumab (anti-human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 [HER2]) combined with chemotherapy as the standard of care for HER2-positive advanced GC [10]. However, HER2-positive cases comprise only 15–20% of the patient population, leaving the vast majority without targeted options. However, recent years have marked the “golden age” of novel targets. Most notably, Claudin 18.2 (CLDN18.2) emerged as a major therapeutic target. In 2024, the FDA and other regulatory bodies approved zolbetuximab, a first-in-class monoclonal antibody targeting CLDN18.2, when the pivotal phase 3 SPOTLIGHT and GLOW trials demonstrated significant survival benefits in CLDN18.2-positive, HER2-negative patients [11, 12]. Furthermore, the fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (FGFR2) pathway has gained renewed attention. In 2025, the phase 3 FORTITUDE-101 trial reported positive topline results for bemarituzumab (anti-FGFR2b) as first-line treatment, validating FGFR2b overexpression as another actionable biomarker [13, 14].

Parallel to targeted therapy, immunotherapy has revolutionized the management of GC. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly PD-1/PD-L1 blockers, have become first-line treatments. The landmark CheckMate-649 and KEYNOTE-859 trials demonstrated that adding nivolumab or pembrolizumab to chemotherapy significantly extends survival, particularly in patients with a high PD-L1 combined positive score (CPS) [15, 16]. The benefits are even more pronounced in the MSI-H/dMMR subgroup, where ICIs can induce durable, long-term remission [17]. Moreover, immunotherapy usage is increasingly expanding into earlier disease stages. In late 2025, durvalumab became the first immunotherapy approved for perioperative treatment in resectable GC based on the MATTERHORN trial, signaling a new era of “total neoadjuvant” approaches [18]. Despite these successes, response rates vary, and resistance, both primary and acquired, remains a significant hurdle, often attributed to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) [19].

To fully achieve the promise of precision oncology, static tissue biopsies are considered insufficient, due to temporal tumor evolution and spatial heterogeneity. This has catalyzed the development of liquid biopsy technologies. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and circulating tumor cells (CTCs) offer a non-invasive window into the dynamic molecular landscape of the tumor. Emerging evidence in 2025 suggests that ctDNA analysis can guide targeted therapy selection and serve as a sensitive marker for minimal residual disease (MRD), potentially sparing patients from unnecessary adjuvant chemotherapy or alerting clinicians to early recurrence [20, 21].

Overall, GC treatment is undergoing a historic transformation driven by molecular profiling. This review aims to provide a comprehensive update on the state of precision oncology in GC. We synthesize the latest evidence on molecular subtypes; critically evaluate clinical data on emerging agents targeting HER2, CLDN18.2, and FGFR2; discuss the expanding role of immunotherapy from metastatic to perioperative settings; and explore the future utility of liquid biopsy in overcoming tumor heterogeneity.

The landscape of molecular classification in GC

TCGA and ACRG classifications

With the advent of high-throughput sequencing technologies, GC classification has progressively shifted from traditional histopathology, including the Lauren classification, towards molecular pathology. Currently, two molecular classification systems are considered the most authoritative internationally: TCGA and ACRG. Although these systems were established based on different population cohorts, they exhibit high complementarity in revealing GC heterogeneity and guiding precision treatment [7, 8].

The TCGA classification, based on multi-omics analysis of 295 patients with gastric adenocarcinoma (predominantly Western), categorizes GC into four molecular subtypes: EBV, MSI, GS, and CIN [7]. The core advantage of the TCGA system lies in its elucidation of distinct molecular drivers, which directly inform targeted therapeutic selection. For instance, the EBV subtype (approximately 9%) exhibits characteristic PIK3CA mutations, extreme DNA hypermethylation, and PD-L1/L2 amplification, suggesting that these patients can potentially benefit from ICIs [7, 9]. The CIN subtype (approximately 50%) is characterized by extensive somatic copy number alterations (SCNA) and TP53 mutations, often enriched with RTK amplifications (e.g., HER2, EGFR, VEGFR), making it the primary target for current receptor-targeted therapies [22, 23]. In contrast, the GS subtype (approximately 20%) generally corresponds to diffuse-type GC pathologically; it lacks obvious targetable driver mutations but is often associated with RHOA mutations or CDH1 alterations and shows poor sensitivity to conventional chemotherapy, representing a significant challenge in current clinical practice [24, 25].

The ACRG classification, established based on gene expression profiling of 300 Korean GC patients, is more reflective of the clinical characteristics of Asian populations. This system divides GC into four subtypes: MSI, MSS/TP53+, MSS/TP53-, and MSS/EMT (Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition) [8]. Unlike TCGA, which focuses heavily on identifying “therapeutic targets,” the primary clinical value of the ACRG classification lies in its superior prognostic stratification and prediction of recurrence patterns. ACRG studies revealed that prognosis was best among patients with the MSI subtype and worst among those with the MSS/EMT subtype [2, 8]. The MSS/EMT subtype is typically diagnosed at a later stage, presents with diffuse histology, and has a significantly higher rate of peritoneal metastasis compared to other subtypes [8]. This classification suggests that for MSS/EMT patients, more intensive postoperative monitoring may be required for peritoneal recurrence or consideration of strategies, including hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC).

Despite the differences in nomenclature, the TCGA and ACRG classifications share significant biological correspondence. The TCGA-MSI subtype overlaps highly with the ACRG-MSI subtype, both indicating a favorable prognosis and sensitivity to immunotherapy; the TCGA-GS subtype largely corresponds to the ACRG-MSS/EMT subtype, both pointing towards poor-prognosis diffuse-type GC [9, 26]. However, they emphasize different clinical applications: TCGA is better suited for guiding drug selection (e.g., HER2 amplification for trastuzumab, MSI/EBV for pembrolizumab), while ACRG is advantageous for predicting recurrence risk and OS [2, 22] (summarized in Table 1). A major bottleneck currently limiting the routine clinical adoption of these classifications is their reliance on whole-genome sequencing or complex RNA sequencing. Recent research has focused on developing simplified surrogates based on immunohistochemistry (IHC) and in situ hybridization (ISH), including using EBER for EBV, MMR proteins for MSI, and substituting p53 IHC for sequencing. These surrogate markers facilitate the implementation of “molecular stratification” in routine pathological practice, thereby translating precision diagnosis and treatment into clinical reality (Fig. 1) [27].

The landscape of molecular subtypes in gastric cancer

Characteristics by subtype

The EBV+ subtype, accounting for approximately 9% of GCs, constitutes a distinct clinical and molecular entity driven by viral oncogenesis. This subtype, biologically defined by extreme DNA hypermethylation (CpG island methylator phenotype, CIMP), is frequently accompanied by specific somatic mutations, most notably in PIK3CA and ARID1A. These provide potential avenues for targeted intervention [28, 29]. A critical characteristic of EBV+ tumors is their “immune-hot” microenvironment. Transcriptomic profiling has revealed robust infiltration of immune cells, particularly CD8 + T cells and macrophages, alongside significant overexpression of immune checkpoint molecules, including PD-L1 and PD-L2 [23]. This heightened immunogenicity renders the EBV+ subtype particularly sensitive to ICIs. Despite possessing aggressive histological features, the susceptibility of this subtype to immune surveillance and immunotherapy allows more favorable prognosis than with other subtypes [23, 28].

The MSI-H subtype arises from defects in the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) system, leading to a hypermutated phenotype with a TMB-H [30]. This genomic instability results in the continuous generation of immunogenic neoantigens, which trigger a strong host immune response characterized by the enrichment of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and activated T-cell signatures [31]. Consequently, MSI-H tumors are considered the most immunogenic subtype of GC. Clinically, patients with MSI-H tumors typically exhibit a better prognosis compared to those with microsatellite stable (MSS) disease [32]. Crucially, this subtype has been established as a robust predictive biomarker for immunotherapy efficacy, with patients demonstrating durable responses to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade even in advanced stages, necessitating its routine testing in clinical practice [30, 31].

The CIN subtype, the largest subgroup, comprise nearly 50% of GC cases. It is characterized by marked genomic instability, including extensive SCNAs and aneuploidy [33]. At the molecular level, CIN tumors are frequently driven by TP53 mutations and focal amplification of RTKs, HER2 (ERBB2), FGFR2, MET, and EGFR [33, 34]. This enrichment of actionable kinase targets positions the CIN subtype as the primary beneficiary of current targeted therapies. For instance, the efficacy of HER2-targeted agents (e.g., trastuzumab) occurs predominantly in this molecular context [34]. However, despite the availability of targeted options, CIN tumors often display an aggressive clinical course with inherent resistance to standard cytotoxic chemotherapy, highlighting the need for continuous development of novel inhibitors targeting bypass signaling pathways [33].

The GS subtype, accounting for approximately 20% of cases, lacks significant chromosomal aberrations or hypermutation, presenting a “quiet” genomic profile [35]. Histologically, this subtype is strongly associated with the diffuse GC subtype (according to Lauren classification), which features poorly differentiated cells and a lack of intercellular cohesion [36]. Molecular drivers in GS tumors are distinct and often include mutations in RHOA, CDH1 (E-cadherin) alterations, and CLDN18-ARHGAP fusions, rather than the kinase amplifications seen in CIN [35]. Clinically, the GS subtype poses a significant therapeutic challenge, is generally associated with the poorest prognosis among the four subtypes and exhibits limited sensitivity to conventional chemotherapy and current targeted therapies [24, 36]. The lack of effective therapeutic targets for GS tumors remains a critical unmet need in precision oncology.

Targeted therapies from standard of care to emerging stars

The cornerstone and evolution

Trastuzumab

The identification of HER2 amplification marked the inception of precision oncology in GC. HER2, an RTK, is overexpressed in approximately 20% of gastric adenocarcinomas, delineating a critical molecular subgroup suitable for targeted intervention [34, 37]. For over a decade, treatment for this population has relied on trastuzumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits HER2 signaling and induces antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) [37]. The pivotal ToGA trial established trastuzumab combined with cisplatin and fluoropyrimidine as the standard first-line regimen, demonstrating a significant improvement in OS compared to chemotherapy alone [38]. This milestone validated the efficacy of molecularly targeted agents in GC, shifting the paradigm from empiric cytotoxic treatment to biomarker-driven strategies [34], as shown in Fig. 2.

However, the clinical trajectory of HER2-targeted therapies in gastric cancer has not been without significant setbacks, highlighting the complexities of this disease compared to HER2-positive breast cancer. Several ambitious phase 3 clinical trials investigating alternative or intensified anti-HER2 strategies have failed to demonstrate an overall survival benefit. For instance, the JACOB trial, which evaluated the addition of dual HER2 blockade (pertuzumab) to trastuzumab and chemotherapy in the first-line setting, narrowly failed to reach statistical significance for its primary OS endpoint, despite a numerical improvement. Similarly, investigations into the small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitor lapatinib yielded negative results in both the first-line (TRIO-013/LOGiC trial) and second-line (TyTAN trial) settings.

Furthermore, while the ToGA trial established the current paradigm, retrospective subgroup analyses reveal critical uncertainties regarding marginal benefits. The survival advantage of trastuzumab was predominantly driven by the highly amplified IHC 3 + subgroup. In contrast, patients with IHC 2+/FISH+ tumors exhibited marginal and sometimes clinically insignificant benefits. This highlights an ongoing clinical uncertainty and the imperative need for more refined patient stratification beyond basic HER2 positivity, emphasizing that binary classification systems may oversimplify the continuous spectrum of HER2 biology.

Mechanisms of resistance

Despite the established benefits of trastuzumab, most patients eventually experience disease progression due to the emergence of resistance. Resistance mechanisms in HER2-positive GC are complex and multifaceted, involving both intrinsic and acquired factors [39]. A primary mechanism involves activation of bypass signaling pathways, including MET or FGFR2 amplification and alterations in downstream effectors like the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, which sustain tumor proliferation despite HER2 blockade [34, 39]. Additionally, GC temporal and spatial heterogeneity poses a significant challenge. HER2 expression can be lost after treatment or vary significantly within the same tumor mass, rendering antibodies relying on uniform surface antigen expression ineffective [31]. These biological hurdles highlight the critical limitations of first-generation HER2-targeted therapies and the necessity for novel agents capable of circumventing these resistance pathways [38, 39].

Antibody-drug conjugates (T-DXd)

To address the limitations of traditional monoclonal antibodies, the field has evolved towards developing antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), and with trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) emerging as a transformative breakthrough [37, 38]. T-DXd comprises a humanized anti-HER2 antibody linked to a potent topoisomerase I inhibitor payload via a cleavable linker. Unlike trastuzumab, T-DXd does not rely solely on signal inhibition; instead, it delivers a cytotoxic payload directly into the cancer cell [38]. Crucially, T-DXd possesses a potent “bystander effect,” where the membrane-permeable payload can diffuse into neighboring tumor cells, effectively killing those with low or heterogeneous HER2 expression that would otherwise escape trastuzumab therapy [31, 38]. T-DXd is a highly effective option for patients who have progressed on prior HER2-targeted regimens, fundamentally reshaping the treatment algorithm for refractory disease [37, 39] (summarized in Table 2).

Claudin18.2: the new frontier

For patients with HER2-negative GC who constitute approximately 80% of the population, targeted therapeutic options have historically been nonexistent. This treatment void was filled validating CLDN18.2 as a novel and actionable biomarker. CLDN18.2 is a tight junction protein normally restricted to the differentiated epithelial cells of the gastric mucosa; however, it is exposed and often overexpressed in gastric adenocarcinoma due to cell polarity disruption during carcinogenesis [40]. Approximately 38% of patients with GC exhibit high CLDN18.2 expression (defined as ≥ 75% of tumor cells with moderate-to-strong membranous staining), making it a substantial targetable subgroup [11]. Unlike HER2, which acts as an oncogenic driver, CLDN18.2 serves primarily as a tumor-specific surface antigen, providing an ideal docking site for monoclonal antibodies to mediate immune destruction without affecting healthy tissues [40, 41].

Zolbetuximab (formerly IMAB362) mechanism and efficacy

Zolbetuximab is a first-in-class chimeric IgG1 monoclonal antibody specifically designed to target CLDN18.2 extracellular domain. Its primary mechanism of action involves binding to CLDN18.2 on the tumor cell surface, thereby triggering potent immune-mediated cell death through ADCC and complement-dependent cytotoxicity [41]. Zolbetuximab clinical efficacy was firmly established in two landmark phase 3 trials: SPOTLIGHT and GLOW. The SPOTLIGHT trial evaluated zolbetuximab in combination with mFOLFOX6 (oxaliplatin, leucovorin, and fluorouracil) in the first-line setting. Results demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in the risk of progression or death (hazard ratio [HR] 0.75) and a meaningful improvement in median OS, compared to chemotherapy alone (18.23 months vs. 15.54 months) [11]. Complementing these findings, the GLOW trial assessed zolbetuximab combined with CAPOX (capecitabine and oxaliplatin). This study similarly achieved its primary endpoint, showing superior progression-free survival (PFS) and an improved median OS of 14.39 months versus 12.16 months in the placebo arm [12].

The successes of the SPOTLIGHT and GLOW trials mark a historic milestone, establishing zolbetuximab plus chemotherapy as the new global standard of care for patients with CLDN18.2-positive, HER2-negative locally advanced or metastatic GC [12, 42]. These results led to regulatory approvals in major markets, including Japan and the United States, as the first successful targeted therapy approval for this patient population in over a decade. Current clinical guidelines now mandate CLDN18.2 status testing alongside HER2, PD-L1, and MMR/MSI testing, at the time of diagnosis, to optimize first-line treatment selection [40, 42]. The robust survival benefits across diverse geographic regions and chemotherapy regimens underscore the transformative potential of targeting CLDN18.2 in precision oncology (summarized in Table 3).

Despite these landmark approvals, the clinical application of CLDN18.2 inhibitors faces critical uncertainties, particularly regarding patient selection and the extent of marginal benefits. The stringent cutoff for positivity utilized in both the SPOTLIGHT and GLOW trials, strictly defined as ≥ 75% of tumor cells exhibiting moderate-to-strong membranous staining, intrinsically excludes a substantial proportion of patients with intermediate or heterogeneous CLDN18.2 expression. For this “low-to-moderate” expressor subgroup, the therapeutic benefit of zolbetuximab remains entirely unproven, representing a significant clinical gap.

Moreover, there is ongoing debate regarding the optimal treatment sequencing for patients who present with overlapping biomarkers, such as simultaneous CLDN18.2 positivity and high PD-L1 CPS or MSI-H status. Subgroup analyses from recent trials have not yet definitively established whether dual-targeted regimens, immunotherapy, or sequential monotherapies offer the best survival advantage in these overlapping subpopulations. This uncertainty underscores the necessity for multi-marker stratification algorithms rather than relying solely on single-target evaluations.

Angiogenesis inhibition: VEGF/VEGFR inhibitors

Tumor-induced angiogenesis (the formation of new blood vessels) is a fundamental hallmark of GC progression, driven largely by the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling pathway. While initial attempts to target the ligand VEGF-A with bevacizumab failed to improve survival in GC (AVAGAST trial), targeting the receptor itself has proven highly effective. Ramucirumab is a fully human IgG1 monoclonal antibody that binds to the extracellular domain of VEGFR-2, preventing the binding of VEGF ligands (VEGF-A, VEGF-C, and VEGF-D) and blocking receptor activation [43]. Ramucirumab efficacy was established in two landmark phase 3 trials. The REGARD trial demonstrated that ramucirumab monotherapy significantly improved median OS (5.2 vs. 3.8 months) and PFS in patients with advanced GC progressing after first-line chemotherapy [43]. Subsequently, the RAINBOW trial evaluated ramucirumab and paclitaxel combination and found even more robust survival benefit (median OS 9.6 vs. 7.4 months) compared to paclitaxel alone [44]. Based on these pivotal results, ramucirumab (either as monotherapy or in combination with paclitaxel) has become the global standard of care as second-line treatment.

For patients who progress beyond second-line therapy, options remain limited. Apatinib (rivoceranib) is a novel small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) that selectively targets the intracellular ATP-binding site of VEGFR-2, thereby inhibiting downstream signaling involved in endothelial cell proliferation and migration [45]. Unlike monoclonal antibodies, apatinib is administered orally and has shown potency in heavily pretreated populations. A randomized phase 3 trial conducted in China demonstrated significantly extended median OS (6.5 vs. 4.7 months) and PFS with apatinib compared to placebo in patients with chemotherapy-refractory advanced GC [45]. While currently approved and widely used in China as third-line treatment, its global application is being further explored in combination strategies.

Beyond their direct anti-angiogenic effects (inducing tumor hypoxia and starvation), both ramucirumab and apatinib are critical in modulating the TME. VEGF blockade can induce “vascular normalization,” which reduces interstitial fluid pressure and improves the delivery of cytotoxic drugs [46]. Furthermore, the VEGF pathway contributes to immunosuppression by inhibiting dendritic cell maturation and promoting regulatory T cell (Treg) infiltration. Consequently, combining angiogenesis inhibitors with ICIs (including PD-1 blockade) represents a promising “new frontier.” This dual blockade can reprogram the immunosuppressive TME into an immunostimulatory one, potentially overcoming resistance to monotherapy immunotherapies [46].

Other potential targets: FGFR2b, c-MET, and DDR pathways

Beyond HER2 and Claudin18.2, the genomic landscape of GC has revealed FGFR2 as a highly promising therapeutic target. Next-generation sequencing has facilitated identification of FGFR2 amplification or overexpression in a specific subset of patients, distinguishing it as an actionable molecular driver [33]. Current research is focused on “deciphering the FGFR2 code,” particularly targeting the FGFR2b isoform, which is predominantly expressed in epithelial cells. Innovative targeted therapies, including monoclonal antibodies and TKIs, are being developed to disrupt this signaling pathway [47]. These agents aim to arrest tumor growth and metastasis in patients who are negative for other major markers, further refining the precision oncology algorithm.

The translation of FGFR inhibition into clinical practice, however, has historically been fraught with significant trial failures and toxicological challenges. Early-phase trials utilizing non-selective, pan-FGFR TKIs frequently resulted in excessive off-target toxicities, such as severe hyperphosphatemia, and yielded disappointing efficacy, leading to the discontinuation of several promising agents in GC. While the isoform-selective monoclonal antibody bemarituzumab has reinvigorated the field, its efficacy appears highly dependent on precise expression thresholds. Subgroup analyses from the FIGHT trial indicated that survival benefits were predominantly observed in patients with substantial FGFR2b overexpression (e.g., ≥ 10% of tumor cells showing staining), leaving considerable clinical uncertainty regarding the utility and marginal benefits for patients with lower or highly heterogeneous expression patterns. This highlights a persistent challenge in precision oncology: defining the exact molecular boundary where the risk-to-benefit ratio justifies targeted intervention.

c-MET proto-oncogene (encoding the hepatocyte growth factor receptor) represents another critical tyrosine kinase target. Similar to FGFR2, MET alterations, including amplification and overexpression, have been identified through advanced molecular profiling as actionable targets [33]. MET clinical relevance extends beyond its role as a primary oncogenic driver, as it is frequently implicated in acquired resistance to HER2-targeted therapies. MET amplification functions as a bypass signaling route, activating downstream survival pathways to evade HER2 blockade [34]. This biological insight suggests that dual inhibition of HER2 and MET may overcome resistance and improve outcomes in refractory cases.

Targeting the DNA damage response (DDR) machinery offers a synthetic lethality approach, particularly for tumors with specific genetic deficits. Molecular subtyping has highlighted mutations in ARID1A, a key component of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, which are frequently observed in EBV-positive GCs [29]. ARID1A deficiency impairs DNA repair mechanisms, potentially rendering these tumors vulnerable to PARP or ATR inhibitors. Additionally, epigenetic silencing of the MLH1 promoter serves as a unique phenotype of MSI, representing a major defect in the MMR system [48]. These DDR-related alterations not only define specific molecular subtypes but also expose therapeutic vulnerabilities that can be exploited by emerging DNA-repair-targeting agents.

Immunotherapy: reshaping the treatment paradigm

PD-L1 expression and the complexities of CPS

For patients lacking MSI-H status, PD-L1 expression evaluated via the Combined Positive Score (CPS) serves as the primary, albeit highly complex, biomarker for guiding immunotherapy. While it is generally accepted that higher PD-L1 expression correlates with enhanced efficacy from the addition of PD-1 inhibitors to chemotherapy, treating CPS as a simple binary threshold fundamentally oversimplifies its context-dependent and trial-specific nature. The predictive value of CPS is intrinsically tied to the specific assay utilized and the trial design. For example, the CheckMate-649 trial (utilizing the 28 − 8 pharmDx assay) demonstrated a clear overall survival benefit for nivolumab plus chemotherapy, primarily driven by the CPS ≥ 5 subgroup. Conversely, the KEYNOTE-859 trial (utilizing the 22C3 pharmDx assay) established efficacy for pembrolizumab starting at a lower threshold of CPS ≥ 1. This assay variability, combined with distinct scoring interpretations by pathologists, introduces significant clinical challenges in harmonizing PD-L1 evaluation across different therapeutic platforms. Furthermore, it is critical to acknowledge the continuous, rather than dichotomous, predictive value of CPS. Clinical benefits from immune checkpoint blockade exhibit a positive, linear correlation with increasing CPS values. Patients with a CPS ≥ 10 derive profound, unequivocal survival advantages, whereas the benefit gradient gradually diminishes as the score decreases. This biological continuum dictates that a rigid cutoff may inappropriately exclude patients who could experience marginal but meaningful benefits, or conversely, overtreat those with border-line positivity who might not respond. Consequently, the clinical utility of ICIs in patients with low PD-L1 expression (CPS < 5 or CPS < 1) remains a subject of intense controversy and clinical uncertainty. Retrospective subgroup analyses from major phase 3 trials suggest that the addition of PD-1 inhibitors provides negligible to no survival benefit for the CPS < 1 population, and highly questionable marginal gains for the CPS 1–4 subgroup, often failing to outweigh the risks of immune-related adverse events. This uncertainty has prompted divergent regulatory responses globally; for instance, while some agencies maintain broad approvals, others have formally restricted the first-line use of pembrolizumab and nivolumab to tumors expressing CPS ≥ 1 and CPS ≥ 5, respectively. Navigating this clinical “grey zone” requires rigorous, individualized risk-benefit assessments and highlights the urgent imperative to discover composite biomarkers that integrate TMB, spatial T-cell profiling, and transcriptomic signatures to better identify true responders within the low PD-L1 subgroup (summarized in Table 4).

The role of TME

The “Hot” vs. “Cold” Tumor Dichotomy: The immune microenvironment in GC plays a critical role in determining patient prognosis and therapeutic response. It functions as a complex network composed of immune cells, stromal components, and the extracellular matrix [49]. A fundamental concept in immuno-oncology is the distinction between “hot” (inflamed) and “cold” (non-inflamed) tumors, a classification that largely dictates the efficacy of ICIs. “Hot” tumors are characterized by a high density of TILs, particularly CD8 + cytotoxic T cells, alongside macrophages and dendritic cells [49]. A high infiltration of CD8 + T cells within the TME is strongly associated with improved survival rates, reflecting a robust pre-existing anti-tumor immune response [49]. Specific molecular subtypes, including the MSI-H group, typically exhibit this phenotype; their high mutational burden generates abundant neoantigens that recruit CD8 + T cells, enhancing the potential for successful immunotherapy outcomes [32]. Furthermore, the presence of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) within these inflamed environments is correlated with favorable immune responses, highlighting the importance of spatial immune organization in sustaining anti-tumor immunity [49].

Mechanisms of the “cold” phenotype

In contrast, “cold” tumors, often exemplified by the MSS subtype, display an immunosuppressive or “immune-desert” landscape that hinders effective treatment [49]. These tumors are characterized by the exclusion of effector T cells or the dominance of immunosuppressive cell populations, including Tregs and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [50]. This immunosuppressive milieu leads to an immune-excluded phenotype, where immune cells are restricted to the stroma and physically prevented from penetrating tumor islets [50]. Additionally, the TME of cold tumors is often enriched with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and dense extracellular matrix components, which act as physical barriers to immune infiltration and facilitate tumor progression and metastasis [51]. These immunosuppressive factors are often linked to poor prognosis and intrinsic resistance to checkpoint blockade therapies [49, 50]. Consequently, a major focus of current research is developing combinatorial strategies, including combining immunotherapy with anti-angiogenic agents or chemotherapy, to remodel the TME, effectively converting “cold” tumors into “hot” ones to restore therapeutic sensitivity [49].

Combination strategies

The combination of ICIs with conventional chemotherapy has emerged as a cornerstone strategy to enhance antitumor immunity. The mechanistic rationale lies in the ability of cytotoxic chemotherapy to induce immunogenic cell death, releasing tumor antigens and enhancing tumor visibility for the immune system. This synergy has been clinically validated; combining immunotherapy with chemotherapy yields significantly better clinical outcomes than monotherapy approaches, particularly in advanced GC where the TME plays a critical role in therapeutic resistance [52]. Addition of PD-1 inhibitors, including nivolumab, to chemotherapy regimens results in higher objective response rates (ORR) and prolonged PFS compared to chemotherapy alone [53]. Furthermore, this synergistic benefit extends to the neoadjuvant setting, where recent retrospective analyses suggest that ICIs improve the pathological response rates of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, potentially offering a curative advantage for locally advanced disease [54].

Dual targeting of oncogenic drivers and immune checkpoints is a promising frontier in precision oncology. Ongoing clinical trials are actively exploring the combination of HER2-targeted therapies with ICIs to address the limitations of single-agent treatments [39]. The biological premise is that HER2 inhibition may modulate the immune microenvironment, potentially increasing T-cell infiltration and PD-L1 expression, thus sensitizing tumors to checkpoint blockade. Research into the immune microenvironment of HER2-overexpressing GC supports this, suggesting that the interplay between HER2 signaling and immune cells can be exploited for therapeutic gain [55]. By blocking tumor growth signals and activating immunity, this strategy aims to overcome the heterogeneity and resistance mechanisms seen with HER2-targeted monotherapy.

Beyond combining ICIs with cytotoxic or targeted agents, growing interest lies in dual immunotherapy and novel combinations to overcome resistance. Immunotherapy resistance remains a major challenge, often driven by complex immune evasion mechanisms within the TME [56]. To counter this, “innovative treatment modalities” are being investigated, including combinations that target non-redundant immune checkpoints or modulate the stromal environment. These advancements support regimens that synergistically improve treatment efficacy by remodeling the immunosuppressive TME [49]. By integrating these diverse modalities, clinicians aim to construct a multi-faceted attack on the tumor, offering new hope for patients unresponsive to standard single-agent checkpoint inhibition.

Precision diagnostics and monitoring

Tissue biomarkers: standards and clinical utility

The identification of predictive biomarkers is fundamental to the implementation of precision oncology in GC, transforming treatment from empiric chemotherapy to molecularly guided strategies [33] (summarized in Table 5). HER2 status remains the primary determinant for targeted therapy selection in the first-line setting. HER2, a receptor tyrosine kinase, is overexpressed in approximately 20% of gastric adenocarcinomas, defining the population eligible for trastuzumab and emerging antibody-drug conjugates [37]. Standard testing involves IHC, with fluorescence ISH (FISH) required for equivocal (IHC 2+) cases. Alongside HER2, PD-L1 expression has become a critical biomarker for stratifying patients for immunotherapy. The Combined Positive Score (CPS), which evaluates PD-L1 staining on both tumor and immune cells relative to the total number of tumor cells, is the standard metric. Clinical evidence indicates that patients with a CPS ≥ 5 derive significant survival benefits from the addition of PD-1 inhibitors to chemotherapy compared to those with low expression [57]. However, the predictive value of PD-L1 remains nuanced, as some patients with low or negative expression may still respond, and the optimal cut-off values can vary between different checkpoint inhibitors [58]. A major diagnostic hurdle is the lack of perfect interchangeability among companion diagnostic assays. The 22C3 pharmDx assay (linked to pembrolizumab) and the 28 − 8 pharmDx assay (linked to nivolumab) exhibit differing sensitivities and staining characteristics. Although harmonization studies are ongoing, the discordance in scoring, particularly around the critical clinical decision points of CPS 1 and CPS 5, can lead to discordant treatment recommendations for the same patient. Therefore, comprehensive pathology reports must explicitly state the specific assay antibody clone utilized, and clinicians must interpret the CPS value strictly within the context of its corresponding clinical trial data to mitigate the risk of therapeutic misallocation.

The CLDN18.2 has recently emerged as a significant biomarker, necessitating its inclusion in routine diagnostic panels. CLDN18.2 is a tight junction protein predominantly expressed in GC tissues but minimally present in healthy tissues, making it a highly specific therapeutic target [40]. Unlike HER2, which drives tumorigenesis, CLDN18.2 functions primarily as a tumor-specific surface antigen. Recent clinical trials have validated its utility, where positivity is typically defined as moderate-to-strong membranous staining in ≥ 75% of tumor cells. The ability of monoclonal antibodies targeting CLDN18.2 to selectively eliminate cancer cells while sparing normal tissues underscores the potential for improved therapeutic indices and reduced systemic toxicity [40].

MSI/MMR testing for MSI or MMR protein deficiency is now mandatory for all newly diagnosed GC. The MSI-H subtype, characterized by a TMB-H, represents a distinct biological cohort with favorable prognosis and exceptional sensitivity to ICIs [30]. These tumors exhibit a robust endogenous immune response due to the generation of immunogenic neoantigens. As clinical studies and meta-analyses demonstrated, patients with MSI-H exhibit superior OS rates and durable responses to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade compared to their counterparts with MSS [59]. Consequently, MSI-H status serves as the strongest predictive biomarker for immunotherapy efficacy, potentially justifying chemotherapy-free approaches in select cases.

Liquid biopsy: beyond CTCs

CTCs as prognostic indicators. CTCs, shed from primary or metastatic tumors into the bloodstream, have emerged as a critical non-invasive biomarker for prognostic assessment in GC. The presence of CTCs in peripheral blood is strongly correlated with advanced tumor stages, lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis, reflecting the aggressive biological behavior of the tumor [60]. A comprehensive meta-analysis has quantified this impact, revealing that CTC-positive patients exhibit significantly shorter OS and PFS compared to their CTC-negative counterparts [61]. Furthermore, technological advancements have enabled the identification of CTCs with distinct phenotypic characteristics, including stem cell-like properties, which are associated with increased metastatic potential and resistance to therapy [60]. Consequently, the detection and monitoring of CTCs allow for a real-time assessment of disease status, enabling clinicians to stratify patients based on risk profiles and potentially identify recurrence earlier than conventional imaging methods [62].

ctDNA current clinical utility in advanced disease

Currently, the established clinical application of ctDNA in gastric cancer is primarily restricted to the advanced and metastatic settings. Comprehensive genomic profiling via ctDNA is increasingly utilized as a complementary diagnostic tool, or a viable alternative, when standard tissue biopsy is unfeasible, poses significant clinical risk, or yields insufficient material. In routine clinical practice, validated ctDNA assays are employed to non-invasively detect actionable targets—such as HER2 or FGFR2 amplifications and MSI status—to guide the selection of first-line therapies. Furthermore, ctDNA analysis is currently an invaluable real-time monitoring tool for identifying acquired resistance mechanisms, such as secondary mutations in the kinase domains, thereby enabling clinicians to dynamically pivot targeted regimens upon disease progression [63].

Investigational perspectives: MRD and ctDNA-guided strategies

Conversely, the application of ctDNA for Minimal Residual Disease (MRD) detection in the localized or perioperative setting remains strictly investigational, albeit representing a transformative future perspective. The integration of advanced molecular profiling techniques, including next-generation sequencing (NGS) of liquid biopsies, has facilitated the identification of tumor-specific genomic alterations that persist after curative-intent surgery. However, translating this detection into actionable, ctDNA-guided treatment strategies is not yet the standard of care. Ongoing prospective clinical trials are rigorously evaluating whether patients with a ctDNA-negative status post-resection can safely be spared the toxicities of adjuvant chemotherapy (treatment de-escalation). Simultaneously, studies are investigating whether ctDNA-positive patients require intensified or novel adjuvant regimens (treatment escalation) to prevent radiological relapse. Until these randomized controlled trials yield definitive overall survival data, ctDNA MRD monitoring, while carrying profound prognostic weight, should not yet routinely dictate or alter adjuvant treatment decisions outside the framework of a clinical trial.

Emerging frontiers: exosomes and multi-omics

The scope of liquid biopsy is further expanding to include the analysis of exosomes and the application of multi-omics integration. Exosomes, small extracellular vesicles containing proteins, RNA, and DNA, are pivotal in intercellular communication and modulation of the TME. Concurrently, the integration of multi-omics data, combining genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic profiles, is revolutionizing the identification of prognostic signatures. For instance, recent studies utilizing machine learning on multi-omics data have revealed distinct prognostic signatures related to programmed cell death, which can predict patient survival and response to immunotherapy [64]. Challenges and future perspectives

Despite the paradigm shift brought about by precision oncology, the development of acquired resistance remains a formidable barrier to long-term survival in GC. While initial responses to targeted therapies like trastuzumab are often promising, disease progression is almost inevitable due to the tumor’s evolutionary adaptability. Resistance to HER2-targeted therapies is frequently driven by the activation of bypass signaling pathways, including MET amplification or FGFR2 activation, and alterations in downstream effectors like the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, which sustain proliferation signals even when the primary target is effectively blocked [34]. Similarly, resistance to immunotherapy presents a complex clinical challenge. The mechanisms underlying resistance to ICIs are multifaceted, often involving the upregulation of alternative immune checkpoints (e.g., TIM-3, LAG-3) or the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells like MDSCs into the TME [56]. Furthermore, the TME itself is critical in mediating therapeutic resistance; as dense extracellular matrix deposition and distinct stromal compositions can physically exclude immune cells and limit drug penetration [52]. Addressing these resistance mechanisms necessitates a transition from monotherapies to rational combination strategies that simultaneously inhibit oncogenic drivers and remodel the immunosuppressive microenvironment [52, 56].

The profound intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH) characteristic of gastric adenocarcinoma is a fundamental challenge to the effective implementation of precision medicine. Gastric tumors are not monolithic entities but rather coexisting diverse sub-clones with distinct genomic and immunological profiles within the same mass [31]. This spatial heterogeneity poses significant limitations for traditional diagnostic workflows, which rely on single-site tissue biopsies. A standard endoscopic biopsy may capture a specific molecular profile (e.g., HER2-positive) while missing subclones that are HER2-negative or harbor distinct driver mutations, leading to discordant treatment responses and eventual failure [31]. Moreover, the immune landscape itself varies spatially; a tumor may exhibit an “immune-hot” phenotype at the invasive margin while remaining “cold” at the core, complicating the predictive value of biomarkers like PD-L1 expression [51]. This variability underscores the inadequacy of static, single-point sampling and highlights the urgent need for more comprehensive diagnostic approaches. These include multi-regional sequencing or integration of liquid biopsies, to capture the full molecular disease complexity and guide more accurate therapeutic decision-making [31, 51].

Beyond the biological hurdles of acquired resistance, the global implementation of precision oncology in GC is profoundly constrained by regional disparities in diagnostic infrastructure and healthcare economics. Geographic variations dictate fundamentally different approaches to biomarker testing between Asia, Europe, and the United States. In the US, supported by comprehensive guidelines and broader commercial insurance coverage, large-panel Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) is increasingly integrated upfront in the diagnostic algorithm for advanced GC. This facilitates the simultaneous evaluation of conventional biomarkers alongside emerging targets like FGFR2 and TMB. Conversely, in many European and Asian healthcare systems—despite East Asia bearing the highest global burden of GC—sequential testing utilizing cost-effective platforms like immunohistochemistry (IHC) and FISH remains the standard practice due to stringent reimbursement constraints. This stepwise approach, while economically pragmatic, can significantly delay the initiation of targeted regimens or fail to identify patients harboring rare, actionable genomic fusions.

Furthermore, the regulatory approval and commercial availability of precision therapeutics exhibit marked geographical asynchrony, leading to distinct regional standards of care. For instance, regulatory agencies such as the FDA (US) and the EMA (Europe) have historically adopted divergent PD-L1 CPS thresholds for the approval of first-line immunotherapy, creating systemic differences in patient eligibility. Similarly, the rollout of novel targeted therapies is often staggered; zolbetuximab, reflecting the acute regional need, received its world-first regulatory approval in Japan prior to its subsequent approval in the US. Moreover, certain locally developed and highly effective therapeutics, such as the VEGFR-2 inhibitor apatinib and various domestic PD-1 inhibitors, are widely utilized as standard later-line therapies within China, yet remain largely investigational or unapproved in Western jurisdictions. These profound regional heterogeneities emphasize that realizing the full potential of precision oncology requires not only scientific innovation but also dedicated global efforts to harmonize diagnostic guidelines, streamline regulatory pathways, and improve equitable access to novel therapeutics across different populations.

Conclusion

Precision oncology has emerged as a pivotal approach in treating GC, demonstrating significant potential for transforming patient outcomes. Integrating molecular subtyping and biomarker identification into clinical practice enables a more personalized treatment strategy, which is crucial given the highly heterogeneous nature of this malignancy. Recent advancements in genomic profiling and biomarker discovery provide valuable insights into distinct GC subtypes, facilitating the identification of patients who are most likely to benefit from specific targeted therapies and immunotherapies.

This shift towards a more individualized approach represents a fundamental paradigm change in oncology, away from the traditional one-size-fits-all model, towards a more stratified treatment framework. By tailoring therapies to the tumor’s specific molecular characteristics, including targeting HER2, Claudin 18.2, or exploiting the immunogenicity of MSI-H status, clinicians can enhance survival rates and improve patients’ quality of life. However, realizing the full potential of this approach requires navigating the complexities of tumor biology and resistance mechanisms. Importantly, the current landscape still harbors significant clinical gaps that must be addressed. A critical unmet need is the management of the Genomically Stable (GS) subtype, which currently lacks dedicated, actionable targets and remains inherently resistant to most established therapies. Additionally, the persistent uncertainties surrounding intermediate biomarker expression, such as HER2 IHC 2+, borderline CLDN18.2 expression, and lower PD-L1 CPS scores, highlight the limitations of current binary diagnostic systems, emphasizing the urgent need to translate successes from “biomarker-high” populations to those with marginal or heterogeneous expression. Continued research and multidisciplinary collaboration will be essential in validating emerging biomarkers, refining combination strategies, and ultimately enhancing the quality of care for patients battling GC.
