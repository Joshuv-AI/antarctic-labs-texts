Clinical guidance for the use of nasogastric tube feeding under restraint in eating disorders care: a systematic scoping review
Authors: Helen West, Oli Williams, Una Foye, Glenn Robert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPersistent dietary restriction can result in people with eating disorders becoming so severely physically and mentally compromised that they are considered a risk to their own life. In these circumstances, individuals may be subjected to involuntary treatments, including nasogastric tube (NGT) feeding under restraint. This systematic scoping review identifies and assesses the evidence available to healthcare professionals to inform clinical practice decisions related to NGT feeding under restraint in eating disorder care, and highlights gaps in knowledge to be addressed in future research.Main textSix electronic databases, six grey literature sites, 12 professional bodies, and four eating disorder conference websites were searched in May 2025. 36 sources met inclusion criteria, including: peer reviewed papers and practice guidelines. Peer-reviewed literature (22) included quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods research studies, and systematic reviews. Grey literature (14) included practice guidance in the form of expert opinion, expert consensus, and practice standards. A narrative review was structured using deductive analysis focused on the following research questions in relation to eating disorders care: (1) where and how is NGT feeding under restraint happening? (2) how are healthcare professionals making decisions about when and how to use NGT feeding under restraint? (3) what are patients, parents/carers, and healthcare professionals’ experiences of NGT feeding under restraint? And (4) what are the patient outcomes of NGT feeding under restraint?ConclusionsThere is clear guidance that NGT feeding under restraint should be delivered minimally, as a lifesaving intervention in line with the legal principle of ‘least restrictive practice’. No comprehensive, multi-disciplinary guidelines or frameworks were identified to support achieving this in practice. Further research is required to improve understanding of who is at risk of receiving NGT feeding under restraint, the long- and short-term risks associated with its use, and what care processes can improve patient outcomes. Future research should support development of multi-disciplinary guidance to improve clinical decision-making and patient outcomes.

Background

Eating disorders (EDs) are a group of complex conditions generally characterised by dietary behaviours that are detrimental to health and driven by experiences of challenging thoughts, feelings, and emotions relating to food and/or the body [1]. They are caused by a combination of biological, psychological, and social factors, have serious physical, psychological, and social impacts, and present significant healthcare challenges [2, 3]. Chief among these challenges is supporting those receiving treatment for EDs in inpatient settings to voluntarily consume enough food and fluids to restore and promote health [4]. When this challenge is deemed insurmountable and there is a perceived risk to life, it is common for patients to be detained under the mental health act and subjected to involuntary treatments including nasogastric tube (NGT) feeding under restraint [5–7]. Delivering NGT feeding under restraint to patients with restrictive EDs is often justified on the grounds of preserving life. However, while it can be lifesaving, it can also cause physical injuries, be traumatising for patients and staff, and may promote resistance to further therapeutic support [8, 9].

International organisations, healthcare professionals, patients, and researchers have all highlighted a need to limit the use of coercive and restrictive interventions in mental health care [10]. In 2015, the United Kingdom’s (UK) Mental Health Act 1983 Code of Practice was updated with clear expectations for mental health services to commit to reducing the use of restrictive interventions, including use of restraint [11]. The need to move away from coercive and authoritarian treatment protocols has been stressed in research on EDs specifically, with a call to focus on collaborative decision-making to improve treatment outcomes [12]. However, the clinical decision-making process in EDs treatment can be complex, and healthcare professionals regularly face ethical dilemmas relating to involuntary or coercive treatment and the physical and mental health of people in their care [13]. Decisions related to NGT feeding under restraint are fraught with these complexities and the associated risks are compounded by differing knowledge and priorities of various staff, patients, and families/carers involved in the treatment process. The unique combination of each patient and treatment context means this intervention can have vastly different outcomes ranging from positive (promoting/supporting recovery) to detrimental (unintentional exacerbation of condition and/or additional harms, e.g., trauma). Decision-making is further complicated by key operational concepts such as ‘lifesaving circumstances’, ‘recovery’ and ‘successful treatment’ being poorly and/or variously defined and open to interpretation [14, 15].

Despite the complexities and potential iatrogenic consequences, NGT feeding under restraint is an under-researched intervention in EDs treatment and there is a lack of consensus or evidence-based guidance on best practice [5, 16, 17]. Indeed, it is currently unclear what information is available to EDs clinicians to inform their clinical practice.

This international scoping review aims to map the extent and types of evidence that are available to healthcare professionals to inform clinical practice decisions related to NGT feeding under restraint in EDs care, and to highlight gaps in the current knowledge base that can be addressed in future research. The research questions guiding this inquiry are:where and how is NGT feeding under restraint happening in eating disorders care?how are healthcare professionals making decisions about when and how to use NGT feeding under restraint in eating disorders care?what are patients, parents/carers, and healthcare professionals’ experiences of NGT feeding under restraint in eating disorders care?what are the patient outcomes of NGT feeding under restraint in the treatment of eating disorders?

Methods

This systematic scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis: updated methodological guidance for conducting scoping reviews and reported as per the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) framework [18].

Six electronic databases (Table 1, 2), six grey literature sites (Table 3), 12 professional bodies (Table 4), and four EDs conference websites (Table 5) were searched systematically for evidence and guidance on the use of NGT feeding under restraint in the treatment of people with EDs. Those without a formal ED diagnosis but who received this intervention due to risks related to disordered eating were also included. Reference lists of all included articles were back searched, and database searches were used for forward citation tracking to identify further studies. The search had no geographical limits but was limited to sources published in English from 2015 onwards, when the UK Mental Health Act 1983 Code of Practice was updated with clear expectations for mental health services to commit to reducing the use of restrictive interventions, including NGT feeding under restraint. Professional bodies

A scoping approach provides a preliminary assessment of the research area with the aim of identifying and determining the nature and extent of research literature [20]. This was deemed the most appropriate method to identify and assesses the evidence available to healthcare professionals to inform clinical practice decisions related to NGT feeding under restraint and to highlight gaps in the current knowledge base that can be addressed in future research [18]. Due to the heterogeneity identified amongst the communication sources used by healthcare professionals in practice—including peer reviewed literature and textual evidence other than published research, such as policies, expert opinion and guidelines—a narrative review of the evidence was chosen to allow the researchers to incorporate a wide variety of sources in their description and examination of the topic [19]. The narrative review was structured using deductive analysis focused on answering the four research questions (as stated above).

Search strategy and study selection

The population, concept, and context framework were used to establish search terms and eligibility criteria as recommended by JBI [21].

The eligible population included people with an eating disorder diagnosis, or those with another psychiatric diagnosis in combination with disordered eating (eating patterns that do not meet the frequency, duration, and/or psychological criteria for a diagnosable eating disorder). The scope was broadened to include disordered eating to capture instances where people without a formal diagnosis of an eating disorder refuse nutrition support in healthcare settings and the use of NGT feeding under restraint may be considered.

The concept was evidence and guidance that focused on the use of restraint (physical, chemical, and mechanical) for the intervention of NGT feeding. This scoping review maps evidence and frameworks that are available to inform clinical decision-making about the use of restraint to deliver NGT feeding. Therefore, evidence and guidance that focused on general considerations for the use of restraint (in the absence of administering NGT feeding) was excluded.

The context was international healthcare settings. Articles were eligible for inclusion if they were available in English and published from 2015 onwards. Studies published prior to 2015 were only included if they were referenced for use in national or international guidelines and only the latest versions of international or national guidelines were included.

Search terms

Key words used: Nasogastric tube feeding, restraint, restrictive practice, eating disorders, anorexia.

To initially refine the search strategy, a preliminary search was undertaken on Google Scholar to identify articles on the topic. The text words contained in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles and their index terms used to describe the studies were subsequently used to develop the full search strategy for Medline. This search strategy was reviewed with a librarian at King’s College London, before being adapted for use in each database. A protocol for this study was pre-registered on OSF: 10.17605/OSF.IO/HEQ8M.

Study selection

All identified sources were uploaded into Rayyan software and duplicates removed [22]. A three-person panel reviewed all titles and abstracts against the inclusion criteria; HW 100% and GR and OW 50% each. Following blinded review, any disagreements were resolved by all three panel members reviewing the full text and reaching consensus through discussion. Full texts that were reviewed but did not meet inclusion criteria were excluded with reasons for their exclusion documented and reported.

Data extraction and evidence mapping

Due to the diversity of the literature, data were extracted from included papers using an iterative approach. Data extracted were recorded on a standardised data extraction sheet that was adapted from the JBI template data extraction instrument for source details, characteristics, and results. The data extraction sheet was piloted with 10% of full texts and discussed between the review team (HW, OW & GR). It included citation information, detail relevant to population, concept, and context (as described above), study methods, study quality, and key findings relevant to review questions. Data extraction was completed by the lead researcher (HW) and checked by another member of the review team (UF).

Full text sources that did not meet inclusion criteria were excluded and reasons for their exclusion were documented and reported. 7. Offering of alternative, less coercive, means of nutrition. Patients should understand that at any time they can choose to receive their nutrition in a less coercive way, for example voluntarily through an ngt, orally via a sip feed or oral diet

8. Minimise coercion – restraint should be carefully graded (for instance, hand holding if possible, rather than holding down, use of soft surfaces to reduce force of restraint)

11. Regularly review need for ngt feeding under restraint. Ngt feeding under restraint should be provided as few times as needed. Clinicians should review the need for this regularly together with patients and families and constantly

Current practice and intervention landscape

Current practice and intervention landscape

Seven sources explored current practice and intervention landscape [5, 17, 23–27]. In the UK, one survey and two audits relating to the practice of NGT feeding under physical restraint were completed across a five-year period, in Mental Health Units, Children and Adolescent inpatient units, and Paediatric wards [5, 23, 24]. In the first, a survey was conducted across general adolescent inpatient mental health units and specialist EDs inpatient services in the UK and Ireland between September 2015 and June 2016. It found that 34% of the mental health units surveyed were able to deliver NGT feeding under restraint. Various feeding practices were observed across these units. When no physical intervention was needed, NGT feeding was administered 3 to 6 times daily (mean 3.4). When feeding under restraint was necessary, mental health units decreased their daily feeds to 2 to 3 (mean 2.2) and specialist EDs units (SEDU) reduced feeds to 1 to 3 times per day (mean 2.2) [23]. The second survey audited mental health inpatient units in England (n = 134) between June 2020 and May 2021 [5]. The authors stated that NGT feeding under restraint was ‘not uncommon’ (p.322), with 622 patients receiving NGT feeding under restraint over a 12-month period (2020–2021). Other notable findings from this audit were that although there was a considerable age range of people receiving the intervention (11 to 60 years of age), 78% of restraints occurred in child and adolescent mental health services (CAMHS) with considerable variation in the duration of use with individual patients; from just once during an inpatient stay to daily for 312 weeks (mean 29.1 weeks) [5]. In the last of the three surveys, an audit of the use of feeding under restraint for young people with mental health disorders was undertaken in paediatric wards across England between 1st April 2022 and 31st March 2023. It reported that over 12-months 55 of 135 units (38.5%) delivered NGT feeding under restraint, mostly to young people with a diagnosis of AN. The length of time the intervention was used for, the number of restraints per day, and the staff used to deliver restraints (mental health nurses, paediatric nurses, security staff, parents/carers and nursing/healthcare assistants) showed significant variation in practice between units. Most incidences were carried out under Section 3 of the UK Mental Health Act. However, a small number were carried out under the UK Children’s Act or court order, and 9 people had no legal framework in place, only parental consent [24].

A systematic review which described the use of NGT feeding in young people with EDs noted that NGT feeding was most often used in young people due to medical instability. They found some evidence that the need for restraint may be linked to long admissions (average of 170 days) and that in one study, 66% of young people who required NGT feeding required restraint. The lack of research in this area was noted [17]. A clinical audit in the UK retrospectively reviewed data relating to young people with EDs admitted to a general adolescent mental health unit found that 19 out of 46 received NGT feeding under restraint (41.3%), and that average length of stay for people who required NGT feeding was over twice as long as those who did not (20 compared to 9 weeks) [25]. Neither of these studies described types of restraint used for NGT feeding.

To explore whether co-occurring psychiatric diagnosis might increase the risk of people receiving NGT under restraint, a retrospective case note analysis was undertaken at a specialist EDs unit in the UK [26]. Of the 61 patients in this sample, nine were restrained and four had comorbidities. The number of feeds delivered under restraint appeared to be greater for young people with co-morbidities, despite not having significantly longer hospital stays; however, type of restraint used in these cases was not explored and a larger sample would be needed to confirm these findings. Finally, in Denmark a national register review aimed to describe frequency of feeding under restraint in AN care. They found 18% of admissions experienced at least one involuntary measure, with tube feeding being the most prevalent (16,733 episodes across 238 patients). It is not clear from the reported data how many of these feeds required restraint or what type of restraint was used [27]. Only a minority of studies provided details on the types of feeding regiment (continuous/bolus) or volumes of feed being delivered when feeding under restraint [23, 29, 30, 34].2.Practice guidance and decision-making relating to NGT feeding under restraint

2.Practice guidance and decision-making relating to NGT feeding under restraint

Practice guidance and decision-making relating to NGT feeding under restraint

Eighteen practice guidelines, including sources exploring clinical decision-making related to NGT feeding under restraint were identified. Two of these were quality standards documents published by the UK’s Royal College of Psychiatry, one focusing on adult EDs inpatient services and one on children and adolescent EDs inpatient services [28, 29]. Both documents recommend: (i) NGT feeding under restraint should only be used in life-threatening situations, or as part of a carefully considered and regularly reviewed multi-disciplinary care plan, (ii) a second opinion should be sought if there is repeated use of restraint for NGT feeding, (iii) staff and patients involved in restrained NGT feeding should be offered post-incident support, and (iv) staff should receive training around prevention and management of violence and aggression (including training for the use of restraint for NGT feeding). The adult document included additional standards relating to the assessment of physical and psychological risks relating to NGT feeding under restraint and monitoring of vital signs during restrained feeding. Neither document provides guidance on selecting the most suitable restraint method [28, 29].

The UK’s Royal College of Psychiatry (2022) produced practice guidance for the management of Medical Emergencies in Eating Disorders (MEED) [7]. Like other practice guidelines [28–30], the MEED authors recommend NGT feeding under restraint should only occur as a last resort and expert advice should be sought (at a minimum) from a Psychiatrist and Specialist Dietitian. Physical restraint is mentioned, but other methods are not [7]. The British Dietetic Association (BDA) produced a consensus-based guideline for NGT feeding under restraint focused on how dietetic enteral feeding practices should be adapted after the decision to feed under restraint has been made. It includes advice on management of refeeding syndrome, least restrictive feeding practices, and care planning. It states that NGT feeding under restraint should always be a last resort and a consultant-led, multi-professional decision, made in collaboration with the patient [30]. In an opinion piece published in the UK’s Royal College of Psychiatry’s Newsletter, two authors provided reflections on the use of these BDA (2020) guidelines. They highlighted what they saw as 11 clinical issues relating to the use of NGT feeding under restraint that can be broadly grouped as: (i) consideration of clinical necessity, (ii) least restrictive options, (iii) staff consensus and solidarity, (iv) calm and compassionate communication (v) delivering feeds with dignity. They also highlighted the need for the use of appropriately trained staff in restraint delivery, and the importance of good communication and support for patients, staff, and families. Issues related to the most suitable restraint intervention (physical, mechanical, or chemical) were not discussed [31].

Two case commentaries from the USA that explored clinical decision making were identified [32, 33]. The first, a narrative review and clinical case discussion in the treatment of adults with EDs in the USA, explored how case law informed their clinical decision-making, and what outcomes from compulsory treatment can be found in the literature. They expressed concern that there is a reluctance from health professionals to initiate involuntary treatment, as this could lead to fatal outcomes, and that there are no criteria available to clinicians to support decisions around clinical risks [32]. The second examined the role of force in compassion for young people hospitalised with EDs. They described steps taken to support a clinical decision around enteral feeding with restraint, which included: (i) establishing a therapeutic alliance, (ii) evaluating options, (iii) ensuring a shared understanding of illness, and the clinical, ethical and legal rationale for forced treatment, (iv) preparing the patient for what to expect, and (v) compassion as a primary value. They stressed that the intervention may have risks and should not be undertaken lightly. Restraint methods were not discussed [33].

A qualitative study explored the decision to deliver an NGT feed under physical restraint with clinicians from a range of professional backgrounds. Two main categories of decision-making were identified: (i) quick decisions – referred to instances where there is limited MDT discussion due to the perceived urgency to respond to risks caused by the impact of not eating, and (ii) slow decisions – referred to a more coherent or thoughtful approach in which all other options were exhausted in a systematic way, and instances where there was no set plan for how and when to use NGT feeding under restraint rather it was discussed by teams over a long period of time, with this then informing a quick decision to be made when deemed necessary. Experiences indicated that although a slower approach had the potential to be more coherent and systematic, when there was no clear plan of how and when restraint would be used, slow approaches were sometimes experienced as more coercive, with patients reporting that they were ‘threatened’ with a feeding tube, and then abruptly fed when staff had ‘had enough’ [8].

The publication of the BDA (2020) guidelines was followed by a UK team using three case studies to illustrate different clinical scenarios and demonstrate the use of the dietetic guidelines in practice [34]. They highlighted the importance of multi-disciplinary team decision-making with a focus on clinical reasoning and rationale for the use of restraint, as well as exploring least restrictive feeding practices in the different scenarios. Practical guidance from Fuller et al. (2023) for children’s nurses working in acute non-specialist settings on the use of NGT feeding under restraint explores reasons for medical instability in young people with EDs, why food refusal occurs, and provides advice on legal frameworks, communication, practical adaptations to feeding and physical restraint delivery, as well as post intervention monitoring [35]. In addition to this, a website has been developed by the NHS learning hub to disseminate this information and support UK paediatric teams to safely support those with EDs on their wards [36]. Multi-disciplinary team decision-making was explored in a fictional case study set in the UK that considered ethical, legal, and practical aspects of using NGT feeding under physical restraint for young people with EDs on paediatric wards. It stressed the importance of dietetic practice adaptations and multi-disciplinary team working, as well as exploring some of the difficulties teams may face when attempting to feed in this setting [37]. A clinical commentary explored the clinical and ethical aspects of the decision-making process around involuntary treatment, including NGT feeding under restraint, for people with longstanding EDs. It highlighted the growing evidence base for ‘harm reduction approaches’ (that seek to reduce harm and prioritise wellbeing, instead of focusing on full recovery) for people with ‘severe and enduring anorexia nervosa’ and outlined legal, ethical, and medical challenges clinicians face with this patient group. In particular, the authors discussed the complex nature of capacity with people with severe malnutrition, and the potential for involuntary treatment to further strengthen ED thoughts and behaviours, making it potentially counterproductive for some patients in the long term [38]. A case report described a 17-year-old patient with anorexia who would only tolerate NGT feeding with significant chemical restraints. It highlighted how the ego-syntonic nature of AN makes capacity decisions for young people complex, especially as they approach 18 years of age [39].

Ethical issues and the role of an ethicist in the complex decision-making process around NGT feeding under restraint have been explored in three commentaries of a case study [40–42]. All highlighted the unique complexities of determining capacity and person-centred, values-based actions in people with EDs, as well as the need for balancing risks against benefits when feeding under restraint. The practical advice offered was: (i) pausing to assess risk to life and broader risks to health related to malnutrition, (ii) bringing staff together to name tensions and acknowledge good intentions, (iii) creating environments where it is safe for staff to share and disagree, (iv) including a wide variety of skill sets in discussions, (v) planning ahead for scenarios where these decisions come up so people know the steps to take, who to include in the discussions, and what ethical questions to ask to the team, and (vi) determining elements of a meaningful care plan. Although written with an emphasis on ethics, these commentaries prioritised assessing acute risks first, with a focus on thinking about risk versus benefit. Finally, a qualitative study in the UK explored clinician, patient, and parent/carer perspectives on good practice for NGT feeding under physical restraint through 36 semi-structured qualitative interviews. The themes of good practice from these interviews included references to individualising care to set shared goals with patients, including agreeing plans about how NGT feeding under physical restraint could be prevented and when it should be used [43].3.Staff and service user experiences of NGT feeding under restraint

3.Staff and service user experiences of NGT feeding under restraint

Staff and service user experiences of NGT feeding under restraint

Seven studies have sought to better understand the impact of NGT feeding under physical restraint by exploring staff, patient, and family experiences [8, 44–49]. Kodua et al. (2020) exploration of experiences focused on nursing assistants in the UK who had administered NGT feeding under physical restraint to young people with AN. Staff reported that it was unpleasant, causing emotional and physical exhaustion as well as injury. Staff felt pressure and responsibility around their ability to perform the restraint and the success of the procedure, and conflict occurred between staff when colleagues administered restraint ineffectively. It was noted that nursing assistants coped variously through detachment and talking with others, as well as becoming desensitised to the intervention over time [44]. Similarly, a small (n = 12) qualitative study conducted by Brinchmann et al. (2024) found that nursing staff held concerns around providing good care during physical restraint episodes, as well as ethical concerns around the practice, especially when delivered at a body mass index (BMI) which was not considered life threatening [45]. A study conducted in the Netherlands explored staff experiences from a wider variety of professional backgrounds including psychiatry, psychology, and nursing, highlighting the impacts on all involved [48]. It described the differing roles and responsibilities of staff, and emotional impacts of the work, illuminating the risk of staff burnout and complicated power struggles that can occur both between staff and patients, and within staff teams. The importance of clear, practical guidelines, and a moral and ethical framework to work from, were emphasised as well as the need for education, shared decision-making practices, and clear professional boundaries. A qualitative study in Denmark of seven women with AN who had experienced multiple involuntary treatment events, including NGT feeding under physical restraint, found that it was not felt to be helpful at the time, and that it had a lasting impact on those who received it including fear, trauma, and avoidance of treatment settings. However, some people subsequently changed their perspective, describing it as a helpful or necessary step in their treatment [8].

A series of qualitative studies reviewed patient, parent, and staff experiences of NGT feeding under physical restraint in England [46, 47, 49]. The first, undertaken in children’s hospitals in England highlighted that non-clinical, untrained, hospital security staff are sometimes used to deliver NGT feeding under physical restraint, and the potential for this to have a negative impact on security teams, patients, parents, and staff [46]. The second examined the impact of NGT feeding under physical restraint on people with lived experience of EDs, carers, and staff. They found that all groups found the experience traumatising in some way, and that it may result in post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) for some patients [47]. This study noted that the intervention could have iatrogenic harms that damage relationships between patients and staff and between patients and other patients, as well as reinforcing illness identities. However, participants in each group (patients, parents, and staff) recognised that NGT feeding under physical restraint could be lifesaving and that in some instances despite being difficult, it was necessary and helpful. The authors concluded that short- and long-term risks for patients need to be balanced against short-term clinical outcomes, and that ethical and clinical justification for use is needed. The final paper used secondary data analysis to explore experiences of parents whose child is receiving NGT feeding under physical restraint. They found parents experience a range of conflicting emotions, notably relief and shame. Parents understood the intervention to be necessary but found it traumatic. Some parents in this study participated in the restraint of their child [49]. Finally, a case report in an adult medical ward in the UK explored the difficulties of delivering NGT feeding under restraint in non-specialist settings. It reports difficulties related to navigating capacity and consent, ethical dilemmas, and the potential for harm, both in delivering the intervention to people of very low weight with severe illness, and through increased resistance to treatment [50].4.Intervention outcomes for NGT feeding under restraint

Intervention outcomes for NGT feeding under restraint

Three sources explored the impact of NGT feeding under restraint on treatment outcomes [51–53]. Researchers in Norway conducted a hospital records review and five-year follow up with the aim of describing use of physical restraint in a specialist EDs hospital for adolescents and to determine if this was associated with weight gain through admission and 5 years later [51]. In the sample of 38 patients, they found 21% (n = 8) experienced meal related restraints, with four people experiencing ten or more episodes. No differences were observed in terms of illness duration, age of onset of illness, or age at admission between those who received restraint and those who did not. The intervention was not associated with a higher rate of weight gain during admission and at 5 years follow up the intervention was significantly associated with persisting EDs diagnosis. A conference abstract detailing a case review of 12 people with AN noted that enteral feeding with restraint resulted in weight gain to target weight, and acceptance of tube feeding once these patients reached their target weight [52]. However, no further details of this programme could be found, and it is unclear if or how the patients were restrained for feeding. A systematic scoping review explored the outcomes associated with NGT feeding in people with a diagnosis of AN or Atypical AN. In studies included that explored restraint, they found evidence which included people with lived experience of EDs reporting restraint to be an aspirational standard of illness, and feelings of being ‘stuck’ due to recurring EDs thoughts and feelings. They also noted that self-harm or purging after restrained feeds could lead to further restraint, and that tube feeding could become part of people’s illness identity, with some people subsequently having difficulty transitioning to oral diet. Outcomes related to the type of restraint used were not explored [53].

Discussion

This review highlights striking variations in the delivery of NGT feeding under restraint and a lack of research on which to base clinical decision-making. In the UK, clinical guidance states that NGT feeding under restraint should be delivered as a ‘lifesaving intervention’ and in line with the principle of ‘least restrictive practice’ [7, 30]. Whilst UK survey data indicates that NGT feeding under restraint is most often used for around one week, some people may receive this intervention for years [5], indicating that within EDs care, ‘lifesaving’ is variably interpreted and practiced. This highlights the need for clearer practice guidelines which includes advice on how to determine when NGT feeding under restraint can legitimately be justified on grounds of preserving life, and when this is not the case and what to do instead. Furthermore, there is a need for better understanding of what puts somebody at risk of receiving NGT feeding under restraint, what conditions increase the risk of people receiving this intervention for long periods, and what clinical practices are supporting or preventing reliance on restraint to provide adequate nutrition [26, 27]. Future research should prioritise understanding the service-level context in which NGT feeding under restraint is required. Examining national variation in access to specialist inpatient eating disorder services alongside the incidence of NGT feeding under restraint, could demonstrate whether inequities in service provision contributes to use of this intervention. Limited access to local specialist inpatient care could lead to delayed admissions, greater medical deterioration and consequently higher likelihood of NGT feeding under restraint. Furthermore, preliminary evidence that people with psychiatric co-morbidities may be at greater risk of receiving NGT feeding under restraint indicates that flexible, person-centred care may be an important factor. Limited flexibility in single-diagnosis manualised models of care at a community level could also be contributing to this risk [25, 26].

Clinical decision-making regarding the use of NGT feeding under restraint is complicated by the interplay between physical and mental health aspects of restrictive EDs. This complexity raises challenges relating to the quality and safety of NGT feeding under restraint delivery, where decision-making falls broadly across medical, nutritional, psychological, legal, and ethical areas of expertise, sometimes with the need to consider other co-occurring conditions, such as autism and diabetes [25, 38, 39, 42, 54, 55]. Challenges include determining capacity in patients who are malnourished, and the possibilities for shared decision-making with patients who have values and goals which are aligned with their illness, and which are not necessarily compatible with preserving life. In the UK, MEED guidance is available to support doctors to undertake risk assessments in medical emergencies for people with EDs. In these assessments, ‘red flag’ risk markers are described as ‘impending risk to life’ – though no single risk factor alone is adequate to assess overall risk and red flag risks are not evenly weighted. Significantly, clinical judgement is required for interpretation of these risks. The guidance states that medical risk assessments must ‘balance risks in physical, psychological and social domains while paying attention to matters of insight, motivation, consent and the legal framework for intervention’, (7, pp. 28). However, no guidance is available in the MEED document for this broader assessment of risk and there is no evidence about if/how these broader assessments of risk are being undertaken in practice. Given that individual clinicians are likely to have variable tolerances for risk, there is currently no evidence that shows how risk markers are being interpreted in practice, and how this is influencing care decisions about restrictive interventions such as NGT feeding under restraint. Data about the risks and benefits of different restraint modalities is also limited. Considering all these challenges, a multi-professional framework that guides complex discussions and decisions with clinicians and patients with varying expertise, experience, and disciplinary training would have great utility. The findings of this review demonstrate the need for multidisciplinary decision-making as a way of overcoming gaps in disciplinary skill sets and supporting more thoughtful, effective, and safer practice but this is neither currently the norm nor easily achieved [47, 56].

Outcome data for clinicians to base their assessment of risk–benefit on are lacking. Clausen and Jones’ (2014) review of involuntary treatment for people with AN emphasised the need for large, well-designed, adequately powered studies exploring the use, frequency, and effects of involuntary treatment. However, determining what counts as a ‘successful’ outcome for this intervention is key to both improving the decision-making process, and designing research questions that test them [57]. Currently, a small amount of published research and practice guidance has a strong focus on stabilising physical health and short-term weight gain as the markers of successful treatment, with no details of how clinicians can integrate individual formulation, treatment goals, and broader social and ethical domains, as well as potential long-term impacts into their care decisions [34, 52]. Longitudinal studies that follow the treatment/recovery trajectories of patients who received NGT feeding under restraint would help to address this knowledge gap and support clinicians to make better decisions for and with patients.

Restrictive EDs presentations can be complex, and the reasons for food and fluid refusal can vary widely, even within diagnosis criteria [58, 59]. Researchers have identified the need to improve the evidence base to support the development of more individualised treatments for people with EDs, particularly as current treatments have only modest overall success rates—with people who are neurodivergent or have co-occurring illnesses having disproportionately poorer treatment outcomes [59–62]. Qualitative research identified in this review, that explores patient, parent, and staff experiences of NGT feeding under restraint points to potential short- and long-term harms of the intervention, including exacerbating treatment resistance and PTSD. This emphasises the need to give greater attention in the decision-making process to potential for NGT feeding under restraint to produce iatrogenic harms. Improving use of NGT feeding under restraint relies on better understanding of the need to at times adapt established treatments and adopt a greater willingness to create more flexible, transdiagnostic approaches that improve clinical understanding of the function of food and fluid refusal. This should include accounting for the differing needs of people throughout the neurodiversity spectrum and not uncritically conflating divergence with disorder. This would allow for treatment adaptations that support a greater diversity of people to avoid the need for NGT feeding under restraint and help shift focus to longer-term impacts of interventions like NGT feeding under restraint on (dis)engagement in treatment and persistence of EDs symptoms.

Limitations

This review focused specifically on NGT feeding under restraint, which falls within a much broader category of involuntary treatment. There may be non-specific evidence and practice guidance available to clinicians that focuses on approaches to involuntary treatment in EDs more broadly. However, this was outside the scope of this literature review which justified its focus on NGT feeding under restraint due to the unique challenges raised when making practice decisions around enteral feeding with restraint in the context of EDs patients. The reviewed literature originated largely from the UK, USA, and parts of Europe – it is likely that this is at least partially due to the exclusion of literature not published in English. Although some of the challenges reported will be generalisable, we do not have enough information on how NGT feeding under restraint is governed and practiced internationally to be certain. Some source materials did not define the methods of restraint they were exploring.

Future research recommendations

Almost all sources in this review identified a scarcity of research on this complex and high-risk area of practice. Recommendations from the literature included the need to better understand who is at risk of needing this intervention, how care practices impact on frequency and duration of NGT feeding under restraint, and what care processes can improve decision-making, patient outcomes, and reduce risks to all involved. Specific suggestions include: developing a better understanding of the risks and benefits of advance directives for NGT feeding under restraint and care planning exit strategies for feeding [7], more qualitative research exploring what evidence and frameworks clinicians use to make decisions around NGT feeding under restraint in practice – and the variability in their interpretation and application, and longitudinal studies exploring long-term outcomes and experiences of people with EDs who have undergone involuntary NGT feeding and their perception of the relative benefits and harms over time. This review highlights the need for developing a comprehensive understanding of this complex intervention and the development of multi-disciplinary guidance to support clinical decision-making and more effective and safer practice. It has been strongly recommended that such multidisciplinary guidance should be developed with service-user and carer involvement [21]. Conclusions

Currently, there are no comprehensive guidelines or frameworks which support clinical decision-making around NGT feeding under restraint in EDs care that cover the medical, nutritional, psychological, legal, and ethical aspects of care. The development of a multi-disciplinary framework that guides clinicians with varying disciplinary expertise and practice experience to have complex discussions and make well-justified clinical decisions would benefit from the creation of a stronger evidence-base to be informed by but could ultimately support more ethical, safer, and effective practice.
