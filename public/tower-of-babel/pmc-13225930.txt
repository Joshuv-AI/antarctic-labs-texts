An Unusual Cause of Abdominal Pain
Authors: Yusaku Kajihara, Mamoru Munakata
Published: 2026

An 86-year-old man presented to the gastroenterology clinic with a 1-week history of abdominal pain in the right upper quadrant. His vital signs were normal. On examination, his abdomen was soft and nontender. Abdominal ultrasonography suggested a large visceral aneurysm. Subsequent computed tomographic angiography (CTA) of the abdomen showed two aneurysms in the pancreaticoduodenal arcade: an inferior pancreaticoduodenal artery aneurysm (PDAA) measuring 54 by 50 mm and a posterior superior PDAA measuring 32 by 21 mm (Fig. 1A). Celiac artery (CA) stenosis was also confirmed (Fig. 1B, arrows). Obstruction of the CA, mainly owing to compression by the median arcuate ligament (MAL) (i.e., MAL syndrome [MALS]), contributes to the formation of aneurysms by the development of collaterals from superior mesenteric artery. A diagnosis was made of PDAAs due to CA compression. Dissection of the MAL as well as ligation and resection of the aneurysms was performed. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient was doing well.

(A) Computed tomographic angiography (CTA) of the abdomen showing two aneurysms in the pancreaticoduodenal arcade: an inferior pancreaticoduodenal artery aneurysm (PDAA) measuring 54 by 50 mm (asterisk) and a posterior superior PDAA measuring 32 by 21 mm (arrowheads). (B) CTA confirming celiac artery stenosis (arrows).

Visceral artery aneurysms (VAAs) are rare but clinically important conditions because the mortality rate of ruptured VAAs is high.1 In particular, PDAAs account for less than 2% of VAAs and are prone to rupture even at less than 10 mm.2,3 Therefore, treatment (e.g., coil embolization, ligation and resection with end-to-end anastomosis) is indicated, regardless of their size.4 CA stenosis is associated with 50% to 60% of PDAAs; when findings on CTA suggest MALS, median arcuate ligament dissection is considered in addition to the above-mentioned treatment.4
