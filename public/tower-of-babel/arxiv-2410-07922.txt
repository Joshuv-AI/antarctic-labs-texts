Solutions for k k -generalized Fibonacci numbers using Fuss-Catalan numbers
S. R. Mane

Abstract
We present new expressions for the k k -generalized Fibonacci numbers, say F k ​ ( n ) F_{k}(n) . They satisfy the recurrence F k ​ ( n ) = F k ​ ( n − 1 ) + ⋯ + F k ​ ( n − k ) F_{k}(n)=F_{k}(n-1)+\dots+F_{k}(n-k) . Explicit expressions for the roots of the auxiliary (or characteristic) polynomial are presented, using Fuss-Catalan numbers. Properties of the roots are enumerated. We quantify the accuracy of asymptotic approximations for F k ​ ( n ) F_{k}(n) for n ≫ 1 n\gg 1 . Our results subsume and extend some results published by previous authors. We also present a basis (or ‘fundamental solutions’) to solve the above recurrence for arbitrary initial conditions. We comment on the use of generating functions and multinomial sums for the k k -generalized Fibonacci numbers and related sequences. We note that the resulting multinomial sums are Dickson polynomials of the second kind in several variables. We also present what may be a new identity for companion matrices.

Introduction

The origin of this paper is somewhat curious.
The author coauthored a paper
[
3
]
on the success run waiting times of Bernoulli trials.
Recently, the author noticed that, for the special case
p=\frac{1}{2}
(
p
being the success parameter of the Bernoulli trials),
elementary transformations yielded the solution for the so-called ‘
k
-generalized’ Fibonacci numbers, say
F_{k}(n)
.
These numbers satisfy the recurrence

F_{k}(n)=F_{k}(n-1)+\dots+F_{k}(n-k)\,.

(1.1)

The usual Fibonacci numbers are the special case
k=2
.

Our main contribution is to display
explicit series solutions for all the roots of the characteristic equation of the above recurrence, for arbitrary
k\geq 2

(leveraging formulas from
[
3
]
).
Solutions in radicals are known for special cases such as the usual Fibonacci, tribonacci and tetranacci numbers.
It has long been known that the characteristic equation has a unique positive real root (the ‘principal root’),
whose magnitude exceeds that of all the other roots.
Some authors, e.g. Wolfram
[
12
]
, have derived expressions for the principal root and/or upper and lower bounds for it.
We present expressions for
all
the roots and moreover enumerate several properties of the roots.
For example,
(i) there are no pure imaginary roots,
and (ii) the smaller the real part of a root (less positive or more negative), the smaller its amplitude.
We solve an associated Vandermonde system of equations and derive a Binet-style formula for the solution of the recurrence.
(Other authors have also published the same or similar Binet-style formulas,
e.g. Dresden and Du
[
4
]
, but the roots had to be computed numerically, for example via a root-finding algorithm.)

We then apply our formalism to treat related problems published by other authors.
For example, Spickerman and Joyner
[
11
]
solved the recurrence eq. (
1.1
),
but with the initial values
1,1,2,4,8,\dots
.
We generalize from powers of
2
to powers of an arbitrary number
\mu
.
Our solution is presented in Sec.
6
.

Next, in Sec.
7

we present a set of ‘basis sequences’ or ‘fundamental solutions’ of the recurrence eq. (
1.1
).
The usual scenario is to solve eq. (
1.1
) only for the initial conditions
F_{k}(0)=\dots=F_{k}(k-2)=0
and
F_{k}(k-1)=1
,
i.e. the initial
k
-tuple
(0,\dots,0,1)
.
We present solutions for the other initial
k
-tuples
(1,0,\dots,0)
through
(0,\dots,0,1,0)
.
To the author’s knowledge, such a set of basis sequences has not been published in the literature.
We leverage work from papers on solutions of linear recurrences, in particular by Wolfram
[
13
]
.

Sec.
8
reviews the use of a companion matrix to solve linear recurrences with constant coefficients,
mainly to make contact with the material in Sec.
9
.
In this context, employing a lemma by Wolfram
[
13
]
,
we present what may be a new identity for companion matrices.
See Prop.
8.1
.

Finally, in Sec.
9
we remark on the use of generating functions and multinomial sums to solve linear recurrences with constant coefficients.
In this case, our presentation is more of an independent confirmation of known results,
to demonstrate the application of our formalism for basis sequences.
We treat the Narayana, Padovan and Perrin sequences.
A referee kindly brought to the author’s attention that the multinomial sums in Sec.
9

are Dickson polynomials of the second kind in several variables.

2
Roots of the characteristic equation

Eq. (
1.1
) is a homogeneous linear difference equation and its characteristic equation is

x^{k}-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}x^{j}=0\,.

(2.1)

The corresponding auxiliary (or characteristic) polynomial is

A(x)=x^{k}-x^{k-1}-\dots-1
.
Several authors
[
12
,
9
,
10
]
have shown that the roots of
A(x)
are simple.
Let us denote the roots of
A(x)
by
\zeta_{j}
,
j=0,\dots,k-1
.
Then we express
F_{k}(n)
as a weighted sum of powers of the roots as follows
(i.e. a Binet-style formula)

F_{k}(n)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}c_{j}\zeta_{j}^{n}\,.

(2.2)

Here
\{c_{0},\dots,c_{k-1}\}
is a set of coefficients which do not depend on
n
,
but they do depend on
k
and the initial conditions, i.e. the values of
F_{k}(0)
through
F_{k}(k-1)
.
It is standard to employ the initial conditions
F_{k}(0)=\dots=F_{k}(k-2)=0
and
F_{k}(k-1)=1
.
We shall begin with this, but later we shall treat alternative initial conditions,
by Spickerman and Joyner
[
11
]
.
In Sec.
7
, we present a complete set of ‘fundamental solutions’ for the recurrence eq. (
1.1
),
i.e. a basis which can be used to solve for arbitrary initial conditions.

Our main result is to present explicit expressions for the roots
\zeta_{j}
,
in terms of so-called ‘Fuss-Catalan numbers’
(see the text by Graham, Knuth and Patashnik
[
6
]

for relevant definitions, formulas and identities).
The connection is somewhat surprising.
Dilworth and Mane
[
3
]
(hereafter ‘DM’) published a paper on the success run waiting times of Bernoulli trials.
The recurrence for the probability mass function
f_{k}(n)

is (
[
3
]
eq. (1)), with
p\in(0,1)
,
q=1-p
and
k\geq 2
,

f_{k}(n)=qf_{k}(n-1)+pqf_{k}(n-2)+p^{2}qf_{k}(n-3)+\cdots+p^{k-1}qf_{k}(n-k)\,.

(2.3)

Set
p=q=\frac{1}{2}
and
f_{k}(n)=F_{k}(n)/2^{n}
; then elementary manipulations yield eq. (
1.1
).
Hence the solution for the
k
-generalized Fibonacci numbers
F_{k}(n)

equals
2^{n}
times the probability mass function
f_{k}(n)
of success run waiting times of Bernoulli trials,
with
p=q=\frac{1}{2}
.
The auxiliary polynomial associated with eq. (
2.3
) is

\mathcal{A}_{p}(x)=x^{k}-qx^{k-1}-pqx^{k-2}-\cdots-p^{k-1}q
.
Barry and Lo Bello
[
1
]
showed that the roots of
\mathcal{A}_{p}(x)
are simple,
which goes to show that there are fruitful connections between different fields of research.
Let us denote the roots of
\mathcal{A}_{p}(x)
by
\lambda_{j}
,
j=0,\dots,k-1
.
Then
\zeta_{j}=2\lambda_{j}
, with
p=q=\frac{1}{2}
.
The following properties of the roots were listed by DM (
[
3
]
Prop. 13).

1.

It is known that
\mathcal{A}_{p}(x)
has a unique positive root, which has a larger magnitude than all the other roots
[
5
]
.
We denote it by
\lambda_{0}
below.

2.

If
k
is odd, there are no other real roots. If
k
is even, there is exactly one real negative root.

3.

For all
k
, the remaining roots come in complex conjugate pairs.

4.

There are no pure imaginary roots.

Wolfram
[
12
]
proved the first three results above for the case of the
k
-generalized Fibonacci numbers.
The last item may not be so well-known.
DM employed the terms ‘pricipal root’ for
\lambda_{0}

and ‘secondary roots’ for the other
\lambda_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,k-1
.

DM obtained expressions for the roots
\lambda_{j}
as power series,
whose coefficients are Fuss-Catalan numbers (
[
3
]
Theorem 1).
They identified three cases, viz. (i)
p<k/(k+1)
, (ii)
p=k/(k+1)
and (iii)
p>k/(k+1)
.
We require only the first case, because we fix
p=\frac{1}{2}
and
k/(k+1)>\frac{1}{2}
for all
k>1
.
First define, for
n\geq 1
,

b_{n}=\frac{\Gamma(n-1+n/k)}{n!\Gamma(n/k)}\,.

(2.4)

The Fuss-Catalan numbers are defined as follows (
[
3
]
eq. (9))

A_{m}(\nu,r)=\frac{r}{\Gamma(m+1)}\frac{\Gamma(m\nu+r)}{\Gamma(m(\nu-1)+r+1)}\,.

(2.5)

Then
b_{n}
is a Fuss-Catalan number (
[
3
]
eq. (10))

b_{n}=-A_{n}(1+1/k,-1)\,.

(2.6)

The principal root
\zeta_{0}
is given by (
[
3
]
eq. (8))

\zeta_{0}=2-2k\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}b_{mk}\,(p^{k}q)^{m}=2-2k\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{b_{mk}}{2^{m(k+1)}}\,.

(2.7)

The secondary roots
\zeta_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,k-1
are given by (
[
3
]
eq. (7))

\zeta_{j}=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}\,(e^{2\pi ij/k}pq^{1/k})^{n}=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}\,\biggl(\frac{e^{2\pi ij/k}}{2^{(k+1)/k}}\biggr)^{n}\,.

(2.8)

Proposition 2.1
.

Wolfram (
[
12
]
Theorem 3.9) derived the following sum for the principal root.
The root was expressed as
\zeta_{0}=2(1-\varepsilon_{k})
and

\varepsilon_{k}=\sum_{i\geq 1}\binom{(k+1)i-2}{i-1}\frac{1}{i2^{(k+1)i}}\,.

(2.9)

Wolfram’s solution is equivalent to our expression in eq. (
2.7
).

Proof.

From eq. (
2.4
), note that

b_{mk}=\frac{\Gamma(mk-1+m)}{(mk)!\Gamma(m)}=\frac{(mk+m-2)!}{(mk!)(m-1)!}=\frac{1}{mk}\binom{(k+1)m-2}{m-1}\,.

(2.10)

Next using eq. (
2.7
), we deduce

\begin{split}\varepsilon_{k}&=k\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{b_{mk}}{2^{(k+1)m}}\\
&=k\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{mk}\binom{(k+1)m-2}{m-1}\frac{1}{2^{(k+1)m}}\\
&=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\binom{(k+1)m-2}{m-1}\frac{1}{m2^{(k+1)m}}\,.\end{split}

(2.11)

This equals eq. (
2.9
), substitute
i
for
m
.
∎

However, Wolfram did not publish expressions for the secondary roots.
Spickerman and Joyner (
[
11
]
Sec. 3) computed the roots numerically
(to
4
decimal places) for
2\leq k\leq 10
.
Using eqs. (
2.7
) and (
2.8
), we confirm their numbers are correct.

It remains now to derive expressions for
F_{k}(n)
for specific initial conditions,
and also to derive asymptotic expressions for
n\gg 1

(in which case the solution is dominated by the contribution from the principal root).
To do so, it will be helpful below to work with the polynomial
B(x)=(x-1)A(x)
for the following reason.
Note that

\begin{split}A(x)&=x^{k}-x^{k-1}-\dots-1\\
&=x^{k}-\frac{x^{k}-1}{x-1}\\
&=\frac{x^{k+1}-2x^{k}+1}{x-1}\,.\end{split}

(2.12)

Hence
B(x)=x^{k+1}-2x^{k}+1
is a trinomial, which is easier to manipulate. However, it contains an extraneous root
x=1
.
Note that
A(1)=1-k\neq 0
, i.e.
x=1
is not a root of
A(x)
, hence
B(x)
has no repeated roots.

3
Properties of roots

We may read off several properties of the roots from DM
[
3
]
.
First, the principal root lies in the interval (
[
3
]
Props. 8 and 11)

2-\frac{2}{k+1}<\zeta_{0}<2-\frac{1}{2^{k}}\,.

(3.1)

Wolfram (
[
12
]
Lemma 3.6) derived the bounds

2(1-2^{-k})<\zeta_{0}<2\,.

(3.2)

Eq. (
3.1
) has a tighter upper bound and
eq. (
3.2
) has a tighter lower bound.
Combining them, we obtain the improved bounds

2-\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}<\zeta_{0}<2-\frac{1}{2^{k}}\,.

(3.3)

Next, DM (
[
3
]
Prop. 14) showed that the secondary roots satisfy
|\lambda_{j}|<p
.
Setting
p=\frac{1}{2}
, this implies
|\zeta_{j}|<1
for
j=1,\dots,k-1
.
Wolfram (
[
12
]
Lemma 3.6) obtained a tighter lower bound

3^{-1/k}<|\zeta_{j}|<1\,.

(3.4)

This corrects a misprint in
[
12
]
, where the lower bound is given as
3^{-k}

(but the value
3^{-1/k}
is stated correctly in the
proof
of Lemma 3.6 in
[
12
]
).
Next, the relative magnitudes of
distinct
roots are as follows (
[
3
]
Prop. 16)

1.

|\zeta_{j_{1}}|=|\zeta_{j_{2}}|
if and only if
\zeta_{j_{1}}=\bar{\zeta}_{j_{2}}
.

2.

\Re(\zeta_{j_{1}})<\Re(\zeta_{j_{2}})
if and only if
|\zeta_{j_{1}}|<|\zeta_{j_{2}}|
.

3.

For even
k
, the negative real root has the most negative
real part, hence the smallest amplitude of all the roots.

Spickerman and Joyner (
[
11
]
Sec. 3) computed the roots numerically
for
2\leq k\leq 10
and their numbers satisfy the above relations.

Distribution of the roots in the complex plane.

Observe the following limits as
k\to\infty
:
(i) for the principal root,
\limsup_{k\to\infty}\zeta_{0}=2
(from eq. (
3.3
)) and
(ii) for all the secondary roots,
\limsup_{k\to\infty}|\zeta_{j}|=1
(from eq. (
3.4
)).
Hence all the secondary roots approach the unit circle (from the inside),
but the principal root approaches
2
(from below).
A graph of the roots in the complex plane is plotted in Fig.
1
, for
k=40
.
For
k\gg 1
, the secondary roots are also distributed approximately uniformly around the unit circle.
A graph of the arguments of the secondary roots, i.e.
\arg(\zeta_{j})/(2\pi)
,
is plotted in Fig.
2
, for
k=40
.
The points lie close to a straight line.

4
Vandermonde

First recall eq. (
2.2
).
We set up a Vandermonde system of equations to solve for the coefficients
c_{j}
as follows

\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\dots&1\\
\zeta_{0}&\zeta_{1}&\dots&\zeta_{k-1}\\
\zeta_{0}^{2}&\zeta_{1}^{2}&\dots&\zeta_{k-1}^{2}\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
\zeta_{0}^{k-1}&\zeta_{1}^{k-1}&\dots&\zeta_{k-1}^{k-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}\\
c_{1}\\
c_{2}\\
\vdots\\
c_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}F_{k}(0)\\
F_{k}(1)\\
F_{k}(2)\\
\vdots\\
F_{k}(k-1)\end{pmatrix}\,.\end{split}

(4.1)

We begin with the initial conditions
F_{k}(0)=\dots=F_{k}(k-2)=0
and
F_{k}(k-1)=1
.
(We treat the initial condition by Spickerman and Joyner
[
11
]
in Sec.
6
.)
The solution is (
[
3
]
, eq. (60))

c_{j}=\frac{1}{\prod_{s\neq j}(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{s})}\,.

(4.2)

Note that

B(x)=(x-1)\prod_{s=0}^{k-1}(x-\zeta_{s})\,.

(4.3)

Hence

B^{\prime}(x)=\frac{B(x)}{x-1}+\sum_{s=0}^{k-1}\frac{B(x)}{x-\zeta_{s}}\,.

(4.4)

Then, noting that
B(\zeta_{j})=0
,

B^{\prime}(\zeta_{j})=(\zeta_{j}-1)\prod_{s\neq j}(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{s})\,.

(4.5)

Also
B^{\prime}(x)=(k+1)x^{k}-2kx^{k-1}
, thus
B^{\prime}(\zeta_{j})=((k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k)\zeta_{j}^{k-1}
.
Hence

\begin{split}c_{j}&=\frac{1}{\prod_{s\neq j}(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{s})}=\frac{\zeta_{j}-1}{B^{\prime}(\zeta_{j})}=\frac{\zeta_{j}-1}{((k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k)\zeta_{j}^{k-1}}\,.\end{split}

(4.6)

Hence the solution for
F_{k}(n)
is

F_{k}(n)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{\zeta_{j}-1}{(k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k}\zeta_{j}^{n-k+1}\,.

(4.7)

This is our expression for the
k
-generalized Fibonacci numbers, as a sum over powers of the roots of the auxiliary polynomial.
The expressions for special cases such as the tribonacci numbers (
k=3
) and tetranacci numbers (
k=4
) are known in radicals.
However eq. (
4.7
) presents a unified formula for all
k\geq 2
.
The use of Fuss-Catalan numbers furnishes a systematic formula to compute the roots to arbitrary accuracy.

Remark 4.1
.

The Binet-style sum in eq. (
4.7
) also works for
n<0
.
The recurrence can be run backwards to negative values of
n
.

Remark 4.2
.

An expression equivalent to eq. (
4.7
) was published by Dresden and Du (
[
4
]
Theorem 1),
where the roots were denoted by
\alpha_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,k
.
However, they did not exhibit expressions for the roots.
They denoted the
k
-generalized Fibonacci numbers by
F_{n}^{(k)}
, with the recurrence

F_{n}^{(k)}=F_{n-1}^{(k)}+\dots+F_{n-k}^{(k)}
, but with the initial conditions
F_{n}^{(k)}=0
for
n<1
and
F_{n}^{(k)}=1
for
n=1
.
The relation to our notation is
F_{k}(n)=F_{n-k+2}^{(k)}
.

5
Asymptotic solution

For fixed
k
and
n\gg k
, the asymptotic solution is given by retaining only
the principal root
\zeta_{0}
in eq. (
4.7
).
The details of the analysis are given in DM (
[
3
]
Corollary 4).
Use
B(\zeta_{0})=0
, i.e.
\zeta_{0}^{k}(\zeta_{0}-2)+1=0
to deduce
\zeta_{0}^{-k}=2-\zeta_{0}
.
Then (c.f.
[
3
]
, eq. (16), case
p\neq k/(k+1)
)

\begin{split}F_{k}(n)&\asymp\frac{\zeta_{0}-1}{(k+1)\zeta_{0}-2k}\zeta_{0}^{n-k+1}\\
&=\frac{(2-\zeta_{0})(\zeta_{0}-1)}{(k+1)\zeta_{0}-2k}\zeta_{0}^{n+1}\,.\end{split}

(5.1)

Following DM, we demand that for fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
the magnitude of the contribution to
F_{k}(n)
in eq. (
4.7
)
from all the secondary roots combined
is less than
\varepsilon
times the contribution from the principal root.
This is achieved if
n\geq N(p,k)
, where (
[
3
]
, eq. (17))

N=k+\frac{\ln(\varepsilon/(k-1))}{\ln\kappa}\,.

(5.2)

In our case
\kappa
is given by (
[
3
]
, eq. (18), case
1/(k+1)<p<k/(k+1)
)

\kappa=\min\biggl\{\frac{k+1}{2k},\,1-\delta\biggr\}\,.

(5.3)

Here (
[
3
]
, eq. (19))

\delta=\frac{1}{(k+1)^{1/k}}\biggl[1+\frac{1}{2^{(k+1)/k}k}-\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{2^{2(k+1)/k}k^{2}}+\frac{\cos(2\pi/k)}{2^{1/k}k}}\biggr]\,.

(5.4)

Then
\delta\to 0
as
k\to\infty
, hence the expression for
\kappa
simplifies to
\kappa=(k+1)/(2k)

and we obtain the bound

N=k+\frac{\ln(\varepsilon/(k-1))}{\ln((k+1)/(2k))}\,.

(5.5)

Remark 5.1
.

The above analysis was leveraged from
[
3
]
.
For the specific application to
k
-generalized Fibonacci numbers, Dresden and Du
[
4
]
derived a simpler expression.
They showed (
[
4
]
Theorem 2) that the contribution of all the secondary roots is less than
\frac{1}{2}
in magnitude,
and
F_{k}(n)
can be obtained from the principal root
\zeta_{0}
alone as follows.
We employ our notation in eq. (
4.7
), whence

F_{k}(n)=\left\lfloor\biggl(\frac{\zeta_{0}-1}{(k+1)\zeta_{0}-2k}\zeta_{0}^{n-k+1}+\frac{1}{2}\biggr)\right\rfloor\,.

(5.6)

6
Alternative initial conditions: Spickerman and Joyner

Spickerman and Joyner
[
11
]
solved the recurrence eq. (
1.1
) as follows.
First to avoid confusions of notation, we follow them and write
u_{n}
in place of
F_{k}(n)
.
They employed the initial conditions

(u_{0},\dots,u_{k-1})=(1,1,2,4,\dots,2^{k-2})
, i.e.
u_{0}=1
and
u_{n}=2^{n-1}
for
1\leq n\leq k-1
.
Hence the next term is
u_{k}=1+1+2+4+\dots+2^{k-2}=2^{k-1}
.
Let us change this slightly and shift the indices down by one (and discard
u_{0}
) and say instead

(v_{0},\dots,v_{k-1})=(1,2,4,\dots,2^{k-1})
, i.e.
v_{n}=2^{n}
for
0\leq n\leq k-1
.
Let us generalize this even more and replace
2
by
\mu
,
and write
w_{n}=\mu^{n}
for
0\leq n\leq k-1
.
Then the solution for
c_{j}
in the Vandermonde matrix equations in Sec.
4
is easy;
it is the Lagrange polynomial
c_{j}=L_{j}(\mu)
.
The Lagrange polynomial
L_{j}(x)
is

L_{j}(x)=\frac{(x-\zeta_{0})\dots(x-\zeta_{j-1})(x-\zeta_{j+1})\dots(x-\zeta_{k-1})}{(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{0})\dots(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{j-1})(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{j+1})\dots(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{k-1})}\,.

(6.1)

This can be obtained from the auxiliary polynomial, written in the form
(see eq. (
2.12
))

A(x)=(x^{k+1}-2x^{k}+1)/(x-1)
.
The denominator of
L_{j}(x)
is
D_{j}=A^{\prime}(\zeta_{j})
and does not depend on
x
.
The numerator of
L_{j}(\mu)
is
N_{j}(\mu)=A(\mu)/(\mu-\zeta_{j})
, provided
\mu
is not a root of
A(x)
.
For now, we assume
\mu
is not a root of
A(x)
.
Then the numerator is

N_{j}(\mu)=\frac{\mu^{k+1}-2\mu^{k}+1}{(\mu-1)(\mu-\zeta_{j})}\,.

(6.2)

The derivative of
A(x)
is

A^{\prime}(x)=\frac{(k+1)x^{k}-2kx^{k-1}}{x-1}-\frac{x^{k+1}-2x^{k}+1}{(x-1)^{2}}\,.

(6.3)

The denominator is given by (note that the second term in
A^{\prime}(x)
vanishes for
x=\zeta_{j}
)

D_{j}=\frac{(k+1)\zeta_{j}^{k}-2k\zeta_{j}^{k-1}}{\zeta_{j}-1}=\frac{(k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k}{\zeta_{j}-1}\zeta_{j}^{k-1}\,.

(6.4)

Hence the coefficient
c_{j}
is

c_{j}=\frac{N_{j}(\mu)}{D_{j}}=\frac{\mu^{k+1}-2\mu^{k}+1}{(\mu-1)(\mu-\zeta_{j})}\frac{\zeta_{j}-1}{(k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k}\frac{1}{\zeta_{j}^{k-1}}\,.

(6.5)

For
\mu=1
, set
\mu=1+\varepsilon
and take the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
to obtain

\lim_{\mu\to 1}c_{j}=\frac{1-k}{(1-\zeta_{j})}\frac{\zeta_{j}-1}{(k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k}\frac{1}{\zeta_{j}^{k-1}}=\frac{k-1}{(k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k}\frac{1}{\zeta_{j}^{k-1}}\,.

(6.6)

What if
\mu
is a root of
A(x)
?

Actually this is easy.
If
\mu=\zeta_{i}
then by construction of the Lagrange polynomials,

L_{i}(\zeta_{i})=1
and
L_{j}(\zeta_{i})=0
for
i\neq j
.
Hence
c_{i}=1
and
c_{j}=0
for
i\neq j
, i.e.
c_{j}=\delta_{ij}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Our expression in eq. (
6.5
), evaluated for
\mu=2
, is equivalent to Spickerman and Joyner’s solution
[
11
]
.

Proof.

Spickerman and Joyner treated only the case
\mu=2
.
We know that
A(2)=1
, hence
2
is not a root of
A(x)
, whence

\begin{split}c_{j}&=\frac{2^{k+1}-2^{k+1}+1}{(2-1)(2-\zeta_{j})}\frac{\zeta_{j}-1}{(k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k}\frac{1}{\zeta_{j}^{k-1}}\\
&=\frac{1}{1/\zeta_{j}^{k}}\frac{\zeta_{j}-1}{(k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k}\frac{1}{\zeta_{j}^{k-1}}\\
&=\zeta_{j}\frac{\zeta_{j}-1}{(k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k}\,.\end{split}

(6.7)

The expression for
u_{n}
by Spickerman and Joyner is (unnumbered, second last line on p. 328 of
[
11
]
)

u_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{(\alpha_{j}^{k+1}-\alpha_{j}^{k})\alpha_{j}^{n}}{2\alpha_{j}^{k}-(k+1)}\,.

(6.8)

They denoted the roots by
\alpha_{j}
, whereas we say
\zeta_{j}
.
We thus express eq. (
6.8
) in the form
u_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}d_{j}\zeta_{j}^{n}
, where

\begin{split}d_{j}&=\frac{(\zeta_{j}^{k+1}-\zeta_{j}^{k})}{2\zeta_{j}^{k}-(k+1)}\\
&=\zeta_{j}^{k}\frac{\zeta_{j}-1}{2\zeta_{j}^{k}-(k+1)}\,.\end{split}

(6.9)

Because of the index shift of
n
by
1
, the relation of
w
to
u
is
w_{n}(\mu=2)=u_{n+1}
.
Our claim is therefore
c_{j}\zeta_{j}^{n}=d_{j}\zeta_{j}^{n+1}
, or
c_{j}=d_{j}\zeta_{j}
.
Employing eqs. (
6.7
) and (
6.9
) yields

\begin{split}\frac{c_{j}-d_{j}\zeta_{j}}{\zeta_{j}(\zeta_{j}-1)}&=\frac{1}{(k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k}-\frac{\zeta_{j}^{k}}{2\zeta_{j}^{k}-(k+1)}\\
&\propto(2\zeta_{j}^{k}-(k+1))-\zeta_{j}^{k}((k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k)\\
&=2\zeta_{j}^{k}-(k+1)-(k+1)\zeta_{j}^{k+1}+2k\zeta_{j}^{k}\\
&=2\zeta_{j}^{k}-2(k+1)\zeta_{j}^{k}+2k\zeta_{j}^{k}\\
&=0\,.\end{split}

(6.10)

Our claim is validated.
∎

Note that although Spickerman and Joyner

7.1
k
-generalized Fibonacci

The
k
-generalized Fibonacci numbers
F_{k}(n)
are the solution
of the recurrence eq. (
1.1
) for only one set of initial conditions,
viz.
F_{k}(0)=\dots=F_{k}(k-2)=0
and
F_{k}(k-1)=1
.
We would like a set of
k
‘basis sequences’ or ‘fundamental solutions’

\{B_{k,i}(n),\,i=0,\dots,k-1\}

of the recurrence eq. (
1.1
),
where the sequence
B_{k,i}(n)
satisfies the initial conditions

B_{k,i}(n)=\delta_{in}
for
n=0,\dots,k-1
.
The
k
-generalized Fibonacci numbers
F_{k}(n)

are the last member of the basis, viz.
F_{k}(n)=B_{k,k-1}(n)
.

Wolfram
[
13
]
solved the problem in a more general setting.
Wolfram treated the homogeneous linear recurrence (
[
13
]
, eq. (2.1))

f(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{k-j}f(n-j)\,.

(7.1)

Here
\{a_{0},\dots,a_{k-1}\}
are a set of coefficients which do not depend on
f
but
can
depend on
n
.
Let us define
\mathscr{W}_{i}(n)
to be the solution of
eq. (
7.1
) with the initial values

\mathscr{W}_{i}(n)=\delta_{in}
for
n=0,\dots,k-1
.
(For brevity, we omit explicit mention of
k
and
a_{0},\dots,a_{k-1}
.)
The set of sequences
\{\mathscr{W}_{i}(n),\,i=0,\dots,k-1\}
are a set of
fundamental solutions of the recurrence eq. (
7.1
).
Wolfram proved that (
[
13
]
Lemma 2.2)

\mathscr{W}_{i}(n)=\sum_{j=0}^{i}a_{i-j}\mathscr{W}_{k-1}(n-j-1)\qquad\qquad(i=0,\dots,k-2)\,.

(7.2)

Hence it is only necessary to solve the recurrence eq. (
7.3
) for the last basis function
\mathscr{W}_{k-1}(n)
.

All the other basis functions can be obtained from it. This is a key insight.

We treat only constant coefficients in this paper.
Let
\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}\}
be a set of constant coefficients and reexpress eq. (
7.1
) as follows.
(Note the indexing of the
\beta_{j}
, to maintain consistency with other usage of indexing in this paper.)

f(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{j}f(n-j)\,.

(7.3)

We define
W_{i}(n)
to be the solution of
eq. (
7.3
) with the initial values

W_{i}(n)=\delta_{in}
for
n=0,\dots,k-1
.
Then, from eq. (
7.2
),

W_{i}(n)=\sum_{s=0}^{i}\beta_{k+s-i}W_{k-1}(n-s-1)\qquad\qquad(i=0,\dots,k-2)\,.

(7.4)

We shall employ
W_{i}(n)
and eq. (
7.4
) in the rest of this paper.

Proposition 7.1
.

The basis sequences for the
k
-generalized Fibonacci numbers are given by

B_{k,k-m}(n)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{\zeta_{j}^{m}-2\zeta_{j}^{m-1}+1}{(k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k}\zeta_{j}^{n-k+1}\qquad(m=1,\dots,k)\,.

(7.5)

Proof.

The
k
-generalized Fibonacci recurrence eq. (
1.1
) is the special case
\beta_{1}=\dots=\beta_{k}=1
of eq. (
7.3
).
It follows immediately from eq. (
7.4
), where our last basis function is
F_{k}(n)
, that

B_{k,i}(n)=\sum_{s=0}^{i}F_{k}(n-s-1)\,.

(7.6)

We employ the expression in eq. (
4.7
) and set
i=k-m
.
In the line tagged by
(*)
below,
we employ the auxiliary equation to deduce
\zeta_{j}^{-k-1}=2\zeta_{j}^{-1}-1
, because
\zeta_{j}^{k+1}-2\zeta_{j}^{k}+1=0
(see eq. (
2.12
)).

\begin{split}B_{k,k-m}(n)&=\sum_{s=0}^{k-m}F_{k}(n-s-1)\\
&=\sum_{s=0}^{k-m}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{\zeta_{j}-1}{(k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k}\frac{\zeta_{j}^{n-k+1}}{\zeta_{j}^{s+1}}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{\zeta_{j}-1}{(k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k}\zeta_{j}^{n-k+1}\biggl(\frac{1}{\zeta_{j}}\sum_{s=0}^{k-m}\frac{1}{\zeta_{j}^{s}}\biggr)\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{\zeta_{j}-1}{(k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k}\zeta_{j}^{n-k+1}\frac{1-\zeta_{j}^{m-k-1}}{\zeta_{j}-1}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{(k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k}\zeta_{j}^{n-k+1}(1-\zeta_{j}^{m}(2\zeta_{j}^{-1}-1))\qquad\qquad(*)\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{(k+1)\zeta_{j}-2k}\zeta_{j}^{n-k+1}(\zeta_{j}^{m}-2\zeta_{j}^{m-1}+1)\,.\end{split}

(7.7)

Rearranging terms in the numerator yields eq. (
7.5
).
∎

Remark 7.2

Observe that eq. (

The dependence on

To the author’s knowledge, eq. (

Next, given a tuple of initial values

We can leave it at this, because eq. (

However, Wolfram’s formalism
[

\begin{split}W(n,k,\vec{\gamma})&=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\gamma_{i}W_{i}(n)\\

8
Companion matrix

This section was prompted by an insightful comment by a referee.
It contains alternative formalism for the solution of linear recurrences with constant coefficients.
Our exposition follows Chen and Louck
[
2
]
.
Recall the recurrence in eq. (
7.3
), reproduced here for ease of reference.

f(n)=\beta_{1}f(n-1)+\cdots+\beta_{k}f(n-k)\,.

(8.1)

The characteristic polynomial of the recurrence in eq. (
8.1
) is

p_{k}(x)=x^{k}-\beta_{1}x^{k-1}-\beta_{2}x^{k-2}-\dots-\beta_{k}
.
The companion matrix
C_{k}
of
p_{k}(x)
is defined as follows (
[
2
]
, eq. (2.1))

C_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}&\beta_{2}&\beta_{3}&\dots&\beta_{k-1}&\beta_{k}\\
1&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&1&0&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\dots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\dots&1&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(8.2)

The characteristic equation of the matrix
C_{k}
is
p_{k}(\lambda)=0
, which is also
the characteristic equation of the recurrence in eq. (
8.1
).
(For the special case
\beta_{1}=\dots=\beta_{k}=1
,
the Fuss-Catalan series for the roots
\zeta_{j}
,
j=0,\dots,k-1
in
eqs. (
2.7
) and (
2.8
)
are the eigenvalues of the companion matrix
C_{k}
.)
Then (
[
2
]
Theorem 3.1) the
(i,j)
entry
c_{i,j}^{(n)}
in the matrix
C_{k}^{n}
is given by
(
[
2
]
eq. (3.1), note that the indexing is
1\leq i,j\leq k
)

c_{i,j}^{(n)}=\sum_{i_{1}+2i_{2}+\dots+ki_{k}=n-i+j}\frac{i_{j}+\dots+i_{k}}{i_{1}+\dots+i_{k}}\binom{i_{1}+i_{2}+\dots+i_{k}}{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}}\,\beta_{1}^{i_{1}}\dots\beta_{k}^{i_{k}}\,.

(8.3)

Also,
c_{i,j}^{(n)}
is defined to equal unity if
n=i-j
.
The companion matrix can be employed to iterate the solution of a
linear recurrence with constant coefficients as follows (
[
2
]
, Sec. 7).
Iterating
n\geq 1
times yields (
[
2
]
eq. (7.3))

\begin{pmatrix}f(n+k-1)\\
f(n+k-2)\\
\vdots\\
f(n)\end{pmatrix}=C_{k}^{n}\,\begin{pmatrix}f(k-1)\\
f(k-2)\\
\vdots\\
f(0)\end{pmatrix}\,.

(8.4)

The value of
f(n+k-i)
in left-hand column vector
is given by the dot product of the
i^{th}
row of the matrix
C_{k}^{n}
with the
k
-tuple of initial values
f(0)
through
f(k-1)
.
For fixed
1\leq j\leq k
,
if the initial
k
-tuple is
(f(0),\dots,f(k-1))=(\dots,\delta_{ij},\dots)
, where “
\dots
” denotes zeroes,
the solution for
f(n+k-i)
is given by
c_{i,j}^{(n)}
,
1\leq i\leq k
.
It is evident that the matrix elements in the
j^{th}
column,
1\leq j\leq k
,
are the elements of the
(k-j)^{th}
basis sequence of the recurrence, viz.
W_{k-j}(n-k-i)=c_{i,j}^{(n)}
, for
1\leq i\leq k
.

Proposition 8.1
.

Wolfram’s Lemma 2.2
[
13
]

can be employed to express
c_{i,j}^{(n)}
for
j=2,\dots,k
in terms of the entries
c_{i,1}^{(\dots)}

in the first column of the companion matrix as follows

c_{i,j}^{(n)}=\sum_{s=0}^{\min(k-j,n-1)}\beta_{j+s}c_{i,1}^{(n-s-1)}\qquad\qquad(j=2,\dots,k)\,.

(8.5)

Proof.

Transcribe the notation in eq. (
7.4
), accounting for differences in the indexing.
∎

Hence for
n>1
it is only necessary to compute the entries in the first column of the companion matrix from scratch;
all the rest are linear combinations of those numbers.
This may be computationally helpful.
Eq. (
8.5
) has been numerically tested and validated.
Wolfram’s paper
[
13
]
was published in 2000, i.e. recently,
hence the above identity may not be known in the literature for companion matrices.
It is not mentioned by Chen and Louck
[
2
]
, whose paper is dated 1996.

Generating functions and multinomial sums

9.1
General remarks

We remark on alternative expressions using generating functions and multinomial sums.
A referee kindly brought to the author’s attention that
the multinomial sum in eq. (
9.4
) below is
a Dickson polynomial of the second kind in several variables.
The connection to Dickson polynomials is shown below.

1.

In this section, the arguments of all functions are nonnegative integers.
One can run all the recurrences backwards,
but the multinomial sums displayed below are valid only for nonnegative arguments.

2.

To avoid ambiguity, we apply a circumflex, e.g.
\hat{f}
, to denote the solution of a recurrence
when the initial
k
-tuple is
(0,\dots,0,1)
.

9.2
k
-generalized Fibonacci

Lee, Lee, Kim and Shin
[
7
]

derived the Vandermonde set of equations, and published the formal solution, but did not find the roots explicitly.
Their last formula (unnumbered) expresses the
k
-generalized Fibonacci numbers using multinomial sums.
In terms of our notation,
F_{k}(0)=\dots=F_{k}(k-2)=0
,
F_{k}(k-1)=1
and for

\begin{split}\hat{F}_{k}(n+k-1)&=\sum_{i_{1}+2i_{2}+\dots+ki_{k}=n}\frac{(i_{1}+i_{2}+\dots+i_{k})!}{i_{1}!i_{2}!\dots i_{k}!}\\

(9.1)

The proof is given by employing the generating function for the
k
-generalized Fibonacci numbers

G(z)=\frac{z^{k-1}}{1-z-z^{2}-\dots-z^{k}}\,.

(9.2)

Expand the denominator using the multinomial theorem and collect terms to obtain the coefficient of

P(n)
are
1,1,1,2,2,3,4,5,7,9,12,16,\dots
.

9.6
Perrin sequence

The Perrin and Padovan sequences satisfy the same recurrence, but with different initial values.
We write ‘
Q(n)
’ to avoid confusion with the Padovan sequence
P(n)
.
The initial values are
Q(0)=3
,
Q(1)=0
and
Q(2)=2
and for
n\geq 3
the recurrence is

Q(n)=Q(n-2)+Q(n-3)\,.

(9.11)

Because the recurrence eq. (
9.11
) is the same as eq. (
9.8
),
the basis functions are the same as in Sec.
9.5
,
viz.
W^{P}_{i}(n)
,
i=0,1,2
.
Using the given initial values, the solution is

\begin{split}Q(n)&=3W^{P}_{0}(n)+2W^{P}_{2}(n)\\
&=3W^{P}_{2}(n-1)+2W^{P}_{2}(n)\\
&=3\bigl(\,\underbrace{W^{P}_{2}(n-1)+W^{P}_{2}(n)}_{=W^{P}_{2}(n+2)}\,\bigr)-W^{P}_{2}(n)\\
&=3W^{P}_{2}(n+2)-W^{P}_{2}(n)\,.\end{split}

(9.12)

We can express the Perrin numbers as a linear combination of the Padovan numbers.
One choice (not unique) is
Q(n)=4P(n)+2P(n+1)-3P(n+2)
.
The first few values of
Q(n)
are
3,0,2,3,2,5,5,7,10,12,17,22,\dots
.
