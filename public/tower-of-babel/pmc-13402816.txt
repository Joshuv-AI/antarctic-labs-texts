Explainable machine learning-based investigation for influential parameters towards interfacial capacity of spliced glass fiber reinforced polymer bars
Authors: Ali Alatify, Abdulaziz Alqurashi, Muath Alsuhaibani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSplicing glass fiber reinforced polymer (GFRP) bars is commonly encountered in practical applications due to limitations associated with rebar length. In turn, this affects the bond stress maintaining the equilibrium between concrete and reinforcement. Also, there is a lack of estimating the bond strength of spliced GFRP-reinforced beams employing machine learning approaches and interpreting the effective parameters. Thus, this paper predicts interfacial capacity of spliced GFRP-reinforced beams using machine learning (ML) models including ordinary least square regression (OLS), artificial neural network (ANN), and extreme gradient boosting algorithm (XGBoost), and compares those models against the established models. A database compromising 120 samples was collected from relevant experimental studies conducted on beam tests. XGBoost model gave the best performance out of the remaining ML models with an R2 of 0.97 for the training set and an R2 value of 0.93 for the testing set, while the comparative models failed in comparison. Also, a refined investigation of the parameters that influence bond strength was carried out through an explainable artificial intelligence (xAI) approach, specifically SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) by employing XGBoost model. Based on SHAP analysis, bar tensile strength, splice length of the rebar, and bar diameter were the most influential features on the bond resistance. Accordingly, a proposed model to estimate the bond strength of spliced GFRP bars as a function of most contributing parameters was developed and proposed to compute the bond strength in case of spliced GFRP bars. As a result, the proposed equation demonstrated an acceptable performance to predict in comparison with the existing empirical models.Keywords: GFRP, Bond strength, Splice, Machine learning, SHAP, Explainable artificial intelligence (xAI)Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Glass fiber reinforced polymer (GFRP) rebars are widely employed in bridges as a sustainable alternative due to inevitable issues associated with conventional steel reinforced concrete structures, particularly in severely corrosive environments. Besides, GFRP bars possess higher tensile strength and lighter weight in comparison with conventional steel rebars as well as nonconductive to heat and electricity. However, GFRP reinforcing bars are manufactured with a limited length for the ease of transportation which leads to splicing the bars at different structural applications such as change in bar diameter and construction joints1,2. Therefore, overlapping GFRP bars impacts the bond behavior of the reinforcement to concrete as the serviceability of GFRP-reinforced concrete members relies on a proper resistance within the rebar-concrete interface3. Equilibrium of a structural member is maintained by adequate bond strength between the concrete substrate and the reinforcement since disparity in strain of constituent materials after loading urges bond stress within the interface which is confronted by mechanical interlocking, chemical adhesion, and friction responses.

Endeavors are exerted to examine the bond strength between spliced GFRP bars and concrete by primarily conducting experimental programs which require time and cost as well as physical effort. Tighiouart et al.2 investigated the effects of bar size and splice length on the bond strength between GFRP bars and concrete and found that the splice length should be 1.3 of the development length as a requirement for the load transfer. Also, previous researchers studied the effect of splice length, bar size, bar surface treatment, and type of concrete on bond capacity of the GFRP bars1,3–5. In addition, the bond strength was experimentally evaluated due to the influence of splice length, surface deformation, modulus of elasticity, axial rigidity, and bar casting position6. Wu et al.7 carried out an experiment investigating the impact of the spacing of stirrups along the bar splice length, bar diameter, loading type, and type of spliced bar on the bond behavior of GFRPs. However, there is no comprehensive experimental work which encompasses the majority of the factors affecting the bond behavior of spliced GFRP bars. Machine learning approaches, therefore, are data-based techniques which have capabilities to combine multiple input parameters and predict the bond resistance of GFRP reinforcement in addition to minimizing the cost accompanied by experimental programs.

Machine learning (ML) approaches are adopted and utilized in different aspects pertain to structural engineering. A lot of studies had utilized machine learning approaches in structural applications8–11. Farouk et al.12 established a dataset comprising of 333 pull-out tests for predicting the ultimate bond strength between ultra high-performance concrete (UHPC) and steel reinforcement bars using machine learning models, namely improved eliminate particle swamp optimization hybridized artificial neural network (IEPANN), particle swamp optimization hybridized artificial neural network (PANN), support vector regression (SVR), and multiple linear regression (MLR), and conducted sensitivity analysis with Monte Carlo simulation. After comparing machine learning models with different design codes and empirical models, it was concluded that IEPANN reflected acceptable accuracy based on the evaluation metrics (correlation coefficient (R), mean absolute error (MAE), root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and normalized Reference Index (RI)). A database consisting of 159 data points was collected to estimate the bond strength between GFRP bars and concrete using artificial neural network (ANN) and genetic programming (GP), and it was found that ANN and GP models exhibited a reliable predictive ability of a MAE of 1.06 and 0.76 MPa, respectively13. Golafshani et al.14 developed artificial neural network (ANN) and fuzzy logic (FL) models seeking the prediction of the bond strength of spliced steel bars in concrete. Zhang et al.15 estimated the bond strength of fiber reinforced polymer (FRP) bars to seawater sea sand concrete (SWSSC) based on ensemble learning models, namely, random forest (RF) and XGBoost, with including 13 input parameters. Moreover, FRP-to-concrete bond strength under diverse exposure conditions was estimated utilizing machine learning approaches (linear regression (LR), decision tree (DT), random forest (RF) and XGBoost) with incorporating 12 parameters (concrete strength (f’c), width of concrete block (bc), bonded length (Lf), bonded width (bf), thickness (tf), elastic modulus (Ef) and tensile strength (ff) of FRP, the exposure type (Et), exposure condition (Ec), temperature (T), relative humidity (RH), and exposure duration (ED))16. The bond strength between ultra-high performance concrete (UHPC) and reinforcing bars was predicted using a combination of nine machine learning models classified into three types linear models, tree models, and artificial neural networks (ordinary least squares, ridge regression, Bayesian linear regression, support vector machine, decision tree, random forest, AdaBoost, gradient boosting decision tree, and artificial neural network)17. Despite the previous reported studies, there are limited efforts dedicated to appraising influence of parameters on the bond strength of GFRP bars and concrete employing machine learning methods, especially the interfacial capacity between spliced GFRP rebar and concrete in beam tests. Also, there is a lack of experimental data which necessitates machine learning predictive models to elucidate the effective factors influencing the bond behavior of spliced GFRP bars.

Despite the efforts towards investigating bond strength, there is a lack of estimating the bond strength of spliced GFRP-reinforced beams employing machine learning approaches and interpreting the effective parameters involved in this aspect.

In this paper, the aim is to establish a database estimating the bond strength of spliced GFRP-reinforced concrete beams using data-driven machine learning models to take advantage of such soft computing techniques in mitigating the cost and time allied with experimental projects in addition to analyzing the contributing features towards the bond strength of spliced GFRP-reinforced beams. Data from experimental studies carried out on spliced GFRP-reinforced concrete beams were collected to train and validate data-driven models which were composed of 120 instances. The experimental data were gathered from beam bending tests reported in relevant academic papers. Three machine learning models, ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, artificial neural network (ANN) and extreme gradient boosting tree (XGBoost) were utilized for training and testing the database; SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), then, was performed to analyze and explain the effect of input variables on the bond strength. Accordingly, an empirical equation to predict the bond strength was proposed based on most influential parameters.

Methodology

Dataset development

For establishing a database in order to predict the bond strength of the spliced GFRP bars and during the data collection phase, 165 data instances were collected from multiple experiments conducted on spliced GFRP reinforced concrete beams: Tekle et al.4, Tighiouart et al.2, Harajli and Abouniaj3, Esfahani et al.5, Pay et al.6, Rakhshanimehr et al.18, Wu et al.7, Zemour et al.19, Aly20, Al-Salloum et al.21, Abbas et al.22. Initially, we have used relevant keywords to include several studies that are published in distinguish journals. We selected the studies by including the ones that have standardized the collection of data points. These standardization procedures are studies using the same measuring metrics, number of parameters, and achieving the same output. Ultimately, eleven studies are included in this research. Table 1 shows the summary of the collected data. After compiling data, they were cleaned including discarding unsuitable data (excluding specimens that do not include transverse reinforcement within the shear span, or overlapped GFRP bars, and removing replicated beams), filling in some data with missing items rationally, and then integrating the clean data to finally constitute the database with a total of 120 samples that can be used for machine learning models. Because the parameters have different scales, training the model directly would let large-magnitude parameters dominate and slow convergence. To prevent this, the input parameters were standardized so each parameter has zero mean and unit standard deviation. The data driven from experiments are categorized into nine input parameters which include bar surface treatment, bar diameter (db), splice length (), concrete cover (C), beam width (b), beam height (h), concrete compressive strength (, elastic modulus of rebar (), and rebar tensile strength (, in addition to the output which is the bond stress of the GFRP rebar-concrete interface (). Also, all data collected were obtained from beam tests, and the statistics of each individual variable are given in Table 1.

Evaluation metrics

The evaluation of the models was judged by calculating root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and determination coefficient (R2). RMSE calculates the sum of squared differences between the observed data and the estimated ones and divides the sum by the sample size, while MAE determines the sum of absolute differences of the actual and predicted data divided by the sample size. R2 measures the degree of fitting between the predicted and observed data and highlights the variance in the model. Those can be obtained by Eqs. (1)–(3) for MSE, MAE, and R, respectively.

Two nonlinear machine learning models (ANN and XGBoost) and one linear model (OLS) which were selected to estimate the bond strength of spliced GFRP behavior, are described in this section as well as SHAP analysis.

Artificial neural network (ANN)

Artificial neural network ANN is an intelligent mathematical system imitating information depicted by a biological system23 which facilitates the predictive process of a certain matter24. ANN is predominant for solving different tasks such as regression, classification and pattern recognition25 and broadly applied for prediction problems in structural engineering26–28. Typically, ANN is composed of multiple layers (input layer, hidden layer, and output layer), and each layer is formed of a number of neurons that link layers with weighted values. An activation function is required for computing the calculations in the hidden layers, and this activation function adopts a nonlinear pattern such as sigmoid, or tanh.

XGBoost

Extreme gradient boosting algorithm (XGBoost) is an ensemble machine learning algorithm known for its adaptability and performance that incorporates errors involved with the weak models into training process series to perform prediction29. In addition to taking into account the weakness of the model, XGBoost is distinguished from the other decision tree algorithms by the use of the simple form of the expansion of second-order Taylor formula accompanied with the loss function. Plus, potential overfitting is avoided due to the inclusion of a regular term for the objective function. The detailed theory behind XGBoost model can be found in15,28,29.

SHapley additive exPlanations (SHAP)

The prediction produced by machine learning models needs to be explained in order to assess the contribution of each individual input feature to the outcomes. For this reason, an explainable artificial intelligence (xAI) approach can be implemented which is known in practice for long time until being introduced with this unique term30. Some of the earlier xAI methods attempted to interpret the complexity of ML approaches were Layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP) and local interpretable model agnostic explanations (LIME)30. Currently, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) is one of the latest xAI techniques which can interpret the feature importance by allocating a value reflecting the significance of each input parameter on predictions31. SHAP is widely recognized for its analytical precision and conceptual clarity32,33, and its ability to provide consistent interpretable explanations of complex machine learning model predictions on local and global dimensions34. Implementation

The data were collected from previous reports to train and validate ML models (one linear model and two nonlinear models) and also compared against the ACI design equation and some previously published models for the bond strength of FRP bars. After establishing a database of 120 instances, a random split of the dataset (80% for training set (96 samples) and 20% for testing set (24 samples)) was used with ten repeated train and test experiments to assess estimation accuracy by reducing the bias that can arise from randomly dividing data into training and testing sets. OLS, ANN, and XGBoost were developed and trained using the training dataset and then evaluated utilizing the testing data according to the evaluation metrics. It should be noted that the parameters related to each ML model were optimized and adjusted during the training phase which resulted in the optimal model properties (Table 2). In this paper, the ANN model was optimized by repeatedly training the data and adjusting the network parameters until reaching an optimal solution with forward propagation, and a sigmoid function was adopted as an activation function with two hidden layers consisting of 50 neurons for each hidden layer. With respect to the XGBoost hyperparameter settings in this paper, the number of trees was set to 80 estimators with a learning rate of 0.01, while the maximum depth of the tree was set to 6. After achieving the best ML models, a comparison between those models versus the existing models was made. A parametric analysis utilizing SHAP technique was performed to appraise the effect of each input on the bond strength of spliced GFRP bars considering the optimal ML model. Results and analysis

The performance of the ML models was assessed based on the evaluation metrics described in the previous section to measure their effectiveness on prediction. According to Pearson correlation coefficients in Fig. 1, it seems there is no linear relationship between the bond strength of spliced GFRP bars and the input parameters, but rather it is a nonlinear relationship.

Model assessment

In this study, ten repeated experiments were applied to the dataset for each algorithm with random seed initialization, and the average and standard deviation results from the ten runs are presented in Table 3 which shows the average results of ten experiments. Machine learning models (ANN and XGBoost) markedly outperform OLS and the empirical formulations (ACI, Mousavi, Yoo). XGBoost achieved the best performance (training: RMSE = 0.182, MAE = 0.110, R2 = 0.97; testing: RMSE = 0.335, MAE = 0.251, R2 =0.93), closely followed by ANN model (training: RMSE = 0.187, MAE = 0.132, R2 = 0.97; testing: RMSE = 0.407, MAE = 0.309, R2 =0.91). OLS yielded moderate predictive ability (test: R2 = 0.77) while the empirical models exhibit very large errors and near‑zero R2, indicating poor fit to the collected experimental data. The relatively small gap between training and testing errors for ANN and XGBoost suggested good generalization and limited overfitting, whereas empirical methods consistently underpredicted or overpredicted across both sets. Figure 3 further assesses the XGBoost model; the scatter plot of all data demonstrates tight clustering around the ideal 1:1 trend, and the testing residual plot shows residuals concentrated near zero with no obvious systematic bias8, supporting the numeric metrics that XGBoost provided accurate and unbiased predictions across the dataset; the error declined with the number of estimators (Fig. 3c). The tree‑based ensemble model (XGBoost) captured the complex relationships in the splice‑GFRP beam data better than ANN, linear regression, and the established empirical formulas.

SHAP analysis

The effect of each input parameter was evaluated by the SHAP analysis. Since the performance of the XGBoost model on prediction was the best among the rest of the models, it was chosen to conduct the parametric analysis. It is worth noting that the SHAP values mean the contribution of each individual parameter to the average bond strength as in Eq. (5) where the positive and negative summation of the SHAP value was added to or subtracted from the average bond strength. A summary plot showing the SHAP values associated with the input parameters is depicted in Fig. 4 which represents the fluctuation in magnitude from low to high. It has been found that the bar tensile strength, the splice length of the rebar, and the bar diameter, were the most influential factors on the bond strength of spliced GFRP rebars followed by the rest of the parameters. This is attributed to the effectiveness of the mechanical and geometric properties of the GFRP rebars. The influence of each individual parameter towards the bond strength of spliced GFRP-reinforced beams was particularly discussed in this section.

Summary plot of SHAP analysis.

Bar surface treatment

The effect of four sorts of bar surface treatment was assessed in this paper which they are grooved, sand coated, helically wrapped and ribbed rebars. Figure 5 illustrates the contribution of each different surface treatments. For the grooved rebars, the specimens resulted in constant effects indicating steady resistance. In the case of sand coated and ribbed surfaces, the contribution was unfavorable and mitigated the bond strength since they exhibited the lowest bond strength due to the absence of mechanical interlocking, relying primarily on weak adhesion and friction. However, helically wrapped bars were significantly beneficial for increasing the bond capacity as the surface irregularities enhance mechanical interlock and resistance to slip. Overall, albeit helically wrapped surface enhanced bond resistance, it can be noticed that the effect of the bar surface is minor which is supported by a conclusion that stated bond strength is not controlled by the bar surface6.

SHAP values for bar surface.

Bar diameter

Figure 6 is about the effect of the bar diameter on the bond strength. As the bar diameter decreased, the bond strength increased since the highest SHAP value was given when the bar diameter was 10 mm; obviously, at larger diameters the bond strength declined in a nonlinear degradation pattern. This observation implies that the bond between the concrete and the reinforcing GFRP bars was weakened by larger bar size due to developing gaps around the rebar5 which yields in a dissipated bond force around the bonded region.

SHAP values for bar diameter.

Splice length

The effect of the splice length on the bond strength is shown in Fig. 7. It is noted that the trend was quite evident, pointing out that the bond strength was mitigated with larger splicing length. As the splice length ranged between 180 mm and 500 mm, the SHAP values were positive while the opposite was noticed when the lapping length exceeded 500 mm. This aligns with the findings reported previously4,6, and it can be justified by the fact that the bond strength is inversely proportional to the splice length.

SHAP values for splice length.

Concrete cover

The contribution of concrete cover to the bond stress is shown in Fig. 8. It was noticed that the SHAP value increased with increasing the concrete cover. Before a concrete cover of 30 mm, the majority of specimens showed negative SHAP values after which positive values were observed although three samples with a cover of 38 mm resulted in negative. That is because a larger concrete cover leads to a higher resistance by concrete to the circumferential stress caused by the rebar. Therefore, a larger concrete cover was favourable for the bond strength, and it can be stated the relationship between concrete cover and the interfacial capacity was linear.

Beam width

The influence of the beam width on the bond strength is shown on Fig. 9. As the beam width increases, bond strength remains at an almost uniform rate due to enhanced confinement and reduced splitting tendencies. However, after 150 mm, the effect was varying between positive and negative values which is not apparent to conclude a conspicuous tendency. This necessitates a further investigation to elucidate the effect of beam width on bond resistance of spliced GFRP-reinforced beams.

SHAP values for beam width.

Beam height

As for the effect of beam height, Fig. 10 represents that a smaller beam depth can be advantageous for lifting the bond resistance. When the height increased, the SHAP values decreased accordingly with a decaying trend pointing out that a larger beam height would weaken the bond strength.

SHAP values for beam height.

Concrete compressive strength

SHAP values for concrete compressive strength are depicted in Fig. 11. An ascending trend was observed for the effect of the concrete compressive strength on the bond stress which reflected a linear increase in the bond strength as the concrete strength increased regardless of some data points that showed reverse forecasting. This observation is consistent with recent findings38. From mechanical point of view, higher concrete strength produces higher resistance to pullout force during the bar slippage to retain equilibrium thereby elevating the bond strength. Constituents of concrete play a crucial role in enhancing the bond capacity, especially the concrete that does not involve coarse aggregate in its mix reinforces bonding to the GFRP bars by mitigating voids around bonded zones due to having condensed mix.

SHAP values for concrete strength.

Elastic modulus of rebar

Figure 12 is showing SHAP values for elastic modulus of the spliced GFRP bar and measuring its impact on the bond strength. Up to 40 GPa, most of the samples fell in negative for SHAP values with a linear gradual growth; this observation is supported by specimens with an elastic modulus of 66 GPa which represented positive values improving the bond capacity. Thus, a higher tensile elastic modulus leads to increasing interfacial resistance in a linear pattern6.

Bar tensile strength

As shown in Fig. 13, the effect of bar tensile strength on the bond capacity of GFRP rebars is significant. As expected, tensile strength of rebar was the most contributing feature to bond stress (Fig. 4). The SHAP values gradually increased from around − 1.5 to 0 due to the increment of tensile strength from 180 MPa to 400 after which the contribution became explicit with positive SHAP values. This affirms that the higher bar tensile strength is crucial for boosting the bonding capacity within the concrete-GFRP interface. The influence of bar strength on bond strength formed an ascending tendency. Also, it is worth mentioning that increasing bar strength in tandem with enlarging reinforcement area would lead to augmenting the pullout force which is resisted by concrete strength within the surface of interface.

Limit for design and practice

Although the proposed model provides a simplified and reasonably accurate prediction of bond strength, several limitations should be considered before employing it in structural design practice. The model was empirically developed based on a particular experimental dataset; therefore, its utilization is contingent upon the input ranges covered by the dataset in this paper. Also, it should be noted that the coefficients and were optimized using the GRG method for the current dataset which means that these coefficients may not remain optimal for other datasets, but rather there may be a need for recalibration.

It is worth noting that a future work to improve the proposed model is recommended by creating a user-friendly tool such as a graphical user interface (GUI), a standalone application, or a web-based platform.

Conclusion

This paper established a dataset to estimate the bond strength of spliced GFRP-reinforced beams utilizing three ML models (OLS, ANN, and XGBoost). Input features were analyzed using an explainable artificial intelligence (xAI) approach (SHAP) to appraise the effect of each parameter. Also, a proposed equation to predict the bond stress was developed according to the most contributing parameters and compared against the empirical and XGBoost models. As a result, the following findings can be concluded:

XGBoost model demonstrated an outstanding performance among the ML models for both training and testing datasets and was employed for SHAP analysis.
