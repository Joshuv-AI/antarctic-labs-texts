Competing Endogenous RNA Networks Reveal Long Non-coding RNAs as Potential Prognostic Biomarkers in Gastric Cancer: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Sadra Salehi-Mazandarani, Mohammad Hossein Donyavi, Amirhossein Vedaei, Alireza Najimi, Ziba Farajzadegan, Parvaneh Nikpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Gastric cancer (GC) represents a global healthcare challenge. Recently, many competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network studies have elucidated critical long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) as potential prognostic biomarkers in GC. Since there is no systematic review and meta-analysis regarding the lncRNAs as ceRNA in GC, we propose the current study.Materials and Methods:The Web of Science, Embase, PubMed, Scopus, ProQuest, and Google Scholar databases were searched to collect eligible ceRNA network studies in which lncRNAs were reported as prognostic biomarkers until 20th September 2024. Prognostic values of highly reported lncRNAs were examined. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis, and publication bias were evaluated.Results:Totally, 56 studies were included in the systematic review. Based on these studies, the association of 350 unique lncRNAs with overall survival of GC patients were evaluated. 28 studies were eligible for meta-analysis. Four lncRNAs including ADAMTS9-AS2, AL391152.1, CCDC144NL-AS1, and PVT1 were the most reported prognostic biomarkers. Meta-analysis confirmed the prognostic effect of CCDC144NL-AS1 (hazard ratio (HR) = 1.577 (95% confidence interval (CI): 1.382-1.801) in GC.Conclusion:Our meta-analysis demonstrated potential application of lncRNA CCDC144NL-AS1 based on the ceRNA network studies as a promising prognostic biomarker in GC. Further studies are needed to elucidate the functions of this lncRNA in GC.Registration:The review protocol was registered on The International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42022360864).

INTRODUCTION

According to the information of the global cancer statistics, gastric cancer (GC) has the fifth highest rates of incidence and mortality among the different types of cancers.[1] The incidence of GC is variable in different countries.[2] Most of the cases are diagnosed in eastern Asia such as Japan and China, Eastern Europe, and Central and South America.[2] Although earlier detection and reduced Helicobacter pylori (HP) infection have led to the decreased incidence of GC, it is still a serious concern.[3] Despite the improvement in treatment methods, the survival rate for GC patients remains low.[2] GC is a complex disease, and many factors participate in its initiation and progression.[4] The risk of GC has a direct association with increased age and is higher in men rather than in women.[4] HP infects nearly half of the worldʼs population and is a primary risk factor in GC. Some other risk factors for this type of cancer include blood type A, familial adenomatous polyposis syndrome, Peutz–Jegher syndrome (PJS), salt intake, smoking, and alcohol.[45]

Identification of novel non-invasive prognostic biomarkers with high sensitivity and specificity has always been a hot topic in the field of cancer research. Therefore, many studies have introduced novel prognostic biomarkers and evaluated their accuracy in GC. One of the promising types of prognostic biomarkers in GC that have recently received great attention, are long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs).[6]

lncRNAs are a group of noncoding RNAs with a length of longer than 200 nucleotides.[7] Although they are not translated to proteins, they have critical physiological roles in cellular differentiation, stress, aging, proliferation, apoptosis, and transcriptional regulation.[8] Dysregulation of lncRNAs in cancers including GC has been identified.[6] They affect tumor formation, progression, and metastasis.[9] Studies have revealed the diagnostic and prognostic role of lncRNAs in GC. For instance, in a study on 1314 samples, meta-analysis showed that exosomal lncRNAs are potential diagnostic biomarkers in GC.[10] The potential role of lncRNAs as potential biomarkers in GC has been reviewed.[11] One of the most studied ways by which lncRNAs participate in tumorigenesis is their role as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs). In fact, lncRNAs as well as other types of RNAs such as messenger RNAs (mRNAs) and circular RNAs (circRNA), which have the same microRNA (miRNA) response elements (MREs), compete with each other for binding to the miRNAs.[12] This crosstalk among various kinds of RNAs forms the ceRNA networks that can promote tumorigenesis. The study of these networks can provide a deep view of molecular and cellular characteristics underlying GC progression and introduce novel prognostic biomarkers.

Over the last years, many studies have been conducted to identify ceRNA networks in GC. These studies mostly have some special steps consisting of data retrieval, identification of differentially-expressed RNAs (DERs), prediction of DERs interactions, ceRNA network construction, functional enrichment and finally survival analyses. Because of high accessibility to the different methods and databases for survival analysis of the RNA expression data, survival analysis has been somewhat an inseparable part of ceRNA network studies. As these studies frequently retrieve data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)[13] and gene expression omnibus (GEO)[14] with large sample sizes, their results are highly reliable. The survival analyses in these types of studies have revealed the association between many lncRNAs and the overall survival (OS) of GC patients. For example, in one of these studies, Tang et al.,[15] identified differentially-expressed mRNAs, miRNAs, and lncRNAs in GC based on the TCGA data and constructed a ceRNA network including 69 lncRNAs, 204 mRNAs, and 10 miRNAs. By performing survival analysis on the lncRNAs of the network, they reported the association of 13 lncRNAs with OS of GC patients. In another study, using TCGA data, a lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA ceRNA network in GC was established. Then, survival analysis revealed a significant association of seven lncRNAs (ADAMTS9-AS2, ARHGEF26-AS1, HOTAIR, HOTTIP, LIC00052, NKX2-1-AS1, and VCAN-AS1) from the network with OS of GC patients.[16] Due to the lack of a systematic review and meta-analysis regarding lncRNAs which have been reported as potential prognostic biomarkers from ceRNA network studies in GC, this meta-analysis set out to investigate the prognostic value of these lncRNAs.

Literature search strategy

Online literature search was conducted up to September 20, 2024, in the Web of Science, Embase, PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane Central Library using a developed search query [Supplementary File 1] (https://doi.org/10.17632/9nsm2gt5cm.1). Gray literature was also searched on ProQuest and Google Scholar. Furthermore, reference lists of included studies were manually searched for studies potentially meeting the eligibility criteria.

Screening, quality assessment, and data extraction

Records were imported to EndNote software version. X8.0.1 and duplicated studies were omitted. Two reviewers (SSM and MHD) independently screened the titles and abstracts to select the relevant studies. Subsequently, the full texts of the selected studies were evaluated based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreement was solved through discussion with another reviewer (PN) until a consensus was reached.

After full-text examination, the quality of the remaining studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for cohort studies.[18] This checklist consists of 11 questions with possible responses: Yes, No, Unclear, or Not Applicable. The critical appraisal was independently conducted by two reviewers (SSM and MHD). In cases of disagreement that could not be resolved through discussion, a third reviewer (PN) was invited to participate. Studies that scored greater than 7 (each “Yes” answer counted as one point, while “No” counted as zero), were considered high-quality and were included in the next step (data extraction).

An Excel datasheet was designed for data extraction, and two reviewers (SSM and MHD) independently extracted the data from the included studies. Extracted information consists of the name of the first author, year of publication, country of origin (the country of the corresponding author), origin of samples, sample size, the name of lncRNA (s), hazard ratio (HR), P value, 95% confidence interval (CI), follow-up time, and references. A third reviewer (PN) was consulted in case of disagreement. Univariate and multivariate OS analysis reports assessing the association of a single lncRNA with various clinical variables—such as age, radiation therapy, TNM stage, histologic grade, and tumor size—were included in the current study. Basis of HR examination (high expression group of lncRNA divided by low group or vice versa) was also considered. In case of missing information, authors were contacted via email.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using STATA software version. 17.0. In all of the included studies in the meta-analysis part, the basis of HR examination was clear and similar (high expression group/low expression group). Due to the high number of lncRNAs in the systematic part, in meta-analysis, lncRNAs that were identified at least in three studies (according to both univariate and multivariate survival analysis) with reported HR, CI, and P value, were analyzed. Forest plots were utilized to estimate the association of the expression of these lncRNAs with OS of GC patients.

I2 index was used to assess the between-study heterogeneity, If the heterogeneity was substantial (>50%, P value < 0.05), the random effect model would be selected, otherwise, the fixed effect model would be utilized. lncRNAs with HR > 1 and HR <1 were interpreted as risk and protective factors, respectively. These interpretations were based on statistically significant CIs. For lncRNAs as risk factors, GC patients with higher expression of them, have shorter OS while for lncRNAs as protective factors, GC patients with higher expression of them have longer OS. P value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant for the survival results. In addition, Beggs and Egger’s tests[19] were performed to assess the publication bias, and P value < 0.05 was considered as significant publication bias. Sensitivity analysis was performed using leave-one-out meta-analysis (iteratively excluding individual studies) for the meta-analyzed lncRNAs.

Overall, 56 studies were recruited in the current study after selection by the mentioned criteria. These studies included 172 lncRNAs (including unique and repeated lncRNAs). Meta-analysis reveals CCDC144NL-AS1 as a prognostic biomarker in GC

lncRNAs reported based on at least three studies were considered as highly frequently reported lncRNAs. Among the 28 studies with reported HR, CI, and P value, 13 studies introducing high frequently reported lncRNAs were included in the meta-analysis[15162627282930313233343536] [Supplementary File 4] (https://doi.org/10.17632/9nsm2gt5cm.1). Meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the prognostic significance of four lncRNAs, including CCDC144NL-AS1, PVT1, ADAMTS9-AS2, and AL391152.1, in GC. These lncRNAs were selected based on the lncRNAs being reported in at least three independent studies with available HRs, 95% CI, and P value. The pooled analysis revealed that CCDC144NL-AS1 is significantly associated with OS in GC patients, with a combined HR of 1.577 (95% CI: 1.382-1.801, P value < 0.001), indicating its potential as a prognostic biomarker. This association was consistent across all five studies, showing no heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%, P value = 0.928), and the fixed-effects model was used for pooling. For PVT1, the pooled HR is 1.146 (95% CI: 1.033-1.273, P value = 0.010), suggesting a modest but statistically significant association with poor clinical outcomes. However, high heterogeneity was observed among the studies (I² = 91.8%, P value < 0.001), and results were therefore pooled using a random-effects model. Due to asymmetry in the funnel plot, the trim-and-fill method was applied. After adjustment, the association lost statistical significance, suggesting that the original finding may have been influenced by publication bias. Therefore, the prognostic value of PVT1 should be interpreted with caution. In contrast, ADAMTS9-AS2 shows no significant association with survival (HR = 0.947, 95% CI: 0.796-1.127, P value = 0.542), and although AL391152.1 demonstrates a trend toward poor prognosis (HR = 1.165, 95% CI: 0.980-1.385, P value = 0.083), this result does not reach statistical significance. Both lncRNAs exhibit substantial heterogeneity (I² = 87.2% and I² = 88.6%, respectively; both P value < 0.001). Forest plots summarizing these findings are shown in Figure 3.

Heterogeneity assessment

Heterogeneity across studies was evaluated using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. For CCDC144NL-AS1, no significant heterogeneity was observed (I² = 0.0%, Q = 0.88, P value = 0.928), indicating a high level of consistency in effect sizes across the included studies. In contrast, substantial heterogeneity was detected among studies for the remaining lncRNAs: PVT1 (I² = 91.8%, Q = 48.61, P value < 0.001), ADAMTS9-AS2 (I² = 87.2%, Q = 15.67, P value < 0.001), and AL391152.1 (I² = 88.6%, Q = 17.60, P value < 0.001). These findings suggest considerable variability in the prognostic effects of these lncRNAs across studies, warranting cautious interpretation of the pooled estimates and supporting the use of random-effects models for meta-analysis. Publication bias

Publication bias was first assessed using Begg’s test. No significant evidence of publication bias was observed for any of the assessed lncRNAs. However, given the small number of studies (n ≤ 5), these tests may be underpowered to detect bias. The funnel plot for CCDC144NL-AS1 appeared symmetrical. Although the funnel plots for ADAMTS9-AS2 and AL391152.1 appeared asymmetric, the trim-and-fill analysis did not impute any missing studies (data not shown), suggesting no statistically detectable bias, though this conclusion is limited by the small number of studies. For PVT1, the pooled HR initially indicated a potential prognostic effect. However, due to visual asymmetry in the funnel plot, the trim-and-fill method was applied to account for possible publication bias. Following this adjustment, the estimated effect size decreased and lost statistical significance, suggesting that the original results may have been influenced by missing studies. Therefore, the findings regarding PVT1 should be interpreted with caution. Publication bias was furthermore assessed using Egger’s test. No significant publication bias was found for ADAMTS9-AS2, CCDC144NL-AS1, and PVT1. However, the Egger’s test for small-study effects of AL391152.1 was significant (P value = 0.019), indicating potential publication bias. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimates. For CCDC144NL-AS1, the leave-one-out method showed that the pooled HR remained stable, confirming the reliability of the findings. For PVT1, the pooled estimate demonstrated relative stability despite high heterogeneity. For ADAMTS9-AS2, the pooled estimate indicated minimal changes when individual studies were omitted. For AL391152.1, omitting the study conducted by Tang et al.[15] had the most substantial impact, changing the pooled estimate, while omitting the other studies resulted in minimal changes. These results suggest that the findings for CCDC144NL-AS1 are highly robust, while the results for the other lncRNAs should be interpreted with caution due to substantial heterogeneity and potential publication bias.

DISCUSSION

GC is one of the most common types of cancer around the world.[1] Despite current treatment options such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, GC mainly reduces the quality of life. The survival rate of GC patients has improved in recent years; however, it is still poor.[2] Therefore, identification of novel biomarkers to predict the survival rates of GC patients is needed.

In most of the ceRNA network studies, in which the competition among various types of RNAs for binding to the same miRNAs is examined, the association of expression of ceRNAs with the survival of GC patients is evaluated.[3032] To select the most relevant prognostic ceRNAs, researchers apply different strategies including selection of hub RNAs (mostly based on the topological features of the ceRNA networks), differentially expressed RNAs or RNAs interacting with other well-known RNAs. They perform univariate and multivariate survival analyses. The results of multivariate analysis are more reliable than univariate because they consider cofounding effects of clinical variables such as age and sex.[37]

Furthermore, most of these studies use databases like TCGA and GEO for data retrieval or analysis. These databases have mostly large sample sizes with long follow-up times, which makes their results highly reliable.[3839] Up to now, a large number of ceRNA network studies have been published, and many types of RNAs have been reported as potential prognostic biomarkers in GC. One of the most reported of these RNAs is lncRNAs. lncRNAs have been shown to be dysregulated in GC, affecting GC cells’ progression, migration, metastasis, and apoptosis.[4041] Furthermore, lncRNAs have been introduced as potential prognostic and diagnostic biomarkers in GC.[1011]

To review and evaluate the accuracy of the reported lncRNAs as prognostic biomarkers in GC based on the ceRNA network studies, we conducted the current systematic review and meta-analysis. We searched literature looking for ceRNA network studies in GC reporting lncRNAs as prognostic biomarkers. Fifty-six studies were included in our systematic review, with average follow-up time of approximately 120 months.

Due to the high number of lncRNAs (350 unique lncRNAs) we considered frequently reported lncRNAs for meta-analysis. We assessed lncRNAs which were reported at least in three studies in meta-analysis. Among the included studies in the current meta-analysis four,[26272829] five,[3031323334] three,[151635] and three[153536] studies introduced CCDC144NL-AS1, PVT1, ADAMTS9-AS2, and AL391152.1 as prognostic biomarkers in GC, respectively. Meta-analysis of these four lncRNAs revealed that CCDC144NL-AS1 is significantly associated with OS in GC. Meta-analysis showed that patients with higher expression of CCDC144NL-AS1 have shorter OS. Regarding PVT1, our findings suggest that it may have a modest association with adverse clinical outcomes. However, this potential prognostic value should be interpreted with caution. The observed variability across studies was considerable, and signs of publication bias were detected. After adjusting for this bias, the association was no longer statistically significant. These observations highlight the need for further studies with larger, more methodologically consistent cohorts to clarify the clinical relevance of PVT1. Meta-analysis results for ADAMTS9-AS2 and AL391152.1 were not significant. Heterogeneity assessment indicated variability in the effect sizes for PVT1, ADAMTS9-AS2, and AL391152.1 lncRNAs. This can be originated from different reasons such as: (1) differences in studied populations (such as geographical diversity of patients, different types of treatment protocols, and different tumor stages), (2) different methodology, (3) sample size (studies with small sample sizes may not be reliable), and (4) biological diversity of the samples (different tumors may represent different characteristics and expression changes of lncRNAs. More studies are required to unveil the precise importance of these lncRNAs in the survival of GC patients. Here, we discussed these lncRNAs in cancer, focusing on GC.

CCDC144NL-AS1 is an important player in human cancer development.[42] Sheng et al.[43] indicated that higher expression of CCDC144NL-AS1 associates with shorter OS in GC. Silencing of CCDC144NL-AS1 significantly suppressed the cell proliferation and migration of GC cells, and also enhanced the chemosensitivity of GC cells to 5-fluorouracil. In agreement with studies showing that higher expression of CCDC144NL-AS1 is associated with shorter OS of patients with GC, the prognostic value of CCDC144NL-AS1 has been furthermore implicated in several other types of cancers including non-small cell lung cancer,[44] colorectal,[45] and ovarian[46] cancers.

A large number of studies have uncovered the association of lncRNA plasmacytoma variant translocation 1 (PVT1) with cancer development.[47] PVT1 has a close relation with tumor initiation, proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transformation, invasion, and apoptosis, as well as poor prognosis and resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy in cancer.[47] Kong et al.[48] showed that PVT1 can serve as an independent prognostic biomarker in GC. They revealed that the expression of PVT1 is increased in GC and is associated with advanced TNM stage and expanded invasion of tumoral cells. They also indicated that PVT1 plays a critical role in G1/S arrest of the cell cycle through targeting EZH2 and epigenetically regulating the expression of p15 and p16 genes. Recently, it has been identified that suppression of PVT1, along with chemotherapy treatment, can potentially promote apoptosis and decrease the growth, migration, and drug resistance of GC cells.[49] In a meta-analysis published in 2021, including eight studies and 747 patients, GC patients with higher expression of PVT1 showed poorer OS.[50]

lncRNA ADAM metallopeptidase with thrombospondin type 1 motif 9 antisense RNA 2 (ADAMTS9AS2) has different roles in human cancers; however, it is mostly identified as a tumor suppressor.[51] Its altered expression is associated with proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, and migration in cancer.[52] ADAMTS9AS2 is downregulated in GC.[53] It can regulate the expression of Speckle‐type POZ protein (SPOP), resulting in suppression of GC cells’ proliferation.[53] SPOP is an E3 ubiquitin ligase adaptor that inhibits tumor progression.[54] Overexpression of ADAMTS9-AS2 increases cisplatin sensitivity of GC cells by sponging miR-223-3p and subsequently, induction of NLRP3 mediated pyroptotic cell death.[55] Besides, lower expression of ADAMTS9AS2 showed an association with shorter OS in GC.[55] ADAMTS9AS2 has a tumor suppressive role in other types of cancer such as liver[56] and triple-negative breast cancers.[57] ADAMTS9AS2 has also been reported to induce tumor progression. For instance, in tongue squamous cell carcinoma, it can promote cancer progression by sponging miR-600, leading to an increased amount of EZH2. Its increased expression is also associated with poorer survival of these patients.[58]

In a study conducted by Song et al.,[59] eleven 5-methylcytosine-related lncRNAs (m5C-lncRNAs) including AL391152.1 in GC were identified. Then, they constructed a prognostic model of these lncRNAs with a high level of sensitivity and accuracy in forecasting the survival of GC patients. AL391152.1 is also associated with N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification, which is another common epigenetic modification of RNA, playing a critical role in the carcinogenesis of GC.[36] AL391152.1 controls the expression of cyclins through which regulates G2/M phase of the GC cell cycle.[36] APOBEC3 family members are mostly dysregulated in cancerous cells and their mutations are associated with cancer risk.[60] In a study conducted by Qi et al.,[61] an APOBEC3-related lncRNA prognostic signature (APOBEC3-related LPS) including AL391152.1 was introduced in GC. They divided the samples into high and low-risk groups based on APOBEC3-related LPS and revealed that GC cells’ characteristics such as immune response and mutational rate are different in these groups.

This meta-analysis had several strengths. Firstly, most of the included studies had a large sample size as well as considerable follow-up time (a mean of 120 months). Secondly, most of the evaluated lncRNAs had other important features in their reported studies like being differentially expressed and involving in cancer-related pathways. Thirdly, there was a variation among the studies in the origin of samples (such as TCGA, GEO, KM plotter, UALCAN, and clinical samples), which can potentially provide better evidence of the validity of the obtained results in our meta-analysis. However, there were also some limitations: First, heterogeneity assessment revealed differences among the included studies in the meta-analysis. Secondly, some of the included studies did not have complete information (sample size, P value, HR, or CI) even after sending emails, so they could not be entered into the meta-analysis. Thirdly, few studies had performed multivariate analysis, which considers clinical variables and makes the results more precise and reliable.

CONCLUSION

Many ceRNA network studies have revealed that dysregulation of lncRNA expression may predict the survival rate of GC patients. The current study was the first systematic review and meta-analysis regarding the lncRNAs that have been introduced as potential prognostic biomarkers in GC based on ceRNA network studies. This meta-analysis indicated the potential prognostic value of CCDC144NL-AS1 in GC. Further studies are required to unveil the biological functions of CCDC144NL-AS1 and the other reported survival-related lncRNAs in GC.
