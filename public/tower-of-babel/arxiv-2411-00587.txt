The Stacey–Roberts Lemma for Banach Manifolds
Peter KRISTEL a and Alexander SCHMEDING b

Abstract
The Stacey–Roberts lemma states that a surjective submersion between finite-dimensional manifolds gives rise to a submersion on infinite-dimensional manifolds of smooth mappings by pushforward. This result is foundational for many constructions in infinite-dimensional differential geometry such as the construction of Lie groupoids of smooth mappings. We generalise the Stacey–Roberts lemma to Banach manifolds which admit smooth partitions of unity. The new approach also remedies an error in the original proof of the result for the purely finite-dimensional setting.

Introduction

Manifolds of mappings are among the most important examples of infinite-dimensional manifolds with far ranging applications from geometric hydrodynamics, to shape analysis and infinite-dimensional Lie theory.
A fundamental property of these manifolds is that a smooth mapping between manifolds induces a smooth mapping between the associated manifolds of mappings, the push-forward.
To illustrate this, consider smooth (finite-dimensional) manifolds
X
,
M
,
N
and a smooth map
\varphi\colon M\rightarrow N
.
Denote by
C^{k}(X,M)
the maps of class
C^{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.
This set can be turned into an infinite-dimensional manifold with the property that the pushforward

\varphi_{\ast}\colon\ C^{k}(X,M)\rightarrow C^{k}(X,N),\qquad\varphi_{\ast}(f)=\varphi\circ f

is smooth.
It is now of interest to understand which properties of
\varphi\colon M\rightarrow N
lift to properties of the pushforward
\varphi_{\ast}
.
For example, if
\varphi
is a submersion, an embedding or a proper map, then
[
1
, Theorem E]
establishes sufficient conditions on the manifolds and the differentiability index
k
for
\varphi_{\ast}
to be of the same type. In particular, the following result, originally introduced by
[
31
, Corollary 5.2]
for generalised calculi on infinite-dimensional manifolds, is known in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
30
, Lemma 2.24]
).

Lemma

(Stacey–Roberts lemma)
.

Let
\varphi\colon M\rightarrow N
be a smooth surjective submersion between finite-dimensional manifolds and
X
a finite-dimensional smooth manifold. Then the pushforward

\varphi_{\ast}\colon\ C^{\infty}(X,M)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(X,N)

is a submersion.

Recall that manifolds of smooth mappings are infinite-dimensional manifolds modelled on non-Banach locally convex spaces.
In the setting of manifolds of smooth maps, smoothness of the pushforward has to be understood in the sense of Bastiani calculus, while submersions are defined à la Hamilton
[
19
, Defenition 4.4.8]
(see also Appendix
A
) as maps admitting submersion charts.
This distinguishes the case of spaces of smooth maps from the case of
C^{k}
-mappings for
k<\infty
which are Banach manifolds. We note that for these Banach manifolds an easier proof of a statement analogous to the Stacey–Roberts lemma can be given using section, see
[
1
]
for a detailed proof in the case
k<\infty
.

Manifolds of (smooth) mappings occur naturally in areas such as shape analysis or the
analysis
of PDE via the Ebin–Marsden approach (also cf.
[
30
]
). In these settings, the Stacey–Roberts lemma becomes a versatile tool for infinite-dimensional differential geometry. To illustrate this, the following list contains an overview of known use cases for the Stacey–Roberts lemma. Namely, the Stacey–Roberts lemma is an important tool in the construction of

•

(infinite-dimensional) Lie groupoids
[
1
]
,

•

Lie rackoids integrating Courant algebroids
[
24
]
,

•

bisection groups and infinite-dimensional symmetry groups related to Lie groupoids
[
2
,
3
,
29
]
,

•

smooth loop stacks and Hom stacks of orbifolds
[
27
,
28
]
,

•

smooth mapping stacks in derived differential geometry
[
32
]
,

•

the square root velocity transform for shape spaces with values in homogeneous spaces in shape analysis
[
10
]
.

In the setting of Bastiani calculus, the proof of the Stacey–Roberts lemma appeared first in
[
2
]
but as Steffens noted in private conversation with the second author the published proof contains a gap (a solution to this problem was then proposed in
[
32
]
).
In the present article, we remedy this problem and provide a complete and streamlined proof of the Stacey–Roberts lemma. Moreover, we shall establish the following generalised version of the statement.

Theorem A

(Stacey–Roberts lemma for Banach manifolds)
.

Let
X
be a
\sigma
-compact manifold and
M
,
N
be
C^{\infty}
-paracompact manifolds modeled on Banach spaces.
If
p\colon M\rightarrow N
is a smooth surjective submersion, then the pushforward

p_{\ast}\colon\ C^{\infty}(X,M)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(X,N),\qquad p_{\ast}(f)=p\circ f

is a submersion.

Here
C^{\infty}
-paracompactness means that every open cover of the manifold (as a topological space) admits a subordinate smooth partition of unity. It implies in particular, that the model spaces of the manifolds
M
,
N
admit smooth norms and it ensures the existence of a local addition on the Banach manifolds. This is a crucial requirement to construct the manifolds of mappings considered here.

In the original proof of the Stacey–Roberts lemma for finite-dimensional target manifolds in
[
2
]
, Riemannian structures on the target manifolds were exploited. These tools are not available on the Banach manifolds which generally admit only weak Riemannian structures (see
[
30
]
). It turns out that an alternative proof can be given using the language of sprays on anchored bundles. As Theorem
A
allows us to expand the scope to (certain) Banach manifolds, also the mentioned applications of the Stacey–Roberts lemma generalise to this broader regime. At the end of the present article we shall provide examples of applications which generalise essentially by the same proof to the Banach setting.

2
Preliminaries on manifolds of mappings

In this section, we recall results on manifolds of smooth mappings from a finite-dimensional manifold
X
to a smooth (possibly infinite-dimensional) manifold
M
. These manifolds cannot be modeled on Banach spaces, hence we need the generalisation of calculus known as Bastiani calculus. See Appendix
A

for a brief recollection and references to the literature on calculus on infinite-dimensional manifolds.

Let
X
be a locally compact (thus finite-dimensional), paracompact smooth manifold, and
{E\rightarrow X}
a smooth vector bundle (whose fibres are possibly locally convex spaces).
We write
\Gamma(E)
for the
set of smooth sections
of
E
.
We write
0\in\Gamma(E)
for the zero section.
Let
{K\subseteq X}
.
Then we consider the subspace of smooth
sections supported in

K
, i.e.,

\Gamma_{K}(E):=\bigl\{\sigma\in\Gamma(E)\mid\sigma|_{X\setminus K}=0\bigr\}.

Topologize
\Gamma_{K}(E)
with the compact-open
C^{\infty}
-topology induced by the inclusion
\Gamma_{K}(E)\subseteq C^{\infty}(M,E)
, cf.
[
21
]
.
We further define

\Gamma_{c}(E):=\bigcup_{K\subseteq M\text{ compact}}\Gamma_{K}(E).

As the spaces
\Gamma_{K}(E)
are ordered by inclusion of the compact sets
K
, the
space of compactly supported sections

\Gamma_{c}(E)
becomes the locally convex inductive limit of the
\Gamma_{K}(E)
. The resulting topology on
\Gamma_{c}(E)
is indeed a Hausdorff vector topology by
[
16
, Lemma F.19 (c)]
.

To construct charts for
C^{\infty}(X,M)
around
f\in C^{\infty}(X,M)
, where
M
is a possibly infinite-dimensional manifold, consider the space of sections of the pullback bundle
f^{*}TM\rightarrow X
. Charts are then constructed with the help of a local addition on
M
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold.
A
local addition
on
M
consists of a pair
(U,\Sigma)
, where
U\subset TM
is an open neighbourhood of the zero section
M\subset TM
, and
\Sigma\colon U\rightarrow M
,
such that

•

\Sigma
restricts to the identity on
M\subset TM
,

•

the map
\theta_{\Sigma}\colon U\rightarrow M\times M,\theta_{\Sigma}(v):=(\pi_{M}(v),\Sigma(v))
is a diffeomorphism onto an open subset of
M\times M
.

To motivate the terminology of local addition, consider the following (trivial) example. Let
V
be a locally convex vector space, and set
U=V\times V\cong TV
. The map
\Sigma(v,w)=v+w
is then a local addition.

Canonical charts for manifolds of mappings.

Let
(U,\Sigma)
be a local addition on
M
.
We let
U^{\prime}:=\theta_{\Sigma}(U)\subseteq M\times M
.
Set
O_{f}=\Gamma_{c}(f^{*}TM)\cap C^{\infty}(X,U)
, and

O^{\prime}_{f}=\bigl\{g\in C^{\infty}(X,M)\mid(f,g)(X)\subseteq U^{\prime}\bigr\}.

One can show that
O_{f}
is an open subset of
\Gamma_{c}(f^{\ast}TM)
. Similar statements hold for
O^{\prime}_{f}
and a suitable topology on
C^{\infty}(X,M)
, see
[
17
, proof of Theorem 1.4]
for details.
We then obtain an inverse pair of maps

\displaystyle\phi_{f}\colon\ O_{f}\rightarrow O^{\prime}_{f},\quad\tau\mapsto\Sigma\circ\tau,\qquad\phi_{f}^{-1}\colon\ O^{\prime}_{f}\rightarrow O_{f},\quad g\mapsto\theta_{\Sigma}^{-1}\circ(f,g).

(2.1)

By
[
17
, Theorem 1.4]
, these mappings form a smooth atlas for
C^{\infty}(X,M)
.

Proposition 2.2

(special case of
[
17
, Theorem 1.4]
)
.

Let
X
be a paracompact finite-dimensional manifold and
M
be a Banach manifold which admits a local addition.
Then the canonical charts form a smooth atlas for a manifold structure on
C^{\infty}(X,M)
. This manifold structure does not depend on the choice of local addition.

The following result is standard (see, e.g.,
[
30
, Section 2]
), but a proof for it has, to the best of our knowledge, not been published for the setting (non-compact source manifold and infinite-dimensional manifold as target) considered in the present article. We include its proof for the readers convenience.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
X
be a
\sigma
-compact manifold and
M,N
locally convex manifolds which admit local additions. If
f\colon M\rightarrow N
is smooth, then the pushforward

f_{\ast}\colon\ C^{\infty}(X,M)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(X,N),\qquad f_{\ast}(g)=f\circ g,

is smooth.

Proof.

Since smoothness is a local property, we may test it in suitable charts. Let
\Sigma_{M}
and
\Sigma_{N}
be the local additions on
M
and
N
respectively. For
g\in C^{\infty}(X,M)
and
f\circ g
, we pick the canonical charts
\bigl(O^{\prime}_{g},\phi_{g}\bigr)
and
\bigl(O^{\prime}_{f\circ g},\phi_{f\circ g}\bigr)
with respect to the choice of local additions. Then the pushforward becomes in these canonical charts

\phi_{f\circ g}\circ f_{*}\circ\phi^{-1}_{f}\colon\ \Gamma_{g,c}\rightarrow\Gamma_{f\circ g,c},\qquad\tau\mapsto\bigl(\theta_{\Sigma_{N}}^{-1}\circ f\circ\Sigma_{M}\bigr)_{*}(\tau).

Now the mapping
\theta_{\Sigma_{N}}^{-1}\circ f\circ\Sigma_{M}\colon g^{\ast}TM\supseteq g^{\ast}U_{\Sigma_{M}}\rightarrow(f\circ g)^{\ast}TN
is a smooth map defined on an open neighborhood of the zero section, takes the zero section to itself and descends to the identity. Hence the smoothness of the pushforward follows from Glöckner’s
\Omega
-lemma with parameters
[
16
, Theorem F.23]
which is applicable due to
[
16
, Lemma F.19 (c)]
since
X
is
\sigma
-compact.
∎

3
Geometric constructions on Banach manifolds

In this section, we consider the target manifolds of the manifolds of mappings. To generalise the Stacey–Roberts lemma we need geometric tools on Banach manifolds.
We start with a section on connections and related objects on Banach manifolds.
While all of these results are standard in the finite-dimensional setting, it is worth pointing out the validity of these results on Banach manifolds which are
C^{\infty}
-paracompact.
This means that the manifolds are paracompact as topological spaces, and every open covering admits a subordinate smooth partition of unity. In particular, the model Banach spaces thus admit smooth bump functions, see
[
7
]
.
In infinite dimensions this is a restriction, see
[
30
, Appendix A.4]
and the references therein. On
C^{\infty}
-paracompact Banach manifolds, the typical local-to-global arguments employing smooth partitions of unity are available.

By a
Banach vector bundle

Definition 3.3

3.2
Sprays on anchored bundles

For our construction of manifolds of mappings, local additions are needed. Since Banach manifolds in general do not admit a natural Riemannian manifold structure (whose exponential map can be used as a local addition), another source for local additions is needed. Sprays and their exponential mappings
[
23
]
, provide a convenient way to replace the missing structure. We need a generalisation of the classical sprays to sprays of anchored Banach bundles, see
[
4
,
8
,
9
]
.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
\pi\colon E\rightarrow M
be a Banach vector bundle with typical fibre
F
. Together with a vector bundle morphism
\rho\colon E\rightarrow TM
, the
anchor
, we call the triple
(\pi,E,\rho)
an
anchored Banach bundle
(
over
M)
.

Example 3.6
.

Every vector subbundle of
TM
becomes an anchored bundle with respect to the trivial anchor given by inclusion (cf.
[
8
, Example 3.2]
).
If
p\colon M\rightarrow N
is a surjective submersion, there are several anchored bundles associated to
p
. The vertical bundle
\mathcal{V}
and for every Ehresmann connection the horizontal bundle
\mathcal{H}
are subbundles, hence anchored. Note that an Ehresmann connection described as the section
\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}
of
(\pi_{M},{\rm d}p)
turns

\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\colon\ p^{\ast}TN\rightarrow\mathcal{H}\subseteq TM

into an anchored bundle.

Anchored Banach bundles are related to, but weaker structures than Banach Lie algebroids (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
6
]
). The anchor allows us to generalise second order differential equations to vector bundles as follows.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
(\pi,E,\rho)
be an anchored Banach vector bundle.
A smooth map
S\colon E\rightarrow TE
on
E
is called a
semispray
if it satisfies

(S1)

\pi_{E}\circ S=\id_{E}
, where
\pi_{E}\colon TE\rightarrow E
is the projection (i.e.,
S
is a vector field).

(S2)

S

lifts the anchor
in the sense that
T\pi\circ S=\rho
.

For every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
we let
h_{\lambda}\colon E\rightarrow E
be the vector bundle morphism which in every fibre
E_{x}
is given by multiplication with
\lambda
.
If a semispray satisfies the condition

(S3)

S\circ h_{\lambda}=Th_{\lambda}(\lambda S),\qquad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R},

then we call
S
a
spray
on the anchored Banach bundle.

Example 3.8
.

For the trivial anchored bundle
(\pi_{M},TM,\id_{TM})
, a spray as per Definition
3.7
is just a spray in the usual sense of differential geometry
[
23
, Chapter IV, Section 3]
.
In particular, if
(M,g)
is a strong Riemannian manifold
[
30
, Section 4]
, then
TM
admits a spray, called the
metric spray
compatible with the Riemannian metric
g
.

Since sprays are vector fields, the notion of relatedness (via a smooth map) makes sense for them. We will say that sprays
S_{1}
,
S_{2}
are
\varphi
-related
if they are
\varphi
-related as vector fields, i.e.,
T\varphi\circ S_{1}=S_{2}\circ\varphi
holds.
Given a vertical isomorphism
\varphi
, the next Lemma
3.9
constructs a
\varphi
-related spray to a given spray.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
(\pi_{i},E_{i},\rho_{i})
be anchored Banach vector bundles on
M
.
Let
\varphi\colon E_{1}\rightarrow E_{2}
be a
(
vertical
)
isomorphism of anchored Banach bundles on
M
, i.e.,
\pi_{1}=\pi_{2}\circ\varphi
and
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}\circ\varphi
.
If
S\colon E_{1}\rightarrow TE_{1}
is a spray, then
T\varphi\circ S\circ\varphi^{-1}\colon E_{2}\rightarrow TE_{2}
is a spray.
Moreover,
S
is
\varphi
-related to
T\varphi\circ S\circ\varphi^{-1}
.

Proof.

We compute

\displaystyle\pi_{2}\circ T\varphi\circ S\circ\varphi^{-1}=\varphi\circ\pi_{1}\circ S\circ\varphi^{-1}=\id_{E_{2}},\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle T\pi_{2}\circ T\varphi\circ S\circ\varphi^{-1}=T\pi_{1}\circ S\circ\varphi^{-1}=\rho_{1}\circ\varphi^{-1}=\rho_{2},

and finally

\displaystyle T\varphi\circ S\circ\varphi^{-1}\circ h_{\lambda}

\displaystyle{}=T\varphi\circ S\circ h_{\lambda}\circ\varphi^{-1}=T\varphi\circ Th_{\lambda}(\lambda S)\circ\varphi^{-1}

\displaystyle{}=Th_{\lambda}\circ T\varphi\circ(\lambda S)\circ\varphi^{-1}=Th_{\lambda}\circ\lambda\bigl(T\varphi\circ S\circ\varphi^{-1}\bigr).\qed

∎

For bundles over a
C^{\infty}
-paracompact base, a local to global argument yields the following.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
(\pi,E,\rho)
be an anchored Banach bundle on a manifold
M
which is
C^{\infty}
-paracompact, then there exists a spray on the anchored bundle.

Proof.

Let
F
be the typical fiber of the vector bundle
E\rightarrow M
.
Let
\{U_{i},\varphi_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a trivializing cover for
E\rightarrow M
, i.e.,
U_{i}\subseteq M
open, and
\varphi_{i}\colon E|_{U_{i}}\xrightarrow{\cong}U_{i}\times F
.
For every
i\in I
, we thus obtain an anchored Banach bundle
A_{i}:=\bigl(\mathrm{pr}_{1},U_{i}\times F,\rho\circ\varphi_{i}^{-1}\bigr)
.
Pick a continuous and symmetric bilinear map
B\colon F\times F\rightarrow F
.
For every
i
, the map

\displaystyle S_{i}\colon\ U_{i}\times F\rightarrow TU_{i}\times F\times F\cong T(U_{i}\times F),\qquad S_{i}(x,v)=\bigl(\rho\bigl(\varphi_{i}^{-1}(x,v)\bigr),v,B(v,v)\bigr)

By Lemma

By using a smooth partition of unity, one then obtains a globally defined section

That this defines a spray follows from the fact that the set of sprays is a convex subset of the set of sections of

∎

Anchored bundles are a setting in which one can make sense of sprays and differential equations whose right-hand side is given by a spray.
Other natural examples of sprays on anchored bundles arise in the theory of Lie groupoids and Lie algebroids, see

The following definition builds on the usual proof for the existence of solutions to ordinary differential equations. In the classical settings of sprays on the tangent bundle the proofs are recorded in
[

Definition 3.11
.

For a spray

on an anchored bundle
(\pi,E,\rho)
over

, we denote its

(in the sense of flows of vector fields) by

\Fl^{S}\colon\ \mathbb{R}\times E\supseteq D\rightarrow E,\qquad(t,v)\mapsto\Fl_{t}^{S}(v),

with
\{0\}\times E\subseteq D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\times E

\partial_{t}\Fl_{t}^{S}(v)=S\bigl(\Fl_{t}^{S}(v)\bigr)

Since

0_{E}
by (

), there exists an open neighborhood

of

in

\exp_{S}\colon\ \Omega\rightarrow M,\qquad\exp_{S}=\pi\circ\Fl^{S}_{1}.

Let us now state some properties of the flow curves of a spray. For this we define

which in the Lie algebroid literature are also called

[

Definition 3.12

Let

be an anchored bundle over

-curve

\rho(c(t))=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\pi(c(t)),\qquad\forall t\in I.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
(\pi,E,\rho)
be an anchored bundle over
M
with a spray

. Then

(a)

every integral curve of

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}c(t)=S(c(t))

(b)

if for

the restriction of
\exp_{S}
to the fibre, then
T_{0_{x}}\exp_{S}^{x}=\rho|_{\pi^{-1}(x)}^{T_{x}M}
.

Proof.

(a) For

, let

be the interval on which
\beta_{v}(t)=\Fl_{t}^{S}(v)
is defined. Assume that
{s,t\in\mathbb{R}}
, such that for

the interval

contains
st
.
The usual proof for flows of ordinary differential equations (see
[

which carries over) shows that this happens if and only if
{s\in I_{\beta_{v}(t)}}
and then

\displaystyle\pi\Fl_{s}^{S}(tv)=\pi\Fl^{S}_{st}(v).

(3.2)

By construction

is the integral curve of

We compute the derivative of

via the chain rule and (

Using

Hence integral curves are anchored paths.
(b) The classical proof from
[

works. Compute the fibre derivative of the
\exp_{S}
along a ray

by using the chain rule and (

\displaystyle T_{0_{x}}\exp_{S}(v)

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\right|_{t=0}\exp_{S}(tv)=\left.\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\right|_{t=0}\pi\Fl^{S}_{1}(tv)=\left.\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\right|_{t=0}\pi\Fl^{S}_{t}(v)

where we used (

).
∎

Stronger results along the lines of Lemma

Obviously, if

Every

4
Proof of the Stacey–Roberts lemma

In this section, we establish the main theorem, namely that the pushforward by a submersion becomes a submersion between the manifolds of mappings.

Plan of proof for Theorem

.

Let
p\colon M\rightarrow N
be a surjective submersion between

-paracompact Banach manifolds. For any
\sigma
-compact manifold

is a submersion, i.e., that there are submersion charts for

.
The manifold structure on

and

is determined by an atlas of canonical charts constructed from local additions on

and

Idea.

Construct
\Sigma_{M}

Applications of the generalised Stacey–Roberts lemma

There are several constructions in the literature where the use of the original Stacey–Roberts lemma restricted the construction to manifolds of mappings with finite-dimensional target. The new version allows one to generalise these results now with the same proof to Banach manifolds. We mention explicitly that

•

The construction of current groupoids

from
[
1
, Theorem 3.2]
can be carried out now for Banach Lie groupoids, removing the restriction to finite-dimensional target Lie groupoids.

•

The bisection group of a Banach Lie groupoid
\mathcal{G}=(G\rightrightarrows M)
over a finite-dimensional base manifold
M
always is a submanifold of
C^{\infty}(M,G)
with the proof as in
[
2
, Theorem 1.3]
.

As both direct applications of the Stacey–Roberts lemma involve Banach Lie groupoids we would like to mention that relevant examples have recently been investigated, see
[
6
,
11
,
18
,
20
]
and the references therein.
For the readers convenience we describe the construction of the submanifold structure in more details to showcase a typical application of the Stacey–Roberts lemma.

Application example: Submanifolds of manifolds of mappings.

Let us start with a general result for a surjective submersion where we recover a version of
[
25
, Proposition 10.10]
for more general manifolds.

Lemma 4.4
.

For a surjective submersion
p\colon M\rightarrow N
from a
C^{\infty}
-paracompact Banach manifold
M
to a
\sigma
-compact manifold
N
, the space of submersion sections

\Gamma_{p}:=\{\sigma\in C^{\infty}(N,M)\mid p\circ\sigma=\id_{N}\}

is a splitting submanifold.

Proof.

As
p
is a submersion, the Stacey–Roberts lemma implies that
p_{\ast}
is a submersion. Hence
\Gamma_{p}=(p_{\ast})^{-1}(\{\id_{N}\})
is a splitting submanifold by the regular value theorem
[
15
, Theorem D]
.
∎

We now return to the bisections of a Banach Lie groupoid
\mathcal{G}=(G\rightrightarrows M)
, where
G
is
C^{\infty}
-paracompact over a
\sigma
-compact base
M
. Denote by
s,t\colon G\rightarrow M
the source and target map of the groupoid which both are submersions. Then by definition, the bisection group is the set

\operatorname{Bis}(\mathcal{G}):=\{\sigma\in C^{\infty}(M,G)\mid s\circ\sigma=\id_{M},\,t\circ\sigma\in\Diff(M)\}.

Now it is well known that the diffeomorphism group
\Diff(M)\subseteq C^{\infty}(M,M)
is an open set, cf.
[
25
]
. Hence
O:=(t_{\ast})^{-1}(\Diff(M))
is open in

by Proposition
2.3
and we deduce that
\operatorname{Bis}(\mathcal{G})=\Gamma_{s}\cap O
is a split submanifold of

by Lemma

. Alternatively, one could also have noted that the restriction
(s_{\ast})|_{O}
is a submersion as a consequence of the Stacey–Roberts lemma for

.

With a bit more work, the rest of the proof can be generalised to see that this structure turns the bisection group into a Lie group (the original proof uses results on composition which exist in the literature only for finite-dimensional targets but could be generalised).

The application of the Stacey–Roberts lemma showcased in this section is rather typical as it provides an out of the box argument as to why certain generic constructions (which are available in the infinite-dimensional setting for our notion of submersion, see
[
15
]
for statements and proofs) on function spaces provide (sub)manifolds. Further examples along this line are provided by the Hom-stack constructions in
[
27
,
27
]
. There a Hom-stack of maps from a manifold into a Lie groupoid is constructed and shown to be represented by a Lie groupoid. To obtain the Lie groupoid, one however needs to resolved the target groupoid as a Čech groupoid and construct from the associated covering data a family of fibre-products of mapping spaces. Individually, these fibre-products become manifolds by the usual argument after applying the Stacey–Roberts lemma to the pushforwards used in the construction of the fibre-products.

Appendix A
Infinite-dimensional calculus and manifolds

In this appendix, we recall some basic definitions of infinite-dimensional calculus.
As stated in the beginning, we will mostly work on Banach manifolds, but the manifolds of mappings cannot be modelled on Banach spaces. Hence we need to base our article on a generalised calculus, the so called Bastiani calculus
[
5
]
, see, e.g.,
[

,

]
for introductory expositions.

Definition A.1
.

Consider locally convex spaces
E
,
F

and a map
f\colon U\rightarrow F
on an open subset
U\subseteq E
.
Write

D_{y}f(x):={\rm d}f(x;y):=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\biggr|_{t=0}f(x+ty)

for the directional derivative of
f
at
x\in U

in the direction

, if it exists.
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
.
If

is continuous, and the iterated directional derivatives

{\rm d}^{j}f(x;y_{1},\dots,y_{j}):=(D_{y_{j}}\cdots D_{y_{1}}f)(x)

exist for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

j\leq k
,
x\in U
and
y_{1},\dots,y_{j}\in E
,
and the maps
{\rm d}^{j}f\colon U\times E^{j}\to F

are continuous, then

is called

.
A map of class
C^{\infty}
is also called

.

The chain rule holds in the usual form for Bastiani differentiable mappings (see, e.g.,
[
30
, Proposition 1.23]
), whence it makes sense to define manifolds as in the finite-dimensional setting via charts. Hence a continuous mapping

between (possibly infinite-dimensional, smooth) manifolds is smooth if, locally in suitable pairs of charts the map is Bastiani smooth.

All manifolds and Lie groups considered
in the article are modeled on locally
convex spaces which may be infinite-dimensional,
unless the contrary is stated. If we say that a manifold or a vector bundle is a Banach manifold or a Banach vector bundle we mean that all modelling spaces (or fibres in the bundle case) are Banach spaces. See
[
23
]
for a discussion of differential geometry on Banach manifolds. All manifolds are assumed to be smooth and Hausdorff.
We write
\pi_{M}\colon TM\rightarrow M
for the tangent bundle with bundle projection

.

In the Bastiani setting, the notion of submersion has to be defined as in
[

, see also
[

]
.

Definition A.2
.

Let
f\colon M\rightarrow N
be a smooth map between manifolds, we say that
f
is a
submersion
, if for every
x\in M
there exist manifold charts of
M
around
x
and of
N
around
f(x)
which conjugate the map
f
to a projection onto a complemented subspace of the model space of
M
. Pairs of charts with these properties will be called
submersion charts
for the submersion
f
.

We refer to
[
15
]
for a discussion of the notion of submersion in the infinite-dimensional setting and its properties.
In particular, with this definition of a submersion fibre-products of manifolds can be defined as in the finite-dimensional setting. In particular, the usual construction for a submersion
p\colon M\rightarrow N
and a smooth map
g\colon L\rightarrow M
yields (see
[
30
, Lemma 1.60]
) that the fibre-product

M{}_{p}\hskip-2.5pt\times_{g}L:=\{(x,y)\in M\times M\mid p(x)=g(y)\}

is a submanifold of
M\times M
. If
g=p
, we also write shorter
M\times_{p}M
for this fibre-product.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
p_{i}\colon M_{i}\rightarrow N
,
i=1,2
, be surjective submersions, then the identification
T(M_{1}\times M_{2})\cong TM_{1}\times TM_{2}
restricts to a diffeomorphism

T((M_{1}){}_{p_{1}}\hskip-5.0pt\times_{p_{2}}(M_{2}))\cong(TM_{1}){}_{Tp_{1}}\hskip-5.0pt\times_{Tp_{2}}(TM_{2}).

Proof.

This question is clearly local, so we pick submersion charts on
W_{i}\subseteq M_{i}
,
i=1,2
, for
p_{i}
such that
W_{i}\cong U\times V_{i}
, with
U\subseteq E
,
V_{i}\subseteq F_{i}
open in locally convex spaces and
p_{i}
becomes the projection onto the first component with respect to this splitting. Suppressing the submersion charts, the embedding
M_{1}\times_{N}M_{2}\subseteq M_{1}\times M_{2}
becomes

\iota\colon\ U\times V_{1}\times V_{2}\rightarrow(U\times V_{1})\times(U\times V_{2}),(u,v_{1},v_{2})\mapsto((u,v_{1}),(u,v_{2})).

Passing to the tangent, we obtain the embedding
T\iota

\displaystyle U\times V_{1}\times V_{2}\times(E\times F_{1})\times(E\times F_{2})

\displaystyle\rightarrow((U\times V_{1})\times E\times F_{1})\times((U\times V_{2})\times E\times F_{2}),

\displaystyle(u,v_{1},v_{2},x,y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle\mapsto((u,v_{1},x,y_{1}),(u,v_{2},x,y_{2})).

Moreover, as
Tp_{i}|_{W_{i}}\colon(U\times V_{i})\times E\times F_{i}\rightarrow U\times E,((u,v_{i}),x,y_{i})\mapsto(u,x)
, we see that as sets (hence also as manifolds)
T\iota(T((W_{1}){}_{p_{1}}\hskip-3.99994pt\times_{p_{2}}\hskip-1.99997pt(W_{2}))))=(TW_{1}){}_{Tp_{1}}\hskip-3.99994pt\times_{Tp_{2}}\hskip-1.99997pt(TW_{2})
as claimed.
∎
