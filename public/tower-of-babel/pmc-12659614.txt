Online machine learning model for predicting delirium risk in elderly patients with chronic kidney disease: development and preliminary validation
Authors: Lang Gao, Guangdong Wang, Xingyi Yang, Yuanshuo Ge, Shijun Tong, Weijie Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDelirium frequently complicates elderly chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients due to multifactorial vulnerability. Early detection in geriatric intensive care unit (ICU) settings is challenged by traditional assessments' communication deficits. Machine learning refines predictions through multidimensional pattern recognition. We develop an interpretable online tool for timely high-risk delirium identification, guiding interventions to enhance outcomes.MethodsElderly chronic kidney disease patients were selected using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes, with delirium defined per Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU). Baseline traits, vitals, lab parameters, comorbidities, and clinical scores were collected. Dual feature selection employed Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (Lasso) regression and Boruta. Eight machine learning models underwent assessment via Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) and calibrated curves. The superior model was interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values and deployed as a web-based risk calculator.ResultsTen key predictors were identified through dual feature selection. The Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) model demonstrated good calibration and sustained high predictive accuracy in both internal and external validation. SHAP analysis revealed Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, and sedative usage as clinically significant predictors. The finalized model was deployed as a clinically applicable web-based risk calculator.ConclusionThis study’s prediction model accurately assesses delirium risk in elderly CKD patients, showing robust performance and clinical utility. Its web-based dynamic calculator supplies personalized risk evaluation in ICU settings, supporting early identification and proactive interventions to enhance clinical management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03455-w.

Introduction

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) refers to persistent structural or functional kidney impairment lasting over three months, with notably high prevalence among the elderly. The core pathological mechanism stems from irreversible nephron damage, wherein surviving nephrons initiate a vicious cycle through compensatory hemodynamic changes and increased metabolic load, driving disease progression. Neurological complications rank among the most prevalent issues in CKD patients. Uremia emerging in advanced stages induces severe neurological dysfunction: Accumulated uremic toxins disrupt physiological processes, manifesting as cognitive decline, seizures, and even loss of consciousness. These sequelae substantially impair patients' quality of life and diminish daily functional capacity. The global prevalence of CKD is rising, affecting approximately 700 million people. The consequent escalation in healthcare burdens and individual economic expenditures positions CKD as a critical public health challenge [1–3].

Delirium represents an acute cerebral failure syndrome prevalent among hospitalized elderly patients, characterized by abrupt attentional deficits and fluctuating consciousness. Its incidence is substantial: 30% in general medical patients aged ≥ 70 years, 15%–25% in post-major-surgery cohorts, and exceeding 75% in mechanically ventilated ICU populations. Short-term impacts include prolonged ICU stays, increased hospitalization duration, and higher readmission rates; long-term consequences involve persistent cognitive impairment secondary to cerebral dysfunction [4–6]. Current evidence identifies delirium as a common neurological complication in elderly CKD patients. The underlying pathophysiology likely involves multifactorial mechanisms: uremic toxin accumulation, oxidative stress, electrolyte imbalances, chronic inflammatory states, and blood–brain barrier disruption. These processes may precipitate progressive deterioration of cognitive reserve, thereby significantly elevating susceptibility to delirium [7, 8]. Clinical management of elderly ICU patients with delirium remains challenging. Conventional cognitive assessment tools are often difficult to administer effectively due to communication barriers induced by mechanical ventilation and sedative therapy. This assessment gap may not only reduce quality of life but also exacerbate financial burdens on family caregivers [9]. Consequently, early identification and prevention in elderly CKD patients at high risk for delirium has become a critical clinical objective.

The advancement of artificial intelligence, coupled with accumulating geriatric cohort studies and electronic health record resources, has expanded the application prospects of machine learning in aging research. Compared with traditional statistical techniques, machine learning methods more effectively integrate rapidly expanding healthcare datasets, significantly enhancing computational efficiency and discriminatory accuracy of predictive models. While conventional statistical models suffice for simple linear problems, they exhibit limitations in resolving multiple nonlinear relationships and typically accommodate only small sample sizes with inadequate capacity for high-dimensional data processing [10]. In contrast, machine learning algorithms can manage massive high-dimensional data, identify complex interaction patterns among variables, and screen core predictors of clinical outcomes, thereby substantially optimizing predictive accuracy [11, 12]. This study aims to develop an interpretable machine learning-based online prediction tool for assessing delirium risk in elderly CKD patients. The tool will empower clinicians to rapidly identify high-risk individuals during early stages, enabling timely intervention adjustments to reduce delirium incidence and ultimately improve patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Data collection and ethics

This study employed data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) IV (v3.1) and MIMIC-III (v1.4) databases. MIMIC-III comprises de-identified health records for approximately 60,000 ICU patients admitted to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) between 2001 and 2012. MIMIC-IV contains clinical data for nearly 95,000 BIDMC ICU patients spanning 2008 to 2022. Prior to accessing these databases, the investigators completed the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) Program in human subjects research ethics, provided by the National Institutes of Health, and obtained certification (ID: 66,380,198). The study adheres to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and international ethical standards. Given the de-identified nature of the MIMIC data, institutional review board approval and patient informed consent were waived.

Study cohort

The study design is detailed in Fig. 1. We identified chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients aged ≥ 65 years using International Classification of Diseases codes (ICD-9 and ICD-10). Delirium during ICU hospitalization was assessed with the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU). CAM-ICU diagnosis of delirium requires the concurrent presence of: (1) acute onset with fluctuating symptoms; (2) inattention; (3) disorganized thinking; and (4) altered level of consciousness. Patients unable to cooperate with assessors were excluded. This study defines CKD-related delirium as a CAM-ICU-confirmed delirium episode occurring during ICU hospitalization in CKD patients. Exclusion criteria comprised: ICU stay < 24 h; history of dementia or schizophrenia; prior ICU admission; > 30% missing key variables; and significant outliers in vital sign recordings. Inclusion of variables

Data extracted from the MIMIC-IV and MIMIC-III databases encompassed patient details across 43 variables. These included demographic characteristics and baseline clinical information (Age, Weight, Gender, ICU_Day), vital signs recorded within the first 24 h following ICU admission (Heart Rate (HR), Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP), Diastolic Blood Pressure (DBP), Respiratory Rate (RR), Oxygen Saturation (SPO2), Temperature), and established critical illness severity scores (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)). Additionally, laboratory parameters measured during the initial 24 h post-ICU admission were incorporated: Red Blood Cell Count (RBC), White Blood Cell Count (WBC), Platelet count, Hematocrit (HCT), International Normalized Ratio (INR), Prothrombin Time (PT), Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (APTT), Creatinine, Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN), Anion Gap (AG), Lactate, pH, PO2, PCO2, Bicarbonate, Calcium, Magnesium, Phosphate, Chloride, Potassium, Sodium, and Glucose. Documented comorbidities comprised Acute Kidney Injury (AKI), Chronic Bronchitis (CB), Cerebrovascular Accident (CVA), and Tumor history. Finally, therapeutic interventions administered within the first 24 h in the ICU were captured: Continuous Renal Replacement Therapy (CRRT), Mechanical Ventilation (MV), Vasopressors (VP), and Sedatives.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). Group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data are expressed as counts (percentages), with between-group differences assessed by chi-square or Fisher's exact tests. Based on mortality data from the MIMIC-IV database, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted for both groups, and survival differences were analyzed using the log-rank test. The MIMIC-IV dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%) and an internal validation set (30%), while MIMIC-III data served for external validation. To balance outcome class distribution in the training set, we implemented undersampling, oversampling, and Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), with missing data handled by multiple imputation. An overview of data collection items is provided in Table S1. To limit redundancy and constrain model complexity, we incorporated a Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (Lasso) penalty into the objective function, shrinking some coefficients to zero. This is equivalent to imposing an L1 constraint on the coefficient vector, thereby achieving feature selection and model simplification while improving external transportability and generalizability. The penalty strength was determined via 20-fold cross-validation, and the final hyperparameter was chosen according to the lambda.1se criterion to balance predictive performance with structural parsimony [13–17]. In parallel, we applied the Boruta wrapper method using random forests as the base learner: for each original variable, a corresponding “shadow” feature was generated and their importance scores were repeatedly compared (up to 200 iterations or until convergence), retaining only predictors that consistently outperformed their shadows. This procedure reduces overfitting and bias stemming from multicollinearity. The feature set confirmed by both approaches demonstrated superior discrimination and robustness during validation. The resulting set is compact and interpretable, facilitating deployment as an online risk calculator with practical operability and clinical feasibility [18, 19]. Details are provided in Table S2.

We developed eight machine learning models for delirium risk prediction, encompassing the following algorithms: Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Neural Network (NN), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and Categorical Boosting (CatBoost). The dataset was partitioned into a model training set and an internal validation set, with the latter used for model performance comparison and optimal model selection. Hyperparameter optimization was performed via grid search with tenfold cross-validation; detailed model parameters are provided in Table S3. Models underwent multidimensional evaluation in both internal and external validation, with metrics including: Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), DeLong's test, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, F1-score, calibration curves, and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA). Additionally, the optimal model was compared against commonly used disease severity scores in the ICU.。

For the best-performing model, we employed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to interpret its prediction mechanism. The SHAP summary plot visually displays global feature importance, while individual SHAP force value analysis quantifies the direction and magnitude of each feature's contribution to predictions for single instances. Based on this model, we developed a streamlined web-based calculator using the Shiny package in R. This clinical-grade tool provides a dynamic interface for clinicians to input patient parameters and generates real-time delirium risk predictions, facilitating clinical translation. All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.2, with statistical significance threshold set at two-sided p < 0.05.

Feature selection

To identify key predictors of delirium in elderly CKD patients, this study employed two complementary feature selection strategies: the Boruta algorithm and Lasso regression. First, the Boruta algorithm systematically evaluated feature importance, identifying 30 significant variables including GCS score, SOFA score, sedative use, ICU length of stay, sodium, calcium, and phosphate levels (Fig. 3A, B). In contrast, Lasso regression—a penalized compression estimation method—simultaneously performed variable selection and model complexity adjustment by optimizing its objective function. Using 20-fold cross-validation, the optimal regularization coefficient lambda.1se was determined as 0.01389504, ultimately selecting 13 features with significant predictive value (Fig. 3C, D). Comparative analysis of both selection methods revealed overlapping features consistently identified by both approaches. Model development and performance

This study employed eight machine learning algorithms (including LR, SVM, GBM, NN, CatBoost, XGBoost, AdaBoost, and LightGBM) to develop predictive models for delirium in elderly CKD patients using the training dataset. To address data imbalance in the training set, the SMOTE algorithm was applied for correction. Model performance was evaluated on internal and external independent validation datasets, with detailed metrics (Accuracy, Sensitivity, Specificity, Precision, F1-Score) provided in Table 2. DeLong's test revealed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) in AUC between internal and external validation sets for all models except LightGBM. Comparative analysis of AUC values demonstrated that GBM achieved optimal discriminative performance: AUC 0.805 (internal validation) and 0.732 (external validation) (Fig. 4A, B). Calibration curves indicated excellent agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes for GBM, with close alignment to the ideal reference line (Fig. 4C, D). Decision curve analysis further confirmed that GBM provided higher net clinical benefit across a wide threshold probability range (Fig. 4E, F). Collectively, GBM was selected as the optimal model due to its superior performance, demonstrating substantial clinical utility potential for risk stratification and clinical decision support. Discussion

This study developed and validated a machine learning prediction model to assess delirium risk in elderly CKD patients in the ICU, utilizing data from the MIMIC-IV and MIMIC-III databases. Based on 47 clinical variables recorded within 24 h of ICU admission, feature selection was performed using Lasso regression and the Boruta algorithm, followed by the construction of eight machine learning models. Results demonstrated that the GBM algorithm achieved optimal predictive performance, with AUC values of 0.805 and 0.732 in internal and external validation sets, respectively, confirming the model's robustness and accuracy. Furthermore, compared to existing clinical scores—Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPSII), Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS), and Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS)—the GBM model exhibited superior performance in predicting delirium among elderly CKD patients. To elucidate the model's decision-making mechanism, SHAP analysis was applied to visualize feature contributions. Based on this model, we developed an online risk calculator to enable real-time, individualized risk assessment; this tool has the potential to facilitate early clinical intervention, thereby improving outcomes for ICU patients.

Elderly patients with CKD frequently exhibit systemic accumulation of urea, creatinine, and guanidine compounds due to significantly impaired renal excretion. Current evidence indicates that these uremic toxins directly compromise central nervous system function by disrupting blood–brain barrier integrity and inducing neuroinflammatory responses. Neurotoxic manifestations range from neurocognitive impairments—such as confusion, lethargy, and impaired attention—to delirium in severe cases [20]. Delirium is associated with substantially increased mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and heightened healthcare burdens in geriatric populations [21]. Nevertheless, clinical identification of delirium in elderly CKD patients within ICU remains challenging. Widely adopted delirium assessment tools, including the CAM-ICU and Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist (ICDSC), face implementation barriers. These include insufficient clinician awareness of clinical risks, operational complexity requiring nursing expertise, time-intensive observational demands exacerbating clinical workloads, and dependency on patient cooperation. Particularly in agitated patients, limited comprehension or inability to follow instructions often precludes completion of assessments, leading to underdetection [6, 22, 23]. To address these limitations, this study developed a web-based calculator utilizing an optimized predictive model to facilitate rapid early delirium identification in ICU-admitted elderly CKD patients, potentially improving clinical outcomes.

Current research features multiple models for assessing delirium risk in ICU patients. However, existing models predominantly target sepsis or postoperative recovery populations and inadequately address unique risk factors in elderly CKD patients admitted to ICU [24–27]. This study achieves methodological and clinical advances through the development and validation of a novel prediction model with an integrated online calculator. Tesia et al. [28] identify delirium as a prevalent neuropsychiatric complication among CKD patients, reporting a 19.2% incidence rate in this population. Gnatta et al. [29] systematically review evidence establishing elderly CKD as a major risk factor for postoperative delirium. However, their study does not examine CKD-delirium causality, merely categorizing delirium within the neuropsychiatric symptom spectrum of CKD patients. Since their conclusions rely on correlational data from primary studies, causal inference remains unsubstantiated. Tang et al. [30] utilized LASSO regression to identify predictors including GCS score, sedative use, DBP, SPO2 and CKD constructing an XGBoost based model for early delirium prediction in elderly ICU patients. Their key predictors strongly align with our results. Notably, this model demonstrated robust performance only in internal validation, with no external validation assessing generalizability. In contrast, our prediction model significantly outperformed Tang et al.'s in both internal and external validation cohorts, evidenced by AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Gu et al. [31] developed a nomogram based on variables such as the SOFA score, mechanical ventilation, and lactate to estimate the risk of sepsis-associated delirium. In contrast, our study targets older adults with CKD and treats the occurrence of sepsis as one of the candidate predictors incorporated into both the model and the online risk calculator, rather than as the sole inclusion criterion. This strategy conceptualizes sepsis as a high-risk phenotype within a heterogeneous cohort, aligning the approach more closely with real-world clinical practice and improving the model’s applicability and generalizability. We applied machine learning methods to capture potential nonlinear relationships and interactions, and we enhanced interpretability through feature-importance analyses. On this basis, the model demonstrated robust discriminative performance and can support early risk stratification and clinical decision-making for older adults with CKD. Focusing on a population with multiple vulnerabilities that has been underrepresented in prior work, we built and externally validated an interpretable machine-learning model using routinely available clinical variables from the first 24 h after admission. We also provide an online calculator to lower the barrier to use, enabling rapid bedside assessment and facilitating multicenter replication and re-validation. Taken together, our study makes a meaningful contribution to the delirium-prediction literature across four dimensions: population selection, predictor framework, methodological implementation, and translational pathway.

This study employed a systematic review of delirium risk factor literature to preliminarily identify potential predictors. Subsequently, these candidate variables were comprehensively extracted from research databases [25, 26, 31]. To achieve precise feature selection and enhance model robustness, a dual screening strategy combining Lasso regression with the Boruta algorithm was implemented to identify the most predictive features. This approach improved the clinical applicability potential of the model while significantly mitigating overfitting risks. Key predictors ultimately selected included GCS, SOFA score, and sedative usage. Using these predictors, we constructed and compared eight machine learning models. The optimization pipeline integrated grid search hyperparameter tuning, tenfold cross-validation, and SMOTE to address class imbalance. Model performance was compared using DeLong’s test to evaluate the statistical significance of pairwise AUC differences on the external validation set. The test revealed no statistically significant AUC difference between GBM and LightGBM models (P > 0.05), likely attributable to their shared gradient boosting framework [32, 33]. Notably, DeLong’s test for statistical significance may inadequately reflect clinically meaningful AUC differences, particularly when actual differences approach but fall below predefined significance thresholds. Moreover, during external validation the model’s discriminative ability decreased from an AUC of 0.805 in internal validation to 0.732. This magnitude of decline is largely attributable to several explainable sources of heterogeneity. First, cohort era and composition differed: MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV cover different admission periods and have distinct ICU types and case-mixes; among elderly CKD patients, illness severity, infectious burden, and treatment strategies have evolved over time, producing temporal drift in feature–outcome relationships. Second, measurement and recording systems were not fully aligned: the two databases differ in laboratory platforms, data granularity, medication and procedure coding schemes, and patterns of missingness, which reduced the identifiability and stability of some variables in the external dataset. Third, residual optimism from the development phase may persist: despite cross-validation and grid-based hyperparameter tuning, internal estimates can retain a small optimistic bias, with expected attenuation when transported to an independent cohort. Taken together, we interpret the lower external AUC as a consequence of explainable cross-database heterogeneity rather than evidence of model failure. Considering accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, GBM performed best among candidate algorithms and was therefore selected as the final prediction model.

Clinical interpretability of machine learning models is critical for medical practice. However, this field has long faced challenges of insufficient interpretability. The inherent "black-box" nature of many algorithms—characterized by intrinsic opacity and potential prediction bias risks—hinders their widespread clinical adoption [34]. To address this, our study introduced SHAP to provide global and local model interpretations, enhancing visualization and overall transparency. Compared to traditional feature-weight-based methods, SHAP demonstrates superior consistency, robustness, and stronger interpretative stability across diverse model architectures [35, 36]. Furthermore, SHAP quantifies feature contributions with mathematical fairness: each feature’s importance is evaluated independently of the presence or ordering of other features, ensuring comparability across samples and features. We established a unified decision interpretation framework based on SHAP value analysis. This approach precisely quantifies individual predictors’ contributions to model outputs, significantly improving model transparency and clinical interpretability to enhance acceptance and trustworthiness in clinical practice [37, 38].

This model identified three key predictors of delirium in CKD patients: GCS, SOFA score, and sedative usage. Among these, the GCS—a validated tool for assessing consciousness levels—emerged as one of the most significant predictors. Jeong I et al. [39] demonstrated significantly lower GCS scores in delirium patients versus non-delirium controls, indicating that reduced GCS scores correlate with increased delirium risk. Specifically, our study revealed substantially increased delirium severity in patients with GCS scores < 13. This finding aligns with our results. SHAP dependence plot analysis further elucidated that GCS scores ≤ 3 typically indicate severe brain injury, whereas scores ≥ 14 reflect normal consciousness. Notably, delirium risk was substantially elevated at GCS scores between 4 and 13, a pattern validated in other delirium prediction models [40, 41].

In this study, elevated SOFA scores correlated with higher SHAP values, indicating their critical role in predicting delirium risk among elderly CKD patients—specifically, increased SOFA scores associate with heightened delirium risk. SOFA score elevation typically reflects progressive underlying infections and evolving multiple organ dysfunction syndrome. Previous studies have confirmed that high SOFA scores significantly correlate with increased delirium risk in critically ill patients [42–44]. Consequently, SOFA has been incorporated into multiple delirium risk stratification or prediction models as a key indicator of organ dysfunction severity and delirium risk [45, 46]. Additionally, we observed that sedative administration within 24 h of ICU admission constituted a risk factor for delirium in elderly CKD patients. Thus, sedative use should be minimized or avoided when tolerated in this population. Current evidence shows no consensus regarding sedatives' impact on delirium incidence in critical illness. Some studies suggest sedatives may be independent risk factors for ICU delirium [47, 48]; however, others report early sedation reduces delirium incidence [49, 50]. These findings are not contradictory—clinical practice emphasizes individualized sedation protocols: intensifying sedation to stabilize vital signs during severe stress, then reducing sedation depth as conditions stabilize to facilitate neurological recovery.

In summary, SHAP methodology substantiates personalized delirium management strategies for elderly CKD patients. By quantifying feature contributions to predictions, it significantly enhances model interpretability and provides intuitive clinical decision support. Visualized SHAP values enable clinicians to comprehend key variables and their interactions, thereby improving decision transparency and establishing a scientific basis for personalized risk management. Such interpretable machine learning approaches hold significant value for advancing precision medicine in clinical practice.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although external validation was performed in a subset of the MIMIC III database, differences between the two databases in admission periods, ICU types, treatment strategies, and documentation systems mean that the model’s accuracy and generalizability across different settings still require further testing. Such heterogeneity and potential temporal drift may change the distribution of predictors and their relationships with outcomes, thereby affecting external performance. Therefore, before broader implementation, independent validation is needed in large multicenter prospective cohorts. Second, although we systematically identified and screened candidate predictors of delirium, certain important variables were not captured at the database level, including educational attainment and history of alcohol use [51, 52], as well as clinical contextual factors that are difficult to quantify comprehensively in these databases, such as cognitive status, sleep and pain management, and environmental or nursing related factors [53, 54]. These omissions may introduce residual confounding and bias risk estimates. Future studies should use prospective designs and more granular data collection to include and control for these unmeasured confounders. Third, delirium outcomes were primarily ascertained from database records, and measurement error and misclassification cannot be completely ruled out, which may influence both discrimination and calibration. Multi source validation, standardized assessment tools, and rigorous quality control procedures may help reduce this risk in subsequent research. Finally, the performance potential of more complex machine learning algorithms may not have been fully realized given the training sample size and class imbalance. We applied SMOTE in the training set to mitigate imbalance and used cross validation with grid based hyperparameter tuning to limit overfitting, but SMOTE can affect representation of true disease prevalence and calibration characteristics. Future work should include larger and more representative real world populations, along with prospective external validation and model updating, to further improve robustness. Despite these limitations, the online risk prediction model developed in this study has potential clinical value for early identification of delirium risk among older patients with chronic kidney disease. It may support clinical decision making and resource allocation and provides a foundation for multicenter validation and iterative model refinement.

Conclusion

This study developed a prediction tool based on a GBM model to assess the risk of delirium in elderly patients with CKD and validated its potential as a clinically reliable instrument. The model has been deployed as a web-based application, facilitating its use by healthcare professionals and enabling translation into clinical practice. This tool demonstrates high predictive accuracy and holds significant clinical implications. It can assist medical staff in optimizing clinical management decisions, thereby improving patient outcomes and quality of life.
