When Should LLMs Be Less Specific? Selective Abstraction for Reliable Long-Form Text Generation
Shani Goren, Ido Galil, Ran El-Yaniv, Technion

Abstract
LLMs are widely used, yet they remain prone to factual errors that erode user trust and limit adoption in high-risk settings. One approach to mitigate this risk is to equip models with uncertainty estimation mechanisms that abstain when confidence is low. However, this binary “all-or-nothing” approach is excessively restrictive in long-form settings, often discarding valuable information. We introduce Selective Abstraction (SA) , a framework that enables LLMs to trade specificity for reliability by selectively reducing the detail of uncertain content. We first formalize SA through the lenses of selective risk and coverage. We then propose Atom-wise Selective Abstraction , a claim-level instantiation that decomposes responses into atomic claims (short, self-contained statements each expressing a single fact) and replaces uncertain atoms with higher confidence, less specific abstractions. To evaluate this framework, we develop a novel end-to-end pipeline for open-ended generation that instantiates risk as factual correctness and measures coverage using an information-theoretic measure of retained information. Across six open-source models on the FactScore and LongFact-Objects benchmarks, atom-wise SA consistently outperforms existing baselines, improving the area under the risk-coverage curve (AURC) by up to 27.73% over claim removal, demonstrating that reducing specificity can boost accuracy and reliability while preserving most of their original meaning.

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated incredible performance across a broad spectrum of text generation tasks
[
3
,
13
,
6
]
. Despite these advances, LLMs remain prone to factual errors, generating content that appears fluent and coherent, but is factually incorrect or unsupported by underlying knowledge
[
18
,
40
,
16
,
22
]
.
As LLMs are increasingly deployed in high-stakes domains such as healthcare and law, factual mistakes can erode user trust and limit adoption. Ensuring trustworthy generation is therefore a central challenge for their reliable real-world use.

A widely studied approach to this problem is uncertainty estimation, which aims to quantify a model’s confidence in its outputs. In classification, uncertainty estimates support selective prediction
[
7
]
, where the model predicts only when confidence is sufficiently high and abstains otherwise. In text generation, a similar idea appears as
selective generation

[
34
]
, which allows the model to abstain from answering when confidence is low. This approach is effective for short outputs such as question answering, where it is reasonable to treat the response as a single decision.
However, selective generation does not scale well to long-form generation. Long responses often contain many factual claims with varying levels of uncertainty, sometimes within a single sentence. For example, the sentence “Amelia Earhart was an American aviator born on July 24, 1897” bundles multiple claims about profession, nationality, and date of birth. An LLM may be uncertain about the exact date while remaining confident about the month, the year, or any of the other details. In a long-form setting (e.g.: generating an entire biography, which may consist of dozens of sentences), abstaining from the entire generation is excessively restrictive, as it results in no content being generated at all. Even removing an entire sentence can discard valuable content. This highlights the need for a more fine-grained approach.

In long-form generation, a common failure mode is a single overconfident detail within an otherwise reasonable answer. In February 2023, Alphabet lost roughly $100 billion in market value in the wake of Google’s Bard debut, as a high-profile demo response drew scrutiny for a factual mistake
1
1

1

Reuters, “Alphabet shares dive after Google AI chatbot Bard flubs answer in ad”, Feb. 8, 2023.
. Asked about James Webb Space Telescope discoveries, Bard confidently claimed JWST took the “very first pictures” of an exoplanet, an assertion off by nearly two decades. Had Bard recognized uncertainty about whether JWST was “first” and instead generated “JWST has taken pictures of exoplanets” or “JWST has contributed important exoplanet discoveries”, the response would have remained informative while avoiding the hallucination. This incident illustrates a critical insight: not all information carries equal risk. Being overly specific in the wrong places can have dramatic consequences, while targeted abstraction, here removing just two words, maintains usefulness without sacrificing reliability.

We introduce
Selective Abstraction
, a framework that enables LLMs to reduce the amount of information in long-form generations when faced with uncertainty. Rather than relying on binary all-or-nothing decisions, Selective Abstraction retains informative content by reducing the specificity of uncertain parts, yielding outputs that are less specific but more reliable.

The key contributions of this paper are as follows:

1. We introduce
Selective Abstraction
, a general framework for trading specificity for reliability in long-form text generation, formalized through selective risk and coverage.

2. We propose
atom-wise selective abstraction
, a claim-level instantiation that selectively replaces uncertain claims with higher-confidence abstractions rather than removing them entirely.

3. We develop an
end-to-end evaluation pipeline
for selective abstraction in open-ended factual generation, instantiating risk via factual correctness and coverage via an information-theoretic measure of retained content.

4. We empirically demonstrate, across six open-source LLMs and two long-form factual benchmarks, that
atom-wise selective abstraction consistently improves the risk-coverage trade-off
, outperforming multiple baselines.

5. We propose a
risk-guided threshold selection algorithm
that selects a
confidence threshold
to meet a user-defined target risk with high probability, and evaluate it empirically.

Selective Prediction.

We view selective prediction as a starting point for formalizing Selective Abstraction. In classic selective prediction
[
7
,
12
]
, a classifier may abstain when uncertain. Formally, a selective model is a pair
(f,g)
, where
f
is a classifier and
g
is a selection function deciding whether to predict or abstain. A common choice defines
g
via a confidence score
\kappa
and threshold
\theta
:

g_{\theta}(x|\kappa,f)=\mathds{1}[\kappa(x,\hat{y}|f)>\theta]
, where
\hat{y}=f(x)
.
Thus, the model predicts only when its confidence exceeds
\theta
. Selective prediction induces a trade-off between
risk
and
coverage
. Risk is defined with respect to a given loss, e.g., the 0/1 loss, and is measured only over the samples that are predicted. Coverage is the fraction of samples that are not rejected. By rejecting uncertain samples, the selective model aims to reduce risk at the cost of lower coverage.

Hierarchical selective classification

[
14
]
extends selective classification to a hierarchical setting. Under uncertainty, the model can retreat to a less specific ancestor label rather than abstaining (e.g.: predicting “Dog” instead of “Golden Retriever”), thereby using the hierarchy to guide a reduction in the information content of predictions. This is conceptually aligned with our objective of trading specificity for reliability, rather than relying on a binary abstention decision.

Selective Generation.

Most existing work on uncertainty estimation for text generation focuses on tasks where the generated text is at most a few tokens long, such as machine translation, text summarization, and question answering (QA)
[
9
,
24
,
48
]
. Among these, QA is the most relevant to our setting. However, even in open-ended QA benchmarks, the generated answers are typically short, e.g. TriviaQA
[
19
]
and CoQA
[
33
]
. This allows for a binary decision: either accept or reject the model’s output. This approach, known as
selective generation

[
34
]
, serves as the NLP analogue of selective classification
[
9
,
10
,
25
,
42
,
43
]
. While these tasks are considerably easier to evaluate, the uncertainty estimation methods developed for them do not generalize well to long-form text generation
[
50
]
, which makes for a much more realistic use case for LLMs.

Evaluation of Long-form Generation.

In our work, quantifying
risk
(Section
4.1
) requires evaluating the reliability of the generated response. Throughout this paper, we use factual correctness as our notion of reliability. Most existing work in this research thread is focused on mitigating hallucinations in long-form text via fact-checking pipelines. These methods typically decompose model outputs into smaller units and verify them using external tools, such as retrieval systems or auxiliary LLMs.
Earlier frameworks such as FactScore laid important groundwork but are constrained by their design: FactScore
[
28
]
relies on static retrieval and entity-level assumptions, while SAFE
[
47
]
depends on the costly Google Search API and follows a rigid Google-Search loop with a predetermined number of queries per claim.
In contrast, our fact-checking agent (Section
4.1
) supports unconstrained Wikipedia search, and relies only on free publicly available resources, enabling large-scale evaluation.

An agentic approach to factuality evaluation has recently been adopted by OpenAI
2
2

2

https://cdn.openai.com/gpt-5-system-card.pdf
, which verifies claims using commercial models accessed via a paid API.
To reduce cost, they verify multiple claims within a single LLM call, trading off per-claim focus, since evidence and reasoning must be shared across claims and can degrade performance
[
17
]
. In contrast, our agent verifies each abstraction independently, enabling targeted evidence gathering and focused per-claim reasoning.

Uncertainty-Aware Fact-Checking.

Other works explicitly incorporate uncertainty estimation into fact-checking pipelines.
41
detects LLM hallucinations by extracting key parts of the output using an external model, and verify the uncertain parts against an external knowledge source.
31
proposed to fact-check complex statements by decomposing them into simpler subtasks and generating reasoning programs to verify these statements.
8
identify uncertain tokens in long-form outputs, though they stop short of editing the generation.

Granularity-driven Generation and Evaluation.

Recent work has begun to explore the relationship between granularity, uncertainty, and factuality in language model outputs, though mostly in narrow contexts.

49
observe that factual questions can be answered correctly at multiple levels of granularity, yet standard QA evaluation considers only a single reference level, penalizing less specific yet still correct responses. They find that models tend to be overly specific and propose reducing granularity by aggregating multiple sampled answers into a higher-level response. While conceptually aligned with our view that reducing information can mitigate factual errors, their approach is limited to QA and relies on sampling, making it impractical for long, open-ended generation.

They further introduce a relative informativeness metric defined only within alternative granularities of the same answer. In contrast, our information measure is computed independently of other claims, making it interpretable and applicable to open-ended generation, and consequently allowing comparisons across statements of varying granularity that are not related by abstraction.

Other works treat specificity as a one-sided metric, emphasizing maximal detail while overlooking its trade-off with correctness under uncertainty.
In conversational systems, specificity is often regarded as inherently desirable for making interactions more engaging, and annotators are guided to rate responses such that specific ones are preferred
[
1
,
39
]
. This narrow view also appears in factual QA settings
[
21
,
37
]
, where both human and LLM-as-a-judge annotators are instructed to favor more specific outputs.
15
evaluate specificity via masked-token prediction, but their formulation is restricted to narrow outputs and does not capture variable granularity in longer text.

3
Selective Abstraction

Selective abstraction (SA)
reduces the specificity of uncertain information in generated text, with the goal of improving its reliability.
In this section, we formalize the selective abstraction framework at a conceptual level.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be a language model. Given a prompt
p
, denote the language model’s generated text response
3
3

3

Formally,
\mathcal{M}(p)
induces a distribution over responses and
T\sim\mathcal{M}(p)
. Throughout this paper, we write quantities that depend on
T
(e.g., risk, coverage and information) as functions of a realized response, but they should be interpreted as expectation over the generation randomness.
:
\mathcal{M}(p)=T
.
We define selective abstraction as:

SA(p,\mathcal{M})=T^{\prime}
,
a process that uses
\mathcal{M}
to output
T^{\prime}
, a response to
p
such that, ideally
\text{Accuracy}(T^{\prime})>\text{Accuracy}(T)
, at the possible cost of information loss,
I(T^{\prime})\leq I(T)
where
I(\cdot)
denotes an information measure over texts (see Section
4.2
for definition).
We keep this definition broad to encompass any mechanism that uses uncertainty to reduce the amount of information expressed in the model’s response.

Selective abstraction can be performed using an explicit confidence scoring function, denoted
\kappa(t|\mathcal{M})
, that numerically quantifies the model’s confidence assigned to a text
t
(where

t
is any text string, including but not limited to the generated response). For a detailed discussion of the role of the confidence function, see Appendix
E
. A threshold
\theta
may also be specified to control the degree of information reduction: when
(\kappa,\theta)
are specified, the resulting response
T^{\prime}
is required to satisfy
\kappa(T^{\prime})\geq\theta
. We denote this by

SA(p,\mathcal{M},\kappa,\theta)=T^{\prime}
.

We evaluate the abstractive behavior of SA using
risk
and
coverage
.
Risk can be quantified via any user-chosen loss function that reflects reliability, for instance, the hallucination rate.
Coverage is defined as the fraction of retained information,

\phi(T^{\prime},T)=\frac{I(T^{\prime})}{I(T)}.

Varying the threshold
\theta
induces a risk-coverage trade-off. Higher thresholds will reduce coverage while decreasing risk. In Section
4
, we formalize the concrete risk and coverage definitions used in our experiments.

3.1
Atom-wise Selective Abstraction

Long-form responses contain many distinct pieces of information, so selective abstraction must operate at a finer granularity.
We therefore propose a “claim-level” instantiation of SA, as a procedure that enables control of information reduction.
Concretely, we selectively replace low-confidence claims with less specific, higher-confidence abstractions. All prompts used throughout this section are provided in Appendix
B
.

Stage 1: Generation.

Given a prompt
p
, we generate an initial response
T=\mathcal{M}(p)
.

Stage 2: Atomization.

We follow prior work
[
29
,
36
,
51
,
27
,
28
]
and decompose the text into
atomic claims
4
4

4

Throughout this paper, we use
atomic claims
and
atoms
interchangeably.
, which are short, standalone statements, each expressing a single piece of information.
Let
\mathcal{A}_{T}=(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
denote the set of atoms extracted from
T
. The decomposition is performed by prompting the generating LLM with few-shot examples.

Stage 3: Abstraction.

This is the core stage of our framework. It operates over the atoms produced in Stage 2 and consists of three steps: (1) atom confidence elicitation, (2) abstraction generation, and (3) selection.

(1) Atom Confidence Elicitation.

We obtain the model’s confidence score for each atom. Let
\kappa_{\text{atom}}(a_{i}\mid p,T,\mathcal{M})
denote a confidence scoring function that quantifies the confidence of the model
\mathcal{M}
in
a_{i}
, conditioned on the prompt
p
and the original response
T
.
The primary confidence function we use throughout the paper is
verbalized
, meaning that the LLM is explicitly prompted to report its confidence verbally. However, our method can be used with any confidence function. For discussion on alternative confidence functions, see Appendix
E
. For each response, the model is provided with the full list of atoms in a single prompt and asked to assign a confidence score to each atom.

(2) Abstraction Generation and Confidence Elicitation.

Let
\theta
denote a confidence threshold.
For each atom with
\kappa_{\text{atom}}(a_{i}\mid p,T,\mathcal{M})<\theta
, we prompt the generating model to produce an abstraction sequence:

Ab(a_{i})=\bigl(a_{i}^{(0)},a_{i}^{(1)},\ldots,a_{i}^{(j)},a_{i}^{(j+1)}=\top\bigr),

where
a_{i}^{(0)}=a_{i}
, each
a_{i}^{(j+1)}
is less specific than
a_{i}^{(j)}
, and
\top
corresponds to full abstention (a zero-information abstraction).
We then obtain an abstraction-level confidence score
\kappa_{\text{abs}}(a_{i}^{(j)}\mid p,T,\mathcal{M})
for each abstraction in the sequence by prompting the model to score the list of abstractions, conditioned on the atom confidence
\kappa_{\text{atom}}(a_{i}\mid p,T,\mathcal{M})
(We discuss the motivation for this choice in Appendix
C
).

(3) Selection.

After obtaining confidence scores for all abstractions, we perform a selection step that determines the appropriate level of abstraction for each atom. The goal of this step is to reduce information only as needed, ensuring that the confidence of each selected abstraction exceeds the threshold
\theta
.
Formally, for each atom
a_{i}
, select the most specific abstraction
a_{i}^{(j^{*})}
that satisfies the threshold:

a_{i}^{(j^{*})}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad j^{*}=\arg\min_{j}\left\{\kappa_{\text{abs}}\left(a_{i}^{(j)}\mid\mathcal{M},p,T\right)>\theta\right\}.

If no such
j
exists, abstain:
a_{i}^{(j^{*})}=\top
.
This step yields a single selected abstraction (or abstention) per atom.

Stage 4: Reconstruction.

In the final step, we prompt the LLM to reconstruct the selected abstractions into a coherent text.
T^{\prime}
, yielding a revised version of
T
that preserves high-confidence content while abstracting uncertain details.

4
Evaluating Selective Abstraction

Evaluating selective abstraction requires quantifying both risk and information, which is inherently difficult in open-ended generation. In such settings, a ground truth may not exist, and even when it does, there is often no clear way to quantify how much information remains. Although selective abstraction can, in principle, be applied to any kind of text generation, defining meaningful measures of risk, and especially information, is not always possible.
For this reason, we evaluate selective abstraction on factual benchmarks, which provide a controlled setting in which both risk and information allow well-defined measurements. This choice reflects an evaluation constraint rather than a limitation of the proposed method. Our goal is to establish an experimental foundation for studying selective abstraction. Appendix
I
provides additional qualitative examples on non-factual generation.
In this section, we specify the concrete instantiations of
risk
and
information
used for the factual evaluations in this paper.

4.1
Risk

Let
A(T)=(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be the set of atomic claims extracted from a response
T
.
For each atom
a_{i}
, we assign a correctness label:

\mathrm{correct}(a_{i})=\begin{cases}1&\text{$a_{i}$ is supported by evidence,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We then define risk on
T
as:

R(T)=1-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{correct}(a_{i}).

To annotate
\mathrm{correct}(a_{j})
, we adopt an agentic fact-checking approach that verifies abstractions against Wikipedia.
The agent searches for evidence supporting each abstraction and labels it as
supported
when such evidence is found, or as
unsupported
when the abstraction is explicitly refuted or when no supporting evidence can be identified (prompts are provided in Appendix
D.1
).
Our approach relies only on publicly available resources, using the free Wikipedia API
5
5

5

https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/API:Action_API
together with the open-source model gpt-oss-120b
[
30
]
as the agent’s backbone. This enables dynamic, reasoning-based annotation while supporting large-scale evaluation.
To validate our risk evaluation pipeline, we manually evaluated 102 sampled claims
6
6

6

This validation covers claims uniformly sampled across models, confidence levels, and factual correctness labels. The agent labeled 50 as correct and 52 as incorrect.
against human judgments, yielding an overall F1 score of 0.93.

4.2
Coverage and Information

Let
A(T)=(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and

A(T^{\prime})=(a^{\prime}_{j})_{j=1}^{m}
denote the sets of atomic claims extracted from an initial response
T
and an abstracted response
T^{\prime}
, respectively. We define the information content of a text as the sum of the information of its distinct atomic claims (after deduplication). Accordingly,

\phi(T^{\prime},T)=\frac{I(T^{\prime})}{I(T)}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}I(a^{\prime}_{j})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(a_{i})}.

We quantify the information encoded in an atomic claim by estimating how strongly it narrows down the set of compatible entities, that is, how much new information it contributes about an entity.
For example, consider the claim
“Alan Turing was born in London”
. We view it as specifying a subject-independent condition,
born in London
, and thus as defining a subset of entities that satisfy this condition. Relative to a broad reference set,
People
, the claim restricts the compatible entities from all people to the smaller subset of people born in London.
Practically, this corresponds to asking how many people were born in London out of all people, and using that ratio as a proxy for how informative the claim is.

Formally, each claim
c
has the form
[\mathrm{entity}][\mathrm{predicate}]
. We measure information by interpreting the
\mathrm{predicate}
span as a subject-independent condition over entities of the appropriate type.
This induces: (i) a domain of discourse
\mathcal{E}
, the set of all entities of the same type as
\mathrm{entity}
(e.g., if
\mathrm{entity}
is a person, then
\mathcal{E}=\{\text{all people}\}
), and (ii) a predicate
P_{c}:E\to\{0,1\}
derived by removing the specific entity from
c
.
We then define:

\mathcal{E}_{c}\;=\;\{\,e\in\mathcal{E}\;:\;P_{c}(e)=1\,\},\mathcal{E}_{c}\subseteq\mathcal{E}.

In our example, for
c=
“Alan Turing was born in London”,
\mathcal{E}
is the set of all people, and
\mathcal{E}_{c}
is the subset of people born in London.

We adopt an information-theoretic perspective and measure the uncertainty associated with each set by computing its entropy. Assuming a uniform prior
7
7

7

This is a simplifying assumption with known limitations, since real-world entity frequencies are not uniform, but it enables estimating entropy from set size alone without introducing extra assumptions about how likely different entities are.
within each set, the entropy of a set
\mathcal{E}
is:

H(\mathcal{E})=-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}|}\log{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}|}}=\log({{|\mathcal{E}|}}).

We then define the information encoded in
c
as the relative reduction in entropy:

I(c)=1-\frac{H(\mathcal{E}_{c})}{H(\mathcal{E})}=1-\frac{\log({{|\mathcal{E}_{c}|}})}{\log({{|\mathcal{E}|}})}.

I(c)
increases as
c
narrows the set of compatible entities. By construction, trivial claims (
\mathcal{E}_{c}=\mathcal{E}
, for instance: “X is a person”) carry zero information.

Our notion of information aligns with prior work that views information as uncertainty reduction
[
4
,
5
,
35
]
.
This perspective also offers a useful intuition: our measure resembles information gain in decision-tree splits.
Each atomic claim narrows the set of compatible entities, and
I(c)
quantifies the resulting uncertainty reduction. We emphasize that this analogy is given for intuition only: unlike decision trees on a fixed dataset, our splits are defined over an open-world entity set.

To estimate
|\mathcal{E}_{c}|
and
|\mathcal{E}|
automatically, we use Wikidata
[
46
]
, one of the largest publicly available knowledge bases, which stores structured information about real-world entities and can be queried using SPARQL, a standard query language for structured knowledge bases.
We use the SPINACH agent
[
26
]
, which translates natural-language questions into SPARQL queries and executes them against the Wikidata Query Service to estimate the sizes of the corresponding entity sets. Since obtaining verified real-world counts is infeasible in an open-world setting, we use Wikidata as a best-available proxy for these set sizes.

Continuing the example, we query Wikidata for the total number of people (
12{,}352{,}844
) and for the number of people born in London (
20{,}313
). Using these values,

I(c)=1-\frac{\log(20{,}313)}{\log(12{,}352{,}844)}\approx 0.39.

For comparison,
“born in England”
corresponds to
135{,}340
entities, giving
I\approx 0.28
, consistent with
“born in London”
being more specific than
“born in England”
.

Remarks.

1. These counts should not be read as real-world demographics, since Wikidata is sparse and does not cover all entities. Still, it is a reasonable proxy for measuring such counts at scale. The procedure we describe can be done with any knowledge source that can produce such counts, including a more complete database if one becomes available.

2. Importantly, our framework is agnostic to the definition of information: an alternative information measure can be plugged in without altering SA itself. In fact, SA (and the threshold selection algorithm in Section
7
) can be applied without any information measure; in this case, retained information is not quantifiable, and coverage cannot be reported.

4.3
RC Curves and AURC

Selective abstraction induces a trade-off between reliability and specificity, measured by risk and coverage. We summarize this trade-off using
Risk-Coverage (RC) curves

[
7
]
, which plot the risk of retained content as a function of coverage. An example RC curve is shown in Figure
1
(right).

Given a confidence scoring function
\kappa(\cdot)
and a threshold
\theta
,
SA
produces an abstracted response
T^{\prime}(\theta)
. For each
\theta
, we compute its coverage
\phi(T^{\prime}(\theta),T)
and risk
R(T^{\prime}(\theta))
, as defined in the preceding subsections. Varying
\theta
over the range of
\kappa
traces an RC curve.

We summarize each RC curve using the
area under the RC curve (AURC)
, defined as

\mathrm{AURC}\;=\;\int_{0}^{1}R(\phi)\,d\phi,

where
R(\phi)
denotes the risk achieved at coverage
\phi
. In practice, since evaluation is performed on a finite set of examples, we sweep over all distinct attainable thresholds
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, induced by the confidence scores in the evaluation set, which yields a finite sequence of operating points with coverages
\{\phi_{k}\}
(sorted by increasing order). If the minimum observed coverage is strictly positive, we add an anchor point at
\phi=0
with risk equal to the risk at the minimum observed coverage. This prevents methods that only attain a narrow, high-coverage range from appearing artificially better due to integrating over a smaller portion of
[0,1]
, and ensures AURC comparisons reflect performance over a common coverage domain.

Lower AURC indicates a better risk-coverage trade-off because it corresponds to lower risk on average across the full coverage range.

We compare our proposed abstraction method,

(1) Selective Redaction:

(2) Inline Abstraction:

(3) Self-Revision:

T

(4) Additional
\theta

and

Results

Across all models and datasets, atom-wise selective abstraction consistently achieves a better risk-coverage trade-off than the competing approaches considered in this work. This advantage is evident in summary metrics, through lower AURC (Table
1
), and is also visible in the risk-coverage curves (Figures
7
and
8
in Appendix
G
).
These results suggest that gradual, atom-wise reduction is an effective and controllable mechanism for reducing information in a way that improves reliability while preserving coverage.

Compared to the redaction baseline, atom-wise SA achieves substantially lower AURC (Table
1
): the average improvement in AURC across models is 17.43% on FactScore and 13.77% on LongFact. The maximal improvement we have measured was for gpt-oss-120b, where the improvement was 27.73% and 21.02% on FactScore and LongFact, respectively.
The risk-coverage curves (Figures
7
and
8
in Appendix
G
) further confirm these gains: atom-wise SA yields a smooth trade-off that dominates redaction and the other baselines across the full coverage range, consistently achieving lower risk at the same coverage.

We note that the relative improvements on LongFact are smaller than on FactScore because LongFact generations start at a lower initial risk at full coverage, leaving less headroom for improvement. One plausible reason FactScore starts at higher risk is that FactScore prompts, which ask for biographies, push LLMs toward generating highly specific factual details, whereas LongFact questions may often be answered in broader terms.

Notably, the models with the largest AURC improvements are the same on both benchmarks, gpt-oss-120b and qwen3-235b-thinking, and the least improved model is also the same, llama-3.3-70b-instruct. This cross-benchmark agreement suggests that abstraction performance may be driven by model-specific properties.

7
Threshold Selection for Target Risk

Thus far, we have studied the trade-off between risk and coverage induced by atom-wise SA.
However, practical deployment typically requires fixing a specific confidence threshold
\theta
, which calls for a principled method for choosing it.
In this section, we propose an algorithm for choosing
\theta
to satisfy a user-specified target risk.
Our approach is inspired by split conformal prediction
[
45
]
, using a calibration set to select a confidence threshold that, with high probability, guarantees that the risk on unseen test samples does not exceed a desired target. The full algorithm and its theoretical guarantees are provided in Appendix
H
. Here, we describe the practical procedure and present empirical results.
The algorithm takes as input a language model
\mathcal{M}
, a target risk level
\alpha
, and a calibration set of prompts
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}=\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. It outputs a confidence threshold
\hat{\theta}
such that the atom-wise selective abstraction achieves risk at most
\alpha+\epsilon
on new samples, with confidence at least
1-\delta
.
Concretely, the algorithm first queries
\mathcal{M}
on each
p_{i}
to obtain an initial response
T_{i}
, and decomposes it into atomic claims
A(T_{i})
, forming a consolidated calibration set of atoms,
A_{cal}=\bigcup_{i}A(T_{i})
. For each atom
a_{k}\in A_{cal}
, the algorithm computes a critical threshold
\theta_{k}
, defined as the minimal confidence level at which the abstraction selected for
a_{k}
is correct. In particular, if the original atom
a_{k}
is already correct, then
\theta_{k}=0
.
Finally, the algorithm outputs
\hat{\theta}
, chosen as a quantile of the calibration thresholds.

Empirical Results.
We evaluate the proposed algorithm on a range of target risks using the models and datasets detailed in Section
5
. We repeat each experiment (dataset + model combination) 100 times, sampling a fresh calibration set at each iteration. Each calibration set comprises
30\%
of the benchmark prompts. We report dataset-level results averaged over all models and repetitions (600 runs per dataset).
Figure
4
shows that the algorithm balances the risk-coverage trade-off, with empirical risk consistently falling close to the target risk. We observe some variability, which is expected given the relatively small amount of calibration and test data, and we expect larger datasets to yield more stable results.

8
Concluding Remarks

This work argues that reliability in long-form generation is not a binary choice between answering and abstaining, but a trade-off between specificity and correctness. We introduce Selective Abstraction as a general framework for navigating this trade-off, and instantiate it through an atom-wise procedure that selectively reduces the information in uncertain claims.
Our empirical evaluation across six open-source LLMs and two open-ended factual benchmarks, shows that atom-wise selective abstraction consistently outperforms standard baselines, including redaction, inline abstraction, and self-revision. Specifically, we observed improvements in AURC of up to 27.73%.
By operating at the level of individual claims, the proposed method avoids abrupt information loss from claim deletion and provides targeted, claim-specific control over uncertainty in long-form outputs.

Future work.

(1) Confidence scoring function: the selection and thresholding of abstractions inherently depend on the quality of these scores. While we justify our use of verbal confidence and compare it to alternatives in Appendix
E
, we recognize that verbalized scores are imperfect, and expect stronger confidence scoring to further enhance performance.
(2) Treating atoms as independent is a common and practical default; however, modeling dependencies between claims (e.g., via a dependency graph) may further improve abstraction quality.

Fundamentally, we view Selective Abstraction as a step toward generation paradigms that reason about the amount of detail in a response. In high-stakes settings, less specific responses are preferable to confident errors.

Atomization Prompt

# Role and Objective

You are an Atom Extraction Assistant. Your task is to break down a given text into short, self-contained sentences called atoms. Each atom should capture exactly one entity and one piece of information about that entity.

# Definition of an Atom

Each atom must capture exactly one entity and one piece of information about that entity. The atom must be in the format:
[entity] [information about the entity].
Both the entity and the information must be enclosed in square brackets.

# Instructions

- Each atom must capture exactly one entity and one piece of information about that entity.

- Atoms must be as fine-grained as possible: each should express a **single, indivisible fact**.

- Do not merge multiple facts into one atom, even if they appear in the same clause or sentence.

- Ensure that each atom is independent and can be easily understood without relying on other atoms.

- If a sentence presents multiple facts—such as nationality, profession, or achievements—split them into separate atoms.

- The entity must be a specific, concrete noun or noun phrase. Avoid vague terms like ”he”, ”she”, ”they”, or ”these”. Entities should be simple and specific, such as ”Albert Einstein”, ”the Eiffel Tower”, ”the Mona Lisa”, ”Australia”, ”Apollo 11”, etc.

- These guidelines are crucial to ensure that each atom can be independently searched in knowledge bases to verify its factual accuracy.

- Ensure that every piece of information given in the text is captured in the resulting atoms.

# Output Format

Output a numbered list of atoms:

1. [Entity] [Fact].

2. [Entity] [Fact].

…

# Examples

## Example 1

Input Text:

Jennifer Coolidge is an American actress. Known for her work in the comedy genre, Coolidge is the recipient of several accolades, including a Golden Globe Award and two Primetime Emmy Awards.

Atoms:

1. [Jennifer Coolidge] [is an American].

2. [Jennifer Coolidge] [is an actress].

3. [Jennifer Coolidge] [is known for her work in the comedy genre].

4. [Jennifer Coolidge] [has received several accolades].

5. [Jennifer Coolidge] [has received a Golden Globe Award].

6. [Jennifer Coolidge] [has received two Primetime Emmy Awards].

# Role and Objective

You are a careful and critical evaluator. Your task is to assess the factual correctness of each statement and provide a confidence score that reflects how likely it is that the statement is true.

# Instructions

You will be given a list of factual statements. For each one, produce a short explanation of your reasoning, then provide a confidence estimate between 0 and 100.

- Do not repeat the statement in the reasoning.

# Confidence Scoring Guidelines

- Reasoning should explain why the fact is likely true, plausible, or questionable.

- If you are unsure, assign a lower score and use your reasoning to clarify.

- Do not fabricate information.

# Reasoning Steps

1. Read the statement carefully.

2. Consider whether it expresses a known fact, plausible claim, or questionable statement.

3. Use your internal knowledge and reasoning to assess plausibility and factuality.

4. Write a brief explanation of your reasoning.

5. Assign a confidence score based on your belief that the statement is true.

# Review and Guidance

Think step by step. For each statement, explain your reasoning clearly and concisely before giving your final confidence score.

Appendix C
Abstraction Generation and Confidence Elicitation: Additional Discussion

This appendix expands on Section
3
(Selective Abstraction), focusing on Stage 3 (Abstraction) in the end-to-end pipeline. In this stage, low-confidence atoms are abstracted into less specific alternatives in order to trade specificity for reliability. We clarify the motivation behind Step (2), which constructs a sequence of abstraction candidates for each atom and elicits confidence scores for these candidates conditioned on the atom’s original confidence.

Why generate a sequence of abstractions?

Selective abstraction requires choosing a minimally abstracted variant that satisfies the confidence threshold. Generating a sequence of abstractions with gradually reduced specificity provides a discrete, ordered set of candidates for this selection. This formulation makes thresholding well-defined: rather than relying on a single unconstrained rewrite, the method can select the most specific abstraction whose confidence exceeds the threshold. In addition, eliciting confidences jointly for related abstractions encourages a coherent progression in confidence as information is reduced, aligning abstraction depth with reliability.

Why condition abstraction confidence on the atom’s confidence?

Conditioning abstraction confidence elicitation on the atom’s original confidence serves two purposes. First, it anchors the abstraction confidences to the uncertainty of the underlying factual content, encouraging abstraction confidence to reflect reduced specificity rather than a complete re-evaluation of factual correctness. Second, this conditioning helps maintain comparability across atoms by evaluating each atom–abstraction group relative to its own baseline confidence, reducing drift caused by prompt-level or statement-level variability. Together, these effects support a stable relationship between abstraction depth and confidence, which is essential for consistent threshold-based selection.

Additionally, assigning confidence scores within the same elicitation call may help relatively calibrate abstraction confidences across atoms by anchoring them to a common scale.

On the Importance of Choosing a Confidence Scoring Function

The choice of the confidence scoring function
\kappa
is critical, as it directly influences the quality of the downstream abstractions. The confidence function determines which atoms are selected for abstraction and the degree to which they are abstracted, thereby shaping the trade-off between information and reliability.

E.1
Comparison Metrics

RC Curves and AURC.

We first compare confidence scoring functions directly by the risk-coverage trade-offs they induce: a
\kappa
that achieves a better trade-off (lower AURC) is preferable
[
7
]
.

Ranking (AUROC).

Ranking captures the extent to which the confidence score induces a partial order on samples, where correct samples are assigned higher confidence scores than incorrect samples
[
11
]
. In our setting, this property is central to selective abstraction: a
\kappa
that ranks well will ensure that the atoms that are most likely to be incorrect are abstracted first, since they will be assigned the lowest confidence values. Conversely, poor ranking leads to inefficient abstraction, where correct atoms are unnecessarily abstracted while incorrect atoms are retained.

We quantify ranking quality using the area under the ROC curve (AUROC), which measures the probability that a randomly chosen correct atom receives a higher confidence score than a randomly chosen incorrect atom. AUROC therefore reflects how well
\kappa
separates correct from incorrect content across all possible thresholds. High AUROC implies that abstraction can be focused on genuinely risky atoms, yielding substantial risk reduction for a given loss of information.

E.2
\kappa
Comparison

We compare the verbalized confidence score used in this paper to the following confidence scores:

1.

Logprob-based scores
8
8

8

In atom-wise SA, the atomization stage produces atomized text conditioned on the original response. Although the atom tokens largely overlap with the original response, we cannot directly use the atomization logits, since they reflect the model’s behavior under the atomization prompt rather than its confidence in the original generation. Instead, we estimate a logprob-based proxy post hoc by feeding the atomized text back into the LLM and extracting the log-probabilities associated with each atom. While these probabilities need not match those assigned during the original generation, they provide a close proxy for the model’s confidence in the factual content of each atom.
:
We evaluate a range of confidence scores derived from token-level log-probabilities of the generated text. These include sequence-level aggregates such as log-likelihood and (inverse) perplexity, as well as more local measures such as the minimum token log-probability. In addition, we consider simple statistics over token log-probabilities (e.g., mean, median, standard deviation, and interquartile range), and related rank-based signals. Figure

Taken together, these results highlight that ranking quality is the primary driver of effective selective abstraction. Verbalized confidence performs best on both AURC and AUROC, yielding the strongest risk-coverage trade-off and the most reliable ordering of atoms by correctness likelihood. For this reason, we adopt verbal confidence as the default confidence scoring function throughout this work.
