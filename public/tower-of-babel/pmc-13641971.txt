Evaluation of image quality in conventional intraoral radiographs acquired with a stationary intraoral tomosynthesis system
Authors: Débora Costa Ruiz, Marcela Tarosso Réa, Gabriela Gavilan Hadid, William C Scarfe, Gustavo Machado Santaella, Christiano de Oliveira-Santos, Deborah Queiroz Freitas, Sergio Lins de-Azevedo-Vaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo objectively evaluate the image quality of conventional two-dimensional intraoral radiographs acquired with a stationary intraoral tomosynthesis (s-IOT) system under different tube voltage settings and post-processing adjustments.Materials and MethodsTwenty-four radiographs of an acrylic-block phantom were obtained using a s-IOT system coupled to a size-2 CMOS sensor, with standardized exposure parameters (7 mA and 0.16 seconds of exposure) under two conditions: two tube voltages (60 and 70 kVp) and, with (“current image”) and without (“current frame”) post-processing image export modes. Brightness, noise, and uniformity were quantitatively assessed using ImageJ (U.S National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) software. Twenty-four radiographs of a dental digital quality assurance phantom were obtained under the same conditions to evaluate spatial and contrast resolution, and percentage of radiographic contrast. All image quality parameters were compared between export modes and tube voltages using two-way repeated-measures Analysis of Variance followed by Sidak post-hoc tests (P<0.05).ResultsCurrent image radiographs had higher brightness and uniformity, lower percentage of radiographic contrast and noise compared with current frame radiographs. Spatial resolution ranged from 9 lp/mm at 60 kVp to 10 lp/mm at 70 kVp, irrespective of post-processing adjustments. Contrast resolution was superior in radiographs without post-processing adjustments, regardless of the kVp.ConclusionRadiographs acquired with the s-IOT system without post-processing adjustments show lower brightness and higher contrast, but increased noise and reduced uniformity compared with post processed images. Spatial resolution was unaffected by post-processing adjustments but was improved by increasing exposure to 70 kVp.Keywords: Radiography, Dental; Carbon; Dental Equipment; Nanotubes; Quality Control

Introduction

Intraoral radiography is the most widely used imaging modality in dental practice complementing the clinical diagnostic workflow in the detection and assessment of a broad spectrum of conditions affecting the teeth and their supporting structures.1,2 The primary limitation of this imaging modality is it provides a two-dimensional representation of three-dimensional anatomy because of superimposition of dental and adjacent anatomical structures.3 This may reduce its diagnostic accuracy for various conditions, such as root resorption and root fracture.3,4

Stationary intraoral tomosynthesis (s-IOT) has been proposed as an alternative approach to overcome these constraints. In this system, multiple X-ray sources are spatially distributed within a common housing and directed towards a single sensor providing multiple image projections. These single projections are reconstructed using iterative algorithms to generate a tomosynthetic stack of slices, allowing independent visualization of different planes of the three-dimensional anatomy which can be viewed individually or assembled to prove a volumetric dataset.5,6,7,8 Until recently an s-IOT intraoral imaging system (PORTRAY 3D Intraoral device, Surround Medical Systems, Morrisville, NC, USA) was commercially available which used carbon nanotube field-emission source array arranged linearly over an angular span of approximately 12 degrees, coupled to a dedicated complementary metal-oxide semiconductor (CMOS) sensor.6 The s-IOT system can reduce proximal enamel overlap in bitewing radiographs, enhancing the visualization of interproximal tooth surfaces and potentially improving diagnostic accuracy.6

Yet an underexplored feature of this technology is the assembly of individual tomosynthetic images to create conventional two-dimensional radiographs.9 This allows clinicians to integrate advanced tomosynthesis into a conventional intraoral imaging system.9 In the conventional 2D operating mode, acquisition parameters such as tube voltage (kVp) can be adjusted. Moreover, the Portray system allows export of reconstructed images with different degrees of post-processing depending on the selected export mode: “current frame” and “current image”. When the “current frame” option is selected, the exported image corresponds more closely to the original acquired data, with minimal additional processing. The “current image” option incorporates post-processing adjustments intended to optimize image visualization for anatomical interpretation and clinical diagnosis. The potential influence of this post processing or tube voltage on image quality has not yet been investigated. Therefore, the present study aimed to objectively evaluate the image quality of conventional radiographs acquired with an s-IOT system, comparing images with and without post-processing adjustments under different kVp settings.

Materials and Methods

Brightness, noise and uniformity assessment

Twenty-four radiographs (n = 24) were obtained using an s-IOT intraoral imaging system (PORTRAY 3D Intraoral device, Surround Medical Systems, Morrisville, NC, USA) coupled to the system’s proprietary size-2 CMOS sensor, with an active imaging area of 35.32 × 26.64 mm. An acrylic block phantom (7 cm × 7 cm × 2.5 cm) was positioned parallel 3 mm from the CMOS sensor at a source-to-sensor distance of 40 cm. All exposures were performed with standardized tube current (7 mA) and exposure time (0.16 sec) at two tube voltage settings (60 and 70 kVp).

Images were exported both with (“current image”) or without (“current frame”) post processing. Therefore, consisted of four experimental groups: 1) “current frame” at 60 kVp (n = 6), 2) “current frame” at 70 kVp (n = 6), 3) “current image” at 60 kVp (n = 6), and 4) “current image” at 70 kVp (n = 6). All radiographs were exported in 8-bit depth and saved in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format for subsequent analysis (Figs. 1 and 2).

Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software (ImageJ 1.x, National Institutes of Health, MD, USA) by a single oral and maxillofacial radiologist. Three objective image quality parameters were assessed: brightness, noise, and uniformity.

For brightness and noise measurements, a square (14 mm × 14 mm) region of interest (ROI) was positioned at the center of each radiograph (Fig. 3). The mean gray value of this ROI represented image brightness, whereas the standard deviation (SD) of the gray values within the ROI represented image noise.10,11,12

Matrix of representative images illustrating the assessment of brightness, noise, and uniformity using the acrylic block phantom, and the evaluation of spatial resolution, contrast resolution, and percentage of radiographic contrast using the DDQA phantom. Orange squares denote the regions of interest, and the orange line indicates the profile line used for spatial resolution analysis.

Uniformity was measured using the SD of the gray values in five square (4 mm × 4 mm) ROIs. One was placed in the center of the image and four symmetrically distributed near the upper and lower borders (Fig. 3). Lower SD values implied greater uniformity.10,11,12 To ensure reproducibility across the assessment, the macro function of ImageJ was employed to identically position all ROIs in every image.

Spatial resolution, contrast resolution and percentage of radiographic contrast

Twenty-four radiographs (n = 24) were acquired from a dental digital quality assurance (DDQA) phantom (Dental Imaging Consultants LLC, San Antonio, TX, USA). The phantom comprises three components: 1) a line-pair pattern (5–20 lp/mm) to assess spatial resolution; 2) two rows of six cylindrical wells to evaluate contrast resolution—one with constant depth and varying diameters (0.20–2.5 mm) and the other with constant diameter and varying depths (0.125–0.75 mm); and 3) a step-wedge with one step of air, five aluminum steps of increasing thickness, and one step of to determine the percentage of radiographic contrast.

All radiographs were acquired using the same s-IOT system, acquisition geometry and exposure parameters. The sample therefore comprised four experimental groups: 1) “current frame” at 60 kVp (n = 6), 2) “current frame” at 70 kVp (n = 6), 3) “current image” at 60 kVp (n = 6), and 4) “current image” at 70 kVp (n = 6). All images were exported in 8-bit depth and saved in DICOM format for subsequent analyses (Figs. 1 and 2).

All 24 radiographs were analyzed using ImageJ software. To assess spatial resolution, a rectangular profile line 25 mm in length and 50 pixels in width was drawn on each image, crossing the center of the line pair patterns (Fig. 3). The gray values along this profile were extracted. For each experimental group, the mean gray values obtained from the six images were plotted, generating four graphs. In these graphs, the y-axis corresponded to the mean gray values, whereas the x-axis represented the distance along the 25-mm profile, thereby depicting the spatial resolution range from 5 to 20 lp/mm.13,14,15 By consensus, two oral and maxillofacial radiologists evaluated the spatial resolution limit graphs (Fig. 4).

Matrix of representative graphs generated based on the mean gray values extracted from the profile lines drawn on each radiograph, considering the different export modes and kVp settings tested.

Contrast resolution was evaluated using the two rows of cylindrical wells of the DDQA phantom. By consensus, two oral and maxillofacial radiologists assessed each radiograph and recorded the number of wells that could be clearly identified in each row for each experimental condition. The total number of visible wells was used as an indicator of contrast resolution.13,14,15

For the assessment of percentage of radiographic contrast, a single oral and maxillofacial radiologist placed two square (4 mm × 4 mm) ROIs on the aluminum step-wedge. One ROI was centered on the fifth aluminum step (ROI 1) and a second centered on the second aluminum step (ROI 2). Statistical analysis

Spatial resolution was evaluated based on the data presented in six graphs, while contrast resolution was assessed by counting the number of visible wells. The data were expressed using descriptive analysis. The values of brightness, noise, uniformity, and percentage of radiographic contrast were compared between the export modes and kVp settings tested with Analysis of Variance (Two-Way Repeated Measures ANOVA) and Sidak test. Significance level was set at 5%. The power analysis of the test was 90% for all the variables evaluated. Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS 25.0 software program (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Radiographs exported as “current image” demonstrated significantly higher brightness, lower noise, greater uniformity and lower percentage of radiographic contrast compared with those exported as “current frame”. An increase in kVp resulted in a decrease in brightness in the “current image”, and a slight increase in the “current frame”. Moreover, 70 kVp settings were associated with increased noise and reduced uniformity for both export modes. For the percentage of radiographic contrast, increasing kVp also increased the values, irrespective of the export mode.

Figure 4 shows a graphic representation of spatial resolution for “current image” and “current frame” according to different kVp values. The peak of each wave corresponds to a specific line pair. Each peak should demonstrate 5 distinct oscillations to be considered as having that specific line pair resolution. Although the peak gray values varied with kVp (reaching 9 lp/mm at 60 kVp and 10 lp/mm at 70 kVp), no differences in were observed between the two image export modes.

Differences in contrast resolution were observed between the “current image” and “current frame” export modes. In the row of wells with identical depth and varying diameters, all six wells were visualized in images exported as “current frame” at both kVp settings. In contrast, for the “current image” format, five wells were discernible at 60 kVp, whereas all six wells were visualized at 70 kVp. For the row of wells with identical diameters and varying depths, no wells were visualized in the “current image” at 60 kVp and two wells at 70 kVp. Conversely, in the “current frame” format, two wells were visualized at 60 kVp and three wells at 70 kVp.

Discussion

This is the first study to evaluate a carbon nanotube-based s-IOT system for acquiring conventional intraoral radiographs. Because the “current frame” mode exports images with minimal processing, whereas the “current image” mode applies post-processing adjustments, an objective investigation was warranted. The present findings demonstrate that radiographs exported using “current image” mode exhibit higher brightness, but lower contrast compared with those exported using the “current frame” mode.

The data obtained for the two image quality parameters may directly affect the interpretation of radiographic images. Previous investigations examined the influence of brightness and contrast on radiographs acquired with conventional wall-mounted X-ray units for different diagnostic tasks, including the detection of root resorptions, caries lesions, and periapical lesions.16,17,18 Overall, these studies reported that variations in brightness and contrast did not significantly alter diagnostic accuracy for these conditions. However, clinicians reported to prefer radiographs with lower brightness and higher contrast for the assessment of proximal caries lesions.16 In light of these observations, it may be hypothesized that radiographs exported as “current frame” are more suitable for diagnosis than those exported as “current image”

Based on reduced gray-level variability, radiographs exported using the “current image” mode demonstrated lower noise and greater uniformity than those exported as “current frame”. These features are generally regarded as advantageous from a technical standpoint. However, greater image homogeneity does not inherently imply superior diagnostic performance. Oliveira-Santos et al.19 (2021) reported that radiographs with increased noise resulting from image enhancement filters did not compromise the diagnosis of internal or external root resorptions. Although contrast enhancement through filtering is often accompanied by increased noise, such processing may still facilitate the differentiation of adjacent anatomical structures.19

Similarly, previous authors have shown that radiographs acquired with handheld X-ray devices present lower uniformity than those obtained with wall-mounted units, yet this difference did not affect the detection of caries lesions.20,21 Therefore, lower noise and higher uniformity associated with the “current image” export mode should be interpreted cautiously, as improvements in these technical parameters do not necessarily correspond to clinically relevant gains in diagnostic performance.

Hypothetically increasing tube voltage would attenuate the differences between the two export modes, resulting in more comparable image characteristics between radiographs exported as “current frame” and “current image”. However, the present results indicate that even a 10 kVp increase was insufficient to eliminate the differences between the export modes. Nevertheless, a clear pattern emerged: increasing kVp exerted a greater influence on radiographs exported as “current image” (with post-processing adjustments) than on those exported as “current frame” (without post-processing adjustments). Specifically, in the “current image” mode, brightness values decreased markedly while the percentage of radiographic contrast increased. Such changes in gray-level values were not observed in radiographs exported as “current frame”.

In addition to the acrylic block, the present study employed a DDQA phantom. Unlike ex vivo models based on anatomical structures, such as mandibles and teeth, the use of standardized phantoms allows the acquisition of reproducible and controlled data, resulting in more precise metrics. No differences in spatial resolution were detected between the two export modes when using the DDQA phantom. In both conditions, spatial resolution was 9 lp/mm at 60 kVp and increased to 10 lp/mm at 70 kVp. This finding is consistent with the physics of X-ray production, as higher tube voltage generates a beam with greater mean photon energy, the CMOS sensor, which may improve the differentiation of closely adjacent structures in resulting images.14

Clark at al.22 (2017) concluded that moderate image sharpening can improve spatial resolution, whereas excessive sharpening does not yield further gains. Based on these findings, it could be expected that different export modes might influence spatial resolution. However, such an effect was not observed. Notably, the spatial resolution values obtained with the s-IOT system and the CMOS sensor were higher than those typically reported for photostimulable phosphor plates (PSP) receptors, for which resolution has been shown to range approximately from 5 to 8 lp/mm in earlier studies.14,23 This finding is expected, once PSP receptors generally have lower spatial resolution compared to other intraoral imaging systems.24 High spatial resolution is a critical imaging characteristic, as it has been associated with improved diagnostic accuracy for conditions such as external root resorption and root fracture.25,26

The contrast resolution results obtained from the row of wells with identical depth but varying diameters indicate that the “current frame” export mode provided more consistent visualization of low-contrast, small-diameter structures, as all six wells were identifiable at both 60 and 70 kVp. In contrast, when radiographs were exported as “current image”, the smallest-diameter well could not be visualized at 60 kVp, whereas all six wells were observed at 70 kVp. This finding suggests that at 60 kVp, the reduced photon energy results in lower signal levels at the CMOS sensor, which, when combined with the “current image” mode, may diminish the contrast resolution.

The findings derived from the row of wells with identical diameters and varying depths further emphasize the impact of both tube voltage and export mode on contrast resolution. When the “current image” export mode was used, no wells were discernible at 60 kVp, whereas two wells became visible at 70 kVp. In contrast, the “current frame” mode enabled the identification of two wells at 60 kVp and three wells at 70 kVp. From a clinical perspective, these observations suggest that, when using the s-IOT system, higher tube voltage settings produce radiographs with greater reliability for image interpretation.

Objective studies such as the present one provide foundational evidence that helps guide subsequent research, particularly in the early stages of understanding the performance of emerging imaging systems. The current study serves as an initial step toward characterizing the behavior of the s-IOT system when operated as a conventional X-ray device. However, the authors emphasize that clinical and ex vivo investigations are equally important, as they are necessary to determine how these objective findings translate into real diagnostic scenarios involving patients. Therefore, future studies should investigate the combined influence of tube voltage and post-processing adjustments on diagnostic tasks that are highly dependent on adequate radiographic contrast (e.g. caries lesions and root resorptions). Such studies are particularly pertinent considering previous research reporting that increasing tube voltage from 63 to 70 kVp did not adversely affect the assessment of alveolar bone levels in cases of periodontal bone loss, suggesting that variations in exposure parameters may impact on specific diagnostic tasks in different ways.27 Until such evidence becomes available, clinicians are encouraged to acquire radiographs using both available post-processing options. Because these adjustments remain under the clinician’s control (through selection of either “current frame” or “current image”) using both export modes allows direct comparison of the objective differences identified in this study under clinical conditions. This approach may also enhance radiographic interpretation by incorporating the complementary information provided by each image.

The conceptual and mathematical foundations of tomosynthesis were first established in 1935.1 Since then, the technique has been applied in several medical fields, including digital breast and chest tomosynthesis, tomosynthesis-guided radiation therapy, and stationary computed tomography for security screening.1 More recently, tomosynthesis has been introduced into oral and maxillofacial radiology, which have been shown to provide high-resolution in the buccal-lingual direction information of dental structures with radiation doses comparable to those of conventional intraoral radiography.5 Within this context, the present study provides novel evidence on the use of the s-IOT system in two-dimensional mode, offering guidance for the selection of post-processing adjustments and tube voltage settings.

Based on these findings, conventional two-dimensional intraoral radiographs acquired with the carbon nanotube-based s-IOT system without post-processing adjustments (exported as “current frame”) demonstrated lower brightness and higher contrast, but also greater noise and reduced uniformity compared with post-processed radiographs (exported as “current image”). Spatial resolution did not differ between export modes; however, it was higher at 70 kVp. Additionally, variations in tube voltage appeared to have a greater influence on radiographs exported as “current image”. Clinicians should consider the specific image characteristics associated with post-processing adjustments to select the most appropriate option for the diagnostic task assessed.
