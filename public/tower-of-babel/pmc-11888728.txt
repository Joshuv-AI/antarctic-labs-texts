Administration of Antibiotics for Root Canal Treatment – A Questionnaire Based Survey Amongst Dentists
Authors: Nagarajan Geethapriya, Alagarsamy Venkatesh, Shekar Shobana, Gold Pearlin Mary, S Sarath Sarathy, Ramu Shobhana
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:This study aims to assess the use of antibiotics among dentists in performing endodontic procedures.Methodology:A cross-sectional survey was conducted using a pretested, structured, and validated questionnaire approved by the institutional ethics committee. It included questions on demographics, choice, frequency, and duration of antibiotics use in endodontic practice. The survey link was distributed electronically. Results were tabulated and analyzed using descriptive statistics.Results:Only 6.5% of them reported that root canal treatment does not require an antibiotic regime. Clinical years of experience do not have any significant impact on antibiotic prescribing practices among dentists.Conclusion:The survey reveals issues with the inadvertent use of antibiotics, including their types and frequency, and highlights gaps in theoretical knowledge and antibiotic-prescribing practices among general dentists.

INTRODUCTION

Endodontic infections, caused by microbial invasion of the dental pulp, pose significant challenges in dental practice. Although irrigants and intracanal medicaments can eliminate microorganisms from the root canal,[1] systemic antibiotics are often used to prevent complications. However, inappropriate use can lead to adverse effects, antimicrobial resistance, and unnecessary healthcare risks. Dentists prescribe about 10% of all antibiotics, contributing to global resistance issues.[2] Antibiotics are ineffective for pain or swelling from symptomatic apical pathosis unless systemic involvement is present and are recommended only for febrile or immunocompromised patients. Recent AAE guidelines stress that misuse leads to the development of multidrug-resistant bacteria.[3] This study examines current practices and perceptions regarding antibiotic use in endodontics through a survey of dental practitioners.

METHODOLOGY

A cross-sectional survey, approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee, was conducted among general dental practitioners, postgraduates, academicians, and specialists. Using a validated, pretested questionnaire, the survey explored demographics, antibiotic use in endodontics, preferred antibiotics, and guideline awareness. The questionnaire was assessed for content, construct, and face, achieving a content validity score of 0.82. The sample size of 172 was calculated with a 95% confidence interval and a 5% margin of error. Out of 500 dentists invited, 323 responded, resulting in an 86% response rate. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and Chi-square tests.

RESULTS

Results showed that 75.5% of respondents used textbooks and published literature for antibiotic-related information, 52.6% relied on CDE programs, and 46.4% used the internet. While 76.5% cited overuse, unnecessary use, and inadequate dosages as causes of antibiotic resistance, only 34.7% acknowledged that antibiotic use in root canal treatments contributes to resistance. Regarding medication practices, 57% prescribed both antibiotics and analgesics, 15.5% prescribed only analgesics, and 13.3% prescribed only antibiotics for root canal treatment. DISCUSSION

The discussion interprets survey results within the context of existing literature, highlighting discrepancies between guidelines and clinical practices regarding antibiotic use in endodontics. Antibiotics aim to eliminate bacteria, manage flare-ups, and prevent systemic infections. Decisions on prescribing antibiotics should be based on clinical assessments and diagnostic findings, such as periapical radiolucencies or systemic involvement.

The AAE guidelines recommend selective antibiotic use for cases with systemic involvement (e.g., swelling, fever) or high risk of infection risk.[3] Common antibiotics include penicillin derivatives, cephalosporins, and metronidazole, selected based on microbial sensitivity testing.[2] Inappropriate use, such as overuse and misuse, contributes to antibiotic resistance.[45] Our study found that 74.6% of respondents prescribed antibiotics for localized dentoalveolar abscesses and 44.6% for irreversible pulpitis with pain, where alternative treatments could be effective. Overuse and inadequate dosages can damage the host response and lead to resistance.[6] Incomplete courses and prophylactic use prior to dental procedures also contribute to resistance, affecting future treatments.

Antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) is crucial for raising awareness of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and optimizing antimicrobial use. AMS aims to improve antibiotic use, promote better prescribing practices, and build healthcare professionals’ capacity for rational antibiotic use.[7] In endodontics, infections often resolve quickly if the cause is addressed. Long-term systemic antibiotic use can disrupt natural flora and lead to resistant strains that require higher doses for eradication. Systemic antibiotics may not effectively reach the infection site in necrotic pulp or infected root-filled teeth due to limited blood supply.[8] They are also ineffective in reducing pain or swelling from symptomatic apical periodontitis.[9] The lack of collateral circulation in dental pulp creates a unique environment for bacterial survival.[10]

Our study found that 47.4% of practitioners believed antibiotics were necessary for root canal treatment, and 56.3% thought they expedited healing and prevented complications, despite evidence suggesting otherwise. Additionally, 55.4% felt oral antibiotics complemented intracanal antibiotics in reducing symptoms. Topical antibiotics, such as minocycline, ciprofloxacin, and metronidazole have gained popularity due to the limitations of systemic antibiotics.[1112] Intracanal use of clindamycin is less effective than conventional calcium hydroxide.[13] Our study showed 18.8% of practitioners used topical antibacterial gels or pastes.

In conclusion, while antibiotics are valuable for managing complex endodontic infections and preventing systemic issues, their use should be evidence-based and aligned with AMS principles. This survey identifies gaps in current practices and perceptions, highlighting areas for improvement in antibiotic prescribing practices and in combating antimicrobial resistance.
