MindForge: Empowering Embodied Agents with Theory of Mind for Lifelong Cultural Learning
Mircea Lică *, Ojas Shirekar *, Baptiste Colle, Chirag Raman

Abstract
Embodied agents powered by large language models (LLMs), such as Voyager, promise open-ended competence in worlds such as Minecraft. However, when powered by open-weight LLMs they still falter on elementary tasks after domain-specific fine-tuning. We propose MindForge , a generative-agent framework for cultural lifelong learning through explicit perspective taking. We introduce three key innovations: (1) a structured theory of mind representation linking percepts, beliefs, desires, and actions; (2) natural inter-agent communication; and (3) a multi-component memory system. Following the cultural learning framework, we test MindForge in both instructive and collaborative settings within Minecraft. In an instructive setting with GPT-4, MindForge agents powered by open-weight LLMs significantly outperform their Voyager counterparts in basic tasks yielding 3 × 3\times more tech-tree milestones and collecting 2.3 × 2.3\times more unique items than the Voyager baseline. Furthermore, in fully collaborative settings, we find that the performance of two underachieving agents improves with more communication rounds, echoing the Condorcet Jury Theorem. MindForge agents demonstrate sophisticated behaviors, including expert-novice knowledge transfer, collaborative problem solving, and adaptation to out-of-distribution tasks through accumulated cultural experiences. The code can be found at https://github.com/tapri-lab/mindforge .

Early attempts to equip machines with mental-state reasoning drew on deep learning

LLM-based Multi-Agent Systems

LLMs have demonstrated overwhelming success in performing a variety of single-agent tasks ranging from question answering and text generation to code synthesis and complex reasoning in embodied settings
[

,
39
,
40

. This success has started a growing interest in developing LLM-based multi-agent systems

,

[

[

, designed for collaborative reasoning, find that current LLM agents are not suited for multi-agent collaboration in long-horizon embodied scenarios, a gap that

Single-agent Frameworks

Recent research on long-horizon tasks in single-agent systems differs architecturally from

’s unified collaborative learning framework. For example, ExpeL
[
45
]
and CLIN
[

3
Background

Voyager

Voyager agents consist of three main components: (1) an automatic curriculum generator, (2) a skill library, and (3) an iterative prompting mechanism. The automatic curriculum proposes progressively complex tasks, driving continuous learning. The skill library stores and retrieves learned behaviors, facilitating knowledge accumulation and transfer. The iterative prompting mechanism generates and refines executable code based on environmental feedback, allowing for adaptive behavior. Voyager demonstrated significant capabilities in item discovery, technology progression, and skill generalization. This system established a new benchmark in open-world AI agents, showcasing the potential for lifelong learning and adaptation in complex, dynamic environments. The Voyager framework serves as a base for our lifelong
collaborative
learning agents within Minecraft.

BigToM Causal Template

The BigToM causal template
[
23
]
, provides a structured framework for representing Theory of Mind (ToM) scenarios as causal graphs. This template comprises key components including context, desire, percept, belief, causal event, and resulting actions. By representing ToM scenarios in this causal structure, the template allows for systematic manipulation of variables such as an agent’s perceptions, beliefs, and actions in response to environmental changes. This approach enables the creation of diverse, well-controlled ToM scenarios, capturing the complex causal relationships between an agent’s mental states and their behavior in social situations. The causal graph representation facilitates a more nuanced and systematic exploration of ToM reasoning, allowing for the examination of various inference patterns such as forward belief, forward action, and backward belief inferences in social cognitive tasks.

4.1
Structured Representation of Own Mental States: Beliefs, Desires, and Intentions

For
MindForge
agents to develop effective theory of mind capabilities, they must first possess structured representations of their own mental states.
This explicit modeling of the self is crucial for
recursive social reasoning
: enabling agents not only to reason about their own beliefs, goals, perceptions, and how these might lead to their actions, but also to form a similar mental model for other agents.
This mirrors findings in developmental psychology, where children first develop an awareness of their own thoughts, feelings, and goals before being able to infer the mental states of others
[
52
,
53
]
. This is further supported by neuroscientific research that has also identified distinct neural mechanisms for self-cognition and social cognition
[
54
,
55
]
.

We draw on the classical Belief-Desire-Intention (BDI) framework
[
56
]
and operationalize it using the BigToM causal template (
Section

3
)
[
23
]
. In
MindForge
,
beliefs
represent the agent’s current understanding of the world inferred from sensory perception, including interactions with other agents.
Desires
encode the agent’s goals, both generated by an automatic curriculum and those arising from collaborative interactions.
Intentions
correspond to concrete executable actions, implemented as generated code—and so, consistent with BigToM
[
23
]
, we treat “intentions” and “actions” interchangeably in implementation. However, we note that this equivalence does not always hold conceptually: intentions refer to internal commitments or plans, while actions are observable behaviors that may diverge from intentions. For a deeper treatment of this distinction, see
[
57
]
.

Belief Structure.

We categorize a
MindForge
agent’s beliefs into four key categories, illustrated in
Figure

3
: i.
perception-related beliefs
, formed from direct sensory input via the Minecraft API; ii.
task-related beliefs
, inferred from the agent’s reflection on its upcoming objectives; iii.
interaction-related beliefs
, derived from chat messages during task execution; and iv.
partner-related beliefs
, which track others’ mental states. Each belief type is generated via a separate LLM call, with prompts conditioned on current percepts, memory retrievals, and conversation history (see
Appendix

F
). This modular belief formation feeds into the causal ToM template
Figure

2(a)
, which governs downstream action generation. Structured self-modeling thus serves as the foundation for cultural learning and perspective-taking.

4.2
Theory of Mind: Modeling Partner Perspectives

Building on the structured representation of their own mental states,
MindForge
extend this modeling to others via Theory of Mind—the capacity to attribute beliefs, desires, and intentions to others.
Each
MindForge
agent achieves this by maintaining a partner-specific BigToM graph within its own beliefs, allowing it to reason about the internal state of each conversational partner (see
Figure

3
). These representations guide how an agent interprets and responds to incoming communication.

During collaboration, an agent uses its internal model of its partner to simulate what the partner knows, believes, or needs. The underlying LLM receives this partner model along with the conversation history to generate responses incorporating the collaborator’s point of view. As interaction proceeds, the partner beliefs are continuously updated using new received through the communication channel (
Figure

6
).
This dynamic modeling of partner mental states endows
MindForge
agents with one of the most crucial abilities within the cultural learning framework: reasoning about partner perspectives
[
14
,
15
]
. For instance, an expert agent can diagnose a novice’s misconception and tailor its feedback accordingly, while a novice can ask more targeted questions with fewer digressions by simulating what the expert knows. See
Section

B.1
for more details on how perspective-taking is integrated into the communication module, and in-game examples.

4.3
Communication Medium

To take advantage of representations of each other’s mental models and collaborate effectively, agents need to be able to communicate with each other. We operationalize communication in natural language, integrating the
MindForge
framework’s communication system with Minecraft’s native in-game chat functionality.
Specifically, once a
MindForge
agent fails to complete a task, it initiates a communication round with its partner where it leverages its BigToM representation to ask for help. This system enables agents to exchange textual information and, crucially, to integrate feedback from collaborators directly into their belief systems. Concretely, we utilize the Mineflayer API
[
58
]
to extract and process chat data from the Minecraft environment. By leveraging the game’s existing chat system, the framework ensures that collaborative interactions occur in a context-appropriate manner, closely mirroring human-to-human communication within Minecraft.

To handle the resulting conversation, we use a dedicated LLM instance within the
MindForge
agent which is tasked with synthesizing and interpreting the conversation into the structured BigTOM BDI template, generating both interaction beliefs and partner beliefs (see prompts in
Appendix

F
). This instance does not directly influence any other parts of the agent.

4.4
Memory Subsystems

A
MindForge
agent’s memory consists of three subsystems drawing on research about human memory: (i)
episodic
, (ii)
semantic
, and (iii)
procedural
memory.

Episodic memory
deals with pertinent information about the agent’s past attempts at a task and social interactions with other agents.
Recollections of past episodes serve as context for humans to interpret others’ behaviors, leading to divergent future actions in the same situation in response to different recollections
[
59
]
.
Humans also tend to summarize information from their social interactions, but at the same time are capable of preferentially accessing episodes when the credibility of certain summaries is low due to the lack of evidence
[
60
]
. Moreover, findings also suggest that there are processes in the brain for updating these summaries as new episodes transpire
[
61
,
60
]
. Inspired by complementary learning systems theory, Larimar
[
62
]
enhances LLMs with a distributed episodic memory module, enabling dynamic knowledge updates without any gradient-based learning. Furthermore, Optimus-1 integrates past experiences in a multimodal memory storage mechanism and abstracted experience pool used for summarization of past experiences. For a
MindForge
agent, the role of episodic memory is twofold. Firstly, it stores details about its partners revealed during previous conversations that can be exploited for perspective-taking in future communication. Secondly, we store past actions that led to failure and retrieve them based on their similarity with the task at hand. Thus, a
MindForge
agent can track both the progress of its partner and learn from past failures.

Semantic memory
constitutes an individual’s store of knowledge about the world. The content of semantic memory is abstracted from the experience and is therefore said to hold summarized generalizations, likely without references to specific experiences
[
63
,
60
,
61
]
. For a
MindForge
agent, the semantic memory stores information related to the task and environment. It stores information relevant to completing a task; for instance, the prerequisites for being able to mine dirt in the game world. Such a piece of information is not necessarily tied to one specific episode. Rather, it is generally useful information about the world and can be distilled from the episodic memory.

Finally,
procedural memory
stores the skills that the agent learns and is analogous to the skill library present in the original Voyager framework
[
11
]
. The skills stored in the procedural memory are reusable pieces of code that the agent can use for solving problems, allowing the agent to take on more complex tasks while avoiding re-learning elements previously used successfully.
As illustrated in
Figure

2(a)
, episodic and semantic memory stores influence the agent’s beliefs, while procedural memory provides the routines that inform the agent’s next action. Additional information about the memory subsystems can be found in
Section

B.2
, where we provide implementation details, and an ablation for the episodic memory component, where we show that omitting it from
MindForge
leads to a drop in success rate.

5.3
Introducing Collaboration:
MindForge
Agents

Having shown that PEFT-based imitation learning fails to improve agent capabilities, we evaluate our proposed
MindForge
framework. We adopt an instructive learning approach within the cultural learning framework
[
15
]
, where an expert agent guides a novice through communication and perspective-taking. This multi-agent collaboration effectively bridges the performance gap between open-weight LLMs and GPT-4, addressing the failure cases identified in
Figure

4

Feasibility: Human (Expert) Collaboration Improves Performance

To establish feasibility, we first conduct a wizard-of-oz study where a human expert interacts with an open-weight LLM-powered
MindForge
agent through the in-game chat. As shown in
Figure

5
, human expert collaboration achieves on average a
+57.85\%
increase in task completion rate for dirt collection over the non-interactive baselines. This demonstrates both the viability and flexibility of our framework in enabling productive human-agent interactions for collaborative tasks.

GPT-4 (Expert) Collaboration Maintains Performance Benefits

We replace the human expert with a GPT-4-powered
MindForge
agent that collaborates with an open-weight LLM agent. The expert agent infers its partner’s failure cases through perspective-taking based on natural language communication in the game chat. Unlike human collaboration, agents cannot directly access each other’s internal beliefs and must rely on Theory of Mind through communication to model their counterpart’s mental state. This perspective-taking enables the expert to accurately diagnose and address the weaker agent’s misconceptions. Notably, this setup generalizes the classic notion of knowledge distillation beyond logits or model weights: here, distillation unfolds through social interaction, where the expert agent transmits structural knowledge, corrects false beliefs, and shapes the student’s internal representations in real time. As shown in
Figure

5

16.1\%
increase in task completion rates for dirt collection and a
13\%

5.4
MindForge
Lifelong Learning

We evaluate
MindForge
’s lifelong learning capabilities against both GPT-4 and open-weight Voyager
[
11
]
variants. The progression through increasingly complex technological achievements (from wooden to diamond tools) provides a rigorous test-bed for assessing lifelong learning and collaboration abilities of
MindForge
. We follow the methodology from Voyager and evaluate performance on three key milestones: (1)
wooden tools
, (2)
stone tools
and (3)
iron tools
.

In line with the findings in
Table

1
,
Table

3
highlights the performance gap between GPT-4 powered Voyager and its open-weight variants, with only Llama 3.1-70B
[
71
]
reaching the
wooden tools
milestone. On the other hand,
MindForge
shows substantial improvement across the board both in terms of achieved milestones and efficiency (number of iterations). Notably, Llama 3.1-70B
[
71
]
powered
MindForge
achieves two additional tech-tree milestones, nearing the performance with the original GPT-4 powered Voyager
[
11
]
. We observe a steady increase in efficiency (number of iterations) as the size of the LLM increases: Llama 3.1-70B
[
71
]
powered
MindForge
is
1.62\times
more efficient than its Llama 3.1-8B counterpart for reaching the
wooden tools
milestone, suggesting that larger models can better utilize the collaborative learning framework. Furthermore, to understand the impact of collaboration in a mixed-expertise collaborative setting, we experiment with a non-collaborative
MindForge
ablation. Notably,
MindForge
agents without collaboration require on average
15
more prompting iterations to reach the
wooden tools
milestone and
34
iterations to reach the
stone tools
milestone. Thus, the collaborative framework not only enhances task completion rates but also enables more efficient acquisition of complex skills. We further test the generalization capabilities of
MindForge
agents in
Section

A.2
where we show how even after collaboration finishes, the agents can reuse the knowledge in out-of-distribution tasks.

5.5
MindForge
Collaborative Learning Setting

While prior sections focused on instructive learning from stronger peers, we now consider a more challenging setup: collaboration between two
MindForge
agents of
matched capabilities
, each powered by the same type of open-weight LLM. This setting reflects mutual cultural learning without access to an expert where the agents must bootstrap improvements solely through interaction, thereby mimicking certain qualities of emergence.
Critically, we evaluate not individual trajectories, but instead focus on
population-level performance
: after
N
rounds of communication, what fraction of agent pairs from the total pool succeed in completing the task? As shown in
Figure

5
, when agents begin from unprimed states, collaboration often leads to stagnation or even degradation which mirrors the
“blind leading the blind”
dynamic, where flawed mental models are mutually reinforced.

However, even minimal prior expert instruction—such as a single round of GPT-4–guided collaboration—can prime agents for more effective peer interaction, even if they still fail when acting alone. As shown in
Figure

7
, we pair Mixtral-8x7B agents that each received
N
communication rounds of GPT-4 guided trial before interacting. We observe that the fraction of successful agents steadily increases with additional communication rounds between themselves (Mixtral agents). From a
62\%
baseline population succeeding, we reach
79\%
of the population succeeding after seven turns, showing a
17\%
increment. This result reflects a form of test-time compute scaling, where richer interaction, not larger models, drives the emergent improvements across the group.

This also aligns with the Condorcet Jury Theorem
[
73
]
, which predicts that collective decisions improve when individuals exceed a baseline level of competence. Here, we see a distributed variant of this principle in action: once agents cross a minimal competence threshold, the communication induced bootstrapping can yield reliable gains at the population level.

6
Conclusion

The
MindForge
framework demonstrates that cultural learning can overcome the limitations of open-weight LLMs in embodied environments. Integrating theory of mind, natural communication, and structured memory systems enables
MindForge
agents to learn from both mixed and matched expertise peers and overcome fundamental failure modes in perception and code generation. Our experiments show that collaboration significantly improves performance on lifelong learning scenarios, with agents collecting
2.3\times
more unique items and achieving
3\times
more tech-tree milestones when paired with expert agents. Crucially, in the collaborative setting, we show that the fraction of successful agents scales as a function of communication rounds; revealing emergent gains through perspective taking and interaction. These results suggest that social learning could reduce embodied AI’s dependence on large proprietary models, opening new paths for democratized agent development aligned with Green AI principles
[
74
]
.

Appendix C
Implementation details

C.1
Perspective Taking

Perspective-taking represents a central part of our framework since it allows
MindForge
agents to take advantage of the internal causal structure of other agents in order to aid more efficient and grounded communication. In practice, perspective-taking corresponds to an LLM call where the prompt contains instructions and relevant information required to understand the internal state of other
MindForge
agents during communication.
Figure

8
showcases how perspective-taking integrates with the overall communication mechanism.

Figure 8
:
Flow of
MindForge
communication components used to generate a natural language response. The agents’ internal process consists of two stages: (i) internal representation update and (ii) perspective-taking. Leveraging both processes the agent can then generate a natural language response and send it through the Minecraft chat.

Once the agent receives a message in the Minecraft chat, a first LLM call is executed where the agent updates the stateful internal representation about the agent that sent the message. This update process is shown in
Figure

6
. Subsequently, the updated internal representation is used as context together with the rest of the conversation to take perspective of the situation of the other
MindForge
agent. Similar to the first stage, perspective-taking is implemented as a LLM call, with the following prompt:

Prompt for perspective-taking

You are a Minecraft agent named {{name}} and you are having a conversation with another agent named {{other_name}}.

Based on the current conversation and your knowledge about the other agent, {{other_name}}, take the other agent’s perspective to assess and describe your current understanding, knowledge state, and likely needs from {{other_name}}’s perspective.

Here is the current conversation between you and {{other_name}}:{conversation}

Here is your mental model of {{other_name}}: {world_model}

C.2
Episodic Memory

We implement episodic memory as a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) using LangChain
[
67
]
. Specifically, we embed all the episodes where the agent failed to complete the task using
text-embedding-ada-002
. An episode consists of the context used to generate the action, the code itself and the corresponding critic message. Subsequently, when the agent generates a new action, the RAG is queried to retrieve the
k=5
most relevant failure episodes with respect to the task at hand. Lastly, to ensure we do not populate the context window with too many tokens, we generate a summary (using an LLM) of the retrieved episodes and feed this summary in the context used to generate the action. Below you can find the corresponding prompts for generating the episodic summary:

System message

You are a helpful assistant tasked with summarizing past experience episodes and pointing out the causes of failure. Create a concise summary.

Prompt message

Please summarize these episodes and why they failed:{combined_episodes}

C.3
Procedural Memory

The procedural memory has an identical design and functionality with the skill library introduced in Voyager
[
11
]
. Once a task gets successfully completed, we store the corresponding piece of code such that we can later reference it in adjacent tasks. This becomes increasingly important in tasks that require multiple steps, like crafting a pickaxe: the agent needs to first create a crafting table, create wooden sticks and only then attempt to craft the pickaxe. When we run the
MindForge
framework in an open-ended setting, the procedural memory allows the agent to continuously evolve and create an increasingly large collection of reusable skills.

C.4
Semantic Memory

As described in
Section

4.4
, semantic memory stores certain beliefs the agent has about the environment, coming from experience with the environment and the other
MindForge
agents. Whenever
MindForge
attempts a new task, it creates a certain belief about how the task should be solved. For example, the agent might believe it requires tools to mine a block of dirt. This belief is then added to the context of the LLM when the environment action is generated. As highlighted in
Section

1
, one of the primary failure cases of Voyager are the false beliefs that come from this LLM generation. However, compared to Voyager
[
11
]
, we leverage the communication with other
MindForge
agents such that these beliefs get updated and refined. Below we showcase an example of false belief correction when trying to collect a block of wood:

Initial belief inside the semantic memory

{"How to mine 1 wood log in Minecraft?": "Answer: To mine 1 wood log in Minecraft, you need to use an axe."}

Updated belief after communication

{"How to mine 1 wood log in Minecraft?": "Answer: To mine 1 wood log in Minecraft, you need to punch a tree with your bare hands."}

In addition to task-related memory, the
MindForge
agent maintains a stateful set of beliefs about the other agents in the environment that are constantly updated across episodes. An example of this is presented in
Figure

6
, where the agent updates its representation based on the contents of the conversation. Thus, keeping track of various information and facts about other
MindForge
agents acts as a semantic memory retrieval when agents communicate.
