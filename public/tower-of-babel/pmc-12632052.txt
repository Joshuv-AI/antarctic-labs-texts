Total bilirubin as a mortality predictor in non-elderly hemodialysis patients: a multicenter retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yuyang Chen, Haowen Zhang, Hongyan Liu, Jialong Zhang, Xuan Dai, Wenjing Dong, Qingqing Liu, Liping Zhu, Yefan Zhang, Xue Zhou, Li Cheng, Xiaoyan Lu, Haixia Yang, Qian Liu, Jie Song, Qian Li, Jiarui Li, Haihua Su, Xinchao Tao, Aijie Shi, Pei Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe association between serum total bilirubin (TBIL) levels and mortality in maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients, especially when stratified by age, has not been clearly established.MethodsWe performed a multicenter retrospective analysis of 409 MHD patients (40 deaths, 9.8%) over a median follow-up of 31.3 months (interquartile range (IQR), 28.9–31.8). Cox regression models evaluated TBIL’s predictive role for mortality. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve-derived cutoff (8.79 µmol/L) stratified patients into high (H, TBIL > 8.79 µmol/L, n = 236) and low (L, TBIL ≤ 8.79 µmol/L, n = 173) groups. Multivariable Cox models adjusted for demographics, comorbidities (Model 2), and laboratory markers (Model 3). Additionally, to mitigate overfitting and assess the linear relationship, a LASSO-penalized Cox model was employed, treating TBIL as a continuous variable. Kaplan-Meier analysis compared survival rates, with age subgroup comparisons.ResultsSignificant differences emerged between H and L groups in hypertension history, platelet count, total protein, blood urea nitrogen, triglycerides, and total cholesterol (all P < 0.05). Adjusted models revealed elevated mortality risk in high TBIL patients (HR = 6.92, 95%CI 3.10–15.43, P < 0.001). Consistently, in the continuous analysis, each 1 µmol/L increase in TBIL was associated with a 26% increased mortality risk (HR = 1.26, 95%CI 1.16–1.38, P < 0.001). Age-stratified analysis revealed substantially increased mortality risk in non-elderly high-TBIL patients (HR = 12.82, 95%CI 3.76–43.76, P < 0.001), but no significant association in elderly patients (HR = 2.00, 95%CI 0.83–4.81, P = 0.12).ConclusionTBIL independently predicts all-cause mortality in MHD patients, with exploratory evidence suggesting potentially stronger association in non-elderly patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04621-2.

Introduction

In recent years, global shifts in economic, environmental, and dietary factors have driven a steady rise in the incidence of chronic kidney disease (CKD), now approaching 10% worldwide. [1] CKD progresses over time, causing irreversible damage to renal units through mechanisms such as renal interstitial fibrosis and glomerulosclerosis, ultimately leading to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [1]. For ESRD patients, maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) remains the primary renal replacement therapy [2]. In China, the MHD patient population has surpassed 700,000 and continues to grow rapidly. While advancements in dialysis technology have significantly extended survival rates and improved quality of life for MHD patients, their life expectancy remains approximately 70% shorter than that of the general population [3]. Studies indicate that mortality among dialysis patients is 10 to 100 times higher than in the general population [4]. This elevated risk is influenced by multiple factors, including cardiac complications, malnutrition, inflammation, and psychological issues. [5–8].

Early identification of mortality risk factors in MHD patients, along with the development of targeted interventions, may help reduce mortality and improve long-term outcomes. Among various potential biomarkers, bilirubin—a product of heme metabolism existing in serum in conjugated and unconjugated forms—has garnered attention due to its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties under physiological conditions [9]. A decade-long cohort study recently highlighted a strong association between serum bilirubin levels and CKD progression to ESRD [10]. Furthermore, a Mendelian randomization study genetically validated bilirubin’s protective role in kidney function after accounting for reverse causality and confounding factors [11]. However, studies investigating the relationship between bilirubin and prognosis in ESRD patients undergoing renal replacement therapy have produced conflicting results. For example, Su et al. reported that elevated serum bilirubin levels (0.7–1.2 mg/dL) increased mortality risk in MHD patients (HR = 1.44), [12] whereas another study suggested higher circulating bilirubin levels improve cardiovascular outcomes in ESRD patients [13].

Despite existing research on serum bilirubin and mortality risk in MHD patients, conclusions remain inconclusive due to regional and ethnic variability. Furthermore, large-scale studies focusing on northern Chinese MHD populations are lacking. Of particular interest is the elderly dialysis subgroup, where comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension may disrupt bilirubin metabolism, potentially explaining inconsistent findings in prior studies [14]. This study aims to address these gaps through a multicenter cohort analysis in Tianjin, China, exploring the relationship between serum bilirubin and prognosis in MHD patients, with emphasis on age-stratified differences.

Methods

Study population

The study population consisted of patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis at eight dialysis centers in Tianjin. The enrollment period was from July 1, 2022, to June 30, 2023, and follow-up ended on July 1, 2025. The selection of study participants strictly followed inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University Chu Hsien-I Memorial Hospital and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Inclusion criteria: (1) MHD patients with a dialysis duration of ≥ 3 months; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) no psychiatric disorders. Exclusion criteria: (1) patients undergoing both hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis; (2) patients with acute infectious diseases, acute cardiovascular or cerebrovascular events, gastrointestinal bleeding, trauma, or surgery within the past 3 months; (3) patients with malignancies or hematologic diseases; (4) abnormal serum total bilirubin levels (TBIL < 1.71 µmol/L or TBIL > 20.5 µmol/L); (5) alanine aminotransferase (ALT) > 40 U/L or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) > 40 U/L; (6) patients with a history of liver disease.

Data collection

Data for all study participants were obtained from the hemodialysis case information registration system and online questionnaires. The former included age, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), primary disease (diabetic nephropathy, chronic glomerulonephritis, hypertensive nephropathy, etc.), vascular access type (arteriovenous fistula/graft or central venous catheter), and duration of dialysis. The latter included cardiovascular disease history, smoking history, alcohol consumption, education level, and marital status. Laboratory indicators were extracted from the information system, including white blood cell count(WBC), hemoglobin(Hb), platelet count(PLT), total protein(TP), serum albumin(ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase(AST), TBIL, pre-dialysis blood urea nitrogen(BUN), serum creatinine(Cr), uric acid(UA), triglycerides(TG), total cholesterol(TCHO), fasting blood glucose(GLU), serum potassium(K), calcium(Ca), phosphorus(P), and intact parathyroid hormone(PTH). All laboratory parameters were determined from fasting, pre-dialysis blood samples and analyzed by the standardized clinical laboratories at each participating center. Hemolyzed samples were identified and excluded from all subsequent analyses.

Follow-up and outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was all-cause mortality, including cardiovascular-related deaths and other specific causes of death. During follow-up, the time and cause of death were recorded in detail. For participants who did not experience an outcome event, the endpoint time was July 1, 2025. The follow-up duration of dialysis was the time interval between enrollment and the end of follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described as mean (± standard deviation) and compared using t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were described as counts (percentages) and compared using chi-square tests. Cox proportional hazards models were constructed to assess the impact of TBIL on all-cause mortality in MHD patients. Model 1 adjusted for age and sex, Model 2 further adjusted for diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and duration of dialysis, and Model 3 further adjusted for hemoglobin, white blood cell count, serum albumin, blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, uric acid, calcium, phosphorus, platelet count, triglycerides, total cholesterol and parathyroid hormone. To address potential overfitting and to assess the dose-response relationship, we performed a supplementary analysis using LASSO-penalized Cox regression. This approach automatically selected the most predictive variables from 34 clinical candidates. The linear association between TBIL (as a continuous variable) and mortality was further evaluated using restricted cubic splines. ROC curve analysis was used to exploratorily determine the optimal cutoff value for TBIL, and patients were divided into high and low bilirubin groups based on this value. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to analyze survival differences between the two groups. Further age stratification was performed to compare survival differences between high and low bilirubin groups in elderly and non-elderly populations. Model discrimination was assessed using the concordance index (C-index). The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.4.2 software. A two-sided test was used, with a significance level of α = 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 409 MHD patients were included, with a mean age of 59 years. During follow-up, 40 deaths occurred, including 24 cardiovascular-related deaths and 16 non-cardiovascular deaths (6 due to infection and 10 due to other causes). The median follow-up time was 31.3 months (IQR 28.9–31.8 months). Among the enrolled patients, 265 (64.8%) were male and 144 (35.2%) were female. Primary diseases included diabetic nephropathy (111 cases, 27.1%), hypertensive nephropathy (97 cases, 23.7%), primary glomerular disease (44 cases, 10.8%), and other causes (67 cases, 16.4%). The mean duration of dialysis was 36 months. Based on the ROC curve, the optimal cutoff value for TBIL was 8.79 µmol/L (AUC = 0.723, 95%CI: 0.636–0.809)(Fig. 1). The stability of this cutoff was further supported by bootstrap internal validation (1000 resamples), which showed good reproducibility (95% CI width: 3.38 µmol/L) (Supplementary Material 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Patients were divided into high bilirubin (H) (TBIL > 8.79 µmol/L, 236 cases) and low bilirubin (L) (TBIL ≤ 8.79 µmol/L, 173 cases) groups. Statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of hypertension history, PLT, TP, BUN, Cr, UA, TG, and TCHO levels (all P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in age, sex, duration of dialysis, primary disease, vascular access, clinical frailty score, smoking history, alcohol consumption, education level, marital status, Hb, Ca, or PTH levels (Table 1).

Relationship between serum total bilirubin and All-Cause mortality

TBIL as a categorical variable

In our study population, univariate Cox analysis showed that for every 1 µmol/L increase in TBIL, the risk of all-cause mortality increased by 20% (HR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.12–1.29). Compared to patients with low TBIL levels, those with high TBIL levels had a 346% higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 4.46, 95% CI: 2.30–8.64). After adjusting for age and sex in Model 1, the risk remained at 329% (HR: 4.29, 95% CI: 2.21–8.32). Model 2 further adjusted for comorbidities and duration of dialysis, and the risk increased to 385% (HR: 4.85, 95% CI: 2.44–9.63). Model 3 further adjusted for hemoglobin, white blood cell count, serum albumin, blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, uric acid, calcium, phosphorus, and parathyroid hormone, and the risk increased to 569% (HR = 6.92, 95%CI 3.10–15.43, P < 0.001) (Table 2). The survival curves for the high and low bilirubin groups are shown in Fig. 2.

To assess the balance between groups, we calculated the standardized mean difference (SMD) for all variables. Among the 28 variables, 14 (50.0%) had an SMD < 0.1 (indicating good balance), 8 (28.6%) had an SMD between 0.1 and 0.2 (minor imbalance), and 6 (21.4%) had an SMD ≥ 0.2 (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2). All variables with SMD ≥ 0.2 (TBIL, PLT, Cr, TCHO, Hypertension, BUN) were included as adjustment factors in our multivariate Cox proportional hazards model (Model 3), which supports the reliability of the results. We also performed internal validation of the multivariate Cox model (Model 3) using the bootstrap method. The original HR was 6.92 (95% CI: 3.10–15.43), the bootstrap mean HR was 8.57, and the bootstrap 95% CI was 3.21–19.88. These results indicate that TBIL > 8.79 µmol/L is a risk factor with significant statistical association and good robustness (Supplementary Material 2).

The complete results of the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model (Model 3) are presented in Supplementary Material 3. It is noteworthy that a high bilirubin level was the strongest independent predictor of all-cause mortality, with a predictive strength notably greater than other conventional prognostic factors, such as high serum phosphorus (HR: 1.77; 95% CI: 1.02–3.07), advanced age (HR per 1-year increase: 1.04; 95% CI: 1.01–1.08), and anemia (HR per 1 g/dL decrease in hemoglobin: 1.03; 95% CI: 1.01–1.06). Additionally, the adjusted model identified low serum calcium as a predictor associated with very high risk (HR: 7.17), though its 95% confidence interval was wide (1.65–31.24), indicating that the precision of this estimate requires further confirmation.

Furthermore, we used the Fine-Gray competing risks model to analyze the differential effects of TBIL level on cardiovascular versus non-cardiovascular mortality. High TBIL level was significantly associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular mortality (subdistribution hazard ratio [SHR] = 10.27; 95% CI: not reliably estimable; p < 0.001), but was not significantly associated with non-cardiovascular mortality (SHR = 1.82; 95% CI: 0.05–64.55; p = 0.290) (Supplementary Material 4). This finding supports the view that TBIL primarily serves as a cardiovascular-specific risk marker, suggesting that elevated TBIL levels specifically increase the risk of cardiovascular death without a significant effect on non-cardiovascular mortality. It should be noted that the confidence interval for cardiovascular mortality was wide, which may be due to sample size limitations or an extreme effect size, though this does not affect the interpretation of statistical significance. This analysis provides important evidence regarding the cardiovascular toxicity mechanism of TBIL and further strengthens the main conclusion of this study.

In addition, we analyzed the association between bilirubin as a continuous variable and all-cause mortality risk. The results showed that each 1 µmol/L increase in TBIL was associated with a 6% increase in mortality risk (HR: 1.06; 95% CI: 1.03–1.09; p < 0.001). When analyzing bilirubin by quintiles (Q1–Q5), using the lowest quintile (Q1) as reference, the results demonstrated a clear increasing trend in HR with higher TBIL levels. The highest quintile (Q5) had a 10.38 times higher mortality risk compared to the lowest quintile (HR: 10.377; 95% CI: 3.061–35.181; p < 0.001), which was highly statistically significant. A trend test using median values within each TBIL quintile was significant (P for trend < 0.001), further supporting a significant dose-response relationship between TBIL levels and mortality risk (Supplementary Material 5 and Supplementary Fig. 3).

TBIL as a continuous variable and robustness check

To address potential overfitting and to corroborate the primary findings, we performed a supplementary analysis using LASSO-penalized Cox regression (Supplementary Fig. 4). Complete results of this analysis—including the 18 selected variables, model performance (C-index: 0.869; likelihood ratio test: P < 0.001), and continuous TBIL association (HR = 1.26 per 1 µmol/L)—are detailed in Supplementary Material 6. The linear dose-response relationship was confirmed by restricted cubic spline analysis (P for nonlinearity = 0.47; Supplementary Material 7 and Supplementary Fig. 5). The proportional hazards assumption was validated (Global test P = 0.425).

This consistent association, observed through a distinct statistical methodology that mitigates overfitting concerns, supports the robustness of our primary finding that elevated TBIL is an independent predictor of mortality.

Relationship between age and All-Cause mortality

We found that in all adjusted models, apart from bilirubin, increasing age was also consistently and positively associated with a higher risk of mortality. Therefore, we further tested whether there was an interaction effect between bilirubin and age. The results showed that across all patients, higher bilirubin levels independently increased the risk of death, with a slight trend of diminishing effect as age increased (HR decreased by 0.3% per year of age), although this did not reach statistical significance. The results remained similar after adjusting for confounding factors(Table 3).

We further explored whether there were differences in all-cause mortality between high and low bilirubin groups in non-elderly and elderly populations. Age stratification analysis showed that in non-elderly patients, there was a significant difference in all-cause mortality risk between the high and low bilirubin groups (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3). The risk of all-cause mortality in the high bilirubin group was 11.82 times higher than that in the low bilirubin group (HR: 12.82, 95% CI: 3.76–43.76, P < 0.001). In elderly patients, there was no significant difference in all-cause mortality risk between the high and low bilirubin groups(HR = 2.00, 95%CI: 0.83–4.81, P = 0.12).

Discussion

Bilirubin, the terminal metabolite of heme catabolism, serves as both a potent endogenous antioxidant and immunosuppressant [15–17]. Its ability to inhibit lipid peroxidation and scavenge oxygen free radicals may delay atherosclerosis progression [18]. Population studies demonstrate that mildly elevated serum bilirubin levels in non-dialysis individuals confer protective effects against coronary/peripheral atherosclerosis and adverse outcomes in cardiovascular disease, malignancies, and respiratory disorders - benefits attributed to its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [19–21].

However, this protective relationship appears context-dependent. In non-alcoholic fatty liver disease patients, Han et al. identified a nonlinear U-shaped association between TBIL and all-cause mortality, with elevated risk emerging above specific thresholds [22]. Similarly, Sagheb et al. observed increased mortality with higher bilirubin levels in ischemic stroke patients [23]. The relationship remains particularly controversial in ESRD populations undergoing renal replacement therapy. While some studies suggest low TBIL independently protects against all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in peritoneal dialysis (PD) patients, [24] others report contradictory findings [25]. A retrospective analysis of 740 PD patients by Zhan et al. found elevated bilirubin significantly correlated with 5-year mortality, [26] while Yang et al.‘s study of 3,740 PD patients revealed U-shaped mortality patterns across TBIL quintiles, with optimal survival (24% mortality) at 0.5–0.6 mg/dL levels [27].

In HD populations, evidence remains limited and conflicting. A 2017 retrospective cohort of 47,650 chronic HD patients stratified by TBIL levels (0.1–0.3, 0.3–0.7, 0.7–1.2 mg/dL) demonstrated 14% higher mortality in the highest TBIL group versus the lowest after 27.6 ± 12 month follow-up. Multivariable adjustments for comorbidities and nutritional markers strengthened this association (18–44% mortality increase) [12]. This contrasts with smaller HD studies showing divergent outcomes [28].

The discrepant findings across dialysis populations may be explained by several factors, including the potential for a non-linear or U-shaped association, differences between PD and HD modalities, and heterogeneity in adjusting for confounders. However, in our HD cohort, restrictive cubic spline analysis did not support a U-shaped relationship but instead demonstrated a significant, positive linear association between TBIL and mortality. This relationship held across multiple statistical approaches and remained robust in a LASSO-penalized Cox model that mitigated concerns of overfitting, thereby strengthening the credibility of our primary finding.

Our multicenter observational cohort of 409 chronic HD patients in Tianjin (a major northern Chinese municipality) found consistent associations between hyperbilirubinemia and elevated all-cause mortality across adjustment models. Age-stratified analysis revealed this association persisted only in non-elderly patients. We hypothesize this paradoxical association reflects HD-induced oxidative stress amplification, [29, 30] where elevated bilirubin may indicate overwhelming oxidative damage exceeding its antioxidant capacity. From a pathophysiological perspective, bilirubin metabolism involves both liver and kidney functions. Its levels appear to be influenced by multiple factors, including hepatic processing capacity, renal elimination, and overall oxidative stress. While our findings indicate an association between bilirubin levels and mortality, the exact mechanisms behind this relationship require further exploration. This includes understanding how different bilirubin forms might play distinct roles, and how they interact with accumulated uremic toxins.

The strength of our study lies in its multicenter cohort design conducted in Tianjin, one of the major municipalities in northern China, which enhances the representativeness of the hemodialysis population in this region. Furthermore, we performed age-stratified analyses, which revealed that bilirubin levels were associated with all-cause mortality in non-elderly patients but not in elderly patients.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The primary limitation is the retrospective design. We were unable to obtain gold-standard measures of dialysis adequacy, such as Kt/V, for all patients. Although we adjusted for serum BUN, Cr, and UA levels—biomarkers closely correlated with dialysis efficiency—in multivariate models, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Additionally, our study could not include some dialysis-related parameters (such as dialyzer membrane type and uremic toxin exposure) that might have acted as confounding factors. The association between total bilirubin and mortality requires further validation in prospective studies with standardized assessment of dialysis adequacy.

An important limitation is the lack of data on unconjugated bilirubin, which is particularly relevant given that recent evidence suggests only the lipid-soluble unconjugated form possesses potent antioxidant effects. 30 This omission may have implications for interpreting the biological mechanisms underlying our findings.

Secondly, variations in laboratory testing and clinical practices across participating centers resulted in high missing rates (> 90%) for several potentially important indicators—such as β2-microglobulin, α1-microglobulin, atrial natriuretic peptide, and haptoglobin—as these were not routinely measured in all centers. Consequently, these variables could not be included in the primary analysis. Similarly, inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) were not routinely measured at all centers, despite their recognized relevance to mortality prediction and systemic inflammation in dialysis patients, leading to systematic missingness. Although white blood cell count (WBC) was used as a surrogate marker for adjusting inflammatory status, the lack of specific inflammatory biomarkers may introduce unmeasured confounding.

Additionally, abdominal ultrasonography was not routinely performed for all enrolled patients, limiting our ability to systematically exclude underlying hepatobiliary pathologies. Although strict clinical exclusion criteria were applied at enrollment (see Methods) to ensure a study population with normal baseline liver function, and although routine imaging is not standard practice for asymptomatic maintenance hemodialysis patients, the absence of systematic imaging data may still contribute to residual confounding. We recommend that future prospective studies incorporate standardized imaging protocols where feasible to provide more definitive evidence and further validate our findings.

Moreover, this study was conducted solely in Tianjin, China. While the multicenter design within this region enhances local representativeness, the generalizability of our results may be limited. Significant variations exist across China in economic development, dietary habits, access to healthcare, and clinical practices. Furthermore, as the study population predominantly comprised Han Chinese individuals from northern China, caution is warranted when extrapolating the findings to populations from southern China or other ethnic groups. Future studies involving broader geographical and ethnic populations are needed to evaluate the external validity and generalizability of our results.

Another important limitation is the lack of systematically collected data on specific causes of death other than cardiovascular mortality. This precluded exploration of potential associations between bilirubin levels and specific mortality causes, such as infectious deaths, which may hold clinical significance. Prospective studies with detailed cause-of-death registration are essential to clarify the mechanisms underlying the observed associations.

Future prospective studies should collect comprehensive data on dialysis adequacy, bilirubin subtypes, inflammatory markers, and causes of death. This information will be essential to understand the mechanisms behind our observations and to validate the findings in different populations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that elevated total bilirubin levels are significantly associated with increased all-cause mortality among non-elderly MHD patients (HR: 12.82, 95% CI: 3.76–43.76, P < 0.001), while no significant association was observed in the elderly subgroup (HR: 2.00, 95% CI: 0.83–4.81, P = 0.12). These exploratory results suggest that the prognostic value of bilirubin may be age-dependent in the hemodialysis population. Further research is warranted to validate these observations and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms, particularly through investigating the potential differential effects of unconjugated and conjugated bilirubin on clinical outcomes in HD patients.
