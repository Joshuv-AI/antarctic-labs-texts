A decentralised forward-backward-type algorithm with network-independent heterogenous agent step sizes
Matthew K. Tam, Liam Timms, Lele Zhang

Abstract
Consider the problem of finding a zero of a finite sum of maximally monotone operators, where some operators are Lipschitz continuous and the rest are potentially set-valued. We propose a forward-backward-type algorithm for this problem suitable for decentralised implementation. In each iteration, agents evaluate a Lipschitz continuous operator and the resolvent of a potentially set-valued operator, and then communicate with neighbouring agents. Agents choose their step sizes independently using only local information, and the step size upper bound has no dependence on the communication graph. We demonstrate the potential advantages of the proposed algorithm with numerical results for min-max problems and aggregative games. Keywords: distributed optimisation, network independent step size, forward-backward algorithm, min-max problems. MSC2020: 47N10, 65K15, 47H05, 47J22, 91A10

Introduction

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a real Hilbert space. Consider a connected graph of
N
agents coordinating to find a zero of a finite sum of monotone operators given by

\text{find~}z\in\mathcal{H}\text{~s.t.~}0\in\sum_{i=1}^{N}(A_{i}+B_{i})(z),

(1)

where
A_{i}:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
is maximally monotone (potentially set-valued) and
B_{i}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is monotone and
L_{i}
-Lipschitz continuous for
i=1,\dots,N
. In this work, we consider a decentralised distributed setting where agent
i
can evaluate
B_{i}
and the resolvent of
A_{i}
, and agents communicate with each other if connected by an edge in the graph. Problems of this kind arise, for example, when the data is partitioned into subsets and allocated to agents, such as route choice in road networks
[
23
]
, binary classification
[
1
]
, and generative adversarial networks
[
6
]
. Of particular importance to this work are
min-max problems
of the form

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{H}_{1}}\max_{v\in\mathcal{H}_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(u)+g_{i}(u,v)-h_{i}(v),

(2)

where
\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
\mathcal{H}_{2}
are real Hilbert spaces,
f_{i}:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
and
h_{i}:\mathcal{H}_{2}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
are proper lsc convex functions, and
g_{i}:\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is a convex-concave function with Lipschitz continuous gradient. Assuming (
2
) has a saddle-point and the subdifferential sum rule holds for
\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i}
, solutions of (
2
) can be characterised as solutions of (
1
) with

z=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2},\quad A_{i}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\partial f_{i}(u)\\
\partial h_{i}(v)\end{pmatrix},\quad B_{i}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\gradient_{u}g_{i}(u,v)\\
-\gradient_{v}g_{i}(u,v)\end{pmatrix}.

(3)

This follows from the first-order optimality condition for (
2
) and
[
28
, Eqn. 2.19]
. In this context, the operator
B_{i}
is known as the
saddle operator
associated with
g_{i}
.

In this work, we focus on solving (
1
) using decentralised algorithms of forward-backward-type with fixed step sizes. One such algorithm is
PG-EXTRA

[
31
,
35
]
, applicable when
B_{1},\dots,B_{N}
are cocoercive with constants
1/L_{1},\dots,1/L_{N}
respectively. Given a
mixing matrix

W=(w_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
(Definition

) and a step size
\alpha<(1+\lambda_{\text{min}}(W))/(\max_{i}\{L_{i}\})
, where

\left\{\begin{aligned} v_{i}^{k}&=B_{i}(x_{i}^{k}),\\
z_{i}^{k+1}&=z_{i}^{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{ij}x_{j}^{k}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\overline{w}_{ij}x_{j}^{k-1}-\alpha(v_{i}^{k}-v_{i}^{k-1}),\\
x_{i}^{k+1}&=J_{\alpha A_{i}}(z_{i}^{k+1}),\end{aligned}\right.\quad\forall i=1,\dots,N,

(4)

where
(\overline{w}_{ij})=\overline{W}=(I+W)/2
and
J_{A_{i}}=(I+A_{i})^{-1}
denotes the
resolvent
of
A_{i}
. Here the variables
v^{k}_{i}
,
x^{k}_{i}
, and
z^{k}_{i}
are local variables belonging to agent
i
, and the coefficients
w_{ij}
are nonzero only if agents
i
and
j
are neighbours in the communication graph. Thus, communication between agents occurs only if the agents are neighbours. Denote

\mathbf{x}^{k}=(x_{1}^{k},\dots,x^{k}_{N}),\quad\mathbf{z}^{k}=(z_{1}^{k},\dots,z^{k}_{N}),

A(\mathbf{x})=A_{1}(x_{1})\times\dots\times A_{N}(x_{N}),\quad B(\mathbf{x})=(B_{1}(x_{1}),\dots,B_{N}(x_{N})),

(5)

\mathbf{W}=W\otimes I,\text{~and~}\overline{\mathbf{W}}=\overline{W}\otimes I,

where
\otimes
denotes the
Kronecker product
. Using this notation, (
4

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbf{v}^{k}&=B(\mathbf{x}^{k}),\\
\mathbf{z}^{k+1}&=\mathbf{z}^{k}+\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}^{k}-\overline{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x}^{k-1}-\alpha(\mathbf{v}^{k}-\mathbf{v}^{k-1}),\\
\mathbf{x}^{k+1}&=J_{\alpha A}(\mathbf{z}^{k+1}).\end{aligned}\right.

(6)

In the context of (
1
), PG-EXTRA suffers a number of drawbacks.

1.

The single-valued operators must be cocoercive.
Cocoercivity is often too restrictive an assumption in this context. For example, in (
3
) the saddle operator
B_{i}
is not cocoercive even for bilinear functions.

2.

Agents must use homogeneous step sizes
. The step size,
\alpha
, is determined by the smallest cocoercivity constant across agents’ single-valued operators. This means agents cannot easily exploit the curvature of their local functions.

3.

The step size upper bound is graph-dependent
. The upper bound depends on the minimum eigenvalue of
W
, which depends on the communication graph and the method of constructing
W
. Consequently, agents require knowledge beyond their local information
[
15
]
.

Various papers in the literature have proposed algorithms to resolve one or more of these issues, although none have resolved all of three in the context of (
1
). To resolve the cocoercivity drawback of PG-EXTRA,
20
proposed an algorithm that only requires the operators
B_{i}
to be
L_{i}
-Lipschitz continuous for all
i=1,\dots,N
. Given a mixing matrix
W
and a step size
\alpha<(1+\lambda_{\text{min}}(W))/(4\max_{i}\{L_{i}\})
, the main iteration of
[
20
, Alg. 1]
takes the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{k}

\displaystyle=2B(\mathbf{x}^{k})-B(\mathbf{x}^{k-1}),

(7a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{z}^{k}+\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}^{k}-\overline{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x}^{k-1}-\alpha(\mathbf{v}^{k}-\mathbf{v}^{k-1}),

(7b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=J_{\alpha A}(\mathbf{z}^{k+1}).

(7c)

Note that (
6
) and (
7
) are very similar. Indeed, the only difference is the
extragradient-type
term for the forward operator in (
7a
). This term arises because convergence of (
7
) follows from the
primal-dual-twice-reflected
algorithm, whereas convergence of (
6
) follows from the
Condat–Vũ
algorithm
[
20
, §2]
. There are algorithms besides (
7
) that can resolve the first drawback of PG-EXTRA (see, for example,
[
6
,
29
]
). However, the convergence analysis of these follow from the algorithm presented by
22
, which is restricted to the setting of (
2
).

To resolve the step size drawbacks of PG-EXTRA when the operators
B_{1},\dots,B_{N}
are cocoercive with constants
1/L_{1},\dots,1/L_{N}
respectively,
15
proposed the
NIDS
algorithm. In this algorithm, agent step sizes are represented as a diagonal matrix
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N})
, where
\alpha_{i}
is the step size for agent
i
. Given a mixing matrix
W
, step sizes satisfying
\alpha_{i}<2/L_{i}
, and parameter
\beta\leq 1/\max_{i}\{\alpha_{i}\}
, denote
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}=(I-(\beta/2)\Lambda(I-W))\otimes I
and
\bm{\Lambda}=\Lambda\otimes I
. The main iteration for NIDS takes the form

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbf{v}^{k}&=B(\mathbf{x}^{k}),\\
\mathbf{z}^{k+1}&=\mathbf{z}^{k}-\mathbf{x}^{k}+\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}\big(2\mathbf{x}^{k}-\mathbf{x}^{k-1}-\bm{\Lambda}(\mathbf{v}^{k}-\mathbf{v}^{k-1})\big),\\
\mathbf{x}^{k+1}&=J_{\bm{\Lambda}A}(\mathbf{z}^{k+1}).\end{aligned}\right.

(8)

To aid comparison between the previous three algorithms, observe that the
\mathbf{z}^{k+1}
updates in (
6
), (
7
), and (
8
) are very similar. Indeed, the
\mathbf{z}^{k+1}
updates in (
6
) and (
7
) can be expressed as

\mathbf{z}^{k+1}=\mathbf{z}^{k}-\mathbf{x}^{k}+\overline{\mathbf{W}}\big(2\mathbf{x}^{k}-\mathbf{x}^{k-1}\big)-\alpha(\mathbf{v}^{k}-\mathbf{v}^{k-1}),

and further,
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}=\overline{\mathbf{W}}
when
\alpha_{1}=\dots=\alpha_{N}
and
\beta=1/\max_{i}\{\alpha_{i}\}
. Thus, except for the step sizes, the only difference is the information communicated by agents to their neighbours: in (
6
) and (
7
) this information is the most recent resolvent evaluation, whereas in (
8
) it is a linear combination of the current and previous resolvent and forward evaluations. The convergence analysis of (
8
) follows from the
Davis–Yin
algorithm
[
11
,
30
, §11.3]
. There are few algorithms other than (
8
) that can resolve the second and third drawbacks of PG-EXTRA (see, for example,
[
37
,
36
]
), and they only apply in settings more limited than (
1
), such as convex minimisation. In this work, we focus on algorithms that are applicable to (
1
) in the general setting of monotone inclusions, as the first drawback of PG-EXTRA is not a concern in, for example, minimisation problems.

It would be tempting to try resolving the three drawbacks of PG-EXTRA by directly combining the ideas of (
7
) and (
8
). A first attempt of this would involve replace the forward evaluation in (
8
) with an extragradient evaluation as in (
7
). However, in the context of (
1
), the convergence analysis of such an algorithm does not directly follow from the Davis–Yin algorithm, which relies on cocoercivity of the single-valued operators. Instead, we propose the following algorithm (Algorithm
1
). Given a mixing matrix
W
, step sizes
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N}
with
\alpha_{i}<1/(8L_{i})
, and parameter
\beta<\|\Lambda^{1/2}((I-W)/2)\Lambda^{1/2}\|
, the main iteration for our algorithm takes the form

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbf{v}^{k}&=2B(\mathbf{y}^{k})-B(\mathbf{y}^{k-1}),\\
\mathbf{z}^{k+1}&=\mathbf{z}^{k}-\mathbf{x}^{k}+\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}\big(2\mathbf{x}^{k}-\mathbf{x}^{k-1}-\bm{\Lambda}(\mathbf{v}^{k}-\mathbf{v}^{k-1})\big),\\
\mathbf{x}^{k+1}&=J_{\bm{\Lambda}A}(\mathbf{z}^{k+1}),\\
\mathbf{y}^{k+1}&=\mathbf{x}^{k}+\mathbf{z}^{k+1}-\mathbf{z}^{k}.\end{aligned}\right.

(9)

This algorithm resolves all three drawbacks of PG-EXTRA in the context of (
1
) without introducing additional assumptions on the operators. We wish to emphasise an important difference between this iteration and (
7
) and (
8
): the forward operator is not evaluated at
\mathbf{x}^{k}
as with the other methods, but rather it is evaluated at another point
\mathbf{y}^{k}
.

Throughout this paper, let
\mathcal{H}
be a real Hilbert space with scalar product

. The product space

Graphs and mixing matrices

Let
\preceq
represent the

,

represent the

,

Given real symmetric matrices

(M_{1}M_{2})\otimes I=(M_{1}\otimes I)(M_{2}\otimes I)

Proof.

(i) See, for example,

(iii) See, for example,

In the context of distributed algorithms like those discussed in Section
1
, communication amongst agents is modelled by a connected graph
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, where
\mathcal{V}=\{1,\dots,N\}
is the set of nodes and
\mathcal{E}
is the set of edges. Each agent is represented by a node, and communication is represented by edges: two agents

can communicate if
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}
. For a given node
i\in\mathcal{V}
,
\mathcal{N}(i)
denotes the set of
neighbours
of node
i
and
\mathcal{N}^{2}(i)
denotes the set of nodes whose distance from node

is two or less, defined by

\mathcal{N}(i)=\{i\}\cup\{j\in\mathcal{V}\,\rvert\,(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}\},\quad\mathcal{N}^{2}(i)=\bigcup_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}\mathcal{N}(j).

Observe that

if and only if
j\in\mathcal{N}(i)
. The definitions of the set of neighbours and distance-two neighbours both have
i\in\mathcal{N}(i)
and

for notational convenience.

A round of communication can be modelled by agents forming a linear combination of variables communicated by neighbouring agents, for example

in (

), when the matrix

Definition 2.1

(Mixing matrix

]
)

Given a connected graph
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
with
\mathcal{V}=\{1,\dots,N\}
, a matrix

One method of constructing a mixing matrix is the

where
\mathcal{L}
is the graph Laplacian of

and

. For a discussion of other choices for the mixing matrix, see
[
16
,
31
]
.

In the remainder of this paper, we will abuse notation in the following way: for a real symmetric matrix

, we will denote the product

by

2.2
Operators

Set-valued operators are denoted by
A:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
and single-valued operators by
B:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
. For an operator
A:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
, its
domain
is
\dom A=\{x\in\mathcal{H}\,\rvert\,A(x)\neq\varnothing\}
and its
range
is
\ran A=A(\mathcal{H})
. The
graph
of
A
is

\mathrm{gra}A=\{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}\,\rvert\,x^{\prime}\in A(x)\},

and the
inverse
of
A
is defined through its graph,

\mathrm{gra}(A^{-1})=\{(x^{\prime},x)\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}\,\rvert\,(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathrm{gra}A\}.

An operator
A:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
is
monotone
if

\langle x-y,x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}\rangle\geq 0\quad\forall(x,x^{\prime}),(y,y^{\prime})\in\mathrm{gra}A,

and a monotone operator
A:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
is
maximally monotone
if there does not exist a monotone operator
A^{\prime}:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathrm{gra}A\subset\mathrm{gra}A^{\prime}
. Given a maximally monotone operator
A:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
, its
resolvent
,
J_{A}=(I+A)^{-1}
, is a single-valued operator with
\dom J_{A}=\mathcal{H}

[
21
]
. An operator
B:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is
L
-
Lipschitz continuous
if
L\geq 0
and

\|B(x)-B(y)\|\leq L\norm{x-y}\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{H},

and
\beta
-
cocoercive
if
\beta>0
and

\langle x-y,B(x)-B(y)\rangle\geq\beta\|B(x)-B(y)\|^{2}\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{H}.

Cocoercive operators are Lipschitz continuous, following from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. However, the converse is not always true. For example, skew-symmetric operators are Lipschitz continuous and never cocoercive
[
19
]
. Given a differentiable convex function
f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
, the operator
B=\gradient f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is
1/L
-cocoercive if and only if it is
L
-Lipschitz continuous
[
3
]
. If, however, an operator
B:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is not the gradient of a convex function, then cocoercivity can only be guaranteed with additional restrictive assumptions such as strong monotonicity
[
5
, Remark 5]
.

2.3
Convex functions

For a function
f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
, its
domain
is
\dom f=\{x\in\mathcal{H}\,\rvert\,f(x)<+\infty\}
and it is
proper
if
\dom f\neq\varnothing
and
f(x)>-\infty
for all
x\in\dom f
. Given a proper lsc convex function
f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, its (convex)
subdifferential
at a point
x\in\mathcal{H}
is the set-valued operator

\partial f(x)=\begin{cases}\{u\in\mathcal{H}\,\rvert\,\langle y-x,u\rangle+f(x)-f(y)\leq 0\,,\forall y\in\mathcal{H}\},&x\in\dom f,\\
\varnothing,&x\notin\dom f.\end{cases}

The operator
\partial f:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
is maximally monotone
[
27
]
, and if a function
f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable then
\partial f(x)=\{\gradient f(x)\}
. Given a nonempty closed convex set
C\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, the
indicator function
of
C
is the proper lsc convex function
\iota_{C}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
defined by

\iota_{C}(x)=\begin{cases}0&x\in C,\\
+\infty&x\notin C.\end{cases}

The
normal cone
of
C
,
N_{C}:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
, is the subdifferential of
\iota_{C}
.

2.4
Properties of monotone operators and inclusions

We now state some general results on monotone operators and monotone inclusions that will be useful in the convergence analysis of Algorithm
1
, detailed in Section
3
. The first result establishes a sufficient condition for positive definiteness of a certain operator.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
S,T:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be self-adjoint operators. If
c>\|TS^{2}T\|
, then
cI-ST^{2}S\succ 0
.

Proof.

Suppose
c>\|TS^{2}T\|=\|TS\|^{2},
where equality follows from
S
and
T
being self-adjoint. Using the definition of the operator norm, we then have

c\|x\|^{2}>\|TS\|^{2}\|x\|^{2}\geq\|TSx\|^{2}\iff\big\langle\big(cI-ST^{2}S\big)x,x\big\rangle>0,\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{H}\setminus\{0\},

which establishes positive definiteness.
∎

The second result establishes a sufficient condition for the operators in (
5
) defined on the product space to be maximally monotone, as well as a specific case where operator composition preserves maximal monotonicity and Lipschitz continuity.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
T_{1},\dots,T_{N}:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
be operators and
D=\mathrm{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{N})\otimes I
with
d_{1},\dots,d_{N}>0
. Define the operator
T:\mathcal{H}^{N}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}^{N}
by

T(\mathbf{x})=T_{1}(x_{1})\times\dots\times T_{N}(x_{N}).

Then the following statements hold.

1.

If
T_{1},\dots,T_{N}:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
are maximally monotone, then
DTD
is maximally monotone.

2.

If
T_{i}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is
L_{i}
-Lipschitz continuous for all
i=1,\dots,N
, then
DTD
is
\max_{i}\{d_{i}^{2}L_{i}\}
-Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

1
Suppose
T_{1},\dots,T_{N}
are maximally monotone. Then
T
is maximally monotone
[
4
, Prop. 20.23]
. Since
D
is self-adjoint and
\ran D=\mathcal{H}^{N}
, it follows that
DTD
is maximally monotone
[
4
, Cor. 25.6]
.

2
Suppose that
T_{i}
is
L_{i}
-Lipschitz continuous for all
i=1,\dots,N
. Then, for all
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{N}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{H}^{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\|(DTD)(\mathbf{x})-(DTD)(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|(d_{i}T_{i})(d_{i}x_{i})-(d_{i}T_{i})(d_{i}x_{i}^{\prime})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}^{2}L_{i}^{2}\|d_{i}x_{i}-d_{i}x_{i}^{\prime}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i}\{d_{i}^{4}L_{i}^{2}\}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}.

Thus,
DTD
is
\max_{i}\{d_{i}^{2}L_{i}\}
-Lipschitz continuous,
establishing the result.
∎

The third result is an extension of
[
20
, Prop. 4.1,]
, where an inclusion in
\mathcal{H}
of the form (
1
) is reformulated as an inclusion in the product space
\mathcal{H}^{N}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
T_{1},\dots,T_{N}:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{c}^{k}+\mathbf{a}^{k}-\mathbf{a}^{k-1}-\Gamma(\mathbf{v}^{k}-\mathbf{v}^{k-1})-\beta\Gamma K^{2}\Gamma\big(2\mathbf{a}^{k}-\mathbf{a}^{k-1}-\Gamma(\mathbf{v}^{k}-\mathbf{v}^{k-1})\big),

\displaystyle=\mathbf{c}^{k}-\mathbf{a}^{k}+(I-\beta\Gamma K^{2}\Gamma)\big(2\mathbf{a}^{k}-\mathbf{a}^{k-1}-\Gamma(\mathbf{v}^{k}-\mathbf{v}^{k-1})\big).

(31)

Applying
\Gamma
to both sides of (
31
) and using the definitions of
\mathbf{x}^{k}
,
\mathbf{y}^{k}
,
\mathbf{z}^{k}
, and
K
, we obtain (
19b
):

\mathbf{z}^{k+1}=\mathbf{z}^{k}-\mathbf{x}^{k}+\left(I-\beta\Lambda\frac{I-W}{2}\right)\big(2\mathbf{x}^{k}-\mathbf{x}^{k-1}-\Lambda(\mathbf{v}^{k}-\mathbf{v}^{k-1})\big),\quad\forall k\geq 1.

Thus, we have established (
19a
)-(
19d
). It remains to show the initialisation (
18a
)-(
18d
).

To this end, first note that by definition
\mathbf{z}^{0}=\Gamma\mathbf{c}^{0}
,
\mathbf{y}^{-1}=\Gamma\mathbf{b}^{-1}
, and
\mathbf{y}^{0}=\Gamma\mathbf{b}^{0}
. From (
26
), we have
\mathbf{x}^{0}=J_{\Lambda A}(\mathbf{z}^{0})
, and
\mathbf{x}^{1}=J_{\Lambda A}(\mathbf{z}^{1})
, which establishes (
18a
) and (
18d
). Since
\mathbf{y}^{-1},\mathbf{y}^{0},\tilde{\mathbf{c}}^{0}
were arbitrary, we may, in particular, choose
\mathbf{y}^{-1}=\mathbf{y}^{0}
, which implies
\mathbf{v}^{0}=B(\mathbf{y}^{0})
, and

\tilde{\mathbf{c}}^{0}=M^{-1}K(2\mathbf{x}^{0}-\mathbf{z}^{0}-\Gamma^{2}\mathbf{v}^{0})=M^{-1}K\Gamma(2\mathbf{a}^{0}-\mathbf{c}^{0}-\Gamma\mathbf{v}^{0}),

(32)

which is well-defined since
M\succ 0
. Substituting (
32
) into (
27a
) gives
\mathbf{u}^{0}=0
. Substituting this into (
27b
) gives

\mathbf{y}^{1}=\Gamma\mathbf{b}^{1}=\Gamma(2\mathbf{a}^{1}-\mathbf{c}^{1}-\Gamma\mathbf{v}^{0})=2\mathbf{x}^{0}-\mathbf{z}^{0}-\Lambda\mathbf{v}^{0}
, which establishes (
18b
). Finally, (
18c
) follows from (
29
), which completes the derivation of Algorithm
1
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

The convergence of Algorithm
1
is independent of the communication graph, and agents are allowed to choose the largest step sizes permitted by their single-valued operators according to
\alpha_{i}<1/(8L_{i})
. In fact, even when agents use homogeneous step sizes, graph-independent step sizes can allow agents to use a larger step size than in (
7
). To see this, suppose
\alpha=\alpha_{1}=\dots=\alpha_{N}
and
W=I-(1/\tau)\mathcal{L}
, where
\tau>(1/2)\lambda_{\text{max}}(\mathcal{L})
. Additionally, let

\tau<\frac{2}{3}\lambda_{\text{max}}(\mathcal{L}).

(33)

We then have

\tau<\frac{2}{3}\lambda_{\text{max}}(\mathcal{L})\iff\lambda_{\text{min}}(W)=1-\frac{1}{\tau}\lambda_{\text{max}}(\mathcal{L})<-\frac{1}{2}\iff\frac{1+\lambda_{\text{min}}(W)}{4L}<\frac{1}{8L}.

Thus, the step size upper-bound for Algorithm
1
is greater than that of (
7
) when the mixing matrix is chosen according to (
10
) and (
33
). Note that it is always possible to choose
\tau
in this manner since
\lambda_{\text{max}}(\mathcal{L})>0
for connected graphs
[
8
, Lem. 1.7]
. Furthermore, with this choice of
\tau
,
W
is a special case of the Laplacian-based constant edge weight matrix (
10
) where that parameter
\tau
has an upper bound.

4.1
Robust least squares

In the first numerical experiment, we consider the
robust least squares problem

[
13
]
. Given a data matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
and noisy vector
\tilde{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
u
minimises the worst-case error in the least-square sense. We assume the noise in
\tilde{v}
is bounded in norm by some constant
\delta>0
. Consider a population of
N
agents such that the data matrix and the noisy vector are partitioned across the agents. In other words, suppose
d=\sum_{i}d_{i}
and denote

M=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}M_{1}&\dots&M_{N}\end{array}\right],\quad\tilde{v}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{v}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{v}_{N}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad v=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
\vdots\\
v_{N}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
M_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}\times p}
and
\tilde{v}_{i},v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}

i
.
Under this notation, the distributed robust least squares problem can then be formulated as

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\max_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}

\displaystyle\,\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|M_{i}u-v_{i}\|^{2},

(34a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|v_{i}-\tilde{v}_{i}\|\leq\delta.

(34b)

Since (
34
) is not concave in

, we follow the approach of
[
33
]
and solve a penalty formulation. Given a parameter
\lambda>0
, the penalty formulation of (
34
) is

\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\max_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(u,v)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|M_{i}u-v_{i}\|^{2}-\lambda\|v_{i}-\tilde{v}_{i}\|^{2}.

(35)

Note that when

is convex-concave. Moreover, when

and

has full column rank,

1
) with

z=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad A_{i}(z)=\binom{0}{0},\quad B_{i}(z)=2\begin{bmatrix}M_{i}^{\top}M_{i}&-M_{i}^{\top}E_{i}\\
-E_{i}^{\top}M_{i}&(\lambda-1)E_{i}^{\top}E_{i}\end{bmatrix}z-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
2\lambda E_{i}^{\top}\tilde{v}_{i}\end{pmatrix}.

A memory-efficient approach for aggregative games

In this section, we propose an alternative implementation of Algorithm
1
for
aggregative games

[
10
]
. This implementation exploits the problem’s aggreative structure to reduce the number of variables needed as compared to a direct application of Algorithm
1
. Precisely, we consider an aggregative game between
N
noncooperative players where player
i
seeks to

\displaystyle\displaystyle\min_{u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\,

\displaystyle g_{i}(u_{i})+f_{i}\big(u_{i},\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}\big),

(38a)

s.t.

\displaystyle u_{i}\in\Omega_{i},

(38b)

\displaystyle M\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}\leq b,

(38c)

Here the function
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is proper lsc convex and
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex with
L_{i}
-Lipschitz continuous gradient. The constraint set
\Omega_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
is nonempty closed convex,
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
, and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We assume
Slater’s condition

[
7
, 3.2.7]
holds for (
38
) as detailed in the following assumption.

Assumption 1

(Slater’s condition)
.

There exists
(u_{1},\dots,u_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\dots\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
{u_{i}\in\dom(g_{i}+\iota_{\Omega_{i}})}
for all
i=1,\dots,N
, and

M\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}<b
.

In this work, solutions to (
38
) are understood in the sense of
generalised Nash equilibria

[
12
]
. Precisely, a feasible point
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{N})
is said to be a
generalised Nash equilibrium
of (
38
) if

g_{i}(u_{i})+f_{i}(u_{i},\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j})\leq\inf\left\{g_{i}(\bar{u}_{i})+f_{i}(\bar{u}_{i},\bar{u}_{i}+\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}u_{j})\,\big|\,\bar{u}_{i}\in\Omega_{i},\,M\bar{u}_{i}\leq b-M\sum_{j\neq i}u_{j}\right\}\hfill\forall i=1,\dots,N.

(39)

In other words, if a point is a Nash equilibrium then players have no incentive to unilaterally change strategy. The following proposition characterised Nash equilibrium in terms as zeros of an inclusion.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds. Then
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\dots\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a generalised Nash equilibrium of (
38
) if and only if there exists a dual variable
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\dots\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
(u,v)
satisfies

\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}\in\begin{pmatrix}\partial(g_{i}+\iota_{\Omega_{i}})(u_{i})\\
N_{\mathbb{R}^{p}_{\geq 0}}(v_{i})\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\gradient_{u_{i}}f_{i}(u_{i},\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j})+M^{\top}v_{i}\\
b-M\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}\end{pmatrix},\quad\forall i=1,\dots,N.

(40)

Proof.

Note that
u_{i}\mapsto f_{i}(u_{i},\,u_{i}+u^{\prime})+g_{i}(u_{i})+\iota_{\Omega_{i}}(u_{i})
is convex for all
u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and that
f_{i}
has full domain. Hence, as Assumption
1
holds, the
Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions
for (
38
) are necessary and sufficient for (
39
)
[
7
, Thm. 3.2.8, Prop. 3.2.3]
, and are given by

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\in\partial_{u_{i}}\big(g_{i}(u_{i})+\iota_{\Omega_{i}}(u_{i})+f_{i}(u_{i},\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j})+\langle v_{i},M\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}-b\rangle\big),

(41a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq M\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}-b,

(41b)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq v_{i},

(41c)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle v_{i},M\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}-b\rangle,

(41d)

Since
(u,v)\mapsto\langle v_{i},M\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}-b\rangle
has full domain, the subdifferential sum rule
[
4
, Cor. 16.48]
applied to (
41a
) yields

0\in\partial(g_{i}+\iota_{\Omega_{i}})(u_{i})+\gradient_{u_{i}}f_{i}(u_{i},\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j})+M^{\top}v_{i},\quad\forall i=1,\dots,N,

which is the first component of (
40
). Using the definition of
N_{\mathbb{R}^{p}_{\geq 0}}

[
2
, Eqn. 3.10]
, (
41b
)-(
41d
) can be expressed compactly as

0\in N_{\mathbb{R}^{p}_{\geq 0}}(v_{i})-\big(b-M\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}\big),\quad\forall i=1,\dots,N,

which is the second component of (
40
). This completes the proof.
∎

To state the proposed algorithm, let
\mathcal{H}=(\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
and

z_{i}=\binom{u_{i}}{v_{i}},\quad z=\begin{pmatrix}z_{1}\\
\vdots\\
z_{N}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{H},\quad A_{i}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}A_{i1}(z_{1})\\
\vdots\\
A_{iN}(z_{N})\end{pmatrix},\quad B_{i}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}B_{i1}(z_{1},\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{j})\\
\vdots\\
B_{iN}(z_{N},\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{j})\end{pmatrix},

(42a)

where the operators
A_{ij}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
B_{ij}:(\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
are defined by

\displaystyle A_{ii}(z_{i})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\partial(g_{i}+\iota_{\Omega_{i}})(u_{i})\\
N_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}(v_{i})\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle A_{ij}(z_{i})

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall j\neq i,

(42b)

\displaystyle B_{ii}(z_{i},\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j})

\displaystyle=\left.\begin{pmatrix}\gradient_{u_{i}}f_{i}(u_{i},s)+\gradient_{s}f_{i}(u_{i},s)+M^{\top}v_{i}\\
b-Ms\end{pmatrix}\right|_{s=\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}},

\displaystyle B_{ij}(z_{i},\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{j})

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall j\neq i.

(42c)

Using this notation, we then observe that the system of inclusions (
40
) can be compactly expressed as (
1
). Moreover, using the chain rule, we note that
\nabla_{u_{i}}f_{i}
in (

) can be expressed as

\gradient_{u_{i}}f_{i}(u_{i},\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j})=\big(\gradient_{u_{i}}f_{i}(u_{i},s)+\gradient_{s}f_{i}(u_{i},s)\big)\big|_{s=\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}}.

for the aggregative game (

Choose

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{0}

\displaystyle=J_{\Lambda A}(\mathbf{z}^{0})

(43a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{1}

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{x}^{0}-\mathbf{z}^{0}-\Lambda\mathbf{v}^{0},

(43b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{z}^{0}+\mathbf{y}^{1}-\mathbf{x}^{0},

(43c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{1}

\displaystyle=J_{\Lambda A}(\mathbf{z}^{1})

(43d)

\displaystyle\mu^{1}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{N}(i)}y^{1}_{ij},

\displaystyle\forall i

\displaystyle=1,\dots,N,

(43e)

\displaystyle\bar{y}^{1}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mu^{1}_{i}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}y^{1}_{ij},

\displaystyle\forall i

\displaystyle=1,\dots,N.

(43f)

for

do

Denote
v^{k}=(v^{k}_{ii})_{i=1}^{N},z^{k}=(z^{k}_{ii})_{i=1}^{N},x^{k}=(x^{k}_{ii})_{i=1}^{N},\mu^{k}=(\mu^{k}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\in(\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}.

for

Numerical experiment: virtual power plant

In this section, we numerically compare
[
20
, Alg. 1]
, as presented in (
7
), and our proposed Algorithms
1
and
2
in coordinating a
virtual power plant (VPP)
as an aggregative game. Specifically, we consider a VPP consisting of
N
noncooperative players, each operating a power bank, which are connected via the local power grid. For further details on VPPs, see
[
34
, §3E]
. In each time period
t=1,\dots,p
, players must decide how much electricity to buy (charging their power banks) and how much to sell (discharging their power banks) to satisfy their personal electricity usage while minimising their total cost over the time horizon. This model extends the electric vehicle charging game presented by
[
23
, §6]
, which allows charging but not discharging. We seek a vector that determines when and how much players charge and discharge their power banks such that players have no incentive to individually deviate from this schedule.

6.1
Problem formulation

We represent a charging schedule for the player population using
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{N})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2p}\times\dots\times\mathbb{R}^{2p}
, where the charging (
u_{i}^{+}
) and discharging (
u_{i}^{-}
) decisions for player
i
are denoted

u_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}^{+}\\
u_{i}^{-}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2p},\quad u_{i}^{+}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}^{+}(1)\\
\vdots\\
u_{i}^{+}(p)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\quad u_{i}^{-}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}^{-}(1)\\
\vdots\\
u_{i}^{-}(p)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

where
u_{i}^{+}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
(resp.
u_{i}^{-}(t)
) denotes the amount of electricity drawn from the grid (resp. discharged to the grid) by player
i=1,\dots,N
in time period
t=1,\dots,p
. Under this notation, we formulate the VPP coordination problem as an aggregative game, where the problem for player
i
is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\min_{u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2p}}\,

\displaystyle u_{i}^{\top}Q_{i}u_{i}+p_{i}^{\top}u_{i}+\left\langle\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}^{+}-u_{j}^{-}+m,\,u_{i}^{+}-u_{i}^{-}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle 0\leq u^{+}_{i}(t)\leq\overline{u}^{+}_{i}(t),

\displaystyle\forall t=1,\dots,p,

(51b)

\displaystyle 0\leq u^{-}_{i}(t)\leq\overline{u}^{-}_{i}(t),

\displaystyle\forall t=1,\dots,p,

(51c)

\displaystyle l^{\text{low}}_{i}(t)\leq\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left(e_{i}^{+}u^{+}_{i}(s)-\frac{1}{e_{i}^{-}}u^{-}_{i}(s)\right)\leq l^{\text{up}}_{i}(t),

\displaystyle\forall t=1,\dots,p,

(51d)

\displaystyle-m(t)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(u^{+}_{j}(t)-u^{-}_{j}(t)\right)\leq K(t)-m(t).

\displaystyle\forall t=1,\dots,p.

(51e)

The objective (
51a

[
18

5

7
Conclusion

We proposed a decentralised forward-backward type algorithm (Algorithm
1
) for finding a zero of a finite sum of (potentially) set-valued operators single-valued operators. This algorithm improves on several aspects of the PG-EXTRA algorithm: only requiring agents’ single-valued operators to be Lipschitz continuous, allowing agents to use heterogeneous step sizes, and only requiring agents to use local information in determining these step sizes. We also proposed a memory-efficient variant of this algorithm for aggregative games, which uses fewer variables than previous approaches (see, for example,
[
32
, Alg. 1]
). Numerical experiments on robust least squares, zero-sum games, and coordinating a virtual power plan illustrated the performance of our proposed algorithms and validated the convergence results. Future directions include improving the step size bound for Algorithm
1
, which arises from the backward-forward-reflected-backward algorithm
[
26
]
, allowing variable stepsizes to permit locally Lipschitz continuous operators
B_{i}
rather than globally Lipschitz continuous, and extending Algorithm
1
to time-varying mixing matrices for communication graphs that evolve through time.
