AI-assisted haematology: machine learning–based prediction of iron-deficiency anaemia from reticulocyte maturation indices
Authors: Hesamaddin Kamalzadeh, Niloofar Choobin, Ali Haghighat, Majid Teremmahi Ardestani, Mobina Farhanpoor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIron-deficiency anaemia (IDA) is the most prevalent hematological disorder globally, significantly impairing physical capacity and cognitive development. Early detection is crucial; however, conventional diagnostic methods often have limitations. Machine learning (ML) can enhance predictive accuracy by analyzing complex hematological parameters, including reticulocyte maturation indices. This study aimed to develop and compare multiple ML algorithms for IDA prediction and to identify the most effective model for potential clinical use.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study examined anonymized data from 444 adults (20–59 years) at Shahid Mohammadi Hospital, Iran, collected between November 2024 and January 2025. Five supervised ML algorithms including random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), decision tree (DT), naive Bayes (NB), and CatBoost were trained using a 70/30 train–test split. Stratified 10-fold cross-validation and the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) were applied for hyperparameter optimization and class-imbalance adjustment. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, AUC-ROC, and the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). SHapley additive explanations (SHAP) values were used to characterize feature importance.ResultsDual-criteria labelling identified 350 of 444 participants (79%) as having IDA. In 10-fold cross-validation of the training set (n = 310), CatBoost achieved the highest AUC-ROC (0.98), followed by RF (0.97), NB (0.91), SVM (0.90), and DT (0.88). On the independent test set (n = 134), CatBoost maintained superior performance, with an AUC-ROC of 0.93 (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.89–0.97), sensitivity of 0.92 (95% CI 0.88–0.96), specificity of 0.86 (95% CI 0.74–0.94), and MCC of 0.73 (95% CI 0.60–0.84). SHAP analysis identified haemoglobin (HGB), haematocrit (HCT), and red blood cell count (RBC) as the most influential predictors, underscoring the model’s physiological interpretability.ConclusionML models, especially CatBoost, accurately predict IDA using standard blood tests and reticulocyte indices. The use of explainable artificial intelligence (AI) enables physiologically interpretable predictions, which can significantly support earlier detection, more efficient resource use, and improved clinical decision-making. These results underscore the potential of incorporating reticulocyte maturation markers into ML-based diagnostic tools in hematology, offering a promising future of more efficient and effective resource utilization in clinical settings.

Introduction

AI and ML are reshaping the field of haematology. These computational approaches emulate human cognitive functions, including pattern recognition, reasoning, and decision-making, and have substantially improved the analysis of high-dimensional laboratory data [1]. AI technologies now support automated blast classification, transfusion risk stratification, and, notably, the early detection of IDA [2, 3]. ML, a data-driven subset of AI, has become the most widely applied methodology in this domain, offering the prospect of more accurate and timely diagnostic practices.

IDA, the most common nutritional deficiency worldwide, arises when iron availability is inadequate to support normal HGB synthesis and oxygen transport [4]. The condition affects nearly one-third of the global population, resulting in impaired physical and neurocognitive performance and generating substantial economic losses due to reduced productivity and increased healthcare use [5–10]. In southern Iran, Hormozgan Province has among the highest national IDA prevalence rates, approximately 30%, attributable largely to food insecurity, socioeconomic disadvantage, and environmental factors that limit iron absorption [11, 12].

Although oral or intravenous iron therapy is inexpensive, delayed diagnosis contributes to preventable morbidity. Conventional laboratory biomarkers, including HGB, ferritin, and transferrin saturation, typically decline only after iron stores are significantly depleted [13]. Reticulocyte maturation indices, in contrast, reflect real-time erythropoietic activity and respond to iron restriction days to weeks earlier [14]. Integrating these dynamic biomarkers into ML frameworks may shift IDA detection from a reactive to a predictive paradigm. Such an approach could facilitate earlier and more effective interventions, reduce reliance on costly treatments and hospitalizations, and ultimately decrease healthcare utilization.

Recent investigations have demonstrated the capacity of ML-based methods to enhance hematologic diagnostics. The Inception-v3 model achieved 84% accuracy in emergency department anaemia screening [15]. The CatBoost algorithm reached 97% accuracy in estimating anaemia severity among pregnant women in Ethiopia [16]. SVM and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) models similarly achieved accuracy rates exceeding 95% in differentiating IDA from thalassemia traits [17, 18]. Despite these promising findings, challenges such as data heterogeneity, stemming from variations in patient demographics and clinical characteristics, and limitations in model interpretability continue to impede routine clinical adoption [17].

This study aims to develop and externally validate an ML model incorporating reticulocyte maturation indices to enable earlier and more accurate identification of IDA. The proposed framework seeks to improve diagnostic precision beyond conventional rule-based methods and to establish a scalable foundation for population-level anaemia screening programs.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective cross-sectional study aimed to develop and validate ML models for predicting IDA using haematological data, including reticulocyte maturation indices. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hormozgan University of Medical Sciences and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and complete anonymization of patient data, ensuring adherence to established ethical standards.

Data source and study population

Laboratory records were retrospectively extracted from the Laboratory Information System (LIS) of the haematology laboratory at Shahid Mohammadi Hospital, Bandar Abbas, Hormozgan Province, Iran. The dataset included all consecutive haematology referrals processed between November 2024 and January 2025. Among 719 referrals, 650 were clinician-initiated evaluations for suspected anaemia, and 69 were routine preoperative or antenatal screenings; none had a predefined diagnosis of IDA at the time of referral. Each specimen underwent a complete blood count (CBC) with automated reticulocyte maturation profiling and a comprehensive iron panel, including serum ferritin, serum iron, and total iron-binding capacity (TIBC).

Inclusion criteria

Adults aged 20–59 years with a clinical diagnosis of IDA were enrolled. Anaemia was defined according to World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines as HGB concentrations below 13 g/dL in men and below 12 g/dL in non-pregnant women [19]. Iron deficiency was defined as serum ferritin levels below 15 ng/mL [20]. Additional iron indicators, including serum iron concentration and transferrin saturation, were evaluated. Transferrin saturation below 20% indicated insufficient iron availability for erythropoiesis. Only participants with complete laboratory data, including immature reticulocyte fraction (IRF) and low maturity reticulocyte (LMR) counts, were included. These parameters are established biomarkers of bone marrow function and erythropoietic activity.

Exclusion criteria

Participants with comorbidities potentially confounding study outcomes, including hematologic malignancies (e.g., leukaemia, lymphoma), chronic kidney disease, active infections, and autoimmune disorders, were excluded. Individuals who received blood transfusions within three months or iron therapy within six weeks before laboratory evaluation were also excluded. Participants with incomplete or unreliable laboratory records or conditions affecting iron metabolism or erythropoiesis were omitted to maintain dataset integrity.

Data cleaning and preprocessing

Quality deficiencies in the raw clinical dataset were addressed through a rigorous data cleaning and preprocessing pipeline to ensure analytical validity [21, 22], as illustrated in Fig. 1. Following initial cleaning, the data were partitioned into training and test sets using stratified random sampling to preserve the prevalence of IDA. Data preprocessing was applied strictly within the training data of each cross-validation fold, with no operations performed on the full dataset prior to splitting. This fold-specific approach further safeguards against data leakage, ensuring that the model remains fully blind to the validation and test partitions and allowing an unbiased assessment of predictive performance [23, 24]. ML algorithms were then trained on the processed data, with hyperparameter tuning performed to optimize model performance. The final tuned models were evaluated on the independent test set.

Data cleaning

Data cleaning, which involves identifying and addressing non-standard elements such as duplicate records and missing values, is crucial for the performance of ML pipelines [25, 26]. The dataset was carefully examined for duplicates and missing values. Duplicate records were removed to prevent redundancy and reduce potential bias, and records with more than 70% missing data were excluded to minimize bias from incomplete information [27].

Data splitting

The dataset was split into a 70% training subset and a 30% test subset using stratified random sampling, preserving class prevalence and limiting sampling bias [28].

Data preprocessing

Data preprocessing involved missing-value imputation, outlier handling, feature engineering, transformation, selection, and re-balancing of classes to produce analysis-ready data. First, missing values, which can bias clinical datasets and reduce model reliability [27], were imputed using clinically appropriate descriptive statistics (mean, median, or mode) selected according to distribution and domain knowledge. K-nearest-neighbour imputation was applied as a sensitivity check. Next, outliers, common in healthcare data, were identified using a Z-score threshold of |Z| > 3 [29, 30].

Subsequently, feature engineering generated derived variables, including haematological ratios and composite indices suggested by clinicians, to capture additional physiological information and enhance discriminative power [31]. Each candidate feature underwent a two-stage review: first, a haematologist confirmed physiological plausibility and relevance to IDA; second, statistical screening (variance, correlation with IDA, and cross-validation stability) removed uninformative or redundant variables.

Feature transformation prepared the dataset for algorithmic processing while maintaining interpretability [32]. This involved two sequential steps: feature encoding and feature scaling. Categorical variables were converted to numeric form using dummy encoding, and numeric variables were min–max scaled to the [0, 1] range to mitigate bias arising from heterogeneous feature scales [33].

Feature selection was conducted using a correlation-matrix heatmap, a model-agnostic filter. The heatmap displayed pairwise associations and the relationship of each feature with IDA, guiding the selection of a parsimonious, clinically relevant predictor subset [34, 35].

Finally, class imbalance, a common challenge in clinical diagnostics where positive cases are often underrepresented relative to controls [36], was addressed using SMOTE to oversample the minority class. This approach improves model sensitivity and generalizability while minimizing overfitting to the majority class [37, 38].

Model development, optimization, and validation

Five supervised ML algorithms, including SVM, RF, NB, DT, and CatBoost, were chosen for their documented utility on complex, heterogeneous medical data [28, 39–41]. Hyperparameters were tuned using stratified 10-fold cross-validation, with fold-averaged metrics guiding selection and preventing overfitting. Model performance was evaluated on the independent test set using a comprehensive set of metrics, including accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, and AUC-ROC. The MCC was additionally employed as a balanced metric that incorporates all four confusion matrix components, providing reliable assessment even for imbalanced datasets. MCC values range from − 1 (complete disagreement) to + 1 (perfect prediction), with 0 representing random performance. For all metrics, 95% CIs were estimated via bootstrapping to quantify uncertainty and robustness.

Model interpretation via SHAP

SHAP, a model-agnostic framework, was employed to quantify each feature’s contribution to individual predictions, enhancing clinical interpretability [38]. For each prediction, feature influence was estimated by averaging marginal contributions across all feature subsets using an empirical conditional distribution [42]. Global importance was calculated by ranking features according to their mean absolute SHAP values, expressed as normalized percentages. To optimize computational efficiency while maintaining clinical relevance, SHAP analysis was restricted to the best-performing model. All interpretations were derived exclusively from the independent test set to ensure unbiased and reliable results. Integrating SHAP explanations into the workflow provided predictive accuracy and the transparency required for clinical deployment, yielding actionable insights with comprehensive visualizations and standardized reporting [43].

Software and computational environment

Descriptive statistics were calculated using SPSS version 21. ML pipelines were implemented in Python 3.9 within the Jupyter Notebook environment, employing the scikit-learn, NumPy, pandas, and Matplotlib libraries.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

The initial dataset contained 719 patient records. After applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 700 records remained. Thirty duplicate entries were then removed, leaving 670 unique records. A further 226 records more than 70% missing laboratory values were excluded, resulting in a final analytic cohort of 444 patients. This systematic preprocessing produced a high-quality dataset suitable for downstream ML analyses.

The cohort had a balanced sex distribution, with 221 women (49.8%) and 223 men (50.2%), and a mean age of 49 years.

IDA was labelled using both quantitative and qualitative criteria. Quantitatively, IDA-positive status required serum ferritin less than 15 ng/mL or serum iron less than 50 µg/dL in combination with TIBC greater than 400 µg/dL. Qualitatively, CBC analyser flags indicating iron deficiency, microcytosis, anisocytosis, or hypochromia were also considered. Patients meeting any numeric or textual criterion were assigned IDA = 1; all others were assigned IDA = 0. Applying this dual definition to the final cohort resulted in 350 IDA-positive (79%) and 94 IDA-negative (21%) cases (Fig. 2).

The cohort was split into training (n = 310) and test (n = 134) subsets via stratified random sampling to preserve the IDA prevalence. Baseline demographics and laboratory variables, including age, sex, HGB, ferritin, and reticulocyte maturation indices, did not differ between subsets (all p > 0.05; Table 1). These haematologic changes characterise IDA and highlight reticulocyte maturation indices as potentially useful predictors.

Across the training and test cohorts, no statistically significant differences were observed in any baseline variable (all p > 0.05), indicating well-matched groups and minimal risk of covariate shift. Nevertheless, the wide HGB standard deviation in the test set and the marked skewness of ferritin measurements suggest that transformation or normalisation should precede model development.

When analysed by diagnosis, IDA patients (n = 350) differed significantly from non-IDA patients (n = 94) in HGB, IRF, LMR, serum ferritin, and PLT (all p ≤ 0.034). These features are likely to serve as strong discriminators in classification models and may require regularisation to prevent overfitting. The skewed ferritin distribution and apparent ceiling effect (upper limit 833.71 ng/mL) further support the use of transformation or censoring before model integration. In contrast, MPV, age, and sex did not differ between groups, suggesting limited discriminative value.

Distribution of reticulocyte maturation fractions

IDA patients exhibited higher fractions of high-fluorescence reticulocytes (HFR: 7.55 ± 8.47% vs. 2.69 ± 2.90%, p < 0.001) and medium-fluorescence reticulocytes (MFR: 13.97 ± 5.22% vs. 12.03 ± 3.65%, p < 0.001) than non-IDA patients, resulting in an elevated IRF (21.69 ± 12.84% vs. 14.70 ± 6.10%, p < 0.001). Correspondingly, the low-fluorescence reticulocyte (LFR) proportion was reduced in IDA (77.55 ± 14.37% vs. 85.29 ± 6.09%, p < 0.001).

Data preprocessing outcomes

Data preprocessing, including outlier handling, feature engineering, transformation, selection, and class rebalancing, was performed exclusively within each cross-validation training fold, preventing information leakage and producing analysis-ready data.

Records with more than 70% missing values were excluded, and the remaining variables were imputed using a strategy that integrated distributional properties with clinical relevance. Biomarkers exhibiting pronounced right-skewed distributions, such as ferritin and transferrin saturation, were log-transformed and imputed using fold-specific medians. Continuous variables with approximately symmetric distributions (HGB, HCT, and RBC) were imputed using variable-wise means, whereas skewed continuous variables, including MPV, platelet distribution width (PDW), immature platelet fraction (IPF), and reticulocyte indices, were imputed using the median. Categorical variables were imputed using the mode. All preprocessing steps dependent on empirical distributions, including imputation, log transformation, and the computation of scaling parameters, were conducted exclusively within the training fold of the cross-validation pipeline. This fold-specific approach ensured that parameter estimates remained unbiased and were not influenced by information from the held-out or test sets. To assess potential imputation bias, a sensitivity analysis was performed using k-nearest neighbour imputation (k = 5), applied exclusively within the training portion of each fold after log transformation and scaling. Differences in cross-validated ROC-AUC, sensitivity, and specificity were consistently below 0.5%, indicating a negligible effect on model discrimination and confirming the robustness of the selected imputation strategy.

Outlier handling was carefully considered due to its notable impact on model performance [29]. Observations with |Z|> 3 were identified and retained to preserve clinically meaningful extreme values, which are crucial for generalizability. Their influence was mitigated through log transformation and median-based imputation, ensuring the reliability of our study.

Feature engineering generated eight physiologically informed composite features; each derived exclusively from the training subset of the corresponding cross-validation fold. Candidate predictors were retained only if they met all of the following fold-specific criteria: (i) confirmed physiological relevance by a board-certified haematologist, (ii) sufficient variability within the fold (non-zero variance), and (iii) a minimum absolute Pearson correlation of 0.05 with the IDA outcome.

Critically, feature transformation, including feature encoding and scaling, was performed exclusively within each training fold of the cross-validation process to prevent data leakage and ensure unbiased model evaluation. Within each fold, numerical variables were normalized to the [0,1] range using min–max normalization, and categorical features were converted into binary indicator variables via dummy encoding.

Feature selection was conducted on the training data using correlation analysis, guided by a correlation heatmap (Fig. 3). Predictors with pairwise correlation coefficients exceeding |r| = 0.85 were considered highly correlated and removed to minimize multicollinearity and improve model interpretability [44]. From each correlated cluster, a single representative variable was retained, resulting in the exclusion of nine redundant predictors and yielding a refined set of 36 predictors for model development.

Finally, class imbalance within the training subsets was addressed using SMOTE applied inside the cross-validation folds. This method generates synthetic minority class instances through linear interpolation between existing samples, thereby balancing the class distribution and enhancing model sensitivity to clinically underrepresented outcomes. Recent studies have demonstrated the efficacy of integrating SMOTE within cross-validation pipelines to mitigate overfitting and promote generalizable predictive performance [38].

Model performance comparison

Model performance differed substantially across classifiers, as reflected in accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC-ROC values. In the stratified 10-fold cross-validation, CatBoost achieved the highest mean AUC-ROC (0.98), demonstrating the strongest discriminative ability. RF followed closely with an AUC-ROC of 0.97, whereas DT showed the lowest performance (AUC-ROC, 0.88). NB and SVM demonstrated moderate performance, with AUC-ROCs of 0.91 and 0.90, respectively. These differences were consistent with the remaining evaluation metrics. CatBoost obtained an accuracy of 0.92, sensitivity of 0.89, and specificity of 0.96, indicating balanced classification. RF showed similar accuracy (0.92) with slightly higher sensitivity (0.91) and slightly lower specificity (0.94). In contrast, SVM and DT recorded lower overall values. NB performed poorest, with an accuracy of 0.72 and a sensitivity of 0.47, underscoring its limited suitability for this task (Table 3).

On the independent test set, ensemble-based models outperformed simpler classifiers, with CatBoost achieving the highest predictive performance (AUC-ROC 0.93, 95% CI 0.89–0.97; sensitivity 0.92, 95% CI 0.88–0.96; specificity 0.86, 95% CI 0.74–0.94; MCC 0.73, 95% CI 0.60–0.84), surpassing RF (AUC-ROC 0.92, 95% CI 0.88–0.96; sensitivity 0.93, 95% CI 0.89–0.96; specificity 0.68, 95% CI 0.55–0.79; MCC 0.63, 95% CI 0.52–0.73). SVM, DT, and NB exhibited progressively lower predictive ability (Table 4). Figure 4 presents the ROC curves for the five trained models. While all classifiers, except for the DT, achieve AUCs greater than 0.83, CatBoost consistently occupies the upper-left region of the curve, achieving the largest AUC of 0.93 and demonstrating a notably steeper ascent at lower false-positive rates. This enhanced sensitivity–specificity trade-off supports the overall performance metrics, indicating that CatBoost discriminates IDA more effectively than the other models.

Discussion

Summary of principal findings

Five ML classifiers were evaluated for the detection of IDA using routine haematological indices, with CatBoost emerging as the top performer. Through stratified ten-fold cross-validation, CatBoost achieved an AUC-ROC of 0.98, outpacing other classifiers: RF at 0.97, SVM at 0.90, DT at 0.88, and NB at 0.91. On the external test set, CatBoost retained robust discrimination, achieving an AUC-ROC of 0.93 (95% CI 0.89–0.97), a sensitivity of 0.92 (95% CI 0.88–0.96), a specificity of 0.86 (95% CI 0.74–0.94), and a MCC of 0.73 (95% CI 0.60–0.84). These results provide additional evidence for the model’s robustness when applied to high-dimensional CBC data. They also align with recent studies indicating that ML can pair high discriminative performance with clinical interpretability [45, 46].

The cohort originated from a tertiary referral hospital, resulting in an IDA prevalence of approximately 79%. This prevalence reflects consecutive enrolments from obstetric, anaemia, and paediatric haematology clinics in which iron status evaluations are routinely conducted. Nationally representative estimates from NHANES 2017–2020 indicate that absolute iron deficiency occurs in 34% of women aged 18–50 years and 3% of age-matched men, yielding an average prevalence of about 14% in the adult population [47]. Because predictive values vary with prevalence, recalibration of the reported sensitivity and specificity, either through threshold adjustment or model retraining, is required before deploying the model in community screening settings.

Interpretation and clinical relevance

CatBoost meets WHO benchmarks for anaemia screening while minimising both false-negative and false-positive referrals. Maintaining this balance is critical, as distorted trade-offs can lead to increased downstream morbidity and healthcare utilisation [48]. Previous ensemble approaches that integrated multiple CBC indices, rather than individual biomarkers, achieved AUC-ROC values of 0.87 in unselected cohorts and 0.93 following the inclusion of reticulocyte haemoglobin equivalent (Ret-He) [49–51]. The present findings extend this approach by yielding a fully interpretable model suitable for clinical implementation.

SHAP decomposition indicated that the model’s predictions are based on physiologically relevant variables. HGB and HCT contributed approximately 30% of the total predictive weight, consistent with WHO diagnostic thresholds and providing reassurance that the algorithm aligns with established pathophysiology [6, 7, 20]. RDW-CV and reticulocyte maturity indices (LFR, IRF, HFR) effectively captured anisocytosis and delayed cytoplasmic maturation at the pre-anaemic stage [7, 8, 13, 50, 51]. Granulocyte and platelet measures (IMG%, PDW, IPF) further refined risk estimation, reflecting compensatory thrombocytosis and left-shifted granulopoiesis [7, 8, 19]. Taken together, these findings illustrate how explainable AI can translate early bone marrow adaptations into clinically actionable diagnostic insights across diverse populations [43].

Comparison with previous studies

Gradient-boosting frameworks continue to outperform alternative learners on structured clinical data [45, 52]. CatBoost’s native handling of categorical and ordered features routinely yields higher accuracy and better generalisability than RF or XGBoost [28]. In Ethiopian antenatal cohorts, CatBoost surpassed DT, RF and XGBoost for anaemia detection [16]; comparable gains have been reported in paediatric leukaemia [46] and thalassaemia-trait differentiation [29]. Although RF achieved strong discrimination, its specificity (0.68) lagged behind CatBoost (0.86), generating more false-positive referrals in low-prevalence settings. An equivalent specificity gap has been observed in cardiology and musculoskeletal imaging when features are heterogeneous [53, 54]. Simpler models such as DT fail to capture non-linear interactions among erythroid, myeloid and platelet variables [14, 55]. Well-calibrated ensemble models can, moreover, detect iron deficiency before HGB concentrations decline, underscoring the clinical value of early AI-assisted screening [43, 51].

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

The model’s strengths include its potential for external validation, interpretability through SHAP values, and adherence to WHO guidelines for the diagnosis of anaemia, ensuring benchmark sensitivity and specificity.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the high prevalence of IDA (79%) reflects a tertiary-care referral bias, which may affect predictive performance in community or primary care settings; threshold recalibration or model retraining may be required for these populations. Second, the dataset predominantly comprises women of reproductive age (68%), limiting generalisability to elderly men or patients with chronic kidney disease, who commonly present with anaemia of inflammation. Third, although ferritin was used as the reference standard, its role as an acute-phase reactant may reduce accuracy; incorporating additional biomarkers such as soluble transferrin receptor or hepcidin could improve ground-truth labelling.

Moreover, the model currently provides only binary classification without quantifying the degree of iron deficiency. Extending this approach to a regression framework could facilitate personalized iron replacement therapy. The absence of external validation further restricts generalizability. Future research should prioritise validation of the model using independent, multicentre cohorts representing diverse populations to ensure broader generalisability and robustness. Additionally, studies should investigate integration into clinical workflows to facilitate early diagnosis, guide treatment decisions, and optimise patient outcomes.

Conclusion

This study developed an interpretable ML model to predict IDA using a rigorously pre-processed dataset of 444 patients. Patients with IDA exhibited lower HGB and ferritin levels, alongside elevated reticulocyte indices, which served as key discriminators for classification.

Among the classifiers evaluated, CatBoost demonstrated the highest predictive performance, achieving an independent test-set AUC-ROC of 0.93 (95% CI 0.89–0.97), with a sensitivity of 92% and specificity of 86%. SHAP analysis identified HGB and HCT as the most influential predictors, with additional contributions from reticulocyte and platelet indices, supporting both model interpretability and physiological plausibility.

These findings suggest that explainable ensemble ML can accurately replicate and extend traditional diagnostic frameworks for IDA. The model’s robust performance and physiologically coherent predictions indicate its potential utility in early detection, risk stratification, and targeted clinical management, illustrating how explainable AI can translate complex haematological patterns into actionable decision support in clinical practice.
