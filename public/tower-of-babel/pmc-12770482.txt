The role of nitric oxide synthase in biofilm formation by Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Yi Huang, Yu Cheng, Yingying Shi, Xiaoqiong Wang, Samira Khodi Aghmiuni, Ayesha Serwat, Zhengliang Zhang, Xueqin Shu, Baolin Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStaphylococcus aureus is capable of colonizing diverse environments and forming biofilms. In this study, we found that the knockout of NO synthase (NOS) impaired biofilm integrity, resulting in weakened biofilms. We found the deletion of the nos reduced intracellular NO levels, which subsequently altered the S-nitrosylation modification levels of the proteome. This alteration was particularly pronounced in the S-nitrosylation modification of the cysteine residue at position 12 of the MgrA. This modification reduced the binding affinity of MgrA to the promoter of the acetyltransferase gene (icaA), resulting in reduced levels of poly-N-acetyl-β-(1–6)-glucosamine (PIA), a key component of biofilms formed by S. aureus, thereby further weakening biofilm formation. Consequently, we conclude that NOS in S. aureus S-nitrosylates the MgrA through the synthesis of endogenous NO. This process strengthens the interaction between MgrA and the icaA promoter (PicaA), thereby enhancing the synthesis of major polysaccharides in biofilms and promoting biofilm formation.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Microbiology

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus is a significant human pathogen and was recognized by the World Health Organization as one of the six ESKAPE pathogens (Enterococcus faecium, S. aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp.)1,2. This bacterium can cause a wide spectrum of infections, ranging from superficial skin infections to severe conditions, such as endocarditis and sepsis3,4. Studies indicate that S. aureus intermittently colonizes the skin and nasal cavities of approximately 60% of healthy individuals, while 20–25% experience continuous colonization5–7. Colonization by S. aureus is closely linked to infection and can manifest in various forms. Notably, chronic infections that are difficult to eradicate are often associated with S. aureus biofilm formation. This bacterium can colonize human tissues, such as bones and heart valves, through biofilm development, leading to conditions like osteomyelitis and endocarditis. Furthermore, S. aureus can establish biofilms on medical implants, including catheters, artificial joints, and pacemakers. Such infections often require surgical removal of the implants and extended hospitalization, which significantly increases the cost of treating S. aureus infections8–11.

A biofilm is a community of microorganisms that adhere to either biotic or abiotic surfaces. Bacteria within biofilms produce an extracellular matrix (ECM) composed of proteins, polysaccharides, and extracellular DNA (eDNA). This matrix encases the bacteria in a protective, sticky layer that enhances their resilience to environmental stress. When bacteria are released from the matrix, they can secrete substantial amounts of virulence-related factors, leading to acute infections. This characteristic adds to the challenges of treating biofilm-associated infections12–14. Studies have revealed that the deletion of mgrA significantly affects biofilm formation in S. aureus via both PIA-dependent and PIA-independent pathway15,16. This regulatory effect stems from the dual role of mgrA: it not only directly enhances the expression of the ica operon but also modulates upstream regulatory factors. For instance, mgrA suppresses the expression of rbf, thereby indirectly promoting the activation of the ica operon and facilitating the formation of biofilms17. Recent research has shown that nitric oxide (NO), the gas signaling molecule, plays a role in biofilm dispersion, which is linked to the concentration of cyclic di-GMP (c-di-GMP) within cells18,19. However, some studies have indicated that low concentrations of NO can promote the formation of bacterial biofilms. The mechanisms underlying this enhancement remain unclear.20–22. Additionally, prior research has indicated that the concentration of NO synthesized by S. aureus is relatively low, with the bacterium’s NOS serving as one of its primary source of endogenous NO23–25. Consequently, we utilized NOS to investigate how low-concentration NO enhances biofilms and to explore the regulatory mechanisms involved26–29. NO can react with free sulfhydryl groups in proteins, leading to S-nitrosylation, which modifies protein function. This S-nitrosylation can influence various processes, including bacterial virulence, antibiotic resistance, and the ability to withstand oxidative stress. For example, it has been shown to enhance the virulence of Vibrio cholerae30, increase vancomycin resistance in S. aureus31, and help Escherichia coli cope with oxidative stress32. Research has demonstrated that bacterial NOS can significantly inhibit biofilm formation in Staphylococcus epidermidis by downregulating key targets, including fibrinogen-binding protein (Fbe), accumulation-associated protein (Aap), IcaR, sigma factor B (SigB), and IcaA. Additionally, bacterial NOS not only negatively modulates biofilm-forming capacity but also effectively impairs the dispersal potential of established biofilms33–37. Previous studies have shown that NOS is crucial in regulating various physiological processes in S. aureus. For example, nos can be co-transcribed with pdt, which encodes a prephenate dehydratase that contributes to resistance against oxidative stress. When NOS is deleted, the bacteria’s ability to evade the immune system is weakened, resulting in reduced abscess formation in mice31,38. However, the specific mechanism by which NOS affects S. aureus biofilm formation has not been previously reported. In this study, we demonstrated that S. aureus XN108 can enhance biofilm formation via NOS, and this enhancement is mediated through S-nitrosylation. S-nitrosylation has been shown to improve the binding affinity between the bacterial global regulatory factors MgrA and PicaA, which subsequently increases the expression level of acetyltransferase IcaA. This increase in IcaA expression promotes the PIA synthesis, ultimately leading to enhanced bacterial biofilm formation.

Results

nos affects the structure of the biofilm

To investigate the regulatory role of NOS in biofilm formation, we first constructed the nos knockout strain Δnos from the vancomycin-intermediate Staphylococcus aureus (VISA) XN108 and assessed biofilm formation ability after 24 h using crystal violet staining. The results indicated that knocking out nos significantly weakened S. aureus biofilm formation (Fig. 1B, C), while the presence or absence of nos did not significantly impact bacterial growth at 24 h (Fig. 1A). This suggests that NOS does not influence biofilm through bacterial growth, but rather affects biofilm structure. To confirm this, we introduced the plasmid pALC (Table S1), encoding green fluorescent protein (GFP), into the bacteria and examined the biofilm structure using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) to observe 24-h biofilm (Fig. 1D, E). Figure 1H showed that the knockout of nos significantly weakened the biofilm structure. In particular, Fig. 1F reveals that the biofilm architecture of the Δnos strain was markedly looser in Δnos than that of the WT strain. Furthermore, the thickness and volume of the biofilm were also significantly reduced (Fig. 1G, I), while the knockout of nos did not substantially affect the bacterial growth (Fig. 1A). This finding suggests that a reduction in the biofilm matrix among the bacteria within Δnos destabilizes the biofilm structure, ultimately leading to its weakening.

NOS regulates the production of polysaccharide intercellular adhesin (PIA)

As previously noted, a biofilm comprises bacteria and a secreted matrix. The primary components of the S. aureus biofilm matrix include PIA, surface adhesion proteins (CWA proteins), and eDNA39,40. The biofilm structure is closely associated with the ECM secreted by bacteria41, which consists of extracellular polysaccharides, proteins, and eDNA. In S. aureus, the main component of the biofilm matrix is the poly-N-acetyl-β-(1–6)-glucosamine, also known as PIA42. Our above results indicated that NOS has an impact on biofilm matrix synthesis (Fig. 1D–I). To determine whether NOS affects PIA in the biofilm matrix, which contributes to biofilm structure, we conducted an RNA-seq analysis on the S. aureus WT and Δnos strains. The RNA-seq results revealed a significant downregulation of genes involved in PIA synthesis, particulary icaABC, which is known to be involved in PIA synthesis and transport following nos knockout (Fig. 2A, B, Table S3). In contrast, genes associated with protein synthesis in the biofilm, such as fnbA, clfA, sasG, and ebh43–45, did not exhibit significant changes, suggesting that nos knockout primarily affected PIA content rather than protein components within the biofilm (Fig. S1). We then extracted PIA from both WT and Δnos strains and performed dot hybridization detection, which confirmed that nos knockout led to a significant reduction in PIA content (Fig. 2C, D). Additionally, we constructed an overexpression plasmid in pRMC2 (Table S1) for icaABC and transformed it into the Δnos strain. Following induction with anhydrotetracycline (ATC), we found that the overexpression of icaABC in the WT strain resulted in a significant enhancement of biofilm formation. In contrast, in the Δnos strain, the overexpression of icaABC not only restored the impaired biofilm but also led to its excessive accumulation (Figs. S2 and S7), suggesting that NOS-mediated biofilm regulation is associated with the ica operon. As this phenomenon can be attributed to the critical role of icaABC in PIA biosynthesis, these findings suggest that NOS can regulate biofilm structure by modulating bacterial PIA content. Given that PIA synthesis is substantially reduced in the absence of NOS, we conclude that NOS can enhance biofilm structure by promoting PIA content, thereby enhancing biofilm integrity.

NOS enhances the binding of MgrA to the icaA promoter (PicaA)

Since NOS lacks a DNA-binding domain, it cannot directly bind to the icaA promoter46. To understand how NOS regulates icaA expression, we first conducted a DNA pull-down assay, which identified several upstream regulatory factors of icaA (Fig. S3, Table 1 and Fig. S4). Among these factors, we focused on MgrA, a known regulator of icaA, and observed that NOS knockout significantly reduced the binding affinity of MgrA to the PicaA (Fig. 3B, C). Notably, the expression levels of mgrA remained unchanged (Fig. 3A), suggesting that NOS enhances the ability of MgrA to bind to PicaA independently of its expression levels.

S-nitrosylation of MgrA enhances biofilm formation

Since NOS does not modulate the binding affinity of MgrA by altering its expression (Fig. 3A), and given that NOS is associated with NO production, which is known to affect protein S-nitrosylation, we assessed the NO levels in the strain and observed a significant reduction in the Δnos strain (Fig. S4). NOS likely affects MgrA function via NO-mediated S-nitrosylation. Previous collaborative research with Shu demonstrated that NOS influences the S-nitrosylation modification of MgrA31. In this study, western blot analysis further confirmed this finding (Fig. S5). Notably, upon NOS knockout, other modifications like oxidation and carbamidomethylation on MgrA showed minimal changes, while S-nitrosylation was completely abolished (Table S5). This indicates that NOS primarily modulates MgrA function via S-nitrosylation. To investigate whether the S-nitrosylation of MgrA regulates biofilm formation, we conducted a molecular docking analysis to simulate the binding of the S-nitrosylation site of the MgrA protein to PicaA following the removal of its S-nitrosylation modification (Fig. S6). The results showed that the binding sites of MgrA and PicaA shifted and the binding energy between these two proteins decreased after the loss of the S-nitrosylation modification. Previous studies have shown that MgrA can positively regulate the expression of icaA17, indicating that the weakening of the biofilm in Δnos is likely due to the loss of S-nitrosylation, which subsequently reduces the binding affinity of MgrA to PicaA. To further validate this observation, we used the previously constructed XN108 mgrA(C12S) strain, in which the 12th cysteine residue of the MgrA protein was replaced with serine, leading to a loss of S-nitrosylation capability31,47. Our results showed that this mutation did not significantly affect bacterial growth (Fig. 4C), but it eliminated S-nitrosylation of the MgrA protein (Table S6) and notably weakened biofilm formation (Fig. 4D, E), similar with the effects observed in Δnos (Fig. 1B, C). To further examine how MgrA S-nitrosylation influences biofilm formation, we used CLSM to visualize the biofilms. The results demonstrated a significant weakening of the biofilm in the mgrA(C12S) strain (Fig. 4F, G, J). This weakening led to a notable reduction in both biofilm thickness and volume (Fig. 4I, K), as well as an increase in biofilm roughness. These findings were consistent with those observed in the previous Δnos strain (Fig. 1D–I). Overall, these results suggest that the S-nitrosylation of MgrA plays a regulatory role in biofilm formation by influencing its matrix synthesis.

S-nitrosylation can enhance the binding ability of MgrA to PicaA

Overexpression of the icaABC genes in the mgrA(C12S) strain led to a significant increase in biofilm formation (Figs. S7A and S7B). In contrast, deletion of the icaA gene resulted in a substantial reduction in biofilm biomass in the WT strain, whereas the biofilm of the mgrA(C12S) strain remained largely unchanged (Fig. S7C and S7D). These results indicate that icaABC is the target through which the MgrAC12S protein regulates biofilm formation. Subsequently, we knocked out nos in the mgrA(C12S) strain and found that neither the expression levels of icaABC nor the total biofilm biomass showed significant alterations (Figure S8). This finding demonstrates that the S-nitrosylation of MgrA acts as the downstream effector through which NOS regulates biofilm formation.

Then, we conducted RNA-seq assay and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) on the WT and mgrA(C12S) strains. Our analysis showed a significant reduction in the expression of acetyltransferase (IcaA), which is crucial for PIA synthesis (Fig. 5A–C). Importantly, MgrA S-nitrosylation did not significantly affect its own expression (Fig. 5B), consistent with findings from the NOS knockout strain in our previous studies. Furthermore, the results of PIA in WT and mgrA(C12S) strains indicate that following the loss of S-nitrosylation in MgrA, there is a decrease in PIA production (Fig. 5D and E). These findings demonstrated that the S-nitrosylation modification of MgrA can effectively regulate the expression of icaA. To explore the mechanisms, we expressed and purified both the WT-MgrA protein and the MgrAC12S protein. Our results demonstrated that the promoter-binding ability of the MgrAC12S protein to the icaA promoter was significantly reduced compared to the WT-MgrA protein (Fig. 5F, G). The LC-MS/MS analysis revealed that the WT-MgrA protein underwent S-nitrosylation, whereas the MgrAC12S mutant did not (Table S6). As a result, the binding ability of MgrA to PicaA was diminished following the loss of S-nitrosylation modification, resulting in the downregulation of icaA expression. This result aligns with our prior research findings. Consequently, we conclude that NOS can enhance the binding affinity of MgrA to PicaA through the S-nitrosylation modification induced by NO, which is synthesized by the bacteria itself. This process subsequently increases the expression level of icaA, ultimately leading to an enhancement in PIA synthesis within the biofilm matrix.

NOS regulates biofilm formation through S-nitrosylation modification of MgrA in S. aureus NCTC8325

Since NOS is ubiquitously distributed among various S. aureus with exceptionally high sequence conservation, XN108 is both a VISA and a methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA). For this reason, we selected the methicillin-sensitive S. aureus(MSSA) NCTC8325 to improve the general applicability of our research. As a well-characterized MSSA strain commonly used in S. aureus research, NCTC8325 allows for greater generalizability of our findings. As illustrated in Fig. S9, both MgrA and IcaA proteins are present in NCTC8325 with remarkable sequence homology, suggesting that the NOS-mediated biofilm regulation mechanism through S-nitrosylation of MgrA may also operate in this strain. Our findings demonstrate that nos deletion in NCTC8325 had no significant impact on bacterial growth (Fig. 6A), however, it substantially impaired biofilm formation (Fig. 6B, C). When observing samples under CLSM, the biofilm of the Δnos mutant strain appeared as sporadic fluorescent spots rather than the continuous biofilm seen in the WT strain (Fig. 6E, F). This observation indicated that the deletion of nos significantly weakened the biofilm, leading to a substantial reduction in bacterial adhesion and cohesion. As a result, only scattered bacterial cells were observed, rather than bacterial clusters connected by polysaccharides. qRT-PCR analyses revealed that NOS deficiency led to significant downregulation of ica operon genes (Fig. 6D), indicating that NOS regulates biofilm formation in NCTC8325 by modulating ica operon expression. Furthermore, comparative analysis of the MgrA protein in the WT and Δnos strains showed markedly reduced binding affinity of MgrA to the ica operon promoter in the mutant strain (Fig.6G, H). Given the high conservation of MgrA between NCTC8325 and XN108 (Fig. S9), the observed impairment in promoter binding capacity in NCTC8325 can be similarly attributed to the loss of S-nitrosylation modification at the MgrA Cys12 residue. In conclusion, our results demonstrate that NOS enhances biofilm formation not only in VISA but also in MSSA strains through a conserved mechanism: S-nitrosylation of MgrA strengthens its binding to the icaA promoter, upregulates icaA expression, promotes PIA synthesis, and consequently enhances the biofilm-forming ability of bacteria.

S-nitrosylation, rather than serine mutations, results in alterations in biofilm formation and protein binding capacity

The serine mutation of MgrA, rather than S-nitrosylation, may influence the structural integrity of MgrA, resulting in experimental outcomes consistent with our findings. Therefore, we mutated the cysteine residue of MgrA to alanine and examined the biofilm of the mutant strains. The results showed that mutating the cysteine residue of MgrA to alanine led to reduced biofilm formation ability in the mutant strain. Consistent with the serine mutant strain, the expression of icaA in the alanine mutant strain was significantly downregulated, and the binding affinity of the MgrAC12A protein to PicaA was also moderately reduced (Fig. S9). The diminished binding affinity of MgrA to PicaA following a mutation is attributable to the absence of S-nitrosylation rather than the serine mutation, as the alanine mutation produces a similar effect. This suggests that MgrA S-nitrosylation rather than serine mutation, enhances icaA expression and PIA synthesis by improving MgrA’s binding affinity to PicaA, thereby reinforcing biofilm formation.

Discussion

Host-derived NOS and NO are recognized as key players in regulating drug resistance in S. aureus48–50. NO produced by host immune cells exhibits broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against various pathogens and inhibits the expression of virulence factors, such as Hld and PSMs in staphylococci by suppressing the bacterial agr operon and quorum sensing (QS) system, thereby attenuating staphylococcal virulence47,51. Conversely, bacterial-derived NO has been shown to enhance bacterial oxidative stress tolerance by protecting against reactive oxygen species (ROS) toxicity, thereby promoting immune evasion, antibiotic resistance, and ultimately improving bacterial survival52. Given these contrasting roles, understanding the interplay between host cell-derived NOS/NO and bacterial biofilms is critical for advancing therapeutic strategies.

Previous studies have reported that NOS also affects drug resistance in S. aureus52,53 and modulates sensitivity to oxidative and heme stress54. In addition, NOS regulates electron transfer to maintain membrane bioenergetics and plays a vital role in aerobic respiratory metabolism and cell physiology24,55. These findings suggest that bacterial NOS or NOS-derived NO is essential for regulating various physiological phenotypes of S. aureus, highlighting the importance of further exploration into NOS regulatory mechanisms. Furthermore, NOS can synthesize NO, NO-mediated S-nitrosylation could broadly impact other physiological processes, warranting further investigation in future research.

The study reveals that bacterial NOS modifies the global transcriptional regulator MgrA through S-nitrosylation. This post-translational modification significantly enhances the binding affinity of MgrA to the promoter region of the acetyltransferase gene icaA. The resulting upregulation of icaA increases poly-N-acetylglucosamine (PIA) synthesis, ultimately promoting robust biofilm formation (Fig. 7). To abolish S-nitrosylation of MgrA, we replaced the cysteine residue subject to this modification with serine and alanine. Both mutations led to a significant reduction in biofilm formation and decreased the binding affinity of MgrA to the icaA promoter (Figs. 4, 5, and S10), indicating that the loss of S-nitrosylation in MgrA impairs biofilm formation. Given that S-nitrosylation is modulated by NOS, we further demonstrated that the NOS inhibitor L-NAME effectively inhibits biofilm formation in S. aureus (Fig. S11). L-NAME-mediated inhibition of NOS reduces NO production, thereby attenuating protein S-nitrosylation56–59. Overall, these results confirm that NOS regulates biofilm formation through the S-nitrosylation of MgrA.

Molecular mechanisms through which nitric oxide synthase (NOS) regulates biofilm formation.

S. aureus has evolved NOS to produce nitric oxide (NO). In the presence of NOS, MgrA undergoes S-nitrosylation of cysteine thiols, which activates transcriptional enhancement signals for downstream genes, such as icaA, promoting polysaccharide synthesis and resulting in biofilm thickening. The icaA, encoding a glucosaminyltransferase, is responsible for synthesizing poly-N-acetylglucosamine (PIA), which is the main component of the biofilm matrix. PIA, also known as intercellular adhesin, is a crucial structural component of biofilms.

Importantly, this regulatory mechanism is not restricted to the vancomycin-intermediate strain (VISA) XN108, but is also conserved in the methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) strain NCTC8325 (Fig. 6). These findings demonstrate that this NO-mediated regulatory cascade is widely conserved in S. aureus: nitric oxide synthase (NOS) enhances biofilm synthesis through S-nitrosylation of the transcriptional regulator MgrA, which strengthens its binding affinity to the icaA promoter, upregulates ica operon expression, and ultimately promotes poly-N-acetylglucosamine (PIA) production. Consequently, combining NOS inhibitors with other antibiotics may represent a promising strategy for clinically managing severe S. aureus infections. However, as L-NAME is also a potent inhibitor of mammalian NOS60, further research is necessary to develop NOS inhibitors specific to bacterial enzymes.

In summary, this study provides valuable insights suggesting that bacteria may exploit host NOS to enhance biofilm synthesis during infection, presenting a promising avenue for future research and potential advancements in antibiotic treatment strategies.

Methods

Bacterial strains, plasmids, and growth conditions

The bacteria and plasmids used in this study are detailed in Table S1. XN108 is a clinically validated vancomycin-resistant S. aureus (VISA) strain. S. aureus and Escherichia coli strains employed in this research were stored at −80 °C and were inoculated, activated, and subcultured prior to experimental use. S. aureus strains were grown in tryptic soy broth (TSB; 211825, Difco) at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm or on TSA plates (TSB with 15 g/L of Agar A). E. coli strains were cultured in LB medium (10 g tryptone, 5 g yeast extract, and 5 g NaCl per liter) or on LA plates (LB with 15 g/L of Agar A) at 37 °C, also with shaking at 220 rpm. When necessary, antibiotics were added to the culture medium at the following concentrations: 150 μg/mL of ampicillin sodium salt, 50 μg/mL of kanamycin sulfate, and 15 μg/mL of chloramphenicol.

Construction of mutants and overexpression strains of S. aureus

We created mutant strains and overexpression strains using the temperature-sensitive plasmid pBTs and overexpression plasmid pRMC2 based on previously described protocols61,62. DNA fragments upstream and downstream of the mutation site were amplified from the genomic DNA of the XN108 WT strain. The primers nos-up-F-KpnI/nos-up-R and nos-down-F/nos-down-R-SacI were used to knock out the nos gene. The primers pRicaABC-F-KpnI/pRicaABC-R-HpaI were used to overexpress the icaABC genes. To introduce a point mutation, converting the cysteine at position 12 of MgrA to serine (alanine), the primer pairs mgrA(C12S)(A)-up-F-KpnI/mgrA (C12S)(A)-up-R and mgrA(C12S)(A)-down-F/mgrA(C12S)(A)-down-R-SalI were used. The DNA fragments were digested with the specified endonucleases (Thermo Scientific) and cloned into pBTs and pRMC2 using T4 ligase (EL0013, Thermo Scientific). The resulting plasmid was first introduced into E. coli Trans1-T1 for replication at 37 °C, then transformed into S. aureus strain RN4220 for modification, and subsequently into S. aureus strain XN108. Allelic replacement mutants were selected from these transformations. Overnight cultures containing S. aureus plasmids were diluted 1:200 in TSB with 15 μg/mL chloramphenicol and grown at 30 °C, then cultured in antibiotic-free TSB at 42 °C to induce recombination. Cultures incubated at 42 °C were selected, then plated on TSA with 15 μg/mL chloramphenicol, and single colonies were isolated. These colonies were subsequently grown in antibiotic-free TSB at 30 °C for two generations to confirm plasmid loss. Cultures were then diluted and plated on TSA with 100 ng/mL chlortetracycline. Single colonies were again isolated, grown in antibiotic-free TSB, and then cultured in TSB with 15 μg/mL chloramphenicol at 37 °C in 96-well plates. Clones that grew only in antibiotic-free TSB were validated as allelic replacement mutants through PCR and sequencing.

Construction of the protein expression plasmids

As mentioned previously31, to construct the protein expression plasmids pETmgrA and pETmgrA(C12S), the primers pro-MgrA-F-BamHI and pro-MgrA-R-SalI were used to amplify the full-length mgrA and mgrA(C12S) gene fragments from the genomic DNA of the XN108 WT and mgrA(C12S) mutant strains, respectively. The resulting DNA fragments were digested with the specified endonucleases and cloned into the expression vector pET28a (+). The recombinant plasmid was then transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3).

For the construction of the protein expression plasmid pLIMgrA-His, the full-length mgrA gene was amplified from XN108 WT genomic DNA using the primers pro-pLIMgrA-His-R-BamHI and pro-pLIMgrA-His-F-EcoRI. The amplified fragments were digested with specified endonucleases and cloned into plasmid pLI50. The resulting plasmid was initially introduced into E. coli Trans1-T1 for replication, then transformed into S. aureus strain RN4220 for further modification before being introduced into both the XN108 WT and Δnos strains.

Growth curves

As mentioned63, for the S. aureus XN108 WT strain, the nos knockout strain Δnos, and the nos complement strain Cnos, single colonies were inoculated into fresh TSB medium and cultured overnight. The overnight cultures were then transferred to Erlenmeyer flasks containing 20 ml of fresh TSB, adjusted to an initial OD600 of 0.05, and shaken at 220 rpm at 37 °C, with OD600 measurements taken hourly over a 12 h period.

Biofilm formation and analysis

Biofilm formation was evaluated using a microplate assay as described previously15. Briefly, overnight cultures were diluted 1:200 in fresh TSB and inoculated in triplicate into flat-bottom 96-well polystyrene plates (Costar), with 200 μl per well. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, wells were gently washed three times with water to remove unattached cells, stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 30 min, and washed again. Biofilms were visualized by photographing the inverted plates, and biofilm quantification was performed by dissolving the crystal violet stain in glacial acetic acid for 30 min and measuring the OD560 with an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay reader (Bio-Tek).

For CLSM, biofilms were cultivated in 35-mm confocal dishes (Biosharp) and analyzed using a Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope following gentle washing64,65. Various strains were transformed with the GFP plasmid pALC for fluorescence detection and cultured at 37 °C in fresh TSB supplemented with 15 μg/ml chloramphenicol and 0.2% glucose. CLSM was conducted using a 20x panchromatic objective on the Zeiss LSM 980 system (Carl Zeiss, Germany). Fluorescence was excited with a 488 nm argon laser, and Z-stacks were collected at 1 μm intervals with a 515 ± 15-nm emission bandpass filter. Confocal images were acquired using Zeiss ZEN 2012 software, and three-dimensional biofilm images were reconstructed and analyzed with BilfilmQ software66.

PIA extraction and detection

To detect PIA extracted from S. aureus, samples were blotted onto a nitrocellulose membrane (GE Healthcare) according to established protocols67. To prepare cell surface extracts, the cells were cultured in TSB for 24 h, their optical density was measured, and equal amounts of cells were resuspended in 50–200 μl of 0.5 M EDTA (pH 8.0). The cells were then incubated at 100 °C for 5 min and then centrifuged. Following this, 40 μl of the supernatant was treated with 10 μl of 20 mg/mL proteinase K (Sangon Biotech) for 30 min at 37 °C to reduce nonspecific background. The resulting extracts were spotted onto nitrocellulose membranes, dried, and then soaked in a blocking solution (Epizyme). Membranes were incubated in PBS containing 0.8 mg/ml biotin-conjugated wheat germ agglutinin (WGA-biotin; Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature and washed four times with PBS. PIA then was detected using horseradish peroxidase-linked streptavidin followed by chemiluminescence detection (Thermo).

Molecular docking analysis

As described in previous studies68,69, first, we used Swiss-Model to perform homology modeling on known protein sequences, thereby generating a three-dimensional structural model of the protein. Subsequently, we employed the H + + 3 online server for protonation under neutral conditions (pH = 7.0). This step simulated the protonation state of the protein under physiological conditions, enabling a more accurate description of its chemical properties and interactions. We then assigned Amber14SB charges to the proteins using UCSF Chimera software. This process involved calculating charge distributions based on atom types and their surrounding environments to better simulate the protein’s interactions with other molecules. Next, we utilized the professional protein-DNA docking tool HDOCK to conduct molecular docking. Docking serves as a crucial method for studying the interactions between proteins and other molecules, and can reveal the binding partners and functions of proteins within organisms. During the docking process, we applied the experience-based iterative scoring function ITScorePP for both molecular docking and configuration scoring. Finally, we selected the configuration with the highest score and confidence for further analysis. To enhance our understanding and visualization of the protein’s structural characteristics, we employed PyMOL 2.41 software for 3D mapping analysis.

DNA pull-down assay

DNA pull-down analysis was performed as described previously70,71, with minor modifications. Briefly, a biotin-labeled DNA fragment encompassing the promoter region was amplified from the genomic DNA of S. aureus XN108 using the primers Pica-F and Pica-R-biotin. As a control, a DNA fragment corresponding to the icaA promoter sequence was amplified using the same primers. S. aureus cultures grown for 24 h were collected and resuspended in lysis buffer (10 mM) containing a protease inhibitor (Sangon), 1 U/mL DNase I (Promega), and 40 U/mL lysozyme (AMBI). The lysis buffer was composed of HEPES (pH 7.0), 200 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 10 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM dithiothreitol. The resuspension was incubated with shaking at 37 °C until clear, then subjected to ultrasound lysis. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant containing total protein was collected and stored at −80 °C for later use. For binding, 50 µL of Bioeast Mag-SA magnetic beads (Bioeast) were washed three times with ice-cold PBS. After separating the beads from the liquid and discarding the supernatant, the DNA-protein mixture was added to the Bioeast Mag-SA beads. The beads were resuspended and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. Following incubation, the beads were again separated from the liquid, the supernatant was discarded, and the beads were collected. The pellet was resuspended in 30 µL of lysis buffer and boiled for 10 min. The captured proteins were separated using SDS-PAGE and subsequently silver-stained. Protein bands were excised, digested with trypsin, and analyzed by mass spectrometry using a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). The sequence and peptide fingerprint data were analyzed using the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database.

Total RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

As demonstrated in prior research72, overnight cultures of S. aureus were diluted in fresh TSB to an OD600 of 0.05 and grown for 24 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 1 min, followed by treatment with 0.9 mL RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa) and lysis with 0.1 mm silica beads using a FastPrep-24 system (MP Biomedicals). The PrimeScript RT kit (TaKaRa), including a gDNA eraser, was used to remove residual DNA and synthesize cDNA. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on a StepOne real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) with SYBR Ex Taq master mix (TaKaRa). The amount of cDNA detected was normalized to the abundance of hu cDNA73. All qRT-PCR assays were repeated at least three times.

RNA-seq assay

As shown in previous studies74, total RNA was isolated using Trizol Reagent (Invitrogen Life Technologies), and the quality and integrity of the RNA were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent). The Zymo-Seq RiboFree Total RNA Library Kit was used to remove rRNA from the total RNA. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random oligonucleotides and SuperScript III, followed by second-strand synthesis with DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. Remaining overhangs were converted to blunt ends through exonuclease/polymerase activity, and the enzymes were subsequently removed. After adenylation of the 3′ ends, Illumina PE adapter oligonucleotides were ligated to enable hybridization. To select cDNA fragments of the desired length (400–500 bp), library fragments were purified with the AMPure XP system (Beckman Colter, Beverly, CA, USA). DNA fragments with adapters on both ends were enriched using the Illumina PCR Primer Cocktail in a 15-cycle PCR reaction. The products were further purified with the AMPure XP system and quantified using the Agilent high-sensitivity DNA assay on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent). The final sequencing library was then sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

The quality of the raw sequencing data in FASTQ format was first evaluated, followed by data filtering using fastp (v0.22.0) to obtain clean reads. This process involved the removal of adapter-containing sequences, poly-N reads, and low-quality reads. Subsequent analyses were performed exclusively using high-quality clean data. The reference genome and corresponding gene annotation files were retrieved from a public genome database. A genome index was constructed using Bowtie2 (v2.5.1), and the filtered reads were aligned to the reference genome using the same tool (available at: http://bowtie-bio.sourceforge.net/index.shtml). Gene-level read counts were quantified using HTSeq (v0.9.1) to determine baseline gene expression. To ensure comparability of gene expression levels across different genes and samples, expression values were normalized using FPKM (fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq (v1.38.3), with transcripts exhibiting ∣log2FoldChange∣ > 1 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were classified as differentially expressed mRNAs. The results, including volcano plots and MA plots, were visualized using the ggplot2 package in the R language.

Protein expression and purification

As described in previous research31, we purified a total of four proteins: WT-MgrA and Δnos-MgrA from the XN108 strain (Fig. 3), as well as MgrA WT and MgrA C12S expressed in E. coli (Fig. 6).

The WT-MgrA and Δnos-MgrA proteins were protected from light until the labeling reagent was added. The WT strain and Δnos strain containing pLIMgrA-His were constructed for expressing His-tagged MgrA. Overnight cultures of these bacterial cells were diluted into 100 mL of TSB containing 15 μg/mL chloramphenicol to reach an OD600 of 0.05 and then grown at 37 °C for 24 h. Bacteria were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 15 min, resuspended in 10 mL of lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl) with 100 μg/mL lysostaphin (AMBI), and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h with shaking at 220 rpm. Samples were centrifuged at 5000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. The lysate underwent ultrasound treatment for 20 s, followed by incubation on ice for 30 min. After a second centrifugation at 5000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected, and nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid agarose solution (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) was used for protein separation and purification.

The MgrA-WT and MgrAC12S proteins were expressed and purified from E. coli. The BL21 (DE3) strain containing the protein expression plasmids pETMgrA or pETmgrA(C12S) was cultured at 37 °C in LB medium supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin until an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.6–0.8 was reached. The culture was then shifted to 16 °C and induced overnight with 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). Following induction, cells were harvested by centrifugation and lysed via sonication in a lysis buffer consisting of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 300 mM NaCl. Protein purification was performed using a nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid agarose solution (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Eluted proteins were collected with an elution buffer (pH 8.0) containing 200 mM imidazole, 50 mM Tris-HCl, and 300 mM NaCl. Imidazole was removed from the eluent using a Centrifuge Biomax-5 column (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Protein purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE, and protein concentration was determined via a BCA assay using bovine serum albumin as the standard.

S-nitrosylated protein detection by western blotting

As mentioned in previous studies, S-nitrosylated proteins were detected using an S-nitrosylation Western blotting kit (Thermo), with modifications based on previous studies31,47. Prepare a protein sample at a concentration of 1.0 mg/mL. For each 200 μL sample, add 7.5 mL of HENS buffer, 480 μL of 25% SDS, and 160 μL of 2 M methyl methanethiosulfonate (MMTS). Vortex the mixture vigorously for 1 min and incubate at 50 °C for 90 min, vortexing every 5 min to block free cysteine thiols. Precipitate the protein by adding six volumes of acetone prechilled to −20 °C, and freeze the mixture at −20 °C for at least 1 h to remove MMTS. Subsequently, centrifuge the samples at 10,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, wash three times with 2 mL of acetone, and resuspend in 50 μL of HENS buffer. To label S-nitrosylated proteins, add 1 μL of the labeling reagent and 2 μL of 1 M L-sodium ascorbate to the sample, then incubate at 30 °C with shaking for 90 min. To stop the labeling process, precipitate the protein by adding six volumes of prechilled acetone and freeze at −20 °C for at least 1 h. Resuspend in 40 μL of HENS buffer. The S-nitrosylation levels of proteins at equal concentrations were assessed by western blotting. S-nitrosylated proteins were identified using an anti-tandem mass tag (TMT) antibody (Thermo) at a 1:1000 dilution, and total protein levels were measured with an anti-His antibody (BOSTER) at a 1:5000 dilution.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

As mentioned in previous studies75, for binding assays, 3′-FAM-tagged DNA fragments containing the promoter region were amplified from the genomic DNA of S. aureus XN108 and incubated with varying amounts of MgrA in 8 μL of lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl) for 1 h. After incubation, 2 μL of gel loading buffer was added, and the mixture was run on a 3.5% native polyacrylamide gel in 1× Tris-borate-EDTA buffer. Bound bands were detected using the Amersham Typhoon NIR (GE) and analyzed with ImageQuant TL 8.1 software, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

LC-MS/MS

As mentioned76, for identifying the MgrA protein in S. aureus, data were collected using the Ultimate 3000/Q Exactive HF-X high-resolution liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry system.

Chromatographic separation: solvent A is 0.1% formic acid in water, and solvent B is 80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The liquid chromatography gradient is as follows: 0–2 min, 4–8% B; 2–35 min, 8–28% B; 35–55 min, 28–40% B; 55–56 min, 40–95% B; 56–66 min, 95% B; flow rate, 600 nL/min. Chromatography column: ACQUITY UPLC HSS C18 Column (100 Å, 1.8 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm).

Mass spectrometry acquisition parameters: primary mass spectrometry settings include a resolution of 60,000, an AGC target of 3e6 with a maximum IT of 20 ms, and a scan range of 300–1800 m/z. Secondary mass spectrometry parameters include a resolution of 15,000, an AGC target of 5e4, a maximum IT of 22 ms, a top N of 40, and an NCE/stepped NCE of 30.

For the identification of the MgrA protein in E. coli, data were collected using the Easy-nLC 1200/Orbitrap Exploris 480 high-resolution liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry system.

Chromatographic separation: Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water, and Mobile phase B was 80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The liquid chromatography gradient was set as follows: 0–2 min, 4–8% B; 2–35 min, 8–28% B; 35–55 min, 28–40% B; 55–56 min, 40–95% B; 56–66 min, 95% B. The flow rate was 600 nL/min. The column used was a 75 μm i.d. ×25 cm NanoViper C18 (1.9 μm, 100 Å).

Mass spectrometry acquisition: The first-level mass spectrometry parameters were set to a resolution of 120,000, AGC target as standard, maximum IT of 20 ms, and a scan range of 300–1800 m/z. The second-level mass spectrometry parameters included a resolution of 15,000, an AGC target, a standard, a maximum IT of 22 ms, and an NCE/stepped NCE of 30.

Database searches were conducted against the NCBI S. aureus strain XN108 database, containing 2992 entries, along with a reverse decoy database. Trypsin/P was selected as the cleavage enzyme, allowing up to two missed cleavages. The precursor ion mass tolerance was set to 20 ppm for the first search, while the fragment ion mass tolerance was 0.02 Da. The carbamoyl methyl group on cysteine was designated as a fixed modification. Variable modifications included carbamidomethyl (C), oxidation (M), acetyl (protein N-term), and -SNO (C). The false discovery rate was set to less than 1%, and the minimum peptide score was set to greater than 40.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Details on the number of biological replicates and statistical methods are provided in the figure legends. Group significance was assessed using a t-test, with all error bars representing SEM. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of ≤ 0.05.
