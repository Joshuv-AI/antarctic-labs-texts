Mechanistic insights into alarm therapy for nocturnal enuresis: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Sofie Axelgaard, Michelle Werenberg Ginnerup, Luise Borch, Laura Bech Polcwiartek, Birgitte Ryom Nielsen, Eva Bech Raaberg, Niels Henrik Bruun, Søren Hagstrøm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe gold standard treatment for nocturnal enuresis includes alarm treatment, although the exact mechanism behind its effectiveness remains uncertain. The aim of this study was to investigate whether the time to alarm activation progressively increases during treatment, and to explore whether alarm treatment causes a transition from enuresis episodes to nocturia.MethodsWe included children with nocturnal enuresis undergoing successful alarm treatment from 01.01.2016 – 10.01.2023 at Aalborg University Hospital, Denmark. Data, including alarm times and episodes of nocturia, was obtained using standardized forms and bladder diaries and analyzed using Kaplan–Meier plots and Poisson regression.ResultsThe study included 141 children, with 50% achieving dryness after 42 days of treatment and 25% within 31 days. The time to first alarm increased by 37.4 min [95% confidence interval: 10.8–64.08] within the first 6 weeks of treatment.The proportion of children experiencing nocturia reduced from 40% in the first week of treatment to 28% in the final week. An inverse relationship was observed between pre-treatment maximum voided volume and treatment duration.ConclusionA substantial proportion of children who underwent alarm treatment achieved complete dryness within a short period after initiating therapy. During treatment, enuresis episodes occurred progressively later in the night, and no shift from enuresis to nocturia was observed. Introduction

Nocturnal enuresis (NE) is a prevalent condition among children, impacting 15–20% of 5-year-olds and 5–10% of 7-year-olds [1]. NE is categorized into monosymptomatic NE (MNE) and non-monosymptomatic NE (NMNE), the latter being more frequently linked to small bladder capacity [2].

The exact pathophysiology of NE remains unclear, but evidence points to a mismatch between nocturnal urine production (NUP) and bladder storage capacity, along with disturbed sleep arousal [2]. Though physically benign, NE can cause emotional distress, impacting daily life, mental health, self-esteem, and family dynamics, ultimately reducing the child’s overall quality of life [3, 4].

NE treatment includes desmopressin, an antidiuretic hormone analogue, and alarm treatment, depending on the underlying pathophysiology [2, 5, 6]. Desmopressin is preferred for nocturnal polyuria, while alarm treatment proves more effective for children with small bladder capacity [2].

The moisture-activated enuresis alarm conditions the child to wake upon urination by sounding an alarm. Previous studies suggest a 50–80% efficacy for alarm treatment [7–9]. Despite its effectiveness, alarm therapy can present practical challenges, including occasional device malfunction, difficulties in consistently waking the child, and potential disruption to other family members’ sleep.

Despite many studies on alarm treatment for NE, its precise mechanism remains unclear. Behavioral conditioning and changes in arousal response likely contribute, as the treatment lowers arousal thresholds and links bladder fullness to waking [10]. Some studies suggest this may increase functional bladder capacity (FBC) [11–13]. However, whether this allows children to retain their full nighttime urine volume or shifts enuresis to nocturia remains unclear [14]. Previous research relies on pre- and post-treatment bladder diaries, but, to our knowledge, only few studies have examined real-time changes during the night in children undergoing alarm treatment.

This study aimed to examine whether the time to alarm activation progressively increases during treatment and to explore whether alarm treatment leads to a transition from enuresis episodes to nocturia in children successfully treated with the alarm.

Methods

Study design and population

Starting in 2016, caregivers of children with NE undergoing alarm treatment at Aalborg University Hospital, Denmark, were asked to document alarm times and nocturia instances throughout the entire treatment period using a standardized form provided by the department (Table 1, Supplementary Information [SI]). These data were used for a retrospective cohort study of children who underwent alarm treatment from 01.01.2016—01.10.2023. Eligible participants were children diagnosed with NE according to ICCS criteria [2, 15], identified through ICD-10 codes DR329 (urinary incontinence) or DR329A (enuresis nocturna), and who had been prescribed alarm treatment for NE.

Some children were on stable concomitant medication for NE prior to inclusion (desmopressin, solifenacin, or mirabegron); medication regimens were unchanged during the study. Children aged 5–15 years were included if they achieved 14 consecutive dry nights and were excluded if their follow-up charts were incomplete, reporting was irregular, or if treatment required initiation of new pharmacological therapy alongside alarm treatment.

Registers, covariates, and definitions

A clinical database was created in REDCap [16], capturing sex, age, NE predisposition, enuresis type, and prior treatments obtained from electronic medical records (NordEPJ, Systematic, Aarhus, Denmark). Before starting treatment, families completed a bladder diary to determine the maximum voided volume (MVV) and nocturnal urine production (NUP), adjusted for expected bladder capacity (EBC) based on age. Small bladder was defined as a MVV below 65% of EBC, and polyuria was defined as NUP above 130% of EBC [2]. Bladder diary results guided treatment decisions at the study centre: alarm therapy was offered to children with normal or low NUP, whereas desmopressin was generally favoured for children with increased NUP. In some cases, children commenced alarm therapy after discontinuation of desmopressin due to limited effect, while in other cases physicians continued desmopressin and used alarm treatment as an add-on.

As an adjunctive measure during the study, all children were advised to restrict evening fluid intake (with ~ 2/3 of daily fluids consumed before 16:00). Timed daytime voiding and constipation treatment were not newly initiated as part of alarm therapy; children continued these interventions only if they had already been established prior to the study.

The Rodger alarm system was used, with families submitting forms every third week. In line with Danish recommendations, caregivers were instructed to sleep in the same room as the child throughout treatment and were responsible for recording alarm activations and nocturia episodes. Treatment continued until achieving 14 consecutive dry nights [2].

The quantity of alarm activations and episodes of nocturia, along with their respective timing, was obtained from the completed forms. For timing analyses, bedtime was assumed to be 20:00 for all children, as exact sleep onset was not recorded.

Outcome

Outcomes included the time to achieve 14 consecutive dry nights, the time from bedtime to the first alarm, and the occurrence of nocturia episodes.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were reported as counts with percentages and compared using the chi-squared test. Continuous variables were reported as medians with 25th and 75th percentiles. A Kaplan–Meier plot illustrated treatment duration, and restricted mean success time (RMST) was reported by gender, age groups, and MNE status. The effect of MVV/EBC was analyzed using regression with robust variance estimation. Nighttime was defined from 20:00 until the first alarm or 08:00 and visualized via cumulative incidences on days 1, 14, and 28. Mixed regression estimated the average increase in nighttime duration before an alarm over six weeks, while mixed Poisson regression estimated the relative incidences of alarm plus nocturia and nocturia alone. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. All analyses were performed using STATA 18 (StataCorp, 2023).

Ethics approval

This research study was conducted retrospectively using data obtained for clinical purposes. The study was authorized by the Hospital management at Aalborg University Hospital and registered on the list of ongoing research projects in the North Denmark Region (ID-number: 2023–038469).

Results

We identified a total of 315 children who were subscribed to alarm treatment during the study period (Fig. 1).

Study cohort flow diagram

A total of 174 children (55%) were subsequently excluded leaving us with 141 children accessible for data analysis.

Demographic findings and clinical characteristics

Demographic data and clinical characteristics for the study population are presented in Table 1.

The majority were males with a median age of 8.1 years at the time of diagnosis. The median age at initiation of alarm treatment was 9.0 years, corresponding to an interval of 0.9 years. This reflects that children with NMNE were first treated for constipation and/or daytime urinary incontinence, and those with nocturnal polyuria were typically offered desmopressin before alarm therapy. A minor delay may also occur due to waiting time for device availability.

Duration

Twenty-five percent of participants achieved complete dryness within 31 days, while half attained complete dryness within the first six weeks of treatment (Fig. 2).

Kaplan–Meier plot for having 14 consecutive days with no alarm

The median time to achieve dryness was 5.0 weeks [95% CI: 4.0–6.43] for females and 6.43 weeks [95% CI: 5.57–7.14] for males (p = 0.48). No age difference in the time to successful treatment was observed. Children with NMNE achieved dryness as fast as children with MNE (Fig. 2).

An inverse association between MVV/EBC and treatment duration was observed, with treatment duration decreasing by 1.7 days for every 0.1 increase in MVV/EBC (Fig. 3).

Progression of alarm treatment

The course of alarm treatment was assessed by the estimated probability of having an alarm during the night at 1 st night compared to 14th night and 28th night of treatment as shown in Fig. 4.

Cumulative incidence plots for having an alarm during the night at different nights

On the first night, the alarm tended to activate around midnight, and by 6 am, there was an 80% probability that the alarm had gone off. In total, the probability of having an alarm at 1 st night was 90%. After 14 days of treatment, the likelihood of the alarm activating during the night had decreased to 35%, and it dropped further to 17% after 28 days.

Moreover, we observed an increase of 6.24 min per week (slope = 0.104 h, 95% CI: [0.030–0.178]) in the time between bedtime and the expected time for the first alarm during the first six weeks of treatment (p = 0.002; Fig. 5).

Nocturia

The total number of nighttime voids decreased significantly from 669 in the first week to 97 in the last week of treatment (p < 0.001). The number of nocturia episodes remained nearly the same, with 100 episodes in the first week and 97 in the final week (p = 0.83). However, the number of children experiencing at least one episode of nocturia per week decreased from 57 children (40.4%) during the first week of treatment to 39 children (27.7%) in the final week of treatment. Discussion

In this carefully selected cohort of children with NE who fully responded to alarm treatment, 50% achieved complete dryness within the first six weeks and 31% by day 31, both defined as 14 consecutive dry nights. Alarms were triggered progressively later at night, and there was no evidence that alarm treatment caused a shift from enuresis to nocturia. Additionally, we observed an inverse association between baseline MVV and the duration of treatment.

Demographics and baseline characteristics

The male-to-female ratio of 1.6:1 aligns with previous studies [17, 18]. We found that 35.5% of children had NMNE, almost similar to reports from Butler et al. [19] (31.5%) and Apos et al. [18] (29%). The slightly higher NMNE rate may reflect our secondary center setting, where children with complex incontinence unresponsive to desmopressin are referred [20]. The participants in our study were selected for alarm treatment based on bladder diary results; favoring it for children with small bladder capacities who often have daytime urinary issues.

At the time of diagnosis, we observed an MVV/EBC of 0.7 [IQR: 0.5–0.8] and an NUP/EBC of 0.8 [IQR: 0.7–1.0]. NUP and MVV were largely balanced, with a slight tendency toward higher NUP, indicating that mismatches between these measures likely did not explain enuretic episodes.

However, Borg et al. [21] showed that children with MNE and normal daytime MVV may still experience frequent enuretic episodes, suggesting that MVV may not always correlate well with nighttime bladder capacity in NE cases.

Enuresis alarm treatment duration

Fifty percent of the children achieved complete dryness within 42 days of treatment, shorter than the mean of 62.1 ± 30.8 days reported by Apos et al. [19] in their large audit of 2861 medical records. Differences in treatment approaches likely explain this variation. In Australia, alarms are the first-line treatment, while in Denmark treatment is tailored to underlying NE pathophysiology.

Treatment duration did not depend on age or MNE/NMNE status, consistent with Apos et al. [19]. However, their study found a higher success rate for MNE compared to NMNE after one treatment period [19].

Importantly, we observed that lower pre-treatment MVV/EBC was associated with longer treatment durations. This finding is clinically relevant for setting family expectations about treatment timelines and reinforces the role of bladder capacity in alarm treatment outcomes.

Progression of alarm treatment

During the first six weeks of treatment, we observed a gradual delay in the first enuresis episode after sleep onset and a reduction in the number of children experiencing nocturia. The progressive delay in first alarm activation – averaging 6.24 min per week – parallels the “first uninterrupted sleep period” (FUSP) described by Karamaria et al. in desmopressin-treated children [22]. Incorporating this concept in future alarm studies may facilitate direct comparison and improve understanding of early-night dynamics.

Without post-treatment NUP data, we cannot determine whether the delay reflects increased nocturnal bladder capacity (NBC) or reduced NUP. Prior work suggests alarm therapy does not alter NUP [11], but may increase both daytime and nighttime bladder capacity [11–13], although evidence is limited by small sample sizes. Importantly, the observed delay may also result from altered arousal–micturition dynamics—where successful awakening and inhibition of voiding extend the interval to the first wetting episode—resulting in an increase in NBC. In the study by Larsson et al. [23] that used a smartphone application for enuresis registrations, no systematic change in enuresis latency or differences between responders and non-responders was seen within the first 4 weeks of alarm treatment. The discrepancy with our findings may reflect differences in patient selection, adherence, and methodology, but it also highlights the complexity of interpreting enuresis latency and underscores the need for prospective studies combining continuous monitoring of nocturnal voiding, arousal, and NUP to clarify underlying mechanisms.

Nocturia

Nocturia is common in children, reported in 35% of treated NE cases by Goessaert et al. [24], and in 31.6% of children in a population-based study by Warner et al. [25]. A slightly higher prevalence of nocturia, around 40%, has been observed in adults and tends to increase with age [26]. Previous studies investigating if childhood NE leads to nocturia in adulthood point towards different directions [11, 14, 27] demonstrating the need for further studies addressing this relationship.

In our study, children with nocturia episodes decreased from 40.4% in the first treatment week to 27.7% in the final week. A finding emphasizing that alarm treatment does not seem to lead to a shift of enuresis to nocturia episodes, thus a direct connection between enuresis and nocturia can be questioned. This is in line with Larsson et al. [23], who reported that nocturia episodes remained low and did not differ significantly between responders and non-responders during the first four weeks of alarm therapy.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has limitations. It was retrospective and observational, with a potential risk of selection bias, as 32% of children were excluded due to incomplete registration forms. Data collection compliance decreased over time, possibly underestimating treatment duration. Additionally, the absence of post-treatment bladder diaries prevents us from evaluating changes in MVV/EBC and NUP/EBC induced by alarm treatment. Furthermore, non-responders to alarm treatment were not included due to incomplete diaries, preventing direct comparison and limiting assessment of treatment effects in this group.

However, the study has notable strengths. Nightly data throughout alarm treatment enabled precise tracking of treatment progress. All patients underwent standardized assessments at a single centre, ensuring consistent use of the Rodger alarm and minimizing treatment variability. The cohort study design with prospective follow-up reduced information bias in alarms and nocturia episodes.

Conclusion

Half of the children achieved complete dryness within six weeks, and 25% were fully dry after 31 nights, both outcomes defined as 14 consecutive dry nights. Enuresis episodes occurred progressively later at night. Nocturia did not increase during treatment, and its prevalence remained comparable to population rates.

The observed delay in enuresis episodes may suggest an increase in FBC and could equally reflect altered arousal–micturition dynamics. Given the lack of post-treatment diaries, this interpretation must be made with caution, underscoring the need for prospective studies with detailed monitoring of bladder function and NUP.

Finally, reduced baseline bladder capacity was associated with longer treatment duration, a clinically relevant finding that may help set realistic expectations for families regarding treatment timelines and outcomes.
