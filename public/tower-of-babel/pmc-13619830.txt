Molecular detection and genetic characterization of Giardia duodenalis in temple stray cats in Bangkok, Thailand
Authors: Vy Thi Tuong Le, Pornkamol Phoosangwalthong, Minh-Trang Thi Hoang, Tawin Inpankaew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionGiardia duodenalis is a ubiquitous enteric protozoan infecting humans, domestic animals, and wildlife worldwide. In Thailand, high densities of stray animals represent an important management challenge. However, molecular epidemiological data regarding stray cats remain limited. This study estimated the prevalence of G. duodenalis, characterized the identified genetic assemblages, and explored factors associated with PCR positivity in temple stray cats in Bangkok, Thailand.MethodsFecal samples were collected from 500 stray cats residing across 43 temples in 24 districts of Bangkok. Detection of G. duodenalis was performed using nested PCR targeting the small subunit ribosomal RNA (SSU rRNA) gene. Positive samples were further characterized by genotyping at the glutamate dehydrogenase (gdh) and triosephosphate isomerase (tpi) loci. Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using the Maximum Likelihood method based on the Tamura 3-parameter model. Associated factors were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsThe overall prevalence of G. duodenalis was 10.8% (54/500; 95% CI: 8.4–13.8%), with positive cases identified in 12 of the 24 surveyed districts and 19 of the 43 sampled temples. Eight isolates were successfully genotyped, identifying assemblage B (sub-assemblage BIV, n = 4), assemblage F (n = 3), and assemblage A (sub-assemblage AI, n = 1). The absence of human contact was identified as an exploratory variable associated with higher odds of PCR positivity (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 8.80; 95% CI: 2.15–35.98; P = 0.002), while demographic factors (age, sex), geographic zone, and deworming history showed no statistically significant associations.DiscussionAssemblages previously reported in humans and various animals were detected among a small number of successfully genotyped isolates, while the source, direction of transmission, and the reservoir status of temple stray cats could not be determined.

Introduction

Giardiasis is a widespread intestinal parasitic infection of significant veterinary and public health concern, recognized as one of the most common waterborne and foodborne diseases (Jerez Puebla et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2023). It is estimated that 200 million people are infected globally (Certad et al., 2017). Giardiasis transmission can occur through the fecal-oral route, either directly via contact with feces from infected humans or animals, or indirectly through the consumption of food and water contaminated with infective cysts (Ballweber et al., 2010; Dixon, 2021). Clinical manifestations range from asymptomatic to acute or chronic infection, with common symptoms including diarrhea, weight loss, abdominal pain, and malabsorption (Jerez Puebla et al., 2017; Kurnosova et al., 2024).

G. duodenalis is the only species within the genus Giardia known to infect both humans and a wide variety of domesticated and wild mammals (Guadaño Procesi et al., 2022; Sprong et al., 2009). Despite morphological similarities, molecular genotyping has classified the species into eight distinct genetic assemblages (A-H) (Kraft et al., 2017; Rahimi et al., 2023; Zajaczkowski et al., 2021). Assemblages A and B are considered zoonotic, infecting a broad range of hosts including humans and various animals (Cai et al., 2021; Tangtrongsup et al., 2020), whereas assemblages C–H exhibit host specificity. Assemblages C and D primarily infect canids, assemblage E is found in domestic and wild ungulates, assemblage F mainly infects felines, and assemblages G and H are commonly found in rodents and seals, respectively (Ryan et al., 2021).

Sub-assemblages have been further characterized within the zoonotic assemblages A (AI, AII, and AIII) and B (BIII and BIV) (Feng and Xiao, 2011; Piekara-Stępińska et al., 2020; Ryan and Cacciò, 2013). Sub-assemblage AI is considered zoonotic (Faria et al., 2017; Hernández et al., 2019), and AII mainly infects humans but retains zoonotic potential (Ryan and Cacciò, 2013), while AIII is primarily restricted to wild hoofed animals (Cacciò et al., 2008; Feng and Xiao, 2011; Iwashita et al., 2020). Sub-assemblages BIII and BIV are commonly found in humans (Zajaczkowski et al., 2021) and have also been reported in various animal hosts (Sprong et al., 2009). The broad host range of G. duodenalis increases the risk of cross-species transmission, making human giardiasis a continuing public health concern (Hatam-Nahavandi et al., 2025).

Companion animals may play a role in the zoonotic transmission dynamics of giardiasis (Sun et al., 2023). Globally, G. duodenalis infection in cats has an estimated pooled prevalence of 14.5% (Sun et al., 2023). Notably, cats can harbor not only the feline-adapted assemblage F but also the zoonotic assemblages A and B, and occasionally canine-adapted assemblages C and D (Li et al., 2024; Saleh et al., 2019; Yun et al., 2023). Thailand has a large population of abandoned and stray cats (Phoosangwalthong et al., 2023). They often lack veterinary attention and are exposed to extensive environmental contamination, increasing their susceptibility to parasitic infections (Phoosangwalthong et al., 2022). These stray cats frequently reside in public areas, particularly temples, facilitating contact with humans and other animals. This close contact with humans and shared environment make this population highly relevant for molecular epidemiological investigation.

In Thailand, G. duodenalis has been reported in humans (Kitvatanachai et al., 2021; Pipatsatitpong et al., 2018; Siripattanapipong et al., 2011; Tungtrongchitr et al., 2010), livestock (Inpankaew et al., 2015; Khurajog et al., 2014), wildlife (Sricharern et al., 2016; Tangtrongsup et al., 2019), companion animals, including dogs (Inpankaew et al., 2007; Khine et al., 2021; Rojekittikhun et al., 2014; Tangtrongsup et al., 2017, 2020), and cats (Jitsamai et al., 2021; Rojekittikhun et al., 2014; Tangtrongsup et al., 2020), as well as exotic pets (Kamyingkird et al., 2022). Despite these reports, epidemiological information on G. duodenalis infection in cats in Thailand remains limited. To date, few studies have investigated G. duodenalis infection in owned and sheltered cats in Thailand, while stray cat populations remain uninvestigated. Among the existing studies, only one performed molecular characterization (Tangtrongsup et al., 2020), whereas the remaining studies relied solely on microscopic examination or coproantigen detection without genotyping (Jitsamai et al., 2021; Rojekittikhun et al., 2014).

The present study aimed to determine the prevalence of G. duodenalis in temple stray cats in Bangkok, Thailand, characterize the identified genetic assemblages using molecular methods, and explore factors associated with PCR positivity.

Materials and methods

Study area and sample collection

A cross-sectional study was conducted from June to December 2015 among temple stray cats in Bangkok, Thailand. Temple stray cats were defined as unowned cats residing within the temple grounds, comprising both semi-domesticated and feral cats. Based on a stratified random sampling method, 43 temples that host an accessible population of stray cats were randomly selected from 24 districts, with one to three temples per district. A minimum sample size was calculated using the formula n=N1+N(e)2 (Yamane, 1967), assuming a large population size (N = 90,000) with a 5% margin of error (e = 0.05). To account for potential sample loss or degradation, the target was increased to 500 samples. Samples were collected from 2 to 25 cats per temple based on convenience sampling. Fecal samples were collected individually from the rectum of each cat and sampled cats were temporarily marked to ensure each cat contributed only one sample. Samples were immediately placed into labeled plastic bags, and maintained at 4 °C during transportation to the Department of Parasitology, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Kasetsart University.

A structured questionnaire was used to interview the monks or animal caretakers to collect information on age, sex, deworming history, human contact, and sampling zone. Human contact was operationally defined by the cat’s habitual, daily behavioral tolerance toward people. Cats were classified as “yes” if they accepted routine feeding, physical handling, or close proximity to caretakers. Cats were classified as “no” if they exhibited strictly feral behavior, actively avoided human approach, and relied exclusively on scavenging.

This study protocol was approved by the Kasetsart University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee under the Ethical Review Board of the Office of the National Research Council of Thailand (Approval No. ACKU60-VET-006). Informed consent was obtained from the monks or animal caretakers before sample collection.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from approximately 200 mg of each fecal sample using the E.Z.N.A.® Stool DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek Inc., Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and eluted in 100 µL of elution buffer. DNA concentration and purity were measured using a BioSpectrometer (Eppendorf AG, Hamburg, Germany) and stored at -20 °C until further analysis.

Molecular detection

A nested PCR assay targeting the small subunit ribosomal RNA (SSU rRNA) locus was utilized for the initial screening of G. duodenalis (Hopkins et al., 1997; Read et al., 2002). Positive samples were subsequently characterized by amplifying the glutamate dehydrogenase (gdh) (Cacciò et al., 2008) and triosephosphate isomerase (tpi) loci (Sulaiman et al., 2003) to identify specific assemblages and sub-assemblages. PCR amplifications were performed in a final volume of 25 µL. The reaction mixture for the SSU rRNA assay consisted of 16.8 µL of nuclease-free water, 2.5 µL of 10× PCR buffer, 0.75 µL of MgCl2 (50 mM), 0.5 µL of dNTPs (10 mM), 1.0 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 0.2 µL of i-Taq™ DNA Polymerase (5 U/µL; iNtRON Biotechnology, Seongnam, South Korea), 1.25 µL of DMSO, and 1.0 µL of template DNA (genomic DNA for the primary round, or the primary amplicon for the secondary round). For the gdh and tpi assays, the reaction mixture was identical except that DMSO was not included, and the volume of nuclease-free water was adjusted to 18.05 µL. A confirmed G. duodenalis-positive DNA sample was included as a positive control, and nuclease-free water was included as a negative control in each PCR run. The secondary PCR products were visualized by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel.

Sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

The positive amplicons were purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and submitted to AITbiotech (AITbiotech Pte. Ltd., Singapore) for sequencing. Chromatograms were visually inspected, and consensus sequences were generated using SnapGene® Viewer version 8.2.2 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA). Sequences were compared with reference sequences available in the GenBank database using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST; https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). Multiple sequence alignments and phylogenetic analyses were performed using MEGA 12 (Kumar et al., 2024). A Maximum Likelihood tree was constructed based on the Tamura 3-parameter substitution model with 1,000 bootstrap replicates.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Prevalence was estimated with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using the Wilson score method. To evaluate factors associated with PCR positivity, univariable analysis was performed using Pearson’s Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Variables with P < 0.25 in the univariable analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Cluster-adjusted analyses were attempted but failed to converge due to sparse data. Standard multivariable logistic regression was retained. Final results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% CIs. Associations were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Prevalence of G. duodenalis in stray cats

Based on nested PCR amplification of the SSU rRNA gene, the overall prevalence of G. duodenalis in the sampled stray cat population was 10.8% (54/500; 95% CI: 8.4–13.8%). The parasite was detected in 12 of the 24 surveyed districts (50.0%) and in 19 of the 43 sampled temples (44.2%). The highest prevalence was observed in Bang Sue district at 30.0% (3/10; 95% CI: 10.8–60.3%), followed by Khlong Sam Wa at 27.6% (8/29; 95% CI: 14.7–45.7%) and Sai Mai at 27.3% (9/33; 95% CI: 15.1–44.2%) (Table 2, Figure 1).

Sequence analysis and genotyping of G. duodenalis

Representative SSU rRNA sequences showed 100% identity to the G. duodenalis reference sequence KT595651. Among the 54 positive samples, 7.4% (4/54) and 11.1% (6/54) were successfully genotyped at the gdh and tpi loci, respectively. Overall, genotyping was achieved for eight isolates: two isolates were characterized at the gdh locus only, four isolates at the tpi locus only, and multilocus genotyping data were available for two isolates.

Assemblage B (sub-assemblage BIV) was identified in 50.0% (4/8) of the genotyped isolates across the gdh (n = 2; 99.37–99.59% identity to OM116001) and tpi (n = 2; 99.59–100% identity to ON037509) loci. Assemblage F was detected in 37.5% (3/8) of the isolates, with two isolates at the gdh locus (99.79–100% identity to EF507608.1) and three isolates at the tpi locus (99.38–99.59% identity to AB569392.1), including the two multilocus-characterized isolates. Assemblage A (sub-assemblage AI) was found in a single isolate (12.5%) at the tpi locus (99.58% identity to ON037510).

Complete concordance was observed between BLAST sequence identities and phylogenetic analyses. The sequences generated in this study clustered with their respective reference sequences for sub-assemblages AI and BIV, and assemblage F (Figures 2, 3). Factors associated with PCR positivity of G. duodenalis

Discussion

G. duodenalis is among the most common intestinal protozoa in humans and a diverse range of animals (Adam, 2021; Feng and Xiao, 2011). Globally, it has been reported in cats across 23 countries with a pooled prevalence of 14.5% (Sun et al., 2023). In this study, the prevalence of G. duodenalis in stray cats was 10.8% (54/500; 95% CI: 8.4–13.8%). This prevalence is comparable to findings from Australia at 10.1% (Yang et al., 2015), Southern China at 9.8% (Zheng et al., 2015), and Spain at 9.2% (Gil et al., 2017). While lower prevalences have been reported in Iran at 1.5% (Karimi et al., 2023), South Korea at 3.8% (Kwak and Seo, 2020), and parts of China at 2.0–5.8% (Li et al., 2019; Li et al., 2024; Pan et al., 2018), considerably higher infection rates are documented with prevalences reaching 17.9% in Germany (Sommer et al., 2018), 25.0% in Mexico (Veyna-Salazar et al., 2023), 27.9% in Jordan (Mukbel et al., 2024), and 29.9% in Bangladesh (Nahar et al., 2026). This significant variation in prevalence is also documented in Thailand. A study reported a high prevalence of 27.3% (Tangtrongsup et al., 2020), whereas significantly lower infection rates were found at 3.9% (Jitsamai et al., 2021) and 0.3% (Rojekittikhun et al., 2014). Methodological differences may explain these discrepancies. Tangtrongsup et al. (2020) employed a highly sensitive combination of diagnostic techniques, including zinc sulfate centrifugal flotation, immunofluorescent assay (IFA), and molecular analysis. This approach can yield a higher prevalence than studies relying solely on coproantigen ELISA testing (Jitsamai et al., 2021) or conventional microscopic examination (Rojekittikhun et al., 2014). In addition to diagnostic sensitivity, the prevalence of G. duodenalis depends on the studied population, environmental conditions, and geographic locations (Enemark et al., 2020; Piekara-Stępińska et al., 2020; Tangtrongsup et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021).

Determining the G. duodenalis assemblage and sub-assemblage is crucial for understanding its zoonotic potential (Guadaño Procesi et al., 2022; Zajaczkowski et al., 2021). While the multi-copy SSU rRNA gene provides superior sensitivity, its highly conserved sequences are primarily utilized for confirming the presence of G. duodenalis DNA rather than resolving specific sub-assemblages (Jerez Puebla et al., 2017; Zajaczkowski et al., 2021). Achieving high-resolution genotyping requires highly discriminatory single-copy genes, including gdh and tpi (Thompson and Ash, 2016; Zajaczkowski et al., 2021). In this study, assemblages B (n = 4), F (n = 3), and A (n = 1) were detected in the surveyed temple stray cats. While the host-adapted assemblage F is predominantly reported in cats globally (Ito et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2023), zoonotic assemblage B emerged as the most frequently detected assemblage among successfully genotyped isolates in this study, followed by assemblage F. This finding aligns with recent epidemiological data from stray cats in Jordan and South Korea (Mukbel et al., 2024; Yun et al., 2023). This apparent similarity should be interpreted cautiously due to potential differences in study populations, sampling strategies, and genotyping methods. The detection of zoonotic assemblages A and B is consistent with findings from various regions, including Germany (Sommer et al., 2018), Japan (Iijima et al., 2018), Spain (Barrera et al., 2026), and Bangladesh (Nahar et al., 2026).

Within assemblages A and B, all successfully genotyped isolates in this study belonged to sub-assemblages AI and BIV, respectively, both of which are considered to have high zoonotic potential (Sprong et al., 2009). Among felines, AI is frequently documented, occasionally followed by AII (Barrera et al., 2026; Iijima et al., 2018; Li et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2015). While the broader assemblage B is frequently reported in dogs and other animals, assigning feline isolates to a specific sub-assemblage is rarely reported, though Barrera et al. (2026) previously identified sub-assemblage BIII in Spanish cats. This limitation is largely attributed to the high genetic polymorphism within assemblage B, which frequently results in double peaks in sequence chromatograms, making definitive identification highly challenging (Zajaczkowski et al., 2021). The detection of sub-assemblages AI and BIV in this study carries particular epidemiological relevance in Thailand, where both sub-assemblages have been found among humans, domestic animals, and wildlife (Kamyingkird et al., 2022; Pipatsatitpong et al., 2018; Siripattanapipong et al., 2011; Tangtrongsup et al., 2019, 2020; Tungtrongchitr et al., 2010). Furthermore, a case of human infection with the feline-adapted assemblage F has been reported in the country (Kotepui et al., 2025). The identification of these assemblages mirrors prior findings in Bangkok temple communities, where concurrent anthroponotic, zoonotic, and dog-specific cycles have been previously proposed (Traub et al., 2009). Although previous epidemiological studies in the region identified cat contact as a risk factor for human giardiasis (Boontanom et al., 2011), the current cross-sectional data cannot determine if the sampled cats actively contribute to zoonotic transmission. As stray cats naturally congregate and share common soil or sand areas for defecation within temple grounds, it is theoretically possible that environmentally robust cysts may accumulate in these communal sites (Barrera et al., 2026; Hatam-Nahavandi et al., 2025). However, genotyping data alone is insufficient to definitively confirm the source of infection or active cross-species transmission. Confirming a zoonotic cycle requires further epidemiological investigation into food, water, and environmental contamination, combined with genetic comparisons of isolates from humans and animals living in the same communities (Cai et al., 2021).

In the surveyed temple stray cat population, the absence of human contact was identified as an exploratory factor associated with G. duodenalis PCR positivity. This association may be influenced by socioecological, environmental, and nutritional factors. Stray cats that avoid human contact might exhibit more feral behaviors, potentially relying on scavenging and hunting wild prey. These prey animals, including rodents, which have been reported to harbor assemblages A, B, and F (Li et al., 2023), and reptiles, identified as hosts for zoonotic assemblage B in Thailand (Sutthikornchai et al., 2025), represent plausible sources of exposure. Scavenging activities could theoretically increase the likelihood of exposure to environmental sources that might be contaminated with infective cysts, such as water and soil (Li et al., 2023). In contrast, human interaction may serve as a proxy variable for caregiving. Temple cats that tolerate human contact might be fed by monks and local people, potentially receiving better food and cleaner drinking water. This caregiving could reduce their environmental exposure and reliance on hunting. Furthermore, nutrition can modulate susceptibility to G. duodenalis (Gutiérrez and Bartelt, 2024), as inadequate protein intake has been linked to increased susceptibility to giardiasis (Silva et al., 2009). Since these specific scavenging, caregiving, environmental, and nutritional variables were not directly evaluated in this study, they remain untested hypotheses that require further investigation.

Study zones showed no statistically significant association with G. duodenalis PCR positivity, consistent with previous studies (Gil et al., 2017; Karimi et al., 2023; Nahar et al., 2026). In this study, G. duodenalis was detected across 12 districts and multiple zones. This widespread distribution, combined with the free-roaming behavior of these stray cats, may explain the zone-independent pattern. Deworming history was not associated with PCR positivity, corroborating findings that are widely reported (Nahar et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2015). The absence of an association can be attributed to the broad timeframe over which treatments were recorded and potential misclassification of the specific anthelmintics administered (Le et al., 2026). In our study, age and sex were not significantly associated with G. duodenalis PCR positivity. This demographic-independent pattern has been widely documented for age (Gil et al., 2017; Guadaño Procesi et al., 2022; Li et al., 2019; Nahar et al., 2026; Piekara-Stępińska et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2023) and sex (Gil et al., 2017; Guadaño Procesi et al., 2022; Nahar et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2023; Veyna-Salazar et al., 2023; Yun et al., 2023). Although some studies have reported age and sex as statistical predictors for G. duodenalis infection, the literature remains highly inconsistent. While kittens are more susceptible to G. duodenalis (Pan et al., 2018; Veyna-Salazar et al., 2023), other research has reported a higher prevalence in elderly cats compared to young cats (Yang et al., 2015). The immunological vulnerability that makes kittens more susceptible to G. duodenalis (Kraft et al., 2017) can be offset by the more frequent roaming activities of adult cats for hunting and mating, likely leading to the lack of an age association (Le et al., 2026). Regarding sex, male cats have been reported to have higher infection rates than females (Li et al., 2019), while another study found females to be more susceptible than males (Lider et al., 2025). These differences likely reflect the variability of the studied populations and environments. Given that stray cats have frequent contact with other animals and contaminated environments, this shared ecological exposure can result in similar PCR positivity rates between both sexes.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Since individual cats within the temples were selected via convenience sampling, the findings should be generalized only to the sampled temple stray cats rather than to the wider stray cat population of Bangkok. The reliance on a single cross-sectional sampling may have underestimated the true prevalence of G. duodenalis due to the intermittent shedding of cysts. Sparse data prevented the convergence of cluster-adjusted models, meaning potential temple-level clustering could not be statistically controlled. Additionally, the exploratory association identified between PCR positivity and the absence of human contact was based on a highly imbalanced subgroup of only 15 cats; the resulting wide confidence intervals necessitate cautious interpretation. The relatively low genotyping success rate for the tpi and gdh loci in this study introduces additional diagnostic uncertainty. Such amplification challenges are well-recognized methodological constraints when amplifying single-copy G. duodenalis genes directly from fecal samples (Barrera et al., 2026), and were likely compounded by prolonged DNA storage in this study. Because of these constraints, although no mixed assemblage infections were detected in the present study, their presence cannot be entirely ruled out. Addressing these diagnostic challenges is critical in Thailand, where molecular techniques are underutilized, restricting the generation of comprehensive epidemiological data regarding G. duodenalis assemblage distribution, which is essential for fully understanding host specificity and transmission sources (Kotepui et al., 2025).

To our knowledge, this is the first molecular epidemiological study of G. duodenalis in temple stray cats in Thailand, providing baseline data on its prevalence and the detection of both host-adapted and zoonotic assemblages. Exploratory analysis identified the absence of human contact as a factor associated with PCR positivity. Future studies incorporating paired sampling across human, animal, and environmental interfaces are necessary to elucidate actual transmission pathways and to evaluate potential One Health interventions.
