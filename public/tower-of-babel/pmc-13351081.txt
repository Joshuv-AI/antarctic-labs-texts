Microbial drivers of ammonium accumulation in Holocene sediments of the Pearl River Delta
Authors: Meiqing Lu, Jiu Jimmy Jiao, Xin Luo, Xiaoyuan Feng, Wenzhao Liang, Shengchao Yu, Yanling Qi, Zhanghua Wang, Hailong Li, Meng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Elevated ammonium concentrations in deltaic groundwater pose a widespread environmental challenge, yet the microbial mechanisms linking depositional history to ammonium dynamics remain poorly understood. The Pearl River Delta, with the highest naturally occurring groundwater ammonium concentrations documented globally, provides a unique natural system to investigate these processes. Here, by integrating geochemical and metagenomic data, we show that fermentation-related genes are the most prevalent across all depositional zones, suggesting fermentation as the potential primary pathway for ammonium production, with the functional potential declining as sedimentary organic matter becomes increasingly recalcitrant with sediment age. Secondary mechanisms shift from nitrate reduction to nitrite ammonification across terrestrial-to-marine-dominated zones, reflecting salinity-driven metabolic partitioning. Notably, the marine-derived genus Brevirhabdus emerges as a key taxon linking depositional history to present-day biogeochemistry, demonstrating remarkable metabolic versatility. These findings demonstrate that paleo-depositional and hydrogeological evolution fundamentally shape microbial landscapes and dictate groundwater quality in deltaic systems worldwide.

Introduction

Delta ecosystems are among the most productive areas on Earth, supporting biodiversity and the livelihoods of over 500 million people worldwide1. Formed at river mouths through complex depositional dynamics, these systems archive sedimentary records that capture the interplay between terrestrial and marine processes over millennial timescales2. Beyond their ecological significance, deltas serve as critical biogeochemical interfaces where land-sea interactions govern elemental cycles3. Many major deltas, including the Red River Delta, Yangtze River Delta, and Pearl River Delta (PRD), underwent significant development during the Holocene in response to post-glacial sea-level changes4,5. The evolution of these deltaic systems represents a complex interplay of multiple forcing factors, including sea-level fluctuations, inherited basin morphology, sediment flux, and fluvial-coastal dynamics, with anthropogenic influences becoming increasingly dominant over the past two millennia2,5,6.

Elevated ammonium (NH4+) in groundwater poses a critical environmental challenge in global deltaic systems7–15. In these settings, groundwater NH4+ concentrations commonly exceed 10 mg L–1 (Fig. S1), substantially above typical inland values and far exceeding the drinking water threshold of 1.5 mg L–1 (Class V, GB/T 14848-2017, China). This geogenic NH4+ is primarily derived from the microbial mineralization of sedimentary nitrogen-containing organic matter (NOM)8,15–19. In oxygen-depleted subsurface environments, microorganisms utilize alternative electron acceptors—nitrate (NO3-), iron (Fe3+), sulfate (SO42-), and carbon dioxide (CO2)—in the order of decreasing thermodynamic favorability while metabolizing NOM (Fig. S2)20–22. When these external electron acceptors become limited, fermentation processes increasingly dominate, providing a critical pathway for NOM degradation21–23. Concurrently, nitrogen cycling processes such as dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium (DNRA) and anaerobic ammonium oxidation (anammox) can significantly modulate NH4+ concentrations (Fig. S2)24,25. These dynamics are shaped by both initial depositional conditions and post-depositional processes25. Despite extensive research on delta systems, critical gaps remain regarding: (1) the specific microbial metabolic pathways regulating NH4⁺ dynamics across varied depositional environments, (2) the key microbial communities mediating these processes, and (3) their adaptive strategies throughout long-term deltaic evolution.

The PRD, located at the northern margin of the South China Sea in southeastern China, provides a unique natural system to address these knowledge gaps. As one of the world’s largest and most dynamic river deltas, it exhibits exceptionally elevated groundwater NH4⁺ concentrations, reaching up to 390 mg L–1 (Fig. 1a)8, markedly surpassing concentrations in typical deltaic systems and representing the highest naturally occurring levels documented globally (Fig. S1). Previous studies have indicated that three synergistic factors underpin this extreme accumulation: (1) organic-rich Holocene sediments provide abundant nitrogen substrates26; (2) rapid Holocene sedimentation rates preserve organic matter from aerobic mineralization27; and (3) low-permeability sediments restrict groundwater flow, fostering the decadal-to-millennial accumulation of NH4⁺8. However, while these geological and hydrological factors provide the framework for NH4⁺ accumulation, the underlying microbial metabolic processes governing NH4⁺ dynamics remain poorly understood. Previous studies have documented the delta’s complex geological evolution during the Holocene: rapid transgression in the early Holocene (~9000–6800 cal. yr BP), a relative sea-level highstand in the mid-to-late Holocene (~6800–2000 calibrated years before present, cal. yr BP), and accelerated coastal progradation in the late Holocene (~2000 cal. yr BP to present)4. Building upon Late Pleistocene tidal river channel deposits, this evolution produced sedimentary facies transitioning from early Holocene transgressive estuarine systems to late Holocene deltaic progradation (Figs. S3–S5)28. Subsequent hydrogeochemical evolution29,30 was superimposed upon these inherited depositional architectures, establishing a pronounced land-sea gradient (Fig. 1b). This gradient is robustly reflected in sediment electrical conductivity (EC, a proxy for groundwater salinity; R² = 0.81, p < 0.001, Fig. S6). We hypothesize that this gradient fundamentally dictates microbial community structure and functional potential, thereby regulating NH4+ dynamics across the deltaic subsurface8,29.

This study integrates geochemical and metagenomic approaches to decipher the microbial mechanisms underlying NH4+ dynamics in the PRD subsurface system, spanning Late Pleistocene tidal river channel deposits and diverse Holocene depositional sequences. Our objectives are to: (1) characterize microbial community composition and functional potential across distinct depositional zones; (2) identify the key metabolic pathways and microbial taxa regulating NH4+ dynamics; and (3) elucidate how microbial communities adapt to changing depositional environments over geological timescales. By integrating these multi-disciplinary data, we aim to reveal the fundamental microbial processes contributing to NH4+ enrichment within the deltaic subsurface, with broader implications for understanding NH4+ cycling in global deltaic systems.

Results and discussion

Land-sea gradients and geochemical characteristics

To investigate the microbial mechanisms contributing to NH4+ enrichment across the land-sea gradient, sediment cores were drilled from three sites in the PRD—Shunde (SD), Huangpu (HP), and Minzong (MZ)—using a contamination-minimizing double-casing drilling system (Fig. S7; Supplementary Information (SI) S1.1). These cores encompass deposits spanning ~13,000 years of deltaic evolution, capturing the transition from Late Pleistocene tidal river channels to Holocene delta plain environments (Figs. S3–S5)28. A total of 36 sediment samples were systematically selected for metagenomic and geochemical analyses based on lithological characteristics and stratigraphic distribution.

Based on depositional environments and contemporary hydrogeochemical characteristics, samples were classified into three zones (Fig. 1b; Supplementary Tables 1–2; Supplementary Data 1–2): (1) the terrestrial-dominated zone (TE: delta plain and delta front, n = 13), characterized by freshwater-dominated advective flow; (2) the transitional zone (TR: delta front slope and tidal river channels, n = 10), characterized by heterogeneous brackish-to-saline conditions reflecting both contemporary and paleo-marine influences; and (3) the marine-dominated zone (MA: prodelta and estuarine bay, n = 13), characterized by saline diffusion-dominated conditions preserving relict marine porewater. This classification synthesizes sediment properties (lithology, grain size, and EC) with groundwater geochemistry (salinity, stable isotopic compositions of oxygen (δ18O) and hydrogen (δ2H)) to distinguish hydrogeological regimes (detailed in SI S2.1-2.2).

Each zone displayed distinct hydrogeological regimes that fundamentally controlled biogeochemical processes (Supplementary Data 1–2; all values reported below are medians). The TE zone represents a freshwater-dominated, advection-driven system, comprising recent terrestrial deposits (< 1500 cal. yr BP). Low EC (0.59 mS cm–1), low salinity (0.47 practical salinity units, PSU), and relatively depleted isotopic signatures (δ¹⁸O: −4.8‰; δ²H: −32.55‰) indicate continuous groundwater renewal through vertical precipitation infiltration and inland lateral recharge29,31. This active circulation maintains freshwater dominance and prevents the accumulation of marine-derived solutes.

The TR zone functions as a complex transition environment characterized by heterogeneous brackish-to-saline hydrochemistry. This zone encompasses two depositional settings with contrasting histories: late Holocene delta front slope deposits (1900–540 cal. yr BP) positioned at the active terrestrial-marine boundary, and Late Pleistocene tidal river channels (~13,000–9800 cal. yr BP). The latter were originally deposited under weak marine influence but were subsequently transformed by the Holocene marine transgression29,31. Despite these contrasting origins, both sub-environments now exhibit brackish-to-saline hydrochemistry reflecting terrestrial-marine interactions, though the underlying mechanisms and timing differ (details in SI S2.2). Notably, the transformed tidal river channels preserve a distinctive geochemical signature where sediment properties retain terrestrial characteristics (Fig. S5) while porewater chemistry indicates marine overprinting. Together, these contrasting depositional processes and post-depositional hydrogeological evolution produce spatially heterogeneous conditions that support diverse microbial metabolisms.

The MA zone preserves a diffusion-dominated environment with minimal advective flow, where restricted circulation enables millennial-scale preservation of relict marine porewater. This zone comprises early to mid-Holocene marine deposits (9800–1800 cal. yr BP), including estuarine bay (9800-4000 cal. yr BP) and prodelta deposits (4000–1800 cal. yr BP) containing abundant marine biogenic indicators (Fig.S5) that record the Holocene transgression4. High EC (9.50 mS cm–1), high salinity (26.81 PSU), and enriched isotopic signatures (δ18O: −1.99‰; δ2H: −14.7‰) are consistent with preserved Holocene marine porewater29, further corroborated by numerical modeling, which shows nearly constant salt mass over the past 6000 years29. Fine-grained sediments severely restrict groundwater flow, creating a regime where advective transport is negligible and diffusion dominates32,33, enabling ancient seawater signatures to persist over millennial timescales and creating isolated, oxygen-depleted microenvironments conducive to anaerobic processes.

Sediment geochemistry revealed systematic variations reflecting the contrasting depositional histories across the three zones (Fig.S8 and Supplementary Data 1; Supplementary Table 1; all values reported below are medians on a dry weight basis). The TE zone exhibited elevated sediment nitrate (NO3-: 1.19 mg kg⁻¹), likely derived from anthropogenic sources delivered via active groundwater recharge29,34,35. In the TR zone, concentrations of sediment NH4⁺ (31.54 mg kg⁻¹), total nitrogen (TN: 0.04%), total carbon (TC: 0.64%), total inorganic carbon (TIC: 0.18%) were notably lower, primarily reflecting coarse-grained, sand-dominated facies of Late Pleistocene tidal river channels, which limited organic matter sequestration36. In contrast, the MA zone was characterized by significantly higher concentrations of sediment NH4+ (123.90 mg kg⁻¹) and sulfate (SO42-: 5.53 g kg⁻¹), with substantial enrichment in TN (0.13%), TC (1.86%), total inorganic carbon (TIC: 0.54%), and total organic carbon (TOC: 1.34%). These elevated concentrations result from fine-grained marine sediments that both promote organic matter preservation and restrict groundwater flow, enabling long-term accumulation of degradation products8. Principal component analysis (PCA) on the sediment geochemical dataset (Fig.S9) demonstrated that the persistent legacies of depositional history and post-depositional hydrogeochemical evolution influenced contemporary biogeochemical patterns over millennial timescales (detailed in SI S2.3).

Groundwater geochemistry revealed complementary hydrogeochemical characteristics (Supplementary Data 2; all values reported below are medians). NH4+ concentrations in groundwater increased progressively from the TE zone (3.59 μmol L⁻¹) through the TR zone (113.34 μmol L⁻¹) to the MA zone (130.02 μmol L⁻¹) (Fig. S10a)29. Notably, groundwater and sediment NH4+ (KCl-extractable NH4⁺ from fresh sediment, including both adsorbed and pore-water pools) showed zone-specific coupling patterns, with the TR zone as a notable exception. In the TR zone, elevated groundwater NH4+ (113.34 μmol L⁻¹) coexists with minimal sediment NH4+ (31.54 mg kg⁻¹). This decoupling reflects heterogeneous lithology within the zone—particularly in the tidal river channel deposits, which exhibit low sediment NH4⁺ (19.35 mg kg⁻¹) but relatively high groundwater NH4+ (111.63 μmol L⁻¹). The coarse sandy texture of these sediments limits organic matter retention36 while enhancing hydraulic conductivity, facilitating advective transport of NH4+-enriched groundwater from overlying units29,31. In contrast, the MA zone exhibits coupled sediment-water behavior where high groundwater NH4+ (130.02 μmol L⁻¹) aligns with substantial sediment NH4+ pools (123.90 mg kg⁻¹), as fine-grained lithology both promotes organic matter preservation and restricts flow, causing NH4+ from in-situ degradation to accumulate through diffusion-dominated transport8,32.

The aromaticity of dissolved organic matter (a254) increased from the TE zone (12.18 m⁻¹) to the TR (108.33 m⁻¹) and MA zones (94.29 m⁻¹) (Fig. S10b), with the TR zone showing the highest values. Despite this spatial variability, a254 and groundwater NH4+ showed a strong overall correlation (R² = 0.90, p < 0.001; Fig. S10c). Rather than implying a direct source relationship, this correlation suggests they track the same progressive diagenetic process: NH4+ accumulates as the product of labile fraction mineralization8, while a254 reflects the relative enrichment of degradation-resistant aromatic compounds37. The TR zone’s elevated a254 was primarily attributed to older Late Pleistocene tidal river channel deposits, where prolonged diagenetic aging of organic matter would have generated highly aromatic DOM.

Groundwater NO3- and SO42- were only detectable in the TE zone (NO3-: 0.03 μg L–1; SO42-: 4.80 mg L–1), falling below detection limits in TR/MA zones. While continuous recharge sustains these electron acceptors in the TE zone, their depletion in TR/MA zones reflects long-term microbial consumption with negligible replenishment due to restricted groundwater flow29. Groundwater ferrous iron (Fe2+) and dissolved sulfide distributions indicated distinct iron-sulfur cycling across zones. At the observed porewater pH conditions (6.39-7.68; Fig. S8m), dissolved sulfide existed predominantly as H₂S and HS⁻. Fe2+ concentrations peaked in the TR zone (6.40 mg L–1) compared to the MA (3.75 mg L–1) and TE (2.35 mg L–1) zones, while dissolved sulfide was highest in the MA zone (0.15 mg L–1) and nearly depleted in the TR zone (0.01 mg L–1). This decoupling—elevated Fe2+ with concurrent dissolved sulfide depletion in the TR zone—indicates iron-sulfide mineral precipitation38. Dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC), methane (CH4), and CO2 in groundwater increased progressively from the TE zone through the TR zone to the MA zone39. A detailed discussion of these hydrogeochemical parameters is provided in SI S2.4.

Post-depositional diagenetic processes substantially modified sediment organic matter characteristics across all zones. Organic matter characteristics reflected complex dynamics shaped by both input sources and post-depositional modification. The δ13C values of TOC in sediments (−29.4‰ to −22.12‰) and TOC/TN ratios (3.1 to 16.18) (Fig. S11a) plotted predominantly within the freshwater particulate organic carbon (POC) field, with smaller portions in transitional and marine POC ranges, indicating dominant freshwater and terrestrial organic matter inputs with minor marine contributions4,28,40. However, lower TOC/TN ratios in the studied sediments compared to other surface sediments in the PRD41–44 indicated significant early diagenetic alteration45,46.

Multiple lines of geochemical evidence indicate progressive diagenetic transformation with burial depth and sediment age. Strong negative correlations between sediment δ13C and both depth (R² = 0.519, p < 0.001; Fig. S11b) and sediment age28,40 (R² = 0.545, p < 0.001; Fig. S12) indicated systematic carbon isotopic shifts during prolonged burial. This trend reflected progressive diagenetic alteration through selective preservation, where 13C-enriched labile components underwent preferential degradation while 13C-depleted recalcitrant compounds became relatively enriched47–50. Supporting evidence includes: (1) increasing dissolved organic matter aromaticity (a254) in groundwater with sediment depth (R² = 0.422, p = 0.006; Fig. S11c)37, reflecting the accumulation of refractory aromatic compounds; and (2) elevated concentrations of DIC, CH4, and CO2 in deeper groundwater (Supplementary Data 2)39, indicating active organic matter degradation51.

The progressive δ13C depletion with sediment depth and age can result from two mechanisms: (1) preferential degradation of 13C-enriched labile components with selective preservation of 13C-depleted recalcitrant components52,53; and (2) incorporation of 13C-depleted microbial necromass54–56. In our system, the observed δ13C trends indicate that selective preservation dominates. Recalcitrant aromatic compounds (e.g., lignin) are typically depleted in 13C by 2-6‰ relative to labile polysaccharides and proteins57. Consequently, the preferential degradation of 13C-enriched labile fractions naturally shifts the bulk residual organic matter toward more negative δ13C values. This mechanism is well-supported by both laboratory incubations demonstrating that extended microbial degradation induces 13C-depletion52 and field observations documenting similar patterns in sedimentary archives53,58. Crucially, the inverse relationship between sediment δ13C and groundwater DOM aromaticity (a254) with depth (Fig. S11b, c) confirms this mechanism, indicating accumulation of 13C-depleted aromatic residues concurrent with exhaustion of 13C-enriched labile substrates.

Microbial community characteristics

Metagenomic analysis recovered 1584 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) with ≥ 50% completeness and ≤ 10% contamination (Supplementary Data 3). After dereplication (clustered at 95% average nucleotide identity)59, we obtained 770 representative MAGs, comprising 682 bacterial and 88 archaeal genomes (Figs. 1c, d, and S13; Supplementary Data 4; SI S2.5).

Bacterial MAGs dominated the recovered genomes (91.45% of recovered MAGs; Fig. S13a), with Pseudomonadota (formerly Proteobacteria) as the predominant phylum. Their relative abundance increased progressively from the TE zone (A: 50.55%; B: 81.13%) through the TR zone (C: 86.22%, F: 92.25%) to the MA zone (D: 92.24%; E: 96.98%) (Figs. 1c and S13d; Supplementary Data 4.8). This dominance pattern across depositional environments aligned with observations from analogous deltaic systems, including the Bengal Delta and Rhône Prodelta, where Pseudomonadota demonstrated adaptability to environmental gradients, particularly organic matter availability19,20. In our system, Pseudomonadota abundance correlated significantly with depth (Spearman correlation: r = 0.76), δ13C (r = −0.74), and EC (r = 0.6) (Fig. S14a), consistent with ecological adaptation to deeper environments characterized by elevated salinity, advanced diagenetic processes, and recalcitrant organic matter60–63.

In contrast, Bacteroidota (3.75%) and Chloroflexota (3.11%), the second and third most abundant phyla, displayed inverse distributional patterns relative to Pseudomonadota (Fig. 1c). Bacteroidota abundance decreased from the TE zone (A: 4.17%; B: 6.71%) through the TR zone (C: 4.61%; F: 2.29%) to the MA zone (D: 2.69%; E: 2.0%) (Fig. 1c), correlating positively with NO3- (r = 0.59) but negatively with depth (r = −0.57) (Fig. S14a). Given that Bacteroidota distribution was significantly influenced by anthropogenic activities64–66 and that shallow sediment NO3- was primarily derived from terrestrial input34,35, this pattern suggests a substantial influence of land-based human activities on Bacteroidota distribution. Similarly, Chloroflexota exhibited higher abundance in the TE zone (A: 13.85%, B: 2.01%) compared to the TR zone (C: 0.61%, F: 0.18%) and MA zone (D: 1.68%; E: 0.31%) (Fig. 1c), correlating positively with δ13C (r = 0.61) but negatively with depth (r = −0.66) and median grain size (D50; r = −0.54) (Fig.S14a). Together, these bacterial phyla demonstrated strong environmental responsiveness, with their distributions primarily shaped by depth, organic matter characteristics, the land-sea gradient, and anthropogenic inputs.

While bacterial communities showed such pronounced environmental responses, archaeal communities displayed markedly different patterns reflecting distinct ecological strategies. Archaeal MAGs (8.55% of total MAGs; Fig.S13a) were dominated by Thermoproteota (average relative abundance: 85.95% within archaeal communities, mainly Bathyarchaeia: 80.10%) (Figs. 1d and S13b, S13c; Supplementary Data 4.7 and 4.9). Bathyarchaeia were commonly associated with marine anoxic sediments67,68. Unlike the clear terrestrial-marine gradient observed in bacterial phyla, Thermoproteota exhibited more complex spatial distribution patterns across the depositional environments: slightly lower abundance in the delta plain (A: 89.07%) compared to the delta front to estuarine bay (B: 96.37%; C: 95.36%; D: 92.77%; E: 92.97%), showing a subtle decreasing trend from delta front to prodelta and estuarine bay. The relatively reduced Thermoproteota abundance in the delta plain likely reflected its shallower depth and freshwater-dominated conditions, where active groundwater transport may introduce dissolved oxygen, creating elevated redox conditions (less favorable for Bathyarchaeia, which are predominantly anaerobic) compared to other environments68,69. The gradual decrease from delta front (B) to prodelta and estuarine bay (D and E) aligned with the observed negative correlation between Thermoproteota and depth (r = −0.61) and positive correlation with δ13C (r = 0.48), suggesting limited adaptation to environments characterized by advanced diagenetic alteration and recalcitrant organic matter.

Notably, Thermoproteota showed a substantial reduction in the tidal river channel (F: 49.16%) (Figs. 1c and S13b; S13c), reflecting the distinct depositional history of these sediments, which originated under weak marine influence conditions less favorable for Bathyarchaeia68. Conversely, Hadarchaeota exhibited remarkable enrichment in the tidal river channel (F: 50.84%), displaying a clear increasing trend from delta front and estuarine bay environments (B: 1.45%; C: 3.36%; D: 3.82%; E: 7.03%) and correlating positively with depth (r = 0.92) and EC (r = 0.7) but negatively with δ13C (r = −0.76) (Fig. S14b), indicating strong adaptation to deep, saline environments with recalcitrant organic matter70. This adaptability likely enabled their progressive abundance increase throughout sedimentary succession from the delta front to estuarine bay environments. The substantial enrichment in the tidal river channels suggests ecological advantages conferred by early depositional and prolonged burial conditions.

The contrasting spatial patterns between bacterial and archaeal communities suggested fundamentally different ecological and evolutionary strategies. To investigate these differences at the molecular level and elucidate their divergent responses to terrestrial and marine influences, we analyzed protein isoelectric points (pI), which reflect adaptation to specific ionic conditions71,72. Archaeal communities exhibited significantly higher pI values compared to bacterial communities (7.71 vs. 7.05, p < 0.01; Fig. S15), potentially reflecting differences in evolutionary trajectories and physiological constraints73. This difference may reflect retention of protein characteristics that evolved under ancient oceanic conditions characterized by elevated sodium concentrations and pH values, highlighting the more conservative evolutionary trajectory of archaea relative to bacteria73. These molecular differences translated into distinct ecological patterns. Archaeal PI values correlated primarily with nutrient-related factors such as TN and TOC (Fig.S16), suggesting their current spatial distribution was mainly influenced by nitrogen and carbon-associated nutrient availability74,75. In contrast, bacterial communities demonstrated greater biochemical plasticity, with pI values correlating with diverse geochemical variables (NO3-, SO42-, S2-, TOC, EC, and δ13C; Fig. S16), indicating more responsive adaptation to dynamic environmental conditions76. Collectively, these molecular-level differences underscore the distinct adaptive strategies employed by bacteria and archaea in response to deltaic environmental gradients.

Fermentation genes exhibited the highest abundance across all zones (median RPKM: TE 1005.48, TR 809.41, MA 786.01), substantially exceeding other NH4+-associated metabolic pathways (Fig. 2b, c; Supplementary Table 3), suggesting fermentation might represent the primary NH4+-generating pathway. This interpretation was further supported by the positive relationship between fermentation gene abundance and sediment NH4+ concentrations (Spearman r = 0.42, Fig. 2d). Spatially, fermentation gene abundance was significantly higher in the TE zone compared to TR and MA zones (ANOVA, p < 0.05), and decreased with depth (R² = 0.425, p < 0.001; Spearman r = −0.67, Fig. 2c, d) and sediment age (R² = 0.473, p < 0.001; Fig.S17). This decline correlated positively with δ13C values (Spearman r = 0.71; Fig. 2d), indicating reduced fermentation potential as organic matter becomes more recalcitrant. This interpretation is mechanistically supported by the transformation pathways of N-containing organic molecules observed in aquifer systems16,79. Molecular-level studies have identified that ammonium production is driven by the stepwise degradation of N-rich organic compounds into N-depleted aromatic residues16. Crucially, investigations into sedimentary controls have demonstrated that “less-humified, N-rich sedimentary organic matter” is a prerequisite for intensive mineralization79. Consistent with these mechanisms, our observation of increasing DOM aromaticity (a254) and sediment δ13C depletion with depth indicates the accumulation of recalcitrant aromatic residues and the concurrent exhaustion of the labile N-rich substrates. However, groundwater NH4+ concentrations increased progressively from TE to MA zones (Fig.S10a; Supplementary Data 2), contrasting with the spatial trend in fermentation metabolic potential. This pattern reflects the cumulative effects of fermentation and other anaerobic degradation pathways coupled with hydrogeological retention. In the MA zone, low-permeability sediments restrict groundwater flow, allowing generated NH4+ to accumulate over millennia. Groundwater NH4+ distribution thus integrates biogeochemical production (dominated by fermentation) with hydrogeological control, demonstrating that NH4+ accumulation reflects both microbial metabolic potential and physical transport constraints.

Nitrate reduction genes showed the second highest abundance in the TE zone (median RPKM: TE 561.5, TR 310.2, MA 127.86), declining significantly along the TE to MA gradient (ANOVA, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2a–c). This gene abundance exhibited strong negative correlations with EC (r = −0.77) and depth (r = −0.65), positive correlation with NO3- availability (r = 0.62) and δ13C (r = 0.66) (Fig. 2d), suggesting that nitrate reduction potential may be suppressed under elevated salinity conditions but enhanced by greater substrate availability and more labile organic matter at shallow depths61,80–83. Consistent with this spatial pattern of nitrate reduction potential, groundwater NO3- concentrations (Supplementary Data 2) were detectable in the TE zone, where nitrate reduction potential was elevated, but fell below detection limits in TR/MA zones where this potential declined. This spatial correspondence suggests that continuous NO3- delivery through active recharge sustains NO3- availability for nitrate reduction in the TE zone29, while NO3- depletion and salinity stress collectively constrain this metabolic potential in TR/MA zones.

In contrast, nitrite ammonification genes showed elevated abundance in TR (594.25) and MA zones (774.51), exceeding nitrate reduction genes and representing the second highest abundance among NH4+-producing pathways in these zones (Fig. 2a–c; Supplementary Table 3). Notably, nitrite ammonification and nitrate reduction exhibited contrasting spatial patterns (Fig. 2c) and opposing environmental correlations: nitrate reduction gene abundance correlated negatively with EC (r = −0.77) but positively with NO3- (r = 0.62), whereas nitrite ammonification showed the inverse (EC: r = 0.50; NO3-: r = −0.57) (Fig. 2d). These contrasting patterns revealed differential environmental controls: nitrate reduction required sufficient NO3- substrate and was inhibited by high salinity, whereas nitrite ammonification was favored under high-salinity and nitrate-depleted conditions80,84–86. Groundwater NO2- distribution supported the spatial pattern of nitrite ammonification potential: NO2- concentrations in groundwater were higher in TR/MA zones, where nitrite ammonification potential was significantly elevated, but were lower in the TE zone, where this potential was correspondingly reduced (Supplementary Data 2; detailed in SI S2.4). This spatial correspondence suggests that NO2- accumulation in TR/MA zones provides an accessible substrate for nitrite ammonification in these zones.

Sulfate reduction gene abundance demonstrated notable stability across environmental gradients, showing no significant spatial variation among zones (ANOVA, p > 0.05) and only weak depth correlation (R² = 0.112, p = 0.046) (Fig. 2c). In the MA zone, this pathway (275.7 RPKM) exceeded nitrate reduction (127.86 RPKM) (Fig. 2a–c; Supplementary Table 3), indicating its greater relative importance for NH4+ production compared to nitrate reduction under high-salinity conditions. Previous studies have associated sulfate reduction with NH4+ accumulation in high-salinity groundwater87. Our gene abundance data demonstrated that while this pathway showed stable genetic potential across zones, its relative importance varied with local geochemical conditions, including salinity gradients and electron acceptor availability88. Groundwater sulfur speciation highlighted the increasing importance of sulfate reduction in this zone: SO42- was depleted in TR/MA zones but detectable in the TE zone, while dissolved sulfide reached maximum concentrations in the MA zone (Supplementary Data 2). This substrate depletion coupled with product accumulation indicates that sulfate reduction consumption exceeds supply in the MA zone.

To identify the specific microbial taxa mediating these metabolic processes, we analyzed the taxonomic composition of microorganisms encoding genes for NH4+-associated metabolic pathways (Fig.S18a; Supplementary Data 7). Pseudomonadota, particularly the class Gammaproteobacteria, emerged as the dominant phylum across four key metabolic pathways, with relative contributions ranging from 72.19% to 96.60% (Fig. S18b). This phylum is well documented for its metabolic versatility in anaerobic environments and nitrogen cycling89. At the order level, Burkholderiales, known for diverse respiratory capabilities90, contributed substantially to fermentation (relative contribution: 24.97%), nitrate reduction (52.93%), and sulfate reduction (47.31%), whereas Rhodobacterales, which include facultative anaerobes capable of DNRA91,92, contributed substantially to nitrite ammonification (35.62%) (Fig.S18b). At the family level, Rhodobacteraceae, a group well-characterized for versatile anaerobic metabolism93, emerged as a significant contributor to fermentation (18.20%), nitrite ammonification (35.62%), and sulfate reduction (28.37%), while Burkholderiaceae_B, commonly associated with denitrification94, was predominantly involved in nitrate reduction (32.56%) (Fig. S18b).

At the genus level, Brevirhabdus emerged as a pivotal taxon across multiple processes: fermentation (14.02%), nitrite ammonification (33.23%), and sulfate reduction (22.82%) (Fig. S18b). This genus shared 98.9% 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity with previously described strains from Pacific hydrothermal vent sediments (Figs. S19 and S20a) (Supplementary Data 8)95 and was notably abundant in the MA zone (Fig. S20b). The ecological success of Brevirhabdus was underpinned by sophisticated adaptive mechanisms, including diverse salt tolerance strategies, versatile energy generation pathways (aerobic/anaerobic respiration and fermentation), specialized ABC transporter systems, extensive nitrogen and sulfur metabolism networks, advanced DNA repair mechanisms, and complex motility systems (flagella and chemotaxis apparatus) (Fig.S20c; Supplementary Data 9)96. These comprehensive adaptations suggest that Brevirhabdus represents marine-derived microorganisms that have persisted over millennial timescales, potentially shaping contemporary NH4+ dynamics (detailed in SI S2.6).

Collectively, these findings revealed systematic metabolic zonation across deltaic subsurface environments, reflecting the integration of depositional history, post-depositional processes, and microbial adaptation. Fermentation genes exhibited consistently elevated abundance, suggesting that fermentation may represent the dominant pathway for organic matter decomposition and NH4+ production, consistent with observations across diverse coastal subsurface environments97–99. While previous studies have linked microbial degradation to NH4+ enrichment without specifying the dominant pathway8,16, our metagenomic analysis identified fermentation as the potential predominant mechanism, with gene abundance systematically declining with depth and sediment age, consistent with progressive organic matter recalcitrance.

The spatial shift from elevated nitrate reduction potential in the TE zone to elevated nitrite ammonification potential in the TR/MA zones revealed metabolic partitioning along environmental gradients, consistent with patterns observed in analogous coastal systems80,81,86,100. This zonation transcended thermodynamic electron acceptor depletion sequences83,101, reflecting the integrated influence of depositional conditions, organic matter characteristics, and groundwater chemistry. Critically, our analysis revealed that marine-derived microorganisms such as Brevirhabdus, having adapted over millennial timescales, contribute to contemporary biogeochemical cycling. This temporal perspective—integrating depositional history, post-depositional processes, and microbial adaptation—provided a mechanistic framework for understanding NH4+ accumulation in deltaic subsurface systems.

Microbial community shifts and evolutionary patterns of key functional genes

Microbial community composition and key functional gene distribution varied significantly along the land-sea gradient, as revealed by analysis of similarity (ANOSIM: R = 0.64, p = 0.001 for community composition; R = 0.47, p = 0.001 for key functional genes; Fig. 3a–f). The TE zone displayed the highest within-zone heterogeneity (median Bray-Curtis dissimilarity: 0.90 for community composition; 0.31 for key functional genes), compared with the TR zone (0.80; 0.26) and the MA zone (0.75; 0.28) (Fig. 3b, e; Supplementary Data 10). This variability reflects the integration of distinct depositional sub-environments, depth-dependent diagenetic processes, and temporal changes spanning ~13,000 years of deltaic evolution. Environmental factors collectively explained 37.77% of microbial community variation and 65.81% of key functional gene variation, with EC, depth, and δ13C identified as the three primary factors (Fig. 3c, f; Supplementary Data 11). EC (salinity proxy) emerged as a dominant factor, consistent with previous observations in estuarine and coastal ecosystems102–104. Notably, environmental variables explained significantly greater variance in key functional gene distribution (65.81%) than in community structure (37.77%) (Fig. 3c, f), indicating that functional genes were more responsive to environmental gradients105,106. Evolutionary analysis revealed predominantly low nonsynonymous-to-synonymous substitution ratios (pN/pS: range: 0.002-1.648; median: 0.04), with 99.91% of genes exhibiting pN/pS <1 (Fig.S21; Supplementary Data 12), indicative of widespread purifying selection107,108. Single-nucleotide variant (SNV) density was similarly low (range: 0.034-186.73 SNVs/kbp; median: 8.11 SNVs/kbp), consistent with marine sedimentary environments (0.76-123 SNVs/kbp), with only one gene exceeding this previously reported range107. High major allele frequencies (MAF) were prevalent across the dataset (range: 0.5-1.0; median: 0.94), comparable to values for key metabolic genes in analogous sedimentary environments107. Collectively, these patterns indicated strong genetic conservation and adaptive allele fixation in core metabolic functions108.

To investigate the adaptive evolution of functional genes across subsurface gradients, we integrated gene-level and pathway-level evolutionary metrics. While only a small fraction of individual genes (2.68-6.37%) showed significant differentiation (Supplementary Table 4), distinct evolutionary responses were evident at the pathway level along the land-sea gradient, with fermentation uniquely showing depth-dependent patterns (Figs. 4 and S2.7). This implies that adaptation operates through coordinated adjustments across multiple genes rather than dramatic changes at individual loci (detailed in SI S2.8). Specifically, fermentation genes displayed unique depth-dependent evolutionary patterns (Fig. 4a). Independent of the land-sea gradient, fermentation genes showed elevated pN/pS ratios with increasing depth. This trend, coinciding with reduced gene abundance in deeper sediments (Fig. 2c), suggests relaxed purifying selection or adaptive optimization for resource utilization under limiting labile organic matter conditions56,109,110. Furthermore, elevated pN/pS ratios likely reflect reduced selection efficiency associated with smaller effective population sizes in deep sediments, facilitating the accumulation of mildly deleterious mutations111.

In contrast, nitrite ammonification and nitrate reduction genes were primarily shaped by salinity gradients. Both pathways exhibited elevated SNV densities and pN/pS ratios in the TE zone, which decreased in the high-salinity TR and MA zones (Fig. 4b, c), indicating strong purifying selection driven by osmotic stress. However, despite these similar evolutionary signatures, their ecological distributions diverged. Nitrate reduction gene abundance declined toward the high-salinity MA zone, whereas nitrite ammonification gene abundance increased (Fig. 2c). This paradox highlights a divergence in adaptive capacity: nitrite ammonifiers likely possess superior osmoregulatory mechanisms that allow proliferation under high salinity86,112, whereas nitrate reducers face population decline due to limited osmoregulatory capacity80,86. Thus, similar selective pressures (osmotic stress) can yield contrasting ecological outcomes depending on the underlying physiological capabilities of the microbial groups.

Sulfate reduction genes showed higher proportions of depth-responsive genes (Supplementary Table 4) but no significant pathway-level depth trend (Fig. 4d), indicating complex evolutionary dynamics. Spatial patterns in pN/pS ratios along the land-sea gradient likely reflect depth heterogeneity rather than salinity effects: the TR zone includes predominantly deep tidal river channel samples, which may confound zone-level comparisons (Fig. 2d).

These findings collectively demonstrate how environmental gradients drive coordinated evolutionary adaptation in deltaic subsurface systems. Depositional history and geochemical transitions emerge as primary drivers of community structure and functional gene distribution, reflecting long-term adaptation to transgressive-regressive cycles over millennial timescales. Our integrated evolutionary framework demonstrates how geological processes shape microbial adaptation, providing a mechanistic basis for predicting microbial responses to future environmental change in deltaic systems worldwide.

Methodological limitations and future directions

Several methodological limitations should be noted. Gene abundance represents metabolic potential rather than actual gene expression or activity, nor does it directly quantify in situ metabolic rates. Additionally, correlations between metabolic potentials and environmental parameters may reflect multiple coupled factors rather than simple causal relationships.

Despite these limitations, our interpretations were supported by multiple lines of evidence (geochemical measurements, spatial patterns, temporal trends) and were consistent with established biogeochemical principles. Future studies integrating metatranscriptomics, stable isotope probing, and process rate measurements would further refine quantification of pathway-specific contributions to NH4+ dynamics.

In summary, through integrated geochemical and metagenomic analyses, we revealed how depositional history and post-depositional processes fundamentally shaped microbial community structure and NH4+ dynamics in deltaic subsurface environments (Fig. 5). Initial depositional conditions established persistent biogeochemical architectures that endure across millennial timescales, while subsequent hydrogeochemical evolution drove a progressive transition from terrestrial to marine influence across the deltaic transect. Archaeal and bacterial communities exhibited fundamentally different adaptive strategies: archaea displayed evolutionary conservatism, whereas bacteria showed greater biochemical plasticity. Fermentation exhibited the highest gene abundance across all zones and likely represents a primary NH4+-generating pathway. Beyond fermentation, metabolic partitioning was evident in nitrate reduction and nitrite ammonification pathways: nitrate reduction ranked second in the TE zone, while nitrite ammonification ranked second in the TR and MA zones. Sediment EC, depth, and δ13C emerged as three primary factors that influenced both community structure and functional potential, integrating the effects of salinity gradients with progressive organic matter diagenesis over millennial timescales. Critically, we identified the marine-derived genus Brevirhabdus as a key taxon linking depositional history to contemporary NH4+ dynamics. Evolutionary analysis revealed widespread purifying selection maintaining core metabolic functions, with pathway-specific adaptive responses: fermentation genes exhibited depth-dependent selection patterns reflecting substrate recalcitrance associated with progressive diagenesis and sediment aging, while nitrate reduction and nitrite ammonification responded primarily to salinity gradients. Methods

Study sites, sediment core drilling, and sediment sample collection

Three sediment cores were collected from the PRD, South China, at sites SD (113°21'57.3“E, 22°48'8.83“N, 9.93 m depth), HP (113°25'50.3“E, 22°44'3.53“N, 17.70 m depth), and MZ (113°32'5.68“E, 22°38'21.20“N, 44.29 m depth) (Fig. 1a). The cores span ~13,000 cal yr BP to present, documenting the evolution of the PRD from tidal river channels to delta plain environments (Figs. 1b and S3–S5). A total of 36 sediment samples were selected across these cores for metagenomic and geochemical analyses based on lithological characteristics and stratigraphic distribution. Based on depositional environments, sediment properties, and contemporary hydrogeochemical characteristics, samples were classified into three zones (detailed in Results and Discussion: Land-sea Gradients and Geochemical Characteristics, and SI S2.1-2.2): (1) the terrestrial-dominated zone (TE, n = 13); (2) the transitional zone (TR, n = 10); and (3) the marine-dominated zone (MA, n = 13). All cores were collected from non-protected areas in November 2019, and samples were fully utilized in the analyses presented in this study.

Cores were drilled using a rotary double-casing drilling system designed to minimize contamination (Fig. S7). The system consisted of an outer steel casing in contact with the formation and an inner polyvinyl chloride (PVC) liner (Ø 90 mm) that was physically isolated from the drilling interface, protecting core sediments from external contamination. The liner was pre-sealed at the bottom and equipped with lateral access ports at 20-cm intervals (sealed during drilling). These ports enabled subsequent in situ geochemical measurements while minimizing atmospheric exposure. Upon retrieval of each 1-m segment, we removed the bottom seal, discarded the surficial layer (~2 cm), and immediately collected samples from the interior using sterile tools. Microbiological subsamples (50 g) were placed in sterile sealed bags, immediately stored on dry ice (−78 °C), transported to the laboratory, and maintained at −80 °C until DNA extraction; geochemical subsamples (~100 g) were stored in sterile bags, kept in a cooler during transport, and maintained at 4 °C until analysis. Detailed contamination prevention protocols, drilling apparatus specifications, and sampling procedures are provided in SI S1.1.

Groundwater sampling

Groundwater samples were collected from the HKU Multi-Level Groundwater Monitoring System installed at the three sites after completion of sediment coring113. Before sampling, each port was purged using a peristaltic pump until physicochemical parameters (salinity, pH, and EC) stabilized. A total of 16 groundwater samples were collected: SD (n = 4, 3.1–9.0 m), HP (n = 3, 3.10–12.4 m), and MZ (n = 9, 4.35–35.25 m) from the Holocene sedimentary layer (Supplementary Data 2).

Sediment geochemical analyses

In situ measurements of sediment porewater pH, EC, and oxidation-reduction potential (ORP) were conducted immediately upon core retrieval at pre-installed access ports (20-cm intervals) using portable meters calibrated daily with standard solutions (Figs.S7 and S1.2). Sediment samples were analyzed for extractable chemical species (NH4+, NO2-, NO3-, available phosphorus (AP), manganese (Mn2+), SO42-, and S2-) in this study. Sediment elemental (TN, TC, TOC, TIC), carbon isotope composition of TOC (δ13C), and grain size data were obtained from our previous study28. All sediment concentrations are reported on a dry-weight basis unless otherwise stated. Detailed analytical procedures, instrument specifications, and quality control protocols are provided in SI S1.2.

Groundwater geochemical analyses

Groundwater salinity, NH4+, δ18O, δ2H, DIC, CO2, and CH4 data were obtained from our previous studies29,39. For this study, additional parameters were measured: NO2-, NO3-, SO42-, DOM aromaticity (a254), dissolved sulfide, and Fe2+. Samples for NO2-, NO3-, SO42-, and DOM analyses were filtered (0.45 μm) into sterile centrifuge tubes, kept in a cooler during transport, and maintained at 4 °C until analysis. Dissolved sulfide and Fe2+ were measured immediately after sample collection using HACH reagent kits (2244500-CN and 103769-CN, respectively) to prevent oxidation. All analytical methods, calibration procedures, and quality assurance/quality control protocols are provided in SI S1.3.

Geochronology

Sediment cores were dated using accelerator mass spectrometry radiocarbon (AMS 14C) analysis of shells, charcoal, plant remains, and wood fragments (SD: 2 samples; HP: 6 samples; MZ: 13 samples). Conventional radiocarbon ages were calibrated using Calib 8.2 with IntCal20114 for terrestrial samples and MARINE20115 for shells, with a marine reservoir correction of ΔR = −89 ± 49 years116,117. Age-depth models were constructed using Bayesian analysis in the rbacon package (v3.5.2)118. Details are provided in SI S1.4.

DNA extraction, sequencing, and metagenomic analysis

Community genomic DNA was extracted from 20 g of sediment using the PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit (MoBio). The integrity and purity of the extracted DNA were evaluated using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, while DNA concentration and purity were quantified using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer and a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Metagenomic libraries with an insert size of 350 bp were constructed, and their concentrations were measured using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer. The libraries were sequenced using the PE150 strategy on the Illumina Nova6000 platform (Guangdong Magigene Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China), generating approximately 30 Gbp (2 × 150 bp) of raw data per sample.

Raw sequencing data were processed using FastQC (v0.12.1)119 for quality assessment and Trimmomatic (v0.39)120 for adapter removal and quality filtering. Clean data from all 36 samples were individually assembled using MEGAHIT v1.1.3121 (--k-min 27 --k-max 107 --k-step 10 --min-contig-len 1000). Metagenomic binning was performed using the “Binning module” in metaWRAP v1.3.2122 (-l 2000 --metabat2 --maxbin2 --concoct) and refined using the “Bin_refinement module”. The quality of MAGs was assessed with CheckM2 (v1.0.1)123, and MAGs with completeness <50% or contamination >10% were removed.

GTDB-Tk (v2.3.2) with the GTDB R214 database was used for taxonomic classification124, and dRep (v2.6.2; -sa 0.95 -nc 0.3) was applied for dereplication to obtain representative MAGs (rMAGs)59. Functional annotation of rMAGs was performed using METABOLIC (v4.0)77. The Reads Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads (RPKM) values for rMAGs and target genes were calculated using CoverM (v0.7.0) in genome mode (parameters: --mapper minimap2-sr --min-read-per cent-identity 0.95 --min-read-aligned-per cent 0.75 --contig-end-exclusion 0) and contig mode (parameters: rpkm --mapper minimap2-sr --min-read-per cent-identity 0.95 --min-read-aligned-per cent 0.75), respectively78.

To assess the metabolic potential and taxonomic composition of each NH4+-associated process, we used METABOLIC-4.077 with default settings to identify relevant genes in MAGs. For each metabolic pathway (Fig.S2), we calculated metabolic potential by summing RPKM values of all constituent genes. To determine the taxonomic contribution to each pathway, we followed the approach of Zhou et al.77: (1) identified all MAGs harboring pathway-specific functional genes, (2) calculated total RPKM values of pathway genes in each MAG, (3) summed RPKM values for all MAGs belonging to each taxonomic group (at phylum, class, order, family, and genus levels), and (4) calculated relative contribution of each taxon by: (RPKM sum for taxon / total RPKM sum for all taxa in that pathway) × 100%. This approach quantifies the potential metabolic contribution of each taxonomic group based on functional gene abundance (Fig.S17; Supplementary Data 7), noting that these represent genomic potential rather than direct measurements of in situ metabolic activity.

For spatial analysis, we compared pathway potentials across the three environmental zones (TE, TR, and MA) using zone-specific means, with statistical significance of inter-zone differences determined by one-way ANOVA with post-hoc Duncan’s multiple range test (p < 0.05)125. To investigate depth-dependent patterns, we employed linear regression analysis between sediment depth and metabolic potential for each pathway, with relationships considered significant at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.1.0).

Evolutionary analyses

Filtered reads from each sample were mapped to all species-cluster representative MAGs concatenated together using Bowtie (v1.1.2; default parameters)126. Nucleotide metrics, including SNVs/kbp, pN/pS ratio, and major allele frequency were calculated from these mappings using the profile module of the inStrain program (v1.8.0; –profile mode; default parameters) at the gene level127.

We conducted a two-tiered evolutionary analysis encompassing both gene-level differentiation and pathway-level trends. At the gene level, we quantified differential selection across ecological zones (TE, TR, and MA) using one-way ANOVA, testing the null hypothesis of equal means across zones for each metabolic gene. Genes were classified as exhibiting significant zone-specific patterns only when meeting strict dual criteria: p < 0.05 and R² ≥ 0.4. We similarly assessed depth-dependent evolution through linear regression analysis, with sediment depth as the predictor variable and gene metrics as the response variable. Significance was determined using identical thresholds (p < 0.05 and R² ≥ 0.4), and regression analysis was restricted to genes with data from at least three sampling depths to ensure robust trend estimation. For each metabolic pathway, we then calculated the proportion of genes showing significant responsiveness to both ecological zones and depth gradients.

At the pathway level, we aggregated evolutionary metrics (pN/pS ratios and SNV density) by computing pathway-specific averages across all samples. We then systematically analyzed these pathway averages for zone-specific differentiation using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for post-hoc comparisons (significance threshold: p < 0.05). To characterize depth-dependent evolutionary trajectories, we employed linear regression analysis between sediment depth and evolutionary metrics for each pathway at individual sampling points, with relationships considered significant at p < 0.05. This complementary analytical approach enabled us to detect both gene-specific selection signatures and broader pathway-level evolutionary patterns across the marine deltaic sediment gradients. All statistical analyses were implemented in R (version 4.1.0).

Protein isoelectric point determination and phylogenetic analysis

Protein isoelectric points (pI) of both bacterial and archaeal predicted proteins were calculated for each protein sequence using the ‘pepstats’ tool from the EMBOSS (European Molecular Biology Open Software Suite)128. The mean pI value was then calculated for each genome to obtain genome-level isoelectric point characteristics.

To elucidate the evolutionary relationships of Brevirhabdus, we conducted phylogenetic analyses using the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) framework124. All genomes belonging to the family Rhodobacteraceae (including Brevirhabdus) were retrieved from GTDB (the R214 database), and ten reference genomes from the family Aestuariivirgaceae were selected as outgroups to root the tree (Supplementary Data 8). Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the GTDB-Tk (v2.3.2)124, which employs a set of 120 bacterial marker genes for taxonomic classification and phylogenetic inference. The ‘gtdbtk infer’ command was used to place the genomes within the GTDB reference tree. This approach utilizes pplacer to place query genomes into a reference phylogeny based on maximum likelihood. For a more focused analysis, we extracted the subtree containing all Brevirhabdus genomes to generate a genus-level phylogenetic tree. Bootstrap support values are represented by circle sizes at the nodes, with larger circles indicating higher support. The tree visualization was performed using “iTOL (Interactive Tree of Life, https://itol.embl.de/)”129 and the R package “itol.toolkit”130, with a scale bar representing 0.1 substitutions per site.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.0.4)131, unless otherwise specified. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was used to assess microbial community structure and functional gene similarities132. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was conducted using the adonis function (999 permutations) from the “vegan” package to assess the influence of depositional environments and cores on microbial community structure and key functional genes133. Canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) was utilized to examine potential associations between environmental variables and microbial community structure or key functional genes using the “vegan” package134.
