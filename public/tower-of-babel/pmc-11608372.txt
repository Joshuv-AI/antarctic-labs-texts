Optimal design and experimentation of full tailings and cementitious materials: a case study of a lead-zinc mine in China
Authors: Jiang Guo, Yanni Yu, Jiachuang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
As mineral resources become increasingly scarce and environmental awareness grows, mining companies urgently need cost-effective and environmentally friendly filling methods for mines. The use of ultrafine tailings combined with various binders at different ratios plays a key role in determining the rheological properties and mechanical strength of cemented paste backfill. This paper provides parameter basis for the design of a mine filling system, firstly, different filling materials and proportions were selected, and basic physicochemical property tests, along with cementitious material analysis, were conducted to determine the key physical and chemical properties and mineral composition of the materials. Next, the rheological properties, transport performance, and bleeding characteristics of the filling slurry were tested to assess the feasibility and process requirements for self-flow transport through pipelines. The results indicated that the transportation performance of the slurry was better when gelling powder was used as the binder. Finally, the compressive, shear, and tensile strengths of the filling blocks were tested under varying slurry mass concentrations and cement-tailings ratios at different curing times. This helped identify the optimal filling concentration and proportions needed to meet the strength requirements of the filling structure. The final study found that the sample with a cement-tailings ratio of 1:6, a mass concentration of 68%, and a gelling powder as the binder reached the strength requirements for mine filling after 28 days. This research offers valuable insights for other mining companies seeking to implement similar environmentally sustainable and cost-effective filling technologies, promoting wider adoption and advancements in the field.

Introduction

In the process of mineral processing, tailings are often used to fill the empty spaces in mines to maintain their structural stability. However, since these tailings contain various chemical agents, they pose significant environmental challenges. While isolation pillars are typically retained in the mine to ensure structural stability, the cost of using filling technology to recover these economically valuable pillars needs to be considered. The challenge of adopting efficient, scientific, and low-cost filling methods while ensuring safety has become a key area of research in the mining industry1,2. The development of mineral resources has significantly contributed to the growth of national economies, particularly over the past few decades. With the rise of mining technologies and increased awareness of environmental concerns, cemented paste backfill (CPB) technology has become more widespread in underground mining operations due to its economic and environmental benefits3,4. CPB involves depositing tailings, which would otherwise harm the environment, back into the mined-out areas, providing safer mining spaces, increasing production efficiency, and enhancing extraction rates. In addition, it addresses safety issues, environmental pollution, and economic costs associated with surface tailings disposal5–7.

Backfill mining is an effective method for utilizing solid waste from mines. Waste is processed into a slurry or paste and then used to fill mined-out voids, achieving the goal of waste management, mitigating surface subsidence, and supporting mine stability. In China, cement tailings backfill (CTB) is a widely adopted method8, consisting mainly of dewatered tailings, binders (such as Portland cement and fly ash), and mixing water. The process of preparing cemented tailings backfill (CTB) involves multiple stages, including surface preparation, transport, placement, and solidification underground9,10. One key challenge is how to effectively transport fresh CTB to underground voids while ensuring that its short-term and long-term strength is sufficient to support underground structures at minimal cost11,12. Therefore, conducting fill proportioning experiments to study how different tailings slurry ratio parameters affect rheological properties (such as yield stress and viscosity) and strength performance (e.g., compressive strength and deformation behavior) is crucial. This research plays a vital role in improving fill performance and stability, reducing costs, and protecting the environment13,14. Several parameters—solid content, tailings particle size distribution, water quality, curing temperature, mixing procedures, and binder chemical composition—are critical to the placement, transport, solidification, and strength development of CTB15–18. Increasing the solid content (tailings and cement) enhances mechanical strength, while decreasing moisture content reduces slurry viscosity, affecting flowability and pumpability. However, higher moisture content weakens the paste’s strength19,20. Moreover, the type of cement used significantly affects the unconfined compressive strength (UCS) of the slurry21. Extensive research has explored the selection and dosage of CTB components22,23. Sulfide minerals in deposits such as lead–zinc ores can degrade the strength of cementitious materials and reduce structural stability due to sulfide oxidation. Dong et al.24 demonstrated that while sulfides initially increase strength, they can cause long-term strength reduction. Xu et al.25 found that flocculants negatively affect the rheological behavior and mechanical strength of fresh CTB. Further studies by Deng et al.26 examined the rheological properties of ultrafine cemented backfill (UCB), identifying its thixotropic characteristics under prolonged steady-state shear.

Scholars have also explored how CPB curing conditions (e.g., curing temperature, stress, and drainage conditions) affect its performance27–29. For example, Cui et al.30 studied how initial temperature, curing stress, and drainage conditions influence the early mechanical and thermal properties of tailings cementitious materials. Liu et al.31 examined the impact of mixing time on both fresh and hardened CTB properties, showing that longer mixing times increase yield stress, promote binder hydration, and improve early mechanical strength. Recent research primarily focuses on understanding how individual factors affect CPB performance, often overlooking the combined effects of ultrafine tailings and different binders on rheological properties and mechanical strength. This gap in research limits the ability to provide comprehensive theoretical support for practical engineering applications using ultrafine tailings. Tailings’ particle size, morphology, and microstructure significantly influence the flowability, strength, and crack resistance of backfill materials. Thus, further research is critical to improving CTB performance and enhancing the utilization of mine tailings.

This study aims to fill the research gap by investigating how ultrafine tailings affect slurry rheology and the mechanical properties of the fill body under various parameter ratios. Specifically, this research compares the effects of ultrafine tailings with three different types of binders, #325cement, #425cement, and gelling powder, on the rheological properties of fresh CTB under varying solid content conditions. Additionally, the study examines how varying amounts of water-reducing agents influence the yield stress of fresh CTB. The paper also explores the effects of these binders on CTB mechanical properties (including uniaxial compressive strength, tensile strength, and triaxial compressive strength) at different solid contents and curing times. Through this investigation, the study seeks to identify optimal parameter ratios that satisfy filling strength requirements, enhance resource utilization, and reduce costs in practical engineering applications.

Project overview and pre-experiment preparation

Baogai Mine is located in Lengshui Town, Guixi City, situated on the western slope of the northern Wuyi Mountains. The terrain transitions from higher elevations in the southeast to flat plains near Poyang Lake in the northwest. The main ridges in the mining area generally extend in a northeast direction, with the landscape primarily consisting of low mountains and hills. There are no identified karst formations. The area is predominantly composed of hard to semi-hard intact rock blocks and layers, which exhibit high mechanical strength, integrity, and stability. The mining area is generally free from significant geological issues that could impact mining operations. It is classified as having moderate technical conditions for mining, considering hydrogeological, engineering, and environmental geological factors. The primary goal of this study is to evaluate the consolidation characteristics, rheological properties, and strength indicators of the tailings and cementitious materials from Baogai Mine for use as filling materials. The study also recommends optimal filling ratios that meet the requirements for various filling applications, providing critical parameters for the design of filling systems.

The cementitious materials used in this filling experiment study include volcanic ash portland pozzolana cement (PP325#), ordinary portland cement (P.O425#), gelling powder, and filling aggregate consisting of tailings from Baogai Mine’s selected plant. Rheological properties tests

Water secretion rate test and slump test

Water secretion rate test is used to evaluate the ability of the fill slurry to separate water during static conditions, with the bleed volume serving as an indicator of slurry stability. First, moisten the container with a capacity of 86ml (internal diameter 55mm, height 60mm) using a damp cloth. Then, pour the filling slurry into the container in one go and vibrate it on a vibration table for 20 s to remove air bubbles. Next, gently smooth the excess slurry at the top of the container with a blade, ensuring that the top of the slurry is about 20mm below the rim, and cover the container to prevent evaporation. After covering, measure the total weight of the slurry and container, accurate to the gram. After letting it sit for a day, tilt the container by raising one side about 10mm, and use a pipette to extract the bleed water. Afterward, place the container flat, cover it again, and measure the total weight of the slurry and container after the bleed water has been extracted. Finally, calculate the total bleed rate.

Based on the seepage rate determination experiments, the influence of slurry concentration and sand-to-cement ratio on seepage rate is analyzed, with data results shown in Fig. 3. Overall, under most conditions, experimental data indicate a seepage rate of less than 1%. The difficulty in separating water from the slurry results in good homogeneity of the filling slurry, with fewer inter-slurry pores, better adhesion, and higher quality. Consequently, the overall mechanical strength of the filling slurry is enhanced.Fig. 3Influence of mass concentration and cement-tailings ratio on bleeding rate.

Yield stress test

Yield stress tests on the filling slurry were conducted according to the mix proportion scheme in Table 4. The tests utilized an Anton Paar MCR72 rheometer, primarily measuring yield stress and torque. Part of yield stress test results for the filling slurry are shown in Fig. 5. The yield stress values of the filling slurry were mostly above 400 Pa. Under the conditions of using gelling powder as a binder, with a cement sand ratio of 1:6 and 1:12, and a mass concentration of 68%, the yield stress values were 155.9 and 116.0, respectively, which were significantly lower than the yield stress of the slurry when using 325 cement with the same proportioning parameters. This indicates that the pumping performance is better. With a constant cement-tailings ratio, the yield stress of the filling slurry increased with increasing mass concentration, and the rate of change gradually accelerated. With a constant mass concentration, the yield stress of the filling slurry initially increased and then decreased as the cement-tailings ratio decreased.Fig. 5Yield stress test results of filling slurry with different cement-tailings ratios and concentrations (local).

The influence of slurry concentration on seepage rate exhibits significant variability without clear patterns. Sand-to-cement ratio has a notable impact on seepage rate, with the peak occurring approximately in the range of 1:6 to 1:8. As the sand-to-cement ratio exceeds 1:8, the seepage rate decreases. Conversely, when the sand-to-cement ratio drops below 1:20, the seepage rate tends to rise. Based on Fig. 6, it is observed that with a constant sand-to-cement ratio, the yield stress of filling slurries increases with higher mass concentrations, with the rate of change gradually accelerating. Conversely, at a constant mass concentration, reducing the sand-to-cement ratio of the filling slurry initially increases and then decreases the overall yield stress. Specifically, when the sand-to-cement ratio is 1:4, the minimum yield stress of the filling slurry is 394.45 Pa, increasing to a maximum of 1109.2 Pa. At a ratio of 1:15, the minimum and maximum yield stresses are 1058.6 Pa and 2383.1 Pa, respectively, representing increases of 168.4% and 114.8%. This indicates that maintaining a sand-to-cement ratio of 1:15 results in higher overall yield stress of the filling slurry, which also increases with higher mass concentrations. For sand-to-cement ratios of 1:8 and 1:15, with a slurry concentration of 75% and the addition of water reducing agents, the yield stress decreases. The yield stress decreases as the amount of water reducing agent increases.

Vecosity test

The viscosity of the samples was also measured using the MCR72 rheometer. The working principle is as follows: a motor drives the rotor to rotate at a constant speed through a gearbox. When the rotor rotates in a liquid, the liquid exerts a viscous torque on the rotor. The higher the viscosity of the liquid, the greater the viscous torque; conversely, the lower the viscosity, the smaller the torque. This viscous torque acting on the rotor is detected by a sensor, and the viscosity of the test sample is calculated by the computer.

The relationship between shear rate, viscosity, and shear stress is illustrated in Fig. 7. Under varying shear rates, the viscosity exhibited similar trends with changes in mass concentration and cement-tailings ratio. As the shear rate increased, the viscosity of the filling slurry decreased. A positive correlation was observed between the viscosity and mass concentration of the slurry; as mass concentration increased, viscosity also increased. The viscosity increased with a decreasing cement-tailings ratio, peaking at a ratio of 1:15. When the cement-tailings ratio fell below 1:15, the viscosity decreased further. At cement-tailings ratios of 1:8 and 1:15 and a slurry concentration of 75%, the viscosity decreased with the addition of a water-reducing agent.Fig. 7Relationship diagram between shear rate, viscosity, and shear stress of filling slurry.

Relationship diagram between shear rate, viscosity, and shear stress of filling slurry.

The viscosity of the filling slurries increases with decreasing sand-to-cement ratio, peaking at a ratio of 1:15. When the ratio is less than 1:15, the viscosity of the filling slurries decreases with decreasing sand-to-cement ratio. The results of the rheological properties research are roughly consistent with the findings of the literature mentioned earlier. Unlike their research, our study further explored the critical point of cement tailings ratio (i.e. 1:15), where viscosity actually decreases when the ratio is below 1:15. In addition, with the addition of water reducing agents, we observed a significant decrease in slurry viscosity, especially under cement-tailings ratio conditions of 1:8 and 1:15, indicating that water reducing agents have a positive effect on reducing slurry viscosity and improving flowability. This result extends the existing literature on the influence of additives on the rheological properties of slurries and demonstrates the effectiveness of water reducing agents at specific ratios. In addition, this chapter expands the discussion on yield stress in the literature and explores the influence of water reducing agents on yield stress, which have been less studied in existing literature, further filling the gap in the field of slurry optimization design.

Strength performance tests

Uniaxial compressive strength test

A full understanding of the mechanical response of cement tailings backfill (CTB) under various loading scenarios is beneficial for mining engineering applications34–38. The preparation of samples is crucial for the strength test of the filling body. The heterogeneity, internal discontinuities, and bubbles within the samples significantly impact their strength. Considering the effects of cement-tailings ratio, slurry concentration, and curing age, and to ensure the sample quality met experimental requirements, a total of 432 filling body samples were cast. The samples were prepared in two batches: the first batch included all samples with cement-tailings ratios of 1:4–1:10. After demolding the first batch, the second batch was cast with cement-tailings ratios of 1:12–1:30. Additional samples were prepared using 425 cement and gelling powder as binders, with a slurry concentration of 68% and cement-tailings ratios of 1:6 and 1:12.

After the test blocks were cured for the specified periods (3 days, 7 days, 28 days, 60 days), their uniaxial compressive strength was measured. The uniaxial compressive strength tests were conducted using a WDW-2000 universal testing machine. The specimens were tested under axial compression, with 2–3 samples tested for each condition. Prior to the test, a prestress of 0.1 kN was applied to ensure full contact between the machine platen and the specimen. The test used constant displacement loading at a rate of 0.5 mm/min and was terminated when the post-peak stress dropped to approximately one-third of the peak stress. After the test, the axial stress was released, the fracture surface was observed, and the next set of tests was conducted. Tensile strength test

The specimen preparation procedure for tensile strength testing is identical to that of uniaxial compressive strength testing, with the difference being the specimen dimensions of 7 mm * 7 mm * 7 mm. After curing for 28 days, the tensile strength of the specimens was measured. Due to the very low tensile strength of the fillings, the Brazilian tensile test was used to indirectly measure the tensile strength of the fillings. The Brazilian tensile test was conducted on the WDW-2000 universal testing machine. During the test, two 2 mm diameter steel rods were placed on the top and bottom sides of the specimen (Fig. 14), following international standards for rock mechanics measurements, ensuring uniform tensile stress distribution across the specimen. Given the significantly lower tensile strength of the fillings compared to their compressive strength, it can be concluded that the failure mode under these conditions is tensile failure.Fig. 14Experimental diagram of tensile strength of Brazilian splitting measurement specimen.

Experimental diagram of tensile strength of Brazilian splitting measurement specimen.

Three types of binders, namely 325# cement, 425# cement, and gelling powder, were selected for testing. The specimens were prepared with a slurry concentration of 68% and cement-tailings ratios of 1:6 or 1:12. Each condition was tested with 2–3 specimens. Before testing, a pre-load of 0.1 kN was applied to ensure full contact between the testing machine’s head and the specimen. The testing method involved constant displacement loading at a rate of 0.5 mm/min, with the test ending when the specimen developed macroscopic vertical cracks and the stress suddenly dropped. After unloading the axial stress post-test, the fracture surfaces were examined before proceeding to the next set of experiments.

Triaxial compressive strength test

The specimen preparation procedure for triaxial compressive strength testing was consistent with that of uniaxial compressive strength testing. After curing for 28 days, the specimens were tested for triaxial compressive strength. Triaxial compressive strength testing was conducted on a MTS815 rock mechanics testing machine at Central South University (Fig. 16). This machine is capable of conducting triaxial and uniaxial mechanical property tests on materials at temperatures ranging from room temperature to 200 °C. It features functions such as triaxial loading, confining pressure, pore pressure, temperature simulation, and acoustic emission, enabling simulation of conditions in deep seas and underground. The machine has a maximum axial load capacity of 2600 kN and can apply a maximum confining pressure of 140 MPa, using various control methods including displacement, load, and strain control.Fig. 16MTS-815 mechanical testing system.

MTS-815 mechanical testing system.

During triaxial testing, prepared specimens of backfill material (Φ50mm * h100mm) were wrapped with heat-shrink tubing and placed into the pressure chamber. Prior to testing, a pre-load of 0.1 kN was applied to ensure full contact between the specimen and the testing machine’s platens. The test began with applying a confining pressure (0.2 MPa, 0.4 MPa, 0.6 MPa) at a loading rate of 0.05 MPa/s. Subsequently, axial loading was applied at a displacement rate of 0.5 mm/min until failure of the specimen. Key parameters such as axial stress, axial displacement, axial strain, confining pressure, and circumferential strain were continuously monitored and recorded in real-time. Stress–strain curves, stress-time curves, and strain–time curves were generated accordingly.

The duration for testing one specimen in triaxial conditions was approximately 2.5 h. Due to practical constraints such as laboratory scheduling and the curing period of the backfill material, the triaxial tests were conducted on the following conditions: (1) 325# cement, sand-to-cement ratio of 1:6, slurry mass concentration of 70%; (2) 325# cement, sand-to-cement ratio of 1:12, slurry mass concentration of 70%; (3) gelling powder, sand-to-cement ratio of 1:6, slurry mass concentration of 68%; and (4) gelling powder, sand-to-cement ratio of 1:12, slurry mass concentration of 68%. Each condition involved testing three specimens, totaling twelve specimens, to determine their triaxial compressive strength under confining pressures of 0 MPa (uniaxial), 0.2 MPa, 0.4 MPa, and 0.6 MPa. The influence of different confining pressures on the strength of filling specimens

Figure 17 displays stress–strain curves of filling specimens with 325# cement as binder and sand-to-cement ratios of 1:6 and 1:12 under confining pressures of 0 MPa (uniaxial), 0.2 MPa, 0.4 MPa, and 0.6 MPa. When the sand-to-cement ratio is 1:6, the elastic modulus of the specimen gradually decreases with increasing confining pressure (0.2 MPa, 0.4 MPa, 0.6 MPa). The stress–strain curve of the specimen under 0.2 MPa confining pressure exhibits post-peak characteristics within the range of the testing machine, showing a stable long-term strength of approximately 1.8 MPa. As the confining pressure increases to 0.4 MPa and 0.6 MPa, the peak strength and post-peak characteristics were not measured due to the limited range (6 mm) of the extensometer on the rock testing machine, but the trend of the stress–strain curve suggests an increase in peak strength. Compared to conditions without confining pressure, the application of confining pressure significantly enhances the strength and ductility of the filling specimen under triaxial conditions, indicating higher strength and greater deformation capability.Fig. 17Stress strain curves of filling specimens with different confining pressures.

Stress strain curves of filling specimens with different confining pressures.

When the sand-to-cement ratio is 1:12, under the same axial strain, the lateral strain of the specimen decreases with increasing confining pressure. This is understandable as confining pressure increases the specimen’s resistance to lateral deformation, thereby limiting lateral strain. Similarly, increasing confining pressure is expected to increase the strength and ductility of the specimen. A comparison of the yield stages between specimens under uniaxial and triaxial conditions reveals that the increase in confining pressure primarily enhances the strength during the yield stage. Specimens under uniaxial conditions typically experience collapse and failure after entering the yield stage due to lack of lateral confinement, whereas specimens under triaxial conditions benefit from lateral confinement, which restricts lateral damage and thereby enhances their triaxial strength.

Figure 18 displays stress–strain curves of filling specimens with gelling powder as binder and sand-to-cement ratios of 1:6 and 1:12 under confining pressures of 0 MPa (uniaxial), 0.2 MPa, 0.4 MPa, and 0.6 MPa. Unlike the specimens with 325# cement, all specimens in this batch achieve triaxial peak strength within 2% axial strain, indicating significantly lower ductility compared to specimens with 325# cement but superior peak strength. Increasing confining pressure similarly enhances both peak strength and elastic modulus of the specimens. Compared to specimens under uniaxial conditions, those under triaxial conditions with solidified powder as binder exhibit noticeable residual strength. The residual strength of specimens with solidified powder and sand-to-cement ratios of 1:6 and 1:12 is approximately 2 MPa and 1 MPa, respectively. Additionally, the specimens demonstrate significant ductility, maintaining good load-bearing capacity within 7% axial strain. Hence, 7% strain can be considered a threshold condition for extreme deformation of filling specimens.Fig. 18Stress strain curves of filling specimens with different confining pressures.

Conclusions

Through physical and chemical properties tests, rheological experiments, and strength experiments on tailings, it is shown that using gelling powder as a binder is more beneficial for the rheological and strength properties of the slurry for Baojia Mining tailings. And by comprehensively comparing the rheological and strength indicators under different parameters, it was found that the sample after 28 days with a cement-tailings ratio of 1:6, a mass concentration of 68%, and a binder of gelling powder met the filling structural requirements of the mine.

The specific impact of various parameters on the performance of the slurry is shown as follows:

The particle size distribution of the tailings reveals a low content of coarse particles and a high content of fine particles, which is not ideal for building backfill strength. However, the mineral composition primarily consists of quartz and feldspar. The filtrate contains low levels of harmful substances and exhibits minimal toxicity. As a result, the full tailings from Baojia Mining are suitable for use as backfill material, fulfilling the necessary criteria for backfill applications from a physicochemical perspective.

The impact of the cement-tailings ratio on the bleeding rate of the filling slurry is not significant. The bleeding rate reaches its peak when the cement-tailings ratio falls within the range of 1:6 to 1:8. When the cement-tailings ratio exceeds 1:8, the bleeding rate decreases, but if it falls below 1:20, the bleeding rate increases again. Additionally, as the cement-tailings ratio decreases, the slump of the filling slurry gradually increases, peaking around a ratio of 1:8 to 1:10. Beyond this point, the slump decreases as the cement-tailings ratio continues to drop. There is a negative correlation between the spread of the filling slurry and its mass concentration, with higher shear rates resulting in decreased viscosity.

The speed at which the filling slurry is conveyed has a significant influence on the resistance in the pipeline. When the flow speed exceeds 2.5 m/s, the pressure drop in the pipeline shows an almost linear relationship with the flow speed. Furthermore, the required pressure per unit length of the horizontal straight pipes varies with the pipe diameter. Smaller pipe diameters require more pressure at the same flow rate, and as the flow speed increases, the pressure needed for smaller pipes rises sharply. Therefore, to reduce pressure loss, increasing the pipe diameter is recommended. Based on these calculations, a mass concentration of 68–70% for the filling slurry with a conveying speed of 1.2 m/s to 1.8 m/s is deemed optimal.

Triaxial strength tests show that the compressive strength of 28-day cured paste backfill samples with 68% slurry concentration and cement-tailings ratios of 1:6 and 1:12 exceeds 1.25 MPa. The confining pressure significantly improves the load-bearing capacity of the filling body. Samples with larger cement-tailings ratios and higher confining pressures display better load-bearing capacity and resistance to deformation. The failure mode of the samples with gelling powder is characterized by local shear failure, with the shear plane forming a small angle to the axial stress. When gelling powder is used for full tailings backfill, attention must be paid to the potential for shear failure in the filling body. Compared to ordinary Portland cement (425# and 325#), gelling powder proves to be more suitable for Baojia Mining’s full tailings, offering higher uniaxial compressive strength at all curing ages. Specifically, the 28-day compressive strength of samples with a cement-tailings ratio of 1:6 and 68% slurry concentration reaches 3 MPa, meeting the strength requirements for step-by-step mining operations.

This study focuses on the influence of the size distribution of tailings particles on the properties of backfill materials, but factors such as tailings particle morphology, specific surface area, and mineral composition may have important effects on the mechanical properties, rheological properties, and long-term stability of backfill materials. In the future, I will continue to study the effects of different particle properties on the flowability and strength of backfill materials through detailed analysis, in order to further improve the optimization design of backfill materials.
