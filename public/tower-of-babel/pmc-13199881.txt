Improved anticancer efficacy of plant leaf extracts from homa environment: A preliminary study
Authors: Priya Sharad, Shireen Masood, Osaid Masood, Alka Kumari, Ulrich Berk, Monisha Banerjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedicinal plants have been the most important component of agro-ecosystems and the natural environment from the very beginning of life. Air, water, and soil have been significantly contaminated, adversely affecting the production and development of plants. Homa therapy, based on traditional Vedic knowledge, purportedly restores balance to nature and eliminates environmental pollutants. However, this approach must be comprehended in light of current scientific findings.ObjectiveThis study examined the comparative efficacy of leaves extracted from plants in Homa and non-Homa (control) environments for their anticancer potential in a cervical cancer cell line.Materials and methodsThe leaves of the selected medicinal plants–Murraya koenigii (curry), Ocimum sanctum (tulsi), Aegle marmelos (bael), and Azadirachta indica (neem), were collected from both locations, subsequently dried, and extracted using Soxhlet apparatus. The leaf extracts were examined for cytotoxicity in HeLa cells using the MTT assay. The rate of cell migration was assessed with a wound healing assay. Subsequently, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation was evaluated using DCFDA. Additionally, gene expression was checked using quantitative polymerase chain reaction.ResultsOur findings indicate that Homa environment plants are significantly (p < 0.05) more effective against cervical cancer, as their IC50 values were comparatively low. The intracellular ROS generation assay demonstrated that Homa environment plants produced more ROS (p < 0.05) in cancer cells than those of non-Homa plants. Moreover, inhibited cell migration was more noticeable (p < 0.05) in Homa plants than non-Homa plants. The genes, SOD1, CAT, and MMP9 were found to be significantly (p < 0.05) downregulated in Homa grown leaf extracts. The study's findings offer promising preliminary trends that the Homa farming environment may improve certain plants' therapeutic qualities, including their capability to fight against cancer.Keywords: Agnihotra, Cellular assays, Cervical cancer, Gene expression, Medicinal plants

Introduction

Medicinal plants have been a source of biologically active phytochemicals for ages, employed as raw materials or purified compounds to cure various diseases. Alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, and polyphenols from these medicinal herbs have antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and anticancer effects [1]. Medicinal plants support sustainable healthcare practices since their production and usage are less harmful to the environment than synthetic pharmaceuticals [2,3]. However, urbanization, increasing environmental pollution, and stress have reduced the efficacy of these medicinal plants.

Plants adapt to environmental stress by using their antioxidant properties, which protect chloroplasts and other photosynthesis-related cellular components from damage due to oxidative stress. But environmental factors, including extreme temperatures, radiation, and pollution, can reduce antioxidant levels in medicinal plants [4]. These stresses can cause an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can overwhelm the plant's antioxidant defenses. Environmental pollutants, including pesticides, industrial chemicals, and heavy metals, may accumulate in the soil and water where medicinal plants are cultivated. These pollutants are assimilated by the plant and sequestered in its tissues, thereby modifying its chemical makeup and diminishing its health benefits [5]. In addition to pollutants, pesticides and herbicides may significantly affect the soil and water quality and increase toxic substances in medicinal plants [6].

The convention on biological diversity, often known as the Rio Convention, suggests using all methods, including traditional knowledge, to solve these challenges [7]. Homa therapy, based on vedic wisdom, purifies the atmosphere by restoring nature. This method used by Rishis and Maharishis was successful in ancient times. Homa farming employs a straightforward approach known as 'Agnihotra,' where 'Agni' signifies 'fire' and 'hotra' denotes 'healing' (a detailed description of Homa therapy following Agnihotra is provided in Supplementary File 1). It involves the recitation of a Sanskrit mantra at a specific time of day before a holy fire [8]. Agnihotra ash is believed to balance pH and enhance the soil's essential bacterial flora along with purifying water from pathogenic bacteria [9]. Experts believe that Homa farming is a scientific approach that emphasizes atmospheric fumigation, a crucial element in maintaining crop health against diseases [10]. This therapy serves to restore the natural balance and produce a type of microclimate surrounding the farm. It has been demonstrated to help purify the atmosphere and enhance product output and quality by utilizing the most fundamental Yagya, known as Agnihotra, which is synchronized with the biorhythm of dawn and sunset [9].

During photosynthesis, Photosystem II (PSII), a protein complex that splits water molecules using light energy, is the compartment most prone to abiotic stresses. This stress diminishes the electron transport chain, thereby resulting in a decrease in photosynthesis [11]. Homa organic farming (Agnihotra atmosphere) has been shown to improve PSII efficiency by minimizing damage and helping in repair and protection processes [12]. The Agnihotra was also found to dramatically improve photochemical efficiency, vegetation morphology, and the amount of active reaction center processes [12].

Although Homa therapy has demonstrated improved environmental conditions and more substantial plant quality, there is currently no evidence to suggest that Homa therapy promotes the therapeutic properties of plants cultivated in a Homa environment. Hence, an in vitro study on the cervical cancer cell line, HeLa, was designed to investigate the enhanced therapeutic (anti-cancerous) properties of some medicinal plants from Homa organic farming in comparison to those collected from 4 to 5 km away in a non-Agnihotra atmosphere.

Materials and methods

Collection and details of plant samples

The selected plants for this study have natural therapeutic properties and require the same climatic and soil conditions for their optimum growth (Table 1). Four different plant species types selected for this study were Murraya koenigii (curry), Ocimum sanctum (tulsi), Aegle marmelos (bael), and Azadirachta indica (neem). The samples were collected from two locations: one from Homa Therapy Goshala in Maheshwar, Madhya Pradesh, India, and the other from a site located 4–5 km away in a non-Agnihotra environment. After collection, the samples were encoded and sent for identification: CB (Control Bael), CC (Control Curry), CN (Control Neem), CT (Control Tulsi), HB (Homa Bael), HC (Homa Curry), HN (Homa Neem), and HT (Homa Tulsi).

All collected plant samples were preliminarily identified with the help of a species key available in published flora, based on taxonomic characters and the traditional knowledge system. To get quality plant material, herbarium voucher specimens of each species were collected and processed to prepare herbarium sheets following the standard herbarium techniques proposed by Jain and Rao (1977) and finally deposited in the herbarium of the CSIR-Institute of Himalayan Bioresource Technology (CSIR-IHBT), Palampur, Himachal Pradesh, India, as recognized by the New York Botanical Garden (acronym –PLP, 2007) for authentication of reference material. Samples were identified as Aegle marmelos (Bael), Murraya koenigii (Curry), Azadirachta indica (Neem), and Ocimum sanctum (Tulsi) with herbarium numbers PLP-108921, PLP-108922, PLP-108923, PLP-108924, PLP-108925, PLP-108926, PLP-108927, and PLP-108928 for CB, CC, CN, CT, HB, HC, HN, and HT, respectively.

Description of site

The Homa Therapy Goshala, covering a 36,422 m2 area in Maheshwar, M.P., India, was selected as a site to examine the impact of Agnihotra on naturally occurring vegetation. It is located in Khargone district, Madhya Pradesh, India, between Maheshwar and Mandleshwar (75.6210° East, 22.1736° North). Agnihotra has been conducted there consistently since 2001 [9,29,30]. For comparison, the same plants were chosen at a non-Agnihotra location situated 4–5 km away from the Homa Therapy Goshala.

Reagents

Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), antibiotic-antimycotic (Ab/Am) solution, fetal bovine serum (FBS), trypan blue, and 0.25 % trypsin-EDTA solution were provided by Gibco (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFDA), 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide tetrazolium salt (MTT), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and phosphate buffered saline (PBS) were obtained from Sigma Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA), and molecular-grade ethanol and Methanol from Lab Chem Quality Solution. RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis kit, RNAiso Plus (Takara, Japan), and DyNAmo ColorFlash SYBR Green qPCR kit were obtained from Thermoscientific.

Preparation of plant extracts

Leaves were cleaned thoroughly with running tap water and were allowed to soak in water for 15 min to remove mud and other particles. Leaves were then cleaned with distilled water and dried for 8–10 days in shade at room temperature (Fig. 1) and then ground into a powder. The extracts were prepared using the solvents methanol (for O. sanctum and A. marmelos) and 75% ethanol (for M. koenigii and A. indica) in a Soxhlet apparatus [21,[31], [32], [33]]. The extraction procedure was carried out in 6–8 working hours for two to three days at a temperature of 60–70°C using a heating mantle. Maintenance of HeLa cell line

The HeLa (HPV18+) human cervical cancer cell line was acquired from the National Centre for Cell Science (NCCS) in Pune, India. HeLa cells were grown in DMEM with 10 % FBS and 1 % antibiotic-antimycotic solutions in an incubator with 5 % CO2 and 95 % humidity at 37 °C. The presence of mycoplasma contamination was monitored by routine evaluation. Cells with 12–15 passages were employed in subsequent experiments.

Cell viability via MTT assay

The DMEM-cultured HeLa cells were allowed to reach confluency (70–80%). The cells were separated with trypsin and seeded into 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells/well in triplicate. Following a 24-h incubation period at 37°C to promote adhesion, the HeLa cells were exposed to several concentrations of different leaf extracts of both environments: M. koenigii (curry), O. sanctum (tulsi), A. marmelos (bael), and A. indica (neem) at 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, and 80 μg/ml. After an incubation of 24 h, 10 μl of MTT (5 mg/ml) was added and incubated for another 3 h. After aspirating the medium, 100 μl of DMSO was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals, and live cell proportions were estimated using a BioTek Epoch 2 microplate reader at 540 nm [34]. Wound healing assay

Cancer cell migration was assessed using the wound healing assay [35]. Cells were cultured for 24 h in 6-well plates. When cells reached 70–80% confluency, a 200 μl sterile tip was used to scratch at the center of each well. The cells were exposed to M. koenigii, O. sanctum, A. marmelos, and A. indica extracts from both environments at a concentration of 60 μg/ml for 24 h. Images from the scratch site recorded migratory area variation from 0 to 24 h. The images were taken at magnification × 200 using inverted phase contrast microscopy (Nikon Eclipse Ti2, Japan). Measurements of the wound area were taken using the ImageJ (Fiji) program.

Intracellular ROS measurement

Intracellular ROS production was measured using dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate DCFDA [35,36]. In 12-well plates, 1 × 105 cells per well were seeded with fresh medium for 24 h. Cells were treated at a concentration of 60 μg/ml with all the leaf extracts from Homa and non-Homa environments (M. koenigii, O. sanctum, A. marmelos, and A. indica) and incubated for another 24 h. The medium was carefully removed, and wells were washed with 1 × PBS. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 30 min in the dark after adding 10 μM DCFDA to each well. The cells were examined under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ts2, Japan) after washing twice with 1 × PBS. Magnification × 200.

RNA isolation and real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from HeLa cells using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA purity and concentration were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Wilmington, DE, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Real-time PCR was performed using the DyNAmo Flash SYBR Green 2X master mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) along with gene-specific primers for SOD1, CAT, MMP9, and GAPDH (primer sequences provided in Supplementary file 2). Fold changes were calculated using the ΔΔCt method using GAPDH as an endogenous control for the relative measurement of gene expression [37]. All reactions were conducted using three duplicates each.

Statistical analysis

The IC50 values were calculated by nonlinear regression analysis using GraphPad Prism 8.0. The percent cell viability was fitted to a four-parameter logistic (4PL) model (log[inhibitor] vs. response - variable slope). The curve estimated the top and bottom plateaus, hill slope, and logIC50. The fitted parameters were used to automatically calculate the IC50. Results are displayed as mean ± SEM. All treatment groups were compared with their respective controls using the analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a Tukey Multiple Comparison Test and unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001 indicating significant differences). The software programs used to perform statistical analysis were ImageJ (Fiji) software, Microsoft Excel, and GraphPad Prism v. 8.0.

MTT assay analysis

Results of the MTT assay revealed that treatment of HeLa cells with the leaf extracts of plants from the Homa environment showed significantly more cytotoxicity as compared to those from the control environment treatments (Fig. 2). Whereas no toxicity was observed in the normal HaCaT cell line. The methanolic extracts of O. sanctum and A. marmelos of Homa environment were found to have lower half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values than those of their respective control sites, indicating their better efficacy against the HeLa cervical cancer cell line. Similarly, the ethanolic extracts of M. koenigii and A. indica of Homa site resulted in enhanced anticancer potential against the cervical cancer cell line compared to that of the control environment plants. As mentioned in Table 3, lower IC50 values were observed in the case of extracts prepared from plants grown in the Homa environment than those of control environment plants. Evaluation of wound healing assay

The capacity of the cells to migrate was assessed by means of an inverted phase contrast microscope. For the wound-healing potential of HeLa cells, plant extracts from Homa and control environments were compared after 24 h of scratching. Consequently, the results indicate a significant inhibition of wound healing or migration in HeLa cells treated with Homa environment plant extracts, in contrast to the plant samples acquired from the control environment (Fig. 3). As seen in Fig. 3b, the percentage scratch area of leaf extracts from control environment showed lower scratch percentage than those of leaf extracts collected from Homa farming environment.

Intracellular ROS generation in HeLa cell line

Cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were monitored after 24 h in both control and Homa environment-treated cells. After treatment with 60 μg/ml of M. koenigii, O. sanctum, A. marmelos, and A. indica of control and Homa environment, the DCF-fluorescence intensity in the HeLa cells was found to be significantly higher in the cells treated with Homa environment plant extracts. As shown in Fig. 4, leaf extracts from both environments generated intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the HeLa cells; however, extracts from the Homa environment exhibited substantially higher ROS production than those from non-Homa(control) environment.

Discussion

Pollution is a major issue that impacts all living forms, including animals and plants. It alters the morphology and chemical composition of plants, leading to a reduction in their medicinal effectiveness. Homa organic farming approaches solve this problem by purifying the atmosphere and increasing the productivity of plants grown in these areas [9,11,12]. The present work was a comparative study to evaluate whether the leaves of four medicinal plants, viz., Murraya koenigii (curry), Ocimum sanctum (tulsi), Aegle marmelos (bael), and Azadirachta indica (neem), grown in Homa environment enhanced the anti-cancerous property.

Plant samples were extracted using two different solvents in order to maximize the yield. Literature indicated that the solvent employed for the leaf extract of Ocimum sanctum (tulsi) and Aegle marmelos (bael) was methanol, while ethanol was used for Murraya koenigii (curry) and Azadirachta indica (neem), since these solvents demonstrated better yield compared to others [21,[31], [32], [33]]. Based on the calculated percentages of total yield from all extracts, a higher yield was noted in the plants cultivated under Homa organic farming compared to the control, with the exception of Azadirachta indica (neem) extract.

The cytotoxic effects of M. koenigii, O. sanctum, A. marmelos, and A. indica leaf extracts of both collection sites were assessed on human HeLa cells for a 24-h period at varying concentrations. Our results indicated that the plants collected from the Homa-therapy environment, where Agnihotra is routinely practiced, showed significantly enhanced anti-cervical cancer efficacy, particularly in M. koenigii and A. marmelos, as evidenced by their lower IC50 values in comparison to those from control (non-Homa) environments. The anticancer effects may be possibly due to enhancement of phytochemical constitution, like alkaloids, flavonoids, and phenolics, in the plants grown in Homa environment [38,39].

Cancer cells produce ROS due to their greater metabolic rate and its increased concentration is crucial for the growth, proliferation, and metastasis of cancer cells [40]. Excessive ROS may induce oxidative stress in cancer cells, potentially resulting in cell death [41]. The current study demonstrates that treatment with Homa organic farming plant samples results in significant elevation in the production of ROS in cervical cancer cells (Fig. 4). Cancer cells, unlike normal cells, have a deficient antioxidant system necessary for mitigating excess ROS and preventing related cellular damage [41]. In this study, the antioxidants like SOD1 and CAT were also found to be downregulated in Homa-treated groups as compared to non-treated groups particularly in M. koenigii, O. sanctum, and A. marmelos.

This study also assessed the impact of Homa environment plants on the migratory capability of cervical cancer cells by using the “wound healing” assay [42]. Results indicated that plants from Homa organic farming markedly inhibited the migratory capacity of cervical cancer cells. According to the observations, M. koenigii and O. sanctum of Homa environment exhibited a maximum scratch area of 92.83% and 90.91%, demonstrating their ability to block the migration of cervical cancer cells (Fig. 3b). Moreover, M. koenigii and O. sanctum in a controlled environment have higher scratch area compared to A. marmelos and A. indica. The significant downregulated expression of MMP9 in Homa grown M. koenigii leaf extracts also supported its enhanced anti-migration ability.

Our observations indicated that plants from Homa organic farming may be more effective than those from non-Homa environments in decreasing cell proliferation, reducing cell viability, increasing ROS levels, and preventing cell migration in cervical cancer cells. Further study may be required to substantiate the efficacy of the therapeutic properties of these plants in the Homa environment.

The drugs and treatment options currently available do not fully cure or reverse the adverse effects of the disease [[43], [45]]. Additionally, current cancer therapies have socioeconomic costs associated with prolonged attendance, and hospital expenses escalate each year [44]. This necessitates the formulation of safer alternative medicines that have been utilized since antiquity. Homa method of cultivation is low-cost and its results in cancer inhibition may yield a low-input, non-chemical means of enhancing plant extract biomedical application.

Limitations

Since, it was a preliminary study, it did not evaluate the specific bioactive compounds in the extracts. The study also does not evaluate the anti-cancer potential and combinatorial effects in in vivo models and long-term toxicities to draw conclusive results.

Conclusion

The Homa habitat has shown an improvement in the anticancer effects particularly in M. koenigii and A. marmelos. However, the effect of other environmental factors like soil nutrients, pollutants or microclimatic conditions remain untested. Future research may be undertaken to identify and quantify specific bioactive compounds and include other cancer models for anti-cancerous validation.
