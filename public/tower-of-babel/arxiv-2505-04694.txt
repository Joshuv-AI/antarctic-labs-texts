Abstract
We present clumi+ , a self-consistent, multi-probe methodology for reconstructing the mass distribution in and around galaxy clusters by combining gravitational lensing and dynamical observations. Building on the joint-likelihood framework of Umetsu 2013 , clumi+ integrates weak-lensing shear and magnification data with projected escape velocity measurements in the cluster infall region, yielding tighter constraints on the gravitational potential without relying on equilibrium assumptions. The mass distribution is modeled using a flexible, piecewise-defined convergence profile that characterizes the azimuthally averaged surface mass density within the lensing field, transitioning to a projected power-law form at larger radii where phase-space constraints complement lensing. Additional strong-lensing constraints are incorporated via central aperture-mass measurements, enabling full-scale mass reconstruction from the cluster core to the outskirts. We validate clumi+ using synthetic weak-lensing and phase-space data for a massive cluster from the IllustrisTNG simulations, demonstrating unbiased recovery of projected and three-dimensional mass profiles and achieving 10 % \% –30 % \% improvement in precision at large radii. As a case study, we apply clumi+ to Abell 2261, combining Subaru and Hubble Space Telescope weak+strong lensing data with spectroscopic measurements from the Hectospec Cluster Survey. This analysis demonstrates the power of multi-probe, equilibrium-free modeling for robust cluster mass reconstruction.



I
Introduction

Galaxy clusters represent the largest self-gravitating systems in the universe, assembled through hierarchical merging governed by the growth of cosmic structure. As such, clusters play a central role in testing models of background cosmology and structure formation
(
Allen et al. 2011
;
Pratt et al. 2019
)
. A key requirement for cluster-based cosmological studies, such as cluster abundance measurements
(
Rosati et al. 2002
)
, is the precise characterization of the dark matter distribution in and around cluster halos, which encodes critical information about the physics of structure growth. In this context, the standard
\Lambda
cold dark matter (
\Lambda
CDM) paradigm and its viable extensions
(
Spergel & Steinhardt 2000
;
Schive et al. 2014
)
predict distinct, testable signatures in the density and velocity structure of galaxy clusters
(
Taylor & Navarro 2001
;
Hjorth & Williams 2010
;
Aung et al. 2023
;
Williams et al. 2025
)
.

Gravitational lensing provides a direct and robust probe of the mass distribution in clusters, which is dominated by dark matter. Strong lensing produces highly distorted and multiply imaged background galaxies in the dense cores of massive halos
(
Kneib & Natarajan 2011
;
Meneghetti 2021
)
, whereas weak lensing maps the projected mass distribution on larger scales by measuring coherent distortions and magnification effects in background galaxy populations
(
Bartelmann & Schneider 2001
;
Umetsu 2020
)
.

A key advantage of weak lensing is its ability to constrain the projected mass distribution without relying on assumptions about the dynamical state of the system
(
Jee et al. 2005
;
Bradač et al. 2006
;
Jauzac et al. 2012
)
. Accordingly, weak lensing has been established as a critical tool for calibrating observable–mass relations in cluster samples selected through X-ray, Sunyaev–Zel’dovich (SZ) effect, or optical surveys
(
Applegate et al. 2014
;
Hoekstra et al. 2015
;
Okabe & Smith 2016
;
Sereno et al. 2017
;
Bocquet et al. 2019
;
Umetsu et al. 2020
;
Chiu et al. 2022
;
Grandis et al. 2024
)
.

Spectroscopic data offer an independent and complementary avenue for probing cluster mass distributions by characterizing the velocity structure around clusters in projected phase space. Redshift diagrams constructed from spectroscopic measurements of tracer galaxies enable dynamical mass estimates through a variety of techniques. In particular, Jeans modeling and virial analyses infer cluster masses from the observed line-of-sight velocity dispersion of member galaxies
(
Mamon et al. 2013
;
Biviano et al. 2013
;
Pizzuti et al. 2016
;
Sereno et al. 2025
)
, under assumptions of dynamical equilibrium and orbital velocity anisotropy. In contrast, the caustic technique identifies the outer envelope of the galaxy distribution in redshift space
(
Diaferio & Geller 1997
;
Diaferio 1999
;
Serra et al. 2011
)
, which traces the projected escape velocity profile and enables mass estimates extending into the infall regions
(
Geller et al. 1999
;
Geller et al. 2013
;
Rines et al. 2013
)
. Joint analyses combining lensing and dynamical tracers have also been employed to test models of modified gravity in cluster outskirts
(
Pizzuti et al. 2017
;
Butt et al. 2024
;
Butt et al. 2025
)
and to constrain the orbital anisotropy of galaxy populations within clusters
(
Lemze et al. 2009
;
Stark et al. 2019
)
.

In this paper, we develop
clumi+
(short for Cluster Lensing Unbiased Multi-probe Inversion with spectroscopic constraints), an extended multi-probe mass reconstruction framework that builds on the
clumi
algorithm introduced by
Umetsu et al. 2011
and
Umetsu 2013
(see also
Umetsu et al. 2015
and
Umetsu et al. 2018
for a two-dimensional implementation). The original framework combines weak-lensing shear and magnification profiles with strong-lensing aperture mass constraints, enabling flexible modeling of cluster surface mass density profiles using a piecewise-defined approach, with minimal prior assumptions.
clumi+
expands this formalism by incorporating projected escape velocity measurements derived from redshift diagrams. It is especially powerful in the cluster outskirts and infall regions, where spectroscopic phase-space information provides complementary constraints on the three-dimensional (3D) mass distribution in an equilibrium-free manner. This extension enhances reconstruction precision and facilitates physical interpretation of dynamical structure, including the dynamical boundary of cluster halos
(
Diemer & Kravtsov 2014
;
Pizzardo et al. 2024
)
.

II
Cluster–Galaxy Lensing Theory

The effects of weak gravitational lensing on background galaxies are characterized by the convergence,
\kappa
(the isotropic magnification term), and the complex shear with spin-2 rotational symmetry,
\gamma=|\gamma|e^{2i\phi_{\gamma}}
, in two-dimensional (2D) projection. For a comprehensive review of cluster galaxy weak lensing, see
Umetsu 2020
, and for strong lensing, refer to
Meneghetti 2021
and
Natarajan et al. 2024
.

II.1
Basics of Cluster–galaxy Weak Lensing

The lensing convergence is defined as
\kappa=\Sigma/\Sigma_{\mathrm{cr}}
, where
\Sigma
is the surface mass overdensity of a lens in units of the critical surface density for gravitational lensing:

\Sigma_{\mathrm{cr}}(z_{l},z_{s})=\frac{c^{2}}{4\pi G}\frac{D_{s}}{D_{l}D_{ls}}=\frac{c^{2}}{4\pi GD_{l}\beta},

(1)

with
c
the speed of light,
G
the gravitational constant, and
D_{l}(z_{l})
,
D_{s}(z_{s})
, and
D_{ls}(z_{l},z_{s})
the observer–lens, observer–source, and lens–source angular diameter distances, respectively. The geometric lensing efficiency,
\beta(z_{l},z_{s})=D_{ls}/D_{s}
, determines the strength of lensing as a function of the lens redshift
z_{l}
and source redshift
z_{s}
, where we set
\beta(z_{l},z_{s})=0
for unlensed sources with
z_{s}\leqslant z_{l}
. The shear and convergence thus depend on both
(z_{l},z_{s})
and the image position
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}=\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\perp}/D_{l}
. For conciseness, we omit the explicit dependence on
z_{l}
in the following discussion.

The gravitational shear
\gamma=\gamma_{1}+i\gamma_{2}
is directly observable from the image ellipticities of background galaxies in the weak-lensing limit,
|\kappa|\ll 1
. The primary observable in weak lensing is the reduced shear
g=g_{1}+ig_{2}
,

g=\frac{\gamma}{1-\kappa},

(2)

which remains invariant under the global transformation
\kappa(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\to\lambda\kappa(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})+1-\lambda
and
\gamma(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\to\lambda\gamma(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
with an arbitrary constant
\lambda
for a given source plane. The term
1-\lambda\equiv\kappa_{0}
represents an additive constant convergence. This transformation, known as the mass-sheet degeneracy
(
Schneider & Seitz 1995
)
, can be broken or alleviated, for example, by measuring the magnification factor in subcritical regions, outside the critical curves:

\mu=\frac{1}{(1-\kappa)^{2}-|\gamma|^{2}}.

(3)

The magnification factor
\mu
transforms as
\mu(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\to\lambda^{-2}\mu(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
.

The reduced shear
g_{1,2}
can be decomposed into the tangential component
g_{+}=\gamma_{+}/(1-\kappa)
and the
45^{\circ}
-rotated cross-shear component
g_{\times}=\gamma_{\times}/(1-\kappa)
with respect to a given reference point. The tangential- and cross-shear components (
\gamma_{+},\gamma_{\times}
) averaged around a circle of radius
\theta
satisfy the following identities for an arbitrary choice of the center:

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\overline{\kappa}(<\theta)-\kappa(\theta)\equiv\frac{\Delta\Sigma(r_{\perp})}{\Sigma_{\mathrm{cr}}},

(4)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\times}(\theta)

\displaystyle=0,

where
\kappa(\theta)=\Sigma(r_{\perp})/\Sigma_{\mathrm{cr}}
is the azimuthally averaged convergence at radius
\theta
and
\overline{\kappa}(<\theta)=\overline{\Sigma}(<r_{\perp})/\Sigma_{\mathrm{cr}}
is the average convergence interior to
\theta
.
In Equation (
4
), we have introduced the excess surface mass density
\Delta\Sigma(r_{\perp})=\overline{\Sigma}(<r_{\perp})-\Sigma(r_{\perp})
.
In the absence of higher-order effects, the azimuthally averaged
\times
-component of weak lensing is expected to vanish. Thus, the presence of
g_{\times}
distortions serves as a null test for systematic errors.

II.2
Source Redshift Distribution

For weak-lensing analysis, we consider a population of source galaxies characterized by their intrinsic (unlensed) redshift distribution,
\overline{N}(z_{s})
.
Different selection criteria and quality cuts are typically applied for measuring shear and magnification effects, leading to distinct
\overline{N}(z_{s})
distributions for each effect.
We denote the redshift distribution functions for the shear and magnification measurements as
\overline{N}_{g}(z)
and
\overline{N}_{\mu}(z)
, respectively.

We express source-averaged moments
\langle\beta^{n}\rangle_{X}
(
n=1,2,\dots
) for a given population (
X=g,\mu
) as

\langle\beta^{n}\rangle_{X}=\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dz\,\overline{N}_{X}(z)\beta^{n}(z_{l},z)\right]\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dz\,\overline{N}_{X}(z)\right]^{-1}.

(5)

In general,
\overline{N}(z)
can include foreground galaxies. The contribution from unlensed objects with
\beta=0
is thus accounted for in the calculation of
\langle\beta^{n}\rangle_{X}
.

We introduce the relative lensing efficiency of each source population:

\langle W^{n}\rangle_{X}=\langle\beta^{n}\rangle_{X}/\beta_{\infty}^{n}

(6)

with
\beta_{\infty}=\beta(z_{l},z_{s,\infty})
defined with respect to a reference source in the far background
(
Bartelmann & Schneider 2001
)
. We use a reference redshift of
z_{s,\infty}=20,000
, as adopted in the CLASH program
(
Umetsu et al. 2014
;
Merten et al. 2015
)
. The associated critical surface density is

\Sigma_{\mathrm{cr},\infty}(z_{l})=\frac{c^{2}}{4\pi GD_{l}(z_{l})}\frac{1}{\beta_{\infty}(z_{l})}.

(7)

Hereafter, we use the far-background convergence
\kappa_{\infty}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\equiv\Sigma(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\perp})/\Sigma_{\mathrm{cr},\infty}
to describe the projected mass distribution of the cluster lens.

II.3
Weak Lensing: Tangential Distortion

From shape measurements of background galaxies, we construct azimuthally averaged radial profiles of the tangential distortion,
g_{+}(\theta)
, and the cross component,
g_{\times}(\theta)
, in subcritical regions as functions of cluster-centric angular radius
\theta
. The source-averaged expectation value for the reduced tangential shear,
\langle g_{+}\rangle(\theta)
, can be approximated as follows
(
Umetsu 2020
)
:

\displaystyle\langle g_{+}\rangle(\theta)

\displaystyle=\left[\int\!dz\,N_{g}(z)g_{+}(\theta,z)\right]\left[\int\!dz\,N_{g}(z)\right]^{-1}

(8)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\langle\gamma_{+}\rangle(\theta)}{1-f_{g}\langle\kappa\rangle(\theta)},

where

\langle\kappa\rangle(\theta)=\langle W\rangle_{g}\kappa_{\infty}(\theta)
and
\langle\gamma_{+}\rangle(\theta)=\langle W\rangle_{g}[\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\theta)-\kappa_{\infty}(\theta)]
are the source-averaged convergence and tangential shear, respectively, and
f_{g}=\langle W^{2}\rangle_{g}/\langle W\rangle_{g}^{2}
is a dimensionless correction factor of the order unity.
1
1

1

Gravitational lensing observables generally depend nonlinearly on the lensing potential. Consequently, averaging over the source redshift distribution introduces nonlinear effects. In the weak-lensing regime, however, these effects are typically well approximated by low-order corrections involving the moments
\langle W^{n}\rangle
.

II.4
Weak Lensing: Magnification Bias

In the presence of gravitational lensing, the source counts observed for a given magnitude cut
m_{\mathrm{cut}}
are modified depending on the intrinsic slope of the source luminosity function. For details, see Section 5 of
Umetsu 2020
.

II.4.1
Density Depletion and Enhancement

The magnification bias for a flux-limited sample of background galaxies is defined as the ratio of their magnified counts
n_{\mu}(\theta|<m_{\mathrm{cut}})
around a cluster lens to the unlensed mean counts
\overline{n}_{\mu}(<m_{\mathrm{cut}})
without lensing:

\displaystyle\langle b_{\mu}\rangle(\theta)=\frac{n_{\mu}(\theta|<m_{\mathrm{cut}})}{\overline{n}_{\mu}(<m_{\mathrm{cut}})}=

\displaystyle\left[\int\!dz\,N_{\mu}(\theta,z|<m_{\mathrm{cut}})\right]

(9)

\displaystyle\times\left[\int\!dz\,\overline{N}_{\mu}(z|<m_{\mathrm{cut}})\right]^{-1},

where
N_{\mu}(\theta,z)
and
\overline{N}_{\mu}(z)
are the lensed and unlensed redshift distribution functions of the flux-limited source sample, respectively (Section
II.2
).

The mass-sheet degeneracy can be lifted by combining shear and magnification measurements (Equations (
8
) and (
9
)), provided the unlensed source background density
\overline{n}_{\mu}(<m_{\mathrm{cut}})
is known or can be estimated from data.

In weak lensing, the change in magnitude,
\delta m=2.5\log_{10}\mu
, due to magnification is small compared to the range of magnitudes over which the slope of the luminosity function varies. The source counts
\overline{N}_{\mu}(<m_{\mathrm{cut}})
can thus be locally approximated by a power law at a given cutoff magnitude
m_{\mathrm{cut}}
. Accordingly, we can interpret the magnification bias observed in subcritical regions by
(
Umetsu 2020
)

\langle b_{\mu}\rangle(\theta)\simeq\langle\mu^{-1}\rangle^{1-\alpha}(\theta),

(10)

where
\alpha
is the logarithmic count slope evaluated at
m_{\mathrm{cut}}
:
2
2

2

Instead of
\alpha
, a notation of
s\equiv 0.4\alpha
is often used in the literature
(
Broadhurst et al. 2005
, e.g.,)
. The count slope
\alpha
is related to the faint-end slope
\alpha_{\mathrm{LF}}
of the source luminosity function as
\alpha=-(\alpha_{\mathrm{LF}}+1)
.

\alpha(m_{\mathrm{cut}})=2.5\frac{d\log_{10}\overline{N}_{\mu}(<m)}{dm}\Bigg|_{m_{\mathrm{cut}}},

(11)

The term
\langle\mu^{-1}\rangle
represents the source-averaged inverse magnification,
which is approximated by
(
Umetsu 2020
)

\langle\mu^{-1}\rangle(\theta)=\left[1-\langle\kappa\rangle(\theta)\right]^{2}-\langle\gamma_{+}\rangle^{2}(\theta),

(12)

with
\langle\kappa\rangle(\theta)=\langle W\rangle_{\mu}\kappa_{\infty}(\theta)

and
\langle\gamma_{+}\rangle(\theta)=\langle W\rangle_{\mu}[\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\theta)-\kappa_{\infty}(\theta)]
.

Equation (
10
) shows that a net count depletion (
\langle b_{\mu}\rangle<1
) occurs when
\alpha<1
, as the geometric area distortion dominates in magnification bias. In this depletion regime, a key advantage is that the effect is largely insensitive to the detailed form of the source luminosity function. For a maximally depleted population with
\alpha=0
, the bias factor simplifies to
\langle b_{\mu}\rangle=\langle\mu^{-1}\rangle
. Conversely, in the density enhancement regime (
\alpha>1
), interpreting the observed lensing signal requires precise knowledge of the intrinsic luminosity function
(
Chiu et al. 2020
)
, particularly in the nonlinear regime where the amplification factor is large (e.g.,
\mu\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{>}}\;$}1.5
).

The net magnification effect on source counts is expected to vanish when
\alpha=1
. Hence, lensing magnification provides a natural null test
(
Chiu et al. 2020
;
Umetsu et al. 2022
)
, enabling an assessment of residual biases that may be present in the lensing-selected sample.

II.4.2
Multi-population Magnification Bias

Deep multi-band photometry across a broad wavelength range enables the selection of distinct background galaxy populations in color–color space
(
Medezinski et al. 2010
;
Medezinski et al. 2018
)
. Since a fixed flux limit corresponds to different intrinsic luminosities at different redshifts, the source counts of color-selected populations sample different regimes of magnification bias, from density enhancement to depletion. Incorporating independent count measurements from multiple source populations offers two key advantages for cluster lensing analyses. First, it improves the statistical precision of lensing constraints
(
Umetsu 2013
)
. Second, it reduces sensitivity to the intrinsic angular clustering of background galaxies, as distinct source populations are expected to be spatially uncorrelated
(
Broadhurst 1995
)
.

As demonstrated by
Umetsu 2013
, both
clumi
and
clumi
+ support magnification bias constraints from multiple background populations in a joint likelihood framework. In this study, however, we restrict our analysis to a single source population in the depletion regime for simplicity.

II.5
Strong Lensing: Aperture Mass

Detailed modeling of the deflection field,
\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}\psi(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
, using sets of multiply lensed images with spectroscopic redshifts enables precise determination of the locations of critical curves. The lensing potential
\psi(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
is given by

\psi(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\frac{1}{\pi}\int\ln(|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime}|)\,\kappa(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime})\,d^{2}\theta^{\prime},

(13)

where critical curves correspond to regions where the Jacobian determinant vanishes,
\det[\mathsf{A}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})]=0
, and
\mathsf{A}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
is the Jacobian matrix of the lens mapping. The locations of these critical curves provide tight constraints on the projected mass enclosed within them.

In this context, the term Einstein radius commonly refers to the size of the outer critical curve
(
Meneghetti 2021
)
. The effective Einstein radius is defined as

\theta_{\mathrm{Ein}}=\sqrt{A_{\mathrm{cr}}/\pi},

(14)

where
A_{\mathrm{cr}}
is the area enclosed by the outer critical curve.

The aperture mass enclosed within a cylinder of radius
\theta
is defined as

M_{\mathrm{ap}}(<\theta)=\Sigma_{\mathrm{cr},\infty}D_{l}^{2}\int_{|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime}|<\theta}\!\kappa_{\infty}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime})\,d^{2}\theta^{\prime},

(15)

When evaluated near the Einstein radius
\theta_{\mathrm{Ein}}
,
M_{\mathrm{ap}}(<\theta)
becomes less sensitive to assumptions about the underlying mass model and is tightly constrained by strong-lensing observations
(
Umetsu et al. 2012
;
Remolina González et al. 2021a
;
Remolina González et al. 2021b
)
. This makes it a key observable in the strong-lensing regime
(
Coe et al. 2010
)
.

As the Einstein radius
\theta_{\mathrm{Ein}}(z)
increases with source redshift
z
, strongly lensed images from sources at different redshifts form nested configurations spanning a broader radial range. Consequently, multiple sets of strongly lensed images from different source redshifts can be used to constrain
M_{\mathrm{ap}}(<\theta)
over an extended radial range
(
Coe et al. 2012
;
Umetsu et al. 2012
;
Umetsu et al. 2016
)
.

III
Projected Escape Velocity Formalism

III.1
Caustic Technique and Projected Phase Space

Complementing gravitational lensing, redshift diagrams in projected phase space provide a powerful probe of the velocity structure around galaxy clusters. In this study, we focus on the identification and use of “caustic” boundaries, which delineate the velocity envelope of tracer galaxies in redshift space. These boundaries trace the projected escape velocity profile, which is directly related to the underlying 3D gravitational potential of the cluster.

The caustic technique
(
Diaferio & Geller 1997
;
Diaferio 1999
)
identifies these escape velocity edges from the distribution of galaxies in projected phase space. The resulting caustic amplitude,
\mathcal{A}(r_{\perp})>0
, is determined as a function of the projected cluster-centric radius
r_{\perp}
, and interpreted as a local estimate of the Newtonian escape velocity:
3
3

3

Escape velocity,
v_{\mathrm{esc}}(r)
, is defined by the gravitational potential
\Phi_{\mathrm{N}}(r)
at radius
r
, independent of the dynamical state or orbits of tracer galaxies. Even in the infall region, where tracers are unbound and on first approach,
v_{\mathrm{esc}}(r)
remains a well-defined upper bound for bound motion. In the caustic algorithm, this quantity reflects a kinematic threshold, not the binding status of the tracers.

\mathcal{A}^{2}(r_{\perp})=\frac{v_{\mathrm{esc}}^{2}(r_{\perp})}{\mathcal{G}}=\frac{-2\Phi_{\mathrm{N}}(r_{\perp})}{\mathcal{G}},

(16)

where
\Phi_{\mathrm{N}}(r_{\perp})
is the Newtonian gravitational potential, and
\mathcal{G}
is a dimensionless
depletion factor
that quantifies the reduction of the observed velocity envelope relative to the true 3D escape velocity. The caustic technique has been tested and calibrated against cosmological simulations
(
Diaferio 1999
;
Serra et al. 2011
;
Pizzardo et al. 2023
)
, including dedicated tests on the TNG300-1 run of the IllustrisTNG simulations
(
Pizzardo et al. 2023
)
.

Equation (
16
) reflects the Newtonian interpretation of escape velocity adopted in
clumi+
, which neglects cosmic expansion and acceleration. While this approximation is not directly validated, the reliability of
clumi+
is assessed through its ability to recover unbiased mass profiles in both two and three dimensions, using synthetic observations of a realistic cluster from the IllustrisTNG simulation suite (Section
V
).

Throughout this study, we refer to
\mathcal{A}(r_{\perp})
as the
projected escape velocity
, following the convention of the caustic technique
(
Diaferio 1999
)
. This quantity characterizes the amplitude of the observed velocity envelope in redshift space and is distinct from the true 3D escape velocity.

III.2
Physical Interpretation of the Depletion Factor
\mathcal{G}

In the caustic algorithm developed by
Diaferio 1999
, the depletion factor
\mathcal{G}
is interpreted as a
geometric
effect arising from the orbital velocity anisotropy of tracer galaxies. Under the assumption of negligible cluster rotation,
4
4

4

When the cluster rotation is negligible,
\langle v^{2}_{\theta}\rangle=\langle v^{2}_{\mathrm{los}}\rangle
and
\langle v_{r}^{2}\rangle=\langle v^{2}\rangle-2\langle v^{2}_{\mathrm{los}}\rangle
, allowing the escape velocity anisotropy to be characterized via the line-of-sight velocity dispersion.

it is related to the velocity anisotropy parameter
\beta_{v}(r)
by

\mathcal{G}=\frac{\langle v_{\mathrm{esc}}^{2}\rangle(r_{\perp})}{\langle v^{2}_{\mathrm{esc,los}}\rangle(r_{\perp})}=\frac{3-2\beta_{v}(r_{\perp})}{1-\beta_{v}(r_{\perp})},

(17)

where the brackets
\langle\cdot\rangle
denote an ensemble average over tracer velocities within a local 3D volume,
v_{\mathrm{esc,los}}
represents the line-of-sight component of the escape velocity, and
\beta_{v}
is defined as
(
Binney & Tremaine 2008
)

\beta_{v}(r)=1-\frac{\langle v^{2}_{\theta}\rangle(r)}{\langle v^{2}_{r}\rangle(r)},

(18)

with
v_{r}
and
v_{\theta}
denoting the radial and tangential components of the 3D velocity of a tracer galaxy. Theoretically,
\beta_{v}
and
\mathcal{G}
span characteristic values associated with distinct orbital configurations: purely radial orbits (
\beta_{v}\to 1
,
\mathcal{G}\to\infty
), isotropic orbits (
\beta_{v}=0
,
\mathcal{G}=3
), and purely tangential orbits (
\beta_{v}\to-\infty
,
\mathcal{G}\to 2
). The anisotropy parameter
\beta_{v}
is related to the depletion factor
\mathcal{G}
via
\beta_{v}=(\mathcal{G}-3)/(\mathcal{G}-2)
.

More recent studies
(
Halenka et al. 2022
;
Rodriguez et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, however, suggest that
\mathcal{G}
may primarily reflect statistical undersampling of galaxy velocities near the caustic edges, rather than purely geometric effects from orbital anisotropy. Within the
clumi+
framework, we adopt an agnostic stance on the physical interpretation of
\mathcal{G}
, treating it as a free parameter to be empirically constrained from the data. Nevertheless, we use the original
Diaferio 1999
formulation to motivate a plausible prior range for
\mathcal{G}
in our Bayesian analysis (Section
IV.5
).

III.3
Focus on the Infall Region

Our analysis focuses on the outer infall regions of galaxy clusters, specifically at
r>R_{\mathrm{inf}}\equiv 2\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
, a scale comparable to the
R_{\mathrm{200\mathrm{m}}}
radius of high-mass clusters with
M_{200\mathrm{m}}\sim 10^{15}\,h^{-1}\,M_{\odot}

(
Umetsu & Diemer 2017
)
. In these regions, redshift diagrams provide robust sampling of tracer galaxies, enabling reliable measurements of the projected escape velocity
(
Serra et al. 2011
)
. Cosmological simulations of hierarchical structure formation suggest that
\mathcal{G}
varies only weakly with cluster-centric distance at these scales, remaining approximately constant due to the coherent infall motion of surrounding galaxies
(
Diaferio 1999
;
Serra et al. 2011
)
. Crucially, as long as
\mathcal{G}
is effectively constant at
r>R_{\mathrm{inf}}
, the
clumi+
algorithm yields robust inferences, independent of any specific interpretation of this depletion factor. Even in cases where the velocity anisotropy parameter varies significantly, for example
\beta_{v}=0.3\pm 0.2
in cluster outskirts
(
Abdullah et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, the corresponding variation in the caustic depletion factor is modest, with
\mathcal{G}=3.4^{+0.6}_{-0.3}
.

Notably, the dynamical boundary of galaxy clusters—commonly referred to as the splashback radius,
R_{\mathrm{sp}}
—is predicted to be located at
R_{\mathrm{sp}}\sim R_{200\mathrm{m}}
for accreting halos such as galaxy clusters
(
Diemer & Kravtsov 2014
;
More et al. 2015
)
. The splashback radius marks a physical transition separating the multistream intra-cluster region from the outer infall region
(
Gunn & Gott 1972
;
Diemer & Kravtsov 2014
;
More et al. 2015
)
. In these outer regions, the 3D density profile around halos follows a power-law form extending out to
r\sim 10\,R_{200\mathrm{m}}

(
Diemer & Kravtsov 2014
;
Diemer 2023
;
Diemer 2025
)
. This motivates our adoption of a projected power-law model to describe the external mass distribution beyond the lensing aperture (see Section
IV.2
).

IV
Methodology: The CLUMI+ Algorithm

In this section, we introduce
clumi+
, a new methodology for modeling the mass distribution around galaxy clusters using a self-consistent, multi-probe framework. Building on the joint-likelihood formalism developed in the original
clumi
algorithm
(
Umetsu et al. 2011
;
Umetsu 2013
)
,
clumi+
extends the approach to incorporate projected escape velocity constraints in addition to lensing observables.

The underlying mass model characterizes the azimuthally averaged radial profile of the lensing convergence,
\kappa_{\infty}(\theta)=\Sigma(r_{\perp})/\Sigma_{\mathrm{cr},\infty}
, where
r_{\perp}=D_{l}\theta
is the projected cluster-centric radius. Within the lensing field,
\kappa_{\infty}(\theta)
is represented as a piecewise-constant function defined over concentric annuli, enabling a free-form reconstruction of the projected mass distribution. Beyond the lensing aperture, the model transitions to a projected power-law profile to describe the outer mass distribution. This choice is motivated by theoretical results from cosmological
N
-body simulations, which find that the 3D density profile of cluster halos follows an approximately power-law behavior in the infall region
(
Diemer & Kravtsov 2014
;
Diemer 2023
;
Diemer 2025
)
.

IV.1
Multi-probe Cluster Constraints

The
clumi+
framework combines constraints from lensing and dynamical observations, extending the methodology of
clumi

(
Umetsu 2013
)
. Specifically,
clumi+
jointly analyzes projected escape velocity measurements
\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{esc}}}
alongside weak-lensing observables:
\{\langle g_{+,i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{WL}}}
and
\{\langle n_{\mu,i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{WL}}}
. Additionally, both
clumi+
and
clumi
can incorporate strong-lensing aperture-mass constraints,
\{M_{\mathrm{ap},i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{SL}}}
.

Each weak-lensing profile is measured in
N_{\mathrm{WL}}
annular bins over the range
\theta\in[\theta_{\mathrm{min}}^{(\mathrm{WL})},\theta_{\mathrm{max}}^{(\mathrm{WL})}]
centered on the cluster. The total number of independent constraints is
N_{\mathrm{data}}=2N_{\mathrm{WL}}+N_{\mathrm{SL}}+N_{\mathrm{esc}}
,
where
N_{\mathrm{SL}}
represents the number of available strong-lensing constraints and
N_{\mathrm{esc}}
denotes the number of projected escape velocity constraints in the outer infall region at
r_{\perp}>R_{\mathrm{inf}}=2\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
(see Section
IV.4
).

The minimum radius for lensing constraints,
\theta_{\mathrm{min}}
, is set by the availability of strong-lensing data. If strong-lensing constraints are available (
N_{\mathrm{SL}}>0
), we set
\theta_{\mathrm{min}}=\theta_{\mathrm{min}}^{(\mathrm{SL})}
. Otherwise, in the absence of strong-lensing constraints (
N_{\mathrm{SL}}=0
), the minimum radius is determined by weak-lensing data,
\theta_{\mathrm{min}}=\theta_{\mathrm{min}}^{(\mathrm{WL})}
. The maximum radius for lensing constraints is
\theta_{\mathrm{max}}=\theta_{\mathrm{max}}^{(\mathrm{WL})}
.

IV.2
Mass Model

The mass model in
clumi+
is described by a set of parameters,
m
, which characterize the azimuthally averaged projected mass distribution in terms of the lensing convergence
\kappa_{\infty}(\theta)
. This includes the average convergence within the innermost circular aperture of radius
\theta_{\mathrm{min}}
, defined as

\overline{\kappa}_{\infty,\mathrm{min}}\equiv\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\theta_{\mathrm{min}}),

(19)

along with a piecewise-constant convergence profile,
\{\kappa_{\infty,i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, defined over a set of
N
annular bins covering the lensing aperture. For full details, see Appendix
A
.

Following the formalism of
Umetsu 2013
, we define the
(N+1)
-dimensional lensing signal vector as

\mbox{\boldmath$s$}=\left\{\overline{\kappa}_{\infty,\mathrm{min}},\kappa_{\infty,i}\right\}_{i=1}^{N},

(20)

where

N=N_{\mathrm{SL}}+N_{\mathrm{WL}}

(21)

is the total number of radial bins spanning the interval
\theta\in[\theta_{\mathrm{min}},\theta_{\mathrm{max}}]
, with
N_{\mathrm{SL}}
and
N_{\mathrm{WL}}
denoting the number of strong- and weak-lensing bins, respectively.

This piecewise-defined convergence model provides a flexible, free-form representation of the projected mass distribution within the lensing field. It enables direct predictions for the full set of lensing observables: the aperture masses
\{M_{\mathrm{ap},i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{SL}}}
, the tangential reduced shear profile
\{\langle g_{+,i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{WL}}}
, and the magnification bias profile
\{\langle n_{\mu,i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{WL}}}
(see Appendix
A.1
for details).

At this stage, the mass model adopted in
clumi+
is formally equivalent to that of the original
clumi
algorithm
(
Umetsu 2013
)
, and serves as the foundation for incorporating additional dynamical constraints in the outer regions.

To extend the mass model beyond the radial range directly probed by lensing data, we include an external convergence component defined by a power-law profile:

\Theta(\theta-\theta_{\mathrm{max}})\,\kappa_{\infty,\mathrm{ext}}\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta_{\mathrm{max}}}\right)^{-q},

(22)

where
\kappa_{\infty,\mathrm{ext}}
is the normalization at
\theta_{\mathrm{max}}
,
q
is the logarithmic slope, and
\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function, defined as
\Theta(x)=1
for
x\geqslant 0
and
\Theta(x)=0
otherwise. This term accounts for the contribution of mass beyond the lensing aperture and is essential for modeling the gravitational potential in the cluster infall regions, where phase-space information becomes increasingly informative.

In the
clumi+
framework, the complete set of model parameters,
m
, consists of the piecewise-defined convergence parameters
s
describing the lensing field, the external power-law parameters
\kappa_{\infty,\mathrm{ext}}
and
q
, and the depletion factor
\mathcal{G}
associated with the escape velocity profile:

\mbox{\boldmath$m$}=\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$s$},\mathcal{G},q,\kappa_{\infty,\mathrm{ext}}\right\}.

(23)

This
(N+4)
-dimensional parameter vector enables self-consistent predictions for both lensing and dynamical observables, bridging the cluster core and outer infall regions in a unified modeling framework.

To evaluate the likelihood contribution from the projected escape velocity, we model the gravitational potential,
\Phi_{\mathrm{N}}(r)
, in the outer infall regions where caustic amplitude measurements provide additional constraints. As outlined below, we compute
\Phi_{\mathrm{N}}(r)
from the underlying mass distribution under the assumption of spherical symmetry in the outer infall regions, allowing us to predict the escape velocity profile,
v_{\mathrm{esc}}(r)=\sqrt{-2\Phi_{\mathrm{N}}(r)}
, for a given mass model
m
(see Appendix
A.2
for details).

The total mass enclosed within a 3D radius
r
is related to the Newtonian potential via

\Phi_{\mathrm{N}}(r)=-\int_{r}^{\infty}\!\frac{GM(<r^{\prime})}{r^{\prime 2}}\,dr^{\prime}\simeq-\int_{r}^{r_{\infty}}\!\frac{GM(<r^{\prime})}{r^{\prime 2}}\,dr^{\prime},

(24)

where we truncate the integral at a finite outer boundary,
r_{\infty}=20\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
, corresponding to approximately
10\,R_{200\mathrm{m}}
for high-mass clusters. This choice is motivated by theoretical studies indicating that a power-law description of the outer mass distribution becomes inaccurate on such scales, where statistical contributions from large-scale structure begin to dominate. At these radii, the 2-halo term derived from the matter power spectrum provides a more accurate representation of the density field
(
Diemer & Kravtsov 2014
)
. Nevertheless, for
r>20\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
, the contribution to the cluster potential
\Phi_{\mathrm{N}}(r)
from these distant structures is expected to be negligible and can be safely ignored.

To obtain
M(<r)
from the projected mass distribution, we use the Abel integral transform
(
Binney & Tremaine 2008
;
Umetsu et al. 2011
;
Coles et al. 2014
)
:

M(<r)=\pi r^{2}\overline{\Sigma}(<r)-4\int_{r}^{r_{\infty}}\!Rf\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)\Sigma(R)\,dR,

(25)

where
f(x)=(x^{2}-1)^{-1/2}-\tan^{-1}(x^{2}-1)^{-1/2}
,
\overline{\Sigma}(<r)
is the mean surface density within radius
r
, and
\Sigma(R)
is the azimuthally averaged surface mass density.
The first term corresponds to the projected (cylindrical) mass within
radius
r
,
M_{\mathrm{ap}}(<r)=\pi r^{2}\overline{\Sigma}(<r)
,
while the second term removes the contribution from mass elements at

r^{\prime}>r
, effectively deprojecting the cylindrical mass to obtain the
enclosed 3D mass. In our numerical implementation, we truncate the integral at
r_{\infty}=20\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
.

Notably, it is sufficient to evaluate the 3D mass profile
M(<r)
at
r>R_{\mathrm{inf}}=2\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
, where the escape velocity measurements
\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{esc}}}
are incorporated into the joint likelihood (Section
IV.4
). In contrast, the projected mass distribution
\Sigma(r_{\perp})
receives integrated contributions along the line of sight. Therefore, anchoring the outer 3D potential through velocity observables constrains the line-of-sight mass contributions and breaks degeneracies in the projected lensing observables. This coupling leads to improved stability and precision in the reconstruction of the convergence profile
\kappa_{\infty}(\theta)
, particularly in the outer halo regions.

IV.3
Calibration Parameters

clumi+
accounts for calibration uncertainties in the multi-probe analysis through a set of nuisance parameters. These include the relative lensing efficiencies of the weak-lensing source populations,
\langle W\rangle_{g}
and
\langle W\rangle_{\mu}
, along with the unlensed count normalization and logarithmic count slope for magnification bias,
\overline{n}_{\mu}
and
\alpha
, respectively (Section
III
):

\mbox{\boldmath$c$}=\{\langle W\rangle_{g},\langle W\rangle_{\mu},\overline{n}_{\mu},\alpha\}.

(26)

These four parameters are treated as calibration nuisance parameters and marginalized over in the joint likelihood analysis.

Combining the mass model and calibration nuisance parameters, the total number of free parameters in the joint likelihood analysis is
N+8
:

\mbox{\boldmath$p$}=\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$m$},\mbox{\boldmath$c$}\right\}.

(27)

IV.4
Joint Likelihood Function

In a Bayesian framework, we sample from the posterior probability density function of the model parameters,
p
, given the observed data,
d
, denoted as
P(\mbox{\boldmath$p$}|\mbox{\boldmath$d$})
. The expectation values of any statistical function of
p
converge to those derived from the marginalized posterior distribution,
P(\mbox{\boldmath$p$}|\mbox{\boldmath$d$})
. Bayes’ theorem relates the posterior probability to the prior and likelihood as

P(\mbox{\boldmath$p$}|\mbox{\boldmath$d$})\propto P(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})P(\mbox{\boldmath$d$}|\mbox{\boldmath$p$}),

(28)

where
P(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})
is the prior distribution of the parameters, and
\mathcal{L}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})\equiv P(\mbox{\boldmath$d$}|\mbox{\boldmath$p$})
is the likelihood function, describing the probability of the data given the model parameters.

For a joint analysis of weak-lensing and escape velocity measurements, the total likelihood function
\mathcal{L}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})
is given by

\mathcal{L}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WL}}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{esc}}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$}),

(29)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WL}}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})=\mathcal{L}_{g}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})\mathcal{L}_{\mu}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})
.
Here,
\mathcal{L}_{g}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})
,
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{esc}}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})
correspond to the likelihood functions for the observables
\{\langle g_{+,i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{WL}}}
,
\{\langle n_{\mu,i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{WL}}}
, and
\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{esc}}}
, respectively.

If strong-lensing aperture-mass constraints
\{M_{\mathrm{ap},i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{SL}}}
are available, the total likelihood function is extended to

\mathcal{L}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WL}}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SL}}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{esc}}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$}).

(30)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SL}}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})
represents the contribution from strong-lensing constraints.

The corresponding log-likelihoods, up to constant terms, are given by

\displaystyle\ln\mathcal{L}_{g}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{WL}}}\frac{[\langle g_{+,i}\rangle-\widehat{g}_{+,i}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})]^{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{+,i}},

(31)

\displaystyle\ln\mathcal{L}_{\mu}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{WL}}}\frac{[\langle n_{\mu,i}\rangle-\widehat{n}_{\mu,i}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})]^{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{\mu,i}},

(32)

\displaystyle\ln\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SL}}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{SL}}}\frac{[M_{\mathrm{ap},i}-\widehat{M}_{\mathrm{ap},i}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})]^{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{ap},i}},

(33)

\displaystyle\ln\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{esc}}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{esc}}}\frac{[\mathcal{A}_{i}-\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$})]^{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{\mathcal{A},i}},

(34)

where
\widehat{g}_{+}
,
\widehat{n}_{\mu}
,
\widehat{M}_{\mathrm{ap}}
, and
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
are the theoretical predictions for the respective observables, each determined by the model parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$m$},\mbox{\boldmath$c$}\}
. A detailed description of these estimators is provided in Appendix
A.1
.

The uncertainty
\sigma_{+,i}
for
\langle g_{+,i}\rangle
primarily arises from intrinsic ellipticity variance and shape measurement errrors
(
Umetsu 2020
)
. The uncertainty
\sigma_{\mu,i}
for
\langle n_{\mu,i}\rangle
accounts for both Poisson counting errors and variance due to the intrinsic clustering of the source population
(
Umetsu et al. 2022
)
. The strong-lensing aperture mass estimates
M_{\mathrm{ap},i}
and their uncertainties
\sigma_{\mathrm{ap},i}
are derived from detailed modeling of the deflection field,
\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}\psi(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
, based on sets of multiply lensed images
(
Umetsu et al. 2016
)
.

Additionally, we extract measurements of the projected escape velocity,
\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{esc}}}
, from the caustic amplitude profile
\mathcal{A}(r_{\perp})
at
r_{\perp}>R_{\mathrm{inf}}=2\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
, as identified in redshift diagrams using the caustic technique. To minimize covariance between radial bins, we sample data points
\mathcal{A}_{i}
at uniform intervals along the
r_{\perp}
axis, setting the interval to match the median radial smoothing length
\overline{h}_{r}
of the adaptive kernel used to construct the continuous phase-space density distribution
(
Pizzardo et al. 2023
)
. Crucially, we account for a 20
\%
scatter due to projection effects by incorporating it in quadrature into the uncertainty
\sigma_{\mathcal{A},i}
as this contribution often dominates the total error budget
(
Serra et al. 2011
)
.

Finally, we note that the
clumi+
framework, like
clumi
, is readily extensible to ensemble cluster analyses, enabling joint modeling of stacked weak-lensing and phase-space profiles for statistical samples of galaxy clusters.

IV.5
Priors

clumi+
adopts uninformative, uniform priors for all model parameters. This choice ensures that the inference is driven primarily by the data, with minimal reliance on prior assumptions. As a result, the model remains flexible and robust, provided that the input data vectors used in the joint analysis are themselves unbiased.

The uniform priors for the mass model parameters
m
are specified as follows:

\displaystyle\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\theta_{\mathrm{min}})

\displaystyle\in[0,5],

(35)

\displaystyle\kappa_{\infty}(\theta_{i})

\displaystyle\in\begin{cases}[0,5],&\text{for }\theta_{i}\in[\theta_{\mathrm{min}}^{(\mathrm{SL})},\theta_{\mathrm{max}}^{(\mathrm{SL})}],\\
[0,1],&\text{for }\theta_{i}\in[\theta_{\mathrm{min}}^{(\mathrm{WL})},\theta_{\mathrm{max}}^{(\mathrm{WL})}].\end{cases}

\displaystyle\kappa_{\infty,\mathrm{ext}}

\displaystyle\in[0,1],

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle\in[0,2],

\displaystyle{\cal G}

\displaystyle\in[2,15],

where
clumi+
assumes that weak-lensing measurements
\{\langle g_{+,i}\rangle,\langle n_{\mu,i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{WL}}}
are obtained in subcritical regions with
\kappa_{\infty}(\theta)<1
(Section
II
). The upper prior bound of
5
on the central convergence parameter and the piecewise-defined convergence parameters in the strong-lensing regime is chosen to conservatively encompass the range expected for cluster-scale lenses
(
Merten et al. 2015
, e.g.,)
.

A uniform prior on the depletion factor
\mathcal{G}
is mathematically natural and optimal for unbiased inference of the gravitational potential, since
-2\Phi_{\mathrm{N}}=\mathcal{G}\mathcal{A}^{2}
under the assumption that
\mathcal{A}(r_{\perp})
is accurately measured. The prior range adopted for
\mathcal{G}
spans a factor of
7.5
in gravitational potential magnitude at fixed
\mathcal{A}
, thereby allowing for a broad range of possible line-of-sight mass contributions.

In the geometric interpretation of
\mathcal{G}
as a correction for orbital velocity anisotropy (Section
III
), this prior maps nonlinearly to a range in the velocity anisotropy parameter,
\beta_{v}

(
Wojtak et al. 2009
)
, extending from
-\infty
to
\approx 0.92
. This range broadly encompasses the spectrum of orbital configurations expected for cluster-scale halos
(
Serra et al. 2011
;
Lemze et al. 2012
;
He et al. 2024
)
, ensuring that the prior on
\mathcal{G}
remains sufficiently unrestrictive for empirical inference.

Additionally, we marginalize over the calibration parameters
c
using uniform priors, thereby propagating uncertainties associated with the observational characterization of source galaxy populations in shear and magnification measurements (Section
IV.3
).

IV.6
Implementation

We implement
clumi+
using a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) approach with Metropolis–Hastings sampling and convergence diagnostics, following
Umetsu et al. 2011
. As summary statistics for each model parameter, we adopt the biweight estimator of
Beers et al. 1990
to characterize the central location of marginalized one-dimensional (1D) posterior distributions
(
Sereno & Umetsu 2011
)
.

Biweight statistics offer robustness against outliers by assigning higher weights to data points closer to the central location of the distribution, thereby reducing the influence of extreme values
(
Beers et al. 1990
)
. The biweight center provides a reliable measure of central tendency, particularly for skewed or non-Gaussian distributions. Unlike the maximum a posteriori estimator, which corresponds to the mode, the biweight center approximates the median in the presence of asymmetry, as seen in lognormal-like posteriors typical of low signal-to-noise lensing bins (Section
V.4
).

The statistical covariance matrix
C^{\mathrm{stat}}
for the piecewise-defined convergence parameters (Equation
20
) is estimated from the posterior MCMC samples as:

\left(C^{\mathrm{stat}}\right)_{ij}=\mathrm{Cov}(s_{i},s_{j})=\langle(s_{i}-\langle s_{i}\rangle)(s_{j}-\langle s_{j}\rangle)\rangle.

(36)

V
Validating CLUMI+ with Synthetic Observations

We validate
clumi+
using synthetic observations of a massive galaxy cluster extracted from the IllustrisTNG suite of cosmological hydrodynamical simulations
(
Pillepich et al. 2018
;
Springel et al. 2018
;
Nelson et al. 2019
)
. The goal is to assess the robustness of the
clumi+
framework under realistic cluster conditions, including triaxiality, substructure, and projection effects.

Specifically, the validation tests examine whether the algorithm can recover unbiased estimates of the projected surface mass density
\Sigma(r_{\perp})
and the 3D enclosed mass profile
M(<r)
over the radial domain probed by multi-probe data, using synthetic weak-lensing and phase-space observables derived from the simulation. In
clumi+
, escape velocity information is incorporated under three working assumptions (Sections
III
and
IV
):

1.

a Newtonian interpretation of the escape velocity;

2.

constancy of the caustic depletion factor,
\mathcal{G}
, in the infall region (
r>R_{\mathrm{inf}}
);

3.

spherical symmetry of the 3D mass distribution in the infall region (
r>R_{\mathrm{inf}}
),

where we adopt
R_{\mathrm{inf}}=2\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
throughout this study.

Although these assumptions are not tested independently, accurate recovery of both projected and three-dimensional mass profiles provides empirical validation of the
clumi+
framework under realistic conditions.

;

)
is a set of gravo-magnetohydrodynamical simulations based on the

z=127

(

\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.6911
, matter density
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=0.3089

302.6

clumi+

M_{200\mathrm{c}}=3.57\times 10^{14}\,h^{-1}\,M_{\odot}
at a redshift of
z_{\mathrm{cl}}=0.21
(FOF ID #3). This halo serves as an ideal test case, enabling a clear evaluation of
clumi+
performance with minimal external complexities.

Analyses were performed along three orthogonal projections (
X
,
Y
, and
Z

.

V.2
Synthetic Weak Lensing Observations

V.2.1
Projected Mass Maps

We generate a 2D projected mass map for each of the three orthogonal projections of the TNG300-1 cluster. To construct each synthetic mass map, we project the 3D positions of the matter elements along the corresponding projection axis and sum their masses in 2D bins of celestial coordinates. Below, we outline our procedure in detail.

We define a cubic volume centered on the cluster, with a side length of 20 Mpc (
\simeq 24.2
comoving Mpc) at
z_{\mathrm{cl}}=0.21
. From the raw simulation data, we extract the 3D coordinates of all matter elements within this volume, including all four matter species implemented in TNG300-1. Each set of 3D coordinates is then projected along a selected axis. We define a reference frame in which the observer is positioned along the projection axis, with the cluster center as the origin. The original comoving 3D coordinates of the matter elements,
r_{\mathrm{cl},i}
, are measured relative to the cluster center.

The comoving distance between the cluster center and the observer is given by

r_{\mathrm{cl}}=\frac{c}{H_{0}}\int_{0}^{z_{\mathrm{cl}}}\!\frac{dz}{E(z)},

(37)

where
E(z)=\left[\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}(1+z)^{3}+\Omega_{\Lambda}\right]^{1/2}
is the dimensionless expansion rate in a flat
\Lambda
CDM cosmology.

The transformed 3D positions are then given by

\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\mathrm{cl}}+\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\mathrm{cl},i},

(38)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\mathrm{cl}}
is directed along the projection axis and connects the observer to the cluster center. Setting the celestial coordinates of the cluster center to
(\alpha_{\mathrm{cl}},\delta_{\mathrm{cl}})=(\pi/2,0)

V.5
Precision Gain from Joint
clumi+
Analysis

One of the principal advantages of
clumi+
is its improved precision in mass reconstruction, achieved by incorporating escape velocity information from projected phase-space observations. To demonstrate this, we performed a lensing-only
clumi
analysis of the synthetic weak-lensing data,
\{\langle g_{+,i}\rangle,\langle n_{\mu,i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{WL}}}
, for the TNG300-1 cluster and quantified the resulting gains in the precision of the piecewise-defined convergence parameters,
\mbox{\boldmath$s$}=\{\kappa_{\infty,\mathrm{min}},\kappa_{\infty,i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

We define the precision gain as the ratio of inverse posterior uncertainties,
\sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{WL+esc}}/\sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{WL}}
, where
\sigma_{\mathrm{WL+esc}}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{WL}}
denote the marginalized posterior standard deviations from the joint
clumi+
and lensing-only
clumi
analyses, respectively. This metric quantifies the statistical improvement achieved by incorporating projected escape velocity measurements into the mass reconstruction.

The results are shown in Figure
8
. On average, we find a
10\%
–
30\%
improvement in precision in the outer regions where the weak-lensing and phase-space data sets overlap (
r_{\perp}>2\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
). Notably, within this overlapping regime, the level of precision gain increases with cluster-centric radius, reflecting the growing relative constraining power of the escape velocity measurements at larger scales. This trend suggests that broader radial overlap between weak-lensing and spectroscopic data will yield even greater precision gains, underscoring the potential of the
clumi+
framework for future wide-field survey applications.

We also find that the magnitude of the precision gain is sensitive to projection effects, which can induce discrepancies between weak-lensing and escape velocity constraints. In the
Y
projection, for example, two massive substructures infalling along the line of sight and located outside the main halo produce elevated caustic amplitudes (see Section
V.3.2
). In particular, a sharp spike in the caustic amplitude around
r_{\perp}\sim 2.3\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
is not well described by the smoothly varying gravitational potential
\Phi_{\mathrm{N}}(r)
assumed in the model. As a result, the precision gain is locally suppressed in radial regions where the lensing and dynamical constraints are in tension (compare Figures
3
and
8
), illustrating the impact of projection effects on the fidelity of mass reconstruction.

We emphasize that chance alignments involving line-of-sight infall or cluster–cluster interactions may reduce the statistical gains achievable from joint modeling, but they do not introduce bias into the reconstructed mass profile. On the contrary, localized discrepancies between lensing and dynamical observables can serve as diagnostics for identifying projection effects associated with line-of-sight structure. The
clumi+
framework remains robust in such scenarios, owing to its reliance on complementary, physically distinct probes of the gravitational potential.

VI
Application to Cluster Observations: Abell 2261

In this section, we apply the
clumi+
algorithm to multi-probe observations of the galaxy cluster Abell 2261 (hereafter A2261) at
z=0.225
, a well-studied, massive system targeted by both the CLASH program
(
Postman et al. 2012
)
and the Hectospec Cluster Survey
(
Rines et al. 2013
, HeCS;)
. Specifically, we combine the CLASH weak- and strong-lensing data products from
Umetsu et al. 2016
with projected escape velocity constraints derived from HeCS spectroscopic observations presented by
Pizzardo et al. 2021
(see also
Rines et al. 2013
). We note that strong-lensing constraints were not included in the simulation-based validation presented in Section
V
, but are incorporated here for completeness in the analysis of real observational data.

A2261 is part of the X-ray-selected CLASH subsample, which comprises 20 clusters with X-ray temperatures exceeding 5 keV and exhibiting a high degree of regularity in their X-ray morphology. The cluster has an effective Einstein radius of
\theta_{\mathrm{Ein}}=23.1\arcsec
for sources at
z_{s}=2

(
Zitrin et al. 2015
;
Umetsu et al. 2016
)
. Its mass has been estimated as
M_{500\mathrm{c}}=(11.0\pm 2.1)\times 10^{14}\,h^{-1}\,M_{\odot}
from a joint weak- and strong-lensing analysis assuming a spherical NFW profile
(
Umetsu et al. 2016
)
(see also
Coe et al. 2012
). A2261 is also identified as a massive SZ cluster detected by the
Planck
satellite, with a mass estimate of
M_{500\mathrm{c,SZ}}=(5.5\pm 0.2)\times 10^{14}\,h^{-1}\,M_{\odot}
based on the
Planck
SZ scaling relation
(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2014
)
, which is calibrated using X-ray observations and hydrodynamical simulations under the assumption of hydrostatic equilibrium.

The substantial discrepancy between the lensing and SZ mass estimates suggests that A2261 is likely not in dynamical equilibrium. This highlights the importance of employing multi-probe approaches that do not rely on equilibrium assumptions for accurate mass reconstruction.

VI.1.2
Hectospec Spectroscopic Data

For the phase-space analysis, we utilize galaxy redshifts from HeCS
(
Rines et al. 2013
;
Sohn et al. 2021
)
, which provides dense spectroscopic sampling of galaxies around galaxy clusters, ideally suited for applications of the caustic technique. HeCS leverages archival data from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) and the
ROSAT
All-Sky Survey (RASS). Specifically, X-ray cluster catalogs derived from RASS were used to define flux-limited cluster samples, which were subsequently matched to the imaging footprint of SDSS Data Release 6
(
Adelman-McCarthy et al. 2006
, DR6;)
. The multiband SDSS photometry enabled the identification of candidate cluster members using the red-sequence technique.
To obtain reliable redshift measurements beyond
z\sim 0.1
where SDSS spectroscopic sampling becomes sparse, HeCS employed the Hectospec multi-object spectrograph
(
Fabricant et al. 2005
)
on the MMT 6.5 m telescope to obtain dedicated spectroscopic observations of cluster-associated galaxies.

Pizzardo et al. 2021
present a caustic analysis for the HeCS cluster catalog using the method implemented by
Serra et al. 2011
, which refines the original algorithm developed by
Diaferio 1999
. The updated caustic profiles show good agreement with those reported by
Rines et al. 2013
, with differences well within the

VI.3
Discussion

The application of
clumi+
to A2261 demonstrates the effectiveness of combining weak and strong lensing with projected phase-space information for cluster mass reconstruction. The resulting mass profile is consistent with previous CLASH analyses while achieving improved precision in the outer regions, where lensing constraints alone become increasingly limited. This case study highlights the practical utility of
clumi+
for joint modeling based on complementary observables.

Since the Subaru lensing aperture is limited to
D_{l}\theta_{\mathrm{max}}\approx 2.4\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
, which is comparable to the adopted boundary of the infall region,
R_{\mathrm{inf}}=2\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
, there is minimal radial overlap between the lensing and dynamical data sets. Nevertheless, the addition of projected escape velocity measurements, extending out to
r_{\perp}\sim 4.8\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
, yields improvements in the precision of the piecewise-defined convergence parameters,
\mbox{\boldmath$s$}=\{\kappa_{\infty,\mathrm{min}},\kappa_{\infty,i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, particularly at large radii. In the outer regions (
r_{\perp}\gtrsim 1\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
), we find precision gains of
\sim 20\%
(Figure
11
). Notably, the lensing and phase-space constraints exhibit good mutual consistency with the joint solution inferred from
clumi+
(see Figures
15
and
16
in Appendix
C
), consistent with the validation results from the IllustrisTNG cluster simulations.

Although the inclusion of projected escape velocity constraints yields clear improvements in the precision of the mass profile at large radii (Figure
11
), we find that the inferred NFW parameters,
M_{200\mathrm{c}}
and
c_{200\mathrm{c}}
, are statistically consistent in precision values with those obtained by
Umetsu et al. 2016
using strong and weak lensing alone. This behavior is expected, since the NFW parameters are primarily sensitive to the density structure within the halo, whereas the precision gains from
clumi+
occur predominantly in the outer and infall regions. These findings underscore the complementarity of lensing and phase-space information and highlight the specific utility of
clumi+
in probing outer density structure beyond the virial radius.

In particular, such precision gains in the outskirts are especially valuable for constraining the steepening feature in the radial density profile associated with the splashback radius,
R_{\mathrm{sp}}
, as probed by cluster–galaxy weak lensing
(
Umetsu & Diemer 2017
;
Chang et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
. The splashback radius marks a physically motivated boundary of the cluster halo, corresponding to the location where recently accreted matter reaches its first apocenter, giving rise to a sharp drop in the density profile
(
Diemer & Kravtsov 2014
;
More et al. 2015
)
. Resolving this feature with high fidelity requires precise measurements over a broad radial range extending from the halo region into the infall regime. In this context,
clumi+
offers a promising avenue for detecting and characterizing splashback features in ensemble cluster analyses, by harnessing the combined constraining power of stacked lensing and phase-space observables across the full dynamical range of the cluster environment.

For A2261, as well as for all three projections of the TNG300-1 cluster, we find consistently high inferred values of the caustic depletion factor in the infall region, with
\mathcal{G}\gtrsim 10
(Figures
5
and
10
). In the geometric interpretation of the caustic technique (Section
III
), such values correspond to strongly radial orbital anisotropy, with velocity anisotropy parameters
\beta_{v}\gtrsim 0.88
, or equivalently
\sqrt{\langle v_{\theta}^{2}\rangle/\langle v_{r}^{2}\rangle}\lesssim 0.35
. These results may reflect genuine dynamical features in the cluster outskirts, where infall and radial streaming motions dominate the tracer population.

Alternatively, this trend may indicate a mild systematic overestimation of the 3D escape velocity,
v_{\mathrm{esc}}
, at fixed projected amplitude
\mathcal{A}
, due to the adoption of the Newtonian relation
v_{\mathrm{esc}}(r)=\sqrt{-2\Phi_{\mathrm{N}}(r)}
, which neglects the effects of cosmic expansion and acceleration
(
Miller et al. 2016
;
Rodriguez et al. 2024
;
Benisty et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. In this context, future extensions of the
clumi+
framework that incorporate these effects may help disentangle such contributions and refine the interpretation of phase-space observables in the infall regime.

VII
Summary and Conclusions

We have presented
clumi+
, a self-consistent, multi-probe framework for reconstructing the mass distribution in and around galaxy clusters by combining gravitational lensing and dynamics (Sections
II
and
III
). In particular,
clumi+
highlights the power of combining redshift surveys with wide-field weak-lensing data to probe the cluster mass distribution. Building on the joint-likelihood formalism of
Umetsu 2013
,
clumi+
integrates lensing profiles with projected escape velocity measurements in cluster outer regions. This approach enables constraints on the gravitational potential over a wide radial range—from the cluster core to the infall regions—without relying on equilibrium assumptions.

To achieve this broad radial coverage,
clumi+
incorporates strong-lensing aperture-mass constraints in the central region, weak-lensing shear and magnification profiles at larger radii, and projected escape velocity measurements in the outer infall regime (Section
IV
). The mass distribution is modeled using a flexible, piecewise-defined convergence profile within the lensing aperture, which transitions to a projected power-law form at larger radii. This hybrid prescription enables a self-consistent integration of observables across distinct physical regimes. Importantly,
clumi+
breaks both the mass-sheet degeneracy in lensing (
\lambda=1-\kappa_{0}
; Section
II.1
) and the velocity-scaling degeneracy in phase-space analysis (
\mathcal{G}
; Section
III.1
) by combining physically distinct tracers of the gravitational potential.

The
clumi+
framework is readily extensible to ensemble cluster analyses, enabling joint modeling of stacked weak-lensing and phase-space profiles for statistical samples of galaxy clusters. This capability makes it well suited for precision studies of outer halo structure, dynamical boundary features such as the splashback radius (Section
VI.3
), and population-level trends in the mass distribution of clusters.

We validated
clumi+
using synthetic weak-lensing and phase-space observations of a massive cluster drawn from the IllustrisTNG simulation suite (Section
V
). The reconstructed mass profiles closely match the true simulation inputs across all three orthogonal projections, both in two and three dimensions, demonstrating robust performance even in the presence of triaxiality and projection effects. In the infall regions (
r_{\perp}>R_{\mathrm{inf}}=2\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
), where escape velocity data are incorporated, we achieved precision gains of
10\%
–
30\%
relative to lensing-only reconstructions (Section
V.5
).

Within the radial regime where weak-lensing and phase-space constraints overlap, the precision gain increases with radius, suggesting that broader radial overlap between the two data sets will further enhance the statistical power of the joint analysis. This highlights the importance of coordinated spectroscopic and imaging surveys with overlapping coverage to fully exploit the capabilities of the
clumi+
framework in future wide-field applications.

Furthermore, the framework demonstrates robustness against projection effects that can impact the identification of caustic boundaries, particularly those caused by chance alignments of line-of-sight substructures (Section
V.5
). While such configurations may locally reduce statistical precision, they do not bias the inferred mass profiles. This robustness arises from the use of complementary, physically distinct probes of the gravitational potential, which respond differently to line-of-sight contamination. As a result, discrepancies between lensing and dynamical tracers can serve as diagnostics of complex line-of-sight structure rather than sources of systematic error.

As a first application to real data, we analyzed the galaxy cluster Abell 2261 at
z=0.225
, combining Subaru and
HST
weak+strong lensing data with HeCS spectroscopic measurements (Section
VI
). The reconstructed profile spans
r_{\perp}\in[30,4000]\,\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{kpc}
and is consistent with previous CLASH lensing-only results, while achieving improved precision in the outer regions. These results highlight the utility of
clumi+
in systems where equilibrium assumptions may not apply.

In summary,
clumi+
provides a flexible, data-driven framework for mapping the mass distribution of galaxy clusters across a wide range of physical scales, achieving improved precision with minimal modeling assumptions. Its ability to integrate lensing and dynamical information makes
clumi+
particularly well suited for cosmological applications in the era of wide-field imaging and spectroscopic surveys—such as DESI
(
Collaboration et al. 2016
)
, Subaru PFS
(
Takada et al. 2014
)
, DESI II
(
Schlegel et al. 2022
)
, LSST
(
Ivezić et al. 2019
)
,
Euclid

(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2024
)
, and the
Roman
High Latitude Survey
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
—where accurate, unbiased mass calibration across large cluster samples is essential for precision cosmology
(
Tam et al. 2022
)
.

In future work, we plan to extend the
clumi+
framework to incorporate the effects of cosmic expansion acceleration in modeling escape velocities, thereby enhancing its applicability to accurate reconstructions of phase-space structure in the cluster outskirts and infall regions.

Appendix A
Cluster Mass Model

This appendix details the hybrid mass model implemented in
clumi+
(Section
IV.2
), which combines a piecewise-defined convergence profile within the lensing aperture with an external power-law component to characterize the projected mass distribution at larger radii.

A.1
Discretized Expressions for Cluster Lensing Profiles

We derive discretized expressions for the lensing observables used in this work (Section
II
), based on the azimuthally averaged convergence profile
\kappa_{\infty}(\theta)
, modeled as a piecewise-constant function across radial bins.

Let
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N+1}
denote a set of
(N+1)
concentric radii spanning the full range
\theta\in[\theta_{1},\theta_{N+1}]\equiv[\theta_{\mathrm{min}},\theta_{\mathrm{max}}]
, dividing the radial domain into
N=N_{\mathrm{SL}}+N_{\mathrm{WL}}
annular bins. The average convergence within radius
\theta_{i}
is then given by the discretized estimator:

\displaystyle\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{min}}}{\theta_{i}}\right)^{2}\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\theta_{\mathrm{min}})

(A1)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{\theta_{i}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\Delta\ln\theta_{j}\,\overline{\theta}_{j}^{2}\,\kappa_{\infty}(\overline{\theta}_{j}),

where
\Delta\ln\theta_{j}=(\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j})/\overline{\theta}_{j}
, and
\overline{\theta}_{j}
denotes the area-weighted center of the
j
th bin in
[\theta_{j},\theta_{j+1}]
.

In the strong-lensing regime, the constraints are provided in the form of aperture masses
M_{\mathrm{ap}}(<\theta_{i})
at
N_{\mathrm{SL}}
inner radii
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{SL}}}
:

M_{\mathrm{ap}}(<\theta_{i})=\pi(D_{l}\theta_{i})^{2}\Sigma_{\mathrm{cr},\infty}\,\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\theta_{i}),

(A2)

where
\Sigma_{\mathrm{cr},\infty}=c^{2}/(4\pi GD_{l}\beta_{\infty})
is the far-background critical surface mass density for lensing (Section
II.2
).

For the weak-lensing regime (
i=N_{\mathrm{SL}}+1,\dots,N
), the observables are azimuthally averaged in annular bins centered at
\overline{\theta}_{i}
. The discretized forms of the tangential reduced shear and inverse magnification are:

\displaystyle\langle g_{+}\rangle(\overline{\theta}_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle W\rangle_{g}\left[\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\overline{\theta}_{i})-\kappa_{\infty}(\overline{\theta}_{i})\right]}{1-f_{g}\langle W\rangle_{g}\kappa_{\infty}(\overline{\theta}_{i})},

(A3)

\displaystyle\langle\mu^{-1}\rangle(\overline{\theta}_{i})

\displaystyle=\left[1-\langle W\rangle_{\mu}\kappa_{\infty}(\overline{\theta}_{i})\right]^{2}

\displaystyle-\langle W\rangle_{\mu}^{2}\left[\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\overline{\theta}_{i})-\kappa_{\infty}(\overline{\theta}_{i})\right]^{2}.

Under the piecewise-constant assumption, the interior average
\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\overline{\theta}_{i})
at the center of the
i
th radial bin is given by

\displaystyle\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\overline{\theta}_{i})=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\theta_{i}}{\overline{\theta}_{i}}\right)^{2}\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\theta_{i})

(A4)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\theta_{i+1}}{\overline{\theta}_{i}}\right)^{2}\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\theta_{i+1})\Bigg],

with
\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\theta_{i})
and
\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\theta_{i+1})
computed using Equation (
A1
).

All lensing observables in
clumi+
(Section
II
) are thus uniquely determined by a set of
N+1
piecewise-defined convergence parameters:

\mbox{\boldmath$s$}=\left\{\kappa_{\infty,\mathrm{min}},\kappa_{\infty,i}\right\}_{i=1}^{N},

(A5)

where
\kappa_{\infty,\mathrm{min}}\equiv\kappa_{\infty}(<\theta_{\mathrm{min}})
represents the central convergence interior to
\theta_{\mathrm{min}}
, and
\kappa_{\infty,i}\equiv\kappa_{\infty}(\overline{\theta}_{i})
denotes the convergence in each radial bin.

A.2
Predicting the Escape Velocity

Including the external convergence component defined in Equation (
22
), the full radial convergence profile
\kappa_{\infty}(\theta)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\kappa_{\infty}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\overline{\kappa}_{\infty,\mathrm{min}}\,\Theta(\theta_{\mathrm{min}}-\theta)

(A6)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\kappa_{\infty,i}\,\Theta(\theta-\theta_{i},\theta_{i+1}-\theta)

\displaystyle+\kappa_{\infty,\mathrm{ext}}\,\Theta(\theta-\theta_{\mathrm{max}})\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta_{\mathrm{max}}}\right)^{-q},

where
\Theta(x)
denotes the Heaviside step function, and
\Theta(x,y)\equiv\Theta(x)\Theta(y)
restricts support to the interval
[\theta_{i},\theta_{i+1})
.
The first term represents the average convergence within the innermost aperture (
\theta<\theta_{\mathrm{min}}
), parameterized by
\overline{\kappa}_{\infty,\mathrm{min}}
. The second term describes the piecewise-constant convergence profile over the lensing annuli
\theta\in[\theta_{\mathrm{min}},\theta_{\mathrm{max}}]
, with bin-averaged values
\kappa_{\infty,i}
. The final term models the projected mass distribution outside the lensing aperture (
\theta\geqslant\theta_{\mathrm{max}}
) as a power-law profile with normalization
\kappa_{\infty,\mathrm{ext}}
and logarithmic slope
q
.

By construction, the external convergence term contributes only for
\theta>\theta_{\mathrm{max}}
and does not affect lensing observables within the lensing field. To constrain this outer component, complementary information beyond lensing is required. Within the
clumi+
framework, projected escape velocity measurements at
r_{\perp}>R_{\mathrm{inf}}
provide such constraints by probing the gravitational potential in the cluster outskirts.

For
\theta\geqslant\theta_{\mathrm{max}}
, the average convergence interior to
\theta
is given by

\displaystyle\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\theta)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{max}}}{\theta}\right)^{2}\Bigg[\overline{\kappa}_{\infty}(<\theta_{\mathrm{max}})

(A7)

\displaystyle+\frac{2\kappa_{\infty,\mathrm{ext}}}{2-q}\left(\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta_{\mathrm{max}}}\right)^{2-q}-1\right)\Bigg],

where we assume
0<q<2
to ensure convergence of the potential integral. The corresponding projected aperture mass,
M_{\mathrm{ap}}(<\theta)
, is computed using Equation (
A2
).

The spherically enclosed mass profile
M(<r)
is obtained via the Abel integral transform (Equation (
25
)), and the Newtonian gravitational potential
\Phi_{\mathrm{N}}(r)
is then computed from Equation (
24
).

Given a set of model parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$m$}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$s$},\mathcal{G},\kappa_{\infty,\mathrm{ext}},q\}
, the projected escape velocity profile as a function of
r_{\perp}=D_{l}\theta
is given by

\mathcal{A}(r_{\perp})=\sqrt{\frac{-2\Phi_{\mathrm{N}}(r_{\perp})}{\mathcal{G}}},

(A8)

where
\mathcal{G}
is the depletion factor relating the Newtonian escape velocity to the observable caustic amplitude (Section
III
).
