Association between body mass index percentile and biological maturation: dental and skeletal age in an orthodontic population
Authors: Saadet Cinarsoy Cigerim, Turkan Sezen Erhamza, Kubra Arslan Carpar, Seda Kotan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the relationship between body mass index (BMI) percentile and biological maturation indicators, including skeletal age, dental age, and tooth development, in children from two distinct regions of Türkiye.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included 1011 orthodontic patients aged 6–18 years from Eastern Anatolia (n = 582) and Central Anatolia (n = 429). BMI percentiles were calculated using age- and sex-specific reference values and categorized as underweight, normal weight, overweight, or obese. Skeletal maturation was assessed using the Björk–Grave–Brown hand-wrist method and cervical vertebral maturation staging, while dental age was estimated using Demirjian’s method with Williams scoring. Statistical analyses included independent samples t-tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, one-way ANOVA, and linear regression.ResultsChildren from Eastern Anatolia exhibited higher BMI percentiles, whereas those from Central Anatolia demonstrated significantly greater skeletal and dental ages. Girls showed higher chronological and skeletal ages than boys, while dental age and BMI percentile did not differ significantly by sex. Significant differences in skeletal age–chronological age discrepancy across BMI categories were observed in girls but not boys. Regression analysis revealed significant negative associations between BMI percentile and biological maturation indicators in both sexes.ConclusionHigher BMI percentiles were associated with delayed skeletal and dental maturation. Regional differences suggest environmental and nutritional influences on growth, highlighting BMI as a relevant adjunct parameter in orthodontic growth assessment and treatment planning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08529-7.Keywords: Body mass index (BMI), Dental age, Skeletal age, Growth and development

Background

Orthodontics encompasses the evaluation and management of individuals from the prepubertal period through adulthood and is therefore closely associated with growth and development [1]. Accurate prediction and assessment of growth are essential in orthodontic treatment planning, as growth and developmental stages influence the timing of growth modification therapy, extraoral appliance use, extraction versus non-extraction decisions, retention strategies, and orthognathic surgery [2].

Growth and development are interrelated yet distinct biological processes. Growth refers to increases in body size due to cellular proliferation and enlargement, whereas development involves the functional maturation of tissues and organs. Both processes are influenced by genetic and environmental factors [3, 4]. Concrete examples of factors that affect growth and development include hormonal regulators such as growth hormone, insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), thyroid hormones, and sex steroids, all of which play pivotal roles during childhood and adolescence [3]. Environmental determinants such as chronic disease, psychological stress, physical activity, sleep quality, and socioeconomic conditions have also been shown to significantly modulate growth trajectories [4, 5]. For instance, growth hormone deficiency leads to short stature, whereas hyperthyroidism may accelerate skeletal maturation; similarly, prolonged undernutrition causes stunted growth, while excessive adiposity may alter the timing of pubertal development [5, 6].

Among environmental determinants, nutritional status plays a particularly important role during childhood, and both overnutrition and malnutrition can affect growth trajectories [5, 6]. Consequently, biological age is often considered more informative than chronological age when evaluating individual growth and development [7]. Indicators such as skeletal age, secondary sexual characteristics, anthropometric changes, and dental maturation are commonly used to estimate biological age [8].

Body mass index (BMI) is a widely accepted measure for classifying underweight, overweight, and obesity, calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m²) [9, 10]. In pediatric populations, BMI must be interpreted using age- and sex-specific percentile curves because of dynamic growth patterns [11, 12]. Skeletal maturation is traditionally assessed using hand-wrist radiographs [13, 14], whereas cervical vertebral maturation evaluation on lateral cephalometric radiographs has been proposed as an alternative to reduce additional radiation exposure [15].

Dental maturation, assessed by eruption timing or calcification stages, is another reliable indicator of biological age [16]. Notably, several systemic and environmental factors are known to interfere with dental development and calcification stages. Hormonal imbalances, including hypothyroidism and growth hormone deficiency, have been associated with delayed tooth formation, while precocious puberty and hyperadrenalism may accelerate dental development [16, 17]. Nutritional deficiencies, particularly in vitamins D and C, calcium, and phosphorus, impair enamel and dentin formation, thereby affecting calcification stages [17, 18]. Furthermore, chronic systemic diseases, low birth weight, and premature birth have been linked to enamel hypoplasia and delayed dental maturation [18, 19]. Unlike tooth eruption, which is more susceptible to local environmental influences such as loss of primary teeth and arch crowding, calcification stages are considered more stable and thus frequently used as complementary indicators in growth assessment [16, 19, 20].

Previous studies examining the association between BMI and biological maturation have produced conflicting results [21–23]. Although several reports indicate accelerated skeletal and dental maturation in overweight and obese children, these findings may vary by population characteristics, nutritional quality, and environmental factors [21, 22].

Additionally, regional disparities in socioeconomic conditions and dietary patterns may influence the relationship between BMI and biological maturation [23]. Further evidence from diverse ethnic and geographic cohorts has demonstrated considerable heterogeneity, suggesting that this association is population-dependent and may be modulated by hormonal and nutritional mediators [24, 25].

Moreover, limited evidence exists on the simultaneous evaluation of dental age, hand-wrist skeletal maturation, and cervical vertebral maturation in large orthodontic populations from different geographic regions. In the relevant group where accurate growth assessment is critical, further investigation is warranted.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to evaluate the association between BMI percentile and biological maturation indicators, including skeletal and dental age, among children in two distinct regions of Türkiye.

Methods

This retrospective cross-sectional study included 1011 individuals aged 6–18 years who presented to the Departments of Orthodontics at Van Yuzuncu Yil University Faculty of Dentistry and Kirikkale University Faculty of Dentistry between 2018 and 2022. The sample comprised 582 children from Van (Eastern Anatolia; Group 1) and 429 from Kirikkale (Central Anatolia; Group 2). Because the study population consisted of children seeking orthodontic treatment, the sample represents a clinically relevant orthodontic cohort rather than the general pediatric population. Routinely obtained lateral cephalometric, panoramic, and hand-wrist radiographs, taken for diagnostic and treatment planning purposes prior to orthodontic treatment, were used in the study, ensuring that no additional radiation exposure was required.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Van Yuzuncu Yil University Faculty of Medicine Ethics Committee (Decision no: 2019/06-02), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was based on retrospective analysis of clinical records and radiographs obtained during routine orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning. Therefore, additional informed consent to participate was not required.

Radiographs obtained on the same day were included to ensure methodological consistency. Patients were excluded for chronic systemic disease, endocrine disorder, or malnutrition affecting growth and development; impacted, transposed, or congenitally missing teeth; previous permanent tooth extraction; craniofacial anomalies or syndromes; congenital or acquired malformations of the hand-wrist, dentomaxillofacial, or cervical vertebral regions; radiographic artifacts that impaired evaluation; or a history of trauma involving the hand-wrist, cervical, or craniofacial regions.

Chronological age, height, and weight were obtained from anamnesis records. Height and weight measurements were recorded at the time of the initial orthodontic examination using standardized procedures. Height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a wall-mounted stadiometer (Seca 217, Seca GmbH, Hamburg, Germany) with patients in the Frankfort horizontal plane, without shoes. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a calibrated digital scale (Seca 813, Seca GmbH, Hamburg, Germany). All measurements were performed by a trained clinical assistant following a standardized protocol to minimize inter-session variability. BMI was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m²). Age- and sex-specific BMI percentiles were determined using reference values reported by Bundak et al. [26] for Turkish children (Figs. 1 and 2).

Skeletal age was determined using the Björk, Grave, and Brown (BGB) method (Fig. 3) on hand-wrist radiographs and the cervical vertebral maturation (CVM) method (Fig. 4) on lateral cephalometric radiographs. For dental age determination, seven teeth in the left mandibular quadrant were evaluated according to Demirjian’s tooth development stages, and these stages were scored using Williams’ scores. Dental age was calculated as the sum of the scores for the seven teeth (Fig. 5). Regarding participants older than 16 years, Demirjian stage H (root apex closure) was applied as the terminal stage for all applicable teeth, consistent with established practice in similar studies. As the Willems scoring system assigns maximum values corresponding to mature tooth formation, participants aged 16–18 years were evaluated with the understanding that dental age estimation may be subject to ceiling effects at the upper end of the age range. This limitation is acknowledged and discussed further in the Discussion section.

All radiographic assessments were performed on digital images displayed on a calibrated monitor (24-inch, 1920 × 1080 resolution) under standardized conditions (dimmed ambient lighting, consistent screen brightness and contrast settings). Magnification tools were used when necessary for precise landmark identification. All skeletal, cervical vertebral, and dental maturation assessments were performed by two calibrated orthodontic specialists (SCC and TSE) independently. Prior to the main assessment, both observers evaluated a randomly selected subset of 50 radiographs to establish calibration. Intra-observer reliability was assessed by re-evaluating 15% of the total sample (n = 152) by each observer after a minimum interval of two weeks. Inter-observer agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Intra-observer kappa values were 0.88 and 0.91 for observers 1 and 2, respectively, and the inter-observer kappa was 0.86, all indicating excellent agreement. ICC values for skeletal age were 0.93 (intra-observer) and 0.91 (inter-observer), and for dental age were 0.95 and 0.92, respectively, confirming high reliability of the assessments.

The age distribution of the study sample was as follows: 6–9 years (n = 142, 14.0%), 10–12 years (n = 274, 27.1%), 13–15 years (n = 388, 38.4%), and 16–18 years (n = 207, 20.5%). The distribution was not perfectly uniform across age groups, with a higher representation of participants in the 13–15 year age range, reflecting the typical age profile of patients seeking orthodontic treatment. This age distribution was taken into account when interpreting findings, particularly for older participants where dental age estimation methods may have ceiling effects.

Although a prospective sample size calculation was not performed due to the retrospective nature of the study, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.2. The analysis confirmed that the study was adequately powered (statistical power = 0.98) to detect the observed effect sizes at α = 0.05, supporting the sufficiency of the sample size for the primary regression analyses.

The study population consisted of children seeking orthodontic treatment; therefore, the sample reflects a clinically relevant orthodontic population rather than the general population. However, radiographs were obtained as part of routine diagnostic procedures before treatment, and the large sample size enabled a reliable evaluation of BMI–maturation relationships. This limitation was taken into account when interpreting the findings.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as the mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum for continuous variables, and as percentages for categorical variables. Normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test.

For normally distributed variables, independent samples t-tests were used to compare chronological, dental, and skeletal ages between sexes, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed variables, including BMI percentile values.

Comparisons of chronological, dental, and skeletal ages across BMI categories were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey post hoc tests. For non-normally distributed variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test with multiple-comparison procedures was applied.

Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square test. Linear regression was used to evaluate the effect of BMI percentile on biological maturation indicators, including dental age, skeletal age, and tooth development variables. Intra- and inter-observer reliability were quantified using Cohen’s kappa coefficient for ordinal staging data and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for continuous age variables, as described in the assessments subsection above.

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The present study shows that higher BMI percentiles are associated with delayed skeletal and dental maturation in both sexes. Notably, regional differences were observed: children from Eastern Anatolia had higher BMI percentiles but lower biological age indicators than those from Central Anatolia. These findings suggest that the relationship between BMI and biological maturation is not uniform across populations and is likely modulated by contextual and environmental factors.

Previous research has generally reported accelerated skeletal and dental maturation in overweight and obese children. For instance, Hilgers et al. and Zangouei et al. reported advanced dental development in children with higher BMI [22, 26], while Must et al. observed earlier tooth eruption in obese cohorts [27]. Similarly, Modesto et al. identified advanced dental age in overweight children, potentially mediated by hormonal factors such as FGF18 [28], and Mack et al. demonstrated a positive association between BMI percentile and both dental and cervical vertebral maturation (CVM) in an orthodontic population [23]. A recent case–control study likewise reported significantly higher dental ages in overweight and obese children compared with normal-weight peers [22]. However, evidence also indicates heterogeneity in this relationship, suggesting that BMI–maturation associations may be population-dependent and influenced by environmental and socioeconomic conditions [29, 30].

In contrast to the prevailing literature, the present study found a negative association between BMI percentile and biological maturation indicators. This divergence may reflect differences in population genetics, environmental exposures, or cohort-specific confounders. Factors such as body fat distribution, physical activity levels, and hormonal profiles may modulate biological responsiveness to adiposity differently across populations.

An important observation in the present study was the apparent non-linearity of the BMI–maturation relationship, particularly in girls. Underweight girls demonstrated higher skeletal and dental ages than normal-weight peers, while obese girls showed more advanced maturation than obese boys but not necessarily greater maturation than normal-weight girls. This pattern challenges a simple linear interpretation and warrants careful consideration. One plausible explanation relates to the differential hormonal profiles across BMI extremes: underweight girls may have elevated cortisol and altered hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activity, which can paradoxically accelerate skeletal maturation in the short term while impairing long-term growth [31, 32]. At the other extreme, obese girls may experience elevated estrogen levels derived from peripheral aromatization of androgens in adipose tissue, which is known to accelerate epiphyseal closure and skeletal maturation [33, 34]. This sex-specific endocrine divergence may explain why the pattern was observed in girls but not boys. These findings underscore the need to avoid interpreting the BMI–maturation relationship as strictly linear or unidirectional and instead to consider it within a broader, hormonally and nutritionally mediated framework.

Regional comparisons provided further insight into this heterogeneity. Although children from Eastern Anatolia had higher overall BMI percentiles, their skeletal and dental ages were consistently lower than those from Central Anatolia. Within-category analyses revealed cross-regional differences independent of BMI classification, suggesting that geographic and socioeconomic context modulates maturation. Crucially, because BMI reflects total body mass rather than nutritional adequacy, elevated BMI may coexist with micronutrient deficiencies that impair endochondral ossification and growth regulation. This phenomenon, often described as the ‘double burden of malnutrition,’ has been increasingly reported in middle-income populations and may contribute to delayed biological maturation despite higher BMI [31–33].

From a nutritional perspective, the Eastern Anatolian region of Turkey has been documented to have higher rates of nutritional inadequacy despite relatively higher body weight, partly due to diets rich in calorie-dense but micronutrient-poor foods [30]. Micronutrient deficiencies — including inadequate vitamin D, zinc, and iodine — are known to impair skeletal growth and dental calcification independently of total caloric intake [17, 18]. This nutritional profile may explain the paradox of higher BMI co-existing with delayed biological maturation in Group 1, and is consistent with the ‘double burden’ hypothesis. In contrast, Central Anatolian children may have benefited from more nutritionally diverse diets, contributing to more advanced skeletal and dental maturation despite lower BMI percentiles. Future studies incorporating dietary assessment tools and serum micronutrient measurements alongside BMI would substantially strengthen the understanding of this relationship.

Sex-specific differences were also evident. Underweight girls had significantly higher skeletal and dental ages than normal-weight girls, a pattern not observed in boys. Additionally, obese girls showed more advanced maturation than obese boys. These results may reflect sex-specific endocrine interactions involving leptin, estrogen, and the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, which influence development and may mediate differential sensitivity to adiposity [33, 34].

An important limitation of the present study is the absence of direct assessment of key confounding variables, including pubertal status, dietary patterns, physical activity levels, and socioeconomic status. Pubertal stage, in particular, is a major determinant of skeletal and dental maturation and is closely related to BMI, especially in girls. Without controlling for pubertal status, it is possible that some of the observed associations reflect the shared influence of puberty on both BMI and maturation rather than a direct BMI effect. Similarly, physical activity and dietary quality independently influence bone development and growth hormone secretion, and their absence from the model may have introduced residual confounding. Socioeconomic status, which correlates with both nutritional quality and access to healthcare, is another variable that future studies should incorporate. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings, and the authors recommend that future studies use structured dietary assessment, validated physical activity questionnaires, and pubertal staging (e.g., Tanner stages) alongside BMI evaluation.

For skeletal maturation assessment, this study used both hand-wrist radiography and CVM staging. Because these radiographs were part of routine orthodontic records, their combined use enabled cross-validation without additional radiation exposure. Previous studies have validated CVM staging against hand-wrist assessment, supporting the methodological robustness of these findings [14, 15].

The low R² values observed in regression analyses (0.009–0.018) indicate that BMI percentile accounted for only a small fraction of the variance in biological maturation. From a clinical perspective, these effect sizes imply that BMI alone is a weak predictor of skeletal or dental maturation, and its standalone use in clinical decision-making would be inappropriate.

Although statistically significant, these findings should be interpreted cautiously: the statistical significance is driven in part by the large sample size (n = 1011), which increases power to detect even small effects that may have limited practical relevance. This finding underscores the multifactorial nature of growth, governed by a complex interplay of genetic, hormonal, and environmental determinants [3, 4]. Similar weak correlations have been reported in other orthodontic populations [23]. Consequently, BMI should be interpreted as an adjunct factor within a broader developmental framework rather than a primary predictor.

With respect to studies conducted in Turkish orthodontic populations using the same estimation methods, Hedayati and Khalafinejad reported that in an Iranian sample using Demirjian’s method, BMI correlated positively with dental maturation, contrasting with the present findings [24]. Within the Turkish context, Mack et al. utilized Demirjian’s method alongside CVM staging in a comparable orthodontic cohort and reported a positive BMI–maturation association [23]. The divergence between those findings and the present study’s negative associations further reinforces the conclusion that this relationship is population-specific and mediated by environmental and nutritional factors unique to each geographic and socioeconomic context. Establishing normative BMI–maturation reference data specific to the Turkish pediatric population would be a valuable direction for future research.

From an orthodontic perspective, accurate assessment of biological maturation is essential for optimizing treatment timing. Our findings indicate that BMI percentile may influence maturation patterns and should be considered a relevant parameter in orthodontic growth evaluation. Incorporating nutritional assessment into clinical protocols may enhance treatment planning, particularly for growth-modification therapy and the timing of functional appliance use.

The novelty of this study lies in the simultaneous evaluation of dental age, hand-wrist skeletal maturation, and cervical vertebral maturation in a large, multi-regional orthodontic cohort. Furthermore, the identification of a negative association between BMI percentile and biological maturation, which contrasts with much of the existing literature, underscores the importance of population-specific growth assessment. Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between BMI and maturation, and the orthodontic patient-based sample may limit generalizability to the broader pediatric population. Future longitudinal studies incorporating hormonal biomarkers and detailed nutritional assessments are warranted to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of biological maturation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, higher BMI percentiles were negatively associated with skeletal and dental maturation in children from two regions of Turkey. These findings suggest that BMI may influence the timing of biological maturation and should be considered in orthodontic growth assessment and treatment planning. Regional differences underscore the importance of environmental and nutritional factors in growth evaluation. However, the low explanatory power of BMI (R² = 0.009–0.018) emphasizes that it should be regarded as an adjunct parameter rather than a primary determinant of maturation. The non-linear patterns observed, particularly in girls across BMI extremes, highlight the need for sex-specific and population-specific interpretation of BMI–maturation relationships. Further longitudinal, multi-regional studies are needed to confirm these findings and explore the underlying mechanisms.
