Surgical anatomy, biomechanics and clinical outcomes of latissimus dorsi transfer for irreparable rotator cuff tears: a review
Authors: Jean Michel Hovsepian, Marco A Cartaya, Johannes Glaser, Nicola Oehler, Igor Solovyov, Charbel Abi Mansour, Julio Carruyo Avila, Stephan Vogt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLatissimus dorsi transfer is a well-established joint-preserving option for irreparable rotator cuff tears in young, active patients without glenohumeral arthritis. Originally described by Gerber in 1988 for posterosuperior tears, indications now also include anterosuperior reconstruction, either as isolated transfer to the lesser tuberosity or in combination with the teres major. No recent review has integrated surgical anatomy, biomechanics, fixation strategies, clinical outcomes and patient selection in a single resource.Main textWe reviewed the anatomy, biomechanics, surgical technique, outcomes, and patient selection for latissimus dorsi transfer, drawing on the PubMed/MEDLINE literature through January 2026. For posterosuperior tears, the transfer reduces pain and improves external rotation, with sustained gains at follow-up beyond ten years. Patients with pseudoparesis show the largest functional gains. Subscapularis insufficiency, Goutallier 3–4 fatty infiltration of the teres minor, and prior failed repair predict poor outcome. For anterosuperior tears, anterior latissimus dorsi transfer or combined latissimus dorsi and teres major transfer restores internal rotation and reduces anterior subluxation, with outcomes comparable to pectoralis major transfer and a line of pull that better matches the subscapularis. Arthroscopic and open techniques produce comparable functional outcomes in published series.ConclusionsLatissimus dorsi transfer is the most biomechanically rational joint-preserving option for irreparable rotator cuff tears in younger, active patients without glenohumeral arthritis. Reverse shoulder arthroplasty remains the salvage option when arthritis is present or in older patients. Patient selection is the most important determinant of outcome.Keywords: Latissimus dorsi transfer, Irreparable rotator cuff tear, Tendon transfer, Posterosuperior cuff tear, Anterosuperior cuff tear

Background

Irreparable rotator cuff tears (IRCTs) are a difficult problem in shoulder surgery and account for a significant share of chronic shoulder dysfunction [1]. These tears, defined by the inability to achieve adequate tendon-to-bone repair due to massive tissue loss, poor tissue quality, or severe muscle atrophy, pose significant therapeutic dilemmas for orthopaedic surgeons [2, 3]. Anterosuperior tears involving the subscapularis impair internal rotation and anterior stability, while posterosuperior tears involving the supraspinatus and infraspinatus compromise both shoulder elevation and external rotation [3]. Up to 30% of all rotator cuff tears are classified as irreparable [2]. Affected patients frequently present with pseudoparalysis, defined as the inability to actively elevate the arm above shoulder level despite preserved passive range of motion. Left untreated, IRCTs progress to functional decline, rotator cuff arthropathy, and chronic pain that disables daily life and work [1].

When conservative management fails, surgical options have traditionally included debridement, partial repair, patch augmentation, and superior capsular reconstruction [2, 3]. These techniques, however, often fail to restore reliable shoulder function, particularly in younger and more active patients [4]. This gap has pushed surgeons toward tendon transfer, which restores active motion by biomechanical substitution [2].

Gerber and colleagues first introduced latissimus dorsi transfer (LDT) for irreparable rotator cuff tears in 1988, drawing on principles established in brachial plexus surgery [4, 5]. The rationale was biomechanical: in its native position, the latissimus dorsi acts as an internal rotator, adductor, and extensor of the humerus. By transferring the tendon to the greater tuberosity, its force vector is redirected, converting the muscle into an external rotator and humeral head depressor that works synergistically with the deltoid and an intact subscapularis to restore balanced glenohumeral mechanics [1, 4].

Since that initial report, clinical series, biomechanical work, and long-term outcome data have built up the evidence base [6–8]. Arthroscopic-assisted and fully arthroscopic techniques have refined the procedure and reduced approach-related morbidity [9, 10]. Nevertheless, questions remain regarding patient selection, the role of concomitant procedures, and the long-term durability of functional gains [3, 11].

This review draws on a literature search of the PubMed/MEDLINE database performed in January 2026, covering publications from January 2000 through December 2025, with seminal anatomical and historical references cited for contextual purposes. Search terms combined “latissimus dorsi transfer” and “latissimus dorsi tendon transfer” with “irreparable rotator cuff tear” and “massive rotator cuff tear”, using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and Boolean operators. Clinical series, biomechanical studies, anatomical investigations, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses were included; case reports, studies without functional or biomechanical outcomes, and series in which latissimus dorsi transfer was performed exclusively in combination with reverse shoulder arthroplasty without isolated transfer data were excluded. Findings are organized by thematic domain: surgical anatomy, biomechanics, surgical techniques, clinical outcomes, and patient selection.

Surgical anatomy of the latissimus dorsi

Origin, course, and insertion

The latissimus dorsi (LD) is the largest muscle of the upper trunk. It originates from the spinous processes of the lower six thoracic vertebrae via an aponeurosis, the posterior layer of the thoracolumbar fascia, the iliac crest, the lower three to four ribs, and a small slip from the inferior angle of the scapula. The muscle has three regions: superior, middle, and inferior. Inferior fibers are the longest, with fascicle lengths between 183.5 and 243.5 mm. Mean muscle volume is approximately 262 cm3 and mean weight 170 g [12–14].

The LD tendon undergoes an approximately 180° twist along its long axis before inserting on the floor of the bicipital groove of the humerus. Cadaveric studies report a mean tendon length of 7.3 cm and mean insertional width of 3.3 cm. Three insertion patterns relative to the teres major (TM) have been described: completely separate (66.6%), loosely bound (25%), and a common musculotendinous unit (8.3%). Fascial connections between the LD and the long head of the triceps are also reported [15].

Relationship with the teres major and surrounding structures

The TM inserts on the medial lip of the bicipital groove, just superior to the LD. This proximity permits combined harvest of both tendons when augmented transfer is planned. The axillary arch of Langer, a musculotendinous slip arising from the upper border of the LD, is present in approximately 5.3% of axillae and must be recognized during dissection to avoid inadvertent neurovascular injury [16, 17].

Neurovascular anatomy

The LD is classified as a Mathes–Nahai type V muscle, with one dominant pedicle and multiple secondary segmental perforators. The thoracodorsal artery, vein, and thoracodorsal nerve (TDN) enter the muscle at its upper lateral margin at a mean of 39.1 mm from the superior margin and 23.8 mm from the lateral margin. The thoracodorsal artery has a mean length of 9.7 cm and bifurcates consistently into medial and lateral intramuscular branches [18, 19].

The TDN arises from the posterior cord of the brachial plexus (C6–C8) and provides exclusive motor innervation to the LD. Its mean length is 7.8 cm. It always branches proximal to the artery and enters the muscle approximately 13.1 cm medial to the humeral insertion, giving two (53.8%) or three (46.1%) terminal branches that run superficial to the accompanying vessels [20, 21].

During tendon mobilization, the thoracodorsal pedicle must be identified before scapular adhesion release and circumferential dissection of the muscle. The neurovascular hilum is located at a mean of 85 to 140 mm proximal to the humeral insertion of the tendon, defining the safe working zone during proximal mobilization [22]. Tethering from fascial bands and scapular attachments can place the pedicle under tension during this step if not adequately protected. Preservation of the pedicle is essential not only for muscle viability but also for maintaining active motor function of the transfer, as denervation of the LD results in a purely passive tenodesis effect with substantially inferior clinical outcomes [23].

Anatomical hazards relevant to tendon transfer

Two major nerves are at risk during LD tendon mobilization. The radial nerve lies directly anterior to the tendon, approximately 2.9 cm medial to the superior edge and 2.3 cm medial to the inferior edge of the humeral insertion. The axillary nerve sits approximately 1.4 cm proximal to the superior border of the TM. Both distances decrease with the shoulder in internal rotation and abduction: the radial nerve closes to 2.0 cm and the axillary nerve to 1.8 cm. This position represents the highest-risk moment during tendon delivery. The posterior branch of the axillary nerve additionally crosses superficially over the transferred tendon during subdeltoid tunnel passage and requires identification during arthroscopic steps [22, 24]. Biomechanics of latissimus dorsi transfer

Shoulder biomechanics in rotator cuff-deficient shoulders

The glenohumeral joint depends on a balance of muscular forces to maintain stable articulation. The rotator cuff muscles work in concert to generate two interdependent force couples. In the coronal plane, the deltoid pairs with the supraspinatus and infraspinatus to produce abduction while compressing the humeral head against the glenoid, a mechanism known as concavity-compression [25]. In the axial plane, the subscapularis and the infraspinatus/teres minor pair resist medial-lateral translation and control internal-external rotation.

Massive posterosuperior tears disrupt both couples simultaneously. Cadaveric studies demonstrate that supraspinatus-deficient shoulders require up to twice the deltoid force to initiate abduction and exhibit 4 to 8 mm of additional superior translation [26]. Additional infraspinatus deficiency further amplifies posterior and superior migration. The clinical consequences are pseudoparalysis, external rotation lag, and progressive cuff tear arthropathy from eccentric glenoid loading. In fully irreparable tears, unopposed deltoid and pectoralis major forces drive superior humeral migration and anterosuperior subluxation, providing the primary rationale for tendon transfer.

Native muscle function

In its native position, the LD acts as a powerful internal rotator, adductor, and extensor of the humerus, with its greatest torque capacity between 30° and 50° of elevation. Its contribution to abduction is minimal, and its activation pattern is out of phase with the prime abductors. Mean maximum adductor moment arm is 37.3 mm and maximum internal rotation moment arm is 9 mm [27]. These characteristics make the LD biomechanically unsuitable as a direct abductor, but well suited for redirection toward external rotation and humeral depression after surgical transfer.

Functional changes after transfer

Rerouting the LD tendon from the bicipital groove to the posterosuperior footprint fundamentally alters its line of action relative to the glenohumeral rotation axis. Computational models show that transfer to the infraspinatus footprint consistently produces the largest external rotation moment arm and the most favorable fiber excursion ratio for posterosuperior reconstruction [28]. Transfer to the teres minor footprint generates suboptimal external rotation torque and is not recommended [28].

Cadaveric simulator studies confirm that LDT restores the posterior component of the transverse force couple, reduces superior and anterosuperior humeral head translation during abduction, and recenters glenohumeral contact patterns [29]. In-vivo three-dimensional kinematic studies demonstrate improved scapulothoracic and glenohumeral motion after transfer, though complete biomechanical normalization is rarely achieved [27].

The transfer carries inherent trade-offs. Rerouting reduces native adduction, extension, and internal rotation capacity, and decreases the fiber excursion ratio during elevation, increasing the risk of over-tensioning. Whether LDT functions as a true active motor unit or predominantly through a tenodesis effect remains debated. Electromyographic studies have demonstrated synchronous in-phase activation of the transferred muscle during external rotation and abduction in patients with good clinical results, supporting at least partial active motor function [7].

For anterosuperior tears involving the subscapularis, anterior-directed LDT attenuates anterosuperior migration and restores the anteroposterior force couple. The biomechanical rationale for this application was established by Elhassan et al. in a cadaveric feasibility study, which confirmed that transferring the LD to the lesser tuberosity produces a force vector closely approximating that of the subscapularis, given the muscle’s inferomedial origin on the posterior chest wall [30]. This is a fundamental advantage over pectoralis major transfer, whose anterior chest wall origin generates a force vector nearly perpendicular to the subscapularis regardless of the routing technique employed [31]. A computational biomechanical analysis by Kontaxis et al. further demonstrated that the LD produces a superior internal rotation moment arm compared to both pectoralis major and pectoralis minor transfers in subscapularis-deficient shoulders [32]. In a dynamic cadaveric model, Berthold et al. confirmed that anterior LDT significantly improves glenohumeral kinematics and reduces anterior humeral head migration [33]. When the LD is combined with the TM for anterosuperior tears, Baek et al. demonstrated superior restoration of glenohumeral kinematics and internal rotation moment arm compared to isolated LDT, attributed to the TM’s consistent humeral head depressive effect independent of abduction angle [34].

Fixation site, implant selection, and biomechanical considerations

Tendon-to-bone fixation in LDT is technically demanding given the inherent thinness and flat configuration of the LD tendon, which makes it susceptible to suture cut-through, consistently identified as a primary mode of early fixation failure. Tubularization of the tendon prior to fixation increases its effective cross-sectional diameter and distributes suture forces over a larger surface area, reducing this risk regardless of the fixation method chosen [35].

The position of fixation on the humeral head carries direct biomechanical consequences on the external rotation moment arm generated by the transfer. A cadaveric study by Bargoin et al. demonstrated that fixation at the infraspinatus footprint maximizes external rotation moment arm at 90° of abduction, while an anterolateral position relative to the supraspinatus footprint produces greater external rotation at 0° of abduction [36]. Fixation at the teres minor footprint produces lower external rotation torque compared with lateral greater tuberosity positioning in several simulated conditions and may therefore be less favorable depending on the functional objective of the transfer [28]. These position-dependent differences indicate that fixation site should be selected based on the patient’s primary functional deficit.

Three fixation strategies have been described for bone attachment: transosseous sutures, suture anchors, and interference screws. A cadaveric biomechanical study by Diop et al. comparing interference screw versus suture anchor fixation after tendon tubularization demonstrated higher construct stiffness and lower bone-tendon displacement during cyclic loading with the interference screw technique, with no significant difference in ultimate load to failure between the two methods [35].

Regarding anchor configuration, Ernstbrunner et al. compared knotted, knotless, and triple-anchor constructs in a cadaveric model of arthroscopically assisted LDT. The triple-anchor construct, consisting of two knotless anchors and one additional all-suture anchor at the apex of the humeral head, achieved the highest ultimate load to failure (431 ± 78 N) compared to knotted (326 ± 52 N, p = 0.022) and knotless dual-anchor (353 ± 105 N, p = 0.129) constructs. Failure mode analysis showed anchor pull-out as the predominant mode in knotted constructs, while suture pull-out through the tendon was more frequent in knotless configurations, reflecting the vulnerability of the thin LD tendon to suture-tendon interface failure. Addition of a third all-suture anchor is particularly indicated in osteoporotic bone [37].

The optimal arm position during fixation has not been directly evaluated in biomechanical studies of LDT in the native shoulder. The functional consequences of fixation in varying degrees of abduction and rotation on construct tensioning and transfer excursion remain undefined, representing a gap in the current biomechanical literature.

Surgical techniques

Open versus arthroscopic approaches

Three surgical approaches have been described for posterosuperior LDT: open, arthroscopically assisted, and fully arthroscopic. The original open technique, introduced by Gerber et al. in 1988, employed a dual-incision approach: a superior deltoid-splitting incision for rotator cuff assessment and greater tuberosity preparation, and a separate axillary incision for tendon harvest and mobilization [5]. Single-incision open modifications were subsequently described, reducing approach-related morbidity while maintaining adequate tendon exposure [38].

Arthroscopically assisted techniques have progressively replaced open approaches at high-volume centers. These allow assessment and treatment of concomitant intraarticular pathology, preserve deltoid integrity, and facilitate visualization during subdeltoid passage and tendon delivery to the footprint. Fully arthroscopic techniques have more recently been described, reporting comparable short-term outcomes to open and arthroscopically assisted approaches [39–42]. No prospective randomized comparison between open, arthroscopically assisted, and fully arthroscopic techniques currently exists for posterosuperior tears.

For anterosuperior LDT targeting the subscapularis, open, arthroscopically assisted, and fully arthroscopic techniques have similarly been described [43–45]. The deltopectoral approach provides direct access to the lesser tuberosity and allows clear identification of the LD tendon and its separation from the TM. Arthroscopic assistance allows concomitant intraarticular assessment and more precise tendon passage to the lesser tuberosity footprint. Fully arthroscopic combined LDT and TM transfer has been described with promising early results [45]. No prospective randomized comparison between techniques exists for anterosuperior tears.

Tendon mobilization and fixation

The LD tendon is released from its insertion at the bicipital groove with the arm in maximal internal rotation, which delivers the tendon into the surgical field and maximizes safe exposure. The thoracodorsal neurovascular pedicle must be identified before circumferential dissection, given its location 85 to 140 mm proximal to the humeral insertion. Adequate mobilization is confirmed when the tendon tip reaches the posterolateral border of the acromion with at least 2 cm of excursion. The tendon is then passed through the interval between the posterior deltoid and the infraspinatus/teres minor toward the greater tuberosity footprint [5, 38].

Multiple fixation strategies have been described, including transosseous sutures, suture anchors, and interference screws. Suture anchors are currently the standard fixation method in contemporary practice. Knotless systems demonstrate improved bone fixation compared to knotted techniques, and biomechanical data support the use of at least two anchors, with triple-anchor constructs demonstrating superior load-to-failure. The addition of a third all-suture anchor is particularly indicated in osteoporotic bone [37]. For anterosuperior LDT, the tendon is redirected anteriorly to the lesser tuberosity footprint of the subscapularis. The separation of the LD from the TM during harvest is essential when isolated LD transfer is planned, as combined harvest increases the risk of axillary nerve impingement given the increased bulk of the musculotendinous unit [30]. Fixation principles are identical to those for posterosuperior reconstruction: tubularization, secure anchor fixation, and avoidance of suture cut-through through the thin LD tendon.

Tensioning and arm position

Arm positioning during fixation is a critical technical step that directly influences transfer tensioning and postoperative function. In published posterosuperior series, fixation is most commonly performed with the shoulder in 30° to 60° of abduction and neutral to slight external rotation, aiming to approximate the resting length of the transferred muscle while avoiding over-tensioning, which restricts fiber excursion and increases glenohumeral contact pressure, and under-tensioning, which yields a functionally inadequate transfer [5, 38]. For anterosuperior LDT, fixation is performed with the arm in internal rotation to replicate the functional resting position of the subscapularis [43, 46].

Tendon orientation during subdeltoid passage requires careful attention. Rotation or twisting of the tendon along its long axis during transfer fundamentally alters its line of action and moment arm relative to the glenohumeral rotation axis, potentially compromising external rotation moment generation after fixation. Marking the tendon with a suture or skin marker before passage to confirm correct orientation at the footprint is a widely recommended technical precaution [39, 41].

Clinical outcomes

Posterosuperior irreparable rotator cuff tears: pain, function, and range of motion

LDT for posterosuperior tears produces significant improvements in pain, active range of motion, and patient-reported outcomes. The systematic review by Namdari et al. and the meta-analysis by Velasquez Garcia et al., focusing on mid- to long-term results with a minimum four-year follow-up, both demonstrate meaningful gains in CMS, forward flexion, abduction, and external rotation, with patient satisfaction exceeding 90% at long-term follow-up [6, 47]. Memon et al. specifically reviewed arthroscopically assisted LDT and confirmed favorable functional outcomes with this technique, supporting the adoption of minimally invasive approaches without compromising results [48]. External rotation represents the most consistent functional gain following posterosuperior LDT.

Long-term follow-up

Gerber et al. established the long-term durability of LDT in a landmark series with a minimum ten-year follow-up, confirming sustained improvements in pain and shoulder function in well-selected patients [49]. Kany et al. extended this evidence with a series of 147 shoulders followed for 10 to 20 years, reporting satisfactory clinical scores at final follow-up and a conversion rate to RSA of 12% [50]. Velasquez Garcia et al. noted a trend toward progressive glenohumeral osteoarthritis over time, though this did not reach statistical significance in pooled analysis, and highlighted that patients with pseudoparetic shoulders achieve greater functional gains than those with better preoperative function [6].

Complications and failure predictors

Across systematic reviews, the overall complication rate following posterosuperior LDT ranges from 9% to 13%, with a revision rate of approximately 6%, the most frequent revision procedure being conversion to RSA [6, 47]. The most common complications include seroma, superficial wound infection, and axillary wound dehiscence, most of which resolve without reoperation. Transfer rupture is the most clinically significant complication and the primary cause of revision surgery. Permanent neurologic injury is uncommon [6, 47]. Kany et al. reported a conversion rate to RSA of 12% at a minimum ten-year follow-up in 147 shoulders [50]. Predictors of failure across series include subscapularis insufficiency, advanced teres minor fatty infiltration (Goutallier grade 3 to 4), prior failed rotator cuff repair, and absence of synchronous postoperative EMG activation of the transferred muscle [6, 47, 49]. Anterosuperior irreparable rotator cuff tears

Clinical evidence for anterosuperior LDT is more limited than for posterosuperior reconstruction but favorable across published series. Elhassan et al. reported the largest multicenter series to date of LDT for irreparable subscapularis tears, demonstrating significant improvements in pain, forward flexion, internal rotation, and patient-reported outcomes across open and arthroscopically assisted techniques. Improvement in subscapularis-specific clinical examination maneuvers was observed in the majority of patients, though complete reversal of the lift-off and belly-press tests was achieved in a minority. Concomitant irreparable supraspinatus tear, fixed anterior subluxation, and arthritic changes were identified as negative prognostic factors [43]. Mun et al. confirmed significant functional improvement following open LDT in a dedicated subscapularis series, with improvement in internal rotation range of motion and belly-press test conversion in 75% of patients [44].

In a direct comparative study, Baek et al. demonstrated that while both isolated LDT to the lesser tuberosity and combined latissimus dorsi and teres major (LDTM) transfer to the greater tuberosity produce significant functional improvements, the combined technique is superior in restoring internal rotation strength, subscapularis-specific examination results, and anterior glenohumeral stability [46]. Baek et al. further confirmed the efficacy of combined LDTM transfer across both anterosuperior and posterosuperior indications, supporting its use as a versatile reconstructive option [51].

Long-term data for anterosuperior LDT are emerging. Baek et al. reported the most comprehensive long-term series to date, with a mean follow-up of 8.7 years in 26 patients, confirming sustained improvements in clinical scores, internal rotation range and strength, and active range of motion compared to preoperative values. The rate of cuff tear arthropathy progression remained low-grade, and only one patient (3.8%) required conversion to RSA at final follow-up. No neurological complications were associated with the procedure [52].

Minor early complications including seroma, superficial infection, and wound dehiscence are the most frequent and typically resolve without reoperation. Tendon transfer rupture is the most clinically significant complication and may require conversion to RSA. Temporary neurologic complications, particularly transient axillary nerve palsy, have been reported but resolve in most cases. Permanent neurologic injury is uncommon across all published series [43, 44, 46].

Patient selection

Posterosuperior tears

LDT is indicated in patients with massive irreparable tears causing partial pseudoparesis or external rotation lag sign, with preserved passive range of motion (forward flexion > 80°), intact subscapularis, no significant glenohumeral arthritis (Hamada grade ≤ 2), and functional deltoid [40, 49, 53]. Goutallier grade 3 or higher fatty infiltration of the teres minor, subscapularis insufficiency, prior failed rotator cuff repair, and active forward flexion below 70° after three months of physiotherapy are consistent predictors of poor outcome and represent relative to absolute contraindications depending on severity [6, 40]. The ideal candidate is a younger, active patient in whom joint preservation is a priority and the subscapularis is intact.

Anterosuperior tears

The indications are in patients with Lafosse grade 4 or higher tears, anterior instability or internal rotation pseudoparesis, preserved glenohumeral joint (Hamada ≤ 2), and functional deltoid [43, 53, 54]. Anterosuperior static humeral escape, advanced glenohumeral arthritis, and permanent axillary nerve palsy are contraindications. Combined LDTM transfer addresses both internal rotation strength and anterior stability more effectively than isolated LDT, though the increased transfer bulk requires careful assessment of axillary nerve impingement risk on a case-by-case basis [45, 52]. Discussion

LDT has established itself as the most biomechanically sound joint-preserving option for irreparable rotator cuff tears in young, active patients without glenohumeral arthritis. The anatomy, biomechanics, and clinical outcomes reviewed here support its role as a first-line tendon transfer in appropriately selected patients, for both posterosuperior and anterosuperior tear patterns.

Anatomical and biomechanical implications

The thoracodorsal pedicle location 85 to 140 mm proximal to the humeral insertion defines the safe working zone during mobilization and must guide every step of the dissection. Violating this zone risks denervation, converting an active motor transfer into a passive tenodesis with substantially inferior outcomes. The proximity of the radial and axillary nerves during tendon delivery, particularly with the arm in internal rotation and abduction, demands systematic nerve identification before passage regardless of approach.

Biomechanically, the transfer works because it restores the posterior component of the transverse force couple, reduces superior humeral migration, and recenters glenohumeral contact. It does not fully normalize shoulder mechanics. External rotation is the most reliably restored function. The decision to fix at the infraspinatus versus supraspinatus footprint should be driven by the patient’s primary functional deficit, as fixation site directly determines the dominant external rotation moment arm generated by the transfer.

Posterosuperior tears

Posterosuperior LDT has the most extensive outcome data of any tendon transfer for the shoulder. Functional gains are meaningful, durable, and confirmed at minimum ten-year follow-up by Gerber et al. [49] and at 10 to 20 years by Kany et al. [50]. Patients with pseudoparetic shoulders gain the most. Subscapularis insufficiency, advanced teres minor fatty infiltration, and prior failed repair consistently predict poor outcomes and should be considered relative to absolute contraindications depending on their severity [6, 47].

Partial repair restores partial transverse force couple balance through margin convergence and represents the least invasive option before LDT [55, 56]. A systematic review of 643 patients confirmed consistent functional improvement but structural failure approaching 50% [56], and long-term follow-up showed progressive osteoarthritis and fatty infiltration, with Goutallier stage 2 as the feasibility cutoff for primary repair [57]. When directly compared to LDT, pain relief is equivalent but transfer yields superior strength and function in younger patients [58]; in Goutallier stage 3 or higher tears the two procedures show comparable outcomes, with teres minor fatty infiltration the only independent predictor of worse results in both [59]. External rotation lag or advanced muscle degeneration favors LDT. Lower trapezius transfer has emerged as a biomechanically compelling alternative to LDT for posterosuperior tears. Cadaveric studies by Omid et al. and Muench et al. demonstrated superior external rotation moment arm, more anatomic line of pull, and better restoration of glenohumeral kinematics compared to LDT [60, 61]. A key advantage is in-phase activation with the external rotators, making it a true functional replacement rather than a redirected internal rotator. Clinically, Elhassan et al. reported the first outcomes series, and Baek et al. showed better external rotation and lower arthritis progression rates at two years compared to LDT [62, 63]. A systematic review by de Marinis et al. confirmed comparable complication rates to other alternatives [64].

From a technical standpoint, lower trapezius transfer is more straightforward than LDT. The harvest avoids the critical neurovascular structures encountered during LD mobilization, specifically the thoracodorsal pedicle, the radial nerve, and the axillary nerve, which demand meticulous identification at every step of the dissection. The main limitation of lower trapezius transfer is the need for an intercalary graft, most commonly an Achilles tendon allograft, to bridge the gap between the muscle and the greater tuberosity, adding cost and limiting accessibility in centers without allograft availability [61, 62]. Long-term data remain limited. Lower trapezius transfer should be considered in younger patients with isolated external rotation deficit and lag signs, while LDT remains the more established option with proven long-term durability.

Superior capsular reconstruction (SCR) restores superior glenohumeral stability through a static spacer effect, but it does not restore active external rotation. When external rotation deficit is the main complaint, SCR is not the right option. Graft thickness is a critical technical variable: constructs thinner than 6 mm fail to adequately stabilize the joint, which largely explains the inconsistent results seen with commercially available thin dermal allografts in North America [65, 66]. A survey of US shoulder surgeons confirmed declining SCR utilization, citing cost, technical complexity, and heterogeneous outcomes [67].

When compared directly to LDT, both procedures produce comparable functional improvements. A prospective randomized trial found similar short-term outcomes, with LDT showing less radiographic arthritis progression [68]. A systematic review confirmed that SCR produces better functional scores and greater acromiohumeral distance improvement, while LDT carries a higher infection rate but comparable reoperation rate [69]. At minimum five-year follow-up, no significant difference in patient-reported outcomes was identified between the two procedures [70]. LDT is preferred when active external rotation restoration is the primary goal; SCR may be more appropriate in patients without external rotation lag.

Biologic SCR using the long head of the biceps tendon as autograft has emerged as a less costly and technically accessible alternative to conventional SCR, eliminating allograft dependence while providing adequate graft thickness, with early series reporting favorable outcomes. No direct comparative data with LDT currently exist for this technique [71, 72].

The subacromial balloon spacer reduces superior humeral migration through a temporary biodegradable implant, with consistent short-term functional improvement reported across multiple series [73, 74]. The randomized controlled trial by Verma et al. demonstrated non-inferiority to partial repair at two years [75]; the randomized trial by Metcalfe et al. reported no benefit over debridement alone, a finding most plausibly explained by inclusion of patients with subscapularis tears and mean preoperative forward elevation below 90 degrees, both outside accepted indications for the device [76]. It does not restore active external rotation and is best suited for older or lower-demand patients with preserved forward elevation, intact subscapularis, and no pseudoparalysis. Direct comparative evidence with LDT is absent; Velasquez Garcia et al. described a combined LDT plus balloon technique for bidirectional deficiency, though clinical outcomes data remain lacking [77].

Tuberoplasty represents a further simplification, covering the greater tuberosity with a dermal allograft to prevent acromiohumeral contact. It does not restore active function and is best suited for older or lower-demand patients with isolated supraspinatus tears seeking primarily pain relief [78].

Biologic patches including the tuberoplasty concept and acellular dermal matrix augmentation represent adjunctive rather than reconstructive strategies and are generally considered for partial coverage of large defects or to reinforce primary repairs rather than as standalone treatments for fully irreparable tears [79–81].

Muscle advancement achieves superior static stability and acromiohumeral distance improvement but carries higher retear and reoperation rates than LDT; Lang et al. confirmed in a systematic review comparable functional scores between the two techniques, with LDT showing superior external rotation gains and lower structural failure rates [82–84].

When LDT fails, RSA is a reliable salvage. Marigi et al. demonstrated that RSA after failed tendon transfer leads to significant improvements in pain, range of motion, and patient-reported outcomes, with isolated LD transfer showing the lowest complication rate among transfer types preceding RSA. Prior LDT does not preclude RSA and does not worsen outcomes compared to primary RSA. The transferred tendon can be left in place or detached depending on the intraoperative findings and implant positioning [85, 86].

Anterosuperior tears

For anterosuperior tears with irreparable subscapularis deficiency, LDT is the most biomechanically sound option. Its posterior line of pull from the chest wall closely approximates the subscapularis force vector, which is why it outperforms pectoralis major transfer in restoring internal rotation strength and anterior stability. Luo et al. confirmed in a systematic review that LDT produces significantly better clinical outcomes than pectoralis major transfer for irreparable subscapularis tears [87]. Pectoralis major transfer, despite its longer track record, generates a force vector nearly perpendicular to the subscapularis regardless of routing technique, which limits its functional effectiveness particularly in the presence of anterior subluxation [31, 32].

For partial subscapularis insufficiency, specifically Lafosse grade 3 tears, pectoralis minor transfer is a viable joint-preserving alternative. Originally described by Paladini et al. and refined arthroscopically by Cartaya et al., this technique uses the pectoralis minor’s posterior origin to generate a traction vector closer to the subscapularis than the pectoralis major [88, 89]. Its application is limited to less severe tear patterns, and clinical evidence remains restricted to short-term series. When supraspinatus deficiency coexists with subscapularis insufficiency, isolated pectoralis minor transfer is generally insufficient.

Combined LDTM transfer for anterosuperior tears addresses both internal rotation strength and anterior glenohumeral stability more effectively than isolated LDT. However, the increased bulk of the combined transfer elevates the risk of axillary nerve impingement, and this must be weighed against the functional benefit on a case-by-case basis [45, 52].

When anterosuperior LDT fails, RSA remains the salvage procedure of choice. The tendon transfer does not create adhesions that compromise arthroplasty implantation, and conversion is technically straightforward through the deltopectoral interval [85].

Limitations

This review has the limitations typical of a narrative format. The included literature is predominantly retrospective, with significant heterogeneity in patient selection criteria, surgical technique, fixation method, and outcome reporting. No randomized controlled trials comparing LDT to alternative procedures have been performed. Most long-term series originate from high-volume academic centers, limiting generalizability to community practice. The anterosuperior literature is substantially less mature than the posterosuperior evidence base, and direct comparative data between LDT and alternative reconstructions for subscapularis deficiency remain sparse.

Future directions

Several questions remain unanswered. Prospective comparative trials between LDT and lower trapezius transfer for posterosuperior tears are still needed, as current evidence rests on retrospective series with limited follow-up. Biologic augmentation combining SCR with LDT and the role of structured neuromuscular rehabilitation also remain without prospective validation. For anterosuperior tears, the optimal fixation site, arm position during tensioning, and the indications for combined versus isolated transfer all require higher-quality evidence. Finally, the sequencing of joint-preserving and arthroplasty procedures in patients who have undergone prior LDT merits larger, longer-term study.

Conclusions

LDT remains the most biomechanically rational joint-preserving option for irreparable rotator cuff tears in younger patients without glenohumeral arthritis, with functional gains confirmed at minimum ten-year follow-up for posterosuperior tears and emerging long-term data supporting its use in anterosuperior deficiency. Patient selection is the critical determinant of outcome: subscapularis integrity, teres minor muscle quality, and the absence of glenohumeral arthritis define the ideal candidate, and no surgical refinement compensates for poor patient selection. When these conditions are met, RSA remains available as a reliable salvage, and ongoing advances in arthroscopic technique and combined transfer strategies continue to expand its application.
