Mapping the sources and strands of marine debris via particle modelling and In-situ sampling approaches in archipelagic countries
Authors: Noir P Purba, Ibnu Faizal, David Christie, Munawir B Pratama, Darryl A Valino, Marine K Martasuganda, Titin Herawati, Ajeng Wulandari, Sanny T Utami, Noor CD Aryanto, Muhammad H Ilmi, Raffy R Alfarez, Buntora Pasaribu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMarine debris (MD) is a global issue that remains unresolved in Indonesia where the accumulation of this kind of pollution poses a significant threat to the marine ecosystem and health of the ocean. Understanding the sources and locations of stranded debris is crucial in identifying the required regulations and mitigation strategies. This study used the hypothetical sources to identify the stranded debris along the coastline of Selayar Island and its surrounding seas. Using a combination of simulation and in-situ sampling methods, the simulation considered 13 hypothetical sources based on external factors such as river locations and surface ocean currents. This simulation result was then validated the simulation against beach litter observations and interview results with people. The results successfully mapped the sources and stranded MD in Selayar Island, indicating that the majority of MD originates from the island itself and the surrounding areas, influenced by oceanographic factors. The most frequently encountered locations of stranded MD are in the western coastal region including tourism areas and coastal ecosystem. Due to the oceanographic conditions, the stranded debris has varied quantities over the months, and higher in December represent the northwest monsoon (NWM) season, exceeding levels observed during the southeast monsoon (SEM). The study also identified that the main sources of debris are from Selayar Island and rivers from surrounding islands. This study also confirmed that oceanographic conditions such as surface ocean currents and wind pattern influenced the spreading of MD in Selayar Island. Introduction

Marine debris (MD) often known as marine litter, refers to solid materials that are discarded in marine and coastal ecosystems and are mostly made up of plastics. MD can be classified based on their size into different categories: mega (> 1 m), macro (2.5 cm to < 1 m), meso (5 mm to < 2.5 cm), micro (0.1 μm to < 5 mm), and nano (< 0.1 μm)1,2. As mega debris breaks down into smaller particles and settle in the water column, seabed, ecosystem and coastal regions. It can enter the food web, impacting marine ecosystem and lead to decrease ocean health and harms marine life. Furthermore, recent research have found that micro debris and nano debris have been impacts human health and reducing economies direct and indireclty3–5. This issue has still become an unresolved problem over the last four decades6–8. The amount of debris has been observed increasing exponentially in the surface ocean, water column, on the seafloor, and along the coastal shore6,9. Based on existing data, microplastics will have increased by 58.2%, from 2,510,400 metric tons in 2020 to approximately 3,967,756 metric tons in 205011.

The majority factors that lead MD stranded in the ocean is direct consequence of irresponsible human activities in the land, and is subsequently transported to the marine environment11,12. Due to the characteristic of MD is passive particles, the spreading to other regions influenced by the ocean dynamics. The oceanographic conditions such as the wind pattern, ocean currents, tides, water density, and other external factors influenced the pathways in the ocean body13,14. Using these parameters, several studies have stated that surface debris is transported to other regions following the monsoon patterns15 and Ekman currents16,17. However, identifying the original source and pathways of MD remains a major challenge, which limits our understanding of how debris circulates and is transported in the ocean due to complex circulation17,18. It is necessary to identify the flow of these substance their respective sources to identify and predict their trajectory resulting into the deposition to the marine environment18. This information will be valuable for range of studies, policy-making, and conservation efforts5,19. The economic impact of marine plastic pollution, particularly its effect on marine natural resources and ecosystems, varies from $3,300 to $33,000 per metric ton of marine plastic in the ocean each year20. By knowing the quantities, sources, and stranded locations of marine debris, a more aggressive and collaborative approach is necessary to meet the target of MD reduction such as managing Marine Protected Areas5,19.

In the last decade, models and in situ data have been developed to observe the movement of floating and drifting of debris in the marine environment21,22. In situ data can be using from GLOBAL drifter program and floating drifter23,24, artificial debris25, and aerial imaginary26,27. In model simulations, several tools have been studied to understand and determine the sources and trajectories of marine debris in the ocean using oceanographic parameters as input28,29. The initial data is provided by global repository such as Estimation of circulation and climate of the ocean (ECCO), Hybrid co-ordinate model (HYCOM), Ocean surface current analysis (OSCAR), Surface currents from diagnostics (SCUD30, Simple ocean data assimilation model (SODA), navy layered ocean model (NLOM)31,32. The development of this simulation based on lagrangian trajectory analysis that developed and enhanced by several institutions such as particle tracking model (PTM)33, OceanParcels v2.035, (General NOAA Operational Modelling Environment) GNOME15,16. These systems operate in a similar fashion to track the trajectories of marine debris in the ocean. However, MD direction monitoring is often influenced by distinct geographical regions, oceanographic processes, high cost, and difficult to replicate32. For these reasons, a rapid, directed, and a low-cost tool is critical for updating the high accuracy of tracing marine debris data collection that precisely accommodates the sources or entry points of marine debris in the ocean. For example, it was reported that GNOME (General NOAA Operational Modelling Environment) is a free software utilized to track the oil spill-trajectory developed by the Hazardous Materials Response Division and also capable of modelling the marine debris trajectory13,15,35,36. GNOME tools incorporated with the oceanographic process have advantages in monitoring debris pathways in the archipelagic region such as Japan and Indonesia13,15. Thus, it has been challenging to identify the origin of marine debris that trigger the source and pathways since the variability factors confound the interpretations of the data collected in the recent decade. The combination of recent software with real-time in situ samplings may facilitate understanding the complexity of marine debris trajectories. Up to the present time, however, the incorporation of recent combination of simulation and in situ samplings still need enhancement.

As one of the world’s largest archipelagic nations, it is significantly affected by MD37. With highest marine biodiversity, Indonesia’s marine health ranking is positioned at 152 out of 220 countries due and one of it because of increasing of marine pollution38. The reported various sources of the debris found in Indonesian seas were mainly from local activities, industrialization, tourism, and agriculture, as well as from other countries12,15,39. Based on previous study, the amount of MD in Indonesian waters ranges from 0.52 ± 0.36 million tons (Mt) per year40. The current situation poses as one of the major obstacles to achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 14.1 with target reducing marine pollution of all kinds and target of ecosystem restoration goals in 2030.

Specifically, due to its location between Pacific and Indian Ocean, ocean circulation has a critical role in the distribution of marine debris in Indonesia11,24. The existence of various currents system such as monsoon currents, eddies, and Indonesian Throughflow cause a dynamic and temporal variation in circulation patterns. Correspondingly, determining the source of the debris that washed up on the beach can be challenging. Based on previous finding, stranding of debris was reported to be high during northwest monsoon (Dec to Feb) and the southeast monsoon (Jun to Aug)29,41. Furthermore, the dominant category of these stranded debris found during these monsoon seasons are plastic and foams40,42.

One of the important islands in the eastern of Indonesian Seas is Selayar Island. It lies in the southern part of Sulawesi Island, located between Flores Sea and Banda Sea. Coral reefs and the abundant marine biodiversity of Selayar Island are well-known features. It is part of the region known as the Coral Triangle, which has the greatest diversity of marine life region in the world43. As a result, to preserve its marine ecosystems, the island has been designated as a regional marine conservation zone. This island also knows as part of Indonesia Throughflow pathways, centre of eddies, and influenced by monsoon currents (Fig. 1)44,45.

Notably, the complementary data on MD sources and pathways, regulation, and best practices provides insights of debris mitigation46,47. In contrast, very few studies are available combining the GNOME model analysis and in situ sampling to examine the marine debris trajectory in Selayar Island. To the best of our knowledge, a study of the characteristics of marine debris, including its economic impact, has been published48. Recent publications have stated that the simulation of using a GNOME model significantly facilitated understanding the complexity of marine debris trajectory in the Indonesian sea5,15. Unfortunately, to date, no simulation studies have investigated the combination of the model and in situ sampling to understand the source and entry points of marine debris in Selayar Island and surrounding seas. A comprehensive analysis of debris trajectory should scientifically advance our knowledge to understand the source and entry point of marine debris in the ocean. This study aims to investigate the trajectory of marine debris on Selayar Island by combining the GNOME model and in situ sampling. We assessed the baseline information of marine debris behaviour in Selayar Island based on ocean currents, winds, and diffusion as the main driving forces. In addition, we examined in situ sampling of marine debris in Selayar Island by collecting and recording the debris distribution and conducting the interview with the local society to gather the evidence of marine debris present in the coastal of Selayar Island.

Materials and methods

Geographical area

Selayar Island is located in southern Sulawesi and is one of the important destinations for tourism. The people mainly live in the west of the island, while the eastern part is overgrown with coastal vegetation including mangrove, seagrass, and coral reefs49. The island extends from north to south with a length of 86.30 km and is bordered by the Banda Sea on the east side and the Flores Seas on the west side (Fig. 1). Selayar Island is home to over 90,000 people, most of whom live in Banteng, which is located in the western portion of the island.

This region is predominantly influenced by the Asian-Australian monsoon, which originates from Asia and Australia continent via the Indonesian archipelagos. The Asian monsoon reaches its peak intensity from Dec to Feb and the Australian monsoon blows in the opposite direction and reaches its most intensity between Jun to August every year50. The characteristics of waters in this region are influenced by the presence of dominant ocean currents from the Makassar Strait through Flores and Selayar Island to the Banda Sea, which is part of the Indonesian Throughflow (ITF)45,51. In addition, eddies currents has been identified with 100 km to 200 km with clock and anti-clock wise pattern that affect circulation patterns in this region44.

Previous studies have found that debris stranded on the beach may originate from other regions due to oceanographic conditions24,52. Therefore, based on the circulation pattern surrounding the Selayar Island, this study chooses 13 hypothetical sources as starting points. The determination of starting positions relies on two main factors: 1) Multiple studies have identified rivers in Selayar and the adjacent island of Sulawesi as the primary sources of marine debris entering the ocean (Stations 1, 2, and 12)48,53. The other starting positions (Stations 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 13) are in the waters located surrounding Selayar Island. We assumed that these starting positions following the surface currents patterns and flow to Selayar Island. In details, station 11 represents starting point where ocean currents from the Makassar Strait, while Stations 8, 9, and 10 represent the exchange of water masses between the Java Sea, Makassar Strait, and Masalembo. Furthermore, Station 7 represents the circulation of eddies and the ITF. Stations 4, 5, and 6 represent the exchange of water masses from the Banda and Flores Sea which is also become the ITF pathways.

Data

In-situ data collection and beachsurvey

The in-situ sampling was conducted concurrently with a beach clean-up campaign on the west coast of Selayar Island from January 11th to January 15th, 2022. The campaign engaged teachers, high school students, local NGOs, and undergraduate students with approximately 48 participants. A standard log sheet form was used to identify the type of macro debris (> 2.5 cm) (www.marinedebris.id). Other equipment is GPS handheld Garmin 78s, transect meter, trash bag, and gloves. Field sampling and citizen science training was conducted on the Benteng Beach on the west coast adjacent to the port and tourist beach. Before conducting the beach clean-up, participants were provided with insights into marine debris and undertook a simulation of collecting MD using the scientific method. This simulation takes place indoors to ensure that participants can properly implemented the method to reduce data bias (Appendix 1: documentation).

Questionnaires and interviewing methods were held with participants and the community of beach clean-up as corresponded. The participants are 64 residents who have lived on this island for more than five years (Table 1). Due to different ages between participants, the class activities divided into two groups which the first group is teacher or instructor, volunteer, and NGO. The second group is students which also become the target of citizen science collecting debris.

Debris particles modelling

There are 13 hypothetical starting points to build the model based on surface ocean currents conditions (Fig. 1). The name of station started from 1 to 13 following clockwise direction. Modeling of marine debris using hypothetical sources has been successfully carried out by previous researchers in the Java Sea based on oceanographic inputs5,54. The simulation was carried out using GNOME (General NOAA Operational Modelling Environment) software which is developed and provided by the NOAA (National Oceanographic and Atmospheric Administration). GNOME model proofed has high accuracy and low computing power, especially for modelling oil spills and marine debris transport13,55,56.

The GNOME model approach includes particles with various windage values representing diverse debris types and behaviours (please see: https://response.restoration.noaa.gov/oil-and-chemical-spills/oil-spills/response-tools/gnome-suite-oil-spill-modeling.html). The term “windage” means the combination of sail area and drag. Commonly, low windage represent slow movement including woods, and high windage such as unoccupied inflatable life raft. The data used as input movers used the Euler scheme with 24 h timestep to describe the trajectories of surface debris. In this case, for characteristic of debris based on windage (1–4%) provided by NOAA 5711. The pollutant setting used is non-weathering, with the number of debris coming out of each station as much as 10 kg. During this process, GNOME version 1.3.11 was used with a map provided in NOAA’s WebGNOME (https://gnome.orr.noaa.gov). The input parameters were added in GNOME software to support scenarios of MD movement including: wind, surface currents, amount and location of starting points, and random diffusion. Wind and current data that were used in 2021. Wind data were provided by NOAA/NCEP Global Forecast System (GFS) Atmospheric Model with three hourly and resolution of 0.50°. The ocean currents and wave data were downloaded from Copernicus Marine Environment Monitoring Service (CMES) with hourly data and a resolution of 0.083°. The ocean tides were downloaded from https://srgi.big.go.id/tides. The statistical results of wind and current data for the two monsoons can be seen in Table 2.

Data Analysis

Generally, the primary inputs to run a model in GNOME are ocean currents, winds, and diffusion. To identify the zonal movement (u velocity) and meridional (v movement) velocity components, ocean parameters are added together in each timestep, using a forward Euler scheme (a 1st-order Runge-Kutta method). The movers are given a point (x, y, z, t) and return a displacement (Δx, Δy, Δz) at t) 5711.

Where Δt = t - t1 is the time elapsed between timesteps; y is the latitude in radians; 111,120.00024 is the number of meters per degree of latitude (assumes 1’ latitude = 1 nautical mile); and (Δx, Δy) are the 2-D longitude and latitude displacement, respectively, at the given depth layer z. We used 24 h timestep and each month’s simulation. All statistical analysis and visualization were conducted using R software5812. This study used a preliminary test (Normality and Homogeneity test) to determine whether the data meets normal assumptions. Followed by Kruskal-Wallis test is used to define the debris particles from land (South Sulawesi coastal and Selayar Island)and sea (Flores Sea and Banda Sea), which influence accumulated solid debris on Selayar coastal in two different monsoon times (p < 0.05).

The analysis results of particle movement will then be compared with questionnaire data and field data to demonstrate the alignment between hypothetical sources (such as potential origins or points of release) and stranded debris found in Selayar Island. This comparison aims to establish the correlation and consistency between the observed patterns of particle movement and the actual data obtained from surveys and field observations, thereby providing insights into the possible sources and pathways of the debris found in Selayar Island.

Results

Debris characteristics and hotspot mapping

The interview and questionnaire scores combined total of 50.16% indicate that the correspondent’s knowledge of marine debris is sufficient. When it comes to the identification of waste size categories and the factors influencing their distribution, correspondents are still lacked to have an advanced knowledge. However, most participants are aware of the kinds of waste that pose a threat to the environment. Following this, the participants can utilize as a guide to locate areas along the coastline where debris has been found. There were 18 debris hotspots in the western part of Selayar Island and 5 in the eastern part (Fig. 2). The debris hotspots were higher in western part where most of the population lives, while in the eastern part, debris hotspots were in tourist regions. The population of Selayar Island in 2022 was 89,883, according to the BPS Kabupaten Selayar. The district with the highest population, Benteng, is situated on the western side of the island with 25,397 residents (https://selayarkab.bps.go.id/). According to the tourism data (https://pariwisata.kepulauanselayarkab.go.id), the number of tourist visits was 20,962 in 2022 and 20,941 in 2023.

Coastal Debris hotspot based on questionnaire results (red dots) and in-situ validation (star icons). Most of the spots is located in the western side of the island. Maps were created using Qgis ver 3.28.13 (Qgis Development Team,- http://qgis.osgeo.org, 2024).

Based on the questionnaire findings, it was observed that among the 18 identified hotspots in the western region, three of them (hotspots) are situated on Gusung Island. The remaining hotspots are predominantly concentrated along the coastal areas, encompassing various locales of daily human activities such as tourist beaches, bustling ports, and densely populated residential zones. Furthermore, interviews revealed the dominant concentrations of MD in estuarine regions located in the northern region of the island. Insights from interviews highlighted the recurring issue of substantial MD accumulation at river estuaries, particularly during periods of heightened rainfall from December to February. Based on the data obtained from the Selayar database, it can be concluded that the months of December to February are characterized by a higher amount of rainfall on Selayar Island. December has the most amount of rainy days, with 16 days, followed by January with 13 days, and February with 12 days. The precipitation levels are significantly elevated throughout these months. The month of December has the most recorded rainfall, with 439.00 mm. It is closely followed by January, which has a rainfall of 415.00 mm, and February, with a rainfall of 364.00 mm (https://selayarkab.bps.go.id/).

The primary types of debris encountered by participants primarily comprised plastic and foam materials. These observations were further validated through in-situ sampling exercises conducted across multiple designated locations. From beach clean-up activities in the Benteng site, the most common types of MD also similar with other stations in the coastal of Selayar Islands such as plastic and foam. This plastic MD can be in the form of 500 ml mineral water, shampoo packaging, straws, plastic glass, sandals, and other plastic packaging with average wight debris about 9.5 2.7 item/m2 and weight about 229.2 109.9 g/m2 in Selayar Island36. Furthermore, the type of foam can be in the form of glass foam and other foams. Some of these debris are intact and some have been damaged. In addition, several pieces of wood were also found along the beach.

According to the results of interviews with participants, this MD came from other islands and came from rivers in the northern part of the island during NWM. The minimal occurrence of debris hotspots in the eastern part is attributed to the inherent challenges of accessing this coastal area, coupled with its status as a less frequented tourist destination. Despite these factors, insights gleaned from interviews have revealed that debris is still present in this locale, particularly during observations conducted during the Southeast Monsoon (SEM) conditions.

Monthly variation of marine debris pathways

In general, the simulation revealed that each starting point contributed to the accumulation of debris in Selayar Island and its surrounding islands, including from the coastlines of South Sulawesi and Banda Sea. It is observed that the pattern of debris pathways varies widely from month to month (Fig. 3).

In the early NWM season (December), the movement of marine debris is dominantly toward the northeast regions. The debris mostly came from stations 1 and 12, particularly those from Selayar coastal and the river in Makassar. From January to February, most of the particles from various starting points move towards the northeast regions, specifically Flores Sea and Banda Sea. At the end of the month, some debris remains in the surface water whether the others debris showed stranded in onshore of Selayar Island. Along the coast of South Sulawesi, stranded debris indicated from several stations including Selayar Island (2, 3), Flores and Makassar Strait (9, 10, 11), and Sulawesi itself (12, 13). From the simulation showed that simulated debris accumulation on the coast of South Sulawesi is found in large amounts compared to Selayar Island. Stranded debris was also found in several small islands in the southern of Selayar Island and primarily originating from Flores Sea (10), Makassar Strait (11), and Makassar River (12).

From March to May (the transition monsoon), debris movements became more varied compared to previous monsoon. However, stranded debris in Selayar Island was still found dominant in the western regions. Debris from the Flores Sea (9,10) tends to move eastwards towards the Banda Sea following the surface currents. In April, a change in particle movement direction compared to previous months was observed. The dominant direction is moved to the western side of the island. Meanwhile, debris from stations 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12 moved to Makassar Strait. For Stations in the eastern of Selayar Island (4, 5, 6), the northwest movement was also observed, but adhered on Sulawesi Island, precisely in the eastern part. In addition, simulated debris from stations from Selayar Island (1,2) are moving toward to the southeast.

When the SEM commenced in June, it became evident that debris accumulation was increasing significantly on the eastern side of Selayar Island, with no debris found along its western coast. In the southern part of Selayar Island, stranded debris was predominantly observed from stations 1 and 2, while along the eastern coast, it primarily originated from Selayar itself (2, 3), the eastern seas (4, 5, 6), and the southwestern region (7). Conversely, debris originating from stations in Flores and the Makassar Strait (8, 9, 10, 11, 12) predominantly moved northwest towards the Makassar Strait in large formations. From July to August, the movement of simulated debris in all stations showed a dominant direction inclined towards the northwest regions.

In September, the debris movement exhibited complexity, showing no clear direction resulting into no significant accumulation of debris at one point. The simulated debris towards two different directions. Several pathways of debris move southeast toward the three small islands near Selayar Island, while others move west toward the Makassar Strait. The density of debris is lower than in previous months. The originated debris from Flores Sea and Makassar Strait (8, 9, 10, 11, 12) moved northwest towards the Makassar Strait. In October, the movement of debris was dominant towards the northeast regions. The most significant change could be observed on the east side of Selayar Island. This result indicates that MD moved from southeast towards three smalls islands near Selayar island and west towards the Makasssar Strait in September and debris predominantly moved northeast, particulary affecting the east side of Selayar island and South Sulawesi. Debris continued moving northeast, heavily impacting the west coast of Selayar island, originating from various region including Flores Sea and Makassar Strait in November.

The source and stranded of accumulated marine debris

All the simulated debris particles which found and stranded on Selayar Island come from all station sampling within a different range of the total debris accumulated based on different months and monsoon seasons. The debris particles discovered on Selayar Island through simulations were collected from various sampling stations, covering a wide range of debris accumulation across different months and monsoon seasons. In general, according to the simulation results, it is evident that debris from 13 stations accumulates along all the coastlines of Selayar Island, with the amount of MD fluctuating each month. Among these stations, one particular station situated in southeastern Selayar (Station 7) is notable, as its impact is confined to the months of June and July (Fig. 4).

In details, Station 1 which is located in on Selayar Island, influenced the MD distribution on the western stretch, spanning from approximately Benteng to the southern section. Similarly, stations 2 and 3, which are in the northern part of Selayar Island, impact the western coastline up to the northern area. Stations 4, 5, and 6, situated in the northeastern and eastern regions of the island, influence western region of Selayar Island, while stations 7 and 8 affect the southern region. Stations 9, 10, and 11, located in the Flores Sea and Makassar Strait, predominantly impact the coastal zones on the western side. Meanwhile, stations 12 and 13, found in South Sulawesi, influence both the western and northern regions of the island.

During NWM (Dec-Feb), the debris particles accumulated on the west coast of Selayar. The majority of the debris originated from stations 1 and 2, with small amounts come from Stations 9, 10, 11, and 12. In the southern Selayar, debris came from station 8. In December, the dominant debris shifted to the northeast and east of Selayar waters with the accumulated particles originating from station 2. However, throughout the peak of west monsoon time (December), almost all stations (except station 2) had a minor contribution to the accumulation of MD on the west coast.

In the transition period in March to May, accumulated debris was observed in Selayar from Stations 1, 2, 9, 10, 11, 12, and 13. In March, marine debris movement expansion was detected at each station. The debris coming from Stations 3, 4, 5, and 6 moved northwest towards Bone Bay. In April, the dominant current shifted to the west and the accumulated debris had decreased significantly in the western part of Selayar Island, with debris present from station 1 only. In contrast, the accumulated debris had begun on the east side of Selayar Island, particularly from station 3. The types of debris present predominantly originated from Stations 3 and 5. Accumulated debris in large quantities were also found on the west side of a small island near Selayar sourced from stations 1, 2, 8, and 12.

In the SEM (rJun-Oct) Station 3 had the highest contribution of debris, followed by Station 5 (Apr-Sep). In the NWM period, stations 1, 2, 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12 had the most influence, with stations 1 and 2 have a large contribution to MD stranded in Selayar Island. Station 2 regularly supplied debris particles from October to March but unexpectedly contributed in June with minimal percentage. Station 1 contributed particles almost consistently throughout the year, with the highest contribution during October to March. On the other hand, Stations 4,6,7,13, had small percentage contribution all year, with Stations 4 and 7 having minimal contribution between May and August.

The average accumulated particles of solid debris during one-year simulation data were 89.43 (SD = 216.46). In particular, for specific monsoon time, the average accumulated debris in SEM is 42.44 (SD = 95.95), and NWM is 168.82 (SD = 353.07). These results showed that the total debris particles were higher in NWM time than in SEM. As the preliminary test showed a lack of normal distribution, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare marine debris samples from the land and sea during two monsoon seasons (SEM and NWM) that were stranded on the Selayar coast. The results revealed statistical differences among sample groups, indicating that the accumulated debris differs significantly between the two seasons. However, there was no significant difference in accumulated debris particles among months within each monsoon season (SEM, p = 0.891; NWM, p = 0.06). In general, the number of stranded debris on Selayar Island increased significantly in December (> 5000 particles) (Fig. 5). Moreover, from January to October, the accumulated debris particles were less than 1000 particles, then in November increased to 2730 particles and reached the highest peak in December. Discussion

The findings obtained from interviews and sampling procedures conducted on Selayar beach underscore the prevalence of macro debris in the area. Notably, significant concentrations of debris hotspots are observed more frequently along the western coastline of the island in comparison to its eastern counterpart (Figs. 2 and 3). This corroborates are in accordance with previous research findings indicating that the highest densities of debris were recorded along the west coast during the months of February and March, with debris weighing between 100 and 399 g (about 14.07 oz)/m2 49. The abundance of debris discovered on the western coast of Selayar Island can be attributed to two main factors. One contributing aspect is the existence of westward-flowing rivers that traverse residential zones on the island. This is confirmed by the findings of the questionnaires, which indicate that there is an increase in the quantity of debris on the beach during the rainy season (December to February). The second aspect is the influence of monsoon winds that occur during the same months. These winds cause the marine debris to accumulate on the western beaches and prevent it from moving beyond the island. Furthermore, the types of macro debris commonly encountered on Selayar Island predominantly include foam and plastic packaging materials. Similar categories of debris are also reported on various other beaches across Indonesia, including Pangandaran, Kupang, Banten, the Thousand Islands, and Raja Ampat15,42. These collective observations highlight a concerning trend wherein household MD appears to be the primary contributor to MD across these regions.

In general, the debris stranding and movement on Selayar Island were affected by oceanographic factors such as wind and surface currents. It has been reported that the trajectories of marine debris patterns vary in direction during the Australian and Asian Monsoon seasons, depending on the time of the year. NWM winds blow from December to February, while SEM winds blow from June to August yearly6. The similar patterns of stranded debris occurred in January and February, where the movement of debris in both months tend to move towards the northeast (Fig. 2). During this period, the wind moves from the west (Flores Sea) towards the Banda Sea. This pattern similarly reported in previous studies indicates that the stranded debris in Selayar is influenced by wind and ocean currents carrying the debris to the west of Selayar island36. In March, the debris was docked in south Selayar Island through winds blown from Asia region. This coincides with literature reporting the persistence of NWM sometimes until March and April, even with weakened intensity37. However, the movement patterns highlighted a change of direction in April, compared to the eastern movement observed from January to March. Our study demonstrated the major debris movement on Selayar Island shifted towards a westward direction. The debris moved towards the Makassar Strait and then passed through the Flores Sea towards Bone Bay in October. Furthermore, during the transitional monsoon period, the wind were reported the unclear patterns, which also occurred in September to November each year6.

The simulation results showed a significant increase in MD that occurred in December (Fig. 3). Furthermore, the modelled stranded marine debris during NWM and SEM is also supported by field sampling conducted in January, wherein debris was stranded on the west coast. In addition, interviews with the community shows that the debris stranded on the west coast comes from other areas (Fig. 2). Simulations with GNOME also showed the presence of complex circulation in this water area. The influx of water masses from the Makassar strait also significantly impacted the movement of MD. These ocean currents occurred throughout the year and pointed to the west and south sides of Selayar Island and joined the water masses from the Java Sea59,60. This can also be seen from the pattern of MD distribution where not all MD will move westward when SEM. Some particles are still in the waters and do not reach the island of Selayar and move in other directions. Furthermore, debris particles forming a circular pattern are caused by the current of eddies around the island of Selayar. The formation of these eddies is a result of the convergence of currents, leading to the formation of a circular motion. The circular motion observed in the waters of Selayar Island is a result of the convergence of currents originating from the Java Sea, Makassar Strait, and Banda Sea. These spinning currents can either be long-lasting or short-lived, and their movement is influenced by the prevailing environmental circumstances. These counter clock eddies events usually occur in February and April44. This incident significantly affected the number of debris stranded on the island of Selayar and its surroundings. Therefore, it’s possible that eddies will cause trash that has been abandoned on Selayar Island to return to the island.

Although the debris on Selayar Island could originate from multiple sources, the dominant origin is Selayar itself (Fig. 4). This is in contrast from previous studies in several locations in Indonesia, such as Muara Gembong38 6014Java, and Bali21; wherein the it was reported other region sources. This can be attributed to the geographical characteristics of the island. Its length from north to south is 86.30 km is significantly larger compared to the width of the island (~ 19.41 km). The geographical location of Selayar island spans approximately 86.30 km from north to south, which is significantly larger compared to its width of about 19.41 km. Furthermore, the dominant ocean currents in these waters flow from west to east, making the island prone to debris deposition compared to other locations. The field survey shows that plastic MD in the form of 500 ml of beverage packaging was the most common debris. From the search results, this MD producer comes from the Makassar area and its surroundings. MD from the Makassar river has been studied by previous researchers and has a significant impact on the surrounding sea61. Rivers in Makassar pass through big cities and residential areas. It is a main source of debris that will flow into the sea.

The problem of marine debris has gained significance and garnered significant attention from international communities. Stranded debris on Selayar island affected local economic impacts the local economy including losses of marine tourism industry and business, as well as a decrease in fishery catches due to accumulation of debris pollution48. Simulations of MD in Selayar island and other waters region suggest that MD stranded on beaches couldoriginate from various regions. Therefore, collaboration between local governments is needed to address this waste problem. In this case, educating younger generation about the threat of debris to ecosystems is crucial. Our study demonstrated that the workshops targeting high school students and teachers revealed an awareness of the problems and impacts of marine debris. However, there are still difficulties in handling them such as ingrained behaviour, a lack of trash bins on the islands, and lack of will to protect environment. In addition to educating the community, assistance is also needed in training to develop the by-products from MD through blue innovation technology. This could include initiatives related to “waste to energy” or “waste to the rising economy”, providing communities with income opportunities from MD.

In developing countries such as Indonesia, plastic packaging for food wrapping and toiletries is still common in the household. It is based on a relatively cheap and easily available price. Small packaging products such as shampoo sachets and 500 ml beverage packaging are common ones found in stalls. It also shows that these two types of debris are the most abundant on the beach. For this reason, packaging changes can be made with an approach to related industries. The usage of packaging from plastics material could be substituted with packaging from biodegradable material. Indonesia currently has a national target (National Plan on Plastic), which is to reduce MD entering the oceans by 70% by 2025 and 0% by 204041. In line with this, the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) specific target 14.1 emphasizes having the smallest possible impact on the marine environment, including pollution from marine debris. For this reason, the handling of the issue of marine debris starts from the knowledge of the origin of MD and where the MD is stranded. With Indonesia’s seas connected to the Pacific and Indian Oceans, regionally joint observations are needed62,63. In Indonesia, a marine debris database is already used as baseline study data for the government to determine related policies19,42.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the comprehensive results of marine debris sources and stranded highlighted the various quantities of debris derived from different starting points, particularly originating from Selayar Island. In addition, we found that the highest quantities of debris were elevated in December, influenced by the Northwest Monsoon (NWM) Season. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate marine debris sources and stranded debris by combining modelling and in-situ observation in Indonesia. The findings of this study not only provide a new understanding of the sources of marine debris in archipelagic countries but also facilitate guidance for policymakers in formulating strategies to reduce debris pollution entering the ocean to 0% by 2040. Future investigations are needed to develop joint observations among archipelagic countries to expand the database and implement effective approaches to optimize debris pollution mitigation entering the ocean.
