Coping, Subjective Burden and Mental Health in Family Carers of Dependent Children and Adolescents: A Systematic Review With Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Juan Carlos Muñoz‐Cruz, Catalina López‐Martínez, Vasiliki Orgeta, Rafael Del‐Pino‐Casado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionCaring for dependent children and adolescents places a substantial emotional and psychological burden on families. Coping strategies may influence the mental health of family carers.AimTo systematically review how coping strategies relate to subjective burden, anxiety, and depressive symptoms in family carers of dependent children and adolescents.MethodSystematic review and random‐effects meta‐analysis were performed, searching PubMed, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and LILACS (up to June 2025). Observational studies examining the association between coping strategies and subjective burden, anxiety, or depression in family members caring for dependent children aged under 18 were included. Study quality was assessed via sampling method, instrument reliability, and confounder control.ResultsThirty‐eight studies (42 samples; 4548 participants) were included. Dysfunctional coping was consistently associated with more depressive symptoms, anxiety, and subjective burden. In contrast, functional coping was related to fewer depressive symptoms and subjective burden. Emotion‐focused coping and its individual strategies were generally associated with less psychological distress. Problem‐focused coping showed mixed results.DiscussionDysfunctional coping appears to represent a risk to carers' mental health, whereas functional coping—particularly emotion‐focused strategies—might be protective. However, all studies were cross‐sectional.Implications for PracticeAssessing and encouraging adaptive strategies, especially those focused on emotion coping, could improve carers' mental health. Trial Registration: International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews: PROSPERO: CRD42024533044.Keywords: adolescent, caregiver burden, caregivers, children with disabilities, coping skills, mental health, meta‐analysis

Introduction

Although health advances in recent decades have significantly reduced perinatal and infant mortality, the prevalence of chronic illnesses and disabilities among children and adolescents continues to rise (World Health Organization and United Nations Children's Fund 2023). In this context, the term dependent children and adolescents refers to those living with chronic health conditions, disabilities or developmental difficulties that limit their ability to perform age‐appropriate daily activities independently (Mattson et al. 2019). Consequently, these children and adolescents often require intensive and sustained care provided by third parties, most commonly parents or other relatives (World Health Organization and United Nations Children's Fund 2023). It is currently estimated that hundreds of millions of parents worldwide provide care for children with chronic health conditions and disabilities (Olusanya et al. 2022), frequently with limited access to formal services and support, particularly in low and middle‐income settings (He et al. 2024; Semrau et al. 2016).

Providing intensive daily care is a source of significant distress for family carers, increasing risk of mental health problems (World Health Organization and United Nations Children's Fund 2023). The long‐term and demanding nature of the caregiving role often leads to subjective caregiver burden, defined as the carer's personal appraisal of the physical, emotional, and psychological strain associated with providing care (Del‐Pino‐Casado et al. 2018). In line with this, family carers of dependent children and adolescents consistently report higher levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms compared with carers of children or adolescents without limitations in their developmental functioning (Scherer et al. 2019).

Previous theoretical models (Lazarus and Folkman 1984; Pearlin et al. 1990) developed to explain what factors influence the stress process of family carers of children with disabilities (Raina et al. 2004) highlight the key role of coping responses in managing stress related to caregiving. Coping refers to cognitive and/or behavioural efforts that people engage in to cope with situations that exceed their personal and social resources (Lazarus and Folkman 1984). Carver et al. (1989) and Moos (1988) classified coping strategies into functional/approach ones, such as problem‐focused (actively aimed at resolving stress) and emotion‐focused (regulating emotional responses) (Lazarus and Folkman 1984), and those associated with distancing or escaping from stressors, known as dysfunctional/avoidant coping responses.

In recent decades, several studies have investigated how coping relates to the mental health of family carers of dependent children and adolescents (Fairfax et al. 2019; Lai and Oei 2014). Despite accumulating evidence, however, key knowledge gaps remain (Fairfax et al. 2019; Lai and Oei 2014), such as the strength of these associations and quality of studies conducted to date. We therefore have no knowledge about how different coping dimensions and their individual strategies impact on the mental health of carers, which is key for informing future interventions (Koumarianou et al. 2021).

On the other hand, it has been recognized that various factors, such as the type of chronic condition affecting the child or adolescent or the socio‐economic characteristics of family carers, may influence coping responses and mental health outcomes (Fairfax et al. 2019; Lai and Oei 2014). However, although these factors have been identified, the specific interactions and their impact on psychological outcomes have not been fully explored. Understanding, therefore, how different sources of heterogeneity may moderate the relationship between coping strategies and carers' mental health is key. This approach could help to identify which moderating factors may moderate effects of psychological interventions.

Aim

The main objective of this study was to undertake the first systematic review investigating the associations between different coping strategies used by family carers of dependent children and adolescents, and their association with caregiver burden and mental health outcomes. Additionally, a secondary aim was to explore how potential sources of heterogeneity influence the observed estimates and review the quality of evidence to date.

Method

A systematic review with meta‐analysis was conducted. The guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐analyses (PRISMA) statement (Page et al. 2021) were followed. This review was registered in PROSPERO.

Search Method

We conducted searches for all publications up to June 2025 in international databases (PubMed, PsycINFO, LILACS and CINAHL) using key terms of coping, caregivers, anxiety, burden and depression, without applying any date restrictions or other limitations (see Appendix S1). We additionally conducted a reverse search of literature cited in relevant reviews and articles to ensure no studies were missed and contacted the authors of unpublished research to request missing data.

Eligibility Criteria

The studies included met the following criteria: (a) original research with an observational design, (b) analysing the association between at least one coping strategy or coping dimension and symptoms of anxiety, depression and/or subjective caregiver burden, (c) reporting a correlation coefficient or sufficient statistical data to calculate one (such as standardized mean differences, odds ratios, etc.), (d) in family carers of children and adolescents (under 18 years of age) who required support due to a chronic condition (i.e., autism, cancer, diabetes etc.) or limitations in their stage of development. We included studies published across settings in English, Spanish, French or Portuguese.

Data Extraction

The extracted data included: study design, sample size, sampling method, country of residence, relationship of the family carer, cause of dependency and age of the care recipient, coping strategy evaluated, measurement scales used for the different variables of interest, and reported effect sizes and their statistical significance. Two reviewers (J.C.M.‐C. and C.L.‐M.) independently conducted data extraction (inter‐rater reliability, percent agreement: 98.6%). Any mismatches were resolved by reaching an agreement with a third reviewer (R.D.‐P.‐C.).

We classified coping following the studies of Lazarus and Folkman (1984), Moos et al. (1990) and Carver (1997) (A conceptual diagram of this classification can be found in Muñoz‐Cruz et al. (2024)): (a) problem‐focused coping: active coping strategy, planning and seeking instrumental support; (b) emotion‐focused coping: acceptance, humour, positive reappraisal, religious coping, self‐control and seeking emotional support; and (c) dysfunctional coping: avoidance, behavioural disengagement, denial, distancing, mental disengagement, resignation, self‐blame, self‐distraction, substance use, venting and wishful thinking. Two additional dimensions were created: (d) functional coping, which encompassed problem‐focused and emotion‐focused coping strategies and (e) social support seeking, which integrated both emotional and instrumental support seeking.

Quality Assessment

We assessed the methodological quality of the included studies based on criteria adapted from Boyle (1998) and Viswanathan et al. (2013), including: (a) use of probability sampling to minimize selection bias; (b) use of reliable and validated instruments to address classification bias (defined as internal consistency with Cronbach's alpha > 0.6 and content validity for the target population) and (c) adjustment for at least one objective burden‐related variable to control for confounding. For longitudinal studies, two additional criteria were applied: (d) a minimum follow‐up duration of 6 months and (e) retention of at least 80% of the original sample at follow‐up. Two reviewers (J.C.M.‐C. and C.L.‐M.) independently assessed study quality (inter‐rater reliability, percent agreement: 99%) with any disagreements resolved by consensus in consultation with a third reviewer (R.D.‐P.‐C.).

We identified objective burden of care as the key confounding variable to be controlled for (Geng et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2023; Raina et al. 2005), but additionally considered the following variables across studies: (a) characteristics of the child or adolescent (e.g., level of functional ability, presence of behavioural problems or cognitive disorders) and (b) caregiving intensity (e.g., number of hours of care provided per day) (Aneshensel et al. 1995). A study was considered to have a low risk of confounding bias if it controlled for at least one of these variables in its design or analysis (Viswanathan et al. 2013) given the strong intercorrelation observed among objective burden measures (Pinquart and Sörensen 2003). When statistical adjustments were reported, we considered the risk of confounding bias to be minimal if the variation between the observed estimate before and after such adjustment was less than 10% (Rothman et al. 2008).

Certainty Assessment

To evaluate the robustness of the findings, we applied the Grading of Recommendations Assessment Development and Evaluation (GRADE) guidelines (Atkins et al. 2004) focusing on inconsistency, imprecision, and risk of publication bias. Inconsistency was assessed through the degree of heterogeneity observed across studies. For imprecision (Meader et al. 2014), we considered both the total number of studies included in each meta‐analysis—categorized as small (< 5), medium (5–10), or adequate (> 10)—and the mean sample size—classified as low (< 100 participants), intermediate (100–300 participants), or high (> 300 participants). Potential publication bias was examined through visual inspection of funnel plots and supported by statistical testing.

Analysis

We performed a meta‐analysis of correlation coefficients using a random‐effects model (Hedges and Vevea 1998) to account for variability across study populations, such as differences in gender, age, or cause of dependency. In addition, studies reporting alternative statistical metrics that could be converted into correlation coefficients were also included, following appropriate transformations to derive the necessary estimates.

Statistical heterogeneity was measured using Cochran's Q test (Cochran 1954); we estimated what percentage of between‐study variability was not due to chance using the degree of inconsistency (I 2). Publication bias was evaluated using Egger's test (Egger et al. 1997) when meta‐analysis included 10 or more studies (Borenstein 2019). Additionally, we examined funnel plots and executed the Trim and Fill method (Duval and Tweedie 2000) to estimate the effect size under the assumption of no publication bias.

We additionally carried out sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of the results (Cooper et al. 2019) by undertaking subgroup analyses to evaluate whether there were differences due to: (a) study design (cross‐sectional or longitudinal), (b) cause of dependency (e.g., diabetes, cancer, autism, etc.), (c) income of the country of residence (high, upper‐middle, lower‐middle or low income) according to World Bank (2024), and (d) methodological quality, specifically controlling for confounding, selection and classification biases. Effect sizes were interpreted as negligible if below 0.09, small if between 0.10 and 0.29, moderate if 0.30 to 0.49, and large if above 0.50 (Cohen 1988). However, these thresholds were guidelines and not absolute benchmarks, and we interpreted effect sizes in clinical and contextual terms, particularly in light of the cumulative impact of small effects in caregiving research (Pearlin et al. 1990). All analyses were conducted using Comprehensive Meta‐analysis Software 3.3.070 (Biostat Inc.).

Results

We retrieved 1987 records, excluding 296 duplicates, leaving 1701 for review. After excluding 316 irrelevant articles, 1345 did not meet inclusion criteria, with one study being redundant, leaving 28 studies meeting inclusion criteria. We identified 10 studies by additional records which increased our total of included studies to 38 (see Figure 1) (References of these studies are available in Appendix S2).

Flow diagram.

Table 1 summarizes the features of the 38 studies included, reporting on 42 independent samples with 4548 participants. All studies were cross‐sectional in design. The main family carer was mostly the mother, followed by fathers or other family members (i.e., grandparents). Four studies did not specify the caregiving relationship (Joshi et al. 2025; Kohlsdorf and Costa‐Junior 2011; Nadi Sakhvidi et al. 2024; Ulusoy et al. 2021). The causes of dependency were autism spectrum disorder (eight samples), cancer (six samples), Attention‐Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), cerebral palsy and developmental disorders, with three samples each, diabetes mellitus type 1 and rare or chronic diseases, with two samples each, and intellectual disability, epilepsy, bone fractures, Familial Mediterranean Fever, special needs, Joubert Syndrome, Down Syndrome, Asperger's Syndrome, cystic fibrosis and chronic pain with one sample each.

Functional coping showed a statistically significant negative association with depressive symptoms (combined correlation coefficient [r] = −0.213; 95% confidence interval [95% CI] [−0.354, −0.062]; N = 631 from eight samples; see Figure 2), with low heterogeneity across studies and a low risk of publication bias. However, this effect was not robust in sensitivity analyses. In contrast, neither problem‐focused coping nor social support seeking was significantly related to depressive symptoms.

Forest plot—depression and coping dimensions.

Among individual problem‐focused coping and social support seeking strategies, only planning was statistically associated with fewer depressive symptoms (r = −0.193; CI (95%) = [−0.347, −0.028]; N = 730 from nine samples). This association was consistent across all studies, but sensitive in the analysis of eliminating one study at a time. Subgroup analyses suggested stronger effects in upper‐middle‐income countries (r = −0.367; CI (95%) = [−0.474, −0.249]; two samples) than in high‐income countries (r = −0.035; CI (95%) = [−0.176, 0.107]; four samples). However, the number of samples was small, so these results should be interpreted cautiously. The other individual problem‐focused and support‐seeking strategies were not significantly associated with depressive symptoms (Figure S1 in Appendix S5).

We identified a significant negative association between emotion‐focused coping and depressive symptoms (r = −0.276; CI (95%) = [−0.486, −0.037]; N = 606 from nine samples; see Figure 2) with no statistical heterogeneity across studies, but with unstable results after sensitivity analysis and potential publication bias. Subgroup analyses indicated stronger associations among family carers of children with cancer (r = −0.633; CI (95%) = [−0.685, −0.576]; two samples) compared with those with autism spectrum disorder (r = −0.300; CI (95%) = [−0.448, −0.136]; two samples). We also found differences between family carers living in high income (r = −0.402; CI (95%) = [−0.583, −0.183]; six samples) and upper‐middle income (r = 0.189; CI (95%) = [−0.090, 0.441]; two samples) countries. However, subgroup analyses included a low number of samples, limiting the results.

Within this dimension (see Figure S1 in Appendix S5), acceptance (r = −0.180; CI (95%) = [−0.313, −0.041]; N = 644 from eight samples), humour (r = −0.164; CI (95%) = [−0.254, −0.071]; N = 450 from six samples), and positive reappraisal (r = −0.291; CI (95%) = [−0.435, −0.133]; N = 957 from 11 samples) were each significantly associated with fewer depressive symptoms, with no or low heterogeneity, although these effects showed instability in sensitivity analyses. Analyses of publication bias revealed a higher risk of bias for positive reappraisal and a lower risk for acceptance and humour.

Higher use of dysfunctional coping was significantly and robustly associated with increased levels of symptoms of depression (r = 0.441; CI (95%) = [0.383, 0.496]; N = 2090 from 20 samples; see Figure 2), with no evidence of statistical heterogeneity or publication bias. This association was consistent across caregiving contexts (autism spectrum disorder, ADHD and cancer).

Most individual dysfunctional coping strategies were positively and significantly associated with depressive symptoms (Figure S2 in Appendix S5): self‐distraction (r = 0.228; CI (95%) = [0.116, 0.334]; N = 297 from three samples), behavioural disengagement (r = 0.306; CI (95%) = [0.127, 0.466]; N = 515 from seven samples), self‐blame (r = 0.454; CI (95%) = [0.365, 0.535]; N = 346 from four samples), venting (r = 0.307; CI (95%) = [0.134, 0.462]; N = 557 from eight samples), and avoidance (r = 0.440; CI (95%) = [0.343, 0.528]; N = 302 from three samples).

Anxiety Symptoms and Coping

Table 3 summarizes all analyses examining the association between different ways of coping and anxiety symptoms. As with depressive symptoms, only the most relevant findings are presented below due to the extensive number of comparisons. Funnel plots can be found in Appendix S5.

Overall, most functional coping dimensions were not significantly associated with anxiety symptoms (see Figure 3). Specifically, no statistically significant associations were observed for functional coping (r = −0.081; CI (95%) = [−0.308, 0.154]; N = 361 from five samples), problem‐focused coping (r = 0.041; CI (95%) = [−0.120, 0.201]; N = 504 from five samples), emotion‐focused coping (r = −0.056; CI (95%) = [−0.139, 0.029]; N = 556 from five samples) and social support seeking (r = 0.002; CI (95%) = [−0.126, 0.130]; N = 296 from three samples).

Forest plot—anxiety and coping dimensions.

Among the individual strategies classified within functional dimensions, only acceptance and positive reappraisal showed significant associations with lower anxiety symptoms (Figure S3 in Appendix S5). Acceptance presented a negative correlation (r = −0.192; CI (95%) = [−0.337, −0.039]; N = 512 from seven samples), with no statistical heterogeneity detected across studies. Similarly, positive reappraisal was associated with fewer anxiety symptoms (r = −0.150; CI (95%) = [−0.276, −0.018]; N = 512 from seven samples), with low heterogeneity. However, both results were weak in the sensitivity analyses and indicated a risk of publication bias, as reflected by asymmetric funnel plots.

In contrast, dysfunctional coping emerged as the only coping dimension consistently associated with higher anxiety symptoms. The association was moderate to strong (r = 0.467; CI (95%) = [0.376, 0.549]; N = 765 from nine samples; see Figure 3), highly robust, and homogeneous across studies, with no evidence of publication bias. Subgroup analyses revealed comparable effect sizes among carers of children with cancer (r = 0.535; CI (95%) = [0.241, 0.739]; two samples) and those caring for children with autism spectrum disorder (r = 0.426; CI (95%) = [0.290, 0.545]; three samples), indicating that this association was consistent across caregiving contexts.

Among the different dysfunctional coping strategies examined (Figure S3 in Appendix S5), venting and behavioural disengagement were significantly associated with higher anxiety symptoms. Venting showed a moderate positive association (r = 0.373; CI (95%) = [0.135, 0.571]; N = 365 from six samples). Behavioural disengagement was also positively associated with anxiety (r = 0.212; CI (95%) = [0.026, 0.384]; N = 323 from five samples). Absence or low heterogeneity was found across studies in both results, with a lack of robustness and risk of publication bias. No significant associations were found for substance use, mental disengagement or denial.

Subjective Caregiver Burden and Coping

Table 3 presents all analyses conducted to explore the relationship between coping and subjective caregiver burden. Key findings are summarized below. Funnel plots are presented in Appendix S4.

The dimension of functional coping was significantly associated with lower subjective caregiver burden (r = −0.206; CI (95%) = [−0.319, −0.087]; N = 449 from three samples; see Figure 4), with no statistical heterogeneity across studies. However, the results showed limited robustness and indications of potential publication bias, which substantially reduced the estimated effect size after adjustment using the Trim and Fill method.

Forest plot—burden and coping.

In contrast, problem‐focused coping was positively associated with subjective caregiver burden (r = 0.128; CI (95%) = [0.023, 0.230]; N = 355 from three samples; see Figure 4). This association was consistent across studies, relatively robust and showed no evidence of publication bias. Importantly, analyses at the strategy level revealed a more nuanced pattern within this dimension. Planning was significantly associated with lower subjective caregiver burden (r = −0.282; CI (95%) = [−0.398, −0.158]; N = 408 from four samples; see Figure 4), with no heterogeneity but some indications of publication bias. Similarly, active coping as an individual strategy showed a strong inverse association with subjective caregiver burden (r = −0.438; CI (95%) = [−0.575, −0.278]; N = 153 from two samples; see Figure 4), although these findings were based on few samples.

The overall emotion‐focused coping dimension was not statistically related to subjective caregiver burden (r = 0.064; CI (95%) = [−0.107, 0.232]; N = 255 from two samples). Nevertheless, two individual strategies within this dimension were associated with lower subjective caregiver burden (Figure 4). Positive reappraisal showed a moderate negative correlation (r = −0.390; CI (95%) [−0.526, −0.234]; N = 408 from four samples) with minimal heterogeneity across studies and a possible risk of publication bias. Likewise, emotional support seeking was associated with reduced subjective caregiver burden (r = −0.387; CI (95%) [−0.618, −0.189]; N = 266 from two samples), although these results were based on a small number of studies.

Finally, dysfunctional coping was consistently associated with greater levels of subjective caregiver burden (r = 0.246; CI (95%) = [0.178, 0.312]; N = 769 from six samples; see Figure 4). This association was robust, homogeneous across studies, and only minimally affected by potential publication bias after adjustment by the Trim and Fill method.

Discussion

This systematic review with meta‐analysis offers novel evidence by quantitatively synthesizing the associations between various coping dimensions and mental health outcomes in family carers of dependent children and adolescents. To our knowledge, no previous reviews have comprehensively examined these relationships in paediatric settings, considering different dependency situations and a range of clinical conditions. Our review was also representative of all family carers of children and adolescents with disabilities, although most studies included parents and specifically mothers, which is consistent with the scientific literature (Spurr et al. 2023).

In general terms, the results show that coping strategies are differentially related to mental health and subjective burden in this population. Notably, the patterns observed are very similar to those described in previous meta‐analyses of family carers of dependent adults and older adults, suggesting a certain stability of these associations beyond the age of the care recipient (Muñoz‐Cruz et al. 2023a, 2024). This consistency reinforces the idea that coping is a key component of the family caregiving experience in different life contexts.

With regard to publication bias analyses, in several cases (see Figure 3), visual inspection of funnel plots suggested some grade of asymmetry. However, Egger's tests were not significant, and application of the Trim and Fill method did not result in significant changes in the combined effect estimates. This pattern is not uncommon in meta‐analyses based on a small number of studies and may reflect the effects of small studies, between‐study heterogeneity or chance, rather than true publication bias. In accordance with current methodological guidelines, their results should be interpreted with caution (Afonso et al. 2024).

Problem‐Focused Coping

Problem‐focused coping results showed a heterogeneous pattern. At the dimensional level, an association with higher levels of subjective caregiver burden was observed, while no clear relationships with symptoms of anxiety or depression were identified. These findings are consistent with previous reviews of family carers of adults and older people, where associations between problem‐focused coping and psychological distress have been inconsistent (Muñoz‐Cruz et al. 2023a, 2023b, 2024).

From a theoretical perspective, this can be explained by the degree of perceived control over stressors. According to the transactional model of stress and coping (Lazarus and Folkman 1984), problem‐focused strategies are most effective when the situation can be modified. In paediatric contexts with chronic and uncontrollable demands, constant attempts to solve problems may increase the perception of burden without translating into emotional improvements.

In addition, the small number of studies included in our meta‐analysis examining the relationship between problem‐focused coping and subjective caregiver burden may be limited, with many of the studies not controlling for confounding bias through objective burden variables or levels of perceived social support (Del‐Pino‐Casado et al. 2018). This may be key, as studies (Pushpa P et al. 2021; Suresh et al. 2019) that examined the association between this form of coping and caregiving burden (objective and subjective burden) did find a statistical association of a negative direction; so objective burden variables—such as functional capacity of the child or adolescent or the number of hours of daily care provided—may have influenced our results on subjective caregiver burden if not adequately controlled for. Thus, it is possible that our results are inconclusive due to a possible confounding bias and the small number of studies conducted to date.

On the other hand, when analysing specific strategies within this dimension, such as planning and active coping (strategy), associations with lower levels of subjective burden were observed. This indicates that not all problem‐focused strategies operate in the same way, which may reflect underlying mediators or moderators. For example, self‐efficacy (Ramírez‐Ochoa and Orellana‐Yáñez 2018), which has been positively associated with problem‐focused coping and better health outcomes in this population (Lu et al. 2022), may mediate, alongside proactive coping, the relationship between caregiver burden and psychological distress (Wang et al. 2024). Therefore, family carers with higher self‐efficacy seem to develop more effective problem‐focused coping strategies, buffering the emotional impact of caregiving (Wang et al. 2024).

Our results highlight the need to study individual coping strategies and not only broad dimensions of coping (Muñoz‐Cruz et al. 2023a). Consequently, it is important to identify which coping strategies are most effective for different stressors and care situations (Muñoz‐Cruz et al. 2023b). Future research should focus on these areas to develop interventions tailored to the diverse needs of family carers.

Emotion‐Focused Coping

Employing emotion‐focused coping was accompanied by lower symptoms of depression in family carers of dependent children and adolescents. However, the absence of a statistically significant association between this form of coping with symptoms of anxiety and subjective burden in our study could be due to the small number of studies included in our meta‐analyses, leading to imprecise results and possibly low statistical power.

Similar to carers of adult relatives and older adults (Muñoz‐Cruz et al. 2023a, 2023b, 2024), several of the individual emotion‐focused coping strategies, such as acceptance of the stressful situation and positive reappraisal, were associated with fewer symptoms of depression, anxiety, and subjective burden. Although the observed effects were modest, their interpretation should be placed within the context of family caregiving, characterized by prolonged and cumulative stress. Even small or moderate associations can have clinical relevance if they persist over time (Pearlin et al. 1990).

Therefore, positive reappraisal may help parents adapt to stressful situations, potentially mediating the relationship between parental stress and quality of life (Ni'matuzahroh et al. 2021). Acceptance, by enabling individuals to adjust to persistent and unchangeable circumstances, may reduce psychological struggle, which may explain its association with lower emotional distress in family carers of dependent children and adolescents (Nakamura and Orth 2005).

We also found that humour and seeking emotional support were strategies significantly related to fewer depressive symptoms and subjective burden, respectively. Therefore, emotion‐focused coping might not only be effective when the stressor is considered uncontrollable (Lazarus and Folkman 1984) but could also be adaptive in regulating emotions arising from a controllable stressor. This may explain why, when combined with problem‐focused coping (functional coping dimension), depressive symptoms and subjective caregiver burden tend to decrease (Muñoz‐Cruz et al. 2023a, 2024).

Our review contributes significantly to the existing literature by offering additional evidence on the influence of emotion‐focused coping on the mental health of family members caring for dependent children and adolescents. Although some beneficial effects of coping strategies like positive reappraisal and acceptance have already been documented (Muñoz‐Cruz et al. 2023a, 2023b, 2024), our meta‐analysis reinforces these findings in this specific group of carers.

Dysfunctional Coping

Dysfunctional coping showed positive and consistent associations with symptoms of anxiety, depression and subjective caregiver burden. These results are consistent with previous reviews describing how the predominant use of avoidance or denial strategies is linked to higher levels of psychological distress and impaired well‐being (Muñoz‐Cruz et al. 2023a, 2023b, 2024). In our study, the associations were moderate for anxiety and depression, suggesting that dysfunctional coping can be considered a relevant indicator of psychological vulnerability in family carers of both children and adults, increasing the risk of a poor quality of life (Fairfax et al. 2019).

From a theoretical point of view, these findings fit with the transactional model of Lazarus and Folkman (1984), which suggests that adopting dysfunctional strategies rather than confronting problems directly may limit family carers' ability to seek support or effective solutions to caregiving difficulties, which can further perpetuate distress (Cheng and Chong 2024).

Differences According to Socioeconomic Context

Subgroups analyses indicated that associations between coping and mental health varied according to country income level. Emotion‐focused coping was more strongly related to better outcomes in high‐income countries, while planning showed a clearer relationship with lower subjective burden in upper‐middle‐income countries. These findings, although they should be interpreted with caution due to the limited number of studies, are consistent with ecological models that emphasize how access to resources and support networks modulates the perceived effectiveness of coping strategies (He et al. 2024; Semrau et al. 2016).

Limitations

Although our review presents important findings, there are nevertheless several limitations. First, we cannot offer conclusions on causality due to the cross‐sectional design of all the studies included in our review. Most studies to date used non‐probabilistic sampling, which means we are not able to extrapolate these results to larger populations. Secondly, the small number of studies in several of our meta‐analyses may give less reliable results; this may have affected the reliability of the analyses applied to evaluate publication bias. Thirdly, most of the subgroup analyses were based on a small number of studies, which limits the statistical power and stability of the results. Finally, few studies controlled for confounding bias using objective burden variables, and we were unable to use adjusted estimators or undertake subgroup analyses between studies that did or did not control for this bias.

Conclusion

Dysfunctional coping is strongly linked to negative emotional consequences in family members caring for dependent children and adolescents, whereas adopting emotion‐focused coping strategies may produce better mental health outcomes. Our findings indicate that interventions that discourage dysfunctional coping and facilitate emotion‐focused strategies should be part of care plans and supportive interventions for carers. Early detection of dysfunctional coping is important, as it is linked to higher anxiety and depression. Health professionals should assess coping strategies systematically and implement tailored interventions to enhance adaptive mechanisms. On the other hand, our results for problem‐focused coping and carer outcomes remain inconclusive and more heterogeneous. However, given the cross‐sectional nature of our results, we cannot conclude causality. Future studies using longitudinal designs and adequately controlling for confounding variables are urgently needed. Additionally, improving access to support resources is essential to protect carers' mental health and reduce healthcare system burdens.

Implications for Clinical Practice

Our cross‐sectional results reinforce the importance of early detection of the use of dysfunctional coping strategies among family carers, as these patterns are consistently linked to increased levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms. In the assessment phase, health professionals, especially nurses, should systematically assess carers' coping mechanisms at the same time as their mental health is assessed.

In terms of interventions that mental health nurses and other health professionals should undertake with this population, our review suggests that promoting emotion‐focused coping strategies (e.g., acceptance and positive reappraisal) and reducing dysfunctional behaviours, such as avoidance or substance use, could be associated with improved mental health among family carers. Psychological support should emphasize the improvement of coping skills via psychoeducation or cognitive behavioural therapy (Koumarianou et al. 2021). Furthermore, since coping effectiveness appears to differ according to accessibility to healthcare resources, interventions should be adapted to the distinct needs and characteristics of different groups of carers.

Regarding implications for future research, although this review provides a length of evidence on how coping strategies relate to psychological outcomes among family carers, important research questions remain. First, longitudinal research is required to better understand the influence of coping strategies on mental health over time. Secondly, qualitative studies could help to explore more broadly the subjective experience of carers in relation to their coping responses. Lastly, due to the mixed results reported for problem‐focused coping, future studies should investigate potential factors that influence its effectiveness, such as self‐efficacy, social support, or objective caregiver burden (Pushpa P et al. 2021; Ramírez‐Ochoa and Orellana‐Yáñez 2018; Suresh et al. 2019). By understanding how these variables interact, interventions could be designed in a more personalized way allowing for a better approach to the challenges faced by carers.

Finally, policy makers should develop and implement strategies to improve access to resources and support for family carers, particularly in helping them manage stress, with the aim of preventing psychological distress related to the demands of caregiving. This would not only contribute to improving carer well‐being, but would also help to prevent already stretched health and social care systems.

Relevance Statement

Family carers of dependent children and adolescents are often psychologically distressed. The present systematic review with meta‐analysis is pioneering in quantifying how different coping strategies relate to subjective burden, anxiety and depression symptoms in this population. Dysfunctional coping correlates with poorer mental health, while emotion‐focused strategies like acceptance and positive reappraisal are associated with better outcomes. These findings are relevant for mental health nurses, who support the early identification of harmful coping and the promotion of adaptive responses. Results have implications for intervention design, personalized support and health care planning in the context of increasing paediatric chronic and developmental illnesses.
