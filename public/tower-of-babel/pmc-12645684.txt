Relationship between dog erythrocyte antigen (DEA) and susceptibility of dogs to babesiosis
Authors: Olufunke Omowunmi Adebayo, Michael Irewole Takeet, Ibironke Kofoworola Oyewusi, Adenrele Olalekan Adebayo, Remo G Lobetti, Ebenezer Babatunde Otesile
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCanine Babesiosis causes a fatal disease marked by haemolytic anaemia. Of the identified dog blood groups, some have been found to affect the host's susceptibility/resistance to certain infections. However, limited information exists on the influence of canine blood types on tolerance of dogs to babesiosis. This study assessed the prevalence of different Dog Erythrocyte Antigen (DEA) 1 blood group in Abeokuta, Nigeria, and evaluated potential relationship between DEA 1 blood types and susceptibility to babesiosis. 200 blood samples (1 mL each) were collected from client-owned dogs. Alvedia® test kit was used to identify DEA 1.1, DEA 1.2 and DEA 1.0 genotypes. Deoxyribose nucleic acid (DNA) was extracted from each sample, and Babesia species detected using Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR). Data were analyzed for relationship between blood types and Babesia infection using Chi-square. DEA 1 positive dogs accounted for 63.5%, a significantly higher proportion than DEA 1 negative dogs (36.5%) in the study population. Boerboel, Rottweiler, Caucasian, and local breeds showed significantly higher frequency (73.3% to 86.4%) of DEA 1 positive blood types compared to DEA 1 negative types (13.6% to 26.7%). In contrast, German shepherd breed showed no significant difference in frequency of DEA positive (51.2%) and DEA negative (48.8%) dogs. Additionally, no significant sex-based differences were observed in DEA 1 blood type distribution. The prevalence of Babesia species in DEA 1 positive dogs (63.0%) was not significantly different (p > 0.05) from that in DEA 1 negative dogs (60.3%). These findings suggested that the DEA 1 blood group may not influence the tolerance or susceptibility of dogs to Babesia infection.Keywords: Dog, Babesiosis, Blood group, Dog Erythrocyte Antigen

Introduction

Economically, babesiosis stands among the most significant tick-borne infections, impacting both domestic and wild animals as well as humans, particularly in humid and subtropical regions worldwide [1, 21]. Globally, it is recognized as one of the major life-threatening piroplasmoses of dogs, primarily caused by Babesia canis and B. gibsoni, and typically characterized by haemolytic anaemia among other clinical signs [16]. According to Koster et al. [11], anaemia in canine babesiosis serves as the basis for classifying the disease into two forms: uncomplicated, where clinical symptoms are solely due to anaemia, and complicated, where anaemia is accompanied by dysfunction of other organs.

The pathophysiology and clinical manifestations of malaria, a human disease caused by Plasmodium falciparum, an intraerythrocytic haemoparasite similar to Babesia species, have been reported to share several similarities with those of canine babesiosis [4, 12]. Studies have also shown that the human ABO blood group influences the clinical outcome of Plasmodium infections, with individuals of blood group ‘O’ exhibiting a protective advantage against P. falciparum malaria. This protection is linked to a reduction in rosetting activity associated with blood type ‘O’ [15, 22]. Considering the parallels in the pathogenesis and clinical profiles of human malaria and canine babesiosis [4, 12], it becomes pertinent to explore whether canine blood groups similarly influence the clinical outcomes of infections caused by various Babesia species.

A better understanding of the different blood types across animal species is essential, as transfusing incompatible blood can cause severe to fatal transfusion reactions [19]. Conducting blood typing and compatibility testing prior to transfusion significantly reduces the risk of adverse reactions caused by blood incompatibility. Consequently, knowledge of breed-specific blood types can support effective selection of suitable blood donors when needed.

The Dog Erythrocyte Antigen (DEA) system is recognized as the most clinically significant [8, 9] because the DEA 1.1 antibodies are capable of causing acute haemolytic, potentially life-threatening transfusion reactions. Dogs are rapidly sensitized by the first incompatible transfusion [5] but 8 DEAs (DEA 1.1, DEA 1.2, DEA 3, DEA 4, DEA 5, DEA 6, DEA 7 and DEA 8) have been described [2].

In Nigeria, research on canine blood group systems remains scarce, with only limited work addressing Dog Erythrocyte Antigens (DEA). The foundational study by Nottidge et al. [14] provided baseline data on DEA distribution among indigenous Nigerian dogs. However, subsequent studies have not expanded on these findings, leaving a substantial knowledge gap regarding the role of DEA in disease susceptibility, especially canine babesiosis that is endemic in Nigeria and constitutes a major cause of morbidity and mortality among dogs. Despite the disease’s high prevalence and clinical importance, no published studies have examined the possible relationship between DEA types and susceptibility or resistance to Babesia infection in Nigerian dog populations. Hence, this study investigated whether specific DEA phenotypes influence the prevalence or clinical outcomes of canine babesiosis in Nigeria.

Materials and methods

Studied animals and sample collection

A cross-sectional study was conducted on dogs presented for treatment at two government owned veterinary hospitals and one private veterinary clinic in Abeokuta, Ogun State, and a private veterinary clinic in Lagos, Lagos State, Nigeria. Two hundred client-owned dogs with history and clinical signs suggestive of canine babesiosis such as fever, pale mucous membranes, tick infestation, lymph node or splenic enlargement, jaundice, and anorexia were included. Dogs that have been treated with antibiotics or antiprotozoal drugs two weeks prior to presentation were excluded. For each blood sample, the name, age, sex and breed of the dog were recorded.

One millilitre of venous blood was collected from the cephalic or jugular vein of each subject into anti-coagulated disodium ethylene diamine tetra-acetic acid (EDTA) bottles for the determination of the DEA status and the extraction of genomic DNA from whole blood.

Determination of dog erythrocyte antigen type

The Dog Erythrocyte Antigen 1 (DEA 1) status of each dog was determined using an immunochromatographic assay kit, Lab TEST DEA 1 Alvedia® (Alvedia Veterinary Diagnostics, France), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 10 μL of fresh anticoagulated whole blood was placed in the sample well of the test cassette using a calibrated micropipette. Two to three drops (approximately 200 μL) of the supplied dilution buffer were then added to the same well to facilitate migration of the sample across the test strip. The cassette was placed on a flat surface at room temperature and allowed to develop for 2 min. After this period, the presence of red bands on the membrane was visually examined. The appearance of a control band indicated that the test was valid, while the presence or absence of a test band indicated the DEA 1 genotype. Dogs were classified as DEA 1-positive when both the control and test bands were visible, and as DEA 1-negative when only the control band was visible. Invalid tests (absence of the control band) were repeated using a fresh cassette and blood sample.

The membrane was then placed on the result form to interpret the result. In positive cases, the DEA 1 line showed different intensity: weak, or strong. Specifically, a dog with an intense red line in front of DEA 1 arrow on the membrane is regarded as strongly positive and designated as DEA 1.1. A sample with a weak or faint red line in front of DEA 1 arrow is regarded as weakly positive and classified as DEA 1.2 positive dog. A blood sample with the absence of the red line in front of DEA 1 arrow is classified as a DEA negative dog.

Parasitological examination by the polymerase chain reaction

Deoxyribose nucleic acid amplification

Genomic DNA was extracted from 100 μl whole blood using Quick-gDNA™ Mini-Prep Plus kit (Zymo Research Corporation, Irvine, CA, USA). Three mililitres aliquots of blood samples (frozen at −20 °C) were allowed to stand at room temperature until thawed. The procedure used for the DNA purification was according to the instruction manual of the test kit. Approximately 400 µl of Genomic lysis buffer was added to 100 µl of blood (ratio of 4:1). The mixture was vortexed using a vortex mixer SA8 (Stuart, UK) for six seconds and allowed to stand for 10 min.

The mixture was transferred to a Zymo-Spin™ IIC column (Zymo Research Quick-DNA™ Miniprep kit) in a collection tube and then centrifuged using the spectrafuge 24D centrifuge (Labnet International, USA) at 10,000 x g for one minute. Following the protocols, the eluted DNA was obtained and then stored in the freezer at −20 °C for 24–48 h before use.

The PCR reaction was performed in a thermocycler (BioRad My Cycler®, USA) with a final volume of 25 μl containing equivalent of 20 ng of genomic DNA, 12.5 μl of 2 × PCR Master Mix (Bioneer®), 7.5 μl nuclease free water and 0.5 μl (40 ng) each of the forward and reverse primer sets. Amplification conditions using PIRO-A and B primers set were: 94 °C for 5 min, 35 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, 57.8 °C for 1 min and 72 °C for 1 min, with final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

The DNA of a known positive blood sample for Babesia species from a naturally infected dog obtained from the Veterinary Teaching Hospital, Federal University of Agriculture, Abeokuta and nuclease-free water were used as positive and negative controls, respectively.

Ten microliters of the PCR products were electrophoresed through 1% agarose gel stained with Ethydium bromide in 1 × Tris–Acetate EDTA (TAE) buffer and visualized on UV transiluminator (Spectroline®, USA).

Samples with the same band size as that of the positive control (400 bp) were regarded as positive for Babesia species.

Data analysis

The prevalence of the different Babesia subspecies was expressed as proportion of the total sample at 95% confidence interval (CI). The chi-square (χ2) test was used to analyze the association between the Dog Erythrocyte Antigen 1 (DEA 1) status of the sampled dogs and the prevalence of babesiosis. This test compared the observed frequencies of DEA 1-positive and DEA 1-negative dogs among cases with clinical babesiosis against the expected frequencies, in order to determine whether any observed differences were statistically significant. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of a significant association. All statistical analyses were done using SPSS 20.0 (IBM Statistics).

Results

DNA amplification result

The electrophoresed PCR products of the amplified DNA revealed band size of 400 bp, the expected band size for Babesia species. This was obtained from 124 (62%) of the blood samples.

Prevalence of DEA 1 Types in the study population

The DEA ALVEDIA laboratory test employed in this study revealed that of the 200 dogs studied, 70 (35%) had DEA 1.1, 57 (28.5%) had DEA 1.2 and 73 (36.5%) were DEA negative (Table 1). Both positive DEA (1.1 and 1.2) types constituted 63.5% of all the dogs studied and were significantly (p < 0.05) higher in frequency than that for the DEA negative dogs.

Relationship between Sex of Dog and DEA 1 genotypes

In the overall dog population studied, there was no significant (p > 0.05) difference between the proportion of the combined DEA positive types (DEA 1.1 and 1.2) in all male dogs (64.3%) and the females (62.4%)(Table 2). Conversely, females had a significantly (p < 0.05) higher proportion of the weak positive DEA 1.2 (34.1%) than male dogs (24.3%).

Relationship between DEA types and prevalence of Babesia

The prevalence of Babesia species within the blood types were as follows; DEA 1.1 (58.6%), DEA 1.2 (68.4%) and DEA 1 negative (60.3%). The prevalence of Babesia species for combined DEA 1 positive types (63.0%) was not significantly (p > 0.05) different from that for DEA negative blood type (60.3%). Thus overall, the DEA blood group did not seem to significantly influence the susceptibility of the dogs studied to babesiosis.

Discussion

A clear understanding of how DEA types influence dogs' susceptibility to Babesia infection is essential for enhancing disease risk assessment, guiding safe blood transfusion practices, and informing selective breeding strategies. It can also provide valuable insight into host–pathogen interactions that drive disease dynamics. Therefore, this study explored the impact of DEA types on the prevalence of babesial infection in dogs.

The high prevalence of Babesia species observed in this study is notably higher and does not align with the findings of Takeet et al. [18], who also employed molecular techniques to assess Babesia infections in naturally infected dogs in Ogun State, Nigeria. This discrepancy in detection rates may be attributed to differences in sampling methods—while Takeet et al. collected samples randomly from the general dog population presented for routine check-up, the present study focused on dogs presented for clinical evaluation and treatment at the Veterinary Teaching Hospital and veterinary clinics. This underscores the importance of using sensitive diagnostic techniques for accurate detection and evaluation of canine babesiosis.

Differences in the prevalence of DEA blood types among different breeds of dogs in different geographical locations has been reported [13]. In this study, the frequencies of the three DEA genotypes, DEA 1.1, DEA 1.2, and DEA 1-negative, did not differ significantly, likely reflecting the diverse breed composition of the sampled dogs. However, when the two positive DEA types (1.1 and 1.2) were grouped together, their combined frequency was significantly higher than that of DEA-negative dog. Spada et al. [17] observed that the prevalence of DEA 1 has been estimated at about 50% overall internationally and that Corso dogs have signfcantly higher DEA 1-negative prevalence than most canine populations earlier studied.

The most frequently represented breed in this study was the German Shepherd Dog (GSD). Previous research in South Africa by van der Merwe et al. [20] reported notable breed-related variations in DEA 1 blood group frequencies. In contrast, GSDs in this study showed nearly equal frequencies of DEA 1-positive (51.2%) and DEA 1-negative (48.8%) types. This differs from both the South African study and a report from Portugal by Ferreira et al. [10], which found a 100% DEA-negative rate among GSDs. Notably, most GSDs in this and the South African study were hospital cases without verified pedigree, suggesting that such discrepancies may be attributed to variations in breed purity. Meanwhile, the high prevalence of DEA 1-positive types observed among Boerboels, Caucasians, and Rottweilers (ranging from 73.3% to 86.4%) in this study aligns with findings from South Africa [20], Portugal [10], and California, USA [3]. In a previous study in Ibadan, Nigeria, Nottidge et al. [14] recorded a prevalence of 39.9% DEA 1-positive amongst the local breed which contrasts the 81.3% reported for the same breed in this study.

There was no significant difference between the proportion of either DEA 1-positive or DEA 1-negative types between male and female dogs in this study suggesting that sex does not influence DEA 1 genotypes. Previous studies conducted in Brazil [7], Portugal [10] and California, USA [3] revealed similar results. However, a higher prevalence of DEA positive types was recorded in female dogs compared to males in a study in Zimbabwe [6].

In this study, the prevalence of Babesia species among DEA 1-positive dogs (63.0%) was not significantly different from that in DEA 1-negative dogs (60.3%). Similarly, Dhliwayo et al. [6] found no significant association between DEA 1 blood group and babesiosis. These findings suggest that the DEA 1 group may not influence susceptibility to Babesia infection. However, as a single-point screening for parasitaemia may not reliably predict the full course or outcome of an infection, further research is needed to determine whether this observation applies solely to Babesia infectivity or also extends to its pathogenicity in dogs.

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. The dogs sampled were restricted to those presented to selected veterinary hospitals and clinics in Abeokuta and Lagos, which may not reflect the broader dog population, particularly free-roaming or rural dogs that rarely receive veterinary care. The inclusion of only dogs with clinical signs suggestive of babesiosis may have excluded asymptomatic or subclinical carriers, thus possibly affecting the assessment of the true relationship between DEA status and susceptibility to infection.. Furthermore, dogs that had recently received antiprotozoal or antibiotic treatment were excluded, potentially biasing the sample toward acute, untreated cases. Being a cross-sectional study, the design could only demonstrate associations between DEA types and babesiosis at a single time point, without accounting for temporal or seasonal variations. Lastly, reliance on client-reported histories may have introduced some information bias. Despite these limitations, the study provides important preliminary insights into the possible role of DEA in susceptibility to canine babesiosis in Nigeria, and highlights the need for broader, multicenter, and longitudinal investigations.

In conclusion, this study provides valuable insight into the distribution of DEA blood types among various dog breeds presented to veterinary hospitals and their relationship with Babesia infection. While DEA 1-positive dogs were more prevalent in the studied dogs, no significant association was found between DEA type and Babesia infection rates. Breed and sex differences were evident, suggesting complex genetic influence. The findings emphasize the need for further genetic characterization of local breeds and more comprehensive studies on DEA-related disease susceptibility. Such research will enhance diagnostic accuracy, treatment strategies, and breed-specific health management in canine populations.
