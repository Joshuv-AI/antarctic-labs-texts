Stampede detection and crowd analysis using CNN-LSTM and farneback optical flow
Authors: Geetanjali Bhola, Sumit Srivastava, Hith Rahil Nidhan, Sanyam Kathed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stampede incidents in densely populated environments remain a critical challenge for public safety, resulting in severe casualties and significant disruption. This paper presents a novel, data-driven framework for automated stampede detection and crowd risk classification, leveraging the integration of Farneback optical-flow computation with a hybrid CNN-LSTM architecture. Two complementary datasets, UCSD Anomaly detection dataset and Agoraset dataset, were combined to capture a broad spectrum of crowd behaviors and densities. The proposed system classifies crowd states into four distinct risk levels: normal, moderate, dense, and risky, thereby offering a more granular assessment than traditional binary models. The model was trained and evaluated on a balanced dataset of 10,000 annotated frames, with rigorous preprocessing and augmentation to ensure robustness. Experimental results demonstrate an accuracy of 99.75%, further cross-dataset evaluation on the UMN benchmark assesses the robustness under domain shift conditions. While the approach shows strong potential for real-time deployment in public event management and emergency response, current limitations include computational latency and challenges in ultra-dense, occluded scenarios.

Introduction

The catastrophic nature of crowd disasters has emerged as one of the most pressing challenges in contemporary public safety management, with stampede incidents representing the most devastating manifestation of crowd-related emergencies1 Historical records document numerous tragic events where dense human gatherings have resulted in mass casualties, from the 1954 Kumbh Mela tragedy claiming over 400 lives to more recent incidents such as the 2013 Prayagraj crush and the 2021 Astroworld Festival disaster. Further, implementations have been tested to detect such incidents and anomalies using various methods2. These events underscore a fundamental reality: as global urbanization accelerates and mega-events proliferate, traditional crowd management methodologies prove increasingly inadequate to address the complex dynamics of large-scale human assemblies3,4 . The transition from reactive disaster response to proactive prevention has become imperative, driving the evolution of sophisticated technological solutions that can anticipate, detect, and mitigate potential stampede conditions before they escalate into catastrophic events5.

The technological landscape of stampede detection has undergone a remarkable transformation over the past decade and a half, evolving from rudimentary surveillance systems to sophisticated artificial intelligence-driven platforms capable of real-time crowd behavior analysis. This evolution reflects broader advancements in computer vision, machine learning, and sensor technologies, which have collectively enabled the development of systems that can process vast amounts of visual and sensor data to identify patterns indicative of dangerous crowd conditions4,6. The urgency of this technological advancement is particularly evident in the context of mega-events such as the Maha Kumbh Mela 2025, which anticipates over 400 million attendees, representing a scale of human gathering that demands unprecedented precision in crowd monitoring and management 1.Fig. 1Evolution of stampede detection technologies (2009-2025).

The fundamental challenge in stampede detection lies in the complex, nonlinear nature of crowd dynamics, where individual behaviors aggregate into collective phenomena that can rapidly transition from stable to chaotic states6,7. Understanding these dynamics requires sophisticated analytical frameworks that can capture both the physical and psychological aspects of crowd behavior, integrating insights from fluid dynamics, social psychology, and computational modeling8,9. The social force model10, first introduced by Helbing and colleagues, provided a foundational framework for understanding how individual pedestrian movements are influenced by repulsive and attractive forces from other individuals and environmental obstacles. This physics-based approach laid the groundwork for subsequent developments in computational crowd analysis, establishing the conceptual foundation upon which modern detection systems are built.

Evolution of stampede detection technologies

The technological evolution of stampede detection systems can be categorized into three distinct eras, each characterized by fundamental shifts in methodological approaches and computational capabilities2,6 . The Traditional Computer Vision Era (2009-2015) was dominated by classical image processing techniques, including optical flow analysis, background subtraction, and particle advection methods. These approaches, while computationally efficient, were limited by their reliance on handcrafted features and threshold-based detection mechanisms that struggled to adapt to the variability inherent in real-world scenarios1,8.

The Deep Learning Era (2016-2020) marked a paradigmatic shift toward data-driven approaches, with convolutional neural networks (CNNs)11 and recurrent architectures demonstrating superior performance in crowd density estimation and behavior classification tasks12,13. This period witnessed the emergence of sophisticated architectures capable of learning complex feature representations directly from raw video data, significantly improving detection accuracy while reducing the need for manual parameter tuning9,14. The integration of temporal modeling through Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks15 enabled systems to capture dynamic patterns in crowd behavior, moving beyond static density estimation to dynamic behavior prediction.

The current AI Integration Era (2021-2025) represents the convergence of multiple technological streams, including transformer architectures, edge computing, Internet of Things (IoT) sensors, and multimodal data fusion6,16. This era is characterized by the deployment of large-scale, real-world systems capable of processing multiple data streams simultaneously while maintaining real-time performance requirements. The integration of edge computing has enabled distributed processing architectures that can handle the computational demands of modern AI algorithms while addressing privacy and latency concerns17.

Previous studies report high accuracy for different datasets of isolated data, however, the requirements for operational stampede prevention include all of the following: (a) A graded risk output instead of binary anomaly flags; (b) Robustness to occlusion and lighting changes; (c) Inference strategies that can be deployed in near real-time on constrained hardware. The focus of this paper is to answer the following research questions: Can a multi-channel dense optical flow feature set combined with compact scalar motion descriptors improve the effectiveness of discrimination between the intermediate state of crowds compared to the use of single stream models?Will a hybrid CNN-LSTM network that fuses the per-frame spatial features and short term temporal features maintain a high level of detected performance while allowing for unimpeded inference latency?What is the total time required (optical flow extraction + feature extraction + model inference) on commercially available hardware, and are these times applicable to practical early-warning windows?These research questions form the basis for the future work regarding method, evaluation, and experiment design found later in this paper. A dense crowd with a dense occlusion may not be detected by traditional object detection methods, and motion-centric indicators (flow acceleration, flow divergence, flow entropy) provide a simple means of measuring the global dynamics of the crowd prior to experiencing a physical collapse. A rich combination of multi-channel dense optical flow, a lightweight CNN encoder, and short LSTM temporal context is designed to (i) take advantage of the spatial motion structure per-frame, (ii) utilize short-term temporal coherence without incurring the cost of tracking individuals, and (iii) facilitate batch processing to permit greater efficiency of model size and inference processing time. Literature review

The foundational work which introduced the application of social force models to abnormal crowd behavior detection, establishing a physics-based framework that remains influential in contemporary research10. Their approach utilized optical flow computation to estimate interaction forces between virtual particles representing crowd members, enabling the detection of abnormal behaviors through force flow analysis15. This seminal work demonstrated that crowd anomalies could be detected without explicit object tracking, avoiding the computational complexity associated with individual trajectory analysis in dense crowd scenarios15. Building upon these foundations, Cob-Parro et al. (2015) developed a threshold-free stampede detection system that combined optical flow entropy analysis with machine learning classifiers. Their methodology employed a stacking classifier approach, integrating Random Forest and Support Vector Classification (SVC) algorithms to achieve 88% real-time detection accuracy on the UMN and PETS-2009 datasets. The significance of this work lies in its elimination of environment-specific threshold requirements, enabling more generalizable deployment across diverse crowd scenarios1. The particle advection approach represented another significant advancement in holistic crowd analysis methodologies16. By treating crowd flow as a fluid dynamic system, their framework enabled the segmentation of high-density crowds based on coherent structures, providing insights into crowd instabilities without requiring individual tracking. This approach demonstrated the potential for borrowing concepts from fluid dynamics to understand crowd behavior at macroscopic scales.

The application of deep learning to crowd analysis marked a revolutionary advancement in detection capabilities, with CNN-based architectures demonstrating superior performance in density estimation and behavior classification tasks6,16. Rafi Haque et al. (2017) demonstrated that CNN-based crowd counting models outperformed traditional erosion methods by 34% in high-density scenarios, utilizing pixel analysis and head/torso detection for accurate population estimation. Their work established the superiority of learned feature representations over handcrafted descriptors in complex crowd environments. The introduction of transformer architectures to crowd analysis represented another significant breakthrough, with Sun et al. (2019) developing Deformable DETR systems specifically designed to address small target detection challenges in crowded environments. Their enhanced model achieved 34% improvement in small-target detection accuracy while maintaining real-time processing speeds through SwiGLU activation function optimization6. This work addressed a critical limitation in existing systems: the detection of individuals in heavily occluded scenarios typical of dense crowds 2.Fig. 2Performance comparison of stampede detection methods.

Performance comparison of stampede detection methods.

The integration of temporal modeling through LSTM networks has enabled more sophisticated behavior analysis capabilities, with Altowairqi et al. (2023) achieving 97.84% accuracy through combined Fully Convolutional Network (FCN) and LSTM architectures8. Their approach demonstrated the importance of temporal context in distinguishing normal from abnormal crowd behaviors, moving beyond static density analysis to dynamic pattern recognition. The significance of this work extends beyond accuracy improvements to include enhanced localization capabilities for suspicious activities.

Contemporary research increasingly emphasizes multimodal approaches that integrate diverse data sources to achieve comprehensive crowd monitoring capabilities6. Chang et al. (2022) developed a comprehensive risk prediction framework that combined Point-to-Point Networks (P2PNet), YOLO object detection, and DBSCAN clustering algorithms to classify crowd risk levels into distinct categories: Crowd Crush, Crowd Wave, and Crowd Collapse9. Their multi-algorithmic approach demonstrated the feasibility of real-time detection using ensemble methods, moving away from single-algorithm solutions toward integrated frameworks.

In their work, Cob-Parro and colleagues (2025)18 developed a novel framework using dense optical flow (DOF) as input to a deep learning algorithm to identify stampedes, achieving superior classification accuracy through utilizing pixel-level motion continuity as opposed to individual feature-based tracking. They also demonstrated that DOF representations hold significant potential to be used for tracking group movement behaviours, particularly in densely populated environments where it is difficult to track individual objects accurately. However, the focus of their study was primarily on binary abnormality detection and did not provide the ability to perform fine-grained risk stratification, as required for progressive intervention systems.

Altowairqi, et al. (2026)19 developed an architecture combining C3D and LSTMs, augmented by attention modules, to address the limitations of previous works on spatiotemporal anomaly detection (STAD). Their models were able to improve temporal representation learning by integrating volumetric convolutional networks with sequence modelling, providing strong results on publicly available benchmark datasets. However, the high computational requirements for implementing 3D convolutional and attention layer modules impose limitations on the scalability of these methods for large-scale or edge-based implementation under multi-camera observation environments. On the other hand, Altowairqi et al. (2024)20 proposed a method to track sparse features by using a neural network to classify those features with the help of hand-crafted feature descriptors such as SIFT, FAST and AKAZE and dimension reduction methods for Retinex. This method had high accuracy on the datasets that were evaluated, however, as the method relied on stable keypoints being extracted from images, it demonstrated a high level of sensitivity to occlusion and the variation of crowd density in real life environments that are susceptible to stampedes 3.Fig. 3Data sources distribution in modern stampede detection systems.

The emergence of edge computing solutions has addressed critical limitations related to latency and privacy in crowd monitoring systems17. These distributed architectures enable local processing of sensitive crowd data while maintaining real-time performance requirements, addressing growing concerns about privacy and data sovereignty in public surveillance applications.

Contemporary research trends and innovations

Recent developments in stampede detection research reflect increasing sophistication in both algorithmic approaches and real-world deployment strategies7. Ma et al. (2024) introduced multimodal large language models (MLLMs) for disaster assessment, demonstrating strong correlation with ground-truth seismic data while integrating image-text signals from social media sources21. This work represents a significant advancement in leveraging crowdsourced data for emergency response applications.

The development of attention mechanisms has significantly enhanced detection performance in challenging scenarios, with recent implementations of Convolutional Block Attention Modules (CBAM) in YOLO architectures achieving improved accuracy for middle and far-distance crowd recognition16. These advances address fundamental limitations in existing systems related to scale variation and occlusion handling.

The real-world deployment of AI-powered crowd management systems at the Maha Kumbh Mela 2025 represents a landmark achievement in large-scale stampede prevention technology7. The implementation utilized over 2,760 CCTV cameras with 500 AI-powered units capable of real-time crowd density analysis and predictive analytics. This deployment demonstrated the practical viability of computer vision-based crowd management at unprecedented scales, providing valuable insights for future implementations.

Challenges and limitations in current research

Despite significant technological advances, contemporary stampede detection systems continue to face fundamental challenges that limit their effectiveness and deployment scope17,22. Occlusion remains the most frequently cited limitation, with dense crowd scenarios creating significant challenges for individual detection and tracking algorithms6,22. This challenge is particularly acute in high-density gatherings where traditional computer vision approaches fail to maintain acceptable accuracy levels6.Fig. 4Key challenges in stampede detection research.

Computational complexity represents another significant barrier to widespread deployment, with advanced deep learning systems requiring substantial processing resources that may not be available in many deployment scenarios6,12. The tension between accuracy requirements and computational constraints continues to drive research toward more efficient architectures and edge computing solutions17.

Real-time processing requirements create additional constraints, particularly for systems that must analyze multiple video streams simultaneously while maintaining low latency for emergency response applications5,22. Many current systems struggle to balance processing depth with speed requirements, leading to compromises in either detection accuracy or response time5.

This research integrates two complementary crowd surveillance datasets, the UCSD Pedestrian Dataset23 and the Agoraset Dataset24 to establish a comprehensive analysis framework. The UCSD dataset contributes diverse real-world scenarios captured in academic environments, while Agoraset provides specialized high-density scenarios from complex public spaces. Both the datasets are standardized and have been implemented to academically evaluate crowd anomalies and analysis as well as stampede detection. The Agoraset dataset tries to mimic the realistic crowd behaviour and movement. A critical preprocessing transformation involved converting all RGB frames to grayscale using the luminosity method (Y = 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B). This optical flow dataset is the primary input to the model for subsequent feature extraction and temporal modeling. To ensure balanced representation across crowd density states, the combined dataset of 10,000 frames was manually annotated by according to a four-tier classification system. The four classes are: normal (low density, free movement), moderate (medium density, slight congestion), dense (high density, restricted movement), and risky (critical density, potential stampede conditions). Each classification tier was populated with roughly 2,500 individual frames, systematically aggregated from multiple distinct spatial scenarios. The final preprocessed dataset incorporated the optical flow sequences, organized into 16-frame temporal segments. To evaluate the model with new datasets, in addition to the training & validation datasets, cross-dataset evaluation was done with publicly available UMN dataset, which has several scene sequences depicting sudden, abnormal crowd dispersal. Enhanced optical flow extraction

The proposed methodology employs a multi-channel optical flow extraction approach that processes individual RGB channels separately before combining them for enhanced motion representation. Initially dense Farneback flow on all 3 RGB channels is computed and then take the average of these 3 per-channel motion estimates to obtain v_avg, which provides a stronger value than just computing on luminance or one channel. This averaging process helps reduce noise due to channel-specific lighting changes (e.g., highlight glare, shadow), resulting in one solid yet reliable motion estimate in both magnitude/direction statistics that has been averaged over different lighting conditions. The optical flow extraction enables the model to determine the magnitude of movement in each frame. This helps in evaluating the motion of the crowd or the associated flow of pattern. For consecutive frames It and It+1, optical flow is computed using the Dense Farneback method across each color channel. Network architecture design

The hybrid CNN-LSTM architecture combines the advantages of spatial feature extraction with time-series modeling. Time distributed convolutional layers learn local motion textures from a flow map, while LSTM layers operate as a Temporal aggregation to identify short-term patterns that can help to distinguish between coherent, chaotic and stampede prone motion. This design avoids developing end to end 3D-CNN’s or transformer networks that, while still powerful, require far greater amounts of compute and memory that can restrict edge ability to deploy. The scalar features are quantized and concatenated as an additional means of creating lower dimensional complementary signals, thus improving model interpretability and lessening the pressure on sequence modeling requirements. To provide empirical support for the design decisions discussed above, there will be a set of ablation studies applied in order to compare both (i) CNN-LSTM with scalar descriptors, (ii) CNN-LSTM without scalar descriptors and (iii) a baseline of a lightweight 3D CNN.

Scalar feature processing branch

The four computed scalar features undergo quantization and binarization. The first three features, flow acceleration, flow divergence, and the scene changes (SSIM), are quantized from the range 0–1 decimal points to 8-bit values. Whereas the fourth feature, motion entropy, is quantized from 0–4 to 8-bit values. This was done after the range of these features was determined from the combined dataset. Each of these 8-bit values is converted to binary representation, resulting in 32-bit feature vectors per frame. The main objective of this is to provide a low-bandwidth alternative to feature vectors, by making it easy to create binary codes for feature vectors, and (2) to provide a compact representation for the scalar motion features while still providing enough resolution to discriminate between the different threshold values required

Qualitative and real-world video evaluation

Along with conducting quantitative validation of the system, qualitative evaluations were performed utilizing actual video samples that were not previously used in the training phase of the project in order to assess the performance of the framework on previously unused real-world video input data. This evaluation process was run to investigate whether or not the spatiotemporal representations learned by this method, when evaluated on real-world test data not included in the training process of the project, would generalize to produce reasonable and understandable outputs while working under realistic crowd monitoring conditions.

To do this, a previously unseen test video, which consisted of a total of 129 frames captured at 30 frames per second (FPS), was used for the performance evaluation. After preprocessing, 120 frames were kept for the evaluations. In total, there were 119 optical flow frames and 20 temporal sequences of 16 frames each that were analysed as input data to the framework. The complete inference pipeline (optical flow extraction, feature extraction, and the subsequent CNN–LSTM inference phase) for this evaluation was run automatically and without any user intervention, as can be seen in the system-generated outputs created in the terminal window of the system.

As indicated in Figure 11, the qualitative results of the testing of the framework on the previously unseen video sample are shared. Overall, the results from the model conclude that the general state of the crowd was determined to be unsafe based on the high level of confidence of 99.75% associated with this classification, along with very low confidence (low probabilities of classification) for the normal, moderate, and dense states of the crowd. The predictions are consistent with observed motion instability, high degrees of flow divergence, and increasing amounts of motion entropy seen in the actual video, which may indicate a potential risk to the crowds being observed.

The UMN sequences used for validation purposes were run through the same preprocessing pipeline outlined in Section 4.1. The optimized threshold successfully prioritized safety-critical events, achieving an 80% recall for abnormal sequences. However, the model demonstrates its ability to detect abnormal dispersal behaviour on a dataset that included only a subset of UML specific patterns not used during training, and demonstrates that the learned spatiotemporal representations of optical flow reflect general dynamics of instability in crowds rather than particularities of any given dataset.

Discussion

The experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed multi-modal approach for crowd density classification. The model’s ability to achieve convergent training while maintaining reasonable validation performance indicates successful integration of optical flow features with scalar motion characteristics. The classification results reveal interesting patterns in crowd behavior recognition. The predominant classification suggests that the model has learned to identify intermediate crowd states effectively. The distribution across risk categories indicates the model’s capability to distinguish between different crowd density levels.

While in-domain validation results indicate near-perfect separability across the four risk classes, cross-dataset evaluation on the UMN dataset reveals a measurable performance degradation under domain shift conditions. The observed reduction in AUC suggests that although the model captures general motion instability dynamics, its decision boundary is partially dataset-dependent. The contrast between near-perfect in-domain ROC curves and moderate cross-domain AUC highlights the sensitivity of spatiotemporal crowd representations to camera geometry and scene semantics. This highlights the intrinsic challenge of transferring crowd behavior models across heterogeneous environments characterized by differing camera perspectives, density distributions, and illumination conditions. The cross-validation process for the UMN dataset, although limited to ten temporal sequences, acts as an initial, qualitative assessment of the out-of-distribution behavior for the model. The results demonstrate that the global decision boundaries remain sensitive to camera angle and behavioral changes, while the underlying motion instability features retain the capability to detect high-risk anomalies without any pre-existing domain knowledge.

The system sends risk labels or alerts to the cloud, reducing the need for high bandwidth and low latency as frames can be processed locally via edge devices. The model can also be deployed as an inference-optimized container at the surveillance fleet hubs, thus decreasing reliance on the cloud for functioning. In a typical configuration of the model workflow, live feeds from cameras would go through a lightweight pre-processing module that continuously buffers short-duration sequences of video, and subsequently extracts flow and scalar feature sets from the frame batches before forwarding the resultant inference-ready batches to the model for analysis.

The system processes on a 16-frame temporal window, taking approximately 0.173 seconds to process and delivering an effective throughput of 12.77 FPS on CPU-only hardware. This, while not delivering true frame-level real-time processing at 30 FPS, is more than sufficient to deliver on the latency requirements of a true decision-level early warning system, where risk estimation is performed on short temporal windows rather than individual frames. A detection latency of less than one second is more than sufficient for many safety-critical applications of crowd monitoring systems.

Although these results were generated using an twenty four-thread CPU with a batch size of eight, the pipeline has a modular architecture that allows for the easy parallelization of the pipeline across many camera streams. Each video feed can be assigned to a unique process. As such, simultaneous inference can be conducted on many video feeds. NVIDIA GPUs such as the NVIDIA RTX 4070 will also provide support for the simultaneous inference of eight to eleven video feeds at near real-time speed if the proper system to optimize the use of shared queues, and batch scheduling is used, as stated above. As future work will demonstrate this configuration, and demonstrate distributed inference across edge devices for scalable, low-latency collection of risk information about crowds.

Limitations and future directions

The model’s execution time was significantly high, and for complete processing indicates computational complexity that may require optimization for real-time deployment. In the future, research should focus on architecture-level strategies for optimizations. These include pruning, quantization, and operator fusion. Additionally, hardware-aware neural design may be optimized to minimize latency and memory. Other methods such as weight sharing in the form of parameter hashing, low-rank factorization of weight matrices, parallel inference, and knowledge distillation can be used. Concurrently, light-weight architectures such as GhostNet30 and ShuffleNet31 can be adopted. Edge-focused deployment options are also viable such as on-device inference, model partitioning, and asynchronous stream processing. Other additional leverage points may include being able to robustly handle occlusion and domain shifts through semi-/self-supervised adaptation, establishing calibrated early-warning thresholds through uncertainty estimation, and privacy-preserving analytics that facilitate reliable, real-time operation within multi-camera, bandwidth-constrained environments.

There are a few other aspects which warrant consideration to make it more robust and scalable. The first aspect is scaling the dataset. While 10,000 frames provide substantial training data and fulfilling validation needs, larger-scale datasets could improve generalization capabilities. Apart from this the real-time processing can be further optimized. Current processing speeds may not meet real-time requirements for critical applications. The current data is limited to Environmental Variations; Testing the model across diverse environmental conditions like lighting, weather, camera angles would strengthen robustness validation of the model.

The model does not directly detect individuals or track them; therefore, it does not impose a privacy risk associated with surveillance techniques; however, visual data will contain sensitive context and the deployment of anonymization strategies such as on-device processing or using only flow-based inference (e.g., not reconstructing images) should be considered to ensure the protection of privacy. Federated learning for local training and fine-tuning of models at different locations at the same time without transferring raw video also has the potential to meet user privacy and regulatory requirements; future studies should investigate combining differential privacy with encrypted inference pipelines. The future of stampede detection technology lies in the convergence of multiple technological paradigms, including artificial intelligence, Internet of Things, augmented reality, and advanced sensor networks28,32. The integration of these technologies promises to create comprehensive crowd management ecosystems capable of addressing the full spectrum of crowd safety challenges33. Although the model demonstrates reasonable performance on the UMN dataset without retraining, the reduced AUC score suggests that spatiotemporal representations remain partially domain-specific. The UMN evaluation was conducted on a limited number of abnormal sequences, and future work should incorporate larger, more diverse external benchmarks to better quantify generalization robustness. Domain adaptation strategies such as CORAL alignment, adversarial feature adaptation, or self-supervised fine-tuning may further improve cross-environment transferability. The proposed methodology establishes a foundation for advanced crowd safety systems with potential applications in public event management, transportation hubs, and emergency response planning.

Conclusion

The proposed work presents a hybrid CNN-LSTM approach for multi-tiered risk assessment of crowds using dense Farneback optical flow and other scalar motion descriptors to produce a four-category taxonomy (normal, moderate, dense, and risky) that will allow for progressive risk stratification useful for early intervention. By leveraging multiple optical flow channels, compact scalar features, and LSTM layers, the system captures both the spatial structure of motion and the short-term dynamics of instability without having to track individuals explicitly. Through testing on both UCSD and Agoraset datasets, our results have been validated in-domain with overall training classification accuracy of 99.75%, validation classification accuracy of 99.23%, and very good area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) separability across all categories. The system processes on a 16-frame temporal window, taking approximately 0.173 seconds to process and delivering an effective throughput of 12.77 FPS on CPU-only hardware. This, while not delivering true frame-level real-time processing at 30 FPS, is more than sufficient to deliver on the latency requirements of a true decision-level early warning system, where risk estimation is performed on short temporal windows rather than individual frames. A detection latency of less than one second is more than sufficient for many safety-critical applications of crowd monitoring systems.

Using the UMN benchmark for cross-dataset assessments provides additional information on the overall performance of the model when it is transferred from one data set to another. The model was able to provide reasonable results without retraining on new datasets but after using a ROC Curve and Youden’s J on the developed thresholds, determined that a significant proportion of abnormal events were also detected correctly. The validation showed the model is still performing worse than previous validation trials conducted with the model using only within-domain data but validates that the learned representation of motion for this model captures patterns of instability in crowds that are able to transfer to different data sets instead of being data-set specific artifacts. Continued research will focus on reducing the computational bottleneck of developing dense flow fields at run-time while mitigating the influence of the heterogeneous characteristics of data from a diverse population on models learned based on merging multiple sources of data. Future studies will also explore the use of light-weight approximations of motion, adapting datasets from different domains, and validating developed thresholds using uncertainty methods as well as using large-scale multi-domain validations to develop a practical methodology for providing an early warning of predictive risk through a combination of graduated risk assessments and computationally efficient spatiotemporal modeling in dense crowds through scalable and deliverable systems.
