Efficacy and safety of telitacicept combined with standard of care in patients with proliferative lupus nephritis: a real-world retrospective study
Authors: Ting Tian, Aifei Zhang, Xiaoxia Liu, Pengjia Wu, Na Li, Leyan Zeng, Jun Liu, Longyan Qin, Jiashun Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of telitacicept combined with standard of care (SoC) as induction therapy for proliferative lupus nephritis (LN) patients in a real-world setting.MethodsA total of 112 patients with biopsy-proven International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society Class III/IV (with or without Class V) LN were retrospectively enrolled. Subjects were divided into two groups: the telitacicept group (n=56) receiving telitacicept plus SoC and the control group (n=56) receiving SoC alone. The primary endpoints were the rates of complete renal response (CR) and partial renal response (PR) at week 24, while the secondary endpoints included CR and PR rates, serological profiles, disease activity markers, glucocorticoid tapering and safety assessments at weeks 12 and 52. To minimise selection bias and confounding factors, propensity score-based inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was employed. The adequacy of the covariates after IPTW was evaluated via standardised mean differences.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups. The telitacicept group demonstrated significant improvements in disease activity markers, including serum albumin, 24-hour urinary protein and urinary leucocyte and erythrocyte counts. At week 24, the telitacicept group achieved significantly higher CR rate (67.9% vs 39.3%, p=0.004) and PR rate (83.9% vs 67.9%, p=0.023) compared with the control group. Glucocorticoid dosages, systemic lupus erythematosus disease activity index 2000, British Isles Lupus Assessment Group and Physician’s Global Assessment scores were significantly reduced in the telitacicept group. Furthermore, multivariate Cox regression analysis identified telitacicept treatment as an independent predictor for achieving CR (HR=4.43; 95% CI, 2.25 to 8.73; p<0.001).ConclusionsThis real-world study demonstrates that telitacicept combined with SoC significantly improves renal response rates during the induction phase of proliferative LN while reducing glucocorticoid exposure. Consequently, telitacicept may represent a promising and effective therapeutic option for the management of proliferative LN.Keywords: Systemic Lupus Erythematosus, Lupus Nephritis, Therapeutics

Introduction

Lupus nephritis (LN) has a reported lifetime incidence of 20% to 60% in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)1 and remains a primary driver of disease progression and mortality.2 3 Although the current standard of care (SoC), consisting of glucocorticoids (GC) combined with immunosuppressants such as cyclophosphamide (CTX) or mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), has improved outcomes, challenges persist. Evidence indicates that 13% to 37% of patients who achieve adequate disease control still experience acute deterioration of renal function within 3 to 5 years,4 5 and 10% to 30% of LN patients ultimately progress to end-stage renal disease.6 7 Furthermore, conventional SoCs of LN are frequently associated with significant toxic action. For instance, the prolonged use of GC elevates the risks of infections, cardiovascular events, metabolic disturbances (eg, diabetes mellitus and osteoporosis) and cumulative organ damage.8 9 Therefore, exploring novel therapeutic strategies that maintain efficacy while minimising GC exposure holds crucial clinical importance for improving the long-term prognosis of patients with LN.

The 2024 Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines recommend a triple immunosuppressive regimen for the induction therapy of proliferative LN, which includes GC and immunosuppressants, with the potential addition of belimumab.1 The 2025 update for the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) 2025 further specifies that immunomodulatory therapy for SLE patients with renal involvement should be stratified based on disease severity, choosing between monotherapy and early combination therapy.10 Additionally, it incorporates belimumab, obinutuzumab and rituximab into the biologic treatment framework. These strategies indicated that targeted therapies focusing on B-cell aberrant activation, survival and clearance are now cornerstone strategies for active LN. Data from the Belimumab International Study in Lupus Nephritis (BLISS-LN) trial demonstrated that belimumab add-on therapy during the induction phase achieved a primary efficacy renal response of 53% at week 104 in Asian patients with LN,11 suggesting that combining biologic agents in the induction therapy of proliferative LN may further enhance renal response rates. Mechanistically, B-cell activating factor (BAFF, also known as B-lymphocyte stimulator (BLyS)) and a proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL) are crucial for B-cell maturation, activation and plasma cell survival; the BAFF/APRIL axis is thus central to B-cell development and proliferation.12 While belimumab, currently used to manage LN, targets soluble BAFF, telitacicept represents a novel biologic that concurrently blocks both BAFF and APRIL. This dual-blockade mechanism profoundly disrupts the BAFF/APRIL pathway, thereby suppressing B-cell maturation and differentiation, inhibiting plasma cell generation and ultimately reducing autoantibody production.

Telitacicept has demonstrated favourable systemic disease control in phase II and III SLE trials, yielding SLE responder index-4 (SRI-4) response rates of 68.3% and 67.1%, respectively.13 14 However, the absence of independent subgroup analyses for LN, specifically proliferative LN, leaves a critical gap in the evidence base regarding its renal-specific clinical utility. Given its dual blockade of the BAFF/APRIL signalling pathway, telitacicept possesses strong theoretical potential for LN treatment. Although our prior single-arm retrospective study offered evidence of its efficacy and safety in LN,15 these findings warrant further validation due to the limitations inherent in its design. To address the paucity of comparative real-world evidence, this study employs a retrospective controlled design based on real-world data to systematically evaluate the effectiveness and safety of a telitacicept-based triple immunosuppressive regimen vs conventional SoC in patients with proliferative LN.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study enrolled patients with International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society (ISN/RPS) Class III/IV (with or without Class V) LN treated at the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University between January 2018 and July 2025. Patients in the control group were enrolled between January 2018 and July 2025 and received treatment with GC, antimalarials and immunosuppressants. Patients in the telitacicept group were collected between January 2022 and July 2025 and were treated with telitacicept plus GC, antimalarials and immunosuppressants. This research was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (2026068K) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients were included if they: (1) were ≥18 years; (2) fulfilled the 2019 EULAR/American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria for SLE; (3) had renal involvement presenting with a 24-hour urinary protein >0.5 g or a urine protein/creatinine ratio (PCR) >0.5 g/g and a biopsy-proven diagnosis of ISN/RPS Class III/IV active proliferative LN (with or without Class V); (4) received treatment ≥12 weeks. Patients were excluded if they had: (1) primary renal disease or other secondary glomerular diseases (eg, diabetic nephropathy, hypertensive nephropathy, Henoch-Schönlein purpura nephritis); (2) uncontrolled hypertension, diabetes mellitus, haematological disorders or severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction; and (3) a history of renal replacement therapy, major organ transplantation or hematopoietic stem cell/bone marrow transplantation. Pregnant or lactating women were also excluded from this study.

Treatment protocol

All patients received SoC, which consisted of antimalarials, medium-to-high-dose GC (0.3–1.0 mg/kg) and either MMF (1.0–1.5 g/day) or CTX (0.5 g biweekly or 1.0 g every 4 weeks). The telitacicept group received telitacicept at a dose of 160 mg subcutaneously once weekly, initiated within the first 3 months of induction therapy and the control group received SoC alone. The medication regimens were individualised, with dosages adjusted by attending physicians based on clinical manifestations and laboratory findings. The concomitant use of antihypertensive agents and renin-angiotensin system inhibitors was permitted.

Efficacy assessment

Treatment efficacy for SLE was evaluated using the SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K) score and SRI-4 response rate. SRI-4 response was defined as: (1) a reduction of ≥4 points in the SLEDAI-2K score from baseline; (2) no new British Isles Lupus Assessment Group (BILAG) A organ domain score or no more than one new BILAG B organ domain score compared with baseline; and (3) no worsening in the Physician’s Global Assessment (PGA) score compared with baseline (increase of <0.3 points).

Treatment efficacy for LN was categorised as follows:16 (1) complete renal response (CR): 24-hour urinary protein level <0.5 g/day or PCR <0.5 g/g, with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) within ±10% to 15% of baseline or stable; (2) partial renal response (PR): ≥50% reduction in 24-hour urinary protein to <3.0 g/day or ≥50% reduction in PCR to <3.0 g/g, and eGFR within ±10% to 15% of baseline or stable; and (3) no renal response (NR): failure to achieve either CR or PR within 6 to 12 months following treatment initiation. The overall response rate was calculated as the (number of patients with CR+number of patients with PR) / total number of patients×100%.

Data collection

We systematically collected baseline demographic and disease-specific variables, including age, sex, LN duration, renal pathology classification, extra-renal involvement, GC dosage, immunosuppressant regimens and SLEDAI-2K scores. Laboratory parameters were assessed at baseline and at weeks 0, 12, 24 and 52, including 24-hour urinary protein, serum albumin (ALB), haemoglobin, serum creatinine (Scr), eGFR, anti-dsDNA, complement C3 and renal response rate.

Safety evaluations included assessment of telitacicept-related adverse events (AEs) such as infusion/injection-site reactions, hypersensitivity, infectious complications, leucopenia and gastrointestinal symptoms.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was determined by the total number of eligible patients available during the specified period. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 and R 4.4.1. After assessing normality with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, continuous data were summarised as mean (SD) and compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test; categorical data were reported as counts (percentages) and analysed using Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. A two-sided p value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To minimise selection bias due to confounding factors, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was used to adjust for baseline differences. Covariates included in the model were age, sex, eGFR, complement C3/C4, hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) utilisation, anti-dsDNA antibodies, renal pathology classification and anti-Sm antibodies. Covariate balance post-weighting was verified via standardised mean differences (SMD<0.1). IPTW was applied to adjust baseline comparisons, clinical trends and GC equivalent dose analyses, evaluated by weighted multivariable logistic and linear regressions. For clinical outcomes, Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests were used to assess the time to and cumulative probability of achieving CR and PR. Cox proportional hazards models were established to determine hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with covariates screened via univariate analysis (p<0.05) and validated for the proportional hazards assumption. To handle longitudinal repeated-measures data, multilevel mixed-effects regression models with patient-level random effects were used to control for intra-class correlation (ICC) and heterogeneity. Finally, prespecified subgroup analyses evaluated treatment effect consistency across strata of baseline 24-hour urinary protein (>3.0/24 hours vs ≤3.0 g/24 hours), eGFR (>90 vs ≤90 mL/min/1.73 m²), SLEDAI-2K (>12 vs ≤12), BILAG (>16 vs ≤16), PGA (>2.5 vs ≤2.5) and serum ALB (>30 g/L vs ≤30 g/L), with Cox models re-fitted to yield subgroup-specific HRs and 95% CIs. As a sensitivity analysis, baseline disease characteristics and week 24 CR rates were compared between patients receiving CTX and MMF. A multivariable logistic regression model was further fitted to assess whether the immunosuppressive regimen affected the association between telitacicept treatment and CR.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 112 patients were enrolled in this study, all of whom had undergone renal biopsy. Baseline characteristics were largely balanced across both groups. The telitacicept group was characterised by significantly higher age, activity index (AI), chronicity index (CI), ALB, complement C3 and C4 levels and more frequent musculoskeletal involvement (p<0.05). In contrast, higher PGA scores were observed in the control group (p<0.05; table 1). There were no significant differences in the other indicators.

At baseline, all patients received GC therapy. The median GC dose was 42.50 (30.00–50.00) mg in the telitacicept group and 50.00 (50.00–50.00) mg in the control group. The predominant induction therapy consisted of GC in combination with either MMF or CTX. Notably, a lower proportion of patients in the telitacicept group received CTX induction compared with the control group (26.79% vs 50.00%). HCQ was administered as background therapy; however, owing to factors such as medication-related AEs, the actual utilisation rates were 87.50% in the telitacicept group and 94.63% in the control group.

To optimally minimise selection bias, baseline imbalances across the two groups were adjusted using the propensity score-based IPTW (PS-IPTW) approach, followed by re-assessment via sensitivity analyses. Post-weighting, inter-group differences for most variables were no longer statistically significant (p>0.05). Notably, SMDs for complement C3/C4 and regimens of GCs and immunosuppressants were reduced to <0.1, demonstrating that baseline heterogeneity was substantially improved. Nevertheless, some residual imbalances persisted, as evidenced by SMDs remaining >0.1 for age, PGA score, musculoskeletal involvement, ALB, AI, CI and GC dose (table 2).

Patients receiving CTX were older and had higher baseline BILAG and PGA scores than those receiving MMF, whereas no significant differences were observed in baseline SLEDAI-2K, 24-hour urinary protein, Scr, eGFR, ALB or complement levels. Efficacy assessment

Clinical study endpoints

Dynamic assessments of renal response at weeks 12, 24 and 52 revealed that the telitacicept-based regimen was superior to SoC in both early induction remission and long-term efficacy maintenance. At week 12, the telitacicept group demonstrated a more rapid onset of action and achieved a significantly higher CR rate compared with the control group (41.1% vs 8.9%, p<0.001), while the NR rates (30.4% vs 48.2%) remained non-significant (p=0.082). This therapeutic divergence widened by week 24, where the telitacicept group exhibited a markedly higher CR rate (67.9% vs 39.3%, p=0.004) and a significantly lower NR rate (14.3% vs 32.1%, p=0.044), underscoring enhanced intermediate-term disease control via telitacicept-based regimen. By the 52-week endpoint, the robust efficacy of the telitacicept group was maintained, yielding a CR rate of 84.1% versus 63.0% in controls (p=0.036). Although the NR rate was lower in the telitacicept group at week 52 (6.8% vs 18.5%), statistical significance was not reached (p=0.134), a finding likely driven by mid-course treatment adjustments in non-responders or varying follow-up lengths (figure 1).

Clinical efficacy outcomes at weeks 12, 24 and 52.

After applying IPTW, clinical outcomes at weeks 12, 24 and 52 aligned with the primary analysis, further corroborating that the telitacicept regimen outperforms SoC in accelerating remission induction and sustaining long-term therapeutic benefits (online supplemental figure 1). Overall, compared with SoC, telitacicept can significantly increase the CR rate within the first 24 weeks of treatment and reduce the risk of NR in the medium term, thereby bringing more significant early and medium-term clinical benefits.

To evaluate the longitudinal dynamics of renal response, a Kaplan-Meier analysis was applied to the IPTW-adjusted sample. The results indicated that CR in the telitacicept group was consistently superior to that in the control group. The cumulative incidence of PR was significantly greater in the telitacicept group (log-rank p=0.0023), with this therapeutic divergence manifesting early and persisting throughout the follow-up (figure 2). Meanwhile, telitacicept conferred an equally pronounced advantage in achieving the CR endpoint (log-rank p=0.0014). Notably, while the CR curve for the control group plateaued, the telitacicept group demonstrated a sustained, progressive increase in the cumulative incidence of CR over time (figure 3).

To further evaluate whether residual baseline imbalances influenced the observed treatment effect, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed using IPTW-weighted logistic regression with further adjustment for baseline PGA score, AI and CI. In the IPTW-only model, telitacicept treatment was associated with a higher probability of achieving CR at week 24 (OR=3.96, 95% CI 1.42 to 11.07, p=0.010). After additional adjustment for PGA, AI and CI, the association remained statistically significant, although the effect estimate was slightly attenuated (adjusted OR=3.27, 95% CI 1.08 to 9.88, p=0.038). These findings suggest that residual differences in baseline disease activity and renal pathological characteristics may have partially contributed to the observed treatment effect but were unlikely to fully explain the superior renal response observed in the telitacicept group.

To identify predictors of the CR, variables were initially screened using the Random Survival Forest (RSF) algorithm. Key contributors to CR prediction included 24 hours urinary protein, PGA, musculoskeletal involvement, IgM and ALB. These factors and groups were subsequently integrated into a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model. After adjustment for potential confounders, telitacicept treatment emerged as a robust independent predictor of CR (HR=4.432; 95% CI 2.250 to 8.730; p<0.001), demonstrating that the telitacicept group significantly accelerated the attainment of CR compared with the control group. Clinical parameter assessment

Assessments of disease activity revealed that the telitacicept group achieved a deeper therapeutic response during remission induction. While both groups exhibited a progressive decline in BILAG scores over the treatment course, the reductions in the telitacicept group were markedly more pronounced and durable. Specifically, from week 12 to week 52, BILAG scores in the telitacicept group dropped from 5.71 (±4.33) to 0.38 (±0.49), while a decrease from 9.02 (±1.63) to 3.50 (±4.17) in the control group. At week 52, the telitacicept group maintained a significantly lower BILAG score than the control group (0.38±0.49 vs. 3.50±4.17, p<0.001). Additionally, the telitacicept group yielded significantly higher SRI-4 response rates at weeks 12 and 24 (p<0.05). Collectively, these results underscore the superiority of telitacicept therapy in controlling disease activity, thereby driving patients toward a state of low disease activity that closely approximates clinical CR (figure 2).

Longitudinal assessments of ALB revealed more rapid improvements in nutritional status and renal protein loss within the telitacicept group. By week 52, serum ALB levels in the telitacicept group had risen to 44.02±3.52 g/L, higher than the 42.92±4.18 g/L observed in the control group. Notably, this advantage was evident as early as week 12, with ALB reaching 40.08±4.20 g/L in the telitacicept group, significantly outperforming the 36.10±5.67 g/L in the control group (p<0.001). Given the baseline differences in complement levels, changes from baseline in C3 and C4 (ΔC3 and ΔC4) were additionally analysed. No significant differences in ΔC3 or ΔC4 were observed between the telitacicept and control groups at weeks 12, 24 or 52 (all p>0.05).

Throughout the follow-up period, no significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding Scr, eGFR, urinary white blood cell (U-WBC) or anti-dsDNA antibodies (p>0.05). Compared with the control group, the telitacicept group achieved significantly higher SRI-4 response rates, ALB and C3 levels at weeks 12 and 24 (p<0.05), alongside significantly higher C4 levels at week 12 (p<0.05). Regarding 24-hour urinary protein, the telitacicept group exhibited a continuous and pronounced decline. At week 12, it decreased substantially to 698.92±612.48 mg, significantly lower than the 1740.87±1240.39 mg in the control group (p<0.001). This decline continued at week 24 (296.76±211.24 mg vs. 637.99±548.31 mg, p<0.001) and was maintained at a low level of 141.74±80.99 mg at week 52, remaining significantly superior to the control group (367.51±369.35 mg, p<0.001) (figure 5).

Comparison of GC equivalent dose

To evaluate the impact of the interventions on GC tapering, we longitudinally tracked GC equivalent doses across both groups. Monitoring revealed that the telitacicept group facilitated an accelerated tapering of GCs from the early stages. Specifically, at week 12, the median GC dose in the telitacicept group plummeted to 20.00 (15.00–30.00) mg/day, significantly lower than the 40.00 (35.00–45.00) mg/day in the control group (p<0.001). This therapeutic divergence was sustained at week 24 (10.00 (10.00–15.00) vs 25.00 (15.00–30.00) mg/day) and week 52 (7.50 (5.00–10.00) vs 10.00 (10.00–15.00) mg/day). Collectively, these findings underscore a robust steroid-sparing effect of the treatment. By driving a deeper clinical remission (week 52 BILAG score: 0.38 vs 3.50), this approach effectively minimises the long-term side effect risks driven by cumulative high-dose GC exposure (figure 6).

Adverse reaction

Safety analysis revealed that AEs were recorded in 30.36% (17/56) of patients in the control group versus 19.64% (11/56) in the telitacicept group (table 3). Infections constituted the majority of these events across both arms. For the control group, principal AEs comprised herpes zoster (n=5, 8.92%), respiratory (n=4, 7.14%) and urinary tract infections (n=4, 7.14%). There were also individual cases (1.78% each) of genital warts, vaginitis, tuberculous lymphadenitis and avascular necrosis of femoral head. Conversely, AEs in the telitacicept arm primarily involved respiratory tract infections (n=6, 10.71%), with only one case (1.78% each) of urinary tract infection and herpes zoster. Furthermore, IgA and IgM levels were significantly higher in the control group (p<0.05), whereas IgG levels at weeks 12 and 24 were significantly higher in the control group (p<0.05). Ultimately, the treatment regimen demonstrated a lower overall AE rate compared with the control group, with no new safety signals emerging from the available data.

Discussion

This real-world study systematically evaluated the clinical value of telitacicept combined with SoC for the induction therapy of proliferative LN. The findings demonstrate that adding telitacicept to SoC significantly improves the 24-week CR rate, achieving 67.9% compared with 39.3% in the control group (p=0.004). Notably, the telitacicept-treated cohort exhibited a more rapid and sustained reduction in 24-hour proteinuria at both weeks 12 and 24, underscoring its potential to drive earlier and robust renal protection. Given current therapeutic gaps, specifically suboptimal response rates and high GC exposure, these data support telitacicept as a pivotal therapeutic strategy for optimising outcomes in patients with proliferative LN.

LN represents one of the most severe types of organ involvement of SLE. Proliferative LN, in particular, exhibits high disease activity and a substantial risk of progression to end-stage renal disease, persisting as a major clinical challenge. While the precise pathogenesis of LN remains incompletely understood, mounting evidence underscores the central pathogenic role of aberrant B cell activation.17 These aberrantly activated B cells differentiate into plasma cells, generating various pathogenic autoantibodies. The subsequent deposition of immune complexes within the glomeruli triggers the complement cascade, culminating in severe inflammation and tissue injury.2 Consequently, precision therapeutics targeting B cell pathways have emerged as a cornerstone in the evolving landscape of SLE and LN management. Current strategies include anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies, BAFF inhibitors and dual-targeted strategies that concurrently block BAFF and APRIL signalling.1 13 18–20 Notably, although rituximab failed to achieve its primary endpoints in some randomised controlled trials, it remains widely integrated into real-world clinical practice, particularly for managing refractory phenotypes, severe haematological involvement and resistant LN.19 Additionally, while early anti-CD20 agents like rituximab yielded mixed results in randomised trials, the next-generation anti-CD20 antibody obinutuzumab recently demonstrated remarkable efficacy in the NOBILITY and REGENCY trials, confirming the clinical value of profound B-cell targeted therapy in LN.21 22 However, anti-CD20 therapies primarily target CD20+B cells while sparing CD20- long-lived plasma cells, which continue to secrete pathogenic autoantibodies.23 To overcome these limitations, dual-targeted agents such as telitacicept have emerged.

Extensive evidence indicates that BAFF and APRIL are persistently upregulated in both the plasma and renal tissues of patients with LN, fostering a local microenvironment conducive to B cell and plasma cell survival.24–26 Moreover, intra-renal BAFF expression positively correlates with disease severity of LN.27 28 Specifically, in proliferative LN (Class III/IV), the expression of BAFF protein and APRIL mRNA is markedly elevated, while lower levels were observed in membranous LN (Class V) or healthy kidneys.27 29 This enhanced tubular BAFF expression is consistently corroborated by both animal models and human proliferative LN biopsies.26 Mechanistically, dual blockade of the BAFF and APRIL signalling pathways should theoretically confer a more comprehensive suppression of the B cell lineage than selective BAFF inhibition. Telitacicept, a recombinant human TACI-Fc fusion protein comprising the extracellular soluble domain of TACI fused to a human IgG Fc fragment, functions by concurrently neutralising BAFF and APRIL. This dual antagonism disrupts downstream B cell signalling, thereby impeding the survival and differentiation of aberrantly activated B cells and plasma cells, ultimately curtailing autoantibody production.13 30 31 This mechanism of action plausibly accounts for the accelerated reduction in proteinuria, and more pronounced reductions in serum immunoglobulin levels observed in the treatment cohort of the present study.

In comparison with previous studies, the present study observed that telitacicept combination therapy demonstrated highly favourable efficacy in achieving renal response. However, cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because differences in response definitions, study designs, background therapies and patient characteristics may substantially influence reported outcomes. For example, an open-label extension study of the BLISS-LN belimumab trial reported a CR rate of 48%,32 while a Phase III clinical trial conducted in East Asian populations showed a 52-week CR rate of 62%.33 In our study, patients treated with telitacicept achieved CR rates of 67.9% and 84.1% at weeks 24 and 52, respectively, with an increased proportion of responders observed as early as week 12. The favourable outcomes observed in our cohort may be partly explained by differences in clinical setting and treatment characteristics. Our pragmatic response definition and comprehensive background therapy, including antimalarial treatment, RAS blockade when clinically indicated and guideline-consistent immunosuppression, may have contributed to the observed response rates. In addition, the retrospective real-world design and exclusion of patients with severe comorbidities may have resulted in a population with relatively preserved baseline organ function. Importantly, despite this favourable treatment background, telitacicept demonstrated additional benefit compared with SOC alone, supporting its potential role as an adjunctive therapy during the early induction phase of proliferative LN. This finding is consistent with emerging treatment strategies emphasising early combination therapy and treat-to-target approaches in LN management,1 10 emphasising the importance of early intervention and early treat-to-target in the management of LN. Unlike belimumab, telitacicept may provide a broader mechanistic foundation for suppressing aberrant B-cell activation, plasma cell generation and autoantibody production. Therefore, our results support the promising clinical application of a dual-target B-cell inhibition strategy in the induction treatment of proliferative LN, although its long-term benefits and safety profile warrant further validation through prospective studies.

Beyond renal response, this study demonstrated a pronounced benefit regarding GC tapering. Longitudinal analysis based on the IPTW-weighted cohort revealed that the telitacicept group achieved rapid GC tapering during the early phases of treatment. Specifically, by week 12, the mean GC dose in the telitacicept arm had decreased to 23.6±2.8 mg/day, significantly lower than the 40.1±2.9 mg/day observed in the control arm. This downward trajectory continued at week 24, with the telitacicept group dose dropping to 13.1±1.9 mg/day (vs 25.6±3.4 mg/day in the control group). By week 52, the GC dose in the telitacicept group had reached a safe maintenance level of 7.2±0.9 mg/day, whereas the control group remained at a comparably higher dose of 12.8±2.2 mg/day. These findings strongly indicate that this regimen facilitates accelerated GC tapering during early induction and sustains a lower dosage profile throughout the subsequent follow-up period. Given the well-established correlations between prolonged GC exposure and increased risks of infections, osteonecrosis and cardiovascular events, this robust steroid-sparing effect holds substantial clinical significance. Concurrently, regarding the safety profile, the overall incidence of AEs did not increase in the telitacicept group, demonstrating favourable overall tolerability. Although levels of immunoglobulins, particularly IgG, declined during telitacicept therapy, the overall infection rate observed over 52 weeks did not exceed that of the SOC group. While these results provide encouraging signals regarding safety, they should be interpreted cautiously owing to the limited sample size and short-term follow-up.

Moreover, the telitacicept effectively attenuated overall systemic disease activity. SLEDAI scores in the telitacicept arm exhibited a progressive decline to 6.00, 4.22 and 2.56 at weeks 12, 24 and 52, respectively, contrasting with 10.39, 7.36 and 5.13 in the control arm. Correspondingly, SRI-4 response rates were significantly superior in the telitacicept cohort across all time points: 76.36% versus 35.71% at week 12, 89.09% versus 69.64% at weeks 24 and 93.02% versus 79.63% at weeks 52 (all p<0.05). This underscores that the regimen not only improved renal outcomes but also played a positive role in controlling systemic disease activities. Our observations corroborate existing literature. For instance, a cohort of 13 LN patients demonstrated a marked reduction in baseline SLEDAI-2K scores post-treatment.34 Likewise, a study on patients with concurrent SLE, LN and nephrotic syndrome observed a median SLEDAI reduction from 13 to 4 following 12 months of telitacicept therapy.35 Furthermore, an East Asian subgroup analysis of belimumab similarly reported an enhanced primary renal response rate at 52 weeks.36 Collectively, these data reinforce the therapeutic promise of B-cell-targeted interventions in LN. However, while telitacicept outperformed previous literature in specific efficacy parameters, the lack of head-to-head studies precludes definitive claims regarding its comparative superiority over biologics such as belimumab. Large-scale, prospective, randomised controlled studies are urgently warranted to substantiate these findings.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, given its single-centre retrospective design, selection bias and residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. The relatively favourable response observed in our cohort may partly reflect patient selection, treatment patterns and differences in clinical practice compared with prospective randomised controlled trials. Second, the relatively small sample size may compromise the statistical stability of certain outcome estimates. Third, the follow-up duration was insufficient to properly assess long-term renal survival, disease relapse and rare AEs. Therefore, future multicentre, prospective, randomised controlled studies are warranted to further validate the efficacy and safety of telitacicept in the induction therapy of proliferative LN. Such studies are also necessary to determine the optimal target populations and timing of intervention across patients with varying pathological patterns and baseline risk profiles.

In conclusion, the present study supports that the triple immunotherapy combining telitacicept with SoC serves as a promising intensified therapeutic strategy for the induction treatment of proliferative LN. This regimen improves early and intermediate-term renal response rates, accelerates the reduction of proteinuria, decreases disease activity and facilitates faster GC tapering. Pending support from higher-level evidence-based medicine in the future, telitacicept is poised to become an important therapeutic option in the induction treatment of proliferative LN.
