Stay in Play: a FIFA decision aid for football participation during pregnancy
Authors: Sinead Dufour, Emma Brockwell, Ivi Casagrande, Margie H Davenport
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveGiven the uncertain risk of football participation during pregnancy, this study sought to develop and validate a clinical decision aid to support informed participation.MethodsDevelopment of the decision aid was founded on the knowledge-to-action cycle through the application of the intervention mapping and knowledge transfer scheme. Five steps were followed: (1) scientific literature review; (2) identifying participation barriers and facilitators; (3) developing the objective and approach; (4) identifying the key health screening components and (5) external review by experts working in football (physicians, physiotherapists and coaches), followed by a preliminary usability, content and criterion-validity assessment.ResultsWe recommend discontinuing full-contact football beyond 12 weeks of pregnancy. Players may continue modified training, including low-intensity contact, in the absence of medical contraindications. Consultation and testing of the decision aid took place with 34 end users, including players, coaches and medical staff. These perspectives spanned 12 countries, confirming the high utility for players and their multidisciplinary teams (MDTs) at all levels. Strong support was determined across all aspects of the aid; overall (94% approval), a five-stage approach (97% approval) and the Pregnancy Medical and Performance Debrief Form (100% approval). The case study affirmed 94% respondent proficiency in using the decision aid. Qualitative feedback from the respondents was also used to enhance clarity of the decision aid.ConclusionThe Fédération Internationale de Football Association Stay in Play During Pregnancy decision aid is designed for amateur to professional-level football players and their MDTs to support informed and quality decision-making about football participation during pregnancy.Keywords: Pregnancy, Football

Introduction

Football is a fast-paced, high-impact, physically demanding sport requiring a combination of strength, speed, endurance, agility and tactical skills where players are pushed to their physical limits during training and matches. The increased visibility and associated status of women’s football have allowed players to extend their careers well into their late 20s and 30s, which often overlaps with peak reproductive age. In the last decade, numerous high-profile players have started families and successfully returned to elite-level football in the months after childbirth. Yet, pregnancy continues to be stigmatised as a state of fragility, defaulting to restrictive guidance when it comes to sport participation.1 In addition to creating unnecessary fear, the existing restrictive guidance is unsubstantiated. To the contrary, the existing literature demonstrates the vast health benefits of ‘staying in play’, by adapting football participation, to maintain physical activity throughout pregnancy for amateur and professional players alike.2,4 Continued participation in football training is further supported by recent studies demonstrating that short bouts of high-intensity exercise (eg, intervals exceeding 90% of maximal heart rate) and longer bouts of vigorous (submaximal) exercise are not associated with adverse fetal or maternal outcomes, and the risk of adverse outcomes during contact sport is low.3 5 6 Thus, there remains an enormous opportunity to promote health and prevent morbidity, as well as support safe and achievable return-to-play postpartum through ongoing adapted football participation during pregnancy.7

In June 2024, the Fédération Internationale de Football Association (FIFA) released updated maternity regulations for female football players, which provide an essential policy framework to support players to continue to participate in adapted football during and following pregnancy.8 9 Later that year, the Fédération Internationale des Associations de Footballeurs Professionnels (FIFPro) published an overview for professional players outlining the importance of adapting football participation during pregnancy and the postpartum period.8 However, a significant barrier to continuing adapted football during pregnancy is the lack of sport-specific information regarding maternal/fetal safety, as well as a lack of practical guidance on when and how to adapt participation versus stopping altogether.10 This gap often leads players to discontinue football during pregnancy.

In addition, the transition to becoming a mother-player can bring challenges, including a potential loss of identity and increased risk of anxiety and/or depression.1 Staying connected with their team and maintaining training may counter these challenges, while also maintaining football-specific fitness and neurocognitive patterning.11 Recent data suggest that continued sport participation during pregnancy reduces postpartum injury risk and improves pregnancy outcomes.12 13 However, decisions regarding football participation during pregnancy are complex and require a careful risk-management approach. Such an approach involves balancing several factors: maintenance of sport-specific fitness, reducing injury risk to the player and ensuring fetal safety, in addition to considering other contextual constraints related to each individual player. The objective of this paper was to develop a football-specific decision aid to support quality decision making for players and their multidisciplinary teams (MDTs) with scalability from low-to-high-resource settings at the amateur to professional level.

Methods

This FIFA Stay in Play During Pregnancy decision aid was founded on the knowledge-to-action (KTA) cycle,14 through application of the intervention mapping and knowledge transfer scheme using the following five phases: Phase 115—conducting an academic search to identify relevant studies on football during pregnancy2 11 14 16 17; Phase 2—using the results of Phase 1 to identify barriers and key considerations to elite sport participation during pregnancy10; Phase 3—defining the objective and planning the approach for the decision aid’s development; Phase 4—identifying the key health screening components for the decision aid and Phase 5—conducting a preliminary assessment of the usability, criterion- and content-validity of the decision aid. Please see the online supplemental file 1 for detailed information related to both methods and results, alongside practical guidance for pregnant players.

Developed by Graham and colleagues, the KTA cycle is a theoretically robust framework that is frequently applied in the field of implementation science.18 The KTA cycle maps out an iterative process that includes the synthesis, dissemination, exchange and ethically sound application of knowledge to improve the implementation of health and related practices. From the perspective of the KTA framework, identifying the ‘know-do gap’—the disconnect between what research tells us is best practice and what we actually do in practice—is imperative to implementing evidence-based knowledge into practice and needs to be established prior to widespread implementation.19 The KTA framework has two main parts, knowledge creation (three phases) and knowledge in action (seven phases), that guide the development of tools based on rigorous knowledge synthesis followed by their iterative evaluation and refinement in practice.

Phase 1: evaluation of the scientific literature

We conducted a search of academic literature in PubMed using the general search strategy of (postpartum OR postnatal) AND (rehabilitation OR recovery) AND (athlete* or sport*) from database conception until February 2024. Studies of any design or language were considered. The authors also recently published a narrative review of original research covering key considerations for football participation during pregnancy that also informed the development of the decision aid.11 This phase aligned with the knowledge creation aspect of the KTA framework.

Phase 2: identifying barriers and facilitators to sport participation in pregnancy

Barriers and facilitators to sport participation in pregnancy were identified in the literature search in Phase 1.

Phase 3: developing the objective and approach of the decision aid

The working group (authors) met at FIFA headquarters (Zurich, Switzerland) in February 2024, where they collaborated and consulted with stakeholders to define the objective and methodological approach of the decision aid development. The group consisted of experts with applied, clinical and research experience, spanning multiple disciplines, working with amateur and professional football players. The research group used the findings of the literature review to identify all potential key screening domains to support the health of athletes during pregnancy. Key moderating factors that would influence decisions about progression and exit criteria for pregnant players were identified (eg, the development of contraindications, athlete request), categorised, discussed and subsequently prioritised based on levels of supporting evidence. Subsequently, available screening questionnaires were matched with the prioritised screening areas. Consultation with external content experts facilitated this process.

Phase 4: identifying key screening components of the decision aid

At the meeting in Zurich, the authors identified the key components of the decision aid following the review of the literature completed in Phases 2 and 3, alongside consultation with external experts. These considerations represent potential levers for targeted support, designed to help players adapt and continue their football participation safely. To enhance communication, specific questionnaires were also identified. If multiple validated options existed, the shortest or most commonly used tools for athletes were chosen. The individual’s responses on the chosen screening tools would ultimately guide the next steps with respect to adapted football participation, for example, consulting with a healthcare provider. This entire phase contributes to the preliminary tool creation phase, serving as the direct output of the knowledge generation component within the KTA framework.

Phase 5: preliminary assessment of usability, criterion- and content-validity

A preliminary assessment of the usability, criterion- and content-validity of the decision aid was assessed in Phase 5. Initially, the decision aid was piloted (in English), with five individuals working in professional-level football (sports medicine physician, physiotherapist and coaches). This initial pilot stage enabled the collection of feedback relative to the content validity, usability, appropriateness, clarity, length and format for use with amateur and professional football players. Following a revision of the decision aid based on this preliminary feedback, broader consultation on the usability and criterion validity was conducted via electronic survey (research electronic data capture(REDCap)). Respondents were identified as individuals with expertise in football from diverse regions around the world with experience at the amateur to professional levels. Individuals met the inclusion criteria if they possessed the required expertise and were sufficiently proficient in English to complete the decision aid. The survey began with a high-level overview of how to use the Stay in Play During Pregnancy decision aid. Next, respondents were presented with a short case study of a pregnant football player outlining how they responded to each question on the decision aid. Respondents then were asked about their opinion regarding the usability of the decision aid, the five-stage approach to training and the Pregnancy Medical and Performance Debrief Form (eg, were they easy to follow, would they be useful for a pregnant player) using a nine-point Likert scale (eg, 1—not useful, 5—somewhat useful and 9—extremely useful). Respondents had the option to provide additional feedback via open box responses, which was used to further refine the decision aid. Through a case study, we also assessed criterion validity to confirm the decision aid’s recommendations accurately aligned with appropriate actions and produced the intended results. In this case study, respondents were presented with a scenario and asked to choose the correct course of action from four options (eg, needs to stop training and exercising, and speak to her healthcare provider; can continue training and exercising but needs to speak to her healthcare provider; can continue training and exercising but needs to speak to her MDT; and no additional action needed, she can go to the next part of the decision aid) at each step of the decision aid. Demographic data were analysed as mean and SD or the frequency and percentage (IBM SPSS Statistics V.29). Open-ended responses were analysed using an inductive approach to thematic coding in order to identify patterns within the data following the framework of Braun and Clarke.20 Coding was conducted independently by multiple researchers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus. Feedback was then incorporated into the decision aid. To support the practical application of the decision aid, specifically related to progression and regression guidance through the steps, practical advice on modalities and training adaptations for football participation during pregnancy was developed (ie, the five-stage approach to training).

Equity, diversity and inclusion

The all-female author group were from Brazil, Canada and the UK. They represented researchers, physiotherapists and a physical performance coach. We surveyed a diverse range of individuals from low-to-middle-income countries, across amateur to professional levels, from a variety of cultures to scope broad and encompassing perspectives (table 1).

Results

Phase 1: review of the scientific literature

When performing a review of the scientific literature, we identified a recent systematic review and meta-analysis that specifically examined the impact of continued sport participation during pregnancy in elite athletes.18 Health outcomes, including risk of gestational diabetes, pre-eclampsia and small or preterm neonates, between elite-level athletes and their less active counterparts (ie, general obstetric population) were analysed and reported.21 These data were found to triangulate numerous guidelines, systematic reviews and meta-analyses in the general obstetric population, demonstrating that continued engagement in exercise following conception is low risk.1416 17 22,29 These data were supported by a narrative review published on pregnancy-specific considerations for football.11 A study of contact sport participation during pregnancy identified a low risk of adverse events, even after experiencing a ‘hard hit’, further supporting the continuation of football in early pregnancy.3 Currently, there are no pregnancy-specific frameworks on sport participation; however, the conducted literature review highlighted the athlete-identified need for an individualised approach to sport participation.1 10

Phase 2: identifying barriers and facilitators to sport participation in pregnancy

No football-specific papers examining barriers and facilitators to sport participation during pregnancy were identified within the search strategy outlined in Phase 1. However, a recent scoping review of barriers and facilitators to sport participation in elite athletes during pregnancy was identified and used to inform Phase 2.10 Key barriers and facilitators identified by this review included the need to foster supportive environments. Specifically, providing flexible training and support to the athlete, and to provide clear, evidence-based information to athletes, coaches, support staff and organisational staff were found to be needed participation facilitators.10 30 The development and associated implementation of the new FIFA maternity regulations for female football players was identified as a key football-specific facilitator of football participation during pregnancy.9

Phase 3: developing the objective and approach of the decision aid

The FIFA Stay in Play During Pregnancy decision aid was developed with the underlying principle that although players experience significant biological, psychological and social adaptations during their pregnancy, these changes do not need to translate to ceasing all football participation. Based on extensive empirical evidence supporting the health and performance benefits of sport participation, pregnant players should be empowered to continue to train in an adapted fashion unless they screen positive for a contraindication or they choose to cease participation.11 Through discussion at the Zurich meeting, it was decided that the founding principles of this decision aid would: (1) prioritise the athlete voice in all decisions; (2) support safe participation for players; (3) facilitate continued participation in team training when possible; (4) consider pregnancy-related symptoms and dysfunction; (5) recognise football participation requires a cognitive chaos approach and (6) use currently available questionnaires validated for the pregnancy context whenever possible.

Phase 4: identifying key screening components of the decision aid

A flow chart of how to use the FIFA Stay in Play During Pregnancy decision aid is illustrated in figure 1. Following best practice guidelines for physical activity and sport participation in pregnancy, our initial processes confirmed that the development of contraindications would necessitate pausing training until medical consultation has been sought.31 While there have been longstanding concerns about exposure to contact during pregnancy, a recent study found a low risk of adverse outcomes with continued participation in football.3 As there was limited evidence supporting safety or harm after ~12 weeks of pregnancy, we upheld the International Olympic Committee recommendation to discontinue high-intensity, competitive match play after 12 weeks of pregnancy.32 Continued participation in competitive match play after this point should only be considered through shared decision-making and understanding potential risks/benefits. However, because substantially reducing athletic training can increase the risk of pregnancy complications and raise the risk of injury postpartum, continued football training, including exposure to controlled, low-intensity or low-impact contact, is encouraged.12 13 Beyond medical factors, a postpartum-specific Delphi study identified key biopsychosocial considerations to postpartum exercise that the author group applied to pregnancy.33 Combined with the scoping review identified in Phase 2, we did not identify additional screening components. Additional considerations were not identified through the process of consultation with external experts who assessed the content validity or the qualitative responses from the broader consultation with stakeholders. Therefore, the key screening components included identifying contraindications to physical activity during pregnancy, mental health, fear of movement and pelvic health. They also had validated questionnaires to determine whether medical consultation was required. Additional biopsychosocial considerations were identified and incorporated within the decision aid via a yes/no response (eg, poor sleep, social/emotional support) using a check box format.

The selection of tools to assess contraindications, mental health, fear of movement and pelvic health within the decision aid was based on currently available questionnaires and clinical tests validated for pregnancy whenever possible. Global physical activity guidelines recommend screening for contraindications to prenatal exercise. Currently, two screening tools exist; however, the Get Active Questionnaire for Pregnancy was selected as it has established psychometric properties (test–retest reliability score=0.759, inter-rater reliability=0.953, sensitivity=90% and specificity=98.3%)34 35 and has been translated into a dozen languages. Mental health was assessed using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). Although it was primarily developed for postpartum populations, it has been validated for use in pregnancy.36 Importantly, the EPDS is short (10 questions) and includes an embedded anxiety questionnaire. Fear of movement was critical to consider due to the long-standing connection between fear and pregnancy-related lumbopelvic pain, in addition to the newly and importantly established connection related to fear with respect to running and stress urinary incontinence within the perinatal period.37 Two questionnaires were considered to assess fear of movement, the Fear Avoidance Belief Questionnaire (FABQ) and the Tampa Kinesiophobia scale, as both tools have been assessed, deemed to be psychometrically sound and offer comparable screening of fear of movement with regard to both upper and lower extremity musculoskeletal pain. Both scales also triangulate with the related construct of pain catastrophisation.38,40 Although the Tampa scale is cited more frequently within the sports medicine literature and thus more familiar in sport settings, much of the supporting literature pertains to upper extremity sports. Importantly, the Tampa Kinesiophobia scale has not been researched specifically within the context of pregnancy or the broader perinatal period. Alternatively, the FABQ is specifically validated and recommended for use in the context of perinatal lumbopelvic pain41 and for this reason, the Physical Activity Subscale of the FABQ, which is comprised of four questions (questions 2–5), most optimally fits the purpose of the decision aid.41 Further details related to the FABQ can be found in the online supplemental file 1. The authors considered numerous validated questionnaires that have been developed to assess pelvic floor health and screen for pelvic floor dysfunction (PFD). Since the decision aid aims to comprehensively identify factors impacting player participation and performance rather than quantify symptom severity or capture broader health-related consequences, many existing tools were not suitable as they focused on one aspect of PFD, examined severity or assessed quality of life. One of the strongest tools from a psychometric properties standpoint is the International Consultation on Incontinence Short Form (ICIQ-SF).42 This tool is concise, but it only screens for urinary leakage, which is just one aspect of PFD. Although several comprehensive and reliable PFD tools exist, none have been validated with athletes. Further, the comprehensive scope of these tools compromises the ease of administration. Alternatively, the PFD Sentinel is a newly developed tool specific to the female athletic population.31 It is concise (five-symptom checklist) while also comprehensive, identifying a breadth of pelvic floor symptoms. Although not psychometrically43 tested to the degree of the ICIQ-SF, the PFD Sentinel was developed through an international Delphi consensus process, confirming content validity, and pairs with the load and impact battery,36 a series of physical tests aimed to determine readiness of load and impact movements such as running.44 For these reasons, the PFD Sentinel was considered to best fit the purpose of the Stay in Play in Pregnancy Decision Aid. Those who have a positive screen or have any concerns regarding their pelvic health are directed to speak to their healthcare provider (eg, pelvic health physiotherapist) as treatment, including education, pelvic floor muscle training, tissue support (pessary or compression wear) and movement strategy and exercise modification, can facilitate more optimal participation and performance. Further information can be found in the online supplemental file 1.43 After identifying the key screening components, a draft version of the decision aid was developed with four components: (1) general information about the player; (2) screening for health conditions where medical guidance is needed; (3) screening for biopsychosocial considerations to football participation and (4) guidance on football participation during pregnancy (online supplemental table 1). After completing Objective 1, all players move to Objective 2 (figure 2). Objective 2 represents the only component of the decision aid that could directly lead to the guidance that continuing to play is not advisable due to the identification of a contraindication for exercise in pregnancy. Objective 3 (figure 3) informs the support and tailoring aspect of participation, and subsequently, all players move to Objective 4 (figure 4), which provides practical guidance and suggestions on how players can stay in play through the stepwise scheme of progression and regression (figure 5). The online supplemental table 1 and online supplemental file 2 provides practical advice on modalities and training adaptations for the five stages of football participation during pregnancy outlined in figure 4. An onwards communication tool was also developed as both a collaborative document for the decision aid and as a stand-alone tool to support clear communication about decisions between the player and MDT (figure 6). A detailed explanation of the decision aid and the onwards communication tool is presented in the online supplemental file 1. A case study demonstrating the application of the decision aid, communication tool and the five stages of football participation during pregnancy is provided.

Phase 5: preliminary assessment of usability, criterion- and content-validity

Five external subject matter experts in football (a physician, two physiotherapists and two performance coaches) reviewed the documents for content validity. Following their review, additional considerations/components were not identified, but feedback to improve the clarity and format of the decision aid, communication tool and training guidance table was incorporated. 34 respondents, from 12 countries, provided feedback on the decision aid (see table 1). Respondents were 38.2±7.3 years of age, primarily female (82%) with experience at the amateur (29%), semiprofessional (15%) or professional level (56%). Respondents included athletes (35%), healthcare providers (35%), coaches (12%) and support staff (17%). Most respondents (88%) found the decision aid easy to follow. However, based on feedback from the four participants who found it difficult, we addressed these concerns through specific improvements. We developed a flowchart (see figure 1) that outlines a clear pathway of actions based on player responses. Participants also suggested using simpler language and providing more obvious directions for players, such as incorporating globally recognised stop and caution signs, and dedicating a larger section to these instructions. Additional content-related considerations were not identified. This feedback was incorporated into the subsequent version of the decision aid. Respondents indicated that the decision aid (94%), five-stage approach (97%) and the Pregnancy Medical and Performance Debrief Form (100%) would be useful to guide a pregnant player and their MDT from the amateur to professional-level football.

The criterion validity of the decision aid was assessed using a standard case study of a fictitious player where 88% appropriately completed Objective 2 (contraindications). In Objective 3 (biopsychosocial considerations), the appropriate action for mental health (88%), fear of movement (91%), pelvic health (88%) and additional biopsychosocial considerations (100%) was identified. Using the qualitative feedback of the respondents during this consultation phase, additional modifications to the decision aid were made (eg, using simpler language, clearer colour schemes and larger text to direct the action based on the decision aid). Overall, the observed agreement indicated that 94% of the time the observed responses led to the correct actions as laid out by the decision aid. The FIFA Stay in Play During Pregnancy decision aid requires implementation and associated testing to determine the acceptability, feasibility and validity of this decision aid across player levels (amateur vs professional) from around the world.

Discussion

Physical activity during pregnancy is established to be safe and beneficial3 22 45; however, there remains a gap in providing practical guidance to support quality shared decision-making related to maintaining or adapting football participation. The preliminary assessment of the FIFA Stay in Play During Pregnancy decision aid suggested its utility spanning amateur to professional levels. 94% of individuals followed the correct action based on the case study responses to the decision aid. Given the high level of agreement with the usability of this decision aid described herein, it is projected that the decision aid will translate to improved participation and performance for pregnant footballers from amateur to elite levels. The ongoing evaluation of the implementation of the decision aid as per the KTA framework, spanning all seven phases of action, will provide further insight. Longitudinal evaluation of the developed decision aid and associated tools is essential for further refinement and continued evaluation to ensure their use and applicability across cultures and competition levels.

Strengths and limitations

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of the development process of the FIFA Stay in Play During Pregnancy decision aid. First, the surveys and preliminary validation were conducted with a small pool of respondents and drafted in English. Future work to identify the usability across cultures and more diverse populations is required to further refine the decision aid. Currently, there is limited empirical evidence specific to pregnant football players. Despite the fact that this decision aid was designed specifically for football players and takes into consideration position-specific adaptations, the needs of all football players during pregnancy may not be fully captured. As future research evolves, this decision aid will also need to adapt and be tested. It was designed to be flexible to allow for such adaptation. Finally, online supplemental table 1 provides practical advice on modalities and training adaptations for the five stages of football participation during pregnancy. However, the suggestions are not the only options for exercises or adaptations. As such, players and their MDT can use the progression as inspiration and adapt to suit the unique preferences of their athlete. Building on acknowledged limitations, a key strength of this decision aid as it now exists relates to the rigorous and robust development process. By framing the decision aid on implementation science frameworks, we ensured a process that was necessarily iterative to ensure the final tool was not only clinically effective, but also practical and scalable for real-world use. The global perspective of this work represents another strength which was further enhanced through international stakeholder engagement. Such a collaborative, multifaceted approach was essential in creating a tool that is both relevant to football culture and widely applicable.

Clinical implications

This decision aid is designed to support pregnant football players and their MDT in making informed and collaborative decisions regarding continued participation in football during pregnancy. In addition, the development and use of the accompanying Pregnancy Medical and Performance Debrief Form serves to facilitate clear communication and ensure that records of discussions and decisions are documented in a transparent, structured and consistent manner.

Conclusion

The FIFA Stay in Play During Pregnancy decision aid is an evidence-informed tool to support players and their MDT to engage in a shared decision to maintain appropriate football participation during pregnancy. The decision aid, communication tool and table outlining participation guidance are expected to empower players from the amateur to elite levels to make quality decisions during pregnancy.
