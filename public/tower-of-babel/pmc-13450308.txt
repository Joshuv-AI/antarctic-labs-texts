On-chip waveguide-integrated light sources using MoS2/WSe2 moiré superlattices on a silicon photonic platform
Authors: Xingyu Huang, Hanlin Fang, Shima Kadkhodazadeh, Monia Runge Nielsen, Youqiang Huang, Qiaoling Lin, Zhipei Sun, Martijn Wubs, Sanshui Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Moiré superlattices have been widely explored as a unique platform for investigating exotic physics, but their potential applications in integrated photonics remain unexplored. In this work, we demonstrate a waveguide-integrated light source operating at telecom wavelengths with high temporal coherence, enabled by molybdenum disulfide (MoS2)/tungsten diselenide (WSe2) moiré superlattices coupled to a silicon photonic nanobeam cavity. Our results reveal that, at room temperature, the moiré potential in MoS2/WSe2 superlattices strongly boosts light-emission intensity, extends interlayer-exciton lifetime, and redshifts the emission wavelength. Our demonstrated device shows excellent coherence properties with near-unity interference visibility and a coherence length of about 3.8 millimeters, substantially exceeding the performance of previously reported two-dimensional–material light sources. Moreover, these devices exhibit stable performance under ambient conditions, operating within the fiber communication O-band. This work paves the way for leveraging moiré superlattices as a promising material for photonic integrated circuits, highlighting their potential to enhance light-matter interactions and expand the capabilities of on-chip photonic technologies.

INTRODUCTION

The van der Waals structure of two-dimensional (2D) materials enables the creation of atomically thin monolayers, where excitons exhibit large binding energies and excellent tunability, paving the way for studying exciton physics (1) and developing excitonic devices (2–4). Monolayer transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) feature a direct bandgap, enabling efficient light emission (5). In addition, their lack of dangling bonds facilitates stacking of van der Waals heterostructures with emergent properties (6, 7) and seamless integration with various material platforms, e.g., silicon nitride and silicon (8).

In vertically stacked TMD heterobilayers, the type-II band alignment leads to the formation of spatially separated yet bound electron-hole pairs, known as interlayer excitons (IXs) (9, 10). The diverse combinations of TMD monolayers enable IX emissions to cover a very broad wavelength range, from visible to mid-infrared (11). Moreover, IXs can be generated electrically (12), making them suitable for integrated light sources. Coupling IXs to optical cavities has allowed the observation of stimulated emission (13–15). Furthermore, moiré superlattices created in twisted TMD heterostructures can effectively engineer the band structure, tailoring IX emissions (16, 17) and enabling the exploration of novel exciton physics (7, 18, 19). The resulting moiré potential can increase the binding energy and the density of states of IXs, which are advantageous for energy-efficient light source applications (20). However, the photoluminescence (PL) intensity of IXs in moiré superlattices is typically weaker than that of intralayer excitons in monolayers (10, 21–23), especially at room temperature. The potential of moiré superlattices in advancing integrated light sources thus far remains an open question.

In this work, by stacking high-quality MoS2/WSe2 heterobilayers with different twist angles, we observe that both PL intensity and exciton lifetime of IXs increase with decreasing twist angle at room temperature. This unusual twist-angle–dependent behavior is attributed to the exciton localization due to the presence of moiré potential, which effectively suppresses nonradiative recombination. Furthermore, to examine the potential of the brightened IXs in integrated light sources, we design an asymmetric silicon nanobeam cavity that ensures a high quality factor and efficient coupling to a waveguide. The resulting superlattice-cavity devices exhibit narrow single-mode emission in the optical fiber communication (OFC) O-band (1260 to 1360 nm) at low pump powers, with a high side-mode suppression ratio (SMSR). In addition, device performance remains stable during the 9-month measurement period. By performing a Mach-Zehnder interference experiment, an interference visibility of nearly unity and a coherence length of about 3.8 mm are measured. These findings open avenues for integrated light sources, particularly in silicon photonics.

RESULTS

Brighter IX with longer lifetime

The MoS2 and WSe2 monolayers are prepared on polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrates and then stacked to obtain high-quality heterobilayers with different twist angles (0.1° to 2.2°), which are characterized by polarization-resolved second-harmonic generation (SHG) measurements (see fig. S1). We note that all the measurements in this work are performed at room temperature. Figure 1A shows the PL spectra of the MoS2/WSe2 heterobilayers in comparison to those of 1L-WSe2 and 1L-MoTe2, which are characteristic 2D monolayer materials with light emission in the visible and near-infrared wavelength range, respectively. Here, the PL emission of the heterobilayers originates from IXs formed through rapid charge transfer between monolayers because of type-II band alignment (see the inset of Fig. 1A) (24), featuring a strong PL quenching of intralayer excitons (fig. S2). The PL peak intensity of IXs is similar to that of intralayer excitons in 1L-MoTe2 for a twist angle of 2.2° and becomes comparable to that of 1L-WSe2 when the twist angle is reduced to 0.1°. This IX emission is even stronger when comparing integrated PL intensities (fig. S3). The high intensity of the IX peak stands in marked contrast to previous report (21), where IX intensity was found to be comparable to that of intralayer exciton from MoS2/WSe2 heterostructures, a common signature of insufficient charge transfer. Meanwhile, the similar absorption coefficients of MoS2, WSe2, and MoTe2 (25, 26) allow us rule out the possibility that the photoexcited carrier densities at the excitation wavelength of 640 nm would differ much. Moreover, 1L-MoTe2 was freshly exfoliated before the PL measurements, whereby we avoid the effect of sample degradation over time (27).

To understand the physical mechanism dominating such a strong PL emission from dipolar IXs, we characterize the exciton lifetime of the heterobilayers with different twist angles through time-resolved PL measurements (see Fig. 1B). The measured lifetime (τtotal) reflects the total decay rate, which is the aggregate result of competing radiative and nonradiative processes. Theoretically, the radiative exciton lifetime is expected to decrease with twist angle because of the decreasing momentum mismatch (28). For an emitter with unity PL quantum yield, an increase in lifetime is inherently associated with diminished PL intensity. However, we find that the measured IX lifetime and PL intensity at room temperature increase simultaneously with decreasing twist angle and that τtotal reaches about 53 ns at 0.1° (see Fig. 1C), which is substantially larger than that in previous studies (10, 13, 17). This counterintuitive trend suggests that the decay dynamics is predominantly governed by nonradiative recombination and that the observed extension of IX lifetime at smaller twist angles results from the suppression of nonradiative decay, i.e., the notable improvement of the internal quantum yield (see note S1).

Decreasing the twist angle has been found beneficial for the creation of atomic reconstruction, leading to a periodic strain distribution with enhanced moiré potential (17). The resulting deep moiré potential traps can hinder the transport of IXs (29–31) driven by repulsive dipole-dipole interaction (32), thereby reducing the likelihood of their capture by nonradiative centers (see the left panel of Fig. 1D). Here, the creation of a moiré superlattice is visualized by our transmission electron microscopy (TEM) measurements (see fig. S4), and the existence of the moiré potential is further confirmed by the observation of multiple IX states at high pump powers (see fig. S5) (17, 22, 33). We therefore conclude that the unusual twist-angle–dependent exciton lifetime increase can be attributed to the fact that the moiré potential plays an important role in suppressing nonradiative recombinations via localizing IXs (see the right panel of Fig. 1D), thereby boosting the internal quantum yield. MoS2/WSe2 heterobilayers with near-zero twist angles exhibit a combination of prolonged exciton lifetimes, enhanced PL intensity, and emission wavelengths extending into the telecommunication O-band, holding great promise for integrated light sources on the silicon photonics platform.

Asymmetric nanobeam cavity

To explore O-band integrated light sources using MoS2/WSe2 moiré superlattices, we designed an asymmetric silicon photonic crystal nanobeam cavity supporting waveguide coupling (see Fig. 2A for the fabricated device and fig. S6 for the design parameters). Specifically, the cavity mode is aligned with the moiré-enhanced IX emission peak in the O-band regime, ensuring efficient spectral overlap. The number of unit cells on the right side of the cavity [denoted as NR; see Fig. 2A for the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image] is smaller than that on the left side, and the cavity is connected to a silicon waveguide. This design allows us to achieve practically one-sided on-chip emission from the asymmetric cavity into the waveguide. In addition, a silicon taper and an SU-8 waveguide are used to achieve high broadband butt-coupling efficiency (34, 35). The signal from the SU-8 waveguide is collected by a lensed fiber and connected to a spectrometer (see Fig. 2B). The bottom inset of Fig. 2A shows the localized resonant mode around the cavity center and the evanescent coupling into the silicon waveguide. The cross-polarization scattering method (36) is commonly used to measure the optical modes of a photonic cavity. However, it becomes ineffective for our cavities, since they are not underetched. Instead, we characterize the cavity mode by measuring silicon PL under a relatively high optical pump power (up to 5 mW) because of the indirect bandgap of silicon.

The existence of the cavity mode with waveguide coupling is confirmed by the observation of a single emission peak. We note that the precise evaluation of the cavity Q-factor is largely limited by the spectral resolution of the grating spectrometer. A grating (150 grooves/mm) is used to detect the cavity mode excited by the weak PL signal of silicon. Therefore, the measured Q-factors are lower than the true values of the cavities. With this cavity design, increasing NR enhances the Q-factor, while the waveguide-coupled PL signal correspondingly decreases. Figure 2C illustrates the dependence of the cavity Q-factor on NR. To obtain a feasible coupling efficiency while maintaining a relatively high Q-factor, we strategically reduce NR and use a large number of periods on the left side (NL = 35). NR = 20 is chosen because it contributes to a relatively high Q-factor (see Fig. 2C) and an efficient waveguide coupling (72%), evidenced by the cavity-enhanced PL of silicon (see Fig. 2D). The overall coupling efficiency to the lensed optical fiber can be further enhanced by optimizing the cavity-waveguide coupling, the waveguide taper profile, and the SU-8 cladding geometry (34, 37).

Waveguide-integrated light sources

MoS2/WSe2 heterobilayers with near-zero twist angles are chosen to ensure light emission in the O-band and are then transferred to the top surface of the asymmetric cavity by the van der Waals pickup method. To improve the mode overlap between IXs and the cavity, we adopted only the top hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) layer rather than the typical sandwich encapsulation structure (22, 33, 38). Figure 3 (A and B) shows the schematic diagram and the optical micrograph of the device, respectively. We emphasize that the coverage of the heterostructure does not affect the formation of the cavity mode, which is confirmed by the PL mapping measurements. The bottom plot of Fig. 3B presents that the cavity-enhanced emission is spatially confined within an area of ∼1.5 μm by 1.5 μm at the center of the cavity.

Figure 3C shows the PL spectra obtained via free-space and waveguide coupling. Compared to the free-space PL signal, the spectrum acquired through waveguide coupling exhibits a markedly lower background (SMSR ∼ 21) while maintaining nearly the same peak intensity at the cavity mode. This contrast indicates that the background in the free-space collection originating from heterobilayer regions is not efficiently coupled to the cavity. Photons emitted by IXs at wavelengths resonant with the cavity are (i) spatially confined and enhanced by the cavity and (ii) efficiently redirected into the waveguide via evanescent coupling. As a result, our asymmetric cavity design not only enhances the IX PL emission but also improves the spectral purity of the cavity-enhanced PL intensity in the OFC O-band. Similar characteristics are observed in other devices that we fabricated (see fig. S7). Furthermore, the device demonstrates strong linear polarization (see the inset of Fig. 3C and fig. S8), suggesting efficient coupling of IX emissions into the cavity mode and highlighting its potential for polarization-sensitive photonic applications, including quantum optics and optical communication systems.

To systematically investigate the output properties of the device, we vary the pump power and record the PL spectra using a grating (1200 grooves/mm; see Fig. 4A). This grating provides a high spectral resolution of ∼0.1 nm for characterizing the emission linewidth of the cavity mode. As the pump power increases, the peak intensity of the cavity mode grows substantially faster than the PL background (see also fig. S9), resulting in a high SMSR crucial for optical communication. Notably, the cavity mode centered at 1294 nm consistently dominates the PL spectra across all pump powers. By fitting the emission peak with a Lorentzian function (see fig. S10), we extract the linewidth (Δλ) and wavelength (λ) (see Fig. 4B). At a low pump power of 6.4 μW, a linewidth of ∼0.2 nm is obtained. This value is narrower than that reported for free-space–coupled TMD-based light sources (39). Crucially, the negligible optical absorption of the heterobilayer (40) ensures that the total Q-factor is dominated by the cavity, thereby preserving this narrow emission linewidth. Furthermore, both the linewidth and wavelength remain nearly constant as the pump power increases, indicating that the device performs well as a heat sink. The operating lifetime of the device is another figure of merit for practical device applications. To validate the long-term robustness of the device, we monitored the waveguide-coupled PL over 9 months under ambient conditions. As quantified in Fig. 4C, the variation of the PL intensities is negligible, confirming that the device is robust against air oxidation. This operational resilience positions the superlattice-cavity devices as promising candidates for reliable integrated light sources. To characterize the coherent property of the device, we use a fiber-coupled Mach-Zehnder interferometer (see fig. S11A) and record the emission intensity of the cavity mode as a function of the path delay (see fig. S11B). Figure 4D shows a strong periodic modulation of intensity with optical delay path, yielding an interference visibility of nearly unity around the zero-delay position. The coherence length of about 3.8 mm is measured by further increasing the path delay while monitoring the visibility and fitting the data with a Gaussian function, as shown in Fig. 4E. Device 2 (∼0.5° twist angle) similarly exhibits near-unity visibility and a coherence length of 3.0 mm under the same pump power of 128 μW (see fig. S12). These coherence lengths substantially exceed those of previously reported room-temperature 2D-material devices (41). This long coherence length (Lc) is a direct consequence of the integrated high-Q cavity and extended emission wavelength, as theoretically governed by the relationship Lc∝λ2/Δλ=λ⋅Q (42).

DISCUSSION

In summary, we demonstrated that moiré superlattices offer advantages for integrated light sources, featuring enhanced light emission and prolonged lifetime at telecom wavelengths. Through precise twist angle control of the MoS2/WSe2 moiré bilayers, we successfully redshift the IX emission peak beyond 1300 nm, aligning it with the standard OFC O-band. These demonstrated superlattice-cavity devices exhibit a pronounced cavity mode in the OFC O-band, with an SMSR of up to 25 and a narrow linewidth of 0.2 nm at a pump power as low as 6.4 μW. Furthermore, they exhibit ideal interference fringes and a long coherence length, making them well suited for optoelectronic applications such as information encoding with optical modulators (43). Further studies on improving the PL quantum yield of moiré superlattices with posttreatment processes (44) and strain engineering (45) could potentially improve the performance for the IX-based integrated light source operating in the OFC C-band (1530 to 1565 nm). In addition, the cavity design with a small footprint is beneficial for developing ultracompact light source arrays. Last but not least, device performance remains stable for at least 9 months of observation.

Furthermore, spatially dependent lifetime measurements (fig. S13) indicate that moiré superlattices away from the cavity exhibit IX lifetimes on the order of several nanoseconds, notably shorter than tens-of-nanoseconds lifetimes observed on PDMS (Fig. 1C). This marked reduction in lifetime is mainly ascribed to the emergence of nonradiative recombination centers during the transfer process. The blue dots in fig. S13 (C and D) represent the IX peak intensity after excluding cavity effects, revealing spatial inhomogeneity in the density of nonradiative recombination centers. For the moiré superlattices on the cavity, we also observe a strong reduction in IX lifetime and intrinsic emission intensity (fig. S13, A and B). However, the fabrication stochasticity prevents us from distinguishing the contributions of Purcell-enhanced decay from those of extrinsic defects. These uncertainties also hinder a systematic investigation of device performance as a function of the moiré twist angle. We anticipate that future improvements in fabrication will yield brighter IXs with extended lifetimes, creating a promising pathway toward high-efficiency devices. Our results position this architecture as a strong candidate for integrated light sources featuring on-demand wavelength emission.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample fabrication

The monolayers of MoTe2, MoS2 and WSe2 that we studied here were exfoliated from flux bulk material (2D semiconductors) on the PDMS stamps using the standard Scotch tape method (46). The MoS2/WSe2 heterostructure was stacked using an all-dry transfer technique (47). Care was taken to rotationally align the MoS2 and WSe2 monolayers based on their clear and straight edge under the optical microscope. The MoS2/WSe2 heterostructure was encapsulated on the top with hBN using the van der Waals pick-up method and subsequently transferred to the silicon cavity. This top-side hBN encapsulation was specifically chosen to prevent degradation and contamination while maximizing the coupling efficiency between the MoS2/WSe2 heterostructure and the silicon cavity.

The silicon nanobeam cavity and the inverse taper were fabricated on a 220-nm silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafer from Soitec. A 180-nm layer of CSAR electron beam resist was spin coated (4000 rpm for 60 s) on the silicon substrate. The desired pattern was defined in the resist using electron-beam lithography (JEOL 9500; 100 kV) and subsequently transferred into the 220-nm silicon layer via reactive ion etching. The residual resist was removed using 1165 solvent, followed by gentle oxygen plasma cleaning. To fabricate the SU-8 polymer waveguides (5 μm–by–5 μm cross section), a 5-μm-thick ultraviolet (UV) photoresist (SU-8 2005) was spin coated onto the existing silicon structures. The pattern was exposed using UV lithography (Maskless Aligner 150, Heidelberg Instruments), developed, subjected to UV flood exposure (MA6 Aligner, SÜSS MicroTec), and then hard baked at 90°C for 15 hours to prevent SU-8 waveguide breakage (35).

Optical measurements

All measurements were conducted at room temperature. PL was excited using a 640-nm continuous-wave laser (PicoQuant LDH-IB-640B) focused through a 50× objective lens (numerical aperture = 0.65; Olympus LCPLN50XIR), yielding a spot size of ∼1.5 μm. The emitted signal was filtered by long-pass spectral filters and dispersed using a Czerny-Turner monochromator (Andor SR-500i) equipped with a cooled InGaAs array detector (Andor DU491A-1.7). For waveguide-coupled PL characterization, a lensed fiber (3.5-μm spot diameter; OZ Optics) was aligned to the SU-8 waveguide under the same 50× objective. A grating (150 grooves/mm) was applied for wide-range spectra measurements, and the finer gratings (1200 grooves/mm) were mainly applied for cavity mode property analysis.

Time-resolved PL used a time-correlated single-photon counting system. Samples were excited by a 640-nm pulsed laser (PicoQuant LDH-IB-640B; <90-ps pulse width and 10-MHz repetition rate). The emission signal was fiber coupled to a home-built filtering system (0.2-nm bandwidth), whose operational wavelength range was adjusted by rotating the grating. The filtered signal was subsequently directed to a single-photon detector (PDM-IR) from micro photon devices (MPD). The coherence length of the light source was measured using a fiber-coupled Mach-Zehnder interferometer. By scanning the retroreflector to vary the optical path difference, the interference signal was recorded by a single-photon detector (MPD PDM-IR).

TEM measurements

High-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) images were recorded using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Spectra instrument equipped with a field emission electron gun and aberration correction. The TEM instrument was operated at 120 kV to avoid severe knock-on damage to the sample. The electron probe convergence angle and diameter were ∼24 mrad and 170 pm, respectively, and the HAADF detector collection angle was set to ∼45 mrad. The acquired image (see fig. S4) was denoised in the postprocessing using an open-source deep convolutional neural network approach using tk_r_em, a Python toolkit based on TensorFlow, which is proposed in (48).

Numerical simulation

The photonic cavity design adopts a topological nanobeam architecture, first introduced in the pioneering work (49). Building on our previous design (17, 27), we retained the essential SiO2 substrate layer, which ensures both mechanical stability for van der Waals material integration and enhanced heat dissipation capability. Using finite-difference time-domain simulations (Lumerical, Ansys Inc.), we systematically optimized the design by sweeping NR and recording the Q-factor to achieve a good trade-off between Q-factor and waveguide coupling efficiency.
