Diagnostic accuracy of CT-based radiomics models in differentiating lung cancer from tuberculosis in pulmonary lesions: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hadi Sahrai, Jamal Behnood, Mansoureh Baradaran, Amirreza Khalaji, Ali Norouzi, Farzaneh Shojaeshafiei, Seyedeh Mahdieh Seyed Ebrahimi, Sanam Mohammadzadeh, Zanyar HajiEsmailpoor, Ramin Shahidi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDistinguishing lung cancer (LC) from pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) on CT is difficult. We synthesized evidence on radiomics, clinical, and combined models for LC–TB discrimination.MethodsPubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Scopus were searched to August 2025 following PRISMA. Metrics were harmonized to LC-positive/TB-negative and pooled with a bivariate random-effects model. Study quality was assessed with QUADAS-2 and METRICS.ResultsFourteen retrospective studies (4281 participants) were included. Radiomics models (11 validations cohorts) achieved pooled sensitivity 0.80 (95% CI 0.74–0.86) and specificity 0.83 (0.75–0.88); SROC AUC 0.88 (0.85–0.91). At a 25% pre-test probability, radiomics models corresponded to post-test probabilities of 61% and 7%. Deeks’ funnel asymmetry test showed no small-study effects (p = 0.88). Clinical-only models performed more modestly (sensitivity 0.60, specificity 0.80, AUC 0.77). Combined radiomics + clinical models performed best (sensitivity 0.82, specificity 0.93, AUC 0.90). Head-to-head comparison showed higher specificity for radiomics versus clinical models (p = 0.02), and higher sensitivity for combined models versus radiomics (p < 0.001) without a clear specificity difference (p = 0.41). Prespecified subgroup analyses indicated that models retaining > 10 radiomic features, developed in cohorts restricted to nodules < 3 cm, and using non-contrast CT tended to perform better, whereas externally validated cohorts showed lower accuracy than internal test sets, and both nodule-size spectrum and CT acquisition phase emerged as major contributors to between-study heterogeneity.ConclusionsCT-based radiomics adds discriminative value beyond clinical variables, and integrating clinical information with radiomics yields the most favorable overall accuracy while highlighting the importance of broader external validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15446-5.Keywords: Radiomics, Lung cancer, Tuberculosis, Artificial intelligence, Computed tomography

Introduction

Lung cancer (LC) and tuberculosis (TB) persist as the leading causes of lung disease-related death in developing countries, demanding urgent attention. Both conditions are closely linked to regional variations in the human development index [1]. According to the 2020 Global Cancer Statistics (GLOBOCAN), lung cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer worldwide in 2022 [2]. Lung cancer also remained the leading cause of cancer-related deaths, accounting for 18% of cancer-related mortality and 11.4% of new patients globally [3]. Notably, the overlapping clinical presentations of LC and TB often lead to misdiagnosis, with LC frequently mistaken for pulmonary TB. This diagnostic challenge can delay appropriate treatment, resulting in the administration of ineffective therapies [4].

Chronic TB inflammation, a recognized potential carcinogenic factor, is a pressing area of research in the co-occurrence of TB and LC [5, 6]. Previous investigations have shown that TB and LC can manifest simultaneously in a small proportion of cases [7, 8]. The complexity of this relationship is further demonstrated by the immunosuppressive effects of various lung cancer therapeutics, which can precipitate mycobacterial infection [9, 10]. Contemporary challenges in differential diagnosis have arisen from two main factors: the widespread use of antibiotics and an aging population. These factors have led to a higher incidence of irregular pulmonary masses in TB patients. These masses can exhibit features that resemble peripheral lung malignancies, which may cause confusion when distinguishing them from neoplastic disease [11].

Computed tomography (CT) serves as a fundamental imaging tool in the differential diagnosis of LC and TB [12]. Despite the expertise of skilled radiologists, the similar radiological characteristics between LC and TB frequently lead to misdiagnosis based on CT findings [13]. Several chest CT features commonly associated with TB, including nodular patterns, consolidation, irregular margins, and spiculations can closely mimic primary LC [14]. Moreover, in patients with known cancer, non-calcified granulomas with tubercular features may be misinterpreted as metastatic nodules [15]. These diagnostic challenges underscore the urgent need for alternative, non-invasive techniques to improve the differential diagnosis of LC and TB.

Radiomics represents an innovative radiological approach that transforms qualitative multimodal imaging analysis into quantitative data, using high-throughput feature extraction and machine learning algorithms to construct predictive models and nomograms [16, 17]. By integrating discriminative imaging features with these computational methods, radiomics can improve disease diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment-response assessment [18, 19], and several studies have shown radiomics-based analyses to outperform traditional radiologist interpretation in specific diagnostic tasks [20–22]. when applied to lung masses, this non-invasive technique captures key biological characteristics, including gene expression, internal heterogeneity, cell morphology, and molecular phenotypes, thereby facilitating more accurate characterization of ambiguous lesions [23–25]. Recent investigations have specifically leveraged quantitative radiomic features from CT images to enhance the differential diagnosis between LC and TB, supporting the role of advanced image-analysis techniques in distinguishing lung cancer from tuberculosis and improving diagnostic precision in patients with solitary solid pulmonary nodules [4, 26, 27].

This study aims to evaluate the diagnostic performance of radiomics models for differentiating LC from TB. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first systematic review and meta-analysis differentiating LC and TB using diagnostic radiomics models.

Methods and materials

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Diagnostic Test Accuracy Studies (PRISMA-DTA) guidelines [28] and focused on studies that assessed the accuracy of radiomics in differentiating LC from TB [29]. The review was registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024619748).

Search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Scopus databases from inception to August 29, 2025, in accordance with PRISMA-DTA guidelines. We applied the following query: (Lung[tiab] OR Pulmonary[tiab]) AND (Cancer*[tiab] OR Carcinoma*[tiab] OR Adenocarcinoma[tiab] OR Neoplas*[tiab] OR Nodule*[tiab] OR Mass*[tiab]) AND (“Radiomics”[mesh] OR Radiomic*[tiab]) AND (Tuberculosis[tiab]). Following the database searches, the retrieved records were imported into the Rayyan platform, and duplicate entries were systematically removed prior to screening [30]. We used EndNote reference management software to organize the citations. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, and then assessed the full texts of potentially eligible articles against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Finally, we manually reviewed the reference lists of all included studies to identify any additional relevant articles. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion with a senior author (HS).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We applied the following inclusion criteria: (i) Original human studies. (ii) Development or evaluation of a CT-based radiomics model derived from pre-treatment thoracic CT (non-contrast or contrast-enhanced) to differentiate LC from TB. (iii) Reporting, or allowing derivation, of diagnostic accuracy metrics (sensitivity, specificity, or sufficient data to construct a 2 × 2 table). (iv) Use of a reference standard independent of the index test, with histopathology for LC and microbiological and/or histopathological confirmation for TB. (v) Full-length, peer-reviewed articles. We applied the following exclusion criteria: (i) Review articles, commentaries, editorials, letters, case reports, and conference abstracts. (ii) Studies not directly evaluating radiomics-based differentiation between LC and TB. (iii) Non-human studies or purely technical/methodological radiomics papers without clinical diagnostic accuracy data.

Data extraction

After completion of the search and screening, we extracted data using standardized Microsoft Excel forms. Two reviewers independently collected study characteristics, radiomics model characteristics, and outcome data for meta-analysis. The study-level extraction items included first author and year; country; study design; number of recruiting centers; case counts for tuberculosis and lung cancer; validation cohort type (internal, external, both, or not reported); nodule-size criterion; and reference standards for TB and LC. Imaging and modeling variables included scanner manufacturer; CT acquisition phase; modeling type (ML or DL); region-of-interest (ROI) dimensionality; ROI segmentation method and software; feature-extraction software; counts of selected versus extracted features; feature-selection algorithm; and the final classifier used.

For quantitative synthesis, we recorded the number of positive and negative cases, sensitivity, specificity, and the 2 × 2 table components: true positives (TP), false negatives (FN), true negatives (TN), and false positives (FP). To standardize class orientation, we first documented each study’s definition of the positive class and then harmonized all metrics to LC-positive/TB-negative. In studies that defined TB as the positive class, we converted the reported metrics to an LC-positive framework by swapping sensitivity and specificity and remapping the 2 × 2 confusion matrices. All extracted information was checked by a senior author to ensure completeness and accuracy. For each study, we used the performance metrics of the best-performing AI model. When required data were not explicitly reported, we derived the missing values using the top-left method [31, 32], that is, by selecting the operating point on the ROC curve closest to the ideal top-left corner and reconstructing the 2 × 2 table from the corresponding sensitivity, specificity, and sample size. We rounded decimals to the nearest whole number, which may result in minor discrepancies relative to the original reports.

Quality assessment

Two authors independently examined the included studies using two separate quality assessment tools: the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2), and the METhodological RadiomICs Score (METRICS).

We assessed the risk of bias and applicability issues with the QUADAS-2 tool [33]. The QUADAS-2 assesses four categories in each article: patient selection, index test, reference test, and flow and timing, using signaling questions tailored to the review question. Further, we used a novel quality assessment tool for radiomics studies, METRICS, to evaluate the radiomics process of the papers included [34]. This scoring system assesses the methodology of radiomics studies through 30 questions across nine fields (provided in Supplementary material), such as study design, imaging data, image processing and feature extraction, metrics and comparison, testing, feature processing, modeling preparation, segmentation, and open science. Items and classifications are prioritized in descending order of value. One of the following rating categories is applied to the included studies: 0 ≤ score < 20%, which indicates “very low” quality; 20 ≤ score < 40%, which indicates “low” quality; 40 ≤ score < 60%, which indicates “moderate” quality; 60 ≤ score < 80%, which indicates “good” quality; and 80 ≤ score ≤ 100%, which indicates “excellent” quality. We calculated METRICS scores using the online calculator (https://metricsscore.github.io/metrics/METRICS.html).

Statistical analysis

We used STATA 15 software for meta-analysis. We pooled the primary diagnostic metrics (sensitivity, specificity, and summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC)) of radiomics-based, clinical and combined models with a 95% confidence interval. We employed the I² statistic to estimate levels of heterogeneity. Values of 0–25%, 25–50%, 50–75%, and 75–100% indicate very low, low, moderate, and high degrees of heterogeneity among the studies, respectively [35]. We explored heterogeneity using six prespecified moderators: (i) segmentation strategy (automatic/assisted vs. manual), (ii) selected-feature count (>10 vs. ≤ 10), and (iii) validation setting (independent external test set vs. internal-only), (iv) nodule-size spectrum (studies restricted to nodules < 3 cm vs. those including larger or mixed-size nodules), (v) primary modeling algorithm (logistic-regression–only models vs. multiple-model or non-logistic approaches), and (vi) CT acquisition protocol (non-contrast CT only vs. contrast-enhanced or multiphase CT). Subgroup analyses were restricted to radiomics-based models; clinical-only and combined (radiomics + clinical) models were synthesized using overall pooling only. The Deek’s funnel and Fagan plots were created to evaluate publication bias and clinical relevance. We used validation cohorts; when a study reported both internal and external validation metrics, we preferentially extracted external validation results to enhance generalizability. To avoid optimism from apparent performance, we excluded “entire-cohort” estimates from the primary meta-analysis and pooled only held-out internal and external validation cohorts.

Results

Study selection

The database search identified 155 unique records (Scopus = 31; Web of Science = 31; PubMed = 25; Embase = 68). After removal of 71 duplicates, 84 records underwent title/abstract screening. Of these, 44 were excluded (title screening = 39; abstract screening = 5). We assessed 40 full-text articles for eligibility and excluded 26 with reasons: no direct LC–TB discrimination (n = 15), abstract only (n = 3), TB vs. metastasis (n = 1), no radiomics workflow (n = 2), overlapping cohorts (n = 3), and no CT-only metrics (n = 2). Finally, fourteen studies met inclusion criteria for qualitative synthesis and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram of study selection

Study characteristics

Fourteen retrospective studies met inclusion criteria, enrolling 4281 participants (TB = 1,940; LC = 2,341). Most were conducted in China (13/14) with one study from India [36], and recruitment settings were evenly divided between single-center [4, 11, 36–40] and multicenter designs [26, 41–46] (7/14 each). Five studies—each multicenter—reported both internal and external validation [26, 41–44], six reported internal-only evaluation [4, 37, 39, 40, 45, 46], and three did not report a distinct validation cohort [11, 36, 38]. Nodule-size criteria were heterogeneous across studies: most cohorts restricted lesions to < 3 cm (five studies) [26, 37, 41, 44, 46], with additional thresholds of < 4 cm (three studies) [38, 40, 45] and < 8 cm (one study) [39]; one cohort specified ≥ 3.0 cm [11], and another allowed 1–8 cm [43]. Three studies did not report a size limit [4, 36, 42]. LC was confirmed pathologically in all studies, while TB was primarily confirmed microbiologically and/or histopathologically, with occasional clinical corroboration. Across these studies, radiomics pipelines were broadly similar. Machine-learning classifiers predominated, with one deep-learning classifier [43] and one study not reporting model type [36]. ROIs were mostly 3D (12/14) with limited 2D use [11, 38]. Segmentation was primarily manual, with semi-automatic [41, 46] and automatic [26] approaches less common. Most analyses used non-contrast CT alone; multiphase or enhanced protocols appeared in three studies [11, 44, 45], arterial-only in one [42], and phase was not reported in one [4]. Scanner vendors were diverse (Siemens, GE, Toshiba/Canon, Philips), reflecting real-world heterogeneity. The most frequent segmentation tools were ITK-SNAP and 3D Slicer (five studies each). Feature extraction most often used PyRadiomics (four studies) or Artificial Intelligence Kit (two studies), with single-study use of MaZda, IBEX, SERA, in-house pipelines, 3D Slicer, uAI Research Portal, Python and was not reported in one study. LASSO was the dominant feature-selection method, typically following univariate screening (correlation, ICC, Mann–Whitney U, mRMR). Logistic regression was the prevailing classifier, with occasional naïve Bayes, SVM, and random forest comparators. Reported selected-feature counts ranged from 5 to 23, while extracted-feature counts ranged from 107 to 3,668. Imaging and model-level details are provided in Table 2. Across the included studies, the final radiomics signatures were dominated by texture-based features, particularly those derived from gray-level co-occurrence (GLCM), run-length (GLRLM), size-zone (GLSZM), and related dependence or difference matrices (GLDM, GLDZM, NGLDM, NGTDM). First-order intensity statistics and shape descriptors were also frequently retained, often in combination with basic intensity histogram or gradient-derived features. Quality assessment

QUADAS-2

Risk of bias on QUADAS-2 was favorable overall (Fig. 2). Patient selection was low risk in all studies. For the index test, 7 studies were low risk, 4 were unclear, and 3 were high risk. The reference standard domain was low risk in all studies. Flow and timing was low risk in 7 studies, unclear in 4, and high risk in 3. Applicability concerns were minimal: patient selection and reference standard showed low concern in all studies, while the index test showed low concern in 11 studies and high concern in 3 studies.

QUADAS-2 risk-of-bias and applicability summary

METRICS

Methodological quality by METRICS was acceptable overall (scores 34.2%–94.5%; five studies rated excellent, five good, two moderate, two low; Fig. 3). The main shortfalls were concentrated in a few domains: multicenter datasets were not universal; the interval between imaging and the reference standard was often not reported and fully automated segmentation was rare. Descriptions of preprocessing, use of standardized feature-extraction software, and transparency of extraction parameters were inconsistent across studies. End-to-end deep learning pipelines were uncommon and, when used, lacked formal robustness assessment. Filtering of non-robust features was variably applied, and explicit handling of potential confounders appeared in only a subset of reports. External testing was present in several but not all studies. Open-science practices were notably limited: only one study provided data access, two shared code, and none released deployable model packages. Detailed scoring is provided in Table 2S.

Meta-analysis of diagnostic performance

Radiomics models

Radiomics models showed good diagnostic accuracy. Across 11 validation cohorts, the pooled sensitivity was 0.80 (95% CI 0.74–0.86) and specificity was 0.83 (95% CI 0.75–0.88), with moderate heterogeneity for both metrics (I² for sensitivity 72%, I² for specificity 66%) (Fig. 4). The SROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.88 (95% CI 0.85–0.91) (Fig. 5). On a Fagan nomogram with a 25% pre-test probability, a positive result produced an LR + of about 5 and a post-test probability of 61%, while a negative result produced an LR − of about 0.24 and a post-test probability of 7% (Fig. 6). Deeks’ test indicated no small-study effects (p = 0.88) (Fig. 7). In subgroup analyses, adding an automatic algorithm to segmentation performed similarly to manual-only workflows: sensitivity 0.85 vs. 0.78 and specificity 0.87 vs. 0.80; differences were not significant, and the joint bivariate contrast was also null (LRT χ² = 1.82, p = 0.40). Models constructed with more than 10 selected features tended to perform better than those using 10 or fewer, with pooled sensitivity 0.83 vs. 0.79 and specificity 0.96 vs. 0.77; although univariable contrasts were not individually significant, the joint model confirmed an overall effect (LRT χ² = 16.22, p < 0.001). Studies evaluated on an independent external test set showed lower pooled values—sensitivity 0.76 and specificity 0.78—than studies reporting internal validation only, which yielded 0.84 and 0.87; these univariable differences were significant, but the effect did not persist in the joint model (LRT χ² = 2.95, p = 0.23). When stratified by nodule-size spectrum, studies restricted to small nodules (< 3 cm) showed higher pooled sensitivity and specificity than those including larger or mixed-size nodules (0.83 vs. 0.72 and 0.85 vs. 0.82, respectively); although single-parameter contrasts for sensitivity and specificity were not statistically significant (p = 0.33 and p = 0.37), nodule size significantly contributed to between-study heterogeneity in the joint model (LRT χ² = 25.54, p < 0.001; I² = 92%). Classification performance was broadly similar across primary modeling approaches: studies using logistic-regression–only models had pooled sensitivity and specificity of 0.81 and 0.82, compared with 0.78 and 0.86 in studies using multiple or non-logistic classifiers; while specificity was marginally higher in the latter group (p = 0.03), this difference was not retained in the multivariable joint model (LRT χ² = 0.92, p = 0.63). Subgrouping by CT acquisition protocol suggested comparable sensitivity between non-contrast-only and contrast-enhanced or multiphase CT (0.79 vs. 0.78) and a trend toward lower specificity for contrast-enhanced/multiphase studies (0.77 vs. 0.86); again, univariable tests for sensitivity and specificity were not significant (p = 0.11 and p = 0.76), but CT phase emerged as an important driver of heterogeneity in the joint model (LRT χ² = 16.43, p < 0.001; I² = 88%) (Fig. 8).

Clinical models

Clinical (non-radiomics) models showed only fair discrimination. Across six studies, the pooled sensitivity was 0.60 (95% CI 0.49–0.71) and the pooled specificity was 0.80 (95% CI 0.71–0.87), with substantial heterogeneity for sensitivity (I² = 80.87%) and moderate heterogeneity for specificity (I² = 61.44%); individual study estimates are displayed in the paired forest plots (Fig. 9). The summary ROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.77 (95% CI 0.73–0.81), underscoring modest overall accuracy for distinguishing LC-positive from TB-negative cases (Fig. 10).

Combined models

Combined models that integrate radiomics with clinical variables achieved the strongest overall accuracy. Across six studies, the pooled sensitivity was 0.82 (95% CI 0.76–0.87) and the pooled specificity was 0.93 (95% CI 0.84–0.97). Between-study heterogeneity was low for sensitivity (p = 0.08; I² = 49.6%) and high for specificity (p < 0.001; I² = 82.5%). Study-level estimates are shown in the paired forest plots (Fig. 11). The summary ROC curve demonstrated AUC = 0.90 (95% CI 0.87–0.92), indicating excellent discrimination between LC-positive and TB-negative cases (Fig. 12).

Comparative performance of radiomics, clinical, and combined models

Across model families, combined (radiomics + clinical) models achieved the highest overall accuracy, radiomics-only models performed well, and clinical-only models were modest. In a bivariate meta-regression directly contrasting radiomics vs. clinical approaches, Sensitivities were statistically indistinguishable between radiomics and clinical models (0.80 vs. 0.61; p = 0.97), despite the higher point estimate for radiomics, whereas specificity was higher for radiomics significantly. (0.83 vs. 0.80; p = 0.02). The joint model supported an overall difference between these families (likelihood-ratio test χ² = 8.20, p = 0.02) with residual heterogeneity (I² = 76%). In the comparison of combined vs. radiomics models, sensitivity was higher for combined models (0.83 vs. 0.80; pₛₑₙₛ = 0.00), while specificity did not differ statistically despite a favorable point estimate (0.92 vs. 0.83; pₛₚₑ = 0.41). The joint model did not detect a global difference (χ² = 3.77, p = 0.15; I² = 47%). Taken together with the family-wise SROC findings (AUC: combined 0.90; radiomics 0.88; clinical 0.77), these results indicate that CT-based radiomics adds discriminative value over clinical features alone, and that integrating clinical variables with radiomics further improves sensitivity without a clear trade-off in specificity.

Discussion

Our meta-analysis demonstrates that CT-based radiomics provides robust diagnostic performance for differentiating lung cancer from tuberculosis, with combined radiomics-clinical models achieving the highest accuracy (sensitivity 0.82, specificity 0.93, AUC 0.90). Our radiomics-only models also demonstrated strong performance (sensitivity 0.80, specificity 0.83, AUC 0.88), outperforming clinical-only models (AUC 0.77) and showing comparable or superior results to other lung nodule characterization meta-analyses, such as Shi et al. [47] who reported AUC 0.91 for malignant versus benign pulmonary nodules. These findings align with similar performance reported across diverse clinical applications, including gastric cancer lymph node metastasis prediction (AUC 0.90) [48], ovarian cancer differential diagnosis (AUC 0.90) [49], and breast cancer applications (AUC 0.90) [50].

The comparative meta-regression analysis revealed important insights into the incremental value of different modeling approaches, showing that radiomics models achieve significantly higher specificity than clinical models (p = 0.02) while maintaining sensitivity, which is crucial for reducing false-positive findings and avoiding unnecessary invasive procedures. In TB-endemic clinical practice, this improvement in specificity means that TB granulomas mimicking cancer are less likely to be labeled as malignant, thereby reducing referrals for unnecessary invasive biopsies or surgical resections. At the same time, preserving good sensitivity helps limit the opposite scenario in which lung cancer is misclassified as TB, which can delay oncologic treatment and expose patients to prolonged, ultimately unnecessary antituberculosis regimens. Furthermore, combined models demonstrated a significant sensitivity improvement over radiomics-only models (p < 0.001) without compromising specificity, highlighting the complementary nature of radiomics and clinical information. This pattern of superior performance for combined approaches is consistently observed across multiple meta-analyses, including gastric cancer lymph node prediction [48], and intracerebral hemorrhage prognosis [51] suggesting a general principle that integrating imaging-based radiomics features with clinical data maximizes diagnostic accuracy and clinical utility across diverse medical scenarios.

The subgroup analysis showing that models with >10 selected features demonstrated superior performance contrasts with conventional radiomics practice, in which studies typically retain fewer than 10 features despite initially extracting hundreds. Ge et al. [52] reported that the average number of selected features in CT lung cancer radiomics studies is < 10. The superior performance of models with >10 features in our analysis suggests that such aggressive feature reduction may be overly conservative for LC–TB differentiation, potentially discarding valuable discriminative information. This may reflect the complex pathophysiological differences between lung cancer and tuberculosis, which likely require a more comprehensive feature set to capture subtle imaging patterns that distinguish these entities.

In addition, the finding that nodule size significantly contributed to between-study heterogeneity (I² = 92%) is noteworthy. High heterogeneity is a well-recognized challenge in radiomics meta-analyses [53, 54], and one study has shown that radiomic features from smaller nodules are more sensitive to variations in CT acquisition parameters, which may further exacerbate heterogeneity across studies [55]. Taken together, these results indicate that nodule size functions not only as a performance-modifying factor but also as a primary source of variability that should be explicitly accounted for in future radiomics research.

Our subgroup analysis also revealed an important signal regarding CT acquisition protocols. The trend toward lower specificity in contrast-enhanced studies (0.77 vs. 0.86) is consistent with evidence that non-contrast CT can yield better diagnostic performance for radiomic signatures in pulmonary nodules, as contrast enhancement may obscure underlying texture features [56]. Moreover, CT phase was identified as a major driver of between-study heterogeneity (I² = 88%, p < 0.001), in line with extensive evidence that variation in acquisition protocols is a key barrier to radiomics reproducibility [57].

Finally, the observation that external validation settings showed lower pooled estimates on univariable analysis, although not significant after adjustment, is consistent with well-documented challenges in radiomics generalizability. Hatt et al. [58] highlighted that multicentric radiomic studies face significant challenges due to the sensitivity of radiomic features to variability in scanner models, acquisition protocols, and reconstruction algorithms, with external validation often revealing performance degradation compared to internal validation. This pattern underscores the inherent difficulty in maintaining model robustness across different imaging environments and patient populations, where factors such as scanner heterogeneity, protocol variations, and population differences can substantially impact feature reproducibility and model performance.

Our quality assessment revealed mixed methodological rigor across the included studies, with QUADAS-2 demonstrating generally favorable risk of bias while METRICS highlighted several critical deficiencies requiring attention. The QUADAS-2 assessment showed that while patient selection and reference standard domains achieved low risk across all studies, notable concerns emerged in index test evaluation (21% high risk, 29% unclear) and flow and timing (21% high risk, 29% unclear). The index test concerns likely stem from inadequate blinding of radiomics model developers to clinical outcomes, a challenge inherent to retrospective radiomics studies where researchers often have access to complete patient records during model development. The flow and timing issues predominantly reflect unclear reporting of intervals between imaging acquisition and reference standard application, which is particularly problematic in tuberculosis diagnosis where disease progression can significantly alter imaging characteristics over time.

The METRICS assessment revealed more substantial methodological limitations, with scores ranging from 34.2% to 94.5%, indicating considerable heterogeneity in study quality. The identified shortfalls reflect systemic challenges in radiomics research implementation. The lack of multicenter datasets in many studies represents a critical limitation, as Kocak et al. [34] emphasized that multicenter validation is essential for assessing model generalizability across different imaging protocols and patient populations. The inconsistent reporting of imaging-to-reference standard intervals and rare use of fully automated segmentation highlight the field’s ongoing struggle with standardization, as noted by Lambin et al. [59] in their radiomics standardization guidelines. The variability in preprocessing descriptions and feature extraction transparency reflects the complexity of radiomics workflows and the absence of universally adopted reporting standards, despite initiatives like the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI). The limited adoption of end-to-end deep learning pipelines and their lack of formal robustness assessment may be attributed to the computational complexity and expertise requirements, as well as the “black box” nature of deep learning models that complicates clinical interpretation. The inconsistent application of non-robust feature filtering and inadequate handling of potential confounders suggests insufficient awareness of statistical best practices in radiomics research, potentially reflecting the interdisciplinary nature of the field where clinical researchers may lack advanced machine learning expertise. Most concerning was the severely limited open-science practices, with minimal data sharing, code availability, and model deployment, which significantly hampers reproducibility and clinical translation. This limitation likely stems from institutional data protection policies, competitive research environments, and the lack of standardized platforms for radiomics model sharing, as highlighted by Zwanenburg et al. [60] in their call for improved reproducibility in radiomics research.

This systematic review and meta-analysis have several limitations. Although fourteen studies were included, the number contributing to each pooled analysis was modest (radiomics, n = 11 validations; clinical, n = 6; combined, n = 6), limiting power for subgroup and meta-regression contrasts. All studies were retrospective and geographically concentrated (13 from China, 1 from India), which may constrain generalizability to other settings. Despite an even split between single- and multicenter designs, external validation was available only in a subset, and some syntheses necessarily relied on internal test sets. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial, reflecting variation in CT protocols, nodule-size spectra, segmentation approaches, and feature-processing pipelines.

Conclusion

CT-based radiomics shows good diagnostic performance for differentiating lung cancer from tuberculosis, helping prioritize patients for biopsy or short-interval follow-up in TB-endemic settings. Across validation cohorts, radiomics models achieved sensitivity 0.80, specificity 0.83, and AUC 0.88. Clinical models performed worse, whereas combined radiomics and clinical models achieved the best performance, with sensitivity 0.82, specificity 0.93, and AUC 0.90. These findings support using combined models within a multiparametric decision framework instead of relying solely on clinical variables. Radiomics signatures were dominated by texture features, complemented by first order intensity and shape descriptors, indicating that intralesional heterogeneity and gray level spatial patterns provide key discriminative information. Models that retained more than 10 radiomic features, used non-contrast CT protocols, and focused on smaller nodules tended to perform more reliably. Externally validated cohorts consistently showed lower accuracy than internal test sets, indicating overfitting and limited generalizability. Heterogeneity related to nodule size spectrum, CT acquisition phase, and variability in radiomics pipelines underscores the need for multicenter studies with harmonized CT protocols, shareable workflows, and robust external validation before routine clinical adoption.
