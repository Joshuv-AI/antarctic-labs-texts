Scaling up the integration of the environment and wildlife sectors for enhanced One Health outcomes in Southeast Asia
Authors: Jonathan M Sleeman, Scott H Newman, Carla R Baker, Jeffrey Gilbert, Anouska A Kinahan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmerging infectious diseases (EIDs) are increasing in frequency due to anthropogenic pressures such as habitat loss, land-use change, biodiversity decline, and climate change. Southeast Asia, with its high biodiversity, large livestock population, increasing human population, rapid development, and expanding agriculture, is one of the global hotspots for EIDs and as such offers a strategic opportunity for the region to place itself as a leader in pandemic prevention. The One Health approach, which emphasizes the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health, presents a vital framework for addressing these risks. However, despite growing international commitment through the One Health Joint Plan of Action and the ASEAN Leaders’ Declaration on One Health Initiative, environmental and wildlife sectors remain underrepresented in policy influence, governance, and resource allocation. This paper presents a roadmap for mainstreaming and integrating wildlife and environment sectors into One Health in Southeast Asia, shifting from reactive outbreak response to proactive prevention by addressing upstream drivers of disease emergence. Field based, or bottom-up approaches include community-based interventions, land use planning, integrated surveillance, and sustainable food production while top-down measures focus on policy harmonization, capacity enhancement, regulatory reform, cross-sectoral governance, workforce development, and data sharing. An additional core principle is that existing mandates and priorities of each sector be fully integrated into national One Health strategies, action plans, and policies, ensuring inclusive and effective governance. We advocate for upstream preventative actions, where threats to ecosystems and health are mitigated at source, to the greatest extent possible, rather than waiting for crises to unfold. Finally, this paper calls for aligning One Health with the Multilateral Environmental Agreements to ensure coherent policy action. By institutionalizing environmental considerations into One Health systems, countries can move beyond project-based efforts toward sustainable, multisectoral governance. Southeast Asia is well positioned to lead this transformation and can serve as a global model of how integrated, preventive, and environmentally conscious One Health approaches can reduce pandemic risk and promote planetary health.Keywords: One Health integration, Wildlife and environment sectors, Emerging infectious diseases (EIDs), Governance, Upstream disease prevention, Biodiversity, Southeast Asia, Multisectoral coordination

Introduction

Emerging infectious diseases (EIDs) of zoonotic origin pose an increasing threat to global health, food security, and biodiversity [1, 2]. Over 70% of EIDs originate from animals, many from wildlife, and their frequency is rising due to anthropogenic pressures such as land-use change, biodiversity loss, climate change, and intensified livestock production [3, 4]. Southeast Asia, as both a hotspot for biodiversity and zoonotic emergence, sits at the forefront of these converging threats [3]. The COVID-19 pandemic has further highlighted the vulnerabilities of current public health systems and underscored the critical need for coordinated, multisectoral strategies that address disease risks at their ecological source [5]. The One Health approach offers such a framework, recognizing the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health [6]. Furthermore, the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) released a declaration at the 16th meeting of the Conference of the Parties outlining actions to implement the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework that will also contribute to the improvement of health and well-being by addressing drivers of biodiversity loss, which is also threatened by EIDs [7].

While One Health has gained political and scientific momentum in recent years, particularly through the efforts of the Quadripartite organizations, i.e., the Food and Agricultural Organization of the United Nations (FAO), the World Health Organization (WHO), the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH), and the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP), it remains largely dominated by human and animal health agendas [8]. Although these sectors increasingly incorporate elements of biodiversity conservation, climate resilience, and food security, integration of environmental considerations remains partial and unevenly operationalized [9]. Further, despite the launch of the One Health Joint Plan of Action (OH JPA) in 2022 [10] and increased awareness following recent zoonotic outbreaks, the environment and wildlife sectors are still underrepresented or underutilized in policy, governance, and field implementation [11]. This underrepresentation is a critical oversight, as the wildlife-livelihoods-health nexus is an intrinsic part of human existence in the region. Biodiversity in Asia and the Pacific is essential for providing food, water, and health security to 4.5 billion people, with wildlife contributing approximately 20.1% of household income in various forest-dependent communities. Recognizing this nexus is vital for shifting from reactive responses to a model of sustainable coexistence [12]. Consequently, the imbalance limits the ability of countries to effectively address upstream drivers of disease emergence and misses critical opportunities for prevention [13], which we define as actions that reduce zoonotic disease risk at its ecological and socio-economic source, including land-use planning, wildlife trade regulation, ecosystem protection, and sustainable agri-food systems, rather than downstream outbreak detection and response.

This paper outlines a roadmap to elevate environmental integration within the One Health framework to parity in policy attention, resource allocation, and decision-making authority with a specific focus on Southeast Asia. It presents a review of the normative frameworks guiding One Health sectors, identifies key drivers of EIDs, and makes an economic and public health case for shifting priorities from outbreak response toward risk prevention. It further provides practical strategies for implementation, including community-level (bottom-up) and policy-level (top-down) interventions, and outlines suggested sector-specific roles, responsibilities, and co-benefits of collaboration.

The purpose of this paper is to guide governments, development partners, and technical agencies in institutionalizing an environmentally integrated One Health approach. It supports the implementation of the ASEAN Leaders’ Declaration on One Health [14] and aligns global goals such as the Sustainable Development Goals [15], the CBD, and the WHO Pandemic Treaty [16]. By highlighting both mutual benefits and co-benefits, ranging from improved health outcomes and food security to biodiversity conservation and climate resilience, the paper demonstrates why environmental integration is an economically and scientifically sound strategy for pandemic prevention. This paper serves as a call to action: to move One Health from a concept of multisector cooperation to a structured, financed, and operational system that can prevent pandemics before they emerge.

While previous frameworks, including the Manhattan Principles, the Berlin Principles, and the Quadripartite’s One Health Joint Plan of Action, have emphasized the importance of integration, practical operationalization remains limited. Existing guidance largely articulates why integration is necessary but provides less specificity on how environmental and wildlife sectors can be institutionally embedded within national One Health systems. This paper advances the field by moving beyond conceptual endorsement toward a structured, implementation-oriented roadmap. We translate global principles into actionable governance, regulatory, surveillance, financing, and workforce interventions tailored to the Southeast Asian context. By combining bottom-up risk mitigation with top-down institutional reform, and explicitly aligning One Health implementation with Multilateral Environmental Agreements and national development frameworks, this paper offers a pragmatic pathway for institutionalizing environmental integration within durable, multisectoral governance systems.

Background

Historical evolution of One Health

The conceptual roots of One Health trace back to ancient observations of the importance of the human-animal-environment interface. In its modern formulation, the approach gained traction through the Wildlife Conservation Society’s (WCS) “One World, One Health” initiative and the formulation of the Manhattan Principles [17]. These were later expanded through the Berlin Principles, which emphasized the importance of environmental integration [18]. The global outbreaks of highly pathogenic avian influenza (H5N1) in the early 2000s further catalyzed unprecedented collaboration between Ministries of Health and Agriculture, supported by significant international funding [19]. The formation of the Tripartite (FAO, WHO, WOAH) formalized this collaboration, focusing initially on zoonoses, food safety, and antimicrobial resistance (AMR). In 2022, the inclusion of UNEP led to the formation of the Quadripartite, thereby institutionalizing the environmental dimension within One Health. The One Health High-Level Expert Panel [6] subsequently developed a comprehensive definition that integrates environmental sustainability, ecosystem resilience, and human well-being [10].

In addition, broader socio-economic and systemic drivers also play a critical role, including human demographics and behavior, economic development, governance challenges, technological change, and inequalities in health systems. Each of these factors increases the frequency and intensity of contact between wildlife, livestock, and human populations, thereby enhancing spillover risk. For instance, land-use change alone is estimated to account for nearly one-third of all EIDs globally [21]. Additionally, biodiversity loss can alter host-pathogen dynamics, sometimes amplifying disease transmission in degraded, simplified ecosystems [22, 23].

Economic rationale for prevention

There is a compelling economic case for prioritizing disease prevention over response. According to estimates by Dobson et al. [24], the annual global cost of preventive strategies targeting EID spillover ranges between USD 22–31 billion, substantially lower than the estimated USD 12.5 trillion economic cost of the COVID-19 pandemic. Even conservative investment scenarios demonstrate that pandemic risk could be halved at just 2% of the economic cost incurred by COVID-19.

Preventive interventions focusing on the upstream drivers outlined above, such as regulating wildlife trade, reducing deforestation, and enhancing biosecurity in animal farming, and sustainable livestock transformation generate substantial co-benefits [25, 26]. These include climate change mitigation, biodiversity protection, food and nutrition security, and sustainable livelihoods. Many of these interventions align with the globally recognized Nature-based Solutions (NbS), which are increasingly embedded within climate and biodiversity policy frameworks [27, 28].

Southeast Asia: a priority region

Southeast Asia is both a high-risk region for disease emergence and a strategic opportunity for global leadership in One Health. With a population exceeding 680 million, high livestock densities (especially poultry and swine), and rich, but threatened, biodiversity, the region is at the nexus of several EID risk factors [29]. It is also among the largest hubs for illegal wildlife trade [30] and is experiencing rapid urbanization and land-use transformation. For example, studies indicate that agricultural workers in Southeast Asia are significantly more likely to be exposed to novel pathogens, particularly in areas dominated by monoculture plantations [31]. Additionally, the region is emerging as a global epicenter for AMR, due in part to the unregulated use of antibiotics in aquaculture and agriculture, and insufficient wastewater treatment infrastructure [32, 33].

Given these factors, Southeast Asia would benefit from the integration of the environment and wildlife sectors into the One Health approach as equal partners alongside public and animal health. This would require sectoral mandates to be harmonized and joint investments in upstream interventions. To that end, the OH JPA (2022–2026) by the Quadripartite provides a strategic framework [6]. It includes dedicated action tracks for environmental integration, pandemic prevention, AMR mitigation, and food safety, among others. The ASEAN Leaders’ Declaration on One Health further reinforces regional commitment to embedding this integrated framework into policy and practice [14] and the Animal Production and Health Commission for Asia and the Pacific (APHCA), aiming to transform livestock production to be safer, more sustainable, climate smart and efficient, also provides a specific framework to balance food and nutrition security with environmental interests [34].

Vietnam illustrates the complex intersection of economic growth, wildlife trade, and zoonotic disease risk [35]. It is recognized as a global center for wildlife trade and wildlife farming, functioning as both a source, and a primary transit hub, for the international trafficking of wild animals and their products, particularly to markets in China [36, 37]. This production and trade creates multiple high-risk interfaces where pathogens can spillover from wildlife to humans. Concurrently, rapid agricultural intensification, exemplified by the expansion of over 7,000 commercial wildlife farms, and land-use changes such as forest conversion have altered ecosystems and increased direct contact between humans, livestock, and wild species [38].

Vietnam’s One Health coordination mechanisms were largely catalyzed by the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome (SARS) and H5N1 avian influenza outbreaks, leading to the formalization of the approach through the 2010 Hanoi Declaration [38]. While the One Health Partnership for Zoonoses (OHP) has successfully established multi-sectoral collaboration between the Ministry of Health and the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development, the system remains weighted toward human and animal health surveillance [38]. Integration of environmental and biodiversity data remains a significant gap, as environmental sectors were only more formally integrated in later stages of the OH framework [38, 39] and most recently by government ministries merging into the Ministry of Agriculture and Environment.

Despite legal advancements, enforcement of wildlife trade regulations is often hampered by unclear or contradictory policies and strong economic incentives that discourage a total ban on trade [40]. Policymakers frequently balance conservation goals against the perceived economic benefits of wildlife farming, which can lead to poor biosecurity and risky practices [40].

Thus, the region is well-placed to serve as a global exemplar in One Health implementation. Adopting an ecosystem-based One Health approach would facilitate the integration of biodiversity monitoring into existing platforms, moving beyond reactive disease surveillance to proactive prevention [39, 41]. This illustrates that strengthening the alignment between public health and environmental authorities is critical to addressing the underlying drivers of disease emergence, such as habitat change and wildlife supply chain vulnerabilities [41]. By recognizing and operationalizing the interdependence between ecosystem integrity and disease prevention, the One Health approach offers a pathway toward sustainable development, enhanced resilience, and long-term health security.

Sectoral frameworks guiding One Health implementation

There are international frameworks, policy instruments, and mandates that guide the normative work of the four Quadripartite sectors: public health (International Health Regulations [42] and the Pandemic Treaty [16]; animal health, food and agriculture production (Agreement on the Application of Sanitary and Phytosanitary Measures (SPS Agreement of the World Trade Organisation), Codex Alimentarius, Terrestrial Animal Health Code and Aquatic Animal Health Code [43]); wildlife and the environment (for example, the CBD [37] and the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework and associated National Biodiversity Strategic Action Plans (NBSAPs), the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species [44], the Convention on Migratory Species [45] and the Ramsar Convention [46].

Full implementation of a multisectoral One Health approach requires shared understanding and alignment of these frameworks at global, regional, national and sub-national levels. This is particularly relevant in Southeast Asia, where multisectoral cooperation is critical to managing emerging infectious disease (EID) risks [47], agri-food systems transformation, climate change, biodiversity loss, environmental pollutants, and AMR. In addition, intersecting risks from biodiversity loss, zoonotic spillovers, and agricultural expansion demand coordinated and multisectoral governance approaches [48]. By building a shared foundation rooted in international frameworks and the SDGs, the One Health approach can evolve from siloed to systemic action.

Mainstreaming wildlife and environment into One Health: overcoming barriers and creating opportunities

Recognizing the environment as a One Health priority

Integrating the wildlife and environment sectors into the One Health framework is not only urgent but foundational to mitigating EID risks [49, 50]. Transforming One Health from a largely biomedical concept to a fully interdisciplinary paradigm requires a recalibration of sectoral relationships. Resolving the longstanding sectoral silos and structural asymmetries requires mutual understanding of each sector’s mandates, priorities, and challenges. Deeper collaboration can be achieved by highlighting shared responsibilities, recognizing barriers to cooperation, and identifying mutual and co-benefits of an integrated One Health approach. In particular, the environmental and wildlife agencies should be elevated to equal partnership with public health and animal health sectors. This shift is especially critical in regions such as Southeast Asia, where anthropogenic pressures on ecosystems are significant drivers of EIDs [51].

The traditional One Health model, as previously described, would benefit from evolving into a more balanced, proactive system that addresses upstream drivers of disease emergence and other threats to the environment. Some progress has been made integrating data from wildlife, livestock and humans [52]; however, One Health has faced challenges in fully institutionalizing sustained cross-sectoral collaboration during non-emergency (“peacetime”) periods. [53]. To achieve full integration, the environment and wildlife sectors should embed One Health principles into their core mandates, and equally the human and livestock health sectors should incorporate ecological principles into their mandates. Only through such structural realignment and mutual recognition of each other’s mandates, can countries develop or operationalize systems that mitigate risk at source, rather than merely respond to outbreaks or emergencies.

Understanding sectoral responsibilities and identifying synergies

Central to mainstreaming the wildlife and environment sector into One Health is the need for mutual recognition of each sector’s role in addressing EID drivers [50]. Below are the specific responsibilities of public health, animal health, agriculture, and environment sectors as they relate to key upstream drivers. Multidimensional understanding of responsibilities enables the identification of synergies and shared areas of interest that form the basis for sustained cooperation.

Wildlife trade, consumption, and farming

These practices increase direct contact between humans and wild animals, enabling zoonotic pathogen spillover [54]. The wildlife sector is responsible for regulating legal trade, enforcement against illegal trade, and monitoring wildlife health [37]. However, gaps in coordination with public health and veterinary services often result in missed early warning opportunities [55]. Agriculture and food safety agencies may also play a more significant role when wildlife is farmed or sold for consumption.

Habitat fragmentation and land use change

Land conversion for agriculture, mining, and infrastructure development creates novel interfaces where wildlife, livestock, and people interact [56]. Environmental or wildlife agencies are responsible for protecting, managing and monitoring natural resources and to mitigate threats to these resources, such as habitat fragmentation and pollution. Although healthy ecosystems provide multiple benefits to humans and are essential to well-being and health, environmental priorities are often perceived to be in conflict with economic development. Collaboration is essential to minimize unintended health consequences of development [57].

Agricultural expansion and livestock farming

As agricultural frontiers expand, risks of disease transmission increase, particularly in intensive or poorly regulated systems [56]. The agriculture sector holds responsibility for implementing biosecurity, regulating livestock density, and promoting sustainable practices. Public health agencies are concerned with foodborne and zoonotic risks, while environmental actors are focused on reducing unsustainable land and water use that negatively impacts landscape resilience and biodiversity conservation.

Climate change, pollution, urbanization, and globalization

Environmental degradation, AMR, and climate-driven vector (or host) shifts pose cross-sectoral threats [58–60]. Public health and animal health sectors require real-time data on environmental changes to forecast and mitigate disease outbreaks. The environment sector focuses largely on climate mitigation and adaptation to support human and biodiversity resilience to the negative impacts of climate change and through policy and conservation action addresses environmental pollution on land, water and in the air. The environment sector also benefits from integrating disease risk models into conservation planning and pollution control measures.

Identifying sectoral challenges and barriers

Despite these identified shared goals, institutional and operational barriers have historically impeded One Health integration. These structural and institutional asymmetries contribute to fragmented data systems, incompatible terminologies, and lack of trust, all of which undermine collaborative potential [61]. Public health actors often operate under emergency-driven mandates and are held accountable for measurable health outcomes. As such, the long-term ecological interventions promoted by the environmental sector may appear tangential to public health core priorities. In addition, public health agencies may lack technical expertise in ecology or wildlife biology. Veterinary services are often under-resourced and fragmented across public and private actors. In many Southeast Asian countries, informal backyard livestock systems and informal food markets are poorly monitored, compounding disease surveillance and food safety challenges [62]. Agricultural ministries may prioritize productivity over ecosystem health, leading to trade-offs that are rarely evaluated. Furthermore, priorities are focused on diseases in livestock, however, transmission is bidirectional between livestock and wildlife [63]. Environmental agencies often lack political influence and budgetary support relative to the health sectors. Their mandates are broad and typically not framed in health terms, resulting in historically limited engagement with One Health. Wildlife health surveillance systems are underdeveloped, and legal authority over wildlife varies widely across jurisdictions [55]. Furthermore, environment agencies may lack knowledge about health protocols, disease epidemiology, or laboratory networks.

From understanding to action

We propose a two-pronged parallel strategy for advancing integration. The first is creating opportunities for grassroots collaboration, or a “bottom-up” approach. Local and national risk mitigation efforts, such as joint disease surveillance of wildlife markets, integrated land-use planning, or community-based conservation provide practical entry points for cross-sectoral collaboration. These initiatives create proof of concept models that can be scaled and inform national policies. The second is high-level institutional change, a “top-down” approach. To ensure sustainability, local actions should be reinforced by top-level policy, legislation and governance such as an inter-ministerial coordination platform, joint funding mechanisms, and harmonized legal frameworks. Leadership from the ASEAN Secretariat and the Quadripartite is critical to institutionalizing such change at a regional level. These approaches are mutually reinforcing: bottom-up efforts build trust and showcase effectiveness, while top-down reforms provide enabling environments and political legitimacy (Fig. 1).

Several case studies in Southeast Asia demonstrate effective multisectoral collaboration in controlling wildlife trade-related EID risks, particularly in response to avian influenza and SARS (for example [64]). Successful interventions in countries like Vietnam and Indonesia have also demonstrated how coordinated hygiene upgrades in live wildlife markets can reduce risks of diseases such as avian influenza and Salmonella, while also improving consumer confidence [65, 66]. Ongoing regional initiatives include FAO-backed farmer field schools and One Health AMR action plans that support food security, climate resilience, and pollution reduction [67]. Finally, examples from Malaysia and Thailand demonstrate how participatory land-use planning can integrate health and environmental criteria to minimize EID risks [68, 69].

Multisectoral engagement is essential for the success of these bottom-up approaches as no single agency can manage upstream EID risks alone. Sustainable interventions require coordinated involvement of environment, agriculture, animal health, and public health sectors, and local governments and communities. Bottom-up approaches would benefit from national policies that prioritize decentralization of One Health actions, funding, and capacity-building to sub-national actors. Shared planning, surveillance, training, and financing mechanisms across all focus areas would also be beneficial. Risk mitigation measures should be framed in terms of their contributions to economic stability, biodiversity conservation, climate resilience, and food security to instill the philosophy of a shared mission.

In summary, the bottom-up approach is a critical complement to top-down policy coordination and offers immediate, scalable opportunities to demonstrate the effectiveness of integrated One Health strategies. Its success will depend on inclusive governance, local ownership, sustained funding, and a continued emphasis on evidence-based risk mitigation.

Operationalizing an environmentally integrated One Health approach through high-level strategic interventions (“top-down”): the enablers

A structured, top-down strategy for institutionalizing a fully integrated One Health approach through national and regional leadership is also needed [70]. In Table 3, we outline seven high-level intervention areas that we consider as enablers designed to embed the environment and wildlife sectors into One Health policy, legislation, governance, capabilities and programming, and provide representative actions and metrics, all of which are consistent with the Guide to Implementing the One Health Joint Plan of Action at the National Level [71]. These enablers, or interventions aim to overcome systemic fragmentation and elevate One Health from isolated projects to a nationally coordinated and sustainably financed system. Institutionalizing environmental considerations in One Health should go beyond awareness-raising or ad hoc collaboration. It requires formal mechanisms, regulatory coherence, joint policy development, integrated surveillance systems, investment in research and workforce capacity, and societal participation. Together, these seven interventions form a cohesive framework to support and scale up risk reduction measures for EIDs, particularly in Southeast Asia. These enablers are also assessed at national level, as part of a One Health situation analysis undertaken through the use of the FAO One Health Assessment Tool (OHAT) which to date, has been rolled-out in multiple countries in Asia, Africa and South America [72].

Suggested top-down actions to enable the operationalization of One Health at the national and sub-national levels

Establish a One Health Coordination Mechanism Inclusive of All Sectors

The cornerstone of a functional One Health system is an inclusive, intersectoral coordination platform that enables decision-making, priority setting, and resource allocation across the public health, animal health, agriculture, environment, and wildlife sectors [10].

• Develop or strengthen a formal, government-led coordination mechanism with high-level representation from all relevant sectors

• Ensure the mechanism is anchored in legislation or policy to guarantee sustainability beyond project cycles

• Appoint a dedicated One Health Secretariat responsible for convening meetings, managing communication, resolving conflicts, and monitoring progress

• Official designation of a national One Health coordination body

• Sectoral mandates and responsibilities are clearly defined and integrated into the governance framework; mutual and co-benefits identified

• Regular cross-sectoral meetings are institutionalized or operationalized.

Enhance Regulatory Frameworks and Enforcement Mechanisms

Fragmented legal mandates and weak enforcement are major barriers to addressing the upstream drivers of EIDs [73]. This intervention focuses on ensuring legislation is in place and harmonizing regulations across sectors and strengthening the capacity for enforcement.

• Conduct a legal and regulatory gap analysis across relevant One Health sectors

• Align and revise laws related to wildlife trade, land use, antimicrobial use, food safety, disease control, and waste management and pollution

• Enhance enforcement capabilities through joint inspections, training, and interagency collaboration

• Identify incentives to administer and enforce legislation

• Regulatory frameworks and legislation updated and harmonized

• Enforcement agencies are equipped and trained to apply integrated health-environment regulations

• Legal provisions for wildlife and environmental surveillance are established

Develop or Update Cross-Sectoral Policies, Strategies or Action Plans

Integrated One Health policies, strategies and action plans are critical to ensuring coherence across ministries and facilitating resource mobilization. These interventions support the development of overarching policy frameworks and both a strategy and roadmap that align sectoral mandates with national development goals.

• Formulate national One Health policies and action plans in a process that includes all relevant sectors and ensure environment and wildlife priorities are included

• Mainstream One Health into national development plans, health security strategies, biodiversity action plans, and agricultural policies

• Align national policies with the One Health Joint Plan of Action [10] and ASEAN One Health frameworks [14]

• Wildlife and environment sector professionals participate in and contribute to the process of revising or developing the national One Health strategy

• Wildlife and environment sector priorities are included in national One Health priorities

• Endorsement of integrated national One Health policy

• Clear alignment between One Health objectives and international commitments (e.g., SDGs, Multilateral Environmental Agreements, IHR)

• Inclusion of One Health in national budget planning processes

Strengthening Surveillance Systems for Biodiversity and Population Monitoring for Disease

Disease surveillance has traditionally focused on human and livestock populations [55]. This intervention addresses the critical gap in wildlife and environmental surveillance which is essential for early detection of spillover risks.

• Expand existing disease surveillance systems to include wildlife health monitoring and associated environmental indicators

• Integrate databases across sectors to support real-time data sharing, risk assessment and early warning systems

• Leverage community-based and citizen science approaches to enhance data collection at the ecosystem level

• Functioning wildlife and ecosystem health surveillance systems are in place

• Cross-sectoral early warning platform is developed

• Surveillance data are used to inform land-use and conservation policies

Advance Integrated Data Collection and Data Sharing across All Sectors

Effective One Health governance depends on robust evidence and the ability to translate knowledge into action. This intervention promotes coordinated multisectoral surveillance and the establishment of data sharing across sectors using interoperable data platforms.

• Undertake joint risk assessments among environment, wildlife, livestock and public health sectors

• Ensure integrated surveillance is planned and implemented among the environment, wildlife, livestock and public health sectors

• Develop and adopt standardized data sharing protocols or SOPs to enable cross-sectoral data and information sharing

• Establish a national One Health research agenda with prioritized topics including spillover dynamics, climate-disease linkages, and NbS

• Facilitate multisectoral collaborative applied research initiatives across universities, ministries, and international organizations

• Joint risk assessments and surveillance undertaken

• SOPs or mechanisms established for standardized data and information sharing

• National repository of One Health-relevant data is launched

• Operational One Health research platform is established

• Funding mechanisms created or identified for joint research projects

Strengthen One Health Workforce Capacity

One Health implementation requires a skilled and multidisciplinary workforce. This intervention seeks to build capacity across sectors through education, training, and institutional reform at academic and in-service levels.

• Develop cross-disciplinary curricula in veterinary medicine, public health, wildlife biology, environmental sciences, and related fields

• Ensure that in-service training programs are available to relevant professionals in the environment, wildlife, livestock and public health sectors

• Institutionalize or operationalize joint training programs for field staff, policymakers, and technical experts

• Create career pathways and incentive structures to retain One Health professionals in the public sector

• One Health is integrated into academic and in-service professional training institutions

• Workforce needs assessment completed and capacity-building strategy implemented

• In-service professional development is incentivized and rewarded

• Increased number of qualified personnel in key One Health roles across sectors

Implement a Comprehensive Whole-of-Society Approach to One Health

Community engagement, public awareness, and multistakeholder partnerships are essential to the success and legitimacy of One Health interventions [74]. This final area promotes inclusive participation at all levels.

• Develop communication strategies tailored to different stakeholder groups (e.g., policymakers, farmers, indigenous communities)

• Support community-led initiatives for disease prevention, conservation, and sustainable land use

• Involve the private sector and civil society in co-developing solutions and scaling best practices

• Public awareness campaigns launched and evaluated

• Civil society is represented in One Health governance platforms

• Documented increase in community uptake of prevention measures (e.g., farm biosecurity)

These enablers or interventions provide a strategic blueprint for transforming One Health from an aspirational concept into a sustainable, institutionalized system. They collectively address the legal, policy, operational, and societal foundations required to reduce EID risks through upstream action. Implementing these interventions will not only strengthen pandemic preparedness and health security but also generate co-benefits for biodiversity conservation, climate resilience, food security, and sustainable development. Success of these enablers will depend on strong political will, adequate financing, and sustained commitment to inclusive governance. These interventions are not stand-alone actions but interdependent components of a systemic change process. By adopting this integrated approach, countries can build resilient, equitable, and health-secure societies that are better prepared to face the complex challenges of the twenty-first century.

Conclusions

Pandemic prevention, health security, sustainable development, and biodiversity conservation are inextricably linked and can only be achieved through multisectoral coordination that places equal weight on environment, animal (domestic livestock and wildlife), and human health. A central theme of our paper is the transition from a response-focused model to one that prioritizes upstream prevention of zoonotic disease emergence that addresses threats to the health of the environment, and ensures long-term commitment through policy, legislation, funding, and collaborative implementation. Prevention is not only more cost-effective than outbreak response but also yields co-benefits for climate change mitigation and adaptation, biodiversity protection, and food security. As mentioned, one of the major gaps in traditional One Health implementation has been the underrepresentation of the environment and wildlife sectors. There is a compelling case that these sectors should be elevated to equal status alongside public health and animal health actors, both in governance and operational practice. The complementary bottom-up and top-down approaches illustrate that implementation should occur simultaneously at community, sub-national, national, and regional levels. Bottom-up measures, including risk mitigation at markets, farms, and forest edges, should be complemented by high-level actions such as regulatory reform, policy alignment, establishment of governance and infrastructures, and cross-sectoral coordination platforms. One Health should not remain a project-based, donor-driven agenda. Its principles and mechanisms should be embedded in national legislation, budgeting processes, workforce development, and inter-ministerial governance frameworks.

We recommend that governments institutionalize and/or operationalize multisectoral One Health coordination platforms that equally include environment, wildlife, agriculture, and public health authorities. These platforms should be formalized through policy or legislation. One Health objectives can then be incorporated into biodiversity action plans, national development strategies, climate policies, and sector-specific mandates. Dedicated financing and inclusion in national budget cycles are critical for sustainability. Integrated surveillance systems should include wildlife and environmental health indicators and collaborative research on EID drivers, AMR, and NbS should be supported through national and regional initiatives. Education and training programs should prepare a multidisciplinary workforce that can operate across health, environmental, and agricultural domains. Finally, community participation, public awareness, and civil society engagement are essential to ensure local ownership and effectiveness of One Health interventions.

Southeast Asia is well positioned to lead globally on One Health due to its high biodiversity, zoonotic disease risk, and regional cooperation mechanisms such as ASEAN. A transformative, preventive, and environmentally integrated One Health system is not only possible but urgently needed to prevent the next pandemic and ensure long-term planetary health.
