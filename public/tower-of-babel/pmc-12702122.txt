Zinc-copper bimetallic organic framework nanosheets for synergistic treatment of vitiligo by inhibiting oxidative stress and promoting melanin production
Authors: Jian Chen, Jing Zhang, Mengmeng Hou, Huili Liu, Haojie Cheng, Jiaman Wu, Shouren Zhang, Baocheng Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Vitiligo is a prevalent acquired depigmenting disorder with complex pathogenesis, and current treatments targeting individual mechanisms often yield unsatisfactory results. Herein, a multifunctional Cu-Zn bimetallic organic framework nanosheets (Cu/Zn-TA) with capacities of catalyzing dopamine polymerization, improving tyrosinase (TYR) activity and scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS), have been developed for the treatment of vitiligo. Zn2+ nodes in the nanosheets can act as Lewis acid center to polarize phenolic hydroxyl group in dopamine through Lewis acid-base addition reaction. And combined with Jahn-Teller distortion derived from the introduction of Cu2+, dopamine was easily oxidized and polymerized by capturing Zn2+ from the nanosheets. Additionally, the Cu-Zn caused synergistic effect can enrich dopamine around the nanosheets through Lewis acid-base coordination, and enhance electrons transfer from the hydroxyl group of dopamine to the dual-core copper center of TYR, leading to the enhancement of TYR activity for oxidizing dopamine to dopachrome. Moreover, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets exhibited multienzyme activities for scavenging various ROS, owing to the Cu2+/Cu+ redox cycle and the coordination between terephthalic acid (TA) molecules and Zn2+. Benefiting from the synergy of multiple therapy mechanisms, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can effectively treat vitiligo in a H2O2-hydroquinone induced vitiligo mouse model, through protecting melanocytes from oxidative damage and increasing both exogenous and endogenous melanin content. This study provides valuable insights into vitiligo treatment based on bimetallic organic frameworks.

Introduction

Vitiligo is a global chronic depigmentation disorder of skin with clinical characterization of white patches that starkly contrast with the surrounding area [1]. Though vitiligo is generally asymptomatic, its significant psychosocial impact can cause patients considerable burden and social isolation in daily life [2]. The pathogenesis of vitiligo is still elusive, but accumulated evidence confirms this disease is associated with oxidative stress and melanin synthesis abnormalities [3,4]. Oxidative damage accompanied by the increased cytokines, can destruct melanocytes which provide special organelles melanosomes for the synthesis and transport of melanin [5]. In addition, tyrosinase (TYR), a copper-containing monooxygenase, plays a key role in melanin synthesis by catalyzing the oxidation of tyrosine via L-dihydroxyphenylalanine to dopaquinone, ultimately forming the melanin pigment. Thus, amelioration of oxidative damage and upregulation of TYR activity are crucial for treating vitiligo [6,7]. Currently, various antioxidant drugs, such as cinnamaldehyde, maclurin, minocycline have been employed to treat vitiligo by protecting melanocytes from oxidative damage [8]. Additionally, flavonoid compounds, like chalcones, show promise in treating vitiligo by increasing TYR activity, which enhances the formation of epidermal melanin [9]. Except boosting endogenous melanin, melanin-like substances have been explored to treat vitiligo by supplying exogenous melanin. Polydopamine (PDA) nanoparticles, as synthetic melanin, have been reported to treat vitiligo, since they can be taken up by keratinocytes and transported intracellularly, provide long-lasting color in a nontoxic manner, and scavenge excess reactive oxygen species (ROS) [7,10,11]. Owing to the complex pathogenesis of vitiligo, current therapeutic medicines that mainly focus on a single factor often yield unsatisfactory therapy outcomes. Consequently, a synergistic therapy approach is urgently required for the effective treatment of vitiligo.

Recently, metal organic frameworks (MOFs) formed by the self-assembly of transition metal ions and organic ligands, have been utilized as artificial enzymes in biomedicine due to their numerous catalytically active sites [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Coordinatively unsaturated metal sites in MOFs typically function as Lewis acid centers for various catalytic processes, such as coordinating with phenolic oxygen atoms and facilitating the assembly of dopamine oligomers during polymerization [16]. Moreover, our research indicates that Zn-MOFs can improve TYR activity by concentrating the substrate through Lewis acid sites. Metals with variable valence states have been widely used as metal nodes in constructing MOFs to scavenge ROS due to their redox cycle [[17], [18], [19]]. To further improve the catalytic efficiency of MOFs and impart multiple functions, bimetallic MOFs have been developed by integrating a second type of metal ions into the nodes of frameworks [20]. The substitution of the original metal nodes with second metal ions can tune the physicochemical properties of the MOFs by causing geometrical distortions and rearranging electron cloud [21]. In comparison with conventional 3D bulk MOF crystals, 2D MOF nanosheets exhibit superior catalytic efficiency because they have larger surface area to provide more accessible active sites for substrate contact [22,23]. Among various methods for preparing 2D MOF nanosheets, surfactant-assisted synthesis stands out for its high yield and large-scale production capabilities [24]. Though MOFs have made significant advancements in the treatment of skin disorders, their clinical applications remain constrained by several drawbacks. Firstly, the absence of multiple therapeutic functions renders MOFs challenging to effectively treat skin diseases characterized by multiple pathogenic mechanisms. Secondly, inappropriate particle size and surface electrical properties of MOFs can lead to suboptimal skin penetration, thereby limiting the quantity of drug delivered transdermally and diminishing therapeutic efficacy. Thirdly, the biotoxicity of metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), stemming from toxic metal ions and ligands, as well as instability in physiological environments, remains a critical issue that must be addressed prior to clinical application. Therefore, MOFs designed for vitiligo therapy must focus on addressing the aforementioned issues.

Zn2+ and Cu2+ are ideal metal nodes for creating MOFs used in biomedicine, because both of them are integral to various biological process, including playing vital roles in numerous proteins and enzymes [25]. For instance, Cu2+ and Zn2+ are essential to maintain the stability of TYR and tyrosinase-related proteins 1 (TYRP1), respectively [26]. Meanwhile, Cu2+ and Zn2+ are the key ingredients of superoxide dismutase (SOD) in living organisms, forming histidine-bridged bimetallic Cu-Zn site to enhance antioxidative activity [27,28]. Terephthalic acid (TA) is a widely used ligand in constructing MOFs due to its diverse topology and numerous coordination patterns. Additionally, TA molecules can scavenge hydroxyl radicals (•OH) by capturing •OH to form hydroxylated terephthalate [29].

In this study, a multifunctional 2D bimetallic MOF nanosheets (Cu/Zn-TA) was designed for the treatment of vitiligo through effectively scavenging excess ROS, catalyzing dopamine polymerization and promoting TYR activity (Scheme 1). Zn-TA nanosheets were obtained by combining Zn2+ and TA with surfactant and ultrasonication assisted method. And Cu2+ can replace Zn2+ in the MOFs through cation exchange because of their high electronegativity, which facilitates the formation of covalent bonds [20]. Owing to Lewis acid sites caused by Zn2+ nodes and Jahn-Teller distortion caused by Cu2+ nodes, the Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can coordinate with phenolic hydroxyl groups of dopamine via Zn-O bond, and then catalyze the oxidation and polymerization of dopamine, leading to the increase of exogenous melanin content. Meanwhile, Zn2+ nodes can enrich dopamine around the nannosheets through Lewis-acid coordination. And Cu2+ nodes can promote the transfer of electrons from hydroxyl group of dopamine to the dual-core copper center of tyrosinase through ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT). Owing to this synergistic effect, the activity of TYR can be improved to upregulate endogenous melanin content. Moreover, owing to the Cu2+/Cu + redox cycle and the coordiantion between TA molecules and Zn2+, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets exhibit SOD-like activity, catalase (CAT)-like activity and •OH scavenging ability. These properties aid in reducing oxidative stress and providing anti-inflammatory effects to protect melanocytes. Materials and methods

Materials

All the chemical agents were analytical reagents (AR). Zinc acetate (Zn(OAc)2), cupric acetate (Cu(OAc)2), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), terephthalic acid (TA), hydroquinone and 2,2-azino-bis(3-ethyl-benzothiazole-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt (ABTS) were obtained from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Methylene blue (MB) were purchased from the Tianjin Kermel Chemical Reagent Company (Tianjin, China). Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30 %) was purchased from Sichuan Xilong Science Co., Ltd (Sichuan, China). Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM), phosphate buffered solution (PBS) and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were obtained from Wuhan Prosa Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). B16 cells were gifted by Henan Shengxiang Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd (Henan, China). Anti-tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), anti-chemokine interleukin-1β (IL-1β), anti-chemokine interleukin-10 (IL-10) were purchased from Solarbio Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). MC-1 cells and medium purchased from Keycell Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China).

Synthesis of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets

Firstly, 40 mL of distilled water containing 0.3 g of PVP was vigorously stirred at room temperature, followed by adding 1 mmol of Zn(OAc)2. After stirring for 1 h, the solution was transferred to ultrasonic processor. Under intense ultrasound, 5 mL of solution containing TA (0.1 M) and NaOH (10 mg/mL) was dropwise added into the solution and kept ultrasonication for another 1 h. Subsequently, the above mixture solution was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected for sonification again (1 h). After repeating the ultrasonication (1h)-centrifugation (1000 rpm, 10 min)-collection of supernatant cycle three times, the solution was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 3 min. Then, 10 mL of Cu(OAc)2 (0.05 M) solution was dropwise added into the collected supernatant with stirring for 1 h. Finally, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets were obtained by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min, washing with distilled water, and drying at 60 °C.

Characterization

The powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on an X-ray diffractometer (D8-Advance) equipped with Cu-Kα radiation. The morphology of the as-synthesized samples was observed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL JEM F200) and scanning electron microscope (SEM, FEI Quanta 250 FEG). The UV–vis absorption spectra were recorded on a Hitachi U4100 spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Japan). The FTIR spectra were acquired by FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS5). The concentration of copper ions were measured using inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectroscopy (iCAP 7600 ICPOES, ThermoFisher). The zeta potential was characterized with Malvern zetasizer Nanoseries (Nano ZS90). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy of C 1s, O 1s, Zn 2p and Cu 2d of the samples were obtained from ESCAlab250, Thermal Fischer. The temperature-programmed desorption of ammonia (NH3-TPD-MS) characterization was performed with automatic chemisorption analyzer (BelCata II, Japan).

Measurement of catalytic activity on dopamine polymerization

1 mL of Zn-TA or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets (0.5 mg/mL) were respectively added into 4 mL of dopamine hydrochloride aqueous solution with different concentrations (2.5, 5, 10, 15, 20 μg/mL). After incubation for 2, 4, 6, 8 h, the absorbance intensity of the mixture at 660 nm, corresponding to polydopamine were measured by UV–vis spectrophotometer. In addition, the catalytic effect of different concentrations of Zn-TA or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets (80, 100, 200, 300, 400 μg/mL) on dopamine polymerization was also investigated with the same method.

Measurement of tyrosinase activity

The tyrosinase activity was detected by measuring the oxidation rate of dopamine. In detail, 0.01 mL of dopamine hydrochloride (0.1 M) was mixed with 1.54 mL of distilled water and incubated at 25 °C for 10 min. Then, 0.4 mL of the sample solution was further added into the above mixture, followed by the final addition of 0.05 mL of mushroom tyrosinase solution. Subsequently, the absorption intensity of the mixture at 475 nm, corresponding to the formation dopachrome, was monitored in real-time for 5 min.

Cyclic voltammetry measurements

The cyclic voltammetry measurements were conducted through a CHI660D (CHI Instruments, Shanghai, China) electrochemical working station in a PBS electrolyte solution (pH 6.0, 0.1 M) containing dopamine hydrochloride (0.5 mg/mL). The scan rate was set as 100 mV/s with potential ranging from +0.4 V to −0.4V. The working electrode was prepared as follows. First, 80 μL of Zn-TA or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets solution (10 mg/mL, 95 % ethanol and 5 % naphthol) was deposited on a fluorinated-tin-ooxide (FTO) conducting glass. After dried at room temperature, 50 μL of mixed solution containing tyrosinase (4 mg/mL) and glutaraldehyde (1.25 %) was cast onto the modified surface. The prepared electrode was further dried at room temperature for 1 h, then washed with distilled water, dried with a nitrogen flow and stored at 4 °C for further use. Platinum plate and Ag/AgCl were used as counter electrode and reference electrode, respectively.

Measurement of hydroxyl radical scavenging activity

The •OH scavenging ability was measured in system containing H2O2 and FeSO4, which can generate •OH through Fenten reaction. And methylene blue (MB) was selected as •OH indicator since it can be discolored by •OH with its absorption peak at 664 nm decreased. Briefly, 40 μL of MB solution (1 mg/mL) was added into Cu/Zn-TA or Cu-TA nanosheets solution with different concentrations. Then, 80 μL of FeSO4 (10 mM) and 180 μL of H2O2 (10 mM) were successively added into the above mixture, followed by adjusting the final volume to 4 mL with distilled water. After kept standing for 3 min, the mixed solution was centrifugated and the supernatant was measured by UV–vis spectrophotometer to quantify the remained •OH. The scavenging rate of •OH was calculated using the following formula:scavengingrate(%)=[(A2−A1)/(A0−A1)]×100%,where A0 was the absorbance of pure MB, A1 and A2 were the absorbance of MB incubated in the Fe2+/H2O2 Fenton system without and with nanosheets, respectively.

Measurement of superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activity

2-(2-Methoxy-4-nitrophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-(2,4-disulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium Sodium Salt (WST-8) assay kit was employed to investigate the SOD-like activities of Zn-TA or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets. Firstly, 40 μL of aqueous solution containing Zn-TA or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets was mixed with 320 μL of WST-8/xanthine oxidase working solution. The final concentrations of Zn-TA or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets in the mixed solution were respectively set as 30, 40, 50, 100 μg/mL. Subsequently, 40 μL of working solution was added into the above mixture to initiate the reaction by co-incubating at 37 °C for 30 min. Finally, the absorbance of solution at 450 nm was investigated through UV–vis spectrophotometer.

Measurement of catalase (CAT)-like activity

The catalase-like activity of Cu/Zn-TA or Zn-TA nanosheets was evaluated by measuring the generated oxygen at room temperature with a dissolved oxygen meter (JPBJ-608, Leici, China). Briefly, 5 mL of H2O2 (30 %) was added into 5 mL of Cu/Zn-TA or Zn-TA nanosheets solution (1 mg/mL) under vigorous stirring. Then, the generated oxygen content was determined within 30 min.

In vitro assessment of skin penetration

Fresh skin from Bama miniature pig was washed with PBS to remove extraneous subcutaneous fat, and cut into 1.5 × 1.5 cm2 pieces. Subsequently, rhodamine B-labeled Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets (200 μg/mL) was applied onto the skin samples topically. Here, PBS and rhodamine B in PBS were set as negative and positive controls. The treated pig skins were incubated in a humidity chamber at 32 °C for 8 h. After washed with PBS three times and dried, the skin samples were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, followed by cryo-section. The sectioned tissues were visualized through a confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). The images were analyzed by using ImageJ software.

In addition, Franz diffusion cells were employed to further investigate the skin permeation deposition. Bama miniature pig skins were placed in Franz diffusion cells, where stratum corneum side and layer side were made to face the donor chambers and receptor chambers (effective diffusion area 2.30 cm2), respectively. Under magnetic stirring (300 rpm/min) at 32 °C, 1 mL of PBS was added into receptor chambers, and 1 mL of Zn/Cu-TA nanosheets suspensions (500 μg/mL) was added into donor chambers. At designed time points (1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24 h), 1 mL of PBS was withdrawn from receptor chambers for analysis with ICP-OES. Meanwhile, 1 mL of fresh PBS was added again.

Cell culture

Mouse melanoma cells B16 were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium containing 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % antibiotic-antimycotic (penicillin-streptomycin, 10000 U mL−1) solution at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. Immortalized human melanocytes MC-1 were cultured in 254 culture medium (Gibco) containing 1 % human melanocyte growth additive-2 (HMGS-2, Gibco, S0165) and 1 % antibiotic-antimycotic (penicillin-streptomycin, 10000 U mL−1) solution at 37 °C with 5 % CO2.

Cytotoxicity test

Cytotoxicity experiments were performed with the methyl thiazolyl tetrazolium (MTT) method. B16 cells or MC-1 cells (1 × 105 cells per well) were seeded into 96-well plates. After overnight incubation, the cells were cultured with a series of concentrations of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets (5–100 μg/mL) for another 24 h. Then, MTT solution (10 μL) was added into each well for further 4 h of incubation. Subsequently, the supernatant was discarded accompanied by the addition of DMSO into each well. Finally, the absorbance at 490 nm for each well was recorded by a microplate reader.

Cellular uptake

MC-1 cells (1 × 105 cells per well) were seeded into 6-well plates. After overnight incubation, rhodamine B-labeled Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets (200 μg/mL) were added into the culture medium for another 4 h of co-incubation. Then, the cells were stained with (4′,6-diamidino-2- phenylindole) DPAI, washed with PBS and fixed by glutaraldehyde (2.5 %). At last, the cells were observed through inverted fluorescence microscope, and ImageJ software was used to analyze the images.

Measurement of intracellular ROS

MC-1 cells (1 × 105 cells per well) were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured overnight. After that, the cells were cultured with H2O2 (0.75 μmol/mL) for 4 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing Cu/Zn-TA or Zn-TA nanosheets (200 μg/mL), and cells were further incubated for another 6 h. Subsequently, 2,7-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) was added into the culture medium to incubate with cells at 37 °C for 20 min. At last, the cells were washed with PBS and observed through fluorescence microscopy.

Assessment of anti-inflammatory activity in vitro

B16 cells (1 × 105 cells per well) were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured overnight. After that, the cells were cultured with H2O2 (0.75 μmol/mL) for 4 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing Cu/Zn-TA or Zn-TA nanosheets (200 μg/mL), and cells were further incubated for another 6 h. Finally, the supernatant was collected for detecting cytokines by commercial mouse IL-10, TNF-α and IL-1β ELISA Kit (Solarbio, China).

Assessment of tyrosinase activity

B16 cells (1 × 105 cells per well) were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured overnight. After that, Cu/Zn-TA or Zn-TA nanosheets (200 μg/mL) were added into the culture medium for another 6 h of incubation. Subsequently, the cells were washed with PBS, followed by adding 50 μL of 1 % Triton X-100 into each well. Cells were put into ultracold refrigerator for 30 min, and then taken out for thawing. After repeating the above steps three times, 50 μL of 0.25 % L-Dopa was added into each well with incubated in a shaker at 37 °C for 2 h. Finally, the absorbance of each well at 475 nm was recorded by a microplate reader.

Assessment of melanin generation

B16 cells (1 × 105 cells per well) were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured overnight. After that, Cu/Zn-TA or Zn-TA nanosheets (200 μg/mL) were added into the culture medium for another 6 h of incubation. Subsequently, 100 μL of NaOH solution (0.2 M) was added into each well with incubated in a shaker at 37 °C for 1 h. Finally, the absorbance of each well at 490 nm was recorded by a microplate reader.

In vivo animal studies

All animal experiments have been approved by the Animal Care and Utilization Committee of Hubei Academy of Preventive Medicine (202510026). C57BL/6 (8 weeks old, approximately 25 g, female) purchased from Hubei Laboratory Animal Research Center were used in this study.

Animal model and treatment method

The mice were firstly cohoused for one week, and then treated with H2O2 (5 %) and hydroquinone (HQ) (5 %) to establish vitiligo model. Briefly, the mice were anesthetized with 5 % chloral hydrate (350 mg/kg), followed by shaving their dorsum with depilatory cream. After washed with saline solution, H2O2 (5 %) and hydroquinone (5 %) were sequentially applied on the dorsal skin of mice, each 1 h apart. The combination treatment was repeated twice a day for 30 days. Then, the mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6) as follows: 5 % H2O2 + 5 % hydroquinone + Vaseline, 5 % H2O2 + 5 % hydroquinone + Cu/Zn-TA (dispersed in Vaseline), 5 % H2O2 + 5 % hydroquinone + Zn-TA (dispersed in Vaseline). In addition, healthy mice treated only with Vaseline were set as negative control group (n = 6). The Cu/Zn-TA or Zn-TA nanosheets were dispersed in Vaseline (2 mg/mL) and applied on the dorsal skin of mice (100 mg/kg, twic a day) 2 h after H2O2 and hydroquinone applied on the skin of back. The above treatments were repeated for 7 days, and then the mice were euthanized with skin tissues harvested for analysis.

The uniform dispersion of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets in Vaseline was obtained as follows. Firstly, 2 mg of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets were ground and dispersed in 200 μL of glycerin through vigorous ultrasonication. Then, the aforementioned glycerin was combined with 800 μL of commercial Vaseline Lotion via mechanical agitation until the Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets were uniformly dispersed within the resulting emulsion. Neither phase separation nor aggregation was observed after the emulsion has been standing for 7 days (Fig. S1).

Antioxidant analysis in vivo

The harvested skin tissues were washed with cold PBS and the fat was scraped off by scalpel. Then, the skin tissues were cut into small pieces and incubated in DEME containing collagenase/dispase overnight at 4 °C. After that, the excess enzyme solution was removed and the skin pieces were washed with cold PBS, accompanied by redispersing in cold DMEM. The epidermis and dermis layers were gently separate by forceps, followed by placing in 0.25 % Trypsin-EDTA solution for 45 min of incubation at 4 °C. After trypsin digestion, cold DMEM containing DNase (0.2 mg/mL) and 15 % FBS was added into the above solution to inhibit trypsin activity. The mixture was vibrated for 10 s, and then passed through a 100 mm nylon mesh to remove the large debris. The obtained cells in the digestion mixture were centrifuged and the cell pellet were washed once. Finally, the intracellular ROS was stained with DCFH-DA and analyzed by flow cytometer.

Anti-inflammatory activity in vivo

The harvested skin tissues were washed with cold PBS and the fat was scraped off by scalpel. After that, the tissues were homogenized with RIPA lysate, and then centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min. Finally, the supernatant was collected, followed by detection of anti-inflammatory (IL-10) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) by commercial ELISA Kits (Solarbio, China).

Tyrosinase activity in vivo

The harvested skin tissues were washed with cold PBS and the fat was scraped off by scalpel. After that, the tissues were homogenized with RIPA lysate, and then centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min. Subsequently, 50 μL of 1 % Triton X-100 was added into the supernatant, which was then placed in an ultracold refrigerator for 30 min before being taken out for thawing. After repeating the above steps three times, 50 μL of 0.25 % L-Dopa was added into the supernatant with incubated in a shaker at 37 °C for 2 h. Finally, the absorbance at 475 nm was recorded by a microplate reader.

Melanin generation in vivo

The harvested skin tissues were washed with cold PBS and the fat was scraped off by scalpel. After that, the tissues were homogenized with RIPA lysate, and then centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min. Subsequently, 100 μL of NaOH solution (0.2 M) was added into the supernatant, followed by incubation in a shaker at 37 °C for 1 h. Finally, the absorbance at 490 nm was recorded by a microplate reader.

Histological analysis

The harvested skin tissues were fixed in 4 % formalin solution for 48 h. Subsequently, the samples were successively dehydrated in a series of concentrations of ethanol and dimethylbenzene. After that, the samples were embedded in paraffin, followed by cutting into sections (8 μm) for Hematoxylin–Eosin (H&E) and Masson-Fontana staining. In addition, the H&E analysis for main organs, including heart, liver, lung, spleen and kidney was performed with the same method.

Statistical analysis

The results are shown as the mean ± standard deviation of the mean. The drawing software is GraphPad Prism and Origin. Student's t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used in statistical significance analysis. Values with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant (∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001).

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets

The Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets were synthesized through a postsynthetic exchange approach, aided by surfactant and ultrasonication. Firstly, Zn-TA nanosheets were prepared by mixing sodium salt of TA solution with PVP solution containing Zn(OAc)2 under intense ultrasonication. Due to the regulating effect of PVP, Zn2+ can coordinate with the deprotonated carboxyl group in TA and further self-assemble into Zn-TA nanosheets [30,31]. Meanwhile, ultrasonication was employed to reduce the size of Zn-TA nanosheets by fragmenting them [32]. Subsequently, the obtained Zn-TA nanosheets were incubated in solution of Cu(OAc)2 at room temperature for exchanging Zn2+ with Cu2+. The observed color of Zn-TA nanosheets was changed from white to paled green after Cu2+ entering into the nodes of frameworks (Fig. S2). The morphology of as-prepared samples was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As depicts in Fig. S3, Zn2+ ions coordinate with TA to form micron-sized building blocks without the participant of PVP and ultrasonication. However, the Zn-TA MOFs synthesized in the presence of PVP (without ultrasonication) exhibit a 2D rhomboid-shape with lateral dimensions of 2.67 ± 0.44 μm (Fig. S4), indicated the regulation effect of PVP on the formation of 2D nanosheets. And the employment of ultrasonication can break the nanosheets into irregular-shape pieces with smaller lateral dimensions of 266 ± 58 nm (Fig. 1a–c), which make them to satisfy the size range of 200–300 nm for dermal drug delivery [33,34]. Moreover, both SEM and TEM images (Fig. 1b–d) show that the surface of Zn-TA nanosheets become rough after Zn2+ in the MOFs are exchanged with Cu2+. The signals of C, O, Zn and Cu are exhibited from the dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) spectra of Zn/Cu-TA nanosheets (Fig. S5). And the corresponding elemental mapping images (Fig. 1e) further reveal their uniform distribution in Zn/Cu-TA nanosheets. Atomic-force microscopy (AFM) image (Fig. S6) reveals Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets characterized by lateral dimensions of up to 205 nm and thicknesses ranging from 3.6 to 4.5 nm. The crystalline structure of the synthesized materials was characterized by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. As depicted in Fig. 1f, the position of main diffraction peaks of Zn-TA nanosheets are align with the previously reported XRD pattern of MOF-5 (CCDC 938392) [35]. However, the order of diffraction peak intensity has a significant change due to the preferred orientation of Zn-TA nanosheets [36,37]. Meanwhile, the XRD pattern of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets resembles that of Zn-TA nanosheets, suggesting that the introduction of Cu2+ doesn't alter the crystal structure of Zn-TA nanosheets. This similarity may be due to the comparable ionic radius of Cu2+ (0.72 Å) and Zn2+ (0.78 Å) [38]. According to the reported structure of MOF-5 [39], it can be inferred that Zn-TA nanosheets possess a symmetric unit consisting of Zn4O tetrahedron linked by TA molecules (Fig. S7). In the Zn4O tetrahedron, a single O atom is bonded to four Zn atoms, with each edge of the tetrahedron capped by a -CO2 group. To further investigate the structure of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets, all spin-polarized density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna ab-initio simulation package (VASP) [40]. The exchange-correlation interactions were described within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA), employing the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional [41]. The electron-ion interactions were treated using the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method [42], with a plane-wave cut-off energy set to 400 eV. To account for van der Waals interactions, Grimme's DFT-D3 dispersion correction method was adopted [43]. During structural optimization, all atomic models were fully relaxed until the convergence criteria for the total energy and residual forces were below 10−4 eV and 0.03 eV/Å, respectively. The Brillouin zone was sampled using a 2 × 2 × 2 Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh for this process. The unit cell of pristine Zn-TA nanosheets is consisting of 32 Zn, 104 O, 192 C, and 96 H atoms (Fig. S7). According to the ICP-OES result that the contents of Zn2+ and Cu2+ in Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets are 24.9 % (w/w) and 8.9 % (w/w) (Fig. 1k), 8 Zn atoms were replaced by Cu atoms to simulate the Cu-doped systems, including the random doping and motif doping (4 Zn atom in the fundamental building unit). As illustrated in Figs. S8–9, structural models featuring motif doping exhibit lower relative energies compared to those of randomly doped structural models. And the stimulated XRD patterns corresponding to motif doping models (Fig. S8) are consistent to that of Zn-TA nanosheets. These results suggested that Cu atoms preferred to replace Zn atoms in fundamental building unit.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was employed to characterize the surface chemistry of synthesized materials. The XPS survey scan spectra (Fig. S10) clearly display the signals assigned to C, O and Zn elements in both Zn-TA and Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets. Whereas high-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectra of Zn 2p in Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets show a clear shift to higher energy (0.4 eV) compared to Zn-TA nanosheets (Fig. 1g). This shift occurs because the electronegative Cu2+ attract electrons, reducing the electron density of Zn2+ [44]. In addition, high-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectra reveal peaks at approximately 935 eV and 955 eV, which are attributed to Cu 2p of Cu2+ [45], exclusively in the spectrum of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets (Fig. 1h). It's noted that the binding energies of Cu 2p in Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets are attributed to Cu-O bond instead of Cu-Cu bond (932.5 eV and 952.4 eV) [46], indicating that Cu2+ is coordinated with terephthalic acid instead of simply absorbed on Zn-TA nanosheets. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis showed that the typical peaks at 1680 cm−1 and 1290 cm−1 corresponding to σ C=O and δ O-H vibrations in the carboxyl group of TA are absent in Zn-TA nanosheets (Fig. 1i). Instead, peaks at 1374 cm−1 and 1582 cm−1 associated with symmetric (νs(COO-)) and anti-symmetric (νas(COO-)) vibrations of carboxylate appeared, and the peak at 655 cm−1 assigned to symmetric stretching vibration of Zn-O-Zn is also observed, indicating the successful coordination between Zn2+ and carboxyl group of TA [44,47]. Additionally, a peak at 925 cm−1 attributed to Cu-O stretching vibration emerged after incorporating Cu2+ into the nanosheets [48]. This finding further confirmed that Cu2+ coordinated with the carboxyl group of TA rather than merely adsorbing onto the Zn-TA nanosheets.

Fig. 1j shows that the zeta potential of Zn-TA nanosheets is changed from −1.8 mV to −4.8 mV after doping Cu2+ into Zn-TA nanosheets. Given that skin is negatively charged under normal physiological conditions [49], the negative charge of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets help them to minimize nonspecific adhesion to the stratum corneum and avoid clumping to block the skin passage. And based on the reports that negative surface charge can facilitate follicular uptake [50], Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets with negative zeta potential may have deep skin penetration through hair follicles.

Ability of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets for catalyzing dopamine polymerization

It has been found that transition metal ions can mediate the polymerization of dopamine (Dopa), leading to the formation of transition-metal-ion polydopamine complex [51]. And the metal centers in MOFs, usually acting as Lewis acidic sites, can coordinate with phenolic oxygen atoms which act as Lewis bases [[52], [53], [54]]. Inspired by this, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets were hypothesized to have potential for catalyzing the polymerization of dopamine. To verify this speculation, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets (100 μg/mL) were incubated with dopamine (20 μg/mL) solution for 8 h, and the absorbance of the mixture solution were monitored by UV–vis spectrophotometer. Compared to pure dopamine solution or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets solution, the color of the above mixture solution gradually darkens with the incubation time increases (Fig. 2a). And the UV–vis absorption spectra (Fig. 2b) shows that there is an obvious absorption peak around 660 nm appearing in the spectra of solution containing dopamine and Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets. Additionally, the absorption peak exhibits red-shifts and increases in intensity as the incubation time extends (Fig. 2b). While there are no peaks existing in the spectra of pure dopamine solution or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets solution within 8 h (Fig. 2c and d). These results indicate that Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can catalyze the polymerization of dopamine. To evaluate the influences of metal exchange in the nanosheets on catalyzing dopamine polymerization, the absorbance intensity of dopamine solution incubated with free TA or Zn-TA nanosheets was also detected. Fig. S11 demonstrates that the dopamine solution exhibits no significant absorption peak after incubation with free TA for 8 h, indicating that TA molecules lack the ability to catalyze dopamine polymerization. On the other hand, the absorption intensity of dopamine solution mixed with Zn-TA nanosheets is lower than that mixed with Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets after incubating for the same period of time. Meanwhile, the performances of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets and Zn-TA nanosheets for catalyzing dopamine polymerization with using different concentrations of dopamine or nanosheets were also investigated with the same method. As shown in Fig. S12, both Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets and Zn-TA nanosheets can increase the absorption intensity of dopamine solution with the dopamine concentrations rise from 2.5 μg/mL to 20 μg/mL. For the system with constant dopamine concentration, the increase of the absorption intensity was positively correlated with Cu/Zn-TA or Zn-TA nanosheets concentration within 200 μg/mL (Fig. S13). And under optimal reaction conditions (20 μg/mL of dopamine and 200 μg/mL of nanosheets) with 8 h of incubation, the absorption intensity increase induced by Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets (ΔAbs. = 0.86) is significantly higher than that caused by Zn-TA nanosheets (ΔAbs. = 0.52). To further investigate the mechanism of dopamine polymerization catalyzed by Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets, the polymerisates were collected for various characterizations. SEM image (Fig. S14a) and corresponding EDS spectrum (Fig. S14b) show that the polymerisates are irregularly spherical with C, O and Zn elements coexisted in them. The emergence of Zn signal in the polymerisates implies that Zn2+ chelated with dopamine oligomers is occurred during the polymerization process. And unlike the amorphous structure of standard polydopamine (PDA) [55], the XRD pattern of polymerisates shows clear diffraction peaks (Fig. 2e) which are not matched with dopamine or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets, indicated the crystal strucuture of the polymerisates. The FTIR spectra (Fig. 2f) show that the peaks at 1606 cm−1 and 1506 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching of aromatic C-C bonds in indole and C-N bending in indoline of standard PDA, are also present in the spectrum of the polymerisates [56]. Beyond that, the peaks at 1018 cm−1 and 880 cm−1 assigned to Zn-O stretching vibrations are observed in the spectrum of the polymerisates [57]. Since the Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets are insoluble in acid solutions, the generated PDA were fully separated from Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets by dissolving them in concentrated nitric acid and quantitatively analyzed via ICP-OES. The result exhibits that (16.8 ± 0.29)% of Zn2+ have been transferred from Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets to the produced PDA after 8 h of reaction. These results further suggested that in the process of dopamine polymerization catalyzed by Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets, Zn2+ are chelated with PDA units to form Zn2+-PDA complexes. Given that the catalytic efficiency of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets is higher than that of Zn-TA nanosheets, the role of Cu2+ ions in catalyzing dopamine polymerization was investigated from the Lewis acid centers and Jahn-Teller distortion. The temperature-programmed desorption of ammonia (NH3-TPD-MS) characterization was performed to elucidate the acidity of Zn-TA nanosheets and Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets. As depicted in Fig. 2g, both Zn-TA nanosheets and Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets exhibit weak acid sites, with NH3 desorption peaks around 150 °C. And combined with the similar peak integration areas, it can be found that replacing Zn2+ with Cu2+ in Zn-TA nanosheets has no significant effect on their acid content. Excluding the influence of the strength and content of Lewis acid on catalytic efficiency, the improvement in catalytic dopamine polymerization when Cu2+ is replaced with Zn2+ in Zn-TA nanosheets can be ascribed to Jahn-Teller distortion (Fig. 2h). Owing to the d9 electronic configuration of Cu2+, the introduction of Cu2+ into Zn-TA nanosheets induces the length of axial Cu-O bonds being strongly elongated, while the other equatorial Cu-O bonds being slightly decreased [58]. This intense distortion at the Cu site will be transferred to the adjacent Zn nodes through the shared organic ligands. The regular tetrahedral bond angles and lengths at the Zn node were thus forced to change, causing the Zn-O bond to weaken and become unstable. This makes it easier for the Zn-O bond to break after coordinating with dopamine. Based on the above analysis, the mechanism of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets to catalyze dopamine polymerization was illustrated in Fig. 2i. Specifically, Zn2+ as a Lewis acid center in Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets tends to accept electron pairs offered by phenolic hydroxyl group in dopamine, which acts as a Lewis base center, inducing the formation of Lewis acid-base adduct. During this process, the O-H bond in the phenolic hydroxyl group becomes polarized, resulting in significant weakening of the bond between the hydrogen and oxygen atoms. Water molecules can then easily capture the protons, deprotonating the phenolic hydroxyl group in dopamine. And the generated phenoxide anions can bind more strongly to Zn2+ than carboxylate anions due to the formation of a more stable chelate ring with two adjacent phenolic hydroxyl groups [59]. Moreover, the Jahn-Teller distortion derived from the introduction of Cu2+ into Zn-TA nanosheets makes Zn2+ more vulnerable to attack and removal by competing ligands. Accompanied by the coordination between Zn2+ and phenolic hydroxyl groups via Zn-O bond, dopamine was gradually oxidized and polymerized, forming Zn-PDA complexes [60].

Ability of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets for upregulation of tyrosinase activity

Inspired by the ability of MOFs to enhance the catalytic performance of enzymes [61], the activity of tyrosinase in the presence of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets was assessed by using a spectrophotometric assay with dopamine hydrochloride as a substrate [62]. Fig. 3a depicts that the dopamine solution has low absorbance intensity at 475 nm after incubating with tyrosinase for 3 min. However, with Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets added, the absorbance intensity of the solution significantly increase within the same time of incubation, and the color of the solution changes from colorless to reddish brown. These results indicate that Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can promote tyrosinase to oxidize dopamine into dopachrome. To further investigate the factors affecting the tyrosinase-directed oxidation of dopamine, the initial rate of dopamine oxidation was examined in the presence of 0.5 μmol/mL of dopamine, 25 μg/mL of tyrosinase and different samples, including TA (100 μg/mL), Cu-TA MOFs (100 μg/mL), Zn-TA nanosheets (100 μg/mL), Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets (100 μg/mL), Cu2+ (10 μg/mL, consistent with the Cu2+ content in Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets) or Zn2+ (20 μg/mL, consistent with the Cu2+ content in Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets). As shown in Fig. 3b, the initial rate of dopamine oxidation catalyzed by tyrosinase rises from 0.018 μmol/mL/min to 0.1 μmol/mL/min with addition of Zn-TA nanosheets. Similarly, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can assist tyrosinase to raise the oxidation rate to 0.094 μmol/mL/min. Whereas, Cu-TA MOFs have minimal impact on tyrosinase activity, as the oxidation rate of dopamine in their presence along with tyrosinase was only 0.024 μmol/mL/min. Additionally, Zn2+, Cu2+ and TA respectively exhibit positive effect (0.062 μmol/mL/min), negligible effect (0.022 μmol/mL/min), and negative effect (6.83 × 10−5 μmol/mL/min) on tyrosinase activity for catalyzing dopamine oxidation. The strong inhibition of tyrosinase caused by TA can be ascribed to its effective chelation of Cu2+ in tyrosinase [63]. However, this inhibition effect can be reduced after coordinating TA with metal ions. Furthermore, the concentration influences of Zn-TA nanosheets, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets or Cu-TA MOFs on the initial rate of dopamine oxidation catalyzed by tyrosinase was assessed. Fig. 3c,d, Fig. S15 and Table S1show that the oxidation rate of dopamine remains largely unchanged with variations in the concentration of Cu-TA MOFs up to 200 μg/mL, while it positively correlates on Zn-TA nanosheets or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets up to 100 μg/mL. To reveal the influence of Zn-TA nanosheets or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets on tyrosinase enzymatic reaction kinetics, steady-state kinetic assay were conducted with varying concentration levels of dopamine (0.04, 0.08, 0.1, 0.5, 1 μmol/mL). Based on the Michaelis-Menten reaction and the Lineweaver-Burk plot, the maximum initial velocity (Vmax) and Michaelis Effect (Km) were determined. As shown in Fig. 3e,f and Fig. S16, the Km value of tyrosinase is 0.1 μmol/mL, and further increased to 0.37 μmol/mL or 0.64 μmol/mL after combining with Zn-TA nanosheets or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets. This result indicates that the affinity between tyrosinase and dopamine decreases in the presence of Zn-TA nanosheets and Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets, likely due to the enrichment of the nanosheets on dopamine through Lewis acid-base coordination. Since the coordination between Zn2+ in the nanosheets and phenolic hydroxyl group of dopamine can polarize the O-H bond in the phenolic hydroxyl group, dopamine becomes more susceptible to oxidation by tyrosinase [64]. This effect is confirmed by the significant increase in the Vmax value of tyrosinase from 0.059 μmol/mL/min to 0.33 μmol/mL/min or 0.46 μmol/mL/min when Zn-TA nanosheets or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets are present. And it can be seen that Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets are more effective in enhancing the catalytic rate of tyrosinase compared to Zn-TA nanosheets, which is due to the ability of Cu2+ to change valence, acting as an electron transfer medium to facilitate the redox process in catalytic reactions [65]. To further confirm this hypothesis, electrodes constructed by the combination of tyrosinase with Zn-TA nanosheets or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets were characterized by cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurement in the presence of dopamine. As illustrated in Fig. 3g, the oxidation peak currents in the CV plot of the Cu/Zn-TA-TYR electrode are significantly higher than those of the Zn-TA-TYR electrode. Meanwhile, the electrochemical activity of pure Cu/Zn-TA electrode was also evaluated with the same method. And the CV plot (Fig. S17) depicts no significant redox peak currents. Combining the above results with the report that tyrosinase activity relies on its dual-core copper center [63], it can be concluded that Cu2+ in Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can engage in the Cu2+/Cu+ redox cycle to improve electron transfer between dopamine and tyrosinase. In addition, the distinct reduction peak visible in the plot of Zn-TA-TYR electrode is not detectable in the plot of Cu/Zn-TA-TYR electrode. This is because Cu2+ is more prone to be reduced than dopaquinone in the negative potential region, thereby consuming electrons and hindering the reduction of dopaquinone. Combined with the finding that Cu-TA MOFs have negligible impact on improving the catalytic efficiency of tyrosinase, it can be concluded that Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can enhance the catalytic rate of tyrosinase through the synergistic effect of Zn2+ and Cu2+. In detail (Fig. 3h), dopamine was accumulated around Zn2+ through Lewis acid-base coordination, leading to the polarization of phenolic hydroxyl groups and making them more susceptible to oxidation. Then Cu2+ facilitates the transfer of electrons from the polarized hydroxyl group of dopamine to the dual-core copper center of tyrosinase through ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) [45], accelerating dopamine oxidation through the Cu2+/Cu+ redox cycle. Hence, the collaboration between Cu2+ and Zn2+ in Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets enhances tyrosinase activity, facilitating the oxidation of dopamine to dopachrome. Moreover, the Vmax/Km ratio for tyrosinase under various conditions was calculated to comprehensively assess the catalytic efficiency. And the results show that free tyrosinase has a lower value of 0.59 mM−1 min−1 compared to both Zn-TA nanosheets (0.89 mM−1 min−1) and Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets (0.75 mM−1 min−1). Compared to Zn-TA nanosheets, the reduced value of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets stems from their higher capacity to catalyze dopamine polymerization, which restricts dopamine substrate binding to tyrosinase.

Ability of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets for scavenging ROS

The capacity of TA molecules to trap •OH by forming hydroxylated terephthalate may endow Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets the ability to scavenge •OH [29]. This property was further investigated through colorimetric assay by employing methylene blue (MB) as •OH indicator. Because MB can be degraded in the presence of •OH, its absorption intensity at 664 nm greatly diminished from 1.87 to 0.24 in the •OH generation system composed of Fe2+ and H2O2 (Fig. 4a) [66]. However, the addition of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets (200 μg/mL) can make the absorption intensity of MB recovered to 1.21, indicating the •OH scavenging ability of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets. And the •OH elimination rate of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets is concentration dependent within 400 μg/mL, reaching a high of 61.79 % (Fig. 4b). In addition, the •OH scavenging ability of TA molecules and Zn-TA nanosheets was also assessed with the same method to further investigate the factors influencing •OH scavenging ability of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets. As shown in Fig. 4c and Fig. S18a, the •OH scavenging rate of Zn-TA nanosheets (200 μg/mL) is similar to that of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets (200 μg/mL), and is much higher than that of TA molecules (200 μg/mL), which only recover the absorption intensity of MB to 0.4. This result suggests that the •OH scavenging ability of TA molecules can be enhanced by coordinating and assembling with Zn2+. Since Zn2+ as Lewis acid center in Zn-TA nanosheets can reduce the electron cloud density of the benzene ring, it makes the bond energy of C-H bond reduced [67]. Then, H atoms in the benzene ring of TA are inclined to undergo hydrogen atom transfer reactions with •OH, inducing the improvement of •OH scavenging efficiency. On the other hand, the replacement of Zn2+ with Cu2+ in Zn-TA nanosheets has negligible impact on their •OH scavenging ability. Fortunately, the complexes containing Cu2+ can act as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) due to the Cu2+/Cu+ redox cycle [17], which endows Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets with the potential to eliminate •O2− and H2O2. Firstly, Cu2+/Cu+ redox cycle for SOD/CAT-like activity was confirmed by electrochemical measurement in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.0) with addition different concentrations of H2O2. As shown in Fig. S19, the Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets modified glass carbon electrode (Cu/Zn-TA-GCE) exhibits an anodic peak at 0.08 V and cathodic peak at −0.3 V, which are attributed to the electrochemical reaction of copper ions [70]. And with the concentration of H2O2 increased, both the corresponding anodic peak current and cathodic peak current are enhanced. Here, in the anodic pathway, Cu + undergoes electrochemical oxidation to form Cu2+ via the reaction 2Cu+ + H2O → 2Cu2+ + 2H+ + 2e−. The observed increase in anodic peak current in the presence of H2O2 can be attributed to the reduction of Cu2+ to Cu + by H2O2, which elevates the local concentration of Cu+ at the electrode surface [71,72]. Similarly, considering the cathodic pathway where Cu2+ is electrochemically reduced to Cu+ (i.e., Cu2+ + 2H+ + e− → Cu+ + H2O), it can be concluded that the increased cathodic peak current in the presence of H2O2 results from the elevated Cu2+ concentration at the electrode surface, which arises from the oxidation of Cu + to Cu2+ mediated by H2O2. These results confirm the regeneration of Cu(II) and Cu(I) in Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets during their reaction with H2O2. To evaluate the SOD-like activity of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets, the dismutation of •O2− catalyzed by Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets was detected by WST-8 (sodium,4-[2-(2-methoxy-4-nitrophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)tetrazol-2-ium-5-yl]benzene-1,3disulfonate) assay kit. The WST-8 can be reduced by •O2− which is generated via the xanthine/xanthine oxidase system, leading to the formation of WST-8 formazan with a prominent absorption peak at 450 nm. Fig. 4d depicts that Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can scavenge •O2− to inhibit the reduction of WST-8, as the absorption intensity of WST-8 formazan was significantly diminished after the addition of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets into the system. And the •O2− scavenging rate of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets is positively correlated with the nanosheets concentration up to 100 μg/mL, reaching a high of 95.8 % (Fig. 4e). Additionally, the effectiveness of Zn-TA nanosheets and TA molecules in eliminating •O2− was also investigated using the same method to further assess the role of Cu2+ in the nanosheets for SOD-like activity. And compared to Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets (100 μg/mL), which reduce the absorption intensity of WST-8 formazan from 0.395 to 0.017, Zn-TA nanosheets (100 μg/mL) and TA molecules (100 μg/mL) can only decrease the values to 0.344 and 0.25 (Fig. 4f and Fig. S18b), respectively, indicating their weak •O2− scavenging ability. This result further confirms that Cu2+ significantly contributes to the ability of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets to scavenge •O2−. Subsequently, the CAT-like activity of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets, Zn-TA nanosheets and TA molecules was evaluated by using a dissolved oxygen meter to detect the dissolved oxygen contents in H2O2 solution. As illustrated in Fig. 4g, the O2 content in both pure H2O2 solution and H2O2 solution mixed with TA molecules exhibits negligible changes within 30 min, indicating that TA molecules cannot catalyze the decomposition of H2O2 into O2. However, the addition of Zn-TA nanosheets (500 μg/mL) into the solution can make the O2 content increased from 7.76 mg/L to 18.3 mg/L after 30 min of incubation. This enhancement is ascribed to the interaction between H2O2 and Zn2+ as a Lewis acid center [73]. Moreover, the O2 content can even raise to 35.4 mg/L after incubating with Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets for 30 min, further revealing the positive effect of Cu2+ on the CAT-like activity of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets. Since SOD can catalyze the disproportionation of •O2− to generate H2O2, which can then be decomposed by CAT to produce O2 and H2O, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets, with their multi enzyme-like activities (SOD-like activity, CAT-like activity and •OH scavenging ability), can synergistically scavenge ROS by switching H2O2, •OH, and •O2− to H2O and O2 (Fig. 4h).

As illustrated in Fig. 5a, the cell viabilities of both B16 cells and MC-1 cells can reach 100 % even after 24 h of co-incubation with 100 μg/mL of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets, indicating a good biocompatibility. Fluorescence microscopy revealed that the cells co-incubated with Cu/Zn-TA-RhB nanosheets exhibit significant red fluorescence compared to those incubated with PBS (Fig. 5d). Moreover, the antioxidant ability of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets was evaluated in an in vitro oxidative stress model by pre-treating MC-1 cells with H2O2 (0.75 μmol/mL). Fig. 5b shows that the cell viability of MC-1 cells drops to 75.3 % after the treatment of H2O2. However, the addition of Zn-TA nanosheets can make the cell viability recover to 100.7 %, indicating the elimination of ROS. Moreover, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets display a stronger antioxidant capacity than Zn-TA nanosheets, which can increase the cell viability to 116 %. In addition, the cell viability exceeding 100 % in presence of Zn-TA nanosheets or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can be ascribed to Cu2+ and Zn2+, which are essential for promoting cell proliferation and supporting basic biochemical reactions [74]. Interestingly, cells treated with both Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets and H2O2 exhibit higher viability compared to those treated solely with H2O2 or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets. Since low concentrations of H2O2 can enhance the proliferation rate of normal cells [75], the increased cell viability observed with the combination of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets and H2O2 is attributed to the consumption of H2O2 by the nanosheets. This reduces H2O2 concentration to an optimal level that promotes MC-1 cell proliferation. To further prove the ROS elimination ability of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets, the ROS specific probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) was employed to quantify the intracellular ROS level in MC-1 cells with different treatments. As observed in Fig. 5e,f and Fig. S20, the intracellular ROS levels increase distinctly after H2O2 treatment with mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) raises from 15.26 to 161.35. However, the co-incubation with Zn-TA nanosheets or Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can induce the MFI values significantly decreased to 66.95 or 34.79. This indicates that Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets have a superior ROS-scavenging capacity compared to Zn-TA nanosheets. Encouraged by the good antioxidant capacity, the potential anti-inflammatory effect of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets was further investigated through ELISA assay to quantify the typical secretion of cytokines. Compared to the normal MC-1 cells, H2O2 treatment unsurprisingly accelerate the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α and IL-1β), while inhibit the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 5c–g,h). On the contrary, the treatment of Zn-TA nanosheets can reduce the secreted content of TNF-α stimulated by H2O2. By comparison with Zn-TA nanosheets, the treatment of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can further distinctly inhibit the secretion of TNF-α and IL-1β, and greatly promote the secretion of IL-10. Taken together, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets possess both excellent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory capacity in vitro, which is crucial for treating vitiligo.

In addition, the intracellular TYR activity was assessed by using levodopa (L-dopa), which exhibits a typical absorption peak at 475 nm after interacting with TYR [76]. As illustrated in Fig. 5i, the Zn-TA nanosheets and Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets treated cells respectively showed 1.19-fold and 1.31-fold greater TYR activity than the PBS treated group. Subsequently, the intracellular melanin content was investigated by the sodium hydroxide lytic cell method with a characteristic absorption peak at 490 nm [76]. In comparison with the PBS treated cells, the Zn-TA nanosheets and Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets treated groups respectively possess 1.38-fold and 1.67-fold greater melanin content (Fig. 5j). Taken together, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets possess strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory capacities, while also improving TYR activity and upregulating melanin content in vitro, making them promising for vitiligo treatment.

Then, the formed nanosheets were dispersed in Vaseline emulsion and topically applied onto the back of mice with their dorsal hair shaved. The temperature of the dorsal skin was monitored at different time points after applying the nanosheets. At each measurement, the temperature corresponded to that of the mouse's back following 1-min exposure to near-infrared. As illustrated in Fig. 6a and b, the skin temperature of mice rose from 35 °C to 41 °C within 1 h of applying Cu/Zn-TA-IR780 nanosheets, and further increased to 45 °C after 4 h of application. Though the skin temperature diminished after applying the nanosheets for 8 h, it remained at 43 °C, which is significantly higher than that of untreated mice (35 °C). The ex vivo skin penetration of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets was assessed by directly observing their location in porcine skin, a suitable biomimetic system of human skin [77]. In detail, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets were labeled with Rhodamine B (Cu/Zn-TA-RhB) and topically applied on the pig skin for 8 h of incubation. Subsequently, the skin was washed with PBS, sliced and observed through fluorescence imaging. As shown in Fig. 6c–e, the pig skin topically applied with PBS (control group) have negligible fluorescence signal in the entire skin tissue. And the free RhB has limited penetration depth as the signal intensity decreases dramatically beyond a depth of 300 μm. However, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets and Zn-TA nanosheets exhibit similar fluorescence distributions, maintaining high signal intensity across a depth range of 0–600 μm. These results indicate that both Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets and Zn-TA nanosheets exhibit deep penetration into both the epidermis and dermis. As is known that melanocytes rest in the basal layer of the epidermis [78], the deep penetration of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets into the skin allows them to enter into melanocytes, promoting melanin production. And this function can be sustained due to their long-term skin retention. Though Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can enter into dermis, their signal intensity decreases along with the skin depth beyond 600 μm, indicates the limit penetration depth of the nanosheets. In addition, Franz diffusion cells were introduced to further assess the percutaneous penetration ability of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets. Pig skin was clamped in the Franz cells and the content of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets passed through the skin was measured through ICP-OES. As shown in Fig. S21, no signal of Zn2+ can be detected and only 0.17 % of Cu2+ was monitored within 24 h of diffusion. This result further confirms that Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets cannot penetrate the entire dermis, thus reducing the side effects caused by transdermal absorption (Fig. 6d).

Treatment of vitiligo in H2O2-hydroquinone induced mouse model

Encouraged by the promising in vitro data, the vitiligo therapy effect of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets was further evaluated in vivo. The vitiligo mouse model was established using a combination of H2O2 to cause oxidative damage to melanocytes, and hydroquinone to inhibit TYR activity [79,80]. While this model primarily recapitulates oxidative stress damage and tyrosinase inhibition, it may not fully encompass the intricate autoimmune pathology of human vitiligo. As shown in the experimental procedure (Fig. 7a), mice were applying H2O2 and hydroquinone for 37 consecutive days. On the 31st day of this modeling, mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6), and respectively applied Vaseline emulsion (1.25 mL), Vaseline emulsion containing Zn-TA nanosheets (1.25 mL, 2 mg/mL) as well as Vaseline emulsion containing Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets (1.25 mL, 2 mg/mL) twice a day (Fig. S22). The results (Fig. 7b) show that white patches are clearly visible on the dorsal skin of vitiligo mice treated with pure Vaseline, and the area of these patches progressively expands over time. In contrast, treatment with Zn-TA nanosheets inhibits the formation of white patches, with only a few visible after 7 days of treatment. Furthermore, mice treated with Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets exhibit negligible white patches throughout the entire treatment period, indicating a promising therapeutic effect on vitiligo. Results of the hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. 7c–e) show that the skin of vitiligo mice treated with Vaseline has mild incomplete keratinization, with the disappearance and necrosis of epidermal cell nuclei. In addition, there is extensive inflammatory infiltration in the dermis, and a reduction in the number of skin appendages. Nevertheless, the treatment of Zn-TA nanosheets can diminish inflammatory infiltration and normalize the accessory structures, such as hair follicles and sebaceous glands. Notably, the skin remedies by Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets only exhibits slight variation compared to that of normal mice. Moreover, the quantitative analysis of epidermal thickness demonstrates that the combination of H2O2 and hydroquinone can cause serious epidermal hypertrophy effect, increasing thickness to 2.5 times that of normal skin. And the treatment of Zn-TA nanosheets only slightly reduces the hyperplasia, with thickness remaining at 2.2 times that of normal skin (Fig. 7d). Fortunately, the skin treated with Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can restore epidermal thickness to the same level as normal skin. Further analysis of the skin with Masson-Fontana staining (Fig. 7c–f) establishes that the melanocyte area ratio in the vitiligo mouse model treated by Vaseline dramatically decreases from 2.75 % to 0.29 % in comparison with normal skin. While the treatment of Zn-TA nanosheets and Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can restore melanin, with melanocyte areas of 0.86 % and 1.13 % detected, respectively. And compared with the vitiligo mouse model treated by pure Vaseline, some melanin particles are obviously observed to migrate to the surface of the skin after the treatment of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets (Fig. S23). Conclusion

In summary, a multifunctional 2D bimetallic MOF nanosheets (Cu/Zn-TA) was designed for the treatment of vitiligo by replacing Zn2+ with Cu2+ in Zn-TA nanosheets, synthesized using surfactant assistance and ultrasonication. Due to the Lewis acid center caused by Zn2+, the obtained Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can make O-H bond in the phenolic hydroxyl group of dopamine polarized. Meanwhile, the Jahn-Teller distortion derived from Cu2+ makes Zn2+ in the nanosheets susceptible to capture by phenoxide anions. This combined effects facilitate the catalysis of dopamine polymerization by Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets. Additionally, the activity of tyrosinase-directed oxidation of dopamine was greatly enhanced by Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets. The addition of Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets increased the Vmax of tyrosinase from 0.059 μmol/mL/min to 0.46 μmol/mL/min. This phenomenon arises from the synergistic effect, including dopamine enrichment around Zn2+ through Lewis acid-base coordination, and the enhanced electrons transfer from the polarized hydroxyl group of dopamine to the dual-core copper center of tyrosinase caused by Cu2+. Furthermore, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets exhibited intrinsic SOD-like activity, CAT-like activity, and •OH scavenging capacity due to the coordination between TA and Zn2+, and Cu2+/Cu+ redox cycle. Benefiting from these properties, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets effectively scavenged ROS in H2O2 stimulated MC-1 cells, leading to decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines and increased anti-inflammatory cytokines. Meanwhile, the TYR activity in MC-1 cells was enhanced, along with an increase in melanin content, following co-incubation with Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets. Owing to the appropriate size with lateral dimensions under 300 nm, Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets can penetrate the basal layer of the epidermis and enter into melanocytes, but they cannot penetrate the entire dermis to cause side effects. The in vivo experiments confirmed that Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets not only protected melanocytes from oxidative damage, but also increased melanin content through both exogenous generation and endogenous stimulation, resulting a positive therapeutic effect on vitiligo in a H2O2-hydroquinone induced mouse model. And the H&E staining for major organs from Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets treated mice showed low in vivo toxicity. Overall, the developed bimetallic Cu/Zn-TA nanosheets pave a promising approach for vitiligo treatment based on the synergistic effect of multiple mechanisms.
