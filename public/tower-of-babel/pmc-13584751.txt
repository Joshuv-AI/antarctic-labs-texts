Understanding Postmarketing Requirements and Commitments for CYP3A‐Mediated Drug–Drug Interactions: An Analysis of FDA‐Approved Drugs From 2015 to 2024
Authors: Jingjing Yu, Ichiko Petrie, Sophie Argon, Katie Owens, Isabelle Ragueneau‐Majlessi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAddressing knowledge gaps at drug approval through drug–drug interaction (DDI)‐related postmarketing requirements (PMRs) and commitments (PMCs) is essential for safe and effective use. This analysis reviewed CYP3A‐related PMRs and PMCs, the most evaluated enzyme in postmarketing studies, to characterize their scope. CYP3A‐related PMRs, PMCs, and labeling recommendations were identified for small‐molecule new molecular entities approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (2015–2024) using the Certara Drug Interaction Database (DIDB). The latest product labels from Drugs@FDA were reviewed for updates. Drugs were categorized as CYP3A substrates, inhibitors, or inducers based on PMR/PMC language. Of 332 approved drugs, 51 (15%) had CYP3A‐related PMRs/PMCs, totaling 91 studies (53 PMRs, 37 PMCs, and 1 unspecified). About two‐thirds were oncology drugs, including 25 kinase inhibitors. As substrates, 37 drugs had 65 requests: 26 with inhibitors (all PMRs) and 39 with inducers (30 PMCs, 8 PMRs, and 1 unspecified); 20 included physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling. As precipitants, 25 drugs had 26 requests: 12 as inhibitors (11 PMRs, 1 PMC), 14 as inducers (8 PMRs, 6 PMCs), with only 1 PBPK option. Requests most often involved studies with a CYP3A substrate (N = 24), moderate inducer (N = 21), strong inducer (N = 15), strong inhibitor (N = 10), and moderate inhibitor (N = 9). Initial labeling recommendations were issued for 60 studies (65%; 35 drugs), and 53 studies (58%; 29 drugs) resulted in label updates. Overall, CYP3A‐mediated DDIs remain a key focus of postmarketing evaluation, particularly for oncology drugs. PMRs and PMCs are critical for resolving uncertainties and informing label updates.

Introduction

Drug–drug interactions (DDIs) remain a major source of variability in drug exposure and clinical response, with important implications for both safety and efficacy. Among the enzymes involved in drug metabolism, cytochrome P450 3A (CYP3A) plays a particularly prominent role, contributing to the metabolism of approximately 30%–50% of marketed drugs [1]. Its involvement is even more pronounced in oncology, where CYP3A is implicated in the metabolism of 94% of kinase inhibitors approved between 2011 and 2022 [2]. The potential for CYP3A‐mediated DDIs is routinely evaluated during drug development. However, despite extensive pre‐approval assessment, residual uncertainties often remain at the time of regulatory approval, especially when clinical studies are constrained by ethical, logistical, or scientific limitations. To address these knowledge gaps, regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) may issue postmarketing requirements (PMRs) and postmarketing commitments (PMCs) [3, 4]. The phrase of PMRs and PMCs refers to studies and clinical trials that sponsors conduct after approval to gather additional information about a product's safety, efficacy, or optimal use. PMRs include studies and clinical trials that sponsors are required to conduct under one or more statutes or regulations, while PMCs are studies or clinical trials that a sponsor has agreed to conduct, but that are not required by a statute or regulation [5]. These studies are intended to generate additional data to better characterize DDI risk, refine dosing recommendations, and inform labeling. In particular, CYP3A‐mediated interactions are frequently the subject of such requests due to the enzyme's broad substrate specificity and susceptibility to inhibition and induction. Postmarketing studies may include in vitro evaluations, clinical DDI trials, physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling, or other approaches to evaluate the magnitude and clinical relevance of interactions.

While PMRs and PMCs play a critical role in ensuring the safe and effective use of newly approved drugs, there has been limited evaluation of their scope and focus, particularly in the context of CYP3A‐mediated DDIs. A better understanding of the types of requests issued by regulatory authorities could provide insights into common gaps in pre‐approval packages and inform future drug development strategies. The objective of this study was to evaluate PMRs and PMCs associated with FDA‐approved drugs from 2015 to 2024 that pertain to CYP3A‐mediated clinical DDIs, with the aim of characterizing the nature, frequency, and scientific rationale underlying these requests. In addition, comparisons between initial and updated labels resulting from these requested studies were conducted to assess the impact of PMRs and PMCs on drug labeling over time.

Methods

CYP3A‐related PMRs, PMCs, and associated original labeling recommendations were identified for small‐molecule new molecular entities (NMEs) approved by the FDA from 2015 to 2024 using drug monographs obtained from Certara Drug Interaction Database (DIDB, https://www.druginteractionsolutions.org/). The drug monographs for these NMEs were identified using the DIDB “All NDAs and BLAs in DIDB” query and were subsequently reviewed manually. During the manual review, relevant PMRs, PMCs, and associated labeling recommendations were extracted and the DDI information was further analyzed based on each drug's role in requested studies (i.e., substrates, inhibitors, or inducers), study approach (i.e., clinical trials, PBPK, or in vitro), and type of co‐administered drugs evaluated (e.g., CYP3A strong inhibitors, CYP3A sensitive substrates). Drugs were classified as CYP3A substrates, inhibitors, or inducers based on the language in the PMRs and PMCs. Of note, if a drug was requested to be studied as a CYP3A inhibitor, it was included as an inhibitor in this analysis even if the completed study later identified it as an inducer. For drugs demonstrating both inhibitory and inductive effects on CYP3A in vitro, where the requested study did not specify the role, classification was based on the net effect observed in completed PMRs or PMCs; if such data were unavailable, they were categorized as inhibitors for the purposes of this analysis.

The latest product labels were retrieved from Drugs@FDA (search conducted in August 2025) to evaluate whether the requested studies resulted in updates to labeling. Label changes were categorized as follows: (1) study details updated with no change to the original labeling recommendations; (2) labeling recommendations downgraded (e.g., from avoiding co‐administration to dose adjustment or monitoring; from avoiding moderate or strong CYP3A inducers to avoiding strong inducers only); (3) original labeling recommendations removed; and (4) labeling recommendations upgraded (e.g., from no recommendation to the addition of guidance such as dose adjustment, monitoring, or avoidance of co‐administration). The FDA publicly available Postmarketing Requirements and Commitments Database (Postmarketing Requirements and Commitments: Downloadable Database File | FDA) was consulted to get current status information of the requested studies; however, although this database is updated quarterly, it does not provide a complete historical record of the final disposition of all PMRs and PMCs across the 2015–2024 study period. In particular, FDA indicates that fulfilled and released commitments are displayed for no more than 1 year after fulfillment or release. Therefore, for commitments identified from historical NDA reviews that are no longer present in the current database, their final disposition (i.e., fulfilled vs. released) cannot necessarily be determined from the current database alone. Consequently, the absence of study results or corresponding labeling changes in the latest product label should not be interpreted as evidence that the requested study had no impact on labeling. The commitment may have been released, or it may have been fulfilled without resulting in a labeling update following FDA review of the study findings.

Results

A total of 332 small‐molecule NME drugs were approved by the FDA between 2015 and 2024. Nearly half of these drugs (N = 151) were identified as CYP3A substrates, inhibitors, or inducers based on the DIDB DDI summaries. Among them, 51 drugs (34%) were associated with CYP3A‐related PMRs or PMCs, including 26 evaluated as substrates, 14 as precipitants (inhibitors and/or inducers), and 11 as both substrates and precipitants. This number slightly exceeded the total number of drugs associated with transporter‐related clinical PMRs and PMCs during the same period, highlighting the continued regulatory emphasis on characterizing CYP3A‐mediated DDIs. This observation is consistent with the central role of CYP3A in drug metabolism and its potential to cause clinically significant DDIs, making comprehensive CYP3A evaluation a key component of regulatory assessment. All CYP3A‐related PMRs and PMCs involved clinically relevant evaluations. Separately, postmarketing in vitro studies were requested for four drugs and five metabolites to evaluate their potential as substrates, inhibitors, or inducers of common drug‐metabolizing enzymes. However, none of these studies specifically evaluated CYP3A.

In total, 91 studies were requested, comprising 53 PMRs, 37 PMCs, and one unspecified (PMR or PMC) (Figure 1). This unspecified request, for omaveloxolone, involved a clinical study with a moderate CYP3A inducer. Regarding the status of the 91 requested studies, 24 studies requested from 2018 to 2024 were included in the FDA Postmarketing Requirements and Commitments Database (content current as of July 31, 2026), including 5 fulfilled, 1 released, 6 delayed, 7 ongoing, and 5 pending. The remaining 67 studies (74%), requested from 2015 to 2023, were no longer displayed in the current database and therefore could not be further classified as fulfilled or released based on the current database alone. When expressed as a proportion of all small‐molecule NMEs approved each year, the percentage of drugs with CYP3A‐related PMRs or PMCs showed a modest increase between 2018 and 2021 and again in 2024 (Figure 1). This temporal pattern is consistent with the continued emphasis on CYP3A‐DDI evaluation in FDA guidance issued during this period, although multiple factors likely contributed to the observed trend. Among the 51 drugs, 34 (70%) were indicated for oncology and 25 (50%) were kinase inhibitors (Figure 2). Drugs as Substrates

Requested Studies With Inhibitors

Twenty‐one drugs were requested to be evaluated with CYP3A inhibitors, corresponding to a total of 26 studies. Among these, 10 drugs were requested to be studied with strong CYP3A inhibitors, 1 with a dual P‐gp and strong CYP3A inhibitor, 9 with moderate CYP3A inhibitors, 2 with dual P‐gp and moderate CYP3A inhibitors, 2 with CYP3A inhibitors of unspecified potency, and 1 with dual CYP3A and CYP2C19 inhibitors. All these studies were issued as PMRs (Table 1).

For drugs with PMRs involving strong CYP3A inhibitors, in vitro data indicated that these drugs were primarily metabolized by CYP3A; however, only three (pacritinib, repotrectinib, and tazemetostat) had been evaluated clinically with a CYP3A inhibitor (e.g., moderate inhibitor, combined strong CYP3A inhibitor and P‐gp inhibitor, suboptimal dose of a strong CYP3A inhibitor) at the time of approval [6, 7, 8]. For drugs with PMRs involving moderate CYP3A inhibitors, the effects of strong CYP3A inhibitors had been investigated for most agents. However, such studies were not conducted for a subset of drugs such as lurbinectedin, pacritinib, and repotrectinib [6, 7, 9]. Notably, a substantial proportion of these drugs (13/21; including 2 metabolites) were also P‐gp substrates in vitro, prompting requests to assess the combined effects of P‐gp and CYP3A inhibition for selected agents. The combined effect on P‐gp together with CYP3A was requested for a few drugs. For example, pralsetinib, a kinase inhibitor indicated for non‐small cell lung cancer, demonstrated a 3.51‐fold increase in AUC and 1.84‐fold increase in C max when co‐administered with itraconazole, a dual P‐gp and strong CYP3A inhibitor. Given that unchanged pralsetinib was the predominant entity detected in plasma, urine, and feces, and considering the significant magnitude of pralsetinib increase, inhibition of P‐gp likely contributed to this DDI. Consequently, PMRs were issued to evaluate the effects of moderate CYP3A inhibitors and combined P‐gp and moderate CYP3A inhibitors on pralsetinib PK using PBPK modeling and simulations [10]. Similar PMRs were issued for repotrectinib, another kinase inhibitor indicated for the treatment of non‐small cell lung cancer [7].

Regarding evaluation approaches, 11 (31%) of the PMR studies had the option to be conducted using PBPK modeling and simulations. Specifically, PBPK approaches were more frequently applied to studies involving moderate CYP3A inhibitors (including dual moderate CYP3A and P‐gp inhibitor), with 8 of 11 such studies (73%) recommending PBPK evaluation. For example, brigatinib, a kinase inhibitor indicated for the treatment of non‐small cell lung cancer, showed a 2.01‐fold increase in AUC following itraconazole co‐administration. It was recommended to avoid concomitant administration with strong CYP3A inhibitors, but no specific labeling recommendation was provided for moderate CYP3A inhibitors [11]. Consequently, a PMR was issued to evaluate the effect of a moderate CYP3A inhibitor on the PK of brigatinib using PBPK approach [11].

From a safety perspective, initial labeling recommendations were given prior to the availability of PMR study results. Among the 35 PMRs, nearly half (N = 16) were associated with labeling recommendations. Most of them recommended avoiding co‐administration (N = 12), followed by dose adjustment (N = 3) and contraindication (N = 1). Pacritinib, a kinase inhibitor for the treatment of myelofibrosis, was the only drug for which co‐administration with strong CYP3A inhibitors was contraindicated.

In the most recent product labels, 25 (71%) of the 35 PMRs had study results updated, leading to revisions of the original labeling recommendations. Among the initial labeling recommendations to avoid co‐administration (N = 12), eight were subsequently updated. Specifically, five were downgraded to avoidance of only certain CYP3A inhibitors (N = 1), monitoring and dose modification (N = 2), or complete removal of the restriction (N = 2), leaving only three drugs with study details added but the original recommendation unchanged. For example, lurbinectedin, an antineoplastic agent indicated for the treatment of metastatic small cell lung cancer, had its recommendation revised from avoiding strong CYP3A inhibitors to avoiding co‐administration with only grapefruit and Seville oranges. Similarly, tepotinib, a kinase inhibitor indicated for non‐small cell lung cancer, was initially recommended to avoid concomitant use with dual strong CYP3A inhibitors and P‐gp inhibitors. Following completion of the PMR study, no clinically significant differences in the PK of tepotinib were observed when co‐administered with itraconazole. Consequently, the original restriction on CYP3A inhibitor co‐administration was removed [12]. Among the original dose adjustment labeling recommendations (N = 3), two were updated in the most recent labels and both were downgraded. Cannabidiol, indicated for the treatment of seizures associated with Lennox–Gastaut syndrome, Dravet syndrome, or tuberous sclerosis complex, initially included recommendations for dose reduction when co‐administered with moderate or strong CYP3A inhibitors. Following evaluation of PMR study results, these dosing restrictions were removed [13]. In contrast, nine PMRs had no initial specific labeling recommendations. Of these, five (involving four drugs) were later updated to include labeling changes, all related to studies with moderate CYP3A inhibitors or dual P‐gp inhibitors. These updates introduced new restrictions, either avoiding co‐administration (i.e., brigatinib and pralsetinib) or adjusting dose when used concomitantly (i.e., larotrectinib and pexidartinib) [14, 15, 16, 17].

Requested Studies With Inducers

Thirty‐seven drugs were issued PMR/PMCs to be evaluated with CYP3A inducers of varying potency, corresponding to a total of 39 studies. In contrast to studies with inhibitors discussed above, PMCs (n = 30) were far more common, accounting for nearly 80% of all requests (n = 39) (Table 1).

Most requests evolved evaluation with moderate CYP3A inducers (N = 21). Two thirds of these drugs (N = 14) have already been evaluated with rifampin, a strong CYP3A inducer, in clinical DDI studies; two exceptions (doravirine and zanubrutinib) were evaluated using PBPK modeling and simulations [18, 19]. Interestingly, among drugs for which studies were requested with strong CYP3A inducers (N = 15), 3 (erdafitinib, asciminib, and pacritinib) had prior rifampin data [6, 20, 21]. For these drugs, additional studies were requested to address different study designs (clinical vs. PBPK) or alternative dosing regimens (e.g., higher or repeated doses). For instance, rifampin co‐administration had no significant effect on the AUC of asciminib, a kinase inhibitor indicated for chronic myeloid leukemia, when a single 40 mg dose was administered. However, since this was the lowest clinical dose, a PMC was issued to evaluate rifampin effects on asciminib at a higher 200 mg single dose [21]. In another case, pacritinib (single 400 mg dose) showed reductions in C max and AUC of 51% and 88%, respectively, when a single 400 mg dose was co‐administered with rifampin. A PMR was issued to conduct PBPK analyses to assess the impact under multiple‐dose conditions. Only one drug, quizartinib (a kinase inhibitor indicated for acute myeloid leukemia), fell into the weak inducer category. It had previously been studied with efavirenz (a moderate CYP3A inducer), which reduced the AUC of quizartinib and its metabolite AC886 by 89% and 96%, respectively. Consequently, a PMC was issued to evaluate the effect of a weak CYP3A inducer.

Similar to the inhibitors group, a substantial proportion of these drugs (27/37, 73%) were also P‐gp substrates in vitro. However, no studies were specifically requested with dual CYP3A and P‐gp inducers. This likely reflects the fact that commonly used CYP3A index inducers also induce P‐gp, and the in vivo contribution of P‐gp is generally expected to be minimal for most of these drugs [22, 23].

Regarding evaluation approaches, nine studies (23%) were allowed evaluation using PBPK modeling, eight of which involved moderate CYP3A inducers. PBPK was typically requested in specific scenarios. For instance, ripretinib was required to be evaluated with rifampin via a clinical DDI study, whereas the effect of a moderate inducer was to be assessed using PBPK modeling [24]. It is commonly observed that the effect of a strong CYP3A inducer has been evaluated using clinical trials in the submission, while the effect of a moderate CYP3A inducer was requested to be evaluated using PBPK modeling, for example, abemaciclib, brigatinib, and neratinib [11, 25, 26].

As with inhibitors, initial labeling recommendations were frequently provided before PMR/PMC results became available, largely for clinical risk management. Among the 39 PMR/PMCs, nearly 70% (N = 26) were associated with labeling recommendations: most advised avoiding co‐administration (N = 21), followed by dose adjustments (N = 4) and one contraindication. Notably, pacritinib, which had a contraindication label with strong CYP3A inhibitors, was also contraindicated with strong CYP3A inducers [6].

In the most recent product labels, 25 of the 39 PMR/PMCs (64%) had updated study results, leading to revisions of initial recommendations. Among the 21 initial “avoid co‐administration” recommendations, 15 were updated: 4 added further detail (e.g., consider alternative therapies or specific dose adjustments), 6 incorporated study results without changing the recommendation, 2 narrowed the restriction to specific inducers, and 3 removed the restriction entirely. For example, the PMC study with carbamazepine showed a 35% decrease in tepotinib AUC and an 11% decrease in C max [27]. These changes are not considered clinically meaningful; consequently, the restriction on strong CYP3A inducers was removed [12]. Similarly, restrictions with moderate inducers were removed for pacritinib and palbociclib [28, 29]. Among the four initial dose‐adjustment recommendations, three were later refined: one added more detailed guidance (cannabidiol), one removed a pre‐initiation cessation requirement (doravirine), and one (pimavanserin) was strengthened to avoidance. For pimavanserin, an atypical antipsychotic indicated for hallucinations and delusions associated with Parkinson's disease psychosis, co‐administration with rifampin reduced C max and AUC by 71% and 91%, respectively, while PBPK simulations predicted reductions of approximately 60%–70% with efavirenz. These findings led to an updated recommendation to avoid concomitant use with strong or moderate CYP3A inducers [30]. In contrast, 14 PMR/PMCs initially had no specific labeling recommendations. Of these, seven were later updated to include label changes, all involving moderate CYP3A inducers. Most updates (N = 6) introduced new recommendations to avoid co‐administration, while one recommended dose adjustment (e.g., dose doubling during co‐administration).

Drugs as Inhibitors

Twelve drugs were requested to be evaluated as a CYP3A inhibitor, using either a sensitive CYP3A substrate (N = 9) or a CYP3A substrate of unspecified sensitivity (N = 3), comprising 12 studies (Table 2). Of these, 11 were issued as PMRs, and only the study for alpelisib was issued as a PMC. As expected, all of them inhibited CYP3A in vitro. Notably, four drugs also induced CYP3A in vitro while two drugs showed downregulation of CYP3A. Regarding the evaluation approach, only one request (revumenib) was considered suitable for assessment using PBPK, whereas the remaining 11 relied on clinical DDI studies. Sodium phenylbutyrate/taurursodiol had a specific request, recommended to evaluate using a cocktail DDI study design that included an appropriate probe substrate for CYP3A.

Although clinical impact on CYP3A was initially unknown, precautionary labeling recommendations for concomitant use with CYP3A substrates were provided for 8 of the 12 drugs (67%). Most recommendations advised avoiding co‐administration (N = 5), followed by dose adjustment (N = 2), and monitoring for toxicity (N = 1).

In the most recent product labels, 6 (50%) of the 12 requested studies had updated results, leading to revisions of the original labeling recommendations. Among the initial labeling recommendations to avoid co‐administration (N = 5), three were subsequently revised, all reflecting a reduction in restriction. Specifically, the co‐administration restriction was completely removed for erdafitinib and osimertinib, kinase inhibitors indicated for urothelial carcinoma and lung cancer, respectively. Results from the completed PMRs demonstrated no clinically meaningful effects on the exposure to midazolam or simvastatin [31, 32]. Notably, both agents exhibited mixed inhibition and induction of CYP3A4 in vitro. Erdafitinib weakly inhibited and induced CYP3A (value not provided), and osimertinib competitively inhibited (IC50 = 5.1 μM) and induced CYP3A (45% of positive control). For fexinidazole, in vitro studies indicated inhibition of CYP3A (IC50 = 35.4–45.1 μM), and static mechanistic modeling predicted that fexinidazole co‐administration could increase midazolam exposure 2.2‐ to 2.4‐fold. In addition, fexinidazole and its metabolites also caused concentration‐dependent decreases in CYP3A4 mRNA expression of up to 55.8% at concentrations up to 100 μM. Although initially characterized and evaluated as a CYP3A inhibitor, a clinical DDI study with midazolam demonstrated an overall net induction effect on CYP3A with decreases in midazolam C max and AUC of 39% and 57%, respectively [33]. These findings indicate that fexinidazole acts as a moderate inducer of CYP3A in vivo. Accordingly, the original labeling recommendation was revised. While concomitant use with CYP3A substrates is still not recommended due to the induction effect, the updated label advises that, if co‐administration cannot be avoided, patients should be monitored for lack of efficacy of sensitive CYP3A substrate drugs. Furthermore, if concomitant use of oral contraceptives is unavoidable, an alternative contraceptive that is not affected by enzyme inducers or additional nonhormonal contraception is recommended [34].

Among the drugs with original monitoring and dose adjustment recommendations (N = 3), only avacopan was updated in the most recent labels. Avacopan, an adjunctive treatment for severe active anti‐neutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibody‐associated vasculitis, exhibited both in vitro inhibition (direct and time‐dependent inhibition) and induction of CYP3A, and has a long half‐life (~4 days) with accumulation. An initial DDI study under fasted conditions indicated weak CYP3A inhibition, with a 1.81‐fold increase in midazolam AUC. However, due to a significant positive food effect (1.71‐fold increase in avacopan AUC under a high‐fat meal) and the recommendation for administration under fed conditions, a clinical DDI study with a sensitive CYP3A substrate such as simvastatin was requested and subsequently conducted to evaluate the effect of steady‐state avacopan with food on simvastatin PK. This study showed a 3.53‐fold increase in simvastatin AUC, confirming avacopan as a moderate CYP3A inhibitor under fed conditions. Accordingly, the label was updated to recommend limiting simvastatin dose and considering dose reduction for other CYP3A substrates when co‐administered [35].

Among the drugs with no initial labeling recommendations (four PMRs and one PMC), two drugs (both kinase inhibitors) were later updated in the most recent label. For alpelisib, results from the PMC study confirmed the absence of a clinically meaningful inhibitory effect on CYP3A. For rucaparib, co‐administration increased midazolam AUC 1.38‐fold without affecting C max, indicating a weak inhibitory effect on CYP3A [36, 37]. Accordingly, it has been updated to state that concomitant use of CYP3A substrates with rucaparib may increase systemic exposure to these substrates and, consequently, the risk of adverse reactions. If co‐administration is unavoidable and the substrate has a narrow therapeutic index, where small changes in exposure may result in serious adverse reactions, a dose reduction of the substrate is recommended in accordance with its approved prescribing information [37].

Drugs as Inducers

Thirteen drugs were issued PMRs or PMCs to evaluate their potential as CYP3A inducers, using either a sensitive CYP3A substrate (N = 8), a CYP3A substrate of unspecific sensitivity (N = 4), or oral contraceptives (N = 2), comprising 14 studies (Table 2). Unlike drugs as inhibitors, over half were issued as PMRs (N = 8) and six as PMCs. All drugs induced CYP3A in vitro, except for elafibranor, for which induction was not observed in vitro but a clinical study with simvastatin suggested a weak induction of CYP3A. Notably, four drugs also inhibited CYP3A in vitro, namely enasidenib, encorafenib, pacritinib, pexidartinib. All requests involved clinical DDI studies, including three dedicated cocktail studies (leniolisib, pacritinib, and pralsetinib).

For the 12 drugs evaluated with a CYP3A substrate (sensitive or sensitivity unspecified), the original labeling recommendations included avoidance of co‐administration (N = 4), caution with CYP3A substrates and/or hormonal contraceptives (N = 2), caution with CYP3A substrates and avoid hormonal contraceptives (N = 1), or had no recommendations (N = 5). In the most recent product labels, 5 (42%) of the 12 requested studies incorporated updated study results. These updates lead to downgraded recommendations (pacritinib), upgraded recommendations (enasidenib and pexidartinib), removal of initial recommendations (brigatinib), or inclusion of additional study details without changing recommendations (encorafenib).

Among drugs initially labeled to avoid co‐administration, only pacritinib was subsequently updated. Results from the completed PMR demonstrated that co‐administration with multiple dose pacritinib decreased midazolam C max and AUC by 60%. Consequently, the labeling recommendation was downgraded to recommend monitoring for reduced efficacy of sensitive CYP3A substrates and to advise modification of contraceptive strategies [28]. Brigatinib initially carried a precautionary labeling recommendation based on in vitro study results, indicating that co‐administration of CYP3A substrates with brigatinib, including hormonal contraceptives, could decrease substrate concentrations and lead to loss of efficacy. However, results from the completed PMC demonstrated no clinically meaningful effect on midazolam exposure. Consequently, the original labeling recommendation was removed [14]. Among the five drugs with no initial labeling recommendations, two oncology agents (enasidenib and pexidartinib) were later updated with new recommendations. Both exhibited in vitro CYP3A inhibition and induction. For enasidenib, the completed PMR showed that multiple‐dose administration decreased IV midazolam C max and AUC by 23% and 43%, respectively. A greater reduction in oral midazolam exposure is expected due to the induction of intestinal CYP3A‐mediated first‐pass metabolism. As a result, the labeling was updated to recommend avoiding co‐administration with certain CYP3A substrates where small exposure changes could reduce efficacy, unless otherwise specified, and to advise the use of effective nonhormonal contraception during treatment and for 2 months after the last dose [38]. Similarly, the completed PMC for pexidartinib demonstrated moderate CYP3A induction based on midazolam interaction data, leading to comparable updates in labeling recommendations [17].

Discussion

This detailed review of PMRs and PMCs for recent drug approvals demonstrated that CYP3A‐mediated DDIs remain a key focus in the post‐approval setting, particularly for oncology drugs. The predominance of oncology drugs highlights the unique challenges in this therapeutic area. It also likely reflects the accelerated development and approval pathways commonly used for oncology drugs, where comprehensive DDI characterization may be deferred to the postmarketing setting to enable earlier patient access. Furthermore, oncology therapies, particularly kinase inhibitors, frequently exhibit complex PK, including high dependence on CYP3A metabolism, narrow therapeutic index, and co‐administration with multiple concomitant medications. These factors collectively increase both the likelihood of clinically meaningful DDIs and the difficulty of fully characterizing them prior to approval. The high representation of oncology drugs in this analysis therefore reflects both scientific complexity and regulatory pragmatism.

A key finding of this analysis is the distinct regulatory approach to CYP3A inhibition versus induction, suggesting a risk‐based regulatory strategy. According to the FDA, PMRs and PMCs are treated differently under the law, with distinct terminologies used to distinguish studies that are required from those that a sponsor has committed to conduct that are not required. Following the FDA Amendments Act in 2007, postmarketing studies and clinical trials can be required to assess a known serious risk or signals of serious risk related to the use of the drug or to identify an unexpected serious risk when available data indicate the potential for a serious risk [5]. This distinction is evident in drugs approved over the past decade. When drugs were evaluated as CYP3A substrates, all postmarketing studies involving CYP3A inhibitors were requested as PMRs, reflecting a regulatory concern for potential toxicity due to increased drug exposure when co‐administered with inhibitors. In contrast, nearly 80% (30 of 39 studies) of studies involving CYP3A inducers were requested as PMCs. This pattern suggests that, although characterization of CYP3A induction remains an important regulatory objective, these studies were more frequently addressed through sponsor commitments rather than statutory requirements. A similar pattern was observed when drugs were evaluated as precipitants. For drugs as CYP3A inhibitors, 12 of 13 studies were designated as PMRs, whereas for inducers, only 7 of 12 were PMRs. These differences highlight, within the context of drug interactions, how the FDA prioritizes postmarketing study obligations based on the anticipated clinical impact: PMRs are generally reserved for scenarios with higher potential for serious harm or clinically significant interactions, whereas PMCs are often requested in situations where additional characterization is important, but the risk is less acute or can be managed in the postmarketing setting. However, when evaluating the completed studies and their resulting updates in the most recent labeling, no clear distinction or consistent trend between PMRs and PMCs was observed in this analysis.

Another notable observation is that PMRs and PMCs frequently address residual uncertainties despite the availability of pre‐approval clinical or PBPK data. For example, many drugs with PMRs involving moderate CYP3A inhibitors had already been evaluated with strong inhibitors, and conversely, several drugs with inducer‐related requests had prior data with rifampin. These follow‐up requests are often aimed at refining the magnitude of interaction under different conditions (e.g., moderate vs. strong perpetrators, single vs. multiple dosing, or alternative study designs). This pattern underscores that pre‐approval DDI packages, while generally robust, may not fully capture all clinically relevant scenarios, particularly when extrapolation across perpetrator strength or dosing regimens is required. It also reflects a cautious regulatory approach, favoring empirical confirmation over reliance on extrapolation alone.

Among drugs evaluated as CYP3A substrates, the most common requests were to study interactions with moderate CYP3A inducers or inhibitors, followed by strong inducers or inhibitors. It is relatively common that only worst‐case scenarios involving strong CYP3A inhibitors or inducers are assessed during NDA submission, with evaluation of additional scenarios deferred to the postmarketing setting. Notably, a substantial number of drugs were not evaluated with any CYP3A inhibitors at all. Given that most of these drugs are indicated for cancer treatment, there is often an urgent need to make therapies available to patients with life‐threatening conditions, where timely access may take precedence over completing the full spectrum of DDI studies prior to approval. In such cases, it is reasonable for certain interaction assessments to be addressed postmarketing through PMRs or PMCs to ensure both rapid access and continued characterization of safety and efficacy. It is worth noting that 28 of 37 drugs were also substrates of P‐gp in vitro. However, most regulatory requests focused on co‐administration with CYP3A inhibitors. Only one drug was specifically evaluated with a dual P‐gp and strong CYP3A inhibitor, and two drugs with dual P‐gp and moderate CYP3A inhibitors. These patterns suggest that P‐gp may not play a dominant role in the overall disposition of many of these drugs, and that inhibition of P‐gp is less likely to contribute clinically significantly to the changes in exposure. Futibatinib, a kinase inhibitor approved in 2022 for the treatment of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, is one example. Futibatinib was identified as a substrate of both CYP3A and P‐gp in vitro. Co‐administration with itraconazole increased futibatinib C max 1.51‐fold and AUC 1.41‐fold. A clinical DDI study with a P‐gp inhibitor was requested as a PMR to specifically evaluate the effect of P‐gp inhibition. In a study published in 2024, co‐administration with the P‐gp inhibitor quinidine had no clinically significant effect on futibatinib exposure (AUCR 1.17; CmaxR 1.08) [39]. In addition, itraconazole, a strong CYP3A inhibitor commonly used in these studies, is also a P‐gp inhibitor. Therefore, even when P‐gp inhibition is not explicitly specified in the study design, use of itraconazole can effectively assess a scenario involving concurrent inhibition of both CYP3A and P‐gp. Similarly, commonly used CYP3A inducers also affect P‐gp, making it difficult to disentangle their individual contributions. These findings highlight the growing complexity of DDI assessment and the need for integrated evaluation strategies.

As precipitants, 15 of 25 drugs were both inhibitors and inducers of CYP3A in vitro. Due to the limitations of basic and mechanistic static models, it is challenging to reliably predict the clinical relevance of such dual modulation from in vitro findings alone. Dedicated DDI studies to fully characterize the net effect were not conducted at the time of approval. This likely reflects the mechanistic complexity of concurrent inhibition and induction, as well as practical constraints in prioritizing comprehensive interaction studies during development, particularly when clinical significance is uncertain or the expected changes in exposure may be modest. Continued advancement and validation of PBPK and other dynamic modeling approaches may help better integrate competing mechanisms and improve the prediction of net clinical DDI risk. Such developments could support more informed decision‐making when dedicated clinical studies are warranted.

Regarding the evaluation approach for drugs as CYP3A substrates, approximately one‐third of the requests could be addressed using PBPK modeling and simulations with CYP3A inhibitors or inducers, suggesting substantial regulatory confidence in the use of these models to support DDI assessments. PBPK modeling offers a mechanistic framework to integrate in vitro data, drug properties, and clinical physiology, enabling prediction of complex interaction scenarios that may be difficult or impractical to study clinically. There was a clear preference for PBPK application in moderate interaction scenarios, suggesting that PBPK is increasingly accepted as a supportive or alternative approach when the expected interaction magnitude is modest. In contrast, for all drugs evaluated as precipitants (N = 25), only one case included the option to use a PBPK approach. This may reflect greater uncertainty in modeling dual inhibitory and inductive effects, as well as challenges in adequately characterizing precipitant potency and dynamic enzyme regulation. In such cases, model performance is highly dependent on the availability and quality of in vitro parameters and clinical validation data. Overall, these findings reinforce that while PBPK has become an integral component of DDI assessment, it is not yet a universal substitute for clinical studies, particularly in high‐risk or uncertain contexts.

To safely and effectively use these new medications, labeling recommendations were provided for approximately two‐thirds of the requested studies to manage potential DDI risk in the absence of evidence. Avoidance of co‐administration was the most common initial recommendation, although such restrictions may limit patient access to newly approved therapies. Interestingly, more than half of the requested studies resulted in subsequent labeling updates following completion of PMRs or PMCs and incorporation of new evidence into the most recent labeling. Notably, among drugs that initially carried an avoidance‐of‐coadministration recommendation, more than half were later revised to less restrictive guidance, including monitoring, dose adjustment, or complete removal of the restriction. This trend suggests that pre‐approval labeling often adopts a conservative stance in the face of uncertainty, which is subsequently refined as more definitive data become available. These evidence‐based refinements enhance pharmacotherapy by improving both drug safety and pharmacoequity, ensuring that patients, especially those with comorbidities and polypharmacy who are often at highest risk of exclusion from therapy, can more equitably access effective treatments with appropriately managed risk. Conversely, for drugs that initially had no specific labeling recommendations regarding DDIs, completion of PMRs or PMCs led to the introduction of new guidance, including dose adjustments, monitoring strategies, or, in some cases, newly identified avoidance for co‐administration. Collectively, these observations illustrate the dynamic nature of drug labeling where the postmarketing evidence plays a critical role in refining recommendations linked to DDIs and in optimizing drug safety in real‐world clinical use.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this analysis. The study is based on publicly available regulatory documents and curated database entries, which may not fully capture the rationale underlying specific PMR or PMC decisions or the complete context of regulatory discussions. The classification of drugs as CYP3A substrates, inhibitors, or inducers was based on the language of the requests and available study outcomes, which may not always reflect the full complexity of their mechanistic profiles, particularly for drugs exhibiting mixed inhibition and induction. Also, the latest product labels available at the time of the review (August 2025) were used to track updates related to the requested studies. Consequently, some product labels may have been revised after our review, and additional study results or labeling changes may not be reflected in this analysis. Furthermore, as described in the method section, some studies may have been completed without corresponding updates to the product labels. As a result, the latest product label may not fully capture all study outcomes, particularly when completed studies do not lead to changes in labeling recommendations. Furthermore, the categorization of label changes (e.g., “upgraded” or “downgraded”) involves a degree of interpretation and may not fully capture the clinical nuance or context of the recommendations. Despite these limitations, the current findings offer valuable insights into regulatory practices and highlight areas for future research and methodological refinement.

Regarding future perspectives, although substantial progress has been made in characterizing CYP3A‐mediated DDIs during drug development, this analysis demonstrates that important uncertainties may remain at the time of approval, leading to the issuance of PMRs and PMCs. Earlier completion of clinically relevant DDI studies, when feasible, could further optimize initial prescribing information and reduce the need for postmarketing evaluations. However, as described earlier, some DDI questions cannot be fully addressed before approval because of evolving clinical knowledge, limited patient availability, ethical considerations, or the need to evaluate the drug under clinically relevant dosing conditions that emerge later in development. Advances in model‐informed drug development are expected to further reduce such uncertainties [40]. PBPK has become an increasingly important component of regulatory DDI assessment and can support evaluation of untested clinical scenarios, alternative dosing regimens, and difficult‐to‐study populations. However, as illustrated by several examples in this analysis, the reliability of PBPK predictions remains dependent on adequate clinical data for model verification and accurate characterization of drug‐specific properties. Continued improvements in model qualification, mechanistic understanding, and regulatory acceptance are expected to further expand the role of PBPK in supporting labeling recommendations and reducing the need for additional postmarketing studies.

Finally, although CYP3A‐mediated interactions accounted for a major proportion of DDI‐related PMRs and PMCs, transporter‐mediated interactions also represent an important component of regulatory assessment. Previous analyses of transporter‐related postmarketing studies have demonstrated their contribution to optimizing clinical dosing recommendations and labeling [41, 42, 43]. These studies have reported that approximately 48%–85% of transporter‐based PMRs and PMCs are ultimately fulfilled and that incorporation of study findings into product labeling occurs, on average, approximately 2.5 years after study completion. These observations highlight that the impact of postmarketing DDI studies extends beyond study completion and may continue to influence product labeling for years after drug approval. Future comparative analyses of CYP3A‐ and transporter‐related PMRs and PMCs across therapeutic areas, including assessment of fulfillment status and timelines for labeling implementation, will provide further insights into evolving regulatory priorities and help optimize DDI assessment strategies to support safe and effective use of new medicines.
