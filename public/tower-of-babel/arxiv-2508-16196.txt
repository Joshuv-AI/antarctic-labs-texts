A motivic approach to rational p p -adic cohomologies Thanks: The authors are partially supported by the PRIN 20222B24AY “The arithmetic of motives and L L -functions”
Federico Binda, Alberto Vezzani

Abstract
. We survey over some recent applications of motivic homotopy theory in the definition and the study of p p -adic cohomology theories. In particular, we revisit the proof of the p p -adic weight-monodromy conjecture for smooth projective hypersurfaces in light of the motivic definition of nearby cycles and monodromy operators.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
From the special fiber to the generic fiber

One of the first motivations for the introduction of analytic geometry over non-archimedean fields is the attempt of defining a
p
-adic Weil cohomology theory (as opposed to the
\ell
-adic étale cohomology with
\ell\neq p
) for varieties over a finite field
k
of characterstic
p
, and the study of the associated zeta functions.

Following ideas of Dwork, Monsky and Washnitzer
[
47
]
introduced a way to attach to an affine, smooth variety over
k
a vector space
H^{i}_{MW}(X/K)
over
K=\Frac W(k)
. This can be thought as an “overconvergent” de Rham cohomology of a (dagger rigid analytic) generic fiber of a weakly complete smooth lift of
X
over
W(k)
.

We may interpret Monsky-Washnitzer’s plan was as a way to define a “functor”

(1.1)

\Sm/k\leadsto\FSm/\mathcal{O}_{K}\leadsto\RigSm/K\leadsto\RigSm^{\dagger}/K

with
K
being the
p
-adic non-archimedean field
W(k)[1/p]
, and where
\Sm
[resp.
\FSm
resp.
\RigSm
resp.
\RigSm^{\dagger}
] stands for the category of smooth varieties [resp. formal schemes, resp. rigid analytic variety, resp. dagger varieties] over the the base.

Nonetheless, of all the arrows sketched above, only the middle one is an actual functor. As a matter of fact, the existence of some formal lift is only granted locally, for affine varieties
X
. It is not even enough to work locally (that is, considering the Zariski topos): indeed, even if affine smooth lifts are unique up to isomorphism, their “weak completions” depend on a choice of coordinates for
X
. The same ambiguity exists when trying to lift maps between affine smooth varieties.

It was only thanks to Berthelot’s introduction of the overconvergent site
[
13
]
that Monsky–Washnitzer cohomology could be turned into a well-defined functorial cohomology theory, extendable to arbitrary varieties over
k
and with the usual properties of a Weil cohomology theory (a Künneth formula, finite dimensionality etc.).

We propose an alternative way to “straighten up” the squiggly arrows of (
1.1
) based on motivic homotopy theory. Indeed, by results of Elkik
[
28
]
and Arabia
[
3
]
, we know that potential lifts of maps are unique,
up to homotopy
. Such results suggest that one should consider a more flexible environment, in which not only can one work Zariski-locally (or even étale locally) but also
up to homotopy
. More or less by definition, this environment is the so-called category of (derived, étale, rational) motives that we will denote by
\DA
or
\RigDA
or
\RigDA^{\dagger}
in the algebraic resp. analytic resp. dagger setting.

The aim of
Section

3
, in which we collect results from
[
59
,
44
,
29
]
is to sketch how one can prove the following results.

Proposition 1.2
.

There is a functor

\xi\colon\Sm/k\to\DA(k)\to\RigDA^{\dagger}(K)

Whenever
X/k
admits a weak formal smooth lift
\mathfrak{X}^{\dagger}
over
W(k)
, then
\xi(X)\simeq\mathsf{M}(\mathfrak{X}^{\dagger}_{K})
.

We can use this functor to define Monsky–Washnitzer/rigid cohomology and prove even new features of them. In what follows, we let
\Fr/S
be the category of proper frames over
S
in the sense of Berthelot.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
S
be a scheme over
k
. There is a functor

\xi\colon\Sm/S\to\DA(S)\to\lim_{(T,\bar{T},\mathfrak{P})\in\Fr/S}\RigDA^{\dagger}(]{T}[^{\dagger}_{\mathfrak{P}})

which, composed to an overconvergent relative de Rham functor gives rise to a cohomology theory:

R\Gamma_{\rig}\colon\colon\Sm/S\to\DA(S)\to\lim_{(T,\bar{T},\mathfrak{P})\in\Fr/S}\QCoh(]{T}[^{\dagger}_{\mathfrak{P}})^{\op}.

computing (relative) rigid cohomology
R\Gamma_{\rig}(X/(T,\bar{T},\mathfrak{P}))
.

In particular, if
S=\Spec k
we get an absolute Monsky–Washnitzer realization:

R\Gamma_{\rig}\colon\Sm/k\to\mathcal{D}(K)^{\op}.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
S
be a scheme over
k
.

(1)

If
S=\Spec k
, then (absolute) Monsky–Washnitzer cohomology is well-defined, functorial, has étale and h-descent, and can be extended to arbitrary varieties by putting
R\Gamma_{\rig}(X/K)=R\Gamma_{\rig}(\mathsf{M}_{k}(X))
. It has the structure of a
\varphi
-module, and is finite dimensional whenever
X
is a smooth quasi-compact rigid variety, or the analytification of an algebraic variety.

(2)

Relative rigid cohomology has étale descent and h-descent. If
X/S
is smooth and proper, then
R^{i}\Gamma_{\rig}(X/(T,\bar{T},\mathfrak{P}))
is a vector bundle over
]T[^{\dagger}_{\mathfrak{P}}
and is equipped with a canonical structure of an overconvergent
F
-isocrystal.

We note that point (
1
) was essentially established by Berthelot and Chiarellotto–Tsuzuki, so that motivic methods help re-visit classical resuls. On the other hand, point (
2
) answers positively to an open conjecture by Berthelot which was previously known only in the (log-)liftable case, and for a special class of frames
[
13
,
57
,
55
]
.

1.2.
From generic fibers to special fibers

One of the classical ways to define an analytic variety over a non-archimedean field arises when one has a generically smooth family
X_{t}
of varieties over a curve
C
over a field
k
of characteristic
0
. When restricting around an infinitesimal “punctured neighborhood” of a point
0\in C
, one ends up with a formal model of a smooth rigid analytic variety over
k(\!(t)\!)
, having a (potentially singular) special fiber
X_{0}
over
k
. By standard resolution of singularities, one may even suppose that such special fiber is semi-stable (at least after an extension of scalars).

By classic work by Clemens
[
21
]
, Schmid
[
53
]
, Steenbrink
[
56
]
, Saito
[
52
]
et al. when
k=\mathbb{C}
, the limit Hodge cohomology of the elements of the family
\lim_{t\to 0}H(X_{t})
can be expressed in terms of a nearby cycle functor
\Psi X
, introduced either via passing to a universal covering and using the functor
\iota^{*}j_{*}
, or as an explicit bi-complex of Hodge modules depending on the geometry of
X_{0}
. It comes equipped with a monodromy operator.

In the arithmetic situation, by replacing the punctured complex disc and the non-archimedean field
\mathbb{C}(\!(t)\!)
with a local field
K
, this last procedure has been translated to the study of
\ell
-adic cohomology by Rapoport–Zink
[
51
]
, interpreting the Weil-Deligne cohomology of the generic fiber in terms of
\ell
-adic cohomology of a bi-complex
\Psi X
, equipped with a monodromy operator. Similarly, this procedure was adapted to the
p
-adic case, with the introduction of a so called Hyodo–Kato cohomology, by work of Hyodo–Kato
[
37
]
, Mokrane
[
46
]
, Nakkajima
[
49
]
, Große-Klönne
[
34
]
, Colmez–Niziol
[
22
]
and Ertl–Yamada
[
30
]
. Once again, Hyodo–Kato cohomology of the (rigid analytic) generic fiber can be described as rigid cohomology of a bi-complex
\Psi X
, and comes equipped with a monodromy operator.

In all three circumstances, the strategy is then to consider a “nearby cycle functor”

\Psi\colon\RigSm/K\leadsto\Sm/k+\text{monodromy}

and then define/study a “limit” cohomology for
X/K
via a cohomology theory for
\Psi X
. Once again, this functor is not really well-defined, as typically the nearby cycle functor is given by some bi-complex of objects in the category of
coefficients
(Hodge modules,
\ell
-adic sheaves or
\varphi
-modules) albeit defined using the geometry of the special fiber.

Also in this setting, working with a motivic category allows one to straighten up this arrow again into a well-defined functor, that we will discuss in
Section

4
based on results of
[
14
,
15
]
:

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
C
be an algebraically closed non-archimedean field, with valuation group equal to
\mathbb{Q}
and residue field
k
. There is an equivalence of categories

\RigDA(C)\simeq\DA(k)_{N}

where the category on the right hand side is the category of locally nilpotent “monodromy maps”
M\xrightarrow{N}M(-1)
with
M
in
\DA(k)
.

In particular, if
X
is a quasi-compact rigid variety or an algebraic variety over a local field
K
, then up to replacing
K
with a finite extension,
X
is canonically associated to a motive
\Psi X\in\DA(k)
equipped with a monodromy operator.

This provides a geometric (yet, motivic) universal way to perceive and define the various functors
\Psi
on each realization, and even prove new facts about the associated “limit cohomologies”.

Corollary 1.6
.

Hyodo–Kato cohomology for rigid analytic varieties over
C
can be defined as
R\Gamma_{\rig}(\Psi X)
without recourse to log-geometry. It is canonically equipped with a weight filtration and fits in a Clemens-Schmid chain complex.

Once again, while the first point of the previous corollary admits some variations which were already known, the second point is new, and answers positively to a conjecture by Flach–Morin
[
31
]
.

1.3.
On the
p
-adic weight-monodromy conjecture

As a matter of fact, this “motivic” intepretation of the Hyodo–Kato cohomology also sheds some new light on the proof of the
p
-adic weight monodromy conjecture for smooth projective hypersurfaces, obtained in
[
15
]
based on Scholze’s strategy
[
54
]
. The most precise statement goes as follows:

Theorem 1.7

(
[
15
]
)
.

Let
K
be a finite extension of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and
Y
be a smooth scheme-theoretic complete intersection inside a projective smooth toric variety over
K
. Then the p-adic weight-monodromy conjecture holds for
Y
.

Indeed, one of the technical ingredients of the main proof of
[
15
]
, is the compatibility of the motivic tilting equivalence
\RigDA(C)\simeq\RigDA(C^{\flat})
from
[
60
]
with the Hyodo–Kato realization functors based on log-geometry. We will show that this check can alternatively be done based on the identification of
1.5
and some detection principles developed in
[
14
]
.

We will revisit the strategy of the above theorem in
Section

4.4
, by making some generalizations of certain intermediate results in light of the equivalence of
1.5
. As such, this section contains some original results (see the proof of
4.32
).

2.
Properties of motivic categories

In this section, we list all the “formal” properties of motivic categories that we use for our explicit, cohomological applications later on. We will survey them based on
[
9
,
10
,
44
,
14
]
.

2.1.
Basic definitions

Motivic sheaves are defined by means of a universal property, that we now recall.

Definition 2.1
.

We will consider the following
\infty
-categories of motivic sheaves.

(1)

Let
S
be a scheme locally of finite Krull dimension. We write
\DA(S)=\DAet(S;\mathbb{Q})
for the
\infty
-category of hypercomplete étale motives over
S
, with rational coefficients as defined in
[
6
, Section 3]
by putting
\Lambda=\mathbb{Q}
. It is constructed from the full sub-
\infty
-category
\DA^{\eff}_{\et}(S;\mathbb{Q})
of
\mathrm{Shv}_{\et}(\Sm/S;\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{Q}))
spanned by those objects which are local with respect to the collection of maps
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbb{A}^{1}_{Y})\to\mathbb{Q}(Y)
, by formally inverting the tensor product operation with the Tate twist
\mathbb{1}(1)
.

(2)

Let
S
be a rigid analytic space locally of finite Krull dimension, in the sense of
[
32
, Definition II.2.2.18]
. We write
\RigDA(S)=\RigDA_{\et}(S;\mathbb{Q})
for the
\infty
-category of hypercomplete rigid analytic motives over
S
with respect to the étale topology, as defined in
[
10
, Definition 2.1.15]
. Its construction is analogous to (
1
), by replacing
\Sm/S
with
\RigSm/S
, and the affine line
\mathbb{A}^{1}
by the closed unit disk
\mathbb{B}^{1}
.

These are stable, compactly generated monoidal
\infty
-categories, underlying a six-functor formalism, see
[
4
,
5
,
10
]
.

Remark 2.2
.

In the setting of (
2
), we will mostly be interested in the case where
S=\Spa(K,\mathcal{O}_{K})=\Spf(\mathcal{O}_{K})^{\rig}
, for
K
a complete non-archimedean field with a perfect residue field. Here, for a quasi-compact and quasi-separated
formal scheme
\mathfrak{X}
, we write
\mathfrak{X}^{\rig}
or
\mathfrak{X}_{\eta}
for the Raynaud generic fiber. We will simply write
\RigDA(K)
for
\RigDA(\Spf({\mathcal{O}_{K})^{\rig}})
. Note that in this case by
[
10
, Lemma 2.4.18]
the non-hypercomplete version of the construction of
\RigDA
agrees with the one considered above, and the reader can simply ignore the difference between the two.

2.2.
Weil cohomology theories in algebraic and analytic geometry

We follow the exposition of Ayoub
[
9
, §1]
, and quickly recall the notion of mixed Weil cohomology theory, as introduced by Cisinski and Déglise
[
19
, §2]
, specialized to our setting of
\mathbb{Q}
-linear stable
\infty
-categories.

We begin by recalling the following basic definition.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
k
be a field (resp. let
K
be a complete non-archimedean local field). A monoidal
realization functor
or simply a realization is a morphism

R\Gamma^{*}\colon\DA(k)\to\mathscr{D}^{\otimes}

(resp.,
R\Gamma^{*}\colon\RigDA(K)\to\mathscr{D}^{\otimes}
) in
{\rm{CAlg}(\rm{Pr}^{L})}
, where
\mathscr{D}^{\otimes}
is presentable monoidal stable
\infty
-category. We will mostly be interested in the case where
\mathscr{D}^{\otimes}\in\Mod_{\Mod_{\mathbb{Q}}}({\rm{Pr}^{L}})
is
\mathbb{Q}
-linear. A “classical” example is given by
\mathscr{D}^{\otimes}=\Mod_{\Lambda}
, for

\Lambda\in\CAlg(\Mod_{\mathbb{Q}})^{\rm cn}
(i.e., a commutative connective dg
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra).

Remark 2.4
.

To give a realization functor for
\DA(k)
is thus equivalent to give a symmetric monoidal functor
\Sm/k\to\mathscr{D}^{\otimes}
satisfying étale descent,
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-invariance and Tate stability. Similarly, to give a realization functor for
\RigDA(K)
is equivalent to give a symmetric monoidal functor
\RigSm/K\to\mathscr{D}^{\otimes}
satisfying rig-étale descent,
\mathbb{B}^{1}
-invariance and Tate stability.

Realizations that are
\Mod_{\Lambda}
-valued are classically introduced in terms of cohomology theories for algebraic (resp. analytic) varieties in the following sense.

Definition 2.5

(
[
9
, Definition 1.1 and 2.1]
)
.

Let
k
be a field (resp. let
K
be a complete non-archimedean local field), and let
\Lambda\in\CAlg(\Mod_{\mathbb{Q}})^{\rm cn}
.

A
mixed Weil cohomology theory
for algebraic
k
-varieties (resp. for rigid analytic
K
-varieties) is a presheaf
\Gamma_{W}
of commutative
\Lambda
-algebras on
\Sm/k
(resp. on
\RigSm/K
) satisfying the following properties:

(1)

For any
X\in\Sm/k
(resp.
X\in\RigSm/K
) the natural morphism
\Gamma_{W}(X)\to\Gamma_{W}(X\times\mathbb{A}^{1})
(resp.
\Gamma_{W}(X)\to\Gamma_{W}(X\times\mathbb{B}^{1})
) is an equivalence;

(2)

The
\Gamma_{W}(\pt)
-module
\cof(\Gamma_{W}(\pt)\to\Gamma_{W}(\mathbb{P}^{1}))
is invertible;

(3)

For every
X,Y\in\Sm/k
(resp. for every
X,Y\in\RigSm/K^{\qcqs}
) there is an equivalence

\Gamma_{W}(X)\otimes_{\Gamma_{W}(\pt)}\Gamma_{W}(Y)\simeq\Gamma_{W}(X\times Y);

(4)

\Gamma_{W}(-)
has étale descent.

Note that
\Gamma_{W}
is automatically an object of
\DA^{\rm eff}(k;\Lambda)
(resp. of
\RigDAeff(K;\Lambda)
).

Remark 2.6
.

Any mixed Weil cohomology theory for algebraic or for analytic varieties gives rise naturally to a commutative algebra object
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{W}
in
\DA(k;\Lambda)
(resp. in
\RigDA(K;\Lambda)
), satisfying some extra assumptions. See
[
9
, Proposition 1.9, 2.5]
and
[
19
, 2.1.5]
. In fact, the categories of
Weil spectra
and that of
\Lambda
-valued Weil cohomology theories
[
9
, Definition 1.2]
are equivalent. See
[
9
, Theorems 1.16, 2.12]

Remark 2.7
.

Given any monoidal realization functor
R\Gamma^{*}
as in
2.3
, write
R\Gamma_{*}
for its right adjoint. We denote
R\Gamma_{*}(\mathbb{1})
by
E_{\Gamma}
. Since
R\Gamma^{*}
is monoidal,
R\Gamma_{*}
is lax monoidal. In particular, it carries commutative algebra objects to commutative algebra objects, so that
E_{\Gamma}\in\CAlg(\DA(k))
(resp. in
\CAlg(\RigDA(K))
).

The assignment
R\Gamma^{*}\mapsto E_{\Gamma}
determines in fact an equivalence of categories between the category of algebraic (resp. analytic)
\Mod_{\Lambda}
-valued realization functors and the category of mixed Weil spectra in
\DA(k)
(resp. in
\RigDA(K)
). See
[
9
, Theorems 1.21, 2.16]
.

We list a series of examples of classical realization functors. In what follows, we keep the convention that
k
is a field and
K
is a complete non-archimedean local field.

Example 2.8

(
\ell
-adic realizations)
.

Let
\ell
be a prime invertible in
k
(resp. in
K
). The classical
\ell
-adic étale cohomology is an example of mixed Weil cohomology theory: for every
X\in\Sm/k
(resp.
X\in\RigSm/K
), write
R_{\ell}(X)\in\mathcal{D}_{\proet}(k,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
for the object computing the
\ell
-adic étale homology of
X
, see e.g.
[
6
, Théorème 6.9]
in the algebraic context. The assignment
X\mapsto R_{\ell}(X)
satisfies all the assumptions of
2.5
and gives rise to a monoidal functor
\DA(k)\to\mathcal{D}_{\proet}(k,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
(resp.
\RigDA(K)\to\mathcal{D}_{\proet}(k,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
).

One can describe more accurately the
\ell
-adic étale realization (both in the algebraic and in the analytic setting), and over a general base, by means of the so-called rigidity theorem: in this language, the étale realization arises as the left adjoint to the canonical functor induced by the inclusion of the small étale site into the big étale site. See
[
10
, §2.10]
and
[
11
, Theorem 3.2]
in the analytic setting and the already mentioned
[
6
]
in the algebraic setting for the compatibility of the
\ell
-adic realization with the six-functor formalism.

Example 2.9

(Betti and Hodge realization)
.

Let
k=\mathbb{C}
. For a finite type
\mathbb{C}
-scheme
X
, let
X^{\rm an}
be the set of
\mathbb{C}
-points
X(\mathbb{C})
, equipped with the classical complex analytic topology. The functor
X\mapsto X^{\rm an}
can be promoted to a symmetric monoidal functor
\mathsf{Bti}\colon\DA(\mathbb{C})\to\DA^{\rm an}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\Mod_{\mathbb{Q}}
, where
\DA^{\rm an}(\mathbb{C})
is the (stabilization of the) category of
\mathbb{D}^{1}_{\mathbb{C}}
-local sheaves on
\mathbb{C}
-smooth analytic spaces. By
[
7
, §2]
it is equivalent to the classical derived
\infty
-category of
\mathbb{Q}
-modules. For any smooth
\mathbb{C}
-scheme
X
, the homology groups
H_{*}(\mathsf{Bti}(\mathsf{M}(X)))
compute the singular homology of
X(\mathbb{C})
.

The Betti realization can be further enriched to obtain a mixed Hodge realization. Let
\MHS^{p}_{\mathbb{Q}}
be the Abelian category of polarisable mixed Hodge structures and let
\mathcal{D}^{b}(\MHS^{p}_{\mathbb{Q}})
be its bounded derived stable
\infty
-category. The presheaf defined by sending
X\in\Sm/\mathbb{C}
to the object
R\Gamma_{\rm Hodge}^{*}(X)\in\mathcal{D}^{b}(\MHS^{p}_{\mathbb{Q}})
computing the singular cohomology of
X
equipped with its natural mixed Hodge structure is a mixed Weil cohomology theory. As such, it can be promoted to a symmetric monoidal (cohomological) realization functor

\DA(\mathbb{C})\to\mathcal{D}^{b}(\MHS^{p}_{\mathbb{Q}})^{\rm op}.

Restricting to the subcategory of compact objects
\DA(\mathbb{C})_{\omega}
and pre-composing with the duality functor
M\mapsto M^{\vee}
gives the homological Hodge realization functor
\DA(\mathbb{C})_{\omega}\to\mathcal{D}^{b}(\MHS^{p}_{\mathbb{Q}})
. Since
\DA(\mathbb{C})\simeq\Ind(\DA(\mathbb{C})_{\omega})
, this can be further extended to a symmetric monoidal functor
\DA(\mathbb{C})\to\Ind\mathcal{D}^{b}(\MHS^{p}_{\mathbb{Q}})
taking values in the Ind-derived
\infty
-category. As for the
\ell
-adic realization, it turns out that also the Hodge realization can be defined in the relative setting, in a way that is compatible with the six-functor formalism. See
[
58
, Theorem 4.4]
.

Example 2.10

(de Rham realization)
.

Let
k
be a field of characteristic zero.
The presheaf
X\mapsto R\Gamma(X,\Omega_{X/k})
is another example of mixed Weil cohomology, giving rise to a monoidal (cohomological) realization functor
\dR\colon\DA(k)\to\mathcal{D}(k)^{\rm op}
. We denote by
R\Gamma_{{\rm dR},k}
the covariant realization functor obtain by pre-composing the restriction of
\dR
to the subcategory of compact objects with the duality functor, as in
2.9
, further extended to a unique monoidal, compact-preserving, colimit-preserving functor
R\Gamma_{{\rm dR},k}\colon\DA(k)\to\mathcal{D}(k)
.

2.4.
Frobenius data

We start by recording the following fact about algebraic (resp. analytic) motives. Recall that a morphism
e\colon S^{\prime}\to S
of schemes is a universal homeomorphism if for any
Y\to S
, the base change
Y\times_{S}S^{\prime}\to Y
is a homeomorphism. It is well-known that this is equivalent to require that
e
is integral, radicial and surjective. For example, the canonical closed immersion
X_{\rm red}\to X
is a universal homeomorphism, as it is any closed embedding
Z\to X
defined by a nilpotent sheaf of ideals. In positive characteristic, both the absolute and the relative Frobenius are examples of universal homeomorphisms.

This notion has an exact analogue in the analytic world: we say that a morphism of rigid analytic spaces
S^{\prime}\to S
is a universal homomorphism if it is quasi-compact, quasi-separated, surjective and radicial. See
[
10
, 2.9.1]
.

Universal homeomorphisms of schemes induce equivalences of étale sites
[
1
, Exposé VIII, Théorème 1.1]
. This fact has the following motivic generalization, sometimes known as the “semi-separatedness property” of the stable motivic categories.

Theorem 2.13

(
[
6
, Theorem 1.2]
, resp.
[
10
, Corollary 2.9.11]
)
.

Let

be a universal homeomorphism of schemes (resp. of rigid analytic spaces with

locally of finite Krull dimension). Then, the base change functor

e^{*}\colon\DA(X)\to\DA(X^{\prime})\qquad\left(\text{resp. }e^{*}:\RigDA(X)\to\RigDA(X^{\prime})\right)

is an equivalence. In particular,
\DA(X)\simeq\DA(X^{\Perf})
.

We obtain immediately an equivalence between the associated cohomological motives of
X,X^{\prime}
as follows.

Corollary 2.14
.

Let
X\to X^{\prime}
be a universal homeomorphism of schemes over
S
. Then the map
\mathsf{M}_{S}^{coh}(X^{\prime})\to\mathsf{M}_{S}^{coh}(X)
is invertible.

.

2.5.
The spreading out

The functor

has the property of converting limits of certain

Theorem 2.17

(
[

)
.

Let

be a cofiltered inverse system of

in the sense of Huber (see

Then, the obvious functor

\underset{i}{\varinjlim}\,\RigDA(X_{i})\to\RigDA(X_{\infty}),

where the colimit is taken in

Remark 2.19
.

We like to call this a “spreading out” theorem as it ultimately says that any compact motive over

has a model over some

. For example, by putting

to be
\Spa L
with

in

\RigDA(\mathbb{C}_{p})\simeq\varinjlim_{[L:K]<\infty}\RigDA(L).

We remark that the analogous statement would not hold if we simply considered the big étale sites over
\mathbb{C}_{p}
and over the
L

\Spa\mathbb{C}_{p}\to\mathbb{B}^{1}_{\mathbb{C}_{p}}
hitting an element of

which is trascendental over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
doesn’t admit any model over any

Also, by putting

to be the affinoid open neighborhoods of a
K

in some rigid variety
S
over
K
, we deduce

\RigDA(x)\simeq\varinjlim_{x\in U\subset S}\RigDA(U).

In this case, the explicit quasi-inverse of the canonical pull-back functor

is given by
\Pi^{*}
i.e. the pull-back along the structural morphism
\Pi\colon U\to\Spa K
. Informally, this says that any compact motive

over
S
becomes constant around each rational point, i.e. for any sufficiently small open neighborhood

of

, one has

.

We note that the “spreading out” formula in this last case (a rational point

as the limit of its open neighborhoods) follows immediately from the localization triangle (Section
(v)
(v)) and plays an important role in proving finiteness results of de Rham cohomologies (see
[
44
, Theorem 4.46]
, and even
[
63
]
for an algebraic analogue).

The following is basically a restatement of the spreading out theorem, in terms on cohomological motives.

.

Proof.

We let

. By

, the functors

\displaystyle\simeq\varinjlim\map(f_{i0}^{*}\pi_{0}^{*}C,f_{i0}^{*}M)

By putting

and

Corollary 2.21
.

Let

be a qcqs weak limit of qcqs adic spaces over

The canonical map
\varinjlim\mathsf{M}^{\rm coh}(X_{i})\to\mathsf{M}^{\rm coh}(X_{\infty})
is an equivalence in

The previous result is particularly useful when one knows that the maps in the direct system induce equivalences in the homotopy category, e.g. if they are Frobenius lifts (see

). If they are finite, we also deduce a special relation between the motive of the limit and each motive of the elements in the tower.

Corollary 2.22
.

Let

be a projective system of qcqs smooth rigid varieties over

are finite surjective, and let

be a qcqs adic space such that

is a direct summand of the motive
\mathsf{M}^{\rm coh}(X_{\infty})

Proof.

We may and do assume that all the spaces involved are connected. We recall that, based on the equivalence between motives with and without transfers

We then deduce that the motive
\mathsf{M}(X_{0})
is a direct summand of each
\mathsf{M}(X_{i})

\mathsf{M}^{\rm coh}(X_{0})
is a direct summand of each

∎

Let

The point

\mathsf{M}_{K}(X_{U})\simeq\mathsf{M}_{K}(X_{s}\times U)=\mathsf{M}_{K}(X_{s})

\mathsf{M}^{\rm coh}(X_{U})=\mathsf{M}^{\rm coh}(X_{s})

∎

The aim of this section is to prove

3.1.
Formal motives vs algebraic motives

We first show how Elkik and Arabia’s solutions to the (local) “lifting problems” in the Monsky–Washnitzer approach to rigid cohomology can be condensed into suitable motivic equivalences.

The first result is due to Ayoub (see e.g.,
[

Theorem 3.1

(
[
10
, Theorem 3.1.10]
)

Let
\mathfrak{S}
be a formal scheme. The functors

(3.2)

\sigma^{*}:\FDA(\mathfrak{S})\rightleftarrows\DA(\mathfrak{S}_{\sigma}):\sigma_{*}.

induced by taking special fibers, are equivalences of
\infty

Indeed, for a formal scheme
\mathfrak{S}

\mathfrak{S}_{\sigma}
in

is empty and so by the localization triangle we obtain that the natural transformation

There is also a log-generalization of the equivalence provided by

(resp.

(resp.
\mathrm{Shv}_{s\acute{e}t}(\Sm/\mathfrak{S};\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{Q}))

S
(resp. log formal schemes over
\mathfrak{S}
). This is the exact generalization to the log setting of

. Formally inverting the Tate twist

we obtain the non-effective version

(resp.

). See
[

for an extended discussion,
[

]

for non-necessarily fine log structures on formal schemes.

Theorem 3.3

(
[

, Theorem 2.22]
)

Let

is a quasi-coherent integral log formal scheme. Assume that at least one of the following holds:

(1)

is fine;

(2)

The underlying scheme

has finite topological Krull dimension.

Then the functors

(3.4)

\sigma^{*}:\mathrm{logFDA}(\mathfrak{S})\rightleftarrows\mathrm{logDA}(\mathfrak{S}_{\sigma}):\sigma_{*}.

induced by taking special fibers, are equivalences of
\infty

3.2.
Dagger motives vs analytic motives

The possibility to “lift” varieties over
\mathfrak{S}_{\sigma}
to (formal) motives over
\mathfrak{S}
and hence to (analytic) motives over
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}
is not yet enough to produce a motivic

has non-trivial higher cohomology.

This obstacle is classically overcome as follows. Any smooth rigid analytic variety

4.
Motivic nearby cycles and applications

4.1.
Analytic motives vs algebraic motives with monodromy

In the classical literature (see
[
38
]
) monodromy operators are certain nilpotent endomorphisms between cohomology groups
H^{*}(X)
(e.g. de Rham cohomology,
\ell
-adic cohomology), where
X
arises as a fiber of a family over a disk, or
X
is a variety defined over a local field. They come from the action of a generator of a fundamental group
\pi^{1}(\Delta^{*})\simeq\mathbb{Z}
or a topological generator of tame inertia groups (isomorphic to
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
). If one equips the groups

with suitable extra structures (in the de Rham case: a mixed Hodge module structure, in the
\ell
-adic case: the structure of a representation over
\Gal(k)
) then it makes sense to define (positive and negative) twists of them, and it turns out that the monodromy maps are actually morphisms of the form

N\colon H^{*}(X)\to H^{*}(X)(-1)

hence their nilpotence (for weight reasons).

We can then define a category of monodromy operators as follows:

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be stable
\infty
-category, and

be an exact functor. We let
(-n)
be the iterated functor

.

(1)

We let
\mathscr{C}^{(-1)}_{lax}
to be the
\infty
-category whose objects are pairs
(X,f)
with
X\in\mathscr{C}
and
f\colon X\to X(-1)
. Mapping complexes are defined in the obvsious way, i.e.

\map((X,f),(Y,g)))=\fib(\map(X,Y)\xrightarrow{f^{*}(-1)-g_{*}}\map(X,Y(-1)).

(2)

We define
\mathscr{C}_{N}
as the
full sub-
\infty
-category of
\mathscr{C}^{(-1)}_{lax}
spanned by objects
(X,f)
with ind-nilpotent morphism
f
, that is, such that

\colim(X\to X(-1)\to X(-2)\to\cdots)=0.

The following facts are proved in
[
14
, Section 2]
.

Remark 4.2

(See
[
14
, Remark 2.20]
)

If

is compactly generated, then also

is. Its compact objects are given by pairs

with

Remark 4.3

(See

Remark 4.4

We note that there is a natural adjunction

which, in good situations, satisfies the hypotheses of a Barr–Beck–Lurie functor, thus identifiying the category

with a category of modules

Let

be a complete non-archimedean field with a perfect residue field

Theorem 4.5

(
[

Let

We can highlight the main points of the proof as follows.

4.4.
The weight-monodromy conjecture

One of the first applications of the theory of perfectoid spaces and the almost purity theorem is Scholze’s proof of the weight-monodromy conjecture for the

-adic cohomology of smooth proper varieties obtained as the intersection of smooth hypersurfaces in projective smooth toric varieties

[

]
(see
[

K

of characteristic

-adic cohomology groups

have the property that the action of Frobenius on the

is pure of weight

[

-adic cohomology groups are replaced by the Hyodo-Kato cohomology groups

-modules over

.

In
[

Definition 4.21

A (smooth) toric variety over a field

Remark 4.22
.

Every abstract normal separated toric variety can be realized as the toric variety associated to a fan
\Sigma

be a free abelian group of finite rank, and write

is a finite collection of strongly convex rational polyhedral cone

subject to the condition of being closed under taking faces (i.e., for each

Writing

, we can associate to each

Remark 4.23
.

A basic example of a toric variety is given by the projective space

: this is obtained by the usual gluing of the affine schemes

in all statements below.

Remark 4.24

The multiplication by

The motivic nearby cycle can also be used to give a uniform treatment (in the

Let us begin by casting the problem in the complex analytic case.
Let

is equipped with Steenbrink’s limit Hodge structure

from
[

. The kernel of the monodromy operator captures some cohomological information of the singular space

. More precisely, one has the following result:

Theorem 4.35
