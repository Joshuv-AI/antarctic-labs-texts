RETRACTED ARTICLE: Increased WNT10B/FOXO6 signaling promotes cell fate transition in renal tubular cells to aggravate renal inflammation and fibrosis
Authors: Jinhua Miao, Jiemei Li, Ping Meng, Xiaolong Li, Jiewu Huang, Ye Liang, Xian Ling, Xiaoxu Wang, Weiwei Shen, Shan Zhou, Hongquan Peng, Yunfang Zhang, Youhua Liu, Fan Fan Hou, Lili Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbnormal cell fate transition determines cell instability, which can contribute to disease development. In chronic kidney disease, many renal tubular cells exhibit senescent phenotypes. The fundamental mechanisms of this fate transition remain undetermined. Here we discover that WNT10B, a ligand of the Wnt family, accelerates CKD progression through tubular senescence and proinflammatory microenvironments. Mechanistically, WNT10B mediates metabolic reprogramming from fatty acid oxidation to glycolysis, thus promoting cell senescence and cytokine secretion. Genetic ablation of Wnt10b in male CKD mouse model effectively inhibits cell senescence, inflammation, and fibrogenesis by maintaining metabolic homeostasis. Conversely, Wnt10b transgene in tubular cells aggravates metabolic imbalance, and promotes tubular senescence and fibrogenesis. Forkhead box O6 (FOXO6), a DNA-binding transcription factor, mediates the signal transduction cascade of WNT10B. ChIP-, bulk RNA-, and single-nucleus RNA- sequencing and biological assays verify that FOXO6 transcriptionally modulates PPARA and PKM to control metabolic reprogramming and regulate senescence fate transition in tubular cells. Therefore, the study reveals that WNT10B/FOXO6 signaling controls tubular cell senescence fate, and sheds a light on potential intervention targets to protect against CKD.Subject terms: Kidney diseases, Cell signalling

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD), the fifth chronic disease, greatly affects 10% of the global population, leading to millions of deaths annually1,2. Therapeutic approaches for CKD remain limited. Renal fibrosis, referring to the pathological hallmark of various CKD, is closely involved with multiple cell types, especially renal tubular cells and macrophages2–4. Dysregulated communication between tubular cells and macrophages drives a proinflammatory environment, thereby contributing to irreversible kidney fibrosis5,6.

Tubular epithelial cells (TECs), the predominant cells of kidney, are highly differentiated epithelial cells. TECs are vulnerable to the insults of ischemia, toxins, hypoxia, as well as metabolic disorders7,8. In CKD, TECs frequently exhibit senescence phenotype, resulting in premature ageing state of the kidney9,10. Besides cell cycle arrest, senescent TECs also secrete large amounts of cytokines, attracting macrophages and other immune cells, to create a proinflammatory microenvironment, thereby worsening kidney scarring11–13.

Mitochondrial injury contributes to cell fate conversion, particularly senescence fate transition14,15. Our group and others have reported mitochondrial dysfunction promotes TECs senescence16,17. As essential organelles, mitochondria take over adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production and aerobic metabolism of substances, such as fatty acids and glucose18. Along with disease severity, mitochondria are progressively damaged in CKD19. Under the superimposed hypoxic environment in CKD, TECs reprogram their metabolism, switching from the aerobic oxidation of fatty acids to anaerobic glycolysis. PPARα (Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha) and PKM2 (Pyruvate kinase isozyme type M2), the key regulators of fatty acids oxidation (FAO) and glycolysis, respectively, strongly participate in metabolic reprogramming20,21. However, their modulators need to be fully clarified.

Wnt/β-catenin, a conserved signaling, not only affects kidney development, but also involves in many diseases22–24. Upon Wnt, β-catenin moves into nucleus and initiates target genes expression through binding transcription factors. We have identified various targets of β-catenin, such as the renin-angiotensin system (RAS), B7-1, osteopontin (OPN), and cannabinoid receptor 2 (CB2), etc.25–27. Of note, Wnt/β-catenin crucially participates in TECs senescence, both accelerated10 and natural ageing28, which associates with the aforementioned genes in addition to mitochondrial dysfunction.

Among 19 Wnt(s) in mammals, we previously found WNT9A regulates TECs senescence10. Whereas, the key Wnt masterly controlling TEC senescence in CKD needs full clarification. Furthermore, β-catenin typically interacts with CREB-binding protein (CBP) co-activator and TCF/LEF transcription factors to promote the transcription of downstream genes. Whereas, ICG-001, a small molecule disrupting β-catenin/CBP complex, selectively inhibiting TCF/LEF transcriptional activity, only partially restored mitochondrial metabolism29, suggesting other transcription factors involving in β-catenin signaling. Of interest, the forkhead box O family (FoxOs), the transcription factors intimately correlated with mitochondria and ageing30,31, also link to β-catenin cascade32. However, the relationship between FoxOs and β-catenin in mitochondrial metabolism in CKD remains elusive.

Herein, we observed that WNT10B is a crucial ageing factor contributing to CKD. WNT10B, via β-catenin/FOXO6 pathway, activates metabolic reprogramming to facilitate senescence fate transition in TECs. A pro-inflammatory microenvironment is subsequently formed and fibrogenesis is triggered. Our study provides a key mechanism for defining accelerated ageing in CKD and identifies WNT10B as a prominent therapeutic target.

Results

WNT10B strongly links up with tubular cell senescence and renal inflammation

We first analysed the transcriptome data (GSE175759) of microdissected human kidney tubulointerstitial tissues (n = 90) (Supplementary Fig. S1A). In 68 CKD samples, the senescence markers CDKN1A (p21CIP1) or CDKN2A (p16INK4A), etc., were upregulated, along with increased FN1 (fibronectin), COL1A1 (collagen, type I, alpha 1), and other fibrosis-related genes. Multiple WNT genes, including WNT16, WNT11, WNT5B, WNT5A, WNT2B, WNT10A, WNT4 and WNT10B were also significantly induced in CKD (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1B, C).

Intersection analysis was then conducted on seven genesets: the genes related to senescence (CDKN1A and CDKN2A-positive), fibrosis (FN1 and ACTA2-positive), Wnt signaling (KEGG and GO databases), and upregulated genes in CKD group. Interestingly, WNT10B, WNT4, SFRP2, and AXIN2, were presented in the core area (Fig. 1B). Considering we previously found WNT10B is upregulated in natural ageing kidney28, we assumed WNT10B is involved in TECs senescence in CKD. Consistent with our hypothesis, upregulated WNT10B, in CKD patients, was positively correlated with CDKN2A and FN1, and inversely correlated with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (Fig. 1C–E), which was also testified in other 2 datasets (Supplementary Fig. S1D).

In human biopsies, WNT10B and p16INK4A were colocalized, and positively correlated, in renal tubules in different types of CKD (Fig. 1F,~ G), coinciding with fibrotic lesions (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. S1E). Of note, WNT10B increased in various segments of tubules in CKD patient (Fig. 1H). Besides kidney tissues, we also found WNT10B was strongly increased in urine samples from CKD patients, accompanying with fibronectin secretion in the urine (Fig. 1I).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was then performed to define WNT10B-related signals. Besides senescence, WNT10B contributes to inflammation pathways of M-CSF signaling and macrophage activation, but downregulates mitochondria-related pathways, such as oxidative phosphorylation and FAO (Fig. 1J). The correlation analysis showed WNT10B was positively correlated with kidney injury, extracellular matrix (ECM), inflammation, macrophage differentiation, and other scores (Supplementary Fig. S1F). In line with these findings, WNT10B was positively correlated with several classic inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 1K). Moreover, WNT10B-positive tubules (arrow) were surrounded by CD68+ macrophages (arrowhead) (Fig. 1L).

Several CKD mouse models, ischemia-reperfusion (IRI) mice, unilateral ureter obstruction (UUO) and adriamycin nephropathy (ADR) mice, were established. As shown, WNT10B was progressively increased in renal tubules in CKD mice, accompanied by cell senescence (Supplementary Fig. S1G–M). Overall, these results suggest WNT10B critically participates in tubular senescence, renal inflammation and fibrogenesis.

Tubular-specific ablation of Wnt10b alleviates tubular senescence, inflammation, and renal fibrosis

Wnt10bcko mice were established by hybridizing Ksp-Cre mice to Wnt10bfl/fl mice (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S2A). Both Wnt10bfl/fl and Wnt10bcko mice presented normal kidney structure, suggesting normal kidney development (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Unilateral IRI surgery was operated (Fig. 2A). Renal WNT10B and active β-catenin strongly decreased in Wnt10bcko mice (Fig. 2B, C and Supplementary Fig. S2C, D), as well as diminished serum creatinine (Scr) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) (Fig. 2D).

GSEA unveiled cellular senescence was inhibited in Wnt10bcko mice (Supplementary Fig. S2E). Consistently, senescence markers p16INK4A, 19ARF, together with γH2AX, were downregulated in Wnt10bcko mice, plus senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity (Fig. 2E, F).

We then performed gene ontology (GO) enrichment in groups of mice. As shown, immune response, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and several macrophage activation pathways were increased in Wnt10bfl/fl/UIRI mice, but were inhibited in Wnt10bcko/UIRI mice (Fig. 2G). Immune cell infiltration analysis also showed Wnt10bcko mice resisted macrophage activation and accumulation in UIRI model (Supplementary Fig. S2F). Concomitantly, the transcription of inflammatory cytokines, such as Csf2, Tnf, Ifng, and Ccl2, was blocked in Wnt10bcko mice (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, F4/80 staining, Sirius red, and fibronectin staining revealed that Wnt10bcko/UIRI mice blocked macrophage accumulation and exhibited less fibrogenesis (Fig. 2I–K). Western blotting showed renal fibronectin and α-SMA were decreased in Wnt10bcko mice (Fig. 2L). These results suggest WNT10B triggers tubular senescence and a proinflammatory microenvironment to accelerate fibrogenesis.

Tubular-specific ablation of Wnt10b prevents metabolic reprogramming and preserves mitochondrial homeostasis

To investigate WNT10B-regulated pathways, intersection analysis was performed between two genesets (the upregulated genes in Wnt10bcko/UIRI vs Wnt10bfl/fl/UIRI group; the downregulated genes in Wnt10bfl/fl/UIRI vs Wnt10bfl/fl/Sham group). 574 genes (with Wnt10b suppressed) were obtained and utilized for pathway enrichment and heatmap analyses. As shown, Wnt10b significantly inhibited fatty acid metabolism, mitochondrial biogenesis, and PPAR signaling (Fig. 3A). Of note, compared to UIRI mice, Ppara (PPARα) and Ppargc1a (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-gamma coactivator-1α, PGC-1α), two key factors modulating FAO were preserved; however, Pkm and Hk2 (Hexokinase 2), two crucial kinases mediating glycolysis, were blocked in Wnt10bcko mice (Fig. 3B). Western blotting and immunofluorescence showed Wnt10bcko mice preserved PGC-1α, PPARα, and CPT1A (Carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A), the rate-limiting enzyme of FAO, but inhibited HK2 and PKM2, the key rate-limiting enzymes of glycolysis (Fig. 3C–E and Supplementary Fig. S2G). Nile red staining showed Wnt10bcko mice inhibited lipid droplets accumulation (Supplementary Fig. S2H).

Mitochondrial homeostasis was also assessed. Mitochondrial outer membrane protein TOMM20 staining, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis, and MitoTracker and MitoSox staining showed Wnt10bcko mice preserved mitochondrial integrity and mass, and inhibited mitochondrial ROS production (Supplementary Fig. S2G, H).

Metabolomics was performed in Wnt10bcko/UIRI and Wnt10bfl/fl/UIRI mice. 17 metabolite superclasses (Supplementary Fig. S2I, J) were identified. Metabolic sets enrichment analysis (MSEA) indicated that these metabolites were strongly related with longevity regulation, FAO, senescence-related glycolysis, and FoxO pathways (Fig. 3F). Wnt10bcko mice reduced the levels of glycolysis-related metabolite of L-(+)-lactic acid (Fig. 3G). Consistently, chemiluminescence analyses verified Wnt10bcko mice downregulated several key metabolites from glycolysis process, for example, fructose-6-phosphate, phoshoenolpyruate, pyruvate, and lactate (Fig. 3H). Of interest, we found Wnt10bcko mice induced lipolysis of branched (3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaric acid), short-chain (adipic acid), and long-chain saturated fatty acids (octadecanoic acid). Moreover, tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) intermediate metabolites, such as itaconic acid and cis-aconitate, were also decreased in Wnt10bcko mice (Fig. 3G), suggesting increased fatty acid metabolism and restored TCA cycle.

UUO model, with ectopically expressed Wnt10b gene, was established. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S3, upon Wnt10b overexpression, FAO was further reduced, while tubular cellular senescence and fibrogenesis were further exacerbated. Furthermore, Wnt10b-overexpressed CKD model was also administered with mitoquinone (MitoQ), a mitochondrion-targeted antioxidant. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S4, MitoQ counteracted the effects of Wnt10b through blocking metabolic reprogramming, cellular senescence and fibrogenesis. These results further strengthen the key role of WNT10B in tubular metabolic reprogramming and cell senescence.

FOXO6 essentially mediates WNT10B/β-catenin transduction in metabolic reprogramming and tubular senescence

Primary tubular cells, from Wnt10b knock-in loxP mice (Wnt10b(cki)fl/fl) and wildtype mice, were isolated and cultured (Fig. 4A). The cells were transduced with Cre-expressing adenovirus (AdV-CMV-Cre), with a high infection efficiency of over 98%, to generate tubular cells with transgenic Wnt10b (Supplementary Fig. S5A–C). As shown, transgenic Wnt10b significantly decreased Ppargc1a, Ppara, Cpt1a, Acox1, and Tomm20, but upregulated Hk2 and Pkm mRNA levels (Fig. 4B). Western blotting showed active β-catenin was increased, while PGC-1α and PPARα were inhibited after transgene of Wnt10b (Fig. 4C).

MitoTracker, MitoSox, and Nile red staining showed Wnt10b transgene decreased mitochondrial mass, and triggered mitochondrial ROS and lipid accumulation (Supplementary Fig. S5D and Fig. 4D). Furthermore, Wnt10b transgene induced γH2AX foci in nucleus, showing DNA double-strand breaking (Fig. 4D), and triggered p16INK4A, p19ARF, and fibronectin upregulation (Fig. 4D, E).

Primary tubular cells from β-catenin loxP mice (β-cateninfl/fl) mice (Supplementary Fig. S5E) were isolated and transduced with AdV-CMV-Cre to establish β-catenin knockout tubular cells. Some cells were further treated with adenovirus expressing Wnt10b (AdV-Wnt10b) (Fig. 4F). As shown, β-catenin knockout cells restored Ppargc1a and Ppara mRNA levels, and inhibited Pkm mRNA expression upon AdV-Wnt10b (Fig. 4G). Similar results were observed when PGC-1α and PPARα proteins were assessed (Supplementary Fig. S5F). β-catenin knockout cells downregulated fibronectin and p16INK4A protein expression as well (Supplementary Fig. S5F).

TCF/LEF or FoxO family are both reported to be involved in β-catenin signaling32,33. Transcriptosome and qPCR analysis were first conducted in Wnt10bcko mice to determine key transcription factor(s) involved in WNT10B/β-catenin signaling. Of interest, Foxo6 mRNA and FOXO6 protein were significantly induced in Wnt10bfl/fl/UIRI mice but inhibited in Wnt10bcko/UIRI mice (Supplementary Fig. S5G–J).

Co-immunoprecipitation assay was further performed to identify the key role of FOXO6 in WNT10B/β-catenin downstream signaling. As shown in Fig. 4H and Supplementary Fig. S5K, Wnt10b triggered β-catenin binding with FOXO6, but not other transcription factors. In addition, FOXO6 was induced in Wnt10b-transgenic primary cells, and bound with β-catenin in the nucleus (Fig. 4I, J and Supplementary Fig. S5L). Whereas, in β-catenin knockout cells, Wnt10b lost its effect on activating FOXO6 (Fig. 4K and Supplementary Fig. S5M). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) showed FOXO6 is involved in metabolic pathways, longevity, and cellular senescence (Supplementary Fig. S5N). The progressive increase in β-catenin and FOXO6 in CKD were also testified in UUO mice (Supplementary Fig. S5O).

HKC-8 cells, a human tubular cell line, were also adopted to testify the role of WNT10B/FOXO6 signaling in cell senescence. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S6A–J, ectopic WNT10B or β-catenin triggered FOXO6, decreased FAO, and increased glycolysis to induce cell senescence and fibrogenesis. KYA1797K, the degradation agent of β-catenin, abolished the effects of WNT10B (Supplementary Fig. S6K). Furthermore, FOXO6 augmented WNT10B signaling. Senolytic drugs, dasatinib and quercetin34, restored PGC-1α, CPT1A, and inhibited HK2, and decreased lactate production in WNT10B or WNT10B/FOXO6-expressed cells (Supplementary Fig. S6L, M). These results indicate WNT10B/FOXO6 signaling critically mediates metabolic reprogramming and tubular cell senescence.

Increased WNT10B/FOXO6 signaling promotes renal fibrosis in clinical nephropathy and is associated with cell senescence

Sequential sections from CKD patients were adopted to elucidate the key role of WNT10B/FOXO6 signaling in clinical nephropathy. As shown, WNT10B and FOXO6 were predominantly co-localized, positively correlated, in tubules in various CKD patients (Fig. 5A, B). Along with progressive stages of CKD, WNT10B and FOXO6 were also nearly concomitantly increased in tubules (Fig. 5C).

FOXO6, p16INK4A staining were performed in sequential sections from CKD patients. Sirius red staining was also conducted to assess fibrotic lesions in the same patient. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was assessed in FOXO6-expressed cells. As shown, ectopic FOXO6 decreased the capacity of basal and maximal respiration, proton leakage, and ATP production. However, the PPARα agonist fenofibrate restored these parameters (Fig. 6C, D). FOXO6 inhibited PPARGC1A transcription by binding to its multiple promoter loci (Supplementary Fig. S7B–D), and downregulated OXPHOS proteins of ATP5A, UQCRC2, MTCO1, SDHB, and NDUFB8 (Fig. 6E and Supplementary Fig. S7E). In WNT10B-expressed cells, interference of FOXO6 preserved PPARα, PGC-1α, and TOMM20 expression (Supplementary Fig. S7F, G), and retarded fibronectin and γH2AX (Fig. 6F).

Transcriptome analysis was performed in FOXO6-expressed cells. As shown, FOXO6 is highly involved in increased Wnt signaling, cellular senescence and glucose metabolism pathways, and decreased FAO activity and mitochondrial biogenesis (Supplementary Fig. S7H–J). Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) was performed in FOXO6-expressed HEK 293T cells to clarify the specific DNA binding of FOXO6. FOXO6 group exhibited substantial differences in signal intensity across genic region (Fig. 6G and Supplementary Fig. S7K) and boosted occupancy of the promoter region on chromatin (Fig. 6H). KEGG analysis of FOXO6-targeted genes revealed FOXO6 was enriched in fibrosis and senescence pathways (Fig. 6I). We determined chromatin features of 293T cells (Supplementary Fig. S7L), and identified FOXO6 directly binds to the transcription elongation region of PPARA gene, and the active promoter site of PKM as well. qPCR analysis further demonstrated ectopic FOXO6 inhibited PPARA, but triggered PKM mRNA expression (Fig. 6J, K). In addition, the expression of key genes of FAO pathway, CPT1A, CPT2, and ACOX2, was also inhibited in FOXO6-expressing cells (Supplementary Fig. S7M).

The role of FOXO6 in glycolysis was further testified. As shown, lactate dose- and time-dependently triggered p16INK4A (Supplementary Fig. S8A, B). Transcriptome showed lactate decreased mitochondria-related mRNAs, and induced a variety of senescence-related mRNAs (Supplementary Fig. S8C, D). Lactate further triggered ROS production, reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, and induced SA-β-gal activity (Supplementary Fig. S8E–G). Furthermore, 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG), an analog of glucose to inhibit HK2, inhibited p16INK4A in WNT10B or WNT10B/FOXO6-treated cells (Supplementary Fig. S8H). These further demonstrates the vital role of FOXO6 in metabolic reprogramming and cell senescence.

Foxo6 KO mice (Foxo6−/−) and their littermates (Foxo6+/+) (Supplementary Fig. S9A) were subjected to the UIRI model (Fig. 7A, B and Supplementary Fig. S9B). The Scr and BUN levels were greatly decreased in Foxo6−/−/UIRI mice (Fig. 7C). Sirius red staining revealed Foxo6−/−/UIRI mice inhibited fibrogenesis (Fig. 7D and Supplementary Fig. S9C).

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) was conducted in Foxo6−/−/UIRI and Foxo6+/+/UIRI mice (Fig. 7E). 25,890 total kidney single-cells were obtained, representing 15 clusters according to marker genes (Fig. 7F and Supplementary Fig. S9D). Foxo6−/− or Foxo6+/+ mice were also performed with UIRI model and conducted to Wnt10b-expressing plasmid (Supplementary Fig. S10A). As shown, ectopic Wnt10b mediated metabolic reprogramming and senescence in tubular cells, and induced proinflammatory microenvironment and renal fibrosis in Foxo6+/+ mice. However, Foxo6−/− mice resisted these effects (Supplementary Fig. S10B–M). These further suggest the mediating role of FOXO6 in WNT10B signaling.

Hence, we discovered WNT10B/FOXO6 signaling mediates renal fibrosis. In CKD, WNT10B switches on, triggering β-catenin/FOXO6 pathway, which induces metabolic reprogramming, and then triggers senescence fate transition in tubular cells. As a result, a proinflammatory microenviroment is established and irreversible fibrotic scarring has occurred (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Cellular senescence is a critical event during CKD progression35,36. Cell metabolic disorder mutually aggravates tubular senescence, resulting in a proinflammatory microenvironment. This would irreversibly lead to renal fibrosis37. However, the inductive factor of tubular senescence has not been clarified.

Wnt/β-catenin signaling highly links to tubular cell senescence10,28. However, the detailed mechanisms are undefined. Wnts (wingless-related integration sites) constitute a set of 19 members in mammals. In the adult kidney, Wnt ligands exist primarily in a quiescent state, but are activated in pathological conditions38. We have discovered WNT9A is a trigger for tubular senescence10. Whereas, the key candidates of Wnt(s) related to tubular senescence and CKD need to be fully demonstrated.

In this study, conjoint analysis revealed WNT10B is strongly involved in cellular senescence and fibrogenesis in CKD. In consistency, we previously found WNT10B is strongly induced in natural ageing kidney28. However, the role of WNT10B in tubular senescence and CKD, as well as the underlying mechanisms, are unknown. Herein, we found that WNT10B induces metabolic reprogramming and mitochondrial dysfunction in tubular cells. As a classic Wnt ligand, WNT10B still depends on β-catenin to transmit signals39. However, FOXO6, a member belonging to the forkhead box O family40, is identified to mediate WNT10B/β-catenin signaling. Herein, we found FOXO6 controls metabolic reprogramming and cell senescence fate transition in tubular cells.

Cell fate transition is a fundamental process through which cells acquire specialized, reprogrammed, or altered functions41. Upon different fates, cellular properties of morphology, differentiation capacity, and biological features can clearly change42. Senescence is a kind of fate transition in rejuvenating cells, which results in the loss of proliferation in cells. Thus, senescent cells acquire mesenchymal characteristics and SASP phenotype43,44. The mechanisms underlying TECs' senescence fate are related to telomere loss, stress, and organelle dysfunction45,46. However, the mediators of TECs senescence fate transition are still in mystery.

Wnt(s) upregulation and β-catenin activation, primarily in renal tubular cells in CKD, are hallmarks of renal fibrosis47. Recent studies, including ours, showed several Wnt(s) directly or indirectly trigger the communication between tubular and mesenchymal cells26,48. Besides these, Wnt(s) are aslo highly involved in the disruption of lipid metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction, both the mediators of senescence28,49. Notably, senescent cells potently secrete proinflammatory and fibrotic ingredients, which are encapsulated in exosomes or not, to alter the microenvironment, thereby affecting disease progression47. However, in CKD, the mechanisms of Wnt(s) in disrupting tubular cell metabolism and its relation with cell senescence fate transition are not elucidated.

Kidney needs high energy to fulfill metabolic functions50. Rich mitochondria in the kidney facilitate aerobic oxidation of FAO to supply abundant ATP. Through this, renal tubular cells adjust the water, electrolytes, and acid-base balance51,52. However, the hypoxic and ischemic environment under CKD conditions, trigger anaerobic glycolysis in tubular cells, i.e., leading to metabolic reprogramming. This transition is a key mediator of CKD53. Of note, large quantities of metabolites would be released to mediate immune cells or fibroblasts activation54. PPARα is the key transcription factor regulating FAO55, while PKM2 is a rate-limiting enzyme obligating in glycolysis56. Interestingly, we revealed WNT10B regulates PPARα and PKM2 both through FOXO6, exhibiting the opposite effects. These result in metabolic reprogramming in tubular cells, promoting cell senescence fate transition and CKD progression.

Tubular cell senescence accelerates renal fibrosis10,28,35,57. Senescent cells lose proliferation capacity, but gain a strong ability of secretion, the Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype (SASP) effect. SASP molecules are quite complex. Generally, proinflammatory/fibrotic factors dominate SASP components in renal tubular cells58. In this study, we found CSF is derived from senescent tubular cells, which induces macrophage activation. Thus, through FOXO6/CSF signaling network, WNT10B triggers paracrine actions to exaggerate the harmful effects of tubular cell senescence, initiating a cascade reaction. These findings indicate the dominant role of WNT10B in CKD.

The FOXO families closely correlate with longevity and metabolism. Among FOXOs, FOXO6 is highly involved in glucose and lipid metabolism40. FOXO6 expresses in the fatty liver and skeletal muscle atrophy59,60. In addition to gluconeogenesis and lipid metabolism, FOXO6 is also intimately related to glycolysis and inflammation61,62. However, the role of FOXO6 in kidney diseases is unknown yet. We found FOXO6 induces metabolic reprogramming and grasps senescence fate in renal tubular cells, supplying a new insight for revealing renal tubular senescence.

Another thing is the increased levels of WNT10B and FOXO6 mRNA were lower than protein levels. These indicate WNT10B and FOXO6 may undergo post-translational modifications, the key process for protein maturation, activation, and stable conformations with tertiary/quaternary structures63. Moreover, the increased mRNA translation or decreased protein degradation cannot be excluded. Clarifying the mechanisms would facilitate therapeutic strategies for CKD. Furthermore, a long-term CKD model, such as 5/6 nephrectomy, could be used to assess the long-term effects of WNT10B/FOXO6 activation. This would be studied in the future. We adopted Ksp-cre mice to induce Wnt10b ablation in tubular segments. Co-staining showed WNT10B was deleted in distal tubules and collecting ducts, and most proximal tubules, confirming the efficient ablation.

Overall, we identified the fundamental mechanisms of tubular senescence in CKD. This study suggests targeting WNT10B/FOXO6 signaling would be promising for treating CKD.

Methods

Human specimens study

The section slides were from kidney biopsies of 30 patients, including lupus nephritis, membranous nephropathy, IgA nephropathy, focal segmental glomerular sclerosis, diabetic kidney disease, which were provided by Huadu District People’s Hospital. Para-cancerous kidney tissues from patients undergoing radical nephrectomy, were collected as control. Urine samples were acquired from patients with CKD (stage 3–5) or age/sex-matched controls. All procedures received approval from the Clinical Ethics Committee on Human Subjects of Huadu District People’s Hospital (Approval No.2024200) and informed consents were obtained from all participants. Relevant demographic and clinical characteristics of these patients are summarized in Supplementary Table S1 and 2.

Animal study

All animal procedures were conducted under standard housing conditions and environment, with the approval of the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanfang hospital, Southern Medical University (Protocol No. NFYY-20230608-004). Considering the potential interference of hormonal fluctuations in estrous cycles in female mice, this study was designed and performed exclusively using male mice. All mice were housed in a room at Nanfang Hospital Animal Center, with 12-h light-dark diurnal cycle, controlled temperature (20–25 °C) and humidity (40–60%), and were provided free access to standard rodent chow and water. The mice were randomly assigned to either the control or experimental groups at the beginning of each experiment. For sample collections, mice were euthanized with pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg).

Six to eight-week-old male C57BL/6J and BALB/c mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Peking, China). Wnt10b conditioned knockout loxP mice (Wnt10bfl/fl) (Cat no. TOS190424MG1), Ksp-cre mice (Cat no. C001027), Wnt10b conditioned knock-in loxP mice (Wnt10b(cki)fl/fl) (Cat no. KIMR210831MG1), β-catenin specific knockout loxp/loxp mice (β-cateninfl/fl) (Cat no. CKOAIB191118MG1) and Foxo6 systematic knockout mice (Foxo6−/−) (Cat no. KOAIV190725MG3) generated by C57BL/6J strain background were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences Company (Guangzhou, China). Tubule-specific Wnt10b knockout mice were generated by crossing Wnt10bfl/fl mice with Ksp-cre mice, and genotyping was performed by PCR using genomic DNA isolated from tail biopsies of 4-week-old offsprings, the PCR primers for genotyping are listed in Supplementary Table S3. Overall, the detail of animal models in this study are provided below.

For ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) model, 6–8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice, weighing 20–25 g, were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium via intraperitoneal injection, and followed by bilateral clamping of renal pedicles for 30 min using microaneurysm clamps. Mice were euthanized at designated time points (1, 3, 7 days after surgery). The sham-operated were used as controls (IRI-0 day group).

For Adriamycin (ADR)-induced CKD model, 6-week-old male BALB/c mice, weighing 20–22 g, were intravenously administration of doxorubicin hydrochloride (10.5 mg/kg; Mym Biological Technology, cat. 25316-40-9), and were euthanized at designated time points (1, 3, 5 weeks after injection).

For unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) model, 6–8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice by ligating the left ureter under pentobarbital anesthesia, and were euthanized at designated time points (3, 7, 14 days after injection). Sham-operated mice served as controls (UUO-0 day group).

For unilateral ischemia-reperfusion injury (UIRI) model, 6–8-week-old male mice (Wnt10bcko, Foxo6−/− and their littermate controls) were used. Briefly, mice were anaesthetized with pentobarbital sodium before surgery. Unilateral kidney ischemia model was conducted by clamping the left renal pedicles, lasting for 35 min, with body temperature at 37–38 °C. After clamp removal, reperfusion of the kidney was visually confirmed. All models received unilateral nephrectomy (UNX) of the right kidney at day 10, and all mice were euthanized at day 11. The sham-operated mice served as experimental controls.

For ectopic expressing Wnt10b in UIRI model, 6–8-week-old C57BL/6J mice were received pcDNA3.1-Flag tagged-mouse Wnt10b plasmid (pWnt10b, 1.5 μg/kg) or empty vector injection at day 4 after UIRI surgery by a hydrodynamic-based tail vein injection. For ectopic expressing Wnt10b in UUO model, 6–8-week-old C57BL/6J mice were received Wnt10b plasmid (1.5 μg/kg) or empty vector injection 1 day prior to the UUO surgery. For mitoQ inhibition study in UIRI model, 6–8-week-old C57BL/6 J mice were administered with 1 mg/kg mitoQ (Focus Biomolecules; cat. 10-1363) via intraperitoneal injection following the administration of Wnt10b plasmid at day 4 after the surgery. The oligonucleotide sequences of plasmids are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen measurements

Serum creatinine (Scr) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels were tested by an automatic chemistry analyzer (Beckman Coulter, AU480) with commercial kits (Leadman Ltd). The values were reported as mg/dl.

Cell culture and treatments

The immortalized human renal tubular cell line, HKC-8, was kindly provided by Dr. Lorraine C. Racusen (Johns Hopkins University) and grown in DMEM/F12 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; VivaCell, cat.C04001-500) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (VivaCell, cat.C3420-0100) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For transient transfection experiments, cells, at 80%–90% confluence, were transfected with either pcDNA3.1-Flag tagged-plasmids encoding human WNT10B (pWNT10B), β-catenin (pDel-βcat), FOXO6 (pFOXO6), or with gene-specific siRNA or the corresponding empty vector (GenePharma, China). The oligonucleotide sequences of plasmids and siRNA are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Lactic acid sodium (HY-B2227B, MCE) was administered into HKC-8 cells. HKC-8 cells were also treated with 1 μM 2-deoxy-D-glucose(2-DG, HY-13966, MCE) for 24 h following transfection with pWNT10B or pWNT10B plus pFOXO6 plasmids. For senolytic treatment, transfected HKC-8 cells were treated with dasatinib (5 nM; Selleck, cat. S1021) and quercetin (10 μM, Sigma, cat. PHR1488) for 24 h.

Isolation of primary mouse tubular cells

Primary mouse tubular cells were isolated from Wnt10b conditional knock-in loxP mice (Wnt10b(cki)fl/fl), β-catenin conditional konckout loxP mice (β-cateninfl/fl) or Foxo6 systematically konckout mice (Foxo6−/−). In brief, minced renal cortices were digested with collagenase IV (0.75 mg/ml; Gibco, cat.17104-019) for 30 min at 37 °C. The digestate was then filtered through a 100 μm Cell strainer and followed by Percoll density centrifugation (32% Percoll). After washing twice with DMEM/F-12 and confirmation of the tubular morphology under light microscope, the tubules were resuspended in complete DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 10% FBS and 2% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were cultured for 48–72 h for confluency before adenovirus transfection.

Adenovirus transfection assay

Wnt10b-knockin cells were constructed in primary tubular cells, isolated from Wnt10b(cki)fl/fl mice, with transduction with an adenovirus carrying Cre recombinase under the CMV promoter (AdV-CMV-Cre; Genechem, China, cat. GCD0320409) or a negative control adenovirus (AdV-NC), at MOI of 100 in 2 mL of complete medium. After 12 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, and the cells were cultured for an additional 60 h before harvest for analysis. Primary cells were also isolated from Foxo6−/− mice, and then transfected with adenovirus carrying Wnt10b cDNA (AdV-Wnt10b, Genechem, Shanghai, China). In addition, primary cells from β-cateninfl/fl mice were incubated with AdV-CMV-Cre for 12 h to delete the ctnnb1 (β-catenin) gene, and the cells were immediately transfected with AdV-Wnt10b (or empty vector) for another 60 h.

Western blotting analysis

Protein expression was analyzed by Western blotting. Renal cortical tissues and cell pellets were homogenized by lysis buffer and then centrifuged to remove insoluble debris. Protein concentrations were quantified by BCA assay. Respective protein was separated by 10% or 12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The PVDF membrane was incubated with antibodies as indicated (Supplementary Table S4). The visualization with ECL was through a manual X-ray system (FujiFilm, cat. SUPER-RX-N-C). Image J software (National Institutes of Health, USA; V1.53) was used to quantify densitometry. The full unedited western blotting gels are provided in Source Data file.

Immunohistochemistry staining

Immunohistochemical staining were performed on 3 μm-thick paraffin-embedded sections. After dewaxing, hydration and antigen retrieval, the sections were incubated in 10% donkey serum, for 45 min, for blocking. Primary antibodies (listed in Supplementary Table S4) were added overnight at 4 °C. Biotin-conjugated secondary antibodies were added for 1 h at room temperature (Jacksom ImmunoResearch Laboratories). Signals were developed using the DAB peroxidase substrate kit, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. The images were photographed by Olympus BX53 microscope with EMCCD camera (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) under ×400 magnification. p16INK4A, WNT10B, and FOXO6 were stained, one by one, on three sequential slides from one patient. The signals were tested by semi-quantification. Briefly, 10–20 randomly selected images of each slice were taken throughout the tissue by a single observer who was blinded to the protocols. The integrated optical density (IOD) values were then calculated using Image Pro Plus software V6.0 (Media Cybernetivs, Inc., Rockville, USA). A mean value was generated from each section.

Immunofluorescence staining

Fresh frozen kidney specimens were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT). The sectioned (4 μm) specimens were performed for immunofluorescence staining. Kidney cryo-sections, primary tubular cells and HKC-8 cells cultured on the coverslips were fixed (4% paraformaldehyde, 15 min), and permeabilized and blocked by 0.2% Triton X-100 and 10% donkey serum. Primary antibodies were operated overnight at 4 °C, followed by fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibody (Jacksom ImmunoResearch Laboratories), for 1 h at room temperature. DAPI stained the Nuclei.

For paraffin-embedded sections (3 μm), the protocol was similar to that described for immunohistochemistry staining except for the secondary antibodies. Images were taken by confocal microscopy (Leica TCS SP2 AOBS, Leica Microsystems, Buffalo Grove, IL). The F4/80 signal was quantified as the percentage of positive staining area measured in 8–10 non-overlapping fields. A mean value was calculated for each section.

Sirius red staining

For Sirius red staining, paraffin-embedded kidney sections (5 μm) were immersed in Picro-Sirius Red staining solution (PHYGENE®, cat. PH1098) to assess kidney fibrosis. Images (10–20, randomly selected) were taken by Olympus microscope at ×400 magnification and quantified, as the percentage of signal-positive area over the total tissue area per digital image via Image Pro Plus software. A mean value was calculated for each section.

MitoSox and MitoTracker staining

Live cells cultured on the coverslips or fresh frozen kidney sections (3 μm) were subjected to MitoSox and MitoTracker staining using MitoSox Red (InvitrogenTM) and MitoTracker Red (InvitrogenTM), following the manufacture’s protocols. For live cells, MitoTracker reagent was added to a final concentration of 200 nM for 1 h at 37°C, and MitoSox reagent was added to a final concentration of 2 μM for 30 min. For tissue staining, freshly isolated kidneys were directly frozen in OCT and sectioned at 3 μm. The sections were immediately incubated with 3 μM MitoTracker reagent (diluted in PBS) for 30 min, or 5 μM MitoSox reagent for 10 min. After staining, slices were washed with PBS, fixed in 4% PFA, and processed for fluorescence photography immediately.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase(SA-β-gal) staining

The SA-β-gal staining was conducted on either fresh frozen kidney sections (3 μm) or cells cultured on coverslips using a commercial kit (Cell Signaling Technology, cat.#9860), following the manufacturer’s instructions. After incubating with the fixative reagent for 15 min, slices were incubated with the staining solution overnight at 37 °C. Samples were subsequently coverslipped with a water-based mounting medium, and images were taken by Olympus microscope at ×400 magnification.

Nile red staining

The Nile red staining was conducted on either fresh frozen kidney sections (5 μm) or cells cultured on coverslips for lipid droplets detection with a commercial kit (Sigma, cat. 72485). After fixing (4% paraformaldehyde, 10 min), slices were incubated with the working solution (1 μg/ml, diluted in DAPI) in dark and mounted with fluorescence antifade medium. Images were subsequently captured by confocal microscopy.

Transmission electron microscopy

For transmission electron microscopy analysis, renal cortical tissues from mouse and cell pellets were fixed in 1.25% glutaraldehyde/phosphage buffer, followed by resin embedding and ultrafine sectioned. Mitochondrial ultrastructure and abundance were observed and photographed with an electron microscope (JEOLJEM − 1010, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from renal cortical tissues or cells, using the TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY). cDNA was synthesized using the Reverse Transcription System kit (Vazyme, cat. R323-01). qPCR was performed on an ABI PRISM 7000 Sequence Detection System (Applied Biosystems) using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, cat. Q341-02/03). Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated by normalizing the threshold cycle (CT) values to β-actin and then to the control group to obtain the fold change. The primers are listed in Supplementary Table S5. For mtDNA analysis, total DNA was extracted with a commercial mitochondrial DNA isolation kit (Saint-Bio, cat. 12021). The relative mtDNA copy number was quantified by qPCR using primers for the mitochondrial genes (listed in Supplementary Table S5) and normalized to ribosomal protein s18 (RSP18).

Nuclear protein extraction and co-immunoprecipitation assay

Nuclear fractions were isolated using a commercial kit (BestBio, cat.BB-3102). For the co-immunoprecipitation procedure, protein lysates (500 μl) were immunoprecipitated with 5 μg β-catenin antibodies (BD Biosciences, cat. 610154) or control mouse IgG (Proteintech, cat. B900620), plus protein A/G plus agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat. sc-2003) overnight at 4 °C. The precipitated complexes were washed 5 times with washing buffer, eluted by boiling in SDS sample buffer for 10 min, and subsequently analyzed by immunoblotting. All the coimmunoprecipitation experiments were repeated at least three times. Primary antibodies used are shown in the Supplementary Table S4.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-PCR assay

Using the SimpleChIP® Plus (Magnetic Bead) Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. 9005), ChIP assay was performed. Briefly, cross-linked chromatin was fragmented by micrococcal nuclease digestion to yield fragments corresponding to 1–5 nucleosomes. The chromatin was then immunoprecipitated with 2 μg Flag antibody (MBL, M185-3), positive control H3 antibody, or negative mouse control IgG antibody coupled to magnetic beads, respectively. Precipitated DNA was purified and analyzed by PCR using GoTaq Green Master Mix (Promega, cat. M7123) with specific gene primers (listed in Supplementary Table S5).

JC-1 Mitochondrial Potential analysis

HKC-8 cells were harvested after specific treatments, and washed with PBS. For JC-1 probe staining (Thermo Fisher, cat. T3168), 1 × 106 cells were resuspended in 400 μl JC-1 staining solution (5 μM), and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, followed by analysis in a BD FACS Calibur flow System and quantified as the ratio of red (JC-1 aggregate, 590 nm) to green (JC-1 monomer, 520 nm) fluorescence intensity. The relative mitochondrial membrane potential level was normalized to the controls.

Intracellular ROS detection

HKC-8 cells were treated with/without Lactic acid sodium, and washed with PBS. For DCFH-DA probe staining (Beyotime Biotechnology, S0034S), 1 × 106 cells were collected and treated with DCFH-DA (10 μM) for 30 min (37 °C, 5% CO2), followed by analysis in BD FACS Calibur flow for determining cell proportions. The relative mitochondrial membrane potential level was normalized to the controls.

Beta-galactosidase activity detection

HKC-8 cells were harvested after specific treatment, and stained using a LacZ beta-Galactosidase Intracellular Detection Kit (Abcam, cat. ab189816), followed by analysis in BD FACS Calibur flow system for determining cell proportions. Dead cells were excluded by Propidium iodide (PI), and the relative value was normalized to the controls.

Supernatant ATP measurement

Intracellular ATP concentrations in HKC-8 cells were measured by a commercial kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, cat. S0027). Cells were harvested and lysed after specific treatment, followed by mixing the lysates with the ATP assay working solution. The emitted luminescence (RLU) value was measured using a BioTek microplate reader system with GEN 5 software, ATP concentration was quantified from the standard curve, and normalized to the protein concentration.

Metabolic molecules measurement

Lactate, pyruvate, phosphoenolpyruvate, and fructose-6-phosphate concentrations of renal cortical tissue or HKC-8 cells were assessed by WST-8 Lactate Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, cat. P0393S), Amplex red pyruvate assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, cat. S0299S), Amplex red phosphoenolpyruvate assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, cat. S0311S), and WST-8 fructose-6-phosphate assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, S0183S). The concentration was calculated from the standard curve and normalized to the protein concentration (accessed by BCA).

5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) staining

HKC-8 cells were cultured on the coverslips and transfected with pWNT10B plasmid or empty vector. Cell proliferation was determined using a commercial kit BeyoClick™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit with Alexa Fluor 488 (Beyotime Biotechnology, cat. C0071S). Images were taken by confocal microscopy.

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) analysis

The mitochondrial energy metabolism of HKC-8 cells transfected with pFOXO6 plasmid or co-treated with Fenofibrate (1 μM, Sigma, cat.F6020) was assessed using the Seahorse XF cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent, cat. 103647-100). Cells were grown in XF96 microplates and subjected to indicated treatments for 24 h before assessment. After equilibrating with mixture Seahorse XF DMEM medium solution for 1 h, the OCR level was measured on a Seahorse XF96 Analyzer, with sequentially injections of etomoxir at 4 μM, and 1.5 μM oligomycin, FCCP (1 μM), as well as 0.5 μM rotenone and antimycin (Agilent, Cat. 103672-100). The result was calculated by Wave software (V), then normalized to the protein concentration (accessed by BCA).

Gene expression omnibus (GEO) database and Nephroseq database analysis

The RNA-seq profiling of microdissected tubulointersititial tissue from 90 kidney samples (68 from CKD patients and 22 from nephrectomy controls) were obtained from GEO database with accession number GSE175759. For intersection analysis, CDKN1A, CDKN2A, FN1 and ACTA2 positively correlated genes based on batch analysis of Spearman correlation test (rs;> 0.25, P < 0.05); up-regulated deferentially expressed genes in CKD samples based on DESeq2 algorithm (V1.38.3) (Log2FoldChange > 0, P < 0.05); reference genesets were acquired from GO “Canonical Wnt signaling” and KEGG “Wnt signaling pathway”. GSVA and limma R package (V3.54.2) were used to determine functional pathways that differed significantly between WNT10B/FOXO6-positive and WNT10B/FOXO6-negative groups. The RNA-seq data (Illumina platform) from Nephroseq database were directly downloaded from the website (https://www.nephroseq.org/) with the indicated database name.

RNA-sequencing analysis

Renal cortical tissues or HKC-8 cells were adopted total RNA using a TRIzol system. The procedure of establishing cDNA libraries and sequencing were completed at Novogene (Beijing, China). Differential expression analysis of two groups (three biological repeats per condition) was performed using the DESeq2 R package (V1.38.3). For each condition, three biological repeats were included. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between two groups were acquired from DESeq2 algorithm with the parameter of P value < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis, including GO, KEGG and Reactome pathway enrichment was performed on up- or down-regulated DEGs by the ClusterProfiler R package (V4.6.2), with a significance threshold of P value < 0.05. GSEA of all genes was performed by GSEA software (V4.3.2) by molecular signatures database (MsigDB) genesets.

Metabolomics

Non-targeted metabolomic profiling was performed by Shanghai APT Biotechenology Co., Ltd, including sample preparation and LC-MS/MS analysis. In brief, renal cortical tissues from Wnt10bcko and Wnt10bfl/fl mice after UIRI surgery were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. 80 mg of tissue was weighed on ice, transferred to an Eppendorf tube(2 ml) containing five ceramic beads and homogenized in 200 μl of H2O, followed by adding 800 μL methanol/acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) for metabolite extraction. The mixture was centrifuged for 20 min in a condition of 14000 × g at 4 °C. The supernatant was then dried in a vacuum centrifuge, and re-dissolved in 100 μL acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v). The solution was centrifuged for 15 min to obtain the supernatant for analysis. The LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using UHPLC (Vanquish UHPLV, Thermo) coupled to a Orbitrap ExplorisTM 480. The raw MS data were collected and converted to MzXML format using ProteoWizard MSConvert before importing into freely available XCMS software. Parameters for peak picking were listed: centWave m/z = 10 ppm, peakwidth = c(10,60), prefilter = c(10,100). Parameters for peak grouping were listed: bw=5, mzwid=0.025, minfrac=0.5. CAMERA (Collection of Algorithms of MEtabolite pRofile Annotation) was used for annotation of isotopes and adducts. Only the variables having more than 50% of the nonzero measurement values in at least one group were kept. Compound identification of metabolites was performed by comparing of accuracy m/z value (<10 ppm), and MS/MS spectra with an in-house database established with available authentic standards. Data were then subjected to sum-normalization and multivariate data analysis. The 7-fold cross-validation and response permutation testing were used to evaluate the robustness of the model. The variable importance in the projection (VIP) value of each variable in the orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model was calculated to indicate its contribution to the classification. Unpaired Student’s t test was applied to determine the significance of differences between two groups of independent samples. VIP > 1 and p value < 0.05 were used to screen significant changed metabolites. All metabolites were subjected to Metabolite set enrichment analysis (MSEA) based on KEGG and RaMP-DB metabolite set library by using MetaboAnalyst database (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca/), fold enrichment ratio value were calculated by Statistic Q/Expected Q, and P value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant enriched pathway.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing analysis

The whole kidney samples from Foxo6−/− or its littermate control after UIRI procedure were collected flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. The Single-Nucleus RNA sequencing was performed by Novogene (Beijing, China). Raw data were processed using the 10X Genomics Cell Ranger pipeline (V3.0) and aligned to the mouse mouse reference genome (mm10) with default parameters. The Seurat (V4.2.3) was used to filter out genes expressed in less than three nuclei, nuclei where less than 200 genes were detected, and nuclei that have more than 20% mitochondrial unique molecular identifiers (UMI). Data were normalized and scaled using SCTransform and integrated using canonical correlation analysis (CCA) method. For dimensional reduction and clustering, highly variable genes were selected and the principal components analysis (PCA) using the ElbowPlot function and then clustered by graph-based algorithm at a resolution of 0.6. Cell clusters were annotated based on the expression level of canonical cell types-specific marker genes (presented in Supplementary Fig. S7). Cell distributions of each cell-type subsets were measured.

For analysis of all tubular cells, a new Seurat-object was built based on the subset function. Cell cycle of each tubular cell-type was assessed by Scran package (V4.2.2). Profibrotic, senescence, FAO, and glycolysis score of each subset were tested by AUCell algorithm (V1.20.2) with published genesets. DoRothEA algorithm (V1.7.4) were used to assess the activity of transcription factors. For analysis of proximal tubular cells, Monocle2 (V2.2.3) package were used for pseudotemporal analysis and to discover the different cell-state transitions in proximal tubular cells with or without Foxo6 gene silencing. The pseudotime model were constructed by selected ordering genes with a criterion of dispersion_empirical ≥ 1.5 × dispersion_fit & mean_expression ≥ 0.1. The BEAM function in Monocle2 and GO enrichment analysis were used to define the different gene clusters of cells from State 4 or State 5.

For intercellular communication between all senescence tubular cell-types and macrophage, CellChat objects were established using the createCellChat function (V1.6.1). After setting the Secreted Signaling pathways in CellChatDB.mouse as the database, objects of two groups were subjected to identify putative interaction pairs with default parameters. DEGs analysis in macrophage were performed using the FindMarkers function in Seurat, and the down-regulated genes (p < 0.05) were used for GO enrichment analysis.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-sequencing analysis

Human embryo kidney cell line (HEK 293T) was transfected with pFOXO6 plasmid or an empty vector for 24 h. Cells were cross-linked with formaldehyde, and then subjected to lysing, chromatin isolation, and cleaverage by sonication, followed by immunoprecipitation using a ChIP-Grade Anti-Flag antibody. After reversing cross-links, the purified DNA was subjected to sequencing libraries establish. Cells without precipitation of each condition were served as input control for background library. All libraries were constructed and sequenced by Novogene.

Raw reads were transformed using the Illumina Hiseq platform, and processed through FastQC (V0.11.5) and Fastq (V0.19.11) software to obtain clean reads. Clean reads were aligned to the GRch38 human reference genome using BWA (V0.7.12). Peaks were called using MACS2 (V2.1.0) with P value threshold of 0.005. Deeptools (V3.2.1) software were used to generate profile plots of peak summit density around transcript start sites (TSS). For peak annotation, including genomic feature distribution and nearest gene assignment, were analyzed by ChIPseeker R package (V1.34.1)64. KEGG analysis of FOXO6-targeted genes were conducted by KOBAS (V3.0) software with a significant threshold of P value < 0.05.

For genome-wide chromatin state annotation, publicly available ChIP-seq datasets for histone modifications (H3K4me1, H3K4me3, H2K27ac, H3K27me3, H3K9me3, and H3K36me3) in HEK293T cells were downloaded from the SRA database using accession numbers (SRR14636314, SRR14636315, SRR14636334, SRR14636335, SRR14636336, SRR14636337, SRR14636361, SRR14636362, SRR14636363, SRR949471, SRR949472). For ChromHMM (V1.24) annotation, the following parameters were used: java -mx1600M -jar ChromHMM.jar BinarizeBam hg38; java -mx4000M -jar ChromHMM.jar LearnModel 8 hg38.

Statistics and reproducibility

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software (version 26.0). For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way ANOVA was used, followed by LSD post-hoc test (for homogenous variance) or Games–Howell post-hoc test (for non-homogeneous variances). Two-tailed independent t test and Wilcoxon test (for sequencing data) were conducted to determine the statistical significance between two groups. Correlations were performed via two-tailed Spearman analysis. Statistical significance was identified when P value < 0.05. All statistical data were presented in Source data file.

All experiments were independently repeated to ensure reproducibility. Representative micrographs are from multiple biological repeats as indicated in Figure legends.
