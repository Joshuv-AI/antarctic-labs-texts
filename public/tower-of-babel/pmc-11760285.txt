Crovalimab in the paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria treatment landscape
Authors: Alexander Röth, Austin G Kulasekararaj, Phillip Scheinberg, Jun-ichi Nishimura
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTParoxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) is an acquired, rare, life-threatening hematopoietic stem cell disorder that causes stem cell-derived cells to be vulnerable to complement-mediated lysis and manifests as hemolytic anemia, thrombosis, and peripheral blood cytopenias. C5 inhibitors, eculizumab, and ravulizumab, are recognized as the current standard of care for PNH treatment in countries where they are available. Crovalimab (PiaSky®), which is approved for the treatment of PNH, is a novel anti-C5 inhibitor with an every-4-weeks, low-volume, subcutaneous maintenance dosing regimen with the possibility for self-administration. Data from three phase III studies highlight the overall favorable benefit-risk profile of crovalimab, showing that crovalimab has promising potential to address the unmet medical and socioeconomic challenges in the PNH treatment landscape.KEYWORDS: Crovalimab, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, PNH, complement inhibitor, anti-C5 recycling monoclonal antibody, subcutaneous self-administration, every-4-weeks dosing

Introduction

Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) is an acquired, rare, life-threatening, hematopoietic stem cell disorder with an estimated yearly incidence of 1.3 cases per million and a prevalence of 1.59 cases per 100,000 globally [1–6]. In the US and EU combined, 11,700 estimated patients live with PNH [4,5,7].

PNH results from the clonal expansion of stem cells harboring a somatic PIGA mutation, which disrupts the anchoring of complement regulatory proteins CD55 and CD59 on the surface of PNH blood cells [8–10]. About one third of patients who carry PNH clones have classic PNH, defined as having clinical manifestations of hemolysis or thrombosis [6,11,12]. The complement system plays an essential role in the innate immune response by forming the first line of defense against pathogens [13]. Complement response can be activated via one of three pathways by a cascade of protein interactions that converge at a C3-based proximal pathway followed by a shared C5-based terminal pathway [14]. Deficiency of CD59 (also known as membrane inhibitor of reactive lysis [MIRL], which blocks the formation of the membrane attack complex) and CD55 (or decay-accelerating factor [DAF], which controls early complement activation by inhibiting C3 and C5 convertases) on the surface of PNH erythrocytes causes these blood cells to be vulnerable to complement-mediated lysis, leading to intravascular hemolysis – the hallmark of classic PNH [6,13]. PNH manifests as hemolytic anemia, thrombosis, and peripheral blood cytopenias, with thrombosis and renal complications being statistically significant risk factors for mortality [1,15–17].

Patients with PNH experience poor quality of life. According to data from the International PNH Registry, PNH impacts daily living, with 17% of individuals not working or working less than before their diagnosis and 23% hospitalized within the past 6 months due to PNH-related complications [18]. Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy – Fatigue Scale (FACIT-Fatigue) and European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) global health/quality of life scores from the International PNH Registry show that patients with PNH experience considerable disease-related fatigue and lower overall quality of life than the general population [19].

Terminal complement inhibitors that prevent C5 activation, eculizumab and ravulizumab, are globally recognized as the current standard of care for the treatment of patients with PNH with symptomatic intravascular hemolysis or thrombosis in countries around the world where these drugs are available [20–22]. C5 inhibitors effectively control intravascular hemolysis in most patients with PNH, offering them an almost normal life expectancy [21,23,24]. The C3-targeting proximal complement inhibitor, pegcetacoplan, has also been approved in some countries for the treatment of adult patients with PNH, including in patients with suboptimal response to C5 inhibitors [20,25]. Oral factor B inhibitor, iptacopan, was approved for the treatment of adult patients with PNH, including patients with insufficient response to a C5 inhibitor, in Europe, China, Japan, United Arab Emirates, and the US [26–29]. Oral factor D inhibitor, danicopan, was approved as an add-on therapy to C5 inhibitor treatment when patients have insufficient response to these treatments in Japan, Europe, and the US [30–32]. For patients who are ineligible or lack access to complement inhibitor therapies, the primary treatment is best supportive care, including supplementary iron and folic acid, transfusions, and in some cases, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation [1,33–35].

This review reports the pharmacology, clinical efficacy, and safety of crovalimab, a novel C5 inhibitor with an every-4-weeks subcutaneous (SC) maintenance dosing regimen that is currently undergoing clinical investigation in patients with PNH. Here, we aim to provide a comprehensive overview for clinicians and clinical scientists, alongside practical considerations for using this drug.

Overview of the field

At present, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is the only curative treatment for PNH but is rarely performed or offered as initial therapy due to risks of high morbidity and mortality [1,3,36]. Patients receiving C5 inhibitors, despite being the standard of care for PNH treatment, have several unmet medical and socioeconomic needs. Approximately 34–51% of patients with PNH who are treated with eculizumab continue to require regular transfusions due to underlying extravascular hemolysis, bone marrow failure, or breakthrough hemolysis [3,23,37,38]. A small subset of patients with PNH carries C5 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP; e.g., c.2654G→A [Arg885His]) that can prevent the binding of eculizumab or ravulizumab to C5 [39,40]. Further, the requirement for life-long intravenous (IV) infusions (e.g., every 2 weeks with eculizumab or every 8 weeks with ravulizumab) has a negative impact on the quality of life of patients and their caregivers [6,18,21,23,41]. Although ravulizumab is also approved with a weekly SC infusion dosing in a few countries, plans to deliver SC administration for adult patients with PNH have been discontinued [21,41–43]. In the UK, Germany, and France, there are substantial mean annual total costs to patients receiving C5 inhibition therapy (€3557.70), their caregivers (€1740.40), and the healthcare system (€350,122.70) [44]. Hence, alternative treatment options and appropriate socioeconomic support are still needed for patients with PNH receiving C5 inhibitor treatment.

The C3 inhibitor, pegcetacoplan, can control both intravascular and extravascular hemolysis [25,45]. Notably, C3-mediated extravascular hemolysis, a regular iatrogenic consequence of C5 inhibition, is clinically significant (defined as hemoglobin levels ≤9.5 g/dL and moderate or severe symptomatic fatigue, with or without a blood transfusion in the last 12 months) in 21% of patients treated with C5 inhibitors for ≥3 months [46]. Rapid increase in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels occurred in all phase III PEGASUS study participants who experienced breakthrough hemolysis, indicating the possible risk of severe intravascular hemolysis due to the accumulation of PNH red blood cells with this treatment [25]. In the open-label extension of the phase III PEGASUS study, 23% of patients treated with pegcetacoplan experienced acute hemolytic events, with 8% discontinuing treatment due to hemolytic events [45]. Overall, the long-term safety profile of pegcetacoplan (up to 3 years [47]) is less established compared with C5 inhibitors, which have accumulated 15 years of real-world experience [24,35,45,48,49]. Further, pegcetacoplan requires self-administration of relatively large-volume (20 mL) of subcutaneous infusions lasting 30–60 minutes twice weekly (every 3 days or 3 times a week in the event of breakthrough hemolysis) [49–51], putting a treatment burden on patients.

Factor B and D inhibitors have shown great promise in PNH treatment. Iptacopan, an oral proximal complement inhibitor of factor B, showed superior efficacy compared with eculizumab or ravulizumab and good tolerability in patients with PNH and residual anemia despite C5 inhibitor treatment in the phase III APPLY-PNH study [52], which were sustained in patients who received iptacopan for 48 weeks [53]. In the single-arm phase III APPOINT-PNH study, iptacopan resulted in a clinically meaningful increase in hemoglobin levels and had a favorable safety profile in patients who were naive to complement inhibitor treatment [52]. Phase II studies on oral factor D inhibitors, danicopan and vemircopan, provided proof of concept of these treatments in PNH [54,55]. Danicopan, as an add-on treatment to eculizumab or ravulizumab, significantly improved hemoglobin response versus placebo in patients with PNH and clinically significant extravascular hemolysis in the phase III ALPHA study, which was maintained in patients who received treatment for 48 weeks [56]. In November 2023, the clinical development of vemircopan in PNH was terminated due to the lack of efficacy [43]. Despite the potential of iptacopan and danicopan in PNH treatment, the long-term safety profile of factor B and D inhibitors are not as established as that of C5 inhibitors. Additionally, treatment adherence to these oral therapies may be challenging due to the twice- and thrice-daily dosing with factor B and D inhibitors, respectively [55,57,58]. Lack of compliance to these treatments, when used as a monotherapy, could also lead to an increased risk of severe breakthrough hemolysis caused by the large population of accumulated PNH erythrocytes [59]. The combination of cemdisiran (an N-acetylgalactosamine-conjugated small interfering RNA that suppresses C5 production by the liver) and pozelimab (a fully human monoclonal antibody that inhibits C5) has also shown promising safety and efficacy in patients with PNH switching from eculizumab in a phase II study [60]. Further investigation of this combination is anticipated.

Despite significant advances in the PNH treatment landscape, many patients worldwide still lack access to approved complement-inhibitor therapies [3,22]. These patients receive supportive treatments that do not modify the pathophysiology or the natural history of PNH, and long-term use can result in a wide range of adverse effects or complications [1,33,35]. Although retrospective analyses have shown that primary prophylaxis with anticoagulation can reduce the risk of thrombosis [61,62], the risk–benefit of this approach must be carefully examined considering the possibility of serious, and even fatal, hemorrhage [1,35,61]. In the absence of complement inhibitor treatment, patients with PNH have a median survival of 10–22 years, with a 5-year mortality of 33% with supportive treatment [24,63–65]. Thus, there remains a need for complement inhibitors with increased accessibility.

Introduction to crovalimab & regulatory affairs

Crovalimab is complement inhibitor, developed by Chugai Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., a member of the Roche Group, that is marketed under the trade name PiaSky®. PiaSky is currently approved for use in China for patients 12 years and older who have not been previously treated with complement inhibitors, in Europe for adult and pediatric patients 12 years of age or older with a weight of 40 kg and above, in Japan for patients with body weight of 40 kg and above, regardless of age or prior C5 inhibitor treatment, and in the US for patients 13 years and older with body weight of at least 40 kg [66–69]. As of September 2023, filing applications have been accepted in several countries and submissions to other regulatory authorities worldwide are ongoing [70].

Crovalimab is being investigated in patients with PNH in several clinical trials, as summarized in Tables 1 and 2. The first crovalimab clinical trial was the open-label adaptive phase I/II COMPOSER (NCT03157635) study in healthy volunteers (Part 1) as well as complement blockade-naive (Parts 2 and 4A) and C5 inhibitor-treated (Parts 3 and 4B) patients with PNH (Table 1) [40,71,72]. Promising results from this study led to the phase III COMMODORE program, which includes the COMMODORE 1 (NCT04432584), COMMODORE 2 (NCT04434092), and COMMODORE 3 (NCT04654468) studies (Table 2) [33,73–75]. Both COMMODORE 1 and COMMODORE 2 are randomized studies with an additional non-randomized arm [73,74]. COMMODORE 3 is a single-arm study of crovalimab conducted in China [33,75]. Results presented in this review paper are from the randomized arms unless otherwise specified. The primary treatment period was 24 weeks for all three studies and all patients continuing after Week 25 received crovalimab [33,73,74].

Pharmacology

Crovalimab, also known as RO7112689, SKY59, or PiaSky, is a novel humanized anti-C5 inhibitor developed using the Sequential Monoclonal Antibody Recycling Technology – Immunoglobulin (SMART-Ig) [71,76]. SMART-Ig combines isoelectric point, neonatal, Fc receptor, and pH-dependent affinity engineering to prolong antibody half-life in the plasma and reduce the required therapeutic drug dosage (Figure 1) [71,76–78], enabling low-dose and low-volume subcutaneous administration. Like eculizumab and ravulizumab, crovalimab can provide clinical benefits to patients with PNH by C5 inhibition [21,23]. Notably, crovalimab binds to the C5 β-chain, a different epitope from eculizumab and ravulizumab, and can block C5 activation in patients who carry the C5 SNP [71,76,79]. Both ravulizumab and crovalimab were designed with a prolonged half-life [71,76,78]. Crovalimab increases its half-life via the recycling technology that enables pH-dependent dissociation from C5 in acidic endosomes [71,76,78–80]. In addition to the recycling technology, crovalimab utilizes isoelectric point engineering to facilitate rapid receptor-independent uptake via endocytosis, thereby binding and eliminating more free C5 at lower doses [71,76–78].

Crovalimab has a weight-based tiered dosing regimen optimized based on results from COMPOSER and subsequently used in the COMMODORE 1, 2, and 3 studies (Table 3) [33,71–74]. In an analysis of healthy volunteers and treatment-naive patients from COMPOSER and COMMODORE 3, the pharmacokinetics of crovalimab was best described by a linear, two-compartment model with first-order elimination and absorption [81]. Clearances and volumes of distribution of crovalimab were allometrically scaled by body weight, whereby patients with lower weight may experience comparatively higher drug exposure than those with higher weight, supporting the effectiveness and safety of the weight-based tiered crovalimab dosing regimen for the treatment of adult and adolescent patients with PNH [81]. Patients in COMMODORE 2 who were complement inhibitor naive achieved complete terminal complement activity inhibition (as measured per liposome immunoassay), which was generally sustained during the primary treatment period [74]. Complete terminal complement activity inhibition was maintained in COMMODORE 1 patients who switched from eculizumab to crovalimab [73]. In both studies, free C5 levels were generally maintained in the crovalimab arm [73,74].

Clinical efficacy

Data from Parts 2 (n = 10) and 3 (n = 19) of COMPOSER showed that crovalimab resulted in sustained terminal complement pathway inhibition in treatment-naive and C5 inhibitor-treated patients with PNH, respectively [71]. The efficacy of crovalimab, in terms of intravascular hemolysis control, hemoglobin stabilization, and transfusion avoidance, was maintained over a 3-year median treatment duration in the open-label extension of COMPOSER, which included 43 of 44 patients from Parts 2, 3, and 4 [72]. Four of 44 patients who carry the C5 SNP (all of the Asian descent) had a lack of response to previous eculizumab therapy that was consistent with the typical poor hemolysis control experienced with eculizumab or ravulizumab in patients carrying the C5 SNP [21,39,40]. All four patients achieved sustained terminal complement inhibition and hemolysis control with crovalimab, per the efficacy data from all patients in COMPOSER [40,71].

The phase III COMMODORE program showed that crovalimab provides rapid improvement in intravascular hemolysis control and reduces transfusion needs in patients who are naive to C5 inhibitor therapy, and maintains disease control in those who switched from another C5 inhibitor (Table 4) [33,73–75]. COMMODORE 2 demonstrated that crovalimab is an effective treatment for patients with PNH, with non-inferior hemolysis control and transfusion avoidance compared with eculizumab in patients naive to C5 inhibitor treatment [74]. COMMODORE 3 showed consistent efficacy results for hemolysis control and transfusion avoidance endpoints in C5 inhibitor-naive patients [33,75]. In COMMODORE 1, patients who switched from eculizumab to crovalimab maintained disease control [73].

Per secondary efficacy endpoints, crovalimab was non-inferior to eculizumab for breakthrough hemolysis and hemoglobin stabilization in COMMODORE 2 (Table 4) [74]. COMMODORE 3 had consistent efficacy findings for breakthrough hemolysis and hemoglobin stabilization [33,75]. Patients in COMMODORE 1 who switched from eculizumab to crovalimab continued to maintain disease control with regards to breakthrough hemolysis and hemoglobin stabilization [73]. As seen with all complement inhibition therapies [21,25,38,59], the risk of breakthrough hemolysis remains with crovalimab.

Additionally, the COMMODORE program supports the treatment benefit of crovalimab with patient-reported fatigue (Table 4) and quality of life (QoL) data. Clinically meaningful improvement in mean FACIT-Fatigue score from baseline (improvement of ≥5 points [82]) was observed in both treatment arms at Week 25 in COMMODORE 2, with a numerically greater improvement in the crovalimab than eculizumab arm (adjusted mean change, 7.8 vs 5.2) [74]. In COMMODORE 3, rapid and clinically meaningful improvement in mean fatigue was observed by Week 2 and was maintained through Week 49 [33,75]. Patients who were C5-inhibitor experienced in COMMODORE 1 reported an adjusted mean change from baseline at Week 25 of 1.1 in the crovalimab arm and −2.6 in the eculizumab arm [73]. In an analysis of patient-reported QoL, C5 inhibitor-naive patients in COMMODORE 2 showed improvements in all EORTC Item Library (EORTC IL-40) symptoms and in the functioning and global health status (GHS)/QoL scales of the EORTC Quality of Life Questionnaire Core 30 (EORTC QLQ-C30) for both arms [83]. Numerically, a greater improvement from baseline was observed with crovalimab versus eculizumab for most scales [83]. These improvements in QoL with crovalimab were maintained during the crovalimab extension period [83]. C5 inhibitor-experienced patients randomized to the crovalimab or eculizumab arms in COMMODORE 1 maintained baseline levels of PNH symptoms, functioning, and GHS/QoL scores throughout the primary treatment period, as well as with crovalimab during the crovalimab extension period [83].

Safety and tolerability

Safety results of crovalimab from Parts 2 and 3 of COMPOSER were consistent with the known safety profile of C5 inhibition [71]. In patients receiving long-term crovalimab in the COMPOSER open-label extension, the median treatment duration from baseline was approximately 3 years and 42 of 44 patients (95%) experienced ≥1 adverse event (AE) [72]. Treatment-related AEs occurred in 14 patients (32%), with two patients (5%) who experienced treatment-related serious AEs [72]. There were no reports of meningococcal infections, deaths, or AEs leading to withdrawal of treatment [71,72]. AEs in patients with the C5 polymorphism were consistent with those in the overall COMPOSER population [40].

Pooled safety data from COMMODORE 1, COMMODORE 2, and COMMODORE 3 are summarized in Table 5 [73–75,84]. The median treatment duration was 44.4 weeks for patients in the crovalimab group and 22.1 weeks for those in the eculizumab group. In patients receiving crovalimab and eculizumab, there were 522 AEs per 100 patient years (PY) and 583 AEs per 100 PY, respectively [84]. There were 7.4 serious infections per 100 PY in patients receiving crovalimab (6.1 naive; 9.3 switched) and 14.1 in those receiving eculizumab, with no meningococcal infections reported [84]. The most frequently reported AEs were coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19; crovalimab: 15% naive, 17% switched; eculizumab: 10%), upper respiratory tract infection (crovalimab: 23% naive, 5% switched; eculizumab: 9%), and pyrexia (crovalimab: 11% naive, 11% switched; eculizumab: 7%); comparisons across treatment groups need to account for the differences in treatment exposure [84]. A higher proportion of C5 inhibitor-naive patients appeared to have experienced laboratory abnormality AEs (e.g., decreased neutrophil count and decreased white blood cell count) and upper respiratory tract infection compared with C5 inhibitor-switched patients or those receiving eculizumab [84]. This observation was due to local reporting practices in China. In addition, patients in the eculizumab arm were stabilized on treatment before enrollment and were less likely to experience AEs such as infusion-related reaction [73]. A medical review of these imbalances suggests that the observed differences are unlikely to be clinically significant. Overall, pooled safety results of the COMMODORE program showed that the safety profile of crovalimab was consistent with that of eculizumab in patients with PNH, and was comparable between patients who were C5 inhibitor naive and those who switched from a C5 inhibitor to crovalimab, except for the known risk of developing transient immune complex reactions in some patients switching between C5 inhibitors that bind to different epitopes [84]. These findings also add to existing evidence on the safety of C5 inhibitors.

Patients with hemolytic PNH who switch between C5 inhibitors are unable to undergo a washout period before switching due to risks of recurrent intravascular hemolysis and thromboembolic events [85]. As crovalimab binds to a different C5 epitope from eculizumab and ravulizumab, transient immune complexes comprising eculizumab/ravulizumab-C5-crovalimab motifs will form at the time of switching between C5 inhibitors (Figure 2) [85]. The formation of transient immune complexes may cause crovalimab concentrations in the serum to fall below the target level and can lead to transient immune complex reactions [85]. Currently, there are no known baseline biomarkers or demographic variables that can identify patients who are at a high risk of transient immune complex reactions.

Formation of transient immune complexes when switching between C5 inhibitors.

In Part 3 of COMPOSER, all 19 patients switching from eculizumab to crovalimab had transient immune complexes, with 2 patients who experienced mild or moderate vasculitis skin reactions [71]. Both events suggest transient immune complex reactions and resolved under continued crovalimab treatment [71,85]. During the open-label extension period of COMPOSER, there were no new occurrences of transient immune complex reactions in patients who switched from eculizumab to crovalimab (Parts 3 and 4B) [72]. One possible explanation for this observation is that the weight-based tiered dosing of crovalimab was optimized to reduce the formation and circulation time of large transient immune complexes [72]. Patients who entered the open-label extension period initially remained on their originally assigned dosing schedule and were subsequently required to receive the optimized dosing to ensure that all patients received a comparable drug exposure across the continuum of body weights (Table 3) [72]. One patient in the treatment-naive group discontinued from the study due to a lack of efficacy and subsequently experienced a transient immune complex reaction when switching from crovalimab to eculizumab [72].

Transient immune complex reactions occurred in 7 of 44 patients (16%) in the randomized crovalimab arm of COMMODORE 1 [73]. No grade 4 or 5 transient immune complex reactions were reported and all events resolved with no modifications to crovalimab treatment. The most common signs and symptoms of transient immune complex reactions reported were abnormalities in the skin and joints (urticarial rashes, arthralgia, and vasculitis), with no renal involvement [73]. In the non-randomized arm of COMMODORE 1, one patient experienced transient immune complex reactions when switching from crovalimab to ravulizumab [73].

A pooled analysis of patients in COMMODORE 1 and COMMODORE 2 who switched from eculizumab or ravulizumab to crovalimab (n = 185; includes patients who were randomized to the eculizumab arm of each study and switched to crovalimab after the primary treatment period and patients in the non-randomized arm of COMMODORE 1) showed that transient immune complex reactions occurred in 18% of patients, with a median time to onset of 1.6 weeks (range, 0.7–4.4) and median time to resolution of 1.9 weeks (range, 0.4–34.1) [84]. Most transient immune complex reactions were grade 1–2, with grade 3 events reported in 7% of patients [84]. The most commonly reported manifestations of transient immune complex reactions were arthralgia (9%) and rash (6%), with no renal manifestations [84]. Overall, the formation of transient immune complexes and resultant transient immune complex reactions from switching between C5 inhibitors did not result in unexpected safety events or clinically relevant loss of efficacy.

In general, transient immune complex reactions that may occur when switching between C5 inhibitors typically involve the skin, are mild to moderate in severity, and are self-limiting [85]. Mild-to-moderate dermal reactions can be managed with topical betamethasone and oral fexofenadine [85], whereas treatment of severe transient immune complex reactions involves systemic corticosteroids.

With the availability of new treatment options in recent years, practical considerations should be noted when deciding to use crovalimab. Before switching patients between C5 inhibitors, clinicians should consider the patient’s preference, confirm the patient’s pregnancy status, and evaluate the quality of treatment response with ongoing C5 inhibition.

There is currently no data on switching from C3 inhibitors to C5 inhibitors. Transient immune complex reactions will not form in such a situation in view of the target being different. However, it was observed in the PEGASUS study that patients who switched from eculizumab to pegcetacoplan had significantly higher hemoglobin levels than those who continued receiving eculizumab [25]. Hence, there may be a risk of a clinically significant drop in hemoglobin levels when switching from C3 inhibitors to crovalimab. Further investigation of the switch from C3 inhibitors to C5 inhibitors is warranted.

Self-administration

In the COMMODORE program, patients or caregivers were allowed to self-administer the SC maintenance dose of crovalimab starting at Week 9, after in-clinic training and confirmation of proficiency by a healthcare professional [33,73,74]. Between study Weeks 9 and 25 in COMMODORE 2, 17.1%–31.0% of the SC crovalimab administrations were performed by the patient or caregiver [74]. There was an increase in administrations by the patient or caregiver after Week 25, with an overall increase in off-site administrations [74]. No medication errors specifically due to self-administration were reported [74]. In COMMODORE 3, the percentage of SC drug administrations performed by patients or their caregivers increased from 3.9% at Week 9 to 38.0% at Week 25 [33]. Self-administration increased substantially after the primary treatment period and was >90% at Weeks 29, 37, and 45, when study visits were not required during crovalimab administration [33]. Administrations by patients or caregivers mainly occurred off-site and did not result in medication errors leading to dose modification and injection site reactions [33]. In COMMODORE 1, 11.6%–15.8% of SC drug administrations between Weeks 9 and 25 were performed by the patient or their caregiver [73]. Administrations by the patient or their caregiver tended to increase over time after Week 25 [73]. At clinical cutoff, there were no medication errors specifically due to self-administration [73]. Overall, these results demonstrate the feasibility of crovalimab SC self-administration. The approval of PiaSky self-administration currently varies across regulatory agencies [66,68,69].

Patient preference

Overall, patients in COMMODORE 1 and COMMODORE 2 preferred treatment with crovalimab to other C5 inhibition therapies [73,74]. In exploratory analyses, crovalimab was preferred over eculizumab by 84–96% of randomized patients who switched from eculizumab to crovalimab in COMMODORE 1 and COMMODORE 2, and who completed a patient preference questionnaire after 17 weeks of treatment with crovalimab [73,74]. The top reasons selected for crovalimab preference were “The way treatment was given was easier,” “There were fewer hospital visits associated with treatment,” “Time to administer treatment was shorter,” and “Quality of life was better” [73,74]. In the non-randomized arm of COMMODORE 1, crovalimab was preferred by 8 (57%) of 14 eculizumab-experienced patients assessed (including patients with the C5 polymorphism and those previously on high-dose eculizumab) and 9 (60%) of 15 ravulizumab-experienced patients evaluated, with similar reasons for crovalimab preference provided [86]. Although Treatment Satisfaction and Medication Questionnaire-9 (TSQM-9) results showed that randomized patients in both studies had comparable levels of treatment satisfaction with crovalimab and eculizumab, and perceived both drugs as having similar efficacy, crovalimab was rated as being more convenient [86]. These findings suggest that crovalimab has the potential to offer a less burdensome treatment option than existing therapies for this disease that requires lifelong treatment.

Real-world evidence

Real-world data on crovalimab is currently unavailable since it was only recently approved for the treatment of PNH this year [66–69]. Studies are ongoing to better understand the real-world PNH treatment landscape [44,87]. The COMMODORE Burden of Illness study aims to quantify the real-life socioeconomic burden of PNH on patients, focusing on those receiving treatment with IV C5 inhibitors, eculizumab and ravulizumab [44]. Preliminary results from the UK, Germany, and France suggested that patients with PNH who were receiving treatment with IV C5 inhibitors (73% received eculizumab and 24% received ravulizumab) continued to experience considerable disease burden that translated into substantial costs and reduced QoL [44]. These costs are borne by the healthcare system, patients, or caregivers, which include fees for treatment, hospitalization, and travel, as well as the loss of income from difficulties working. The COMMODORE Cohort study is currently collecting prospective real-world data on the use, safety, and effectiveness of eculizumab and ravulizumab in patients with PNH in the USA [87]. Disease burden and outcomes are also collected [87]. Of note, a cost-effective analysis of PNH treatment in the US showed that pegcetacoplan was associated with lifetime cost savings of $1,176,808 and $213,062 compared with eculizumab and ravulizumab, respectively, as well as additional quality-adjusted life years of 0.25 and 0.24 [88]. Data from these studies will provide a benchmark for the real-world impact of crovalimab in the treatment of PNH.

Conclusions

In conclusion, C5 inhibitors remain the standard of care for treating PNH, in countries where they are available, but have several unmet medical and socioeconomic needs. Crovalimab is a novel anti-C5 inhibitor that is approved for the treatment of PNH. Crovalimab has an every-4-weeks, low-volume, SC maintenance dosing regimen, with the possibility for self-administration. The COMPOSER study showed promising results in its primary analysis, including in patients carrying the C5 SNP and its open-label extension with a median treatment duration of approximately 3 years. In C5 inhibitor-naive patients in COMMODORE 2, crovalimab was non-inferior to eculizumab in both co-primary efficacy endpoints of hemolysis control and transfusion avoidance, as well as secondary endpoints of breakthrough hemolysis and hemoglobin stabilization, and had a comparable safety profile. Efficacy and safety data from C5 inhibitor-naive patients in the COMMODORE 3 study supported the findings in COMMODORE 2. COMMODORE 1 showed that the safety profile of crovalimab in C5 inhibitor-experienced patients with PNH was consistent with that in those who were C5 inhibitor naive, except for self-limiting transient immune complex reactions that were unique to patients switching between C5 inhibitors. Further, patients who switched between C5 inhibitors maintained disease control. In summary, existing data highlight the overall favorable benefit-risk profile of crovalimab. These results further confirm that crovalimab is efficacious and well tolerated in patients with PNH and has promising potential in addressing several unmet medical and socioeconomic challenges in the PNH treatment landscape.
