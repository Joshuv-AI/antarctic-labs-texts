CYP27A1 suppresses brain metastasis via ferroptosis in lung adenocarcinoma: a six-gene signature predicting the immunotherapy response and clinical outcomes
Authors: Lu Xu, Liyou Huang, Zihao Liu, Xiaojie Jiang, Linchun Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) brain metastasis has limited therapeutic options and a poor prognosis. This study aimed to identify molecular drivers, construct a prognostic model, and assess immunotherapy response.Methods:We integrated scRNA-seq data from 4 LUAD patients (GSE117570) and bulk RNA-seq data from the TCGA-LUAD cohort. Brain metastasis-associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified via Seurat. CellChat was used to analyse intercellular communication. A LASSO Cox prognostic model was constructed, and the function of CYP27A1 was validated in vitro.Results: Six brain metastasis-linked genes (CD9, CYP27A1, HLA-DQB1, PEBP1, PECAM1, and TUBB) formed a risk model. High-risk patients had worse overall survival (HR = 1.91, p < 0.0001) and a reduced immunotherapy response. scRNA-seq revealed that M1 macrophages are involved in metastasis, with CYP27A1 significantly downregulated in LUAD cells. Functionally, CYP27A1 overexpression inhibited proliferation, migration, and invasion by inducing ferroptosis via iron homeostasis and lipid peroxidation (p < 0.05). High-risk patients presented lower immune scores/stromal components. A nomogram integrating the risk score, stage, and EGFR status showed robust 1–3 year predictive accuracy (AUC: 0.749–0.780).Conclusions:CYP27A1 is implicated as a suppressor of LUAD brain metastasis via ferroptosis. The six-gene model facilitates risk stratification, and macrophage-driven microenvironment remodelling informs potential immunotherapy strategies, advancing LUAD precision oncology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04112-2.

Introduction

Lung cancer is a common and deadly disease worldwide, and brain metastasis is a leading cause of death. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) has a relatively high risk of brain metastasis, occurring in 14.4% of cases [1]. With average survival times of 6 months and 3.8 months, patients with LUAD brain metastasis, especially those over 65 years, have a poor prognosis [2, 3]. Molecular analysis has been emphasized in predicting patient prognosis and customizing personalized treatment plans. The identification of tumor gene mutations can aid in the development of targeted therapies, which have shown promising results in prolonging the survival of patients with LUAD brain metastases [4]. Early detection, prompt action, and tailored therapy are essential to improve outcomes in this disease.

Advances in single-cell technology have made it a significant asset in cancer studies, with scRNA-seq enabling high-resolution analysis of cellular subtypes and revealing the complex ecosystem of lung adenocarcinoma metastases [5]. Bulk RNA-seq enables the investigation of overall tumor gene expression, revealing variations between molecular and tumor subtypes [6]. Clinical research on LUAD brain metastasis is relatively limited. Some studies have used scRNA-seq data to extract the molecular features of immune cells to predict the prognosis and response to immunotherapy in cancer patients [7]. Single-gene RNA sequencing analysis is used to identify key genes and potential biomarkers that may improve patient prognosis and treatment [4]. One study identified LUAD patients at high risk of brain metastasis through targeted tumor DNA sequencing, RNA sequencing technology, etc [8]. In addition, miRNAs and proteomics have been used to explore the pathogenesis of LUAD brain metastasis [9]. However, the key genes and mechanisms driving this process remain elusive, as highlighted by a recent single-cell atlas of human brain metastases which revealed a distinct microenvironment but poorly understood tumor-immune crosstalk [10].

Immunotherapy is now widely used to treat lung cancer, but the effectiveness of this treatment is a major concern.The heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment (TME), particularly the diversity and functional states of immune cells, has been increasingly recognized as a critical determinant of immunotherapy response [2]. Biomarkers such as tumor mutation burden (TMB) and PD-L1 expression are widely used [11], but they do not fully reflect the heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment [12], which limits their benefit to a limited number of patients [13]. Therefore, exploring new biomarkers and predictive models to help select appropriate treatments for patients with lung cancer brain metastasis is highly important.

In this study, scRNA-seq data from LUAD brain metastases were analysed to understand the tumor heterogeneity of patients, and 45 single-cell-specific brain metastasis-related genes were identified. Cell subpopulation and enrichment analyses were performed, and a prognostic model for LUAD was constructed using six key prognostic genes.

Materials and methods

Single-cell data download

Single-cell sequencing data for LUAD patients were obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) [14], specifically the dataset GSE117570 [15]. The dataset was sequenced via 10x Genomics technology on the Illumina NextSeq 500 platform, with the species being Homo sapiens. The study included a total of 4887 cells. The cells were taken from four patients with NSCLC (non-small cell lung cancer).

Download of genes related to brain metastasis

The GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) [16] was queried for genes associated with brain metastases via the term “brainmetastases”, and genes with relevance scores above 10 were selected, yielding 784 genes. For more details, see Supplementary Table 1.

Bulk RNA-seq data download

Using the TCGAbiolinks package [17], we downloaded TCGA LUAD bulk RNA-seq data for coanalysis with single-cell sequencing. In total, 510 LUAD samples and 58 control samples were downloaded from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), with 58 patients having both tumor tissue and adjacent tissue. The downloaded data also included clinicopathological survival information for 497 LUAD samples, including age, survival status, follow-up time, and stage (Table 1). The prognostic modelutilized a training set comprising a total of 497 patients with comprehensive overall survival (OS) data. TCGAbiolinks facilitated the download and preprocessing of the data.

Clinicopathological characteristics of LUAD patients stratified by risk score

To validate LUAD, we employed the GSE37745 [18] dataset downloaded from GEO. The GPL570 platform ([HG-U133_Plus_2] Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array) was utilized to sequence a dataset consisting of 226 patients with NSCLC from Homo sapiens. The analysis included all samples but lacked information about adjacent noncancerous tissue. The survival information for these patients was downloaded to verify the accuracy of the prognostic model.

To further validate LUAD, we obtained the GSE50081 [17] dataset comprising 181 Homo sapiens patients with NSCLC. These patients were sequenced on the GPL570 platform ([HG-U133_Plus_2] Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array). The analysis incorporated all the samples, excluding the information of adjacent noncancerous tissue. Survival information from these patients was downloaded to validate the prognostic model.

Single-cell data processing

Raw UMI count data are used for single-cell analysis, and the R package Seurat(Version 5.3.0) [19] is commonly utilized for processing. Here, we implemented the standard processing pipeline of this R package. First, genes with no detectable expression in any cell were excluded from the initial dataset. Subsequently, stringent quality control filters were applied to the single-cell RNA sequencing data to remove low-quality cells and empty droplets. Only cell barcodes meeting all of the following criteria were retained for downstream analysis: total UMI counts between 200 and 5,000, detection of more than 200 unique genes, a library complexity (log₁₀[genes per UMI]) greater than 0.9, and a mitochondrial gene proportion below 20%. To increase precision and expedite downstream analysis, gene feature selection was executed through the utilization of the FindVariableFeatures function. This particular function employs the mean.var.plot method to choose genes that display significant variability. Next, to derive features from the highly variable genes, the technique of principal component analysis (PCA) [20] was implemented. The JackStraw and Elbow approaches were then employed to ascertain the optimal number of principal components needed. The cells underwent an unsupervised clustering process through the application of the Louvain algorithm method, executed via the FindClusters function. The results of the clustering analysis were then visually presented via t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) [21]. The FindAllMarkers algorithm was employed to identify specific genes associated with each cell cluster. A corrected p value < 0.05 and an absolute log2FC >2 were used as significance thresholds. Marker genes for LUAD tissue cell types were obtained from published literature [22], and CellMarker [23] was used to define cell clusters.

Enrichment analysis

The examination of genes’ functional traits across various levels and dimensions, such as biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC) [24], is commonly conducted via Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis. Similarly, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) is a well-known database that offers insights into genomes, biological pathways, diseases, and drugs [25]. To perform GO functional annotation, the researchers in this study used the R package clusterProfiler(Version 4.16.0) [26, 27]. In addition, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the significantly differentially expressed genes in the single-cell data from the GSE117570 dataset. The objective was to detect highly enriched biological processes with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Furthermore, the researchers utilized the R package DOSE(Version 3.28.1) [28] to execute Disease Ontology Semantic and Enrichment analysis on the same dataset, with the aim of identifying considerably enriched biological processes that share Disease Ontology (DO) semantic similarity.

Brain metastasis-related subpopulation analysis

The brain metastasis-associated genes and the DEGs between single-cell subpopulations were intersected to obtain single-cell differential brain metastasis-associated genes. To identify the activity of these single-cell differential brain metastasis-associated genes in each cell, the AUCell package [29] was used. For downstream analysis, the brain metastasis-associated cell subpopulation of LUAD was chosen on the basis of its highest activity score.

Cell communication analysis

Cell communication analysis could help us understand the interactions between cells and decipher the communication network between cells [30]. It reveals the interactions among various types of cells in the development process, explores the immune microenvironment of tumors, and mines potential therapeutic targets for diseases. To analyse the interactions among different cell clusters within brain metastasis-related cell subpopulations, the R software package CellChat(Version 2.1.0) [31] was used. The aggregateNet function in CellChat was used to calculate the communication network between subpopulations, and the netVisual_circle function was used to visualize the strength and quantity of interactions between subpopulations.

Detection of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) linked to brain metastasis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in tumours and adjacent tissues from the TCGA dataset were analysed via the limma package [32] to identify genes associated with brain metastasis in LUAD. Significant DEGs were identified on the basis of |logFC| > log2(1.5) and adj. P value < 0.05 thresholds. To identify significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between brain metastasis-associated cell subgroups and other cell groups, differential expression analysis was performed at the single-cell level. By intersecting the significant DEGs from single-cell RNA-seq, bulk RNA-seq, and brain metastasis-related genes, we obtained a list of DEGs associated with brain metastasis in LUAD.

Constructing a prognostic risk model for LUAD

The TCGA dataset was utilized to create a scoring system for brain metastasis-related genes in LUAD patients. First, we screened candidate genes from DEGs associated with brain metastasis via univariate Cox regression (UniCox). The most important candidate genes were then selected via univariate Cox regression via the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression method, which was implemented via the R package glmnet(Version 4.1–10) with specific parameters configured for optimal selection. Only the selected trait genes with nonzero coefficients were used to construct the prognostic model, and a screening process was subsequently performed to determine the optimal prognostic model. In conclusion, we developed a formula for a differential risk score for brain metastasis using the normalized and weighted gene expression values of feature genes’ penalty coefficients. Construction of the nomogram model

The goal was to create an individualized forecast assessment for individuals with brain metastasis utilizing a scoring system that relies on clinical and pathological characteristics. To evaluate the predictive performance of the scoring method for overall survival, we conducted both univariate and multivariate Cox analyses on the TCGA dataset. The chosen scoring method was utilized to formulate a clinical prediction chart (nomogram) through implementation of the R package rms(Version 8.0–0) [33]. A calibration curve was constructed to compare the predicted values with the actual survival rates to assess the effectiveness of the nomogram. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the impact of the model on patient prognosis in terms of decision-making ability via the ggDCA R package(Version 1.1) [34].

Immune cell enrichment score

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is formed by a complex network of interactions. Objective evaluation of immune cell infiltration in patients is critical for disease research and treatment prognosis. Using the CIBERSORTX algorithm, the researchers analysed the presence of 22 immune cells in each group. The immune cell composition in these groups was illustrated via box plots, and the Wilcoxon test was used to compare immune cell proportions. Technical term abbreviations were explained upon first use. Additionally, this study conducted Spearman correlation analysis to examine the relationships between feature genes and immune cells. To quantify immune activity in tumor samples by means of gene expression data, the ESTIMATE algorithm was utilized. To determine the variability in immune scores between patients with high and low LUAD scores in the TCGA-LUAD dataset and to investigate the correlation between the gene expression levels of feature genes and immune scores, the R estimate package (Version1.0.13) [35] was used.

Prediction of treatment response

In accordance with previous reports, the correlations between differential brain metastasis risk scores and potential immune treatment markers, including immune checkpoint blockade (ICB)-related genes [36–38], IFN-γ pathway markers [39], m6A regulatory factors [39], and m5C regulatory factors [40, 41], were explored via the Wilcoxon test in the TCGA-LUAD dataset.

Cell culture

Human lung cancer cell lines (A549 and H1299) and a normal bronchial epithelial cell line (BEAS-2B) were purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Technology Co., Ltd. (CL-0016 and CL-0165) and Wuhan Ehnzyme Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (SNL-203), respectively. The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, 11875085) or DMEM (Gibco, 11965092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, 10099141 C) at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. For subculture, cells were seeded at 3 × 10⁵ cells/well in 25T flasks (Corning, 430168) and passaged every 48 h using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, 25200056) upon reaching 90% confluency. The key reagents used included cell freezing medium (Beyotime, C0210), PBS (Gibco, 10010023), and sterile pipette tips (Nanjing Yifeixue Biotechnology Co., Ltd.).

Construction of CYP27A1 Overexpression/Knockdown cell lines

The full-length cDNA of CYP27A1 was subsequently cloned and inserted into the PCDH-CMV-GFP-Puro vector (NovoPro, V006738) via homologous recombination. For knockdown, shRNA sequences targeting CYP27A1 were designed as follows: shRNA#1 forward, GCTTTCAATGAGGTGATTGAT, reverse, ATCAATCACCTCATTGAAAGC; shRNA#2 forward, GCACCAGTTACAGGTGCTTTA, reverse, TAAAGCACCTGTAACTGGTGC. These sequences were annealed and ligated into the pLKO.1-Puro vector (Wuhan Miaolin Biotechnology, DB00024) with T4 DNA ligase. Lentiviral particles were generated in 293T cells (Wuhan Procell, CL-0005) by cotransfecting the plasmids pMDG (NovoPro, V010404), pCAG-Δ8.9 (Beyotime, D8216), and the target vector (1:3:9 ratio) via Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher, L3000015). Viral supernatants were collected after 48 h and used to infect A549/H1299 cells. Stable lines were selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin (Beyotime, ST551) for 72 h. Key reagents included BamHI/EcoRI restriction enzymes (Thermo Fisher), a DNA gel extraction kit (TIANGEN, DP210), and an endotoxin-free plasmid extraction kit (TIANGEN, DP118).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR)

Total RNA was extracted via TRIzol (Beyotime, R0016), followed by chloroform (Ningbo Juhua Chemical Co., Ltd.) phase separation and isopropanol (Ningbo Juhua) precipitation. cDNA was synthesized via a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, R312-01). qPCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (YESEA, 11201ES03) on a QuantStudio 5 system (Applied Biosystems). The sequences of primers used were as follows: GAPDH forward, GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT, reverse, GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG; CYP27A1 forward, CGGCAACGGAGCTTAGAGG, reverse, GGCATAGCCTTGAACGAACAG; SLC7A11 forward, TCTCCAAAGGAGGTTACCTGC, reverse, AGACTCCCCTCAGTAAAGTGAC; and GPX4 forward, GAGGCAAGACCGAAGTAAACTAC, reverse, CCGAACTGGTTACACGGGAA. The reactions were performed in 96-well plates (Beyotime, FTUB337).

Western blotting

The cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) containing PMSF (Beyotime, ST507). Protein concentrations were determined via a BCA kit (YESEN, 20200ES76). Proteins (30 µg/lane) were separated on SDS‒PAGE gels (Epizyme, PG113) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Beyotime, FFP39) via Omni-Flash transfer buffer (Epizyme, PS201). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk (Beyotime, P0216) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: CYP27A1 (Affinity, DF3571; 1:1000), SLC7A11 (Affinity, DF12509; 1:1000), GPX4 (Affinity, DF6701; 1:1000), and GAPDH (Affinity, AF7021; 1:5000). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Affinity, S0001/S0002; 1:5000) were used for detection, and signals were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Beyotime, P0018S).

Cell proliferation assays

For the MTT assays, the cells (3 × 10⁴/well) were treated with 20 µL of MTT reagent (Beyotime, C0009S) for 4 h, followed by formazan dissolution in DMSO. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm. For the CCK-8 assays, the cells were treated with erastin (20 µM, Rechem, 571203-78-6) or ferrostatin-1 (0.4 µM, MCE, HY-100579) and incubated with CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, C0038) for 1 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Migration and invasion assays

For the scratch assays, confluent monolayers in 6-well plates (Beyotime, FCP060) were scratched with a pipette tip. Migration was quantified at 0, 12, 24, and 48 h via ImageJ. For Transwell invasion assays, cells (2.5 × 10⁵/mL) were seeded in Matrigel-coated chambers (Corning, 354248). Invaded cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime, C0121), and counted under a microscope (Oscience Microsystems).

ROS, lipid peroxidation, and Fe²⁺ detection

ROS levels were measured via DCFH-DA (Beyotime, S0033M; 10 µM). Lipid peroxidation was assessed with BODIPY 581/591 C11 (Beyotime, S0043M). The Fe²⁺ levels were quantified via colorimetric analysis at 550 nm via an Iron Assay Kit (Applygen, E1046).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The cells were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Biosharp, BL911A), dehydrated in ethanol (30–100%), and embedded in epoxy resin (EMbed 812, Haidechuangye Biotechnology, 14120). Ultrathin Sects. (50–100 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate (Qingdao Haibo Biotechnology) and imaged via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Oscience Microsystems).

Statistical analysis

R software (https://www.r-projec t.org/, version 4.1.2) was used for all data calculations and statistical analyses in this study. For comparisons between two groups of continuous variables, differences between nonnormally distributed variables were analysed via the Mann‒Whitney U test (i.e., Wilcoxon rank sum test). Correlation analyses were performed via Spearman correlation analysis via the cor function of the R software package unless otherwise stated. Cox regression analysis was primarily performed with the survival package, the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated with the survivalROC package, forest plots were generated with the forestplot package, and rms plots were generated with the rms package. All statistical p values were two-tailed, with differential gene screening for single-cell and bulk RNA-seq being considered statistically significant at a p value < 0.05 and p value criterion values for the remaining statistical tests being based on the narrative of the text.

Results

Single-cell subpopulation identification

Our overall analysis process is shown in Fig. 1. First, 4 LUAD tissues from the GSE117570 dataset contained a total of 4887 cells. All the cells were processed via the R package Seurat. First, 788 highly variable genes were selected (Fig. 2A), and 35 principal component features were extracted (Fig. 2B). Using these results, the 4887 cells were clustered, with a resolution set at 0.5 as the clustering standard. Unsupervised clustering algorithms were used to divide the cells into 19 subpopulations (Fig. 2C). The annotation of cells was carried out using cell type markers (Fig. 2D), and the unsupervised clustering of cell clusters highly overlapped with the given cell type. The t-SNE method revealed clear boundaries for each cell cluster, indicating distinct biological features and significant heterogeneity.

Identification of specific brain metastasis-related genes

To determine which cell cluster among the 19 clusters had the highest correlation with brain metastasis, we performed differential analysis of the 19 cell subtypes and obtained the intersection of genes that showed expression differences (adj. P value < 0.05, | log2FC |>1) between cell subtypes and genes associated with brain metastasis, resulting in 45 single-cell-specific brain metastasis-related genes (Fig. 3A).

Enrichment analysis of the 45 single-cell-specific brain metastasis-related genes revealed their biological functions in LUAD development. KEGG analysis revealed their involvement in processes such as malaria, rheumatoid arthritis, viral protein interaction with cytokines and cytokine receptors, influenza A, and the IL-17 signalling pathway (Fig. 3B). GO analysis revealed their influence on diseases such as intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, infection, meningioma (benign, no ICD-O subtype), adult meningioma, and tubulointerstitial fibrosis (Fig. 3C). These genes affected processes such as cell adhesion, leukocyte activation, gliogenesis, leukocyte migration, and response regulation (Fig. 3D); cellular components such as the granule membrane, alpha granule, extracellular matrix, granule lumen, and vesicle lumen (Fig. 3E); and molecular functions such as cytokine and receptor activities (Fig. 3F). These findings highlight the significant impact of these 45 genes on LUAD development and brain metastasis.

The upregulated and downregulated states of the 45 single-cell-specific brain metastasis-related genes were found to vary among different cell subtypes and types. Among various cell types, macrophages presented significant upregulation of HLA-DQB1, endothelial cells presented significant upregulation of MYC and TCF4, and stromal cells presented significant downregulation of MALAT1 (Fig. 3G). In different cell clusters, TSPAN1 was significantly upregulated in cell subtype 6, CXCR4 was upregulated in cell subtypes 2 and 10, and HLA-DRB1 was upregulated in cell subtype 5 (p < 0.05, Fig. 3H). The Go semantic similarity of the 45 genes [42] was calculated via the “GOSemSim” R package(Version 2.34.0), which revealed that GRN, CLU, FN1, and TSPAN1 were highly related to the functions of multiple genes (Fig. 3I).

Brain Metastasis-Related subpopulations of cells

The activity of 45 single-cell genes associated with brain metastases was calculated for each cell type via AUCell software. Initially, the cells were stained according to their subpopulation. Figure 4A shows that the activity of these genes was noticeably different among different cell subpopulations, with subpopulations 3, 4, 5, 7, and 8 having lighter staining and subpopulations 2, 6, and 17 having darker staining. Furthermore, 45 genes were assessed for activity in each of the cell types. With macrophages and stromal cells showing lighter staining and T cells and B cells showing darker staining, gene activity differed significantly between cell types (Fig. 4B). The ridge plot indicated that subpopulation 5 presented the highest gene expression, whereas subpopulation 16 presented the lowest expression (Fig. 4C). The ridge plot also indicated that these genes had the highest expression in macrophages (Fig. 4D). Consequently, we suggest that macrophages may be brain-metastasis-related subpopulations of cells with respect to these genes.

Communication between macrophages and other cell types

We used the CellChat package to analyse cell-to-cell communication to understand the interaction between brain metastasis-associated macrophages and other cell types. We found that macrophages interact strongly with stromal cells, epithelial cells and endothelial cells and that epithelial cells interact strongly with stromal cells and macrophages (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition, we observed strong macrophage-endothelial cell and macrophage-epithelial cell regulatory relationships. (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Fig. 1). These findings suggest that the regulation of macrophages affects the formation and development of tumor cells through their interactions with stromal, epithelial, and endothelial cells. The regulation between each cell type and the other five cell types was also examined (Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition, analysis of receptor and ligand pairs between macrophages and other cell types revealed that macrophages primarily receive regulatory signals from stromal, endothelial and epithelial cells (Fig. 5C) but are also able to issue regulatory signals to regulate all other cell types, particularly stromal, endothelial, and epithelial cells (Fig. 5D).

The formation and progression of epithelial cells are significantly influenced by the crucial role of macrophages. We further subdivided the macrophage subgroups to examine the expression of brain metastasis-related genes in macrophages. Initially, we obtained 1,403 macrophages and classified them into five subgroups via t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). Each cell cluster had significantly different biological characteristics, indicating a high degree of heterogeneity (Fig. 5E). To reveal the biological differences between the different cell clusters, we annotated the cell clusters with specific cell type marker genes. The findings indicated that the macrophages consisted of M0, M1, and M2 macrophages. (Fig. 2F). We identified specific genes for each macrophage cluster and observed significantly high expression of FABP4 and RBP4 in cell cluster 11 (p < 0.05, Fig. 5G). In addition, in each macrophage cluster, the gene expression levels of 45 brain metastasis-related genes specific to individual cells were examined. The results revealed that MMP9 and S100B were highly expressed in cell cluster 3, whereas APOE, TREM2, and SPP1 were differentially expressed in cell cluster 8 compared with the other clusters. Moreover, the gene expression patterns of CXCR4, HIF1A, NFKB1, SLC2A3, PPARG, TYMP, NR4A2, CYP27A1, BSG, PMP22, and MME were significantly different in cell cluster 11 compared with the other clusters. Cell cluster 11 corresponded to M1 macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 2). On the basis of our findings, we deduced that M1 macrophages play a crucial role in the development of brain metastasis in LUAD. To investigate the differences in and prognostic significance of M1, M0 and M2 macrophages in LUAD, we performed differential analysis of single-cell populations of these macrophage subtypes via the limma package. With respect to the customary evaluation standards, we used |logFC| > log2(1.5) and adj. A P value < 0.05 was used to select 748 DEGs (Fig. 5H).

Developing a predictive model for LUAD

We performed a differential gene expression profiling study on the TCGA dataset and compared tumor tissues with adjacent nontumour tissues via the limma package. Our results identified 7750 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that met the criteria of |logFC| > log2(1.5) and adj. P value < 0.05. Among these DEGs, 3725 were upregulated, whereas 4025 were downregulated (Fig. 6A). We found that these DEGs effectively distinguished tumor tissue from adjacent nontumour tissue (Fig. 6B).

Nomogram model

To confirm the prognostic relevance of the risk score prediction model in LUAD patients, we performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. Both analyses demonstrated that risk stratification (HR > 1, p < 0,05), tumor stage (HR > 1, p < 0,05) and EGFR status (HR > 1, p < 0,05) were independent prognostic factors for overall survival in LUAD patients, with risk stratification having the greatest impact on survival (Fig. 8A-B; Table 5). To enhance outcome prediction for LUAD patients, we developed a nomogram (Fig. 8C) that combines the risk score with typical clinicopathological indicators. The nomogram model’s stability was confirmed by consistent predictions with actual patient prognosis at 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals (Fig. 8D). The ability of the nomogram model to predict patient survival was demonstrated by a time‒ROC curve, with a 1-year AUC value of 0.749, a 3-year AUC value of 0.780, and a 5-year AUC value of 0.701 (Fig. 8E). Additionally, decision curve analysis (DCA) revealed that the combined effect of risk score + EGFR + tumor stage had a greater impact on five-year survival than did tumor stage alone. In addition, the risk score was more predictive of five-year survival than was EGFR alone. The nomogram model (risk score + EGFR + tumor stage) provided the most valuable prognosis for LUAD patients (Fig. 8F). Overall, our study demonstrated a strong association between the predicted and observed values, indicating that the nomogram accurately predicted the prognosis of LUAD patients. This model, which incorporates the risk score and common clinicopathological indicators, can provide clinicians with precise prognostic information for individualized treatment and improved patient outcomes.

Immune infiltration analysis

Our study aimed to compare immune cell composition in patients with high and low risk scores. Twenty-two immune cell categories in normal and LUAD samples from the TCGA-LUAD dataset were assessed via the CIBERSORT algorithm. The results revealed lower immune cell numbers in LUAD patients than in normal controls (p < 0.05, Fig. 9A). The immune cell content was significantly lower in the high-risk LUAD samples than in the low-risk samples (p < 0.05, Fig. 9B).

Linking immune checkpoints and risk scores

The prognostic risk score is an independent risk factor for LUAD. It is significantly correlated with various immune cell infiltrates, immune scores, and tumor purity in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) patients. These findings collectively indicate that the prognostic risk score may serve as a potential predictor for immunotherapy response. To evaluate its utility in predicting immunotherapy response in LUAD patients, we first analysed the expression of 50 immune modulators and assessed their correlation with the prognostic risk score. Notably, the expression levels of more than half of these modulators differed significantly between the high-risk and low-risk LUAD patient groups (p < 0.05; Fig. 10A). This included significantly lower expression of CD44, CD47, CD48, CD70, and CD80 in the high-risk group (p < 0.05; Fig. 10A). Furthermore, the correlation patterns between the expression levels of the six signature genes and immune modulator genes differed markedly between the high-risk and low-risk groups. Specifically, PEBP1 showed fewer significant correlations with immune modulator gene expression in the high-risk group (p < 0.05; Fig. 10B). Conversely, in the low-risk group, PEBP1 was significantly negatively correlated with ADORA2A, BTLA, CD200, CD200R1, and CD244 (r < 0, p < 0.05; Fig. 10C). These results suggest the significant potential of the prognostic risk score in assessing the immunotherapy response in LUAD patients.

Biology experiment verification

To investigate the role of the feature gene CYP27A1 in LUAD, we conducted qRT‒PCR analyses on A549, H1299, and normal human bronchial epithelial (BEAS-2B) cell lines. The results demonstrated that the mRNA expression level of CYP27A1 in lung cancer cell lines was significantly lower than that in normal epithelial cells (Fig. 11A). Further WB analyses confirmed that the protein expression level of CYP27A1 in lung cancer cell lines was also significantly lower than that in normal cells (Fig. 11B). These preliminary expression validations increased our confidence in performing functional studies on CYP27A1 in lung adenocarcinoma. Consequently, we further investigated the impact of CYP27A1 on the proliferation, migration, and invasion of lung adenocarcinoma cells. To evaluate the effectiveness of CYP27A1 knockdown and overexpression, we conducted qRT‒PCR and Western blotting analyses. The results indicated that the expression level of CYP27A1 in the knockdown group (sh-CYP27A1) was significantly reduced, whereas it was significantly elevated in the overexpression group (OE-CYP27A1) (Fig. 11C-F). Initially, we validated the effect of CYP27A1 on lung cancer cell proliferation. The MTT assay results revealed that the growth capacity of A549 and H1299 cells was significantly lower in the CYP27A1-overexpressing group than in the control group. Conversely, knockdown of CYP27A1 resulted in a marked increase in the growth capacity of both A549 and H1299 cells (Fig. 11G). Next, we used a wound healing assay to assess how CYP27A1 affects lung cancer cell migration. Following 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h of incubation, we measured the cell migration distance as an indicator of migration ability. The results indicated that in A549 and H1299 cells, the migration distance of CYP27A1-overexpressing lung cancer cells was significantly shorter than that of control cells, reflecting a notable decrease in migratory ability; in contrast, knockdown of CYP27A1 resulted in a significantly longer migration distance, indicating enhanced migration capacity (Fig. 11H-I). Finally, we utilized a Transwell assay to assess the effects of CYP27A1 on the invasive properties of lung cancer cells. The results revealed that the number of invading A549 and H1299 cells with CYP27A1 overexpression was significantly lower than that of the control group (p < 0.05), whereas the invasiveness of cells with CYP27A1 knockdown was significantly greater (p < 0.05, Fig. 11J-K). These findings suggest that CYP27A1 may suppresses the proliferation, migration, and invasion of lung adenocarcinoma cells.

CYP27A1 modulates ferroptosis in lung cancer cells via regulation of iron homeostasis and lipid peroxidation

Our previous bioinformatics analysis revealed enrichment of the ferroptosis pathway, prompting further mechanistic investigations into its regulatory components. On the basis of these findings, we explored the functional role of CYP27A1 in ferroptosis regulation through a series of experimental approaches. Compared with the control, CCK-8 assays demonstrated that knockdown of CYP27A1 (shCYP27A1) significantly increased A549 cell viability, whereas treatment with erastin (a ferroptosis inducer) or ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor) reversed this effect, reducing or restoring viability, respectively. Conversely, overexpression of CYP27A1 (OE CYP27A1) markedly suppressed proliferation, with erastin exacerbating and Fer-1 mitigating this suppression (Fig. 12A). The levels of intracellular Fe²⁺, a hallmark of ferroptosis, were reduced in shCYP27A1 cells but elevated in OE CYP27A1 cells; these trends were amplified by erastin and attenuated by Fer-1 (Fig. 12B). Similarly, the levels of lipid peroxidation and ROS, critical drivers of ferroptosis, were diminished in shCYP27A1 cells but increased in OE CYP27A1 cells, with erastin and Fer-1, respectively enhancing or suppressing these effects (Fig. 12C-D). Western blot analysis revealed that shCYP27A1 upregulated the ferroptosis inhibitors SLC7A11 and GPX4, whereas OE CYP27A1 downregulated both proteins, with erastin counteracting and Fer-1 reinforcing these expression patterns (Fig. 12E-F). Transmission electron microscopy further confirmed the role of CYP27A1: shCYP27A1 cells exhibited normal mitochondrial morphology unless treated with erastin, which induced mitochondrial shrinkage and cristae loss, whereas OE CYP27A1 cells displayed ferroptosis-associated mitochondrial damage that was exacerbated by erastin and rescued by Fer-1 (Fig. 12G-H). Collectively, these findings underscore CYP27A1 as a potential central regulator of ferroptosis in lung cancer through the modulation of iron metabolism, redox balance, and mitochondrial integrity.

Discussion

There are limited treatment options for patients with brain metastases from LUAD, resulting in poor patient prognosis. Owing to the unique microenvironment of the brain, the development of LUAD brain metastases is a complex and selective process [43]. Identifying the key genes and molecular mechanisms of LUAD brain metastasis and developing novel treatment strategies are essential for improving patient outcomes. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology is an effective approach for comprehensively understanding tumor heterogeneity, critical genes, and mechanisms of brain metastasis. Previous reports have demonstrated that scRNA-seq can be effectively used to study the transcriptome of lung cancer cells, classify different cancer and immune cell types, and explore their interactions. Therefore, scRNA-seq technology holds great promise for advancing our understanding and treatment of LUAD brain metastases, and this technology has also revealed the gene regulatory mechanisms and drug discovery information involved in lung cancer development and transformation [44–46]. However, research on LUAD brain metastases is still limited, with only partial exploration in identifying brain metastatic cells, transcriptome changes, gene regulatory processes, immune cells and the tumor microenvironment. The exact therapeutic targets and mechanisms for LUAD brain metastasis require further in-depth research.

Our study innovatively used single-cell and bulk RNA-seq to understand cell heterogeneity in lung cancer patients, and by further characterizing cell subpopulations associated with brain metastasis and identifying key genes affecting brain metastasis prognosis, we integrated clinical information to develop a prognostic scoring model. In addition, we predicted the efficacy of immunotherapy via immune cell analysis. Our study identified key prognostic genes and developed a risk scoring model that holds potential for aiding in clinical decision-making and treatment planning for this disease.

We first focused on the heterogeneity of LUAD brain metastasis patients. Single-cell subpopulation identification was performed via single-cell technology, and the TSNE method was used for visualization, which revealed that the cell clusters presented markedly different biological characteristics, indicating significant heterogeneity between the clusters. Previous studies [47, 48] have confirmed the substantial heterogeneity of lung cancer, suggesting that the brain metastasis of LUAD may involve multiple cell subpopulations and biological processes. By analysing 7,750 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), 784 brain metastasis-related genes, and 748 single-cell DEGs, we identified 33 key genes that are commonly differentially expressed in both single-cell and bulk RNA-seq data, shedding light on the complex nature of LUAD brain metastasis and laying the groundwork for further investigations of potential therapeutic targets. Through our univariate Cox regression analysis, we identified 8 prognostic genes, of which 6 genes (CD9, CYP27A1, HLA-DQB1, PEBP1, PECAM1, and TUBB) were highlighted. To better predict the outcome of LUAD patients, these genes were integrated into a prognostic risk scoring model. The correlation between these genes and lung cancer prognosis is the focus of our current study.

Some of the genes we studied have been explored in prior research. For example, PEBP1 (RKIP) is a gene that represses the RAF1/MEK/ERK pathway and has been identified as a metastasis inhibitor in pancreatic cancer [49–51]. It prevents raf1 phosphorylation and MEK activation by disrupting the interaction between these two kinases [52]. Our study validated these findings and demonstrated the efficacy of including PEBP1 in the prognostic risk score for LUAD patients.

PECAM1 is a cell adhesion molecule involved in cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration and immunity. It has been classified as a tumor suppressor gene, and low expression of PECAM1 is associated with an unfavourable prognosis in LUAD patients [53]. Furthermore, our investigation revealed a substantial association between PECAM1 levels and clinical outcomes in LUAD patients. Reduced PECAM1 expression was associated with poorer prognosis in our study [54]. While ongoing research continues to explore PECAM1, evidence suggests its involvement in immune regulation and potential role in modulating T-cell-mediated immune responses, which has significant implications for understanding the function of the immune system in both healthy and diseased states [55]. We found a significant association between this gene set and prognostic risk scores. Notably, patients with high and low risk scores presented marked differences in the expression levels of certain key genes associated with m6A methylation and m5C regulatory factors. These findings underscore the importance of these genes in assessing the antitumour potential of immune cells and their role in governing the biological mechanisms of LUAD, providing a basis for detecting and treating this type of cancer with immunotherapy.

In conclusion, our study confirms previous findings regarding PEBP1 and PECAM1 and provides new perspectives on the potential involvement of these proteins in immune regulation and tumorigenesis in LUAD. The use of these genes in the prognostic risk scoring model offers a promising approach to improve patient prognosis and facilitate the development of targeted immunotherapies for this specific cancer. Further investigations are needed to comprehensively understand the mechanisms underlying the functions of these genes in LUAD and their likely therapeutic implications.

In this study, we performed enrichment analysis on a set of 33 genes to identify their associations with various biological processes, cellular components and pathways involved in lung cancer. Our analysis revealed that these genes are associated with various biological processes, including the activation of glial cells, which have been shown to be critical for promoting tumor growth and chemical resistance. Previous studies have demonstrated the ability of cancer cells to activate astrocytes through functional gap junctions, resulting in the release of cytokines that promote tumor growth [56]. Moreover, our investigation revealed a correlation between these genes and specific cellular components, such as endocytic vesicles, which are essential for various physiological processes and intercellular communication. In addition, previous studies have highlighted the potential of exosomal ncRNAs as clinical biomarkers and targets for therapeutic intervention by highlighting their functional role in cancer initiation and progression, particularly in lung cancer [57]. In addition, our investigation revealed various pathways associated with these genes. The ability of antigen-presenting cells to process and present antigens determines the efficacy of immunotherapy [58]. The MHC class II molecules on the surface of malignant cells can facilitate antigen presentation to helper T lymphocytes, which can increase the ability of the immune system to fight tumors [59], and the transfection of the CIITA gene can rapidly improve MHC in small cell lung cancer and neuroendocrine tumors. EB virus infection has also been linked to lung cancer, with several studies demonstrating its association with immune tolerance and activation of oncogenic pathways [60, 61]. Pathway analysis of high-expression samples revealed suppression of immune pathways and activation of oncogenic pathways, suggesting that EB virus directly regulates tumor pathways and promotes tumor development.

In summary, our research underscores the importance of recognizing and studying the biological processes, cellular components and pathways associated with lung cancer to improve our understanding of its origin and progression. Understanding these pathways may provide valuable insights for developing innovative ways to diagnose and treat lung cancer.

The tumor microenvironment is a complex network of cells and factors, including immune cells. Tumor tissue is a heterogeneous mixture of neoplastic and nonneoplastic cells, and tumor purity indicates the content of tumor cells within the tissue. Notably, immune cells represent a significant portion of the nonneoplastic cells present in tumor tissue, and their presence could have an impact on the expression of genes associated with the immune system. When analysing immune-related genes, it is important to consider factors such as tumor purity and the immune score.The efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors has been associated with TMB and immune system-related genes, so it is important to measure the extent of immune cell infiltration via indicators such as tumor purity, immune score and immune cell content to better understand the efficacy of immunotherapy.

One study revealed that tumors with low purity have greater numbers of immune cells. The immune response induced by these cells can increase the mutation rate of tumor cells and improve the efficacy of immunotherapy [62]. However, low tumor purity is an independent prognostic factor indicating a poor prognosis in patients with colorectal cancer [63]. Our analysis of high-risk samples suggested an inverse relationship between typical gene expression levels and immune cell abundance. The risk score we developed shows a strong association with immune infiltration, as demonstrated by our rigorous examination of tumor purity and the immune score. In particular, a significant and highly positive association was found for the risk score and tumor purity, whereas a significant and highly negative association was found for the risk score and immune score. These findings are consistent with previous research in this area. Focusing on immune-regulatory genes associated with risk scores, our study revealed that the risk score may partially predict the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. Specifically, the high-risk group had a positive relationship with the levels of genes associated with these risk scores. These genes can impair the tumor-fighting capabilities of immune cells, thereby compromising the therapeutic efficacy of immune-based interventions.

Therefore, the prognostic risk score we constructed is not only a powerful prognostic marker but also a potential predictive marker for immunotherapy. Accurately quantifying immune cell infiltration and considering factors such as tumor purity and the immune score are essential for predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy and developing effective prognostic markers. Our analysis underscores the importance of genes associated with immunity and immune cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment for understanding cancer progression and developing effective therapies.

Previous studies [64, 65] have demonstrated the pivotal role of CYP27A1 in cholesterol metabolism, which is intricately linked to tumor biology. Numerous investigations have revealed that aberrant cholesterol levels can impact cell membrane fluidity, signal transduction, and the proliferation and metastasis of cancer cells. Therefore, elucidating the influence of CYP27A1 on these mechanisms may provide insights into the potential biological underpinnings of lung cancer brain metastasis. During biogenic analysis, a comparison of six characteristic genes highlighted a particularly pronounced disparity in CYP27A1 expression across different clinical stages and prognoses. Considering experimental resources and time constraints, we selected one gene for comprehensive investigation to ensure exhaustive and thorough examination of its functionality. The expression level of CYP27A1, a member of the cytochrome P450 family, was found to be significantly lower in cancer cells than in normal bronchial epithelial cells. This study identified CYP27A1 as a potential tumor suppressor in lung adenocarcinoma. This observation suggests a crucial molecular‒phenotype relationship in which molecular regulation by CYP27A1 directly influences the phenotypic characteristics of lung cancer cells, particularly in terms of proliferation, migration, and invasion. The downregulation of CYP27A1 in lung adenocarcinoma cells is correlated with enhanced cell proliferation, as demonstrated by MTT assays. These findings indicate that CYP27A1 may play a pivotal role in cell cycle regulation, potentially through pathways that govern cell growth and division. Furthermore, the suppression of migration and invasion upon CYP27A1 overexpression suggests its role in modulating the metastatic potential of lung cancer cells. The mechanisms underlying these effects may involve the regulation of specific genes associated with cell motility and invasion, suggesting that CYP27A1 acts as a molecular switch that can alter the aggressive behavior of lung adenocarcinoma cells. CYP27A1 has been identified as a crucial regulator in the context of lung adenocarcinoma, particularly through its modulation of ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation. Our data indicate that CYP27A1 influences iron homeostasis, which in turn may affects cellular proliferation and survival. Specifically, the downregulation of CYP27A1 in lung cancer cells leads to increased intracellular Fe²⁺levels, promoting cell viability and enhancing sensitivity to ferroptosis inducers such as erastin. These findings suggest that CYP27A1 could acts as a molecular switch, where its expression levels might influence the balance between cell survival and death. Moreover, the interaction of CYP27A1 with ferroptosis-related proteins, such as SLC7A11 and GPX4, further elucidates its role in regulating oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation. The upregulation of these ferroptosis inhibitors upon CYP27A1 knockdown highlights a compensatory mechanism that allows cancer cells to evade ferroptotic death, thereby promoting tumor growth and metastasis. Conversely, the overexpression of CYP27A1 diminishes the levels of these inhibitors, reinforcing the notion that CYP27A1 serves as a suppressor of tumorigenic processes through its influence on ferroptosis. In summary, the molecular pathway involving CYP27A1 underscores its potential as a therapeutic target in lung adenocarcinoma. By potentially modulating iron metabolism and ferroptosis, CYP27A1 not only contributes to the understanding of lung cancer biology but also opens avenues for exploring novel treatment strategies aimed at enhancing ferroptotic cell death in cancer therapy.

The focus of this paper is on CYP27A1; however, future studies will aim to validate other signature genes. This endeavor will contribute to the construction of a comprehensive molecular network for lung cancer brain metastases, facilitating a deeper understanding of gene interactions. Ultimately, our goal is to establish a model that systematically evaluates the effects of multigene cooperation and thereby provides novel possibilities for the treatment of lung cancer.

This study has certain limitations that should be considered. The analysis relied on public transcriptomic datasets, which included a relatively small cohort of LUAD patients with brain metastasis. Thus, validating our prognostic model in a larger, prospective clinical cohort is an essential next step. Similarly, the clinical utility of the risk score requires further assessment with more detailed patient immunotherapy records. Experimentally, future work should employ LUAD-specific cell lines or patient-derived organoids to strengthen the biological relevance of our findings. Finally, the precise mechanism by which CYP27A1 regulates ferroptosis and its interactions within the tumor microenvironment merit further investigation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our single-cell study identified subtypes and genetic characteristics of LUAD patients with brain metastases and established a novel prognostic model using six genes that shows potential for predict patient prognosis and may provide valuable insights into the response to immunotherapy. Our findings provide new insights for clinical practice.
