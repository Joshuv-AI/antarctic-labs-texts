Structure–function coupling changes in first-episode, treatment-naïve schizophrenia correlate with cell type-specific transcriptional signature
Authors: Guanqun Yao, Jingjing Pan, Ting Zou, Jing Li, Juan Li, Xiao He, Fuquan Zhang, Yong Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFirst-episode schizophrenia (FES) is a complex and progressive psychiatric disorder. The etiology of FES involves genetic, environmental, and neurobiological factors. This study investigates the association between alterations in structural–functional (SC-FC) coupling and transcriptional expression in FES.MethodsThis study encompassed a cohort of 214 participants, comprising 111 FES patients and 103 healthy controls (HC). Furthermore, we examined the abnormalities within SC-FC coupling in FES and their correlations with meta-analytic cognitive terms, neurotransmitters, and transcriptional patterns through partial least squares regression (PLS), involving similarity with other psychiatric disorders or psychiatric-related diseases, functional enrichments, special cell types, peripheral inflammation, and cortical layers.ResultsFES patients exhibited lower SC-FC coupling in the visual, sensorimotor, and ventral attention networks compared to controls. Furthermore, case–control t-maps revealed a negative correlation with neurotransmitters such as serotonin and dopamine, while showing a positive correlation with opioids. Positive t-maps were associated with cognitive functions, including reasoning, judgment, and action, whereas negative t-maps correlated with cognitive functions such as learning, stress, and mood. Moreover, there was a significant overlap between genes linked to abnormalities in SC-FC coupling and those dysregulated in inflammatory bowel diseases. PLS2- genes linked to SC-FC coupling demonstrated significant enrichment in pathways related to immunity and inflammation, as well as in cortical layers I and V. Conversely, PLS2 + genes were primarily enriched in synaptic signaling processes, specific excitatory neurons, and layers II and IV. Additionally, changes in SC-FC coupling were negatively associated with gene expression related to antipsychotics and lymphocytes.ConclusionsThese findings offer a new perspective on the complex interplay between SC-FC coupling abnormalities and transcriptional expression in the initial phases of schizophrenia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-024-03714-3.

Background

Schizophrenia (SCZ) ranks among the most debilitating and economically burdensome mental health conditions, marked by a spectrum of manifestations, such as positive or negative symptoms, behavioral disorders, and cognitive impairments [1–3]. Typically, the onset of first-episode SCZ (FES) occurs during adolescence, characterized by pronounced prepsychotic developmental anomalies, neurostructural and neurofunctional irregularities, as well as heightened genetic susceptibility [4, 5]. Recurrent or prolonged psychotic episodes can detrimentally impact the patient’s neurological integrity, cognitive abilities, and overall prognosis [6]. The etiology of FES remains elusive, necessitating an exploration of the biological mechanisms at both the macroscopic brain network and molecular levels.

A plethora of studies have identified brain abnormalities in FES [7, 8]. These studies revealed specific variations including changes in cortical thickness, gray matter volume, as well as alterations in structural covariance and functional networks [9–11]. Notably, these abnormalities were prominent in areas including the precuneus, prefrontal cortex, and default network (DMN) [12, 13]. Additionally, our preceding study on thalamic functional connectivity gradients in early-onset SCZ (EOS) patients highlighted significant disruptions in macroscopic thalamic functional organization [14]. This disruption was associated with modified thalamocortical interactions within unimodal or transmodal networks. Furthermore, the Morphometric Inverse Divergence (MIND) network has revealed aberrant structural similarities in the pericalcarine cortex and multiple networks in EOS [15]. Nonetheless, previous studies predominantly focused on single-modality features, neglecting the integration of multi-modal characteristics such as structural and functional attributes, which is essential for a thorough understanding of the neurobiological mechanisms underlying FES.

Structural-functional (SC-FC) coupling refers to the way in which the white matter interconnection patterns of cortical regions underpin the strength of its functional communication, demonstrated by the association between the robustness of functional connectomes and corresponding structural connectomes in two nodes [16, 17]. This phenomenon exhibits distinct hierarchical and cellular structural principles, particularly evident in primary sensory (unimodal) cortices, where SC-FC coupling is pronounced [18]. However, this coupling diverges in higher-order transmodal regions, including those in the DMN [19]. This divergence, theorized to facilitate abstract reasoning by shielding it from the granular signaling characteristic of initial sensory processing stages, reflects a relative decoupling in these higher-order areas [20]. SC-FC coupling variations are observed during brain maturation and in psychiatric and neurological disorders, highlighting their relevance to cognitive processes [16, 21, 22]. Nevertheless, research on SC-FC coupling alterations in FES patients remains scarce.

Recent evidence indicates that transcriptional patterns significantly influence the brain, particularly in forming metabolically demanding and functionally crucial connections [23, 24]. The comprehensive whole-brain gene expression atlas, sourced from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) database, has offered avenues for connecting microcosmic disease-associated gene expression with macroscopical brain changes in various psychiatric disorders [25–27]. Moreover, our latest research has explored the relationship between macroscale architectural variations and regional transcriptional patterns in EOS patients, involving the morphometric similarity network (MSN) and MIND approaches [15, 28]. However, the comprehensive assessment integrating SC-FC coupling alterations and regional gene expression pattern for FES patients remains to be established.

In this study, we explored the association between alterations in SC-FC coupling and transcriptional expression in FES patients. Initially, we integrated functional connectivity and white matter structural connectivity to identify unique SC-FC coupling patterns in FES. We then examined the relationship between alterations in SC-FC coupling, neurotransmitter variations, and meta-analytic cognitive terms. Utilizing the AHBA datasets, we investigated the connection between SC-FC coupling alterations in FES and their transcriptional profiles through the partial least squares regression (PLS). Specifically, we employed permutation tests to ascertain significant gene similarities between SC-FC coupling alterations in FES patients and gene expression abnormalities in other psychiatric disorders or psychiatric-related diseases. Furthermore, we undertook a functional enrichment analysis to elucidate the ontological pathways associated with genes related to FES. Additionally, we also evaluated the spatial correlation between peripheral inflammation markers and altered SC-FC coupling in FES patients. Finally, we identified specific cell types and cortical layers to assess their contributions to transcriptomic relationships linked to SC-FC coupling changes in FES.

Methods

Participants

This study included 214 individuals ranging from 7 to 30 years, consisting of 111 treatment-naïve FES and 103 healthy controls (HC). Evaluation of the FES were conducted by clinicians at the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University, and the HC group was sourced from surrounding neighborhoods. The diagnosis of FES conformed to the criteria outlined in the Fourth Edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV) and was validated by two psychiatrists using Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV (SCID) following at least 6 months of follow-up [29]. Then, each participant underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Among the FES cohorts, 16 participants lacked complete scale evaluation on the Positive and Negative Symptom Scale (PANSS), a tool for assessing SCZ severity [30]. After completing the MRI and PANSS assessments, FES patients initiated a treatment regimen with second-generation antipsychotic medications. Inclusion criteria for FES included (i) age ranging from 7 to 30 years, right-handedness; (ii) DSM-IV diagnosis of SCZ; and (iii) initial SCZ diagnosis without taking any psychotropic drugs. Exclusion criteria encompassed (i) a family background of mental health disorders or organic brain diseases; (ii) coexisting Axis-I or Axis-II disorders; (iii) SCZ diagnosis for over a year; and (iv) contraindications for MRI scanning. HC was selected based on the absence of familial psychiatric disorders or brain organic diseases, and no history of psychiatric medication use.

Imaging acquisition and quality assurance

All imaging modalities were subjected to stringent quality assurance [31]. Before diffusion processing, each volume of raw datasets was visually inspected for imaging quality. Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) images showing translations greater than 2.5 mm or rotations exceeding 2.5° were excluded to minimize head movement effects. Frame displacements (FD) were calculated, and frames with displacements above 0.50 were discarded. The study involved a total of 231 participants. Following the exclusion of subjects with missing images or poor image quality, 214 participants were retained for analysis. Details concerning imaging acquisition were available in Additional file 1: Imaging acquisition.

Construction of the structural connectomes

DWI images underwent pre-processing with the assistance of MRtrix3.0 [32]. This process included denoising, removing Gibbs artefacts, correcting eddy currents and motion, and rectifying the bias field [33, 34]. Diffusion tensor metric was computed, and constrained spherical deconvolution was carried out. Alignment of raw T1-weighted images with DWI scans utilized NiftyReg, which was succeeded by a five-tissue anatomical segmentation using MRtrix’s 5ttgen script. Subsequent anatomically constrained tractography produced 0.2 million streamlines through the iFOD2 algorithm, incorporating dynamic seeding techniques. These streamlines were cut off at the interface between gray and white matter. To minimize biases, the SIFT2 algorithm (spherical deconvolution-informed filtering of tractograms) was implemented. We employed the final set of streamlines to build a structural brain network. Connections within this network, adjusted by both streamline count and cross-sectional areas, were integrated into 400 × 400 undirected, weighted matrices [35].

Construction of the functional connectomes

The rs-fMRI images underwent preprocessing using SPM12 (www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/) and DPARSF (http://www.restfmri.net) [36, 37]. To ensure signal stability, we discarded the first ten time points. We then applied slice-timing and motion corrections to all images. Furthermore, the imaging was aligned to a standard template using echo-planar imaging, resampled to 3-mm isotropic voxels, and underwent smoothing with a Gaussian kernel possessing a 6-mm full-width at half-maximum. We performed linear detrending and removed confounders such as signals from white matter, Friston-24 motion parameters, and cerebrospinal fluid from the voxel time series. Temporal bandpass filtering occurred within the 0.01–0.08 Hz frequency range. Furthermore, 400 × 400 weighted adjacency matrices for all images were created by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients among all regions.

Calculation of SC-FC coupling

For all participants, we derived regional connectivity profiles from both functional and structural connectomes, depicting these as vectors indicating connectivity strength from a singular region to all other regions. SC-FC coupling at each region was quantified by determining the Spearman rank correlation across the non-zero values within the corresponding regional functional and structural connectomes [16]. For each participant, global SC-FC coupling was assessed by averaging the SC-FC coupling values across all regions. Furthermore, to assess the association between regional SC-FC coupling and SC-FC coupling both within- and between-network, SC-FC coupling was computed separately for each region, delineating within- and between-network associations [38]. Specifically, SC-FC coupling of within-network for each node was determined by calculating the Spearman correlation of the functional and structural connections linking that node to other nodes within the same network. Conversely, SC-FC coupling of between-network was determined similarly, but focused on the connections between the specified node and nodes outside its designated network.

Case–control analysis of SC-FC coupling

To assess differences in SC-FC coupling between FES and HC, we employed a general linear model (GLM) incorporating sex and age as covariates. Subsequently, we performed the two-sided t-tests (contrast: FES vs HC). Furthermore, subnetworks within the functional Yeo 7 networks were analyzed to delineate SC-FC coupling disparities between FES and HC [39]. Additionally, FES patients were categorized into two cohorts: minor FES (under 18 years at first onset) and adult FES (18 years or older at first onset). The SC-FC coupling in these subgroups was compared using a GLM, adjusting for sex and age. Spearman’s correlation assessed associations between PANSS scores and SC-FC coupling in aberrant nodes and networks among FES patients. For correcting multiple comparisons, we performed the false discovery rate (FDR) method with a p-value threshold set at < 0.05.

To investigate the developmental trajectories of FES in relation to age, a quadratic nonlinear model was applied to analyze both the global SC-FC coupling and the SC-FC coupling within the functional network. This model was selected based on well-established evidence that age-related brain changes follow nonlinear patterns [40, 41], aiming to identify significant differences in the rate of age-related changes between patients and controls. Details on the quadratic nonlinear model were provided in the Additional file 1: Quadratic nonlinear model.

Neurotransmitters related to SC-FC coupling

JuSpace (https://github.com/juryxy/JuSpace) serves as an effective tool for conducting spatial correlation analysis across different modalities of neuroimaging data [42]. We employed JuSpace to investigate the neurochemical underpinnings of neural correlates associated with behavioral inhibition by analyzing spatial relationships between t-maps and nuclear imaging-derived indicators of diverse neurotransmitter systems [43]. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was calculated between neurotransmitter maps and the t-maps throughout the standard Neuromorphometrics Atlas. To assess whether the correlation coefficients for participants from various groups markedly differed from the expected random distribution, exact permutation-based p-values were calculated (10,000 permutations, adjusted for spatial autocorrelation). For correcting multiple comparisons, we applied the FDR method with a p-value threshold set at < 0.05. Details regarding neurotransmitters were available in the Additional file 1: Neurotransmitters.

Cognitive terms related to SC-FC coupling

The Neurosynth platform (https://neurosynth.org/) was employed to explore the relationships between meta-analytic cognitive terms and changes in SC-FC coupling among FES patients. Neurosynth is a well-validated and widely utilized framework which contains a broad set of cognitive term-to-activation mappings in datasets and allows for drawing quantitative inferences of brain–behavior association via forward inferences (quantify the probability of specific brain regions given the presence a particular term) and reverse inferences (the probability of a term occurring in an article given the presence of activation in a particular brain region) [44]. The Neurosynth filtered out non-cognitive terms, retaining a list of 123 cognitive terms [45]. Threshold t-maps derived from SC-FC coupling comparisons were classified into two categories: FES-positive and FES-negative maps. Subsequently, Neurosynth’s “decoder” function outputs a probabilistic measure of the association between t-value maps and cognitive terms [46, 47]. This measure, referred to as “r”, indicated the probability of a specific term being reported in studies, given observed activation at a particular voxel. The analysis primarily focused on identifying the top-ranked cognitive terms based on the association metric (r), specifically selecting the top 30 terms.

Estimation of gene expression profiles

Gene expression profiles were acquired from the AHBA microarray datasets (http://human.brain-map.org), comprising 3702 regional samples from 6 human postmortem brains. These donors aged from 24 to 57 years and included six left hemisphere and two right hemisphere tissues [48]. Hence, we selected the gene expression profiles from the left hemisphere for subsequent analysis. Subsequently, the Python abagen toolbox (https://github.com/rmarkello/abagen) was employed to map the gene expression data onto 200 brain regions within the left hemisphere [49]. This strategy was performed followed by the standardized protocol with seven steps: gene symbol conversion, noise data filter, coincident probe selection, missing data supplement, brain coupling matching, sample normalization, and reliable gene identification [50]. Consequently, after this process, we obtained a transcriptional matrix encompassing 200 brain regions and 15,631 genes.

Spatial connection between transcription and SC-FC coupling

To explore the potential spatial correlation between transcription and SC-FC coupling abnormities in FES, we conducted the PLS regression via setting the expressional levels across 15,631 genes as predictors and the differential t-maps from 200 regions as responses [51]. The significant PLS components (PLS2) were chosen as the optimal interpretational degree of the covariance at low-dimensional levels, within the high-dimensional data matrix. Subsequently, a spatial autocorrelation analysis was performed using a permutation test involving 10,000 iterations to determine whether the elucidative covariance between the t-statistic region maps and PLS2 weights exceeded the expectation by chance. Moreover, to assess the variability of genes within the PLS2 component, we employed the bootstrapping method with 10,000 bootstrap samples [52]. The gene’s Z-value was identified by dividing each region’s weight according to the corresponding bootstrap standard error, and then, all genes were ranked to screen PLS2-weighted genes with FDR-corrected p < 0.05 [53]. Finally, the spatial correlation analysis was performed with Spearman methods between t-statistic maps of SC-FC coupling and PLS2 weights.

Functional annotation of PLS2-weighted genes

To further explore the functional features of PLS2-weighted genes, we utilized the online tool (called Metascape, https://metascape.org/) to perform various enrichment analyses [54]. Additionally, we explored potential correlations between abnormal SC-FC coupling patterns in FES and gene dysregulation in psychiatric disorders or psychiatric-related diseases by assessing the overlap of PLS2 significant genes with differential gene expressions (DGEs) obtained from Gandal et al. [55]. These disorders included major depressive disorder (MDD), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), adult-SCZ, bipolar disorder (BD), and alcohol abuse disorder (AAD). The gut–brain axis theory suggests that inflammation-mediated IBD is associated with the pathogenesis of various psychiatric disorders, potentially sharing common pathogenic genes [56–59]. Dysregulated genes of each disorder were selected based on adjusted p value < 0.05 and log2 (fold change) > 0. To validate the significance of the overlapped genes, we employed permutation tests to assess the unilateral p value for the mean PLS2-Z scores of the shared genes in each disease [52], as detailed in the Additional file 1: Correlations with psychiatric disorders or psychiatric-related diseases. The FDR adjusted p value < 0.05 was applied to control for false positives.

Assigning PLS2-weighted genes to special cell types, peripheral inflammation, and cortical layers

To assign PLS2-weighted genes to specific cell types, peripheral inflammation, and cortical layers, we performed an integrated multi-angle enrichment analysis through multiple methods. Firstly, we obtained the marker gene lists of seven canonical brain-cell types from five independent single-cell RNA sequencing studies [60], including microglia, oligodendrocyte precursors (OPCs), endothelial cells, oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, and inhibitory and excitatory neurons. Then, the single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm was performed to identify ssGSEA scores as quantificationally cellular levels in each coupling with the “Gaussian” regression and “gsva” algorithm using the GSVA package [61]. To further verify the cellular specificity linked to SC-FC coupling changes, we overlapped the PLS-weighted genes with marker genes of these cortical cells [62]. Furthermore, we validated these findings using permutation tests. We also performed the functional enrichment analysis using the Metascape tools based on the overlapped genes in each cell type. Subsequently, we further quantified levels of five peripheral inflammation markers and anti-psychotics using the ssGSEA algorithm based on features from previous Yu et al. [63] and Nalls et al. [64] studies, including leukocyte, monocyte, lymphocytes, neutrophils, and basophils. The spatial correlation was also evaluated between inflammation markers and abnormal SC-FC coupling in FES. Finally, to investigate the potential relationships between abnormal SC-FC coupling patterns and different cortical layers, we employed the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) method using the “GSEABase” and “clusterProfiler” packages [65]. Characteristic lists of six different cortical layers were obtained from a published transcriptomic study [66].

Null model

To address potential confounding effects resulting from spatial autocorrelation, we applied a null model based on the spin test [67]. This approach generates null Spearman correlation coefficients through the spherical projection of spatial maps, which are randomly rotated while preserving their spatial integrity. In our analysis, a null distribution was established by performing 10,000 spin permutation tests across all regions. We then calculated the pspin value by determining the proportion of instances in which the null correlation coefficients exceeded the observed values.

Reproducibility validation analyses

To confirm the robustness and reliability of our results, we performed a series of validation analyses: (i) assessing the consistency of the t-maps in SC-FC coupling after adjusting for FD; (ii) splitting the FES patient group randomly into two subgroups and comparing the t-maps in SC-FC coupling for each subgroup with HC; (iii) categorizing all participants into minor and adult groups and evaluating the t-maps in SC-FC coupling between FES and HC for both minors and adults separately; (iv) examining the consistency of associations between the t-maps of SC-FC coupling and transcriptional expression profiles using five genes associated with somatostatin (SST) interneurons [68]; and (v) confirming the dependability of functional annotations through a multi-gene list meta-analysis using SCZ-related genes from genome-wide association studies (GWASSCZ) [69], as described in the Additional file 1:Validation of functional annotation’s robustness.

SC-FC coupling alterations in FES patients

The regional SC-FC coupling distribution map indicated that extreme SC-FC coupling values in HC were predominantly observed in areas with higher values, whereas in FES patients, these extremes were more frequent in areas with lower values (Fig. 2a). Averaging SC-FC coupling across all regions for each participant, FES patients exhibited a lower global SC-FC coupling relative to HC (t = − 2.26, p = 0.025). Notably, the average SC-FC coupling among all subjects for each region showed a spatially positive association between HC and FES patients (r = 0.96, pspin < 0.001, Additional file 1: Fig. S1b), following a gradient from the visual networks (VN) to the DMN (Fig. 2b). Additionally, both between- and within-network SC-FC couplings were quantified (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). There were spatial associations between case–control t-values in the regional SC-FC coupling and both those in within-network (r = 0.50, pspin < 0.001) and between-network coupling (r = 0.80, pspin < 0.001). The t-map between-network SC-FC coupling displayed a spatial correlation with the within-network SC-FC coupling (r = 0.26, p = 0.009). Therefore, our further analysis concentrated on the regional aspects of SC-FC coupling. Nevertheless, no significant associations emerged after applying the FDR correction.

Associations between SC-FC coupling alterations and neurotransmitter distribution

Our study demonstrated spatial correlations between t-maps and neurotransmitter distribution (Fig. 3c). Following FDR correction, the t-maps in SC-FC coupling demonstrated significant correlations with several neurotransmitters: 5HT2a (r = − 0.28, p = 0.010), D2 (r = − 0.33, p = 0.003), GABA type a (GABAa, r = − 0.38, p = 0.003), μ-opioid receptor (MU, r = 0.47, p = 0.019), and noradrenaline transporter (NAT, r = − 0.44, p = 0.010). Additionally, the comparison between minor and adult FES revealed significant spatial correlations with 5HT2a (r = 0.26, p = 0.011), serotonin transporter (SERT, r = − 0.22, p = 0.033), D1 (r = − 0.28, p = 0.007), D2 (r = 0.26, p = 0.010), dopamine transporter (DAT, r = − 0.33, p = 0.003), 6-fluoro-(18F)-L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine, (FDOPA, r = − 0.23, p = 0.023), GABAa (r = 0.29, p = 0.004), MU (r = − 0.53, p = 0.013), NAT (r = 0.25, p = 0.012), and vesicular acetylcholine transporter (VAChT, r = − 0.26, p = 0.012).

Correlations between case–control t-maps in SC-FC coupling and meta-analytic cognitive terms

Our investigation focused on the correlations between case–control t-maps in SC-FC coupling and meta-analytic cognitive terms (Fig. 3d, Additional file 1: Table. S6). The t-map, reflecting higher SC-FC coupling in FES patients, correlated with cognitive features predominantly related to reasoning, judgment, action, working memory, rule, and decision. Conversely, the t-map representing lower SC-FC coupling in FES patients was primarily associated with cognitive aspects concerning learning, stress, mood, intelligence, and reward anticipation.

Transcriptional signatures related to SC-FC coupling changes

The second PLS component (PLS2) effectively interpreted the most variations (18.5%) in SC-FC coupling changes in FES patients, significantly exceeding the random expectation (pperm = 0.007) (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Interestingly, the distribution of the PLS2 score maps displayed obvious consistence with the case–control t-maps (Fig. 4a) and exhibited significant spatially positive correlations with t-values of SC-FC coupling (r = 0.45, pspin < 0.001, Fig. 4b). Subsequently, by ranking the Z scores of each gene with FDR-adjusted p < 0.05, we successfully identified a total of 2478 PLS2-weighted genes, including 1337 PLS2 + (Z > 2.65) and 1142 PLS2- (Z < − 2.65) genes (Fig. 4c). Additionally, IBD-related genes exhibited stronger positive PLS2 weights while there were no significant correlations with other five psychiatric disorders (FDR-adjusted pperm < 0.05, Fig. 4d). Functional annotation analysis exhibited that PLS2 + genes were prominently enriched in processes linked to synaptic signaling and signal transduction, including “Neuronal System,” “synaptic signaling,” “small GTPase mediated signal transduction,” and “modulation of chemical synaptic transmission” (Fig. 4e). Conversely, PLS2- genes were majorly enriched in immune and inflammation-related pathways, such as “Adaptive Immune System,” “Cytokine Signaling in Immune system,” “antigen processing and presentation of endogenous peptide antigen,” and “Human cytomegalovirus infection” (Fig. 4f). These findings supported a potential connection between PLS2-weighted genes and pathogenesis of FES patients at functional aspects. Reproducibility validation of FES-related abnormalities in SC-FC coupling and transcriptional patterns

To confirm the reliability of our results, we investigated the influence of FD on the observed case–control differences in SC-FC coupling (Additional file 1: Fig. S6). Notably, the disparities in SC-FC coupling between FES and HC, controlling for FD, remained highly correlated with those unadjusted for FD (r = 0.99, pspin < 0.001). To assess potential data heterogeneity, we randomly divided the FES patients into two subgroups and compared their SC-FC coupling with HC (Additional file 1: Fig. S7). The t-map for FES patients showed significant spatially positive correlations with both subgroup1 (r = 0.89, pspin < 0.001) and subgroup2 (r = 0.87, pspin < 0.001). Considering the broad age range in this study, we categorized participants into minor and adult groups. The SC-FC coupling t-maps for FES patients and HC were then analyzed separately for minors and adults (Additional file 1: Fig. S8). There were no statistically significant differences in sex or age between patients and controls in both the adults and minors. The t-values of the main differential brain regions in both groups were found to be in the same direction (Additional file 1: Table. S8-9). The t-map across the entire age group demonstrated spatially positive correlations with both the minor group (r = 0.85, pspin < 0.001) and the adult group (r = 0.55, pspin < 0.001).

In addition, we employed five canonical biomarkers of SST interneurons, including CCK, NPY, PVALB, SST, and VIP, to evaluate the strength of the association between the t-maps in SC-FC coupling and transcriptional levels. It demonstrated that NPY and SST were significantly positively linked to the t-values of SC-FC coupling (SST: r = 0.50, pspin < 0.001, NPY: r = 0.31, pspin = 0.006; Additional file 1: Fig. S9). Lastly, to confirm the validity of disease-related functional annotations, we conducted a multi-gene list meta-analysis with genes related to GWASSCZ. This analysis revealed several shared synaptic signaling-related processes and pathways between PLS2-weighted and GWASSCZ genes, encompassing terms like “regulation of trans-synaptic signaling,” “synaptic signaling,” “neuron projection development,” and “Neuronal System”. These findings aligned with the functional annotation particularly noted in the PLS2 + genes (Additional file 1: Fig. S10).

Discussion

This study constitutes a comprehensive analysis of the intricate interaction between SC-FC coupling alterations and transcriptional expression in FES patients. We identified abnormal SC-FC coupling patterns in the VN, SMN, and VAN across various brain regions in FES, which are associated with alterations in neurotransmitter distribution and cognitive impairments. Moreover, our findings demonstrated a spatial association between t-maps and gene expression patterns, as indicated by PLS2 scores. Furthermore, a significant overlap was observed between genes associated with SC-FC coupling changes and those dysregulated in IBD. Genes linked to SC-FC coupling (PLS2- genes) showed significant enrichment in immunity and inflammation-related pathways, as well as in cortical layers I and V. Conversely, genes associated with PLS2 + were primarily enriched in synaptic signaling processes, specific excitatory neurons, and layers II and IV. Additionally, changes in SC-FC coupling were negatively associated with gene expression related to anti-psychotics and lymphocytes. These findings highlight the complex interplay between macroscopic SC-FC coupling abnormalities and microscopic transcriptional patterns in the initial phases of FES.

In this study, we measured the coupling strength between functional and structural connectomes across cortical brain areas in FES patients. Our findings indicated a global decrease in SC-FC coupling, notably in regions including the precuneus (responsible for visuospatial processing) [70], cingulate gyrus (related to emotion formation and processing) [71], middle frontal gyrus (critical for executive functions and working memory) [72], and inferior temporal gyrus (essential for recognizing visual objects) [73]. Additionally, the diminished SC-FC coupling across various functional networks implied that individuals with FES might exhibit abnormalities in primary sensory (unimodal) cortices during the early stages of SCZ [74]. Furthermore, our analysis revealed that, in contrast to adulthood, the minor stage of FES was characterized by extensive coupling compensation from primary sensory (unimodal) cortices to higher-order transmodal regions. Brain changes develop rapidly from childhood to adolescence as myelination and synapse pruning [75, 76]. Consistent with these studies, our analysis of developmental trajectories suggests a gradual decline in the coupling of higher-order cognitive networks in FES with increasing age. However, we found no associations between SC-FC coupling alterations and PANSS scores in FES, indicating that coupling abnormalities might precede the appearance of clinical phenotypes during the initial phases of SCZ.

Alterations in SC-FC coupling were spatially associated with the distribution of multiple neurotransmitters. This suggests that aberrant coupling may disrupt certain neurotransmitters, potentially contributing to the diverse clinical manifestations observed in FES [43]. Moreover, our meta-analysis confirmed that regions predominantly exhibiting SC-FC coupling alterations in FES patients are significantly linked to cognitive terms, encompassing various higher cognitive functions such as reasoning, judgment, action, learning, stress, and intelligence [77, 78]. This finding underscores a potential association between impaired connectome coupling and cognitive deficits in FES patients. Additionally, our analysis of reproducibility demonstrated that the observed differences were not influenced by variables such as age range, FD, and data heterogeneity, thereby validating the reliability of our findings.

The functional enrichment analysis, using significant PLS2-weighted genes, clearly elucidated the transcriptional features underlying SC-FC coupling abnormities in FES. Notably, PLS2 + genes displayed major enrichment in pathways linked to synaptic signaling and signal transduction processes. Synaptic signaling, playing a crucial role in maintaining synaptic stability and maturation, has been reported to participate in the potential mechanisms underlying SCZ through changing neuron connectivity, activity, and co-transmission [79]. Utilizing a multi-gene list meta-analysis according to GWASscz-related genes, we further confirmed the credibility of functional enrichment within several shared synaptic signaling-related processes with PLS2-weighted genes, underscoring the necessity of genetic factors in regulating synaptic signaling and neural development during the FES. Meanwhile, PLS2- weighted genes were notably enriched in immunity and inflammation-related pathways, implying the potential involvements of inflammation in the pathogenesis of FES. Immune system abnormalities have been reported associated with SCZ, including cytokine-related pathways [80] and the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) [81]. Double-blind randomized clinical trials further demonstrated that adjunctive treatment with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory could improve the psychopathology of patients [82]. Concerning the immune cell parameters, Drexhage et al. detected higher levels of proinflammatory gene signatures from monocytes in SCZ patients, and the monocytosis was significantly associated with the severity of psychotic symptoms [83, 84]. Additionally, levels of lymphocytes were also found to prospectively predict poorer recovery outcomes in positive symptoms in SCZ patients after 6-month anti-psychotic treatment [85]. Previous studies have indicated that SCZ patients who have a history of IBD are at an increased risk of inadequate management of psychotic symptoms [86]. Furthermore, a shared genetic basis and causal relationship may exist between these two disorders [87]. In alignment with these published studies, our results revealed that PLS2-weighted genes exhibited a stronger association with DGEs of IBD, and SC-FC coupling abnormities showed a significantly negative correlation with gene expression of antipsychotics and lymphocytes.

Cellular irregularities and the abnormal locations of brain neurons are crucial in the onset and progression of psychiatric diseases, such as ASD, MDD, BD, and SCZ [88]. Utilizing the specific marker genes for distinct cell subtypes and cortical layers, our study unveiled significant enrichment of PLS2 + genes in excitatory neurons. The hypothesis of excitatory–inhibitory (E-I) balance offers insights into the mechanisms underlying neuropsychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders [89]. Conversely, the disturbance of E-I balance is implicated in the mechanisms underlying psychiatric disorders including ASD, SCZ, and intellectual disability [90]. Furthermore, our study indicated that PLS2 + weighted genes were primarily concentrated in cortical layers II, III, and IV, while enrichments of layers I and V were observed for PLS2- weighted genes. Our result suggests that the distinctive distribution of different neurons and various connections within abnormal SC-FC couplings may provide valuable insights into the underlying pathogenesis in FES [91].

However, there are still some limitations that need to be considered. Firstly, the AHBA datasets have certain limitations, such as limited sample size (six donors), and only two individuals offering samples from the right hemisphere, potentially introducing biases in statistical analysis. Although we exclusively utilized the transcriptional signatures from the left hemisphere in subsequent analysis, there still lacks connections between gene signature and SC-FC coupling alterations in the right hemisphere. Secondly, despite conducting multiple correlation analyses to explore potential mechanisms of SC-FC coupling abnormities, including evaluation of neurotransmitter, peripheral inflammation, and the enrichment, specific regulatory mechanisms require further investigation and validation through molecular or animal experiments. Thirdly, although our study encompassed a broad range of topics, this breadth somewhat limits the depth of discussion. In future research, we plan to expand our experiments based on these findings, focusing on one or two specific areas. Finally, the limited number of images underscores the necessity for future research involving larger cohorts with a more focused age range to further validate the potential associations between brain structural and functional changes and the molecular mechanisms in FES patients.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study comprehensively identified abnormalities in SC-FC coupling associated with cognitive functions, neurotransmitter activity, and specific transcriptional patterns in FES patients. Specifically, our findings demonstrated that FES patients had SC-FC coupling abnormalities in the VN, SMN, and VAN across various brain regions. These abnormalities correlated with impairments in advanced cognitive functions and alterations in several neurotransmitters. The specific transcriptional pattern revealed substantial enrichment in pathways related to synaptic signaling and inflammation, spatially negative associations with inflammation factors, and specific relationships with excitatory neurons and cortical layers. Collectively, these findings significantly enhance our understanding of SC-FC coupling changes in FES, and further offer crucial insights into examining the complex connections between macrostructural and transcriptional patterns in FES patients.
