Morphology dependent antibacterial activity of zinc oxide nanoparticles against clinically relevant bacteria
Authors: Naila Zubair, Rasha Mohammed Sajet Al-Oqaili, Muhammad Junaid Yousaf, Aisha Siddique, Fuad A Awwad, Emad A A Ismail, Farrah Shams, Adil Aldhahrani, Nizwa Itrat, Farhad Ali, Devendra Pratap Rao, Hina Khalid, Fawad Ali, Fawziah A Al-Salmi, Muneefah Abdullah Alenezi, Husna Irfan Thalib, Jumana Hussain Timraz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study focuses on the antibacterial activity of ZnO nanoparticles, evaluating variations based on morphology. Interest in ZnO nanoparticles arises from their high surface-to-volume ratio that enables effective interaction with bacterial cells. Their antibacterial properties depend on size and nanostructure, with enhanced activity attributed to increased surface area, promoting maximum contact with microbial membranes, cellular damage, and growth inhibition. Zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles with two distinct shapes—ellipsoidal nanorods (Z1) and 3D microspheres (Z2)—were synthesized using a simple, template-free aqueous precipitation method. Zinc nitrate and hexamethylenetetramine (HMT) served as starting materials. The nanoparticles were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). The point of zero charge (PZC) was determined by the salt addition method. SEM images showed that particle shape changed with reaction time. XRD confirmed a hexagonal wurtzite crystal structure, with average crystallite sizes of 22.09 nm for Z1 and 27.18 nm for Z2. FT-IR spectra showed Zn–O bond vibrations between 540 and 411 cm−1. Antibacterial activity was evaluated using the agar well diffusion method against Gram-positive (Streptococcus mutans, Staphylococcus aureus) and Gram-negative (Escherichia coli, Enterobacter cloacae) bacteria. The synthesized ZnO nanoparticles exhibited significantly higher antibacterial effects than commercial ZnO, which showed no activity at tested concentrations (0.25, 0.50, and 0.75 µg/µL). At 0.75 µg/µL, Z1 nanorods produced inhibition zones of 30 mm (E. coli), 28 mm (E. cloacae), 28 mm (S. mutans), and 30 mm (S. aureus). Z2 microspheres showed even stronger effects: 35 mm, 32 mm, 30 mm, and 31 mm, respectively. These findings demonstrate the superior antibacterial properties of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles, particularly the 3D microspheres, highlighting their potential in antimicrobial coatings and biomedical applications.Keywords: ZnO nanoparticles, Controlled precipitation method, Antibacterial activity, Zone of inhibition, Point of zero chargeSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology

Introduction

Bacterial infections pose a major global health concern, particularly with the rapid emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains1. The overuse and misuse of antibiotics have accelerated bacterial resistance, rendering conventional antimicrobial treatments less effective2. MDR bacteria employ various resistance mechanisms, including enzymatic degradation of antibiotics, reduced membrane permeability, active efflux pumps, biofilm formation, and genetic mutations that alter antibiotic targets3. As a result, there is an urgent need for alternative antimicrobial agents that exhibit high efficacy, stability, and a lower potential for resistance development4.

Nanotechnology has emerged as a powerful tool in antimicrobial research, offering novel solutions for combating bacterial infections4. The most important antibacterial activity of ZnO nanoparticles stems from multiple mechanisms, including the generation of reactive oxygen species that cause oxidative stress and physical damage to the bacterial cell membrane, and release of toxic zinc ions. These effects together result in significant structural changes in bacteria (shrinkage, distortion, and collapse), and the shape of individual nanoparticles plays a role in their bactericidal action5. Among various nanomaterials, metal and metal oxide nanoparticles have gained significant attention due to their unique physicochemical properties and potent antibacterial activity6. Zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles have been widely studied for their broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties, low toxicity, high stability, and biocompatibility. ZnO nanoparticles exhibit antibacterial effects through multiple mechanisms, including the release of Zn²⁺ ions, the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and direct interactions with bacterial cell membranes, leading to oxidative stress and membrane disruption7. These multimodal mechanisms significantly reduce the likelihood of bacterial resistance, making ZnO nanoparticles a promising alternative to conventional antibiotics8. ZnO is a group II-VI semiconductor material with a wide bandgap of 3.37 eV and a high exciton binding energy of 60 meV9. It exists in different crystal structures, including hexagonal wurtzite, zinc blende, and rock salt, with the hexagonal wurtzite phase being the most thermodynamically stable10. Due to its excellent physicochemical properties, ZnO nanoparticles have been explored for diverse applications, including antimicrobial coatings, drug delivery, wound healing, biosensors, environmental remediation, and optoelectronics11. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) classifies ZnO as a generally recognized as safe (GRAS) material, further supporting its biomedical and environmental applications12.

Because a complete understanding of the exact antibacterial mechanisms is necessary, only then can one truly maximize their therapeutic application against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria like Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa13,14.

In the context of sustainable development, this study aligns with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (UN SDGs), particularly Goal 3 (Good Health and Well-Being) by contributing to the development of alternative antimicrobial agents to combat infectious diseases15. Additionally, Goal 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation) and Goal 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production) are addressed through the potential application of ZnO nanoparticles in water purification and environmentally friendly synthesis approaches15. The antibacterial activity of ZnO nanoparticles is strongly influenced by their physicochemical properties, including particle size, morphology, surface charge, crystallinity, and synthesis method16. Studies have demonstrated that smaller ZnO nanoparticles exhibit enhanced antibacterial activity due to their higher surface-area-to-volume ratio, which increases bacterial interaction. Additionally, the shape and crystallinity of ZnO nanoparticles affect their ROS generation and Zn²⁺ ion release, which are crucial factors in bacterial inhibition17.

The controlled precipitation method, using hexamethylenetetramine (HMT) as a precursor, is a widely used chemical approach for synthesizing ZnO nanoparticles due to its cost-effectiveness, simplicity, and ability to yield well-defined nanostructures18. HMT plays a dual role in the synthesis process: (i) it serves as a slow-releasing source of ammonia, which hydrolyzes to generate hydroxyl ions, thereby facilitating Zn²⁺ complexation and Zn(OH)₂ formation, and (ii) it regulates nucleation and growth kinetics, promoting specific morphologies such as ellipsoidal nanorods at shorter reaction times and hierarchical 3D microspheres at prolonged reaction durations19. The morphology of ZnO nanoparticles is further influenced by reaction temperature, where higher temperatures enhance nucleation rates and crystal growth, leading to variations in crystallite size and antibacterial activity20. The concentration of zinc nitrate in the precursor solution also affects the final yield and properties of ZnO nanoparticles, influencing their structural and functional attributes21. To improve the biocompatibility and biodegradability of ZnO nanoparticles, surface modification with biocompatible coatings (e.g., polymers or peptides) can reduce toxicity and enhance stability22. Additionally, green synthesis methods using natural extracts or biomolecules can promote eco-friendly production. Tailoring size, shape, and surface charge further optimizes their interaction with biological systems23. The green synthesis methods can be scaled up for large-scale production of ZnO nanoparticles by optimizing reaction conditions, such as temperature, pH, and precursor concentrations, and employing continuous flow or automated systems24. However, challenges like maintaining uniformity in size, shape, and properties during scaling must be addressed25. Despite the established antimicrobial properties of ZnO nanoparticles, commercial ZnO powders often exhibit negligible antibacterial activity within the tested concentration range26. This discrepancy highlights the importance of optimizing synthesis parameters to enhance bioactivity27. The point of zero charge (PZC) of ZnO nanoparticles plays a crucial role in their interaction with bacterial cells, influencing electrostatic attraction, surface adsorption, and subsequent antibacterial effects28.

This study investigates the synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles via the controlled precipitation method and evaluates their antibacterial efficacy using the agar well diffusion assay29. The structural, morphological, and functional properties of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles are comprehensively analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)30. Antimicrobial activity is assessed against clinically relevant Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial strains, and the performance of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles is compared to commercial ZnO. Recent studies have further highlighted the critical role of nanoparticle morphology in determining antibacterial performance31. ZnO nanorods exhibited stronger antibacterial activity than spherical particles, owing to their higher surface area and aspect ratio, which enhanced membrane interaction and ion release32. Similarly, the hierarchical ZnO structures, such as flower-like morphologies, generate higher levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), resulting in superior antimicrobial action33,34. The morphology directly influences Zn²⁺ ion dissolution kinetics and bacterial membrane disruption pathways34. These findings strengthen the rationale for exploring morphology-engineered ZnO nanostructures for biomedical use. This research aims to elucidate the correlation between ZnO nanoparticle morphology and antibacterial efficacy, providing insights into their potential biomedical applications, including wound dressings, antimicrobial coatings, and drug delivery systems35. By addressing key synthesis parameters and antibacterial mechanisms, this study contributes to the rational design of ZnO-based antimicrobial materials with enhanced performance and broader36.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Zinc nitrate [Zn(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O] and hexamethylenetetramine (HMT) were procured from reputed chemical suppliers. All reagents, including zinc nitrate hexahydrate (99.9% purity) and hexamethylenetetramine (98% purity), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Distilled water was used for the preparation of all solutions to ensure the absence of contaminants that could interfere with nanoparticle synthesis or bacterial assays37.

Bacterial strains

The bacterial strains used in this study included Escherichia coli, Enterobacter cloacae, Streptococcus mutans, and Staphylococcus aureus. These strains were obtained from the Department of Biochemistry, Abdul Wali Khan University, Mardan, Pakistan. The bacterial cultures were maintained on nutrient agar plates and stored at 4 °C for further use38.

Synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles

ZnO nanoparticles were synthesized using a hexamethylenetetramine (HMT)-assisted method36. Briefly, an aqueous solution of HMT was prepared by dissolving 2.0 g of HMT in 60 mL of distilled water. Separately, a zinc precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 24.0 g of zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O) in distilled water. The HMT solution was then slowly added to the zinc precursor solution under continuous stirring at 60 °C. The reaction mixture was maintained at this temperature for 2 h to allow the hydrolysis of HMT, which released ammonia (NH₃) and formaldehyde (CH₂O), controlling the pH and directing the growth of ZnO nanostructures. The formation of a white precipitate indicated the successful synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles. The precipitate was separated by vacuum filtration and washed thoroughly with ethanol and deionized water to remove any unreacted precursors or impurities. The washed precipitate was dried in an air oven at 70 °C for 6 h and subsequently calcined at 400 °C for 1 h to obtain crystalline ZnO nanoparticles. To investigate the effect of HMT concentration on the properties of the synthesized nanoparticles, three samples were prepared by varying the HMT content: 0.5 g, 2.0 g, and 4.0 g. A control sample (pure ZnO) was also synthesized without the addition of HMT for comparison. Throughout the synthesis process, the pH of the reaction mixture was maintained at 739.

Characterization of ZnO nanoparticles

The synthesized ZnO nanoparticles were characterized using various analytical techniques to determine their morphological, structural, and physicochemical properties40. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed using a JEOL JSM-5910 instrument (Japan) to examine the surface morphology of the nanoparticles41. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was conducted to identify functional groups42 and confirm the formation of ZnO nanoparticles in the synthesized samples, with spectra recorded using a Shimadzu IR Prestige-21 spectrometer through the KBr pellet method18. The crystallinity and phase composition of the nanoparticles were analyzed through X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a JEOL JDX-3532 diffractometer operated at 30 mA current and 40 kV voltage, utilizing Cu-Kα radiation with a wavelength of 1.54 Å43 The thermal stability of the ZnO nanoparticles was assessed through thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential thermal analysis (DTA) using a Perkin Elmer Diamond series instrument44 The specific surface area was determined using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method with a NOVA 2200e surface area analyzer (Quantachrome, USA). Furthermore, the point of zero charge (PZC) of the ZnO nanostructures was determined using the salt addition method, which is critical for optimizing the pH conditions necessary for antibacterial activity evaluation45.

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles was evaluated against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial strains using the agar well diffusion method46. The selected bacterial strains included S. aureus and S. mutans as representatives of Gram-positive bacteria, while E. coli and E. cloacae were tested as Gram-negative strains. Fresh bacterial cultures were prepared at a concentration of 7.5 × 10⁶ CFU/50 µL and uniformly spread onto agar plates to ensure even bacterial growth. The bacterial inoculation concentration was standardized by adjusting the optical density (OD) of the bacterial suspension to a specific value (e.g., OD600 ≈ 0.1, corresponding to 7.5 × 10⁶ CFU/50 µL) using a spectrophotometer47. This ensured consistent bacterial density across all antimicrobial activity tests. Wells of 8 mm diameter were carefully created using sterile inverted microtips, and a 20 µL suspension of ZnO nanoparticles was introduced into each well at varying concentrations of 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75 µg/µL. The inoculated plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h under standard conditions to allow bacterial growth and interaction with the nanoparticles. The antibacterial activity was assessed by measuring the zone of inhibition around the wells, which indicated the extent of bacterial susceptibility to the ZnO nanoparticles. The efficacy of the synthesized nanoparticles was compared against ciprofloxacin, which was used as a positive control48. Ciprofloxacin was selected as the positive control due to its broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, well-documented efficacy against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, and its widespread use as a standard reference in antimicrobial studies49. The stability of the ZnO nanoparticle dispersion was ensured by using a suitable dispersing agent, such as surfactants and sonication, to prevent aggregation50. The dispersion was monitored for uniformity and stability throughout the antimicrobial activity tests. To ensure the reliability of the results, all experiments were performed in triplicate, minimizing variability and enhancing the reproducibility of the findings51.

XRD analysis of ZnO nanoparticles

The crystalline and phase composition of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD). The XRD patterns for the Z1 and Z2 samples were recorded over a 2θ range of 10°–80° (Fig. 3). The presence of sharp diffraction peaks confirmed the crystalline nature of the ZnO nanoparticles. All the observed diffraction peaks matched well with the standard reference data (ICCD No. 50664), verifying the formation of ZnO in its pure phase. The diffraction peaks appeared at 2θ values of 31.73°, 34.29°, 36.24°, 47.65°, 56.67°, 62.69°, and 66.93°, corresponding to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), and (200) lattice planes, respectively. These reflections are characteristic of the hexagonal wurtzite crystal structure of ZnO. Notably, no additional peaks corresponding to secondary phases or impurities were detected, confirming the high purity of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles.

FT-IR analysis of ZnO nanoparticles

FT-IR spectroscopy was used to identify the functional groups present on the surface of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles and to confirm their formation. The FT-IR spectra were recorded over a wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm− 1. Several absorption bands were observed in this range due to the vibrational frequencies of various functional groups present in the sample. A strong absorption band observed between 445 and 556 cm− 1 was assigned to the Zn–O stretching vibration, confirming the successful synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles. The presence of hydroxyl (-OH) functional groups was indicated by a broad absorption band in the range of 3200–3500 cm− 1, corresponding to O–H stretching vibrations. Additionally, absorption bands observed in the region of 1400–1600 cm− 1 were attributed to O–H bending vibrations (Fig. 4). These functional groups suggest the presence of surface hydroxyl species, which may influence the dispersion and stability of ZnO nanoparticles.

TGA analysis of ZnO nanoparticles

To evaluate the thermal stability and decomposition behavior of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted. The samples were heated from ambient temperature to 800 °C under a controlled atmosphere, and the corresponding TGA curves are presented in Fig. 5. The TGA curves reveal that the primary weight loss occurred in the temperature range of 30–400 °C. This weight reduction is attributed to the desorption of physically adsorbed water molecules and residual organic species on the surface of the ZnO nanoparticles. Notably, beyond 400 °C, no further weight loss was observed, indicating the thermal stability of the ZnO nanostructures at higher temperatures (Fig. 5).

Point of zero charge (PZC) of ZnO nanoparticles

The PZC of ZnO samples was determined using the salt addition method, and the results are presented in Fig. 7. ∆pH vs. initial pH (pHi) curves for all ZnO samples intersected at ∆pH = 0, indicating a PZC value of approximately 9.3. Below this pH, ZnO nanoparticles exhibited a net positive charge, while above this pH, they carried a net negative charge. Slight variations in ∆pH values among different ZnO samples were observed, reflecting differences in surface charge density. These differences suggest that the PZC of ZnO samples is composition-dependent, while the surface charge at the same pH varies with particle shape and size. The results confirm that ZnO nanoparticles exhibit charge-dependent behavior, which is crucial for their interaction in various applications (Fig. 7).

Antibacterial activity

Zone of inhibition analysis

The antibacterial activity of ZnO nanoparticles (Z1 and Z2) was evaluated against E. coli, E. cloacae, S. mutans, and S. aureus using the agar well diffusion method. The inhibition zones were measured at three different concentrations (0.25, 0.50, and 0.75 µg/µL) to assess the dose-dependent antibacterial response of the nanoparticles. The results, as summarized in Table 2, demonstrate that both ZnO formulations exhibited significant antibacterial effects, with Z2 consistently showing greater inhibition than Z1 across all tested concentrations. A clear dose-dependent trend was observed, where increasing the concentration of ZnO nanoparticles led to a corresponding increase in bacterial growth inhibition. Among the tested bacterial strains, S. aureus exhibited the highest susceptibility to ZnO nanoparticles, with Z2 producing an inhibition zone of 31.39 ± 0.05 mm at the highest concentration (0.75 µg/µL). This suggests that Gram-positive bacteria, particularly S. aureus, are more vulnerable to ZnO nanoparticles. Conversely, E. cloacae displayed the lowest sensitivity, with an inhibition zone of 21.92 ± 0.1 mm for Z2 at the lowest concentration (0.25 µg/µL). This variation in bacterial susceptibility may be attributed to differences in the structural composition of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial cell walls, which influence their interactions with ZnO nanoparticles. The antibacterial performance of Z1, although significant, was consistently lower than that of Z2 at all concentrations. For instance, at 0.75 µg/µL, the inhibition zone of Z1 against S. aureus was 27.64 ± 0.07 mm, whereas Z2 produced a larger inhibition zone of 31.39 ± 0.05 mm under the same conditions. The enhanced antibacterial activity of Z2 suggests that specific physicochemical characteristics, such as particle size, morphology, and surface charge, play a crucial role in determining the efficacy of ZnO nanoparticles against bacterial pathogens.

Comparative analysis with previous studies

To contextualize the antibacterial efficacy of the synthesized ZnO NPs, a comparison was made with previous investigations (Table 3). Various synthesis methods such as precipitation, sol-gel, hydrothermal, and co-precipitation techniques have been reported in the literature, yielding ZnO NPs of different morphologies and antibacterial activities. The disc diffusion and agar well diffusion methods were commonly employed to assess antibacterial potential against pathogens such as E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and S. aureus. Previous studies have reported ZOI values ranging from as low as 1.5 mm (co-precipitation method) to as high as 27 mm (sol-gel and hydrothermal methods) at different concentrations. For instance, ZnO NPs synthesized via the sol-gel method exhibited inhibition zones of 7.2 mm for E. coli at 100 µg/mL, whereas hydrothermally synthesized ZnO nanotubes showed a ZOI of 27 mm for Pseudomonas aeruginosa at 3.75 mg/mL. Comparatively, the present study demonstrates superior antibacterial activity, with ZOI values of 25 mm to 28 mm for the controlled precipitation method at a significantly lower concentration of 0.25 µg/mL. ZnO nanorods synthesized in this study exhibited ZOI values of 25 mm for E. coli, E. cloacae, S. mutans, and S. aureus. The 3D microsphere ZnO NPs demonstrated even higher inhibition, particularly against S. mutans (28 mm) and E. coli (27 mm). These results suggest that the controlled precipitation method used in this study effectively enhances the antibacterial properties of ZnO NPs, making them a potential candidate for antimicrobial applications. The significant inhibition observed at lower concentrations compared to previously reported ZnO NPs highlights the improved efficacy of our synthesized nanoparticles.

Discussion

The synthesis and characterization of ZnO nanoparticles provide deep mechanistic insights into their nucleation, growth dynamics, structural evolution, and functional performance, particularly their antibacterial activity63. The reaction time during synthesis serves as a fundamental parameter influencing the physicochemical properties of ZnO nanoparticles, including their morphology, crystallinity, surface charge, and aggregation behavior, all of which contribute to their antimicrobial efficacy64. The synthesis and characterization of ZnO nanoparticles delineate an in-depth mechanistic analysis of nucleation, growth dynamics, structural evolution, and their functional performance within the specific frame of their antibacterial activity. Intensive studies carried out recently on nanocomposites and modified ZnO also affirm that synthesis parameters, material properties, and ultimately biological performance are closely linked. Such scientific notice can be viewed, for example, in the application of green synthesis methods with plant extracts as effective reducing and capping agents, as demonstrated65 in their research on Ag- and Se-dual-doped ZnO-CaO-CuO nanocomposites14,66 and Ag-doped ZnO–MgO–CaO nanocomposites67. These investigations further support that the addition of dopants and construction of multi-component systems modify the morphology and crystalline structure of the material to yield superior photocatalytic and cytotoxic effects, as is understood for the improvement of a composite substance to enhance the effectiveness of a core material by modifying specific properties, specific surface area particularly, an important factor for antibacterial mechanism.

Moreover, the study of Kermani et al. on sol-gel synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles connected processing conditions with the properties of the final product. Their results showed that increasing the calcination temperature resulted in higher nanoparticle sizes, which directly correspond to their photocatalytic and antibacterial properties. This grants importance in accurately controlling the synthesis parameters to modify the physical properties of the nanoparticle for the best functional efficiency. Their research indicates that the produced ZnO nanoparticles exhibit potent antibacterial and anticancer properties, making them strong candidates for medical use65.

The detailed characterization of ZnO nanoparticles is consistent with the general body of research that explores their structural evolution and growth. The outcome of these studies strengthens the point that the functional performance features of ZnO nanoparticles, such as their antibacterial efficacy, are intrinsically related to their structural, morphological, and chemical properties, which can be controlled in a precise manner through the intelligent selection of synthesis methods and conditions. The biocompatibility and biodegradability of ZnO nanoparticles can be improved by surface modification with biocompatible coatings (e.g., polymers, peptides, or lipids) and employing green synthesis methods using natural extracts63. However, potential cytotoxicity concerns related to ZnO nanoparticles include the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and cellular damage at high concentrations68. The surface modifications and controlled dosing can mitigate these effects, enhancing biocompatibility for biomedical applications69. Optimizing size, shape, and surface charge further enhances their interaction with biological systems while reducing toxicity70. ZnO nanoparticle research holds immense potential in biomedical, environmental, and energy applications, driven by their unique physicochemical properties71. However, challenges such as toxicity, scalability, stability, and environmental impact must be addressed to fully realize their potential72. Therefore, future studies should focus on green synthesis, mechanistic understanding, and standardization to advance this promising field73. In current studies, the controlled synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles via HMT decomposition in an aqueous medium was governed by a series of chemical equilibria that dictated the availability of reactants and the kinetics of nanoparticle formation. HMT acts as a pH buffer and a slow-release ammonia source, decomposing thermally or hydrolytically to produce ammonia (NH₃), which in turn hydrolyzes in water to generate hydroxyl ions (OH⁻)73. These hydroxyl ions facilitate the in situ formation of zinc hydroxide intermediates [Zn(OH)₂], which serve as precursors to ZnO nanoparticles73. The transformation of Zn(OH)₂ to ZnO occurred via dehydration, with the removal of water molecules leading to structural reorganization and phase transition. The kinetics of nucleation and growth are highly dependent on the local supersaturation of Zn²⁺ and OH⁻74, which was modulated by the decomposition rate of HMT.

The morphological evolution of ZnO nanoparticles is a dynamic process driven by the interplay between nucleation, crystal growth, Ostwald ripening, and self-assembly. At shorter reaction times (15–30 min), rapid nucleation dominates, leading to the formation of anisotropic ZnO nanorods75. This growth preference arises due to the intrinsic anisotropy of ZnO’s wurtzite crystal structure, where the [001] direction exhibits faster growth compared to lateral facets, resulting in elongated nanostructures76. The presence of hydroxyl groups on the ZnO surface further influenced the preferential adsorption of Zn²⁺ ions, directing growth along specific crystallographic planes. As the reaction progresses (45–60 min), secondary growth mechanisms, including Ostwald ripening and self-assembly, become more pronounced77.

The formation of 3D microspheres at prolonged reaction times was conducted by self-assembly mechanisms that involve electrostatic and van der Waals interactions between primary ZnO nanorods78. The driving force for self-assembly stems from surface energy minimization, where individual nanorods aggregate into clusters that reorganize into spherical architectures. This self-assembly process was further influenced by the presence of residual organic species or surface hydroxyl groups, which can act as binding sites, facilitating the oriented attachment of nanorods. In addition to electrostatic interactions, the self-assembly of ZnO nanorods into 3D hierarchical structures was promoted by dipole-dipole interactions arising from the intrinsic polar nature of the ZnO lattice. The wurtzite structure of ZnO exhibits spontaneous polarization along the c-axis, resulting in differential surface charges on opposite crystal planes. These charge imbalances create attractive forces between adjacent nanorods, guiding their alignment and fusion into larger, more complex structures79. The resulting 3D microspheres exhibited highly porous architecture with an increased surface area, enhancing their reactivity and functional properties, particularly their interactions with bacterial cells.

XRD analysis confirmed the formation of pure-phase ZnO nanoparticles (JCPDS card No. 36-1451) with a hexagonal wurtzite structure, exhibiting sharp diffraction peaks indicative of high crystallinity. The variation in peak intensities between the nanorods (Z1) and microspheres (Z2) reflects differences in preferred crystallographic orientation, influenced by growth dynamics and surface interactions. The increase in crystallite size from 22.09 nm (Z1) to 27.18 nm (Z2) was consistent with the hierarchical self-assembly process, where secondary growth mechanisms contributed to the formation of larger, more complex structures. The structural evolution aligns with theoretical models describing anisotropic growth in ZnO, where preferential deposition along the c-axis dominates in early-stage formation, while lateral growth becomes more significant in extended reaction conditions80. The presence of surface hydroxyl groups, identified through FT-IR spectroscopy, played a crucial role in the dispersion, stability, and reactivity of ZnO nanoparticles. Hydroxyl groups, evident in the broad absorption band between 3200 and 3500 cm−1, facilitated hydrogen bonding and surface interactions, which are critical in determining biological activity. The presence of Zn-O functional groups, confirmed by FT-IR peaks in the range of 400–500 cm−1, is directly linked to antibacterial activity. These groups facilitated the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which damage bacterial cell membranes and protein81. TGA further confirmed the stability of ZnO nanoparticles, with minimal weight loss beyond 400 °C, indicating that the synthesized nanoparticles are suitable for applications requiring thermal resilience, such as catalysis and high-temperature coatings.

The hierarchical ZnO microspheres (Z2) exhibited a significantly higher BET surface area (69.157 m²/g) compared to nanorods (Z1, 37.235 m²/g), demonstrating that morphology dictates surface accessibility and potential for bacterial interactions. The PZC analysis revealed that ZnO nanoparticles exhibit pH-dependent charge behavior, with a PZC of approximately 9.3. This property influences interactions with bacterial cell walls, which are negatively charged due to the presence of lipopolysaccharides in Gram-negative bacteria and teichoic acids in Gram-positive bacteria82. The ability of ZnO nanoparticles to modulate their surface charge enhances their affinity for bacterial membranes, facilitating membrane disruption and cellular penetration83. Determining the PZC is crucial as it influences the surface charge and interaction of ZnO nanoparticles with bacterial cells28. A pH near the PZC enhances adsorption and antibacterial efficacy by optimizing electrostatic interactions84.

The antibacterial efficacy of ZnO nanoparticles was evaluated against both Gram-positive (S. aureus, S. mutans) and Gram-negative (E. coli, E. cloacae) bacteria, revealing a dose-dependent inhibition effect using disc diffusion and agar well diffusion methods85. While these methods are widely used for preliminary screening, they have certain limitations86. Variability in diffusion rates due to nanoparticle size and solubility, as well as potential uneven distribution in the agar, might affect the reproducibility and accuracy of the results87. Additionally, the disc diffusion method may be influenced by the loading efficiency of nanoparticles onto the discs88. Despite these limitations, both methods provided consistent and valuable insights into the antibacterial efficacy of the nanoparticles. The hierarchical microspheres (Z2) demonstrated superior antibacterial activity compared to nanorods (Z1), attributed to their increased surface area and structural complexity, which enhances bacterial adhesion and nanoparticle-cell interactions. The antibacterial mechanism is multifaceted, involving Zn²⁺ ion release, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and direct membrane interactions. ROS, including hydroxyl radicals (⋅OH), superoxide anions (O₂⁻), and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), are produced through ZnO-mediated photocatalysis and surface redox reactions89. These highly reactive species induce oxidative stress, leading to lipid peroxidation, protein denaturation, and nucleic acid damage, ultimately resulting in bacterial cell death90. The nanoscale size of ZnO enhances its ability to penetrate bacterial membranes, amplifying its bactericidal effect26. The results highlighted the potential of hierarchical ZnO nanostructures for applications in antimicrobial coatings, wound dressings, and biomedical devices. Future studies should focus on elucidating molecular-level interactions governing bacterial inhibition and exploring potential synergistic effects between ZnO nanoparticles and conventional antibiotics, which could further enhance antimicrobial efficacy. The findings from this study provide a mechanistic foundation for the rational design of ZnO-based antimicrobial materials, paving the way for their application in healthcare and environmental sanitation11. Furthermore, Future studies could explore the application of machine learning techniques to predict the antibacterial activity of ZnO nanoparticles based on their physicochemical properties91. By leveraging datasets encompassing size, morphology, surface charge, and other parameters, predictive models could be developed to optimize nanoparticle synthesis and performance. This approach holds promise for accelerating the discovery of next-generation antibacterial nanomaterials92.

The Fig. 10 illustrates the antibacterial mechanism of ZnO nanoparticles (ZnO NPs), highlighting both direct and indirect pathways leading to bacterial cell death. Initially, ZnO NPs interact with the bacterial cell membrane, causing structural disruption and increased permeability, ultimately leading to cell wall destruction. Simultaneously, ZnO NPs facilitate the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide anions (O₂⁻), hydroxyl radicals (⋅OH), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which induce oxidative stress within the bacterial cell. The ROS attack critical biomolecules, resulting in protein denaturation, enzyme inhibition, and severe DNA damage, impairing cellular function and leading to bacterial death. The combined effects of direct membrane damage and intracellular oxidative stress make ZnO NPs highly effective antimicrobial agents, disrupting bacterial homeostasis and inhibiting growth.

Conclusion

The synthesis, characterization, and antibacterial activity of ZnO nanoparticles were systematically investigated, providing mechanistic insights into their formation, structural evolution, and functional performance. The reaction time during synthesis emerged as a critical determinant of morphology, crystallinity, and surface properties, which directly influence antibacterial efficacy. Controlled decomposition of hexamethylenetetramine (HMT) in an aqueous medium facilitated the formation of ZnO nanoparticles with tunable morphologies, ranging from nanorods to hierarchical 3D microspheres. This structural evolution was governed by nucleation kinetics, crystal growth, Ostwald ripening, and self-assembly processes, modulated by reaction conditions. The hierarchical ZnO microspheres (Z2) demonstrated superior antibacterial activity compared to nanorods (Z1), attributed to their higher surface area, enhanced surface reactivity, and greater interaction with bacterial membranes. The antibacterial mechanism was found to be multifaceted, involving Zn²⁺ ion release, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and direct membrane disruption, leading to oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, protein denaturation, and DNA damage, ultimately resulting in bacterial cell death. The pH-dependent surface charge further enhanced bacterial affinity, facilitating cellular penetration and membrane destabilization.

These findings highlight the importance of optimizing synthesis parameters to fine-tune the physicochemical properties of ZnO nanoparticles for specific biomedical applications. The hierarchical ZnO microspheres, with their high surface area and structural complexity, demonstrate strong potential for antimicrobial coatings, wound dressings, and biomedical devices. Future research should focus on elucidating the molecular-level interactions between ZnO nanoparticles and bacterial cells using advanced spectroscopic and imaging techniques. Additionally, exploring the synergistic effects of ZnO nanoparticles with conventional antibiotics and developing composite materials incorporating ZnO could further enhance antimicrobial efficacy. Long-term stability, cytotoxicity, and biocompatibility studies are also essential for translating these findings into real-world applications in healthcare, environmental sanitation, and advanced functional materials.
