Printable polymer nanocomposites for scalable and architected radiative cooling
Authors: Kai Zhou, Songtao Tang, Pranto Karua, Fukang Wu, Sungmin Hong, Diya Patel, Gwendolyn M. Reeser, Donald M. Cropek, Paul V. Braun, Lili Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Space cooling and lighting together consume 25% of global electricity, yet existing daytime radiative coolers are mostly limited to porous planar coatings that block visible light and lack durability. Here, we introduce rheology-optics coupling as a design principle that links polymer viscoelasticity to particle dispersion and optical scattering. Guided by this principle, we develop printable polydimethylsiloxane-zirconium oxide composites that achieve solar reflectance ( ~ 97.3%) and mid-infrared emissivity ( ~ 96.9%) comparable to the best reported values, despite a low filler loading of only ~4.5 vol.%. These scalable coatings provide up to 7.4 oC sub-ambient cooling and cut electricity use by 37% versus commercial paint in pilot-scale testing, while withstanding mechanical, thermal, and environmental stresses. Beyond planar coatings, the rheology-tunable polydimethylsiloxane-zirconium oxide ink enables direct ink writing of daylight-regulating architectures that deliver sub-ambient radiative cooling while admitting diffuse daylight for illumination, reducing both cooling and lighting demand. This work provides a practical and versatile platform for radiative cooling.

Introduction

Space cooling and lighting are among the largest drivers of global electricity demand, together accounting for nearly one quarter of total use, which impose heavy loads on power grids and drive greenhouse gas emissions1–5. Air conditioning dominates residential demand, while in commercial buildings electric lighting often exceeds cooling loads6,7. With rising global temperatures and accelerating urbanization, reducing both cooling and lighting energy use has become an urgent technological and societal priority3–5.

Daytime radiative cooling offers a passive strategy to reduce cooling demand by reflecting solar heat while emitting thermal radiation to the cold sky, enabling sub-ambient cooling under direct sunlight8–26. This strategy has shown broad potential across building envelopes10,11, wearable fabrics21–23,25, electronics25,27, and industrial systems28,29. Despite this promise, nearly all reported high-performance daytime radiative cooling materials are fabricated as opaque planar films8–20. While effective at rejecting solar heat, these designs block visible light, preventing integration into windows, skylights, or other daylighting systems30,31. In buildings, this limitation can force greater reliance on electric lighting, reducing potential net energy savings 32,33.

Beyond blocking light, existing materials face fundamental barriers that restrict their practical impact. Most high-performance radiative cooling materials are realized as porous coatings optimized for solar scattering (Table S1), resulting in flat, light-blocking formats9–17,20–25. However, high porosity inherently limits geometric versatility and mechanical robustness, leaving these films sensitive to humidity34–36 and prone to environmental degradation and performance loss36–38. Attempts to improve durability using fluorine-containing polymers9,13,37,39 introduce additional constraints, as they depend on costly, specialized chemistries that pose growing environmental concerns40–42. Nonporous polymer composites with particle fillers have also been explored, but they generally underperform in optical performance19,43–49 and typically rely on either costly silver backing layers19,45,46 or high particle loadings (>25 vol.%)47–49. Such high loadings inevitably cause brittleness and poor processability50,51, reflecting a deeper conceptual gap in processing control. Previous studies have largely treated particle-polymer composites as simple mixtures, focusing on optical metrics while overlooking how the polymer matrix and processing conditions govern particle dispersion and stability. Consequently, scattering efficiency at low filler content remains poorly controlled. Collectively, these limitations reveal the absence of a fundamental framework connecting material structure, processing, and optical performance. This critical gap has hindered the progress of radiative cooling toward broadly deployable systems.

Here, we uncover rheology-optics coupling as a design principle that fundamentally links polymer viscoelasticity to particle dispersion and optical scattering in daytime radiative cooling composites. Leveraging this principle, we formulate a scalable and printable polydimethylsiloxane-zirconium oxide (PZO) ink that achieves state-of-the-art solar reflectance (~97.3%) and mid-infrared emissivity (~96.9%) at a remarkably low particle loading of 4.5 vol.%, enabling up to 7.4 °C sub-ambient cooling. Field tests show 37% lower cooling energy use compared to commercial white paint, corresponding to potential national-scale savings of 95–178 TWh annually and avoidance of 64–119 Mt CO2 emissions. Beyond scalable planar coatings, the rheology-tunable PZO ink also enables direct ink writing (DIW) of daylight-regulating radiative cooling architectures that unite sub-ambient cooling with controlled daylight transmission (Fig. 1a). This dual-function design directly addresses the trade-off between thermal regulation and natural illumination, providing a pathway to reduce both cooling and lighting electricity demand. In addition, the ink supports additive manufacturing of intricate, customized 3D geometries, from breathable cooling masks to patterned thermal facades and fluidic thermal systems, demonstrating broad multifunctionality and geometric freedom for diverse practical applications. Results

Rheology-directed control of optical scattering

Efficient daytime radiative cooling requires materials that scatter sunlight strongly across the solar spectrum, while maintaining high mid-infrared emissivity. Our key finding is that this performance depends not only on the choice of high-index fillers, but also on how polymer rheology shapes particle dispersion and size distribution during processing. By tuning viscoelasticity, the matrix controls particle breakup, dispersion, and sedimentation, which in turn determines the collective scattering behavior of the composite 52,53.

To identify optimal fillers, we simulated scattering efficiencies of common dielectric nanoparticles in a polymer host (refractive index n = 1.4). Zirconium oxide (ZrO2) was identified as uniquely effective (Fig. 1b), combining a high refractive index (n = 2.1–2.2) with negligible UV absorption54,55. In contrast, other high-index oxides like TiO2 and ZnO suffer from strong UV absorption56,57, while low-index oxides including Al2O3, SiO2, MgO and BaSO4 (n = 1.4–1.8) provide only weak scattering (Figs. S1 and S2). As a result, ZrO2 achieves broadband scattering efficiencies that not only surpass conventional pigments, but also far exceed those of air pores (Fig. 1c). This indicates that high-index ZrO2 can, in principle, enable highly reflective composites that outperform porous designs. Importantly, scattering efficiency strongly depends on particle size, e.g., submicron particles dominate in the UV-visible range (λ < 0.8 μm), whereas micron-sized particles enhance near-infrared scattering (λ > 0.8 μm). Thus, achieving broadband solar reflectance requires a hierarchical size distribution that can be stabilized within the polymer matrix.

Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) was selected as the host matrix due to its strong emissivity in the 8–13 μm atmospheric window and its tunable viscoelasticity45. We compared three formulations with distinct rheological properties (Fig. 1d), i.e., L-PDMS (low viscosity, hexane-diluted Sylgard 184), M-PDMS (medium viscosity, Sylgard 184), and H-PDMS (high viscosity, SE1700), and observed a direct coupling between rheology and microstructure. In L-PDMS, ZrO₂ particles were finely dispersed with minimal aggregation, but the size distribution was narrow, with most particles ≤0.5 μm (Figs. 1e and h and S3)43,44. In M-PDMS and H-PDMS, reduced shear intensity limits agglomerate breakup, resulting in broader, more balanced distributions extending to 0.7–1.5 μm (Figs. 1f, g, i and j and S3) 52,53.

Critically, L-PDMS and M-PDMS suffered severe sedimentation, with particles settling at the bottom during curing (Figs. 1e, f and S3). In contrast, H-PDMS suppressed sedimentation owing to its pronounced shear-thinning behavior and high zero-shear viscosity, which immobilized particles during processing (Fig. 1g and S3)58,59. The resulting microstructure exhibited uniform spatial dispersion with a broad effective size distribution. This rheology-mediated stabilization strategy provides a fundamental design principle for tuning optical scattering in nonporous composites, enabling broadband solar reflectance and scalable manufacturing within a single material platform. Unlike prior studies that treated particle dispersion primarily as a processing variable, here we establish rheology–optics coupling as a mechanism that directly links polymer viscoelasticity to particle breakup, hierarchical size distribution, and collective scattering behavior. This coupling allows high reflectance at only ~4.5 vol.% filler, overcoming the long-standing need for either metallic backings or >25 vol.% particle loadings in radiative cooling coatings.

Broadband optical response and cooling performance

The effect of rheology on filler dispersion is evident in the optical response. Incorporating ZrO₂ into PDMS converts the polymer from transparent to bright white (Fig. 2a), but the level of reflectance depends strongly on matrix viscoelasticity (Fig. 2b). L-PDMS, representative of the low-viscosity systems commonly used in prior radiative cooling coatings43,44, achieved only 91.5% solar reflectance due to narrow size distribution and severe sedimentation. M-PDMS improved modestly but still suffered from settling of larger aggregates. In contrast, H-PDMS immobilized particles during curing, producing spatially uniform, stable dispersions with broad size distributions. This microstructure enabled superior optical properties, reaching a weighted average solar reflectance of ~97.3% over 0.3–2.0 μm and a weighted average mid-infrared emissivity of ~96.9% over 8–13 µm (Fig. 2b and c), comparable to state-of-the-art daytime radiative coolers (Fig. S4, Tables S1 and S2). Notably, this was achieved with only ~4.5 vol.% ZrO2 at a practical film thickness of 2 mm (Figs. The superiority of ZrO2 as a filler is further confirmed by direct comparison with other oxides (Fig. 2c). Consistent with simulations (Figs. 1b, S1 and S2), ZrO₂-based composites delivered the highest reflectance, outperforming both low-index scatterers (Al₂O₃, SiO₂, MgO, and BaSO₄) and UV-absorbing oxides (TiO₂ and ZnO). These results establish ZrO₂ as the optimal filler and show that rheology-optimized PDMS matrices can fully harness its broadband scattering for scalable, high-performance radiative cooling.

Beyond optical performance, the rheology-optimized PZO ink exhibits shear-thinning behavior that allows smooth extrusion during deposition and rapid viscosity recovery for shape retention, enabling advanced fabrication via DIW with minimum feature size of ~200 µm60. We highlight DIW here as a versatile demonstration platform for architected multifunctionality. Equally important, the low filler loading and rheology control also make the ink compatible with conventional scalable coating methods, such as slot-die coating, blade-casting, screen printing, and roll-to-roll processing (Table S3). Thus, rheology-optics coupling provides a unifying design principle that not only achieves state-of-the-art performance, but also supports both industrial-scale planar coatings and DIW fabrications of multifunctional architectures.

We evaluated the daytime radiative cooling performance of PZO by printing a 20 cm × 20 cm film and testing it outdoors on a building roof in Urbana, Illinois (Fig. 2d). The PZO film, along with a white commercial paint film and a mylar film as controls, was placed on a custom-built apparatus consisting of Styrofoam block wrapped in mylar (Fig. S7). As shown in Figs. 2e and S8, the PZO film achieved an average of 6.6 °C sub-ambient cooling near noontime (10 AM to 2 PM) under peak solar irradiance of 868 W/m261, while both controls remained at or above ambient temperature. A 4-day continuous test confirmed the reliability of its cooling performance, consistently delivering an average sub-ambient reduction of 7.4 °C (Fig. S9). Notably, PZO maintained strong cooling performance both day and night, highlighting its ability to effectively reject solar heat due to its high solar reflectance. The cooling power of PZO was experimentally measured at an average of 104 W/m2 (Figs. 2f and S10), comparable to the best daytime radiative coolers.

To probe real-world impact, we applied the PZO ink coating to the exterior of a shed and compared it against two control sheds, one uncoated and one coated with commercial paint (Fig. 2g), under identical conditions. Each shed was equipped with a portable air conditioner to maintain 25 °C, and cooling energy consumption was monitored. Over a one-month testing period (September 24–October 22, 2024), the PZO-coated shed showed substantial reductions in cooling demand, cutting electricity use by 70% relative to the uncoated shed and 37% relative to the commercial paint-coated shed (Fig. 2h). We note that the sheds were not insulated to the degree of residential buildings, and thus the results should be interpreted as a proof-of-concept demonstration rather than a direct building-scale model. Nevertheless, extrapolating the relative reductions to U.S. residential cooling suggests possible annual electricity savings of 95.4–177.7 TWh and 64.0–119.4 million tons of avoided CO2 emissions (Fig. S1,1 and Table S4)62. While these values represent an order-of-magnitude estimate, they underscore the transformative potential of scalable PZO coatings to reduce both electricity use and emissions at national scale.

Architected structures for dual cooling and daylighting

While planar PZO films exhibit excellent radiative cooling performance, the unique advantage of this ink is its ability to form architected structures. A persistent challenge in building design is balancing thermal regulation with natural daylighting. To illustrate this capability, we used DIW to create a “LumiCool” structure by directly printing tilted PZO fin arrays onto transparent glass substrates (Fig. 3a, b). This geometry leverages the broadband solar reflectance of PZO to block high-angle mid-day sunlight, while still admitting low-angle and diffuse light. To quantify this effect, we simulated the daily direct sunlight duration (Tdsd) as a function of panel tilt angle (θ = θ₁ + θ₃, assuming θ₁ = θ2) and length-to-spacing ratio (R = Length/Spacing) (Fig. 3b, c). The results showed that increasing fin tilt or spacing ratio reduces Tdsd, leading to reduced daytime solar heat gain. When θ exceeds 95° and R > 2.9, Tdsd fell below 1.5 h per day. While admitting low-angle light may slightly increase indoor temperatures during summer mornings or evenings, it provides a beneficial passive heating effect in cold seasons, particularly at high latitudes with long winters.

For experimental validation, we fabricated small-scale house models (200 × 150 × 150 mm) with three rooftop configurations (Fig. 3a). The LumiCool module consisted of a glass panel (130 × 120 mm) with DIW-printed PZO fins inclined at θ₁ ≈ 35° and R ≈ 3, mounted at a roof tilt angle of θ₂ = 60° (Fig. 3a and d). Two control models were also prepared, one with fully transparent glass and another with a continuous planar PZO film. To isolate rooftop effects, all other surfaces were coated with PZO film.

Outdoor testing confirmed the dual functionality of LumiCool. During peak solar hours, it maintained indoor temperatures 2–3 °C below ambient, comparable to the fully PZO-covered roof, whereas the transparent roof overheated by 3–10 °C above ambient (Fig. 3e). At the same time, LumiCool preserved stable indoor illuminance near 1000 lux from 11 AM to 8 PM (Fig. 3f), providing comfortable and functional lighting. In contrast, the fully coated roof reduced illumination to near-darkness (<100 lux), while the transparent roof produced excessive brightness (>5000 lux), causing glare and visual discomfort. Importantly, the LumiCool architecture is tunable, as adjusting the width (W) and spacing (S) allows designers to tailor the balance between lighting comfort and thermal regulation. These results demonstrate the potential of printable PZO ink to create a new class of dual-function architectures and façade systems that unite broadband radiative cooling with optimized daylighting, capabilities beyond the reach of conventional planar coatings.

Expanding multifunctional radiative cooling systems

The versatility of the PZO ink extends radiative cooling into a wide range of multifunctional and geometrically complex applications. For building integration, DIW enables simplified fabrication of structured wall coatings with intricate surface profiles that can be used to enhance cooling performance on vertical facades. As a demonstration, we printed sawtooth-like gratings with ~1 mm pitch and ~33° tilt in a single step (Fig. 4a). These geometries replicate the core structural motifs used in previously reported angularly asymmetric thermal emitters, which traditionally require multi-step lithographic or molding processes63,64. Note that our demonstration focuses on the geometric fabrication stage, without the final directional IR-reflective coating employed in those systems. In addition to performance optimization, DIW demonstrations show the potential for aesthetic and architectural customization. As shown in Fig. 4b, a variety of high-fidelity 2D patterns, including logos, tiled motifs, and pictorial illustrations, were printed with excellent resolution, expanding the design freedom for radiative cooling surfaces that are both functionally efficient and visually distinctive. These demonstrations showcase the design flexibility of the ink for creating architected building components while simplifying fabrication.

The adaptability of the ink also supports conformal and lightweight designs for wearables and electronics systems. Radiative cooling masks can be printed with tunable filament spacing, which combine breathability with passive cooling capability (Fig. 4c). Complex geometries such as honeycomb panels and cylindrical enclosures were also fabricated (Fig. 4d), demonstrating form-fitting cooling shells for irregular components in communication nodes, drones, or autonomous vehicles. These examples highlight the ability of DIW with PZO ink to deliver customized, high-performance passive cooling solutions for next-generation wearable and electronic platforms.

Finally, DIW of PZO ink facilitates seamless integration of radiative cooling with fluidic thermal management. Printed grooved arrays with shallow trenches can promote condensation by increasing the nucleation area and allowing continuous droplet drainage, sustaining high condensation flux. Channeled slabs, printed monolithically without interfacial joints, create enclosed pathways that retain and transport water while limiting heat gain from the surroundings, thereby preserving low temperatures for cold-water generation and storage (Fig. 4e). Together, these topographically tailored structures illustrate the capability of PZO ink via DIW to unite radiative cooling with fluid transport, advancing applications in steam condensation, dew harvesting, and cold-water production.

Durability for real-world deployment

Beyond multifunctional design flexibility, practical deployment also requires long-term durability. The PZO composite displayed exceptional mechanical resilience, withstanding up to 500% strain without failure (Figs. 5a and b and S12), owing to the flexible silicone matrix and the absence of stress-concentrating pores that are prone to fracture. In abrasion tests following ASTM standard D4060, PZO showed nearly half the mass loss of commercial paint after 2000 cycles, underscoring superior wear resistance for long-lasting performance (Fig. 5c, Fig. S13 and Table S5). Furthermore, to ensure versatility across architectural surfaces, the ink was directly printed onto diverse substrates, including wood, glass, acrylic, and metal, without pretreatment (Fig. S14). The ink adhered strongly to the substrates, forming uniform coatings with pull-off adhesion strength (ASTM D4541) comparable to commercial paints (Figs. 5d and S15). The composite also retained optical performance under repeated thermal cycling (–15 °C to 50 °C) and remained stable at extreme cold (–196 °C; Fig. 5e and S16). Under accelerated UV exposure over a month65, no degradation was observed (Fig. 5f and S17). Discussion

We establish rheology-optics coupling as a design principle for scalable, high-performance radiative cooling. This fundamental link between polymer viscoelasticity and optical scattering in particle composites enabled a printable PDMS-ZrO2 (PZO) ink with state-of-the-art solar reflectance (97.3%) and mid-infrared emissivity (96.9%) at only ~4.5 vol.% filler loading. This low loading avoids brittleness and poor adhesion in highly filled paints, while supporting both industrial-scale coating methods (e.g., blade coating, slot-die, screen printing, roll-to-roll) and direct ink writing (DIW). With DIW, the PZO ink enables architected multifunctional systems, including daylight-regulating radiative cooling architectures (LumiCool), patterned thermal facades, wearable cooling masks, conformal electronic enclosures, and fluidic thermal devices. The PZO composite also exhibits exceptional durability against mechanical, thermal, and environmental stresses, ensuring stability for real-world deployment. Pilot-scale field tests confirmed substantial reductions in cooling demand compared to conventional coatings, translating to gigawatt-hour electricity savings and tens of millions of tons of avoided CO2 emissions at national scale. Together, these results establish rheology–optics coupling as a key design framework that unites fundamental optical control, scalable manufacturing, and architectural versatility, advancing radiative cooling from simple coatings toward practical, multifunctional systems poised to address urgent global needs in sustainable buildings, electronics, wearables, and industrial systems.

Methods

Materials and fabrication methods

H-PDMS and M-PDMS refer to SE1700 base and Sylgard 184 base, respectively. L-PDMS was obtained by further diluting Sylgard 184 base with hexane at a weight ratio of 4:1 (hexane:Sylgard 184). For the PZO ink formulations, the weight ratios of PDMS to ZrO₂ particle fillers were set at 10:2, 10:3, and 10:4, corresponding to volume fractions of approximately 3.0%, 4.5%, and 5.9%, respectively. The curing agent to polymer base ratio was kept at 1:10 by weight. All components were thoroughly blended using a centrifugal mixer (Speedmixer, FlackTek) at 2000 rpm for 2 min to remove the bubbles. The particle fillers were purchased (US Research Nanomaterials, Inc.) and used as received. Fig. S19 presents the Scanning electron microscope (SEM) image of the commercial ZrO₂ particles. The ZrO₂ sample consists mainly of fine particles with diameters ranging from 0.1 to 0.4 µm. In addition to these primary particles, larger features (~2–3 µm diameter) are observed, indicating significant particle aggregation.

Direct ink writing process

DIW fabrication was conducted at room temperature using a customized printing system (AGS-1000, Aerotech Inc). During the printing process, the gantry system, which carries the ink cartridge, moves along the X-Y-Z directions, while a pressure dispenser connected to the ink cartridge drives the ink through the tapered dispensing tip (200 μm—1 mm diameter) onto the substrate. Printing paths for the designed patterns were created using computer-aid design software (AutoCAD) and converted to G-code using commercial software (CADFusion). Post-printing curing was carried out either at room temperature for 24 h or in a heated oven (e.g., 6 h at 70 °C) to enable thermal crosslinking and solidify the printed structure.

Rheology measurement

The viscosity of the composite ink was measured by a rheometer (DHR-3; TA Instrument, Inc.) equipped with a flat geometry plate (25 mm diameter). The temperature was maintained at 20 °C throughout the measurements. Prior to testing, the ink was carefully dispensed onto the plate to avoid introducing air bubbles, and any excess ink was removed. The measurement gap was set to 1000 μm, and each composition was tested at least three times to ensure reliable results. All viscosity measurements were conducted within 1 hour of mixing.

Morphology and optical characterization

SEM images and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy elemental mapping were acquired using a Hitachi S4800. UV–Vis-NIR reflectance spectra were measured using a Varian Cary 5 G spectrometer equipped with an integrating sphere (DRA 2500, Agilent). Infrared emissivity and reflectance were characterized by an FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Nicolet iS50 FTIR) equipped with a gold integrating sphere (PIKE Technology).

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) analysis

Micro-CT was performed using a Zeiss Xradia 630 Versa system housed at the Beckman Institute for Advanced Science and Technology, University of Illinois Urbana–Champaign. Rectangular specimens (~5 × 2 × 0.5 mm³) were cut from the cured PDMS–ZrO₂ composites and mounted on the rotary stage for scans at an isotropic voxel size of 500 nm and a field of view of 1016 μm × 1016 μm. Scans were collected with a 25 W Tungsten source X-ray beam at a tube voltage of 80 kV and 1601 sequential projections with an exposure time of 13 s over 180° rotation. The radiographic projections were manually corrected by the alignment of center shifts and reconstructed using XradiaReconstructor (Carl Zeiss). The reconstructed 3D volume of film composite was imported into Avizo version (version 2019.1, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for volume rendering to visualize the internal dispersion of ZrO₂ particles within the PDMS matrix.

LumiCool house model test

The LumiCool rooftop tests were performed on a small-scale house model (200 × 150 × 150 mm), as shown in Fig. 3. Each LumiCool module consisted of a 130 × 120 mm soda-lime glass substrate, onto which an array of parallel, evenly spaced PZO panels was printed using direct ink writing (DIW). The panels featured an inclination angle of θ₁ ≈ 35° and a length-to-spacing ratio of R ≈ 3 (Fig. 3d). The modules were installed on both roof slopes of the test house at a fixed tilt angle of θ₂ = 60°. For comparison, two additional small-scale house models of identical dimensions were prepared as control groups: one with a fully transparent glass rooftop (Fig. 3a, left), and the other with a rooftop uniformly covered by a PZO film of ~3 mm thickness (Fig. 3a, center). All other exposed surfaces of the tested houses were covered with identical PZO films. K-type thermocouples were used to continuously monitor the temperature on the inner surface of each rooftop.

Water contact angle measurement

Water contact angle measurements were performed using a Ramé-Hart Model 250 Contact Angle Goniometer. Reported values were the average of measurements taken at four different locations on the same sample.

Abrasion and pull-off tests

The abrasion test was conducted according to ASTM D4060. Mass loss of the samples was measured at 500-cycle intervals throughout the 2000-cycle test using CS-10 calibrase with a 500 g load. Adhesion strength was evaluated on various substrates using the pull-off test following ASTM D4541, Method E, with sample thickness kept below 2 mm.

Outdoor thermal measurements

Outdoor thermal measurements were performed using a custom-built experimental apparatus made of Styrofoam boxes. The setup was placed on a wood table covered with Mylar film to minimize parasitic heat loss. The measurements were conducted on the roof of a building in Urbana, Illinois of the U.S. (40.11° N, 88.21° W; 225 m altitude). The temperatures of the PZO film, commercial paint, and Mylar film were measured by fast-response T-type surface thermocouples (SA1-T-72; Omega Engineering Inc.), while the ambient air temperature was recorded by a sheathed T-type thermocouple (TTIN-116G-12; Omega Engineering Inc.). All temperature data were recorded with a data acquisition device (DAQ, USB2408; Measurement Computing Corp.). The radiative cooling power measurement was performed using a similar setup. An electrical heater was placed beneath the testing sample, with its heating power adjusted in real-time using a LabVIEW program. The program monitored the temperatures of the PZO film and ambient air, compared their difference, and adjusted the heating power to ensure the film temperature closely matched the ambient temperature. By maintaining equal temperatures for the PZO film and ambient air, convective and conductive heat transfer were eliminated. Under these conditions, the electrical heating power, fully dissipated through thermal radiation, equated to the net radiative cooling power.

Electricity consumption measurement

To evaluate large-scale applicability and energy-saving potential, three identical sheds were installed at the U.S. Army Construction Engineering Research Laboratory (CERL) in Champaign, IL. The sheds were assigned to three experimental groups: one coated with PZO radiative cooling ink, one with commercial white paint, and one left uncoated. Each shed was equipped with a portable air conditioner (LP1021BHSM, LG Electronics) and set to maintain an internal temperature of 25 °C. The air conditioner units were connected to power data loggers (ELITEpro XCTM, Dent Instruments) to record electricity usage at one-minute intervals. The collected data was analyzed to compare the power consumption of the PZO coated shed against that of uncoated and white-painted sheds.

Tensile tests

Tensile testing was performed using a dynamic mechanical analyzer (DMA, Q800, TA Instruments) on DIW-printed samples with approximate dimensions of 40 × 2 × 1 mm. The samples were prepared by printing at room temperature, followed by thermal curing at 70 °C for 6 h. The DMA tests were conducted with a preload of 0.1 N and a loading rate of 3 N/min, up to a maximum possible load of 18 N at 20 °C.

Thermal stability tests

Thermal stability was evaluated through thermal cycling and extreme-cold tests. The thermal cycling test involved 15 consecutive cycles of 24-h heating, followed by 24-hour cooling. Heating was performed in a forced-convection oven at 50 °C, while cooling was conducted in a freezer at −15 °C. For the extreme cold test, the PZO samples were immersed in liquid nitrogen for 24 h.

UV, water, and flame resistance tests

The UV exposure test was conducted using a custom-built setup. UV radiation, with a total power density of ~13.5 W/m2 in the UVA and UVB ranges, was provided by a UVB 150 lamp. The samples were exposed to UV irradiation for 30 days, simulating approximately 46 days of outdoor radiation in Florida. The temperature during the test was maintained at 21 °C. The waterproof performance of the PZO coating was assessed by immersing a PZO-coated plywood sample in water for several days and measuring water absorption. An identical, non-coated plywood sample served as a control. For the flame resistance test, a free-standing PZO sample was vertically positioned and exposed to a butane lighter flame. The PZO sample exhibited no signs of ignition after 10 s of exposure.
