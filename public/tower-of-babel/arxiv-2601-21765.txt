Abstract




Mean-field Variational Bayes for Sparse Probit Regression

Abstract

We consider Bayesian variable selection for binary outcomes under a probit link with a spike-and-slab prior on the regression coefficients.
Motivated by the computational challenges encountered by Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) samplers in high-dimensional regimes, we develop a mean-field variational Bayes approximation in which all variational factors admit closed-form updates, and the evidence lower bound is available in closed form.
This, in turn, allows the development of an efficient coordinate ascent variational inference algorithm to find the optimal values of the variational parameters.
The approach produces posterior inclusion probabilities and parameter estimates, enabling interpretable selection and prediction within a single framework.
As shown in both simulated and real data applications, the proposed method successfully identifies the important variables and is orders of magnitude faster than MCMC, while maintaining comparable accuracy.

Introduction

From the seminal work of
1
, Bayesian binary regression has attracted sustained attention over the past years, with a wide variety of computational strategies for probit and logit links, including sampling
(
8
;
11
;
6
;
13
)
or variational approximations
(
9
;
5
;
7
;
14
)
.
See also
4
and
2
for reviews addressing possible computational approaches.
In high-dimensional problems, a central inferential task is variable selection, often leveraging sparsity-inducing priors.
This model-based approach produces posterior inclusion probabilities and parameter estimates within a single coherent framework, but places heavy demands on posterior computation: as a consequence, standard Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) can face convergence issues or be prohibitively slow as
p
grows or when
p\gg n
.

In this contribution, in order to overcome the above-mentioned limitations of the sampling approaches, we develop a variational Bayes procedure for sparse probit regression with a spike-and-slab prior based on binary masks
(
10
)
.
To this aim, let
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
be the binary response and
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
the
p
-dimensional vector of covariates for units
i=1,\ldots,n
.
For
j=1,\ldots,p
, define the variable
\gamma_{j}\in\{0,1\}
indicating whether predictor
j
has an effect on the output (
\gamma_{j}=1
) or not (
\gamma_{j}=0
).
Thus, exploiting the characterization of the probit model in terms of partial observations of latent Gaussian variables
(
1
)
, we write the Bayesian probit model with binary masks as

\begin{split}y_{i}&=\mathbbm{1}\{z_{i}>0\},\qquad z_{i}\mid\bm{\beta},\bm{\gamma}\overset{ind}{\sim}\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta},\,1),\quad i=1,\ldots,n,\\
\bm{\beta}&\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\bm{0},\nu^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{p}),\qquad\gamma_{j}\overset{iid}{\sim}\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\rho),\quad j=1,\ldots,p,\end{split}

(1)

where
\mathbbm{1}\{A\}
denotes the indicator function of the event
A
,
{\mathbf{I}}_{p}
is the
p
-dimensional identity matrix,
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p})^{\top}
,
\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{p})^{\top}
, and
\bm{\Gamma}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\gamma})
, while
\nu^{2}>0
and
\rho\in(0,1)
represent known hyperparameters.
We thus encode selection through a binary mask in the linear predictor
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta}
while keeping
\bm{\beta}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\bm{0},\nu^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{p})
independent of
\bm{\gamma}
.
Note that if we set
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}=\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta}
,
\tilde{\beta}_{j}=\gamma_{j}\beta_{j}
has a mixture prior with a point mass at
0
with probability
1-\rho
and
\mathcal{N}(0,\nu^{2})
with probability
\rho
, which coincides with the classical spike-and-slab prior representation for
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}
.

The interest is in inferring which variables truly affect the output and possibly the intensity of such an effect.
Calling
{\mathbf{y}}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
, we thus study the posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs),
\Pr[\gamma_{j}=1\mid{\mathbf{y}}]
, and the posterior distribution of
\gamma_{j}\beta_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,p
.
These quantities do not admit a closed-form expression and might be computationally intensive to obtain via MCMC methods.
To overcome these limitations, in Section
2
we propose a mean-field variational Bayes approach, leveraging on methods developed by
10
for linear models.

2

Mean-field variational Bayes for sparse probit

In the proposed approach, we approximate the joint posterior distribution
p(\bm{\beta},{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\gamma}\mid{\mathbf{y}})
of model (
1
) with the following mean-field variational Bayes (MFVB) approximation

q(\bm{\beta},{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\gamma})=q(\bm{\beta})\;q({\mathbf{z}})\;\prod_{j=1}^{p}q(\gamma_{j}).

The form of the optimal factors is then given by the mean-field equations; see, e.g.,
3
.
According to those equations, if
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\theta}_{K})
is the vector of all model parameters, partitioned into
K
subcomponents
\bm{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\theta}_{K}
, and the posterior distribution
p(\bm{\theta}\mid{\mathbf{y}})
is approximated by the variational density
q(\bm{\theta})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}q(\bm{\theta}_{k})
, then the optimal variational approximation
q(\bm{\theta}_{k})
satisfies
\log q(\bm{\theta}_{k})=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\theta}_{-k})}\left[\log p(\bm{\theta}_{k}\mid\bm{\theta}_{-k},{\mathbf{y}})\right]+\text{const}
, where
\bm{\theta}_{-k}
denotes the vector
\bm{\theta}
with the
k
-th subcomponent
\bm{\theta}_{k}
removed; this notational convention will be used also in the following.
We now derive the form of the optimal
q(\bm{\beta})
,
q({\mathbf{z}})
, and
q(\gamma_{j})
,
j=1,\ldots,p
.
We denote by
\phi(t)
and
\Phi(t)
the standard normal density and cumulative distribution function, respectively.
Similarly,
\phi_{p}({\mathbf{x}};\bm{\Sigma})
denotes the density of a
p
-variate Gaussian random variable with mean
\bm{0}
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
, evaluated at
{\mathbf{x}}
.

2.1

The optimal variational factor
q(\bm{\beta})

From the optimal mean-field equations
(
3
)
, the optimal
q(\bm{\beta})
must satisfy

\log q(\bm{\beta})=\mathbb{E}_{q({\mathbf{z}})q(\bm{\gamma})}\big[\log p(\bm{\beta}\mid{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\gamma},{\mathbf{y}})\big]+\text{const},

where the full conditional
p(\bm{\beta}\mid{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\gamma},{\mathbf{y}})=p(\bm{\beta}\mid{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\gamma})
coincides with the posterior in a Gaussian regression with design
{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Gamma}
and unit error variance.
See also
10
.
One can derive it easily since, in general,

\log p(\bm{\beta}\mid{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\gamma})=\log p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\beta},\bm{\gamma})+\log p(\bm{\beta})+\text{const}.

Calling
{\mathbf{X}}=[{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, for model (
1
) it holds

\displaystyle\log p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\beta},\bm{\gamma})+\log p(\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta}\right)^{\top}\left({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2\nu^{2}}\,\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\beta}+\text{const},

where the quadratic term can be expanded as

\displaystyle\left({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta}\right)^{\top}\left({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}-2\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}+\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}-2\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}+\bm{\beta}^{\top}(\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{G}}\bm{\Gamma})\bm{\beta},

with
{\mathbf{G}}:={\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}
.
Hence

\displaystyle\log p(\bm{\beta}\mid{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\gamma})

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\beta}^{\top}\big(\nu^{-2}{\mathbf{I}}_{p}+\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{G}}\bm{\Gamma}\big)\bm{\beta}+\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}+\text{const},

i.e.,
p(\bm{\beta}\mid{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\gamma})=\phi_{p}(\bm{\beta}-{\mathbf{V}}\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}};{\mathbf{V}})
with
{\mathbf{V}}=(\nu^{-2}{\mathbf{I}}_{p}+\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{G}}\bm{\Gamma})^{-1}
.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\log q(\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q({\mathbf{z}})q(\bm{\gamma})}\big[-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\beta}^{\top}\big(\nu^{-2}{\mathbf{I}}_{p}+\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{G}}\bm{\Gamma}\big)\bm{\beta}+\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}\big]+\text{const}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\beta}^{\top}\big(\nu^{-2}{\mathbf{I}}_{p}+\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\gamma})}[\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{G}}\bm{\Gamma}]\big)\bm{\beta}+\bm{\beta}^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\gamma})}[\bm{\Gamma}]{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{q({\mathbf{z}})}[{\mathbf{z}}]+\text{const},

from which we can already recognize the
\log
of a multivariate Gaussian kernel.
In order to write conveniently the parameters, we define
\bar{{\mathbf{z}}}=\mathbb{E}_{q({\mathbf{z}})}[{\mathbf{z}}]
and
{\mathbf{W}}=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\gamma})}[\bm{\Gamma}]=\operatorname{diag}({\mathbf{w}})
, with
{\mathbf{w}}=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\gamma})}[\bm{\gamma}]\in[0,1]^{p}
and
\bm{\Omega}=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\gamma})}[\bm{\gamma}\bm{\gamma}^{\top}]={\mathbf{W}}(\,{\mathbf{I}}_{p}-{\mathbf{W}}\,)+{\mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}
, so that
\Omega_{jj}=w_{j}=\mathbb{E}_{q(\gamma_{j})}[\gamma_{j}]
and
\Omega_{jk}=w_{j}w_{k}
for
j\neq k
.
Exploiting now the fact that

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{G}}\bm{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=({\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Gamma})^{\top}({\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Gamma})=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1}{\mathbf{X}}_{:1}&\cdots&\gamma_{p}{\mathbf{X}}_{:p}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1}{\mathbf{X}}_{:1}&\cdots&\gamma_{p}{\mathbf{X}}_{:p}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\big[\,\gamma_{j}\gamma_{k}\,{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}_{:k}\,\big]_{j,k=1}^{p}={\mathbf{G}}\odot(\bm{\gamma}\bm{\gamma}^{\top}),

where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard (elementwise) product and
{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}
represents the
j
-th column of
{\mathbf{X}}
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\gamma})}[\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{G}}\bm{\Gamma}]={\mathbf{G}}\odot\bm{\Omega}
.
Thus, in conclusion, the optimal variational factor
q(\bm{\beta})
satisfies

q(\bm{\beta})=\phi_{p}(\bm{\beta}-\bm{\mu};\bm{\Sigma}),

(2)

where
\bm{\Sigma}=\big(\nu^{-2}{\mathbf{I}}_{p}+{\mathbf{G}}\odot\bm{\Omega}\big)^{-1}
and
\bm{\mu}=\bm{\Sigma}\,{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\bar{{\mathbf{z}}}
.
Note that since
{\mathbf{G}}={\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\succeq 0
and
\bm{\Omega}=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\gamma})}[\bm{\gamma}\bm{\gamma}^{\top}]\succeq 0
, the Schur product theorem gives
{\mathbf{G}}\odot\bm{\Omega}\succeq 0
.
Hence
\nu^{-2}{\mathbf{I}}_{p}+{\mathbf{G}}\odot\bm{\Omega}
is positive definite for any
\nu^{2}>0
.
In case some
w_{j}
degenerate to zero, simplifications similar to the ones presented in the supplementary material for the full conditional distribution hold.

2.2

The optimal variational factor
q({\mathbf{z}})

Starting with the computation of the full conditional of
{\mathbf{z}}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})^{\top}
, notice that

\begin{split}p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\beta},\bm{\gamma},{\mathbf{y}})&\propto p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\beta},\bm{\gamma})p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{z}})\\
&\propto\prod_{i=1}^{n}\phi(z_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta})\mathbbm{1}\{z_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{y_{i}}\},\end{split}

where
\mathcal{A}_{y_{i}}=(0,\infty)
if
y_{i}=1
and
(-\infty,0]
otherwise.
That is, adapting
1
, conditionally on
(\bm{\beta},\bm{\gamma},{\mathbf{y}})
the
z_{i}
are independently distributed as Gaussian random variables with mean
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta}
and unit variance, truncated to the set
\mathcal{A}_{y_{i}}
.
From the optimality equations, for
{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{A}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
, where
\mathcal{A}_{{\mathbf{y}}}:=\mathcal{A}_{y_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{A}_{y_{n}}
, one gets

\displaystyle\log q({\mathbf{z}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log q(z_{i}),

where, for
z_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{y_{i}}
,

\displaystyle\log q(z_{i})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\beta})q(\bm{\gamma})}\big[\log\phi(z_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta})\big]+\text{const}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\beta})q(\bm{\gamma})}\big[(z_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta})^{2}\big]+\text{const}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}z_{i}^{2}+z_{i}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\beta})q(\bm{\gamma})}\big[\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta}\big]+\text{const}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}z_{i}^{2}+z_{i}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\gamma})}\big[\bm{\Gamma}\big]\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\beta})}\big[\bm{\beta}\big]+\text{const}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}z_{i}^{2}+z_{i}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}+\text{const}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\big(z_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}\big)^{2}+\text{const}.

Hence, the optimal variational approximation
q({\mathbf{z}})
factorizes as
q({\mathbf{z}})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}q(z_{i})
, with

q(z_{i})\propto\phi(z_{i}-m_{i})\;\mathbbm{1}\{z_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{y_{i}}\},

where
m_{i}={\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}
.
Thus,
q(z_{i})
is the density of a Gaussian random variable
\mathcal{N}(m_{i},1)
truncated to
\mathcal{A}_{y_{i}}
.
Letting
k_{i}=2y_{i}-1\in\{-1,1\}
, standard formulas for the moments of truncated Gaussian random variables give

\displaystyle\bar{z}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(z_{i})}[z_{i}]=m_{i}+k_{i}\,\lambda(k_{i}m_{i}),

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(z_{i})}[z_{i}^{2}]

\displaystyle=1+m_{i}\,\bar{z}_{i},

(4)

where
\lambda(t)=\phi(t)/\Phi(t)
denotes the inverse Mills ratio.
We only need
\bar{{\mathbf{z}}}=(\bar{z}_{1},\ldots,\bar{z}_{n})^{\top}
in (
2
); the second moment in (
4
) is useful for monitoring the evidence lower bound (ELBO), which is reported in the Supplementary Material.

The expressions (
3
) and (
4
) follow from standard properties of truncated Gaussian distributions.
Indeed, let
T\sim\mathcal{N}(m,1)
and condition on
T>0
.
Then,
\mathbb{E}[T]=m+\lambda(m)
and
\operatorname{Var}(T)=1-m\,\lambda(m)-\lambda(m)^{2}
.
Hence
\mathbb{E}[T^{2}]=\operatorname{Var}(T)+\mathbb{E}[T]^{2}=1-m\,\lambda(m)-\lambda(m)^{2}+(m+\lambda(m))^{2}=1+m\,\mathbb{E}[T]
.
In case we condition on
T\leq 0
, we have
\mathbb{E}[T]=m-\lambda(-m)
and
\operatorname{Var}(T)=1+m\,\lambda(-m)-\lambda(-m)^{2}
instead.
Adapting the previous computations, we get
\mathbb{E}[T^{2}]=1+m\,\mathbb{E}[T]
also in this case.
Equations (
3
)-(
4
) give a concise representation of these results.

2.3

The optimal variational factor
q(\gamma_{j})

First, notice that the full conditional of
\gamma_{j}
takes the following form:

\begin{split}p(\gamma_{j}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\beta},{\mathbf{y}})&\propto p(\bm{\beta})p(\bm{\gamma})p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\beta},\bm{\gamma})p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{z}})\\
&\propto p(\bm{\gamma})p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\beta},\bm{\gamma})\\
&\propto\rho^{\gamma_{j}}(1-\rho)^{1-\gamma_{j}}\exp\left\{-\tfrac{1}{2}({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta})^{\top}({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta})\right\},\end{split}

and consequently

\log p(\gamma_{j}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\beta},{\mathbf{y}})=\gamma_{j}\text{logit}(\rho)-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta}+\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}+\text{const}.

(5)

Before isolating
\gamma_{j}
in the full conditional and taking the variational expectation to compute
q(\gamma_{j})
, let us recall that if
V\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)
then
p(v)=p^{v}(1-p)^{1-v}\propto\left(\tfrac{p}{1-p}\right)^{v}\propto\exp\left\{v\cdot\text{logit}(p)\right\}
and thus
\log p(v)=v\cdot\text{logit}(p)+\text{const}
.
Let us now isolate the term
\gamma_{j}
in the second and third terms in (
5
).
It holds
{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta}=\gamma_{j}\beta_{j}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}+\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\gamma_{j^{\prime}}\beta_{j^{\prime}}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j^{\prime}}
, thus

\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}=\gamma_{j}\beta_{j}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}+\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\gamma_{j^{\prime}}\beta_{j^{\prime}}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j^{\prime}}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}=\gamma_{j}\beta_{j}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}+\text{const},

and

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{l=1}^{p}\gamma_{l}\beta_{l}{\mathbf{X}}_{:l}\right)^{\top}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{p}\gamma_{k}\beta_{k}{\mathbf{X}}_{:k}\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{j}\beta_{j}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}^{\top}\sum_{k=1}^{p}{\mathbf{X}}_{:k}\gamma_{k}\beta_{k}+\left(\sum_{l\neq j}\gamma_{l}\beta_{l}{\mathbf{X}}_{:l}^{\top}\right)\gamma_{j}\beta_{j}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}+\text{const}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{j}\beta_{j}^{2}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}+2\gamma_{j}\beta_{j}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}^{\top}\left(\sum_{l\neq j}\gamma_{l}\beta_{l}{\mathbf{X}}_{:l}\right)+\text{const}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{j}\beta_{j}^{2}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}+2\gamma_{j}\beta_{j}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}_{-j}\bm{\Gamma}_{-j,-j}\bm{\beta}_{-j}+\text{const},

where
{\mathbf{X}}_{-j}
corresponds to
{\mathbf{X}}
with the
j
-th column
{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}
removed, and
\bm{\Gamma}_{-j,-j}
is the matrix
\bm{\Gamma}
with the
j
-th row and the
j
-th column removed.
Putting it all together, we obtain

\log p(\gamma_{j}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\beta},{\mathbf{y}})\\
=\gamma_{j}\left[\text{logit}(\rho)-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta_{j}^{2}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}+\beta_{j}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}^{\top}({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{X}}_{-j}\bm{\Gamma}_{-j,-j}\bm{\beta}_{-j})\right]+\text{const}.

Taking the expectation with respect to all the other variational distributions, we obtain that the optimal
q(\gamma_{j})
must satisfy

\begin{split}\log q(\gamma_{j})&=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\beta})q(\bm{\gamma}_{-j})q({\mathbf{z}})}\left[\log p(\gamma_{j}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\beta},{\mathbf{y}})\right]+\text{const}\\
&=\gamma_{j}\cdot\left[\text{logit}(\rho)-\tfrac{1}{2}(\Sigma_{jj}+\mu_{j}^{2})G_{jj}+\mu_{j}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}^{\top}\bar{{\mathbf{z}}}-\sum_{k\neq j}(\Sigma_{jk}+\mu_{j}\mu_{k})w_{k}G_{jk}\right]\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\text{const},\end{split}

from which we obtain that the optimal variational factor
q(\gamma_{j})
is the probability mass function of a
\operatorname{Bernoulli}(w_{j})
random variable, with
w_{j}=\operatorname{expit}(\eta_{j})
and

\eta_{j}:=\operatorname{logit}(\rho)\;+\;\mu_{j}\,{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}^{\top}\bar{{\mathbf{z}}}\;-\;\frac{1}{2}\,(\Sigma_{jj}+\mu_{j}^{2})\,G_{jj}\;-\;\sum_{k\neq j}(\Sigma_{jk}+\mu_{j}\mu_{k})\,w_{k}\,G_{jk}.

(6)

3

The algorithm

The optimal values of the variational parameters can be obtained via a coordinate ascent variational inference (CAVI) algorithm, where the parameters are sequentially updated until their relative change or the change in the ELBO becomes negligible.
The routine is given in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1
MFVB for sparse probit regression

1:

Input:
{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p},\,{\mathbf{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
\nu^{2}
,
\rho
.

2:

Precompute
{\mathbf{G}}\leftarrow{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}
. For
i=1,\dots,n
:
k_{i}\leftarrow 2y_{i}-1
.

3:

Initialize
{\mathbf{w}}
and
\bm{\mu}
(e.g., set
w_{j}\leftarrow\rho
,
\mu_{j}\leftarrow 0
for
j=1,\ldots,p
).

4:

Set
{\mathbf{W}}\leftarrow\operatorname{diag}({\mathbf{w}})
;
\bm{m}\leftarrow{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}
; for
i=1,\dots,n
:
\bar{z}_{i}\leftarrow m_{i}+k_{i}\,\lambda(k_{i}m_{i})
.

5:

repeat

6:

\bm{\Omega}\leftarrow{\mathbf{W}}({\mathbf{I}}_{p}-{\mathbf{W}})+{\mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}

7:

\bm{\Sigma}\leftarrow(\nu^{-2}{\mathbf{I}}_{p}+{\mathbf{G}}\odot\bm{\Omega})^{-1}
;
\bm{\mu}\leftarrow\bm{\Sigma}{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\bar{{\mathbf{z}}}

8:

\bm{m}\leftarrow{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}
;  for
i=1,\dots,n
:
\bar{z}_{i}\leftarrow m_{i}+k_{i}\,\lambda(k_{i}m_{i})

9:

For
j=1,\dots,p
: compute
\eta_{j}
by (
6
) and set
w_{j}\leftarrow\operatorname{expit}(\eta_{j})

10:

{\mathbf{W}}\leftarrow\operatorname{diag}({\mathbf{w}})

11:

until the relative change in the ELBO or in
(\bm{\mu},{\mathbf{w}})
is below the tolerance

12:

Return:
(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
, inclusion probabilities
{\mathbf{w}}
, and ELBO.

To run the algorithm, one needs to set the two hyperparameters
\nu^{2}
(prior variance for
\bm{\beta}
) and
\rho
(prior inclusion probability of the variables).
In both the simulation studies and the application below, these hyperparameters are set according to the following data-driven procedure.
First, for a given
\rho
value, we set
\nu^{2}=\nu_{0}^{2}/(\rho\cdot p)
, in order to control the prior variance of the linear predictor to be close to
\nu_{0}^{2}
, under the assumption that the predictors have been standardized (as it is always advisable in practice) or generated with unit variance.
In the following, we will use
\nu_{0}^{2}=25
.
Then, after potentially dividing the data into a training and test set, the optimal value of
\rho
is chosen via a
K
-fold cross-validation on the training set only, where the allocation of observations to folds is stratified by output value
y_{i}
to have approximately the same fraction of
y_{i}=1
in each fold, close to the fraction throughout the whole training dataset.
For each value of
\rho
in a grid of possible values, typically ranging from
0.05
to
0.5
with a step size of
0.05
, we compute the average cross-validation deviance

\text{dev}_{CV}=\dfrac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\text{dev}_{k},

where
\text{dev}_{k}=-2\sum_{i\in I_{k}}[y_{i}\log\hat{p}_{i}+(1-y_{i})\log(1-\hat{p}_{i})]
,
I_{k}
is the set of indices of the observations assigned to fold
k
, and
\hat{p}_{i}=\hat{\Pr}[y_{i}=1\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{-\text{FOLD}(i)}]
is the estimated probability of success for the
i
-th training observation, when the model is fitted on the set of all the training observations, excluding the ones in the fold to which the
i
-th observation belongs, whose index set is denoted
\text{FOLD}(i)=\{I_{k}\colon i\in I_{k}\}
.
Calling
{\mathbf{W}}^{(-\text{FOLD}(i))}
and
\bm{\mu}^{(-\text{FOLD}(i))}
the variational mean estimates for
\bm{\Gamma}
and
\bm{\beta}
obtained from the MFVB algorithm fitted on
{\mathbf{y}}_{-\text{FOLD}(i)}
, we compute
\hat{p}_{i}
using the plug-in estimate
\hat{p}_{i}=\Phi({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{W}}^{(-\text{FOLD}(i))}\bm{\mu}^{(-\text{FOLD}(i))})
.

We investigate the performance of the proposed algorithm in selecting the appropriate variables and compare its performance with MCMC sampling methods, where

6

Conclusion

In this paper, we presented a mean-field variational Bayes (MFVB) method for sparse binary classification problems with a probit link.
Sparsity is induced thanks to the use of a spike-and-slab prior on the coefficients; see also
10
.
As shown in simulated and real data applications, the proposed CAVI algorithm can be easily implemented thanks to closed-form updates and is on average two to three orders of magnitude faster than MCMC procedures, leading to massive computational gains.
When the number of observations
n
is sufficiently large relative to
p
, both MFVB and MCMC select the same variables and lead to comparable solutions both in terms of parameter estimates and predictive probabilities.
In scenarios where
p>n
, the MFVB tends to favor more parsimonious solutions, where fewer variables are selected, while MCMC tends to be more conservative, including more variables, although with smaller associated posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs).
Further research directions could consider more structured approximations, as done in
7
for Bayesian probit models with a Gaussian prior, or more complex models, including, e.g., mixed-effects, where accurate and scalable approximations are of utmost importance
(
15
)
.

S.2

Gibbs sampler

We summarize here the key steps of the blocked Gibbs sampler used in the paper.
The form of the full conditionals of
\bm{\beta},{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\gamma}
is available in closed form, as shown in the manuscript.
Actually, it is convenient to rewrite the full conditional for
\bm{\beta}
by isolating the coefficients associated with the active and inactive covariates.
Calling
S:=\{j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}\colon\gamma_{j}=1\}
the set of active covariates, one can partition
\bm{\beta}
into
\bm{\beta}_{S}=(\beta_{j}\colon\ j\in S)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{|S|}
and
\bm{\beta}_{S^{c}}=(\beta_{j}\colon\ j\in S^{c})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-|S|}
, where
S^{c}:=\{j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}\colon\gamma_{j}=0\}
denotes the set of inactive covariates.
Partitioning the vector
\bm{\zeta}={\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}
in the same way, one gets

\log p(\bm{\beta}\mid{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\gamma})=-\tfrac{1}{2\nu^{2}}\bm{\beta}_{S^{c}}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{S^{c}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\beta}_{S}^{\top}\big(\nu^{-2}{\mathbf{I}}_{|S|}+{\mathbf{G}}_{S}\big)\bm{\beta}_{S}+\bm{\beta}_{S}^{\top}\bm{\zeta}_{S}+\text{const},

where
{\mathbf{G}}_{S}
is obtained from the matrix
{\mathbf{G}}={\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}
by selecting the rows and columns whose indices are in
S
.
Thus, calling
{\mathbf{B}}_{S}=\nu^{-2}{\mathbf{I}}_{|S|}+{\mathbf{G}}_{S}
, conditionally on
({\mathbf{z}},\bm{\gamma})
, one gets

\bm{\beta}_{S^{c}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p-|S|}(\bm{0},\nu^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{p-|S|})\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{\beta}_{S}\sim\mathcal{N}_{|S|}\left({\mathbf{B}}_{S}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}_{S},{\mathbf{B}}_{S}^{-1}\right),

that is,
\bm{\beta}_{S^{c}}
is distributed according to the prior distribution, while
\bm{\beta}_{S}
follows the posterior distribution of the reduced model in which only the active variables are considered.
This is particularly useful when implementing MCMC sampling schemes, significantly reducing the dimension of the problem.

To improve mixing and efficiency,
\bm{\gamma}
and
\bm{\beta}
are updated jointly.
This makes the sampling of the inactive coefficients
\bm{\beta}_{S^{c}}
unnecessary, since they will not appear in the linear predictor: we can then set them equal to
0
.
Thus, we update
(\bm{\gamma},\bm{\beta})
by first updating
\bm{\gamma}
by sampling each coordinate
\gamma_{j}
from its collapsed full conditional
p(\gamma_{j}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},{\mathbf{z}},{\mathbf{y}})=p(\gamma_{j}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},{\mathbf{z}})
and then sample
\bm{\beta}_{S}
from the full conditional
p(\bm{\beta}_{S}\mid\bm{\gamma},{\mathbf{z}})
above.
Finally,
{\mathbf{z}}
is updated by sampling from its full conditional.
Thus, we derive below the full conditional
p(\gamma_{j}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},{\mathbf{z}})
and then summarize the steps of the pseudocode in Algorithm
2
.
Conditionally on
(\bm{\gamma}_{-j},{\mathbf{z}})
,
\gamma_{j}
is a Bernoulli random variable with probability of success given by

\Pr[\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},{\mathbf{z}}]=\dfrac{p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},\gamma_{j}=1)\Pr[\gamma_{j}=1]}{p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},\gamma_{j}=1)\Pr[\gamma_{j}=1]+p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},\gamma_{j}=0)\Pr[\gamma_{j}=0]},

so that

\operatorname{logit}\{\Pr[\gamma_{j}=1\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},{\mathbf{z}}]\}=\log p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},\gamma_{j}=1)-\log p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},\gamma_{j}=0)+\operatorname{logit}(\rho).

Thus, a key quantity is
\log p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma})
.
This can be easily computed by recalling that
{\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{X}}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\beta}+\bm{\varepsilon}={\mathbf{X}}_{:S}\bm{\beta}_{S}+\bm{\varepsilon}
, where
\bm{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}_{n}(\bm{0},{\mathbf{I}}_{n})
and
{\mathbf{X}}_{:S}
is obtained by selecting from
{\mathbf{X}}
the columns whose indices are in
S
.
Thus, marginalizing out
\bm{\beta}_{S}
we get
{\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma}\sim\mathcal{N}_{n}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}(S))
, where
\bm{\Lambda}(S)={\mathbf{I}}_{n}+\nu^{2}{\mathbf{X}}_{:S}{\mathbf{X}}_{:S}^{\top}
.
We can avoid working with
n
-dimensional matrices by exploiting the following properties.
Using the matrix determinant lemma, one has

\begin{split}\det\bm{\Lambda}(S)&=\det({\mathbf{I}}_{|S|}+\nu^{2}{\mathbf{X}}_{:S}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}_{:S})\det({\mathbf{I}}_{n})\\
&=\det((\nu^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{|S|})\cdot{\mathbf{B}}_{S})\\
&=\nu^{2|S|}\det({\mathbf{B}}_{S}).\end{split}

Exploiting Woodbury’s identity, we can also write
\bm{\Lambda}(S)^{-1}={\mathbf{I}}_{n}-{\mathbf{X}}_{:S}{\mathbf{B}}_{S}^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}_{:S}^{\top}
, so that, recalling that
\bm{\zeta}={\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}
, it holds

\log p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma})=-\tfrac{1}{2}\big(|S|\log\nu^{2}+\log\det{\mathbf{B}}_{S}\big)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bm{\zeta}_{S}^{\top}{\mathbf{B}}_{S}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}_{S}+\text{const},

where the constant term is independent of
\bm{\gamma}
.

In the Gibbs update for
\gamma_{j}
, we need
\log p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},\gamma_{j}=1)
and
\log p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},\gamma_{j}=0)
while keeping
\bm{\gamma}_{-j}
at its most recent values.
To avoid recomputing both quantities at every coordinate, we store the current log-marginal and update it online.

Let
S=\{k:\gamma_{k}=1\}
as above and write the log-marginal as
L(S)=\log p({\mathbf{z}}\mid S)
for notational simplicity.
At the start of iteration
t
, compute once

L\leftarrow L\big(S^{(t-1)}\big)=\log p\big({\mathbf{z}}^{(t-1)}\mid\bm{\gamma}^{(t-1)}\big).

Then update
\gamma_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,p
, as follows.
For each
j
, define

S_{+}=S\cup\{j\},\qquad S_{-}=S\setminus\{j\}.

There are two cases:

Case 1:
\gamma_{j}^{(t-1)}=0
(
j\notin S
).
The current
L
already equals

L=L(S)=\log p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},\gamma_{j}=0).

Compute only the “addition” value

L_{+}=L(S_{+})=\log p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},\gamma_{j}=1).

Sample
\gamma_{j}^{(t)}
using
L
and
L_{+}
.
If
\gamma_{j}^{(t)}=0
, leave
L
unchanged; if
\gamma_{j}^{(t)}=1
, update the cache and the active set:

L\leftarrow L_{+},\qquad S\leftarrow S_{+}.

Case 2:
\gamma_{j}^{(t-1)}=1
(
j\in S
).
The current
L
equals

L=L(S)=\log p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},\gamma_{j}=1).

Compute only the “deletion” value

L_{-}=L(S_{-})=\log p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma}_{-j},\gamma_{j}=0).

Sample
\gamma_{j}^{(t)}
.
If
\gamma_{j}^{(t)}=1
, keep
L
unchanged; if

\gamma_{j}^{(t)}=0
, update:

L\leftarrow L_{-},\qquad S\leftarrow S_{-}.

Thus, at each coordinate, we evaluate only the alternative state’s log-marginal, while the current state’s value is the stored
L
.
When a flip occurs, both
S
and
L
are updated to stay up to date.
See Algorithm
2
for the full procedure.

Algorithm 2
Blocked Gibbs sampler for sparse probit regression

1:

Input:
{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p},\,{\mathbf{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
\nu^{2}
,
\rho
; iterations
T
, burn-in
B
.

2:

Precompute:
{\mathbf{G}}\leftarrow{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}
;  define
\log p({\mathbf{z}}\mid S)
via
{\mathbf{B}}_{S}=\nu^{-2}{\mathbf{I}}_{|S|}+{\mathbf{G}}_{S}
and
\bm{\zeta}={\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}
:

3:

\quad\log p({\mathbf{z}}\mid S)\leftarrow-\tfrac{1}{2}\big(|S|\log\nu^{2}+\log\det{\mathbf{B}}_{S}\big)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bm{\zeta}_{S}^{\top}{\mathbf{B}}_{S}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}_{S}.

4:

Initialize
\bm{\gamma}^{(0)}
and
\bm{\beta}^{(0)}
; draw
{\mathbf{z}}^{(0)}
from
p({\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma}^{(0)},\bm{\beta}^{(0)},{\mathbf{y}})
.

5:

for
t=1,\dots,T
do

6:

\bm{\zeta}\leftarrow{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{z}}^{(t-1)}
;
S\leftarrow\{j:\gamma^{(t-1)}_{j}=1\}
;
L\leftarrow\log p({\mathbf{z}}^{(t-1)}\mid S)

7:

(
\bm{\gamma}\mid{\mathbf{z}}
)

8:

for
j=1,\dots,p
do

9:

if
\gamma^{(t-1)}_{j}=1
then

10:

S_{-}=S\setminus\{j\}
;
L_{-}\leftarrow\log p({\mathbf{z}}^{(t-1)}\mid S_{-})

11:

\ell_{1}\leftarrow L+\log\rho
;
\ell_{0}\leftarrow L_{-}+\log(1-\rho)
;
r_{j}\leftarrow\ell_{1}-\ell_{0}

12:

Draw
\gamma^{(t)}_{j}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\operatorname{expit}(r_{j}))

13:

if
\gamma^{(t)}_{j}=0
then

14:

S\leftarrow S_{-}
;
L\leftarrow L_{-}

15:

end if

16:

else

17:

S_{+}=S\cup\{j\}
;
L_{+}\leftarrow\log p({\mathbf{z}}^{(t-1)}\mid S_{+})

18:

\ell_{0}\leftarrow L+\log(1-\rho)
;
\ell_{1}\leftarrow L_{+}+\log\rho
;
r_{j}\leftarrow\ell_{1}-\ell_{0}

19:

Draw
\gamma^{(t)}_{j}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\operatorname{expit}(r_{j}))

20:

if
\gamma^{(t)}_{j}=1
then

21:

S\leftarrow S_{+}
;
L\leftarrow L_{+}

22:

end if

23:

end if

24:

end for

25:

(
\bm{\beta}\mid{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\gamma}
) Set
\bm{\beta}^{(t)}_{S^{c}}\leftarrow\bm{0}
(avoid sampling inactive coefficients).

26:

If
|S|>0
then form
{\mathbf{B}}_{S}
and draw

\bm{\beta}^{(t)}_{S}\sim\mathcal{N}\big({\mathbf{B}}_{S}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}_{S},\,{\mathbf{B}}_{S}^{-1}\big).

27:

(
{\mathbf{z}}\mid\bm{\gamma},\bm{\beta},{\mathbf{y}}
)
For
i=1,\dots,n
, draw
z^{(t)}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}^{(t)}\bm{\beta}^{(t)},1)
truncated            to
(0,\infty)\ \text{if }y_{i}=1,\ \text{and to }(-\infty,0]\ \text{if }y_{i}=0
, where
\bm{\Gamma}^{(t)}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\gamma}^{(t)})
.

28:

Store: If
t>B
, save
\bm{\gamma}^{(t)}
,
\bm{\beta}^{(t)}
.

29:

end for

30:

Return: draws of
\bm{\gamma}
and
\bm{\beta}
.
