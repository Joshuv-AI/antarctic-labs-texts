Study on the influencing factors and intervention strategies of public panic buying during public health emergencies
Authors: Heting Wu, Ruirui Xie, Lili Ding, Huiling Dong, Guifang Ren
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo examine the prevalence and influencing factors of panic buying of emergency supplies among the public during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) epidemic and provide a reference for public responses.MethodsUsing a self-designed questionnaire on panic buying during COVID-19, we conducted an online voluntary anonymous survey among adult internet users (≥ 18 years) in China from June to July 2022. Descriptive analysis, χ2 test and logistic regression analysis were used to explore the influencing factors of public panic buying.ResultsThe high-risk categories for panic buying included younger individuals, women, students, and those with lower economic status (P < 0.05). Media factors, psychological panic, reference group, psychological value expectation, group identification, and social control ability were identified as important factors influencing the occurrence of public panic buying (P < 0.05). Among those factors, media influence, psychological panic, reference groups, psychological value expectations, and group identity increased the influence on panic buying, however, social control ability decreased the influence on panic buying.ConclusionsPublic panic buying behavior can have a negative impact on social stability, and it is crucial to reduce the occurrence of such behavior. Based on the empirical results of this study, we can propose intervention measures from several aspects, including media factors, psychological panic, reference group, psychological value expectation, group identification, and social control ability. However, this study only examined the risk factors influencing panic buying behavior. Future research could further analyze the pathways of influence among these factors.

Introduction

Since the outbreak of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), it has become a global crisis [1]. The initial stage of the outbreak witnessed a surge in confirmed cases, leading to the significant and rapid spread of the epidemic. In China, the outbreak coincided with the Spring Festival travel season, exacerbating the demand for emergency supplies and resulting in a severe shortage of protective materials and daily goods. This shortage prompted panic buying behavior among the public, which had serious implications for social stability, economic order, business operations, and consumer psychology [2, 3]. The public is not only the subject of crisis response, but also the main participant in panic buying [4]. It is crucial to analyze the factors contributing to public panic buying during COVID-19 to effectively guide public behavior during public health emergencies.

Public panic buying is a common behavior during public health emergencies and has received significant attention from scholars. Some researchers from both domestic and foreign contexts have discussed the formation mechanism and influencing factors of group buying, approaching the topic from the perspectives of economics, sociology, and psychology [5, 6]. Research on panic buying primarily focused on its occurrence mechanism, influencing factors, and evolutionary patterns. In this regard, two research approaches have been adopted: specific case analysis and mathematical modeling. Some researchers have investigated these areas to gain a deeper understanding of panic buying during public health emergencies [7]. They have used various theories, such as prospect theory, cumulative prospect theory, and evolutionary game theory, to describe the psychological expectations that lead to public panic buying. They also analyzed the mechanisms that trigger this behavior [8, 9]. However, the application of these theories must be based on a reference point. When applying these theories to practical problems, the selection of reference points often lacks sufficient theoretical support.

Research on the influencing factors primarily used questionnaire surveys to examine various aspects, including risk perception [10], media factors [11], severity of the epidemic [12], and social influence [13]. However, the evolution of panic buying events was not solely influenced by external factors, but also by internal factors within the general public, such as expected regret, anxiety and fear [14, 15], and emotional psychology [16]. However, it was worth noting that the existing literature rarely reflects research on these internal factors. Existing studies on the evolution of panic buying events have primarily focused on the impact of external factors, such as rumor spread [17] and media factors [18]. In conclusion, these studies provide valuable references for exploring the influencing factors of panic buying during public health emergencies.

Sudden panic buying is characterized by impulsive buying behavior, a reaction that consumers exhibit owing to various factors. This impulsive behavior is influenced by several intervening factors, and the decision to participate in panic buying is ultimately determined after careful consideration. This logical reasoning aligns well with the Dholakia impulse consumption model [19], which explains the formation and execution of impulsive buying behavior. In this study, the factors influencing impulse consumption are categorized into individual, group, situational, and psychological. The selection of each variable is illustrated in Fig. 1. Individual factors include personal feature. Group factor include reference group and group identity. Situation factor include characteristics of COVID-19. Limiting factor include social support. Cognitive psychology include psychological panic and psychological expectation. Based on the existing literature, this study also includes media factors, social trust, and social control ability in the model to analyze their influence on public panic buying behavior.

In summary, there has been relatively limited research conducted by domestic scholars on panic buying behavior during public health emergencies. Although there were some research foundations, the level of research was relatively shallow, with the majority of studies being theoretical analyses that lack empirical research. On the one hand, the existing literature primarily focused on studying public panic buying from a single dimension by examining external environmental or internal psychological factors. There was a lack of literature analyzing the interaction between these two factors, despite the fact that panic buying was a result of the joint action of both the environment and individuals. Furthermore, previous studies have predominantly focused on regional research within specific emergency contexts rather than providing a systematic analysis in the field of public health emergencies. With an increasing frequency of public health emergencies, panic buying has become more prevalent, resulting in complex manifestations, varying group sizes, intricate influence mechanisms, and diverse consequences. Therefore, it is imperative for researchers to conduct comprehensive and in-depth discussions on the panic buying specific to different types of events.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedure

For this study, a web survey method was employed from June 1 to July 1, 2022, targeting adults aged 18 years and above in Shandong Province, China. All respondents were required to read and acknowledge informed consent forms. A statement to confirm that all participants were approved by the Medical Ethics Association of Shandong Second Medical University (2024YX255). Considering the causes of the epidemic and the context in China, it was anticipated that panic buying would be more prevalent among urban residents and middle-aged to elderly individuals. To address this, a quota sampling method was employed in Shandong Province, using age and urban-rural composition data from the seventh national population census as the basis for division. In the final analysis, a total of 1600 questionnaires were distributed, and 1591 were successfully collected, resulting in a recovery rate of 99.43%. After carefully reviewing the collected questionnaires and eliminating those that did not meet the necessary criteria. For example, we excluded invalid questionnaires that lacked key variables (such as age and urban-rural status) as well as those completed in less than 3 min, as these questionnaires exhibited significant quality issues. A total of 1318 valid questionnaires were obtained, resulting in an effective questionnaire rate of 82.375%.

Survey tools

The questionnaire comprised two parts, with a total of 39 questions. The first part included basic information such as gender, age, area of residence, education level, occupation, economic status, and health status. The second part focused on panic buying and its influencing factors. There are two questions regarding public panic buying behavior. The influencing factors of panic buying included ten dimensions, with three questions for each dimension, totaling 30 questions. These factors included the status of panic buying for essential items and medical protective supplies, epidemic factor, media factor, psychological panic, reference groups, psychological expectations, group identity, social support, social control ability, group efficiency, and risk perceptions. After the reliability test, the overall Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.845, and the Cronbach’s α coefficients for each dimension were all above 0.8,the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value was 0.899, the approximate χ2 was 40112.770, the Bartlett spherical test was P < 0.001, extracting the common factor greater than 1, and the cumulative variance of the common factor was 66.076%, indicating that the variables had good contribution to the questionnaire and the validity of the questionnaire was good.

Measure

The dependent variable in this study was public panic buying. In the questionnaire, participants were asked whether they engaged in panic buying of essential items (such as vegetables, food, etc.) or medical protective items (such as masks, disinfectants, etc.). The response options ranged from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” with corresponding values of 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. A cumulative addition method was employed to integrate the dependent variables. This involved calculating the average scores for buying essential items and medical protective items ([buying essential items + buying medical protective items]/2). Responses categorized as “strongly disagree,” “disagree,” and “neutral” were combined as “no panic buying” with a value of 0. Responses categorized as “agree” and “strongly agree” were combined as “panic buying” with a value of 1.

The summation method was used to integrate the independent variables. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was utilized to assess the internal consistency of the variables influencing public panic buying. The results indicated that the variables exhibited good internal consistency reliability and that each independent variable could be obtained through summation. For instance, the calculation formula for the reference group variable was as follows: total score of the reference group = (surrounding crowd + network public opinion + relatives and friends) / 3. A higher score indicated a greater influence of the reference group. Similar calculations were applied to the other independent variables.

Statistical analysis

In this study, SPSS20.0 statistical software was used to process data and univariate analysis using χ2 test and t test to identify high-risk group of panic buying. The study employed binary logistic regression analysis to examine the impact of various factors, including demographic variables, epidemic factors, media factors, psychological panic, reference group, psychological value expectation, risk perception, group identity, social trust, social support, and social control ability on the occurrence of public panic buying. Statistical significance was set at a significance level of P < 0.05.

Results

Basic information of the sample

In the 1318 samples that were surveyed, it was found that women accounted for a higher percentage (68.8%) compared to men (31.2%). The majority of individuals in the middle-aged group fell within the age range of 36 to 50 years (46.8%). Furthermore, a higher proportion of the surveyed population resided in urban areas (71.2%) as opposed to rural areas (28.8%). The category with the highest percentage of individuals with education was college or undergraduate (59.8%). The highest proportion (39.7%) was among those with a monthly income of 5001 to 10,000 RMB. Additionally, the occupation of public institutions accounted for the largest proportion, representing 27.9% of the total. In relation to the health status, the percentage of individuals reporting good health was 39.3%, whereas the proportion of the surveyed population progressively declined as their health status worsened. Regarding concerns about the epidemic, a significant proportion of the public (44.8%) expressed a high level of concern about the spread of rumors related to the epidemic. In terms of panic buying, 391 individuals (39.1%) reported purchasing essential household items during the outbreak, while 580 individuals (44%) admitted to stockpiling medical protective items. Multifactor analysis of the public panic buying

The objective of the multifactor analysis was to identify the variables that could potentially influence the public panic buying. In the regression analysis, the study examined the statistical differences of various independent variables, such as age, gender, occupation, monthly income level, psychological panic, epidemic factor, media factor, group reference, psychological value expectation, risk perception, group identity, social trust, social support, and social control ability. In this study, public panic buying was used as the dependent variable (assignment: no panic buying = 0, panic buying = 1) to conduct a logistic regression analysis. The entry method was forward LR, and the reference group was the last group.

The fitting results of the regression model

Log-likelihood value and pseudo-coefficient of determination

The results showed that the log-likelihood value of -2 was 1472.675, Cox & Snell R2 value was 0.213, which was greater than 0.05, and Nagelkerke R2 value was 0.287. This results showed that only the main effect of the respective variables can meet the demand and no additional independent variables were included. Furthermore, Hosmer and Lemes how tests demonstrated that the χ2 value was 14.039 with P > 0.05, indicating a better fit of this logistic regression model.

Collinearity diagnosis

We included the variables with statistically significant differences from the descriptive analysis and univariate analysis into the model for collinearity diagnosis to identify high correlations among the independent variables. If there is a collinearity issue among the variables, it may lead to unstable parameter estimates and increased standard errors, thereby affecting the model’s interpretability and predictive power. The tolerance and variance inflation factors were used as important criteria to determine whether the model had multicollinearity. When the tolerance is less than 0.1, the variance inflation factor is greater than 5, indicating that the model may have severe collinearity. In this study, the variable with the smallest tolerance was the epidemic factor with a value of 0.332. Additionally, the variable with the largest variance inflation factor was the epidemic factor, with a value of 3.012. These findings suggest that the epidemic factor may have multiple collinearities with other independent variables, leading to its removal from the analysis. In conclusion, the possibility of multicollinearity in this model, in addition to the epidemic factor, was very small. See Table 4.

The results of the regression analysis

Regression analysis showed that age significantly affected public panic buying (P < 0.05). Compared with the public over 50 years of age, the public aged 36 to 50 was more likely to engage in panic buying, with a likelihood of 1.753 times higher than that of the public over 50 years of age. Gender significantly affected public panic buying (P < 0.05), and women were 0.674 times more likely than men to engage in panic buying. Occupational activity significantly affected public panic buying (P < 0.05). The results showed that the number of medical workers was 1.653 times higher than that of agricultural workers, and the number of students was 1.950 times higher than that of agricultural workers. The media factor significantly affected public panic buying (P < 0.05). The media factor positively impacted public panic buying. During the epidemic period, the public, who were more affected by untimely and incomplete media reports, as well as low transparency, were more likely to engage in panic buying. Psychological panic significantly influenced public panic buying (P < 0.05). The public who panicked about their own health, life essentials, and medical protective items during the outbreak were more likely to purchase them.

The reference group had a significant effect on public panic buying (P < 0.05). The reference group had a positive impact on public panic buying. The public, who were more affected by the opinions of those around them, online public opinions, relatives, and friends, were more likely to engage in panic buying. Psychological value expectations significantly affected public panic buying (P < 0.05). The public who were more affected by commodity prices, psychological anxiety, and expectations of commodity value during the epidemic were more likely to engage in panic buying. Group identity significantly affected public panic buying (P < 0.05). Group identity positively affected public panic buying. During the pandemic, individuals who strongly identified with a particular group were more likely to engage in panic buying.

Social control ability significantly affected public panic buying (P < 0.05). Social control ability negatively influenced public panic buying. The more effective the government’s control measures, media reports, prevention and control effect by medical institutions, and community involvement were, the less likely panic buying occurred. Conclusion and discussion

The high-risk groups for panic buying were identified as low-age individuals, women, students, and those with low financial standing. The aforementioned groups were the primary participants in panic buying, and their behavioral characteristics directly influenced the progress and outcomes of group panic buying. Younger age cohorts exhibited a higher propensity to participate in panic buying, a finding consistent with prior scholarly investigations [20]. Older individuals, by virtue of their extensive life experiences and exposure to various crisis events, typically exhibited a higher level of composure and possessed better coping mechanisms when faced with outbreaks. Women were also identified as having a higher risk of panic buying [21, 22]. Considering that women often play a dominant role in household consumption decisions, they pay more attention to purchasing daily consumer goods. Furthermore, women tend to have lower psychological resilience and tolerance than men, making them more susceptible to panic and irrational panic buying. Students were identified as having a high risk of panic buying. This can be attributed to their younger age, lower income levels, limited life experiences, susceptibility to online rumors, and difficulty critically evaluating information. These factors make students more prone to blindly following others’ behaviors. Individuals with poor economic conditions were also found to be at high risk for panic buying [23, 24]. As they were more heavily impacted by the pandemic and struggled to meet their basic daily needs, they were more likely to engage in panic buying to ensure their physiological health and safety.

The media factor was identified as an external environmental factor that contributed to public panic buying. The dissemination of media information played a significant role in the occurrence and development of group panic buying. Group panic buying is an irrational behavior that often arises in situations where information is unclear. While the root cause of group panic buying was the pandemic, the spread of media information, particularly rumors, can greatly influence the scale, progression, and consequences of such behavior [25]. Preliminary research has indicated that comprehensive media reporting, diverse reporting channels, and the provision of accurate, transparent, and timely information were crucial in preventing mass behavior [26–28]. Ensuring that media reports are reliable and informative can mitigate panic buying tendencies. The media serves as a vital channel through which the public obtains information about the pandemic and remains updated regarding its latest developments. This is also reflected in the findings of the present study.

Psychological panic was identified as a significant risk factor for public engagement in panic buying. According to impulsive consumption theory, consumers’ cognitive and psychological states play a crucial role in determining whether impulsive consumption occurs. Individuals who experienced positive psychological emotions were less likely to engage in impulsive consumption. However, impulsive consumption behavior was more likely to occur when they experienced negative psychological emotions. Panic behavior, which is a negative emotional response in crisis situations, can easily trigger public panic buying. During the COVID-19 pandemic, public fear was triggered, leading to an increase in cognitive load and affecting individuals’ psychological value expectations. This, in turn, posed challenges for social management by the government and relevant community departments, ultimately resulting in irrational behaviors such as panic buying.

The reference group played a significant role in influencing public panic buying behavior. In impulsive consumption theory, the group factor was considered a central element that influenced impulsive consumption. Similarly, public panic buying behavior was influenced by the behavior and opinions of others within their reference group. Panic buying was characterized by quick purchase decisions made without extensive thinking or research. In this process, individuals were easily influenced by other group members, leading to impulsive and irrational buying behaviors. The theory of herd behavior suggested that public behavior was influenced not only by individual subjective factors but also by the behavior patterns of others. In times of crisis, the irrational and unscientific behavior of the majority of the public was often influenced by the behavior of the surrounding crowd, online public opinion, and the actions of relatives and friends.

Psychological value expectations played a crucial role in determining public participation in panic buying. According to impulsive consumption theory, consumers’ cognitive and psychological states were key factors in deciding whether impulsive consumption occurs. Psychological value expectation refers to the psychological reaction of the public in crisis and can significantly impact their tendency to engage in panic buying. Niu Jinyu demonstrated that during the COVID-19 period, the incidence of price expectations affecting public panic buying and hoarding was 37.4% and 43.1%, respectively [29]. Sun Duoyong utilized prospect theory to analyze the value of goods based on price, and constructed a psychological value expectation model to systematically explain the decision-making mechanism behind public panic buying [30–32]. This highlighted the importance of psychological value expectations as a factor influencing public participation in panic buying during an epidemic.

Group identity plays a significant role in influencing public panic buying. Group identification is crucial when making risky decisions. In crisis situations, even if each person faces specific circumstances when other group members tend to adopt a particular behavior, individuals are more likely to make the same judgment [33]. In essence, group buying is not a formal group but rather an informal and unorganized temporary public. People come together based on shared interests and goals, forming temporary group norms [34, 35]. For instance, during panic buying, the public perceives themselves as members of a temporary group and aligns their risk judgments with those of other group members. They identify the opinions and behaviors of other group members, leading to large-scale buying behaviors.

Therefore, in the event of unforeseen incidents, it is imperative for the public, media, and government to collaborate in order to proactively prevent the occurrence of sudden buying events and effectively respond to those that have already occurred. First, it is crucial to focus on the high-risk group engaged in panic buying and enhance the identification and intervention processes. Relevant authorities should closely monitor the behavioral patterns of these groups prior to the occurrence of such events. Second, it is imperative to improve public health education to enhance individuals’ scientific judgment regarding risk perception and shield them from the potential influence of group identification and reference groups. Therefore, it is imperative for the public to adopt a proactive approach, enhance their awareness of prevention, and minimize instances of panic buying. Third, the media promptly disseminated epidemic prevention and control policies, thereby mitigating the public’s psychological expectations of panic buying. The media should play the benefits of new media platforms, promptly adapt their agendas to meet public demands, effectively communicate information to the public, and mitigate irrational panic. Fourth, the government enhances risk communication with the public to guarantee market supply and oversight. In the event of an outbreak, the government should anticipate the fluctuations in market demand for panic buying goods, proactively increase material stockpiles, and effectively communicate the availability of sufficient material reserves to the public. The aforementioned measures are crucial in mitigating public panic and preventing panic buying.

Limitations

This study has two main shortcomings. In the context of public health emergencies, the influencing factors of public panic buying were very complex, involving variables of multiple dimensions of life. This study draws on the literature and Dholakia impulse consumption model to cover as many important influencing factors as possible. However, there may still be the problem of missing variables, especially the government factors, institutional factors, and legal factors, which was the deficiency of this study. Second, this study only discusses the influencing factors of public panic buying, and does not deeply analyze the mechanisms and paths of various factors; therefore, further research is required.
