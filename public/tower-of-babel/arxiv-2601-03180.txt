Strongly finitary metric monads are too strong
Jiří Adámek

Abstract
. Varieties of quantitative algebras are fully described by their free-algebra monads on the category 𝐌𝐞𝐭 \operatorname{\bf Met} of metric spaces. For a longer time it has been an open problem whether the resulting enriched monads are precisely the strongly finitary ones (determined by their values on finite discrete spaces). We present a counter-example: the variety of algebras on two ε \varepsilon -close binary operations yields a monad which is not strongly finitary. A full characterization of free-algebra monads of varieties is: they are the semi-strongly finitary monads, i.e., weighted colimits of strongly finitary monads (in the category of finitary monads). We deduce that strongly finitary endofunctors on 𝐌𝐞𝐭 \operatorname{\bf Met} are not closed under composition.



1.
Introduction

Quantitative algebras
, which are algebras acting on metric spaces
with nonexpansive operations, were introduced by Mardare, Panangaden and Plotkin
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
as a foundation for semantics of probabilistic or stochastic systems. A basic tool for presenting classes of quantitative algebras are
c
-basic quantitative equations for a cardinal number
c
. We concentrate on the case
c=1
: these equations have the form
t=_{\varepsilon}t^{\prime}
for terms
t
,
t^{\prime}
and a real number
\varepsilon\geq 0
.
An algebra satisfies that equation iff every computation of the terms
t
and
t^{\prime}
yields results of distance at most
\varepsilon
.
A
variety
of quantitative algebras is a class presented by a set of
1
-basic quantitative equations. A prominent example in loc. cit. is the variety of quantitative semilattices.

Every variety
\mathcal{V}
has free algebras on all metric spaces. It thus yields a monad
T_{\mathcal{V}}
on the category
\operatorname{\bf Met}
of metric spaces. Moreover,
\mathcal{V}
is isomorphic to the corresponding category
\operatorname{\bf Met}^{T_{\mathcal{V}}}
of Eilenberg-Moore algebras. (Example: for quantitative semilattices
T_{\mathcal{V}}X
is the finite power-set endowed with the Hausdorff metric.) It has been an open problem for some time to characterize monads of the form
T_{\mathcal{V}}
, see e.g.
[
17
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
23
]
or
[
4
]
.
All strongly finitary monads of Kelly and Lack (
3.1
below) are of that form. One could expect that, conversely, every quantitative monad of a variety is strongly finitary.
Indeed, the analogous statement
is true for a number of other basic categories, e.g. sets, ultrametric spaces
[
2
]
or posets
[
14
]
. But
\operatorname{\bf Met}
is an exception: in Section 8 we present a simple variety
\mathcal{V}
such that
T_{\mathcal{V}}
is not strongly finitary: it consists of two binary operations which are
\varepsilon
-close.

It follows that a composite of strongly finitary monads on
\operatorname{\bf Met}
is not always strongly finitary. Indeed in
[
4
]
it is proved that the compositionality of strongly finitary monads would imply that each
T_{\mathcal{V}}
is strongly finitary.

Definition
.

A monad on
\operatorname{\bf Met}
is
semi-strongly finitary
if it is a weighted colimit of strongly finitary monads in
\operatorname{\bf Mnd}_{f}(\operatorname{\bf Met})
, the category of
\operatorname{\bf Met}
-enriched finitary monads.

Main Theorem.
Monads of the form
T_{\mathcal{V}}
are precisely the semi-strongly finitary ones.

For the category
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
of complete metric spaces, an analogous result holds: varieties of complete quantitative algebras correspond precisely to the weighted colimits of strongly finitary monads. A number of important cases actually yield strongly finitary monads on
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
, e.g. the Hausdorff monad (given by the variety of complete quantitative semilattices) with
T_{\mathcal{V}}X
the space of compact subsets of
X
carrying the Hausdorff metric. In fact, all monads
T_{\mathcal{V}}
of varieties
\mathcal{V}
presented by ordinary equations
(\varepsilon=0)
and of varieties
\mathcal{V}
of unary algebras
are strongly finitary (Sections 6 and 7).

Related Work.
We have announced our example of a variety
\mathcal{V}
not having a strongly finitary free-algebra monad in July 2025 at the conference CT25 in Brno
[
1
]
. Independently, a similar example was annouced by Mardare et al.
[
18
]
, Example 8.3, in September 2025 at the conference GandALF 2025 in Valletta, Malta.

The unpublished paper
[
4
]
with the extended abstract
[
5
]
contains some incomplete arguments. The co-authors unfortunately do not intend publishing a revised version. This leads us to provide new (and, as it happens, much simpler) proofs in Section 4 and 5 below. We also repeat some of the introductory material of
[
4
]
in Section 3.

An alternative approach to varieties of quantitative algebras is presented by J. Rosický
[
23
]
, who uses algebraic theories. A characterization of the corresponding monads is formulated in loc.cit. as an open problem. Corollary 5.3 of
[
23
]
implies that strongly finitary metric monads preserve reflexive coequalizers. This does not seem to follow from our results below.

2.
Finitary and Strongly Finitary Functors

We work here with the monoidal closed categories
\operatorname{\bf Met}
of metric spaces and
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
of complete metric spaces. Finitary and strongly finitary endofunctors, substantially used in subsequent sections, are discussed. Properties of directed colimits can be found in the Appendix.

2.1 Notation
.

\operatorname{\bf Met}
denotes the category of (extended) metric spaces. Objects are metric spaces extended in the sense that the distance
\infty
is allowed. Morphisms are nonexpansive functions
f\colon X\to Y
: for all
x
,
x^{\prime}\in X
we have
d(x,x^{\prime})\geq d\big(f(x),f(x^{\prime})\big)
.

\operatorname{\bf CMet}
is the full subcategory of complete spaces: every Cauchy sequence converges.

2.2 Remark
.

(1)
\operatorname{\bf Met}
is a symmetric monoidal closed category, where the tensor
product

X\otimes Y

is the cartesian product with the
sum metric
:

d\big((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big)=d(x,x^{\prime})+d(y,y^{\prime})\,.

(In contrast, the categorical product
X\times Y
is the cartesian product with the
maximum metric
: the maximum of
d(x,x^{\prime})
and
d(y,y^{\prime})
.) The monoidal unit
I
is a singleton space. The hom-space

[X,Y]

is the space of all morphisms
f\colon X\to Y
with the
supremum metric

d(f,f^{\prime})=\sup_{x\in X}d\big(f(x),f^{\prime}(x)\big)\quad\mbox{for}\quad f,f^{\prime}\colon X\to Y\,.

(2) A
\operatorname{\bf Met}
-
enriched
(or just enriched)
category
is a category with a metric on every hom-set making composition nonexpanding (with respect to the addition metric). A (
\operatorname{\bf Met}
-)
enriched functor

F
between enriched categories is a functor which is locally nonexpanding: for all parallel pairs
f
,
g
in the domain category we have
d(Ff,Fg)\leq d(f,g)
. Enriched natural transformations are the ordinary ones (among enriched functors).
Thus enriched monads are those with the enriched underlying endofunctor. This follows from
\operatorname{\bf Met}(I,-)

being naturally isomorphic to
\operatorname{Id}
.

(3) Given enriched categories
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, the category
[\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}]
of all enriched functors
F\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
and natural transformations is enriched: the distance of natural transformations
f,g\colon F\to F^{\prime}
is
d(f,g)=\sup\limits_{X\in\mathcal{A}}d(f_{X},g_{X})
.

2.3 Notation
.

(1) For every metric space
X
we denote by
|X|
its underlying set.

Conversely, every set is considered as the
discrete space
: all non-zero distances are
\infty
. (This space is complete.) For every space
X
we thus have a morphism

i_{X}\colon|X|\to X\quad\mbox{in}\ \operatorname{\bf Met}

carried by the identity.

(2) By the
power

X^{n}
of a space
X
we always mean the categorical product (with the maximum metric).

(3) Every natural number is considered to be the set
\{0,\dots,n-1\}
.

Directed colimits (indexed by directed posets)
in the categories
\operatorname{\bf Met}
and
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
are not set-based. Consider for example the
\omega
-chain of spaces
M_{n}=\{a,b\}
with
d(a,b)=2^{-n}
(and
\operatorname{id}
as connecting maps). Then
\operatorname{colim}M_{n}
is a singleton space.
A concrete description of directed colimits can be found in the Appendix.

All of the above has an obvious analogy for complete metric spaces: if
X
and
Y
are complete, then so are
X\otimes Y
and
[X,Y]
.
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
-enriched categories and functors are defined as above. Finitary
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
-enriched functors are again those preserving directed colimits.
When speaking about
enriched categories
we always mean enriched over either
\operatorname{\bf Met}
or
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
. Analogously for enriched functors. Where necessary,
we distinguish the two cases explicitly. But usually (except concrete examples) the arguments for
\operatorname{\bf Met}
and
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
are the same.

2.4 Definition
.

An endofunctor is
finitary
if it preserves directed colimits.

A monad is finitary if its underlying functor is.

2.5 Example
.

(1) The endofunctor
(-)^{n}
of the
n
-th categorical power is finitary on
\operatorname{\bf Met}
as well as
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
(for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
). This follows easily from Proposition A2 in the Appendix.

(2) A coproduct of finitary functors is finitary: coproducts commute with colimits.

(3) For every metric space
M
the endofunctor
M\times
- on
\operatorname{\bf Met}
or
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
is finitary. The conditions of Proposition A2 are easy to verify.

(4) The
Hausdorff endofunctor

\mathcal{H}\colon\operatorname{\bf CMet}\to\operatorname{\bf CMet}
is finitary
[
6
]
, Example 3.13. It assigns to a space
X
the space
\mathcal{H}X
of all compact subsets with the Hausdorff metric

d_{\mathcal{H}}(A,B)=\max\big\{\sup_{a\in A}d(a,B),\sup_{b\in B}d(b,A)\big\}\,,

where the distance
d(a,B)
is (as usual)
\inf_{b\in B}d(a,b)
.
(In particular,
d_{\mathcal{H}}(A,\emptyset)=\infty
if
A\neq\emptyset
.) On morphisms
f\colon X\to Y
the Hausdorff functor is given by
A\mapsto f[A]
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an enriched category with a full enriched subcategory
K\colon\mathcal{A}_{0}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{A}
. We recall the concept of (enriched)
Kan extension
. Suppose that the restriction functor

K\cdot(-)\colon[\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}]\to[\mathcal{A}_{0},\mathcal{A}]

has an enriched left adjoint. Then this adjoint is denoted by

F\mapsto\operatorname{\bf Lan}_{K}F\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}\qquad(\mbox{for}\ F\colon\mathcal{A}_{0}\to\mathcal{A})

and is called the
left
\operatorname{\bf Kan}
extension
along
K
. Thus
T=\operatorname{\bf Lan}_{K}F
is an enriched endofunctor equipped with a natural transformation
\tau\colon F\to TK
with the obvious universal property.

2.6 Notation
.

The full embedding of the category of finite sets (or finite discrete spaces) is denoted by

K\colon\operatorname{\bf Set}_{f}\to\operatorname{\bf Met}\quad\mbox{or}\quad K\colon\operatorname{\bf Set}_{f}\hookrightarrow\operatorname{\bf CMet}\,.

The following definition is analogous to the definition presented by Kelly and Lack
[
13
]
for locally finitely presentable categories.

2.7 Definition
.

An enriched endofunctor
T
of
\operatorname{\bf Met}
or
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
is
strongly finitary
if it is obtained from its restriction
TK
to finite discrete spaces via the left Kan extension:

T=\operatorname{\bf Lan}_{K}TK\,.

A monad is strongly finitary if its underlying endofunctor is.

2.8 Remark
.

Every strongly finitary monad is finitary (Theorem
3.17
and Corollary
3.16
), but not conversely (Proposition
8.6
).

We now recall a condition characterizing strong finitarity, proved in
[
4
]
.
We first need the following

.

For every metric space
X
and every
\varepsilon\geq 0
we denote by
\Delta_{\varepsilon}X\subseteq|X|^{2}
the

\varepsilon

are denoted by

respectively. Recall the identity-carried morphism

2.11 Remark
.

By
[
4
]
, Proposition 2.20, every space
X
in
\operatorname{\bf Met}
is a weighted colimit of discrete spaces.
The domain
\mathcal{B}
of that diagram is the linearly ordered real interval
(0,\infty)
, enlarged by two cocones with domain
0
, denoted by
l_{\varepsilon}
,
r_{\varepsilon}\colon\varepsilon\to 0
(
\varepsilon>0
). The diagram
D_{X}\colon\mathcal{B}\to\operatorname{\bf Met}
takes
0
to
|X|
and
\varepsilon>0
to

. The weight
B\colon\mathcal{B}^{\operatorname{op}}\to\operatorname{\bf Met}
takes

to
\{p\}
and
\varepsilon

where

are as expected. We have
X=\operatorname{colim}\limits_{B}D_{X}
.

Analogously for
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
: for every complete space
X
we have
X=\operatorname{colim}\limits_{B}D_{X}
.

2.12 Examples
.

(1) The finitary endofunctor
TX=X^{n}
is strongly finitary on
\operatorname{\bf Met}
or
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
(for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
).
Indeed,
Ti_{X}=\operatorname{id}
, and a function
f\colon|X^{n}|\to Y
satisfying (
2.1
) is clearly nonexpanding,
f\colon X^{n}\to Y
.

In contrast, if
M=\{0,1\}
is the space with
d(0,1)
finite, then the functor
T=\operatorname{\bf Met}(M,-)
, assigning to
X
the subspace of
X\times X
on
\Delta_{\varepsilon}X
, is not strongly finitary: the morphism
Ti_{M}
is not surjective.

(2) A coproduct of strongly finitary functor is strongly finitary. Coproducts in
\operatorname{\bf Met}
or
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
are disjoint unions with distance
\infty
between elements of distinct summands. It is easy to verify that if all summands fulfil a.–c. of Proposition
2.10
, then so does the coproduct.

(3) The Hausdorff endofunctor
\mathcal{H}\colon\operatorname{\bf CMet}\to\operatorname{\bf CMet}
is strongly finitary, see Section 7.

(4) For every metric space
M
the endofunctor
T=M\times-
is strongly finitary. Given a nonexpanding map
f\colon M\times|X|\to Y
satisfying (
2.1
), then
f\colon M\times X\to Y
is also nonexpanding. This means nonexpanding in each component, and
f(m,-)
is clearly nonexpanding.

(5) If
T
is a strongly finitary functor, then for every space
M
the functor
X\mapsto M\otimes TX
is also strongly finitary.

3.
Varieties of Quantitative Algebras

We recall varieties of quantitative algebra from
[
19
]
.
Every variety
\mathcal{V}
is known to be isomorphic to the category
\operatorname{\bf Met}^{T_{\mathcal{V}}}
of Eilenberg-Moore algebras, where
T_{\mathcal{V}}
is the free-algebra monad.
We prove that
T_{\mathcal{V}}
is enriched and finitary. (In the reverse direction, we later prove that every semi-strongly finitary monad has the form
T_{\mathcal{V}}
.) We conclude that varieties bijectively correspond to semi-strongly finitary monad.

Throughout our paper
\Sigma=(\Sigma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denotes a finitary signature:
\Sigma_{n}
is the set of
n
-ary operation symbols.

3.1 Definition

(
[
19
]
)
.

A
quantitative algebra
is a metric space
A
endowed with nonexpanding operations

\sigma_{A}\colon A^{n}\to A\qquad(\sigma\in\Sigma_{n})

with respect to the maximum metric on
A^{n}
. It is
complete
if
A
is a complete metric space.

3.2 Notation
.

The category of quantitative algebras and nonexpanding homomorphisms is denoted by

\Sigma\mbox{-}\operatorname{\bf Met}\,.

Analogously for its full subcategory

\Sigma\mbox{-}\operatorname{\bf CMet}

of complete quantitative algebras.

3.3 Example
.

Term algebras. In universal algebra the free
\Sigma
-algebra on a set
V
of variables is the algebra
T_{\Sigma}V
of terms. Terms are either variables, or composite terms
\sigma(t_{i})_{i<n}
for
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}
and an
n
-tuple
(t_{i})_{i<n}
of terms. The
depth

\delta
of a variable or a constant is
0
, and the term
s=\sigma(t_{i})_{i<n}
for
n\geq 1
has depth

\delta(s)=1+\max_{i}\delta(t_{i})\,.

Analogously, for the free quantitative algebra
T_{\Sigma}X
on a space
X
: its underlying algebra is the algebra
T_{\Sigma}|X|
of terms. Let us call terms
t
and
t^{\prime}

similar
if we can obtain
t^{\prime}
from
t
by changing some variables. Thus, all pairs of variables are similar. And terms similar to
\sigma(t_{i})
are precisely the terms
\sigma(t_{i}^{\prime})
with
t_{i}
and
t_{i}^{\prime}
similar for each
i
. The metric

d_{X}^{\ast}

of the free quantitative algebra
T_{\Sigma}X
is defined recursively as follows:

d_{X}^{\ast}(t,t^{\prime})=\begin{cases}d_{X}(t,t^{\prime})&\mbox{if\ \ }t,t^{\prime}\in X\\
\max\limits_{i<n}d_{X}^{\ast}(t_{i},t_{i}^{\prime})&\mbox{if}\ \ t=\sigma(t_{i})\ \mbox{and}\ t^{\prime}=\sigma(t_{i}^{\prime})\\
\infty&\mbox{if}\ \ t\ \mbox{is not similar to}\ t^{\prime}\end{cases}

3.4 Lemma

(
[
2
]
, Remark 2.4)
.

The quantitative algebra
(T_{\Sigma}X,d_{X}^{\ast})
is free: for every quantitative algebra
A
and every nonexpanding function
f\colon X\to A
there is a unique extension to a nonexpanding homomorphism

f^{\#}\colon(T_{\Sigma}X,d_{X}^{\ast})\to A\,.

Indeed, the classical extension
f^{\#}
in universal algebra is nonexpanding with respect to the metric
d_{X}^{\ast}
.

For a complete metric space
X
the situation is analogous: the space
T_{\Sigma}X
has the complete metric
d_{X}^{\ast}
above. This is the free algebra on
X
in
\Sigma
-
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
.

3.5 Corollary
.

The monad
T_{\Sigma}
of free quantitative algebras on
\operatorname{\bf Met}
or
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
is strongly finitary. It preserves surjective morphisms.

Indeed, let
\approx
be the similarity equivalence on
T_{\Sigma}|X|
. Then
T_{\Sigma}X
is the coproduct of spaces
X^{n}
, one copy for every equivalence class of
\approx
of terms on precisely
n
variables. Moreover, this coproduct is independent of
X
. In other words, the functor
T_{\Sigma}
is a coproduct of functors
(-)^{n}
, one copy of each equivalence class of
\approx
of terms on
n
variables. By (1) and (2) in Example
2.5
,
T_{\Sigma}
is strongly finitary. Preservation of surjective morphisms is clear.

3.6 Definition

(
[
19
]
)
.

A (1-basic)
quantitative equation
is an expression
t=_{\varepsilon}t^{\prime}
, where
t
and
t^{\prime}
are terms in
T_{\Sigma}V
for a finite set
V
of variables, and
\varepsilon\geq 0
is a real number.

A quantitative algebra
A

satisfies
this equation provided that every interpretation
f\colon V\to A
of the variables fulfils

d_{A}\big(f^{\#}(t),f^{\#}(t^{\prime})\big)\leq\varepsilon\,.

A
variety

\mathcal{V}
(aka 1-basic variety) of quantitative algebras is a full subcategory
of
\Sigma
-
\operatorname{\bf Met}
specified by a set of quantitative equations. Thus, a quantitative algebra lies in
\mathcal{V}
iff it satisfies each of the given equations.

We write
t=t^{\prime}
in place of
t=_{0}t^{\prime}
, and call such equations
ordinary
.
In
[
20
]
the number
\varepsilon
was assumed to be rational. But this makes no difference: if
\varepsilon>0
is irrational, choose any dicreasing sequence
\varepsilon(n)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, of rationals converging to
\varepsilon
. Then the equation
t=_{\varepsilon}t^{\prime}
is equivalent to the set of equations
t=_{\varepsilon(n)}t^{\prime}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

3.7 Examples
.

(1) Quantitative monoids.
This is the variety presented by the usual signature (of a binary operation and constant
{e}
) and the usual ordinary equations:
(xy)z=x(yz)
,
xe=x
and
ex=x
. Observe that by definition of
\Sigma
-
\operatorname{\bf Met}
this means that multiplication is nonexpanding:

d(xy,x^{\prime}y^{\prime})\leq\max\{d(x,x^{\prime}),d(y,y^{\prime})\}\,.

(2)
Actions of quantitative monoids.

Let us recall that an action of a classical monoid
M
on a set
X
is a mapping from
M\times X
to
X
(notation
(m,x)\mapsto mx
) whose curryfication is a monoid homomorphism from
M
to the composition monoid
\operatorname{\bf Set}(X,X)
. Analogously, given a quantitative monoid
M
, its (quantitative)
action
on a metric space
X
is a nonexpanding homomorphism from
M
to
[X,X]
in
\operatorname{\bf Met}
. This is a monoid action such that
d(mx,my)\leq d(x,y)
for all
(x,y)\in X^{2}
, and
d(mx,m^{\prime}x)\leq d(m,m^{\prime})
for all
(m,m^{\prime})\in M\times M
.

This is a variety of quantitative
\Sigma
-algebras, where
\Sigma
consists of unary operations
m(-)
for
m\in M
. It is presented by the usual ordinary equations:

m(m^{\prime}x)=(mm^{\prime})x\quad\mbox{and}\quad ex=x\,,

together with the following quantitative equations

mx=_{\varepsilon}m^{\prime}x\quad\mbox{where}\quad\varepsilon=d(m,m^{\prime})\,.

(3)
Quantitative semilattices.
By a semilattice we mean a join-semilattice with a bottom. Equivalently, a commutative and idempotent monoid.

Quantitative semilattices are semilattices acting on a metric space with non-expanding binary joins. In other words, commutative and idempotent quantitative monoids.

(4)
Small metric spaces
. For the empty signature,
\Sigma
-
\operatorname{\bf Met}
is simply
\operatorname{\bf Met}
. The quantitative equation

x=_{\varepsilon}y

presents all metric spaces of diameter at most
\varepsilon
.

Every variety
\mathcal{V}
is equipped with the obvious forgetful functor
U_{\mathcal{V}}\colon

\mathcal{V}\to\operatorname{\bf Met}
.

All the above has an analogous formulation for complete spaces. A variety
\mathcal{V}
is a subcategory of
\Sigma
-
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
specified by a set of quantitative equations.

3.8 Theorem

(
[
19
]
, Sections 6 and 7)
.

(1)
In every variety
\mathcal{V}
of quantitative algebras each space
X
generates a free algebra
F_{\mathcal{V}}X
. That is, the forgetful functor

U_{\mathcal{V}}\colon\mathcal{V}\to\operatorname{\bf Met}

has a left adjoint
F_{\mathcal{V}}\colon\operatorname{\bf Met}\to\mathcal{V}
.

(2)

3.11 Proposition
.

For every variety
\mathcal{V}
the monad
T_{\mathcal{V}}
is enriched.

Proof.

Let morphisms
f
,
g\colon X\to Y
have distance
d(f,g)=\delta
. We prove that

d\big(T_{\mathcal{V}}f(s),T_{\mathcal{V}}g(s)\big)\leq\delta

holds for all
s\in T_{\mathcal{V}}X
; thus we have
d(T_{\mathcal{V}}f,T_{\mathcal{V}}g)\leq d(f,g)
. Indeed, denote by

the set of all

satisfying the desired inequality. Then
S
contains the image of
\eta_{X}\colon X\to T_{\mathcal{V}}X
: if

, then

d\big(T_{\mathcal{V}}f(s),T_{\mathcal{V}}g(s)\big)=d\big(f(s_{0}),g(s_{0})\big)\leq\delta\,.

And

is closed under all operations: given

and

, then for

we get

T_{\mathcal{V}}f(s)=\sigma_{T_{\mathcal{V}}X}\big(T_{\mathcal{V}}f(s_{i})\big)\,,

and analogously for

. Since

is nonexpanding, this implies

There exists no proper subset of
T_{\mathcal{V}}X
which contains the image of
\eta_{X}
and is closed under operations. Thus

, as claimed.
∎

4.1 Definition
.

A

over
\operatorname{\bf Met}
or
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
is an enriched category together with an enriched functor

from
\mathcal{K}
to
\operatorname{\bf Met}
or

which is faithful:

A

is an enriched functor such that

U_{\mathcal{K}}=U_{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}\cdot F\,.

Examples of concrete categories are varieties, and monadic categories
\operatorname{\bf Met}^{T}
or
\operatorname{\bf CMet}^{T}

4.2 Theorem

(

]

Every variety

4.10 Lemma
.

Let

be concrete functors with the corresponding monad morphisms
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\colon T_{\mathcal{W}}\to T_{\mathcal{V}}
. Then

d(G,G^{\prime})=d(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\,.

Proof.

We denote the monads
T_{\mathcal{V}}
and
T_{\bar{\mathcal{V}}}
by
(T,\mu,\eta)
and
(\bar{T},\bar{\mu},\bar{\eta})
, respectively. The free algebra

is mapped by

to

GA\equiv\bar{T}Tm\xrightarrow{\gamma_{Tm}}TTm\xrightarrow{\mu_{m}}Tm

For every
n
-tuple in it,

, we have, by Remark
4.3
(3) that

is given by

Analogously for
G^{\prime}A
. Thus,

t_{GA}(f)=\mu_{m}\cdot Tf\cdot\gamma_{n}\cdot k\quad\mbox{and}\quad t_{G^{\prime}A}(f)=\mu_{m}\cdot Tf\cdot\gamma^{\prime}_{n}\cdot k\,.

Consequently,

d\big(t_{GA}(f),t_{G^{\prime}A}(f)\big)\leq d(\gamma_{m},\gamma^{\prime}_{n})\,.

Since this holds for all

, we have proved

d(G,G^{\prime})=\sup d\big(t_{GA}(f),t_{G^{\prime}A}(f)\big)\leq d(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\,.

To prove the reverse inequality, we verify

d(\gamma_{n},\gamma^{\prime}_{n})\leq d(G,G^{\prime})\quad\mbox{for all \ $n\in\mathbb{N}$}

We apply the above to

m=n\quad\mbox{and}\ \ f=\eta_{n}\colon n\to T_{\mathcal{V}}n

to get

t_{GA}(f)=\mu_{n}\cdot T\eta_{n}\cdot\gamma_{n}\cdot k=\gamma_{n}\cdot k\,,

and, analogously,

∎

5.

Appendix: Directed Colimits in
\operatorname{\bf Met}
and
\operatorname{\bf CMet}

A poset is directed if every finite subset has an upper bound. Colimits
of diagrams with such domains are called directed colimits. The following proposition was formulated in
[
4
]
, but the proof there is incomplete.

A1 Proposition.

Let
D=(D_{i})_{i\in I}
be a directed diagram in
\operatorname{\bf Met}
with
objects
(D_{i},d_{i})
and
connecting morphisms
f_{ij}\colon D_{i}\to D_{j}
for
i\leq j
. A cocone
c_{i}\colon D_{i}\to C
(
i\in I
) of
D
is a colimit iff

(1) It is collectively surjective:
C=\bigcup\limits_{i\in I}c_{i}[D_{i}]
.

(2) The distance of elements
x,x^{\prime}
of
C
is given by

d(x,x^{\prime})=\inf_{j\geq i}d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(y),f_{ij}(y^{\prime})\big)\,.

Here
i\in I
is an arbitrary index and
y,y^{\prime}\in D_{i}
are arbitrary elements with
x=c_{i}(y)
and
x^{\prime}=c_{i}(y^{\prime})
.

Proof.

(a) Sufficiency: suppose that conditions (1) and (2) hold. For every cocone
h_{i}\colon D_{i}\to X
(
i\in I
) we are to find a morphism
h\colon C\to X
with
h_{i}=h\cdot c_{i}
(
i\in I
). (Uniqueness
then follows from Item (1)). Define the value of
h
in
x\in C
as follows:

h(x)=h_{i}(y)\quad\mbox{for any}\quad i\in I\quad\mbox{and}\quad y\in D_{i}\quad\mbox{with}\quad x=c_{i}(y)\,.

(a1) This is independent of the choice of
i
, since the poset
I
is directed. We now verify independence of the choice of
y
. Suppose
x=c_{i}(y^{\prime})
. Then we derive
c_{i}(y)=c_{i}(y^{\prime})
from Condition (2) applied to
x^{\prime}=x
. We namely verify, for every
\varepsilon>0
, that

d_{i}\big(c_{i}(y),c_{i}(y^{\prime})\big)<\varepsilon\,.

In fact, we know that the infimum of all
d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(y),f_{ij}(y^{\prime})\big)
is
d(x,x)=0
. Thus, some
j\geq i
fulfils

d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(y),f_{ij}(y^{\prime})\big)<\varepsilon\,.

Since
c_{i}=c_{j}\cdot f_{ij}
, we get
d_{i}\big(c_{i}(y),c_{i}(y^{\prime})\big)<\varepsilon
.

(a2) The morphism
h
is nonexpanding. Indeed, given
d(x,x^{\prime})=\varepsilon
in
C
, since
I
is directed, we have
y,y^{\prime}\in D_{i}
with
x=c_{i}(y)
and
x^{\prime}=c_{i}(y^{\prime})
, using Item (1). We prove
d\big(h(x),h(x^{\prime})\big)\leq\varepsilon
by verifying

d\big(h(x),h(x^{\prime})\big)<\delta+\varepsilon\quad\mbox{for each}\quad\delta>0\,.

By Condition (2) there exists
j\geq i
with

d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(y),f_{ij}(y^{\prime})\big)<\varepsilon+\delta\,,

and we apply
c_{j}=c_{j}\cdot f_{ij}
, again.

(a3) The equality
h_{i}=h\cdot c_{i}
is clear.

(b) Necessity: suppose that the cocone
(c_{i})
is a colimit. We verify Conditions (1) and (2). The metric of
C
is denoted by
d_{C}
.

(b1) Denote by
m\colon C^{\prime}\hookrightarrow C
the subspace of
C
on the union of all
c_{i}[D_{i}]
. Then we have nonexpanding maps
c_{i}^{\prime}\colon D_{i}\to C^{\prime}
(
i\in I
) forming a cocone of
D
with
c_{i}=m\cdot c_{i}^{\prime}
. Let
h\colon C\to C^{\prime}
be the unique morphism with
c_{i}^{\prime}=h\cdot c_{i}
(
i\in I
). Then
m\cdot h\cdot c_{i}=c_{i}
(
i\in I
) implies
m\cdot h=\operatorname{id}
, thus,
C^{\prime}=C
.

(b2) To verify Condition (2), we denote the infimum in it by
\bar{d}(x,x^{\prime})
, and prove in (b3) that it is well defined and forms a metric.
This concludes the proof: we derive that
d_{C}=\bar{d}
. Indeed,
d_{C}\leq\bar{d}
follows from
c_{i}=c_{j}\cdot f_{ij}
:

\displaystyle\bar{d}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=d_{C}\big(c_{i}(y),c_{i}(y^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle=d_{C}\big(c_{j}\cdot f_{ij}(y),c_{j}\cdot f_{ij}(y^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle\leq d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(y),f_{ij}(y^{\prime})\big)\,.

To verify
d_{C}\geq\bar{d}
, it is sufficient to observe that each
c_{i}
is nonexpanding with respect to
\bar{d}
:

\displaystyle d_{i}(y,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=d_{i}\big(f_{ii}(y),f_{ii}(y^{\prime})\big)\qquad f_{ii}=\operatorname{id}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{j\geq i}d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(y),f_{ij}(y^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle=d_{C}\big(c_{i}(y),c_{i}(y^{\prime})\big)\,.

Thus, we have
h\colon(C,d)\to(C,\bar{d})
with
h\cdot c_{i}=c_{i}
(
i\in I
). In other words,
h=\operatorname{id}
, and
d_{C}\geq\bar{d}
.

(b3) We now prove the promised facts about
\bar{d}
.

(i)
\bar{d}(x,x^{\prime})
is independent of choice of
i
and
y,y^{\prime}\in D_{i}
. Since
I
is directed, the independence of
i\in I
is clear. We thus just need to prove that, given
z,z^{\prime}\in D_{i}
with
c_{i}(z)=x=c_{i}(y)
and
c_{i}(z^{\prime})=x^{\prime}=c_{i}(z^{\prime})
, then the two corresponding infima are equal. By symmetry, we only show that

\inf_{j\geq i}d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(y),f_{ij}(y^{\prime})\big)\leq\inf_{j\geq i}d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(z),f_{ij}(z^{\prime})\big)\,.

(The running index
j
can be used on both sides since
I
is directed.) We again verify that for every
\varepsilon>0
the inequality with the righ-hand side enlarged by
\varepsilon
holds. From
c_{i}(z)=c_{i}(y)
, Condition (2) yields an index
j^{\prime}\geq i
with

d_{j^{\prime}}\big(f_{ij^{\prime}}(y),f_{ij^{\prime}}(z)\big)<\varepsilon/2\,.

Analogously for
z^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
. Moreover, we can
assume
j^{\prime}=j
, since the poset
I
is directed. From the triangle inequality in
D_{j}
we obtain

\displaystyle d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(y),f_{ij}(y^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(y),f_{ij}(z)\big)+d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(z),f_{ij}(z^{\prime})\big)+d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(z^{\prime}),f_{ij}(y^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\ \varepsilon/2+d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(z),f_{ij}(z^{\prime})\big)+\varepsilon/2

as desired.

(ii) The function
\bar{d}
is clearly symmetric, and it fulfils
\bar{d}(x,x)=0
. Since
\bar{d}\geq d_{C}
, that for all
x\neq x^{\prime}
we have
\bar{d}(x,x^{\prime})>0
.

It remains to prove the triangle inequality for
\bar{d}
. Given
x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}
in
C
, we can find
i\in I
containing correponding elements
y,y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime}
in
D_{i}
. Since a directed infimum of sums (of reals) equals the sum of the corresponding infima, we get

\displaystyle\bar{d}(x,x^{\prime})+\bar{d}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\inf_{j\geq i}\big\{d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(y),f_{ij}(y^{\prime})+d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(y^{\prime}),f_{ij}(y^{\prime\prime})\big)\big\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{j\geq i}d_{j}\big(f_{ij}(y),f_{ij}(y^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle=d(x,x^{\prime\prime})\,.

∎

A2 Corollary
(Directed colimits in
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
).
A cocone
c_{i}\colon D_{i}\to C
(
i\in I
) of a directed diagram in
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
is a colimit iff

(1) It is collectively dense:
C=\overline{\bigcup\limits_{i\in I}c_{i}[D_{i}]}
.

(2) The metric of
C
is given by the formula (2) of Proposition A1.

Proof.

The full subcategory
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
is reflective in
\operatorname{\bf Met}
: for every metric space
X
its Cauchy completion
X\hookrightarrow X^{\ast}
is reflection. Indeed: for every complete space
Y
and every nonexpansive map
f\colon X\to Y
the unique continuous extension
\bar{f}\colon X^{\ast}\to Y
is easily seen to be nonexpanding.

(a) Sufficiency. If Conditions (1) and (2) hold, then for
C^{\prime}=\bigcup\limits_{i\in I}c_{i}[D_{i}]
, a dense subspace of
C
, we restrict the maps
c_{i}
to nonexpanding maps
c_{i}^{\prime}\colon D_{i}\to C^{\prime}
. The resulting cocone (in
\operatorname{\bf Met}
) is a colimit of
D
in
\operatorname{\bf Met}
due to Proposition A1. Since colimits in
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
are obtained by applying the Cauchy completion to the corresponding colimits in
\operatorname{\bf Met}
, and since
C=(C^{\prime})^{*}
, we conclude that
(c_{i})
is a colimit cocone in
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
.

(b) Necessity. Given a colimit cone
c_{i}\colon D_{i}\to C
(
i\in I
) in
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
, then
C=(C^{\prime})^{*}
for the corresponding colimit cocone
c_{i}^{\prime}\colon D_{i}\to C^{\prime}
(
i\in I
) in
\operatorname{\bf Met}
. From the fact that the latter cocone satisfies (1) and (2) of Proposition A1, we conclude that the above (1) and (2) hold for the cocone
(c_{i})
.
∎

A3 Corollary.

Every space
X
in
\operatorname{\bf Met}
or
\operatorname{\bf CMet}
is the directed colimit of the diagram
D_{X}
of all of its finite subspaces.

That is, the objects of
D_{X}
are the finite subspaces, and morphisms
f\colon A\to B
are the inclusion maps (whenever
A\subseteq B
). The colimit cocone consists of the inclusion maps into
X
. The verification of the properties (1) and (2) above is easy.
