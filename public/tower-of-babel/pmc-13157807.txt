Prevalence and determinants of drug-resistant pulmonary tuberculosis in the MENA region: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Oumar Abakar Mahamed, Noussissy Sandokane Hounnoukon, Soraya Laraqui Houssaini, Imane El Faziki, Meryeme Kirat, Karima El Rhazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) is a constraining challenge for tuberculosis (TB) control worldwide. The Middle East and North African (MENA) region represents a significant part of the global MDR-TB burden.AimTo estimate the pooled prevalence of MDR-TB and its determinants in the MENA region.MethodsWe searched for studies published in English and French on the subject up to 31 January 2026 on Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus and Cochrane, without time restriction. Original studies reporting data on the prevalence of MDR-TB in individuals living in the MENA region were selected. The meta-analysis was done using the random effects model considering the heterogeneity among the included studies and I2 statistic was used to assess the heterogeneity.ResultsA total of 1239 articles were identified and 25 studies from 6 countries were included in this review. The prevalence of MDR-TB in the MENA region ranged from 0% (95% CI 0% to 4.1%) to 17.1% (95% CI 10.6% to 25.4%). The pooled prevalence was 3.54% (95% CI 2.18% to 5.72%) with a high heterogeneity, I2=95.6%; 95% CI 94.4% to 96.5%. Previous exposition to TB treatment, HIV infection, smoking and the presence of comorbidities were the most reported associated factors.ConclusionThis review underscores the persistence of MDR-TB in the MENA region, suggesting insufficiency in TB control. Multisectoral interventions integrating strong prevention measures, standardised treatment protocols and measures to enhance treatment adherence should be implemented.Keywords: Tuberculosis

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) constitutes a major global public health problem and is the ninth cause of mortality worldwide.1 TB is also the first cause of mortality due to a single pathogen, especially in developing countries, causing 1.25 million deaths worldwide by 2023. Among people living with HIV, it is the principal cause of mortality and is one of the main pathogens linked to death due to antimicrobial resistance.2

Antimicrobial resistance is one of the greatest challenges facing humanity. Resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis constitutes a serious threat and compromises efforts to control the TB epidemic on a global scale. In 2018, the WHO estimated the new cases of rifampin-resistant tuberculosis to nearly 500 000 worldwide, with the majority presenting with multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB). Only one-third of these cases have been detected and notified. In the same year, 3.4% of newly diagnosed cases and 18% of previously treated cases involved people with MDR-TB or rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB).3 Globally, the annual incidence of MDR/RR-TB in 2023 was estimated to be 400 000 (95% CI 360 000 to 440 000) per year.4 In 2021, the Middle East and North African (MENA) region accounts for 8% of the global MDR-TB burden, with only 8% of the cases have been treated.5

In its Sustainable Development Goals, the United Nations set the target of halting the TB epidemic by 2030. The specific targets for the elimination of TB include a ‘90% reduction in TB-related deaths and an 80% reduction in the incidence rate by 2030’.6 From 1990 to 2019, a decrease of 42.19% of TB cases was observed in the MENA region, but this evolution is threatened by the rise of MDR-TB cases, hindering the efforts towards TB elimination.5 7

MDR-TB strain is spreading continuously due to TB mismanagement and ongoing transmission between individuals.8 It is associated with high treatment failure and mortality rates in the MENA region.9,12

The most cited risk factors include previous TB treatment, age ≥40 years, unemployment, prior treatment failure, non-adherence to TB treatment, HIV positive status and lack of adequate knowledge about TB.7 10 Conversely, young age, living in urban settings and higher educational attainment are known as profiles with a lower incidence of MDR-TB.13

The MENA region has social, economic and cultural particularities that can influence transmission dynamics, care-seeking behaviour and treatment outcomes, offering valuable insights for optimising strategies to MDR-TB management and control.

In order to optimise the development of strategies to combat TB, it is vital to gain an in-depth understanding of first-line drug resistance. To the best of our knowledge, there are no systematic reviews and meta-analyses on the subject in the region.

The aim of this study is to carry out a systematic review and meta-analysis on the prevalence and determinants of MDR-TB in the MENA region.

Method

A systematic review and meta-analysis of studies on the prevalence and determinants of MDR-TB in the MENA region was performed in accordance with the 2020 ‘Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews (PRISMA)’ guidelines.14 The review protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024617879).

Search and selection process

A systematic research for original articles was conducted in databases, namely in Web of Science (https://www.webofscience.com), PubMed (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), Scopus (http://www.scopus.com) and Cochrane (http://www.thecochranelibrary.com). We used the keywords for ‘Prevalence’, ‘Drug Resistant TB’ and ‘North African and Middle East’ countries combined using Boolean operators. All published studies until 31 January 2026 were considered in this review. The PRISMA flowchart presents the selection process in detail in figure 1. The full research equation is provided in online supplemental table S1 as supplemental material.

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews (PRISMA) flowchart of the search process.

Selection criteria

We included original studies reporting data on drug-resistant TB, with a sample size >50, and which reported prevalence and determinants of drug-resistant pulmonary TB and conducted in the MENA region; studies with sufficient data; in English or French; and studies with their full text available were included in this review. There was no time restriction. Only observational studies were included in this review. Ecological, laboratory and animal studies, pilot studies, clinical trials, literature and systematic reviews, research protocols, letters to the editor, commentaries, viewpoints, case series, case reports, books, chapters of books, correspondences, conference abstracts and off-topic studies were excluded. Studies with extrapulmonary TB and those on children were also excluded.

‘MDR-TB is defined as TB strains with simultaneous resistance to isoniazid and rifampicin. Previously treated cases include patients who have received prior treatment for TB for a period of one month or more. Monoresistance is defined as resistance to a single first-line anti-TB drug. Rifampicin resistance is the resistance to rifampicin and isoniazid resistance the resistance to it, with or without resistance to other anti-TB drugs’.15

The identified articles from the databases were sent to Zotero software and exported to Excel after a first deduplication.

Data extraction

It was done by different researchers on Excel 2021 with a checklist. The extracted data were: first author’s name, year of publication, study location, study design, sample size, age (mean), gender (% male), method used, prevalence of drug-resistant TB (number of mono-resistant cases to isoniazid or rifampicin, MDR-TB cases, type of cases) and main associated factors.

Methodological quality assessment

It was performed using ‘the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal tools for Studies Reporting Prevalence Data’,16 according to the following criteria: precision and validity of questions, representativeness of the study population (sample frame, participants selection, sample size, response rate), statistical analyses appropriateness and methodological weaknesses. Two reviewers conducted it separately, and disagreements were discussed until a consensus was obtained.

Statistical analysis

The data were structured to perform a meta-analysis in order to estimate the prevalence of MDR-TB. The heterogeneity of the studies was then assessed using the I2 statistic. To explore differences in effect sizes based on study characteristics, a meta-regression analysis was conducted, considering independent variables such as sample size, study region (MENA), publication year, study quality score, prior TB treatment (Yes/No) and study design (cross-sectional or case–control/cohort). The Begg’s test was used to check publication bias; Forest plots were drawn. Analyses were performed using R V.4.4.217 with the ‘meta’ package using the ‘metaprop’ (meta-analysis for proportions), ‘metabias’ (to evaluate publication bias) and ‘metareg’ (for the meta-regression analysis) functions.

Results

Search results

At the end of the search, 1239 articles were identified. After deduplication and an initial elimination of documents from outside the MENA region (n=762), books and book chapters (n=31), title and abstract selection excluded 249 articles based on the inclusion criteria. Seven articles were eliminated because their full texts were not available. We read the full text of 46 articles for eligibility and 21 were excluded. Finally, 25 studies were included for the systematic review and 24 for the meta-analysis (figure 1).

Studies’ characteristics

Of the 25 included studies,18,42 published between 2000–2025, 10 were conducted in Iran,18,27 5 in Saudi Arabia,28,3141 3 in Israel,32,34 4 in Turkey,35,3742 2 in Sudan38 39 and 1 in Egypt.40 24 of the 25 studies were cross-sectional and the remaining 1 was a case–control study27 (table 1). The sample size ranged from 55 to 9463 (mean=939.79, median=300, sum=32 018). The mean quality assessment score was 7.48 and ranged from 5 to 9 (median=8). 22 studies (88.0%) scored 7–9 points and 3 studies (12.0%) scored 5–6 (online supplemental table S2).

MDR-TB Prevalence

In the 24 studies included in the quantitative synthesis (sample size: 22 555), the MDR-TB prevalence ranged from 0% (95% CI 0% to 4.1%) to 17.1% (95% CI 10.6% to 25.4%). The I2 test showed a high heterogeneity (I2=95.6%; 95% CI 94.4% to 96.5%). The pooled prevalence, estimated using the random effects method, was 3.54% (95% CI 2.18% to 5.72%) (figure 2). The Begg and Mazumdar test for publication bias did not show the existence of publication bias (p=0.19). The funnel plot is presented in online supplemental figure S-1 as supplemental material.

Forest plot of the meta-analysis on MDR-TB prevalence in the Middle East and North African region. MDR-TB, multidrug-resistant tuberculosis.

The pooled prevalence of resistance to isoniazid was 5.86% (95% CI 3.38% to 9.98%); I2=96.9% (95% CI 96.1% to 97.6%); Begg and Mazumdar correlation test: p=0.55. The rifampicin resistance pooled prevalence was 2.46% (95% CI 1.26% to 4.75%); I2=97.1% (95% CI 96.3% to 97.7%); Begg and Mazumdar correlation test: p=0.34. The forest plots of the isoniazid and rifampicin resistance prevalence are provided as figure 3.

Forest plots of the meta-analysis on isoniazid and rifampicin resistance prevalence in the Middle East and North African region.

A subgroup analysis considering the study region (North African vs Middle East) was performed. It showed a pooled prevalence of 15.50% (95% CI 12.95% to 18.44%) in the North African region and 2.78% (95% CI 1.72% to 4.46%) in the Middle East region, (p<0.0001) (figure 2).

In the meta-regression analysis, only sample size and study region had significant effect on the prevalence of MDR-TB. It showed a positive but not significant association with the quality of study (JBI score), suggesting that it did not explain the heterogeneity in the prevalence estimates (table 2). Determinants of MDR-TB

The determinants of MDR-TB were reported in a limited number of studies with different type of effect sizes which restricted quantitative pooling. A history of TB treatment was the most cited (5 studies out of 25) and the most significant risk factor (ORa: 1.4–19.9).28 31 Other factors included HIV infection (p<0.01),26 smoking (p=0.02),25 the presence of comorbidities (p=0.01)30 and some demographic factors such as male sex (p<0.01) and young age (p=0.01–0.05).26 27 31 They are presented in table 1.

Discussion

The pooled prevalence of MDR-TB in the MENA region was estimated at 3.54% (95% CI 2.18% to 5.72%) with a high heterogeneity (I2=95.6%; 95% CI 94.4% to 96.5%). This finding is lower than the MDR-TB in Iran which ranged from 5.1% to 11.3% and the global pooled prevalence reported by Salari et al, which was estimated at 11.6% (95% CI 9.1% to 14.5%).43 44 The lower prevalence observed in this study compared with that of Salari et al could be explained by our focus on the MENA region, whereas that review included several regions (Eastern Europe, South America, Central Asia and East and Southern regions of Africa) heavily affected by MDR-TB.45,47 The observed heterogeneity could be explained by study region and sample size differences as indicated in the meta-regression results, which may influence both exposure and outcomes.

The Molla et al meta-analysis exhibits a higher prevalence of MDR-TB in East Africa than other regions worldwide. This study reported a pooled MDR-TB prevalence in new and previously treated cases of 4% and 21%, respectively.46 Higher prevalence of MDR-TB in previously treated cases is due to failures in system and patient adherence (insufficient treatment monitoring, inadequate, incomplete or interrupted treatment) in their initial treatment, leading to dominant strains.8 48 49 Nevertheless, higher rates of MDR-TB were noted in Latin America and the Caribbean region50 and in some west and southern African regions.45 51 Regional differences, such as variations in TB control programmes, HIV rates and treatment adherence, may explain this MDR-TB prevalence difference between these regions.

Key MDR-TB determinants reported in the included studies were previous TB treatment,25 27 28 31 32 HIV infection,26 smoking,25 comorbidities30 and demographic factors such as male sex and young age.26 27 31 A prior TB treatment was reported as one of the most important factors of MDR-TB in this review, consistent with data in the literature showing that persons with a TB treatment history are up to 10 times more likely to develop MDR-TB than new cases.8 52 In the MENA region, this association may be exacerbated by TB cases mismanagement, disruption of TB treatment and poor treatment adherence. These factors contribute to treatment failure, leading to the development of resistant strains. HIV infection has been identified as a determinant of MDR-TB in this review. This finding is in line with current knowledge that HIV increases vulnerability to TB and complicates treatment outcomes.53,55 Although HIV prevalence is relatively low in the region, the risk may be underestimated due to underdiagnosis, stigma and the concentration of cases among marginalised populations. Smoking has been found to be a risk factor of MDR-TB. This supports claims that it impairs immunity and increases the risk of treatment failure.56,58 This factor is particularly concerning in the region due to the high prevalence of smoking and its relative social and cultural acceptance among men in certain countries of the region. Comorbidities like diabetes and other chronic diseases were associated with a high prevalence of MDR-TB in this study. This finding is in accordance with international data showing that diabetes alters immunity and worsens TB treatment outcomes.59 60 This risk factor is of critical importance in the region, where diabetes prevalence is among the highest in the world. The coexistence of TB and non-communicable diseases represents a double burden for health systems, already limited in resources. Demographic factors such as male gender and young age have also been cited as determinants of MDR-TB in the region. Current knowledge suggests that men and young adults are more exposed to TB due to their occupational involvement, smoking and healthcare-seeking and treatment compliance behaviours.61 62 This trend is similar in the region, where men and young adults are predominant smokers and hold high-risk jobs, and are likely to migrate for work, which increases the risk of exposure and treatment interruption.

Numerous studies were conducted on risk factors of MDR-TB. Asgedom et al reported prior anti-TB treatment, HIV infection and male sex as predictors of MDR-TB.63 Similarly, Pradipta et al noticed previous TB disease and treatment as a risk factor64 and Mesfin et al observed an association between high risk of MDR-TB and HIV seropositivity.65 Furthermore, Molla et al and Maatoug et al highlighted additional risk factors, including poor living conditions such as being migrants, high-risk lifestyle, medical history, diabetes and HIV seropositivity.46 66 Contrary to our finding, some meta-analyses reported that individuals aged 30 or 40 years and above are at a significantly increased risk of developing MDR-TB.64 67

Strengths and limitations

This represents the first systematic review and meta-analysis focusing on MDR-TB and its associated factors that offers a comprehensive synthesis of MDR-TB data in the MENA region. It demonstrates methodological rigour with no evidence of publication bias, enhancing the relevance of its findings. By highlighting key region-specific factors associated with MDR-TB prevalence, it may inform prevention interventions and ultimately contribute to optimise TB control in the region. However, it has some limitations and results should be considered with caution. A main limitation of the meta-analysis lies in the heterogeneity among the studies. Meta-regression identified study region and sample size as important sources of variability, which could affect the accuracy of pooled prevalence estimates. A significant proportion of the selected studies were retrospective and compiled hospital cases, which could have implications for the representativeness of the estimates. Furthermore, the restriction to studies published only in English and French, combined with the under-representation of studies from certain MENA countries, constitutes both language and data availability limitations. Finally, it was difficult to fully confirm the independence of participants in studies conducted in similar regions, as individual data were not available.

Despite these limitations, this review is the first to provide an overview of the prevalence and determinants of MDR-TB in the MENA region. It thus provides an important basis on which future research and the development of prevention policies can rely.

Conclusion

Between 2000–2024, the MDR-TB prevalence in the region was estimated at 4.0%, with significant heterogeneity across studies. Key risk factors contributing to MDR-TB in the region were previous TB treatment, HIV seropositivity, smoking, the presence of comorbidities, male sex and young age. These findings offer critical and evidence-based insights to inform public health policy, strategic planning and future research. Addressing MDR-TB in the MENA region demands multisectoral and targeted interventions that integrate standardised treatment protocols, treatment adherence strategies, tobacco control policies and specific strategies for high-risk populations. Furthermore, reversing the trend of MDR-TB in the MENA region requires coordinated efforts, political commitment and evidence-based policymaking grounded in a solid understanding of the local determinants driving TB resistance.
