Assessment, feedback, and grading practices and philosophies of introductory biology instructors
Authors: Michele L. Lemons, Bryan M. Dewsbury, Lindsay B. Wheeler, Sarah R. Cavanagh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile pedagogical research often focuses on assessment, feedback, and grading (AFG) practices, we lack a clear understanding of how instructors both conceptualize and implement these elements of teaching in their courses. To explore on-the-ground interpretations of AFG in college courses, we conducted one-on-one Zoom interviews with 27 undergraduate introductory biology instructors at 2- and 4-year institutions in the United States. Participants were asked to describe the purpose of AFG and to share examples of their AFG practices. Using a dialogic, reflexive coding process, we found that instructors’ goals for AFG were multidimensional and sometimes contradictory. For instance, some viewed grading as a motivational tool, while others saw it as discouraging motivation. Additionally, the boundaries between assessment, feedback, and grading were often blurred: grading was sometimes described as feedback, and vice versa. In some cases, instructors’ AFG philosophies were not clearly translated into AFG practices. These findings underscore the need to better distinguish and articulate the roles of AFG in undergraduate biology instruction (and beyond). Clarifying these concepts can enhance communication between instructors, students, and pedagogical researchers, which could help lead to supporting more effective learning strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Efforts to quantify the degree to which students have learned academic content are as old as the institution of higher education itself. In the early days of the college classroom, professors required students to commit whole texts to memory (usually ones the professors had written). Exams involved students reciting passages aloud verbatim, with the grade provided by the professor who single-handedly determined the accuracy of the performance (1). The power of the lecturer over the students almost defined that time period. The term “lecturer” shares the same epistemological root as the “lectern,” commonly used in churches (2). There was an almost perfect holiness associated with the lecturer being judge and jury, with grades based nearly solely on what counted as intelligence in their eyes.

Contemporary understandings of pedagogy, especially more recently in the sciences, have emphasized the need for a more dialogic approach to teaching (3–5). Here, instead of a unidirectional flow of information from instructor to student over the course of a semester, students are provided multiple opportunities to engage in dialog. Dialog can occur using several mechanisms, including the administering of case studies, pedagogical approaches like think-pair-share, or other interrupted lecture approaches like clicker questions. This philosophy of education dovetails with our current understanding of how human beings make meaning of themselves and the world, in active discourse with one another (6).

Assessment in this newer, dialogic paradigm is not always something that happens at a single time point, where a single quantitative measure captures the complex comprehensive learning experience (e.g., a score on a multiple-choice final exam). Assessment is a diffuse set of activities that, depending on the individual, can refer to different practices that are sometimes called something else. For example, the terms “assessment,” “feedback,” and “grading” (AFG) have been sometimes used interchangeably and other times to describe unique activities. For each of these activities, however, some information flows back to the student and instructor about where the students are in terms of their intellectual and social growth. The frequency and nature of how this information is shared can have critical implications for the opportunities that students have to be successful in a given course. Take for instance the act of giving feedback. In the literature, feedback usually refers to the oral or written qualitative information meant to describe what the instructor is seeing in the student’s work and what in their expert opinion could be improved (7, 8). The literature on identity contingencies (the ways in which identity psychologically impacts academic performance) has identified how feedback style (tone and clarity) can impact how students cultivate growth mindsets, develop a sense of belonging, and in general see a path for growth in their discipline (9).

Current discussions of AFG have mostly focused on the particulars of each term. Pedagogical researchers have developed rich repositories of various classroom assessment techniques with respect to learning (see reference 10) and assessing the affective dimension of the classroom (e.g., see references 11 and 12). Educational psychology research has demonstrated the importance of quality feedback, and the literature on the impacts of various approaches to grading (or not grading at all) is growing (13, 14). What is currently lacking is a clear understanding of how everyday instructors conceptualize these terms and operationalize them in their classrooms. In this article, we explore this very question in the classrooms of biology instructors. We were interested in understanding their individual perceptions of each term and how each activity manifests in their classrooms, unique to their contexts. We explored the following specific research questions:

METHODS

Development of questions

We developed prescreening (Appendix S1) and interview questions (Appendix S2) in partnership with the steering committee members of our incubator National Science Foundation Research Coordination Network for Undergraduate Biology Education grant. Over a series of virtual meetings and digital correspondence, we generated and then refined interview questions to provide face and content validity for the questions. The majority of interview questions were intended to (i) gather information about AFG practices used in introductory biology classrooms and to (ii) understand instructors’ views regarding the purpose of AFG.

Participant recruitment and selection

We selected 27 interview participants from a sample of 297 introductory biology instructors who completed an online survey about assessments, grading, and feedback (S. R. Cavanagh, M. L. Lemons, H. L. Urry, B. M. Dewsbury, J. R. Eyler, L. Fergusson-Kolmes, J. M. Lang, and L. B. Wheeler, submitted for publication). Survey participants were recruited via listservs, informal biology teaching networks, social media, and direct contact with biology department chairs across the United States from 15 March to 14 July 2022.

At the end of the survey, participants were invited to volunteer for a 45- to /60-minute interview on Zoom with one of the two co-PIs (Cavanagh and Lemons). Participants willing to participate in this interview completed a prescreening survey (Appendix S1) that collected data about identity, positionality, institution type, and grading practices (Table 1). We used prescreening responses to attempt to select a somewhat diverse set of instructors—diverse in background (institution type, position, gender, and race) and diverse in pedagogical practices. To select participants with varying degrees of pedagogical innovation, we focused on their grading practices. Questions about grading approaches more easily lend themselves to a binning or sorting approach because the literature offers several categories (examples: traditional, blend of traditional and alternative, all alternative). This binning process does not translate as readily for topics of assessment and feedback, which have a greater potential for definition conflation compared to grading.

The coding process

Authors engaged in an open coding process of a selection of the interview responses to identify practices and philosophies pertaining to AFG. We first coded the same subset of three interviews individually and then met in person to engage in dialogic coding and reflexivity. Authors compared individually identified codes and themes and subsequently identified similarities and differences in their interpretation.

We coded for practices by identifying and quantifying different recognizable approaches in the interviewees’ responses with respect to assessment and feedback. For example, terms such as “Kahoot,” “Poll Everywhere,” “in-class surveys,” and “raising hands to indicate answer” were recorded, discussed, and then pooled into a category named “in-class polling.”

Coding of philosophies, however, required a more “grounded” axial approach where several themes were extracted from the interviewees’ responses. During the reflexivity process, all authors compared their findings and discussed discrepancies and divergent viewpoints until we reached a consensus on how responses should be interpreted. For example, authors independently identified themes from responses about the purpose of feedback. These responses were shared with the group and included the following: “feedback guides instructor choices moving forward,” “feedback can inform instructor,” “feedback provides pulse check,” and “feedback can provide clarity to instructor.” These responses were used to generate the code “feedback guides instruction” that was used in this study. The authors developed a coding spreadsheet to record subsequent coding work.

Afterwards, two authors (Dewsbury and Lemons) coded interviews for responses about AFG practices, and two authors (Cavanagh and Wheeler) coded the interviews for emergent themes about philosophies of AFG. All authors coded separately and met as a full group to engage in further dialogic reflexivity to ensure there was consistency in the coding process. RESULTS

What types of assessments are offered?

When asked about types of assessments offered, two categories of assessments reported the most (25/27 interviewees) were summative exams and student work in the form of assignments completed during and/or outside of class (Table 3). Assignments done during and outside of class included a diverse set of answers, including weekly problem sets, homework, case studies, discussion board posts, article summaries, and more. Many (n = 22) interviewees reported using quizzes/mini exams, and 14 interviewees described the use of in-class polling, which included immediate response systems (clickers, Kahoot, Poll Everywhere, etc.) and non-tech options such as in-class polling by raising hands. Some instructors (n = 9) specifically described work done prior to class for the purpose of preparing or priming students for class work; this category included preclass quizzes, surveys, or preclass papers. Some instructors (n = 6) described reflective assessments that promote reflection or metacognition, including exam wrappers, narrative evaluations, and reflection papers. Three instructors described peer evaluation and participation as a form of assessment.

What is the goal of assessment?

When asked to describe the purpose of assessment, instructors provided a variety of answers that ranged from assessment serving as a signal of learning, as a source of motivation, and as a foundation for future courses. After careful discussion, six codes were identified: (i) signal to instructor: learning of students, (ii) signal to instructor: progress in learning over time, (iii) signal to student: learning where they are, (iv) signal to student: what is important to learn, (v) to motivate, and (vi) to serve as a foundation for future courses (Table 4).

What is the purpose of assessment?

Assessment as signals to instructors and to students

Some interviewees described assessment as a signal to instructors that reveals the extent of student learning at a given time (Table 4). An interviewee (Adam) described the purpose of assessment as giving “the instructor a gauge of how well the students are learning the material.” Other interviewees described assessment as a signal to instructors that revealed student progress or growth over time (Table 4). A participant (Erin) said the purpose of assessment is to explain, “here’s your progress towards [a] goal.”

Some instructors described the purpose of assessment as a signal to students that informs them of what they know and what they might not yet know (Table 4). One interviewee (Mara) said assessment informs “the student to be aware of what things are making sense to them, and what things are not.”

Some participants described assessment as a signal to students that helps them prioritize content and better understand what is important to learn (Table 4). A participant (Nate) described assessment as “the currency of the realm. It shows that it’s a priority … [assessments] show them what’s valuable.”

Assessment motivates and provides foundation for future work

Participants described assessment as an opportunity to motivate students (Table 4). One instructor (Maisy) said, “if students don’t think … they’re going to be assessed for what they know … they’re not going to put the effort in.” Some interviewees described assessment as a way to prepare students for future courses or careers (Table 4). A participant (Maya) said assessment helps to “make sure that they can advance to the next course.” In summary, instructors described assessment as a multifunctional tool that serves both instructors and students.

How do instructors provide feedback?

Participants were asked to describe ways they offered feedback. The largest number (n = 15) of instructors described offering written feedback on exams (Table 3). Other participants described offering feedback as verbal feedback during class (n = 11) and in office hours (n = 6), while others described teaching assistants and learning assistants offering feedback to students (n = 8) and implementing peer feedback (n = 6). Other instructors said they offered feedback as sharing an answer key (n = 7), using class time for class discussion of exam/quiz questions (n = 4), and providing opportunities for exams/quiz corrections (n = 6). Some participants described offering feedback on drafts of student work (n = 4), providing feedback on discussion board responses (n = 5), and responses to student reflection work (n = 2). In summary, reported feedback practices were diverse and included written and oral formats, individual feedback and feedback to an entire class, and using assistants as a method to offer feedback (Table 3). Some forms of feedback used time in class (reviewing commonly missed quiz questions), while many forms of feedback were provided outside of class (discussion board responses, comments on student work, providing answer keys, etc.)

What is the purpose of feedback?

When asked about the goal of feedback, six codes emerged: (i) guides instruction, (ii) focuses/guides student learning, (iii) a tool for individualized student instruction, (iv) offers care, (v) builds student confidence, and (vi) strategic (e.g. timely, transparent, and thoughtful).

Feedback guides instruction

Some instructors described feedback as a tool to guide their instruction, that is, to better know whether they should move on or linger on a concept (Table 5). An instructor (Renee) said, “The purpose of assessment is twofold: it’s to see if the students are getting it, but it’s also to see if our mode of instruction is adequate to help them get it.” Participants were asked if they considered student performance on assessments as feedback on how well (or not) they taught the material. The answers to these questions ranged greatly. For example, one instructor said, “If 90% of you [students] missed it, that’s all on you. It’s not on me,” suggesting this instructor views student performance independently of the efficacy of his/her instruction. In contrast, other instructors used student performance to guide their instructional choices. For example, one participant (Lai) said it is important “to talk about those questions which are frequently missed by the classroom …. Clearly that’s a problem area … let’s go over this concept one more time.”

What is the purpose of feedback?

Feedback guides student learning and provides individualized instruction

Participants described feedback as an opportunity to inform students of mistakes and to teach them how to learn from mistakes (Table 5). One interviewee (Louise) asked, “How can you learn to correct a mistake if no one shows you how?” Another interviewee (Nate) commented, “That’s an essential part of learning, you know, to figure out whether they understood, to help them get their understanding in the right place.”

Participants also described feedback as an opportunity to provide personalized instruction (Table 5). An interviewee (Hana) said feedback can “target because everybody comes in with their different strengths. So, feedback is a good way of allowing them to kind of pinpoint the areas they really still have to work on and show them also the places where they are already successful.”

Feedback offers care and builds student confidence

Consistent with feedback as care (Table 5), a participant (Maisy) said that feedback allows students to “see that I really did put the time into reading their answers, I do care.” Other instructors described feedback as building student confidence. For example, an instructor (Harper) said, “So I think the feedback, it just helps reassure them.” Another interviewee (Hana) said, “I definitely have some students who are very hard on themselves ... and so, I think that sometimes that feedback is important early in the semester to give some students a little bit more confidence than they have coming in.”

Feedback as strategic (timely, sufficiently frequent, actionable, and providing transparency)

Instructors described features of effective feedback, including timeliness, transparency, thoughtfulness, and having an actionable tone (Table 5). One instructor (Nate) explained, “the nice thing about both the online stuff and the clickers, they get immediate feedback. And when I was in college, you know, you turn in the problems set, and then you didn’t find out for weeks whether you got it right or not.” The same instructor offered a striking analogy that illustrates the importance of timeliness of feedback. The person said, “If I was learning to sing, and if I had to like record myself, then mail it off somewhere, and then wait two weeks to find it if it was on a on key or not, you would never learn to sing at that rate, which is sort of the way I learned biology.”

Other instructors described clarity and transparency as key elements of effective feedback. One participant (Erin) said, “If I want to be a fair judge, then I should be clear about my criteria well in advance of anything summative … feedback is a way of communicating those criteria in a way that’s more concrete.” Other instructors shared the importance of providing feedback that is actionable and by providing opportunities for revision. Others shared the importance of limiting feedback so as not to overwhelm students. One (Sienna) said, “I don’t think that you need to be super in-depth with your feedback everywhere, but you have to be strategic.” Interestingly, some instructors expressed an interest in improving feedback; one (Helena) said, “I will just say the first thing I wrote down when I was reading through these [questions] is … I could do a better job.”

What is the purpose of grading?

Participants were asked to describe the goal of grading. Answers to this question were wide-ranging and suggested some participants supported the practice of traditional grading; some considered the positive and negative impacts of traditional grading, while still others were opposed to alternative grading practices. As one example of the latter, a participant (Louise) described ungrading (a practice that purposefully eliminates or decreases the use of points or grades on student work) as “hooey.” Seven codes emerged from participants’ answers: grades as (i) representing learning, (ii) representing effort, (iii) a source of motivation, (iv) demotivating or demoralizing, (v) arbitrary or meaningless, (vi) guiding future and serving as weed-out or a sorting process, and (vii) a signal to others of learning (Table 6).

What is the purpose of grading?

Grading as a signal of student learning and student effort

Some interviewees described grading as a tool to measure the degree of student learning (Table 6). An instructor (Erika) said, “I think that the purpose of grades is to quantify the level of understanding of the student that the student has of the material,” and another (Maisy) described the purpose of grading as showing “you’re actually taking something in, you’re actually getting it…You can’t say you took biology if you didn’t learn anything about biology.”

Other instructors described grading as a tool to evaluate student effort (Table 6). One interviewee (Louise) said grading allowed students “to assess how they’re approaching the course material, if they’re putting in the right amount of effort.” The same instructor described grading as a measure of effort with this analogy, “I’m going to give you as many tools, but ultimately you’re the one who runs this race and you’re the one who decides how it’s run.”

Grading as (de)motivating

Some participants described grading as a way to encourage students to learn (Table 6). One instructor (Maya) said, “I think a lot of student motivation is external. So, it is performing as well or better than peers.” Another instructor said, “students love grades, and I think that comes down to a human nature of wanting that gold sticker. Right? You want objective criteria that you’re doing well.”

Other instructors shared a blended response. One (Sienna) said, “I think you know they can be used as a motivational tool for some students, but I think they only really motivate in the wrong way.” Other instructors clearly described grades as demoralizing and demotivating for students. One participant (Adam) said, “If a student is thinking of the grade as the most important part of the class, it can be really demotivational and hurtful if they don’t do as well as they expected and I’ve seen students kind of spiral.” Another participant described grades as demotivating due to society viewing grades as a signal of personal worth. An instructor (Robin) elaborated on this point and said, “They’ve been taught for 18 years that this [grade] is how I can value my achievement, can value myself, can pick a career, can decide if I am worthy …. if I don’t get straight A’s, I have no value.” Interestingly, another participant noted society’s perception of grades as a problem but did not view grading itself as problematic. This instructor (Louise) explained, “Grading is not the problem, the problem is our culture that assigns personal value to grades.”

Grading as meaningless or unhelpful

Some instructors viewed grading as arbitrary or meaningless (Table 6). One interviewee (Lai) said, “I have to say they [grades] don’t mean anything. Because a very bright student may not turn out to be a good doctor, but a B or C student may.” Similarly, other instructors shared their frustrations with grading, one Aisha said, “There’s so many factors that affect the grade that aren’t related to academics, especially at a community college,” and “You know if I had it my way, I would just totally get rid of grades and give every student individual feedback on everything.”

Grades as important for students’ futures

Grades were described as being connected to scholarships, graduate school entry, and other tangibles outside the classroom (Table 6). An instructor (Louise) described grades as being important to “long term success in terms of scholarships” and “letters of reference.” Another instructor (Rene) described the admission process of graduate and medical schools as a “weed-out process” that is heavily reliant on grades and test scores. The same instructor described this reliance on grades and test performance as being “baked into the cake, and the med schools aren’t changing anytime soon,” suggesting the enduring nature of the problem.

Grades as a signal to inform others or as a method to rank students

Lastly, instructors described grading as a signal that informs society about student ability (Table 6). An instructor (Adam) said, “I think the point of grades is supposed to be some measurement that we all agree on that says a student is, did this well in a particular course,” and another (Robin) said grading was “I think, for better for worse, it’s ranking … it’s saying, okay, this person is ready for medical school.”

In summary, during one-on-one interviews, instructors described their philosophies and practices of AFG in their classes. Coding these interviews led to the identification of distinct themes for assessment (Table 4), feedback (Table 5), and grading (Table 6), which revealed diversity in interpretation of AFG philosophies, practices, and meaning.

DISCUSSION

Our exploration of how biology instructors conceptualize assessment, feedback, and grading practices revealed diverse interpretations of each term, and even more diverse implementation approaches with respect to each. While there is some epistemological overlap in how the terms tend to be classically defined, the degree of perception overlap differed among those interviewed, and those differences were associated with instructors using unique pedagogical approaches.

The perceptive overlap was evident in instructor responses to our prompt questions. Thematic analysis of the instructors’ articulations of their AFG philosophy and practices reveals that, by and large, they view grading itself as a form of feedback. This is evidenced by the emergence of similar themes in responses to questions specifically asking about assessment and to those specifically asking about feedback. For example, instructors’ articulation of the importance of assessment as a “signaling” mechanism and feedback as a “guide” were descriptively very similar when the instructors were prodded to elaborate. However, there seems to be a recognition that grades can serve the additional purpose of signaling to the student where they stand on the hierarchy of students headed in a similar academic context. Comparable themes were also seen in how respondents described the role of assessment and the role of feedback. Both had the potential to motivate and to guide the learner, but assessment was viewed as more of a “signal,” in that there was some immediacy to its purpose for both student and instructor. As Hargreaves (15) suggests, the very act of assessment indicates to the student that the assessed element of the learning experience is one worth taking seriously. The signal thus serves multiple purposes. It simultaneously communicates to both instructor and student where the student is on their learning journey and what potentially additional things the instructor needs to do to move them forward.

Another key emergence from our interviews is the gap between AFG’s epistemological roots and what actually plays out in the classroom. Assessment, e.g., is defined as “the act of attributing value to an item” (16). Value can be represented by several things in the context of a course. In some cases, a numerical value is assigned with the assumption that its comparison to a “perfect” total is indicative of the quality of the assessed item. In most courses, this percentage is then converted into a letter grade. Where possible, that assessment may have a qualitative component, thus allowing for more attention to nuance, a deeper sense of “why” the item was adjudicated to be of a certain level of quality. Therefore, grading in its most traditional sense is a representation of the results of the assessment and nothing more. The attribution of quality to an item as an activity does not itself presume that the result of the process is communicated to the individual who produced the item. That communication process is what, by definition, feedback is.

When instructors were asked about their assessment and grading philosophies in particular, many referenced not only assigning a value but also communicating the why behind the value, with suggestions of how that value could be improved or maintained moving forward. The practices that instructors reported that actually exist in their courses indicate something contrary to the aspirations they espoused in their philosophy. The reality of their course structure was that it only allowed for assessment mechanisms that simply communicated attribution value without much qualification for why that attribution was made. The infrastructural context of most biology courses in higher education is such that bandwidth is only available for assessment mechanisms that yield static values and little time to communicate to students anything beyond the values themselves (17). This pernicious cycle means that grades become feedback, and feedback becomes grades, and the low quality or non-existence of the dialog that gives the value (grades) meaning robs both instructor and student of the opportunity to fine-tune the signal the assessments can be sending.

Feedback is the linchpin between instructor and student, in that the nature of the feedback process attributes value to the overall learning journey. Unfortunately, as reported by our participants and evident from the literature on barriers to instruction (18), the professional capital of college instructors and their teaching contexts do not allow for instructors to engage in that process as frequently as they may desire.

The key takeaway from instructors’ reporting of their practice, therefore, is that the educational research community needs to pay more careful attention to the delineation of assessment, feedback, and grading as connected but separate processes. The attribution of quality to the artifacts that represent students’ level of understanding of a learning outcome is in itself a highly technical, even psychometric, process where reliability and validity need to be attended to. Representing the outcome of the assessment process by a quantitative or categorical measure is one (and perhaps the most common) option, but by definition, qualitative descriptions are more likely to capture the why behind the representation. Where possible, these qualitative descriptions can be their own more comprehensive representations of value. How those representations or attributions are communicated to students is a separate process, the quality of which is highly dependent on situational contexts. The instructor, however, needs to think beyond the attribution step and articulate in the course design how, where, and when the dialog around the value attribution will take place. This is where institutional support is crucial. If the time is not present, however, to engage in rich discourse, the default is the delivery of the single variable value, i.e., numerical grade, a bare-bones proxy of what useful feedback could be. The “creation” of this time to provide meaningful and effective feedback comes from structural changes like reduced class sizes, better compensation for outside-of-the-classroom interactions, less reliance on traditional grading systems, and professional development support for developing quality feedback approaches (17). The ungrading movement, e.g., may suppose that these traditional grading systems should be sunsetted entirely (19)!

Our study suggests that assessment, feedback, and grading, as key components of the classroom, iterative, novice-to-mastery journey require closer examination, both in terms of how the biology community conceptualizes them and how they show up in everyday instruction. This focus will help us better understand not only the ways in which they can be deployed to better support student learning but also the ways in which institutional structures can be designed and marshaled to sustain their effectiveness in the long-term.
