Anastasia V. Vikulova

Abstract
. We construct an example of a field and a smooth del Pezzo surface of degree 2 2 over this field without points such that its automorphism group is isomorphic to PSL 2 ​ ( 𝔽 7 ) × ℤ / 2 ​ ℤ , \mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, which is the largest possible automorphism group of del Pezzo surfaces of degree 2 2 over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero.



1.
Introduction

It is natural to expect that for a given field
\mathbf{F}
a variety
X
over
\mathbf{F},
which does not have
\mathbf{F}
-points, has much less automorphisms than
X_{\overline{\mathbf{F}}}
has. Let us show some examples which demonstrate such a behavior.

Recall that a surface over a field
\mathbf{F}
is called a
Severi–Brauer surface
if it is isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}^{2}
over the algebraic closure of
\mathbf{F}.
A Severi–Brauer surface without points is called non-trivial Severi–Brauer surface.

Theorem 1.1

(cf.
[
7
, Theorem 1.3]
)
.

Let
G
be a finite group. If there is a field
\mathbf{F}
of characteristic zero and a non-trivial Severi–Brauer surface
S
over
\mathbf{F}
such that the group
\mathrm{Aut}(S)
contains a subgroup isomorphic to
G,
then there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
divisible only by primes congruent to
1
modulo
3
such that
G
is isomorphic to a subgroup of the group
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\times(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}).

Therefore, Theorem
1.1
shows that the set of finite subgroups in the automorphism group of a non-trivial Severi–Brauer surface over a field
\mathbf{F}
of characteristic zero is
strictly
contained in the set of finite subgroups of the automorphism group of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
over the algebraic closure of
\mathbf{F}
.

Over the field of rational numbers
\mathbb{Q}
we can say more about finite subgroups of the automorphism group of a non-trivial Severi–Brauer surface.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
9
, Theorem 1.3]
)
.

Let
V
be a non-trivial Severi–Brauer surface over the field
\mathbb{Q}
and let
G
be a finite group. Then
G
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(V)
if and only if
G\subset\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}.

A result of the same kind is known for smooth cubic surfaces without points.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
6
, Theorem 1.4(i)]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{F}
be a field of characteristic zero and let
S
be a smooth cubic surface over
\mathbf{F}.
Suppose that
S
has no points over
\mathbf{F}.
Then the group
\mathrm{Aut}(S)
is abelian.

However, if we consider smooth del Pezzo surfaces of degree
2,
the situation will change dramatically. Recall that the largest automorphism group of del Pezzo surfaces of degree
2
over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero isomorphic to
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
(see, for instance,
[
3
, § 8.7.3]
). The goal of this paper is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
S
be a surface defined by the equation

w^{2}+(x^{4}+y^{4}+z^{4})-\frac{3}{2}(1-\sqrt{-7})(x^{2}y^{2}+x^{2}z^{2}+y^{2}z^{2})=0

over
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})
in the weighted projective space
\mathbb{P}(2,1,1,1)
with homogeneous coordinates
w,x,y,z
such that
\deg(w)=2
and
\deg(x)=\deg(y)=\deg(z)=1.
Then the following holds:

(i)

S
is a smooth del Pezzo surface of degree
2;

(ii)

one has
\mathrm{Aut}(S)\simeq\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z};

(iii)

\mathrm{Pic}(S)\simeq\mathbb{Z};

(iv)

S
does not have rational points.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we give some auxiliary lemmas which we need in the proof of the main theorem. Here and below by
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and by
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
we denote
2
-adic integers and rational numbers, respectively.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
5
, Chapter II, Theorem 4]
)
.

Let
x=2^{n}u
be an element of
\mathbb{Q}_{2}^{*},
where
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
u\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{*}.
For
x
to be a square it is necessary and sufficient that
n
is even and
u\equiv 1\mod 8.

Lemma 2.2
.

For the field
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
we have
\sqrt{-7}\in\mathbb{Q}_{2}
and the following congruence relation holds:

\pm\sqrt{-7}\equiv\pm 181\mod 128.

Proof.

Indeed, by Lemma
2.1
the number
\sqrt{-7}
does belong to
\mathbb{Q}_{2},
because

-7\equiv 1\mod 8.

Take
t\equiv\pm 181\mod 128,
we get

t^{2}+7=181^{2}+7=2^{15}\equiv 0\mod 128.

∎

Lemma 2.3
.

There is a valuation
|\cdot|_{v}
on
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})
such that the completion
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})_{v}
of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})
with respect to this valuation is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Q}_{2}.
Moreover, one can choose an isomorphism
\theta:\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})_{v}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{Q}_{2}
so that

\theta(\sqrt{-7})\equiv 181\mod 128

and

\theta\left(\frac{3}{2}(1-\sqrt{-7})\right)\equiv-14\mod 64.

Proof.

Let
|\cdot|_{2}
be a valuation of the field
\mathbb{Q}
such that for
a\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

|a|_{2}=2^{-\mathrm{ord}_{2}(a)},

where
\mathrm{ord}_{2}(a)
is the highest power of
2
dividing
a.
Let us consider the completion of the field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})
with respect to a valuation extending the valuation
|\cdot|_{2}
from
\mathbb{Q}.
By
[
2
, Chapter II, §10]
there are at most
[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7}):\mathbb{Q}]=2
extensions of
|\cdot|_{2}
to
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7}),
say
|\cdot|_{v_{i}}.
If
L_{i}
is a completion of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})
with respect to
|\cdot|_{v_{i}},
then we have

\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{Q}_{2}\simeq\prod_{i}L_{i}.

Since
-7
is a square in
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
by Lemma
2.2
, we obtain

(2.1)

\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{Q}_{2}\simeq\mathbb{Q}_{2}[T]/(T^{2}+7)\simeq\mathbb{Q}_{2}\times\mathbb{Q}_{2}.

Therefore, we get
L_{i}\simeq\mathbb{Q}_{2}.
By Lemma
2.2
one of the roots of the equation

t^{2}+7\equiv 0\mod 128

is

t_{1}=181=1+2^{2}+2^{4}+2^{5}+2^{7}.

Therefore, we have the desired isomorphism and the following congruence relation

(2.2)

\frac{3}{2}(1-t_{1})\equiv\frac{3}{2}(1-1-2^{2}-2^{4}-2^{5}-2^{7})\equiv-2-2^{2}-2^{3}=-14\mod 64.

∎

Remark 2.4
.

From (
2.1
) we get that there are two extensions of the valuation
|\cdot|_{2}
on
\mathbb{Q}
to
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7}).
The completions of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})
with respect to these extending valuations are isomorphic to
\mathbb{Q}_{2}.
If
|\cdot|_{v_{1}}
and
|\cdot|_{v_{2}}
are these two extensions and
\theta_{1}:\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})_{v_{1}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{Q}_{2}
and
\theta_{2}:\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})_{v_{2}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{Q}_{2}
are two isomorphisms, then

\theta_{1}(\sqrt{-7})=\sqrt{-7}\equiv 181\mod 128\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{2}(\sqrt{-7})=-\sqrt{-7}\equiv-181\mod 128,

as the Galois group
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})/\mathbb{Q})
acts in this way.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let

(2.3)

f(w,x,y,z)=w^{2}+(x^{4}+y^{4}+z^{4})+14(x^{2}y^{2}+x^{2}z^{2}+y^{2}z^{2}).

Assume that
w,x,y,z
are integer numbers and at least one of them is odd. Then

(2.4)

f(w,x,y,z)\not\equiv 0\mod 64.

Proof.

The proof is by reductio ad absurdum. Hence, note that
w^{2}+x^{4}+y^{4}+z^{4}
should be even. So we have
3
possibilities:

(1)

w,x,y,z
are odd;

(2)

w
is odd, one of
x
,
y
and
z
is odd and the other two are even;

(3)

w
is even, one of
x
,
y
and
z
is even and the other two are odd.

In the first and in the second cases we get that
f(w,x,y,z)\equiv\pm 2\mod 8,
so that
f(w,x,y,z)\not\equiv 0\mod 64.
In the third case without loss of generality we assume that
x
is even and
y
and
z
are odd. So we write

(2.5)

w=2\tilde{w};\quad x=2\tilde{x};\quad y=1+2\tilde{y};\quad z=1+2\tilde{z},

where
\tilde{w},\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{z}\in\mathbb{Z}.
Let us rewrite the right hand side of (
2.3
) as

w^{2}+x^{2}(x^{2}+14y^{2})+y^{2}(y^{2}+14z^{2})+z^{2}(z^{2}+14x^{2}).

Let us reduce it modulo
64.
Using (
2.5
) we obtain

\displaystyle x^{2}(x^{2}+14y^{2})=4\tilde{x}^{2}(4\tilde{x}^{2}+14+14\cdot 4\tilde{y}(\tilde{y}+1))\equiv 16\tilde{x}^{4}+2^{3}\cdot 7\tilde{x}^{2}\mod 64;

\displaystyle y^{2}(y^{2}+14z^{2})=(1+4\tilde{y}(\tilde{y}+1))(1+4\tilde{y}(\tilde{y}+1)+14+14\cdot 4\tilde{z}(\tilde{z}+1))\equiv

\displaystyle\equiv 15+2^{3}\cdot 7\tilde{z}(\tilde{z}+1)\mod 64;

\displaystyle z^{2}(z^{2}+14x^{2})=(1+4\tilde{z}(\tilde{z}+1))(1+4\tilde{z}(\tilde{z}+1)+2^{3}\cdot 7\tilde{x}^{2})\equiv

\displaystyle\equiv 1+2^{3}\cdot 7\tilde{x}^{2}+2^{3}\tilde{z}(\tilde{z}+1)\mod 64.

From these three congruences we get that

(2.6)

\begin{gathered}w^{2}+x^{2}(x^{2}+14y^{2})+y^{2}(y^{2}+14z^{2})+z^{2}(z^{2}+14x^{2})\equiv\\
\equiv w^{2}+16+2^{4}\cdot 7\tilde{x}^{2}+16\tilde{x}^{4}\mod 64.\end{gathered}

So if
w\not\equiv 0\mod 4,
then the value (
2.6
) is not congruent to
0
modulo
64.
Thus, assume that
w\equiv 0\mod 4,
i.e.
w=4\tilde{w},
where
\tilde{w}\in\mathbb{Z}.
It is enough to reduce

(2.7)

\tilde{w}^{2}+1+3\tilde{x}^{2}+\tilde{x}^{4}

modulo
4.
We have

3\tilde{x}^{2}+\tilde{x}^{4}\equiv 0\mod 4

and

\tilde{w}^{2}+1\equiv 1\quad\text{or}\quad 2\mod 4.

Therefore, the value of (
2.7
) is not congruent to
0
modulo
4.
As a result, the right hand side of (
2.3
) is not congruent to
0
modulo
64,
as was to be shown.

∎

Corollary 2.6
.

The equation

(2.8)

w^{2}+(x^{4}+y^{4}+z^{4})+14(x^{2}y^{2}+x^{2}z^{2}+y^{2}z^{2})=0

does not have non-zero solutions in
\mathbb{Q}_{2}.

Proof.

Assume the contrary. Then without loss of generality we can look for a solution of (
2.8
) over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
with the condition that one of
w,x,y,z
is odd. Indeed, if
(2w,2x,2y,2z)
is a solution of (
2.8
), we get

\displaystyle 4w^{2}+16(x^{4}+y^{4}+z^{4})+16\cdot 14(x^{2}y^{2}+x^{2}z^{2}+y^{2}z^{2})=

\displaystyle=4(w^{2}+4(x^{4}+y^{4}+z^{4})+4\cdot 14(x^{2}y^{2}+x^{2}z^{2}+y^{2}z^{2}))=0.

Therefore,
w^{2}
is divisible by
4.
Thus, we have
w=2\tilde{w}
for
\tilde{w}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and

(2w,2x,2y,2z)=(4\tilde{w},2x,2y,2z).

It is a solution if and only if
(\tilde{w},x,y,z)
is a solution.

So let
(w,x,y,z)
be such solution of (
2.8
). Then

(w,x,y,z)\mod 64

is a solution of (
2.8
) modulo
64.
However, by Lemma
2.5
this equation does not have a non-zero solution modulo
64
such that at least one of the coordinates
w,x,y,z
is odd. This contradiction proves the corollary.

∎

Lemma 2.7
.

The group
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})
lies in
W(\mathrm{E}_{7}),
it is a simple group and its centralizer in
W(\mathrm{E}_{7})
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

First of all, note that

(2.9)

W(\mathrm{E}_{7})\simeq\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathrm{Sp}_{6}(\mathbb{F}_{2})

by
[
10
, §3.12.4]
. We have the natural inclusion
\mathrm{PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{2})\subset\mathrm{Sp}_{6}(\mathbb{F}_{2}),
which maps the matrix
M\in\mathrm{PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
to the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}M&0\\
0&(M^{-1})^{\mathrm{T}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{Sp}_{6}(\mathbb{F}_{2}).

As there is an isomorphism
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})\simeq\mathrm{PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
by
[
10
, §3.12.1]
, we obtain the inclusion

\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})\subset W(\mathrm{E}_{7})

by (
2.9
). The group
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})
is a simple group by
[
10
, §3.3.1]
.

Let us consider a subgroup in
W(\mathrm{E}_{7})
isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}
such that it also lies in the subgroup
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})
of the Weyl group. By
[
8
, Table 1]
elements of order
7
correspond to the conjugacy class
A_{6}
in
W(\mathrm{E}_{7})
and by
[
1
, Table 10]
the cardinality of the set of this conjugacy class is equal to
2^{9}\cdot 3^{4}\cdot 5.
As

|W(\mathrm{E}_{7})|=2^{10}\cdot 3^{4}\cdot 5\cdot 7,

we get that the stabilizer (with respect to conjugation) of an element of order
7
in
W(\mathrm{E}_{7})
is of order
14.
This means that the centralizer of
\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}
in
W(\mathrm{E}_{7})
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/14\mathbb{Z}.
Since
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})
does not have a subgroup of order
14,
this also means that the centralizer of
\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})
lies in

\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})\subset\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathrm{Sp}_{6}(\mathbb{F}_{2}).

But
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})
is a simple group. Therefore, its centralizer in the Weyl group
W(\mathrm{E}_{7})
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}.

∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

In this section we prove Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let us prove
(i)
. The smoothness of the surface
S
can be proved by direct computation of partial derivatives. By
[
3
, Proposition 6.3.6]
the surface
S
is a del Pezzo surface of degree
2.

Let us prove
(ii)
. It is well-known that the anticanonical linear system of
S
gives a double cover
\phi_{|-K_{S}|}:S\to\mathbb{P}^{2}
which is ramified over a smooth quartic curve
C
. In our case the equation of the quartic
C
is

(3.1)

(x^{4}+y^{4}+z^{4})-\frac{3}{2}(1-\sqrt{-7})(x^{2}y^{2}+x^{2}z^{2}+y^{2}z^{2})=0

in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
with homogeneous coordinates
x,y,z.
The Geiser involution
\gamma,
which is a deck transformation of the cover
\phi_{|-K_{S}|}
and acts on
S
as

\gamma:(w,x,y,z)\mapsto(-w,x,y,z),

gives us a non-trivial element in
\mathrm{Aut}(S).

From
[
4
, Chapter 1]
we get that
\mathrm{Aut}(C)\subset\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7}))
is generated by the signed permutation matrices

-\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\\
\end{pmatrix},\;\;\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
1&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix},\;\;\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&-1\\
\end{pmatrix}

and the matrix

\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&\frac{-1-\sqrt{7}}{2}\\
\frac{-1+\sqrt{7}}{2}&\frac{-1+\sqrt{7}}{2}&0\\
-1&1&\frac{1+\sqrt{7}}{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}

which act on the equation of
C
by the identity. This gives us the natural embedding

\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})\subset\mathrm{Aut}(S).

Since the Geiser involution
\gamma\in\mathrm{Aut}(S)
and any element in
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})\subset\mathrm{Aut}(S)
commute with each other, we get the embedding

(3.2)

\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathrm{Aut}(S).

By
[
3
, § 8.7.3]
we get that
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
is the largest automorphism group of a del Pezzo surface of degree
2
over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, so (
3.2
) is an isomorphism, as desired.

Let us prove
(iii)
. To prove that
\mathrm{Pic}(S)\simeq\mathbb{Z}
one should prove that

(3.3)

\mathrm{Pic}(\bar{S})^{\mathrm{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7}))}\simeq\mathbb{Z},

where
\bar{S}=S\times_{\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7}))}\mathrm{Spec}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}),
because we have the obvious inclusion

\mathrm{Pic}(S)\subseteq\mathrm{Pic}(\bar{S})^{\mathrm{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7}))}.

To prove (
3.3
) note that the image of the Galois group
\mathrm{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7}))
in the Weyl group
W(\mathrm{E}_{7})
and the automorphism group
\mathrm{Aut}(S),
which lies in
W(\mathrm{E}_{7})
by
[
3
, Corollary 8.2.40]
, commute with each other. So we need to consider the centralizer of
\mathrm{Aut}(S)\simeq\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{7})\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
in
W(\mathrm{E}_{7}).
By Lemma
2.7
it is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and it is obvious that it is generated by the Geiser involution. This means that the image of the Galois group
\mathrm{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7}))
in the Weyl group
W(\mathrm{E}_{7})
is either trivial, or isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\simeq\langle\gamma\rangle.

To prove that it is not trivial it is enough to show that that there is an exceptional curve on
S
which is not defined over
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7}).
According to the basic theory of smooth del Pezzo surfaces of degree
2
(see, for instance,
[
3
, §8.7.1]
) the surface
S
has
56
lines which are the inverse image of
28
bitangents of the branch curve
C.
It is not hard to see that the line in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
defined by the equation

x+y+z=0

is a bitangent of
C.
So the inverse image of this bitangent are two lines which are defined by the equations

w\pm i\left(\frac{3+\sqrt{-7}}{2}\right)(x^{2}+xy+y^{2})=0.

They are not defined over
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7}),
hence, the image of
\mathrm{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7}))
in the Weyl group
W(\mathrm{E}_{7})
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\simeq\langle\gamma\rangle.
As it is generated by the Geiser involution which interchanges the two lines in the inverse image of
any bitangent of
C
, we get

\mathrm{Pic}(\bar{S})^{\mathrm{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7}))}\simeq\mathbb{Z}.

Let us prove
(iv)
. To prove that
S
does not have a rational point over the field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})
it is enough to prove that it does not have a rational point over some completion of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7}).
Let us consider the valuation
|\cdot|_{2}
on
\mathbb{Q}.
Let
|\cdot|_{v}
be one of the extensions of
|\cdot|_{2}
on
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})
(which was defined in Lemma
2.3
). Then by Lemma
2.3
for the completion
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})_{v}
of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})
with respect to
|\cdot|_{v}
we have an isomorphism

\theta:\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})_{v}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{Q}_{2}.

This isomorphism satisfies

\theta\left(\frac{3}{2}(1-\sqrt{-7})\right)\equiv-14\mod 64.

So to prove that
S
does not have a rational point over the field
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
it is enough to prove that

w^{2}+(x^{4}+y^{4}+z^{4})+14(x^{2}y^{2}+x^{2}z^{2}+y^{2}z^{2})=0

does not have a solution over
\mathbb{Q}_{2}.
However, by Corollary
2.6
it does not have a non-zero solution. Quod erat demonstrandum.

∎
