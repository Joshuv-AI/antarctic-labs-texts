The Sphericity Deviation Score: Is It a Useful and Reproducible Parameter to Assess the Femoral Head Sphericity in Perthes Disease?
Authors: Chiara Blatti, Armend Fejzulai, Anders Wensaas, Sahar Toumie, Vito Pavone, Terje Terjesen, Jennifer Laine, Stefan Huhnstock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe sphericity deviation score (SDS) is a continuous parameter to describe femoral head shape in patients with Perthes disease. The aims of this study were to evaluate the reproducibility of SDS and its association with the 3-group Stulberg classification.MethodsAnteroposterior and frog-leg lateral radiographs of 41 healed Perthes hips were assessed. Six observers with varying levels of experience, independently performed SDS measurements on two occasions, 2 weeks apart. Associations between SDS and the modified 3-group Stulberg classification were tested using ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests and Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (rho). Intra- and interobserver agreement was assessed with Bland–Altman 95% limits of agreement (LoA), and reliability was analyzed by intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).ResultsThe median SDS values in hips with a round femoral head were significantly higher when measured by inexperienced observers than those measured by experienced observers (30 vs 17; P < 0.001). The SDS values did not significantly correlate with the Stulberg classification among inexperienced observers. Among experienced observers, a strong correlation was found (Spearman's rho 0.670; P < 0.001); however, only 2 of 3 observers were able to distinguish between round and oval hips. Interobserver agreement for SDS was unacceptable (LoA >40) for inexperienced observers and marginal (LoA 20-40) for experienced observers. Interobserver reliability was poor for inexperienced observers (ICC 0.15; P = .02) and moderate for experienced observers (ICC 0.69; P < .001).ConclusionsSDS is a complex parameter that did not consistently differentiate between femoral head shapes when used by inexperienced observers. Even among experienced pediatric orthopaedic researchers, SDS showed marginal interobserver agreement. The method should be used only by experienced observers in scientific studies.Key Concepts(1)The Sphericity Deviation Score (SDS) correlates significantly with the Stulberg classification only when measured by experienced pediatric orthopaedic researchers.(2)SDS is a complex and time-consuming parameter that often failed to distinguish clearly between adjacent Stulberg categories (e.g., round vs. ovoid hips).(3)Among inexperienced observers, the limits of agreement (LoA) of SDS measurements were unacceptable and the reliability (ICC) was poor.(4)SDS should be used only by experienced observers in scientific studies. Even among experienced researchers, interobserver LoA was marginal, indicating that further methodological refinements are desirable.Level of EvidenceIII, retrospective cohort studyKeywords: Pediatrics, Hips, LCPD (Legg-Calvé-Perthes Disease), Perthes, Sphericity deviations score

Introduction

The prognosis of Perthes disease correlates with residual deformity of the femoral head (FH) and acetabulum at healing. In 1981, Stulberg et al. [1] introduced a classification system categorizing FH deformity at skeletal maturity into 5 groups based on sphericity and acetabular congruency: round head (Class I), round with coxa magna/brevis (Class II), ovoid but congruent (Class III), flat head with flat congruent acetabulum (Class IV), and flat head with an incongruent joint (Class V). Several long-term studies have shown strong associations between Stulberg class and osteoarthritis (OA) risk [1,2] and the need for total hip replacement [3].

Although widely used, the Stulberg classification has limitations. Neyt et al. [4] reported inconsistent group assignment. Wiig et al. [5] introduced a modified 3-group version, merging classes I–II (round hips) and IV–V (flat hips), which improved reproducibility. However, distinction between borderline groups (round vs. ovoid, ovoid vs. flat) remained difficult [6,7], likely due to its categorical nature.

In 2013, Shah et al. [7] proposed the sphericity deviation score (SDS), a continuous measure of FH roundness and ellipsoidal deviation using anteroposterior (AP) and frog-leg lateral (FLL) radiographs. The authors presented distinctly different SDS values for Perthes hips classified as round (Stulberg class I: mean SDS 2.1 and Stulberg class II: mean SDS 4.1), ovoid (mean SDS 22.0) and flat hips (Stulberg class IV: mean SDS 43.3 and Stulberg class V: mean SDS 69.1). SDS showed a strong association with the Stulberg classification, and the method has also been found to be reliable in multiple-observer studies [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Prior studies have assessed reliability using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) [[7], [8], [9], [10]], but none evaluated measurement agreement with 95% limits of agreement (LoA) using the Bland–Altman method [11]. Agreement and reliability reflect different aspects of reproducibility: ICC captures consistency in ranking between observers, while the Bland–Altman method evaluates how closely repeated measurements agree in absolute terms. Despite acceptable ICCs in prior studies, wide LoA undermines reproducibility in clinical or research settings. To our knowledge, no previous study has assessed both agreement and reliability of SDS, nor investigated its reproducibility across multiple centers and among observers with varying levels of experience. Thus, further research is needed to address both measurement agreement and generalizability across institutions and observer profiles.

Methods

We identified 250 patients diagnosed with PD between 1948 and 1995 with complete AP and FLL radiographs at 5-year follow-up through a systematic search of the radiographic archive at Oslo University Hospital. Using a random-number generator [12], 51 patients were selected. 10 patients were excluded: 3 due to shielding of key landmarks and 7 due to incomplete reossification of the femoral head. In bilateral cases (N = 6), 1 side was randomly selected. Thus, a total of 41 Perthes hips and 35 contralateral healthy hips were included. Skeletal maturity was assessed with the modified Oxford system [13,14] by evaluating the triradiate cartilage and the greater and lesser trochanters, and was considered reached when fusion was present in at least two of these three physes.

Observers

Six observers from four institutions performed SDS measurements independently. They were grouped by experience:

Inexperienced observers:

Observer 1 (ST): 2nd-year resident, without pediatric orthopaedic experience.

Observer 2 (AF): board-certified orthopaedic surgeon, pediatric orthopaedic fellow.

Observer 3 (CB): 3rd-year resident, engaged in Perthes research.

Experienced researchers:

Observer 4 (AW): pediatric orthopaedic consultant, with a pediatric hip research focus.

Observer 5 (SH): pediatric orthopaedic consultant, with a Perthes research focus.

Observer 6 (JL): pediatric orthopaedic consultant, with a Perthes research focus.

SDS measurement

All radiographs were anonymized, digitized, and converted to DICOM format (Sectra Workstation, Version 25.2, ©Sectra AB, Sweden). These were distributed to observers and imported into their clinical radiographic systems. Five patients were excluded at one institution due to import failure. All observers received a written tutorial on calibration and SDS measurement (Supplement). Inexperienced observers also received one-to-one instruction and practice using 2 sample cases. SDS was measured twice per hip, with a minimum 2-week interval. Images were cleared of markings before repeat measurement. Observer 4 measured SDS in all healthy hips (N = 35).

Calibration was performed using the distance between the lateral acetabular margins on AP and FLL views (Fig. 1A and B). After calibration, the observers adjusted the radiographs to maximize visualization of the femoral head on AP and FLL views by zooming in on the region of interest. The measurement technique followed Siddesh et al. [8] instructions. Reference points were placed at the medial and lateral FH growth plate margins (Fig. 1C). The maximum inscribed circle (MIC) was drawn (Fig. 1D), followed by the concentric minimum circumscribed circle (MCC) (Fig. 1E). Radii from both were recorded in AP and FLL view (rMIC AP, rMCC AP, rMIC FLL and rMCC FLL) (Fig. 1E).

3-group Stulberg classification

Femoral head shape was classified into round, ovoid, or flat (modified 3-group) by observers 4 and 5. In 8 disagreements, TT served as third rater. Final classification was based on majority vote. The modified classification was validated for use in immature hips (at healing stage) and was found to be a strong predictor of long-term radiographic outcome [6]. Inexperienced observers did not take part in the Stulberg classification because we assumed that the classification would be more correct when assessed by experienced observers as shown in a previous study [5].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous data were tested for normal distribution with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Parametric data were presented as mean (SD) and nonparametric as median (range). Paired t-tests compared SDS mean values within the same Stulberg group across observers. Differences across all 3 Stulberg groups were analyzed with one-way ANOVA (with Levene's test) or Kruskal–Wallis tests, depending on the distribution of the data, for each observer. Post hoc pairwise comparisons between Stulberg groups were performed with Bonferroni correction. Spearman's rho was used to assess correlation between SDS and Stulberg classification. All tests were 2-sided and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Intra- and interobserver agreement of the SDS measurements was analyzed with the Bland-Altman method [11], calculating mean differences and SD between each pair of observations. The 95% limits of agreement (LoA) is mean difference in SDS ± 1.96 SD, and the LoA width was calculated as the difference between mean SDS +1.96 SD and mean SDS -1.96 SD. We defined the LoA width into acceptable (<20), marginal (20-40), and unacceptable (>40) based on previously published SDS differences between femoral head shapes, where transitions between femoral head shape categories (Stulberg classification) occurred at approximately 20 SDS point intervals [7,8].

Reliability was analyzed using the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC), single rating, absolute-agreement with a 2-way random-effects model [15]. ICC values were interpreted as described by Koo et al. [16]: <0.5, poor; 0.50-0.75, moderate; 0.75-0.90, good; and >0.90, excellent.

Results

Among 41 patients (34 boys, 7 girls), mean age at diagnosis was 6.5 years (SD 2.1). At radiographic assessment of SDS and the modified Stulberg classification, the patients had a mean age of 11.8 years (SD 2.5). Skeletal maturity was reached in 7 patients at that point. Based on the modified 3-group Stulberg classification, 22 hips were round, 15 ovoid, and 4 were flat.

SDS

The median SDS values for Perthes hips (2nd measurement session) by all the six observers are presented in Table 1. In round hips, inexperienced observers significantly reported higher SDS values (median 30) compared with experienced observers (median 17; P < 0.001). Median SDS in ovoid hips was 27 (range 23-34) and for flat hips 49 (range 30-57); there were no significant differences between experienced and inexperienced observers. Median SDS in healthy hips (all classified as round) was 9. The 3 components of SDS (RE AP, RE FLL, ED) contributed approximately equally to the SDS score (Supplement).

Reproducibility of SDS

The intra- and interobserver 95% LoA for each pair of SDS measurements are presented in Table 3. Intraobserver agreement among inexperienced observers was unacceptable, with a 95% LoA width >40. Among experienced observers, intraobserver LoA width ranged from 25 to 28 (marginal agreement). Interobserver agreement was unacceptable among inexperienced observers (LoA >40) and marginal among experienced observers, with LoA width between 28 and 35 (marginal agreement) (Table 3). Interobserver agreement between inexperienced and experienced observers ranged from 32 (marginal agreement) and 78 (unacceptable agreement).

Discussion

SDS is a continuous parameter that aims to evaluate femoral head sphericity in Perthes disease. Our study demonstrated that SDS was significantly associated with the 3-group Stulberg classification only when assessed by experienced pediatric orthopaedic researchers. Two out of three experienced observers found significant differences in SDS values between adjacent Stulberg groups (round vs ovoid FH, ovoid vs flat FH), indicating that differentiation between adjacent groups could be inconclusive. Experienced observers had marginal interobserver agreement (LoA) and good interobserver reliability (ICC), whereas interobserver agreement and reliability was poor for inexperienced observers.

SDS measurements

The median SDS value for round hips in our study was 17 for experienced observers and was therefore higher than those reported by previous authors (mean SDS 1.9 to 4.7) [7,8]. We analyzed each of the 3 components of SDS and found that the 2 roundness errors and ellipsoid deformation contributed equally to the SDS value. In our protocol, reference points were placed at the medial and lateral ends of the FH growth plate, in line with Siddesh et al. [8]. The original study by Shah et al. [7] used slightly different reference points, which in part may explain the discrepancy. Kang et al. [9] proposed a modified SDS protocol based on radiographs of only the involved hip. They used the central axis of the femoral neck as reference line but did not specify how this line was drawn. They reported a median SDS of approximately 14 for round hips (exact value not specified in the publication but shown in Fig. 3). In line with Kang et al. [9], we identified 2 major sources that can erroneously increase SDS values. First, the use of the growth plate as an anatomic landmark for the reference points can be challenging in healed Perthes hips, especially in those with advanced skeletally maturation and closed physis. Second, it was not addressed by Shah et al. [7] how to approach the inward concavity of the fovea capitis with the maximum inscribed circle (MIC). Both uncertainties potentially lead to increased SDS values and an overestimation of the FH deformity. In accordance with this, healthy hips in our study had higher SDS values (median 9) than previously reported (mean 1.5) [8]. SDS values for ovoid and flat hips in our study (experienced observers) were consistent with earlier reports [7,8]: median 25 for ovoid hips vs 22-23 in previous literature and 48 for flat hips vs 35-64.

Association between SDS and the 3-group Stulberg classification

Our findings showed that SDS values were not consistently associated with FH shape as defined by the 3-group Stulberg classification. 2 of the three experienced observers were able to distinguish clearly between all 3 Stulberg categories, but SDS values from less experienced observers did not reliably reflect the Stulberg classification. This contrasts with findings by Shah et al. [7], who reported a strong correlation between SDS and the 5-group Stulberg classification, particularly in distinguishing spherical (Class I–II) from aspherical hips (Class III–V). Notably, they also found SDS effective in differentiating ovoid from flat hips. In our study, this distinction was only confirmed by experienced observers, suggesting that SDS interpretation may depend on observer expertise. Differentiating round from ovoid hips is clinically relevant since long-term studies have shown OA in 0–22% of round hips vs 37-61% of ovoid hips [2,6,17,18]. Outcome is also more favorable in ovoid than in flat hips [2,6], underlining the prognostic importance of accurate morphological assessment.

Reproducibility of SDS

Since SDS is a rather complicated measurement, we were somewhat surprised that a good interobserver reproducibility was found in previous studies using the ICC method [[7], [8], [9]] However, the ICC score can be good if there is a large variation in the range of measurement values, as is the case in SDS measurements. In such studies, a more relevant aspect of reproducibility is the actual differences (in figures) between the measurements by various observers, which is assessed by the 95% Limits of agreement [11]. This method, which has not been used in previous studies on SDS, showed quite wide LoA widths. Experienced observers had marginal interobserver LoA widths (28-35), while the ICC values were good (0.79-0.88). Inexperienced observers had insufficient interobserver reproducibility with large LoA widths (>40) and poor to moderate ICC values. This indicates that the SDS method should be used only by experienced observers. However, even in expert hands, agreement remained borderline and needs improvement, which poses challenges for its adoption in scientific studies. Our results are useful for comparison with those of future studies, where the LoA method should be included, and also for the discussion about the suitability of the SDS as a research tool.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, no a priori power analysis was performed. Although our sample size was rather small (41 patients), it was comparable with previous reproducibility studies [9,10]. Second, the distribution of hips across Stulberg categories was uneven, but reflected the natural disease spectrum. The distribution of hips according to the modified Stulberg classification did not differ much from the distribution in a population-based Perthes study [19], with 54% round femoral heads in both studies, 37% ovoid hips vs. 28%, and 10% flat femoral heads in the present study vs. 18% in the population-based study. Nevertheless, if the sample size had been larger, the statistics would have been more reliable, especially in analyses involving hips with flat femoral heads. Third, based on the poor interobserver agreement among the inexperienced observers, the pre-study briefing of SDS measurements in this group could have been more extensive. Finally, SDS measurements were performed using clinical radiographic systems rather than the image analysis software used in previous studies. Shah et al. [7] applied a specific imaging software (Digimizer, MedCalc Software, Mariakerke, Belgium, Version 4.1.1.0), which enables precise visual alignment of AP and FLL views during calibration. This functionality is not available in most clinical radiographic systems, where alignment must be done manually by exact calibration. While this may introduce some variability, we believe our method better reflects real-world clinical conditions and thus improves generalizability.

Conclusion

SDS is a complex and time-consuming parameter that did not consistently distinguish between the groups of the 3-group Stulberg classification. When applied by inexperienced observers, SDS was not reproducible. Even among experienced pediatric orthopaedic surgeons, agreement was only marginal, raising concerns about its reliability in research settings. Further refinements are needed to improve reference point placement, radiographic alignment, and the fitting of measurement circles to ensure reproducibility and wider applicability.
