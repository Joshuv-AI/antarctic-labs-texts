Association of Sugammadex Exposure with Venous Thrombosis Risk Following Primary Total Knee Arthroplasty: A Multi-Institutional Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Kuo-Mao Lan, Chia-Li Kao, Ying-Jen Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAlthough sugammadex may facilitate early postoperative mobilization and transiently influence coagulation parameters, its association with venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains unclear.Patients and methodsThis multi-institutional retrospective study utilized the TriNetX research network to identify individuals aged ≥45 years who received rocuronium and underwent primary TKA between 2010 and 2024. Patients were categorized by reversal agent: sugammadex or neostigmine. The neostigmine group served as the active comparator throughout the study. After 1:1 propensity score matching, 22,844 pairs were analyzed. The primary outcome was VTE incidence at 14 days. Secondary outcomes included deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), pneumonia, urinary tract infection (UTI), postoperative nausea/vomiting (PONV), and mortality at 14 and 90 days.ResultsSugammadex use was associated with a significantly lower risk of VTE at 14 days (odds ratio[OR] 0.60, 95%confidence interval [CI] 0.49–0.74, p<0.001; absolute risk difference, −0.41 percentage points), with lower risks observed for both DVT (OR 0.65, p=0.001) and PE (OR 0.58, p=0.001). This association persisted at 90 days (OR 0.72, 95% CI 0.62–0.84, p<0.001; absolute risk difference, −0.50 percentage points). Sugammadex was also associated with lower rates of pneumonia (p=0.018), UTI (p<0.001), and PONV (p=0.005) at 14 days, without significant differences in mortality. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses yielded results consistent with the primary findings.ConclusionUse of sugammadex was associated with a decreased risk of VTE after primary TKA. Given the observational design and potential for residual confounding, these hypothesis-generating findings warrant confirmation through prospective randomized trials to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and clinical implications.Keywords: sugammadex, venous thromboembolism, deep vein thrombosis, pneumonia, total knee arthroplasty

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is one of the most commonly performed orthopedic procedures worldwide, with over 700,000 procedures conducted annually in the United States alone.1 Although TKA successfully improves the quality of life of patients with end-stage knee osteoarthritis,2 it carries significant risks of postoperative complications, including deep vein thrombosis (DVT).3 In large cohort studies, the incidence of DVT within 90 days after TKA ranges from 0.41% to 1.86%;3–5 however, when diagnostic imaging, such as ultrasound or venography, is employed, the rates may increase to 25.6%–46.8%,6,7 potentially resulting in life-threatening pulmonary embolism (PE) and greater healthcare resource utilization. The prothrombotic state induced by surgical trauma, coupled with perioperative inflammation and postoperative immobility, contributes significantly to this elevated thrombotic risk.8–10 Consequently, identifying modifiable perioperative factors that might reduce DVT incidence is a critical area of investigation with substantial clinical implications.

Neuromuscular blockade management during anesthesia has evolved significantly over the past decade with the introduction of sugammadex, a modified γ-cyclodextrin designed specifically to encapsulate and rapidly reverse aminosteroid neuromuscular blocking agents, particularly rocuronium.11–13 Unlike traditional anticholinesterase reversal agents, such as neostigmine, sugammadex offers several potential clinical advantages that may collectively promote early postoperative mobilization. The selective binding mechanism of sugammadex has been associated with a reduced incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV),14–16 a lower risk of urinary retention,17,18 and decreased pain scores19 compared to neostigmine-based reversal regimens. These benefits may facilitate earlier ambulation and participation in physical therapy following TKA, potentially modifying the risk of immobility-related complications.20 Furthermore, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Tsai et al21 revealed that sugammadex administration transiently prolonged prothrombin time (PT), suggesting that it may induce subtle changes in the coagulation cascade. This direct effect on hemostatic parameters, combined with the indirect benefits of improved recovery allowing earlier mobilization, raises the possibility that sugammadex may influence thrombotic risk in the perioperative period through multiple mechanisms. However, whether sugammadex use specifically affects the risk of postoperative venous thromboembolism (VTE) (including DVT or PE) following TKA remains inadequately explored.

This multi-institutional retrospective study investigated whether sugammadex exposure was associated with a reduced incidence of acute VTE following primary TKA compared to neostigmine. By analyzing a large dataset from diverse healthcare settings and employing propensity score matching to address potential confounding factors, we sought to provide preliminary evidence regarding this potentially clinical relationship.

Methods

Data Sources and Ethical Statement

This multi-institutional retrospective study utilized data from the TriNetX research network, a federated global health research platform that provides access to de-identified electronic health records (EHRs) from over 160 million patients across more than 150 healthcare organizations worldwide.22 TriNetX integrates data from diverse clinical settings, including academic medical centers, community hospitals, and specialty care facilities, enabling comprehensive analyses across various patient populations. The platform standardizes data using common terminologies (SNOMED, ICD, LOINC, and RxNorm), allowing for the consistent identification of diagnoses, procedures, laboratory values, and medications. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB number: No. 11403-E03). Because the study utilized de-identified data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the institutional review board.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

We identified patients aged ≥ 45 years who underwent primary TKA between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2024. Patients who received rocuronium as a neuromuscular blocking agent during the procedure were included. Patients were categorized into two groups based on the reversal agent used: the sugammadex group included patients who received sugammadex as the reversal agent (regardless of dosage), whereas the neostigmine group comprised patients who received neostigmine. Exposure to rocuronium (RxNorm 68139), sugammadex (RxNorm 1726988), and neostigmine (RxNorm 7315) was ascertained using RxNorm medication concepts in TriNetX. Patients were assigned to the sugammadex or neostigmine group if the corresponding reversal agent was recorded on the same date as the index primary TKA encounter. Because TriNetX does not provide reliable medication administration timestamps within the operative encounter, the exact timing of reversal-agent administration relative to surgery could not be further specified.

We excluded patients who received both sugammadex and neostigmine for reversal to ensure clear differentiation between the treatment groups. Patients were also excluded if they had any of the following conditions within one month prior to surgery: pneumonia, acute kidney injury, sepsis, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, respiratory failure, severe anemia (hemoglobin level < 7 g/dL), pulmonary atelectasis, or COVID-19. These exclusions were implemented to minimize confounding by pre-existing conditions that could independently influence the risk of postoperative complications. Patients with a previous history of end-stage renal disease, stage 5 chronic kidney disease, DVT, PE, thrombocythemia, or thrombocytosis were also excluded.

Data Collection

Demographic information, comorbidities, laboratory data, and medication use data were extracted using the TriNetX platform. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, race, and body mass index (BMI). Comorbidity data encompassed essential (primary) hypertension, overweight and obesity, neoplasms, diabetes mellitus, ischemic heart disease, chronic kidney disease, nicotine dependence, liver disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, COVID-19, alcohol-related disorders, anemia, vitamin D deficiency, and malnutrition. Baseline COVID-19 referred to a prior documented history of COVID-19 outside the 1-month preoperative exclusion window. BMI ≥30 kg/m2 was analyzed as an anthropometric variable derived from recorded BMI values, whereas “overweight and obesity” was analyzed separately as a diagnosis-based comorbidity category captured from EHR diagnosis codes. Including both variables helped capture obesity-related risk from complementary sources and reduced reliance on potentially incomplete diagnosis coding. Laboratory values collected included hemoglobin concentration, albumin levels, hemoglobin A1c, C-reactive protein, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Information on medication use was also gathered, focusing on platelet aggregation inhibitors, anticoagulants, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, angiotensin II inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) analogs, and sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors. These categories captured available pharmacological antithrombotic information, with aspirin generally included under platelet aggregation inhibitors and agents such as low-molecular-weight heparin, direct oral anticoagulants, and warfarin included under anticoagulants. However, because antithrombotic drugs were analyzed as broad medication categories, individual agents were not separately examined. TriNetX also could not determine prophylactic versus therapeutic indication, mechanical prophylaxis, or duration of thromboprophylaxis.

Propensity Score Matching

To minimize the impact of confounding variables and selection bias, we employed propensity score matching. A propensity score was calculated for each patient using a logistic regression model that incorporated all baseline characteristics, including demographic variables, comorbidities, laboratory values, and medication use. Patients in the sugammadex group were matched 1:1 with patients in the neostigmine group using a greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of 0.1 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. The quality of matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with values < 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance between groups. The adequacy of matching was also evaluated by visual inspection of propensity score density plots to confirm overlapping distributions between groups.

Primary and Secondary Outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of VTE, encompassing DVT and PE, within 14 days after TKA. VTE events were identified using ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes recorded in the TriNetX electronic health record system, including the I82.4 code family for lower-extremity DVT and the I26 code family for PE. We did not restrict ascertainment by encounter setting; therefore, both inpatient and outpatient coded diagnoses were captured when available in participating healthcare organizations. Diagnostic imaging confirmation was not required because imaging results were not consistently available in TriNetX. Secondary outcomes included urinary tract infection, postoperative nausea/vomiting (PONV) (ICD-10-CM R11), postoperative use of anticoagulants, all-cause mortality, and pneumonia (ICD-10-CM codes J12–J18). Postoperative anticoagulant use was included as an exploratory secondary measure to describe postoperative antithrombotic management; it was not used to define or confirm VTE events. Because TriNetX could not distinguish prophylactic from therapeutic indication or determine the timing of anticoagulant initiation relative to VTE diagnosis, this measure should be interpreted cautiously. To capture extended postoperative risks, we further evaluated primary and secondary outcomes at 90 days. Patients were followed from the index date until the occurrence of the outcome of interest, death, or the end of the respective follow-up window, whichever came first.

Sensitivity and Subgroup Analyses

We performed three pre-specified sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of the results. Model I focused on procedures conducted at academic medical centers to assess whether institutional factors, standardized care pathways, or case complexity influenced outcomes. Model II restricted the analysis to patients who underwent surgery between 2016 and 2024 to evaluate whether temporal changes in clinical practice, including evolving thromboprophylaxis protocols and increased sugammadex adoption, affected the observed associations. Model III excluded patients who received perioperative blood transfusions to eliminate potential confounding from bleeding complications and associated coagulation changes that might independently affect thrombotic risk. Pre-specified subgroup analyses examined treatment effects across sexes (male versus female) and age categories (45–75 years versus >75 years) to identify potential effect modification.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics are summarized descriptively, with continuous variables reported as means with standard deviations and categorical variables as counts with percentages. The study size was determined by the number of eligible patients available in the TriNetX network during the study period; no formal sample size calculation was performed because this was a retrospective database study. BMI and laboratory variables were analyzed using clinically relevant thresholds available in TriNetX. These dichotomized thresholds were selected to reflect commonly used clinical cutoffs and to improve comparability across participating healthcare organizations. Patients with missing BMI or laboratory data were not imputed; missingness was handled according to the TriNetX analytic framework, and variables were included in propensity score matching when available. All analyses were performed using the TriNetX Analytics Platform, which provides access to de-identified electronic health records from participating healthcare organizations. The platform’s integrated statistical functions were used to generate descriptive summaries, conduct cohort matching, and estimate outcome measures. For both 14-day and 90-day outcomes, effect sizes were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For the primary outcome of VTE at 90 days, an additional time-to-event analysis was performed, with results presented as a hazard ratio (HR) with 95% CI, and a Kaplan–Meier survival curve was constructed to display the cumulative probability of remaining VTE-free over the 90-day follow-up period. For the primary outcome, statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value <0.05. Secondary outcomes were considered exploratory; therefore, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied.

Results

Patient Selection and Baseline Characteristics

The initial cohort comprised 53,797 patients who underwent primary TKA and received rocuronium, with 26,414 in the sugammadex group and 27,383 in the neostigmine group (Figure 1). A total of 22,844 matched pairs were obtained after 1:1 propensity score matching. Prior to matching, notable imbalances existed between the groups, including overweight and obesity (30.7% vs 22.4%), chronic kidney disease (8.6% vs 5.8%), and history of COVID-19 (4.5% vs 1.6%) (Table 1). Primary and Secondary Outcomes at 14 and 90 Days

At the primary 14-day endpoint, sugammadex administration was associated with a significantly reduced risk of VTE compared with neostigmine (OR 0.60, p<0.001), with reductions observed in both DVT (OR 0.65, p=0.001) and PE (OR 0.58, p=0.001) (Table 2). In exploratory secondary analyses, sugammadex use was also associated with lower rates of pneumonia (OR= 0.70, p=0.018), urinary tract infection (OR=0.68, p<0.001), and PONV (OR 0.78, p=0.005). As an exploratory secondary medication-use measure, postoperative anticoagulant use was lower in the sugammadex group (28.75% vs 43.72%; OR 0.52, p<0.001). No significant difference in mortality was observed between the groups (OR 1.19, p=0.508).

Sensitivity Analyses

In the academic medical center–restricted analysis (Model I; n=15,857 per group), sugammadex was associated with lower VTE risk (OR=0.67, p=0.001), driven primarily by PE reduction (OR 0.54, p=0.001), while the DVT-specific reduction was not statistically significant (OR 0.82, p=0.19) (Table 4). In the temporal restriction to 2016–2024 (Model II; n=20,937 per group), VTE association remained significant (OR 0.71, p=0.003). The analysis excluding patients with perioperative blood transfusions (Model III; n=22,264 per group) demonstrated consistent protective associations across VTE (OR 0.64, p<0.001), DVT (OR 0.71, p=0.008), and PE (OR 0.61, p=0.004). No significant mortality differences were observed across any of the sensitivity models.

Subgroup Analyses

Sex-based analysis demonstrated consistent VTE risk reduction in both females (OR 0.67, p=0.018; n=15,023 per group) and males (OR 0.58, p<0.001; n=9693 per group), with no significant interaction (p=0.529) (Table 5). Age-stratified analysis showed VTE risk reduction in both the 45–75 year (OR 0.69, p = 0.011; n = 13, 072 per group) and > 75 year (OR 0.61, p = 0.003; n = 7, 602 per group) groups, without significant interaction (p=0.584) (Table 6). Notably, pneumonia reduction was observed only in patients aged > 75 years (OR 0.58, p=0.015) but not in younger patients (OR 0.88, p=0.526), although the interaction did not reach statistical significance (p=0.196).

Discussion

This multi-institutional retrospective study of 22,844 propensity score-matched pairs found that sugammadex exposure for rocuronium reversal was associated with a significantly lower risk of VTE following primary TKA compared to neostigmine. This association was observed for both DVT and PE at 14 days and persisted through the 90-day follow-up, as corroborated by time-to-event analysis. Lower rates of pneumonia, urinary tract infection, and PONV were observed as exploratory secondary associations and should be interpreted cautiously because no adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed. The overall VTE association was broadly supported by sensitivity and subgroup analyses, which were conducted to examine robustness and potential effect modification rather than to provide confirmatory evidence. As hypothesis-generating evidence, this study provides the first large-scale demonstration of a potential relationship between sugammadex exposure and lower thrombotic risk following major orthopedic surgery.

Sugammadex has multiple clinical benefits that may contribute to improved postoperative recovery profiles. Accumulating evidence indicates that sugammadex is associated with a significantly lower incidence of PONV compared with neostigmine.14–16 This reduction is clinically relevant, as PONV can hinder early mobilization and delay postoperative recovery.23 In addition, sugammadex administration has been linked to lower rates of urinary retention,17,18 which can also delay postoperative mobilization.24 Consistently, a retrospective cohort study by Tan et al found that patients receiving sugammadex demonstrated a shorter postoperative ambulation time.25 This improved recovery profile could be particularly relevant in orthopedic procedures, where early mobilization is crucial for preventing thromboembolic complications.26 Additionally, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Tsai et al demonstrated that sugammadex transiently prolongs prothrombin time,21 suggesting a potential direct effect on coagulation parameters. However, the clinical relevance of this coagulation effect for VTE prevention remains speculative because the reported PT prolongation appears mild and short-lived, whereas thrombotic risk after TKA may evolve over days to weeks. The present study did not directly measure ambulation time, physical therapy participation, neuromuscular recovery, PT/aPTT values, or thrombin-generation markers. Therefore, improved postoperative recovery and transient coagulation changes should be considered plausible but unconfirmed hypotheses rather than established mechanisms. In particular, transient coagulation changes alone are unlikely to fully explain the association observed through 90 days.

We selected 14 days as the primary endpoint because this timeframe captures the acute perioperative period when the convergence of surgical trauma–induced hypercoagulability, endothelial injury, and limited postoperative mobility places patients at the highest risk for thromboembolic events.8–10 At this critical window, VTE risk was 40% lower in patients receiving sugammadex than in those receiving neostigmine, with reductions observed for both DVT and PE. However, the absolute difference was modest, corresponding to a 0.41-percentage-point lower 14-day VTE risk (0.62% vs 1.03%). This association persisted at the exploratory 90-day endpoint, with a 0.50-percentage-point lower VTE risk (1.28% vs 1.78%), suggesting that the relationship between sugammadex and lower thrombotic risk may extend beyond the immediate postoperative period. Notably, this is the first large-scale observational study to report such an association in the TKA population. While prior studies have examined sugammadex in the context of postoperative recovery and respiratory outcomes,27–29 its potential relationship with thrombotic complications following orthopedic surgery has not been previously investigated. Given the high annual volume of TKA procedures and the clinical burden of postoperative VTE,1,3 these hypothesis-generating findings warrant further investigation to determine whether sugammadex may serve as a complementary factor within existing thromboprophylaxis strategies.

A notable observation in this study was the substantially lower use of postoperative anticoagulants in the sugammadex group than in the neostigmine group at both 14 and 90 days after surgery. This finding should not be interpreted as evidence of lower prophylactic anticoagulant use alone, because postoperative anticoagulant prescriptions may reflect both thromboprophylaxis and treatment initiated after clinically recognized VTE events. This difference requires cautious interpretation, as several explanations may contribute to this pattern. One possibility is that clinicians perceived fewer clinical indicators of thrombotic risk in patients receiving sugammadex, leading to less frequent use of pharmacological thromboprophylaxis. Conversely, the higher anticoagulant use in the neostigmine group may partly reflect therapeutic anticoagulation following DVT or PE, creating potential reverse causation. In addition, institutional practice patterns may also play a role. Institutions that have adopted sugammadex may also have implemented broader perioperative management strategies, such as enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways, which emphasize early mobilization and optimized perioperative care and may consequently reduce reliance on prolonged pharmacological thromboprophylaxis. Therefore, postoperative anticoagulant use should be regarded as an exploratory descriptive finding rather than a mediator or explanatory factor for the VTE association.

The exploratory association between sugammadex exposure and lower pneumonia risk at both 14 and 90 days is consistent with previous studies reporting respiratory benefits associated with sugammadex.27–29 Residual neuromuscular blockade, even at subclinical levels, is known to impair protective airway reflexes and compromise effective cough generation. By facilitating more complete reversal of neuromuscular function, sugammadex may reduce the vulnerability to micro-aspiration events and improve postoperative pulmonary mechanics, although a direct causal relationship cannot be established from observational data. Notably, despite the observed associations with lower VTE and pneumonia risk, no significant difference in all-cause mortality was identified at either 14 or 90 days. This finding is not unexpected, given the relatively low baseline mortality following elective TKA and the limited statistical power to detect differences in rare events within the study timeframe. Additionally, the multifactorial nature of postoperative mortality, influenced by patient comorbidities, surgical complications, and overall care quality, may attenuate the detectable impact of any single perioperative intervention.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, as with all retrospective studies using administrative data, there is potential for unmeasured confounding despite rigorous propensity score matching. Several factors that are particularly important in TKA-associated VTE risk could not be reliably captured, including specific thromboprophylaxis regimens, agent selection, dose, duration, mechanical prophylaxis, surgical technique, tourniquet use or duration, operative time, anesthesia practice variation, train-of-four monitoring, and ERAS-related mobilization protocols. In particular, the comparison between sugammadex and neostigmine may have been influenced by temporal changes and institution-level perioperative practices. Although we performed sensitivity analyses restricted to academic medical centers and the 2016–2024 period, these analyses could only partially address such confounding. Because TriNetX cannot provide institution-level sugammadex adoption rates or detailed perioperative care protocols, further stratification by high-sugammadex-use institutions was not feasible. Second, follow-up was based on coded encounters within participating TriNetX healthcare organizations; therefore, outcomes managed outside these networks may not have been captured, potentially leading to underestimation of event rates. In addition, missing BMI and laboratory data were not imputed, which may have introduced residual imbalance or reduced adjustment precision for variables with incomplete availability. Third, medication dosing information was limited, precluding dose-response analyses that might clarify whether the observed associations vary with sugammadex dosage. Fourth, the timing of DVT prophylaxis initiation and the specific agents used were not standardized across institutions, introducing potential variability in baseline thrombotic risk. Although baseline antithrombotic medication use was included in propensity score matching, detailed thromboprophylaxis strategies were unavailable. Individual antithrombotic agents were not separately analyzed, and TriNetX could not determine prophylactic versus therapeutic indication, mechanical prophylaxis, or duration of thromboprophylaxis. Because thromboprophylaxis strategy is a major determinant of postoperative VTE after TKA, this limitation may have introduced residual confounding. Fifth, while we excluded patients with recent preceding complications, residual confounding by indication cannot be entirely eliminated, as clinicians may have preferentially selected sugammadex for patients perceived to be at a higher risk for complications. Sixth, this study relied on ICD-10-CM codes for outcome ascertainment, and the accuracy of VTE identification depends on the completeness and consistency of clinical documentation across participating institutions. Because imaging confirmation was not consistently available, we could not verify whether coded VTE events were confirmed by ultrasound, venography, or computed tomography pulmonary angiography. Therefore, both under-coding and over-coding of VTE events are possible, particularly for asymptomatic or surveillance-detected thromboses. Differential ascertainment bias also cannot be excluded; for example, if patients receiving sugammadex had faster postoperative recovery or fewer symptoms prompting diagnostic imaging, VTE events may have been less likely to be detected or coded in this group. In addition, the use of the I82.4 code family may not capture all clinically relevant postoperative DVT events, particularly if events were coded under other venous thrombosis categories or with different levels of coding granularity. Thus, the observed association should be interpreted as a code-based association rather than a fully adjudicated imaging-confirmed VTE outcome. Finally, our study could not assess functional outcomes, such as time to ambulation, patient-reported recovery measures, or cost-effectiveness, which would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the clinical value of sugammadex in the TKA setting.

In conclusion, this multi-institutional retrospective study found that sugammadex exposure for rocuronium reversal was associated with a lower risk of VTE following primary TKA, with the VTE association persisting through the 90-day follow-up and remaining consistent across sensitivity and subgroup analyses. These associations may relate to the transient effects of sugammadex on coagulation parameters and facilitation of early mobilization through improved neuromuscular recovery. However, given the observational design and potential for residual confounding, particularly from unmeasured thromboprophylaxis, surgical, anesthesia, and ERAS-related factors, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating. Prospective randomized trials are warranted to confirm these associations, elucidate the underlying mechanisms, and determine whether sugammadex can meaningfully complement existing thromboprophylaxis strategies in orthopedic surgery.
