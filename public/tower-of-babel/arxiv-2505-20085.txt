Abstract
. Artificial Intelligence (AI) is one of the major technological advancements of this century, bearing incredible potential for users through AI-powered applications and tools in numerous domains. Being often black-box (i.e., its decision-making process is unintelligible), developers typically resort to eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques to interpret the behaviour of AI models to produce systems that are transparent, fair, reliable, and trustworthy. However, presenting explanations to the user is not trivial and is often left as a secondary aspect of the system’s design process, leading to AI systems that are not useful to end-users. This paper presents a Systematic Literature Review on Explanation User Interfaces (XUIs) to gain a deeper understanding of the solutions and design guidelines employed in the academic literature to effectively present explanations to users. To improve the contribution and real-world impact of this survey, we also present a framework for Human-cEnteRed developMent of Explainable user interfaceS (HERMES) to guide practitioners and academics in the design and evaluation of XUIs.

1.
Introduction

Recent years have witnessed a remarkable interest in the field of
AI
(
AI
), which has become a prominent topic in both academia and industry. The rapid advancements in
AI
technologies have led to their widespread adoption across various sectors, including healthcare, finance, and transportation. The
ML
subfield of
AI
has gained particular attention due to its ability to provide discriminative and predictive capabilities, enabling the development of sophisticated applications such as image recognition, natural language processing, and autonomous systems. The increasing complexity of
ML
models has raised concerns about their interpretability and transparency, in particular for those models that are based on deep learning architectures. These models often operate as “black boxes,” making it challenging for users to understand their decision-making processes
(
Guidotti et al. 2019
)
. This has led to a growing demand for
XAI
(
XAI
) techniques that can elucidate the decision-making processes of these models.

Black-box
ML

(

(

(

)
. Most of these regulations advocate the need for
AI

explainable

(

)
. In this context,
XAI
may be beneficial.

is a sub-field of

DARPA
(
DARPA
) in 2017
(
Gunning and Aha 2019
)
, which focuses on “opening” black-box models, making their inner workings clear, explainable, and interpretable
(
Guidotti et al. 2019
;
Gunning and Aha 2019
)
. Although different techniques for explaining black-box models exist (as will be detailed in
Section

), a common and precise definition of “good” explanations has yet to be established. Several studies have pointed out that the majority of the work on

;

;

, effectively framing

(

.
To fill this gap,

(
HCI
) research has recently begun to focus on AI-enabled applications and

, as testified by the birth of the research field of

(

Since the first conceptualisation of

2.
Background

This section introduces the background of our work. It provides an overview of the field of
XAI
, reprising a classification of methods already available in the literature that can guide in understanding the results of this

. Furthermore, it explores the need for a human-centred process and its implications for

.

2.1.
Explainable Artificial Intelligence

The increasing use of complex and opaque
AI
models, such as deep learning architectures, has resulted in the emergence of “black-box” systems. The decision-making processes of such systems are often difficult to interpret, even for
AI
engineers.
XAI
confronts this issue by offering techniques that enhance the understandability of
AI
model predictions for humans
(
Guidotti et al. 2019
)
. This increased clarity fosters trust and accountability
(
Shin 2021
;
Ferrario and Loi 2022
)
, while also mitigating the risks associated with automated decision-making in high-stakes situations.

In
XAI
, explanations can be classified as either
global
or
local
.
Global
explanations offer insights into how a black-box model operates, aiding users in understanding its overall functioning
(
Guidotti et al. 2019
)
. In contrast,
local
explanations focus on understanding the model’s predictions for individual cases
(
Guidotti et al. 2019
)
. Additionally, the type of data processed by the
AI
model also influences explanations.
XAI
methods are most commonly applied to tabular, text, and image data, but they can also handle other types of data such as time series
(
Theissler et al. 2022
)
, audio
(
Akman and Schuller 2024
)
, and graphs
(
Nandan et al. 2025
)
. A possible classification of
XAI
techniques, based on the type of explanation used, is the following
(
Guidotti et al. 2019
;
Adadi and Berrada 2018
)
:

Interpretable models:

A small set of interpretable models (e.g., decision trees, decision rules, and linear models) can be used to explain more complex models by approximating their global or local behaviour. For example, a decision tree can approximate a complex model behaviour, allowing its interpretation as a chain of
if-then-else
rules.

Features Importance:

Explanations may be provided by describing each feature’s weight (i.e., importance) in the decision-making process. Such techniques are referred to as “posthoc” since they can be employed
after
the complex model has been built. Examples of such techniques are
LIME
(
LIME
)
(
Ribeiro et al. 2016
)
and
SHAP
(
SHAP
)
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017
)
. The main difference between the two approaches is that while
LIME
can only provide local explanations,
SHAP
can also produce global explanations. Both techniques are model-agnostic since they can be applied to any existing model.

Saliency Masks:

Saliency masks highlight subsets of the original instance (e.g., set of pixels of an image or words in a sentence) mainly responsible for a certain outcome. Thus, a “salient mask” visually highlights the aspects of the input instance determining the outcome. Saliency masks are generally used to explain deep neural networks; e.g.,
CAM
(
CAM
)
(
Zhou et al. 2016
)
, and its generalisation
Grad-CAM
(
Grad-CAM
)
(
Selvaraju et al. 2020
)
, highlight the contents of an image based on an attention mechanism, which is visualised as an explanation. Saliency masks are also helpful for text:
rationales

(
Lei et al. 2016
)
are a short and coherent piece of text (e.g., a sentence) that represents a sufficient subset of words that can be used to predict the original text on their own.

Sensitivity Analysis:

This technique can be used to inspect black-box models by evaluating the correlation between the uncertainties of their inputs and outputs
(
Saltelli 2002
)
. Sensitivity analysis is generally used to develop visual tools for inspecting black boxes.

Partial Dependence Plot:

PDP
are graphical representations showing the relationship between one or more input features and the model prediction while averaging the remaining features’ effects.
PDP
help understand how changes in certain input features affect the output of a black-box model. Individual Conditional Expectation
(
Goldstein et al. 2015
)
is an extension of
PDP
that allows for visualising the relationship between a feature and the prediction outcome, considering every individual instance in the dataset, instead of averaging over all instances.

Prototype-based Explanations:

A
prototype
(or
archetype
) is an object that is representative of similar instances (such as instances belonging to a certain class). Together with the outcome, these explanations return a prototype, which is very similar to the classified record, to highlight the rationale behind the prediction. Prototypes can be obtained, e.g., as a result of the averages of the features of a set of points
(
Fouché and Langit 2011
)
.

Counterfactual Explanations:

Counterfactual explanations illustrate how minimal changes to input features can alter the model’s prediction. They answer “what-if” questions by providing examples of how the input instance needs to be changed into a similar one to arrive at a different class in the output.
DiCE
(
DiCE
) is a method that generates multiple diverse counterfactual instances for a given instance to help users understand different ways to achieve a desired outcome
(
Mothilal et al. 2020
)
.

Neurons Activation:

These interpretability techniques investigate how individual neurons (or groups of neurons) in neural networks respond to specific inputs. Among the most common neuron activation methods are
activation maximisation
techniques
(
Nguyen et al. 2016
;
Erhan et al. 2010
)
. These methods search for inputs that highly activate specific neurons or layers of a neural network model to unveil trained recognition patterns; this can produce a global interpretable model and can be particularly useful for image recognition models to identify which image patches are responsible for certain neural activations.

2.2.
Towards Human-Centered Explainable Artificial Intelligence

Although XAI aims at addressing the challenges posed by AI-based systems, which, despite their high precision, frequently remain confined to research settings, it often falls short of producing
usable
systems that present their outcomes and explanations in a reliable, safe, and trustworthy manner. In real-world contexts, humans are generally unwilling or unable to trust
black-box
systems that do not provide insight into their decision-making processes
(
Combi et al. 2022
)
. This lack of transparency prevents end-users from questioning
AI
decisions, potentially allowing harmful biases to go unnoticed
(
Stahl et al. 2023
)
.

In response to these challenges, a new field of study has emerged in recent years at the intersection of
HCI
and
AI
, namely
HCAI

(
Desolda et al. 2024b
)
.
HCAI
proposes a new perspective on the interaction with
AI
, aiming to augment rather than replace humans and their expertise
(
Shneiderman 2020
)
.
HCAI
systems are designed to be ethically aligned, reflect human intelligence, and consider human factors
(
Xu 2019
)
. Recent research in
HCAI
suggests the need for a truly human-centered process in the design of
AI
systems, showing how there is no “one-size-fits-all” approach when designing
AI
systems, as different user goals bring the need for a different level of automation and control in a system
(
Esposito et al. 2024
)
. One of the milestones of
HCAI
is the adoption of
HCI
methods for the design and development of
HCAI
. In particular,
HCAI
stresses the importance of user studies in eliciting requirements and validating final systems.

HCD
(
HCD
) is the general model adopted in
HCI
for the design of systems that satisfy users’ needs and expectations; it specifies that users are involved from the very beginning of the planning stage, and identifying user requirements becomes a crucial phase
(
Rogers et al. 2023
)
. Various
HCD
-based design processes are now available, such as
UCD
Sprint
(
Larusdottir et al. 2022
)
.
HCD
requires understanding who will use the system, where, and how to do what. The system is then designed by iterating a design-evaluation cycle. Being the design based on empirical knowledge of user behaviour, needs, and expectations, it is possible to avoid serious mistakes and to save re-implementation time to correct such mistakes. In
HCAI
, adopting
HCD
is necessary when a direct interaction between users and the system is expected (e.g., in consumer applications, educational software, or health-related support).

In this context, well-designed human-centred Human-
AI
interfaces are essential for the success of an
AI
system
(
Shneiderman 2022
;
Raees et al. 2024
;
Desolda et al. 2024a
;
Esposito et al. 2024
)
. Furthermore, when an
AI
system is deployed in high-risk domains like medicine, explainability is crucial
(
Gerdes 2024
;
the Precise4Q consortium et al. 2020
;
Combi et al. 2022
)
. Therefore, a thoughtfully designed, human-centered
XUI
is necessary.

2.3.
Explanation User Interfaces

The notion of an interface mediating between the
XAI
algorithm and the user was initially introduced by
DARPA

(
Gunning and Aha 2019
)
.

DARPA
divides the
XAI
process into two different stages: the generation of the explanations themselves, and the presentation of such explanations through
UI
. The definition of this two-stage process is shared in the academic literature.
Danilevsky et al. 2020
differentiate between explanation techniques and explanation visualizations: the former involves the generation of ‘rough’ explanations (usually propounded by
AI
researchers), while the latter concerns the ways ‘rough’ explanations can be presented to users
(
Danilevsky et al. 2020
)
.

A rigorous definition of
XUI
is formulated by
Chromik and Butz 2021
, who define a
XUI
as
<<the sum of outputs of an XAI system that the user can directly interact with. An XUI may tap into the
ML
(
ML
) model or may use one or more explanation-generating algorithms to provide relevant insights for a particular audience>>

(
Chromik and Butz 2021
)
. In general,
XUI
can be designed for various forms of explanation, both local and global, and can be presented in textual or visual form, or a combination of the two
(
Bove et al. 2023
)
.
XUI
can also leverage other modalities such as sound and tangible interfaces
(
Schneider et al. 2021b
;
Colley et al. 2022
;
Schuller et al. 2021
)
.

According to the definition of
XUI

(
Chromik and Butz 2021
)
, two key aspects are fundamental to their development:
interactivity
and
adaptability
for different types of users. Interactivity, in particular, plays a central role in the explanation process
(
Miller 2019
)
. Research in
HCI
highlights the importance of designing interfaces that allow users to explore explanations freely
(
Abdul et al. 2018
)
, ask follow-up questions, and access additional details as needed
(
Krause et al. 2016
)
. Achieving this level of usability in real-world scenarios requires continuous user involvement throughout the engineering process, from the initial system design to the final testing phases
(
Rogers et al. 2023
)
. However, despite its emphasis in
HCI
, user-centred design remains relatively underexplored in
XAI
and
XUI
, highlighting a critical gap in the field.

XUI
fall into the category of interfaces designed for AI-infused systems. For these interfaces, traditional
UI
principles remain essential; however, the unique and dynamic behaviour of AI systems necessitates the development of new guidelines tailored to their specific characteristics
(
Amershi et al. 2019
)
.

2.4.
Existing Literature Reviews

Many
SLR
(
SLR
) and surveys have explored topics closely related to our
SLR
, underscoring the growing relevance and urgency of advancing
XAI
to ensure its practical implementation in real-world scenarios. These reviews often adopt distinct perspectives, focusing on different aspects of the topic. Several studies emphasise the concept of interactivity:
(
Chromik and Butz 2021
)
focuses on interaction design principles for
XUI
, offering a structured analysis of user interactions and design guidelines. Similarly,
(
Bertrand et al. 2023b
)
categorises interactive explanation techniques based on user evaluation metrics, focusing on interactivity and its effects on user perception.
(
Raees et al. 2024
)
further advances the argument by examining interactivity in
XAI
systems through the lens of Human-Centred AI literature, proposing the evolution of
XAI
into Interactive
AI
. Another study
(
Muralidhar et al. 2023
)
, examines the components of transparency in
AI
systems, aiming to enhance user interpretability and proposing strategies for designing systems that are more interpretable and explainable to users. The evaluation of
XAI
applications through user studies represents another important area of focus.
(
Rong et al. 2024
)
investigates how these studies are conducted, while
(
Al-Ansari et al. 2024
)
analyses the role of
HCI
techniques in improving
XAI
goals.

HCI
remains central to the exploration of explanation design in

(
Ferreira and Monteiro 2020
)
, whose main purpose is to investigate who the recipients of
AI
explanations are, the motivations for providing them, and the methods for aligning explanations with user needs.
The intersection between
XAI
and
HCI
is also investigated in
(
Alaqsam and Sas 2024
)
, where the authors highlight the opportunities for
HCI
concerning
XAI
tools.
Furthermore, studies like those by
(
Laato et al. 2022
)
and
(
Haque et al. 2022
)
focus on how explanations can be effectively tailored to specific user categories, particularly non-technical and end users, addressing critical gaps in accessibility and usability.

Finally,
(
de Souza Filho et al. 2024
)
narrows its scope to reliance-aware explainable user interfaces, synthesising secondary studies to highlight design solutions to foster appropriate reliance in
XAI
-assisted decision-making.
Our review examines the literature on
XUI
, leveraging both algorithmic foundations and human-centred design principles.
Specifically, our research investigates three aspects: (i) the contextual elements that guide the design of
XUI
solutions, (ii)
AI
and
XAI
methodologies commonly employed in the development of
XUI
, and (iii) the evaluation methods, driven by
HCI
principles, used to assess
XUI
effectively. Moreover, we also discuss the design principles that emerge from the literature.
Unlike prior works that often treat these aspects in isolation, our review seeks to integrate these dimensions to identify design trends among human-centred
XUI
and
XAI
algorithms by synthesising insights from 146 papers retrieved from IEEE, ACM, and Scopus.
Our contribution lies in providing a comprehensive overview that progresses the discussion on
XUI
by connecting the technical, design, and evaluation components in a unified way, addressing the need for integrated solutions in real-world
XAI
applications.

Planning the SLR

Planning an

3.1.1.
Formulation of the Research Question

The main goal of our
SLR
is to assess the current state of research about
XUI
and to understand how AI models, tasks, different domains, and users affect the way
XUI
are designed and evaluated. With this objective in mind, we formulated the following research questions:

(RQ1)

What influences the design of
XUI
?

(RQ2)

What
XAI
solutions are used to develop
XUI
?

(RQ3)

What solutions are used to evaluate
XUI
?

(RQ4)

What can guide the design of
XUI
?

3.1.2.
Definition of the Search Strings

We formulated our search strings by deriving keywords from two main sources:

(i)

an initial assessment of some of the most highly cited papers on
XUI
, and

(ii)

the authors’ expertise in the subject matter.

The search strings were designed around three primary concepts aligned with our research questions. Each concept, along with its synonyms and variations, was included to ensure comprehensive coverage of the literature.

•

XAI
(
XAI
)
: This encompasses all techniques that provide insights into the inner workings of black-box models or involve the design of inherently interpretable (white-box) models
(
Guidotti et al. 2019
)
. To capture relevant studies, we included variations of terms such as “
XAI
” and “
XAI
”. The stem “expla*” was employed to include the latter and also terms like “explainability”, “explanations”, and related variations.

•

Human-Centered Design
: High-quality user interfaces require a human-centered approach. To capture this aspect, we made sure to include keywords that could encompass terms such as “human-centred”, “human-centered”, “user-centred”, and “user-centered”, accommodating both British and American spellings. The broader terms “human” and “user” were thus used to ensure inclusivity.

•

Interactive User Interfaces
: Since
XUI
inherently involve user interfaces, relevant literature must address this aspect. Keywords like “interface” and the stem “interact*” were included to cover terms such as “interactivity”, “interaction”, and “interactive”.

The resulting final search string was defined as follows:

("xai" OR "expla*")
AND ("user" OR "human")
AND ("interface" OR "interact*")

This formulation ensures a comprehensive yet precise retrieval of relevant studies, aligning with the scope of our
SLR
.

3.2.1.
Literature Review Execution

The activity was performed from June 2024 to December 2024, following the process depicted in
fig.

1
, which mainly consists of two phases:

(1)

Phase 1 – Digital Library Search
: We searched the identified sources using the previously described search strings.

(2)

Phase 2 – Backward and Forward Snowballing Search
: We checked references and citations of the publications resulting from the previous phase and publications that cited publications from Phase 1
(
Wohlin 2014
)
.

The initial search across the digital library yielded a total of 7084 potentially relevant publications. After a duplicate check, a dataset of 6095 publications was obtained. After filtering, using the previously defined inclusion criteria, 1023 publications were excluded as they did not meet the quality criteria, leaving a final dataset of 5072 candidates. After this first filtering step, the title and abstract were analyzed, resulting in the exclusion of a further 4658 publications, reducing the number to 414. Filtering was conducted independently by three researchers, and conflicts were treated as relevant and moved to the next step. The publications were then carefully analyzed by accessing their full text, narrowing down to 142 valid publications for the review. After forward and backward snowballing, an additional 148 candidates were retrieved and filtered down to 4 using the same inclusion criteria. In conclusion, the final dataset comprises a total of 146 publications.
