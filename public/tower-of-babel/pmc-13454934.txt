Direct and efficient Fischer–Tropsch synthesis on a defect-engineered cobalt-incorporated zirconium metal–organic framework
Authors: Mahmood Poodine, Ali Akbar Mirzaei, Ali Reza Oveisi, Ebrahim Mollashahi, Maryam Akbari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A defective metal–organic framework (MOF), UiO-66, was synthesized and functionalized with cobalt cations anchored directly to its Zr-oxo nodes. The resulting material, Co@DUiO-66, acts as a highly active catalyst for Fischer–Tropsch (FT) synthesis without requiring a pyrolysis step. In contrast to the standard practice of using pyrolyzed MOF derivatives, this work demonstrates the direct use of a metallated MOF for this reaction. Detailed characterization (TGA, DSC, FT-IR, EDX, elemental mapping, FESEM, XRD, ICP-OES, ICP-MS, CHN, TEM, and BET) confirms that the porosity is maintained and that cobalt remains uniformly dispersed at the atomic level. Under industrial conditions (220 °C, 20 bar, GHSV of 2400 mL gcat−1 h−1), the catalyst achieved a high CO conversion of 82.67%, comparable to other cobalt-MOF derivatives. This high activity results from the unique, site-isolated Co2+ centers and the preserved, high-surface-area framework, which together improve catalyst stability and longevity during FT reactions. The direct-use approach removes the energy-heavy pyrolysis step, simplifying production and offering significant economic and industrial benefits. This research introduces a new strategy for designing efficient, site-specific, non-pyrolyzed MOF catalysts for important processes like FT synthesis.

Introduction

The Fischer–Tropsch (FT) synthesis is a catalytic process that converts synthesis gas (a mixture of H2 and CO) into liquid transportation fuels. A key advantage of FT fuels is their lower pollutant emissions compared to conventional petroleum-derived fuels, due to the absence of sulfur and other impurities. As such, FT technology represents a major alternative pathway for producing clean liquid and gaseous fuels.1,2 While several metals including iron, cobalt, nickel, and rhodium exhibit catalytic activity for FT synthesis, cobalt and iron are the most practically significant. Nickel tends to promote methane formation and can produce toxic nickel carbonyl, which deactivates catalysts.3,4 Rhodium is prohibitively expensive. Between cobalt and iron, cobalt is more costly but operates at a lower optimal temperature range (180–250 °C vs. 250–300 °C for iron). Cobalt-based catalysts are widely preferred due to their high activity, long-term stability, and superior selectivity for desired long-chain hydrocarbons (C5+).5–8 Iron-based catalysts, in contrast, show higher selectivity toward olefins and oxygenated compounds.9,10 Conventional FT catalysts are often supported on oxides such as silica,11 titanium oxide,12 and zeolites.13 However, strong metal-support interactions can sometimes negatively impact catalytic performance.14 Recent advancements have established metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) as a versatile and promising class of materials, finding important applications as both catalyst supports and catalyst precursors due to their ultra-high surface areas, tunable porosity, and structural versatility.15–19

Almost all MOF compounds are pyrolyzed for use in FT synthesis.20–22 However, due to the high pyrolysis temperatures required (typically above 400 °C), the preparation of catalysts from these materials is not economically viable. Zirconium-based MOFs, such as UiO-66, offer exceptional thermal and chemical stability, attributed to strong Zr–O bonds and robust coordination between the zirconium clusters and organic linkers.23,24 UiO-66 exhibits thermal stability up to approximately 450 °C.25 Given this high thermal stability, the catalyst can be used directly in catalytic processes such as FT synthesis, thereby eliminating the need for high-temperature pre-treatments (pyrolysis) and offering significant economic advantages. Notably, this inherent stability also allows for the controlled introduction of defects through post-synthetic treatments, such as acid washing with HCl.26 Treatment with HCl selectively removes a fraction of organic linkers, creating coordinatively unsaturated zirconium sites and increasing porosity without collapsing the framework. These “defect-engineered” UiO-66 materials often exhibit enhanced catalytic properties due to a higher density of accessible active sites and improved mass transport, making them even more attractive for demanding applications like Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.27 Cobalt supported on zirconia-based materials exhibits strong metal–support interactions that inhibit cobalt sintering and oxygen adsorption, thereby maximizing the accessible active cobalt surface during reaction.28,29 Such systems also enhance C5+ selectivity and suppress methane formation.30 Although UiO-66 has been explored as a catalyst in various applications,31–33 there is no reported use of defect-engineered UiO-66 in FT synthesis to date. Studies on defective UiO-66 in other catalytic reactions indicate that introduced defects can increase the density of active sites, improve mass transport through modified pore architecture, and consequently enhance catalytic performance in particular, defect engineering has been recognized as a means to tailor pore structure and functionality.34,35 In this work, we incorporate cobalt into a defective UiO-66 framework, adapting and modifying the method reported by Löbbert et al.36 and evaluate the resulting composite as a catalyst for FT synthesis. Result and discussion

Material characterization

The identification of functional groups and bonds in the samples was accomplished using FT-IR spectroscopy. FT-IR studies on freshly prepared Co@DUiO-66 showed a stretching vibration around the 3400 cm−1 absorption region, which was previously attributed to the –OH stretching vibration associated with the defect site. In addition, as can be seen in Fig. 2, the –OH stretching vibration peak is strongly reduced after using this catalyst in the FT synthesis. Considering that the catalyst reduction process was carried out at 350 °C and at this temperature it is possible to remove this functional group, the reason for the reduction of the stretching vibration around the 3400 cm−1 absorption region can be attributed to the removal of the –OH functional group during the catalyst reduction. In infrared spectroscopy, the presence of an absorption band in the range of 1600–1650 cm−1 can be considered as an indicator of molecular water, suggesting the presence of residual water molecules on the nodes.36 The strong peak observed in defective UiO-66 at approximately 1400 cm−1 is attributed to the symmetric vibrations of the carboxylate group in the terephthalic acid ligand.37,38 Upon Co incorporation, this peak is broadened and reduced in intensity in the Co@DUiO-66 spectrum (Fig. 2). This perturbation is consistent with Co(ii) coordination to the zirconium-oxo nodes (Zr–O–Co), which modifies the electronic environment of the nodes and indirectly affects the adjacent carboxylate groups.36,39 While direct observation of Co-oxo vibrations is challenging due to their weak intensity, the observed spectral changes provide indirect evidence for successful Co grafting to the defect sites of the UiO-66 framework. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 2, the FTIR absorption spectra of the Co@DUiO-66 catalyst remain largely unchanged throughout fixed-bed reactor operation, with the only notable variation occurring in the –OH stretching region around 3400 cm−1. This decrease can be attributed to the removal of hydroxyl groups during the reduction process, as well as to possible changes in the coordination environment of cobalt, to which these hydroxyl groups are coordinated. These observations indicate that the catalyst maintains its structural integrity and preserves its functional groups under the applied process conditions, including reduction and reaction temperatures, without significant degradation. Consequently, the Co@DUiO-66 catalyst demonstrates good stability under the FT synthesis conditions employed.

FTIR spectra of defective UiO-66 and as-prepared Co@DUiO-66, and Co@DUiO-66 after FT process.

Thermal stability and behavior of the as-prepared Co@DUiO-66 catalyst were investigated using TGA and DSC analysis (Fig. 3). The TGA curve shows three distinct weight-loss stages.40 The first stage (100–150 °C) corresponds to the evaporation of residual water trapped in the catalyst pores, consistent with an endothermic peak in the DSC trace. The second stage (200–350 °C) is attributed to removal of the remaining DMF solvent, also reflected by an endothermic DSC signal. The final weight loss, beginning around 450 °C, corresponds to decomposition of the organic linkers and structural collapse. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) showed a total weight loss of about 47.4% over the temperature range up to 800 °C. Since the FT operating temperatures reported in this work are below 350 °C, the Co@DUiO-66 fresh catalyst demonstrates excellent thermal stability under the applied process conditions.

TGA and DSC analyses of unused Co@DUiO-66 catalyst in argon atmosphere.

XRD analysis was performed to determine the crystalline phases in the compounds. Phase identification was performed using X'Pert HighScore Plus software, and diffraction patterns were compared with the Crystallography Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) reference database. The XRD patterns for the defective UiO-66, unused Co@DUiO-66 and the used Co@DUiO-66 compound specifically show distinct peaks at 2θ angles of 7.3°, 8.5°, and 25.7°, which correspond to the (111), (020), and (006) Miller planes, respectively. These peaks are characteristic of the zirconium terephthalate phase and confirm the successful synthesis of the UiO-66 framework in these compounds.41 According to Fig. 4, the XRD pattern of the unused Co@DUiO-66 catalyst exhibits peaks at 2θ angles of 31° (220), 44.8° (400), and 56° (422), corresponding to the Co3O4 phase. These peaks are in good agreement with the standard XRD pattern (JCPDS, No. 74-1656). Meanwhile, for the used Co@DUiO-66 catalyst, in addition to the peak at 2θ angle 31° (220) related to the Co3O4 phase, it has peaks at angles 44.2° (111) and 51.5° (200), which are related to the cobalt metal phase with a cubic structure (β-Co) (JCPDS, No. 15-0806). It is noteworthy that the intensity of the peak corresponding to the Co3O4 phase decreased after using the catalyst in the reactor, and a new β-Co phase appeared as well, which indicates chemical changes in the catalyst during the reduction process and its subsequent use in the reactor during the Fischer–Tropsch process. Furthermore, a comparison of the XRD patterns for the Co@DUiO-66 catalyst (as-prepared) before and after the reaction (Fig. 4) reveals only negligible changes, demonstrating that the catalyst maintains its structural integrity under the operating conditions of the FT process.

XRD patterns of (a) as-prepared Co@DUiO-66, (b) Co@DUiO-66 after reactor testing, and (c) defective UiO-66.

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) was employed to examine the morphological features of the Co@DUiO-66 catalyst before (as-prepared) and after reaction in the fixed-bed reactor. As shown in Fig. 5, the catalyst particles exhibit a uniform spherical morphology both before (as-prepared) and after testing. While a slight decrease in average particle size is observed after the reaction (from approximately 200 nm to 180 nm), the overall morphology remains intact. This observation aligns with the XRD results, which indicated minimal structural change. The retention of uniform particle morphology and the absence of significant agglomeration further demonstrate the good stability of the catalyst under FT synthesis conditions. Additionally, the spherical particles display a notable surface roughness, which may contribute to the enhanced catalytic performance by providing increased active surface area and improved accessibility for reactants.

FESEM images of Co@DUiO-66 (a) as-prepared and (b) after use in the reactor.

The elemental composition and distribution within the Co@DUiO-66 catalyst were characterized using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) and elemental mapping on both the as-prepared catalyst and the catalyst after use in the reactor (Fig. 6 and 7). These observations confirm the successful synthesis of the catalysts. The EDX characterizations of the as-prepared and used catalysts, given in Fig. 6 and 7, indicate the presence of Co, Zr, C, and O. Elemental mapping revealed an excellent dispersion of zirconium and cobalt throughout the material.

This value indicates the presence of defects, as the theoretical C/Zr molar ratio for pristine UiO-66 is 8. Using the mass percent of zirconium and applying eqn (2) and (3), the degree of defect for the Co@DUiO-66 catalyst is 1.62. This means that for each Zr6 cluster, about 1.62 terephthalate ligands are removed. This defect value is also approximately confirmed using the mass percent of carbon.

A standard method for calculating the degree of defect based on TGA analysis has been proposed by Bueken et al.42 and Katz et al.26 and is widely regarded as the most reliable approach for determining the number of missing linkers in UiO-66-type materials. Following this method, we obtained a defect degree of 2.84 missing linkers per Zr6 node for the Co@DUiO-66 catalyst. This method relies on the mass loss between the node dehydration plateau (∼300 °C, corresponding to Zr6O4(OH)4 → Zr6O6) and the linker decomposition step (∼500 °C). A key advantage of this approach is its independence from residual solvent content, as all solvents are completely removed below 200 °C. Accordingly, the TGA-derived value is adopted as the primary quantification of defects, while the elemental analysis-based estimate serves only as qualitative confirmation of the presence of defects.

ICP-MS analysis also confirmed the presence of chlorine in the as-prepared Co@DUiO-66 catalyst, which likely originates from the hydrochloric acid or zirconium chloride employed during synthesis. Following its use in the FT synthesis, ICP analysis revealed that the chlorine mass percentage decreased to 0.0013%.

The spherical morphology observed in electron microscopy images reveals a rough and porous surface. This surface texture is consistent with the high specific surface area determined by BET analysis. Both BET and BJH analyses confirm the micro/mesoporous nature of the material, with a specific surface area of 1036.5 m2 g−1 and an average pore size of 2.78 nm. The measured pore structure confirms the catalyst's suitability for FT synthesis.

Porosimetry analysis of the as-prepared Co@DUiO-66 catalyst is presented in Fig. 8. The N2 adsorption isotherm (Fig. 8a) exhibits Type I behavior, characteristic of microporous materials, yet shows a substantially increased uptake at high relative pressures (P/P0). This enhanced adsorption can be attributed to the presence of missing-linker defects, which reduce framework density and increase porosity. The high reversibility between the adsorption and desorption branches indicates a thermodynamically reversible sorption process, often associated with pores near the microporous–mesoporous boundary. To further characterize the pore structure, the t-plot diagnostic method was employed (Fig. 8d). The pronounced curvature observed is indicative of capillary condensation within mesopores, confirming the mesoporous character of material. This conclusion is supported by the Langmuir plot (Fig. 8c), which shows a downward deviation at higher relative pressures a signature of multilayer adsorption and capillary condensation in mesopores. The BJH analysis data (Table 1) corroborates the presence of pores in this size regime.

TEM analysis of the unused (as-prepared) Co@DUiO-66 catalyst (Fig. 9) shows a crystalline morphology consistent with the underlying UiO-66 structure. While the image shows some textural and contrast changes, no discrete nanoparticles are observed. The absence of discrete particle features, together with the preservation of the integrity of the UiO-66 crystallites, supports the interpretation that the incorporated metals are highly dispersed in the framework, likely existing as isolated ions or small nanoparticles coordinated to the defect sites, rather than as a separate, discrete phase.

Catalyst performance in FT synthesis

We investigated the catalyst activity in a fixed-bed micro-reactor. First, we reduced the catalyst before carrying out the FT reactions. To reduce the catalyst, one gram of the catalyst was kept at 350 °C for 12 h (a temperature increase rate of 2 °C min−1 was used to reach this temperature), at 1 bar and a hydrogen gas flow rate of 50 mL min−1 to ensure good regeneration. It has been shown that one of the desirable properties of zirconium in the catalyst support is that it provides better reducibility to the catalyst, and higher reducibility leads to an increase in the specific surface area43 To obtain optimal operating conditions based on better catalyst conversion and higher C5+ selectivity, we tested the performance of the Co@DUiO-66 catalyst under different conditions (Tables 2 and 3), based on which the optimal conditions were obtained including H2/CO = 1, T = 220 °C, P = 20 bar, and GHSV = 40 (mL gcat−1 h−1).

We compared the performance of the optimized catalyst with that of a defect-free UiO-66(Co) catalyst.44 The defective catalyst demonstrates superior CO conversion and C5+ selectivity. By comparing the BET analysis data, we notice a clear difference in the specific surface area and pore volume data between the flawless and the defective catalyst, such that the specific surface area for the defective catalyst is 1036.5 m2 g−1, while for the defect-free catalyst it is 375 m2 g−1. The pore volume values are 0.61 cm3 g−1 for the Co@DUiO-66 and 0.32 cm3 g−1 the defect-free catalyst. This significant difference in the specific surface area and pore volume measurement data for the two catalysts confirms that the defects introduced into the Co@DUiO-66 catalyst have effectively increased the porosity in this catalyst compared to the defect-free catalyst, and has improved the performance of this catalyst in the Fischer–Tropsch process.

Its performance was also evaluated in comparison with other reported cobalt–zirconium catalysts prepared under different conditions (Table 4). It is noteworthy that although the Co@DUiO-66 catalyst was directly used in the FT process without thermal pretreatments such as calcination or pyrolysis, it nevertheless showed relatively higher CO conversion and hydrocarbon selectivity than the calcined or pyrolyzed catalysts, which is probably due to the increased surface area and better accessibility to the active phase resulting from the defect-rich structure. The catalyst also maintained its activity during the 72 h test, indicating its good stability.

Conclusions

In this work, we prepared a cobalt-containing catalyst supported on defective, missing-linker UiO-66 using a modified metallation approach. For the first time, this defect-engineered MOF was directly employed as a support for FT synthesis without any prior pyrolysis or calcination. The performance of this catalyst was compared to a counterpart prepared from defect-free UiO-66. The results demonstrate that the Co@DUiO-66 catalyst exhibits superior FT activity and C5+ selectivity, highlighting the beneficial role of linker vacancies in enhancing catalytic performance. Notably, this work presents a more economical and streamlined preparation route by eliminating the need for high-temperature thermal pre-treatments common to many FT catalyst precursors. Under similar reaction conditions, the defective catalyst performs competitively with other reported catalysts. Its high activity at low temperatures, combined with favorable C5+ selectivity, underscores its potential for both further research and industrial application. These findings establish defective UiO-66 as a promising support for FT synthesis and open new avenues for catalyst design. Future work should focus on the precise control of defect engineering, detailed mechanistic studies of low-temperature operation, and investigations into the long-term stability and scalability of this catalyst system. The strategy of utilizing defective MOFs as supports is also likely applicable to other catalytic reactions that benefit from high metal dispersion and tunable porosity.

Experimental

Materials

The materials used in the synthesis process included 37% hydrochloric acid (purity 99.999%), DMF (purity 99.8%), and hexane (purity 96%), all purchased from Merck. Cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (purity 99.999%), terephthalic acid (purity 95%), zirconium chloride (purity 99%), acetone (purity 99.8%), and ethanol (purity 99.8%) were purchased from Shalu Company. Additionally, double-distilled water was used throughout the synthesis processes.

Synthesis of defective UiO-66 (DUiO-66)

We prepared defective UiO-66 based on the work of Katz et al.,26 with a slight modification. Accordingly, we took ten 30 mL glass vials and added 125 mg of ZrCl4 to each. Then, we added 5 mL of dimethylformamide (DMF) solvent and 1 mL of HCl 37% solution to each vial, and then stirred the vials in an ultrasonic device for 30 min. After initial sonication, 123 mg of terephthalic acid and 10 mL of dimethylformamide (DMF) are added to each vial. The mixtures are then sonicated for an additional 30 minutes and placed in an oven at 80 °C for 16 h. After heating and cooling the vials, the white precipitate from each vial is filtered and washed twice with 20 ml of dimethylformamide and then twice with 20 ml of ethanol. Then, the resulting precipitate is placed at 90 °C for three h to remove the solvents, and after this time, the resulting precipitate is placed at 170 °C for another 3 h to obtain a white precipitate of defective UiO-66. Fig. 10 shows a schematic of the synthesis of defective UiO-66 and its metallization with cobalt metal (Co@DUiO-66).

(a) Synthesis of defective UiO-66 (D-UiO-66), (b) post-synthetic metallization with cobalt nitrate.

Defective UiO-66 metallization

In this step, based on the previous work,36 with a slight modification, we prepared cobalt-based defective UiO-66. In this step, we dispersed one gram of defective UiO-66 in 80 mL of hexane by ultrasonication for 20 min, and added 0.6 mL of 0.7 M Co(NO3)2·6H2O solution dropwise to the mixture under vigorous stirring. After 2 h, the resulting purple precipitate was separated from the solvent by filtration and at the same time, it was washed first twice with 20 ml of ethanol and then twice with 20 ml of acetone. After that, the precipitate was placed at 90 °C for 12 h. To remove the nitrate components, the precipitate was placed at 250 °C in an inert argon gas atmosphere for 4 h.456

Catalytic evaluation

The catalyst (1.0 g) was loaded into a stainless steel tubular fixed-bed microreactor. The grades of feed gases used are as follows: carbon monoxide gas is grade 3.7 (equivalent to 99.97% purity) and hydrogen gas is grade 5 (equivalent to 99.999% purity). Before activity testing, it was subjected to in situ reduction for 12 h at 350 °C under a hydrogen atmosphere (1 bar, 50 mL min−1). Following catalyst reduction, the reactor was prepared for operation by gradually increasing the temperature, switching the feed gas from hydrogen to synthesis gas mixture, and adjusting to the desired operating pressure. A Thermo Finnigan Trace GC gas chromatograph (Finnigan, USA) with two independent channels was used to analyze products and determine catalyst performance via feedstock conversion percentage. Channel 1 featured two packed columns in series (molecular sieve followed by physical adsorption) for separating and quantifying feed components and CO2. Channel 2 used a capillary column with a flame ionization detector (FID) to quantify C1–C5 hydrocarbon products. Prior to experiments, the GC was calibrated with three standard gas mixtures to establish a standard curve. For analysis, reactor effluent passed through a condenser to remove heavy compounds (C5+), with remaining light gases (C1–C5) routed directly to the GC for online measurement. Conversion percentage was calculated based on CO peak areas and inlet/outlet volumetric flow rates. The conversion, hydrocarbon product selectivity, and CO2 selectivity were obtained using eqn (4)–(6), respectively. In eqn (4), inlet and outlet denote the inlet and outlet gas flow rates from the reactor bed, respectively, while C represents the concentration. In eqn (5), F and n correspond to the molar flow rate and the number of moles of each product, respectively.

Identification methods

XRD pattern analysis was performed with a Bruker Advance D8 diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation, collecting data at 0.02° intervals with 0.2 seconds per step. Specific surface area was determined via nitrogen physisorption using a Quantachrome analyzer, applying the BET method to adsorption data up to 0.3 atm. Prior to analysis, all samples were degassed under a nitrogen flow at 250 °C for 3 hours. Chemical functional groups were identified with a Bruker TENSOR II ATR-FTIR spectrophotometer, which operates in reflectance mode without requiring sample preparation. Surface morphology and elemental mapping were investigated using a TESCAN MIRA3 LMU FESEM equipped with an EDX detector, capable of up to 10 000 00× magnification. The elemental composition of the mass was performed using ICP-OES analyses with a PerkinElmer Optima 2000DV instrument, ICP-MS with a PerkinElmer Elan 9000 instrument and CHN analysis with a TruSpec model instrument made in the United States. Thermal stability was assessed via TGA/DSC on a TA Instruments Q600 analyzer with a 10°C min−1 heating rate under argon. Finally, nanostructural changes in catalysts were examined using a Philips CM120 TEM operated at 100 kV with a magnification of up to 1 800 00×.
