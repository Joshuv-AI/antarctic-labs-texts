Association and predictive ability between significant perioperative cardiovascular adverse events and stress glucose rise in patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery
Authors: Jingfang Lin, Yingjie Chen, Maokai Xu, Jianghu Chen, Yongxin Huang, Xiaohui Chen, Yanling Tang, Jiaxin Chen, Jundan Jiang, Yanling Liao, Xiaochun Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe predictive importance of the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), which is composed of admission blood glucose (ABG) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), has not been fully established in noncardiac surgery. This study aims to evaluate the association and predictive capability the SHR for major perioperative adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in noncardiac surgery patients.MethodsIndividuals who underwent noncardiac surgical procedures between 2011 and 2020, including both diabetic and non-diabetic patients, were identified in the perioperative medicine database (INSPIRE 1.1) and classified into tertiles based on their SHR. The connection between the SHR and the risk of MACEs was studied using Cox proportional hazards regression analysis, then restricted cubic spline (RCS) was employed to assess the association’s form. Additionally, the SHR’s incremental predictive utility for MACEs was assessed by the C-statistic, continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), thereby quantifying the enhancement in predictive accuracy brought by incorporating the SHR into existing risk models. Feature importance and predictive models were generated utilizing the Boruta algorithm and machine learning approaches.ResultsA total of 5609 patients were enrolled. With an upwards shift in SHR vertices, the rate of perioperative MACEs and cardiac death event steadily rose. The RCS analysis for perioperative MACEs and cardiac death event both indicated J-shaped associations. Inflection points occurred at SHR = 0.81 for MACEs and SHR = 0.97 for cardiac death. The model’s fit improved significantly, with a continuous NRI of 0.067 (95% CI: 0.025–0.137, P < 0.001) and an IDI of 0.305 (95% CI: 0.155–0.430, P < 0.001). When SHR was added as a categorical variable (> 0.81), the C-statistic increased to 0.785 (95% CI: 0.756–0.814) with a ΔC-statistic of 0.035 (P = 0.009), a continuous NRI of 0.007 (95% CI: 0.000-0.021, P = 0.016), and an IDI of 0.076 (95% CI -0.024-0.142, P = 0.092). In the Boruta algorithm, variables identified as important features in the green area were incorporated into the machine learning models development.ConclusionsThe SHR was related with an increased risk of perioperative MACEs in patients following noncardiac surgery, highlighting its potential as a useful and reliable predictive tool for assessing the risk of perioperative MACEs.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-024-02542-2.

Introduction

Perioperative severe cardiovascular adverse events in non-cardiac surgery patients pose a significant threat to public health. Perioperative myocardial injury (PMI), often occult and manifesting within 48 h postoperatively, affects up to 8% of patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery [1, 2], complicating early diagnosis and timely intervention. The majority of patients do not present with classic symptoms such as chest tightness or discomfort, which complicates early diagnosis and timely intervention [3].This diagnostic challenge impairs postoperative recovery, reduces quality of life, and increases the risk of mortality. Therefore, enhancing the prediction and prevention of perioperative cardiovascular events is paramount to improving clinical outcomes and patient quality of life [4].

Dysregulated glucose metabolism is strongly associated with increased incidence of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular events [5]. Short-term hyperglycemia heightens susceptibility to oxidative stress and inflammation, promoting atherosclerotic plaque instability and rupture, thus escalating the risk of cardiovascular complications [6, 7]. Preoperative anxiety and acute trauma activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and sympathetic nervous system, resulting in elevated preoperative blood glucose levels, a phenomenon known as stress hyperglycemia [8, 9]. For diabetic patients, the median preoperative blood glucose is 7.0 mmol/L [10]. Additionally, over 25% of patients without prior diabetes diagnoses present with abnormal preoperative fasting blood glucose levels [11], and a cohort study involving 61,536 non-cardiac surgery patients found that 3.1% of non-diabetic patients had preoperative blood glucose levels exceeding 8 mmol/L [10]. Surgical procedures themselves act as significant stressors, amplifying neuroendocrine dysfunction and exacerbating blood glucose fluctuations [12]. Postoperative inflammation, along with pain and anxiety, further induces insulin resistance and stimulates gluconeogenesis, thereby elevating blood glucose levels and increasing the risk of perioperative cardiovascular events, particularly in diabetic patients [9]. In non-cardiac surgery patients, preoperative blood glucose levels exceeding 11.1 mmol/L have been associated with a 2.1-fold increase in overall 30-day mortality and a 4-fold increase in 30-day cardiovascular mortality [13]. Therefore, understanding preoperative stress-induced hyperglycemia levels is crucial for predicting perioperative cardiovascular events.

Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) is a widely recognized marker reflecting average blood glucose over the previous 8–12 weeks, and serves as an indicator of long-term glycemic control. The combination of admission blood glucose (ABG) and HbA1c has led to the development of a novel biomarker, the Stress Hyperglycemia Ratio (SHR), a novel biomarker that effectively stratifies baseline blood glucose levels and assesses stress-induced hyperglycemia [14]. Previous studies have demonstrated the SHR plays a significant predictive role in acute myocardial infarction, heart failure, and critical illness [15–19], and is also closely associated with surgical outcomes [20]. This study aims to elucidate the relationship and predictive capacity of SHR in identifying major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in non-cardiac surgery patients, utilizing data from the INSPIRE database.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective analysis utilized perioperative data from the INSPIRE database (version 1.1), a comprehensive perioperative medical database maintained by a South Korean academic institution. The INSPIRE database, spanning from 2011 to 2020, includes detailed medical records of approximately 130,000 patients who underwent surgery under anesthesia, providing an extensive source of high-quality perioperative information. The study adhered strictly to the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies.

The study population comprised individuals aged 18 years and above who underwent noncardiac surgery (including gastrointestinal, orthopaedic, neurosurgery, obstetric, gynaecological, otolaryngology, thoracic and urology procedures) between 2011 and 2020, identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Procedure Coding System (ICD-10-PCS). Patients were excluded if they lacked preoperative results for ABG or HbA1c; presented with acute myocardial infarction, acute stroke, or acute heart failure during hospitalization prior to surgery; or had more than 20% of missing data.

Clinical outcomes

The primary endpoint of this study was the incidence of MACE within 30 days after surgery, which included cardiac death, perioperative myocardial infarction, acute heart failure, and perioperative stroke. Each component of MACE was also evaluated individually as secondary endpoints.

Cardiac death was defined as any instance of cardiac-related mortality occurring from the intraoperative period through the postoperative phase. Perioperative myocardial infarction and perioperative stroke were identified using ICD-10 codes I21 and I64, respectively. Acute heart failure was defined as the new onset of heart failure following surgery, identified by ICD-10 code I50. Diagnostic information for these events was obtained from the diagnosis table of the INSPIRE database.

Study variables

Data extraction was performed using PostgresSQL (version 13.8), utilizing Structured Query Language (SQL) for precise information retrieval. The extracted latent variables were classified into five categories: (1) Demographics: including age, sex, and body mass index (BMI); (2) Comorbidities: including diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, carotid artery stenosis, valvular heart disease, atrial fibrillation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), renal insufficiency, hepatic insufficiency, prior stroke, prior myocardial infarction, and cancer; (3) Medications: including β-blockers, statins, calcium channel blockers (CCB), anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs, and antidiabetic drugs (insulin, sulfonylureas, biguanides, thiazolidinediones, and DPP-4 inhibitors); (4) Surgery details: surgical status (emergency or elective surgery), type of surgery (including gastrointestinal, orthopedic, neurosurgery, obstetric, gynecological, otolaryngology, thoracic and urology procedures), high-risk surgery, as defined by the Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI), includes intraperitoneal, intrathoracic, and suprainguinal vascular surgeries, anesthesia type, and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification; (5) Laboratory tests: collected during the first examination upon admission, including HbA1c, ABG, hemoglobin, albumin, white blood cells, platelets, and creatinine. SHR was calculated using the formula: SHR = ABG / (28.7 × HbA1c − 46.7), where ABG is expressed in mg/dL and HbA1c in percentage [18].

Statistical analysis

Variables with more than 20% missing data were excluded from the analysis, while those with fewer than 20% missing data were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) to ensure unbiased estimates. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated to assess multicollinearity among variables, with a threshold of 5 used to exclude variables to mitigate collinearity issues. VIF measures the extent to which the variance of a regression coefficient is inflated by the presence of multicollinearity in the model. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were reported as means ± standard deviations (SDs) and analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA). For variables not following a normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney U test or the Kruskal–Wallis test was used.

Univariate (Model 1) and multivariate Cox proportional hazard models were constructed to evaluate SHR’s association with clinical outcomes, considering SHR as both a continuous and categorical variable. Models were partly (models 2) or fully (model 3) adjusted for age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, emergency status, type of surgery, type of anesthesia, diabetes status, hypertension status, hyperlipidaemia status, carotid artery stenosis status, valvular heart disease status, atrial fibrillation status, COPD, renal insufficiency status, hepatic insufficiency status, prior myocardial infarction, IOH status, β-blocker status, insulin status and antidiabetic drug status. The median value of the tertiles was employed as a quasi-continuous variable in the models to determine trends’ p-values.

The occurrence rates of MACEs across different SHR tertiles were assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, and group differences were assessed with log-rank tests. To explore potential nonlinear associations between SHR and patient outcomes, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were performed, using four knots (at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles). If a nonlinear relationship between the SHR and perioperative MACE risk was revealed by the RCS, we further calculated the inflection point and analyzed the relationship on either side of this point using a two-segment Cox proportional hazards model. The determination of the inflection points was conducted using the “segmented” package in R based on a likelihood ratio test and bootstrap resampling. RCRI is frequently applied clinically to assess cardiac complication risk in noncardiac surgical patients. The predictive performance enhancement of the RCRI by the SHR was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, the area under the ROC curve (AUC), C-statistic, continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), alongside likelihood ratio tests to compare model fit improvements with the inclusion of the SHR, which was treated either as a continuous variable or as a categorical variable determined by inflection points identified using RCS. SHR was analyzed both as a categorical and continuous variable. C-statistics, continuous NRIs, and IDIs calculations utilized the “survIDINRI” package in R. The “survival ROC” package was employed for a time-dependent ROC curve analysis.

Feature selection was conducted using Boruta’s algorithm, which identifies key variables by comparing the Z value of each true feature with that of corresponding “shadow features.” Features with significantly higher Z values than shadow features were deemed “important” (green area), while those without significant differences were marked as “unimportant” (red area) [14]. SHR was also analyzed as a predictor variable within this framework. Selected variables were used to develop predictive models for perioperative MACE risk. The dataset was divided into training and validation sets in a 7:3 ratio. Various machine learning algorithms were applied, including Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), Decision Tree, Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Neural Network (NN), Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), selected based on their performance in similar prediction tasks. Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using grid search and random search methods to optimize model performance. The performance of each model was evaluated using ROC curves and corresponding AUC values. Clinical usefulness was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA), and calibration curves were generated to evaluate the accuracy of risk predictions.

Sensitivity analyses were also performed: First, patients with missing data were excluded to verify the robustness of the primary outcomes. Second, we performed propensity score matching for patients across different tertiles to conduct the primary analysis. All statistical tests were two-sided, with significance set at p < 0.05. Data analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 24.0) and R (version 4.3.2).

The correlation of the SHR with perioperative MACEs

Kaplan–Meier survival curves (Fig. 1) depicted a progressively increasing incidence of perioperative MACEs and cardiac death with higher SHR values. Patients in the highest tertile had a significantly greater risk of MACEs and cardiac death compared to those in tertiles 1 and 2 (all log-rank p < 0.001), underscoring the potential utility of SHR as a prognostic marker. When SHR was analyzed as a continuous variable, it emerged as a significant risk factor for perioperative MACEs across three models: the unadjusted Model 1 (HR, 1.97 [1.73, 2.25], p < 0.001), the partially adjusted Model 2 (HR, 1.94 [1.69, 2.22], p < 0.001), and the fully adjusted Model 3 (HR, 1.38 [1.18, 1.62], p < 0.001) (Table 2). Similarly, when SHR was categorized into tertiles, patients in the highest SHR tertile (tertile 3) also had a significantly higher risk of perioperative MACEs compared to those in the lowest tertile (all p < 0.001). The RCS analysis of MACEs (Fig. 2A and B) and cardiac death both (Fig. 2C and D) further revealed J-shaped associations between the SHR and risk, with inflection points occurring at 0.81 and 0.97, respectively. The incremental predictive value of SHR

Table 3; Fig. 4 demonstrated the improved predictive performance for perioperative MACEs with the integration of the SHR. Specifically, the inclusion of SHR significantly improved the predictive performance of the RCRI model for 30-day postoperative MACEs, increasing the AUC from 0.778 to 0.825 (Fig. 4C), equivalent to 1 additional high-risk patient being correctly reclassified for every 20 patients evaluated. Similarly, the baseline risk model showed an AUC increase from 0.786 to 0.818 (Fig. 4D), equivalent to 1 additional high-risk patient being correctly reclassified for every 25 patients evaluated. Time-dependent ROC analysis further highlighted this improvement, with the time-dependent AUC (iAUC) for the RCRI model combined with the SHR rising to 0.850, compared to 0.810 for the RCRI model alone (Fig. 4A). Similarly, the iAUC of the baseline risk model with the SHR was 0.845, significantly higher than 0.804 for the baseline model (Fig. 4B). In addition, Figure S2 compared the improved predictive performance of SHR in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients. The inclusion of SHR significantly enhanced the predictive performance of the RCRI model, increasing the AUC from 0.706 to 0.768 in diabetics, and from 0.782 to 0.825 in non-diabetic patients. Sensitivity analysis

The analysis using complete data demonstrated a gradual increase in perioperative MACE and cardiac death rates with increasing SHR tertiles (Figure S5). Elevated SHR was significantly associated with a higher risk of both perioperative MACE (Table S4).

In the propensity score-matched cohort, similar trends were observed. SHR continued to be significantly associated with an increased risk of perioperative MACE and cardiac death, reinforcing the findings (Table S5 and Table S6).

Discussion

This study examined the correlation and predictive value of SHR in assessing the risk of MACE and cardiac death in patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery. The results showed that SHR significantly impacted the occurrence of MACE within 30 days postoperatively, especially in high-risk or emergency surgeries. ROC analysis and machine learning models revealed that incorporating SHR improved predictive accuracy for cardiovascular risk. These findings suggest that SHR may be a key predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes during the perioperative period. The increase in MACE and cardiac mortality appears linked to the inflammatory response triggered by blood glucose fluctuations [21, 22]. Blood glucose variations, metabolic dysregulation, and oxidative stress activate inflammasomes (e.g., NLRP3) [23, 24], releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) and adipokines (leptin, resistin), leading to tissue damage, insulin resistance [25], systemic inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction [26], which elevate the risk of postoperative cardiovascular events.

We also observed a J-shaped relationship between SHR and both MACE and cardiac mortality. Specifically, when SHR exceeded 0.81, MACE risk rose, and when SHR surpassed 0.97, cardiac mortality increased significantly. Our analysis confirmed this relationship, which aligns with findings from a previous study on the association between SHR and short-term mortality risk in patients with acute myocardial infarction [27]. Similarly, a study of 5562 acute coronary syndrome patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) found a J-shaped or U-shaped relationship between SHR and adverse outcomes [28]. Previous study demonstrated optimizing SHR levels in non-cardiac surgical patients may improve postoperative outcomes [29, 30]. Therefore, for patients with preoperative SHR exceeding the threshold, physicians can tailor preoperative management based on SHR, optimize the timing of surgery, and implement preventive strategies, including enhanced perioperative glucose management and continuous glucose monitoring, to reduce the risk of MACEs, ultimately improving patient prognosis and quality of life [31–33].

Incorporating SHR into the RCRI or baseline models improved MACE prediction. While the RCRI is widely used in non-cardiac surgeries, it often lacks the precision needed for individualized risk assessment [34, 35]. The VISION cohort data, for example, showed that among patients aged 45 and older, 1 in 12 without traditional RCRI risk factors still developed cardiac complications after major non-cardiac surgery [36]. Many studies have explored the role of biomarkers in enhancing the predictive capacity of RCRI in non-cardiac surgery settings. The value of brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) for preoperative risk assessment and cardiac troponin I (cTnI) for detecting prognosis-related myocardial ischemia has been well established [37, 38]. Compared to traditional cardiac biomarkers, SHR may be more suitable as a preoperative screening tool due to its cost-effectiveness and universal applicability. For patients identified with elevated stress levels, additional monitoring with BNP and cTnI, alongside enhanced glucose management, could help control blood glucose within an acceptable range before surgery and reduce the risk of perioperative cardiovascular events. SHR offers a promising alternative for personalized risk assessment, enabling the identification of high-risk patients who might otherwise be overlooked [39].

We have confirmed that the preoperative initial SHR is a valuable predictor of perioperative cardiovascular events. Nevertheless, SHR fluctuates throughout the perioperative period (including intraoperatively and postoperatively). Over time, continuous monitoring of SHR may more accurately capture changes in risk compared to the initial SHR. However, in current clinical practice, the real-time acquisition of glucose data and the necessary training pose challenges to the implementation of dynamic SHR monitoring. Given its simplicity and effectiveness, the initial SHR value is a useful tool for identifying high-risk patients for perioperative cardiovascular events, and facilitates continuous SHR monitoring and timely interventions for these patients [20]. Moreover, more than 210 million people worldwide have undiagnosed potential diabetes, and about 3% of surgical patients are found to have undiagnosed diabetes [9]. These patients often present with long-term glucose abnormalities and insulin resistance, leading to elevated HbA1c levels. Their ABG tend to follow the same trend as HbA1c. As a result, their SHR values are generally lower compared to those with stress-induced hyperglycemia. Using SHR and HbA1c together is beneficial in distinguishing between stress-induced hyperglycemia and undiagnosed diabetes, allowing for more targeted treatments.

limitations of the study

Although this study provides valuable insights, several limitations should be noted. First, the observational design limits our ability to establish a clear causal relationship between SHR and MACE. Further prospective or mechanistic studies are needed to explore the underlying mechanisms. Second, our analysis focused on baseline SHR values at admission, without accounting for dynamic changes in SHR during the perioperative period, which warrants further investigation. While we used machine learning for prediction, validation was based on internal data only. External or multicenter validation is recommended to improve generalizability. Finally, the study’s regional context—based on South Korean data—limits its broader applicability. Ethnic, genetic, and lifestyle factors may influence SHR’s predictive value, necessitating large-scale, multicenter studies in diverse populations to assess its broader relevance.

Conclusion

SHR has been significantly implicated as a predictive factor for perioperative major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in non-cardiac surgery patients. The integration of SHR into existing risk prediction models, notably the Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI), markedly improved their ability to forecast perioperative MACEs. Additionally, machine learning algorithms have validated SHR as a pivotal predictor, facilitating the construction of robust postoperative risk assessment models. Importantly, future research endeavors should investigate the potential for SHR management optimization to mitigate perioperative MACEs in this patient population.
