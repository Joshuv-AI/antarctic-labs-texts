Feasibility of vault configuration of the Neuroform Atlas stent in the treatment of bifurcation intracranial aneurysm: clinical observation and hemodynamic simulation
Authors: Bin Lv, Xiangyuan Zhang, Jiawen Zhu, Rong Zou, Xiangyu Cao, Zhihua Du, Xinfeng Liu, Rongju Zhang, Xiaochang Leng, Jens Fiehler, Adnan H Siddiqui, Hua Jin, Zhijun Liu, Jianping Xiang, Jun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoil prolapse and stent-induced vascular deformation during single stent-assisted coiling (SSAC) of bifurcation intracranial aneurysms (BIAs) may compromise branch vessel patency. The vault configuration achieved via the Neuroform Atlas stent (NAS) is a clinically observed deployment pattern that may contribute to aneurysm neck coverage while maintaining branch vessel patency; however, its morphological and hemodynamic characteristics remain insufficiently characterized. This study aimed to provide a preliminary quantitative description of vault configuration.MethodsThis retrospective study included 35 BIAs treated with single NAS-assisted coiling. Vascular morphological characteristics were evaluated via digital subtraction angiography (DSA) at pretreatment, posttreatment, and follow-up time points. In addition, patient-specific virtual stenting and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations were performed in six cases (with seven protected branch vessels) for quantitative characterization of the branch-protective efficacy conferred by the vault.ResultsAll branch vessels protected by the vault configuration remained patent during follow-up. No significant differences were observed in vessel diameters or vascular angles across pretreatment, posttreatment, or follow-up imaging (all P values >0.05). Hemodynamic analysis showed that branch-flow responses varied across anatomical configurations. In exploratory simulations, six of seven simulated branches demonstrated smaller flow reductions in the vault models than in the corresponding no-vault models, with a mean branch-flow reduction 5.12% lower than that observed in the no-vault models; however, the magnitude of the differences varied considerably across cases.ConclusionsThe vault configuration of the Neuroform Atlas appears to be a technically feasible deployment pattern in selected cases and was associated with preserved branch-vessel patency in this cohort. Virtual deployment and CFD analyses suggested that vault-associated flow alterations may vary according to local vascular anatomy. However, the observed hemodynamic findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and further validation in larger cohorts is needed.Keywords: Neuroform Atlas stent (NAS), vault configuration, bifurcation intracranial aneurysms (BIAs), hemodynamic analysis

Introduction

Intracranial aneurysm rupture remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with cerebrovascular disease. Bifurcation intracranial aneurysms (BIAs) are particularly challenging to treat endovascularly because effective aneurysm occlusion must be achieved while the patency of the incorporated branch vessels is preserved (1).

Single stent-assisted coiling (SSAC) is commonly used for the treatment of BIAs. However, coil prolapse into the parent or branch vessels may occur during embolization, potentially compromising branch flow. Although double stent-assisted coiling can provide stronger neck coverage and reduce coil herniation risk, the technique is clinically complex and is associated with increased metal density at the overlapping stent segments, which may increase the likelihood of thromboembolic events (2-5).

The Neuroform Atlas Stent System (NAS; Stryker Neurovascular, Fremont, CA, USA) is an open-cell, laser-cut, self-expanding stent characterized by good vessel conformability and a relatively low radial force. These structural properties allow the device to adapt closely to complex vascular geometries (6-9). In selected anatomical configurations, deployment of the NAS may permit partial stent strut herniation into the aneurysm neck region, forming a characteristic construct known as the vault deployment pattern. Conceptually, the vault configuration leveraging the open-cell design to allow controlled strut herniation shares similarities with “shelf” or “elbow” neck protection techniques in promoting aneurysm neck coverage.

Analogous deployment strategies have been described in previous case reports and technique notes. However, this literature is primarily focused on procedural feasibility and angiographic observations, while quantitative characterization of the associated morphological and hemodynamic features remains limited (6,10). One major challenge in studying vault deployment is that the deployed stent architecture cannot be readily visualized via conventional angiographic imaging. As a result, the spatial relationship between the stent, aneurysm neck, and incorporated branch vessels is often inferred indirectly, limiting detailed mechanistic evaluation. Computational approaches, including patient-specific virtual stenting and computational fluid dynamics (CFD), may provide a framework for investigating device-vessel interactions and flow alterations that are otherwise difficult to assess in vivo.

Thus, to further characterize this clinically observed deployment pattern—the vault configuration—we retrospectively analyzed a single-center cohort of patients with BIAs treated with NAS-assisted single coiling. In addition to clinical and morphological assessment, patient-specific virtual stenting and CFD simulations were performed in a subset of cases to examine the potential morphological and hemodynamic characteristics associated with vault deployment.

The objective of this study was not to determine a comparative effectiveness or establish clinical superiority over alternative treatment strategies; rather, the purpose of this work was to generate a preliminary quantitative characterization of the vault configuration and to ascertain whether this deployment pattern may be associated with measurable, anatomy-dependent alterations in local vascular geometry and flow distribution. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0958/rc).

Methods

Patient population

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Chinese PLA General Hospital (approval No. S2022-357-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

We retrospectively reviewed all consecutive patients who underwent endovascular treatment for BIAs from July 2022 to September 2024 at the Department of Neurology of the Chinese PLA General Hospital. During this period, multiple endovascular strategies were available for the treatment of BIAs. Treatment decisions were made by a multidisciplinary neurovascular team based on aneurysm morphology, branch incorporation patterns, vascular anatomy, and operator judgment. Consequently, the cohort represents patients in whom NAS-assisted SSAC with vault deployment was considered technically feasible according to routine clinical practice. The study cohort was then screened according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were (I) branch-incorporated BIAs; (II) treatment with single NAS-assisted coiling; and (III) completion of at least 6 months of angiographic follow-up. Meanwhile, the exclusion criteria were (I) concomitant use of other stents or flow diverters; (II) nonstandard postoperative antiplatelet therapy; and (III) incomplete clinical data.

After these criteria were applied, 35 patients were included in the final analysis. Clinical data including demographic characteristics, procedural details, imaging data, and follow-up outcomes were collected. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) results were independently assessed by three experienced neurointerventionalists, and discrepancies were resolved via discussion.

Perioperative treatment details

All procedures were performed by senior operators. Systemic heparinization was initiated with a bolus of 50 U/kg followed by continuous infusion at 1,250 U/h. A 6-F long sheath (Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA) was introduced into the internal carotid artery, followed by advancement of a 6-F Navien intermediate catheter (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) for distal support. A working projection was selected via three-dimensional rotational angiography to evaluate aneurysm morphology and parent vessel dimensions. An SL-10 microcatheter (Stryker Neurovascular, Fremont, CA, USA) and an Echelon-10 microcatheter (Medtronic) were navigated into the distal parent artery and aneurysm sac, respectively, through use of a Synchro-14 guidewire (Stryker Neurovascular). The NAS was subsequently deployed via the SL-10 microcatheter. In most cases, the selected stent diameter was slightly larger than the mean diameter of the parent artery to obtain sufficient efficacy of vault configuration.

Perioperative antiplatelet management differed according to rupture status. Patients with unruptured aneurysms received dual antiplatelet therapy (clopidogrel 75 mg/day and aspirin 100 mg/day) for 5 days before the procedure. A routine thromboelastogram was suggested to evaluate the actual effectiveness of antiplatelet agents before the surgery. For ruptured aneurysms without preoperative antiplatelet preparation, tirofiban (0.1 mL/kg) was administered intravenously (4–5 mL/h) immediately after stent deployment and maintained for 24–48 hours. After stent implantation, the patients were also required to undergo 3 months of dual antiplatelet therapy and an additional 6 months of aspirin monotherapy.

Outcomes and complications

DSA was performed immediately after the procedure and at 6-month follow-up to assess aneurysm occlusion status and vessel patency. Angiographic results were independently evaluated by at least two experienced neurointerventionalists according to the Raymond-Roy occlusion classification (RRC). Neurological outcomes were assessed as per the modified Rankin Scale. Significant parent artery stenosis was defined as ≥50% luminal stenosis (11).

Morphological measurement

To evaluate vascular morphological changes following NAS deployment, vessel angles and diameters of incorporated branches were measured on pretreatment, posttreatment, and follow-up two-dimensional DSA images (12). The main vessel was defined as the artery supplying the target BIA. The vessel in which the distal end of the NAS was deployed was defined as the stent branch. The stent angle (α) was defined as the two-dimensional angle between the main vessel and the stent branch. The branch angle (β) was defined as the angle between the main vessel and each incorporated side branch.

Due to limitations in accurately measuring three-dimensional vessel diameters from DSA images, two-dimensional vessel widths were used as surrogate descriptors. The morphological analysis was intended to evaluate gross geometric changes rather than precise three-dimensional anatomical quantification. Dm, Ds, and Dt represented the diameters of the main vessel, stent branch, and target side branches, respectively. First, a line segment was drawn across the main vessel at the junction where it entered the aneurysm sac to measure Dm. A perpendicular line (Lm) was then constructed through the midpoint of Dm. Ds and Dt were measured in the same manner, and their corresponding perpendicular lines (Ls and Lt) were constructed through the respective midpoints. The vascular angle α was measured as the angle between Lm and Ls, and angle β was measured as the angle between Lm and Lt (Figure 1).

Virtual deployment and hemodynamic models

Patient-specific BIA geometries were reconstructed from three-dimensional rotational angiography datasets (13). The NAS geometry was generated based on manufacturer specifications. Virtual stent and coil deployment were simulated according to a previously validated high-fidelity virtual stenting technique (14). The proximal and distal landing zones were determined via multi-angle DSA. The reconstructed vault configuration was qualitatively compared with Vaso-CT imaging when available. The purpose of this comparison was to ensure geometric plausibility rather than to serve as formal quantitative validation.

For hemodynamic simulations, six cases were selected from the study cohort based on the availability of high-quality three-dimensional angiographic datasets. Although retrospectively selected, these cases encompassed a range of anatomical configurations observed within the cohort. To assess the potential influence of vault configuration on branch hemodynamics, two models reflecting deployment conditions were used: (I) a NAS-treated model corresponding to clinical treatment and (II) a no-vault comparison model generated by modifying the virtual stent configuration to eliminate vault protrusion while preserving vascular geometry, stent size, and coil distribution. This no-vault model was constructed as an internally controlled mechanistic reference condition for exploring the potential hemodynamic influence of vault protrusion rather than to represent a clinically observed alternative deployment strategy or a direct comparator for clinical decision-making. For each case, three models were analyzed: pretreated geometry, an NAS-treated vault model, and a no-vault comparison model.

Hemodynamic simulations were conducted by solving the steady-state Navier-Stokes equations under the assumptions of homogeneous, incompressible, laminar, Newtonian blood flow (density 1,056 kg/m3; dynamic viscosity 0.0035 kg/m·s) (15). Vessel walls were assumed to be rigid and to have no-slip boundary conditions. Zero gauge pressure boundary conditions were uniformly applied at outlets across all comparative models (16). These assumptions were adopted to facilitate internally consistent comparisons among deployment configurations and should be interpreted as simplifications of physiological conditions rather than patient-specific representations of in vivo hemodynamics. Mesh resolution was refined for stent (0.03 mm), coil (0.1 mm), and vascular domains (0.16 mm).

All simulations were performed with the computational angiography-based simulation technology suite AneuPlan (ArteryFlow, Hangzhou, China) (17).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna) and RStudio software (Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA, USA). Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Longitudinal changes in morphological parameters across pretreatment, posttreatment, and follow-up time points were evaluated via repeated-measures analysis of variance with Greenhouse-Geisser correction. Given the exploratory nature of the study and the limited sample size, statistical analyses were restricted to the clinical and morphological datasets. Computational simulation results were summarized descriptively without formal hypothesis testing. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Technical procedural success was achieved in all cases (100%). Immediate postprocedural angiography demonstrated RRC grades of I in 8 (22.9%) patients, II in 4 (11.4%) patients, and III in 23 (65.7%) patients. Perioperative complications were observed in 2 (5.7%) cases. One patient developed intra-stent thrombosis during the coiling procedure and was successfully treated with urgent tirofiban administration, after which satisfactory branch patency was restored. The other event involved stent migration, which did not result in clinical symptoms or angiographic deterioration.

At the 6-month angiographic follow-up, complete aneurysm obliteration (RRC grade I) was observed in 26 (74.3%) patients, aneurysm neck remnant (RRC grade II) in 3 (8.6%) patients, and residual aneurysm sac (RRC grade III) in the remaining 6 (17.1%) patients. Deterioration in RRC grade was observed in 6 (17.1%) patients, among whom 3 (8.6%) underwent retreatment. Three (8.6%) patients had >50% parent artery stenosis (51.6%, 62.3%, and 72.3%, respectively) without clinical symptoms. These individuals were recommended to undergo prolonged follow-up surveillance. Notably, all branch vessels intended to be protected by the vault configuration remained patent during follow-up observation (Table 1).

Notably, the degree of branch-flow preservation was not uniform across cases. In some branches, the vault configuration was associated with substantially smaller flow reductions than were the corresponding no-vault models, whereas in others, only minor differences were observed. This variability suggests that the hemodynamic influence of vault deployment is strongly dependent on local vascular anatomy and does not represent a uniform effect across all bifurcation configurations.

Furthermore, in the two wide-neck (≥4 mm) aneurysms included in the cohort (18), larger neck inflow areas were accompanied by higher relative inflow and inflow concentration index values compared with the narrow-neck aneurysms. These observations suggest that aneurysm neck morphology may contribute to differences in baseline flow environments and may therefore influence how vault deployment affects local hemodynamics.

Discussion

BIAs remain technically challenging because of the risks of coil herniation and branch-vessel compromise. Various endovascular strategies have been proposed to address these challenges. Techniques such as the barrel configuration can increase metal coverage at the aneurysm neck to facilitate occlusion (19) but may carry risks of stent migration or thrombotic complications (20). Y-stent-assisted coiling has yielded favorable angiographic occlusion rates (21), yet the technique can be technically demanding due to the need for catheterization of the second branch and the potential for incomplete expansion at the overlapping segments (22). Other strategies, including waffle-cone deployment (23) or horizontal stenting (24), have also been described, although these approaches may involve complex manipulations or potential recurrence related to coil instability.

In this context, the vault configuration achieved with the NAS represents a clinically observed deployment pattern that may occur under selected anatomical conditions. Owing to the open-cell design of the device, this configuration may modify how the deployed stent interacts with the aneurysm neck and incorporated branch vessels; however, its morphological and hemodynamic characteristics remain incompletely characterized (25,26).Recently, virtual deployment and CFD approaches have increasingly been used to investigate device-vessel interactions that cannot be directly visualized with conventional angiography. Previous studies have applied these methods to flow diverters (27), stent-assisted coiling (28-30), and aneurysm hemodynamics. In our study, this framework was extended to NAS-assisted coiling in bifurcation aneurysms, providing a preliminary means of visualizing and quantitatively characterizing a clinically observed deployment pattern that is otherwise difficult to evaluate in vivo. Although these observations remain exploratory, they illustrate how patient-specific virtual modeling may help generate mechanistic hypotheses regarding vault deployment and its potential hemodynamic implications.

Building on this framework, we combined clinical observation, patient-specific virtual stenting, and CFD simulations to explore the feasibility, morphology, and hemodynamic characteristics associated with vault deployment. Importantly, the objective of this work was not to compare vault deployment with alternative treatment strategies or to establish clinical superiority but rather to provide a preliminary quantitative framework for investigating a deployment phenomenon that is typically assessed qualitatively in routine practice. Within the simulated cohort, vault deployment was generally associated with smaller branch-flow reductions than were those observed in the corresponding no-vault comparison models. However, the magnitude of these differences varied considerably across cases, and one branch exhibited the opposite tendency. These findings suggest that the hemodynamic influence of vault deployment is not uniform but appears to depend on the underlying vascular anatomy. Because the no-vault models were artificially constructed and do not represent clinically observed alternative treatments, the CFD findings should be interpreted as mechanistic observations intended to generate hypotheses rather than as evidence of clinical benefit.

The expanded CFD analysis further illustrated the anatomical heterogeneity of the simulated cohort. For example, the wide-neck aneurysms included in this series exhibited larger neck inflow areas and higher inflow concentration indices than did the narrow-neck aneurysms. In addition, the degree of branch-flow preservation varied across anatomical configurations. Although the limited number of cases precluded formal subgroup analyses, these observations suggest that anatomical factors may contribute to the variability of vault-related flow alterations. Future studies with larger simulation cohorts will be required to determine whether reproducible hemodynamic patterns exist across different anatomical subgroups.

The morphological analysis demonstrated only limited changes in the measured vessel diameters and angular parameters following treatment. These findings may reflect the conformability of the NAS to bifurcation anatomy. However, because the measurements were derived from two-dimensional angiographic projections, subtle three-dimensional geometric changes might not have been captured. Therefore, the morphological findings should be interpreted primarily as descriptive observations that accompany the hemodynamic analyses rather than as conclusive evidence regarding the geometric effects of vault deployment or their clinical relevance.

Strengths and limitations

The primary strength of this study lies in the integration of clinical observation, patient-specific virtual stenting, and CFD analysis to investigate a deployment phenomenon that is difficult to characterize with conventional imaging alone. Rather than evaluating treatment effectiveness, the study provides a preliminary quantitative description of vault deployment and its potential hemodynamic implications.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we employed a retrospective, single-center design, and treatment selection was determined by aneurysm morphology, vascular anatomy, and operator judgment. Consequently, the cohort reflects cases considered anatomically suitable for NAS-assisted vault deployment rather than an unselected BIA population, introducing an inherent risk of selection bias.

Second, the study did not include a comparator cohort treated with conventional stent-assisted coiling, alternative stent configurations, or other endovascular strategies. Therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence that vault deployment is superior to other treatment approaches. Rather, the study was designed to characterize the feasibility and hemodynamic features of a specific deployment configuration observed in clinical practice.

Third, the clinical cohort was relatively small, which might have limited the statistical power for detecting subtle longitudinal morphological changes, particularly for parameters approaching statistical significance. Consequently, the absence of statistically significant differences should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that NAS deployment results in no geometric alteration. Larger prospective studies with greater statistical power are required to validate these observations and more precisely characterize treatment-related morphological changes. Similarly, the CFD analysis was limited to seven protected branches and was therefore intended to provide exploratory mechanistic observations rather than support statistical inference or establish definitive hemodynamic relationships.

Fourth, the no-vault comparator was an artificially constructed reference model and does not represent a clinically observed treatment strategy. Accordingly, the CFD findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating observations and not as direct evidence of clinical benefit.

Fifth, the simulations were performed under several simplifying assumptions. These assumptions may influence the absolute magnitude of simulated flow parameters and may not fully reproduce patient-specific physiological hemodynamics. Nevertheless, because identical assumptions were applied across comparative models within each case, the relative differences observed between the vault and no-vault configurations may still provide useful information regarding the relevant mechanisms.

Finally, all protected branches remained patent during follow-up, and no branch-occlusion events were observed. Therefore, we could not determine whether the modest hemodynamic differences identified by CFD are associated with clinically meaningful outcomes such as branch stenosis, ischemic complications, or long-term vessel patency. Future prospective studies incorporating larger cohorts, longer follow-up durations, and more clinically diverse outcomes are required to further determine the potential clinical significance of vault deployment.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort, the vault configuration of the NAS was successfully deployed in selected patients with BIAs and was associated with preserved branch-vessel patency during follow-up. Patient-specific virtual stenting and CFD modeling provided a means of visualizing and quantitatively characterizing this clinically observed deployment pattern. The simulation findings suggested that vault deployment may influence local flow distribution in an anatomy-dependent manner; however, these observations were derived from a small number of exploratory models and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating only. Further studies with larger cohorts and more comprehensive hemodynamic evaluation are required to clarify the potential clinical relevance of these findings.
