Abstract
Consider a point-to-point communication system in which the transmitter holds a binary message of length m m and transmits a corresponding codeword of length n n . The receiver’s goal is to recover a Boolean function of that message, where the function is unknown to the transmitter, but chosen from a known class ℱ \mathcal{F} . We are interested in the asymptotic relationship of m m and n n : given n n , how large can m m be (asymptotically), such that the value of the Boolean function can be recovered reliably? This problem generalizes the identification-via-channels framework introduced by Ahlswede and Dueck. We formulate the notion of computation capacity, and derive achievability and converse results for selected classes of functions ℱ \mathcal{F} , characterized by the Hamming weight of functions. Our obtained results are tight in the sense of the scaling behavior for all cases of ℱ \mathcal{F} considered in the paper.



I
Introduction

We consider the problem of computing Boolean functions over noisy channels. To motivate the problem, consider the following simple example of a Battery Management System in an electrical vehicle, where a sensing unit reports a binary status vector
{\bf b}=({\bf b}_{1},{\bf b}_{2},{\bf b}_{3})\in\{0,1\}^{3}
,
with
{\bf b}_{1}
,
{\bf b}_{2}
, and
{\bf b}_{3}
indicating over–voltage, under–voltage, and
over–temperature conditions of the battery, respectively. Depending on the operational objective, the controller does not require the full vector
{\bf b}
, but instead only a Boolean function of
{\bf b}
, transmitted through a possibly noisy channel. For example, in
driving mode
the controller needs to evaluate

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{drive}}({\bf b}):={\bf b}_{1}\lor{\bf b}_{3},

where
0
means “false”,
1
means “true”, and
\lor
is the logical OR operator. The function
f_{\mathrm{drive}}
equals
1
(signals an alarm) whenever either an over–voltage (
{\bf b}_{1}=1
) or an over–temperature (
{\bf b}_{3}=1
) event occurs.
In
charging mode
, the controller must determine whether charging is not permissible,
represented by

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{charge}}({\bf b}):={\bf b}_{1}\lor({\bf b}_{2}\land{\bf b}_{3}),

where
\land
is the logical AND operator. The function
f_{\mathrm{charge}}
equals
1
(signals an alarm) when either over–voltage
({\bf b}_{1}=1)
, or the abnormal
“under–voltage and hot” (
{\bf b}_{2}=1
and
{\bf b}_{3}=1
) condition occurs.

The Boolean Function Computation (BFC) problem is an abstraction of this example, where the receiver aims to recover one of several distinct Boolean functions of the same underlying binary sequence. To be precise, consider a point-to-point communication system where the transmitter chooses a message represented by binary sequences of length
m
, and the decoder chooses a Boolean function
f:\{0,1\}^{m}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
from a family
\mathcal{F}
. Here we assume that the set
\mathcal{F}
is known to both the encoder and the decoder, but the function
f
(chosen by the decoder) is unknown to the encoder. Let
i\in\{0,1\}^{m}
denote the message at the transmitter, which is encoded to a codeword
X^{n}
of length
n
and is transmitter through a noisy channel. With the channel output
Y^{n}
, the decoder is required to reliably decide if
f(i)=0
or
f(i)=1
.

The BFC problem is a generalization of the
identification via channel
problem studied in a seminal paper by Ahlswede and Dueck
[
2
]
. The goal of identification is for the decoder to decide if the transmitted message is
a particular one
. It is easy to see that the identification problem is equivalent to an instance of BFC problem with a suitable choice of function class
\mathcal{F}
(details in Section
II-A
). After the appearance of
[
2
]
(and
[
1
]
where feedback is considered), many works have extend the identification problem to other set-ups. For point-to-point channels, a line of more recent work including
[
12
]

[
7
]
investigate the identification problem when the encoders are restricted to a deterministic function of the message, for which the original work
[
2
]
only gives a very brief treatment. Explicit code constructions for identification are investigated in
[
13
]

[
3
]
and further improved in
[

]
and
[
10
]
. Extending the results to multi-user scenarios are considered in
[
5
]
for multiple-access channels and
[
6
]
for broadcast channels. The readers are referred to the recent survey
[
14
]
for a more comprehensive summary of known results.

Particularly relevant to our work is
[
5
]
, where a general information transfer problem is formulated. While a specialization of this model is equivalent to the Boolean function computation problem, only partial results are obtained in
[
5
]
. We make the connection and comparison explicit in Section

.

The main contribution of this paper is to characterize the possible scaling law of
m
and
n
for the BFC problem. We show that the scaling behavior heavily depends on the possible functions in
\mathcal{F}
the receiver aims to decode. Specifically, we consider
\mathcal{F}
consisting of functions
f
that have a
Hamming weight
equal or smaller than a certain threshold (denoted by
S
), where the Hamming weight is defined to be the cardinality of the pre-image of
1
under
f
. With this characterization, we are able to give explicit results on the scaling behavior of
m
with respect to
n
for different values of
S
. Roughly speaking,
m
scales exponentially with
n
if
S
is small (as in the identification problem, which corresponds to
S=1
), while it scales linear with
n
when
S
is large (the same behavior as in Shannon’s transmission problem). When varying
S
from large to small values,
m
can scale linearly, quasi-linearly, polynomially, sub-exponentially, and exponentially with
n
, as shown in Table
I
. These results are obtained by establishing the achievability and converse results on the computation rate in Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
, respectively. While the achievability and converse results coincide exactly only for special cases, they are tight in the sense of the scaling behavior for all cases of
\mathcal{F}
considered in this paper.

Given a positive integer
M
, we use
[\![M]\!]
to denote the set of integers
\{1,\ldots,M\}
. For a given set
\mathcal{X}
, we use
|\mathcal{X}|
to denote the cardinality of the set.

II
Problem formulation

We consider a channel with the input alphabet
\mathcal{X}
and the output alphabet
\mathcal{Y}
. A channel
W(\cdot|x^{n})
is viewed as a conditional distribution (transition kernel) over
\mathcal{Y}^{n}
, given the input codeword
x^{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
. We use
\mathcal{F}_{m}
to denote the set of Boolean functions of
m
inputs,
\mathcal{F}_{m}:=\{f:\{0,1\}^{m}\rightarrow\{0,1\}\}
. Consider the communication problem where a
binary
message, represented by a binary sequence of length
m
, is chosen and transmitted (after encoding) by the transmitter. A Boolean function (unknown to the transmitter) is chosen by the receiver, and the goal is to reliably recover its function value of the transmitted message. The notion of Boolean function computation (BFC) code is formally defined as below.

Definition 1
(Boolean function computation (BFC) code)

Let
m,n
positive natural numbers. An
(n,m,\mathcal{F},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
Boolean function computation (BFC) code consists of encoders
(Q_{i})_{i\in\{0,1\}^{m}}
and decoding sets
(D_{j})_{j\in[\![|\mathcal{F}|]\!]}
, such that

•

\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{m}
is a set of Boolean functions with
m
inputs

•

Q_{i}
is a probability distribution on
\mathcal{X}^{n}
for all
i\in\{0,1\}^{m}

•

D_{j}\subset\mathcal{Y}^{n}
for all
j\in[\![|\mathcal{F}|]\!]

•

false negative error:
{\bf 1}_{f_{j}(i)=1}Q_{i}W(D_{j}^{c})\leq\lambda_{1}
for all
i,j

•

false positive error:
{\bf 1}_{f_{j}(i)=0}Q_{i}W(D_{j})\leq\lambda_{2}
for all
i,j

where we define
QW(D):=\int W(D|x)Q(dx)
.

Let
f^{-1}[1]
denote the preimage of
1
under
f
. The cardinality of the the preimage
f^{-1}[1]
is called the Hamming weight of the Boolean function
f
. We consider the class of
constant weight Boolean function
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(S):=\{f\in\mathcal{F}_{m}:|f^{-1}[1]|=S\}

where
S
can take integer values between
0
and
2^{m}
, and

|\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(S)|={2^{m}\choose S}
.
Similarly, we define the set of functions whose Hamming weight is
smaller or equal to

S
, and
larger or equal to

S
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(\leq S):=\{f\in\mathcal{F}_{m}:|f^{-1}[1]|\leq S\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(\geq S):=\{f\in\mathcal{F}_{m}:|f^{-1}[1]|\geq S\}

As we will see in the sequel, depending on the relationship between
S
and
m
, the number of messages that can be reliably computed via the channel exhibits different scaling behaviour with respect to
n
. To this end, we define the achievable computation rate as follows.

Definition 2
(Achievable computation rate)

Given a scaling function
L:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
and a set of Boolean functions
\mathcal{F}
, we say
R
is an achievable computation rate for
\mathcal{F}
with the scaling function
L
, if for all
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
, all
\gamma>0
and all sufficiently large
n
, there exists an
(m,n,\mathcal{F},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
BFC code satisfying

\displaystyle m>L(R-\gamma,n).

To relate this to more familiar notions, the choice
L(R,n)=Rn
corresponds to the case when
m
scales linearly with
n
(as in the classical transmission problem where the number of messages
2^{m}
scales as
2^{n}
). The choice
L(R,n)=2^{Rn}
corresponds to the case when
m
scales exponentially with
n
(as in the identification problem). We will see other scaling functions in Theorem
1
and
2
. We point out that the scaling behaviour of
m
(w.r.t
n
) is determined by the
form
of the scaling function, where as the rate
R
is the coefficient at the front of
n
in the scaling function. For example,
L(R,n)=Rn,L(R,n)=Rn^{2}
and
L(R,n)=2^{Rn}
indicates that
m
scales linearly, quadratically, and exponentially with
n
, respectively.

Definition 3
(Computation capacity)

For a given scaling function
L
and a set of functions
\mathcal{F}
, the supremum of the achievable computation rate for
\mathcal{F}
, denoted by
C_{\text{BFC}}
, is called the (Boolean function) computation capacity of the channel for
\mathcal{F}
with the scaling function
L
.

Before presenting the main results, we first give examples of the function class
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(S)
for different choices of
S
, and comment on connections to semantic evaluations in propositional logic, and other existing problem formulations in the literature.

II-A

Examples:
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(S)
and
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(\leq S)
for different
S

In this subsection, we use the notation
{\bf b}=({\bf b}_{1},{\bf b}_{2},\ldots,{\bf b}_{m})
where each
{\bf b}_{i}
is a Boolean variable.

Example 1
(
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(c)
)

Let
c
be a constant not depending on
m
. The function in the class
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(c)
evaluates to
1
only if the input message
{\bf b}
comes from a set of size
c
. A special case is
c=1
, where the functions in the class
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(1)
can be expressed explicitly as

\displaystyle f_{j}^{id}({\bf b}):=\prod_{i\in A_{j}}{\bf b}_{i}\cdot\prod_{i\in A_{j}^{c}}(1-{\bf b}_{i})

(1)

for all subsets
A_{j}\subseteq[\![m]\!],j=1,\ldots,2^{m}
. Computing
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(1)
is equivalent to the identification via channel problem
[
2
]
(more details in Section
II-C
). Indeed,
f_{j}^{id}({\bf b})=1
if and only if
{\bf b}
is the unique message that corresponds to the set
A_{j}
(and there are in total
2^{m}
of them).

Example 2
(
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(\leq cm^{\beta})
)

Let
c
and
\beta>0
be some constant not depending on
m
. If we ask the question: “are there exactly
\beta
inputs equal to
1
”? This question can be formulated by the Boolean function

\displaystyle h_{\beta}({\bf b}):={\bf 1}_{\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\bf b}_{i}=\beta}

The weight of this function is
{m\choose\beta}
which is upper and lower bounded as
\frac{m^{\beta}}{\beta^{\beta}}\leq{m\choose\beta}\leq\frac{m^{\beta}}{\beta!}
. Hence this function belong to the class
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(\leq cm^{\beta})
for the choice
c=1/\beta!
. Similarly, the question “are there at most
\beta
input equal to
1
” corresponds to the Boolean function

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{\beta}({\bf b}):={\bf 1}_{\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\bf b}_{i}\leq\beta}

These functions are known as the threshold function, whose weight is upper bounded as
\sum_{i=1}^{\beta}{m\choose i}\leq(\frac{e}{\beta})^{\beta}m^{\beta}
. They belong to the set
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(\leq cm^{\beta})
with
c=(\frac{e}{\beta})^{\beta}
.

Example 3
(
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(c2^{\gamma m})
)

Let
c
and
\gamma\in(0,1]
be some constant not depending on
m
. If we want to identify the
t
-th bit of the message, we can use the function

\displaystyle f_{t}^{bit}({\bf b}):={\bf b}_{t}

(2)

It is easy to see that the set of functions
f_{t}^{bit},t=1,\ldots,m
belongs to the class
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(\frac{1}{2}2^{m})
. As another example, consider the function

\displaystyle f_{S_{k}}^{\text{AND}}({\bf b}):=\prod_{i\in S_{k}}{\bf b}_{i}

where
S_{k}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}
with cardinality
k
. This function performs the logic AND operation on a subset (of size
k
) of inputs. The weight of this class of function is
2^{m-k}
. When
k\geq(1-\gamma)m
, they belong to the class
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(\leq 2^{\gamma m})
.

Example 4
(ranking)

Let
int:\{0,1\}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
be the function that maps a binary sequence to an integer, where the sequence represents the binary expansion of that integer. In other words

\displaystyle int({\bf b}):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}2^{m-i}

(3)

Then we can define the ranking function

\displaystyle f_{r}^{\text{rank}}({\bf b}):={\bf 1}_{int({\bf b})\leq r}

(4)

for
r=0,\ldots,2^{m}-1
. It is clear that the set of functions
f_{r}^{\text{rank}}
belong to the set
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(r+1)
.

II-B

Formulae in propositional logic

The notion
semantic communication
roughly refers to the communication problems where only the “meaning”, or semantics of messages is to be communicated, rather than the entirety of the message. Semantics exist in the context of languages, with
propositional logic
being perhaps the simplest form, where there are only two semantic values,
\mathsf{True}
(denoted by
\mathsf{T}
) and
\mathsf{False}
(denoted by
\mathsf{F}
). In this subsection we comment on the connection between Boolean function computation and the semantic evaluation in propositional logic.

In the setup of propositional logic
[
8
]
,
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}
denotes a finite set of
propositional atoms
. The set of
well-formed formulae

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P})
is defined recursively as the smallest set of finitely long tuples of elements of
\mathcal{P}\cup\{\neg,\lor,\land,(,)\}
with the properties:

•

for every
p\in\mathcal{P}
, we have
p\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P})
,

•

if
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P})
, then
\neg(\varphi)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P})
,

•

if
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P})
and
\psi\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P})
, then
(\varphi)\lor(\psi)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P})
and
(\varphi)\land(\psi)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P})
,

where
p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
, and
\neg
,
\land
,
\lor
are the logical connectives
negation
,
conjunction
, and
disjunction
. For convenience, we do not always write out all the parentheses of every formula (when the order of operations is clear by common conventions), and we also define the connectives

•

\varphi\to\psi:=\neg\varphi\lor\psi

(implication)

•

\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi:=(\varphi\land\psi)\lor(\neg\varphi\land\neg\psi)

(equivalence)

•

\varphi\oplus\psi:=(\varphi\land\neg\psi)\lor(\neg\varphi\land\psi)

(exclusive or)
.

A
truth assignment
is a function
\tau:\mathcal{P}\to\{\mathsf{T},\mathsf{F}\}
assigning a truth value to each propositional atom.
The
semantics
of formulae is given by the evaluation map
v_{\tau}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P})\to\{\mathsf{T},\mathsf{F}\}
, defined recursively via

\displaystyle v_{\tau}(p_{i})

\displaystyle=\tau(p_{i})

\displaystyle v_{\tau}(\neg\varphi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathsf{T}\quad\text{if }v_{\tau}(\varphi)=\mathsf{T}\\
\mathsf{F}\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle v_{\tau}(\varphi\land\psi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathsf{T}\quad\text{if }v_{\tau}(\varphi)=\mathsf{T}\text{ and }v_{\tau}(\psi)=\mathsf{T}\\
\mathsf{F}\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle v_{\tau}(\varphi\lor\psi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathsf{T}\quad\text{if }v_{\tau}(\varphi)=\mathsf{T}\text{ or }v_{\tau}(\psi)=\mathsf{T}\\
\mathsf{F}\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In this way, each formula
\varphi
defines a Boolean-valued function of its atomic propositions under the truth value assignment
\tau
. We call two formulae
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}

tautologically equivalent
, if they have the same truth value on all truth assignment of the atoms, namely
\forall\tau,v_{\tau}(\varphi_{1})=v_{\tau}(\varphi_{2})
. Otherwise we call them
tautologically distinct
. With
m
atoms, there are infinitely many formulae, but there only exist
2^{2^{m}}
tautologically distinct formulae, with
2^{m}
being the number of possible truth value assignments.

The key observation is that Boolean function computation can be viewed as evaluating the semantics of a chosen formula by the receiver, where the truth assignment is determined by the transmitter. Specifically, let
{\bf b}=({\bf b}_{1},\ldots{\bf b}_{m})\in\{0,1\}^{m}
denote the message at the transmitter, and we associate each coordinate
b_{i}
with an atom
p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
and define the truth assignment
\tau_{{\bf b}}(p_{i})=\mathsf{T}
if
{\bf b}_{i}=1
and
\tau_{{\bf b}}(p_{i})=\mathsf{F}
if
{\bf b}_{i}=0
. Any Boolean function
f\in\mathcal{F}_{m}
can then be represented by a propositional formula
\varphi_{f}
such that for every input
{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
,

\displaystyle f({\bf b})=1\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;v_{\tau_{{\bf b}}}(\varphi_{f})=\mathsf{T}

Thus, evaluating the Boolean function
f
on the message
{\bf b}
is equivalent to evaluating the propositional formula
\varphi_{f}
under the truth assignment
\tau_{{\bf b}}
. Furthermore, there is a one-to-one mapping between the
2^{2^{m}}
different Boolean functions and
2^{2^{m}}
tautologically distinct formulae. The logic
AND
function defined in Example
3
gave an example of this connection. As another simple example, the formula that calculates the parity of three atoms

\displaystyle\varphi_{\text{parity}}:=p_{1}\oplus p_{2}\oplus p_{3}

corresponds to the Boolean function

\displaystyle f_{\varphi_{\text{parity}}}({\bf b}):=({\bf b}_{1}+{\bf b}_{2}+{\bf b}_{3})\pmod{2}.

Example 5
(Hamming weight of DNF)

Disjunctive normal forms (DNF) is a canonical form of formulae consisting of a disjunction of one or more conjunctions of one or more literals (an atomic or its negation). For example,
(p_{1}\land p_{2}\land\neg p_{3})\lor p_{4}
is in DNF where as
\neg(p_{1}\land p_{2})
is not. It is known that
[
8
, Coro. 15C]
in propositional logic, any well-formed formula in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P})
is tautologically equivalent to a formula in DNF. If we have a DNF with
t
disjoint conjunction terms (i.e. no two conjunction terms contain the same atoms), and each conjunction contains
k
atoms, then the Hamming weight of this DNF is
t2^{m-k}
. In general if there are overlapping atoms in different conjunction terms, the Hamming weight of this DNF is smaller or equal to
t2^{m-k_{\text{min}}}
where
k_{\text{min}}
is the number of atoms in the shortest conjunction term.

II-C

Connection to identification via channels and other Ahlswede models in
[
5
]

The Boolean function computation problem is a generalization of the identification via channels problem. A
(n,N,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
identification (ID) code is a collection
\{(Q_{i},D_{i}),i=1,\ldots,N\}
with probability distributions
Q_{i}
on
\mathcal{X}^{n}
and
D_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}
such that the two types of error probability satisfy

\displaystyle Q_{i}W(D_{i}^{c})\leq\lambda_{1},i=1,\ldots,N

\displaystyle Q_{i}W(D_{j})\leq\lambda_{2},i,j=1,\ldots,N,i\neq j

As discussed in Example
1
above, an
(n,N,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
ID code (assuming
N
is a power of
2
for simplicity) is equivalent to an
(n,\log N,\mathcal{F},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
BFC code where
\mathcal{F}
contains the functions
f_{j}^{id},j=1,\ldots,2^{m}
defined in (
1
).

In
[
5
]
, Ahlswede proposed several models which generalize the identification via channel problem. In all models, there is a family of partitions
\Pi=\{\pi_{j}\}_{j}
where each
\pi_{j}=\{P_{j1},\ldots,P_{jr}\}
is a partition of the messages
\mathcal{M}
, where we call
P_{ji}
a member of the partition
\pi_{j}
. The communication problem is described as follows:

•

the transmitter picks a message
m
from
\mathcal{M}
, and transmit a codeword associated to the message
m

•

upon receiving the channel output, for any partition
\pi_{j}
from the family
\Pi
, the decoder needs to decide which member of the partition
\pi_{j}
contains the true message
m

Identifying the set of messages
\mathcal{M}
with
\{0,1\}^{m}
, our model is a special case of the above model, where each partition
\pi_{j}
corresponds to a Boolean function
f_{j}
, which partitions
\mathcal{M}
into two (i.e.
r=2
) disjoint sets, corresponding to
f^{-1}[1]
and
f^{-1}[0]
, respectively. In particular, the
K
-identification problem
[
5
]
(see Model 3 below) is identical to our problem with the choice of functions
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(K)
. We repeat the communication models discussed in
[
5
]
(in its original numeration) below, and connect them to the BFC problem.

•

Model 2 (identification):
\Pi_{I}=\{\pi_{j}:\pi_{j}=\{\{j\},\mathcal{M}\backslash\{j\}\},j\in\mathcal{M}\}
. This corresponds to our problem with the choice of functions to be
f_{j}^{id}
defined in (
1
). Notice that we have

\displaystyle\{j\}=(f_{j}^{id})^{-1}[1]

•

Model 3 (
K
-identification):
\Pi_{K}=\{\pi_{\mathcal{S}}:\pi_{\mathcal{S}}=\{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M}\backslash S\},|\mathcal{S}|=K,\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{M}\}
. This corresponds to our problem with the choice of functions to be the set
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(K)
. Here we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=f^{-1}[1]\text{ for }f\in\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(K)

•

Model 4 (ranking):
\Pi_{R}=\{\pi_{r}:\pi_{r}=\{\{1,\ldots,r\},\{r+1,\ldots,2^{m}\}\},1\leq r\leq 2^{m}\}
. This corresponds to our problem with the choice of functions
f_{r}^{\text{rank}}
defined in (
4
). Notice that

\displaystyle\{1,\ldots,r\}=(f_{r}^{\text{rank}})^{-1}[1]

•

Model 5 (general binary questions) :
\Pi_{B}=\{\pi_{\mathcal{A}}:\pi_{\mathcal{A}}=\{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M}\backslash\mathcal{A}\},\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{M}\}
. This corresponds to our problem with the set of functions
\{f_{\mathcal{A}},\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{M}\}
, where
f_{\mathcal{A}}
is the function whose preimage under
1
is the set
\mathcal{A}
.

•

Model 6 (
t
-th bit):
\Pi_{C}=\{\pi_{t}:\pi_{t}=\{\{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{m}:{\bf b}_{t}=1\},\{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{m}:{\bf b}_{t}=0\}\},t=1,\ldots,m\}
. This corresponds to our problem with the choice of functions to be
f_{t}^{bit}
defined in (
2
). Notice that

\displaystyle\{{\bf b}:{\bf b}_{t}=1\}=(f_{t}^{bit})^{-1}[1]

Lemma 1

Given an
(n,m,\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(S),\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
BCF code, we can construct an
(n,m,\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(2^{m}-S),\lambda_{2},\lambda_{1})
BCF code.

Proof:

Given the encoder
Q_{i},D_{j}
of the
(n,m,\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(S),\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
BCF code, and for each function
f_{j}\in\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(S)
, we define a new set of functions
\tilde{f}_{j}=1-f_{j}
, and the corresponding encoders and decoders as

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{i}

\displaystyle:=Q_{i}

\displaystyle\tilde{D}_{j}

\displaystyle:=D_{j}^{c}

Clearly, the set
\{\tilde{f}_{j}\}_{j}=\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(2^{m}-S)
, and it holds
\tilde{f}_{j}^{-1}[1]=f_{j}^{-1}[0]
,
\tilde{f}_{j}^{-1}[0]=f_{j}^{-1}[1]
. To characterize the error probability of the new code, we have

\displaystyle{\bf 1}_{\tilde{f}_{j}(i)=1}Q_{i}W(\tilde{D}_{j}^{c})={\bf 1}_{f_{j}(i)=0}Q_{i}W(D_{j})\leq\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle{\bf 1}_{\tilde{f}_{j}(i)=0}Q_{i}W(\tilde{D}_{j})={\bf 1}_{f_{j}(i)=1}Q_{i}W(D_{j}^{c})\leq\lambda_{1}

where the inequalities follow from the definition of the
(n,m,\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(S),\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
BCF code.
∎

A similar result holds for the set of function
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(\leq S)

Lemma 2

\displaystyle N\geq=H(\lambda,M^{\prime})M^{\prime-1}\left(\frac{1-\epsilon}{2\epsilon}\right)^{\lceil\lambda M^{\prime}\rceil}

V
Proof of the converse

For the converse results, we follow the idea briefly discussed in
[
5
]
. The strategy is to show that any
(n,m,\mathcal{F},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
BFC code can be converted to a standard identification code, and the known converse result on identification code will imply a converse result for the BFC code. Recall the definition of the
(n,N,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
ID code in Section
II-C
. Define
N^{*}(n,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
to be the maximal
N
such that an
(n,N,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
ID code exists. We have the following known converse result for ID codes.

Theorem 3
(
[
2
,
4
]
)

Let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
such that
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}<1
. Then for every
\delta>0
and every sufficiently large
n

\displaystyle N^{*}(n,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\leq 2^{2^{n(C+\delta)}}

where
C
is the Shannon capacity of the channel.

To prove the result in Theorem
2
, we in fact show that there is a subset
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(S)
such that any BFC code for the set
\mathcal{F}
satisfy the statement in Theorem
2
. Specifically, we prove the following result.

Theorem 4
(Converse for a subset)

Assume the channel
W(\cdot|x^{n})
has a Shannon capacity
C>0
. Then there exists a set of constant weight functions
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(S)
, such that the computation capacity
C_{\text{BFC}}
for
\mathcal{F}
satisfy the statements in Theorem
2
.

Since
\mathcal{F}
is contained in
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(S)
, any converse result on the code
(n,m,\mathcal{F},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
automatically implies a converse result on the code
(n,m,\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(S),\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
, which proves Theorem
2
. The proof of Theorem
4
relies on the following Lemma
4
, which is proved using Lemma
5
(Gilbert’s bound for constant weight sequences) in Appendix. Lemma
4
states that we can find a subset
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(S)
which has essentially the same size as
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(S)
, but whose elements have small overlaps in their pre-images. The latter property allows us to convert a BFC code into a good identification code.

Lemma 4

For any integer
m\geq 1
,
1\leq S\leq 2^{m-1}
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
, there exists a set of constant weight functions
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\text{W}}(S)
satisfying the following properties

•

for any
f_{i},f_{j}\in\mathcal{F}
, it holds that
|f_{\ell}^{-1}[1]\cap f_{j}^{-1}[1]|\leq\alpha S

•

\log|\mathcal{F}|\geq\log{2^{m}\choose S}-\log{2^{m}\choose\lceil(1-\alpha)S\rceil-1}-S

Proof:

Any function
f\in\mathcal{F}_{m}
can be represented by a binary sequence
{\bf f}
of length
2^{m}
, where the
i
-th position of
{\bf f}
is
1
if and only if
f(i^{\prime})=1
. Here
i^{\prime}
is the dyadic representation of
i
, i.e., the unique binary sequence of length
m
such that
i=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}2^{j}i^{\prime}_{j+1}
. In other words,
{\bf f}
is defined via
{\bf f}_{i}:=f(i^{\prime})
. It is clear that if
f
has a Hamming weight
S
, the corresponding vector
{\bf f}
also has Hamming weight
S
. So we can apply Lemma
5
by identifying
T=2^{m}
and
G=\mathcal{F}
.
∎

Proof:

We consider a set of functions
\mathcal{F}
with properties specified by Lemma
4
, and consider an
(n,m,\mathcal{F},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
BFC code characterized by
(Q_{i})_{i\in\{0,1\}^{m}},(D_{j})_{j\in[\![|\mathcal{F}|]\!]}
for this class of functions.

We first show that we can convert the above BFC code to an identification code as follows. Specifically, the new identification code is defined to be the encoder/decoder pairs
\{(\tilde{Q}_{j},D_{j}),j\in[\![|\mathcal{F}|]\!]\}
where encoder
\tilde{Q}_{j}
is defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{j}(x^{n}):=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i\in f_{j}^{-1}[1]}Q_{i}(x^{n})

Now we characterize the error probability of this constructed identification code. The mis-identification error is

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf x}}\tilde{Q}_{j}({\bf x})W(D_{j}^{c}|{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf x}}\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i\in f_{j}^{-1}[1]}Q_{i}({\bf x})W(D_{j}^{c}|{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i\in f_{j}^{-1}[1]}\sum_{{\bf x}}Q_{i}({\bf x})W(D_{j}^{c}|{\bf x})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i\in f_{j}^{-1}[1]}\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}

where the inequality holds because
f_{j}(i)=1
hence
\sum_{{\bf x}}Q_{i}({\bf x})W(D_{j}^{c}|{\bf x})\leq\lambda_{1}
, by the property of the BFC code. The last equality holds because
|f_{j}^{-1}[1]|=S
as
f_{j}
has Hamming weight
S
.

The wrong-identification error is, for
\ell\neq j
,

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf x}}\tilde{Q}_{\ell}({\bf x})W(D_{j}|{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf x}}\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i\in f_{\ell}^{-1}[1]}Q_{i}({\bf x})W(D_{j}|{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S}\left(\sum_{i\in f_{\ell}^{-1}[1]\cap f_{j}^{-1}[1]}\sum_{{\bf x}}Q_{i}({\bf x})W(D_{j}|{\bf x})+\sum_{i\in f_{\ell}^{-1}[1]\backslash f_{j}^{-1}[1]}\sum_{{\bf x}}Q_{i}({\bf x})W(D_{j}|{\bf x})\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{S}\left(|f_{\ell}^{-1}[1]\cap f_{j}^{-1}[1]|+\sum_{i\in f_{\ell}^{-1}[1]\backslash f_{j}^{-1}[1]}\lambda_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\frac{|f_{\ell}^{-1}[1]\cap f_{j}^{-1}[1]|}{S}+\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}\alpha+\lambda_{2}

where in (a) we use the upper bound
\sum_{{\bf x}}Q_{i}({\bf x})W(D_{j}|{\bf x})\leq 1
and the BFC code error property, step (b) follows
|f_{\ell}^{-1}[1]\backslash f_{j}^{-1}[1]|\leq S
, and step (c) follows from the first property stated in Lemma
4
.

Therefore, we have shown that an
(n,m,\mathcal{F},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
BFC codes (with properties of
\mathcal{F}
prescribed in Lemma
4
) implies the existence of an
(n,|\mathcal{F}|,\lambda_{1},\alpha+\lambda_{2})
ID code for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Now fix some
\alpha\in(0,1-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})
. Theorem
3
states that for any
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
such that
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}<1
, it holds that

\displaystyle\log|\mathcal{F}|\leq 2^{n(C+\delta)}

(12)

for every
\delta>0
and every sufficiently large
n
.

Now we lower bound
|\mathcal{F}|
in terms of
m
using the property of
\mathcal{F}
shown in Lemma
4
for different cases of

, which will, in turn, gives an upper bound on

.

Case 1 (constant
S
).
To apply the result in Lemma
4
, we need
S\leq 2^{m-1}
, which is clearly satisfied for
m
large enough. In this case we have

\displaystyle\log|\mathcal{F}|

\displaystyle\geq\log{2^{m}\choose S}-\log{2^{m}\choose\lceil(1-\alpha)S\rceil-1}-S

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\geq}}S\log\frac{2^{m}}{S}-(\lceil(1-\alpha)S\rceil-1)\log 2^{m}-S

\displaystyle\geq S\log\frac{2^{m}}{S}-(1-\alpha)S\log 2^{m}-S

\displaystyle=S(\alpha m-\log S-1)

(13)

\displaystyle=m\underbrace{(\alpha S-m^{-1}S\log S-S/m)}_{A(m)}

where in (a) we used
(n/k)^{k}\leq{n\choose k}\leq n^{k}
. Combining the above inequality with (

), we know that the following inequality holds

\displaystyle mA(m)\leq 2^{n(C+\delta)}

and every sufficiently large

and sufficiently large

. Since the above inequality holds for all

.

Case 2 (

\displaystyle\geq S(\alpha m-\log S-1)

\displaystyle=cm^{\beta}(\alpha m-\log c-\beta\log m-1)

Case 4
:

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\geq}}S\log\frac{2^{m}}{S}-d\log(2^{m}e/d)-S

\displaystyle\geq S\log\frac{2^{m}}{S}-(1-\alpha)S(\log e2^{m}-\log d)-S

\displaystyle\geq S\log\frac{2^{m}}{S}-(1-\alpha)S(\log e2^{m}-\log((1-\alpha)S-1))-S

(14)

\displaystyle=m2^{m^{1/b}}(c\alpha-cm^{-1}\log c-cm^{1/b-1}-cm^{-1}-cm^{-1}(1-\alpha)\log e-cm^{-1}(1-\alpha)\log((1-\alpha)S-1))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}m2^{m^{1/b}}\underbrace{\left(c\alpha+c\alpha m^{1/b-1}+cm^{-1}(1-\alpha\log(1-\alpha-S^{-1}))-cB/m\right)}_{A(m)}

where in (a) we used

, and in (b) we define

. Combining the above inequality with (

Lemma
5
and its proof

The following lemma is used to prove Lemma
4
, which will be used in the proof of the converse result in Theorem
4
.

Lemma 5
(Gilbert’s bound for constant weight sequences)

Let
T,S
be two natural numbers such that
1\leq S\leq T/2
. For any
\alpha\in(0,1]
, there exists a set
G
of
T
-length binary sequences with the following properties

•

for any
a,b\in G
,
|a\cap b|\leq\alpha S

•

\log|G|\geq\log{T\choose S}-\log{T\choose\lceil(1-\alpha)S\rceil-1}-S

Proof:

By the Gilbert bound for constant weight sequences with a weight
S
, we know from
[
9
, Theorem 7]
that for any integer
0\leq d\leq S
, there exists a set
G
of binary sequences where
d_{H}(a,b)\geq 2d
for any
a,b\in G,a\neq b
and

\displaystyle|G|\geq\frac{{T\choose S}}{\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}{S\choose i}{T-S\choose i}}

To see the first claim, notice that two sequences
a,b
with weight
S
and Hamming distance
2\delta
has an intersection
|a\cap b|=S-\delta
. To see this, we count the total weights of the two sequences in two different ways. First note that the total weights of the two sequences is
2S
, as both sequences have weight
S
. On the other hand, the total weights can also be expressed as
2|a\cap b|+2\delta
, where
2|a\cap b|
is the number of positions where the two sequences take the value
1
, and
2\delta
is the number of positions where only one of the two sequences takes the value
1
. We let
d=\lceil(1-\alpha)S\rceil
. Then it holds that
|a\cap b|=S-d_{H}(a,b)/2\leq S-d\leq\alpha S
as
d\geq(1-\alpha)S
.

To show the lower bound, notice that we have for
i=1,\ldots,d-1

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}{S\choose i}{T-S\choose i}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}{S\choose i}{T\choose i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}{S\choose i}{T\choose d-1}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{S}{T\choose d-1}

where (a) holds because for
i=1,\ldots,d-1
with
d-1\leq(1-\alpha)S\leq S\leq T/2
, we have
{T\choose i}\leq{T\choose d-1}
.

∎
