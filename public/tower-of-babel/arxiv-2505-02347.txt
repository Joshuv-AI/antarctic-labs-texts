Temporal Robustness in Discrete Time Linear Dynamical Systems
Nilava Metya, Ankit Shah, Arunesh Sinha

Abstract
Discrete time linear dynamical systems, including Markov chains, have found many applications including in security settings such as in cybersecurity operations center (CSOC) management and in managing health risks. However, in these two scenarios, there is uncertainty about the time horizon for which the system runs. This creates uncertainty about the cost (or reward) incurred based on the state distribution when the system stops. Given past data samples of how long a system ran, we theoretically analyze the cost incurred at the stop of the system as a distributional robust cost estimation task in a Wasserstein ambiguity set. Towards this, we show an equivalence between a discrete time Markov Chain on a probability simplex and a global asymptotic stable (GAS) discrete time linear dynamical system, allowing us to base our study on a GAS system only. Then, we provide various polynomial time algorithms and hardness results for different cases in our theoretical study, including a novel proof of a fundamental result about Wassertein distance based polytope. We experiment with real world data in CSOC domain and prior data in health domain to reveal the benefits of our model and approach.

Introduction

Discrete time linear dynamical systems, prominently Markov chains, have found many uses in machine learning

Yet, in many applications the challenge lies not only in characterizing long-run properties, but also in reasoning about system performance within a fixed wall-clock horizon, where the effective number of discrete steps is itself uncertain due to variability in human behavior, external conditions, or contextual factors.
Addressing this horizon uncertainty is crucial for robust short-term prediction, risk estimation, and cost assessment, particularly in domains central to national and human security, such as cybersecurity operations and public health. We illustrate this challenge in both contexts below.
A Cybersecurity Operations Center (CSOC) is a centralized facility where analysts continuously monitor, investigate, and respond to security alerts. A CSOC is critically important as the frontline defense for protecting organizational assets, ensuring resilience against cyber threats, and maintaining trust in digital infrastructure. Most large organizations, including national defense agencies, operate CSOCs as integral components of their security infrastructure. Within these centers, analysts contend with a continuous stream of alerts
generated by intrusion detection systems. This alert-handling process

The stop time of this overtime chain is uncertain, depending on external factors such as analyst availability, for which some past data may be available. From past data on overtime behavior, one can estimate a nominal but uncertain probability distribution over the possible stopping times. At termination, the cost depends jointly on the backlog distribution and the number of overtime steps, creating a direct need for robust estimation under uncertain time horizons to ensure resilient and cost-effective cybersecurity operations.A similar phenomenon arises in health security, where stochastic models, such as Markov chains, of infectious disease spread, such as those used during a pandemic (natural-disaster-driven threats disrupting access to healthcare, food, and energy resources), inform resource allocation and resilience planning

U

Proposition 2

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{U},\bm{q}\in\mathcal{P}}\;\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}q_{t}\left\langle\bm{c},\bm{x}_{t}\right\rangle=\max_{i\in[U]}\max_{\bm{q}\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}q_{t}\langle\bm{c},M^{t}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{0}+\bm{u}_{i})\rangle

6
Approach

We describe our approach for finite and infinite support of

in the two subsections next. Finite support could arise when
\hat{\bm{p}}
is taken as the empirical distribution and infinite support when
\hat{\bm{p}}
is an estimated parametric distribution, given data samples. WLOG and to avoid cumbersome notation, we assume that
\mathcal{T}=\left\{1,2,\cdots,T\right\}=[T]
, where

6.1
Finite Support: Robust Cost Estimation

Naive approach
: The naive way of solving the
RCE
problem is to evaluate
\left\langle\bm{c},M^{t}\bm{x}_{0}\right\rangle
for each
t\in\mathcal{T}
. It is known that the dot product of two vectors (of size
{\mathcal{O}}(n)
) has
{\mathcal{O}}(n)
time complexity, and the standard approach of
n
dot products to compute a matrix-vector product has
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2})
time complexity. Using the fact that
M^{t}\bm{x}_{0}=M\times M^{t-1}\bm{x}_{0}
, a naive approach would require
T
matrix-vector and
T
dot products, yielding a time complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}T)
.
Prior results based approach
: A slightly better approach can be obtained by observing that
M^{t}\bm{x}_{0}=M\times M^{t-1}\bm{x}_{0}
involves multiplying the same matrix
M
with different vectors. A prior result
[
43
]
has shown that by preprocessing the matrix
M
, the time complexity of
T
matrix-vector products is
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2+\varepsilon}+Tn^{2}/(\varepsilon\log n)^{2})
for any small positive constant
\varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
. Thus, if
T
is larger than
n
, then the preprocessing step of
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2+\varepsilon})
can be beneficial.
Our approach
: We take this approach further in an algorithm that we call Small and Big Strides (SaBS) shown in Algorithm
1
. We start with the observation that
\langle c,M^{t}\bm{x}_{0}\rangle=\langle M^{\top}c,M^{t-1}\bm{x}_{0}\rangle
, and applying this recursively
\langle c,M^{t}\bm{x}_{0}\rangle=\langle(M^{\top})^{j}c,M^{t-j}\bm{x}_{0}\rangle
. Let
B=\lfloor\sqrt{T}\rfloor
. First, we compute
M^{B}
(line 2) by successive squaring
M^{1},M^{2},M^{4}
, a popular approach in number theory
[
22
]
. Then, preprocess
M^{B},M^{\top}
and
M
for later use in multiple matrix-vector products. Then, precompute
M^{B}\bm{x}_{0}
,
M^{2B}\bm{x}_{0}
,
\ldots,M^{B^{2}}\bm{x}_{0}
and
(M^{\top})^{1}\bm{c},\ldots,(M^{\top})^{B-1}\bm{c}
. Then, using the fact that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\langle c,M^{t}\bm{x}_{0}\rangle=\langle(M^{\top})^{t\mod B}c,M^{\lfloor t/B\rfloor B}\bm{x}_{0}\rangle}
, we can compute all
\langle c,M^{t}\bm{x}_{0}\rangle
for each
t\in\{1,\ldots,B^{2}\}
using the precomputed values in the loop at line 6. The remaining powers from
B^{2}+1
to

is computed in loop starting at line 7.We achieve a better runtime dependence on
T

Lemma 1
.

~\forall~\varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)

time, where

is the time complexity of matrix multiplication. (best known

currently is

(
44
)
).

Proof.

The complexity of finding
M^{B}
(line 1) is
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{\omega}\log B)
as there are about
\log B
squares and multiplications in the successive squaring (matrix products). Precomputing
B

{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2+\varepsilon}+Bn^{2}/(\varepsilon\log n)^{2})
time. Similarly, precomputing the matrix transpose powers in line 4 takes
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2+\varepsilon}+Bn^{2}/(\varepsilon\log n)^{2})
time. The first for loop (line 5) has time complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}(nT)
because of one dot product in the loop that runs
B^{2}
times. The second loop (line 7) does a matrix-vector product with the same matrix

\sqrt{T}

(
42

2\times 2

J_{i}^{t}=r_{i}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}\cos t\theta_{i}&-\sin t\theta_{i}\\
\sin t\theta_{i}&\cos t\theta_{i}\end{bmatrix}
.We consider
\left\langle\bm{c},PJ^{t}P^{-1}\bm{x}\right\rangle
as a function of
t
which is
\sum_{i=1}^{q}r_{i}^{t}(u_{i}\cos t\theta_{i}-v_{i}\sin t\theta_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{p}w_{j}\lambda_{j}^{t}
where
u_{i},v_{i},w_{j}
only depend on
\bm{c},\bm{x},P
(please see the details of the standard but tedious steps in Appendix
B
). Let
(u_{i},v_{i})=(d_{i}\cos\eta_{i},d_{i}\sin\eta_{i})
where
\eta_{i}\in[0,2\pi)
and
d_{i}=\sqrt{u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2}}
. So the expression we consider is

g(t)=\left\langle\bm{c},PJ^{t}P^{-1}\bm{x}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{q}d_{i}r_{i}^{t}\cos(t\theta_{i}+\eta_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{p}w_{j}\lambda_{j}^{t}.

(5)

Note that the
RCE
problem here is to solve
\displaystyle\max_{t\in\mathbb{N}}g(t)
.

Main Approach.

We first start by finding a
t_{0}
such that
g(t_{0})>0
. Then, since our problem is for a GAS system with
r_{i},|\lambda_{j}|<1
, we know that
g(t)
converges to
0
as
t\to\infty
. Thus, from basic real analysis, there must exists a
n_{0}
such that
\forall t^{\prime}>n_{0}
,
|g(t^{\prime})|<g(t_{0})
. Our approach is to compute
g(t)
for
t=1
to
n_{0}
and choose the maximum among these. Next, we show a construction of
n_{0}
, given
t_{0}
.

Proposition 4
.

Given
t_{0}
, for any positive integer
n_{0}
more than
\Big\lceil\log_{\zeta}{\frac{g(t_{0})}{\sum\left|d_{i}\right|+\sum\left|w_{j}\right|}}\Big\rceil
, where
\zeta\coloneqq\max\left\{\left|\lambda_{p}\right|,r_{q}\right\}
, we have
\forall t^{\prime}>n_{0},|g(t^{\prime})|<g(t_{0})
.

Proof.

\zeta=\max\left\{\left|\lambda_{p}\right|,r_{q}\right\}<1
. For
t^{\prime}>n_{0}\geq\log_{\zeta}{\frac{g(t_{0})}{\sum\left|d_{i}\right|+\sum\left|w_{j}\right|}}
, since
\zeta<1
, we have
\displaystyle\zeta^{t^{\prime}}<\frac{g(t_{0})}{\sum\left|d_{i}\right|+\sum\left|w_{j}\right|}
whence
\displaystyle\left|g(t^{\prime})\right|\leq\sum\left|d_{i}\right|r_{i}^{t^{\prime}}+\sum\left|w_{j}\lambda_{j}^{t^{\prime}}\right|\leq\left(\sum\left|d_{i}\right|+\sum\left|w_{j}\right|\right)\zeta^{t^{\prime}}<g(t_{0})
.∎

The correctness of the approach is not hard to see. Since we already found
t_{0}
with
g(t_{0})>0
and
g(t^{\prime})<g(t_{0})
for
t^{\prime}>n_{0}
(note that
n_{0}\geq t_{0}
is implied), then any time step
t^{*}
for which
g(t^{*})
is maximum it must be such that
1\leq t^{*}\leq n_{0}
. Also, there is a special case when there is no such
t_{0}
that can be found, and then
g
takes the maximum value of
0
at
\infty
. Next, we explain how to find such a
t_{0}
or infer that there is no such
t_{0}
. We consider two cases, based on which one among
r_{q}
or
|\lambda_{p}|
is dominating for large
t
in function
g
.
Case 1
:
r_{q}<\left|\lambda_{p}\right|
. Then
w_{p}\lambda_{p}^{t}
is the dominating term in
g
. Dividing
g(t)
by
\left|\lambda_{p}\right|^{t}
we obtain

\frac{g(t)}{\left|\lambda_{p}\right|^{t}}=\sum_{i=1}^{q}d_{i}\big(\frac{r_{i}}{\left|\lambda_{p}\right|}\Big)^{t}\cos(t\theta_{i}+\eta_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}w_{j}\Big(\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\left|\lambda_{p}\right|}\Big)^{t}+w_{p}(\sgn\lambda_{p})^{t}.

We show that the LHS
\frac{g(t)}{\left|\lambda_{p}\right|^{t}}
gets quite close to the dominating term
w_{p}\left(\sgn\lambda_{p}\right)^{t}
for large
t
; using triangle inequality we quantify an estimate of the error
\left|\frac{g(t)}{\left|\lambda_{p}\right|^{t}}-w_{p}(\sgn\lambda_{p})^{t}\right|
as follows:

Proposition 5
.

\left|\frac{g(t)}{\left|\lambda_{p}\right|^{t}}-w_{p}(\sgn\lambda_{p})^{t}\right|\leq\beta\varepsilon^{t}
with
\displaystyle\varepsilon\coloneqq\frac{\max\{r_{i},\cdots,r_{q},\left|\lambda_{1}\right|,\cdots,\left|\lambda_{p-1}\right|\}}{\left|\lambda_{p}\right|}<1
and
\displaystyle\beta\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{q}\left|d_{i}\right|+\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\left|w_{j}\right|
.

With this approximation and error in mind, we present upper bounds on
t_{0}
. The core idea is to bound
\beta\varepsilon^{t}
for large
t
, which is possible as
\varepsilon<1
. Based on this idea, we prove the following lemma for different signs of
w_{p}
and
\lambda_{p}
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\varepsilon,\beta
be the same as in Proposition
5
. For the first three cases below, there is a cutoff beyond which a
t_{0}
can be found and an explicit formulation for
t_{0}
exists. The final case yields a cutoff beyond which such a
t_{0}
does not exist.

•

w_{p}\!>\!0,\lambda_{p}\!>\!0:

\forall t>\log_{\varepsilon}\big(\frac{w_{p}}{\beta}\big)
,
g(t)>0
. Thus,
t_{0}=1+\max\big\{\big\lceil\log_{\varepsilon}\big(\frac{w_{p}}{\beta}\big)\big\rceil,1\big\}
.

•

w_{p}\!>\!0,\lambda_{p}\!<\!0:

\forall t>\frac{1}{2}\log_{\varepsilon}\big(\frac{w_{p}}{\beta}\big)
,
g(2t)>0
. Thus,
t_{0}=2+2\max\big\{\big\lceil\frac{1}{2}\log_{\varepsilon}\big(\frac{w_{p}}{\beta}\big)\big\rceil,1\big\}
.

•

w_{p}\!<\!0,\lambda_{p}\!<\!0:

\forall t>\frac{1}{2}\log_{\varepsilon}\big(\frac{-w_{p}}{\beta}\big)
,
g(2t+1)>0
. Thus,
t_{0}=2\max\big\{\big\lceil\frac{1}{2}\log_{\varepsilon}\big(\frac{-w_{p}}{\beta}\big)\big\rceil,1\big\}+1
.

•

w_{p}\!<\!0,\lambda_{p}\!>\!0:

\forall t>\log_{\varepsilon}\big(\frac{-w_{p}}{\beta}\big)
,
g(t)<0
.

In the final case, instead of searching for
t_{0}
, we can directly search for
n_{0}
: search among
1\leq n_{0}\leq\big\lfloor\log_{\varepsilon}\big(\frac{-w_{p}}{\beta}\big)\big\rfloor
for where
g(n_{0})
is positive and is maximized. If the search returns empty (meaning for all
t
,
g(t)<0)
, then no
t_{0}
or
n_{0}
exists for this last case and
g
is maximized at
\infty
with value
0
.

Proof.

We prove each bullet point respectively as follows.

•

w_{p}>0,\lambda_{p}>0
:
t_{0}>\log_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{w_{p}}{\beta}\right)\implies\varepsilon^{t_{0}}<\frac{w_{p}}{\beta}
. The last implication is because
\varepsilon<1
. Since
w_{p},\lambda_{p}>0
, Proposition
5
gives
\left|\frac{g(t_{0})}{\left|\lambda_{p}\right|^{t_{0}}}-w_{p}\right|<w_{p}
. This means
\frac{g(t_{0})}{\left|\lambda_{p}\right|^{t_{0}}}>0
whence
g(t_{0})>0
.

•

w_{p}>0,\lambda_{p}<0
:We use a similar reasoning as above. If
t_{0}
is even and
\frac{t_{0}}{2}>\frac{1}{2}\log_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{w_{p}}{\beta}\right)
, that is,
t_{0}>\log_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{w_{p}}{\beta}\right)
then Proposition
5
gives us
\left|\frac{g(t_{0})}{\left|\lambda_{p}\right|^{t_{0}}}-w_{p}\right|\leq\beta\varepsilon^{t_{0}}<w_{p}
which again implies that
g(t_{0})>0
.

•

w_{p}<0,\lambda_{p}<0
:Again, use similar reasoning as the first bullet point.
t_{0}
being odd here ensures that
w_{p}(\sgn\lambda_{p})^{t_{0}}=-w_{p}>0
. Here we again have
t_{0}>\log_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{-w_{p}}{\beta}\right)
. Proposition
5
turns into
\left|\frac{g(t_{0})}{\left|\lambda_{p}\right|^{t_{0}}}+w_{p}\right|<-w_{p}
whence
g(t_{0})>0
.

•

w_{p}<0,\lambda_{p}>0
: If
t>\log_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{-w_{p}}{\beta}\right)
then
\varepsilon^{t}<\frac{-w_{p}}{\beta}
as earlier. For such
t\in\mathbb{N}
, Proposition
5
gives
\frac{g(t)}{\left|\lambda_{p}\right|^{t}}-w_{p}\leq\beta\varepsilon^{t}<-w_{p}
whence
g(t)<0
. So if
g(t)<0
for each
t\leq t_{0}\coloneqq\left\lfloor\log_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{-w_{p}}{\beta}\right)\right\rfloor
then
g
is always negative on
\mathbb{N}
.

The claim of searching to find
n_{0}
in the final case is straightforward from the description.∎

Case 2
:
r_{q}>\left|\lambda_{p}\right|
. Similar to case 1, we consider

\frac{g(t)}{r_{q}^{t}}=\sum_{i=1}^{q-1}d_{i}\Big(\frac{r_{i}}{r_{q}}\Big)^{t}\cos(t\theta_{i}+\eta_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{p}w_{j}\Big(\frac{\lambda_{j}}{r_{q}}\Big)^{t}+d_{q}\cos(t\theta_{q}+\eta_{q}).

We again characterize the long-range behavior of the function with respect to
t
using the triangle inequality, but the dominating term being
d_{q}\cos(t\theta_{q}+\eta_{q})
here.

Proposition 6
.

\left|\frac{g(t)}{r_{q}^{t}}-d_{q}\cos(t\theta_{q}+\eta_{q})\right|\leq\gamma C^{t}
with
\displaystyle C\coloneqq\frac{\max\{r_{1},\cdots,r_{q-1},\left|\lambda_{1}\right|,\cdots,\left|\lambda_{p}\right|\}}{r_{q}}<1
and
\displaystyle\gamma\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{q-1}\left|d_{i}\right|+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left|w_{j}\right|
.

Unlike the previous case, the dominating term here is a cosine function with a certain phase
\eta_{q}
and frequency
\theta_{q}
. In the previous case we could easily control the term dependent on
t
(which was
\left(\sgn\lambda_{p}\right)^{t}
) by simply considering the two cases of the sign of
\lambda_{p}
. This is significantly harder in this case because of the behavior of cosine terms for input angles that are
integral multiples
of the frequency
\theta_{q}
. To avoid irrationality issues (for example arithmetic modulo
\pi
),
we measure angles in degrees
. We show that depending on the phase and frequency, one can carefully control the integers
t
in order to get the expected behavior. More technically, we have the following bound for
t_{0}
in this case.

Lemma 3
.

Given angles are measured in degrees and
\theta_{q}=a/b\in\mathbb{Q}
with
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
\gcd(a,b)=1
,there exist integers
n\neq 0,l
such that
an+360bl=\gcd(360,a)=g
, computable in
{\mathcal{O}}(\log(\min(a,b)))
time. Let
C,d_{q},\gamma
be as in Proposition
6
. Then, for any integer
p\in(0,90b)
,
t_{0}
is upper bounded by

\frac{n(p-bc)+\left|n\right|\left(s_{0}+\max\left\{1,\lceil D\rceil\right\}\right)360b}{g},~~~\text{where}

s_{0}=1+\max\Big\{0,\Big\lceil\frac{\sgn(n)(cb-p)}{360}\Big\rceil\Big\}
,
D+s_{0}=\frac{g\log_{C}\big(\frac{\left|d_{q}\right|}{2\gamma}\big)-n(p-cb)}{360b\left|n\right|}
and
c=135+\lfloor\eta\rfloor-\sgn(d_{q})\cdot 90
.

Proof.

Proof SketchWe show in the full proof that
\exists k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
s.t.
\cos(k\theta_{q}+\eta_{q})\geq 0.5
and
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}

\cos(k^{\prime}\theta_{q}+\eta_{q})\leq-0.5
. The full proof involves the use of the number theory results including Bezout’s identity, parameters of which can be computed by Extended Euclid’s algorithm, which takes
{\mathcal{O}}(\log(\min(a,b)))
in our case. Then, we use these results for the two different possible sign of
d_{q}
in Proposition
6
to get the bound above.∎

Hardness
: The above results show that the steps required depend on the input problem, and can be arbitrarily large (e.g., for
\zeta
nearly
1
in Prop.
4
). We formalize this by a NP Hardness result. Before stating the result, we present a brief discussion about the decision version of an optimization problem and why both are equivalent when considering nondeterministic polytime (NP) hardness. Formally, an optimization problem has a polytime algorithm iff its corresponding decision version has a polytime algorithm
(
9
)
. In brief, for an optimization problem of
\max F(x)
with the constraint that
x\in X
,the corresponding decision version is to decide whether there exists
x\in X
satisfying
F(x)\geq\alpha
for given input
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.In fact, if there is a polytime (in size of inputs) algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
to answer the latter, for any inputs
\alpha,F,X
, then given any additive accuracy parameter
\varepsilon>0
, one can use bisection (binary search) by calling
{\mathcal{A}}
about
{\mathcal{O}}\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
times (overall still polytime) in order to solve the optimization problem upto additive
\varepsilon-
accuracy.The other way is not hard to see and can be found in textbooks
(
9
)
.In this work, the decision version of
RCE
asks whether there exists
t\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\left\langle\bm{c},M^{t}\bm{x}\right\rangle\geq\alpha
, given inputs
n\in\mathbb{N},\bm{c}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n},\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n},M\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n},\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
with
\rho(M)<1
. We use
\mathbb{Q}
instead of
\mathbb{R}
to stick to the Turing model of computation. Indeed, the next NP Hardness result shows that the
RCE
problem for infinite support is not easy in general, unless
\text{P}=\text{NP}
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
M\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
with
\rho(M)<1
,
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
,
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
, it is NP-hard to decide whether there exists
t\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\left\langle\bm{c},M^{t}\bm{x}\right\rangle\geq\alpha
.

Proof.

The following decision problem is known to be NP-hard from
[
5
]
: (
DIRCYC
) Given a directed graph
G
on
n
nodes, is there an integer
t^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
G
has no directed path of length
t^{*}
from node
1
to node
n
?We will show a reduction from this problem to the problem of our interest.Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and graph
G
be an input to
DIRCYC
. We describe an input to our problem as follows. Let
A
be the adjacency matrix of
G
. It is well known that
p_{i}^{(j)}\coloneqq\bm{e}_{1}^{\top}A^{j}\bm{e}_{i}=(A^{j})_{1i}
is the number of directed walks of length
j
from
1
to
i
in
G
. Let
r=1+\max\limits_{j}\sum\limits_{i}A_{ij}
. Proposition
8
(in appendix) for
p=1
implies
\rho(A)\leq r-1
. So
\frac{1}{r}A
has all eigenvalues of size
<1
.Take inputs as
n,M=\frac{1}{r}A,\bm{x}=\bm{e}_{n},\alpha=0,\bm{c}=-\bm{e}_{1}
.Note that for any
t\in\mathbb{N},\left\langle\bm{c},A^{t}\bm{x}\right\rangle=p_{n}^{(t)}
for the aforementioned inputs.We claim
\exists~t^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
for which
G
has no directed path from
1
to
n
of length
t^{*}
iff
\exists~t^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\left\langle\bm{c},M^{t^{*}}\bm{a}\right\rangle\leq\alpha
. Indeed,
\exists~t^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\left\langle\bm{c},M^{t^{*}}\bm{a}\right\rangle\geq\alpha\iff-\frac{p_{n}^{(t^{*})}}{r^{t^{*}}}\geq 0\iff p_{n}^{(t^{*})}\leq 0\iff p_{n}^{(t^{*})}=0\iff G
has no directed path from
1
to
n
of length
t^{*}
.∎

Given the above, unsurprisingly, the lemma below shows that there exists instances for which the
RCE
problem for infinite support is not solvable in polytime following our approach. For special choices of inputs
\bm{c},\bm{x},M
, the objective
\left\langle\bm{c},M^{t}\bm{x}\right\rangle
can be controlled so that the
first time

t^{*}
that the objective becomes positive is arbitrarily large. However, we also show in Appendix
C
that a constant upper bound for
t^{*}
is possible for the special case of
n=2
.

Lemma 4
.

For every
k=1,2,\cdots
, there are inputs
\bm{c}_{k},\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},M_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
\rho(M_{k})<1
such that
\inf\{t\!\in\!\mathbb{N}~|~\left\langle\bm{c}_{k},M_{k}^{t}\bm{x}_{k}\right\rangle\!>\!0\}\geq k
. In particular, the infimum exists (for these special inputs).

6.4
Infinite Support: Distributional Robust Cost Estimation

The NP hardness result for
RCE
already shows that the
DRCE
problem is hard, as
RCE
is a special case of
DRCE
. In fact, note that representing the distribution
\hat{\bm{p}}
over infinite support is possible on a computer only as a parametric distribution. Here, noting that a geometric distribution with parameter
\rho
captures the first occurrence of an event, we show results for distributions represented by a geometric distribution with the success event indicating the stoppage of the GAS system. We rely on the fact that the Wasserstein-1 distance between two geometric distributions can be written in terms of the parameters
[
10
]
. Similar results as below can be obtained for other parametric distributions, such as Poisson or negative binomial, that admit a closed form formula for Wasserstein-1 distance in terms of the parameters.Let
\hat{\rho}
be the parameter for geometric distribution
\hat{\bm{p}}
, and we restrict the distributions in the ambiguity set of Equation
3
to be from the geometric family, thus, we write
\bm{q}_{\rho}
to indicate the parameter
\rho
for any
\bm{q}\in\mathcal{P}
. By geometric distribution
q_{\rho}(t)=(1-\rho)^{t-1}\rho
. Then, from standard results
[
10
]
, we get
W_{1}(\bm{q}_{\rho},\hat{\bm{p}})=|1/\rho-1/\hat{\rho}|
, which further implies the restriction that
\frac{\hat{\rho}}{1+\hat{\rho\xi}}\leq\rho\leq\frac{\hat{\rho}}{1-\hat{\rho\xi}}
. Then, using the fact that
g(t)
converges to
\bm{0}
, we can choose a
n_{0}
and optimize for
\sum_{t=1}^{n_{0}}g(t)q_{\rho}(t)
to obtain the following arbitrary additive approximation.

Proposition 7
.

Let
\displaystyle n_{0}=\big\lceil\log_{\zeta}{\frac{\varepsilon}{\sum\left|d_{i}\right|+\sum\left|w_{j}\right|}}\big\rceil
, where
\zeta=\max\left\{\left|\lambda_{p}\right|,r_{q}\right\}
, for small
\varepsilon
. Let
\delta=(1-\rho)^{n_{0}}
, then
\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}g(t)q_{\rho}(t)-\sum_{t=1}^{n_{0}}g(t)q_{\rho}(t)\right|\leq\varepsilon\delta
.

Proof.

We obtain
n_{0}
directly from application of from Proposition
4
by replacing
g(t_{0})
by
\varepsilon
. Then, the CDF for
\displaystyle\bm{q}_{\rho}
is
1-(1-\rho)^{t}
. Thus,
\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=n_{0}+1}^{\infty}g(t)q_{\rho}(t)\right|\leq\varepsilon\sum_{t=n_{0}+1}^{\infty}q_{\rho}(t)=\varepsilon\delta
.∎

We note that
\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n_{0}}g(t)q_{\rho}(t)
may be a non-convex function in
\rho
and we can use projected gradient ascent with random restarts to aim to find the maximum.

Conclusion

We proposed a pertinent distributional robust cost estimation problem in GAS systems with an uncertain time horizon, and presented theoretical results to solve it that also yielded fundamental general theory results. A number of promising research directions can be pursued, such as exploring different ambiguity sets, time horizon robustness in control problems, and combining with uncertainty in other problem parameters, enabling resilient, data-driven strategies that enhance security, operational reliability, and the protection of critical infrastructure and population-level resources.

\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{p}\geq\left\lVert A\bm{u}\right\rVert_{p}=\left\lVert\lambda\bm{u}\right\rVert_{p}=\left|\lambda\right|=\rho(A)
.∎

All different eignevalues assumption
: Note that for a GAS system the highest magnitude eigenvalue determines the rate of convergence to
\bm{0}
. Thus, if the there are two eigenvalues exactly same with the highest magnitude, perturbing one of them so that magnitude reduces by
\varepsilon
for arbitrarily small
\varepsilon
does not affect the convergence to
\bm{0}
or the rate of convergence. The other non-highest magnitude eignevalues can also be perturbed similarly with no effect on the convergence to
\bm{0}
or the rate of convergence.
How do
u_{i},v_{i},w_{j}
only depend on
\bm{c},\bm{x},P
?
Recall
M=PJP^{-1}
where
J
is the real Jordan form of
M
and
P
is a real invertible matrix. The expression
\left\langle\bm{c},M^{t}\bm{x}\right\rangle=\bm{c}^{\top}PJ^{t}P^{-1}\bm{x}
. Consider
\bm{\sigma}\coloneqq P^{\top}\bm{c}
,
\bm{\tau}\coloneqq P^{-1}\bm{x}
. Then
\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{c},M^{t}\bm{x}\right\rangle=\bm{\sigma}^{\top}J^{t}\bm{\tau}=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\sigma_{2q}\\
\sigma_{2q+1}\\
\vdots\\
\sigma_{2q+p}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}r_{1}^{t}J_{1}^{t}\\
&\ddots&&\text{\Large 0}\\
&&r_{q}^{t}J_{q}^{t}\\
&&&\lambda_{1}^{t}\\
&\text{\Large 0}&&&\ddots\\
&&&&&\lambda_{p}^{t}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tau_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\tau_{2q}\\
\tau_{2q+1}\\
\vdots\\
\tau_{2q+p}\end{bmatrix}=\sum_{i=1}^{q}r_{i}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{2i-1}\\
\sigma_{2i}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}J_{i}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}\tau_{2i-1}\\
\tau{2i}\end{bmatrix}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}^{t}\sigma_{2q+j}\tau_{2q+j}
.Each summand in the first summation looks like
\displaystyle r_{i}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{2i-1}\\
\sigma_{2i}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}J_{i}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}\tau_{2i-1}\\
\tau{2i}\end{bmatrix}=r_{i}^{t}\left(\cos t\theta_{i}(\tau_{2i-1}\sigma_{2i-1}+\tau_{2i}\sigma_{2i})-\sin t\theta_{i}(\tau_{2i}\sigma_{2i-1}-\tau_{2i-1}\sigma_{2i})\right)
. Defining
u_{i}\coloneqq\tau_{2i-1}\sigma_{2i-1}+\tau_{2i}\sigma_{2i},v_{i}\coloneqq\tau_{2i}\sigma_{2i-1}-\tau_{2i-1}\sigma_{2i},w_{j}\coloneqq\tau_{2q+j}\sigma_{2q+j}
for each
1\leq i\leq q,1\leq j\leq p
gives
\left\langle\bm{c},M^{t}\bm{x}\right\rangle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{q}r_{i}^{t}(u_{i}\cos t\theta_{i}-v_{i}\sin t\theta_{i})+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}w_{j}\lambda_{j}^{t}
. Moreover, the
u_{i},v_{i},w_{j}
’s are defined in terms of
\bm{\sigma},\bm{\tau}
which only depend on
P,\bm{c},\bm{\tau}
which all have only real entries.

Appendix C
Special case for
n=2

In this special case for the infinite support, we can provide a better bound on
t_{0}
.Recall that if
M
has purely imaginary eigenvalues with the angle parameter of its Jordan form given by
\theta
,
r=\det M>0
,
\left\langle\boldsymbol{c},M^{t}\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle=r^{t}(a_{1}\cos t\theta-a_{2}\sin t\theta)
where
a_{1},a_{2}
are completely determined by
\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x},M
. We let
d>0,\kappa>0,\alpha\in(0,2\pi),\gamma\in(0,2\pi)
be such that
a_{1}+ia_{2}=de^{i\alpha}
and
\ln r+i\theta=\kappa e^{i\gamma}
. We
assume

\alpha,\theta,\gamma\in\mathbb{Q}
.

Lemma 6
.

If
\theta\in(0,\pi)
then for
\displaystyle t_{0}\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{-\frac{\pi}{2}-\alpha+2\pi\cdot\left\lceil\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}+\frac{1}{4}\right\rceil}{\theta}\right\rceil
we have
g(t_{0})>0
. Consider
\displaystyle\left\{x_{m}\coloneqq\frac{\frac{\pi}{2}-\alpha-\gamma+m\pi}{\theta}\nonscript\;\;|\;\nonscript\;m\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}
. Moreover for
\displaystyle m^{*}\coloneqq 1+\left\lceil\frac{\theta\log_{r}\left(\frac{g(t_{0})}{\left|\sin\gamma\right|d}\right)+\alpha+\gamma-\frac{\pi}{2}}{\pi}\right\rceil
, we have
\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}g(t)=\sup_{1\leq t\leq x_{m^{*}}}g(t)
.

Proof.

For non-real complex eigenvalues,
J=r\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}
. Then,
J^{t}=r^{t}\begin{bmatrix}\cos t\theta&-\sin t\theta\\
\sin t\theta&\cos t\theta\end{bmatrix}
whence
\left\langle\boldsymbol{c},M^{t}\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle=r^{t}(a_{1}\cos t\theta-a_{2}\sin t\theta)
where
a_{1},a_{2}
are constants completely determined by the inputs
\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x},M
.Let’s try to see where
r^{t}(a_{1}\cos t\theta-a_{2}\sin t\theta)
maximizes.First extend to a real function
g(x)\coloneqq r^{x}(a_{1}\cos x\theta-a_{2}\sin x\theta)=\Re(e^{ix\theta}(a_{1}+ia_{2}))
so that
g^{\prime}(x)=r^{x}\Re\left(e^{ix\theta}\cdot(\ln r+i\theta)\cdot(a_{1}+ia_{2})\right)
where
\Re(\cdot)
denotes the real part of complex numbers.Let’s name the above complex numbers as
u\coloneqq\ln r+i\theta=\kappa e^{i\gamma}
and
a\coloneqq a_{1}+ia_{2}=de^{i\alpha}
where
\kappa,d>0
and
\alpha,\gamma\in\left(0,2\pi\right)
. But
r\in(0,1),\theta\neq\pi
means that
\gamma\in\left(\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}\right)
and
\sin\gamma\neq\frac{\pi}{\kappa}
. With this we have
g(x)=r^{x}\cos(x\theta+\alpha)
. We assumed
\alpha,\theta\in\mathbb{Q}
.Now we find all those
x
such that
g^{\prime}(x)=0
and
g(x)>0
(such an
x
automatically satisfies
g^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 0
). Let’s first focus on the solutions of
g^{\prime}(x)=0
, that is, all
x
satisfying
\Re\left(e^{i\left(x\theta+\alpha+\gamma\right)}\right)=\frac{1}{\kappa d}\Re\left(e^{ix\theta}ua\right)=0
.The solutions are
\displaystyle\left\{x_{m}\coloneqq\frac{\frac{\pi}{2}-\alpha-\gamma+m\pi}{\theta}\nonscript\;\;|\;\nonscript\;m\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}
.They satisfy

g(x_{m})=(-1)^{m}r^{x_{m}}d\sin\gamma.

Thus
\left\{\left|g(x_{m})\right|\right\}_{m\geq 0}
is a strictly decreasing sequence in terms of
m
. In fact, the local maxima over the positive reals are exactly the points
\left\{x_{2m}\right\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. The issue in finding
\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}g(t)
is that these
x_{m}
’s are merely real numbers.However, if we could find some
t_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
g(t_{0})>0
, taking
\varepsilon=g(t_{0})
gives an
N
such that
g(x_{m})<\varepsilon~\forall~m>N
, whence the required supremum occurs only over
\mathbb{N}\cap[1,x_{N-1}]
. Now take
t_{0}
to be as in the statement of the lemma.For brevity let’s call
p\coloneqq\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}+\frac{1}{4}
and
q\coloneqq\lceil p\rceil
(this choice of
q
makes
t_{0}>0
). Clearly
t_{0}\theta+\alpha\geq 2\pi q-\frac{\pi}{2}
. It was earlier assumed that
\alpha,\theta\in\mathbb{Q}
so the last inequality is strict. This means that
\displaystyle\frac{-\frac{\pi}{2}-\alpha+2\pi q}{\theta}\notin\mathbb{Z}
.

The following thus holds:

\displaystyle\frac{-\frac{\pi}{2}-\alpha+2\pi q}{\theta}<t_{0}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{-\frac{\pi}{2}-\alpha+2\pi q}{\theta}+1

\displaystyle\implies 2\pi q-\frac{\pi}{2}<t_{0}\theta+\alpha

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\pi}{2}+2\pi q+\theta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle[\because~\theta<\pi]}}{{<}}2\pi q+\frac{\pi}{2}.

It thus stands that
\Re\left(e^{it_{0}\theta}\cdot a\right)=d\cos(t_{0}\theta+\alpha)>0
which has the same sign as
g(t_{0})
.For the latter part, notice that if
\displaystyle m\geq m^{*}
then
x_{m}>\log_{r}\left(\frac{g(t_{0})}{d\left|\sin\gamma\right|}\right)
whence
\ln\left|g(x_{m^{*}})\right|\leq\ln\left|g(x_{m})\right|=x_{m}\underbrace{\ln r}_{<0}+\ln(d\left|\sin\gamma\right|)<\log_{r}\left(\frac{g(t_{0})}{d\left|\sin\gamma\right|}\right)\ln r+\ln(d\left|\sin\gamma\right|)=\ln\left(g(t_{0})\right)
. So
t>x_{m^{*}}
implies that
g(t)\leq\left|g(x_{m^{*}})\right|<g(t_{0})
. The first inequality here is because if
x\geq x_{k}
is a real number then
\left|g(x)\right|=r^{x}\left|\cos(x\theta+\alpha)\right|\leq r^{x}\leq r^{x_{k}}\cdot 1=r^{x_{k}}\cdot\left|\cos(x_{k}\theta+\alpha)\right|=\left|g(x_{k})\right|
.∎
