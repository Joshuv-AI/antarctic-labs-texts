Effect of stress ball use before cholecystectomy surgery on surgical fear, anxiety and comfort of patients: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Hatice Merve Alptekin, Seval Ulubay, Seda Cansu Yeniğün Akbulut
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThinking about surgery generally causes fear and anxiety among patients. These negative feelings may affect the healing process. This study aimed to investigate the effects of a stress ball on surgical fear, anxiety and comfort among patients undergoing cholecystectomy.MethodThis randomized controlled study was performed with 90 patients from 03 April to 30 November 2024 in the preoperative waiting unit of an operating room in a public hospital. The intervention group received stress balls before surgery, and the control group received no intervention. The participants were randomly allocated to the intervention and control groups. Data were collected before and after the intervention via the Patient Description Form, Surgical Fear Questionnaire, Visual Analog Scale-Anxiety (VAS-A) and General Comfort Questionnaire. The data were analysed via the SPSS version 27.0 program. Descriptive statistics, t tests, Pearson chi-square tests, and stepwise multiple linear regression were applied, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsIn terms of the measures taken after the stress ball intervention, patients in the intervention group had significantly lower surgical fear (t = 8.607; p < 0.001) and anxiety (t = 10.776; p < 0.001), whereas the general comfort level was greater (t = 15.765; p < 0.001). When the surgical fear subscales were investigated, the intervention group had lower values for both short- and long-term surgical fear than did the control group (t = 8.480 and 8.613; p < 0.001).ConclusionStress balls appear to be effective, low-cost, mobile and practical tools for reducing preoperative fear and anxiety while increasing patient comfort. Their integration into routine nursing practice as a nonpharmacological intervention could contribute to better emotional preparedness and potentially improve overall surgical outcomes.Trial registration noNCT06345768 (Registration date:02.04.2024) https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06345768.

Introduction

Gallstones are estimated to affect 6–20% of the global population, with the condition being approximately twice as common in women as in men [1, 2]. Cholecystectomy is the standard treatment for these patients and can be performed via either open or laparoscopic techniques. Currently, the laparoscopic approach is preferred over open surgery for patients with surgical indications [3]. Over the past two decades, laparoscopy has become the most widely used method due to its advantages, including shorter hospital stays, greater postoperative patient satisfaction, reduced work loss, fewer complications, and better cosmetic outcomes [4].

Although laparoscopic cholecystectomy is associated with less trauma, bleeding, and postoperative complications, patients may still experience anxiety during the preoperative period due to factors such as anticipated pain, physical discomfort, changes in body image, functional limitations, and fear of postoperative disability [5, 6]. Anxiety is described as a distressing feeling of fear or worry that can be perceived as life-threatening [7]. In surgical patients, anxiety can increase the physiological stress response and the likelihood of postoperative complications, thereby delaying recovery. It can also contribute to symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, fatigue, tachycardia, and respiratory problems, which may hinder patient participation in postoperative care and adherence to treatment [8, 9].

In addition to anxiety, patients may experience surgical fear before the operation [10–12]. Menevşe et al. [12] reported that patients frequently experience surgical fear prior to surgery. This fear often begins when the procedure is explained to the patient and may intensify after hospitalization [13]. The level of fear varies among patients, with one study reporting that 60–80% of surgical patients experience fear that adversely affects the surgical process [14]. The stress hormones released in response to fear activate the autonomic nervous system, producing symptoms such as sweating, flushing, crying, and pallor. Elevated preoperative fear is associated with more severe postoperative pain and greater analgesic requirements [15].

The negative physical, psychological, and social effects experienced during the surgical process [16, 17] can reduce patient comfort at every stage of care [16]. Patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy should therefore be carefully assessed for anxiety, as they may not always express their concerns openly. It is important to explain to patients who anxiety is a natural response to surgery and to evaluate their anxiety levels systematically [18, 19]. Surgical nurses play a key role in providing holistic care to patients with preoperative anxiety and fear, thereby facilitating faster recovery and improving patient comfort [20]. Interventions designed to increase comfort may have a greater-than-expected impact on both comfort and recovery in surgical patients [17].

In recent years, several nonpharmacological strategies have been developed to reduce preoperative anxiety and fear [12, 21]. These include educational interventions, virtual reality devices, and therapeutic approaches, all of which have been shown to be effective in reducing anxiety and fear during the perioperative period [12, 21]. Among these strategies, stress balls serve as a simple cognitive distraction tool to help manage anxiety [22]. The stress ball stimulates numerous nerves and muscles in the hand and surrounding area that are directly connected to the brain through mechanical pressure. This stimulation reduces stress and anxiety levels by decreasing the release of stress hormones; it can also provide relaxation and relief [23, 24]. Cognitive‒behavioral techniques (CBTs) are structured psychotherapy approaches aimed at restructuring maladaptive thought patterns and require active cognitive engagement. The literature demonstrates that CBT is particularly effective in the treatment of mental disorders such as anxiety and depression, providing long-term benefits and reducing relapse rates [25, 26].

In contrast, tactile distraction methods such as stress balls offer rapid and low-effort interventions in situations of acute stress or heightened anxiety; however, they may not yield effects as strong or enduring as those of CBT in the management of chronic or deeply ingrained stress responses. While tactile distraction can provide short-term relief through sensory modulation mechanisms, CBT targets the core cognitive processes that maintain stress and anxiety, thereby achieving greater long-term efficacy [25, 26]. Despite the growing evidence on both CBT and tactile distraction methods, research specifically examining the preoperative use of stress balls in surgical settings remains scarce. However, a review of the literature revealed no studies examining the effects of stress ball use before laparoscopic cholecystectomy on patients’ preoperative anxiety, surgical fear, and comfort. Therefore, the aim of this original research was to investigate the effects of stress ball use before laparoscopic cholecystectomy on patients’ anxiety, surgical fear, and comfort during the preoperative period.

Methods

The CONSORT checklist was followed (Fig. 1). Additionally, the study protocol was registered with the ClinicalTrials.gov Protocol Registration system (NCT06345768).

Research type

This study was performed as a randomized controlled, two-branch, parallel-design, nonblinded study on patients who underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Patients meeting the inclusion criteria for the study and agreeing to participate were randomly allocated to the stress ball (intervention group) or control group. All stages of the study were completed in accordance with the Consolidated Standards for Reporting Trials (CONSORT) procedures.

Study setting and sample

This study included patients who underwent planned laparoscopic cholecystectomy in a public hospital from 03 April 2024 to 30 November 2024. The hospital where the research was performed has 350 beds. After deciding on surgery, patients were admitted to the ward one day before the planned operation and prepared for the operation. The operating room where the study was performed was divided into three sections. These are the first section, where patients are taken before the operation (preoperative waiting unit), the second section, where patients are operated on (operating room), and the third section, where patients are observed after surgery (recovery unit). Patients were given stress balls in the preoperative waiting unit. The preoperative waiting unit where the research was performed is a unit where 7 patients can be monitored on trolleys and serves an average of 100 patients per day. From 08.00 to 16.00, 1 nurse and 3 anaesthesia technicians worked in this unit.

The population for the research included all patients aged 18 years and older admitted to the general surgery ward of the hospital between the dates of the study and planned laparoscopic cholecystectomy surgery. Power analysis was performed to determine the sample number. The power analysis was conducted on the basis of the effect size (d = 0.70) reported in a similar study by Amiri et al. [27], titled “Effect of spiritual care on anxiety and fear of orthopedic surgery patients.” This effect size was considered appropriate for the primary outcome measure of anxiety level in our study. Using α = 0.05 and power (1-β) = 0.95, the required sample size was calculated with G*Power 3.1.9.2 software, resulting in a total of 90 participants. Consequently, 45 patients were included in both the intervention and control groups.

Randomization and blinding

A total of 90 patients with planned laparoscopic cholecystectomy meeting the inclusion criteria for our study were allocated to two groups: the intervention group (n = 45) and the control group (n = 45). Randomization was completed via the block randomization method. For groups with a homogeneous distribution, the computer-supported simple randomization method was used. For this purpose, 90 blocks were created via the functions at the internet address “https://www.random.org/integer-sets/”. In each block, 5 participants were assigned to the intervention group (Group B), and 5 participants were assigned to the control group (Group A). In the next step, the 90 blocks from 1 to 90 were given 9 numbers via the “RANDOMBETWEEN” function in the Excel application, and 9 blocks were randomly determined for use in randomization. The blinding method was not applied during the study. However, support was received from an independent expert statistician for the analysis of the data and to ensure the accuracy of the results. The statistician was included in the study with the aim of performing data analysis in an objective and reliable way.

Data collection tools

Data for the research were collected via the Patient Description Form, Surgical Fear Questionnaire, Visual Analog Scale-Anxiety (VAS-A) and General Comfort Questionnaire.

Patient description form

This form, prepared by the researchers in light of the literature, inquiries about variables related to the sociodemographic features of the patients (age, gender, educational status, marital status, employment status, smoking and alcohol use, etc.) [7, 20, 28].

Surgical fear questionnaire

This tool was developed by Theunissen et al. [29] with the aim of determining the level of fear that emerges due to surgical procedures related to the short-term and long-term outcomes of surgery. The scale had Turkish validity and reliability, as examined by Bağdigen and Karaman Özlü [30]. The scale comprises 8 items with 11-point Likert ratings ranging from 0 (I am not afraid at all) to 10 (I am very afraid) for each item. On the scale, with two subscales comprising four items each, items 1 to 4 measure fear of short-term outcomes of surgery, and items 5 to 8 measure fear of long-term outcomes of surgery. The Surgical Fear Questionnaire provides subscale scores by adding the scores for each item on the short-term (SFQ-S) and long-term (SFQ-L) subscales, and total scores are obtained by adding the subscale scores. The scores of the subscales range from 0 to 40, and the total scores range from 0 to 80. As scores increase, patients’ fear also increases. A Turkish validity‒reliability study of the questionnaire reported a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.93 [30]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha value was found to be 0.99.

Visual analog scale-Anxiety scale (VAS-A)

In this study, the VAS-A was used to evaluate the anxiety of patients with planned laparoscopic cholecystectomy. This scale was developed by Cline et al. [31] and Davey et al. [32] and can be used to assess anxiety. On the scale, one end represents an individual with “most anxiety possible”, and the other end represents “no anxiety”. It comprises a 10 cm horizontal line where an individual may mark their position between these two endpoints.

General comfort questionnaire

The General Comfort Questionnaire (Kolcaba, 2003) was developed on the basis of comfort theory, created by Kolcaba in 1992, with the aim of detecting the comfort levels of patients [33]. The scale is structured on the basis of a taxonomic framework encompassing three levels and four dimensions of the comfort component. It can be applied to identify comfort needs, evaluate nursing interventions aimed at increasing comfort levels, and achieve expected results related to improved comfort. The Turkish validity and reliability study was performed by Kuğuoğlu and Karabacak [34]. The general comfort questionnaire consists of 48 items that collect information on three different subscales: Relaxation (17 items), Relief (16 items), and Overcoming (15 items). Responses to the items on the Likert-type scale range from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). Twenty-four items contain positive statements, and 24 contain negative statements. The minimum score on the scale is 48, and the maximum score is 192. The overall score is calculated by summing the scores from all the items, and the average score is obtained by dividing the total score by the number of items. As the scale scores increase, the level of comfort increases. The Turkish version of the scale was developed by Kuğuoğlu and Karabacak in 2008, and Cronbach’s alpha was found to be 0.85 [34]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated as 0.756.

Data collection

Data were collected from patients meeting the inclusion criteria with planned laparoscopic cholecystectomy in the general surgery clinic of a public hospital. Patients were informed about the research and provided consent. In accordance with randomization, patients were subsequently given the patient description form, surgical fear questionnaire, visual analog scale-anxiety questionnaire and general comfort questionnaire nearly 40 min before the operation in the preoperative waiting unit (pretest). These forms were applied to all patients included in the research by the researchers via the face‒to‒face interview technique.

Stress ball group (Intervention group)

The researcher obtained 6 cm-diameter, moderate hardness, compressible balls made of silicone material in similar colors, mainly blue and green tones, some of which combined both colors featuring an Earth motif, before the study. After patients in the intervention group applied the scale tools in the pretest, the intervention stage began. Patients were given stress balls. When patients were given information about the stress ball application, they were called squeeze balls with the aim of not inferring that the process was stressful. The participants were given the right to choose the stress ball (color and pattern preference). Patients were taught to count from one to three and to squeeze and relax the ball and were told to continue in this way until the end of the intervention. Additionally, they were requested to pay attention to the squeeze ball and squeeze with focus. The participants were asked to repeat this process for a total of 30 min, and the researcher remotely observed them during the intervention to ensure that the stress ball was used correctly and safely. After completing the intervention for thirty minutes, the patients again completed the surgical fear questionnaire, VAS-A and general comfort questionnaire (posttest). The stress balls were provided specifically to each patient. Patients were allowed to select their stress balls. The balls were not taken back from the patients.

Control group

Patients included in the control group had no intervention performed after completing the scale tools on the pretest, and 30 min later, the Surgical Fear Questionnaire, VAS-A and General Comfort Questionnaire were administered (posttest). Patients in the control group were not given stress balls for the pretest or posttest. No intervention was applied to patients in the control group. The scales were completed by researchers via face‒to-face interviews in the preoperative waiting unit.

Data analysis

When the findings obtained in the study were analysed, the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27 program (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analyses. The skewness and kurtosis coefficients were calculated to determine whether the scores obtained for continuous variables had a normal distribution. The calculated skewness and kurtosis values were in the interval from − 1.5 to + 1.5. The categorical variables are presented as frequencies (n, %), and the continuous variables are presented as the means and standard deviations. For group comparisons, an independent samples t test was used to compare two groups, and a paired samples t test was used for within-group repeated measures. Comparisons of qualitative variables were performed via the Pearson chi-square test. To determine the effects of the independent variables on the dependent variables, a stepwise multiple linear regression model was used. The results were evaluated in the 95% confidence interval, whereas significance was evaluated below p < 0.05 (two-sided).

Ethical considerations

Before beginning the research, written permission was obtained from the Samsun University Non-Invasive Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: GOKAEK 2024/3/4, Date: 31/01/2024), from the institution where the study was conducted. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to data collection, all participants were informed about the study, and written informed consent was obtained. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed that they could withdraw at any time without affecting their medical care. All patient data were stored on an encrypted computer accessible only to the research team. In compliance with local regulations, the data will be retained for two years and then permanently destroyed.

Among the patients, 66 (73%) were married, 59 (66%) had a high school education or higher, 50 (56%) were actively employed, 34 (38%) had a diagnosed chronic disease, and 46 (51%) had previous surgical experience. When the distribution of harmful habits was investigated, 49% of patients smoked, whereas 23% consumed alcohol (Table 1).

Comparisons between groups

Measures before the implementation found no statistically significant difference in the degree of surgical fear, anxiety and general comfort level among the groups (p > 0.05). Measures after implementation revealed that surgical fear (t = 8.607; p < 0.001) and anxiety (t = 10.776; p < 0.001) were significantly lower and that general comfort levels (t = 15.765; p < 0.001) were greater in the intervention group. When the surgical fear subscales were investigated, the intervention group had lower short- and long-term surgical fear scores than did the control group did (t = 8.480 and 8.613; p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Comparisons within groups

In the intervention group, after the implementation, patients experienced a -22.91 [95% CI=-26.63; -19.19] unit fall in total surgical fear and a -3.58 [95% CI=-4.15; -3.00] unit fall in anxiety, with a 0.64 [95% CI = 0.54; 0.75] increase in comfort compared with before the implementation (t = 12.415 to 12.749; p < 0.001). In the control group, there was a 3.16 [95% CI = 1.41; 4.90] unit increase in surgical fear, a 0.53 [95% CI = 0.28; 0.79] increase in anxiety and a -0.11 [95% CI=-0.17; -0.05] unit decrease in comfort after implementation (t = 3.640 to 4.249; p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Stepwise multiple linear regression model

Before intervention

According to the results of the stepwise multiple linear regression model, before the intervention, the independent variable that reduced the general comfort of patients was anxiety (β=-0.379; t=-3.843 and p < 0.001); the independent variable that increased anxiety was surgical fear (β = 0.967; t = 35.701 and p < 0.001). When the R2 values for the model were investigated, anxiety explained 14.4% of the variation in comfort levels, and surgical fear explained 93.4% of the variation in anxiety levels (Table 3).

After intervention

According to the results of the stepwise multiple linear regression model, the use of a stress ball increased patient comfort (β = 0.812; t = 13.032 and p < 0.001) and reduced anxiety (β=-0.815; t=-13.196 and p < 0.001) and surgical fear (β=-0.818; t=-13.215 and p < 0.001). Additionally, it was determined that having previous surgical experience (β = 0.138; t = 2.227 and p = 0.029) increased surgical fear. When the R2 values were investigated, the use of the stress ball explained 66% of the increase in comfort levels and the reductions in anxiety and surgical fear of patients (Table 3).

Discussion

Undergoing surgery is an experience causing worry and fear in patients. Preoperative fear is defined as an emotion that emerges in many patients waiting to undergo surgery. Some patients may experience fear as a result of uncertainty. Additionally, this may be due to thoughts such as being bedridden postoperatively, being dependent on caregivers, experiencing pain, waking during surgery or not being able to wake postoperatively. Waiting during the preoperative period and being separated from family members during this process may also affect anxiety [10, 35, 36]. Although laparoscopic cholecystectomy is a minimal invasive surgery, patients experience anxiety and fear in the preoperative period [37].

In this study, the use of a stress ball in the preoperative period before cholecystectomy was determined to have a positive effect on short-term surgical fear and long-term surgical fear. Before the intervention, fear levels were high in both the intervention and control groups, whereas after the intervention, fear levels in the intervention group were moderate. There were no studies related to the use of a stress ball preoperatively. Similarly, Menevşe and Yayla [12] applied the emotional freedom technique to patients before laparoscopic cholecystectomy and reported that both the long-term and short-term fears of patients were reduced. As the emotional freedom technique reduces anxiety and fear, assisting patients in relaxing, the effect of the stress ball may involve a similar relaxing and fear-reducing mechanism. In this study, the stress ball relaxed the motor muscles and distracted the patients, which may have been effective in reducing surgical fear. H1a was confirmed in this study.

In this study, the use of a stress ball before cholecystectomy surgery was determined to have a positive effect on anxiety. In both groups before the intervention, anxiety levels were high, whereas after the intervention, anxiety levels in the intervention group were moderate. The use of a stress ball appears to be effective in reducing anxiety about different surgical interventions. The use of a stress ball during gynecological surgery [38], during angiography [22], and during endoscopy [39] was determined to reduce anxiety. In these studies, patients appeared to use the stress ball while waiting for surgery under local anaesthesia to end. In this study, patients used stress balls while waiting for surgery to begin in the waiting room for cholecystectomy surgery. While waiting, one in four patients is known to experience severe anxiety [40]. The use of a stress ball appears to be a suitable method to reduce the anxiety and fear of patients during this process. On the other hand, watching virtual reality-supported videos [21], listening to music [41], and aromatherapy [42] as a distraction method before cholecystectomy surgery has been shown to reduce the anxiety level of patients. Compared with these methods, the effectiveness of the stress ball is thought to be due to its sensory distraction feature. Squeezing the stress ball creates a tactile sensation that allows patients to exert direct control over the object. This, in turn, increases their sense of empowerment. This can be explained by mechanisms such as tactile stimulation and the promotion of muscle relaxation through the activation of sensory nerves in the hands, all of which may help regulate the nervous system and reduce anxiety [23, 38]. Furthermore, while cognitive‒behavioral techniques require training, stress balls are an inexpensive, safe and accessible method that can be used in the short-term preoperative period [23, 39]. It has been found to be effective and functional because it can distract patients during the preoperative period. Thus, H1b was confirmed.

In this study, the use of a stress ball before cholecystectomy surgery was determined to increase the comfort levels of patients. Before the intervention, patients in both the intervention and control groups had moderate levels of comfort, whereas after the intervention, the comfort levels of the intervention group increased. Kasar et al. [28] reported that the use of a stress ball for hemodialysis patients did not cause a change in their comfort levels. The reason for this may be that it was applied to a group with more chronic diseases. Comfort is affected by diverse factors. Most patients in our study did not have chronic disease, and this situation may have affected the comfort of the patients. Additionally, the timing of the intervention may have been effective. As a result, interventions performed in this process may provide more rapid outcomes. There was no study encountered which used a stress ball to increase comfort before cholecystectomy surgery. The H1c hypothesis in this study was confirmed.

Comfort is defined as (1) calming of distress or sadness, (2) being relieved of distress, (3) being a comforting person or thing, (4) relaxing without worry, (5) facilitating life and (6) reducing grief by uplifting, calming, or inspiring hope. The four stages affected by comfort are (1) physical, (2) psychospiritual, (3) environmental and (4) sociocultural [43]. Comfort theory advocates for the need for nurses to intervene with a holistic approach to meet the needs of patients because comfort is affected by psychological factors [44]. In this study, the comfort levels of patients appeared to be negatively impacted by anxiety. Similarly, studies have shown that in the preoperative period, patients worry about topics such as the noise of devices and apparatus [44] and the feeling of pain [45]. These concerns negatively affect the comfort of patients [44]. In this study, it appeared that surgical fear was a source of preoperative stress. Similarly, studies have shown that surgical fear (uncertainty, complications, loss of control, pain, etc.) and preoperative anxiety are associated [10, 11]. Additionally, a meta-analysis by Abate et al. [35] revealed that patients with a fear of complications had four times greater anxiety. In this study, it appears that previous surgery was a factor in reducing fear. With the reduction in the number of unknowns, patients’ fear response may have been reduced.

Strengths and limitations

One of the strengths of this study is that it is the first study to assess the effect of the use of a stress ball during the preoperative period. Furthermore, these findings are expected to enhance the available literature and increase awareness of this topic. This study has several limitations that should be considered. The anxiety scale subjectively evaluates the anxiety levels of patients, which may be susceptible to social desirability bias in self-reported responses. Future studies should use objective measurement methods such as cortisol levels, heart rate and blood pressure. Second, the study was conducted exclusively with patients who underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy at a single center, which may restrict the applicability of the results to other surgical populations or settings. Differences in patient demographics, surgical methods, and institutional practices could lead to variations in outcomes. Therefore, further research in diverse surgical contexts and multiple centers is needed to confirm these findings. Third, owing to the nature of the intervention, complete blinding of participants was not possible, creating a potential bias that could influence subjective outcomes such as anxiety and comfort. Implementing assessor or data analyst blinding in future studies could help reduce this potential bias. Finally, this study monitored the short-term effects and did not evaluate long-term outcomes. This situation comprises a limitation in terms of observing the permanent effects of the intervention.

Conclusion

This study emphasized that the use of a stress ball during the preoperative period plays an important role in reducing surgical fear and anxiety and increasing the comfort levels of patients. These findings reveal that the use of a stress ball is a simple, low-cost and effective method. Intense stress and anxiety experienced in the preoperative period increase patients’ fears about the surgical process, and this situation may lead to a decrease in comfort levels. However, the construction of the stress ball, which facilitates focus and reduces tension, assisted patients in more calmly passing through this process. In nursing practice, the popularization of this type of nonpharmacological method may significantly contribute to reducing the negative emotions experienced by patients in the surgical process and improving general health outcomes. The use of a stress ball in the preoperative period should be included more in nursing care as an effective, economic and easily applied tool to manage fear and anxiety. From a clinical standpoint, integrating stress ball use into preoperative nursing care protocols may be beneficial. Specifically, it is recommended that the intervention be initiated approximately 30 min prior to surgery preoperatively, with standardized, nurse-led instructions to ensure proper technique and adherence. The incorporation of this practice into structured preoperative education programs could enhance both patient engagement and intervention efficacy. Although these results are promising, they should be considered preliminary findings. The results obtained in this study need to be supported by further research with larger and more diverse patient groups. These studies provide a more comprehensive assessment of the effectiveness of stress balls in surgical care.
