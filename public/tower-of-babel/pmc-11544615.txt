Hydrogen-Bond-Assisted Catalysis: Hydroxylation of Paclitaxel by Human CYP2C8
Authors: Dongxiao Yue, Elvis Wang Hei Ng, Hajime Hirao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPaclitaxel (PTX, or Taxol), a chemotherapeutic agent widely employed in the treatment of various cancers, undergoes metabolic transformations through the cytochrome P450 enzymes CYP3A4 and CYP2C8. CYP3A4 catalyzes the aromatic hydroxylation reaction of PTX, whereas CYP2C8 demonstrates a distinct reactivity pattern, producing 6α-hydroxypaclitaxel via alkane hydroxylation. Despite the significant impact of PTX metabolism on its anticancer efficacy, the detailed mechanisms underlying these transformations have remained largely unclear. In this study, we employed hybrid quantum mechanics and molecular mechanics (QM/MM) calculations to elucidate the mechanism of PTX metabolism by human CYP2C8. Our QM/MM results reveal that the hydroxylation of PTX by CYP2C8 follows an atypical rebound mechanism. Either of the two hydrogen atoms at the C6 position of PTX can be abstracted, leading to a common radical intermediate. Although the subsequent rebound barrier is unusually high, stereochemical scrambling is unlikely, as the rebound barrier for the formation of the 6α-hydroxylated PTX—the actual product—is significantly lower than that for the 6β-hydroxylated metabolite. Thus, product selectivity is determined by the non-rate-determining rebound step. Furthermore, the hydroxyl group at the C7 position of PTX plays a catalytic role by facilitating the hydrogen abstraction and rebound steps. Our study also confirms a pronounced stability of the transition state in the high-spin sextet spin state, enabled by the enzyme’s specific substrate positioning.

Introduction

Paclitaxel (PTX), also known as Taxol, is a taxane-based chemotherapy agent renowned for its efficacy against various cancers, including breast and ovarian cancer.1−3 This drug operates by inhibiting microtubule disassembly, effectively disrupting cell division and thus curtailing cell proliferation. Discovered in the 1960s from the bark of the Pacific Yew tree, PTX was approved for clinical use in 1992.3 Over the years, PTX has been extensively utilized and remains a cornerstone in oncology due to its remarkable efficacy and continuous advancements in production, formulation, and delivery strategies.4−6

Like many other drugs, PTX eventually undergoes metabolic transformations within the human body. Experimental evidence indicates that the hepatic metabolic transformations of PTX are catalyzed primarily by two distinct cytochrome P450 (P450) isoforms: CYP3A4 and CYP2C8.7−10 As illustrated in Scheme 1, the metabolic sites and reaction types differ between these P450 isoforms. CYP3A4 facilitates aromatic hydroxylation at the para position of the phenyl ring connected to the 3′-carbon, resulting in the formation of 3′-p-hydroxypaclitaxel. On the other hand, CYP2C8 targets the C–H bond at the 6-carbon, catalyzing alkane hydroxylation to produce 6α-hydroxypaclitaxel.

Chemical Structures of PTX and its Metabolites in CYP3A4 and CYP2C8 Metabolism

Understanding the metabolic profiles of drugs or drug candidates is crucial for elucidating their pharmacological properties and enhancing the quality of drug discovery processes.11 However, metabolic processes generally involve complex chemical reactions, where the quantum mechanical behavior of electrons plays a much more significant role than the nonbonded protein–ligand interactions typically addressed by conventional molecular docking methods for computational drug design. Our research group has been exploring the electronic aspects of P450s in drug metabolism, examining their metabolic transformation mechanisms12 and enzyme inhibition processes.13−15

A detailed understanding of PTX metabolism is particularly important due to its strong correlation with anticancer activity, neurotoxicity, and other adverse effects.16−18 Despite its significance, mechanistic studies on the P450-catalyzed metabolism of PTX remain limited, leaving a considerable gap in knowledge. One notable exception is our recent investigation into the human CYP3A4-catalyzed aromatic hydroxylation of PTX, which utilized a hybrid quantum mechanics and molecular mechanics (QM/MM) approach. This computational study provided valuable mechanistic insights, revealing that the reaction begins with σ-complex formation, followed by the generation of either an epoxide or ketone.12 Although the mechanistic insights gained from the CYP3A4 metabolism cannot be directly applied to the distinctly different metabolic process in CYP2C8, the same computational techniques can be utilized to explore these intricate metabolic pathways. Building on our CYP3A4 study, this work delves into the mechanistic details of the CYP2C8-catalyzed metabolism of PTX, with a particular focus on the alkane hydroxylation reaction within the enzyme.

Notably, computational studies on alkane hydroxylation reactions catalyzed by various P450s, using density functional theory (DFT) and QM/MM calculations, have significantly advanced our understanding of the reactive Compound I (Cpd I) species over the past more than two decades.19−37 However, most of these computational studies have concentrated on bacterial P450s, with only a few examining drug metabolism in human P450s—a crucial function directly related to human health.24 Human P450s differ from bacterial P450s in several key aspects: their active-site volumes and substrate compounds are substantially larger, and obtaining X-ray crystal structures is more challenging, resulting in less abundant structural information. Consequently, despite the conserved catalytic cycle across organisms,38,39 the metabolic transformations of drug compounds remain much less understood. This underscores the need for further QM/MM investigations into human P450s to enhance metabolism-guided drug design.11 The study of CYP2C8-catalyzed metabolism of PTX aligns with this crucial research direction.

Computational Methods

Model Preparation

Currently, a crystal structure for the complex between CYP2C8 and PTX is not available. Therefore, we initially selected and compared four crystal structures of human CYP2C8 complexed with ligands of varying sizes from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, http://www.rcsb.org/)—specifically, PDB codes 2NNH, 2NNI, 2VN0, and 2NNJ.40 The 2NNH, 2NNI, 2VN0, and 2NNJ structures contain 9-cis retinoic acid (50 × 2 = 100 atoms), montelukast (77 atoms), troglitazone (58 atoms), and felodipine (44 atoms), respectively. Despite the different sizes of the bound ligands, these four crystal structures exhibited remarkable similarity (Figure S1), not only in the backbone structure but also in the conformations of amino acid residues. Consequently, the impact of active site flexibility on PTX binding was not deemed as significant as in the case of CYP3A4.12

For molecular docking simulations, we employed MOE 2020.41 Consistent with our previous approach,12 we utilized the AMBER10:EHT force field, the proxy triangle and London dG methods for the first step, and the rigid receptor and affinity dG methods for the second step. As anticipated, the docking simulations yielded similar results across the different structures. However, since the 2NNJ structure produced a slightly higher docking score for the highest-ranked pose, we chose to use the 2NNJ structure for molecular dynamics (MD) simulations.

MD simulations were used to refine the docking structure, following essentially the same approach as in our previous study.12 For MD simulations, we employed Amber2042 and previously reported force-field parameters for P450 intermediates.43 Additionally, we utilized the parameters for PTX that we had recently used.12,44 We performed MD simulations on the pentacoordinate ferric–heme complex and then Cpd I, both in complex with PTX. Further details of MD simulations are provided in the Supporting Information.

QM/MM Calculations

For our QM/MM mechanistic analysis of the CYP2C8-catalyzed hydroxylation of PTX, we employed the two-layer ONIOM(QM:MM) method.45,46 The initial geometry of the CYP2C8–PTX complex was derived from the above-described preparatory docking and MD simulations. To reduce computational costs associated with QM/MM calculations, solvent water molecules beyond the enzyme sphere were removed. The procedure for defining the “real system” and the “model system” for ONIOM calculations followed the same approach as in our previous study.12 Consequently, the system contained 7956 atoms in total, including 156 atoms treated quantum mechanically using DFT (Figure S4). To our knowledge, this is among the largest numbers of DFT-treated atoms in QM/MM studies of P450s to date. All ONIOM QM/MM calculations were conducted using Gaussian 16 software.47 The Gaussian-default AMBER and TIP3P parameters were used for the MM description of amino acid residues and water molecules throughout the ONIOM calculations.48,49 The force-field parameters used for PTX and the heme group are the same as in MD simulations. Several low-lying spin states (doublet, quartet, and sextet) were examined in this study. The ONIOM mechanical-embedding (ONIOM-ME) scheme, based on the B3LYP/6-31G(d) DFT method, was used for geometry optimization and vibrational frequency calculations, while the electronic-embedding (ONIOM-EE) scheme and the B3LYP/def2-TZVP DFT method were used for single-point energy calculations.50−56 For simplicity, the ONIOM energies from geometry optimization and single-point energy calculations are referred to as E1 and E2, respectively. Free-energy correction values (Gcorr) at 298.15 K and 1 atm were obtained from frequency calculations. Additionally, the DFT-D3BJ method was used to calculate the dispersion energy (Edisp) of the QM atoms in the model system.57,58 The sum of E2, Edisp, and Gcorr was defined as free energy G and utilized for comparing the relative stabilities of different species.

Results and Discussion

Binding Poses of PTX

Figure 1a highlights the two hydrogen atoms at the C6 position of PTX that are potential sites of H-abstraction. Additionally, Figure 1b displays two representative binding poses (A and B) of PTX within the active site of CYP2C8, obtained from molecular docking simulations. Previous experimental results in the literature,59,60 further confirmed by our reexamination using NMR spectroscopy (Figure S2), indicate that the metabolite formed by CYP2C8-mediated metabolism of PTX is 6α-hydroxypaclitaxel (Scheme 1). Therefore, the C–Hα bond (Figure 1a) may be considered the site of hydroxylation. However, in our molecular docking simulations, pose A was identified as a primary binding structure (Figure 1b), with the Hβ atom assuming a suitable position for hydrogen abstraction by the putative oxo ligand of Cpd I. In this pose, the Hα atom points away from the oxo ligand. In contrast, in another pose (pose B), the Hα atom is positioned closer to the oxo ligand, aligning better with the experimental findings. Despite this alignment, the docking score for pose B (−14.1 kcal/mol) is slightly lower than that for pose A (−15.5 kcal/mol). This apparent contradiction can be explained by considering the relative stability of PTX in its isolated state. Binding affinity is fundamentally a reflection of the free energy difference between the states before and after complex formation, but docking scores may not fully account for the ligand’s conformation prior to complexation. Our additional DFT calculations showed that the PTX structure in pose B is more stable than that in pose A in the isolated state by 7.9 kcal/mol (Table S1), suggesting that pose B represents a more stable binding mode. Therefore, pose B was selected for further QM/MM analysis. Although pose A does not appear to represent a catalytically relevant pathway leading to the experimentally observed 6α-hydroxypaclitaxel product, the reaction of this pose has been examined elsewhere.61

(a) Illustration of two distinct hydrogen atoms (Hα and Hβ) at the C6 position of PTX. (b) Representative poses (A and B) obtained from molecular docking simulations. Values in parentheses indicate the docking scores (in kcal/mol).

After refining the structure of pose B through MD simulations, the structure was further optimized using the QM/MM method. Figure 2a presents the optimized structure of the reactant complex (RC) between CYP2C8 Cpd I and PTX (1) in the doublet spin state. In this optimized structure, PTX is comfortably positioned within the active-site cavity of CYP2C8, demonstrating good structural complementarity. The taxane moiety is situated in a large space above the heme. The two-dimensional ligand interaction diagram in Figure 2b further shows that the benzamide group at the C3’ position of PTX is well accommodated in a small subpocket defined by residues including Ser103 and Thr107. The benzoate at C2 fits within a small space surrounded by Ser100, Pro367, and other residues. Although direct hydrogen bonds between PTX and residues are not observed, water molecules act as bridges, facilitating interactions between PTX and several surrounding residues such as Ser103, Asn204, Ala297, Glu300, and Gly365. Additionally, Ile113, Leu208, Val296, Val362, and Val366 play an important role in creating a hydrophobic environment that is crucial for substrate binding.

QM/MM optimized structure of the RC 1 in the doublet spin state. (a) A surface model is used to highlight the available space for PTX. (b) 2D ligand interaction diagram for 1.

The orientation of PTX within the active site suggests a suitable positioning of the hydrogen atoms at C6 for the subsequent C–H bond activation (Figure 2a). The distances between the hydrogen atoms and the oxo ligand are short: the O–Hα distance is 2.66 Å, and the O–Hβ distance is 2.78 Å. The O–Hα and O–Hβ distances remained stable during the MD simulation (Figure S3). A notable feature of this RC structure is a hydrogen bond formed between the hydroxyl group at the C7 position of PTX (Scheme 1) and the oxo ligand of Cpd I. The presence of this H-bond interaction raises an intriguing question about its potential impact on the H-activation reactivity during the metabolic transformation, which will be explored in detail later.

H-Abstraction Step

In general, P450-catalyzed alkane hydroxylation begins with H-abstraction from a C–H bond.19−21 PTX has two hydrogen atoms at the C6 position that can potentially be abstracted by Cpd I (Figure 1a). To assess the energy barriers associated with these two modes of H-abstraction, we examined the relevant free energy profiles. Figure 3 illustrates these profiles, showing that the H-abstraction reactions from the C–Hα and C–Hβ bonds proceed through transition states 22* and 22b*, respectively, in the doublet spin state. The barrier heights for these pathways are similar, with relative energies of 22.9 kcal/mol for 22* and 23.0 kcal/mol for 22b*. Using pure ONIOM energies (E1 or E2), calculated as E = EMM,real + EQM,model − EMM,model, these barrier heights are around 28 kcal/mol or higher, which are higher than typically observed for P450 reactions (Table S9).20,23−36 This suggests the presence of geometric strain in PTX within the enzyme active site during the reaction. However, the inclusion of dispersion and free-energy corrections together lowers the free energy barrier for the reaction (Table S6). DFT calculations using an active-site model also showed that the H-abstraction barrier is only about 10 kcal/mol, further highlighting the significant influence of the enzyme active site (Table S3). A detailed examination of the PTX structure reveals that the Hα and Hβ atoms occupy the equatorial and axial positions of the cyclohexane ring, respectively, resulting in slightly different local electronic environments for these hydrogen atoms. Consequently, abstracting a hydrogen atom from the equatorial position via 22* was calculated as more favorable in terms of E1 and E2 values (Table S6). Species 22b* is stabilized by its relatively small Gcorr value, likely reflecting the higher flexibility of this transition-state structure. As a result, these two transition states exhibit comparable stability, and H-abstraction could occur at either C–H bond. Deuterium experiments may help verify this theoretical prediction.

QM/MM-calculated free energy profiles (in kcal/mol) for the H-abstraction from the C–Hα and C–Hβ bonds.

Figure 4 displays the optimized structures of transition states 2* and 2b*. The C···H···O segment in both transition states deviate somewhat from linearity, with bond angles (∠C–H–O) of 150.4° in 22* and 147.6° in 22b*. After transition state 22b*, we obtained intermediate 23b, which is less stable than 23 by 5.3 kcal/mol (Table S6). A closer inspection of these intermediates shows that 23b is essentially a rotamer of 23, differing only in the orientation of the hydroxyl ligand of the FeOH moiety (Figure S5). Therefore, 23b can readily convert to 23, indicating that both H-abstraction pathways converge to form the common intermediate 23. Consequently, in this mechanistic scenario, the H-abstraction step does not determine the α/β-selectivity.

Overall Reaction Pathways for Hydroxylation

Figure 5 presents the complete free energy profiles for the C–Hα hydroxylation reaction at the C6 position of PTX. The reaction follows a rebound mechanism, with H-abstraction identified as the rate-determining step.62,63 At the H-abstraction transition state (2*), the three examined spin states display similar stability: the sextet species has the lowest energy at 20.9 kcal/mol, followed by the quartet state at 22.3 kcal/mol and the doublet state at 22.9 kcal/mol. Typically, H-abstraction in the sextet spin state benefits from an exchange-enhanced mechanism, whereby one electron in the C–H σ bond orbital (ϕ) shifts to the σ*z2 orbital of iron(IV)-oxo, resulting in five unpaired electrons in the d-block (Scheme S1).22 While this exchange-enhanced mechanism reduces the energy barrier, sextet transition states are often calculated to be slightly higher in energy compared to their lower-spin counterparts. In contrast, our recent study on CYP3A4-catalyzed aromatic hydroxylation of PTX demonstrated that the doublet and sextet transition states exhibit relative energies of 17.3 and 16.9 kcal/mol, respectively, showing that the sextet state is marginally more stable than the doublet state.12 In the current system, the sextet transition state’s higher stability is even more pronounced, with 62* lying a few kcal/mol lower in energy than the other transition states. This suggests a higher likelihood of the involvement of the sextet state in the P450 reactivity in the current system compared to others. The enhanced stability of the sextet transition state can be attributed to the steric environment within the active site. The substrate is positioned and oriented such that the reactive C–H bond approaches the iron(IV)-oxo species directly from above, enhancing the overlap of the σ*z2 orbital of iron(IV)-oxo with the ϕ orbital of the C–H bond. This configuration is advantageous for the stability of the sextet state during the H-abstraction process.22

QM/MM-calculated free energy profile (in kcal/mol) for the CYP2C8-catalyzed PTX hydroxylation.

Another notable observation in the energy diagram presented in Figure 5 is the relatively high rebound barriers. Typically, DFT and QM/MM calculations predict very low rebound barriers for P450-catalyzed alkane hydroxylation reactions.23,25−31,34 Specifically, the doublet state often exhibits zero or minimal barriers, although Hui et al. recently reported relatively high rebound barriers of about 6 kcal/mol in the P450BM3-catalyzed hydroxylation of a cyclic terpene substrate in the doublet spin state (Table S9).31 In the case of the other higher spin states, barriers are generally a few kcal/mol at most.21−26 In the current system, the rebound barrier is around 3 kcal/mol without the protein environment (Table S3). In contrast, the QM/MM-calculated rebound barriers are significantly higher than typically observed, consistently surpassing 12 kcal/mol across all spin states. These notably heightened rebound barriers are attributed to the steric constraints imposed by the protein surrounding in CYP2C8, which increase the energy required for the rebound step.

A finite rebound barrier can potentially result in the scrambling of the stereochemistry in the product.63 To delve deeper into this issue, we examined the pathway for the formation of the 6β-hydroxylated metabolite from 23 via rebound. Figure 6 compares the rebound pathways toward the product complexes containing 6α-hydroxypaclitaxel (25) and 6β-hydroxypaclitaxel (25b). The figure shows that the rebound barrier for the alternative pathway in the doublet spin state via 24b* is 15.9 kcal/mol, significantly higher than the rebound barrier for the 6α-hydroxylated metabolite formation (12.3 kcal/mol). These differing barrier heights prevent the scrambling process, explaining why only the 6α-hydroxylated metabolite is obtained experimentally. The dissociation of the radical intermediate from the active site is also unlikely, as the large substrate is held tightly by nonbonded interactions (Figure 2).64 Product complex 25b was calculated to be more stable than 25 by a few kcal/mol. Additional DFT calculations for the isolated hydroxylated PTX molecules showed that 6α-hydroxypaclitaxel is slightly more stable than 6β-hydroxypaclitaxel. These results indicate that the thermodynamic stability of these isomers is not significantly different (Table S2). Figure 7 illustrates the optimized structures of the transition states (24* and 24b*, respectively) for the formation of 6α-hydroxylated and 6β-hydroxylated metabolites. Notably, the length of the forming C–O bond in 24* (2.40 Å) is longer than in 24b* (2.31 Å), indicating that the transition state is earlier and thus more stable in the case of 6α-hydroxylated metabolite formation, further corroborating the experimental findings.

Catalytic Role of the Hydroxyl Group

Next, we examined the catalytic role of the hydroxyl group at the C7 position of PTX, which forms a hydrogen bond with the oxo ligand of Cpd I (Figure 2a). To discern the effect of this hydrogen bond, we prepared a modified dehydroxylated model of 1 in which the hydroxyl group is replaced by hydrogen (1h). Using this model, we explored the CYP2C8-catalyzed hydroxylation process in the doublet spin state (Figure 8). The first step of the reaction again involves H-abstraction. The figure demonstrates that the H-abstraction barrier for this dehydroxylated substrate is 26.3 kcal/mol, which is noticeably higher than that for the hydroxylated counterpart (22.9 kcal/mol, see Figures 3 and 5). A previous QM/MM study demonstrated that H-bonding of a water molecule to the oxo ligand of P450 Cpd I has a catalytic influence on H-abstraction processes, lowering their barrier heights by about 4 kcal/mol.65 Here, we observe a similar catalytic role of the hydroxyl group within the substrate itself, which lowers the H-abstraction barrier by 3.4 kcal/mol. Interestingly, a prior systematic experimental study highlighted that the hydroxyl group at C7 plays an insignificant role in the inhibition of microtubule disassembly, as its esterification did not significantly alter the inhibitory activity.66 Thus, while the C7 hydroxyl group does not seem to play a major role in binding to the β-tubulin subunit of microtubules, our QM/MM results suggest that it has a substantial impact on the CYP2C8-catalyzed metabolic process of PTX by lowering the barrier for the rate-determining step. These findings suggest that this hydroxyl group holds the key to modulating the metabolic profile of PTX in the metabolism by CYP2C8.

QM/MM-calculated free energy profile (in kcal/mol) for the reaction of a PTX analogue in which the –OH group at the C7 position is replaced by –H.

This computational experiment also revealed that the energy gap between the transition states for the rebound steps to form the 6α- and 6β-hydroxylated products (3.5 kcal/mol) remains largely unaffected by replacing the OH group with H in PTX. Therefore, the hydrogen-bond effect of the hydroxyl group does not influence product selectivity. However, comparing the rebound barriers for the two substrates (Figures 6 and 8) shows that the hydrogen bond significantly lowers the rebound barrier by 4.4 kcal/mol. The reduced rebound barrier with the hydroxylated substrate (PTX) can be attributed to the “pull” of the substrate radical toward the hydroxide ligand over iron, possibly enhanced by an electric field effect.67−69 Overall, the hydroxyl group in PTX plays a crucial catalytic role throughout the reaction, facilitating both the H-abstraction and rebound steps. The predicted reduced reactivity of the dehydroxylated substrate could be validated through further experiments.

Conclusions

In summary, our QM/MM calculations revealed an atypical rebound mechanism in the CYP2C8-catalyzed alkane hydroxylation of PTX, a key metabolic process in PTX chemotherapy. A distinctive catalytic effect of the hydroxyl group at the C7 position of PTX was identified, as this hydroxyl group significantly lowers both the H-abstraction and rebound barriers through hydrogen bonding with Cpd I, highlighting its critical role in modulating the metabolic profile of PTX. Further evidence of noncanonical behavior is seen in the significantly higher rebound barriers for the formation of 6α-hydroxypaclitaxel and 6β-hydroxypaclitaxel compared to typical P450 reactions. Despite these elevated rebound barriers, the distinct barrier heights for these processes ensure that product stereochemistry is determined at this non-rate-determining step, effectively preventing stereochemical scrambling. Additionally, the specific positioning and orientation of PTX within CYP2C8 notably stabilizes the pathway in the high-spin sextet state for H-abstraction to an unprecedented extent. This study represents a significant advancement in understanding the complex chemistry of human P450s and lays the foundation for more precise, metabolism-based drug design.
