Dataset for the study of gender disparities in publishing on energy
Authors: Matúš Mišík, Veronika Oravcová, Kateryna Yakovenko, Raffaele Mattera, Péter Vakhal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGender differences are studied in academic literature from various angles, including the variances in publishing strategies between male and female authors. In many research communities, the quantity and quality of publications are detrimental to career development, academic position, and other factors. The quality of publications is, however, difficult to evaluate, and the impact factor of a journal is often considered a proxy for the journal’s quality. This article presents a dataset of 1842 articles published between 2004 and 2017 on the topic of energy in Central and Eastern Europe in journals with assigned impact factors and listed in the Web of Science database. Previously published studies relied on machine-assisted assignment of gender, which has limited accuracy in assigning gender. We have manually collected English-language articles and coded the gender of their authors to overcome these challenges. This dataset presents data on articles (journal in which they were published, year of publication, impact factor of the journal, research area, etc.), as well as their authors (name of the first author and their gender, number of co-authors, names of all co-authors) and their gender. The article “Gender and authorship in energy studies: Is there an impact?” [1] uses this data as the principal source for its analysis.Keywords: Gender, Energy, Impact factor, Disparities, Academia, Research

Background

The role of gender in academia has been studied from various perspectives, including collaboration patterns [2], representation imbalances [3], career advancement [4], salaries [5], and numerous other aspects [6]. However, limited attention has been given to gender issues in connection with publishing, although several articles have been written on this topic (for example, [7,8]). This is somewhat surprising, given that gender disparities have been identified in existing research [9] and by journal editors [10].

One issue articles examining gender in academic settings struggle with is the machine-assisted assignment of gender. This process utilises pre-existing lists of first names with assigned genders [11] and is thus limited to those social and geographical areas that are covered by such lists. Moreover, it is unable to handle initials or first names that are assigned to different genders in different countries (for example, Andrea; see below). This results in a limited ability of this approach to assign gender correctly [12,13].

To overcome these challenges, our research team manually coded the dataset. We coded the gender of all authors, as well as the main characteristics of the articles. This allowed us to develop a unique dataset that can be used not only to examine gender issues in energy studies but also to address methodological questions related to the differences between manual coding and machine-assisted gender assignment. This can help develop tools to improve gender assignment with the help of automatic tools, making the time-consuming manual assignment of gender obsolete.

Data Description

The raw data unequivocally demonstrate that the number of male authors publishing within the field of energy studies significantly exceeds that of female authors (Fig. 1). Initial observations suggest that, on average, men publish in journals with higher impact factors than women. This disparity persists even following logarithmic transformation of the data. However, the situation is considerably more nuanced; when examining distributions across various breakdowns, they clearly exhibit a bimodal shape for both genders. This indicates a form of clustering or segmentation that may be independent of the lead author's gender (Fig. 2).

Our own investigations [1] also suggest that researchers can be divided into two primary cohorts (presumably based on experience): those currently publishing in lower-tier journals, and those who typically place their scientific communications in outlets with higher impact factors. The potential for mobility between these two groups appears to be relatively constrained. Our study [1] presented only a fraction of the possible analytical methodologies. The available data provide opportunities for performing segmentation (clustering), mixed-effects models, quantile or ordinal regressions, as well as other ANOVA-based analyses.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The dataset encompasses all publications on energy in countries of Central and Eastern Europe, published between 2004 and 2017, in journals with an impact factor indexed in the Web of Science database. The period from 2004 to 2017 was selected to reflect the post-EU accession phase for most CEE countries, during which funding structures, international collaboration patterns, and publication incentives changed significantly. The impact factor is for the year 2017, i.e., the last year of the data collection period, and represents a five-year impact factor.

We have chosen the 2017 impact factor for several reasons. This was the last year of our database, so it had the newest impact factor and therefore served as a single reference point for all articles included in the analysis. Moreover, there has been a significant ‘inflation’ in the impact factor during the studied period. A direct comparison of individual years would be problematic, as high values in one year may appear low in another. While this would still allow us to compare journals within a given year, it would be more problematic to make comparisons across the whole examined period. One possible alternative would be to work with ranks of individual journals; however, this would pose additional challenges (for example, an increased number of journals included in the database each year). Using the 2017 impact factor allowed us to assess the journal's performance and compare articles across the entire examined period. We acknowledge the challenges to choosing this year and therefore include them among the limitations of the dataset.

We collected the data using two keyword sets in the Web of Science search engine. The first word was always ‘energy,’ and the second was the name of a country from the region (Bulgaria, Croatia, the Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia) or an adjective (e.g., ‘Bulgarian’). We have also used a set of words describing the region and its parts during our search (Balkans, Baltic, Baltics, Central Europe, CEE, CEEC, Visegrad, V4). We acknowledge that this strategy may overlook broader multinational analyses where the region is included but not explicitly named (for example, in EU-level comparisons where the CEE region is included but not explicitly named). It also focuses on English-language publications in indexed journals, which may favour authors more integrated into Western-oriented academic networks.

In this way, we manually created 30 partial databases (11 based on names of the countries, 11 based on their adjectives and eight based on the names connected to the region) based on the results of the searches in the Web of Knowledge that we manually checked (the database did not enable direct export of data when we collected the data, as it does nowadays). We included only articles in journals with an impact factor. We excluded articles written in languages other than English and those that did not examine energy as a market commodity, but instead viewed energy from a medical, biological, or particle physics perspective. Altogether, we collected 1842 articles from 416 journals.

We acknowledge potential biases in how we processed our data. We made the assumption that we can determine the gender of an author based on their name, taking into consideration national specificities. For example, in Italy, the name Andrea is typically a male name, whereas in Slovakia, it is often used as a female name. Similarly, Ivica and many other names are typically assigned to different genders in various countries. We assumed that we would be able to do this not only for European names, but also for people from other regions. Although our dataset includes articles on Central and Eastern Europe, scholars worldwide have been writing extensively on this region.

Thus, we coded authors’ gender based on their first name. This was, in most cases, without any problem. However, when we were uncertain due to regional and/or linguistic variation, we consulted publicly available institutional information: we visited the academic profiles of those authors on their institutes’ websites, searched for their CVs or bios, and consulted their photos and personal pronouns posted and used on these websites. To minimise the possibility of error, two researchers coded the gender of all authors. In cases of disagreement or uncertainty, the coders discussed the case and revisited available sources before assigning the final code. Although we hope we have correctly assigned gender to all authors in this manner, our dataset provides full names of all authors, which are clearly linked to a particular publication, allowing future researchers to verify the assigned gender. We acknowledge that such an approach has its limitations; we discuss them in the following section.

Limitations

We understand that our dataset is limited in terms of time, space, and the research area it covers. Therefore, it must be utilised in such a way that it is understood that the dataset covers only a limited number of observations. When it came to coding, we used first names, which was, in most cases, a smooth process; however, when the gender was unclear, we relied on photos and mentions of personal pronouns in CVs and bios, which could lead to inaccuracies. We, however, were unable to obtain this information from the authors themselves; only recently did self-identification of authors become part of the submission process. Also, we approached gender from a binary perspective. Although this is a common approach when examining gender issues in general, the current trend acknowledges non-binary, gender-diverse, or transgender authors [14].

Moreover, we assumed that the best way to operationalise the quality of one’s work is via the use of impact factor measurement. This is, without doubt, a controversial assumption, and we acknowledge that the impact factor is not the only scientometric tool for assessing an article's impact on knowledge and academia [15]. The impact factor is used here not as a normative measure of research quality, but as an indicator of journal-level visibility and recognition within the international system of academic publishing. Moreover, our decision to use the 2017 five-year impact factor may also pose a limitation, as this indicator does not capture each journal's impact factor for the year in which an article was published. Future research can shed light on this issue by adding publication-year impact factors or year-specific journal ranks to the database.

Lastly, due to the research design, we were unable to examine book chapters, books, and journals in non-indexed journals or those indexed, but without an impact factor. This, however, is connected to choosing the impact factor as the operationalisation of international recognition of one’s research production.
