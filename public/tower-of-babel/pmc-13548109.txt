Surgical augmentation of gluteal tendon tears: a systematic review of techniques, indications and comparative clinical outcomes
Authors: Cédric Bonte, Damien Van Quickenborne, Emmanuel Audenaert, Arne Burssens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeWe performed a thorough, evidence-based systematic review evaluating and comparing the outcomes of several surgical augmentation procedures for tears of the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus in patients with GTPS.MethodsIn November 2022, a systematic search of five electronic databases was performed. The methodological quality of the eligible studies was assessed using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS). The extracted data consisted of various outcomes, such as standardized patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), hip abductor muscle strength, biomechanical variables, patient satisfaction, complications and failure rates.ResultsSeventeen articles published in 2010 or later were included, all non-comparative and of low methodological quality (level of evidence IV). All studies performed open surgical augmentation of gluteal tendon tears with a variety of augmentation methods. The majority of clinical and functional outcomes improved significantly following augmentation surgery. Conflicting evidence was present regarding the association between preoperative fatty infiltration and postoperative outcomes. There was a substantial variability in the reporting of tear severity and tendon involvement. Retear rates ranged from 0 to 8.7% in 16 studies, with one outlier of 54.5%. The overall complication rate, excluding retears, ranged from 0 to 8.3%.ConclusionWe observed encouraging results following various augmentation procedures, validating the efficacy of these techniques. The heterogeneity regarding several methodological aspects complicated a qualitative comparison of the included studies. Future prospective, comparative studies should incorporate GTPS-specific, standardized rehabilitation protocols and outcome measures. Investigating correlations between tear severity or fatty degeneration and postoperative outcomes should be prioritized.

Introduction

Greater trochanteric pain syndrome (GTPS) is a clinical entity that encompasses several causes of lateral hip pain. Most studies demonstrate that it has a high prevalence among middle-aged, post-menopausal women and is characterized by several risk factors and associated conditions. GTPS is a prevalent and severely debilitating disorder impacting work participation, physical activity and quality of life, and therefore, it could be considered equally important as osteoarthritis (OA) (1). Because many of these conditions present themselves as persistent lateral hip pain together with tenderness during palpation of the peritrochanteric region, GTPS is often wrongfully simplified as trochanteric bursitis (2). However, gluteal tendinosis and tears have recently been recognized as an important cause of GTPS (3).

The gluteus medius (GMed) and gluteus minimus (GMin) tendons are exposed to compressive forces at their insertion on the greater trochanter during abduction, causing microtraumata and potentially evolving into a chronic tendinopathy over time. Matrix degradation in chronic tendinopathies diminishes the tendons’ tensile load-bearing capacity predisposing them to partial- or full-thickness tears, even at relatively low physical demands. In addition to being a consequence of chronic gluteal tendinopathy, gluteal tendon tears may also occur due to iatrogenic factors, including intraoperative damaging of the superior gluteal nerve or postoperative failure of the abductor muscle repair, both known complications of total hip arthroplasty (THA) (4). Over time, chronic retraction, muscle atrophy, and fatty infiltration may develop, making surgical repair more technically challenging and reducing the potential for biological healing.

Surgical treatment is generally considered in recalcitrant cases of GTPS where conservative management fails to adequately restore function and relieve pain (5). Multiple open and arthroscopic interventions have been described for the repair or reconstruction of gluteal tendon tears in patients with native or prosthetic hips. The surgical intervention of choice depends on several preoperative determinants, such as neurological integrity and fatty infiltration (FI) of the gluteal muscles and the type of hip (native vs prosthetic) (6). Direct repair of the gluteal tendons has been associated with favorable outcomes, but unfavorable tissue quality, retraction of the tendon, or persistent fatty degeneration can limit the durability of the repair.

With the aim of reducing retear rates and improving tendon healing, especially for more advanced gluteal tendon tears, various augmented repair techniques have been described using synthetic grafts or allografts. These can provide biomechanical and/or biological support to promote healing of the poorly vascularized tendons (7). Despite the large variety of surgical techniques for gluteal tendon augmentation, there is currently no comprehensive synthesis dedicated specifically to augmentation and there is a lack of comparative studies evaluating the relative effectiveness of each intervention. Additionally, although these approaches have gained popularity, the indications for augmentation are not well established.

Existing systematic reviews have primarily evaluated outcomes of direct repair, with only brief descriptions of augmentation techniques (8, 9). Moreover, the literature lacks clarity regarding whether augmentation improves outcomes over direct repair. Similarly, it remains unclear whether tear severity, the presence of fatty infiltration or the chronicity of symptoms influences postoperative outcomes after augmented repair. This lack of evidence hampers the ability of surgeons to refine indications for augmentation and to apply existing management algorithms such as those from Kenanidis and colleagues (10).

Therefore, the objective of this study is to perform a systematic review of the literature evaluating and comparing the present surgical techniques and outcomes of each augmentation procedure for gluteal tendon tears. Prior to the initiation of our review process, we hypothesized that the existing augmentation procedures for gluteal tendon tears would demonstrate comparable effectiveness in terms of clinical and functional outcomes. Moreover, we posited that both tear severity and the degree of fatty infiltration would significantly correlate with the outcomes of gluteal tendon augmentation surgery. Finally, this review sought to determine whether current evidence supports existing management frameworks and whether augmentation techniques offer advantages over direct repair.

Materials and methods

The Database of Abstracts of Reviews of Effects, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and PROSPERO were searched. No previously performed systematic review studies could be identified, specifically investigating surgical augmentation of gluteal tendon tears. Following a preliminary search of the existing literature, the protocol for our systematic review was pre-registered on PROSPERO, an international prospective register of systematic reviews (registration number: CRD42022375886). We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) as a guideline for this systematic literature search (11).

In November 2022, a systematic search of five electronic databases – including PubMed, Embase, Ovid MEDLINE, Cochrane Library and Science Direct – was performed by the lead reviewer (C B) for all studies reporting on augmentation of gluteal tendon tears. The following PubMed search term was constructed: (“gluteal tendon” OR “gluteus medius tendon” OR “hip abductors” OR “hip abductor tendon” OR “buttocks” OR “gluteus minimus” OR “gluteus medius” OR “hip rotator cuff”) AND (“surgery” OR “open surgery” OR “repair” OR “reconstruction” OR “augmentation” OR “hip rotator cuff repair” OR “gluteus medius repair” OR “hip abductor repair”) AND (“tendon injury” OR “tear” OR “hip abductor tear” OR “gluteus medius tendon tear”). Identical search strategies were performed on all databases implementing and combining the selected keywords.

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they reported postoperative outcomes and/or complication data on open or endoscopic surgical augmentation of gluteus medius and gluteus minimus tendon tears. There was no restriction regarding publication date, country, patient age, race or gender. Studies in patients with both native and prosthetic hips were allowed. Randomized and non-randomized controlled clinical trials, prospective and retrospective observational cohort studies, case–control studies and case series studies were included. Studies in languages other than English, French, Dutch or German were excluded. Case reports, reviews, clinical examples and expert opinions were dismissed from further analysis. Furthermore, we also excluded abstract-only papers, editorials, conferences, author responses, theses and books. Articles without available abstract or full text, duplicate studies and studies where the data were not well extracted were all excluded.

All articles identified through the electronic search of the selected databases were screened using the online review tool Rayyan (12). Following the removal of duplicates, evaluation of titles and abstracts was done to select the eligible studies that met the predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. If the abstract did not contain sufficient information for us to decide whether the article should be included, we evaluated the full-text version. The remaining articles went on to full-text review for further assessment of eligibility. Additionally, the reference lists of these articles were screened to find articles not included in the electronic search. Other members of the review team evaluated this process, and any disagreements over the eligibility of a particular study were resolved through discussion within the review team.

The risk of bias of each included article (all were non-comparative) was assessed by the lead reviewer (C B) using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS), a validated instrument for the assessment of methodologic quality. Other members of the review team evaluated these scores, and disagreements were resolved through discussion during a consensus meeting.

All data were extracted by the lead reviewer (C B) using a standardized extraction form, which was subsequently verified by the other members of the review team. The collected data consisted of study characteristics, patient demographics, condition of the hip joint, preoperative conservative treatment, pre- and/or postoperative US or MRI evaluation, classification of hip abductor tendon lesions, as well as the type of augmented surgery, outcomes, follow-up time and failure/complication rates. Clinical outcomes included patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), such as the Oxford Hip Score (OHS), (modified) Harris Hip Score ((m)HHS), visual analog scale – pain (VAS – pain), Short-Form Health Survey (SF-12/36), Victorian Institute of Sport Assessment – Gluteal (VISA – G), Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (HOOS), Hip Outcome Score (HOS), the International Hip Outcome Tool (iHOT), Merle d’Aubigné score and the Lower-Extremity Activity Scale (LEAS). Other outcome measures that were extracted were satisfaction ratings and Global Rating of Change (GRC). Functional outcome measures, such as the 6-min walking test (6MWT), maximal isometric hip abduction strength, limb symmetry index (LSI) and 30-s SLS test, were also included. If biomechanical variables were reported, these were also extracted for further analysis. Because the study outcomes were not measured according to similar metrics, the extracted data did not allow for meta-analysis and therefore a narrative synthesis was undertaken.

Results

Database search

A total of 4,308 results were obtained through searching of the electronic databases. Duplicate removal left 3,091 articles for subsequent title and abstract screening. This led to exclusion of 3,046 articles, leaving 55 manuscripts for thorough full-text reviewing. Reference screening of these articles yielded two additional articles that met our eligibility criteria. Finally, thorough scrutinizing of the full-text articles resulted in a total of 17 studies that fulfilled the inclusion criteria and hence were included in our systematic review. PRISMA flow diagram.

MINORS quality assessment

The quality of each included study (all were non-comparative) was assessed using the MINORS, resulting in an average score of 11.6/16 (range: 8–14). (Supplementary data (see section on Supplementary Materials given at the end of the article)). Through internal discussion, adequate reporting of appropriate endpoints was defined as the presence of at least one validated PROM assessing function and pain, registered pre- and postoperatively. Although most studies collected the data prospectively, the lack of prospective calculations of study size in many of the included articles negatively affected the methodological quality of their study designs. The follow-up period was deemed adequate when patients were followed up over a period of minimally 1 year. Three of the included studies failed to meet this threshold (13, 14, 15). The average follow-up time was 26 months (range: 6–72), with two studies reporting a loss to follow-up rate of more than 5% of the study population (14, 16).

Study and patient characteristics

As previously mentioned, all included studies were non-comparative. Of the 17 included studies, 11 had a prospective case series design (13, 14, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25), whereas the remaining 6 were retrospective case series, indicating that the overall level of evidence was low (15, 16, 26, 27, 28, 29). All studies were conducted between 2010 and 2022. There was a significant variability in the quantity of included patients, with a median population size of 22 patients (range: 7–142). The mean age varied from 60.3 to 76.8 years, with women making up the vast majority of patients in all studies. Reporting of body mass index revealed that the majority of patients were either overweight or obese. Ten studies performed augmented repair on native hips only (15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 29), three involved prosthetic hips only (26, 27, 28), and four studies included both patients with native and prosthetic hips (13, 23, 24, 25).

In the studies reporting the duration of symptoms preoperatively, the average period prior to augmented repair was 36.5 months (range: 18–49.2). The diagnosis of gluteal tendon tears was confirmed by preoperative imaging (MR or US) in all but two studies (26, 28). Additionally, two studies performed hip radiography in their patients, with Fehm et al. even performing a hip arthrogram (26, 27). When reported, the majority of participants had a positive Trendelenburg sign at initial clinical presentation. Preoperative treatment consisted of analgesia, corticosteroid injections and physiotherapy (15, 17, 18, 19, 20, 22, 26, 27, 29). Patients who were refractory to these conservative treatment measures were eligible for surgical augmentation when a gluteal tendon tear was present.

Surgical augmentation procedures

In all included articles, open surgical augmentation of gluteal tendon tears was performed. While we did come across technique descriptions of endoscopic augmented repair, we did not identify any adequate studies that actually included patients undergoing endoscopic augmented repair. Importantly, in three of the reviewed studies, not all patients received augmented repair (13, 16, 29). Different to the augmented repair procedures performed by all other included studies, Caviglia et al. did not restore continuity between the gluteal tendons and the greater trochanter. Instead, they used a synthetic mesh sutured to the GMed, vastus lateralis and tensor fascia lata (TFL) to reinstate continuity between these muscles without fixing the gluteal muscles to their anatomical insertion on the greater trochanter (28).

The other studies, which conducted augmented repair, can be broadly categorized into four groups according to the type of augmentation material employed, using either synthetic grafts or allografts. Augmentation using a synthetic ligament was most frequently reported (13, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 29). Three studies used an acellular human dermal allograft matrix to augment gluteal tendon repair (15, 16, 24). Three other articles described the use of a bio-inductive resorbable or nonresorbable collagen patch for augmented gluteal tendon repair (14, 23, 25). Similar to the studies reporting on augmentation with acellular human dermal allograft matrix, an on-lay augmentation was performed in all three studies by suturing the collagen patch to the repaired gluteal tendon to complete the procedure.

The fourth and final category of augmented repair methods found in the articles included in this review involved the use of fresh-frozen human tendino-osseous allografts. Drexler et al. preoperatively harvested a complete knee extensor mechanism allograft consisting of the quadriceps tendon, patella, patellar tendon and tibial tubercle (27). Conversely, Fehm et al. used an Achilles tendon allograft with attached calcaneal bone to augment gluteal tendon repair (26).

Postoperative rehabilitation protocols

The reporting of postoperative rehabilitation protocols exhibited significant variation within the studies included. The large majority of studies restricted patients to partial weight-bearing during the first 6–12 weeks following surgery before allowing gradual progression to full weight-bearing (13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 22, 23, 25, 26, 28, 29). However, in two of the included studies, patients were instructed to bear full weight with the aid of crutches as soon as tolerated after surgery (21, 24). In contrast, Drexler et al. forbid weight-bearing during the first 6 weeks postoperatively, followed by a gradual progression to full weight-bearing over a period of 10 weeks (27). Five of the 17 included studies used an abduction brace for 6–12 weeks postoperatively (14, 15, 16, 26, 27). Additionally, prohibition or avoidance of active abduction during 6–12 weeks was explicitly stated in seven studies (13, 15, 23, 24, 25, 27, 29).

Classification of tears and degree of fatty infiltration

As stated in the Introduction, both MRI and US can be used for identifying gluteal tendon tears. Importantly, factors such as tear severity, muscle atrophy and FI should be taken into account during preoperative assessment, as they could potentially impact the result of augmented gluteal tendon surgery. In our review, only one study did not report tear severity (23). Tear severity grading was done using MRI, intraoperative assessment or hip arthrography. The description of tear severity varied significantly across all studies. Despite the heterogeneity in the reporting of tear severity, we attempted to provide a uniform overview of tear severity reported in the included studies. Following a consensus meeting with the review team, we decided to consider a tear as ‘full thickness’ when 100% of the tendon thickness was torn, while partial-thickness tears were defined by less than 100% of the tendon thickness being torn. Furthermore, we differentiated between tears of the GMed and GMin, or a combination of both, whenever the study reported specific tendon involvement.

Although FI is considered as a possible prognostic factor with regard to gluteal tendon tears, only 5 of the 17 included articles analyzed and reported the presence of this degenerative phenomenon (14, 15, 20, 23, 25). Only 2 of these 5 articles investigated the correlation between FI and clinical outcomes (20, 25). Fink et al. found a statistically significant correlation between the degree of FI and both muscle force of the abductors and functional and gait scores assessed by the mHHS (25). On the contrary, Ebert et al. found no correlation between the degree of FI and PRO functional capacity, perceived improvement from surgery or satisfaction 2 years postoperatively (20).

Clinical outcomes

Various clinical outcomes were documented across all included studies, such as standardized PROMs, hip abductor muscle strength and quality, biomechanical variables, patient satisfaction, complications and failure rates.

Patient-reported outcomes

Only one study reported the Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (HOOS) (13). There was a significant improvement of the mean HOOS in all five domains. Merely two studies reported the Hip Outcome Score (HOS) and International Hip Outcome Tool (iHOT) as PROMs for evaluating surgical augmentation of gluteal tendon tears (14, 15). Browning et al. did not register preoperative PROs, and therefore, they could not calculate whether the improvement in PROs was statistically significant (15). Eight out of the 9 patients in Day et al.’s study completed pre- and postoperative PROM assessment, which showed a significant improvement in HOS Activities of Daily Life (HOS-ADL) and iHOT-33 at 6 months after surgery with each of these results meeting predefined minimal clinically important difference (MCID) criteria. There was no significant improvement in HOS Sports Subscale (HOS-SS) at 6 months postoperatively (14).

Six of the included studies used the OHS for assessing clinical outcomes following augmented gluteal tendon repair (17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22). They all observed significant improvements in OHS at 12 and/or 24 months post-surgery. The HHS appeared as a PROM in nine of the included studies (16, 18, 19, 20, 22, 23, 24, 26, 27), whereas the mHHS was used in only four studies (14, 15, 17, 25). The HHS improved significantly after surgery in all seven studies where statistical analysis of significance was performed. While Fehm et al. and Fink et al. also reported improvements in values of HHS, no calculation of statistical significance was done by them (23, 26). mHHS improved in all studies reporting this measure; however, statistical significance or adequacy of effect sizes was only reported in the articles by Ebert et al. and Day et al. (14, 17).

Apart from commonly used PROMs, such as the mHHS and OHS, one study conducted by Ebert et al. investigated the internal and external responsiveness of a GTPS-specific PROM known as the VISA-G. Similar to the mHHS and OHS, the VISA-G showed large (>1) standardized effect sizes at 3, 6 and 12 months postoperatively, confirming that the internal responsiveness of the VISA-G outcome measure was certainly adequate. Moreover, the authors observed a relatively strong correlation between the change score for the VISA-G and the change scores for the mHHS and OHS, respectively. Interestingly, the VISA-G was not subject to ceiling effects at 12 months post-surgery as opposed to the mHHS and OHS, making it a better parameter for evaluating patient recovery during long-term follow-up (17).

With hip pain being one of the most debilitating aspects of gluteal tendon tears, we analyzed which articles reported a VAS for pain. In total, 10 articles incorporated this outcome measure, with 6 of them observing a statistically significant improvement (18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 24). The other four articles did not perform statistical analysis of the observed pain improvement (15, 23, 25, 29). Requicha et al. reported that patients with full-thickness tears experienced significantly worse pain at the final follow-up (29). Alternative to a VAS for pain, Davies et al. evaluated pain by asking patients to score the percentage improvement in pain relief following surgery, reaching an average of 90% after 5 years (16). Two other studies separately reported the pain subset of the mHHS and HHS, but statistical significance was not calculated (25, 26).

Two articles used the Merle d’Aubigné score for assessing patient-reported outcomes (27, 28). Caviglia et al. observed a significant improvement in this PROM from pre- to post-surgery (28). Drexler et al. chose this PROM, modified to encompass walking aids used by their patients, to assess patient mobility. A significant improvement in mean mobility score was seen (27). Finally, the LEAS was used as a PROM in merely one study. There was a significant improvement in LEAS scores at 1 and 5 years post-surgery (16).

Abductor muscle strength

The evolution in abductor muscle strength was registered by ten studies (16, 18, 19, 20, 22, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28). Five of them conducted manual muscle strength testing, rating muscle force using the 0- to 5-point MRC scale (16, 24, 25, 26, 27). Manually assessed muscle strength improved in all five studies; however, only two of them proved the statistical significance of the observed improvement (16, 27). Differently, four studies objectively quantified maximal isometric hip abductor strength measured in an upright standing position, which was also represented in the LSI, a relative measure comparing the abductor strength of the affected and unaffected legs (18, 19, 20, 22). Both parameters significantly improved after surgery in all four studies.

Functional and biomechanical outcomes

Four of the 17 included studies were unique in their utilization of the 6MWT or 30-s SLS test to assess functional outcomes (18, 19, 20, 22). Regarding the 6MWT, all four studies observed significant improvements in 6- min walking capacity (expressed as the distance covered after 6 min of walking) at 12 and/or 24 months follow-up.

Uniquely, the study by Huxtable et al. is the only one of the included studies to quantify specific biomechanical variables, such as pelvic tilt, lateral pelvic shift, pelvis-on-femur (POF) angle and trunk lean. There was no significant improvement of these biomechanical variables when comparing preoperative and 12-month postoperative values. Although several correlations were found between the reported clinical and functional outcomes, no significant correlations between biomechanical variables and other reported outcomes could be identified (22).

Complications and failure rates

The efficacy of surgical augmentation procedures is dependent on the amount of patients experiencing retears in the years following surgery. However, two studies did not report retear rates (22, 28). Most studies only performed MRI detection of retears when patients clinically presented with symptoms suggesting surgical failure, such as increasing lateral hip pain. Five studies explicitly reported the absence of clinical retears without postoperative MRI confirmation (15, 21, 23, 24, 26). A total of seven studies reported clinical retear rates varying from 1.6 to 8.7% (13, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 29). There were only three studies where a postoperative MRI was performed in each patient to verify the integrity of the augmented hip abductors (14, 25, 27).

Besides retears, several other complications were reported by the included studies, such as hematoma, wound infections, dysesthesia and deep venous thrombosis. Assuming each complication occurred in a different patient, the overall complication rate (excluding retears) ranged from 0 to 8.3%. Notably, five studies did not mention whether there were any complications following surgery other than retears (13, 17, 22, 26, 28).

Discussion

Our study aimed to systematically review the literature to evaluate and compare the current surgical techniques and outcomes of various surgical augmentation procedures for gluteal tendon tears. The principal finding of this study is that the large majority of the included studies, using a wide array of augmentation techniques, reported significant improvements in both clinical and functional outcomes with complication and failure rates similar to those of direct repair procedures. However, the assessment of the relative effectiveness was challenging given the substantial methodological heterogeneity. Comparison across augmentation constructs revealed that synthetic ligament augmentation is the most extensively studied method and consistently produces favorable improvements in pain and function (13, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 29). Dermal allografts and collagen patches also demonstrated improvements, although the evidence is limited to small case series with variable methodologies (14, 15, 16, 23, 24, 25). Tendino-osseous allografts were used primarily in cases with severe tissue deficiency or post-arthroplasty abductor insufficiency and were associated with more variable outcomes, likely reflecting the complexity of these cases rather than the technique itself (26, 27). Overall, none of the augmentation categories demonstrated clear superiority over the others.

Additionally, the lack of standardized reporting of tear severity and extent of fatty infiltration together with the limited and conflicting evidence on this matter impeded us from rejecting or withholding our second hypothesis regarding the correlation between both tear severity and fatty infiltration and outcomes of gluteal tendon augmentation. All 17 included studies were non-randomized, non-comparative studies reflecting the low level of evidence in the current literature on this topic. This parallels other reviews regarding repair and augmentation of gluteal tendon tears (7, 8, 10). These reviewed studies were published in 2010 or later, illustrating the increasing interest in gluteal tendon tears and their surgical augmentation in recent years.

Adequate sample sizes are required to ensure enough power for detecting mean changes in outcome scores. Six of the included studies did not manage to recruit over 20 patients, limiting the statistical validity of their results (14, 15, 23, 24, 26, 27). Moreover, only six studies reported to be adequately powered in order to detect clinically meaningful changes in their primary outcome measure (17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22). Unsurprisingly, the demographic characteristics of the included patients reflected the higher occurrence of gluteal tendon tears in middle-aged to elderly women as reported in the literature (3, 30). Overall, the overrepresentation of females in all of the included studies restricts the generalizability of their results to the male population. It is worth noting that there was a significant overlap in included patients in the four studies by Ebert et al. (17, 18, 19, 20).

Variability in exclusion criteria was evident among the studies included in this review. The comparison of outcomes following repair surgery for degenerative versus iatrogenic tears after THA may be difficult (7). In this systematic review, some articles focused on augmenting gluteal tendon tears that occurred following THA, while others specifically investigated degenerative tears excluding patients with previous THA. Additionally, four studies included patients with both native and prosthetic hips; however, no separate analysis of outcomes was done for these two categories of gluteal tendon tears (13, 23, 24, 25). Moreover, two studies even performed THA simultaneous with the augmentation procedure in some of their patients (13, 29). Only one of them performed a separate analysis of patients receiving isolated gluteal tendon surgery and those undergoing gluteal tendon repair combined with THA (29). They demonstrated the importance of this distinction, as postoperative retears were more frequent in the patients receiving simultaneous THA during gluteal tendon repair. Caviglia et al. combined gluteal tendon augmentation with THA revision surgery, leading to a similar problem of potential confounding (28). The average duration of symptoms prior to surgery was over 3 years. While conservative treatment may effectively manage GTPS in a significant number of patients, it is essential for physicians to be aware that gluteal tendinopathy represents the most common cause of this condition (30, 31). Early diagnosis and timely reassessment of patients with lateral hip pain refractory to conservative treatment measures are important to avoid unnecessary delay of surgical interventions given the significant impact of GTPS on quality of life (3). Merely nine of the reviewed studies mentioned which preoperative conservative treatments the patients received (15, 17, 18, 19, 20, 22, 26, 27, 29). Moreover, detailed descriptions regarding the type and extent of conservative treatments administered to each patient were often lacking. Given the potential impact of preoperative treatment variability on surgical outcomes, preoperative treatment measures should be standardized and consistent for all included patients (7).

As mentioned in the Results section, three studies failed to meet the threshold of a minimum 1-year follow-up period in all patients (13, 14, 15). Long-term follow-up is needed to allow a correct estimation of the durability of each gluteal tendon augmentation construct. Some studies illustrated this, as several retears occurred beyond 1 year of follow-up (18, 20). Moreover, an extended follow-up period is required to validate the lasting nature of the obtained improvements in quality of life and clinical and functional outcomes. Notably, this systematic review included two studies with loss to follow-up rates of more than 5% of the study population (14, 16). Given the small size of the study population in most of the included articles, a minor loss to follow-up can quickly affect the reliability of the results. Hence, caution is needed when interpreting the results of these two studies.

Postoperative rehabilitation protocols differed significantly among the included studies, with varying instructions regarding weight-bearing restrictions, the use of abduction braces and the type and timing of passive/active hip ROM and resistance exercises. A recent systematic review formulated recommendations that provide a useful guide for therapists and may serve as the foundation for standardized reporting of postoperative rehabilitation protocols in forthcoming research (32).

The diversity of the tear severity grading systems was yet another element that complicated comparison between the included studies. A dichotomous distinction between partial- and full-thickness tears seemed to be the most simple, feasible and reproducible tear classification system. Of the 17 included studies, two investigated the correlation between tear severity and postoperative outcomes (16, 29). Davies et al. found no statistically significant difference in postoperative HHS values based on tear severity (16). Requicha et al. reported that full-thickness tears were significantly associated with higher pain scores postoperatively (29). In the current literature, other studies have also investigated the correlation between tear severity and outcomes after gluteal tendon repair. Several articles reported that there was no correlation between tear severity and postoperative outcomes after endoscopic direct repair of gluteal tendon tears (33, 34, 35, 36). Thus, there seems to be some conflicting evidence regarding the association between tear severity and postoperative outcomes following gluteal tendon repair, which is why future studies on augmentation of gluteal tendon tears should further analyze this potential correlation. Moreover, to enable a qualitative comparison between studies, it is essential to establish standardized reporting of tear severity and specify tendon involvement of the GMed and/or GMin, as these factors might impact the outcome of gluteal tendon augmentation.

Despite the significant prevalence of GTPS, there is a lack of condition-specific patient-reported outcome measures evaluating the impact of GTPS on pain, function and overall disability (37). Many of the PROMs reported in the included studies were developed for hip OA and assessment of outcomes following THA (e.g. HHS, OHS, HOOS and Merle d’Aubigné score), while other PROMs were developed or modified for evaluating outcomes following hip arthroscopy (e.g. HOS and mHHS) (38). Moreover, the large variety of PROMs used in the included studies further complicated the comparison between them.

Multiple studies observed significant improvements in OHS, HHS and mHHS values following augmentation of gluteal tendon tears. However, the clinical relevance of the observed improvements in PROMs is equally, if not, more important than the level of statistical significance. The MCID is defined as the minimal change in score which patients deem meaningful and which would cause clinicians to deliberate a change in treatment (39). Future research on augmentation of gluteal tendons should consider these MCID thresholds as they are essential in power calculations for ascertaining the required study size. Accurate evaluation of the clinical relevance of improvements in patient-reported outcomes following gluteal tendon augmentation can only be achieved by taking into account population-specific MCID values for each PROM.

Fearon et al. developed a GTPS-specific PROM, VISA-G, which was proven to be a reliable and valid instrument for gauging the degree of disability in patients with GTPS (37). Ebert et al. used the VISA-G to assess patients before and after gluteal tendon augmentation using a synthetic ligament (17). There was no significant difference in postoperative improvement compared with the OHS and mHHS.

Patients with GTPS have similar levels of pain, function and disability compared with patients with hip OA (3). Moreover, a study of Fearon et al. suggested that activity limitations seen in patients with GTPS and hip OA may be primarily attributed to pain rather than the underlying hip joint or soft tissue pathology (40).

It has also previously been established that patients with gluteal tendinopathy exhibit changes in trunk and pelvic kinematics while walking (41), whereas no significant differences in hip, pelvic and trunk angles have been observed in patients performing a sit-to-stand test (42). This implies the importance of assessing kinematic changes in patients with gluteal tendon tears during tests that involve a phase of SLS, as this increases loading on the gluteal tendons (42). Huxtable et al. found no significant improvement following gluteal tendon augmentation in any of the biomechanical variables during a 30-s SLS test (22). Future research should assess these altered kinematics via a dynamic test (e.g. walking) rather than a static 30-s SLS test.

An important question for clinical decision making is whether augmentation provides superior outcomes compared with direct repair. Systematic reviews have reported that the average retear rate in studies performing open (mostly direct) repair ranged from 4.1 to 8.9% (8, 9, 43). This is in line with the findings of our review, with retear rates varying from 0 to 8.7% in 16 of the included studies. Remarkably, the study by Drexler et al. reported retears in no less than 6 of the 11 included patients (27). However, caution is warranted when interpreting these results given the disparities in employed surgical techniques, both regarding the various types of augmentation procedures and regarding the fact that not all patients received augmentation in each study. Improvements in clinical outcome scores also appeared comparable between augmentation and direct repair (8, 9, 43). No included study compared augmentation with direct repair directly, making definitive conclusions impossible. As such, augmentation cannot currently be said to offer superior outcomes compared with direct repair. Despite the wide range of augmentation techniques described, explicit indications were rarely articulated. The available evidence suggests that augmentation may be a reasonable option in case of chronic, full-thickness tears with significant retraction; in cases where tissue quality was poor or fatty infiltration was present; after failure of previous direct repairs; and in patients with abductor deficiency following total hip arthroplasty. These findings provide insight into common clinical practices, but the lack of controlled comparisons prevents confirmation of these indications. No study validated or tested any decision-making algorithm, such as that proposed by Kenanidis and colleagues, and therefore, the current literature should further investigate a specific algorithm for determining when augmentation should be used (10).

Our study presents several limitations. First, despite our comprehensive literature review including additional screening of bibliographies, there remains a potential for relevant and qualitative studies not being included, which could have introduced bias into our findings. Second, only a small and heterogeneous group of studies met our inclusion criteria, with substantial variability in biomechanical and clinical outcomes preventing the performance of a meta-analysis. Finally, the overall methodological quality was low, as three of the included studies failed to meet the threshold of a minimal 1-year follow-up and two studies reported a loss to follow-up rate of more than 5% of the study population. Taken together, these limitations indicate that the current evidence is fragmented, methodologically limited, and insufficient to support definitive conclusions regarding the (relative) effectiveness of augmentation techniques. Consequently, the findings of this review should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, by synthesizing the available evidence, it establishes a baseline understanding while clearly identifying key methodological shortcomings. Furthermore, we provide concrete recommendations to improve the design and rigor of future studies, which may help guide the advancement of this field.

Conclusion

Our systematic review included 17 studies with a low level of evidence performing open gluteal tendon augmentation. These studies used a great variety of augmentation methods, with the majority reporting significant improvements in clinical and functional outcomes as well as decent levels of patient satisfaction and perceived improvement postoperatively. Variability regarding several methodological aspects, such as inclusion criteria, reporting of tear severity and fatty infiltration, pre- and postoperative treatment protocols, employed outcome measures and (the lack of) retear confirmation through imaging, complicated a qualitative comparison of the included studies. Throughout our discussion, we formulated multiple suggestions concerning these aspects to enhance the quality of forthcoming research on surgical augmentation of gluteal tendon tears. Future prospective studies of optimal methodological quality should incorporate GTPS-specific, standardized rehabilitation protocols and outcome measures. Moreover, investigating correlations between tear severity or fatty degeneration and postoperative outcomes should be prioritized as these characteristics could have a bearing on the selection of the most suitable surgical treatment method. Furthermore, comparative designs are necessary to identify the additional benefit of augmentation methods with the goal of establishing an evidence-based surgical treatment protocol for patients with gluteal tendon tears.
