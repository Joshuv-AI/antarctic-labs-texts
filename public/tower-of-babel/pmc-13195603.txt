Outcomes of Kidney Transplants From Standard Criteria Donors With Diabetes: A Propensity Score-Matched Study
Authors: Pedro Henrique Pepato, Marina Colella, Renato Demarchi Foresto, Helio Tedesco-Silva, José Medina Pestana, Lúcio Requião-Moura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale & ObjectiveFew studies have evaluated the impact of using donors with diabetes, which often leads to higher rates of organ refusal by transplant centers, based on the concern that the disease may negatively impact graft quality and the recipient outcomes. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate 5-year outcomes of kidney transplant recipients (KTRs) who received kidneys from donors with diabetes but who met the standard United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) criteria.Study DesignObservational and longitudinal cohort study.Setting & ParticipantsKTRs who received kidneys from deceased donors meeting standard UNOS criteria between 2013 and 2017 at a large-scale Brazilian center of transplantation.ExposurePresence of diabetes in the donor.OutcomesFive-year estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, mL/min/1.73 m2) and graft survival.Analytical ApproachComparison of KTRs from donors with (DM+) and without (DM−) diabetes, involving the entire cohort and after a 1:2 propensity score-matched analysis. eGFR was compared using generalized estimated equation and graft survival by Fine and Gray subdistribution hazards models.ResultsOf 3,059 KTRs, 1,931 (63.1%) met the standard UNOS criteria, 73 being DM+ (3.8%). Before matching, the 1- and 5-year eGFRs were 42.5 and 34.6 among DM+, whereas they were 50.8 and 44.8 among DM−, respectively, with a significant mean difference in all eGFR measures (−9.07; P = 0.001), but with no interaction between presence of diabetes and time (P = 0.54). All demographic disparities between groups were controlled by propensity score. After matching, the 1- and 5-year eGFRs were 42.5 and 34.6 among DM+, whereas they were 46.1 and 38.8 among DM−, respectively, with no mean difference in all eGFR measures (−3.65; P = 0.26) and also with no interaction between presence of diabetes and time (P = 0.91). The 5-year graft survival was 84.6% for KTRs of DM+ vs. 88.1% (subdistribution HR = 1.40; P = 0.25) before and 84.3% (subdistribution HR= 1.02; P = 0.96) after propensity score matching.LimitationsRetrospective and single-center study.ConclusionsFive-year eGFR and graft survival were inferior among KTRs of donors with diabetes; however, when the key demographic disparities were controlled using propensity score matching, no impact was observed in long-term outcomes when donors with diabetes are used.Index Words: Deceased donors, diabetes, graft function, kidney transplant recipients, standard criteria donor

For patients with kidney failure, kidney transplantation offers better outcomes compared with dialysis in terms of mortality, cardiovascular events, and quality of life.1 However, a significant gap exists between the number of patients with kidney failure on kidney transplant waiting lists and the availability of deceased donor organs.2 In response to this shortage, the expanded criteria donor (ECD) classification was developed to enlarge the donor pool by utilizing organs that might otherwise be discarded.3 Although transplants from ECDs carry a higher risk of graft loss and lower graft function over time compared with those from standard criteria donors (SCDs),3,4 they still offer a better survival rate than remaining on the waiting list and continuing dialysis.5, 6, 7

Despite being one of the leading causes of chronic kidney disease globally, diabetes is not included in the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) classification for SCDs or ECDs.3 On the other hand, it has been incorporated into the Kidney Donor Risk Index, which estimates graft survival and guides organ distribution decisions in some countries.8 Few studies have evaluated the impact of using donors with diabetes; it often leads to higher rates of organ refusal by transplant centers, based on the concern that the disease may negatively impact recipient outcomes.9 Donors with diabetes have been associated with lower graft and recipient survival rates.10,11 However, in recent studies, other factors than donors’ diabetes, such as the duration of diabetes, time on dialysis, and the presence of diabetes in the recipient, were also associated to these outcomes.12,13 Optimizing the use of these organs could be a viable strategy to increase the number of transplants performed.

Brazil has the largest public kidney transplant program in the world, and unlike the other top 3 countries, >80% of kidney transplants come from deceased donors.14 Only donations after brain death are accepted, and although nearly two-thirds meet the UNOS standard criteria, the average kidney donor profile index (KDPI) is 70%, with 8% of donors having a prior diagnosis of diabetes.15 These unique circumstances present an opportunity to evaluate the long-term outcomes of kidney transplant recipients (KTRs) who receive organs from donors with diabetes classified as SCDs. Therefore, this study investigated the impact of using donors with diabetes on KTR outcomes in a single Brazilian center, using a propensity score matching strategy to control for other confounding variables that may be present in donors classified as SCD.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Hospital do Rim, Brazil. The study included all KTRs who received a kidney from an SCD between January 1, 2013 and December 31, 2017, with a follow-up period extending up to 5 years after transplantation. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee at the Federal University of São Paulo, identification number CAEE 54829421.6.0000.5505, and approval number 6260942.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The study considered all adult KTRs (aged >18 years) who received a kidney from an SCD between 2013 and 2017. In the Brazilian allocation system, only organ donations after brain death are eligible. Exclusion criteria included patients who underwent combined kidney transplants with another solid organ and those who received a kidney graft from an ECD. Details about source of information, variables of interest, immunosuppression local practices, and prophylaxis are presented in the Supplementary Material (Item S1).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was graft function within 5 years of transplantation. The secondary outcome was death-censored graft survival.

Definitions

According to the local protocol, kidneys from donors with diabetes are accepted after pretransplant biopsies to evaluate the presence and severity of histological lesions. Although no formal biopsy-based acceptance criteria are applied, the biopsy findings inform the transplant team’s decision-making process. For routine histological analysis, all tissue samples are fixed in 10% buffered formalin and processed using a rapid tissue protocol with a Leica ASP 300S tissue processor. Sections are cut at a thickness of 3 μm using a Luptec microtome. Four histological stains are performed for each biopsy: hematoxylin and eosin, Masson’s trichrome, Jones’ silver, and periodic acid–Schiff (PAS). Because the study included patients transplanted between 2013 and 2017, all available biopsy slides were re-evaluated and reclassified according to the Banff 2017 criteria for preimplantation kidney biopsies. Only slides accessible for re-review were included in this reassessment.16

Graft function was assessed using the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) calculated by the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation.17 Since the KDPI is not yet validated in Brazil, the American KDPI calculator was used for risk estimation.8 Our center has routinely calculated the KDPI/Kidney Donor Risk Index for each deceased donor, with this data being included in medical reports and institutional datasets. For the present study, all data were extracted from these records and rechecked. In cases where the Kidney Donor Risk Index/KDPI were not available in these sources (n = 555, 28.6%), they were retrospectively calculated using US data corresponding to the period of data collection and curation.

Acute rejection was defined as biopsy-proven according to the Banff criteria or as presumed acute rejection, which was defined when recipients were empirically treated for acute rejection and graft function improved after treatment.18 Our center does not perform protocol biopsies; all biopsies are conducted based on clinical indications.

Statistical Analysis

Sample size determination is detailed in the Supplementary Material (Item S1). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test assessed the normality of continuous variables. Because the variables did not follow a normal distribution, they were summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as absolute frequencies and percentages. Patients were initially stratified based on the presence of diabetes in the donors. Continuous variables were compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test, while categorical variables were compared using the χ2 or Fisher exact test.

The primary outcome, eGFR, was evaluated over 1 to 5 years posttransplant using linear generalized estimating equations. Comparisons accounted for time, group (presence or absence of diabetes in donors), and the interaction between group and time, with adjustments made using the Bonferroni test. The missing eGFR values were first imputed using last observation carried forward for recipients who died or were lost follow-up and imputed zero for those who had graft failure. As an additional approach, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was applied for recipients who died or were lost to follow-up. The MICE model was customized using fully conditional specification with up to 10 imputations, employing linear regression for continuous variables. The final estimates were derived using pooled analyses across the imputed datasets.

For the secondary outcome, graft survival stratified by donor diabetes status was estimated using Fine and Gray subdistribution hazards model accounting for death as a competing event. Both primary and secondary outcomes were analyzed for the entire cohort and after matching. A 1:2 propensity score matching approach was employed for the matched analysis to control for confounding variables, considering the nearest neighbor. Variables that significantly differed between donors with and without diabetes in the full cohort (P < 0.05) were included in the matching process, along with human leukocyte antigen-DR matching between donor and recipient, which was included irrespective of its association in the univariate analysis due to its relevance in the local allocation system. Pre-match imbalance was also checked using standardized mean differences. Then, the following variables were considered in the propensity score model: donor age, presence of arterial hypertension, cause of brain death, and human leukocyte antigen-DR compatibility. After matching, covariate balance between groups was reassessed using standardized mean differences, with an absolute standardized mean difference <0.20 considered indicative of acceptable balance.

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 29; IBM), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05 and a 95% confidence interval. Propensity score matching and Fine and Gray test were conducted using the MatchIt and cmprsk packages in R (version 4.1.3; R Core Team, 2022).19,20

Results

Between 2013 and 2017, a total of 3,469 donors were offered by the organ procurement organization in our region, resulting in 6,582 kidneys being offered for transplantation. Among all donors, 300 had diabetes (8.6%), and of all kidneys, 588 were from donors with diabetes (8.9%). Of the total kidneys offered, 1,267 were discarded for various reasons (18.2%), including 232 from donors with diabetes (18.3%). Specifically, considering kidneys from donors with diabetes (n = 588), the discard rate was 39% (n = 232). Among all effective donors, 2,217 were classified as SCD, corresponding to 4,124 kidneys. Of these, 454 were discarded (11.0%), including 41 from donors with diabetes. Thus, 9.0% of the discarded kidneys from SCD were from donors with diabetes, and among SCDs with diabetes, the discard rate was 25.3%.

From this pool of donors, our center performed 3,161 kidney transplants, and 1,931 from SCDs were included in the analysis. Within this group, 73 donors had diabetes (DM+ group), while 1,858 did not (DM− group). Figure 1 illustrates a detailed population diagram.

Baseline Characteristics

The mean donor age was 41.0 (IQR, 30.0-47.0) years, with 16.8% aged between 50 and 59. Most donors were male (61.8%) and White (52.1%). The main cause of brain death was cerebrovascular events (47.1%), followed by trauma (41.4%). Additionally, 24.2% of the donors had arterial hypertension, and 48.2% had terminal creatinine levels >1.5 mg/dL. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics stratified by donor diabetes status. Among donors with diabetes, kidney biopsies of 48 cases (65.7%) were re-evaluated and classified according to the 2017 Banff criteria. No changes were observed in the glomerular basement membrane, and 97.9% of the samples showed no mesangial expansion. Regarding vascular fibrous intimal thickening changes, 41.7% and 10.4% of the samples presented with cv1 and cv2 lesions, respectively. Arteriolar hyalinosis was observed in 37.5% (ah1) and 14.6% (ah2) of cases. Findings related to other compartments are detailed in Table 2.

The recipients’ median age was 48.5 (IQR 38.0-57.7) years, 59.8% of them were male, and 44.9% were White. The mean cold ischemia time was 23 hours, 98% received thymoglobulin as induction therapy, and the maintenance regimen was composed of tacrolimus, azathioprine, and prednisone in 49.4%. There were no significant differences in recipient characteristics, human leukocyte antigen compatibility, or initial immunosuppression based on the donor’s diabetes status (Table 1).

Posttransplant Events

The frequency of delayed graft function was 59.2%, with a numerically higher occurrence in recipients of organs from donors with diabetes, although this difference was not statistically significant (67.1% vs 58.9%, P = 0.16). The time between transplantation and the last dialysis session posttransplant was comparable between both groups, averaging 5 days (P = 0.57). Additionally, as determined by the preemptive strategy criteria, the need for cytomegalovirus treatment was 37.8% and did not differ significantly between the 2 groups (P = 0.28). Finally, the frequency of acute rejection was not different: 19.2% and 17.9% in KTRs of donors with and without diabetes (P = 0.77; Table 1).

Outcomes

A total of 9,655 measures of eGFR (in mL/min/1.73 m2) were analyzed throughout 1 to 5 years posttransplant (with 1,931 measurements at each time point, considering imputation). The eGFR for the entire cohort showed a significant decline over time, decreasing from 46.6 (95% CI, 44.1-49.1) at 1 year to 39.7 (95% CI, 36.9-42.5) at 5 years posttransplant, representing a slope of −5.94 (95% CI, −6.77 to −5.12; P < 0.001). When stratified by the donor’s diabetes status, 9,290 repeated measures were analyzed for recipients of donors without diabetes and 365 for recipients of donors with diabetes. The average difference in eGFR between recipients of donors with and without diabetes was −9.07 (95% CI, −13.3 to −3.24; P = 0.001). Specifically, recipients of donors without diabetes had a 1-year eGFR of 50.8 (95% CI, 49.7-51.8), declining to 44.8 (95% CI, 43.6-46.0) at 5 years. In contrast, recipients of donors with diabetes had a 1-year eGFR of 42.5 (95% CI, 36.5-46.4), which further declined to 34.6 (95% CI, 29.1-40.1) at 5 years. Although recipients of donors with diabetes consistently had lower eGFR values at all time points, there was no significant interaction between group and time (P = 0.54), indicating that the rate of decline over time was similar in both groups (Fig 2A). The results of eGFR estimated after MICE imputation was similar, as presented in Fig 2B and detailed in the Supplementary Material.

Analysis After Propensity Score Matching

The 1:2 ratio propensity score matching resulted in 73 participants in the DM+ group and 146 participants in the DM− group. The propensity score distribution is illustrated in the Supplementary Material (Fig S1), and the comparisons of variables post-matching are detailed in Table S1.

A total of 1,095 eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m2) measurements were analyzed, with 219 at each time point (including imputed data): 365 and 730 from recipients of donors with and without diabetes, respectively. Among all patients, a reduction of −7.31 (95% CI, −9.87 to −4.75) was observed (P < 0.001) from year 1 to 5. However, there was no significant difference between the 2 groups, with a mean difference in eGFR of −3.65 (with vs without diabetes, respectively, 95% CI, −9.94 to +2.64; P = 0.26). Figure 2C illustrates graft function over time for each group, with no significant difference in the interaction between group and time (P = 0.91). Recipients of donors without diabetes had a 1-year eGFR of 46.1 (95% CI, 42.3-50.0), which declined to 38.8 (95% CI, 34.8-42.8). In contrast, recipients of donors with diabetes started with a 1-year eGFR of 42.5 (95% CI, 37.6-47.4), which further declined to 34.6 (95% CI, 29.1-40.0). The results of eGFR estimated after MICE imputation was similar, as presented in Fig 2D and detailed in the Supplementary Material.

After matching, 1-year graft survival among donors with diabetes was 97.3% (vs 92.3% for donors without diabetes), and 5-year graft survival was 84.6% (vs 88.3% for donors without diabetes) (Fig 3B). Using the Fine and Gray analysis for competing graft survival risk, we did not observe a difference between groups (DM+ vs DM−): 1.02; 95% CI, 0.51-2.03; P = 0.96.

Discussion

Diabetes in potential donors is associated with a 26% higher discard rate for kidneys offered to transplant centers due to concerns about its impact on long-term outcomes for recipients.2,9 In this study, we investigated the effect of donors with diabetes on recipients’ 5-year graft function and survival, focusing exclusively on recipients of SCD kidneys as classified by the UNOS criteria. Our findings indicate that, after controlling for confounding variables, the presence of diabetes in donors did not negatively affect recipients’ outcomes.

Among SCDs, only 3.8% were reported to have a diabetes diagnosis, a frequency significantly lower than that observed in the general adult Brazilian population.21 This rate is also lower than that reported in other studies evaluating deceased donors that did not differentiate between SCDs and ECDs.11,22,23 This disparity is not unexpected when restricting the cohort to SCDs, given the association between diabetes prevalence and advancing age.24 Despite including only KTRs from SCDs, nearly 20% of the donors were >50 years old, half died due to cerebrovascular events, half had terminal creatinine levels >1.5 mg/dL, and a quarter had a prior diagnosis of arterial hypertension. These findings underscore that Brazilian SCDs may not align closely with the conventional definition of “standard.”

Given that the follow-up period was limited to 5 years posttransplant, graft function was chosen as the primary outcome, as a surrogate for longer-term outcomes. Early in the 2000s, Becker et al25 investigated the use of kidneys from donors with type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus. They observed a higher frequency of proteinuria in recipients of donors with diabetes; however, the impact on long-term graft function was not assessed. More recently, a single-center study of 1,223 transplants performed between 2008 and 2018 found that eGFR was lower in recipients without diabetes of kidneys from donors with diabetes compared with the pairs of recipients and donors without diabetes.23 In our pre-matching analysis, recipients of kidneys from donors with diabetes exhibited a lower graft function as early as the first year posttransplant. However, this difference was not observed after matching, suggesting that the lower graft function was likely attributable to other donor conditions, such as advanced age, arterial hypertension, and evidence of target organ damage, such as cerebrovascular death, all factors associated with an increased risk of chronic kidney injury.

Although KTRs from donors with diabetes demonstrated lower graft function at all time points prior to matching, the decline in graft function between 1 and 5 years was not more accelerated compared with recipients of kidneys from donors without diabetes. This trend remained consistent after matching. Factors such as prolonged dialysis duration, immunosuppressive regimen choice or adherence, and diabetes as the underlying cause of kidney failure have been linked to a steeper decline in graft function.26,27 Despite having a lower baseline renal function, recipients of kidneys from donors with diabetes showed a satisfactory trajectory over time, as graft function decline tends to be more pronounced in patients with higher baseline values.28 Some allocation systems, such as the US model that combines KDPI with the Estimated Post-Transplant Survival score, direct lower-quality kidneys to candidates with limited life expectancy who would still derive a survival benefit from transplantation compared with remaining on dialysis.29 This approach may increase the utilization of kidneys from donors with diabetes. In contrast, the Brazilian allocation system does not yet incorporate donor risk scores or recipient prognostic profiles; KDPI has been validated only recently by our group, and the Estimated Post-Transplant Survival score remains unvalidated.30 Therefore, although kidneys from diabetic donors performed well in our study, the current evidence is insufficient to support adopting similar allocation changes in Brazil.

No significant differences in graft survival were observed between the groups, even before matching. In contrast, an analysis of the UNOS database, which included patients transplanted in the United States between 1994 and 2014, revealed that using kidneys from donors with diabetes increased the risk of all-cause graft loss by 21% and recipient death by 19%. Notably, younger recipients of diabetic grafts experienced worse outcomes compared with older recipients of nondiabetic grafts.11 Similarly, Ahmad et al,10 using propensity score matching, reported an increased risk of graft failure, death-censored graft failure, and patient mortality for KTRs of donors with diabetes. However, despite these differences in relative risk, the authors emphasized the slight absolute difference and the lack of impact on overall patient survival. Interestingly, diabetes was not identified as a risk factor for graft loss in studies that defined ECDs.3 On the other hand, Mohan et al13 found lower graft survival among recipients of SCD kidneys from donors with diabetes. Despite this, the outcomes remained superior to those of recipients of kidneys from ECDs without diabetes. These findings suggest that the impact on graft survival is more closely associated with factors such as long-standing donor diabetes, advanced donor age, the presence of other noncommunicable conditions, moderate to severe histological lesions observed in preimplantation biopsies, and recipient-specific factors, including pre-existing diabetes and prolonged time on the transplant waitlist.9,11

A peculiar aspect of the Brazilian transplant program is the high rates of delayed graft function, even though donation after circulatory death is not permitted. This is not a characteristic unique to our center. A multicenter Brazilian study, which included 3,992 kidney transplants performed across 18 centers in the country between 2014 and 2015, reported an average delayed graft function rate of 54%.31 Among the main risk factors identified for this high rate were aspects related to donor management in intensive care units and the duration of cold ischemia time.32,33 This characteristic is not necessarily associated with the country’s continental dimensions, as kidney allocation is primarily carried out within defined geographic regions (microregions or states), with only sporadic cross-country allocation. Furthermore, preservation techniques such as machine perfusion, which helps reduce delayed graft function rates, have not been incorporated into the national transplant program.34

We acknowledge that our study has some limitations, including its retrospective design, reliance on single-center data, peculiarities in immunosuppressive regimens, and relatively short follow-up period. Additionally, important information regarding donor diabetes assessment prior to organ procurement, such as duration of diabetes, urinary protein-creatinine or albumin-creatinine ratios, and hemoglobin A1c levels,35 was not included because these variables were either not collected, unavailable, or imprecisely documented in organ procurement organization records. This limitation hampers the development of standardized practice guidelines for selecting kidneys from diabetic donors. Although this is a single-center study, our center performs almost 20% of all kidney transplants in Brazil, providing a representative sample of donor and recipient demographics nationwide. Therefore, the outcomes from this study reflect the reality of the Brazilian health care context. They may not be directly applicable to other settings, as local policies, allocation rules, and clinical approaches possibly influence them. Furthermore, the risk of retrospective bias due to the retrospective design was mitigated by the absence of missing data for all demographic variables of interest. The low number of donors with diabetes in the cohort could pose some challenges for interpreting the results and limits the statistical power of subgroup analyses; however, we focused on the difference of the 5-year eGFR as the primary outcome, ensuring a sufficient sample size to test our primary hypothesis. Finally, our local practice for histological evaluation in donors with diabetes may differ from those used in other countries. In our cohort, kidneys from SCDs with diabetes had a discard rate of 39% versus 18.2% overall, underscoring the central role of biopsy findings in decision making and the need for caution when extrapolating these results to other settings.

In conclusion, when analyzing only SCDs and performing paired analyses, donor diabetes did not significantly impact graft function or survival within 5 years posttransplant. Nevertheless, diabetes in donors was associated with poorer markers of graft quality, including advanced age, hypertension, and cerebrovascular causes of brain death. Thus, utilizing kidneys from donors with diabetes yields acceptable outcomes when guided by a comprehensive assessment of the donor’s overall characteristics.
