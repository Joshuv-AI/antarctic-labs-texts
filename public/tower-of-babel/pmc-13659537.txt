Are there true and false classifications in noninvasive liver disease assessment for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease?
Authors: Tatsuki Ichikawa, Satoshi Miuma, Hisamitsu Miyaaki
Published: 2026

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) currently affects approximately 30% of the global adult population and has emerged as a major healthcare burden worldwide (1). Given its increasing prevalence, noninvasive approaches have largely replaced liver biopsy for identifying patients at risk of progressive liver fibrosis and liver-related complications (2). Current clinical guidelines recommend a stepwise screening strategy using noninvasive liver tests or noninvasive liver disease assessments (NILDAs) to stratify fibrosis risk in patients with MASLD (3,4). In patients with obesity or type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), the fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4) is generally used as the initial screening tool, followed by liver stiffness measurement (LSM) using vibration-controlled transient elastography when FIB-4 is ≥1.3. Patients with LSM values ≥8 kPa are considered at elevated risk for advanced fibrosis and liver-related events (LREs), warranting further treatment and surveillance, whereas patients with FIB-4 values <1.3 are typically monitored in primary care settings at one- to two-year intervals. This sequential screening strategy has proven to be both cost-effective and clinically beneficial for hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance (5,6).

However, the current FIB-4/LSM combination should not be considered the definitive model for fibrosis stratification, and continued refinement of NILDAs and their combinatorial use remains necessary (7). In this regard, Rabbat et al. recently provided important insight into the limitations of the current two-step strategy through a retrospective analysis of 12,950 patients with MASLD who underwent vibration-controlled transient elastography across 16 tertiary referral centers (8). Their study highlighted substantial discordance between FIB-4 and LSM values in real-world practice. Specifically, among patients who underwent liver biopsy, those with FIB-4 <1.3 but LSM ≥8 kPa—defined by the authors as the “false-negative” group—frequently demonstrated advanced fibrosis, including cirrhosis, and had a significantly higher incidence of LREs during long-term follow-up compared with patients with both FIB-4 <1.3 and LSM <8 kPa (the “true-negative” group).

Importantly, Rabbat et al. reported that approximately 30% of patients exhibited discordant FIB-4 and LSM results, suggesting that LSM may more accurately reflect histological disease severity in certain clinical settings (8). Nevertheless, although patients with discordant findings showed an increased fibrosis burden, the overall incidence of LREs remained relatively low compared with the concordant high-risk group. Indeed, the subgroup with both FIB-4 ≥1.3 and LSM ≥8 kPa demonstrated the highest risk of LREs among all categories, reinforcing the prognostic relevance of concordantly abnormal NILDAs. Thus, while the findings of Rabbat et al. do not invalidate the currently recommended two-step screening algorithm, they suggest a greater diagnostic weight for LSM and raise important questions regarding the relative role of FIB-4 within fibrosis risk stratification.

Indeed, Rabbat et al. further demonstrated that 11% of the total cohort had false-negative results, defined as FIB-4 <1.3 despite LSM ≥8 kPa, a proportion that cannot be overlooked in clinical practice (8). The prevalence of false-negative FIB-4 results is likely even greater in tertiary referral centers, where patients with advanced fibrosis are more commonly encountered (2). Although the 2023 American Gastroenterological Association guidelines recommend universal FIB-4 screening followed by second-line LSM assessment in all patients with steatotic liver disease (3), the updated 2026 Clinical Care Pathway acknowledges that FIB-4 may be bypassed in favor of direct second-line testing, such as LSM or enhanced liver fibrosis score evaluation, particularly in high-risk populations including patients with T2DM (7). Similar alternative screening strategies have also been proposed in other studies (9). For example, even in patients with FIB-4 <1.3, LSM-based screening has demonstrated the ability to identify stage F2–3 fibrosis with high probability in patients with T2DM, overweight, or obesity (9).

These findings suggest that direct use of imaging-based NILDAs, including LSM, may be advantageous in high-risk populations, particularly in tertiary care settings where advanced fibrosis is prevalent. However, such observations should not be interpreted as evidence that FIB-4 lacks clinical value. Because age is included in the numerator of the FIB-4 formula, FIB-4 values increase with advancing age, reducing specificity and increasing false-positive rates in older individuals. Accordingly, a higher cutoff of 2.0 has been recommended for patients aged ≥65 years (3). Furthermore, the 2023 American Gastroenterological Association guidelines recommend second-stage noninvasive testing even in patients with FIB-4 <1.3 when multiple metabolic risk factors, including T2DM or metabolic syndrome components, are present (3). Therefore, FIB-4 should not be interpreted in isolation, but rather within the broader clinical context of patient risk stratification. However, as age is included in the numerator of the FIB-4 formula, younger individuals tend to have structurally lower scores, which carries a risk of overlooking progressive fibrosis. Consequently, it has been reported that diagnostic accuracy is reduced in adults aged 35 and under, and caution is therefore required (3,4).

Notably, Rabbat et al. also showed that patients with both FIB-4 ≥1.3 and LSM ≥8 kPa had a 21-fold increased risk of LREs, whereas those with FIB-4 <1.3 but LSM ≥8 kPa had only a 4.12-fold increased risk (8). This marked prognostic difference suggests that combined assessment using FIB-4 and LSM may provide superior prognostic discrimination compared with LSM alone. Although the detailed characteristics of the false-negative group—including the distribution of LSM values and the influence of age—remain insufficiently explored, these findings indicate that the two-step NILDA approach may be valuable not only for fibrosis screening but also for prognostic stratification. Indeed, additional stratification based on second-stage LSM values has been shown to correlate with overall survival and hepatocellular carcinoma risk (6,10). Thus, in tertiary referral settings, the combined use of two NILDAs may function not merely as a screening tool but as a more comprehensive method of disease severity assessment.

The therapeutic landscape of MASLD and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) has also undergone substantial transformation in recent years. Most notably, pharmacologic therapy for MASH has now received approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (11,12). However, treatment efficacy remains imperfect, with approximately 35–70% of patients failing to achieve adequate therapeutic response (13). Therefore, careful patient selection is essential when considering MASH-directed therapy. To identify patients with MASH and stage F2–3 fibrosis who are most likely to benefit from treatment, liver biopsy is no longer considered mandatory in routine clinical practice; instead, treatment eligibility is increasingly determined using NILDA-based algorithms (11,12,14).

Among available NILDAs, LSM has received particular emphasis, with values of 8–12 kPa explicitly cited as thresholds supporting treatment consideration in recently published recommendations (11,12). Nevertheless, previous studies have demonstrated that a two-step strategy incorporating FIB-4 followed by LSM identifies 74% of biopsy-confirmed F2–3 fibrosis cases eligible for MASH treatment, whereas LSM alone identifies only 56% (9). These findings suggest that the combined use of FIB-4 and LSM may outperform isolated LSM assessment when determining therapeutic candidacy.

Taken together, the report by Rabbat et al. underscores the limitations of relying solely on FIB-4 in high-risk clinical populations, particularly in tertiary referral settings where advanced fibrosis is more prevalent. In such settings, direct LSM-based evaluation may be justified in selected patients, similar to current approaches used in obesity and T2DM. However, the prognostic and therapeutic value of combined FIB-4 and LSM assessment should not be underestimated. In the emerging era of approved MASH therapies, a two-step screening strategy incorporating both FIB-4 and LSM remains highly useful for identifying treatment candidates and stratifying prognosis. Therefore, FIB-4—like LSM—should not be regarded as a “false” NILDA, but rather as a valid and essential component of comprehensive MASLD assessment. Although FIB-4 is recommended as an initial assessment in a two-stage evaluation, it is also true that in young people, obese individuals and patients with diabetes, assessment using FIB-4 alone should be avoided and a more accurate test LSM should be considered (15). To optimize the diagnosis and treatment of MASLD/MASH moving forward, hepatologists must recognize the strengths and limitations of each NILDA and promote their broader implementation among general practitioners and clinicians managing diabetes and obesity.
