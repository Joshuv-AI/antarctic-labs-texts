Universal and Efficient Quantum State Verification via Schmidt Decomposition and Mutually Unbiased Bases
Yunting Li, Huangjun Zhu

Abstract
Efficient verification of multipartite quantum states is crucial to many applications in quantum information processing. By virtue of Schmidt decomposition and mutually unbiased bases, here we propose a universal protocol to verify arbitrary multipartite pure quantum states using adaptive local projective measurements. Moreover, we establish a universal upper bound on the sample complexity that is independent of the local dimensions. Numerical calculations further indicate that Haar-random pure states can be verified with a constant sample cost, irrespective of the qudit number and local dimensions, even in the adversarial scenario in which the source cannot be trusted. As alternatives, we provide multiple simpler variants that can achieve similar high efficiencies.

Variants of the SD protocol
—The SD protocol described above is based on a given order of successive SD, which determines the order of adaptive local projective measurements. Any permutation of the

MUB protocol

—As an alternative, the first

Universal QSV in the adversarial scenario
—Now, the preparation device may be controlled by a malicious adversary, and QSV is much more challenging. Fortunately, any verification protocol for the nonadversarial scenario can be extended to the adversarial scenario with a small sample overhead thanks to Theorem 6 in Ref.
Zhu and Hayashi 2019a
. Suppose
\Omega
is a verification operator for
|\Psi\rangle
with spectral gap
\nu
; let
p=\nu/\mathrm{e}
and
\Omega_{p}=p+(1-p)\Omega
, where
\mathrm{e}
is the base of the natural logarithm. Then
|\Psi\rangle
can be verified efficiently in the adversarial scenario by virtue of
\Omega_{p}
and random permutations, and the number of tests required to achieve infidelity
\varepsilon
and significance level
\delta
satisfies
Zhu and Hayashi 2019a

N\leq\frac{\ln[(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}\delta^{-1}]}{(1-\nu+\mathrm{e}^{-1}\nu^{2})\nu\varepsilon}.

(6)

If
\Omega
is tied to the SD protocol, then
\nu(\Omega)\geq 2^{1-n}
by Theorem
1
, so
N\leq 2^{n}\varepsilon^{-1}\ln(\delta^{-1})
when
\varepsilon,\delta\leq 1/5
. Numerical simulations further show that
the CSD, SMUB, and SCMUB protocol can verify Haar-random pure states with constant sample costs in both the nonadversarial and adversarial scenarios as illustrated in Fig.

Summary
—We proposed a universal protocol, the SD protocol, for verifying arbitrary multipartite pure quantum states using adaptive local projective measurements. Its performance is guaranteed by a rigorous upper bound on the sample complexity, independent of the local dimensions. Numerical calculations further show that it can verify Haar-random pure states with a constant sample cost even in the adversarial scenario. As alternatives, we introduced multiple simpler variants based on MUB, which can achieve similar performance. Numerical calculations indicate that a generic Haar-random pure state can be verified efficiently even if we can only access a constant number of distinct tests based on local projective measurements. Our work offers valuable insights on the power of local projective measurements in QSV and useful tools for verifying multipartite pure states, which may find applications in many tasks in quantum information processing, such as quantum benchmarking, quantum computation, quantum simulation, and quantum machine learning.

|B_{i}\rangle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}t_{j}^{i}|b_{j}^{i}\rangle|c_{j}^{i}\rangle

(S1)

be the SD of
|B_{i}\rangle
,
where
t_{j}^{i}
are the Schmidt coefficients satisfying
\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}(t_{j}^{i})^{2}=1
, while
\{|b_{j}^{i}\rangle\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}
and
\{|c_{j}^{i}\rangle\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}
are the Schmidt bases for the second and third parties, respectively. Then the target state can be expressed as

|\Psi\rangle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}s_{i}|a_{i}\rangle|B_{i}\rangle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}s_{i}t_{j}^{i}|a_{i}\rangle|b_{j}^{i}\rangle|c_{j}^{i}\rangle.

(S2)

Suppose the first party performs the projective measurement in the Schmidt basis
\{|a_{i}\rangle\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}
and obtains outcome
i
. Then the reduced state of the remaining parties is
|B_{i}\rangle
. Now, if the second party performs the projective measurement in the Schmidt basis
\{|b_{j}^{i}\rangle\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}
and obtains outcome
j

|c_{j}^{i}\rangle
. Finally, the third party can perform the binary measurement
\{|c_{j}^{i}\rangle\langle c_{j}^{i}|,1-|c_{j}^{i}\rangle\langle c_{j}^{i}|\}

\{|c_{j}^{i}\rangle\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}

\mathbf{m}=(0,0)\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}

P_{(0,0)}=\sum_{i|s_{i}>0}\sum_{j|t_{j}^{i}>0}|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|\otimes|b_{j}^{i}\rangle\langle b_{j}^{i}|\otimes|c_{j}^{i}\rangle\langle c_{j}^{i}|.

(S3)

Alternatively, the second party can perform the projective measurement in a basis that is mutually unbiased with
\{|b_{j}^{i}\rangle\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}
. For concreteness, here we shall focus on the Fourier basis with respect to the Schmidt basis:

|\tilde{b}_{j}^{i}\rangle=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\omega^{jl}|b_{l}^{i}\rangle,

(S4)

where
\omega=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}/d}
,
but other MUB are equally good. If the second party obtains outcome
j
, then the reduced state of the third party reads

|\tilde{c}_{j}^{i}\rangle=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}t_{l}^{i}\omega^{-jl}|c_{l}^{i}\rangle,

(S5)

which can be singled out by a suitable binary projective measurement. The resulting test projector, labeled by the string
\mathbf{m}=(0,1)
, reads

P_{(0,1)}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}|s_{i}>0}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|\otimes|\tilde{b}_{j}^{i}\rangle\langle\tilde{b}_{j}^{i}|\otimes|\tilde{c}_{j}^{i}\rangle\langle\tilde{c}_{j}^{i}|.

(S6)

To introduce the remaining two test projectors, let
\{|\tilde{a}_{i}\rangle\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}
be the Fourier basis with respect to the Schmidt basis
\{|a_{i}\rangle\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}
, that is,

|\tilde{a}_{i}\rangle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{ij}|a_{j}\rangle/\sqrt{d}
. Let

|\tilde{B}_{i}\rangle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{-ij}s_{j}|B_{j}\rangle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{-ij}s_{j}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}t_{l}^{j}|b_{l}^{j}\rangle|c_{l}^{j}\rangle,

(S7)

and construct SD as follows:

(S8)

where
u_{j}^{i}
are the Schmidt coefficients of
|\tilde{B}_{i}\rangle
, while
\{|\bar{b}_{j}^{i}\rangle\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}
and
\{|\bar{c}_{j}^{i}\rangle\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}
are the corresponding Schmidt bases. Then the target state

can also be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}|\tilde{a}_{i}\rangle|\tilde{B}_{i}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}u_{j}^{i}|\tilde{a}_{i}\rangle|\bar{b}_{j}^{i}\rangle|\bar{c}_{j}^{i}\rangle.

(S9)

Suppose the first party performs the projective measurement in the basis

and obtains outcome
i

|\tilde{B}_{i}\rangle
.
If the second party performs the projective measurement in the Schmidt basis

for each outcome
i

, reads

P_{(1,0)}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}|u_{j}^{i}>0}|\tilde{a}_{i}\rangle\langle\tilde{a}_{i}|\otimes|\bar{b}_{j}^{i}\rangle\langle\bar{b}_{j}^{i}|\otimes|\bar{c}_{j}^{i}\rangle\langle\bar{c}_{j}^{i}|.

(S10)

If instead the second party performs the projective measurement in the Fourier basis
\{|\gamma_{j}^{i}\rangle\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}
with

|\gamma_{j}^{i}\rangle=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\omega^{jl}|\bar{b}_{l}^{i}\rangle

(S11)

for each outcome
i

\mathbf{m}=(1,1)
, reads

P_{(1,1)}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}|\tilde{a}_{i}\rangle\langle\tilde{a}_{i}|\otimes|\gamma_{j}^{i}\rangle\langle\gamma_{j}^{i}|\otimes|\zeta_{j}^{i}\rangle\langle\zeta_{j}^{i}|,

(S12)

where

|\zeta^{i}_{j}\rangle=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}u_{l}^{i}\omega^{-jl}|\bar{c}_{l}^{i}\rangle.

(S13)

S1.2
Proof of Theorem 1

Proof of Theorem 1.

We shall prove Theorem 1 by induction on the number
n
of parties. For simplicity of description we assume that the total Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
can be expressed as a tensor power
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes n}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{d}
has dimension
d
, but the basic idea of our proof can be applied to a more general setting.

Suppose
|\Psi\rangle
has the following decomposition with respect to the first party and the remaining
n-1
parties:

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}s_{i}|a_{i}\rangle|B_{i}\rangle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}|\tilde{a}_{i}\rangle|\tilde{B}_{i}\rangle;

(S14)

here the first decomposition is a SD, and
\{|\tilde{a}_{i}\rangle\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}
is a basis on
\mathcal{H}_{d}
that is unbiased with the Schmidt basis
\{|a_{i}\rangle\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}
. To simplify the notation, the verification operator
\Omega_{\mathrm{SD}}
is abbreviated as
\Omega
. By definition
\Omega
can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\Omega=2^{1-n}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}P_{\mathbf{m}},

(S15)

where
P_{\mathbf{m}}
are test projectors featured in the SD protocol (see the main text and Sec.
S1.1
).

If
n=2
, then
|\Psi\rangle
is a bipartite pure state and we have
\Omega=(P_{0}+P_{1})/2
, where the two test projectors
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
are given in Eq. (4). It is straightforward to verify that
P_{1}
has rank
d
and
\operatorname{tr}(P_{0}P_{1})=1

Li et al. 2019
. Let
\bar{P}_{m}=P_{m}-|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|
for
m=0,1
, then
\bar{\Omega}=\Omega-|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|=(\bar{P}_{0}+\bar{P}_{1})/2
. In addition,
\bar{P}_{0}
and
\bar{P}_{1}
are mutually orthogonal projectors, and
\bar{P}_{1}
has rank
d-1\geq 1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\beta(\Omega)=\|\bar{\Omega}\|=\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{P}_{0}+\bar{P}_{1}\|=\frac{1}{2},\quad\nu(\Omega)=\frac{1}{2},

(S16)

which implies Eq. (5).
If
|\Psi\rangle
is entangled, then both
\bar{P}_{0}
and
\bar{P}_{1}
have rank at least 1, which implies that

\displaystyle\|p\bar{P}_{0}+(1-p)\bar{P}_{1}\|=\max\{p,1-p\}\geq\frac{1}{2}\quad\forall\,0\leq p\leq 1.

(S17)

So the choice
p=1/2
(which means
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
are chosen with uniform probabilities) is optimal.

Next, suppose the result
\nu(\Omega)\geq 2^{1-n}
holds for all
n
with
2\leq n\leq\tilde{n}
for some positive integer
\tilde{n}\geq 2
and let
n=\tilde{n}+1
. Then
P_{\mathbf{m}}
can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle P_{\mathbf{m}}=\begin{cases}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}|s_{i}>0}|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|\otimes P_{i,\mathbf{m}}&m_{1}=0,\\[4.30554pt]
\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}|\tilde{a}_{i}\rangle\langle\tilde{a}_{i}|\otimes\tilde{P}_{i,\mathbf{m}}&m_{1}=1,\end{cases}

(S18)

where
P_{i,\mathbf{m}}
(
\tilde{P}_{i,\mathbf{m}}
) are test projectors featured in the SD protocol applied to
|B_{i}\rangle
(
|\tilde{B}_{i}\rangle
). Let

\displaystyle\Omega_{i}=2^{2-n}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}|m_{1}=0}P_{i,\mathbf{m}},\quad\tilde{\Omega}_{i}=2^{2-n}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}|m_{1}=1}\tilde{P}_{i,\mathbf{m}},\quad\forall\,i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}\mbox{ with }s_{i}>0,

(S19)

\displaystyle\nu_{k}=2^{1-k},\quad\beta_{k}=1-\nu_{k}\quad\forall\,k=2,3,\ldots,n;

(S20)

then
\Omega_{i}
(
\tilde{\Omega}_{i}
) is a verification operator of
|B_{i}\rangle
(
|\tilde{B}_{i}\rangle
). In addition, by induction hypothesis we can deduce that

\displaystyle\Omega_{i}\leq\nu_{n-1}|B_{i}\rangle\langle B_{i}|+\beta_{n-1},\quad\tilde{\Omega}_{i}\leq\nu_{n-1}|\tilde{B}_{i}\rangle\langle\tilde{B}_{i}|+\beta_{n-1}.

(S21)

Now, if
|\Psi\rangle
is regarded as a bipartite pure state between the first party and the remaining
n-1
parties, then the two test projectors featured in the SD protocol read [cf. Eq. (4) in the main text]

\Pi_{0}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}|s_{i}>0}|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|\otimes|B_{i}\rangle\langle B_{i}|,\quad\Pi_{1}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}|\tilde{a}_{i}\rangle\langle\tilde{a}_{i}|\otimes|\tilde{B}_{i}\rangle\langle\tilde{B}_{i}|.

(S22)

In addition,
(\Pi_{0}+\Pi_{1})/2
is a verification operator of
|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|
with spectral gap
1/2
[cf. Eq. (
S16
)], that is,

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|\leq\frac{1}{2}(\Pi_{0}+\Pi_{1})\leq\frac{1}{2}(|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|+1).

(S23)

Combining the above results we can deduce that

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}|s_{i}>0}|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|\otimes\Omega_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}|\tilde{a}_{i}\rangle\langle\tilde{a}_{i}|\otimes\tilde{\Omega}_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}|s_{i}>0}|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|\otimes\left(\nu_{n-1}|B_{i}\rangle\langle B_{i}|+\beta_{n-1}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}|\tilde{a}_{i}\rangle\langle\tilde{a}_{i}|\otimes\left(\nu_{n-1}|\tilde{B}_{i}\rangle\langle\tilde{B}_{i}|+\beta_{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\nu_{n-1}(\Pi_{0}+\Pi_{1})+\beta_{n-1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\nu_{n-1}(|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|+1)+\beta_{n-1}=\nu_{n}|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|+\beta_{n},

(S24)

where
\nu_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
for
k=n-1,n
are defined in Eq. (
S20
).
This equation implies that
\nu(\Omega)\geq 2^{1-n}
, which in turn implies Eq. (5) and completes the proof of Theorem 1.
∎

S1.3
Verification of GHZ states

Here we clarify the performance of the SD protocol applied to the
n
-qudit GHZ state. It turns out that the SD protocol with uniform probabilities can only achieve spectral gap
2^{1-n}
, which saturates the lower bound in Theorem 1 and decreases exponentially with
n
. Nevertheless, the SD protocol with optimized probabilities can achieve spectral gap
1/2
, which is independent of the local dimension and qudit number.

Recall that the
n
-qudit GHZ state in
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes n}
can be expressed as follows:

|G_{d}^{n}\rangle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}|i\rangle^{\otimes n},

(S25)

which can be abbreviated as
|G\rangle
if there is no danger of confusion.
Here the computational basis for each party is also a Schmidt basis and the eigenbasis of the generalized Pauli operator
Z=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{i}|i\rangle\langle i|
. In addition, the Fourier basis coincides with the eigenbasis of the generalized Pauli operator
X=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}|i+1\rangle\langle i|
and is composed of the following kets:

|\tilde{a}_{i}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{ij}|j\rangle,\quad i=\mathbb{Z}_{d}.

(S26)

By virtue of the Fourier basis the GHZ state
|G\rangle
can also be expressed as follows:

|G\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}|\tilde{a}_{i}\rangle\otimes|\tilde{B}_{i}\rangle,\quad|\tilde{B}_{i}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{-ij}|j\rangle^{\otimes(n-1)}.

(S27)

Note that
|\tilde{B}_{i}\rangle
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
are GHZ-like states.

In general, when the SD protocol is applied to the GHZ state
|G\rangle
, each conditional reduced state that may appear in the verification procedure is either a product state or a GHZ-like state. In addition, the measurement basis of each party (including the last party) is the eigenbasis of either
Z
or
X
. In this special case, the SD protocol can actually be realized by nonadaptive Pauli
Z
and
X
measurements. If all the first
n-1
parties perform
X
measurements, then the last party performs an
X
measurement as well; otherwise, the last party performs a
Z
measurement. The
2^{n-1}
tests featured in the SD protocol can be labeled by strings in
Z_{2}^{n-1}
as in the general case. Here we are particularly interested in the two tests labeled by
\mathbf{0}=0^{n-1}
(which means all
Z
measurements) and
\mathbf{1}=1^{n-1}
(which means all
X
measurements); the two corresponding test projectors read

P_{\mathbf{0}}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}(|i\rangle\langle i|)^{\otimes n},\quad P_{\mathbf{1}}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\left(X^{\otimes n}\right)^{i},

(S28)

which have rank
d
and
d^{n-1}
, respectively. In addition, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(P_{\mathbf{0}}P_{\mathbf{1}})=1,\quad P_{\mathbf{m}}\geq P_{\mathbf{0}}\quad\forall\,\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}\setminus\{\mathbf{1}\}.

(S29)

Let
\bar{P}_{\mathbf{0}}=P_{\mathbf{0}}-|G\rangle\langle G|
and
\bar{P}_{\mathbf{1}}=P_{\mathbf{1}}-|G\rangle\langle G|
; then
\bar{P}_{\mathbf{0}}
and
\bar{P}_{\mathbf{1}}
are projectors that are mutually orthogonal.
If all tests
P_{\mathbf{m}}
for
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}
are performed with uniform probabilities, then

\Omega=2^{1-n}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}P_{\mathbf{m}}\geq\frac{2^{n-1}-1}{2^{n-1}}P_{\mathbf{0}}+\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}P_{\mathbf{1}},

(S30)

which implies that
\nu(\Omega)\leq 2^{1-n}
. In conjunction with Theorem 1 we can deduce that
\nu(\Omega)=2^{1-n}
, which saturates the lower bound in Theorem 1.

Next, we show that the spectral gap achieved by the SD protocol can be enhanced exponentially if the test probabilities are optimized. Suppose we perform the test
P_{\mathbf{m}}
with probability
p_{\mathbf{m}}
with
\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}p_{\mathbf{m}}=1
. Then

\Omega=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}p_{\mathbf{m}}P_{\mathbf{m}}\geq(1-p_{\mathbf{1}})P_{\mathbf{0}}+p_{\mathbf{1}}P_{\mathbf{1}},

(S31)

which implies that

\nu(\Omega)\leq\min\{1-p_{\mathbf{1}},p_{\mathbf{1}}\}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(S32)

Here the upper bound is saturated when
p_{\mathbf{1}}=p_{\mathbf{0}}=1/2
and
p_{\mathbf{m}}=0
for all
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}\setminus\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}\}
.

S3
MUB and CMUB protocols

In this section we illustrate the performance of the MUB and CMUB protocols and their variants in verifying GHZ states and Dicke states.

S3.1
Test projectors and verification operators

Recall that the MUB protocol consists of
2^{n-1}
projective tests, and each test is labeled by a string
\mathbf{m}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}
, where
m_{k}=0
means the measurement in the computational basis for party
k
, while
m_{k}=1
means the measurement in a basis mutually unbiased with the computational basis, say, the eigenbasis of the generalized Pauli operator
X
. The corresponding test projector
Q_{\mathbf{m}}
can be expressed as follows:

Q_{\mathbf{m}}=\sum_{\mathbf{o}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{n-1}}\Pi_{o_{1},m_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Pi_{o_{n-1},m_{n-1}}\otimes|\psi_{n,\mathbf{o},\mathbf{m}}\rangle\langle\psi_{n,\mathbf{o},\mathbf{m}}|,

(S39)

where
\Pi_{o_{k},m_{k}}
for each
k=1,2,\ldots,n-1
denotes the projector onto the
o_{k}
-th basis state associated with the computational basis (when
m_{k}=0
) or MUB (when
m_{k}=1
), and
|\psi_{n,\mathbf{o},\mathbf{m}}\rangle
is the conditional reduced state of the target state
|\Psi\rangle
for party
n
given the measurement setting
\mathbf{m}
and outcome
\mathbf{o}
.
Suppose the measurement setting
\mathbf{m}
is chosen with probability
q_{\mathbf{m}}
with
\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}q_{\mathbf{m}}=1
, then the overall verification operator reads

\Omega_{\mathrm{MUB}}=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}q_{\mathbf{m}}Q_{\mathbf{m}}.

(S40)

For the uniform probability distribution, that is,
q_{\mathbf{m}}=2^{1-n}
for all
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}
, we have
\Omega_{\mathrm{MUB}}=2^{1-n}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}Q_{\mathbf{m}}
. By contrast, the CMUB protocol consists of only two tests labeled by the strings
\mathbf{m}=0^{n-1}
and
\mathbf{m}=1^{n-1}
, respectively.

Recall that the respective eigenbases of
X
,
Z
, and
Y=\mathrm{i}XZ
are mutually unbiased, from which we can construct the 3MUB protocol and 3CMUB protocol (when the measurement bases are correlated). If
d

S3.2
Verification of GHZ states

Here we clarify the performance of the MUB protocol applied to the
n
-qudit GHZ state

|G_{d}^{n}\rangle
in Eq. (
S25
). For concreteness, we focus on the MUB protocol based on generalized Pauli
Z

X

Z
coincides with the computational basis, while the eigenbasis of
X
coincides with the Fourier basis presented in Eq. (
S26
).
For the GHZ state
|G_{d}^{n}\rangle
, therefore, the MUB protocol is equivalent to the SD protocol (see Sec.

). Accordingly, the MUB protocol with uniform test probabilities can only achieve spectral gap
2^{1-n}
, while the MUB protocol with optimized probabilities can achieve spectral gap
1/2
, which is independent of the local dimension and qudit number. Note that the optimal MUB protocol consists of only two distinct tests and coincides with the CMUB protocol with uniform test probabilities.

If the local dimension
d
is a prime, then the respective eigenbases of
X,XZ,XZ^{2},\dots,XZ^{d-1},Z
form a complete set of MUB as mentioned above, from which we can construct a cMUB protocol. For the
n
-qudit GHZ state, some variant of the cMUB protocol was studied earlier. According to Ref.
Li et al. 2020
, the cMUB protocol with optimized test probabilities can achieve spectral gap
d/(d+1)
, which is the maximum spectral gap achievable by local operations and classical communication and increases monotonically with the local dimension
d
.

S4.1
Test projectors and verification operators

The SMUB protocol is constructed by randomizing the choice of the last party in the MUB protocol, as illustrated in Fig.
S6
. It is composed of
2^{n-1}n
tests, and each test is labeled by the choice of the last party
k
and a string
\mathbf{m}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}
, which specifies the measurement setting of the other
n-1
parties. In analogy to Eq. (
S39
), the corresponding test projector can be expressed as follows:

Q_{\mathbf{m}}^{k}=\sum_{\mathbf{o}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{n-1}}\Pi_{o_{1},m_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Pi_{o_{k-1},m_{k-1}}\otimes|\psi_{k,\mathbf{o},\mathbf{m}}\rangle\langle\psi_{k,\mathbf{o},\mathbf{m}}|\otimes\Pi_{o_{k+1},m_{k+1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Pi_{o_{n},m_{n}},

(S41)

where
\Pi_{o_{l},m_{l}}
for each
l\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{k\}
denotes the projector onto the
o_{l}
-th basis state associated with the computational basis (when
m_{l}=0
) or MUB (when
m_{l}=1
),
and
|\psi_{k,\mathbf{o},\mathbf{m}}\rangle
is the conditional reduced state of the target state
|\Psi\rangle
for party
k
given the measurement setting
\mathbf{m}
and outcome
\mathbf{o}
.

Suppose party
k
is chosen with probability
p_{k}
and, for a given
k
, the test
Q_{\mathbf{m}}^{k}
is chosen with probability
q_{\mathbf{m}}^{k}
, assuming that
\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}=1
and
\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}q_{\mathbf{m}}^{k}=1
for each
k
. Then the verification operator associated with party
k
reads

\Omega_{\mathrm{MUB},k}=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}q_{\mathbf{m}}^{k}Q_{\mathbf{m}}^{k},

(S42)

and the overall verification operator reads

\Omega_{\mathrm{SMUB}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}\Omega_{\mathrm{MUB},k}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}p_{k}q_{\mathbf{m}}^{k}Q_{\mathbf{m}}^{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}\tilde{q}_{\mathbf{m}}^{k}Q_{\mathbf{m}}^{k},

(S43)

where
\tilde{q}_{\mathbf{m}}^{k}=p_{k}q_{\mathbf{m}}^{k}
. If all tests are chosen with the same probability, then

\Omega_{\mathrm{MUB},k}=2^{1-n}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}Q_{\mathbf{m}}^{k},\quad\Omega_{\mathrm{SMUB}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Omega_{\mathrm{MUB},k}=\frac{2}{2^{n}n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}Q_{\mathbf{m}}^{k}.

(S44)

The test projectors and verification operators of the 3SMUB, SCMUB, and 3SCMUB protocols (and other variants) can be constructed in a similar way. Note that the 3SMUB protocol is composed of
3^{n-1}n
tests, and each test
Q_{\mathbf{m}}^{k}
is labeled by the choice of the last party
k
and a string
\mathbf{m}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{n-1}
, which specifies the measurement setting of the other
n-1
parties. The test set of the SCMUB (3SCMUB) protocol is contained in the counterpart of the SMUB (3SMUB) protocol.

S6
Verification protocols beyond MUB

According to the previous discussions, symmetrization and more local bases can usually increase the spectral gap, especially when the local dimension
d
is small. In this section we are particularly interested in verification protocols whose test numbers do not increase with the qudit number
n
, so more local bases are crucial for enhancing the efficiency.
However, each MUB in dimension
d

d+1
bases, so we need to consider verification protocols beyond MUB if more local bases are required. Here, we present some preliminary results for the special case
d=2
, in which the performance of the CMUB and 3CMUB protocols are not satisfactory as reflected in Fig.

Figure S14:
Averaged spectral gaps achieved by the 4C-Tet, 6C-Ico, 4SC-Tet, and 6SC-Ico protocols and six variants of the MUB protocol (under uniform probability distributions) for
n
-qubit Haar-random pure states.

In the case of a qubit, each Bloch vector (or point on the Bloch sphere) determines a (orthonormal) basis and the corresponding rank-1 projective measurement (note that two antipodal points determine the same basis essentially). For example, from
the six vertices of a regular octahedron inscribed in the Bloch sphere we can generate three bases that are mutually unbiased and thereby construct multiple variants of the 3MUB protocol. In a similar spirit we can generate four bases from the vertices of a regular tetrahedron (Tet):

\bm{u}_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1,1,1),\quad\bm{u}_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1,-1,-1),\quad\bm{u}_{3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(-1,1,-1),\quad\bm{u}_{4}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(-1,-1,1).

(S51)

By virtue of these bases and correlated measurements
we can construct the 4C-Tet and 4SC-Tet protocols in analogy to the 3CMUB and 3SCMUB protocols. Likewise, we can generate six bases from six of the twelve vertices of a regular icosahedron (Ico):

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+g^{2}}}(1,g,0),\quad

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+g^{2}}}(-1,g,0),\quad

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+g^{2}}}(0,1,g),

(S52)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+g^{2}}}(0,-1,g),\quad

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{5}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+g^{2}}}(g,0,1),\quad

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{6}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+g^{2}}}(g,0,-1),

where
g=(1+\sqrt{5})/2
.
By virtue of these bases and correlated measurements we can construct the 6C-Ico and 6SC-Ico protocols. Note that the four protocols 4C-Tet, 6C-Ico, 4SC-Tet, and 6SC-Ico consist of 4, 6,
4n
, and
6n
distinct tests, respectively.

Increasing the number of local bases tend to increase the average spectral gap as expected. For the CMUB, 3CMUB and 4C-Tet protocols, the average spectral gaps decrease monotonically with
n

n

n

In addition, the 6SC-Ico protocol is the most efficient among the ten protocols, although its test number increases only linearly with
n

S7
Comparison with the HPS protocol
Huang et al. 2024

Recently, HPS proposed a protocol that can certify almost all
n
-qubit pure states using single-qubit measurements
Huang et al. 2024
. In this Protocol (Level-
m
), all
n
parties except for
m
parties (chosen uniformly at random) perform Pauli
Z
measurements, then each of the remaining parties performs one of the three Pauli measurements (
X
,
Y
, and
Z
) randomly. After
N
runs, one can compute the shadow overlap based on the measurement outcomes. The state prepared is accepted if the shadow overlap is larger than a certain threshold and rejected otherwise. To verify the target state
|\Psi\rangle
within infidelity
\varepsilon
and significance level
\delta
, the sample cost (number of measurements) required reads
T=2^{2m+4}\frac{\tau^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\ln(\frac{2}{\delta})
, where
\tau
is the relaxation time of a Markov chain determined by the target state
|\Psi\rangle
.
If
|\Psi\rangle
is a Haar-random pure state, then
\tau=\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
with probability exponentially close to 1.

In general, the HPS protocol cannot be formulated in the framework of standard QSV considered in this work. Nevertheless, HPS also proposed a variant of the Level-1 protocol (
m=1
), which is compatible with the framework of standard QSV
Li
: all
n
parties except for one party (chosen uniformly at random) perform Pauli
Z
measurements, then the last party performs the projective measurement onto the conditional reduced state of the target state. In this way, the sample cost can further be reduced to
T=32\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon}\ln(\frac{1}{\delta})
; here
1/\tau
corresponds to the spectral gap in the framework of standard QSV.

Although the HPS protocol can certify almost all
n
-qubit pure states with
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
samples, it cannot certify certain important multiqubit states directly, such as the GHZ state and Dicke states, because of the divergence of the relaxation time
\tau
.
In the perspective of standard QSV, this means the spectral gap is zero. Consider the GHZ state
|G_{d}^{n}\rangle
defined in Eq. (
S25
) for example. The
n
test projectors and the verification operator featured in the HPS protocol are all equal to

\Omega=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}(|i\rangle\langle i|)^{\otimes n},

(S53)

so the spectral gap is equal to zero. In this special case, nevertheless, this problem can be resolved by modifying the measurement bases using the Hadamard gate.
Next, consider the Dicke state
|D_{d}^{n}(\mathbf{n})\rangle
defined in Eq. (
S34
). Now, the
n
test projectors and the verification operator featured in the HPS protocol are all equal to

\Omega=\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{n})}|\mathbf{j}\rangle\langle\mathbf{j}|,

(S54)

so the spectral gap is equal to zero too. It is possible to increase the spectral gap by modifying the measurement bases; however, a universal nontrivial lower bound for the spectral gap is still not available. By contrast, the spectral gap achieved by our SD (CSD) protocol with uniform test probabilities
is lower bounded by
2^{1-n}
for all
n
-qudit pure states (including
n
-qubit pure states). Moreover, for Haar-random pure states, the spectral gaps (sample complexities) are lower (upper) bounded by a universal constant according to extensive numerical calculations.
