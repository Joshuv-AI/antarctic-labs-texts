Wettability Alteration of Berea Sandstone for Gas Condensate Applications
Authors: Shabeeb Alajmei
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractGas condensate reservoirs can suffer significant decreases in production due to the buildup of liquid in the vicinity of the wellbore, which occurs when the bottom-hole flowing pressure drops below the dew point pressure. Consequently, the generated liquid hydrocarbons can hinder the movement of the produced gas by adhering to the surfaces, thereby creating a condensate bank. One potential method for mitigating the issue of condensate banking involves the injection of chemical treatment and the alteration of wettability from a liquid-wet state to an intermediate gas-wet state. This study conducted an experimental investigation of the impact of fluorochemical treatment on altering the wettability from liquid-wetting to intermediate gas-wetting. The wettability of the Berea sandstone was analyzed before and after chemical treatment over a temperature range of 25–83 °C. The outcrop core samples of Berea sandstone used in this investigation exhibited an average porosity and permeability of 20% and 100 mD, respectively. The experimental results indicate that the application of chemical treatment has the potential to alter the wettability of Berea sandstone, transitioning it from a state of liquid-wetting to gas-wetting at standard temperatures. The chemical treatment alters the wettability from liquid-wet to intermediate gas-wet at higher temperatures. Furthermore, the alteration of wettability substantially improves the mobility of the oil phase and decreases the residual saturation of the oil, thereby aiding the reduction of liquid accumulation around the wellbore. According to this research, altering the wettability of the rock surrounding the wellbore in gas condensate reservoirs from a state of strong liquid-wet to gas-wet has the potential to enhance the deliverability of gas wells and improve injectivity in the field.

Introduction

The oil and gas sector is putting more effort into developing new technologies and solutions to improve hydrocarbon production. One method is to stimulate gas-condensate wells by altering the wettability and removing condensate banking. Gas reservoirs are classified into various categories based on their composition, pressure, and temperature. Gas condensate reservoirs induce the formation of liquid hydrocarbons near the wellbore by lowering the pressure below the dew point, as production continues. Once formed, the condensate is confined by capillary pressure and poor liquid mobility causes the condensate to adhere to the grain surface. In addition, drilling and workover activities and the presence of a nearby water aquifer are the main sources of water accumulation near the wellbore.

The formation of liquid banking significantly reduces the productivity of gas reservoirs by having richer gas as the bottom-hole pressure falls below the dew point pressure.1 Bang et al.2 reported the gas relative permeability could experience up to a 95% reduction due to liquid banking near the wellbore, thereby stopping gas production entirely in many cases.3 However, the condensate bank formed around the wellbore can be treated using gas injection or surface/interfacial tension reduction or can be delayed by hydraulic fracturing and horizontal completion.4−11 Under reservoir conditions, increasing condensate saturation is highly correlated with a reduction in the relative gas permeability in the presence of an immobile liquid.12 The presence of 19% connate water during condensate formation may decrease the relative permeability of the gas by 60%.13

To increase the gas productivity of the wells, it may be effective to permanently change the wettability of the area around the wells from liquid-wet to gas-wet. Wettability is defined based on the strength of the interfacial tension between the solids and liquids. The strong interfacial tension in the wetting fluid causes the fluid to form a 180° contact angle with the grain surface. Conversely, nonwetting fluids produce a contact angle close to zero. The interaction of the grain surfaces with both the hydrocarbon components and brine chemistry determines the degree of wettability alteration by depositing organic material or adsorbing polar components.14 However, the contact angle is highly influenced by the roughness of the rock surface.15 Li et al.16 investigated the effect of surface roughness numerically. Their work included a three-dimensional (3D) model to correct for NMR T2 reduction. Another theoretical model was developed to investigate the wettability using molecular dynamics.17 They found a strong relationship between the hydrophobicity and the fluorine quantity.

Several studies have been conducted to determine the best chemical treatment to increase the relative permeability of gas and eventually increase gas production.17−23 Few experiments have been conducted to evaluate the ability of different surfactants to alter the wettability of different formations, including chalks.24 A copolymer emulsion synthesized with acrylate increased gas mobility by mitigating the liquid-blocking effect.25 The spontaneous imbibition experiments indicated a reduction in the gas recovery after treatment from 33 to 10% and from 12 to 5% using water and oil, respectively.

A few studies have been conducted using chelating agents, viscoelastic, and Gemini surfactants to alter the wettability of carbonate formations.26,27 Jafarbeigi et al.28 investigated the effect of reducing the interfacial tension on altering the wettability of carbonate formations. The use of nanofluids increased the hydrocarbon recovery by 26%.

Lately, there has been a growing interest within the energy industry toward wettability alteration. This interest emerges not just from the need to enhance well productivity but also to explore the potential for CO2 sequestration.29,30 Mehdiyev et al.31 modeled wettability alteration during carbonated water flooding for CO2 sequestration and utilization.

In this paper, two distinct solvents were combined with a chemical surfactant at certain weight percentages, and the mixture was then tested at ambient temperature and at a maximum temperature of 180 °F (83 °C).32 Using this combination, the wettability of the Gray Berea (GB) core samples changes from liquid-wet to intermediate gas-wet or preferably gas-wet. Various techniques have been used to assess the efficiency of the treatments including spontaneous decane imbibition, contact angle, and two-phase relative permeability measurements.

Experimental Methodology

Material

The effect of the chemical treatment on the multiphase flow was evaluated using deionized water to simulate the water phase, whereas the gas phase was represented by nitrogen. Normal decane (n-C10) with a specific gravity of 0.73 at T = 25 °C was used as a proxy for the condensate (liquid) phase in all the tests. The surfactant used in the chemical treatment comprises up to 90% fluorinated chemicals that adhere to the surface of the sandstone rock. The fluorochemical group is used to resist both the water and oil components, allowing it to change the wettability from liquid-wet to gas-wet. The main purpose of the anionic and nonionic groups is to make the polymer soluble in water.22

Experimental Setup

Contact Angle

One method to ascertain whether chemical treatment has altered wettability is to conduct a contact angle test.33−36 The treated core plug was positioned on a stage, and a digital camera was placed nearby to simultaneously capture the contact angle. To quantify the contact angles of water and condensate, a drop of either deionized water or normal decane was placed on top of the treated core plug using a pipet at approximately 25 °C. Then, the contact angle was calculated by doubling the maximum elevation angle estimated using ImageJ software. In the treated core plug, the water surfaces made contact angles ranging from 110 to 140°, while contact angles between 45 and 80° were formed when surfaces were oil-wet.37

Spontaneous Imbibition

Spontaneous imbibition measurements were performed under ambient pressure and temperature, and the ultimate recovery was measured after approximately 24 h. Before chemical treatment, a reference spontaneous imbibition of the cores was performed. A second spontaneous imbibition was performed after treating the core plug to examine the recovery by comparing the two different imbibition behaviors using normal decane as the wetting phase. Spontaneous imbibition tests were performed by hanging the air-saturated dry core plug beneath an electronic balance and submerging it in a normal decane, as shown in Figure 1.

Spontaneous imbibition apparatus.

Core Flooding

Core flooding experiments were performed at temperatures ranging from 25 to 83 °C. The core was inserted into the core holder, which was then placed in an oven for elevated temperature applications. Figure 2 shows a schematic of the core flooding apparatus. The confining pressure applied to the core was set to 200 psi and monitored over time. To introduce initial water saturation to the core, a small volume of deionized water was injected shortly before treatment injection at a constant injection rate of 2 cc/min. The core plug was then aged inside the core holder for 12 h at a specified temperature. After the core was aged by chemical treatment, air was injected to mimic the gas in the reservoir.

Contact Angle

Treating the BGB core sample with 2% of the chemical surfactant created a contact angle of 30° with deionized water. However, the core sample remained oil-wet at a 0° contact angle. Another contact angle measurement was performed by treating the Berea sample with 4% chemical treatment, which changed the wettability and increased the contact angles of deionized water and normal decane to 71 and 73°, respectively. The contact angles of normal decane and deionized water were unexpectedly reduced to 47 and 61°, respectively, by increasing the percentage of the chemical surfactant to 8%. Spontaneous Imbibition

Figure 6 shows the gas recovery in fractions by conducting spontaneous oil imbibition before and after treating the Berea core sample (BGB5–1–1) with 2% chemical treatment. This shows that the gas recovery was reduced after the chemical treatment. However, the ultimate gas recovery after treatment was significant, at over 60%. The oil spontaneous imbibition of the treated Berea sand (BGB4) showed similar behavior. However, the recovery of the treated sample showed a trend very similar to that of the untreated sample and reached a higher ultimate gas recovery, close to 70%, compared to the result of using 2% chemical treatment. The spontaneous oil imbibition of the treated Berea sand (BGB4) exhibits comparable characteristics. However, the recovery of the treated sample followed a similar pattern, toward the end of the experiment, to that of the untreated sample, and achieved a greater final gas recovery of close to 70% compared to the result of using 2% of the chemical treatment, as shown in Figure 7. Treating Berea sandstone (BGB2) with 8% chemical treatment increased the gas recovery even more than did the untreated sample, as shown in Figure 8. This implies that the wettability changed as a result of the Berea core plugs being treated with varying percentages of chemical treatment.

Effect of Temperature

The same chemical treatment was used to alter the wettability of the Berea sandstone at an elevated temperature of 180 °F (83 °C). Table 2 shows the weight measurements of the mixed solvents and chemical treatments for GB2–2. The core samples were subjected to chemical treatment at high temperatures for approximately 12 h. Figure 9 shows the spontaneous imbibition before and after treatment of the core sample with the chemical mixture listed in Table 2. The normal decane imbibition increased slightly at the beginning of the experiment, allowing more decane to enter the core sample. This reverse effect of the chemical effect diminished at the end of the imbibition experiment after reaching thermal stability. However, the contact angle experiment (Figure 10) shows very low contact angles of 10 and 0° between the rock sample and both deionized water and normal decane, suggesting strong liquid-wetting. This is evidence that the contact angle measurements alone do not reflect the wettability state. Wettability is strongly affected by temperature, interfacial tension, pore-throat radius, and wetting angle.14,35,38

Relative Permeability

This was the final experiment to assess the viability of the treatment, and it was performed on 6 in. cores. The initial core was left untreated to produce a base relative permeability curve and compare it with the relative permeability curves of the treated cores. The Berea sandstone was saturated with normal decane before the relative permeability measurements. Figure 11 shows the gas and oil relative permeabilities for both the untreated Berea core sample (BGB3) and the treated sample (BGB4) with 4% chemical treatment. Although the oil saturation at the point where both oil and gas relative permeabilities are equal did not indicate a considerable drop, the residual oil saturation after treating the core sample with 4% chemical treatment decreased from 0.37 to 0.31. This reduction in the residual oil saturations implies an increase in the oil recovery after treating the reservoir rock. In addition, the chemical treatment alters the wettability from oil-wet to intermediate gas-wet. This is attributed to the effect of the chemical treatment, which repels more residual oil to become movable oil and reduces the effect of condensate banking. Furthermore, treating the core sample with 8% chemical treatment reduced the residual oil saturation from 0.37 to 0.2. Figure 12 clearly depicts the drop in oil saturation from 0.6 to 0.5, which is the point at which the relative permeabilities of the gas and oil are the same. At residual oil saturation, the relative gas permeability increased from 0.8 to 0.86. Additionally, the oil saturation at residual gas saturation increased from 0.3 to 0.58. This allows more oil particles to move to the wellbore and further eliminates the banking of the condensate around the wellbore. The results from the study are consistent with the results obtained by Tang and Firoozabadi.22

Conclusions

In this investigation, the ability of the chemical treatment to alter the wettability of the Berea sandstone from oil-wet to intermediate gas-wet and gas-wet conditions was investigated by considering different methods, including contact angle, spontaneous imbibition, and relative permeability experiments.

The key findings of this research are as follows:

Using a mixture of propylene glycol and isopropanol as a solvent for the chemical surfactant was ineffective in altering the wettability of the Berea sandstone at elevated temperatures.

Using a mixture of propylene glycol and isopropanol as a solvent for the chemical surfactant was ineffective in altering the wettability of the Berea sandstone at elevated temperatures.

Using a chemical surfactant with a mixture of 2-butoxyethanol and ethanol was successful in altering the wettability of Berea sandstone at high temperatures from liquid-wet to intermediate gas-wet. Furthermore, the chemical treatment altered the wettability from strong liquid-wet to gas-wet under surface conditions.
