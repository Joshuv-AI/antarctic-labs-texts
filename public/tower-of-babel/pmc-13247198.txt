Parental expectations and nocebo by proxy effects in adolescent orthodontic treatment outcomes
Authors: Mette Schmidt, Lene Vase, Kasper Dahl Kristensen, Peter Stoustrup
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examines the nocebo effect in orthodontics by assessing how parental orthodontic experiences and attitudes toward dental care influence children’s pain and discomfort at treatment onset. Consecutive orthodontic patients aged 12–18 years were included in this prospective observational cohort study. Parents’ prior orthodontic experiences, perceptions of dental care, and pain expectations were assessed by questionnaire. Children completed the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS) and the Spielberger State Trait Anxiety Inventory for Children (STAIC) before fixed appliance bonding. Follow-up questionnaires 3–5 days post-bonding assessed pain, dysfunction, medication use, and oral health impact (OHIP-14). Non-parametric tests and regression analyses were used to assess associations between parental expectations and patient-reported outcomes. Seventy patients (mean age 14.1 ± 1.7 years; 51.3% females) were included. Greater pain medication use was reported among patients whose parents had prior orthodontic experience (p = 0.038). Parental associations of orthodontic care with pain were linked to higher patient anxiety and pain catastrophizing. Regression analyses confirmed associations between parental expectations and patient orofacial dysfunction, anxiety, pain catastrophizing, and oral health impact. The study indicates a proxy nocebo effect in orthodontics, where parental experiences and perceptions influence children’s pain during treatment. Addressing parental perceptions may help reduce this effect and improve treatment experiences for children and adolescents.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

The nocebo effect is characterized by negative outcomes associated with a treatment, caused by factors such as negative expectations, and is perceived as the negative counterpart to the placebo effect1. In recent medical research, the nocebo effect, has gained attention, particularly in health care settings where it has been shown to be induced by clinicians to the patient2–5. This influence often manifests when describing medication side effects, leading to an increased experience of those effects6,7. Although limited research exists on this phenomenon in dentistry and orthodontics8, current evidence suggests that factors such as verbal suggestion9, the patient-provider relationship10, patient expectations11,12 and anxiety likely play a role in dental and orthodontic practice, as well as in other clinical contexts.

Pain emerges as the predominant adverse effect associated with orthodontic treatment using fixed appliances13. This discomfort may discourage certain patients from initiating or continuing with treatment and may additionally cause a negative impact on their compliance during the treatment process14. Recently, there has been a growing focus on developing methods to prevent unnecessary pain in orthodontics. Studies are now investigating how orthodontists convey information about treatment to patients9. This aligns with trends in the broader medical field, recognizing that the nocebo effect contributes significantly to health implications15.

Pain is a complex and multifaceted experience that can be viewed from various perspectives16. It is influenced by several psychological factors, including personality traits, anxiety, and depression17. Research suggests that these factors significantly impact how individuals perceive and respond to pain18. To better understand this relationship, researchers have developed questionnaires that assess anxiety levels and the tendency to catastrophize pain. Numerous studies have found strong correlations between pain and high scores on these scales19. One such tool, the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS), has shown significant overlap with symptoms of anxiety and depression20,21. Studies focusing on children have revealed that their tendency to catastrophize pain is closely linked to their baseline levels of trait anxiety22,23. Moreover, this catastrophic thinking has been identified as a strong predictor of both pain and disability six months later24.

The potential contribution of parental-induced nocebo effect in their children is termed “nocebo by proxy”25. Despite the crucial role as primary caregivers in pain management the effect remains unclear. Existing literature suggests a nocebo by proxy effect25, but there is a notable scarcity of clinical studies exploring this phenomenon.

This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between a child’s pain experience during the early stages of orthodontic treatment and their parents’ previous experience with treatment and perceptions of general dental care. The suggested hypothesis is that parents recalling negative or painful orthodontic experiences may inadvertently shape their child’s expectations, resulting in an elevated perception of pain and discomfort during treatment.

Materials and methods

Subjects

This prospective observational cohort study enrolled consecutive patients, aged 12–18 years, treated at the Section of Orthodontics, Section of Dentistry and Oral Health, Aarhus University, Denmark. All patients with an appointment for starting orthodontic treatment with fixed appliances at the post-graduate clinic at the Sections of Orthodontics from September 2022 to October 2023 were invited to participate in the study. The participants were identified from a convenience sample of patients who met the criteria for tax-funded orthodontic treatment set by the Danish Health Authorities. The participants came from both urban and suburban backgrounds, and their characteristics are comparable to those of the 25% of Danish teenagers who receive tax-funded orthodontic treatment. Non-participation in this study did not affect any aspect of the planned orthodontic treatment.

Prior to enrolment, all participants and their parents were provided with standard pre-orthodontic information, including risks related to orthodontic care and advice regarding use of pain medication after bond-up if needed. Participants provided informed written consent. To avoid introducing bias, the term ‘nocebo’ was not mentioned in the information provided to patients and parents regarding the aim of the project. Parental consent was obtained for participants below the age of 15 years in agreement with Danish regulation on questionnaire-based research. In all cases, parental consent was obtained, and they were provided with relevant information.

Patients were excluded if they were unable to respond to the questionnaires due to cognitive impairment. Additionally, individuals undergoing treatment with functional appliances, aligners and other removable orthopedic appliances were excluded.

Due to the lack of prior research on this specific topic, a formal sample size calculation was not conducted. As a result, the sample size was determined based on feasibility and availability of participants within the study population. This approach may limit the statistical power of the study and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Treatment and information

Patients enrolled in the study were only those who were planned for bonding with conventional full fixed orthodontic appliances (FFA). However, no distinction was made in relation to whether temporary anchorage devices for skeletal anchorage were used in the first bonding session. Written and verbal information regarding any pain associated with the placement of appliances was provided to the patient and their parents.

Study design

Eligible patients and parents accepting the invitation to participate were asked to complete individual electronic questionnaires at the day of bonding of fixed orthodontic appliance. In addition, patients were asked to complete electronic follow-up questionnaires at 3–5 days after bonding of orthodontic appliance. A Research electronic database (Redcap) (Vanderbilt University, USA) was established to facilitate data collection. Within this database, a series of seven distinct questionnaires were configured to be completed in a specific sequence. Participants were requested to attend at the orthodontic clinic 10 min prior to their initial bonding appointment to complete the questionnaires on their personal devices. Questionnaires prior to orthodontic treatment.

Questionnaires completed by parents

Both parents were invited to separately answer the survey. However, it was only necessary for one parent to complete the questionnaires in order for inclusion. At the time of bonding of the orthodontic appliance, parents were requested to individually fill out a survey that covered various aspects, including personal history of orthodontics, their general perception of orthodontic and dental care, as well as their expectations concerning their child’s forthcoming treatment. Based on the questionnaire responses from the parents, we defined two outcome scores for each patient: (1) A score reflecting the parent’s general association of orthodontic and dental treatment with pain and discomfort, and (2) a score representing the parent’s expectations of pain and discomfort associated with the current orthodontic treatment.

For both outcomes, parents were asked to select one of the following response options: 0 = no, 1 = minor, 2 = moderate, and 3 = severe. The final score for each outcome was determined by the highest score reported by either of the patient’s two parents. In cases where only one parent completed the online questionnaire, that parent’s responses were used to determine the score for both outcomes. Additionally, if only one parent responded, they were asked to provide information on the spouse/partner’s prior experiences with orthodontic treatment.

Questionnaire completed by patients at bonding of orthodontic appliance

Immediately before placement of the FFA, the child completed translated versions of the validated Spielberger State Trait Anxiety Inventory for children (STAIC)26 and the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS)27.

The Danish-translated and validated version of the State Trait Anxiety Inventory scale was employed to evaluate the participants’ anxiety levels. The assessment utilized the 40-items STAIC, which comprises two distinct subscales, each containing 20 items. One subscale measures state anxiety, reflecting the current emotional state of the child/adolescent (STAIC-C1), while the other assesses trait anxiety (STAIC-C2), reflecting a stable aspect of an individual’s personality28.

In the state anxiety inventory, participants were instructed to reflect on their present feelings. Conversely, for the trait anxiety inventory, individuals were prompted to consider their general emotional state. Response options for each item spanned a range from one to three: 1= “hardly-ever”, 2= “sometimes”, and 3= “often”. The sum of the questionnaire is obtained by summing the responses to each of the items. The total questionnaire score was calculated from the scores of the subscales and the questionnaire score ranges between 20 and 60, with higher scores indicating greater levels of anxiety.

Furthermore the participants filled out the validated Danish version of the widely utilized Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS), which assesses cognitive-affective responses to anticipated or actual pain29,30. The PCS consists of 13 self-reported statements, where participants rate the intensity of each item on a scale from 0 to 4, where 0 = “not at all”, 1 = “to a slight degree”, 2 = “to a moderate degree”, 3 = “to a great degree”, and 4 = “all the time”. A higher score on this scale indicates higher levels of pain catastrophizing.

Both conventional and self-ligating bracket systems were used in the included patients. As a general principle, orthodontic treatment was initiated with a round nickel–titanium archwire, typically 0.014 or 0.018, depending on the clinical situation.

Questionnaires completed 3–5 days after initial orthodontic bonding

Three to five days after the placement of the fixed appliances, participants received an e-mail with two additional questionnaires. To be included in the analysis, the questionnaires had to be completed within this time frame.

The follow-up questionnaires aimed to evaluate the overall pain experience perceived by the child/adolescent patient during the specific period, where multiple studies have shown that patients are most likely to experience pain/discomfort14,31–33.

Participants were asked five questions to investigate their pain experience, which are listed below:

To further explore the possible negative effect of pain after applying FFA, the participants were asked to complete a modified version of the Danish Oral Health Impact Profile (OHIP-14) questionnaire to assess the perception of oral disorders on their well-being of the child. The OHIP-14 consists of 14 questions that cover a range of oral health-related issues, including functional, psychological, and social dimensions that can be affected with FFA34. Respondents are asked to rate the extent to which they have experienced specific problems or difficulties related to their oral health over a certain period. The questions are structured in a way that allows individuals to express the frequency or severity of their experiences, typically using a Likert-type scale (e.g. “never”, “hardly ever”, “occasionally”, “fairly often”, “very often”). For the present study we used the general combined score as the outcome and did not conduct any subdomain analyses34.

Patients who did not automatically complete the follow-up questions were once reminded to do so by telephone within the 3–5 day timespan.

The second part of this study assessed the association between parental general perception of orthodontics and general dentistry with pain and discomfort, as well as anxiety and pain catastrophizing, at baseline prior to treatment initiation and after the first 3–5 days of fixed appliance orthodontic treatment. Specifically, we evaluated inter-group differences at baseline to assess the potential influence of parental perception on latent levels of state and trait anxiety, as well as tendencies to catastrophize about pain, before the initiation of treatment.

Statistics

Visual data distribution methods such as Q-Q plots and histograms were used to assess the data distribution which was found to be non-normally distributed. Wilcoxon rank sum tests and chi-square tests were used for the evaluation of inter-group differences. Fisher´s exact test was used in tests where one or more cells had a frequency of less than 5 cases. Binomial logistic regression analyses were used to examine the potential relationship between various patient-related dichotomous metrics (dependent variables: Pain during orthodontic care, consumption of painkillers, and orofacial dysfunction) and the degree of parental general associations of orthodontic/general dental treatment with pain and discomfort (no, minor, moderate, severe pain) (independent variable). Linear regression analyses and scatterplots were created to assess the relationship between continuous patient metrics (dependent variables: Pain intensity, degree of orofacial dysfunction, STAIC-C1, STAIC-C2, pain catastrophizing questionnaire score, OHIP-14) and the degree of parental general associations of orthodontic/general dental treatment with pain and discomfort (independent variable). Patients with missing data were contacted by email within the 3–5 days period. Patients were excluded from the data analyses if they failed to comply with the completion of the questionnaire. The statistical evaluation was conducted using StataCorp. Stata Statistical Software: Release 15. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC, 2023.

Ethics

Participation in the project was voluntary and oral and written consent was given by both patients and parents prior to inclusion. The study was conducted in accordance with the Danish Health Authority’s regulations for questionnaire-based studies in healthcare. According to Danish legislation on questionnaire- based studies ethical approval is deemed unnecessary and the current study therefore does not require specific approval from a Research Ethics Committee (Komitéloven § 14). The handling of data was approved by the Danish Data Protection Agency through an internal notification from Aarhus University (2022-0367531/3255) and complied with applicable data protection regulations.

The study was reported in adherence with the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines35.

Results

A convenience sample of 121 eligible consecutive patients were prospectively invited to participate in the project. After revision of the electronic questionnaire feedback from patient-parents’ dyads, we were able to include 70 patients in the present study; mean age 14.1 years (SD: 1.7 years), 51.3% females, inclusion rate: 58%. The most frequent reason for exclusion was patients failing to respond to follow up e-mails, even though they received an additional phone call reminding them to do so.

None of the included patients received temporary anchorage devices during treatment initiation or at the initial bonding session.

Discussion

The present study provides valuable insights into the impact of parental experiences and expectations on patient outcomes and coping mechanisms in relation to pain and discomfort in the early stages of orthodontic treatment. Our findings suggest that a nocebo effect by proxy may occur in orthodontics, as parents’ previous orthodontic experiences and overall perceptions of dental care significantly influence their children’s pain experiences during treatment (Table 2). To our knowledge, this is the first study to report on the potential negative impact of parental influence in teenage orthodontic patients.

Our analysis revealed that children whose parents had prior orthodontic experience consumed more pain medication than those whose parents did not. While this association was statistically significant (p = 0.038), its practical significance requires careful consideration. The confidence intervals for the prevalence of pain medication use were broad and overlapping (patients with parents who had prior orthodontic experience: 69.23%, 95% CI = 56.8–81.7%; patients with parents with no history of orthodontic care: 41.18%, 95% CI = 17.8–64.6%), suggesting variability in the observed trends.

Additionally, the effect sizes of the regression models (R² = 0.09 and 0.07) indicate a modest explanatory power of the independent variables. This suggests that while parental orthodontic experience and perceptions of pain and discomfort are associated with the dependent variable (pain medication use), other unmeasured factors may play a more substantial role. These findings underscore the importance of interpreting the results within the broader clinical and behavioral context, rather than relying solely on statistical significance. To enhance the practical relevance of these results, we propose that future research consider additional variables—such as socio-economic status, pain management strategies, and parental attitudes toward medication—that may contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of pain medication use in this population.

An increased use of pain medication might be a protective behavior, as previously observed in studies on abdominal pain, where parental recollection of their own pain led to more protective behavior toward their children36,37. Parents with prior orthodontic experience may be better equipped to recognize their child’s pain and suggest appropriate use of analgesics, which can help manage discomfort effectively. However, while protective behaviors can be beneficial, they may also lead to greater functional disability and increased anxiety in children, particularly in cases of chronic pain38,39.

Our study also examined the role of parental anxiety and perceptions in shaping their children’s anxiety and pain catastrophizing. Parents who perceived orthodontic or general dental treatment as notably painful or uncomfortable had children who scored significantly higher on measures of trait anxiety (STAIC-C2) and pain catastrophizing (Table 2). These findings align with previous research in pediatric dentistry, where parental dental anxiety has been shown to influence children’s anxiety40,41 and even physiological responses, such as heart rate, during dental procedures42. Additionally, parental attitudes have been linked to patient compliance in orthodontic settings, further highlighting the importance of parental influence on children’s experiences43.

Interestingly, our study found that while parental influence was significant, the state anxiety of the children (STAIC-C1)—reflecting momentary fear—remained unaffected (Table 2). This suggests that parental influence may be more related to shaping children’s general disposition toward anxiety rather than transmitting specific fears in particular situations. This observation is consistent with findings that suggest a genetic component to trait anxiety in children44.

Our findings also indicate that parental perceptions did not significantly affect children’s reported pain intensity in the initial days after treatment began. This result is consistent with previous research showing a weak correlation between anxiety sensitivity and pain intensity in healthy children45. It suggests that while parental influence is important, other factors, such as the child’s individual pain threshold and coping mechanisms, also play a role.

While the study provides valuable insights, several limitations should be considered. The response rate for completing the full sequence of questionnaires was 58%, which raises the possibility of response bias, potentially favoring participants with higher pain experiences. This response rate may in part reflect respondent burden related to the length and complexity of the questionnaires. Nevertheless, the response rate is reasonably robust for a study of this nature, particularly given the number of validated instruments included46. Another limitation is that in 40 parental dyads, only one of the two parents completed the parental questionnaires. This lack of input from both parents may have affected the results, and the potential influence of stepparents was not considered. Additionally, the study did not include a validated state-trait anxiety evaluation for the parents, nor did it investigate the presence of a family history of chronic pain conditions, which could have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing the nocebo effect. Furthermore, the study did not differentiate between the influence of fathers and mothers, despite evidence suggesting that mothers may have a greater impact on their children’s anxiety levels40. The influence of parental gender on the child/patient was not considered in the present study. Finally, the inclusion/exclusion criteria did not account for whether fixed appliances were placed at the same session as skeletal anchorage devices, which could have influenced pre-treatment anxiety and subsequent pain experiences47,48. Another limitation of this study is the absence of a formal sample size calculation. Due to the lack of prior research in this field, we were unable to base our sample size on existing estimates. While our sample size was determined based on feasibility, this may impact the generalizability and statistical power of the results. Future studies should consider conducting a formal sample size calculation to ensure adequate power and to allow for more robust conclusion.

Our study highlights the important role of parental influence on children’s experiences of pain and anxiety during orthodontic treatment. However, it also highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of this relationship. While our findings suggest a potential nocebo effect in parental expectations influencing pain perception, it is important to note that this effect was not observed consistently across all analyses. Therefore, caution should be exercised in generalizing these results, as other factors may also play a significant role in shaping patient pain experiences beyond the nocebo effect alone. Future research should explore the clinical relevance of these findings, considering factors such as the focus of control of pain, gender differences in pain perception, and the influence of parental roles. Moreover, understanding the potential positive aspects of parental involvement, such as the timely administration of analgesics, could provide a more balanced perspective on the nocebo effect in orthodontics.

Although the present study focuses on parental experiences and expectations as a source of proxy nocebo effects, pain perception during orthodontic treatment is known to be multifactorial. We acknowledge that several factors may influence pain perception. However, it is not feasible to measure and control for all potential determinants within a single study. Previous studies have shown that pain experiences may differ depending on malocclusion traits (e.g. crowding) and the type of orthodontic appliance used, such as fixed appliances or clear aligners49,50. In addition, individual factors including biological characteristics such as sex, as well as psychological factors like personality traits and motivation, may influence how pain is perceived51–53. These factors were not examined in the present study but should be considered when interpreting the results as well as in future research. Parental perceptions may therefore represent one important component within a broader framework where treatment-related and individual factors together shape adolescents’ pain experiences during orthodontic care.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the study found that parents’ previous orthodontic experiences were associated with a significantly higher consumption of painkillers. Additionally, the overall parental expectations of “moderate”/”severe” pain and discomfort with orthodontics and general dental treatment had a notable impact on their children’s trait anxiety, tendency to catastrophize pain, and functional disabilities in chewing and speaking after initiation of orthodontic treatment.

The present study highlights the importance of understanding the nocebo effect from parents to their children in an orthodontic setting. Awareness of the mechanisms of the nocebo effect by proxy becomes crucial when working to minimize its impact in both orthodontic and medical settings. While factors like information, framing, and the clinician-patient relationship are within our control, creating a secure environment for parents is also important when treating children and adolescents. We hypothesize that increased focus to reduce parental anxiety may in turn positively influence their children.

The findings emphasize the complexity of this interrelationship, highlighting the need for more studies to comprehensively understand these dynamics and inform strategies to mitigate the impact of the nocebo effect in orthodontic and medical practices.
