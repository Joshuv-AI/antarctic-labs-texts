Uncovering the mediating role of CSF metabolites in the gut microbiota–glioblastoma axis via causal mediation analysis
Authors: Ying zi Gao, Yang Zhang, Chunliu Li, Lintao Dong, Zhen Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlioblastoma is a lethal brain tumor with poor prognosis and limited early diagnostic tools. The gut microbiota and its metabolites may influence glioblastoma progression through the gut-brain axis.MethodsWe employed Mendelian randomization and Generalized Summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization to explore causal relationships among 473 gut microbial taxa, 338 cerebrospinal fluid metabolites, and glioblastoma risk in Finnish individuals. Mediation analysis was conducted to identify key pathways linking gut microbes, metabolites, and glioblastoma development.ResultsCoprobacillus cateniformis was positively associated with glioblastoma (OR = 1.7363,95%CI:1.0541–2.8600,P = 0.030). The cerebrospinal fluid metabolite S-1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate was negatively associated with glioblastoma (OR = 0.7270,95%CI:0.5506–0.9598,P = 0.024). Additionally, Coprobacillus cateniformis was negatively associated with S-1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate (OR = 0.8933, 95%CI:0.7994–0.9982,P = 0.046). Mediation analysis indicated that S-1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate served as a mediator in the pathway through which Coprobacillus cateniformis promotes glioblastoma development, with a mediation effect ratio of 6.1%.ConclusionThis study reveals a potential causal pathway linking Coprobacillus cateniformis to glioblastoma through S-1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate. These findings highlight a novel gut–cerebrospinal fluid–brain axis and suggest potential microbial and metabolic biomarkers for glioblastoma diagnosis and therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04290-5.

Introduction

The human gut is home to the vast majority of the body’s microorganisms, including bacteria, archaea, fungi and viruses. It has been proposed that the human digestive system plays an important role in brain development [1, 2].In addition, dysregulation of the gut microbiota may trigger aberrant immune signaling, leading to imbalances in host homeostasis and even driving the progression of central nervous system diseases [3].Researchers have termed the concept that describes how the gut microbiota interacts with the brain and causes alterations in the state of the central nervous system as the gut-brain axis. With deeper research, the important role of the gut microbiota in this complex axis has been gradually recognized. Gut microbes are not only involved in the regulation of local digestive functions, such as nutrient absorption, vitamin synthesis, bile and hormone metabolism, and carbohydrate fermentation, but they are also capable of influencing the immune response, inflammatory response, and systemic metabolic state of the host through microbial metabolites, imbalances in the composition of bacterial flora, and other mechanisms that have an impact on the brain in terms of diseases [4].There is growing evidence that the gut can also influence brain development and function in a reverse manner through microbial-derived metabolites, which include short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and amino acid metabolites (e.g., glutamate, glutamine, and tryptophan), among others [5, 6].Braniste found that transplanting mice with Clostridium tyrobutyricum or supplementation with sodium butyrate both significantly reduced blood-brain barrier permeability. Thus, metabolites produced by the gut microbiota are essential for the formation and maintenance of the blood-brain barrier [7]. In another study, it was found that stimulation of germ-free mouse brains by lipopolysaccharide induced an inflammatory response, and microglia in the mouse brain exhibited an immature phenotype. However, when the drinking water of these mice was supplemented with SCFAs, both the number and maturation of microglia returned to normal levels [8]. It has also been shown that gut microbes can secrete tryptophan metabolites that inhibit Central Nervous System (CNS) inflammation by activating the aromatic hydrocarbon receptor signaling pathway in astrocytes [9]. All of these findings collectively demonstrate that the gut microbiota and its metabolites play a key regulatory role in CNS homeostasis, and that dysregulation of the gut microbiota can lead to disturbances in CNS homeostasis, which in turn induces neurological disorders, including brain tumors.

A recent review reported that the gut microbiota not only influences the development and progression of GBM, but also enhances its sensitivity to therapeutic drugs. For example, Ruminococcaceae may promote the polarization of microglia toward the more anti-tumorigenic M1 phenotype, thereby increasing temozolomide sensitivity and presenting a potential therapeutic target. Additionally, certain gut microbiota-derived metabolites, such as SCFAs, can enhance the anti-tumor activity of CD8⁺ T cells by upregulating mTOR expression and promoting the production of effector molecules including CD25, IFN-γ, and TNF-α [10].Gliomas, classified as “cold tumors” by suppressing anti-tumor immune responses, account for 80% of all malignant brain tumors, with glioblastoma (GBM) being the most devastating type [11, 12]. Even with comprehensive treatment, the median survival of GBM patients is only 12–15 months [13]. In addition, the lack of specificity of early symptoms of GBM, coupled with the difficulty of distinguishing benign and malignant lesions on conventional imaging, has led to a large number of patients being diagnosed in the progressive stage and missing the opportunity for early intervention. Therefore, there is still a lack of effective strategies for early detection of GBM and improvement of long-term survival of GBM patients. This complex microbe-metabolite-brain tumor signaling network described above provides us with new research ideas and potential directions for biomarker screening.

Previous studies are mostly observational and have certain limitations. Their results were affected by confounding factors and reverse causality. However, Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis can better remove the effects of potential confounders such as environmental factors, dietary habits, and lifestyle than previous observational studies [14]. Generalized Summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization(GSMR), on the other hand, has more significant advantages: it improves the efficiency of the analysis by taking into account linkage disequilibrium (LD) between genetic variants and uses the instrument-dependent heterogeneity (HEIDI) test to detect aberrant instruments and to eliminate polyvalent single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [15]. The types of gut microbial exposure data used by others in established studies are more limited and tend to use plasma metabolites as a reflection of microbial function. In the present study, a richer and more comprehensive range of gut microbial species were selected to explore the potential causal relationship between them and the occurrence of GBM through GSMR and MR. Compared with plasma metabolites, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) metabolites can more directly reflect the metabolic status of CNS, and we selected CSF metabolites as mediators to deeply analyze their regulatory roles in the gut microbe-GBM axis.

Materials and methods

Study design

All data utilized in this study were derived from previously published literature and approved by relevant institutional ethics review boards. Therefore, no additional ethical approval was required. Clinical trial number: not applicable. Ethics, Consent to Participate, and Consent to Publish declarations: not applicable. The detailed information is shown in Table 1. The study methodology followed the STROBE-MR checklist (in STROBE-MR checklist in the Supplementary Materials). Detailed results are presented in both the main text and supplementary files. We selected 473 gut microbial taxa as exposures and GBM from the FinnGen database as the outcome. For results showing significant associations, reverse Mendelian randomization analyses were conducted to rule out reverse causality from GBM to the gut microbiota in the Finnish population. Subsequently, we explored 338 CSF metabolites as potential mediators to determine whether they were involved in the causal pathway, and we calculated the proportion of mediation accordingly.

Data sources

The GBM data used in this study were obtained from the Finnish FinnGen database (https://www.finngen.fi/en, Release R11). The control group excluded all individuals with any cancer diagnosis to minimize potential confounding from cancer-related factors. A total of 508 glioblastoma cases and 345,118 cancer-free controls were included in the analysis [16].

Our gut microbiota data were obtained from a GWAS of 5,959 individuals in the FINRISK 2002 cohort, a population-based study from Finland. Microbial profiling was performed using shallow shotgun metagenomic sequencing of stool samples. Taxonomic composition was represented as relative abundances, and to account for the compositional nature of microbiome data, the relative abundance values were centered log-ratio transformed before GWAS analysis [17].

GWAS data for 338 CSF metabolites were obtained from the Wisconsin Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center and the Wisconsin Registry for Alzheimer’s Prevention studies. These samples were collected via lumbar puncture in the morning while fasting, then processed and stored at 80℃ [18].

MR analysis

We employed three Mendelian randomization methods—MR-Egger, weighted median, and inverse variance weighted (IVW)—to investigate the causal relationships among gut microbiota, CSF metabolites, and GBM. SNPs reaching genome-wide significance (P < 1 × 10⁻⁵) were selected as instrumental variables (IVs) for gut microbiota and CSF metabolites. To explore the reverse causality, we identified SNPs associated with GBM using a genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻6. These SNPs were then clumped based on LD using a window size of 10,000 kb and an r² threshold of < 0.001. LD estimates were derived from European populations in the 1000 Genomes Project. We assessed heterogeneity among IVs using Cochran’s Q statistic and evaluated potential horizontal pleiotropy using both the MR-Egger intercept test and the MR-PRESSO method. Finally, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the robustness of the MR results [19–21].

GSMR approach

In the GSMR (Generalized Summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization) analysis, we selected genome-wide significant SNPs (P < 1 × 10⁻⁵) for each gut microbial feature using a clumping algorithm with an LD r² threshold of 0.05. The European samples from Phase 3 of the 1000 Genomes Project were used as the reference panel for LD estimation.For the reverse MR analysis using GBM GWAS summary statistics, we selected independent SNPs using an LD threshold of r² < 0.05 and a genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻6. The HEIDI-outlier method was then applied to detect and remove SNPs with potential horizontal pleiotropy. The significance threshold for the HEIDI-outlier filtering was set at P < 0.01.

Mediation analysis

Figure 1 illustrates the analytical framework of the study. First, using both MR and GSMR methods, we identified five gut microbial taxa that showed significant causal relationships with GBM. The effect of Coprobacillus cateniformis on GBM was defined as the direct effect. Next, we investigated the causal effects of CSF metabolites on GBM. Finally, we examined the causal relationships between Coprobacillus cateniformis and CSF metabolites to determine which metabolite(s) may mediate the causal pathway from Coprobacillus cateniformis to GBM.The total effect was decomposed into a direct effect (not mediated by CSF metabolites) and an indirect effect (mediated through CSF metabolites). The proportion of the mediation effect was calculated by dividing the indirect effect by the total effect.All analyses and data visualizations were performed using R version 4.3.1 with the packages TwoSampleMR, foreach, gsmr2, and ggplot2.

Result

Association of 473 gut microbiota with GBM

We primarily used the IVW method because it provides a robust estimate of the causal effect by combining information from multiple instrumental variables while accounting for their individual variances.Through MR analysis, 21 gut microbial taxa associated with GBM were identified(Supplementary Table S1). Subsequently, GSMR was used to further validate the results, and 16 taxa with GSMR P values greater than 0.05, unclear names, or rare occurrence were excluded (Supplementary Table S4 and S5). In Fig. 2, using IVW algorithm of MR, Bifidobacterium infantis (OR = 0.3944, 95% CI: 0.1994–0.7802, P = 0.007) and Eubacterium F sp000434115 (OR = 0.6119, 95% CI: 0.4056–0.9233, P = 0.193) were significantly negatively associated with GBM, whereas Coprobacillus cateniformis (OR = 1.7974, 95% CI: 1.1030–2.9290, P = 0.0186), Coprobacillus (OR = 1.8834, 95% CI: 1.1944–2.9700, P = 0.006), and Enterobacteriaceae (OR = 1.9868, 95% CI: 1.2592–3.1350, P = 0.003) were significantly positively associated with GBM.

All SNPs used as instrumental variables for the microbial taxa involved in the MR and mediation analyses had F-statistics above 10, with average instrument strength exceeding 20 and R² values greater than 0.1% (In Supplementary Table S1 and S3). In addition, GSMR analysis revealed that the five gut microbial taxa exhibited consistent causal relationships with the IVW results. Subsequently, we conducted a reverse MR analysis treating GBM as the exposure and the five gut microbial taxa as outcomes. No significant associations were observed using either the IVW or GSMR methods(In Fig. 2).

Association of cerebrospinal fluid metabolites with GBM

In the MR analyses, all SNPs used as instrumental variables for cerebrospinal fluid metabolites had F-statistics greater than 10, with average instrument strength exceeding 20 and R² values above 0.1% (In Supplementary Table S3).We conducted a MR analysis to explore the causal relationships between 337 CSF metabolites and GBM, and identified 14 CSF metabolites that were significantly associated with GBM(In Supplementary Table S4). Among them, S-1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate was significantly negatively associated with GBM (OR = 0.7270, 95% CI: 0.5506–0.9598, P = 0.024)༈In Fig. 3༉.

Association of Coprobacillus cateniformis with S − 1−pyrroline − 5−carboxylate

We treated the five gut microbial taxa as exposures and the 14 CSF metabolites as outcomes to investigate potential causal relationships(In Supplementary Table S3). In Fig. 3,IVW results showed that only Coprobacillus cateniformis was significantly negatively associated with S-1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate (OR = 0.8933, 95% CI: 0.7994–0.9982, P = 0.046).Subsequently, we analyzed the mediating role of S-1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate in this pathway. The results showed β₁*β₂ = 0.03, β_direct = 0.55, β_all = 0.58, and the mediating effect ratio was 6.1%.

Sensitivity analysis

We used Cochran’s Q test and funnel plots to assess heterogeneity in the analysis. The funnel plots showed no asymmetry, and all Q P-values were greater than 0.05, indicating no heterogeneity (In Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Fig. 1).We also applied the MR-Egger intercept test and the MR-PRESSO global test to examine horizontal pleiotropy, both of which showed no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (as shown in Supplementary Table S2).The influence of each SNP on the overall causal estimate was evaluated using leave-one-out analysis (Supplementary Figure S2).

Results after FDR correction

The aforementioned results did not demonstrate statistical significance after the implementation of FDR correction for multiple comparisons.

Discussion

Our study verified the significant correlation between five gut microorganisms and GBM by MR and GSMR analysis. Subsequently, we used cerebrospinal fluid metabolites as mediators to further explore their association with GBM. After screening the cerebrospinal fluid metabolites with significant associations, we continued to analyze the association between gut microorganisms and these metabolites, and finally found that Coprobacillus cateniformis could mediate the occurrence and development of GBM through S-1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate. Similar to other cancers, GBM regulates intracellular metabolites via distinct mechanisms, leading to the reprogramming of metabolic homeostasis. In turn, some metabolites regulated by the gut flora can also regulate glioma growth by altering the epigenetic profile of tumor cells [22].

Coprobacillus cateniformis is a Gram-positive bacterium belonging to the phylum Firmicutes (thick-walled bacteria). Most studies have shown that patients with GBM have a reduced Firmicutes to Firmicutes (F/B) ratio, a change that may lead to metabolic imbalances and decreased levels of circulating SCFA, which in turn promotes the development of GBM [23, 24]. However, high F/B ratios may trigger a pro-inflammatory response in some cases, e.g., in patients with ulcerative colitis, elevated F/B ratios are thought to exacerbate inflammation and promote disease progression [25]. Yu et al. found that fecal abundance of Coprobacillus cateniformis was significantly higher in patients with rosacea treated with probiotics. Yu et al. found a significant increase in the abundance of Coprobacillus cateniformis in the feces of patients treated with probiotics for rosacea and an improvement in patient symptoms [26]. In addition, Cao et al. suggested that Coprobacillus cateniformis reduces the expression of Programmed Death-Ligand 2 in tumor tissues, thereby improving the efficacy of anti-Programmed Death-Ligand 1 immunotherapy [27]. However, controversy remains regarding the biological role of Coprobacillus cateniformis. A case report from Korea suggests that the bacterium may cause bacteremia, especially in people at high risk for gastrointestinal infections, suggesting that Coprobacillus cateniformis may be a potential opportunistic pathogen [28]. In addition, a study by Magdy El-Salhy et al. found a significant decrease in the abundance of Coprobacillus cateniformis in the feces of patients with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) who underwent fecal microtransplantation, as well as relief of IBS symptoms [29]. The above findings are consistent with the findings of the present study. It is worth noting that biological pathways such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis have certain commonalities in different tissues and organs. Therefore, in combination with the role of Coprobacillus cateniformis in a variety of diseases, we hypothesize that it may play a certain contributing role in the occurrence and development of GBM. This hypothesis deserves further study to explore the specific mechanism of Coprobacillus cateniformis in the progression of GBM and its potential clinical significance.

S-1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate and 1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate (P5C) are two nomenclature variants referring to the same compound.P5C is a key intermediate in the metabolism of proline and glutamate, and in GBM, multiple enzymes related to glutamate metabolism are aberrantly expressed, affecting tumor proliferation, metabolic adaptations, and treatment tolerance [30].High expression of Branched Chain Amino Acid Transferase 1 promotes glutamate metabolism and is associated with poorer prognosis; high expression of Glutathione Synthetase may enhance antioxidant capacity and drug resistance. For example, L-Proline Dehydrogenase catalyzes the oxidation of L-Proline to produce P5C. However, the role of P5C in cellular metabolism, oxidative stress regulation, and the tumor microenvironment has not yet been fully clarified.Yutao Yan’s metabolomic analysis revealed that prostate cancer cells release P5C, which promotes immune evasion by increasing ROS levels and suppressing T cell function [31]. Bai Cui and colleagues found that Pyrroline-5-carboxylate reductase 1 promotes the accumulation of proline by enhancing the production of P5C, which in turn activates the cyclic guanosine monophosphate–protein kinase G signaling pathway, ultimately enhancing tumor cell proliferation and drug resistance [32].Interestingly, Adam Kazberuk et al. found that in breast cancer cells, proline dehydrogenase can oxidize proline into P5C, a process that may be accompanied by the production of either ATP (promoting cell survival) or ROS (promoting apoptosis). Specifically, in MCF7 cells, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs activate the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma → proline dehydrogenase pathway, leading to P5C generation, ROS accumulation, and the initiation of the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway, thereby inhibiting the proliferation of breast cancer cells [33].Regrettably, no experimental studies have yet been published to elucidate the mechanistic role of P5C in GBM. Future studies could investigate the potential role of P5C in GBM progression by measuring its levels in the cerebrospinal fluid of GBM patients using metabolomics, and experimentally validating its association with oxidative stress, glycolytic metabolism, and related functional pathways. In summary, we hypothesized that the intestinal microorganism Coprobacillus cateniformis may cause changes in the immune microenvironment of the brain by modulating the intestinal immune system, which in turn activates or inhibits certain specific immune cells (e.g., Th1, Th17, regulatory T cells, and monocytes, etc.). Levels of the metabolite P5C released by these immune cells into the cerebrospinal fluid are reduced, and P5C is protective against GBM. Therefore, the reduction of P5C may further promote proliferation, invasion, or immune escape of GBM, thereby accelerating GBM disease progression.

This study has the following strengths; our study is one of the few to directly explore the gut microbe-cerebrospinal fluid metabolite-GBM association, breaking through the traditional limitation of focusing only on local tumor plasma metabolism. Data from a larger number of 473 gut microorganisms were used, allowing for a broader analysis. Despite breakthroughs in immunotherapy in GBM, GBM is a highly immunosuppressive tumor. Immune checkpoint inhibitors are currently the most successful immunotherapies, and Programmed Death-Ligand 1(PD-L1) inhibitory therapies can be augmented by Bifidobacterium [34]. Meanwhile, anti- Cytotoxic T-Lymphocyte-Associated protein 4(CTLA-4) antibody therapy can lead to changes in intestinal flora, and oral administration of Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron enhances the effect of anti-CTLA-4 immunotherapy, both suggesting that modulation of intestinal flora may be a new strategy for brain tumor therapy [35], and thus our results may provide new GBM diagnostic or therapeutic Biomarkers. And MR and GSMR were used for mutual validation, which further enhanced the causal inference ability and reliability of the study. However, this study still has some limitations. Currently, GBM-related public databases are relatively limited and lack sufficient independent datasets for repeated validation, which makes the reproducibility of the results of the study somewhat affected. The samples used in this study were derived from individuals of European ancestry, which may limit the applicability of our findings to other populations. Additionally, the gut microbiota GWAS data included individuals aged 25–74, whereas the incidence of GBM primarily occurs between ages 45–70. Compared to younger individuals, middle-aged individuals show a significant reduction in core gut microbiota members such as Bifidobacterium, suggesting that the partial mismatch in age ranges may introduce some bias in estimating the causal relationship between genetic effects and phenotypes [36].Despite failing to pass the strict threshold for FDR correction, we still believe that the observation may be biologically significant. For example, despite the failure of statistical significance to reach the preset threshold, these results are consistent with our existing literature support or experimental observations and may represent potentially important associations. Indeed, in many practical biomedical studies, extremely stringent statistical thresholds may sometimes deprive sensitivity to those potentially novel findings, especially in research areas with complex mechanisms and multiple potential confounders. Second, the proportion of mediating effects was only 6.5%, suggesting that this pathway only partially explains the role in the development of GBM, and the mediators that accounted for a larger proportion of the mediation still need to be further explored. Finally, the CSF metabolomics data used in this study were from a cohort of patients with Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Previous studies have identified disease-specific metabolic features in AD and GBM. For example, Ruocheng Dong et al. reported that various lipids—including sphingolipids, phosphatidylcholines, and lysophospholipids—as well as carbohydrates such as N-acetylneuraminate, arabitol/xylitol, arabinose, and erythronate, are involved in the metabolic pathology of AD. Daniel H. Wang et al. found that carnitine, uridine, aminobutanal, and acetylcholine are significantly associated with GBM-related metabolic alterations [37, 38].Although AD and GBM differ in pathophysiology and their metabolomic profiles may be different, both diseases share common features such as oxidative stress and inflammation. Therefore, the use of these CSF data may provide preliminary insights into GBM-related metabolic dysregulation.In future studies, we plan to collect cerebrospinal fluid samples from Asian GBM patients for metabolomic sequencing to identify significantly altered CSF metabolites.
