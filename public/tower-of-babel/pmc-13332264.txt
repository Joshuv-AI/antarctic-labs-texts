Primary bronchotomy for removal of a distal bronchial foreign body in a cat
Authors: Alizée Gerard, Thomas Maufras, Tiare Delaune
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Case summaryA 3-year-old, spayed female domestic shorthair cat was presented for acute onset cough and dyspnoea. Thoracic radiography revealed a radiopaque foreign body proximally in the right caudal main bronchus. Initial attempts at endoscopic retrieval using a flexible bronchoscope were unsuccessful, as the foreign body migrated progressively distally into the right caudal lobe bronchus, precluding bronchoscopic removal. The cat subsequently underwent a right fifth intercostal-space lateral thoracotomy. A primary bronchotomy was performed to retrieve a small stone, and the bronchus was closed with a single-layer, simple interrupted pattern, using monofilament suture. Postoperative management included analgesia, antibiotics and restricted activity. The cat recovered uneventfully. At a 15-day follow-up, clinical signs had resolved and the cat remained in good general condition.Relevance and novel informationTracheobronchial foreign bodies are uncommon in feline patients, and reports describing primary bronchotomy for their removal are limited in dogs and non-existent in cats. Although bronchoscopic extraction is generally favoured because of its minimally invasive nature, this case illustrates a scenario in which surgical intervention is indicated, particularly when endoscopic access is compromised by distal migration of the foreign body or a narrow bronchial lumen. This report details the surgical approach, intraoperative management and postoperative care after primary bronchotomy in a cat. The findings provide practical guidance for veterinary surgeons confronted with similar cases and contribute to the scarce literature on surgical management of bronchial foreign bodies in cats.

Case description

A 3-year-old, spayed female domestic shorthair cat was presented to the referring veterinarian with a 5-day history of coughing and dyspnoea. The cat was afebrile and showed dyspnoea with intercostal retraction. She had been previously healthy and had been observed playing with stones in the yard. Thoracic radiography revealed a focal interstitial pulmonary pattern in the right middle lung lobe and an 8 mm × 5 mm radiopaque foreign body (FB) located in the proximal right caudal main bronchus (Figure 1). The cat was referred for removal of the FB.

Right (a) lateral and (b) dorsoventral thoracic radiographs showing a focal interstitial pulmonary pattern in the right middle lung lobe and an 8 mm × 5 mm radiopaque foreign body located in the proximal right caudal main bronchus

On arrival, the cat showed no respiratory distress and the clinical examination was unremarkable. An intravenous catheter was placed in the cephalic vein, and premedication consisted of midazolam (0.2 mg/kg IV) and butorphanol (0.2 mg/kg IV). Anaesthesia was induced with propofol (2 mg/kg IV) and maintained with isoflurane in oxygen. Several attempts to retrieve the FB were made using an flexible bronchoscope (length 890 mm, outer diameter 5.9 mm, BF Type TE2; Olympus). Although the FB was visualised (Figure 2), it migrated distally into the right caudal lobe bronchus after multiple unsuccessful attempts, making endoscopic retrieval impossible because of the small bronchial diameter. The cat developed mild laryngeal oedema, and methylprednisolone (1 mg/kg IV) was thus administered. Surgical removal of the FB was recommended to the owners.

Bronchoscopic image of a mineral foreign body located in the proximal right caudal main bronchus

A right fifth intercostal lateral thoracotomy was performed. The FB was visualised in the right caudal lobe bronchus. The affected lung lobe was well aerated and appeared normal. The FB was gently advanced by external manipulation into the right main bronchus. A no. 11 scalpel blade was used to make an incision on the lateral aspect of the bronchus, and the stone was retrieved using Mosquito forceps (Figure 3). The bronchial incision was closed in a single layer in a simple interrupted manner using a slowly absorbable monofilament suture (polydioxanone USP 4-0 and 5-0) (Figure 4). The thoracic cavity was filled with warm sterile saline, and a leak test was performed. Although discrete leakage of fine air bubbles was observed intraoperatively under positive-pressure ventilation at 20 mmHg, this was expected to subside after the intervention as it was absent at lower pressures. An 8 Fr thoracostomy tube was placed and pleural negative pressure was restored. The thoracotomy was closed routinely and the cat was transferred to the intensive care unit.

(a) Intraoperative view of the right caudal main bronchus with the foreign body visible (asterisk); (b) lateral bronchial incision and retrieval of the foreign body using Mosquito forceps

Intraoperative view of closure of the bronchial incision in a single layer using a simple interrupted pattern with a slowly absorbable monofilament suture (polydioxanone USP 4-0 and 5-0)

Postoperative treatments included antibiotics (sulbactam–ampicillin 30 mg/kg IV q8h) and a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (meloxicam 0.05 mg/kg IV q24h). Analgesia during the first 24 h consisted of a constant rate infusion (CRI) of fentanyl (1 µg/kg/h) and ketamine (0.3 mg/kg/h). The chest tube was removed the following day, and the fentanyl and ketamine CRI was tapered off. Morphine (0.2 mg/kg IV) was administered as needed q4h when the pain score exceeded 5/20 based on the Glasgow Composite Measure Pain Scale. After 3 days, the cat was discharged with a total 10-day course of antibiotics (amoxicillin–clavulanic acid 12.5 mg/kg PO q12h), a 5-day course of meloxicam (0.05 mg/kg PO q24h) and buprenorphine as needed (20 µg/kg PO q8h). Restricted activity was advised for 4 weeks. At the 15-day follow-up with the referring veterinarian, the cat was in good condition and the cough had resolved.

Discussion

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first reported case of primary bronchotomy for FB removal with lung lobe preservation in a cat, with less than a handful of papers published on the subject in dogs.1–3

Tracheobronchial FBs are uncommon in veterinary medicine but can be life-threatening. Although most tracheal FBs are expected to be expelled through coughing (according to a study, 20/22 FBs introduced artificially in dogs were coughed out after 1–4 days), 4 some may migrate further into the bronchial tree.1–4 Clinical signs in cats are variable and can depend on the location of the FB in the tracheobronchial system: tracheal FBs may cause stridor, whereas bronchial FBs will more commonly cause wheezing.5,6 Coughing is usually the main complaint in cats and can be present in all FB locations. However, it has been shown that the more distal an FB is, the less the animal has an urge to cough. 7 In the case presented in our report, although the cat initially coughed and was dyspnoeic, the absence of notable signs on admission could be explained by migration of the FB further along the airways after the initial consultation. Moreover, the size of the FB will also have an impact on the signs observed; if the FB is small and does not completely obstruct the tracheal or bronchial lumen, only mild clinical signs may be present. 5

Other signs may include pyrexia, dyspnoea and cyanosis; in severe cases, respiratory distress will occur when the FB is too large.3,8,9 Complete airway obstruction can lead to death.

The diagnosis is often made by thoracic radiography with, depending on studies, a reported success rate of 66–92% for accurate localisation.5,8–11 Bronchial FBs are as common as tracheal FBs in cats and are, according to most authors, more frequently located in the right lung lobes (and most notably in the right caudal and accessory lobes).5,6,11,12 This is likely due to the right mainstem bronchus being parallel with the trachea. Furthermore, the right caudal lung lobe bronchus is also more in line with the right main bronchus than the right cranial and middle bronchi. This anatomical predisposition to FB is in accordance with the present case report. An FB diagnosis can also be made using bronchoscopy, which not only provides direct visualisation of the airway and assessment of mucosal damage, but also allows for tracheobronchial FB retrieval in up to 83% of cats.11,13 Retrieval is performed using either a rigid or flexible bronchoscope and grasping forceps. However, given the size of the feline airway, the bronchoscope frequently obstructs the airway lumen, and the instrumentation for extraction may be too big. In this case, a Foley catheter can be used in place of grasping forceps, with the balloon positioned distal to the FB and gently retracted orad.8,9,14 The size and shape of the FB can also influence whether retrieval is successful. In our case, multiple unsuccessful attempts at retrieval were responsible for distal migration of the FB, rendering the use of a Foley catheter or grasping forceps impossible. Fluoroscopy, which has been described as a means of guiding forceps placement without the use of a bronchoscope (thereby reducing airway obstruction by instruments),8,9,14 was not available in our centre.

Bronchoscopy can be complicated intraoperatively by haemorrhage, exudate, cardiac and respiratory movement, and postoperatively by haemorrhage, pneumonia, atelectasis, bronchial dilatation, pneumomediastinum, dyspnoea unresponsive to oxygen, non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema (due to airway obstruction), and mucus or inflammatory blockage of the secondary bronchi.3,9 Therefore, when time to retrieval is too long, and/or when there is an increased risk of complications, alternative techniques must be considered.

Non-invasive methods, such as placing the animal in a head-down position and tapping the thorax, have been reported but are unreliable.6,15

Surgical intervention may be required when bronchoscopic removal fails or may also be selected as a first-line treatment in cases of pneumothorax, empyema or pulmonary consolidation. 9 Primary bronchotomy is preferred over lung lobectomy when FBs are located near the carina or within the main bronchi, provided there is no involvement of the pulmonary parenchyma.1,3,8,9 In the present case, thoracic radiography did not reveal pulmonary lesions, which was confirmed by intraoperative visual evaluation of the caudal lung lobes, supporting the decision to perform a bronchotomy to preserve lung tissue.

This lung-sparing approach allows for preservation of maximal pulmonary function and normal respiratory physiology. This is particularly relevant in young animals with a long, anticipated lifespan and potential for future respiratory disease. It represents a clear advantage over lung lobectomy, which results in irreversible loss of functional parenchyma and may be unnecessarily aggressive when lung tissue is unaffected.

Bronchotomy may lead to postoperative pneumothorax; however, there are limited reports on this topic in the veterinary literature. Because tracheal and bronchial mucosal wounds start to epithelialise within 2–8 h, and a transitional epithelium arises within 48 h, 16 we expected this possible complication to be temporary. Accordingly, although discrete air leakage was observed after suturing the bronchotomy wound intraoperatively, the thoracostomy tube produced no air for 24 h and was removed without incident.

Other complications to be expected include those associated with intercostal thoracostomy, such as rib fractures, transient lameness and pulmonary abscessation (reported both in bronchoscopic and surgical retrieval).3,15 Postoperative antibiotic therapy is therefore recommended owing to the possibility of pneumonia; the choice of antibiotic should ideally be based on bacteriological examination.

Empirical first-line antimicrobial therapy was maintained for 10 days in our case because of concerns regarding potential postoperative pulmonary abscessation and infectious complications after multiple bronchoscopic retrieval attempts combined with an open primary bronchotomy. Furthermore, although the FB was considered technically inert, it originated from the environment and may have been contaminated with exogenous pathogens, particularly given its retention within the airways for approximately 5 days before removal. Current veterinary antimicrobial stewardship guidelines recommend restricting antibiotic use to intraoperative prophylaxis in clean-contaminated procedures unless there is clear evidence of infection.¹7 Therefore, the justification for prolonged antimicrobial therapy in this case may be questioned, and close clinical monitoring with antimicrobial treatment initiated only if complications developed might have represented a more appropriate approach.

Prognosis after retrieval is generally good. Animals are commonly discharged on recommendations similar to those after general soft tissue surgery, including rest, anti-inflammatory treatment and antimicrobial therapy.

Conclusions

Primary bronchotomy is a viable technique for removing FBs in cats, in the absence of associated pulmonary parenchymal lesions. It allows for the preservation of lung lobes and seems to be associated with few specific postoperative complications. Primary bronchotomy is poorly documented in veterinary medicine but is usually performed in cases where bronchoscopic retrieval is unsuccessful or contraindicated.
