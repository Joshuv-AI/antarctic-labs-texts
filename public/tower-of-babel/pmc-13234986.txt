Pharmacological mechanisms underlying neurovascular alterations related to resistant and refractory migraine: CGRP pathways and beyond
Authors: Damiana Scuteri, Michalis Kodounis, Masahito Katsuki, Burcu Bezgal, Natalia Bednarczyk, Savvas Konstantinos Alevetsovitis, Justyna Wołos, Mariana Henriques, Teresa Ascensão Pinheiro, Antoinette MaassenVanDenBrink, Christian Lampl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMigraine is the fourth leading cause of disability worldwide. It impairs patients’ physical, emotional and social functioning, as well as their productivity. This is also due to poor pharmacological control of attacks in difficult-to-treat patients. Despite several therapeutic strategies available, including the game-changing calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)-targeted small-molecule and biotechnological drugs, 40% of patients remain resistant or even refractory. The aim of this review is to highlight the neurovascular mechanisms underlying poor response or nonresponse to antimigraine drugs, with particular focus on the phenomena related to the CGRP pathway.MethodsEligibility criteria, search strategy and information sources were established a priori: PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, Web of Science and ScienceDirect were searched for studies published from database inception to the date of the last search on July 05th, 2025.ResultsNon-response to antimigraine treatment is closely linked to clinical and neurobiological factors that increase the overall disease burden. Underlying these challenges are mechanisms involving genetic and epigenetic modifications that contribute to inter-individual variability in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. Additionally, CGRP as well as other neuropeptides, implicated in the neurovascular basis of migraine, may play a significant role. Targeting these mechanisms may help overcome treatment resistance and refractoriness, as defined by the European Headache Federation. Combination therapies, including the latest available treatments, might represent a promising strategy. Thus, they deserve further investigation to confirm efficacy without worsening the safety profile.ConclusionsA deeper understanding of the neurovascular pharmacology and pathophysiology of migraine is essential to pave the way for more personalized and effective treatments. tudies are needed to clarify the neuropharmacological, genetic, and epigenetic mechanisms that underlie resistance and refractoriness to pharmacological therapies, affecting the CGRP pathway both directly and indirectly.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background: the burden of migraine and refractoriness

Migraine is the fourth leading cause of disability worldwide across all age groups [1]. Although pharmacological treatments for migraine have advanced substantially, many patients still fail to achieve meaningful clinical improvement despite exhausting numerous therapy options across both acute and prophylactic treatments. In particular, specific abortive treatments are designed to provide rapid relief during an attack. An effective abortive drug should provide relief from pain and the most bothersome symptom associated with the attack within 2 h. However, around 40% of patients experience insufficient efficacy [2–4], often due to complex neurovascular pharmacological factors. For prophylactic treatment, between 15% and 25% of chronic migraine patients do not reach a 50% reduction in headache days per month after at least two generally adequate treatments. In certain subgroups with additional risk factors, the non-response rate exceeds 30% [5, 6]. Resistant or refractory migraine is closely associated with clinical and neurobiological factors that impair therapeutic response and amplify the overall disease burden. To bring order to the heterogeneity in this field and better address these challenges, the European Headache Federation (EHF) introduced standardised definitions for the terms “resistant” and “refractory” migraine [7]. The two conditions exist independently yet share overlapping characteristics, as patients fail to respond to multiple therapeutic approaches. Based on EHF and the European Migraine & Headache Alliance (EMHA) the following definitions have been provided [8]: resistant migraine is defined as having failed at least 3 classes of migraine preventatives and suffering from at least 8 debilitating headache days per month for at least 3 consecutive months without improvement; the definition can be based on a review of medical charts. Refractory migraine is defined as having failed all available preventatives and suffering from at least 8 debilitating headache days per month for at least 6 consecutive months. Drug failure may include lack of efficacy or lack of tolerability. Furthermore, the brain’s overall pain-processing and emotional state can significantly diminish the effectiveness of even peripherally-acting anti-inflammatory drugs [9]. Factors likely predisposing to treatment refractoriness can also not be directly linked to resistance to preventive treatments, but represent risk factors for migraine worsening and chronification as pain-related comorbidities associated with central sensitisation (Fig. 1) [10].

Methods

Eligibility criteria, search strategy, and information sources were established in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 recommendations [11]. PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, Web of Science. and ScienceDirect were searched for studies published from database inception to the date of the last search on July 05th, 2025. The following keywords were used to perform the search: “migraine pathophysiology”, “neurovascular migraine”, “migraine” and “refractory/resistance” and “efficacy/pharmacokinetic” and NSAIDs/triptans/gepants/ditans/propranolol/topiramate/monoclonal antibodies”, “CGRP pathway migraine”, “K-ATP channels migraine”, “ATP-sensitive potassium channels”, “BK channels migraine”, “CGRP-independent migraine”, “PACAP”, “CGRP and PACAP pathways”, “AMY1 receptor”, “non-canonical CGRP receptor” and “amylin in migraine”, “migraine comorbidities”. Based on the a priori established eligibility criteria, original articles, reviews, systematic reviews and meta-analyses, editorials, commentaries, and book chapters published in English and available in full text were included in the results.

Neuropharmacology of abortive treatments

First-line therapies: how do these drugs work and why they might not work

NSAIDs inhibit prostaglandin synthesis, a possible key mediator of inflammation in migraine pathophysiology. The pharmacokinetics of commonly used NSAIDs for migraine treatment, such as ibuprofen, naproxen, and diclofenac, are generally characterised by rapid absorption and high oral bioavailability, though there is variability across agents [12]. Despite their widespread use and established role as first-line options, NSAIDs are ineffective in around half of patients with migraine [13]. The efficacy of NSAIDs is profoundly influenced by the overall clinical state of migraine, including attack severity and the presence of psychiatric comorbidities. These comorbidities are not merely co-occurring conditions but may actively impair analgesic response, potentially through shared neural circuits, such as limbic system abnormalities, that modulate pain processing. Triptans primarily act as serotonin 5-HT1B/1D receptor agonists and, besides their vasoconstrictive properties, may exert their effects by inhibiting neuropeptide release from peripheral trigeminal afferents and blocking pain signal transmission within the trigeminocervical complex [14]. Over 30–40% of patients report insufficient relief or poor tolerability with a given triptan [15]. A primary factor contributing to triptan non-response is probably pharmacokinetic variability (Table 1). These individual differences in drug metabolism and transport can result in sub-therapeutic drug concentrations at the target site, irrespective of the prescribed dose. Furthermore, the timing of administration is critical for triptan efficacy. These medications are most effective when taken early in an attack, ideally when pain is still mild, to preempt the development of central sensitisation [16]. Delaying administration significantly diminishes their effectiveness [16]. Adding to this challenge, migraine attacks themselves can induce gastric stasis, which further impairs the absorption of oral abortive medications, thereby contributing to treatment failure [17]. The necessity of early intervention, coupled with potential absorption issues, underscores a crucial “window of opportunity” for triptan effectiveness, a window that is directly shaped by the dynamic progression of migraine pathophysiology.

Pharmacokinetic and metabolic characteristics of triptans

Central sensitisation, characterised by altered processing of sensory input within the central nervous system (CNS), is a major neuropharmacological determinant of triptan non-response [18, 19]. Patients who develop cutaneous allodynia, in which normally innocuous stimuli, such as brushing hair or light touch, become painful, are less likely to respond to triptans [18]. This clinical observation indicates that once central sensitisation is established, typically 60–120 min into an attack, the second-order neurons in the spinal trigeminal nucleus transition from dependence on peripheral sensory signals to autonomous activity as pain generators [18, 19].

Individual variability in 5-HT1B/1D receptor expression or coupling could theoretically influence triptan efficacy. Targeted genetic studies have identified associations, including a genetic link in a small number of families with migraine with aura, showing decreased expression of the 5-HT1D receptor, which could potentially affect triptan response [20]. However, the role of the 5-HT1D receptor in the clinical response to sumatriptan remains uncertain, as the selective 5-HT1D receptor agonist PNU-142,633 has shown no clinical efficacy [21]. Additionally, polymorphisms in 5-HT transporters, particularly in the serotonin transporter gene-linked polymorphic region (5-HTTLPR) of the SLC6A4 gene, have been identified and associated with migraine susceptibility [22]. The presence of the short (S) allele in 5-HTTLPR has been linked to reduced serotonin reuptake efficiency, potentially resulting in altered serotonergic tone and enhanced vulnerability to migraine attacks. The S allele is associated with reduced transcriptional efficiency of the serotonin transporter, leading to lower serotonin reuptake capacity and potentially heightened susceptibility to migraine attacks due to serotonergic dysregulation. Although these polymorphisms are primarily associated with migraine susceptibility rather than direct treatment refractoriness, they may indirectly influence triptan response by modulating baseline serotonergic function [23]. In contrast, earlier genetic studies in the late 1990s found no significant association between polymorphisms in the 5-HT1B [24] or 5-HT1F [25] receptor genes and clinical response to sumatriptan, suggesting that these receptor variants alone do not account for interindividual differences in efficacy. In addition to 5-HT receptor and transporter polymorphisms, variability in monoamine oxidase A (MAO-A) may contribute to inter-individual differences in triptan pharmacokinetics and response. In fact, the structural similarity between triptans and serotonin may explain, at least in part, the MAO-A metabolism of some triptans [26]. MAO-A activity is influenced by variable numbers of tandem repeat (uVNTR) polymorphisms located in the gene’s promoter region. The high-activity (3.5- or 4-repeat) and low-activity (3-repeat) alleles result in differential enzyme expression levels, potentially altering the first-pass metabolism of MAO-A substrates. Although current evidence specifically linking MAO-A polymorphisms to clinical triptan response is sparse, these functional variants may modulate systemic exposure and efficacy in a subset of patients, warranting further investigation into their role as pharmacogenomic biomarkers in triptan therapy [27]. Ditans, such as lasmiditan, represent a new class of acute migraine treatments [28]. A crucial distinction from triptans is their highly selective agonism at the 5-HT1F receptor, coupled with a lack of vasoconstrictive activity.

CGRP is fundamental in migraine as it activates its Gαs protein-coupled receptor formed by the calcitonin receptor-like receptor (CLR), the receptor activity-modifying protein (RAMP) 1 and the receptor component protein (RCP) [29], and requires the fusion protein of the extracellular domains of the human G protein-coupled receptor calcitonin receptor-like receptor CALCRL to induce vasodilation. Other important mechanisms have been proposed to explain the role of the CGRP pathway in migraine progression. Newer anti-CGRP therapies, including gepants such as atogepant, rimegepant, ubrogepant, and zavegepant, offer improved pharmacokinetics [30–36] (Table 2) and more sustained effects [37, 38]. Atogepant, currently under clinical trial for acute treatment (NCT06241313, start date 2024-03-25), is used as a prophylactic medication; ubrogepant and zavegepant are used as acute medications; and rimegepant is used as both an acute and a prophylactic medication. CGRP-bypassing or downstream cAMP-mediated mechanisms could contribute to refractoriness to gepants. Clinical evidence demonstrates that migraine attacks can be provoked by cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)-mediated mechanisms, for example, through the administration of cilostazol [39], even when the CGRP receptor is effectively blocked by agents like erenumab [40]. This directly shows that blocking CGRP, while effective for many, is not a universal solution because other pathways can still trigger migraine.

Other neuropeptides, such as Pituitary Adenylate Cyclase-Activating Polypeptide (PACAP) and Vasoactive Intestinal Polypeptide (VIP), are also known to increase intracellular cAMP levels and can provoke migraine attacks [40, 41]. This might prompt the development of multi-target therapies or sequential treatment strategies that address these alternative pain pathways, although in such an approach, safety should obviously be closely studied.

Preventative therapies

These medications include several nonspecific drug classes, such as beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, anticonvulsants, antidepressants and Onabotulinumtoxin A. Reasons for non-response to these pharmacological agents are often multifactorial and may reflect that the targets of these drugs, which are unclear in many cases, may not be prominently involved in every patient receiving them. Further, the pharmacokinetics of conventional migraine preventives demonstrate considerable heterogeneity, which may contribute to inter-individual variability in efficacy, tolerability, and drug interactions (Table 3).

One significant reason for apparent resistance to these drugs is their non-specific mechanisms of action, which can also lead to a wide range of tolerability issues. These adverse effects frequently lead to premature discontinuation of the medication rather than true pharmacological resistance at the target site. For example, tricyclic antidepressants, while effective, are associated with anticholinergic side effects and a lower threshold for overdose, limiting their first-line use [42]. Similarly, topiramate can cause cognitive dysfunction and paresthesia, leading to patient non-adherence [43]. This highlights that resistance in this context is often a practical problem of drug tolerability and adherence. Finally, an insufficient dose or treatment duration can mimic true pharmacological resistance [44]. Patients may discontinue medication prematurely due to side effects or a lack of immediate perceived benefit before an adequate trial period or dose escalation has been completed. This underscores the importance of careful titration and patient education in maximizing the effectiveness of these older, broadly acting preventive agents. Onabotulinumtoxin A inhibits the release of CGRP and other pro-nociceptive neurotransmitters by cleaving SNAP-25, a key protein involved in exocytosis [45, 46]. However, cases of non-response occur during medication overuse, in long-standing disease and comorbidity with depressive symptoms [47]. OnabotulinumtoxinA may be a useful tool to combine with gepants [48, 49]. All the gathered mechanisms involved in non-response to current treatments are summarized in Table 4.

Individual genetic polymorphisms may also play a role by affecting drug-metabolising enzymes or target receptors, thereby influencing drug concentrations or response. For instance, insertion/deletion (I/D) polymorphisms in the angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) gene alter ACE enzymatic activity, potentially leading to differences in baseline angiotensin II levels. Such variability could theoretically influence the efficacy of angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) such as candesartan, used for migraine prevention. However, although an association between the ACE I/D polymorphism and migraine susceptibility has been reported [50], robust clinical evidence linking ACE genotype to response to candesartan treatment remains lacking [51, 52]. Similarly, CYP2D6 polymorphisms significantly affect the metabolism of beta-blockers such as propranolol and metoprolol: poor metabolisers exhibit higher plasma concentrations and may experience increased side effects, whereas ultra-rapid metabolisers may experience reduced efficacy. Polymorphisms in the β1-adrenergic receptor gene (ADRB1) have also been suggested to modulate the pharmacodynamic response to beta-blockers, although their clinical relevance in migraine prophylaxis is still under investigation [53].

CGRP/R-targeting monoclonal antibodies (mAbs)

Lack of or poor response to CGRP-targeted therapies can arise from incomplete CGRP/R blockade [54], activation of compensatory pathways, or CGRP-independent mechanisms. While CGRP is a key mediator in migraine pathophysiology, other neuropeptides or pain pathways may become more prominent or compensatory in non-responders, diminishing the effect of CGRP blockade [55]. Variability in receptor or ligand characteristics can also play a role. Although anti-CGRP mAbs target either the CGRP ligand or its receptor, some patients may not respond to one class and could benefit from switching to another due to subtle differences in target engagement or individual pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic profiles (Table 5). However, robust evidence for this specific switching benefit remains scarce [56, 57]. An important observation is the phenomenon of “ultra-late response.” A significant percentage of patients (15.7%) achieve a ≥ 50% reduction in monthly migraine days (MMDs) only after 24 weeks of anti-CGRP mAb treatment, although it is not certain whether this is due to drug response [58]. This finding fundamentally redefines the concept of “resistance” and challenges the conventional 12-week efficacy assessment period typically used in clinical trials and for reimbursement criteria. Patients classified as “non-responders” based on early assessments might, in fact, be ultra-late responders who require a longer duration of therapy to achieve benefit. These ultra-late responders often present with a more complex clinical profile, including higher body mass index (BMI), a longer history of medication overuse, a greater number of comorbidities (including psychiatric disorders), and less common unilateral pain [58]. In particular, a possible reason for non-response is that CGRP mAbs are underdosed [59]. This complex clinical picture suggests a more entrenched migraine phenotype that may necessitate a longer duration of the CGRP pathway blockade to induce sufficient neuroplastic changes, overcome chronic sensitisation, or rebalance dysregulated pain networks. The arbitrary nature of current time limits for efficacy assessment and reimbursement may thus lead to premature discontinuation of potentially effective treatment.

The balance between central and peripheral CGRP action is also relevant in understanding non-response to these drugs. If central CGRP-independent mechanisms of pain processing are dominant, peripheral CGRP blockade might be less effective [60]. The intricate interplay between CGRP and glutamate, the brain’s primary excitatory neurotransmitter, is a critical neuropharmacological limitation of CGRP-only blockade in addressing central sensitisation [61]. Glutamate plays a crucial role in enhancing CGRP release and promoting both peripheral and central sensitisation. This relationship is complex and bidirectional: increased glutamate levels can act upstream to trigger CGRP release, and CGRP, in turn, can amplify glutamate signalling and receptor sensitivity, creating a sustained feedback loop of neuronal excitation and sensitisation. If CGRP blockade alone does not adequately address these upstream, glutamate-driven mechanisms, especially when central sensitisation is dominant, can contribute significantly to treatment resistance.

The presence of cutaneous allodynia, a clinical manifestation of central sensitisation, is particularly predictive of treatment failure with anti-CGRP medications. This suggests that for patients with established central sensitisation, CGRP-targeted treatments may be insufficient to reverse the chronic migraine state if the underlying glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity is not addressed. In addition, individual factors such as body weight, metabolism, and the development of anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) can lead to variability in drug plasma concentrations, potentially affecting therapeutic response [57]. OnabotulinumtoxinA is part of one of the most clinically relevant dual therapies, when combined with gepants and anti-CGRP/R mAbs, for chronic migraine [48, 49]. These agents exert their effects at distinct, but intersecting levels of the trigeminovascular system. When used in combination with mAbs, they may enhance efficacy by simultaneously reducing neuropeptide release and blocking downstream receptor activation [62]. This dual mechanism is particularly relevant in chronic migraine, where both peripheral and central sensitisation are implicated. The distinct mechanisms of these agents enable them to be used in rational, complementary combinations. For instance, onabotulinumtoxin A may reduce CGRP release upstream, while monoclonal antibodies block its receptor-mediated action downstream [62]. A clinical study assessing the efficacy and safety of combining atogepant or mAbs with onabotulinumtoxinA for the preventive treatment of chronic migraine is currently ongoing [63]. A very recent pharmacological approach that warrants investigation is serotonergic modulation by the classic psychedelic psilocybin [64]. Novel randomised clinical trials, endowed with adequate sample size, are needed to assess the efficacy and safety of all these potential, also non-pharmacological, treatments in difficult-to-treat patients and to define the neuropharmacological, genetic and epigenetic mechanisms [35, 65] involving the CGRP pathway directly or indirectly and at the root of resistance and refractoriness to pharmacological therapy. A CGRP inhibitor may also be combined with topiramate to address cortical excitability and central sensitisation.

For individuals in whom excitatory neurotransmission plays a dominant role, as indicated by features such as aura, photophobia, or allodynia, glutamate-modulating agents such as topiramate or memantine may be valuable adjuncts [66, 67]. In such patients, combining cortical excitability modulators with CGRP-targeted agents may blunt both peripheral initiation and central amplification of migraine attacks [66, 67]. Therefore, a forward-looking approach involves identifying patients with non-CGRP-dominant phenotypes, in whom future therapies targeting pathways such as PACAP may be used alongside CGRP blockade [68].

Potential approaches to overcome resistance

Despite the availability of various migraine treatments, a significant number of patients continue to experience inadequate relief or adverse effects. This has prompted exploration of new therapeutic strategies, including novel pharmacological agents, combination therapies and non-pharmacological interventions. The frequent occurrence of comorbidities, such as hypertension or depression, often necessitates polypharmacy, which increases the need to carefully manage potential drug–drug interactions. To optimise outcomes and reduce the risk of complications, managing these pharmacological interactions is crucial. Personalised medicine—incorporating clinical characteristics and individual preferences—is becoming a cornerstone in the treatment of refractory migraine. This approach improves drug selection, enhances prediction of therapeutic response, and reduces the risk of adverse effects, ultimately improving outcomes in complex clinical scenarios [69]. An important example of individualised therapy is hormonal treatment in women experiencing menstrually-related migraine. Tailoring treatment to the menstrual cycle can effectively reduce attack frequency and severity. For instance, initiating estrogen supplementation (e.g., via transdermal patches or gels) initiated two days before menstruation can stabilise hormonal fluctuations and prevent the rapid estrogen decline that often triggers migraines. Alternatively, short-term perimenstrual prophylaxis (“miniprophylaxis”) using NSAIDs or triptans for 2–5 days prior to menstruation has demonstrated efficacy in preventing hormonally-triggered migraines [70]. These strategies highlight the importance of integrating hormonal dynamics into personalised migraine management for women.

Pharmacological targets that remain unaddressed by current prophylactics

The discovery of vasoactive neuropeptides, including the pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP), CGRP, and the vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), has significantly advanced understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms in migraine. PACAP promotes vasodilation in cranial arteries and sensitises trigeminovascular afferents. Therapeutic exploration of the PACAP axis is in the spotlight, particularly in refractory migraine. These findings align with the current understanding of migraine pathophysiology. Elevated CGRP levels may indicate its role in initiating trigeminovascular responses and enhancing cranial vessel dilation [71]. The simultaneous augmentation of VIP and PACAP-38 suggests stimulation of smooth muscle receptors and upregulation of intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), leading to vasodilation and activation of the trigeminal pain pathway [71]. In a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 38 participants, Al-Karagholi et al. investigated whether PACAP-38 and CGRP act through overlapping mechanisms in migraine pathophysiology. Patients were randomised to receive either an intravenous infusion of eptinezumab (a monoclonal antibody against CGRP) or placebo, followed two hours later by an intravenous infusion of PACAP-38 to experimentally induce a migraine attack. The incidence of migraine within 24 h—the study’s primary endpoint—did not differ significantly between the eptinezumab and placebo groups, nor did secondary measures such as headache intensity or vascular changes. These findings indicate that blockade of CGRP does not prevent PACAP-38–induced migraine attacks, suggesting that PACAP-38 triggers migraine via a pathway that is likely independent of CGRP signaling and may represent a distinct therapeutic target [72]. The AMY₁ receptor is a complex receptor, initially described in the context of metabolic signaling and recently implicated in pain regulation. The high-affinity binding of CGRP to the AMY receptor adds to its relevance as a complementary or alternative signaling route when canonical CGRP receptors are blocked [73, 74]. Notably, the AMY₁ receptor acts as a dual receptor, responding not only to CGRP but also strongly to amylin, a neuroendocrine hormone structurally related to CGRP and increasingly linked to migraine mechanisms. Gepants such as rimegepant inhibit both receptor types: this broader antagonism, combined with the fact that small molecules probably can access internalized CGRP receptors, while mAbs cannot do so [75]. may explain why some patients respond to gepants after mAb failure, assuming the AMY₁ receptor is expressed in migraine-relevant tissues accessible to these small molecules [76]. Importantly, amylin may engage additional signalling cascades, particularly under chronic or pharmacologically altered conditions.

Conclusions and future perspectives

A deeper understanding of migraine pathophysiology, including factors such as genetic predisposition and neuroinflammatory mechanisms, may pave the way for more personalised and effective interventions [77, 78]. Resistant or unresponsive migraine is closely associated with clinical and neurobiological factors that impair therapeutic response and amplify the overall disease burden. Specifically, patients with resistant or refractory migraine are reported to have a longer disease duration and a higher prevalence of chronification than treatment responders. In fact, this not only contributes to diagnostic accuracy but also facilitates systematic documentation, targeted research efforts, and a more equitable allocation of resources. The monoclonal antibody targeting PACAP Lu AG09222 [79] is currently under clinical investigation for migraine prevention. However, clinical and pharmacokinetic data on PACAP-targeting are limited. Potassium channel function, particularly via ATP-sensitive potassium (K+ATP) channels, has been implicated in migraine pathophysiology by regulating neuronal excitability and vascular tone. Activation of K+ATP channels (e.g., by levcromakalim) can trigger migraine attacks, suggesting that K+ATP channel inhibition may offer a novel preventive approach [80]. Pharmacokinetic data for K+ATP inhibitors remain limited, as these agents are still in early clinical development. Combination therapy might represent a valuable pharmacological tool: it may increase side effects, as multiple targets are modified simultaneously, but, if managed properly and carefully, this approach with synergistic and additive mechanisms might be explored to overcome non-response. For instance, combining CGRP inhibitors, such as mAbs and small-molecule antagonists (gepants), with other agents like onabotulinumtoxinA has shown promise in enhancing efficacy by targeting both the CGRP peptide and its receptor, since endowed with proven safety [81]. Moreover, episodic migraine is more amenable to CGRP-targeting therapy than chronic migraine, but onabotulinumtoxinA is effective in chronic migraine likely for its interference with the expression of high-threshold mechanosensitive ion channels on the surface of nerve cells [82] and to offer long-lasting anti-hyperalgesic effects to mechanical and cold stimuli in neuropathic pain, as experimentally proven in the spared nerve injury model [83]. Additionally, combining oral conventional prophylactics, such as propranolol or topiramate, with CGRP-targeting therapies may provide synergistic effects, particularly in patients with comorbid conditions. However, further large-scale trials are needed to establish safe and effective combination protocols [84]. Recognizing and managing these contributing factors is essential for early prognosis and the design of safe individualized treatment strategies (Fig. 2).
