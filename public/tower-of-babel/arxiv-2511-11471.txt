Laminar boundary layers over small-scale textured surfaces Journal: Applied Mathematics and Computation
Samuel D. Tomlinson, Demetrios T. Papageorgiou

Abstract
We develop a model for steady, laminar boundary layers over small-scale textured surfaces. Although the texture is small relative to the boundary-layer thickness, it modifies the flow via a slip length. We use matched asymptotic expansions to simplify the problem, dividing the flow into outer, boundary-layer and inner regions. The far-field behaviour of the inner problem yields a slip boundary condition for the boundary layer. We derive an asymptotic solution valid when the slip length is small. For arbitrary slip lengths, we develop a numerical method combining Chebyshev collocation and finite differences. We apply this framework to canonical small-scale textured surfaces, including superhydrophobic surfaces and riblets, and utilise existing analytical slip formulae. We demonstrate how slip can effect the boundary layer’s velocity field, wall shear stress and displacement thickness. Our approach enables computationally inexpensive modelling of small-scale textured surfaces in applications ranging from microfluidics to marine transport.

Introduction

In high-Reynolds-number flow, a thin boundary layer exists near the wall where viscous effects are significant, even though inertia dominates the bulk flow. This fundamental structure, formalised by Prandtl in 1904, enables simplification of the Navier–Stokes equations via matched asymptotic expansions, separating the viscous near-wall region from the outer inviscid flow
[
22
]
. Within this viscous near-wall region, normal diffusion balances streamwise advection, allowing the flow to be described by a reduced model: the boundary-layer equations. For steady, incompressible flow over a flat plate, the boundary-layer thickness scales as
\hat{\delta}\sim(\hat{\nu}\hat{x}/\hat{U})^{1/2}
, where
\hat{\nu}
is the kinematic viscosity,
\hat{x}
the streamwise coordinate and
\hat{U}
the free-stream velocity
[
26
]
. Downstream, the boundary layer grows in thickness as the velocity adjusts from the free-stream value
\hat{U}
to the no-slip condition at the wall. For a uniform free-stream velocity over a smooth, infinitely long flat plate, the Blasius solution emerges, providing an exact similarity reduction of the boundary-layer equations to an ordinary differential equation (ODE)
[
6
]
.

The classical boundary-layer framework assumes a smooth, impermeable surface and a no-slip condition at the wall. However, many natural or engineered surfaces, such as superhydrophobic surfaces (SHSs), liquid-infused surfaces (LISs), riblets and porous surfaces, exhibit texture that can modify the viscous near-wall flow
[
14
,
12
,
4
,
10
,
21
,
3
]
. These textures typically have a characteristic normal height
A
and streamwise period
\epsilon
, which may be small relative to the boundary-layer thickness
\delta
, yet their cumulative effect can be significant, as they alter near-wall shear and flow resistance, ultimately influencing the boundary-layer flow
[
7
]
. When a separation of scales exists (i.e.,
\epsilon\ll\delta
), the small-scale texture can be homogenised, meaning its effect is captured through an effective boundary condition for the boundary-layer problem, rather than being explicitly resolved. A common effective boundary condition in this context is a slip boundary condition, characterised by a slip length
\lambda
, which encapsulates the geometry and flow properties of the small-scale texture by modulating the velocity at the wall
[
14
,
12
,
4
,
10
,
21
,
3
]
. The effect of slip on the boundary layer depends on the relative magnitude of
\lambda
and
\delta

[
26
,
35
]
. When
\lambda\ll\delta
, slip acts as a small perturbation to the classical boundary layer, and Blasius’ solution remains valid to leading order. When
\lambda\sim\delta
, slip modifies the classical boundary-layer structure, causing Blasius’ solution to break down. When
\lambda\gg\delta
, homogenisation of the surface fails, and the texture must be resolved directly.

Flow over a small-scale textured surface divides into three regions, reflecting the balance between inertial and viscous forces and forming the basis for constructing matched asymptotic solutions
[
22
,
35
]
. The outer region, governed by the Euler equations, is inviscid and unaffected by the wall. Viscous effects are negligible here, and the velocity field is uniform, reflecting the dominant inertial dynamics in the bulk. The middle region corresponds to the boundary layer, where inertial and viscous forces are both significant and balanced. Here, the velocity adjusts to match the outer inviscid flow and inner viscous region. The boundary layer has a thickness of
O(\delta)
and governs the wall shear stress responsible for drag. The inner region lies closest to the wall, with a thickness of
O(\epsilon)
, corresponding to the small-scale texture
[
16
,
35
]
. In the inner region, inertial effects are negligible compared to viscous forces, and the texture must be fully resolved. The flow is governed by the Stokes equations, and its far-field behaviour determines the slip length
\lambda
in the boundary layer. Using matched asymptotic expansions, these three regions can be connected to construct a solution valid across all scales, allowing the small-scale texture to be replaced by a slip boundary condition in the boundary-layer equations.

In particular, a variety of small-scale textured surfaces have been investigated using multi-scale modelling and experiments
[
7
]
. On superhydrophobic surfaces (SHSs), slip arises from gas pockets trapped between ridges or pillars
[
13
]
. The slip length for SHSs depends on the small-scale texture geometry, gas fraction and alignment relative to the flow direction. Analytical and semi-analytical models describing SHS slip behaviour include those by Philip
[
20
]
, Lauga and Stone
[
14
]
and Ybert et al.
[
40
]
. Liquid-infused surfaces (LISs), in which the small-scale texture is filled with a second immiscible fluid, exhibit slip lengths that depend not only on geometry but also on viscosity contrast, interfacial tension and capillary effects (Wong et al.
[
38
]
). Studies by Hardt and McHale
[
12
]
, Sundin and Bagheri
[
30
]
and Van Buren and Smits
[
8
]
have extended SHS-style analyses to LISs. Riblets have long been employed for turbulent drag reduction and can also produce slip effects in laminar flows. Their influence is anisotropic and can be modelled using slip tensors that account for the alignment of the riblets relative to the flow
[
16
,
4
,
5
,
37
]
. Other periodic textures have been analysed using periodic homogenisation, yielding expressions for slip in Feuillebois et al.
[
10
]
, Zhou et al.
[
41
]
, and Sharma et al.
[
27
]
. For random textures, stochastic homogenisation and empirical fits to experiments have been used to develop models for slip
[

,

23

In this work, we develop a model for steady, two-dimensional (2D) laminar boundary layers over small-scale textured surfaces. Assuming the texture is small compared to the boundary layer thickness, we homogenise the surface’s effect into a slip length
\lambda

[
7
]
, resulting in a slip boundary condition of the form
u=\lambda u_{y}
, which modifies Blasius’ solution
[
35
]
. We derive an asymptotic solution valid for
\lambda/\sqrt{x}\ll 1
and construct a numerical solution for
\lambda/\sqrt{x}=O(1)
using Chebyshev collocation in the normal direction and finite differences in the streamwise direction
[
36
]
. This asymptotic–numerical approach efficiently and accurately resolves flows for arbitrary slip lengths.
Our method predicts boundary layer profiles and wall shear stresses without explicitly resolving the small-scale surface texture, reducing computational cost. Applications of this model include drag-reduction technologies, ranging from microfluidic device optimisation to engineered surfaces for marine transport. We demonstrate the utility of this approach by applying it to small-scale periodic textures with known analytical slip lengths.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. In Section
2

3

4

5

6

3
Model

To simplify the governing equations (
9
)–(
14
), we exploit the assumed scale separation between the textured surface and the boundary-layer flow. For SHSs, we consider the asymptotic regime
\epsilon\ll\text{Re}^{-1/2}\ll 1
. This inequality is justified for specific applications in Section
5.3
. The flow is analysed by decomposing the domain into multiple regions, each governed by a distinct balance of inertial and viscous forces. For riblet geometries, we further assume
A\ll\epsilon\ll\text{Re}^{-1/2}\ll 1
, where
A
is the non-dimensional amplitude of the riblets, ensuring that the riblet height acts as a small perturbation within the textured scale. Under these scalings, the flow near the surface can be treated as locally periodic, modulated by the slowly varying boundary-layer shear defined in Section
3.2
. The leading-order boundary value problem, derived from (
9
)–(
14
), is solved using a combination of asymptotic expansions and numerical computations. The objective is to determine how small-scale period textures influence the development of the boundary-layer.

To this end, the flow is decomposed into three distinct regions, each associated with a dominant physical mechanism and an characteristic spatial scale. The outer region (Section
3.1
), or inviscid layer, is defined by
x=O(1)
and
y=O(1)
, where viscous effects are negligible. The middle region (Section
3.2
) corresponding to the boundary layer, is characterised by
x=O(1)
and
y=O(\text{Re}^{-1/2})
, where viscous–inertial interactions are significant. The inner region (Section
3.3
), located near the wall and around the small-scale texture, with
x=O(\epsilon)
and
y=O(\epsilon)
, where viscous effects dominate. The governing equations are analysed in each region under the appropriate scaling, solutions are constructed, and then matched asymptotically in the overlapping limits. This composite asymptotic solution is consist across regions and captures the multi-scale structure of the boundary layer.

3.1
Outer region (inviscid region)

In the outer region, the flow is governed by inviscid dynamics, corresponding to the bulk flow where the influence of the wall is negligible and inertia dominates. In this region, the spatial coordinates scale as
x=O(1)
and
y=O(1)
, with velocity components
u=O(1)
and
v=O(1)
. The flow and pressure fields are expanded in powers of

Substituting the expansion (

\displaystyle u^{*}_{x^{*}}+v^{*}_{y^{*}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle u^{*}u^{*}_{x^{*}}+v^{*}u^{*}_{y^{*}}+p^{*}_{x^{*}}

\displaystyle=0,

(17)

\displaystyle u^{*}v^{*}_{x^{*}}+v^{*}v^{*}_{y^{*}}+p^{*}_{y^{*}}

\displaystyle=0.

(18)

At the lower boundary

v^{*}=0.

(19)

At the upper boundary, the free-stream condition from (

(20)

as

u^{*}=1,\hskip 10.00002ptv^{*}=0.

This solution represents the undisturbed bulk flow and serves as the far-field condition for the boundary layer flow in the middle region, which in turn defines the slowly varying shear field that drives the inner, locally periodic problems.

3.2
Middle region (boundary layer)

In the middle region, corresponding to the boundary layer, inertia and viscosity are balanced in the normal direction, while streamwise viscous terms are negligible for
\text{Re}\gg 1
. We stretch the normal coordinate as

y^{\prime}=\text{Re}^{1/2}y,

(22)

reflecting the classical boundary-layer thickness scaling
\delta=O(\text{Re}^{-1/2})
for high-Reynolds number flows
[
26
]
. Correspondingly, we scale and expand the space and velocity fields:

x=x^{\prime},\hskip 10.00002ptu=u^{\prime}(x^{\prime},\,y^{\prime})+\dots,\hskip 10.00002ptv=v^{\prime}(x^{\prime},\,y^{\prime})/\text{Re}^{1/2}+\dots,

(23)

with

and

9
) and retaining leading-order terms as

\displaystyle u^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}}+v^{\prime}_{y^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle u^{\prime}u^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}}+v^{\prime}u^{\prime}_{y^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=u^{\prime}_{y^{\prime}y^{\prime}},

(25)

where

These equations describe the dominant physics within the thin viscous layer adjacent to the wall, where the velocity adjusts to satisfy the boundary condition at

u^{\prime}\to 1\hskip 10.00002pt\text{as}\hskip 10.00002pty^{\prime}\to\infty.

3.3
Inner region (viscous roughness region)

The inner region corresponds to the small-scale texture, capturing both surface variations and the associated viscous flow. We define the stretched coordinates

3.4
Matching between middle and inner regions

In the overlapping region, the inner solution (

4
Methods

4.1
Numerical solution

We solve the steady, incompressible, 2D boundary-layer equations subject to a slip boundary condition, as specified in (
24
,
26
,
42
). Although (
24
,
26
,
42
) were derived under the assumption that
\epsilon\ll\text{Re}^{-1/2}
(implying

to assess the range of validity of the asymptotic solution discussed in Section

[

The normal domain

36
]

is chosen to capture the full boundary-layer development, ensuring that numerically

as

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=[\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\,\,\,\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\,\,\,\mathbf{q}^{\prime}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3(N_{\eta}+1)}

D_{\eta}
and
D^{2}_{\eta}
are constructed using standard Chebyshev-collocation methods and scaled to the physical domain
[
36
]
. At each streamwise step
x^{\prime}_{i}
, the boundary-layer equations (
24
,
26
,
42
) are discretised to form a linear system:

\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{b},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{L}_{1}&\mathbf{L}_{2}&\mathbf{L}_{3}\\
\mathbf{M}_{1}&\mathbf{M}_{2}&\mathbf{M}_{3}\\
\mathbf{N}_{1}&\mathbf{N}_{2}&\mathbf{N}_{3}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{3(N_{\eta}+1)\times 3(N_{\eta}+1)},

(43)

where each submatrix
\mathbf{L}_{i},\,\mathbf{M}_{i},\,\mathbf{N}_{i}
encodes diffusive, advective and continuity contributions for the respective velocity components. Boundary conditions are applied by directly replacing the corresponding rows of
\mathbf{A}
and entries of
\mathbf{b}
. At the wall (
y^{\prime}=0
), we impose the slip condition
u^{\prime}=\lambda u^{\prime}_{y}
and no penetration
v^{\prime}=0
; at the outer boundary (
y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}_{\infty}
), the velocity is set to match the free stream
u^{\prime}=1
. Nonlinear terms, such as
u^{\prime}\partial u^{\prime}/\partial y^{\prime}
, are treated using a fixed-point iteration, where the previous iteration’s solution is used to linearise the system. The iteration proceeds until the relative residual, defined as
\tau=\left\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime(n)}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime(n+1)})\right\|_{1}/r
, falls below a prescribed tolerance of
10^{-4}
, with a relaxation factor of
r=0.5
applied to improve convergence stability. Based on convergence tests, we set
y^{\prime}_{\infty}=30
and use
N_{\eta}=30
collocation points in the normal direction and
N_{x}=1000
streamwise steps over
x^{\prime}\in[0,\,30]
. The resulting scheme combines spectral accuracy in
y^{\prime}
with implicit integration in
x^{\prime}
, providing an efficient, stable and accurate solution for boundary layers with arbitrary slip lengths.

4.2
Key quantities of interest

Key quantities of interest are the displacement thickness
\delta(x^{\prime})
and the wall shear stress
\tau(x^{\prime})

[
26
]
. The displacement thickness is evaluated from the velocity field using Clenshaw–Curtis quadrature based on Chebyshev collocation weights
[
36
]
. These quantities characterise how texture-induced slip alters the boundary-layer structure and wall shear, providing key indicators of surface performance
[
26
]
. The displacement thickness represents the distance by which the effective wall position must be shifted to conserve the free-stream mass flux, and is defined as

\delta=\int_{0}^{y^{\prime}_{\infty}}(1-u^{\prime})\,dy^{\prime}.

(44)

The wall shear stress is defined as the normal gradient of the streamwise velocity at the wall,

\tau=u^{\prime}_{y^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},\,0),

(45)

and quantifies the local tangential force per unit area exerted by the fluid on the surface. Together,
\delta
and
\tau
serve as key diagnostics for assessing the influence of small-scale surface texture and for validating the asymptotic predictions.

4.3
Asymptotic solution

As
\lambda\ll 1
, we have
\lambda/\sqrt{x^{\prime}}=\epsilon\beta\text{Re}^{1/2}/\sqrt{x^{\prime}}\ll 1
as
x^{\prime}=O(1)
. In this limit, we solve the boundary-layer equations (
24
,
26
,
42

\lambda
, obtaining corrections to Blasius’ solution:

u^{\prime}=u_{0}+\lambda u_{1}/\sqrt{x^{\prime}}+\lambda^{2}u_{2}/x^{\prime}+\dots,\hskip 10.00002ptv^{\prime}=v_{0}+\lambda v_{1}/\sqrt{x^{\prime}}+\lambda^{2}v_{2}/x^{\prime}+\dots.

(46)

4.3.1
Leading-order solution

Substituting (
46
) into (
24
,
26
,
42

\eta=y^{\prime}/\sqrt{x^{\prime}}
, the leading-order velocity can be written as

u_{0}=f_{\eta},\hskip 10.00002ptv_{0}=(\eta f_{\eta}-f)/(2\sqrt{x^{\prime}}),

(47)

where
f=f(\eta)
satisfies the Blasius ordinary differential equation (ODE) and boundary conditions:

2f_{\eta\eta\eta}+ff_{\eta\eta}=0,\hskip 10.00002ptf(0)=0,\hskip 10.00002ptf_{\eta}(0)=0,\hskip 10.00002ptf_{\eta}(\infty)=1.

(48)

Here,
v_{0}
is determined from continuity. The leading-order displacement thickness (
44
) is

\delta_{0}=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-f_{\eta})\,dy^{\prime}=\sqrt{x^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-f_{\eta})\,d\eta\approx 1.72\sqrt{x^{\prime}},

(49)

and the leading-order wall shear stress (
45
) is

\tau_{0}=u_{0y^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},\,0)=f_{\eta\eta}(0)\sqrt{x^{\prime}}\approx 0.33/\sqrt{x^{\prime}}.

(50)

4.3.2
First-order correction

At
O(\lambda/\sqrt{x^{\prime}})
of (
46
) in (
24
,
26
,
42
), we introduce the fist-order correction

u_{1}=g_{\eta},\hskip 10.00002ptv_{1}=(\eta g_{\eta}-g)/(2\sqrt{x^{\prime}}),

(51)

where
g=g(\eta)
satisfies an ODE, linearised about the Blasius profile
f
, and boundary conditions

2g_{\eta\eta\eta}+fg_{\eta\eta}+f_{\eta\eta}g=0,\hskip 10.00002ptg(0)=0,\hskip 10.00002ptg_{\eta}(0)=-f_{\eta\eta}(0),\hskip 10.00002ptg_{\eta}(\infty)\to 0.

(52)

The first-order correction redistributes the velocity profile but does not change the wall gradient, as
g_{\eta\eta}(0)=0
.

4.3.3
Second-order solution

At
O(\lambda^{2}/x^{\prime})
of (
46
) in (

,
26
,
42

u_{2}=h_{\eta},\hskip 10.00002ptv_{2}=(\eta h_{\eta}-h)/(2\sqrt{x^{\prime}}),

(53)

where
h=h(\eta)

f
and first-order correction

2h_{\eta\eta\eta}+fh_{\eta\eta}+f_{\eta\eta}h+gg_{\eta}=0,\hskip 10.00002pth(0)=0,\hskip 10.00002pth_{\eta}(0)=-g_{\eta\eta}(0),\hskip 10.00002pth_{\eta}(\infty)\to 0.

The corrected displacement thickness (

) is

\delta=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-f_{\eta}+\lambda g_{\eta}/\sqrt{x^{\prime}}+\dots)\sqrt{x^{\prime}}\,d\eta=\sqrt{x^{\prime}}(1.72-\lambda/\sqrt{x^{\prime}}+\dots),

(55)

and the corrected wall shear stress (

\tau=u^{\prime}_{y^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},\,0)=(f_{\eta\eta}(0)+\lambda g_{\eta\eta}(0)/\sqrt{x^{\prime}}+\lambda^{2}h_{\eta\eta}(0)/x^{\prime}+\dots)/\sqrt{x^{\prime}}\\

Discussion

We have developed a framework for steady, 2D laminar boundary layers over small-scale textured surfaces, in which the surface geometry is incorporated through a homogenised slip length. This formulation combines matched asymptotic expansions with numerical computation, enabling the flow to be resolved without directly simulating the small-scale texture. A main contribution of this work is a slip-corrected boundary-layer model, in which the Prandtl boundary-layer equations are solved with a slip boundary condition of the form
u=\lambda u_{y}
imposed at the wall. This slip boundary condition captures the influence of small-scale surface texture through a macroscopic parameter, enabling efficient prediction of drag and boundary-layer quantities. The slip length is obtained by homogenising the inner viscous layer and is valid under the assumption that the texture period
\hat{P}
is small compared to the flow scale
\hat{L}
, i.e.,
\epsilon=\hat{P}/\hat{L}\ll 1
. Under this scale separation, the small-scale texture perturbs the boundary-layer flow only through its averaged effect.

We have derived an asymptotic solution for the velocity field valid when the slip length is small compared with the boundary-layer thickness, i.e.,
\lambda/\sqrt{x}\ll 1
, giving a perturbation to the Blasius solution with the correction arising from wall slip. This condition arises by comparing the slip velocity, of order
\lambda u_{y}
, with the streamwise velocity
u
in the outer region. When
\lambda/\sqrt{x}
is small, slip acts as a weak perturbation and the similarity structure is preserved. To extend the model to cases where
\lambda/\sqrt{x}=O(1)
, we developed a numerical scheme that employs Chebyshev collocation in the wall-normal direction and implicit marching in the streamwise direction, which provides stability and high accuracy in resolving steep velocity gradients near the wall. Numerical results (Fig.
3
–
4
) confirm the asymptotic predictions in the regime
\lambda/\sqrt{x}\ll 1
and extend them to larger slip lengths. Both approaches show that the slip-corrected velocity field, wall shear stress and displacement thickness deviate most strongly from the Blasius solution near the leading edge, where the boundary layer is thin and velocity gradients are steep, before converging downstream as
\lambda/\sqrt{x}
decreases.

The present analysis is restricted to steady, 2D, laminar boundary layers. While appropriate for many applications, the current framework does not account for unsteady or three-dimensional effects
[
31
]
, which could be incorporated in future extensions. The assumption of a high Reynolds number with a thin boundary layer justifies the use of Prandtl boundary-layer theory, but excludes low-Reynolds-number regimes where viscous effects dominate the entire flow domain. In practice, textured surfaces may also be random, aperiodic or hierarchical
[
25
,
23
,
9
]
. Although the present analysis focuses on periodic textures, homogenisation methods can be extended to disordered geometries using stochastic or statistical approaches
[
25
,
21
]
. Another idealisation concerns the flatness of the liquid–gas interface on SHSs, which underlies Philip’s analytical slip-length expression
[
20
]
. In experiments, meniscus curvature and deformation are often significant, leading to deviations from the theoretical slip predictions
[
13
,
40
]
. Similarly, the asymptotic model for riblets, expressed as
\beta=-\pi^{2}A^{2}/(4\epsilon^{2})
, assumes small amplitude (
A\ll\epsilon
) and low curvature
[
16
,
4
]
. When this assumption is violated, for example at larger riblet amplitudes, the effective slip may differ from the asymptotic prediction

Despite these limitations, the framework provides a foundation for more general problems. It can be extended to transitional or turbulent boundary layers
[
32
,
33
]
, where slip modifies near-wall turbulence structures, or to time-dependent flows
[
31
]
, such as pulsatile or start-up boundary layers. The slip boundary condition may also be generalised to a tensorial form to represent anisotropic textures such as grooves or riblets aligned with the flow direction
[
2
]
. By coupling the present boundary-layer formulation to macroscopic flow solvers, it is possible to incorporate microscale surface effects into large-scale simulations without explicitly resolving the surface geometry
[
11
,
7
]
. Representative applications include SHSs and LISs, where analytical slip-length expressions are available for simple geometries
[
13
,
20
,
14
,
40
,
38
,
12
,
30
,
8
]
and riblets, whose small-amplitude slip effects can be derived asymptotically
[
2
,
10
,
41
,
27
]
. Porous surfaces, where slip follows from Brinkman theory;
[
3
,
18
,
17
]
; and rough or randomly textured surfaces, where homogenised or empirical slip lengths are used
[
25
,
21
,
23
,
9
]
, can also be accommodated. Soft or deformable substrates, and those subject to Marangoni stresses
[
34
]
, may also be accommodated, although only approximate or experimental slip lengths are typically available
[
28
,
29
,
19
]
. The ability to represent a wide variety of surface types within a single laminar boundary-layer framework highlights the versatility of the present approach.

In conclusion, this work establishes a framework for modelling laminar boundary-layer flows over periodically textured surfaces, incorporating slip lengths derived from homogenisation. By connecting small-scale surface geometry to large-scale flow dynamics, the model enables accurate prediction of wall shear stress and drag without explicitly resolving of the texture. This capability is directly relevant to the design and optimisation of microfluidic channels, drag-reducing coatings and engineered surfaces operating in laminar flow regimes.
