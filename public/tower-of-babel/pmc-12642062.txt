Interdisciplinary value-based cognitive behavioral treatment for people with persistent pain after posttraumatic spinal cord injury (project VALIANT): protocol for an intervention study using multiple baselines single-case experimental design
Authors: Anders Orup Aaby, Tonny Elmose Andersen, Sophie Lykkegaard Ravn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinal cord injury (SCI) often leads to persistent pain and significant challenges in personal, social, and occupational life, requiring interdisciplinary rehabilitation. Despite advances in pain understanding, effective treatment for the impact of pain on daily living remains sparse. This study addresses the need for effective interventions with the aim of developing and testing an interdisciplinary values-based cognitive behavioral therapy (V-CBT) program for individuals with SCI and persistent pain, also with an objective to apply fine-grained data collection to explore effects and change processes.MethodsThis study is a replicated multiple baselines single-case experimental design (SCED). Nine participants with SCI and persistent pain will be block-randomized to 1, 2, or 3-week baseline phases, thus serving as their own controls. The intervention comprises a manualized V-CBT program with 10 sessions, delivered online or face-to-face based on participant preference, by an interdisciplinary team (psychologist and physiotherapist). The main aim of the intervention is to promote value-based behaviors. Data collection includes daily online self-reports as well as measurements at pre-treatment, post-treatment, and three-month follow-up. Structured visual analysis and statistical methods assess treatment effects, emphasizing within-case changes and individual responses.DiscussionThis study possesses several strengths and limitations. A key strength is the interdisciplinary team, consisting of experienced physiotherapists and a psychologist, ensuring a high standard of care. The SCED methodology enables detailed tracking of individual treatment responses, allowing for a nuanced analysis of efficacy and change processes. The integration of a pre-post design with a three-month follow-up enhances the study’s robustness, while the relatively large sample size for a SCED study strengthens its reliability. Additionally, the optional online format improves accessibility for individuals with mobility challenges or those in remote areas, potentially increasing participation and adherence. However, limitations include the absence of professionals such as occupational therapists or physicians, which could provide a more holistic rehabilitation approach. The online format, while increasing reach, may introduce technical barriers and limit therapeutic engagement. SCED designs also require frequent data collection, which may lead to participant fatigue and dropout, impacting data completeness. Despite these challenges, the study offers valuable insights into SCI pain management.Trial registrationNCT06599632 (Clinicaltrials.gov; registered 13 September 2024).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03637-5.

Background

Approximately 3,000 citizens in Denmark live with severe consequences of spinal cord injury (SCI) [1]. Beyond paralysis, these complications often encompass spasticity, chronic pain, gastrointestinal disorders, and an array of psychological challenges [2]. Globally, millions are impacted by SCI, with projections indicating a continued rise [3]. Nearly half of Denmark’s new annual SCI cases are of traumatic origin [1], with traffic accidents identified as one of the leading causes internationally [4]. SCI is irreversible and remains incurable, resulting in persistent disability and related symptomatology. The sudden life-changing nature of SCI imposes significant constraints on daily activities, leaving many individuals struggling to adapt to new realities. Consequently, many lose connections to both employment [5] and social networks [6], with up to 30% facing clinical levels of anxiety and depression for as long as a decade post-injury [7, 8]. Beyond the immediate impacts, SCI often leads to secondary complications and sequelae, further diminishing quality of life.

Physical rehabilitation is critical for recovery; however, enduring symptoms necessitate an interdisciplinary approach that incorporates psychological rehabilitation as an essential component. Adaptation can be facilitated by establishing rehabilitation goals rooted in individual life values [9–11]. By prioritizing meaningful pursuits and preserving core aspects of life despite physical limitations, individuals can enhance their post-injury adjustment and mitigate the adverse impact of pain on daily living. This approach requires an interdisciplinary focus on acceptance of what cannot be changed while redefining meaningful activities and life roles—a process aligned with the World Health Organization’s (WHO) concept of “participation” [12].

In a recent randomized controlled trial (RCT), we examined a values-based cognitive behavioral therapy (V-CBT) program designed to prevent long-term consequences of another posttraumatic condition with chronic pain and disability, namely whiplash injuries [13]. The results revealed significant improvements in terms of, among others, levels of disability, pain, anxiety, and depression [13]. Unfortunately, effective interventions to improve daily life and self-management for individuals with persistent SCI-related pain are limited. Therefore, there is a pressing need to adapt and extend the V-CBT intervention for this population, testing its effectiveness within an interdisciplinary framework that emphasizes acceptance, value-driven goals, and physical rehabilitation. Additionally, this study explores the potential of offering both online and face-to-face sessions to accommodate participant preferences. Online therapy offers the promise of greater accessibility, particularly for individuals facing mobility challenges or residing in remote areas, by enabling them to participate from their homes and potentially increasing adherence and convenience.

Unlike traditional RCTs, this study employs a single-case experimental design (SCED) to offer a more nuanced exploration of treatment effects and change processes [14]. Although RCTs are widely used in clinical research, they may be limited in capturing the personalized nuances of treatment responses, especially in chronic conditions such as SCI. The diverse experiences and responses of individuals with chronic pain may be obscured by group-level data in RCTs, potentially missing critical insights into heterogeneous responses [15]. By adopting a SCED, this study aims to address these limitations by examining within-subject changes over time, providing a more detailed understanding of individual responses to V-CBT [16]. The repeated measures inherent to this design enable detection of temporal patterns and individual differences in treatment response, which may otherwise remain hidden in traditional group analyses. SCEDs have been successfully applied to chronic pain research to explore efficacy and treatment processes [17–22], with further large-scale studies underway [23].

The goal of this study is to develop and evaluate an interdisciplinary V-CBT program, which can be delivered online or face-to-face based on participant preference, tailored for individuals with persistent SCI-related pain. Our objectives include assessing the program’s treatment efficacy and investigating associated change processes. We hypothesize that participants will exhibit improvements in primary outcomes, such as value-based living and pain interference, as well as in secondary outcomes, including pain catastrophizing, positive and negative affect, stress, fatigue, pain severity, pain acceptance, and self-efficacy. We further anticipate that these improvements will be maintained at post-treatment compared to pre-treatment levels and will persist at a three-month follow-up. Change processes will be examined through data-driven visual analyses without predefined hypotheses.

Methods

This study is part of a larger initiative, the VALIANT project, which focuses on the development and evaluation of an interdisciplinary V-CBT program. The program is specifically tailored to individuals with persistent pain associated with whiplash injuries (addressed in a separate publication) and individuals with SCI-related persistent pain, as explored in this study. While the program for the whiplash group is delivered in-person, the SCI group can choose between participating virtually through online videoconferencing or attending face-to-face sessions.

This study employs a replicated multiple baseline SCED. This means that participants are randomized to different waiting periods (i.e., baseline phases) and therefore act as their own control [14]. This design is particularly useful in evaluating treatment effects in situations where the treatment is expected to have lasting effects that cannot be removed after it has been introduced [24]. As such, all participants will receive treatment after completing the baseline phase. The baseline lengths are set to either 1, 2, or 3 weeks (i.e., tiers), and participants are block-randomized, so each tier has the same number of participants. This design is complemented by a pre-post design with a three-month follow-up. Participants and sample size

This study will include nine participants. While power calculations are not typically performed in studies using SCED methodology, including nine participants is awarded maximum points according to risk-of-bias guidelines [25], providing strong evidence for demonstrating treatment effects.

To be eligible for inclusion in this study, participants must meet a defined set of criteria. They must be 18 years or older, speak and read Danish, and have an acquired SCI. The injury can present as paraplegia or tetraplegia, whether complete or incomplete. Participants must also experience daily pain, be therapy-ready, and motivated to try this specific treatment approach. Additionally, they must be willing to engage in homework tasks and respond to daily questions. Weekly attendance in treatment sessions is required, with participants having the option to choose between online and face-to-face formats. Exclusion criteria include the presence of significant concerns identified during the physiotherapeutic assessment, being in an acute or sub-acute stage of injury, or having current pressure ulcers. Individuals with a known and unstable psychiatric condition, syringomyelia, or pregnancy are also excluded from participation.

Enrollment and randomization

Participants are recruited via two patient organizations: ‘Accident Victims Denmark’ and ‘RYK’, a specialized rehabilitation hospital: ‘Specialized Hospital for Polio and Accident Victims’ (henceforth Specialized Hospital), and a website dedicated to knowledge sharing about SCI: ‘Rygmarvsskade.info’. Written material will be sent to people who express an interest in participating. People who are still interested after reading the written material will be invited to a recruitment interview with the first author on a ‘first come first served’ basis. Participants are invited to have a significant other or counsel present at the recruitment interview. It is made clear that participation is voluntary, and that participants can continue any other treatment they are currently involved in. At the recruitment interview, it is made sure that the person meets all eligibility criteria, and, if this is the case, information about the project is provided and questions are answered. If all criteria are met, they are given a two-day consideration period. Everyone who then wishes to participate signs an informed consent and is allocated a project-ID. After inclusion, participants are block-randomized to a 1, 2, or 3-week baseline phase using computer-generated random numbers.

Intervention

The treatment will be anchored with experienced rehabilitation clinicians at Specialized Hospital in Denmark and will be delivered individually via secure online videoconferencing or as face-to-face sessions, based on participant preference. The treatment is a manualized adaptation of the V-CBT protocol previously tested in an RCT for preventing chronic whiplash-associated disorder (WAD) [13]. In this context, manualized refers to the treatment being delivered according to a structured therapist manual that specifies the content and sequence of each session, provides standardized worksheets and guidance for the clinicians, and ensures consistency and fidelity across participants while still allowing for individualized adaptation within each session. Initially tailored for an interdisciplinary setting for chronic WAD, the intervention was refined through focus group interviews with individuals living with persistent WAD, whose feedback shaped the content, reduced participant burden, and optimized daily assessment items for SCEDs. For this study, the protocol was further adapted for SCI through consultations with the clinical team, who provided input on the principles and content of the 10 sessions. It is thus built on a value-based cognitive behavioral foundation but combines different approaches and principles to adapt and improve V-CBT for people that live with persistent pain after SCI. The intervention will take place in an interdisciplinary setting, supported by a team of three highly experienced clinicians: one psychologist and two physiotherapists. The psychologist has over 10 years of clinical experience and specialist training in cognitive behavioral therapy, while the physiotherapists each have more than 25 years of experience in rehabilitation. Additionally, they all contributed to the parallel study on WAD. To prepare for WAD study, the physiotherapists underwent training in Cognitive Functional Therapy [26], including participating as observers in clinical sessions with a physiotherapist well-trained in this method.

In total, the treatment has 10 sessions with a duration of approximately 1 h. Each participant will be assigned the same psychologist and one of the two physiotherapists for consistency and individualized attention throughout the treatment period. In the first three sessions, the psychologist will introduce the program and focus on two main elements. First, the psychologist will educate in up-to-date pain science and theory in a personalized manner utilizing Socratic dialogue, also validating the participants’ subjective experiences and pain stories. Based on pain science and theory, simple key messages were developed. Second, the psychologist will explore the participants’ values and related challenges and goals. The aims of the first three sessions are to foster self-efficacy, provide better understanding of pain, facilitate insight into what is most important and valuable to them in their lives, and set value-based goals for the treatment. In the following seven sessions, physiotherapists trained in the V-CBT program, Motivational Interviewing (MI) skills [27], and Socratic dialogue will work with participants to further explore and facilitate behavioral changes towards values-based activities and ensure that these activities are integrated week by week into the participants’ everyday lives. It consists of two main components: one focuses on talk therapy, which includes evaluating homework, exploring the theme of the day, and introducing upcoming worksheets. The other emphasizes practical techniques, such as breathing exercises and gradual exposure to physical activities, aimed at fostering bodily confidence and reducing fear of movement. For some participants, exposure to physical movements and activities will be a central focus, helping to build bodily security and enabling greater engagement in value-driven activities within their daily lives. For others, this component will be less needed.

The core focus of these sessions is to promote engagement in values-based activities by addressing and overcoming psychological, physical, social, and practical barriers that may impede participants from fully participating in their lives. Common challenges include fear-avoidance beliefs and behaviors, patterns of “boom and bust,” and not optimally utilizing social support. To address these barriers, participants receive guidance on pacing principles, energy management, and gradual exposure to activities and movements, all designed to enhance their ability to engage in meaningful, value-driven pursuits. This process is reinforced through structured homework assignments (30–60 min) between sessions, with consistent follow-up to ensure progress and integration of the techniques into daily life (for a full program overview see Table 1). Training for the V-CBT program was initially conducted during the parallel WAD study and included a 5-hour workshop for the psychologist and three 5-hour workshops (15 h total) for the physiotherapists. For the current SCI study, additional training was provided through two online meetings of 1–2 h for the psychologist and a two-hour online meeting in addition to a 3.5-hour workshop specifically for the physiotherapists. All workshops were structured around the treatment manual, emphasizing the core components of facilitating exposure to value-based activities and developing effective communication skills essential for the program.

Training sessions for both the WAD and SCI studies incorporated role-playing exercises with live supervision and immediate feedback after each activity. Beyond their role as training opportunities, these workshops were a central part of the intervention development process. They served as collaborative forums for discussing treatment elements, collecting feedback, and making iterative refinements to the protocol. This approach ensured that the intervention was continually enhanced, integrating both practical insights and participant feedback to maximize its relevance and effectiveness.

To ensure that the treatment is delivered as intended, fidelity is assessed by clinicians systematically filling out a checklist after every session. This includes checking off handouts, exercises, and homework to be delivered. Additionally, the psychologist and physiotherapists will receive biweekly (group) supervision with at least two members of the project team. A subset of sessions will be video-recorded for supervision purposes.

Data collection and measurement scales

Both questionnaire and interview data are collected in this study. Questionnaire data are collected to assess treatment efficacy on primary and secondary outcomes as well as temporal relationships between variables, while interview data are collected for evaluation purposes where both participants and the therapists are interviewed.

In terms of questionnaire data, a range of descriptive data is collected, including sex, age, marital status, educational background, job status, when and how the injury occurred, injury level (paraplegia or tetraplegia), and injury type (complete or incomplete). For the SCED, 14 items are collected every day by self-report online via SurveyXact (https://rambollxact.dk/surveyxact). Participants receive their own personalized link and are asked to respond every day at a time that suits them best. Before responding to the items, participants are required to state what date their response is in reference to. Data completion is checked daily on weekdays, and participants receive a text message with a reminder when there is no response. Participants are allowed to respond to the daily items up to one day late. If they are more than one day late in responding, this is regarded as missing data.

The daily items were mostly developed by the author group with inspiration from complete measurement scales for these concepts, and revised based on feedback from experts within the field of chronic pain and WAD patient focus groups. The 14 items cover two primary outcomes: value-based living (based on the Engaged Living Scale [28]) and pain interference on general activities, relations with other people, and sleep (based on Brief Pain Inventory [29]) as well as 8 secondary outcomes: pain catastrophizing (based on Daily Pain Catastrophizing Scale [30]); positive and negative affect, fatigue, stress (based on prior diary studies [31]); pain related acceptance (based on Chronic Pain Acceptance Questionnaire [32]); pain related self-efficacy (based on Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire [33]); and pain severity (based on Brief Pain Inventory [29]). Participants can also note in a text box if something of relevance has happened that day (e.g., illness). All items are responded to on an 11-point rating scale ranging from 0 = completely disagree to 10 = completely agree. Pain is rated on a similar scale but anchored to 0 = no pain and 10 = worst possible pain. The order that the items are presented in will be the same each day. All daily items are provided in Table 2 below. Data completion will be checked manually every weekday by a research assistant or a project team member. Participants with missing data will be contacted by text message to ensure consistent responses.

In addition to the daily data collection, data from full measurement scales are collected pre-treatment, post-treatment, and at three-month follow-up. These measures cover value-based living measured with the Engaged Living Scale (ELS) [28]; pain interference with all items from Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) [29]; disability with Pain Disability Index (PDI) [34]; health-related quality of life with EuroQoL-5D-5 L [35]; depression with Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [36]; anxiety with the Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) [37]; stress with Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) [38]; pain acceptance with Chronic Pain Acceptance Questionnaire (CPAQ-8) [32]; pain self-efficacy with Short Form Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire [33]; sleep quality with Karolinska Sleep Questionnaire (KSQ) [39]; pain severity with four 0–10 numeric rating scale (NRS) for worst, mildest, average, and current pain; self-perceived improvement with Global Rating of Change (GROC) [40]; and satisfaction with treatment (self-developed NRS-item from 0 = not at all satisfied to 10 = completely satisfied).

After each session, participants are further asked to respond to three questionnaires: Helpful Aspects of Therapy (HAT) [41] (only the first two items); Credibility/Expectancy Questionnaire (CEQ) [42]; and Working Alliance Inventory (WAI) [43]. The latter is also filled out by the therapist. These data are also collected via SurveyXact.

After the three-month follow-up period, all participants are invited to an evaluation interview. These are individual and semi-structured and are expected to take 1 h to complete. They will center on the participants’ experiences of the content, delivery, and structure of the treatment and study design. Furthermore, the focus will be on what facilitators and barriers participants experienced during the treatment. Further, the three clinicians are invited to a 2-hour focus group interview to explore their experiences with delivering the treatment. All interviews are audio recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Patient and public involvement

In the initial stages of this research project, patients were actively involved in the design and planning of the WAD study. Through focus group interviews with individuals living with persistent WAD, we gained valuable insights into the treatment content, daily assessment items, and practical aspects of conducting the study. This engagement ensured that the research remained relevant and practical for participants and helped refine the V-CBT program to minimize participant burden. Specifically, patients contributed to the selection of daily self-report items and suggested ways to make data collection both meaningful and manageable.

For this study, patient and public involvement was not directly conducted. However, the knowledge and feedback obtained during the WAD study informed and shaped this study. Similarly, the outcome measures, study design, and recruitment strategies were influenced by the practical and meaningful approaches identified in the WAD study. Patients were not directly involved in the choice of methods or dissemination plans for this study.

Data analysis

A combination of structured visual analysis and statistical analyses will be employed to determine treatment efficacy. First, structured visual analysis will be conducted per individual separately to examine within-phase and between-phase patterns in terms of level, trends, variability, immediacy, overlap, and consistency as recommended by Kratochwill and colleagues [44]. Furthermore, individual level effect size measures will be calculated using both non-overlap and mean-based approaches. In terms of non-overlap approaches, based on recommendations outlined by Fingerhut and colleagues [45], Tau-U is used if structured visual analysis shows no significant baseline trend, whereas the baseline corrected Tau-adj [46]. is used if significant baseline trend is present. Additionally, the within-case standardized mean difference is calculated for each participant separately [47, 48].

Change processes will be explored by overlaying the time series data of different measures. This temporal mapping will help in identifying the sequence of change processes initiated by the intervention. The visual analysis findings will be documented systematically. Each participant’s data will be reviewed individually, followed by a synthesis of patterns observed across participants. This approach allows us to capture the nuanced and individualized responses to the V-CBT program, providing a comprehensive understanding of how the treatment influences change over time.

Data collected after each treatment session will be analyzed descriptively and graphically to illustrate trends over the course of the intervention. The HAT will be used qualitatively to identify participants’ perceived helpful events and themes, while quantitative scores from the CEQ and WAI will be summarized and visually inspected for changes across sessions. These data will also be explored in relation to daily and outcome-level measures to identify temporal patterns linking alliance, expectancy, and perceived helpfulness to changes in primary and secondary outcomes.

Questionnaire data collected at pre-treatment, post-treatment, and three-month follow-up will be summarized descriptively and visually to explore change trajectories across time. Given the small sample size (n = 9), formal inferential statistics will not be emphasized. Instead, the focus will be on identifying consistent patterns and clinically meaningful trends that may inform future research hypotheses and guide the design of larger-scale studies. Individual-level change will also be examined using descriptive indicators such as mean differences, graphical representations, and reliable change indices where appropriate.

The qualitative interviews will be analyzed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis in an organic six-phase manner to develop overall patterns and themes in data [49, 50]. This will enable identification of key themes regarding participant experiences, treatment acceptability, and perceived mechanisms of change.

Risk of bias and reporting

In the planning and design of this SCED study, the internal validity subscale of the Risk of Bias in N-of-1 Trials (RoBiNT) scale was used as guidance. The RoBiNT scale is a critical appraisal tool evaluating methodological quality in SCEDs [51]. Table 3 provides full details on the design elements and expected rating under the condition that all decisions are followed as intended. The internal validity is expected to score 6/14 points, which is regarded as fair methodological rigor [25]. In terms of reporting, the study will be reported in accordance with the Single-Case Reporting Guideline In Behavioral Interventions (SCRIBE) [52].

Ethics and dissemination

This study has been reviewed and approved by The Regional Committees on Health Research Ethics for Southern Denmark (ID: S-20240061). The ethical considerations include ensuring the safety and well-being of the participants throughout the study. The intervention will be conducted by experienced clinicians at Specialized Hospital, which has extensive experience in rehabilitation. Participants will be fully informed about the study’s purpose, procedures, and potential risks, and written informed consent will be obtained prior to their inclusion in the study. Participants can continue or proceed with other potential interventions during the trial period. The study adheres to all relevant ethical guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants will have the right to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences to their ongoing care.

Given the potential burden of daily data collection on participants, we will implement measures to mitigate fatigue and ensure data quality, such as regular follow-ups and allowing flexibility in response times. The privacy and confidentiality of participants’ data will be strictly maintained. All data will be pseudonymized and securely stored in accordance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Data will be collected using SurveyXact, which is GDPR-approved via University of Southern Denmark. Due to the high-fidelity nature of the individualized data collected and based on legal counsel, datasets will not be deposited in a repository.

The findings from this study will be disseminated through various channels to maximize impact and reach. Results will be published in peer-reviewed scientific journals, regardless of whether they are positive, negative, or inconclusive. Additionally, we aim to communicate the findings to broader audiences, including patients, their families, healthcare professionals, and the public. This will be achieved through articles in patient and professional magazines, social media, and presentations at national and international conferences.

Discussion

SCI imposes a substantial health burden, characterized by chronic pain, functional limitations, and a host of related complications that significantly impact personal, social, and occupational aspects of life. A values-based cognitive behavioral approach holds promise for individuals with persistent SCI-related pain, potentially enhancing participation in meaningful, value-driven activities despite ongoing challenges. This study employs a SCED to offer a more granular examination of treatment efficacy, change processes, and factors influencing individual responses to intervention. To our knowledge, this is the first effort to investigate the effectiveness and change processes of V-CBT within a SCED framework specific to SCI. However, it builds upon a body of impactful studies that have effectively utilized this design to establish efficacy and clarify change processes in chronic pain contexts [18–23].

This study possesses several strengths and limitations. Key strengths include: (1) an experienced interdisciplinary clinical team of two physiotherapists and a psychologist delivering the intervention, (2) detailed data collection inherent in SCEDs, enabling exploration of temporal relationships and change processes alongside efficacy, (3) engagement of a patient focus group and expert feedback from chronic pain and SCED specialists (see acknowledgments) during the design phase (WAD study), (4) a relatively large sample size for a SCED study, (5) the concurrent design within each group, which offers better control of external confounding factors, (6) integration of SCED with a pre-post design featuring a three-month follow-up, (7) cost-effectiveness of the overall study design, and (8) the potential of offering both online and face-to-face delivery formats, ensuring accessibility for individuals with mobility challenges or those residing in remote areas, while allowing others to opt for in-person participation.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. While the interdisciplinary team is robust, it lacks representation from other relevant professional groups such as occupational therapists, nurses, or physicians. Inclusion of these professionals could provide a more holistic approach, potentially enhancing participants’ ability to engage with their lives and overcome both physical and mental barriers. Additionally, while having a highly experienced team is advantageous, it may also limit the external validity or generalizability of the findings, as similar positive effects may not be as easily achieved in less specialized clinical settings.

Offering both online and face-to-face delivery formats ensures flexibility and accessibility, though each format presents its own challenges. Technical issues such as internet connectivity, familiarity with digital tools, and varying levels of comfort with virtual platforms may hinder full participation. Additionally, some therapeutic interventions may be more effective in person, particularly when addressing nuanced physical cues and relational dynamics that are challenging to replicate online.

From a design perspective, SCEDs can be demanding for participants due to the need for daily data collection, which may lead to participant fatigue, reduced motivation, and potential data gaps or lower data quality. To mitigate this risk, we emphasize this requirement during participant screening and maintain regular follow-up through an assistant; however, this strategy is not foolproof and still places considerable demands on participants. To reduce the burden, participants may submit data one day late if necessary. Another challenge inherent to the design is participant dropout. In this study, a multiple-baseline design relies on participants randomized to different baseline durations (e.g., one, two, or three weeks), with each tier controlling for external confounders. Therefore, a single dropout weakens this control and, by extension, the study’s conclusions. A mitigating factor is the inclusion of nine participants, allowing for up to nine demonstrations of treatment effects, as highlighted by the Risk of Bias in N-of-1 Trials (RoBiNT) scale, which awards maximum points for three or more demonstrations of treatment efficacy [25].

Practical limitations include the planned weekly progression of treatment sessions, which could present scheduling challenges due to other clinician responsibilities, illnesses, holidays, or participant conflicts, potentially delaying data collection. Furthermore, the study’s stringent eligibility criteria, including fluency in Danish, may limit cultural diversity among participants. This lack of cultural diversity could impact the generalizability of the V-CBT intervention’s positive outcomes to broader, more varied populations and cultural settings.
