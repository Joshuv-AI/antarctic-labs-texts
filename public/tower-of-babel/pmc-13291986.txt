Fast and Flexible 3D Molecular Design Framework for Novel Organic Optoelectronic Materials
Authors: Guojiang Zhao, Zheng Cheng, Kele Xu, Tianjiao Wan, Ming Feng, Shuqi Lu, Hongshuai Wang, Zijian Gao, Qi Ou, Liang Hu, Dawei Feng, Zifeng Zhao, Zhifeng Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organic optoelectronic materials (OOMs) are pivotal for advancing technologies such as organic photovoltaics and light-emitting diodes. Traditional methods for discovering new OOMs are inefficient and limited by chemical space exploration. We introduce O 2 -GEN, a novel framework leveraging a 3D pretraining backbone trained on a diverse data set of over ten million molecules, enabling comprehensive exploration of chemical space. O 2 -GEN is effective at generating novel fused-ring systems and conjugated fragment assemblies. It significantly outperforms existing models in these specific tasks in speed and chemical structural validity, particularly for larger molecules. The framework supports both global and local generation modes, allowing for the design of new molecules or modifications of existing molecules. Additionally, O 2 -GEN integrates a property selector fine-tuned with density functional theory data, enabling precise multiproperty screening. Overall, our O 2 -GEN allows the construction of data sets with multiproperty-biased distributions tailored to specific application scenarios, facilitating the discovery of novel optoelectronic materials.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, organic optoelectronic devices have drawn substantial research investments and have achieved remarkable commercial success, such as organic photovoltaics (OPVs) − and organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs). − Organic optoelectronic molecules (OOMs), − serving as the fundamental constituents of organic optoelectronic devices, confer on these devices inherent attributes, including high efficiency, flexibility, lightweight, solution processability, and low cost. Thus, the exploration of novel OOM structures constitutes one of the primary driving forces propelling the progress within the organic optoelectronic device field.

OOMs typically consist of conjugated fused-ring systems or conjugated fragment assemblies. This structural arrangement forms the basis for their optoelectronic activities. Evidently, the novelty of OOMs emerges from the diversity of the conjugated fused-rings and the variety of connection modalities between the conjugated fragments. Both factors exert an impact on the electronic structure of isolated molecules and packing modes in the condensed state, further dictating the optoelectronic properties of OOMs and the performance of the associated devices. −

The discovery of new OOMs has traditionally relied on expert-driven, trial-and-error methods, which, despite their success, present challenges in terms of efficiency and systematic exploration of vast chemical space. , Recent advancements in deep learning , have transformed such processes into in silico rational design, accelerating the discovery of pharmaceutical compounds, − crystals, , energy materials and small molecules. Building upon these advances, deep generative models have emerged as a powerful tool for molecular design. However, existing deep generative models encounter significant difficulties when dealing with OOMs. Variational Autoencoders (VAE) − and Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN), − which utilize sequential or graph-based molecular representations, face challenges in generating accurate three-dimensional (3D) molecular conformations, which are important for the optoelectronic properties of OOMs. Recent endeavors integrating 3D-equivariant networks with Diffusion , and Flow − have demonstrated promising outcomes in small molecule generation, yet may face difficulties in scaling to larger fused-ring or conjugated fragment assemblies molecules. When applied to OOMs, they yield moderate validity results. A significant advancement was made by Tomer Weiss et al., who proposed a diffusion model using the graph of rings (GOR) representation, which significantly improves the generation efficiency and validity for fused-ring molecules. However, this approach primarily addresses the cata-condensed systems, which are just a subset of the fused-ring systems, and does not fully explore the chemical space associated with fragment assemblies, limiting the novelty of the generated molecules.

To address the above limitations and facilitate a more comprehensive design of OOMs, we propose O 2 -GEN, the first generalizable 3D generative framework specifically designed to generate chemically valid, structurally diverse OOMs for both global and local design tasks. O 2 -GEN combines an innovative virtual particle initialization strategy with a powerful 3D pretraining backbone, addressing the key limitations of existing generative models. This innovation enhances the efficiency and effectiveness of generating fused-ring and fragment-assembly molecules. In validation tasks on two fused-ring data sets, O 2 -GEN rapidly generated new structures with exceptional validity and novelty. Larger molecules composed of conjugated fragment assemblies are essential for designing complex optoelectronic materials. Due to the lack of relevant data sets, we constructed AOMAD to address this gap. On this data set, O 2 -GEN generates complete molecules approximately 32 times faster than diffusion models and about 25 times faster than flow models, with a notable improvement in the integrality of fragment assemblies.

Furthermore, O 2 -GEN incorporates an efficient and accurate property selector by fine-tuning the pretrained 3D molecular structure backbone with a limited amount of Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculation data. The inclusion of DFT data is crucial as it provides a quantum-mechanical, first-principles basis for molecular property prediction, enabling the selector to accurately screen generated molecules based on multiple downstream properties with near DFT-level precision, ensuring alignment with experimentally relevant properties. Through these means, the overarching objective of generating new-structured molecules with multiobjective characteristics can be realized. In particular, our framework has great flexibility, allowing designated local region modification of a known structure to empower researchers for fine-tuning molecular properties. Overall, the introduction of O 2 -GEN not only fills the gap in existing generative models for OOMs design but also opens new pathways for the efficient design of organic optoelectronic materials.

Results

Overview of O 2 -GEN

O 2 -GEN (Figure ) encompasses a molecule generator specifically engineered for OOMs design, one (or multiple, depending on specific requirements) property selector, and a 3D pretrained backbone network shared by both components. By combining a generator with property-based selectors, O 2 -GEN can efficiently produce a vast number of candidate molecules and select those that meet predefined property criteria. This enables the generation of molecules that simultaneously satisfy multiple tailored property requirements.

The workflow ofO 2-GEN. The 3D pretraining backbones extract the structural information on OOMs from the pretraining data. The Generator create new molecules guided by the template molecule shapes and the prior structural information from the 3D pretraining backbones. The Selector is obtained by fine-tuning the 3D pretraining backbones with DFT property data.

3D Pretraining

The pretraining data (Figure a) consist of the open-source optoelectronic molecular data sets with pure condensed fused-ring molecules (COMPAS-1X, PAS) and aromatic ring assemblies (CMR, Harvard Organic Photovoltaic Database, Ir complexes, TADF). The detailed data set names and their information are provided in Supporting Table 1. In addition, considering that many applications of OOMs require larger molecular weights, we developed a data set named AOMAD (Aromatic Optoelectronic Molecular Assembly Database) through expert-guided fragment assembly (see Section ) to enhance the representativeness of molecular distributions in pretraining data sets. The pretraining data set encompasses a total of over ten million molecules, including both conjugated molecular assemblies and fused ring systems, carefully selected to ensure the utmost diversity of OOMs.

We opted for the self-supervised pretraining representation learning approach of 3D molecular structures (Figure b) to better characterize the chemical space and the structural relationships of OOMs. This allows the 3D pretraining backbone to simultaneously learn the diversity of both the fused-ring structures and their connection modes.

Following insights from previous works, − we analyzed our 3D pretraining backbone’s learned representations using the t-SNE method. The distribution of randomly selected 80,000 molecules from the validation set is colored by the molecular weights. As illustrated in Figure c, the map displays the wide chemical space captured by this backbone. The highlighted molecules show that those with closer distances on the t-SNE map exhibit higher similarities in molecular shapes and functional groups. This confirms the backbone’s ability to identify complex molecular structures, rather than merely clustering them based on molecular weight. It is crucial for OOMs generation and property prediction.

Generator Details and Performances

The generator is inspired by the VD-GEN method. Virtual Particles (VPs) are uniformly initialized within a predefined space and iteratively moved to regions of high-probability atomic distribution (Figure a). This movement is guided by the pretrained 3D backbone. Unlike traditional molecular generation models, VPs do not directly represent specific atoms. Instead, VPs represent abstract representations of atoms or atomic clusters in a molecule, exploring a broader chemical space and progressively approaching the target atomic distribution.

Comparison of inference times for generating 10,000 molecules by baseline model(s) and O 2 -GEN on (c) the COMPAS-1X and PAS data sets or (e) AOMAD data sets.

In contrast to VD-GEN, which focuses on structure-based drug design, O 2 -GEN innovatively applies VPs to the design of OOMs and introduces a fine-grained initialization strategy. By considering the near-planar structures of optoelectronic molecules, this strategy enables more precise initialization of the VPs’ distribution. Compared to diffusion models and flow models, O 2 -GEN simplifies the complex exploration space through this fine-grained initialization, making it more efficient in handling the differences in the multiring conjugated structures of OOMs. This approach resolves the difficulties these models face in capturing the relationships between molecular atoms and rings, preventing the collapse of ring structures.

Additionally, depending on various application scenarios, the generator can utilize specific molecular structures as initial templates to define the sampling space. By leveraging these domain-relevant structural priors, the virtual particles converge more efficiently during the generative process, thereby improving the speed, chemical validity, and functional relevance of the generated OOMs. Such features also enable us to modify and regenerate any part of a specific molecule, demonstrating the high flexibility of the generator. Next, we will evaluate the performance across different data sets. To ensure a fair comparison among different models, we fine-tuned the generator on the corresponding data sets prior to evaluation.

Generation Task on Fused-Ring Molecule Databases

The section demonstrates the framework’s performance in generating novel and feasible fused-ring molecules. The framework was first tested on data sets like COMPAS-1X and PAS, which contain fused-ring molecules with medium size. COMPAS-1X contains cata-condensed polybenzenoid hydrocarbons (cc-PBHs) with a number of atoms ranging from 12 to 72, while PAS displays a range from 10 to 66 in heterocyclic structures. The atomic length distributions are available in Supporting Figure 1. We benchmarked against GaUDI due to its state-of-the-art performance in generating polycyclic molecules using EDM and Graph of Rings (GOR) representations. To validate our framework, we generated 10,000 molecules per data set using the OOMs generator, assessing them based on the following metrics: ,, Validity (the proportion of generated molecules that pass RDKit’s SanitizeMol checks, which verify valence, aromaticity, atom and bond properties, implicit hydrogens, and charges), Uniqueness (percentage of unique valid molecules), Novelty (percentage of valid molecules not in the training set), and V&U&N (the fraction of valid, unique, and novel molecules that are not present in the training set).

As shown in Table , both O 2 -GEN and GaUDI have V&U&N of over 97% on the PAS data set. However, on the COMPAS-1X data set, GaUDI’s unique and novelty performance drops significantly, while O 2 -GEN maintains a V&U&N close to 100%. To account for this difference, we characterized the two data sets using the molecular fingerprint method. The average fingerprint similarity of the COMPAS-1X and PAS data sets (Figure b and Supporting Figure 2) is 0.99 and 0.67 among their original molecules, respectively. This result indicates that the molecular structural diversity of the COMPAS-1X data set is extremely limited, while the PAS data set covers a much larger chemical space.

We further analyze and demonstrate the differences between the molecules generated by O 2 -GEN and GaUDI. On the COMPAS-1X data set (Supporting Figure 3), the atomic length distribution of molecules generated by GaUDI closely resembles that of the original data, almost entirely adhering to the pattern of cc-PBHs molecules with atomic length intervals of 6n. In contrast, the distribution of molecules generated by O 2 -GEN is much smoother, indicating the emergence of a significant number of molecules outside the distribution of the original data set. Specifically, the novel molecules (Supporting Figure 3e) generated by O 2 -GEN include various substituents, nonhexagonal ring structures, and peri-condensed (where one atom is shared by three rings) fused-rings, which explains the source of O 2 -GEN’s remarkable novelty. As a result (Figure b), generated molecules show an average molecular fingerprint similarity of 0.75 and a significantly lower average fingerprint similarity of 0.05 between the generated molecules and those in the original data set. A similar trend of change in molecular fingerprint similarity can also be observed on the PAS data set (Supporting Figure 2). Detailed analysis of generated molecules on PAS is depicted in Supporting Figure 4.

The above analysis implies that O 2 -GEN can explore a much larger chemical space via a 3D pretraining backbone and VPs method, while GaUDI is strongly restricted by the diversity of the training data. Additionally, due to the use of the Graph of Rings (GOR) approachs,the baseline GaUDI model produced 107 cationic molecules, whereas O 2 -GEN produced zero when generating 10,000 molecules on the COMPAS-1X data set (which contains no charged species), indicating its ability to produce molecules with more predictable chemical validity and structural consistency.

Furthermore, the O 2 -GEN framework significantly accelerates the speed of molecular generation. As shown in Figure .c, when generating 10,000 molecules, compared with GaUDI, the molecular generation speed of O 2 -GEN increases by 130% and 344% respectively on the COMPAS-1X and PAS data sets.

Nevertheless, the task of learning the distribution of fused-rings and the fragment connection modalities of molecules in FORMED and AOMAD poses a great challenge to the baseline models. The molecules generated by these methods frequently exhibit a large number of ring breakages, overlaps, and other situations that violate the basic chemical rules, as shown in Supporting Figure 5, resulting in low validity. In contrast, O 2 -GEN still maintains a validity of nearly 97% on both data sets.

Besides, baseline models often produce numerous fragments that pass the validity check but are disconnected from each other. To more clearly evaluate the models’ ability to generate large OOMs with integral structures, we define two new metrics (details refer to the Section ): the connected ratio, which is the proportion of valid molecules where all fragments are connected to each other; and the Avg. Rings/Mol, which is the average number of rings in each valid molecule. On the FORMED data set, 86.11% of the molecules generated by O 2 -GEN are intact, while this ratio for other baseline models ranges from 30.43% to 51.90%. On the AOMAD data sets, due to the increase in molecular size and the number of rings, the connection ratio of molecules generated by the three baseline models is close to 0, while O 2 -GEN still remains above 40%. Overall, the introduction of pretraining 3D information and the VPs method enables our generator to construct connected fragments assembled OOMs more effectively. The detailed comparison for the FORMED and AOMAD data sets, along with the corresponding generated data sets, is shown in Supporting Figures 6 and 7.

To provide additional context, we further compared O 2 -GEN against a broader set of recent baselines on AOMAD, covering flow-based methods (PropMolFlow: Validity 80.17%, Connected Ratio 4.74%; Flowmol: Validity 50.06%, Connected Ratio 16.09%), a fragment assembly based method (MolMiner: Validity 90.54%, Connected Ratio 42.54%, Inference Time 33.04 h), and a conjugation-aware method (STGG-AL: Validity 88.31%, Connected Ratio 35.10%). While MolMiner achieves a Connected Ratio close to O 2 -GEN, it requires ∼25× longer inference time and generates significantly fewer rings per molecule (8.05 vs 15.62). O 2 -GEN remains the only model that achieves top performance simultaneously across validity, structural integrity, ring complexity, and generation speed. Furthermore, we used the molecular fingerprint method to depict the specific characteristics of the novelty of the molecules generated by O 2 -GEN. As shown in Figure d, the average fingerprint similarity between the most similar molecules in the original data set is 0.93, while the similarity between the molecules we generated and the most similar molecule pairs in the original data set is only 0.17. This indicates that our framework has the ability to generate novel large-scale OOMs in a broader chemical space.

While these baseline models exhibit efficient training and inference capabilities for small molecules, they face difficulties in converging within a limited time when dealing with complex, large fragment-assembled OOMs. Even with training durations significantly longer than those of O 2 -GEN, their progress remains far slower. This discrepancy extends to the inference stage: for generating 10,000 molecules, EDM, GeoLDM, and GeoBFN require inference times of up to 42.40, 41.57, and 32.17 h, respectively, whereas O 2 -GEN takes only 1.3 h. Selector Details and Performances

Accurate and fast property screening is crucial for identifying OOMs that simultaneously meet multiple property targets. Our selector is based on the reported molecular pretraining framework, Uni-Mol. It shares the same 3D pretrained backbone with the generator (Figure a). By fine-tuning with specific DFT data, it can meet the prediction requirements for specific properties.

On the basis of the demand for predicting the properties of practical OOMs, we selected key optical and redox properties such as the Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital Energy (HOMO), Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital Energy (LUMO), Ionization Potential Energy (IP), Electron Affinity Energy (EA), and Energy of Absorption Maximum (Abs Max) as the prediction targets. Through finetuning with the thousands of DFT-level data points generated with O 2 -GEN, the correlation coefficient R 2 of all five properties between the predicted values of our selector and the DFT results all exceeded 0.89, with the mean absolute error (MAE) ranging from 0.04 to 0.12 eV (Supporting Figure 8). For detailed calculation procedures, refer to the Section . All of these results demonstrate that our selectors are capable of predicting related properties with DFT accuracy.

To validate the selector’s reliability when the generator explores regions outside the training distributiona central concern when selecting “best” candidates from large generated poolswe conducted external validation on an independent literature data set without fine-tuning. Our HOMO/LUMO selector achieved a GAP MAE of 0.28 eV, outperforming literature baselines (KRR: 0.56 eV, SVM: 0.60 eV, PR: 0.42 eV) that were specifically fine-tuned on that data set. Uncertainty quantification reveals that 95% of predictions fall within ± 0.81 eV, and reliability scoring shows 84.84% of samples achieve high reliability (>80 points). We identified high-error samples (10.03% with errors >0.65 eV) primarily in highly fused scaffolds beyond typical coverage. These results collectively define the applicability domain of our selector: prediction confidence is well-established for molecules within the validated chemical space, but users should exercise caution when interpreting screening results for structures with highly fused scaffolds or other features substantially outside this domain, where confidence may decrease. Full details on error distributions, confidence intervals, and domain applicability are provided in Supporting Information (Selector Validation Under Distribution Shift).

Case Study

The Nonfullerene Acceptor Generation of OPV

Global Generation

To further validate the ability of O 2 -GEN to create novel OOMs in practical scenarios, we selected the fused-ring core of the star nonfullerene acceptor Y6 , in the OPV field as the template molecule and generated 600 brand-new molecules. Figure a shows representative molecules in the generated data set. Details of the statistical distribution of the structural data are presented in Supporting Figure 9.

Global and local modes of O 2 -GEN to generate OOMs.

Compared with the original template molecule, the generated molecules successfully retained a similar molecular size and contour, avoiding excessive divergence in chemical space. At the same time, the generation model created a large number of novel and reasonable structures, such as new heteroatoms in rings, peri-condensed fused-ring structures (i.e., an atom is shared by three rings simultaneously), and new fragment connection patterns.

Supporitng Figure 10 depicts the IP, EA, and Abs Max distributions for these molecules based on the selector prediction. These metrics were chosen because they can be calculated using quantum chemistry methods and tested experimentally, thus holding clear physical significance. The distribution peaks of IP and EA shifted by more than 1 eV toward higher and lower energy, respectively, while the corresponding Abs Max expanded by over 2 eV, indicating that the generated molecules possess sufficient diversity and novelty. Considering the tendency of heteroatom insertion and the transformation of large fused-rings into single-bond-connected aromatic fragments, the average conjugation of the generated molecules is somewhat weakened relative to that of the input molecule. This structural change aligns well with the property prediction results. We note that for generated candidates with structural features substantially beyond the selector’s training distributionparticularly highly fused polycyclic systemsprediction confidence may be reduced, and these candidates should be prioritized for further expert evaluation and, ultimately, experimental verification.

Local Generation

From the perspective of experimental field experts, global generation could produce a large number of molecules with reasonable chemical structures; however, they are far from the expected properties. These molecules cannot directly assist researchers in quickly focusing on specific needs. Considering that the classic paradigm of scientific research is to make improvements based on successful cases, O 2 -GEN’s local generation mode supports an arbitrary part of the template molecule as a variable region for generation. Compared with the global generation mode, local generation further restricts the chemical space, thus improving the functionality of the generated structures. On the other hand, only synthesizable molecules have application value. The global generation mode of the O 2 -GEN model does not consider the feasibility of synthetic methodologies or routes. However, molecules generated through local generation can be derived from successfully synthesized molecules or building blocks, and ideally, they also have better synthesizability.

We take the terminal group of Y6 as an example to demonstrate the partial generation ability of O 2 -GEN and examine the obtained 521 molecules. The newly generated terminal groups exhibited rich and novel structures, as shown in Figure b1. Compared with the template molecule, the number and type of rings, the types of substituents, and the heteroatoms also showed greater richness. Furthermore, we selected 6 atoms from half of the ring (Figure b2) to further test the local generation mode for fused-ring fragments. Under this condition, the chemical space explored was further compressed. Nevertheless, O 2 -GEN still presented numerous novel and valuable structures among 150 generated samples. As illustrated in Supporting Figure 10, the properties of the generated molecules are clustering around those of the input molecule, demonstrating that the local generation mode enables fine-tuning of specific properties through local structural adjustments.

Considering the requirement for wet experiments, nonfullerene acceptors should have precise energy level matching with the donors and the adjacent charge-extraction layer; thus, the ideal parameters depend on the researcher’s objectives. The diverse structures from both modes ensure the novelty of the molecules and the variety of downstream properties, creating sufficient scope for multitarget property screening.

However, for highly complex fused-ring systems such as Y6, the widely used synthesizability metrics (e.g., RAScore, SAscore, SCScore, SYBA) often reach their limits of application (detailed in the Supporting Information under the section Synthesizability Evaluation of Generated Molecules), making it difficult to clearly distinguish between achievable complexity and unrealistic complexity. This limitation reflects a fundamental challenge in the field: existing evaluation tools are primarily calibrated based on smaller-scale structures reported in the literature, while data for highly novel, heavily fused systems that define the OOM design frontier is extremely scarce, rendering existing evaluation tools often ineffective for such systems.

The Fluorescent Molecule Generation

To rigorously validate the chemical reliability of O 2 -GEN without being confounded by metric deficiencies, we incorporated an additional case study using fragment-assembled fluorescent molecules. These fluorescent molecules fall within the reliable calibration range of above-mentioned synthesizability metrics while still representing meaningful design challenges, enabling us to test whether O 2 -GEN’s selection logic is truly grounded in chemical reality or merely optimizing surrogate scoring metrics.

In this case study (Figure ), we used the typical D-π-A structure fluorescent molecule B5 as a structural template, keeping its donor moiety intact while employing O 2 -GEN’s local generation mode to diversify the acceptor and π-bridge portions. We selected B5 for two reasons: first, it represents a medium complexity regionmore challenging than barely fused drug-like molecule generation but not subject to metric failure like highly fused OOMs; second, its design logic (balancing HOMO–LUMO gap, stability, and emission properties) faithfully reflects the multiobjective trade-offs inherent in OOM design, making it a relevant surrogate system for testing our framework’s decision-making capabilities.

(a1) Example of excessive fusion; (a3) Example of conjugation break. (b) 105 candidate molecules evaluated by quantum chemical methods for emission energy (Transition Energy, eV) and transition dipole moment (TDM, Debye). (c) Obtaining potential candidate molecules with expected good luminescence based on preset emission energy and TDM criteria, including one previously reported molecule.

Starting from an initial set of 962 unique generated molecules, we implemented a three-stage screening pipeline to ensure structural integrity and functional viability (Figure .-a). The first stage was a functionality screen (restricting the HOMO–LUMO gap to 2.5–4.0 eV to avoid excessive fusion (Figure a1) or conjugation breaks (Figure a3)); the second stage was a photostability screen (requiring IP superior to the B5 template to mitigate photo-oxidation risks); the third stage was a synthesizability screen (requiring performance on all four metricsRAscore, SAscore, SCScore, SYBAto match or exceed the template molecule). This rigorous screening pipeline narrowed the candidate pool to 105 high-quality molecules (screening ratio 10.9%) that are not only theoretically optimized but also chemically plausible.

To further substantiate the practical potential of these candidates, we performed TD-DFT calculations (ORCA 6.0.1, PBE0/def2-SVP, CPCM solvent model simulating toluene environment) on the 105 filtered structures to optimize S 1 state geometries and evaluate vertical emission energies and transition dipole moments (TDM) (Figure b). By targeting a broad emission spectrum range (430–1000 nm, corresponding to transition energies of 1.24–2.88 eV and covering the common emission bands from blue to near-infrared) and high transition rates (requiring TDM > 3 D for λ > 500 nm, and TDM > 7 D for λ < 500 nm to ensure sufficient emission rates, 500 nm wavelength corresponding to transition energy of 2.48 eV), we identified 29 high-potential candidates with expected high luminescence.

A subsequent structural search on the Reaxys (Elsevier) platform revealed that among these 29 molecules (Figure c), 28 are structurally novel and unreported compounds, while one had been previously disclosed in patents (JP2000297068A, JP4213832B2). The rediscovery of a patented molecule in a generation task provides compelling evidence for the chemical intuition learned by O 2 -GENthis molecule was identified through our multistage screening pipeline (functionality + stability + synthesizability) without any prior knowledge of its existence. This finding suggests that our selection criteria show meaningful alignment with experimental synthetic accessibility and functional requirements. While direct experimental synthesis is needed for definitive validation, these results indicate that our framework can navigate chemical space toward practically plausible designs.

Discussion

The performance of organic optoelectronic devices relies on OOMs. Although deep learning has sped up material discovery, current deep generative models face difficulties with fused-ring molecules and conjugated fragment assemblies. Our proposed framework, O 2 -GEN, provides a solution to address these challenges and offers a powerful tool for the needs of experimental researchers.

While O 2 -GEN excels in generating novel structures, the synthesizability of these molecules remains a critical consideration for practical applications. Our systematic evaluation (detailed in the Supporting Information) using four established metrics (RAScore, SAscore, SCScore, and SYBA) reveals a fundamental boundary in current cheminformatics: these tools, primarily calibrated on drug-like chemical space, often reach their reliability limits when confronting the highly fused, complex scaffolds that define the frontier of OOM design. While the most complex OOMs (e.g., Y6 core) still exceed the reliable range of current synthesizability assessment tools, this fluorescent molecule case study establishes an important fact: O 2 -GEN’s generation-selection workflow can produce chemically realistic candidates with improved experimental plausibility, though direct experimental synthesis and validation remain important future steps for confirming actionability.

Accordingly, for the most structurally complex OOMs, experimental expert judgment remains indispensable, as computational synthesizability metrics alone cannot guarantee synthetic feasibility. For OOM design, we recommend a dual-layer strategy: using O 2 -GEN’s local generation mode to decrease structural deviations from known synthesizable scaffolds, thereby enhancing synthetic feasibility; and applying domain expert chemical judgment to the final candidate list, using our predicted properties and synthesizability scores as decision-support tools rather than absolute filtering criteria.

The O 2 -GEN framework offers several key advantages over existing methods. The incorporation of a 3D pretrained backbone allows the model to capture the complex structural relationships and electronic properties of OOMs, leading to novel generation and accurate predictions. The VPs method provides a fast and maneuverable way to generate molecules, endows the flexibility of O 2 -GEN in both global and local generation modes, and provides researchers with a powerful tool for fine-tuning molecular properties. This is particularly valuable in the context of organic optoelectronics, where small structural modifications can have a profound impact on device performance. The ability to generate molecules with specific properties, such as precise energy levels or absorption maxima, opens up new possibilities for the in silico design of tailored materials for specific applications.

Besides, the potential applications of O2 -GEN may extend beyond organic optoelectronics. The framework’s core design principles3D pretraining on domain-specific molecular distributions combined with virtual particle-based generationare in principle transferable to other molecular design domains, such as drug discovery, catalysis, and energy storage, though dedicated domain-specific training data and independent validation would be prerequisites for reliable application in each case. By leveraging the principles of rational design and high-throughput screening, O 2 -GEN represents a promising computational platform that could contribute to the discovery of novel materials across a wide range of disciplines, subject to the domain adaptation steps outlined above.

Methods

Data

Details of AOMAD (Aromatic Optoelectronic Molecular Assembly Database):

We utilized optoelectronic molecular fragments commonly cited in the literature to construct a candidate molecular database based on predefined rules. Specifically, conjugated molecules were assembled from multiple building blocks, including 11 Core units (C), 40 Spacer units (S), 42 Terminal units (T), and 4 π-fragments (π). The binding sites of each fragment are marked with “X” in Supporting Figure 11, and we algorithmically interconnected these binding sites across different fragments to generate novel molecules.

As a supplement to existing open-source data sets, we designed the TS′πSCSπS′T molecular architecture to ensure broad molecular weight diversity in the AOMAD library, resulting in 2,956,800 unique structures. Following molecular validity screening via RDKit 2024.3.1 and manual verification, coupled with structural optimization using the MMFF94 force field, we retained 1,677,622 distinct configurations. This approach expands the chemical space of pretrained molecular structural data, enhances the diversity and comprehensiveness of the optoelectronic material library, and ensures superior performance for O 2 -GEN.

Metrics Definitions

Validity: the proportion of generated molecules that pass the standard RDKit SanitizeMol check. SanitizeMol performs valence checking, aromaticity determination and Kekulization, atom and bond property verification, implicit hydrogen adjustment, and charge computation to ensure the chemical correctness of the molecule. Molecules that pass all these checks are considered valid.

Connected-Ratio: the proportion of generated molecules in which all fragments are connected to form a single cohesive molecule. Specifically, we use RDKit’s GetMolFrags function to fragment the generated molecules. If the molecule contains only one fragment, it is considered “connected.” If multiple fragments are present, it is deemed “disconnected.” This approach helps mitigate potential biases arising from molecular fragmentation or isolated atoms.

Avg. Rings/Mol: the average number of rings present in all valid molecules. Ring detection is carried out using RDKit’s SSSR (Smallest Set of Smallest Rings) algorithm. For edge cases, such as molecules without rings, we assign a value of 0, which is naturally reflected in the final average.

RDKit 2024.3.1 was used for all calculations.

3D Pretraining Backbone

To systematically investigate the structural properties of OOMs and efficiently explore their extensive chemical space, we have utilized Uni-Mol for 3D pretraining. Uni-Mol is a universal 3D framework for molecular representation learning that effectively learns and outputs 3D positional information through pretraining with a large volume of unlabeled data. The model takes atom types and atomic coordinates as inputs, where atomic representations are initialized via an embedding layer. Pairwise representations are derived from invariant spatial positional encoding based on pairwise Euclidean distances, ensuring global rotational and translational invariance (SE(3) -invariance). Within the model, these representations interact through the self-attention mechanism of the Transformer, allowing for the capture of both local and global molecular features.

During pretraining, Uni-Mol focuses on two core tasks: 3D position reconstruction and masked atom prediction, both aimed at capturing the deep structural characteristics of OOMs. In the 3D position reconstruction task, the model learns molecular geometry by correcting perturbed atomic coordinates. Specifically, it recovers the original unperturbed 3D coordinates from randomly distorted inputs. To encode 3D positional information, Uni-Mol constructs spatially invariant position encodings based on the Euclidean distances between atomic pairs. These encodings facilitate the interaction between atomic and pairwise representations within the multihead self-attention mechanism. Following the formulation in Uni-Mol, for any given atom pair (i, j), the update at layer (l + 1) is defined as 1 qijl+1=qijl+∑h=1HQil,h(Kjl,h)Td Here, q ij l denotes the pairwise representation at layer l, while Q i l,h and K j l,h represent the query and key embeddings of atoms i and j in the multihead attention mechanism, respectively. The term d corresponds to the hidden representation dimension, and H denotes the number of attention heads.

In the pair-to-atom communication process, the model incorporates pairwise representations as bias terms in the self-attention mechanism, further enhancing its ability to model 3D structural information. The computation is formulated as 2 Attention(Qil,h,Kjl,h,Vjl,h)=softmax(Qil,h(Kjl,h)Td+qijl−1,h)Vjl,h where V j l,h represents the value embedding, and q ij l–1,h denotes the pairwise representation from the previous layer.

To further refine molecular geometry, Uni-Mol learns to recover atomic positions from perturbed coordinates. The objective of this task is to compensate for relative atomic displacements, thereby predicting accurate 3D coordinates, formulated as 3 x̂i=xi+∑j=1n(xi−xj)ReLU((qijL−qij0)U)W

Here, x i and x̂ i denote the original and predicted coordinates, respectively. The pairwise representations q ij 0 and q ij L correspond to the initial and final states, while U and W are trainable projection matrices. In the masked atom prediction task, the model needs to identify randomly masked atomic types within OOMs. This process helps the model learn both local atomic environments and global molecular interactions. Through these pretraining tasks, Uni-Mol serves as the 3D pretraining backbone of O 2 -GEN, effectively encoding key structural information for downstream tasks such as molecular generation and property screening.

O 2 -GEN’s Generator

To perform OOMs generation, two 3D pretraining backbones are utilized, providing efficient and accurate atomic-level representations by leveraging extensive 3D structural information. This enhances the structural validity and convergence speed of generated molecules. The generator in O 2 -GEN is inspired by the VD-GEN, which initializes VPs uniformly within a predefined space. Under the guidance of the 3D pretraining backbone, these VPs are iteratively moved toward regions with high-probability atomic distributions. During generation, the initialization of VPs can be tailored to specific applications by incorporating predefined molecular structures as templates, thereby defining the sampling space.

First, given the prediction space S, n VPs are randomly and uniformly distributed in the space (where n represents the number of atoms in the reference molecule). Let V 0 denote the initialized VPs. Given V 0, their distribution is updated by moving them in 3D space to approximate the real atomic distribution. The movement of VPs follows an iterative process, where in each iteration, the model takes the positions and types of VPs from the previous step as input and predicts their updated positions and types. This process, referred to as Particle Movement (PM), can be formulated as 4 Vr+1=fpm(Vr,S;θpm),⁣Vr={(xir,yir)}i=1n where V r represents the set of n virtual particles at iteration r, and f pm is an SE(3) -equivariant model that takes 3D coordinates as input, corresponding to our 3D pretraining backbone. The term θ pm denotes the learnable parameters. The variables xir∈Rt and yir∈R3 represent the atomic type and coordinates, respectively, where t is the number of atomic types. The number of iterations in the particle movement stage is denoted as R 1. Since V R 1 generated in the PM stage is an approximation of atomic distributions rather than molecular distribution, a Molecule Extraction (ME) step is required to refine VPs into meaningful molecular representations. It employs the pretraining backbone h me to filter and aggregate n virtual particles into m atoms, formulated as 5 W0=hme(VR1,S;θme)={(x̂i0,ŷi0)}i=1m where θ me represents learnable parameters, and W 0 denotes the extracted molecular structure composed of m atoms.

Since some VPs may deviate significantly from their target positions, those exceeding a predefined threshold z are filtered out. Next, the model predicts the merging probability between each pair of VPs and constructs a probability matrix to cluster VPs accordingly. Once the initial molecular structure W 0 is obtained, further optimization of its 3D coordinates is required to refine the molecular geometry. Similar to Particle Movement, this step, referred to as Molecule Refinement (MR), follows an iterative process in which atomic positions are continuously adjusted using a different set of model parameters. The refinement process is formulated as 6 Wr+1=fmr(Wr,S;θmr),⁣Wr={(x̂ir,ŷir)}i=1m where f mr is an SE(3) -equivariant model with learnable parameters θ mr, corresponding to our second 3D pretraining backbone. The term W r represents the atomic set at iteration r, and R 2 denotes the total number of refinement iterations, ultimately yielding the final generated molecule.

VPs Initialization

Given the near-planar nature of most OOMs, our approach begins with an operation that rotates the molecular coordinates closer to the x–y plane, utilizing an equivariant rotation matrix transformation. This process aims to improve the training process by aligning with OOMs’ intrinsic geometrical properties and facilitating improved comprehension of atomic distributions. We utilize a method based on rotation matrices and Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) for rotating the three-dimensional coordinates of a given molecule to be as near parallel to the x–y plane as possible:

First, we calculate the centroid C⃗ of the molecular coordinates to identify the geometric center of the molecule. The centroid is derived from the arithmetic mean of all coordinate points in the matrix Y∈Rn×3 11 C⃗=1N∑i=1Nyi where N is the number of atoms. This step is essential for relocating the coordinate points to a coordinate system centered at the centroid.

To achieve the goal of aligning the molecule as closely as possible with the x–y plane, we identify a normal vector N⃗ that indicates the molecule’s present orientation. This vector is derived by performing SVD on the coordinates once they have been adjusted by subtracting the centroid. Within the matrix V, which contains the right singular vectors from this decomposition, the vector N⃗ is identified as the column corresponding to the smallest singular value. This column, usually the final one in V, serves to represent N⃗, defining the molecular orientation in relation to the x–y plane.

Given N⃗ and its relation to the z-axis, we employ Rodrigues’ rotation formula to devise a rotation matrix R that orients N⃗ with the z-axis. This formula is represented as 12 R=I+K+K21−cs2 Here, I denotes the identity matrix, and K is the skew-symmetric matrix based on the cross product v⃗ = N⃗ × z⃗, where z⃗ = [0,0,1]. The dot product c = N⃗ · z⃗ measures the cosine of the angle between N⃗ and z⃗, and s = ∥v⃗∥ is the magnitude of v⃗, indicating the sine of the angle between N⃗ and z⃗. The skew-symmetric matrix K is constructed from v⃗’s components v x , v y , v z as follows 13 K=[0−vzvyvz0−vx−vyvx0]

Then, by multiplying the matrix Y with the rotation matrix, we obtain the rotated coordinates Y′, thereby bringing the molecular representation closer to the x–y plane. This is mathematically represented as 14 Y′=Y·R

Next, we construct the initial VPs space for molecules oriented to closely align with the x–y plane. The number of virtual particles (VPs) is determined by the hyperparameter k vp, with the formula 15 n=N·kvp

where N is the number of atoms, and k vp is the hyperparameter controlling the number of virtual particles. This means that the number of virtual particles can be flexibly adjusted according to the complexity of the molecule, allowing adaptation to molecules of different sizes. They are not individual atoms, but abstract representations of molecular atomic distributions, which can correspond to a single atom, a cluster of atoms, or even molecular fragments. Virtual particles are dynamically adjusted during the generation process, with their size and structure changing to adapt to various molecular generation tasks. We present two efficient sampling strategies. For the coarse-grained sampling strategy, this approach involves sampling VPs within a broadly predefined space, without any prior molecular structure information. During training, the number of VPs is dynamically adjusted according to the total number of atoms in the molecule, falling within specified lower and upper ratio bounds. This adjustment allows the VPs to adapt to the size of the molecule. A cubic space is constructed using atomic coordinates, within which VPs are uniformly generated. The VPs are then continuously shifted by the generator to model the actual atomic distribution. Furthermore, to reduce resource consumption, the initial space for VPs can be defined based on the smallest volume constructed by projecting the molecular atomic coordinates on the x, y, and z axes (this can be implemented via PCA). During inference, this predefined space distribution from the training set is used to initialize space for generation.

For the fine-grained sampling strategy, we employ prior spatial structural information for effective generation and structural refinement. Specifically, we construct resource-optimization spatial grids based on the spatial structure information on template molecules and initialize VPs within these grids around each atom to better simulate the complex atomic distribution of such OOMs. This approach enables faster iterative generation of the desired structural OOMs.

In our further research, we found that the template sampling molecule method already provides a sufficient novelty of molecule. The fine-grained sampling strategy is adopted as the default sampling method. The local generation mode is based on a fine-grained sampling strategy applied to specified regions of the template molecule.

We utilize a specialized 3D pretraining backbone tailored for OOMs to build a regression model. This model learns generated molecular conformations and their properties through DFT calculations. Due to the high cost of DFT calculations, only a subset of samples undergo DFT calculations to obtain their properties. Uni-Mol’s generalization capabilities enable broader applicability with minimal data. Finetuning Data Production

All Density Functional Theory (DFT) and Time-Dependent Density Functional Theory (TD-DFT) calculations are performed using Gaussian 16. The functional employed is B3LYP, and the basis set chosen is def2-SVP. Dispersion corrections are considered using the D3BJ method during the calculations.

To obtain the IP and EA of the molecule, we first optimize its molecular structure and calculate the molecular energy E(N). Subsequently, we compute the total energies of the molecule in its anionic and cationic states, denoted as E(N + 1) and E(N – 1), respectively. The IP is then determined as IP = E(N – 1) – E(N), while the EA is given by EA = E(N) – E(N + 1).

To determine the Abs Max, we perform TD-DFT calculations on the optimized structure. Specifically, we compute the excitation energies of the 30 lowest-energy excited states and identify the excitation energy corresponding to the highest oscillator strength as the Abs Max.
