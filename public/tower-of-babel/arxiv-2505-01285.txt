Polyhedral realisations of finite arc complexes using strip deformations
François Guéritaud, Pallavi Panda

Abstract
We study infinitesimal deformations of complete hyperbolic surfaces with boundary and with ideal vertices, possibly decorated with horoballs. “Admissible” deformations are the ones that pull all horoballs apart; they form a convex cone of deformations. We describe this cone in terms of the arc complex of the surface: specifically, this paper focuses on the surfaces for which that complex is finite. Those surfaces form four families: (ideal) polygons, once-punctured polygons, one-holed polygons (or “crowns”), and Möbius strips with spikes. In each case, we describe a natural simplicial decomposition of the projectivized admissible cone and of each of its faces, realizing them as appropriate arc complexes.



1
Introduction

A hyperbolic surface
\Pi
comes with a deformation space
\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
, sometimes called its Fricke-Klein space (or Teichmüller space in the context of conformal geometry), the space of hyperbolic metrics on
\Pi
up to isotopy.
This space
\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
has been known since at least the time of Koebe and Poincaré to be diffeomorphic to a smooth open ball, whose dimension depends on the topological type of
\Pi
.
It carries a wealth of geometric structures, both differential and large-scale, that have been studied by many authors.

We may also talk about
infinitesimal
deformations of a hyperbolic metric on
\Pi
.
While falling squarely in the differential study of
\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
(see e.g. Kerckhoff’s solution to the Nielsen realization problem
[
Ker83
]
), this idea has also proven crucial, for instance, in the study of
3
-manifolds with complete flat Lorentzian metrics
[
GLM09
]
,
[
DGK16
]
.
The hyperbolic surfaces
\Pi
of interest in that context are typically compact with geodesic boundary, and the deformations that give rise to “good” Lorentzian metrics are the ones that
infinitesimally lengthen
every geodesic loop in
\Pi
, in a uniform sense.
These are traditionally called
admissible
deformations; they form a convex cone in any fiber of
\mathrm{T}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
.

The main result of
[
DGK16
]
is a realization theorem of admissible deformations in terms of the
arc complex
of
\Pi
. This clique simplicial complex, of dimension one less than
\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
, first introduced by Harvey
[
Har81
]
, is naturally defined in terms of the topology of
\Pi
. In general, it is an infinite complex without any nice piece-wise linear manifold structure. Nonetheless, Hatcher
[
Hat91
]
proved that it is always contractible. Furthermore, Harer
[
Har86
]
proved that there exists an open dense subset, called the pruned arc complex, that is homeomorphic to an open ball. The latter is used in
[
DGK16
]
to parametrize admissible deformations up to scaling.

In this paper we study those hyperbolic surfaces
\Pi
that give rise to
finite
arc complexes, and interpret these complexes precisely in terms of a generalized notion of admissible deformation.
In order to capture many finite cousins of the associahedron, we must allow the hyperbolic surface
\Pi
to have so-called “spikes”, i.e. regions isometric to a corner of an ideal hyperbolic polygon.
An ideal polygon in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is itself a possible (and important) case of such a surface
\Pi
, hence our somewhat unusual choice of letter.
We will also “decorate” some of these spikes with
horoballs
: the horoball data is seen as part and parcel of the “metric”
m
on
\Pi
. This was first introduced by Penner in his work on Decorated Teichmüller Theory
[
Pen02
]
,
[
Pen87
]
.
A deformation of
m
, or vector of
\mathrm{T}_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
, will be called admissible if it lengthens not only every loop, but also every inter-horoball distance.
(This will be our working definition of admissibility, mostly ignoring here the connections to Lorentzian geometry.)
Our main theorem is:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Pi
be a (decorated) surface with finite arc complex, and
m\in\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
a hyperbolic metric.
There is a natural parametrization of the closed polyhedron of (weakly) admissible deformations
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}

in the positive projectivization
\mathbb{P}^{+}\mathrm{T}_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
, by an appropriate arc complex in
\Pi
.
Moreover, there is an explicit description of the faces of
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
in terms both of lengthening properties, and of arc subcomplexes.

In order to state the result precisely (Theorem
3.2
below), we will need to make a number of definitions in Section
2
.
Nevertheless, we can describe right away the class of spiked surfaces
\Pi
(ignoring decorations) to which Theorem
1.1
applies: these are

(1) the spiked disks (ideal polygons),

(2) once-punctured spiked disks (once-punctured polygons,

(3) once-holed spiked disks (“crowns”), and

(4) spiked Möbius strips.

In particular, their fundamental groups are all either trivial or cyclic.
Among complete connected hyperbolic surfaces with nonempty, geodesic boundary and finite, nonzero area, these are the only examples with finite arc complexes that have spikes. In
[
Pan24
]
and
[
Pan23
]
, it was shown that the interior of the arc complex parametrises the projectivised admissible cone. In
[
Pan23a
]
and
[
Pan24a
]
, it was shown that the full arc complexes of these four types of decorated surfaces are closed balls. Consequently, the projectivised strip map was shown to be a homeomorphism between the full arc complex and
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
.

If we include spikeless surfaces then there are two more cases with finite arc complexes: the three-holed sphere and two-holed projective plane. These two surfaces have

\dim\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}=2

(or less if we allow some of the holes to be converted to punctures) and are easy to treat separately, as was done in
[
DGK16
, §6]
.

Note that our surfaces may have punctures (also called “cusps”: regions isometric to the quotient of a horoball by a parabolic isometry), as exemplified by (2) in the above list; but we do not consider any “horoball decorations” on the cusps themselves, only on the spikes.

For surfaces with infinite arc complexes, a full counterpart of Theorem
1.1
does exist but it is quite a bit more delicate to state (and prove).
We will deal with it in future work.

Plan of the paper

In Section
2
we define most objects needed to make Theorem
1.1
precise.
In Section

we state and prove the main result, Theorem

.
The remaining sections explore consequences and special cases.
In Section
4
we characterize the facets (top-dimensional faces) of the admissible cone.
In Section

B
, we illustrate Theorem
3.2
and Proposition
3.3
in the case of a fully decorated 2-crown
\widehat{\Pi_{2}^{\circledcirc}}
.

Definitions

2.1
Topological type and dimension

For
g\geq 0
, let
\mathsf{S}_{g}
be the connected sum of
g
copies of the torus (or standard surface of genus
g
), with
\mathsf{S}_{0}
the
2
-sphere.
For
h\geq 1
, let also
\mathsf{N}_{h}
be the connected sum of
h
copies of the projective plane.
A complete hyperbolic surface
\Pi
with nonempty geodesic boundary and finite (positive) area can always be obtained, up to homeomorphism, from some (unique) surface
\mathsf{S}_{g}
or
\mathsf{N}_{h}
, by removing the interiors of disjoint closed disks
D_{1},\dots,D_{\ell}
, and then attaching to each boundary loop
\partial D_{i}
either a punctured disk
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,+\infty)
, or an annulus
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,1]
, or an
n_{i}
-
crown
which is an annulus minus
n_{i}\geq 1
points of
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\{1\}
.
(Gluings happen along
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\{0\}
.)

The “missing” point at infinity of a punctured disk
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,+\infty)
gives rise to a so-called puncture of
\Pi
, surrounded by a cusp region.
The annulus boundaries
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\{1\}
give rise to the compact (geodesic) boundary components of
\Pi
, while the
n_{i}
open segments in the boundary of an
n_{i}
-crown give rise to
n_{i}
noncompact geodesic boundary components of
\Pi
: metrically, two consecutive of these
n_{i}
segments are infinite geodesics that become asymptotically close near their common end.
(For
n_{i}=1
it could be the two ends of one geodesic.)

Finally, when
n_{i}>0
, we may decorate
r_{i}
of the
n_{i}
spikes at the
i
-th hole with horoballs.
If
\ell^{\prime}\leq\ell
is the number of punctures, then the deformation space of such a surface
\Pi
has dimension

N_{\Pi}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}6g-6+2\ell^{\prime}+3(\ell-\ell^{\prime})+\sum_{i}n_{i}+r_{i},&\text{if $\Pi$ is orientable}\\
3h-6+2\ell^{\prime}+3(\ell-\ell^{\prime})+\sum_{i}n_{i}+r_{i},&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

which is non-negative.
Note that our assumption of nonempty boundary means
\ell>\ell^{\prime}
; in particular
\ell\geq 1
.
In fact our initial assumption that
\Pi
be a hyperbolic surface with
positive
area is slightly
stronger
than
N\geq 0
and
\ell>\ell^{\prime}
:

— When
(g,\ell,\ell^{\prime})=(0,1,0)
then
\Pi
is an
n_{1}
-gon and
N_{\Pi}=n_{1}+r_{1}-3
.
The demand of positive area requires
n_{1}\geq 3
, slightly stronger than
N_{\Pi}\geq 0
.

— When
(g,\ell,\ell^{\prime})=(0,2,0)
, then
\Pi
is an annulus with
n_{1}+n_{2}
spikes and
N_{\Pi}=(n_{1}+n_{2})+(r_{1}+r_{2})
: positive area requires
n_{1}+n_{2}\geq 1
, stronger than
N\geq 0
.

— When
(h,\ell,\ell^{\prime})=(1,1,0)
, similarly
\Pi
is an
n_{1}
-spiked Möbius strip and
N_{\Pi}=n_{1}+r_{1}
: positive area requires
n_{1}\geq 1
, stronger than
N_{\Pi}\geq 0
.

— In all other cases, one may check that
N_{\Pi}\geq 0
already implies that the surface of the corresponding topological type can be realized as a hyperbolic surface with positive area.
In particular, when
(g,\ell,\ell^{\prime})=(0,2,1)
, then
\Pi
is a punctured
n_{1}
-gon and
N_{\Pi}=n_{1}+r_{1}-1
: positive area requires
n_{1}\geq 1
,
equivalent
to
N_{\Pi}\geq 0
(recall
0\leq r_{1}\leq n_{1}
).

2.2
Notation

The four special cases just enumerated are in fact, essentially, the ones Theorem
1.1
will apply to (i.e. the surfaces with finite arc complexes).
Since we will mostly focus on these surfaces, we introduce special notation for them.
Each notation in the table below represents a finite family of topological types, depending on how the
r
decorations are distributed among the
n
spikes.
The rightmost column can be ignored for the moment.

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr\text{Notation}&\text{\# spikes}&\text{\# decorations}&\text{Family of surfaces}&N_{\Pi}&\#\text{ simple }\beta\\
\hline\cr{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond}&n\geq 3&r\geq 0&\text{ Ideal $n$-gon}&n+r-3&\dfrac{r(r-1)}{2}\\[8.00003pt]
\Pi^{\times}_{n,r}&n\geq 1&r\geq 0&\text{ Punctured $n$-gon}&n+r-1&r^{2}\\[5.0pt]
\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc}&n\geq 1&r\geq 0&\text{$n$-spiked crown}&n+r&r^{2}+1\\[5.0pt]
\mathcal{M}_{n,r}&n\geq 1&r\geq 0&\text{$n$-spiked M\"{o}bius strip}&n+r&\dfrac{r(r+1)}{2}+r^{2}+1\\[5.0pt]
\hline\cr\end{array}

(In keeping with the previous section, a
crown
is an annulus with
0
spikes on one of its boundary components.)

It is straightforward to check from the classification above that these are exactly the hyperbolic surfaces with spikes that do
not
contain any two-sided simple loop other than the boundary.
By contrast, any surface that does possess such a loop must have infinite mapping class group (because of Dehn twist), hence infinitely many arcs.

:
The four families of surfaces with finite arc complexes. The index
n
refers to the number of spikes: here
\Pi^{\Diamond}_{6},\Pi^{\times}_{3},\Pi^{\circledcirc}_{3}~,\mathcal{M}_{1}
.

concreteness, we now describe the four types of surfaces directly (see Figure
1
).

Ideal polygons.

An ideal
n
-gon, denoted by
\Pi_{n}^{\Diamond}
, is the convex hull in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
of
n\geq 3
points in the boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.
This surface is homeomorphic to a disk with
n
points removed from its boundary.
The infinite hyperbolic geodesic joining two consecutive spikes is called an
edge
.

Once-punctured polygons.

A once-punctured
n
-gon, denoted by
\Pi_{n}^{\times}
, with (
n\geq 1
), is obtained from an ideal (
n+2
)-gon by identifying two consecutive edges via a parabolic isometry of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
fixing their common endpoint.
The resulting surface is homeomorphic to a punctured disk with
n
points removed from its boundary.

Crowns.

The surface obtained by removing
n\geq 1
points from one boundary component of an annulus
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,1]
, is called a
crown
and is denoted by
\Pi_{n}^{\circledcirc}
.
Its boundary consists of one simple closed curve and
n
open intervals.
Its fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\Pi_{n}^{\circledcirc})\simeq\Z
is generated by a loop
b
in the free homotopy class of the simple closed curve.
We can specify a hyperbolic structure on
\Pi_{n}^{\circledcirc}
in the following way:
let
\gamma\in\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
be a hyperbolic element whose axis is a bi-infinite geodesic, denoted by
l
.
It divides the boundary circle
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{2}
into two open intervals.
Let
x_{1}
be a point in one of them, and choose
(n-1)
distinct points
x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
on the same interval between
x_{1}
and its image
\gamma\cdot x_{1}
.
The points
\gamma^{k}\cdot x_{i}
, for
(k,i)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
, all lie on the same side of
l
; their closed convex hull is invariant under
\langle\gamma\rangle
, and its quotient is a complete finite-area hyperbolic surface with geodesic boundary, homeomorphic to
\Pi_{n}^{\circledcirc}
.
If
\rho:\pi_{1}(\Pi_{n}^{\circledcirc})\longrightarrow\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
is the holonomy representation, then
\rho(b)=\gamma
.
The images of the ideal points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
in the quotient are called
spikes
.

Möbius strip with spikes.

The non-orientable surface obtained by removing
n\geq 1
points from the boundary of a Möbius strip, is called a spiked Möbius strip.
Its orientation double cover is an annulus minus
n
points on each boundary component.
One can put a hyperbolic structure on this surface in the following way:
consider an ideal
n+2
-gon with marked vertices
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+2}
.
Take any two distinct edges
e,e^{\prime}
of the polygon.
Let
l
be a geodesic that intersects
e
and
e^{\prime}
such that the angles of intersection sum to
\pi
, and let
d
be the distance between
l\cap e
and
l\cap e^{\prime}
.
Let
h\in\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{R})
be the glide reflection along
l
with translation length
d
.
Then the quotient
\Pi_{n+2}^{\Diamond}/\sim
, where for every
z\in e
we set
z\sim h\cdot z
, is a metrically complete finite-area hyperbolic surface.

2.3
Admissible deformations

2.3.1
Horoball connections

Definition 2.1
.

Consider a surface
\Pi
as in the table above, with
r
of its
n
spikes decorated by horoballs. A bi-infinite geodesic joining two decorated spikes is called a
horoball connection
.
The
length
of a horoball connection is defined as the hyperbolic length of the geodesic segment subtended by the two horoballs.
A horoball connection is called
simple
if it has no self-intersection. A simple horoball connection is called
separating
if it decomposes the surface into two nonempty subsurfaces.

Remark 2.2
.

In the surfaces
{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond},\Pi^{\times}_{n,r},\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc},\mathcal{M}_{n,r}
the number of simple horoball connections is finite;
but except in
{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond}
the number of nonsimple ones is infinite (for
r>0
).

Definition 2.3
.

For
\Pi\in\Pi^{\times}_{n,r},\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc},\mathcal{M}_{n,r}
, a separating horoball connection is called
maximal
if one of the two subsurfaces is homeomorphic to
{\Pi}_{n+1,r+1}^{\Diamond}
.
(For
\Pi\in{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond}
there is no corresponding notion.)

Remark 2.4
.

Some simple horoball connections can be sides of
\Pi
; we count them as separating.
For
\Pi\in{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond},\Pi^{\times}_{n,r},\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc}
, all simple horoball connections are separating.
For
\Pi\in\mathcal{M}_{n,r}
, there exist both separating and non-separating simple horoball connections.

2.3.2
The admissible cone

Let
\mathcal{H}(\Pi)
be the set of all horoball connections and closed loops of the surface

\Pi
.
Then we can define the following smooth positive function for every
\beta\in\mathcal{H}(\Pi)
:

\begin{array}[]{cccl}l_{\beta}:&\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)&\longrightarrow&\R_{>0}\\
&m&\longmapsto&\text{length of $l_{\beta}$ with respect to $m$.}\end{array}

Definition 2.5
.

Given a hyperbolic metric
m
on
\Pi
, a vector in the tangent space
\mathrm{T}_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
is called an
infinitesimal deformation
of
\Pi
.

Definition 2.6
.

An infinitesimal deformation
v
is said to be
admissible
if there exists a constant
K>0
such that for all
\beta\in\mathcal{H}(\Pi)
,

\mathrm{d}l_{\beta}(v)\geq Kl_{\beta}.

(The condition is vacuously true if
\mathcal{H}(\Pi)=\varnothing
, e.g. for undecorated polygons
\Pi={\Pi}_{n,0}^{\Diamond}
.)

Definition 2.7
.

The
admissible cone
, denoted by
\Lambda(m)
, of a metric
m\in\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
on a partially decorated surface
\Pi
, is defined to be the set of all admissible deformations of
m
.

The admissible cone is open and convex in
\mathrm{T}_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
.
In the case of a decorated polygon
\Pi\in{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond}
, there are only finitely many horoball connections, say
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p(r)}
.
Thus the admissible cone is the intersection of finitely many open half-spaces:

\Lambda(m)=\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^{p(r)}\,\{dl_{\beta_{i}}>0\}.

Definition 2.8
.

A
weakly admissible
infinitesimal deformation is by definition an element of the
closure
of the admissible cone.

Notation 2.9
.

For a horoball connection or nontrivial closed curve
\beta
in
\Pi
, we define

H_{\beta}(m):=\{u\in\mathrm{T}_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)\mid\mathrm{d}l_{\beta}(m)(u)=0\},

usually abbreviated to
H_{\beta}
when the metric
m
is obvious and/or irrelevant.
For any subset
W\subset\mathrm{T}_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
, we denote by
W^{+}
its positive projectivisation
\{\R_{+}^{*}w~|~w\in W\smallsetminus\{0\}\}
in the sphere of directions (rays) of
\mathrm{T}_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
: for instance,
H_{\beta}^{+}
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
\beta
be a horoball connection of a decorated hyperbolic surface
\Pi
, with universal covering map
\pi:\widetilde{\Pi}\rightarrow\Pi
.
Then the
subsurface filled by

\beta
is the quotient
\Pi_{\beta}
by
\pi_{1}(\Pi)
of the union of all convex hulls in
\widetilde{\Pi}
of connected components of
\pi^{-1}(\beta)
.
This notion depends on a choice of hyperbolic metric
m
on
\Pi
, but is independent of
m
up to isotopy of
\Pi
.

If the horoball connection
\beta
is simple then the filled “subsurface”
\Pi_{\beta}
is reduced to
\beta
.
Otherwise,
\Pi_{\beta}
is a genuine subsurface with geodesic boundary in
\Pi
, embedded except possibly at some boundary loops of
\Pi_{\beta}
that can be matched in pairs (when two of the convex hulls in
\widetilde{\Pi}
share a boundary geodesic).
We can define similarly the subsurface filled by any finite collection of horoball connections
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}
, via convex hulls of connected components of
\pi^{-1}(\beta_{1}\cup\dots\cup\beta_{k})
.

2.3.3
Subsurfaces

Given a hyperbolic surface
\Pi\in{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond},\Pi^{\times}_{n,r},\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc},\mathcal{M}_{n,r}
, selecting a subset
E
of
n^{\prime}\leq n
spikes of
\Pi
, including
r^{\prime}\leq r
decorated ones, defines another surface
\Pi^{\prime}\in{\Pi}_{n^{\prime},r^{\prime}}^{\Diamond},\Pi^{\times}_{n^{\prime},r^{\prime}},\Pi_{n^{\prime},r^{\prime}}^{\circledcirc},\mathcal{M}_{n^{\prime},r^{\prime}}
.
(Here we assume that
n^{\prime}
is large enough that
\Pi^{\prime}
is indeed a hyperbolic surface, in the sense of Section
2.1
).
Restriction of hyperbolic metrics determines a smooth, natural projection
\pi:\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)\rightarrow\mathfrak{D}(\Pi^{\prime})
.

If this subset
E
of spikes contains all the decorated spikes, then given any metric
m\in\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
and its image
m^{\prime}:=\pi(m)\in\mathfrak{D}(\Pi^{\prime})
, the linear projection

\mathrm{d}\pi:\mathrm{T}_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)\rightarrow\mathrm{T}_{m^{\prime}}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi^{\prime})

restricts to a map between admissible cones:
(\mathrm{d}\pi)^{-1}(\Lambda(m^{\prime}))=\Lambda(m)
.
This is just a way of saying that admissibility of an infinitesimal deformation does not depend on the infinitesimal motions of the undecorated spikes, since these do not span any horoball connections.
For general
E
one has only
(\mathrm{d}\pi)^{-1}(\Lambda(m^{\prime}))\supset\Lambda(m)
, i.e. an admissible deformation of
\Pi
projects to an admissible deformation of
\Pi^{\prime}
.

If
E
is
equal
to the set of decorated spikes, i.e.
n^{\prime}=r^{\prime}=r
, then the admissible cone
\Lambda(m^{\prime})\subset\mathrm{T}_{m^{\prime}}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi^{\prime})
is properly convex, i.e. its closure contains no line: this is true because if both a vector
v
and its negative belong to
\overline{\Lambda(m^{\prime})}
, then
\mathrm{d}l_{\beta}(v)=0
for every horoball connection
\beta
, hence
v=0
because the lengths of horoball connections smoothly determine the metric
m^{\prime}
.
One then has
\Lambda(m)\simeq\Lambda(m^{\prime})\oplus\mathbb{R}^{n-r}
as convex sets, and likewise
\overline{\Lambda(m)}\simeq\overline{\Lambda(m^{\prime})}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{n-r}
.
(In general any convex cone has such a decomposition, essentially unique, as a sum of a properly convex cone and an
\mathbb{R}
-vector space.)

Remark 2.11
.

When investigating the faces of the convex spherical polytope
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
below, each face will thus be a suspension of a properly convex projective polytope with the sphere
(\mathbb{R}^{n-r})^{+}\simeq\mathbb{S}^{n-r-1}
, accounting for free motion of undecorated spikes.
When
n>r
this includes the smallest-dimensional face
\mathbb{S}^{n-r-1}
situated “at infinity” (suspended with the empty set), representing deformations that
only
move the nondecorated spikes.
All other faces (defined as intersections of
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
with supporting great spheres) are topologically closed balls, containing
\mathbb{S}^{n-r-1}
in their boundary.

(Here
\mathbb{S}^{0}
is as usual a pair of antipodal points; and we can also set
\mathbb{S}^{-1}:=\varnothing
in order to extend patterns consistently to the fully decorated case
n=r
.)

2.3.4
Numbers of horoball connections and closed curves

We can now count the simple horoball connections and simple closed curves present in the four types of surfaces:

\bullet
In polygons
{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond}
we can index simple horoball connections by unordered pairs of distinct (decorated) spikes, forming their endpoints.

\bullet
In punctured polygons
\Pi^{\times}_{n,r}
we can index simple horoball connections in
ordered
pairs of spikes, possibly equal (the order is dictated by which side of the horoball connection contains the puncture).

\bullet
In holed polygons
\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc}
we can use a similar indexation scheme, and the surface also contains a simple closed geodesic.

\bullet
Finally in Möbius strips
\mathcal{M}_{n,r}
, separating horoball connections are the ones disjoint from the unique simple closed (core) geodesic and can be indexed in ordered pairs of spikes (possibly equal), while nonseparating horoball connections, which cross the core, can be indexed in unordered pairs of spikes (also possibly equal).

These tallies of simple horoball connections and simple closed curves are summarized in the rightmost column of the table in Section
2.2
above.

2.4
Arc complexes

The other main ingredient of Theorem
1.1
is the arc complex of a (spiked, partially decorated) hyperbolic surface
\Pi
.
We now give the definitions.

An
arc
in
\Pi
is a proper geodesic embedding
\alpha
of a closed interval or ray
I\subset\R
into
\Pi
, relative to its boundary (i.e.
\alpha(I)\cap\partial\Pi=\alpha(\partial I)
), and subject to the conditions below:

1.

If
I=[a,b]
, then the arc
\alpha
is a finite geodesic segment connecting two sides of
\Pi
.
We rule out the case that these two sides are consecutive and share an undecorated spike (i.e. that
\alpha
separates off a single, undecorated spike from the rest of the surface).

2.

If
I=[a,\infty)
, then the arc
\alpha
is a hyperbolic geodesic ray in
\Pi
such that
\alpha(a)\in\partial\Pi
, and the infinite end converges to a spike of
\Pi
.
We require that that spike be decorated.

To emphasize these two conditions we will sometimes speak of
permitted
arcs (although by default we use “arc” as a strict synonym).

Let
\mathscr{A}
be the set of all arcs of the two types above.
The
arc complex
of the partially decorated hyperbolic surface
\Pi
is a simplicial complex
\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)
whose 0-simplices are given by the arcs in
\mathscr{A}
seen up to isotopy fixing the boundary (setwise), and for
k\geq 1
, every
k
-simplex is given by a
(k+1)
-tuple of pairwise disjoint and distinct isotopy classes.
A simplex
\sigma
is said to be
filling
if the arcs corresponding to
\sigma^{(0)}
decompose the surface into topological disks with at most one decorated spike, and punctured disks with no decorated spikes.
The
pruned arc complex
of a polygon
\Pi
, denoted by
\mathcal{PA}(\Pi)
, is the union of the interiors of the filling simplices of the arc complex
\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)
.

Definition 2.12
.

Given a hyperbolic surface
\Pi\in{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond},\Pi^{\times}_{n,r},\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc},\mathcal{M}_{n,r}
, we consider a subset
\vec{\beta}=\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{h}\}
of the collection formed by the boundary horoball connections of
\Pi
and the closed simple loop of
\Pi
(if present).
The
marked
arc complex, denoted by
\mathcal{A}({\Pi,\vec{\beta}})
, is the subcomplex of the arc complex
\mathcal{A}({\Pi})
generated by the permitted arcs that do not intersect any
\beta_{i}
.

2.5
Strip deformations

An arc
\alpha:I\rightarrow\Pi
in
\Pi
defines an infinitesimal deformation of
\Pi
, as follows.
Choose a point
p_{\alpha}
in the interior of
\alpha(I)
.
Suppose, first, that
I
is a compact interval.
We may cut
\Pi
along the geodesic
\alpha(I)
and insert a
strip
of hyperbolic plane along
I
, of minimal width
t\geq 0
achieved precisely at
p_{\alpha}
.
(A strip is the region bounded by two bi-infinite geodesics with disjoint closures.)
This moves the ideal vertices and decorations of
\Pi
, but we can use their new positions to define a new hyperbolic metric
m_{t}\in\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
.

When
I
is noncompact, say
I=[0,+\infty)
, we make a similar cut-and-paste definition of the metric
m_{t}
, except that the shape we insert along
\alpha(I)
is a
parabolic strip
, isometric to the region of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
bounded by two mutually asymptotic lines, with horocyclic width
t
at the depth of
p_{\alpha}
.

In both cases, the construction can be performed equivariantly on all lifts of
\alpha(I)
to the universal cover of
\Pi
: this defines also a new holonomy representation for
m_{t}
.
By definition, the strip deformation associated to
\alpha
is the class of
t\mapsto m_{t}
in
\mathrm{T}_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
.
Extending linearly, this lets us define, for every metric
m\in\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
, the
strip map

f:\mathcal{A}(\Pi)\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{+}(\mathrm{T}_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi))

(1)

which takes values in the weakly admissible deformations.
(Weak admissibility follows from the observation that the length of a closed curve or horoball connection can only go
up
if it intersects one or more of the arcs supporting the deformation; in fact this increase can be measured trigonometrically
[
DGK16
, §2.1]
).
Here is a key fact:

Proposition 2.13
.

For any
\Pi\in{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond},\Pi^{\times}_{n,r},\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc},\mathcal{M}_{n,r}
endowed with a hyperbolic metric
m
, the
strip map
f:\mathcal{A}(\Pi)\rightarrow\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
is a homeomorphism.

We refer to
[
Pan23a
]
for a proof (the statement there is limited to
\Pi\in{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond},\Pi^{\times}_{n,r}
, but the proof only uses the finiteness of the arc complex
\mathcal{A}(\Pi)
). Proposition
2.13

fails
in general for surfaces with infinite arc complexes, although
f
still establishes a homeomorphism from the pruned arc complex
\mathcal{PA}(\Pi)
to the interior of
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
.
Proposition
2.13
contains in particular the following facts:

•

The largest number of vertices in a simplex
\sigma
of the arc complex
\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)
is
N_{\Pi}
, the dimension of the deformation space
\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
.

•

For any such
\sigma
, the strip deformations associated to its vertices form a basis of
\mathrm{T}_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
.

3.1
Spread subsets

Theorem
3.2
below will express the faces of the projectivized admissible cone
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
in terms of certain subcomplexes of the arc complex
\mathcal{A}(\Pi)
.
To describe the subcomplexes of interest, we need to make some definitions.

Definition 3.1
.

Consider
\Pi\in{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond},\Pi^{\times}_{n,r},\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc},\mathcal{M}_{n,r}
, and a nonempty closed subset
P\subsetneq\Pi
with connected components
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}
.
We say that
P
is
spread
if

•

every
P_{i}
is either a simple closed geodesic loop, or a horoball connection, or a subsurface with geodesic boundary and positive area whose spikes (taken among those of
\Pi
) are all decorated; and

•

every component of
\Pi\smallsetminus P
intersects
\partial\Pi
.

See Figure
2
for an illustration.
Given a spread subset
P\subset\Pi
, the metric completions of the connected components of
\Pi\smallsetminus P
are hyperbolic surfaces
\Pi_{1},\dots,\Pi_{\kappa}
.
For each
\Pi_{\iota}
, we let
\vec{\beta}_{\iota}
be the (nonempty) collection of horoball connections and geodesic loop(s) in
\partial\Pi_{\iota}
that are contained in
P
.
By construction, the simplicial complex

\mathcal{A}(\Pi,P):=\mathcal{A}(\Pi_{1},\vec{\beta}_{1})\Join\dots\Join\mathcal{A}(\Pi_{\kappa},\vec{\beta}_{\kappa})

(2)

embeds simplicially in the arc complex
\mathcal{A}(\Pi)
.
It can be described as the subcomplex of arcs that do not meet
P
.

3.2
Statements

Here is the main result of this paper, refining Proposition
2.13
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\Pi\in{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond},\Pi^{\times}_{n,r},\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc},\mathcal{M}_{n,r}
be a hyperbolic surface with
n
spikes and
r\leq n
decorations, with metric
m
. Then:

•

The positively projectivized, weakly admissible deformations in
\mathbb{P}^{+}(\mathrm{T}_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi))
form a finite compact polytope
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
, homeomorphic to the arc complex
\mathcal{A}(\Pi)
via the strip map
f
of (
1
).

•

The faces of
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
are exactly the
f(\mathcal{A}(\Pi,P))
, where
P\subset\Pi
ranges over spread subsets.

•

For any spread subsets
P,P^{\prime}\subsetneq\Pi
,

\left.\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathrm{(i)}&\mathcal{A}(\Pi,P)\subset\mathcal{A}(\Pi,P^{\prime})&\iff P\supset P^{\prime}\\
\mathrm{(ii)}&\mathcal{A}(\Pi,P)\subsetneq\mathcal{A}(\Pi,P^{\prime})&\iff P\supsetneq P^{\prime}.\end{array}\right\}

(3)

The dimension and topology of a face

can be deduced from homeomorphicity of

and from the following, strictly topological result.

.

Let
\Pi\in{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond},\Pi^{\times}_{n,r},\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc},\mathcal{M}_{n,r}
be a partially decorated surface, and

(hence

).
Let

(see Section

).
Then:

1.

For

,
the marked arc complex
\mathcal{A}({\Pi,\vec{\beta}})
is PL-homeomorphic to:

\varnothing
if

;

(b)

the sphere

if

or

or

For
\Pi\in\Pi^{\times}_{n,r}

\varnothing

(b)

(c)

the closed ball
{\mathbb{B}}^{N}

For

Remark 3.4
.

Spherical cases: in case 1.(b), when

the

decorated spikes are necessarily consecutive, connected by the single horoball connection
\beta_{1}=\beta_{h}\in\vec{\beta}
.
In cases 3.(b) and 4.(b), the collection
\vec{\beta}
is necessarily reduced to the core curve.

Remark 3.5
.

To determine
\mathcal{A}(\Pi,P)
for a spread subset
P\subset\Pi
as in Theorem
3.2
, one needs to apply Proposition
3.3
to the components
(\Pi_{\iota},\vec{\beta}_{\iota})
of
\Pi\smallsetminus P
, and combine results by suspension as in the formula (
2
).
In such applications, the “empty” cases (a) of the Proposition never arise, since by definition of spread subsets the component of
\Pi\smallsetminus P
corresponding to
\Pi_{\iota}
intersects
\partial\Pi
(along an edge that cannot belong to
P
) so
\vec{\beta}_{\iota}
cannot be all of
\partial\Pi_{\iota}
. The “spherical” cases (b) occur only in very specific situations:

•

1.(b) when
\Pi_{\iota}
is cut off from
\Pi
by a single boundary-parallel horoball connection that skips only undecorated horoballs (hence
(r_{\iota},h_{\iota})=(2,1)
).
This happens for

if and only if

and

is largest possible, namely equal to the convex hull of all decorated punctures of
\Pi
.
Then, the corresponding sphere

represents all possible motions of the nondecorated spikes.
See also Remark
2.11
.
For any other
P
the definition of
\mathcal{A}(\Pi,P)
as iterated suspension (
2
) contains at least one ball term, and is therefore a ball.

•

3.(b) and 4.(b) when
\Pi
is undecorated (
r=0
) and
P
is reduced to the core loop
c
.
Then,
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
is a whole hemisphere
\simeq{\mathbb{B}}^{n-1}
, defined by the lengthening of

, with boundary the sphere

; note that
\mathcal{A}(\Pi,P)
is then simplicially equivalent to
\mathcal{A}(\Pi^{\times}_{n,0})
.

Note that our definition of spread sets

supposes

.
If we did allow

, then

would be simply the whole arc complex

The first statement of Theorem

: see
[
Pan23a
]
.
Case 2 follows.

For Case 3, we distinguish two subcases. First, if the boundary loop
c
belongs to
\vec{\beta}
, then

\mathcal{A}(\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc},\vec{\beta})
identifies with

\mathcal{A}(\Pi^{\times}_{n,r},\vec{\beta}\smallsetminus\{c\})
so we are reduced to Case 2; this covers in particular the implications of 3.(a) and 3.(b).
If
c\notin\vec{\beta}
, we build from
\Pi\in\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc}
a disk
\Pi^{\prime}
with
r
red and
n-h
blue vertices on its boundary, and an extra blue vertex in the center.
The diagonal complex
S
is now formed by boundary-to-boundary
and
boundary-to-center arcs,
(r+n-h)^{2}
arcs in total.
It is shown in
[
Pan24a
]
(see also Theorem
A.10
below) that the subcomplex
S^{\prime}\subset S
spanned by blue-blue and blue-red arcs is always homeomorphic to
{\mathbb{B}}^{r+n-h-1}
(in particular, it always contains the top-dimensional simplex spanned by all center-to-boundary arcs).
Case 3 follows.

For Case 4, similarly if the core curve
c
belongs to
\vec{\beta}
, then
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{M}_{n,r},\vec{\beta})
identifies with
\mathcal{A}(\Pi^{\times}_{n,r},\vec{\beta}\smallsetminus\{c\})
because the complement of the core curve of the Möbius strip is an annulus; and therefore we are reduced to Case 2: this covers in particular the implication in Case 4.(b).
If
c\notin\vec{\beta}
, we consider a Möbius strip
\Pi^{\prime}
with
r
red and
n-h
blue vertices on its boundary, and the associated diagonal complex
S
(containing
(r+n-h)(r+n-h-1)
separating diagonals, which are isotopic into the boundary without being boundary edges; and
(r+n-h)(r+n-h+1)/2
nonseparating ones, which cross the core curve).
It is shown in
[
Pan24a
]
(see also Theorem
A.13
below) that unless all vertices are red, the subcomplex
S^{\prime}\subset S
spanned by blue-blue and red-blue diagonals is homeomorphic to
{\mathbb{B}}^{r+n-h-1}
.
Case 4 follows, and Proposition
3.3
is proved.

Note that the triangulations of closed balls given by the above references are all
collapsible
, a strong combinatorial condition.
We do not use that fact in this paper.
∎

4
The facets of
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}

Consider a hyperbolic surface
\Pi\in{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond},\Pi^{\times}_{n,r},\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc},\mathcal{M}_{n,r}
, a hyperbolic metric
m
on
\Pi
, and the polyhedron
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
of weakly admissible deformations.
By definition, a
facet
of
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
is a face of codimension
1
.
In this section we study which horoball connections (and geodesic loops) define facets: in other words, we classify the situations in which (
2
) gives a complex of dimension
N_{\Pi}-2
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The hyperplane
H_{\beta}^{+}
intersects
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
along a facet for every simple horoball connection
\beta
except when
\Pi=\mathcal{M}_{n,r}
and the horoball connection
\beta
is maximal (Definition
2.3
).

Remark 4.2
.

There are exactly two types of simple horoball connections that are
not
spread subsets:

\bullet
When
\beta
is maximal in a Möbius strip
\Pi=\mathcal{M}_{n,r}
, hence bounds off a 1-spiked Möbius strip
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
disjoint from
\partial\Pi
(i.e. the exceptions in Lemma
4.1
);

\bullet
When
\beta
is maximal in punctured polygon
\Pi=\Pi^{\times}_{n,r}
, hence bounds off a punctured monogon
\Pi^{\times}_{1,1}
. Such a
\beta

does
define a facet of the polyhedron
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
(associated to the spread subset
P:=\Pi^{\times}_{1,1}
), essentially because
\Pi^{\times}_{1,1}
is rigid; but see Lemma
4.4
below for a different way in which such
\beta,P
are exceptional.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Let
\beta
be a simple horoball connection.
First, we suppose that
\beta
is separating.
Then
\beta
decomposes the surface into two subsurfaces which we denote by
\Pi^{1}
and
\Pi^{2}
.
The projective hyperplane
H_{\beta}^{+}
, of dimension
N-2
, intersects
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
along a facet if and only if

\dim\left(H_{\beta}^{+}\cap\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}\right)=N-2.

The left hand side is at least
\dim\left(\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{1},\beta})\Join\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{2},\beta})\right)
: indeed,
any point in
\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{1},\beta})\Join\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{2},\beta})
gives rise to a strip deformation whose support is disjoint from
\beta
, hence which belongs to
H_{\beta}^{+}\cap\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
; and the component strip deformations are linearly independent by
[
Pan24
]
.

Thus, let us compute the dimensions of
\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{1},\beta})
and
\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{2},\beta})
, using Proposition
3.3
.
We treat the four types of surfaces in turn.

•

\Pi\in{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond}
: In this case,
\dim H_{\beta}^{+}=N-2=n+r-5
.
There exist integers
n_{1}
and
r_{1}
such that
\Pi^{1}\in{\Pi}_{n_{1},r-r_{1}+2}^{\Diamond}
and
\Pi^{2}\in{\Pi}_{n-n_{1}+2,r_{1}}^{\Diamond}
.
Then we have that

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{1},\beta})\simeq{\mathbb{B}}^{n_{1}+r-r_{1}+2-4-1}={\mathbb{B}}^{n_{1}+r-r_{1}-3},\\
\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{2},\beta})\simeq{\mathbb{B}}^{n-n_{1}+r_{1}-3}.\end{array}

Here, we have that
3\leq n_{1}\leq n-1
.
Since
\beta
connects two distinct decorated spikes, we also have that
2\leq r_{1}\leq r
.
So for
i=1,2
, we get that
\dim\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{i},\beta})\geq 0.

Hence,

\begin{array}[]{l}\dim\left(\mathcal{A}({{\Pi}_{n-n_{1}+2,r_{1}}^{\Diamond},\beta})\Join\mathcal{A}({{\Pi}_{n_{1},r-r_{1}+2}^{\Diamond},\beta})\right)\\
=(n-n_{1}+r_{1}-3)+(n_{1}+r-r_{1}-3)+1\\
=n+r-5.\end{array}

Therefore,
H_{\beta}^{+}
intersects
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
along a facet.
This actually finishes the case
\Pi\in{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond}
, as all simple horoball conections are separating in such
\Pi
.

•

\Pi\in\Pi^{\times}_{n,r}
: In this case,
\dim H_{\beta}^{+}=n+r-3
.
There exist integers
n_{1}
and
r_{1}
such that one of the subsurfaces is an ideal polygon
\Pi^{1}\in{\Pi}_{n-n_{1}+2,r_{1}}^{\Diamond}
and the other one is a once-punctured polygon
\Pi^{2}\in\Pi^{\times}_{n_{1},r-r_{1}+2}
. Again, we have that

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{1},\beta})\simeq{\mathbb{B}}^{n-n_{1}+r_{1}-3},\\
\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{2},\beta})\simeq{\mathbb{B}}^{n_{1}+r-r_{1}+2-2-1}={\mathbb{B}}^{n_{1}+r-r_{1}-1}.\end{array}

Here, we have
1\leq r_{1}\leq r+1
and
1\leq n_{1}\leq n-1
.
When
n_{1}=1
and
r_{1}=r+1
, we have that
\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{2},\beta})
is empty.
This is illustrated in the right panel of the first row of Fig.
3
.
In that case, we get
\dim H_{\beta}^{+}\cap\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}=n-1+r+1-3=n+r-3
.

When
n_{1}\geq 2
and
r_{1}\leq r
, we have

\begin{array}[]{l}\dim\left(\mathcal{A}({{\Pi}_{n-n_{1}+2,r_{1}}^{\Diamond},\beta})\Join\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{\times}_{n_{1},r-r_{1}+2},\beta})\right)\\
=(n-n_{1}+r_{1}-3)+(n_{1}+r-r_{1}-1)+1\\
=n+r-3.\end{array}

Hence we have that
H_{\beta}^{+}
intersects
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
along a facet.
Again this finishes the case
\Pi\in\Pi^{\times}_{n,r}
, as all simple horoball connections are separating for such
\Pi
.

•

\Pi\in\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc}
: In this case,
\dim H_{\beta}^{+}=n+r-2
.
There exist integers
n_{1},r_{1}
such that one of the subsurfaces is an ideal polygon
\Pi^{1}\in{\Pi}_{n-n_{1}+2,r_{1}}^{\Diamond}
and the other one is a one-holed polygon
\Pi^{2}\in\Pi_{n_{1},r-r_{1}+2}^{\circledcirc}
.
Again, we have that

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{1},\beta})\simeq{\mathbb{B}}^{n-n_{1}+r_{1}-3},\\
\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{2},\beta})\simeq{\mathbb{B}}^{n_{1}+r-r_{1}+2-1-1}={\mathbb{B}}^{n_{1}+r-r_{1}}.\end{array}

Here, we have
1\leq r_{1}\leq r+1
and
1\leq n_{1}\leq n-1
.
This implies that the two marked arc complexes are always non-empty.
The case

\begin{array}[]{l}\dim\left(\mathcal{A}({{\Pi}_{n-n_{1}+2,r_{1}}^{\Diamond},\beta})\Join\mathcal{A}({\Pi^{\times}_{n_{1},r-r_{1}+2},\beta})\right)\\
=(n-n_{1}+r_{1}-3)+(n_{1}+r-r_{1})+1\\

Hence we have that

intersects
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
along a facet.
Again this finishes the case

Lemma 4.3
.

For two distinct simple horoball connections
\beta,\beta^{\prime}
that define facets of
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
, these facets are themselves distinct.

Proof.

This follows e.g. from the second and third points of Theorem
3.2
, or from the fact that we can find a permitted arc that crosses
\beta
but does not cross
\beta^{\prime}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose
\Pi\in{\Pi}_{n,r}^{\Diamond},\Pi^{\times}_{n,r},\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc},\mathcal{M}_{n,r}
.
Given a non-simple horoball connection
\beta
of the surface
\Pi
, the hyperplane
H_{\beta}^{+}
does not form a facet of
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
, unless
\Pi\in\Pi^{\times}_{n,r}
and
\beta
is a horoball connection with identical endpoints that turns
k\geq 2
times around the puncture.

Proof.

We need to prove that for every non-simple horoball connection
\beta
, we have

\dim H_{\beta}^{+}\cap\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}=\dim H_{\beta}^{+},

if and only if
\beta
is a horoball connection of
\Pi^{\times}_{n,r}
with equal endpoints that turns around the puncture.

Firstly, we remark that non-simple horoball connections exist only for
\Pi\in\{\Pi^{\times}_{n,r},\Pi_{n,r}^{\circledcirc},\mathcal{M}_{n,r}\}
. These are the horoball connections that turn
k
times around the puncture or core curve.
Let
S
be the subsurface filled by the non-simple horoball connection
\beta
.
If
S
is the entire surface
\Pi
then
H_{\beta}^{+}\cap\Lambda(m)^{+}=\varnothing
, in which case the lemma holds trivially.
So we may assume that
S
is strictly contained in
\Pi
.
Then
S
is one of the four types of surfaces. See Fig.
4
.
Let
\beta_{0}
be a boundary horoball connection of
S
.
Then
H_{\beta}^{+}\cap\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}\subset H_{\beta_{0}}\cap\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
, because if the support of a strip deformation is disjoint from
\beta
it is disjoint from
\beta_{0}
.
Therefore

\dim H_{\beta}^{+}\cap\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}\leq\dim H_{\beta_{0}}^{+}\cap\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}.

The equality holds only when any permitted arc disjoint with
\beta_{0}
is disjoint with
\beta
. In Fig.
4
, this is illustrated in the top left panel and in the bottom panel. in either of the two cases:

•

When
S=\Pi^{\times}_{1,1}
and
\beta
is a horoball connection that turns
k\geq 1
times around the puncture (with equal endpoints).

•

When
S=\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
and
\beta
is a horoball connection that turns
k\geq 1
times around the core curve (with equal endpoints).

In the second case, from the proof of Lemma
4.1
we know that
\dim H_{\beta_{0}}^{+}\cap\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}<\dim H_{\beta_{0}}^{+}
, so neither
H_{\beta}^{+}
nor
H_{\beta_{0}}^{+}
forms a facet of
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
.

When
\dim H_{\beta}^{+}\cap\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}<\dim H_{\beta_{0}}^{+}\cap\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+},
from Lemma
4.1
, we get that
\dim H_{\beta}^{+}\cap\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}<N-2
, where

is the dimension of
\mathrm{T}_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
.
So
H_{\beta}^{+}
does not form a facet of
\Lambda(m)
.
∎

5
Fully decorated surfaces

In the fully decorated cases, the admissible cone is a properly convex projective polyhedron, i.e. a convex polyhedron that is contained and bounded in some affine chart.
We show that the vertices of these polytopes correspond to spike-to-edge arcs.
We also show that for
n
large, all the vertices are non-simple, i.e. have more incident facets than the ambient dimension.

5.1
Fully decorated ideal polygons

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\Pi=\widehat{\Pi}_{n}^{\Diamond}
, for
(n\geq 3)
and
m\in\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
.
Then all the vertices of the projectivised admissible cone
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
are given by
[f_{\alpha}(m)]
, where
\alpha
is a spike-to-edge arc.

Any vertex of
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}

Firstly, we prove that the second case is not possible.
Let

be an edge-to-edge arc,
separating the surface into
\Pi^{1},\Pi^{2}
.
Let

be the maximal family of hyperplanes containing

\mathcal{H}_{f_{\alpha}}=\{H_{\beta}\mid\beta\in\mathcal{H}(\Pi^{1})\sqcup\mathcal{H}(\Pi^{2})\},

Suppose that
\alpha

of

Let

be a (permitted) spike-to-edge arc obtained from

Consequently,

Next we prove that any spike-to-edge arc forms a vertex.
In the right panel of Fig.

Then

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\Pi=\widehat{\Pi}_{n}^{\Diamond}
and
m\in\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
.
Then for
n\geq 6
, all the vertices of
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
are non-simple.

Proof.

Let
v
be a vertex of
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
. It is non-simple if and only if it is contained in more than
2n-4
facets of
\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
. From Lemma
5.1
, we know that
v=[f_{\alpha}(m)]
, where
\alpha
is a spike-to-edge arc. From Lemma
4.1
, we know that every facet is of the form
H_{\beta}\cap\overline{\Lambda(m)}^{+}
, where
\beta
is a simple horoball connection. We also know that the point
v
is contained in a facet if and only if there is a
\alpha
is disjoint from
\beta
. So we need to show that for
n\geq 4
, there are at least
2n-4
horoball connections that are disjoint from
\alpha
.

Now, the arc
\alpha
divides the surface into two fully decorated polygons:
\Pi^{1}:=\widehat{\Pi}_{k}^{\Diamond}
and
\Pi^{2}:=\widehat{\Pi}_{n-k+1}^{\Diamond}
, where
2\leq k\leq n-1
. Then, the total number of horoball connections disjoint from
\alpha
is given by the
degree

\displaystyle d(k):=|H(\Pi^{1})|+|H(\Pi^{2})|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}[k(k-1)+(n-k+1)(n-k)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}[2k^{2}+n^{2}-2k-2nk+n].

Then

if and only if
(k,n)=(2,3),(2,4),(3,4),(3,5)
.
So we conclude that for
n\geq 6
, the total number of horoball connections disjoint from
\alpha
is more than
2n-4
, thus proving the lemma.

Appendix A
Topology of arc complexes

Here we review some auxiliary material, especially the ballness results used in Section
3.3
to prove Proposition
3.3
.
Depending on the type of surface some of these proofs are scattered in the literature, but we provide a unified treatment here.

Topological surfaces.

For
n\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{n}
a closed disk with
n\geq 1
marked points on its boundary. For
n\geq 3
, this surface, when endowed with a convex Euclidean metric, becomes the usual convex polygon.
For
n\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\times}
, a closed once-punctured disk with
n\geq 1
marked points on its boundary.
For
n\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\circledcirc}
, the closed disk
\mathcal{P}_{n}
with one marked point in its interior labeled as
0
. We will refer to this topological surface
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\circledcirc}
as an orientable
crown
.
For
n\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathcal{M}_{n}
the Möbius strip with
n
marked point on its boundary. We will refer to this surface as the
non-orientable crown
.

Given a surface
S
of the four above types, we consider all possible colourings of its marked points with two colours, say red
(R)
and blue
(B)
, so that there is a vertex of each colour. Furthermore, in the case of orientable crowns, the internal marked point is always colored blue. Such a colouring is called a
bicoloring
. A bicoloring is called
non-trivial
if there is at least one
R-R
diagonal and at least one
R-B
diagonal.
We denote by
\mathcal{Y}({S})
the subcomplex of the arc complex
\mathcal{A}({S})
spanned by the
R-B
and
B-B
diagonals only. We call these diagonals
permitted
and the
R-R
diagonals are called
rejected
. A simplex of
\mathcal{Y}({S})
using permitted diagonals is called
permissible
.

Definition A.1
.

In an orientable crown
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\circledcirc}
, any arc that does not have an endpoint on the vertex
0
, is called a
boundary arc
or a b-arc in short. In a non-orientable crown
\mathcal{M}_{n}
, any arc that decomposes the strip into one orientable and one non-orientable subsurface is called a boundary arc or b-arc. In either case, a non-boundary arc is called
core arc
or simply
c-arc
.

Remark A.2
.

A c-arc of a non-orientable crown
\mathcal{M}_{n}
always intersects its one-sided core curve. Similarly, any c-arc of a one-holed polygon
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\circledcirc}
intersects the two-sided core curve of
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\circledcirc}\smallsetminus\{0\}
.

Notation A.3
.

•

A b-arc of a (resp. non-orientable) crown
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\circledcirc}
(resp.
\mathcal{M}_{n}
) decomposes the surface into two tiles: one homeomorphic to a polygon and one homeomorphic to a (resp. non-orientable) crown with at most
n+1
vertices. Given two vertices
i,j
such that
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
, there are at most two b-arcs joining them. When
j-i>1
, let
a_{i}^{j}
be the b-arc whose polygonal tile is homeomorphic to
\mathcal{P}_{j-i+1}
, containing the vertices
i,i+1,\ldots,j
; its non-polygonal tile is homeomorphic to
\mathcal{P}_{n-j+i+1}^{\circledcirc}
(resp.
\mathcal{M}_{n-j+i+1}
), containing the vertices
j+1,\ldots,n,1\ldots,i
. When
j-i<n-1
, we denote the second b-arc by
a_{j}^{i}
.

•

Any c-arc of the surface
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\circledcirc}
joins the internal point
0
with some vertex
i\in\llbracket{1,n}\rrbracket
in the boundary. So these arcs are going to be denoted by
c_{i}
.

•

For
i\in\llbracket{1,n}\rrbracket
and
S=\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\circledcirc},\mathcal{M}_{n}
, we denote by
M_{i}
the maximal b-arc with both its endpoints at the vertex labeled
i
.

•

For
i\in\llbracket{1,n}\rrbracket
and
S=\mathcal{M}_{n}
, we denote by
L_{i}
the maximal c-arc with both its endpoints at the vertex labeled
i
.

•

Given two vertices
i,j
of
\mathcal{M}_{n}
such that
1\leq i<j\leq n
, we denote by
(i,j)
the unique c-arc joining these two vertices.

•

An arc of
S=\mathcal{P}_{n},\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\circledcirc},\mathcal{M}_{n}
is called
minimal
if it separates a disk homeomorphic to triangle
\mathcal{P}_{3}
from the surface.

•

The subcomplex
\mathcal{Y}_{C}(\mathcal{M}_{n})
of
\mathcal{Y}({\mathcal{M}_{n}})
generated by permitted c-arcs only is called the permitted internal arc complex.

•

In the orientable crown
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\circledcirc}
, let
\Delta_{n-1}
be the
(n-1)
-dimensional simplex of
\mathcal{A}({\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\circledcirc}})
generated by all the arcs that join one boundary marked point to the blue marked point in the interior. This simplex is always present in
\mathcal{Y}({\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\circledcirc}})
, irrespective of the bicoloring.

Definition A.4
.

Let
\sigma
be a simplex of the arc complex
\mathcal{A}({S})
of a surface
S
. Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
be pairwise disjoint and distinct arcs such that the 0-simplices of
\sigma

\sigma^{(0)}=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{k}\{[a_{i}]\}
.
Then we say that
\sigma
decomposes the surface

where
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{p}
are the connected components.

A.1
Previous results

A simplicial complex

is called a

d
-manifold with boundary
if the link of each 0-simplex is either a combinatorial
(d-1)
-sphere or a combinatorial
d-1
-ball and there exists a 0-simplex such that its link is of the latter kind.

Collapses

Let
X
be a simplicial complex and
\sigma
a simplex of
X
. The
face-deletion
of
\sigma
in

is the subcomplex defined as
\operatorname{fdel}(X,\sigma):=\{\eta\in X\mid\sigma\nsubseteq\eta\}
. In particular, the face deletion of a 0-simplex

in
X
is denoted by
X\smallsetminus\{v\}
.
Let
\sigma\subsetneq\tau\in X
be two simplices such that
\tau
is the only maximal simplex containing
\sigma
. Then
\sigma
is called a
free face
of
X
. The tuple
(\sigma,\tau)
is called a
collapsible pair
. The complex
X
is said to

onto its subcomplex
\operatorname{fdel}(X,\sigma)
, if
\sigma
is a free simplex of
X
. The complex
X
is said to be
collapsible
if there is a finite sequence of simplicial collapses leading to a 0-simplex. By
X\searrow Y
, we mean that the complex
X
simplicially collapses onto the complex

.
Whitehead
[
Whi39
]
showed that if

are two simplicial complexes such that
X\searrow Y
, then
X
has the same homotopy type as
Y
. A 0-simplex
v\in X
is said to be
vertex-dominated
if there exists another 0-simplex
v^{\prime}\in X
such that
\operatorname{Link}(v,X)=v^{\prime}\Join L,
where
L
is a subcomplex of
X
. In other words, any maximal simplex containing
v
must also contain
v^{\prime}
. A complex
X
is said to
strongly collapse
on

if the 0-simplex

is vertex-dominated in
X
. We will denote this operation as
X\Searrow X\smallsetminus\{v\}
.

The set

Now prove this claim by induction on

Base step:
The set
\mathcal{B}_{1}
comprises of all the permitted maximal arcs of
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\circledcirc}

\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\circledcirc}

Induction step:
Suppose that for

Corollary

Proof.

\sigma

\sigma

homeomorphic to

\displaystyle\dim\sigma

\displaystyle=k-4+(n-k+2-1)+2

\displaystyle=n-1.

.

(n-2)
-simplex

\sigma

\mathcal{M}_{1}

A.3
Bicolored non-orientable crown

Next we prove the ballness of the permitted arc complex of a bicolored

Theorem A.12

For

, the subcomplex
\mathcal{Y}({\mathcal{M}_{n}})
of the arc complex
\mathcal{A}({\mathcal{M}_{n}})
of a non-trivially bicolored non-orientable crown

n-1

In order to prove the above theorem, firstly we show that,

and every non-trivial bicoloring, the permitted inner arc complex

We prove the statement by induction on the number of vertices
n
.
When

1
and one red vertex at
2
. In this case, the inner arc complex is the 1-simplex generated by
[L_{1}]
and
(1,2)

Next we suppose that the statement holds for all
n^{\prime}\in\llbracket{1,n}\rrbracket
and for all non-trivial bicolorings. Given the surface
\mathcal{M}_{n}

-simplex generated by the permitted c-arcs
