Pre-pregnancy BMI and gestational weight gain: determinants of birthweight of term neonates in central Sri Lanka, a low-middle-income country
Authors: M. G. D. V. K. Kiridana, P.D.M.K.C. Pathirage, K.R.P Karunathilake, G.U Vidanapathirana, V.P Wickramasinghe, C Rathnayake, S. U.B Tennakoon, N.S. Kalupahana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) is increasing across South Asia, yet maternal underweight also remains a persistent public health concern. Low birthweight (LBW) of newborns too remains a challenge amidst high maternal BMI. Understanding the impact of maternal BMI and gestational weight gain (GWG) on offspring weight is clinically relevant in the setting of double burden of malnutrition, which is underexplored in low-income populations, creating a research gap.MethodsThis prospective cohort study was conducted at Teaching Hospital Peradeniya, Sri Lanka on 1537 pregnant women and their term newborns, to evaluate the association of maternal BMI and GWG with newborn weight across different maternal BMI categories. Pregnant women were recruited by convenient sampling technique from August 2021 to September 2023 and followed up through pregnancy. Newborns were measured at birth. Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS version 27 using Multiple Linear Regression, Spearman Correlation, and Binary Logistic Regression.ResultsPre-pregnancy BMI was higher in, 54.8% and lower in 13.8% of women, respectively. GWG in 55.4% was below the Institute of Medicine recommendation while it was higher in 11.2%. Of the newborns, 14.8% had LBW and 6.12% had high birthweight (HBW). Odds were significantly higher for a high BMI woman to have a HBW neonate (aOR = 2.147; 95% CI: 1.127–4.091, p < 0.05) and an underweight woman to have a LBW neonate (aOR = 1.989 95% CI: 1.184–3.340, p < 0.05) compared to a normal BMI woman. The odds were significantly higher to have LBW newborns to women with inadequate GWG, compared to women with normal GWG (aOR = 1.568, 95% CI: 1.054–2.333, p < 0.05). Both maternal pre-pregnancy BMI (β = 17.44,95% CI: 11.621–23.259) and GWG (β = 14.424, 95% CI: 9.986–18.861) significantly predicted newborn birthweight (p < 0.05).ConclusionPre-pregnancy BMI and GWG positively predicted newborn birthweight. Both high and low pre-pregnancy BMI are associated with HBW and LBW, respectively and inadequate GWG is associated with LBW newborns. Strategic planning to achieve healthy pre-pregnancy BMI among women of childbearing age and to optimize GWG across all maternal BMI categories are recommended as priorities in delivering maternal and child healthcare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28808-w.

Introduction

Birthweight of an individual is an important determinant of future health outcome [1]. Normal birthweight (NBW) is defined as a birthweight between 2.50 and 3.50 kg. Being born < 2.50 kg is defined as low birthweight (LBW) [2] and is either caused by a preterm birth or small for gestational age or both [3]. According to the global estimates of the WHO and UNICEF, nearly 20 million LBW infants are born annually, resulting in approximately 15% prevalence of LBW worldwide [4, 5].

LBW is a risk factor for increased neonatal morbidity and mortality. Additionally, the prevalence of LBW serves as an indicator of a country’s maternal health and nutritional status. Reduction of LBW by 30% is one of the global nutrition targets of the WHO and is expected to be achieved by 2030 [6].As southern Asia contributes to approximately half of the global burden of LBW, determining the contributory factors towards LBW is a priority in relevant countries including Sri Lanka. Determinants of LBW in Sri Lanka have been reported previously through extended analysis of Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) 2006/2007 data where a slow downward trend in LBW has been found [7].

In a different context, the global epidemic of obesity among women in the childbearing age has also emerged as a major health concern in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) in South Asia. While pre-pregnancy underweight is implicated in causing pre-term birth (being born less than 37 weeks of gestation), LBW, and small-for-gestational-age (SGA) (birthweight less than 10th percentile for the gestational age) in the offspring, pre-pregnancy obesity is shown to increase the risk for high birthweight (HBW), (defined as a birthweight > 3.50 kg), large for gestational age (LGA) (defined as birthweight > 90th percentile), and macrosomia (defined as birthweight > 4.00 kg) [2]. Maternal obesity can also result in SGA births with or without fetal growth restriction and several studies have shown that a high maternal BMI confers a multi-directional impact on newborn outcomes [8]. According to the concept of Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD), being born LGA or SGA makes a child vulnerable to developing obesity and adverse metabolic outcomes later in life [9].

In addition to pre-pregnancy body-mass-index (BMI), gestational weight gain (GWG) has also been implicated as a crucial factor determining the offspring’s health. A desirable weight gain during gestation is considered effective in achieving optimal fetal growth as well as a healthy body composition in later life. Suboptimal GWG continues to be a concern in LMIC countries which are likely to be contributing towards LBW. Prior studies from Sri Lanka have recorded inadequate GWG in 57.0% of women and excessive GWG in 8.7% [10]. Since maternal weight and GWG are modifiable factors that affect foetal growth, and subsequent offspring health, understanding their effect on newborn birthweight would be beneficial in planning robust strategies to mitigate both low and high birthweights in newborns and achieve global nutrition targets. Hence, this prospective cohort study was conducted to evaluate the association of maternal pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG with birthweight of term-born infants in central Sri Lanka.

Methods

This prospective cohort study was conducted to evaluate the association of maternal pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG with birthweight of term newborns. The study setting was the Teaching Hospital Peradeniya (THP), a tertiary health care institute in the central province of Sri Lanka, the second most populated province in the country, consisting of a multi-ethnic community. Pregnant women were recruited by convenient sampling technique from the antenatal clinic of THP between August 2021 and September 2023. A total of 1537 pregnant women who consented were recruited for a precision of 5% and a confidence level of 95% [11]. Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, University of Peradeniya (2020/EC/49). The study was conducted according to the principles of the Declarations of Helsinki.

Women with diagnosed chronic medical illnesses, psychiatric disease, multiple pregnancies, miscarriages, still births and who were unable to follow up were excluded. Their parity, socio-demographic characteristics, along with their employment status, household income, medical history, behavioral habits of pregnant women such as screen time and sleeping hours per day and maternal food habits including the frequency of sweet, salty and oily food intake were taken from an interviewer-administered questionnaire by medically trained research assistants. Pregnant women were followed up until confinement. Only 1143 mother-infant pairs were selected for the final analysis since some newborns were also excluded after birth due to pre-term births (PoA ≤ 37 weeks), neonatal congenital anomalies and neonatal intensive care admissions (Additional File 1). Only the newborns born at term (PoA > 37 weeks), were recruited at birth. This study adheres to the Strengthening of the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology statement for cohort studies.

The weight of the pregnant woman measured at the first antenatal visit to the Medical Officer of Health clinic was used as the proxy for pre-pregnancy weight and was obtained from the pregnancy record. Mothers’ height was measured at the time of interview using a standard stadiometer, and the BMI was calculated based on the above measurements. Using the WHO BMI cutoffs for Asian populations, pregnant women were categorized into obese (> 27.5 kgm− 2), overweight (23.0–27.5 kgm− 2), normal (18.5–22.9 kgm− 2), and low (< 18.5 kgm− 2) [12] based on their BMI value at booking visit, equating it to be their pre-pregnancy BMI [13]. The height of the women was categorized as short if it was < 1.45 m, and as tall stature if the height was > 1.55 m [14]. Total GWG was calculated by subtracting the pre-pregnancy weight, from the weight measured on admission for delivery. GWG was classified as inadequate and excess according to the IOM recommendation [15]. The adequate gestational weight gain was defined as: 12.7–18.1 kg for the underweight; 11.3–15.9 kg for the normal, 6.8–11.3 kg for the overweight and 5–9.1 kg for obese women categorized according to pre-pregnancy BMI. Newborn infants were classified into the three birthweights: HBW (≥ 3.50 kg), NBW (2.50–3.49 kg), and LBW (< 2.50 kg) [2]. Birthweight was measured to the nearest 0.01 kg using an electronic scale (Seca 334, Germany).

For the statistical analysis, IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 was used with p < 0.05 as the significance level at a 95% confidence interval (CI). Exposure variables such as pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG were assessed before the occurrence of the outcome (newborn birthweight) which is consistent with a cohort study.

The normality of the continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and presented with medians with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Maternal age, ethnicity, parity, maternal height, monthly family income and gender of the neonate were considered as potential associated factors that determine newborn birthweight, based on previous literature and a priori based on biological plausibility.

Bivariate analysis was initially performed to find the unadjusted associations. Spearman’s correlation test was performed to assess the correlation between two continuous variables such as newborn birthweight and maternal pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG. Either Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test was performed to find the differences in the median NBW and median GWG across different categorical variable groups such as ethnicity, maternal pre-pregnancy BMI etc. The associations between different categorical variables were evaluated using Chi-Squared test of Independence. Binary Logistic regression models were fitted to find the adjusted odds ratios (aOR) as the main effect measure with 95% CI while keeping LBW and HBW newborns as outcomes. Modified Poisson regression model was fitted to find the adjusted relative risk (aRR). Maternal pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG were considered the main exposure variables, while the above-mentioned confounding factors were also simultaneously added to the model. Normal pre-pregnancy BMI group and normal GWG group were considered the reference group.

Multiple linear regression was performed to assess the independent effect of pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG on newborn birthweight included as a continuous variable. Above-mentioned confounding factors were added as the covariates.

Missing data was imputed using Multiple Imputations with the assumption of missing at random.

Results

Sociodemographic details of the pregnant mothers included in the study are presented in Table 1. Table 2 presents the distribution and relationship of maternal characteristics between birthweight categories. The median pre-pregnancy BMI was 23.2 kgm− 2 (17.6–26.4). Of the pregnant women, 54.8% (n = 627) had high BMI with 35.9% (n = 410) being overweight and 19.0% (n = 217) obese (Table 1). Only 13.8% (n = 158) were underweight prior to pregnancy. The median newborn birthweight was 2.9 kg (2.4–3.2) with 79.1% (n = 904) having a NBW, while 14.8% (n = 169) and 6.1% (n = 70) of the neonates were LBW and HBW, respectively (Table 2). GWG was recorded only in 1060 pregnant women. Out of them, 56.4% (n = 598) had inadequate, and 11.7% (n = 124) had excess GWG (Table 1).

Birthweight of neonates born to underweight women was significantly lower than those born to women of other BMI categories (p = 0.001). There was a significant weak positive correlation between pre-pregnancy BMI and newborn birthweight (r = 0.137; p = 0.001). Maternal height also showed a significant weak positive correlation with newborn birthweight (r = 0.217, p = 0.001).

Table 4 presents the adjusted multiple linear regression analysis, that shows maternal pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, maternal height, parity, maternal ethnicity and gender of the newborns statistically and significantly predicted newborn birthweight (F (10, 1050) = 12.441, p = 0.001, R2 = 0.106). Each 1 kgm-2 increase in pre-pregnancy BMI would lead to 17.4 g increase in offspring birthweight (β = 17.44,95% CI: 11.621-23.259, p = 0.001), while each 1 kg increase in GWG would lead to 14.4 g increase in offspring birthweight (β = 14.424, 95% CI: 9.986- 18.861, p = 0.001) (Table 4).

Furthermore, inadequate GWG was significantly associated with higher likelihood of delivering LBW newborns compared to adequate GWG. Discussion

Analysis of this prospective cohort study reveals that maternal pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, maternal height, ethnicity and parity are significant predictors of newborn birthweight. The birthweight of term-born infants tends to be higher with increasing maternal BMI and GWG. HBW were more in the high BMI group of mothers, and LBW births were more in the low BMI group, demonstrating a linear trend.

The present study found a prevalence of 14.8% of LBW, and 6.12% of HBW. Mettananda et al. in their recent multicenter Sri Lankan study reported a comparatively higher prevalence of LBW at 20.4% of which 64.0% and 37.0% were SGA and preterm respectively [16]. Several studies across the world have demonstrated a positive correlation between maternal BMI and newborn birthweight. A recent study from Russia found a moderate positive correlation between maternal pre-pregnancy BMI and newborn birthweight (r = 0.32; p< 0.001) [17]. They found that according to multiple linear regression analysis, both maternal weight and height were significant predictors of newborn birthweight (p< 0.001) [17]. A systematic review, and meta-analysis of nearly 1.6 million mothers across China reported an increased risk of SGA with low maternal BMI (OR = 1.75; 95% CI: 1.51–2.02; p< 0.001; I2 = 80.0%) [18]. They also found a statistically significant association between maternal overweight/obesity with macrosomia (OR = 1.91; 95% CI, 1.75–2.09; p < 0.001; I2 = 78.7%) and LGA (OR = 1.88 (95% CI: 1.64–2.15; p < 0.001; I2 = 79.1%).

Kramer et al. studied the temporal trends in mean SGA and LGA in the Canadian population over two decades, where they found a significant rise in LGA with a decline in SGA births in association with increases in maternal BMI, GWG, and a reduction in cigarette smoking [19]. While the rising trends of maternal BMI and offspring weight have become a temporal association in Western populations, the high prevalence of SGA irrespective of maternal BMI status is yet a concern in Southeast Asian populations, even in a background of high maternal BMI. However, with the current trend of rising maternal BMI, a changing pattern of offspring birthweight could be anticipated in these populations too.

Among 1143 pregnant mothers in the present study, 54.8% were found to be either overweight or obese, while low pre-pregnancy BMI was seen only in 13.8%. Our maternal obesity figures are much higher than a recent estimate by Kent et al., where Asia demonstrated the lowest prevalence of maternal obesity overweight/obesity: 28.5% (95% CI: 18.3–41.5%). [20] A recent Sri Lankan study among females within the 15–49-year age group reported prevalences of underweight at 11.3% and overweight and obesity at 33.1% and 12.9%, respectively [21].

Wickramasingha VP et al. reported of 30.0% prevalence of high pre-pregnancy BMI in a recent multi-region population study on the island [22]. This maternal cohort of the Sri Lanka Child Growth Cohort (SLCGC) and our study cohort share socio-demographic similarities. Further, the prevalence of underweight mothers was reported at rates of 15.0% and 13.8% in the two studies, respectively. Another recent study involving the Sri Lanka Mother and New-born Growth (S-MaNGro) cohort reported pre-pregnancy underweight, overweight, and obesity at 14.4%, 24.4%, and 7.9% respectively [23], demonstrating a similar trend with the present study. Notably higher prevalence of maternal obesity found in our population is likely explained by the Asian BMI cut-offs being used in our analysis. According to the Sri Lanka Field Handbook (2016), the prevalence underweight women has declined from 23.8% in 2012 to 18.8% in 2016 and the prevalence of overweight women (BMI > = 25 kg/m2) has increased from 16.2% to 23.7% during the same period [24]. This tendency of demographic transition may also explain the greater prevalence of maternal overweight and obesity among the present study population.

Out of the 627 high BMI mothers in our maternal cohort, only 7.9% (n = 50) gave birth to neonates with HBW, compared to 12.4% (n = 78) who delivered neonates with LBW. Maternal pre-pregnancy obesity is considered a risk factor for SGA in addition to LGA and macrosomia. Several of the prior studies have shown a positive relationship between maternal undernutrition and SGA, or LBW, whereas only a few studies have presented associations between maternal obesity and SGA [25]. Most of them have been retrospective studies. In concordance with our finding of the occurrence of LBW deliveries among 12.4% of high BMI mothers, a recent Polish study also demonstrated multiple birthweight outcomes of the newborn infants of overweight/obese mothers. Among obese women, they reported a 17-fold higher adjusted LBW risk (aOR = 17.42 (1.5 − 202.6), p= 0.022) [8]. Deshmukh et al. reported a significant occurrence of both LGA and SGA among 500 obese Indian women (p< 0.05) [25]. Nevertheless, several confounding factors, such as PIH, diabetes, and antepartum hemorrhage, have been identified that could influence the newborn birthweight [25]. Further studies are needed to investigate for factors affecting the foetal growth in high BMI mothers.

In our prospective cohort study, 56.4% (n = 598) of women had GWG below the IOM recommendation for BMI, while 11.7% (n = 124) had GWG above the recommendation, and it was evident that the pre-pregnancy BMI significantly predicted the GWG. However, further studies will be needed to clarify this association. The recent Sri Lankan study by Pathirathna et al. reported a higher occurrence of LBW among women with underweight BMI whose GWG fell below the IOM recommendation. However, no such association was seen among women in other BMI categories [23]. Inconsistency in GWG in relation to pre-pregnancy BMI is reported in many studies.

Regarding the GWG and newborn birthweight, we found a statistically significant positive correlation between birthweight and GWG (r = 0.135, p < 0.05,) which agrees with several studies. In our maternal cohort, 67.5% women who had newborns with LBW had inadequate GWG. Among the women in our study who delivered NBW newborns, only 32.5% (n = 275) had a record of normal GWG, while 55.3% (n = 468) achieved weight gains below, and 12.3% (n = 104) above the IOM recommendation. Overall inadequacy of GWG in LMICs has been reported in several other regional studies [26, 27].

Maternal height is a significant predictor of birthweight. Sri Lanka has reported of an average maternal height of 1.52 m in the DHS 2016 with a higher prevalence of short stature among socioeconomically disadvantaged communities in the central province [7]. The mean height of our maternal cohort was 1.55 m, though comparison with the national report is restricted by the spread of the sample. However, it’s important to explore whether the mean maternal height in the country has increased over the years and contributed towards reduction in LBW prevalence. It is widely believed that addressing the female child’s nutrition is a window of opportunity to uplift maternal nutritional status. National health promotion programs implemented thus far have shown proven benefit in improving the birthweight. These interventions may have enhanced the nutritional status of pregnant women and diminished the prevalence of birth defects [28, 29].

The present study found the newborn weight of primiparous mothers tend to be lower than multiparous women which is consistent with prior studies [30, 31]. This association was seen independent of their pre pregnancy BMI and GWG. Further research is needed to explore its occurrence. In addition, LBW showed a significant association with the Tamil ethnicity which consisted of 6.4% of the maternal sample. Labour force of tea estates in the central province consists of Tamil people who have been identified as a socioeconomically disadvantaged group in the country contributing towards national maternal and child health indices. However, our Tamil maternal sample consisted of both estate and non-estate community thus prevent us from making assumptions on the effect of ethnicity on term birthweight.

It is well evident from this study how maternal nutritional status affects the offspring’s birthweight. In countries like ours, affected by a double burden of malnutrition, maternal obesity poses an additional risk of LBW as a birth outcome in addition to HBW births. As adverse long-term health implications are common in individuals born either LBW or HBW, it is urgent to implement robust community-based interventions and antenatal care pathways to prevent both underweight and obesity among women of childbearing age and optimize GWG as measures of primordial prevention of NCDs in the long run. Enhancing the knowledge and awareness of GWG among pregnant women and women of reproductive age would also be impactful on achieving the targets. Studies have shown that the presence of knowledge on the GWG recommendation among pregnant women optimizes weight gain [32].

A recent study in the local setting has explored maternal perception about their own BMI status. They found that overweight pregnant women were the most likely to underestimate their weight status indicating that lack of perception of one’s weight status may contribute to the rising prevalence of high BMI among women in the childbearing age group in the country [33].

Strengths of this prospective cohort study are the diverse characteristics of the maternal and newborn populations, with mothers of low, normal, and high BMI categories achieving different GWGs, along with their newborn infants belonging to different birthweight categories. We have reported on the impact of maternal BMI, and GWG on newborn birthweight after adjusting maternal age, maternal height, maternal ethnicity, parity, and gender of the newborn as the confounding factors. A limitation of our study is that we could not adjust for all the potential confounding factors such as type of employment, intercurrent illnesses of the mother, level of physical activity, dietary habits and field level interventions that may have affected the GWG. Furthermore, pregnancy-related complications like pregnancy-induced hypertension, gestational diabetes, and anemia were not common among our maternal cohort which prevented us from obtaining solid evidence on the effect of these complications on GWG and newborn weight. We also did not analyze the effect of gestation period which might have a significant impact on the BW. In addition, as the study was conducted in the Central Province, the results may not be representative of the entire Sri Lankan population. While the present findings share similarity with reports from other regions in the country, it highlights the increasing tendency of maternal BMI in the studied population. Further multi-region prevalence studies will inform if this finding could be generalized.

Conclusions

Both maternal pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG significantly and positively predicted term newborn birthweight. This highlights the importance of public awareness and monitoring maternal nutrition preconceptionally and during pregnancy. It is recommended that national maternal health care programs address pre-pregnancy and inter- pregnancy periods to optimize maternal BMI and the pregnancy itself to achieve healthy weight gain. Strengthening interventions during the pre-pregnancy and antenatal period would enhance reducing both high and low birth weights which contribute towards both maternal and neonatal outcomes in central Sri Lanka.
