Closed-Form Input Design for Identification under Output Feedback with Perturbation Constraints
Jingwei Hu, Dave Zachariah, Torbjörn Wigren, Petre Stoica

Abstract
In many applications, system identification experiments must be performed under output feedback to ensure safety or to maintain system operation. In this paper, we consider the online design of informative experiments for ARMAX models by applying a bounded perturbation to the input signal generated by a fixed output feedback controller. Specifically, the design constrains the resulting output perturbation within user-specified limits and can be efficiently computed in closed form. We demonstrate the effectiveness of the method in two numerical experiments.



I
INTRODUCTION

The design of informative experiments is of central importance in system identification, especially when considering its role in control-oriented modeling
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. For such purposes, the optimal experiments under varying assumptions should be performed in closed-loop
[
1
,
3
,
4
]
. Existing methods for closed-loop input design are broadly divided into two classes: The first class applies external excitation signals, such as references or input perturbations
[
5
,
6
]
. The second class designs the controller and the excitation jointly to shape the input spectrum and the input-error cross-spectrum
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
. Closed-loop system identification has also been analyzed and unified under a common prediction error framework
[
8
]
.

Design in the frequency domain exploits the structure of asymptotic error expressions and the resulting spectrum is synthesized into a time-varying exogenous signal. For instance, the asymptotic error covariance of prediction error methods can be expressed as a frequency-domain integral
[
9
]
. In closed-loop settings, these expressions are reformulated in terms of the joint spectrum of input and prediction errors, which enable the design of an input spectrum
[
6
]
. Common approaches include finite-dimensional parameterizations of the input and controller spectra
[
6
,
7
]
, and partial correlation methods that enforce spectral validity through constrained correlation sequences
[
10
]
.

In contrast, time-domain methods – typically embedded within a model predictive control framework – are designed for operation under finite data and real-time constraints. For these methods, experiment design is coupled with the controller and the excitation is shaped using persistent excitation conditions or objectives based on information metrics
[
11
,
12
]
. Some formulations impose persistent excitation using matrix inequalities
[
13
]
, while others target application-oriented information thresholds
[
14
,
15
]
. A key motivation is sample efficiency: the ability to collect sufficient information for identification while minimizing disturbance, often allowing the excitation to be turned off once the estimation goal is met
[
11
]
. However, the tight coupling between excitation and control objectives often leaves limited flexibility to explicitly trade off information gain and system perturbation resulting from the experiment.

We propose a time-domain experiment design method for closed-loop systems with a fixed, known linear controller. Our contributions are:

•

The online design of a control input perturbation which limits the cost of experiment by incorporating user-defined constraints on the resulting output perturbation.

•

A computationally efficient closed-form solution to the design problem that uses estimates from a recursive prediction error method.

The method is readily applicable as it does not require any modification of the existing controller and can use existing prediction error methods. On the other hand, as more information is gathered about the system it enables the tuning of improved controllers
[
16
]
. Moreover, we propose a means of tracking the informativeness of the experiment online so that the input perturbations can be turned off once sufficient information has been collected. We further demonstrate that the method can effectively limit the output perturbation while gaining informative experiments, even matching the performance of standard, unconstrained pseudo-random binary signals (
prbs
). In the numerical experiments, we illustrate how this is achieved by a constraint-dependent distribution of signal energy in the frequency domain.

III
METHOD

A standard objective in input design is to minimize the error covariance matrix
P_{t+1}
of the estimate
\widehat{\theta}_{t+1}
. At each time step
t
, we aim to solve the following design problem:

\begin{split}\min_{d_{t}}&\>\det P_{t+1}\\
\text{s.t. }&\>d_{\min}\leq d_{t}\leq d_{\max}\\
&\>\delta_{\min}\leq\delta_{t+1}\leq\delta_{\max}.\\
\end{split}

(8)

III-A

Output perturbation constraints

The output perturbation is shaped by the unknown (load) sensitivity function
G_{d}

[
18
, ch. 11]
:

\delta_{t}=\underbrace{\frac{G(q;\theta)}{1+G(q;\theta)K(q)}}_{G_{d}(q;\theta)}d_{t}=\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}\widetilde{g}_{i}d_{t-i}.

(9)

Thus the constraint (
7
) can be imposed by deriving the impulse response of the sensitivity function.

Let the controller be expressed as
K(q)=\frac{L(q)}{M(q)}
, where

L(q)=\ell_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\ell_{i}q^{-i}\quad M(q)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{m}}m_{i}q^{-i}.

(10)

Then the sensitivity function (
9
) can be represented as:

G_{d}(q;\theta)=\frac{B(q;\theta)M(q)}{A(q;\theta)M(q)+B(q;\theta)L(q)}\equiv\frac{\widetilde{B}(q;\theta)}{\widetilde{A}(q;\theta)}

(11)

where the coefficients for the polynomials
\widetilde{A}
and
\widetilde{B}
and are given by convolutions:

\begin{split}\widetilde{b}_{i}&=\sum_{j=0}^{i}b_{j}m_{i-j},\\
\widetilde{a}_{i}&=\sum_{j=0}^{i}a_{j}m_{i-j}+\sum_{j=0}^{i}b_{j}\ell_{i-j},\end{split}

(12)

omitting
\theta
for notational convenience. These are readily computable given the parameter vector in (
3
).

To obtain the impulse response in (
9
), we use the relation

\widetilde{A}(q)G_{d}(q)=\widetilde{B}(q).

(13)

By multiplying with
d_{t}
on both sides, we obtain the relation:

\begin{split}\delta_{t}&=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{b}+n_{m}}\widetilde{b}_{i}d_{t-i}-\sum_{i=1}^{\max(n_{a}+n_{m},n_{b}+n_{\ell})}\widetilde{a}_{i}\delta_{t-i}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{b}+n_{m}}\widetilde{b}_{i}d_{t-i}-\sum_{i=1}^{\max(n_{a}+n_{m},n_{b}+n_{\ell})}\widetilde{a}_{i}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{g}_{j}d_{t-i-j}\Big).\end{split}

(14)

Comparing with (
9
), this gives a recursive relation for the impulse response:

\widetilde{g}_{i}=\widetilde{b}_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i}\widetilde{a}_{j}\widetilde{g}_{i-j},

where
\widetilde{g}_{0}=\widetilde{b}_{0}=0
.

Suppose the experiment starts at time
t-k+1
, then the output perturbation equals

\delta_{t+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{g}_{i}d_{t+1-i}=\widetilde{g}_{1}d_{t}+h_{t}

(15)

where

h_{t}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{g}_{2}&\widetilde{g}_{3}&\cdots&\widetilde{g}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}d_{t-1}\\
d_{t-2}\\
\vdots\\
d_{t-k+1}\end{bmatrix}

is fixed at time
t
. Eq. (
15
) enables expressing the output perturbation constraint in (
8
) as

\delta_{\min}-h_{t}(\theta)\leq\widetilde{g}_{1}(\theta)d_{t}\leq\delta_{\max}-h_{t}(\theta),

(16)

where we have made the dependence on
\theta
explicit.

We note that since the closed-loop system is assumed to be exponentially stable, the past samples
d_{t-k+1}
have negligible impact on
\delta_{t+1}
for large
k
. In the practical implementation of (
16
), one can therefore use a large but fixed horizon
k
and store only the last
k
samples of
d_{t}
. Then
\widetilde{g}_{1}(\theta)
and
h_{t}(\theta)
are re-evaluated with plug-in estimates
\widehat{\theta}_{t}
at each time step.

III-B

Closed-Form Solution

Under the prediction error framework, the large-sample error covariance matrix of
\widehat{\theta}_{t+1}
is given by

P_{t+1}=\frac{\lambda}{t+1}\big(\mathbb{E}[\psi_{t+1}\psi_{t+1}^{\top}]\big)^{-1},

where
\psi_{t+1}
is the (negative) gradient of the one-step-ahead prediction error
[
17
, ch 7.]
:

\psi_{t+1}\equiv-\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{t+1}}{\partial\theta}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left[\frac{A(q;\theta)}{C(q;\theta)}\widetilde{y}_{t+1}-\frac{B(q;\theta)}{C(q;\theta)}\widetilde{u}_{t+1}\right].

(17)

Since the element-wise derivatives are obtained by filtered signals

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{t+1}}{\partial b_{i}}&=-\frac{1}{C(q;\theta)}\widetilde{u}_{t-i+1}\\
\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{t+1}}{\partial a_{i}}&=\frac{1}{C(q;\theta)}\widetilde{y}_{t-i+1}\\
\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{t+1}}{\partial c_{i}}&=-\frac{1}{C(q;\theta)}\varepsilon_{t-i+1},\end{split}

(18)

the negative gradient in (
17
) can be computed recursively as:

\psi_{t+1}=\varphi_{t+1}-c_{1}\psi_{t}-\dots-c_{n_{c}}\psi_{t-n_{c}+1},

(19)

where we define the vector

\begin{split}\varphi_{t+1}&=[\widetilde{u}_{t},\dots,\widetilde{u}_{t-n_{b}+1},-\widetilde{y}_{t},\dots,-\widetilde{y}_{t-n_{a}+1},\\
&\qquad\varepsilon_{t},\dots,\varepsilon_{t-n_{c}+1}]^{\top},\end{split}

see also
[
3
, ch 10.3]
.

Let
\widehat{\psi}_{t+1}
denote the negative gradient evaluated at
\widehat{\theta}_{t}
and let
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{t}
be the estimated prediction error. Then using relation (
19
) with estimates enables the re-use of previous estimates and thus an efficient online evaluation. The unknown covariance matrix
P_{t+1}
is estimated as

\widehat{P}_{t+1}=\widehat{\lambda}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{t+1}\widehat{\psi}_{i}\widehat{\psi}_{i}^{\top}\Big)^{-1},

(20)

where
\widehat{\lambda}
denotes the estimated variance of
e_{t}
.

Using the estimated covariance matrix
\widehat{P}_{t+1}
and (
16
) in (
8
), the design problem can now be expressed as:

\begin{split}\min_{d_{t}}&\det(R^{-1}_{t}+\widehat{\psi}_{t+1}\widehat{\psi}_{t+1}^{\top})^{-1}\\
\text{s.t. }&d_{\min}\leq d_{t}\leq d_{\max}\\
&\delta_{\min}-h_{t}\leq\widetilde{g}_{1}d_{t}\leq\delta_{\max}-h_{t},\end{split}

(21)

where
R_{t}\equiv(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\widehat{\psi}_{i}\widehat{\psi}_{i}^{\top})^{-1}
can be computed recursively. To arrive at a closed-form solution of (
21
), we begin by partitioning the matrix
R_{t}
as

\begin{bmatrix}R_{11}&R_{12}\\
R_{12}^{\top}&R_{22}\end{bmatrix},

(22)

where

R_{11}>0
is a scalar. We split the gradient vector in similar corresponding manner:

\widehat{\psi}_{t+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\tau_{t+1}\\
\xi_{t+1}\end{bmatrix},

where
\tau_{t+1}
is a scalar.

Theorem 1

The perturbation that solves (
21
) is given by:

\boxed{d_{t}^{*}=\begin{cases}d_{l}&\text{if $-\frac{R_{12}\xi_{t+1}}{R_{11}}-\widehat{u}_{t}>\frac{d_{l}+d_{u}}{2}$}\\
d_{u}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}}

(23)

where

\widehat{u}_{t}\equiv u_{t}-\widehat{c}_{1}\tau_{t}-\dots-\widehat{c}_{n_{c}}\tau_{t-n_{c}+1},

d_{l}=\max\left(d_{\min},\ \min\left(\frac{\delta_{\min}-h_{t}}{\widetilde{g}_{1}},\ \frac{\delta_{\max}-h_{t}}{\widetilde{g}_{1}}\right)\right)

and

d_{u}=\min\left(d_{\max},\ \max\left(\frac{\delta_{\min}-h_{t}}{\widetilde{g}_{1}},\ \frac{\delta_{\max}-h_{t}}{\widetilde{g}_{1}}\right)\right).

Note that the quantities in (
23
) are evaluated using the current estimate
\widehat{\theta}_{t}
. The above expressions of
d_{l}
and
d_{u}
take into account the possibility of
\widetilde{g}_{1}
being negative.

Proof:

Using (
19
), we have that

\begin{split}\widehat{\psi}_{t+1}&=\begin{bmatrix}\tau_{t+1}\\
\xi_{t+1}\end{bmatrix}\\
&=\begin{bmatrix}\underbrace{u_{t}-\widehat{c}_{1}\tau_{t}-\dots-\widehat{c}_{n_{c}}\tau_{t-n_{c}+1}}_{\widehat{u}_{t}}\\
\xi_{t+1}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}d_{t}\\
0\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}

(24)

Next, using the matrix determinant lemma the objective in (
21

\begin{split}\det(R^{-1}_{t}+\widehat{\psi}_{t+1}\widehat{\psi}_{t+1}^{\top})^{-1}&=\det(R_{t})/(1+\widehat{\psi}_{t+1}^{\top}R_{t}\widehat{\psi}_{t+1}).\end{split}

It therefore suffices to maximize the quadratic form

\widehat{\psi}_{t+1}^{\top}R_{t}\widehat{\psi}_{t+1}\geq 0
. Substituting (

\begin{split}\max_{d_{t}}&\;R_{11}d_{t}^{2}+2(R_{11}\widehat{u}_{t}+R_{12}\xi_{t+1})d_{t}\\
\text{subject to}&\;d_{l}\leq d_{t}\leq d_{u}.\end{split}

(25)

The quadratic function is convex and symmetric with respect to the point:

Therefore its maximum is located at one of the boundaries of the feasible interval

, we can conclude

If

III-C

Tracking Informativeness of Experiment

Since the cost of the experiment in terms of output perturbation is constrained, it is possible to run longer experiments than with unconstrained designs. A user would however need to track their informativeness in order to determine when to stop.

One method is to track the estimation error of some property of the unknown system, e.g., its static gain:

G_{0}(\theta)\equiv\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{b}}b_{i}}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{a}}a_{j}}.

The error variance of the plug-in estimate
\widehat{G}_{0}
is then estimated using the first-order approximation:

\sigma^{2}(\theta)=\frac{\partial G_{0}}{\partial\theta}\>P_{t}\>\frac{\partial G_{0}}{\partial\theta}^{\top},

(26)

where the gradient is given by

\frac{\partial G_{0}}{\partial b_{i}}=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{a}}a_{j}},\frac{\partial G_{0}}{\partial a_{i}}=-\frac{G_{0}}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{a}}a_{j}},\frac{\partial G_{0}}{\partial c_{i}}=0.

Then

\rho^{\alpha}_{t}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\frac{\sigma(\widehat{\theta}_{t})}{|\widehat{G}_{0}|}\times 100

(27)

approximates the percentage error magnitude of
\widehat{G}_{0}
, relative to the size of an
(1-\alpha)
-level confidence interval. By tracking
\rho^{\alpha}_{t}
, the user obtains an estimate of how informative the experiment is for the static gain. Other related measures can be derived in a similar manner.

V
CONCLUSION

We have derived an online experiment design method for the identification of
armax
systems operating with output feedback controllers. The method yields informative perturbations to the input signal while accurately constraining the resulting output perturbation to user-defined levels, unlike standard
prbs
designs. It can be implemented in closed form with recursively computed quantities, making it a practical method for safe and efficient experiment design.
