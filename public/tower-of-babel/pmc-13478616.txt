Apodized fiber Bragg grating filters for efficient QKD channel separation in QKD-over-DWDM systems
Authors: Namwook Joe, Taeho Woo, Suckwoo Shin, Kyungtaek Lee, Ju Han Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study theoretically investigates the optimal filter configuration for separating a quantum key distribution (QKD) channel from classical C-band communication channels in a discrete-variable (DV) QKD-over-dense wavelength division multiplexing (DWDM) communication system with an O-band QKD channel. Three filter configurations are analyzed and compared in terms of quantum bit error rate (QBER), secret key rate (SKR), and fiber link distance: two cascaded 1310-nm bandpass filters, a 1310-nm uniform fiber Bragg grating (FBG) with a 1310-nm circulator, and a 1310-nm apodized FBG with a 1310-nm circulator. The findings reveal that the optical filter configuration incorporating a Gaussian apodized FBG exhibits the best performance with an isolation of ~ 93 dB between 1310 nm and 1550 nm wavelengths. This optimal performance can be attributed to the Gaussian apodization profile, which effectively suppresses spectral side-lobes to enhance channel isolation while maintaining a lower insertion loss compared to the cascaded bandpass filter configuration. For 50 classical WDM channels, the QKD fiber link distance is 35.4 km with a Gaussian apodized FBG, while a configuration incorporating two cascaded 1310-nm bandpass filters yields only 18.3 km. Finally, the impact of adopting a raised-cosine apodized FBG to further increase filter channel isolation is examined. Despite a substantial increase in channel isolation by using a raised-cosine apodized FBG filter, QKD fiber link distance yields negligible improvement for this system with a QKD channel in O-band, although this filter may be highly useful for QKD channels in C- or L-bands.

Introduction

Quantum key distribution (QKD) has been extensively investigated over the past decades as it enables the exchange of cryptographic keys with security guaranteed by the uncertainty principle. Since Bennett and Brassard proposed the first QKD protocol in 1984,1 numerous implementations of QKD have been explored across various communication links, including fiber-optic links,2–4 ground-to-air free-space links,5 terrestrial free-space links,6,7 and ground-to-satellite links.8,9.

Most fiber-based QKD demonstrations have relied on dedicated dark fibers, hindering their practicality. For scalable QKD deployments and expansion to point-to-point links to networked configurations, it is desirable to integrate QKD with existing fiber infrastructure alongside classical communication traffic.10 A major challenge for such implementation lies in separating QKD signals from classical wavelength division multiplexing (WDM) communication systems, a task that becomes particularly difficult in dense WDM (DWDM) topology.11–15 The state-of-the-art DWDM communication systems operate in the C-band with 100 GHz–50 GHz channel spacing on spectral grids for WDM applications of DWDM frequency grid.16 When QKD and DWDM classical channels coexist in a standard single-mode fiber (SMF28), the large difference between quantum and classical signals induces noise at the quantum receiver. First, the limited channel isolation performance of commercial WDM demultiplexers can cause a significant crosstalk noise from multiple classical DWDM channels.17 Second, Spontaneous Stokes and anti-Stokes Raman scattering from classical channels imposes considerable noise.18 Third, four-wave mixing (FWM) induced by DWDM classical channels further imposes significant noise on the quantum channel when the quantum channel wavelength is allocated near the classical channels.11 While FWM-induced noise is less invasive than that of Raman-induced noise, proper channel allocation is required to mitigate the impact of FWM secondary photons on the QKD channel. Fourth, erbium-doped fiber amplifiers (EDFAs) incorporated in practical DWDM communication systems generate substantial amplified spontaneous emission (ASE), which can be detrimental to QKD performance when QKD and classical channels share the same path. Therefore, a configuration of bypassing EDFA has been devised19; however, it introduces additional insertion loss and operational complexity to such an implementation.

The commonly employed QKD-over-DWDM communication systems are implemented through three schemes: (1) discrete-variable (DV) QKD channels in the O-band,20 (2) DV QKD channels in the C-band,21,22 (3) continuous-variable (CV) QKD channels in the C-band.23,24 The O-band allocation of QKD channels simplifies the implementation of such integrated systems by reducing classical channel crosstalk through a ~ 240 nm spectral separation between the classical communication and QKD channels. However, this scheme has a fundamental issue of limited quantum key propagation distance due to the high propagation loss of standard single-mode optical fiber at 1300 nm. In contrast, the C-band allocation of QKD channels enables longer QKD transmission distances than O-band implementations. However, they face noise issues from classical channel crosstalk, ASE of EDFA, spontaneous Raman scattering, and four-wave mixing crosstalk. To mitigate these effects, a time-gated quantum key detection scheme for DV QKD channels needs to be employed,18 while a coherent quantum key detection scheme is required for CV QKD channels.25.

Effective implementation of these systems requires high-performance optical filters at the receiver to ensure strong channel isolation, regardless of whether QKD channels are placed in the O- or C-band. Commonly used optical filters or demultiplexers for the separation of QKD channels from classical DWDM communication channels are based on arrayed waveguide gratings (AWGs)26 or liquid-crystal on silicon (LCoS) wavelength-selective switches (WSSs).27 However, the channel isolation of commercially available AWGs or LCoS-WSSs is only 25 ~ 50 dB,26 with a few AWG-based demultiplexers achieving up to ~ 80 dB.17 Recently, optical fiber Bragg gratings (FBGs) have been explored28 as a low-cost alternative to traditional AWG- or WSS-based filters. However, their limited isolation requires cascaded configurations of multiple bandpass filtering devices to sufficiently separate QKD and classical communication channels, which reduces QKD transmission distance and increases filter insertion loss, since the channel isolation values of the filtering devices are not high enough for successful recovery of transmitted quantum keys.

It is well-known in the field of fiber-optic communication systems that the apodized FBG filter can provide high channel isolation and high side-lobe suppression, which are suitable for communication channel isolation in DWDM systems.29 However, apodized FBG filters have rarely been employed in practical DWDM communication systems since they do not allow for a multi-wavelength channel demultiplexing function, which is essential for DWDM systems. Note that AWGs or WSSs are commonly used as practical filtering devices for DWDM systems. The motivation of this work is that an apodized FBG filter with high channel isolation could be an excellent filtering device for such systems, in which the separation of a QKD channel does not require a multi-wavelength channel demultiplexing function. To the best of the authors’ knowledge there has been no investigation of using apodized FBG filters for this application.

This study theoretically evaluates the potential of using a single filter based on apodized FBGs as an alternative to cascaded configurations of multiple filtering devices for separating QKD and DWDM classical communication channels. This analysis focuses on a DV QKD-over-DWDM communication system with a QKD channel in the O-band, comparing three filter configurations: two cascaded 1310-nm bandpass filters, a 1310-nm uniform FBG with a 1310-nm fiber-optic circulator, and a 1310-nm apodized FBG with a 1310-nm fiber-optic circulator. The system performance of the filter configurations is assessed in terms of quantum bit error rate (QBER), secret key rate (SKR), and the corresponding fiber link distance. Results reveal that a Gaussian apodized FBG with a fiber-optic circulator achieves a channel isolation of ~ 93 dB between 1310 nm and 1550 nm wavelengths, sufficient for reliable QKD channel detection. This design outperforms both configurations of two cascaded 1310-nm bandpass filters and a 1310-nm uniform FBG with a 1310-nm fiber-optic circulator. Finally, this study also investigates the impact of a further increase in FBG filter channel isolation between QKD and DWDM classical channels by adopting a raised-cosine apodization.

System configuration

Schematic of QKD-over-DWDM Systems

This study considers a BB84 QKD protocol integrated into a DWDM communication system with a QKD channel in the O-band (Fig. 1). The QKD channel is assigned a wavelength of 1310 nm, while the classical DWDM communication channels follow the wavelengths satisfying the DWDM 100 GHz ITU Grid, in which the channel separation wavelength interval is 0.8 nm. The classical communication channels are in the C-band (1528.77 ~ 1568.36 nm), including two distillation channels, two data encryption channels, and additional classical communication channels. To better reflect practical WDM telecommunication conditions, the average optical power of each classical WDM channel is set to 0 dBm at the input end of a fiber link, unlike previous demonstrations for such hybrid quantum-classical architectures22 that reduced the average optical power of each C-band WDM channel to below − 20 dBm at the transmitter. In the proposed system configuration (Fig. 1), the average optical power of each C-band WDM channel at the transmitter is 5.7 dBm. Classical DWDM communication channels are multiplexed and demultiplexed at the Alice and Bob sides using a single AWG, with the number of downstream and upstream channels N varying from 2 to 25 to evaluate the performance of our proposed QKD channel filter. The QKD channel is assumed to have only a downstream transmission, with the classical DWDM communication channels and the downstream QKD channel multiplexed and demultiplexed through a 1310/1550-nm wavelength division multiplexer (WDM). On the Bob side, the QKD channel is separated from classical DWDM communication channels using a high-isolation filter after the WDM demultiplexer (DEMUX). The filtered QKD signal is detected with a QKD receiver based on a single photon avalanche photodiode (SAPD). Optical filter configurations for QKD channel

Effective QKD channel separation in such an integrated system requires a high-isolation bandpass filter. Since commercially available optical thin-film bandpass filters exhibit a channel isolation of approximately 30 ~ 40 dB, even though a 1310-nm QKD channel is located ~ 240 nm away from the C-band WDM channels, using a single, commercial bandpass filter cannot provide a channel isolation sufficient to effectively suppress the classical WDM channel-induced crosstalks. To address this limitation, we propose the use of an apodized FBG. The fabrication of FBGs is a well-established technique in optical communications. In particular, Gaussian apodized FBGs are currently available as commercial products from various manufacturers. This study evaluates three optical filter configurations for QKD channel separation: two cascaded bandpass filters (Fig. 2(a)), a uniform 1310-nm FBG with a 1310-nm centered fiber-optic circulator (Fig. 2(b)), and the proposed apodized 1310-nm FBG with a 1310-nm centered fiber-optic circulator (Fig. 2(c)). The insertion loss of a bandpass filter is assumed to be 1.2 dB at 1310 nm, considering the commercially available bandpass filter parameters. The insertion losses of a fiber-optic circulator are set at 1.2 dB. The channel isolation between the 1310-nm and 1550-nm wavelengths is set to be 8 dB between Ports 1 and 2 or between Ports 2 and 3. The insertion loss and channel isolation values of the fiber-optic circulator are confirmed by experimentally measuring the parameters of a commercially available 1310-nm fiber-optic circulator.

The FBG length, peak reflectivity, center wavelength, and 3-dB bandwidth are set at 14 mm, 99.9%, 1310 nm, and ~ 0.56 nm, respectively. The FBG length of 14 mm was selected based on practical fabrication constraints provided by commercial manufacturers. To ensure a saturated reflectivity of over 99.9%, a grating length of at least 10 mm is typically required. Considering that a typical phase mask has a length of 15 mm with a maximum effective grating length of approximately 14 mm, we adopted 14 mm as the upper limit for the design. The refractive index modulation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\Delta\:}n$$\end{document} of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:3\times\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document} is sufficient to achieve high reflectivity and isolation, and is readily attainable in high-germanium fibers, boron-germanium-codoped fibers, or two mentioned fibers hydrogenated. Regarding sensitivity, our analysis indicates that for a design target of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\Delta\:}n=3\times\:\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document}, a fabrication tolerance of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\pm\:\:5\%$$\end{document} results in a maximum channel isolation deviation of only 0.5 dB. However, if the error increases to 10% (specifically a reduction in modulation), the reflectivity may decrease for the fixed 14 mm grating length, which could potentially lead to increased signal insertion loss. However, FBGs designed for high refractive index modulation—typically using high-germanium doped fibers—exhibit higher thermal sensitivity compared to those fabricated with standard SMF. Assuming a thermal sensitivity of 9.7 pm/°C for a standard SMF-based FBG,34 a temperature rise of 50 °C induces a center wavelength shift of approximately 0.48 nm. In contrast, high germanium-doped fibers experience a larger wavelength shift under the same thermal conditions due to their increased thermo-optic coefficient. Consequently, considering the limited FBG bandwidth, the use of high-germanium fibers implies that signal misalignment could potentially occur at temperature variations approaching 50 °C, suggesting that thermal stability is a factor to be considered for optimal operation. In our proposed system, the spectral separation between the target QKD channel (1310 nm) and the classical channel (1550 nm) is approximately 240 nm. Due to this large channel spacing, variations in channel isolation caused by strain induced wavelength shifts are negligible.

When evaluating the impact of fabrication tolerance on system performance, decreasing the FBG length from 14 mm to 10 mm (at a fixed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\Delta\:}n$$\end{document} of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:3\times\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document}) maintained the reflectivity above 99.9% and resulted in a negligible isolation drop of only ~ 0.2 dB, leaving the QBER and SKR unaffected. When the external environment requires such high channel isolation safety, the use of the raised-cosine apodization filter may be considered.

However, our design utilized a sufficiently large refractive index modulation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\Delta\:}n\ge\:3\times\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document}) and grating length (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{g}$$\end{document} = 14 mm) to operate in the saturated reflectivity regime. As shown in Fig. 5, our simulation confirmed that even with Gaussian or raised-cosine apodization, the peak reflectivity remained above 99.9%. Consequently, the excessive insertion loss caused by apodization is negligible (< 0.04 dB). Moreover, although the passband width narrows slightly due to apodization, it remains sufficient (~0.56 nm) to accommodate the QKD signal linewidth.

Theoretically, the maximum allowable QBER for secret key distillation in the BB84 QKD protocol is 11% with consideration of error correction and privacy amplification.22,36 In practice, this value is lower than the theoretical value due to finite-size effects and non-ideal error-correction efficiency. A practical value of 9% is adopted, consistent with the experimental setup in [22]. The distillation limit (dashed line in figure) indicates the maximum QBER that can be distilled, and serves as a benchmark for various filter configurations. For secret keys that are essential for QKD channel encryption, a 256-bit secret key is assumed to be updated every 60 s to divide the symmetric key between Alice and Bob, requiring a SKR of 256 bits/60 seconds = 4.27 bps per channel. Conclusion

This study theoretically investigated the optimum filter configuration for separating the QKD channel from classical C-band WDM channels in a DV QKD-over-DWDM communication system with a QKD channel in the O-band. Specifically, this work considered a standard optical communication environment where the optical power of classical channels are not attenuated purposely, aiming to address a more realistic implementation scenario. Three filter configurations are evaluated in terms of QBER, SKR, and fiber link distance: two cascaded 1310-nm bandpass filters, a 1310-nm uniform FBG and a 1310-nm fiber-optic circulator, and a 1310-nm apodized FBG and a 1310-nm fiber-optic circulator. The optical filter configuration composed of a Gaussian apodized FBG and a 1310-nm fiber-optic circulator achieves channel isolation of ~ 93 dB between 1310 nm and 1550 nm wavelengths, sufficient for reliable detection of QKD channels with multiple classical WDM channels. With 50 classical WDM channels, the QKD fiber link distance is 35.4 km, nearly matching the 36.6 km distance achieved with only four classical C-band WDM channels, which can be used for data encryption and distillation in our proposed architecture. This fiber link distance represents a substantial enhancement over the conventional filter configuration of two cascaded 1310-nm bandpass filters, which yield 18.3 km with 50 WDM channels and 31.0 km with four WDM channels.

Finally, the impact of further increasing FBG filter channel isolation by adopting a raised-cosine function was also investigated. For the investigated system configuration with a QKD channel in the O-band, no significant improvement in QKD fiber link distance is observed, despite its high channel isolation performance (210 dB). However, such a high isolation filter from raised-cosine apodized FBG would be highly useful for QKD channel separation in QKD-over-WDM systems with QKD channels in the C- or L-bands. In such cases, a Gaussian apodized FBG cannot provide sufficient channel isolation when the wavelength separation between QKD and classical WDM channels is very small.
