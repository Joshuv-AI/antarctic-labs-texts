KnowledgeTrail: Generative Timeline for Exploration and Sensemaking of Historical Events and Knowledge Formation CCS: Human-centered computing Visualization systems and tools CCS: Human-centered computing Graphical user interfaces CCS: Human-centered computing Empirical studies in interaction design
Sangho Suh, Rahul Hingorani, Bryan Wang, Tovi Grossman

Abstract
. The landscape of interactive systems is shifting toward dynamic, generative experiences that empower users to explore and construct knowledge in real time. Yet, timelines—a fundamental tool for representing historical and conceptual development—remain largely static, limiting user agency and curiosity. We introduce the concept of a generative timeline: an AI-powered timeline that adapts to users’ evolving questions by expanding or contracting in response to input. We instantiate this concept through KnowledgeTrail, a system that enables users to co-construct timelines of historical events and knowledge formation processes. Two user studies showed that KnowledgeTrail fosters curiosity-driven exploration, serendipitous discovery, and the ability to trace complex relationships between ideas and events, while citation features supported verification yet revealed fragile trust shaped by perceptions of source credibility. We contribute a vision for generative timelines as a new class of exploratory interface, along with design insights for balancing serendipity and credibility.

1.
Introduction

In an era where generative AI is transforming how people search, learn, and create, interactive systems are beginning to shift from static information presentation toward more dynamic, exploratory experiences. From diagram generation and semantic search to interactive storytelling and design tools
(
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Suh et al. 2023
;
Suh et al. 2025
)
, we see growing interest in systems that help users make sense of complex information spaces by generating and adapting content on demand. However, many foundational interfaces—such as timelines—have remained largely static, constrained by pre-curated content and linear presentation.
How would we design a system that can guide users to build their own timelines as they explore a topic? What would the exploration pattern look like? What would they need?

Timelines are visual representations that have long served as powerful cognitive artifacts for organizing information temporally, helping people identify sequences, patterns, and historical context. However, traditional timelines are constrained by pre-curated content, offering only a limited view into a topic and failing to adapt to evolving questions and needs. Even when they stimulate curiosity, they cannot support users in following that curiosity beyond the existing set of data. This makes them ill-suited for open-ended learning, where exploration and connection-making are essential.

This gap is especially salient in light of constructivist learning theories, which emphasize that learners actively build understanding through inquiry and sensemaking. As generative systems open new possibilities for responsive, user-driven interactions, we have an opportunity to reimagine the timeline not as a static artifact, but as a dynamic interface for exploring knowledge alongside the user’s evolving interests
(
Suh et al. 2023
)
.

To take advantage of this opportunity, we explore the notion and potential of generative timeline: a timeline that grows, shrinks, and restructures in response to user exploration. Powered by large language models (LLMs), generative timelines enable users to pose questions, generate new events, and uncover relationships between them—turning the timeline into a co-creative space. This reimagines timelines not just as tools for presentation, but as canvases for dynamic exploration and evolving understanding.

To explore this potential and understand how to design for it, we developed KnowledgeTrail, an interactive system that instantiates generative timelines. KnowledgeTrail allows users to explore both breadth and depth of topics, tracing how historical events, theories, discoveries, and socio-political shifts connect across time.

We conducted a two-part user study to evaluate KnowledgeTrail. The first study, with eight participants, examined how the system supports exploration, sensemaking, and engagement. Participants used KnowledgeTrail to investigate topics, compare their experience to a baseline (ChatGPT), and reflect on the usefulness of dynamic timeline exploration. The second study, with four participants, focused on how the integration of source citations impact their trust in AI-generated information and overall experience with KnowledgeTrail. Together, these studies provide insights into building and designing dynamic timeline-based systems. In summary, our contributions include:

•

The concept of a
generative timeline
, which leverages generative AI to move beyond static representations and enable timelines that can dynamically expand, contract, and reorganize in response to user input.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Timeline: Visualizing Sequence, Duration, and Chronological & Genealogical Relationships

The timeline representation was first introduced by Joseph Priestly in his 1765 book
A Chart of Biography
, which enabled us to depart from the then-predominant format of text, which makes it hard to discern the connections between the ideas and events webbed across the text. By displaying elements in a sequential order and spatially, it gave us a graphic tool that can effectively illustrate not just dates but also the sequence, duration, and relationships between elements over time
(
Shipman et al. 2001
;
Grossman et al. 2010
;
Burch et al. 2008
;
Plaisant et al. 1996
;
Nguyen et al. 2014
;
Kerr 2003
;
Bui et al. 2007
;
Zhao et al. 2012
)
. Fast forward to modern day, many researchers built on this affordance to develop interactive systems. For instance, Chronicle
(
Grossman et al. 2010
)
uses a timeline for exploring graphical document workflows. Lifelines
(
Plaisant et al. 1996
)
provides interactive timeline visualizations of personal life events, and SchemaLine
(
Nguyen et al. 2014
)
uses timelines to enhance the sense-making process.

Timelines have also been applied to genealogical relationships, supporting the study of family trees, research lineages, and the evolution of ideas
(
McGuffin and Balakrishnan 2005
;
Liu et al. 2017
;
Matejka et al. 2012
;
Wang et al. 2019b
)
. Links or arrows are often used to illustrate connections and relationships between elements and convey the tracing of the history (path). For example, GenealogyVis
(
Liu et al. 2017
)
is a visual analytic system designed to facilitate the analysis of family history and evolution in China. Citeology
(
Matejka et al. 2012
)
visualizes citation networks of HCI papers, creating family trees of research through referenced and referencing papers. These works demonstrate the timeline’s capacity to represent both chronological order and genealogical connections.

Despite their usefulness, timeline visualizations are still not widely available, in part due to the difficulty of constructing them manually or automatically using computational methods — especially interactive versions that can dynamically adapt to users’ evolving information needs
(
Martschat and Markert 2018a
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Hasegawa et al. 2024
)
.

2.2.
Timeline Construction using NLP and LLM-based Approaches

The task of automatically constructing timelines as opposed to manually constructing them has a long history in natural language processing (NLP), where they focused on summarizing text from multiple documents and constructing timelines based on select documents — known as
timeline summarization
(TMS) task
(
Chieu and Lee 2004
;
Martschat and Markert 2018b
;
Yu et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2021
;
You et al. 2023
)
, reflecting a longstanding and shared interest in going beyond the limitations of manually creating timelines. Earlier methods focused on extracting and ordering events from large corpora, using information extraction, clustering, and summarization techniques to produce concise timelines of news or historical events.

More recently, large language models (LLMs) — which excel at summarization tasks — have opened new possibilities, and researchers recently investigated how LLMs perform compared to traditional NLP approaches on constructing timelines from documents. Sojitra et al. performed this evaluation and found that LLMs achieve competitive results compared to current state-of-the-art NLP-based methods
(
Sojitra et al. 2024
)
, suggesting their potential as engines for timeline construction.

Our work extends this line of research by shifting focus from performance (e.g., accuracy, coverage) to user experience — asking not only
whether
LLMs can construct timelines, but also
how
such capabilities can enrich exploration and sensemaking. Specifically, KnowledgeTrail positions LLMs as generative partners in interactive timeline building, enabling users to dynamically expand, contract, and reorganize events in real time. This shift from automated extraction to interactive co-construction highlights the potential of generative AI not only to retrieve information but also to scaffold curiosity-driven exploration and knowledge formation.

2.3.
Constructivist Foundations for Curiosity-Driven Exploration

But why should we support curiosity-driven exploration? What is a theoretical grounding for this? One answer lies in constructionism, a philosophy that has had a profound impact on education and related fields. While our work is not primarily grounded in education, exploring information necessarily entails learning, making educational perspectives highly relevant. Constructivist theories emphasize that knowledge is actively constructed by learners rather than passively absorbed
(
Papert 1993
;
Resnick 1998
;
Suh et al. 2020
)
. In the context of history education, constructivist approaches focus on helping learners build their own understanding of historical events, connections, and narratives
(
Whelan 2006
)
. Barton and Levstik argue that effective history learning involves not just memorizing facts and dates, but engaging in historical thinking—analyzing causes and effects, understanding multiple perspectives, and contextualizing events within broader socio-cultural frameworks
(
Barton and Levstik 2003
;
Barton and Levstik 2004
)
. The broader notion of historical thinking similarly emphasizes inquiry-based learning, where students actively investigate questions, analyze evidence, and construct their own historical narratives
(
Voet and De Wever 2016
)
. Yet, traditional approaches often struggle to support these practices, presenting history as a fixed narrative rather than a dynamic web of interrelated events and interpretations
(
VanSledright 2010
)
.

Our work is informed by these constructivist principles, particularly the emphasis on self-directed exploration and connection-making. We draw on these ideas to design interfaces that foreground exploration and personal meaning-making. Rather than presenting a fixed narrative, our approach uses generative timelines to scaffold user agency and dynamic inquiry, allowing learners to co-construct their own timeline that adapt to their questions and interests.

2.4.
Generative and Co-Creative Interfaces

With the advent of LLMs and the opportunities it opens up, interest in designing novel UIs that take advantage of their generative capabilities has grown in both product development and research. Commercial UI designs remain limited, often featuring plain text boxes
(
ope 2024
)
or simple chat interfaces
(
cha 2024
)
. In contrast, HCI research has introduced novel designs that introduce varied input/output formats and interaction styles
(
Kim et al. 2023
;
Suh et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Suh et al. 2023
;
Chung et al. 2022
;
Brade et al. 2023
;
Masson et al. 2023
;
Arawjo et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2022
;
Wu et al. 2023
)
.

Several systems have leveraged these approaches to support knowledge exploration. Sensecape
(
Suh et al. 2023
)
abstracts and organizes model outputs through features such as question generation and semantic zoom, aligning with constructivist approaches to guided discovery learning. Graphologue
(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
converts model responses into diagrams, using visual representations to scaffold conceptual understanding. Luminate
(
Suh et al. 2024
)
and related systems
(
Brade et al. 2023
)
further structure outputs into higher-level dimensions, enabling users to navigate and make sense of information through organized representations. While effective for abstraction and organization, these systems do not address the unique challenges of historical exploration, where temporal ordering and causal connections are central to understanding.

A further challenge for LLM-powered information systems, including those used in education, is factual accuracy. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) combines models with trusted sources to reduce hallucinations, while interface designs such as citations, source attributions, and confidence cues help users evaluate information generated and formatted by LLMs
(
Lewis et al. 2020
;
Gao et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
. For example, as shown in Fig.
2
, Perplexity AI and ChatGPT provide inline citations to web sources
(
per 2025
)
, and Claude allows users to retrieve citations from their own uploaded documents
(
cla 2025
)
. These approaches underscore the importance of grounding generative outputs in verifiable evidence. Yet, most existing designs focus primarily on providing verification, without examining how users perceive and use such mechanisms—or how they could be integrated into dynamic exploratory environments like timelines, where users need to both trust and build upon generated information.

KnowledgeTrail builds on these developments by incorporating established techniques and features such as semantic zoom, question generation, and citation links for verification
(
Shneiderman 2003
;
Walter et al. 2013
;
Suh et al. 2023
;
Suh et al. 2024
)
, while examining them in the underexplored space of AI-enhanced timelines and analyzing how users perceive and use them.
Unlike prior work focused on enhancing output presentation or organization
(
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Suh et al. 2023
)
, KnowledgeTrail engages users in the dynamic construction and refinement of timelines. This user-driven approach supports constructivist principles by encouraging active exploration, curiosity-driven learning
(
Berlyne 1960
)
, and the co-construction of historical narratives—core practices in constructivist theories of knowledge formation
(
Papert 1993
;
Resnick 1998
)
.

3.
KnowledgeTrail

In this section, we first present the design goals that guided the development of KnowledgeTrail, followed by an overview of its user interface and features, and two example workflows to demonstrate the described features.

3.1.
Design Considerations

Building on prior work in history learning, AI-powered exploration, and constructionist approaches, we formulated four design goals (
DG
s) to guide the development of KnowledgeTrail.

•

DG. 1
:
Support Curiosity-Driven Exploration and Knowledge Building.

Constructivist learning principles emphasize that knowledge is actively built rather than passively received
(
Papert 1993
;
Resnick 1998
)
. Drawing on this foundation, we aimed to design a system that encourages curiosity-driven exploration and helps users dynamically build their own historical timelines through self-directed exploration. By leveraging LLMs’ generative capabilities, the system should allow users to follow their curiosity, exploring both breadth (discovering other events across different contexts and time) and depth (diving deeper into a set time frame by exploring additional historical knowledge in that time)
(
VanSledright 2010
;
Voet and De Wever 2016
;
Suh et al. 2023
)
.

•

DG. 2
:
Facilitate Understanding of Historical Causality and Connection.
Historical thinking requires understanding how events relate to one another through cause and effect relationships
(
Seixas and Morton 2012
)
. Our goal was to design features that help users recognize and explore connections between historical events
(
Montanero and Lucero 2011
)
. The system should visualize temporal sequences while also revealing genealogical relationships between events, enabling users to trace how ideas, discoveries, and developments influence one another across time—a key aspect of understanding knowledge formation processes
(
Whelan 2006
)
.

•

DG. 3
:
Enable Intuitive Navigation Across Multiple Dimensions of Historical Knowledge.

Historical exploration involves moving fluidly between different temporal, spatial, and conceptual levels. The system should provide an interface that supports navigation across these dimensions — including time, space, and abstraction (level of detail) — to avoid cognitive overload and help users maintain focus during exploration
(
Montanero and Lucero 2011
;
Walter et al. 2013
;
Voet and De Wever 2016
;
Suh et al. 2023
)
.

•

DG. 4
:
Support Critical Evaluation of Historical Information.

Given the challenges of factual accuracy in LLM-generated content, we aimed to design features that scaffold critical evaluation. The system should provide mechanisms, such as citations and provenance cues, that help users assess the reliability of generated content and integrate this evaluation into their broader knowledge-building process
(
Lewis et al. 2020
;
Gao et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
per 2025
)
.

3.2.
User Interface & Interactions

In designing KnowledgeTrail, we focused on developing interactions that could sustain curiosity-driven exploration. This included: dynamic event generation triggered by user queries, contextual expansion of timeline nodes, relationship discovery between selected events, semantic zooming to reveal patterns at different scales, and guided question generation to prompt further inquiry. Each interaction was designed to scaffold the user’s exploration process while maintaining their agency in directing the learning journey—a key principle for constructivist learning environments.

4.
Evaluation of KnowledgeTrail

We conducted two user studies to evaluate how KnowledgeTrail supports exploration, sensemaking, and critical evaluation. Our aim was to understand how users engage with generative timelines to explore and connect events, and how specific features shape their experience and perceived utility. Based on pilot sessions, we found that evaluating both generative timeline interactions and citation features within a single study led to participant fatigue due to the length of the sessions. We therefore separated the evaluation into two studies: one focusing on the core generative interactions and another on the role of citation features. This separation also allowed us to isolate the effects of each design choice and study them in greater depth.

Study 1 (Sec
5
).
The first study used a version of KnowledgeTrail without citation features. This design choice allowed us to focus on how users navigated, expanded, and connected events without being distracted by verification features, following prior work that intentionally excluded citations to foreground interaction
(
Suh et al. 2023
)
. Our goal was to observe how curiosity-driven exploration unfolded and whether the system fostered serendipitous discoveries and connections. In this study, participants also used ChatGPT for similar tasks, so that we can compare their experiences with a baseline conversational interface.

Study 2 (Sec
6
).
The second study introduced citation features to examine how verification scaffolds influenced exploration. This study complemented the first by shifting attention from exploration alone to how users critically evaluated and trusted the information presented. While Study 1 highlighted the potential of generative timelines for open-ended inquiry, Study 2 explored how source links supported critical assessment of generated content and shaped participants’ confidence in their findings.

Together, these two studies provided complementary insights: the first revealed the exploratory potential of generative timelines, while the second demonstrated how verification features can support trust and critical evaluation.

Baseline Rationale.
ChatGPT was chosen as a baseline because it allows us to isolate the underlying model’s effect (both share the same LLM) and focus on evaluating the novel system design of KnowledgeTrail. While we considered using static, pre-curated timelines as a baseline, we opted for ChatGPT because static or interactive timelines lack the dynamic exploration capabilities that KnowledgeTrail enables. Our goal in this study was to explore how KnowledgeTrail qualitatively enhances the use of commonly available LLM tools for historical exploration. Additionally, comparing KnowledgeTrail with static timelines may have limited the depth of the comparison, as static timelines do not offer the same interactive and generative capabilities. Future work may consider static timelines as ground truth examples to evaluate the relevance of events generated by KnowledgeTrail.

They received $30 for this 1.5-hour study.

6.3.
Procedure

Each session lasted approximately 90 minutes. Participants completed a short pre-study survey to share their baseline trust in AI-generated information, verification habits, and prior exposure to AI systems with citations. 6.4.
Results

We evaluated how the citation feature supported trust and verification. Survey questions asked participants to rate its usefulness, and interviews and post-study analysis of recordings revealed how they engaged with source links in practice.

6.4.1.
General experience of using KnowledgeTrail with citation links.

Like participants in Study 1, all participants reported positive experience, finding the system easy to learn (1 Strongly Agree, 3 Agree) and easy to use (3 Agree, 1 Neutral). They also felt the system helped them discover new topics (2 Strongly Agree, 2 Agree), learn how to explore new topics (3 Strongly Agree, 1 Agree), and explore knowledge formation process (2 Strongly Agree, 2 Agree). All but one participant shared that comparing KnowledgeTrail to ChatGPT, they prefer to use KnowledgeTrail to explore and make sense of various topics (2 Strongly Agree, 1 Agree, 1 Neutral).

6.4.2.
Perceived usefulness of citations.

All participants used citations links throughout the session and found citations useful (
M
=6.3/7), adding that they helped confirm the accuracy of the information (3 Strongly Agree, 1 Agree), verify or cross-check the system’s responses (1 Strongly Agree, 3 Agree), and improved the overall experience (3 Strongly Agree, 1 Agree), while not slowing them down (2 Strongly Agree, 2 Agree). For some, the very presence of links created reassurance, even if they did not click on all of them and did not rate the quality of the links highly (
M
=4.7/7). In general, the presence of citations were said to have increased their trust in the information provided by the system (
M
=5/7 | 1 Not at all, 7 Extremely). One participant explained: “Even if I didn’t open all the links, just knowing they were there made me trust the results more” (V2). Another remarked that the citations helped them check whether the system was “making things up” (V1). Although hypothetical, when asked what their experience would have been without citation features, all participants said this would have negatively affected their experience, underscoring the importance of citations to their sense of trust and engagement.

6.4.3.
Quality & relevance of citations.

While participants valued having citations, they were also attentive to the quality of the sources. Although ratings of citation quality (
M
=4.7/7) and relevance (
M
=5.7/7) were not low, participants emphasized that perceived quality shaped whether they would actually use the citations. This shows that while citations improved transparency, the credibility of the underlying sources still shaped overall trust. An interesting observation was that participants differed in what they considered credible: for example, V2, a PhD student, dismissed Wikipedia as inappropriate for academic use (“Wikipedia is not really the most reliable source from an academic perspective…”), whereas others saw it as reasonable or even trustworthy. V3 described Wikipedia as such, saying: “Wikipedia is a great start… it gives me good citations right to the Wikipedia page, and obviously from those points, you can jump off to anywhere,” and V4 stated, “I tend to just believe if the link is from Wikipedia, I usually believe that it’s… quite, you know, truthful.” This suggests that individual circumstances shaped how participants evaluated the perceived credibility of the sources. Moreover, even small issues, such as encountering an invalid or missing link or past experience with hallucination, were enough to lower participants’ confidence in the citation feature, indicating that trust in citations was fragile and easily undermined. Reflecting these concerns, V2 suggested that the system could let users choose the types of sources it returns (e.g., academic articles versus general web search results), which they felt would make the citations more appropriate for different contexts of use.

6.4.4.
Verification behaviors and use of citation links.

Whereas participants in Study 1 largely accepted generated content at face value, those in Study 2 described actively inspecting links when content seemed uncertain. For example, V1 noted, “You can’t say things are absolute truth” and reported following citations to confirm claims. Participants explained that they were especially motivated to check sources for unfamiliar topics and for relationships between historical events suggested by the system.

We observed two main patterns in how they used citation links. First, participants clicked citations either to learn more about a topic of interest or simply to confirm that the links worked. Second, in cases where the system generated connections between events, participants occasionally used citations to validate the correctness of those claims. For example, one participant clicked a source after the system suggested a connection between two historical events, explaining that they wanted to confirm whether the relationship was accurate. These behaviors show that citations supported both exploratory learning and critical verification.

7.
Discussion

In this work, we introduced KnowledgeTrail, an interactive system that uses LLMs to generate a dynamic timeline for exploring historical events and knowledge formation processes. Our user study revealed that KnowledgeTrail facilitates creative and meaningful exploration by helping users uncover relationships between topics and enabling flexible, dynamic engagement. Participants appreciated the system’s ability to link related events, saying that it led to deeper understanding and new discoveries.

Below, we discuss the limitations of this work and potential directions for future work, and the broader implications of integrating generative timelines into interactive systems.

7.1.
Limitations & Future Work

We outline several limitations in our two-part study. Firstly, although all participants utilized all features and found the system “easy to use” and “intuitive,” it is challenging to truly measure the outcome from using our system with a single study session. Thus, we plan to evaluate the long-term impact and usefulness of the system with a longitudinal study. While KnowledgeTrail is grounded in constructivist principles, our evaluation also focused on usability, exploration behaviors, and user experience. Future work could examine its impact on learning outcomes more directly. Moreover, the participants in our user study were from a relatively focused demographic group consisting of young students and professionals in their 20s and 30s with prior experience using AI tools for learning. Exploring its usefulness with other user bases, for instance, K-12 students in classroom settings and also with an older population would be beneficial. Below, we further discuss important future directions to advance this research.

Intelligent Support.
All participants praised how KnowledgeTrail aided in uncovering the relationships among various topics. A notable feature highlighted was its ability to automatically link an existing node to another, revealing connections that facilitate new learning. We aim to enhance KnowledgeTrail by enabling it to maintain the context of explored topics. Furthermore, we plan to incorporate steps within the generative pipeline to involve identifying and linking existing topics that are related to the newly generated nodes. Moreover, we believe there is an opportunity for the system to analyze the set of events and provide suggestions for unexplored, related topics that could deepen the user’s understanding or open new avenues for exploration. We could also explore different edge designs to inform the differences in the types and strengths of relationships between events. These improvements would further support users’ ability to engage with content dynamically and meaningfully.

Flexibility.
Our current system design has prompted LLM to generate 4 to 8 events while future work could further extend to allow more flexibility in the number of events generated. We learned from our user study that this could potentially help accommodate user preferences and account for different kinds of topics that may require more or fewer events to be revealed. For example, when a prompt is “The History of the Roman Empire,“ the important events that occurred over 500 years of history inherently need more events compared to a topic that may span only 100 years (e.g., “United States around the 19th century”). There were also instances where participants desired more events to be added. For example, after receiving 8 events (3 discoveries, 3 inventions, 2 theories) from “significant discoveries in thermodynamics in the 20th century,” P5 asked whether it was possible to get more “detailed” events. Therefore, extending the system to allow for more customized settings and flexibility will be beneficial.

Ensuring Factual Accuracy.

As with any applications using generative models, our system can occasionally observe hallucination in LLM generation. While solving hallucination is not within the scope of this study, we recognize its importance, especially for applications like KnowledgeTrail that assist information consumption and learning.

To mitigate this, our system incorporated a citation feature that attaches references to generated content. This reflects a state-of-the-art practice now adopted in commercial tools such as Perplexity and ChatGPT, and participants in our study found citations valuable for improving transparency and trust. At the same time, our findings revealed limitations: source quality varied, invalid or missing links undermined confidence, and perceptions of credibility differed across contexts (e.g., students dismissing Wikipedia vs. others treating it as trustworthy). Participants suggested the ability to select the types of sources the system should return (e.g., academic articles vs. general web search), which could make the feature more appropriate for different use cases. Future work should evaluate how such configurable citation designs affect user trust, critical engagement, and learning outcomes in generative timeline interfaces.

Future work will include evaluating how these source citation elements impact users’ ability to critically evaluate historical information while maintaining the benefits of curiosity-driven exploration. Additionally, we plan to investigate how different approaches to source presentation affect user trust and learning outcomes in generative timeline interfaces. More broadly, future work could consider leveraging more capable models or adapting current models to similar tasks using techniques like retrieval-augmented generation
(
Lewis et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Gao et al. 2023
)
or fine-tuning
(
Ding et al. 2023
;
Dodge et al. 2020
;
Ziegler et al. 2019
)
.

New Applications Using Generative Timeline.

The concept of a generative timeline opens up exciting possibilities for new applications. One potential use is in story writing, where the generative timeline can assist in creating prequels and sequels by dynamically generating events to expand narratives in both directions. Similarly, in video authoring tools, users could interact with a timeline interface to expand or condense video content, offering flexible narrative control. Moreover, we only explored the design of a basic timeline and have not looked at how we might design for comparative timelines, where users have multiple timelines in the view. Given the many potential forms of timelines, as explored in
(
Brehmer et al. 2016
)
, future work could explore the use of generative timelines in other forms of timelines. These applications demonstrate the potential for generative timelines to enhance various creative experiences across diverse media.

Prompt Engineering.

Our study also underscored the importance of prompt engineering in the system to ensure that the generated output aligns with the user’s intentions. For example, when participants explored topics with which they were somewhat familiar, they might have had expectations about what needed to be generated and added to the canvas. Occasionally, the output would not align with their expectations. To address these issues, some participants had to update the context field to add specific context to steer the generation of events. For instance, as shown in Figure
11
, P5 wanted future events that “Euler’s fluid dynamics equation (1757)” contributed to. When the context was initially “how it evolved,” it generated events prior to this equation. P5 then had to update the context to “how it evolved after this equation’s derivation.” However, as many studies have shown, prompt engineering can be difficult for users, especially novices
(
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
;
Dang et al. 2022
)
. Future work can explore methods to further support users’ prompt engineering.

Refining Logic in Event Generation.

Beyond highlighting relationships, an important question remains regarding the logic governing which events should be generated and which events or entities should be connected. Before the advent of LLMs, the task of determining not only which events to include but also which events are related to one another has been challenging and crucial. Our work acknowledges the dangers of allowing LLMs to autonomously decide both what to generate and how to establish connections, as this may result in biased, incoherent, or incomplete timelines. While these questions are beyond the scope of the current implementation of KnowledgeTrail, we acknowledge the potential risks involved for future work. Future iterations of the system will need to consider metrics such as event coverage, coherence between events, and the logic of their connections, borrowing from prior work on generating event timelines from corpora
(
Shahaf et al. 2012
;
Shahaf and Guestrin 2012
;
Wang et al. 2019a
)
. These considerations could help guide which events to extract and display and which ones to connect.

7.2.
Implications

Education

Using generative AI (specifically LLMs) to support learning is in its infancy, with many lingering questions about the best ways to use AI to support learning. This same question arose when we asked P8, a former history teacher, to share her thoughts on whether she thinks KnowledgeTrail could be a useful tool for history learning. While she believed this would be a great tool for learning history, she raised fundamental questions about the use of AI tools for learning in general, such as how we ought to assess students’ comprehension, how we would know whether they are absorbing the knowledge, and whether it is acceptable to let them explore topics with which they have no prior knowledge. We leave these questions as future work for KnowledgeTrail.

Designing for credible and configurable citations.

Our findings also highlight the need to carefully design how citation features are integrated into generative timelines. While citations generally increased participants’ trust, their credibility judgments varied widely depending on context: students emphasized the need for academically acceptable references, while others were satisfied with more general-purpose sources such as Wikipedia. Thus, one promising direction is to provide configurable options that let users select the types of sources they prefer (e.g., academic articles, journalistic pieces, or general web search results), making the system more adaptable across contexts of use. More broadly, this underscores that generative timelines should not only generate events but also scaffold critical engagement with their sources.

Static vs. Generative Timeline Interactions.

Generative timelines represent a new genre of dynamic systems that foreground user agency, enable serendipitous discovery, and adapt to evolving knowledge needs. We view this as part of a broader shift toward interfaces that scaffold exploration rather than merely present information. This interaction model offers flexibility and personalization, which static timelines cannot match.

It is also important to distinguish generative timelines from recent work on automated timeline summarization. NLP- and LLM-based approaches (e.g., Sojitra et al.
(
Sojitra et al. 2024
)
) have focused on producing concise, static timelines from document corpora. While valuable for compressing and organizing large amounts of text, these approaches treat timelines primarily as outputs to be consumed. In contrast, our work reimagines the timeline as an interactive medium—a space that users can expand, contract, and restructure in response to their curiosity. This reframing shifts the role of AI from summarizer to generative partner, and the role of the user from passive reader to active co-constructor.

One notable implication of this shift is the potential for serendipitous discoveries, as users uncover unexpected yet meaningful connections across events. Designing systems that embrace and enhance such serendipity will be crucial for unlocking the full potential of generative timelines. While our work has focused on historical exploration, the same interaction model could be extended to other domains, such as storytelling and video creation, revealing the broader space of possibilities that generative timelines open for user-driven exploration.

Design

KnowledgeTrail leverages chronological and genealogical representations to display the sequence of historical events and highlight the connections between pieces of knowledge. While these representations are not new, our work shows how LLM-based interactions can be integrated to foster deeper understanding of information with temporal and interdependent properties. Prior timeline generation efforts have typically relied on pre-existing documents for event extraction
(
Yan et al. 2011
;
Shahaf et al. 2013
;
Fulda et al. 2015
;
Bedi et al. 2017
)
. In contrast, the generative capabilities of LLMs allow for dynamic construction, refinement, and exploration of timelines beyond fixed sources, which was previously not possible.

This shift could have significant implications for future interactive systems with similar goals. For example, in systems like KnowledgeTrail, designed to support the exploration and sensemaking of historical events and knowledge formation, guiding users to discover connections between multiple topics may engage them in deeper learning. Additionally, interactive systems that support data exploration could also benefit from these insights, as data often shares similar temporal and interdependent characteristics.

8.
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced the idea of generative timelines and instantiated it through KnowledgeTrail, demonstrating how an LLM-powered interface can enable open-ended exploration, reveal hidden relationships, and foster serendipitous insights. By integrating insights from prior research in history learning and information visualization, KnowledgeTrail empowers users to explore both the chronological relationships between historical events and the genealogical connections in knowledge formation processes. Through two user studies, we examined how generative timelines support exploration, sensemaking, and trust. The first study showed how participants used KnowledgeTrail to creatively explore history and knowledge formation, reporting that the system enabled serendipitous discoveries and deeper understanding. The second study focused on the citation feature, revealing that while citations increased trust and supported verification, credibility judgments varied depending on participants’ professional and educational contexts, and trust was fragile when links appeared invalid or unfamiliar.

To our knowledge, this is the first work to investigate how generative AI can be incorporated into an interactive tool for dynamic timeline construction and historical exploration. While prior approaches have ranged from manually authored timelines to NLP- and LLM-based summarization of events from documents, these efforts treat timelines primarily as static outputs. KnowledgeTrail introduces a different perspective: the timeline as an interactive and generative medium that evolves alongside the user’s curiosity. By reframing timelines in this way, we highlight a path forward for interactive systems that not only present information but actively scaffold exploration, sensemaking, and discovery.
Taken together, our findings suggest that designing generative timelines requires balancing two complementary goals: enabling serendipitous discoveries that spark curiosity, and ensuring credible, context-appropriate citations that sustain trust and critical engagement.
