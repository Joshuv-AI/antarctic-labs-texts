Divergent series in Gauss’s diary and their extensions
Kiyoshi Sogo

Abstract
Two divergent series in Entry 7 of Gauss’s diary are extended systematically by introducing additional parameters. Rogers-Fine identities, Ramanujan’s continued fractions and Heine’s transformation relations of basic hypergeometric series are applied to find equivalent alternative series, which enable us to compute the sums of divergent series in question.

Introduction

In 1796 Gauss wrote, in Entry 7 of his mathematical diary
[
7
,
4
]
, two formulas such that

\displaystyle 1-2+8-64+\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{1+}\ \frac{2}{1+}\ \frac{2}{1+}\ \frac{8}{1+}\ \frac{12}{1+}\ \frac{32}{1+}\ \frac{56}{1+}\ \frac{128}{1+}\ \cdots,

(1.1)

\displaystyle 1-1+1\cdot 3-1\cdot 3\cdot 7+1\cdot 3\cdot 7\cdot 15-\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{1+}\ \frac{1}{1+}\ \frac{2}{1+}\ \frac{6}{1+}\ \frac{12}{1+}\ \frac{28}{1+}\ \cdots,

(1.2)

where the right hand sides are continued fractions, which we use the notation

\displaystyle\frac{d_{0}}{1+}\ \frac{d_{1}}{1+}\ \frac{d_{2}}{1+}\ \cdots=\frac{d_{0}}{1+\frac{d_{1}}{1+\frac{d_{2}}{1+\cdots}}}.

(1.3)

They are the
q=2
case of

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n(n+1)/2}=\frac{1}{1+}\ \frac{q}{1+}\ \frac{q^{2}-q}{1+}\ \frac{q^{3}}{1+}\ \frac{q^{4}-q^{2}}{1+}\ \cdots,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(q;q)_{n}=\frac{1}{1+}\ \frac{q-1}{1+}\ \frac{q^{2}-q}{1+}\ \frac{q^{3}-q}{1+}\ \frac{q^{4}-q^{2}}{1+}\ \cdots,

(1.5)

respectively, where
(\alpha;q)_{n}
is
q
-Pochhammer symbol defined by

\displaystyle(\alpha;q)_{0}=1,\

\displaystyle(\alpha;q)_{n}=(\alpha;q)_{n-1}\cdot(1-\alpha q^{n-1}),\quad(n\geq 1)

(1.6)

which gives
(q;q)_{0}=1,\ (q;q)_{n}=(1-q)\cdots(1-q^{n}),\ (n\geq 1)
.
For the simplicity, the parameter
q
is assumed for a while to be real and non-negative number.

Since the left hand side of (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) are divergent when
q>1
, we can suppose that here Gauss proposed
problems to find
sums of divergent series
similarly as Euler did before
[
3
,
8
]
.
The purpose of the present article is to give answers to (
1.1
) and (
1.2
).
In addition we wish to propose their extensions such that

\displaystyle S_{\rho}^{(1)}(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n[(\rho-2)n-(\rho-4)]/2},\quad(\rho=3,4,5,\cdots),

(1.7)

\displaystyle=1-q+q^{\rho}-q^{3\rho-3}+\cdots,

\displaystyle S_{\kappa}^{(2)}(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n}(q;q^{\kappa})_{n},\quad(\kappa=1,2,3,\cdots),

(1.8)

\displaystyle=1+q(1-q)+q^{2}(1-q)(1-q^{1+\kappa})+\cdots,

and give values of them for
q>1
, as well as for
q<1
.
It should be noted that the power
n[(\rho-2)n-(\rho-4)]/2
in (
1.7
) is called
\rho

polygonal number
series,
that is,
\rho=3
triangle,
\rho=4
square,
\rho=5
pentagon etc.,
and the first case of Gauss (
1.4
) corresponds to
\rho=3
trigonal number series.

We should also note that (
1.8
) is
x=q
case of the power series

\displaystyle S_{\kappa}^{(2)}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(q;q^{\kappa})_{n}x^{n}

(1.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-}\ \frac{(1-q)x}{1-}\ \frac{q(1-q^{\kappa})x}{1-}\ \frac{q^{\kappa}(1-q^{1+\kappa})x}{1-}\ \frac{q^{1+\kappa}(1-q^{2\kappa})x}{1-}\ \cdots,

and the second case of Gauss (
1.5
) is given by setting
\kappa=1
and
x=1
,
i.e.
,
S_{1}^{(2)}(1)
.
The continued fraction expression in the right hand side will be derived later.

2
Answers to Gauss problems

2.1
First group of problems – polygonal number series

By using Rogers-Fine identity
[
16
,
5
,
10
]

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^{n}q^{n(n+1)/2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(xq;q^{2})_{n}}{(xq^{2};q^{2})_{n}}\cdot(xq)^{n},

(2.1)

at
x=-1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n(n+1)/2}=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n}\cdot\frac{(1+q)\cdots(1+q^{2n-1})}{(1+q^{2})\cdots(1+q^{2n})}.

(2.2)

Obviously both sides are convergent for
q<1
. But for
q>1
,
although the left hand side is divergent, the right hand side becomes by substituting
q=p^{-1}

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n}\cdot\frac{(1+q)\cdots(1+q^{2n-1})}{(1+q^{2})\cdots(1+q^{2n})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{(1+p)\cdots(1+p^{2n-1})}{(1+p^{2})\cdots(1+p^{2n})}.

(2.3)

The right hand side of (
2.3
) is
almost convergent
when
p<1
,
i.e.
,
q>1
,
because it is similar to the alternating series of
Q_{\infty}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}Q_{\infty},\qquad Q_{\infty}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1+p^{2n-1}}{1+p^{2n}}\right),

(2.4)

whose sum is given by
\frac{1}{2}Q_{\infty}
in the sense of Cesàro sum
[
8
]
.
Therefore by using (
2.3
) we can
define and replace
the left hand side for
q>1

by the right hand side for
p=q^{-1}<1
.

Result of numerical experiment of partial sums for
p=0.5\ (q=2)
is given by

\displaystyle S_{N}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{n=1}^{N}(-1)^{n}\frac{(1+p)\cdots(1+p^{2n-1})}{(1+p^{2})\cdots(1+p^{2n})}

(2.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=+1.0759457568\cdots\quad(N=100),\\
&=-0.2208954963\cdots\quad(N=101),\end{split}

(2.6)

repeating these two values alternately as
N
increases.
The above values are convergent already for
N=50
up to the given ten digits.
Since
Q_{\infty}\simeq S_{N}-S_{N+1}
for large even
N
, we can check the equality

\displaystyle Q_{\infty}=1.296841253\cdots=1.0759457568\cdots+0.2208954963\cdots

(2.7)

by computing
Q_{\infty}
independently by (
2.4
).
In other words, we can say for large even
N

\displaystyle S_{N}\simeq Lim+\frac{1}{2}Q_{\infty},\quad S_{N+1}\simeq Lim-\frac{1}{2}Q_{\infty},

(2.8)

and the value of
Lim
is given by the average for
p=0.5,\ N=100
,

\displaystyle 1-2+2^{3}-2^{6}+\cdots

\displaystyle=Lim

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{2}\left(S_{N}+S_{N+1}\right)=0.4275251302\cdots,

(2.9)

which is our answer to Gauss problem 1,
i.e.
, (
1.1
).

Now let us consider some extensions of this first problem in Entry 7 of Gauss’s diary.
If we change
q\rightarrow q^{a},\ x\rightarrow xq^{-b}
in (
2.1
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^{n}q^{an(n+1)/2-nb}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^{n}q^{(a-b)n}\ \frac{(xq^{a-b};q^{2a})_{n}}{(xq^{2a-b};q^{2a})_{n}}.

(2.10)

By setting
a=\rho-2,\ b=\rho-3
and
x=-1
, the above equality becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{\rho}^{(1)}(q)&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n[(\rho-2)n-(\rho-4)]/2}\\
&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n}\ \frac{(-q;q^{2\rho-4})_{n}}{(-q^{\rho-1};q^{2\rho-4})_{n}}\\
&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}p^{(\rho-3)n}\frac{(-p;p^{2\rho-4})_{n}}{(-p^{\rho-1};p^{2\rho-4})_{n}},\end{split}

(2.11)

where
p=q^{-1}
as before. In this way, we arrive at the following theorem.

Theorem 1
For
\rho=3,4,5,\cdots
, we have (
p=q^{-1}
)

\displaystyle S_{\rho}^{(1)}(q)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n[(\rho-2)n-(\rho-4)]/2}

\displaystyle\quad=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n}\frac{(1+q)\cdots(1+q^{1+(2\rho-4)(n-1)})}{(1+q^{\rho-1})\cdots(1+q^{\rho-1+(2\rho-4)(n-1)})}

(2.12)

\displaystyle\quad=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}p^{(\rho-3)n}\frac{(1+p)\cdots(1+p^{1+(2\rho-4)(n-1)})}{(1+p^{\rho-1})\cdots(1+p^{\rho-1+(2\rho-4)(n-1)})},

which gives (
2.2
), (
2.3
) when
\rho=3
, as it should be.

Let us consider
q
as a complex number. When
\rho>3
, the first line and the second line in (
2.12
) are convergent
for
|q|<1
and coincide with each other.
Now the second line is convergent even for
|q|>1
, which is equivalent to the third line for
|p|<1
, because the third line is
just a rewriting of the second line by setting
q=p^{-1}
. And since the third line is convergent for
|p|<1

i.e.
,
|q|>1
,
we can say the third line is
analytical continuation
of the first line.
In other word, our method of computing sums of divergent series is analogous to the method of
\zeta
regularization based on
the Riemann’s functional equality (the duality formula)
[
13
]
, which was derived also by analytical continuation.

For the concreteness let us give several explicit formulas for
\rho\geq 4
. For
\rho=4
we have (
p=q^{-1}
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{4}^{(1)}(q)&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n^{2}}\\
&=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n}\frac{(1+q)\cdots(1+q^{4n-3})}{(1+q^{3})\cdots(1+q^{4n-1})}\\
&=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}p^{n}\frac{(1+p)\cdots(1+p^{4n-3})}{(1+p^{3})\cdots(1+p^{4n-1})}.\end{split}

(2.13)

Interestingly the second and the third lines have the completely same form.
Such phenomenon can be called
self-duality
, because
S_{4}^{(1)}(q)=S_{4}^{(1)}(q^{-1})
.
In fact for
q=p=0.5
both has the same value

\displaystyle 1-2^{-1}+2^{-4}-2^{-9}+\cdots

\displaystyle=1-2+2^{4}-2^{9}+\cdots

\displaystyle=0.5605621040\cdots.

(2.14)

Next for
\rho=5
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{5}^{(1)}(q)&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n(3n-1)/2}\\
&=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n}\frac{(1+q)\cdots(1+q^{6n-5})}{(1+q^{4})\cdots(1+q^{6n-2})}\\
&=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}p^{2n}\frac{(1+p)\cdots(1+p^{6n-5})}{(1+p^{4})\cdots(1+p^{6n-2})},\end{split}

(2.15)

which gives numerically for
q=p=0.5

\displaystyle\begin{split}&1-2^{-1}+2^{-5}-2^{-12}+\cdots=0.5310060977\cdots,\\
&1-2+2^{5}-2^{12}+\cdots=0.7181272344\cdots.\end{split}

(2.16)

2.2
Second group of problems – Pochhammer symbol series

The second Gauss problem is to find the value of

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(q;q)_{n}=1+(1-q)+(1-q)(1-q^{2})+\cdots

(2.18)

for
q=2
. However the sum of this series is
divergent
even for
q<1
, because
(q;q)_{\infty}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n})

is finite. Therefore our answer to Gauss problem 2, is
divergent
against the expectation.
More details and other examples of divergent series are discussed in a recent article by the present author
[
18
]
.

Let us consider next

\displaystyle S_{\kappa}^{(2)}(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n}(q;q^{\kappa})_{n}

(2.19)

\displaystyle=1+q(1-q)+q^{2}(1-q)(1-q^{1+\kappa})+\cdots,

which can be written in a continued fraction form such that

\displaystyle S_{\kappa}^{(2)}(q)=\frac{d_{0}}{1+}\ \frac{d_{1}}{1+}\ \frac{d_{2}}{1+}\ \frac{d_{3}}{1+}\ \cdots,

(2.20)

\displaystyle d_{0}=1,\ d_{1}=q^{2}-q,\ d_{2}=q^{2+\kappa}-q^{2},\ d_{3}=q^{2+2\kappa}-q^{1+\kappa},\ \cdots,\

\displaystyle d_{2m}=q^{2\kappa m+2-\kappa}-q^{\kappa m+2-\kappa},\ d_{2m+1}=q^{2\kappa m+2}-q^{\kappa m+1},

(2.21)

by using the Rogers formula
[
15
]
, which is explained next.

For the sake of completeness let us give the Rogers formula, which was found earlier by Muir
[
11
]
.
Suppose we have an equality

\displaystyle c_{0}+c_{1}x+c_{2}x^{2}+c_{3}x^{3}+\cdots=\frac{e_{0}}{1-}\ \frac{e_{1}x}{1-}\ \frac{e_{2}x}{1-}\ \frac{e_{3}x}{1-}\ \cdots,

(2.22)

then the coefficients
e
’s in the right hand side are given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&e_{0}=\alpha_{0},\ e_{1}=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{0}},\ e_{2}=\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{0}},\ e_{3}=\frac{\alpha_{3}\alpha_{0}}{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{1}},\ \cdots,\\
&e_{n}=\frac{\alpha_{n}\alpha_{n-3}}{\alpha_{n-1}\alpha_{n-2}},\quad(n\geq 3),\end{split}

(2.23)

where
\alpha
’s are determined in terms of
c
’s by

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=c_{0},\ \alpha_{1}=c_{1},\ \alpha_{2}=\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}c_{0}&c_{1}\\
c_{1}&c_{2}\end{array}\right|,\ \alpha_{3}=\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}c_{1}&c_{2}\\
c_{2}&c_{3}\end{array}\right|,\ \cdots

whose general expressions are given by (
m\geq 1
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\alpha_{2m}=\left|\begin{array}[]{cccc}c_{0}&c_{1}&\cdots&c_{m}\\
c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&c_{m+1}\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
c_{m}&\cdots&\cdots&c_{2m}\end{array}\right|,\ \\
&\alpha_{2m+1}=\left|\begin{array}[]{cccc}c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&c_{m+1}\\
c_{2}&c_{3}&\cdots&c_{m+2}\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
c_{m+1}&\cdots&\cdots&c_{2m+1}\end{array}\right|.\end{split}

(2.28)

Such is the Rogers (or Muir-Rogers) formula.

Applying these to the power series

\displaystyle S_{\kappa}^{(2)}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(q;q^{\kappa})_{n}x^{n}=\frac{e_{0}}{1-}\ \frac{e_{1}x}{1-}\ \frac{e_{2}x}{1-}\ \frac{e_{3}x}{1-}\ \cdots,

(2.29)

we obtain
(m\geq 1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&e_{0}=1,\ e_{1}=1-q,\ e_{2}=q(1-q^{\kappa}),\ e_{3}=q^{\kappa}(1-q^{\kappa+1}),\ \cdots,\\
&e_{2m}=q^{\kappa m+1-\kappa}(1-q^{\kappa m}),\quad e_{2m+1}=q^{\kappa m}(1-q^{\kappa m+1}),\end{split}

(2.30)

which give (
2.21
) by setting
x=q
, which was announced before in (
1.9
).

Now to proceed further, we have to borrow results of Ramanujan’s continued fraction. Ramanujan wrote in his
Lost Notebook

[
2
,
1
]
the following identity

\displaystyle\frac{G(aq,\lambda q;b,q)}{G(a,\lambda;b,q)}=\frac{d_{0}}{1+}\ \frac{d_{1}}{1+}\ \frac{d_{2}}{1+}\ \frac{d_{3}}{1+}\ \cdots,

(2.31)

where he set

\displaystyle\begin{split}&G(a,\lambda;b,q)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n(n+1)/2}}{(q;q)_{n}}\frac{(a+\lambda)\cdots(a+\lambda q^{n-1})}{(1+bq)\cdots(1+bq^{n})},\\
&G(aq,\lambda q;b,q)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n(n+3)/2}}{(q;q)_{n}}\frac{(a+\lambda)\cdots(a+\lambda q^{n-1})}{(1+bq)\cdots(1+bq^{n})},\end{split}

(2.32)

then he derived

\displaystyle\begin{split}&d_{0}=1,\ d_{1}=aq+\lambda q,\ d_{2}=bq+\lambda q^{2},\ d_{3}=aq^{2}+\lambda q^{3},\ \cdots,\\
&d_{2m}=bq^{m}+\lambda q^{2m},\quad d_{2m+1}=aq^{m+1}+\lambda q^{2m+1},\quad(m\geq 1),\end{split}

(2.33)

an alternative proof of which is given in
[
18
]
.

Such is called the Ramanujan’s continued fraction, which Ramanujan used reversely to obtain many identities by choosing
parameters
a,\lambda,b
and
q
variously. For example, if we set
a=b=0,\ \lambda=1
we obtain the Rogers-Ramanujan identity

\displaystyle\frac{1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n(n+1)}}{(1-q)\cdots(1-q^{n})}}{1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n^{2}}}{(1-q)\cdots(1-q^{n})}}=\frac{1}{1+}\ \frac{q}{1+}\ \frac{q^{2}}{1+}\ \frac{q^{3}}{1+}\ \cdots,

(2.34)

which was reported in 1919 by Ramanujan
[
12
]
, while Rogers found it earlier in 1894
[
14
]
by using a different method.

If we compare (
2.33
) with (
2.21
), we find the replacements in (
2.33
)

\displaystyle q\rightarrow q^{\kappa},\quad a\rightarrow-q^{1-\kappa},\quad\lambda\rightarrow q^{2-\kappa},\quad b\rightarrow-q^{2-\kappa},

(2.35)

give the continued fraction (
2.21
), because

\displaystyle\begin{split}&d_{2m}=bq^{m}+\lambda q^{2m}\rightarrow-q^{2-\kappa}q^{\kappa m}+q^{2-\kappa}q^{2\kappa m}=q^{2\kappa m+2-\kappa}-q^{\kappa m+2-\kappa},\\
&d_{2m+1}=aq^{m+1}+\lambda q^{2m+1}\rightarrow-q^{1-\kappa}q^{\kappa(m+1)}+q^{2-\kappa}q^{\kappa(2m+1)}=q^{2\kappa m+2}-q^{\kappa m+1}.\end{split}

For the later convenience, let us discuss here how Ramanujan’s
G(a,\lambda;b,q)
is obtained from Heine’s
q
-hypergeometric series

\displaystyle{}_{2}\phi_{1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;q,\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha;q)_{n}(\beta;q)_{n}}{(\gamma;q)_{n}(q;q)_{n}}\ \tau^{n}.

(2.36)

If we take a limit of
\alpha\rightarrow\infty,\ \tau\rightarrow 0
with a constraint
\alpha\tau=-qx
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\ {}_{2}\phi_{1}(-qx/\tau,\beta,\gamma;q,\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n(n+1)/2}}{(q;q)_{n}}\frac{(\beta;q)_{n}}{(\gamma;q)_{n}}\ x^{n},

(2.37)

which is equal to
G(a,\lambda;b,q)
when
\beta=-\lambda/a,\ \gamma=-bq
and
x=a
, that is,

\displaystyle G(a,\lambda;b,q)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\ {}_{2}\phi_{1}(-aq/\tau,-\lambda/a,-bq;q,\tau).

(2.38)

Hereafter we wish to write

\displaystyle\begin{split}G_{0}&=G(a,\lambda;b,q)=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\ {}_{2}\phi_{1}(-aq/\tau,-\lambda/a,-bq;q,\tau)\\
&=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-aq/\tau;q)_{n}(-\lambda/a;q)_{n}}{(-bq;q)_{n}(q;q)_{n}}\ \tau^{n},\end{split}

(2.39)

\displaystyle\begin{split}G_{1}&=G(aq,\lambda q;b,q)=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\ {}_{2}\phi_{1}(-aq^{2}/\tau,-\lambda/a,-bq;q,\tau)\\
&=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-aq^{2}/\tau;q)_{n}(-\lambda/a;q)_{n}}{(-bq;q)_{n}(q;q)_{n}}\ \tau^{n}.\end{split}

(2.40)

Next useful formulas are the transformation relations found by Heine
[
9
]
,

\displaystyle{}_{2}\phi_{1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;q,\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\beta;q)_{\infty}(\alpha\tau;q)_{\infty}}{(\gamma;q)_{\infty}(\tau;q)_{\infty}}\ {}_{2}\phi_{1}(\gamma/\beta,\tau,\alpha\tau;q,\beta),

(2.41)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\gamma/\beta;q)_{\infty}(\beta\tau;q)_{\infty}}{(\gamma;q)_{\infty}(\tau;q)_{\infty}}\ {}_{2}\phi_{1}(\alpha\beta\tau/\gamma,\beta,\beta\tau;q,\gamma/\beta),

(2.42)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\alpha\beta\tau/\gamma;q)_{\infty}}{(\tau;q)_{\infty}}\ {}_{2}\phi_{1}(\gamma/\alpha,\gamma/\beta,\gamma;q,\alpha\beta\tau/\gamma),

(2.43)

which are (1.4.4), (1.4.5), (1.4.6) of the book by Gasper and Rahman
[
6
]
,
and we set
(\alpha;q)_{\infty}=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}(1-\alpha q^{n})
.
Since
{}_{2}\phi_{1}
has symmetry of
(\alpha,\beta)
exchange, we have further

\displaystyle{}_{2}\phi_{1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;q,\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\alpha;q)_{\infty}(\beta\tau;q)_{\infty}}{(\gamma;q)_{\infty}(\tau;q)_{\infty}}{}_{2}\phi_{1}(\gamma/\alpha,\tau,\beta\tau;q,\alpha),

(2.44)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\gamma/\alpha;q)_{\infty}(\alpha\tau;q)_{\infty}}{(\gamma;q)_{\infty}(\tau;q)_{\infty}}\ {}_{2}\phi_{1}(\alpha\beta\tau/\gamma,\alpha,\alpha\tau;q,\gamma/\alpha).

(2.45)

These five transformation relations can be applied to Ramanujan function
G_{0}
and
G_{1}
.

Let us take
\kappa=1
case for the preliminary test, by using
a=-1,\ \lambda=q,\ b=-q
. Then we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&G_{0}=G(-1,q;-q,q)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{q^{n(n+1)/2}}{(1-q^{2})\cdots(1-q^{n+1})}\\
&G_{1}=G(-q,q^{2};-q,q)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{q^{n(n+3)/2}}{(1-q^{2})\cdots(1-q^{n+1})},\end{split}

(2.46)

which are transformed by (

3
Concluding remarks

Motivated by Entry 7 of Gauss’s diary, two groups of series
S_{\rho}^{(1)}(q)
and
S_{\kappa}^{(2)}(q)
,

\displaystyle S_{\rho}^{(1)}(q)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n[(\rho-2)n-(\rho-4)]/2},\quad(\rho=3,4,5,\cdots)

(3.1)

\displaystyle S_{\kappa}^{(2)}(q)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n}(q;q^{\kappa})_{n},\quad(\kappa=1,2,3,\cdots)

(3.2)

are considered. They are convergent for
q<1
, but are divergent for
q>1
.
Our idea is to find another equivalent expression for the series and replace the original series by them.
Such series are found as follows,

\displaystyle S_{\rho}^{(1)}(q)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n}\ \frac{(-q;q^{2\rho-4})_{n}}{(-q^{\rho-1};q^{2\rho-4})_{n}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}p^{(\rho-3)n}\frac{(-p;p^{2\rho-4})_{n}}{(-p^{\rho-1};p^{2\rho-4})_{n}},

(3.3)

\displaystyle S_{\kappa}^{(2)}(q)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{q^{n(\kappa n+4-\kappa)/2}}{(q;q^{\kappa})_{n+1}}=-\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{p^{(\kappa-1)n+1}}{(p;p^{\kappa})_{n+1}}\right),

(3.4)

which are (
2.12
) of
Theorem 1
, and (
2.62
) of
Theorem 2
.
In the right hand side, we set
p=q^{-1}<1
when
q>1
.
This idea of introducing
p=q^{-1}
was inspired by Slater’s book
[
17
]
, specifically her discussions in §3.2
on convergence property of basic hypergeometric series.

Our method of computing sums of divergent series is based on the concept of
analytical continuation
,
which is described in the previous section after the statements of
theorem 1
and
theorem 2
.
Therefore the uniqueness of the results can be attributed to the uniqueness theorem of analytical continuation or

identity theorem
.

Our logic of computing sums of divergent series can be interpreted in another way.
Euler used
[
8
,
3
]

\displaystyle\begin{split}&1-x+x^{2}-x^{3}+\cdots=\frac{1}{1+x}=x^{-1}-x^{-2}+x^{-3}-\cdots\\
&\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}x^{n}=0,\\
&1+x+x^{2}+x^{3}+\cdots=\frac{1}{1-x}=-x^{-1}-x^{-2}-x^{-3}-\cdots\\
&\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x^{n}=0,\end{split}

(3.5)

when he wrote
1-2+4-8+\cdots=1/3
, and
1+2+4+8+\cdots=-1
.
Since such trick is almost the same with the present method, our method may be called
modified Euler’s method
,
because the left hand side series for
|x|<1
and the right hand side series for
|x|>1
in (
3.5
) are
analytical continuations of the central function
(1+x)^{-1}
or
(1-x)^{-1}
.
And the curious equalities in (
3.5
) should be rewritten, in modern terminology, by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\ e^{2\pi iny}=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta\left(y-(m+\frac{1}{2})\right),\\
&\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{2\pi iny}=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(y-m),\end{split}

(3.6)

where we set
x=e^{2\pi iy}
and
\delta(y)
is Dirac’s delta function. The right hand sides of (
3.6
) vanish
except
y
is half-integer or integer respectively.
