Spectrum and outcomes of thrombotic microangiopathies in nephrology: a 17-year cohort from a North African center
Authors: Sanda Mrabet, Ghada Dardour, Raja Boukadida, Sidina Emah, Wissal Sahtout, Dorsaf Zellama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThrombotic microangiopathies (TMA) are rare but life-threatening conditions characterized by microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and organ injury. They are encountered in nephrology due to their renal manifestations. Data from North African populations remain scarce.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective observational study over 17 years (2006–2023) in a nephrology department in Tunisia. We included 36 patients with confirmed TMA based on clinical and/or histological criteria. We analyzed demographics, clinical presentations, laboratory findings, renal histology, etiologies, treatments, and outcomes.ResultsThe mean age was 35.6 ± 11.3 years, and 55.6% were female. Hypertension was present in 83.3% of cases, and neurological symptoms in 66.7%. Acute kidney injury occurred in 80.6%, with stage 3 KDIGO in most cases. Etiologies were dominated by malignant hypertension (33.3%), pregnancy-related TMA (30.6%), drug-induced TMA (13.9%), and less commonly autoimmune, infectious, or atypical/typical hemolytic uremic syndromes. Renal biopsy, performed in 12 cases, revealed acute and chronic TMA lesions. Treatments included antihypertensive therapy (86.1%), plasma exchange (16.7%), corticosteroids (22.2%), and rituximab (2.7%). No patient received eculizumab. Renal recovery varied widely by etiology, with complete recovery in 90.9% of pregnancy-related cases and none in malignant hypertension. The overall rate of adverse outcomes (death or end stage renal disease (ESRD)) was 58.3%.ConclusionTMA is a heterogeneous condition with diverse etiologies and outcomes. Background

Thrombotic microangiopathies (TMA) comprise a spectrum of clinical conditions sharing common pathological features of microvascular thrombosis leading to microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and end-organ damage [1]. Although rare, they constitute an important cause of acute kidney injury (AKI) and are associated with high morbidity and mortality [2]. The two classic forms - thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) - have become increasingly recognized as part of a broader syndrome with diverse etiologies [2].

While primary TMA syndromes, such as thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and complement-mediated TMA (formerly referred to as atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, aHUS), have been extensively studied in Western populations [3], secondary forms associated with malignant hypertension, pregnancy, medications, or autoimmune diseases remain less characterized, particularly in North African populations.

Recent advances in complement genetics and targeted therapies (e.g., eculizumab) have revolutionized TMA management [4], yet access to these treatments remains limited in resource-constrained settings.

This study aimed to describe the epidemiological, clinical, biological, and histological characteristics of TMA in a Tunisian cohort, with particular focus on etiological distribution and long-term renal outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective descriptive study including all patients admitted to the nephrology-dialysis department of Sahloul university hospital, Sousse, Tunisia between January 2006 and December 2022 for TMA management. Among 47 identified cases, 36 met inclusion criteria after excluding 11 for incomplete data.

Diagnostic criteria [1]

TMA was diagnosed based on the presence of all three of the following criteria:

Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (schistocytes ≥ 1%, hemoglobin < 10 g/dL, LDH > 2×ULN, haptoglobin < 0.1 g/L).

Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (schistocytes ≥ 1%, hemoglobin < 10 g/dL, LDH > 2×ULN, haptoglobin < 0.1 g/L).

Thrombocytopenia (< 150,000/mm³).

Thrombocytopenia (< 150,000/mm³).

Organ involvement (renal, neurological, or gastrointestinal).

Organ involvement (renal, neurological, or gastrointestinal).

Organ involvement was defined as acute dysfunction of the kidney (AKI stage ≥ 2), central nervous system (seizures, confusion, stroke), or gastrointestinal tract (abdominal pain, diarrhea, bleeding).

Although schistocytes and low haptoglobin were considered key diagnostic markers, their absence did not preclude inclusion if other features consistent with TMA were present. Diagnosis was based on a combination of available laboratory and clinical findings, adapted to local resource constraints.

Due to limited access to ADAMTS13 assays, complement testing, and genetic analysis, diagnostic criteria were adapted pragmatically based on available clinical and laboratory data. Diagnosis was supported by surrogate scoring systems (PLASMIC, French score), renal biopsy findings, and disease evolution, in accordance with context-specific practices and published recommendations for resource-limited settings.

Etiological classification followed current guidelines

TMA syndromes were classified as either primary (idiopathic or complement-mediated) or secondary (associated with identifiable clinical triggers such as malignant hypertension, pregnancy, autoimmune disease, or drug exposure), in accordance with the updated framework proposed by Aigner et al. [2], and consistent with international guidelines such as KDIGO and ISTH.

Definitions

Malignant hypertension was defined as a hypertensive emergency characterized by a marked elevation in blood pressure (typically ≥ 180/120 mmHg) associated with acute target-organ damage, including retinal hemorrhages, papilledema, acute kidney injury, or hypertensive encephalopathy, in accordance with KDIGO guidelines on blood pressure management in CKD patients [5].

Malignant hypertension was defined as a hypertensive emergency characterized by a marked elevation in blood pressure (typically ≥ 180/120 mmHg) associated with acute target-organ damage, including retinal hemorrhages, papilledema, acute kidney injury, or hypertensive encephalopathy, in accordance with KDIGO guidelines on blood pressure management in CKD patients [5].

Data collection

Variables included demographic characteristics, clinical presentation, biological parameters (hematological, renal, immunological), histological findings (when available), treatment modalities, and short- and long-term outcomes.

Individual-level data for all patients, including demographics, laboratory parameters, renal biopsy findings, treatment, and outcomes, are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS v26. Given the descriptive nature of the study and the limited sample size, no formal statistical comparisons between subgroups were performed. Results are presented as counts, percentages, means ± standard deviation, or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate.

Results

A total of 47 patients were initially identified with TMA admitted to the nephrology department between 2006 and 2023. After excluding 11 patients due to missing data, the final cohort included 36 patients.

The mean age of the cohort was 35.6 ± 11.3 years (range 18 to 71), with a slight female predominance (56%, sex ratio M/F = 0.8). Nearly 28% of patients had no relevant past medical history. Hypertension and chronic kidney disease were the most common comorbidities (see Table 1). Among women, 6 had a history of miscarriage and 5 had a history of preeclampsia.

Among the five drug-induced cases, one remarkable case involved a young man treated with interferon-beta for relapsing multiple sclerosis, who developed TMA in the setting of acquired complement factor I deficiency. This patient experienced AKI, hemolytic anemia, and severe hypertension. Histological findings showed acute and chronic TMA lesions, including mesangiolysis and onion-skin arteriolar changes. Complement analysis confirmed isolated factor I deficiency.

The management of patients with TMA was based on a combination of general supportive measures and etiology-targeted therapies. Antihypertensive treatment was required in 86.1% of cases, with 41.9% of patients requiring a four-drug regimen to achieve blood pressure control, particularly in the context of malignant hypertension or pregnancy-related TMA. All patients received dietary sodium restriction (2–5 g/day), with fluid restriction implemented in cases of oliguria. Etiological treatment varied depending on the underlying cause and is detailed in Table 2. Emergency renal replacement therapy was initiated in 12 patients due to persistent anuria (n = 4), severe hyperkalemia (n = 5), or acute pulmonary edema (n = 3). Hematological complications, particularly severe anemia (hemoglobin < 7 g/dL or symptomatic anemia), were managed with transfusions of leukocyte-depleted, phenotype-matched red blood cells in eight patients. Neurological complications such as eclampsia with seizures were managed using intravenous magnesium sulfate in combination with valproic acid. None of the patients received eculizumab due to availability constraints.

Clinical and renal outcomes varied by etiology (Table 2). Pregnancy-associated TMA had the most favorable prognosis, with 90.9% achieving full renal recovery. Conversely, malignant hypertension-related TMA had poor renal outcomes, with 83.3% progressing to end-stage kidney disease. All patients with autoimmune TMA died within the first week. The overall incidence of major adverse events (death or ESRD) was 58.3%.

The patient who presented an interferon-beta–associated TMA exhibited hematological improvement within ten days of treatment discontinuation, but no renal recovery was observed, and chronic hemodialysis was initiated. A follow-up immunological workup at two months revealed normalization of factor I levels, supporting the hypothesis that interferon beta triggered TMA through the production of anti–factor I autoantibodies.

Discussion

This 17-year retrospective study offers critical insights into the clinical spectrum, histological features, and outcomes of TMA in a Nephrology setting in North Africa—a region where epidemiological data on TMA remain scarce. The predominance of secondary TMA, particularly those associated with malignant hypertension and pregnancy, mirrors findings in other low- and middle-income countries, where access to preventive care, early diagnostics, and specific treatments remains limited [1, 2]. In contrast, primary forms such as TTP and complement-mediated TMA, which are well-characterized in Western populations [3, 4], were underrepresented in our cohort, likely due to diagnostic limitations such as lack of ADAMTS13 activity testing and complement profiling.

Differentiating complement-mediated TMA from secondary forms remains challenging, particularly in patients with overlapping clinical features or comorbidities. Recent evidence suggests that complement activation may contribute to the pathogenesis of secondary TMA, including pregnancy-associated TMA, hypertensive emergencies, and autoimmune conditions. In our cohort, the classification relied on exclusion of known triggers and clinical evolution, but we acknowledge that complement dysregulation may underlie a subset of cases traditionally considered secondary. This nuance underscores the need for broader access to complement diagnostics and supports a more integrated view of TMA pathophysiology [8]. Recent KDIGO consensus recommendations further expand this understanding by highlighting the role of complement across a wide spectrum of kidney diseases, including TMA syndromes [9].

The clinical presentation in our patients was severe, with a high frequency of hypertension (83.3%), neurological involvement (66.7%), and AKI in over 80% of cases. These findings highlight the renal tropism of TMA and the critical role of nephrologists in early recognition and management. The presence of hemolytic anemia, schistocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and low haptoglobin confirmed the microangiopathic process. Such features align with recent diagnostic guidelines emphasizing the need for prompt identification of TMA based on clinical and laboratory criteria to prevent irreversible organ damage [10, 11].

Renal histopathology revealed a spectrum of acute and chronic TMA lesions, including fibrin thrombi, mesangiolysis, duplication of basement membranes, glomerulosclerosis, and “onion-skin” arteriolar changes. This coexistence of active and chronic lesions suggests diagnostic delays and possibly recurrent or subacute TMA episodes. Studies from sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America report similar histologic patterns, where chronic vascular injury is common due to late referrals and missed early presentations [12, 13]. Importantly, recent studies emphasize that glomerular injury in TMA may be underestimated and that detailed histologic classification may aid in predicting prognosis and guiding treatment [14, 15].

Despite its diagnostic value, renal biopsy remains challenging in TMA due to bleeding risk. In our series, three patients developed perirenal hematomas, one of which necessitated urgent embolization. These complications are consistent with reports identifying TMA as a high-risk scenario for renal biopsy, particularly in the setting of thrombocytopenia, uncontrolled hypertension, and vascular fragility [11]. The current KDIGO guidelines recommend careful patient selection, blood pressure control, and pre-procedural correction of coagulopathy when performing biopsy in suspected TMA [6].

Malignant hypertension accounted for the largest share of etiologies (33.3%) and was associated with the poorest renal prognosis—over 80% progressed to ESRD. This is in line with literature identifying malignant hypertension not only as a cause of secondary TMA but also as a perpetuator of endothelial injury and irreversible vascular damage [16, 17]. In contrast, pregnancy-related TMA—including preeclampsia, eclampsia, and HELLP syndrome—showed excellent renal outcomes, with 90.9% achieving full recovery. These cases typically resolve after delivery, but require close postpartum monitoring to distinguish from other causes such as TTP or complement-mediated TMA, which may be masked by the physiological changes of pregnancy [18, 19].

Pregnancy-related TMA includes a spectrum of entities such as preeclampsia, HELLP syndrome, and complement-mediated TMA occurring during or after pregnancy. While HELLP and preeclampsia typically respond well to delivery, other forms—particularly those without classical features—may be underrecognized and associated with poorer outcomes. Data from the aHUS registry highlight that postpartum TMAs lacking clear obstetric triggers may reflect underlying complement dysregulation and require targeted therapy. Emerging biomarkers such as the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio have shown promise in distinguishing preeclampsia from other TMA syndromes, although their use remains limited in resource-constrained settings [8].The relatively low number of pregnancy-related cases in our cohort, despite high regional birth rates, may reflect referral bias, diagnostic challenges, or underreporting in emergency settings.

The poor renal outcomes observed in the malignant hypertension subgroup raise the possibility that some cases may represent unrecognized complement-mediated TMA. Recent studies have shown that complement activation can contribute to endothelial injury in malignant hypertension, and that a subset of patients may benefit from complement blockade despite the absence of classical complement-mediated TMA features. In particular, failure of renal recovery despite adequate blood pressure control should prompt consideration of underlying complement dysregulation. This hypothesis is supported by Morelle et al., who demonstrated complement activation in secondary TMAs, including MHT-associated forms, and emphasized the diagnostic overlap between complement-mediated TMA and hypertensive TMA [8].

Drug-induced TMA represented 13.9% of our cohort. These cases are increasingly reported worldwide due to the expanding use of immunosuppressants, anti-angiogenic therapies, and interferons. Drug-induced TMA may result from two principal mechanisms: immune-mediated injury, often involving drug-dependent antibodies targeting endothelial cells (e.g., quinine, ticlopidine), and direct dose-dependent toxicity leading to endothelial dysfunction and microvascular thrombosis (e.g., gemcitabine, calcineurin inhibitors). These mechanisms vary by agent and may influence clinical presentation and reversibility [20]. One of our cases, which has already been published by our team, involved a young male treated with interferon-beta for relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis who developed TMA in the context of an acquired complement factor I deficiency [21]. This case contributed to elucidating the mechanism of interferon-beta–associated TMA, which was mediated by the production of anti–factor I autoantibodies leading to uncontrolled complement activation and endothelial injury. This illustrates the expanding understanding of immune dysregulation in drug-induced TMA, where complement involvement may be underrecognized [22].

In the absence of ADAMTS13 activity testing, the diagnosis of TTP was excluded based on clinical presentation and validated scoring systems. The PLASMIC score and the French score were retrospectively applied to assess the probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency. Patients with low scores and atypical clinical features (e.g., predominant renal involvement, absence of neurological signs) were classified as non-TTP. This approach reflects real-world diagnostic strategies in resource-limited settings and has been supported by recent validation studies.

Access to therapy remains a critical issue. None of our patients received eculizumab, the first-line treatment for complement-mediated TMA, due to unavailability. This mirrors the therapeutic inequality observed globally and highlights the need for cost-effective alternatives in low-resource countries [23]. Plasma exchange was used in selected cases, although its efficacy is limited outside of TTP or antibody-mediated forms. Corticosteroids were employed empirically in autoimmune and infectious TMA. Several recent trials support the addition of immunosuppression and complement inhibitors in complement-mediated TMA, but access to these therapies is often lacking in our setting [24, 25].

The prognosis in our series varied widely by etiology. While pregnancy-related TMA had favorable outcomes, malignant hypertension and autoimmune causes had dismal renal and patient prognoses. These findings are in line with Cavero et al., who demonstrated in a multicenter cohort that TMA associated with malignant hypertension is often linked to underlying pathological mechanisms such as complement dysregulation or secondary triggers rather than isolated essential hypertension, and is associated with worse renal outcomes [17]. The overall rate of major adverse outcomes (death or ESRD) was 58.3%. This is consistent with other studies from similar healthcare systems and underscores the urgent need for early diagnosis, etiological stratification, and equitable access to advanced diagnostics and therapeutics [26].

Our study also contributes to the ongoing debate regarding the classification of TMA syndromes. Recent expert consensus suggests a shift from a binary primary/secondary model to one that incorporates triggers, genetic predisposition, and the role of complement dysregulation in diverse clinical contexts [27]. In this regard, systematic access to ADAMTS13 assays, complement gene sequencing, and biomarker-based stratification could significantly enhance precision medicine in TMA, even in secondary forms.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective and single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings. The relatively small sample size, inherent to the rarity of thrombotic microangiopathies, reduced the statistical power for subgroup analyses, including sex-stratified comparisons. Consequently, potential differences in clinical presentation or outcomes between male and female patients could not be formally assessed.

Second, some diagnostic criteria could not be systematically applied due to missing data at presentation. Not all patients had complete laboratory workup at admission, particularly schistocyte counts and haptoglobin levels. This reflects real-world constraints in emergency settings and underscores the need for flexible, context-adapted diagnostic strategies in resource-limited environments.

Third, the lack of systematic access to ADAMTS13 activity assays and complement genetic testing limited the precision of etiological classification in several cases. Finally, therapeutic decisions were influenced by drug availability and local protocols, which may differ from international standards and affect treatment comparability.

Conclusion

TMA remains a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge in nephrology. Etiological diversity and outcome variability underscore the importance of rapid identification and specific management strategies. Access to advanced therapies such as complement inhibitors remains a priority to improve prognosis in low-resource countries. Furthermore, we plan to continue and expand our registry in the future, with the aim of facilitating case referral and, when resources allow, incorporating advanced diagnostic tools such as genetic screening in collaboration with specialized centers.
