SRSUPM: Sequential Recommender System Based on User Psychological Motivation
Yicheng Di, Yuan Liu, Zhi Chen, Jingcai Guo

Abstract
Sequential recommender infers users’ evolving psychological motivations from historical interactions to recommend the next preferred items. Most existing methods compress recent behaviors into a single vector and optimize it toward a single observed target item, but lack explicit modeling of psychological motivation shift. As a result, they struggle to uncover the distributional patterns across different shift degrees and to capture collaborative knowledge that is sensitive to psychological motivation shift. We propose a general framework, the Sequential Recommender System Based on User Psychological Motivation (SRSUPM), to enhance sequential recommenders with psychological motivation shift-aware user modeling. Specifically, the Psychological Motivation Shift Assessment (PMSA) quantitatively measures psychological motivation shift; guided by PMSA, the Shift Information Construction models dynamically evolving multi-level shift states, and the Psychological Motivation Shift-driven Information Decomposition decomposes and regularizes representations across shift levels. Moreover, the Psychological Motivation Shift Information Matching strengthens collaborative patterns related to psychological motivation shift to learn more discriminative user representations. Extensive experiments on three public benchmarks show that SRSUPM consistently outperforms representative baselines on diverse sequential recommender tasks.

Introduction

In sequential recommender systems, the goal is to infer users’ next psychologically driven target items by modeling the latent dependencies in historical interaction sequences
Zhang et al. 2024c
. Modern sequential recommenders typically learn a low-dimensional user representation to describe the user’s current psychological preference state and rank candidate items accordingly. To improve representation quality, a large body of work adopts deep sequence models, especially Transformer-based architectures, often enhanced with contrastive objectives
Li et al. 2025
. Meanwhile, incorporating user and item side information has become an important direction for strengthening sequence modeling and representation expressiveness
Hong et al. 2025
. Real-world next-item prediction is often one-to-many. Prior work addresses this non-determinism via uncertainty or stochastic modeling, preference instability, or causal disentanglement, but it rarely explicitly quantifies which motivation changes and by how much for a specific history–target pair.

In practice, training data for sequential recommender are commonly constructed via a sliding-window mechanism, which segments a user’s interaction history into input subsequences and corresponding target items
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025b
. Under this standard formulation, most methods share a similar paradigm, regardless of whether they rely only on item IDs
Hou et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025a
or further incorporate side information
Ye et al. 2025
. Specifically, they encode each input subsequence into a user representation and maximize its matching consistency with the target item embedding, so that the representation is progressively shaped to reflect the psychologically motivated preference signaled by the next interaction.

However, this paradigm implicitly assumes that a user’s psychological motivation evolves smoothly over time
Yuan et al. 2025
, i.e., the psychological motivation reflected by the input subsequence is largely consistent with that of the target item. This assumption is often violated in practice: users may exhibit psychological motivation shift
Zhang et al. 2024a
, where subsequent behaviors correspond to substantially different psychological motivations than those suggested by the recent history. Existing efforts that model multi-faceted or multi-granularity motivations
Han et al. 2024
do not explicitly address psychological motivation shift as the underlying driving factor. Other recent methods employ Gaussian distributions to characterize instability and uncertainty of user interests
Lv et al. 2024
, yet they mainly focus on local fluctuations at the interaction level and may overlook systematic mismatch between an entire input subsequence and its target caused by psychological motivation shift. More broadly, existing uncertainty-, stochasticity-, and causality-based methods rarely quantify the subsequence–target shift, nor do they condition collaborative learning on such shift when aligning users.

We argue that modeling user behavior from a psychological motivation shift perspective offers clear advantages.
By linking different next items to distinct motivation compositions and their mismatch with recent context, it provides an interpretable view of the one-to-many future
Wang and Shen 2024
;
Balsells-Rodas et al. 2024
.
Moreover, motivation shifts can be efficiently quantified using semantic side information as an annotation-free training signal, and further facilitate collaborative learning by aligning users only when their motivations and shift intensities are compatible, thus avoiding over-smoothing shift-sensitive patterns
Ma et al. 2024
;
Liao et al. 2025
;
Wu et al. 2024
. From this perspective, as depicted in Fig.
1
, we identify two key challenges brought by the psychological motivation shift. First, CH1: Psychological Motivation Shift. In real-world scenarios, the same historical context can lead to diverse subsequent items, which correspond to different degrees of psychological motivation shift. Under the conventional user representation learning paradigm, models are usually optimized with a single observed target item, so the learned representation is steered toward fitting only one specific shift level. This makes it difficult to simultaneously characterize multi-level psychological motivation shift and uncover the latent distributional patterns and uncertainties across different shift degrees. Second, CH2: Collaborative Learning Limitation. Collaborative learning in sequential recommender often regards users associated with the same target item as similar and aligns their representations to the same target embedding. Such a process typically overlooks users’ differences in psychological motivation composition and future psychological motivation shift levels, implicitly assuming similar evolution patterns. Consequently, the model cannot distinguish shift-sensitive users from relatively stable ones, nor can it effectively capture shift-sensitive collaborative knowledge for fine-grained recommendations.

To address these challenges, we propose a generic framework, the Sequential Recommender System Based on User Psychological Motivation (SRSUPM), which can be seamlessly integrated into a wide range of backbone sequential recommenders under the conventional representation learning paradigm. Specifically, we design a Psychological Motivation Shift Assessment (PMSA) to quantitatively measure each user’s psychological motivation shift degree. Based on PMSA, the Shift Information Construction builds multi-level shift representations on top of the backbone representation to describe users’ potential psychological states under different shift levels, while the Psychological Motivation Shift-driven Information Decomposition decomposes and regularizes representations across shift levels to better model the distribution of psychological motivation shift. Furthermore, we develop the Psychological Motivation Shift Information Matching, which enforces a stricter shift-aware similarity criterion to align corresponding shift representations and explicitly capture collaborative knowledge sensitive to psychological motivation shift.

In summary, our main contributions are as follows:
(1) We propose SRSUPM, a generic framework tailored to psychological motivation shift, which can be integrated into various backbone sequential recommenders to model shift-aware user evolution and collaboration.
(2) We design Psychological Motivation Shift Assessment, Shift Information Construction, and Psychological Motivation Shift-driven Information Decomposition to quantify motivation shift and construct multi-level shift representations with distributional regularization.
(3) Extensive experiments on three real-world datasets demonstrate that SRSUPM achieves significant improvements across multiple representative backbone models.

2
Related Work

2.1
Sequential Recommender

In sequential recommender systems, most methods follow a user representation learning paradigm that encodes interaction sequences into user representations and scores candidate items for next-item prediction
Liu et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
. Representative backbones include RNN-based models such as GRU4Rec
Hidasi et al. 2015
, CNN-based Caser
Tang and Wang 2018
, and Transformer-based SASRec
Kang and McAuley 2018
. Recent studies further incorporate self-supervised and contrastive objectives to enhance representation robustness and discriminativeness, e.g., Meta-SGCL
Hao et al. 2024
, IOCLRec
Wang et al. 2025
, and IBLSRec
Niu et al. 2025
. However, these methods mainly optimize the alignment between a single user vector and the next-item target, and thus rarely capture the distributional patterns associated with different degrees of psychological motivation shift.

2.2
Psychological Motivation Modeling

User preferences evolve with time and context, and psychological motivation shift has attracted growing attention in sequential recommender
Xie et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024c
. Existing studies mainly follow two directions: distributional or causal modeling of preference instability, such as STOSA
Fan et al. 2022
and CDR
Wang et al. 2023
, and multi-motivation modeling for complex user behaviors, such as IMSR
Wang and Shen 2023
and PoMRec
Dong et al. 2025
. However, they rarely explicitly model the intensity of psychological motivation shift or characterize its latent distributional patterns across different shift degrees. Our work addresses this gap by enabling distribution-aware modeling of psychological motivation shift and shift-sensitive collaborative learning.

Problem Formulation

In this paper, we define a set of users as
M=\{m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{|M|}\}
and a set of items as
N=\{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{|N|}\}
. To effectively measure changes in users’ psychological motivations, we introduce multi-label category information with a global category set
G=\{g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{|G|}\}
. Each item
n_{k}\in N
is associated with a category set
G_{n_{k}}\subseteq G
. For each user
m\in M
, the input sequence is
H_{m}=\{h_{1}^{m},\ldots,h_{o}^{m}\}
, where
o
denotes the sequence length and
h_{s}^{m}=(n_{s}^{m},G_{n_{s}^{m}})
is the interaction at time step
s
. Formally, given
H_{m}
, we aim to infer
n_{o+1}^{m}
at time step
o+1
:

n_{o+1}^{m}=\underset{n_{k}\in N}{\operatorname{argmax}}\mathcal{T}(n_{k}\mid H_{m}).

(1)

Methodology

We propose SRSUPM to model psychological-motivation shifts as a general plug-in framework applicable to most sequential recommender models. Using SASRec
Kang and McAuley 2018
as the backbone, Fig.
2
shows the overall architecture.

4.1
Psychological Motivation Shift Assessment

We operationalize psychological motivation shifts using item category labels. We define the psychological motivation shift degree (PMSD) and apply a deconstructed bucket-mapping function to map continuous PMSD into discrete shift levels.

4.1.1
Psychological Motivation Shift Degree

We generate input-target pairs via a sliding window
Qu et al. 2025
. We measure the motivation correlation between
H_{m}
and
n_{o+1}^{m}
by the intersection-over-union of their category sets and convert it to PMSD, defined as follows:

\mathrm{PMSD}(H_{m},n_{o+1}^{m})=1-\frac{|G_{n_{o+1}}^{m}\cap\bigcup_{s=1}^{o}G_{n_{s}}^{m}|}{|G_{n_{o+1}}^{m}|},

(2)

where
G_{n_{o+1}^{m}}
is the category set of the target item
n_{o+1}^{m}
, and
\bigcup_{s=1}^{o}G_{n_{s}^{m}}
is the union of the category sets of all interacted items in
H_{m}
. The second term of PMSD represents the overlap between the target item’s categories and those of the input sequence, with a lower overlap implying a higher PMSD. If the target item’s categories are absent in the input sequence, it indicates a complete shift in the user’s psychological motivation. PMSD ranges from 0 to 1.

4.1.2
Psychological Motivation Shift Deconstruction

To characterize multi-level psychological motivation shifts, we design a deconstructed bucket-mapping function that partitions PMSD into
V
discrete levels
\{1,2,\ldots,V\}
. For a PMSD value
a
, the mapping is defined as follows:

b=\text{bucket}(a)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }a=0\\
\lceil(V-2)\cdot a\rceil+1,&\text{if }0<a<1\\
V,&\text{if }a=1\end{cases}.

(3)

PMSD
\in\{0,1\}
indicates no shift and complete shift when PMSD equals
0
and
1
, mapped to levels
1
and
V
, respectively. For
0<
PMSD
<1
, we evenly split
(0,1)
into
V-2
sub-intervals and assign them to levels
\{2,\ldots,V-1\}
in order. We denote the deconstructed measurement as
b=\mathrm{PMSA}(H_{m},n_{o+1}^{m})
, as illustrated in Fig.
2
.

4.2
Individual Information Encoding

In SRSUPM, we use an existing sequential recommender as the backbone to learn user representations from input sequences. For ID-based backbones such as SASRec, the input consists only of item IDs. Since SRSUPM can also be combined with side-information-aware backbones by incorporating category-side information and replacing the backbone network. Implementation-wise, the interaction sequence is embedded into dense vectors and fed into a sequential encoder to obtain the user representation, formalized as:

\mathbf{z}_{m}=\mathbf{E}\mathbf{N}(H_{m};\mathcal{B}),

(4)

where
\mathbf{EN}(\cdot)
is the backbone encoder
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathbf{z}_{m}\in\mathcal{R}^{d}
is user
m
’s
d
-dimensional latent representation.

4.3
Shift Information Construction

Most sequential recommender methods implicitly assume stable psychological motivations when learning user representations, while users’ motivations can shift over time. Although traditional representation learning may adapt
\mathbf{z}_{m}
toward the target item, it does not explicitly capture the intensity of such shifts. To model shifting psychological motivations, we propose a Shift Information Construction (SIC), which takes the original user vector
\mathbf{z}_{m}
as input and outputs the corresponding shift information:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}=\mathrm{SIC}(\mathbf{z}_{m}),

(5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{SIC}(\mathbf{a})=\mathrm{LN}(\mathrm{Dropout}(\mathbf{a}+\mathrm{LinearProj}(\mathbf{a}))),

(6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{LinearProj}(\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b},

(7)

where
\mathrm{LinearProj}(\cdot)
injects complementary semantics via
\mathbf{a}+\mathrm{LinearProj}(\mathbf{a})
, with
\mathrm{LN}(\cdot)
and
\mathrm{Dropout}(\cdot)
for stability and regularization. We run
V
independent SIC modules in parallel on
\mathbf{z}_{m}
to obtain
\{\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{v}\}_{v=1}^{V}
, where
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{v}=\mathrm{SIC}_{v}(\mathbf{z}_{m})\in\mathcal{R}^{d}
, each targeting a distinct shift intensity under subsequent decomposition constraints.

4.4
Psychological Motivation Shift-driven Information Decomposition

During training, Psychological Motivation Shift-driven Information Decomposition (PMSID) aims to capture the latent distribution of users’ psychological motivation shifts over
V
intensity levels. For user
m
, we compute the shift distribution as the normalized attention scores between the target item embedding and each shift-information vector:

\mathrm{softmax}([\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{n_{o+1}}^{m},\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{V}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{n_{o+1}}^{m}]).

However, without explicit decompositional supervision,
\{\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{v}\}
may collapse to homogeneous representations, leading to poorly separated weights. To prevent this, we introduce supervision from the psychological motivation shift assessment. Specifically, we obtain the shift level

b=\mathrm{PMSA}(H_{m},n_{o+1}^{m}),

and design a decomposition objective that, using
\mathbf{x}_{n_{o+1}}^{m}
as an anchor, pulls
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{b}
closer while pushing the remaining vectors away, thereby enforcing level-wise distinguishability:

\mathcal{L}_{dec}=-\sum_{(H_{m},n_{o+1}^{m})\in\mathcal{S}}\log\frac{\exp(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{b}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{n_{o+1}^{m}})}{\sum_{l=1}^{V}\exp(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{l}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{n_{o+1}^{m}})},

(8)

where
\mathcal{S}
is the training set. Using
b
and
\mathbf{x}_{n_{o+1}^{m}}\in\mathcal{R}^{d}
as supervision and anchor, Eq. (
8
) enforces separation between
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{b}
and
\{\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{v}\}_{v\neq b}
, yielding level-discriminative SIC representations and a jointly optimized shift distribution for inference.

4.5
Psychological Motivation Shift Information Matching

Even for the same target item, users with different histories can exhibit different psychological motivation shift levels and motivation compositions. Traditional user-standard learning in sequential recommenders often ignores such fine-grained differences, limiting their ability to exploit shift-driven collaborative signals. To address this, we propose Psychological Motivation Shift Information Matching. We define shift-driven similar users as those who share the same target item and have consistent shift levels between the target item and the input sequence, i.e., similar PMSD values and category-overlap ratios, which makes their psychological-motivation components more comparable. For user
m
with
b=\mathrm{PMSA}(H_{m},n_{o+1}^{m})
, the similar-user set is defined as:

\!\!\!\!\mathbf{Y}_{m}\!\!=\!\{m^{\prime}\!\mid\!m^{\prime}\!\!\in\!M\!\!,n_{o+1}^{m^{\prime}}\!\!=\!n_{o+1}^{m},\text{PMSA}(H_{m^{\prime}}\!,n_{o+1}^{m^{\prime}})\!\!=\!b\}.

(9)

We sample
m^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Y}_{m}
, compute
\{\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m^{\prime}}^{v}\}_{v=1}^{V}
with the shared backbone and SIC, and sum-pool them to obtain the basic views of
m
and
m^{\prime}
with superscript
cn
:

\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{cn}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{V}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{\nu},\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m^{\prime}}^{cn}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{V}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m^{\prime}}^{\nu}.

(10)

For contrastive learning, we construct two views: an unsupervised augmentation view with superscript
mq
and a supervised sampling view with superscript
hh
. To reduce overhead, we apply a lightweight model-level augmentation
\mathrm{Dropout}
to the basic view of user
m
to obtain the augmented view, and use the basic view of the sampled similar user
m^{\prime}
as the supervised view:

\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{\textit{mq}}=\mathrm{Dropout}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{\textit{cn}}),\quad\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{\textit{hh}}=\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m^{\prime}}^{\textit{cn}}.

The two views form a positive pair, while views from different users form negative pairs. We employ InfoNCE
He et al. 2020
as the contrastive objective, given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{mat}=\sum_{m\in M}\mathcal{L}_{ce}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{mq},\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{hh})+\mathcal{L}_{ce}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{hh},\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{mq}),

(11)

\displaystyle\resizebox{19218570}{}{$\!\!\!\!\!\mathcal{L}_{ce}(\!\mathbf{a}_{m}^{1},\mathbf{a}_{m}^{2}\!)\!\!=\!\!-\!\log\frac{\exp(\mathrm{h}(\!\mathbf{a}_{m}^{1},\mathbf{a}_{m}^{2})\!)}{\exp(\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{a}_{m}^{1}\!,\mathbf{a}_{m}^{2})\!)\!+\!\sum_{m^{\!-\!}\in\!\mathcal{A}\!\!}\exp(\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{a}_{m}^{1}\!,\mathbf{a}_{m^{\!-\!}}^{2}))}$},

(12)

\displaystyle\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{a}_{m}^{1},\mathbf{a}_{m}^{2})=\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{a}_{m}^{1})\cdot\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{a}_{m}^{2}),

(13)

where
(\mathbf{a}_{m}^{1},\mathbf{a}_{m}^{2})
is the positive pair,
\mathcal{A}
are in-batch negatives, and
\mathrm{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
is normalized inner-product similarity. Eq. (
11
) aligns shift-driven similar users to enhance collaborative learning under psychological motivation shifts.

4.6
Prediction and Model Optimization

Leveraging the shift information that captures different degrees of users’ psychological motivation changes, we use candidate item embeddings
\mathbf{x}_{n_{k}}\in\mathcal{R}^{d}
to instantiate users’ latent psychological motivation shift distributions, formalized as:

\mathbf{f}_{m,k}=\text{softmax}([\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{n_{k}},...,\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{V}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{n_{k}}]),

(14)

where
\mathbf{f}_{m,k}\in\mathcal{R}^{V}
. We use
\mathbf{f}_{m,k}
to aggregate an integrated psychological-motivation representation for
(m,n_{k})
and compute its preference score:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{z}}_{m,k}=\sum_{v=1}^{V}\mathbf{f}_{m,k}^{v}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{m}^{v},

(15)

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{m,k}=y(m,n_{k})=\overline{\mathbf{z}}_{m,k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{n_{k}}.

(16)

With this instantiated distribution,
\overline{\mathbf{z}}_{m,k}
more reliably captures the psychological motivation intensity of user
m
toward item
n_{k}
. The prediction in Eqs. (
14
-
16
) is applied to all candidate items in parallel.

n_{o+1}^{m}=\underset{n_{k}\in N}{\operatorname{argmax}}y(m,n_{k}).

(17)

For user
m
, we compute the probability distribution over candidate items as

\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{m}=\mathrm{softmax}(\hat{p}_{m,1},\ldots,\hat{p}_{m,|N|})\in\mathcal{R}^{|N|}.

We then optimize the recommender with cross-entropy loss over
\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{m}
, updating model parameters as:

\mathcal{L}_{rs}=-\sum_{m\in M}\sum_{k=1}^{|N|}\mathbf{p}_{m}[k]\log\left(\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{m}[k]\right),

(18)

where
\mathbf{p}_{m}
is a one-hot ground-truth label. We train SRSUPM with a multi-task objective that combines the recommender, decomposition, and matching losses:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{rs}+\gamma_{1}\mathcal{L}_{dec}+\gamma_{2}\mathcal{L}_{mat},

(19)

where
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are weighting coefficients that balance the corresponding loss terms.

5.3
Ablation Study (RQ3)

To evaluate the contribution of each component in SRSUPM, we conduct ablation studies on the SASRec-based instantiation and consider four variants: (1) w/o PMSID&PMSIM, removing both Psychological Motivation Shift-driven Information Decomposition and Psychological Motivation Shift Information Matching; (2) w/o PMSIM, removing Psychological Motivation Shift Information Matching; (3) w/o PMSID, removing Psychological Motivation Shift-driven Information Decomposition; (4) w/o PMI, replacing
\bar{z}_{m,k}
in Eq. (
16
) with mean pooling over all psychological motivation shift representations. The results are reported in Table
4
.
Overall, all variants outperform the original SASRec on R@10 and N@10 across three datasets, validating the effectiveness of modeling psychological motivation shifts. The full SRSUPM consistently achieves the best performance, indicating that PMSID and PMSIM are complementary. Removing either module leads to clear performance degradation, with w/o PMSID generally performing worse than w/o PMSIM, suggesting that shift-driven decomposition plays a more critical role, while PMSIM further enhances performance by exploiting shift-consistent collaborative signals. In addition, the inferior results of w/o PMI show that mean pooling dilutes dominant motivation signals, whereas
\bar{z}_{m,k}
better captures the prevailing psychological motivation under the current context.

V

V

and

\gamma_{1}

6
Conclusion

This work proposes SRSUPM, a plug-and-play framework for modeling users’ temporal psychological motivation shifts in sequential recommender. We introduce PMSA to quantify shift degrees, construct SIC to learn multi-level shift representations and their latent distribution, and further apply PMSID and PMSIM to enforce level-wise decomposition and shift-driven matching. Experiments on three real-world datasets with multiple backbones show consistent and significant gains. Future work includes finer-grained shift modeling and integrating causal and online updating mechanisms.
