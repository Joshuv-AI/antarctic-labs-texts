Hierarchical modeling of gravitational-wave populations for disentangling environmental and modified-gravity effects
Shubham Kejriwal, Enrico Barausse, Alvin J. K. Chua

Abstract
The upcoming Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA) will detect up to thousands of extreme-mass-ratio inspirals (EMRIs). These sources will spend ∼ 10 5 \sim 10^{5} cycles in band, and are therefore sensitive to tiny changes in the general-relativistic dynamics, potentially induced by astrophysical environments or modifications of general relativity (GR). Previous studies have shown that these effects can be highly degenerate for a single source. However, it may be possible to distinguish between them at the population level, because environmental effects should impact only a fraction of the sources, while modifications of GR would affect all. We therefore introduce a population-based hierarchical framework to disentangle the two hypotheses. Using simulated EMRI populations, we perform tests of the null vacuum-GR hypothesis and two alternative beyond-vacuum-GR hypotheses, namely migration torques (environmental effects) and time-varying G G (modified gravity). We find that with as few as ≈ 20 \approx 20 detected sources, our framework can statistically distinguish between these three hypotheses, and even indicate if both environmental and modified gravity effects are simultaneously present in the population. Our framework can be applied to other models of beyond-vacuum-GR effects available in the literature.

In this paper, we propose for the first time a framework to detect and distinguish between the two main classes of beyond-vacuum-GR effects using Bayesian hierarchical modeling, even when both classes of effects are present in the system simultaneously. The first class includes effects such as astrophysical environments, which introduce, e.g., corrections to the CO’s orbit through tidal resonances
Bonga et al. 2019
or migration torques
Barausse and Rezzolla 2008
;
Kocsis et al. 2011
;
Barausse et al. 2014
;
Speri et al. 2023
;
Copparoni et al. 2025
. These effects can be independently realized across all sources, and may only be present in a fraction of the total population. We collectively refer to such effects as
local
in this paper. The second class of effects includes fundamental physics modifications that are universal in nature, i.e., present in all sources simultaneously. In many cases, these corrections are controlled by an intrinsic “strength” constant across the population, such that GR is recovered when the strength parameter assumes a null value. An example is the time-varying Newton’s constant
G
(see e.g.
Yunes et al. 2010
;
Chamberlain and Yunes 2017
), which yields GR when the rate of change of
G
vanishes, i.e.
\dot{G}=0

Brans and Dicke 1961
, deviations from the GR predictions may also depend on the nature of the sources and/or its mass and spin
Sotiriou and Zhou 2014
;
Dima et al. 2020
;
Maselli et al. 2020
, but are still expected to affect the whole EMRI population. We refer to this class of effects as
global
in this paper.

This classification allows us to model local and global effects at different levels in a hierarchical framework. We can then perform hypothesis testing to compare the null vacuum-GR hypothesis with the local and global effect hypotheses, to test the preference of the data for one over another. Explicitly, the hypotheses that we will consider are:

•

Vacuum-GR hypothesis

(
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}_{v}}
)
: all sources evolve according to vacuum-GR dynamics;

•

Local effect hypothesis

(
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}_{\ell}}
)
: a nonzero fraction of the sources are affected by a local (e.g. astrophysical environment) effect, while the rest follow vacuum-GR;

•

Global effect hypothesis

(
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}_{g}}
)
: all sources experience a global (e.g. modified gravity) correction to the vacuum-GR dynamics.

I.1
Executive summary of results

In this paper, we apply the above framework to specific models of local and global effects. For the local effect, we choose migration torques, which are induced on the CO due to the presence of an accretion disk around the MBH. For the global effect, we select the time-varying
G
model, with
\dot{G}~[\rm yr^{-1}]
the constant intrinsic strength of the effect. We construct four different EMRI populations,
P_{v}
,
P_{\ell}
,
P_{g}
, and
P_{\rm mix}
. The first three,
P_{v}
,
P_{\ell}
, and
P_{g}
, are generated assuming
\mathcal{H}_{v}
,
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{g}
as the truth, respectively. The fourth “mixed” population
P_{\rm mix}
is constructed such that all sources experience a time-varying
G
, while a fraction of them are simultaneously affected by migration torques. We also consider three different population sizes, with 100, 500, and 1000 sources, of which
\approx 17\%
are observable. We find that even for the population with 100 sources (of which
17
are observable), the hierarchical framework can reliably recover the correct hypothesis under which the population was constructed, and even indicate the concurrent presence of local and global effects in the population.

We also gauge the robustness of our method for different choices of local and global effect parameters in the true population. We find that if the fraction
f
of the population with a local effect is
\geq 0.9
, the recovered intrinsic strength of time-varying
G
, i.e.
\dot{G}~[\rm yr^{-1}]
, may be significantly biased. Furthermore, we highlight a special case where both local and global effects are present in the population simultaneously, but they effectively “cancel” each other out. In such unique scenarios, the log-Bayes factor
\log\mathcal{B}^{g}_{\ell}
, which quantifies the data’s preference for the global effect hypothesis over the local one, is
\approx 0.0
, and the hierarchical framework struggles to distinguish between the two effects. The results are presented in more detail in Sec.
V
.

Background

II.1
Bayesian hierarchical modeling

Consider a population of
N_{\rm pop}
GW sources modeled by a set of hyperparameters
\vec{\lambda}
. Each source is modeled by a set of parameters
\vec{\theta}
, distributed according to some prior probability density function (pdf),
p(\vec{\theta}|\vec{\lambda},\mathcal{H})
. Here,
\mathcal{H}
is the assumed hypothesis. Of these
N_{\rm pop}
sources, only a subset of size
N_{\rm obs}<N_{\rm pop}
will satisfy a chosen detectability criterion, such that they are considered to be “observed”. The measured data
\vec{d}_{i}
of the
i^{\rm th}
observed sources is represented by its maximum-likelihood estimate (MLE),
\hat{\theta}_{i}
, where
i=1,\ldots,N_{\rm obs}

Mandel et al. 2019
.
1
1

1

This setup, based on
Mandel et al. 2019
, assumes that each
i^{\rm th}
source has its own datastream
\vec{d}_{i}
, which does not directly apply to the LISA context, where all sources will overlap in the datastream instead. We can still apply this framework if we assume
\vec{d}_{i}
to be the
residual
of the
i^{\rm th}
source when all other sources are subtracted from the full datastream, i.e.,
\vec{d}_{i}=\vec{d}-\sum_{j\neq i}\vec{h}(\hat{\theta}_{j})
. Here,
\vec{d}
is the full LISA datastream and
\vec{h}(\hat{\theta}_{j})
is the single-source GW template evaluated at
\hat{\theta}_{j}
.
At the MLE point, the individual source likelihood
p(\vec{d}_{i}|\vec{\theta}_{i},\mathcal{H})
maximizes.

Given the set of measured data for the detected sources
\{\vec{d}_{i}\}
, the (hyper)posterior pdf
p(\vec{\lambda}|\{\vec{d}_{i}\},\mathcal{H})
of the hyperparameter set
\vec{\lambda}
can be written according to Bayes theorem as
Gelman et al. 2013

\displaystyle p(\vec{\lambda}|\{\vec{d}_{i}\},\mathcal{H})=\frac{p(\{\vec{d}_{i}\}|\vec{\lambda},\mathcal{H})\pi(\vec{\lambda}|\mathcal{H})}{\mathcal{Z}(\{\vec{d}_{i}\}|\mathcal{H})}.

(1)

Here
p(\{\vec{d}_{i}\}|\vec{\lambda},\mathcal{H})
is the hyperlikelihood of
\vec{\lambda}
,
\pi(\vec{\lambda}|\mathcal{H})
is its hyperprior, and
\mathcal{Z}(\{\vec{d}_{i}\}|\mathcal{H})
is the evidence for the data under the hypothesis, written explicitly as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\{\vec{d}_{i}\}|\mathcal{H})=\int{\rm d}\vec{\lambda}~p(\{\vec{d}_{i}\}|\vec{\lambda},\mathcal{H})\pi(\vec{\lambda}|\mathcal{H}).

(2)

Assuming that individual sources are realized independently in the population, and using the correspondence between
\vec{d}_{i}
and
\hat{\theta}_{i}
, we can write the hyperlikelihood as an effective product of single-source hyperlikelihoods
Mandel et al. 2019

\displaystyle p(\{\vec{d}_{i}\}|\vec{\lambda},\mathcal{H})=\prod_{i=1}^{N_{\rm obs}}\frac{p(\hat{\theta}_{i}|\vec{\lambda},\mathcal{H})}{\alpha(\vec{\lambda}|\mathcal{H})},

(3)

where
\alpha(\vec{\lambda}|\mathcal{H})
encodes the selection biases in the observation of these sources. With this, the hyperposterior of
\vec{\lambda}
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle p(\vec{\lambda}|\{\vec{d}_{i}\},\mathcal{H})=\frac{\pi(\vec{\lambda}|\mathcal{H})}{\mathcal{Z}(\{\vec{d}_{i}\}|\mathcal{H})}\times\prod_{i=1}^{N_{\rm obs}}\frac{p(\hat{\theta}_{i}|\vec{\lambda},\mathcal{H})}{\alpha(\vec{\lambda}|\mathcal{H})}.~

(4)

II.2
Hypothesis testing

We are interested in testing the vacuum-GR hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{v}
against the alternate beyond-vacuum-GR hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{a}
where
a=\ell,g
. These hypotheses can be modeled in a nested framework, with
\mathcal{H}_{v}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{a}
. The equality holds when a general subset of hyperparameters
\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}\subseteq\vec{\lambda}
under
\mathcal{H}_{a}
assumes a fiducial null value
\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}_{0}
. Then, we can write the Bayes factor for the data’s preference of
\mathcal{H}_{v}
over
\mathcal{H}_{a}
as the Savage-Dickey ratio
Gelman et al. 2013
;
Dickey 1971
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{a}^{v}=\frac{p(\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}=\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}_{0}|\{\vec{d}_{i}\},\mathcal{H}_{a})}{\pi(\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}=\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}_{0}|\mathcal{H}_{a})};~a=\ell,g.~

(5)

Here, the numerator is the marginalized hyperposterior of
\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}
at
\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}_{0}
, and the denominator is the corresponding marginalized hyperprior. Substituting the hyperposterior (
4
) and the evidence (
2
), we can rewrite
\mathcal{B}^{v}_{a}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{v}_{a}=\frac{\prod_{i}p(\hat{\theta}_{i}|\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}=\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}_{0},\mathcal{H}_{a})}{\int{\rm d}\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}~\prod_{i}r_{\alpha}~p(\hat{\theta}_{i}|\vec{\lambda}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}_{a})\pi(\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}|\mathcal{H}_{a})}~

(6)

where we define
r_{\alpha}:=\alpha(\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}=\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}_{0}|\mathcal{H}_{a})/\alpha(\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}|\mathcal{H}_{a})
.

In the following, we set
r_{\alpha}=1
, effectively treating
\alpha(\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}|\mathcal{H}_{a})
as a constant. As argued more formally in Appendix
A
, this assumption holds when
\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}
is a set of beyond-vacuum-GR effect hyperparameters, e.g., the intrinsic strength of time-varying
G
. This is because such effects only perturbatively modify the detectability of a source in the population. Setting
r_{\alpha}=1
mitigates some computational expense in the calculation of the Bayes factor, as the selection function involves an integral over source parameters over the full space. The integrand includes the detection probability
p_{\rm det}(\vec{\theta}|\mathcal{H})
, which is typically a numerical function in the GW context and hence expensive to compute. If
p_{\rm det}(\vec{\theta}|\mathcal{H})
can be cheaply calculated, this factor may be restored feasibly in future studies (see, e.g.,
Chapman-Bird et al. 2023
).

In Eq. (
6
), the single source hyperlikelihood
p(\hat{\theta}_{i}|\vec{\lambda}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}_{a})
can be expressed as a marginal over
\vec{\theta}
,

\displaystyle p(\hat{\theta}_{i}|\vec{\lambda}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}_{a})=\int{\rm d}\vec{\theta}~p(\hat{\theta}_{i}|\vec{\theta},\mathcal{H}_{a})\pi(\vec{\theta}|\vec{\lambda}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}_{a}).~

(7)

Here, under the hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{a}
,
p(\hat{\theta}_{i}|\vec{\theta},\mathcal{H}_{a})
is the likelihood of
\vec{\theta}
under the data
\hat{\theta}_{i}
,
\pi(\vec{\theta}|\vec{\lambda}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}_{a})
is the prior pdf of
\vec{\theta}
given
\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}
used in the analysis, and the integral is over the full space of
\vec{\theta}
. While this explicit integral evaluation can make hypothesis testing expensive, we can analytically approximate it for the models of our interest, as we shall describe in Sec.
IV.2
.

For simplicity, we additionally assume flat priors on the model hyperparameters
\vec{\lambda}
, such that
\pi(\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}|\mathcal{H}_{a})=\pi(\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}=\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}_{0}|\mathcal{H}_{a})
. Hence, the final form of the Bayes factor used for hypothesis testing under our assumptions is

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{v}_{a}=\frac{1}{\pi(\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}=\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}_{0}|\mathcal{H}_{a})}\frac{\prod_{i}p(\hat{\theta}_{i}|\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}=\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}_{0},\mathcal{H}_{a})}{\int{\rm d}\vec{\lambda}^{\prime}\prod_{i}p(\hat{\theta}_{i}|\vec{\lambda}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}_{a})}.~

(8)

III
Setup

III.1
Source model

In this paper, we model individual sources as Kerr EMRIs evolving in circular and equatorial orbits. Our model allows for generic corrections to the vacuum-GR dynamics from migration torques and time-varying
G
effects. We model these effects as simple power-law corrections to the leading-order GR flux of the axial component of the angular momentum
Speri et al. 2023
,

\displaystyle\dot{L}=\dot{L}_{\rm GR}\left(1+A_{\ell}\left(\frac{p}{10M}\right)^{n_{\ell}}+A_{g}\left(\frac{p}{M}\right)^{n_{g}}\right).

(9)

Here
p
is the instantaneous semi-latus rectum of the CO’s orbit and
M
is the MBH mass;
A_{\ell}
is the effective strength of the migration torque effect, with typical values
\sim 10^{-5}

Barausse et al. 2014
;
n_{\ell}
is the post-Newtonian (PN) order at which the local effect enters the evolution, and is
\approx 8
for migration torques in
\alpha
-disks
Barausse et al. 2014
;
A_{g}
is the strength of the time-varying
G
effect, with current best constraints
\lesssim 10^{-12}~[\rm yr^{-1}]

Chamberlain and Yunes 2017
, and
n_{g}
is its PN order in the evolution. For time-varying
G
,
n_{g}=4
, denoting a
-4
PN order correction
Chamberlain and Yunes 2017
;
Speri et al. 2023
. Note that the flux balance equation reduces to vacuum-GR as
A_{\ell},A_{g}\to 0
. We emphasize that such parametric constructions are also available for other beyond-vacuum-GR effects in the literature
Kocsis et al. 2011
;
Barausse et al. 2014
;
Barausse et al. 2016
;
Arun et al. 2022
;
Cole et al. 2023
;
Duque et al. 2025a
;
Speri et al. 2024a
;
Kejriwal et al. 2023
.

In addition to those described above, the EMRI model has the following parameters:
a
, the dimensionless spin of the black hole;
\mu
, the mass of the CO;
d_{L}
, the luminosity distance of the source, or equivalently the redshift
z\equiv z(d_{L})
;
(\theta_{S},\phi_{S})
, the sky localization angles;
(\theta_{K},\phi_{K})
, the spin orientation angles; and
\Phi_{0}
, the initial azimuthal orbital phase.

When inferring sources in the population, we only consider a subset of these parameters. We choose two vacuum-GR parameters,
\vec{v}:=\{\ln M,z\}
, two local effect parameters,
\vec{\ell}:=\{A_{\ell},n_{\ell}\}
, and one global effect parameter
\vec{g}:=\{A_{g}\}
. Hence we can define the source parameter set
\vec{\theta}:=\{\vec{v},\vec{\ell},\vec{g}\}
for data analysis.

III.2
Population model

Given the source model, the EMRI population can be described by
N_{\rm pop}
draws of the source parameters
\vec{\theta}\sim\pi(\vec{\theta}|\vec{\lambda},\mathcal{H})
. We decompose the hyperparameter set similarly to the source parameters,
\vec{\lambda}:=\{\vec{\lambda}_{v},\vec{\lambda}_{\ell},\vec{\lambda}_{g}\}
, and assume independent priors on
\vec{v}
,
\vec{\ell}
, and
\vec{g}
, such that the joint prior
\pi(\vec{\theta}|\vec{\lambda},\mathcal{H})
can be written piecewise,

\displaystyle\pi(\vec{\theta}|\vec{\lambda},\mathcal{H})=\pi(\vec{v}|\vec{\lambda}_{v})\times\begin{cases}1&\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{v}\\
\pi(\vec{\ell}|\vec{\lambda}_{\ell})&\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\ell}\\
\pi(\vec{g}|\vec{\lambda}_{g})&\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{g}\end{cases}.~

(10)

For the vacuum-GR parameters
\vec{v}=\{\ln M,z\}
, we choose a prior pdf
p(\vec{v}|\vec{\lambda}_{v})
that scales with the MBH mass
M
and the source redshift
z
, and also accounts for the size of the comoving volume. Explicitly, we choose (see e.g.
Gair et al. 2010
)

\displaystyle\pi(\vec{v}|\vec{\lambda}_{v})

\displaystyle\propto d_{c}^{2}(z)\frac{{\rm d}n}{{\rm d}\ln{M}{\rm d}z},~

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}n}{{\rm d}\ln{M}{\rm d}z}

\displaystyle:=K\left(\frac{M}{M_{*}}\right)^{\alpha}(1+z)^{\beta}.

(12)

Here, the comoving distance
d_{c}(z)
is related to the redshift
z
given
H_{0}
,
\Omega_{m,0}
, and
\Omega_{\Lambda,0}

Baumann 2022
. Additionally, when calculating the number density of sources,
{\rm d}n/{\rm d}\ln M{\rm d}z
,
K
determines the size of the population, and
M_{*}
sets the scale for the MBH masses. The corresponding hyperparameter set is
\vec{\lambda}_{v}:=\{K,\alpha,\beta\}
, and we fix
M_{*}=3\times 10^{6}M_{\odot}
, following Babak et al.
Babak et al. 2017
.

The local effect parameters
\vec{\ell}=\{A_{\ell},n_{\ell}\}
must be drawn from a distribution such that only a fraction
:=f
of the sources in the population have a non-null value of
\vec{\ell}
, while the remaining
(1-f)
sources follow a vacuum-GR evolution. To incorporate this dependence on
f
, we choose a bimodal distribution for the prior,

\displaystyle\pi(\vec{\ell}|\vec{\lambda}_{\ell}):=(1-f)\delta^{2}(\vec{\ell})+f\mathcal{N}(\vec{\mu}_{\ell}|\vec{\ell},\Sigma_{\ell}).~

(13)

With this choice, a fraction
f
of the sources is chosen from a two-dimensional normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\vec{\mu}_{\ell}|\vec{\ell},\Sigma_{\ell})
with a mean
\vec{\mu}_{\ell}:=(\mu_{A_{\ell}},\mu_{n_{\ell}})
and covariance
\Sigma_{\ell}:={\rm diag}(\sigma_{A_{\ell}}^{2},\sigma_{n_{\ell}}^{2})
. The remaining
(1-f)
draws are identically zero, i.e., they assume the null value of
A_{\ell}=n_{\ell}=0
, enforced by the Dirac-delta
\delta^{2}(\vec{\ell})
. Correspondingly, the local effect hyperparameter set is
\vec{\lambda}_{\ell}:=\{f,(\mu_{A_{\ell}},\mu_{n_{\ell}}),(\sigma_{A_{\ell}},\sigma_{n_{\ell}})\}
.

Finally, for the global effect, we only model the intrinsic strength of time-varying
G
with
\vec{g}=\{A_{g}\}
. Since this strength is modeled to be constant in all sources, we choose a Dirac-delta prior,

\displaystyle\pi(\vec{g}|\vec{\lambda}_{g}):=\delta(A_{g}-\dot{G})~

(14)

where
\dot{G}~[\rm yr^{-1}]
is the value of time-varying
G
at the population level. Hence, the global effect hyperparameter
\vec{\lambda}_{g}=\{\dot{G}\}
. We stress that this distinction between the inferred source parameter
A_{g}
and the hyperparameter
\dot{G}
is important in the data analysis context, since the individual source likelihood only estimates the MLE for
A_{g}
, the value of which may be biased about the true value of
\dot{G}
in the population depending on the assumed hypothesis, the detector-noise realization, etc. A constraint on
\dot{G}
is only obtained hierarchically from these “noisy” estimates of
A_{g}
.

IV
Data analysis techniques

Assuming a fiducial true hypothesis
\mathcal{H}^{*}
, and a corresponding set of true hyperparameters
\vec{\lambda}^{*}
, we can now construct a population of
N_{\rm pop}
sources with inferred parameters
\{\vec{\theta}^{*}\}
drawn from the prior
\pi(\vec{\theta}|\vec{\lambda}^{*},\mathcal{H}^{*})
. Here, we formulate an approximate analysis model to infer these parameters, which can then be used to perform hypothesis testing. For simplicity, we assume the zero-detector-noise realization in this paper, such that our results may be interpreted as averages over many noise realizations drawn from a zero-mean Gaussian process.

IV.1
Obtaining the MLE estimate of observed sources

To claim an observation, we define a detectability criterion based on the optimal signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the signal. Explicitly, we set the criterion
\rho^{2}_{\rm opt}:=\langle\vec{h}(\vec{\theta}^{*})|\vec{h}(\vec{\theta}^{*})\rangle\geq\rho^{2}_{\rm thresh}
. Here
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
is the detector-noise-weighted inner-product
Finn 1992
;
Cutler and Flanagan 1994
, and
\vec{h}
is the GW template evaluated at
\vec{\theta}^{*}
.
\rho_{\rm thresh}
is the fiducial choice of the SNR threshold, which we will quantify below.

The value of the MLE
\hat{\theta}_{i}
for the
i^{\rm th}
observed source depends on the hypothesis assumed in the analysis. If the correct hypothesis
\mathcal{H}^{*}
is assumed, the MLE is simply
\hat{\theta}_{i}=\vec{\theta}_{i}^{*}
. Otherwise, the MLE will be systematically biased away from the truth
Cutler and Vallisneri 2007
;
Cutler and Flanagan 1994
;
Flanagan and Hughes 1998
. Assuming the linearized-signal approximation (LSA)
Vallisneri 2008
, we can approximate these biases by the linear-bias approximation for nested models
Kejriwal et al. 2023
. We provide its explicit form in Eq.
23
of the Appendix
B.1
.

Since the LSA (and hence the linear-bias approximation) holds for “large” SNRs, we can impose that it is valid for the observed sources by setting a similarly large
\rho_{\rm thresh}
. We choose
\rho_{\rm thresh}=20
, which is commonly chosen in the literature (see, e.g.,
Finn 1992
;

IV.2
Approximate hyperlikelihoods

To evaluate the Bayes factor in Eq. (
8
), we must calculate the full-space integral over the source parameters
\vec{\theta}
(
7
) to obtain the single source hyperlikelihood (
3
) in the alternative hypothesis. In general, evaluating this integral can be computationally expensive or practically infeasible. This is especially true in realistic inference setups in GW data analysis, which involve a high-dimensional parameter space.

With the framework constructed above, we show in Appendices
B.2
,
B.3
, and
B.4
that Eq. (
7
) can be analytically approximated under each of the three hypotheses
\mathcal{H}_{v}
,
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{g}
, respectively. Our results make use of the LSA to approximate the individual source likelihood in a given hypothesis
\mathcal{H}
by a normal distribution,

\displaystyle p(\hat{\theta}_{i}|\vec{\theta},\mathcal{H})\approx\mathcal{N}(\vec{\theta}|\hat{\theta}_{i},\Gamma_{i}^{-1}).~

(15)

Here,
\Gamma_{i}
is the Fisher information matrix (FIM), with elements

\displaystyle(\Gamma_{i})_{km}:=\langle\partial_{k}h\big|\partial_{m}h\rangle\big|_{\hat{\theta}_{i}}\approx\langle\partial_{k}h|\partial_{m}h\rangle\big|_{\vec{\theta}_{i}^{*}}

(16)

and
\partial_{k}h:=\partial h/\partial\vec{\theta}^{k}
.

We also validate the constructed approximate hyperlikelihoods against the direct Monte Carlo integration of Eq. (
7
). We describe the validation scheme in detail in Appendix
B.5
. As shown in Fig.
6
of the Appendix, the results are consistent up to stochastic errors in the Monte Carlo integration.

V.2
Robustness check

To more broadly comment on the robustness of the hierarchical modeling framework for decoupling the considered beyond-vacuum-GR effects, i.e., migration torques and time-varying
G
, we now study the variability of the Bayes factor
\mathcal{B}^{g}_{\ell}
as a function of the population’s hyperparameters.

We consider 11 populations of fixed size
N_{\rm pop}=500
. In the first check, we fix
\dot{G}^{*}=10^{-12}[\rm yr^{-1}]
and vary
f^{*}
linearly in the range
[0.0,1.0]
. The setup otherwise is equivalent to that in the previous subsection. As shown in the top panel of Fig.
4
,
\log_{10}(\mathcal{B}^{g}_{\ell})
, which quantifies the data’s preference for the global effect hypothesis over the local, scales inversely with
f^{*}
. This behavior is expected: as
f^{*}
increases in the population, the MLE estimate of the global effect’s source parameter
\hat{A}_{g}
is more biased among sources, and the inferred uncertainty in the hyperparameter
\dot{G}
grows. This makes the hyperposterior on
\dot{G}
more consistent with the null value, and hence the preference of the data for the global effect drops. We also calculate the relative error
r_{\dot{G}}:=|\dot{G}^{*}-\langle\dot{G}\rangle|/\dot{G}^{*}
in all 11 populations. For
f\geq 0.9
, we find that
r_{\dot{G}}\gtrsim 1.0
, i.e., the expectation value
\langle\dot{G}\rangle
incurs significant biases with respect to the truth
\dot{G}^{*}
. This is due to the biases induced by the local effect across a larger fraction of the population.

In the second check, we fix
f^{*}=0.5
and vary
\dot{G}^{*}
linearly in the range
[10^{-13},10^{-12}]
across the 11 populations. As shown in the bottom panel of Fig.
4
,
\log_{10}\mathcal{B}^{g}_{\ell}
scales proportionately with the global effect hyperparameter
\dot{G}^{*}
. At smaller values,
\dot{G}^{*}\lesssim 4\times 10^{-13}~[\rm yr^{-1}]
,
\log_{10}\mathcal{B}^{g}_{\ell}\lesssim 0.0
, i.e., the global effect hypothesis is no longer favored. This is because the data is less sensitive to the global effect if it is too weak, such that the measurement uncertainty in
\dot{G}
grows. Effectively, this analysis suggests that only population realizations with intrinsic strength of the global effect
\dot{G}^{*}\gtrsim 4\times 10^{-13}~[\rm yr^{-1}]
will be discernible in this framework. We highlight that, at the zero crossing point of
\log_{10}\mathcal{B}^{g}_{\ell}
, even though both migration torques and time-varying
G
are present in the population, they effectively “cancel” each other out. In such realizations of the population, the hierarchical framework will struggle to disentangle the two effects without supplementary checks.

VI
Discussion

VI.1
Summary

In this paper, we developed a hierarchical framework for GW populations with beyond-vacuum-GR effects, with the motivation to study how well local effects like astrophysical environments, which may be present in only a fraction of the population, can be decoupled from global ones like modified-GR contributions, which are expected to be present in all sources simultaneously. We consider specific models of local and global beyond-vacuum-GR effects: for the local effect, we choose migration torques induced by an accretion disk around the MBH. For the global effect, we model time-varying
G
, a modified gravity effect present universally with a constant amplitude
\dot{G}[\rm yr^{-1}]
.

Considering four simulated EMRI populations,
P_{v}
,
P_{\ell}
,
P_{g}
, and
P_{\rm mix}
and three different population sizes
N_{\rm pop}=100,500,1000
, we find that the framework consistently recovers the fiducial true hypothesis that was used in constructing these populations, and even indicates whether both local and global effects are present in the population simultaneously. The recovered value of the global effect hyperparameter
\dot{G}
, i.e., the intrinsic strength of the time-varying
G
effect, is consistent with the truth
\dot{G}^{*}
at
\lesssim 0.01\sigma
in all populations. However, the recovered value of the local effect hyperparameter
f
, i.e., the fractional population with migration torques, incurs systematic biases
\sim 3\sigma
and
\sim 1.5\sigma
compared to the true value
f^{*}
in
P_{g}
and
P_{\rm mix}
, respectively, for all three population sizes
N_{\rm pop}=100,500,1000
.

We check that the Bayes factor
\mathcal{B}^{g}_{\ell}
scales proportionally to the intrinsic strength
\dot{G}^{*}[\rm yr]^{-1}
of the global effect in the population, and inversely with the fraction of sources affected by the local effect,
f^{*}
. We also find that the expectation of the global effect hyperparameter
\langle\dot{G}\rangle
may significantly deviate from the ground truth if
f^{*}\geq 0.9
. We further highlighted a scenario where the Bayes factor
\log_{10}\mathcal{B}^{g}_{\ell}\approx 0.0
even when both effects are present in the population, such that the ability to tell them apart with the data is lost. Future studies should study such situations in more detail to further assess the robustness of the hierarchical framework.

VI.2
Future directions and outlook

While we only consider circular-equatorial Kerr EMRIs in this paper, future studies should study the robustness of the hierarchical framework for beyond-vacuum-GR effects in more generic orbits, i.e., including the effects of eccentricity and inclination in the data. Such extensions may reduce parameter space degeneracies and, therefore, the biases incurred in recovering the local and global effect hyperparameters in mixed populations. More accurate models for EMRI waveforms and beyond-vacuum-GR effects are becoming increasingly available
Chapman-Bird et al. 2025
;
Cardoso et al. 2022
;
Duque et al. 2025a
;
Zi and Kumar 2025
;
Duque et al. 2025b
.

When constructing the populations in Sec.
III
, we used independent prior pdfs on different classes of effects, and employed the same priors in the analysis setup. This simplification may not represent reality since the true distribution of the source parameters in the universe will not be known a priori. Additionally, both the local and global effect’s strength may depend on the binary’s vacuum-GR properties, such that the distributions are no longer independent. For more extensive applications of the hierarchical framework, future studies should investigate cases where all population parameters are realistically correlated and are drawn from distributions that differ from the priors used in the analysis.

A broader challenge for the hierarchical modeling framework lies in incorporating it within the proposed global fit pipeline for LISA data analysis
Vallisneri 2009
;
Littenberg and Cornish 2023
;
Katz et al. 2024
. The global fit aims to infer all source types in the data stream simultaneously, and will be expensive to evaluate even in the simplest vacuum-GR hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{v}
. Redoing the global fit for the alternative hypotheses
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
and
\mathcal{H}_{g}
(which also include additional parameters) may not be practically feasible. Yet, it may be possible to perform hypothesis tests in a post-processing style analysis by bias-correcting and importance-weighting the samples obtained in the
\mathcal{H}_{v}
analysis
Kejriwal et al. 2025
. This procedure effectively provides a new set of samples from the hyperposterior in the alternative hypothesis and is particularly well-suited for beyond-vacuum-GR type effects (see
Kejriwal et al. 2025
for more details). Recently, simulation-based inference (SBI) techniques have been proposed for feasible hierarchical modeling of GW sources
Srinivasan et al. 2025
, and may enable efficient modeling and inference of beyond-vacuum-GR effects using source populations.

Finally, we caution that some systematic biases may persist in the hierarchical framework, e.g., due to inaccuracies in waveform modeling which can mimic global effects, as discussed in
Moore et al. 2021
, and should be addressed in future work. In our framework, these systematic biases can be understood to be induced by “local” waveform modeling inaccuracies, which may strongly depend on the source parameters
Khalvati et al. 2025
, biasing the inference of the intrinsic strength
\dot{G}
of the global effect. Our setup can be readily employed to study such systematics in the future. Extensions of the current framework, shaped by these careful considerations, will pave the way for robust and realistic tests of GR in the presence of astrophysical environments with the next generation of GW detectors.

Selection effects for beyond-vacuum-GR hyperparameters

We denote the null vacuum-GR hypothesis as
\mathcal{H}_{v}
and the alternative local and global effect hypotheses jointly as
\mathcal{H}_{a}=\mathcal{H}_{\ell},\mathcal{H}_{g}
. We decompose the hyperparameter set as
\vec{\lambda}=\{\vec{\lambda}_{v},\vec{\lambda}_{a}\}
, and the source parameters
\vec{\theta}=\{\vec{\theta}_{v},\vec{\theta}_{a}\}
, where a subscript
v
denotes vacuum-GR and
a
denotes beyond-vacuum-GR effect parameters. In this notation, the selection function can be written as
Mandel et al. 2019

\displaystyle\alpha(\vec{\lambda}|\mathcal{H}_{a})=\int{\rm d}\vec{\theta}~p_{\rm det}(\vec{\theta})\pi(\vec{\theta}|\vec{\lambda},\mathcal{H}_{a})~

(20)

where
p_{\rm det}(\vec{\theta})
is the probability of detecting a source with parameters
\vec{\theta}
, and
\pi(\vec{\theta}|\vec{\lambda},\mathcal{H}_{a})
is the prior pdf of
\vec{\theta}
for the given hyperparameter set
\vec{\lambda}
in the alternative hypothesis.

Since
\mathcal{H}_{a}
describes a set of (perturbative) beyond-vacuum-GR effects, we argue that the detection probability is approximately independent of
\vec{\theta}_{a}
, such that

\displaystyle p_{\rm det}(\vec{\theta})\approx p_{\rm det}(\vec{\theta}_{v}).

(21)

Additionally, if we assume that the priors of
\vec{\theta}_{v}
and
\vec{\theta}_{a}
are independent, i.e.,
\pi(\vec{\theta}|\vec{\lambda},\mathcal{H}_{a})=\pi(\vec{\theta}_{v}|\vec{\lambda}_{v})\pi(\vec{\theta}_{a}|\vec{\lambda}_{a})
, we get

\displaystyle\alpha(\vec{\lambda}_{a}|\mathcal{H}_{a})\approx\mathcal{C}_{v}\int{\rm d}\vec{\theta}_{a}~p(\vec{\theta}_{a}|\vec{\lambda}_{a}).~

(22)

Here,
\mathcal{C}_{v}:=\int{\rm d}\vec{\lambda}_{v}{\rm d}\vec{\theta}_{v}~p_{\rm det}(\vec{\theta}_{v})\pi(\vec{\theta}_{v}|\vec{\lambda}_{v})

is a constant. Since the remaining integral in Eq. (
22
) is independent of the detection probability, it is effectively calculated over the full space of
\vec{\theta}_{a}
. In this paper, we only consider proper priors for the beyond-vacuum-GR effects, and hence
\int{\rm d}\vec{\theta}_{a}~\pi(\vec{\theta}_{a}|\vec{\lambda}_{a})=1
for arbitrary
\vec{\lambda}_{a}
. Therefore, the selection function
\alpha(\vec{\lambda}_{a}|\mathcal{H}_{a})\approx\mathcal{C}_{v}
is constant.

B.1
Linear-bias approximation

\vec{\theta}_{i}^{*}

, and
\Gamma_{i}^{\psi\varphi}
are submatrices of
\Gamma_{i}
corresponding to the
\vec{\psi},\vec{\psi}

\vec{\psi},\vec{\varphi}
elements, respectively.

\displaystyle\pi(\vec{v}|\vec{\lambda}_{v})_{\rm Taylor}:=~

\displaystyle\pi(\hat{v}_{i}|\vec{\lambda}_{v})+\Delta\vec{v}_{i}^{k}\partial_{k}\pi(\hat{v}_{i}|\vec{\lambda}_{v})

\displaystyle+\Delta\vec{v}_{i}^{k}\Delta\vec{v}_{i}^{m}\frac{\partial_{k}\partial_{m}\pi(\hat{v}_{i}|\vec{\lambda}_{v})}{2}

5

\hat{v}_{i}

26
) holds for typical sources in the results presented in Sec.
V

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\Gamma_{i}^{vv}\right)^{-1}\right)^{km}

for all
