A Super Version of a theorem of Fricke-Klein
Marcel Dang

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the character variety of the supergroup OSp ⁡ ( 1 | 2 ) \operatorname{OSp}(1|2) in the specific case of the free group on two letters as a first step towards an algebraic treatment for the character variety of supergroups. One can interpret this as an analogue of a result of Fricke and Klein, who studied this in the case of SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℂ ) \operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C}) . We give a count of independent generators of the ring of invariants.

Introduction

A result by Fricke and Klein

]
and apparently Vogt
[

]
states, that the

-invariant functions on

are given by the ring

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{tr}(A),\operatorname{tr}(B),\operatorname{tr}(AB)].

In the context of geometric invariant theory, we can interpret this result as
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Spec}(SL_{2})\times\operatorname{Spec}(SL_{2})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{tr}(A),\operatorname{tr}(B),\operatorname{tr}(AB)])\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}}

, i.e.

(2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}(F_{2},SL(2,\mathbb{C}))//SL(2,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}.

In this paper we are aiming to generalize the third interpretation of this result, to the super world. The motivation of this result comes from the fact, that
\operatorname{OSp}(1|2)
is the super generalization of
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
and takes a similar place in the theory of super Riemann surfaces, as
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
does in the classical theory. On the real analytic side of this story, we get something called super Teichmüller space, where each connected component of the space is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{6g-6|4g-4}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}

[
2
]

[
19
]
, where
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
only acts on the odd coordinates.

By ”explicit” computation, we can show that there is a deviation from the classical case and we do not get a variety of the expected dimension
3|2
. A problem of supergeometry is that most of the time the super version of the proof works analogously without adjusting anything. We want to close the gap of the unknown by writing down the super versions of the proof, even if nothing changes, so the reader does not have to mentally go through the classical construction and reason, that it works accordingly in the super case.

The

2.
Fricke-Klein

In this section, we give an elementary proof of the result of Fricke and Klein and apparently Vogt. In more recent times, Goldman reproved this theorem in
[
10
]
, where he used the Cayley-Hamilton theorem as in an essential way in the proof. In this version, we forego the usage of the Cayley-Hamilton theorem and instead make use of a simultaneous conjugation of a pair of matrices into upper and lower triangular forms.
Before we start proving the result of Fricke and Klein, let us first state the aforementioned triangulation lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
A_{0}\in\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
and
B_{0}\in\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
. Then one can always find an element
g\in\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
that acts by simultaneous conjugation on the pair
(A,B)
such that

(3)

\displaystyle gA_{0}g^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\mu&0\\
1&\mu^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\quad,\quad B_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&\kappa\\
0&\lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

Proof.

This splits into two cases.
A
diagonalizable or not. If
A
is diagonalizable, then it must mean that for its eigenvalues
\mu,\mu^{-1}
that
\mu\neq\mu^{-1}
. Consider

\displaystyle C=\begin{pmatrix}0&-x^{-1}\\
x&y\\
\end{pmatrix}

and compute its action by conjugation on
A and B

\displaystyle CAC^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\mu^{-1}&0\\
xy(\mu-\mu^{-1})&\mu\end{pmatrix}\quad,\quad CBC^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}-x^{-1}yc+d&x^{-2}c\\
xya+y^{2}c-x^{2}b-xyd&a+x^{-1}yc\end{pmatrix}

To set the bottom left corner of the first matrix to 1, we want
x=y^{-1}(\mu-\mu^{-1})^{-1}
.
By substituting the
x
, the bottom left corner is equal to

\displaystyle cy^{2}+\frac{a-d}{\mu-\mu^{-1}}-\frac{b}{\mu-\mu^{-1}}y^{-2},

which we want to set to zero. So we can solve a polynomial of degree 4, to get an upper triangular matrix for
B
and a lower triangular matrix
A
, by simultaneous conjugation.

Let
A
not diagonalizable, then it must admit Jordan normal form

(4)

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}\mu&1\\
0&\mu\end{pmatrix}.

A\in\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
, therefore
\mu=1
, which is a case we can treat by hands. Consider

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
0&z\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}z&-y\\
0&x\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&x^{2}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

On the other hand for the arbitrary matrix we get

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
0&z\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}z&-y\\
0&x\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}z(xa+yc)&-y(xa+yc)+x(xb+yd)\\
z^{2}c&-yzc+xzd\end{pmatrix}

Also notice, that

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
0&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
-b&d\end{pmatrix}

First, we set
x=i
, then we choose a solution for the equation
-y(xa+yc)+x(xb+yd)
, which we can do over
\mathbb{C}
. Act by rotation by 90 degree matrix. and we get the pair

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\quad,\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}z(xa+yc)&z^{2}c\\
0&-yzc+xzd\end{pmatrix},

which is in the desired form. So, indeed we can always parametrize into the simultaneous triangular form.
∎

We are now ready to give a simple proof of the theorem mentioned in the introduction.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
A,B\in\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
then all conjugation-invariant polynomial functions
f:SL(2,\mathbb{C})\times SL(2,\mathbb{C})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
are generated by
\operatorname{tr}(A),\operatorname{tr}(B),\operatorname{tr}(AB)
.

Proof.

Consider the coordinate ring of
SL(2,\mathbb{C})\times SL(2,\mathbb{C})
denoted by
\mathbb{C}[G\times G]
and its subring of
G
-invariant polynomial functions, denoted by

. We want to prove

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[H]\xlongrightarrow{\cong}\mathbb{C}[X,Y,Z],

where
X=\operatorname{tr}(A)
,
Y=\operatorname{tr}(B)
and
Z=\operatorname{tr}(AB)
.
First of all note, that if
f\in\mathbb{C}[H]
, then
f
is a polynomial function in the entries of the two matrices
(\xi,\eta)\in SL(2,\mathbb{C})\times SL(2,\mathbb{C})
. We will denote it

. Furthermore, by the triangulation lemma we can bring them into the form

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&\kappa\\
0&\lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\quad,\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}\mu&0\\
1&\mu^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

This parametrization means, that we can view
f\in\mathbb{C}[\lambda,\lambda^{-1},\mu,\mu^{-1},\kappa]
, which makes
\mathbb{C}[H]
into a subring by the obvious inclusion map

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[H]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[\lambda,\lambda^{-1},\mu,\mu^{-1},\kappa].

We can also define the map

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[\lambda,\lambda^{-1},\mu,\mu^{-1},\kappa]

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[X,Y,Z,\Delta_{X},\Delta_{Y}]

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{X+\Delta_{X}}{2}

\displaystyle\lambda^{-1}

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{X-\Delta_{X}}{2}

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{Y+\Delta_{Y}}{2}

\displaystyle\mu^{-1}

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{Y-\Delta_{Y}}{2}

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle\mapsto Z-\frac{X+\Delta_{X}}{2}\frac{Y+\Delta_{Y}}{2}-\frac{X-\Delta_{X}}{2}\frac{Y-\Delta_{Y}}{2},

where
\Delta_{X}=\sqrt{X^{2}-4}
and
\Delta_{Y}=\sqrt{Y^{2}-4}
. We can easily see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[\lambda+\lambda^{-1},\mu+\mu^{-1},\lambda\mu+\lambda^{-1}\mu^{-1}+\kappa]\xlongrightarrow{\cong}\mathbb{C}[X,Y,Z]

If we now consider a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{C}[X,Y,Z]\subset\mathbb{C}[\lambda,\lambda^{-1},\mu,\mu^{-1},\kappa]
, it is easy to see it is
G
-invariant, thus
f\in\mathbb{C}[H]
. Notice that

\displaystyle\lambda-\lambda^{-1}

\displaystyle\mapsto\Delta_{X}

\displaystyle\mu-\mu^{-1}

\displaystyle\mapsto\Delta_{Y}.

So it turns out, that the statement is even stronger, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[\lambda,\lambda^{-1},\mu,\mu^{-1},\kappa]

\displaystyle\xlongrightarrow{\cong}\mathbb{C}[X,Y,Z,\Delta_{X},\Delta_{Y}].

Now we define a map
\psi
, by the composition of the solid arrows in the following diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[H]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[\lambda,\lambda^{-1},\mu,\mu^{-1},\kappa]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[X,Y,Z]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[X,Y,Z,\Delta_{X},\Delta_{Y}]}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eqqcolon\psi}

,

which is surjective as argued earlier. Now to prove it is injective, we again make use of our identifications
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[H]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[\lambda,\lambda^{-1},\mu,\mu^{-1},\kappa]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[X,Y,Z]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[\lambda,\lambda^{-1},\mu,\mu^{-1},\kappa]}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{id}}

.

Now consider the image of a
G
-invariant polynomial
g
. We have
\psi(g(\xi,\eta))=f(X,Y,Z)=0
. This is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{ijk}\alpha_{ijk}X^{i}Y^{j}Z^{k}=\sum_{ijk}\alpha_{ijk}(\lambda+\lambda^{-1})^{i}(\mu+\mu^{-1})^{j}(\lambda\mu+\lambda^{-1}\mu^{-1}+\kappa)^{k}=0,

which we expand in the form

\displaystyle\sum_{ijk}\alpha_{ijk}\sum_{l}^{i}\sum_{m}^{j}\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}=k_{3}=n}^{k}\binom{i}{l}\binom{j}{m}\binom{k}{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}\lambda^{i-2l+k_{1}-k_{2}}\mu^{j-2m+k_{1}-k_{2}}\kappa^{k_{3}}

On the other hand we have the

\displaystyle\sum_{ijkpq}\beta_{ijkpq}\lambda^{i-j}\mu^{k-p}\kappa^{q}=0,

which after relabeling turns into

\displaystyle\sum_{rst}\beta_{rst}\lambda^{r}\mu^{s}\kappa^{t}=0.

We can clearly see, every
\beta_{rst}
is just a sum of the
\alpha_{ijk}

By our condition we know all coefficients
\alpha_{ijk}
must vanish, since there is no relation between

. Therefore
\beta_{rst}=0
. Thus
g=0
and injectivity follows. In total we now have

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[G\times G]^{G}=\mathbb{C}[H]\cong\mathbb{C}[X,Y,Z]

and on the level of spaces we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}(F_{2},SL(2,\mathbb{C}))//SL(2,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}

∎

Remark 3.3
.

We can interpret the odd coordinates geometrically as fat points. To elaborate a little bit on that:

Odd coordinates vanish if you square them, so if we think of a scheme over
\mathbb{C}
of odd dimension

By this analogy, we can think of odd coordinates geometrically as smearing out the point or a first order taylor expansion around the origin.

Example 3.5

\mathbb{P}^{n|m}

Definition 3.6
.

The reduced space of a superscheme
X
is defined as

(6)

\displaystyle X\coloneqq(|X|,\mathcal{O}_{X}/J_{X})=(|X|,\mathcal{O}_{red})

where

is the sheaf of ideals generated by the nilpotent sections of

It is important to note, that
\mathcal{O}_{X}/J\coloneqq a(U\mapsto\mathcal{O}_{X}(U)/J(U))
is the sheafification. However, we still get an isomorphism on stalks between the presheaf and its sheafification and hence we can use results known from commutative algebra with impunity.
This notion is useful, because when we try to generalize our concepts to try to describe more abstract physics, we want to ensure that it does reduce to our known description of phenomena up to that point. However, there is generally an obstruction to being able to do this nicely. Ideally, we want a global section to exist, which is the projection onto the non-super space. So, we will introduce the notion of projected and split superscheme.

Definition 3.7
.

A superscheme X is called projected if the structural sequence
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces J_{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{O}_{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{O}_{X}/J_{X}}

As one might guess, this implies the existence of non-projected superscheme, but contrary to what our intuition might lead us to conclude a lot of supermanifolds are non-projected in even quite simple examples.

Remark 3.8
.

In the category of supermanifolds, it turns out that every real supermanifold is split, due to Batchelors theorem (in a non-natural way). So if we want to look for non-projected objects, we must look at complex supermanifolds.

Definition 3.9
.

Let X be a superscheme of odd dimension m, then there exists a

\displaystyle J_{X}^{0}\supset J_{X}^{1}\supset...\supset J_{X}^{m+1}=0.

\displaystyle Gr(X)\coloneqq(|X|,Gr(\mathcal{O}_{X})).

Lemma 3.11

If a supermanifold X of odd dimension

We follow
[
22

. Let

Proposition 4.1

Let

is simply a tuple of
n

G

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(F_{n},G)=k[G]^{\otimes n}

∎

If we now impose relations on the free group, we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)=\operatorname{Spec}(k[G\times...\times G]/I_{\Gamma}).

The ideal
I_{\Gamma}
is generated by the relations induced by
\Gamma
, which tell you that the images of the generators still have to obey the relations of
\Gamma
, because otherwise it would not be a group homomorphism.
By the quotient nature of
\mathcal{R}(\Gamma,G)
it is not obvious if it is an integral domain, which over
\mathbb{C}
allows you to identify Specm and Spec.
This space algebraically classifies all so-called
G

Definition 4.2

Let
G

\Gamma

(53)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Spec}(\mathcal{R}(\Gamma,G)^{G})

Remark 4.3
.

If we now only consider reductive algebraic groups then it turns out that
\operatorname{Specm}(\mathcal{R}(\Gamma,G)^{G})=\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
is a good categorical quotient and by Nagata’s theorem it is finitely generated. One should remark, that non-reduced points may still exists.
Let
\operatorname{Hom}^{i}(\Gamma,G)
be the subset of irreducible representations. Then we also have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}^{i}(\Gamma,G)//G

is a good geometric quotient, i.e. the set corresponds to the set theoretic quotient, where if we now consider fibers of the quotient map, then we will recover the orbits of
G
.
In general, there should be no expectation, that the full character variety is actually a good geometric quotient, i.e. it is not the orbit space of the representation variety.

Recalling the theorem of Fricke and Klein, we can now reinterpret it as a special case of the character variety of the free group and in particular an explicit solution to the question of finding minimal generators of a character variety.

We can easily see, that there are no obstructions to replacing the algebraic group with an algebraic supergroup. The character variety of

Remark 4.4

Seeing how this construction actually only cares about the Hopf algebras, we can also generalize this question to quantum groups. This will definitely work if we only consider group relations, that can be manipulated in a way such that no inverses appear, because if we consider the quantum group via algebra-valued matrices, then the inverse does not necessarily live in the same algebra. A heuristic construction was already given in

5.
Main Result

Let us return from the geometric world and consider purely algebraic world again, where we now state our main result.
Let

-dimensional super vector space.

Theorem 5.1
.

The
\operatorname{OSp}(V)
-invariant polynomial functions (by conjugation) on
\operatorname{OSp}(V)\times\operatorname{OSp}(V)

Proof.

Let us consider the algebra

(54)

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{End}(V)^{2}]^{\operatorname{OSp}(V)}.

By polarization, we can carry the polynomial invariants to the multilinear invariants. Now we remember that

(55)

\displaystyle((\operatorname{End}(V)\otimes\operatorname{End})^{\vee})^{\operatorname{OSp}(V)}\cong((V\otimes V\otimes V\otimes V)^{\vee})^{\operatorname{OSp}(V)}.

By the first fundamental theorem of the
\operatorname{OSp}(V)
we know that the number of polynomial invariants of

is 10. By the second fundamental theorem of
\operatorname{OSp}(V)
we know that there is one relation for
\operatorname{dim}(V)=1|2
and thus 9 independent generators.
Because the multilinear invariants of matrices and vectors are the same, the polynomial invariants are also the same. By reductivity we have
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces I^{\operatorname{OSp}(V)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{End}(V)^{2}]^{\operatorname{OSp}(V)}}

where

is the defining ideal of
\operatorname{OSp}(V)^{2}
as subvariety of
\operatorname{End}(V)^{2}

\alpha=b\gamma-a\delta,\quad\beta=d\gamma-c\delta,

together with
\operatorname{Ber}(A)=1

\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{End}(V)^{2}]

\displaystyle I^{\operatorname{OSp}(1|2)}=\langle\operatorname{Ber}(A),\operatorname{Ber}(B)\rangle.

9&=x+2\\

This result can be reinterpreted as the fact that the character variety of the free group on 2 letters of
\operatorname{OSp}(V)
has 7 independent generators. Which is a deviation of the result for

There have been results, where in the real analytic case one can prove, that this space is actually of the expected real dimension
6|4
. One can easily see, why this fails in the algebraic case. We have seen that all invariants on the space of matrices, are given by a supertrace. We recall that

(58)

\displaystyle\operatorname{str}:\operatorname{OSp}(V)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{1|0}

and thus we cannot determine any odd generators with the help of traces. We can also interpret as an incarnation of the fact, that there is a natural

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\xi\\
0&1&0\\

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-\xi\\

So this tells us, that if we take the quotient by the full group

One also sees by this calculation, that taking the full ring of invariants can only give you a non-reduced ordinary scheme, since the odd degrees of freedom are not invariant, as we always have to pair two odd coordinates, that will be flipped simultaneously to become an invariant.

This means what is
\operatorname{OSp}(1|2)

\operatorname{{POSp}}(1|2)\coloneqq\operatorname{OSp}p(1|2)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}

Making this change, one also needs to consider the character variety of
