Brevity is the soul of sustainability: Characterizing LLM response lengths
Soham Poddar*, Paramita Koley*, Janardan Misra, Sanjay Podder, Navveen Balani, Niloy Ganguly, Saptarshi Ghosh

Abstract
A significant portion of the energy consumed by Large Language Models (LLMs) arises from their inference processes; hence developing energy-efficient methods for inference is crucial. While several techniques exist for inference optimization, output compression remains relatively unexplored, with only a few preliminary efforts addressing this aspect. In this work, we first benchmark 12 decoder-only LLMs across 5 datasets, revealing that these models often produce responses that are substantially longer than necessary. We then conduct a comprehensive quality assessment of LLM responses, formally defining six information categories present in LLM responses. We show that LLMs often tend to include redundant or additional information besides the minimal answer. To address this issue of long responses by LLMs, we explore several simple and intuitive prompt-engineering strategies. Empirical evaluation shows that appropriate prompts targeting length reduction and controlling information content can achieve significant energy optimization between 25-60% by reducing the response length while preserving the quality of LLM responses.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have seen rapid adoption in different applications, including virtual assistants for daily life, and in scientific research. However, the deployment of these models require massive computational resources, leading to significant energy consumption and carbon emissions.
Although a single inference operation may not consume much energy, such operations are performed enormously many times under deployment.
For example, OpenAI’s ChatGPT processes more than 1B queries per day, which consumes an estimated 0.5GWh of energy. In recent times, ninety percent of the total cloud computing demand for Amazon Web Services was for model inference purposes
Barr 2019
.
Hence, LLM inferences contribute to a significant energy impact
Wu et al. 2022
;
Patterson et al. 2022
and merit further investigation.

Our prior work established that the energy consumption during inference is closely correlated with the length of the response generated by LLMs
Poddar et al. 2025
. Notably, output length has a greater impact on inference energy usage than input length because inputs may benefit from parallel processing and caching on GPUs, whereas output generation is predominantly sequential. Additionally, we demonstrated in
Poddar et al. 2025
that the energy expended during inference is largely independent of task complexity or type, depending largely on the output size. Together, these findings highlight the potential for optimizing inference energy consumption by limiting the output length.

A key consideration in this context is how popular LLMs behave - specifically,
whether LLMs tend to generate more text than necessary for certain tasks
.
To investigate this, in this work, we benchmark 12 LLMs across 5 tasks/datasets spanning different use cases.
We focus on factual question-answering datasets primarily consisting of factual and procedural questions, deliberately excluding opinion-based questions, as determining their minimal response length can be challenging. Our primary finding is that,
for factual questions, LLM-generated responses are significantly longer than the target responses
, regardless of whether the targets are human-written or machine-generated.

It can be argued that longer answers are more useful to the users, since the additional information beyond the core information need – such as explanations, summaries, or conversational enhancements – can enhance user experience.
But it is important to recognize that such enhancements come at the cost of increased energy consumption and environmental costs. A useful analogy is air travel via economy vs. business class, where the core need (timely travel) is distinct from optional enhancements that improve the experience but come at an additional cost.
This is the first work to bring forward the trade-off between energy efficiency and user satisfaction, a pivotal contribution that unveils an entirely new perspective on LLM responses.

Next, we categorize the LLM-generated responses by identifying six distinct information categories through a comprehensive manual survey. This categorization reveals that LLM responses often include, along with the minimal answers, explanations, examples, and reasoning information, as well as redundant or irrelevant text.
In fact, our study reveals that, on average, the core answer constitutes only about 42% of the total responses.
While the extra elements can enhance readability and potentially increase user satisfaction (for instance, users might appreciate supplementary context or a polite add-on), they also incur higher inference energy costs. Consequently, we argue that LLMs should be aligned to generate minimal answers by default, incorporating only a limited amount of additional content and excluding extraneous or conversational tokens.

Finally, we explore several prompt-based strategies to generate more concise responses from LLMs. These include simple directives (such as “Answer briefly”), providing in-context examples to guide the LLM toward optimal response length, specifying a desired output length, and reducing non-essential content such as conversational and irrelevant information.
We evaluate these prompt engineering strategies in terms of how they affect the length, energy, and information content of the LLM responses. We also check how they affect the quality of LLM responses in terms of ROUGE-L F1 scores with the target (gold standard) answers.
Empirical evaluation shows that some of our prompts achieve significant energy optimization by reducing the response lengths, while preserving (or sometimes even improving) the quality of LLM responses.
For instance, one of our best strategies is simply where we direct the LLM to provide only the minimal answer; this strategy achieves up to 88% reduction in LLM responses, which leads up to 58% reduction in energy consumption, while actually improving the response quality.

Related Works

The optimization of energy consumption in large language models (LLMs) has been extensively studied in the literature. Notable white-box approaches include pruning
Kurtić et al. 2024
, quantization
Xiao et al. 2023
;
Frantar et al. 2022
, early exit strategies
Schuster et al. 2022
, and embedding recycling
Saad-Falcon et al. 2022
. Hardware or OS-level optimizations focus on optimal data transfer and device utilization,
for example, caching
Ramírez et al. 2023
, scheduling
Yu et al. 2022
, etc. Black-box approaches include cascading
Chen et al. 2023
, speculative decoding
Leviathan et al. 2023
, input compression
Jiang et al. 2023a
;
Jiang et al. 2023b
, among others. A detailed literature survey has been given in Appendix
A
.

However, the area of output compression remains relatively underexplored, with only a few preliminary efforts in the literature, namely prompt augmentation with generation directives
Li et al. 2024
. In this work, our primary focus is to comprehensively explore the underexplored avenue of output compression for energy-efficient inference.

3.3
Categorizing information in responses

To gain further insight on overly long responses of LLMs, we categorize the information present in the responses.

Low et al. 1973
suggests a taxonomy of irrelevant, relevant, and redundant information for general concept transfer. Following this line of work and our inspection of 300 responses by different LLMs, we develop the following classification scheme for the tokens contained in LLM responses.

1. Minimal answer (
MinAns
):
These tokens provide a concise and to-the-point answer to the query/prompt. Note that the minimal answer can be correct or wrong; we are
not
differentiating between correct and wrong answers in this analysis.

2. Additional helpful information (
AddInfo
):

These include information that adds valuable context and aims to expand the knowledge of the user. Example: User: “What is capital of France?” LLM: “Paris.
It is a center of art and culture
”.

3. Reasoning/Explanations (
Explain
):

Here the LLM states its thought process, logical steps that it followed to answer the query.
Example: User: “What is 2 + 2?” LLM: “
First, you take 2 and add another 2, which equals 4.
Final answer: 4”.

4. Conversational Enhancements (
Convers
):

Tokens that are included to maintain politeness, enhance the conversational tone, or ask users if they require further information on some aspects.
Examples include “
Let me know if you need anything else
”, “
I hope that helps!
”.

5. Redundant Information (
RedInfo
):
In some longer responses, LLMs provide a summary of the previous content at the end. In some other cases, the responses contain rephrased versions of information already given (in the same response).
Example: “Netherlands is the official name of the country as a whole, including Holland and other regions like Friesland, Gelderland.
So, while Holland is part of the Netherlands, Netherlands is the entire country.
”

6. Irrelevant Information (
Irrel
):
At times, LLMs generate tokens unrelated to the user’s query, e.g., when the model hallucinates, or produces repeating patterns of tokens. Example: User: "Who discovered America?" LLM: "Columbus!
By the way, I recently read about American recipes.
"

4
Some common strategies for shortening LLM responses

We now focus on some common strategies for shortening LLM responses to achieve energy-efficient inference. We explore simple, intuitive white-box approaches, including response truncation and fine-tuning of LLMs, as well as black-box strategies based on prompt engineering.

Truncating responses
refers to stopping the generation early, e,g., just after the minimal answer is obtained.
However, we notice that
in about 19% of the responses studied in Section
3.4
, the minimal answer is
not
at the beginning of the LLM response.
In most of these cases, the LLMs output conversational enhancers first to set up the context or for readability, followed by the minimal answer, thus limiting the scope of strategies for truncating responses.

Fine-tuning LLMs:

Next we explore whether simple fine-tuning can help in controlling the response length. Toward that, we performed some basic fine-tuning experiments. We fine-tuned Phi-3-mini (one of the LLMs that generate very long responses, as stated earlier) with a pool of human-written question-answers from the
Dolly
and
GooAQ
datasets, using LoRA with
r=16
, for
100
epochs. The models were trained on the target answer tokens with the hypothesis that it would enable the models to generate responses similar to humans on these particular datasets.
We compare the average ratio of generated and target response lengths for the
Default
model and the fine-tuned model. We observe that
simple fine-tuning is leading to 1.24 times higher ratio for
Dolly
and 1.26 times higher ratio for
GooAQ
, in comparison with the
Default
model
.
This is an unexpected result, where fine-tuning is (slightly) increasing the response length instead of shortening the length.
This behavior could stem from extensive pretraining on large-scale data, making small-scale fine-tuning insufficient to alter its learned patterns.
Additionally, a model’s intrinsic stopping mechanism may not be functioning as intended after the fine-tuning, causing it to overestimate the length for the responses.

Therefore, it is prudent to explore prompt engineering strategies leveraging LLM’s zero-shot capabilities, drawing on our observations in Section
3
. We explore this direction in the next section.

5
Prompt engineering strategies for controlling LLM responses

Here we investigate various prompt engineering strategies
aimed at generating concise LLM responses, thereby reducing inference energy.

5.1
Prompt engineering strategies

We first describe the different strategies we used to control response length. The exact prompts used are given in Table
4
.

BRIEF
:
Following
Li et al. 2024
, the default query is modified by appending “
Answer briefly
” at the end of the query.

BM25-InContext
:
In this method, we provide a set of in-context examples to help LLM in deciding the ideal response length of the given query. Specifically, we provide a sequence of 10 sample queries and their respective gold-answers as in-context examples, followed by the given query. These samples were chosen from the
25K
train set based on the similarity with the given query, as predicted by the BM25+ retrieval model
Trotman et al. 2014
.

Limit-Len
:

In this approach, we specify an estimated length of the response and instruct the LLM to restrict the generation length within that limit. Specifically, we add “
Answer within
\mathcal{X}
words
” at the end of the prompt,
where
\mathcal{X}
is the limit of generation that we obtain through three ways:

(1)
BM25-length
:
Top-k similar samples are selected from the pool of samples using BM25+ model, and
\mathcal{X}
is set to the median of their target lengths.

(2)
GoldResLen
:
To serve as an oracle baseline,
\mathcal{X}
is set to the actual gold-answer length corresponding to the query.

(3)
PredResLen
:
Here we employ a supervised framework for learning the ideal length of a given query. Specifically, we train an encoder-only transformer-based regression model
Deberta-v3-large

He et al. 2021
with scaler output to predict
\mathcal{X}
of the given query. The model is trained with queries and the length of their target (gold standard) responses from the training set.

Limit-Cat
:

Based on our observations regarding the distribution of various categories in LLM responses (see Figure
2a
), we hypothesize that responses can be made shorter and energy-efficient while preserving response quality, by reducing the presence of
Convers
,
RedInfo
, and
Irrel
in the responses.
Accordingly, we design
Limit-Cat
, a set of prompt directives to control the presence of various categories in the response.
We tried several combinations of prompts to restrict the category distribution in the responses. Out of these we report results for the following two strategies which performed the best:

(1)
MinAns
:
Model is asked to provide the minimal answer only.

(2)
MAddNoRed
:
Model is asked to provide the minimal answer, along with some additional information if applicable. It is also explicitly asked
not
to produce redundant text or conversational enhancements

5.2
Results

We empirically validate the efficacy of the prompt engineering strategies against the default prompt, by investigating these questions:
(1) Can the prompt engineering strategies achieve a reduction in LLM response length?
(2) Does the length reduction degrade the response quality?
(3) Are these approaches able to achieve notable reduction in terms of energy?
(4) How do these methods influence the category distribution in the LLM responses?

Effects on response length:

Fig.

to the

MinAns
achieves the highest length reduction across all models with about 60% decrease, followed by

The supervised length estimator
PredResLen
performs at par with the oracle (
GoldResLen
) with superior length reduction for some models, namely mistral (66%), llama-2 (77%), and gemma-2-9b (69%), showing its efficacy in accurate estimation of ideal target length.

BM25-length
and
BRIEF

MAddNoRed

However, some LLMs, like phi-3-small or llama-3-8B fail to respond to such prompts appropriately.

Effect on response quality:

While response quality is abstract and difficult to quantify, we wanted to check if the user’s

basic information need
is being fulfilled in the LLM responses.
To this end, we compute the Rouge-L F1 scores of the LLM responses with respect to the target answers.
While this is no substitute for a human evaluation, this will give us some insights into the quality of responses using different strategies.

We observe that employing the above-mentioned prompting strategies generally improves the F1-scores of the responses upon the default setup in most cases, since the models are instructed to generate more information-dense answers, that better match the target answers.
Specifically, we find that

6
Concluding Discussion

In this work, we comprehensively explore output compression for energy-efficient inference. Our specific contributions are as follows.
(1) We are the first to systematically establish that large language models (LLMs) often generate very long responses which lead to high energy consumptions; We also categorize LLMs based on their generation lengths.
(2) We present the first comprehensive information categorization of the LLM responses, identifying
6
information classes;
(3) Based on our findings, we develop various length-based and category-based prompts and evaluate them extensively in terms of how they affect the length, energy and quality of LLM responses. We observe that appropriate prompts can achieve significant energy reduction by reducing response length, alongside improving response quality.
Overall, we think the present work takes the first important step toward exploring the utility of output compression in reducing the energy consumption of LLMs.

Our work opens up several future directions for further work (more details in Appendix
H
).

(1) What constitutes an ideal response length
under different scenarios/applications should be studied more, and various factors influencing that decision should be identified.
Measuring user satisfaction would likely require different approachs, including human surveys and automated scoring metrics and need a study on its own.

(2) User personalisation:

Another possible direction is to consider user-preferences while optimizing response length, since different users can prefer responses of different lengths for the same question/task. Such personalization is out of scope for the current work.
However, it presents a promising line of future research.

(3) Automatic information categorization:
We attempted to automate the categorization of LLM responses. To this end, we employed OpenAI GPT-4o with a detailed prompt describing all the information categories. We achieved an average F1 score of 0.391 over all tokens. Hence, efficient methods for distinguishing categories in LLM responses should be investigated.
(4) Pre-training:
Model families seem to generate responses with similar category distributions, indicating the pre-training of the LLMs should be revisited to have better control over what information categories should be included in the response.

(5) Model alignment:
Efforts should be made to align LLMs towards generating responses of desirable lengths.
(6) Efficient decoding algorithms
may be employed to control category information within the generated responses in the real-time, to achieve a better compromise between readability and energy-optimality.

Limitations

While this study offers a comprehensive analysis and valuable insights, some limitations should be noted:
(1) Limited applicability to long-answer tasks: The potential for energy optimization through output compression is limited in tasks requiring detailed and lengthy responses, such as creative writing or critical decision-making tasks with explanations. Our approaches are primarily effective for objective, factual queries where concise and brief responses are both sufficient and desirable.
(2) Subjectivity of Ideal Response Length: The notion of an ideal response length is subjective and varies significantly among individuals. As a result, a one-size-fits-all approach may not effectively meet the diverse needs of users. Here we argue that the default behaviour of LLMs can be tuned so that they generate shorter responses, so that energy consumption is minimized. A user can ask for more details or longer responses if she desires.
(3) Use of standard datasets: Our experiments have been performed on publicly available rich data for testing LLMs. However, the LLMs we used might have been pre-trained on parts of these standard datasets, causing them to behave in a certain way. This may lead to slight variations in results for real-world use cases, but the overall findings should be generalizable enough.

Appendix A
Related Works

The optimization of energy consumption in large language models (LLMs) has been extensively studied in the literature.

White-box approaches:
These techniques focus on architectural modifications to LLMs and represent a well-established line of research. Notable methods include pruning
Kurtić et al. 2024
, quantization
Xiao et al. 2023
;
Frantar et al. 2022
, early exit strategies
Schuster et al. 2022
, and embedding recycling
Saad-Falcon et al. 2022
, among others.

Hardware- or OS-level optimizations
focus on improving energy efficiency by leveraging hardware innovations or operating system enhancements.
Yu et al.
Yu et al. 2022
propose an optimal scheduling strategy by making it iteration-level from user-request-level. Flash-attention
Dao et al. 2022
effectively reduces latency by optimizing the number of memory read and write operations required in calculating model states.
Ramirez et al.
Ramírez et al. 2023
employs a student model along with the larger LLM, which gradually gains proficiency in independently handling increasing user requests, mimicking the popular caching technique in OS.

Black-box approaches
avoid altering the underlying model, unlike the white-box models. Instead, they employ multi-level frameworks that incorporate smaller LLMs for specific tasks. Chen et al.
Chen et al. 2023
highlight the efficacy of cascading and multiplexing in reducing inference energy and cost. Leviathan et al.
Leviathan et al. 2023
introduce speculative decoding that accelerates the decoding by running Larger LLMs on the output of approximately smaller models.

Input compression
techniques apply for large-input tasks like legal question answering, multi-document summarization, multi-document retrieval, etc, where the input is excessively high. Jiang et al.
Jiang et al. 2023a
propose LLMLingua, a coarse-to-fine prompt compression method that involves a budget controller to maintain semantic integrity under high compression ratios. LongLLMLingua
Jiang et al. 2023b
further refine the method, resulting in achieving higher compression.

As already shown in literature
Luccioni et al. 2024
;
Poddar et al. 2025
, inference energy is also closely tied to output length, presenting a scope for reducing energy consumption by
Output compression
. However, this area remains relatively underexplored, with only a few preliminary efforts in the literature. For instance, prompt augmentation with generation directives has been investigated as an initial step
Li et al. 2024
. In this work, we aim to comprehensively explore the underexplored avenue of output compression, advancing the state of the art in energy-efficient inference.

Appendix H
Detailed discussion about the scope and potential future work

In this work, we explore the question whether LLMs are generating too much text, which add up the energy consumption and environmental impact of the models.
To this end, we benchmark the responses of various open-source LLMs for factual objective queries. We find that
LLMs are generating long responses
, often orders of magnitude higher than human-written answers.
Revisiting decoding and training strategies with the insights from our experiments can be a promising direction for future research toward devising solutions that produce concise and accurate answers.

Response categorization can play a pivotal role in devising techniques for forcing the models to generate brief (as desired) and accurate answers.
Specifically, we established that the minimal answers contain enough information to achieve accurate responses, while redundant information could be removed completely in most cases.

What constitutes an ideal response
under different scenarios/applications should be studied more, and various factors influencing that decision should be identified.
We argue that the composition of the responses should be based on users preferences too, whether they want concise or explanatory or creative answers.
However, we argue that, from an energy optimality perspective, the LLM should, by default, generate a brief and succinct response for factual objective user queries.

We employed OpenAI GPT-4o for categorizing the responses from the set of
500
annotated examples, which is found incapable to differentiate between certain pair of categories like irrelevant and redundant, reasoning and redundant, highlighting the need for more sophisticated, focused approaches. Overall it only achieved an average F1 score of 0.391 over all tokens.

Effective Methods for distinguishing categories
, between necessary (minimal answer and strongly related additional information), redundant (polite, summary, repetition, reasoning), and irrelevant content should be investigated.
Towards that, we make our annotated LLM responses publicly available. Researchers can employ this dataset to develop such supervised framework (e.g. using Longformer
Beltagy et al. 2020
based models), that can be used to automatically classify LLM responses into different categories, which will remove the human annotation dependency and enable large-scale evaluation of LLM responses.

We found that Minimal answers are sometimes preceded by other categories (polite and conversational enhancements, additional information), limiting the scope for simple early stopping strategies such as stopping after ’k’ tokens and discarding incomplete sentences, for getting the ideal minimal answer.
Simple fine-tuning on these open-domain QA datasets proved ineffective, though it may prove beneficial for certain controlled environments. Fine-tuning with a length penalty may allow models to capture desired lengths better.
Model families seem to generate responses with similar category distributions, indicating the
pre-training of the LLMs should be revisited
to have better control over the information categories in the response.

We also found irrelevant information are present in nearly 20% of responses across all models. This happens when the model is unsure of what to predict next.
Generation can be shortened greatly if the models are stopped from generating irrelevant information, towards which
hallucination detection and prevention

Manakul et al. 2023
;
Ji et al. 2023
are promising starting steps.

Model alignment
have been used to mitigate harmful content/biases in reponses through re-training
Wang et al. 2024
;
Dai et al. 2024
. Efforts should also be made to align LLMs towards generating responses of lengths that are desirable for a given situation.

Prompt engineering strategies show promise in reducing the response length and energy consumption, while preserving both precision and recall of the response for most of the models. Specifically, length-based prompts with sophisticated length estimator (
PredResLen
), along with certain category-specific prompts (
MinAns
and
MAddNoRed
) are quite effective in achieving energy-aware inferences across different use cases. However, they are not enough to effectively control the desired proportions of the information categories.

Efficient decoding time algorithms
may be employed to control category information within the generated response in the real-time, enabling us to achieve a better compromise between readability and energy-optimality.
MLP or RNN-based methods may be trained on our annotated response category data and incorporated into the final language modelling
That will also allow us a finer control for modulating the category distribution in the generated response, enhancing its scope and applicability.
