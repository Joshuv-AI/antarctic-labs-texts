Anterior cervical decompression improves medial plantar pressure in OPLL with gait disturbance: a 24-month retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zichuan Wu, Xuhong Zhang, HanLin Song, Min Qi, Yong Hu, Yang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOssification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) is a major cause of degenerative cervical myelopathy. Whether surgical approach differentially affects lower-limb sensorimotor function is unclear. We linked radiological markers of cord injury with gait biomechanics to compare anterior versus posterior decompression.MethodsRetrospective cohort (single tertiary center, 12/2018–12/2022). Adults with cervical OPLL, gait disturbance, and focal intramedullary T2 hyperintensity (signal change ratio [SCR] ≥ 1.20) were included. Patients underwent anterior (ACDF/ACCF/hybrid) or posterior (laminoplasty/laminectomy-fusion) decompression. The primary endpoint was the 24-month change in medial foot pressure (MFP), the percentage of total plantar load borne by a pre-defined medial mask (first–third metatarsal heads plus the medial heel). Secondary endpoints included MFP at 6 months; SCR regression at 6 and 24 months (CR1, CR2); and the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score. Background

Ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) is ectopic ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament that narrows the cervical canal and compresses neural elements [1, 2]. It most commonly involves the cervical spine and is a major cause of degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM), with higher prevalence in East Asian populations [2, 3].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the reference modality for characterizing cord compression and intramedullary signal change in DCM/OPLL [4, 5]. Hyperintensity of the cervical cord on T2-weighted imaging (intramedullary T2-weighted hyperintensity, ISI) reflects nonspecific but clinically relevant pathology—including edema, inflammation/gliosis, ischemia, and myelomalacia—and has consistent prognostic associations: multisegmental or sharply demarcated ISI predicts worse neurological recovery after decompression, whereas accompanying T1 hypointensity indicates more advanced, chronic injury [4, 6–8].

Gait disturbance is among the most disabling consequences of cervical cord compression [9]. Mechanistically, impaired corticospinal and dorsal column conduction disrupts lower-limb motor control and proprioceptive feedback, altering dynamic balance and step initiation [9–11]. Recent objective analyses show that patients with DCM demonstrate longer anticipatory postural adjustment (APA) phases with shorter step length during gait initiation—features that increase fall risk—and exhibit quantifiable abnormalities in foot pressure patterns [9–11]. In cross-sectional plantar-pressure studies, DCM cohorts display elevated medial and reduced lateral plantar loads compared with controls, consistent with impaired proprioceptive control and altered frontal-plane mechanics [12–15].

Surgical decompression is the mainstay of treatment for OPLL, delivered by either anterior or posterior approaches [2, 16, 17]. Comparative evidence suggests each strategy achieves neurological improvement but with distinct profiles: anterior decompression and fusion can provide greater recovery in selected patients (e.g., high canal-occupying ratio, unfavorable K-line), at the cost of higher approach-related risks; posterior procedures (laminoplasty or laminectomy with fusion) offer broader decompression with generally lower critical complication rates [2, 18–21]. Despite extensive research on patient-reported and neurological scales, the differential impact of anterior versus posterior decompression on lower-limb biomechanics—particularly plantar pressure distribution—remains poorly defined, with only emerging data in broader DCM populations [9–11, 22].

Plantar pressure during natural walking captures lower-limb sensorimotor control. A medial load bias reflects impaired dorsal-column proprioception and frontal-plane control in cervical myelopathy. Medial foot pressure (MFP) is defined as the percentage of total plantar load borne by a preset medial mask (sensels under the first–third metatarsal heads and the medial heel). A higher value indicates more medially biased loading during stance. It complements clinical scales (e.g., JOA) and links MRI findings to functional gait restoration. We hypothesized that, among patients with OPLL and focal intramedullary T2 hyperintensity, anterior (direct ventral) decompression would yield a greater 24-month reduction in medial foot pressure than posterior indirect decompression.

Focusing on the pathophysiologically relevant subgroup of OPLL patients with focal cervical cord ISI on T2-weighted MRI, we aimed to compare how anterior versus posterior cervical decompression affects plantar pressure distribution. By linking radiological cord injury markers (ISI) with objective gait biomechanics, this study seeks to provide functional, mechanistically grounded evidence to inform surgical approach selection in OPLL. In differential diagnosis, ossified or accessory bone structures—congenital or acquired—can mimic or coexist with OPLL and carry morphofunctional implications; imaging-based distinction from benign ossified variants is essential for prognosis and planning [23].

Methods

Study design and oversight

We conducted a retrospective clinical imaging and biomechanics study of consecutive patients with cervical ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) who underwent surgical decompression at the Department of Cervical Spine Surgery, Changzheng Hospital (Shanghai), between 1 December 2018 and 1 December 2022. Pre-operative and post-operative cervical MRI were obtained according to institutional protocols, and patients were followed clinically and biomechanically for approximately 24 months. The study adhered to the STROBE guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Institutional review board approval was obtained (2021SL044). Given the retrospective design, the requirement for written consent for chart review was waived; separate consent was obtained when required for gait/pressure testing.

Cohort assembly and groups

Of 265 screened patients, 63 met eligibility. Patients were categorized by index surgical approach into an anterior cohort (n = 34) and a posterior cohort (n = 29).

Device and acquisition

Plantar loading during natural walking was recorded with pressure-sensing insoles (Beijing Jiakang Zhongzhi Technology Co.; 100 Hz). For each timepoint (preoperative, 6 months, 24 months), patients walked at a self-selected comfortable speed; three valid trials were acquired and averaged. Higher MFP indicates a more medially biased load (Fig. 1).

Medial foot pressure (primary biomechanical outcome)

Region masks were generated by the manufacturer’s segmentation and visually verified for each footprint by two trained assessors blinded to surgical approach.

Trial structure and averaging

At each time point, three valid passes were averaged to a patient-level MFP. Left and right feet were analyzed separately; the bilateral mean MFP was prespecified as the primary measure, with side-specific values examined in sensitivity analyses.

Data sources and variables

Demographics and clinical scales

From the electronic medical record we abstracted sex, age, body mass index (BMI), symptom duration, comorbidities, and smoking status. Neurological status was quantified using the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score for cervical myelopathy (17-point scale) at baseline and follow-up visits. The prespecified clinical endpoint was the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score; pain VAS and manual muscle testing were not analyzed in this study. MRI acquisition and cord signal quantification

Cervical MRI was performed on 1.5-T or 3.0-T systems with sagittal and axial T1- and T2-weighted sequences. Intramedullary T2 hyperintensity (HCS/ISI) was identified visually and quantified using the signal change ratio (SCR):

Patients with SCR ≥ 1.20 were eligible.

Radiographic alignment and canal metrics

Canal narrowing rate (CNR; unitless) was measured on axial images at maximal encroachment as D₂/D₁ (residual canal ÷ osseous canal) (Fig. 3). The C2–7 Cobb angle (degrees) was the angle between lines tangent to the inferior endplates of C2 and C7 (positive values denote lordosis). The C2–7 sagittal vertical axis (SVA; mm) was the horizontal offset from a plumb line through the C2 centroid to the posterosuperior corner of C7 on standing lateral radiographs. The modified K-line interval (mK-line INT; mm) was the minimum perpendicular distance from the most ventral compressive structure to a line connecting the cord midpoints at the C2 and C7 inferior endplates (Fig. 4).

All radiographic parameters were measured by two independent observers (spine fellows) blinded to group assignment and outcomes. Each observer performed two measurements ≥ 2 weeks apart; the average of the two readings was analyzed. Inter-and intra-observer reliability were summarized using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC; two-way random effects, absolute agreement).

Surgical techniques

Anterior procedures (ACDF/ACCF/HDF) provided direct ventral decompression with interbody or corpectomy reconstruction and plate/screw fixation per standard technique. Posterior procedures (LP/LF) provided indirect decompression via posterior cord drift; laminectomy cases were stabilized with lateral mass or pedicle screw–rod constructs when indicated. Approach selection considered deformity, canal-occupying morphology, K-line/mK-line INT, number of involved levels, and surgeon judgment.

Anterior decompression was favored when the ventral ossified mass, high canal-occupying ratio, or K-line (−) morphology suggested limited posterior cord drift or focal ventral beak; posterior procedures (laminoplasty or laminectomy with fusion) were favored for multilevel compression with preserved lordosis and K-line (+) morphology. Final decisions incorporated patient-specific anatomy and surgeon judgment.

Outcomes

The core independent variable was surgical approach (anterior vs posterior). The primary biomechanical endpoint was ΔMFP_{24mo}, defined as the absolute change in percentage points from baseline to 24 months; values > 0 denote a greater reduction in MFP (i.e., clinical improvement). Key secondary endpoints included MFP at 6 months, SCR/CR1/CR2, and JOA/Recovery1/Recovery2 at each postoperative timepoint.

Standardized pain scales (neck/arm VAS) and detailed manual muscle testing were not collected longitudinally beyond the JOA sub-scores; we acknowledge this as a limitation for clinical symptom profiling.

Sample size considerations

This was a fixed-cohort retrospective study (n = 63); therefore, no a priori power calculation was performed. Post hoc precision and detectable-effect considerations are reported with the results to contextualize between-group differences.

Data quality and management

All source data were double-checked against the electronic record. Pedobarographic files were screened for artifacts and completeness before aggregation. Radiographic and MFP reliability metrics (ICC/CV) are reported with the results. Data were de-identified prior to analysis and stored on a secure institutional server with access logging.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were two-sided (α = 0.05). Continuous variables are mean ± SD; categorical variables are n (%). Group differences used independent-samples t-tests (categorical: χ2/Fisher’s). Within-group change used paired t-tests. The prespecified primary endpoint was 24-month MFP. We modeled ΔMFP_24mo (absolute change, percentage points) with linear regression including surgical approach and covariates that met p < 0.10 in screening (preoperative SCR, CR1, preoperative MFP). Sensitivity analyses (complete-case; extended covariates) confirmed robustness. Full statistical details are provided in Supplementary Methods S2.

The primary endpoint (ΔMFP_{24mo}) was the absolute change (percentage points) from baseline MFP to 24 months. We report percentages with one decimal; angles and ratios with two decimals (Cobb, SCR, CNR); and SVA in millimeters. Figures display means with 95% confidence intervals; exact values are in the tables.

Analytic details

Missing MFP values at follow-up were addressed with multiple imputation (m = 20); the primary analysis used complete cases, and imputed analyses yielded materially similar estimates. Sensitivity analyses comprised (i) a speed-matched subset (Δspeed ≤ 5%), and (ii) an extended covariate model that included C2–7 Cobb, SVA, mK-line INT, CNR, age, sex, BMI, smoking, hypertension, and diabetes. Given n = 63 and three timepoints, we did not fit mixed-effects models in the primary analysis to avoid over-parameterization; future multicenter cohorts will be powered for linear mixed modeling.

Results

Cohort and baseline characteristics

Of 265 screened patients, 63 met all criteria and were included (anterior approach = 34; posterior approach = 29). Baseline demographics and preoperative imaging did not differ between groups (Table 2).

Screening and inclusion

We screened 265 records (12/2018–12/2022). Exclusions were: prior cervical surgery (n = 18), no focal intramedullary T2 hyperintensity (SCR < 1.20; n = 102), non-degenerative indication (trauma/infection/neoplasm; n = 12), inadequate follow-up (n = 41), and incomplete plantar-pressure data at required timepoints (n = 29). The final cohort comprised 63 patients (anterior 34, posterior 29). A CONSORT-style flow diagram is provided (Fig. 2).

Radiographic cord signal

At 6 months, regression of hyperintense cord signal (CR1) did not differ significantly between groups (0.11 ± 0.13 vs 0.02 ± 0.24; p = 0.08). At 24 months, the anterior group showed greater regression (CR2) than the posterior group (0.22 ± 0.14 vs − 0.01 ± 0.24; p = 0.001), consistent with lower 24-month SCR (1.26 ± 0.19 vs 1.62 ± 0.33; p = 0.001) (Table 2 and Fig.5).

Medial plantar pressure (primary biomechanical outcome)

Preoperatively, MFP did not differ between groups (52.6 ± 7.4% vs 52.9 ± 7.6%; p = 0.87).

At 6 months, both groups improved (lower MFP) with no between-group difference (49.4 ± 6.8% vs 49.8 ± 6.9%; difference − 0.4% (95% CI − 3.8 to 3.0), p = 0.83). Within-group reductions from baseline were significant for anterior (− 3.2%; paired p ≈ 0.003) and for posterior (− 3.1%; paired p ≈ 0.005).

At 24 months, improvement diverged: the anterior group maintained a larger reduction in MFP than the posterior group (46.1 ± 6.0% vs 50.6 ± 6.4%; between-group difference − 4.5 percentage points, 95% CI − 7.6 to − 1.4; Cohen’s d = 0.73; p = 0.004). Within-group change was − 6.5% for anterior (paired p < 0.001) versus − 2.3% for posterior (paired p ≈ 0.08). Expressed as improvement rates, the 24-month MFP-IR was 12.4% for anterior and 4.3% for posterior (between-group difference ≈ 8.0 percentage points) (Table 2, Figs. 6 and 7).

Neurological outcomes

JOA scores improved in both groups, with a larger gain in the anterior group by 24 months (14.1 ± 1.0 vs 12.6 ± 2.1; p = 0.001). Recovery2 (24-month JOA recovery) was 0.54 ± 0.18 vs 0.28 ± 0.30 (p = 0.001) (Table 2).

Determinants of 24-month improvement in MFP (regression)

In univariable analyses, surgical approach (anterior vs posterior) predicted greater 24-month improvement in MFP (β = + 4.5%, 95% CI + 1.6 to + 7.4; p = 0.003). Higher preoperative SCR (β = + 2.9% per unit, 95% CI + 1.0 to + 4.8; p = 0.003) and higher preoperative MFP (β = + 0.30% per baseline percent, 95% CI + 0.12 to + 0.48; p = 0.001) were also associated with greater improvement; CR1 trended positive (β = + 0.9% per 0.1 unit, 95% CI − 0.08 to + 1.9; p = 0.067) (Table 3).

In the multivariable model including approach, preoperative SCR, CR1, and preoperative MFP, anterior surgery remained an independent predictor of greater 24-month improvement (β = + 3.9%, 95% CI + 1.8 to + 6.0; p = 0.001). Preoperative SCR (β = + 1.4%, 95% CI + 0.4 to + 2.4; p = 0.006) and preoperative MFP (β = + 0.22% per baseline percent, 95% CI + 0.05 to + 0.39; p = 0.012) remained significant; CR1 showed a borderline association (β = + 0.6%, 95% CI − 0.05 to + 1.3; p = 0.071) (Table 3).

At 24 months, anterior surgery yielded greater improvement in medial plantar pressure than posterior surgery, and this effect remained after adjustment for baseline imaging and plantar-loading covariates.

Sensitivity analyses

Results were unchanged when (i) restricting to patients with complete plantar-pressure data at all timepoints (n = 59) and (ii) additionally adjusting the regression for C2–7 Cobb, SVA, mK-line INT, CNR, age, sex, BMI, smoking, hypertension, and diabetes (anterior vs posterior β ≈ + 3.6% to + 4.0%; all p ≤ 0.01).

Sample size considerations and precision

This retrospective cohort had n = 63 (34 vs 29). For the primary between-group endpoint (24-month MFP), the observed mean difference was 4.5% (SE ≈ 1.57%), yielding 95% CI − 7.6 to − 1.4 and two-sided p ≈ 0.004. Given the pooled SD (~ 6.19%) and group sizes, the minimum detectable difference at 80% power and α = 0.05 is ≈ 4.38%, indicating our observed effect exceeded the pre-hoc detectability threshold. The approximate achieved power for the observed effect is ~ 82% (normal approximation). These estimates quantify the precision of the 24-month effect.

Data quality and reliability

Pedobarography completeness. Valid MFP data were available for 63/63 (100%) pre-op, 61/63 (96.8%) at 6 months, and 59/63 (93.7%) at 24 months. Missing follow-up measurements were due to scheduling or technical dropouts. Primary analyses used complete cases; sensitivity analyses with multiple imputation (m = 20) yielded materially similar estimates (Table 4).

Discussion

Principal findings

Both approaches reduced MFP at 6 months; by 24 months, the anterior cohort exhibited a larger reduction aligned with greater T2-signal regression (lower SCR, higher CR2) and larger JOA gains. Thus, surgical approach was associated with differential durability of improvement.

Interpretation and mechanistic context

A medial loading bias is expected when dorsal-column and corticospinal conduction are impaired. Decompression may reduce medial loading if sensorimotor transmission improves [22]. Anterior decompression directly removes the ventral OPLL mass and increases cord–lesion separation, which may restore dorsal-column afference more effectively than posterior indirect decompression that relies on posterior drift while leaving the ventral ossified mass in place—especially when the canal-occupying ratio is high or the K-line is negative [3, 24]. The greater 24-month regression of T2 hyperintensity we observed is consistent with studies showing that intramedullary T2 signal characteristics correlate with postoperative neurological recovery [4, 6, 7]. Taken together, the pattern likely reflects better restoration of dorsal-column afference when the ventral ossified mass is directly removed.

Our primary endpoint—change in medial foot pressure (MFP) at 24 months—captures proprioceptive gait control during natural walking. Both approaches improved MFP at 6 months, while the anterior cohort showed a larger sustained reduction by 24 months, aligned with greater regression of intramedullary T2 hyperintensity and larger JOA gains. Taken together, the pattern likely reflects better recovery of dorsal-column afference when the ventral OPLL mass is directly removed, particularly in morphologies where posterior drift is limited (e.g., high canal-occupying ratio or K-line (−)). These findings may contribute to existing approach-selection frameworks by adding a biomechanical endpoint relevant to everyday ambulation.

Choice of surgical approach and confounding by indication

Approach selection at our center considered deformity, canal-occupying morphology, K-line/mK-line INT, number of involved levels, and surgeon judgment. Baseline demographics and imaging were similar between groups; nevertheless, selection bias is possible in any nonrandomized cohort. To mitigate confounding by indication, (i) we prespecified a single primary endpoint (ΔMFP_{24mo}) to limit multiplicity; (ii) adjusted the primary model for a parsimonious set of a priori predictors (pre-op SCR, CR1, pre-op MFP); and (iii) performed sensitivity analyses with extended covariates (C2–7 Cobb, SVA, mK-line INT, CNR, age, sex, BMI, smoking, hypertension, diabetes) as well as a speed-matched subset. Across these checks, the anterior approach was independently associated with greater 24-month improvement in MFP. We deliberately avoided over-adjustment given n = 63, following the principle that small samples and many covariates can destabilize estimates; therefore, only univariable predictors meeting p < 0.10 entered the multivariable model. We interpret all associations cautiously and do not infer causality.

Device/platform considerations and generalizability

We used in-shoe pressure-sensing insoles at 100 Hz to quantify plantar loading during overground walking. Compared with laboratory force-plate/treadmill systems, in-shoe sensors trade some absolute force fidelity for ecological validity and repeated measurements in routine clinical corridors. Our primary variable—percentage load within a pre-defined medial mask—minimizes device-specific scaling differences and showed excellent inter- and intra-rater reliability (ICC ≥ 0.95). Nonetheless, the lack of synchronized ground-reaction forces and kinematics limits parameter breadth; future studies should integrate spatiotemporal metrics (step length/time, cadence), center-of-pressure progression, and electromyography to broaden mechanistic insight. Independent validations show that instrumented insoles yield reliable plantar load and spatiotemporal metrics compared with laboratory systems, supporting their use for longitudinal clinical monitoring [25–27].

Modeling strategy and repeated-measures inference

With three timepoints and a modest sample, we prioritized a change-score endpoint (ΔMFP_{24mo}) to maximize power and avoid over-parameterization. Linear mixed models (LMMs) can model trajectories and random effects, but with n = 63 and multiple potential confounders, overfitting and unstable variance components are a concern. We therefore present LMM as a prespecified future analytical plan for a larger, multicenter cohort, and we report 6-month values descriptively while focusing hypothesis testing on the 24-month endpoint. This strategy aligns with the fixed cohort and low missingness profile; multiple imputation (m = 20) was used for limited missing follow-ups, and complete-case results were similar.

Scope of outcome measures

Focusing on MFP was an a priori, mechanistically targeted choice to test proprioceptive recovery. We agree that gait is multidimensional and that exclusive use of MFP may under-represent other clinically relevant domains. We therefore frame MFP as a complement to JOA and MRI rather than a replacement and outline an expanded outcome set for future work (instrumented gait, EMG, SSEPs, DTI-MRI), including patient-centered endpoints (falls, participation).

Relationship to prior work

Objective gait studies in DCM report shorter steps, prolonged anticipatory postural adjustments, and medialized plantar loading versus controls; decompression improves kinetic profiles. Our OPLL-restricted cohort with focal ISI extends this evidence by showing an approach-dependent 24-month difference in medial loading [9, 10, 22]. These findings cohere with approach-selection frameworks in which K-line status and canal occupancy guide expectations for posterior drift versus direct ventral removal [1, 18, 20].

Clinical implications

MFP offers a quick, objective read-out of proprioceptive gait control and may assist postoperative monitoring alongside JOA and MRI. When proprioceptive-dominant dysfunction is prominent and ventral compression is morphologically limiting posterior drift, anterior decompression was associated with a more durable correction of medialized loading at 24 months. These observations likely reflect the different mechanisms of decompression and should inform shared decision-making rather than be interpreted as causal [28]. Pairing MFP with SCR/CR2 may provide a practical way to monitor and could help tailor rehabilitation.

Causal interpretation

Because treatment assignment was not randomized, our results should be interpreted as associations conditional on the measured covariates. The observed advantage with anterior decompression at 24 months may reflect both direct decompression of ventral pathology and unmeasured practice-pattern factors.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include a pathophysiologically homogenous OPLL cohort with focal ISI, standardized pedobarography focusing on a priori-defined medial loading, and aligned improvements across biomechanics (MFP), imaging (SCR/CR2), and clinical function (JOA). Limitations include retrospective single-center design, potential selection and residual-confounding bias, and device-/protocol-specific pedobarography that may limit generalizability. Although between-group differences in complications were not powered for inference, operative characteristics (levels, blood loss) differed by design and could partly mediate recovery. Finally, we did not include a nonoperative comparator; natural history effects cannot be fully excluded. These limitations mirror those commonly noted in OPLL and DCM surgical literature and should guide interpretation.

Future directions

Prospective, multicenter studies should (i) stratify by K-line and canal-occupying ratio; (ii) incorporate multimodal outcomes (instrumented gait, EMG, somatosensory evoked potentials, quantitative MRI/DTI); (iii) predefine clinically meaningful change thresholds for MFP; and (iv) capture falls and participation metrics. Sensor-based home monitoring could extend follow-up beyond 24 months and clarify adaptation versus progression after different approaches.

Conclusion

In OPLL with gait disturbance and focal T2 hyperintensity, both approaches improved function at 6 months; anterior decompression produced a larger 24-month reduction in medial foot pressure, with aligned improvements on MRI and JOA. MFP should complement JOA and MRI in follow-up, and prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate MFP thresholds and refine indications.
