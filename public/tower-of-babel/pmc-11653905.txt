Career choice of Saudi medical students: opting for ophthalmology residency programs - perceptions and sociodemographic influencing factors – a cross-sectional study in Northern Saudi Arabia
Authors: Abdulmohsen Almulhim, Ashokkumar Thirunavukkarasu, Bader Alanazi, Abdulrahman Saleh Alfaleh, Shaker O. Alreshidi, Rana Bandar A. Almusib, Lubna Abdulrazaq Zaal Alfaleh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and aimThe present survey aimed to explore northern Saudi medical students’ perceptions and sociodemographic influencing factors in choosing ophthalmology as a career specialty to determine if they are in alignment with the health sector transformation program of the Vision 2030 initiative.MethodsThis study was conducted among 384 medical students from two universities in northern Saudi Arabia (KSA). We used a standard and validated questionnaire to collect the required data. The perception aspect consisted of 20 questions divided into five subdomains: personal and lifestyle factors, residency program aspects, patients’ care, work culture, and financial factors. We used binomial logistic regression to find sociodemographic predictors for choosing ophthalmology as the first choice.ResultsThe present study demonstrated that northern Saudi medical students preferred ophthalmology as the second most preferred specialty (11.2%). Among the motivational factors, the most frequently reported was the influence of faculty members from their college (74.2%), followed by financial aspects (74%) and family and friends (73.7%). The preference to choose ophthalmology was significantly higher among female gender (ref: male, AOR = 2.63, 95% CI = 1.39–4.88, p = 0.001) and students with a GPA of 4.6 and above (ref: GPA less than 3.5, AOR = 3.77, 95% CI = 2.99–5.36, p = 0.023).ConclusionNorthern Saudi medical students’ preference for ophthalmology aligns with Vision 2030 goals. Therefore, it is essential to tailor strategies by policymakers to the medical students according to the identified sociodemographic influencing factors. Furthermore, we recommend prospective studies across all the regions of KSA to identify regional variations.

Introduction

Ophthalmologists play an integral role in the healthcare system, and they offer essential care for visual health, which greatly impacts the quality of life and productivity. Their contribution is invaluable for the management of both common and rare ocular diseases [1, 2]. Furthermore, due to the nature and widespread frequency of eye disorders, the patient care required from an ophthalmologist lasts for many years, as stated by the American Academy of Ophthalmology [3].

The recently implemented health sector transformation program under Vision 2030 by the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) necessitates the assessment of medical education programs and necessary changes that help the students to choose a specialty of their choice and need for the country [4–6]. Vision 2030 was launched in the KSA as a part of the national developmental plan. One of the important pillars of this initiative is the national health sector transformation program, which aims to improve the quality and accessibility of medical services in the country, in turn, improving public health and preventing diseases, including ophthalmology-related diseases [6]. In the KSA, vision-related problems such as diabetic retinopathy, cataracts, glaucoma, dry eye disorders, and age-related macular degeneration are widely prevalent. Moreover, the aging population, increasing screen time among children, and rising prevalence of diabetes mellitus in KSA, several ophthalmologic problems are expected to increase in the future among adults and children [7–9]. These statements indicate the necessity of having more ophthalmologists in the future for the nation’s health transformation program and change in healthcare needs, as ophthalmologists play vital role in delivering care for these problems [10, 11]. Most of the time, undergraduate medical students select their future career specialty during their study period in medical school. Hence, the medical school curriculum and experience play a vital role in encouraging the students to choose the specialty, such as ophthalmology, that the KSA requires to meet the change in healthcare needs.

According to some of the authors, the process of selecting ophthalmology as the residence program for medical students is multifaceted and influenced by numerous factors [12–14]. These factors are generally divided into personal factors and work-related factors. A recent study conducted among Canadian medical students in 2022 by Li et al. reported that more than three-fourths of questions in their survey that is related to ophthalmology had positive responses, and the positive opinion was significantly associated with the student’s interest and exposure to ophthalmology during medical school [12]. According to Madani et al., the workload during the residency training program was one of the critical determinants of selecting an ophthalmology residence program [15].

Considering the ever-changing desire of medical students to select the residency program, continuous assessment of these factors is essential. The literature search about Saudi Vision 2030 and the Saudi health workforce in Google Scholar and other major databases revealed that numerous authors explored various aspects of Vision 2030, including policies and demand for other specialties [16–18]. However, limited research focuses on medical students’ career preferences in ophthalmology within the context of this health sector transformation program. In fact, a recent study in 2024 by Aldebasi et al. stated that only 38% of ophthalmologists are Saudi nationals, and the study highlights disparities in the geographic distribution of ophthalmologists [19]. Furthermore, given the regional variations, in the context of the Vision 2030 Program of Saudi Arabia, which emphasizes the importance of matching the development of healthcare workers with the nation’s health needs, a comprehensive analysis of medical students’ perceptions and associated sociodemographic factors to take ophthalmology as a first choice need to be evaluated. Also, there is a need for region-specific data for policymakers and educational institutions. Hence, the present survey aimed to explore northern Saudi medical students’ perceptions and sociodemographic influencing factors in choosing ophthalmology as a career specialty to determine if they are in alignment with the health sector transformation program of the Vision 2030 initiative.

Materials and methods

Study description

The present study is a cross-sectional study conducted from 15 September 2023 to 15 February 2024. It was done among medical students from two universities in the northern KSA regions. The selected universities were Jouf University and Northern Border University. The present study included all medical students from years 2 to 5 and interns, excluding those unwilling to participate and first-year students.

Sampling strategies

The present study calculated the sample size using Raosoft online sample size calculator [20]. This calculator has been used by numerous authors in the research [21–23]. Raosoft uses the principles of Cochran’s sample size estimator formula (n = z2pq/d2). In this formula, n = minimum required medical students for this study, p = expected proportion, q = 1 – p, and d = 5% margin of error. We have taken the expected proportion as 50% to get the maximum number of required participants. Considering these values, the expected number of participants for the study was estimated as 384. Furthermore, the number of students selected from each university was based on the total number of medical students enrolled at that institution. In the following stages, the required number of students from each academic year was selected based on the total number of students in that academic year using systematic random sampling (according to their registration number order).

Data collection procedures

We started the data collection process after obtaining the clearance letter from the College of Medicine Scientific Committee and ethical approval from LCBE, Jouf University (approval no: 2-04-45). The concerned authorities of the selected college of medicine from the chosen university were approached, and necessary permissions were obtained. The required number of students was selected after obtaining permission from the concerned authorities (as described in the sampling strategies). Initially, our team contacted the student leader of each class to find out the availability of the students. To ensure availability and increase the response rate, the data collectors approached medical students during the self-directed learning period. Moreover, efforts have been made to avoid data collection one week before the exam and during exam time. The data collectors started to collect the data using a standard and validated questionnaire from the students after briefing the study. The authors used the details of the institute-approved informed consent form. The data collectors briefed the participants on the study’s aim and steps to be followed during participation (including the right to withdraw at any time) and the duration of the data collection form. Furthermore, the participants were informed about the anonymity and privacy of data and the overall benefits of the study results for the healthcare sectors. After briefing these details, informed consent was obtained from all participants who were willing to participate. We made the Google form, and the medical students were requested to complete the Google form on the data collectors’ electronic devices. The medical education and ophthalmology faculties prepared this questionnaire through a focus group discussion based on existing pieces of literature [12, 15, 24].

The questionnaire consists of three parts. In the first part, we inquired about sociodemographic characteristics such as age, gender, year of education, grade point average (GPA), taking ophthalmology as an elective during the internship, and their participation in research related to ophthalmology. The second part inquired about the medical student’s first choice of residency training program and future career options. Those who wished to choose ophthalmology responded to the questions related to motivational factors to choose ophthalmology. The third part consisted of twenty questions about participants’ perception of choosing ophthalmology as their career choice. These twenty questions were divided into five subdomains, such as personal and lifestyle factors (3 items), residency program aspects (4 items), patients’ care (5 items), work culture (4 items), and financial factors (4 items). The selected medical students responded on a 5-point Likert’s scale ranging from strongly agree (5 points) to strongly disagree (1 point). We tested the prepared questionnaire among 30 students, and their feedback was that the data collection form was easy and that a student could complete it within 10 min. The Cronbach alpha value of the questionnaire obtained through the pretest analysis was 0.83, indicating a high level of internal consistency of the tool used in the study [25, 26].

Data analysis

The research team completed all descriptive and inferential statistical analysis using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS), IBM Version 21.0, a version widely used by researchers in similar studies [27–29]. The analysis involves descriptive statistics (frequency, proportion, mean, standard deviation) and inferential statistics. The descriptive data, including students’ background characteristics and Likert’s scale responses in perception items, is shown as number and frequency (n, %). Next, a comparison of the responses among each item of the students’ perceptions between those who wished to choose ophthalmology as their first preference and those who did not select it was completed using an independent t-test. Finally, we used binomial logistic regression to find the sociodemographic predictors for choosing ophthalmology as the first choice. All applied statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered a statistically significant value.

Results

During the data collection period, we contacted 465 medical students to participate in the survey to get a minimum sample size of 384 (response rate: 82.6%). Among the participants, the majority of the respondents belonged to the age group of 21 to 23 years, with a mean age of 22.28. Regarding exposure to ophthalmology, 41.7% completed ophthalmology courses in their undergraduate, 9.6% took ophthalmology elective training during an internship, and 20.9% participated in ophthalmology research (Table 1).

Discussion

Choosing the residency program that is their future career among medical students is a challenging task involving several factors. The number of available specialists in medical and surgical specializations, including the ophthalmology department, is crucial in the KSA, as it needs to be in accordance with the ambitious Vision 2030 national health sector transformation program [6]. In the KSA, medical school studies consisted of 6 years (excluding the preparatory year), in which the first three years are basic sciences, the next two years (fourth and fifth) are clinical sciences, and the last year will be internship training in clinical settings [30, 31]. It is worth mentioning that medical students’ right time to choose a specialty is during medical school, particularly the end of the third year, as stated by the American Medical Association [32]. The present study examined the perceptions and factors of northern Saudi medical students in choosing an ophthalmology residency program.

A detailed evaluation among 384 students from two universities in the northern region revealed that internal medicine had the highest percentage of students who wished to pursue their residency program. Approximately11% of the students preferred the ophthalmology residency program as their first choice. Ophthalmology specialty is one of undergraduate medical students’ one of the highly preferred residency programs in the KSA and some other countries [12, 13, 24, 33, 34]. Nonetheless, our study found that it was a higher proportion than other studies [13, 24, 35]. A Nigerian study by Onyekwe and Nwosu reported that 16% of their participants responded that the ophthalmology specialty is their future career [36]. Notably, the high positive intention among Saudi medical students towards ophthalmology is particularly relevant in Vision 2030 contexts, indicating the alignments between the nation’s priorities and medical students’ aspirations [6]. Furthermore, it could be due to perceived professional fulfillment, the nature of ophthalmology practice, and personal choices [12, 37]. Another contributing factor to the higher proportion of people who prefer ophthalmology is motivation from the college faculties. In the present study, the highest ratio of motivational factors was a faculty member (s) of their college, followed by financial aspects, family, and friends, and taking electives during the internship. The possible factor for the family and friends’ role in selecting a specialty could be, perhaps, a cultural factor in KSA, as the family seems to have a significant influence on the decision. This might be attributed to work-life balance and possible advancement found working within the health sector under Vision 2030’s transformation program. Therefore, educators and policymakers must take into consideration the effects that the family and friends’ roles have had before they set out to make policies that would compel students to choose a specialty as the nation demands. Our findings and other research findings indicate that the college’s faculties are not only teachers but also can be role models for medical students through their professionalism, work ethics, clinical skills, and patient care [38–40].

Similar to our study, Yu et al. from China reported that job opportunities, financial benefits, and passion for ophthalmology were the primary motivating factors influencing Chinese medical students to choose ophthalmology as a career [35]. According to Sud et al., personal and professional aspects and passion were the factors that influenced medical students in choosing a career [41]. While evaluating the motivating factors among the medical students influencing factors in choosing ophthalmology and non-ophthalmology career choice among Penn State College of Medicine, Cui et al. stated that professional satisfaction and executing different surgical procedures were the significant factors in choosing ophthalmology among the applicants [37]. On analyzing the motivational factors among Brazilian medical students of different age groups, Gameiro et al. reported that work hours and job satisfaction were the primary motivational factors in choosing ophthalmology. Furthermore, they revealed that these factors did not vary across different generations of participants [33]. Variation from the present study’s findings and other discussed studies indicate that the motivational factors significantly vary across different demographic locations. This emphasizes the significance of conducting a study in northern KSA due to the country’s wide sociocultural and demographic variations.

Regarding medical students’ perception of an ophthalmology career, our findings are in alignment with the study done in Canada by Li et al. In their study, the mean scores of perceptions were high in lifestyle, personal, research, and academic competitiveness [12]. Both studies indicate that early exposure and positive perceptions of lifestyle and career opportunities contribute significantly to students’ interest in ophthalmology. The present study revealed the mean perception scores in the duration of residence training, competitiveness, diverse patient care, and available practice scopes than those who did not wish to select ophthalmology as their first choice. Similar to our study, some authors explored the association between the duration of training and career selection among medical students [42]. In contrast to the present study, a Canadian study reported that the mean score was significantly higher in participants’ perception of the prestige status of ophthalmology career [12]. A study conducted by Parija et al. in India stated that a rapidly advancing field was one of their participants’ major perceived factors [43].

Similar to our research, Gameiro et al. and Li et al. did not find age to be a significant factor in choosing ophthalmology [12, 33]. In contrast, Parija et al. stated in their study that the lower age group was more inclined (positive perception) towards choosing ophthalmology as their career choice [43]. The differences observed across the studies may be attributed to variations in the study settings, such as demographic characteristics and inclusion and exclusion criteria. These variations across the studies once again emphasize the necessity of regional studies and ongoing monitoring of the medical students’ perceptions and factors in choosing a specialty. A high GPA and involvement in research related to ophthalmology again indicate the competitiveness of the residency program. However, evaluating the correlation between high GPA and other predictors can give insights into the medical students’ decision-making process when selecting ophthalmology as a career choice. This is particularly relevant as the available residency program in ophthalmology in KSA is limited. Finally, female gender preference in ophthalmology career choice is a crucial factor identified by the present study. This trend is a positive finding as there is always a need for diverse healthcare workers, especially in a culturally conservative Saudi society, and empowering women, according to the Vision 2030 initiative. Gender is one of the factors in choosing the specialty in some residency programs [44, 45]. A study conducted in Madinah, KSA, reported no significant difference in choosing ophthalmology between males and females [14].

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that northern Saudi medical students preferred ophthalmology as the second most preferred specialty. The highest proportion of the motivational factor was a faculty member (s) of their college, followed by financial aspects. Regarding preference to choose an ophthalmology career, we found that females and those with high GPAs had higher odds of preferring ophthalmology as their future career. The positive intention of the northern Saudi medical students is in alignment with the health sector transformation program of the Vision 2030 initiative. Hence, it is essential to recognize the students’ interests and nurture them by instituting appropriate medical education and healthcare policy. Therefore, individual aspiration can be achieved along with the required healthcare needs. Furthermore, we recommend prospective studies across all the regions of KSA to identify changes in trends and cross-cultural variations and for the generalization of findings.
