Bilevel Analysis of Cost and Emissions Externalities from Data Center Load Shifting
Aron Brenner, Rahman Khorramfar, Nathan Engelman Lado

Abstract
Data centers are emerging as large, flexible electricity consumers capable of shifting computational workloads across locations in response to economic and environmental signals. While this flexibility has potential for emissions reduction, its impact on power system operations depends critically on how such behavior interacts with network constraints and market signals. We develop a bilevel optimization framework in which a data center minimizes a weighted combination of electricity cost and marginal emissions intensity (LME), while the system operator clears economic dispatch under transmission and generation constraints. Focusing on a stylized three-bus power system, we derive closed-form, piecewise-linear expressions for both the data center and system-wide objectives as functions of the data centers’ load shift. These expressions capture threshold-driven regime changes due to congestion and renewable saturation. We identify sufficient conditions under which the data center’s decentralized decisions align with or diverge from socially optimal behavior and characterize the resulting externalities. Our results reveal how system topology and generator asymmetry affect incentive alignment and provide insight into when marginal price or emissions signals may fail to guide flexible loads toward socially beneficial outcomes. Our results offer a tractable starting point for analyzing decentralized flexibility under carbon-aware incentives and suggest directions for improving coordination between flexible loads and system operations.

A key feature of data centers is their ability to flexibly schedule or migrate computing workloads across space and time. This flexibility has been promoted as a tool for reducing emissions and aligning computing activity with low-carbon power availability – an approach already deployed by major technology companies

While workload shifting appears promising from a sustainability perspective, its system-wide implications remain poorly understood. In particular, when data centers independently minimize their own costs or emissions using marginal signals – such as Locational Marginal Prices (LMPs) or Locational Marginal Emissions (LMEs) – their behavior can interact with grid constraints in complex ways. Load shifting that appears beneficial locally may, under transmission congestion or renewable curtailment, impose unintended externalities on other consumers or increase overall system emissions
[
3
,
7
,
10
]
. This raises a critical question:
When does carbon-aware geographic load shifting by flexible consumers help the grid, and when does it harm it?

Answering this question requires understanding how decentralized, carbon-sensitive behavior interacts with network constraints and dispatch incentives. Prior work has laid important groundwork. Some studies focus on carbon accounting and signaling, such as estimating LMEs from LMPs to inform consumer behavior
[
5
,
9
]
. Others have proposed centralized market designs that incorporate consumer carbon preferences into market clearing
[
6
]
. A third line of work evaluates carbon-aware workload shifting heuristics using simulations or empirical traces
[
7
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
. However, these approaches typically assume fixed loads or centralized coordination, and do not analytically characterize how decentralized, strategic behavior by flexible consumers feeds back into system outcomes.
In this work, we fill that gap by modeling a carbon-aware data center that strategically shifts its load across a stylized three-bus network in response to LMP and LME signals. The model captures key features of grid operation—such as congestion, renewable curtailment, and marginal pricing—while remaining analytically tractable.

Our contributions are as follows:

1.

We propose a bilevel optimization model in which a data center minimizes a weighted combination of cost and emissions by shifting load geographically, while the system operator performs economic dispatch. This captures the feedback loop between decentralized load-shifting behavior and system-level price and emissions signals.

2.

We derive a closed-form, piecewise-linear characterization of the data center’s and system’s objectives under load shifting (Theorem
1
), revealing discontinuities and regime shifts due to network constraints.

3.

We identify precise conditions (Propositions
1
,
2
) under which decentralized decisions align with or diverge from socially optimal outcomes, providing insight into when flexible load supports or undermines system decarbonization.

From a technical perspective, our approach tackles the challenge of analyzing a bilevel optimization problem in which the upper-level decision – load shifting by a data center – affects the dual variables (LMPs and LMEs) of a network-constrained economic dispatch problem. While such problems are generally nonconvex and difficult to solve, we leverage sensitivity analysis of the optimal dispatch basis to derive closed-form, piecewise-linear expressions for both the data centers’ and the system’s cost-emission objectives. This structure allows us to precisely characterize the conditions under which marginal signals change due to transmission congestion or renewable saturation, and to identify threshold-driven regime shifts that create potential misalignment between private and social incentives. These results demonstrate that meaningful insight into the externalities of flexible, carbon-aware behavior can be obtained even in stylized models, and offer a foundation for future extensions to more realistic systems.

Together, our results show that even well-intentioned, carbon-aware behavior by flexible consumers can backfire if system constraints are not accounted for. While our model is intentionally stylized, it captures essential features of power system operations that shape incentives for flexible loads. By identifying the conditions under which private incentives align with or diverge from social objectives, our results provide a foundation for designing more effective carbon signals and coordination mechanisms – especially as data centers and other flexible loads become increasingly central to grid decarbonization efforts.

We consider a stylized three-bus power system with two load nodes (buses 1 and 2) and one renewable supply node (bus 0). Each load node has a fixed, inelastic demand
\ell_{i}
as well as a fraction of the flexible data center load
L
, which can be geographically shifted between the two nodes. Let
\delta\in[0,L]
denote the portion of the data center load served at bus 1, with the remaining
L-\delta
served at bus
2

\delta
, the system operator performs economic dispatch to minimize generation cost subject to generator and transmission constraints.

We model this interaction as a bilevel optimization problem: the upper-level decision-maker (the data center) chooses
\delta
to minimize a weighted combination of cost and emissions, while anticipating the system operator’s response. At the lower level, the system operator clears the market and determines LMPs and LMEs, which depend endogenously on the data center’s decision via network flows and congestion. This structure captures the feedback loop between flexible load decisions and the price-emission signals they respond to.

2.2
Load Shifting Problem

We assume data centers choose to shift
\delta
load to bus
1
in order to minimize the combined cost-emissions objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{DC}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\pi})=

\displaystyle\ \alpha_{\mathrm{DC}}(\lambda_{1}\times\delta+\lambda_{2}\times(L-\delta))

\displaystyle+\ (1-\alpha_{\mathrm{DC}})(\pi_{1}\times\delta+\pi_{2}\times(L-\delta))

where
\alpha_{\mathrm{DC}}\in[0,1]
trade-offs between energy costs and emissions.

We note here that both LMPs
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and LMEs

\delta

1

\displaystyle\min_{\delta}\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{DC}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}))

(2a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle 0\leq\delta\leq L

(2b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mathrm{ED}^{*}(\ell_{1}+\delta,\ell_{2}-\delta),

(2c)

where
\mathrm{ED}^{*}(\ell_{1}+\delta,\ell_{2}-\delta)
denotes the optimal solutions to the dual of the economic dispatch problem with
\delta
units of load shifted from bus
2

The bilevel nature of Problem (
2

in the data center objective) and the piecewise constant (i.e., nonconvex) nature of the mapping from

2.3
Externalities of Load Shifting

To measure the impact of data centers’ load shifting on system-wide costs and emissions, we define the social welfare objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{SW}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\pi})=

\displaystyle\ \alpha_{\mathrm{SW}}(\lambda_{1}\times(\ell_{1}+\delta)+\lambda_{2}\times(\ell_{2}-\delta))

\displaystyle+\ (1-\alpha_{\mathrm{SW}})(\pi_{1}\times(\ell_{1}+\delta)+\pi_{2}\times(\ell_{2}-\delta)),

where
\alpha_{\mathrm{SW}}\in[0,1]

In Sec.
3

and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{DW}}(\delta)

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{DC}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{SW}}

3.1
Piecewise-Linear Objectives and Dispatch Regimes

To study these effects, we first characterize the data center and social welfare objectives as a function of the load shift. Let us first define
\eta_{i}^{j}
, which combines the marginal cost and emissions of dispatchable generation at bus
i
for objective
j
. Specifically, we let
\eta_{i}^{j}\coloneqq\alpha_{j}c_{i}+(1-\alpha_{j})e_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
and
j\in\{\mathrm{DC},\mathrm{SW}\}
. We also define the threshold
\tau
as

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\{F_{01}-F_{12},\ -\ell_{0}-F_{02}-F_{12}\}-\ell_{1}.

In effect,
\tau
is the point at which shifting additional load to bus
1
requires generation at bus
1
with marginal cost
\lambda_{1}=c_{1}
and marginal emissions
\pi_{1}=e_{1}
. This occurs when the total load at bus
1
,
\ell_{1}+\delta
, is great enough that either (a) line
(0,1)
is congested (i.e.,
F_{01}=\ell_{1}+\delta+F_{12}
), or (b) the available renewable generation is exhausted (i.e.,
-\ell_{0}=\ell_{1}+\delta+F_{02}+F_{12}
).
This observation allows the partition of the Problem
2
solutions into two regimes: (1)
\delta\leq\tau
with zero marginal costs at bus
1
and (2)
\delta>\tau
with marginal costs and emissions at bus
1
equal to
\eta_{1}^{j}
for objective
j
.

Theorem 1
.

Let the three-bus system with initial load allocation
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})
satisfy the above conditions. Then, the data center cost,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{DC}}(\delta)=\begin{cases}\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{DC}}L-\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{DC}}\delta,&\delta\leq\tau\\

while the social welfare cost,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{SW}}(\delta)=\begin{cases}\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{SW}}\ell_{2}-\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{SW}}\delta,&\delta\leq\tau\\
\eta_{1}^{\mathrm{SW}}\ell_{1}+\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{SW}}\ell_{2}+(\eta_{1}^{\mathrm{SW}}-\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{SW}})\delta,&\delta>\tau.\end{cases}

Theorem

The piecewise structure reveals that while small shifts improve both private and system-level objectives by increasing renewable utilization, larger shifts may reverse these benefits. This analytical structure allows us to precisely identify when data center incentives align with or diverge from socially optimal outcomes.

3.2
Optimal Load Shifting Decisions

Proposition
1
builds on this structure to characterize the optimal load shift decisions under both decentralized and system-optimal objectives. By comparing weighted marginal costs and emissions
\eta_{1}^{j}/\eta_{2}^{j}
, we identify conditions under which the data center chooses to shift its entire load or only up to the congestion/renewable threshold
\tau
. These conditions formalize how system parameters—such as generator characteristics and transmission capacity—shape the incentives for carbon-aware load shifting.

Proposition 1
.

The optimal magnitude of load to be shifted from bus
2
to bus
1
to minimize the data center objective
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{DC}}
is given by

\displaystyle\delta^{*}_{\mathrm{DC}}=\begin{cases}\tau,&\tau\geq L-(\eta_{1}^{\mathrm{DC}}/\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{DC}})L\\
L,&\tau<L-(\eta_{1}^{\mathrm{DC}}/\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{DC}})L\end{cases}

while the optimal magnitude of load to be shifted to minimize the social welfare objective
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{SW}}
is given by

\displaystyle\delta^{*}_{\mathrm{SW}}=\begin{cases}\tau,&\tau\geq L-(\eta_{1}^{\mathrm{SW}}/\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{SW}})(L+\ell_{1})\\
L,&\tau<L-(\eta_{1}^{\mathrm{SW}}/\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{SW}})(L+\ell_{1}).\end{cases}

Proposition
1
shows that shifting
\tau
units of load is optimal for the data centers if either (a) the marginal cost ratio
\eta_{1}^{\mathrm{SW}}/\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{SW}}
) is sufficiently high or (b)
\tau
is high enough that a large quantity of load can be met at bus 1 with zero marginal cost/emissions. Under the social welfare objective, Proposition
1
shows a similar dependence on the ratio of marginal costs,
\eta_{1}^{\mathrm{SW}}/\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{SW}}
. However, the threshold at which shifting
\tau
units of load becomes suboptimal is lower by
(\eta_{1}^{\mathrm{SW}}/\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{SW}})\ell_{1}
. This is intuitive as a cost of
\eta_{1}^{\mathrm{SW}}\ell_{1}
is incurred by the system at the moment that
\tau
units of load have been shifted.

3.3
Externalities and Marginal Signal Misalignment

Finally, we are able to formalize the conditions under which the data center and social welfare objectives are
aligned

\delta^{*}_{\mathrm{DC}}=\delta^{*}_{\mathrm{SW}}

Proposition 2
.

The data center and social welfare objectives are aligned if and only if one of the following cases hold:

1.

\tau\geq L-(\eta_{1}^{\mathrm{DC}}/\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{DC}})L

2.

\tau<L-(\eta_{1}^{\mathrm{SW}}/\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{SW}})(L+\ell_{1})

Conversely, the data center and social welfare objectives are misaligned if and only if the following holds:

\displaystyle L-(\eta_{1}^{\mathrm{SW}}/\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{SW}})(L+\ell_{1})\leq\tau<L-(\eta_{1}^{\mathrm{DC}}/\eta_{2}^{\mathrm{DC}})L.

5
Conclusion

In this paper we present a stylized analytical framework for understanding how carbon-aware data centers interact with power system operations through marginal price and emissions signals. By deriving closed-form expressions for both private and social objectives, we identify when decentralized behavior aligns with or diverges from system-optimal outcomes. Our results highlight the conditions under which carbon-aware load shifting creates externalities and emphasize the role of network constraints in shaping incentives. While simplified, the model captures key dynamics – congestion, renewable curtailment, and marginal signals – that influence the behavior of flexible loads.

Future work could extend this framework along several dimensions: incorporating inter-temporal constraints; analyzing equilibrium behavior among multiple flexible agents; studying the robustness of carbon signals under uncertainty; and generalizing to meshed network topologies. These directions would enable a deeper theoretical and practical understanding of decentralized control and incentive design in power systems with large-scale flexible loads and evolving carbon objectives.
