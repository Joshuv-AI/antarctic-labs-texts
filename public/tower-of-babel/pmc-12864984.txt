Effects of environmental factors on arsenic accumulation in rice under field conditions using a Bayesian state space model
Authors: Kochi Ishito, Ikuko Akahane, Shigeki Kishi, Koji Baba, Noriko Yamaguchi, Keisuke Ono, Ken Nakamura, Satoru Ishikawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRice (Oryza sativa L.) is a major dietary source of arsenic (As) for humans. Understanding the mechanisms of As accumulation in rice is essential for mitigating human exposure. However, the effects of environmental factors on As accumulation in rice have been insufficiently quantified under field conditions. To address these issues, we modeled temporal dynamics of As accumulation in rice plants and grains using a Bayesian state–space model (SSM). In this SSM, As concentrations in flag leaves and rice grains were treated as response variables, whereas four environmental factors, i.e., number of flooding days, temperature, crop evapotranspiration and precipitation, served as explanatory variables. The nonlinear effects of physiological factors were also incorporated into the SSM. The results indicated that among the four environmental factors, flooding days exerted the greatest positive effect on As accumulation in rice plants, with the effect peaking 5–10 days after heading. High temperatures and increased crop evapotranspiration promoted As accumulation, whereas increased precipitation reduced As accumulation. This work is among the first studies to quantify the effects of environmental factors on As accumulation in rice under field conditions, and the findings contribute to the development of region-specific cultivation guidelines for mitigating As exposure through rice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33897-5.Keywords: Arsenic, Rice, Time series analysis, State–space model, Bayesian statisticsSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Arsenic (As) poses a threat to public health because it is highly toxic to living organisms and is widespread in soil and water1–3. The International Agency for Research on Cancer has classified As as a human carcinogen4. Although all crops can absorb As from soil to varying degrees, compared with most crops, paddy rice accumulates significantly higher levels of As2,5. Consequently, rice is a major dietary source of As for humans. Mitigating human exposure to As from rice requires a clear understanding of how As accumulates from soil into rice grains.

Water management in paddy fields is a key environmental factor that influences As accumulation in rice6. Flooding paddy fields induce anaerobic soil conditions. These conditions lead to the reductive dissolution of iron (hydr)oxides, releasing adsorbed As into porewater, mainly as arsenite7. Moreover, the chemical properties of arsenite are similar to those of silicate8. Rice plants, silicon hyperaccumulators, readily absorb arsenite into root cells through silicon influx transporters (OsLsi1) and subsequently translocate it to aboveground tissues through silicon efflux transporters (OsLsi2)8. In contrast, draining paddy fields induce aerobic soil conditions that promote the coprecipitation of As with divalent iron during oxidation. This coprecipitation reduces the As concentration in porewater, leading to a reduction in As accumulation in rice7,9.

The air temperature is another key factor that influences As accumulation in rice10,11. The air and soil temperatures near the ground surface reach equilibrium through heat exchange. Consequently, the increase in air temperature leads to an increase in local soil temperature, which enhances soil microbial activity. This enhanced microbial activity promotes the reductive dissolution of iron oxides, which in turn increases the release of As into porewater12,13. As a result, this process increases As uptake by rice plants.

The effects of water management and temperature on As accumulation in rice have been studied qualitatively but have rarely been quantified. In contrast, the effects of other factors, such as evapotranspiration and precipitation, remain largely unexplored. Previous research has demonstrated that transpiration influences As uptake in As hyperaccumulators (e.g., Pteris vittata L.)14. In addition, a recent study reported that precipitation reduced As accumulation in rice15. Therefore, this study aimed to quantify the effects of four environmental factors, namely, flooding days, temperature, crop evapotranspiration and precipitation on As accumulation in rice.

However, environmental factors cannot fully explain the temporal and interannual variations in As accumulation in rice, suggesting that physiological factors also influence As accumulation in rice. Indeed, the physiological mechanism of rice plants involves the mitigation of As accumulation in aboveground tissues by sequestering it in the vacuoles of nodes where vascular bundles intersect16. The extent of As sequestration increases with As uptake, resulting in a nonlinear (exponential) decrease in As accumulation in rice as uptake increases5,16,17.

Therefore, accounting for the nonlinear effects of this physiological factor is essential for accurately revealing the effects of environmental factors. Nevertheless, previous studies did not account for these nonlinear effects because they relied on linear models18,19. In addition, they relied on experiments under controlled conditions, which may not reflect the complexity and dynamics of actual field conditions. As a result, the effects of environmental factors on As accumulation in rice may have been misestimated in these studies.

One solution to these issues is to apply Bayesian state–space models (SSMs). SSMs can model both linear and nonlinear dynamic systems and quantify the effects of multiple factors while considering their temporal variations20. SSMs comprise the following two types of models: (1) a system model, which describes the temporal changes in the system’s latent states with process noise; and (2) an observation model, which describes the observation of the data from the latent states with observation noise20.

In our SSM, we assumed five sequential latent states representing As accumulation in aboveground rice tissues from heading to harvest (Fig. 1). The transitions between these states were assumed to be influenced by four environmental factors as well as physiological factors. Four environmental factors were selected because they are expected to play a critical role in As accumulation; however, their contributions have not been quantified in previous studies. In addition, these data can be readily obtained from existing meteorological databases and models. This selection also ensures a parsimonious model structure that balances complexity and interpretability. We assumed that the mean temperature during the 20 days after heading, when As uptake is most active21, influences the As accumulation in rice grains. The nonlinear effects of physiological factors on As accumulation in both aboveground rice tissues and grains were incorporated using exponential decay functions, depending on the preceding and final states of As accumulation, respectively (Eqs. 3 and 5).

Schematic of the model structure. States of As accumulation: states of As accumulation in the aboveground tissues of rice plants at each sampling time; As in flag leaves: As concentration in flag leaves at each sampling time; and As in rice grains: As concentration in rice grains.

We considered the As concentration in flag leaves as the index of As accumulation in aboveground rice tissues because it reflects whole-plant As levels21. Flag leaves were collected from rice plants grown at 13 sites with various water management practices from 2014 to 2020. Sampling was conducted at 0, 5, 10 and 21 days after heading.

To quantify how the four environmental factors influence As accumulation in rice under field conditions, we estimated the SSM parameters using the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method. These results are among the first to quantify the effects of the four environmental factors on As accumulation in rice under field conditions and contribute to the development of cultivation guidelines for mitigating As exposure from rice while considering regional characteristics and climate change.

Temperature

The median coefficient value of the mean temperature remained positive after heading and tended to increase with increasing days after heading (Figs. 1 and 4). The 95% CI of the coefficient values did not include 0 in any period, indicating a persistent positive effect. High temperature promoted As accumulation throughout the study period, with the effect intensifying over time.

Crop evapotranspiration

In Period 1, the median coefficient value of crop evapotranspiration was 0.197 (95% CI = 0.0622–0.333), which was higher than that in the subsequent periods and decreased with increasing duration after heading (Figs. 1 and 4). In Periods 3–4, the median coefficient values were near zero (Period 3: -0.00752; Period 4: 0.00194). The 95% CI of the coefficient values included zero in Periods 2–4 (Period 2, 95% CI = -0.0150–0.254; Period 3, 95% CI = -0.0165–0.152; Period 4, 95% CI = -0.0740–0.0790) (Figs. 1 and 4), suggesting that crop evapotranspiration promoted As accumulation primarily in Period 1 and Period 2, with a negligible effect in later periods.

Precipitation

The median coefficient values of precipitation were negative throughout the periods and tended to increase with increasing duration after heading (Figs. 1 and 4). The 95% CI of the coefficient values included 0 in Period 2 (95% CI = -0.238–0.0162). These results suggest that precipitation generally reduced As accumulation in rice plants and that its negative effect decreased with increasing duration after heading. However, the inclusion of zero in Period 2 indicates directional uncertainty rather than a consistent negative effect.

Physiological factors

The median values of the effects of physiological factors on As accumulation in rice plants calculated from the sampled values of the parameters showed an exponential decay (Fig. 5), with an inflection point at the previous As accumulation states between ~ 0 and 5 mg kg− 1. These findings indicate that the amount of As accumulation decreased as the amount of As accumulation at the previous time point increased.

Discussion

In this study, the effects of four environmental factors—flooding days, temperature, crop evapotranspiration, precipitation—on As accumulation in rice under field conditions were quantified, and the nonlinear effects of these physiological factors were considered. The three major findings are as follows:

Among the four environmental factors, flooding days exerted the greatest positive effect on As accumulation in rice plants, with the effect peaking 5–10 days after heading;

Among the four environmental factors, flooding days exerted the greatest positive effect on As accumulation in rice plants, with the effect peaking 5–10 days after heading;

High temperatures and enhanced crop evapotranspiration levels promoted As accumulation in rice plants, whereas increased precipitation reduced it;

As accumulation in rice was associated with a physiological restriction on As accumulation in aboveground tissues, including grains.

Increased days of paddy flooding promoted As accumulation in rice plants. The temporal pattern of this effect is consistent with the relative expression levels of OsLsi1 and OsLsi2 reported in the previous studies23,24. Mechanistically, flooding elevates As concentration in porewater by inducing anaerobic, reductive soil conditions. Therefore, the effect of flooding can be explained by increased As accumulation through the Lsi transporters resulting from elevated As concentration in porewater. In contrast, temporal drainage lowers As concentration in porewater by inducing aerobic, oxidative soil conditions, leading to reductions in As accumulation (Figs. 2 and 3). Our model successfully captured these soil-plant processes governing As dynamics under varying paddy field conditions.

High temperature promoted As accumulation in the rice plants. Its effect tended to intensify as days progressed after heading (Fig. 4). These findings support the hypothesis that elevated temperature promotes As dissolution by accelerating microbial activity. Consistent with this, a previous study reported that high temperature increased the As concentration in porewater more strongly during the ripening stage than during the vegetative stage, attributing this to greater microbial abundance driven by increased amounts of rice root-exuded carbon19.

Although high temperature promoted As accumulation in the rice plants, it reduced As accumulation in the grains during the 20 days after heading. Previous studies, however, reported that high temperature during this period decreased grain weight, thereby increasing the As accumulation in grains through concentration effects18,25. Under the experimental conditions of this study (Table S4, Fig. S3), a significant negative relationship was observed between the mean temperature during the 20 days after heading and thousand-grain weight (Spearman’s rho = -0.540, p = 4.76 × 10− 12, n = 141). This suggests that an increase in grain As concentration due to concentration effects might have occurred. However, no significant correlation was found between grain As concentration and thousand-grain weight (Spearman’s rho = -0.124, p = 0.143, n = 141). Taken together, these observations imply that while higher mean temperature during this period has enhanced As uptake, excessive retention of As in the nodes could have limited its translocation to the grains, thereby offsetting any concentration effect and resulting in the observed negative relationship between temperature and grain As concentration. To elucidate these mechanisms, further studies should examine the expression and functional impacts of sequestration-related genes17,26 (e.g., OsABCC1 and OsPCS1) under various temperature conditions and monitor As concentrations in rice grains and grain weights during the grain-filling period.

Enhanced crop evapotranspiration promoted As accumulation in the rice plants (Fig. 4). Evapotranspiration peaks around heading and decreases thereafter27,28. This decreasing effect indicates that As uptake is accompanied by water uptake driven by plant transpiration (Fig. 4)14. However, we used a constant crop coefficient (Kc) to estimate crop evapotranspiration due to limitations in data for actual changes in Kc, although Kc could vary over time after heading. Consequently, the estimated crop evapotranspiration in this study may not fully capture the dynamic plant physiological conditions. However, in this study, we still observed a strong association between crop evapotranspiration and As uptake by rice. Incorporating a dynamic Kc in future analyses is expected to refine this relationship and provide a more accurate representation of how transpiration influences As accumulation.

Increased precipitation reduced As accumulation in aboveground rice tissues. This temporal effect was opposite to the combined pattern of crop evapotranspiration and flooding days (Fig. 4). This may be explained by precipitation suppressing plant transpiration through leaf wetting from rain or dew29,30. Another mechanism could involve the induction of a Fenton-like reaction by rainwater-borne hydrogen peroxide in paddy soil, thereby lowering the porewater As concentration and reducing plant As uptake15.

In addition to the effects of the four environmental factors, this study also quantified the nonlinear effect of a physiological factor on As accumulation in rice plants depending on the preceding states of As accumulation (Fig. 5). This nonlinear effect may resemble a dilution effect, which refers to a decrease in nutrient concentration as plant biomass increases31. However, dry weights of flag leaves remain stable after heading21, indicating that this pattern cannot be explained simply by biomass-driven dilution. Therefore, this nonlinear effect reflects a physiological restriction on As accumulation in aboveground tissues of rice plants. These results are consistent with those of previous studies, which revealed that As accumulation in aboveground tissues is restricted in rice plants by sequestering As in the vacuoles of nodes8.

Arsenic restriction was greater for grain accumulation than for other aboveground tissues. Compared with total aboveground accumulation, the physiological effect on grain As accumulation exhibited a more pronounced nonlinear pattern (Figs. 5 and 6). The inflection point of the exponential decay curve (Fig. 6) occurred at approximately 2–5 mg kg− 1 As in the plants at harvest. This finding is consistent with that of Williams et al. (2007), who reported that modeled shoot-to-grain As is transformed as an exponential decay with an inflection point in the same range5. Although we used As concentrations in flag leaves in the SSM, the resulting decay curve closely matched that for shoot As levels, including grain As, ensuring the use of As concentration in flag leaves as a reliable proxy for As accumulation states in the aboveground tissues of rice plants.

In this study, the effects of four environmental factors that may control As accumulation in rice were quantified. However, interactions among environmental factors were not considered because our objective was to quantify the individual effects of each environmental factor on As accumulation in rice. The dynamics of As in soil and its uptake by rice are largely driven by interactions among environmental factors rather than by single factors acting independently. For example, high temperature combined with prolonged flooding can accelerate soil microbial activity and enhance reductive conditions, thereby increasing As solubility in porewater. The interaction between temperature and evapotranspiration exerts a synergistic physiological processes governing As transport within the rice plant. Understanding these combined effects is essential for accurately predicting As accumulation under field conditions, and future studies should incorporate multifactorial approaches to capture these complex interactions.

In addition, the model could not include other important factors, such as soil physicochemical properties, which play a critical role in As dynamics because of the need for model parsimony and limitations in data availability. For example, redox potential (Eh) and soil temperature directly influence As dissolution into porewater13,32. The incorporation of such factors in future studies will be necessary for obtaining a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying As accumulation in rice.

Conclusion

In this study, the effects of four environmental factors on As accumulation in rice under field conditions were quantified. The results demonstrated the following:

Flooding days exerted the strongest positive influence on As accumulation in rice plants among the environmental factors examined, with the effect peaking 5–10 days after heading;

Flooding days exerted the strongest positive influence on As accumulation in rice plants among the environmental factors examined, with the effect peaking 5–10 days after heading;

Elevated temperatures and crop evapotranspiration levels promoted As accumulation, whereas increased precipitation reduced it;

Elevated temperatures and crop evapotranspiration levels promoted As accumulation, whereas increased precipitation reduced it;

As accumulation in rice was associated with a physiological restriction on its accumulation in aboveground tissues, including grains.

As accumulation in rice was associated with a physiological restriction on its accumulation in aboveground tissues, including grains.

These findings have important implications for the development of region-specific water and crop management strategies aimed at mitigating dietary As exposure through rice consumption. Furthermore, the modeling framework presented here can be applied to predict As concentrations in rice grains, enabling prediction-based risk assessment and management.

Materials and methods

Paddy rice cultivation and sampling

Rice plants (Oryza sativa L.) were cultivated in experimental paddy fields of agricultural research institutes in 13 regions in Japan from 2014 to 2020, as listed in Table S3, with soil types based on the World Reference Base for Soil Resources (2014) (IUSS Working Group WRB, 2015)33 and 1 mol/L HCl-extractable As concentrations in the soils. Approximately one month after the rice seedlings were transplanted to the flooded paddy fields, the surface water was drained, and the soil was allowed to dry until cracks appeared on the soil surface (mid-summer drainage). The duration of mid-summer drainage varied depending on the weather conditions during the cultivated year. Water management from the heading date to 3 weeks before and after heading included continuous flooding (F) and continuous flooding with temporal drainage (F + D). The timing and duration of temporal drainage varied depending on the weather conditions of the sampling regions. Flag leaves were sampled on the heading date (the day when the ears for 50% of the effective stems had appeared) and 5, 10, and 21 days after the heading stage, except for the fields where flag leaf samples from 5 to 10 days after heading were not collected (Fig. S4). All flag leaves of the 2 selected rice hills were sampled from 2014 to 2016, while the flag leaves of one of the longest effective stems in 15 stubble areas of the rice plants were sampled from 2017 to 2020. Rice grain samples were collected at harvest.

Sample preparation and analysis

Flag leaf samples were dried at 70 °C, cut to 0.5 cm in length and finitely ground using a shaking mill (Shake Master; Bio Medical Science Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Samples (0.2–0.5 g) were decomposed in a mixture of 60–62% nitric acid and 30–35% hydrogen peroxide using a microwave digestion system (ETHOS EASY, Milestone, Italia). The arsenic concentration in the acid-digested solution was determined by inductively coupled plasma‒mass spectrometry (ICP‒MS; Elan DRC-e and NexION300xx, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). The results were averaged and corrected to dry weight using moisture content.

Rice grain samples were dehulled, and unmatured grains passed through a 1.8–1.9 mm sieve were removed. The rice grain samples were finely ground using a shaking mill (Shake Master; Bio Medical Science Inc., Tokyo, Japan). A 0.5 g sample was digested with 5 mL of 60–70% nitric acid in 50 mL of DigiTUBE (SCP Sciences, QC, Canada) and left overnight. It was then heated at 110 °C for 2 h using either a graphite block digestion system (DigiPREP; SCP Science) or an aluminum block bath (TAITEC, Tokyo, Japan). After cooling, 1 mL of 30% hydrogen peroxide was added, and the sample was reheated at 110 °C for 1 h. The digest was diluted to 50 mL with ultrapure water, mixed, and filtered through a polyethersulfone (PES) membrane syringe filter. Indium was added as an internal standard, and total arsenic was quantified by flow injection (FI)‒ICP‒MS (Flexar and NexION 300xx, PerkinElmer). The accuracy was confirmed using certified reference materials, including NMIJ CRM 7532-a, 7533-a, 7502-a, 7503-a, and 7503-b and NIST SRM 1568b. The results were averaged and corrected to dry weight using moisture content.

Air-dried soil passed through a 2 mm sieve (5 g) was mixed with 25 mL of 1 mol·L− 1 HCl preheated to 30 °C in a 50-mL polypropylene centrifuge tube. The mixture was shaken horizontally at 100 rpm for 30 min in a 30 °C incubator. After centrifugation, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm PES membrane. The filtrate was subsequently diluted and analyzed by FI‒ICP‒MS. The results were averaged and corrected to dry weight using moisture content.

Climate data

Climate data, including daily mean temperature and daily precipitation, were obtained from the AMGSDS (Agro-Meteorological Grid Square Data System, NARO)34,35.

The crop evapotranspiration of the rice plants was estimated using the FAO56 package in R36. Data on daily mean wind speed; daily global solar radiation; daily downward longwave radiation; daily mean, maximum and minimum temperature; and elevation were used to estimate crop evapotranspiration and were obtained from the AMGSDS. The crop coefficient for the estimation of crop evapotranspiration was set to 1.05. Multicollinearity is expected among environmental factors. Therefore, we checked it by computing Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients between environmental factors for each period as shown in Fig. S5.

Paddy water status data

Paddy water status was visually confirmed every day. We defined the day when paddy water was visible as the day when the paddy fields were flooded.

The other describes the As accumulation in rice grains, depending on the states of As accumulation at harvest. Bayesian inference

Bayesian statistical methods aim to estimate probability distributions after obtaining data (posterior distributions) by updating the probability distribution set before obtaining data (prior distributions) with the obtained data on the basis of the Bayesian interpretation of probability and Bayes’ theorem.

To estimate the SSM parameters with Bayesian statistical methods, obtaining analytical posterior distributions of the parameters is difficult because of their abundance and complexity. Therefore, drawing samples from the probability distribution with the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method is common for estimating the posterior distributions of the parameters.

In this study, we fitted the model using 4 chains running in parallel with a total of 400,000 MCMC samples. The first 80,000 samples were discarded as a burn-in, and the remaining 320,000 samples were retained. To ensure the reproducibility of the MCMC method, the random seed was set to 10,000.

We assessed the convergence of the MCMC using the Gelman–Rubin statistic (R-hat) and determined that the MCMC converged when the R-hat values were less than 1.122. Model adequacy was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2) computed between the observed values and the posterior predictive medians of As concentrations in flag leaves and rice grains. R2 was calculated for each site or time point as shown in Fig. S1 and Fig. S2.

Analysis environment

We used R 4.4.3, Stan, RStudio, Rtools 4.4, CmdStan 2.36.0 and the cmdstanr package in R to construct the SSM and fit the model to the dataset37–42. In addition, we used the bayesplot package in R to determine the convergence of the MCMC43.
