Expanding the ethnic and geographic spectrum of hereditary pancreatitis: first report of a Tibetan case with compound SPINK1 variants
Authors: Yonghao Chen, Zhen Yu, Jianing Li, Yangjin Ciren, Qiang Wang, Dong Wu
Published: 2025

A 17-year-old girl from Shannan, Tibet, a region located at an altitude of approximately 3,600 meters, presented with a two-month history of intermittent abdominal pain predominantly in the epigastric and right lower quadrant regions. The pain was exacerbated by food intake and was occasionally accompanied by episodes of bile-stained vomiting. There was no history of alcohol use, abdominal trauma, or changes in bowel habits. Her medical history was notable for Graves’ disease, for which she had been receiving methimazole and propranolol.

Physical examination revealed the patient appeared undernourished, with a body mass index of 12.28 kg/m2, indicative of severe malnutrition and tenderness in the epigastric and right lower abdominal quadrants, as well as left eye strabismus. Laboratory evaluation revealed microcytic, hypochromic anemia with a hemoglobin level of 98 g/L (reference range, 115–150 g/L), mean corpuscular volume (MCV) of 62.8 fL (82–100 fL), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) of 17.9 pg (27–34 pg), and MCH concentration (MCHC) of 284 g/L (316–354 g/L), consistent with iron-deficiency anemia. Serum immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4) concentration was 0.26 g/L (reference range, 0.03–2.01 g/L), a result not consistent with autoimmune pancreatitis.

Abdominal computed tomography demonstrated pancreatic atrophy with main ductal dilation and a cystic lesion in the pancreatic body and tail containing hyperdense components suggestive of hemorrhagic pseudocyst. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) revealed irregular narrowing of the main pancreatic duct in the head and neck regions, with segmental dilation up to 0.5 cm in the body and multiple intraductal calculi, findings consistent with chronic pancreatitis.

Genetic analysis identified compound heterozygous variants in the SPINK1 gene (NM_001379610.1). One was a splice-site variant (c.194+2T>C), a partial loss-of-function variant, predicted to interfere with normal messenger RNA (mRNA) splicing and potentially result in aberrant transcript formation. The second variant, c.101A>G (p.Asn34Ser), is a missense variant that has been previously associated with increased risk for chronic pancreatitis, and is in linkage disequilibrium with c.-4141G>T, a causative enhancer variant (1). Segregation analysis revealed that the c.194+2T>C variant was inherited from the patient’s mother, and the p.Asn34Ser variant from the father, establishing a compound heterozygous genotype. No pathogenic variants were detected in PRSS1, CFTR, or CTRC. Neither parent had shown any clinical signs or symptoms of pancreatitis to date. The family pedigree was presented in Figure 1.

Based on clinical presentation, imaging, endoscopic findings, and genetic confirmation, a diagnosis of hereditary pancreatitis associated with compound SPINK1 variants was established. The patient subsequently underwent endoscopic sphincterotomy (EST) followed by endoscopic retrograde pancreatic duct drainage (ERPD), during which a 5 F × 9 cm plastic stent was placed to facilitate pancreatic juice outflow and relieve ductal obstruction. In addition to endoscopic intervention, the patient received comprehensive supportive treatment, including intravenous fluids, acid suppression, analgesia, and empiric antibiotics. Given evidence of exocrine insufficiency and malnutrition, pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) and dietary counseling were initiated. Nutritional supplementation with vitamins and minerals was also provided to address micronutrient deficiencies.

SPINK1 encodes a protease inhibitor that protects acinar cells from premature trypsin activation. Epidemiologic data have demonstrated considerable geographic and ethnic variability in the distribution of pancreatitis-related gene variants. In Western populations, particularly among individuals of European ancestry, pathogenic variants in PRSS1 and CFTR are detected more frequently than SPINK1 variants in patients with hereditary or idiopathic chronic pancreatitis (2-5). In contrast, studies from East Asia suggest a divergent pattern: in Japanese cohorts, SPINK1 variants are the second most frequently observed variants following PRSS1 (6), whereas in Chinese populations, SPINK1 has emerged as the predominant genetic contributor to nonalcoholic chronic pancreatitis (7).

Compound heterozygosity involving SPINK1 variants remains uncommon globally. A nationwide registry-based study in Japan reported similar compound SPINK1 variants in approximately 1.7% of patients with chronic pancreatitis (6). In a large Han Chinese cohort comprising 1,061 individuals with chronic pancreatitis, the prevalence of comparable compound heterozygous variants was 1.26% (7). Importantly, such variants were absent in healthy controls, lending support to their potential pathogenicity. To our knowledge, this is the first documented case of compound heterozygous SPINK1 variants in a Tibetan individual residing at high altitude, representing a previously unreported genotype-phenotype association in this underrepresented group and expanding the geographic and ethnic understanding of this disease. The compound heterozygous variants—involving a splice-site variant and a pathogenic missense variant—likely contributed to early disease onset, chronicity, and pseudocyst complications in this adolescent. The role of altitude-related hypoxia and its possible interaction with inflammatory or oxidative stress pathways in pancreatic disease progression remains speculative but warrants further study.

An intriguing aspect of this case is the patient’s mother remaining asymptomatic despite carrying the predisposing SPINK1 splice-site variant (c.194+2T>C), suggesting a potential genotype-phenotype discrepancy (8). This observation raises the possibility of incomplete penetrance and indicates that ethnic- or region-specific protective factors might modulate disease expression. Notably, the patient’s mother is a lifelong resident of the high-altitude Tibetan plateau, adhering to a traditional diet dominated by butter tea, yak dairy products, and highland barley. In contrast, the patient had been living in a boarding school with a more modernized, Western-style diet, characterized by higher intake of refined carbohydrates and processed foods, with markedly reduced exposure to the unique nutrient composition and microbiome-modulating effects of traditional Tibetan foods. Given the unique dietary patterns observed in the Tibetan population—characterized by high intake of butter tea, yak dairy products, and highland barley—the potential role of nutritional or microbiome-related modifiers warrants further exploration. Such hypotheses may contribute to a more nuanced understanding of gene-environment interactions in pancreatitis susceptibility.

In a word, this case underscores the need to extend genetic screening and pancreatitis research to ethnically underrepresented and geographically remote populations, where improved access to testing and individualized care may enable more equitable and precise diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Moreover, the potential modulatory effects of high-altitude adaptation and traditional Tibetan dietary patterns on pancreatic disease progression warrant further investigation as possible gene-environment interactions.
