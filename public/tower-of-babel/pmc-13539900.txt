Association of Neutrophil Percentage‐to‐Albumin Ratio With Mortality in ICU Patients With Acute Pancreatitis: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Chunyan Wang, Chun Yang, Yingying Zhang, Junliang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo investigate whether the neutrophil percentage‐to‐albumin ratio (NPAR) predicts 28‐ and 365‐day mortality in ICU patients with acute pancreatitis (AP) and improves the performance of existing ICU scoring systems.MethodsWe extracted data on 437 ICU AP patients from the MIMIC‐IV 3.0 database. Selection bias was assessed by comparing included and excluded patients. The optimal NPAR cutoff was determined by maximally selected rank statistics. Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression assessed the NPAR–mortality association, with 200‐iteration bootstrap resampling for internal validation. Time‐dependent AUC, continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) evaluated whether adding NPAR to SAPS II, SOFA, and SIRS improved prediction.ResultsThe optimal NPAR cutoff was 34.6. Included patients had more severe disease than excluded patients (SOFA 6.75 vs. 5.32; 28‐day mortality 17.4% vs. 11.8%). High NPAR (≥ 34.6) was significantly associated with lower survival (log‐rank p < 0.001) and independently predicted 28‐day mortality (HR = 2.043, 95% CI 1.235–3.380, p = 0.005) and 365‐day mortality (HR = 1.749, 95% CI 1.174–2.606, p = 0.006). After bootstrap validation, optimism‐corrected HRs were 1.820 and 1.560, with corrected AUCs of 0.765 and 0.695, respectively. Adding NPAR to SIRS significantly improved AUC (28‐day: 0.613 vs. 0.529, p = 0.011; 365‐day: 0.580 vs. 0.507, p = 0.004), NRI, and IDI (all p < 0.05), whereas adding NPAR to SAPS II or SOFA yielded no significant improvement (all p > 0.05).ConclusionNPAR ≥ 34.6 is an independent predictor of 28‐ and 365‐day mortality in ICU AP patients, with robust internal validation. Combining NPAR with SIRS significantly enhances mortality prediction.Keywords: acute pancreatitis, ICU, MIMIC‐IV, neutrophil percentage‐to‐albumin ratio, prognosis

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP), a frequent reason for hospitalization among digestive disorders, is linked to considerable morbidity and mortality, particularly in ICU‐admitted patients [1, 2]. The morbidity and mortality rates of severe AP in patients requiring ICU treatment are high, approximately 20%–40% [3]. Early detection of AP patients at high risk is crucial to avoid complications like pancreatic necrosis infection, organ failure, and mortality [4]. However, reliable markers and methods for accurately predicting the prognosis of AP are still lacking.

Systemic inflammatory markers, including the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio and C‐reactive protein‐to‐albumin ratio, are independently linked with a poor prognosis in patients with AP. Neutrophils and albumin are closely associated with inflammatory responses [5, 6]. Recently, the neutrophil percentage‐to‐albumin ratio (NPAR) has emerged as a simple and easily accessible marker, demonstrating its potential as an independent predictive index of adverse outcomes in patients with heart failure, sepsis, and renal insufficiency [7, 8, 9].

To date, the association between NPAR and poor prognosis in ICU patients with AP is still uncertain. The main objective of this study was to examine the relationship between NPAR and 28‐ and 365‐day mortality in ICU patients with AP. Additionally, it remains unclear whether NPAR can enhance the predictive power of common ICU scoring systems, such as the SAPS II, SOFA, and SIRS scores, for mortality outcomes in patients with AP.

Methods

Data Source

This study utilized data from MIMIC‐IV 3.0, a database with clinical records of 364 627 patients hospitalized at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) (2008–2022), including 94 458 ICU admissions [10]. The BIDMC's Institutional Review Board approved the sharing of research resources and waived the requirement for informed consent. Yingying Zhang, identified by certificate number 64373576, was granted access to the database. This study's findings are presented following the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational research.

Study Population

This study involved ICU patients diagnosed with AP. Patients hospitalized with AP were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) code 577.0 and the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) codes K85–K85.92.

A total of 7510 AP patients were initially identified from the database, including patients admitted to both general wards and the ICU. After excluding 6230 patients who were not admitted to the ICU or had non‐first ICU admissions (the vast majority of whom were general ward patients rather than ICU patients), 1280 patients with first‐time ICU admission for AP remained. We further excluded: (1) 205 patients with ICU stay ≤ 24 h; (2) 0 patients aged < 18 years; (3) 69 patients with comorbid end‐stage liver disease; (4) 34 patients with end‐stage renal disease; and (5) 535 patients with missing neutrophil percentage or albumin data. Ultimately, 437 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

The diagram shows the process of identifying, screening, and including patients with acute pancreatitis from the MIMIC‐IV 3.0 database, including the exact number of patients excluded at each step and the reasons for exclusion, as well as the final grouping by the NPAR cutoff of 34.60.

Exposure and Outcome

The study's exposure variable was the initial NPAR measurement taken within 24 h of ICU admission, calculated as: [(neutrophil percentage/albumin) × 100(g/dL)]. The median interval from ICU admission to NPAR measurement was 8.2 h (IQR 4.5–14.7 h). The main outcome was 28‐day mortality, with 365‐day mortality as a secondary outcome.

Data Extraction

Data were retrieved using structured query language (SQL), accessible via the GitHub repository (https://github.com/MIT‐LCP/mimic‐iv). The collected data encompassed demographics (age, sex, and BMI), vital signs (mean arterial pressure, heart rate, and respiratory rate), comorbidities (significant cardiopulmonary diseases, chronic pancreatitis, hyperlipidemia, gallbladder stones, and the Charlson comorbidity index), blood tests (white blood cell count, platelet count, neutrophil percentage, hemoglobin level, urea nitrogen, creatinine, and albumin), and ICU baseline scores (SAPS II, SOFA, and SIRS scores). The above data were collected within the first 24 h of the patient's ICU admission. During the ICU stay, administration of vasoactive medications, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), and mechanical ventilation were extracted. The duration of hospitalization, the time spent in the ICU, mortality rates during hospitalization, within the ICU, at 28 days, and at 365 days, with follow‐up commencing from the point of ICU admission, were also considered.

Statistical Analysis

In this retrospective study, the sample size was derived from the accessible data in the repository. Using the 28‐day survival time and outcome of the patients, the optimal threshold for the NPAR was computed utilizing the “surv_cutpoint” function in the R package “survminer” with maximally selected rank statistics. Variables exhibiting > 20% missingness were excluded, whereas those below this threshold were imputed via multiple imputation employing a random forest algorithm. Multicollinearity was evaluated via the variance inflation factor, and variables with a variance inflation factor exceeding five were eliminated.

Continuous variables were tested for normality via the Kolmogorov–Smirnov method. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, non‐normally distributed data were denoted by median (interquartile range), or frequency (percentage). Independent samples t‐test, Chi‐square test, Mann–Whitney U test, or Fisher's exact test were used to compare the two groups according to the types of variables. To assess selection bias, baseline characteristics were also compared between included and excluded patients using standardized mean differences (SMD), with SMD > 0.1 indicating a meaningful difference.

To explore the association between NPAR and patient outcomes, the Boruta algorithm was applied to assess the importance of candidate variables in predicting 28‐day death risk [11]. To evaluate survival differences across NPAR groups, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was conducted for survival outcomes. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to estimate the HR and 95% CI for death at 28 and 365 days in the high‐NPAR group. Three models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, and BMI; Model 3 adjusted for age, white blood cells, hemoglobin, platelets, lactate, total bilirubin, blood urea nitrogen, SOFA score, SAPS II score, Charlson comorbidity index, and use of CRRT and vasoactive drugs.

To assess the risk of overfitting associated with deriving and validating the NPAR cutoff in the same dataset, we performed 200‐iteration bootstrap resampling for internal validation. Optimism‐corrected HRs and AUCs were calculated by subtracting the mean optimism (difference between bootstrap and apparent performance) from the original estimates. Time‐dependent AUCs were computed using inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) to compare the predictive performance of SAPS II, SOFA, and SIRS scores with and without NPAR, and AUC differences were compared using a Z‐test based on the influence function. To further evaluate the incremental value of adding NPAR to existing scoring systems, we calculated net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) with 200 bootstrap perturbations. Statistical significance adhered to the p < 0.05 criteria. All analytical procedures were executed utilizing R software (v4.3.1).

Results

Baseline Data

A total of 7510 AP cases were extracted from the MIMIC‐IV 3.0 database, among which 1280 represented first‐time ICU admissions due to AP. After excluding 205 patients with ICU stay ≤ 24 h, 69 with end‐stage liver disease, 34 with end‐stage renal disease, and 535 with missing neutrophil or albumin data, the study included 437 patients (Figure 1), 247 were male, and 190 were female, with a mean age of 57.96 years (SD = 17.29). Based on the patients' 28‐day mortality, the optimal cutoff value for NPAR was determined to be 34.60, dividing the patients into a high‐NPAR group (NPAR ≥ 34.60, n = 92) and a low‐NPAR group (NPAR < 34.60, n = 345). The high‐NPAR group exhibited a higher mean age (61.55 ± 15.61 vs. 57.01 ± 17.6), lower mean arterial pressure after ICU admission (81.01 ± 18.15 vs. 86.24 ± 20.04), lower hemoglobin levels (10.16 ± 2.57 vs. 11.54 ± 2.54), lower blood calcium levels (7.41 ± 1.06 vs. 8.03 ± 1.23), higher white blood cell counts (18.32 ± 9.68 vs. 14.04 ± 7.63), and a greater proportion of patients receiving vasoactive drugs during hospitalization (46.7% vs. 34.5%). Upon comparing the outcomes between the two groups, the high‐NPAR group exhibited significantly higher proportions of both 28‐day (29.3% vs. 14.2%, p = 0.001) and 365‐day mortality (43.5% vs. 26.7%, p = 0.002). Additionally, the high‐NPAR group had significantly longer hospital stays (17 vs. 13 days, p = 0.011) and a higher incidence of in‐hospital death (28.3% vs. 15.9%, p = 0.007) than the low‐NPAR group (Table 1).

Comparison of Included Versus Excluded Patients

Among the 638 patients who had an ICU stay > 24 h but were subsequently excluded due to end‐stage liver disease, end‐stage renal disease, or missing neutrophil/albumin data, the mean SOFA score was 5.32 ± 3.77, SAPS II was 35.84 ± 15.36, SIRS was 2.85 ± 0.87, and 28‐day mortality was 11.8%, compared with 6.75 ± 4.33, 40.26 ± 17.16, 3.10 ± 0.77, and 17.4% in the included cohort (all SMD > 0.1). These findings indicate that included patients had more severe disease and higher mortality, suggesting that our cohort represents a more severe subset of ICU AP patients (Table S1).

Boruta Feature Selection

Figure 2 presents the results of the Boruta feature selection method. These findings indicate that NPAR is a candidate predictor variable associated with 28‐day death. Moreover, the SOFA score, lactate, total bilirubin, blood urea nitrogen, platelets, age, Charlson comorbidity index, and use of CRRT during the ICU stay were identified as important predictor variables related to 28‐day mortality. In addition to NPAR, the SAPS II score, mean hemoglobin, white blood cells, and the use of vasoactive drugs during ICU stay were candidate predictor variables linked to 28‐day mortality.

Association Between NPAR and 28‐ and 365‐Day Mortality Rates

As illustrated in Figure 3, Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis displayed that the high‐NPAR group exhibited significantly lower 28‐ and 365‐day survival rates compared to the low‐NPAR group (log‐rank p < 0.001).

To investigate the impact of NPAR on mortality, a Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was conducted in ICU patients diagnosed with AP. As shown in Table 2, across all models, the high‐NPAR group consistently demonstrated a significantly elevated risk of 28‐day and 365‐day mortality. In the fully adjusted Model 3, high NPAR was significantly associated with 28‐day mortality [HR (95% CI) 2.043 (1.235, 3.380), p = 0.005] and 365‐day mortality [HR (95% CI) 1.749 (1.174, 2.606), p = 0.006] compared with the low‐NPAR group.

To correct for potential overfitting from deriving and validating the cutoff in the same dataset, we performed 200‐iteration bootstrap resampling. For 28‐day mortality, the original HR was 2.043 and the optimism‐corrected HR was 1.820; the original AUC was 0.820 and the corrected AUC was 0.765. The association between high NPAR and increased mortality (HR > 1) was observed in 99.0% of bootstrap resamples. These results indicate minimal overfitting and robust prognostic value of NPAR (Table 3).

AUC of Common ICU Scores Combined With NPAR

As shown in Figure 4 and Table 4, when predicting 28‐day mortality, the AUC for SAPS II + NPAR [0.632 (0.559–0.705)] was greater than that for SAPS II alone [0.575 (0.496–0.654)]; however, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.075). Likewise, SOFA + NPAR [0.809 (0.758–0.860)] did not significantly outperform SOFA alone [0.793 (0.736–0.849)] (p = 0.147). However, the AUC for SIRS + NPAR [0.613 (0.543–0.683)] was significantly greater than that for SIRS alone [0.529 (0.459–0.599)] (p = 0.011). For 365‐day mortality, similar patterns were observed: SAPS II + NPAR [0.606 (0.547–0.665) vs. 0.565 (0.504–0.625), p = 0.088] and SOFA + NPAR [0.725 (0.673–0.776) vs. 0.710 (0.658–0.763), p = 0.135] showed no significant improvement, whereas SIRS + NPAR [0.580 (0.522–0.638)] was significantly greater than SIRS alone [0.507 (0.450–0.565)] (p = 0.004).

We further evaluated the incremental value of NPAR using NRI and IDI (Table 5). For 28‐day mortality, adding NPAR to SIRS yielded a continuous NRI of 0.324 (95% CI: 0.089–0.542, p = 0.018) and IDI of 0.035 (95% CI: 0.008–0.072, p = 0.022), indicating significant improvement in risk reclassification and discrimination. In contrast, adding NPAR to SAPS II (NRI = 0.182, p = 0.112; IDI = 0.012, p = 0.156) or SOFA (NRI = 0.156, p = 0.178; IDI = 0.008, p = 0.245) did not reach statistical significance. For 365‐day mortality, NPAR also significantly improved reclassification when added to SIRS (NRI = 0.298, p = 0.024; IDI = 0.031, p = 0.031), but not when added to SAPS II or SOFA (all p > 0.05). These findings are consistent with the AUC results and indicate that NPAR provides significant incremental value specifically when combined with SIRS.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that ICU patients with AP and NPAR ≥ 34.60 experienced significantly poorer survival rates, both in the short and long term, relative to patients with NPAR < 34.60. Furthermore, NPAR ≥ 34.60 was identified as an independent predictor for increased mortality at both 28 and 365 days. Although multiple covariates were adjusted, this correlation remained significant. Bootstrap internal validation confirmed the robustness of these findings, with minimal overfitting. Notably, the combination of NPAR with the SIRS score significantly improved the ability to predict 28‐ and 365‐day mortality in ICU patients with AP.

AP begins with localized inflammation in the pancreas, which frequently progresses to systemic inflammation and organ failure, particularly in patients admitted to the ICU. This study observed high mortality rates in these patients, with 28‐ and 365‐day fatality rates of 17.4% and 30.2%, respectively. The exact pathogenesis of AP remains incompletely understood, but likely involves the premature activation of trypsin, disturbance of microcirculation, attraction and activation of leukocytes, and apoptosis and necrosis of pancreatic acinar cells [12, 13]. Inflammatory response is crucial for the development of AP.

Neutrophils, as the first responders to sites of inflammation, play a critical role by releasing pro‐inflammatory cytokines and chemokines [14]. Neutrophils dominate leukocytosis during the early stages of AP [15]. Leukocytosis is one of the criteria for SIRS and an early predictor of AP severity [16]. Therefore, neutrophil infiltration may play a role in the pathophysiology of AP. Decreased serum albumin concentrations are linked to sustained systemic inflammatory responses, malnutrition, surgery, cancer, and kidney disease [17, 18]. Patients with AP often present with reduced albumin levels. Albumin promotes the production of anti‐inflammatory substances, leading to increased consumption during inflammation. In addition, the inflammatory response increases capillary permeability, resulting in albumin loss [19]. A recent meta‐analysis demonstrated that hypoalbuminemia is a significant early marker for persistent organ failure in patients diagnosed with AP [20]. Therefore, NPAR combined with neutrophil and albumin levels may be a potential predictor of prognosis in patients with AP in the ICU.

This study identified that elevated NPAR (≥ 34.60) independently predicted higher 28‐ and 365‐day mortality in AP patients in the ICU. These findings align with previous research demonstrating the effectiveness of NPAR in predicting the prognosis of various conditions, including heart failure, atrial fibrillation, hypertension, hip fracture, and chronic kidney disease [7, 9, 21, 22, 23]. The bootstrap internal validation showed that the optimism‐corrected HR remained substantially elevated (1.820 for 28‐day mortality), and the association was directionally consistent in 99% of resamples, providing strong evidence that the observed association is not a result of overfitting.

Regarding incremental predictive value, adding NPAR to SIRS significantly improved risk reclassification (NRI = 0.324, p = 0.018) and discrimination (IDI = 0.035, p = 0.022) for 28‐day mortality, with consistent results for 365‐day mortality. The SIRS + NPAR model achieved an AUC of 0.613 for 28‐day mortality, significantly higher than SIRS alone (0.529, p = 0.011). The SIRS score, primarily used to evaluate the systemic inflammatory response caused by infectious or noninfectious etiologies, is valuable for assessing the condition and prognosis of critically ill patients. SIRS, SAPS II, and SOFA scores are closely related to the prognosis of patients with AP [24, 25]. However, NPAR combined with SAPS II or SOFA did not significantly improve the predictive ability of these scores. SOFA alone already demonstrated good discrimination (AUC = 0.793 for 28‐day mortality), whereas SAPS II alone had only modest discrimination (AUC = 0.575), and adding NPAR did not significantly improve either (p = 0.147 and p = 0.075, respectively). This may be because the white blood cell count, as one of the criteria of SIRS, has a higher proportional weight in SIRS than in SAPS II or SOFA. Both the percentage of neutrophils in NPAR and the total white blood cell count reflect the progression of inflammation, which runs through the entire pathophysiological process of AP. Consequently, NPAR combined with SIRS may offer greater predictive efficacy.

The comparison between included and excluded patients revealed that the final cohort had higher severity scores and mortality rates than excluded patients. This is expected, as patients excluded due to missing laboratory data may have had less intensive monitoring or milder disease requiring fewer laboratory tests, while those excluded for end‐stage liver or renal disease represent distinct clinical populations with different prognostic profiles. Consequently, our findings may not be directly generalizable to milder AP patients or those in general ward settings, and this selection bias should be considered when interpreting the results.

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective nature necessitates confirmation of the findings through prospective studies. Second, despite efforts to adjust for confounding factors, potential residual confounding remains. Specifically, we were unable to extract AP etiology (biliary, alcoholic, hypertriglyceridemia‐induced, etc.) and organ failure duration (transient vs. persistent), which are key prognostic factors according to the Revised Atlanta Classification; these variables had high missing rates in the database and could not be reliably ascertained. However, our fully adjusted model included SOFA score, which comprehensively reflects the severity of organ failure across six systems. Third, NPAR was measured within 24 h of ICU admission, and patients who died very early before laboratory testing were excluded, which may introduce survivor bias and potentially underestimate the prognostic value of NPAR in the most critically ill patients. Fourth, we were unable to calculate complete AP‐specific prognostic scores such as BISAP, HAPS, or Ranson's criteria, as variables including mental status and pleural effusion were not available in the database; comparisons were limited to ICU‐specific scores (SAPS II, SOFA, and SIRS). Fifth, cause‐specific mortality data were not available, so the association between NPAR and 365‐day mortality should be interpreted with caution—elevated NPAR may reflect baseline frailty or chronic systemic inflammation rather than serving as a specific predictor of pancreatitis‐related long‐term mortality. Finally, this was a single‐database study, and external validation in independent cohorts is needed.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that NPAR ≥ 34.60 is an independent risk factor for increased 28‐day and 365‐day mortality risk in patients with AP admitted to the ICU, with robust results confirmed by bootstrap internal validation. Furthermore, combining NPAR with the SIRS score significantly enhanced the ability of SIRS to predict 28‐ and 365‐day mortality in this patient population.
