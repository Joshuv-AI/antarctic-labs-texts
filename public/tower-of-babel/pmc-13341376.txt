The Effect of Canagliflozin Dose on Renal and Cardiovascular Outcomes in Type 2 Diabetes and High Cardiovascular Risk
Authors: Elias John Elenjickal, Frédéric Baroz, Philippe Boileau, Michael A Tsoukas, Abhinav Sharma, Thomas A Mavrakanas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionPharmacokinetic data suggest a potential dose‐dependent effect of sodium–glucose co‐transporter‐2 (SGLT‐2) inhibitors on surrogate markers of efficacy, but the impact of SGLT‐2 inhibitor dose on renal and cardiovascular outcomes remains uncertain.MethodsIn this post hoc analysis, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of two canagliflozin doses (100 and 300 mg) on hard clinical endpoints using participant‐level data from the CANVAS randomised controlled trial (4330 patients). The primary outcome was a composite of doubling of serum creatinine, renal failure, or death due to renal cause. Secondary outcomes included a composite cardiovascular endpoint; hospitalisation for heart failure; all‐cause mortality; and progression or regression of albuminuria. Safety outcomes included serious hyperkalaemia and acute kidney injury. Cox proportional hazards models were used.ResultsThere was no significant difference in the composite renal endpoint between the two doses: hazard ratio (HR) 0.85; 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.38–1.89 (p = 0.68). Both doses, however, were associated with significant reductions in the incidence of the primary renal endpoint compared with placebo: HR 0.49 (95% CI 0.25–0.95; p = 0.03) for 100 mg and HR 0.41 (95% CI 0.20–0.83; p = 0.01) for 300 mg. There was no difference between the two doses of the drug in any of the secondary endpoints. Importantly, no added safety concerns were identified with either dose.ConclusionsIn this post hoc analysis of the CANVAS trial, we did not observe evidence of a dose‐dependent effect of canagliflozin on renal or cardiovascular outcomes. Both doses reduced the risk of kidney events versus placebo, suggesting clinical benefit at any dose.Keywords: canagliflozin, cardiovascular, dose, heart failure, hyperkalaemia, mortality, renal failure, type 2 diabetes

Introduction

Initially developed for glycaemic control in Type 2 diabetes, sodium–glucose co‐transporter‐2 (SGLT‐2) inhibitors have revolutionised the management of cardiometabolic disease. Beyond their glucose‐lowering effects, these agents have demonstrated robust cardiovascular and renal benefits, prompting a major shift in their therapeutic role. Large, randomised controlled trials have consistently shown that SGLT‐2 inhibitors improve outcomes in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) [1, 2, 3], atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [4, 5, 6, 7], and heart failure [8, 9, 10]. This evolving evidence base has expanded the clinical indications of SGLT‐2 inhibitors well beyond diabetes, establishing them as foundational therapies in cardiorenal protection [11, 12]. While several of these trials allowed dose up‐titration of the SGLT‐2 inhibitor based on clinical judgement [7], none formally evaluated the impact of different doses on hard clinical endpoints, with the notable exception of the EMPA‐REG OUTCOME trial which did not find any difference between the two empagliflozin doses in cardiovascular endpoints [4]. However, pharmacokinetic and mechanistic studies suggest that the effects of SGLT‐2 inhibitors on surrogate outcomes, such as albuminuria, blood pressure (BP), and glycaemic control, may be dose‐dependent [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Therefore, it remains uncertain whether these dose‐dependent effects translate into differences in cardiovascular and renal outcomes.

The CANVAS Program included two separate randomised trials, CANVAS (NCT01032629) and CANVAS‐R (NCT01989754) [22]. In the CANVAS (Canagliflozin Cardiovascular Assessment Study) trial, 4330 participants with type 2 diabetes mellitus and high cardiovascular risk were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to receive canagliflozin 100 mg, canagliflozin 300 mg, or placebo [23]. In CANVAS‐R (Canagliflozin Cardiovascular Assessment Study–Renal), participants were randomly assigned to receive canagliflozin 100 mg or placebo, with the option to up‐titrate to canagliflozin 300 mg (or matching placebo) after 13 weeks based on clinical discretion [24]. The trials were jointly analysed and reported [6]. They enrolled 10 142 participants with type 2 diabetes, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) 7.0%–10.5%, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) > 30 mL/min/1.73 m2, and with at least two additional atherosclerotic risk factors. Canagliflozin reduced the risk of major cardiovascular events by 14% versus placebo and led to a 40% relative risk reduction in the composite renal outcome (sustained loss of renal function, renal failure, or death due to renal cause) [6].

Although the integrated CANVAS Program demonstrated significant cardiovascular and renal benefits with canagliflozin, the comparative effects of the two doses (100 and 300 mg) were not formally evaluated. We therefore restricted this post hoc analysis to the CANVAS trial , which provides a randomised comparison of fixed doses. We excluded CANVAS‐R, as dose escalation was not randomly assigned but based on physician discretion, making it susceptible to treatment selection bias and confounding by indication.

Methods

Study Population

This post hoc analysis included participants exclusively from the CANVAS trial, enabling evaluation of the dose‐dependent effects of canagliflozin on renal and cardiovascular outcomes. This study, carried out under YODA Project #2021–4850, used data obtained from the Yale University Open Data Access Project, which has an agreement with JANSSEN RESEARCH & DEVELOPMENT, L.L.C. The interpretation and reporting of research using this data are solely the responsibility of the authors and does not necessarily represent the official views of the Yale University Open Data Access Project or JANSSEN RESEARCH & DEVELOPMENT, L.L.C.

The original proposal was updated in December 2022 to include the following analyses: CKD progression and selected safety outcomes to be assessed in each cluster and the whole cohort. In addition, we aimed to assess the treatment response to each dose of the medication. All endpoints are detailed in the outcomes section below.

Outcomes

The primary efficacy outcome was the renal composite endpoint of doubling of serum creatinine, renal failure or death due to renal cause. Renal failure was defined as the initiation of dialysis, kidney transplantation, or a sustained drop in eGFR below 15 mL/min/1.73 m2. Secondary outcomes included a cardiovascular composite endpoint of nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, or cardiovascular death; hospitalisation for heart failure; all‐cause mortality; a sustained decline in eGFR of 40% or greater; and progression or regression of albuminuria. Albuminuria progression was defined as a shift from normal (or mildly increased albuminuria, Stage A1) to moderately increased albuminuria (Stage A2) or severely increased albuminuria (Stage A3) or from Stage A2 to A3. Albuminuria regression was defined as a transition to a lower albuminuria category, such as from A3 to A2 or A1 or from A2 to A1. Safety outcomes included the incidence of acute kidney injury and serious hyperkalaemia.

Statistical Analysis

Our primary analysis directly compared outcomes between participants randomised to the canagliflozin 100 and 300 mg arms, excluding those in the placebo group. We emphasise that an arm × dose interaction is not appropriate for comparative analyses when the arms are defined by canagliflozin dose. Therefore, there is no arm × dose interaction.

Baseline patient characteristics are presented as mean (± standard deviation), median (interquartile range) or number (percentage), as appropriate. Absolute rate differences per 100 person‐years and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were estimated using a Wald method, assuming Poisson‐distributed event counts and deriving standard errors from the variance of the incidence rates in each treatment group. Unadjusted Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) under the intention to treat approach. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals and by visual inspection of the corresponding residual plots. Cumulative incidence curves were constructed for each treatment. All statistical tests were two‐sided and a p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In secondary analyses, all models from the primary analysis were further adjusted for age and sex; models evaluating cardiovascular endpoints were additionally adjusted for history of cardiovascular disease. Pairwise comparisons of the primary outcomes between the canagliflozin arms (100 and 300 mg) and placebo were performed using both unadjusted and adjusted Cox proportional hazards models. We also report comparisons of the combined canagliflozin group (100 mg and 300 mg pooled) versus placebo, as these results have not been previously presented; notably, the CANVAS Program did not separately report outcomes from the CANVAS and CANVAS‐R trials.

Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed for the primary composite outcome, the cardiovascular composite endpoint, all‐cause mortality, and hospitalisations for heart failure, according to baseline age (≤ 60 vs. > 60 years), sex (male vs. female), and baseline eGFR (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2). Interaction terms between treatment group and the subgroup variable were included in the Cox models to assess heterogeneity of treatment effect. For interaction testing, we used a nominal significance level of 0.20.

To account for potential violations of the proportional hazards assumption, a sensitivity analysis using the restricted mean survival time was conducted for the primary composite endpoint, the cardiovascular composite endpoint, all‐cause mortality, and hospitalisations for heart failure. Restricted mean survival time was estimated as the area under the Kaplan–Meier survival curve up to a prespecified truncation time of 78 weeks. This time point was selected based on the CANVAS program design, corresponding to the joint close‐out when the last randomised participant had accrued approximately 78 weeks of follow‐up, thereby ensuring stable estimation with adequate numbers of participants at risk across treatment arms. Between‐group differences were assessed using a Wald‐type two‐sample Z‐test, with standard errors derived from the Greenwood plug‐in method [25, 26].

Bonferroni‐adjusted p‐values are also reported to account for multiple testing. Bonferroni‐adjusted p‐values were applied within distinct analytical blocks to account for multiple testing while controlling the family‐wise error rate. Specifically, adjustments were performed for 9 pairwise tests in analyses using canagliflozin 100 mg as the reference (Table 2 and S1), 27 pairwise tests in analyses using placebo as the reference (Table 3 and S2), 12 pairwise tests in subgroup analyses (Figure S1), and 9 pairwise tests in RMST analyses (Table S3). We additionally applied the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate, restricted to outcomes that were statistically significant based on unadjusted p‐values. In cases of discrepancy between Bonferroni‐adjusted and Benjamini–Hochberg–adjusted p‐values, statistical significance was interpreted based on the Benjamini–Hochberg–adjusted p‐values.

The mean age was 61 ± 8 years and 2861 participants (66%) were males (Table 1). The majority of participants (3179; 73%) were White and 3039 (70%) had longstanding diabetes (> 10 years). Hypertension was prevalent in 88% of the cohort, with a mean systolic BP of 136 ± 16 mmHg and mean diastolic BP of 78 ± 10 mmHg. Established cardiovascular disease was present in 2549 participants (59%) and 515 (12%) had a history of heart failure.

The mean eGFR at baseline was 77 ± 19 mL/min/1.73 m2 and 777 participants (18%) had CKD, defined as an eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. The median urine albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR) was 1.3 (0.7–4.1) mg/mmol with 250 (6%) exhibiting severely increased albuminuria (A3).

Approximately half of the cohort was receiving insulin therapy at baseline, but only 96 participants (2%) were on glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists.

Median duration of follow‐up was 74 (72–77) months.

Renal Outcomes

There was no significant difference in the composite renal endpoint between the 300 mg and 100 mg doses: HR 0.85; 95% CI 0.38–1.89; p = 0.68 (Table 2 and Figure 1A). However, both doses of canagliflozin were associated with a significant reduction in the incidence of the composite renal endpoint compared with placebo. The HR for the canagliflozin 100 mg group (versus placebo) was 0.49 (95% CI 0.25–0.95; p = 0.03) and for the 300 mg group (versus placebo) was 0.41 (95% CI 0.20–0.83; p = 0.01) (Table 3).

Cumulative incidence of (A) the composite kidney endpoint and (B) the composite cardiovascular endpoint among participants receiving canagliflozin 100 mg or 300 mg. CFZ, canagliflozin; HR, hazard ratio.

Similarly, both doses of canagliflozin significantly reduced the risk of a sustained 40% decline in eGFR as compared to placebo (canagliflozin 100 mg versus placebo: HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.40–0.85; p = 0.005; canagliflozin 300 mg versus placebo: HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.37–0.80; p < 0.01), with no significant difference between the 300 mg and 100 mg doses (HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.61–1.42; p = 0.74) (Tables 2 and 3).

There was no difference in progression of albuminuria to a higher category between the two doses of canagliflozin: HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.79–1.02; p = 0.11 (Table 2). Progression of albuminuria occurred less frequently with canagliflozin 300 mg as compared to placebo (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.73–0.95; p < 0.01), whereas there was no statistically significant difference between canagliflozin 100 mg and placebo groups (HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.82–1.06; p = 0.26) (Table 3).

Both doses significantly increased the likelihood of regression of albuminuria to a lower category compared with placebo (canagliflozin 100 mg versus placebo: HR 1.60, 95% CI 1.30–1.96; p < 0.001; canagliflozin 300 mg versus placebo: HR 1.59, 95% CI 1.30–1.95; p < 0.001), with no difference between the 300 mg and 100 mg doses (HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.83–1.21; p = 0.99) (Tables 2 and 3).

Cardiovascular Outcomes

The incidence of the composite cardiovascular endpoint was similar with both doses of canagliflozin (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.74–1.08; p = 0.25) (Table 2, Figure 1B). Similarly, the 100 mg dose did not significantly differ from placebo for this endpoint (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.79–1.13; p = 0.53), and this was also the case for the 300 mg dose compared with placebo (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.70–1.02; p = 0.08) (Table 3).

Canagliflozin did not significantly reduce the incidence of hospitalisation for heart failure in this cohort (100 mg vs. placebo: HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.47–1.08; p = 0.11; 300 mg vs. placebo: HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.57–1.26; p = 0.41), with no difference between the 300 mg and 100 mg doses (HR 1.18, 95% CI 0.77–1.81; p = 0.44) (Tables 2 and 3).

There was no difference in mortality reduction rates the two doses (HR 0.92; 95% CI 0.73–1.15; p = 0.46) (Table 2). All‐cause mortality was similar with each dose of the drug compared with placebo (100 mg vs. placebo: HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.71–1.09; p = 0.24; 300 mg vs. placebo: HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.65–1.01; p = 0.06).

Safety Outcomes

The incidence of acute kidney injury was similar across the three treatment groups, with no significant difference between the 300 mg and 100 mg doses (HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.20–4.96; p = 1.00) (Tables 2 and 3). Similarly, the incidence of severe hyperkalaemia was similar across the three treatment arms, and no significant difference was observed between the two canagliflozin doses (HR 0.53; 95% CI 0.23–1.26; p = 0.15) (Tables 2 and 3).

Secondary Analyses

A sensitivity analysis using adjusted hazard ratios (accounting for age, sex, and history of cardiovascular disease) yielded results consistent with the primary analyses (Tables S1 and S2).

The restricted mean survival time analysis truncated at 78 weeks demonstrated no significant differences between the 300 and 100 mg doses for the composite renal endpoint (primary outcome) or for any secondary outcomes, including the composite cardiovascular outcome. The estimated difference between the 300 mg and 100 mg doses was −0.3 days for the composite renal endpoint (95% CI –0.9 to 0.3; p = 0.32) and −1.8 days for the composite cardiovascular endpoint (95% CI –6.5 to 2.9; p = 0.45), indicating comparable effects of the two doses over the follow‐up period (Table S3).

We also present unadjusted and multiplicity‐adjusted p‐values in Supplemental Table S4; Bonferroni‐adjusted p‐values are shown for all outcomes, whereas the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied only to outcomes that were statistically significant on unadjusted analysis.

Subgroup Analyses

We examined the interaction between canagliflozin dose and age, sex or baseline CKD status (eGFR above or below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2) on hard clinical endpoints (Figure S1). No significant interaction was detected in any of the subgroups.

Discussion

In this post hoc analysis of the CANVAS trial, both the 100‐mg and 300‐mg doses of canagliflozin reduced the risk of the composite renal outcome, with no statistically significant difference between doses. None of the secondary endpoints demonstrated clear evidence of a dose‐dependent effect. Rates of adverse events, including acute kidney injury and hyperkalaemia, were similar between doses, supporting the safety of either regimen. Subgroup analyses did not demonstrate effect modification by age, sex, or baseline CKD status.

Our results agree with findings from the EMPA‐REG OUTCOME trial which was the only other large randomised controlled trial that compared different doses of an SGLT‐2 inhibitor on hard cardiovascular endpoints. In this study, although both empagliflozin doses (10 and 25 mg) reduced the composite cardiovascular outcome compared with placebo (pooled HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.74–0.99), there was no statistically significant difference between the two doses, suggesting that a plateau effect might be observed beyond a certain exposure threshold with these agents (Table S5) [4]. We are the first to our knowledge to report the effects of different doses of SGLT‐2 inhibitors on composite renal outcomes.

Pharmacological evidence supports a correlation between SGLT‐2 inhibitor efficacy and drug exposure. In a pharmacokinetic model of dapagliflozin from the DIAMOND trial, a randomised, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, crossover study, each 100 ng·h/mL increase in drug exposure was associated with an additional 2.8% reduction in albuminuria, a 0.1 kg reduction in body weight, and a 0.4 mmHg reduction in systolic blood pressure [13].

Several network meta‐analyses have explored the dose–response relationship of SGLT‐2 inhibitors across a broad range of clinical trials and generally suggest that higher doses may provide incremental benefits on surrogate outcomes such as glycaemic control, blood pressure, and body weight. For example, a network meta‐analysis demonstrated that canagliflozin 300 mg achieved greater reductions in HbA1c (mean difference −0.18%, 95% CI −0.22 to −0.13) and body weight (−0.40 kg, 95% CI −0.79 to −0.01) compared with 100 mg, while empagliflozin 25 mg produced greater weight reduction than 10 mg (−0.29 kg, 95% CI −0.58 to −0.01) without a significant difference in HbA1c. Similarly, dapagliflozin 10 mg was associated with greater HbA1c reduction than 5 mg (−0.13%, 95% CI −0.23 to −0.04), without a corresponding difference in weight [28]. A larger meta‐analysis of 51 randomised controlled trials further supported dose‐dependent improvements in HbA1c, body weight, blood pressure, and the need for glycaemic rescue therapy [18]. In addition, model‐based network meta‐analyses have suggested that the blood pressure–lowering effects of SGLT‐2 inhibitors may follow a dose–response gradient, with canagliflozin and luseogliflozin demonstrating more pronounced effects [29].

However, evidence for surrogate renal outcomes is less consistent. A large Bayesian network meta‐analysis by Hegde et al., including 45 randomised controlled trials (n = 48 067), evaluated the dose–response relationship of SGLT‐2 inhibitors on eGFR and albuminuria. Using Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking Curve (SUCRA)‐based rankings, canagliflozin 100 mg demonstrated the highest probability of benefit for both eGFR (SUCRA ~93%) and albuminuria (SUCRA ~68%), followed by canagliflozin 300 mg, with no consistent incremental benefit observed at higher doses. These findings suggest a plateau effect in renoprotective efficacy at lower doses [17]. Consistent with these observations, in our analysis, albuminuria progression did not differ between canagliflozin 300 mg and 100 mg. Similarly, there was no difference in albuminuria regression between the two doses.

Similarly, safety‐focused network meta‐analyses have not demonstrated a consistent dose–toxicity relationship. While SGLT‐2 inhibitors are associated with class‐related adverse effects such as genital and urinary tract infections, the risk does not appear to systematically increase with higher doses and differences in adverse events are more often drug‐specific than dose‐dependent [30, 31].

These findings, however, should be interpreted with caution. Network meta‐analyses rely on aggregate study‐level data and indirect comparisons across trials that frequently differ in patient populations, background therapies, outcome definitions, and duration of follow‐up. As such, they are inherently susceptible to between‐trial confounding, heterogeneity, and potential violations of the transitivity assumption, which may limit causal inference when comparing doses [32]. Moreover, most available data are derived from short‐term studies and focus predominantly on surrogate endpoints rather than hard clinical outcomes. In contrast, our analysis leverages individual participant‐level data from a randomised controlled trial, in which participants were directly randomised to fixed doses of canagliflozin (100 or 300 mg). This within‐trial comparison minimises confounding, ensures consistency in patient characteristics, follow‐up duration, and outcome adjudication, and provides a more robust framework for causal inference. In addition, none of these network meta‐analyses have looked into the dose effect on hard clinical endpoints. Importantly, our findings indicate that even the lower dose of 100 mg of canagliflozin is effective in preventing adverse renal outcomes—an important consideration in the context of persistent real‐world underutilisation of SGLT‐2 inhibitors driven by safety concerns [33, 34].

A key strength of this analysis is the biologically plausible context in which a dose–response relationship could have been detected. Canagliflozin demonstrates a broader exposure–response profile, with approximately threefold higher area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) at 300 mg compared with 100 mg, along with partial inhibition of SGLT‐1 at higher doses, which may augment natriuretic and hemodynamic effects [35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. In contrast, other SGLT‐2 inhibitors, such as empagliflozin demonstrate dose‐proportional increases in exposure (approximately twofold between 10 and 25 mg) [41, 42]. Taken together, these pharmacokinetic and mechanistic differences suggest that, if a clinically meaningful dose effect were present within the SGLT‐2 inhibitor class, it would be most likely to be observed with canagliflozin. Another major strength is the use of individual participant‐level data from the CANVAS trial, in which patients were randomised in a 1:1:1 ratio to canagliflozin 100, 300 mg or placebo. This design allows for a direct, unbiased comparison between doses within the same population, follow‐up period, and outcome definitions, minimising confounding and eliminating physician‐driven dose selection. Finally, the consistency of findings across sensitivity and secondary analyses supports the robustness of the results.

A key limitation of this analysis is the limited number of events for the composite renal and safety endpoints, resulting in wide confidence intervals around effect estimates. As this was a post hoc analysis, statistical power was limited for detecting modest differences between doses; for example, the power to detect a hazard ratio of 0.85 for the cardiovascular outcome was approximately 0.39. Although power would be greater for larger effect sizes (e.g., a hazard ratio of 0.75), post hoc power calculations are dependent on assumed effect sizes and do not provide additional inferential value beyond the confidence intervals. Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant difference should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence [43]. Although the analysis plan was specified prior to data access, multiple hypothesis testing increases the risk of chance findings. To address this, we applied Bonferroni correction across all outcomes to control the family‐wise error rate. Given the correlation among cardiovascular and kidney endpoints, this approach is conservative and may attenuate estimates of treatment effect [44]. Therefore, we additionally applied the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate for outcomes that were statistically significant on unadjusted analysis. Notably, the application of these multiplicity adjustments did not materially alter the interpretation of the key findings.

Conclusion

In this post hoc analysis of the CANVAS trial, we did not observe evidence of a dose‐dependent effect of canagliflozin on renal or cardiovascular outcomes. Both doses significantly reduced the incidence of the primary composite renal endpoint compared with placebo. These findings suggest that substantial cardiorenal benefits may be achieved with standard dosing, raising the possibility that dose‐escalation beyond that required for glycaemic control may not be necessary to achieve additional clinical benefit. However, given the limitations of this analysis, the results should be considered hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive.
