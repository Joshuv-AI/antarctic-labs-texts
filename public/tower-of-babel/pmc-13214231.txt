Plasma homocysteine and cardiovascular outcomes in postmenopausal women with coronary heart disease: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Peng Wu, Zefeng He, Baozhen Zhu, Bo Wu, Yuru Ma, Ru Yan, Tianshui Ma, Jiawei Yang, Ning Yan, Shaobin Jia, Xueping Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between plasma homocysteine (Hcy) and adverse cardiovascular outcomes in postmenopausal women with coronary heart disease (CHD) remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the prognostic value of Hcy levels in this high-risk population.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 624 postmenopausal women with CHD from Ningxia Medical University General Hospital, with a 3-years follow-up. The association between Hcy and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) was evaluated using multivariate Cox regression. Non-linear relationships were assessed using generalized additive models (GAM) and threshold effect analyses. Survival disparities were analyzed via Kaplan-Meier estimates. Stratified analyses assessed association robustness, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis determined the predictive capacity of Hcy for MACE risk.ResultsDuring follow-up, 133 MACE occurred. Higher Hcy tertiles were associated with increased cumulative MACE risk (log-rank,P < 0.001). The multivariable cox regression revealed a significant positive relationship between Hcy and MACE risk. After full adjustment, each 1 µmol/L increment in Hcy was associated with a 17% increased risk of MACE (HR 1.17, 95%CI:1.12–1.22; P < 0.001). GAM analyses uncovered non-linear relationships (P for non-linearity < 0.05). Threshold analysis identified an inflection point at 11.28µmol/L. Subgroup analyses confirmed the robustness of this association (all P interaction > 0.05). ROC curve analysis demonstrated moderate predictive accuracy for Hcy (AUC 0.729, 95%CI: 0.682–0.777).ConclusionIn this retrospective cohort, Hcy may be independently associated with an increased risk of MACE in postmenopausal women with established CHD. These findings may suggest that Hcy could serve as a prognostic biomarker in this high-risk population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05832-7.Keywords: Homocysteine, Major adverse cardiovascular events, Postmenopausal women, Coronary heart disease, Risk stratification

Background

Coronary heart disease (CHD) is a predominant cause of cardiovascular-related mortality in postmenopausal women worldwide, displaying pathophysiological characteristics that differ from those commonly seen in men [1]. The menopausal transition, characterized by a pronounced decline in circulating estrogen [2], initiates metabolic alterations that contribute to accelerated atherogenesis. Despite receiving optimal pharmacotherapy, postmenopausal women with established CHD remain at a consistently elevated risk for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [3]. This persistent risk underscores the need to identify biomarkers with specific prognostic relevance for this population.

Homocysteine (Hcy), a sulfur-containing amino acid derived from methionine metabolism, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular disease [4]. Hyperhomocysteinemia (HHcy), typically defined as a plasma Hcy concentration ≥ 15 µmol/L, fosters a pathogenic milieu conducive to oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and a prothrombotic state [5]. While epidemiological data consistently position HHcy as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, its prognostic utility appears to vary across different patient cohorts [6]. Postmenopausal women may constitute a distinct subgroup in this context. The estrogen-deficient state following menopause can induce metabolic shifts that potentially disrupt Hcy metabolism and impair the utilization of B vitamins, which are crucial for its clearance [7, 8].

In light of the heightened cardiovascular vulnerability observed in postmenopausal women, this study was designed to investigate the association between plasma Hcy levels and the occurrence of MACE in this high-risk group with confirmed CHD.

Method

Study design and patients

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study including 1627 patients who were diagnosed with coronary heart disease (CHD) at the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University in Yinchuan, Ningxia, China, from January 2017 to December 2018. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University (approval number: KYLL-2024-0088). The Institutional Review Board waived the requirement for informed consent due to the study’s retrospective nature. All patient identifiers were de-identified to ensure confidentiality.

Participant inclusion criteria: (1) CHD meets the diagnostic criteria of WHO [9], (2) women with natural menopause for more than 1 year. Natural menopause was defined as the absence of menstruation for at least 12 consecutive months without other known causes. Women with surgical menopause (e.g., bilateral oophorectomy) or those who were current or past users of hormone replacement therapy (HRT) were excluded. 1003 patients were excluded according to the exclusion criteria, which were 1) male patient with CHD (n = 826),2) women who were not postmenopausal (including those with surgical menopause or HRT use) (n = 94), (3) missing plasma homocysteine data (n = 37), (4) loss to follow-up (n = 46) (Fig. 1). Finally, 624 patients were stratified into three groups according to the tertiles of the Hcy: T1 group ( Hcy < 13.18, n = 208 ); T2 group( 13.18 ≤ Hcy < 17.18, n = 208 ); T3 group( Hcy ≥ 17.18, n = 208 ).

Flowchart illustrating the selection process of study participants

Data collections and definitions

Data were extracted retrospectively from the hospital’s electronic medical record system using a standardized case report form. Collected information encompassed demographic characteristics, clinical history, laboratory results, and medication use. Demographic data included age, weight, height [to calculate body mass index (BMI)], smoking history and drinking history. Clinical history included established diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, stroke and hyperlipemia. Blood samples were analyzed within 24 h of admission under fasting conditions using a Sysmex XN-9000 automated hematology analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan) and an Atellica® Solution fully automatic biochemical analyzer (Siemens Healthineers AG, Erlangen, Germany). Laboratory parameters were uniformly measured during stable periods (fasting blood samples collected within 24 h of admission, excluding cases within 72 h post-acute coronary syndrome). Laboratory tests consisting of white blood cell (WBC), red blood cell (RBC), platelets (PLT), neutrophil(NEUT), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), albumin (ALB), uric acid(UA), serum creatinine(SCr), Glucose, Hcy, total cholesterol(TC), triglyceride(TG); low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), Fibrinogen(Fib), D-dimer(D-D).Besides that, information about medications[β-blockers, antiplatelet, statins, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers(ACEI/ARB), Folic acid].

Definitions of smoking status, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia were based on established clinical criteria, as previously described. Smoking: Consumption of more than 10 cigarettes daily for a minimum of six months [10]. Diabetes Mellitus: Defined by self-reported use of anti-diabetic medication or meeting glycemic criteria: casual blood glucose ≥ 11.1mmol/L, fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0mmol/L, or a 2-hour postprandial glucose ≥ 11.1mmol/L after a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test [11]. Hypertension: Documented history of blood pressure readings ≥ 140/90 mmHg or current use of antihypertensive medication [12]. Hyperlipidemia: Defined asLDL-C ≥ 3.4mmol/L, HDL-C < 1.0mmol/L, TG ≥ 1.7mmol/L, or current use of lipid-lowering medication [13]. All patient management followed contemporary clinical practice guidelines [14]. We have now calculated the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) for all participants using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration 2021 equation without race variable (CKD-EPI 2021 creatinine equation). This equation is based solely on serum creatinine (SCr), age, and sex.

Follow‑up and endpoints

Patients were prospectively followed for three years following hospital discharge. The primary endpoint was the occurrence of MACE within this 3-year period. MACE was a composite endpoint defined as the first occurrence of any of the following: all-cause death, nonfatal MI, rehospitalization due to unstable angina or heart failure, or nonfatal stroke. Follow-up data were collected via outpatient records, hospital readmission documentation, and telephone interviews, and all potential endpoint events were independently adjudicated by two senior cardiologists, with discrepancies resolved by consensus or by a third adjudicator if needed; among the 624 enrolled patients, 46 (7.3%) were lost to follow‑up.

Statistical analysis

In this study, all statistical analyses were performed with R (the R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) and Empower (X & Y Solutions, Boston, MA, USA). All tests were 2-sided, and a P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For continuous variables, mean ± standard deviation (SD) was used for statistical description if they met normal distribution; independent samples t-test was used forinter-group comparison. the median (25–75%) was used for description if the variables did not meet normal distribution; and the rank sum test was used for inter-group comparison. For counting data, the number of cases (%) was used to describe it, the chi-square test was used for comparison between groups, and Fisher’s exact probability was used when the chi-square test was not satisfied. Adjusting for confounding variables using multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) between Hcy level and MACE. Candidate variables were selected based on univariate Cox regression (P < 0.05) and clinical relevance, including age, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, smoking, Hcy, glucose, HDL-C, fibrinogen, neutrophil count, and folic acid use (Table S1). To optimize model parsimony and minimize overfitting, we employed the change-in-estimate method to identify key confounders. Variables whose removal altered the Hcy coefficient by > 10% were retained in the final model: age, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, smoking, glucose, HDL-C, neutrophil count, fibrinogen, eGFR and folic acid use (Tables S2, S3). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), all of which were below 2.0, indicating no substantial collinearity (Table S4). Additionally, considering the significant baseline imbalances in the use of statins, antiplatelet agents, and β-blockers (Table 1) and their well-established prognostic value in CHD, these three medication variables were forcibly included in Model 3, regardless of their statistical selection criteria. We used three models of adjustment: Model 1 Non-adjusted; Model 2: adjust for age, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, smoking; Model 3: adjust for age, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, smoking, glucose, HDL-C, neutrophil count, fibrinogen, eGFR, statin use, antiplatelet use, β-blocker use and folic acid use. An analysis of the nonlinear correlation between Hcy level and MACE was conducted using the generalized additive model (GAM). Two piecewise linear regression models were employed to assess threshold effect of Hcy level on MACE probability. This approach allowed for a more detailed analysis of potential nonlinear associations and cutoff points in the relationship by the log-likelihood ratio. Stratified analyses and interaction tests were performed based on covariates fully adjusted model to investigate factors influencing the link between Hcy level and MACE. The receiver-operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis were conducted to predict the possible risk of MACE.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study cohort

The study cohort comprised 624 postmenopausal women with CHD, with a mean age of 67.3 ± 9.1 years and baseline Hcy level of 17.3 ± 6.2µmol/L. Over the 3-year follow-up, 133 participants (21.3%) experienced MACE.

As detailed in Table 1, patients in the highest Hcy tertile (T3:>17.18µmol/L) exhibited significantly elevated levels of age, NEUT, UA, SCR, eGFR, Hcy, and Fib compared to the lowest tertile (T1:<13.8µmol/L; all P < 0.05). A paradoxical medication pattern emerged across Hcy tertiles: folic acid usage progressively increased, while utilization rates of β-blockers, statins, and antiplatelet agents showed inverse trends.

Notably, MACE incidence demonstrated a dose-dependent escalation with Hcy tertiles (T1: 9.62%; T3: 37.02%; P < 0.05). There were no other indicators significant difference in the three groups (all P > 0.05).

Multivariate cox proportional hazards regression analysis of various models

Table 2 shows the association between Hcy and MACE. We found that higher Hcy were correlated with MACE in both unadjusted and adjusted models. When analyzed as a continuous variable, Hcy remained significantly associated with MACE after full adjustment (adjusted for age, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, smoking, glucose, HDL-C, neutrophil count, fibrinogen, eGFR, statin use, antiplatelet use, β-blocker use and folic acid use). The HR for Hcy was 1.17 (95% CI:1.12–1.22, P < 0.001), indicating that for each unit increase in Hcy, the HRs of developing MACE increased by 17%. Hcy was considered as a categorical variable (Hcy was divided into three groups by tertiles) exposure to evaluate the independent relationship of Hcy and MACE among in postmenopausal women patient with CHD. Model 3 revealed that participants in the T3 group had a significantly higher risk of MACE than those in the T1 group (HR = 6.12, 95%CI:3.30-10.35; P for trend < 0.001).

Threshold effect analysis shown in Table 3 demonstrates a noteworthy nonlinear correlation between Hcy and occurrence of MACE. In Model I (linear analysis), every unit increase in Hcy correlated with 17% elevated risk of MACE (HR:1.17,95%CI:1.12–1.22, P < 0.001). Model II (nonlinear analysis) identified a turning point (K) at Hcy concentration of 11.28 µmol/L. Below this threshold, the risk of MACE rose significantly with Hcy increase (HR: 1.45, 95%CI:1.12–1.85, P = 0.003). However, beyond this threshold, the association weakened, with 15% increase per unit (HR:1.15, 95%CI:1.10–1.26, P < 0.001). The logarithm likelihood ratio test (LRT) confirmed that the nonlinear model (Model II) provided a significantly better fit than the linear model (Model I) (P < 0.05).

Subgroup analysis and interaction results interpretation

To evaluate whether the association between Hcy and MACE was consistent in the overall population and to identify potential differences among subgroups, we conducted subgroup analysis and interaction tests stratified by age, BMI, DM, hypertension, hyperlipemia, stroke, smoking and drinking. In the subgroup analysis, the association between Hcy and MACE was not consistently significant across certain groups (Fig. 4). The area under the ROC curve for the Hcy to predict the incidence of MACE in postmenopausal women patient with CHD was 0.729 (95% CI 0.682–0.777) (Fig. 5). The cut-of value of Hcy to predict MACE was 19.72µmol/L, the sensitivity was 0.549, and the specificity was 0.792.

Discussion

In this study with a total of 624 subjects, we observed a positive correlation between the Hcy and MACE among in postmenopausal women patient with CHD. Whether Hcy was considered a continuous or categorical variable, the Hcy was found to be an independent risk factor for MACE. Interestingly, a nonlinear relationship with a threshold effect was observed, showing a strong association at Hcy levels below11.28µmol/L. Our study highlights that Hcy level is a significant predictor and may help in identifying patients with postmenopausal women CHD at high risk of MACE and guiding further detections and more aggressive treatments.

Ischemic cardiovascular diseases, including CHD, have become a major contributor to the global increase in life years lost and disability-adjusted life years. With China’s large population and an aging demographic, the burden of CHD is expected to increase significantly [15]. While treatment can substantially improve the prognosis of CHD patients, some still experience MACE, especially in postmenopausal women, the underlying causes of which remain unclear. Previous studies have highlighted the role of Hcy as a predictive marker for cardiovascular outcomes. Bo Wang [16] meta-analysis showed that for every 5µmol/L increase in Hcy level, the risk of coronary heart disease increased by 22%.Tanne et al. [17]found that high Hcy levels increase the risk of ischemic events in patients with chronic stable coronary heart disease, with this effect being independent of traditional risk factors. Verhoef et al. [18] noted that postmenopausal women have higher Hcy levels than premenopausal women, and that elevated Hcy in women is associated with a higher risk of CHD, a pattern not seen in men. Natural menopause is defined as the absence of menstruation for 12 consecutive months, excluding other causes, and marks the onset of menopause. This transition involves a crucial phase characterized by significant hormonal and menstrual changes, signifying the permanent cessation of ovarian function and a woman’s shift from the report [19]. Premenopausal women have a lower incidence of coronary heart disease compared to men of the same age. However, after menopause, the risk of coronary heart disease in women increases, the underlying causes of which remain unclear [20, 21]. A study were all women aged 45–75 years, the results shows that the mean homocysteine level was 8.3 µmol/L. Levels rose from 7.8 µmol/L in women under 47 years to 9.0 µmol/L in those over 59. Among premenopausal women, the mean homocysteine level was 7.7 µmol/L, compared to 8.3 µmol/L in postmenopausal women, though this difference was no longer significant after adjustment [22]. Our analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship with a threshold effect at an Hcy level of 11.28µmol/L, beyond which the association with MACE risk was attenuated. The interpretation of this inflection point requires caution. Although it might indicate a biological saturation effect in Hcy-induced vascular injury, a more parsimonious explanation involves methodological considerations [23]. The observed flattening of the curve could partly stem from regression dilution bias, wherein random fluctuations in a single Hcy measurement may lead to an underestimation of the true risk at the highest levels. Furthermore, the dominant contribution of other, unmeasured comorbidities in severe HHcy patients cannot be ruled out. Consequently, this threshold should not be viewed as definitive but rather as a hypothesis-generating finding that merits rigorous investigation in future cohorts with repeated Hcy measurements and comprehensive phenotyping. In addition to these methodological considerations, clinical management patterns also require attention. Furthermore, although the high Hcy group received guideline-directed medications (statins, β-blockers) less frequently, we included these medications (statins, β-blockers) as variables in our model, and Hcy remained a risk factor. Our multivariate analysis adjusted for these treatment differences. The persistence of a significant association between Hcy and MACE suggests that the risk conveyed by elevated Hcy may be independent of, or additive to, the effects of standard pharmacotherapy in this cohort. The clinical utility of Hcy as a stand-alone predictor also warrants careful interpretation. In our cohort, the ROC analysis yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.729 for Hcy in predicting MACE, indicating only moderate discriminative ability. Furthermore, the optimal cutoff value derived from this analysis (19.72µmol/L) requires external validation in independent populations. A critical next step is to evaluate whether Hcy measurement provides incremental predictive value over established risk assessment tools routinely used in clinical practice for postmenopausal women with CHD. Future studies should therefore aim to incorporate Hcy into existing multifactorial risk models (those incorporating age, renal function, and traditional cardiovascular risk factors) and formally assess its added value using metrics such as the Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) or Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI).

Hcy may cause atherosclerosis and adverse cardiovascular events through the following mechanisms: (1) Endothelial damage and dysfunction. Hcy can reduce the activity of reactive oxygen species scavenging enzymes in the human body, promote the synthesis of reactive oxygen species in endothelial cells, inhibit the production of prostacyclin, and lead to impaired vasodilation function; Hcy reduces the production of nitric oxide by affecting the synthesis of nitric oxide synthase; Hcy can also affect the synthesis of endothelial cell DNA, inhibiting the proliferation and repair function of endothelial cells [24]; (2) Promote vascular smooth muscle proliferation. Hcy can cause smooth muscle cells to enter the cell cycle from the quiescent phase and stimulate the synthesis of other growth factors; at the same time, it can also increase the synthesis of serine proteases and collagen, promoting the progression of atherosclerosis; (3) Affect the coagulation mechanism. Hcy inhibits the binding of antithrombin III to endothelial cells, while activating thrombin V, VII, etc., promoting platelet aggregation and leading to thrombosis; (4) Affect lipid metabolism. Hcy promotes the oxidative modification of LDL-C, thereby reducing the activity of thrombin regulatory protein and causing endothelial function damage. Factors affecting serum Hcy levels include low levels of folic acid and certain vitamins in the body and deficiency of enzyme genes involved in Hcy metabolism [25, 26]. In addition, Hcy levels may also be related to sex steroid hormone levels [27]. Serum Hcy levels are related to menopausal status, and its concentration gradually increases during the menopausal stage. Changes in women’s bodies during the menopausal transition may lead to increased Hcy levels in postmenopausal women [28]. The relationship between homocysteine and cardiovascular disease is particularly pertinent in menopausal women, who experience significant hormonal changes that can further amplify cardiovascular risk. The Vulnerability of Menopausal Women. The transition to menopause is associated with a decline in estrogen, a hormone that plays a protective role against CVD. Estrogen is known to enhance endothelial function, regulate lipid profiles, and exert anti-inflammatory effects. With menopause, women often experience increased levels of LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, alongside a decrease in HDL cholesterol, further compounding their cardiovascular risk [29].

Our study highlights that menopausal women with elevated homocysteine levels face an exacerbated risk for MACE, emphasizing the need for tailored risk assessment in this demographic. Identifying and managing elevated homocysteine can be crucial in developing preventive strategies for this population. When compared with other risk factors, it is important to note that while traditional cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes have long been recognized, our findings suggest that homocysteine adds significant prognostic value. However, it is crucial to reconcile this robust association with the findings of previous research—such as HOPE-2, NORVIT, and VISP—which demonstrated that therapeutic lowering of Hcy with B-vitamin supplements did not reduce cardiovascular events in populations with established vascular disease [30–32]. This apparent paradox suggests that elevated Hcy is likely a potent marker of cardiovascular risk, rather than a modifiable causal factor in the advanced stages of atherosclerotic disease. The underlying conditions that drive Hcy elevation (renal impairment, poor nutritional status, or genetic predispositions) may be the true mediators of risk, and Hcy-lowering therapy initiated after the establishment of clinical CHD may be “too little, too late” to reverse existing vascular damage. Therefore, the clinical implications of our findings are primarily diagnostic rather than therapeutic. Hcy is an easily measurable and cost-effective biomarker that could complement existing cardiovascular risk assessment tools, such as the Framingham Risk Score or GRACE Score, particularly in menopausal women where conventional risk factors may not fully capture residual cardiovascular risk. Identifying patients with elevated Hcy could enable intensified management of modifiable risk factors and closer clinical surveillance, potentially reducing the incidence of MACE in this high-risk population. However, our findings should not be interpreted as supporting the routine use of Hcy-lowering therapy for secondary prevention, consistent with the null results of these pivotal RCTs and current clinical guidelines.

Study limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center, retrospective study conducted in a specific regional population in China. This may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnicities and healthcare settings. Second, Hcy was measured only once at baseline. Plasma Hcy levels can fluctuate due to various factors including nutritional status, renal function, and interventions. A single measurement may not accurately reflect long-term exposure, potentially leading to regression dilution bias and an underestimation of the true risk association. Future studies with serial Hcy measurements are warranted to better characterize its prognostic role. Third, the definition of postmenopausal status relied on clinical history rather than hormonal assays. This may have introduced selection bias, as perimenopausal women with ambiguous menstrual histories could have been misclassified. Fourth, despite our efforts to control for confounding factors using available data, we lacked information on several potential confounders, including nutritional status (e.g., folate, B12 levels), genetic factors (e.g., MTHFR genotype). The presence of these unmeasured confounders may affect the stability and interpretation of our results. Fifth, we observed that patients in the highest Hcy tertile were more frequently prescribed folic acid, yet experienced the worst outcomes. This likely reflects clinical practice where folic acid is prescribed to patients already identified with hyperhomocysteinemia, who may constitute a higher-risk subgroup. While we adjusted for folic acid use in our multivariate models, residual confounding by indication may persist, as the binary variable may not fully capture the severity of the condition that prompted treatment. This bias could attenuate the observed association between Hcy and MACE. Finally, multicenter, large-sample prospective cohort studies are required to confirm our findings.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort, Hcy may be independently associated with an increased risk of MACE in postmenopausal women with established CHD. These findings may suggest that Hcy could serve as a prognostic biomarker in this high-risk population.
