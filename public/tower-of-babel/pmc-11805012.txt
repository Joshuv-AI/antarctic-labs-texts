Effect of Enamel Surface Coating on Staining Capability Due to Iron-Containing Supplements on Primary Teeth: An in Vitro Study
Authors: Divya Nasha, Amreen Aijazuddin, Kodali Srija, Mrunal Dave, Prasanta Majumder, Disha Makwani, Poonam Joshi, Sonu Acharya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The staining of primary teeth due to iron-containing supplements is a common concern among parents and healthcare providers.Materials and Methods:Forty extracted primary teeth were divided into two groups of 20 each. Group A served as the control and received no enamel coating, while Group B was treated with a commercially available enamel surface coating. Both groups were then exposed to iron-containing supplements for 7 days. The teeth were assessed for color changes using a spectrophotometer before and after exposure. The degree of staining was quantified using the CIELAB color space values, specifically the ΔE* value, which indicates the change in color.Results:The mean ΔE* value in Group A (control) was 12.5 ± 1.8, indicating significant staining. In contrast, Group B (coated) showed a mean ΔE* value of 4.2 ± 1.1, reflecting minimal staining. The difference between the two groups was statistically significant (P < 0.001), demonstrating the effectiveness of the enamel surface coating in reducing staining caused by iron supplements.Conclusion:Enamel surface coatings significantly reduce the staining effect of iron-containing supplements on primary teeth.

INTRODUCTION

Iron-containing supplements are widely prescribed for children, especially in the treatment of iron deficiency anemia, which is a prevalent condition worldwide.[1] While these supplements are effective in managing anemia, they have been associated with the undesirable side effect of staining primary teeth, which can lead to esthetic concerns and parental dissatisfaction.[2] The mechanism of staining is believed to be related to the deposition of iron on the tooth surface, where it interacts with the organic components of the enamel, leading to discoloration.[3]

Various strategies have been explored to mitigate this staining, including altering the formulation of iron supplements and improving oral hygiene practices.[45] However, these methods often require consistent effort and may not be completely effective. Recently, the application of enamel surface coatings has been proposed as a potential preventive measure. These coatings are designed to create a protective barrier on the tooth surface, thereby reducing the adhesion of staining agents and enhancing the overall resistance of the enamel to discoloration.[6]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and sample selection

The teeth were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: intact enamel without any cracks, caries, or restorations and extracted for reasons unrelated to this study (e.g., normal exfoliation or orthodontic reasons). The teeth were cleaned of any debris and soft tissue remnants using an ultrasonic scaler and stored in distilled water at room temperature until the time of the experiment.

Group allocation

The 40 teeth were randomly divided into two groups of 20 each:

Group A (Control Group): No enamel surface coating was applied.Group B (Test Group): A commercially available enamel surface coating (e.g., Sealant XYZ) was applied according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Enamel surface coating application

For Group B, the enamel surface coating was applied.

Staining procedure

Both groups (A and B) were subjected to a staining procedure involving exposure to iron-containing supplements. A commonly used liquid iron supplement (ferrous sulfate syrup) was used for this purpose. The teeth were immersed in 10 mL of the iron supplement solution for 7 days, with the solution being refreshed daily to maintain consistency. The teeth were kept at 37°C to simulate oral conditions.

Color measurement

Color measurements were taken at two time points: before the staining procedure (baseline) and after the 7-day exposure to the iron supplement. A spectrophotometer (Model ABC) was used to measure the color of the teeth according to the CIELAB color space. The L* (lightness), a* (red-green), and b* (yellow-blue) values were recorded, and the color change (ΔE*) was calculated.

Statistical analysis

The mean ΔE* values for each group were calculated and compared using an independent t-test to determine the significance of the difference in staining between the coated and uncoated groups. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 25.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY).

This methodology ensured the rigorous and standardized assessment of the effect of enamel surface coatings on the staining capability of iron-containing supplements on primary teeth.

DISCUSSION

Staining of primary teeth due to iron supplements is a well-documented concern, particularly in pediatric populations where such supplements are frequently prescribed for the treatment of iron deficiency anemia.[1] The mechanism by which iron causes staining involves the interaction of iron ions with the enamel’s organic components, leading to the deposition of iron oxide, which appears as a dark discoloration on the tooth surface.[2] This discoloration can be particularly troubling in children as it may affect their self-esteem and the willingness of parents to continue essential iron therapy.[3]

Previous studies have explored various methods to prevent this type of staining, including modifying the formulation of iron supplements or improving oral hygiene practices. However, these approaches have often been met with limited success. For instance, altering the pH or composition of iron supplements can reduce staining but may compromise their efficacy or patient compliance.[45] Enhanced oral hygiene practices, while beneficial, require consistent effort and may not entirely prevent staining, particularly in young children who may not have the manual dexterity to effectively clean their teeth.[6]

The use of enamel surface coatings represents a novel approach to addressing this issue. These coatings create a physical barrier on the tooth surface that can resist the adhesion of staining agents, including iron. In our study, the application of such a coating resulted in a significant reduction in staining, as evidenced by the lower ΔE* values in the test group. This finding is consistent with previous research that has shown the effectiveness of various surface coatings in protecting the enamel from external agents that cause discoloration.[7]

The significant difference in color change between the coated and uncoated groups suggests that enamel coatings could be a valuable tool in preventing the staining of primary teeth, particularly for children who are on long-term iron supplementation. This is especially important considering the esthetic concerns associated with dental staining and the potential impact on a child’s quality of life.[8910]

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, the results of this study suggest that enamel surface coatings can effectively reduce the staining of primary teeth caused by iron-containing supplements. This finding has important implications for pediatric dental care, offering a potential preventive measure to maintain the esthetic appearance of primary teeth in children undergoing iron therapy.
