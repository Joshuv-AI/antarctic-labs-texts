Introduction to electrocardiogram signal quality assessment and estimated accuracy for textile electrodes
Authors: Neusa R. Adão Martins, Frederik Bauer, Florent Baty, Maximilian Boesch, Martin H. Brutsche, René M. Rossi, Simon Annaheim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As the use of wearable electrocardiogram (ECG) data for modeling purposes continues to rise, there is a pressing need for signal quality assessment (SQA) algorithms capable of identifying segments of signal from which reliable data can be obtained. Manually annotated ECG data, obtained through expert visual inspection, is often used as reference in the development of ECG SQA algorithms. In this approach, the quality of a signal segment is assessed based on the level of noise present. Yet, the data extracted from noise-corrupted ECG signal segments might still be of sufficient accuracy depending on the target application. The current work proposes a paradigm shift by presenting a SQA algorithm that performs template matching and physiological feasibility checks to determine the quality of ECG signals acquired by textile-based wearable systems. Signal segments were classified into four different quality classes based on the estimated accuracy of RR intervals extracted from the signal segments of each class. Our findings show that the proposed SQA algorithm is effective in identifying ECG signal segments from which accurate RR intervals can be derived, and that the proportion of the data across the different classes is sensitive to different factors known to have an effect on signal quality.

Introduction

The analysis of heart activity based on electrocardiogram (ECG) considering intervals and morphology features has been employed in a variety of settings. These include the detection of cardiac and non-cardiac1 diseases such as atrial fibrillation2, pulmonary embolism3, sleep apnea4, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease5,6. ECG analysis is relevant not only in cardiology, pulmonology and sleep medicine, but also in sports medicine and training control7 as well as drowsiness detection8. Features based on ventricular muscle activation (QRS wave), particularly heart rate and heart rate variability (HRV), have shown great promise in providing information about individuals’ physiological and psychological status. These features have been employed for emotion recognition9, fatigue monitoring10, and biometric identification11.

Continuous, long-term ECG monitoring in free-living environments significantly widens the scope of applications of ECG parameters. It paves the way for real-time monitoring of different conditions and the early detection of changes in physical, mental, and emotional states12. Conventional gel electrodes, however, are not ideal for long-term ECG monitoring as their use over prolonged periods of time results in reduced data quality and can give rise to irritative skin problems13. Consequently, numerous alternative wearable systems for long-term ECG monitoring—for example textile-based wearable sensors—have been proposed in the last decades14,15. Textile-based sensors are flexible, easily adjusting to specific body surface topographies, as well as permeable for air and moisture, thereby minimizing possible skin irritations during long-term use16,17. Their unobtrusive integration into garments provides a comfortable and seamless solution for long-term ECG monitoring in unattended settings18,19, thus having great potential in harnessing big data20. Wearable-based ECG monitoring expands the continuous monitoring of patients towards outpatient/real-world settings. It facilitates the timely identification of any changes in individual data trends that may go unnoticed in short-term assessments. This opens new avenues for early diagnosis, personalized treatment, and optimized therapeutic interventions, ultimately improving patient care.

However, wearable systems, allowing for unattended application and offering ease of use, are more susceptible to noise and artifacts than conventional gel electrodes21–23. On one hand, electrode-skin contact is more unstable in wearable systems due to the application of the electrodes to unprepared skin or the utilization of dry electrodes. On the other hand, when monitoring persons during everyday life, motion is inevitably part of the monitoring sequence and potentially affects it. Detecting and, where possible, correcting signal noise and artifacts is, therefore, crucial in the context of wearable ECG monitoring24.

Several algorithms were proposed to determine the quality of ECG signals24–26. The developed algorithms usually follow a three-stage approach consisting of signal preprocessing, feature extraction, and signal quality classification. The classification is done based on heuristic rules or machine learning. The need for more efficient and accurate data labeling methods, as well as the dependency of algorithm performance on the validation dataset and data acquisition system, are some of the challenges faced in this research field27,28.

Manually annotated ECG data, obtained through expert visual inspection, is often used as reference for validating signal-quality assessment (SQA) algorithms. However, data labeling through expert annotation is time-consuming and requires a high level of knowledge. In this approach, the quality of a signal segment is assessed based on the level of noise present. However, the data derived from ECG segments corrupted by noise might still achieve a level of accuracy that is acceptable for the intended purpose.

While noise-free data may be required for morphological analysis of the ECG signal where all waves should be clearly identifiable (diagnostic quality26), accurate RR intervals can still be derived from data with noise present. Thus, describing the signal quality in terms of the accuracy of the data that can be derived from it might be more relevant as it sets the ground for further use of the data. The quality of acquired signals will ultimately determine the type, accuracy and reliability of the extracted information29–31. This, in turn, will determine if the data is suitable for complex applications like morphological analysis or simpler ones such as heart rate estimation. Explaining signal quality in relation to the data accuracy also prevents additional data loss by ensuring that all pertinent data is used for its intended purpose, as not only the noise-free data can be deemed high-quality and retained for further analysis.

The use of wearable technology for long-term ECG monitoring requires the development of automated SQA algorithms of low computational cost, which assess the ECG signal in real time24,25,28. An easy-to-implement solution is template matching, a trend-based approach that assesses signal quality based on the regularity of the ECG signals27,32,33. ECG signal regularity is measured by calculating the correlation between individual QRS complexes and the average of all detected QRS complexes (template) in each signal segment.

This study investigated the development of an SQA algorithm considering the accuracy of RR intervals estimated from the acquired signal as the reference for signal quality, replacing the need for visual inspection and allowing the estimation of the estimated accuracy of data of each quality class. To this end, a template matching-based method was used to assess ECG signal quality. The devised algorithm considers the entire PQRST complex for SQA and combines it with a physiological feasibility assessment. The algorithm is tested in three datasets containing ECG signals acquired by a textile-based wearable system, both in inpatient and outpatient settings. The impact of various factors affecting data quality on the distribution of data across quality classes is also investigated.

Methods

The proposed ECG signal quality algorithm classifies 15-second segments into four distinct quality classes. The classes are represented by integers from 1 to 4, with 1 indicating the highest, and 4 indicating the lowest quality. As quality descriptive parameters, the Pearson correlation coefficient between the individual QRS and PQRST complexes and the plausibility of the timing of the detected R waves are used. The procedure relies on the positions of the R waves, which are detected prior to the SQA algorithm using an in-house algorithm described in Supplementary Material (see Appendices A.1-A.3 and Supplementary Figures 1–3 for algorithm details, and Appendix A.4 and Supplementary Table 1 for performance evaluation). The unprocessed input signal is split into segments of 15 seconds with an additional overlap of 1 second at the beginning and end of each segment. The duration of 15 seconds was selected due to its balance between time resolution and the robustness of the resulting QRS and PQRST templates. Datasets

Data from three different datasets were utilized to develop and examine the proposed signal quality index (SQI). These datasets are detailed in the following sections.

Dataset 1

In our study, we used a dataset obtained from a prior publication by Fontana et al.38, referred to as Dataset 1. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Ethics Committee East Switzerland (EKOS 15/140), and all patients gave their written informed consent to participate in the study. This dataset comprises 242 overnight ECG recordings from patients undergoing polysomnography (PSG) for suspected sleep apnea. The ECG recordings were conducted over an average duration of 6.4 ± 0.4 hours at a sampling rate of 200 Hz, resulting in 377’000 15-second segments for analysis in total. The data collection involved the use of gel electrodes (Ambu BlueSensor N, Ambu GmbH, Bad Nauheim, Germany) and a novel textile-based wearable ECG monitoring system38,39 applied in the chest region (ECG monitoring belt), concurrently.

Dataset 2

Dataset 2 comprises 52 patients with suspected sleep apnea, who were assigned for PSG, as previously reported by Baty et al.40. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Ethics Committee East Switzerland (EKOS 19/038), and all patients gave their written informed consent to participate in the study. Patient characteristics are detailed in Table 3. The dataset was acquired for a clinical study to investigate the ability to predict the severity of sleep apnea based on ECG and breathing data following a multi-parameter approach. For that reason, the monitoring belt was complemented by optical fiber-based sensors41 for breath analysis (multi-parameter monitoring belt).

The ECG data was recorded overnight, both in a supervised hospital setting at the Cantonal Hospital St. Gallen (night duration 6.2 ± 0.8 hours), Switzerland, and in an unsupervised home setting (night duration 8.0 ± 2.4 hours). The ECG signals were collected using the multi-parameter monitoring belt at a sampling rate of 1 kHz with a Bittium Faros 180 (Bittium Corporation, Oulu, Finland) data logger placed at the sternum. This device also captured 3-axis acceleration data simultaneously at a sampling rate of 25 Hz. In the hospital setting, ECG was additionally recorded by the PSG system using conventional gel electrodes (Ambu BlueSensor N, Ambu GmbH, Bad Nauheim, Germany) at a sampling rate of 200 Hz.

Due to missing or corrupted data, 8 subjects had to be excluded, leaving a total of 44 patients for analysis. Consequently, Dataset 2 included 42 overnight recordings from the hospital (collected from 42 patients) and 79 recordings from home settings (collected from 32 patients), comprising single-night recordings (n = 5), two-night recordings (n = 12), three-night recordings (n = 10), and four-night recordings (n = 5).

Dataset 3

Dataset 3 comprises recordings from 10 subjects engaging in a series of resting and moving activities over 47 minutes. Estimated accuracy of RR interval measurement

To assess the accuracy of RR interval measurements, we compared the RR intervals extracted from 15-second signal segments of various quality classes with the corresponding reference values. We utilized Dataset 1, which comprises data collected concurrently by the ECG monitoring belt and conventional gel electrodes.

A reference dataset was created by selecting Class 1 segments from the gel electrodes data using the SQA algorithm. The average and standard deviation of the RR intervals were calculated for each segment and compared to the corresponding values from the ECG monitoring belt data recorded during the same time frame. Subsequently, each segment of the ECG signal from the ECG monitoring belt was classified as either good or bad depending on the agreement of the average and standard deviation of the RR intervals. A segment was considered to provide an equal estimate of the RR intervals’ average and standard deviation if the difference was less than 40 ms for the average and 60 ms for the standard deviation when compared to the reference values (referred to as the equality criteria). The equality criteria is based on the gold standard for the temporal tolerance for QRS complex detection (150 ms according to ANSI recommendation42,43, see Supplementary Material, Appendix A.6 for details on threshold calculation).

The differences in the average RR interval and standard deviation of RR interval estimates were calculated for each single quality class obtained from the ECG monitoring belt when compared to Class 1 reference data from the gel electrodes. The mean difference in the RR intervals’ average and standard deviation was computed, along with the percentage of segments from the ECG monitoring belt allocated to the respective quality class, including the 95% confidence intervals. The confidence intervals were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method, recognized for its high accuracy and reliability44,45. The calculations were performed on a balanced version of Dataset 1, in which an equal number of good segments (segments meeting the equality criteria) and bad segments (segments failing to meet the equality criteria) were included. After balancing, the dataset contained 96’000 15-second segments.

Impact of data quality on data distribution across quality classes

The impact of various factors on data quality and its attribution to the quality classes was investigated based on data from Datasets 1, 2 and 3 for reference gel electrodes as well as the monitoring belt. Five key factors were considered: the setting in which the patient monitoring took place (inpatient and outpatient setting); the type of activities and movement; the frequency of use; and the severity of sleep apnea.

Impact of the monitoring setting

Dataset 2 was utilized to study how the monitoring setting affects data distribution in the quality classes. The percentage of data in classes 1 to 4 was compared between data collected in inpatient settings, i.e., the sleep lab, and data collected at home (outpatient settings). The statistical significance of the differences was assessed using the Wilcoxon signed rank test. The test results include the number of observations (n), the Z score (Z), p-value (p), and the effect size estimate r.

Influence of body position and movement

The impact of body position and movement on the quality of the ECG signal was examined by analyzing the acceleration data from Dataset 2. Patients’ body position for each signal segment was determined using the 3-axis acceleration data. As the data was collected while patients were sleeping, only sleeping positions were considered, i.e., lying supine, prone, left or right. For all positions, a tolerance of 45\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document} was allowed38. The percentage of data in classes 1 to 4 was compared between data collected while subjects were lying in different positions using the Friedman test. The chi-squared statistic, with the degrees of freedom represented as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2_W$$\end{document}(df), the p-value, and the effect size estimate Kendall’s W (W) are provided. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed rank test with Bonferroni correction.

Movement was measured by computing the magnitude of the first derivative of the acceleration over time, known as jerk46(unit: mg/s where g refers to earth’s gravitational acceleration, s to seconds and m to the factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-3}$$\end{document}). This measure indicates the magnitude of patients’ movement during the ECG recording. For the analysis, the average jerk of the 15-second segments was used and the baseline noise (i.e., jerk recorded by a resting sensor) was subtracted. After that, the data was arbitrarily grouped into bins with a width of 50 mg/s. The amount of segments in each quality class was then calculated for every bin.

The impact of movement on signal quality was further explored in a dataset of healthy subjects (Dataset 3). The data proportions in classes 1 to 4 were calculated and compared using the Wilcoxon signed rank test, for both when participants were at rest and when they were engaged in physical activity.

Influence of frequency of use of the monitoring belts

Repeated usage of monitoring belts can result in a reduction in signal quality38. An analysis was carried out on Dataset 1 to investigate how usage affects the amount of Class 4 data. Dataset 1 contains ECG signals collected by 16 different ECG monitoring belts used multiple times. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to assess the effect of usage on Class 4 data. The test results include the Kruskal–Wallis H-test statistic (H) and corresponding p-value. Furthermore, a correlation analysis was conducted. Spearman’s rho (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document}), along with the significance level, are reported.

Influence of disease severity

For the investigation of the influence of the severity of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) on signal quality, Dataset 1 was used. In the analysis, patients were divided into four groups according to their OSA severity using the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI): none (AHI < 5), mild (AHI > 5 and AHI < 15), moderate (AHI > 15 and AHI < 30), and severe (AHI > 30)47,48. The data proportions in each quality class were compared among the four groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Additionally, correlation analysis was conducted.

Data processing and statistical analysis tools

Data processing was done using MATLAB R2022b/R2023b (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA), and the statistical analyses were conducted with R (version 4.4.1; R Core Team, 2024). Since the data did not follow a normal distribution, only nonparametric tests were used. The nominal significance level was set to 0.05. Unless otherwise stated, data are represented as the median (interquartile range, IQR).

Discussion

The current work presented a SQA algorithm that performs template matching and physiological plausibility checks to determine the quality of the signal within 15-second segments. Each segment was assigned to one of four different quality classes. These quality classes were not characterized in terms of how clean the signal is, as determined by visual inspection, but rather based on the estimated accuracy of data extracted from signal segments of each quality class. The findings show that the proposed SQA algorithm is effective in identifying ECG signal segments from which accurate RR intervals can be derived and that the proportion of the data across the different classes is sensitive to different factors known to have an effect on signal quality.

While highly accurate RR intervals can be estimated from Class 1 and Class 2 signal segments (> 99% of segments in these classes meet the equality criteria when compared to class 1 data obtained from reference gel electrodes, see Table 4), segments assigned to Class 4 should be discarded for further analysis as only 1.02% of segments in quality Class 4 provide accurate estimates. This makes Class 1 and 2 data suitable for applications requiring extremely accurate RR intervals estimation and Class 4 data not recommended for any further use. Class 3 data could be suitable for tasks that do not demand high levels of accuracy (proportion of equal results > 85% in a balanced dataset).

Close assessment of the influence of several factors on the distribution of data within the four classes showed that the proposed algorithm is sensitive to changes in signal quality associated with the monitoring system and set-up as well as to user behavior and health condition. Overall, results show that the use of a wearable system, as the ECG monitoring belt, in an inpatient setting led to a higher percentage of Class 2 and Class 4 data and a lower amount of Class 1 data when compared to gel electrodes (Fig. 4). Although those differences were associated with large effect sizes (r > 0.5), in practice only 1% to 29% (median = 3.02%) of the data acquired by the ECG monitoring belt would have to be discarded as most of the data still falls into classes 1 to 3.

The change of monitoring setting to outpatient widens the IQR of Class 4 data to span from 6 to 40% (median = 16.51%). In unattended settings, the proper attachment of sensors cannot be verified, as patients are responsible for handling the devices themselves. Moreover, sensors in such environments are more vulnerable to environmental factors, such as electromagnetic interference. These combined factors can result in a degradation of signal quality49,50. This issue can be exacerbated by the presence of additional sources of noise in home environments, particularly motion artifacts. Since several physiological parameters are collected during PSG, patients have limited mobility during that assessment. This is not the case in outpatient settings, where patients can move freely. The analysis in patients and healthy subjects (Fig. 6) confirms that a decrease in movement stability (jerk up to 50 mg/s, equivalent to activities such as bicycle riding) results in a reduction of Class 1 data and an increase in Class 4 data. A further increase in jerk up to 150 mg/s, such as during walking, leads to an additional decrease in Class 1 and Class 2 data, which is initially offset by an increase in Class 3 data. However, this only occurs up to jerk levels of around 300 mg/s, which, according to our investigations, is equivalent to intense body movement (e.g., running, doing squats, or jumping jacks).

Body position during sleeping was found to have a small effect on the proportion of data in all quality classes for the multi-parameter monitoring belt (see Fig. 5) but only a negligible effect on data from the gel electrodes. It makes sense, as changes in body position can lead to disruption of the contact between the electrodes and the skin through mechanical deformation of the textile belt, and that’s not the case for the gel electrodes attached to the skin.

Frequent use of monitoring belts can result in sensor degradation, driven by factors such as physical wear, repeated washing or cleaning cycles, and other external influences. In agreement with the findings of Fontana et al.38, it has been observed that the amount of Class 4 data increases with multiple uses of the ECG monitoring belt (Fig. 7). The association found is significant but weak (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document} = 0.28, p < 0.001), likely due to numerous other factors contributing to the link between belt usage and signal quality39.

Finally, OSA severity was found to have an impact on the amount of data in classes 1 and 2 for the gel electrodes and Class 3 for the ECG monitoring belt (Table 7). Increasing AHI is weakly associated with less Class 1 data (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document} = -0.21) and more Class 2 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document} = 0.21) and Class 4 data (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document} = 0.16). Those associations were found on the gel electrodes data but not on the ECG monitoring belt data. Sleep apnea has been shown to increase the propensity to arrhythmias51,52. Given the plausibility criteria contemplated in the proposed SQI, signal segments containing arrhythmic beats are directly assigned to Class 4. Besides, sleep apnea has also been associated with atypical motion during sleep53,54, which might have affected signal quality.

In spite of being implemented in three different datasets, the data used to develop the SQA algorithm was acquired using the same system while subjects were mainly asleep. Furthermore, the estimated accuracy in RR intervals measurement was investigated taking Class 1 gel electrode data as reference. Although template matching and plausibility check have been commonly used for the assessment of the quality of ECG signals, and gel electrodes are the gold standard for ECG monitoring, there is also an instrument error associated with the estimation of RR interval based on Class 1 signal segments. In addition, due to the design of the plausibility check, abnormal beats are automatically identified as outliers. Consequently, the algorithm may have limited applicability in patient populations with frequent arrhythmias or other pathophysiological rhythms.

Also, data from the multi-parameter monitoring belt has been used to investigate the influence of body posture on signal quality. However, as Table 5 reveals, patients spent very little time lying on their right side. This is most likely because the device logger was placed on the right side, leading patients to avoid lying right. All these aspects do affect the presented results and their generalizability. Considering the signal quality requirements of the proposed SQA algorithm (Table 1, it can be assumed that Class 1 and 2 data are of diagnostic quality and, therefore, information regarding other ECG intervals and segments can be accurately extracted from it. While only RR intervals were considered in this analysis further work needs to be conducted to investigate the performance of the current system in estimating other ECG features. In addition, comparisons with expert visual inspection should be conducted to validate the algorithm, along with assessments of its impact on downstream applications such as model development and clinical diagnosis. The estimated accuracy of each quality class also warrants further investigation, particularly when applying the algorithm to data from other wearable systems.

A plethora of research has been done in SQA for ECG signal. However, the state-of-the-art is still to develop and validate the algorithms taking labelling through expert visual inspection as the reference. Considering the increasing use of wearable based data for modelling purposes, we propose in this work to set the focus not only on the level of noise present in the signals but also on the accuracy of the data that can be derived from those signals, as it will affect the further analysis of the data, as shown by Petelczyc et al. 55 for heart rate and HRV analysis. Having SQA algorithms which can identify signal segments from which accurate data can be extracted has benefited from a clinical perspective, as it ensures the maximal use of data with appropriate accuracy for diagnostics. From a sensor development perspective, it provides better understanding of monitoring systems behavior. Due to the gap in the literature in this regard, it is still unclear what is the minimal amount of data and accuracy required for different uses and applications of RR intervals (e.g., heart rate monitoring for emotion status monitoring or heart rate monitoring for arrhythmia detection), but these gaps should be filled as more research is conducted in this area.

Conclusion

In conclusion, numerous factors—the monitoring system, the monitoring environment, the individual’s health condition, body posture, level of activity, sensor usage and handling, as seen in this work—have an influence on the quality of the acquired signal. While the use of well-validated systems is a first step to obtaining high-fidelity data, it does not suffice, as gold standard systems can still be affected by those factors as well. Our results clearly show that gel electrodes can also acquire Class 4 data. On the other hand, depending on the level of noise corruption, different types of information can still be derived from a given signal, depending on the target use of this information. Consequently, the development of SQA algorithms which describe signal quality based on the accuracy and not only on the cleanness of the data would be an important paradigm shift in the field of SQA algorithms, particularly for wearable sensor validation and model development.
