GNL2 plays a critical role in avian reovirus p17-mediated host cellular autophagy and promotes viral proliferation
Authors: Xuechao Tan, Xinzhuo Zhang, Xiong Jiang, Yangyan Ou, Xinyu Liu, Chengcheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Avian reovirus (ARV) is a major pathogen causing viral arthritis, tenosynovitis, malabsorption syndrome, and immunosuppression in chickens, leading to severe economic losses in the global poultry industry. The nonstructural protein p17 is vital for ARV replication and modulates diverse host signaling pathways by interacting with multiple host factors. In our previous work, nucleolar GTP‑binding protein 2 (GNL2), a conserved nucleolar GTPase governing 60S ribosomal subunit maturation and nucleocytoplasmic transport, was screened as a potential binding partner of ARV p17 using yeast two‑hybrid assay. In the present study, the physical interaction between p17 and GNL2 was validated by coimmunoprecipitation (Co‑IP), glutathione S‑transferase (GST) pull‑down, and laser confocal microscopy. Mechanistically, the central region of p17 (amino acids 19–61) directly binds to the GTPase domain of GNL2. Notably, ARV infection or ectopic expression of p17 significantly upregulates GNL2 expression. Functional assays revealed that GNL2 overexpression markedly promotes ARV replication at both RNA and protein levels, whereas GNL2 knockdown remarkably restricts viral proliferation. Furthermore, GNL2 enhances p17‑mediated cellular autophagosome formation, thereby constructing a favorable intracellular milieu for efficient ARV propagation. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GNL2 serves as a key host dependency factor hijacked by ARV p17 to remodel host cellular functions. This study provides new insights into the molecular pathogenesis of ARV and offers potential targets for developing antiviral strategies against ARV infection.

Introduction

Avian reovirus (ARV), a member of the Orthoreovirus genus within the Reoviridae family, is a major etiological agent responsible for multiple clinical syndromes in chickens, including viral arthritis/tenosynovitis, malabsorption syndrome, runting-stunting syndrome, and severe immunosuppression (Liu et al., 2023). ARV infection causes atrophy of immune organs, inflammatory cell infiltration, and increased susceptibility to secondary infections, leading to considerable economic losses worldwide. The ARV genome comprises 10 segments of double-stranded RNA, encoding 12 functional structural and nonstructural proteins. Among them, the nonstructural protein p17 is a multifunctional regulator that dynamically shuttles between the nucleus and cytoplasm. ARV p17 hijacks various host factors to trigger host translation shutoff, cell cycle arrest, autophagy induction, and innate immune suppression, all of which facilitate viral replication and pathogenesis(Li, et al., 2022; Nouda, et al., 2022; Tang, et al., 2022). The ARV p17 protein shuttles between the nucleus and cytoplasm(Costas, et al., 2005; Hung-Chuan et al., 2019), modulating multiple cellular signaling pathways(Chi, et al., 2013; Huang, et al., 2015; Wei-Ru, et al., 2017) and leading to translation shutdown (Huang et al., 2015), cell cycle retardation (Chiu, et al., 2018; Liu, et al., 2005), and autophagosome formation(Chi et al., 2013; Wei-Ru et al., 2017), all of which enhance viral replication. Recently, our team has demonstrated that the p17-modulated molecular chaperone Hsp90/Cdc37 enhances virus replication by protecting viral proteins (i.e., σA, σC, and σNS) from ubiquitin-proteasome degradation(Huang, et al., 2022b, 2022c). However, the complete repertoire of host interactors and the underlying mechanisms by which p17 remodels the host cellular environment remain incompletely understood.

Viruses depend on host cells for survival, utilizing cellular mechanisms for gene replication and viral particle assembly. Despite notable advancements in research on viral regulation of host cell mechanisms, numerous issues are still unanswered. Nucleolar GTP-binding proteins are evolutionarily conserved regulators of ribosome biogenesis, nucleocytoplasmic transport, cell cycle progression, and stress responses(Koji, et al., 2021; Paridaen, et al., 2011). GNL2 (also named NGP-1 or Nug2) is a nucleolar-localized GTPase that functions as a critical checkpoint for the maturation and nuclear export of the 60S ribosomal subunit(Chairini C, et al., 2026; Huang, et al., 2025). GNL2 ensures accurate pre-rRNA processing and ribosome assembly before the nuclear export of mature ribosomes. Accumulating evidence indicates that GNL2 is frequently targeted and hijacked by diverse viruses to suppress host translation and prioritize viral mRNA translation(Chairini C et al., 2026). However, the involvement of GNL2 in ARV infection and its functional interplay with p17 have not been reported.

In this study, we identified GNL2 as a novel host interactor of ARV p17. We confirmed their direct interaction, mapped the precise binding domains, and revealed that p17 upregulates and redistributes GNL2 from the nucleolus to the cytoplasm. Functional experiments demonstrated that GNL2 promotes ARV replication by enhancing p17-mediated host translation inhibition, cell cycle arrest, and autophagy. Collectively, our findings uncover a novel mechanism by which ARV p17 subverts the nucleolar GTPase GNL2 to remodel host cellular functions for optimal viral proliferation, providing new perspectives for antiviral intervention against ARV.

Materials and methods

Cells, virus, and reagents

DF-1, Vero, and HEK293T cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. The pcDNA3.1 expression plasmid was purchased from ThermoFisher (USA) and stored in our laboratory. Trans1-T1 receptor cells, Taq high-fidelity DNA polymerase, pEASY-T3 cloning vector, DNA T4 ligase, and high-glucose DMEM were obtained from TransGene Biotech (Beijing, China). DL Marker and DNA gel recovery kits were purchased from TaKaRa (Dalian, China). The Animal Tissue RNA Extraction Kit and Plasmid Extraction Kit were purchased from Kang Wei Century (Beijing, China). The immunoprecipitation kit and AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix were purchased from Novozymes Biotechnology Co. The mouse anti-Myc monoclonal antibody, rabbit anti-Flag monoclonal antibody, mouse anti-GAPDH monoclonal antibody and 40,6-Diamino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Beijing, China). Rabbit ARV p17 polyclonal antibody was homemade and stored in our lab. Restriction endonucleases Bam HI and Eco RI were purchased from NEB (USA). The ARV strain GX/2010/1 was isolated and preserved in our laboratory, propagated in primary chicken embryo fibroblast (CEF) cells, and titrated using TCID₅₀ assay. The full-length CDS of GNL2 and p17 was amplified from DF-1 cells and ARV genome, respectively, and cloned into pcDNA3.1-Flag, pEGFP-N1, and pGEX-4T-1 vectors to construct recombinant plasmids. Truncated mutants of p17 (aa 19-146, 61-146, 119-146) and GNL2 (GTPase domain, C-terminal domain) were generated using standard PCR-based cloning. All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing.

Yeast two-hybrid screening

Yeast two-hybrid assay was performed using the Matchmaker Gold Yeast Two-Hybrid System (Clontech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The full-length p17 was cloned into pGBKT7 as bait to screen a cDNA library derived from ARV-infected DF-1 cells. Positive colonies were selected on SD/-Leu/-Trp/-His/-Ade medium and identified by DNA sequencing.

Coimmunoprecipitation and western blotting

For Co-IP assay, 293T cells were cotransfected with pEGFP-p17 and pcDNA3.1-Flag-GNL2. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were lysed in Pierce IP lysis buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktail. Cell lysates were incubated with anti-Flag magnetic beads or anti-GFP antibody at 4°C overnight. After extensive washing, immunoprecipitates were eluted and subjected to SDS-PAGE. Cell lysates were prepared by treating them with Pierce IP lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40 and 5% glycerol) containing the protease inhibitor phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride as a protease inhibitor for 30 min on ice. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min to separate them from debris before further denaturation through boiling with 10×SDS-PAGE loading buffer for 5 min. Western blotting was performed using mouse anti-Flag, rabbit anti-GFP, rabbit anti-p17, and mouse anti-GAPDH antibodies, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system.

GST pulldown assay

For GST pulldown assay, GST-p17 and GST control proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) and purified using glutathione sepharose beads. Vero cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1-Flag-GNL2 to express Flag-GNL2. The purified GST or GST-p17 proteins were incubated with cell lysates containing Flag-GNL2 at 4°C for 4 h. After washing, bound proteins were eluted and detected by Western blotting using anti-Flag antibody.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy

HEK293T cells grown on coverslips were cotransfected with p17-EGFP and GNL2-Flag. At 24 h post-transfection, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin. Cells were then incubated with rabbit anti-Flag primary antibody and PE-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were captured using a laser confocal microscope (LSM510 META; Zeiss, Germany).

Overexpression and RNA interference

For overexpression, cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1-Flag-GNL2 or empty vector using TransIntro EL Transfection Reagent (TransGen). For RNA interference, three small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting GNL2 (siGNL2) and negative control siRNA (siNC) were synthesized by GenePharma. Cells were transfected with siRNAs at a final concentration of 50 nM. Knockdown efficiency was determined by qRT-PCR and Western blotting at 48 h post-transfection.

Viral replication analysis

Transfected cells were infected with ARV at an MOI of 1. At indicated time points, cell supernatants and lysates were collected. Viral genomic RNA levels were quantified by qRT-PCR. The expression levels of viral p17 protein were detected by Western blotting. Virus titers were determined by TCID₅₀ assay on DF-1 cells.

Transfection

Vero cells at 70% confluency were transfected with siRNA or Flag-GNL2 using TransIntro EL Transfection Reagent (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) in a 6-well plate. Cellular autophagy analysis

For autophagy analysis, the expression levels of LC3-I/II and Beclin 1 were detected by Western blotting. Autophagic vacuoles were observed under a transmission electron microscope.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme). qRT-PCR was performed using AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with β-actin as the internal reference. The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table 1.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was evaluated by the MTT method. Briefly, Vero cells were inoculated in 96-well culture plates and cultured at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After the cells were washed with PBS, a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL MTT dye was added to each well. 100 μL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals after incubation at 37°C for 4 h. Finally, the value of absorbance was detected by a microtiter plate reader (SpectraMax 190, Molecular Device, USA) at a wavelength of 570 nm.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least two independent biological replicates with consistent results. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison post-hoc test was used to analyze statistical differences among groups via GraphPad Prism 5 software. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

ARV p17 is a multifunctional nonstructural protein that hijacks host cellular machineries to establish a pro-viral microenvironment. In this study, we identified the nucleolar GTPase GNL2 as a novel host interactor of ARV p17. GNL2 is a key regulator of 60S ribosomal subunit maturation and nucleocytoplasmic transport, which is critical for cellular protein synthesis and homeostasis (Huang et al., 2025; Koji et al., 2021). We demonstrated that p17 directly binds GNL2 via its central region (aa 19-61) and targets the GTPase domain of GNL2. ARV infection or p17 expression upregulates GNL2 expression and redistributes GNL2 from the nucleolus to the cytoplasm.

Our results demonstrate that GNL2 directly interacts with ARV p17 and acts as a critical host factor promoting viral proliferation. The physical interaction between GNL2 and p17 was confirmed by Co-IP, GST pull-down, and confocal microscopy, with the central region of p17 binding to the GTPase domain of GNL2. ARV infection and p17 expression significantly upregulate GNL2 expression. Functionally, overexpression of GNL2 enhances ARV replication at RNA and protein levels, whereas GNL2 knockdown suppresses viral proliferation. Moreover, GNL2 strengthens p17-mediated cellular autophagy, creating a favorable environment for viral growth. These findings indicate that GNL2 is hijacked by ARV p17 to support viral replication, providing a new target for anti-ARV strategies.

Notably, GNL2 normally resides in the nucleolus and functions in ribosome biogenesis(Pengfei, et al., 2025). As we all known, that p17 protein participate in the cellular replication, by the interaction with GNL2, cytoplasmic GNL2 may act as a scaffold to stabilize p17 and promote the formation of viral replication complexes. Autophagy may generate a cellular environment that facilitates survival in various ways. Many viruses have been reported to regulate autophagy to facilitate their replication(Hongmei, et al., 2026; Seungwha, et al., 2026; Yanchao, et al., 2026). In addition to its role in normal cellular metabolism and waste removal, autophagy is vital for providing protection in contexts such as pathogen infections and cellular stress(Lígia, et al., 2026). In most cells, four types of autophagy coexist: microautophagy, endosomal microautophagy, chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA), and macroautophagy (Katherine R and Daniel J, 2013). Notably, p17 protein plays a significant role in initiating autophagy through signaling pathways including p53-PTEN-mTORCL, AMPK, and PKR/eIF2 to facilitate viral replication(Huang, et al., 2022a; Li, et al., 2022). In the current study, we found that ARV p17 induce cellular autophagy can be enlarged by GNL2 protein. This discovery may suggest that the complex interactions between the virus and the host cell might be mediated through multiple host proteins and viral protein interactions.

This study expands our understanding of the molecular pathogenesis of ARV and the functional landscape of p17-host interactions. Targeting the p17-GNL2 interaction or GNL2 GTPase activity may represent a promising broad-spectrum antiviral strategy against ARV and other GNL2-hijacking viruses.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we demonstrate that the nucleolar GTPase GNL2 is a critical host dependency factor hijacked by ARV nonstructural protein p17. The p17-GNL2 interaction upregulates GNL2 expression and redistributes GNL2 from the nucleolus to the cytoplasm. GNL2 promotes ARV replication by enhancing p17-mediated host translation inhibition and autophagy. This study provides novel insights into ARV pathogenesis and offers potential targets for antiviral intervention.
