The Impact of Rosenthal Effect-Based Psychological Nursing on Psychological State, Self-Efficacy, and Quality of Life in Patients Undergoing Total Hysterectomy for Uterine Fibroids: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Juan Xiang, Caihong Li, Xiaoqing Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients undergoing hysterectomy for uterine fibroids often experience significant psychological distress that impairs recovery and quality of life. While psychological support is recognized as important, structured and feasible nurse-led interventions remain underutilized.ObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy of a structured psychological nursing intervention based on the Rosenthal effect in reducing self-perceived burden and improving psychological state, self-efficacy, and quality of life in patients undergoing total hysterectomy for uterine fibroids.MethodsA prospective, single-blind, randomized controlled trial was conducted with 160 patients scheduled for total hysterectomy. Participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention group (n=80), which received two structured sessions of Rosenthal effect-based psychological nursing in addition to routine care, or the control group (n=80), which received routine care only. The primary outcome was the change in Self-Perceived Burden Scale (SPBS) scores from baseline to 3-month follow-up. Secondary outcomes included anxiety (SAS), depression (SDS), self-esteem (RSE), self-efficacy (GSES), quality of life (SF-36), physiological stress markers (serum cortisol, HRV), postoperative recovery indicators, and patient satisfaction (NSNS).ResultsThe intervention group showed a significantly greater reduction in SPBS scores over time compared to the control group (group × time interaction, p < 0.001). Significant improvements were also observed in secondary outcomes, including anxiety, self-esteem, self-efficacy, and quality of life at 3-month follow-up (all p < 0.01). Physiological stress markers (cortisol and HRV) improved more in the intervention group. The intervention group also demonstrated earlier ambulation, shorter hospital stays, lower pain scores, and higher nursing satisfaction and treatment adherence (all p < 0.05).ConclusionA Rosenthal effect-based psychological nursing intervention was associated with reduced self-perceived burden and more favorable psychological, physiological, and selected clinical recovery outcomes in patients undergoing hysterectomy for uterine fibroids. This feasible, nurse-led model may provide a practical strategy for strengthening expectation-oriented psychological support within routine perioperative nursing care.

Introduction

Uterine fibroids represent one of the most common benign tumors in women of reproductive age, and hysterectomy remains a definitive treatment for patients with severe or refractory symptoms.1 Beyond the physical burden of surgery, patients undergoing hysterectomy may be psychologically vulnerable because the procedure involves the irreversible removal of an organ closely associated with menstruation, fertility, body integrity, sexual identity, and perceptions of femininity.2,3 Recent evidence indicates that women may experience changes in self-perception, body image, a sense of organ loss, and negative attitudes toward menopause after hysterectomy, while symptomatic uterine fibroids themselves substantially impair mental health and quality of life.4,5 Therefore, perioperative psychological distress in this population should not be viewed merely as a general response to surgery, but as a condition shaped by disease symptoms, reproductive meaning, altered body image, and short-term postoperative dependency.

The surgical experience constitutes a major life stressor, frequently precipitating anxiety, depressive symptoms, fear of postoperative pain, uncertainty regarding recovery, and reduced confidence in self-care. Recent perioperative evidence suggests that structured psychological or psychoeducational approaches, including psychological counseling after hysterectomy, preoperative education, and non-pharmacological anxiety-reduction strategies, can improve anxiety, depression, satisfaction, and recovery-related outcomes.6–8 However, some interventions have limited postoperative durability or require specialized resources, indicating the need for a feasible intervention that can be embedded into routine perioperative nursing practice. Addressing psychological distress in hysterectomy patients is therefore not an ancillary concern but a critical component of holistic perioperative care.

Among the various psychological challenges, the concept of self-perceived burden of disease represents a profound and often overlooked aspect of the patient experience. This construct refers to a distressing emotional state in which individuals feel that they have become a burden to their family or caregivers, often accompanied by guilt, shame, and diminished self-worth.9 In chronic illness and oncology settings, a high self-perceived burden has been associated with poorer quality of life, increased depressive symptoms, and reduced treatment adherence.10 Although the dependency period after elective gynecological surgery is usually shorter than that in chronic disease, hysterectomy patients often require temporary assistance with mobility, pain control, wound care, household activities, and emotional adjustment. This acute dependence may reinforce feelings of guilt and perceived burden, particularly in patients who attach strong personal or family meaning to reproductive health and caregiving roles. However, self-perceived burden remains critically understudied within the short-term surgical recovery context.

Current perioperative nursing care often focuses on procedural information, physical preparation, pain management, and early mobilization, whereas psychological support is frequently delivered in an informal and non-standardized manner. Recent evidence supports the clinical value of nurse-led perioperative behavioral strategies: nurse-led preoperative visits can reduce presurgical anxiety by combining procedural education, emotional support, trust-building, and shared decision-making, while structured preoperative education has been associated with improvements in anxiety, depression, pain-related outcomes, satisfaction, and health-related quality of life.7,11 In patients undergoing hysterectomy, psychological intervention incorporated into postoperative nursing care has also been shown to improve anxiety and depressive symptoms and facilitate recovery-related outcomes.6 Nevertheless, existing interventions rarely target the deeper cognitive-emotional experience of feeling like a burden to family or caregivers. Consequently, there remains a need for a theory-driven, nurse-led intervention that is feasible for routine implementation and specifically addresses self-perceived burden during the hysterectomy recovery trajectory.

The Rosenthal effect, or expectation effect, offers a compelling theoretical foundation for such an intervention. Originating from educational psychology, this principle posits that expectations communicated by an authority figure can shape an individual’s performance, motivation, and self-perception.12 In clinical contexts, positive expectations conveyed by healthcare professionals may influence patient outcomes through cognitive, emotional, and psychobiological pathways that overlap with expectation-related placebo mechanisms.13,14 In this study, we hypothesized that positive expectations conveyed by nurses may help patients reinterpret temporary postoperative dependency as a normal and manageable stage of recovery, rather than as evidence that they have become a persistent burden to their family. Through repeated expectation-oriented communication, emotional validation, and recovery-focused behavioral guidance, the intervention was expected to strengthen self-efficacy, reduce negative cognitive appraisal, and ultimately alleviate self-perceived burden.

Although recent studies have examined perioperative psychological or educational interventions for surgical patients, and some have focused on anxiety, depression, or pelvic floor-related outcomes after hysterectomy, evidence remains limited regarding nurse-led, theory-based interventions that specifically target self-perceived burden as a primary psychological outcome in this population. To our knowledge, no randomized controlled trial has evaluated a Rosenthal effect-based psychological nursing intervention for reducing self-perceived burden among patients undergoing hysterectomy for uterine fibroids. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of this structured intervention on changes in Self-Perceived Burden Scale (SPBS) scores from baseline to 3 months after surgery. Secondary objectives included exploring the intervention’s impact on anxiety, depression, self-esteem, self-efficacy, quality of life, physiological stress markers, and clinical recovery indicators. We hypothesized that, compared with patients receiving standard care, those receiving the Rosenthal effect-based psychological nursing intervention would experience a significantly greater reduction in self-perceived burden and more favorable improvements in secondary psychological and clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study Design

This prospective, single-blind, randomized controlled trial (RCT) was conducted to evaluate the impact of a structured psychological nursing intervention based on the Rosenthal effect on the psychological state, self-efficacy, and quality of life in patients undergoing total hysterectomy for uterine fibroids. The study protocol was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration number: ChiCTR2600117425). A computer-generated randomization sequence was utilized to allocate eligible participants into either the control group or the intervention group, with allocation concealment achieved using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Ethical approval for this research was granted by the Ethics Committee of Yichang Central People’s Hospital (approval number: 2025–551-01). All participating patients provided written informed consent before their enrollment, in full compliance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Population

A total of 160 eligible patients diagnosed with uterine fibroids will be enrolled and randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group. Potential participants will be primarily recruited through the gynecological outpatient clinics. They will be provided with detailed information regarding the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits. Eligibility screening will be conducted by two trained research nurses against the pre-defined criteria. Written informed consent will be obtained from all eligible participants prior to their formal enrollment.

Inclusion Criteria: (1) A confirmed diagnosis of uterine fibroids based on clinical symptoms and imaging (ultrasound or MRI), classified according to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) classification system, and consistent with the Chinese expert consensus on the diagnosis and management of uterine fibroids;15 (2) Presence of surgical indications related to uterine fibroids and scheduled for a total hysterectomy; (3) Undergoing the first surgical treatment for uterine fibroids; (4) Aged between 35 and 55 years; Ability to comprehend the study content and provide voluntary written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Suspected or confirmed malignant lesions of the cervix or endometrium upon preoperative evaluation;15 (2) Comorbid severe cardiovascular, hepatic, renal, endocrine, or hematopoietic system diseases that contraindicate surgery; (3) Received hormonal therapy (eg, GnRH agonists) within three months prior to the study commencement; (4) Comorbid active infectious diseases such as tuberculosis, AIDS, or syphilis; (5) History of documented psychiatric disorders (eg, schizophrenia, major depressive disorder) or cognitive impairment that could interfere with the assessment of psychological interventions;16 (6) Concurrent participation in other systematic psychological therapy or intervention programs; (7) Current involvement in other clinical interventional trials; (8) Pregnancy or lactation; (9) Any other condition deemed by the investigators to compromise participant safety or data integrity.

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1). For the power analysis, a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) was conservatively chosen for the primary outcome. This convention is widely accepted in behavioral and health sciences for identifying a clinically meaningful difference between groups.17 The assumption of a medium effect is further substantiated by findings from a systematic review by Chen et al (2023), which concluded that psycho-educational and psychological interventions consistently show positive effects in reducing the self-perceived burden in patients.18 With a two-sided significance level (α) set at 0.05 and a desired statistical power (1-β) of 80%, the calculation indicated a minimum requirement of 64 participants per group for an independent samples t-test. To account for a potential attrition rate of approximately 20% over the 3-month follow-up period, the recruitment target will be set at 80 participants per group. Therefore, the total planned sample size for this study is 160 participants.

Randomization, Blinding, and Intervention

An independent statistician generated the allocation sequence through computer-based block randomization (block size = 4) to ensure balanced group assignment. Concealment was achieved using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Following informed consent and baseline assessments, participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the control group or the intervention group. Given the nature of the psychological nursing intervention, blinding of participants and the research nurses delivering the intervention is not feasible.19 Participants will be fully aware of the nature of the support they receive as part of the informed consent process, and the nurses must be aware of the protocol to deliver it correctly. However, to minimize detection and performance bias, outcome assessors and data analysts will be blinded to the participants’ group assignments throughout the study.20

Control Group (Routine Care)

Participants in the control group will receive the standard perioperative care provided by the hospital for patients undergoing total hysterectomy. This care is executed by the standard bedside nursing staff (RN). Upon admission, patients receive a standard introduction to the ward, hospital routines, and basic information about the preoperative schedule (eg, fasting times). This is followed by one session of verbal preoperative health education, supplemented with standard hospital leaflets, covering: (1) a general overview of the surgical process (ward to operating room to recovery); (2) basic information about general anesthesia; (3) assurance of postoperative pain management; and (4) general advice on postoperative recovery, such as deep breathing exercises and early mobilization. This routine care does not include structured, proactive psychological support or communication aimed at building positive expectancy.

Intervention Group (Rosenthal Effect-Based Psychological Nursing)

In addition to routine care, participants in the intervention group will receive a structured psychological nursing intervention based on the principles of the Rosenthal Effect (Positive Expectancy). The goal is to build a strong therapeutic relationship, provide emotional and informational support, and cultivate a positive expectation for surgery and recovery. This intervention will be executed by two registered nurses (RNs) with at least 5 years of clinical experience, who have undergone a standardized 2-day training program. The training focuses on therapeutic communication skills, the detailed intervention protocol, and strategies for fostering self-efficacy and positive expectancy.21 The intervention consists of two structured, face-to-face sessions, each lasting approximately 20–30 minutes.

The first session, conducted within 24 hours of admission (1–2 days before surgery), has the objective of establishing a trusting therapeutic relationship, assessing sources of anxiety, and actively building positive surgical expectations. Its communication content includes: (1) initiating a therapeutic dialogue, using active listening to understand the patient’s specific fears, concerns, and expectations; (2) providing clear, personalized information about the surgery to correct misconceptions and reduce fear of the unknown; and (3) applying the Rosenthal Effect by sharing anonymized success stories and positive recovery trajectories of previous patients, while framing the surgical team and nursing care in a highly positive and competent light. The second session, held on the evening before surgery, is designed to reinforce positive expectations, provide a tangible coping skill, and ensure the patient feels prepared and supported. During this session, the nurse will: (1) reiterate confidence in a positive outcome, emphasizing the patient’s own strength and ability to recover well; (2) review the postoperative plan, framing it as a manageable, step-by-step journey back to health; and (3) teach and practice a specific relaxation technique, such as Diaphragmatic (Abdominal) Breathing22 or Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR),23 providing the patient with a tool to manage acute anxiety. Participants will also be given an auxiliary leaflet summarizing the relaxation technique and key positive messages.

Intervention Fidelity

To ensure the intervention is delivered consistently and as intended, a multi-faceted approach to maintaining intervention fidelity will be implemented. This approach is anchored by a detailed intervention manual containing the protocol, key communication points, and a session checklist, which is used during a mandatory 2-day training for intervention nurses that includes role-playing and competency assessments. For execution monitoring, a random 20% of all intervention sessions will be audio-recorded (with participant consent) and subsequently reviewed independently by a senior nursing researcher against the checklist to assess adherence, dose, and quality of delivery. Participant receptiveness and engagement will be assessed post-intervention using a short questionnaire, such as a modified version of the Perioperative Patient-Reported Experience Measures (PREMs).24 Finally, to prevent protocol drift, weekly meetings will be held with the intervention nurses during the recruitment phase to discuss challenges, ensure consistency, and maintain uniformity in delivery.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome of this study is the change in the self-perceived burden of disease from baseline to the 3-month follow-up. This will be assessed using the SPBS. The 10-item Chinese version of the SPBS, validated by Wu Yanyan et al, will be used.25 This scale is composed of three dimensions: physical burden, economic burden, and emotional burden. It is rated on a Likert scale. The scale has demonstrated good psychometric properties in Chinese populations, with a total Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.950, and coefficients of 0.822, 0.916, and 0.893 for the physical, economic, and emotional burden dimensions, respectively. The structural validity is also sound, with three factors explaining 73.080% of the total variance.25 The scale was originally developed by Cousineau et al (2003).9 Higher scores indicate a heavier perceived burden.

The psychological state of the patients will be evaluated using two distinct scales. (1) Depression Level: The Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), developed by Zung (1965),26 will be used to assess the severity of depression before and after the intervention. This is a 20-item self-report scale rated on a 4-point Likert scale based on the frequency of symptoms in the past week. Scores are converted to an index score ranging from 25 to 100, with a cutoff score of 50 indicating clinically significant depressive symptoms. The Chinese version of the SDS has been widely validated and has shown good reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients reported to be between 0.70 and 0.92 in various Chinese populations.27,28 (2) Anxiety Level: The Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), also developed by Zung (1971),29 will be employed to evaluate anxiety levels. Similar to the SDS, the SAS contains 20 items and uses a 4-point Likert scale to produce a raw score (20–80) which is then converted to a standard score (25–100). A standard score of 50 or greater is indicative of clinically relevant anxiety. The Chinese version of the SAS has been validated and demonstrated good reliability with a Cronbach’s α of 0.82 and a content validity index of 0.88 in gynecological cancer patients.30

The Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSE), developed by Rosenberg (1965),31 will be utilized to assess patients’ self-esteem, which serves as an indicator of personal value perception, mental health, subjective well-being, and life satisfaction. This is a 10-item scale with 5 positively worded and 5 negatively worded items, rated on a 4-point Likert scale. Total scores range from 10 to 40, with higher scores reflecting higher self-esteem. The Chinese version of the RSE has shown good psychometric properties, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.83 to 0.89 and a test-retest reliability of 0.76 in various Chinese adult samples.32

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) will be assessed using the Medical Outcomes Study 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36). The SF-36 is a generic HRQoL instrument consisting of 36 items that yield scores across eight domains: physical functioning, role-physical, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role-emotional, and mental health. Each domain is scored from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating a better quality of life. This scale is a reliable and valid patient-oriented outcome assessment tool for evaluating postoperative outcomes in surgical patients.33 A follow-up assessment of HRQoL using the SF-36 will also be conducted 3 months post-surgery.

The incidence of in-hospital complications, specifically (1) bleeding, (2) infection, and (3) urinary retention, will be meticulously recorded and statistically compared between the two groups.

The Newcastle Satisfaction with Nursing Scales (NSNS), developed by Thomas et al (1996),34 will be administered to evaluate patient satisfaction with the nursing care provided. This instrument consists of two subscales: “Experience of Nursing Care” and “Satisfaction with Nursing Care”. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, where a higher score signifies greater satisfaction. The Chinese version of the NSNS has been validated in tertiary hospitals in China, demonstrating good construct validity and reliability for assessing patient satisfaction in the Chinese healthcare context.35

The General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) will be used to measure patients’ self-efficacy, defined as their beliefs in their ability to cope with various difficult demands in life. This 10-item scale, originally developed by Schwarzer & Jerusalem (1995),36 is rated on a 4-point scale. The total score ranges from 10 to 40, with a higher score indicating stronger self-efficacy. The Chinese version of the GSES has been shown to be a reliable and valid instrument, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.91 and 0.90 in samples of Hong Kong university students and Chinese adults with minor health problems, respectively.37

Several key postoperative recovery indicators will be recorded to assess the speed and quality of recovery. These include: (1) Time to first ambulation, the time from the end of surgery to the patient’s first time getting out of bed, will be recorded in hours. (2) Length of hospital stay, the total number of days from admission to discharge, will be calculated. (3) Pain Score (VAS), postoperative pain intensity will be assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) at 24 and 48 hours after surgery, as well as immediately prior to discharge.38 The VAS is a 100-mm horizontal line anchored by “no pain” at one end and “worst imaginable pain” at the other. Patients mark a point on the line that corresponds to their current pain level. Scores are classified as no pain (0–4 mm), mild pain (5–44 mm), moderate pain (45–74 mm), and severe pain (75–100 mm).

Physiological stress responses will be assessed through two main indicators: (1) Serum Cortisol Level, for which venous blood samples will be collected to measure serum cortisol levels before the intervention and prior to discharge; and (2) Heart Rate Variability (HRV), where HRV analysis will be conducted at the same time points as the cortisol measurements (pre-intervention and pre-discharge) to evaluate the autonomic nervous system function as an objective measure of physiological and psychological stress.

Finally, treatment adherence will be assessed using the Medication Adherence Report Scale (MARS), which in this context is used to evaluate patients’ adherence to the overall treatment and rehabilitation regimen. The MARS-5, a 5-item version, is commonly used. It is a self-report measure where patients respond on a 5-point Likert scale (from “Always” to “Never”). Total scores range from 5 to 25, with higher scores indicating better adherence.39 The scale was originally developed by Thompson et al (2000).40

Data Collection and Baseline Assessments

Following enrollment and prior to randomization, comprehensive baseline data will be collected from all participants through a combination of patient interviews, review of electronic medical records, self-reported questionnaires, and physiological measurements. This will include: (1) demographic characteristics such as age, gender, body mass index (BMI), educational level, marital status, and occupation; (2) clinical data encompassing primary diagnosis, disease duration, comorbidities (quantified using the Charlson Comorbidity Index41), history of previous surgeries, and long-term medication use; (3) surgery-related parameters including the specific type of hysterectomy performed, duration of surgery, anesthesia method, and estimated intraoperative blood loss; (4) baseline psychological status and self-efficacy as measured by the SPBS, SDS, SAS, RSE, GSES, and VAS scales; (5) physiological baseline measures, including serum cortisol level and heart rate variability (HRV); and (6) lifestyle factors and social context, including smoking history, alcohol consumption, and level of social support assessed using the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (PSSS),42 a 12-item instrument with good reliability (eg, Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93 for the total scale in a Chinese frontline worker sample) and established structural validity for assessing support from family, friends, and significant others.43

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses will be performed using SPSS Statistics software (Version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The primary analysis will be conducted based on the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, wherein all participants (n=80 per group) will be analyzed within the groups to which they were randomly allocated, regardless of protocol adherence or completion of the study. To manage missing data resulting from participant dropouts and to ensure the integrity of the ITT analysis, multiple imputation (MI) will be implemented for the primary and secondary outcome variables. Prior to analysis, the normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. Normally distributed continuous data will be presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Inter-group comparisons for these continuous outcomes at specific time points will be conducted using independent samples t-tests. For continuous data measured at multiple time points (eg, baseline, post-intervention, 3-month follow-up), intra-group and inter-group comparisons will be performed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA). The assumption of sphericity for RM-ANOVA was evaluated using Mauchly’s test; if the assumption was violated (p < 0.05), the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied to adjust the degrees of freedom. Furthermore, to control for Type I error inflation, the Bonferroni correction was applied for all post-hoc multiple comparisons. Categorical data will be expressed as frequencies and percentages (n, %) and compared between groups using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Correlation analyses between key continuous variables will be assessed using Pearson’s or Spearman correlation coefficients based on data distribution. To identify independent factors influencing quality of life (as measured by the SF-36 total score), a multiple linear regression analysis will be employed, incorporating relevant demographic and clinical variables. A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 will be considered statistically significant for all tests.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that a structured psychological nursing intervention grounded in the principles of the Rosenthal effect was associated with a greater reduction in self-perceived burden among patients undergoing hysterectomy for uterine fibroids, with the between-group difference sustained at the 3-month follow-up. This primary finding is directly aligned with the study objective, which was to evaluate whether an expectation-oriented nursing intervention could reduce Self-Perceived Burden Scale scores during perioperative recovery. The observed improvements in anxiety, depression, self-esteem, self-efficacy, quality of life, physiological stress indicators, postoperative recovery indicators, nursing satisfaction, and treatment adherence further support the broader relevance of this intervention to holistic perioperative care. Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that positive expectation alone determines recovery. Rather, they suggest that structured positive-expectation communication, when embedded into routine perioperative nursing care, may help patients form more adaptive cognitive appraisals of surgery and become more actively engaged in recovery.

The Rosenthal effect provides a clinically plausible framework for understanding these results. In its original formulation, the Rosenthal or Pygmalion effect describes how expectations communicated by an authority figure can influence an individual’s motivation, self-perception, and performance. In the perioperative setting, nurses are among the most continuous and accessible healthcare providers, and their communication may shape how patients interpret surgical risk, postoperative dependency, and their own capacity for recovery. For patients undergoing hysterectomy, temporary dependence on family members and nursing staff may be interpreted negatively as loss of autonomy or as becoming a burden. In this context, repeated positive expectations conveyed by nurses may help patients reframe postoperative dependency as a normal, temporary, and manageable stage of recovery, rather than as evidence of personal failure or family burden. This interpretation is consistent with research showing that Pygmalion effect-based psychological nursing may alleviate negative emotions in clinical populations and with experimental evidence that healthcare providers’ behavior and cognitive mindsets can influence patient responses through socially transmitted placebo-related mechanisms.44,45

Our findings are also consistent with broader evidence that expectations are relevant to psychological and clinical outcomes. Pontén et al reported in an individual participant data meta-analysis that treatment expectations significantly predicted clinical outcomes in psychological interventions, reinforcing the importance of expectancy-related mechanisms in therapeutic settings.46 In our study, the intervention did not consist of specialized psychotherapy, but of structured, recovery-oriented nursing communication designed to foster positive expectations, emotional validation, and confidence in recovery. This distinction is clinically important because such a model may be easier to integrate into routine perioperative nursing workflows than more resource-intensive psychological therapies. While previous studies have established the efficacy of cognitive behavioral therapy, mindfulness-based approaches, and psychological intervention in improving anxiety, depression, or recovery-related outcomes in gynecological and surgical patients,47–49 the present study adds to this literature by focusing on self-perceived burden as a primary outcome and by using the Rosenthal effect as the theoretical basis for a nurse-led intervention.

The pattern of secondary outcomes further supports the internal consistency between the study findings and the proposed psychological mechanism. The intervention group showed greater improvements in self-efficacy and self-esteem, accompanied by reductions in self-perceived burden and anxiety. These outcomes are conceptually linked. When patients believe that recovery is achievable and that temporary assistance is part of a normal healing process, they may feel less guilt, shame, and helplessness. Enhanced self-efficacy may also increase willingness to participate in early ambulation, rehabilitation instructions, and postoperative self-care. This interpretation is supported by our correlation analysis, in which higher General Self-Efficacy Scale scores were positively correlated with better SF-36 scores and negatively correlated with SPBS and SAS scores. The multivariable regression analysis further showed that intervention assignment and baseline self-efficacy were independently associated with 3-month quality of life. However, because this study did not conduct formal mediation analysis, self-efficacy should be interpreted as a plausible explanatory factor rather than a confirmed mediator.

A key strength of this study is that it evaluated not only psychological outcomes but also physiological and clinical recovery indicators. The reductions in serum cortisol and the favorable trend in heart rate variability are compatible with the possibility that improved psychological appraisal may be accompanied by reduced physiological stress activation. This interpretation is consistent with evidence linking preoperative depressed mood or anxiety to autonomic nervous system dysregulation and perioperative heart rate variability changes.50,51 Nevertheless, these physiological markers should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive biological evidence, because psychological intervention, perioperative care, pain control, sleep, and postoperative physical status may all influence stress-related markers. Similarly, the observed earlier ambulation, shorter hospital stay, lower pain scores at 48 hours and discharge, higher nursing satisfaction, and better treatment adherence are clinically meaningful and consistent with an expectation-based recovery model. These findings suggest that patients receiving the intervention may have been more willing to participate in postoperative recovery behaviors, which aligns with patient-engagement principles in Enhanced Recovery After Surgery pathways.52,53 However, these recovery outcomes may also be affected by unmeasured clinical, interpersonal, and institutional factors, and should therefore not be attributed solely to the Rosenthal effect.

By focusing on self-perceived burden, this study extends beyond the conventional perioperative focus on anxiety and depression. Self-perceived burden reflects a relational and self-evaluative psychological state, including feelings of guilt, shame, dependence, and concern about becoming a burden to family members or caregivers. This construct is particularly relevant in the hysterectomy population, because patients may simultaneously experience concerns about body integrity, reproductive identity, family roles, and short-term postoperative dependency. In this respect, the intervention addresses a deeper cognitive-emotional dimension of perioperative recovery. The positive-expectation component may have helped patients reinterpret their surgical experience as an active step toward health rather than as a source of dependency or diminished self-worth. This may explain why improvements were observed not only in SPBS scores, but also in self-esteem, self-efficacy, anxiety, adherence, and longer-term quality of life.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center study conducted in China, the generalizability of our findings to other cultural contexts is limited and must be interpreted with caution. Psychological interventions and the construct of “self-perceived burden” are deeply intertwined with cultural values. In Chinese culture, heavily influenced by Confucian family dynamics, individuals often place a high priority on family harmony and avoiding being a burden to relatives,54 which might inherently amplify the baseline severity of the self-perceived burden and create a larger margin for intervention benefit. Furthermore, the relatively hierarchical nature of the traditional nurse-patient relationship in our healthcare setting may enhance the perceived authority of the nursing staff, thereby potentially amplifying the impact of their positive expectations (the Rosenthal effect) compared to more individualistic or egalitarian Western healthcare cultures.55 Therefore, while this study establishes a foundational proof-of-concept, the magnitude of the intervention’s efficacy may vary cross-culturally. Second, while the outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded, it was inherently impossible to blind the patients and the nurses delivering the intervention, which introduces a potential for performance bias. Third, our assessment relied heavily on self-report scales. Although we included objective physiological markers, future research could benefit from incorporating more behavioral outcomes or biological markers to further substantiate the findings. Looking forward, future research should aim to conduct multi-center trials to validate the effectiveness of this intervention across diverse populations. A particularly promising avenue would be to formally test the mediating role of self-efficacy using statistical mediation analysis, which could provide definitive evidence for the psychological pathway proposed in our discussion. Furthermore, conducting longer-term follow-ups at 6 to 12 months post-surgery is imperative to comprehensively evaluate the true persistence of the intervention’s psychological benefits. Future long-term studies should specifically investigate whether the initial reductions in self-perceived burden and anxiety translate into sustained improvements in overall health-related quality of life and permanent shifts in coping strategies across the extended recovery trajectory. Finally, our findings suggest that successful implementation of this model requires nurses to possess advanced communication skills centered on empathy and positive framing. This highlights a need to integrate training on psychological principles like the expectation effect into standard nursing education and professional development curricula. The applicability and specific adaptations required for this communication style in Western or other diverse cultural backgrounds present a crucial area for future international, multi-center investigations.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial suggests that a structured psychological nursing intervention based on the Rosenthal effect may be a feasible approach for supporting patients undergoing hysterectomy for uterine fibroids. Compared with routine care, the intervention was associated with a greater reduction in self-perceived burden and more favorable changes in psychological status, self-efficacy, selected recovery indicators, and 3-month quality of life. These findings support the potential value of incorporating positive-expectation communication into perioperative nursing care. However, the results should be interpreted in light of the single-center design, the impossibility of participant and intervention-provider blinding, and the exploratory nature of the proposed psychological mechanism. Further multicenter studies with longer follow-up and formal mediation analyses are needed to confirm the durability, generalizability, and mechanism of this intervention.
