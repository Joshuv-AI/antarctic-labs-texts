Diagnostic performance of the blood urea/creatinine ratio in the diagnosis of occult upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage in the ICU
Authors: Keyvan Meghdadi, Hervé Kpeglo, My Linh Tran Minh, Romain Barthelemy, Benjamin Chousterman, Charles de Roquetaillade
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage (UGIH) is a common condition in intensive care unit (ICU), associated with significant morbidity and mortality [1]. ICU patients are exposed to metabolic stress, which compromises mucosal defense mechanisms and predisposes to ulceration and UGIH. The incidence of occult UGIH in ICU patients is challenging to determine but has been reported to range from 5% to 30%, with many cases likely remaining undiagnosed [2]. The gold standard for diagnosing UGIH is esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD). However, EGD carries inherent risks and significant human and financial costs. It is therefore essential to identify patients in whom the suspicion of occult UGIH justifies performing EGD [3]. Blood urea, creatinine concentrations, and the urea-to-creatinine ratio (UCR) have been proposed biomarkers in emergency department settings to better target patients at risk of rebleeding. To our knowledge, the utility of this biomarker has never been studied in the ICU context [4]. Given its widespread availability in critically ill patients, UCR could serve as a simple, readily available, and cost-effective tool to guide the indication for EGD in this high-risk population. We thus aimed to analyze the performance of UCR in predicting a positive EGD in ICU patients with suspected occult UGIH.

Over a five-year period, we reviewed cases of critically ill adults admitted to a tertiary medical ICU who developed occult UGIH and subsequently underwent EGD. Occult UGIH was defined by a decrease in hemoglobin of ≥ 1 g/dL within 72 h prior to EGD. Patients with overt hemorrhage and stage 3 acute kidney injury were excluded. The index test was UCR measured on the day of EGD. A positive EGD was defined as the presence of hemorrhagic lesions (Forrest Ib or II) or the requirement for a hemostatic procedure.

Among 384 EGDs performed, 139 patients met inclusion criteria (Flow-Chart in Supplementary material). 38 patients (27%) had endoscopically confirmed UGIH. The population had a median age of 62 [IQR 49–72] years, with a predominance of men (59%), motives of admission were mostly acute brain injury (54.7%) or sepsis (23%). Most patients were under mechanical ventilation (87.1%) and 48.9% required vasopressors. The vast majority were receiving proton pump inhibitors (70.5%) and enteral feeding (73.3%) at the time bleeding was suspected (Supplementary Table 1).

The median UCR on the day of EGD was 37.7 [26.2–48.4] in patients with confirmed bleeding and 34.2 [24.2–44]) in those without (p = 0.284) (Fig. 1A). When evaluated as a diagnostic test, UCR yielded an area under the ROC curve of 0.58 [0.46–0.69] (Fig. 1B). A threshold of 37.7 provided a sensitivity of 52% and a specificity of 68%, corresponding to likelihood ratios far too weak to influence clinical decision-making (LR 1.63). Analyses of urea or UCR values obtained up to three days before and 1 day after EGD led to similar conclusions, with no discriminative power (Supplementary Fig. 1). Clinical outcomes were not markedly different between groups: length of stay and mortality did not vary significantly according to whether occult UGIH was confirmed.

This study has several limitations. First, in emergency cohorts with overt bleeding, the intragastric blood volume is most likely substantial, which may be responsible for superior urea generation. In contrast, occult bleeding in ICU patients is often intermittent and smaller in volume. Second, critically ill patients are exposed to multiple metabolic perturbations, including increased catabolism, altered hepatic function, hypovolemia and variable renal clearance, all of which can confound interpretation of urea and creatinine levels [5].

Almost 60% of EGDs in this cohort revealed abnormal findings, although only 27% showed bleeding lesions (Supplementary Table 2). This highlights the frequent occurrence of endoscopic abnormalities in ICU patients, ranging from gastritis and esophagitis to non-bleeding ulcers, many of which may not explain the hemoglobin decline. This observation is in line with previous reports indicating that EGD in the ICU often yields equivocal results, even when performed for plausible indications.

These findings must be interpreted with caution given the retrospective and monocentric nature of our study, together with the relatively modest sample size. Only patients who underwent EGD were analyzed, which raises the possibility of verification bias. Nevertheless, the weak diagnostic performance observed for UCR makes it very unlikely that a larger cohort would have substantially modified our conclusion.

In summary, in a mixt ICU population, analysis of UCR showed no meaningful ability to distinguish patients with occult UGIH from those without. Although attractive for its simplicity and physiological rationale, UCR cannot be recommended as a decision-making tool for EGD in this context. Future efforts to refine indications for endoscopy in critically ill patients should likely focus on multimodal approaches rather than isolated biochemical markers.
