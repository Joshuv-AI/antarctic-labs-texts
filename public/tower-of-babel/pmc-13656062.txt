Less complexity, not less protection: reconsidering cerebral protection during short circulatory arrest in aortic arch surgery
Authors: Shingo Kunioka, Hayato Ise, Hiroyuki Kamiya
Published: 2026

Finding the right balance between cerebral protection and technical simplicity

As cardiothoracic surgeons, our ultimate goal is to perform complex operations safely and reproducibly while minimizing devastating postoperative complications. To achieve this, numerous technical refinements and adjunctive procedures have been developed over the past several decades. Although these advances have undoubtedly improved surgical safety, increasing technical complexity may also introduce additional operative manipulations, prolong operative time, and create new procedural pitfalls. Therefore, the objective of cerebral protection should not be to maximize procedural complexity, but rather to achieve the optimal balance between neurological protection and technical simplicity according to the clinical scenario.

Despite remarkable advances in aortic surgery, postoperative stroke remains one of the most devastating complications. The evolution of cerebral protection strategies in aortic surgery reflects this concept. The 2022 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guideline considered cerebral perfusion reasonable during aortic surgery requiring circulatory arrest, while acknowledging that the superiority of a specific perfusion or hypothermic strategy had not been established (1). The 2024 European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (EACTS)/The Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) guideline provides more specific recommendations, supporting high-moderate hypothermia combined with cerebral perfusion for hemiarch replacement and recommending that selective antegrade cerebral perfusion (SACP) be considered for complex aortic arch procedures (2). The guideline also acknowledges a potential role for retrograde cerebral perfusion (RCP) in selected procedures involving short-duration circulatory arrest.

Against this background, the study by Makarem and colleagues provides timely and clinically relevant evidence (3). Using a large multicenter registry, they compared no cerebral perfusion (NCP), antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP), and RCP in patients undergoing elective aortic arch surgery with circulatory arrest lasting less than 30 minutes. Importantly, their findings do not challenge contemporary recommendations supporting SACP for complex arch reconstruction. Rather, they suggest that adjunctive cerebral perfusion may provide benefit even during short periods of circulatory arrest and highlight RCP as a potentially pragmatic alternative in appropriately selected patients when only a brief period of circulatory arrest is anticipated.

Distinct characteristics and technical trade-offs of cerebral protection strategies

Deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA) without cerebral perfusion

DHCA without adjunctive cerebral perfusion represents the simplest cerebral protection strategy. Since its landmark description by Griepp and colleagues in 1975, it has served as the foundation of modern proximal aortic surgery (4). Patients are cooled to deep hypothermia (15–20 °C), allowing temporary circulatory arrest without cerebral perfusion while the distal anastomosis is completed expeditiously (5).

The principal advantage of DHCA lies in its technical simplicity. No additional cerebral perfusion cannulas are required, providing an unobstructed operative field and minimizing manipulation of the supra-aortic vessels. However, cerebral protection relies entirely on metabolic suppression by hypothermia, making this strategy highly time-dependent. Although DHCA has traditionally been considered acceptable for circulatory arrest lasting less than approximately 30 minutes, neurological complications remain a concern, which ultimately led to the development of adjunctive cerebral perfusion techniques such as SACP and RCP (6-8). It is important, however, to distinguish DHCA from the broader category of NCP. NCP refers to circulatory arrest without adjunctive cerebral perfusion and may encompass a range of hypothermic temperatures; therefore, NCP should not necessarily be considered synonymous with DHCA.

SACP

SACP is widely used as a cerebral protection strategy in contemporary aortic arch surgery. The 2024 EACTS/STS guideline recommends cerebral perfusion combined with high-moderate hypothermia for hemiarch replacement and states that SACP should be considered for complex aortic arch procedures (1,2). By providing physiological antegrade cerebral blood flow, SACP extends the safe duration of circulatory arrest while allowing higher systemic temperatures, thereby reducing the adverse effects associated with profound hypothermia (9,10).

Despite these advantages, the technical requirements of SACP depend on the cannulation and perfusion strategy employed. SACP can be established through several approaches, including direct cannulation of the supra-aortic vessels or unilateral perfusion through axillary, subclavian, or innominate artery cannulation. When direct bilateral or trilateral perfusion is employed, however, additional cannulas in the operative field may interfere with arch reconstruction, and manipulation of the supra-aortic vessels may introduce risks of vascular injury or embolization. Although these considerations are generally outweighed by the advantages of SACP in complex arch reconstruction, they remain relevant when selecting a cerebral protection strategy for shorter and less complex procedures.

RCP

RCP has been used as an adjunctive cerebral protection strategy in aortic surgery (11). Under deep hypothermia (approximately 18 °C), oxygenated blood is delivered retrogradely through the superior vena cava while maintaining a central venous pressure of approximately 15–20 mmHg. Although RCP avoids direct cannulation of the supra-aortic vessels, it requires an appropriate venous cannulation strategy that permits isolation and retrograde perfusion through the superior vena cava, which may add procedural steps compared with routine venous cannulation. Thus, the relative technical simplicity of RCP depends on the cannulation strategy and operative setting. The protective effect of RCP remains time-dependent, which should be considered when prolonged circulatory arrest is anticipated. In selected extensive aortic reconstructions, alternative surgical sequencing strategies, such as the arch-first approach, have also been described (12).

The physiological mechanism of RCP remains incompletely understood. Experimental studies suggest that much of the perfusate may be diverted through venous collateral pathways, limiting true nutritive cerebral perfusion (13). Conversely, other investigations have demonstrated evidence of cerebral capillary perfusion during RCP (14). Consequently, many investigators believe that the principal benefits of RCP include not only metabolic support but also the maintenance of cerebral hypothermia and the flushing of air and particulate emboli from the cerebral circulation during circulatory arrest.

Importantly, the recent study by Makarem et al. provides contemporary clinical evidence supporting this concept. In patients undergoing elective aortic arch surgery with circulatory arrest lasting less than 30 minutes, RCP was associated with a significantly lower risk of postoperative stroke compared with NCP (3). These findings are consistent with the discussion in the current EACTS/STS guideline regarding a potential role for RCP in selected procedures involving short-duration circulatory arrest.

Clinical implications of cerebral protection during short circulatory arrest

Makarem et al. analyzed 1,079 patients undergoing elective aortic arch surgery with circulatory arrest lasting less than 30 minutes across 42 centers. Patients were categorized according to the cerebral perfusion strategy as NCP (n=255), RCP (n=264), or ACP (n=560). Postoperative stroke occurred in 3.9% of patients in the NCP group, 1.9% in the RCP group, and 3.9% in the ACP group. After multivariable adjustment, RCP was associated with a significantly lower risk of postoperative stroke compared with NCP (adjusted odds ratio, 0.135; 95% confidence interval: 0.023–0.783; P=0.03), whereas no significant association was observed for ACP compared with NCP. Importantly, the study did not directly compare RCP with ACP, and these findings therefore should not be interpreted as demonstrating equivalence or superiority of RCP over ACP. Perfusion strategy also varied according to surgeon volume, suggesting that procedural experience and institutional practice may have influenced strategy selection.

Approximately 70% of patients in their cohort underwent concomitant aortic valve replacement, indicating that concomitant cardiac procedures were common in the study population. However, this should not be interpreted as establishing the applicability of these findings to any specific operative setting, such as ascending aortic replacement with concomitant valve surgery. Rather, the study provides broader insight into the potential role of adjunctive cerebral perfusion during short-duration circulatory arrest.

For extensive arch reconstruction or when prolonged circulatory arrest is anticipated, SACP remains an important strategy because it permits cerebral protection during more prolonged periods of arch reconstruction.

In contrast, for relatively straightforward procedures requiring only a brief period of circulatory arrest, such as ascending aortic replacement with concomitant aortic valve replacement, RCP may represent a pragmatic option that offers a different balance between procedural requirements and cerebral protection.

Recent evidence further supports this concept. King et al. demonstrated that moderate-hypothermic RCP using higher perfusion pressures achieved neurological outcomes comparable with SACP while preserving cerebral oxygen extraction (15). Together with the findings of Makarem et al., these data suggest that RCP remains a valuable option for carefully selected patients undergoing short-duration circulatory arrest.

Our institutional practice similarly reflects a tailored approach to cerebral protection. SACP is our standard strategy for aortic arch surgery, particularly when extensive reconstruction is required or when the anticipated duration of circulatory arrest is prolonged or uncertain. For more limited procedures requiring only a brief period of circulatory arrest, such as ascending aortic replacement concomitant with aortic valve surgery, we may instead favor RCP at approximately 20 °C. This choice is based not simply on technical simplicity, but on the anticipated arrest duration, operative exposure, and the risks associated with additional manipulation of the supra-aortic vessels. In selected younger patients with small but otherwise healthy supra-aortic vessels, in whom cerebral perfusion cannulation itself may be technically challenging or potentially traumatic, we occasionally perform circulatory arrest without adjunctive cerebral perfusion when the distal anastomosis can be reliably completed within approximately 10 minutes. Thus, although SACP remains our default strategy, RCP—and, in highly selected circumstances, NCP—may be used when their technical characteristics are better suited to the anticipated procedure. This individualized approach reflects our view that cerebral protection should be tailored to both the neurological requirements and technical demands of each operation.

Methodological limitations

Although Makarem et al. analyzed a large multicenter cohort of elective cases with circulatory arrest lasting less than 30 minutes, defining the study population primarily by arrest duration resulted in a heterogeneous mix of operative procedures. Future studies focusing on more homogeneous surgical populations, such as isolated ascending aortic replacement requiring circulatory arrest without concomitant valve procedures, may allow a more direct comparison of cerebral protection strategies.

The lack of detailed classification of neurological events also limits mechanistic interpretation of the findings. The study does not distinguish embolic stroke from injury related to global hypoperfusion. Because embolic washout is considered an important potential mechanism of RCP, distinguishing these mechanisms would have provided greater physiological insight. The clinical utility, optimal perfusion pressure, and physiological effects of RCP therefore warrant further multicenter investigation.

A further consideration is the potential influence of surgeon preference and experience on the selection of cerebral perfusion strategy. Makarem et al. demonstrated that perfusion strategy varied according to surgeon volume: RCP was used in 27% of cases performed by surgeons with >10 arch procedures during the study period, compared with 14% and 10% among surgeons performing ≤1 and 2–10 procedures, respectively. Conversely, NCP was used in 50% of cases performed by surgeons with ≤1 case, compared with 21% among surgeons with >10 cases. These findings suggest that cerebral perfusion strategy may reflect not only patient- and procedure-related factors but also surgeon experience, technical familiarity, and institutional practice. Because surgeon-level effects could not be adequately incorporated into the risk-adjusted analysis, residual confounding by surgeon preference remains possible and may have influenced the observed association between RCP and postoperative stroke.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the study by Makarem et al. does not challenge the current guideline recommendation favoring SACP for complex aortic arch reconstruction. Rather, it reinforces the concept that adjunctive cerebral perfusion should be considered even during short periods of circulatory arrest. While SACP remains the preferred strategy for extensive arch procedures, RCP may represent a practical option for carefully selected patients undergoing short-duration circulatory arrest.
