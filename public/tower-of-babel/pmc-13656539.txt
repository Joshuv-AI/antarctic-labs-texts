Impact of total suspended solids on disinfection efficacy of peracetic acid against Salmonella javiana
Authors: Olabode O. Akindolani, Mohsen Afshari, Tina Dong, Jamie A. Hestekin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peracetic acid (PAA) is frequently utilized in poultry processing as a microbial-control solution to reduce the presence of pathogens in poultry products. Although numerous studies have investigated the influence of acid concentration on microbial inactivation, only a limited number of studies have been documented regarding the effect of total suspended solids (TSS) on the potency of PAA against Salmonella. This study examines the impact of varying TSS concentrations (ranging from 40 to 500 mg/L) on the effectiveness of PAA in reducing Salmonella. At lower pH values (3.6, 6.0, and 7.0), PAA at 30 mg/L achieved complete disinfection of Salmonella in 60 seconds, while at higher pH values (8.0, 9.0, and 10.0), disinfection was faster, occurring within 30 seconds. PAA consumption varied by pH, with higher consumption at neutral and basic pH. Increasing TSS at a fixed pH of 9.5 reduced the antimicrobial efficacy of PAA, with higher TSS concentrations leading to less reduction in Salmonella compared to lower TSS levels over a 10-minute exposure. However, with longer contact times, antimicrobial efficacy improved. The study showed that complete elimination of Salmonella was achievable when TSS levels were less than 40 mg/L, and the disinfection process became significantly faster at TSS levels of 0 mg/L. It was suggested that this phenomenon might occur because the presence of solids could facilitate the accumulation of bacteria as a defense mechanism against disinfection, leading to the decreased efficacy of PAA. These showed that increase in TSS levels had negative effect on the effectiveness of PAA in eliminating Salmonella populations, providing insight into the influence of TSS on disinfection processes. These findings have implications for water treatment facilities and highlight the significance of monitoring TSS levels to ensure effective disinfection using PAA.

Introduction

Foodborne pathogens significantly threaten public health, causing numerous illnesses and economic losses annually. Salmonella, one of the most prevalent foodborne pathogens, is frequently associated with poultry products, making it essential to develop effective methods for its mitigation in the poultry industry (Antunes et al., 2016). Over the last few decades, peracetic acid (PAA) has emerged as a noteworthy disinfectant for wastewater treatment, primarily attributed to its robust antimicrobial characteristics, as highlighted by Kitis (2004), Coyle et al. (2014) and Luukkonen et al. (2014). PAA's appeal is further accentuated by its ability to minimize the formation of disinfection by-products, even in challenging scenarios such as the treatment of combined sewer overflows (CSOs) with poor water quality, as evidenced by studies conducted by Dell'Erba et al. (2007) and the US Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA, 1999). However, it is crucial to acknowledge a significant limitation of PAA – its rapid decay in aqueous solutions, as Yuan et al. (1997) reported. This aspect highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of PAA's stability and decay dynamics for its practical application in wastewater treatment processes.

Poultry processing plants generate wastewater laden with total suspended solids (TSS) through various processes such as de-feathering, evisceration, and washing. TSS encompass diverse solid particles, including organic and inorganic matter, which remain suspended in water (Eryuruk et al. (2018); Yaakob et al. (2018) and Kanafin et al. (2022)). The characteristics of TSS are influenced by the type of sewer system, the overall plant processes, and characteristics of the bird. Excessive TSS in wastewater can impede the efficiency of treatment processes, potentially causing clogs, hindering filtration, and disrupting settling in treatment tanks (Njoya et al. (2019)). Managing TSS ensures smooth wastewater treatment operations and prevents environmental consequences. TSS can significantly affect the distribution and activity of PAA in the aqueous environment, ultimately influencing its ability to reduce Salmonella contamination on chicken products (Dominguez Henao et al. (2018)). A comprehensive investigation into TSS's effects on PAA's efficacy in reducing Salmonella loads is imperative for ensuring the safety of poultry products and enhancing the overall quality of food production processes.

Previous studies have explored the impact of soluble organic constituents, as indicated by macro-parameters such as chemical oxygen demand (COD) and biological oxygen demand (BOD). However, limited attention has been given to the influence of suspended matter despite observations made in various studies (Chhetri et al. (2014, 2016); Falsanisi et al. (2008); Koivunen and Heinonen-Tanski (2005); Lefevre et al. (1992); McFadden et al. (2017); Stampi et al. (2001)). Several researchers have indicated that disinfecting primary effluents and combined sewer overflows (CSOs) requires higher PAA concentrations compared to secondary and tertiary effluents (Chhetri et al. (2016); Gehr and Cochrane, (2002); Koivunen and Heinonen-Tanski (2005); Luukkonen et al. (2014)). Additionally, Chhetri et al. (2016) observed that pretreating CSO to remove suspended solids reduces the amount of disinfectant needed. In their papers, Bohrerova and Linden (2006), Dietrich et al. (2007), and Winward et al. (2008) studied the influence of suspended solids as protective barriers for bacteria. They found that microbial aggregates and microorganisms affixed to or embedded within particles exhibit enhanced resistance to the effects of various disinfectants compared with freely suspended microorganisms. Despite these observations, the influence of suspended solids on PAA disinfection effectiveness remains insufficiently understood. A lot of these studies have focused on municipal wastewater systems, whereas comparatively little research has examined how suspended solids affect Salmonella inactivation under conditions representative of poultry-processing environments.

Falsanisi et al. (2008) reported that about 99% of the total bacterial population in wastewater comprises free-swimming bacteria, while around 1% comprises bacteria associated with particles. They also highlighted the impact of the size of suspended solids in PAA disinfection. They found that the protection provided by TSS varied substantially with particle size, with 0.6 and 1.3 logs of protection observed for solids ranging between 10 and 120 μm and exceeding 120 μm, respectively. Contrary to this, McFadden et al. (2017) suggested that the size of solids in the 10 to 100 μm range had only a minor impact on PAA disinfection. Dominguez Henao et al. (2018) studied the effect of TSS on the inactivation of free-swimming E. coli at various PAA doses. They found an accelerated PAA degradation rate at TSS concentrations above 40 mg/L. This agrees with McFadden et al. (2017), which noted a reduction in the effectiveness of PAA disinfection as the concentration of TSS increases. However, a lack of research has been conducted to shed light on this aspect. While Stampi et al. (2001) stated that the adverse effect on PAA disinfection effectiveness remains moderate when dealing with TSS concentrations ranging from 10 to 40 mg/L, Lefevre et al. (1992) found that PAA demonstrates exceptional disinfection capabilities, even at levels as high as 100 mg/L. Thus, there is a gap in determining TSS on PAA over various concentrations relevant to the poultry industry.

As the poultry industry continues to evolve, driven by consumer demands for safer and higher-quality products, a better understanding of how TSS affects the performance of PAA against Salmonella is essential. Through this research, we hope to contribute valuable insights that inform the development of practical, science-based guidelines for poultry processors, regulatory agencies, and the broader food industry. Ultimately, our findings play a pivotal role in enhancing food safety, public health, and the economic sustainability of poultry production systems.

This research paper addresses the critical knowledge gap regarding the influence of suspended solids on the disinfection efficacy of PAA on Salmonella reduction. PAA decay was assessed at pH values (3.6 to 10.5) to check its relative stability. We explored the interactions between TSS and PAA, considering the impact on Salmonella reduction efficacy. This research can serve as a foundation for improving food safety practices in the poultry industry, potentially leading to more efficient and cost-effective Salmonella mitigation strategies.

Finally, the effect of TSS on PAA disinfection was assessed at a fixed PAA concentration to verify the presence of a potential defense mechanism for bacteria against disinfection afforded by TSS. Previous works by Bohrerova and Linden (2006) and Turolla et al. (2017) suggest that TSS possibly acts as a condensation nucleus which favors bacteria aggregation of bacteria.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Technical grade PAA solution was procured from Lab Alley. Its composition, expressed as weight percentages, consisted of 15% PAA, 23% hydrogen peroxide, and 16% acetic acid. Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) was obtained from Fisher Scientific. The medium was prepared by dissolving 30 g of TSB powder per liter of deionized water with continuous stirring, and the pH was adjusted to 7.0 using 1 N NaOH or 1 N HCl. The solution was then sterilized by autoclaving at 120°C for 20 minutes. After cooling, the sterile broth was utilized for the cultivation of Salmonella. All chemicals used were of reagent grade.

Preparation of Salmonellainoculum and cell enumeration

Salmonella Javiana pure culture was provided by the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) (ATCC BAA 1593). Using an inoculating loop, the Salmonella strain was transferred to a tryptic soy agar plate to establish a stock culture. The plate was incubated using Stabil-therm dry-type bacteriological incubator at 37°C for 24 hours to allow for bacterial growth. Following incubation, a single colony-forming unit (CFU) was isolated from the cultured sample, suspended in 10 mL of tryptic soy broth (TSB) and grown to exponential phase overnight at 37°C for 24 hours with continuous shaking speed of 150 rpm (Thermo Scientific). Viable cells were enumerated as colony-forming units using single plate-serial dilution method after incubation at 37°C for 18–24 hours.

Preparation of PAA solutions

The PAA stock solution was diluted with deionized water to prepare working solutions. Depending on the experimental objective, PAA working concentrations of 5, 30, and 70 mg/L were prepared by adding the appropriate volume of stock solution to deionized water. The PAA concentration of each prepared solution was verified prior to use using a Peroxychem Titration Kit (ChemWorld).

For the PAA stability experiments, solutions containing an initial PAA concentration of 30 mg/L were prepared, as this concentration falls within the operational range of commercial poultry immersion chilling systems and therefore represents a realistic antimicrobial level encountered during poultry processing. The solutions were adjusted to pH values of 3.6, 6.0, 7.0, 8.0, 9.0, 10.0, and 10.5 using 1 M H2SO4 or 1 M NaOH. No TSS was added during these experiments to isolate the effect of pH on PAA stability. Experiments were conducted in 500-mL reactors maintained at room temperature under continuous mixing conditions. Residual PAA concentrations were measured at 3-min intervals over an 18-min experimental period. The monitoring duration was selected to approximate the residence time of poultry carcasses in commercial immersion chillers, thereby enabling evaluation of PAA stability under conditions representative of industrial poultry processing environments.

Preparation of TSS solutions

Solutions of TSS were sourced from two distinct origins: TSS collected from a poultry processing plant representing suspended organic matter such as fat, proteins, and residual poultry tissue, as well as inorganic particulates like grit or processing residues and commercially procured TSS (TSS Standard, 500 ppm TSS, Product No. 8672.6-32, RICCA Chemical Company, Arlington, TX, USA) from Fisher Scientific to ensure consistency and standardization. Standard TSS solutions prepared at defined concentrations were used in controlled laboratory experiments to evaluate the effects of suspended solids on PAA stability and antimicrobial efficacy. Poultry processing water collected from a processing facility was used to validate treatment performance under representative processing conditions. TSS was measured according to USEPA Method 160.2. (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 1999). Samples were filtered through pre-weighed glass-fiber filters, dried at 103–105°C, cooled in a desiccator, and reweighed. TSS concentration was calculated based on dry solids mass and sample volume. TSS solutions of desired concentrations were prepared by diluting a known volume of stock TSS solution with deionized water in a volumetric flask. After adjusting the solution to the final volume, the contents were mixed thoroughly using a magnetic stirrer to achieve a homogeneous suspension.

Experiments on PAA decay

Experiments on PAA decay were conducted at room temperature in 500 mL thoroughly mixed batch reactors, using a magnetic stirrer operating at 200 rpm, to evaluate the stability of PAA under varying pH values (3.6, 6.0, 7.0, 8.0, 9.0, 10.0, and 10.5) representative of those encountered in poultry-processing systems. An initial peracetic acid (PAA) concentration of 30 mg/L was prepared and adjusted to pH values ranging from 3.6 to 10.5. This concentration was selected because it is within the operational range commonly used in commercial poultry immersion chilling systems and is representative of antimicrobial concentrations encountered during carcass processing. Samples were maintained under controlled laboratory conditions, and residual PAA concentrations were measured at predetermined time intervals over an 18-min period. The monitoring period was selected to approximate the residence time of poultry carcasses in commercial immersion chillers and to evaluate PAA performance under industry-relevant conditions. The objective was to quantify the rate of PAA degradation and determine whether pH significantly influences the persistence of the active disinfectant. Understanding PAA decay is important because reductions in disinfectant concentration can affect antimicrobial efficacy and overall process performance during poultry processing operations.

Experiments on Salmonella inactivation

A series of batch disinfection experiments was conducted to evaluate the effects of PAA concentration, pH, TSS concentration, and TSS particle size on the inactivation of Salmonella. All experiments were performed in 500 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing approximately 108 CFU/mL of Salmonella and were conducted at room temperature under continuous mixing to ensure homogenous distribution of bacteria, disinfectant, and suspended solids. Concentration-response experiments were first conducted to establish baseline Salmonella inactivation kinetics by PAA and to identify an appropriate disinfectant concentration for subsequent studies. The selected concentration was then used in a series of experiments designed to evaluate the effects of pH, TSS concentration, and TSS particle size on PAA disinfection performance under controlled laboratory conditions. To evaluate the influence of pH on PAA disinfection efficacy, experiments were conducted using PAA solutions adjusted to different pH conditions over a 150-second contact period. A PAA concentration of 30 mg/L was selected because it represents an operationally relevant concentration commonly used in poultry processing antimicrobial interventions, particularly immersion chilling systems. The pH of the PAA solutions was adjusted to 3.6, 6.0, 7.0, 8.0, 9.0, and 10.0 using 1 M H2SO4 or 1 M NaOH represent acidic, neutral, and alkaline conditions and to correspond with those evaluated during the PAA stability experiments. No TSS were added during this experiment to isolate the effect of pH on PAA antimicrobial activity. The adjusted PAA solutions were prepared immediately before inoculation to minimize changes in disinfectant concentration caused by prolonged exposure to altered pH conditions.

To investigate the effect of TSS on the disinfection efficacy of PAA, tests were carried out at six different TSS concentrations (40, 50, 70, 100, 300, and 500 mg/L) for 10 minutes. The pH of the solutions was adjusted to 9.5 at the start of the tests. Additional experiments were performed to evaluate the influence of TSS particle size on bacterial inactivation using suspended solids with average particle diameters of 5, 20, and 35 μm under the same experimental conditions. At predetermined time intervals, samples were collected and immediately quenched to stop further disinfectant activity prior to microbiological analysis.

The extent of bacterial inactivation after the disinfection tests was quantified as the log-inactivation of viable colony-forming units, log(N/N0), where N0 and N respectively represent the microbial counts before and after disinfection.

Analytical procedures

Concentrations of PAA were measured using a titration kit (Chemworld TK7500-Z). The PAA treatments were adjusted to different pH levels by adding 1 M H2SO4 or 1 M NaOH, and the pH of each treatment was confirmed using a benchtop pH meter. The concentrations of TSS were measured using the methods of Sophonsiri and Eberhard (2004) and Levine et al. (1985).

Microbiological analyses were carried out using the method of Dominguez Henao et al. (2018). Samples were collected in sterile flasks after adding 0.5 mL sodium thiosulfate (0.1 N) and 0.5 mL of bovine catalase solution (600 units/mL) to quench residual PAA and hydrogen peroxide, respectively. Microbiological analyses were repeated twice, and cells were enumerated as colony-forming units by single plate-serial dilution after incubation at 37°C for 18–24 hours.

Experimental design and statistical analyses

All experiments were conducted using a completely randomized experimental design to evaluate the effects of PAA concentration, solution pH, TSS concentration, and TSS particle size on the inactivation of Salmonella Javiana and on PAA stability. The experimental unit was defined as an individual 500-mL Erlenmeyer flask subjected to a specific treatment condition. Experiments were conducted in two independent replicates, with duplicate microbiological analyses performed for each sample. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 11.0.2.92 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). PAA stability data and experiments involving repeated measurements over time were analyzed using repeated-measures mixed-effects models (REML), with treatment, exposure time, and their interaction included as fixed effects and replicate included as a random effect. Exposure time was treated as the repeated factor, and the Geisser-Greenhouse correction was applied when appropriate. The relationship between Ct value and Salmonella survival was evaluated using simple linear regression. The effect of PAA concentration on Salmonella inactivation was analyzed using one-way ANOVA. When significant treatment effects were detected, means were separated using Tukey's multiple-comparison test. Statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

PAA stability

Naturally, PAA decomposes into acetic acid, hydrogen peroxide, and water; therefore, understanding its stability under different environmental conditions is essential before evaluating its antimicrobial efficacy. To determine whether PAA remained sufficiently stable throughout the pH conditions used in subsequent microbial inactivation experiments, a stability study was conducted in 500-mL batch reactors maintained at room temperature. As shown in Fig. 1, PAA concentration declined significantly over time across all tested pH conditions (F(2.671,18.69) = 268.0, P < 0.0001), reflecting the disinfectant's inherent reactivity and decomposition. Concentrations decreased from approximately 30 mg/L at the start of the experiment to 21-24 mg/L after 18 min of exposure. A significant effect of pH was also observed (F(6,7) = 5.092, P = 0.0253); however, this effect was relatively small compared with that of exposure time. Furthermore, the pH × time interaction was not significant (F(16.02,18.69) = 1.582, P = 0.1703), indicating that degradation patterns were similar across the evaluated pH range (3.6-10.5). Residual PAA concentrations generally stabilized between approximately 20 and 25 mg/L after 15 min, demonstrating that PAA maintained substantial activity under both acidic and alkaline conditions. These findings indicate that PAA is sufficiently stable over the time frame relevant to the subsequent disinfection experiments and that differences in Salmonella inactivation cannot be attributed solely to rapid disinfectant degradation. Disinfection kinetics of Salmonella by PAA

To assess Salmonella susceptibility to disinfection with PAA, an inactivation curve was generated by plotting log(NN0), the log of the fraction of culturable bacteria, against the Ct value (disinfectant concentration × contact time) at pH 7.5 (Fig. 2). A near-neutral pH was selected to provide a baseline for evaluating Salmonella inactivation kinetics while minimizing the influence of highly acidic or alkaline conditions. Ct values (mg-min/L) were varied by adjusting PAA concentrations (1, 6, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 mg/L) while maintaining a fixed 3-minute contact time at 60 mg/L TSS. The results demonstrated that Salmonella was highly susceptible to PAA disinfection, with increasing Ct values producing progressively greater reductions in bacterial survival. Linear regression analysis revealed a significant negative relationship between Ct and Salmonella survival (F(1,19) = 93.14, P < 0.0001), described by Eq. (1):(1)log(NN0)=−0.059(Ct)−1.783where N/N0 is the ratio of surviving Salmonella concentration to the initial Salmonella concentration and Ct is disinfectant concentration × contact time. The model explained 83.1% of the variation in microbial survival (R² = 0.8306), and the slope was significantly different from zero (95% CI: −0.0719 to −0.0462). These findings confirm that antimicrobial efficacy increased with increasing exposure dose, as reflected by higher Ct values. Previous studies by Rossi et al. (2007) and Santoro et al. (2005) demonstrated that PAA inactivation of E. coli followed similar trends with log reductions ranging from 1 to 5, depending on factors such as initial microbial concentration, PAA dose, residual disinfectant, and contact time. The current study, which evaluated Salmonella under poultry-processing relevant conditions, decrease in microbial counts with increasing C·t values, reaching approximately 6–7 log reductions at relatively low exposure doses (Ct).

The results of experiments using PAA for the disinfection of Salmonella in the absence of TSS indicate a high efficacy in bacterial reduction. In the absence of TSS, PAA can directly interact with microbial cells, allowing the disinfectant to exert its full oxidative potential. As a result, rapid reductions in Salmonella populations were observed. The absence of organic or inorganic matter, which can interfere with the PAA's activity, likely contributes to the increased effectiveness, as the disinfectant is not consumed by competing reactions with solids.

Similarly, disinfection tests for a concentration-response curve were performed in which different concentrations of PAA at fixed TSS dose interact with Salmonella. The results of the experiment were run in three minutes. Log-inactivation values ranged from -0.08 to -6.8 depending on the applied PAA dose. An initial drop in Salmonella levels up to 4-log reduction suggests that Salmonella is highly susceptible to even small concentrations of PAA, likely due to its strong oxidative properties, which can damage bacterial cell membranes, proteins, and DNA. Statistical analysis confirmed that PAA concentration had a highly significant effect on Salmonella inactivation (F(3,8) = 820.7, P < 0.0001). Tukey's multiple-comparison test showed that all PAA treatments produced significantly greater reductions in bacterial populations than the untreated control (P < 0.0001). Increasing the PAA concentration from 5 to 30 mg/L significantly enhanced microbial inactivation (P < 0.0001); however, no additional reduction was observed when the concentration was further increased from 30 to 70 mg/L (P = 0.8283). Effect of pH variation on Salmonellainactivation

To understand how the variation of pH might affect Salmonella inactivation by PAA, the disinfectant solutions were adjusted to pH values ranging from 3.6 to 10 prior to disinfection. As shown in Fig. 4, substantial reductions in Salmonella populations occurred rapidly across all pH conditions. The ability of PAA to maintain strong antimicrobial activity across both acidic and alkaline conditions represents a significant advantage over many conventional disinfectants used in food-processing and wastewater-treatment applications. Overall, the results demonstrate that PAA is a highly robust disinfectant capable of rapidly inactivating Salmonella over a broad pH range, supporting its application in industrial processing environments where pH conditions may fluctuate considerably.

Effect of TSS on PAA disinfection

The presence of TSS can hinder the effectiveness of PAA by shielding microorganisms, reducing their availability. Understanding this relationship is essential for optimizing treatment protocols while minimizing chemical usage. Fig. 5 shows the relationship between TSS concentrations and microbial reduction dynamics, indicating the effects of TSS concentration on the rate of Salmonella inactivation over time during PAA disinfection. Mixed-effects analysis revealed significant effects of TSS treatment (F(1,4) = 1117, P < 0.0001), exposure time (F(2.211,8.844) = 1034, P < 0.0001), and the TSS × time interaction (F(2.211,8.844) = 142.1, P < 0.0001), indicating that suspended solids altered the kinetics of microbial reduction over time. Without TSS, PAA can directly contact Salmonella cells, leading to rapid and efficient inactivation, as shown by the significant log reduction within 30 seconds and nearly complete inactivation after 3 minutes. In contrast, the presence of 500 mg/L TSS significantly delayed bacterial inactivation and reduced antimicrobial efficacy. Predicted mean log(N/N₀) values were −6.351 for the no-TSS treatment and −3.189 for the 500 mg/L TSS treatment, demonstrating markedly greater bacterial survival in the presence of suspended solids. While increasing exposure time improves Salmonella inactivation in both conditions, the protective effect of TSS significantly delays the disinfection process, requiring more time to achieve similar levels of bacterial reduction. The protective mechanism by TSS to bacteria is not well understood, and several efforts have aimed to clarify it. In their work, Chhetri et al. (2014) and Chhetri et al. (2016) assume that TSS affects disinfection by accelerating the decay of PAA, thereby exposing bacteria to lower disinfectant doses. However, experiments conducted at the same PAA dosage challenged this mechanism, instead demonstrating a possible interaction between bacteria and solids. LeChevallier et al. (1984) showed that up to 20% of dispersed E. coli could attach to porous materials within 20 minutes, gaining protection from disinfectants. From Fig. 5, the effect of TSS on PAA disinfection appears to stem from physical interactions that shield bacteria. The delayed log reduction in bacterial counts with TSS presence supports the hypothesis that TSS impedes direct bacterial exposure to PAA by acting as a barrier, thus reducing efficiency and prolonging the required contact time. In Fig. 6, the reduction of Salmonella concentration is shown for varying TSS concentrations: 40, 50, 70, 100, 300 and 500 mg/L. TSS concentration had a significant effect on Salmonella inactivation (F(5,12) = 442.8, P < 0.0001), while exposure time (F(2.009,33.71) = 3068, P < 0.0001) and the TSS concentration × time interaction (F(14.04,33.71) = 29.81, P < 0.0001) were also highly significant, indicating that the influence of suspended solids became increasingly apparent over the course of treatment. Across all treatments, bacterial reductions increased with exposure time; however, the rate and extent of inactivation decreased as TSS concentration increased. For lower TSS concentrations (40 mg/L and 50 mg/L), almost complete inactivation of Salmonella is achieved between 6 and 8 minutes, reaching around an 8-log reduction. Statistical comparisons showed that treatments containing 40 and 50 mg/L TSS generally achieved significantly greater Salmonella inactivation than those containing 300 and 500 mg/L TSS (P < 0.05), whereas no significant differences were detected between the 300 and 500 mg/L treatments (P > 0.05). These higher concentrations of TSS appear to inhibit the complete inactivation of Salmonella due to the increased shielding effect of the particles. Impact of TSS particle size

The impact of TSS particle size is crucial for optimizing PAA disinfection strategies. To investigate this effect, experiments were conducted using TSS at a concentration of 80 mg/L, with 30 mg/L PAA, and a pH of 9.5 at room temperature (Fig. 7). Statistical analysis demonstrated that particle size significantly affected Salmonella survival (F(2,6) = 11.07, P = 0.0097), while exposure time remained the dominant factor governing microbial inactivation (F(2.031,12.19) = 3797, P < 0.0001). The particle size × time interaction was not significant (F(4.062,12.19) = 2.183, P = 0.1311), indicating that the overall pattern of bacterial reduction over time was similar across all particle-size treatments. As shown in Fig. 7, Salmonella populations declined rapidly under all tested conditions; however, somewhat greater inactivation was observed in systems containing smaller particles. Tukey's multiple-comparison test revealed that the 5-µm particle treatment achieved significantly greater Salmonella inactivation than the 20-µm (P = 0.0025) and 35-µm (P < 0.0001) treatments, whereas no significant difference was detected between the 20- and 35-µm particle sizes (P = 0.1929). Despite these statistically significant differences, the magnitude of the particle-size effect was relatively small compared with the influence of exposure time, and all treatments followed similar inactivation trajectories. Overall, the findings indicate that smaller TSS particles are associated with enhanced Salmonella inactivation during PAA treatment, but that particle size plays a secondary role compared with other factors such as contact time and TSS concentration. Consequently, while particle size can influence PAA efficacy, its impact appears to be limited within the particle size range evaluated in this study.

The results of the study demonstrated that the efficacy of PAA in reducing Salmonella was significantly affected by the concentration of suspended solids. In samples with higher TSS levels, the reduction in Salmonella counts was less effective compared to samples treated with PAA in lower TSS conditions. This suggests that elevated TSS concentrations can reduce the efficacy of PAA treatment. Suspended solids may physically protect Salmonella cells from direct exposure to PAA and increase the oxidant demand of the wastewater matrix, thereby reducing the availability of active disinfectant for microbial inactivation. As a result, lower Salmonella reductions may be observed at higher TSS concentrations. Effective pre-treatment methods to reduce TSS concentration could significantly improve the overall disinfection performance of PAA, leading to more rapid and complete microbial inactivation.

The effect of TSS on PAA disinfection was studied. Increasing TSS concentrations lead to higher PAA decay rates, which are more pronounced at TSS concentrations above 40 mg/L. The results suggest that TSS particles likely provide physical protection for bacteria by limiting disinfectant exposure or by facilitating bacterial association with suspended particles. The shielding effect is most pronounced at the higher TSS concentrations (300 mg/L and 500 mg/L), where the inactivation of Salmonella is slower and incomplete, even after 10 minutes.

Lower TSS concentrations (40 mg/L and 50 mg/L) show a more significant decrease in Salmonella levels by 10 minutes, with most reaching the maximum 8-log reduction. These concentrations are ideal for effective Salmonella inactivation, as they allow for more rapid and complete disinfection. Higher TSS concentrations require longer exposure times to achieve similar levels of inactivation, and even then, complete disinfection may still need to be achieved. When TSS concentrations were below approximately 40 mg/L, rapid and nearly complete inactivation of Salmonella was observed within the tested contact times.

Further studies are encouraged to understand the protection mechanism afforded by TSS to bacteria against disinfection and their role as potential condensation nuclei promoting bacterial aggregation.
