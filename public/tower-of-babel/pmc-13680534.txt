Scaling of dimensions and concentrations of plastics in two Dutch rivers
Authors: Jasmijn Polinder, Frank P. L. Collas, Félix P. Leiva, Stephanie B. Oswald, Margriet M. Schoor, Wilco C. E. P. Verberk, A. Jan Hendriks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plastic pollution raised great environmental concerns due to its persistence and widespread presence. To model ecological impacts, such as ingestion by wildlife, information on the abundance and dimensions of plastic particles is fundamental. Our study examined the relationships between size dimensions (length, area, circumference) of individual particles and the length of particles, their concentration, and cumulative surface area. Plastic particles were collected with trawl nets from upstream and downstream sites in two Dutch rivers in 2023 and 2024. Particle dimensions, including length (defined as the longest axis), surface area, circumference, and the number of particles, were measured. Geometric means of particle concentrations and cumulative surface area concentrations were calculated for equal-sized bins based on length and plotted against particle length on a log-log scale. Reduced major axis and ordinary linear least squares regressions determined the power relationships between size dimensions of individual particles. For length-based bins, we evaluated how concentrations and total surface area co-varied with length classes. Size dimensions were strongly and positively related, indicating differences in shape across size classes. Contrary to previous research, the length of plastic particles was not strongly correlated to their concentration in the Meuse River and varied between sampling locations. The cumulative surface area concentration increased significantly with length. The findings of the study should be viewed with caution, as they were based on two Dutch rivers. Even greater caution is warranted for the relationships between particle length and concentration, and between particle length and cumulative surface area, as they were based solely on measurements in the Meuse.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10661-026-16029-y.

Introduction

Plastic pollution is a global concern, as it harms ecosystems and human health. It is estimated that 8 million tonnes of plastic were produced in 2023, of which most were produced using fossil fuels (Europe, 2024). Annually, between 0.8 and 2.7 million metric tonnes of plastic flow from rivers into marine ecosystems (Meijer et al., 2021). Its versatility drives its widespread usage (Andrady & Neal, 2009), but paradoxically, its durability poses a significant environmental challenge. With plastic production and consumption continuing to rise and climate change driving stronger wind speeds, plastic pollution is expected to intensify as extra plastic is carried into the environment (Tong et al., 2021). To reduce further contamination of aquatic ecosystems, findings from plastic pollution research can enhance knowledge and inform the development of effective mitigation strategies (Azevedo-Santos et al., 2021).

The physical environment is important for the transport of plastic particles. In rivers, plastic transport can be hindered by entrapment in vegetation or displacement to riverbanks (Lotcheris et al., 2024). The fate of plastics is also influenced by hydrological conditions, including water level, flow velocity, and discharge (Oswald et al., 2025; van Emmerik et al., 2018). Beyond hydrological conditions, plastics can also be transported by wind and rain-induced water runoff (Bruge et al., 2018; Castro-Jiménez et al., 2019). Besides the physical environment, the characteristics of the plastic particles themselves, including size, shape, and density, can also influence their transport in the environment (Schwarz et al., 2019). For instance, smaller particles are believed to experience greater turbulence and to disperse more easily through the water column than larger particles (Shamskhany & Karimpour, 2022).

Particle size is an important characteristic of plastics, as size can influence the fate of the plastic particles in the environment (van Emmerik & Schwarz, 2020). They are typically categorised into four size ranges: nanoplastic (< 0.1 µm), microplastic (0.1 µm–5 mm), mesoplastic (5–25 mm), and macroplastic (> 25 mm) (Kershaw et al., 2019; Hanvey et al., 2017; van Emmerik et al., 2020). The impacts of plastics vary with particle size, influencing processes such as ingestion and toxic additive leakage (Lee et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021). Microplastics can originate from the fragmentation of macroplastics, a process known as secondary microplastic formation (Cole et al., 2011). Processes such as ultraviolet light exposure and mechanical breakdown contribute to this fragmentation (Andrady, 2015; Galgani et al., 2015). Additionally, the polymer type influences the fragmentation process (Alva & Thomas, 2025). As macroplastics fragment into smaller particles, they become accessible for ingestion by organisms and are likely to bioaccumulate within food webs (Cole et al., 2011). Therefore, analyses of a broad range of plastic particle sizes are crucial for understanding environmental consequences.

Prioritising macroplastic research is essential, as current evidence indicates that macroplastics account for a significant share of the total plastic mass in rivers (Weideman et al., 2020). However, research on macroplastics in freshwater ecosystems remains limited, and studies focusing on mesoplastics in these environments are even scarcer (Blettler et al., 2018). Only a few studies address more than two size categories (Blettler et al., 2017; Cózar et al., 2014, 2015), though most are conducted in marine ecosystems.

Rivers are important freshwater ecosystems that serve both as conduits, transporting plastic from inland areas to the ocean, and as sinks where plastic may accumulate (Meijer et al., 2021; van Emmerik et al., 2022). Research on marine ecosystems has revealed size-abundance distributions in the ocean’s surface layer. Cózar et al. (2014) identified a unimodal distribution in which particle abundance initially rises with size before declining. However, Suaria et al. (2016) reported a consistently negative relationship between abundance and size. Nevertheless, river size-abundance distributions have not been thoroughly investigated for a large size range. The most common size class visually observed on bridges in rivers ranges from 10 to 50 cm in the top 10 cm of the water column (van Emmerik et al., 2018), and the size distribution pattern resembles that observed by Cózar et al. (2014). Sampling the entire water column yields higher concentrations of plastic particles than surface water sampling (Vriend et al., 2023), possibly influencing the previously obtained size-abundance relationships. It is therefore essential to establish size relationships across a wide size range using plastic particles collected with nets to gain initial insight into size-abundance relationships in rivers.

In ecology, size-abundance relationships, called size spectra, are widely used. These size spectra represent abundance or biomass (Y) as a function of size, typically reported in terms of body mass (X). The abundance and size are negatively correlated and plotted on double-logarithmic axes, usually with logarithmic size bins (Sprules & Barth, 2016). Animals can ingest plastic particles that are up to twenty times smaller than their own length (Jâms et al., 2020). There have been some studies on plastic ingestion in freshwater bodies, but they primarily focus on microplastic ingestion (Collard et al., 2019; Sanchez et al., 2014; Windsor et al., 2019). Evidence has already been presented that plastics are present in freshwater fish (Sanchez et al., 2014). Moreover, macroinvertebrates ingest microplastics in rivers (Windsor et al., 2019), potentially leading to the accumulation of plastics in higher trophic levels. Accurate particle size and concentration measurements serve as the foundation for evaluating exposure risks to human and ecological systems. By linking ingestion risk to particle size, the likelihood of an organism encountering plastics can be estimated more accurately. However, data on various sizes and plastic particle concentrations should be examined first. This is because the size and shape of plastic particles are important characteristics for their transport in the environment (Schwarz et al., 2019). By understanding the size and shape of plastic particles in a specific system, their fate can be more accurately predicted, improving estimates of organisms encountering plastics. Therefore, this paper aims to first identify the size dimensions and size distributions of plastic particles in two Dutch rivers. To do this, we compiled and analysed a unique database of plastic pieces collected from the water column of four sites in two rivers across the Netherlands. Subsequently, we documented the relationship between plastic particle size and abundance, and we compared these size-abundance patterns with those previously reported in marine ecosystems (Cózar et al., 2014; Suaria et al., 2016). Taking these marine size spectra as a starting point, we predicted that we would similarly find largely negative size-abundance relationships in our studied rivers (i.e. larger particle size classes occur in lower concentrations). Our analyses focused on the relationships among the size dimensions of individual plastic particles in two Dutch riverine environments to understand their general shape, and we explored size-abundance relationships for one river using size bins, thus providing a basis for modelling the size-dependent environmental impacts of plastics.

Material and methods

Study area

Plastic monitoring was conducted in two major rivers in the Netherlands, the Meuse and the Rhine. They traverse substantial parts of the Netherlands and enter near Eijsden and Lobith, respectively. Plastic particles were collected during 318 sampling events in both rivers, at upstream and downstream locations, in April and May of 2023 and 2024 for the Meuse, and in 2023 only for the Rhine (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Map of the Netherlands showing sampling locations (blue dots) and major cities (white dots)

Map of the Netherlands showing sampling locations (blue dots) and major cities (white dots)

Sampling sites were located in the municipality of Eijsden (50° 48′ 12.6″ N, 5° 41′ 39.4″ E, Meuse), Sambeek (51° 38′ 23.9″ N, 5° 59′ 07.0″ E, Meuse), Lobith (51° 50′ 55.1″ N, 6° 07′ 54.7″ E, Rhine), and Tiel (51° 52′ 00″ N, 5° 26′ 32.5″ E, Waal) (Fig. 1). Downstream from Lobith, the Rhine bifurcates into the Pannerdensch canal and the Waal, which is the main branch. The location in Tiel is on the Waal, and hence both Lobith and Tiel were treated collectively as ‘Rhine’ during our analyses. To compare across river systems, Eijsden and Sambeek were also combined in the analyses as ‘Meuse’.

Plastic collection

A trawl net was used to collect samples, measuring 4×1 m with a mesh size of 6 mm. Samples were taken at three depths in the water column: surface, middle, and bottom, adjusted to the water depth and any possible obstacles at the bottom of the sampling location. Each sampling event was defined as a single net haul at a single depth, lasting approximately 40 min. The net was fully submerged during sampling in the surface layer. The samples were cleaned, and visible organic matter was removed on board the ship. The sampling method used is illustrated in more detail by Vriend et al. (2023) and Oswald et al. (2025).

Plastic measurements

Plastic particles were arranged on A4 paper and, if necessary, flattened with paperclips in the laboratory. The paper was scanned with a Canon IRIScan Desk Pro 6 scanner (Canon, Japan) along with a set square, resulting in a two-dimensional image of the collected plastic particles. The plastic particle dimensions were measured, including the length (defined by the longest axis), surface area, and circumference, using the software ‘ImageJ’ for Windows (Abràmoff et al., 2004). If plastic particles were too large during plastic measurement, they had to be cut to fit on A4 paper. Therefore, no length or circumference could be measured, only the surface area of that particle. Plastic particles were categorised as microplastic (0.1 µm to 5 mm), mesoplastic (5 to 25 mm), or macroplastic (> 25 mm) based on the measured longest axis length (Kershaw et al., 2019; Hanvey et al., 2017; van Emmerik et al., 2020).

Data analyses

The primary targets of our study were mesoplastics and macroplastics. However, the net’s mesh size retained some plastic particles smaller than the mesh size in the samples. This was likely due to particle aggregation, sometimes with organic matter, during sampling. To ensure accurate analyses, we applied a length threshold based on the diagonal of the mesh size. The mesh size was 6 mm, and using the Pythagorean theorem, the length threshold was set at 8.5 mm.

To evaluate the robustness of our analyses, we also analysed the full data set without a length threshold (presented in the Appendix under all subheadings without a length threshold), which largely confirmed that the patterns observed for smaller plastic particles were consistent with expectations from analyses that employed the size threshold. Analyses of the full dataset excluded two data points due to anomalies that could be attributed to measurement error and inaccurate data entry.

Preliminary analysis showed that including interactions among explanatory variables improved model fit, as indicated by higher adjusted R2 and lower AIC values across all examined relationships. Moreover, the 95% confidence intervals for the slopes from ordinary least squares (OLS) and reduced major axis (RMA) rarely overlapped, indicating that the two methods yielded different estimates. However, similar R2 values were found between OLS and RMA regressions. Significance was assumed when p < 0.05.

Distribution of size dimensions

Linear models were fitted to log10-transformed particle length, surface area, and circumference to evaluate the effects of river and location within the river on plastic particle size dimensions. Subsets were created when the linear model indicated significant differences between rivers and locations within each river. Histograms were generated of the log10-transformed values of particle size dimensions to visualise the distribution by location and river. Welch two sample t-tests (Welch, 1947) compared size dimensions between upstream and downstream locations within both rivers. The arithmetic mean of all size dimensions was calculated, along with the corresponding standard error, for each location to gain insight into the size dimensions at each location.

Relationships between length, surface area, and circumference

Linear models were developed to compare one size dimension with another size dimension, river, location within the river, and year (e.g. Surface area = Length + River + Location + Year). Interactions were examined to comprehend the effects of one explanatory variable on another. The most informative model was chosen based on the lowest Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) value (Akaike, 1973), and a considerable improvement in R2. The explanatory variable year did not meet this criterion, so the 2 years were combined in the analyses.

The explanatory variables or interactions that contributed more than 0.1 to the variation in the slope were selected for visualisation for practical reasons. Relationships between two size dimensions were visualised using OLS and RMA regressions. RMA models were applied because they offer robust slope estimates for log-transformed bivariate allometric data, assuming equal measurement uncertainty for both dependent and independent variables (Warton et al., 2006). Exploring how explanatory variables affect linear relationships is usually done with OLS, so this method is also displayed in the plots.

Size-dimension relationships derived theoretically from known shapes (a circle and a rectangle) were utilised to understand the general shape observed in the plastic particles. Several constants that express the ratio of the rectangle’s length to height were compared to find the best fit. This resulted in different coefficients of the theoretical relationships of several rectangles. The theoretical relationships that best matched the empirical ones were plotted in the appropriate graphs. By visually comparing the parameters of the theoretical relationships with empirical data, the overall shape of the identified plastic particles could be inferred. For the circle, the most optimal theoretical equations surface area = π·(length/2)2 = 0.79*length2 and circumference = 2π*(length/2) = 3.14*length were derived, and for the rectangle, the most optimal theoretical equations surface area = 0.28*length2 and circumference = 2.49*length were derived. Moreover, a change in exponent implies that the shape of the plastic particles changes with size.

Particle concentration and cumulative surface area concentration to length relationships

Particle concentration and cumulative surface concentration relationships could be established for the Meuse, but not for the Rhine, due to incomplete size measurements in the collected samples because of time constraints. Particle concentrations were calculated to establish size-abundance relationships.

Ten equal-sized logarithmic bins were created based on the measured length. The number of size bins used was based on the binning methods applied in ecological size spectra analyses (Sprules & Barth, 2016). As values from multiple sampling events could be in size bins, the geometric mean of particle and cumulative surface area concentration (CAC) for each size bin was calculated along with the average bin length. Relationships were explored using linear models between size and particle concentration, or cumulative surface area concentration, along with location within the river and year. Interactions between parameters were examined, and the best model was selected based on AIC. The explanatory variables that contributed more than 0.1 to the variation in the slope and showed a considerable improvement in R2 were selected for visualisation for practical reasons. The explanatory variable year did not meet this criterion, so the two years were combined in the analyses.

Relationships between size-abundance and size-cumulative surface area were established using OLS and RMA regressions. Because the sampling events captured different packets of water moving through the river system (i.e. they were separated not only in distance but also in time, corresponding to different discharge regimes), particle and cumulative surface area concentration relationships were plotted separately for each sampling location. Welch two sample t-tests (Welch, 1947) were applied to compare particle concentrations and cumulative surface area concentrations between upstream and downstream locations.

Data were gathered in Excel (v. 2411) and processed and analysed in R (v. 4.4.3; R Core Team, 2024). RMA regressions were performed with the ‘lmodel2’ package (Legendre, 2014), while OLS regressions were performed with the ‘stats’ package (Chambers, 1992; Wilkinson & Rogers, 1973). All data and the code to analyse are made available (Polinder et al., 2025).

Results

A total of 9545 plastic pieces were collected during sampling events in April and May of 2023 and 2024. After filtering using the length threshold, 9096 particles were included in the analysis, of which 45.9% were classified as mesoplastic, and 54.1% as macroplastic.

Distribution of size dimensions of individual plastic particles

The linear model, which included a log10-transformed size dimension, river, and location within the river, showed a significant interaction between river and location for all size dimensions (Tables S1 and S2). The three log10-transformed size dimensions (length, surface area, and circumference) visually overlapped between the upstream and downstream locations in both rivers (Figures S1 and S2).

In the Meuse, the length, surface area, and circumference measured upstream were all significantly larger than downstream. In the Rhine, only the length of the plastic particles differed, but in the opposite direction, with larger sizes observed downstream when compared to upstream (Tables S3 and S4).

Discussion

Size and concentration are crucial factors when modelling the impact of plastic pollution, such as risks associated with their ingestion by animals. In this paper, we established scaling relationships among size dimensions, particle concentrations, and cumulative surface-area concentrations, which are necessary components for accurately modelling plastic effects. The size dimensions showed positive relationships, with scaling exponents close to those expected theoretically. Furthermore, in contrast to previous studies, we did not find a negative relationship between length (the longest axis) and particle concentration. Instead, the relationship was either positive or flat, resulting in a positive relationship between cumulative surface area and length.

Beyond two dimensions

Not all plastic particles discovered were two-dimensional. Since some were three-dimensional, relying solely on a two-dimensional method to measure plastic dimensions affected the measured surface area. When considering all three dimensions of a plastic particle, the surface area likely increases. Changes in the measured size dimensions could alter the exponents for the relationships, particularly if changes are non-linear. For example, increased surface area will reduce the exponent relating length to surface area. The extent of change depends on the fraction of three-dimensional particles, which might be very low. Previous research has shown that the largest proportion of plastics found was plastic films, which are usually two-dimensional (Oswald et al., 2023).

When establishing general geometric relationships for particle dimensions, it might also be important to consider other size and shape characteristics. A previous study identified other factors to describe shape variation, including sphericity, roundness, angularity, and convexity (Arasan et al., 2011). Furthermore, ecological size-abundance relationships are typically based on mass and density (White et al., 2007), so measuring the mass of plastic particles might reveal patterns more closely related to ecological size-abundance relationships.

Sampling plastic particles

The study primarily recovered mesoplastic and macroplastic particles due to the 6 mm mesh size during sampling. Nevertheless, a substantial number of microplastic particles could be present at the sampling sites in the Rhine, particularly further upstream in the basin (Mani et al., 2015). Since only minor differences were observed between relationships with and without the length threshold, the interrelationships for the different size metrics detected using the length threshold appear to be valid also for somewhat smaller size bins. Actively capturing microplastics is expected to alter the relationship between length and particle concentration.

Discharge on concentration

Discharge has a complex relationship with the concentration of plastic particles in fluvial systems. One study found that higher discharge leads to lower concentrations of bridge-counted plastic particles due to dilution (van Emmerik et al., 2018), while different studies reported that increased discharge results in higher concentrations of plastic particles, attributed to sampling during high discharge events and sampling in urban areas (Vriend et al., 2023; Wagner et al., 2019). Additionally, plastics can be (re)mobilised by storms or floods (van Emmerik et al., 2022), increasing the concentration of plastic particles. Hence, it is thought that discharge could have affected our plastic concentrations. To better comprehend the processes influencing concentration and discharge at our sampling sites, additional measurements during various discharge events are needed to establish site-specific relationships between plastic particle concentration and discharge.

Plastic particle dimensions

Frequency distributions (Figures S1 and S2) showed considerable overlap in the log10-transformed size dimensions in both rivers. However, significant differences were found in the untransformed size dimensions at upstream and downstream locations in both rivers. The timing of the sampling events at the upstream and downstream locations did not account for the water’s travel time and occurred on different days between different locations. As a result, the water sampled downstream could have had a different concentration than it did upstream. Nevertheless, the overall scaling relationships were very similar across sampling years and locations.

A scaling factor of two can be derived from the geometric relationship in which area scales with length as A ~ L2, suggesting that the shape of the particles remains consistent as their size changes, which holds for all shapes. For this equation, an upper boundary constraint on the exponent (or slope) of 2 is expected and is observed in Fig. 2. The scaling factors at the sampled locations were all below two, indicating that the shape of the plastic particles varies with size, with longer particles being more and more 1 dimensional (e.g. ribbon shape). The scaling factor in the Meuse was closer to two than that of the Rhine, implying that the shapes of the plastic particles in the Meuse do not change as much with changing size as in the Rhine. For microplastics, it was also observed that shape changes with size (Gosselink et al., 2025). For the equations of the circumference, the expected exponent is one, which matches well with the empirically found exponent. However, this appears to be a lower bound, as shown in Fig. 3. This can be due to the chosen parameters on the x- and y-axis. Putting the x-axis parameter on the y-axis, and the y-axis parameter on the x-axis, will result in a different exponent.

The fate of plastic particles in rivers was stated to be influenced by three continuous processes: transport, accumulation and degradation (van Emmerik & Schwarz, 2020). These three processes are composed of other processes. For example, accumulation in rivers is influenced, among other factors, by sedimentation and resuspension of plastic particles (Schwarz et al., 2019). However, plastic particle characteristics can also influence the rates of these processes. For example, particles with a high surface area-to-volume ratio sink more rapidly than those with a low surface area-to-volume ratio due to biofouling (Fazey & Ryan, 2016), thereby increasing the sedimentation rate. These three continuous processes together determine the size and shape of plastic particles in rivers. Plastic fragmentation is a complex process, and no single cause can be identified in this study. However, in this context, we would like to discuss possible contributors to plastic fragmentation in this study.

One possible cause of the different degradation patterns of plastic particles in the Meuse and the Rhine might be the absence of large commercial ships upstream in the Meuse (Burgers, 2023). The absence of large commercial ships upstream in the Meuse may explain the differing exponents observed between the rivers. On the other hand, the Meuse in the Netherlands has weirs (Burgers, 2023), which could increase mechanical breakdown and therefore decrease the exponent. Other differences between the rivers also affect the breakdown of plastic particles, such as flow velocity. Moreover, the upstream and downstream locations in both rivers are not equally distant. The locations in the Meuse differ by about 120 km, whilst in the Rhine they differ by about 60 km. The source location of plastic, and therefore the plastic input into the system, might also be closer to the sampling location in the Meuse than in the Rhine, which could explain lower plastic degradation in the Meuse. Lines for the RMA and OLS regressions overlapped for mesoplastics, suggesting that river or location had a greater influence on micro- and macroplastics. Larger particles contributed to greater variability in the data (Figs. 2 and 3; Figures S3–S6).

Concentration and cumulative surface area across size bins

Equal-sized bins based on measured lengths resulted in an uneven distribution of data points across the size bins. Specifically, size bins at the beginning and end of the length spectrum contained fewer data points. Applying the length threshold did not change this pattern. Adding more size bins would increase the number of points to construct the relationship between length and abundance. However, it also increases the risk of empty size bins and the stronger influence of outliers within the size bin.

No consistent relationships were observed between plastic length and abundance, contrary to previous studies on plastics and size abundance relationships reported in the field of Ecology, which all report consistent inverse relationships between length and abundance (Cózar et al., 2014; Kooi & Koelmans, 2019; Sprules & Barth, 2016; Suaria et al., 2016). Applying the threshold did not change the overall patterns. This indicates that there is a disconnect between particle size and their concentrations in the Meuse River. If future extensive monitoring provides similar results, other relationships, like polynomials, should be considered to describe the relationship between size and abundance.

Previous studies in oceans showed an initial increase in abundance with increasing plastic particle length, followed by a decrease (Cózar et al., 2014, 2015; Ruiz-Orejón et al., 2018). The size range in those measurements (0.20–2000 mm) was broader than in this study (without length threshold: 0.36–684 mm). Examining a broader size spectrum could offer more insights into how size and abundance relate. For example, Kooi and Koelmans (2019) found that microplastics smaller than five µm decreased in abundance with increasing size in surface waters and sediments in marine and freshwater environments. Moreover, the addition of non-living particles, such as microplastics, to traditional size spectra can result in lower slopes (Lins-Silva et al., 2024). Modelling demonstrated that vertical mixing lowered the exponent of the normalised abundance-length relationship (Kaandorp et al., 2021). Data from three depths were combined, which might explain the lower slopes observed in this study compared to those reported by Kooi and Koelmans (2019). Alternatively, the results for riverine plastic reported here may simply reflect a closer proximity to the source of plastic input, which likely consists of predominantly larger plastic particles, and these may not have experienced sufficient wear and tear to break down into smaller particles.

Since upstream and downstream sampling were not timed such that the same packet of water was sampled, it is difficult to make inferences about plastic fragmentation. For the Meuse, we see a size distribution towards smaller particles downstream, indicative of fragmentation, but the opposite is true for the Rhine. Nevertheless, if fragmentation occurred, it should not reduce the total surface area.

Accurate measurement of plastic particle size and concentrations provides the evidence needed for informed regulation and mitigation strategies in freshwater ecosystems. The findings of this study can be a starting point for improving river-system risk assessment. For example, quantifying the likelihood of encountering and ingesting plastic particles can be improved by linking found concentrations of plastic particles and abundance of various animals. Great effort should be dedicated to reducing further plastic contamination. By decreasing plastic input into the environment, substantial financial and ecological benefits can be achieved. Nonetheless, as plastics continue to enter ecosystems, further research on their size and distribution remains necessary. The size of plastic particles remains crucial for evaluating risks associated with plastic pollution, as it influences ingestion, assimilation, and distribution in exposed animals (van Emmerik & Schwarz, 2020). Size-dependent uptake can explain and predict health impacts on animals (Sangkham et al., 2022).

Conclusion

Plastic pollution is a pressing issue that requires further research to understand its environmental impacts fully. Improved characterisation of plastic particles can guide policymakers toward more effective waste management and pollution-reduction practices, for example, because particle size is important for their fate in the environment. By better understanding where particles end up, risk assessment for human and ecosystem health can be improved. This study is the first to describe relationships between size dimensions and to link concentration and length, as well as cumulative surface area and length, for plastics in Dutch rivers. Spatial differences were observed in the measured dimensions and concentrations. Additionally, the cumulative surface area concentration increased with larger particle sizes, while strong positive relationships were observed among the size dimensions. Recommendations for future monitoring are threefold. First, we advocate studying a wider size range and repeatedly sampling the same packet of water to validate our relationships for alternative size classes in different rivers and to estimate rates of fragmentation. Second, analysing different size parameters, such as mass, can provide insights that can be compared to classical size-abundance relationships in natural systems. Third, more effort should be directed toward adequately measuring concentrations and how plastic concentrations are affected by factors such as discharge.
