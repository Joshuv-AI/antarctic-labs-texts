Optimized cluster based routing protocol for IoT enabled healthcare data networks
Authors: Sree Chandra Swarna, Venkata Ratnam Kolluru, Balamuralikrishna Potti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The integration of Internet of Things (IoT) technologies into healthcare has transformed real-time monitoring and data communication. However, IoT-enabled Cognitive Radio Networks (CRNs) face persistent challenges such as high energy consumption, routing delays, and security risks. This paper proposes a novel Grey Wolf Optimization-based Multi-Adaptive Routing Protocol (GWO-MARP) that combines cluster-based routing with adaptive meta-heuristic intelligence. Unlike traditional cluster-based routing approaches that rely on static or energy-weighted selection, GWO-MARP dynamically determines cluster heads through a multi-objective fitness function that balances residual energy, lifetime, link quality, and security cost. The adaptive hunting behaviour of the GWO algorithm guides optimal path formation and ensures secure and energy-aware transmission, making the protocol distinct from existing methods. The proposed method is implemented and evaluated using MATLAB, and its performance is benchmarked against existing protocols including DA-EDC, MT-DQL, and SDL. Experimental results demonstrate significant improvements in throughput (44.30 kbps), end-to-end delay (1.175 ms), packet delivery ratio (99.6%), and energy consumption (0.103 J) for a network of 200 nodes. These outcomes validate the proposed protocol’s capability to support robust, scalable, and energy-efficient data transmission in IoT-enabled healthcare networks.

Introduction

Nowadays, the Internet of Things (IoT) has emerged in numerous real-world applications and is specifically designed to enhance both security and localization performance1. It integrates multiple network technologies that interact with the environment to collect and share information from anywhere2. IoT applications are currently implemented in various domains such as smart agriculture, smart industries, smart cities, smart transportation, and smart healthcare services3. With the rapid growth of IoT applications, the number of connected devices has increased significantly. However, IoT devices are often unable to access unlicensed bands due to limited network capabilities4. Moreover, the available spectrum is insufficient to meet the connectivity demands of these devices5. Therefore, Cognitive Radio Networks (CRNs) are integrated with IoT systems to enhance spectrum efficiency6. This approach offers a promising solution for improving IoT network capacity through Opportunistic Spectrum Access (OSA)7. In addition, spectrum resource allocation and bandwidth utilization are further optimized using this concept8.

Modern healthcare services have incorporated IoT technologies to support remote healthcare systems and improve service prioritization9. Reliable and timely data transmission is essential for delivering healthcare records and supporting telemedicine applications10. A Quality of Service (QoS)-based control strategy with a Software-Defined Networking (SDN) model has been introduced to manage healthcare services effectively. To handle healthcare data efficiently, an OpenFlow-based traffic management module has been developed for the fog computing environment11. Experimental results have demonstrated that the proposed strategy reduces response time and increases throughput12.

In an IoT network, the connected nodes exhibit flexibility, but the increasing number of dynamic route connections can lead to frequent link disconnections or failures due to node mobility13. When IoT nodes change their positions frequently, route disconnections occur, resulting in packet loss. Consequently, packet drops and increased energy consumption become major challenges in IoT networks14. It is therefore crucial to address the problem of route disconnection or failure to minimize packet loss, delay, and excessive energy consumption. Furthermore, IoT-based CRNs operate autonomously, but their nodes are constrained by limited mobility and communication range15, and often have restricted battery capacity. Without an intelligent routing protocol, IoT-based CRNs experience excessive energy consumption, which may lead to network degradation or disconnection16.

Several routing protocols have been proposed for IoT-enabled CRNs, such as Deep Q-Learning with Ant Colony Optimization17, Trust-Aware Protocol (TAP)18, Cognitive Data Transmission Strategy (CDTS)19, and Opportunistic Data Transmission Protocol (ODTP)20, to improve security and QoS performance. However, each of these protocols addresses only specific objectives, leaving several key research issues unresolved. Therefore, it is essential to design an intelligent IoT-CRN framework to achieve optimal performance across multiple network parameters.

A key novelty of GWO-MARP lies in its seamless integration of meta-heuristic hunting dynamics with practical routing decisions, establishing a direct and adaptive bridge between theoretical optimization behaviour and real-world IoT–CRN routing performance.

Process of proposed GWO-MARP methodology

In the proposed GWO-MARP protocol, each stage of the Grey Wolf Optimization process corresponds to a practical routing function. The searching stage represents exploration of candidate nodes to identify potential cluster-heads based on energy, trust, and link stability conditions. The encircling stage refines these candidates by evaluating neighbouring node conditions to select stable cluster-heads. The hunting stage corresponds to routing path selection, where nodes offering balanced delay, reliability, and security are chosen to stabilize multi-hop transmission. Finally, position updating enables adaptive route maintenance, where forwarding paths are dynamically adjusted based on residual energy and network changes, preventing route degradation. This explicit mapping ensures that optimization behaviour directly leads to efficient routing decisions.

In GWO-MARP, the optimization process does not function as a standalone mathematical module. Instead, each computed fitness value directly influences practical routing operations such as selecting stable cluster-heads, minimizing retransmissions, and validating secure forwarding nodes. This integrated design ensures that theoretical optimization behaviour yields tangible improvements in routing efficiency. In the proposed GWO-MARP protocol, the Grey Wolf Optimization process is not used in isolation; instead, it is tightly integrated with routing operations to select optimal cluster heads and determine efficient multi-hop transmission paths. Each wolf in the optimization process represents a potential cluster head (CH) candidate, and its position corresponds to the node’s network state parameters such as residual energy, distance to base station, and link reliability.

Fitness Function Formulation

The multi-objective fitness function FCH​ used for cluster-head selection in GWO-MARP is defined as:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{CH} = w_{1} * \left( {E_{r} / E_{\max } } \right) + w_{2} * \left( {1 - D / D_{\max } } \right) + w_{3} * \left( {L_{q} / Lq_{\max } } \right) + w_{4} * (T_{i} / T_{\max } )$$\end{document}where:

Er: residual energy of node

D: estimated transmission delay

Lq: link quality factor

Ti: trust value of the node

w1, w2, w3, w4: normalized weights (summing to 1)

Nodes with higher FCH values are prioritized as cluster-heads. This formulation enables an adaptive trade-off among energy, delay, reliability, and trust-based security.

For reproducibility, the weights used in the fitness function were selected as w1 = 0., w2 = 0.25, w3 = 0.20, and w4 = 0.20, corresponding respectively to residual energy, transmission delay, link quality, and trust value. Higher priority is assigned to residual energy and delay to ensure network longevity and timely healthcare data delivery, while link quality and trust parameters maintain transmission reliability and security. Nodes are considered eligible for cluster-head selection only if their residual energy exceeds 30% of initial energy and their trust score satisfies Ti > 0.6, preventing unstable or compromised nodes from participating in routing. The Grey Wolf Optimization process terminates when either the maximum iteration limit (100 iterations) is reached or when successive iterations produce negligible fitness improvement (change < 0.5%), ensuring convergence while maintaining computational efficiency.

Secure data transmission and trust mechanism

The proposed GWO-MARP ensures secure data transmission using a two-layer security mechanism integrated into the routing process:Trust-Based Authentication: Each node maintains a dynamic trust score Ti based on historical behaviour such as successful packet forwarding, packet integrity, and residual energy stability. Before cluster head formation, only nodes with Ti​ > Tth​ are considered eligible. This step eliminates unreliable or compromised nodes from participating in routing.Hash-Assisted Data Integrity: During inter-cluster communication, each packet is appended with a lightweight SHA-256 hash generated at the source node. The receiving cluster head verifies this hash before forwarding. If mismatches occur, the packet is dropped and the sender’s trust score is penalized, ensuring authentication and tamper resistance without heavy cryptographic overhead.

The simulation used the Random Waypoint Mobility Model with node speeds of 1–5 m/s in a 3500 × 3500 m2 area. The First-Order Radio Energy Model was adopted with Etx = 2.6 J and Erx = 1.2 J. Constant Bit Rate (CBR) traffic (512-byte packets, 0.5 s interval) was generated for 600 s. The proposed GWO-MARP framework was implemented in MATLAB R2022b. Each simulation was executed for 100 iterations, and the results were averaged over 10 independent runs to ensure statistical consistency. To provide deeper statistical insight into the performance evaluation, we also computed the standard deviation for all key metrics across the 10 independent simulation runs. The variability remained low (standard deviation < 2.5% of the mean) across throughput, end-to-end delay, packet delivery ratio, and energy consumption, confirming the stability and reproducibility of the GWO-MARP protocol. A fixed random seed (Seed = 42) was used across all experiments to maintain reproducibility.

Justification of Simulation Parameters: The selected simulation parameters reflect realistic conditions observed in medium-scale IoT deployments. A network area of 3500 × 3500 m2 allows evaluation under sparse-to-moderate node distributions, which are common in healthcare monitoring systems deployed across hospital campuses or smart community zones. Node densities ranging from 200 to 1000 enable analysis of scalability and congestion behaviour. The mobility model (Random Waypoint, 1–5 m/s) reflects slow-moving human-centric applications such as patient monitoring and staff/device movement. Additionally, the chosen transmission and reception energy values follow the First-Order Radio Model, widely adopted in IoT and CRN simulations. Together, these parameters provide a balanced and realistic framework for evaluating the robustness of the proposed protocol under diverse operating conditions.

Although the experiments employ deterministic simulation settings such as a fixed random seed and a predefined mobility model to ensure reproducibility, minor performance variations were observed across independent simulation runs due to stochastic node movement and dynamic routing decisions. These variations remained within a small margin and did not affect comparative performance trends. Nevertheless, real-world IoT deployments may exhibit higher variability due to unpredictable interference, mobility patterns, and traffic fluctuations. Therefore, the presented results should be interpreted as controlled performance estimates rather than absolute operational guarantees.

Discussion

The results validate the effectiveness of the proposed GWO-MARP framework in addressing key challenges in IoT-enabled Cognitive Radio Networks for healthcare applications. Compared to existing methods such as DA-EDC, MT-DQL, and SDL, GWO-MARP demonstrates superior performance across multiple metrics, including throughput, end-to-end delay, packet delivery ratio, energy consumption, and secure range. The integration of Grey Wolf Optimization for cluster-head selection plays a critical role in reducing energy consumption and delay, while also enhancing the security and stability of data transmission. These improvements directly contribute to higher reliability and efficiency in healthcare data communication.

The incorporation of the multi-objective fitness function and hunting behavior ensures that node selection is both optimal and adaptive, resulting in improved throughput and extended network lifetime. The scalability and adaptability of the protocol further position GWO-MARP as a strong candidate for deployment in real-world IoT healthcare environments.

Importantly, prior GWO-based routing techniques primarily focus on optimizing individual parameters—such as energy or distance—while security, link quality, and trust are treated as secondary considerations. In contrast, GWO-MARP introduces a unified multi-objective fitness function that simultaneously optimizes four routing-critical parameters: residual energy, delay, link reliability, and trust score. The adaptive updates of the alpha–beta–delta wolves further refine routing paths in real time, enabling the protocol to respond effectively to dynamic network conditions. This tightly coupled optimization-to-routing workflow distinguishes GWO-MARP from traditional cluster-based or GWO-only models and reinforces its novelty.

Overall, while the proposed framework achieves strong performance, future extensions may incorporate advanced optimization and enhanced security mechanisms to better address emerging IoT challenges and further improve routing robustness in highly dynamic environments.
