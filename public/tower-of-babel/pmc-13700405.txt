Effects of transcription units and a specific hexanucleotide motif of porcine circovirus-like virus P1 on viral DNA synthesis, viral protein expression, and progeny virus production
Authors: Jingze Song, Zixuan Li, Na Li, Jianping Xie, Hengzhi Zhu, Hongqiao Lai, Zenghao Xiang, Yanan Zhong, Hao Sun, Kongwang He, Sizhu Suolang, Libin Wen, Qi Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPorcine circovirus-like virus P1 has a broad host range and is known to cause post-weaning multisystemic wasting syndrome in pigs, as well as infect and propagate in cattle, goats, dogs, cats, yaks, and rabbits. To investigate the functional importance of its eight open reading frames (ORFs) and the conserved hexanucleotide motif (GGATCC), we generated a panel of site-directed mutants based on the P1 infectious clone rpSK-2P1. These mutants were transfected into PK-15 cells and serially passaged four times. Viral replication capacity and genetic stability were assessed using polymerase chain reaction (PCR), quantitative PCR, and Sanger sequencing.ResultsAll the mutant viruses retained the ability to replicate their genomes and transcribe viral RNA. However, sequencing of viral genomes recovered at the fourth passage revealed selective reversion at multiple mutated sites, indicating a strong replicative advantage conferred by wild-type sequences during sustained propagation. Notably, mutant Y1, which harbored a mutation in ORF1 that encoded the capsid protein (Cap), initially exhibited reduced replication but rapidly reverted upon passaging, restoring Cap expression and fitness.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that although these ORFs and nucleotide sites do not abolish P1 replication, their native sequences play pivotal roles in modulating replication efficiency and maintaining genetic stability, thereby providing key insights into the molecular mechanisms governing P1 replication. Thus, P1 tolerates limited genetic change but is under intense pressure to maintain its wild-type sequence, offering critical insight into the replication strategy and evolutionary constraints of this compact, broad-host-range DNA virus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05740-0.Keywords: Porcine circovirus-like virus P1, Site-directed mutagenesis, Genetic stability, Replication regulation, Immunohistochemistry

Background

Porcine circoviruses (PCVs) are non-enveloped viruses with icosahedral symmetries belonging to the genus Circovirus in the family Circoviridae [1]. Their genomes consist of a single-stranded circular DNA molecule approximately 1.7–2.0 kb in length [2]. To date, five porcine circovirus species (PCV1 to PCV5) have been identified, but only PCV1 to PCV4 are recognized by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV).

PCV1 was first identified in 1974 as a contaminant in the porcine kidney cell line PK-15 (ATCC CCL-31) and is considered nonpathogenic in pigs [2–4]. In contrast, PCV2 is the principal causative agent of porcine circovirus–associated disease (PCVAD), which is associated with a spectrum of clinical syndromes, including post-weaning multisystemic wasting syndrome (PMWS), porcine dermatitis and nephropathy syndrome (PDNS), porcine respiratory disease complex (PRDC), and reproductive failure. Among these, PMWS is the most prevalent and clinically significant manifestation [5–7]. PCV3, which was first described in 2015 [8], has been linked to gastrointestinal, reproductive, neurological, and respiratory disorders [8–10]. While serological evidence suggested the existence of PCV4 as early as 2008, its complete genome was not characterized until 2019 [11]. Emerging data indicate that PCV4 is linked to clinical diseases in swine, particularly gastrointestinal disturbances, respiratory signs, and lesions consistent with PDNS [12, 13]. Recently, a novel circovirus, provisionally designated as PCV5, was identified in pig populations in China [14]. Genomic and phylogenetic analyses revealed that PCV5 was distinct from PCV1–PCV4, showing its closest evolutionary relationship not only to other porcine circoviruses but also to the fur seal feces-associated circular DNA virus, a member outside the traditional Circoviridae family. Epidemiological investigations have further demonstrated that PCV5 is widely circulating in China, especially in the southern regions, and is strongly associated with the severity of clinical disease [14].

Previous studies have predicted that the PCV2 genome contains 11 open reading frames (ORFs) [15], with ORF1 and ORF2 being the two major ones. ORF1 encodes enzymes essential for PCV2 replication and generates multiple mRNA splice variants that also contribute to viral replication [16]. Conversely, ORF2 encodes the capsid protein (Cap), the major structural protein of PCV2, and serves as the primary target for vaccine development. This protein acts as an antigenic target for neutralizing antibodies, thereby blocking viral entry into host cells [17].

Porcine circovirus-like virus P1 is unique among circoviruses in exhibiting a clinical detection rate markedly higher than that of other PCV-related viruses [18] and an exceptionally broad host range; in addition to being pathogenic in pigs, replication has been demonstrated in cattle, goats, dogs, cats, yaks, and rabbits [19–21]. The P1 genome consists of a single-stranded circular DNA molecule, the smallest known among animal viruses, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Except for the 16 nucleotides hypothesized to originate from a porcine endogenous retrovirus, the remaining 632 nucleotides share high sequence homology with PCV2 ORF2. Despite its compact genome size, eight ORFs have been experimentally confirmed. ORF1, ORF2, ORF4, and ORF5 are located on the negative strand at genomic positions 151–493, 492–579, 279–327, and 1–52, respectively, whereas ORF3, ORF6, ORF7, and ORF8 are located on the positive strand at positions 494–599, 263–293, 285–336, and 374–419 [22], respectively. ORF1 is the largest ORF in P1 and encodes a Cap composed of 114 amino acids, with an estimated molecular mass of approximately 12.5 kDa. Its N-terminal amino acid sequence is highly homologous to that of the Cap of PCV2; however, owing to a single-nucleotide deletion relative to PCV2, the C-terminal region lacks homology with PCV2 [23]. Furthermore, while the origin of DNA replication (Ori) in PCV2 contains a canonical stem-loop structure, where the stem is formed by base-pairing of two 11-nt inverted repeats and the loop harbors a highly conserved octanucleotide motif [24], this structural element is entirely absent in P1.

One study established a porcine pancreatic cDNA library and employed yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screening to identify host proteins interacting with the Cap of P1, ultimately identifying four porcine proteins that specifically bind to P1 Cap [25]. Another study demonstrated that P1 infection significantly suppressed Wnt signaling activity, both in vivo and in vitro [26]. Moreover, a study revealed that the Cap of P1 virus participates in pancreatic secretory signaling pathways, thereby modulating the expression of pancreatic amylase, trypsin, and lipase, and consequently disrupting host digestive function [25]. Although substantial progress has been made in elucidating the pathogenic mechanisms of P1, the molecular basis governing its replication and genetic stability remain poorly understood. Therefore, in this study, we performed site-directed mutagenesis of all eight ORFs and a hexanucleotide of the P1 virus to ablate their native functions, thereby enabling a systematic assessment of the contribution of each ORF and nucleotide to viral replication in PK-15 cells. Notably, the hexanucleotide GGATCC corresponds precisely to the recognition site of the restriction enzyme BamHI and forms a palindromic sequence, which is hypothesized to play a role in P1 viral DNA replication and/or DNA damage repair.

Methods

Experimental design

To create a stable infectious clone for mutagenesis studies, the tandem dimeric full-length genomic DNA of the P1 virus was chemically synthesized and cloned into the pBluescript II SK(+) vector, generating the plasmid rpSK-2P1. The rpSK-2P1 plasmid served as the parental template for constructing all subsequent P1 mutants. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed using the Mut Express Universal Fast Mutagenesis Kit with specifically designed primers to introduce defined nucleotide substitutions at the targeted sites. Two categories of mutations were engineered: (i) single-nucleotide substitutions were introduced individually into each of the eight ORFs of P1, with careful design to ensure that no amino acid sequence was altered in any overlapping ORF; and (ii) single-nucleotide substitutions were introduced at six specific genomic positions in the P1 genome, each replaced by its complementary base pair.

To propagate mutants and test their viability, the constructed recombinant mutant plasmids were transfected into PK-15 cells, and the first-passage virus (F1) was then harvested. A portion of the F1 virus was used for nucleic acid extraction, followed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification to assess viral DNA replication. The remaining F1 virus was inoculated into fresh PK-15 cells to generate a second-passage virus (F2), which was subsequently used for serial passaging. All mutant viruses were serially passaged up to the fourth passage (F4), and viral DNA replication was monitored at each passage. Additionally, genomic DNA extracted from F4 viruses was ligated into a standard cloning vector, transformed into Escherichia coli (E. coli) DH5α competent cells, and selected for Sanger sequencing to evaluate the genetic stability of the introduced mutations.

To analyze the replication kinetics of P1 mutant viruses in PK-15 cells, F1 virus stocks were inoculated onto fresh PK-15 monolayers, and cell samples were harvested 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-infection. Viral genomic copies were quantified using quantitative real-time PCR, and growth curves were generated based on the viral copy numbers over time. In a parallel experiment, the F1 virus was re-inoculated into fresh PK-15 cells and cultured for 72 h. Cells were then lysed, and total protein was resolved by SDS-PAGE. Protein bands corresponding to the expected molecular weights of viral proteins were excised and subjected to mass spectrometry to directly identify the repertoire of proteins translated following infection with the mutant virus.

Plasmids, cells, and monoclonal antibodies

This study utilized a P1 isolate (GenBank accession no. EF514716) to construct a dimeric infectious clone. This isolate has been previously confirmed to induce PMWS in piglets [27].

All experiments were performed using PK-15 cells. Cell culture procedure: Ten milliliters of pre-warmed high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% Gibco fetal bovine serum was added to the culture flask. The cells were gently dissociated into a single-cell suspension by pipetting. The cell density was adjusted to approximately 2.5 × 105 cells/mL and seeded at a 1:5 split ratio into a new T25 flask. The cultures were then returned to the incubator and maintained for 48 h.

The study also employed a mouse monoclonal antibody specifically raised against P1, which recognizes the Cap of P1 [28].

Site-directed mutagenesis

To systematically assess the functional importance of each P1 ORF and the hexanucleotide motif (GGATCC), site-directed mutations were introduced into the P1 dimeric circular genome. The molecular clones of the P1 virus, each carrying a deletion of one of the eight ORFs caused by mutations in that specific ORF, were constructed and preserved in our laboratory. In this study, they were sequentially designated Y1, Y2, Y3, Y4, Y5, Y6, Y7, and Y8, respectively [29, 30]. Additionally, six nucleotide-level site-directed mutations were generated and labeled as H1, H2, H3, H4, H5, and H6. This nomenclature was consistently used to denote the corresponding mutant viral genomes during the study.

Site-directed mutations were introduced into the dimeric P1 genome using a Mut Express Universal Fast Mutagenesis Kit (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Mutation-specific primers were designed to contain a 15–21 bp reverse-complementary region flanking the target site and at least 15 bp of a non-complementary sequence harboring the desired mutation (see Section“site-directed mutagenesis oligonucleotide primers”). The plasmid rpSK-2P1 served as the template for high-fidelity PCR amplification using 2× Phanta Flash Master Mix. The 50 µL PCR reaction mixture consisted of 25 µL of 2× Phanta Flash Master Mix, 2 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), and 21 µL of template DNA. The thermal cycling program was as follows: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 62 °C for 5 s, and extension at 72 °C for 5–10 s per kb, with a final extension at 72 °C for 1 min.

The PCR products were treated with DpnI restriction enzyme at 37 °C for 15 min to digest the methylated parental template DNA. If amplification was specific and no non-specific bands were observed, the DpnI-digested product was used directly in the subsequent recombination reaction; otherwise, the product was purified by agarose gel electrophoresis and gel extraction for further use. The recombination reaction was performed in 2× CE Mix V3 (Vazyme, China). DpnI-digested DNA (up to 10 µL total volume) was added according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, and nuclease-free double-distilled water (ddH2O) was used to adjust the final reaction volume to 10 µL. The mixture was then incubated at 50 °C for 5 min. The resulting recombinant plasmids were directly transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells and plated on Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates containing the appropriate antibiotic for the selection of positive clones. Putative positive colonies were verified by colony PCR using the specific primers 81 F and 64R [31], followed by Sanger sequencing to confirm the intended mutations.

Virus passaging and assessment of DNA replication

To initiate viral replication and generate infectious progeny, the constructed recombinant mutant plasmids were transfected into PK-15 cells. At 6 h post-transfection, the culture medium was replaced, and the cells were incubated for an additional 72 h before harvesting the first-passage virus (F1). The F1 virus stock was subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles to release the viral particles. To distinguish newly replicated viral DNA from the transfected plasmid template, a portion of the lysate was used to extract genomic DNA from infected cells using a commercial kit (TIANDZ, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, followed by digestion with DpnI restriction endonuclease to eliminate residual input plasmid DNA. Viral DNA replication was assessed by PCR amplification using the P1-specific primers [31].

An aliquot of the remaining F1 virus was inoculated onto a fresh monolayer of PK-15 cells at a 1:10 dilution. After adsorption for 6 h, the inoculum was removed and replaced with a fresh culture medium. The cells were then incubated for 72 h before harvesting the second-passage virus (F2). A portion of F2 was used for DNA replication analysis, as described above, while the remainder was carried forward for subsequent passaging.

The above passaging procedure was repeated to serially propagate all the mutant viruses up to the fourth passage (F4). At each passage, viral DNA was subjected to PCR amplification using P1-specific primers to monitor replication.

Detection of the P1 ORF1 transcript

To verify continued transcription of ORF1 despite engineered mutations, reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) was performed. F3 virus was inoculated onto fresh PK-15 cell monolayers at a 1:10 dilution ratio. After adsorption for 6 h, the inoculum was replaced with fresh culture medium, and the cells were incubated for 72 h to harvest fourth-passage (F4) virus–infected cells. Total RNA was extracted from F4-infected PK-15 cells using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using HiFiScript gDNA Removal RT MasterMix (CWBIO, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, conventional PCR was performed using ORF1-specific primers, and the resulting amplicons (290 bp) were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis and sequencing of cloned products [22].

Growth curve analysis

To determine the replication kinetics of each mutant, the F1 virus was inoculated into PK-15 cells under the same conditions. Cells were harvested at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-infection to obtain F2 virus–infected samples. Total DNA was extracted using a commercial kit (TIANDZ, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and P1 viral genome copy number was quantified by quantitative PCR (qPCR) using the ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) according to published literature [32]. The sequence of the forward primer is 5’- GAGAGGCGGGTGTTGAAGAT-3’ and the reverse primer are 5’- AAGACCCCCCACTTAAACCC-3’, and the amplification product is l19 bp for P1. Replication curves were plotted as genome copies per milliliter versus time post-infection.

The 20 µL qPCR reaction mixture contained 10.0 µL of 2× ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix, 0.4 µL of forward primer, 0.4 µL of reverse primer, 1.2 µL of cDNA template, and 8.0 µL of nuclease-free ddH2O. The final concentration of each primer was 0.2 µM.

The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s (denaturation) and 60 °C for 30 s (annealing/extension). Melting curve analysis was performed at the end of amplification by gradually increasing the temperature from 60 °C to 95 °C over 60 s to confirm amplicon specificity.

Genetic stability

To evaluate the genetic stability of the introduced mutations after prolonged replication, genomic DNA extracted from the fourth-passage (F4) virus was subjected to PCR amplification according to the literature [31], and the resulting full-length genomic nucleotide sequence fragments (648 bp) were ligated into a standard blunt-end cloning vector using the Zero TOPO-Blunt Cloning Kit (Sangon Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The ligation products were transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells, which were then plated on selective LB agar and incubated for 12–14 h at 37 °C. The cloning products were picked and subjected to Sanger sequencing to assess the genetic stability of the introduced mutations. There were two DpnI-sensitive sites in the rpSK-2P1 DNAs propagated in E. coli. To exclude the possible influence of residual plasmids, we digested the extracted genomic DNA with DpnI enzyme before PCR amplification. At the same time, we also performed PCR amplification on the extracted genomic DNA using T7 and T3 primers.

SDS-PAGE and mass spectrometry analysis

To confirm restoration of Cap expression in the reverted Y1 mutant, Y1 F1 virus was inoculated into PK-15 cells, and cells infected with the Y1 virus were harvested 72 h post-infection (F2). Cell pellets were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with 1% PMSF until the lysate exhibited a viscous and stringy consistency, indicating complete lysis. The samples were then boiled at 100 °C for 10 min in a metal bath, centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 1 min, cooled to room temperature, and loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels consisting of a 15% resolving gel and a 5% stacking gel. Electrophoresis was first conducted at 80 V for the stacking gel, then switched to 120 V for the resolving gel, and followed by staining with coomassie brilliant blue R-250 (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for at least 1 h. The band corresponding to the target protein (12.5 kDa) was excised and subjected to mass spectrometry.

Protein identification was performed using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). The protein sample was digested with trypsin, and the resulting peptides were desalted using a C18 solid-phase extraction column. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The eluted peptides were directly introduced into an Easy-nLC 1200 ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (ThermoFisher Scientific) coupled to a Q Exactive Plus high-resolution mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific) for analysis. Mass spectrometry was performed in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. The high-resolution MS spectrum was acquired with a mass range from 300 to1500 m/z followed by high energy collision dissociation (HCD) fragmentation of the top 20 most abundant peptide ions, with a dynamic exclusion of 25 s. MS2 spectra were recorded at 17,500 resolution with automatic gain control (AGC) target set to 1◊105 and a maximum of 50 ms injection time. The normalized collision energy (NCE) was 28, and the spectra were recorded in profile mode. Raw MS/MS data were processed using the Proteome Discoverer software (version 2.5) and searched against the NCBI Porcine circovirus protein database to identify viral and host proteins.

Immunohistochemistry

To visualize Cap expression and subcellular localization, immunohistochemical staining of infected cells was performed using the SABC-AP Immunohistochemistry Kit (Boster Biological Technology, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, PK-15 cells transfected with mutant viral plasmids were cultured for 72 h, after which the culture medium was removed. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody, mouse monoclonal anti-P1 Cap antibody, diluted to an appropriate concentration. After three washes with PBS, a biotin-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody was applied and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. For detection, the chromogenic substrate was added and allowed to develop at room temperature (25 °C) in the dark, and the reaction time was monitored under a light microscope to prevent overdevelopment. The staining results were observed and documented using bright-field microscopy. Negative control samples were processed in parallel by substituting transfected cells with uninfected (mock) PK-15 cells.

Results

Site-directed mutagenesis at nucleotide sites in the P1 viral genome

For the specific mutation sites of the constructed P1 molecular clones lacking each ORF, see the literature [29, 30]. Briefly, Y1 had an T-to-A mutation at position 495; Y2 had a T-to-C mutation at position 587; Y3 had a T-to-C mutation at position 501; Y4 had an A-to-G mutation at position 334; Y5 had an A-to-G mutation at position 59; Y6 had an G-to-C mutation at position 271; Y7 had an T-to-C mutation at position 292; and Y8 had a G-to-T mutation at position 382. To target the conserved hexanucleotide motif, a series of mutant plasmids (H1 to H6) was generated based on the parental plasmid rpSK-2P1 by introducing single-nucleotide substitutions within the GGATCC-containing region of the P1 genome. Specifically, H1 harbors a G→C substitution at nucleotide position 1 (nt1), H2 carries a G→C substitution at nt2, H3 contains an A→T substitution at nt3, H4 features a T→A substitution at nt4, H5 has a C→G substitution at nt5, and H6 bears a C→G substitution at nt6.

Replication competence of P1 mutant genomes in PK-15 cells

To determine whether the mutations abolished genome replication, PCR analysis of P1 mutant genomes revealed that DpnI-resistant replicative-form viral DNA was detectable in all mutant groups at every passage, confirming their capacity for initial genome replication. However, viral DNA replication markedly declined with increasing passage numbers. Figure 2 presents the PCR results for the first-passage (F1) P1 mutant genomes.

To assess the transcriptional activity of ORF1 in the 14 P1 mutant viruses, total RNA was extracted from PK-15 cells infected with the virus and subjected to RT-PCR. Replication kinetics of P1 mutant viruses in PK-15 cells

To evaluate replication efficiency, first-passage (F1) viruses generated from the 14 mutant plasmids were individually inoculated onto fresh PK-15 cells, and the infected cells were harvested at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-infection. Viral genome copy numbers were quantified by qPCR using P1-specific primers, and growth curves were plotted, as shown in Fig. 4. The results revealed that most P1 mutant viruses exhibited a significant increase in genomic copy number between 48 and 72 h post-infection, reaching peak levels at 72 h. By 96 h, viral copy numbers either declined or plateaued, with statistically significant differences between the groups. These findings demonstrate that the engineered P1 mutant viruses are capable of efficient replication in PK-15 cells and sustain robust proliferation within the first 72 h of infection.

Discussion

The results of this study showed that, although none of the introduced mutations in the eight ORFs or the six targeted nucleotides abolished P1 genome replication in PK-15 cells, nearly all mutants exhibited selective reversion to the wild-type sequence by the fourth passage. This suggests that the original (wild-type) nucleotides confer a replicative advantage during sustained viral propagation in the PK-15 cells. Despite that, none of the targeted ORFs or nucleotide positions appeared to be essential for P1 genome replication, since initial replication occurred in all mutants, the frequent reversion events indicated that these regions significantly influenced viral fitness. Thus, while dispensable for minimal replication competence, these genomic elements play an important modulatory role in the efficiency and stability of P1 replication.

Based on these findings, we infer that the key determinant influencing P1 virus replication resides not only in the viral genome itself, but also in its interaction with host proteins. A study using a yeast two-hybrid system was used to screen for and identify interactions between the Cap of P1 and host cellular proteins. Using P1 Cap as bait against a cDNA library derived from pancreatic tissue of healthy piglets, five potential interacting partners were isolated: endonuclease/exonuclease/phosphatase family protein (EEP), Ral guanine nucleotide dissociation stimulator (Ral GDS), Bcl-2-like protein 12 (Bcl-2-L-12), carbamoyl-phosphate synthase 1 (CPS1), and one protein of unknown function. Among these, the physical interaction between Cap and Ral GDS was confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation in HEK293T cells. These host proteins participate in key biological processes, including signal transduction, apoptosis regulation, metabolic control, and nucleic acid modification, suggesting that P1 may exploit Cap–host protein interactions to enhance viral replication or pathogenesis. These results provide important insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying P1 infection and disease [25].

In prior studies, ORF1 of P1 has been established as the largest open reading frame, encoding a 114-amino-acid Cap essential for virion assembly [23]. In the present study, a site-directed mutation was introduced into ORF1 of the parental plasmid rpSK-2P1 (designated Y1), which was expected to abolish Cap expression, thereby preventing the formation of structurally intact virions and precluding productive replication and passaging. Surprisingly, the Y1 mutant supported stable viral replication and serial passage in PK-15 cells. Kinetic analysis of P1 mutant replication revealed that Y1 exhibited markedly reduced genome copy numbers at the second passage (F2) compared with other mutants, indicating severely impaired replication efficiency at this early stage. Notably, sequencing of the F4 Y1 virus revealed that all randomly selected clones reverted to the wild-type sequence at the mutation site, strongly suggesting selective pressure favoring the restoration of the original nucleotide. Consistent with this, SDS-PAGE followed by mass spectrometry of the target band from F2 Y1-infected cells unambiguously identified the ORF1-encoded Cap. Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining of PK-15 cells infected with first-passage (F1) Y1 virus confirmed robust cytoplasmic expression of the Cap, consistent with the proper translation and subcellular localization. Additionally, RT-PCR performed on RNA extracted from the F4 Y1 virus yielded a specific amplicon of the expected size, demonstrating that the ORF1 transcript remained intact and transcriptionally active despite the engineered mutation. Collectively, these findings indicated that reversion at the Y1 mutation site occurred as early as the first passage, albeit at a low frequency, which was sufficient to permit limited virion production but resulted in significantly reduced viral yields. With successive passages, revertant genomes progressively outcompeted the mutant population because of their replicative advantage, ultimately leading to near-complete reversion by F4. This dynamic underscores the critical role of ORF1 integrity in P1 fitness and highlights the strong selective pressure against mutations that compromise Cap expression, even when initial replication appears to be feasible.

PCV2 has a single-stranded, covalently closed circular DNA genome. The origin of DNA replication (Ori) resides within the large intergenic region (LIR) and features a canonical stem-loop structure essential for replication initiation. This structure comprises two distinct elements: a double-stranded stem formed by base pairing between two 11-nucleotide inverted repeats flanking the loop and a conserved nonanucleotide loop sequence at the apex—AAGTATTAC. Embedded within this loop is a highly conserved octanucleotide core motif, AGTATTAC, which has been rigorously demonstrated to be indispensable for viral DNA replication, viral protein expression, and stable production of progeny virions. This octamer is defined as an essential core element (ECE), with the consensus sequence AxTAxTAC. In this motif, the adenine (A) and thymine (T) residues at specific positions are absolutely invariant—any substitution abolishes function—whereas the two positions denoted by “x” tolerate limited nucleotide variation without compromising the replicative capacity. These mutant viruses were continuously passaged in cells for four passages, and reversion to wild-type virus was either not detected or not examined. In addition to the stem-loop, the Ori region harbors multiple tandem repeats, including a 6-nt motif (CGGCAG) and a 5-nt motif (CACCT), which are thought to modulate replication efficiency, possibly through interactions with host or viral replication factors. Experimental evidence confirms that site-directed mutagenesis of critical nucleotides within the ECE completely abrogates PCV2 DNA replication, establishing the stem-loop as a non-redundant functional module in the viral life cycle, in addition to being the initiation platform for rolling circle replication [24]. Additionally, mutational analyses showed that Rep and Rep’ proteins of PCV2 are absolutely essential for viral protein synthesis and DNA replication; their disruption reduces antigen-positive cells by > 99% and abolishes DNA replication. In contrast, the capsid RNA (CR), Rep3a, Rep3b, Rep3c, NS515, NS672, and NS0 are nonessential, mutations in these units do not affect viral protein production or DNA replication [33].

The genome of P1 virus lacks a stem-loop structure and a replicase associated with replication, as found in PCV2. Although its genomic nucleotide sequence shares high homology with PCV2 ORF2, there is a 16-nucleotide region (CGTTACTAGTGGATCC) exhibiting extremely low homology with PCV2. Within this region, six nucleotides (GGATCC) constitute a potential restriction enzyme recognition sequence [23]. In this study, we introduced transversion mutations into these six nucleotides of interest. Furthermore, using the eight previously constructed molecular clones carrying sequential ORF deletion mutations, we investigated their effects on P1 virus replication and protein synthesis. The results showed that these mutations did not affect P1 virus replication or protein synthesis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that multiple sites in the P1 genome, although not absolutely essential for initial replication, are under strong positive selection in cell culture. Although these mutations do not abolish genome synthesis, reversion mutations, particularly in ORF1 encoding the capsid protein of P1 virus, may indicate that certain nucleotide sites within the viral genome play a beneficial role during viral evolution. This observation suggests that these sites may confer subtle fitness costs or undetected functions, thereby blurring the distinction between ‘essential’ and ‘non-essential’ sites or genes and offering a new perspective on the replication of circular ssDNA viruses.
