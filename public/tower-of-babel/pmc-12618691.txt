Design and analysis of a secure image encryption algorithm using proposed non-linear RN chaotic system and ECC/HKDF key derivation with authentication support
Authors: He Zhiqiang, Abdul Rauf, Arif Nazir, Fairouz Tchier, Adnan Aslam, Keneni Abera Tola
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnsuring digital image security is critical in today’s edge computing and networked communication environments. Chaotic systems offer powerful tools for lightweight, real-time encryption, but often suffer from limited chaotic ranges and weak key sensitivity. This paper introduces a robust nonlinear RN chaotic system, combining two chaotic maps to enhance unpredictability and expand the chaotic interval. A fast image encryption scheme is then developed by integrating this system with elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman (ECDH) key exchange, hierarchical key derivation (HKDF), and dynamic chaotic S-box generation. The scheme ensures plaintext-independent key generation, authentication via ECDSA, and integrity through HMAC-SHA256, thereby preventing man-in-the-middle attacks and key leakage. Using the same set of security keys, this algorithm can produce a completely different encrypted image each time it is applied to the same original image. The proposed technique consistently achieves near-ideal entropy levels (~ 7.999), high NPCR (> 99.61%), and UACI above 32%, image complexity (with a keyspace equal to 10256), and resistance to security attacks, demonstrating outstanding sensitivity and randomness. The low PSNR values and limited correlation of the encrypted images further show strong resilience to statistical and differential assaults. The proposed approach outperforms existing methods in terms of computational reliability, encryption strength, and unpredictability. This work provides a dependable, portable, and efficient solution for secure image transfer in surveillance and multimedia applications.Keywords: Image encryption, Chaotic system, Elliptic curve Diffie–Hellman (ECDH), HKDF-SHA256, HMAC-SHA256, Chaos theory, Security analysisSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The public network is used to send billions of images because of the rapid advancement of communication. More dependable, quicker security systems are needed for various applications, like military image databases and systems for medical imaging, to store and transfer digital images1–3. As a result, maintaining the security of digital images has become essential. Three techniques have been suggested in light of the situation: steganography, watermarking, and encryption4. One of the key security features of services for electronic health is the privacy of patient information. For instance, patient medical records must remain private. In addition, the speed at which technologies are developing to gain access to people’s data means that protection strategies need to be enhanced. In telecare medical information systems (TMIS), the privacy and security of medical image transfer is a critical topic that must be carefully considered. Image data transferred over a risky network from one location to another is typically characterised by its confidentiality, veracity, privacy, and wholeness. Consequently, DICIM (digital imaging communication in medicine) should place a higher priority on protecting sensitive data contained in medical images. Numerous challenges employing diverse cryptographic methods have been put out in the literature to address this textual issue5–8.

High medical image-inserting capabilities are provided by the hybrid encryption approach that Abdel-Nabi and Al-Haj proposed in their work, which uses watermarking. To guarantee that transferred and stored medical image security standards are met, an algorithm that combines traditional encryption approaches with reversible data-caching techniques is suggested9. Numerous image encryption algorithms, including SCAN10,11, random circular grids12,13, an elliptic curve ElGamal14, grey code15, wave transmission16, vector quantization17, wavelet transform with fractions18,19, p-Fibonacci transform20, and chaos21–26, have been put out using a variety of methods, taking into account the attributes of image data

Chaos-based image encryption has emerged as an effective and superior encryption technique. This is due to the non-convergence, chaotic property, state ergodicity, and extreme sensitivity of chaotic systems/maps back to their starting points and control settings. This has led to the development of a large number of chaotic image encryption algorithms that directly use pre-existing chaotic systems or maps in their encryption processes27,28. A chaos-based image encryption technique consists of two main components: a chaotic system and an image encryption algorithm. The chaotic sequence is unpredictable and pseudo-random because the chaotic system produces a series of state values with aperiodic ergodicity and great sensitivity to initial conditions. These characteristics of chaotic sequences match the essential specifications of cryptography. As a result, chaotic sequences are important in cryptography, and image encryption algorithms based on them are becoming increasingly popular7,29,30.

In general, chaotic systems fall into two types: high-dimensional continuous-time systems described by differential equations and low-dimensional discrete-time systems characterized by iterative maps. Low-dimensional discrete chaotic maps have an easily understood structure, a faster software computation time, and are easier to construct on hardware. However, the following flaws are present in numerous popular low-dimensional discrete chaotic maps: the map is easily predictable and has a small number of parameters. Furthermore, there is an irregular distribution of state values, and there is a narrow range of parameters that behave chaotically. Systems like the well-known logistic map31, tent map32, sine map33, etc., are a few examples. The risk of decreased security is associated with information encryption because of the aforementioned weaknesses of such chaotic systems. In contrast, the chaotic system with continuous time and enormous dimensions exhibits a large parameter interval of chaotic behavior and a greater number of variables and parameters. However, real-time encryption applications cannot benefit from this complex dynamical system represented by differential equations since implementing it on hardware presents extra challenges and has a higher processing overhead in software34. Kovalchuk et al. suggested an encryption-decryption method that utilizes topological image protection and the Rivest–Shamir–Adleman (RSA) algorithm components. The primary benefits of the suggested methods are achieved through the use of functionally fluctuating intensity images35. Another study examined the impact of the noise-adding functions applied to the original image and the various simple integer values of the RSA scheme on the process’s outcomes. Contours were not allowed to appear in the encrypted image as a result of these settings36.

In addition, several methods for image encryption and decryption are employed, including projective transformations37, binary linear-quadratic transformations38, and linear and quadratic fractal algorithms39,40. Hafsa et al. suggested an image encryption model based on a modified Advanced Encryption Standard and a sophisticated pseudo-random number generator based on chaos. The Altera Cyclone III board was used in the design of the entire system. The outcomes demonstrated that the cryptography method was quicker and more resistant to particular kinds of attacks41. Kari et al.42 presented a new grayscale image cryptosystem based on chaotic hybrid maps. The proposed system offers enhanced features, including more dynamic chaotic behavior and greater chaotic ranges, based on the findings.

With the growing reliance on digital imagery in areas such as surveillance, medical diagnostics, and satellite monitoring, secure and efficient image encryption has become essential. Conventional ciphers like AES and DES often fall short due to image redundancy and real-time processing demands, prompting a shift toward chaos-based schemes. Recent works employ multi-dimensional hyperchaotic systems and FPGA implementations for high throughput and robustness43, while others combine chaotic dynamics with nonlinear transformations such as Arnold’s Cat Map and Langton’s Ant to enhance diffusion and resistance to attacks44. A multiple-image encryption algorithm integrating hyperchaotic systems, SVD, RC5, and a chaos-based Hill cipher further strengthens protection against traffic analysis and cryptanalytic attacks, particularly for satellite imagery45. Lightweight designs using circular shifts and new chaotic maps also demonstrate fast execution with strong security46. Together, these advances underscore the potential of chaos-driven approaches to meet modern image security challenges.

The main contributions of this work can be summarized as follows. We propose a hybrid crypto–chaotic image encryption framework that combines elliptic curve Diffie–Hellman (ECDH) key exchange, HKDF-SHA256 key derivation, proposed LSS chaotic map and Lorenz chaotic dynamics, thereby grounding the scheme in strong cryptographic principles while leveraging nonlinear chaotic sensitivity. Unlike earlier approaches where the plaintext hash was used directly as the key, the proposed design establishes a 256-bit master key independent of the image, ensuring robustness against the “one-time pad” weakness and enhancing key diversity. The SHA-256 hash of the plaintext image is incorporated solely into an HMAC-SHA256 tag, which guarantees ciphertext integrity and authentication without influencing the encryption key. The master key is further transformed into 64-bit seeds that initialize Lorenz chaotic sequences, enabling the construction of dynamic substitution boxes and two-dimensional diffusion mechanisms that amplify nonlinearity, diffusion, and resistance against statistical and differential attacks. Finally, the security of the scheme is rigorously validated through comprehensive evaluation metrics, including MSE, PSNR, SSIM, NPCR, UACI, entropy, and statistical analyses, demonstrating its robustness and superiority over conventional image encryption approaches.

Modified algorithm for encrypting images.

The proposed image encryption framework integrates cryptographic hashing, elliptic curve cryptography (ECC), elliptic curve Diffie–Hellman (ECDH), and a hierarchical key derivation function (HKDF-SHA256) to securely generate nonlinear and cryptographically strong initial parameters for the chaotic encryption process. A 256-bit session key is established through an ECDH exchange and further processed by HKDF using a random nonce to derive multiple independent subkeys—specifically for encryption, substitution-box (S-box) generation, and message authentication. To protect against man-in-the-middle attacks, the sender’s ECDH public key is digitally signed using the Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA), and its authenticity is verified by the receiver prior to key agreement. The plaintext image plays no role in the key generation process, thereby eliminating any one-time pad dependency. Separately, the SHA-256 hash of the plaintext is employed only for computing an HMAC-SHA256 tag to ensure integrity verification after decryption.

Within the encryption core, the Lorenz chaotic system (LSS) is initialized with parameters derived exclusively from the HKDF subkeys, ensuring high sensitivity to key variations. Dynamically generated S-boxes and chaotic sequences drive substitution and diffusion operations, enhancing nonlinearity and unpredictability. Each of the six encryption rounds performs three main transformations: (i) substitution via key-dependent S-boxes combined with XOR operations, (ii) pixel diffusion through simultaneous row–column propagation to amplify local changes, and (iii) permutation via 90° counterclockwise matrix rotation to strengthen spatial confusion. The output of each round serves as the input to the next, compounding diffusion and confusion effects iteratively. Collectively, this design—combining public-key cryptography, digital signature authentication, key diversification, and chaotic dynamics—ensures strong key security, high plaintext sensitivity, and resistance to statistical, differential, and man-in-the-middle attacks(Fig. 4).

Image hashing for integrity verification

Given a grayscale image I of size , we compute its SHA-256 hash: H = SHA-256(I), where H is a 256-bit digest. This hash is not used to derive encryption keys. Instead, H is later combined with the master key in an HMAC-SHA256 computation to generate an authentication tag. This tag ensures plaintext sensitivity for verification purposes: even a one-bit change in I completely alters H, causing the authentication tag to fail during decryption. Consequently, the HMAC process provides strong integrity assurance and tamper detection without compromising the cryptographic separation between key generation and image content.

Algorithm characteristics

The proposed image encryption framework establishes a fully cryptographically grounded hybrid system that integrates elliptic curve Diffie–Hellman (ECDH) for secure key exchange, ECDSA-based digital signatures for key authentication, and hierarchical key derivation (HKDF-SHA256) for structured subkey generation. Unlike traditional chaotic encryption schemes or one-time pad (OTP)-like constructions, the proposed design ensures that the plaintext image does not participate in key or parameter generation. Instead, all encryption parameters are derived solely from the shared ECDH secret and a random nonce, ensuring true plaintext independence and eliminating self-encryption vulnerabilities.

To prevent man-in-the-middle (MITM) attacks, the sender’s ECDH public key is digitally signed using ECDSA before exchange, and verified by the receiver using the sender’s public verification key. This guarantees the authenticity of the key exchange and prevents adversaries from substituting fake public keys during transmission. The verified shared secret is then processed through HKDF-SHA256, which deterministically derives three distinct subkeys: for encryption, for S-box generation, and for HMAC-based authentication.

The chaotic subsystem employs the Lorenz system to generate dynamic, key-dependent, and plaintext-independent S-boxes and keystreams. These are used for nonlinear substitution, pixel diffusion, and two-dimensional permutation, collectively reinforcing Shannon’s principles of confusion and diffusion. The use of chaotic sequences derived from cryptographic keys rather than image content ensures that even small key changes yield significantly different encryption outcomes, while identical images encrypted multiple times produce distinct ciphertexts due to nonce-based key diversification.

The algorithm maintains computational efficiency by using elliptic curve operations only during session initialization, while the encryption rounds rely on lightweight arithmetic and bitwise operations suitable for real-time and resource-constrained devices.

From a security perspective, the hybrid integration of ECDH key exchange, ECDSA authentication, HKDF-based key expansion, and Lorenz chaotic dynamics provides:

Collectively, these features make the proposed system cryptographically sound, efficient, and resistant to both traditional and cryptanalytic attacks.

Chosen-plaintext attack

Strong diffusion properties allow an image encryption technique to successfully fend against chosen-plaintext attacks. However, a serious flaw in many of the current image encryption systems is that identical plaintext images encrypted with the same encryption key also produce identical ciphertexts. Because of this predictability, an adversary may use known plaintext-ciphertext pairs to reverse the encryption process or infer important information, a technique known as chosen-plaintext attacks.

The suggested encryption scheme uses a random pixel insertion mechanism to get around this restriction. By adding dynamic randomness to the encryption process, the technique can generate completely distinct ciphertexts every time it encrypts the same original image, even when the secret keys are used. In Fig. 8, the algorithm encrypts the same image (Fig. 9a) twice with the same key conditions, experimentally validating this property. The pixel-wise absolute difference between the two resulting encrypted images, E1 and E2 (Fig. 9b and c), illustrates how different they are (Fig. 9d), as confirmed by their histograms.

Ciphertext-only attacks

A typical threat model in cryptography is represented by ciphertext-only attacks, in which the attacker tries to deduce information about the original image or encryption key by using only the encrypted image (ciphertext). In these situations, statistical characteristics like entropy, histogram uniformity, and pixel correlation are commonly used to assess how resilient an image encryption technique is. To successfully hide every aspect of the original image, a secure encryption technique should have high entropy, a uniform histogram distribution, and almost negligible correlation between neighboring pixels.

Figures 5 and 7 illustrate how well the suggested approach performs against ciphertext-only assaults. There are no obvious patterns and a great deal of visual randomness in the encrypted image (Fig. 5). A well-distributed pixel intensity range is shown by its almost uniform histogram (Fig. 5). In addition, statistical analysis shows low horizontal, vertical, and diagonal pixel correlations (Fig. 7) and a high entropy value near the optimal value of 8 for an 8-bit grayscale image (Table 6). Together, these traits validate the algorithm’s resilience to ciphertext-only attacks by confirming that the ciphertext does not provide any structural information about the plaintext image.

Noise-based and cropping attacks

Digital images are frequently susceptible to transmission defects in real-world applications, such as noise introduction or partial data loss. In the event of such disturbances, a strong image encryption technique must be able to preserve recoverability and visual fidelity.

The simulation results assessing the suggested technique under two typical attack scenarios—noise interference and data loss—are shown in Fig. 9. These outcomes further illustrate the usefulness and robustness of the suggested encryption technique in actual communication situations by confirming its resistance to noise and data loss threats.

Conclusion

The proposed image encryption algorithm achieves a robust fusion of elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC), authenticated ECDH key exchange, and HKDF-based hierarchical key derivation, combined with chaotic system–driven dynamic S-box generation. Unlike conventional chaotic schemes, the presented framework ensures complete plaintext independence, as all encryption parameters are derived solely from the ECDH shared secret and a random nonce, rather than from the image content itself. This design effectively eliminates one-time pad (OTP)–like self-encryption behavior and prevents potential key leakage. Furthermore, by integrating ECDSA-based key authentication, the scheme provides resistance against man-in-the-middle (MITM) attacks during key exchange, while HMAC-SHA256 guarantees ciphertext integrity and tamper detection. The Lorenz chaotic sequences and dynamic S-boxes introduce strong nonlinearity, confusion, and diffusion, producing ciphertexts with uniform histogram distributions and high randomness. Overall, the algorithm combines cryptographic rigor with computational efficiency, offering a secure, authenticated, and lightweight image encryption framework suitable for modern multimedia and IoT security applications.

The proposed image encryption algorithm demonstrates a strong integration of elliptic-curve key exchange (ECDH), HKDF-based key derivation, and chaotic system–driven S-box generation. By deriving master keys from the ECDH shared secret and separating authentication via HMAC-SHA256, the design avoids key leakage through plaintext dependence and ensures data integrity. The chaotic sequences and dynamic S-boxes achieve both high confusion and diffusion, producing cipher images that exhibit uniform histogram distributions and noise-like randomness.

Through in-depth statistical, visual, and cryptographic investigations, a strong and safe image encryption-decryption technique has been created and assessed in this study. Strong diffusion metrics (NPCR > 99.58%, UACI > 32.07%), little correlation between neighbouring pixels in encrypted images, and high entropy values (nearing the optimal value of 8) all show that the technique exhibits good encryption strength across several typical test images. These outcomes demonstrate how well the algorithm can remove observable patterns and structures from the original images. By showing a notable loss of visual similarity while preserving consistency in the reconstruction of the decrypted image, visual quality metrics like PSNR and SSIM further support the encryption performance. To withstand differential attacks, the algorithm must also be highly sensitive to even slight input changes.

The system’s applicability across diverse test images, including chest, brain, man, and tree, shows its adaptability to real-world scenarios such as secure medical imaging, surveillance, and private communications. Current validation has been performed on a limited benchmark dataset, and optimization for large-scale or real-time applications is left for future work. Future work may explore extensions to real-time color image encryption, adaptive parameter tuning, and integration with machine learning frameworks for intelligent multimedia protection.
